This guide goes deeper into **3.3.11.3 Nucleophilic properties**, the third subtopic of section 3.3.11 Amines in the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International A2 content, assessed in Unit 4 (Organic 2 and Physical 2) and in the synoptic Unit 5. It brings together two mechanisms you meet elsewhere in the course, nucleophilic substitution and nucleophilic addition–elimination, and asks you to apply them with ammonia and amines as the nucleophile.

## What the specification asks for

- **Amines are nucleophiles.**
- The **nucleophilic substitution** reactions of ammonia and amines with halogenoalkanes to form **primary, secondary and tertiary amines and quaternary ammonium salts**.
- The use of **quaternary ammonium salts as cationic surfactants**.
- The **nucleophilic addition–elimination** reactions of ammonia and primary amines with **acyl chlorides and acid anhydrides**.
- Outline the mechanisms of the substitution reactions and of the addition–elimination reactions of ammonia and primary amines with **acyl chlorides**.

## Why amines are nucleophiles

The lone pair on nitrogen that makes an amine a base (section 3.3.11.2) also lets it attack an electron-deficient carbon. Two kinds of carbon are attacked in this subtopic:

| Electrophilic carbon | Found in | Mechanism | What leaves |
|---|---|---|---|
| sp³ carbon bonded to a halogen, C^δ+–X^δ− | Halogenoalkanes | Nucleophilic substitution | X⁻ |
| Carbonyl carbon bonded to Cl or to O–CO–R | Acyl chlorides, acid anhydrides | Nucleophilic addition–elimination | Cl⁻ or RCOO⁻ |

In both cases the nitrogen ends up with four bonds and a positive charge, so a final step removes H⁺. Usually a second molecule of ammonia or amine acts as the base, which is why equations show two molecules of the nitrogen compound.

## Substitution with halogenoalkanes

Each product still has a lone pair on nitrogen, so it can attack another halogenoalkane. With ammonia and bromoethane:

| Step | Nitrogen compound attacking | Product | Class |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | NH₃ | CH₃CH₂NH₂, ethylamine | primary amine |
| 2 | CH₃CH₂NH₂ | (CH₃CH₂)₂NH, diethylamine | secondary amine |
| 3 | (CH₃CH₂)₂NH | (CH₃CH₂)₃N, triethylamine | tertiary amine |
| 4 | (CH₃CH₂)₃N | (CH₃CH₂)₄N⁺Br⁻, tetraethylammonium bromide | quaternary ammonium salt |

The sequence stops at step 4 because the nitrogen in R₄N⁺ has used its lone pair and has no H to lose. The ratio of reactants decides where the mixture ends up: excess ammonia favours the primary amine; excess halogenoalkane pushes the reaction towards the quaternary salt.

### Worked example 1: outlining the mechanism for step 2

Outline the mechanism for ethylamine reacting with bromoethane.

1. Curly arrow from the **lone pair on N** of CH₃CH₂NH₂ to the δ+ carbon of the C–Br bond.
2. Curly arrow from the **C–Br bond** to Br; the bond breaks heterolytically and Br⁻ leaves.
3. Intermediate: **(CH₃CH₂)₂N⁺H₂**, the diethylammonium ion, with the positive charge on N.
4. A second ethylamine molecule uses its lone pair to remove one H⁺ from N⁺; the N–H bond pair returns to nitrogen.
5. Products: diethylamine and ethylammonium bromide.

`2CH₃CH₂NH₂ + CH₃CH₂Br → (CH₃CH₂)₂NH + CH₃CH₂NH₃⁺Br⁻`

The final step with a tertiary amine forming a quaternary salt has no H⁺ to remove, so the mechanism ends at the ion: (CH₃CH₂)₃N + CH₃CH₂Br → (CH₃CH₂)₄N⁺Br⁻.

### Worked example 2: working backwards to the reagents

Give two different pairs of reagents that could make N,N-dimethylpropylamine, (CH₃)₂NCH₂CH₂CH₃, by nucleophilic substitution.

- Make the last N–C bond to the propyl group: **dimethylamine + 1-bromopropane**.
- Make the last N–C bond to a methyl group: **N-methylpropylamine, CH₃NHCH₂CH₂CH₃, + bromomethane**.

In each case two molecules of the amine are needed, or a further base, to remove the H⁺. Starting from propylamine and excess bromomethane would not stop cleanly at the tertiary amine; the quaternary salt (CH₃)₃N⁺CH₂CH₂CH₃ Br⁻ would also form.

## Quaternary ammonium salts as cationic surfactants

A quaternary ammonium salt with **one long hydrocarbon chain** behaves as a surfactant. An example is hexadecyltrimethylammonium bromide, made from trimethylamine and 1-bromohexadecane:

`(CH₃)₃N + CH₃(CH₂)₁₅Br → CH₃(CH₂)₁₅N⁺(CH₃)₃ Br⁻`

- The **N⁺ head** is ionic and is attracted to water molecules.
- The **long hydrocarbon tail** is non-polar and mixes with oils and grease.
- Because the surface-active part carries a **positive** charge, it is a **cationic** surfactant. Many surfaces, such as fabric fibres and hair, carry negative charges, so the positive heads are attracted to them. This is why such salts are used in fabric softeners and hair conditioners.

A quaternary salt with four short groups, such as tetraethylammonium bromide, has no long tail and does not act as a surfactant.

## Addition–elimination with acyl chlorides and anhydrides

Ammonia gives a primary amide; a primary amine gives an N-substituted amide. With an acyl chloride the by-product is an ammonium chloride salt; with an acid anhydride it is a carboxylic acid (or its ammonium salt).

### Worked example 3: ammonia and propanoyl chloride

Outline the mechanism and give the products.

1. **Arrow 1**: lone pair on N of NH₃ → δ+ carbonyl carbon of CH₃CH₂COCl.
2. **Arrow 2**: C=O π bond pair → O, giving O⁻.
3. **Intermediate**: CH₃CH₂–C(O⁻)(Cl)–N⁺H₃, a tetrahedral carbon with both charges shown.
4. **Arrows 3 and 4**: lone pair on O⁻ re-forms C=O; C–Cl bond pair → Cl, which leaves as Cl⁻.
5. **Arrow 5**: a second NH₃ removes H⁺ from N⁺.
6. Products: **propanamide**, CH₃CH₂CONH₂, and ammonium chloride.

`CH₃CH₂COCl + 2NH₃ → CH₃CH₂CONH₂ + NH₄Cl`

### Worked example 4: an anhydride with a primary amine

Methylamine reacts with ethanoic anhydride. Write the equation and name the organic products.

`(CH₃CO)₂O + CH₃NH₂ → CH₃CONHCH₃ + CH₃COOH`

Products: **N-methylethanamide** and **ethanoic acid**. With a second mole of methylamine, the ethanoic acid is neutralised to methylammonium ethanoate. No mechanism is needed for anhydrides in 9620, but the steps are the same, with an ethanoate ion leaving instead of Cl⁻.

## Telling the two mechanisms apart

| Question to ask | Substitution | Addition–elimination |
|---|---|---|
| What is attacked? | Carbon bonded to a halogen in a halogenoalkane | Carbonyl carbon of an acyl chloride or anhydride |
| Does C=O take part? | No | Yes: it opens, then re-forms |
| Intermediate | R–N⁺ species after the halide leaves | Tetrahedral species with O⁻ and N⁺ |
| Product family | Amine or quaternary salt | Amide |
| Does the product react again? | Yes, it is still a nucleophile | Not in this subtopic; amides are not used as nucleophiles |

## Common mistakes

- Stopping at the primary amine when the question gives excess halogenoalkane.
- Drawing a quaternary salt that still has H on nitrogen, or a lone pair on N⁺.
- Showing only one molecule of ammonia or amine in the equation.
- Calling a quaternary salt with four short groups a surfactant.
- Saying the surfactant is cationic "because it contains bromide"; the cation is the long-chain ammonium ion.
- Calling the acyl chloride mechanism "substitution", or missing the O⁻ in the intermediate.
- Giving HCl as a product of an acid anhydride.

## Exam technique

- **Outline a mechanism**: arrows from the lone pair and from the breaking bond, the charged intermediate, and the loss of H⁺ with the base named.
- **Explain** why a mixture forms: each product still has a lone pair on N.
- **Explain** a surfactant: positive ionic head, long non-polar tail, and what each interacts with.
- **Deduce** reagents: identify the last bond made to nitrogen and choose the halogenoalkane or acyl compound for that group.

The acyl-compound side of these reactions is covered in the [acylation study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-9-2/), and the use of ammonia with halogenoalkanes to prepare amines is in the [amine preparation study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-11-1/).
