## Syllabus coverage

Candidates should be able to:

1. define electronegativity as the power of an atom to attract electrons to itself;
2. explain the factors influencing the electronegativities of the elements in terms of nuclear charge, atomic radius and shielding by inner shells and sub-shells;
3. state and explain the trends in electronegativity across a period and down a group of the Periodic Table;
4. use the differences in Pauling electronegativity values to predict the formation of ionic and covalent bonds. The presence of covalent character in some ionic compounds is not assessed, and Pauling values are given where needed.

## The definition

**Electronegativity is the power of an atom to attract electrons to itself.** In practice it describes how strongly an atom in a covalent bond pulls the shared pair of electrons towards its own nucleus.

Electronegativity is measured on the **Pauling scale**, which has no units. Fluorine, the most electronegative element, has a value of 4.0. Noble gases are usually not given values, because the lighter ones form no compounds.

## The three factors

| Factor | Effect | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Nuclear charge | Higher electronegativity | More protons attract the bonding electrons more strongly |
| Atomic radius | Smaller atom, higher electronegativity | Bonding electrons are closer to the nucleus |
| Shielding by inner shells and sub-shells | More shielding, lower electronegativity | Inner electrons repel the bonding electrons and reduce the attraction they feel |

These are the same factors that decide atomic radius and ionisation energy, which is why the three trends match: small atoms with high nuclear charge and little shielding have high ionisation energies *and* high electronegativities.

## Trend across a period

Pauling electronegativities (given to one decimal place, as in exam data):

| Period 2 | Li | Be | B | C | N | O | F |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Electronegativity | 1.0 | 1.6 | 2.0 | 2.6 | 3.0 | 3.4 | 4.0 |

| Period 3 | Na | Mg | Al | Si | P | S | Cl |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Electronegativity | 0.9 | 1.3 | 1.6 | 1.9 | 2.2 | 2.6 | 3.2 |

**Explanation.** Across a period, nuclear charge increases by one each element. The bonding electrons are in the same shell and the inner shells are unchanged, so shielding stays roughly the same. Atomic radius decreases. The bonding pair is therefore attracted more strongly, and electronegativity increases.

Electronegativity rises steadily across a period, without the dips seen in ionisation energy. Those dips depend on which sub-shell a single electron is removed from; electronegativity is about attracting a bonding pair, so it follows the overall pull of the nucleus.

## Trend down a group

| Group 17 | F | Cl | Br | I |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Electronegativity | 4.0 | 3.2 | 3.0 | 2.7 |

**Explanation.** Down a group, each element has an extra occupied shell. Atomic radius increases, so the bonding electrons are further from the nucleus, and there are more inner shells shielding them. Nuclear charge increases too, but its effect is outweighed by the greater distance and shielding. Electronegativity decreases.

### Worked example: comparing two elements

*Explain why fluorine is more electronegative than chlorine.*

Model answer: Fluorine's atom is smaller than chlorine's, with its outer electrons in the second shell rather than the third [1]. A fluorine atom has fewer inner shells, so there is less shielding of the bonding pair [1]. Although chlorine has a greater nuclear charge, the bonding electrons in fluorine are closer to the nucleus and less shielded, so they are attracted more strongly [1].

*Explain why oxygen is more electronegative than nitrogen.*

Model answer: Oxygen has a greater nuclear charge (8 compared with 7) [1]. The bonding electrons are in the same shell with the same inner shell (1s²), so shielding is similar, and oxygen's atomic radius is smaller [1]. The bonding pair is attracted more strongly by oxygen [1].

Notice the structure: say which factor differs, which stays similar, and conclude about the attraction for the bonding pair.

## Predicting bond type from electronegativity difference

When two atoms bond, the difference in their electronegativities shows how evenly the electrons are shared.

- **Zero difference:** electrons shared equally; a **non-polar covalent** bond, as in Cl₂ or H₂.
- **Small difference:** electrons shared unequally; a **polar covalent** bond. The more electronegative atom carries a partial negative charge, δ−, and the other δ+.
- **Large difference:** one atom pulls the electrons almost completely to itself, forming ions; an **ionic** bond.

There is no sharp cut-off. As a rough guide used in many textbooks, a difference of about 2 or more usually indicates ionic bonding, and a smaller difference indicates covalent bonding, more polar as the difference increases. In the exam, Pauling values are given and you compare them.

### Worked example: classifying bonds

| Compound or bond | Values | Difference | Prediction |
|---|---|---|---|
| KF | K 0.8, F 4.0 | 3.2 | Ionic |
| NaCl | Na 0.9, Cl 3.2 | 2.3 | Ionic |
| MgO | Mg 1.3, O 3.4 | 2.1 | Ionic |
| O–H in water | H 2.2, O 3.4 | 1.2 | Polar covalent, O is δ− |
| H–Cl | H 2.2, Cl 3.2 | 1.0 | Polar covalent, Cl is δ− |
| C–H in methane | H 2.2, C 2.6 | 0.4 | Covalent, only slightly polar |
| Cl–Cl | 3.2, 3.2 | 0 | Non-polar covalent |

### Worked example: ranking bond polarity

Rank C–F, C–Cl, C–Br and C–I by polarity (C 2.6, F 4.0, Cl 3.2, Br 3.0, I 2.7).

1. Differences: C–F 1.4; C–Cl 0.6; C–Br 0.4; C–I 0.1.
2. Most polar to least: **C–F > C–Cl > C–Br > C–I**.
3. In every case the halogen is δ− because it is more electronegative than carbon.

### Worked example: two elements of the same period

Element A has electronegativity 0.9 and element B has 3.2. Both are in Period 3.

1. A is at the left of the period (low electronegativity): sodium. B is near the right: chlorine.
2. Difference 2.3: the compound is ionic, A⁺B⁻ (Na⁺Cl⁻).
3. If B combined with an element of electronegativity 2.6 (sulfur), the difference of 0.6 predicts covalent bonding, as in sulfur chlorides.

## Common mistakes

- Defining electronegativity as the power to attract "an electron" to form a negative ion. That describes electron affinity, a different quantity.
- Giving electronegativity units.
- Explaining the trend across a period with "more shielding". Shielding is roughly constant.
- Explaining the trend down a group with nuclear charge alone. Distance and shielding outweigh it.
- Labelling the less electronegative atom δ−.
- Stating a fixed cut-off as a rule. Compare the given values and reason from them.
- Discussing covalent character in ionic compounds, which this subtopic does not assess.

## Exam technique

- **Define**: use the syllabus wording, "the power of an atom to attract electrons to itself", and add "in a covalent bond" for clarity.
- **Explain a trend**: name all three factors and say which changes and which stays similar.
- **Predict bonding**: calculate the difference, state it, then state the bond type and which atom is δ−.
- **Compare two elements**: write one sentence per factor, then a conclusion.
