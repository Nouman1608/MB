Section 11.5 of the Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 syllabus (2026–2028) has six learning outcomes. You must be able to state that alkenes contain a carbon–carbon double bond and are unsaturated hydrocarbons; describe the manufacture of alkenes and hydrogen by cracking "using a high temperature and a catalyst"; describe the reasons for cracking; describe the aqueous bromine test for unsaturation; state that "in an addition reaction only one product is formed"; and describe the addition of bromine, hydrogen and steam, drawing the products. 5070 has no Core/Extended split, so every outcome is examined for every candidate.

The broader [Petroleum, Alkanes and Alkenes guide](/resources/petroleum-alkanes-and-alkenes/) introduces cracking and the bromine test. This guide goes further: balancing and completing cracking equations, giving reasons for cracking that score, and predicting addition products for propene and the butenes as well as ethene.

## Bonding: the C=C double bond

Alkenes are hydrocarbons whose molecules contain a **carbon–carbon double covalent bond**, C=C. Because at least one carbon–carbon bond is not single, alkenes are **unsaturated**. Their general formula is CₙH₂ₙ.

| Alkene | Structural formula |
|---|---|
| Ethene | CH₂=CH₂ |
| Propene | CH₃CH=CH₂ |
| But-1-ene | CH₃CH₂CH=CH₂ |
| But-2-ene | CH₃CH=CHCH₃ |

The double bond is the **functional group**. It is the reactive site: every reaction in this section happens there.

## Cracking

**Cracking** breaks larger alkane molecules into smaller molecules using a **high temperature and a catalyst**. The products are:

- a smaller alkane and an alkene, or
- an alkene and hydrogen

```
C10H22 → C8H18 + C2H4        (decane → octane + ethene)
C2H6  → C2H4 + H2            (ethane → ethene + hydrogen)
```

Because some products are alkenes, cracking is the main way alkenes are manufactured; very little alkene is present in petroleum itself.

### Balancing and completing cracking equations

Atoms are only rearranged, so the numbers of carbon and hydrogen atoms must be equal on both sides.

**Worked example 1.** Complete the equation: C₁₄H₃₀ → C₁₀H₂₂ + ......

*Answer.* Carbon: 14 − 10 = 4. Hydrogen: 30 − 22 = 8. Missing product: **C₄H₈** [1], which fits CₙH₂ₙ, so it is an alkene (a butene) [1].

**Worked example 2.** Dodecane, C₁₂H₂₆, is cracked to give octane, C₈H₁₈, and ethene only. Write the balanced equation.

*Answer.* Carbon left after octane: 12 − 8 = 4, which is two C₂H₄. Check hydrogen: 18 + (2 × 4) = 26.

```
C12H26 → C8H18 + 2C2H4
```

A quick check: if one product is an alkane (CₙH₂ₙ₊₂) and the starting molecule is an alkane, the other products must be alkenes, because the "+2" hydrogens can only appear once.

### Reasons for cracking

Examiners want reasons that link supply, demand and usefulness:

1. **Fractional distillation produces more long-chain fractions than are needed**, and fewer short-chain fractions than are needed. Cracking converts the less useful long-chain molecules into shorter ones.
2. **Short-chain alkanes are in high demand as fuels**, for example in the gasoline (petrol) fraction.
3. **Alkenes are produced**, which are needed to make polymers (section 11.8) and other chemicals such as ethanol (section 11.6).
4. **Hydrogen is produced**, which is also a useful product.

**Worked example 4.** The table shows the supply of two fractions from fractional distillation and the demand for them. The figures are invented for practice.

| Fraction | Supply / % of petroleum | Demand / % of petroleum |
|---|---|---|
| Gasoline | 20 | 30 |
| Fuel oil | 35 | 20 |

Use the data to explain why fuel oil is cracked. [2]

*Annotated answer.* "The supply of fuel oil (35%) is greater than the demand (20%), but the demand for gasoline (30%) is greater than its supply (20%) [1]. Cracking breaks the long-chain molecules in fuel oil into shorter-chain molecules that can be used in gasoline, so the surplus is converted into a product that is needed [1]."

Quote figures from the table: a data question rewards using the data.

"Cracking makes smaller molecules" describes the process but is not a reason. Say **why** smaller molecules and alkenes are wanted.

## The test for unsaturation

| Hydrocarbon shaken with aqueous bromine | Observation |
|---|---|
| Alkene (unsaturated) | orange to **colourless** |
| Alkane (saturated) | stays **orange** |

The alkene decolourises the bromine because bromine adds across the double bond. "Clear" is not the same as "colourless": aqueous bromine is clear (transparent) but orange. Write colourless.

## Addition reactions

In an **addition reaction**, two molecules join to form **only one product**. The double bond opens, and one new atom or group bonds to each carbon that was in the C=C.

| Reagent | Condition | Product from ethene |
|---|---|---|
| Bromine or aqueous bromine | room temperature | 1,2-dibromoethane, CH₂BrCH₂Br |
| Hydrogen | nickel catalyst | ethane, CH₃CH₃ |
| Steam | acid catalyst | ethanol, CH₃CH₂OH |

```
CH2=CH2 + Br2  → CH2BrCH2Br
CH2=CH2 + H2   → CH3CH3
CH2=CH2 + H2O  → CH3CH2OH
```

Displayed formula of 1,2-dibromoethane:

```
      Br  Br
      |   |
  H - C - C - H
      |   |
      H   H
```

Each product is saturated: the C=C has become C–C. That is why the product of adding bromine no longer reacts with bromine.

The industrial conditions for steam addition to make ethanol (300 °C, 6000 kPa and an acid catalyst) are part of section 11.6; see [alcohols](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-alcohols/).

### Predicting products for larger alkenes

Add one atom or group to **each carbon of the double bond**. Leave the rest of the chain unchanged.

**Worked example 3.** Give the name and structural formula of the product when each reagent reacts with but-1-ene, CH₃CH₂CH=CH₂.

*Answer.*

| Reagent | Product | Structural formula |
|---|---|---|
| Bromine | 1,2-dibromobutane | CH₃CH₂CHBrCH₂Br |
| Hydrogen (Ni) | butane | CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃ |
| Steam (acid catalyst) | butan-1-ol or butan-2-ol | CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂OH or CH₃CH₂CH(OH)CH₃ |

With steam, the –OH can attach to either carbon of an unsymmetrical double bond, so two alcohols are possible. With a symmetrical alkene such as but-2-ene, both carbons are equivalent, so only butan-2-ol forms.

## Common mistakes

- Writing "cracking makes smaller molecules" as a reason for cracking.
- Leaving out the catalyst or high temperature as conditions.
- Saying bromine water turns "clear".
- Writing two products for an addition reaction, such as HBr alongside the dibromo compound.
- Putting both bromine atoms on the same carbon.
- Forgetting the nickel catalyst for hydrogen, or the acid catalyst for steam.
- Writing a cracking equation that does not balance for hydrogen.

## Exam technique: the command words

- **State**: "contains a C=C double bond"; "unsaturated"; "only one product".
- **Describe** (cracking): larger alkanes, high temperature, catalyst, products are smaller alkanes and alkenes (or alkenes and hydrogen).
- **Describe** (test): reagent, the colour before and after for both the alkene and the alkane.
- **Draw**: the product with the former double bond now single and the new atoms on adjacent carbons.
