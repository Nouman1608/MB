## Why this matters in the exam

- Paper 1 is a guided analysis of unseen literary passages "from different text types": 1 h 15 min
  at SL, 2 h 15 min at HL, 35% of the grade at both levels. A poem can appear.
- The brief's assessment objectives include "features of particular text types and literary forms"
  and "ways in which the use of language creates meaning". Poetry analysis tests both directly.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition | Quick example |
|---|---|---|
| Speaker | The voice in the poem; not assumed to be the poet | "I" in a dramatic monologue |
| Addressee | The person or thing spoken to | "you" in a love sonnet |
| Apostrophe | Direct address to an absent person or an object | "O moon, ..." |
| Tone | The speaker's attitude | wry, tender, bitter |
| Iamb / trochee | da-DUM / DUM-da | "a-WAY" / "LIS-ten" |
| Iambic pentameter | Five iambs, ten syllables | "re-MEM-ber ME when I am GONE a-WAY" |
| Iambic tetrameter | Four iambs, eight syllables | Blake's "London", line 1 |
| Enjambment | Sentence runs over the line break | "his hands / remember" |
| Caesura | Pause inside a line | "The kettle clicks. The house..." |
| End-stopped line | Sense pauses at the line end | line ends with a full stop |
| Volta | Turn in argument or mood | line 9 of a Petrarchan sonnet |
| Anaphora | Repetition at the start of lines | "In every..., / In every..." |
| Alliteration | Repeated initial consonants | "fickle, freckled" |
| Assonance | Repeated vowel sounds | "cold, slow, stone" |
| Sibilance | Repeated s/sh sounds | "soft, still, sleeping" |
| Symbol | Concrete thing standing for something larger | a rose for love or beauty |
| Juxtaposition | Contrasting things side by side | "forget and smile" / "remember and be sad" |

## Forms at a glance

| Form | Shape | Typical use of the shape |
|---|---|---|
| Petrarchan sonnet | Octave (abbaabba) + sestet; volta near line 9 | Problem, then response |
| Shakespearean sonnet | Three quatrains + couplet (abab cdcd efef gg) | Argument, then a closing twist |
| Quatrain stanzas | Four-line units, often abab or abcb | Steady, song-like progress |
| Blank verse | Unrhymed iambic pentameter | Speech-like, flexible thought |
| Free verse | No fixed metre or rhyme | Line breaks carry emphasis |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Speaker vs poet.** Rossetti's "Remember" (1862) is spoken by someone imagining her own death.
  Write "the speaker".
- **Form vs structure.** Form is the shape (sonnet, quatrains). Structure is the movement (where it
  turns, what repeats, how it ends).
- **Metre vs rhythm.** Metre is the regular pattern. Rhythm is how the lines actually move, including
  departures from the pattern.
- **Enjambment vs caesura.** Enjambment breaks the line but not the sentence. Caesura breaks the
  sentence but not the line.
- **Metaphor vs symbol.** A metaphor makes an explicit transfer ("mind-forged manacles"). A symbol is
  a literal thing in the poem that also points beyond itself.
- **Alliteration vs assonance.** Consonant at the start of words vs vowel sounds anywhere.
- **Tone vs mood.** Tone belongs to the speaker. Mood is the atmosphere the reader feels.

## Method in steps: reading an unseen poem

```
1  Read twice. Write one sentence: what is the poem about?
2  Voice: who speaks, to whom, in what tone?
3  Find the turn. What changes, and at which line?
4  Form: count lines and stanzas; mark the rhyme scheme.
5  Scan two or three key lines; note any break in the pattern.
6  Circle the strongest images and sound patterns near the turn.
7  Choose 2-3 features that best explain the change in step 3.
```

## Method in steps: the analytical paragraph

```
Claim      -> answers the question in one sentence
Evidence   -> short quotation, embedded in your sentence
Technique  -> precise name of the method
Effect     -> what it does to meaning or to the reader
Link       -> back to the question and the whole poem
```

## Worked reminder: scansion, rhyme and pauses

> The kettle clicks. The house is holding still.
> Upstairs, a door; below, the clock's dry tick.
> You said you'd ring by six. The window-sill
> collects the dark, and every sound is quick.
>
> *Original text written for Marlbridge.*

- **Metre:** each line has ten syllables in a da-DUM pattern: iambic pentameter.
  `the KET-tle CLICKS. the HOUSE is HOLD-ing STILL`
- **Rhyme:** still/sill and tick/quick, so the scheme is abab.
- **Caesura:** full stops inside lines 1 and 3 break the regular metre into short, tense units. The
  speaker is listening, and the pauses make you listen too.
- **Voice:** "You said you'd ring by six" reveals an addressee and a promise. The quatrain is about
  waiting for a call that has not come.
- **Imagery:** "The window-sill / collects the dark" is personification, carried over the line break
  by enjambment. The dark seems to gather slowly, like the speaker's worry.
- **Link:** the steady iambic beat mirrors the ticking clock, while the caesuras show the tension
  underneath the calm.

## Worked reminder: sound tied to meaning

Hopkins's "Pied Beauty" (written 1877, first published 1918) praises "dappled things": things
marked with contrast. The paired opposites "swift, slow; sweet, sour; adazzle, dim" place contrasting
words side by side, split by commas and semicolons. The sound and punctuation enact the idea: variety
itself is the beauty. That link, from sound to idea, is the whole point of noting sound.

## Worked reminder: weak vs strong analysis

Blake's "London" (*Songs of Experience*, 1794; modernised spelling) gives a clear contrast.

- **Weak:** "Blake uses repetition in 'In every cry of every man'. This makes the poem more
  effective." The device is named, but the effect is generic and could fit any poem.
- **Better:** "The anaphora of 'In every' stretches the suffering across the whole city." The effect
  is specific, but it stops short of meaning.
- **Strong:** "The anaphora of 'In every' refuses to single anyone out: man, infant and voice are
  swept into one pattern, so misery becomes the city's normal condition rather than a private
  misfortune. The metaphor 'mind-forged manacles' then names the cause as thought and habit, not
  iron."

The strong version keeps the quotations short and embedded, names the technique precisely, and moves
from effect to an idea about the whole poem. That last step is what turns spotting into analysis.

## Quick self-test

1. How many lines are in the sestet of a Petrarchan sonnet?
2. How many syllables are there in a regular line of iambic tetrameter?
3. Give the rhyme scheme of the original quatrain above.
4. In line 1 of the quatrain, where is the caesura?
5. Name the device in "The window-sill / collects the dark".
6. At which line does the volta of Rossetti's "Remember" fall, and which word signals it?
7. What is the difference between enjambment and caesura?
8. Name the sound device in "fickle, freckled".
9. Why should you write "the speaker" rather than "the poet"?
10. Give the stress pattern of a trochee.
11. What is the difference between form and structure?
12. Hopkins called his own rhythm by what name?

### Answers

1. Six.
2. Eight (four iambs).
3. abab.
4. After "clicks." (the full stop splits the line).
5. Personification (with enjambment carrying it across the line break).
6. Line 9, signalled by "Yet".
7. Enjambment runs the sentence over the line break; caesura is a pause within the line.
8. Alliteration (repeated "f" at the start of both words).
9. The voice in the poem may be a persona or imagined situation, so it cannot be assumed to be the poet.
10. Stressed then unstressed (DUM-da).
11. Form is the poem's shape (type, stanza, rhyme scheme); structure is how it moves and turns.
12. Sprung rhythm.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Spotting a feature and naming it without saying what it does to meaning.
- Claiming a metre from the look of a line instead of counting syllables and marking stresses.
- Saying a rhyme scheme is "regular" without giving the letters or asking why it breaks.
- Writing "enjambment creates flow" instead of explaining what the delayed word adds.
- Missing the volta, so the answer treats the poem as one unchanging mood.
- Dropping quotations in as separate sentences rather than embedding short phrases.
- Retelling the poem stanza by stanza with no controlling claim.
- Offering one fixed meaning for a symbol when the poem supports more than one reading.
- Ignoring the addressee: a hidden "you" often changes the tone of the whole poem.
- Using vague effect words ("interesting", "powerful", "engages the reader") with nothing specific
  behind them.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between form and structure in a poem?**
Form is the poem's shape, such as its type, stanza pattern and rhyme scheme. Structure is how the poem moves: where it turns, what repeats and how it ends.

**What is the difference between metre and rhythm?**
Metre is the regular pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables. Rhythm is how the lines actually move, including departures from that pattern.

**What is the difference between a metaphor and a symbol?**
A metaphor makes an explicit transfer, such as Blake's "mind-forged manacles". A symbol is a literal thing in the poem that also points beyond itself.

**What is a volta?**
A volta is a turn in a poem's argument or mood. In a Petrarchan sonnet it usually falls at line 9, as in Rossetti's "Remember", where line 9 begins with "Yet".
