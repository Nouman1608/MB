This guide goes deeper on one part of Component 2 of Pearson Edexcel International GCSE English
Literature (4ET1): **Section A, Modern Drama**, the first of the two essays on the 1 hour 30 minute
open-book Paper 2. For the whole paper, including the literary heritage essay, start with the
[Component 2 study guide](/resources/igcse-edexcel-english-literature-component-2/).

## What Section A covers

You study one play from the specification's modern drama list and answer **one essay question from a
choice of two** on it. The essay is worth **30 marks**: **15 for AO1** and **15 for AO2**. With two
equal essays in 90 minutes, allow about **45 minutes**, including planning.

The modern drama list currently has five plays: *A View from the Bridge* (Arthur Miller), *An Inspector
Calls* (J B Priestley), *The Curious Incident of the Dog in the Night-time* (Mark Haddon, adapted by
Simon Stephens), *Kindertransport* (Diane Samuels) and *Death and the King's Horseman* (Wole Soyinka).
Set texts change when Pearson revises the specification, so confirm the list for your series.

The exam is **open book**: you may take in the prescribed edition of your play, but it must be
completely unmarked. Component 2 can be taken as a paper-based or onscreen exam; the essay task is the
same.

If your centre follows the coursework route, the same play is assessed through Component 3 Assignment A
instead; the skills on this page still apply, but the conditions differ (see the
[Component 3 study guide](/resources/igcse-edexcel-english-literature-component-3/)).

## The two assessment objectives

- **AO1**: close knowledge and understanding of the play, a critical style and an informed personal
  response. You need an argument that answers the question and is supported by precise reference.
- **AO2**: analysis of language, form and structure and the meanings and effects they create. In a
  play, "form" and "structure" include the dramatic choices that make it theatre.

**AO4 (context) is not assessed in this section.** It is assessed in Section B on the literary heritage
text. You may mention context briefly if it sharpens an interpretation, but marks here come from
argument and analysis of the playwright's methods, so do not spend paragraphs on history.

## Key idea: write about a play as a play

The most common limitation in drama essays is treating the play like a novel: discussing characters as
real people and quoting only dialogue. A playwright works with more tools than words, and AO2 rewards
you for noticing them.

| Dramatic method | Questions to ask |
|---|---|
| Stage directions | What does the playwright tell us about lighting, sound, movement, pauses or tone, and why? |
| Entrances and exits | Who arrives or leaves at a key moment, and what does the timing do? |
| Dramatic irony | What does the audience know that a character does not? |
| Narrators and framing devices | Who speaks to the audience directly, and how does that guide our response? |
| Structure | How do act endings, cliffhangers, time shifts or a circular shape create tension or meaning? |
| Dialogue | Who interrupts, who has the long speeches, who goes silent? |
| Staging and setting | What does the set suggest, and does it stay the same or change? |
| Characters as devices | Does a character carry the playwright's message or test the others? |

Always link the method to the **audience**: "an audience would feel..." or "on stage, this would...".

## Applying the methods to the set plays

Each play offers distinctive dramatic methods to analyse:

- *An Inspector Calls*: the opening stage direction asks for lighting that changes from soft and
  intimate to brighter and harder when the Inspector arrives; the doorbell interrupts Birling's
  speech; dramatic irony arises because an audience after 1945 knows more than characters in 1912.
- *A View from the Bridge*: Alfieri speaks directly to the audience as a narrator and chorus-like
  figure, framing Eddie's story as a tragedy whose ending is foreseen.
- *Kindertransport*: the child Eva and the adult Evelyn share the stage, so past and present overlap
  and the audience sees cause and consequence side by side.
- *The Curious Incident of the Dog in the Night-time*: Stephens uses narration from Christopher's
  writing and highly physical staging to let the audience experience the world from his perspective.
- *Death and the King's Horseman*: music, drumming, dance and ritual language are central to meaning;
  Soyinka's own note to the play cautions against reading it simply as a clash of cultures.

## Worked example 1: a character question

**Question:** How does Priestley present the Inspector as a powerful figure? [30]

**Overview:** Priestley presents the Inspector as a figure whose power comes not from rank but from
moral authority and control of the drama itself: he controls when information is revealed, who speaks
and, finally, what the family is forced to see.

**Plan:**

1. **Entrance:** the timing of the doorbell, interrupting Birling's confident speech about looking after
   oneself, establishes the Inspector as a challenge to Birling's views before he has spoken.
2. **Control of structure:** he questions one character at a time, so each act reveals a new link to the
   victim; this structure builds tension and makes him the engine of the play.
3. **Language and stage directions:** his speech is direct and he takes charge of the conversation,
   which contrasts with the family's attempts to evade.
4. **Exit and ending:** his final speech and departure, followed by the twist of the telephone call,
   leave his status ambiguous and his message with the audience.

**Sample paragraph (paragraph 1):**

> Priestley establishes the Inspector's power through the timing of his entrance. The doorbell rings
> just as Birling is telling the young men that a man should look after himself and his own, so the
> Inspector's arrival seems to answer Birling's selfish view before a word is spoken. The lighting
> change Priestley asks for when the Inspector enters, from a soft, intimate glow to something
> "brighter and harder", suggests that comfortable illusions are about to be exposed. For an audience,
> the Inspector is therefore powerful from the start because the play itself seems to be on his side.

The paragraph makes a claim (AO1), analyses structure and stage directions with their effect on an
audience (AO2) and uses the open-book text precisely.

## Worked example 2: a tension question

If asked how tension is created in a key scene of *A View from the Bridge*, choose a moment where staging
carries meaning, such as the end of Act One, when Marco lifts the chair. Analyse what the audience sees
(a silent physical action replacing words), the shift in power between Eddie and Marco, and how
Alfieri's framing has already told us the story will end badly, so the audience watches with dread.
The method is the same as for any question: claim, method, effect on the audience.

## Open-book technique

- **Know where things are.** Practise finding key scenes, speeches and stage directions quickly from
  act and scene divisions, using a separate study copy for your notes.
- **Use the book, do not copy it.** Quote a few words; long quotations waste time and hide your point.
- **Plan first.** Decide your three or four moments before opening the text, then look up the evidence.
- **Watch the clock.** Searching the play can swallow time; limit yourself to a quick check per point.

## Common mistakes

- Writing about characters as real people instead of as the playwright's creations.
- Quoting only dialogue and ignoring stage directions, structure and staging.
- Retelling the plot scene by scene.
- Adding long context paragraphs, which earn no AO4 credit in this section.
- Spending too long on the drama essay and leaving Section B short. AO4 marks are only available there.
- Arriving with an annotated or marked exam copy, which is not permitted.

## Exam technique and command words

Section A questions often ask **how** the playwright presents a character, relationship or theme, or
invite you to **explore** its importance or the way tension is created. "How" signals AO2: methods and
effects. Your first sentence should answer the question directly. Briefly weigh both questions before
choosing: pick the one that lets you use the scenes you know best and range across the whole play.
