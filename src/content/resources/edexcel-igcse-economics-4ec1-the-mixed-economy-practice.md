These questions cover section 1.1.5, The mixed economy, of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Economics (4EC1) specification, Issue 3, February 2026. There is a single tier of entry. All countries, firms and figures are invented. Read the [mixed economy study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-economics-4ec1-the-mixed-economy/) and the [mixed economy revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-economics-4ec1-the-mixed-economy-revision-notes/) first.

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** What is meant by a mixed economy? **[2]**

**2.** Which of the following is a public good?

A: a seat on a train

B: a lighthouse

C: a private swimming pool

D: a university place

**[1]**

**3.** Define privatisation. **[2]**

**4.** State **one** difference between the public sector and the private sector in terms of ownership. **[2]**

**5.** What is meant by non-rivalry? **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** A village of 2 400 households would benefit from a flood barrier costing 36 000. Each household values the barrier at 25.

**(a)** Calculate the total benefit of the barrier to the village. **[1]**

**(b)** A private firm asks households to pay 25 each voluntarily. Only 900 do so. Calculate the firm's shortfall. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why so few households pay. **[3]**

**(d)** The council instead charges every household an equal share of the cost through local taxes. Calculate the net gain to each household. **[2]**

**7.** The table shows invented data for two countries.

| Country | Government spending (billion) | National output (billion) |
|---|---|---|
| R | 210 | 600 |
| S | 72 | 480 |

**(a)** Calculate government spending as a percentage of national output for each country. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain **one** reason why the relative importance of the public sector may differ between the two countries. **[3]**

**8.** Explain how a mixed economy decides **for whom** goods and services are produced. **[3]**

**9.** Explain why market failure may lead a government to intervene. **[3]**

**10.** Country V privatised its state electricity company. After privatisation the average monthly bill rose from 48 to 54, and the number of employees fell from 15 000 to 12 300.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in the average monthly bill. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in the number of employees. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **one** possible reason why bills rose after privatisation. **[3]**

**11.** Evaluate the effects of privatisation on consumers and the government. **[8]**

**12.** Explain why street lighting is usually provided by the public sector. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** An economy in which resources are allocated by both the private sector [1]; and the public sector (government) [1]. **[2]**

**2.** B [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* A train seat, a private pool and a university place can all exclude non-payers, so none is a public good.

**3.** The transfer of ownership of a business or asset [1]; from the public sector to the private sector [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Private-sector firms are owned by individuals or shareholders [1]; whereas the public sector is owned by the government on behalf of the public [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Both sectors must appear in the answer for the second mark.

**5.** When one person consumes the good [1]; the amount available for others is not reduced [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** 2 400 × 25 = **60 000** [1].

**(b)** Revenue = 900 × 25 = 22 500 [1]; shortfall = 36 000 − 22 500 = **13 500** [1].

**(c)** The barrier is non-excludable [1]; households that do not pay are protected anyway [1]; so many choose to free ride, leaving the firm without enough revenue [1].

**(d)** Tax = 36 000 ÷ 2 400 = 15 per household [1]; net gain = 25 − 15 = **10** per household [1]. **[8]**

**7. (a)** R: 210 ÷ 600 × 100 = **35%** [1]; S: 72 ÷ 480 × 100 = **15%** [1].

**(b)** Political choices differ: Country R's government may choose to provide more free services such as healthcare and education [1]; this requires higher taxes [1]; so public spending is a larger share of output than in S, which leaves more to the private sector [1]. (Also accept: different income levels; history of state ownership.) **[5]**

**8.** The private sector allocates goods to those willing and able to pay [1]; the government changes this through taxes and benefits [1]; and by providing services such as healthcare or education free or below cost to those who need them [1]. **[3]**

**9.** The free market may allocate resources inefficiently [1]; for example, it may not provide public goods at all, or provide the wrong amount of goods with externalities [1]; so government intervenes, through provision, taxes, subsidies or regulation, to improve the allocation [1]. **[3]**

**10. (a)** (54 − 48) ÷ 48 × 100 [1] = **+12.5%** [1].

**(b)** (12 300 − 15 000) ÷ 15 000 × 100 [1] = **−18%** [1].

**(c)** The privatised company aims to make a profit [1]; if it has no competitors it is a monopoly supplier [1]; so it can raise prices without losing many customers [1]. **[7]**

**11.** Consumers, gains: competition may lower prices [1]; and the profit motive may improve quality and service [1]. Consumers, losses: a private monopoly may raise prices [1]; unprofitable services may be cut [1]. Government, gains: one-off revenue from the sale [1]; no longer paying subsidies [1]. Government, losses: loses direct control and any future profits [1]. Judgement: the effects depend on whether competition or regulation follows; a supported conclusion [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluate answers need both groups, both sides and a conclusion that names the deciding factor.

**12.** Street lighting is non-excludable, so people who do not pay still benefit [1]; this creates a free rider problem, so a private firm cannot raise enough revenue [1]; therefore it would not be provided by the market and the government funds it through taxation [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving a public-sector feature without the matching private-sector feature.
- Defining a public good by who supplies it.
- Forgetting to divide by the original value in percentage changes.
- Privatisation evaluations that ignore competition.
- Free rider answers that do not link to the lack of revenue.
