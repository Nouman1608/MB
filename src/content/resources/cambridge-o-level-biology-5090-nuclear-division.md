This guide covers subtopic **16.1 Nuclear division** of Cambridge O Level Biology 5090, 2026-2028 series. The syllabus asks you to understand that chromosomes contain DNA, which carries genetic information in the form of genes; to describe haploid and diploid nuclei; to state that a human diploid cell has 23 pairs of chromosomes; to describe mitosis and outline its roles in growth, repair, replacement of cells and asexual reproduction; to describe stem cells; to state that meiosis is involved in producing gametes and describe it as a reduction division; and to understand that cancers form as a result of uncontrolled cell division. The details of the stages of mitosis and meiosis are not required.

The [Development of organisms and continuity of life topic guide](/resources/o-level-biology-5090-development-of-organisms-and-continuity-of-life/) covers the whole topic. This page goes deeper on nuclear division, with worked counting examples. Use the [16.1 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-biology-5090-nuclear-division-practice/) to test yourself.

## Chromosomes, DNA and genes

The nucleus of a cell contains **chromosomes**. Each chromosome is a long molecule of **DNA** with proteins. DNA carries **genetic information in the form of genes**. A gene is a length of DNA that codes for a protein (this is treated in the DNA subtopic). Chromosomes are usually only visible as separate structures when a cell is about to divide.

Each species has a characteristic number of chromosomes in its body cells. In humans the number is 46. The chromosomes come in pairs, so that one chromosome of each pair came from the mother and one from the father.

## Haploid and diploid nuclei

| Term | Meaning | Human example |
|---|---|---|
| Haploid nucleus | A nucleus containing a **single set** of chromosomes | A sperm or egg nucleus: 23 chromosomes |
| Diploid nucleus | A nucleus containing **two sets** of chromosomes | Most body cells: 46 chromosomes |

In a diploid cell there is a **pair of each type of chromosome**. In a human diploid cell there are **23 pairs**, which makes 46 chromosomes. Haploid is written as n and diploid as 2n, so for humans n = 23 and 2n = 46.

Check: 23 pairs x 2 = 46, and half of 46 is 23.

## Mitosis

**Mitosis is nuclear division giving rise to genetically identical cells in which the chromosome number is maintained.** A diploid cell divides to form two diploid cells. Before the division the cell makes a copy of its DNA, so that each new nucleus receives a full set of chromosomes, identical to the original. You do not need to describe the stages.

Key features:

- one division produces **two** daughter cells;
- the daughter cells are **genetically identical** to each other and to the parent cell;
- the chromosome number is the **same** as in the parent cell (diploid to diploid).

### The roles of mitosis

| Role | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Growth | Mitosis increases the number of cells in a growing organism |
| Repair of damaged tissues | New cells replace those lost in a wound |
| Replacement of dying cells | Cells such as skin cells and red blood cells wear out and are replaced |
| Asexual reproduction | Parent organisms produce genetically identical offspring by mitosis |

### Worked example 1: counting cells

*A single cell divides by mitosis every 20 minutes. How many cells are there after 2 hours?*

1. Number of divisions = 120 minutes divided by 20 = 6.
2. Number of cells = 2 to the power 6 = 64.

Check by doubling: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64. The cells are all genetically identical, because each division copies the DNA exactly.

### Worked example 2: chromosome numbers in mitosis

*A fruit fly body cell has 8 chromosomes. How many chromosomes are in each daughter cell after mitosis?*

Mitosis maintains the chromosome number, so each daughter cell has 8 chromosomes. A common mistake is to halve the number.

## Stem cells

**Stem cells are unspecialised cells that divide by mitosis to produce daughter cells that can become specialised for specific functions.** Unspecialised means the cells are not yet adapted to do one job. When a stem cell divides, some daughter cells stay as stem cells, and others change into specialised cells such as muscle, nerve or blood cells. The change is called differentiation.

Stem cells are important because they provide a supply of new cells for growth and for replacing damaged cells. In an exam, you may be asked how stem cells differ from specialised cells: stem cells can divide and form different cell types, while specialised cells have a fixed function and usually do not divide.

## Meiosis

**Meiosis is a reduction division in which the chromosome number is halved from diploid to haploid, resulting in genetically different cells.** It is involved in the production of **gametes** (sperm and egg cells in animals). Without meiosis, the number of chromosomes would double each generation.

Key features:

- a diploid cell divides to make **haploid** cells (four cells in total);
- the cells are **genetically different** from each other and from the parent cell;
- it takes place in the testes and ovaries in humans.

### Why the chromosome number must be halved

At fertilisation, a haploid sperm nucleus fuses with a haploid egg nucleus, forming a diploid zygote. If gametes were diploid, the zygote would have double the normal number of chromosomes. Halving the number in meiosis means that the chromosome number is restored at fertilisation.

### Worked example 3: following human chromosome numbers

| Cell | Chromosome number |
|---|---|
| Body cell (before meiosis) | 46 |
| Sperm or egg cell (after meiosis) | 23 |
| Zygote (after fertilisation) | 23 + 23 = 46 |
| Daughter cells of the zygote (after mitosis) | 46 |

The chromosome number goes 46, 23, 46, 46. The step from 46 to 23 is meiosis. The step from 23 + 23 to 46 is fertilisation. The later divisions that make the embryo are mitosis.

## Worked example 4: reading a chromosome diagram

*A diagram shows a cell with four chromosomes: two long and two short. Each long one has a partner of the same length, and each short one has a partner of the same length. Is the cell haploid or diploid, and how many chromosomes would be in each cell after mitosis and after meiosis?*

1. The chromosomes are in matching pairs (two long, two short), so there are two sets: the cell is **diploid**, with 2n = 4.
2. After mitosis, each of the two daughter cells has 4 chromosomes (the same two pairs).
3. After meiosis, each of the four cells has 2 chromosomes, one from each pair (one long and one short). They are haploid, n = 2.

The matching of pairs is the clue for diploid. A haploid cell would show four chromosomes that are all different in length.

## Why copying the DNA first matters

Before a cell divides, it copies its DNA. If this did not happen, each daughter cell would receive only half of the genetic information, and the chromosome number would fall at every division. In mitosis the copy is shared out so that each daughter cell gets a full set. In meiosis the cell divides twice after one copying, which is how four haploid cells are produced from one diploid cell. You are not asked to describe these stages, but knowing that copying comes first helps you to explain why mitosis keeps the chromosome number constant.

## Where each type of division happens

| Place | Type of division | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Growing root tip or shoot tip of a plant | Mitosis | Growth by making more cells |
| Skin at the edge of a cut | Mitosis | Repair |
| Bone marrow | Mitosis | Replacement of blood cells from stem cells |
| Testes and ovaries | Meiosis | Production of gametes |
| Fertilised egg and early embryo | Mitosis | Growth and development |

## Comparing mitosis and meiosis

| Feature | Mitosis | Meiosis |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Growth, repair, replacement, asexual reproduction | Production of gametes |
| Number of cells produced | 2 | 4 |
| Chromosome number | Maintained (diploid to diploid) | Halved (diploid to haploid) |
| Genetic make-up | Identical to the parent cell | Different from the parent cell and from each other |
| Where in humans | Nearly all body tissues | Testes and ovaries |

## Cancer

**Cancers form as a result of uncontrolled cell division.** Normally, cells divide only when the body needs new cells, and the process is controlled. If something goes wrong with the control, a cell may divide again and again by mitosis, forming a mass of cells called a **tumour**. Some cancer cells can spread to other parts of the body. The syllabus does not ask for causes in this subtopic, but you should link the disease to mitosis that is not controlled. Ionising radiation and some chemicals can increase the rate of mutation, which is dealt with in the inheritance topic.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that mitosis halves the chromosome number. It maintains it.
- Saying that meiosis produces identical cells. They are genetically different.
- Mixing up "haploid" (one set) and "diploid" (two sets).
- Writing that 46 pairs of chromosomes are in a human cell. There are 23 pairs, giving 46.
- Saying that stem cells are specialised.
- Saying that mitosis produces gametes.
- Describing the stages of mitosis when only the outcome is required.
- Describing cancer as an infectious disease. It is uncontrolled cell division.

## Command words

- **Describe**: give the definition and features of mitosis or meiosis.
- **State**: give the chromosome numbers.
- **Outline**: give the main roles of mitosis briefly.
- **Compare**: give similarities and differences, using a table in your head.
- **Explain**: give the reason why meiosis halves the number.
