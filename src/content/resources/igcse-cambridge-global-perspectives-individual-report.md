This guide covers **Component 2 – Individual Report**, the second of
three assessed components in Cambridge IGCSE Global Perspectives
(0457), 2025-2027 series. Global Perspectives is a skills-based
syllabus -- its own specification states that "knowledge of content is
not assessed" -- so this guide focuses on what the Individual Report
actually requires candidates to produce and how it is assessed, since
there is no content taxonomy to learn in the way there is for a
traditional subject.

## Where this fits in 0457

All 0457 candidates sit Component 1 (Written Exam) and Component 3
(Team Project) alongside Component 2. The Individual Report is
internally set by the candidate's own teacher but externally marked
by Cambridge International, worth 30% of the qualification, and is
the component where candidates work most independently, since it must
be entirely the candidate's own work researched and written outside
timed exam conditions.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE IGCSE GLOBAL PERSPECTIVES (0457) — COMPONENT 2 INDIVIDUAL REPORT**

- **Format**: an internally set, externally marked report worth 60 marks. Candidates choose one topic from the syllabus's topic list, identify a global issue within it that divides opinion, and devise a global research question that becomes the report's title. The finished report must be 1500–2000 words, excluding the bibliography or reference list.
- **Skills assessed**: research, analysis, evaluation, reflection and communication -- the same five skills Global Perspectives assesses throughout the qualification, applied here to independent, candidate-led research rather than exam-room responses.
- **Choosing the issue**: candidates first explore the context of their chosen topic, then select a global issue with present-day impact -- one that raises an ethical dilemma, causes conflict, damage, difficulty or hardship, or simply divides opinion -- before formulating a research question with enough scope to explore a real range of perspectives.
- **Perspectives required**: the report must present local and/or national perspectives alongside global perspectives, showing that the candidate understands not just what different people, groups or nations believe but why their perspectives differ -- for example, contrasting the views of local farmers and local politicians on the same issue.
- **Analysis and a course of action**: candidates must analyse the causes of conflict or disagreement and the consequences of the issue, weigh different possible courses of action, and then propose and justify a single course of action to change, improve or resolve the issue, explaining how it could be implemented and what impact it would have.
- **Evidence and referencing**: all material used must be explicitly cited with a clear, consistently applied referencing system (there is no single required style), and candidates must evaluate their sources -- explaining their value to the research and whether each is reliable and credible.
- **Reflection and conclusion**: the report closes with the candidate's own view on the issue as a whole, reflecting on their research findings, their learning, and the perspectives of others, before directly answering the research question they set themselves.
- **The teacher's role**: teachers guide candidates to choose a feasible, manageable topic, help them formulate a genuinely researchable question, support their research and referencing skills, and monitor time management -- but the report itself must be produced independently, since it is marked externally rather than by the candidate's own teacher.

A useful way to picture the finished report is as a short piece of
applied social science rather than a persuasive essay. A candidate
investigating water scarcity, for example, might frame the question
"Should upstream and downstream countries share responsibility
equally for managing a shared river's water supply?" -- immediately
setting up at least two perspectives (an upstream government
prioritising its own agriculture, a downstream government facing
shortages) that the report can research, compare and evaluate before
proposing a specific course of action such as a joint water-sharing
treaty or an independent monitoring body, and explaining realistically
what implementing it would involve.

## How to approach it

Because the Individual Report is not marked against subject content,
the highest-scoring reports are the ones that satisfy the assessment
criteria explicitly rather than simply being well-written on a topic.
Start with the research question, not the topic: a question like "To
what extent should [named country] restrict [named industry] to
protect [named resource]?" already builds in a specific place, a
specific issue and room for more than one perspective, whereas a
broad topic title on its own ("Climate change") does not give
enough structure to organise 1500-2000 words around. Build the report
in the order the criteria expect -- explain the issue and its
different perspectives first, analyse causes and consequences second,
evaluate possible courses of action third, and reflect and conclude
last -- since examiners are marking against this structure, not
rewarding a well-argued essay that happens to cover the same ground
in a different order. Keep a running reference list from the first
source read, rather than trying to reconstruct it at the end, since
incomplete or inconsistent referencing is one of the most common
reasons reports lose marks on the evaluation criterion.

Because assessment is skills-based rather than content-based, the same
five criteria -- research, analysis, evaluation, reflection and
communication -- apply whatever topic a candidate chooses, which
means past exemplar reports on entirely different topics can still be
useful for seeing what a strong analysis or a well-evaluated source
looks like in practice, even when the subject matter itself is
irrelevant to a candidate's own report.
