These questions cover OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630), Version 4.4, section 3.6.1 (Circular motion), which the specification marks as International A-level only. Read the [study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-circular-motion-angular-speed-and-centripetal-force/) first. Where g is needed, use 9.81 N kg⁻¹. Give answers to a sensible number of significant figures.

## Questions

**1.** **(a)** Convert 150° into radians, as a multiple of π. **[1]**
**(b)** Convert 2.4 rad into degrees. **[1]**

**2.** A fan blade turns at a frequency of 3.5 Hz.
**(a)** Calculate the period of the rotation. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the angular speed of the blade. **[2]**

**3.** The drum of a washing machine has a radius of 0.22 m and spins at 1200 revolutions per minute. For a point on the inside surface of the drum, calculate:
**(a)** the frequency in hertz **[1]**
**(b)** the angular speed **[1]**
**(c)** the linear speed **[1]**
**(d)** the centripetal acceleration **[1]**

**4.** A ball on a light string moves at constant speed in a horizontal circle on a smooth table. Explain why the ball accelerates even though its speed is constant, state the direction of the acceleration, and name the force that causes it. **[4]**

**5.** A 0.25 kg bob travels round a horizontal circle of radius 0.90 m on a smooth table, completing 2.2 revolutions every second.
**(a)** Calculate the angular speed of the bob. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the tension in the string. **[2]**
**(c)** State which force provides the centripetal force. **[1]**

**6.** A 12 g token sits on a flat tray that spins at 60 revolutions per minute. The most friction the tray can exert on the token is 0.060 N.
**(a)** Calculate the angular speed of the tray. **[2]**
**(b)** The token is 0.10 m from the axis. Calculate the friction force needed to keep it moving in a circle, and state whether it stays in place. **[3]**
**(c)** The token is moved to 0.14 m from the axis. Show whether it stays in place. **[2]**

**7.** A car of mass 900 kg travels at 11 m s⁻¹ round a flat circular bend of radius 35 m.
**(a)** Calculate the sideways force the road must exert on the car. **[2]**
**(b)** The tyres can supply at most 4.8 kN. Calculate the greatest speed the car can have on this bend. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the greatest speed on a bend of radius 60 m, assuming the same limit. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain, using F = mv²/r, why a larger radius allows a higher greatest speed. **[1]**

**8.** A rubber bung with a mass of 0.080 kg circles at a radius of 0.50 m on a frictionless table. A light string from the bung passes through a smooth hole in the table to a hanging mass M. For each M, a student times 10 steady revolutions (t).

| M / kg | t for 10 revolutions / s | 1/T² / s⁻² |
|---|---|---|
| 0.10 | 12.69 | 0.621 |
| 0.15 | 10.36 | 0.932 |
| 0.20 | 8.97 | 1.243 |
| 0.25 | 8.02 | 1.555 |
| 0.30 | 7.33 | 1.861 |

**(a)** Explain why the tension in the string equals Mg and why this tension is the centripetal force on the bung. **[2]**
**(b)** For M = 0.20 kg, show that the data are consistent with F = mω²r. **[2]**
**(c)** Use the first and last rows to calculate the gradient of a graph of Mg against 1/T². **[2]**
**(d)** Use your gradient to find the mass of the bung. **[2]**

**9.** A satellite of mass 1200 kg is in a circular orbit of radius 1.06 × 10⁷ m with a period of 1.00 × 10⁴ s (supplied data).
**(a)** Calculate the angular speed of the satellite. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate its orbital speed. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate its centripetal acceleration. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the resultant force on the satellite. **[1]**
**(e)** State what provides this force and its direction. **[1]**

**10.** A mass of 0.18 kg moves in a horizontal circle of radius 0.65 m on a smooth table, held by a string that breaks if the tension exceeds 14 N.
**(a)** Calculate the greatest speed at which the mass can move without breaking the string. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the corresponding angular speed. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the corresponding frequency. **[1]**
**(d)** The string is replaced by one of length 1.3 m and the mass moves at the speed found in (a). Calculate the tension needed and explain the change. **[2]**

**11.** A fairground ride carries a rider of mass 60 kg in a horizontal circle of radius 2.8 m. One revolution takes 3.4 s.
**(a)** Calculate the angular speed. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the speed of the rider. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the resultant force on the rider. **[2]**
**(d)** Express the rider's acceleration as a multiple of g. **[1]**
**(e)** The ride speeds up until one revolution takes 2.4 s. Calculate the new resultant force and the factor by which it has increased. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** (a) 150 × π/180 = **5π/6 rad** [1]
(b) 2.4 × 180/π = **138°** (137.5°) [1]
**[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the conversion factor, π/180 or 180/π, so the working is visible.

**2.** (a) T = 1/f = 1/3.5 = **0.29 s** [1]
(b) ω = 2πf = 2π × 3.5 [1] = **22 rad s⁻¹** (22.0) [1]
**[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Give the unit rad s⁻¹ for angular speed. Hz or "per second" hides the radian.

**3.** (a) f = 1200/60 = **20 Hz** [1]
(b) ω = 2πf = 2π × 20 = **126 rad s⁻¹** [1]
(c) v = ωr = 125.7 × 0.22 = **27.6 m s⁻¹** [1]
(d) a = ω²r = 125.7² × 0.22 = **3.5 × 10³ m s⁻²** (3474) [1]
**[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Convert rpm to revolutions per second before multiplying by 2π, and carry the unrounded ω forward.

**4.** The ball's velocity changes because its direction is always changing [1]. A change of velocity is an acceleration, even at constant speed [1]. The acceleration is directed towards the centre of the circle, at right angles to the velocity [1]. By Newton's second law this needs a resultant force towards the centre, which here is the tension in the string [1].
**[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "It accelerates because it goes round a circle" restates the question. Say that velocity is a vector and its direction changes.

**5.** (a) ω = 2πf = 2π × 2.2 [1] = **14 rad s⁻¹** (13.8) [1]
(b) T = mω²r = 0.25 × 13.82² × 0.90 [1] = **43 N** (43.0) [1]
(c) The **tension** in the string. [1]
**[5]**
*Examiner insight:* In part (c) name a real force. "Centripetal force" does not say what pulls the bob inwards.

**6.** (a) f = 60/60 = 1.0 Hz [1]; ω = 2π × 1.0 = **6.3 rad s⁻¹** (6.28) [1]
(b) m = 12 × 10⁻³ kg and F = mω²r [1]; F = 0.012 × 6.283² × 0.10 = **0.047 N** [1]; this is below the 0.060 N maximum, so the token **stays in place** [1]
(c) F = 0.012 × 6.283² × 0.14 = 0.0663 N [1]; this is greater than 0.060 N, so the token **slips** [1]
**[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Convert grams to kilograms. End with a comparison and a conclusion in words; a force without a statement about staying or slipping leaves the question unanswered.

**7.** (a) F = mv²/r = 900 × 11²/35 [1] = **3.1 × 10³ N** (3111 N) [1]
(b) v = √(Fr/m) = √(4800 × 35/900) [1] = **13.7 m s⁻¹** [1]
(c) v = √(4800 × 60/900) = **17.9 m s⁻¹** [1]
(d) For a given speed, the force needed F = mv²/r is smaller when r is larger, so the friction limit is reached at a higher speed. [1]
**[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Rearrange for v first and take the square root. In part (d), refer to the equation.

**8.** (a) The hanging mass is in equilibrium, so the tension equals Mg [1]. The tension is the only horizontal force on the bung and points to the centre, so it is the centripetal force [1].
(b) For M = 0.20 kg, T = 8.97/10 = 0.897 s, so ω = 2π/0.897 = 7.00 rad s⁻¹ [1]. Then mω²r = 0.080 × 7.005² × 0.50 = 1.96 N, which equals Mg = 0.20 × 9.81 = 1.96 N [1].
(c) ΔF = (0.30 − 0.10) × 9.81 = 1.962 N [1]; gradient = 1.962/(1.861 − 0.621) = **1.6 N s²** (1.58) [1]
(d) F = 4π²mr/T², so gradient = 4π²mr, giving m = gradient/(4π²r) [1] = 1.582/(4π² × 0.50) = **0.080 kg** (0.0801) [1]
**[8]**
*Examiner insight:* In a "consistent with" question, state both values and say they agree.

**9.** (a) ω = 2π/T = 2π/(1.00 × 10⁴) = **6.28 × 10⁻⁴ rad s⁻¹** [1]
(b) v = ωr = 6.283 × 10⁻⁴ × 1.06 × 10⁷ = **6.66 × 10³ m s⁻¹** [1]
(c) a = ω²r [1] = (6.283 × 10⁻⁴)² × 1.06 × 10⁷ = **4.18 m s⁻²** [1]
(d) F = ma = 1200 × 4.185 = **5.02 × 10³ N** [1]
(e) Gravitational attraction of the planet, directed towards the centre of the planet (the centre of the orbit). [1]
**[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep powers of ten visible in the working and match the data's three significant figures.

**10.** (a) v = √(Fr/m) = √(14 × 0.65/0.18) [1] = **7.1 m s⁻¹** (7.11) [1]
(b) ω = v/r = 7.110/0.65 = **11 rad s⁻¹** (10.9) [1]
(c) f = ω/2π = 10.94/2π = **1.7 Hz** (1.74) [1]
(d) At fixed speed, F ∝ 1/r, so doubling the radius halves the tension [1]; T = 14/2 = **7.0 N** [1]
**[6]**
*Examiner insight:* In part (d), give the reason for the change as well as the number.

**11.** (a) ω = 2π/T = 2π/3.4 [1] = **1.85 rad s⁻¹** [1]
(b) v = ωr = 1.848 × 2.8 = **5.2 m s⁻¹** (5.17) [1]
(c) F = mω²r = 60 × 1.848² × 2.8 [1] = **5.7 × 10² N** (574 N) [1]
(d) a = ω²r = 9.56 m s⁻²; a/g = 9.56/9.81 = **0.97** (about 1g) [1]
(e) ω = 2π/2.4 = 2.618 rad s⁻¹ [1]; F = 60 × 2.618² × 2.8 = **1.15 × 10³ N** [1]; the factor is 1151/574 = **2.0**, equal to (3.4/2.4)² because F ∝ 1/T² [1]
**[9]**
*Examiner insight:* F proportional to 1/T² gives a quick check on the factor in the last part.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing m s⁻¹ for angular speed. It is rad s⁻¹.
- Using revolutions per minute directly in the formulas, or the diameter as the radius.
- Squaring v/r instead of squaring v and then dividing by r.
- Writing "centrifugal force" in an explanation, or adding a centripetal arrow to a free-body diagram.
- Saying a circling object has no acceleration because its speed is constant, or not converting grams to kilograms.
- Giving an answer to a "does it stay or slip?" question without a comparison in words.
