The French Revolution, c1780–99 is one of the eight Depth Studies on Paper 1 of Pearson Edexcel
International GCSE History (4HI1). It follows France from the long-term problems of Louis XVI's
monarchy, through the crisis of 1787–89 and the radical republic of the Terror, to Napoleon
Bonaparte becoming First Consul in 1799. In the exam you answer one three-part question on it:
an interpretation (extract) question, an "explain two effects" question and a 16-mark judgement
essay.

## Syllabus coverage

The 4HI1 specification (Issue 5, February 2026) divides this depth study into five key topics:

1. **The origins of the Revolution, c1780–87** — the Enlightenment, the American War of
   Independence, the Three Estates, Louis XVI, Marie Antoinette, financial problems, Necker and
   poor harvests.
2. **Short-term causes, 1787–89** — the Assembly of Notables (1787), Calonne and Brienne, the
   Estates General, the Tennis Court Oath, the storming of the Bastille, the Great Fear and the
   National Assembly.
3. **Developments 1789–92** — the Constituent Assembly, the flight to Varennes, the
   sans-culottes, Girondins and Jacobins, the Legislative Assembly, war with Austria and Prussia,
   the rising of August 1792 and the National Convention.
4. **Convention and Terror, 1792–94** — the Edict of Fraternity, the trial and execution of
   Louis XVI, the Committee of Public Safety, the fall of the Girondins, Robespierre and the
   Terror.
5. **Directory and First Consul, 1794–99** — Robespierre's fall, the Thermidorian Reaction and
   White Terror, the Directory, the coup of 18 Fructidor, the Directory's fall, Napoleon as First
   Consul, and the achievements of the Revolution.

## What were the long-term causes of the French Revolution?

**Society of orders.** France was divided into three Estates. The First Estate (the clergy) and
the Second Estate (the nobility) held privileges, including exemption from the main direct tax,
the *taille*. The Third Estate — everyone else, from wealthy lawyers to landless peasants — was
the vast majority of the population and carried most of the tax burden, along with the Church
tithe and labour duties such as the *corvée*. Educated, prosperous members of the Third Estate
(the bourgeoisie) increasingly resented being shut out of power.

**The Enlightenment.** Writers such as Montesquieu (*The Spirit of the Laws*, 1748, which argued
for a separation of powers), Voltaire (a critic of the Church and of arbitrary power) and Rousseau
(*The Social Contract*, 1762, which grounded authority in the "general will") encouraged people to
question privilege and absolute monarchy.

**The American War of Independence.** France joined the war against Britain in 1778. Victory
brought prestige but enormous debts, and French officers such as Lafayette returned having fought
for a cause built on natural rights and no taxation without representation.

**Louis XVI and Marie Antoinette.** Louis, king from 1774, was well-meaning but indecisive.
Marie Antoinette, an Austrian princess, was unpopular for her spending and her nationality; the
Diamond Necklace Affair (1785) damaged her reputation even though she was not guilty.

**Finance.** The Crown borrowed heavily, and by the late 1780s interest payments swallowed a large
share of revenue. Jacques Necker, in charge of finances from 1777 to 1781, paid for the American
war with loans rather than taxes, and his *Compte rendu* (1781) presented the royal accounts as
healthier than they were. **Poor harvests**, especially the failure of 1788, pushed bread prices to
a peak in the summer of 1789.

## How did the crisis of 1787–89 turn into revolution?

Controller-General Calonne proposed a land tax payable by all landowners. Louis called an
**Assembly of Notables** (February 1787) hoping it would approve the plan; the privileged notables
refused, and Calonne was dismissed. His successor, Loménie de **Brienne**, met the same
resistance from the Parlement of Paris, which declared that only an Estates General could approve
new taxes. In August 1788, with the treasury effectively bankrupt, Brienne announced that the
Estates General would meet, and Necker was recalled.

The **Estates General** opened at Versailles on 5 May 1789. The Third Estate's representation had
been doubled, but the key dispute was whether votes would be counted by order (giving the
privileged two Estates a majority) or by head. On 17 June the Third Estate declared itself the
**National Assembly**; locked out of its hall, its deputies swore the **Tennis Court Oath** on 20
June not to separate until France had a constitution. Louis gave way and ordered the other orders
to join it on 27 June, but troops gathered around Paris. Necker's dismissal on 11 July triggered
the **storming of the Bastille** on 14 July 1789. In the countryside the **Great Fear** (late July
and early August) saw peasants attack châteaux and burn records of feudal dues.

## What changed between 1789 and 1792?

The National (Constituent) Assembly abolished feudal privileges in the August Decrees (4 August
1789) and issued the **Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen** (26 August). In the
October Days (5–6 October 1789) a crowd forced the royal family to move from Versailles to Paris.
The Assembly reorganised France into 83 departments, took over Church lands to back a paper
currency (*assignats*), and passed the **Civil Constitution of the Clergy** (1790), which split the
Church and turned many Catholics against the Revolution. The Constitution of 1791 created a
constitutional monarchy, with the king keeping a suspensive veto and the vote limited to
"active" (tax-paying) citizens.

The **flight to Varennes** (20–21 June 1791), when the royal family tried to escape and was
caught, destroyed trust in the king and strengthened republican feeling. The new **Legislative
Assembly** (from October 1791) was dominated by the debate between the **Girondins**, who pushed
for war, and the **Jacobins**, while the **sans-culottes** — Parisian artisans and workers — became
a powerful force on the streets. France declared war on Austria on 20 April 1792, and Prussia
joined against France. Early defeats, the threats of the Brunswick Manifesto and suspicion of the
king led to the **rising of 10 August 1792**, when the Tuileries palace was stormed and the king
was suspended. A **National Convention**, elected by universal male suffrage, met in September and
abolished the monarchy; France became a republic.

## Why did the Revolution turn to Terror in 1792–94?

The Convention issued the **Edict of Fraternity** (November 1792), offering help to any people
seeking to recover their liberty — a threat to every monarchy in Europe. Louis XVI was tried by
the Convention and **executed on 21 January 1793**. War widened to include Britain and the Dutch
Republic, and a royalist and Catholic rising broke out in the Vendée in March 1793.

The **Committee of Public Safety** (created April 1793) became the effective government. In June
1793 the sans-culottes forced the Convention to expel and arrest leading Girondins, many of whom
were later executed. **Robespierre** joined the Committee in July 1793 and became its best-known
voice, justifying terror as the necessary partner of "virtue" in a republic under threat. The Law
of Suspects (September 1793), price controls (the Maximum) and the *levée en masse* (mass
conscription) followed. Around 17,000 people were officially sentenced to death, and many more
died in prison or in the suppression of the Vendée and of federalist revolts.

## How did the Revolution end in the rise of Napoleon?

Once French armies began winning — notably at Fleurus in June 1794 — the case for terror weakened,
and Robespierre's colleagues feared they would be next. On **9 Thermidor (27 July 1794)** he was
arrested, and he was guillotined the next day. The **Thermidorian Reaction** closed the Jacobin
Club, ended the Maximum and saw a **White Terror** against former Jacobins, especially in the
south.

The Constitution of 1795 created the **Directory**: five Directors and two councils. It achieved
some stability and military success, but it was unpopular, short of money and threatened from
both sides. When royalists did well in the 1797 elections, the Directors used the army in the
**coup of 18 Fructidor** (September 1797) to annul results. Repeated interference with elections,
war weariness and corruption left the regime with little support. In the **coup of Brumaire**
(November 1799), Sieyès and Napoleon Bonaparte overthrew it, and the new constitution named
**Napoleon First Consul**.

The **achievements of the Revolution** you should be able to weigh include the end of feudalism
and legal privilege, the Declaration of Rights, a uniform system of departments and (from 1795)
the metric system, and the principle of national sovereignty — set against the violence of the
Terror and the eventual return of one-man rule.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which key topic is most often the focus of the 16-mark essay?**
Any of the five can be set. The
safest preparation is to know the causes of each turning point (1789, August 1792, the Terror,
Thermidor, Brumaire) well enough to weigh one cause against others.

**Do I need to know Enlightenment writers in detail?**
You need to explain how their ideas
challenged privilege and absolute monarchy, with one or two named examples. Long summaries of
their books will not earn extra marks.

**Is the Great Fear the same as the Terror?**
No. The Great Fear was a rural panic and peasant
rising in the summer of 1789; the Terror was government policy in 1793–94.

**What does "explain two effects" need?**
Two developed effects, each supported with specific
detail and linked clearly back to the event in the question.

## How to revise this depth study

Build a single timeline from 1774 to 1799 and mark the five key topics on it. For every turning
point, list at least three causes and two consequences. Learn the named individuals (Necker,
Calonne, Brienne, Robespierre, Danton, Sieyès, Napoleon) with one precise fact each. Then practise
the three question types using the [practice questions](/resources/edexcel-igcse-world-history-french-revolution-practice/).
