## Questions

**1.** Distinguish between fiscal policy and monetary policy. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why a rise in income tax rates combined with a cut in government spending is a deflationary policy. **[3]**

**3.** Country B has a marginal propensity to save of 0.2, to pay tax of 0.1 and to import of 0.2. The government raises its spending by 12bn.

**(a)** Calculate the multiplier and the total rise in national income. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the net cost to the budget after extra tax revenue. **[2]**

**4.** Country C's central bank cuts its policy rate from 6.0% to 4.5%. Inflation is 2.5% and does not change.

**(a)** Calculate the real interest rate before and after the cut. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain one reason why the cut may not raise aggregate demand by much. **[2]**

**5.** A government bond pays 3 a year. Its price is 60 before the central bank starts buying bonds and 75 afterwards.

**(a)** Calculate the yield before and after. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how this change could affect investment. **[2]**

**6.** A house costs 300,000. The authorities cut the maximum loan-to-value ratio from 95% to 85%.

**(a)** Calculate the deposit a buyer needs before and after. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain the likely effect on aggregate demand. **[2]**

**7.** A bank holds 18bn of liquid assets against deposits of 150bn. The required liquidity ratio rises from 12% to 15%. Calculate the shortfall and state one consequence for the bank's lending. **[3]**

**8.** Explain two roles of a central bank, one of which must be lender of last resort. **[4]**

**9.** The price index in Country D was 108.0 last year and 111.2 this year. The inflation target is 2%.

**(a)** Calculate the inflation rate to one decimal place. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain what the central bank is likely to do to its policy rate and why. **[2]**

**10.** Country E is in recession, its policy rate is close to zero and its government debt is already high. Evaluate whether fiscal policy or monetary policy is more likely to increase output. **[8]**

**11.** In Country F, mps is 0.1, mpt is 0.1 and mpm is 0.05. The government considers either raising spending by 6bn or cutting lump-sum taxes by 6bn.

**(a)** Calculate the multiplier and the rise in income from each option. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain why the results differ and suggest one reason the tax cut may still be chosen. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Fiscal policy is changes in government spending and taxation [1]. Monetary policy is changes in interest rates, the money supply or credit conditions, normally by the central bank [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers that name only the instruments, without saying who uses them or what they change, usually say too little for a distinction; give a difference.

**2.** Higher tax rates reduce disposable income, so consumption falls [1]. Lower government spending reduces an injection into the circular flow [1]. Both reduce aggregate demand, so it shifts left and the price level or output falls; this is deflationary policy, not deflation [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A chain from instrument to AD to the result is stronger than a statement that "demand falls"; write each link.

**3. (a)** Multiplier = 1 / (0.2 + 0.1 + 0.2) = 1 / 0.5 = 2 [1]. Income rises by 12 × 2 = **24bn** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Extra tax = 0.1 × 24 = 2.4bn [1]. Net cost = 12 − 2.4 = **9.6bn** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the withdrawal rates added together before dividing; a bare multiplier of 2 gives the reader nothing to check.

**4. (a)** Before: 6.0 − 2.5 = **3.5%** [1]. After: 4.5 − 2.5 = **2.0%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Households or firms may expect weak demand [1], so investment does not rise even with cheaper borrowing [1]. A different reason would also do: high existing debt means households use any saving to repay it rather than spend. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A reason is strongest when it explains why the usual link breaks; "it might not work" alone is not an explanation.

**5. (a)** Before: 3 / 60 × 100 = **5.0%** [1]. After: 3 / 75 × 100 = **4.0%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Lower yields signal cheaper long-term borrowing [1]. Firms find more projects worthwhile at a lower interest cost, so investment may rise, raising AD [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Say that price and yield move in opposite directions; this link is the core of how quantitative easing works.

**6. (a)** Before: 5% of 300,000 = **15,000** [1]. After: 15% of 300,000 = **45,000** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Fewer buyers can raise the larger deposit, so demand for housing and for mortgage credit falls [1]. Less house-related spending lowers consumption and AD [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Give the effect on spending, not just on house buyers; the question asks about aggregate demand.

**7.** Required liquid assets = 0.15 × 150 = 22.5bn [1]. Shortfall = 22.5 − 18 = **4.5bn** [1]. The bank must hold more liquid assets, so it will have less to lend [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A ratio question needs the required amount first; compare it with the actual holding second.

**8.** Lender of last resort: it provides loans to a bank that has run out of other sources of funds [1]. This protects confidence and stops a run on one bank spreading to others [1]. Second role, banker to the government: it holds the government's accounts and handles payments [1], and helps the government issue and manage its debt [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each role needs a function and a purpose; naming roles in a list earns little by itself.

**9. (a)** (111.2 − 108.0) / 108.0 × 100 = 3.2 / 108.0 × 100 [1] = **3.0%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Inflation is above the 2% target, so the bank is likely to raise the policy rate [1]. Dearer borrowing reduces consumption and investment, reducing demand-pull pressure [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare the figure you calculated with the target before choosing the direction of the rate.

**10.** Monetary policy: the policy rate is near zero, so a further cut is limited [1]. QE could lower long-term yields, but its effect on lending is uncertain when firms expect weak demand [1]. Lags also apply [1]. Fiscal policy: government spending is a direct injection, and the multiplier raises income more than the first-round spend [1]. In a recession there is spare capacity, so extra demand raises output more than prices [1]. Against this, the high debt limits room to borrow, and markets may demand higher interest [1]. Spending could also be slow to start, so the lag may be long [1]. Judgement: fiscal policy is more likely to raise output in this case, if the spending is targeted and its financing is credible, because monetary policy has little room [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation tends to gain credit when it uses the context given (near-zero rate, high debt) and ends in a reasoned judgement, rather than listing general pros and cons.

**11. (a)** Multiplier = 1 / (0.1 + 0.1 + 0.05) = 1 / 0.25 = 4 [1]. Spending: 6 × 4 = **24bn** [1]. Tax cut: households spend 1 − 0.1 − 0.05 = 0.85 at home, so 6 × 0.85 = 5.1bn first round [1]; 5.1 × 4 = **20.4bn** [1]. **[4]**
**(b)** Part of the tax cut leaks into saving and imports in the first round, so the injection is smaller [1]. A tax cut may be chosen because it can be reversed more easily, or because the government wants to raise incentives and let households choose how to spend [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the first-round share for the tax cut before applying the multiplier; the difference of 3.6bn is the point of the comparison.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing an instrument without linking it to aggregate demand.
- Confusing deflationary policy with deflation.
- Forgetting to add all withdrawals before taking the reciprocal.
- Treating a tax cut as if all of it were spent in the first round.
- Using nominal rates when the question gives inflation.
- Mixing up bond price and yield.
- Listing central bank roles without a purpose for each.
- Evaluating with general points that ignore the context supplied.
- Ending an evaluation with no judgement.
