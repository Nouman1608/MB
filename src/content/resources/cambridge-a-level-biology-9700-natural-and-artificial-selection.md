## The natural selection argument

Write it as a chain in which each step causes the next:

1. Populations have the capacity to produce **many more offspring** than the environment can support.
2. Offspring **compete** for limited resources, such as food, mates or nesting sites, and face predators and disease: the **struggle for existence**.
3. Individuals **vary**, partly because they carry different **alleles**.
4. Individuals with alleles that give an advantageous phenotype in that environment are **more likely to survive and reproduce**.
5. They **pass on** these alleles to their offspring.
6. Over many generations, the **frequency of the advantageous alleles increases** in the population.

The environmental factor that makes some phenotypes more successful is called a **selective agent** (or selection pressure), for example a predator, a disease, a food source or an antibiotic.

## Three forces of selection

| Force | Which phenotypes are favoured | Effect on the distribution | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stabilising | The intermediate | Mean unchanged; range narrows | Human birth mass: very small and very large babies have historically had lower survival |
| Directional | One extreme | Mean shifts towards that extreme | Bacteria in the presence of an antibiotic: resistant cells survive |
| Disruptive | Both extremes | Distribution splits towards two peaks | A population living on two contrasting backgrounds, where intermediates are most visible to predators |

**Stabilising selection** is the most common type in an unchanging environment: it keeps a population well adapted. **Directional selection** usually follows a change in the environment. **Disruptive selection** is less common; it can be a step towards the formation of two separate forms.

## Selection or chance?

Allele frequencies can change for reasons other than selection.

- **Genetic drift**: random changes in allele frequency from one generation to the next, because which individuals happen to reproduce is partly a matter of chance. Drift has a large effect in **small** populations and may cause an allele to be lost or to become the only one (fixed), whether or not it is advantageous.
- **Founder effect**: a few individuals start a new, isolated population. Their alleles are only a sample of the original gene pool, so allele frequencies in the new population may differ greatly from the original, and some alleles may be absent.
- **Bottleneck effect**: a population is drastically reduced, for example by disease, a natural disaster or hunting. The survivors carry only some of the original alleles, so **genetic diversity falls**, and it stays low even when numbers recover. Northern elephant seals, hunted to very low numbers in the nineteenth century, are a frequently cited example.

**How to tell them apart in a question.** Selection changes frequencies in a **consistent direction**, linked to an environmental factor, and is seen in large populations. Drift is **random in direction**, is strongest in small populations, and can affect alleles with no effect on survival.

## Antibiotic resistance as natural selection

1. A random **mutation** gives one bacterium an allele for resistance (for example, for an enzyme that breaks down the antibiotic).
2. When the antibiotic is used, it **kills non-resistant bacteria**; the antibiotic is the selective agent.
3. Resistant bacteria **survive** and **reproduce by binary fission**, passing on the resistance allele.
4. With less competition, the resistant population grows; the **frequency of the resistance allele increases**.
5. Resistance alleles on **plasmids** can also pass between bacteria, spreading resistance faster.

This is directional selection. Overuse of antibiotics, and not completing a course of treatment, increase the chances that resistant bacteria are selected.

## The Hardy–Weinberg principle

```
p + q = 1
p² + 2pq + q² = 1
```

p = frequency of the dominant allele; q = frequency of the recessive allele; p² = frequency of homozygous dominant; 2pq = heterozygous; q² = homozygous recessive. The equations will be provided; you must know what each term means and when the principle applies.

**Conditions** for the principle to hold:

- A **large** population (so drift is negligible).
- **Random mating**.
- **No mutation**.
- **No migration** into or out of the population.
- **No selection**: all genotypes are equally likely to survive and reproduce.

### Worked example 1: a rare recessive condition

A recessive condition affects 1 in 2500 people. Estimate the proportion of carriers.

```
q² = 1 ÷ 2500 = 0.0004
q  = √0.0004 = 0.02
p  = 1 − 0.02 = 0.98
2pq = 2 × 0.98 × 0.02 = 0.0392
```

About 0.039, or **1 in 26** people, are carriers. Notice that carriers are far more common than affected people: most copies of a rare recessive allele are hidden in heterozygotes.

### Worked example 2: counting alleles directly

For **codominant** alleles every genotype can be identified, so allele frequencies can be counted rather than calculated from q². A sample of 1000 people has 300 MM, 540 MN and 160 NN.

```
alleles in total = 2 × 1000 = 2000
M alleles = (2 × 300) + 540 = 1140      p = 1140 ÷ 2000 = 0.57
N alleles = (2 × 160) + 540 = 860       q = 860 ÷ 2000 = 0.43
```

Expected genotypes if the population were in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium: p² × 1000 = 325 MM, 2pq × 1000 = 490 MN, q² × 1000 = 185 NN. The sample has more heterozygotes than expected. This could indicate that one of the conditions is not met, for example selection favouring heterozygotes, although a statistical test would be needed to decide whether the difference is significant.

## Selective breeding (artificial selection)

**Principles:**

1. Humans **choose** individuals with the desired characteristic, for example high yield.
2. These individuals are **bred** together.
3. The offspring with the best expression of the characteristic are selected and bred.
4. This is **repeated over many generations**, increasing the frequency of the desired alleles.

Unlike natural selection, **humans** are the selective agent, and the characteristic chosen need not help the organism survive in the wild. Selective breeding usually reduces genetic diversity.

### Disease resistance in wheat and rice

A high-yielding variety is crossed with a variety (or wild relative) that carries alleles for **resistance** to a disease. Offspring are tested by exposing them to the pathogen, and those that are both resistant and high-yielding are selected. They are crossed back with the high-yielding variety and selected again over several generations, until the new variety has the yield of one parent and the resistance of the other.

### Inbreeding and hybridisation in maize

- **Inbreeding**: self-pollinating plants for several generations produces **inbred lines** that are **homozygous** at most loci, and so very **uniform**. But inbred lines lose vigour: they are small and give low yields, because harmful recessive alleles become homozygous (inbreeding depression).
- **Hybridisation**: crossing two **different** inbred lines produces F1 hybrids that are all **genetically identical** (so uniform, which suits mechanical harvesting) and **heterozygous** at many loci (so vigorous, with high yields: hybrid vigour).
- Seed from the hybrids is **not** kept for the next season, because the F2 generation is variable. Farmers buy new F1 seed each year.

### Milk yield in dairy cattle

Only cows produce milk, so a bull's genetic value for milk yield cannot be measured directly. Bulls are assessed by **progeny testing**: the milk yields of their daughters are recorded. The best bulls are then used to fertilise high-yielding cows, often by **artificial insemination**, so one bull can father many calves. Over many generations, milk yield has risen greatly.

## Common mistakes

- Saying organisms "adapt" or "choose" to change. Individuals do not change: **allele frequencies** in the population change.
- Writing "survival of the fittest" without explaining survival **and reproduction** and **passing on alleles**.
- Saying the antibiotic **causes** the resistance mutation. The mutation is random and already present; the antibiotic **selects** it.
- Using p² for carriers. Carriers are **2pq**.
- Forgetting "no selection" or "large population" in the Hardy–Weinberg conditions.
- Saying hybrid maize is homozygous. Inbred lines are homozygous; **F1 hybrids are heterozygous**.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** natural selection (17.2.1): give all six links, naming alleles and allele frequency.
- **Explain** forces of selection (17.2.2): say which phenotype is favoured and what happens to the mean and range.
- **Use** Hardy–Weinberg (17.2.5): always start from q² (the homozygous recessive frequency) unless genotypes can be counted.
- **Outline** breeding examples (17.2.7): main points only, but name the key terms: resistance, inbred lines, hybrid vigour, progeny testing.
