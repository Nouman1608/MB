## Section A

**1.** State the main characteristics of Stage 2 of the demographic transition model. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why the death rate falls before the birth rate during demographic transition. **[3]**

**3.** Explain why the crude death rate may rise in Stage 5 even though health care is good. **[2]**

**4.** Define the term "demographic dividend". **[2]**

**5.** Explain how a fall in infant mortality raises life expectancy at birth. **[2]**

**6.** Outline what is meant by the epidemiological transition. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** The table shows birth and death rates (per 1000) for fictional country V.

| Year | 1960 | 1980 | 2000 | 2020 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Birth rate | 48 | 46 | 38 | 30 |
| Death rate | 22 | 14 | 10 | 8 |

**(a)** Calculate the natural increase rate in 2020 as a percentage. Show your working. **[2]**

**(b)** Identify the stage of the demographic transition model reached by country V in 1980 and in 2020. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest reasons for the change in birth rate between 1980 and 2020. **[3]**

**8.** The table shows infant mortality and life expectancy at birth for fictional country W.

| Year | 1970 | 1990 | 2010 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Infant mortality (per 1000 live births) | 120 | 70 | 30 | 12 |
| Life expectancy at birth (years) | 48 | 58 | 67 | 73 |

**(a)** Describe the changes shown. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain how these changes are linked to development. **[3]**

**9.** Compare the issues faced by countries with youthful populations and countries with ageing populations. **[6]**

**10.** Explain why the demographic transition of many countries has differed from the model. **[5]**

**11.** Explain how an ageing population can bring opportunities as well as problems. **[5]**

**12.** "The demographic transition model is of little use for understanding population change today." Discuss this view. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** The death rate falls rapidly while the birth rate remains high [1], so natural increase is high and population grows rapidly [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Improvements in food supply, clean water, sanitation, vaccination and medicine reduce deaths quickly, especially among children [1]. Birth rates depend on attitudes, family decisions and economic circumstances, which change more slowly [1]. Parents keep having large families until they are confident their children will survive and until urbanisation, education and the cost of children change their choices [1]. **[3]**

**3.** The population is ageing, so a larger proportion of people are in older age groups [1], where the risk of death is higher, so total deaths per 1000 people rise even though each age group is healthier [1]. **[2]**

**4.** The potential boost to economic growth [1] when falling fertility leaves a large share of the population in working ages and fewer dependents, provided there are jobs for them [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Life expectancy at birth is an average of all ages at death, so many deaths in the first year of life pull the average down sharply [1]. When fewer infants die, the average age at death rises quickly, even if adult survival changes little [1]. **[2]**

**6.** The change in the main causes of death as countries develop [1], from infectious diseases, malnutrition and childbirth complications to non-communicable diseases such as heart disease and cancer [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** 30 - 8 = 22 per 1000 [1]; 22 / 10 = 2.2% [1].

**(b)** 1980: Stage 2 (birth rate still high at 46, death rate fallen to 14) [1]. 2020: Stage 3 (birth rate falling, now 30) [1].

**(c)** Lower infant mortality means parents need fewer births to ensure children survive [1]. More girls attending school, and greater access to contraception, delay marriage and reduce family size [1]. Urbanisation and economic change make children more costly and less useful as labour [1]. **[7]**

**8. (a)** Infant mortality fell by 90%, from 120 to 12 per 1000 [1]. Life expectancy rose by 25 years, from 48 to 73 [1]. Both changed fastest between 1970 and 1990, when infant mortality fell by 50 and life expectancy rose by 10 years, and more slowly afterwards [1].

**(b)** Rising incomes and government spending provide clean water, sanitation, vaccination and maternal care, reducing infant deaths [1]. Better nutrition and health services raise adult survival [1]. Falling infant mortality is the main reason for the rise in life expectancy at birth, and both are used as measures of development [1]. **[6]**

**9.** Both face high dependency, but youthful countries have high youth dependency and ageing countries high old-age dependency [1]. Youthful countries need more schools and child and maternal health care [1], whereas ageing countries need more health care for chronic illness, residential care and pensions [1]. Youthful countries risk youth unemployment if jobs are not created [1], while ageing countries face labour shortages and a shrinking tax base [1]. Youthful countries may gain a demographic dividend, while ageing countries may rely on immigration or a higher retirement age [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Death rates in many LICs fell in the twentieth century because of imported medicine and public health programmes, not industrialisation [1]. Transitions have been much faster, so stages are compressed into a few decades [1]. Government policies, such as China's birth limits, have lowered fertility directly [1]. Religious and cultural norms have kept fertility high in some countries for longer than predicted [1]. Diseases such as HIV/AIDS raised death rates in parts of southern Africa, temporarily reversing the transition [1]. **[5]**

**11.** Opportunities: many older people remain healthy and keep working or volunteering [1]. They provide childcare for grandchildren, allowing parents to work [1]. They create demand for new goods and services, such as health care and leisure, creating jobs [1]. Problems: health care and pension costs rise [1], while the working-age population that pays taxes shrinks [1]. **[5]**

**12.** For the view: it is based on Western European and North American history and may not apply elsewhere [1]. It does not include migration, which drives change in many countries today [1]. It cannot predict the timing or speed of transitions, which have been much faster recently [1]. It does not account for policies, culture or disease, which have changed rates independently of development [1]. Stage 5 was not part of the original model, so it did not anticipate natural decrease [1].

Against the view: the basic sequence of falling death rates followed by falling birth rates has occurred in most countries [1]. It allows countries to be compared and helps planners anticipate needs such as schools or pensions [1]. It links population change to development, for example through falling infant mortality [1].

Judgement: the model remains useful as a broad description and as a framework for comparison [1], but it should be used critically, alongside knowledge of each country's policies, culture and migration, rather than as a prediction [1]. **[10]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Placing a country in a stage without quoting the rates that justify it.
- Forgetting to convert per 1000 into a percentage.
- Treating a rising death rate in an ageing country as a sign of poor health care.
- Describing limitations of the model with no examples of countries.
- Writing only about problems of ageing when the question asks about opportunities as well.
