This guide covers subtopic 8.3, Urban air pollution, in Topic 8 of IB Diploma Programme Environmental Systems and Societies, first assessment 2026. The [Topic 8 study guide](/resources/ib-dp-ess-human-populations-urban-systems/) lists primary and secondary pollutants, sets out the steps of photochemical smog, explains thermal inversions and applies the pollution management model. This page builds the data-handling and evaluation skills on top: what each pollutant does to health, how to read a day of monitoring data, why weather and relief make some cities worse, what the 1952 London smog teaches, how urban emissions become acid deposition downwind, and how to judge control measures against health guidelines.

## Pollutants, sources and effects

| Pollutant | Main urban sources | Effects |
|---|---|---|
| Particulate matter (PM10 and PM2.5) | Diesel exhaust, brake and tyre wear, burning wood and coal, construction dust | PM2.5 (2.5 micrometres or less) reaches deep into the lungs and bloodstream; linked to heart and lung disease |
| Nitrogen oxides (NO and NO₂) | High-temperature combustion in vehicle engines and power stations | NO₂ inflames airways and worsens asthma; precursor of ozone and nitric acid |
| Sulfur dioxide (SO₂) | Burning coal and high-sulfur oil | Irritates lungs; forms sulfuric acid |
| Carbon monoxide (CO) | Incomplete combustion, especially in traffic | Reduces the blood's ability to carry oxygen |
| Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) | Fuel evaporation, solvents, paints | Some are toxic; react with NOx to form ozone |
| Tropospheric ozone (secondary) | Formed from NOx and VOCs in sunlight | Irritates eyes and lungs; reduces crop photosynthesis; damages rubber |

**Health guidelines** give a benchmark for data questions. The World Health Organization's 2021 air quality guidelines set annual mean levels of 5 µg/m³ for PM2.5 and 10 µg/m³ for NO₂. Many cities exceed these by several times.

### Indoor air pollution

Air pollution is not only an outdoor problem. Where households cook or heat with wood, dung, charcoal, coal or kerosene on open fires or simple stoves in poorly ventilated rooms, indoor concentrations of particulates and CO can be very high. Women and young children, who spend most time near the fire, are most exposed. Cleaner fuels, improved stoves, chimneys and ventilation are the responses.

## Reading a day of monitoring data

Table 1 shows fictional hourly readings at a roadside monitor on a sunny, still day.

| Time | 06:00 | 08:00 | 10:00 | 12:00 | 14:00 | 16:00 | 18:00 | 20:00 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| NO₂ (µg/m³) | 30 | 85 | 70 | 50 | 40 | 55 | 75 | 45 |
| Ozone (µg/m³) | 20 | 25 | 60 | 110 | 140 | 120 | 70 | 40 |

How to describe and explain it:

1. **NO₂ has two peaks**, 85 µg/m³ at 08:00 and 75 µg/m³ at 18:00, matching the morning and evening rush hours, when traffic emits NO that is oxidised to NO₂.
2. **Ozone peaks once**, at 140 µg/m³ at 14:00, six hours after the NO₂ peak. Sunlight is strongest around midday, splitting NO₂ and releasing oxygen atoms that form ozone.
3. **As ozone rises, NO₂ falls** (from 85 to 40 µg/m³ between 08:00 and 14:00), because NO₂ is being used up in ozone formation.
4. **Ozone falls in the evening**, to 40 µg/m³ by 20:00. Without sunlight no new ozone forms, and fresh NO from evening traffic reacts with ozone (NO + O₃ → NO₂ + O₂), which is also why NO₂ rises again.

That last reaction explains a surprising pattern: ozone is often *higher* in suburbs and the countryside downwind of a city than in the city centre, because there is less fresh NO there to remove it. Use it if a question shows higher ozone away from traffic.

## Why some cities suffer more

Emissions are only half the story; the other half is how well the air disperses pollutants.

- **Wind:** strong winds dilute and carry pollutants away; calm conditions let them build up.
- **Sunlight and temperature:** strong sunshine and heat speed up photochemical reactions, so smog is worst on hot, sunny days.
- **Thermal inversions:** warm air above cool air acts as a lid (see the Topic 8 guide).
- **Relief:** cities in basins or valleys surrounded by mountains, such as Los Angeles and Mexico City, trap air more often, because cool air drains into the basin and inversions form readily.
- **Season:** in cold climates, winter heating with coal or wood adds particulates and SO₂.

A strong explanation combines a source with a weather or relief factor. "Mexico City has many vehicles" is half an answer; "many vehicles emit NOx and VOCs, and the surrounding mountains and frequent inversions trap them while strong sunlight drives ozone formation" is a full one.

## Case study: the London smog of 1952

In December 1952 cold, still weather and a temperature inversion settled over London. Smoke and SO₂ from coal burned in homes, factories and power stations were trapped near the ground for several days, forming a dense smog. Thousands of people died in the following weeks, mostly from heart and lung conditions. The disaster led to the Clean Air Act 1956, which created smoke control areas and encouraged a switch from coal to cleaner fuels.

Use the case to show three things: an inversion turning ordinary emissions into a crisis; the difference between this smoky, sulfurous smog and photochemical smog formed in sunlight; and a legal response at the "alter human activity" and "control release" levels of the pollution management model.

## From urban emissions to acid deposition

SO₂ and NOx do not stay in the city. As they travel downwind they are oxidised and dissolve in water droplets to form sulfuric and nitric acids. These return to the ground as **wet deposition** (acid rain, snow, fog) or **dry deposition** (gases and particles settling on surfaces). Effects include acidified lakes and streams that lose fish and invertebrates, leaching of nutrients from soils and release of toxic aluminium, damage to conifer needles, and erosion of limestone and marble buildings. Because the damage can fall in another region or country, acid deposition is a transboundary issue: the polluter and the victim are different places.

## Evaluating controls with data

### Worked example: comparing with a guideline

A fictional city has an annual mean PM2.5 of 35 µg/m³.

```
Ratio to WHO guideline = 35 ÷ 5 = 7 times the guideline
After a 40% cut:        35 × 0.60 = 21 µg/m³ = 4.2 times the guideline
```

A 40% cut is a large improvement but still leaves the city well above the guideline, so the evaluation should say the measure helps but is not enough on its own.

If the city's daily limit was exceeded on 73 days in a year, that is 73 ÷ 365 × 100 = 20% of days, a useful second measure because short peaks drive hospital admissions.

### Matching controls to sources

| Control | Acts on | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| Catalytic converters | CO, NOx and unburned hydrocarbons from petrol cars | Converts them to CO₂, N₂ and water; ineffective until the engine warms up |
| Diesel particulate filters | PM from diesel vehicles | Diesel engines emit less CO₂ per km than petrol but more NOx and PM, a trade-off |
| Congestion charges and low-emission zones | Traffic volume and the dirtiest vehicles | Fast effect inside the zone; may displace traffic; cost falls hardest on those who cannot replace old vehicles |
| Switching from coal to gas or renewables | SO₂, PM and NOx from power and heating | Large cuts; needs investment |
| Electric buses and vehicles | Exhaust emissions at street level | Brake and tyre particles remain; benefit depends on the electricity mix |
| Pollution alerts | Exposure, not emissions | Protects vulnerable people; does not reduce pollution |

## Common mistakes

- Saying ozone peaks at rush hour. NO₂ does; ozone peaks in the afternoon.
- Expecting ozone to be highest at the busiest roadside; fresh NO removes it there.
- Describing an inversion without explaining why air cannot rise.
- Mixing up the 1952 smog (smoke and SO₂) with photochemical smog (NOx, VOCs and sunlight).
- Calling acid deposition a purely local problem.
- Evaluating a measure without comparing the result to a guideline or a baseline.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** data: give the trend, the peaks with times and values, and any anomaly.
- **Explain** data: link each feature to a source and a process.
- **Calculate:** percentage change from the starting value, ratios to guidelines and the share of days exceeding a limit.
- **Evaluate** a strategy: effect on which pollutant, speed, cost, who pays, side effects, then a judgement against a guideline.
