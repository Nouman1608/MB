This study guide covers the content point "The effect of errors on profit calculations" in section 3.2 Verification of accounting records of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Accounting (9215) specification (version 1.2). The specification says "the effect of errors includes the adjustment of profit or loss for an accounting period following the correction of errors". All amounts on this page are in dollars.

The [correcting errors and effect on profit guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-correcting-errors-and-the-effect-of-errors-on-profit/) shows the basic test: a correction changes profit only if it touches an income or expense account. This page goes further. It separates the effect on **gross profit** from the effect on **profit for the year**, deals with closing inventory errors, and works through a two-column statement of corrected profit and a loss that becomes a profit. The journal entries themselves are taught in the [correcting errors study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-9215-correcting-errors-oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting/). Practise afterwards with the [effect of errors on profit revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-9215-the-effect-of-errors-on-profit-calculations-oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-revision-notes/) and the [effect of errors on profit practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-9215-the-effect-of-errors-on-profit-calculations-oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-practice/).

## Two profit figures

In a trading business's income statement:

```
Revenue (sales − sales returns)
− Cost of sales (opening inventory + purchases − purchases returns
                 + carriage inwards − closing inventory)
= Gross profit
+ Other income (discount received, rent received, commission received)
− Expenses (wages, carriage outwards, discount allowed, repairs, ...)
= Profit for the year
```

So every error falls into one of three groups.

| Error is in... | Gross profit | Profit for the year |
|---|---|---|
| Sales, sales returns, purchases, purchases returns, carriage inwards, inventory | Changes | Changes by the same amount |
| Other income or expenses | No change | Changes |
| Assets, liabilities, capital or drawings only | No change | No change |

An error that moves an amount **between** the two parts (for example, carriage inwards treated as carriage outwards) changes gross profit but leaves profit for the year unchanged, because the same amount is still deducted somewhere.

## Reasoning about direction

Think about whether the error made profit too high or too low, then reverse it.

| If the error left... | Profit was | The correction... |
|---|---|---|
| Income understated (or omitted) | Understated | Adds to profit |
| Income overstated | Overstated | Deducts from profit |
| An expense understated (or omitted) | Overstated | Deducts from profit |
| An expense overstated | Understated | Adds to profit |
| Closing inventory overstated | Overstated (cost of sales too low) | Deducts from profit |
| Closing inventory understated | Understated | Adds to profit |

Check your answer with the correcting journal: crediting an income or expense account raises profit; debiting one lowers it. Both methods must agree.

## Closing inventory errors

Closing inventory is deducted in cost of sales. If it is counted or valued too high, cost of sales is too low and both gross profit and profit for the year are too high by the same amount. The correction reduces both. An inventory error does not pass through the suspense account: inventory is counted, not posted from the ledger, so it does not affect the trial balance totals.

## Errors that touch only the statement of financial position

These leave profit unchanged, even though the accounts were wrong:

- a payment to one supplier posted to another supplier's account (commission between personal accounts);
- cash taken by the owner recorded as a reduction of the loan;
- a customer's receipt credited to the right customer but debited to cash instead of bank.

Saying "no effect on profit" is a correct answer and earns the mark when it is right. Do not invent an effect.

## Worked example 1: a two-column statement of corrected profit

Kestrel Shoes calculated a draft gross profit of 38,400 and a draft profit for the year of 14,250. These errors were then found:

1. Closing inventory was overvalued by 1,200.
2. Goods returned by a customer, 450, were not recorded.
3. Carriage inwards of 380 was debited to the carriage outwards account.
4. The owner's home electricity bill of 290, paid by the business, was debited to the business's electricity account.
5. Repairs to the delivery van of 640 were debited to the motor vehicles account. Ignore depreciation.
6. Discount received of 220 was not recorded.
7. A payment of 1,500 to a supplier was debited to a different supplier's account.

**Step 1: reason out each effect.**

| Error | Reasoning | Gross profit | Profit for the year |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| 1 | Closing inventory too high, so cost of sales too low | −1,200 | −1,200 |
| 2 | Sales returns omitted, so revenue too high | −450 | −450 |
| 3 | Carriage inwards belongs in cost of sales; carriage outwards was overstated by the same amount | −380 | 0 |
| 4 | Private expense is drawings; business electricity overstated | 0 | +290 |
| 5 | Repairs are an expense, wrongly treated as an asset | 0 | −640 |
| 6 | Income omitted | 0 | +220 |
| 7 | Between two supplier accounts | 0 | 0 |

**Step 2: the statement of corrected profit.**

| | Gross profit | Profit for the year |
|---|---:|---:|
| Draft figures | 38,400 | 14,250 |
| 1 Closing inventory overvalued | (1,200) | (1,200) |
| 2 Sales returns not recorded | (450) | (450) |
| 3 Carriage inwards posted as carriage outwards | (380) | 0 |
| 4 Private electricity charged as an expense | 0 | 290 |
| 5 Van repairs treated as a non-current asset | 0 | (640) |
| 6 Discount received not recorded | 0 | 220 |
| 7 Payment posted to the wrong supplier | 0 | 0 |
| **Corrected figures** | **36,370** | **12,470** |

Error 3 shows why the two columns are needed. It reduces gross profit by 380, but because carriage outwards was overstated by the same 380, the effect on profit for the year is nil.

Net change in profit for the year: −1,200 − 450 + 290 − 640 + 220 = −1,780, and 14,250 − 1,780 = 12,470.

## Worked example 2: from a loss to a profit

A trader's draft income statement shows a **loss** for the year of 1,800. Two errors are found: the sales journal was undercast by 2,000, and the wages account was overcast by 600.

```
Draft loss for the year            (1,800)
Add: sales understated               2,000
Add: wages overstated                  600
Corrected profit for the year          800
```

Treat a loss as a negative profit. A correction that adds 2,600 to a loss of 1,800 turns it into a profit of 800. Write the corrected figure with the word "profit" or "loss"; a bare 800 is ambiguous.

## Errors corrected through the suspense account

The suspense account is neither income nor expense, so the suspense side of a journal never changes profit. Only the other side matters. For example, sales undercast by 2,000 is corrected by Dr Suspense, Cr Sales: the credit to sales raises profit by 2,000; the debit to suspense has no effect.

## Common mistakes

- Reversing the direction: an expense that was too high means profit was too low, so the correction **adds**.
- Showing a change in gross profit for an expense such as wages or carriage outwards.
- Forgetting that closing inventory errors affect both gross profit and profit for the year.
- Giving a profit effect for errors between two personal accounts or two asset accounts.
- Counting the suspense side of a correction as a profit effect.
- Adding corrections to a loss as if it were a profit, so the sign of the answer is wrong.

## Exam technique

- **"Prepare a statement of corrected profit"**: start with the draft figure, one line per error with a description, brackets or a minus sign for deductions, and a labelled corrected total.
- **"State the effect on gross profit and on profit for the year"**: give both, even when one is "no effect".
- **"Explain"** an effect: name the account, say whether it was overstated or understated, and conclude what that did to profit.
- Show **0** for errors with no profit effect when a table is asked for; it shows the examiner you considered them.
