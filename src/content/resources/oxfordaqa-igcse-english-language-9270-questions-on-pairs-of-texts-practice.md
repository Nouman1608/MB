## The source pack: a new railway line (all original)

**Source A (rail company leaflet).**

> **Faster. Greener. Closer.** The new Northvale line will cut the journey to the city from ninety
> minutes to thirty-five. Trains will run every fifteen minutes. Construction near Ashby village
> begins in spring, and our community team will be on hand to answer questions. Together, we are
> building the future.

**Source B (village resident's blog).**

> They say "closer", and I suppose it's true: in two years the city will be thirty-five minutes away.
> But the meadow behind the church will be a cutting, and the lane where I learned to ride a bike
> will be a bridge. I went to the community meeting. A young man with a lanyard showed us
> drawings in which the trees were the size of matchsticks. Closer to the city, yes. Further from
> everything else.

**Source C (short story extract).**

> On the last morning before the fences went up, Priya's grandfather took her to the meadow. He did
> not say why. He walked slowly to the oak in the middle, put his palm flat against the bark, and
> stood there for a long time, as if he were taking its pulse. Then he said, "Right," and they went
> home, and he never mentioned it again.

## Section A — Short questions

**1.** Using Sources A and B, list **two** facts about the new line that both sources include. **[2]**

**2.** Explain how the word "closer" is used differently in Source A and Source B. **[3]**

**3.** In Source B, what does the detail of trees "the size of matchsticks" suggest about the writer's
view of the community meeting? **[2]**

**4.** Identify the form of each source, and explain how one difference in form affects the way the
issue is presented. **[4]**

**5.** Which comparison axis would be most useful for comparing Sources A and B, and why? **[2]**

## Section B — Longer questions

**6.** Using Sources A, B and C, summarise what the reader learns about the effects of the new line
on Ashby village. **[6]**

**7.** Compare the writers' attitudes to the new line in Sources A and B. **[8]**

**8.** Compare how the writers of Sources B and C present the meadow. **[8]**

**9.** Source A uses a short slogan; Source C uses a story. Compare how each tries to make the reader
feel about change. **[6]**

**10.** Write **one** integrated paragraph comparing the structure of Source A and Source B. **[4]**

**11.** Evaluate how successfully Source A achieves its purpose, compared with how successfully
Source B achieves its own. **[6]**

**12.** Rewrite this weak answer so that it compares the texts throughout: "Source A is positive. It
says 'Faster. Greener. Closer.' Source B is negative. It talks about the meadow." **[3]**

## Answers

**How these are marked.** Short questions are point-marked as shown. For the longer questions, the
bracketed credits show one way to reach the tariff; other valid points of the same quality earn the
same credit.

**1.** [1] each: the journey to the city will take thirty-five minutes; there is a community team or
meeting for residents.

**2.** In Source A, "closer" is a selling point meaning a shorter journey to the city [1]. In Source B,
the writer repeats the word with scepticism [1] and then reverses it with "Further from everything
else", suggesting the village loses its closeness to its land and memories [1].

**3.** The tiny trees suggest the drawings downplayed the loss of nature [1], so the writer sees the
meeting as presenting a misleading, reassuring picture [1].

**4.** Forms [2 for all three, 1 for two]: A leaflet; B blog; C story extract. Effect of one difference
[2]: for example, the leaflet's slogans and facts present the line as a benefit to be sold, whereas
the blog's first-person anecdote presents it as a personal loss.

**5.** Perspective or tone [1], because the two texts take opposite attitudes to the same facts, so the
comparison reveals how each writer frames the line [1].

**6.** Credit up to 6: faster journeys and frequent trains (A) [1]; construction near the village from
spring (A) [1]; the meadow becoming a cutting and the lane becoming a bridge (B) [1]; doubts about how
honestly the plans were presented (B) [1]; fences going up around the meadow (C) [1]; a sense of
quiet grief among older residents (C) [1].

**7.** Credit up to 8: A is enthusiastic and presents the line as progress, while B is sceptical and
sees loss [2]; A's inclusive "Together, we are building the future" contrasts with B's personal "the
lane where I learned to ride a bike" [2]; A focuses on time saved, while B accepts that fact but
values place and memory more [2]; B echoes and turns A's language ("closer"), so its attitude is
partly a reply to the company's message [2].

**8.** Credit up to 8: both present the meadow as a place of memory and value about to be lost [2]; B
states the loss directly through the contrast "the meadow behind the church will be a cutting", while
C never states it, showing it through the grandfather's silent gesture [2]; B uses a first-person,
slightly bitter voice, while C uses a restrained third-person narrative that leaves the reader to
infer feeling [2]; the image of "taking its pulse" in C presents the oak as alive, which makes the
loss more emotional than B's list of changes [2].

**9.** Credit up to 6: A's slogan compresses change into three positive comparative adjectives, so
change feels exciting and simple [2]; C slows down, using one small scene and a silence, so change
feels like a private loss [2]; A works by instant impression, C by inference, so A aims at approval
and C at sympathy [2].

**10.** One mark for each: a topic sentence naming both texts' structures [1]; evidence of A's
structure, such as the slogan, then facts, then the inclusive close [1]; evidence of B's structure,
such as the concession "I suppose it's true", the losses, then the reversed slogan [1]; a final line
on what the comparison shows, such as that B is built as a reply to A [1].

**11.** Credit up to 6: A's purpose is to promote the line, and its slogans, facts and reassurance
achieve this clearly [2]; B's purpose is to express and share a resident's loss, and its anecdote and
reversed slogan achieve this memorably [2]; a judgement that compares, such as that A is effective
for readers who value speed, while B is more persuasive for readers who value place, because it
answers A's own language [2].

**12.** A rewritten answer that names both texts in one topic sentence [1], gives evidence from each
[1], and states what the comparison shows [1], for example: "Whereas Source A sells the line with
the upbeat slogan 'Faster. Greener. Closer.', Source B describes the meadow becoming 'a cutting', so
the same project is presented as progress by one writer and as loss by the other."

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing about Source A, then Source B, with no link between them.
- Using Source C in Question 7, which names only A and B.
- Comparing on trivial features instead of perspective, method or effect.
- In Question 6, comparing instead of combining.

## Frequently asked questions

**Will every question in Section A involve two texts?**
No. The specification says questions are asked on individual texts and pairs of texts, and at least
one requires two texts.
