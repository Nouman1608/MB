Condensed recall for **6.3 The changing structure of urban settlements** (land use, bid rent and segregation). For full explanations, read the [Core Human Geography study guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-geography-core-human-geography/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| CBD | Central business district: the core of retail, offices and services |
| Bid rent | The rent a land user will pay, which falls with distance from the centre for most uses |
| Functional zonation | Grouping of similar land uses into zones |
| Residential segregation | Separation of groups by income, ethnicity or other traits |
| Gentrification | Renovation of older housing by wealthier incomers |
| Accessibility | Ease of reaching a place |
| Peak land value intersection | The most accessible and expensive point in the CBD |
| Zone in transition | Mixed, often run-down area around the CBD with older industry and housing |
| Filtering | Housing passing down to lower-income groups as it ages |
| Edge city | Concentration of offices and retail at the urban fringe |

## Location of activities

Land use depends on accessibility, land value, planning rules, history and what is already nearby. Retail and offices pay most for central sites; housing, industry and open land locate further out, though large modern sites often choose cheap edge locations with road access.

## Bid rent explained

Each land user has a **bid rent curve**: how much it will pay at each distance from the centre.

- **Retail** gains most from the centre, where the most customers can reach it, so its curve is steep and highest at the centre.
- **Offices** value access to clients and staff, so their curve is slightly less steep.
- **Industry** needs more space and pays less per unit area.
- **Housing** needs space and pays least near the centre, but outbids other uses further out.

Where one curve is above the others, that use wins the land. This produces rings of land use, and explains why buildings rise higher near the centre: high land prices are spread over more floors. Secondary peaks occur at road junctions and suburban centres, where accessibility is locally high.

## Models

| Model | Idea |
|---|---|
| Burgess (concentric) | Rings: CBD, transition, housing zones by income, commuter zone |
| Hoyt (sector) | Wedges along transport routes |
| Multiple nuclei | Several centres with specialised functions |
| LIC/MIC city models (for example Latin American) | Wealthy spine near the centre; informal settlements on the periphery |

They describe cities generally; real cities depart from them, and models fit HIC cities better than LIC ones.

In many LIC and MIC cities the pattern is reversed from Burgess: wealthier groups live near the centre, with its services and security, while poorer migrants settle on the edge, where land is cheaper or unclaimed. The Griffin and Ford model (1980) for Latin American cities shows a commercial spine with elite housing running out from the CBD, surrounded by zones of declining quality towards peripheral squatter settlements.

## Changing CBD

Trends include decentralisation of shops and offices to out-of-town sites, online shopping, pedestrianisation, redevelopment of vacant sites, a shift to services and leisure, and renewal or regeneration. Some CBDs show decline in edge areas and growth in the core.

| Pressure on the CBD | Response |
|---|---|
| Out-of-town retail parks with free parking | Pedestrianisation, covered malls, events |
| Online shopping | More cafés, restaurants, leisure and "experience" retail |
| Congestion and high rents | Park-and-ride, congestion charges, conversion of offices to housing |
| Vacant units | Business improvement districts, mixed-use redevelopment |

The CBD has **assimilation** and **discard** zones: it extends into higher-quality areas on one side and abandons poorer areas on another, so it shifts over time rather than staying fixed.

## Segregation

Causes: income and house price differences, discrimination, housing policy, and wish to live near similar people. Processes: chain moves, filtering (housing handed down to lower-income groups), gentrification, and planning.

| Process | How it produces segregation |
|---|---|
| Income and the housing market | Groups can only afford certain areas |
| Chain migration | New migrants join relatives in particular neighbourhoods |
| Choice | Shared language, places of worship and shops attract a group |
| Discrimination | Exclusion from some areas or jobs |
| Public housing allocation | Concentrates low-income households in estates |
| Gated communities | Wealthy groups separate themselves physically |

Segregation can bring support networks and services for a group, but it can also concentrate disadvantage: poorer schools, fewer jobs nearby and stigma.

## Annotated model paragraph

*"Explain why land use changes with distance from the CBD."*

> The CBD is the most accessible point, so competition for land is highest (**cause**). Retail can pay the most there, so it outbids other uses (**bid rent**). Further out, housing and industry win the cheaper land (**outcome**). But planning and history can override this pattern (**qualification**).

## Exam traps

- Bid rent is a theory about how land users compete, not a map of zones.
- Models are simplifications; always evaluate them.
- Segregation is a pattern, caused by a set of processes; name both.
- Say what a trend means for people, for example displacement from gentrification.
- Do not apply Burgess to LIC cities without noting that wealthy groups often live near the centre there.

## Self-test

1. Define bid rent.
2. Which model uses wedges along routes?
3. Give two reasons shops leave the CBD.
4. What is gentrification?
5. Name one cause of residential segregation.
6. Why do models have limits?
7. Why does retail have the steepest bid rent curve?
8. What is the peak land value intersection?
9. What are assimilation and discard zones?
10. How does the Latin American city model differ from Burgess?
11. What is filtering?

**Answers:** 1. The rent a land user will pay, falling with distance from the centre. 2. Hoyt's sector model. 3. High rents, congestion, online shopping, cheaper edge sites with parking. 4. Renovation of older housing by wealthier people, raising prices. 5. Income differences, discrimination, housing policy. 6. Real cities are more complex and vary with place and time. 7. It depends most on customer access, which is highest at the centre. 8. The most accessible, highest-value point in the CBD. 9. Areas the CBD is extending into, and areas it is abandoning. 10. Elite housing lies near the centre along a spine, and poor squatter settlements are on the edge. 11. Housing passing to lower-income groups as it ages.
