Sub-topics 1.5 and 1.6 of Cambridge IGCSE Environmental Management (0680) ask how countries and households can manage energy resources so that supply lasts and environmental damage falls, and whether fracking is a good way to obtain more natural gas and oil. You need to describe eight named management strategies and four new energy resources, define fracking exactly as the syllabus does, and discuss the benefits and limitations of each. The rest of Topic 1 (rocks, minerals and energy resources) is in the [Natural Resources study guide](/resources/igcse-cambridge-environmental-management-natural-resources/).

## Where this fits in 0680

Topic 1 Natural resources has six sub-topics for examination in 2027, 2028 and 2029. Sub-topic 1.4 classifies energy resources and explains how they generate electricity; 1.5 turns to managing demand and developing new resources; 1.6 deals with one controversial way of extending fossil fuel supply. Expect "describe" questions on named strategies and "discuss the benefits and limitations" questions, often based on a source such as a graph of energy use or a map of a proposed fracking site.

## How can energy resources be managed?

Managing energy means meeting demand while using finite resources more slowly and reducing pollution. The syllabus names eight strategies.

| Strategy | What it involves | Benefit | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Reducing consumption** | Switching off lights and devices, lowering thermostats, using less hot water | Cheap or free; immediate savings | Relies on people changing habits |
| **Insulation** | Loft and cavity wall insulation, double glazing, draught-proofing | Cuts heat loss, so less fuel is burned for heating | Up-front cost; harder to fit in older buildings |
| **Energy-efficient devices** | LED bulbs, efficient appliances, fuel-efficient and electrically propelled vehicles | Same service for less energy; electric vehicles produce no exhaust emissions in use | Higher purchase price; electric vehicles are only as clean as the electricity used to charge them |
| **Exploiting existing energy resources** | Making fuller and more efficient use of resources already available, such as reserves in existing fields or efficient power stations | Uses infrastructure already built | Extends fossil fuel use and its emissions |
| **Education on energy conservation** | Campaigns, school programmes, energy labels on appliances | Low cost; changes long-term behaviour | Effects are slow and hard to measure |
| **Transport policies** | Better public transport, cycle lanes, car sharing, congestion charges | Reduces fuel use and urban air pollution | Expensive to build; unpopular charges |
| **Battery storage** | Storing surplus electricity, especially from solar and wind | Solves part of the intermittency problem of renewables | Batteries are costly and need mined materials |
| **Development of new energy resources** | Investing in hydrogen and heat pumps (below) | Reduces reliance on fossil fuels | Research and new infrastructure are expensive |

## What are the new energy resources?

### Blue and green hydrogen

Hydrogen releases energy when burned or used in a fuel cell, and the only product of using it is water. The syllabus distinguishes two ways of producing it:

- **Blue hydrogen** is produced from **natural gas**. The process releases carbon dioxide, which must be captured and stored for the hydrogen to count as low-carbon. Benefit: uses existing gas supplies and infrastructure. Limitation: still depends on a fossil fuel, and carbon capture is costly and not fully effective.
- **Green hydrogen** is produced using **renewable resources**, by using renewable electricity to split water into hydrogen and oxygen. Benefit: very low emissions across the whole process. Limitation: currently expensive, and needs large amounts of renewable electricity.

Both types are hard to store and transport because hydrogen is a very light, flammable gas.

### Ground and air source heat pumps

- A **ground source heat pump** transfers heat from the ground, through pipes buried in a garden or borehole, to heat a building. The ground stays at a fairly steady temperature, so output is reliable.
- An **air source heat pump** transfers heat from the outside air to heat a building, even in cool weather.

Benefits: they deliver more heat energy than the electrical energy they use, and produce no emissions on site. Limitations: high installation cost, ground source systems need space for digging, and they work best in well-insulated buildings. Unlike geothermal power stations, heat pumps work anywhere, not only in tectonically active regions.

## What is fracking?

The syllabus defines **fracking** as the extraction of natural gas or petroleum from shale rock by hydraulic fracturing. A well is drilled down and then horizontally through the shale layer. Fluid made mainly of water, mixed with sand and chemicals, is pumped in at very high pressure, cracking the rock. The sand holds the cracks open, and gas or oil trapped in the shale flows up the well. Shale is impermeable (see 1.1), which is why the gas cannot escape without fracturing.

## Benefits and limitations of fracking

| Benefits | Limitations |
|---|---|
| Increases domestic supply, improving energy security and reducing imports | Still a fossil fuel, so it adds carbon dioxide and delays the move to renewables |
| Creates jobs and tax revenue | Can cause small earthquakes (induced seismicity) |
| Gas replacing coal in power stations lowers carbon dioxide and sulfur dioxide emissions | Risk of groundwater contamination from fracking fluids or leaking wells |
| Can lower energy prices where production is large | Uses very large volumes of water; wastewater must be treated |
| Can use existing gas pipelines | Methane, a powerful greenhouse gas, can leak; noise, lorry traffic and visual impact for local communities |

In England, the government introduced a moratorium on fracking in 2019 after tremors at a site in Lancashire, an example of how the limitations can outweigh the benefits in public decisions.

## Worked example

A source shows that a country imports most of its gas and has large shale deposits under farmland near a town. Discuss whether it should allow fracking.

```
Benefits    energy security, fewer imports, jobs, gas instead of coal
Limitations earthquakes, groundwater risk near farms and town,
            water use, methane leaks, still a fossil fuel
Judgement   weigh local risks against national benefit; strict regulation,
            monitoring, or investing instead in 1.5 strategies
```

A full-mark answer uses the source details (farmland, town) and reaches a judgement.

## Common mistakes

- Defining fracking loosely as "drilling for gas" without shale rock and hydraulic fracturing.
- Mixing up blue hydrogen (from natural gas) and green hydrogen (from renewable resources).
- Calling heat pumps geothermal power stations.
- Listing strategies without a benefit and a limitation when the question says "discuss".
- Saying electric vehicles produce no pollution at all.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between blue and green hydrogen?** Blue hydrogen is produced from natural gas, with the carbon dioxide ideally captured and stored. Green hydrogen is produced using renewable resources, by splitting water with renewable electricity.

**How does a heat pump save energy?** It transfers existing heat from the ground or air into a building rather than generating heat by burning fuel, so it provides more heat energy than the electricity it uses.

**Why is fracking controversial?** It increases gas and oil supply and energy security, but it can trigger small earthquakes, risks contaminating groundwater, uses large amounts of water and keeps the economy dependent on fossil fuels.
