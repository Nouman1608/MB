## 3.1.6.1 Misallocation of resources

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Market failure | The market system cannot allocate resources efficiently |
| Misallocation | Resources are in the wrong uses, so society gets less than it could from scarce resources |
| Intervention | Government action to counter the misallocation |

### Costs of misallocation

- Welfare lost, because the same resources could have created more value.
- Third parties harmed by costs they did not choose.
- Beneficial goods under-provided.
- Opportunity cost: resources tied up in a poor use cannot be used in a better one.
- Possible damage to resources that future generations need.

### Methods of intervention

The specification does not give a closed list. Use these, and link each to the problem it solves.

| Method | Used for | Effect | Main limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tax | Negative externality | Raises cost, cuts output | Correct level is hard to find |
| Subsidy | Positive externality | Lowers cost, raises output | Taxpayers pay |
| Regulation, laws, bans | Negative externality | Sets limits on activity | Enforcement cost |
| Direct provision | Positive externality | Government supplies the good | Uses public funds |
| Information, education | Either | Changes choices | Slow, uncertain |

Section 3.2.3.4 covers policies to correct positive and negative externalities in the second half of the course.

## 3.1.6.2 Externalities

### Core formulas

| Quantity | Formula |
|---|---|
| Social cost | Add the external cost to the private cost |
| Social benefit | Add the external benefit to the private benefit |
| External cost | What is left of social cost once private cost is taken away |
| External benefit | What is left of social benefit once private benefit is taken away |
| Share of private cost | External cost / private cost × 100 |

Worked reminder. A good has a private cost of 30 per unit and an external cost of 12.

1. Social cost = 30 + 12 = 42.
2. External cost as a share of private cost = 12 / 30 × 100 = 40%.
3. If 500 units are made, total external cost = 500 × 12 = 6,000.

### Four cases

| | Production | Consumption |
|---|---|---|
| Negative | Factory smoke over a village | Litter dropped after a takeaway meal |
| Positive | A farm planting trees that cool nearby land | A household that keeps a front garden its neighbours enjoy looking at |

Both production and consumption can cause negative externalities. Positive externalities exist in both too.

### Reasoning chains

**Negative externality.** Private cost < social cost. The producer ignores the external cost. Supply is higher than the social best. Price too low, output too high. Over-production. Welfare loss.

**Positive externality.** Private benefit < social benefit. Buyers ignore the external benefit. Demand is below social value. Price and output too low. Under-production. Welfare loss.

### Diagram in words

- Negative externality: the social cost curve is above the private cost (supply) curve. The social optimum is at lower output and higher price than the market equilibrium. The gap between the curves is the external cost.
- Positive externality: the social benefit curve is above the demand curve. The social optimum is at higher output and higher price than the market equilibrium.

### Method in steps: finding the best output from a table

1. Add the external amount to get the social column.
2. Find the last block where benefit is at least cost, using the private columns. That is the market output.
3. Repeat using the social columns. That is the social optimum.
4. Compare. For a negative externality, loss = social cost minus benefit on the block between the two outputs. For a positive one, gain = social benefit minus cost on that block.
5. Check whether a tax or subsidy equal to the external amount moves the firm to the social optimum.

Second worked reminder, for a positive externality. A course gives a private benefit of 18 per place and an external benefit of 7.

1. Social benefit = 18 + 7 = 25 per place.
2. External benefit as a share of private benefit = 7 / 18 × 100 = 38.9% (1 decimal place).
3. If the cost per place is 22, buyers who value a place at 18 will not pay for it, yet society values it at 25. The place is not provided, so the market under-provides.

### Using the context

If a question gives a named good, place or firm, name it in your answer. Write "the dye works ignores the cost to the river users" rather than "the firm ignores the cost to others". Use the data in a table or extract too, quoting a figure and what it shows. Then link the point to an effect on output, price or welfare.

### Links to the rest of the course

- Opportunity cost (3.1.1): misallocation means resources are not in their best use.
- Demand and supply (3.1.3): externalities shift the cost or benefit curve that the market ignores.
- Elasticity (3.1.3): the effect of a tax or subsidy on quantity depends on elasticity.
- Competitive and concentrated markets (3.1.5): a market with little competition can also fail to allocate resources efficiently, but this unit focuses on externalities.
- Government objectives and policies (3.2): section 3.2.3.4 returns to policies for positive and negative externalities.

### Tax and subsidy reminders

| Policy | Calculation | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Tax revenue | Tax per unit × units sold | 40 × 300 = 12,000 |
| Subsidy bill | Subsidy per unit × units sold | 6 × 2,000 = 12,000 |
| Percentage change in quantity | PED × percentage change in price | -0.4 × 10 = -4% |

When demand is inelastic, a price rise from a tax cuts quantity only slightly. Revenue is healthy, but consumption barely moves.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Private vs social**: social includes the effect on everyone else.
- **Market failure vs high prices**: a high price is not market failure on its own.
- **Production vs consumption externality**: ask who creates the effect, the maker or the user.
- **Tax vs subsidy**: a tax raises cost and cuts output, a subsidy lowers cost and raises output.
- **Regulation vs tax**: a rule sets a limit, a tax changes the price.
- **Best output vs zero output**: removing all pollution is rarely the social optimum.

## Evaluation phrases that earn credit

- "It depends on how elastic demand is, so the tax may cut consumption only a little."
- "Nobody can price the damage exactly, so a tax may end up too high or too low."
- "The subsidy has an opportunity cost, because the money could have funded something else."
- "A ban acts faster, but enforcement costs money."
- "In the long run, education may change attitudes, so this method may be more effective over time."

## Quick self-test

1. Define market failure.
2. Give the formula for social cost.
3. A firm has a private cost of 18 per unit and an external cost of 7. What is the social cost?
4. Give one example of a negative consumption externality.
5. Give one example of a positive production externality.
6. Why does a negative externality lead to over-production?
7. Why does a positive externality lead to under-production?
8. A subsidy of 6 per unit is paid on 2,000 units. What is the bill?
9. Name one limit of using a tax to correct a negative externality.
10. A tax of 40 per tonne is paid on 300 tonnes. How much revenue is raised?
11. Price rises 10% and PED is -0.4. By what percentage does quantity change?
12. Say whether the best amount of a polluting activity is usually zero.

### Answers

1. The market system cannot allocate resources efficiently, so resources are misallocated.
2. Private cost + external cost.
3. 18 + 7 = **25**.
4. Loud music that disturbs neighbours (or litter, or secondhand smoke).
5. A firm trains workers who later move to other employers (or a farm that plants trees).
6. Producers ignore the external cost, so their costs are below the social cost, supply is too high, and output is above the social best.
7. Buyers ignore the external benefit, so demand is below the social value and output is below the social best.
8. 6 × 2,000 = **12,000**.
9. For example: the right tax level is hard to find, or demand is inelastic so consumption barely falls.
10. 40 × 300 = **12,000**.
11. -0.4 × 10 = **-4%**, a fall of 4%.
12. No. The best level is where social benefit equals social cost, which usually allows some activity.

## Where marks are usually lost

- A definition that says the market "breaks down" but never mentions resources or efficiency.
- Writing about private costs only, so the externality is missing.
- Not saying which way output moves compared with the social best.
- Drawing the social cost curve below the private cost curve.
- Treating pollution as zero-sum, so no explanation of the best level.
- Describing a policy without saying how it changes costs or behaviour.
- Giving only advantages of intervention in an evaluation question.
- Not using the context in the question, such as the named good or place.
- Calculation answers with no working, or the wrong unit.
