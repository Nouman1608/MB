This guide covers section **3.4.1 Organisational structures** of OxfordAQA International GCSE Business (9225), part of Topic 4, Human resources. The specification asks you to understand internal organisational structures and the terms **span of control, chain of command, delayering and delegation**; why businesses have internal structures, including the different **job roles and responsibilities**; the impact of a **tall or flat structure** on how a business is managed; **centralisation and decentralisation**; the appropriateness of different structures; and how structure may affect **communication**. All businesses and figures in this guide are invented.

Condensed notes are in the [revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-organisational-structures-revision-notes/) and questions are in the [practice set](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-organisational-structures-practice/).

## Why businesses need an organisational structure

An **organisational structure** shows how a business is organised: who is in charge of whom, who does which job and how decisions and instructions pass through the business. It is usually drawn as an **organisation chart**, a diagram of job roles joined by lines of authority.

A structure is needed because, without one, people would not know:

- **who makes decisions** and who must follow them
- **what their own job is** and what they are responsible for
- **who to report to** if they have a problem
- **how departments connect**, such as sales, production, finance and human resources.

Larger businesses need more structure than a small business where the owner knows what everyone is doing.

### Job roles and responsibilities

In a typical business there are layers (levels) of employees:

| Level | Typical roles | Typical responsibilities |
|---|---|---|
| Top | Directors, chief executive | Set the aims and long-term strategy; make the biggest decisions |
| Middle | Department or branch managers | Turn strategy into plans; manage budgets and supervisors |
| Lower | Supervisors, team leaders | Organise day-to-day work; supervise staff |
| Operational | Shop assistants, factory workers, drivers | Carry out the tasks that produce goods and services |

Clear job descriptions help each person to understand what is expected, avoid duplication and make sure that every task is covered.

## Key terms

### Chain of command

The **chain of command** is the line of authority from the top of the business to the bottom. It shows who gives orders to whom and who is accountable. A worker reports to a supervisor, who reports to a manager, who reports to a director. A long chain means that instructions pass through many people.

### Span of control

The **span of control** is the number of people who report directly to one manager or supervisor. A manager with eight direct reports has a span of control of eight.

- A **narrow span** (for example 3 or 4) allows close supervision and support, but needs more managers.
- A **wide span** (for example 12 or more) needs fewer managers, but each manager has less time for each person, so workers may need to work independently.

### Delegation

**Delegation** means passing authority to a lower level to carry out a task or make a decision, while the manager remains responsible for the result. A shop manager might delegate the weekly stock order to a trusted assistant.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Frees managers to concentrate on important decisions | The task may be done differently, or less well, than the manager would do it |
| Develops skills and motivates staff who are trusted | Staff may not want extra responsibility or may need training |
| Decisions are quicker because staff nearer the customer can take them | The manager is still responsible if things go wrong |

### Delayering

**Delayering** means removing one or more levels from the structure. The business becomes flatter.

Reasons: reduce costs (fewer managers' salaries), speed up communication, and give more responsibility to the remaining staff. Drawbacks: remaining managers have wider spans and may be overloaded; staff may fear redundancy, which lowers morale; promotion prospects shrink as there are fewer levels; and the business pays redundancy costs.

## Tall and flat structures

### Tall structures

A **tall structure** has many levels and narrow spans of control.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Close supervision and clear chain of command | Instructions and information take a long time to pass up and down |
| Clear promotion path, which can motivate staff | Higher costs, as there are more managers |
| Staff get more help and guidance | Distance between the top and the workers; messages may be changed on the way |
| | Staff may feel little responsibility |

### Flat structures

A **flat structure** has few levels and wide spans of control.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Faster communication between top and bottom | Managers may have too many people to supervise |
| Lower costs, with fewer managers | Fewer promotion opportunities |
| Staff have more responsibility and may feel more motivated | Staff may be unsure who to ask for help |
| Easier for senior managers to hear workers' views | Mistakes may not be noticed quickly |

**Worked example 1: comparing structures.** Both firms have 64 workers.

- **Tall structure (span of control 4):** 1 director, 4 managers, 16 supervisors and 64 workers. That is 4 levels and 85 people in total.
- **Flat structure (span of control 8):** 1 director, 8 managers and 64 workers. That is 3 levels and 73 people in total.

If managers earn $45,000 and supervisors $32,000, the management cost (excluding the director) is:

```
Tall: 4 x $45,000 + 16 x $32,000 = $180,000 + $512,000 = $692,000
Flat: 8 x $45,000                                        = $360,000
Saving from the flat structure = $692,000 - $360,000 = $332,000 a year
```

The flat structure is much cheaper, but each manager supervises eight people instead of four, and the workers have fewer chances of promotion.

## Centralisation and decentralisation

**Centralisation** means that decisions are made by senior managers at the top (often at head office).

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Consistent decisions and standards across the business | Slow, as local managers must wait for approval |
| Senior managers keep control and can see the whole picture | Local managers feel less motivated and may not understand local needs |
| Cost savings by running functions such as buying in one place | Senior managers can be overloaded |

**Decentralisation** means that decisions are passed to lower levels, such as branch or department managers.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Faster decisions that fit local needs | Decisions may differ between branches, harming consistency |
| Motivates managers who feel trusted | Lower-level managers may make poor decisions |
| Frees senior managers to concentrate on strategy | Harder for top managers to keep control |

A bank might centralise rules about lending, but decentralise day-to-day customer service to its branches.

## How structure affects communication

Communication is the passing of information, ideas and instructions.

- **Tall structures** pass messages through many levels. This causes delays and errors and may weaken communication from the bottom upwards, since workers' ideas have to pass through many managers.
- **Flat structures** have fewer levels, so messages travel faster and are less likely to change. Workers can speak directly to senior managers.
- **Centralised businesses** send instructions downwards from the top. **Decentralised** businesses encourage two-way communication.

**Worked example 2: time for a message.** In a tall structure with 5 levels, a message from the director takes one day to pass from each level to the next: 4 days. In a flat structure with 3 levels, the same message takes 2 days. A decision to change a product's price may need to be made within a day, so the delay in the tall structure could cost sales.

## Which structure is appropriate?

| Situation | More suitable structure | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Small shop with five staff | Flat, simple | Owner can supervise directly |
| Large factory with strict safety rules | Taller, more centralised | Close supervision and consistent standards |
| Creative design studio | Flat, decentralised | Staff need freedom and fast decisions |
| Chain of shops in different countries | More decentralised | Local managers understand local customers |
| Business in a crisis needing quick changes | More centralised for a short time | Clear direction from the top |

The right structure can change over time. As a business grows, it may add levels to keep control, and later delayer to reduce costs and speed up decisions.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing span of control with the number of levels. Span is the number of people reporting to one manager.
- Saying a tall structure is always bad or a flat structure always good. It depends on the business.
- Forgetting that delegation passes authority but not responsibility.
- Confusing delayering (removing levels) with downsizing (cutting staff).
- Describing communication problems without linking them to the number of levels.
- Writing about structures in general terms without using the details of the business.

## Exam technique for this section

For **define** questions, give the meaning and, where possible, a short example. For **explain** questions, link the feature to its effect: "A wide span of control means the manager has less time to support each worker, so problems may be missed". For **calculate** questions, show how you worked out the span or the cost. For **evaluate** questions, such as whether a business should delayer, compare cost savings and faster communication with the risks to morale and workload, and make a judgement based on the facts.
