This guide covers **Question 2, the Use of Language task**, in Section A of Cambridge O Level English
Language 1123 Paper 1 Reading. The syllabus says candidates "respond to a series of sub-questions based
on Text A. These are short answers worth a total of nine marks relating to the author's use of
language and the effect this has." It tests a single objective, **R4**: "demonstrate understanding of
how writers achieve effects and influence readers". For the paper as a whole, see the
[Paper 1 Reading study guide](/resources/o-level-english-language-paper-1-reading/). This page is about
the skill itself: saying precisely what a writer's words do.

## Meaning and effect

Every language answer has two layers.

- **Meaning**: what the words say, in plain terms. *"The streets were choked with traffic"* means the
  streets were very crowded.
- **Effect**: what the choice of words makes the reader see, feel or think, beyond the plain meaning.
  *"Choked"* suggests the city is struggling to breathe, as if the traffic were strangling it; the
  reader senses discomfort and something close to danger, not just inconvenience.

Question 2 rewards the second layer. An answer that only gives the meaning is answering a comprehension
question, which belongs to Question 1.

## Connotation: the engine of effect

A word's **denotation** is its dictionary meaning. Its **connotations** are the associations it carries.
*Home* and *house* can denote the same building; *home* carries warmth, belonging and family. Writers
choose words for their connotations. When you explain an effect, ask: what else does this word bring
with it? What would be lost if a plainer word were used?

Compare: *"She walked into the room"*, *"She drifted into the room"*, *"She stormed into the room"*. The
action is the same; each verb gives a different character and mood. Explaining that difference is the
whole of the skill.

## What to look for

The syllabus's reading content asks you to "recognise and respond to linguistic devices, figurative
language and imagery". In a narrative Text A, that usually means:

| Feature | Example (original) | Ask |
|---|---|---|
| precise verbs | the wind *clawed* at the tent | what kind of action, what does it suggest? |
| adjectives and adverbs | a *brittle* silence | what quality does it give? |
| simile | the lake lay *like a sheet of tin* | what is compared, and what does that reveal? |
| metaphor | the classroom *was a furnace* | what does the fusion suggest? |
| personification | the house *watched* them leave | what human quality, and why? |
| sensory imagery | the *sour* smell of wet wool | which sense, what atmosphere? |
| sound | the *hiss and spit* of the fire | what does it make the reader hear? |
| sentence form | *Silence. Then a knock.* | what does length or rhythm do? |
| contrast | bright banners over empty seats | what does the gap reveal? |

Naming the feature is not required for credit and earns nothing on its own. Use the name when it
helps you explain; never let it replace the explanation.

## The three-step answer

1. **Quote precisely**: the word or short phrase, not the whole sentence.
2. **Explain the meaning or image**: what is being said or pictured.
3. **Explain the effect in context**: what it suggests about the character, place or situation, and
   how the reader responds.

## Worked example

The extract is original, written for this guide.

> By noon the station had given up. The departure board flickered and went blank, and the crowd that
> had been jostling on the platform since dawn sagged onto benches, suitcases, the bare concrete.
> Heat pressed down through the glass roof like a hand. Somewhere a baby wailed, stopped, began again.
> An announcer's voice crackled over the speakers, apologised for something no one could hear, and was
> swallowed by the hum of a thousand electric fans.

**(a)** Explain what the writer suggests by saying that "the station had given up". **[2]**

> *Answer:* The station is personified as if it were a tired person who has stopped trying [1]. It
> suggests that nothing is working any more and that the whole place, not just the people, has been
> defeated by the heat and the delay [1].

**(b)** Identify the word in the second sentence which shows how tired the crowd had become, and
explain its effect. **[2]**

> *Answer:* "Sagged" [1]. It suggests bodies collapsing under their own weight, as if the people have
> lost all their energy and simply drop wherever they are, which emphasises how long they have been
> waiting [1].

**(c)** Explain how the writer's language in the last three sentences creates a sense of oppressive
discomfort. **[3]**

> *Answer:* The simile "like a hand" makes the heat feel physical and heavy, as if something were
> pushing the people down [1]. The baby that "wailed, stopped, began again" is shown through a broken,
> repetitive rhythm that mirrors the endless, wearing noise [1]. The announcer's voice is "swallowed",
> suggesting that even official information is lost in the heat and noise, which leaves the travellers
> helpless [1].

*Commentary:* each answer quotes briefly, explains, then connects to the situation. Part (c) selects
three different features from the three sentences, each with its own effect, matching the three marks.

## Writing about sentence structure

Sentence form is language too. Look for a very short sentence after long ones (a sudden stop, shock
or emphasis), a long sentence built from a list (abundance, chaos, breathlessness), repetition at the
start of clauses (insistence, rhythm), or a question (doubt, a character's inner voice). As with
words, say what the shape does in this moment of the story, not just what the shape is.

## Avoid empty effects

Some phrases appear in thousands of answers and earn nothing because they fit any text:

- "It makes the reader want to read on."
- "It creates a vivid image in the reader's mind."
- "It makes the writing more interesting."
- "It helps the reader imagine the scene."

Replace each with the specific image or feeling: not "a vivid image", but "the reader pictures people
collapsing like emptied sacks".

## Common mistakes

- **Meaning only**: correct paraphrase with no comment on effect.
- **Device spotting**: "This is a simile" with nothing after it.
- **Over-long quotation** that hides which words you are discussing.
- **Effects that ignore context**: "red suggests danger" when the red is a child's raincoat in a happy
  scene.
- **One point for several marks**: in a three-mark question, use three distinct choices or develop
  one choice in three clear steps.
- **Wrong words chosen** when asked to identify "a word" or "a phrase": check the instruction and the
  paragraph named.

## Exam technique

- Read the question's line or paragraph reference and stay within it.
- If asked for "a word", give one word; for "a phrase", a few words.
- Use "suggests", "implies", "emphasises", "conveys" to move from meaning to effect.
- Question 2 is 9 marks, about a third of Section A: give it time after Question 1, not the minutes
  left over.
