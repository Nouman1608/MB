**Data for every question.** Standard electrode potentials, E⦵ / V: K⁺/K −2.93; Al³⁺/Al −1.66; H₂O/H₂, OH⁻ −0.83; Fe²⁺/Fe −0.45; Co²⁺/Co −0.28; Ni²⁺/Ni −0.26; Sn²⁺/Sn −0.14; H⁺/H₂ 0.00; Cu²⁺/Cu +0.34; I₂/I⁻ +0.54; Ag⁺/Ag +0.80; Br₂/Br⁻ +1.09; O₂, H⁺/H₂O +1.23; Cl₂/Cl⁻ +1.36; H₂O₂, H⁺/H₂O +1.78. F = 96 500 C mol⁻¹; R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Final answers: 3 significant figures unless a question asks for something else.

## Questions

**1.** **(a)** State the standard electrode potential assigned to the half-cell H⁺(aq) + e⁻ ⇌ ½H₂(g). **[1]**
**(b)** Describe the standard hydrogen electrode, including its conditions. **[2]**

**2.** Use the data.

**(a)** Identify the strongest oxidizing agent among Al³⁺, Sn²⁺, Ag⁺ and Br₂. **[1]**
**(b)** Identify the strongest reducing agent among Al, Fe, Cu and I⁻. **[1]**
**(c)** Deduce which of the metals iron, copper and silver can reduce Sn²⁺(aq) to tin under standard conditions. **[1]**

**3.** A voltaic cell is made from an Al³⁺/Al half-cell and a Ni²⁺/Ni half-cell.

**(a)** Identify the anode and state its polarity. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate E⦵cell. **[1]**
**(c)** Deduce the overall equation for the cell reaction. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate ΔG⦵ for the reaction in (c), in kJ mol⁻¹. **[2]**

**4.** Use E⦵cell to predict whether each reaction is spontaneous as written.

**(a)** Cu(s) + 2H⁺(aq) → Cu²⁺(aq) + H₂(g) **[2]**
**(b)** I₂(aq) + 2Br⁻(aq) → 2I⁻(aq) + Br₂(aq) **[2]**
**(c)** Ni(s) + Sn²⁺(aq) → Ni²⁺(aq) + Sn(s) **[2]**

**5.** Acidified hydrogen peroxide oxidizes iodide ions: H₂O₂(aq) + 2H⁺(aq) + 2I⁻(aq) → 2H₂O(l) + I₂(aq).

**(a)** Calculate E⦵cell. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate ΔG⦵ in kJ mol⁻¹. **[2]**
**(c)** The equation is multiplied by 2. State the new values of E⦵cell and ΔG⦵. **[2]**

**6.** **(a)** A cell is built from a standard X²⁺/X half-cell and a standard Cu²⁺/Cu half-cell. E⦵cell = 0.62 V and X is the negative electrode. Determine E⦵(X²⁺/X) and identify X from the data. **[2]**
**(b)** In a methanol fuel cell, 2CH₃OH(l) + 3O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 4H₂O(l), ΔG⦵ = −1404 kJ mol⁻¹ and 12 mol of electrons are transferred. Calculate E⦵cell. **[2]**

**7.** Aqueous solutions are electrolysed with inert electrodes.

**(a)** For potassium iodide solution, deduce the product at each electrode, with half-equations, and explain the cathode product. **[3]**
**(b)** For potassium sulfate solution, state the product at each electrode and explain the anode product. **[2]**

**8.** **(a)** Concentrated NaCl(aq) is electrolysed with inert electrodes. Write both half-equations and explain why the anode product differs from the E⦵ prediction. **[3]**
**(b)** State and explain the main anode product when the solution is very dilute. **[2]**
**(c)** CuSO₄(aq) is electrolysed with graphite electrodes, then with copper electrodes. Write each anode half-equation. **[2]**

**9.** Water containing a little dilute sulfuric acid is electrolysed with platinum electrodes.

**(a)** Write the half-equation at each electrode. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate E⦵cell for the overall reaction and state what it means for the supply voltage. **[1]**
**(c)** 36 cm³ of hydrogen is collected. State the volume of oxygen collected under the same conditions. **[1]**

**10.** A brass brooch is to be silver-plated.

**(a)** State what is used as the anode, the cathode and the electrolyte. **[2]**
**(b)** Give the cathode and anode half-equations. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the concentration of the electrolyte stays almost constant. **[1]**

**11.** Steel for food cans is tin-plated using a tin anode and tin(II) sulfate solution.

**(a)** Write the cathode half-equation and explain why tin, not hydrogen, forms there. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the tin anode is oxidized in preference to water. **[1]**
**(c)** The tin anode is replaced by a graphite anode. State the new anode product and two changes to the electrolyte as electrolysis continues. **[3]**
**(d)** With the power off, steel in the bath can react: Fe(s) + Sn²⁺(aq) → Fe²⁺(aq) + Sn(s). Calculate E⦵cell and ΔG⦵. **[3]**

**12.** (Links to Reactivity 1.4.) For H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → H₂O(l), ΔH⦵ = −285.8 kJ mol⁻¹. Standard entropies, S⦵ / J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹: H₂(g) 130.7, O₂(g) 205.2, H₂O(l) 70.0.

**(a)** Calculate ΔS⦵. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate ΔG⦵ at 298 K. **[2]**
**(c)** Use (b) to calculate E⦵cell for a hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell, and compare it with the E⦵ data. **[3]**
**(d)** Estimate the cell potential at 353 K, assuming ΔH⦵ and ΔS⦵ do not change. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** **0.00 V**, by convention [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Platinum electrode in [H⁺] = 1.00 mol dm⁻³ [1]; hydrogen at 100 kPa, 298 K [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the platinum and all three conditions; "standard conditions" alone is incomplete.

**2. (a)** **Br₂** (+1.09 V is the most positive) [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** **Al** (−1.66 V is the most negative) [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** **Iron only** (−0.45 V is below −0.14 V) [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Oxidizing agents sit on the left of the half-equations and reducing agents on the right; check the side before ranking.

**3. (a)** **Aluminium** is the anode and it is **negative** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** E⦵cell = −0.26 − (−1.66) = **+1.40 V** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Al(s) → Al³⁺(aq) + 3e⁻ and Ni²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Ni(s), so 6 electrons are needed [1]. **2Al(s) + 3Ni²⁺(aq) → 2Al³⁺(aq) + 3Ni(s)** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** n = 6 [1]. ΔG⦵ = −6 × 96 500 × 1.40 = −810 600 J mol⁻¹ = **−811 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* E⦵cell stays at 1.40 V although the half-equations are multiplied; only n carries the scaling into ΔG⦵.

**4. (a)** E⦵cell = 0.00 − 0.34 = −0.34 V [1]; negative, so **not spontaneous** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** E⦵cell = 0.54 − 1.09 = −0.55 V [1]; **not spontaneous**; the reverse, bromine oxidizing iodide, is [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** E⦵cell = −0.14 − (−0.26) = +0.12 V [1]; positive, so **spontaneous** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the signed E⦵cell before the verdict; a bare "yes" or "no" gives a marker nothing to check.

**5. (a)** E⦵cell = 1.78 − 0.54 = **+1.24 V** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** n = 2 [1]; ΔG⦵ = −2 × 96 500 × 1.24 = −239 320 J mol⁻¹ = **−239 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** E⦵cell is unchanged at **+1.24 V** [1]; n = 4, so ΔG⦵ = **−479 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* In (c), state that E⦵cell does not depend on the amounts written, while ΔG⦵ does.

**6. (a)** Copper is the cathode, so 0.62 = 0.34 − E⦵(X²⁺/X) and E⦵(X²⁺/X) = **−0.28 V** [1]. X is **cobalt** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** E⦵cell = −ΔG⦵ ÷ nF = 1 404 000 ÷ (12 × 96 500) [1] = **+1.21 V** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b), convert kJ to J before dividing, or the answer is a thousand times too small.

**7. (a)** Cathode: **hydrogen**, 2H₂O(l) + 2e⁻ → H₂(g) + 2OH⁻(aq) [1]. K⁺ (−2.93 V) is far more negative than water (−0.83 V), so water is reduced instead [1]. Anode: **iodine**, 2I⁻(aq) → I₂(aq) + 2e⁻ [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** **Hydrogen** at the cathode and **oxygen** at the anode [1]. Sulfate ions are not oxidized, so water (+1.23 V) is oxidized: 2H₂O(l) → O₂(g) + 4H⁺(aq) + 4e⁻ [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote the two competing E⦵ values; "potassium is reactive" is weaker than the numbers.

**8. (a)** Cathode: 2H₂O(l) + 2e⁻ → H₂(g) + 2OH⁻(aq) [1]. Anode: 2Cl⁻(aq) → Cl₂(g) + 2e⁻ [1]. E⦵ alone favours oxygen (+1.23 V against +1.36 V), but the values are close and high [Cl⁻] makes chloride oxidation win [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Mainly **oxygen** [1]; with few chloride ions, water is oxidized as the E⦵ values predict [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Graphite: 2H₂O(l) → O₂(g) + 4H⁺(aq) + 4e⁻ [1]. Copper: Cu(s) → Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* In (c), writing the oxygen equation twice misses that a copper anode is itself oxidized.

**9. (a)** Cathode: 2H⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → H₂(g) [1]. Anode: 2H₂O(l) → O₂(g) + 4H⁺(aq) + 4e⁻ [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** E⦵cell = 0.00 − 1.23 = **−1.23 V**, so the supply must provide more than 1.23 V [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** **18 cm³** (2 : 1 ratio) [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* A negative E⦵cell is expected for electrolysis; say what it means for the supply.

**10. (a)** Cathode: the **brooch**; anode: **pure silver** [1]. Electrolyte: a solution containing **Ag⁺ ions**, such as silver nitrate [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Cathode: Ag⁺(aq) + e⁻ → Ag(s) [1]. Anode: Ag(s) → Ag⁺(aq) + e⁻ [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Ag⁺ forms at the anode as fast as it is removed at the cathode [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the brooch as the cathode; reversed electrodes would strip metal from it instead.

**11. (a)** Sn²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Sn(s) [1]. Sn²⁺/Sn (−0.14 V) is more positive than water (−0.83 V) [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Tin (−0.14 V) is much easier to oxidize than water (+1.23 V) [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** **Oxygen** forms at the anode [1]. The Sn²⁺ concentration falls [1], and the solution becomes more acidic as H⁺ is produced [1]. **[3]**
**(d)** E⦵cell = −0.14 − (−0.45) = **+0.31 V** [1]. n = 2 [1]; ΔG⦵ = −2 × 96 500 × 0.31 = −59 830 J mol⁻¹ = **−59.8 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* For (c), the anode half-equation shows where the H⁺ comes from.

**12. (a)** ΔS⦵ = 70.0 − (130.7 + ½ × 205.2) = **−163.3 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** ΔG⦵ = −285.8 − 298 × (−0.1633) [1] = **−237.1 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** E⦵cell = 237 100 ÷ (2 × 96 500) [1] = **+1.23 V** [1]. From the data, E⦵cell = 1.23 − 0.00 = 1.23 V, so the two routes agree [1]. **[3]**
**(d)** ΔG = −285.8 − 353 × (−0.1633) = −228.2 kJ mol⁻¹ [1]; E = 228 200 ÷ (2 × 96 500) = **1.18 V** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Use ΔS⦵ in kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ in ΔH⦵ − TΔS⦵, then ΔG⦵ in J before dividing by nF.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Subtracting E⦵ values the wrong way round, or reversing a sign and then subtracting.
- Multiplying an E⦵ value by a balancing coefficient.
- Using the ion charge for n instead of the total electrons in the overall equation.
- Mixing J and kJ in ΔG⦵ = −nFE⦵cell.
- Writing a reactive metal such as potassium as a cathode product from aqueous solution.
- Ignoring concentration (NaCl) or electrode material (CuSO₄).
- Making the object the anode in electroplating.
