## What it is and what it contains

An **income statement** (sometimes called a profit and loss account) shows a business's revenue, its costs and the resulting profit **over a period**, usually a year. Unlike the statement of financial position, which is a snapshot on one date, it covers a stretch of time. It follows the accruals convention: revenue is included when earned and costs when incurred, not when cash moves.

| Line | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Revenue** | Sales income for the period |
| **Cost of sales** | Direct cost of the goods sold: opening inventory + purchases − closing inventory (and direct labour for a manufacturer) |
| **Gross profit** | Revenue − cost of sales |
| **Operating expenses** | Running costs: distribution, administration, marketing, depreciation |
| **Other operating income** | Income not from main sales (for example rent received) |
| **Operating profit** | Gross profit − operating expenses + other operating income |
| **Finance costs** | Interest paid on loans |
| **Profit before tax** | Operating profit − finance costs (and plus any interest received) |
| **Tax** | Corporation tax on the profit |
| **Profit for the year (net profit)** | Profit before tax − tax |
| **Dividends** | Part of profit paid to shareholders |
| **Retained profit** | Profit for the year − dividends; kept in the business |

The three measures of profit named in the specification are **gross profit**, **operating profit** and **net profit** (profit for the year).

### Worked example 1: completing an income statement

Brunel Cycles plc has the following information for the year ended 31 December.

- Revenue £1,250,000.
- Opening inventory £80,000; purchases £640,000; closing inventory £95,000.
- Distribution costs £95,000; administrative expenses £180,000 (these include depreciation).
- Rent received £10,000.
- Interest paid £24,000.
- Tax at 25% of profit before tax.
- Dividends £100,000.

**Step 1: cost of sales.** £80,000 + £640,000 − £95,000 = **£625,000**.

**Step 2: complete the statement.**

| | £ |
|---|---|
| Revenue | 1,250,000 |
| Cost of sales | (625,000) |
| **Gross profit** | **625,000** |
| Distribution costs | (95,000) |
| Administrative expenses | (180,000) |
| Other operating income | 10,000 |
| **Operating profit** | **360,000** |
| Finance costs | (24,000) |
| **Profit before tax** | **336,000** |
| Tax (25%) | (84,000) |
| **Profit for the year** | **252,000** |
| Dividends | (100,000) |
| **Retained profit** | **152,000** |

Working: 625,000 − 95,000 − 180,000 + 10,000 = 360,000. 360,000 − 24,000 = 336,000. 25% × 336,000 = 84,000. 336,000 − 84,000 = 252,000.

**Step 3: profit margins.**

- Gross profit margin = 625,000 ÷ 1,250,000 × 100 = **50.0%**.
- Operating profit margin = 360,000 ÷ 1,250,000 × 100 = **28.8%**.
- Net profit margin = 252,000 ÷ 1,250,000 × 100 = **20.2%**.

**Dividend payout** = 100,000 ÷ 252,000 × 100 = 39.7% of profit for the year; the remaining 60.3% is retained to fund growth or reduce borrowing.

Interpretation: for every £1 of revenue, 50p is left after the direct cost of goods; 28.8p after running costs; and 20.2p after interest and tax. The gap between gross and operating margin (21.2 percentage points) shows how much of revenue goes on running costs.

### Worked example 2: analysing the statement year on year

Last year Brunel's results were: revenue £1,100,000, gross profit £520,000, operating profit £280,000, profit for the year £190,000.

| Measure | Last year | This year | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | £1,100,000 | £1,250,000 | +13.6% |
| Gross profit | £520,000 | £625,000 | +20.2% |
| Gross margin | 47.3% | 50.0% | +2.7 points |
| Operating profit | £280,000 | £360,000 | +28.6% |
| Operating margin | 25.5% | 28.8% | +3.3 points |
| Profit for the year | £190,000 | £252,000 | +32.6% |
| Net margin | 17.3% | 20.2% | +2.9 points |

Net operating expenses (distribution + administration − other income) rose from £240,000 (520,000 − 280,000) to £265,000, an increase of 10.4%, which is less than the 13.6% rise in revenue.

**Analysis.** Profit has grown faster than revenue at every level, so the business has become more profitable. The gross margin improved because cost of sales rose by less than revenue (from £580,000 to £625,000, +7.8%). Possible reasons are a price rise, cheaper supplies or a change of product mix. The operating margin improved further because running costs rose more slowly than sales. Whether this is good depends on the cause: if the business cut marketing or maintenance to raise profit, future sales could suffer. Net profit grew fastest because interest and tax changed little in relation to operating profit.

**Evaluation.** Profits are strongly up, which benefits shareholders (more dividends, or a higher share price), lenders (interest is covered easily: operating profit is 15 times the interest bill, 360,000 ÷ 24,000) and employees (a secure business). But the statement does not show cash, and two years of data may not show a trend. Brunel should also compare with competitors and check whether the improvement can continue.

## Evaluating usefulness

| Stakeholder | How they use it |
|---|---|
| Owners and shareholders | Judge profit and dividends; decide whether to invest or sell |
| Lenders | Check that profit covers interest and repayments |
| Managers | Compare with budgets and previous years; set targets |
| Employees | Judge job security and the case for pay rises |
| Suppliers | Judge whether the business is sound enough to extend credit |
| Tax authority | Calculate tax due |
| Competitors and investors | Benchmark margins |

**Strengths.** Standardised format allows comparison between years and businesses; shows the stages of profit; allows margin analysis; required by law for companies.

**Limitations.**

- It is historic.
- It follows accruals, so profit is not cash. A profitable business can still run out of cash.
- It depends on judgement, for example about depreciation and provisions, so profit can be flattered.
- It shows nothing about assets, debts or the effect of future events.
- It ignores non-financial factors such as customer satisfaction or staff morale.
- A single number hides one-off items, for example a large profit from selling a building.

A balanced judgement is that the income statement is essential for judging profit but should be read with the statement of financial position, the cash-flow statement and ratios.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing gross profit with operating profit or net profit.
- Calculating cost of sales without opening and closing inventory.
- Taking tax from operating profit instead of from profit before tax.
- Treating dividends as an expense before profit for the year.
- Presenting a percentage change without saying whether it is good.

## Exam technique

Set out the statement in order with a clear label at each profit line. State the formula for each margin. For analysis, compare changes in percentages with each other (profit grew faster than revenue), give a likely cause and a judgement.
