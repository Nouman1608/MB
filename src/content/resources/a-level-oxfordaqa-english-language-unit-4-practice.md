Unit 4 of OxfordAQA International A-level English Language (9670) is either a 2 hour 30 minute exam
(4a), in which you devise a research question for data provided and write a report, or a non-exam
language investigation (4b). Part 1 below is a full 4a-style task with three invented data sets on a
common theme. Part 2 practises the decisions a 4b investigation needs. Both routes are marked out of 50.

## Part 1: Unit 4a-style data investigation (50 marks)

The theme of the data is **terms of address**. Using **at least two** of the data sets, devise a research
question, then plan and carry out an analysis. Write up your results as a report with the headings Aim(s),
Method, Analysis and Conclusion. **[50]**

**Data set A: emails to a teacher (opening and closing lines, five students, invented)**

| Student | Opening | Closing |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Dear Mr Haddad, | Kind regards, Sara |
| 2 | Hi sir | thanks |
| 3 | Good morning Mr Haddad, | Best wishes, Omar |
| 4 | Hey | thx!! |
| 5 | Hello Mr H, | Many thanks, Lina |

**Data set B: an online customer-service chat (extract, invented)**

> Agent: Hi Daniel, thanks for getting in touch! How can I help today?
> Customer: hi, my order hasn't arrived
> Agent: Sorry to hear that, Daniel. Let me check that for you right away.
> Customer: thanks mate
> Agent: No problem at all! I can see it's out for delivery today.

**Data set C: survey of 40 students, "How would you address a teacher by email?" (invented)**

| Form | Number of students |
|---|---|
| Title + surname (Mr/Ms X) | 22 |
| "Sir"/"Miss" | 9 |
| First name | 2 |
| No address term | 7 |

## Indicative content: Part 1

**Possible research questions (AO1: aims with precision)**
- "How do students vary terms of address and politeness in emails to a teacher, and how far does survey
  data on students' stated preferences match their actual usage?" (A + C)
- "How do address terms construct the relationship between writer and reader in a school email and in a
  commercial chat?" (A + B)

**Method**
- State which data sets are used and why; categorise address terms (title + surname, honorifics such as
  "sir", first name, zero address, informal greetings); compare openings and closings; compare stated
  preference (C) with use (A); note the small, invented samples.

**Analysis (AO2)**

| Level | Points from the data |
|---|---|
| Lexis | Honorifics ("sir", "Mr H") versus title + surname; vocative "mate" in B; clipped "thx" |
| Pragmatics | Politeness: formal openings show negative politeness and respect for status; "Hey" and "Hi sir" mix informality with deference; in B the agent's repeated first-name address builds rapport (synthetic personalisation) while the customer's "mate" claims equality |
| Graphology | Punctuation: "thx!!" and missing capitals in informal emails; the agent's exclamation marks to signal friendliness |
| Discourse | Openings and closings as conventional frames of email genre; the chat's adjacency pairs |
| Data handling | In C, 22 of 40 (55%) choose title + surname, and 7 of 40 (17.5%) no address term; in A, two of five emails (2 and 4) use no title + surname, and the closings range from formal ("Kind regards") to informal ("thx!!") |

**Conclusion**
- For example: formal address dominates students' stated preferences, but actual emails show more
  variation, especially in closings; commercial chats use first names strategically. Conclusions must be
  tentative: five emails and 40 survey responses cannot be generalised.

**Levels (illustrative)**
- Top: precise aim, justified selection, analysis across several language levels with data handling,
  concepts such as politeness theory and synthetic personalisation applied, cautious conclusion, report
  written with control (AO3).
- Middle: clear aim and method; accurate features, some concepts; conclusion linked to the aim.
- Lower: aim vague or missing; description of the data without analysis.

## Answers: Part 2

**1.** For example: "How do official and commercial signs in one shopping street use imperatives and
politeness markers?" [2]; function/use-based [1]. (An attitudes-based alternative: "How do residents feel
about English-only signs in a multilingual area?")

**2.** [4] For example: photograph a defined sample (all signs in one street, or 30 signs of each type),
record location and type, transcribe the wording into a table; issue: photographing private property or
people requires care, and sample size must be manageable within the word limit (section 3.4.2 says about 2,500 words excluding data; the assessment overview says 2,500 to 3,000 words).

**3.** Aim(s); Method; Analysis; Conclusion; References; Appendix (data) [3 for six, 2 for four or five,
1 for two or three].

**4.** "Proves" overclaims from a limited sample, and "no longer" implies change over time that the data
has not measured [2]. Rewrite [1]: "In my sample of 20 messages from five 17-year-olds, informal forms were
more frequent than formal ones, which suggests, though it cannot prove, that these speakers choose informal
language in this context."

## Frequently asked questions

**Must I use all three data sets in Unit 4a?**
No. You use some or all of the data, but at least two of the texts or data sets. No task involves more than
three.

**Is a research question better than an aim?**
The suggested report structure starts with "Aim(s)". A clear research question can be stated as your aim.

**How much numerical analysis is expected?**
Data-handling skills are part of what the unit tests, so simple counts, percentages and comparisons are
useful where the data allows, always interpreted linguistically.
