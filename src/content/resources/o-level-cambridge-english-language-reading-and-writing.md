This guide covers the full content structure of Cambridge O-Level
English Language (1123), for examination 2024-2026 -- the simplest
English Language syllabus in the Cambridge suite, organised into just
two skill strands with no further named sub-sections.

## Where this fits in 1123

Unlike Cambridge's IGCSE and AS & A Level English Language syllabuses,
which break content into multiple papers and named sections, this
O-Level is built around a single, clean division: Reading (Paper 1,
50%) and Writing (Paper 2, 50%). There is no separate literature,
listening or speaking component.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE O-LEVEL ENGLISH LANGUAGE (1123) — READING AND WRITING**

- Topic 1: Reading (Paper 1, 50%) — reading a range of texts and
responding to questions testing comprehension, understanding of
writers' choices and language, and summary skills
- Topic 2: Writing (Paper 2, 50%) — producing pieces of directed and
continuous writing for different purposes and audiences

## How to approach it

Because the syllabus has only two strands, revision can be structured
simply: alternate reading-comprehension practice (working with unseen
passages under timed conditions) with writing practice (drafting full
responses to directed and continuous writing tasks), rather than
splitting revision time across many small topics. Summary writing
within the Reading paper rewards concise, own-words responses, so
practise this as a distinct skill from general comprehension. For the
Writing paper, being clear about purpose and audience before drafting
-- and checking the finished piece against both -- tends to lift marks
more reliably than focusing on vocabulary alone.

## Reading: comprehension and summary

The reading paper tests understanding of an unseen passage at three depths — locating explicit detail, inferring what is implied, and explaining the effect of the writer's language.

A reliable approach: read the questions first, read the passage once for overall sense, then locate the relevant section and read *around* it. Answers to inference questions are rarely in a single sentence.

Where a question says **"in your own words"**, rewording is what earns the mark; lifting the passage's phrasing scores nothing. Match the length of the answer to the marks available — a one-mark question wants a phrase, not a paragraph.

**Summary** is marked on two things: selecting every relevant point, and expressing them concisely in your own words. Exclude examples, repetition and your own opinion, and respect the word limit. A useful method is to list the points first, then join them into continuous prose.

## Reading: language analysis

For "explain the effect" questions, the mark is for the **effect**, not the label.

```
Weak     "The writer uses a metaphor to make it more interesting."
Strong   "The metaphor 'the crowd was a river' suggests the people
          moved as one uncontrollable mass, emphasising the narrator's
          helplessness within it."
```

Quote briefly, explain what the image suggests, then link it to the reader's impression.

## Writing: directed and composition

**Directed writing** provides a form, audience and purpose — a letter, speech, report or article. All three must be honoured: form governs conventions, audience governs register, purpose governs structure.

**Composition** offers narrative, descriptive and argumentative options. Choose on the basis of what you can sustain, not what sounds impressive.

- **Narrative** — a simple plot told well beats an elaborate one told badly. Decide the ending before you start.
- **Descriptive** — build atmosphere through the senses and precise detail; resist the urge to introduce a plot.
- **Argumentative** — a clear line of argument, two or three developed points, and genuine acknowledgement of the counter-argument.

Plan for five minutes. Unplanned writing drifts, and organisation marks go with it.

## Accuracy

Marks divide between content and organisation on one side, and accuracy and range on the other. Recurring errors worth drilling: subject–verb agreement, consistent tense, sentence separation (avoiding comma splices), apostrophes for possession against plurals, and spelling of common words.

**Range** means varied sentence lengths and structures and precise vocabulary — but a simple word used correctly beats an ambitious word used wrongly.

## Worked example

Summarising: *"The scheme reduced congestion by 20%. It also cut air pollution. However, small shopkeepers reported a fall in trade, and some elderly residents found the new routes confusing."*

```
Points to select:
  1. congestion fell by a fifth
  2. air quality improved
  3. small businesses lost trade
  4. older residents struggled with the new routes

In own words, continuous prose:
"Traffic levels dropped by a fifth and the air became cleaner, though
small retailers saw takings decline and older people found the altered
routes difficult to follow."

44 words, all four points, no lifted phrasing.
```

## Common mistakes

Copying from the passage where own words are required. Writing at length for low-mark questions. Including examples or opinion in a summary. Naming a device without explaining its effect. Ignoring one of form, audience or purpose in directed writing. Starting a composition with no ending in mind. Leaving no time to check.

## Quick revision checklist

- Distinguish detail, inference and effect questions and answer each appropriately.
- Answer in your own words and match answer length to the marks.
- Select points for a summary, exclude examples, and stay within the word limit.
- Explain the effect of language rather than labelling devices.
- Match form, audience and purpose in directed writing.
- Plan compositions before writing and reserve time to check accuracy.
