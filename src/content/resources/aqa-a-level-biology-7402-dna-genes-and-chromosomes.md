It is AS content, examined on both AS papers and on A-level Papers 1 and 3. ## What the specification asks for

| Statement | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| Prokaryotic DNA is short, circular and not associated with proteins | Compare with eukaryotic nuclear DNA |
| Eukaryotic nuclear DNA is very long, linear and associated with histones; DNA plus its proteins forms a chromosome | Define chromosome in these terms |
| Mitochondrial and chloroplast DNA is short, circular and not associated with protein | Recognise the similarity with prokaryotes |
| A gene is a base sequence of DNA coding for the amino acid sequence of a polypeptide, or a functional RNA (including rRNA and tRNAs) | Give the full definition |
| A gene occupies a fixed position, a locus, on a particular DNA molecule | Use the term locus correctly |
| A triplet of DNA bases codes for a specific amino acid | Apply to base and amino acid counts |
| The genetic code is universal, non-overlapping and degenerate | Define each property and explain its consequences |
| Much eukaryotic nuclear DNA does not code for polypeptides: non-coding multiple repeats between genes, and introns between exons | Explain why a gene is longer than its coding sequence |

## Packaging: why histones matter

A single human chromosome contains one DNA molecule many centimetres long when stretched out, yet the nucleus is only a few micrometres across. Wrapping the DNA around histone proteins, and then coiling the DNA–histone complex further, packs it into the nucleus. Because the specification defines a chromosome as "a DNA molecule and its associated proteins", a chromosome is not just DNA. During cell division the chromosomes condense further and become visible with a light microscope.

Prokaryotic DNA is much shorter and, in the specification's wording, is not associated with proteins, so it does not form chromosomes in this sense. The same applies to the DNA in mitochondria and chloroplasts, which also have their own smaller ribosomes. The specification asks you to recognise this similarity; you do not need to explain its evolutionary origin.

## Genes: two kinds of product

Students often learn "a gene codes for a protein". The specification is more careful: a gene codes for **either** the amino acid sequence of a polypeptide **or** a functional RNA. Ribosomal RNA (part of ribosomes) and transfer RNA are functional RNAs that are never translated. So:

- A gene for an enzyme's polypeptide → mRNA → polypeptide.
- A gene for tRNA → tRNA, which is used directly in translation.

Also note "polypeptide", not "protein". A protein such as haemoglobin is made of several polypeptides, which may be coded for by different genes.

## Loci and alleles

A **locus** is the fixed position a gene occupies on a particular DNA molecule. Homologous chromosomes carry the same genes at the same loci. Different versions of a gene at a locus are alleles, which you will use in 3.4.3 and 3.7.1. In an exam answer, "position of a gene on a chromosome" is acceptable for locus; "a gene" is not.

## Why a triplet?

There are four DNA bases and 20 amino acids commonly found in proteins.

```
Code of 1 base:   4¹ = 4 combinations    — too few for 20 amino acids
Code of 2 bases:  4² = 16 combinations   — still too few
Code of 3 bases:  4³ = 64 combinations   — enough, with spare
```

So three bases is the smallest group that can code for 20 amino acids. The 64 triplets include stop signals, and the spare triplets mean most amino acids have more than one triplet. This is **degeneracy**.

## The three properties, and what follows from each

| Property | Meaning | Consequence you can use in answers |
|---|---|---|
| Universal | The same triplet codes for the same amino acid in all organisms | A gene from one organism can be expressed in another and give the same polypeptide |
| Non-overlapping | Each base is read as part of only one triplet | A change to one base affects at most one triplet, so at most one amino acid |
| Degenerate | Most amino acids have more than one triplet | A change to one base may produce a triplet coding for the same amino acid, so the polypeptide is unchanged |

The consequences of degeneracy and non-overlapping link forward to gene mutations in 3.4.3 and 3.8.1; the full treatment is in [mutation and meiosis](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-mutation-and-meiosis/).

## Worked example 1: testing for overlap

A sequence of 12 bases is read. How many amino acids would it code for if the code were non-overlapping, and if it were overlapping (starting a new triplet at every base)?

```
Non-overlapping: 12 ÷ 3            = 4 amino acids
Overlapping:     12 − 3 + 1        = 10 amino acids
```

In an overlapping code, each base (except near the ends) would be part of three triplets, so one base substitution could change three adjacent amino acids. Experimental data show single substitutions usually change one amino acid at most, which supports a non-overlapping code. Questions on "interpreting experimental data" use this logic.

## Exons, introns and non-coding repeats

In eukaryotes, much nuclear DNA does not code for polypeptides:

- **Between genes:** non-coding multiple repeats, where a short base sequence is repeated many times.
- **Within genes:** exons are the base sequences that code for amino acid sequences; introns are non-coding sequences that separate the exons. A gene can contain one or more introns.

Prokaryotic genes do not contain introns. In 3.4.2 you will see that introns are transcribed into pre-mRNA and then removed by splicing.

## Worked example 2: gene length and polypeptide length

A polypeptide has 150 amino acids (invented example). In a prokaryote, the coding sequence including a stop triplet is 150 × 3 + 3 = 453 bases. The equivalent eukaryotic gene is 1,860 bases long. If all the extra length is introns, how many bases of intron does it contain, and what percentage of the gene is exon?

```
Intron bases   = 1860 − 453           = 1407
Exon %         = 453 ÷ 1860 × 100     = 24.4%
```

The calculation shows why "the gene contains three times as many bases as the polypeptide has amino acids" is only a minimum: stop triplets, introns and other non-coding sequences all add length.

## Common mistakes

- Saying a gene "codes for a protein" with no mention of polypeptide or functional RNA.
- Defining a chromosome as "a strand of DNA" with no proteins.
- Describing prokaryotic DNA as "in the nucleus" or "linear".
- Forgetting that mitochondrial and chloroplast DNA is also circular and not protein-bound.
- Confusing degenerate (more than one triplet per amino acid) with non-overlapping.
- Saying introns are "removed from DNA"; they stay in the DNA and are removed from pre-mRNA.
- Writing "codon" for a DNA triplet: codons are on mRNA.
