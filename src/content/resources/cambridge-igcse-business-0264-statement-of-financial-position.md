This guide goes deeper on section 5.4 (Statement of financial position) of the Cambridge IGCSE Business 0264 syllabus for examination in 2027, 2028 and 2029. Its content statement, 5.4.1 The main elements of a statement of financial position, lists: non-current assets (e.g. property and machinery); current assets (e.g. inventory, trade receivables, cash); non-current liabilities (e.g. bank loans); current liabilities (e.g. trade payables, overdraft); the concept of capital employed; simple calculations of total assets, total liabilities and working capital; and making decisions based on simple statements of financial position. The syllabus notes that candidates will not be assessed on constructing these statements. The broader [profit and loss and statement of financial position guide](/resources/igcse-business-0264-profit-and-loss-and-statement-of-financial-position/) introduces each element. This page practises classifying items, shows how a financing choice changes the statement, and uses working capital to judge a decision. Revise with the [statement of financial position revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-business-0264-statement-of-financial-position-revision-notes/) and practise with the [statement of financial position practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-business-0264-statement-of-financial-position-practice/).

All businesses and figures below are invented. Money is in dollars.

## What the syllabus asks for

| Content | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| The four main elements | Classify items as non-current or current assets, and non-current or current liabilities |
| Capital employed | Explain the concept and find it from the statement |
| Calculations | Total assets, total liabilities, working capital |
| Decisions | Use the statement to judge whether a business can afford a plan, or how to finance it |

## What the statement shows

A statement of financial position shows what a business **owns** (assets) and what it **owes** (liabilities) at one moment, such as the last day of the financial year. A statement of profit or loss covers a *period*. This difference matters: the statement of financial position is a snapshot, so it can change the day after it is drawn up.

The dividing line between "current" and "non-current" is one year.

| Element | Meaning | Syllabus examples |
|---|---|---|
| Non-current assets | Items owned and expected to be used for more than a year | Property (land and buildings), machinery |
| Current assets | Cash, or items expected to turn into cash within a year | Inventory, trade receivables, cash |
| Non-current liabilities | Amounts owed that are due after more than a year | Bank loans |
| Current liabilities | Amounts owed that are due within a year | Trade payables, overdraft |

Two terms often confuse students:

- **Trade receivables** are amounts customers owe the business for goods sold on credit. They are an asset, because the business will receive the money.
- **Trade payables** are amounts the business owes its suppliers for goods bought on credit. They are a liability, because the business must pay.

## Worked example 1: classifying and calculating

Orrell Engineering lists these items on 31 December.

| Item | Amount | Classification |
|---|---|---|
| Land and buildings | 180,000 | Non-current asset |
| Machinery | 65,000 | Non-current asset |
| Delivery vehicles | 24,000 | Non-current asset |
| Inventory | 21,000 | Current asset |
| Trade receivables | 14,000 | Current asset |
| Cash | 5,000 | Current asset |
| Trade payables | 17,000 | Current liability |
| Overdraft | 9,000 | Current liability |
| Bank loan repayable in 10 years | 70,000 | Non-current liability |

```
Non-current assets  = 180,000 + 65,000 + 24,000 = 269,000
Current assets      = 21,000 + 14,000 + 5,000   =  40,000
Total assets        = 269,000 + 40,000          = 309,000

Current liabilities     = 17,000 + 9,000  = 26,000
Non-current liabilities                   = 70,000
Total liabilities       = 26,000 + 70,000 = 96,000

Working capital  = current assets - current liabilities = 40,000 - 26,000 = 14,000
Capital employed = total assets - current liabilities   = 309,000 - 26,000 = 283,000
```

The working capital formula is given in the syllabus. The syllabus names the concept of capital employed without printing a formula; total assets minus current liabilities is the standard form. Capital employed is the long-term money used to run the business: it is the value of everything the business owns, less what it must pay back within a year.

## Working capital: what the number tells you

Working capital is the money available for day-to-day running: paying suppliers, wages and bills.

| Working capital | What it suggests |
|---|---|
| Positive and comfortable | The business can pay its short-term debts as they fall due |
| Small or negative | It may struggle to pay suppliers and bills; it may need an overdraft or have to delay payments |
| Very large | Money may be tied up unnecessarily, such as in too much inventory or customers who are slow to pay |

Orrell's working capital is positive ($14,000), but look at what it is made of. Only $5,000 is cash. Most current assets are inventory ($21,000) and money owed by customers ($14,000), which take time to turn into cash. A positive figure does not guarantee that bills can be paid this week.

## Worked example 2: how a financing choice changes the statement

Orrell wants a new machine costing $30,000. It has two ways to pay.

**Option A: use the overdraft.** The machine is added to non-current assets, and the overdraft (a current liability) rises by $30,000.

```
Current liabilities = 26,000 + 30,000 = 56,000
Working capital     = 40,000 - 56,000 = -16,000
```

**Option B: take a five-year bank loan.** The machine is added to non-current assets, and non-current liabilities rise by $30,000. Current assets and current liabilities do not change.

```
Working capital = 40,000 - 26,000 = 14,000 (unchanged)
```

**Decision.** Option B is better. The machine will be used for years, so it should be paid for with long-term finance. Using the overdraft would turn working capital negative, leaving Orrell unable to cover its short-term debts from its current assets. The cost of Option B is the interest on the loan, and it adds to the debt Orrell must repay over the next five years.

## Worked example 3: reading two years

Tilbury Toys' current assets and liabilities at the end of two years:

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Inventory | 30,000 | 52,000 |
| Trade receivables | 18,000 | 20,000 |
| Cash | 12,000 | 2,000 |
| Trade payables | 25,000 | 31,000 |
| Overdraft | 0 | 14,000 |

```
Year 1 working capital = (30,000 + 18,000 + 12,000) - 25,000 = 60,000 - 25,000 = 35,000
Year 2 working capital = (52,000 + 20,000 + 2,000) - (31,000 + 14,000) = 74,000 - 45,000 = 29,000
```

Working capital has fallen by $6,000, but the bigger warning is the change inside it. Inventory has risen by $22,000 while cash has fallen by $10,000 and an overdraft of $14,000 has appeared. Tilbury seems to have bought far more stock than it is selling. A sensible decision would be to reduce new orders from suppliers and run special offers to sell surplus toys, turning inventory back into cash.

## Exam technique

The command words in the 0264 syllabus include **calculate**, **define**, **identify**, **state**, **outline**, **explain**, **consider** and **justify**.

- **Identify:** classify an item using the four headings exactly.
- **Calculate:** write the formula in words before the numbers. Check that current liabilities include *both* trade payables and any overdraft.
- **Explain:** link a figure to what it means for the business: "cash has fallen to $2,000, so it may not be able to pay wages without using the overdraft".
- **Consider / justify:** compare options using their effect on the statement (especially working capital), then add a factor the statement does not show, such as interest cost or whether sales are growing.

## Common errors

- Putting trade receivables under liabilities or trade payables under assets.
- Treating an overdraft as a non-current liability. It is repayable on demand, so it is current.
- Forgetting the overdraft when adding up current liabilities.
- Saying a business with positive working capital can definitely pay its bills, without checking how much is cash.
- Confusing the statement of financial position (a moment in time) with the statement of profit or loss (a period).
