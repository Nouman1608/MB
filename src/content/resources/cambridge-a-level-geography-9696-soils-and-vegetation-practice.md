## Section B

**7.** The table shows soil data from the fictional Wadi Harun irrigation scheme, where canal irrigation began in 2000 without field drains.

| Year | Depth to water table (m) | Salt content of topsoil (dS/m) | Wheat yield (tonnes/ha) |
|---|---|---|---|

## Answers

**1.** The rate at which plants store energy as new biomass [1] after allowing for their own respiration [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Plants grow slowly, so damage takes decades to recover [1]. Biological soil crusts and thin topsoil are easily destroyed by trampling or vehicles and then eroded [1]. **[2]**

**3.** They have very deep tap roots [1] that reach groundwater, so they can take up water even in long dry periods [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Stomata open at night, when it is cooler and more humid [1], to take in carbon dioxide, which is stored as an acid [1]. During the day stomata stay closed while the stored carbon is used for photosynthesis, so little water is lost [1]. **[3]**

**5.** They tolerate high salt concentrations in their tissues [1], which keeps their cell solute concentration high enough for water to enter roots by osmosis [1]. Some excrete excess salt through glands on their leaves [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Decomposers such as bacteria and fungi need moisture [1], which is absent most of the time [1]. Little litter is produced and wind removes much of it [1], so nutrients are released only in short bursts after rain [1]. **[4]**

**7. (a)** (3.0 - 1.1) / 3.0 x 100 [1] = 63% fall [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** As the water table rises closer to the surface, topsoil salt content increases [1], from 1.5 dS/m with the water table at 8.0 m to 9.6 dS/m at 1.2 m [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** Irrigation added more water than crops used, and without drains it percolated down [1], raising the water table from 8.0 m to 1.2 m [1]. Near the surface, water rises by capillary action [1] and evaporates in the hot, dry climate, leaving salts behind [1]. Salty soil water causes physiological drought, so wheat yields fell [1]. **[5]**

**8.** Install field drains to lower the water table [1]; limitation: drains are costly and the salty drainage water must be disposed of [1]. Switch to drip irrigation so only the water crops need is applied [1]; limitation: high equipment cost for small farmers [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Pale, waxy or hairy surfaces reflect radiation, reducing heating [1]. Leaves orientated vertically or edge-on to the sun receive less midday radiation [1]. Small leaves lose heat quickly to the surrounding air [1]. Spines and hairs shade the stem surface [1]. Stomata open at night and growth occurs mainly in cooler periods [1]. **[5]**

**10.** Too many animals graze an area, often around boreholes or settlements [1]. Plants are eaten faster than they regrow, so cover is lost [1]. Trampling compacts the soil and breaks surface crusts and roots [1]. Bare soil is exposed to wind erosion and to crusting under raindrops, which lowers infiltration [1]. Topsoil and nutrients are lost, so the land becomes less productive and degraded [1]. **[5]**

**11.** Loss of vegetation exposes bare soil [1]. Raindrop impact forms a surface crust, reducing infiltration [1]. More rain runs off and less is stored, so soil moisture falls [1]. Lower moisture supports fewer plants, so cover falls further [1]. Erosion removes fertile topsoil, so even if rain returns plants struggle to establish [1]. Bare ground has a higher albedo, which some research suggests may reduce local rainfall, adding a climatic feedback [1]. **[6]**

**12.** The real exam marks this essay with levels of response, not point by point. The points below are indicative creditworthy content.

*Introduction:* define desertification as land degradation in drylands [1]; identify natural factors (drought, variability) and human factors (land use) [1].

*Human activity dominant:* overgrazing around boreholes removes cover [1]. Overcultivation and shorter fallows exhaust soils [1]. Fuelwood cutting removes trees and shrubs [1]. Poor irrigation causes salinisation [1]. Population growth and insecure tenure intensify these pressures [1].

*Natural factors dominant:* droughts reduce cover regardless of land use [1]. Rainfall variability is naturally high in semi-arid areas [1]. Regreening when rain returned in parts of the Sahel suggests earlier degradation was partly climatic [1]. Climate change may shift rainfall belts [1].

*Interaction:* drought starts degradation; land use decides whether it recovers [1]. Herd sizes kept high during drought cause lasting damage [1]. Feedbacks such as crusting and albedo link human and natural factors [1].

*Evidence and scale:* human causes dominate locally around settlements and wells [1]; natural factors dominate at regional scale [1]. Successful farmer-led restoration shows human action can reverse it [1].

*Judgement:* neither factor alone explains desertification [1]; human activity converts natural stress into lasting degradation [1]; a reasoned final view on "how far" with named examples [1]. **[20]**
