## 2.1.1 States of matter

| State | Shape | Volume | Can be compressed? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Solid | Fixed | Fixed | Hardly at all |
| Liquid | Takes the shape of the container | Fixed | Hardly at all |
| Gas | Fills the container | Fills the container | Easily |

Changes of state: solid to liquid is melting, liquid to solid is solidification, liquid to gas is boiling (or evaporation), and gas to liquid is condensation. Direct gas-to-solid and solid-to-gas changes are not required.

## 2.1.2 The particle model

### Structure and motion

- **Solid:** particles are packed closely in a regular pattern. Strong forces hold each particle in place, so it only vibrates about a fixed position.
- **Liquid:** particles are close together but in no fixed pattern. They slide past one another. The forces are weaker than in a solid.
- **Gas:** particles are far apart and move quickly in random directions. The forces between them are negligible except during collisions.

### Temperature and absolute zero

A higher temperature means a higher average kinetic energy of the particles, so they move faster. There is a lowest possible temperature, absolute zero, which is −273 °C. At absolute zero the particles have the least kinetic energy they can have.

### Gas pressure

Gas particles collide with the walls of a container. A rebounding particle changes direction, so the wall pushes on it, and by the third law of motion the particle pushes back on the wall with an equal force. Huge numbers of collisions each second give a steady force on each unit of area of the wall. That force per unit area is the pressure.

### The three relationships (qualitative)

1. **Pressure and temperature at constant volume.** Heat the gas and the particles move faster. Collisions with the walls become more frequent and each one is more forceful, so the pressure rises.
2. **Volume and temperature at constant pressure.** Faster particles would hit the walls harder and more often, so for the pressure to stay the same, the volume must increase and the walls are hit less often.
3. **Pressure and volume at constant temperature.** The particles keep the same average speed. A smaller volume means they travel a shorter distance between wall collisions, so they hit the walls more often and the pressure rises.

### The equation p1V1 = p2V2

If the mass of gas and its temperature are both fixed, the product pressure × volume does not change.

**Worked example.** A syringe holds 80 cm³ of air at 105 kPa. The plunger is pulled out slowly until the volume is 140 cm³, and the temperature does not change. Find the new pressure.

```
p1V1 = p2V2
105 × 80 = p2 × 140
p2 = 8400 / 140 = 60 kPa
```

The pressure fell because the volume rose.

**Graph.** At constant temperature, a plot of pressure against volume is a curve that falls steeply and then flattens, never touching either axis. For example, with pV = 1200 (kPa cm³):

| V (cm³) | 20 | 40 | 60 | 120 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| p (kPa) | 60 | 30 | 20 | 10 |

Doubling the volume halves the pressure. Drawing p against 1/V gives a straight line passing through the origin.

## 2.2.1 Thermal expansion

### Particle explanation

When a solid is heated, its particles vibrate with larger amplitude. They push slightly further apart on average, so the solid expands. The particles themselves do not get bigger. In a liquid and in a gas, faster-moving particles also move further apart on average. Gases expand far more than liquids, and liquids expand more than solids, for the same temperature rise. This is the relative order of magnitudes you are asked to state.

### Applications and consequences

- **Liquid-in-glass thermometer:** the liquid in the bulb expands when warmed and rises up a narrow tube. 
- **Railway tracks and bridges:** gaps or rollers let the metal expand on hot days without buckling.
- **Overhead power cables:** they are left slightly slack so that they do not snap when they contract in cold weather.

### Kelvin and Celsius

T (in K) = θ (in °C) + 273

**Worked example.** Convert body temperature of 37 °C to kelvin, and 350 K to degrees Celsius.

```
37 + 273 = 310 K
350 - 273 = 77 °C
```

A change of 1 K equals a change of 1 °C. Absolute zero is 0 K.

## 2.2.2 Specific heat capacity

### Internal energy and temperature

Raising the temperature of an object raises its internal energy. In particle terms, the average kinetic energy of all the particles in the object increases.

### Definition and equation

**Specific heat capacity** is the energy required per unit mass per unit temperature increase.

c = ΔE / (mΔθ), which rearranges to ΔE = mcΔθ.

Use kilograms and joules, so c is in J/(kg °C).

**Worked example 1.** A tea maker has 250 g of water at 80 °C. It cools to 36 °C. Take c for water as 4200 J/(kg °C). How much energy does the water lose?

```
m = 250 g = 0.25 kg
Δθ = 80 - 36 = 44 °C
ΔE = mcΔθ = 0.25 × 4200 × 44 = 46 200 J
```

### Experiment: specific heat capacity of a liquid

1. Weigh an insulated beaker, add a known mass of liquid and record the mass.
2. Place an electric immersion heater and a thermometer in the liquid. Stir.
3. Record the starting temperature, switch on the heater, and note the power (or the current and voltage) and the time.
4. Switch off, keep stirring, and record the highest temperature reached.
5. Energy supplied = power × time. Then c = ΔE / (mΔθ).

**Worked example 2.** A 60 W heater warms 0.50 kg of oil for 5.0 minutes. The temperature rises by 18 °C.

```
E = 60 × 300 = 18 000 J
c = 18 000 / (0.50 × 18) = 2000 J/(kg °C)
```

### Experiment: specific heat capacity of a solid

Use a metal block with two holes. Put the heater in one hole and the thermometer in the other, with a drop of oil in the thermometer hole for good contact. Wrap the block in insulation. Measure mass, power, time and temperature rise as before.

**Worked example 3.** A 100 W heater is on for 3.0 minutes in a 0.80 kg steel block. The temperature rises by 45 °C.

```
E = 100 × 180 = 18 000 J
c = 18 000 / (0.80 × 45) = 500 J/(kg °C)
```

### Why the measured value is usually too high

Some energy leaks to the surroundings, so the temperature rise is smaller than expected. That makes c too large. Insulation reduces the error.

## 2.2.3 Melting, boiling and evaporation

### Energy without a change in temperature

At melting and boiling, energy goes in, but the temperature stays constant until the change is complete. At solidification and condensation, energy goes out, again at constant temperature.

Under standard atmospheric pressure, water melts at 0 °C and its boiling temperature is 100 °C.

### Latent heat

**Latent heat** is the energy that must be supplied or removed for a substance to change state. The energy does not raise the temperature. It increases the potential energy of the particles by pulling them apart against the forces between them. On melting, the particles break out of their fixed positions. On boiling, they separate completely, which needs much more energy.

### Boiling and evaporation

| | Boiling | Evaporation |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | Only at the boiling temperature | At any temperature |
| Where | Throughout the liquid, with bubbles forming | Only at the surface |
| Speed | Rapid | Slow |

### How evaporation works and what affects it

In a liquid, particles have a range of speeds. The more energetic particles near the surface can escape if they are moving fast enough to overcome the attractions of the others. These escaped particles leave the average kinetic energy of the remaining liquid lower, so the liquid cools.

Evaporation is faster when:

- the temperature is higher, since more particles have enough energy to escape
- the surface area is larger, since more particles are near the surface
- air moves over the surface, since it carries away escaped particles that might return.

## 2.3 Transfer of thermal energy

### 2.3.1 Conduction

In a solid, vibrating atoms pass energy to their neighbours through the lattice. This happens in all solids. In metals there are also free (delocalised) electrons. They gain energy, move through the metal, and pass it on in collisions. So metals conduct much better than non-metals.

**Experiment to compare conductors.** Stand rods of different materials, with the same size, in hot water. Stick a pin to the free end of each with a blob of wax. The pin drops off first from the best conductor.

### 2.3.2 Convection

Convection happens in liquids and gases. A heated region of fluid expands, so its density decreases. It rises, and cooler, denser fluid sinks to take its place. A circulating convection current forms.

**Experiment.** Place one small crystal of potassium permanganate at the bottom of a beaker of water, near one edge, and heat the glass directly below it. The purple streak rises over the flame and sinks on the other side. In air, smoke shows the same circulation above a warm object.

### 2.3.3 Radiation

Infrared radiation transfers thermal energy as electromagnetic waves. It does not need a medium, which is how energy reaches Earth across space.

| Surface | Emits | Absorbs | Reflects |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dull black | Best | Best | Least |
| Shiny white or silver | Worst | Worst | Best |

Dull surfaces emit and absorb better than shiny ones. A hotter object, and one with a larger surface area, emits radiation at a greater rate.

**Experiments.**

- **Emitters:** fill a Leslie cube (or identical cans with different surfaces) with hot water. Place a radiation detector or thermometer the same distance from each face. The dull black face gives the largest reading.
- **Absorbers:** place identical dull black and shiny metal plates the same distance from a heater. Attach a small piece of wax or a thermometer to the back of each. The wax on the black plate melts first, or its temperature rises faster.

### 2.3.4 Everyday applications

- **Kitchen pans:** a metal base conducts energy from the hob. The plastic handle is an insulator. Convection spreads energy through the liquid.
- **Heating a room:** a radiator warms the air around it. The air expands, becomes less dense and rises, while cooler air sinks and moves in. A convection current warms the whole room.
- **Infrared thermometer:** it detects the infrared radiation emitted by an object and shows its temperature without touching it.
- **Insulation:** air trapped in small pockets is a poor conductor and cannot circulate. Loft insulation, double glazing and cavity walls use this to cut conduction and convection. Shiny foil reflects radiation. A lid on a hot drink reduces convection and evaporation.

## Common errors

- Saying particles "expand" when heated. They vibrate or move faster. The material expands.
- Using grams for mass in ΔE = mcΔθ.
- Adding 273 to a temperature difference. A rise of 20 °C is a rise of 20 K.
- Saying that "cold rises" or "heat rises". Warm fluid rises because it is less dense.
- Expecting the temperature to climb while a substance is melting or boiling, when it holds steady until the change is complete.
