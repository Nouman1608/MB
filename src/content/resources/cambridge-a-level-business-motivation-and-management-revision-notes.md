## 2.2.1 and 2.2.2 Why motivate; human needs

| Idea | Recall |
|---|---|
| Motivation | The desire to put effort into work |
| Why it matters | Staff deliver the objectives; motivated staff raise productivity and quality, and cut absence and labour turnover |
| Human need | Something a person must have, or strongly wants, for security or fulfilment |
| Met at work | Pay (basic needs), secure contract (safety), team (belonging), promotion (status), challenge (achievement) |
| Not met at work | Insecure contract, isolated or repetitive work, no say, poor conditions |

## 2.2.3 Theories at a glance

| Theorist | Type | Core claim | Practical message |
|---|---|---|---|
| Taylor | Content | Money is the main motivator; break jobs into timed tasks | Pay by output; supervise closely |
| Mayo | Content | Attention, belonging and group norms lift output (Hawthorne studies) | Consult, build teams |
| Maslow | Content | Five needs in order: physiological, safety, social, esteem, self-actualisation | Find which level is unmet |
| Herzberg | Content | Hygiene factors prevent dissatisfaction; motivators create satisfaction | Fix hygiene, then enrich |
| McClelland | Content | Needs for achievement, affiliation, power differ by person | Match tasks to the person |
| Vroom | Process | Effort depends on expectancy, instrumentality and valence | Make the effort-reward link clear and the reward wanted |

### Herzberg sorting

| Hygiene factors | Motivators |
|---|---|
| Pay, working conditions, supervision, company policy, job security | Achievement, recognition, responsibility, the work itself, advancement |

### Vroom in steps

1. Score expectancy (effort leads to performance), between 0 and 1.
2. Score instrumentality (performance leads to reward), between 0 and 1.
3. Score valence (how much the reward is wanted).
4. Multiply: motivational force = expectancy × instrumentality × valence (standard form).
5. If any score is zero, the product is zero.

Reminder: expectancy 0.5, instrumentality 0.8, valence 9 gives 0.5 × 0.8 × 9 = 3.6. Raise only the valence of an unreachable reward and the force does not rise much.

## 2.2.4 Motivation methods

### Financial

| Method | One-line definition | One strength | One weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Time based | Hours worked × hourly rate | Simple, predictable | No link to output |
| Salary | Fixed annual pay | Secure | No extra reward for effort |
| Piece rate | Pay per unit | Rewards output | Quality, rushing |
| Commission | Percentage of sales | Drives selling | Pressure selling, uncertain income |
| Bonus | Lump sum for a target | Focuses effort | Fades; target gaming |
| Profit sharing | Share of profit | Loyalty, team feeling | Weak individual link |
| PRP | Pay tied to assessed performance | Rewards results | Appraisal bias |
| Fringe benefits | Non-cash extras | Retains staff | Cost; uneven value |

Quick calculations (standard forms):

- Time-based pay = hours × rate. Example: 40 × £12.50 = £500.
- Piece-rate pay = units × rate per unit. Example: 620 × £0.75 = £465.
- Commission = rate × qualifying sales. Example: 5% of £8,000 = £400.
- Profit share per person = pool ÷ number of staff. Example: 10% of £60,000 = £6,000, shared by 12 staff = £500 each.

### Non-financial

| Method | What it is | Theory link |
|---|---|---|
| Training | Gives skills and confidence | Maslow esteem, Herzberg advancement |
| Promotion opportunities | A career path | Esteem, McClelland achievement |
| Development | Longer-term growth beyond the current job | Self-actualisation |
| Status | Title, office, standing | Esteem |
| Job re-design | Changing tasks to reduce boredom | Herzberg (the work itself) |
| Team working | Shared tasks and goals | Mayo, McClelland affiliation |
| Empowerment | Authority to decide own work | Responsibility |
| Participation | Involvement in decisions | Mayo, esteem |
| Job enrichment | More planning, responsibility, decisions | Herzberg motivators |

### Employee participation in management and control

- Works council or consultation committee.
- Suggestion schemes and quality circles.
- Team briefings with two-way discussion.
- Employee representatives or worker directors on the board.
- Employee share ownership or co-operative structure.

Judge each by how real the influence is: being informed, being asked, or having a vote.

### Choosing

- Repetitive, short-term, low-paid work: piece rate or bonus can work, but watch quality.
- Skilled, well-paid staff: autonomy, enrichment and development tend to matter more.
- Always say why a method fits this workforce and what could stop it working.

## 2.3.1 Management

### Functions and thinkers

| Item | Recall |
|---|---|
| Four traditional functions | Planning, organising, directing, controlling |
| Fayol (standard form) | Five elements: planning, organising, commanding, coordinating, controlling |
| Mintzberg (standard form) | Ten roles. Interpersonal: figurehead, leader, liaison. Informational: monitor, disseminator, spokesperson. Decisional: entrepreneur, disturbance handler, resource allocator, negotiator |
| Fayol against Mintzberg | Fayol sets out what managers should do; Mintzberg observed what they actually do, which is fragmented and reactive |
| Contribution to performance | Direction, resource allocation, motivation, problem solving, cost and quality control |

Reminder of the output calculation: output per worker = total output ÷ number of workers. A line of 16 workers moving from 960 to 1,080 units a day goes from 60 to 67.5 per worker, a rise of 12.5%. Say what else could explain the change before crediting the manager.

### Styles

| Style | Who decides | Best when | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Autocratic | Manager alone | Crisis; inexperienced staff | Low morale, lost ideas |
| Democratic | Manager after consulting | Skilled staff; complex choices | Slow |
| Laissez-faire | Staff themselves | Expert, self-motivated team | Drift, no direction |
| Paternalistic | Manager, with explained concern for welfare | Staff valuing security | Seen as patronising |

### McGregor

| Theory X manager | Theory Y manager |
|---|---|
| Assumes people dislike work and avoid responsibility | Assumes people can enjoy work and seek responsibility |
| Close control, rules, pay as the lever | Delegation, trust, involvement |
| Usually autocratic | Usually democratic or laissez-faire |

These are a manager's beliefs, not facts about staff.

## Applying theories to a case

| Evidence in the case | Theory to use | Likely advice |
|---|---|---|
| Staff say they feel invisible and unheard | Mayo, Herzberg (recognition) | Consultation, praise, team meetings |
| Complaints about pay and rest areas, not about the work | Herzberg hygiene factors | Fix them, then add motivators |
| One lower-level need is clearly unmet | Maslow | Meet it before offering higher rewards |
| Staff differ widely in what they want | McClelland | Offer a choice of tasks and rewards |
| Staff doubt that effort will be rewarded | Vroom (instrumentality) | Make the link between performance and reward visible |
| Output is simple, countable and repetitive | Taylor | Piece rate, with quality checks |

## Evaluation prompts

- Does the workforce respond more to money or to responsibility?
- Is the cost of the method lower than the gain in output or retention?
- Will the effect last, or fade once the novelty ends?
- Could the method cause a problem such as lower quality or resentment?
- What is the time frame: a quick fix or a lasting change?
- Is there a better alternative for this particular group of employees?

A judgement uses your answers to these prompts. Say which is most important in this case, and why.

## Must-know distinctions

- Content theory (what motivates) against process theory (how motivation is formed).
- Hygiene factor (prevents dissatisfaction) against motivator (creates satisfaction).
- Job re-design (any change to tasks) against job enrichment (more responsibility and decisions).
- Empowerment (authority to act in the job) against participation (a say in wider decisions).
- Profit sharing (whole team) against PRP (the individual).
- Paternalistic (decides, explains, cares) against democratic (consults and shares the decision).
- Fayol (principles) against Mintzberg (observed roles).

## Quick self-test

1. State the five levels in Maslow's hierarchy, from lowest to highest.
2. Classify as hygiene factor or motivator: (a) job security, (b) recognition.
3. Identify the three needs in McClelland's theory.
4. A worker is paid £0.75 per unit and makes 620 units. Calculate pay.
5. A salesperson earns 5% commission on £8,000 of sales. Calculate the commission.
6. State what Vroom means by valence.
7. A Theory Y manager is most likely to use which style?
8. Name the four traditional functions of a manager.
9. Give one reason why profit sharing may do little to raise an individual's effort.
10. Name the three groups of Mintzberg's roles.

### Answers

1. Physiological, safety, social, esteem, self-actualisation.
2. (a) Hygiene factor. (b) Motivator.
3. Achievement, affiliation and power.
4. 620 × 0.75 = £465.
5. 0.05 × 8,000 = £400.
6. How much the individual values the reward on offer.
7. Democratic or laissez-faire.
8. Planning, organising, directing, controlling.
9. Each person's share depends on the whole business's profit, so one person's extra effort changes the pay very little.
10. Interpersonal, informational, decisional.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Listing theories without linking them to the employees in the case.
- Naming a pay method without saying why it fits this workforce.
- Treating money as either always or never a motivator.
- Mixing up hygiene factors and motivators, especially pay.
- Writing "the manager caused the improvement" when the case offers another explanation.
- Calling every consultation democratic management, when the manager may still decide alone.
- A calculation with no working or no unit.
- Evaluation answers that list advantages and disadvantages but never reach a judgement.
