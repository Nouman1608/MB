Section 3.1.1 of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Chemistry (9202) specification, Version 6.2, has two statements. Statement a: matter can be classified in terms of the three states of matter; you should be able to name each inter-conversion process, "describe and explain their inter-conversion in terms of how the particles are arranged and their movement", and understand the energy changes that accompany changes of state. Statement b: evidence for particles comes from simple diffusion experiments, such as bromine and air, ammonia and hydrogen chloride, and potassium manganate(VII) and water. The qualification is untiered, so this applies to every candidate.

The broader [Atomic Structure and the Periodic Table guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-chemistry-atomic-structure-and-periodic-table/) gives a short summary. This guide goes further: it links properties of each state to the particle model, uses melting and boiling point data, reads a heating curve, and explains what each diffusion experiment shows.

## The particle model of the three states

| | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arrangement | regular, closely packed | random, closely packed | random, far apart |
| Movement | vibrate about fixed positions | move around each other | move quickly in all directions |
| Forces between particles | strong enough to hold particles in place | strong enough to keep particles close | very weak |
| Energy of particles | lowest | higher | highest |

### Explaining properties

Each everyday property of a state follows from the model:

- **Solids keep their shape** because particles are held in fixed positions; they can only vibrate.
- **Liquids flow and take the shape of the container** because particles can move past one another, but they keep a fixed volume because the particles stay close together.
- **Gases fill their container and can be compressed** because the particles are far apart with large spaces between them, and they move freely in all directions.
- **Solids and liquids are hard to compress** because the particles are already touching.

## Naming the inter-conversions

| Change | Name | Energy |
|---|---|---|
| solid → liquid | melting | taken in |
| liquid → solid | freezing | given out |
| liquid → gas | boiling (throughout the liquid, at the boiling point) or evaporation (from the surface, below the boiling point) | taken in |
| gas → liquid | condensing | given out |
| solid → gas directly | sublimation | taken in |

Melting and freezing happen at the same temperature for a substance, the **melting point**. Boiling and condensing happen at the same temperature, the **boiling point**.

## Explaining a change of state

A full explanation has three parts: **energy**, **forces** and **arrangement and movement**.

*Melting:* the particles take in energy and vibrate more. When they have enough energy, they overcome some of the forces holding them in fixed positions. The regular arrangement breaks down and particles begin to move around each other, but they remain close together.

*Boiling:* the particles take in more energy and move faster. When they have enough energy, they overcome the forces between them almost completely. They escape from the liquid and spread out as a gas, far apart and moving randomly.

*Condensing and freezing* are the reverse: particles lose energy, move more slowly, and the forces between them pull them closer together or into fixed positions.

The stronger the forces between the particles, the more energy is needed to separate them, so the higher the melting and boiling points.

## Reading a heating curve

When a solid is heated steadily from below its melting point to above its boiling point, a graph of temperature against time has five sections:

1. **Rising**: the solid warms up; particles vibrate more.
2. **Flat at the melting point**: energy is used to overcome forces between particles, not to raise the temperature. Solid and liquid are both present.
3. **Rising**: the liquid warms up.
4. **Flat at the boiling point**: energy overcomes the remaining forces; liquid and gas are both present.
5. **Rising**: the gas warms up.

A cooling curve shows the same flat sections, at the boiling point (condensing) and the melting point (freezing), because energy is given out while the state changes.

## Worked example 1: predicting states from data

*Use the data (values rounded) to state the physical state of each substance at 25 °C.*

| Substance | Melting point / °C | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|---|
| Oxygen | −218 | −183 |
| Bromine | −7 | 59 |
| Ethanol | −114 | 78 |
| Iodine | 114 | 184 |

**Method.** Below the melting point: solid. Between the melting point and the boiling point: liquid. Above the boiling point: gas.

- Oxygen: 25 °C is above −183 °C, so **gas**.
- Bromine: 25 °C is between −7 °C and 59 °C, so **liquid**.
- Ethanol: between −114 °C and 78 °C, so **liquid**.
- Iodine: 25 °C is below 114 °C, so **solid**.

*Extension:* bromine has a higher boiling point than oxygen, so more energy is needed to separate bromine molecules: the forces between bromine molecules are stronger.

## Evidence for particles: diffusion

**Diffusion** is the spreading out of particles from a region where they are more concentrated to a region where they are less concentrated, caused by their random movement. It happens in gases and liquids; it is the evidence that particles exist and are constantly moving.

| Experiment | What is seen | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| Bromine and air | brown bromine vapour slowly fills a gas jar of air, without stirring | gas particles move randomly and mix |
| Potassium manganate(VII) and water | a purple colour spreads slowly from a crystal through still water | particles in a liquid move and mix; slower than in a gas because liquid particles are close together and collide constantly |
| Ammonia and hydrogen chloride | cotton wool soaked in each is placed at opposite ends of a long tube; a white ring of ammonium chloride forms after a few minutes, nearer the hydrogen chloride end | the gases diffuse along the tube and react where they meet; ammonia particles have a lower mass than hydrogen chloride particles, so they move faster and travel further |

## Worked example 2: explaining the ammonia and hydrogen chloride result

*Explain why the white ring forms closer to the end with hydrogen chloride. [3]*

**Model answer.** Ammonia particles (relative formula mass 17) are lighter than hydrogen chloride particles (36.5) [1]. At the same temperature, lighter particles move faster on average [1]. So ammonia diffuses further along the tube in the same time, and the two gases meet nearer the hydrogen chloride end, where they react to form ammonium chloride [1].

*Why it scores:* each mark links an observation to particle behaviour. "Ammonia is faster" without the reason (lower mass) does not earn the middle mark.

## Common mistakes

- **"Particles expand when heated."** The particles stay the same size; they move faster and further apart.
- **"Particles in a solid do not move."** They vibrate about fixed positions.
- **"Boiling breaks bonds in the molecules."** For a simple molecular substance, boiling overcomes the forces *between* molecules; the bonds inside the molecules are not broken.
- **Describing a change of state without energy.** State whether energy is taken in or given out.
- **Saying the temperature rises during melting.** It stays constant until all the solid has melted.
- **Explaining diffusion as "particles want to spread out".** Diffusion results from random movement.

## Exam technique

- **Name**: give the single word for the change (melting, condensing, sublimation).
- **Describe**: what happens to arrangement and movement.
- **Explain**: add the reason, in terms of energy and forces between particles.
- For a data question, quote the values you used: "25 °C is above the boiling point of −183 °C, so oxygen is a gas."
