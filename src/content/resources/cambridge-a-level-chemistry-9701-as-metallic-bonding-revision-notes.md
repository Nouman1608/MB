**Syllabus:** Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027, **AS Level**, subtopic 3.3 Metallic bonding.

## The learning outcome

**Define** metallic bonding as the **electrostatic attraction** between **positive metal ions** and **delocalised electrons**.

Learn it word for word. Each of the three bold phrases is checked by examiners.

## The model in five points

- Metal atoms release their **outer-shell electrons**.
- The atoms become **positive ions**, arranged in a regular **giant lattice**.
- The released electrons are **delocalised**: spread through the whole lattice and free to move.
- The bond is the **attraction** between the lattice of positive ions and the delocalised electrons.
- The attraction acts **in all directions** (non-directional), so the structure is giant and there are no molecules.

## Electrons delocalised per atom

| Metal | Outer electrons | Ion | Delocalised electrons per atom |
|---|---|---|---|
| Li, Na, K | ns¹ | 1+ | 1 |
| Mg, Ca | ns² | 2+ | 2 |
| Al | 3s² 3p¹ | 3+ | 3 |

## Factors affecting the strength of metallic bonding

Stronger attraction when:

1. **higher charge** on the metal ion
2. **more delocalised electrons** per ion
3. **smaller metal ion** (delocalised electrons closer to the nucleus)

| Comparison | Reason | Melting point |
|---|---|---|
| Na → Mg → Al | ion charge +1 → +2 → +3; 1 → 2 → 3 delocalised electrons; ions smaller | 98 °C → 650 °C → 660 °C |
| Li → Na → K | same charge (+1) and one electron each, but ions larger | 181 °C → 98 °C → 63 °C |

Use these for explanations in Topic 4 (giant metallic lattices) and Topic 9 (Period 3 elements).

## Conductivity

- Metals conduct in the **solid** and **liquid** states.
- The **delocalised electrons** are the charge carriers; they move towards the positive terminal when a potential difference is applied.
- The positive ions do **not** move through a solid metal.
- Contrast: an ionic solid does not conduct because its ions are fixed; it conducts only when molten or dissolved.
- **Copper** is the syllabus example of a giant metallic lattice.

## Building a full-mark explanation

For any "explain why metal A has a higher melting point than metal B" question, write three linked sentences:

1. **Particles:** state the ion charge and the number of delocalised electrons per atom for each metal.
2. **Size:** say which ion is smaller.
3. **Link:** so the electrostatic attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons is stronger in A, and more energy is needed to overcome it.

For a conductivity comparison, replace step 3 with: "so A has more delocalised electrons available to move and carry charge."

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Metallic bonding | Electrostatic attraction between positive metal ions and delocalised electrons |
| Delocalised electrons | Electrons not associated with any single atom or bond, free to move through the structure |
| Giant metallic lattice | Regular arrangement of very many positive metal ions within delocalised electrons |
| Non-directional bonding | Attraction acting equally in all directions, not along a line between two atoms |
| Charge carrier | Mobile charged particle that moves to carry an electric current |

## The three strong bonds compared

| Bond | Electrostatic attraction between |
|---|---|
| Ionic | oppositely charged ions |
| Metallic | positive metal ions and delocalised electrons |
| Covalent | nuclei of two atoms and a shared pair of electrons |

## Exam traps

- Write **positive metal ions**, never "metal atoms", in the definition.
- Write **delocalised electrons**; "electrons" alone is not enough.
- A pure metal has **no anions**; do not describe it as ionic bonding.
- In a solid metal only the **electrons** move, not the ions.
- Down Group 1 the number of delocalised electrons per atom stays at **one**; the ion **size** causes the trend.
- "Stronger metallic bonding" without a reason does not answer an "explain" question.
- Avoid "intermolecular forces" for metals: there are no molecules.

## Self-test

1. Define metallic bonding.
2. How many electrons per atom does aluminium contribute to the delocalised electrons?
3. What particles occupy the lattice positions in solid magnesium?
4. State the three factors that make metallic bonding stronger.
5. Explain why aluminium is a better electrical conductor than sodium.
6. Explain why the melting point of potassium is lower than that of sodium.
7. Explain why copper conducts electricity in the solid state.
8. Why does molten sodium still conduct electricity?
9. A student writes "metallic bonding is the attraction between positive and negative ions". Correct it.
10. Calcium forms Ca²⁺ in its metallic lattice. Predict whether calcium has a higher or lower melting point than potassium, and give one reason.
11. Why is it incorrect to say that metals contain molecules?

**Answers:**

1. The electrostatic attraction between positive metal ions and delocalised electrons.
2. Three (3s² 3p¹).
3. Positive magnesium ions, Mg²⁺.
4. Higher ion charge; more delocalised electrons per ion; smaller ion.
5. Aluminium contributes three delocalised electrons per atom compared with one for sodium, so there are more mobile charge carriers.
6. Both form 1+ ions with one delocalised electron per atom, but K⁺ is larger, so the delocalised electrons are further from the nucleus and the attraction is weaker; less energy is needed to overcome it.
7. It has delocalised electrons that are free to move through the lattice and carry charge when a potential difference is applied.
8. Melting does not remove the delocalised electrons, which remain mobile in the liquid.
9. Metallic bonding is the electrostatic attraction between positive metal ions and delocalised electrons; a metal contains no negative ions.
10. Higher: Ca²⁺ has a higher charge, gives two delocalised electrons per atom, and is smaller than K⁺.
11. The attraction acts in all directions through a giant lattice; there are no separate, discrete groups of atoms.
