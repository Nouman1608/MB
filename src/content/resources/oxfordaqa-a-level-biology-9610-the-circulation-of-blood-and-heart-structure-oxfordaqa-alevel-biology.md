## The double circulation

Mammals have a double circulation: blood passes through the heart twice for each complete circuit of the body.

**Tracing a red blood cell from a leg muscle:**

1. Vena cava → right atrium.
2. Through the right atrioventricular valve → right ventricle.
3. Through the semilunar valve → pulmonary artery → lungs, where it is oxygenated.
4. Pulmonary vein → left atrium.
5. Through the left atrioventricular valve → left ventricle.
6. Through the semilunar valve → aorta → arteries → the leg capillaries.

**Why two circuits?** Blood loses pressure as it passes through the narrow capillaries of the lungs. Returning to the heart lets the left ventricle raise the pressure again before blood is sent to the body. The pulmonary circuit can also run at lower pressure, which protects the delicate lung capillaries. Keeping the two sides separate, with the septum between them, stops oxygenated and deoxygenated blood mixing.

**Two thick-wall comparisons to know:**

- Ventricles have thicker walls than atria, because atria pump only into the ventricles while ventricles pump out of the heart.
- The left ventricle wall is thicker than the right, because it must generate a higher pressure to push blood all round the body, not just through the nearby lungs. Both ventricles still pump the same volume per beat.

The cardiac muscle itself is supplied by the coronary arteries, which branch from the aorta.

## Vessels: structure, pressure and function

Blood pressure is highest in the aorta and falls continuously through arteries, capillaries and veins. Each vessel's structure matches the pressure it carries.

| Feature | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Thick elastic layer in arteries | Stretches as the ventricle ejects blood, then recoils, smoothing the pulses into a more even flow and maintaining pressure between beats |
| Thick muscle layer in arteries | Contracts or relaxes to change the lumen and control blood flow to different organs |
| Collagen in artery walls | Resists the high pressure, so the wall does not burst |
| Thin walls and wide lumen in veins | Pressure is low; a wide lumen gives little resistance to flow |
| Valves in veins | Prevent backflow when pressure is low |
| Capillary wall one cell thick | Short diffusion distance for metabolic exchange |
| Capillaries narrow and numerous | Large surface area, slow flow, every cell close to a capillary |

## Tissue fluid: predicting the effect of changes

Two opposing effects act across a capillary wall:

- **Hydrostatic pressure** of the blood pushes fluid out. It is high at the arteriole end and lower at the venule end.
- **Water potential** of the plasma, kept low by plasma proteins that cannot leave, draws water back in by osmosis.

At the arteriole end, hydrostatic pressure wins: water and small solutes are forced out to form tissue fluid. At the venule end, the lower water potential of the plasma wins: water returns by osmosis. The excess drains into lymph vessels and is returned to the blood.

**Worked example 1: high blood pressure.** If blood pressure is raised, hydrostatic pressure at the arteriole end is higher, so more fluid is forced out. Pressure at the venule end is also higher, so less water returns. Tissue fluid accumulates.

**Worked example 2: low plasma protein.** If a person's diet provides too little protein, plasma protein concentration falls. The water potential of the plasma is less negative, so less water returns by osmosis at the venule end. Again, tissue fluid accumulates.

The method for any such question: decide which of the two effects changes, then say whether more fluid leaves, less fluid returns, or both.

## Reading the cardiac cycle from pressure and volume data

The valves open and close passively: a valve opens when the pressure behind it is higher than the pressure in front, and closes when the pressure difference reverses. So every valve movement is found where two pressure lines cross.

| Crossover on the trace | Valve event |
|---|---|
| Ventricular pressure rises above atrial pressure | Atrioventricular valve closes |
| Ventricular pressure rises above aortic pressure | Semilunar valve opens |
| Ventricular pressure falls below aortic pressure | Semilunar valve closes |
| Ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure | Atrioventricular valve opens |

Between the first two events, and between the last two, both valves are closed, so ventricular volume does not change while the pressure rises or falls.

The volume trace gives two key values: the maximum ventricular volume (end of filling) and the minimum (end of ejection). Stroke volume is the difference.

**Worked example 3: from a described trace.** A left ventricle volume trace peaks at 130 cm³ and falls to 60 cm³. One complete cycle lasts 0.75 s.

1. Stroke volume = 130 − 60 = 70 cm³.
2. Heart rate = 60 ÷ 0.75 = 80 beats per minute.
3. Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume = 80 × 70 = 5600 cm³ per minute (5.6 dm³ per minute).

**Worked example 4: rearranging.** During exercise, cardiac output is 18 000 cm³ per minute and heart rate is 150 beats per minute. Stroke volume = 18 000 ÷ 150 = 120 cm³.

**Worked example 5: comparing the two ventricles.** On a pressure trace, the left ventricle peaks at about 16 kPa and the right ventricle at about 3 kPa. Their stroke volumes are equal. Explain.

- The left ventricle has a thicker muscular wall, so it contracts more forcefully and generates a higher pressure, needed to pump blood through the whole body.
- The two sides are in series in the double circulation, so whatever volume leaves the right ventricle must return to the left side. Over time their outputs must match, or blood would build up in one circuit.

## Annotated model answer

**Question:** Explain what causes the semilunar valve in the aorta to open and then close during one cardiac cycle. [4 marks]

**Answer:** The ventricle contracts, so the pressure in the ventricle rises (1). When ventricular pressure rises above the pressure in the aorta, the semilunar valve is pushed open and blood is ejected (1). As the ventricle relaxes, its pressure falls (1). When ventricular pressure falls below aortic pressure, blood starts to flow back and closes the valve, preventing backflow into the ventricle (1).

*Why it scores:* each valve movement is tied to a specific pressure comparison. Answers that say the valve "opens when the ventricle contracts" without the pressure comparison score at most half.

## Common mistakes

- Saying valves are opened by muscle contraction. They respond to pressure differences.
- Saying the left ventricle pumps more blood than the right. It pumps at higher pressure, but the same volume.
- Saying arteries carry oxygenated blood and veins deoxygenated blood. The pulmonary artery and pulmonary vein are the exceptions; define arteries as carrying blood away from the heart.
- Saying elastic fibres "pump" blood. They recoil to maintain pressure and smooth flow.
- Saying plasma proteins leave the capillary in tissue fluid.
- Giving cardiac output in cm³ without "per minute".

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a pathway: name each chamber, valve and vessel in order.
- **Explain** a structure: feature, then what it does, then why that matters for function.
- **Use the data**: find crossovers for valve events; read maximum and minimum volumes; convert cycle time to heart rate.
- **Calculate**: show cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume, with units.
- **Suggest**: for tissue fluid problems, name the force that changes.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does the left ventricle have a thicker wall than the right?**
It must generate a higher pressure to pump blood around the whole body, whereas the right ventricle pumps blood only to the nearby lungs.

**How do you find heart rate from a pressure trace?**
Measure the time for one complete cycle, then divide 60 by that time in seconds to get beats per minute.
