Cambridge A Level Geography 9696 is assessed by four papers of identical size: Paper 1 Core Physical
Geography, Paper 2 Core Human Geography, Paper 3 Advanced Physical Geography Options and Paper 4
Advanced Human Geography Options. Each lasts 1 hour 30 minutes, carries 60 marks, and is weighted 25%
of the full A Level. A Level candidates typically follow a staged route: Papers 1 and 2 (which
together constitute the AS Level) in one series, then Papers 3 and 4 in a later series to complete the
A Level. These notes build a preparation plan around that structure, alongside the site's guides to
[Core Physical Geography](/resources/a-level-cambridge-geography-core-physical-geography/) and
[Core Human Geography](/resources/a-level-cambridge-geography-core-human-geography/).

## The AS papers are half the A Level -- they are not a rehearsal

Because Papers 1 and 2 are usually sat a year before Papers 3 and 4, it is easy to treat them as
practice. They are not: together they are 50% of the final A Level grade. **Exam-preparation
priority**: prepare the AS series as if it were the final one, because for half the qualification it
is. This is the single most consequential structural fact about 9696, and the one most often realised
too late.

## Four equal papers means no paper can be traded off against another

Each paper is exactly 25%. There is no dominant component to concentrate on and no minor one to
absorb a weak performance. **Exam-preparation priority**: in the run-up to each series, audit the two
papers being sat and give the weaker one more time. Because the papers are sat in pairs, this audit
happens twice across the course rather than once, and the second audit should also include a genuine
re-check of core content from Papers 1 and 2 -- advanced option questions frequently assume the core
physical and human processes are still fluent.

## Ninety minutes for sixty marks is 1.5 minutes per mark

Each paper offers 90 minutes for 60 marks, and geography answers at this level require both diagram
work and extended prose. **Exam-preparation priority**: rehearse to that budget with the diagrams
included, since sketching a labelled diagram is not free time. A well-labelled diagram is usually
faster than the equivalent paragraph of prose and often clearer, which makes it a timing tool as much
as a communication one -- but only for candidates who have practised drawing it quickly.

## On the options papers, choose on your case studies, not your interest

Papers 3 and 4 are options papers, and the choice of which options to answer should be governed by
where your evidence is strongest. **Exam-preparation priority**: for each option you have studied,
list the specific case studies you can deploy -- named places, dated events, quantities. Choose the
question you can support with named, specific material rather than the one whose topic you find most
interesting. Interest and evidence are not the same asset in an exam.

## Case studies earn marks through specificity, not through length

The distinguishing feature of a strong A Level geography answer is precise supporting material:
places named, figures quoted, dates given, processes correctly identified. A long description of an
event with no named location does very little. **Exam-preparation priority**: reduce each case study
to a card carrying the place, the date, three quantitative or highly specific details, and the process
it illustrates. Revise the cards, not the original notes -- the card is what you can actually retrieve
under time pressure.

## Worked routine: deploying a case study inside an answer

The routine below is an original model written for this resource, not a reproduction of any official
past paper, mark scheme or examiner report.

```
Step 1 - state the general process first:
Answer the question in general terms in one sentence, so the case
study supports an argument rather than replacing it.

Step 2 - name the place and the date:
"In [named place], in [year]..." -- an unnamed example is treated
as a generalisation, however detailed the description.

Step 3 - give two or three specific details:
Figures, distances, magnitudes, affected populations. This is the
layer that separates A-grade answers from competent ones.

Step 4 - return explicitly to the question:
State what the case study demonstrates about the process the
question asked about. Do not leave the connection implied.

Step 5 - where relevant, note a limitation:
Is this case typical, or exceptional? Recognising that a single
case may not generalise is itself a mark-earning observation on
evaluative questions.
```

Step 4 is the step most often omitted. A vividly recalled case study that is never tied back to the
question demonstrates knowledge without answering anything.

## Before/during exam checklist

- **Before the AS series**: prepare Papers 1 and 2 as final papers, not as practice -- they are 50%
  of the A Level.
- **Before the A2 series**: re-check core physical and human content as well as the options, since
  Papers 3 and 4 assume core processes remain fluent.
- **In both series**: audit the two papers being sat and give the weaker one more time; reduce each
  case study to a card with place, date, three specifics and the process illustrated.
- **During any paper**: budget 1.5 minutes per mark including diagram time; practise diagrams for
  speed so they save time rather than cost it.
- **On the options papers**: choose the question your named case studies support best, not the topic
  you find most interesting.
- **In every case-study paragraph**: name the place and date, give specifics, then state explicitly
  what it shows about the question.

## Self-test

1. What share of the A Level do Papers 1 and 2 carry, and why does that matter for the AS series?
2. Why should the A2 revision audit include core content as well as the options?
3. On what basis should options-paper questions be chosen?
4. Which step of a case-study paragraph is most often omitted, and what is the consequence?

**Answers:** 1. Fifty per cent -- each paper is 25% of the full A Level -- so the AS series is not a
rehearsal and should be prepared for as a final sitting. 2. Because Papers 3 and 4 are advanced
options that assume the core physical and human processes covered in Papers 1 and 2 are still fluent.
3. On where your named, specific case-study evidence is strongest, rather than on which topic is most
interesting. 4. Returning explicitly to the question to state what the case study demonstrates -- an
untethered case study shows knowledge without answering the question set.
