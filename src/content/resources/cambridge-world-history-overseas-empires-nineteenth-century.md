**Key question 1.5 asks why, and with what effects, nations gained and
expanded overseas empires in the nineteenth century.** Cambridge sets
four case areas: the motives for European and US imperialism; French,
British and Belgian rule in Africa and its impact on Africans; the
events of 1857 in India and how they changed British rule; and the
Spanish–American War, after which the USA took over former Spanish
colonies. Exam questions reward comparison between case studies and a
clear judgement on motives.

## Where this fits in 0470 and 2147

This is one of six key questions in Core Content Option A of both
Cambridge IGCSE History 0470 and O Level History 2147 (2024–2026). It
links forward to key question 1.6, because colonial rivalry, such as
the Moroccan crises, raised tension between the Great Powers before
1914. For the whole option, see the
[IGCSE Nineteenth Century study guide](/resources/igcse-world-history-nineteenth-century-1848-1914/)
or the [O Level version](/resources/o-level-world-history-nineteenth-century-1848-1914/).

## Syllabus coverage

The official focus points are:

1. What were the motives behind European and US imperialism?
2. How varied were the impacts of European imperialism on Africans?
3. Why, and with what effects, did Indians resist British rule?
4. Why, and with what effects, did the United States gain former
   Spanish colonies?

Specified content: economic, military, geopolitical, religious and
cultural motives; French assimilation and direct rule (Faidherbe and
Senegal); British indirect rule (Lugard and Nigeria); Belgian private
imperialism (Leopold II and the Congo); the British in India, 1857 and
its consequences; the wars of independence in Cuba and the
Philippines, the Spanish–American War and the Treaty of Paris, the US
debate over imperialism, American control of the Philippines, and
American intervention in Cuba to 1906.

## What were the motives for imperialism?

| Motive | What it meant | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Economic | Raw materials, markets for factory goods, places to invest | Rubber and ivory from the Congo; palm oil from West Africa; cotton |
| Military | Naval bases, coaling stations, control of sea routes | Suez Canal (opened 1869); US interest in Pacific bases |
| Geopolitical | Rivalry and prestige between powers; taking land before a rival did | The "Scramble for Africa"; the Berlin Conference of 1884–85 |
| Religious | Spreading Christianity through missions | David Livingstone; mission schools across Africa |
| Cultural | Belief in a "civilising mission" and racial superiority | Kipling's poem "The White Man's Burden" (1899), written about the Philippines |

A strong answer does not treat motives as a list: economic interests
often needed strategic and national arguments to win support at home,
and missionaries and explorers often arrived before governments did.

## How varied was the impact of European rule on Africans?

### France in Senegal: assimilation and direct rule

**Louis Faidherbe**, governor of Senegal in 1854–61 and 1863–65,
pushed French control inland from the coast, fought the forces of
El Hadj Umar Tall, and in 1857 created the *tirailleurs sénégalais*,
African soldiers in French service. French policy aimed at
**assimilation**: Africans who adopted French language and culture
could, in theory, become French citizens, and the coastal Four
Communes (Saint-Louis, Gorée, Rufisque and Dakar) elected a deputy to
the French parliament. Rule was **direct**, through French officials.
In practice, only a small minority ever gained citizenship; most
Africans were subjects, not citizens.

### Britain in Nigeria: indirect rule

**Frederick Lugard** became High Commissioner of Northern Nigeria in
1900, conquered the Sokoto Caliphate by 1903 and then ruled through
the existing emirs and their courts and taxes. This **indirect rule**
was cheap and kept local rulers in place, but it strengthened
traditional elites and worked badly in the south-east, where there were
no comparable chiefs. Lugard oversaw the amalgamation of Northern and
Southern Nigeria in 1914 and later set out his ideas in *The Dual
Mandate in British Tropical Africa* (1922).

### Belgium and the Congo: private imperialism

The Berlin Conference recognised the **Congo Free State** in 1885 as
the personal possession of **King Leopold II**, not of Belgium. Once
demand for rubber rose in the 1890s, Congolese people were forced to
meet rubber quotas, enforced by the Force Publique through
hostage-taking, mutilation and killing. Millions died from violence,
famine and disease, though estimates vary. After reports by the
British consul **Roger Casement** (1904) and campaigning by
**E. D. Morel's** Congo Reform Association, the Belgian state took the
colony over in **1908**.

**Comparison:** French rule replaced local authority, British rule
used it, and Leopold's regime exploited it for profit; the Congo shows
the most extreme impact, but all three systems took land, labour and
political control from Africans.

## Why did Indians resist British rule in 1857, and what changed?

The rising began at **Meerut on 10 May 1857**, when sepoys of the East
India Company's army rebelled and marched to Delhi, where they
proclaimed the elderly Mughal emperor **Bahadur Shah II** as their
leader. Fighting spread across northern India, including Kanpur and
the siege of Lucknow; the Rani of Jhansi, Lakshmibai, died fighting in
1858.

**Causes:** rumours that the new Enfield rifle cartridges were greased
with cow and pig fat, offending Hindu and Muslim soldiers; annexations
of Indian states, including under Lord Dalhousie's Doctrine of Lapse
and the annexation of Awadh (1856); fears of forced conversion and of
Western reforms; grievances over pay and service overseas.

**Results:** the **Government of India Act 1858** ended East India
Company rule and placed India under the Crown, with a Secretary of
State in London and a Viceroy in India. Queen Victoria's Proclamation
(1858) promised religious toleration and an end to annexations. The
army was reorganised with a higher proportion of British troops, and
Bahadur Shah was exiled to Rangoon, ending the Mughal dynasty. British
rule became more cautious about interfering with Indian customs, but
more distant from Indians.

## Why did the USA gain former Spanish colonies?

- **Cuba:** a war of independence against Spain began in 1895; harsh
  Spanish "reconcentration" camps were reported in sensational US
  newspapers. After the battleship **USS *Maine*** exploded in Havana
  harbour on 15 February 1898, the USA declared war in April.
- **The war:** Commodore Dewey destroyed the Spanish fleet at Manila
  Bay (1 May 1898) and US forces won in Cuba; fighting ended in August.
- **Treaty of Paris (December 1898):** Spain gave up Cuba and ceded
  Puerto Rico, Guam and the Philippines to the USA, which paid
  $20 million for the Philippines.
- **The debate in the USA:** supporters argued for markets, naval
  bases and a duty to "uplift" others; the Anti-Imperialist League
  (including Mark Twain and Andrew Carnegie) argued that ruling
  colonies betrayed American ideals. The Senate ratified the treaty in
  February 1899 by just over the two-thirds needed.
- **The Philippines:** Emilio Aguinaldo had declared independence in
  June 1898; the Philippine–American War (1899–1902) followed, with
  heavy Filipino civilian losses. US rule then brought schools, English
  and an elected assembly (1907), but under American control.
- **Cuba to 1906:** the Teller Amendment had promised not to annex
  Cuba, but the **Platt Amendment** (1901) gave the USA the right to
  intervene and a naval base at Guantánamo Bay; US troops occupied
  Cuba until 1902 and returned in 1906.

## Frequently asked questions

**What were the main motives for imperialism?** Economic gain,
military and naval strategy, rivalry and prestige between nations,
religious missions, and a belief in a "civilising mission".

**What is the difference between direct and indirect rule?** Direct
rule, as in French Senegal, governed through European officials;
indirect rule, as in British Northern Nigeria, governed through
existing African rulers under British supervision.

**What changed in India after 1857?** The East India Company was
abolished, the Crown took direct control through a Viceroy, the army
was reorganised and Britain promised not to interfere in religion.

**Why did the USA fight Spain in 1898?** Sympathy for Cuban
independence fanned by the press, the sinking of the *Maine*, economic
interests in Cuba and a wish for overseas bases.
