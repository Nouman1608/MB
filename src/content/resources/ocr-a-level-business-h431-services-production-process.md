## How a service is produced

A manufacturer turns materials into a product that can be stored and sold later. A service business turns inputs (staff time, knowledge, equipment, premises, information) into an experience or result for a customer. The production process for services differs from manufacturing in several ways.

- **Customer involvement.** The customer is often part of the process, for example a patient describing symptoms or a diner placing an order. Their behaviour affects the output.
- **Simultaneous production and consumption.** The service is made and used at the same time, so there is no chance to check quality before delivery and no stock to fall back on.
- **Perishability and capacity.** Unused capacity (an empty appointment) cannot be stored. Too little capacity means queues and lost customers; too much means idle staff.
- **Variability.** Each delivery differs because people differ, so standard procedures and training are needed.
- **Front office and back office.** The front office is where customers meet staff (a reception desk, a shop floor, a website). The back office is the work they do not see (processing, scheduling, checking). Both must work well.

A typical process runs through stages: **enquiry or booking**, **arrival or access**, **delivery of the service**, **payment**, and **follow-up** (feedback, after-care). Each stage can be mapped, timed and improved. A flow chart of these stages helps a manager spot where customers wait or where errors occur.

### Worked example 1: sizing a service team

A customer-support centre for an online retailer receives **600** calls a day. Each call takes **6 minutes**. Each agent is productive for **400 minutes** a day (the rest is breaks, training and admin). An agent costs **£120** a day.

1. Calls an agent can handle = 400 ÷ 6 = **66.7** calls a day.
2. Agents needed = 600 ÷ 66.7 = **9.0**, so 9 agents.
3. Daily cost = 9 × £120 = **£1,080**, which is £1.80 a call.

**What if there are only 8 agents?** Capacity = 8 × 66.7 = **533** calls. About **67** calls a day (600 − 533) are not answered promptly, and some customers will hang up or wait for long. Saving one agent saves £120 a day, but the cost of lost customers could be much higher. If each unanswered call costs the retailer £5 in lost sales and goodwill, 67 calls cost about £335, which is more than the £120 saved.

**Interpretation.** Because services cannot be stored, the business must match capacity to demand. Demand varies through the day, so the centre may use part-time staff, flexible rotas, call-back systems or online self-service to smooth the peaks. These choices affect cost and service quality.

## Customer service

**Customer service** is the support a business gives customers before, during and after a purchase. In a service business it is part of the product itself, since the way the service is delivered is the service. It includes:

- **Before sale:** clear information, advice, easy booking.
- **During delivery:** friendly, competent staff, speed, accuracy, and handling special requests.
- **After sale:** follow-up, complaint handling, refunds and guarantees.

Its quality can be measured by waiting times, the share of problems solved on first contact, complaints, repeat bookings and satisfaction scores from surveys.

## Importance of high-quality customer service

High-quality customer service is important because it:

- **retains customers**, who are cheaper to keep than to win;
- **supports reputation** and word of mouth, which are worth more when a service cannot be tried in advance;
- **allows a premium price** where customers see better value;
- **differentiates** the business when services are similar;
- **reduces costs** from complaints, repeat visits and refunds.

### Worked example 2: the value of better service

A gym chain has **4,000** members, each paying a membership that gives a contribution of **£37.50** a year (£150 of revenue at 25% contribution). Currently **70%** of members renew each year. It plans to spend **£9,000** a year on extra training and an extra front-desk shift, to lift renewal to **78%**.

1. Members retained now = 4,000 × 0.70 = 2,800. With the plan = 4,000 × 0.78 = 3,120. Extra members retained = **320**.
2. Extra contribution = 320 × £37.50 = **£12,000** a year.
3. Net gain = £12,000 − £9,000 = **£3,000**.

**Lifetime effect.** The average membership lasts 1 ÷ (1 − renewal rate) years.

- At 70% renewal, life = 1 ÷ 0.30 = **3.3 years**, giving a lifetime contribution of 3.33 × £37.50 = **£125**.
- At 78%, life = 1 ÷ 0.22 = **4.5 years**, giving 4.55 × £37.50 = **£170**.

So the lifetime value of a member rises by about £45 (36%).

**Evaluation.** The plan produces a profit in the first year and creates greater long-term value, but the net gain of £3,000 depends on achieving the 8-point rise in renewals. If renewal rose only to 74%, the extra members would be 160 (4,000 × 0.04), the extra contribution £6,000 and the plan would lose £3,000. The result is sensitive to the size of the effect, and some members leave for reasons unrelated to service, such as moving house. The gym should also use the survey scores to check that the service has actually improved.

## Measuring and improving service quality

A service cannot be inspected before it reaches the customer, so managers rely on measures taken during and after delivery. One of the most useful is **first-contact resolution**: the share of customer problems solved at the first contact.

Suppose the support centre in worked example 1 resolves **65%** of its 600 daily calls at the first contact.

1. Calls resolved first time = 600 × 0.65 = **390**, leaving **210** unresolved.
2. Each unresolved call generates on average 1.5 repeat calls, so repeat calls = 210 × 1.5 = **315**.
3. At £1.80 a call, repeat calls cost 315 × £1.80 = **£567** a day.

Better training raises first-contact resolution to **80%**:

4. Unresolved calls = 600 × 0.20 = **120**; repeat calls = 120 × 1.5 = **180**; cost = 180 × £1.80 = **£324** a day.
5. Saving = £567 − £324 = **£243** a day.

The improvement lowers the cost of service and raises satisfaction at the same time, because customers do not have to call twice. This shows that high-quality customer service need not cost more: removing repeat work can pay for itself. Technology can help (a knowledge database for agents, chat bots for simple questions, a system that records previous contacts), but it works only if staff are trained to use it and customers can still reach a person for complicated problems.

## Stakeholders

| Stakeholder | Effect of high-quality service |
|---|---|
| Customers | Better experience, fewer problems, more trust |
| Owners and shareholders | Higher retention and profit; but extra cost |
| Employees | Greater satisfaction if they are trained and supported; pressure if targets are unrealistic |
| Suppliers | Higher, more stable volumes |
| Community | A local reputation for good service |

**Limits and costs.** High-quality service costs money (staff, training, systems) and may not pay if customers mainly want the lowest price. A low-cost airline, for example, deliberately limits service to keep fares down. The right level depends on what the target customers value and on the business's strategy.

## Common mistakes

- Describing services as the same as goods, ignoring simultaneous production and perishability.
- Defining customer service as only a complaints department.
- Claiming that high service always pays. Compare the cost with the retention benefit.
- Forgetting that capacity cannot be stored.
- Ignoring the effects on employees.

## Exam technique

Link the process to the service in the case, use the numbers provided to size capacity or retention, and judge whether the level of service suits the customers and the strategy.
