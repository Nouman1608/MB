These questions cover section 2.3 Training (point 2.3.1) of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Business (4BS1) specification, Issue 2, February 2026. Revise first with the [training study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-training/) and the [training revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-training-revision-notes/) if you need them. Every business is invented and amounts are in dollars. Show your workings.

## Questions

### Section A: shorter questions

**1.** Define off-the-job training. **[2]**

**2.** State the type of training (induction, on-the-job or off-the-job) shown in each situation.

**(a)** A new hotel porter is shown the fire exits and introduced to the team on his first morning. **[1]**
**(b)** A kitchen assistant watches the head chef make a sauce and then makes the next one herself while the chef watches. **[1]**
**(c)** An accounts clerk attends evening classes at a college to gain a bookkeeping qualification. **[1]**

**3.** Give two benefits of training to a worker. **[2]**

**4.** Linwood Bakery trains its 7 counter staff in food safety. An online course costs 45 per person. Each person spends 3 hours away from the counter to complete it, and cover staff cost 16 an hour. **(Calculation)**

**(a)** Calculate the total cost of the training. **[3]**
**(b)** Food wasted through handling errors currently costs 280 a month. The owner expects this to fall by 25%. Calculate the monthly saving. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate, to 1 decimal place, how many months it will take for the saving to cover the cost of the training. **[1]**

**5.** Outline one reason why a building firm must give its employees health and safety training. **[2]**

**6.** Explain one limitation of on-the-job training. **[3]**

**7.** Explain one benefit of training to a business. **[3]**

### Section B: longer questions

**8.** Corlett Logistics is a large business with 400 employees. Last year 72 employees left. After it introduced a training and development programme, 48 employees left the following year. **(Calculation)**

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of employees who left in each year. **[2]**
**(b)** Each leaver costs Corlett 1,500 to replace. Calculate the saving from the fall in leavers. **[2]**
**(c)** Analyse one other benefit of the programme to Corlett Logistics. **[4]**

**9.** Ruby owns a small hairdressing salon with 6 stylists. She pays for her stylists to attend advanced colouring courses. Last year one stylist left soon after a course to open a rival salon nearby. Analyse the impact of this on Ruby's salon. **[4]**

**10.** Pemberly Print has 14 employees and has bought a new digital printing press. It must train 2 operators. It has two options.

- **Option A:** a 4-day off-the-job course at the supplier's training centre costing 1,100 per operator. Cover for each operator costs 150 a day.
- **Option B:** the supplier's technician trains both operators on the job, on site, for 5 days at 380 a day. During those days the press runs at 40% below normal output. A normal day's output earns the business 900 of contribution.

**(a)** Calculate the cost of each option. **[4]**
**(b)** Justify which option Pemberly Print should choose. **[6]**

**11.** Dunstan Foods plc has 1,200 employees and runs several food-processing plants with heavy machinery. Profits have fallen. The directors propose cutting the yearly training budget of 600,000 by 40%, mostly from refresher courses and off-the-job management training. Induction would be kept.

**(a)** Calculate the amount the business would save each year. **[1]**
**(b)** Evaluate whether Dunstan Foods should cut its training budget. **[9]**

**12.** Hallow Warehousing stores and dispatches goods for online retailers.

**(a)** Give two examples of health and safety training that Hallow Warehousing could provide. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain one reason why Hallow Warehousing should keep records of the training each employee has received. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Training that takes place away from the employee's normal work [1], for example at a college, on an external course or in a training room [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The key idea is that the worker stops doing the job in order to learn. Location alone (inside or outside the building) is not the test.

**2. (a)** Induction [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** On-the-job [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Off-the-job [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* In part (b) the assistant learns while doing real work in the real kitchen, which is the mark of on-the-job training.

**3.** Any two of: new or improved skills; greater confidence in the job; a qualification; better chance of promotion or higher pay; safer working [1 each]. **[2]**

**4. (a)** Fees: 7 × 45 = 315 [1]. Cover: 7 × 3 × 16 = 336 [1]. Total = 315 + 336 = **651** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** 280 × 0.25 [1] = **70** a month [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** 651 ÷ 70 = **9.3 months** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* The time away from the counter is a real cost even though it is not on the course invoice. Leaving it out is the most common error here.

**5.** Building work involves hazards such as working at height or with heavy machinery [1], so the firm must train employees in safe methods to comply with health and safety laws and avoid injuries, fines or legal action [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Outline" needs two linked points in context. Tie the reason to the dangers of building work, not to businesses in general.

**6.** The trainer is an experienced employee who must also do their own work [1], so their output falls while they teach [1], and if they work in a poor way, the trainee learns the same bad habits [1]. **[3]**

**7.** Trained employees make fewer mistakes [1], so less material is wasted and fewer products need correcting [1], which lowers costs and can raise profit [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Explain" questions have no context, so a general chain is fine. Each step must follow from the one before.

**8. (a)** Last year: 72 ÷ 400 × 100 = **18%** [1]. Following year: 48 ÷ 400 × 100 = **12%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** (72 − 48) = 24 fewer leavers [1]; 24 × 1,500 = **36,000** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** For example: trained employees work more productively [1], so each of the 400 staff handles more deliveries in the same time [1], which lowers the cost of each delivery [1] and lets Corlett offer competitive prices to its customers or earn more profit [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Analyse" needs a connected chain, and part (c) asks for a benefit other than the saving from fewer leavers. Repeating part (b) would score nothing.

**9.** Ruby paid for the course but lost the skills when the stylist left [1], so the money spent on training was wasted [1]. The new rival salon may take some of Ruby's customers, especially those the stylist served [1], so Ruby's revenue could fall while she also pays to recruit and train a replacement [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Good answers use the case: a small salon with 6 stylists loses a sixth of its team, and the rival is nearby.

**10. (a)** Option A: fees 2 × 1,100 = 2,200 [1]; cover 2 × 4 × 150 = 1,200, so total **3,400** [1]. Option B: technician 5 × 380 = 1,900 [1]; lost contribution 5 × 0.4 × 900 = 1,800, so total **3,700** [1]. **[4]**
**(b)** For Option A: it is 300 cheaper [1], and the operators learn at a training centre with no pressure from live orders [1]. Against Option A: the operators learn on the supplier's machines, which may not be set up the same way as Pemberly's own press [1]. For Option B: the operators learn on the actual press used for customers' jobs, so they can use it fully as soon as training ends [1]. Against Option B: output is 40% lower for 5 days, which could delay orders for a small business with few staff to cover [1]. Judgement: for example, choose Option B, because the extra 300 is small and learning on Pemberly's own press reduces the risk of mistakes on real orders afterwards; or Option A if keeping customer orders on time is the priority [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* "Justify" asks you to recommend one of two options. Use the figures from part (a), give a drawback of each option, and finish with a clear choice and the reason that decides it.

**11. (a)** 600,000 × 0.4 = **240,000** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** For cutting: profits have fallen, so saving 240,000 a year protects profit in the short term [1], and keeping induction means new starters still learn basic safety and rules [1]. Some management training may be postponed without immediate harm to output [1]. Against cutting: the plants use heavy machinery, so fewer refresher courses raise the risk of accidents [1], which could lead to injuries, fines or legal action and damage the company's reputation [1]. The business may not meet health and safety laws if staff are not kept up to date when equipment changes [1]. Fewer opportunities for development may lower motivation, so more of the 1,200 employees may leave [1], adding recruitment and induction costs that reduce the saving [1]. Judgement: for example, Dunstan should not cut refresher safety training because the risks to workers and compliance outweigh the saving, but it could delay some off-the-job management courses until profits recover [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* A strong evaluation separates the different kinds of training in the proposal. Treating "training" as one block misses the point that safety refresher courses carry legal and human risks that management courses do not.

**12. (a)** Any two of: safe lifting and handling; safe use of forklift trucks or other machinery; fire and evacuation procedures; use of protective clothing or equipment; reporting accidents and hazards [1 each]. **[2]**
**(b)** Records show which employees have been trained and when [1], so the business can see when refresher training is due [1] and can show that it has met its duty under health and safety laws if there is an accident or inspection [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling any training on the premises "on-the-job".
- Leaving cover or lost output out of the cost of training.
- Giving benefits to the business when the question asks about the worker.
- Writing about training in general when the question gives a specific business and its figures.
- Ending a "justify" or "evaluate" answer without a decision.
