## The task in one box

| Feature | Section B |
|---|---|
| Texts | The sixteen poems in Part 3 of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE English Anthology |
| Choice | One question from two; each compares two poems (one may let you choose the second) |
| Marks | 30 |
| Assessment objectives | AO2 (language, form, structure) and AO3 (comparison) |
| Recommended time | 40 minutes |

The anthology list changes only when Pearson revises the specification; check it for your issue.

## What the two objectives ask for

- **AO2:** analysis of language, form and structure and the meanings and effects they create, in
  both poems.
- **AO3:** links and connections between the poems, sustained through the whole essay. Credit comes
  from the **relationship** between the poems: what is shared, what differs, and why the difference
  matters.

Context is not one of the objectives here, so use it only in a phrase that supports a point about
the words on the page.

## The sixteen poems at a glance

| Poem (poet) | Focus | Methods to analyse |
|---|---|---|
| *If* (Rudyard Kipling) | Advice on self-control and integrity | One long conditional sentence; regular rhyme; the main clause withheld to the end |
| *Prayer Before Birth* (Louis MacNeice) | An unborn child's fears of a cruel world | Prayer form; repeated opening; stanzas that swell, ending on a short, stark line |
| *Blessing* (Imtiaz Dharker) | Water bursting from a broken pipe in a poor community | Short opening line; sound and listing; religious imagery for water |
| *Search For My Tongue* (Sujata Bhatt) | Fear of losing a mother tongue | Gujarati script set in the poem; extended metaphor of a tongue that grows back like a plant |
| *Half-past Two* (U A Fanthorpe) | A child kept in at school who cannot yet tell time | Third-person child's view; invented compound "time" words; a moment outside time |
| *Piano* (D H Lawrence) | An adult swept back to childhood by a song | Rhyming couplets; the senses of memory; struggle between present and past |
| *Hide and Seek* (Vernon Scannell) | A child whose hiding place becomes isolation | Second-person address; growing darkness; an ending where no one is waiting |
| *Sonnet 116* (William Shakespeare) | Love that does not change with time | Sonnet argument; navigation and time metaphors; closing couplet |
| *La Belle Dame sans Merci* (John Keats) | A knight enchanted and abandoned | Ballad form; question and answer frame; circular ending in a barren landscape |
| *Poem at Thirty-Nine* (Alice Walker) | An adult daughter remembering her father | Free verse; short lines; everyday detail turned into tribute |
| *War Photographer* (Carol Ann Duffy) | A photographer at home after war zones | Regular stanzas; darkroom and religious imagery; contrast with readers' indifference |
| *The Tyger* (William Blake) | Awe at the power that made the tiger | Repeated questions; strong rhythm; final stanza echoing the first with one changed word |
| *My Last Duchess* (Robert Browning) | A duke revealing his control over his late wife | Dramatic monologue; rhyming couplets broken by enjambment; what he lets slip |
| *Half-caste* (John Agard) | A challenge to a label for mixed heritage | Phonetic Caribbean voice; little punctuation; humour and direct questions |
| *Do not go gentle into that good night* (Dylan Thomas) | A plea to a dying father to resist death | Villanelle; two refrains; examples of different men facing death |
| *Remember* (Christina Rossetti) | A dying speaker's request to a loved one | Petrarchan sonnet; volta that changes the request from remembering to forgetting |

## The poems by form

| Form | Poem(s) | Comparison hook |
|---|---|---|
| Shakespearean sonnet | *Sonnet 116* | Argument resolved in a final couplet |
| Petrarchan sonnet | *Remember* | Turn after line 8 changes the request |
| Villanelle | *Do not go gentle into that good night* | Two refrains: insistence, urgency |
| Dramatic monologue | *My Last Duchess* | Speaker reveals more than he intends |
| Ballad | *La Belle Dame sans Merci* | Story told through question and answer |
| Rhyming couplets with repeated questions | *The Tyger* | Awe and unease built through questions |
| A single extended sentence of conditions | *If* | Advice held back until the end |
| Free or broken form | *Half-caste*, *Search For My Tongue* | Layout and language as part of the argument |

## Pairing grid (shared focus, different method)

- *Piano* + *Poem at Thirty-Nine*: an adult remembering a parent; nostalgia versus admiration.
- *Half-past Two* + *Hide and Seek*: a child's perspective; time and confusion versus isolation.
- *Sonnet 116* + *Remember*: lasting love; certainty versus selfless release.
- *My Last Duchess* + *La Belle Dame sans Merci*: power in relationships; control versus enchantment.
- *Search For My Tongue* + *Half-caste*: identity and language; loss and regrowth versus challenge.
- *Do not go gentle into that good night* + *Remember*: facing death; resistance versus acceptance.
- *War Photographer* + *Blessing*: suffering seen from outside; guilt and indifference versus joy amid hardship.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| AO3 | Exploring links and connections between texts: in Section B, sustained comparison |
| Comparative overview | An opening that states the main similarity and difference in one or two sentences |
| Point-by-point structure | Each paragraph compares both poems on one idea or method |
| Volta | A turn in thought, especially in a sonnet |
| Refrain | A repeated line, central to the villanelle |
| Dramatic monologue | A single character speaking to a silent listener |
| Comparative connective | Whereas, similarly, in contrast, both, unlike, however |

## Sentence frames

- "Both poets present X, but where Poet A uses ..., Poet B ..."
- "Unlike the controlled form of ..., the looser structure of ... suggests ..."
- "Similarly, ... yet the effect is different because ..."

## Annotated model paragraph

Question: *Compare how power in relationships is presented in* My Last Duchess *and* La Belle Dame
sans Merci.

> Both poems present a relationship in which one figure holds power over another, but Browning's
> Duke controls his wife, whereas Keats's knight is himself controlled. **[AO3: comparative
> overview]** Browning lets the Duke expose his power through the dramatic monologue: he alone
> draws the curtain from the portrait, so even in death the Duchess can be seen only when he
> allows it. **[AO2: form and detail]** His calm admission that he "gave commands" and then "all
> smiles stopped" hides a likely murder inside an even couplet, so the reader is chilled by what he
> leaves unsaid. **[AO2: language and structure]** Keats, by contrast, gives the voice to the
> powerless figure: the knight answers the questioner's opening stanzas, the pale kings of his dream
> warn that the lady holds him "in thrall", and the closing return to the withered sedge and silent
> birds traps him in a circle. **[AO2: ballad frame and cyclical structure; AO3: contrast of
> voice]** Whereas Browning invites us to judge his speaker, Keats invites us to pity his, so the
> two poems approach power from opposite ends of a relationship. **[AO3: comparative judgement]**

Every sentence keeps both poems in view or prepares the next comparison.

## Traps

- Two separate mini-essays with a comparison added at the end.
- Comparing only content, not methods and effects.
- Choosing a weakly connected second poem.
- Retelling the story of narrative poems.
- Running beyond 40 minutes because the poems feel familiar.
- Copying long lines from the poems provided in the exam instead of picking the word that matters.
- Writing about the poet's life instead of the poem's methods.
- Treating the speaker of a dramatic monologue as the poet.

## Self-test

1. What do the two assessment objectives in Section B require?
2. Name the form of *Do not go gentle into that good night* and one feature of it.
3. Where does the turn come in a Petrarchan sonnet such as *Remember*?
4. What three questions should you ask when choosing your own second poem?
5. What should the final paragraph do?
6. Which poem uses the Gujarati script, and what extended metaphor runs through it?
7. How does the structure of *If* hold back its main point?
8. In *My Last Duchess*, why does the dramatic monologue form matter?
9. What changes between the first and last stanzas of *The Tyger*?
10. Pair *Half-past Two* with another poem and give one similarity and one difference.
11. Is context assessed in Section B? How should you use it?

**Answers:** 1. AO2: analyse language, form and structure and their effects; AO3: compare the poems,
exploring links and differences. 2. A villanelle: nineteen lines with two repeated refrains. 3. After
the eighth line, between the octave and the sestet. 4. Does it share the focus, does it differ enough
in method, and do I know it well enough to quote quickly? 5. Make a comparative judgement (which
presentation is more hopeful, unsettling or convincing, and why) rather than repeat earlier points.
6. *Search For My Tongue*; the tongue (language) as a plant that seems to die but grows back. 7. It is
one long sentence of conditions, so the reward is delayed until the final lines. 8. The Duke speaks
to a silent listener and reveals more than he intends, so the reader judges him through his own
words. 9. The final stanza repeats the first but changes one word, turning a question about whether
the creator could make the tiger into whether he would dare to. 10. For example, with *Hide and
Seek*: both present a child's perspective, but Fanthorpe's child slips into a timeless moment while
Scannell's child is left isolated in the dark. 11. No; only AO2 and AO3 are assessed, so context
should appear only briefly, in support of a point about the poems.
