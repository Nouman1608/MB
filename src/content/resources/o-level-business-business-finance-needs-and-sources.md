## 1. Why businesses need finance (5.1.1)

Finance (also called capital) is money a business uses to pay for what it needs to operate and grow. The syllabus names three main reasons.

**Start-up capital.** A new business must pay for things before it earns any sales revenue. These include premises or a deposit, equipment, an opening stock of goods, licences and early advertising. The owner may also need money to live on while sales build up.

**Capital for expansion.** An existing business may want to open a second site, buy larger machines, launch a new product or sell in a new country. These plans cost money before they bring in extra revenue.

**Additional working capital.** Working capital is the money available for the day-to-day running of the business. A business needs it to buy materials, pay wages and pay bills while it waits for customers to pay. Growth in sales often raises the need for working capital, because the business has to buy more stock and wait longer in total for payment.

The syllabus gives these three as examples. Others, such as replacing worn-out equipment, can appear in a case study.

### Short-term and long-term needs

The syllabus asks you to understand the difference between short-term and long-term finance needs.

| | Short-term need | Long-term need |
|---|---|---|
| Typical period | A short time, often under a year | Several years or more |
| Typical purpose | Day-to-day costs, a temporary cash shortage, seasonal stock, paying suppliers on time | Buying premises or machinery, expansion, start-up |
| Repaid from | Cash coming in from sales in the coming weeks or months | Profits earned over many years from the assets bought |

The rule of matching: use short-term finance for short-term needs and long-term finance for long-term needs. If a business buys a machine that will last ten years using an overdraft that the bank can ask to be repaid at short notice, it takes an unnecessary risk.

## 2. Internal and external sources (5.1.2)

**Internal sources** come from inside the business or from its owners' own resources. **External sources** come from outside the business, from other people or organisations.

### Internal sources, with examples

- **Retained profit.** Profit kept in the business rather than paid out to the owners. It costs no interest and needs no one's permission, but it only exists if the business has made profit and it may be too small for a large project.
- **Owner's savings or capital.** Money the owner puts in. This is the usual first source for a sole trader. It is limited and puts personal wealth at risk.
- **Sale of assets.** Selling land, vehicles or machinery the business no longer needs. It brings in cash quickly without borrowing, but the business loses the asset and can only do it once.
- **Reducing stock or collecting debts sooner.** This turns stock and money owed by customers into cash. It helps short-term needs but may upset customers.

### External sources, with examples

- **Bank loan.** A fixed sum, repaid with interest over an agreed period. It gives a large amount for a long time, but interest must be paid whether or not the business is profitable, and the bank may ask for security.
- **Overdraft.** Permission to spend more than is in the bank account, up to an agreed limit. It is flexible and interest is charged only on the amount used, but interest rates can be high and the bank can withdraw the facility.
- **Trade credit.** Buying goods now and paying the supplier later. It is a form of short-term finance with no interest in many cases, but delaying payment too long can harm supplier relationships.
- **Share issue.** A company sells shares to new investors in exchange for money. This is equity finance, covered below.
- **Grants and investors who take a share in the business.** These are standard examples; the syllabus does not name them.

The syllabus itself names overdraft, debt, equity, micro-finance and crowd-funding. The other items are standard examples that fit its categories.

## 3. Short-term and long-term sources (5.1.2)

Sources are also classed by how long the business has the money.

| Source | Short-term or long-term | Internal or external |
|---|---|---|
| Overdraft | Short-term | External |
| Trade credit | Short-term | External |
| Reducing stock or collecting debts sooner | Short-term | Internal |
| Bank loan (several years) | Long-term | External |
| Share issue (equity) | Long-term | External |
| Retained profit | Long-term (stays in the business) | Internal |
| Sale of assets | One-off, usually long-term | Internal |

Be ready to classify the same source both ways.

### Debt and equity

The syllabus names debt and equity as long-term sources.

- **Debt** is money borrowed from a lender that has to be paid back, usually with interest added. An example is a long-term bank loan. The lender does not become an owner and does not share in the profit. The interest cost continues even in a bad year.
- **Equity** is money put in by owners, either as share capital from shareholders or as an owner's own capital. Nothing has to be repaid on a set date. Shareholders expect a reward as dividends when the company makes profit, and new shareholders share ownership, which can reduce the original owners' control.

A simple loan illustration (simple interest, for teaching only; real loans may calculate interest differently). Ravenwood Print Studio borrows $24 000 at 8% a year for 3 years.

1. Interest per year = 24 000 × 8% = $1 920.
2. Interest over 3 years = $5 760.
3. Total to repay = 24 000 + 5 760 = $29 760.
4. Spread over 36 months this is about $826.67 a month.

The business must find this from its cash flow each month.

## 4. Alternative sources of capital (5.1.2)

Banks do not lend to every business. A small or new one may have no security or track record. The syllabus names two alternative sources.

**Micro-finance** means very small loans, often made to people who cannot get ordinary bank loans, usually for small businesses such as a market stall, a sewing workshop or a small farm. It lets people start or expand a business who would otherwise be shut out. The amounts are small, and repayments can still be heavy for a tiny business.

**Crowd-funding** means raising small amounts of money from a large number of people, usually through a website. The supporters may receive a product, a reward or a share in the business in return. It matters because it needs no bank, it lets the business test demand for a product before producing it, and it spreads the risk. Drawbacks: the target may not be met, and the idea becomes public.

They matter most to small, new or risky businesses and to those that cannot meet a bank's conditions.

## 5. Factors in choosing a source (5.1.2)

The syllabus lists four main factors. Use the word "because" every time you apply one.

1. **Size and legal form of business.** A sole trader cannot issue shares, so relies on savings, loans and retained profit. A company can raise equity by selling shares; a public company can offer them to the general public, whereas a private company's shares cannot be sold to the public in the same way. Small businesses often have fewer choices.
2. **Amount required.** A small sum may be met by an overdraft or retained profit. A very large sum for expansion may need a share issue or a large long-term loan.
3. **Length of time.** Match the period of the source to the period of the need. Short need, short-term source; long need, long-term source.
4. **Existing loans.** If a business already has large borrowings, lenders may refuse more or charge more, and repayments may already strain cash flow. In that case, equity or internal sources may be better.

Cost, speed and loss of control can add support, but do not replace these four.

## 6. Recommend and justify (5.1.2)

Commands such as "recommend" and "justify" test AO4 evaluation. A strong answer has four parts.

1. State the amount, purpose and period from the case.
2. Name the source and say whether it is internal or external, short-term or long-term.
3. Link it to at least one factor from section 5, using the case facts.
4. Show you have weighed it: give a drawback or an alternative you rejected, and say why yours is better here.

### Worked example

Larkhollow Bikes is a sole trader's workshop. The owner, Mirela, wants a cargo bike for deliveries costing 14 500 dollars. She can spare 6 000 dollars of savings and already has a loan whose repayments take most of her profit. The bike will be used for at least five years.

**Recommendation:** use her own savings plus a bank loan repaid over several years.

1. The amount needed from outside is 14 500 − 6 000 = 8 500 dollars, about 59% of the total.
2. The need is long-term (five years or more), so a long-term source fits. An overdraft would be wrong because it is short-term and can be recalled.
3. As a sole trader she cannot issue shares, so equity from new shareholders is not available.
4. Her existing loan is a risk: more debt would raise her repayments. This is why she should put in her own savings first and borrow no more than she must. If the bank refuses, crowd-funding could be an alternative, because the delivery service is popular locally, but it is uncertain.

**Judgement:** a smaller long-term loan plus her own money is best, because it matches the length of the need, keeps borrowing as low as possible given the existing loan, and is available to a sole trader.

## Common errors

- Calling an overdraft long-term finance, or a bank loan always short-term. Match each to its usual use.
- Saying internal sources are free. Retained profit could have been paid to the owners.
- Listing sources without linking them to the case facts.
- Mixing up debt and equity: shareholders are owners; lenders are not.
- Saying crowd-funding and micro-finance are the same. Micro-finance is small loans; crowd-funding is many small contributions.
- Recommending a source that the legal form rules out, such as a share issue for a sole trader.
