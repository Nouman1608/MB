This guide covers sub-topic **4.1 Capital and revenue expenditure and receipts**. The syllabus lists six points: the distinction between capital expenditure and revenue expenditure, and how to account for each; the distinction between capital receipts and revenue receipts, and how to account for each; and "how to identify and calculate the effect on profit of incorrect treatment" and "the effect on asset valuations of incorrect treatment".

The examples follow Brookfield Print Works, owned by Ama Mensah. Amounts are in dollars. Use this guide with the [4.1 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-accounting-0452-capital-and-revenue-expenditure-and-receipts-revision-notes/) and [4.1 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-accounting-0452-capital-and-revenue-expenditure-and-receipts-practice/). Capital expenditure is spread over later years through [depreciation](/resources/cambridge-igcse-accounting-0452-accounting-for-depreciation-and-disposal-of-non-current/).

## 1. Capital expenditure and revenue expenditure

**Capital expenditure** is spending on buying non-current assets, or on improving them so that they do more or last longer. It includes every cost of getting an asset into place and ready for use: delivery, installation, legal fees on buying premises, and testing.

**Revenue expenditure** is spending on the day-to-day running of the business. It includes repairs and maintenance that keep an asset working as before, fuel, insurance, wages and the cost of goods for resale.

The test is what the spending achieves. If it gives the business a new asset or a better one for future years, it is capital. If it is used up in running the business this period, or only restores an asset to its previous condition, it is revenue.

### Worked example 1: a new printing press

In 2027 Brookfield buys a large printing press and receives these bills.

| Item | $ | Treatment | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Purchase price of the press | 32,000 | Capital | Cost of the asset |
| Delivery to the works | 650 | Capital | Getting the asset into place |
| Installation | 1,400 | Capital | Making it ready for use |
| Paper and ink used in test runs | 300 | Capital | Testing before use |
| Extension to the building to house the press | 18,000 | Capital | Improves an existing asset |
| One year's insurance on the press | 820 | Revenue | A running cost of one year |
| Annual service contract | 480 | Revenue | Maintenance |
| Training the operator | 750 | Revenue | Staff cost, not part of the asset |
| Repainting the existing office | 2,200 | Revenue | Restores, does not improve |

Capital expenditure: 32,000 + 650 + 1,400 + 300 + 18,000 = **52,350**. The press account is debited with 34,350 and premises with 18,000. Revenue expenditure: 820 + 480 + 750 + 2,200 = **4,250**, charged to expense accounts.

**How each is accounted for.** Capital expenditure is debited to a non-current asset account and shown in the statement of financial position; its cost reaches the statement of profit or loss gradually, as depreciation. Revenue expenditure is debited to an expense account and transferred in full to the statement of profit or loss for the year.

## 2. Capital receipts and revenue receipts

**Capital receipts** are amounts received that do not come from the normal trading activity: capital introduced by the owner, loans received, and the proceeds from selling a non-current asset.

**Revenue receipts** come from normal business activities and are income of the period: sales, rent received, commission received, interest received and discount received.

| Receipt | Type | Where it is recorded |
|---|---|---|
| Owner pays in 8,000 more capital | Capital | Credit capital account |
| Bank loan of 15,000 received | Capital | Credit loan account (a liability) |
| Old guillotine sold for 1,100 | Capital | Credit disposal account; only any profit or loss on disposal reaches the statement of profit or loss |
| Printing jobs for customers | Revenue | Credit sales |
| Rent from a tenant using spare space | Revenue | Credit rent received |
| Commission for passing work to another printer | Revenue | Credit commission received |

Capital receipts affect the statement of financial position: equity, liabilities or assets. Revenue receipts are income in the statement of profit or loss.

## 3. The effect of incorrect treatment

Every mistake between capital and revenue moves an amount between the two statements. The rule of thumb:

| Mistake | Profit | Asset valuation |
|---|---|---|
| Capital expenditure treated as revenue | Understated | Non-current assets understated |
| Revenue expenditure treated as capital | Overstated | Non-current assets overstated |
| Capital receipt treated as revenue | Overstated | Depends on the receipt (see below) |
| Revenue receipt treated as capital | Understated | Usually no effect on assets |

When depreciation is involved, the amount is not simply the cost: a wrongly capitalised item would have been depreciated, and a wrongly expensed asset has missed its depreciation.

### Worked example 2: four mistakes at Brookfield

Brookfield depreciates machinery at 20% of cost per year, with a full year's charge in the year of purchase. It does not depreciate premises. Its draft profit for 2027 is 46,900.

**(a)** The press installation of 1,400 was debited to repairs. Profit was charged with 1,400 instead of depreciation of 1,400 x 20% = 280. Profit is **understated by 1,120**. The press is shown at 1,400 - 280 = 1,120 too little, so non-current assets are **understated by 1,120**.

**(b)** Repainting the office, 2,200, was debited to premises. No expense was charged, and premises are not depreciated. Profit is **overstated by 2,200**; non-current assets are **overstated by 2,200**.

**(c)** The bank loan of 15,000 was credited to sales. Profit is **overstated by 15,000**. Assets are correct (the bank balance is right), but non-current liabilities are **understated by 15,000**.

**(d)** Rent received of 1,500 was credited to the capital account. Profit is **understated by 1,500**. Assets are unaffected. Ama's closing capital is still correct, because the 1,500 was added to capital directly instead of through profit.

| Correction | Profit | Non-current assets |
|---|---|---|
| Draft profit | 46,900 | |
| (a) Installation expensed | +1,120 | +1,120 |
| (b) Repainting capitalised | -2,200 | -2,200 |
| (c) Loan credited to sales | -15,000 | No effect |
| (d) Rent received credited to capital | +1,500 | No effect |
| **Corrected profit** | **32,320** | Net change -1,080 |

Corrected profit: 46,900 + 1,120 - 2,200 - 15,000 + 1,500 = 32,320. Ama might have taken drawings or applied for a loan believing she earned 46,900. Getting the classification right matters because profit drives these decisions, and because the matching concept requires the cost of an asset to be spread over the years it helps to earn revenue.

## Common mistakes

- Treating delivery, installation or testing of a new asset as revenue expenditure.
- Treating repairs, servicing or redecoration as capital because the asset is a non-current asset.
- Calling the full sale proceeds of an old asset revenue; only a profit on disposal is income.
- Ignoring depreciation when calculating the effect of wrongly capitalised or wrongly expensed items, when a policy is given.
- Saying a loan credited to sales overstates assets; it is liabilities that are wrong.

## Exam technique

- **Identify** questions often give a list of costs: classify every line and say why in a few words, using "gets the asset ready for use" or "restores, does not improve".
- To **calculate** the effect of incorrect treatment, state the direction (overstated or understated), the statement and the amount, for example: "profit for the year is understated by 1,120".
- Where a question gives a depreciation policy, apply it to the item in dispute before stating the effect.
- In **explain** questions, link the classification to the matching concept: capital spending benefits several years, so its cost is spread over them.
