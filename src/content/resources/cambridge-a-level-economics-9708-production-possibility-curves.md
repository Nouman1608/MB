This guide covers sub-topic **1.5 Production possibility curves** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Economics 9708 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. It is AS Level content. The syllabus lists four points: the nature and meaning of a production possibility curve (1.5.1); the shape of the PPC, constant and increasing opportunity costs (1.5.2); causes and consequences of shifts in a PPC (1.5.3); and the significance of a position within a PPC (1.5.4). This guide concentrates on reading opportunity cost from data, on shifts that affect only one good, and on the consequences of choices between capital and consumer goods, which are where answers usually lose marks. All countries and data are invented.

## 1.5.1 What a PPC shows

A **production possibility curve** shows the maximum combinations of two goods (or two groups of goods) that an economy can produce in a given period when all its resources are fully and efficiently employed, with its current technology.

It is a picture of 1.1. **Scarcity** appears as the boundary itself: combinations beyond it cannot be produced. **Choice** appears as the need to pick one point. **Opportunity cost** appears as the amount of one good given up to move along the curve and produce more of the other.

Drawing checklist:

- label each axis with a good and its unit, never "price" and "quantity";
- draw the curve meeting both axes;
- mark and label the points the question uses.

## 1.5.2 The shape of the PPC

**Worked example 1: Country Selm.** Selm produces rice (million tonnes) and computers (thousand units).

| Point | Rice | Computers | Computers given up for the extra 10 rice | Opportunity cost of 1 tonne of rice |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 0 | 80 | — | — |
| B | 10 | 76 | 4 | 0.4 thousand computers |
| C | 20 | 68 | 8 | 0.8 |
| D | 30 | 54 | 14 | 1.4 |
| E | 40 | 32 | 22 | 2.2 |
| F | 50 | 0 | 32 | 3.2 |

The opportunity cost of rice rises at every step: this is **increasing opportunity cost**, and it makes the curve **concave to the origin** (bowed outwards). The reason is that resources are **not equally suited** to both goods. The first workers and land moved into rice are those least useful in making computers, such as fertile farmland and farm workers. As Selm produces more rice, it must switch skilled engineers and factory sites, which add little to rice output but were very productive in computers.

**Constant opportunity cost.** If resources were equally suited to both goods, every 10 tonnes of extra rice would cost the same number of computers and the PPC would be a **straight line**. Example: a workshop whose staff can make either 60 tables or 120 chairs a week, with any mix in between at 2 chairs per table.

**The reciprocal.** The opportunity cost of computers in terms of rice is the reciprocal. Between C and D, 1 tonne of rice costs 1.4 thousand computers, so 1 thousand computers costs 1 ÷ 1.4 = **0.71 million tonnes** of rice.

## 1.5.3 Causes and consequences of shifts

A **shift** means the economy's productive capacity has changed. Causes:

| Outward shift (growth in capacity) | Inward shift (loss of capacity) |
|---|---|
| More factors: population growth, immigration of workers, discovery of new natural resources | Natural disasters, such as earthquakes or floods, destroying capital and land |
| Better factors: education and training (human capital), investment in new capital | War or conflict |
| Improved technology | Emigration of skilled workers |
| Better organisation and enterprise | Depletion of non-renewable resources |

**Shifts that affect one good only.** A change in technology often helps one industry. **Worked example 2:** a new chip design raises Selm's computer output by 25% at every allocation of resources, while rice farming is unchanged.

| Point | Rice | Computers before | Computers after |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 0 | 80 | 100 |
| C | 20 | 68 | 85 |
| D | 30 | 54 | 67.5 |
| F | 50 | 0 | 0 |

The curve pivots outwards along the computer axis but still meets the rice axis at 50. Two consequences follow.

- Selm can now have more computers with the same rice, or the same computers with more rice: at 68 thousand computers it can now produce more than 20 million tonnes of rice. So even rice-lovers gain.
- The **opportunity cost of rice rises**. Between C and D, 10 extra tonnes of rice now cost 85 − 67.5 = 17.5 thousand computers, or 1.75 per tonne, up from 1.4. Each tonne of rice means giving up more of a good the economy has become better at making.

**Consequences of choosing capital goods.** Draw a PPC with **capital goods** on one axis and **consumer goods** on the other. Choosing more capital goods now means less consumption today, but more capital raises capacity, shifting the curve further out in future.

**Worked example 3.** Two invented countries start with identical PPCs. Country Halo chooses a point with a high share of capital goods and, by assumption, its capacity grows 4% a year; Country Ivor chooses mainly consumer goods and grows 1% a year. After 10 years, Halo's capacity is 1.04¹⁰ = **1.48 times** its original level and Ivor's is 1.01¹⁰ = **1.10 times**. Halo's citizens accepted lower consumption at first in exchange for a much larger PPC later. This is the key concept of time applied to 1.5.

Shifts also have wider consequences: an outward shift can mean higher living standards and more employment; an inward shift means lower output whatever the allocation.

## 1.5.4 The significance of a position

| Position | Significance |
|---|---|
| On the curve | All resources fully and efficiently employed; more of one good requires less of the other |
| Inside the curve | Unemployment of resources or inefficient use; more of both goods is possible at no opportunity cost |
| Outside the curve | Unattainable with current resources and technology |

**Worked example 4.** Selm produces 20 million tonnes of rice and 50 thousand computers. Point C shows that with 20 rice it could make 68 thousand computers. Selm is **inside** its PPC: it could produce 18 thousand more computers without giving up any rice. Causes might include unemployed engineers in a recession or factories using outdated methods. Moving to the curve has **no opportunity cost**, which is why reducing unemployment is so valuable.

**A movement from inside to the curve is not a shift.** Capacity is unchanged; existing resources are simply being used. Many answers wrongly describe a fall in unemployment as an outward shift.

**On the curve does not mean "best".** Every point on the curve is efficient in production, but the curve alone cannot say which point society should choose. That depends on what people want, which is a different question.

## Common mistakes

- Labelling axes "price" and "quantity".
- Explaining the concave shape by "diminishing returns" rather than resources not being equally suited to both goods.
- Describing a fall in unemployment as an outward shift.
- Shifting the whole curve when only one industry's technology has improved.
- Forgetting that a point inside the curve allows more of both goods at no opportunity cost.
- Reading opportunity cost as total output rather than the amount given up per extra unit.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate:** opportunity cost = units of good Y given up ÷ extra units of good X gained. Show the subtraction first.
- **Explain, with a diagram:** for a shift, name the cause, draw the new curve, and say whether it pivots or shifts parallel.
- **Analyse:** link the shape to how suitable resources are for each good.
- **Assess** or **Evaluate:** for capital versus consumer goods choices, weigh present sacrifice against future growth, and note that the growth gain depends on the capital being used productively.
