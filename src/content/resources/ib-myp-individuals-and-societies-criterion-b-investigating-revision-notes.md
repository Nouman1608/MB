## What the guide states

- Students develop systematic research skills and processes associated with disciplines in the humanities and social sciences, and strategies for investigating independently and in collaboration.
- Year 5 strands: (i) formulate a clear and focused research question and justify its relevance; (ii) formulate and follow an action plan; (iii) use research methods to collect and record appropriate, varied and relevant information; (iv) evaluate the process and results.
- Maximum 8, in bands 1-2, 3-4, 5-6 and 7-8.
- In the on-screen examination, the Investigating task is worth 26 marks (20 for Criterion B, 6 for Criterion A), out of 80 for the whole examination.

## Top-band wording (Year 5, 7-8)

| Strand | Descriptor | Plain words |
|---|---|---|
| i | a clear and focused research question, thoroughly justifying its relevance with appropriate evidence | specific question and reasons backed by evidence |
| ii | formulates and effectively follows a comprehensive action plan | detailed plan, and you stick to it (or revise it with reasons) |
| iii | research methods to collect and record appropriate, varied and relevant information | right methods, more than one type, nothing irrelevant |
| iv | thoroughly evaluates the investigation process and results | what worked, what did not, how far the question was answered |

Lower bands: question clear or focused (1-2), clear and focused with relevance described (3-4), relevance explained (5-6); plan limited (1-2), partial (3-4), substantial (5-6); information limited (1-2), mostly relevant (3-4), appropriate and relevant (5-6); evaluation limited (1-2), some aspects (3-4), the process and results (5-6).

## Key terms

- **Research question**: the starting point of an investigation. It guides the scope and nature of the research.
- **Action plan**: the steps and information defined to complete the investigation. It may include sub-questions, methods, sources of information and main stages. It can be revised as findings come in.
- **Research methods**: systematic steps for finding relevant information, including selection of sources, questionnaires, surveys, interviews, observation, experiments, measurement and analysis of quantitative data.
- **Primary source**: information you collect yourself (survey, interview, observation).
- **Secondary source**: information collected by others (reports, statistics, books).
- **Sample**: the group of people or items you collect information from.
- **Relevance**: why the question matters, shown with evidence.

## A good research question: checklist

The guide lists seven things to consider.

1. Relevance
2. Manageability
3. Originality
4. Ability to be assessed
5. Availability of resources
6. Student interest
7. Connection with the discipline or subject group

A strong question names the **topic, place, time and aspect**. Compare "Tourism" with "To what extent has the growth of tourism since 2010 improved or worsened daily life for residents of a named coastal town, in terms of jobs, housing costs and crowding?"

## Justifying relevance

Use three steps.

1. **Why it matters**: to whom and why now.
2. **Evidence**: a statistic, a report or a news item that shows the issue is real.
3. **Link**: how it connects to your course, for example the unit on tourism or trade.

## Action plan: components

| Component | What to write |
|---|---|
| Sub-questions | three or four smaller questions that together answer the main one |
| Methods | one for each sub-question, and more than one type overall |
| Sources | named, with type and range |
| Sample | who and how many, and why |
| Timeline | stages with dates or weeks |
| Ethics and safety | permission, anonymity, safe locations |
| Presentation | graphs, maps, report, quotations |
| Review points | when and how the plan will be revised |

## Choosing methods

| Method | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Questionnaire | many opinions, countable | biased wording, small sample |
| Interview | depth, explanation | one view, takes time |
| Observation | real behaviour | single day may not be typical |
| Secondary data | trends and numbers | may be out of date or one-sided |
| Fieldwork measurement | first-hand data | needs accuracy and sampling |

Use at least two different kinds so that findings can be compared (triangulation).

## Recording information

- Source log: author or organisation, title, date, location or URL, why used.
- Notes table: source, key information, origin and purpose, link to sub-question.
- Quotations copied exactly, with page numbers.
- Raw data kept in tables with units and dates.
- Only relevant information recorded.

## Evaluating the investigation

- **Process**: methods, sample, timing, problems met and how they were handled.
- **Results**: how fully the question was answered, how reliable and complete the evidence is, whether sources agree.
- **Improvements**: specific changes (larger sample, additional interview, different time of day).
- **Next steps**: a follow-up question.

Use the frame: "The method of ... was effective because ... but limited because ..., which means that ... I would improve this by ..."

## Command terms

| Term | Guide meaning |
|---|---|
| Formulate | express precisely and systematically the relevant concepts or arguments |
| Justify | give valid reasons or evidence to support an answer or conclusion |
| Investigate | observe, study or make a detailed and systematic examination, in order to establish facts and reach new conclusions |
| Explore | undertake a systematic process of discovery |
| Evaluate | make an appraisal by weighing up strengths and limitations |
| Reflect (glossary) | think about deeply; consider |

## Where marks are usually lost

- A topic or a vague question.
- Relevance stated with no evidence.
- A plan with methods but no sub-questions, sample or dates.
- Methods that cannot answer the question.
- Only one type of source.
- Not recording sources.
- An evaluation that is only descriptive.

## Quick self-test

1. Say why "Is climate change bad?" is not a good research question.
2. Rewrite "Migration" as a focused question for a named country of your choice.
3. Give one strength and one limitation of a questionnaire.
4. Name two items that belong in an action plan.
5. Write one sentence evaluating a small sample.

### Answers

1. It is too broad and has no place or time. It is a judgment, not a question that can be investigated with evidence, and "bad" for whom or in what way is not stated.
2. For example: "What were the main push and pull factors behind the migration of workers from rural areas to the capital city of a named country between 2000 and 2020?"
3. Strength: it collects views from many people and the answers can be counted. Limitation: wording can lead respondents, and a small or unrepresentative sample gives biased results.
4. Any two of: sub-questions, methods, sources, sample, timeline, ethics and safety, presentation.
5. For example: "Only 12 people answered, all from one street, so the results may not represent the whole town, and the conclusion should be treated with caution."

## Frequently asked questions

**Can my research question change during the investigation?**
Your action plan can be revised depending on what you find. If the question changes, explain why in your log and in the evaluation.

**Do I need primary and secondary sources?**
Using both is usually the best way to show varied information, but choose the sources that suit your question.

**How long should the evaluation be?**
Long enough to cover the process and the results, with specific examples. A paragraph for each is often enough.
