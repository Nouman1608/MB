Enzymes are globular proteins that act as biological catalysts: they speed up metabolic reactions by lowering the
activation energy, without being used up. For OCR A Level Biology A (H420) section 2.1.4 you must explain how
enzymes work (lock and key and induced fit), how pH, temperature, enzyme concentration and substrate concentration
change their activity, calculate the temperature coefficient, and explain the roles of cofactors, coenzymes and
inhibitors, including the practical work in PAG4.

This guide covers learning outcomes 2.1.4(a) to (f) of the OCR A Level Biology A specification, Version 4.1 (April
2026), first assessment 2025. It sits in Module 2, Foundations in biology, which is assessed across all three A Level
papers. Use the [enzymes revision notes](/resources/ocr-a-level-biology-enzymes-revision-notes/) for quick recall and

## Why do organisms need enzymes? (a) and (b)

Metabolism is the sum of all the reactions in an organism. At body temperature most of these reactions would be far
too slow without catalysts. Enzymes control both **structure** (for example, enzymes build the polymers that make
cell walls and collagen) and **function** (for example, enzymes in respiration release energy).

| | Intracellular enzyme | Extracellular enzyme |
|---|---|---|
| Where it acts | Inside the cell that made it | Outside the cell that made it |
| OCR example | **Catalase**, which breaks down toxic hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen | **Amylase**, secreted in saliva and by the pancreas, which hydrolyses starch to maltose |

## How do enzymes work? (c)

Each enzyme has a specific **tertiary structure** that forms an **active site**, a small region with a shape
complementary to its substrate. This gives enzymes their **specificity**.

- **Lock and key hypothesis.** The substrate fits exactly into a rigid active site, like a key in a lock.
- **Induced-fit hypothesis.** The active site is not exactly complementary at first. As the substrate binds, the
  active site changes shape slightly to fit more closely. This puts strain on bonds in the substrate, lowering the
  activation energy. Induced fit is the currently accepted model because it explains why some enzymes can bind
  slightly different substrates and how bonds are strained.

The sequence is: enzyme + substrate, then **enzyme-substrate complex**, then **enzyme-product complex**, then enzyme
+ product. The enzyme is unchanged and can be reused. Enzymes speed up reactions by providing a pathway with a
**lower activation energy**.

## How do temperature and pH affect enzymes? (d)(i)

### Temperature

As temperature rises, enzyme and substrate molecules gain kinetic energy, so they collide more often and more
enzyme-substrate complexes form per second: the rate increases. Above the **optimum**, vibrations break hydrogen
bonds and ionic bonds that hold the tertiary structure, so the active site changes shape and the substrate no
longer fits. The enzyme is **denatured**, and the rate falls steeply. At low temperatures enzymes are inactive but
not denatured.

### The temperature coefficient

The temperature coefficient (Q₁₀) compares the rate of a reaction at two temperatures 10 degrees Celsius apart:

```
Q₁₀ = R2 / R1
R2 = rate at (T + 10) degrees Celsius
R1 = rate at T degrees Celsius
```

**Worked example 1.** An enzyme-controlled reaction has a rate of 0.84 units per minute at 20 degrees Celsius and
1.68 units per minute at 30 degrees Celsius. Q₁₀ = 1.68 / 0.84 = **2.0**, so the rate doubles for a 10 degree
rise. For many enzyme-controlled reactions below the optimum, the value is around 2. Above the optimum, the value
falls below 1 because denaturation reduces the rate.

### pH

Each enzyme has an **optimum pH**. Hydrogen ions and hydroxide ions interact with the charged R groups of amino
acids, disrupting **ionic bonds and hydrogen bonds** in the tertiary structure. Small changes from the optimum
reduce activity because the charges in the active site change and the substrate binds less well; large changes
denature the enzyme.

## How do enzyme and substrate concentration affect the rate?

- **Substrate concentration.** As substrate concentration rises, the rate increases because more collisions form
  enzyme-substrate complexes. Eventually all active sites are occupied at any moment (the enzyme is saturated), so
  the rate levels off at a maximum (Vmax). Enzyme concentration is then the limiting factor.
- **Enzyme concentration.** With excess substrate, the rate is proportional to enzyme concentration, because more
  active sites are available. If substrate is limited, adding more enzyme eventually has no effect.

## How do you investigate enzyme activity? (d)(ii) and PAG4

Typical PAG4 investigations use catalase (from yeast or potato) with hydrogen peroxide, measuring the volume of
oxygen in a gas syringe or upturned measuring cylinder, or amylase with starch, timing how long until a sample no
longer turns iodine solution blue-black.

**Serial dilutions.** To make a range of concentrations, each solution is diluted by the same factor from the one
before. For a tenfold series, mix 1 cm cubed of 1.0% enzyme with 9 cm cubed of buffer to make 0.1%, then 1 cm cubed of
that with 9 cm cubed of buffer to make 0.01%, and so on.

**Worked example 2 (initial rate).** In a catalase experiment, 24 cm cubed of oxygen is collected in the first 30 s,
when the curve is still a straight line. Initial rate = 24 / 30 = **0.8 cm cubed per second**. On a curved graph,
draw a tangent at time zero and use its gradient.

**Worked example 3 (rate from time).** If the starch in an amylase experiment disappears after 40 s, a relative
rate is 1 / time = 1 / 40 = **0.025 per second**.

Control variables include the volume and concentration of the other reactant, pH (using a **buffer**) and
temperature (using a water bath). A control with boiled (denatured) enzyme shows that the enzyme is responsible.

## Why do some enzymes need cofactors and coenzymes? (e)

Some enzymes only work when another non-protein substance is present.

| Term | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Cofactor | An inorganic ion that helps the enzyme–substrate complex form or makes the active site the right shape | **Chloride ions for amylase** |
| Coenzyme | A small organic molecule, often derived from a **vitamin**, that carries chemical groups or atoms between enzymes | NAD, derived from the vitamin niacin |
| Prosthetic group | A cofactor permanently (tightly) bound to the enzyme | Zinc ions in carbonic anhydrase |

## How do inhibitors affect enzyme action? (f)

| Type | Where it binds | Effect | Overcome by more substrate? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Competitive | The **active site**; its shape is similar to the substrate | Fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form | Yes: at high substrate concentration the rate approaches the uninhibited maximum |
| Non-competitive | An **allosteric site**, away from the active site | Changes the tertiary structure, so the active site changes shape | No: the maximum rate is lowered |
| Reversible | Held by weak bonds (for example hydrogen or ionic bonds) | The inhibitor can leave, so activity returns | Depends on type |
| Non-reversible | Held by strong (covalent) bonds | Enzyme permanently inactivated | No |

**End-product inhibition.** In a metabolic pathway, the final product inhibits an enzyme early in the pathway. When
there is plenty of product, the pathway slows; when product is used up, the inhibition is removed. This is
negative feedback and stops resources being wasted. For example, ATP inhibits phosphofructokinase, an enzyme
early in glycolysis.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is the induced-fit model preferred to lock and key?**
It explains how binding strains the substrate's bonds and lowers activation energy, and why the active site can
change shape. The lock and key model treats the active site as rigid.

**What is the difference between a cofactor and a coenzyme?**
A cofactor is an inorganic ion, such as chloride for amylase. A coenzyme is an organic molecule, often made from a
vitamin, that carries atoms or groups between reactions.

**How do I calculate the temperature coefficient?**
Divide the rate at the higher temperature by the rate at a temperature 10 degrees Celsius lower. A value of 2 means
the rate doubles.

**Why does the rate level off at high substrate concentration?**
All active sites are occupied at any one time, so the enzyme concentration limits the rate.

## Common mistakes

- Saying enzymes are "killed" at high temperature; they are **denatured**.
- Saying the active site is the "same shape" as the substrate; it is **complementary**.
- Explaining denaturation without naming the bonds broken in the tertiary structure.
- Saying a competitive inhibitor lowers the maximum rate permanently.
- Reading a rate from the end of a curve instead of the initial gradient.
- Forgetting to use a buffer to control pH in a temperature investigation.
