## Questions

**(a)** Define the terms:
- (i) *meritocracy* **[2]**
- (ii) *reserve army of labour* **[2]**

**(b)** Give **two** examples of ascribed status. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **three** ways social class may affect a person's health. **[6]**

**(d)** Explain **three** ways labelling may create or reinforce social inequality. **[6]**

**(e)** Discuss the Marxist explanation of social inequality. **[8]**

**(f)** Evaluate the view that social class is the most important influence on life chances.
**[14]**

### Extra practice (3.1.1 to 3.1.4)

**(g)** Explain the difference between absolute poverty and relative poverty. **[4]**

**(h)** Explain **two** ways the idea of intersectionality helps sociologists understand inequality.
**[4]**

**(i)** Explain **two** reasons why inherited wealth may make a society less open. **[4]**

**(j)** Explain how a self-fulfilling prophecy may affect a student's life chances. **[4]**

**(k)** Explain **two** ways age may affect a person's life chances in employment or housing. **[4]**

## Answers and mark guidance

**(a)(i) [2]** A system in which people's positions depend on their talent and effort (1) rather than
on their family background or other ascribed characteristics (1).

**(a)(ii) [2]** A pool of unemployed or insecure workers that employers can hire when needed and
dismiss when not (1); Marxists argue its existence keeps wages low and workers obedient (1).

**(b) [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: sex; ethnicity; caste; age; a royal or noble title; the family a
person is born into.

**(c) [6]** Three ways, 2 marks each (identify + explain):

- **Housing** — poorer families are more likely to live in damp or overcrowded homes, which is linked
  to illnesses such as respiratory conditions.
- **Diet** — lower income limits access to fresh, healthy food, raising risks of poor nutrition.
- **Working conditions** — manual jobs carry greater risk of injury and physical strain.
- **Access to care** — where healthcare must be paid for, poorer people may delay treatment.
- **Stress** — insecure work and debt raise stress, affecting physical and mental health.
- **Evidence** — the Black Report (1980) and Marmot Review (2010) found a class gradient in health.

**(d) [6]** Three ways, 2 marks each:

- **Stereotypes** — powerful groups such as employers or teachers may judge people by simplified
  views of their class, ethnicity or gender, leading to unfair treatment in hiring or setting.
- **Master status** — a label such as "unemployed" or "ex-offender" overrides other qualities, making
  it harder to gain work or housing (Becker, 1963).
- **Self-fulfilling prophecy** — low expectations lead people to act in line with the label, as
  Rosenthal and Jacobson's study (1968) suggested for teachers' expectations.
- **Media labelling** — negative portrayals of poor groups can shape public attitudes and policy.

**(e) [8]** Levels: 7–8 a clear, developed account using several Marxist concepts with evaluation;
4–6 some explanation; 1–3 basic points.

- The **bourgeoisie** own the means of production; the **proletariat** sell their labour.
- **Exploitation:** workers are paid less than the value of what they produce, and capitalists keep
  the surplus as profit, so wealth concentrates at the top.
- **Ideological control:** education, religion and media spread ideas that make inequality seem fair,
  producing false class consciousness.
- **Reserve army of labour:** unemployment keeps wages low and workers fearful.
- **Poverty trap:** benefit and tax rules can make it hard for poor people to escape poverty.
- **Evaluation:** explains persistent wealth inequality; but underplays gender, ethnicity and age;
  many workers have experienced mobility and rising living standards.

**(f) [14]** Levels: 11–14 balanced, developed and evaluated with a conclusion; 7–10 developed but
less balanced; 4–6 limited; 1–3 basic.

- *For:* class shapes life chances in every area named by the syllabus — education (material and
  cultural deprivation), employment (security and pay), health and life expectancy (class gradient
  shown by the Black Report and Marmot Review), housing (renting, overcrowding); inherited wealth
  passes advantage between generations; Marxists see class as the basic division of capitalist
  society.
- *Against:* **gender** — pay gap, glass ceiling, unpaid care, though women tend to live longer;
  **ethnicity** — discrimination in jobs and housing affects even middle-class minorities; **age** —
  young people face insecure work and high housing costs, older people face ageism; in closed
  systems such as caste or modern slavery, ascribed status outweighs class.
- *Intersectionality:* the factors interact, so asking which is "most important" may be misleading;
  class may shape how gender or ethnicity is experienced.
- *Conclusion:* class is a very strong influence, but its effect depends on how it intersects with
  other inequalities and on the society. Credit a reasoned judgement.

**(g) [4]** **Absolute poverty** means lacking the basic necessities needed for survival (1), such as
food, clean water and shelter, as measured in Rowntree's studies of York (1). **Relative poverty**
means falling below the standard of living normal in one's own society (1), so a person may have food
and shelter but be unable to take part in ordinary activities, as Townsend (1979) argued (1).

**(h) [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each:

- **Avoids over-generalising** — it shows that "women" or "the working class" do not share one
  experience, because class, ethnicity and age divide each group (1 + 1).
- **Explains combined disadvantage** — overlapping inequalities interact, so a working-class minority
  woman may face barriers different from those of either a minority man or a white woman (1 + 1).
- **Improves policy** — measures that target one inequality may miss people affected by several
  (1 + 1).

**(i) [4]** Two reasons, 2 marks each:

- **Advantage from birth** — children of wealthy families inherit property and savings regardless of
  merit, so their position is partly ascribed (1 + 1).
- **Buying opportunity** — wealth pays for private schooling, tutoring, housing in good areas and
  unpaid internships, which help secure top jobs (1 + 1).
- **Networks and power** — wealthy families have contacts and influence that open doors closed to
  others (1 + 1).

**(j) [4]** A teacher may label a student as less able, perhaps because of their class or ethnicity
(1). The teacher may then give them less challenging work or place them in a lower set (1). The
student may come to accept the label and stop trying (1), so their results fall, limiting their
qualifications and later job prospects, which reproduces inequality (1).

**(k) [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each:

- **Young people in employment** — lack of experience and age discrimination lead to low-paid,
  insecure or temporary work (1 + 1).
- **Young people in housing** — high rents and house prices mean many cannot leave home or afford to
  buy (1 + 1).
- **Older people in employment** — ageism in recruitment and redundancy can push older workers out of
  jobs before they choose to retire (1 + 1).
- **Older people in housing** — low pensions can make it difficult to heat or maintain a home
  (1 + 1).

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I use examples from my own country?** Yes — the syllabus encourages local and cross-cultural
examples, and they strengthen "explain" and "evaluate" answers.

**Do I need both theories in (f)?** Not required, but Marxism supports the class argument and
labelling or feminist points support the counter-argument.
