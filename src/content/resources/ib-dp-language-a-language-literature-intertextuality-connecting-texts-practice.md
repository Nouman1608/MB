## Questions

**1.** Define *allusion* and *parody*, and state one difference between them. **[2]**

### Passage A -- poem (original text written for Marlbridge)

> **Recipe for an Empty House**
>
> *Serves one. Preparation time: eighteen years.*
>
> Take one house, still warm.
> Remove two teenagers, one at a time,
> the second in late September.
> Set aside their shoes by the door. Do not put them away.
> Reduce the noise slowly over several weeks
> until only the fridge is speaking.
> Add a dog, if available, to taste.
> Season with phone calls on Sundays.
> Leave to stand. It will not set.

**2.** Identify two conventions of a recipe that Passage A uses. Support each with a quotation.
**[2]**

**3.** Explain how the recipe form shapes the reader's response to the poem's subject. Refer to at
least two quotations. **[4]**

### Passage B -- print advert (original text written for Marlbridge; the brand and people are invented)

> **LOCAL WOMAN SLEEPS THROUGH THE NIGHT FOR FIRST TIME IN YEARS**
>
> *By our Sleep Correspondent*
>
> Residents of Elm Road were stunned this week when Priya Nandy, 44, woke at 7 a.m. feeling
> "completely rested". Ms Nandy, a nurse who works shifts, had struggled to sleep for most of the
> past decade.
>
> "I tried everything," she told reporters. "Then I switched to a Brackenby Cloudrest mattress. The
> first night I didn't wake once."
>
> Experts say the right mattress can make a real difference. Neighbours have reportedly asked to
> borrow it.
>
> *Brackenby Cloudrest. Sleep is news. Try one free for 100 nights at any Brackenby store.*

**4.** Identify three conventions of a news report that Passage B borrows. **[3]**

**5.** Explain two reasons why the advertiser presents Passage B as a news report. Use quotations.
**[4]**

**6.** Identify where Passage B reveals its real text type, and explain the effect of placing this
at the end. **[2]**

### Passage C -- two texts

> **Text C1.** William Shakespeare, Sonnet 18, lines 1--4 (published 1609):
>
> Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?
> Thou art more lovely and more temperate:
> Rough winds do shake the darling buds of May,
> And summer's lease hath all too short a date;

> **Text C2.** Travel advert (original text written for Marlbridge; the island is invented):
>
> **Shall we compare thee to a summer's day?**
> On the Isle of Wenlow, we'd rather show you one. Our winds are gentle, and our season runs from
> May to October. Book a ferry before 30 April and save on your crossing.
> *Isle of Wenlow. More lovely. More temperate.*

**7.** Refer to Text C1 and Text C2.

**(a)** Identify the words Text C2 quotes, or adapts, from Text C1 in its headline. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain how Text C2 changes the meaning of the lines it borrows from Text C1. **[3]**

**8.** Is Passage B better described as a parody or a pastiche of a news report? Justify your
answer with evidence. **[3]**

**9.** Write one comparative sentence that connects Passage A and Text C2, keeping evidence from
both texts in the same sentence. **[3]**

**10.** Guided analysis: *How does Passage B use the conventions of another text type to persuade
its audience?* Write a plan with a thesis and three analytical points, each with evidence. **[8]**

**11.** Comparison plan: *Compare how Passage A and Passage C borrow from other texts, and to what
effect.* Give a thesis, three points of comparison, and a sentence explaining what the comparison
reveals. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** An allusion is a brief, indirect reference to another text, person or event that the reader
is expected to recognise. [1] A parody imitates the style or form of a source in an exaggerated way
in order to mock it; unlike an allusion, it imitates at length and has a target it criticises. [1]
*Examiner insight:* A definition earns credit only if it separates the two terms; "both refer to
another text" describes what they share, not how they differ.

**2.** Any two, each with a quotation: a servings and time line ("Serves one. Preparation time:
eighteen years") [1]; imperative verbs in sequence ("Take", "Remove", "Leave to stand") or cooking
terms ("Reduce", "to taste", "Season") [1].
*Examiner insight:* An "identify" question with "support" needs the quotation as well as the label;
a correct convention with no evidence is incomplete.

**3.** The recipe form is practical and controlled, while the subject, children leaving home, is
emotional. [1] The instructions try to manage loss step by step, as in "Remove two teenagers, one at
a time", which makes the speaker sound calm but numb. [1] The form breaks down where feeling breaks
through: "Do not put them away" refuses the tidy logic of a recipe. [1] The last line, "It will not
set", denies the finished result a recipe promises, so the reader feels the loss is unresolved. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers explain the gap between the borrowed form and the real subject;
weak answers stop at "the poet uses a recipe structure".

**4.** Any three: a headline in capitals ("LOCAL WOMAN SLEEPS...") [1]; a byline ("By our Sleep
Correspondent") [1]; a lead paragraph giving who, where and when, with the person's age ("Priya
Nandy, 44"), or quotations attributed with "she told reporters" [1].
*Examiner insight:* Three different conventions are needed; two examples of the same feature, such
as two quotations, earn one mark.

**5.** News reports are read as factual, so the product claim borrows the credibility of reporting
[1]: the testimonial "I didn't wake once" reads as a witness statement, not a sales line. [1] The
form also creates humour by treating an everyday event as major news [1]: "Residents of Elm Road
were stunned" exaggerates a good night's sleep, making the advert memorable. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Each reason needs its own quotation; one quotation stretched across two reasons
usually earns the explanation mark only once.

**6.** The final italic line gives the brand name, slogan and free-trial offer ("Try one free for
100 nights"), which are advert conventions. [1] Placing them last means the reader absorbs the
"story" before realising it is selling, so the claim is accepted before any resistance to
advertising begins. [1]
*Examiner insight:* "Explain the effect" needs a consequence for the reader, not just the location
of the feature.

**7. (a)** "Shall we compare thee to a summer's day?", adapted from "Shall I compare thee to a
summer's day?" [1]
**(b)** In Text C1 the summer's day is the lesser thing: the beloved is "more lovely", and summer
has "Rough winds" and too short a "lease". [1] Text C2 answers those complaints ("Our winds are
gentle"; a season "from May to October"), so the island's summer now wins the comparison. [1] The
slogan "More lovely. More temperate." moves the praise from a person to a place, turning a love poem
into a courtship of the tourist and flattering readers who recognise the source. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Credit goes to what the borrowing changes; answers that only explain Sonnet 18
itself miss the question.

**8.** Pastiche is the better description: Passage B imitates news conventions closely but does not
criticise journalism. [1] Its real aim is to sell, shown by the closing line "Try one free for 100
nights". [1] There is a light parodic edge, since "stunned" mocks sensational local headlines, but
this humour serves the product rather than attacking the news. [1] (Accept an argued "parody".)
*Examiner insight:* Either label can score; marks go to a clear judgement tested against evidence,
not to the label alone.

**9.** Model: *Both texts borrow a form from outside their own type, but where the poem in Passage A
takes the practical recipe ("Leave to stand") to express grief, the advert in Text C2 takes a love
sonnet ("Shall we compare thee") to sell a holiday.* Credit: a clear point of connection [1];
precise evidence from Passage A [1] and from Text C2 [1] in one sentence.
*Examiner insight:* Evidence from only one text, or two sentences joined by "also", does not show
comparison.

**10.** This task is marked here with indicative points. A real Paper 1 guided analysis is marked
holistically against the IB criteria, not point by point.
Model thesis: *The advert disguises a sales pitch as a local news story so that its claim arrives
with the authority of reported fact.* [1]
- Point 1 -- the headline and byline announce news, not advertising [1], with "LOCAL WOMAN SLEEPS
  THROUGH THE NIGHT" and "By our Sleep Correspondent" [1]; the invented job title is a joke that
  signals the borrowing to alert readers. [1]
- Point 2 -- the lead paragraph's factual details (name, age, street, job) make the customer seem
  real and ordinary [1]; "a nurse who works shifts" invites sympathy and trust. [1]
- Point 3 -- vague attribution ("Experts say", "reportedly") imitates news sourcing without naming
  anyone, so the claim sounds supported but cannot be checked. [1]
- Conclusion links the three points to purpose: the form lowers resistance until the final call to
  action. [1]
*Examiner insight:* The strongest plans keep every point tied to the guiding question ("to
persuade"); points that only describe news features, without saying how they persuade, drift from
the question.

**11.** Indicative points only; a real comparative task is marked holistically against the IB
criteria.
Model thesis: *Both texts borrow a familiar form, but in opposite directions: the literary text
takes a non-literary form to hold back feeling, while the non-literary text takes a literary form
to add prestige.* [1]
- Point 1 (direction of borrowing) -- Passage A imports the recipe ("Serves one") [1]; Text C2
  imports Sonnet 18's opening line and its phrase "more lovely and more temperate". [1]
- Point 2 (kept vs changed) -- Passage A keeps the recipe's imperatives but breaks its promise of a
  result ("It will not set") [1]; Text C2 keeps Shakespeare's words but reverses his verdict on
  summer. [1]
- Point 3 (effect on reader) -- Passage A makes the reader feel the strain between control and
  grief [1]; Text C2 rewards the reader who recognises the source, flattering them into the role of
  the beloved. [1]
- What it reveals: borrowing a form always brings the reader's expectations with it, and each text
  succeeds by either fulfilling or frustrating them. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Woven points, with both texts in each point, show comparison; a plan that
covers Passage A in full and then Passage C leaves the connection unargued.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a convention or intertext with no quotation to support it.
- Explaining the source text (such as Sonnet 18) instead of what the new text does with it.
- Giving two examples of one convention when the question asks for different conventions.
- Calling a text a parody without saying what it mocks.
- Comparing in blocks, one text after the other, instead of weaving both into each point.
- Losing the guiding question in an extended answer and drifting into general description.
- Long, dropped-in quotations instead of short phrases embedded in your own sentence.
