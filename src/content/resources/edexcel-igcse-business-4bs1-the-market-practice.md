These questions cover section 4.2 The market (points 4.2.1 to 4.2.3) of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Business (4BS1) specification, Issue 2, February 2026. Revise with the [study guide on the market](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-the-market/) and the [revision notes on the market](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-the-market-revision-notes/) first if needed. Every business is invented and amounts are in dollars. Give percentages to 1 decimal place where needed.

## Questions

### Section A: shorter questions

**1.** Define product orientation. **[2]**

**2.** State the basis of market segmentation used in each case.

**(a)** A clothing brand designs a range only for people aged 16 to 24. **[1]**
**(b)** A bakery delivers only to homes within 5 kilometres of its shop. **[1]**
**(c)** A car maker sells a luxury model aimed at high earners. **[1]**

**3.** Give two ways a business can build customer loyalty. **[2]**

**4.** Fernlea Cosmetics had sales of 1.26 million in a market worth 18 million. **(Calculation)**

**(a)** Calculate Fernlea's market share. **[1]**
**(b)** Next year the market is forecast to grow by 10% and Fernlea's sales by 15%. Calculate Fernlea's new market share. **[2]**

**5.** Outline one benefit to a business of being market-oriented. **[2]**

**6.** Explain one disadvantage of niche marketing. **[3]**

**7.** Explain one way a business could respond to a fall in customer spending. **[3]**

### Section B: longer questions

**8.** Pulpy Smoothies sold 200,000 in a market worth 800,000 in Year 1. In Year 2 its sales were 220,000 and the market was worth 1,000,000.

**(a)** Calculate Pulpy's market share in Year 2. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage growth in Pulpy's sales and in the market. **[2]**
**(c)** Analyse what these figures suggest about Pulpy's position in the market. **[4]**

**9.** Cranbourne Leisure runs a holiday park. It wants to attract more families with young children. Analyse how market segmentation could help Cranbourne Leisure target these customers. **[4]**

**10.** Hollin Knitwear has 15 employees. It is choosing between two strategies.

- **Option A (niche):** premium hand-finished jumpers, 3,000 a year at 120 each, with a cost of 70 per jumper.
- **Option B (mass):** standard jumpers for a supermarket chain, 25,000 a year at 18 each, with a cost of 14 per jumper.

**(a)** Calculate the yearly profit from each option. **[2]**
**(b)** Justify which option Hollin Knitwear should choose. **[6]**

**11.** Rushmere Books is an independent bookshop. Customers have moved to a large online retailer with lower prices, and a chain bookshop has opened on the same street. Rushmere's sales fell from 280,000 to 238,000 in a year.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage fall in Rushmere's sales. **[2]**
**(b)** Evaluate how Rushmere Books should respond to the increased competition. **[9]**

**12.** **(a)** Give one example of a change in customer needs. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why a business must respond to changing customer needs. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** An approach in which a business develops products based on what it can make or is good at [1], and then tries to find customers for them, rather than starting from customer research [1]. **[2]**

**2. (a)** Age [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Location [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Income [1]. **[1]**

**3.** Any two of: a loyalty scheme or card; good after-sales service; handling complaints well; personalised offers; a members' club; keeping in contact through social media or newsletters [1 each]. **[2]**

**4. (a)** 1.26 ÷ 18 × 100 = **7.0%** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** New market = 18 × 1.10 = 19.8 million and new sales = 1.26 × 1.15 = 1.449 million [1]; 1.449 ÷ 19.8 × 100 = **7.3%** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Apply each growth rate to its own figure first. Adding 15% − 10% = 5% to the old share is a common shortcut that gives the wrong answer.

**5.** The business develops products based on what research shows customers want [1], so its products are more likely to sell and less money is wasted on products that fail [1]. **[2]**

**6.** A niche contains only a small number of customers [1], so sales volumes are limited [1], and if the niche shrinks, for example because tastes change, the business has few other customers to fall back on [1]. **[3]**

**7.** It could introduce a cheaper range or smaller sizes [1], so customers with less to spend can keep buying from it rather than switching to a cheaper rival [1], although profit per sale may fall, so costs need to be watched [1]. **[3]**

**8. (a)** 220,000 ÷ 1,000,000 × 100 = **22.0%** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Pulpy: (220,000 − 200,000) ÷ 200,000 × 100 = **10.0%** [1]. Market: (1,000,000 − 800,000) ÷ 800,000 × 100 = **25.0%** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Pulpy's share fell from 25.0% to 22.0% [1], because its sales grew by 10% while the market grew by 25% [1]. This means rivals are taking most of the new customers in a fast-growing market [1], so Pulpy may need to research what new customers want, as it risks becoming weaker even while its sales rise [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Cranbourne could combine demographics (families with young children) and age to define its segment [1]. Knowing this group, it can design suitable facilities such as play areas and family-sized lodges [1], and promote in places parents see, such as parenting websites or school-holiday offers [1]. This means less promotion is wasted on people who are unlikely to book, so more families book for the same marketing spend [1]. **[4]**

**10. (a)** Option A: 3,000 × (120 − 70) = **150,000** [1]. Option B: 25,000 × (18 − 14) = **100,000** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** For Option A: it earns 50,000 more profit [1], and with 50 profit per jumper, against 4 for Option B, it is less exposed to small cost rises [1]. Against Option A: the premium niche is small, so selling 3,000 jumpers at 120 may be hard if tastes change [1]. For Option B: one large supermarket contract gives steady volume [1]. Against Option B: depending on one powerful buyer is risky, because the supermarket could demand lower prices, and a 4 profit per jumper leaves little room [1]. Judgement: for example, Option A, because a 15-person firm with hand-finishing skills suits a niche, and it earns more profit with less dependence on one customer [1]. **[6]**

**11. (a)** (238,000 − 280,000) ÷ 280,000 × 100 [1] = **−15.0%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Cutting prices: it might win back some price-conscious customers [1], but the online retailer and the chain are much larger and can usually go lower [1], so Rushmere could lose profit on every sale without regaining share [1]. Building relationships: author events, a reading club and staff recommendations offer something rivals find hard to copy [1], which builds loyalty among customers who value personal service [1]. Targeting a niche: specialising, for example in local history or children's books, could make Rushmere the first choice for that segment [1], although a narrower range may lose some general customers [1]. Judgement: for example, Rushmere should avoid a price war and combine customer relationships with a niche speciality [1], because its advantage over large rivals is personal service, not price, so this gives it the best chance of recovering the 15% of sales it has lost [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* With two large competitors, the strongest evaluations explain why competing on price is the weakest option for a small independent firm.

**12. (a)** Any one of: wanting healthier products; wanting less packaging or more environmentally friendly products; wanting to buy online; wanting faster delivery [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** If a business keeps selling products customers no longer want [1], customers switch to competitors who meet the new needs [1], so its sales and market share fall [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Assuming higher sales mean a higher market share.
- Adding or subtracting growth rates instead of recalculating shares.
- Defining niche marketing by the size of the business.
- Listing segmentation bases without showing how they change product, price or promotion.
- Recommending price cuts against large rivals without weighing the risk.
