## 3.2.1.1 Interest rates

### What an interest rate is

An interest rate is what it costs to borrow money, stated as an annual percentage of the sum borrowed. For a saver it is the reward for lending money to a bank: the percentage added to the deposit each year. The two are linked. A bank charges borrowers more than it pays savers, and the gap covers its costs, risks and profit.

Interest for one year = sum × rate.

**Worked example 1 (illustrative).** A saver puts 2,000 currency units (cu) in a deposit account.

* At 3%: 2,000 × 0.03 = 60 cu, so the balance after one year is 2,060 cu.
* At 5%: 2,000 × 0.05 = 100 cu, so the balance is 2,100 cu. That is 40 cu more.

### Reward and cost

**Worked example 2.** A bank pays savers 3% and charges borrowers 8%.

* A saver with 10,000 cu on deposit earns 10,000 × 0.03 = 300 cu a year.
* A borrower with a 10,000 cu loan pays 10,000 × 0.08 = 800 cu a year.
* The bank keeps the 500 cu gap to cover its costs, the risk that some loans are not repaid, and its profit.

### Sharia-compliant alternatives

Islamic finance does not allow riba, which means a fixed charge for lending money, so interest is avoided. Sharia-compliant finance instead links the return to a real trade, an asset or a share of profit. Common forms:

| Form | How it works |
|---|---|
| Profit-sharing (mudarabah) | One party supplies the funds, the other manages the business. Profit is split in an agreed ratio. Financial loss falls on the party that supplied the funds |
| Partnership (musharakah) | Both parties put in funds. Profit is shared in an agreed ratio. Losses are shared according to each party's contribution |
| Cost-plus sale (murabaha) | The bank buys the item and sells it on to the customer at the cost plus an agreed, disclosed mark-up, paid in instalments |
| Leasing (ijara) | The bank owns the asset and the customer pays rent to use it |
| Asset-backed certificates (sukuk) | Investors hold a share in an asset and receive part of the income it produces |

**Worked example 3.** A print workshop needs a press costing 50,000 cu.

* Murabaha: the bank buys the press for 50,000 cu and sells it on for 53,000 cu in 12 instalments. The fixed mark-up of 3,000 cu is 6% of the cost.
* Mudarabah: on a profit of 10,000 cu split 60:40 between bank and workshop, the bank gets 6,000 cu and the workshop 4,000 cu. With no profit, the bank gets nothing.
* A saver in a profit-sharing account may earn 4% one year (80 cu on 2,000 cu) and 2% the next (40 cu): the return is not fixed.

Sharia-compliant finance avoids interest by tying returns to assets, sales or profit. Providers may still price with an eye on market rates, so effects on spending can be similar.

### Effects on consumers: save, borrow or spend

When rates rise:

* Saving becomes more attractive: the reward is bigger, and each unit spent now gives up more future interest (a higher opportunity cost of spending).
* Borrowing becomes more expensive: loans, credit cards and variable-rate mortgages cost more, so fewer people take them out for cars, furniture or housing.
* Spending falls, as saving rises and borrowers have less income left after paying interest.

When rates fall, the reverse happens.

**Worked example 4.** A household has a 100,000 cu variable-rate mortgage and an after-tax income of 24,000 cu a year.

* At 5%: interest is 5,000 cu a year (416.67 cu a month), which is 20.8% of income.
* The rate rises to 7%: interest is 7,000 cu a year (583.33 cu a month), which is 29.2% of income.
* The extra cost is 2,000 cu a year (166.67 cu a month). If it comes out of money that would have been spent, spending falls by up to 2,000 cu.

**Evaluation points.** The effect on spending depends on:

* the size of the change, and whether loans are fixed-rate or variable-rate
* how many households are in debt: savers, such as retired people living on interest, gain from a rise
* inflation: if prices rise faster than the rate paid, saving may still not look rewarding
* confidence: households that fear job loss may save whatever the rate

### Effects on producers: save, borrow or invest

Producers save by keeping retained profit in bank deposits, borrow to expand, and invest in machinery, buildings and technology.

* Borrowing: a higher rate raises the cost of loans.
* Investing: a project is worth doing only if its expected return beats the interest charged, so fewer projects pass at higher rates.
* Saving: a higher rate raises the opportunity cost of using retained profit, because the firm could earn interest on it.

**Worked example 5.** A factory plans to buy a machine for 50,000 cu that is expected to add 4,000 cu to profit each year (an 8% return).

* Loan at 6%: interest is 3,000 cu, so net gain is 4,000 − 3,000 = 1,000 cu. Invest.
* Loan at 10%: interest is 5,000 cu, so net gain is 4,000 − 5,000 = −1,000 cu. Do not invest.

Higher rates therefore reduce borrowing and investment.

**Evaluation points.** Investment also depends on expected sales and confidence. A firm with plenty of retained profit is less sensitive to borrowing costs.

## 3.2.1.2 Government income and expenditure

### Sources of revenue

Governments mainly raise revenue from taxes. Other sources include profits from state-owned enterprises, fees and charges, and the sale of assets. Borrowing is not revenue, because it must be repaid.

Main taxes:

* income tax, on wages and other personal income
* corporation tax, on company profits
* social insurance contributions, on earnings, to fund pensions and benefits
* sales tax or value added tax (VAT), on most goods and services
* excise duties, on specific goods such as fuel, alcohol and tobacco
* import duties (tariffs), on imported goods
* property taxes, on land and buildings

### Areas of spending

Main areas are health, education, social protection (pensions and benefits), defence, transport and other infrastructure, law and order, and interest on the national debt.

**Worked example 6.** The table shows the budget of Country B (illustrative, in billions of cu).

| Revenue | Billions | Spending | Billions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Income tax | 30 | Health | 24 |
| Corporation tax | 12 | Education | 19 |
| Social insurance contributions | 14 | Social protection | 30 |
| Sales tax | 24 | Defence | 9 |
| Excise duties | 8 | Transport | 11 |
| Import duties | 4 | Law and order | 6 |
| Other income | 8 | Debt interest | 6 |
| | | Other | 3 |
| **Total** | **100** | **Total** | **108** |

* Direct taxes on income and profit (income and corporation) are 30 + 12 = 42, or 42%. Indirect taxes (sales, excise, import) are 24 + 8 + 4 = 36, or 36%. Social insurance contributions (14) are also taken from earnings.
* Spending exceeds revenue by 108 − 100 = 8 billion. This is a budget deficit, about 7.4% of spending (8 ÷ 108), and must be financed by borrowing.
* Social protection is 30 ÷ 108 = 27.8% of spending.

Spending has an opportunity cost: more for defence means less for health unless taxes or borrowing rise.

### How taxes and spending affect economic activity

Government spending adds directly to demand, for example when it pays builders to make a road. A tax cut raises disposable income and spending. A tax rise does the opposite.

**Worked example 7.** The 18% middle rate falls to 16%. A worker with 20,000 cu in that band pays 3,200 cu instead of 3,600 cu, a gain of 400 cu. Spending 90% of it adds 360 cu to demand.

### Direct and indirect taxes

| | Direct tax | Indirect tax |
|---|---|---|
| Paid by | The person or firm taxed on income or profit | The consumer when buying, collected by the seller |
| Examples | Income tax, corporation tax | Sales tax, VAT, excise, import duties |
| Depends on | Income or profit | Spending |

If a good costs 80 cu before a 10% sales tax, the tax is 8 cu and the consumer pays 88 cu.

### Progressive and regressive taxes

* Progressive tax: the share of income paid climbs as income climbs.
* Regressive tax: the share of income paid shrinks as income climbs.
* Proportional tax: takes the same percentage at every income.

**Worked example 8.** Country B charges income tax of 0% on the first 9,000 cu, 18% on income from 9,000 to 36,000 cu, and 38% on income above 36,000 cu.

| Income | Working | Tax | Average rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7,000 | below the 9,000 threshold | 0 | 0% |
| 27,000 | (27,000 − 9,000) × 0.18 | 3,240 | 12.0% |
| 55,000 | 27,000 × 0.18 + 19,000 × 0.38 = 4,860 + 7,220 | 12,080 | 22.0% |

Higher incomes face a higher average rate, which makes the tax progressive. The 38% rate applies only to the slice above 36,000 cu, not to the whole 55,000 cu.

**Worked example 9.** Country B charges 10% sales tax.

* Household A has an income of 18,000 cu and spends 13,200 cu (including tax) on taxable goods. The tax in that spending is 13,200 × 10 ÷ 110 = 1,200 cu, which is 6.7% of income.
* Household B has an income of 90,000 cu and spends 33,000 cu on taxable goods. The tax is 3,000 cu, which is 3.3% of income.

B hands over a larger sum yet a smaller share, so in effect the sales tax is regressive. A flat fee is regressive too: 120 cu is 1.0% of a 12,000 cu income but 0.1% of 120,000 cu.

Indirect taxes are not always regressive: a tax on luxury goods can be progressive, because mainly high-income households buy them.

### Evaluating tax choices

* Progressive income tax reduces inequality but may weaken the incentive to earn more.
* Indirect taxes are cheap to collect and can discourage harmful goods, but take a bigger share of low incomes.

## Common errors

* Calling a tax progressive because the rich pay more money. Compare percentages of income.
* Applying a band rate to the whole income instead of the slice inside the band.
* Treating government borrowing as revenue.
* Saying a higher rate always cuts spending. It depends on size, timing and confidence.
* Describing sharia-compliant finance as costless. The bank still earns a mark-up, rent or profit share.
