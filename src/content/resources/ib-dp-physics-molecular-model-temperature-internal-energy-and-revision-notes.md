## Molecular model in one table

| State | Spacing | Motion | Forces |
|---|---|---|---|
| Solid | Touching, fixed positions | Vibration about a point | Strong |
| Liquid | Touching, no fixed positions | Vibration plus sliding past neighbours | Strong but not locking |
| Gas | Far apart | Fast random motion between collisions | Negligible between collisions |

- Solids and liquids resist compression because the particles already touch.
- Gases fill their container and compress easily because of the large gaps.

## Formulas and rules

| Quantity | Relationship | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Density | ρ = m/V | kg m⁻³, volume in m³ |
| Temperature conversion | T / K = θ / °C + 273 | 273.15 for more precision |
| Temperature change | ΔT in K = ΔT in °C | Same size of unit |
| Average kinetic energy | Ek = 3/2 kB T | T in kelvin only |
| Heating | Q = mcΔT | c in J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, no phase change |
| Phase change | Q = mL | L_f fusion, L_v vaporization |
| Constant power | Q = Pt | Energy equals power times time |

## Key ideas to recall

- **Internal energy** is the sum, over all molecules, of the intermolecular potential energy and the random kinetic energy.
- **Temperature** is a measure of the average random kinetic energy per particle (in kelvin). It is not a measure of total internal energy.
- **Direction of transfer** is from the higher temperature to the lower temperature, whatever the sizes of the two bodies.
- **Phase change:** temperature constant, kinetic energy per particle unchanged, potential energy changes.
- **Evaporation** is a surface effect that needs no particular temperature. **Boiling** takes place in the bulk of the liquid at its boiling point.

## Must-know distinctions

| Idea A | Idea B |
|---|---|
| Temperature (average kinetic energy) | Internal energy (total of kinetic and potential) |
| Specific heat capacity (change temperature) | Specific latent heat (change state) |
| Boiling (bulk, fixed temperature) | Evaporation (surface, any temperature) |
| Melting (solid to liquid) | Freezing (liquid to solid) |
| Condensing (gas to liquid) | Evaporation (liquid to gas) |

## Method for a heating calculation

1. List the stages. A new stage starts at every melting or boiling point.
2. For each rise in temperature use Q = mcΔT with the c for that state.
3. For each change of state use Q = mL with the right L.
4. Add the stages. If a heater is involved, use t = Q/P.
5. Check units (kg, J, K) and give the answer to three significant figures unless told otherwise.

## Method for a mixing problem

1. State the assumption: no energy is lost to the surroundings.
2. The hotter body loses energy and the colder body gains the same amount: m₁c₁(T₁ − T) = m₂c₂(T − T₂).
3. Solve for T. A sensible answer sits between the two starting temperatures.
4. Check that no phase change has been crossed.

## Worked reminders

**Density.** A 2.0 litre container (2.0 × 10⁻³ m³) holds 5.4 kg of a liquid: ρ = 5.4 / 2.0 × 10⁻³ = 2.7 × 10³ kg m⁻³.

**Average kinetic energy.** At 620 K, Ek = 3/2 × 1.38 × 10⁻²³ × 620 = 1.28 × 10⁻²⁰ J. At 1500 K it is 3.11 × 10⁻²⁰ J.

**Heating water.** Warming 0.60 kg of water by 50 K needs 0.60 × 4180 × 50 = 1.25 × 10⁵ J. A 500 W heater takes 251 s.

**Latent heat.** Melting 0.90 kg of ice at 0 °C needs 0.90 × 3.34 × 10⁵ = 3.01 × 10⁵ J.

## Heating curves

A heating curve plots temperature against time for a sample heated at constant power.

| Part of the graph | What is happening | Energy goes into |
|---|---|---|
| Sloping line, solid | Temperature rising | Random kinetic energy of vibrating particles |
| Flat line at melting point | Melting | Potential energy as particles separate |
| Sloping line, liquid | Temperature rising | Random kinetic energy, mostly |
| Flat line at boiling point | Boiling | Potential energy as particles move far apart |
| Sloping line, gas | Temperature rising | Random kinetic energy |

- Length of a flat section times power gives Q, and Q/m gives L.
- Gradient of a sloping section is P/(mc), so a steeper line means a smaller specific heat capacity for the same mass and power.
- The boiling stage is much longer than the melting stage because L_v is much larger than L_f.
- On cooling, the same curve is read backwards: condensing and freezing release the energy.

## Two short worked reminders

**Two-stage heating.** Warm 0.40 kg of water from 90 °C to 100 °C, then boil away 0.050 kg of it.

```
Stage 1: 0.40 × 4180 × 10 = 1.67 × 10⁴ J
Stage 2: 0.050 × 2.26 × 10⁶ = 1.13 × 10⁵ J
Total = 1.30 × 10⁵ J
```

**Using the kinetic energy relation backwards.** A gas has atoms with an average kinetic energy of 6.9 × 10⁻²¹ J. Then T = 2Ek / 3kB = 2 × 6.9 × 10⁻²¹ / (3 × 1.38 × 10⁻²³) = 333 K, which is about 60 °C.

## Explaining in words

Answers score best when they name the correct energy store. Use these sentence frames.

- **Warming a solid:** the average random kinetic energy of the particles increases, so the temperature rises.
- **Melting:** the energy supplied increases the potential energy of the particles as the bonds between them are loosened, while the average kinetic energy and temperature stay constant.
- **Compressing a gas is easy, compressing a liquid is hard:** the gas particles are far apart, the liquid particles are already in contact.

## Quick self-test

1. Convert 20 °C to kelvin and 500 K to degrees Celsius.
2. A body is heated from 300 K to 340 K. What is the change in temperature in degrees Celsius?
3. A 0.75 kg block of material occupies 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ m³. Find its density.
4. Find the average kinetic energy of a particle at 750 K (kB = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹).
5. By what factor does the average kinetic energy change when the temperature rises from 150 K to 450 K?
6. A student says the average kinetic energy doubles when a gas goes from 10 °C to 20 °C. Is that correct?
7. Name the two parts of the internal energy of a substance.
8. A 0.25 kg lead block (c = 129 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹) cools from 300 °C to 20 °C. How much energy does it lose?
9. How much energy is needed to boil away 1.5 kg of water at 100 °C? (L_v = 2.26 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹)
10. State what happens to the potential energy and the average kinetic energy of the molecules as ice melts at 0 °C.
11. Block P at 60 °C and a much larger block Q at 40 °C touch. In which direction is the net energy transfer?

### Answers

1. 293 K; 227 °C.
2. 40 °C (a change of 40 K is 40 °C).
3. ρ = 0.75 / 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ = 2.5 × 10³ kg m⁻³.
4. Ek = 1.5 × 1.38 × 10⁻²³ × 750 = 1.55 × 10⁻²⁰ J.
5. It triples (450/150 = 3).
6. No. In kelvin the temperature goes from 283 K to 293 K, a rise of under 4 percent.
7. Intermolecular potential energy and random kinetic energy of the molecules.
8. Q = 0.25 × 129 × 280 = 9.03 × 10³ J.
9. Q = 1.5 × 2.26 × 10⁶ = 3.39 × 10⁶ J.
10. Potential energy rises; average kinetic energy stays the same because the temperature is constant.
11. From P to Q, because P has the higher temperature, even though Q is larger.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using °C in Ek = 3/2 kB T, or doubling a Celsius temperature and calling it a doubling of energy.
- Substituting grams or cm³ without converting to kg or m³.
- Using the specific heat capacity of water for ice or the other way round.
- Leaving out a stage, usually the melting or boiling stage, in a multi-stage heating calculation.
- Writing that the temperature rises during a phase change.
- Explaining a phase change as an increase in kinetic energy of the particles, rather than potential energy.
- Confusing temperature with internal energy, or heat with temperature, in written answers.
- Leaving the unit off the last line, or quoting too many or too few significant figures.
