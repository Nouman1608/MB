Condensed recall for Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 subtopic **9.2 Uses of metals**. For full explanations and worked examples, use the [Uses of metals study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-uses-of-metals/).

## The four required pairings

| Metal | Use | Property (syllabus wording) | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aluminium | Aircraft | Low density | Lighter aircraft; less fuel, more payload |
| Aluminium | Overhead electrical cables | Low density **and** good electrical conductivity | Carries current well; light enough to hang between pylons |
| Aluminium | Food containers | Resistance to corrosion | Container does not corrode or contaminate food |
| Copper | Electrical wiring | Good electrical conductivity **and** ductility | Carries current with little waste; drawn into thin wires |

Two of the four need **two** properties. Learn them as pairs.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Density | Mass per unit volume (g cm⁻³); low density = light for its size |
| Electrical conductivity | Ability to carry electric current, due to delocalised electrons |
| Ductility | Ability to be drawn into wires |
| Corrosion | A metal reacting with substances around it (air, water) so that it is gradually worn away |
| Oxide layer | Thin, tightly bonded aluminium oxide coating that protects aluminium from further reaction |
| Physical property | A property observed without a chemical change (density, conductivity, ductility) |

## Numbers worth knowing

| Metal | Density / g cm⁻³ | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| Aluminium | 2.70 | Low density |
| Iron | 7.87 | About 2.9 times aluminium |
| Copper | 8.96 | About 3.3 times aluminium; better conductor |

mass = density × volume. For equal volumes, a copper cable is about 3.3 times the mass of an aluminium one.

## Why aluminium resists corrosion

Aluminium is reactive (above zinc and iron in the reactivity series), but a thin layer of aluminium oxide forms on its surface in air. The layer is unreactive and tightly bonded, so it keeps air and water away from the metal beneath. This is the same explanation as the "apparent unreactivity of aluminium" in subtopic 9.4.

## Why copper indoors, aluminium overhead

| Requirement | Overhead cables | House wiring |
|---|---|---|
| Good conductor | Needed | Needed |
| Low mass | Essential (long spans between pylons) | Not important |
| Drawn into thin wire | Helpful | Essential |
| Best choice | Aluminium | Copper |

## Answer frame

"[Metal] is used for [use] because it has [property], so [benefit]."

Example: "Copper is used for electrical wiring because it is a good electrical conductor and is ductile, so it can be drawn into thin wires that carry current with little energy wasted."

## Model "explain" answers

**Explain why aluminium is used for overhead cables. [2]**
Aluminium is a good conductor of electricity, so the cables carry current efficiently [1]. It has a low density, so long cables are light enough to be supported between pylons [1].

**Explain why copper is used for electrical wiring. [2]**
Copper is a very good electrical conductor because it has delocalised electrons [1]. It is ductile, so it can be drawn into thin wires without breaking [1].

**Explain why aluminium is suitable for food containers although it is a reactive metal. [2]**
A thin layer of aluminium oxide forms on its surface [1]. This layer stops air and water reaching the metal, so the container resists corrosion [1].

## Exam traps

- "Light" or "lightweight" is weaker than **low density**: use the syllabus term.
- Aluminium is **not** unreactive: its **oxide layer** gives corrosion resistance.
- Overhead cables need **both** low density and good conductivity.
- Wiring needs **electrical** conductivity, not thermal.
- Cost, appearance or availability are not physical properties.
- Do not swap the uses: copper is not the syllabus example for cables, and aluminium is not the example for house wiring.
- In "suggest" questions, quote the data you are given; do not just name the metal.
- Copper (8.96 g cm⁻³) is denser than iron (7.87 g cm⁻³); do not assume iron is the heaviest of the three metals named here.

## Self-test

1. State the property that makes aluminium suitable for aircraft.
2. Give the two properties that make aluminium suitable for overhead cables.
3. Why is aluminium used for food containers?
4. Explain why aluminium resists corrosion even though it is a reactive metal.
5. Give the two properties that make copper suitable for electrical wiring.
6. Define ductile.
7. Copper conducts electricity better than aluminium. Why is aluminium preferred for overhead cables?
8. Calculate the mass of 100 cm³ of aluminium (density 2.70 g cm⁻³).
9. Why is a high density not a problem for copper wiring in a house?
10. Explain, in terms of structure, why metals conduct electricity.
11. Suggest a property that makes a metal suitable for a saucepan base.
12. A student writes "aluminium is used for aircraft because it is cheap". Explain why this does not answer a 5070 question on uses.

**Answers:** 1. Low density. 2. Low density and good electrical conductivity. 3. It resists corrosion. 4. A thin, unreactive layer of aluminium oxide forms on the surface and stops air and water reaching the metal. 5. Good electrical conductivity and ductility. 6. Can be drawn out into wires. 7. Aluminium has a much lower density, so the cables are lighter and easier to support between pylons. 8. 2.70 × 100 = 270 g. 9. The wiring is short and supported by the building, so its mass does not matter. 10. Delocalised electrons move through the structure and carry charge. 11. Good thermal conductivity (or high melting point). 12. Cost is not a physical property; the syllabus requires uses to be explained by physical properties (here, low density).
