These questions cover section **4.4.3 Electrolysis** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) specification, for
teaching from September 2016. The content is assessed on Paper 1. Questions 9, 10 and 12 test half equations,
which the specification marks (HT only), and are labelled **Higher tier only**. Question 8 is based on required
practical 3.

Learn the content first in the [electrolysis study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-electrolysis/)
and the [electrolysis revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-electrolysis-revision-notes/).

## Questions

**1.** Solid sodium chloride does not conduct electricity, but molten sodium chloride does.

**(a)** Explain the difference. **[2]**
**(b)** Give the name for a molten or dissolved ionic compound that conducts electricity. **[1]**

**2.** Molten lead(II) bromide is electrolysed using graphite electrodes.

**(a)** Name the product at the cathode. **[1]**
**(b)** Name the product at the anode. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why the ions move to these electrodes. **[2]**

**3.** Predict the products at the cathode and the anode when each molten compound is electrolysed with inert electrodes.

**(a)** Zinc chloride **[2]**
**(b)** Potassium iodide **[2]**

**4.** Aluminium is extracted from aluminium oxide by electrolysis.

**(a)** Explain why aluminium is not extracted by heating its oxide with carbon. **[1]**
**(b)** The electrolyte is a mixture of aluminium oxide and cryolite. Explain why a mixture is used. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the positive electrodes must be replaced regularly. **[3]**

**5.** A student electrolyses sodium chloride solution using inert electrodes.

**(a)** Name the gas formed at the cathode and explain why a metal is not formed. **[2]**
**(b)** Name the gas formed at the anode and give a test for it. **[2]**
**(c)** Name the substance that remains in solution as the electrolysis continues. **[1]**

**6.** Predict the products at the cathode and the anode when each solution is electrolysed with inert electrodes.

**(a)** Copper(II) sulfate solution **[2]**
**(b)** Dilute sulfuric acid **[2]**
**(c)** Potassium bromide solution **[2]**

**7.** A technician says, "In electrolysis, the metal always forms at the cathode." Evaluate this statement, using one example where it is true and one where it is not. **[3]**

**8.** A student investigates what happens when aqueous solutions are electrolysed using inert electrodes. The hypothesis is: "A metal forms at the cathode only if the metal is less reactive than hydrogen."

**(a)** Describe how the student could test this hypothesis. **[4]**
**(b)** Give **one** result that would support the hypothesis. **[1]**
**(c)** Suggest **one** safety precaution when electrolysing sodium chloride solution. **[1]**

**9.** (Higher tier only) Molten lead(II) bromide is electrolysed.

**(a)** Write the half equation for the reaction at the cathode. **[1]**
**(b)** Write the half equation for the reaction at the anode. **[1]**
**(c)** State whether the lead ions are oxidised or reduced and explain your answer. **[2]**

**10.** (Higher tier only) Aluminium oxide dissolved in cryolite is electrolysed.

**(a)** Write the half equation for the formation of aluminium. **[1]**
**(b)** Write the half equation for the formation of oxygen from oxide ions. **[1]**
**(c)** Combine the two half equations to show the overall equation for the production of aluminium and oxygen from aluminium oxide. Show how you balanced the electrons. **[3]**

**11.** Sodium sulfate solution is electrolysed with inert electrodes.

**(a)** Name the product at each electrode. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why sodium is not produced. **[1]**
**(c)** Suggest what happens to the total amount of sodium sulfate in the solution as the electrolysis continues. **[2]**

**12.** (Higher tier only) Concentrated sodium chloride solution is electrolysed with inert electrodes.

**(a)** Write the half equation for the reaction at the anode. **[1]**
**(b)** Write the half equation for the reaction at the cathode. **[1]**
**(c)** In dilute sodium sulfate solution, oxygen forms at the anode. Write the half equation for the formation of oxygen from hydroxide ions. **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** In **molten** sodium chloride the ions are **free to move** [1]; in the **solid** they are held in a fixed lattice, so there are no mobile charged particles [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** An **electrolyte** [1]

**2. (a)** **Lead** [1]
**(b)** **Bromine** [1]
**(c)** Lead ions are **positive** and are attracted to the **negative** cathode [1]; bromide ions are **negative** and are attracted to the **positive** anode [1]. **[2]**

**3. (a)** Cathode **zinc**, anode **chlorine** [1] [1] **[2]**
**(b)** Cathode **potassium**, anode **iodine** [1] [1] **[2]**

**4. (a)** Aluminium is **more reactive than carbon** (so carbon cannot displace it) [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Aluminium oxide has a **very high melting point** [1]; cryolite **lowers the melting point** of the electrolyte, saving energy and cost [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** The anode is carbon [1]; **oxygen** formed at the anode **reacts with the carbon** [1] to make **carbon dioxide**, so the electrodes burn away [1]. **[3]**

**5. (a)** **Hydrogen** [1]; sodium is **more reactive than hydrogen**, so hydrogen ions are discharged instead [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** **Chlorine** [1]; **damp blue litmus paper** turns red then bleaches white [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** **Sodium hydroxide** (solution) [1]

**6. (a)** Cathode **copper**, anode **oxygen** [1] [1] **[2]**
**(b)** Cathode **hydrogen**, anode **oxygen** [1] [1] **[2]**
**(c)** Cathode **hydrogen**, anode **bromine** [1] [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The anode rule is oxygen unless a halide is present. Sulfate is not a halide.

**7.** The statement is **not always true** [1]. True: in a molten compound such as lead bromide, lead forms at the cathode, or in copper sulfate solution, copper forms because it is less reactive than hydrogen [1]. Not true: in sodium chloride solution **hydrogen** forms at the cathode because sodium is more reactive than hydrogen [1]. **[3]**

**8. (a)** Any four [1] each: use a **range of solutions** with metals above and below hydrogen (for example sodium chloride, copper sulfate, zinc sulfate); use **inert electrodes** (graphite); the **same** voltage and electrodes each time; **observe the cathode** for a metal coating or bubbles; **test any gas** (squeaky pop for hydrogen); **compare** with the hypothesis. **[4]**
**(b)** Copper sulfate solution gives a **copper coating** on the cathode (a metal less reactive than hydrogen) [1] (accept silver nitrate). **[1]**
**(c)** Work in a **well-ventilated area / fume cupboard** because **chlorine is toxic** (or use low concentration) [1]. **[1]**

**9. (a)** Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb [1]
**(b)** 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻ [1]
**(c)** **Reduced** [1], because they **gain electrons** [1]. **[2]**

**10. (a)** Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al [1]
**(b)** 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻ [1]
**(c)** Lowest common multiple of 3 and 4 electrons is 12, so multiply the first by 4 and the second by 3 [1]: 4Al³⁺ + 12e⁻ → 4Al and 6O²⁻ → 3O₂ + 12e⁻ [1]. Overall: **2Al₂O₃ → 4Al + 3O₂** [1]. **[3]**

**11. (a)** Cathode **hydrogen**, anode **oxygen** [1] [1] **[2]**
**(b)** Sodium is **more reactive than hydrogen** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** The amount of sodium sulfate **stays the same** [1] because only **water** is being broken down into hydrogen and oxygen, so the solution becomes more concentrated [1]. **[2]**

**12. (a)** 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻ [1]
**(b)** 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ [1]
**(c)** 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ [1]

## Where marks are usually lost

- Predicting a reactive metal at the cathode in an aqueous solution.
- Forgetting that the halide rule decides chlorine, bromine or iodine at the anode.
- Describing the anode loss as "wearing away" rather than reacting with oxygen to form carbon dioxide.
- Leaving electrons out of half equations, or placing them on the wrong side.
- Writing atoms (Cl, O) instead of molecules (Cl₂, O₂).
