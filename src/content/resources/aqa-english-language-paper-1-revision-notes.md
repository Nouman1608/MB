**What is AQA A-level English Language Paper 1?** Paper 1, Language, the Individual and Society, is a
2 hour 30 minute exam worth 100 marks and 40% of the A-level (7702). Section A asks you to analyse
and compare two texts linked by topic or theme, one contemporary and one older; Section B is a
discursive essay on children's language development (0 to 11 years) based on data.

Condensed for the final weeks. For the full explanation, use the
[Language, the Individual and Society study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-english-language-paper-1/).

## Where Paper 1 sits in the A-level

| Component | Time | Marks | Weighting |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Paper 1: Language, the Individual and Society** | **2 h 30** | **100** | **40%** |
| Paper 2: Language Diversity and Change | 2 h 30 | 100 | 40% |
| NEA: Language in Action | — | 100 | 20% |

At **AS** (7701), Paper 1 is a different paper, Language and the Individual: 1 h 30, 70 marks, with
the same three Section A-style questions (25 + 25 + 20) and no children's language development essay.

## Paper 1 question structure

| Section | Task | Marks |
|---|---|---|
| A: Textual variations and representations | Analysis of **one** text (Text A) | 25 |
| A | Analysis of a **second** text (Text B) | 25 |
| A | **Comparison** of the two texts | 20 |
| B: Children's language development | **One** discursive essay from a choice of **two**; the data for each focuses on spoken, written or multimodal language | 30 |

The two Section A texts are linked by topic or theme: **one contemporary and one older text**. Note
the weighting: the comparison (20) and the Section B essay (30) together make **half the paper**,
so practise them as much as single-text analysis.

## The analytical frameworks — use the terminology

| Level | What to look for |
|---|---|
| **Phonology** | Sound patterning, alliteration, accent features in transcripts |
| **Graphology** | Layout, typography, images, use of space |
| **Lexis and semantics** | Word choice, semantic fields, connotation, formality, jargon |
| **Grammar and syntax** | Word classes, sentence types and length, voice, mood, **modality** |
| **Pragmatics** | Implied meaning, presupposition, politeness, deixis |
| **Discourse** | Structure, cohesion, openings and closings, turn-taking |

Every point: **name the feature with correct terminology → quote it → explain the effect in relation to audience, purpose and context.** A feature named without an effect scores nothing — this three-step structure is the single habit that raises a Paper 1 answer from descriptive to analytical.

## Mode, register and representation

- **Mode** is a spectrum from spoken to written; digital forms sit between (a text message is written in medium, often speech-like in character).
- **Register** varies with formality and field.
- **Idiolect** — an individual's distinctive usage. **Sociolect** — a social group's variety.
- **Representation** — how language constructs a version of people or events. Interrogate naming choices, modifiers, and whether **agency** is expressed or hidden (passives delete the actor). Three tools worth knowing: **modification** (adjectives/nouns attaching evaluative qualities before a group is even named — "young offenders"), **transitivity** (whether a group is the agent doing an action or the patient it happens to, controlling whether they seem active/responsible or passive/vulnerable), and **semantic field** (a cluster of related lexis — a warfare field frames a debate as conflict, an illness field frames it as something to be treated).
- **Audience positioning** — a text constructs a role for its reader without stating a view outright, through **presupposition** (building a claim into a sentence's structure), **inclusive pronouns** ("we", "our") that enrol the reader, **selection and omission** of which voices are quoted, and **naming choices** ("protester" vs "activist" positions the reader differently towards the same person).

## Children's language development

The other half of the Paper 1 content. The specification (3.1.3 on this site; 4.1.3 in the combined
specification PDF) asks you to study the **functions** of children's language; **phonological,
pragmatic, lexical, semantic and grammatical development**; different **genres** of speech and
writing; different **modes** (spoken, written, multimodal); and **theories and research**. Know the
spoken stages (babbling, holophrastic, two-word, telegraphic, post-telegraphic) and the competing theories:

- **Behaviourist (Skinner)** — imitation and reinforcement.
- **Nativist (Chomsky)** — an innate language acquisition device; evidence from **virtuous errors** ("goed", "sheeps") that children cannot have copied.
- **Cognitive (Piaget)** — language follows conceptual understanding.
- **Interactionist (Bruner)** — child-directed speech and social interaction scaffold acquisition.

Virtuous errors are the standard evidence against pure imitation — a reliable point in any acquisition essay.

### Development strands to name in the data

| Strand | What to look for in a transcript or child's writing |
|---|---|
| **Phonological** | Simplification such as deletion of final consonants ("ca" for "cat"), substitution ("wabbit"), reduplication ("dada") |
| **Lexical and semantic** | Early nouns for people, animals and food; **overextension** ("dog" for every animal) and **underextension** (a word used too narrowly) |
| **Grammatical** | Inflections appearing (plural -s, past tense -ed, -ing), over-regularised forms, questions and negatives becoming more complex |
| **Pragmatic** | Turn-taking, politeness forms, using language to request, inform or play |
| **Writing** | Emergent marks, invented (phonetic) spelling, growing control of punctuation, genre conventions learned from reading |

**Method for Section B.** Make a clear line of argument about the question, then support it with
precise evidence from the data, labelled with the right level of language, and evaluate at least
two theories against that evidence rather than describing them. Comment on context: who the child
is speaking or writing to, and what the caregiver contributes.

**Mode in practice.** A scripted news bulletin is written to be spoken: planned, edited, dense with information, complete sentences, no interaction or repair. A live radio phone-in is spontaneous speech: fillers, false starts, overlaps and self-repair, with structure negotiated between participants in real time rather than fixed in advance.

## Exam traps

- Feature-spotting without explaining effect.
- Ignoring mode, audience and purpose.
- Treating theories as settled fact rather than competing positions to evaluate.
- Confusing **accent** (pronunciation) with **dialect** (grammar and vocabulary).
- In the comparison question, dealing with each text in turn instead of comparing **by point**.
- Analysing content rather than language.
- In Section B, describing theories at length without testing them against the data provided.
- Forgetting that one Section A text is older: comment on how its language reflects its period.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long should I spend on each part of Paper 1?**
Marks suggest roughly 35 minutes on each single-text analysis, 30 minutes on the comparison and 45
minutes on the Section B essay, plus reading time; adjust to your own pace in timed practice.

**Is the Section B data always spoken?**
No. The specification says the data may focus on spoken, written or multimodal language, so revise
children's writing and multimodal texts as well as transcripts.

**What does "one contemporary and one older text" mean for my answer?**
The two Section A texts come from different periods, so the comparison can discuss how mode,
audience and representation differ across time as well as in language features.
