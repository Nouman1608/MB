These notes cover two linked key questions from Cambridge IGCSE Sociology (0495) Paper 1: 2.2 *How
does society control us?* and 2.3 *What influences our social identity?* In short: societies use
formal agencies (government, police, courts, prisons, armed forces) and informal agencies (family,
school, peers, media, religion, workplace) to produce conformity through sanctions, while some
people resist through protest groups and subcultures; identity is shaped by age, gender, ethnicity
and class, and increasingly by the digital world and globalisation.

Fuller explanations of 2.3: [what influences our social identity study guide](/resources/igcse-cambridge-sociology-social-identity/).

## Syllabus coverage

0495 syllabus 2025–2027:

- **2.2** 2.2.1 formal and informal social control, positive and negative sanctions; 2.2.2
  consensus and conflict views (functionalism, Marxism, feminism); 2.2.3 effectiveness of formal
  agencies; 2.2.4 methods of formal control (law-making, coercion, digital surveillance, arrest,
  sentencing, imprisonment); 2.2.5 effectiveness of informal agencies; 2.2.6 informal sanctions
  (ostracism, parental rewards, social media "likes", religious rewards and sanctions, school
  punishments, dismissal from a job); 2.2.7 resistance through protest groups and subcultures.
- **2.3** 2.3.1 age, gender, ethnicity, social class; 2.3.2 the digital self; 2.3.3 globalisation;
  2.3.4 postmodernist views of chosen identity.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Social control** | Ways society ensures people follow norms and laws |
| **Formal social control** | Control by official agencies using written rules and laws |
| **Informal social control** | Control through everyday unwritten rules and reactions of others |
| **Positive sanction** | A reward for conforming |
| **Negative sanction** | A punishment for deviating |
| **Coercion** | Use or threat of force |
| **Digital surveillance** | Monitoring through CCTV, phone and internet data |
| **Ostracism** | Excluding someone from a group |
| **Subculture** | A group with norms and values different from mainstream society |
| **Protest group** | Organised group campaigning for or against change |
| **Digital self** | Identity built and presented online |
| **Hybrid identity** | Identity mixing more than one culture |
| **Cultural defence** | Strengthening identity when a culture feels threatened |
| **Pick and mix identity** | Postmodern idea of choosing identity through consumption and lifestyle |

## 2.2 How society controls us

**Formal agencies and methods**

| Agency | Method | Effectiveness point |
|---|---|---|
| Government | Law-making | Laws only work if enforced and seen as legitimate |
| Police | Arrest, surveillance, coercion | Many crimes are never reported or solved |
| Courts | Sentencing | Sentences may deter, but reoffending is common |
| Penal system | Imprisonment | Removes offenders, but may not rehabilitate |
| Armed forces | Coercion in emergencies | Used rarely in democracies; can lose legitimacy |

**Informal agencies and sanctions:** family (parental rewards, grounding), education (praise, school
punishments), peer group (acceptance, ostracism), media (social media "likes", public shaming),
religion (promise of reward, religious sanctions), workplace (promotion, dismissal).

**Consensus and conflict views**

| Perspective | View of social control |
|---|---|
| Functionalism | Control maintains value consensus and social order for everyone's benefit |
| Marxism | Control serves the ruling class; laws and agencies protect capitalism |
| Feminism | Control maintains patriarchy; women are controlled in the home and public spaces |

**Resistance (2.2.7):** people may join protest groups or subcultures because of shared grievances,
a sense of belonging, identity or excitement. Examples include environmental protest groups, youth
subcultures (for example punks in the 1970s), online subcultures (gaming communities, fandoms) and
religious subcultures.

## 2.3 What influences social identity

- **Age:** life stages are socially constructed and vary between cultures (Pilcher, 1995).
- **Gender:** learned through socialisation (Oakley, 1972); changing but unequal.
- **Ethnicity:** shared culture, language, religion; strengthened by community and by
  discrimination (Modood et al., 1997).
- **Social class:** shapes tastes, values and cultural capital (Bourdieu).
- **Digital self:** positives — expression, community, contact across distance; negatives —
  pressure, cyberbullying, privacy risks (boyd, 2014; Turkle, 2011).
- **Globalisation:** homogenisation, hybrid identities, cultural defence; impacts of cultural
  diversity, multiculturalism and global culture.
- **Postmodernism:** identity chosen through consumption and lifestyle; critics say choice is limited
  by money and social position.

## Worked example: an 8-mark "discuss" answer plan

*Discuss how effective informal agencies are in producing conformity.* (practice)

1. Family: early, powerful sanctions; most children absorb norms — but some families fail to control
   teenagers.
2. Peer group: acceptance and ostracism are strong in adolescence — but peers can encourage deviance.
3. Media and social media: likes and shaming shape behaviour — but online spaces also spread
   deviant subcultures.
4. Judgement: informal agencies are highly effective for everyday norms, while serious crime needs
   formal control.

## Common mistakes and exam traps

- Describing agencies without explaining **how** they control (sanctions, methods).
- Saying the police are always effective — consider unreported crime and legitimacy.
- Forgetting **positive** sanctions; control is not only punishment.
- Treating globalisation as only making identities the same.
- Ignoring **resistance** in 2.2; examiners expect reasons why people join protest groups.

## Quick self-test

1. Give one example of a positive and one of a negative informal sanction.
2. Name three formal agencies of social control.
3. Explain the Marxist view of social control in one sentence.
4. Give two reasons why people join subcultures.
5. Give one positive and one negative impact of social media on identity.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between 2.2 and 6.1 in Paper 2?** Both cover agencies of social control;
2.2 focuses on conformity and identity, while Paper 2's crime topic links control to preventing
crime and deviance.

**Are subcultures always deviant?** No — some are simply different, such as fan communities; others
involve rule-breaking.

**Which perspectives should I use for 2.2.2?** Functionalism as the consensus view, Marxism and
feminism as conflict views.
