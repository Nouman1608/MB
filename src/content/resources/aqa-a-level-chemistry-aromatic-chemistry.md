## The bonding in benzene (3.3.10.1)

Benzene has the formula C₆H₆. Its six carbon atoms form a regular hexagon, and each carbon is bonded to
one hydrogen atom.

### Planar ring, equal bonds

Each carbon atom uses three of its outer electrons to form single (σ) bonds: two to neighbouring carbons
and one to hydrogen. Three bonding pairs repel equally, so the bond angles are 120° and the whole molecule
is **planar**.

That leaves each carbon with one electron in a **p orbital**, at right angles to the plane of the ring.
The six p orbitals overlap sideways with each other all the way round. The six p electrons are not
fixed between particular pairs of atoms. They are **delocalised**: spread over all six carbons, in a ring
of electron density above and below the plane.

Because every carbon–carbon bond shares the same delocalised electrons, all six bonds are the same
length. That length is **intermediate between a C–C single bond and a C=C double bond**. This is why
benzene is drawn as a hexagon with a circle inside, not with three separate double bonds.

### The theoretical model: cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene

A model with three localised C=C bonds alternating with three C–C bonds describes
**cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene**, which would have two different bond lengths. It is a useful theoretical
comparison, but not the real structure.

### Thermochemical evidence: enthalpies of hydrogenation

Hydrogenation adds H₂ across C=C bonds. When cyclohexene (one C=C) is hydrogenated to cyclohexane, a
fixed amount of energy is released per C=C bond. If benzene were cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene, you would expect
three times that amount when it is hydrogenated to cyclohexane.

The measured value for benzene is much less exothermic than the prediction. Less energy is released
because benzene starts lower in energy. The difference is the extra stability that comes from
delocalisation.

**Worked example 1.** You are given these enthalpies of hydrogenation:

- cyclohexene + H₂ → cyclohexane, ΔH = −120 kJ mol⁻¹
- benzene + 3H₂ → cyclohexane, ΔH = −208 kJ mol⁻¹

Use the data to show that benzene is more stable than cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene.

1. The triene has three C=C bonds, so its expected value is 3 × (−120) = **−360 kJ mol⁻¹**.
2. The measured value for benzene is −208 kJ mol⁻¹.
3. Difference: −208 − (−360) = **+152 kJ mol⁻¹**. Benzene releases 152 kJ mol⁻¹ less energy than
   predicted.
4. Both reactions give the same product, cyclohexane. So benzene must be 152 kJ mol⁻¹ **lower in energy**
   (more stable) than the triene would be.
5. The reason is delocalisation of the p electrons in benzene.

On an energy level diagram, cyclohexane is at the bottom, benzene 208 kJ mol⁻¹ above it and the triene
360 kJ mol⁻¹ above it. The 152 kJ mol⁻¹ gap is the extra stability.

### Why substitution, not addition

Addition across a ring bond would break up the delocalised system. The product would lose the extra
stability shown above, so addition is energetically unfavourable. In **substitution**, one hydrogen atom
is replaced and the delocalised ring is kept intact. So benzene undergoes substitution reactions in
preference to addition reactions.

In an alkene, the electron density of the C=C bond is concentrated between two carbons. In benzene it is
spread over six. So benzene needs a strong electrophile, usually a positive ion made in the reaction
mixture.

## Electrophilic substitution (3.3.10.2)

Both reactions you need follow the same three-stage pattern. The specification limits this to
**monosubstitution**: one hydrogen on the ring is replaced.

1. **Make the electrophile** (a positive ion) from the reagents.
2. **Attack:** a curly arrow goes from the delocalised ring to the electrophile. The ring's delocalisation
   is partly broken and a positive intermediate forms.
3. **Lose H⁺:** a curly arrow goes from the C–H bond back into the ring. Delocalisation is restored and
   H⁺ is released.

### Drawing the mechanism

- **Arrow 1** starts on or inside the circle of the ring and points to the atom of the electrophile that
  forms the new bond (N in NO₂⁺, C in RCO⁺).
- **The intermediate**: the carbon under attack now carries both H and the new group. Draw a broken
  horseshoe (partial circle) over the other five carbons, with a **+** inside it. The gap in the
  horseshoe faces the carbon under attack.
- **Arrow 2** starts on the C–H bond of that carbon and points into the ring.
- **Product**: a full circle again, with the new group in place of H.

### Nitration

**Reagents:** concentrated nitric acid and concentrated sulfuric acid.

**Making the electrophile.** Sulfuric acid is the stronger acid. It protonates nitric acid, which then
loses water to give the **nitronium ion**, NO₂⁺:

```
HNO₃ + 2H₂SO₄ → NO₂⁺ + H₃O⁺ + 2HSO₄⁻
```

An equivalent form is HNO₃ + H₂SO₄ → NO₂⁺ + HSO₄⁻ + H₂O.

**Mechanism.** Arrow from the ring to the N of NO₂⁺; horseshoe intermediate with + inside, the attacked
carbon carrying H and NO₂; arrow from the C–H bond into the ring. The product is **nitrobenzene**,
C₆H₅NO₂.

**Overall equation:** C₆H₆ + HNO₃ → C₆H₅NO₂ + H₂O.

**Regenerating the catalyst.** The H⁺ lost from the ring combines with HSO₄⁻:
H⁺ + HSO₄⁻ → H₂SO₄. Sulfuric acid is used up in step 1 and re-formed here, so it acts as a catalyst.

**Why nitration matters.** It is an important step in synthesis.

- **Explosives**: compounds with several nitro groups on an aromatic ring, such as TNT
  (2,4,6-trinitromethylbenzene), are explosives. You only need to show monosubstitution.
- **Amines**: a nitro compound can be reduced to an aromatic amine, such as phenylamine from
  nitrobenzene. Aromatic amines are used to make dyes. That reduction belongs to
  section 3.3.11.1 Amines.

### Friedel–Crafts acylation

**Reagents:** an acyl chloride, RCOCl, with **aluminium chloride, AlCl₃**, as the catalyst. Conditions
must be dry, because aluminium chloride reacts with water.

**Making the electrophile.** AlCl₃ accepts a chloride ion from the acyl chloride, forming an
**acylium ion**, RCO⁺:

```
RCOCl + AlCl₃ → RCO⁺ + AlCl₄⁻
```

For ethanoyl chloride: CH₃COCl + AlCl₃ → CH₃CO⁺ + AlCl₄⁻.

**Mechanism.** Arrow from the ring to the carbonyl carbon of RCO⁺ (the carbon with the + charge);
horseshoe intermediate with + inside, the attacked carbon carrying H and COR; arrow from the C–H bond
into the ring. The product is a **phenyl ketone**, C₆H₅COR.

**Regenerating the catalyst.** H⁺ + AlCl₄⁻ → AlCl₃ + HCl. Aluminium chloride is re-formed, so it is a
catalyst. Hydrogen chloride is the by-product.

**Overall equation (ethanoyl chloride):** C₆H₆ + CH₃COCl → C₆H₅COCH₃ + HCl. The product is
**phenylethanone**.

**Why acylation matters.** It forms a new carbon–carbon bond to the ring, so it is an important step in
synthesis.

**Worked example 2.** 3.90 g of benzene is reacted with excess propanoyl chloride, CH₃CH₂COCl, in the
presence of AlCl₃. The mass of organic product is 4.69 g. Use Ar: H = 1.0, C = 12.0, O = 16.0.

1. Product: C₆H₅COCH₂CH₃, **1-phenylpropan-1-one**, C₉H₁₀O.
2. Mr(C₆H₆) = 78.0; n = 3.90 / 78.0 = 0.0500 mol.
3. Mole ratio benzene : product = 1 : 1, so the theoretical amount of product is 0.0500 mol.
4. Mr(C₉H₁₀O) = 134.0; theoretical mass = 0.0500 × 134.0 = 6.70 g.
5. Percentage yield = 4.69 / 6.70 × 100 = **70.0%**.
6. Electrophile: **CH₃CH₂CO⁺**, formed by CH₃CH₂COCl + AlCl₃ → CH₃CH₂CO⁺ + AlCl₄⁻.

### Comparing the two reactions

| | Nitration | Friedel–Crafts acylation |
|---|---|---|
| Reagents | conc. HNO₃ + conc. H₂SO₄ | RCOCl + AlCl₃ (dry) |
| Electrophile | NO₂⁺ (nitronium ion) | RCO⁺ (acylium ion) |
| Catalyst | H₂SO₄ | AlCl₃ |
| Catalyst regenerated by | H⁺ + HSO₄⁻ → H₂SO₄ | H⁺ + AlCl₄⁻ → AlCl₃ + HCl |
| Product from benzene | nitrobenzene | phenyl ketone, C₆H₅COR |
| Use in synthesis | explosives; route to aromatic amines | adds a carbon chain to the ring |

### Practical opportunity: methyl 3-nitrobenzoate

The specification suggests nitrating methyl benzoate as a skills activity. In outline:

1. Dissolve methyl benzoate in concentrated sulfuric acid and cool the mixture in ice.
2. Add a cold mixture of concentrated nitric and sulfuric acids slowly, a few drops at a time, keeping the
   reaction mixture cold. Slow addition and cooling help to limit side reactions such as further
   substitution.
3. Pour the mixture onto crushed ice. The crude solid separates out.
4. Collect the solid by filtration under reduced pressure and wash it with cold water.
5. Recrystallise: dissolve in the minimum volume of hot solvent, filter hot if needed, cool, then filter
   off the crystals and dry them.
6. Measure the melting point. A pure solid melts sharply, close to the data-book value; an impure one
   melts lower and over a range.

Concentrated acids are corrosive: wear eye protection and gloves.

## Common errors

- Drawing benzene with three fixed double bonds when the question asks about delocalisation or bond
  length.
- Working out 3 × (−120) but never saying which compound is **more stable**.
- Saying benzene "has no double bonds so it can't do addition". The correct reason is that addition would
  destroy the delocalised, stabilising ring.
- Starting arrow 1 from a single carbon atom instead of from the delocalised ring.
- Drawing a full circle in the intermediate, or putting the horseshoe gap on the wrong side.
- Leaving the + off the intermediate, or putting it on the electrophile's atom.
- Pointing arrow 1 at an oxygen of NO₂⁺ instead of the nitrogen.
- Writing AlCl₃ + Cl⁻ in the equation for the electrophile but never showing the catalyst being re-formed.
- Calling the acylation product an aldehyde. Benzene + RCOCl gives a **ketone**.
