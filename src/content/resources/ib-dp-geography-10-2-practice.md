## Section A

**1.** Define energy security. **[1]**

**2.** State the four dimensions of energy security in the four As framework. **[2]**

**3.** Outline one way in which energy production depends on water. **[2]**

**4.** Outline why biofuel production can affect food security. **[2]**

**5.** Identify one environmental impact of the expansion of palm oil plantations. **[1]**

## Section B

**6.** Country W imports 3 million tonnes of rice a year. Assume that 2,500 litres of water are needed to grow 1 kg of rice. Country W's renewable freshwater supply is 15 cubic kilometres a year (1 cubic kilometre = 10^12 litres).

**(a)** Calculate the virtual water imported each year, in cubic kilometres. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate this as a percentage of Country W's renewable supply. **[1]**

**(c)** Suggest one risk to Country W of relying on rice imports. **[1]**

**7.** Explain how a dam on a river shared by two countries can improve resource security for one country while reducing it for another. **[4]**

**8.** Explain why the shift to electric vehicles changes, rather than removes, the impacts of resource consumption. **[3]**

**9.** Suggest why a country with large coal reserves may still have low energy security. **[3]**

## Section C

**10.** Examine the arguments surrounding the water-food-energy nexus, with reference to examples. **[10]**

**11.** "Changing trends in resource consumption bring more benefits than costs." Discuss this statement. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Reliable access to sufficient, affordable energy to meet a country's needs [1]. **[1]**

**2.** Availability and accessibility [1]; affordability and acceptability [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Thermal power stations need large volumes of water for cooling [1], so drought or low river levels can force them to cut output; hydroelectric power depends directly on river flow [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Crops such as maize or sugar cane grown for fuel, or the land used to grow them, are not available for food [1], which can reduce supply and raise food prices for poorer consumers [1]. **[2]**

**5.** For example, loss of rainforest habitat for species such as orangutans, or carbon emissions from burning and draining peatland [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** 3,000 million kg x 2,500 litres = 7,500,000 million litres = 7.5 x 10^12 litres [1] = **7.5 cubic kilometres** [1].

**(b)** 7.5 / 15 x 100 = **50%** [1].

**(c)** For example, rising world prices or export bans by producing countries could cut supply, reducing food security [1]. **[4]**

**7.** The upstream country can store water to generate hydroelectricity, improving its energy security [1] and possibly supplying irrigation. The downstream country may receive less water, or water at different times, especially while the reservoir fills [1], reducing water available for irrigation and drinking, so its water and food security fall [1]. The dam on the Blue Nile, which Ethiopia began filling in 2020, shows this tension, with Egypt concerned about flows on which it depends for almost all its fresh water [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Electric vehicles cut demand for oil and reduce exhaust emissions in cities [1], but their batteries require minerals such as lithium and cobalt [1], whose extraction affects water, land and communities in producing regions, such as brine extraction in Chile's Salar de Atacama, so impacts move to different places [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Coal is available, but its use may be increasingly unacceptable because of greenhouse gas emissions and air pollution [1]. Mines may be far from demand or poorly connected, limiting accessibility [1], and the cost of extraction or pollution control can make it unaffordable compared with alternatives, so availability alone does not ensure security [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Indicative content; award one mark per valid, developed point up to 10:

- Defines the nexus as the interdependence of water, food and energy, so action on one affects the others [1].
- Food production needs water for irrigation and energy for pumping, fertiliser and machinery [1].
- Energy production needs water for cooling and hydropower, and land for biofuels [1].
- Water supply needs energy for pumping, treatment and desalination [1].
- Argument that the nexus creates trade-offs: biofuels compete with food, as debated during the 2007 to 2008 food price spike [1].
- Argument that it creates synergies: efficient irrigation saves both water and energy [1].
- Transboundary example: a dam that improves one country's energy security can threaten downstream water and food security [1].
- Different stakeholders value the resources differently, so the nexus is political as well as physical [1].
- Uses accurate located examples, such as the Aral Sea or the Blue Nile dam [1].
- Conclusion: a supported judgement, for example that the nexus makes single-resource policies risky and favours integrated planning [1]. **[10]**

**11.** Indicative content; award one mark per valid, developed point up to 10:

- Benefits: better nutrition and health as diets diversify [1].
- Benefits: wider access to energy improves education, business and quality of life, and reduces indoor air pollution [1].
- Benefits: resource exports bring income and jobs to producing countries [1].
- Costs: environmental damage such as deforestation, aquifer depletion and greenhouse gas emissions [1].
- Costs: price volatility and import dependence reduce security for poorer households and countries [1].
- Costs: resource competition can cause local and international conflict [1].
- Benefits and costs fall on different places: consumers often gain while producing regions bear environmental costs [1].
- Benefits and costs fall over different times: short-term gains may create long-term depletion [1].
- Uses accurate located evidence on both sides, such as palm oil in Indonesia [1].
- Conclusion: a supported judgement on balance, for example that benefits are real but unevenly shared, so the answer depends on who and where [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* "Discuss" rewards answers that say *for whom* consumption trends bring benefits or costs, not only what those benefits and costs are.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Losing powers of ten when converting litres to cubic kilometres.
- Treating virtual water imports as entirely positive, with no mention of new dependence.
- Describing the nexus as three lists rather than linked effects.
- Assuming that large reserves guarantee security.
