## Questions

**1.** Define regulatory capture. **[2]**

**2.** A regulated water supplier in Country E has a price of 50. Inflation is 3.2% and the regulator's efficiency factor X is 1.8.

**(a)** Calculate the new price under an inflation-minus-X cap. **[3]**
**(b)** The next year inflation is 2.0% and X is unchanged. Calculate the price, to two decimal places. **[2]**

**3.** A rail network operator has capital employed of 320m. The regulator allows a return of 7.5% on capital.

**(a)** Calculate the maximum profit allowed and the excess if actual operating profit is 31m. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why this form of control may encourage over-investment. **[2]**

**4.** In a market, the six firms have shares of 28%, 22%, 18%, 14%, 10% and 8%.

**(a)** Calculate the four-firm concentration ratio. **[1]**
**(b)** The firms with 14% and 10% merge. Calculate the new four-firm ratio. **[2]**
**(c)** State and explain one factor, other than concentration, that an authority should consider. **[2]**

**5.** Explain two limitations of competition policy. **[4]**

**6.** A cartel in Country A has annual turnover of 150m. The maximum fine is 8% of turnover. Cartel members gain 9m extra profit a year for 3 years. The probability of detection is 0.4.

**(a)** Calculate the maximum fine. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the expected fine and decide whether the cartel is worthwhile. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the fine, as a percentage of turnover, needed to deter the cartel. **[2]**
**(d)** Suggest one other measure to deter cartels. **[2]**

**7.** A state-owned commuter rail line makes a financial loss of 18m a year. Its external benefits (less congestion and pollution) are valued at 26m a year.

**(a)** Calculate the net benefit to society of keeping the line open. **[2]**
**(b)** Evaluate whether the line should stay in public ownership. **[6]**

**8.** A government sells a state-owned port for 1,200m. The port earns profit of 75m a year. The government uses the proceeds to repay debt costing 3.5% a year.

**(a)** Calculate the sale price as a multiple of annual profit. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the annual net effect on the government's finances. **[3]**
**(c)** Give one reason the sale might still be justified. **[2]**

**9.** After taxi services in Country G are deregulated, the average fare falls from 12.00 to 10.20, and trips rise from 40,000 to 46,000 a month.

**(a)** Work out the percentage change in the average taxi fare. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in trips. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the price elasticity of demand. **[2]**
**(d)** State one risk of deregulation. **[2]**

**10.** A privatised electricity grid in Country H is a natural monopoly. The regulator must choose between a price cap and rate-of-return control. Evaluate the choice. **[9]**

**11.** Two producers of ready-meals in Country J propose to merge. Unit cost is 40 for each firm and the price is 60. The merged firm says unit cost will fall to 35 and that 60% of the saving will be passed on.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage fall in unit cost. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the price after pass-through and its percentage change. **[3]**
**(c)** Evaluate whether the competition authority should block the merger. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** The regulator ends up serving the interests of the industry it oversees [1], instead of the public or consumers [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Say whose interests are served and whose are ignored; "the regulator is influenced" is too vague to separate capture from ordinary lobbying.

**2. (a)** Allowed change = 3.2 − 1.8 = 1.4% [1]. New price = 50 × 1.014 [1] = **50.70** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Allowed change = 2.0 − 1.8 = 0.2% [1]. Price = 50.70 × 1.002 = **50.80** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the allowed rate before the new price, so the reader can see you applied inflation minus X, not inflation alone.

**3. (a)** Allowed profit = 320 × 0.075 = **24m** [1]. Excess = 31 − 24 = **7m** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Allowed profit rises with capital employed [1], so the operator gains by raising capital beyond what is needed, with little reward for cutting costs [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the mechanism (allowed profit tied to capital) to the behaviour (over-investment); naming the term alone does not explain it.

**4. (a)** 28 + 22 + 18 + 14 = **82%** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** New shares: 28, 24, 22, 18, 8 [1]. Four-firm ratio = 28 + 24 + 22 + 18 = **92%** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Any one: ease of entry [1], with a reason such as that new rivals would restrain price [1]. Others: likely cost savings, foreign competition, buyer power. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Concentration is only a screen; answers that stop at the ratio miss the evaluation the question points towards.

**5.** Any two, each with development.
Limitation 1: regulators have incomplete information [1], so they may block a beneficial merger or miss a harmful one [1].
Limitation 2: policy has costs, such as running the authority, legal fees and delays [1], which may exceed the benefit in small cases [1].
Others: loss of economies of scale; firms relocating; difficulty proving intent. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A limitation gains little unless you explain the consequence; "it is expensive" needs a reason why that matters.

**6. (a)** Fine = 0.08 × 150 = **12m** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Total gain = 9 × 3 = 27m [1]. Expected fine = 0.4 × 12 = 4.8m [1]. Since 4.8m is below 27m, the cartel is worthwhile for members [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Fine needed = 27 / 0.4 = 67.5m [1], which is 67.5 / 150 = **45%** of turnover [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Any one: a leniency scheme [1] that raises the chance of detection by rewarding the first member to report [1]. Also damages claims or personal penalties. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare expected fine with total gain, not with one year's gain; the periods must match.

**7. (a)** Net benefit = 26 − 18 [1] = **8m** a year [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** For keeping public: net social benefit is positive at 8m [1]; private firms ignore the external benefits and would close the line [1]. Against: the 18m loss falls on taxpayers [1]; weak cost incentive may mean the loss is larger than needed [1]. Evaluation: the external benefits are estimates and may be wrong [1]; a better answer might be a subsidy to a private operator under a competitive tender, which keeps the benefit and a cost incentive [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A conclusion that weighs the 8m figure against its uncertainty reads as judgement; a list of pros and cons does not.

**8. (a)** 1,200 / 75 = **16** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Interest saved = 0.035 × 1,200 = 42m [1]. Profit given up = 75m [1]. Net effect = 42 − 75 = **−33m** a year [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Any one: efficiency gains under private ownership [1] that raise output or quality or lower cost beyond the 33m loss [1]. Also new investment or competition. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Say whether government finances improve or worsen and by how much; a number with no interpretation leaves the point unmade.

**9. (a)** (10.20 − 12.00) / 12.00 = **−15%** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** (46,000 − 40,000) / 40,000 = **+15%** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** PED = +15 / −15 [1] = **−1.0** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Any one: lower safety or quality [1] because there is less oversight, so consumers may be harmed [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep the sign of the elasticity and say what it means (unit elastic); a magnitude only is incomplete.

**10.** Price cap: allowed rise is inflation minus X [1]; the firm keeps savings, so it has a cost-cutting incentive [1]. Risk: if X is too tight the firm may cut quality or investment [1]; setting X needs information the regulator lacks [1]. Rate of return: limits profit to a return on capital [1]; it suits a long-lived network because it gives investors stable returns [1]; but it rewards over-investment and gives a weak cost incentive [1]. Both are exposed to capture, since the regulator depends on the firm's data [1]. Judgement: for example the cap is better for efficiency if the regulator has good cost information and can monitor quality; rate-of-return control is better if heavy investment is needed and capture risk is managed [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers weigh one tool against the other under stated conditions; describing each in turn, without comparing, limits how far the argument can go.

**11. (a)** (35 − 40) / 40 = **−12.5%** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Saving = 5 per unit; passed on = 0.6 × 5 = 3 [1]. New price = 60 − 3 = **57** [1]. Change = (57 − 60) / 60 = **−5%** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** For allowing: unit cost falls 12.5% and price falls 5% if the promise holds [1]; lower costs from scale [1]. Against: the saving is a claim and pass-through is not guaranteed [1]; the merger reduces rivals and may raise concentration [1]. Evaluation: conditions such as binding commitments on price or asset sales [1]; the decision depends on entry barriers and rivals, and the cost of a wrong decision [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the figures in the question: stating that 40% of the saving is kept as profit shows application.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using inflation alone, or X alone, as the allowed price change.
- Comparing an expected fine with a single year's gain.
- Defining capture in terms of corruption only; the idea is a drift of regulatory interest towards the industry.
- Stating a number without its unit or sign (m, %, minus).
- Listing advantages and disadvantages of privatisation without a verdict.
- Ignoring that a state monopoly can become a private monopoly.
- Percentage changes taken on the new value, not the original.
- Forgetting that remedies and tools each carry their own costs.
