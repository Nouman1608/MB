## Key definitions (B6.2d)

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Genome | All the genetic material of an organism |
| Genetic engineering | A process that modifies the genome of an organism to introduce desirable characteristics |
| Transgenic | Carrying a gene from another species |
| GM (genetically modified) | An organism whose genome has been changed by genetic engineering |
| Desirable characteristic | A useful feature, such as insect resistance, added nutrients or faster growth |

Remember: the change is in the genome, so it is **inherited**.

## Key terms for the steps (B6.2e, Higher Tier only)

| Term | Job |
|---|---|
| Restriction enzyme | Cuts DNA at one particular base sequence |
| Sticky ends | Short unpaired ends left by the cut; they pair with complementary ends |
| Vector | Carries the gene into the host cell (for example a plasmid) |
| Plasmid | Small circular loop of DNA, separate from the main chromosome |
| Ligase | Joins the gene and plasmid by sealing the sugar-phosphate backbones |
| Recombinant plasmid | Plasmid containing the new gene |
| Host bacteria | Cells that take up the plasmid and copy it |
| Antibiotic resistance marker | Gene on the plasmid that lets only plasmid-carrying bacteria grow on antibiotic agar |

### Method in steps (Higher Tier only)

1. Isolate the gene.
2. Cut the gene out with a restriction enzyme, leaving sticky ends.
3. Cut the plasmid with the same enzyme.
4. Mix and join with ligase to make a recombinant plasmid.
5. Add the plasmid to host bacteria.
6. Grow on agar with antibiotic; only transformed bacteria form colonies.

Two points to keep: the **same** enzyme must be used on gene and plasmid, and the marker shows plasmid uptake, not that the right gene is present.

### Worked reminder

After treatment with plasmid, 36 colonies grew on antibiotic agar. Untreated bacteria on plain agar gave 120 colonies. Proportion transformed = 36 / 120 × 100 = **30.0%**. Control plate with untreated bacteria on antibiotic agar should show no colonies; that shows the antibiotic works.

## Benefits and risks (B6.2f)

| Benefits | Risks |
|---|---|
| Higher yield | Gene flow to wild relatives or other crops |
| Fewer insecticide sprays for toxin-making crops | Pest resistance may evolve |
| Added nutrients | Harm to non-target organisms |
| Tolerance of drought, salt or herbicide | Unknown long-term effects, concern over health |
| Less food waste | Cost of seed and control by a few companies |

- **Practical considerations:** cost, testing, regulation, keeping GM and non-GM crops separate, saving seed.
- **Ethical considerations:** moving genes between species, labelling and consumer choice, ownership, fairness to poorer farmers.
- **Misconception to avoid:** genetic engineering does not always raise pesticide use. Toxin-making crops can cut insecticide sprays, while herbicide-tolerant crops can raise herbicide use.

## Genetic modification as a solution (B6.2g)

| Type | Example | How it helps feed more people |
|---|---|---|
| GM crop | Insect-resistant crop | Less crop lost to pests |
| GM crop | Beta-carotene rice | Improves diet quality where vitamin A is low |
| GM livestock | Fast-growing salmon | Reaches market size sooner |
| GM microorganism | Bacteria making chymosin | Steady enzyme supply for cheese making |

Useful calculations: percentage change = change / original × 100; share = part / whole × 100.

## Worked reminders for data questions (B6.2f and B6.2g)

**Percentage change.** A GM variety yields 4.4 tonnes per hectare against 4.0 for the conventional one.
Change = 4.4 − 4.0 = 0.4. Percentage change = 0.4 / 4.0 × 100 = **10%** higher. Always divide by the original (here conventional) value.

**Reading a table.** If a table shows insecticide sprays falling from 5 to 2, say both numbers and the difference: "sprays fell from 5 to 2, a drop of 3, which is 60%". Quoting the figures is what turns a general statement into evidence.

**Reading a graph.** Say what each axis shows, describe the overall trend, quote two values with units, then add a pattern such as "faster after year 5". Do not claim a cause that the graph cannot show: a rise in GM area does not by itself prove the GM crop raised yields.

**Cost and benefit.** Income = yield × price. Profit = income − seed cost − spray cost. Compare both crops on the same area of land, and say the answer applies only to the figures given.

**Faster growth.** If a GM animal reaches market size in 15 months and the conventional one takes 24, time saved = 9 months, which is 9 / 24 × 100 = **37.5%**. Linking this to more animals produced per year in the same space gives the food-supply point.

## How to write a good evaluation (B6.2f)

An evaluation answer usually has four parts:

1. **A benefit with a reason.** "Insect-resistant maize makes its own toxin, so less of the crop is eaten and yield rises."
2. **A risk with a reason.** "Pollen may carry the gene to wild relatives, which could pass the trait to weeds."
3. **A practical or ethical point.** "Seed may need buying afresh each season, a cost that small farms could find hard to meet."
4. **A judgement.** "On balance, the benefit is greater where pests cause large losses, as long as resistance is monitored."

Use specific examples rather than "it is good" or "it is bad". Use comparison words such as "whereas" and "however" to show you are weighing the two sides.

## Examples of GM in food production (B6.2g)

- **Crops:** insect-resistant (toxin gene from a bacterium), herbicide-tolerant, nutrient-enriched (beta-carotene rice), drought-tolerant.
- **Livestock:** a salmon with a growth hormone gene that reaches market size sooner.
- **Microorganisms:** bacteria or fungi that make a protein in a fermenter, such as chymosin for cheese making or insulin for people with diabetes.
- **Why these matter:** the population is growing, farmland is limited, so each hectare, plant and animal must deliver more food or lose less to pests and disease.

When asked to "explain how", always finish the chain: the gene gives the characteristic, the characteristic raises yield or cuts losses, and that means more food from the same resources.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Selective breeding vs genetic engineering:** selective breeding chooses parents within a species over many generations; genetic engineering changes the genome directly and can use a gene from another species.
- **Restriction enzyme vs ligase:** one cuts, one joins.
- **Plasmid vs chromosome:** plasmid is a small separate loop.
- **Vector vs host:** the vector carries the gene; the host is the cell that receives it.
- **Benefit vs risk:** always say who gains or is harmed.

## Quick self-test

1. State what is meant by genetic engineering.
2. Name the enzyme that cuts DNA at a specific sequence. (Higher Tier only)
3. Name the enzyme that joins the gene to the plasmid. (Higher Tier only)
4. What are sticky ends, and why do they matter? (Higher Tier only)
5. Why is an antibiotic resistance gene put on the plasmid? (Higher Tier only)
6. Give one benefit and one risk of an insect-resistant crop.
7. A trial finds yield rises from 5.0 to 6.0 tonnes per hectare. Calculate the percentage increase.
8. A GM crop covers 18 million hectares of a country's 120 million hectares of cropland. What percentage is this?
9. Explain why GM crops do not always lead to more pesticide use.
10. Suggest one ethical concern about genetic engineering in farming.

### Answers

1. A process that modifies the genome of an organism to introduce desirable characteristics.
2. Restriction enzyme.
3. Ligase.
4. Short unpaired ends left by a restriction enzyme. They pair with complementary sticky ends on DNA cut by the same enzyme, so the gene can join the plasmid.
5. It is a marker: only bacteria that took up the plasmid survive on agar containing the antibiotic.
6. Benefit: fewer insecticide sprays or higher yield. Risk: pests may become resistant, or non-target insects may be harmed.
7. (6.0 − 5.0) / 5.0 × 100 = **20%**.
8. 18 / 120 × 100 = **15%**.
9. Crops making their own insect toxin can reduce the need for insecticide sprays. (Herbicide-tolerant crops may do the opposite, so it depends on the trait.)
10. Any one: moving genes between species is unnatural; consumers may lack choice without labelling; a few companies may control seed; poorer farmers may not afford it.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "ligase cuts" or "restriction enzymes join".
- Forgetting that the same restriction enzyme cuts both gene and plasmid.
- Saying the antibiotic resistance gene helps the plant, when it is a marker for bacteria.
- Describing a benefit without linking it to food supply, or a risk without saying what harm could follow.
- Stating that all GM crops use more pesticide, or that none do.
- Leaving out units, or giving a percentage change without dividing by the original value.
- Evaluating with a one-sided list instead of reaching a judgement.
- Using "gene" and "genome" as if they meant the same thing.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a vector in genetic engineering?**
A vector carries the gene into the host cell; a plasmid, a small circular loop of DNA separate from the main chromosome, is a common example.

**What are sticky ends?**
Sticky ends are short unpaired ends of DNA left by a restriction enzyme cut. They pair with complementary sticky ends, which allows a gene to be inserted into a plasmid.

**What is a recombinant plasmid?**
A recombinant plasmid is a plasmid that contains the new gene. Host bacteria take it up and copy it.

**Are the steps of genetic engineering on the Foundation tier?**
No. The main steps of genetic engineering, statement B6.2e, are Higher Tier only in OCR GCSE Biology A (J247). The definition, benefits and risks, and GM as a solution to food demand are on both tiers.
