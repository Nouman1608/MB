## Where Paper 3 sits

Paper 3, 2 h 15, 50 marks, 25% of the A Level. A Level-only, alongside Paper 4. Two compulsory sections, 25 marks each. No dictionaries.

## 3.1 Section A: Language Change

- Development of English from **Early Modern English to Contemporary English**.
- Named processes: **borrowing** (loanwords), **compounding**, **blending**, **affixation**, **conversion/functional shift**, **initialism/acronym**.
- Semantic shift: **broadening** (more general), **narrowing** (more specific), **amelioration** (more positive), **pejoration** (more negative).
- **Prescriptivism** (correct form, deviation = error) vs **descriptivism** (record and analyse actual use, change is natural).
- Evidence: n-gram-style graphs, corpus/word-table data showing change over time.

## 3.2 Section B: Child Language Acquisition

- Stages: **pre-verbal/cooing (0–6 months)** → **babbling (6–12 months)** → **holophrastic/one-word (12–18 months)** → **two-word (18–24 months)** → **telegraphic (24–36 months)** → **post-telegraphic (36 months+)**.
- Theories: **Behaviourist (Skinner)** — imitation/reinforcement; **Nativist (Chomsky)** — innate Language Acquisition Device, "poverty of the stimulus"; **Social interactionist (Bruner)** — carer interaction, Language Acquisition Support System; **Cognitive (Piaget)** — language reflects prior cognitive development.
- **Over-generalisation** ("goed", "sheeps"): applying a regular rule to an irregular form — evidence of rule-based, creative learning, not imitation.

## The shared thread

Both sections are ultimately about rule application and generalisation — a child over-generalising a grammar rule and a language regularising an irregular form over centuries are both evidence that language users actively apply and extend patterns, not simply reproduce input verbatim. Keeping this parallel in mind can strengthen an answer in either section, since it shows the kind of connected understanding examiners reward beyond isolated fact recall.

## Pairing theory with criticism

For Section B specifically, never state a theory without also being ready to state its standard criticism — questions frequently ask for both, and a theory description alone typically only earns partial credit. Build the pairing into your revision from the start: Skinner with "cannot explain novel utterances," Chomsky with "no identified physical basis for the LAD," Bruner with "not all cultures use child-directed speech," Piaget with "cannot explain language despite cognitive impairment."

## Building an evidence bank for Section A

Because Section A rewards specific, illustrated examples over general statements, prepare five or six examples per process before the exam: for borrowing, words like "bungalow" (Hindi) or "algebra" (Arabic); for compounding, "laptop"; for blending, "brunch" or "podcast" (iPod + broadcast); for affixation, "unfriend" or "googleable"; for conversion, the noun "text" becoming the verb "to text"; for semantic shift, "nice" (once meaning ignorant, now positive — amelioration) alongside "silly" (once meaning blessed, now negative — pejoration). Having concrete, ready examples for each process means exam time is spent applying and illustrating rather than searching for evidence under pressure.

## Building an evidence bank for Section B

Similarly for Section B, prepare a short worked illustration of over-generalisation ("goed" for "went", "sheeps" for "sheep") and be ready to explain what it demonstrates: that children apply regular grammatical rules productively to irregular forms, evidence they have internalised a rule rather than simply repeating adult speech — and that this pattern underlies the U-shaped development in which a correct irregular form is briefly replaced by an incorrect regularised one before being relearned correctly.

## Exam traps

- Describing change without naming the specific process (borrowing, affixation, semantic shift).
- Confusing prescriptivism with descriptivism.
- Giving acquisition stages with no approximate ages or defining features.
- Naming a theory without its criticism when asked for both.
- Treating over-generalisation as a simple error rather than evidence of rule learning.

## Reading corpus and n-gram evidence

Section A questions sometimes present evidence in the form of a simple graph or word-frequency table showing how often a word or spelling appeared across different time periods, rather than asking you to recall dates from memory. Practise reading this kind of evidence quickly: identify the overall trend (rising, falling, a sudden spike around a particular period), then link that trend to a plausible cause — a rising frequency around the mid-20th century might suggest a technological or social driver, for instance. The skill being tested is interpreting evidence you are given, not reciting a memorised timeline, so practising with any simple frequency graph — even one you sketch yourself from a handful of made-up data points — builds the right habit.

## Self-test

1. Name four processes of lexical change.
2. Distinguish amelioration from pejoration.
3. Name the six stages of child language acquisition in order.
4. Which theorist proposed the Language Acquisition Device?
5. What does grammatical over-generalisation reveal about how children learn?

**Answers:** 1. Borrowing, compounding, affixation, conversion (or blending, initialism/acronym). 2. Amelioration is a word acquiring more positive connotations; pejoration is a word acquiring more negative connotations. 3. Pre-verbal/cooing, babbling, holophrastic, two-word, telegraphic, post-telegraphic. 4. Chomsky (nativist theory). 5. That children apply and generalise underlying grammatical rules productively, rather than simply imitating adult speech they have heard.

## Keeping the two sections distinct in revision time

Because Section A and Section B draw on genuinely different knowledge (historical linguistics against developmental psychology of language), avoid letting revision time drift toward whichever section feels more comfortable. Both carry equal marks, so a revision timetable that allocates genuinely equal time to language-change examples and acquisition theory-plus-criticism pairs consistently produces a stronger overall result than deep expertise in one section and thin coverage of the other.
