These notes condense section 7.2, *Globalisation and identity*, of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699), Paper 4 Section A. Syllabus strands: the impact of globalisation on identity, including ethnic revitalisation, cultural defence and hybrid identity; cultural divergence versus convergence/homogenisation; and the role of Western ideology in shaping identity and the concept of Westernisation.

Full explanations: [globalisation and identity study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-globalisation-and-identity/).

## Core argument

Globalisation does not push identity in one direction. It can erode local identities (convergence), provoke their defence or revival (divergence), or blend them into new hybrids. Western ideology shapes identities, but selectively and against resistance. A strong essay identifies which outcome fits which groups.

## Frameworks

| Hall (1992): three outcomes | Nederveen Pieterse (2004): three paradigms |
|---|---|
| National identities eroding (homogenisation) | McDonaldization (convergence) |
| Local identities strengthened by resistance | Clash (Huntington) |
| New hybrid identities replace declining ones | Hybridisation (mélange) |

## Identity responses

**Ethnic revitalisation** — renewed pride and revival of language and customs.
- Te reo Māori: Māori-language pre-schools from the early 1980s; Māori Language Act 1987.
- Welsh: Welsh Language Act 1993 put Welsh and English on an equal basis in public business in Wales.
- Evaluation: can be symbolic; may be shaped by global forces such as heritage tourism.

**Cultural defence** — protecting culture against perceived outside threat.
- Bruce: religion as carrier of group identity.
- Giddens (1999): fundamentalism as a defence of tradition.
- Castells, *The Power of Identity* (1997):
  - *legitimising* identity — promoted by dominant institutions;
  - *resistance* identity — built by devalued groups (fundamentalism, ethnic nationalism);
  - *project* identity — new identities that aim to transform society (feminism, environmentalism).

**Hybrid identity** — new identities blending cultures.
- Back, *New Ethnicities and Urban Culture* (1996): south London youth mixing music, language and identity.
- Hall: diaspora identities belong fully to neither origin nor settlement society.
- Bhabha (1994): hybridity and the "third space".
- Evaluation: fits young, urban, mobile groups best; can overlook racism and inequality.

## Convergence versus divergence

| Convergence | Divergence |
|---|---|
| Ritzer: McDonaldization (efficiency, calculability, predictability, control) | Glocalisation |
| Schiller (1969): cultural imperialism | Thussu (2007): **contra-flows** from non-Western media centres |
| Global brands and English as a global language | Minority language revival |
| Barber: McWorld | Tomlinson (1991): active audiences reinterpret imported media |

## Westernisation and Western ideology

- **Westernisation:** spread of individualism, consumerism, secularism and liberal democracy, often presented as the endpoint of modernisation.
- **Ideology:** Western ways presented as superior or universal.
- **Said, *Orientalism* (1978):** the West constructed "the East" as backward and exotic, defining itself as rational and modern.
- **In practice:** consumer identities, global beauty ideals, individualistic choice in marriage, career and religion.
- **Evaluation:** selective adoption (technology yes, values perhaps not); non-Western cultural exports; resistance identities respond directly.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Ethnic revitalisation | Revival of an ethnic group's language, customs and pride |
| Cultural defence | Protecting a culture against perceived external threat |
| Resistance identity | Identity built by excluded groups against dominant forces (Castells) |
| Project identity | New identity aimed at transforming society (Castells) |
| Hybrid identity | Identity combining more than one culture |
| Diaspora | Population dispersed from an original homeland |
| Cultural convergence | Cultures becoming more alike |
| Cultural divergence | Cultures becoming more distinct |
| Cultural imperialism | Dominance of one culture's media and values over others |
| Contra-flow | Media flowing from non-Western centres to global audiences |
| Westernisation | Spread of Western values and lifestyles |
| Orientalism | Western construction of the East as inferior and exotic |

## Worked essay plan

*"Globalisation has strengthened rather than weakened local identities." Evaluate this view.*

1. Set out Hall's three outcomes as the structure.
2. For strengthening: ethnic revitalisation (te reo Māori, Welsh); cultural defence (Bruce, Giddens); resistance identity (Castells).
3. Against: convergence (Ritzer, Schiller); global consumer identities shaped by Western ideology (Said).
4. Third position: hybridity (Back, Hall, Bhabha) — neither strengthening nor erosion.
5. Evaluate: contra-flows and active audiences complicate convergence; responses vary by age, class and place.
6. Judgement: local identities are often strengthened as a reaction, but alongside hybrid and global ones.

## Exam traps

- Treating hybridity, glocalisation and Westernisation as interchangeable.
- Assuming cultural defence is only religious.
- Using Huntington without criticism.
- Describing Western products without discussing **ideology**.
- Ignoring audiences' agency when arguing convergence.
- One-society evidence only; Paper 4 rewards contrasting examples.

## Self-test

1. Give Hall's three possible outcomes of globalisation for identity.
2. Name Nederveen Pieterse's three paradigms.
3. Define ethnic revitalisation and give one example.
4. Distinguish resistance identity from project identity.
5. How does Giddens interpret fundamentalism?
6. What did Back find in south London?
7. What is a contra-flow, and who used the term?
8. What was Tomlinson's criticism of cultural imperialism?
9. Summarise Said's argument in *Orientalism*.
10. Give two ways Western ideology may shape identity.
11. Why might hybridity be less common than some writers suggest?

### Answers

1. National identities erode; local identities strengthen through resistance; new hybrid identities replace declining national ones.
2. Clash, McDonaldization and hybridisation.
3. Revival of an ethnic group's language, customs and pride; for example te reo Māori after the Māori Language Act 1987.
4. Resistance identity defends an excluded group against dominant forces; project identity builds a new identity that aims to transform society.
5. As a defence of tradition in a world where tradition is increasingly questioned.
6. Young people mixed musical styles, language and identities across ethnic boundaries.
7. Media flowing from non-Western centres to global audiences; Thussu (2007).
8. It assumes audiences passively absorb Western meanings, when people reinterpret media through their own culture.
9. Western culture and scholarship constructed the East as backward and exotic, defining the West as rational and modern.
10. Any two: consumer identities around global brands, global beauty ideals, individualistic choice in marriage, career or religion.
11. It fits young, urban, mobile groups best, and racism and inequality limit how freely people can mix identities.
