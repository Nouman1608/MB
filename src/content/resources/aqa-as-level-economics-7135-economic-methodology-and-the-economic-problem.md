This guide covers **3.1.1 Economic methodology and the economic problem** in AQA AS Economics (7135), the first
sub-section of 3.1 The operation of markets and market failure. It has five parts: 3.1.1.1 Economic methodology,
3.1.1.2 The nature and purpose of economic activity, 3.1.1.3 Economic resources, 3.1.1.4 Scarcity, choice and the
allocation of resources, and 3.1.1.5 Production possibility diagrams. The AS wording of these five parts matches
the A-level wording.

The shared [study guide on economic methodology, scarcity and production possibilities](/resources/aqa-a-level-economics-economic-methodology-scarcity-resources-and-production/)
explains each idea in depth. This AS guide is built around the **short-answer and data-response skills** the
sub-section is tested with: classifying statements in an extract, applying the factors of production to a real
business, calculating opportunity cost from a straight-line or curved boundary, and choosing the right movement on
a production possibility diagram. All countries and figures are invented. Condense with the
[revision notes](/resources/aqa-as-level-economics-7135-economic-methodology-and-the-economic-problem-revision-notes/)
and test yourself with the [practice questions](/resources/aqa-as-level-economics-7135-economic-methodology-and-the-economic-problem-practice/).

## 3.1.1.1 Thinking like an economist

The specification describes economics as **a social science** and asks how its methods are similar to, and
different from, those of the natural sciences.

**How an economist investigates a question.** Suppose a city wants to know whether cheaper bus fares will reduce car
use.

1. **Build a model**: lower fares make buses cheaper relative to driving, so some drivers switch.
2. **State assumptions**: other influences, such as fuel prices and incomes, stay the same (**ceteris paribus**).
3. **Form a testable hypothesis**: a 10% fare cut reduces car journeys into the centre.
4. **Test it with evidence**: compare car journeys before and after the cut, or with similar cities that did not cut
   fares.

So far this resembles a natural science. The differences appear at step 4. The economist cannot hold everything else
constant: fuel prices, roadworks and the weather may all change during the trial. People may also change their
behaviour because they know the policy is being studied, or because they expect it to be reversed. Results are
therefore expressed as **tendencies**, and two careful economists can reach different estimates from the same city.

### Positive and normative statements in an extract

A **positive** statement can be tested against evidence. A **normative** statement contains a **value judgement**
about what ought to be. Data-response questions often ask you to find one of each in a passage.

**Original extract (invented):** "Bus use in Westford rose by 6% in the year after fares were cut. Car journeys into
the centre fell slightly. Councillors argue that the subsidy is money well spent, while critics say taxpayers who
never take the bus should not have to pay for it."

- Positive: "Bus use in Westford rose by 6%"; "Car journeys into the centre fell slightly."
- Normative: "the subsidy is money well spent"; "taxpayers who never take the bus should not have to pay for it."

When you quote, copy the exact words and give the reason: "This is positive because it can be checked against
passenger data."

### Value judgements in decisions and policy

The specification says people's views on the best option depend on "the positive consequences of different
decisions" and on "moral and political judgements". In Westford, both sides might accept the 6% figure. The
councillors value cleaner air and help for people without cars; the critics value fairness to taxpayers. The facts
do not settle the argument, because the disagreement is about how much weight each value deserves.

## 3.1.1.2 What economic activity is for

The central purpose of economic activity is **producing goods and services to satisfy needs and wants**. Every
society faces three key decisions. A hospital trust makes all three on a small scale:

- **What to produce:** more hip operations or more mental health services?
- **How to produce:** more nurses, or more automated monitoring equipment?
- **Who benefits:** patients in order of urgency, or in order of waiting time?

## 3.1.1.3 Factors of production in a real business

Applying the four factors to a named business shows understanding better than a list.

| Factor | In a seaside café |
|---|---|
| Land | The site on the seafront; the water and energy it draws on |
| Labour | Baristas, cooks and cleaners |
| Capital | Coffee machines, ovens, tables, the delivery bike |
| Enterprise | The owner, who organised the other factors and risked savings on the business |

Watch the boundary between money and capital: the owner's savings are **not** capital in this sense until they buy
something, such as an oven, that is used to produce other goods.

**The environment is a scarce resource.** Groundwater is a good example. Farms, homes and factories draw on the same
underground supply. If it is pumped faster than rainfall refills it, the water table falls and future users have
less. Using the environment for one purpose reduces what is left for others, so it is scarce like any other
resource.

## 3.1.1.4 Scarcity, choice and opportunity cost

The **fundamental economic problem** is scarcity: limited resources and unlimited wants. Scarcity means choices must
be made, and every choice has an **opportunity cost**, the value of the **next best alternative** given up.

Opportunity cost applies to every decision-maker:

- **A household** choosing a holiday gives up the new sofa it would otherwise have bought.
- **A firm** using its warehouse for storage gives up the rent it could have earned by letting it out.
- **A government** spending on defence gives up the schools or tax cuts the money could have funded.

## 3.1.1.5 Production possibility diagrams with data

### A straight-line boundary: constant opportunity cost

**Worked example (invented).** Country Z's farmland can produce either 600 thousand tonnes of rice or 300 thousand
tonnes of cotton, or any combination on the straight line between.

- Giving up all the rice gains 300 cotton, so **1 tonne of cotton costs 2 tonnes of rice** (600 ÷ 300).
- **1 tonne of rice costs 0.5 tonnes of cotton.**
- Moving from 200 to 250 thousand tonnes of cotton costs 100 thousand tonnes of rice, exactly as it would anywhere
  else on the line.

A straight-line boundary means the opportunity cost is **constant**: every unit of land is equally good at both
crops.

### A curved boundary: rising opportunity cost

Now suppose Country Z has wet lowland that suits rice and dry upland that suits cotton. Switching the first land to
cotton (the upland) costs little rice; switching the last land (the lowland paddies) costs a great deal. The
opportunity cost of cotton **rises** as more is produced, so the boundary bows outwards (concave to the origin).
Most boundaries in examination questions are drawn this way.

### Choosing the right movement

| Event in Country Z | Show it as |
|---|---|
| Farmers switch some land from rice to cotton | A movement along the boundary |
| A drought leaves fields unplanted for a season | A point inside the boundary |
| New irrigation lets all land produce more of both | An outward shift of the whole boundary |
| A new cotton seed raises cotton yields only | A pivot outwards on the cotton axis |
| Flooding permanently ruins some farmland | An inward shift |

### Efficiency on the boundary

Every point on the boundary is **productively efficient**: Country Z cannot grow more rice without growing less cotton.
But not every point is **allocatively efficient**. If the country's people need rice to eat and produce only
cotton, it is on the boundary but producing the wrong mix.

## Common errors

- Writing that a positive statement must be true.
- Copying a sentence from an extract without saying why it is positive or normative.
- Treating money as a factor of production.
- Giving the opportunity cost as everything given up, rather than the next best alternative.
- Inverting the calculation: for "the cost of 1 tonne of cotton", divide the rice given up by the cotton gained.
- Showing a temporary drought as an inward shift of the boundary; idle land is a point inside it.

## Answering AS command words

- **Define:** a precise definition in one or two sentences.
- **Explain:** a reason, linked to a consequence, ideally using the extract or data.
- **Calculate:** show the division and state the units ("tonnes of rice per tonne of cotton").
- **Draw / illustrate:** label axes with the goods and units, label the boundaries and the points.
- **Assess / Evaluate:** separate the positive evidence from the value judgements and reach a reasoned view.
