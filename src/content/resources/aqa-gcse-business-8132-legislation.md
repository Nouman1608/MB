This guide goes deeper into section 3.2.5 Legislation of the AQA GCSE Business (8132) specification, for first teaching from September 2017. It sits in Topic 2, Influences on business, which can be assessed on either written paper. The combined page, [Globalisation and Legislation](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-globalisation-and-legislation/), introduces each law. This page concentrates on what the specification weights most heavily: assessing the effects of the law on a particular business.

Condense it with the [Legislation revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-8132-legislation-revision-notes/) and apply it with the [Legislation practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-8132-legislation-practice/).

## What the specification asks for

| Content | Named laws | What you must do |
|---|---|---|
| Employment law | "national minimum wage/living wage" and "the Equality Act (2010)" | "assess the impact of legislation on businesses, for example cost, training needs, recruitment and the consequences of failure to follow legislation" |
| Health and Safety law | "the Health and Safety at Work Act (1974)" | Also "be aware of the benefits for providing a safe working environment" |
| Consumer law | "including trade descriptions" | As above |

The specification adds: "Only brief knowledge of each law is needed. More emphasis should be placed on the effects of the legislation on businesses." So learn each law in a sentence or two, and spend your effort on impact.

## A framework for impact

The specification's own examples make a reliable checklist. For any law, ask:

1. **Cost:** what does complying cost? Wages, equipment, adaptations, checks.
2. **Training needs:** who needs to learn what? Managers, interviewers, machine operators, sales staff.
3. **Recruitment:** how does the law change hiring? Adverts, interviews, pay offered.
4. **Consequences of failure:** what happens if the business breaks the law? Penalties, compensation, legal costs, damage to reputation, lost sales.

Then add the benefits, because complying is not only a cost. A business that treats staff fairly and keeps them safe often gains through motivation, lower staff turnover and a better reputation.

## Employment law

### National minimum wage and living wage

The government sets legal minimum hourly rates of pay. The National Living Wage is the highest of these rates and applies to older workers; younger workers and apprentices have lower legal minimum rates. The rates are reviewed regularly, so use the figures your teacher gives you rather than learning a number. Separately, some employers choose to pay a voluntary "real Living Wage" set by the Living Wage Foundation, which is higher than the legal minimum. Only the legal rates must be paid.

**Impact:**

- **Cost:** a rise in the legal rate raises the wage bill. The effect is biggest for labour-intensive businesses with many staff on the minimum, such as cafés, care homes, cleaning firms and shops.
- **Knock-on costs:** staff paid slightly above the minimum may expect a rise too, to keep the gap that rewards experience or responsibility.
- **Recruitment and retention:** higher pay can attract more applicants and make staff more likely to stay, cutting recruitment and training costs.
- **Responses:** raise prices, improve productivity, reduce hours, or accept lower profit.

### The Equality Act (2010)

The Act protects people from discrimination because of protected characteristics. There are nine: age, disability, gender reassignment, marriage and civil partnership, pregnancy and maternity, race, religion or belief, sex, and sexual orientation. It covers recruitment, pay, training, promotion and dismissal, and it includes equal pay for men and women doing equal work. Employers must make reasonable adjustments so that disabled employees and applicants are not put at a disadvantage.

**Impact:**

- **Recruitment:** job adverts must not exclude groups unfairly; selection should use criteria linked to the job, such as skills and experience.
- **Training needs:** managers and interviewers need training in fair practice.
- **Cost:** reasonable adjustments, such as ramps, adapted equipment or changed hours; reviewing pay to check for unequal pay.
- **Benefits:** choosing from the widest pool of talent, a more varied workforce with a range of ideas, and a better reputation as an employer.
- **Failure:** an employee or applicant can bring a claim to an employment tribunal, which can order compensation.

## Health and safety law

The Health and Safety at Work Act (1974) places a duty on employers to protect the health, safety and welfare of their employees, so far as is reasonably practicable. Employers are expected to assess risks, provide safe equipment and working conditions, and give information, training and supervision. Employees must take reasonable care of themselves and others and cooperate with their employer. The law is enforced by the Health and Safety Executive and by local authorities.

**Impact:**

- **Cost:** protective equipment, guards on machinery, safety signs, maintenance and inspections.
- **Training needs:** induction safety training for new staff, refresher training, and specialist training for jobs such as operating forklifts.
- **Failure:** inspectors can order changes or stop dangerous work; serious breaches can lead to prosecution and fines; injured staff may claim compensation.

### Benefits of a safe working environment

The specification asks you to be aware of these:

- fewer accidents, so less absence and less disruption to production;
- staff who feel valued and safe, so motivation and productivity improve;
- lower staff turnover, cutting recruitment costs;
- a reputation that helps attract staff and customers;
- avoiding the costs of compensation, fines and legal action.

## Consumer law

The idea of trade descriptions has been part of UK law since the Trade Descriptions Act 1968: a business must not describe goods falsely or in a misleading way, whether on the packaging, in an advert or online. The Consumer Rights Act 2015 says goods must be of satisfactory quality, fit for purpose and as described; if they are not, customers can be entitled to a refund, repair or replacement. Trading Standards officers investigate businesses that break consumer law.

**Impact:**

- **Marketing:** claims about a product, such as "waterproof" or "handmade", must be accurate, so adverts and labels need checking.
- **Operations:** quality control must make sure products match their description.
- **Training needs:** sales staff must describe products accurately and handle returns correctly.
- **Cost:** refunds, repairs and replacements for faulty goods.
- **Failure:** legal action and penalties, and loss of customer trust, which can cost far more than the refund itself.

## Worked example: assessing a minimum wage rise

Figures invented. Two businesses face a rise of 50p an hour in the legal minimum.

| | Larchfield Care Home | Mapleton Software |
|---|---|---|
| Staff on the minimum | 30 | 2 |
| Hours each per year | 1,500 | 1,500 |
| Extra wage cost | 30 × 1,500 × £0.50 = £22,500 | 2 × 1,500 × £0.50 = £1,500 |
| Profit before the rise | £90,000 | £300,000 |
| Extra cost as % of profit | £22,500 ÷ £90,000 = 25% | £1,500 ÷ £300,000 = 0.5% |

The same law has a large effect on the care home and almost none on the software firm. The care home could raise its fees, but many residents may struggle to pay more. It could reduce staff hours, but that could lower the standard of care. Its best response may be to accept lower profit while looking for savings elsewhere, and it may find staff turnover falls, which cuts recruitment costs. This is the kind of weighing an "assess" question rewards.

## Worked example: an annotated paragraph

Question: *Assess the impact of the Health and Safety at Work Act (1974) on a small building firm.*

"Building sites carry high risks, such as falls and heavy machinery, so the firm must provide equipment like harnesses and hard hats and train workers to use them (cost and training). This reduces profit in the short run. However, if a worker were injured, the firm could face compensation claims and the loss of a skilled worker during a busy job (consequence of failure), and a reputation for poor safety could stop customers hiring it. Because the risks are high in building, the cost of compliance is small compared with the cost of an accident, so the overall impact is a necessary cost that protects the business (judgement)."

## Exam technique

- **State or identify:** name a law or an effect; brief knowledge is enough.
- **Explain:** law → requirement → effect on cost, training, recruitment or reputation.
- **Analyse:** follow the effect into a function, for example HR must retrain interviewers.
- **Assess or evaluate:** weigh the costs against the benefits and the cost of failure, decide how significant the impact is for this business, and say why.

## Common mistakes

- Writing long descriptions of the law. The marks are in the effects.
- Quoting old wage rates as if they were current.
- Treating laws only as costs and missing the benefits of compliance.
- Forgetting the consequences of failure.
- Assuming every business is affected equally by a wage rise.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much detail about each law do I need?**
The specification says only brief knowledge of each law is needed. More emphasis should be placed on the effects of legislation on businesses.

**What is the difference between the National Living Wage and the real Living Wage?**
The National Living Wage is a legal minimum that employers must pay. The real Living Wage is a higher, voluntary rate set by the Living Wage Foundation that some employers choose to pay.

**What are the benefits of a safe working environment?**
Fewer accidents and less absence, better motivation, lower staff turnover, a better reputation, and avoiding the costs of compensation and legal action.
