These questions cover section 1.2 of the Cambridge O Level Statistics (4040) syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027: simple random, systematic, stratified and quota sampling, and the use of random numbers. Work from the [1.2 study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-statistics-4040-types-of-sampling/) or the [1.2 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-statistics-4040-types-of-sampling-revision-notes/) if you need to recall a method. All organisations and data are invented. Show your working.

## Section A

**1.** Describe how to use a random number table to select a simple random sample of 20 from a list of 400 people. **[3]**

**2.** State the main difference between stratified sampling and quota sampling. **[2]**

**3.** Explain why a systematic sample needs a random starting point. **[2]**

**4.** The 350 members of a library are numbered 001 to 350. Use the random digits below, read in groups of three from the left, to select a sample of 4 members.

417 032 298 032 156 340 772

**[3]**

**5.** A list of 1,200 voters is used to take a systematic sample of 60.

**(a)** Find the sampling interval. **[1]**

**(b)** The random start is 14. Write down the numbers of the first three voters chosen. **[1]**

**(c)** Find the number of the last voter chosen. **[1]**

## Section B

**6.** A company has 245 staff in sales, 168 in production and 87 in administration.

**(a)** Calculate the number from each department in a stratified sample of 40. **[3]**

**(b)** Describe how the individual staff in each department should then be chosen. **[1]**

**7.** A sports centre has 155 adult members, 103 student members and 62 junior members. A stratified sample of 24 is required.

**(a)** Calculate the exact share for each group. **[2]**

**(b)** Rounding each share to the nearest whole number gives a total that is not 24. Show this, and adjust the numbers so they total 24, explaining your adjustment. **[3]**

**8.** A street has 200 houses, numbered in order. Every 10th house, starting at number 10, is a large corner house. A researcher takes a systematic sample of 20 houses with an interval of 10 and a random start of 10. Explain why the sample is not representative, and suggest how to improve the method. **[3]**

**9.** A market researcher is told to interview 30 men and 30 women in a shopping centre on a Tuesday morning, choosing whom to approach.

**(a)** Name this method of sampling. **[1]**

**(b)** Give **two** reasons why the sample may not represent all adults in the town. **[2]**

**10.** For each situation, name a suitable sampling method and give a reason.

**(a)** A teacher has a numbered list of 90 pupils in Year 10 and wants 15 of them to test a new app. **[2]**

**(b)** A health centre wants opinions from patients in four age groups, which are expected to hold different views, using its patient list. **[2]**

**(c)** A reporter has one hour to ask people in a town square about a new road. **[2]**

**11.** Explain how a calculator's random number function could be used to select a simple random sample of 10 from 750 numbered items. **[2]**

**12.** A head teacher wants the views of 50 pupils on school meals. Years 7 to 11 are expected to have different views. Explain why a stratified sample would be better than a simple random sample here. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Number the people 001 to 400 [1]. Read the random number table in groups of three digits [1], choosing the person with each number, ignoring numbers above 400 and repeats, until 20 have been chosen [1]. **[3]**

**2.** In stratified sampling the individuals in each group are chosen at random [1]; in quota sampling the interviewer chooses them personally [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Without a random start, the first member is chosen by the person taking the sample, so the selection is not random [1]; a random start gives each of the first k members an equal chance, and so gives every member an equal chance of selection [1]. **[2]**

**4.** 417 is above 350, ignore [1]; choose 032, 298 [1]; the second 032 is a repeat, ignore; choose 156 and 340. Sample: 032, 298, 156, 340 [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Writing each number with its decision (chosen, out of range, repeat) earns the method marks even if one reading slips.

**5. (a)** 1200 ÷ 60 = 20 [1].

**(b)** 14, 34, 54 [1].

**(c)** 14 + 59 × 20 = 1194 [1]. **[3]**

**6. (a)** Total = 500 staff. Sales 245/500 × 40 = 19.6, so 20 [1]; production 168/500 × 40 = 13.44, so 13 [1]; administration 87/500 × 40 = 6.96, so 7; total 40 [1].

**(b)** At random within each department, for example by numbering the staff and using random numbers [1]. **[4]**

**7. (a)** Total 320. Adults 155/320 × 24 = 11.625; students 103/320 × 24 = 7.725; juniors 62/320 × 24 = 4.65 [2] (one mark for two correct).

**(b)** Rounding gives 12 + 8 + 5 = 25, one too many [1]. Reduce the share that was rounded up by the most: adults (11.625 rounded up by 0.375) [1]. Sample: 11 adults, 8 students, 5 juniors, total 24 [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Any sensible adjustment that keeps the proportions close is accepted if you explain it; numbers that do not total 24 are not.

**8.** With a start of 10 and an interval of 10, every house chosen is a corner house (10, 20, 30, ...) [1], so the sample contains only large houses and misses the others [1]. Use a simple random sample instead, or shuffle the list before sampling, so the pattern does not match the interval [1]. **[3]**

**9. (a)** Quota sampling [1].

**(b)** On a Tuesday morning, people who work or study then are unlikely to be in the shopping centre [1]. The researcher chooses whom to approach, so may pick people who look approachable, introducing bias [1]. **[3]**

**10. (a)** Simple random sampling [1]; a numbered list exists and there are no groups that need separate representation [1].

**(b)** Stratified sampling [1]; the age groups may differ, so each should be represented in proportion, and the patient list gives a sampling frame [1].

**(c)** Quota sampling [1]; there is no list of people in the square and the survey must be quick [1]. **[6]**

**11.** Use the random function to generate numbers and scale them to whole numbers from 1 to 750 [1], choosing the item with each number and ignoring repeats until 10 items are chosen [1]. **[2]**

**12.** Views differ between year groups [1]. A simple random sample of 50 could by chance include too many pupils from some years and too few from others [1]. A stratified sample takes numbers from each year in proportion to its size, so every year is fairly represented [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- No random start in a systematic sample.
- Equal numbers from each stratum instead of proportional numbers.
- Stratified numbers that do not add up to the sample size.
- Not saying "at random" for choosing within strata.
- Calling a quota sample random.
- Reading random digits in the wrong group size, or counting repeats.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does quota sampling need a list of the population?**
No. Quota sampling only needs the number required from each group; the interviewer finds people to fill each quota, which is why it is quick but open to bias.
