## The five approaches

| Approach | Core assumption | Method | Determinism |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Behaviourist** | Behaviour is **learnt** from the environment | Lab experiments, animals | Environmental |
| **Cognitive** | Mental processes can be studied **scientifically**, by inference | Lab experiments, models | Soft |
| **Biological** | Behaviour has a **physiological** basis | Scans, twin studies | Biological |
| **Psychodynamic** | The **unconscious** drives behaviour | Case studies | Psychic |
| **Humanistic** | People have **free will** | Qualitative interviews | **Rejects determinism** |

**Classical conditioning** — learning by association: NS + UCS → UCR, until CS → CR.
**Operant conditioning** — learning by consequences.

**Negative reinforcement is not punishment.** Both involve something unpleasant, but negative reinforcement *removes* it and therefore **increases** the behaviour; punishment *adds* it and decreases the behaviour. This distinction is examined constantly.

**Social learning theory** adds four mediational processes between stimulus and response — **attention, retention, motor reproduction, motivation** — plus **vicarious reinforcement**, where seeing a model rewarded is enough.

**Psychodynamic:** id (pleasure principle), ego (reality principle), superego (morality principle); psychosexual stages with fixation; defence mechanisms.

**Humanistic:** Maslow's hierarchy culminating in self-actualisation; Rogers's **incongruence** between self-concept and ideal self, caused by **conditions of worth**, treated with **unconditional positive regard**.

## Free will and determinism

| Position | Claim |
|---|---|
| **Free will** | We choose our own behaviour — humanistic |
| **Hard determinism** | All behaviour is caused; free will is an illusion |
| **Soft determinism** | Behaviour is caused but people can exercise conscious choice within those constraints — cognitive |

Types of determinism: **biological** (genes, neurochemistry), **environmental** (conditioning), **psychic** (unconscious conflict).

**The practical stake is real, not abstract.** Determinism makes psychology scientific, since science assumes causation and prediction — but it undermines legal responsibility, because a person who could not have acted otherwise is hard to blame. Free will fits our subjective experience and legal systems, but resists scientific testing.

## Nature and nurture

The modern position is the **interactionist** one: the question is not *which*, but *how they interact*. Two concepts do the work:

- **Diathesis–stress** — a genetic vulnerability (diathesis) is expressed only when triggered by an environmental stressor. It explains why MZ twin concordance for schizophrenia is high but far below 100%.
- **Epigenetics** — environmental factors switch genes on and off without altering the DNA sequence, and those changes can be inherited.

Heritability estimates apply to **populations, not individuals**. Saying "IQ is 50% genetic in this person" is a misreading.

## Reductionism and holism

**Levels of explanation**, from lowest to highest: biological → psychological → social/cultural.

- **Biological reductionism** — explaining behaviour by genes and neurochemistry.
- **Environmental reductionism** — explaining behaviour by stimulus–response links.
- **Holism** — behaviour can only be understood as a whole (humanistic, Gestalt).

Reductionism is scientific and produces testable hypotheses and effective treatments — but risks explaining away meaning. Holism is more complete but harder to test and offers less practical guidance.

## Idiographic and nomothetic

**Idiographic** — the individual case in depth, qualitative, high validity, poor generalisability (psychodynamic case studies, humanistic).
**Nomothetic** — general laws from large samples, quantitative, scientific, but loses the individual (behaviourist, biological, cognitive).

Most modern research combines them: nomothetic findings, illustrated and enriched by idiographic detail.

## Gender and culture bias

| Bias | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Alpha bias** | **Exaggerates** differences between groups |
| **Beta bias** | **Minimises or ignores** differences |
| **Androcentrism** | Male behaviour treated as the norm |
| **Ethnocentrism** | Judging other cultures by one's own standards |
| **Cultural relativism** | Behaviour can only be judged within its own cultural context |

The classic example is **Ainsworth's Strange Situation**: an **imposed etic** — a measure developed in the US and applied elsewhere as though universal — which classifies higher rates of German infants as insecure-avoidant when the behaviour reflects a culture that values early independence.

## Ethical implications and socially sensitive research

Beyond participant protection, consider how findings will be **used**. Research on race and IQ, or on the genetic basis of criminality, may be scientifically defensible yet socially damaging. The debate is not whether such research is ethical to conduct but who funds it, how it is reported, and what policy it invites.

## Exam traps

- Confusing negative reinforcement with punishment.
- Saying the cognitive approach observes mental processes — it **infers** them.
- Treating nature–nurture as a binary rather than an interaction.
- Applying heritability estimates to individuals.
- Confusing alpha with beta bias.
- Describing a debate without applying it to a specific approach or study, which is where the marks are.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish negative reinforcement from punishment.
2. What is soft determinism and which approach holds it?
3. Explain the diathesis–stress model and what it explains about twin studies.
4. Distinguish alpha from beta bias.
5. Why is the Strange Situation described as an imposed etic?

**Answers:** 1. Negative reinforcement removes an unpleasant stimulus and increases the behaviour; punishment adds an unpleasant stimulus and decreases it. 2. The view that behaviour is caused but people can still exercise conscious choice within those constraints; the cognitive approach. 3. A genetic vulnerability is expressed only when triggered by an environmental stressor; it explains why MZ concordance rates for disorders such as schizophrenia are elevated but well below 100%. 4. Alpha bias exaggerates differences between groups; beta bias minimises or ignores them. 5. It was developed within one culture and applied to others as though universal, so behaviours reflecting different child-rearing values are misclassified as insecure attachment.
