**Paper 1 at a glance:** 1 hour 30 minutes, 60 marks. Section A: short answer questions based on the core studies (38 marks). Section B: extended response and essay questions based on the core studies (22 marks). The paper tests the 12 compulsory core studies, the four approaches (biological, cognitive, learning and social) and the AS Level issues and debates.

## The four approaches and their main assumptions

| Approach | Main assumptions (syllabus 3.1.1) |
|---|---|
| **Biological** | Behaviour, cognitions and emotions can be explained in terms of the working of the brain and the effect of hormones, genetics and evolution. Similarities and differences between people can be understood in terms of biological factors and their interaction with other factors. |
| **Cognitive** | Information is processed through the same route in all humans — input, process, output — similar to a computer. People have individual differences in cognitive processing (attention, language, thinking, memory), which can also help explain behaviour and emotion. |
| **Learning** | We all begin life as a blank slate; experiences and interactions with the environment shape behaviour, and these changes are directly observable. We learn through operant conditioning, classical conditioning and social learning, understood through the stimulus-response model. |
| **Social** | Behaviour, cognitions and emotions are influenced by social contexts, social environments and groups, and by the actual, implied or imagined presence of others. |

## The 12 compulsory core studies

Each approach has three core studies. Know which approach each belongs to — the grouping is examined.

**Biological**

| Study | Method and techniques | Psychology investigated |
|---|---|---|
| **Dement and Kleitman** (sleep and dreams) | Experimental and correlational methods; EEGs; REM and non-REM sleep; interview technique | Sleep; dreaming; ultradian rhythms |
| **Hassett et al.** (monkey toy preferences) | Experiment with rhesus monkeys; interactions with stereotypical boys' and girls' toys coded with a behavioural checklist; children's data taken from another study | Sex differences; socialisation; play; the role of hormones |
| **Hölzel et al.** (mindfulness and brain scans) | Experiment with a longitudinal design; MRI scans and self-reports; correlations | Mindfulness; localisation of function |

**Cognitive**

| Study | Method and techniques | Psychology investigated |
|---|---|---|
| **Andrade** (doodling) | Laboratory experiment on whether doodling aids concentration and memory | Attention; memory |
| **Baron-Cohen et al.** (eyes test) | Revision of the 'Reading the Mind in the Eyes' Test with adults including those with Asperger syndrome or high-functioning autism; experiments and correlations | Theory of mind; social sensitivity |
| **Pozzulo et al.** (line-ups) | Laboratory experiment comparing adults and children, using cartoon and human faces in line-ups; interviews and questionnaires | False positive responses; eyewitness testimony |

**Learning**

| Study | Method and techniques | Psychology investigated |
|---|---|---|
| **Bandura et al.** (aggression) | Experiment comparing several variables, with observation to collect data; tested whether children imitate an aggressive adult model | Social learning theory; aggression |
| **Fagen et al.** (elephant learning) | Observation with a behavioural checklist; positive and secondary reinforcement training for a trunk wash in working elephants | Operant conditioning; reinforcement (positive, negative, primary and secondary); shaping; behavioural chaining |
| **Saavedra and Silverman** (button phobia) | Case study of a child; interviews, scales and observations; treatment targeting disgust and fear responses | Evaluative learning; operant conditioning; classical conditioning; phobias |

**Social**

| Study | Method and techniques | Psychology investigated |
|---|---|---|
| **Milgram** (obedience) | Laboratory setting measuring obedience (a 'dependent variable', but no independent variable in the main study); observations and an interview; considers dispositional and situational hypotheses | Obedience; social pressure |
| **Perry et al.** (personal space) | Two experiments comparing oxytocin with a placebo; a computerised Comfortable Interpersonal Distance Scale, a choice between pictures of rooms, and questionnaires | Interpersonal distance (personal space); social hormones; empathy |
| **Piliavin et al.** (subway Samaritans) | Field experiment using observation; how bystanders behave in real-life situations | Bystander apathy; diffusion of responsibility |

For every core study, be ready to give: the psychology investigated, the background, the aim(s), the procedure (research method, sample, design, controls, variables), the ethical issues, the results, the conclusions, and strengths and weaknesses of every element.

**Negative reinforcement is not punishment** (a Fagen et al. and learning-approach trap). Negative reinforcement *removes* something unpleasant and so **increases** a behaviour; punishment *adds* something unpleasant and decreases it.

## The five AS Level issues and debates

Consider each in relation to each core study, where appropriate.

| Issue or debate | Core studies that raise it clearly |
|---|---|
| **The application of psychology to everyday life** | Pozzulo et al. (police line-ups), Saavedra and Silverman (treating a phobia), Fagen et al. (elephant welfare and training), Hölzel et al. (mindfulness), Andrade (doodling and concentration) |
| **Individual and situational explanations** | Milgram (dispositional versus situational hypotheses), Perry et al. (empathy as an individual difference), Baron-Cohen et al. (individual differences in theory of mind), Piliavin et al. (features of the situation and the victim) |
| **Nature versus nurture** | Hassett et al. (hormones versus socialisation in toy preferences), Bandura et al. (aggression learned by imitation), Saavedra and Silverman (a phobia treated through conditioning), Dement and Kleitman (biological sleep stages) |
| **The use of children in psychological research** | Bandura et al., Pozzulo et al., Saavedra and Silverman |
| **The use of animals in psychological research** | Hassett et al. (rhesus monkeys), Fagen et al. (working elephants) |

## A Level issues and debates — for the specialist options, not Paper 1

At A Level the list has eight issues and debates, applied to the **specialist options** on Papers 3 and 4: the application of psychology to everyday life, individual and situational explanations, nature versus nurture, the use of children in psychological research, **cultural differences**, **reductionism versus holism**, **determinism versus free-will** and **idiographic versus nomothetic** (the use of animals is on the AS list only). The four that are new at A Level are summarised here so you have them in one place; they are **not** Paper 1 content.

**Determinism versus free-will**

| Position | Claim |
|---|---|
| **Free will** | People choose their own behaviour |
| **Hard determinism** | All behaviour is caused; free will is an illusion |
| **Soft determinism** | Behaviour is caused, but people can exercise conscious choice within those constraints |

Kinds of cause often distinguished: **biological** (genes, hormones, brain structure) and **environmental** (learning and experience). Determinism supports scientific prediction but sits uneasily with holding people responsible for their actions; free will fits everyday experience and legal responsibility but is hard to test scientifically.

**Reductionism versus holism**

- **Reductionism** explains behaviour by breaking it into simpler parts or a lower level of explanation — for example biological reductionism (neurochemistry, genes) or explaining behaviour by stimulus–response links.
- **Holism** argues behaviour must be understood as a whole, including the person, their thinking and their social context.

Reductionist explanations are testable and can lead to effective treatments but may miss the meaning of behaviour; holistic explanations are more complete but harder to test.

**Idiographic versus nomothetic**

- **Idiographic** — studying the individual in depth, often qualitatively; rich and valid for that person, but hard to generalise.
- **Nomothetic** — seeking general laws from larger samples, usually quantitatively; generalisable, but can lose the individual.

**Cultural differences**

Whether behaviour, and the findings about it, vary between cultures: a study conducted in one culture may not generalise to another, and a measure developed in one culture may not be valid when applied elsewhere. Apply it to the specific studies in the option you are answering on.

## Exam traps

- Placing a core study under the wrong approach.
- Describing a debate in general terms without applying it to a named core study, which is where the marks are.
- Confusing negative reinforcement with punishment.
- Saying Milgram's main study had an independent variable — the syllabus notes it did not.
- Importing A Level debates (free will, reductionism) or content from other boards' specifications (psychodynamic or humanistic approaches, gender bias) into a Paper 1 answer.
