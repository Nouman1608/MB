These notes condense **4.2.1.3 Ionic compounds** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) specification (for
teaching from September 2016, version 1.1). It is Paper 1 content for both tiers, with no Higher-only
statements and no required practical. For explanations and worked examples, use the
[ionic compounds study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-ionic-compounds/); to test yourself, use
the [ionic compounds practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-ionic-compounds-practice/).

## Core facts

- An ionic compound is a **giant structure of ions** (a lattice).
- It is held together by **strong electrostatic forces of attraction** between **oppositely charged ions**.
- The forces act **in all directions** in the lattice; this is **ionic bonding**.
- The ions are arranged in a **regular, repeating** pattern, with positive and negative ions alternating.
- The formula is an **empirical formula**: the simplest whole-number ratio of ions, not a molecule.
- Know the structure of **sodium chloride**; other structures will be given to you.

## Sodium chloride at a glance

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Ions | Na⁺ (smaller) and Cl⁻ (larger) |
| Neighbours of each ion | Six ions of the opposite charge |
| Ratio | 1 : 1, so the empirical formula is NaCl |
| Shape of the repeat | Cubic |
| Molecules present | None |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Lattice | A regular, repeating 3D arrangement of particles |
| Giant structure | A structure with a vast number of particles and no separate molecules |
| Ionic bonding | Electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions, acting in all directions in the lattice |
| Empirical formula | The simplest whole-number ratio of each kind of particle |
| Ball and stick model | Balls for ions, sticks for the attractions between neighbours |
| Space-filling model | Spheres drawn to relative size and packed together |
| Key | The label telling you which symbol or colour is which ion |

## How to recognise an ionic diagram

Ionic if you see **both**:

- two types of particle with **opposite charges** (or a key naming a metal ion and a non-metal ion)
- a **regular, repeating** arrangement that continues past the edge.

Not ionic if you see separate small groups of atoms, a network of one kind of atom joined by lines with no
charges, or positive particles among free electrons with no negative ions.

## Limitations of each diagram

| Diagram | Main limitation for a giant ionic structure |
|---|---|
| Dot and cross | Shows only one pair of ions; suggests separate pairs instead of a lattice; no 3D arrangement |
| Ball and stick | Sticks imply bonds to particular ions; gaps between ions are not real; sizes may be wrong |
| 2D | Only one layer; each ion seems to have four neighbours instead of six |
| 3D space-filling | Inner ions are hidden, so the arrangement is hard to see; forces not shown |
| All of them | Show only a tiny part of a lattice containing a vast number of ions |

Answer pattern: **feature of the diagram + what is really true**.

## Empirical formula method

1. Identify the ions from the key.
2. Count each kind (or count one repeating unit).
3. Simplify the ratio by dividing by the highest common factor.
4. Metal first; subscripts only above 1.
5. Check total charge = 0 if charges are known or given.

**Reminders**

- 4 Ca²⁺ : 8 F⁻ = 1 : 2 → **CaF₂**
- 9 Na⁺ : 9 Br⁻ = 1 : 1 → **NaBr**
- 6 K⁺ : 3 S²⁻ = 2 : 1 → **K₂S**
- 4 Al³⁺ : 6 O²⁻ = 2 : 3 → **Al₂O₃**

If a small drawing gives an odd count such as 13 : 12, it is an edge effect. Use the alternating pattern and
the charges to find the true ratio.

## Exam traps

- **Raw counts as a formula.** Ca₄F₈ is wrong; CaF₂ is right.
- **"Molecule of sodium chloride".** There are no molecules in an ionic lattice.
- **Bonding "between each pair of ions".** Each ion attracts all its oppositely charged neighbours.
- **Assuming 1 : 1.** Only ions with equal and opposite charges combine 1 : 1.
- **Vague limitations.** "It's only a model" earns nothing; name the stick, the gap, the layer or the
  hidden ions.
- **Atoms instead of ions.** The particles in the lattice are ions.

## Quick self-test

1. What type of structure does an ionic compound have?
2. What holds the ions together?
3. In which directions do the forces act?
4. How many oppositely charged neighbours does each ion have in sodium chloride?
5. Which ion is larger in sodium chloride?
6. Why is NaCl called an empirical formula rather than a molecular formula?
7. Give two features of a diagram that show a compound is ionic.
8. State one limitation of a dot and cross diagram for sodium chloride.
9. State one limitation of a ball and stick model.
10. A model shows 6 lithium ions and 3 oxide ions. Give the empirical formula.
11. A model shows 10 Mg²⁺ ions and 20 Br⁻ ions. Give the empirical formula.
12. Why does a 2D diagram underestimate the number of neighbours an ion has?

### Answers

1. A giant ionic lattice (giant structure of ions).
2. Strong electrostatic forces of attraction between oppositely charged ions.
3. In all directions.
4. Six.
5. The chloride ion, Cl⁻.
6. It gives the simplest ratio of ions in a giant lattice; there are no separate molecules.
7. Two kinds of particle with opposite charges; a regular repeating pattern (or a key naming a metal ion and
   a non-metal ion).
8. It shows only one pair of ions, not the giant lattice (or it gives no 3D arrangement).
9. The sticks suggest bonds to particular ions, when the forces act in all directions (or it shows gaps
   between ions that are not really there).
10. Li₂O.
11. MgBr₂.
12. It shows one layer only, so the neighbours above and below are missing.
