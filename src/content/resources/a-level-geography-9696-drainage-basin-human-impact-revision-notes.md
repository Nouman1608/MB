Condensed recall for **1.4 The human impact** on drainage basins: flood risk and its management. For full explanations, read the [Core Physical Geography study guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-geography-core-physical-geography/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Flood risk | Likelihood of flooding combined with the exposure of people and property |
| Hard engineering | Built structures that control the river |
| Soft engineering | Working with natural processes and land-use planning |
| Floodplain zoning | Restricting what can be built on land likely to flood |
| Afforestation | Planting trees, to increase interception and slow runoff |
| Washland | Floodplain deliberately left to store flood water |
| Recurrence interval | Average time between floods of a given size, such as a "1 in 100 year" flood |
| Natural flood management | Using natural features, such as woodland, leaky dams and wetlands, to slow and store water upstream |
| Abstraction | Removal of water from rivers or groundwater for use |

## How people increase flood risk

- **Urbanisation:** impermeable surfaces and drains cut infiltration and speed runoff, giving a shorter lag time and higher peak.
- **Deforestation:** less interception and transpiration, more overland flow, and soil erosion that silts channels.
- **Agriculture:** compaction, ploughing down slopes and removing hedges.
- **Channel changes and building on floodplains:** put more people and property in harm's way.
- **Draining wetlands:** removes natural storage.

People also change the drainage basin in other ways: dams and reservoirs store water and even out flow downstream; abstraction for irrigation, industry and homes lowers flows and water tables; and climate change may make intense rainfall more frequent. Not every human impact raises flood risk: reservoirs and afforestation lower it.

## Physical causes alongside human ones

Prolonged or intense rainfall, rapid snowmelt, saturated or frozen ground, steep slopes and impermeable rock all raise flood risk. A flood usually happens when several of these combine, and human changes then make the response faster or put more people in the way.

## Management strategies

| Strategy | Type | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dams and reservoirs | Hard | Store flood water, may generate power | Costly, trap sediment, affect habitats |
| Embankments / levées | Hard | Raise channel capacity | Can raise flood height downstream; failure is severe |
| Channel straightening or deepening | Hard | Moves water away faster | Passes the problem downstream |
| Flood relief channels | Hard | Divert excess water | Costly, land needed |
| Flood walls and demountable barriers | Hard | Protect a town centre in a small space | Can be overtopped; give a false sense of security |
| Floodplain zoning | Soft | Cheap, reduces exposure | Needs enforcement; limits development |
| Afforestation | Soft | Reduces runoff | Slow to take effect |
| Washlands and wetland restoration | Soft | Natural storage, habitats | Land taken from other uses |
| Natural flood management upstream | Soft | Slows water; cheap per site; habitat gains | Effect is small in very large floods |
| Forecasting and warnings | Soft | Allow evacuation | Does not stop flooding |
| Property-level protection and insurance | Soft | Reduces damage; spreads cost | Insurance can be unaffordable in high-risk areas |

**The levée effect:** building defences encourages more development behind them. When an extreme flood overtops the defences, losses are greater than if the land had been left undeveloped.

## Required case study

The syllabus asks for a **recent river flood event**: its causes, its impacts on people and the environment, and an evaluation of attempts to reduce its impact. Prepare one named event with your own checked facts.

## Worked case study outline: Cumbria floods, December 2015 (UK, HIC)

- **Causes:** **Storm Desmond** brought prolonged, intense rain to already wet uplands of the Lake District; Honister Pass recorded about 341 mm in 24 hours, a UK record at the time. Steep, impermeable upland catchments responded quickly, and rivers including the **Eden**, Kent and Derwent reached very high levels. Towns such as **Carlisle**, Keswick, Appleby and Kendal flooded.
- **Impacts on people:** thousands of homes and businesses flooded; bridges and roads were destroyed or closed, cutting off some communities; power cuts; people displaced for months; high insurance and repair costs.
- **Impacts on the environment:** riverbank and hillslope erosion, landslides, gravel deposited on farmland, damage to habitats and pollution from flooded sewage works.
- **Attempts to reduce impact:** Carlisle had flood defences built after severe floods in 2005, but these were overtopped in 2015. Afterwards the Environment Agency and partners produced a Cumbria flood action plan combining raised and extended defences, better warnings and maintenance, and **natural flood management** upstream (tree planting, leaky dams, peatland restoration).
- **Evaluation:** defences reduced damage in smaller floods but could not cope with an extreme event; upstream measures are cheaper and bring wider benefits, but their effect on very large floods is uncertain; the event raised the question of whether defence standards should rise as heavy rainfall becomes more likely.

**Contrast (MIC):** the **2022 Pakistan floods**, caused by extreme monsoon rainfall, affected around 33 million people and killed over 1700. Weak embankments, poor drainage and limited warnings in low-lying Sindh made impacts far larger, which shows how development level shapes vulnerability.

## Annotated model paragraph

*"Evaluate attempts to reduce the impact of one river flood."*

> Carlisle's defences, built after 2005, were overtopped in 2015 (**limitation of hard engineering**). Upstream natural flood management was added to slow runoff (**response**), though its effect in extreme floods is uncertain (**qualification**). A mix of defences, warnings and land management is likely to be most effective (**judgement**).

## Exam traps

- Floods have both human and physical causes; separate them.
- Evaluate: who benefits, who pays, and does the scheme just move the problem?
- Do not claim hard engineering always fails or soft always works.
- "Recent" means you should know a named event with a date, not a general type of flood.
- A "1 in 100 year" flood can happen in two consecutive years; it is an average probability.
