## Section A

**1.** State the meaning of *stare decisis*. **[1]**

**2.** Identify **three** sources of persuasive precedent. **[3]**

**3.** State what is meant by a decision made *per incuriam*. **[2]**

**4.** Name the case in which the Supreme Court confirmed that the Practice Statement 1966 applies to it. **[1]**

## Section B

**5.** Explain the difference between ratio decidendi and obiter dicta, using *R v Howe* (1987) and *R v Gotts* (1992). **[4]**

**6.** Explain the position of the Court of Appeal (Civil Division) in relation to its own previous decisions. **[5]**

**7.** Explain why the Court of Appeal (Criminal Division) has more flexibility than the Civil Division to depart from its own decisions. **[3]**

**8.** Explain the status of decisions of the Privy Council in English courts, referring to **two** cases. **[4]**

**9.** Outline the development of law reporting and **two** reasons why it is necessary for precedent. **[5]**

**10.** In a fictional case, *Patel v Northshire Council* (2015), the Court of Appeal held that a council owed no duty of care to a person injured on a closed playground. Ruth is injured on a council playground that was open but poorly lit. Her case is heard in the High Court. Explain how the judge might deal with *Patel*. **[6]**

**11.** Explain how the House of Lords used the Practice Statement in *R v G* (2003), and why it uses the power sparingly. **[4]**

**12.** "The doctrine of judicial precedent provides certainty at the expense of justice." Discuss. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** To stand by what has been decided, so like cases are decided alike [1].

**2.** Any three: decisions of lower courts [1]; obiter dicta [1]; dissenting judgments; decisions of the Privy Council; decisions of courts in other common law countries [1].

**3.** A decision made in error [1], because the court overlooked a relevant statute or binding authority [1].

**4.** *Austin v Southwark London Borough Council* (2010) [1].

**5.** The ratio decidendi is the legal principle on which the decision is based, applied to the material facts, and it binds lower courts [1]; obiter dicta are other remarks, including comments on hypothetical facts, and are only persuasive [1]. In *R v Howe* the ratio was that duress is not a defence to murder, and the House of Lords said obiter that it should not be a defence to attempted murder [1]. In *R v Gotts* the House of Lords followed that obiter statement, showing how persuasive obiter can later become binding ratio [1].

**6.** The Civil Division is generally bound by its own previous decisions [1], as held in *Young v Bristol Aeroplane Co* (1944), subject to three exceptions [1]: where there are two conflicting decisions of its own, it may choose which to follow [1]; where its earlier decision conflicts with a later decision of the House of Lords or Supreme Court, it must follow the latter [1]; and where the earlier decision was made per incuriam [1].

**7.** In criminal cases a person's liberty is at stake [1], so it would be unjust to follow a decision in which the law was misapplied [1]. In *R v Taylor* (1950) and *R v Gould* (1968) the court accepted it may depart from its own previous decisions in such cases [1].

**8.** Privy Council decisions are normally persuasive only, but highly persuasive because its members are usually Supreme Court justices [1]; for example *The Wagon Mound (No 1)* (1961) was followed by English courts on remoteness of damage [1]. In *R v James* (2006) the Court of Appeal followed *Attorney General for Jersey v Holley* (2005) rather than a House of Lords decision, because the Privy Council had sat with nine judges to settle the law [1]. In *Willers v Joyce* (2016) the Supreme Court confirmed that the Privy Council can direct that its decision represents English law, in which case courts should follow it [1].

**9.** Early cases were recorded in the Year Books, followed by private reports of varying reliability [1]. The Incorporated Council of Law Reporting, founded in 1865, publishes *The Law Reports*, checked by the judges [1]; since 2001 judgments have neutral citations and are published online [1]. Reasons, any two: precedent depends on knowing exactly what the ratio was [1]; lawyers need reliable reports to advise clients and judges to identify binding authority; reports promote consistency and accessibility [1].

**10.** The High Court is bound by decisions of the Court of Appeal [1], so if the material facts of Ruth's case are sufficiently similar, the judge must follow *Patel* and hold that no duty was owed [1]. However, the judge may distinguish *Patel* on its material facts [1]: in *Patel* the playground was closed, while Ruth's was open to the public, so the council arguably invited users and the reasoning about a closed site may not apply [1]. The judge cannot overrule *Patel*, because only a higher court can do so [1]. If the judge follows *Patel* reluctantly, Ruth could appeal, and the Court of Appeal or Supreme Court could reconsider it [1].

**11.** In *R v Caldwell* (1981) the House of Lords had adopted an objective test of recklessness, which could convict people, including children, who did not foresee a risk [1]. In *R v G* (2003) it used the Practice Statement to depart from *Caldwell*, holding recklessness requires the defendant personally to foresee the risk [1]. The power is used sparingly because frequent changes would undermine certainty [1], on which people rely in arranging their affairs; in *Knuller v DPP* (1972) the House refused to overrule *Shaw v DPP* for this reason [1].

**12.** For the statement: lower courts must follow binding precedents even when they produce unjust outcomes, as before *R v G* when *Caldwell* had to be applied [1]; the Court of Appeal is bound by its own decisions except in narrow *Young* exceptions [1]; the Supreme Court uses the Practice Statement sparingly, as in *Knuller* [1]; overruling only happens if a suitable case is appealed, which can take years [1]. Against: distinguishing allows any court to avoid unjust precedents where facts differ, as in *R v Wilson* [1]; the Practice Statement has corrected injustice, as in *Herrington* and *R v G* [1]; certainty is itself an aspect of justice, because like cases are treated alike (formal justice) [1]; the Criminal Division can depart where liberty is at stake [1]. Conclusion: precedent leans towards certainty, but its mechanisms of flexibility mean justice is usually achieved eventually, if slowly [1] [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- In scenarios, failing to state which court is bound by which before discussing options.
- Saying a lower court can overrule a higher court's decision.
- Distinguishing without naming the specific material fact that differs.
- In essays, failing to link certainty to justice as formal justice.
