Condensed notes on section **3.7.5 Analysing the external environment to assess opportunities and threats: economic change** of AQA A-level Business (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). Section 3.7 is A-level only. The Tarrant Engineering exchange rate example is in the [study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-external-environment-economic-change/); questions are in the [practice set](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-external-environment-economic-change-practice/).

## Specification content

- The impact of changes in the UK and the global economic environment on strategic and functional decision making.
- Economic factors: GDP, taxation, exchange rates, inflation, fiscal and monetary policy, more open trade versus protectionism.
- Understanding economic data, interpreting changes in economic data for the UK and globally, and considering the implications for business.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| GDP | Total value of goods and services produced in a country in a period |
| Recession | Falling real GDP, commonly two quarters in a row |
| Income elasticity of demand (YED) | % change in demand ÷ % change in income |
| Exchange rate | Price of one currency in terms of another |
| Inflation | Sustained rise in the general price level |
| Fiscal policy | Government use of taxes and spending |
| Monetary policy | Use of interest rates and money supply to control inflation and growth |
| Protectionism | Policies protecting domestic producers from imports |

## Quick effects table

| Change | Demand | Costs and finance |
|---|---|---|
| GDP rises | Demand rises, especially for luxuries (YED above 1) | Labour and input costs may rise |
| GDP falls | Demand falls; inferior goods may rise (negative YED) | Spare capacity; price cuts |
| Corporation tax rises | Little direct effect | Lower after-tax return, less investment |
| VAT or income tax rises | Demand falls, more for elastic goods | Pay demands may rise |
| Pound strengthens | Exports dearer abroad, imports cheaper | Imported inputs cheaper |
| Pound weakens | Exports cheaper abroad, imports dearer | Imported inputs dearer |
| Inflation rises | Real incomes squeezed | Input and wage costs rise; planning harder |
| Interest rates rise | Spending on credit falls | Borrowing costs rise; investment falls |
| Government spending rises | Demand rises for suppliers to the state | Possible higher taxes later |
| Protectionism rises | Export markets may shrink | Imported inputs dearer |

## YED guide

- Above 1: luxury (holidays, cars). Demand swings widely with GDP.
- Between 0 and 1: necessity. Demand is stable.
- Negative: inferior good (own-brand basics). Demand rises in a recession.

## Exchange rates

- Weaker pound: exports cheaper abroad; imports dearer; inbound tourism rises.
- Stronger pound: exports dearer; imports cheaper; overseas travel cheaper for UK residents.
- Tools: invoice in sterling; buy currency forward; match costs and revenues in the same currency (natural hedge).
- Remember: the effect depends on how much of the business's costs and revenues are overseas and how price-sensitive customers are.

## Inflation

- **Demand-pull:** spending grows faster than output.
- **Cost-push:** costs of labour, energy or materials rise.
- Real change in pay: (1 + pay rise) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1.
- Businesses with strong brands raise prices more easily.
- Deflation is also a problem: delayed purchases and heavier real debt.

## Policy

| | Fiscal | Monetary |
|---|---|---|
| Who | Government | Central bank |
| Tools | Taxes, public spending | Interest rates, money supply |
| Expansionary | Cut taxes, spend more | Cut interest rates |
| Contractionary | Raise taxes, spend less | Raise interest rates |
| Hits hardest | Public sector suppliers; consumer sectors | Highly geared firms; credit-financed goods |

## Open trade against protectionism

- **Open trade:** bigger markets, cheaper inputs, more competition.
- **Protectionism:** shelters domestic producers; dearer imports; risk of retaliation and shrinking exports.

## Reading data

1. State direction and size.
2. Real or nominal?
3. Percentage or percentage points?
4. Which markets and costs of this business are affected?
5. Limits: forecasts; averages; time lags.

## Model analysis paragraph

"A fall in the value of the pound should help this exporter because its machines become cheaper for overseas buyers, so demand may rise. However, the firm imports components priced in dollars, so those costs rise in sterling terms. The overall effect depends on the share of costs that are imported against the share of sales made abroad. As exports are the larger part of its business, the fall is probably an opportunity, but the firm should plan for volatility."

## Command words

- **Analyse:** link the change to demand, cost or finance, then to a decision.
- **Evaluate:** consider the size, duration and the type of business affected.

## Exam traps

- Link every change to demand, costs or finance, and then to a decision.
- A weaker pound is not automatically good.
- Recessions help some businesses (discount retailers, repair services).
- Fiscal policy is government; monetary policy is the central bank.
- Use "percentage points" for differences between percentages.

## Self-test

1. Define "recession".
2. Demand for a firm's cars rises 9% when incomes rise 3%. Calculate YED and say what type of good this suggests.
3. Explain why a fall in the value of the pound might raise export sales.
4. A UK firm buys €90,000 of goods when £1 = €1.20. Calculate the sterling cost.
5. Calculate the sterling cost if the rate falls to £1 = €1.00.
6. Explain one way rising interest rates could hurt a highly geared business.
7. A pay rise of 4% is given when inflation is 6%. Is real pay rising or falling?
8. Distinguish fiscal policy from monetary policy.
9. Give one way a government spending cut might affect a construction firm.
10. Give two possible disadvantages of protectionism for a UK business.
11. Why might a recession be an opportunity for a discount supermarket?
12. Why should a business be cautious about economic forecasts?

### Answers

1. A fall in real GDP, commonly for two consecutive quarters.
2. 9% ÷ 3% = **+3**; a luxury or normal good with high income elasticity.
3. UK goods become cheaper for overseas buyers, so demand for exports may rise.
4. €90,000 ÷ 1.20 = **£75,000**.
5. €90,000 ÷ 1.00 = **£90,000**, an increase of £15,000.
6. Variable-rate interest payments rise, taking more of its profit and cash.
7. Falling: about 1.04 ÷ 1.06 − 1 = −1.9%.
8. Fiscal policy uses taxes and government spending; monetary policy uses interest rates and money supply.
9. Fewer public contracts for roads, schools or hospitals, so lower demand and possibly redundancies.
10. Any two: dearer imported inputs; retaliation against exports; less competition leading to complacency; supply chain disruption.
11. Customers trade down to cheaper products, so demand for its lower-priced goods rises.
12. They can be wrong, hide differences between regions and sectors, and conditions may change.
