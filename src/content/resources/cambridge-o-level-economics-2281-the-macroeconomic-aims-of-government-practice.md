This set tests section 4.2 **The macroeconomic aims of government** of the Cambridge O Level Economics 2281 syllabus for examination in 2026: the aims, the reasons for them and their criteria, and the possible conflicts between them. Countries and figures are invented. Teaching is in the [macroeconomic aims study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-the-macroeconomic-aims-of-government/) and the [macroeconomic aims revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-the-macroeconomic-aims-of-government-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Which is not one of the macroeconomic aims named in the syllabus?

A: Economic growth

B: Balance of payments stability

C: Maximising the profits of state-owned firms

D: Redistribution of income

**[1]**

**2.** Which best describes the aim of stable prices?

A: No change in any price

B: A low and steady rate of inflation

C: Falling prices every year

D: Prices fixed by the government

**[1]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by a criterion for a macroeconomic aim. **[2]**

**4.** In Country J, nominal GDP rises from 850 to 900 in a year while prices rise by 6%.

**(a)** Calculate real GDP in the second year, in first-year prices. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the real growth rate and state what it shows. **[2]**

**5.** In Year 1, Country J's labour force is 7 million and 0.42 million people are unemployed. In Year 2 the labour force is 7.5 million and 0.3 million are unemployed.

**(a)** Calculate the unemployment rate in each year. **[2]**

**(b)** The government's criterion is an unemployment rate below 5%. State in which year it was met. **[1]**

**6.** Country J's current account deficit is 36 and its GDP is 1,200. Its criterion is a deficit no larger than 3% of GDP. State, with a calculation, whether the criterion is met. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** The table shows data for Country K.

| Indicator | Year 1 | Year 2 | Criterion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Real GDP growth | 0.5% | 3.5% | 2% to 4% |
| Unemployment rate | 8.0% | 5.5% | Below 5% |
| Inflation | 1.2% | 3.8% | 1% to 3% |
| Current account | −0.5% of GDP | −2.5% of GDP | Deficit no more than 3% of GDP |

**(a)** Identify which criteria were met in Year 2. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain what the data suggest about the conflict between full employment and stable prices. **[3]**

**8.** Explain two reasons why a government aims for economic growth. **[4]**

**9.** Explain why a government might choose low inflation as its main aim. **[3]**

**10.** Explain how faster economic growth could worsen a country's balance of payments. **[3]**

**11.** Analyse why the conflict between full employment and balance of payments stability may not arise in some economies. **[4]**

**12.** Discuss whether a government should make reducing unemployment its top macroeconomic aim. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** C [1]. The syllabus aims are growth, full employment, stable prices, balance of payments stability and redistribution of income. **[1]**

**2.** B [1]. Stable prices means low, predictable inflation, not zero. **[1]**

**3.** A measurable standard or target [1] used to judge whether the aim has been met, for example inflation between 1% and 3% a year [1]. **[2]**

**4. (a)** 900 ÷ 1.06 = 849.1 [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** (849.1 − 850) ÷ 850 × 100 = −0.1% [1]. Although nominal GDP rose, real output fell slightly: the whole rise was due to higher prices [1]. **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* Nominal growth of 5.9% looks healthy, which is exactly why growth must be judged on real GDP.

**5. (a)** Year 1: 0.42 ÷ 7 × 100 = 6% [1]. Year 2: 0.3 ÷ 7.5 × 100 = 4% [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Year 2 [1]. **[1]**

**6.** 36 ÷ 1,200 × 100 = 3% [1]. The deficit equals the limit, so the criterion is (just) met [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Growth (3.5% is within 2% to 4%) [1] and the current account (deficit of 2.5% is within 3%) [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Unemployment fell from 8.0% to 5.5% while inflation rose from 1.2% to 3.8% [1]. This fits the conflict: as more workers are hired, labour becomes scarcer, wages and costs rise, and higher demand lets firms raise prices [1]. Inflation has passed its criterion before unemployment has reached its target, so the government faces a trade-off between the two aims [1]. **[3]**

**8.** First: growth raises output and incomes [1], so living standards can rise for the population [1]. Second: a larger economy generates more tax revenue without higher tax rates [1], which can pay for public services such as schools and hospitals [1]. (Credit also: growth creates jobs, reducing unemployment.) **[4]**

**9.** High inflation reduces the purchasing power of money, harming savers and people on fixed incomes [1]. It creates uncertainty, so firms find it harder to plan and may invest less [1]. If prices rise faster than in other countries, exports become less competitive, so low inflation also supports growth and the balance of payments [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Growth raises incomes, so households and firms spend more [1]. Part of the extra spending goes on imports [1]; if exports do not rise as fast, the current account deficit widens, and strong demand may also push up domestic prices, making exports less competitive [1]. **[3]**

**11.** The conflict arises because extra income from new jobs is partly spent on imports [1]. If the new jobs are in export industries, exports rise along with employment, so the current account may improve rather than worsen [1]. If the economy produces most of what its people buy, only a small share of extra income goes on imports [1], and if there is spare capacity, more output need not raise prices and hurt exports [1]. **[4]**

**12.** For: unemployment wastes resources, so output is lower than it could be [1]; it costs the government lost tax revenue and higher benefit spending [1], and long-term unemployment causes poverty and loss of skills [1]. Against: pushing unemployment very low may raise inflation as wages rise [1], and may widen the current account deficit as spending on imports rises [1]. Judgement: reducing unemployment should be the top aim when unemployment is high and there is spare capacity, because the conflicts are then weak; when unemployment is already low and inflation is rising, stable prices may deserve priority [1]. **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* A judgement tied to the economy's current position ("when unemployment is high and there is spare capacity…") is stronger than a general statement that all aims matter.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Judging growth on nominal GDP.
- Dividing unemployed people by those in work instead of by the labour force.
- Defining stable prices as zero inflation.
- Stating a conflict without explaining the chain behind it.
- Treating the named conflicts as certain in every economy.
- Not using the table when the question asks what the data show.
