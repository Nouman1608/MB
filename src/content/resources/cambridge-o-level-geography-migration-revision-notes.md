Condensed notes for sub-topic 1.2 of Cambridge O Level Geography (2217), 2026 series.

## Types of migration

- **Internal** (within a country) vs **international** (across a border).
- **Voluntary** (choice, usually economic) vs **involuntary / forced** (conflict, persecution,
  disaster).
- **Temporary / seasonal** vs **permanent**.
- **Rural–urban** (drives urbanisation) vs **urban–rural** (counter-urbanisation).

```
net migration = immigrants - emigrants
rate per 1,000 = net migration / population x 1,000
```

## Push, pull and obstacles

| | Push | Pull |
|---|---|---|
| Economic | Unemployment, low wages, land shortage | Jobs, higher wages |
| Social | Poor schools and health care | Better services, family networks |
| Political | War, persecution | Safety, freedom |
| Environmental | Drought, floods | Reliable water, lower hazard risk |

- **Intervening obstacles**: cost, distance, visas and immigration law, language, lack of
  information.
- Migrants are often **young adults**, frequently male at first.
- Pull factors are **perceived** and often better than the reality.

## Impacts: always cover both places

**Source area**
- + Remittances; less unemployment and pressure on land; returnees bring skills and savings.
- − Brain drain; loss of young workers; ageing; gender imbalance; separated families.

**Receiving area**
- + Fills labour shortages; taxpayers; young workers slow ageing; cultural diversity.
- − Pressure on housing and services; possible tension or discrimination; some low-paid
  exploitation of migrants.

## Case studies

**Pakistan → Gulf states (voluntary, mostly temporary)**
- Push: limited well-paid work, fast-growing workforce.
- Pull: higher wages; construction and service jobs from oil wealth.
- Source: remittances are a major source of household and national income; fathers absent.
- Receiving: labour for big projects; migrants often have limited rights and live in labour camps.

**Syria → Turkey, Lebanon, Jordan and Europe (forced, from 2011)**
- Push: civil war, bombing, collapse of services.
- Pull: safety and nearness; asylum systems and family links in Europe (Germany, 2015).
- Turkey has received the largest number of Syrian refugees.
- Source: loss of workers and skills. Receiving: pressure on housing, schools and health care.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Migration | A permanent or semi-permanent change of residence |
| Emigrant | A person who leaves a country |
| Immigrant | A person who arrives in a country to live |
| Refugee | Forced to leave their country by persecution, conflict or violence |
| Asylum seeker | Has applied for refugee status and awaits a decision |
| Internally displaced person | Forced from home but still inside their own country |
| Remittances | Money sent home by migrants |
| Brain drain | Loss of skilled, educated workers through emigration |
| Counter-urbanisation | Movement from cities to smaller towns and rural areas |

## Exam traps

- Calling an IDP a refugee.
- Saying refugees move "for a better life".
- One-sided impacts (receiving country only).
- Generic push and pull lists with no named flow.

## Self-test

1. A family flees flooding to a camp 40 km away in the same country. Classify the movement.
2. Give two intervening obstacles.
3. Why are many migrants young adults?
4. Give one demographic impact on a source area that loses many young men.
5. A town gains 2,000 immigrants and loses 2,800 emigrants; population 80,000. Net migration rate?

**Answers:** 1. Internal, forced (involuntary), probably temporary; the family are internally
displaced persons. 2. Any two: cost of travel, distance, immigration laws or visas, language,
lack of information. 3. They have fewer family ties, are more employable and more willing to take
risks. 4. Any one: gender imbalance, a fall in the birth rate, or an ageing population.
5. −800 ÷ 80,000 × 1,000 = **−10 per 1,000**.
