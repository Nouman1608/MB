Condensed notes for sub-topic 1.2 of Cambridge O Level Geography (2217), 2026 series.

## Types of migration

- **Internal** (within a country) vs **international** (across a border).
- **Voluntary** (choice, usually economic) vs **involuntary / forced** (conflict, persecution,
  disaster).
- **Temporary / seasonal** vs **permanent**.
- **Rural–urban** (drives urbanisation) vs **urban–rural** (counter-urbanisation).

```
net migration = immigrants - emigrants
rate per 1,000 = net migration / population x 1,000
```

## Push, pull and obstacles

| | Push | Pull |
|---|---|---|
| Economic | Unemployment, low wages, land shortage | Jobs, higher wages |
| Social | Poor schools and health care | Better services, family networks |
| Political | War, persecution | Safety, freedom |
| Environmental | Drought, floods | Reliable water, lower hazard risk |

- **Intervening obstacles**: cost, distance, visas and immigration law, language, lack of
  information.
- Migrants are often **young adults**, frequently male at first.
- Pull factors are **perceived** and often better than the reality.

## Impacts: always cover both places

**Source area**
- + Remittances; less unemployment and pressure on land; returnees bring skills and savings.
- − Brain drain; loss of young workers; ageing; gender imbalance; separated families.

**Receiving area**
- + Fills labour shortages; taxpayers; young workers slow ageing; cultural diversity.
- − Pressure on housing and services; possible tension or discrimination; some low-paid
  exploitation of migrants.

## Case studies

**Pakistan → Gulf states (voluntary, mostly temporary)**
- Push: limited well-paid work, fast-growing workforce.
- Pull: higher wages; construction and service jobs from oil wealth.
- Source: remittances are a major source of household and national income; fathers absent.
- Receiving: labour for big projects; migrants often have limited rights and live in labour camps.

**Syria → Turkey, Lebanon, Jordan and Europe (forced, from 2011)**
- Push: civil war, bombing, collapse of services.
- Pull: safety and nearness; asylum systems and family links in Europe (Germany, 2015).
- Turkey has received the largest number of Syrian refugees.
- Source: loss of workers and skills. Receiving: pressure on housing, schools and health care.

**Poland → UK (voluntary, from 2004)** (a second economic example)
- Poland joined the European Union in 2004 and the UK was one of the few existing members to open
  its labour market straight away.
- Push: higher unemployment and lower wages in Poland at the time. Pull: jobs in farming, food
  processing, construction, hospitality and care; free movement removed the visa obstacle.
- Receiving: filled labour shortages and paid taxes; some pressure on local services and housing
  in towns with fast arrivals. Source: remittances, but loss of young workers; many later
  returned as the Polish economy grew (return migration).

**Pakistan: forced internal movement**: the 2022 monsoon floods displaced millions of people
inside the country, mainly in Sindh and Balochistan. They were internally displaced persons, not
refugees; many returned once the water receded, although some areas stayed flooded for months. Contrast this with the voluntary flow to the Gulf.

## Explaining a migration: the chain

**Cause at the origin → perception of the destination → obstacles overcome → impact.** For
example: few well-paid jobs in rural Punjab (push) → relatives in Dubai report construction work
at higher wages (pull, chain migration) → recruitment agents and work visas help migrants overcome
cost and law (obstacles) → remittances pay for housing and school fees at home (impact).

## Worked example: impacts on a source area

*Explain the impacts of international migration on the source area.* Model paragraph: "Migrant
workers from Pakistan send remittances home, which families spend on food, school fees and
better housing, so living standards in source villages rise. This money also creates work for
local builders and shopkeepers, a multiplier effect. However, the loss of young men means that
women and older relatives must run farms and households alone, and children grow up with absent
fathers. If skilled workers such as doctors and engineers leave, the source country suffers a
brain drain."

Each point is a chain (impact → consequence), and both positive and negative impacts are covered.

## Evaluation points

- Remittances help families but can create dependence, and can widen inequality between migrant
  and non-migrant households.
- Labour migration to the Gulf is usually temporary, so migrants return with savings and skills,
  but they have little chance of settling permanently.
- Impacts change over time: early arrivals are mostly young men; families or return migration can
  follow later.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Migration | A permanent or semi-permanent change of residence |
| Emigrant | A person who leaves a country |
| Immigrant | A person who arrives in a country to live |
| Refugee | Forced to leave their country by persecution, conflict or violence |
| Asylum seeker | Has applied for refugee status and awaits a decision |
| Internally displaced person | Forced from home but still inside their own country |
| Remittances | Money sent home by migrants |
| Brain drain | Loss of skilled, educated workers through emigration |
| Counter-urbanisation | Movement from cities to smaller towns and rural areas |
| Economic migrant | A person who moves voluntarily to find work or higher wages |
| Push factor | A feature that encourages people to leave an area |
| Pull factor | A feature that attracts people to an area |
| Intervening obstacle | A barrier that makes migration harder, such as cost or immigration law |
| Chain migration | Migration that follows earlier migrants from the same place |
| Return migration | Migrants moving back to the place they came from |

## Exam traps

- Calling an IDP a refugee.
- Saying refugees move "for a better life".
- One-sided impacts (receiving country only).
- Generic push and pull lists with no named flow.
- Writing "jobs" as a push factor; the push is the lack of jobs at the origin.
- Mixing up emigrant and immigrant, which depends on which country you are describing.
- Treating remittances as wholly positive with no mention of dependence or absent family members.

## Self-test

1. A family flees flooding to a camp 40 km away in the same country. Classify the movement.
2. Give two intervening obstacles.
3. Why are many migrants young adults?
4. Give one demographic impact on a source area that loses many young men.
5. A town gains 2,000 immigrants and loses 2,800 emigrants; population 80,000. Net migration rate?
6. Why did migration from Poland to the UK increase after 2004?
7. Give one positive and one negative impact of migration on a receiving country.
8. Explain how chain migration happens.
9. Why were people displaced by Pakistan's 2022 floods not refugees?
10. Give one reason why remittances may not benefit a whole source community equally.

**Answers:** 1. Internal, forced (involuntary), probably temporary; the family are internally
displaced persons. 2. Any two: cost of travel, distance, immigration laws or visas, language,
lack of information. 3. They have fewer family ties, are more employable and more willing to take
risks. 4. Any one: gender imbalance, a fall in the birth rate, or an ageing population.
5. −800 ÷ 80,000 × 1,000 = **−10 per 1,000**. 6. Poland joined the EU in 2004 and the UK opened
its labour market at once, so the visa obstacle was removed while wages in the UK were higher.
7. Positive: for example filling labour shortages or paying taxes; negative: for example pressure
on housing and services, or tension with some local people. 8. Earlier migrants send back
information, money for travel or offers of work and housing, so relatives and neighbours follow.
9. They stayed inside their own country, so they were internally displaced persons. 10. Only
households with a migrant receive the money, so inequality between families may increase.
