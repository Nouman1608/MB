## Section A — Short questions

**1.** Read this original passage.

> *The lighthouse keeper's cottage had one window that faced the land. Mr Harlow had nailed a blanket over it in his
> first winter and never taken it down. Each evening he climbed the ninety steps, wound the lamp, and wrote the same
> word in the log: "Clear."*

List **four** things the passage states explicitly about Mr Harlow or his cottage. **[4]**

**2.** Using the same passage, explain **two** things that can be inferred about Mr Harlow, supporting each with a short quotation. **[4]**

**3.** Read this original passage from a modern travel article.

> *The ferry left forty minutes late, which, the crew assured us, counted as early for the island run. Gulls followed
> the wake all the way across. By the time the harbour came into view, half the passengers had given up on the deck
> and retreated to the café, where the tea was weak and the windows steamed.*

Which **two** of these statements are true according to the passage? Write the letters.

A. The ferry left on time.
B. The crew suggested lateness was normal on this route.
C. All the passengers stayed on deck.
D. The café windows were steamed up.
E. The gulls left before the halfway point. **[2]**

**4.** Explain the difference between an explicit detail and an inference, using one example from the passage in Question 3. **[3]**

**5.** Read this original sentence from an opinion column: *"Everyone knows that young people today read less than any generation before them."* Identify **two** ways in which this claim is unsupported, and suggest **one** kind of evidence that would support it. **[3]**

**6.** Read this original extract, written in the style of a 19th-century letter.

> *"The new mill has brought great prosperity to the town. The owner tells me that his hands are content, and I saw
> for myself several girls singing at the looms."*

Explain how the writer's evidence may be unreliable. **[4]**

**7.** A student writes: *"The writer uses lots of good words to show the setting is scary."* Explain **two** weaknesses in this as a piece of critical reading. **[2]**

## Section B — Longer and structured questions

**8.** Read this original passage.

> *Grandad's allotment was the only tidy thing about him. His coat had lost three buttons and his boots let in the
> rain, but the bean rows ran straight as rulers and not a weed survived a week. When the council letter came, he read
> it twice, folded it into a perfect square, and went out to water the beans as if nothing had happened.*

What impressions does the writer create of Grandad? Give **three** inferences, each supported by evidence. **[6]**

**9.** Using the passage in Question 8, offer **two** different readings of the final sentence, and explain which you find more convincing. **[5]**

**10.** Read this original passage.

> *The classroom emptied at the bell, but Priya stayed, sharpening a pencil that was already sharp. Mr Okafor looked up
> from his marking. "Something on your mind?" She shook her head, then nodded, then laughed at herself. Outside, the
> corridor roared and faded. When she finally spoke, it was about the weather.*

A reader said: *"In this passage the writer presents Priya as confident."* To what extent do you agree? Write a short evaluative response of two developed paragraphs. **[8]**

**11.** Read this original passage from a campaign leaflet.

> *"Last year, the riverside path was used by hundreds of families every weekend. Now the council wants to sell it to
> developers. Our survey of fifty local residents found that forty-two oppose the sale. The council claims the path
> costs too much to maintain, but it has never published the figures. Do we really want our children's playground
> turned into car parking?"*

Sort the claims in this passage into those that are supported by evidence and those that are not, then evaluate how fairly the writer uses evidence overall. **[8]**

**12.** Explain how knowing the context of a text can change your reading of a detail. Use the letter in Question 6 as your example. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Any four of the following, one mark each (4 × [1]): the cottage had one window that faced the land; he nailed a blanket over it; he did this in his first winter; he never took it down; he climbed ninety steps each evening; he wound the lamp; he wrote "Clear" in the log. Inferences (for example "he is lonely") are not credited, because the question asks for what is stated.

**2.** Two marks for each inference, up to 4. For each: a reasonable inference [1] and an apt supporting quotation [1]. For example: he does not want to look at or be reminded of the land, perhaps the life he left [1], because he "nailed a blanket over" the only landward window [1]; his life is unchanging and repetitive [1], shown by writing "the same word" every evening [1]. Also creditable: he is dutiful and reliable ("each evening... wound the lamp").

**3.** B [1] and D [1]. A is false (the ferry was "forty minutes late"); C is false ("half the passengers" left the deck); E is false (gulls followed "all the way across").

**4.** An explicit detail is stated directly in the text [1]; an inference is a conclusion the reader draws from what is suggested [1]. Example: it is explicit that the ferry was forty minutes late, while the crew's joke that this "counted as early" lets the reader infer that the service is usually unreliable [1].

**5.** "Everyone knows" asserts a consensus without any source [1]; "less than any generation before them" is a sweeping comparison offered with no data [1]. Supporting evidence could be a named survey or published reading figures comparing generations [1].

**6.** Up to 4 marks for well-explained points, one mark each: the claim that workers are "content" comes from the owner, who has an interest in presenting the mill well [1]; the writer's own evidence is a single observation of "several girls", too few to represent the whole workforce [1]; singing could have other causes, such as passing the time during hard work, so it does not prove contentment [1]; the writer may have seen only what a visitor was shown, and offers no workers' own views [1].

**7.** It is vague: "lots of good words" names no word, technique or quotation [1]. It asserts an effect ("scary") without explaining how or why any choice creates it, so it is neither an inference nor an evaluation [1].

**8.** Two marks for each of three impressions, up to 6: inference [1] plus apt evidence [1]. For example: he neglects himself but cares deeply about the allotment [1], since his coat "had lost three buttons" while "the bean rows ran straight as rulers" [1]; he is disciplined and controlling about what matters to him [1], as "not a weed survived a week" [1]; he hides worry behind routine [1], folding the council letter "into a perfect square" and watering the beans "as if nothing had happened" [1].

**9.** First reading, with evidence: he is in denial or hiding distress, since "as if nothing had happened" implies something serious has happened [2]. Second reading, with evidence: he is calm and resilient, choosing to carry on with what he values, shown by the deliberate, ordered folding [2]. A reasoned preference that refers to the text, for example that "read it twice" suggests the letter troubled him, so denial is more convincing [1].

**10.** Award up to 8 marks:
- a clear judgement about how far the student agrees, for example "I largely disagree" [1];
- tests the key word "confident" rather than discussing Priya in general [1];
- evidence of nervousness, such as "sharpening a pencil that was already sharp", with the inference that she is delaying [2];
- evidence of indecision, "shook her head, then nodded", with the inference that she cannot bring herself to speak [2];
- evaluates a method, for example the anticlimax of speaking "about the weather", which shows her courage failing at the last moment [1];
- acknowledges a partial case for confidence (she "laughed at herself", showing some self-awareness) before a qualified final judgement [1].

**11.** Award up to 8 marks:
- supported claims identified: the survey of fifty residents with forty-two opposed [1], and the factual statement that the council has not published the figures [1];
- unsupported claims identified: "hundreds of families every weekend" has no source [1], and "children's playground turned into car parking" is speculation presented as the outcome [1];
- evaluation: fifty residents is a small sample and the leaflet does not say how they were chosen [1]; the rhetorical question uses emotive, loaded language rather than evidence [1]; the writer fairly points out a gap in the council's case [1]; overall judgement, for example that the leaflet mixes genuine evidence with exaggeration to persuade [1].

**12.** Context means the time, place and conditions in which the text was written [1]. In a period when mill owners held great power and visitors were often shown around by them, the "content" workers may reflect what the writer was allowed to see [1]. So the singing girls become evidence of the writer's limited or comfortable perspective, rather than proof of good conditions [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing inferences when the question asks only for stated information.
- Quoting without saying what the quotation suggests.
- Agreeing with a statement without testing its key word.
- Calling a text "biased" without naming what is slanted or why it matters.
- Offering a second reading with no evidence behind it.
