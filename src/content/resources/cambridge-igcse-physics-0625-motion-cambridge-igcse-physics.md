This guide teaches section 1.2, Motion, of the Cambridge IGCSE Physics 0625 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. Core statements 1 to 8 cover speed, velocity and the two kinds of motion graph. Supplement statements 9 to 13 add acceleration, deceleration and falling objects; they are examined only on Papers 2 and 4 and are labelled **Extended only**. The [Motion, forces and energy study guide](/resources/igcse-physics-motion-forces-and-energy/) introduces the whole topic; this page works through the graph skills and calculations in more depth.

## Speed, velocity and average speed

**Speed** is the distance travelled per unit time, v = s/t, in metres per second (m/s). **Velocity** is speed in a given direction: a car going round a roundabout at a steady 10 m/s has a constant speed but a changing velocity.

Speeds are often given in km/h. Since 1 km/h = 1000 m ÷ 3600 s, divide by 3.6 to get m/s: 90 km/h = 25 m/s.

**Average speed = total distance travelled ÷ total time taken.** It is **not** the mean of the speeds unless each speed lasts the same time.

**Worked example 1.** A bus travels 12 km at 60 km/h, then another 12 km at 40 km/h. Find its average speed.

```
time 1 = 12 / 60 = 0.20 h      time 2 = 12 / 40 = 0.30 h
average speed = total distance / total time = 24 / 0.50 = 48 km/h
```

The tempting answer, (60 + 40) ÷ 2 = 50 km/h, is wrong: the bus spends longer at the lower speed.

## Distance–time graphs

| Shape of the line | Motion |
|---|---|
| horizontal | at rest |
| straight, sloping upwards | constant speed |
| curving upwards, getting steeper | accelerating |
| curving, getting less steep | decelerating |

The **gradient** of a straight section is the speed: rise (distance) ÷ run (time). Use a large triangle, at least half the length of the line, and read the values from the axes, not by counting squares when the scales are unequal. A steeper line means a greater speed.

## Speed–time graphs

| Shape of the line | Motion |
|---|---|
| horizontal on the time axis (speed = 0) | at rest |
| horizontal above the axis | constant speed |
| straight, sloping upwards | accelerating (at a constant rate) |
| straight, sloping downwards | decelerating (at a constant rate) |
| curved | changing acceleration *(Extended only)* |

The **area under** a speed–time graph is the distance travelled. For the shapes in this syllabus, split the area into rectangles (constant speed: speed × time) and triangles (constant acceleration from or to rest: ½ × base × height).

Do not confuse the two graphs. A horizontal line on a distance–time graph means stopped; a horizontal line on a speed–time graph means moving steadily.

**Worked example 2.** A cyclist starts from rest and speeds up steadily to 8.0 m/s in 10 s, rides at 8.0 m/s for 30 s, then slows steadily to rest in 4.0 s. Find the total distance and the average speed.

```
stage 1 (triangle):   ½ × 10 × 8.0  =  40 m
stage 2 (rectangle):  30 × 8.0      = 240 m
stage 3 (triangle):   ½ × 4.0 × 8.0 =  16 m
total distance = 296 m;  total time = 44 s
average speed = 296 / 44 = 6.7 m/s
```

**Extended only.** From the same graph: stage 1 acceleration = (8.0 − 0) ÷ 10 = **0.80 m/s²**; stage 3 acceleration = (0 − 8.0) ÷ 4.0 = **−2.0 m/s²**, a deceleration of 2.0 m/s². The steeper slope in stage 3 shows the larger magnitude.

### Sketching and plotting

- **Sketch:** labelled axes with quantities and units, the right shape, and key values marked where the question gives them. No grid needed.
- **Plot:** a scale that uses more than half the grid in each direction and goes up in simple steps (1, 2, 5 or 10 per square), points marked as small crosses, and a single straight line or smooth curve of best fit. Do not join dot to dot.

## Acceleration (Extended only)

Acceleration is the change in velocity per unit time, a = Δv/Δt, in m/s². An acceleration of 3.0 m/s² means the velocity increases by 3.0 m/s every second.

- On a speed–time graph, the **gradient** is the acceleration.
- A **straight** sloping line means **constant** acceleration; a **curve** means the acceleration is **changing** (getting steeper: acceleration increasing; flattening: acceleration decreasing).
- A **deceleration is a negative acceleration**. A car braking from 24 m/s to 6.0 m/s in 3.0 s has a = (6.0 − 24) ÷ 3.0 = **−6.0 m/s²**. In a "calculate the deceleration" answer, quote 6.0 m/s²; in "calculate the acceleration", keep the minus sign.

## Free fall and terminal velocity

**Core.** Near the Earth's surface, the acceleration of free fall, g, is approximately constant at about **9.8 m/s²**, whatever the mass of the falling object, provided air resistance is negligible.

**Extended only: without air resistance.** A dropped stone gains 9.8 m/s of speed every second. Its speed–time graph is a straight line through the origin with gradient 9.8 m/s². After 1.5 s its speed is 9.8 × 1.5 = 14.7 m/s, and the area under the graph gives the distance fallen: ½ × 1.5 × 14.7 = **11 m**.

**Extended only: with air or liquid resistance.**

1. At the start the object is slow, drag is small, and the acceleration is close to g.
2. As speed increases, drag increases, so the resultant downward force falls and the acceleration decreases: the speed–time graph curves and becomes less steep.
3. When drag equals weight, the resultant force is zero, the acceleration is zero and the object falls at a constant speed: its **terminal velocity**. The graph is now horizontal.

The same pattern happens much more quickly in a liquid: a steel ball dropped into a tall tube of oil reaches terminal velocity after falling only a short distance, because drag in a liquid is much larger than in air at the same speed. Mark the tube with rubber bands at equal spacings and time the ball between them: equal times show it has reached terminal velocity.

When a skydiver opens a parachute, drag suddenly becomes larger than weight. The resultant force is upwards, so the skydiver **decelerates** (the speed–time line slopes down), drag falls as speed falls, and a new, much lower terminal velocity is reached when drag again equals weight.

## Common mistakes

- Averaging two speeds instead of dividing total distance by total time.
- Reading a horizontal speed–time line as "stopped".
- Finding the gradient of a speed–time graph when the question asks for distance (that needs the area).
- Leaving km/h or minutes in an answer that needs m/s.
- *(Extended)* Saying an object at terminal velocity has "no forces" on it: weight and drag both act; they are balanced.
- *(Extended)* Dropping the minus sign for a deceleration in an acceleration calculation, or keeping it when the question asks for the deceleration.

## Exam technique: command words

- **State** the value of g: about 9.8 m/s². No explanation needed.
- **Describe** the motion shown by a graph: name each stage (at rest, constant speed, accelerating, decelerating) and give values with units from the axes.
- **Calculate**: write the equation, substitute, give the answer with a unit. For a distance from a speed–time graph, show each area separately.
- **Determine** from a graph: show the triangle or the area you used, and the readings.
- **Sketch**: shape and labelled axes; **plot**: points and a best-fit line.
- **Explain** terminal velocity *(Extended)*: link speed → drag → resultant force → acceleration, in that order, and finish with "drag = weight".
