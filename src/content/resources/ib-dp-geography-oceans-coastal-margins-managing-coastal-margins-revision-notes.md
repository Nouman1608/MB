Condensed recall for **2.3 Managing coastal margins** (IB DP Geography, Option B: Oceans and coastal margins). The [Oceans and coastal margins study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-oceans-coastal-margins-study-guide/) gives fuller discussion.

## Management responses

| Approach | Examples | Evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| Hard engineering | Sea walls, rock armour, revetments, groynes, breakwaters | Protect specific assets; expensive; can alter sediment movement; may create a false sense of security |
| Soft engineering | Beach nourishment, dune stabilisation, managed realignment | Work with natural processes; may need repeating or space |
| Planning | Shoreline management plans, integrated coastal zone management | Take a system-wide, long-term view |

**Managed realignment:** defences are deliberately breached so land can flood and form natural buffers such as marsh.

### How each measure works

| Measure | How it works | Main drawback |
|---|---|---|
| Sea wall | Concrete barrier reflects wave energy; a curved face throws waves back | Reflected energy can scour the beach in front, undermining the wall |
| Rock armour (rip-rap) | Large boulders absorb and break up wave energy | Can be unattractive; rocks may shift in storms |
| Revetment | Sloping structure that absorbs wave energy | Needs maintenance; limits beach access |
| Groynes | Barriers across the beach trap sediment moved by longshore drift, widening the beach | Starve beaches further along the coast (downdrift) |
| Offshore breakwater | Breaks waves before they reach the shore | Costly; can change sediment patterns |
| Beach nourishment | Sand or shingle is added to the beach so waves expend energy on it | Must be repeated; dredging can damage offshore habitats |
| Dune stabilisation | Fencing, boardwalks and planting of marram grass trap sand and limit trampling | Needs controls on visitor access |

Wide, healthy beaches and dunes are themselves the most effective natural defence, which is why many strategies aim to keep sediment in the system.

## Shoreline management policy options

- Hold the line
- Advance the line
- Managed realignment
- No active intervention

**Integrated coastal zone management (ICZM)** brings stakeholders together and treats land and sea as one system.

In England and Wales, **shoreline management plans** are drawn up for each sediment cell or sub-cell, so that a decision in one place takes account of its effects on neighbouring coast. Each stretch is given one of the four policy options for the short, medium and long term, which can change over time as sea level rises.

ICZM principles include a long-term view, managing the whole system across the land-sea boundary, involving all stakeholders, working with natural processes and adapting as conditions change.

## Stakeholders and conflict

Residents, local councils, national agencies, developers, businesses, conservation groups, insurers. Interests clash: a homeowner may want a wall while a conservation group favours a restored marsh.

## Cost-benefit analysis

Compares the value of assets protected with the cost of defence. Worked check: benefits 9.0 million against cost 2.4 million gives a ratio of 9.0 / 2.4 = 3.75; above 1 suggests benefits exceed costs.

**Limits:** it struggles to value habitats, heritage and community ties, and can omit effects on neighbouring coast. State this in evaluative answers.

## Case studies

### Mappleton, Holderness coast, England

In the early 1990s, two rock groynes and a rock revetment were built to protect the village of Mappleton and its coast road on one of Europe's fastest-eroding coastlines. The village was protected, but by trapping sediment the groynes reduced the supply moving south, and erosion increased on the farmland beyond. It is a classic example of hard engineering solving a local problem while shifting it downdrift, and of why decisions need to consider the whole sediment cell.

### Medmerry, West Sussex, England

Completed in 2013, this was one of the largest open-coast managed realignment schemes in the United Kingdom at the time. A new, stronger embankment was built inland and the old shingle bank was allowed to breach, creating intertidal habitat that absorbs wave energy and protects homes and roads behind it. Evaluation: it gives long-term protection and new habitat at a lower ongoing cost than holding the old line, but farmland was lost and the approach needs space and public acceptance.

### The Netherlands: Delta Works and the Sand Motor

After the catastrophic North Sea flood of 1953, the Dutch built the Delta Works, a system of dams, sluices and storm surge barriers, including a movable barrier across the Eastern Scheldt that stays open in normal conditions to protect the estuary's tidal ecosystem. More recently, the **Sand Motor** (completed in 2011) placed a very large volume of sand offshore of the South Holland coast so that waves and currents would spread it along the shore over many years. Evaluation: the Dutch approach shows a shift from purely hard defences towards "building with nature", but it depends on high national wealth and strong institutions.

## Model paragraph

*Evaluate the use of groynes as a coastal management strategy.* "Groynes are effective at trapping sediment moved by longshore drift, building a wider beach that absorbs wave energy and protects the coast behind it, as at Mappleton. However, the same process reduces sediment moving downdrift, so neighbouring beaches narrow and erosion can accelerate, as happened south of Mappleton. They are relatively cheap compared with sea walls, but their effects must be judged across the whole sediment cell. Groynes are therefore a useful local measure only when the downdrift consequences are acceptable or managed."

## Exam traps

- Treating hard engineering as always bad and soft as always good.
- Forgetting knock-on effects on sediment supply elsewhere.
- Naming a stakeholder without saying what they want.
- Relying on cost-benefit ratios without criticising what they leave out.
- Saying "no active intervention" means doing nothing for no reason: it is a deliberate policy where defence is not justified.
- Describing beach nourishment as permanent: it needs repeating.
