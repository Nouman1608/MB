## Antibody structure related to function

An antibody is a **glycoprotein** with **quaternary structure**: four polypeptide chains, two long **heavy chains** and two short **light chains**, held together by **disulfide bonds** in a Y shape.

| Part | Structure | Function it makes possible |
|---|---|---|
| Variable region | Tip of each arm; amino acid sequence differs between antibodies, so the shape differs | Forms an **antigen-binding site** complementary to one specific antigen |
| Two binding sites | One at the end of each arm | One antibody can bind two antigens, linking pathogens together (**agglutination**) |
| Hinge region | Flexible section where the arms meet the stem | Arms can move to bind antigens at different distances apart |
| Constant region | Same sequence in all antibodies of a class | Binds to receptors on phagocytes, so bound pathogens are engulfed more easily |
| Disulfide bonds | Covalent bonds between chains | Hold the four chains together; molecule is stable in blood |

### How antibodies protect

- **Agglutination**: clumps of pathogens cannot spread easily and are engulfed by phagocytes several at a time.
- **Neutralisation**: antibodies bind to toxins, blocking their active parts (antitoxins).
- **Blocking**: antibodies bind to the sites a virus uses to attach to host cells, so it cannot enter.
- **Marking**: bound antibodies make pathogens easier for phagocytes to recognise and engulf.

When answering "relate", pair each feature with its function, as in the table.

## Monoclonal antibodies

A **monoclonal antibody** is a single type of antibody produced by a clone of genetically identical cells, so every molecule binds the same antigen.

### The hybridoma method, step by step with reasons

1. **Inject a mouse with the antigen.** Its immune system makes B-lymphocytes (plasma cells) producing antibodies against that antigen.
2. **Remove B-lymphocytes from the spleen.** The spleen is rich in these activated cells.
3. **Fuse them with myeloma cells** (cancer cells). Plasma cells make the antibody but **do not divide**; myeloma cells **divide indefinitely**. The fused **hybridoma** cell does both.
4. **Screen the hybridomas.** Not every fused cell makes the wanted antibody, so cells are separated and those producing the antibody that binds the target antigen are selected.
5. **Clone and culture the chosen hybridoma** on a large scale. Every cell in the clone secretes the same antibody, which is collected and purified.

### Using them in diagnosis

The principle: an antibody binds **only** its specific antigen, and a **label** (fluorescent dye, coloured particle, radioactive marker or enzyme) shows where it has bound.

**Worked example: how a pregnancy test strip works.** Pregnancy produces the hormone hCG, which appears in urine.

1. Urine is added at one end and soaks along the strip.
2. It meets mobile monoclonal antibodies against hCG, attached to coloured particles. If hCG is present, it binds them.
3. Further along, the **test line** has a second set of anti-hCG antibodies fixed to the strip. They bind the hCG–antibody–particle complex and hold it, so a coloured line appears.
4. At the **control line**, fixed antibodies bind the mobile antibodies themselves, whether or not hCG is present. A coloured control line shows the urine has travelled the length of the strip and the test has worked.

No hCG: only the control line appears. Same principle, other uses: finding a pathogen in a blood sample, or locating a blood clot or tumour with a labelled antibody.

### Using them in treatment

The principle is again specificity: the antibody is given to bind a specific antigen on target cells or molecules, and so:

- blocks a receptor or neutralises a molecule; for example, trastuzumab binds a receptor protein (HER2) present in large numbers on some breast cancer cells, blocking signals that stimulate their growth;
- marks the target cells for destruction by the patient's immune system;
- carries an attached drug or toxin directly to the target cells, so fewer healthy cells are harmed.

Mouse antibodies are non-self in humans and would trigger an immune response, so therapeutic antibodies are **humanised**: most of the molecule is altered to match human antibody sequences, keeping only the mouse parts that form the antigen-binding sites.

## Four types of immunity

| | Active (own lymphocytes make antibodies) | Passive (antibodies supplied from outside) |
|---|---|---|
| **Natural** | Infection with the pathogen | Antibodies cross the placenta, or arrive in breast milk |
| **Artificial** | Vaccination | Injection of ready-made antibodies |

| Feature | Active | Passive |
|---|---|---|
| Memory cells formed? | Yes | No |
| Speed of protection | Slow (primary response takes days) | Immediate |
| Duration | Long-term | Short: antibodies are broken down within weeks or months |
| Use | Long-term prevention | Immediate protection, e.g. after exposure to a toxin or to rabies |

## How vaccines give long-term immunity

A vaccine contains **antigens**, not antibodies. These may be a weakened (attenuated) live pathogen, a killed or inactivated pathogen, a harmless form of a toxin (toxoid), or isolated antigen molecules. The antigens cause a **primary immune response** without causing the disease. Memory B and T cells form. If the real pathogen arrives later, the **secondary response** destroys it before symptoms develop.

**Booster doses** cause a further secondary response, producing more memory cells and a higher antibody concentration, so protection lasts longer.

## Vaccination programmes

Vaccinating individuals protects them. Vaccinating a large proportion of a population also protects others:

- **Herd immunity**: if most people are immune, an infected person is unlikely to meet a susceptible one, so chains of transmission break. This protects people who cannot be vaccinated, such as young babies or people with weakened immune systems.
- **Ring vaccination**: when a case appears, everyone around the patient is vaccinated, building a barrier of immune people around the outbreak.

### Why smallpox was eradicated

The World Health Organization declared smallpox eradicated in 1980, after a programme of mass and ring vaccination. Biological features helped: the virus was antigenically stable, so one vaccine worked everywhere; it infected only humans, so there was no animal reservoir; and the disease was easy to recognise, so cases could be found and ringed quickly.

### Why some diseases are harder

- **Antigenic variation**: pathogens such as the influenza virus change their surface antigens, so memory cells from earlier vaccination no longer match; new vaccines are needed.
- **Many strains** with different antigens, each needing its own vaccine.
- **Pathogens inside cells**, such as *Plasmodium* and HIV, are hidden from antibodies.
- **Poor responses** in people who are malnourished or have HIV.
- **Practical and social problems**: vaccines that must be kept cold, remote populations, and low uptake.

## Common mistakes

- Saying a vaccine contains antibodies.
- Calling antibodies from the mother "artificial", or saying passive immunity produces memory cells.
- Describing the antibody's variable region as "the same in all antibodies".
- Leaving out why myeloma cells are used: they divide indefinitely.
- Describing monoclonal antibody uses without the word "specific" or "complementary".
- Explaining herd immunity as "everyone is vaccinated"; the point is that transmission chains break.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Relate** (antibody structure): feature → function, one pair per mark.
- **Outline** (hybridoma; principles of use): the steps in order, each with a short reason.
- **Describe the differences** (immunity types): match like with like: memory cells, speed, duration, examples.
- **Explain** (vaccines; programmes): antigen → primary response → memory cells → fast secondary response; or individual immunity → herd immunity → fewer transmissions.
