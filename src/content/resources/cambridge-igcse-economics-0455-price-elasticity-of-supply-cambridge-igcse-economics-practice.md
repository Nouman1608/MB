These questions cover section 2.8 (price elasticity of supply) of the Cambridge IGCSE Economics 0455 syllabus for examination in 2026. All firms, products and figures are invented. Revise first with the [PES study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-price-elasticity-of-supply-cambridge-igcse-economics/) and the [PES revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-price-elasticity-of-supply-cambridge-igcse-economics-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** The price of a product rises by 20% and quantity supplied rises by 30%. What is the PES? **[1]**

A: 0.67

B: 1.5

C: −1.5

D: 10

**2.** Which of the following would make the supply of a product more price elastic? **[1]**

A: The product is perishable

B: Firms are operating at full capacity

C: Firms hold large stocks of the finished product

D: The product takes two years to produce

**3.** Define price elasticity of supply. **[2]**

**4.** A potter raises her output of bowls from 400 to 460 a month when the price rises from 15 to 18.

**(a)** Calculate the PES. **[2]**

**(b)** State whether supply is elastic or inelastic, with a reason. **[1]**

**5.** Describe the supply curve for a product with perfectly inelastic supply, and give one example. **[2]**

**6.** PES for a type of bicycle is 1.2. The price rises by 10%. A firm was supplying 5,000 a year. Calculate its new output. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**7.** A straight-line supply curve passes through these points: price 6, quantity 140; price 8, quantity 160.

**(a)** Calculate PES between these two prices. **[2]**

**(b)** State whether this line meets the price axis or the quantity axis, and explain how you know. **[2]**

**8.** Explain **two** reasons why the supply of fresh fish is likely to be price inelastic in the short run. **[4]**

**9.** Explain why the price elasticity of supply of a product is likely to be greater in the long run than in the short run. **[3]**

**10.** Demand rises by the same amount in the market for mobile phone cases and in the market for city-centre flats. Analyse why prices are likely to rise more in one market than the other. **[5]**

**11.** Explain **two** ways a manufacturer could make its supply more price elastic, and state one cost of each. **[4]**

**12.** Discuss whether a government subsidy to builders is a good way to reduce the price of housing. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. PES = 30 ÷ 20 = 1.5; it is positive because price and quantity supplied move together. **[1]**

**2.** **C** [1]. Stocks can be released quickly when the price rises. A, B and D all make supply less elastic. **[1]**

**3.** The responsiveness of quantity supplied [1] to a change in the price of the product [1]. **[2]**

**4. (a)** % change in quantity = 60 ÷ 400 × 100 = +15%; % change in price = 3 ÷ 15 × 100 = +20% [1]. PES = 15 ÷ 20 = **0.75** [1].

**(b)** Inelastic, because quantity supplied changed by a smaller percentage than price (PES less than 1) [1]. **[3]**

**5.** A vertical supply curve: quantity supplied stays the same at every price [1]. Example: seats in a stadium for one match, or land in a city centre in the short run [1]. **[2]**

**6.** % change in quantity = 1.2 × 10 = +12% [1]; 5,000 × 1.12 = **5,600 bicycles** [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** % change in price = 2 ÷ 6 × 100 = +33.3%; % change in quantity = 20 ÷ 140 × 100 = +14.3% [1]. PES = 14.3 ÷ 33.3 = **0.43** [1].

**(b)** It meets the quantity axis [1]: PES is less than 1, and a straight supply line with inelastic supply cuts the quantity axis to the right of the origin (continuing the line down, quantity is 80 when price is 0) [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Fish are perishable and cannot be stored for long [1], so sellers cannot hold back stocks and release them when prices rise [1]. The catch depends on boats, crews and weather, which cannot be increased quickly [1], so output cannot rise much within days even if prices rise [1]. **[4]**

**9.** In the short run some factors, such as factory space and machinery, are fixed [1], so output can rise only through overtime or extra shifts. In the long run firms can build new capacity and train more workers [1], and new firms can enter the market, so output responds much more to a price rise [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Supply of phone cases is elastic [1]: they are quick to make, firms often have spare capacity, and stocks are easy to store [1]. Supply of city-centre flats is inelastic [1]: building takes years and land is limited [1]. So the same rise in demand is met mostly by more output of phone cases, with a small price rise, but mostly by a higher price for flats [1]. **[5]**

**11.** Hold larger stocks of finished goods [1]; cost: storage and money tied up in unsold goods [1]. Keep spare capacity or flexible machinery [1]; cost: paying for machines that are often idle [1]. Other acceptable: train multi-skilled workers (cost: training spending). **[4]**

**12.** For: a subsidy lowers builders' costs, so supply of housing increases, which tends to lower the price [1]; it may encourage builders to start projects they would otherwise reject [1]. Against: housing supply is inelastic in the short run because building takes years and land is scarce [1], so the subsidy may raise builders' profits more than output [1]; it costs the government money that could fund other services [1]. Conclusion: a subsidy may help in the long run, especially if combined with measures such as faster planning approval that raise PES, but on its own it is unlikely to reduce prices quickly [1]. **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* Use PES as the reason a supply-side policy works slowly or quickly, then judge it over the short and long run.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving PES a minus sign.
- Using PED determinants, such as substitutes, to explain PES.
- Forgetting to state elastic or inelastic after a calculation.
- Ignoring the time period when explaining why supply responds slowly.
- In "Analyse" questions, describing supply in each market without comparing the effects on price and quantity.
