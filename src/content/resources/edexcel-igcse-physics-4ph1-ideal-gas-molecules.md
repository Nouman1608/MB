This guide covers sub-topic **5(d) Ideal gas molecules**, statements 5.15 to 5.22 of the Pearson Edexcel
International GCSE in Physics (4PH1), Issue 4. It is core content, so it can appear on either paper. The
[Solids, Liquids and Gases study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-physics-solids-liquids-and-gases/) introduces the
gas laws; this page explains *why* each one holds in terms of molecules, where absolute zero comes from, and how to
handle gas-law problems that combine with other parts of Topic 5.

## What 5(d) covers

- **5.15** explain how molecules in a gas have random motion and that they exert a force, and hence a pressure, on the
  walls of a container;
- **5.16** understand why there is an absolute zero of temperature, which is −273 °C;
- **5.17** describe the Kelvin scale of temperature and convert between the Kelvin and Celsius scales;
- **5.18** understand why an increase in temperature results in an increase in the average speed of gas molecules;
- **5.19** know that the Kelvin temperature of a gas is proportional to the average kinetic energy of its molecules;
- **5.20** explain, for a fixed amount of gas, the qualitative relationship between pressure and volume at constant
  temperature, and between pressure and Kelvin temperature at constant volume;
- **5.21** use `p₁/T₁ = p₂/T₂` for a fixed mass of gas at constant volume;
- **5.22** use `p₁V₁ = p₂V₂` for a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature.

## How a gas exerts pressure (5.15)

The molecules of a gas move in **random** directions at high speeds, colliding with each other and with the walls of
their container. When a molecule hits a wall and bounces back, the wall must push on the molecule to reverse its
direction, and the molecule pushes back on the wall with an equal and opposite force (Newton's third law). One collision gives a tiny force, but enormous numbers of molecules hit every part of the wall every
second, giving a steady total force. **Pressure is that force divided by the area of the wall.**

Because the motion is random, collisions happen at the same rate on every wall, so the pressure is the same on all of
them.

## Temperature and molecular speed (5.18, 5.19)

Heating a gas transfers energy to the kinetic store of its molecules, so they move faster on average. The molecules
in a gas do not all have the same speed; temperature is linked to their **average** kinetic energy.

The Kelvin temperature is **proportional** to the average kinetic energy of the molecules:

```
average kinetic energy of molecules ∝ T (in kelvin)
```

So heating a gas from 300 K to 600 K doubles the average kinetic energy of its molecules. Heating it from 20 °C to
40 °C does **not** double it: in kelvin that is 293 K to 313 K, an increase of only about 7%.

## Absolute zero and the Kelvin scale (5.16, 5.17)

If a fixed mass of gas is cooled at constant volume, its pressure falls steadily. A graph of pressure against
temperature in °C is a straight line. Extending the line back to where the pressure would be zero always meets the
temperature axis at about **−273 °C**, whatever gas is used.

**Worked example 1: finding absolute zero from data.** A sealed flask of gas has a pressure of 100 kPa at 0 °C and
136.6 kPa at 100 °C.

```
pressure rise per °C = (136.6 − 100) / 100 = 0.366 kPa per °C
cooling needed from 0 °C to reach zero pressure = 100 / 0.366 = 273 °C
so zero pressure at 0 − 273 = −273 °C
```

Zero pressure would mean the molecules had stopped hitting the walls: they would have the lowest possible kinetic
energy. Nothing can have less, so **no lower temperature is possible**. This temperature is **absolute zero**.

The **Kelvin scale** starts at absolute zero and uses steps the same size as degrees Celsius:

```
T (K) = θ (°C) + 273          θ (°C) = T (K) − 273
```

| Temperature | °C | K |
|---|---|---|
| Absolute zero | −273 | 0 |
| Water freezes | 0 | 273 |
| Room temperature (typical) | 20 | 293 |
| Water boils | 100 | 373 |

## Pressure and volume at constant temperature (5.20, 5.22)

If a fixed mass of gas is squashed into a smaller volume without changing its temperature:

- the molecules' average speed is unchanged (same temperature);
- but they have less distance to travel between collisions with the walls, and there is less wall area, so they hit
  each unit area of wall **more often**;
- so the pressure increases.

Halving the volume doubles the pressure: pressure is inversely proportional to volume, and `p × V` stays constant.

```
p₁V₁ = p₂V₂
```

A graph of `p` against `V` is a curve that gets less steep as `V` increases; a graph of `p` against `1/V` is a straight
line through the origin.

**Worked example 2: a bicycle pump.** A pump contains 150 cm³ of air at 100 kPa. The outlet is blocked and the handle
is pushed in slowly (so the temperature stays constant) until the volume is 50 cm³.

```
p₂ = p₁V₁ / V₂ = 100 × 150 / 50 = 300 kPa
```

## Pressure and temperature at constant volume (5.20, 5.21)

If a fixed mass of gas in a rigid container is heated:

- the molecules move faster on average;
- so they hit the walls **more often**, and each collision is **harder** (each collision exerts a bigger force);
- so the pressure increases. Both effects must be mentioned for a full explanation.

Pressure is proportional to the Kelvin temperature:

```
p₁ / T₁ = p₂ / T₂        (T in kelvin)
```

**Worked example 3: a car tyre.** A tyre has a pressure of 2.4 × 10⁵ Pa at 7 °C. After a long drive the air inside is
at 37 °C. Assume the volume does not change.

```
T₁ = 7 + 273 = 280 K        T₂ = 37 + 273 = 310 K
p₂ = p₁ × T₂ / T₁ = 2.4 × 10⁵ × 310 / 280 = 2.7 × 10⁵ Pa (2 s.f.)
```

## Combining with pressure in liquids

Gas-law questions can link to `p = h × ρ × g` from sub-topic 5(b).

**Worked example 4: a rising bubble.** A diver releases a bubble of volume 2.0 cm³ at a depth of 20 m in fresh water
(ρ = 1000 kg/m³, g = 10 N/kg). Atmospheric pressure is 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa. Assume the temperature of the bubble does not
change. Find its volume at the surface.

```
pressure at 20 m = 1.0 × 10⁵ + 20 × 1000 × 10 = 3.0 × 10⁵ Pa
p₁V₁ = p₂V₂: 3.0 × 10⁵ × 2.0 = 1.0 × 10⁵ × V₂
V₂ = 6.0 cm³
```

The bubble triples in volume as it rises, because the pressure on it falls to a third.

## Common mistakes

- Using °C in `p₁/T₁ = p₂/T₂`. Always convert to kelvin first.
- Explaining a pressure rise on heating with "molecules hit the walls more often" only. Heating also makes each
  collision harder.
- Saying molecules move faster when a gas is compressed at constant temperature. Their average speed is unchanged;
  only the collision rate changes.
- Saying molecules "stop" at absolute zero. They have the minimum possible kinetic energy.
- Forgetting the conditions: both laws need a **fixed mass** of gas, and each holds one quantity constant.
- Using depth pressure without adding atmospheric pressure in a bubble problem: the gas law needs the total pressure.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** gas pressure: random motion → collisions with the walls → each collision exerts a force on the wall →
  pressure = total force ÷ area.
- **Explain** a change in pressure: say what happens to the speed of the molecules (or not), to how often they hit the
  walls, and to how hard they hit.
- **Calculate:** write the gas law, convert temperatures to kelvin, keep volume and pressure units consistent, and
  check the direction of change is sensible (heating should raise pressure; compressing should raise it too).
- **Describe** the Kelvin scale: starts at absolute zero (0 K = −273 °C), same size steps as °C.
