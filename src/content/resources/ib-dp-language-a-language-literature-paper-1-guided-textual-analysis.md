This study guide teaches the method for **Paper 1: guided textual analysis** in IB Diploma
Programme Language A: Language and Literature, at SL and HL. It follows the current IB subject brief
(first assessments for SL and HL 2021). The skills it uses come from two of the course's three areas of
exploration, **Readers, writers and texts** and **Time and space**, so this page maps to both. It is
about method: how to read an unseen non-literary text, plan, structure and time an analysis that
answers the guiding question.

The brief says "passage/passages". Your teacher will tell you how many passages you write about at
your level and show you the paper's exact layout in specimen material, including how the analysis is
guided. This page assumes a guiding question that points you to one aspect of the text. Your teacher will also share the
assessment criteria; this page does not quote them.

## What "non-literary" means here

A non-literary text is written to do a job: sell, inform, warn, persuade, commemorate. The brief
says the passages come "from different text types", so expect variety: adverts, opinion columns,
speeches, infographics, web pages, leaflets, open letters, blogs. Many are **multimodal**: they combine words with images,
layout, colour and typography, and you must analyse those too.

Paper 1 uses skills from two areas of exploration at once:

- **Readers, writers and texts**: how the text is built to act on a particular reader. See the
  [Readers, writers and texts study guide](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-language-literature-readers-writers-texts/).
- **Time and space**: how the moment and place of production and reception shape meaning. See the
  [Time and space study guide](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-language-literature-time-and-space/).

## Step 1: read the guiding question first

Read the guiding question before the passage and underline its key words. They tell you what to
look for, so your first reading is already aimed. Your whole answer, from first sentence to last,
should respond to it.

## Step 2: read the passage three times

Each reading has a different job.

| Reading | Your job | What you write |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Gist | Work out text type, audience, purpose and context | Four short notes at the top of the page |
| 2. Evidence | Find features that answer the guiding question | Underlining and margin notes on effect |
| 3. Argument | Decide what the features add up to | One sentence: your thesis |

On the second reading, look in four places:

- **Language**: word choice and connotation, register (formal or informal), pronouns ("you",
  "we", "our"), sentence types (imperatives, questions, fragments), rhetorical patterns (lists of
  three, repetition, contrast).
- **Structure**: the order of ideas, headings, where the text turns, how it opens and closes.
- **Visual features**: image, what the eye is drawn to first, layout, white space, font size, colour.
- **Context**: who produced the text, where it appeared, when, and who it assumes its reader is.

Annotate for **effect**. "Imperative" is a label; "imperative: makes the job feel simple and
theirs" is the start of a paragraph.

## Worked example

> **Original text written for Marlbridge** -- community web page
>
> [Layout: a wide banner photograph shows an older man and a teenage girl bent over a toaster, both
> laughing. Beneath it are three short sections, each with a bold heading in large type.]
>
> **The Saturday Fix** -- Brackwell Community Hall, every Saturday, 10am to 1pm
>
> **Broken? Bring it.**
> Kettles, lamps, zips, bikes, the radio your grandmother loved. If it used to work, we want to
> meet it.
>
> **Who fixes it? You do.**
> Our volunteers don't take your things away and hand them back. They sit beside you and show you
> how. You leave with your lamp working and a skill you didn't have when you arrived.
>
> **Why bother?**
> Because "throw it away" doesn't mean it goes away. It means it goes somewhere else. Last year,
> Saturday Fix visitors mended 412 items that would otherwise have been thrown out.
>
> Tea is free. Biscuits are free. Advice is free. The only thing we ask you to leave behind is the
> idea that broken means finished.

**Guiding question:** How does the text encourage its readers to take part?

### Reading 1: gist notes

```
Type:     community web page (multimodal: photo + headed sections)
Audience: local residents of Brackwell, any age, not experts
Purpose:  persuade them to come on a Saturday and bring broken items
Context:  local, voluntary, free; set against a throwaway habit
```

### Reading 2: evidence notes

- The headings are questions and answers ("Broken? Bring it."). They answer the reader's doubts
  before the reader can raise them.
- "Who fixes it? You do." turns the reader from customer into repairer.
- The list "Kettles, lamps, zips, bikes" moves from ordinary objects to an emotional one, "the
  radio your grandmother loved".
- "we want to meet it" personifies the objects, which makes the café sound friendly, not technical.
- The photograph pairs an older man and a teenager, so the image shows the mixed ages it invites.
- The final paragraph repeats "free" three times, then turns to a bigger idea: "broken means
  finished".

### Reading 3: thesis

> The web page encourages participation by removing every obstacle a reader might imagine, from
> cost to lack of skill, and then by redefining the reader as someone who can fix things.

The thesis answers the question and gives a structure: obstacles first, identity second.

## Step 3: plan in five minutes

Plan by **idea**, not by device and not line by line. Each body paragraph makes one claim that
answers the guiding question, then uses two or three features as evidence.

```
Thesis: removes obstacles, then redefines the reader as a fixer.
P1  Obstacles answered in advance: question-answer headings,
    "Tea is free. Biscuits are free. Advice is free."
P2  Reader made active: "Who fixes it? You do.", second person,
    "a skill you didn't have when you arrived".
P3  Emotional and communal appeal: "the radio your grandmother
    loved", photo of two generations laughing.
P4  Bigger purpose: "throw it away" doesn't mean it goes away;
    closing line "broken means finished" -> wider value.
```

In 75 minutes, three strong paragraphs beat five thin ones.

## Step 4: structure the analysis

### Introduction

Keep it short, three or four sentences. Name the text type, audience, purpose and context, then
give your thesis.

> This web page advertises a free community repair café to residents of the small town of
> Brackwell. Its purpose is to persuade readers who may feel unskilled to give up a Saturday
> morning and bring a broken object. The page encourages participation by removing every obstacle a
> reader might imagine, from cost to lack of skill, and then by redefining the reader as someone who
> can fix things.

### Body paragraphs

Each paragraph follows the same pattern: **claim → evidence → effect → link**.

1. **Claim**: a sentence that answers the guiding question.
2. **Evidence**: a short quotation, embedded in your own sentence, or a precise visual detail.
3. **Effect**: what the feature does to the reader, and why the writer wants that effect.
4. **Link**: tie the paragraph back to the guiding question.

A model paragraph:

> The page turns its reader from a customer into a participant. The heading "Who fixes it? You do."
> answers its own question in two words, and the reply is surprising, because a reader expects a
> repair service to repair. The second person "you" is repeated through the section, and the
> volunteers are placed "beside" the visitor rather than behind a counter. The promise that visitors
> leave with "a skill you didn't have when you arrived" offers a gain that lasts longer than one
> mended lamp. By making the reader the fixer, the page makes taking part feel like a small
> achievement rather than a favour asked of them.

It never names a device and stops, and never retells the passage. Every quotation is short and
sits inside a sentence of analysis.

### Conclusion

Two or three sentences, no new evidence. Say how the features work **together**, and why the
approach suits this audience, place and moment.

> The page works because every feature points the same way: cost, skill and worth are each dealt
> with before the reader can object. Its final line widens a Saturday repair session into a
> challenge to the habit of throwing things away.

## Step 5: manage your time

The brief sets **1 hour 15 minutes at SL** and **2 hours 15 minutes at HL**. A workable split for
one analysis in 75 minutes:

| Stage | Time |
|---|---|
| Guiding question and three readings | 15 min |
| Thesis and plan | 5 min |
| Introduction | 5 min |
| Three body paragraphs | 40 min |
| Conclusion | 5 min |
| Check quotations and links to the question | 5 min |
| **Total** | **75 min** |

If your paper asks you to write about more than one passage in the HL time, divide the 135 minutes
evenly: roughly 65 minutes per analysis, with 5 minutes spare. Plan each analysis separately. Do
not start the second passage until the first has a conclusion.

## Text-type conventions worth knowing

"Features of particular text types" are part of the objectives, so use the conventions of the type
as evidence.

| Text type | Conventions to comment on |
|---|---|
| Advert | slogan, image, brand name placement, direct address, call to action |
| Opinion column | headline, standfirst, personal voice, anecdote, counter-argument, closing punch |
| Speech | direct address to the audience, repetition, lists of three, reference to the occasion |
| Infographic | headline figure, icons, colour coding, reading path, source line |
| Web page | banner image, headings, short sections, links and buttons |
| Leaflet or poster | hierarchy of type sizes, bullet points, logo, small print |

## Common errors

- **Answering a different question.** A tour of every feature, with no thesis about the guiding
  question, reads as a list.
- **Labelling without effect.** "The writer uses a rhetorical question" earns little until you say
  what it makes the reader think or feel.
- **Long quotations.** Three copied lines hide which words matter. Quote a few words.
- **Ignoring the visuals.** On a multimodal text, skipping the image or layout leaves out half the
  evidence.
- **Forgetting context.** The audience, place and moment explain why a feature works. This is the
  Time and space link.
- **Running out of time.** A missing conclusion is a planning failure. Keep to your split.
