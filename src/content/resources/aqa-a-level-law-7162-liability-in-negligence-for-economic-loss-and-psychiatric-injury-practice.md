These questions test the section 3.3 row on pure economic loss caused by negligent acts and misstatements, and psychiatric injury to primary and secondary victims.

---

## Section A

**1.** Explain the difference between consequential economic loss and pure economic loss. **[2]**

**2.** State the decision in *Weller v Foot and Mouth Disease Research Institute* (1966). **[2]**

**3.** Identify **three** requirements of a special relationship for liability for a negligent misstatement. **[3]**

**4.** Explain what the claimant must prove about the nature of their injury in a psychiatric injury claim. **[2]**

**5.** Explain the decision in *Page v Smith* (1996). **[3]**

**6.** Explain how *White v Chief Constable of South Yorkshire* (1998) affects claims by rescuers. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain why the claimant in *Spartan Steel v Martin* (1973) recovered some losses but not others. **[4]**

**8.** Explain the decision in *Smith v Eric S Bush* (1990) and its importance for liability for negligent misstatements. **[4]**

**9.** Explain the requirement of proximity in time and space for secondary victims, using **two** cases. **[4]**

**10.** Nadia, a structural engineer, writes a report for Pine Homes Ltd on a block of flats it is building, knowing the report will be shown to the bank lending Pine Homes money. The report negligently says the foundations are sound. The bank lends on the strength of it. A year later, cracks appear and the flats lose much of their value. Pine Homes cannot repay the loan. Advise the bank whether it can sue Nadia. **[6]**

**11.** A gas explosion caused by the negligence of Heatwell Ltd destroys a block of flats. Ola, a resident, escapes from the building as it collapses and develops PTSD. Ola's father, Ben, arrives 40 minutes later, while the injured are still being carried out, and sees Ola's flatmate, Cal, his close friend, badly burned. Ben suffers clinical depression. Dee, Ola's cousin, watches the collapse on a news livestream and is distressed for weeks. Advise Ola, Ben and Dee. **[9]**

**12.** "The rules on secondary victims are arbitrary." Discuss. **[6]**

---

## Answers

**1.** Consequential economic loss is financial loss that follows from physical injury to the claimant or damage to their property, such as lost wages [1]. Pure economic loss is financial loss with no such physical damage to the claimant or their property [1].

**2.** A virus escaped from the defendant's premises, causing cattle markets to close [1]. Auctioneers who lost business could not recover because their loss was pure economic loss; none of their own property was damaged [1].

**3.** Any three from: the defendant has special skill or knowledge [1]; the defendant knows the claimant, or a group including the claimant, will rely on the statement [1]; the statement is for a particular purpose known to the defendant [1]; the claimant reasonably relies on it (*Hedley Byrne*, 1964; *Caparo*, 1990).

**4.** The claimant must prove a recognised psychiatric illness, such as PTSD or clinical depression, supported by medical evidence [1]; mere grief, fear or distress is not enough [1].

**5.** The claimant was in a minor car crash caused by the defendant but suffered no physical injury [1]. His chronic fatigue condition returned [1]. He was a primary victim, and because physical injury was foreseeable, he could recover for psychiatric injury even though that kind of injury was not itself foreseeable [1].

**6.** Police officers who helped at the Hillsborough disaster claimed for psychiatric injury [1]. The House of Lords held that rescuers and employees are not automatically primary victims [1]: they are primary victims only if they were in physical danger or reasonably believed they were; otherwise they must meet the *Alcock* controls [1].

**7.** Contractors negligently cut the power cable to the factory [1]. The claimant recovered for the physical damage to the melt in the furnace and for the profit lost on that melt, because that loss was consequential on property damage [1]. It could not recover profits on further melts it would have carried out during the power cut [1], because that was pure economic loss; Lord Denning noted that businesses can insure against such risks [1].

**8.** A surveyor valued a house for a mortgage lender and negligently missed defects; the buyer relied on the valuation [1]. The House of Lords held that a duty was owed to the buyer because the surveyor knew the buyer would very probably rely on it in deciding to buy [1]. The surveyor's disclaimer was subject to the Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977 and failed the reasonableness test [1]. The case shows a duty can be owed to a known third party who is not the client, and that disclaimers do not always protect [1].

**9.** A secondary victim must be present at the event or come upon its immediate aftermath [1]. In *McLoughlin v O'Brian* (1983) a mother who reached the hospital about two hours after a crash and saw her family before they had been cleaned up was within the immediate aftermath [1]. In *Alcock* (1992) a claimant who identified a body at the mortuary about eight hours later was outside it [1]. The requirement is a policy control to limit the number of claimants [1].

**10.** The loss is pure economic loss caused by a statement, so the question is whether there was a special relationship under *Hedley Byrne* (1964) [1]. Nadia has special skill as an engineer [1]. She knew the report would be shown to the bank, an identified party [1], and the purpose was to support the lending decision, known to her [1]. The bank relied on it, and reliance was reasonable for a professional report [1]. Unlike *Caparo*, where auditors did not know of individual investors, Nadia knew of this specific reliance, so a duty is likely and the bank may recover its loss on the loan, subject to any reasonable disclaimer [1].

**11.** **Ola:** she was in the collapsing building, so she was in the zone of physical danger and is a primary victim [1]. PTSD is a recognised illness [1]. Physical injury was foreseeable, so *Page v Smith* applies and she is likely to succeed [1]. **Ben:** he is not in danger, so he is a secondary victim [1]. He arrived while the injured were still being carried out, which is likely the immediate aftermath (*McLoughlin*) [1], and he perceived it with his own senses [1]. However, his depression appears to arise from seeing Cal, a friend, not his child, so he must prove a close tie with Cal; friendship alone is unlikely to be enough, and he did not see Ola injured [1]. **Dee:** she watched a livestream, which is not perception with her own unaided senses (*Alcock*) [1], and distress is not a recognised illness, so she will fail [1].

**12.** Secondary victims must meet the *Alcock* controls [1]. They can be arbitrary: a close tie is presumed for parents but must be proved by siblings, so in *Alcock* a man who lost two brothers failed for lack of evidence [1]. Timing creates sharp lines: two hours was enough in *McLoughlin*, eight hours too late in *Alcock* [1]. Officers in *White* failed, while *Chadwick* had succeeded, largely for consistency [1]. In defence, the rules provide certainty and prevent unlimited liability for disasters seen by millions [1]. Conclusion: the lines are justifiable as policy, but outcomes for individual claimants can be arbitrary, and the Law Commission's 1998 proposals for reform have not been adopted [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling all financial losses pure economic loss.
- Forgetting the disclaimer point in *Hedley Byrne*.
- Applying *Alcock* to a primary victim.
- Ignoring the requirement of a recognised psychiatric illness.
