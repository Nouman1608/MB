Sub-topic 3.2 of the Cambridge O Level Environmental Management (5014) syllabus for 2027 to 2029 has three statements: the sources of water pollution (3.2.1), their impacts on people and the environment (3.2.2), and strategies for improving water quality (3.2.3). The middle statement carries the most weight, because it uses "describe and explain" and lists six impacts, several of which are processes you must be able to give in sequence.

## The five sources of water pollution

The syllabus limits sources to five. For each, know what the pollutant is and how it reaches water.

| Source | Typical pollutants | Route into water |
|---|---|---|
| Domestic waste | Food waste, detergents, oils, household chemicals | Dumped in or near rivers; washed out of open tips by rain |
| Sewage | Human faeces and urine containing organic matter, nutrients and microorganisms | Untreated discharge from sewers, overflowing pits and latrines |
| Plastic waste | Bags, bottles, packaging, fragments | Litter blown or washed into drains and rivers |
| Industrial processes | Heavy metals, toxic chemicals, heated water | Discharge pipes, spills, leaks from storage |
| Agricultural practices | Fertilisers (nitrate and phosphate ions), animal waste, pesticides | Surface run-off and leaching from fields |

Sources are sometimes grouped as **point sources** (a single pipe or outfall, easy to locate and regulate) and **diffuse sources** (run-off from a whole landscape, hard to control). This is a useful idea when you explain why some strategies are harder to apply than others.

## Impacts on people and the environment

### Risk of cholera

Sewage contains bacteria from human waste. Where untreated sewage enters rivers or wells that people drink from, there is a risk of infectious bacterial diseases, and the syllabus names **cholera**. Floods make outbreaks more likely because they mix sewage with drinking water sources. Cholera prevention belongs to sub-topic 3.3, so here focus on the link between the pollution source and the risk.

### Accumulation of toxic substances

Industrial processes can release substances, such as heavy metals, that organisms cannot break down or excrete easily. Two terms describe how they build up, and examiners expect you to keep them separate.

- **Bioaccumulation**: the build-up of a toxic substance in a *single organism over time*, because it takes the substance in faster than it can get rid of it.
- **Biomagnification**: the increase in concentration of a toxic substance *as it is passed up a food chain*, because each consumer eats many organisms from the level below and keeps the toxin from all of them.

Top predators, including people who eat large fish, are most at risk, even if the concentration in the water itself is very low.

### Acid rain in rivers and lakes

Acid rain lowers the pH of rivers and lakes. Many aquatic organisms are sensitive to low pH: fish eggs and young fish may fail to develop, and some invertebrates disappear, which affects the animals that feed on them. The formation of acid rain is covered in Topic 4; in 3.2 you need its effect on organisms in water.

### Leaching, nutrient enrichment and eutrophication

The syllabus names two kinds of material that enrich water: **organic content** (sewage, animal waste, food waste) and **inorganic ions** (nitrate and phosphate from fertilisers). **Leaching** is the washing of dissolved ions down through the soil and into ground water and rivers, especially after heavy rain on recently fertilised fields.

The full eutrophication sequence is:

1. Nutrients enter the water from fertiliser leaching or sewage.
2. Algae grow rapidly, forming an algal bloom on the surface.
3. The bloom blocks light, so plants under the surface cannot photosynthesise and die.
4. Algae also die when nutrients run out.
5. Bacteria decompose the dead plants and algae; their respiration uses up dissolved oxygen.
6. Fish and other animals die from lack of oxygen.

Organic content from sewage adds a shortcut: bacteria start decomposing the organic matter directly, so oxygen falls even before any algal bloom appears.

## Strategies for improving water quality

- **Improved sanitation**: toilets, latrines and sewers that keep human waste away from water sources. Latrines should be sited away from and downhill of wells.
- **Treatment of sewage**: solids are screened and settled out; bacteria are then given air so they break down the organic matter; the cleaner water is released to a river. Treatment removes most organic content and many microorganisms before discharge.
- **Pollution control and legislation**: laws setting limits on what factories and farms may release, permits for discharge pipes, regular monitoring of water quality, fines for breaches, and rules on when and how much fertiliser may be spread.

Each strategy has limits worth mentioning in a longer answer: sanitation and treatment works are expensive to build in fast-growing cities; legislation only works if it is enforced and monitored; and diffuse farm run-off is hard to trace to one polluter.

## Worked example 1: following a toxin up a food chain

*Figures invented for practice.* A factory releases mercury into a bay. Concentrations measured in parts per million (ppm) are:

| Level | Concentration (ppm) |
|---|---|
| Sea water | 0.0002 |
| Plankton | 0.02 |
| Small fish | 0.4 |
| Large fish | 2.0 |
| Dolphin | 10 |

1. Plankton compared with water: 0.02 ÷ 0.0002 = 100 times higher.
2. Small fish compared with plankton: 0.4 ÷ 0.02 = 20 times.
3. Large fish compared with small fish: 2.0 ÷ 0.4 = 5 times.
4. Dolphin compared with water: 10 ÷ 0.0002 = 50,000 times.

Interpretation: the concentration rises at every step, which is biomagnification. The level in the water looks harmless, yet the dolphin carries 50,000 times as much. If a single large fish were tested each year and its mercury level rose over time, that would be evidence of bioaccumulation.

## Worked example 2: oxygen below a sewage outfall

*Figures invented for practice.* Dissolved oxygen in a river was measured above and below a pipe releasing untreated sewage.

| Site | Distance from outfall | Dissolved oxygen (mg per litre) |
|---|---|---|
| A | 200 m upstream | 9.0 |
| B | 100 m downstream | 3.5 |
| C | 1 km downstream | 5.0 |
| D | 5 km downstream | 8.0 |

A good description quotes figures: oxygen falls from 9.0 to 3.5 mg per litre just below the outfall, a drop of 5.5 mg per litre, then recovers to 8.0 mg per litre by 5 km. The explanation: bacteria decomposing the organic matter in the sewage use oxygen in respiration; downstream, the organic matter has been broken down and diluted, and turbulent water and photosynthesis add oxygen again. Fish would be most at risk at site B.

## Common mistakes

- Writing that fertiliser "poisons" fish. In eutrophication, fish die from lack of oxygen.
- Leaving out the bacteria. The oxygen is used by decomposers, not by the algae blocking it.
- Swapping bioaccumulation and biomagnification.
- Giving "clean the river" as a strategy. Name sanitation, sewage treatment or a specific control.
- Describing acid rain formation when the question asks about its effect on organisms in lakes.

## Exam technique

**Describe** sources by naming the activity and the pollutant. **Describe and explain** impacts by giving the effect and then the reason, linked with "because" or "so". For sequence processes such as eutrophication, numbered steps keep the chain in order. In data questions, quote at least two figures with units and work out a difference. Where a question gives a place, refer to it, for example "the factory upstream of the village".
