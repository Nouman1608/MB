These questions cover **section 3.2.3.2 Monetary policy** in the AQA GCSE Economics (8136) specification for first teaching from September 2017: what monetary policy is, how it controls inflation, and how it affects other objectives. There are no tiers. All households, firms and figures are invented. For explanations, see the [monetary policy study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-monetary-policy-aqa-gcse-economics/) and the [monetary policy revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-monetary-policy-aqa-gcse-economics-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following sets the Bank Rate in the UK? **[1]**

A: HM Treasury

B: The Chancellor of the Exchequer

C: The Monetary Policy Committee of the Bank of England

D: The Office for National Statistics

**2.** Which **one** of the following is the most likely result of a rise in the Bank Rate? **[1]**

A: Households borrow more

B: The pound weakens

C: Households save more

D: Business investment rises

**3.** Define monetary policy. **[1]**

**4.** A household has an interest-only mortgage of £210,000. Its interest rate rises from 2% to 4.5%.

**(a)** Calculate the yearly interest at each rate. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage increase in the household's yearly interest payments. **[2]**

**5.** A saver holds £8,000 in an account paying 4.5% interest a year. Inflation is 3%.

**(a)** Calculate the interest earned in one year. **[1]**

**(b)** State the approximate real interest rate. **[1]**

**6.** A firm can borrow £150,000 for a project that would earn £9,000 a year.

**(a)** Calculate the project's yearly return as a percentage of the amount borrowed. **[1]**

**(b)** Using calculations, explain whether the firm should go ahead if the interest rate is 5%, and if it is 7%. **[3]**

**7.** State **two** groups who are likely to lose from a cut in the Bank Rate. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**8.** Explain how a rise in UK interest rates could reduce inflation through its effect on the exchange rate. **[3]**

**9.** Explain how a cut in interest rates could increase economic growth. **[4]**

**10.** Explain **one** conflict between using higher interest rates to reduce inflation and another government economic objective. **[3]**

**11.** In an invented country inflation is 1%, below its 2% target. Unemployment is rising and consumer confidence is low.

**(a)** State the change in interest rates the central bank is likely to make. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why this policy may be less effective than the central bank hopes. **[3]**

**12.** Evaluate whether monetary policy is the best way for a government to control inflation. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** **C** [1]. The government sets the target; the Bank of England's committee sets the rate. **[1]**

**2.** **C** [1]. Saving becomes more rewarding; borrowing and investment fall and the pound tends to strengthen. **[1]**

**3.** The use of interest rates and the supply of money to influence spending in the economy [1]. **[1]**

**4. (a)** At 2%: 0.02 × 210,000 = **£4,200** [1]. At 4.5%: 0.045 × 210,000 = **£9,450** [1].

**(b)** (9,450 − 4,200) ÷ 4,200 × 100 [1] = **125%** [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* A 2.5 percentage-point rise in the rate more than doubles the payments, which is why rate rises cut spending so sharply for borrowers.

**5. (a)** 0.045 × 8,000 = **£360** [1].

**(b)** About 4.5 − 3 = **1.5%** [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** 9,000 ÷ 150,000 × 100 = **6%** [1].

**(b)** At 5% the loan costs 0.05 × 150,000 = £7,500 a year, less than the £9,000 earned, so the project adds £1,500 and should go ahead [1]. At 7% it costs £10,500, more than the £9,000 earned [1], so the project makes a loss of £1,500 a year and the firm should not go ahead [1]. **[4]**

**7.** Any two: savers [1]; people living on interest from their savings, such as some pensioners [1]; importers, if the pound weakens [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Higher UK interest rates attract savers from abroad, who buy pounds, so the pound strengthens [1]. Imports become cheaper in pounds [1], which lowers the prices of imported goods and materials and so reduces inflation [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Lower interest rates make borrowing cheaper [1], so households spend more on goods such as cars and furniture, and firms invest more in machinery [1]. Total demand rises, so firms increase output [1], and real GDP grows; extra investment also raises productive capacity in the longer term [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Higher interest rates reduce spending by households and firms [1]. Firms sell less, so they cut output and need fewer workers [1], so unemployment rises and the objective of full employment is harder to achieve (or growth slows) [1]. **[3]**

**11. (a)** A cut in interest rates [1].

**(b)** With low confidence, households worried about their jobs may not borrow even when loans are cheaper [1], and firms may not invest if they expect weak sales [1]. Spending may therefore rise only a little, and the effect also takes many months to appear [1]. **[4]**

**12.** *For monetary policy:*

- Interest rates can be changed quickly, at any meeting of the committee [1].
- Higher rates reduce borrowing, raise saving and cut investment, so total demand falls and demand-pull inflation slows [1].
- The Bank of England is independent, so decisions are less influenced by elections, which makes its commitment to the target more believable [1].

*Against:*

- It takes many months, often more than a year, to have its full effect [1].
- It does little directly against cost-push inflation, such as rises in world energy prices [1].
- It harms borrowers and can slow growth and raise unemployment [1].
- Fiscal and supply-side policies can also reduce inflation: higher taxes cut demand, and higher productivity reduces costs [1].

*Judgement:* monetary policy is usually the best first tool against demand-pull inflation because it can be changed quickly and by an independent body [1], but for cost-push inflation, supply-side policies that lower costs may matter more, so the best policy depends on the cause [1]. **[9]**

*Examiner insight:* Mention at least one alternative policy, or the judgement has nothing to be "best" against.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying the government sets interest rates.
- Calculating a percentage change from the new figure.
- Stopping at "people borrow less" without reaching demand and prices.
- Forgetting that savers gain from higher rates.
- Not naming the type of inflation when judging the policy.
