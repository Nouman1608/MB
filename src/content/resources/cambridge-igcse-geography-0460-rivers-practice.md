These questions cover topic 2.2 Rivers in Theme 2 (The natural environment) of the Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026. The river and fieldwork data in Question 7 are fictional and invented.

Revise first with the [Rivers study guide](/resources/igcse-geography-rivers/) and the [Rivers revision notes](/resources/igcse-geography-0460-rivers-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Define the term "watershed". **[1]**

**2.** Define the terms "tributary" and "confluence". **[2]**

**3.** Name two processes by which a river transports its load. **[2]**

**4.** Define the terms "infiltration" and "throughflow". **[2]**

**5.** Name two processes of river erosion. **[2]**

**6.** Give two opportunities that a flood plain offers to people. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Students measured a fictional river at three sites, from Site 1 near the source to Site 3 further downstream.

| Site | Width (m) | Mean depth (m) | Mean velocity (m/s) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 4.0 | 0.5 | 0.3 |
| 2 | 9.0 | 1.2 | 0.6 |
| 3 | 15.0 | 2.0 | 0.8 |

**(a)** Calculate the cross-sectional area and the discharge at Site 2. Show your working and give units. **[2]**
**(b)** The discharge at Site 1 is 0.6 m³/s and at Site 3 is 24 m³/s. Describe how the river's characteristics change downstream. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why the velocity increases downstream, even though the gradient becomes gentler. **[2]**

**8.** Explain the formation of an oxbow lake. You may use a labelled diagram. **[5]**

**9.** Explain the formation of a waterfall. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how human activities can increase the risk of river flooding. **[4]**

**11.** Describe and evaluate two methods of managing the impacts of river flooding. **[6]**

**12.** For a named river or rivers you have studied, to what extent do the opportunities of living near the river outweigh the hazards? **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** The boundary (high ground) separating one drainage basin from another [1]. **[1]**

**2.** Tributary: a smaller river or stream that flows into a larger river [1]. Confluence: the point where two rivers meet [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Traction (large boulders rolled along the bed) [1]. Saltation (pebbles bounced along the bed) [1]. Also credit suspension or solution. **[2]**

**4.** Infiltration: water soaking into the soil from the surface [1]. Throughflow: water moving sideways through the soil towards the river [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Throughflow is through the soil; groundwater flow is through the rock below. Mixing them up is common.

**5.** Hydraulic action [1]. Abrasion [1]. Also credit attrition or solution. **[2]**

**6.** Fertile soil from deposited silt, good for farming [1]. Flat land that is easy to build on and to build transport routes across [1]. Also credit water supply for irrigation, drinking or industry. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Cross-sectional area = 9.0 × 1.2 = 10.8 m² [1]. Discharge = 10.8 × 0.6 = 6.48 m³/s [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The river becomes wider, from 4.0 m to 15.0 m [1], and deeper, from 0.5 m to 2.0 m [1]. Velocity more than doubles, from 0.3 to 0.8 m/s, and discharge increases forty times, from 0.6 to 24 m³/s [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Tributaries add water, so the river has more water and a larger, smoother channel [1]. Less of the water is in contact with the bed and banks, so there is less friction, allowing the water to flow faster [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Discharge must have units (m³/s or cumecs); a correct number without units often loses the mark.

**8.** On a meander, the fastest water flows on the outside of the bend, causing erosion and forming a river cliff [1], while slower water on the inside deposits material, forming a slip-off slope [1]. Over time, erosion on the outside of two bends narrows the neck of land between them [1]. During a flood, the river cuts through the neck, taking the shorter, straighter route [1]. Deposition seals the ends of the old bend, cutting it off to leave an oxbow lake [1]. **[5]**

**9.** A waterfall forms where a band of hard (resistant) rock lies on top of softer rock [1]. The softer rock is eroded more quickly, undercutting the hard rock and forming an overhang [1]. The overhang collapses, and the fallen rocks swirl in the plunge pool, deepening it by abrasion [1]. This process repeats, so the waterfall retreats upstream, leaving a gorge [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Building on the flood plain covers soil with impermeable concrete and tarmac, so less water infiltrates and more flows overland [1]. Drains carry water quickly to the river, so it rises faster [1]. Deforestation reduces interception and transpiration, so more rain reaches the river quickly [1]. Farming practices such as ploughing down slopes or overgrazing increase overland flow and add sediment, which reduces channel capacity [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Method 1: building levées or flood walls raises the banks so the river can hold more water [1]. They protect towns behind them [1], but they are expensive and can fail in a very large flood, and may move the flooding downstream [1]. Method 2: afforestation in the upper drainage basin increases interception and infiltration, slowing the flow of water to the river [1]. It is cheap and benefits wildlife [1], but it takes many years for trees to grow and does little in an extreme flood [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Accept other methods, such as dams, flood warnings or land-use zoning; each needs a description and an evaluation for full marks.

**12.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point. Indicative creditworthy content:

Named river with location [1]. Opportunity: fertile flood plain or delta soils for farming [1]. Opportunity: water for irrigation, drinking or industry, transport or hydroelectric power [1]. Hazard: flooding of homes and farmland, with a named event or area affected [1]. Hazard: river bank erosion destroying land or buildings [1]. Management: a named method used on that river, with its effects [1]. Place-specific detail throughout [1]. Judgement: for example, opportunities outweigh hazards for most people because floods are occasional and management reduces their impact, but not for the poorest people living on the most flood-prone land [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Without a named river and specific facts, answers stay in the lowest level even if the general points are correct.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving units off area (m²) and discharge (m³/s).
- Explaining waterfalls without saying which rock is hard and which is soft.
- Oxbow lake answers that skip the meander stage.
- Flood management answers that describe a method without evaluating it.
- Case studies with no named river.
