These notes condense **A2.3 Viruses** from the IB DP *Biology guide*, first assessment 2025. The whole sub-topic is additional higher level. The [A2.3 study guide](/resources/ib-dp-biology-1-a2-3/) explains each idea with worked examples, and the [A2.3 practice questions](/resources/ib-dp-biology-1-a2-3-practice/) test them. ## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Virion | A complete virus particle outside a host cell |
| Capsid | Protein coat enclosing the viral nucleic acid |
| Envelope | Outer membrane taken from the host cell, carrying viral glycoproteins |
| Bacteriophage | Virus that infects bacteria |
| Lytic cycle | Infection that ends with the host cell bursting and releasing virions |
| Lysogenic cycle | Infection in which viral DNA integrates into the host chromosome |
| Prophage | Phage DNA integrated into a bacterial chromosome |
| Induction | Switch from lysogenic to lytic, often triggered by host damage |
| Reverse transcriptase | Enzyme that copies RNA into DNA |
| Retrovirus | RNA virus that uses reverse transcriptase, such as HIV |
| Obligate parasite | Organism or agent that can reproduce only inside a host |

## A2.3.1: shared features

1. Small, fixed size (no growth).
2. Nucleic acid: DNA or RNA.
3. Protein capsid.
4. No cytoplasm.
5. Few or no enzymes.

Consequence: no metabolism of their own, so every virus depends on a host cell.

## A2.3.2: the named viruses

| | Lambda | Coronaviruses | HIV |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nucleic acid | dsDNA | ssRNA | ssRNA |
| Envelope | No | Yes | Yes |
| Host | *E. coli* | Animals incl. humans | Human cells, mainly helper T cells |
| Distinctive | Head and tail; injects DNA | Spike glycoproteins | Reverse transcriptase |

Ways viruses vary: DNA or RNA; single- or double-stranded; enveloped or not; shape of capsid.

## A2.3.3: lytic cycle of lambda

1. Tail attaches to a specific receptor.
2. DNA injected; capsid stays outside.
3. Host ribosomes make viral proteins; host and viral enzymes copy viral DNA.
4. Capsids assemble around DNA.
5. Viral enzymes weaken the wall; the cell lyses and releases virions.

## A2.3.4: lysogenic cycle of lambda

1. Attachment and injection as above.
2. DNA integrated into the host chromosome as a prophage.
3. Repressor protein keeps lytic genes off.
4. Prophage copied every time the host divides.
5. Induction (for example by ultraviolet damage): prophage excised, lytic cycle begins.

### Lytic versus lysogenic in four lines

- Lytic destroys the host; lysogenic does not.
- Lytic copies viral DNA many times; lysogenic copies it once per host division.
- Lytic releases virions quickly; lysogenic releases none until induction.
- Lysogenic spreads by host division; lytic spreads by new infections.

## A2.3.5: several origins

- Viruses are extremely diverse in genome and structure.
- Shared features suit any obligate parasite, so they may be convergent.
- Some viral genes resemble host genes; different virus groups share few genes.
- Hypotheses: escaped host genetic material; reduced cellular parasites.
- Same genetic code as cells: consistent with arising from or alongside cellular life.

## A2.3.6: rapid evolution

**Why fast:** huge numbers of virions; short replication time; high mutation rates (RNA copying enzymes often lack proofreading).

| Virus | How it changes | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Influenza | Mutations in surface protein genes; mixing of genome segments between strains | Vaccines reformulated regularly |
| HIV | Error-prone reverse transcriptase; rapid replication within a patient | Drug resistance; combination therapy |

Mutation is random; selection by immunity or drugs determines which variants spread.

## Worked reminders

**Burst size.** 4.5 × 10⁸ phages released from 1.5 × 10⁶ infected cells: 4.5 × 10⁸ ÷ 1.5 × 10⁶ = **300 per cell**.

**Combination therapy.** If 1 in 10⁵ virions resists drug A and 1 in 10⁵ resists drug B independently, 1 in 10⁵ × 10⁵ = 10¹⁰ resists both. Using both drugs makes resistance far less likely.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Capsid versus envelope.** Every virus has a capsid; only some have an envelope.
- **Integration versus replication.** A prophage is copied once per host division; lytic DNA is copied many times.
- **Mutation versus selection.** Mutation creates variants at random; immunity and drugs select among them.

## Exam traps

- Viruses given cytoplasm, ribosomes or a cell membrane.
- Lambda described as enveloped or as an RNA virus.
- Prophage placed in the cytoplasm.
- "Viruses mutate to avoid the immune system": mutation has no purpose.
- Lysogenic infection described as harmless forever; induction can trigger lysis.
- HIV described as making RNA from DNA; reverse transcriptase makes DNA from RNA.

## Quick self-test

1. List the five features common to all viruses.
2. Why must every virus use a host cell to reproduce?
3. State the type of nucleic acid in lambda, coronaviruses and HIV.
4. Where does a viral envelope come from?
5. What stays outside the cell when lambda infects a bacterium?
6. What is a prophage?
7. What keeps a prophage from starting the lytic cycle?
8. Give one trigger for induction.
9. Why do RNA viruses often have high mutation rates?
10. Why does the influenza vaccine change from year to year?
11. Why is HIV treated with several drugs at once?
12. Give one piece of evidence that viruses have more than one origin.

### Answers

1. Small fixed size; DNA or RNA; protein capsid; no cytoplasm; few or no enzymes.
2. They have no metabolism of their own: no ATP production or protein synthesis.
3. Lambda dsDNA; coronaviruses ssRNA; HIV ssRNA.
4. The host cell's membrane, taken as the virus buds out.
5. The capsid (only the DNA enters).
6. Phage DNA integrated into the bacterial chromosome.
7. A viral repressor protein.
8. Ultraviolet light or other DNA damage to the host.
9. Their copying enzymes lack proofreading, so errors are not corrected.
10. Surface proteins change, so antibodies from earlier vaccines bind less well.
11. A variant resistant to all the drugs at once is very unlikely.
12. Great diversity of structure and genome; shared features could be convergent; some viral genes resemble host genes (any one).
