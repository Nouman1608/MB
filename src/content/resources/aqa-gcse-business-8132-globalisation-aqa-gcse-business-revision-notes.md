## Core recall

### What the specification lists

- How UK businesses compete internationally, offering better designs and higher quality products at lower prices.
- Knowledge of globalisation and the benefits and drawbacks it offers UK businesses.
- The impact of exchange rates on the profit and sales of businesses that import and/or export.
- No exchange rate conversion calculations.

### Globalisation in practice

Exporting, importing, foreign rivals in the UK market, and production overseas, made easier by cheaper transport, communication and e-commerce.

### Two ways to compete abroad

| Route | How it wins sales | Suits |
|---|---|---|
| Better designs | Distinctive look or function; hard to copy; supports a higher price | Craft, fashion, design-led and heritage brands |
| Higher quality at lower prices | Better value than rivals through efficiency, cheaper inputs, larger output | High-volume manufacturers |

Trade-off: design and quality usually add cost, so being best-designed and cheapest at once is difficult.

### Benefits and drawbacks

| Benefits | Drawbacks |
|---|---|
| Larger market and more potential sales | More foreign competition in the UK |
| Risk spread across countries | Longer, less reliable supply chains |
| Cheaper imported materials and components | Shipping, translation and adapting products |
| Lower unit costs from higher output | Exchange rate risk |
| New ideas and methods | Reputation risk from overseas suppliers |

### Exchange rate effects

| | Exporter | Importer |
|---|---|---|
| Pound strengthens | Products dearer abroad; sales may fall, or price cut and profit per item falls | Imports cheaper; costs fall; profit per item may rise |
| Pound weakens | Products cheaper abroad; sales may rise | Imports dearer; costs rise; profit per item may fall |

Memory aid: SPICED, Strong Pound, Imports Cheaper, Exports Dearer.

A business that both imports and exports is partly protected, because the two effects pull in opposite directions.

### Exporter's dilemma when the pound strengthens

- Keep the pound price: protect profit per item, risk losing sales.
- Cut the pound price: protect sales, accept less profit per item.
- Choose by how price-sensitive overseas customers are.

### Linking globalisation to the four functions

- Operations: sourcing parts abroad, managing longer delivery times, holding more stock in case shipments are late.
- Human resources: staff with language skills or knowledge of overseas markets; pressure on jobs if rivals abroad have lower labour costs.
- Marketing: adapting products, packaging and promotion for each country; selling to overseas customers through a website.
- Finance: shipping and insurance costs, income received in other currencies, and the effect of exchange rate changes on profit.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Globalisation | The growing connection between the world's economies, so goods, services, money and information move more easily |
| Export | A good or service sold to a customer in another country |
| Import | A good or service bought from a business in another country |
| Exchange rate | The price of one currency in terms of another |
| Appreciation | A rise in the value of a currency (stronger pound) |
| Depreciation | A fall in the value of a currency (weaker pound) |
| Supply chain | The businesses involved in getting a product from raw materials to customers |
| Competitiveness | How well a business's products compare with rivals' on price, quality and design |
| Premium price | A price above that of rivals, supported by design, quality or brand |

## Exam traps

- **Doing conversions.** You will not be asked to; explain direction instead.
- **Reversing the effect.** Strong pound makes exports dearer and imports cheaper.
- **One-sided globalisation.** It is both an opportunity and a threat.
- **Ignoring the business type.** A café with no trade abroad is barely affected.
- **Forgetting firms that import and export.** The effects partly cancel.
- **Linking only to sales.** The specification says profit and sales: cover both.
- **Generic design claims.** Say how the design differs and why customers value it.

## Self-test

1. Name the two ways UK businesses compete internationally in the specification.
2. Give two benefits of globalisation for a UK manufacturer.
3. Give two drawbacks of globalisation for a UK retailer.
4. What does it mean if the pound strengthens?
5. How does a stronger pound affect a UK business importing coffee beans?
6. How does a weaker pound affect a UK business exporting whisky?
7. Why might an exporter cut its pound price when the pound strengthens?
8. Why is a business that imports and exports less affected by exchange rate changes?
9. Why can design-led businesses compete even when their prices are higher?
10. What does SPICED stand for?
11. An exporter earns £50 per item, with costs of £35. To protect sales after the pound strengthens, it accepts £45. What is its new profit per item?

### Answers

1. Better designs; higher quality products at lower prices.
2. Any two: a larger market, risk spread across countries, cheaper imported components, lower unit costs from higher output, new ideas.
3. Any two: foreign rivals selling into the UK, longer supply chains, exchange rate risk, reputation risk from overseas suppliers.
4. One pound buys more foreign currency.
5. The beans cost fewer pounds, so costs fall and profit per item may rise, or prices can be lowered.
6. Whisky becomes cheaper for overseas buyers, so sales may rise.
7. To stop its price abroad rising, so that customers do not switch to rivals.
8. A stronger pound lowers its import costs while hurting its exports, and a weaker pound does the opposite, so the effects partly cancel.
9. Customers value the distinctive look or function, so they are less likely to switch to cheaper rivals.
10. Strong Pound, Imports Cheaper, Exports Dearer.
11. £45 − £35 = £10 per item, down from £15.
