These questions cover section 1.1.2, Economic assumptions, of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Economics (4EC1) specification, Issue 3, February 2026. There is a single tier of entry. All firms, people and figures are invented. ## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** State the underlying assumption economists make about the aim of businesses. **[1]**

**2.** Which of the following is a reason given in the specification for consumers not maximising their benefit?

A: consumers have unlimited wants

B: consumers sometimes copy others' behaviour

C: consumers pay indirect taxes

D: consumers prefer services to goods

**[1]**

**3.** Define profit. **[2]**

**4.** A supermarket chain pays for free bus transport to its stores for elderly customers. State which reason for not maximising profit this shows. **[1]**

**5.** Jonas has bought the same brand of coffee for ten years. A rival brand of the same quality is now 20% cheaper, but he keeps buying his usual brand. State the reason why Jonas may not be maximising his benefit. **[1]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** The table shows daily figures for a bakery chain's bread.

| Output (loaves) | Price | Total revenue | Total cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 000 | 8.00 | 8 000 | 6 000 |
| 1 500 | 7.00 | 10 500 | 7 000 |
| 2 000 | 6.00 | 12 000 | 8 000 |
| 2 500 | 5.00 | 12 500 | 9 000 |
| 3 000 | 4.00 | 12 000 | 10 000 |
| 3 500 | 3.00 | 10 500 | 11 000 |

**(a)** Calculate the profit at 2 000 loaves and at 2 500 loaves. **[2]**

**(b)** Identify the profit-maximising output and the revenue-maximising output. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the profit given up if managers choose the revenue-maximising output. **[1]**

**(d)** Identify the sales-maximising output if the bakery must not make a loss. **[1]**

**7.** A broadband company offers two 24-month contracts. Contract A costs 30 a month. Contract B costs 25 a month plus a set-up fee of 150. Many customers choose Contract B.

**(a)** Calculate the total cost of each contract over 24 months. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why customers choosing Contract B may not be maximising their benefit. **[3]**

**8.** Explain **one** reason why the managers of a large company might aim to maximise sales rather than profit. **[3]**

**9.** Explain **one** reason why consumers may copy others' behaviour, and how this may stop them maximising their benefit. **[3]**

**10.** A furniture maker donates 10 tables a month to local schools. Each table costs 120 to make and would sell for 200.

**(a)** Calculate the monthly profit the firm gives up by donating the tables instead of selling them. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain **one** way the donations might increase the firm's profit in the long run. **[2]**

**11.** Assess whether the assumption that businesses aim to maximise profit is realistic. **[6]**

**12.** Analyse why the assumption that consumers maximise their benefit helps to explain the shape of the demand curve. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Businesses aim to maximise their profit [1]. **[1]**

**2.** B [1]. **[1]**

**3.** Total revenue [1]; minus total costs [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Prioritising caring for customers [1]. **[1]**

**5.** He has a habit that is hard to give up [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** 12 000 − 8 000 = **4 000** [1]; 12 500 − 9 000 = **3 500** [1].

**(b)** Profit-maximising output: **2 000** loaves (profit 4 000) [1]; revenue-maximising output: **2 500** loaves (revenue 12 500) [1].

**(c)** 4 000 − 3 500 = **500** [1].

**(d)** **3 000** loaves (profit 2 000; at 3 500 the bakery makes a loss of 500) [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Work out profit for every row before choosing; the highest revenue row is not the most profitable.

**7. (a)** Contract A: 24 × 30 = **720** [1]; Contract B: 150 + 24 × 25 = **750** [1].

**(b)** Contract B costs 30 more for the same service [1]; customers may focus on the lower monthly price and fail to include the set-up fee [1]; so they are not good at calculating their benefits and pay more than necessary [1]. **[5]**

**8.** Managers' bonuses or pay may be linked to sales or market share [1]; so they gain personally from a bigger firm [1]; so they may cut prices and raise output beyond the profit-maximising level, accepting lower profit [1]. (Also accept: prestige of running a larger firm; separation of owners and managers.) **[3]**

**9.** Consumers may follow friends or celebrities to fit in or because it saves time researching [1]; they may buy a dearer or less suitable product than they need [1]; so they pay more, or get less satisfaction, than another choice would give [1]. **[3]**

**10. (a)** Revenue forgone 10 × 200 = 2 000, costs 10 × 120 = 1 200 [1]; profit given up = **800** a month [1].

**(b)** Good publicity improves the firm's reputation [1]; so more customers buy from it, raising future revenue and profit [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* In (a), only the profit margin of 80 per table is lost, because the cost of making the tables would be paid either way; accept 800 by either method.

**11.** Realistic: profit is needed to survive and to pay owners, so firms in competitive markets must aim for it [1]; owners (shareholders) can replace managers who make too little profit [1]. Not realistic: managers may revenue or sales maximise because their pay depends on sales [1]; firms may prioritise caring for customers [1]; or carry out charitable work [1]. Judgement: the assumption is a fair guide for small, competitive firms, but less so for large firms run by managers, and some apparent departures may raise long-run profit [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* "Assess" needs weighing; a judgement that says *when* the assumption holds scores better than a flat "yes" or "no".

**12.** If price falls, each unit of money buys more benefit [1]; consumers aiming to maximise benefit buy more of the good [1]; so quantity demanded rises as price falls, giving a downward-sloping demand curve [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Taking the highest-revenue output as the profit-maximising one.
- Omitting set-up fees or one-off costs in total-cost calculations.
- Naming a reason without explaining how it reduces benefit or profit.
- Forgetting that donations may bring long-run gains.
- Assess answers with no judgement.
