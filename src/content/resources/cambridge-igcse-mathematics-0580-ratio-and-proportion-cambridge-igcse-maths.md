This guide covers subtopic **1.11 Ratio and proportion** in Topic 1 Number of the Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics 0580 syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027. The Core (C1.11) and Extended (E1.11) wording is identical: understand and use ratio and proportion to

- give ratios in their simplest form, for example 20 : 30 : 40 in its simplest form is 2 : 3 : 4,
- divide a quantity in a given ratio,
- use proportional reasoning and ratios in context, for example adapting recipes, using map scales and determining best value.

Ratio questions appear on the non-calculator and calculator papers of both tiers. This is a different subtopic from 2.8 Proportion (Extended), which uses algebra and the symbol ∝; that is taught in the [direct and inverse proportion guide](/resources/igcse-mathematics-0580-proportion/). After this page, use the [ratio and proportion revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-mathematics-0580-ratio-and-proportion-cambridge-igcse-maths-revision-notes/) and the [ratio and proportion practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-mathematics-0580-ratio-and-proportion-cambridge-igcse-maths-practice/).

## What a ratio tells you

A ratio compares the sizes of two or more quantities. In a ratio of red to blue paint of 2 : 3:

- for every 2 parts red there are 3 parts blue,
- there are 5 parts altogether, so red is 2/5 of the mixture and blue is 3/5,
- the amount of blue is 3/2 = 1.5 times the amount of red.

The order matters. 2 : 3 for red to blue is not the same as 2 : 3 for blue to red. Always write the ratio in the order the question uses.

## Simplest form

A ratio is in its simplest form when its parts are whole numbers with no common factor other than 1. Three situations need a first step before you cancel:

| The ratio contains | First step |
|---|---|
| decimals | multiply every part by 10, 100, ... until all are whole |
| fractions | multiply every part by the lowest common denominator |
| different units | convert to the same unit, then drop the unit |

### Worked example 1

Write each ratio in its simplest form: (a) 2.4 : 0.6 : 1.8, (b) 3/4 : 2/3, (c) 450 g : 1.2 kg.

```
(a) × 10:          24 : 6 : 18
    ÷ 6 (HCF):      4 : 1 : 3

(b) × 12:          (3/4 × 12) : (2/3 × 12) = 9 : 8

(c) 1.2 kg = 1200 g
    450 : 1200
    ÷ 150 (HCF):    3 : 8
```

In (c), a ratio of 450 : 1.2 would be meaningless because the units differ. Check each answer by asking: is there any whole number that still divides every part?

Sometimes a ratio is more useful in the form **1 : n**, for example to compare two map scales or two mixtures. Divide every part by the first part: 4 : 18 = 1 : 4.5. Here n need not be a whole number.

## Dividing a quantity in a given ratio

Every sharing question is the same three steps once you know what **one part** is worth:

1. Decide which quantity you have been given: the total, one share, or a difference between shares.
2. Divide it by the matching number of parts to find one part.
3. Multiply by each share's number of parts.

### Worked example 2

(a) Share $840 in the ratio 3 : 5 : 4.

(b) The ratio of boys to girls in a club is 5 : 7. There are 18 more girls than boys. How many members are there?

(c) A bag holds red and blue counters in the ratio 3 : 4. Ten red counters are added, and the ratio becomes 1 : 1. How many counters were in the bag at first?

```
(a) Total parts = 3 + 5 + 4 = 12
    One part = 840 ÷ 12 = 70
    Shares: 3 × 70 = $210, 5 × 70 = $350, 4 × 70 = $280
    Check: 210 + 350 + 280 = 840

(b) Difference in parts = 7 - 5 = 2
    2 parts = 18, so one part = 9
    Total = 12 parts = 12 × 9 = 108 members

(c) Red = 3k and blue = 4k.
    After adding 10 red, red = blue:  3k + 10 = 4k, so k = 10
    At first: 30 red and 40 blue, 70 counters
```

In (b), dividing 18 by 12 is the usual error: the 18 is a difference, so it matches the difference in parts. In (c) the number of blue counters does not change, which is why setting up one unknown for "one part" works.

## Proportional reasoning in context

Two quantities are in direct proportion when they increase by the same factor, so their ratio stays the same. Doubling a recipe doubles every ingredient. Two methods both work:

- **Unitary method:** find the value for one unit, then multiply.
- **Scale factor method:** find the multiplier from the old quantity to the new, then apply it to everything.

### Worked example 3: adapting a recipe

A recipe for 6 people uses 240 g of flour, 4 eggs and 150 ml of milk.

(a) Find the amounts needed for 15 people.
(b) Sami has 1 kg of flour and 12 eggs, and plenty of milk. What is the largest number of people he can make the recipe for?

```
(a) Scale factor = 15 ÷ 6 = 2.5
    Flour: 240 × 2.5 = 600 g
    Eggs:  4 × 2.5 = 10
    Milk:  150 × 2.5 = 375 ml

(b) Flour: 1000 ÷ 240 = 4.166..., and 4.166... × 6 = 25 people
    Eggs:  12 ÷ 4 = 3, and 3 × 6 = 18 people
    The eggs run out first, so the largest number is 18 people.
```

Part (b) is a **limiting ingredient** problem: check every ingredient and take the smallest result.

### Worked example 4: map scales

A map has scale 1 : 25 000.

(a) Two villages are 7.4 cm apart on the map. Find the real distance in kilometres.
(b) A real road is 3.2 km long. How long is it on the map?

```
1 : 25 000 means 1 cm on the map is 25 000 cm in real life.

(a) 7.4 × 25 000 = 185 000 cm
    185 000 cm ÷ 100 = 1850 m;  1850 m ÷ 1000 = 1.85 km

(b) 3.2 km = 3200 m = 320 000 cm
    320 000 ÷ 25 000 = 12.8 cm
```

Most map-scale errors are unit errors. There are 100 000 cm in 1 km, so with this scale 4 cm on the map is 1 km.

### Worked example 5: best value

Rice is sold in two sizes: 750 g for $2.85 and 1.2 kg for $4.38. Which is better value? Show your working.

```
Cost per 100 g:
  750 g:   2.85 ÷ 7.5 = $0.38
  1.2 kg:  4.38 ÷ 12  = $0.365

The 1.2 kg bag costs less per 100 g, so it is better value.
```

You could instead find the mass per dollar (263 g and 274 g to 3 s.f.); then the **larger** value is better. Whichever comparison you choose, state it clearly and compare like with like.

## Common mistakes

- Writing the ratio in the wrong order, such as girls : boys when boys : girls was asked.
- Cancelling a ratio with different units, such as 450 g : 1.2 kg as 450 : 1.2.
- Stopping before the simplest form: 12 : 18 is not simplified until it is 2 : 3.
- Dividing a difference by the total number of parts.
- In a recipe question, forgetting to check every ingredient when finding the largest possible number.
- Converting cm to km by dividing by 1000 instead of 100 000.
- In best value, comparing a cost per gram with a mass per dollar, or not saying which option is better.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Write** a ratio in its simplest form: the final answer must have whole-number parts with no common factor.
- **Work out** or **Calculate** shares: show the value of one part. If the final shares are wrong, that line can still earn the method mark.
- **Show (that)** one option is better value: give a comparison value for each option, with units, and then a sentence naming the better one.
- **Explain**: in limiting-ingredient or "can she make enough" questions, refer to the actual numbers you found.
