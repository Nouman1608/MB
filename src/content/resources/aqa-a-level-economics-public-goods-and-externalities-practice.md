## Questions

**1.** Classify each good as a pure public good, a private good, a common resource or a quasi-public good, giving a reason. **(a)** National defence. **[1]** **(b)** A ticketed stadium concert. **[1]** **(c)** The fish stock of an unowned sea. **[1]** **(d)** A road with no toll, which is rarely congested. **[1]**

**2.** Island B has four households. Each values a sea wall at 40. Building it costs 100.

**(a)** Calculate the net social benefit of building the wall. **[2]**
**(b)** Show why no single household will build it alone. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain the free-rider problem in this context. **[2]**

**3.** A bridge has no toll and is quiet most of the day.

**(a)** Explain why it is close to a pure public good. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain two changes that would make it a quasi-public good. **[2]**

**4.** On a village common, the total milk value (in £) from c cows is 60c - 3c². Keeping a cow costs 18 a month.

**(a)** Find the number of cows when anyone can add a cow whenever the average value covers the cost. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the net gain at that number. **[1]**
**(c)** Find the number of cows that maximises the net gain to the village. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the net gain at that number. **[2]**
**(e)** Explain why herders add cows beyond that number. **[3]**

**5.** A quarry in Country C sells stone. Quantity is in thousands of tonnes and values are in £ per tonne. MPB = 52 - 4Q and MPC = 10 + 2Q. The quarry's dust imposes an external cost of 12 per tonne.

**(a)** State the output and price in the unregulated market. **[2]**
**(b)** State the output and price at the social optimum. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the external cost at the market output. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the welfare loss. **[2]**
**(e)** Explain why the market over-produces. **[2]**

**6.** Householders in a town repaint their house fronts. Quantity is in thousands of jobs and values are in £ per job. MPB = 30 - 2Q and MPC = 6 + 2Q. Neighbours gain an external benefit of 8 per job.

**(a)** Find the market quantity and price. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the socially optimal quantity and price. **[2]**
**(c)** State the under-consumption. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the welfare gain from correcting it. **[2]**
**(e)** Explain why the market under-consumes. **[1]**

**7.** State the type of externality and the curve that diverges in each case. **(a)** A firm releases fumes that harm nearby residents. **(b)** A resident plays loud music late at night. **(c)** A firm trains staff who later move to rivals. **(d)** A resident fits a security light that deters crime in the street. **[4]**

**8.** A chemical plant discharges waste into a river that nobody owns.

**(a)** Explain why the absence of property rights leads to an externality and market failure. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain how giving a single owner the rights to the river could change the plant's behaviour. **[2]**

**9.** Three households value flood sirens as follows (£, marginal benefit of siren 1 to 4). Household X: 36, 24, 12, 6. Household Y: 30, 18, 9, 3. Household Z: 24, 15, 6, 3. Each siren costs 20.

**(a)** Calculate the marginal social benefit of each siren and explain your method. **[2]**
**(b)** State the efficient number of sirens. **[1]**
**(c)** Find how many X would buy alone. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the welfare loss from under-provision. **[2]**
**(e)** Explain why Y and Z would not contribute. **[1]**

**10.** Evaluate whether the tragedy of the commons can only be avoided by giving one owner the resource. **[9]**

**11.** Assess whether technological change has reduced the importance of public goods as a cause of market failure. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** (a) Pure public good: non-rival and non-excludable [1]. (b) Private good: rival and excludable by ticket [1]. (c) Common resource: rival, as fish caught are gone, but no one can be excluded [1]. (d) Quasi-public: non-rival when uncongested and non-excludable without a toll, but congestion or tolling would change that [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A label without a reason is weak; name rivalry and excludability for each good.

**2.** (a) Total benefit 4 × 40 = 160 [1]; net = 160 - 100 = **60** [1]. (b) One household gains 40 but pays 100 [1], a net loss of 60, so none builds it alone [1]. (c) Each household benefits whether or not it pays, because no one can be excluded [1]. So each waits for another to pay, nobody does, and the wall is not built even though society would gain 60 [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote 160 against 100 rather than describing free riders in general.

**3.** (a) Quiet, so one vehicle does not reduce others' use: non-rival [1]. No toll or barrier, so non-payers cannot be stopped: non-excludable [1]. (b) Congestion at busy times makes it rival [1]. Camera-based tolling makes it excludable [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Tie each change to a characteristic; "it gets busy" alone does not say what has changed.

**4.** (a) Average value = 60 - 3c = 18, so c = **14** [2]. (b) Value = 840 - 588 = 252; cost = 14 × 18 = 252; net gain = **0** [1]. (c) Marginal value = 60 - 6c = 18, so c = **7** [2]. (d) Value = 420 - 147 = 273; cost = 7 × 18 = 126; net = 273 - 126 = **147** [2]. (e) Each herder gains the average value of an extra cow [1] but ignores the fall in milk from everyone else's cows caused by crowding [1], so the private gain exceeds the social gain until the net gain falls to zero [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Distinguish average from marginal: using average value for part (c) gives 14 again.

**5.** (a) 52 - 4Q = 10 + 2Q, so 42 = 6Q, **Q = 7** [1]; P = 52 - 28 = **24** [1]. (b) MSC = 22 + 2Q; 52 - 4Q = 22 + 2Q, so **Q = 5** [1]; P = 52 - 20 = **32** [1]. (c) 12 × 7 = **84**, i.e. £84,000 [1]. (d) At Q = 7, MSC = 36 and MPB = 24, a gap of 12 [1]; welfare loss = ½ × (7 - 5) × 12 = **12**, i.e. £12,000 [1]. (e) Producers count only MPC, ignoring the 12 of dust cost [1]. So they supply where MPB = MPC, beyond the point where MSC equals MPB [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Shade the triangle between the market and social outputs, not from zero.

**6.** (a) 30 - 2Q = 6 + 2Q, so **Q = 6** [1]; P = 18 [1]. (b) MSB = 38 - 2Q; 38 - 2Q = 6 + 2Q, so **Q = 8** [1]; P = 6 + 16 = 22 [1]. (c) 8 - 6 = **2 thousand** [1]. (d) At Q = 6, MSB = 26 and MPC = 18, a gap of 8 [1]; gain = ½ × 2 × 8 = **8**, i.e. £8,000 [1]. (e) Households ignore the benefit to neighbours, so MSB lies above MPB and consumption stops too soon [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Check that MSB lies above MPB for a positive externality; subtracting the benefit is a common slip.

**7.** (a) Negative production externality; MSC above MPC [1]. (b) Negative consumption externality; MSB below MPB [1]. (c) Positive production externality; MSC below MPC [1]. (d) Positive consumption externality; MSB above MPB [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Give both the type and the curve; one alone leaves the answer half done.

**8.** (a) Nobody owns the river, so no one has the legal power to stop the plant or charge for dumping [1]. The plant pays nothing for the damage, so the cost falls on downstream users [1]. MPC is therefore below MSC, so output is higher than the social optimum [1]. Resources are misallocated, which is market failure [1]. (b) An owner could charge or sue for damage [1], so the plant would face the cost and reduce output or waste towards the efficient level [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Finish the chain at misallocation, not at "so pollution".

**9.** (a) Add the three benefits at each siren: **90, 57, 27, 12** [1]. Public good, so sum vertically [1]. (b) Sirens 1 to 3 have MSB of at least 20; siren 4 (12) does not: **3** [1]. (c) X alone buys while own benefit is at least 20: 36 and 24 qualify, 12 does not [1], so **2** [1]. (d) The missing third siren has MSB 27 against cost 20 [1], so the loss is 27 - 20 = **7** [1]. (e) They can enjoy X's sirens without paying [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Sum across households at each quantity, not across sirens.

**10.** The tragedy occurs because no one owns the resource, so each user ignores the harm to others [1] and use rises past the efficient level, as in a fishery where entry continues until net gain is zero [1]. A single owner bears all the costs of over-use, so would limit use to the level where marginal gain equals marginal cost [1] and could charge for access [1]. But a single owner is not the only answer. Communities can agree and enforce rules among themselves [1], especially where users know each other [1]. Single ownership may be hard to apply to the atmosphere or oceans, which cross borders [1], and private owners may exclude poorer users [1]. Judgement: single ownership suits small resources with clear boundaries; for large or cross-border resources, shared rules are likely to be needed [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* A conclusion naming the conditions under which each approach works is stronger than a repeat of earlier points.

**11.** Technology has made some goods excludable: coded signals let broadcasters charge viewers [1], and camera tolling lets a road owner charge [1]. Firms can then earn revenue, so private supply becomes profitable and the free-rider problem shrinks [1]. But national defence, flood defences and street lighting remain non-excludable [1], so their under-provision remains. Technology may also weaken exclusion, as with unpaid copying of digital files [1]. Excluding people from a non-rival good wastes benefit, since the extra user costs nothing [1], so charging may reduce welfare even when it makes provision profitable [1]. Judgement: technology has moved many goods into the quasi-public group, but the core examples remain, so public goods stay an important though narrower cause of market failure [2]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* A balanced answer sets a specific example on each side, such as broadcasting against defence.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a good "public" because the government supplies it.
- Defining a free rider without saying why no payment results.
- Using average rather than marginal values to find the social optimum of a common resource.
- Adding public-good benefits across quantities instead of across users.
- Moving the wrong curve for a production externality.
- Stating that property rights are missing without explaining the link to misallocation.
- Finishing an evaluation by repeating points instead of judging.
