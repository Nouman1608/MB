These questions cover section **3.3.10.1 Bonding** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is A-level only content, examined on Paper 2 and Paper 3. Learn the ideas first in the [Bonding in benzene study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-benzene-bonding/) and the [Bonding in benzene revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-benzene-bonding-revision-notes/). Where needed, use the enthalpy of hydrogenation of cyclohexene as −120 kJ mol⁻¹ and of benzene as −208 kJ mol⁻¹.

## Questions

**1.** Describe the structure and bonding in a benzene molecule. In your answer, refer to the shape, the bond angles and the p electrons. **[4]**

**2.** The carbon–carbon bond lengths are 0.154 nm in ethane, 0.134 nm in ethene and 0.140 nm in benzene.

**(a)** State what the bond length in benzene shows about the bonds in the ring. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why the Kekulé structure of cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene would be expected to have two different bond lengths. **[2]**

**3.** State the number of sigma bonds in one molecule of benzene. **[1]**

**4.** The enthalpy of hydrogenation of cyclohexene is −120 kJ mol⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate the enthalpy of hydrogenation predicted for cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene. **[1]**

**(b)** The measured enthalpy of hydrogenation of benzene is −208 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the difference between the measured and predicted values and state what it shows. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why this difference is evidence of delocalisation. **[2]**

**5.** Both benzene and cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene react with hydrogen to form cyclohexane. Explain why the argument based on enthalpy of hydrogenation depends on both reactions giving the same product. **[2]**

**6.** The enthalpy of hydrogenation of cyclohexa-1,3-diene is −232 kJ mol⁻¹. Explain why this value is close to the value predicted from cyclohexene, whereas benzene is not. **[2]**

**7.** Explain why benzene does not decolourise bromine water but cyclohexene does. **[3]**

**8.** Explain why benzene undergoes substitution reactions in preference to addition reactions. **[3]**

**9.** A student measures the enthalpy of hydrogenation of cyclohexene as −119 kJ mol⁻¹ and of benzene as −207 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the extra stability of benzene according to these values. **[3]**

**10.** State the difference between the electron density in the C=C double bond of cyclohexene and in the ring of benzene. **[1]**

**11.** Evaluate the Kekulé model of benzene using two pieces of evidence. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Benzene is a planar hexagonal ring of six carbon atoms [1]; each carbon forms three sigma bonds, so the bond angles are 120° [1]; each carbon contributes one electron in a p orbital at right angles to the ring [1]; the p orbitals overlap sideways to form delocalised pi electron density above and below the plane of the ring [1]. **[4]**

**2. (a)** The bonds are intermediate between single and double, and all six are equal [1]

**(b)** In a Kekulé structure there are alternating single and double bonds [1]; a C=C bond is shorter than a C–C bond, so the ring would have alternating long and short bonds and would not be a regular hexagon [1]. **[3]**

**3.** 12 sigma bonds (six C–C and six C–H) [1]. **[1]**

**4. (a)** 3 × (−120) = **−360 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]

**(b)** −208 − (−360) = **+152 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]; benzene is 152 kJ mol⁻¹ more stable than the hypothetical triene (it releases less energy on hydrogenation) [1]

**(c)** If benzene had three localised C=C bonds it would release about 360 kJ mol⁻¹ [1]; the smaller release shows benzene has lower energy than expected, because the electrons are delocalised [1]. **[5]**

**5.** Because both end at the same energy [1]; so any difference in the enthalpy change must be due to a difference in the energy of the starting compounds [1]. **[2]**

**6.** The measured value (−232) is close to 2 × (−120) = −240, so there is little extra stabilisation [1]; in benzene the difference is 152 kJ mol⁻¹, showing a much larger stabilisation caused by the complete ring of delocalised electrons [1]. **[2]**

**7.** Cyclohexene has a localised C=C bond with high electron density [1]; this polarises bromine and reacts by electrophilic addition [1]; in benzene the electrons are delocalised over six carbons, so the electron density is lower and bromine is not polarised enough, and the reaction would destroy the stable ring [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Addition would break the delocalised system of benzene [1]; this would lose the extra stability of about 152 kJ mol⁻¹, which is energetically unfavourable [1]; in substitution a hydrogen atom is replaced and the delocalised ring is kept [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Predicted = 3 × (−119) = −357 kJ mol⁻¹ [1]; difference = −207 − (−357) [1] = **150 kJ mol⁻¹** more stable [1]. **[3]**

**10.** In benzene the electron density is spread around the whole ring, so it is lower between any two carbons than in the localised C=C of cyclohexene [1]. **[1]**

**11.** Evidence against: all six bond lengths in benzene are equal and intermediate between C–C and C=C [1], whereas the Kekulé structure predicts alternating bond lengths [1]; the enthalpy of hydrogenation of benzene is much less exothermic than three times that of cyclohexene [1], showing extra stability not predicted by three localised double bonds; benzene also does not react with bromine water as an alkene does [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* The model fits some facts, such as the formula C₆H₆ and the hexagonal carbon framework, but fails the three key tests: bond lengths, thermochemistry and reactivity.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Drawing alternating bonds and presenting them as the real structure.
- Not stating that the predicted value is three times the cyclohexene value.
- Giving the difference without saying what it means.
- Omitting that both compounds give the same product, cyclohexane.
- Explaining stability by "stronger bonds" rather than delocalisation.
- Stating that benzene cannot undergo addition at all.
