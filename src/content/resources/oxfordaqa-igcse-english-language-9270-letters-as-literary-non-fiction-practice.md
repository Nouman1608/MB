## The text: "Letters from the Survey Camp" (original)

A young woman, Ama, has taken a job as an assistant on a mapping survey in a remote mountain region.
She writes to her older brother, Kofi.

**Section 1: first letter**

> *Camp 2, 14 June*
>
> Dear Kofi,
>
> You said I would last a week. It has been nine days, so you owe me a new pen. The mountains are
> exactly as large as the photographs promised and twice as cold. I share a tent with a botanist who
> talks to her seedlings and snores like a lorry changing gear. The work is measuring, mostly:
> distances, heights, angles, all written into a ledger that I carry like a baby. Everyone here is
> very serious about the ledger. I am beginning to be serious about it too.
>
> Your sister, still alive,
> Ama

**Section 2: second letter**

> *Camp 4, 2 July*
>
> Kofi,
>
> We lost two days to snow. I had not known snow could be loud, but it is; it fills the tent with a
> sort of hiss, like a radio between stations. Dr Mensah has stopped making jokes. Yesterday the
> rope bridge at the river was gone, simply gone, and we walked six hours round. I carried the ledger
> inside my coat. Don't tell Mother about the bridge.
>
> A.
>
> PS. You can keep the pen.

**Section 3: third letter**

> *Base Camp, 20 August*
>
> Dear Kofi,
>
> The survey is finished. Tomorrow a jeep takes us down, and in a week I will be home and you can
> tell me I look thin. I want to tell you something I could not say in my other letters. On the day
> of the bridge, I was afraid in a way I have never been afraid, and I kept walking because the
> ledger had to reach Base Camp, and because the others were walking too. Today Dr Mensah showed me
> our map, all the measurements joined at last. My numbers are in it. Somewhere on that paper is the
> river we walked round. It looks very small.
>
> Your sister,
> Ama

## Section A — Short questions

**1.** From Section 1, list **four** things you learn about life at the camp. **[4]**

**2.** In Section 1, what does the joke about the pen suggest about Ama's relationship with Kofi?
**[2]**

**3.** Explain the effect of the simile "snores like a lorry changing gear". **[2]**

**4.** Compare the greeting and sign-off of Section 2 with those of Section 1. What do the changes
suggest? **[3]**

**5.** Explain what Ama means by the postscript "You can keep the pen". **[2]**

**6.** In Section 2, why might Ama ask Kofi not to tell their mother about the bridge? **[2]**

## Section B — Longer questions

**7.** How does Ama use language in Section 2 to present danger? **[8]**

**8.** How does Ama present her attitude to the ledger across the **whole text**? **[6]**

**9.** How is the text structured to show Ama's changing feelings? You could write about the dates,
the openings and endings of the letters, and the final section. **[8]**

**10.** In Section 3, Ama says she wants to tell Kofi "something I could not say in my other
letters". Explain why she may not have been able to say it earlier, and how this affects your
reading of Sections 1 and 2. **[4]**

**11.** A student said: "These letters are less about mountains than about a sister and brother."
To what extent do you agree? Refer to the **whole text**. **[10]**

**12.** Write **one** paragraph analysing the final two sentences of the text. **[4]**

## Answers

**How these are marked.** Short questions are point-marked as shown. For the longer questions, the
bracketed credits show one way to reach the tariff; precise analysis of methods linked to effects
and to the writer's perspective earns top credit, and other valid points of the same quality earn
the same credit.

**1.** Any four, [1] each: it is very cold; Ama shares a tent; her tent-mate is a botanist; the
botanist talks to her seedlings; the botanist snores; the work is mostly measuring; measurements are
written in a ledger; everyone takes the ledger seriously.

**2.** They tease each other and have made a bet [1], which suggests an affectionate, competitive
relationship in which Kofi doubted her and she enjoys proving him wrong [1].

**3.** The comparison is comic and exaggerated [1], turning a discomfort into a joke for her brother,
which keeps the first letter light and shows her coping through humour [1].

**4.** Section 1 opens "Dear Kofi" and closes with a joking sign-off [1]; Section 2 opens with only
"Kofi" and signs off with an initial [1], suggesting she is tired, shaken or has no energy for jokes
[1].

**5.** The bet no longer matters [1]; after the danger, proving Kofi wrong seems trivial, so the line
quietly shows how serious the experience has become [1].

**6.** To protect their mother from worry [1], which shows Ama is aware of her family's feelings and
is filtering what she tells them, and trusts Kofi with more of the truth [1].

**7.** Credit up to 8: the short opening sentence "We lost two days to snow" states hardship bluntly
[2]; the simile of a radio "between stations" makes the snow unsettling and eerie [2]; "Dr Mensah has
stopped making jokes" shows danger indirectly through a change in a leader's behaviour [2]; the
repetition "gone, simply gone" and the detail of the six-hour walk convey shock at the bridge's
disappearance and the physical cost of the detour [2].

**8.** Credit up to 6: at first the ledger is a slightly comic burden carried "like a baby", and she
notes the others' seriousness with gentle amusement [2]; in Section 2 she protects it inside her coat
during danger, showing it now matters to her [2]; in Section 3 the ledger "had to reach Base Camp",
and her numbers appear in the finished map, so it has become a source of purpose and pride [2].

**9.** Credit up to 8: the dates show weeks passing and the camps moving higher and then down,
tracing the journey [2]; the warm opening and joking sign-off of Section 1 contrast with the clipped
"Kofi" and "A." of Section 2, showing strain [2]; the postscript in Section 2 marks a turning point
in her attitude to the bet [2]; Section 3 returns to "Dear Kofi" and a full sign-off, and moves into
reflection and confession, showing she has recovered and can now tell the truth [2].

**10.** In the moment of danger she may have been too afraid, or too protective of her family, to
describe her fear [2]; knowing this makes the reader re-read the jokes of Section 1 and the brevity
of Section 2 as ways of managing or hiding feeling [2].

**11.** Credit up to 10. A judgement [2]; points supporting the view, such as the teasing bet, the
shared secret from their mother and the final confession addressed to Kofi [2]; points
qualifying it, such as the vivid presentation of cold, snow and the river, which give a strong sense
of place [2]; development showing that the mountains test the relationship, for example the clipped
second letter [2]; a conclusion linking both, such as that the landscape is what makes the sister's
honesty to her brother possible [2].

**12.** Credit up to 4: the river that cost them six hours now "looks very small" on the map [1]; the
contrast between experience and record shows how a map reduces a lived danger to a line [1]; "very
small" may also suggest Ama's fear has shrunk now that she is safe [1]; the quiet, short sentence
ending reflects a calm, thoughtful perspective that contrasts with the jokes of Section 1 [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Ignoring the conventions (greetings, sign-offs, the postscript) as evidence.
- Taking the cheerful first letter at face value.
- Using details from Section 3 for a question that names Section 2.
- In Question 11, agreeing without weighing the importance of the setting.

## Frequently asked questions

**Will the exam text always be a letter?**
No. Letters are one of the forms the specification lists, alongside autobiography, biography, memoir
and travel or adventure writing. Practise with each.
