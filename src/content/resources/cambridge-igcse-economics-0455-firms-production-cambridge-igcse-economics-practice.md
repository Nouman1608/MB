## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define productivity. **[2]**

**2.** Which change would most likely increase a firm's demand for labour?

A: a fall in demand for the firm's product

B: a fall in the price of machines that can replace workers

C: a rise in the productivity of workers

D: a shortage of workers with the right skills

**[1]**

**3.** A bakery produces 3,600 loaves a week with 12 bakers. Calculate labour productivity. **[1]**

**4.** State **two** reasons why a firm might choose labour-intensive production. **[2]**

**5.** Which of the following is a measure of productivity rather than production?

A: total tonnes of steel made in a year

B: number of cars made per worker per month

C: value of all goods sold by a firm

D: number of houses built in a country

**[1]**

**6.** State **two** influences on labour productivity. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**7.** A printing firm employs 20 workers who each produce 150 leaflets an hour.

**(a)** Calculate the firm's total output per hour. **[1]**

**(b)** Demand rises and the firm needs 3,600 leaflets an hour. Calculate how many workers it needs if productivity is unchanged. **[1]**

**(c)** New printers raise productivity to 200 leaflets per worker an hour. Calculate how many workers are now needed for 3,600 leaflets an hour, and explain what this shows about the demand for labour. **[3]**

**8.** A furniture maker can make 400 chairs a week by two methods.

- Method L: 20 workers, each paid a weekly wage W.
- Method K: machines leased for 4,500 a week plus 5 workers, each paid W.

**(a)** Calculate the weekly wage at which the two methods cost the same. **[2]**

**(b)** The weekly wage is 350. State which method is cheaper and by how much. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **one** reason why the firm might keep method L even if it is more expensive. **[2]**

**9.** The table shows invented data for a fish-processing plant.

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Output (tonnes) | 2,400 | 2,640 |
| Workers | 60 | 55 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in production. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate labour productivity in each year and its percentage change. **[3]**

**(c)** Suggest **two** reasons for the change in productivity. **[2]**

**10.** Explain why an economy with low wages and high unemployment may use more labour-intensive production. **[4]**

**11.** Analyse how training workers can increase a firm's productivity and lower its costs. **[5]**

**12.** Discuss whether a car manufacturer should replace many of its workers with robots. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Output per unit of input [1]; for example output per worker per hour [1]. **[2]**

**2.** C [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* More productive workers produce more for each unit of wage, so firms want more of them. B and D reduce demand for labour.

**3.** 3,600 / 12 = **300 loaves per baker** a week [1]. **[1]**

**4.** Any two: labour is cheap and plentiful [1]; output is small or varied [1]; the product needs personal service or craft skill; little finance for machinery; flexibility. **[2]**

**5.** B [1]. **[1]**

**6.** Any two: education and training [1]; amount and quality of capital or technology [1]; motivation; organisation and management; health; working conditions. **[2]**

**7. (a)** 20 x 150 = **3,000** leaflets an hour [1].

**(b)** 3,600 / 150 = **24** workers [1].

**(c)** 3,600 / 200 = **18** workers [1]. Higher productivity means fewer workers are needed for the same output [1]; demand for labour depends on both demand for the product and productivity, so the two changes can partly cancel out [1]. **[5]**

**8. (a)** 20W = 4,500 + 5W [1]; 15W = 4,500, so W = **300** [1].

**(b)** Method L: 20 x 350 = 7,000; method K: 4,500 + 5 x 350 = 6,250 [1]; method K is cheaper by **750** a week [1].

**(c)** Customers may want individually designed furniture that machines cannot make [1], so the firm can charge a higher price that covers the extra cost [1]. (Accept: cannot raise finance for machines; orders vary too much; machines' fixed cost is risky if sales fall, developed.) **[6]**

**9. (a)** (2,640 - 2,400) / 2,400 x 100 = **10%** [1].

**(b)** Year 1: 2,400 / 60 = **40** tonnes per worker [1]; Year 2: 2,640 / 55 = **48** tonnes per worker [1]; change = (48 - 40) / 40 x 100 = **20%** [1].

**(c)** Any two: new machinery or technology [1]; training that improved skills [1]; better organisation of tasks; improved motivation. **[6]**

**10.** With low wages, labour is cheap relative to machinery [1], so the cost per unit of producing by hand is lower [1]. With high unemployment many workers are available, so firms can hire easily [1]; the government may also encourage methods that create jobs [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Training improves workers' skills and knowledge [1], so each worker produces more output per hour [1]. With the same wage spread over more output, labour cost per unit falls [1]. Trained workers make fewer mistakes, reducing wasted materials [1]. Lower costs per unit let the firm cut prices to win sales or raise its profit [1]. **[5]**

**12.** For: robots work continuously and precisely, raising output per hour [1]; at high, steady output the cost per car falls and quality is consistent [1].
Against: robots have a high purchase and maintenance cost, which is a burden if car sales fall [1]; skilled engineers are needed to operate and repair them, and may be scarce [1]; dismissing workers may harm morale and the firm's reputation [1].
Judgement: replacement makes sense where wages are high and output is large and steady, such as on the main assembly line, but tasks needing flexibility, such as finishing custom orders, may still be done better by workers [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing total output with output per worker.
- Using the new value instead of the old as the base for a percentage change.
- Choosing a method on cost alone when the question asks for other reasons.
- Forgetting that higher productivity can reduce the number of workers needed.
- Listing advantages of robots without linking them to the scale of car output.
