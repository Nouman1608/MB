## Questions

**1.** Identify the production process in each case. **(a)** A boatyard builds one yacht to a client's design. **[1]** **(b)** A bottling plant runs one line 24 hours a day making identical bottles. **[1]** **(c)** Ashbourne Vale Pottery makes 60 identical mugs, then 40 identical bowls on the same kiln. **[1]**

**2.** Explain one way that introducing robots could affect a factory's costs. **[2]**

**3.** Dalesford Lamps checks a sample of 400 lamps and finds 18 faulty. **(a)** Calculate the percentage of the sample that is faulty. **[2]** **(b)** Dalesford sells 12,000 lamps a year. 2.5% are returned and each return costs an £18 refund. Calculate the yearly cost of refunds. **[2]**

**4.** **(a)** State what is meant by quality assurance. **[1]** **(b)** Explain one benefit to Dalesford Lamps of using quality assurance rather than only quality control. **[2]**

**5.** Kirkwhite Dental is a practice that sells a service. Analyse why quality matters to it. **[4]**

**6.** Hallbeck Cycles sells in three ways. Last month its sales were: shop (face to face) £38,400, website £52,800, telesales £8,800. **(a)** Calculate the percentage of total sales that came from the website. **[2]** **(b)** Explain one benefit to Hallbeck Cycles of selling online. **[2]** **(c)** State one disadvantage of telesales. **[1]**

**7.** Explain how product knowledge and customer engagement each contribute to good customer service in a bike shop. **[4]**

**8.** A shop sells electrical goods. Explain two ways in which consumer law affects the business. **[4]**

**9.** Westoak Foods makes frozen pies with 9 staff and is choosing a site. Site P is on an estate near a large town: rent £3,600 a month, wages £1,950 per person per month, transport £1,200. Site Q is a rural unit next to the farms that supply its ingredients: rent £2,400, wages £2,300 per person per month because staff are harder to find, transport £950. **(a)** Calculate the total monthly cost of each site and state which is cheaper. **[3]** **(b)** Recommend a site for Westoak Foods. **[6]**

**10.** Ashbourne Vale Pottery is opening a second studio and needs a new clay supplier. Identify the four parts of the procurement role. **[4]**

**11.** Hallbeck Cycles needs 800 frames. A UK supplier charges £62 per frame, delivers in 4 days and delivers on time 99% of the time. An overseas supplier charges £51 per frame, delivers in 35 days and is on time 90% of the time. **(a)** Calculate how much less the overseas order would cost. **[2]** **(b)** Evaluate which supplier Hallbeck Cycles should choose. **[6]**

**12.** Dalesford Lamps makes 300 lamps a week in batches of several designs. A flow line with robots would cost £400,000, save £60,000 a year in wages and cost £15,000 a year to maintain, but it could make only one design. Evaluate whether Dalesford should buy it. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** **(a)** Job production [1]. **(b)** Flow production [1]. **(c)** Batch production [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers usually gain credit for the exact process name, so write "job", "batch" or "flow" rather than a loose description such as "made to order".

**2.** Robots do tasks without being paid wages [1], so labour cost per unit can fall over time, though the machines cost a lot to buy and maintain [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* An "explain" answer tends to gain credit when a point is followed by its consequence, so a bare "it is cheaper" is usually too thin.

**3.** **(a)** Faulty / sample x 100 = 18 / 400 x 100 [1] = **4.5%** [1]. **(b)** Returns = 12,000 x 0.025 = 300 [1]; cost = 300 x £18 = **£5,400** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Showing the formula and each step gives a marker something to credit if there is an arithmetic slip, and the unit (% or £) belongs with each answer.

**4.** **(a)** Building quality into every stage of production to prevent faults [1]. **(b)** It stops faults happening in the first place [1], so fewer lamps are wasted or returned, which cuts costs and protects reputation [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Saying only "it checks lamps" describes quality control. A distinguishing word such as "prevent" or "every stage" shows you know which method you mean.

**5.** Poor quality, such as inaccurate work or long waits, makes patients lose confidence [1] and leave, or write bad reviews, so the practice loses income [1]. Good quality means patients return for check-ups [1] and recommend the practice, which gains new patients and builds its reputation [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* For "analyse", answers tend to gain credit when a chain of reasoning is built (quality, then customer reaction, then business result). Naming a service quality such as accuracy or courtesy keeps the answer in context.

**6.** **(a)** Total = £38,400 + £52,800 + £8,800 = £100,000 [1]; website share = 52,800 / 100,000 x 100 = **52.8%** [1]. **(b)** The website is open at any hour and reaches customers beyond the local area [1], so Hallbeck can sell more without opening more shops [1]. **(c)** Customers may find calls annoying, or cannot see the product [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Finding the total first is the step many answers skip. For part (b), "explain" asks for a benefit and a reason.

**7.** Product knowledge: staff who know the range can recommend the right bike size and answer questions [1], so customers buy suitable products and trust the shop [1]. Customer engagement: listening, replying quickly and asking for feedback [1] makes customers feel valued, so they return and recommend the shop [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Both terms need handling separately. An answer that merges them into "good staff" tends to lose the second half of the credit.

**8.** First way: the goods must be safe [1], so the shop must check or test products and risks costs or reputational damage if unsafe goods are sold [1]. Second way: goods must be of satisfactory quality [1], so faulty items may need a repair, replacement or refund, which adds cost, and ignoring this would harm its reputation [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Describing the effect on the business matters more than naming a law. Keep to general terms, such as "safe" and "satisfactory quality", that the specification itself uses.

**9.** **(a)** Site P: wages 9 x £1,950 = £17,550; total £3,600 + £17,550 + £1,200 = £22,350 [1]. Site Q: wages 9 x £2,300 = £20,700; total £2,400 + £20,700 + £950 = £24,050 [1]. P is cheaper, by £1,700 a month [1]. **(b)** I recommend Site P. P is £1,700 a month cheaper in total [1]. It is near a large town, so staff are easier to recruit and wages lower [1], and it is closer to its market [1]. Site Q is next to the farms, so ingredients arrive fresh and cheaply [1], and the rent is lower, but it needs higher wages and finding staff is harder [1]. Overall, P is better unless the freshness of local ingredients matters more than cost and labour supply [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* A recommendation tends to gain credit when it uses the figures, weighs at least one factor against the other site, and ends in a clear decision with a reason.

**10.** Identifying goods and services to buy [1]; choosing suppliers [1]; ordering goods and services [1]; receiving deliveries from suppliers [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Four separate, clearly named parts are needed. Answers that describe "buying" without separating the stages usually cover fewer than four.

**11.** **(a)** UK: 800 x £62 = £49,600; overseas: 800 x £51 = £40,800 [1]; overseas costs **£8,800** less [1]. **(b)** The overseas supplier saves £8,800 on this order, which would raise profit [1]. But 35 days against 4 days means a longer supply chain, so Hallbeck must order well ahead and hold more stock [1]. At 90% reliability, about one delivery in ten is late, which could halt production and delay customers [1], while the UK supplier at 99% protects customer service [1]. The best choice depends on how costly a delay would be, because late frames could cancel out the saving [1]. I would choose the UK supplier unless Hallbeck can hold enough stock to cover the delay [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* "Evaluate" asks you to weigh both sides and reach a supported judgement. Using the numbers given and naming the supply factors (time, reliability, costs, customer service) strengthens the answer.

**12.** Wage saving of £60,000 less maintenance of £15,000 gives a net saving of £45,000 a year [1]. Output would be steady and quality consistent [1]. But the £400,000 outlay is large compared with the yearly saving [1], and the line could make only one design, so Dalesford would lose its variety if customers want several designs [1]. Fewer workers might be needed, which could affect staff morale [1]. I would buy it only if demand for a single design is high and steady; otherwise the flexibility of batch production is worth keeping [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A judgement is expected for "evaluate". Calculating the net saving shows the numbers have been used, and a conclusion that depends on a stated condition shows reasoning.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a production process loosely instead of using job, batch or flow.
- Saying quality control prevents faults, or giving no reason why quality matters.
- Leaving out the unit (% or £), or the working, in a calculation.
- Giving a benefit with no consequence in an explain question.
- Discussing e-commerce or telesales without any link to the business in the stem.
- Naming laws instead of explaining the effect on safety, quality and reputation.
- Choosing a location or supplier on one factor, such as cost, and ignoring time, reliability and labour.
- Stopping at the comparison and not giving a final recommendation or judgement.
