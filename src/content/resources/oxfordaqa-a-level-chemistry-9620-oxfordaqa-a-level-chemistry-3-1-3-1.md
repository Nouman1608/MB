## What the specification asks for

- Ionic bonding involves electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions in a lattice.
- The formulas of compound ions, for example sulfate, hydroxide, nitrate, carbonate and ammonium.
- You should be able to predict the charge on a simple ion using the position of the element in the Periodic Table, and construct formulas for ionic compounds.

## What an ionic bond is

When a metal reacts with a non-metal, electrons are transferred from the metal atoms to the non-metal atoms. The metal atoms become positive ions (cations) and the non-metal atoms become negative ions (anions). The ionic bond is the **electrostatic attraction between these oppositely charged ions**.

Three features of the definition earn marks:

1. **Electrostatic attraction**: a force between charges, not a shared pair of electrons.
2. **Oppositely charged ions**: not atoms, and not "positive and negative atoms".
3. **In a lattice**: each ion is surrounded by ions of the opposite charge in all directions, so the attraction acts throughout the whole giant structure, not between one particular pair of ions.

The transfer of electrons explains how the ions form; the attraction between the ions is the bond itself. Answers that describe only the transfer often lose the mark for what holds the compound together.

### The lattice

In sodium chloride, each Na⁺ ion is surrounded by six Cl⁻ ions and each Cl⁻ ion by six Na⁺ ions, in a repeating cubic arrangement. There are no NaCl molecules, which is why the formula NaCl simply gives the ratio of ions and why its mass is called a relative formula mass. The structures and physical properties of ionic crystals are treated in subtopic 3.1.3.4.

### Dot-and-cross diagrams

A dot-and-cross diagram for an ionic compound shows:

- each ion separately, in square brackets, with its charge outside the bracket at the top right
- the outer shell of each ion (the metal ion usually drawn with an empty or full outer shell, as the question asks)
- transferred electrons as a different symbol from the non-metal's own electrons.

For magnesium oxide: [Mg]²⁺ and [O]²⁻, the oxide ion having eight outer electrons, two of them shown as crosses from magnesium. For calcium chloride: one [Ca]²⁺ and two separate [Cl]⁻ ions, each with eight outer electrons.

## Predicting the charge on a simple ion

Atoms of elements in the s and p blocks tend to lose or gain electrons to reach the electron configuration of the nearest noble gas.

| Group | Example | Ion | Electrons |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Na | Na⁺ | loses 1 |
| 2 | Mg | Mg²⁺ | loses 2 |
| 13 | Al | Al³⁺ | loses 3 |
| 15 | N | N³⁻ | gains 3 |
| 16 | O | O²⁻ | gains 2 |
| 17 | Cl | Cl⁻ | gains 1 |

Group 14 elements rarely form simple ions, and Group 18 elements do not form ions in ordinary compounds.

For d-block elements the charge cannot be predicted from the group number alone, because they can form ions with different charges. It is shown by a Roman numeral in the name: iron(II) is Fe²⁺, iron(III) is Fe³⁺, copper(II) is Cu²⁺. Silver forms Ag⁺ and zinc forms Zn²⁺.

## Compound ions

A compound ion (also called a molecular or polyatomic ion) is a group of covalently bonded atoms carrying an overall charge. Learn these:

| Name | Formula |
|---|---|
| Sulfate | SO₄²⁻ |
| Hydroxide | OH⁻ |
| Nitrate | NO₃⁻ |
| Carbonate | CO₃²⁻ |
| Ammonium | NH₄⁺ |

The specification lists these as examples, so other common ions such as hydrogencarbonate (HCO₃⁻) and phosphate (PO₄³⁻) may also appear, usually with their formula given.

## Constructing formulas

The total positive charge must equal the total negative charge.

1. Write each ion with its charge.
2. Find the lowest numbers of each ion that make the charges cancel.
3. Write the formula with those numbers as subscripts; put brackets around a compound ion when you need more than one of it.

### Worked example 1: three formulas

- **Aluminium oxide.** Al³⁺ and O²⁻. Lowest common multiple of 3 and 2 is 6: two Al³⁺ (+6) and three O²⁻ (−6). **Al₂O₃**.
- **Calcium nitrate.** Ca²⁺ and NO₃⁻. Two nitrate ions balance one Ca²⁺. **Ca(NO₃)₂**. Without the brackets, CaNO₃₂ would mean something different.
- **Ammonium sulfate.** NH₄⁺ and SO₄²⁻. Two ammonium ions balance one sulfate. **(NH₄)₂SO₄**.

### Worked example 2: annotated model answer

**Question.** Explain how magnesium and fluorine atoms form magnesium fluoride, and describe the bonding in the compound. (4 marks)

**Model answer.** "Each magnesium atom transfers its two outer electrons, one to each of two fluorine atoms ✔. This forms Mg²⁺ ions and F⁻ ions, both with the electron configuration of neon ✔. The formula is MgF₂, because two F⁻ ions balance the charge of one Mg²⁺ ion ✔. The compound is held together by strong electrostatic attraction between the oppositely charged ions throughout a giant lattice ✔."

**Why it scores.** It deals with formation, the ions' charges and configurations, the ratio, and the nature of the bond, each in a separate sentence.

## Common mistakes

- Describing an ionic bond as "a shared pair of electrons" or as "a transfer of electrons" alone.
- Writing "a molecule of sodium chloride".
- Drawing a dot-and-cross diagram with a line or a shared pair between the ions.
- Writing the charge inside the square bracket, or forgetting the brackets.
- Forgetting brackets around compound ions: MgOH₂ instead of Mg(OH)₂.
- Writing the charge in a formula: Na⁺Cl⁻ or Al₂³⁺O₃²⁻. Charges appear on separate ions, not in the formula of the compound.
- Confusing sulfate (SO₄²⁻) with sulfite or sulfide, or nitrate (NO₃⁻) with nitride (N³⁻).
- Predicting a charge for a d-block metal from its group number.

## Exam technique for OxfordAQA command words

- **State** or **Give**: for a charge, give the sign and number, such as 2+ or 3−.
- **Predict**: use the group number and give a brief reason, such as "Group 16, so gains two electrons".
- **Write** or **Construct** a formula: show the ions and charges if the question asks for working.
- **Draw**: for dot-and-cross diagrams, label charges and use different symbols for electrons from different atoms.
- **Explain**: link electron transfer to ion formation, then name the electrostatic attraction in the lattice.
