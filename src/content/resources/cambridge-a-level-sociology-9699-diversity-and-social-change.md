Section 3.2, *Diversity and social change*, is the second half of the theory section of Paper 2 in Cambridge International AS & A Level Sociology (9699). It asks how family and household life is changing, how varied it has become, and how theories and governments respond. The syllabus links it to the key concept *Social change and development*.

The [Paper 2 family guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-sociology-the-family/) summarises this section; this page supplies the explanations, studies and evaluation. Revise with the [3.2 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-diversity-and-social-change-revision-notes/) and practise with the [3.2 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-diversity-and-social-change-practice/).

## What the syllabus lists

- The causes and consequences of changing patterns of marriage, cohabitation, divorce and separation.
- Family and household forms: nuclear, extended, lone-parent, reconstituted and single-person households. Other forms, such as same-sex families and families of choice, may be taught where appropriate to the local context and not restricted by law or regulation.
- Dimensions of family diversity: organisational, cultural and class.
- The debate about the extent of family diversity and the dominance of the nuclear family.
- New Right and postmodernist perspectives on family diversity.
- The state and social policy as influences on the family.

Patterns differ greatly between societies, so use examples from your own society where you can, and avoid treating one country's trends as universal.

## Changing patterns of marriage, cohabitation, divorce and separation

In many societies, especially in Europe and North America, marriage has become later and less universal, cohabitation more common and divorce easier. Elsewhere, marriage remains near-universal and divorce rare. Explain causes and consequences, and say where they apply.

### Causes

| Cause | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Changing attitudes and declining stigma** | Cohabitation, divorce and births outside marriage are less condemned, so people choose them more freely |
| **Secularisation** | Where religion's influence declines, marriage loses some of its sacred meaning and divorce becomes more acceptable |
| **Women's education and employment** | Women's own income means they no longer depend on marriage for economic security and can leave unhappy relationships |
| **Legal change** | Laws making divorce cheaper or easier increase divorce; in England and Wales the Divorce Reform Act 1969 (in force from 1971) allowed divorce on the ground of "irretrievable breakdown" |
| **Rising expectations of marriage** | Fletcher argued that people now expect love and companionship from marriage, so more marriages are judged to have failed |
| **Individualisation** | Ulrich Beck and Elisabeth Beck-Gernsheim (*The Normal Chaos of Love*, 1995) argued that people now build their own biographies, so relationships last only while they meet both partners' needs |

### Consequences

- More **lone-parent** and **reconstituted** families, and more people living in **single-person households**.
- **Serial monogamy**: a series of partners over a lifetime.
- Children may move between households, with step-relatives and half-siblings.
- **Interpretations differ**: the New Right sees instability harming children and society; feminists see divorce as a sign that women can escape unequal or abusive relationships; postmodernists see a widening of choice.

## Family and household forms

A **household** is one person or a group living at the same address and sharing facilities; a **family** is a group linked by kinship, marriage or adoption. Not every household is a family.

| Form | Definition |
|---|---|
| Nuclear | Two generations: parents and their dependent children |
| Extended | Relatives beyond the nuclear family: *vertically* extended (three or more generations) or *horizontally* extended (aunts, uncles, cousins) |
| Lone-parent | One parent living with dependent children |
| Reconstituted | A family in which at least one partner has a child from a previous relationship |
| Single-person | One person living alone |
| Same-sex families and families of choice | Taught where appropriate to the local context; Jeffrey Weeks used "families of choice" for networks of friends and partners who act as family |

## Dimensions of diversity

Robert and Rhona Rapoport (1982) identified five types of family diversity; the syllabus names three of them.

- **Organisational diversity**: differences in how families are structured and run, for example joint or segregated roles, dual-earner couples, or children living between two homes.
- **Cultural diversity**: differences linked to ethnicity and religion, for example the importance of extended kin, arranged marriage or patterns of household formation among different ethnic groups.
- **Class diversity**: differences in resources and relationships, for example middle-class families' ability to pay for childcare and education, or working-class families' greater reliance on nearby kin.

The Rapoports also identified **life-course** and **cohort** diversity, which are useful in evaluation: the same person may live in several family types across their life, and generations differ in their experience.

## How diverse are families? The nuclear family debate

**The case that the nuclear family still dominates**
- Robert Chester (1985) argued that the main change is to a **neo-conventional family**: a nuclear family in which both partners work. Most people still marry or cohabit and raise children with a partner.
- A snapshot of households at one moment exaggerates diversity, because many people pass through nuclear families at some stage of life.
- The nuclear family remains an ideal that shapes advertising, policy and expectations.

**The case that diversity is now the norm**
- Judith Stacey (*Brave New Families*, 1990), studying families in California's Silicon Valley, found women creating new arrangements after divorce, including "divorce-extended families" linked by former marriages.
- Lone-parent, reconstituted and single-person households have grown in many societies.
- Weeks and others describe families of choice as a recognised form where the law allows.

**Evaluation**: the answer depends on the society and the measure used (households at one time, or experience over a lifetime). Diversity is real, but its extent is often exaggerated.

## New Right and postmodernist perspectives

| | New Right | Postmodernist |
|---|---|---|
| View of diversity | A problem: family breakdown harms children and society | A reflection of choice and freedom |
| Ideal | The married, two-parent nuclear family with a breadwinner | No single ideal; no family type is superior |
| Key arguments | Charles Murray linked lone parenthood and welfare dependency to an "underclass"; Norman Dennis and George Erdos (*Families Without Fatherhood*, 1992) argued that absent fathers leave boys without role models | Stacey: women are often the drivers of new family forms; families are now negotiated rather than fixed |
| Policy | Support marriage; reduce welfare that "rewards" lone parenthood | Recognise and support all family forms |

**Evaluating the New Right**: critics argue that poverty, not family structure, explains most disadvantage among children of lone parents; feminists see the New Right ideal as preserving women's dependence; it blames individuals for structural problems.

**Evaluating postmodernism**: critics argue it exaggerates choice, since class, income, law, religion and gender still constrain family life. Anthony Giddens (*The Transformation of Intimacy*, 1992) offers a related "late modern" view: the **pure relationship**, entered for its own sake and lasting while it satisfies both partners.

## The state and social policy

States influence family life through law, tax, welfare and population policy.

- **Population policy**: China's one-child policy, introduced around 1980 and replaced by a two-child policy announced in 2015, sharply limited family size. Romania's 1966 decree restricting abortion and contraception aimed to raise the birth rate.
- **Welfare and tax**: child benefits, parental leave and tax rules can support or discourage particular forms.
- **Family law**: rules on marriage, divorce, adoption and custody decide which relationships the state recognises.

| Perspective | View of state influence |
|---|---|
| Functionalist | Fletcher: welfare and health services support the family and help it perform its functions |
| New Right | Generous welfare undermines the two-parent family by making lone parenthood viable |
| Feminist | Policies often assume a male breadwinner and female carer, reinforcing gender inequality |
| Marxist | Policy maintains a family form that serves capitalism, such as women as a reserve of cheap labour |
| Jacques Donzelot (*The Policing of Families*, 1977) | Social workers, health visitors and doctors use knowledge to monitor and control families, especially poorer ones |

## Common mistakes

- Treating the trends of one country as worldwide.
- Confusing household with family.
- Using "nuclear family decline" as a fact without the counter-arguments of Chester and the life-course point.
- Presenting New Right views without evaluation.
- Leaving out social policy, which the syllabus names as an influence.
