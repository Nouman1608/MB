## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Neophobia | Reluctance to eat unfamiliar foods; thought to protect young children from toxins |
| Taste aversion | Learned avoidance of a food after it has been followed by illness; can form after one pairing |
| Preparedness | Biological readiness to learn some links (taste and illness) more easily than others |
| Lateral hypothalamus (LH) | Area linked to starting eating in the dual-centre model |
| Ventromedial hypothalamus (VMH) | Area linked to stopping eating (satiety) in the dual-centre model |
| Arcuate nucleus | Part of the hypothalamus that receives hormone signals about hunger and energy stores |
| Ghrelin | Hormone released mainly by the stomach when empty; increases hunger |
| Leptin | Hormone released by fat tissue; signals satiety and reduces appetite |
| Leptin resistance | The brain responds weakly to leptin despite high levels |
| Concordance | The rate at which both members of a twin pair share a condition |
| Enmeshment | Family members over-involved with weak boundaries between them |
| Autonomy | Independence; the ability to make one's own decisions |
| Modelling | Imitating the behaviour of a role model |
| Vicarious reinforcement | Learning from seeing another person rewarded |
| Cognitive distortion | Biased or inaccurate thinking, such as seeing one's body as larger than it is |
| Irrational belief | An extreme, rigid belief, such as "any weight gain means failure" |
| Restraint | A deliberate attempt to limit food intake |
| Disinhibition | Breakdown of restraint, leading to overeating |
| Boundary model | Intake is set by a hunger boundary and a satiety boundary, with a self-set diet boundary for dieters |

## Theories and studies at a glance

| Who | What | Finding | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Steiner (1979) | Newborn facial reactions to tastes | Positive to sweet, rejection to bitter | Supports innate preferences, but adults learn to like bitter foods |
| Garcia and Koelling (1966) | Rats: taste or light/sound paired with illness | Taste-illness link learned easily; light/sound-illness not | Shows preparedness; animal study, so generalise with care |
| Bernstein (1978) | Children having chemotherapy given new ice cream | Those who had it before nausea-causing treatment later avoided it | Human evidence of taste aversion; real-world application |
| Birch and Marlin (1982) | Two-year-olds given new fruits repeatedly | More exposure, more preference | Practical value for reducing neophobia |
| Birch (1980) | Preschool children eating with peers | Children shifted vegetable choices towards peers' | Supports social learning; short-term lab-style setting |
| Brown and Ogden (2004) | Parents and children's eating | Snack intake, motives and body dissatisfaction correlated | Correlation; shared genes not ruled out |
| Anand and Brobeck (1951) | LH lesions in rats | Rats stopped eating | Lesions also hit passing nerve fibres |
| Hetherington and Ranson (1940) | VMH lesions in rats | Overeating and obesity | Animal brains differ from human |
| Cummings et al. (2001) | Ghrelin before and after meals | Rises before meals, falls after | Suggests a role in starting meals |
| Wren et al. (2001) | Ghrelin given to people | Food intake increased | Experimental, so suggests cause |
| Montague et al. (1997) | Two cousins unable to make leptin | Severe obesity | Rare cases; most people with obesity have high leptin |
| Holland et al. (1988) | Twins with AN | Higher concordance in MZ than DZ | MZ concordance well below 100% |
| Watson et al. (2019) | Genome-wide study of AN | Several gene locations; psychiatric and metabolic links | Each gene adds small risk |
| Kaye et al. (2009) | Review of brain systems in AN | Serotonin and dopamine differences, even after recovery | Starvation may cause changes |
| Minuchin et al. (1978) | Clinical work with families | Enmeshment, over-protection, conflict avoidance | Observational; may blame parents |
| Bruch (1978) | Clinical account of AN | Lack of autonomy; food restriction as control | Based on case material |
| Becker et al. (2002) | Fijian girls before and after TV | Eating-attitude risk scores rose from 13% to 29% | Measured attitudes, not diagnosis |
| Garner and Bemis (1982) | Cognitive account of AN | Irrational beliefs and reasoning errors | Distortions may be effects of AN |
| Stunkard et al. (1986) | Adopted adults | Weight linked to biological, not adoptive, parents | Separates genes and upbringing |
| Frayling et al. (2007) | FTO gene variant | Two copies: about 3 kg heavier on average | Small effect; polygenic |
| Wang et al. (2001) | Brain scans and obesity | Lower dopamine D2 receptor availability | Correlational; direction unclear |
| Herman and Mack (1975) | Milkshake preload, then ice cream | Restrained eaters ate more after preload | Lab task; low ecological validity |
| Wardle and Beales (1988) | Women with obesity: diet, exercise, none | Diet group ate more in tests | Supports restraint theory |
| Herman and Polivy (1984) | Boundary model | Dieters have wider zone and a diet boundary | Hard to measure boundaries |
| Stunkard and Messick (1985) | Three-Factor Eating Questionnaire | Measures restraint, disinhibition, hunger | Self-report |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Ghrelin vs leptin.** Ghrelin: stomach, "go", rises when empty. Leptin: fat tissue, "stop", reflects energy stores.
- **LH vs VMH.** LH damage: stops eating. VMH damage: overeating.
- **Neophobia vs taste aversion.** Neophobia is avoidance of the new. Taste aversion is avoidance learned after illness.
- **Innate vs learned.** Evolution explains starting preferences; learning explains how they change.
- **Restraint vs disinhibition.** Restraint is the attempt; disinhibition is the collapse of the attempt.
- **Genetic vs neural.** Genetic: inherited risk (twins, adoption, genes). Neural: brain structures and chemicals (hypothalamus, serotonin, dopamine).
- **Explanations of AN vs explanations of obesity.** Learn them separately; AN is a diagnosed eating disorder, while obesity is a body weight category.

## Mini essay plan: "Discuss psychological explanations for obesity." [16]

1. **AO1 (6 marks).** Restraint theory: limiting intake can backfire (Herman and Mack, 1975). Disinhibition: stress, alcohol or a broken rule ends restraint. Boundary model: hunger and satiety boundaries; dieters' diet boundary; once crossed, eating continues to satiety.
2. **AO3 point 1.** Wardle and Beales (1988) support restraint theory with random allocation, which allows cause and effect.
3. **AO3 point 2.** Some people restrain successfully, so the theory over-predicts failure.
4. **AO3 point 3.** Lab taste tests may not reflect daily eating; low ecological validity.
5. **AO3 point 4.** Psychological explanations ignore genes; an interactionist view combines restraint with genetic risk such as FTO.
6. **AO3 point 5.** Practical value: helps explain why strict diets often fail.

Keep each AO3 point to: point, evidence, explanation, link back to the question.

## Quick self-test

1. What is neophobia?
2. Why is taste aversion called "prepared" learning?
3. Which hormone is released by an empty stomach?
4. Where is leptin produced?
5. What happened to rats with VMH damage?
6. Name one genetic study of anorexia nervosa.
7. Which three ideas does the specification list for family systems theory?
8. What did Becker et al. (2002) measure?
9. Give an example of an irrational belief linked to AN.
10. What did Frayling et al. (2007) find?
11. What is disinhibition?
12. In the boundary model, what happens when a dieter crosses the diet boundary?

### Answers

1. A reluctance to eat unfamiliar foods.
2. Animals are biologically ready to link taste with illness, often after one pairing and a delay.
3. Ghrelin.
4. Fat (adipose) tissue.
5. They overate and became obese.
6. Holland et al. (1988), a twin study; or Watson et al. (2019), a genome-wide study.
7. Enmeshment, autonomy and control.
8. Eating attitudes and behaviours in Fijian girls after TV arrived, not diagnosed AN.
9. "If I gain any weight I am a failure", or all-or-nothing thinking about food.
10. Adults with two copies of a higher-risk FTO variant were about 3 kg heavier on average.
11. The breakdown of restraint, so the person overeats.
12. They keep eating until they reach the satiety boundary.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Swapping ghrelin and leptin, or saying both come from the stomach.
- Writing "the hypothalamus controls hunger" without naming LH, VMH or the arcuate nucleus.
- Describing a study without stating what it shows about the explanation.
- Using Becker et al. (2002) as proof of AN cases rather than eating attitudes.
- Listing family systems terms without explaining how enmeshment leads to a struggle for autonomy and control.
- Ignoring the scenario on application questions; marks need its details.
- Evaluating with generic points ("it is reductionist") without explaining why it matters for this explanation.
- Writing about dieting advice instead of theory, or using stigmatising language.
