These questions cover strand 1 of geographical enquiry, "Suitable question for geographical enquiry", in section 3.3.2 of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Geography (9230) specification, Version 4.3, with the planning, background research and health and safety aspects that exam questions can address. For the whole unit, see the [Geographical and fieldwork skills study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-geography-9230-geographical-and-fieldwork-skills/).

> mark tariffs. All places are fictional, except where a question asks about
> your own enquiry.

## Section A

**1.** Define the term hypothesis. **[1]**

**2.** Explain the difference between an aim and a hypothesis. **[2]**

**3.** State **two** factors to consider when choosing a location for fieldwork. **[2]**

**4.** Name **two** secondary sources that could be used for background research before fieldwork. **[2]**

## Section B

Students plan an enquiry on the fictional River Calder Beck, which flows 8 km from moorland to the edge of a town.

**5.** Suggest a suitable hypothesis for their enquiry and explain why it is suitable. **[3]**

**6.** Explain the geographical theory that could underpin your hypothesis in Question 5. **[3]**

**7.** Identify **two** risks of fieldwork on the river and explain how each could be reduced. **[4]**

**8.** Explain why the students should carry out background research before collecting data. **[3]**

Another group wants to investigate the fictional town of Hollin Cross with the question: "What is Hollin Cross like?"

**9.** Explain why this is not a suitable enquiry question, and rewrite it. **[3]**

**10.** For your rewritten question, identify **two** risks of human fieldwork and explain how each could be reduced. **[4]**

**11.** Explain how primary and secondary evidence could be combined in the Hollin Cross enquiry. **[4]**

**12.** Give the title of your individual fieldwork enquiry. Explain why your chosen location was suitable for investigating this question. **[6]**

**13.** "The most important part of any enquiry is choosing the right question." Do you agree? Justify your answer with reference to the enquiry process. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** A testable statement that predicts the result of an enquiry [1]. **[1]**

**2.** An aim states in general terms what the enquiry sets out to find out [1]; a hypothesis is a specific, testable prediction that the data can support or not [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Any two: it clearly shows the feature or process studied [1]; it has enough sites to show change [1]; it is safe and legal to access [1]; it can be reached in the time available [1]; secondary data exist for it [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Any two: large-scale maps [1]; census data [1]; local authority data [1]; GIS or satellite imagery [1]; previous studies or reports [1]. **[2]**

**5.** For example: "River width increases with distance downstream along Calder Beck" [1]. Suitable because width can be measured with a tape at several sites [1], and the 8 km from moorland to town gives enough distance for change to show [1]. **[3]**

**6.** As a river flows downstream, tributaries join it, so the volume of water increases [1]. The channel becomes wider and deeper to carry more water [1], and the theory also predicts that sediment becomes smaller and more rounded through attrition, which the students could test as a sub-question [1]. **[3]**

**7.** Any two, 2 marks each: deep or fast water [1], reduced by checking river levels beforehand and working only in shallow sections [1]; slippery rocks [1], reduced by wearing suitable footwear and moving carefully [1]; water-borne infection [1], reduced by covering cuts and washing hands [1]; sudden rise in water after rain [1], reduced by checking the forecast and leaving the river if levels rise [1]. **[4]**

**8.** It helps them choose safe, accessible sites at suitable intervals, using maps [1]. It provides context, such as the river's length and the land use it flows through, which helps refine the hypothesis [1]. The specification expects background research from secondary sources "before embarking on any data collection", and sources consulted are listed in the bibliography [1]. **[3]**

**9.** It is too vague: it names no variable to measure and no theory, so it cannot be tested [1]. It has no expected pattern, so there is nothing to compare results with [1]. Rewrite, for example: "Do pedestrian numbers decrease with distance from the centre of Hollin Cross?" [1] **[3]**

**10.** Any two, 2 marks each: traffic while counting at roadside sites [1], reduced by standing on pavements away from the kerb and using crossings [1]; approaching strangers [1], reduced by working in pairs in public places and in daylight [1]; crowded pavements [1], reduced by choosing a counting point that does not block the way [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Primary evidence: the students' own pedestrian counts at sites along a route from the centre [1], which give up-to-date local detail [1]. Secondary evidence: maps showing land use or the location of shops along the route [1], which help explain why counts are higher in some places [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Levels-style indicative content:
- States a title and names the place [1].
- Explains that the location clearly showed the feature or process studied [1].
- Explains that it had enough sites for change to be measured, with detail such as distance or number of sites [1].
- Explains that it was safe and accessible, with a precaution [1].
- Notes that secondary data were available for the place [1].
- Acknowledges a limitation of the location, such as one road or one stretch of river, and its effect [1]. **[6]**

**13.** Indicative content:
- Agree: the question decides which data are collected and which theory is tested [1].
- Agree: a vague question cannot produce a clear conclusion, so later stages fail [1].
- Disagree: a good question can still be undermined by poor sampling or inaccurate methods [1].
- Disagree: analysis and evaluation decide how well the question is answered [1].
- Disagree: risk assessment matters because unsafe fieldwork may not be completed at all [1].
- Judgement: the question is the foundation of the enquiry, but each stage depends on the others; a justified view either way is credited [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Hypotheses that are not testable or name no place.
- Theory stated without the pattern it predicts.
- Risks given with "be careful" rather than a specific precaution.
- Section C answers with no title or named location.
- Combining primary and secondary data without saying what each adds.
- Extended answers that agree or disagree without weighing the enquiry stages.
