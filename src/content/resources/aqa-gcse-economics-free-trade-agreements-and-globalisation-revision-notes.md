## Part A: Free trade and free-trade agreements (3.2.4.3)

### Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Free trade | Trade between countries with no barriers imposed by governments |
| Protectionism | Using barriers to shield domestic producers from foreign competition |
| Tariff | A tax on imports, which raises their price |
| Quota | A limit on the quantity of a good that can be imported |
| Free-trade agreement | A deal between countries to remove or cut tariffs and quotas on trade between them |
| Customs union | A free-trade area whose members also charge the same tariff on imports from outside |
| Single market | A customs union that also allows free movement of services, capital and people, with shared rules on products |

Other barriers include **embargoes** (bans on trade with a country), **subsidies** to domestic producers and **regulations** such as product standards and paperwork at borders.

### Arguments for and against free trade

| For | Against |
|---|---|
| Lower prices and more choice for consumers | Jobs lost in industries that cannot compete with cheaper imports |
| Larger markets and economies of scale for firms | **Infant industries** (new ones) may need protection until they can compete |
| Competition makes firms more efficient | **Strategic industries** (food, energy, defence) may be protected to avoid relying on others |
| Countries specialise, so world output rises | Some countries may **dump** goods (sell below cost) to drive out rivals |
| Cheaper imported inputs cut firms' costs | Imports from countries with weak labour or environmental rules can undercut fair producers |

### Calculation: the effect of a tariff

A car is imported for £20,000. A 10% tariff adds 0.10 × 20,000 = **£2,000**, so it sells for **£22,000** (before any other taxes). UK-made cars at £21,000 are now cheaper than the import, so UK producers gain. Consumers pay more, and the government gains tariff revenue.

### The European Union and the UK

- The **EU** is a single market and customs union. There are no tariffs on trade between members, and one common tariff on goods from outside.
- The UK was a member from 1973 until it left on 31 January 2020. Since 1 January 2021 UK–EU trade has been under the **Trade and Cooperation Agreement**: no tariffs or quotas on goods that meet its rules on origin, but new customs checks and paperwork, which are non-tariff barriers.
- The EU remains the UK's largest trading partner, because it is a large, wealthy market close to the UK.
- Since leaving, the UK has made its own agreements, for example with Australia and New Zealand, and has joined the CPTPP, a trade group of Pacific countries.

### Significance and benefits of free-trade agreements

- Firms gain access to a much larger market with lower costs of selling there.
- Consumers get lower prices and wider choice.
- More competition and more foreign investment, as firms can serve the whole area from one base.
- Members can negotiate together, with more bargaining power.
- **Drawbacks:** members give up some control over trade policy, some industries face tougher competition, and trade may be diverted away from cheaper producers outside the agreement.

## Part B: Globalisation (3.2.4.4)

### Main features

**Globalisation** is the growing integration of the world's economies. Its main features are:

- rapid growth of international trade in goods and services
- global supply chains, with parts made in several countries
- the spread of **multinational companies** (MNCs): firms that produce in more than one country
- large flows of investment and money between countries
- movement of workers between countries, and the spread of ideas and culture.

### Factors behind the growth of globalisation

- **New technology:** the internet, mobile communications and computers let firms manage operations worldwide and sell online.
- **Cheaper transport:** container shipping and air freight have cut the cost of moving goods.
- **Multinational companies** looking for new markets, cheaper labour and resources.
- **Fewer trade barriers:** trade agreements and the World Trade Organization (WTO).
- **Freer movement of capital**, so firms can invest abroad easily.
- **Growth of emerging economies** such as China (which joined the WTO in 2001) and India.

### Multinational companies

| Possible benefits to a host country | Possible drawbacks |
|---|---|
| Jobs and training | Profits may be sent back to the home country |
| Investment and new technology | Local firms may be driven out |
| Exports and tax revenue | May pay low wages or harm the environment in poorer countries |
| Spending with local suppliers | May close plants and move if costs are lower elsewhere |

**UK example.** Nissan has built cars in Sunderland since 1986, creating thousands of jobs in the plant and among local suppliers.

### Benefits and drawbacks for the UK and other developed countries

| Group | Benefits | Drawbacks |
|---|---|---|
| Producers | Larger markets, cheaper inputs, economies of scale | More competition from low-cost producers abroad |
| Workers | Jobs in exporting firms and in MNCs locating here | Jobs lost when production moves abroad; pressure on wages in some industries |
| Consumers | Lower prices and more choice | Products may be made under poor conditions; less local variety |

### Benefits and drawbacks for less developed countries

| Group | Benefits | Drawbacks |
|---|---|---|
| Producers | Access to world markets; new technology from MNCs | Local firms may not survive competition; reliance on a few exports |
| Workers | New jobs, often paying more than local alternatives; skills | Low pay, long hours and unsafe conditions in some factories |
| Consumers | More goods and lower prices; incomes rise over time | Gains may be uneven; local culture may be lost |

### Moral, ethical and sustainability considerations

- **Working conditions:** low wages, unsafe factories or child labour in supply chains. Is cheap clothing worth it?
- **Environment:** pollution in countries with weaker rules, deforestation, and carbon emissions from transporting goods long distances.
- **Fair trade:** schemes that guarantee producers a minimum price; some consumers will pay more for ethical products.
- **Responsibility:** UK firms are expected to check their supply chains, and there is pressure from consumers and campaign groups.

### Exam traps

- Free trade and globalisation are linked but different: one removes barriers, the other is the wider process of integration.
- Name the **group** (producers, workers or consumers) and the **country type** (UK or less developed) in every point.
- The UK has left the EU, but the specification still expects you to know the EU's significance and the benefits of agreements like it.
- A tariff makes imports dearer for consumers; it does not make domestic goods cheaper.

## Quick self-test

1. Define free trade.
2. Distinguish between a tariff and a quota.
3. Give two arguments against free trade.
4. A £500 import faces a 20% tariff. What is its new price?
5. What is the difference between a free-trade area and a customs union?
6. When did UK–EU trade move to the Trade and Cooperation Agreement?
7. Give two factors that have contributed to the growth of globalisation.
8. What is a multinational company?
9. Give one benefit and one drawback of globalisation for UK workers.
10. Give two ethical concerns when UK firms buy from abroad.

### Answers

1. Trade between countries without government-imposed barriers such as tariffs and quotas.
2. A tariff is a tax on imports; a quota is a limit on the quantity imported.
3. Any two: job losses in uncompetitive industries, infant industries need protection, strategic industries, dumping, competition from countries with weak standards.
4. 0.20 × 500 = 100, so £600.
5. In a customs union members also share a common tariff on imports from outside; in a free-trade area each sets its own.
6. 1 January 2021.
7. Any two: new technology, cheaper transport, multinational companies, fewer trade barriers, freer movement of capital, growth of emerging economies.
8. A firm with production or operations in more than one country.
9. Benefit: jobs in exporting firms or MNCs. Drawback: jobs lost when production moves abroad.
10. Any two: low pay, unsafe conditions, child labour, environmental damage, emissions from transport.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Listing benefits of globalisation without saying for whom.
- Treating the EU as only a free-trade area: it is a single market and customs union.
- Ignoring the drawbacks for less developed countries, or only giving drawbacks.
- Ethical points with no economic link (cost, price, jobs or the environment).

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between a free-trade area, a customs union and a single market?**
A free-trade area removes tariffs and quotas between members. A customs union also charges the same tariff on imports from outside. A single market adds free movement of services, capital and people.

**What is the difference between a tariff and a quota?**
A tariff is a tax on imports, which raises their price. A quota is a limit on the quantity of a good that can be imported.

**What factors have caused globalisation to grow?**
New technology, cheaper transport, multinational companies seeking new markets and cheaper inputs, fewer trade barriers, freer movement of capital and the growth of emerging economies.

**Does the UK still need to know about the EU for AQA GCSE Economics?**
Yes. The UK left the EU, but the 8136 specification still expects you to know the EU's significance as the UK's largest trading partner and as a single market and customs union.

**Does a tariff make domestic goods cheaper?**
No. A tariff makes imports dearer for consumers; it does not lower the price of domestic goods.
