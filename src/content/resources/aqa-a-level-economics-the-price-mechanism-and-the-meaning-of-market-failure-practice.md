## Questions

**1.** Define market failure and give one example. **[3]**

**2.** In Country C the market for flower bunches has Qd = 840 − 40P and Qs = 60P − 160, with P in pounds.

**(a)** Calculate the equilibrium price and quantity. **[2]**

**(b)** Demand rises to Qd = 940 − 40P. Calculate the shortage at the old price. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain how the three functions of price remove the shortage. **[3]**

**3.** Explain the difference between complete and partial market failure, with one example of each. **[4]**

**4.** A firm can make its output with method A (6 workers, 2 machines) or method B (2 workers, 4 machines) per day. A machine costs £150 per day.

**(a)** Calculate the daily cost of each method when the wage is £100. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the costs when the wage falls to £40 and state which method is chosen. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain what this shows about how goods are produced in a market economy. **[1]**

**5.** Assess the view that the price mechanism is an impersonal method of allocating resources. **[6]**

**6.** A library in Country D charges a fee of £2 per loan. Monthly loans fall from 50,000 to 31,000.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage fall in loans. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the monthly fee revenue. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain how introducing the fee could change the nature of the activity. **[3]**

**7.** Three neighbours would pay £9, £7 and £5 a month for a community alarm. It costs £18 a month. Explain why this is a missing market, using the figures. **[5]**

**8.** A market has marginal private benefit 30 − 0.1Q and marginal private cost 6 + 0.1Q. Each unit imposes an external cost of £4. Calculate the market output, the socially optimal output and the welfare loss. **[6]**

**9.** For each scenario, name the cause of market failure and say whether it is complete or partial. **[4]**

**(i)** A sole supplier restricts output and raises price.

**(ii)** Nobody can be stopped from using a flood barrier, so no firm will build one.

**(iii)** Too few people take up a health screening whose benefits they undervalue.

**(iv)** Households on the lowest incomes cannot afford an essential service that is priced at cost.

**10.** A service costs £18. Household X has a monthly income of £900 and household Y has £300. Calculate the price as a percentage of each income and explain whether the outcome is market failure. **[4]**

**11.** Evaluate whether charging a market price for entry to a public park would improve resource allocation. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Market failure occurs when a market leads to a misallocation of resources [1], so that output or price differs from the socially best level [1]. Example: a factory's smoke harms neighbours who are not compensated, so too much is produced [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition earns most when it uses the idea of misallocation, not just "the market does not work".

**2. (a)** Set demand equal to supply: 840 − 40P = 60P − 160 [1]. So 100P = 1,000, giving **P = £10 and Q = 440** [1].
**(b)** At £10, Qd = 940 − 400 = 540 and Qs = 440 [1]. The shortage is **100 bunches** [1].
**(c)** Signalling: the shortage lets sellers raise price, telling everyone demand has risen [1]. Rationing: the higher price (£11) cuts quantity demanded as marginal buyers drop out [1]. Incentive: higher price and profit raise quantity supplied to 500 until the market clears [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the substitution into the old price; a bare "100" gives nothing to build on if the arithmetic slips.

**3.** Complete failure: the market does not exist [1], for example a public good such as street lighting [1]. Partial failure: a market exists [1] but produces the wrong quantity or price, for example tobacco being over-consumed [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each type needs its own example; a pair of definitions alone usually leaves marks unclaimed.

**4. (a)** A: 6 × 100 + 2 × 150 = **£900** [1]. B: 2 × 100 + 4 × 150 = **£800** [1].
**(b)** A: 6 × 40 + 300 = £540 and B: 2 × 40 + 600 = £680 [1]. Method A is now chosen [1].
**(c)** Firms choose the cheapest method at current relative input prices, without being told [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Cost both methods before choosing one; a conclusion with only one total shown cannot be checked.

**5.** For: the price mechanism treats every buyer alike, needs no official judging need and avoids favouritism [1]; decisions respond quickly [1]. Against: it allocates by ability to pay, so wealthy buyers win over those in greater need [1]; a price reflects willingness, not need [1]. Judgement: the view is strongest for goods with no substitutes, and weaker where incomes are similar [1]; whether this matters is a normative choice [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A one-sided answer, whichever side, tends to stall; weigh both and make a supported judgement.

**6. (a)** Change = 31,000 − 50,000 = −19,000 [1]. Percentage = −19,000 ÷ 50,000 × 100 = **−38%** [1].
**(b)** 31,000 × £2 = **£62,000** [1].
**(c)** Borrowing was once a shared service open to all, and a price turns it into a purchase [1]. Lower-income readers may be excluded, which changes who uses it [1]. Staff and users may think of it as a commercial transaction rather than a public service, so attitudes and incentives change [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the change to incentives or the meaning of the activity; stating only that demand falls does not address the nature of the transaction.

**7.** Total willingness to pay = 9 + 7 + 5 = £21 [1]. Cost is £18, so net benefit is 21 − 18 = **£3** [1]. Provision is worthwhile [1], but no one can be excluded, so each neighbour has an incentive to free ride [1]. A private firm cannot collect revenue, so no market forms. That is a missing market, a complete failure [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the given figures to show that benefit exceeds cost; a general statement about public goods does not use the data.

**8.** Market: 30 − 0.1Q = 6 + 0.1Q [1], so Q = 120 [1]. Social: marginal social cost = 10 + 0.1Q, so 30 − 0.1Q = 10 + 0.1Q [1], giving Q = 100 [1]. Welfare loss = ½ × £4 × (120 − 100) [1] = **£40** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Add the external cost to the cost curve before solving, and state the unit for the welfare loss.

**9.** (i) Monopoly or other imperfection, partial [1]. (ii) Public good, complete (missing market) [1]. (iii) Merit good, partial [1]. (iv) Inequality in the distribution of income, partial [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Give both the cause and the type; naming only one of them leaves the other point unanswered.

**10.** X: 18 ÷ 900 × 100 = **2%** [1]. Y: 18 ÷ 300 × 100 = **6%** [1]. The market allocates at cost, so on narrow efficiency grounds there may be no misallocation [1]. However, allocation follows income, not need, and that is the inequality cause of failure if society judges the outcome unacceptable [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Separate efficiency from fairness; unfairness alone does not prove misallocation without a reasoned link.

**11.** For: a price rations scarce space, reduces overcrowding [1] and raises revenue to fund upkeep [1], giving a signal of what visitors value [1]. Against: exclusion of low-income users, since parks give benefits to the wider community [1]; the park may lose its character as a shared space [1]; collecting fees costs money [1]. Judgement: it depends on how congested the park is, how large the external benefits are and whether low-income users can be helped, for example by free days [1]; the answer should commit to a supported conclusion [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* A conclusion that names the conditions on which the answer depends reads as a judgement; a repeated list of points does not.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining market failure as "when markets do not work" with no mention of misallocation.
- Forgetting to test the old price when finding a shortage or surplus.
- Naming a function of price but not linking it to the buyers or sellers affected.
- Treating "impersonal" as wholly good or wholly bad.
- Giving a type of failure with no example.
- Confusing complete failure with a market that is small.
- Solving for output without adding the external cost to marginal private cost.
- Leaving out units, such as £ or bunches, on a calculation.
