Condensed recall for specification section **3.1.2 Section B: Directed writing** of OxfordAQA International AS and
A-level English Language (9670). For the full planning method and a worked example, use the
[Unit 1 Directed Writing study guide](/resources/a-level-oxfordaqa-english-language-unit-1-directed-writing/).

## The task in brief

- Section B is worth 25 of Unit 1's 50 marks.
- **One writing task from a choice of two.** The two options offer different uses of the source material.
- You write to a **specific brief**, transforming **some or all** of the Section A material into a **new text**.
- You are credited for **sustaining a style appropriate to the task chosen**.
- No commentary: your understanding is shown in the writing itself.
- AO3, using English in different ways, carries the largest weighting in Unit 1.

## Decode the brief: four questions

| Question | Note it as | It decides |
|---|---|---|
| Who will read or hear this? | Audience: age, knowledge, relationship | Register, vocabulary, assumed knowledge |
| What must it do? | Purpose: inform, persuade, advise, entertain, or a mix | Structure and sentence functions |
| What kind of text is it? | Genre | Conventions the reader expects |
| Spoken, written or hybrid? | Mode | Sentence length, cohesion, layout, address |

Also note **where** it will appear (a school website, a local radio slot) and **who** is speaking (the persona).

## Transformation levers

- **Select**: keep only material that serves the new purpose.
- **Reorder**: lead with what the new reader needs most.
- **Re-register**: adjust formality and tenor, e.g. a technical report becomes plain advice.
- **Re-voice**: new persona, new pronouns ("we" for an organisation; "I" for a personal blog).
- **Re-mode**: written to spoken needs signposting, repetition and direct address; spoken to written needs complete
  sentences and explicit reference instead of deixis.
- **Re-genre**: add the target form's conventions and remove the source's.

## Genre conventions bank

| Form | Conventions to include |
|---|---|
| Speech or talk | Greeting, signposting ("First...", "Finally..."), direct address, rhetorical questions, repetition, a memorable close |
| Podcast or radio script | Introduction of speaker and topic, conversational discourse markers, short sentences, sign-off |
| Blog post | Title, personal anecdote, informal tenor, subheadings, an invitation to comment |
| Feature article | Headline, standfirst, lively opening, quotation, balanced development, closing reflection |
| Leaflet | Heading, subheadings, bullet points, imperatives, contact or next-step information |
| Formal letter or email | Salutation, purpose stated early, paragraphed points, polite close |
| Review | Context, criteria, evidence, verdict |
| Diary or journal | Date, first person, reflective present concerns, informal ellipsis |

## Sustaining a style

- Fix **two or three style markers** before writing (e.g. second-person address, short sentences, light humour) and
  check each paragraph uses them.
- Keep the **persona** consistent: a teenage blogger does not suddenly use bureaucratic lexis.
- Keep **tense and person** stable unless a shift is deliberate.
- Watch the **ending**: many responses drift back into essay register in the last paragraph.
- Keep cohesion appropriate to mode: spoken texts use signposts; written texts can use paragraph structure.

## Before-and-after transformations

| Source (written, formal) | Target (spoken talk to 12-year-olds) |
|---|---|
| "Participation in regular physical activity is associated with improved concentration." | "Here's something cool: when you move more, your brain actually gets better at focusing." |
| "Visitors are requested to refrain from touching the exhibits." | "One rule today: look with your eyes, not your hands." |

| Source (spoken transcript) | Target (written article) |
|---|---|
| "so we just (.) we sort of started it in the garage really" | "The business began, modestly, in a garage." |

## Choosing between the two tasks

- Pick the task whose **reader you can picture clearly**.
- Check the source material gives you **enough content** for that task.
- Prefer the task where you know the **genre conventions** well enough to sustain them throughout.

## Exam traps

- Writing an analysis or summary instead of a new text.
- Lifting phrases or structure from the sources.
- A "speech" that reads like an essay; a "leaflet" without headings.
- Register that starts right and drifts.
- Including every source detail instead of selecting.
- Adding a commentary that the task does not ask for.
- Ignoring where the text will appear.

## Self-test

1. How many Section B tasks are there, and how many do you answer?
2. What must the writing be based on?
3. What does the specification say you are credited for?
4. Is a commentary required?
5. Name the four context headings you should decode in the brief.
6. Give three conventions of a speech.
7. What must change when you turn a spoken transcript into a written article?
8. What is re-voicing?
9. Give two ways to keep a style consistent.
10. Transform for a leaflet: *"It is strongly recommended that residents sort their recycling prior to collection."*
11. Why should you not use every detail from the sources?

**Answers:** 1. Two; you answer one. 2. A transformation of some or all of the material in Section A. 3. The ability
to sustain a style of writing appropriate to the task chosen. 4. No. 5. Audience, purpose, genre and mode. 6. Any
three: greeting, signposting, direct address, rhetorical questions, repetition, a memorable close. 7. Spoken features
such as fillers, repairs and deixis are replaced by complete sentences, explicit reference and written conventions
such as paragraphs and a headline. 8. Giving the text a new persona and point of view, with pronouns and tone to
match. 9. Any two: fix style markers in advance and check each paragraph; keep persona, tense and person stable;
check the ending does not drift into essay register. 10. For example: "Sort your recycling before collection day."
11. Because selection for the new purpose is part of transformation; irrelevant detail weakens the new text.
