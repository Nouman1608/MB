## Crude oil (4.7)

**Crude oil** is a thick, dark liquid found underground. It was formed over millions of years from the remains of tiny sea organisms buried under mud and heated under pressure. It is a **mixture of hydrocarbons**, mostly alkanes, with chains of many different lengths. Because it is a mixture, the hydrocarbons are not chemically bonded together, and each keeps its own properties. They can therefore be separated by a physical method that uses their different boiling points. Crude oil is a finite resource and cannot be replaced on a human timescale.

## Fractional distillation (4.8)

Fractional distillation separates crude oil into **fractions**. Each fraction is a mixture of hydrocarbons with similar chain lengths and a similar range of boiling points.

1. Crude oil is heated in a furnace until most of it vaporises. The hot vapour and liquid enter the bottom of a tall **fractionating column**.
2. The column is **hot at the bottom and cooler at the top**.
3. The vapours rise up the column. Each hydrocarbon **condenses** at the level where the temperature falls below its boiling point.
4. Hydrocarbons with **long chains and high boiling points** condense near the bottom. Those with **short chains and low boiling points** rise to the top before condensing.
5. The liquid fractions are drawn off at different heights. The very lightest fraction leaves the top as a gas.

A good explanation says why the molecules separate: "larger molecules have stronger forces between them, so a higher boiling point, so they condense lower down".

## The main fractions and their uses (4.9)

| Fraction | Approximate chain length | Main use |
|---|---|---|
| Refinery gases | 1 to 4 carbons | Heating and cooking (bottled gas); fuel |
| Gasoline | about 5 to 10 | Petrol for cars |
| Kerosene | about 10 to 16 | Fuel for aircraft |
| Diesel | about 14 to 20 | Fuel for lorries, buses, trains and some cars |
| Fuel oil | about 20 to 30 | Fuel for ships and power stations |
| Bitumen | over about 35 | Surfacing roads and roofing |

The chain lengths are approximate guides and are not required by the specification. The order of the fractions and their uses are.

## Trends in the fractions (4.10)

Moving from refinery gases (top) to bitumen (bottom):

| Property | Trend | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Boiling point | Increases | Longer molecules have stronger intermolecular forces |
| Viscosity (thickness) | Increases | Longer molecules tangle more and flow less easily |
| Colour | Gets darker (from colourless or pale yellow to black) | Larger molecules, more impurities |
| Flammability (ease of ignition) | Decreases | Fewer molecules vaporise to mix with air |

Always state the direction of the trend and where it starts and ends, for example "boiling point increases as the carbon chain gets longer, going down the column".

## Fuels and combustion (4.11 and 4.12)

A **fuel** is a substance that releases heat energy when it is burned. Hydrocarbons are good fuels.

**Complete combustion** happens with plenty of oxygen. The products are **carbon dioxide and water**. For example, methane: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O.

**Incomplete combustion** happens when there is not enough oxygen. The products are **carbon monoxide and/or carbon (soot)**, and water. Example: 2CH₄ + 3O₂ → 2CO + 4H₂O. The flame is yellow and sooty, and less heat is released.

**Worked example 1: complete combustion of octane.** Petrol contains octane, C₈H₁₈ (Mr = 114): 2C₈H₁₈ + 25O₂ → 16CO₂ + 18H₂O. Find the mass of carbon dioxide made from 1.00 kg of octane.

1. Moles of octane = 1,000 / 114 = 8.77 mol.
2. From the equation, 2 mol of octane give 16 mol of CO₂, so 1 mol gives 8 mol. Moles of CO₂ = 8 x 8.77 = 70.2 mol.
3. Mass of CO₂ = 70.2 x 44 = 3,088 g = **3.09 kg**.

Burning 1 kg of petrol releases more than 3 kg of carbon dioxide, because each carbon atom gains two oxygen atoms from the air. The mass of the products is greater than the mass of the fuel.

## Carbon monoxide (4.13)

Carbon monoxide is a **colourless, odourless** gas, so it cannot be detected without an alarm. It is **poisonous** because it combines with the red blood cells in place of oxygen. This reduces the capacity of the blood to carry oxygen around the body. Tissues, including the brain and heart, are starved of oxygen, causing headaches, dizziness, unconsciousness and, in severe cases, death. (References to haemoglobin are not required.) Faulty gas heaters and car engines in closed garages are common sources.

## Oxides of nitrogen (4.14)

Air is about 78% nitrogen and 21% oxygen. These gases do not react at normal temperatures. In a **car engine**, the temperature is high enough for nitrogen and oxygen from the air to react, forming **oxides of nitrogen**: N₂ + O₂ → 2NO, and the nitrogen monoxide can be oxidised further to nitrogen dioxide, NO₂. The nitrogen comes from the air, not from the fuel.

## Sulfur dioxide (4.15)

Crude oil contains sulfur compounds as **impurities**, and these remain in some fuels. When the fuel burns, the sulfur is oxidised to **sulfur dioxide**: S + O₂ → SO₂.

**Worked example 2.** 1,000 kg of fuel oil contains 2.0% sulfur.

- Mass of sulfur = 0.020 x 1,000 = 20 kg.
- S (Ar = 32) gives SO₂ (Mr = 64) in a 1 : 1 mole ratio, so 20 kg of sulfur gives 20 x 64 / 32 = **40 kg** of sulfur dioxide.

Removing sulfur from fuels before burning, or removing sulfur dioxide from power station gases, reduces the amount released.

## Acid rain (4.16)

Sulfur dioxide and oxides of nitrogen dissolve in water in the clouds and fall as **acid rain**. Sulfur dioxide forms sulfurous acid and then sulfuric acid; nitrogen dioxide forms nitric acid. Effects: lakes and rivers become acidic, harming fish; trees are damaged; limestone and marble buildings and statues are eroded (the carbonate reacts with acid); and metal structures corrode faster. Ways to reduce it: remove sulfur from fuels, use flue gas desulfurisation, use catalytic converters in cars (which convert oxides of nitrogen into nitrogen) and burn less fossil fuel.

## Cracking (4.17 and 4.18)

**Cracking** breaks long-chain hydrocarbons into shorter, more useful molecules. In **catalytic cracking** the long-chain alkane vapour is passed over a catalyst of **silica or alumina** at a temperature of **600 to 700 °C**. The products are a mixture of **shorter-chain alkanes and alkenes**.

Example equations (each is balanced; check the atoms on both sides):

- C₁₀H₂₂ → C₈H₁₈ + C₂H₄ (decane gives octane and ethene)
- C₁₂H₂₆ → C₈H₁₈ + 2C₂H₄

**Worked example 3.** For C₁₀H₂₂ → C₈H₁₈ + C₂H₄: Mr (decane) = 142; Mr (octane) = 114; Mr (ethene) = 28. Check: 114 + 28 = 142. Mass is conserved, so 1.42 kg of decane gives 1.14 kg of octane and 0.28 kg of ethene if reaction is complete.

**Why cracking is necessary (supply and demand).** Crude oil contains more long-chain hydrocarbons than the market needs and fewer short-chain ones. Demand for gasoline, which has shorter molecules, is much higher than the supply from distillation alone. Cracking converts surplus long chains into more gasoline and into alkenes, which are in demand as raw materials for making plastics (polymers) and other chemicals.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that crude oil is a compound. It is a mixture.
- Stating that fractions are pure substances. Each is a mixture of similar hydrocarbons.
- Saying that the compounds at the top have the highest boiling point.
- Writing that a hydrocarbon "burns in air to give carbon dioxide" without water.
- Claiming that carbon monoxide "reacts with oxygen in the blood". It stops the blood carrying oxygen.
- Saying the nitrogen in oxides of nitrogen comes from the fuel.
- Forgetting the catalyst or temperature range in cracking.
- Balancing a cracking equation incorrectly. Count carbon and hydrogen on each side.

## Exam technique

When asked to explain fractional distillation, include the temperature gradient, the condensing at different heights and the link between chain length and boiling point. For combustion equations, balance carbon first, then hydrogen, then oxygen. For acid rain questions, name the gas, the source and the effect. For cracking questions, give conditions (silica or alumina, 600 to 700 °C) and a balanced equation, and link the purpose to supply and demand.
