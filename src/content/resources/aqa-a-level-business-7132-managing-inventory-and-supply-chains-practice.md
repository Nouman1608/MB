These questions cover section 3.4.5 Making operational decisions to improve performance: managing inventory and supply chains of the AQA A-level Business (7132) specification. All businesses and figures are invented. Revise first with the [inventory and supply chains study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-managing-inventory-and-supply-chains/) and the [inventory and supply chains revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-managing-inventory-and-supply-chains-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** A business uses 40 units of a part each day. The lead time is 5 days and the buffer inventory is 60 units. What is the re-order level? **[1]**

A: 100 units

B: 200 units

C: 260 units

D: 300 units

**2.** Which **one** of the following best describes buffer inventory? **[1]**

A: The largest amount of stock the business can store

B: The amount ordered each time

C: The minimum stock held as a safety margin

D: The stock at the start of the year

**3.** Define the term "re-order level". **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** advantage to a business of producing to order. **[3]**

**5.** Greaves Garden Centre sells compost. It sells 30 bags a day, the lead time is 3 days, the buffer is 50 bags and the re-order quantity is 240 bags.

**(a)** Calculate the re-order level. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the maximum inventory. **[1]**

**(c)** The supplier's lead time increases to 5 days. Calculate what would happen to stock when the delivery arrives, assuming orders are still placed at the original re-order level. **[2]**

**6.** A workshop uses 12,000 units of a part a year. Each order costs £40 to place. Holding one unit in average inventory costs £2 a year. Buffer inventory is 100 units. Average inventory is the re-order quantity ÷ 2 plus the buffer.

**(a)** Calculate the total annual ordering and holding cost if the re-order quantity is 1,000 units. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the total annual ordering and holding cost if the re-order quantity is 2,000 units. **[2]**

**(c)** State which re-order quantity the workshop should prefer, and why. **[1]**

**7.** Explain **one** risk of a business relying on a single supplier. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**8.** Analyse **two** benefits to a small online retailer of outsourcing its delivery service. **[6]**

**9.** Alpine Rise is a ski resort with very busy winters and a quiet summer. Assess whether employing temporary and part-time staff is the best way for the resort to match supply to demand. **[9]**

**10.** Analyse the factors a supermarket chain should consider when choosing suppliers of fresh fruit. **[6]**

**11.** Explain **two** difficulties of managing a supply chain that stretches across several countries. **[4]**

**12.** A car manufacturer is deciding whether to hold large buffer inventories of components. Evaluate whether it should do so. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** **C** [1]. (40 × 5) + 60 = 260 units. **[1]**

**2.** **C** [1]. Buffer inventory is the minimum stock kept as protection against late delivery or unexpected demand. **[1]**

**3.** The level of stock at which a new order is placed [1], calculated as usage during the lead time plus the buffer inventory [1]. **[2]**

**4.** There is no unsold finished stock, so storage costs and the risk of goods becoming obsolete are avoided [1]. Cash is not tied up in stock, so the business has more money for other uses [1]. Products can also be tailored to each customer's wishes, which may allow a higher price [1]. **[3]**

**5. (a)** Usage during lead time = 30 × 3 = 90 bags [1]. Re-order level = 90 + 50 = **140 bags** [1].

**(b)** Maximum inventory = buffer + re-order quantity = 50 + 240 = **290 bags** [1].

**(c)** Usage during 5 days = 30 × 5 = 150 bags [1]. Stock would be 140 − 150 = **−10**, so the buffer is used up and the centre would run out about a third of a day before the delivery arrives, leaving 10 bags of unmet demand [1]. **[5]**

**6. (a)** Orders = 12,000 ÷ 1,000 = 12; ordering cost = 12 × £40 = £480 [1]. Average inventory = 500 + 100 = 600; holding cost = 600 × £2 = £1,200; total = **£1,680** [1].

**(b)** Orders = 6; ordering cost = £240. Average inventory = 1,000 + 100 = 1,100; holding cost = £2,200 [1]; total = **£2,440** [1].

**(c)** 1,000 units, because the total cost is lower (£1,680 against £2,440), the saving on ordering being outweighed by higher holding costs [1]. **[5]**

**7.** If the single supplier fails, for example through a strike or financial collapse, the business has no alternative source [1], so production could stop and customers be let down [1]. Accept: a single supplier may use its bargaining power to raise prices. **[2]**

**8.** Benefit 1: lower fixed costs and no need for investment [1]. A small retailer does not need to buy vans or employ drivers [1], so costs vary with the number of orders, and cash is saved for stock and marketing [1]. Benefit 2: access to expertise and flexibility [1]. A courier firm has routes, tracking systems and spare capacity [1], so the retailer can cope with peak periods and give customers tracking information without building it itself [1]. **[6]**

**9.** For: demand in winter is high and in summer low, so temporary and part-time staff mean wage costs fall when demand falls [1]. Seasonal workers can be recruited quickly and without long-term contracts [1]. It avoids redundancy costs in the summer [1]. Against: seasonal staff may have less training and commitment, so service quality may suffer [1]. Recruiting and training new staff every year is costly and takes time [1]. Skilled instructors may go elsewhere if offered only seasonal work [1]. Other methods may help: off-peak pricing, summer activities or outsourcing some services [1]. Judgement: temporary and part-time staff are a sensible main method for peaks, but the resort should keep a core of permanent skilled staff, and encourage returning seasonal workers to protect quality [1], while developing summer activities so that the resort earns more year round [1]. **[9]**

**10.** Quality and freshness [1], because fruit is perishable and customers judge the supermarket on it. Reliability and speed of delivery [1], because late delivery means waste and empty shelves [1]. Price and payment terms [1], which affect margins. Location and transport [1], as nearer suppliers give fresher produce and lower transport emissions. Ethical and environmental standards [1], since customers expect fair labour practices and sustainable farming. **[6]**

**11.** Difficulty 1: length and complexity [1]. Delays at borders, shipping problems or events in one country can interrupt supply, with long lead times meaning large buffers or the risk of stock-outs [1]. Difficulty 2: controlling quality and ethical standards [1]. The business cannot easily inspect distant suppliers, so poor working conditions or environmental damage may go unnoticed, harming its reputation [1]. Accept: exchange rate changes, different laws, communication difficulties. **[4]**

**12.** For: a large buffer protects against late deliveries or a supplier's failure, so the production line is not stopped [1]. A stop in production is very costly because of idle staff and missed delivery dates [1]. Components can be bought in bulk at a discount [1]. It protects against sudden demand rises [1]. Against: holding large stocks costs money in storage space, insurance and finance [1]. Parts may become obsolete if designs change [1]. Large inventories hide problems such as poor quality or unreliable suppliers [1]. It conflicts with lean production aims [1]. Components may be damaged or stolen [1]. Factors: how reliable the suppliers are and how long the lead times, the cost of a stoppage compared with holding costs, and how quickly designs change [1]. Judgement: a moderate buffer is wise for critical, long-lead-time components, because the cost of stopping the line is high [1], but not for items that are cheap to get quickly or likely to become outdated; the buffer should be reviewed regularly using supplier performance data [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* Show the buffer inventory calculation in the re-order level, and always link inventory choices to the cost of running out in the case.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving the buffer out of the re-order level.
- Treating buffer inventory as the same as maximum inventory.
- Saying outsourcing is always cheaper, with no mention of control.
- Choosing suppliers on price alone.
- Suggesting "hold less stock" without weighing stock-outs.
- Not applying general points to the business named.
