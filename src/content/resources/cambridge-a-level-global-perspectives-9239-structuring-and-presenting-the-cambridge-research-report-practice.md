These questions practise the "Structuring and presenting the Cambridge Research Report" section of Component 4 in Cambridge International AS & A Level Global Perspectives & Research (9239), 2026-2028, where the report "should contain an introduction, main body and conclusion". For the component as a whole, see the [Cambridge Research Report study guide](/resources/a-level-global-perspectives-9239-component-4-cambridge-research-report/).

> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge.** The Research Report is coursework, so these questions practise the structuring decisions behind it; they are not past-paper questions. All sources and data are fictional. Marks are for practice only.

Revise first with the [structuring and presenting study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-global-perspectives-9239-structuring-and-presenting-the-cambridge-research-report/).

## Section A

**1.** State **three** things the syllabus says the report must contain or be. **[3]**

**2.** State the maximum word count for the report, and what happens to work beyond it. **[2]**

**3.** Name **two** referencing systems the syllabus says many reports will use. **[2]**

**4.** State **two** items that are not included in the word count. **[2]**

## Section B

**5.** A candidate's report has the following sections: Introduction (300 words), Perspective A (1,400), Perspective B (1,300), Conclusion (900), Evidence appendix (800), plus a title and a bibliography of 400 words. Calculate the number of words that count towards the limit, assuming the appendix counts, and say whether the report is within the limit. **[3]**

**6.** A candidate's introduction states only: "This report is about renewable energy." Identify **three** things it should include and write a better opening sentence for a report on the question "Should countries invest more in solar power than in nuclear power?" **[4]**

**7.** Read this conclusion.

> "In conclusion, solar power is cheap and nuclear power is expensive. A new study in 2025 also found that nuclear plants are unsafe. So solar is better."

Identify **three** weaknesses and say what a better conclusion would do. **[5]**

**8.** A candidate cites sources like this in different places: "(Smith 2020)", "[1]", and "According to a website". Explain what is wrong and what the candidate should do. **[3]**

**9.** A candidate copies a paragraph from an online article, changes three words and does not cite it. Explain **three** possible consequences. **[3]**

**10.** The syllabus requires the report to be written for a non-specialist audience. A candidate's draft says: "The Gini coefficient of the cohort increased." Rewrite the sentence for a non-specialist reader, giving a short definition. **[3]**

**11.** A candidate has written 5,400 words. Suggest **three** ways to bring the report within the limit without losing the argument. **[3]**

**12.** "The structure of the report matters less than its research, because AO3 is worth only 15%." Evaluate this claim. **[8]**

---

## Answers

**1.** Any three [3]: an introduction [1]; a main body [1]; a conclusion [1]; references [1]; a title in the form of a question [1]; continuous prose [1]; an accurate word count stated [1]. **[3]**

**2.** 5,000 words [1]; work beyond the maximum will not be marked [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Any two: Harvard [1]; Chicago [1]; APA [1]. **[2]**

**4.** The title [1] and the bibliographical references [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Count: 300 + 1,400 + 1,300 + 900 + 800 = **4,700** words [2], because the title and bibliography are not counted. 4,700 is within the 5,000 limit [1]. **[3]**

**6.** It should state the research question and why it matters [1]; explain how and why the question was developed [1]; define the key concepts and outline the structure [1]. Opening sentence, for example: "This report asks whether countries should invest more in solar power than in nuclear power, a choice that affects energy costs, safety and climate targets for the next fifty years." [1] **[4]**

**7.** Any three [3]: it does not answer the question as asked (a comparison of investment) [1]; it introduces new evidence ("a new study in 2025") that should have been in the body [1]; it gives no reasons or evidence for the claims [1]; there is no reflection on the limits or certainty of the conclusion [1]; there is no reference to the perspectives or the judgement reached [1]. A better conclusion would answer the question directly, state a judgement with its main reasons, and reflect on limits and further research [2]. **[5]**

**8.** Different styles are mixed [1], and a website is named without author, date or full reference [1]. The candidate should choose one system (for example Harvard, Chicago or APA) and use it consistently, with a full bibliography entry for each source [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Any three [3]: this is plagiarism, passing off someone else's work as one's own [1]; Cambridge uses plagiarism detection software, so it may be detected [1]; the Declaration of Authenticity would be false [1]; the report may not be accepted as the candidate's own work [1]. **[3]**

**10.** For example: "Income inequality, which can be measured by a figure called the Gini coefficient (a number between 0 and 1 where higher means less equal), rose among the group studied." [3] One mark for avoiding unexplained jargon, one for a short definition, one for a clear sentence that keeps the meaning. **[3]**

**11.** Any three [3]: remove repetition [1]; shorten descriptive passages and keep the argument and evidence [1]; cut an example that does not support a main point [1]; tighten sentences and remove unnecessary words [1]; remove a weak third perspective [1]. **[3]**

**12.** Indicative content, up to 8 marks: up to 2 for the case for the claim, up to 4 for the case against it, and 2 for a justified judgement.

For: AO3 is 15% of the marks, much less than AO1 (65%) [1]; a candidate with strong research but average structure can still earn many marks [1].

Against: structure helps the reader follow the argument, and the syllabus says the report must be organised so that the argument is well communicated [1]; poor structure makes it harder for the examiner to find the analysis, perspectives and judgements that earn AO1 and AO2 marks [1]; referencing is part of AO3 and is needed to show the use and assessment of sources [1]; work beyond 5000 words is not marked, so a weak structure can lose marks outright [1].

Judgement, for example: AO3 is a smaller share, but structure supports every other criterion, so it deserves serious attention [2]. **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 5**: counting the title or bibliography, or forgetting to add all the sections.
- **Question 7**: introducing new evidence in the conclusion, or simply repeating the introduction.
- **Question 8**: mixing citation styles.
- **Question 10**: using the technical term without explaining it.
- **Question 12**: using only the 15% figure without explaining how structure affects the other criteria.
