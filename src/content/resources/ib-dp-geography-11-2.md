## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Describe and explain the main types of global flow: goods, services, capital, people and information.
- Explain how networks are structured and why some places become hubs.
- Examine how flows connect places unevenly and who controls them.
- Interpret maps and data showing flows between places.

## Measuring how connected a network is

Geographers describe a network as **nodes** (places) joined by **links** (routes). A simple measure of connectivity is the **beta index**:

**beta index = number of links / number of nodes**

A value below 1 means a branching network with no loops, where losing one link can cut places off. A value above 1 means alternative routes exist, so the network is more connected and more resilient.

### Worked example: a regional air network

Figures are invented.

```
Year 1: 6 airports (nodes), 5 routes (links)
  beta index = 5 / 6 = 0.83
  A branching network: one route lost can isolate an airport

Year 2: the same 6 airports, 8 routes after new services open
  beta index = 8 / 6 = 1.33
  Loops now exist: most airports can be reached by more than one path
```

The rise from 0.83 to 1.33 shows a more connected network. The evaluation: the beta index counts links but not their capacity or frequency, so one daily flight counts the same as a busy motorway; it says nothing about cost or who can use the links. It is a starting point for comparing networks, not a full measure of interaction.

## Why flows concentrate in hubs

Many networks are organised as **hub-and-spoke** systems. Instead of linking every pair of places directly, flows travel to a central hub and are redistributed. Airlines, container shipping and parcel delivery all use this structure.

- **Advantages:** fewer links are needed to connect many places; large volumes through the hub lower the cost per unit; hubs can offer frequent connections.
- **Disadvantages:** journeys are less direct; the hub becomes a single point of failure; places far from a hub gain less.

Hubs gain **cumulative advantages**. Concentrated flows attract logistics firms, finance, offices and skilled workers, which attract more flows. This is why Dubai's airport and ports, or Singapore's port, generate economic activity far beyond transport itself. Places on the spokes, or outside the network entirely, may be bypassed even when they are physically close to the hub.

## Migrant networks

Migration is a flow of people, but it also creates networks that channel further flows.

- **Chain migration.** Early migrants send information and money home, help newcomers find jobs and housing, and so lower the cost and risk of moving for others from the same place. Flows therefore run along well-worn corridors between particular origin and destination areas.
- **Return flows.** Migrant networks carry remittances, ideas, skills and investment back to origin areas.
- **Institutions shape the flow.** In several Gulf states, the **kafala** sponsorship system has tied many migrant workers' legal residence to a specific employer, giving employers great control over workers. This is a clear example of a gatekeeper shaping a flow and the power relations within it.

Networks explain why migration does not follow distance alone: a village may send most of its migrants to one distant city because of ties built over decades.

## The changing geography of production

The geographer Peter Dicken used the term **global shift** for the movement of manufacturing from older industrial countries to newly industrialising economies, especially in East Asia, from the late 20th century. Three newer trends complicate the picture:

| Trend | Meaning | Driver |
|---|---|---|
| Offshoring | Moving production or services to another country | Lower labour and land costs |
| Reshoring | Bringing production back to the home country | Rising foreign wages, automation, policy incentives |
| Nearshoring | Moving production to a nearby country | Shorter supply chains, trade agreements, lower risk |

Disruptions such as the COVID-19 pandemic and rising trade tensions led many firms to weigh resilience against cost. For an exam answer, the key point is that flows of goods and capital are not fixed: they respond to costs, risks and policy, so the map of global production keeps changing.

## Reading flow data

Flow maps use arrows whose width is proportional to the size of the flow. When describing one:

1. State the largest flows, with values and direction.
2. Identify the main origin and destination regions.
3. Note asymmetry: flows in one direction are often much larger than the return flow.
4. Identify places with few or no flows.

A short worked comparison shows why asymmetry matters. In an invented country with a GDP of 40 billion dollars, remittance inflows of 6 billion dollars equal 6 / 40 x 100 = 15% of GDP, while aid of 1.2 billion dollars equals 1.2 / 40 x 100 = 3%. Remittances are five times larger than aid (6 / 1.2 = 5), so the country depends more on its migrant network than on donor governments.

## Annotated model answer

*Explain why some places become hubs in global networks.* **(sample paragraph)**

"A hub forms where routes converge because of location, such as Singapore's position on the Strait of Malacca **[initial advantage]**. Once flows are concentrated there, the cost per unit falls and frequent connections attract shipping lines and airlines that want access to many destinations **[economies of scale explained]**. These flows then draw logistics firms, banks and skilled workers, which add services that attract still more flows **[cumulative advantage]**. Government policy, such as investment in port capacity and open trade rules, reinforces the process **[human factor]**, so a hub's importance grows through feedback rather than location alone **[explained conclusion]**."

## Common mistakes

- Calculating the beta index the wrong way round (nodes divided by links).
- Treating every place in a network as equally connected.
- Explaining migration only by distance or income, without networks.
- Describing flows as fixed rather than responding to costs, risks and policy.
- Reading a flow map without noting the size of return flows or places left out.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a flow map with the largest flows, their values and direction, and any asymmetry.
- **Explain** hubs using linked causes: initial advantage, economies of scale, cumulative growth.
- **Examine** questions on flows reward asking who controls them and who benefits.
- **Evaluate** a connectivity measure by stating what it counts and what it leaves out.
- Use precise terms such as node, link, hub, gatekeeper and corridor.
