## Definitions to learn (3.1.12.1 to 3.1.12.4)

- **Acid**: a proton donor. **Base**: a proton acceptor.
- **Acid–base equilibrium**: an equilibrium involving the transfer of protons.
- **Strong acid / strong base**: fully dissociated in aqueous solution.
- **Weak acid / weak base**: dissociates only slightly in aqueous solution.
- **pH**: −log₁₀[H⁺].
- **Kw**: the ionic product of water, [H⁺][OH⁻]. It is derived from the equilibrium constant for the
  dissociation of water.
- **Ka**: the dissociation constant for a weak acid. **pKa** = −log₁₀ Ka.
- **Buffer**: a solution that keeps an approximately constant pH despite dilution or the addition of small
  amounts of acid or base.

## Key equations

| Quantity | Equation | Units |
|---|---|---|
| pH | pH = −log₁₀[H⁺] | none |
| [H⁺] from pH | [H⁺] = 10^(−pH) | mol dm⁻³ |
| Ionic product of water | Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] | mol² dm⁻⁶ |
| Strong base | [H⁺] = Kw / [OH⁻] | mol dm⁻³ |
| Pure water | [H⁺] = [OH⁻] = √Kw | mol dm⁻³ |
| Weak acid HA | Ka = [H⁺][A⁻] / [HA] | mol dm⁻³ |
| Weak acid alone | [H⁺] = √(Ka × [HA]) | mol dm⁻³ |
| pKa | pKa = −log₁₀ Ka; Ka = 10^(−pKa) | none |
| Acidic buffer | [H⁺] = Ka × [HA] / [A⁻] | mol dm⁻³ |
| Half-neutralisation | pH = pKa | none |

Use Kw = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² dm⁻⁶ at 298 K unless a question gives another value.

## Proton transfer in one line

In NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻, water is the acid and NH₃ the base going forwards; NH₄⁺ is the acid and OH⁻
the base going backwards. Name the proton donor and acceptor in **both** directions.

## Kw and temperature

- [H₂O] is effectively constant, so Kw = Kc × [H₂O].
- Kw increases with temperature, so the dissociation of water is endothermic.
- At higher temperature, pure water has pH below 7 but is **still neutral**: [H⁺] = [OH⁻].

## Method in steps: pH of a strong acid or strong base

1. Strong monoprotic acid: [H⁺] = concentration of acid.
2. Strong base (NaOH, KOH): [OH⁻] = concentration of base.
3. For a base, [H⁺] = Kw / [OH⁻].
4. pH = −log₁₀[H⁺], to 2 decimal places.

## Method in steps: pH of a weak acid

1. Write Ka = [H⁺][A⁻] / [HA].
2. Assume [H⁺] = [A⁻], so Ka = [H⁺]² / [HA].
3. Assume [HA] at equilibrium = starting concentration (only slight dissociation).
4. [H⁺] = √(Ka × [HA]); then pH.

Going the other way (Ka from pH): [H⁺] = 10^(−pH), then Ka = [H⁺]² / [HA]. Give Ka with units,
mol dm⁻³.

## Method in steps: pH after mixing a strong acid and a strong base

1. Moles of H⁺ and moles of OH⁻.
2. Subtract to find which is in excess and by how much.
3. Divide by the **total** volume in dm³.
4. Excess H⁺: pH directly. Excess OH⁻: use Kw first.

## Method in steps: titration results

1. Ignore the rough titre. Average the concordant titres.
2. Moles of the known solution = concentration × titre (dm³).
3. Use the equation ratio (1 : 1 for monoprotic acid with NaOH).
4. Concentration = moles / volume of the unknown (dm³).

## pH curves (3.1.12.5)

| Titration | Start pH | Vertical section | Equivalence pH | Indicator |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Strong acid + strong base | Low | Long | About 7 | Methyl orange or phenolphthalein |
| Weak acid + strong base | Higher | Alkaline range | Above 7 | Phenolphthalein |
| Strong acid + weak base | Low | Acidic range | Below 7 | Methyl orange |
| Weak acid + weak base | Higher | None sharp | Unclear | None suitable; use a pH meter |

Colour change ranges: methyl orange 3.1–4.4; phenolphthalein 8.3–10.0. An indicator works when its whole
range sits inside the vertical section.

**Sketching tips**: mark the equivalence volume on the x-axis; show the flat buffer region for a weak acid
with strong base; the curve levels off as excess base or acid is added.

## Required practical 9

- Calibrate a pH meter with buffers of known pH.
- Pipette the acid into a beaker; add the base from a burette in small portions, swirling and recording pH
  each time.
- Use smaller additions near the end point.
- Plot pH against volume. Equivalence is the midpoint of the steep section.
- Weak acid run: pH at half the equivalence volume = pKa, so Ka = 10^(−pH).
- Repeat with a strong acid and a weak base.

## Buffers (3.1.12.6)

| Type | Made from | Example | Added H⁺ | Added OH⁻ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Acidic | Weak acid + its salt | CH₃COOH / CH₃COONa | H⁺ + A⁻ → HA | OH⁻ + HA → A⁻ + H₂O |
| Basic | Weak base + its salt | NH₃ / NH₄Cl | NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺ | NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ → NH₃ + H₂O |

Why the pH barely changes: the buffer holds large amounts of the acid and its salt (or base and its
salt). Small additions change the ratio [HA]/[A⁻] only slightly. Dilution leaves the ratio unchanged.

An acidic buffer can be made by **partially neutralising a weak acid** with a strong base: HA left =
starting HA − OH⁻ added; A⁻ formed = OH⁻ added.

**Applications**: blood (carbonic acid–hydrogencarbonate keeps pH 7.35–7.45), fermentation, fabric
dyeing, calibrating pH meters.

## Method in steps: pH of an acidic buffer

1. Find moles of HA and A⁻ (from the mixture, or after a reaction with added acid or base).
2. Added H⁺: HA goes up, A⁻ goes down by the same amount. Added OH⁻: the reverse.
3. [H⁺] = Ka × moles HA / moles A⁻ (volume cancels).
4. pH = −log₁₀[H⁺].

## Must-know distinctions

- **Strong vs concentrated**: strong means fully dissociated; concentrated means a lot of acid per dm³.
- **Weak acid alone vs buffer**: alone, [H⁺] = [A⁻]; in a buffer, [A⁻] comes mainly from the salt.
- **Neutral vs pH 7**: neutral means [H⁺] = [OH⁻]; pH 7 is neutral only at 298 K.
- **Equivalence point vs end point**: equivalence is where the moles match the equation; the end point
  is where the indicator changes colour.
- **Ka vs pKa**: larger Ka means stronger acid; larger pKa means weaker acid.

## Quick self-test

1. Define a Brønsted–Lowry base.
2. Calculate the pH of 0.0040 mol dm⁻³ HNO₃.
3. Calculate [H⁺] in a solution of pH 4.85.
4. Calculate the pH of 0.0125 mol dm⁻³ NaOH at 298 K.
5. Ka = 3.2 × 10⁻⁶ mol dm⁻³. Calculate pKa.
6. pKa = 3.40. Calculate Ka.
7. Calculate the pH of 0.120 mol dm⁻³ HA, Ka = 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³.
8. State whether the equivalence pH of a strong acid–weak base titration is above or below 7, and name a
   suitable indicator.
9. A buffer holds 0.300 mol HA and 0.100 mol NaA (Ka = 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³). Calculate its pH.
10. Explain why diluting a buffer hardly changes its pH.
11. At temperature T, Kw = 1.2 × 10⁻¹³ mol² dm⁻⁶. Calculate the pH of pure water at T.
12. In RP9, the pH at half the equivalence volume is 3.70. Calculate Ka.

### Answers

1. A proton acceptor.
2. pH = −log₁₀(0.0040) = **2.40**
3. 10^(−4.85) = **1.41 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³**
4. [H⁺] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ / 0.0125 = 8.0 × 10⁻¹³; pH = **12.10**
5. pKa = **5.49**
6. Ka = 10^(−3.40) = **3.98 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³**
7. [H⁺] = √(2.0 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.120) = 1.55 × 10⁻³; pH = **2.81**
8. **Below 7**; **methyl orange**.
9. [H⁺] = 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.300/0.100 = 3.0 × 10⁻⁵; pH = **4.52**
10. [HA] and [A⁻] fall by the same factor, so their ratio, and [H⁺] = Ka[HA]/[A⁻], stay the same.
11. [H⁺] = √(1.2 × 10⁻¹³) = 3.46 × 10⁻⁷; pH = **6.46** (still neutral).
12. pH = pKa, so Ka = 10^(−3.70) = **2.00 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using √(Ka[HA]) for a buffer instead of Ka × [HA]/[A⁻].
- Forgetting to use the total volume after mixing two solutions.
- Leaving out units for Ka (mol dm⁻³) or Kw (mol² dm⁻⁶).
- Calling hot pure water acidic because its pH is below 7.
- Writing the Ka expression with [H₂O] in it, or with [H⁺]² before stating the assumption.
- Choosing an indicator whose range only partly overlaps the vertical section.
- Sketching a weak acid–strong base curve with an equivalence pH of 7.
- Not adjusting moles of HA and A⁻ after acid or base is added to a buffer.
- Rounding [H⁺] heavily before taking the log.
