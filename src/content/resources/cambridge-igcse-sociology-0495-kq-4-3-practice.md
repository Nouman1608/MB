## Section A

**1.** Define the term *cohabitation*. **[2]**

**2.** Define the term *boomerang children*. **[2]**

**3.** Give **two** reasons why birth rates have fallen in many societies. **[2]**

**4.** Give **two** alternatives to marriage. **[2]**

**5.** Define the term *child-centredness*. **[2]**

**6.** Give **two** reasons why people may have children later in life. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain **three** reasons why divorce rates have increased in many societies. **[6]**

**8.** Explain **three** ways in which industrialisation may have affected family structures. **[6]**

**9.** Explain **three** ways in which the role of children in the family has changed. **[6]**

**10.** Discuss how the internet has affected patterns of marriage and relationships.

Your answer should include:
- at least three developed points with evidence. **[8]**

**11.** Discuss how changes in the status and power of women have affected family life.

Your answer should include:
- at least three developed points with evidence. **[8]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that marriage is becoming less important in society.

Your answer should include:
- at least three arguments for the view and three arguments against the view
- a conclusion. **[14]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1. [2]** A couple living together in a sexual relationship (1) without being married or in a civil partnership (1).

**2. [2]** Adult children who have left the parental home (1) and then return to live with their parents, for example because of housing costs or debt (1).

**3. [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: contraception; women's education and careers; the cost of raising children; lower infant mortality, so fewer births are needed; later marriage; secularisation weakening religious expectations of large families.

**4. [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: cohabitation; singlehood; lone parenthood; civil partnership.

**5. [2]** Family life organised around the needs and interests of children (1), with parents investing more time, money and emotional attention in each child, usually in smaller families (1).

**6. [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: longer time in education; establishing a career first; later marriage or partnership; high housing costs; reliable contraception; medical help with fertility.

### Section B

**7. [6]** Three reasons, 1 for identifying and 1 for developing each:
- **Legislation**: divorce has become legally easier and cheaper; in England and Wales the 1969 Act allowed divorce for "irretrievable breakdown" and no-fault divorce began in 2022.
- **Female empowerment**: paid work and education give women financial independence, so they can leave unhappy marriages.
- **Secularisation**: fewer people see marriage as a sacred, lifelong bond, so divorce carries less moral weight.
- **Changing attitudes**: less stigma attached to divorce; higher expectations of personal happiness in marriage.
- **Role of the internet**: easier to meet new partners, which may encourage people to end unsatisfying relationships.

**8. [6]** Three ways, 1 + 1 each:
- **Shift towards the nuclear family**: Parsons argued industry needed geographically mobile workers, so the isolated nuclear family replaced the extended family.
- **Loss of production function**: work moved from the home to factories, so the family stopped being a unit of production.
- **Extended families in early industrial towns**: Anderson found kin shared housing and helped migrants find work in Preston in 1851.
- **Separation of home and work**: early industrial families developed segregated roles, with men in paid work and women at home (Young and Willmott).
- **Urbanisation**: migration to cities split households, with kin keeping contact at a distance as modified extended families.

**9. [6]** Three ways, 1 + 1 each:
- **From asset to economic burden**: children once worked for the family; now they are dependent for longer and expensive to raise.
- **Child-centredness**: with fewer children, parents invest more time and money in each, and children's needs shape family decisions.
- **Longer dependence**: extended education keeps young people dependent into their twenties.
- **Boomerang children**: adult children return home because of housing costs or insecure work.
- **More say in decisions**: children are consulted more about family decisions and spending.

**10. [8]** Levels: 7–8 good knowledge, three developed points with evidence, terms used well; 4–6 some knowledge, most points supported, some undeveloped; 1–3 limited, descriptive.
Indicative content:
- **Online dating** widens the pool of potential partners beyond the local community, class or ethnic group.
- It may reduce the role of families in choosing partners, affecting arranged marriage in some communities, although some families use matrimonial websites to support arrangements.
- Social media can create opportunities for new or former relationships, which may contribute to breakdown.
- Online information and support make separation and divorce less isolating.
- Long-distance relationships can be maintained online.
- Evaluation: the internet is one factor among many; legislation, female empowerment and changing attitudes may matter more.

**11. [8]** Levels as Question 10. Indicative content:
- Financial independence lets women leave unhappy marriages, contributing to rising divorce, and to lone-parent and reconstituted families.
- Women delay marriage and childbearing to study and build careers, contributing to later parenthood and smaller families.
- Dual worker families are more common, encouraging more joint conjugal roles and the symmetrical family.
- Feminists argue change is incomplete: women in paid work still face the dual burden or triple shift.
- More women choose childlessness or singlehood.
- Evaluation: the extent of change varies by class, culture and society.

**12. [14]** Levels: 12–14 excellent knowledge, three developed and evidenced points on each side, balanced, with a conclusion; 8–11 good knowledge, developed points on both sides but less balance; 4–7 some knowledge, simple two-sided or one-sided answer; 1–3 limited and descriptive. A one-sided answer cannot score more than 6.
*For the view:*
- Falling marriage rates in many industrial societies and rising cohabitation as a long-term alternative.
- Later marriage and more singlehood, with living alone becoming socially acceptable.
- Rising divorce suggests marriage is no longer seen as lifelong.
- Secularisation weakens the religious meaning of marriage.
- More children are born outside marriage with little stigma.
*Against the view:*
- Most people still marry at some point in their lives, and many cohabiting couples later marry.
- High remarriage and serial monogamy show people value marriage even after divorce.
- Civil partnerships, and same-sex marriage where legal, show demand for legal recognition of relationships.
- In many societies and communities, marriage remains central and divorce rare, supported by cultural expectations.
- Weddings remain significant cultural and economic events.
*Conclusion:* a supported judgement, for example that marriage has become a choice rather than an expectation in many industrial societies, but remains important to most people and dominant in many cultures.
