## Questions

**1.** Define a negative output gap. **[2]**

**2.** Country S has potential output of 1,250 and actual output of 1,200 (illustrative units). Calculate the output gap as a percentage of potential output, and state what it suggests about unemployment. **[3]**

**3.** The table gives points on a short-run Phillips curve for Country M (illustrative).

| Unemployment (%) | 4 | 5 | 7 | 9 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Inflation (%) | 7.0 | 5.0 | 3.0 | 2.5 |

**(a)** Calculate the points of inflation gained per point of unemployment saved when unemployment falls from 5% to 4%, and lost when it rises from 7% to 9%. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the curve is steeper at low unemployment. **[2]**

**4.** In Country T the price level index rises from 110 to 114.4 while real output falls from 900 to 864 after a rise in imported fuel costs.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in the price level and in real output. **[2]**
**(b)** Using AD/AS analysis, explain why a change in aggregate demand cannot cure both problems. **[2]**

**5.** Country U has the long-run rate of unemployment of 5%. Inflation follows: inflation = expected inflation − 0.8 × (unemployment − 5). Workers expect this year's inflation to equal last year's. Expected inflation is 1.5% in year 1 and the government holds unemployment at 3.5%.

**(a)** Calculate inflation in years 1, 2 and 3. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain what this shows about the short-run Phillips curve and the long-run, L-shaped curve. **[3]**

**6.** In Country X, inflation = expected inflation − 0.4 × (unemployment − 5), where 5% is the long-run rate. Inflation and expected inflation are both 6%. The central bank holds unemployment at 10% and expectations follow last year's inflation.

**(a)** Calculate inflation in years 1 and 2. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the excess unemployment, in percentage-point years, per point of inflation removed. **[2]**
**(c)** State one reason a credible inflation target could lower this cost. **[1]**

**7.** Country V has GDP of 2,000 and a current account deficit of 20. GDP grows by 60. The marginal propensity to import is 0.25 and exports rise by 8.

**(a)** Calculate the new deficit. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why this illustrates a conflict between macroeconomic objectives. **[2]**

**8.** Country Y trains workers and cuts barriers to hiring, which lowers its long-run rate of unemployment from 7% to 6%. Expected inflation is 3% and inflation = expected inflation − 0.5 × (unemployment − long-run rate).

**(a)** Calculate inflation at 6% unemployment before and after the reform. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how supply-side policy can ease the conflict between unemployment and inflation. **[4]**

**9.** Evaluate the monetarist/supply-side view that the major macroeconomic objectives are compatible in the long run. **[9]**

**10.** Country W has potential output of 1,000. Actual output is 1,032 and inflation has been rising.

**(a)** Calculate the output gap. **[1]**
**(b)** Evaluate whether the government should tighten policy. **[9]**

**11.** Estimates of Country Z's potential output are 1,000 (by one agency) and 1,040 (by another). Actual output is 1,010.

**(a)** Calculate the output gap on each estimate. **[2]**
**(b)** State why this matters for policy. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** Actual output is below potential output [1], so the economy has spare capacity [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition that names both actual and potential output and gives the direction of the difference is the safest route; "output is low" on its own is too vague.

**2.** Gap = (1,200 − 1,250)/1,250 × 100 [1] = **−4.0%** [1]. Spare capacity suggests high cyclical (demand-deficient) unemployment [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers tend to lose credit by dividing by actual output or leaving off the minus sign; show the formula before the numbers.

**3. (a)** 5% to 4%: (7.0 − 5.0)/1 = **2.0 points per point** [1]. 7% to 9%: (3.0 − 2.5)/2 = **0.25 points per point** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** At low unemployment labour is scarce and firms compete for workers, so wages and prices rise faster [1]. At high unemployment there is slack, so each extra point of unemployment does little to wage growth [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Dividing by the change in unemployment, not just reading the inflation change, is the step that shows you understand the trade-off ratio.

**4. (a)** Prices: (114.4 − 110)/110 × 100 = **4.0%** [1]. Output: (864 − 900)/900 × 100 = **−4.0%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The cost shock shifts short-run AS left, raising the price level and cutting output [1]. Raising AD restores output but lifts prices further; cutting AD lowers prices but deepens the output loss [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming the shift (short-run AS, not AD) and the direction on each variable usually gains more credit than a general statement that "the economy suffers".

**5. (a)** Year 1: 1.5 − 0.8 × (3.5 − 5) = **2.7%** [1]. Year 2: 2.7 + 1.2 = **3.9%** [1]. Year 3: 3.9 + 1.2 = **5.1%** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Holding unemployment below the long-run rate pushes inflation above expectations [1]. Expectations then rise and the short-run curve shifts up each year [1]. Unemployment can be held down only with ever-faster inflation, so there is no permanent trade-off and the long-run curve is vertical at 5% [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Linking the numbers to a shift of the curve, rather than describing a rise in inflation alone, shows the idea of expectations.

**6. (a)** Year 1: 6 − 0.4 × (10 − 5) = **4%** [1]. Year 2: 4 − 2 = **2%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Excess unemployment is 5 points for 2 years, which is 10 point-years [1]. Inflation fell by 4 points, so 10/4 = **2.5 point-years per point** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Expectations fall faster, so inflation drops sooner and unemployment need not stay high as long [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Giving the unit (point-years) and showing both the numerator and denominator makes a ratio answer easy to follow.

**7. (a)** Imports rise by 0.25 × 60 = 15 [1]. Net change = 8 − 15 = −7 [1]. New deficit = 20 + 7 = **27** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Growth raises incomes and import spending faster than exports [1]. So the growth objective worsens the current account balance objective, at least in the short run [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A statement of conflict gains credit when it names both objectives and gives the transmission link, here rising income pulling in imports.

**8. (a)** Before: 3 − 0.5 × (6 − 7) = **3.5%** [1]. After: 3 − 0.5 × (6 − 6) = **3.0%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Supply-side measures such as training lower structural unemployment [1], moving the vertical long-run curve left [1]. The short-run curve shifts inward, so the same unemployment comes with lower inflation [1]. This eases the conflict without demand-led pressure on prices [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers say both curves move and tie the shift to a stated policy rather than writing "supply-side policy helps".

**9.** Points that can earn credit, in a developed chain:
Long-run AS is fixed by supply-side factors, so AD changes affect only prices [1]. Expectations adjust, so the long-run Phillips curve is vertical and there is no lasting trade-off [1]. Low, stable inflation reduces uncertainty and encourages investment [1], raising long-run AS and growth [1], which cuts structural unemployment [1]. Competitiveness helps the current account [1]. Against: wages and prices are sticky, so a negative gap can persist [1]; long unemployment erodes skills and may raise the long-run rate [1]. Judgement: the view fits demand-pull better than shocks, and the answer depends on the length of "the long run" and the credibility of policy [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation tends to gain credit when it ends in a supported judgement about time and circumstances, not just a list of "however" points.

**10. (a)** Gap = (1,032 − 1,000)/1,000 × 100 = **+3.2%** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** The positive gap, scarce labour and rising inflation suggest overheating, so tighter fiscal or monetary policy would reduce AD and ease prices [1]. The Phillips curve implies this costs jobs and output [1]. Rising inflation can shift the short-run curve up as expectations rise, so waiting raises the later cost [1]. Against: the gap is an estimate of potential and may be overstated [1]. If inflation comes from cost-push pressures, tightening mainly lowers output [1]. Supply-side policy may be better in the long run but is slow [1]. Tightening risks unemployment and slower growth [1]. The current account may improve as import demand falls [1]. Judgement: tighten modestly if the gap is confirmed and inflation is demand-led, with supply-side support [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers earn credit for judgement when they say what evidence would change the decision, such as the cause of inflation or doubt about potential output.

**11. (a)** On 1,000: (1,010 − 1,000)/1,000 × 100 = **+1.0%** [1]. On 1,040: (1,010 − 1,040)/1,040 × 100 = **−2.9%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The estimates give opposite signs, so policy might tighten or expand depending on the estimate [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Working both gaps from the same actual figure shows the effect of the estimate, rather than just asserting that estimates are uncertain.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing the output gap by actual instead of potential output.
- Describing stagflation as a movement along the short-run curve.
- Giving a trade-off ratio without dividing by the change in unemployment.
- Not showing that expectations drive the shift of the curve.
- Writing "supply-side policy helps" without naming a policy or the curve that moves.
- Evaluating with a list and no judgement.
- Forgetting that potential output is estimated.
- Treating short run and long run as the same time period.
