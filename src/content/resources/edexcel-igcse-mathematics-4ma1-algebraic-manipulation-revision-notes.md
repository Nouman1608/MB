Quick-recall notes for section 2.2, Algebraic manipulation, in Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Mathematics A (4MA1). Foundation statements A to F (substitution, like terms, single brackets, common factors, two brackets, factorising x² + bx + c) are for both tiers. Higher statements A to E (more brackets, any quadratic, algebraic fractions, completing the square, proof) are Higher tier only. For explanations and worked examples, read the [algebraic manipulation study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-mathematics-4ma1-algebraic-manipulation/); for exam-style questions, use the [algebraic manipulation practice questions](/resources/edexcel-igcse-mathematics-4ma1-algebraic-manipulation-practice/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Like terms | Terms with the same letters to the same powers, such as 3ab² and −ab² |
| Expand | Multiply out brackets |
| Factorise | Write as a product of factors; the reverse of expanding |
| Factorise fully | No common factor is left inside any bracket |
| Highest common factor (HCF) | The largest number and highest powers of letters that divide every term |
| Quadratic expression | An expression whose highest power of x is x², such as 3x² − x + 4 |
| Difference of two squares | x² − a² = (x − a)(x + a) |
| Algebraic fraction | A fraction with letters in the numerator, the denominator or both |
| Completed square form | a(x + b)² + c |
| Proof | A general argument that holds for every allowed value |

## Foundation checklist (both tiers)

```
Substitute         brackets round every value:  a = −4 → (−4)² = 16
Like terms         x²y and xy² are different terms
Minus a bracket    −3(2x − 5) = −6x + 15
Common factor      18p²q − 24pq³ = 6pq(3p − 4q²)
Two brackets       (3x − 4)(2x + 5) = 6x² + 7x − 20
Squared bracket    (x − 7)² = x² − 14x + 49
x² + bx + c        product c, sum b:  x² − 2x − 48 = (x − 8)(x + 6)
Difference of squares   x² − 49 = (x − 7)(x + 7)
```

**Higher checklist**

```
Three brackets      two first, simplify, then the third
                    (x + 3)³ = x³ + 9x² + 27x + 27
ax² + bx + c        common factor first; then split using a × c
                    12x² − 7x − 10 = (3x + 2)(4x − 5)
Simplify fraction   factorise top and bottom, cancel whole brackets
                    (x² − x − 6)/(2x² + 3x − 2) = (x − 3)/(2x − 1)
Add or subtract     common denominator, subtract the WHOLE second numerator
Divide              multiply by the reciprocal, then cancel
Complete square     x² + bx + c = (x + b/2)² − (b/2)² + c
                    a ≠ 1: take a out of the x² and x terms only
Proof               n, 2n, 2n + 1, n + 1; simplify; conclude in words
```

## Reading the completed square

| Form | Turning point | Minimum or maximum |
|---|---|---|
| (x − 3)² + 5 | (3, 5) | minimum value 5 at x = 3 |
| 15 − 2(x − 2)² | (2, 15) | maximum value 15 at x = 2 |
| (x + 4)² − 13 | (−4, −13) | minimum value −13 at x = −4 |

The sign inside the bracket is the opposite of the x-coordinate: (x + 4)² is smallest when x = −4.

## Choosing a factorising method

1. Is there a common factor? Take it out first: 3x² − 12x − 36 = 3(x² − 4x − 12) = 3(x − 6)(x + 2).
2. Two terms, both squares, with a minus? Difference of two squares.
3. Coefficient of x² is 1? Product c, sum b.
4. Coefficient of x² is not 1 (Higher)? Split the middle term using a × c.

## Exam traps

- **Sign of the subtracted numerator.** In 4/(x − 3) − 2/(x + 1), the numerator is 4(x + 1) − 2(x − 3) = 2x + 10, not 2x − 2.
- **Cancelling terms.** (x + 6)/(x + 2) cannot be simplified; only common factors cancel.
- **Not fully factorised.** 6x² + 9x = 3x(2x + 3); 3(2x² + 3x) loses a mark.
- **Completing the square with a ≠ 1.** In −2[(x − 2)² − 4] + 7, the −4 becomes +8.
- **(x − 7)²** is never x² − 49.
- **Proof by example.** Checking n = 1, 2, 3 is not a proof.
- **Unfinished proof.** End with a sentence: "8(2n + 1) is a multiple of 8 for every integer n."

## Checking by substitution

After expanding or factorising, put x = 1 (or 2) into both forms.

```
12x² − 7x − 10 at x = 2:   48 − 14 − 10 = 24
(3x + 2)(4x − 5) at x = 2:  8 × 3 = 24      agrees
```

Use x = 2 rather than x = 0 or x = 1 when checking powers: at x = 1 every power of x equals 1, so a wrong power would not show up.

## Self-test

1. Simplify 6ab + 4a − 2ab − 7a.
2. Expand and simplify 4(3x − 2) − 3(2x − 5).
3. Factorise fully 14x²y + 21xy².
4. Expand and simplify (x − 7)².
5. Factorise x² − 2x − 48.
6. Factorise x² − 81.
7. (Higher) Expand (x + 3)³.
8. (Higher) Factorise 12x² − 7x − 10.
9. (Higher) Simplify (x² − x − 6)/(2x² + 3x − 2).
10. (Higher) Write 4/(x − 3) − 2/(x + 1) as a single fraction.
11. (Higher) Write x² − 6x + 14 in the form (x + p)² + q.
12. (Higher) Prove that (n + 1)² − (n − 1)² is a multiple of 4 for every integer n.

### Answers

1. **4ab − 3a**.
2. 12x − 8 − 6x + 15 = **6x + 7**.
3. **7xy(2x + 3y)**.
4. **x² − 14x + 49**.
5. **(x − 8)(x + 6)**.
6. **(x − 9)(x + 9)**.
7. **x³ + 9x² + 27x + 27**.
8. **(3x + 2)(4x − 5)**.
9. **(x − 3)/(2x − 1)**.
10. **(2x + 10)/[(x − 3)(x + 1)]**, or 2(x + 5)/[(x − 3)(x + 1)].
11. **(x − 3)² + 5**.
12. (n² + 2n + 1) − (n² − 2n + 1) = 4n, which has a factor of 4, so it is a multiple of 4 for every integer n.
