This guide teaches section **3.1.5.4 Effect of concentration and pressure** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Paper 2 (and Paper 3). The specification states the effect qualitatively: a higher concentration, or a higher pressure of a gas, increases the collision frequency. The skill is to explain how this influences the rate in a complete and precise way, and to say what does **not** change. The [Kinetics study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-kinetics/) covers the whole topic. Recall is in the [Effect of concentration and pressure revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-effect-of-concentration-and-pressure-revision-notes/), and questions are in the [Effect of concentration and pressure practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-effect-of-concentration-and-pressure-practice/).

## Concentration

Concentration is the amount of substance in a given volume of solution. Doubling the concentration means twice as many particles in each cubic decimetre.

When the concentration of a reactant in solution increases:

1. There are **more particles in the same volume**, so the particles are closer together on average.
2. They **collide more frequently**.
3. The frequency of **successful** collisions increases, because the proportion of collisions with enough energy is unchanged.
4. The **rate increases**.

The key phrase is "more frequent collisions". Do not write that "there are more collisions with sufficient energy" without saying that this is because there are more collisions altogether, in the same proportion.

### What does not change

At a fixed temperature the distribution of energies is the same, so the **proportion** of particles with energy equal to or greater than Eₐ does not change. The activation energy is unchanged. Only the number of collisions per second changes. This is the main difference between concentration and temperature as factors.

### Worked example 1: dilution

25.0 cm³ of 1.50 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid is made up to 100 cm³ with water. The new concentration is

```
c = 1.50 × 25.0 ÷ 100 = 0.375 mol dm⁻³
```

The concentration has fallen to a quarter, so there are a quarter as many acid particles per unit volume and the rate of reaction with a metal or a carbonate is lower, because collisions between the acid and the other reactant are less frequent.

## Pressure of a gas

For gases, a higher pressure means the same number of molecules in a smaller volume. This is a higher **concentration** of gas. At constant temperature the concentration of an ideal gas is proportional to its pressure, because

```
pV = nRT   so   n/V = p ÷ RT
```

### Worked example 2: pressure and concentration

The concentration of a gas at 298 K is calculated at two pressures. (R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹)

```
At 100 kPa: n/V = 1.00 × 10⁵ ÷ (8.31 × 298) = 40.4 mol m⁻³ = 0.0404 mol dm⁻³
At 200 kPa: n/V = 2.00 × 10⁵ ÷ (8.31 × 298) = 80.8 mol m⁻³ = 0.0808 mol dm⁻³
```

Doubling the pressure doubles the concentration, so the collision frequency rises and so does the rate.

### The explanation for pressure

1. A higher pressure of gas puts the **molecules closer together**, with more molecules in the same volume.
2. They **collide more frequently**.
3. More collisions are successful per second.
4. The rate of reaction increases.

### Pressure does not affect liquids and solids

Changing the pressure on a reaction between solids, or involving only liquids or solutions, has almost no effect because the particles are already close together and do not compress. The effect applies to reactions in which **at least one reactant is a gas**.

### Worked example 3: which reaction is affected?

Which of these reactions is likely to have its rate increased by increasing the pressure?

(a) Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)
(b) 2NO(g) + O₂(g) → 2NO₂(g)
(c) NaOH(aq) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l)

Only (b), because the reactants are gases. In (a) and (c) the reactants are solid or in solution, and the gas formed in (a) does not affect the rate.

## The rate changes as a reaction proceeds

As a reaction goes on, the reactants are used up, their concentration falls, and the collision frequency falls. Therefore **the rate of reaction decreases with time**. The graph of the amount of a product against time is steepest at the start and levels off as the reaction finishes.

### Worked example 4: continuous monitoring

Marble chips (calcium carbonate) react with excess hydrochloric acid, and the volume of carbon dioxide is measured every 30 s:

CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)

| Time / s | 0 | 30 | 60 | 90 | 120 | 150 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Volume of CO₂ / cm³ | 0 | 38 | 62 | 78 | 88 | 94 |

The mean rate in each 30 s interval is the increase in volume divided by 30:

| Interval / s | 0–30 | 30–60 | 60–90 | 90–120 | 120–150 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mean rate / cm³ s⁻¹ | 1.27 | 0.80 | 0.53 | 0.33 | 0.20 |

The rate decreases steadily because the concentration of acid decreases as it reacts. At any instant the rate is the **gradient of the tangent** to the volume–time curve at that point. The initial rate is the gradient at t = 0, the steepest part.

### Worked example 5: comparing two concentrations

The experiment is repeated with acid of twice the concentration. The acid is in excess in both experiments, and the mass of marble, the temperature and the size of the chips are the same. Describe how the curve changes.

The initial gradient is steeper, because the more concentrated acid gives more frequent collisions with the marble surface. The final volume is the **same**, because the marble is the limiting reagent and gives the same amount of carbon dioxide. The curve reaches its plateau sooner.

## Other ways to monitor the reaction

| Method | Measures | Suitable when |
|---|---|---|
| Gas syringe | Volume of gas | A gas is produced |
| Mass loss on a balance | Loss of gas from the flask | A heavy gas such as CO₂ is lost |
| Colour change with a colorimeter | Absorbance | A coloured species changes concentration |
| Titration of samples | Concentration | The reaction can be quenched |

## Common mistakes

- Saying that increasing the concentration gives the particles more energy.
- Saying "more particles have energy greater than Eₐ" when only the number of collisions per second has risen, not the proportion.
- Saying that a higher pressure increases the rate of a reaction in solution.
- Writing "more collisions" with no mention of "per unit time" or "frequency".
- Writing that pressure increases "the surface area".
- Forgetting that the rate decreases during a reaction as the reactants are used up.
- Saying that the activation energy changes.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Explain**: more particles in the same volume, so more frequent collisions, so more successful collisions per second, so a higher rate.
- **Describe** the effect on the curve: steeper at the start; same final volume if the limiting reagent is unchanged.
- **Suggest** why the rate falls with time: the concentration of reactant falls.
- **Calculate** a rate from a graph: draw a tangent and find its gradient.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does the proportion of successful collisions not change with concentration?**
The temperature is the same, so the energy distribution of the particles is the same.

**Does a higher concentration change the total amount of product?**
Only if the reactant whose concentration changed is the limiting reagent.

**Does stirring have the same effect as concentration?**
Stirring can increase the rate in heterogeneous reactions by bringing fresh reactant to the surface, but it does not change the concentration.
