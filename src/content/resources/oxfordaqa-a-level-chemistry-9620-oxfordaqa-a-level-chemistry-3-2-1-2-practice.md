These questions cover **3.2.1.2 Physical properties of Period 3 elements** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification: trends in atomic radius, first ionisation energy and melting point from Na to Ar, explained in terms of structure and bonding. This is International AS content, assessed in Unit 1. Data are rounded values from standard data tables.

## Section A

**1.** Write an equation, with state symbols, for the first ionisation energy of silicon. **[1]**

**2.** State and explain the trend in atomic radius from sodium to chlorine. **[3]**

**3.** Explain why argon has a higher first ionisation energy than chlorine. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why the first ionisation energy of aluminium is lower than that of magnesium. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why silicon has a much higher melting point than phosphorus. **[3]**

**6.** State what is overcome when sulfur melts. **[1]**

## Section B

**7.** Use the data table to answer this question.

**(a)** Explain the increase in melting point from sodium to aluminium. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why the melting points of phosphorus, sulfur and chlorine are in the order S > P > Cl. **[3]**

**8.** Four consecutive Period 3 elements, labelled J, K, L and M in order of increasing proton number, have first ionisation energies of 1012, 1000, 1251 and 1521 kJ mol⁻¹.

**(a)** Identify element K. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why K has a lower first ionisation energy than J. **[2]**

**(c)** State the group of element M. **[1]**

**9.** Describe the shape of a graph of first ionisation energy against proton number from sodium to argon. **[3]**

**10.** Compare and explain the melting points of aluminium and silicon. **[3]**

**11.** A student writes: "Chlorine melts at a lower temperature than sulfur because the Cl–Cl bond is weaker than the S–S bond." Evaluate this statement. **[2]**

**12.** Argon has the highest first ionisation energy in Period 3 but the lowest melting point. Explain why these two properties do not follow the same trend. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** **Si(g) → Si⁺(g) + e⁻** [1]

**2.** Atomic radius decreases [1]; nuclear charge increases while the outer electrons are in the same shell with similar shielding [1]; so the outer electrons are attracted more strongly and pulled closer to the nucleus [1]

**3.** Argon has one more proton (greater nuclear charge) [1]; with similar shielding (same outer shell), so its outer electron is held more strongly [1]

**4.** Aluminium's outer electron is in a 3p sub-shell, while magnesium's is in 3s [1]; 3p is higher in energy (and partly shielded by the 3s electrons), so less energy is needed to remove it [1]

**5.** Silicon is macromolecular (giant covalent); many strong covalent bonds must be broken to melt it [1]; phosphorus is molecular (P₄) [1]; melting phosphorus only overcomes weak van der Waals forces between molecules [1]

**6.** Van der Waals forces between S₈ molecules [1]

**7. (a)** All three are metallic; ion charge increases (Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺) and ions get smaller [1]; the number of delocalised electrons per atom increases (1, 2, 3) [1]; so attraction between the ions and the delocalised electrons gets stronger, and more energy is needed to overcome it [1]

**(b)** All three are molecular, and melting overcomes van der Waals forces between molecules [1]; S₈ has more electrons than P₄, which has more than Cl₂ [1]; more electrons give stronger van der Waals forces, so more energy is needed to separate the molecules [1]

**8. (a)** **Sulfur** (J = P, K = S, L = Cl, M = Ar) [1]

**(b)** In K the electron is removed from a 3p orbital containing a pair of electrons [1]; repulsion between the paired electrons makes it easier to remove [1]

**(c)** **Group 0** (Group 18) [1]

**9.** A general increase from sodium to argon [1]; a drop from magnesium to aluminium [1]; a smaller drop from phosphorus to sulfur, with argon the highest [1]

*Examiner insight:* A description of the graph should name both dips. Mentioning only "an increase" earns one mark.

**10.** Silicon (1687 K) has a much higher melting point than aluminium (933 K) [1]; aluminium is metallic: melting overcomes metallic bonding (attraction between Al³⁺ ions and delocalised electrons) [1]; silicon is macromolecular, and many strong covalent bonds must be broken, which needs more energy [1]

**11.** The statement is incorrect: neither Cl–Cl nor S–S covalent bonds are broken on melting [1]; sulfur's melting point is higher because S₈ molecules have more electrons than Cl₂ molecules, so the van der Waals forces between them are stronger [1]

**12.** Ionisation energy depends on how strongly a single atom holds its outer electron, and argon has the greatest nuclear charge with similar shielding [1]; melting point depends on the forces between particles [1]; argon exists as separate atoms with only weak van der Waals forces between them, so little energy is needed to melt it [1]

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying that covalent bonds break when molecular elements melt.
- Saying shielding decreases across a period.
- Explaining both ionisation energy dips with the same reason.
- Leaving out state symbols in ionisation energy equations.
