This guide goes deeper on one section of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Biology (9610) specification, Version 5.1: **3.1.11 Biodiversity within a community**, in Unit 1, The diversity of living organisms. Unit 1 is part of the International AS and counts towards the full International A-level.

The section covers two ways of measuring biodiversity (genetic and species diversity), a formula you must recall and use, and an evaluation of human impact. Test yourself afterwards with the [biodiversity practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-biology-9610-biodiversity-within-a-community-practice/).

## What the specification asks

| Section | Content |
|---|---|
| 3.1.11.1 | Genetic diversity measured by comparing base sequences of DNA or mRNA, frequency of alleles, and amino acid sequences of proteins; interpreting sequence data to suggest relationships |
| 3.1.11.2 | Species richness; the index of diversity, d = N(N - 1) / sum of n(n - 1); human activities that reduce biodiversity; balance between food production and conservation |

You must be able to recall the formula for the index of diversity and use it to calculate values from given data.

## Genetic diversity

**Genetic diversity** is the variety of genetic information within or between species. It can be measured by comparing:

- the **base sequence** of DNA or of mRNA;
- the **frequency of specific base sequences or alleles** in populations;
- the **amino acid sequence** of the proteins that are encoded.

Within a species, a population with many alleles of its genes has high genetic diversity. A population that has been reduced to a small number of individuals will have lost alleles and will have lower genetic diversity.

Between species, comparing DNA or protein sequences shows how similar the organisms are. Organisms with more similar sequences have a more recent common ancestor, as in the previous section of the specification.

**Worked example 1: interpreting a protein sequence comparison.** A short region of a protein has 10 amino acids. Compared with species M, species N has a different amino acid at 1 position and species O has differences at 4 positions.

1. Percentage identical to M: species N = 9 / 10 = 90 %; species O = 6 / 10 = 60 %.
2. Species N has a more similar sequence to M, so it is likely to be more closely related.
3. The conclusion is limited by the small stretch of protein. A longer sequence would give a more reliable result.

**Worked example 2: allele frequency.** In one population of a plant, a gene has three alleles with frequencies 0.5, 0.3 and 0.2. After a population decline, the survivors have only two alleles, with frequencies 0.8 and 0.2. The population has lost one allele, so it has lost genetic diversity, and one allele is now much more common.

## Species diversity

### Species richness

**Species richness** is a measure of the number of different species in a community. It is simply a count. Its limitation is that it takes no account of how many individuals of each species there are. A community with one abundant species and several very rare species has the same species richness as a community where all the species are equally common.

### The index of diversity

An **index of diversity** describes the relationship between the number of different species in a community and the number of individuals in each species. One example is:

d = N(N - 1) / sum of n(n - 1)

where **N** is the total number of organisms of all species and **n** is the total number of organisms of each species. The sum is taken over every species.

A higher value of d means a more diverse community. The index is higher when there are more species and when the individuals are spread more evenly between them.

Follow the same procedure every time:

1. List the number of individuals of each species (n).
2. Find N, the total.
3. Calculate N(N - 1).
4. For each species calculate n(n - 1). Add them to find the sum.
5. Divide N(N - 1) by the sum.

Note that a species represented by one individual contributes zero to the sum, because 1 x 0 = 0.

**Worked example 3: a woodland.** Five species of ground beetle were caught in pitfall traps in a woodland: 12, 8, 5, 3 and 2 individuals.

1. N = 12 + 8 + 5 + 3 + 2 = 30.
2. N(N - 1) = 30 x 29 = 870.
3. n(n - 1): 12 x 11 = 132; 8 x 7 = 56; 5 x 4 = 20; 3 x 2 = 6; 2 x 1 = 2. The sum is 132 + 56 + 20 + 6 + 2 = 216.
4. d = 870 / 216 = **4.03**.

**Worked example 4: a plantation.** The same method in a conifer plantation gave four species with 26, 2, 1 and 1 individuals.

1. N = 26 + 2 + 1 + 1 = 30.
2. N(N - 1) = 870.
3. n(n - 1): 26 x 25 = 650; 2 x 1 = 2; 0; 0. The sum is 652.
4. d = 870 / 652 = **1.33**.

The plantation has four species, but one species dominates, so its index is much lower than the woodland's. The index is 4.03 against 1.33, so the plantation is about a third as diverse by this measure.

**Worked example 5: why richness is not enough.** Communities A and B each have three species and 30 individuals. In A, the numbers are 10, 10 and 10. In B, they are 28, 1 and 1.

- For A, N(N - 1) = 870 and the sum is 3 x 90 = 270, so d = 3.22.
- For B, the sum is 28 x 27 + 0 + 0 = 756, so d = 1.15.

The two communities have the same species richness but very different indices, because B is dominated by one species.

### Sampling for the index

Species counts come from a sample of the community, so the same sampling rules as in section 3.1.6 apply. Use random sampling, an adequate sample size and a method that suits the organisms, for example quadrats for plants and pitfall traps for ground beetles. Mobile organisms need a method such as the capture and release of marked individuals. A small sample can miss rare species, so the index may be an underestimate.

## Human activities that reduce biodiversity

Many human activities reduce biodiversity. Examples to be able to use:

- **Deforestation and habitat loss** for agriculture, mining or building, which remove habitats and species.
- **Monoculture**, where one crop replaces a diverse community, reduces the number of species and the genetic variety within crops.
- **Use of pesticides and herbicides**, which kill non-target species and reduce food for other organisms.
- **Overharvesting**, for example overfishing, which can drive species to extinction.
- **Pollution and eutrophication**, which change conditions so that sensitive species disappear.
- **Introduction of non-native species**, which can outcompete local species.
- **Removal of hedgerows** and enlargement of fields, which removes habitat corridors.

Reduced biodiversity can have ecological effects, such as the loss of food sources for other organisms and the loss of genetic diversity that could support adaptation, and human effects, such as loss of potential medicines.

## The balance between food production and conservation

The specification asks you to consider the **balance between human food production and conservation**. Farming feeds a growing population and also reduces biodiversity. The issue is how to produce enough food without causing unacceptable loss.

| Approaches that favour food production | Approaches that favour conservation |
|---|---|
| Large fields and monocultures that are efficient to harvest | Hedgerows and field margins that provide habitats |
| Pesticides and fertilisers to raise yields | Reduced or targeted use of pesticides; integrated pest control |
| Clearing land for more crops | Protected areas; leaving land uncleared |
| High-yield varieties of a few crops | Keeping varied crops and traditional varieties for genetic diversity |

A balanced answer gives both sides and reaches a judgement. A reasonable conclusion is that compromises, such as leaving field margins uncultivated and reducing pesticide use, can conserve biodiversity at a small cost to yield, and that the choice involves economic and social, as well as biological, factors.

## Common mistakes

- Using only the number of species when asked for a diversity index.
- Writing the formula as N divided by n, or forgetting the minus 1.
- Squaring the numbers instead of calculating n(n - 1).
- Forgetting that a species with one individual contributes zero to the sum.
- Saying that a larger index means fewer species. A higher value means greater diversity.
- Describing conservation as the only goal in a "balance" question.
- Giving "pollution" as a cause of reduced biodiversity without saying how.

## Exam technique

- **Show the table of n and n(n - 1)** in calculations, as it earns method marks.
- **Compare using numbers**: "the woodland has an index of 4.03, higher than the plantation's 1.33, so it is more diverse".
- **Explain why**: link the index to the dominance of one species.
- **In evaluation answers** give at least two points on each side and a conclusion.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to recall the formula?**
Yes. The specification states that you should recall the formula for the index of diversity and use it to calculate values from given data.

**What is a good value of d?**
There is no single threshold. The index is used to compare communities, or one community at different times. Higher means more diverse.
