Learn the lists exactly: three named SRAS factors and six named LRAS factors.

## The concept of aggregate supply

Aggregate supply is the total planned real output that all producers in an economy are willing and able to supply at each possible general price level, over a period of time. The diagram has the **general price level** on the vertical axis and **real national output** (real GDP) on the horizontal axis.

### Why SRAS slopes upward

In the short run, many costs are fixed by contracts: wage rates, rents and some input prices do not change straight away. If the general price level rises while those costs stay put, the price firms receive rises faster than their costs, so producing extra output becomes more profitable and firms expand. In addition, pushing more output from a fixed stock of capital raises the cost of each extra unit, so firms need higher prices to supply more. Both reasons give an upward-sloping SRAS curve.

### Movement or shift?

| Event | Effect | Why |
|---|---|---|
| General price level rises | Movement up along SRAS | The curve is drawn for given costs; only the price level has changed |
| World oil price rises | SRAS shifts left | Energy costs rise at every output level |
| Home currency depreciates | SRAS shifts left | Imported raw materials and components cost more |
| A tax on firms' energy use is cut | SRAS shifts right | Costs per unit fall |
| New production technology spreads | LRAS shifts right (SRAS usually too) | Capacity rises, and unit costs fall |
| Net inward migration of workers | LRAS shifts right | The labour force grows |

The rule: a change in the **price level** moves the economy along the curve. A change in **costs** shifts SRAS. A change in the **quantity or quality of resources** shifts LRAS.

## Short-run AS: the three named factors

**Costs of raw materials and energy.** These enter the costs of almost every firm, through fuel, electricity, transport and materials.

**Worked example 1.** A manufacturer's unit cost is 50 (currency units, invented). Energy makes up 10 of this. A rise in world gas prices raises the energy cost to 13.

```
new unit cost = 50 − 10 + 13 = 53
percentage rise = 3 ÷ 50 × 100 = 6%
```

If this happens across the economy, SRAS shifts left: at every price level firms supply less.

**Exchange rates.** An **appreciation** lowers the home-currency price of imports; a **depreciation** raises it.

**Worked example 2.** A firm imports a component priced at $30. The exchange rate rises from 1 unit = $1.20 to 1 unit = $1.50.

```
before: 30 ÷ 1.20 = 25 units
after:  30 ÷ 1.50 = 20 units
fall in cost = 5 ÷ 25 × 100 = 20%
```

Lower import costs shift SRAS right. For evaluation, note that the same appreciation makes exports dearer abroad, reducing net exports and so AD. The overall effect on real output depends on which change is larger.

**Tax rates.** Taxes that add to firms' costs, such as taxes on employment or indirect taxes on inputs, shift SRAS left when they rise and right when they fall. Explain the link to costs in your answer.

## Long-run AS: the two shapes

LRAS shows the economy's **productive capacity**: the output it can produce when all resources are fully employed.

### Classical LRAS

The classical curve is **vertical** at the full-employment level of real output. The reasoning is that in the long run wages and prices are flexible. If output falls below capacity, unemployed workers accept lower wages, costs fall, and output returns to full employment. Long-run output is therefore set by the quantity and quality of resources, not by AD. A rise in AD raises only the price level in the long run.

### Keynesian LRAS

The Keynesian curve has three sections:

1. **Horizontal** at low output: there is spare capacity and unemployment, so firms can expand output without raising costs or prices.
2. **Upward sloping**: as output rises, shortages of skilled labour and materials (bottlenecks) push up costs, so output and the price level rise together.
3. **Vertical** at full capacity: no more output is possible, so extra AD only raises prices.

The key Keynesian claim is that wages and prices do not adjust quickly downward, so the economy can stay below full employment for a long time.

### Why the shape matters

| Situation | Classical prediction | Keynesian prediction |
|---|---|---|
| AD rises in a recession | Little lasting change in output; prices rise | Output rises with little or no inflation |
| AD rises near full capacity | Prices rise | Prices rise |
| Implied policy focus | Supply-side policy to raise capacity | Demand-side policy can raise output when capacity is spare |

## Long-run AS: the six named factors

| Factor | How it shifts LRAS |
|---|---|
| State of technology | New methods raise output from the same inputs: right |
| Productivity | More output per worker or per hour: right |
| Education and skills | Better-trained workers raise productivity and fill skill gaps: right |
| Government regulations and tax | Lighter regulation or lower taxes on enterprise can raise the incentive to invest and work: right. Heavier regulation can raise costs and reduce capacity: left |
| Demography and net migration | More working-age people raise the labour force: right. An ageing population with fewer workers: left or slower growth |
| Competition policy | Stronger competition pushes firms to cut costs and innovate: right |

**Worked example 3.** Country E has 1.5 billion hours of work a year and produces 40 per hour (invented). Training raises output to 42 per hour.

```
before: 1.5 bn × 40 = 60 bn
after:  1.5 bn × 42 = 63 bn
rise in capacity = 3 ÷ 60 × 100 = 5%
```

LRAS shifts right by 5%. With AD unchanged, the price level falls and real output rises.

### Factors that shift both curves

New technology and higher productivity reduce unit costs straight away (SRAS right) and raise capacity (LRAS right). An energy price rise is usually treated as a short-run cost shock; if it lasts and makes some capital uneconomic to run, it can reduce capacity too. Saying which curve moves, and over what time, earns analysis marks.

## Annotated model answer

**Question (8 marks).** Examine the likely impact of increased government spending on education and training on long-run aggregate supply.

**Answer.**

More spending on education and training raises the skills of the labour force, which is one of the specification's named LRAS factors. *(Knowledge.)* Workers with better skills produce more output per hour, so productivity rises, and the economy can produce more with the same number of workers. LRAS shifts to the right, from LRAS₁ to LRAS₂, raising potential real output. *(Analysis: a chain from skills to productivity to capacity, with the diagram described.)* If AD is unchanged, the price level falls and real output rises. *(Analysis: consequence.)*

However, the effect takes years: students trained now enter the labour force later, so LRAS shifts slowly. *(Evaluation: time lag.)* The impact also depends on whether the training matches the skills firms need, and on whether trained workers stay in the country, since net emigration of skilled workers would reduce the gain. *(Evaluation: conditions.)*

Examine needs a chain of reasoning and a brief assessment; this answer gives both in a short space.

## Common mistakes

- Saying a rise in the price level shifts AS.
- Giving a demand-side factor, such as consumer confidence, as a cause of an AS shift.
- Drawing SRAS shifting when the factor changes capacity, or LRAS shifting for a temporary cost change.
- Labelling the horizontal axis "quantity" instead of real national output.
- Describing the Keynesian curve without explaining why each section has its shape.
- Forgetting that an exchange rate change also affects AD through net exports.

## Exam technique

The specification's command word table gives the tariffs: define (2), calculate (2 or 4), draw (4), explain (4), analyse (6), examine (8), discuss (14), and evaluate or to what extent (20).

- **Explain (4):** knowledge, application and a two-stage chain of reasoning; no evaluation needed.
- **Analyse (6):** a fuller chain of reasoning, with a diagram where it helps.
- **Examine (8):** analysis plus a brief assessment.
- **Discuss (14) and evaluate (20):** developed chains of reasoning, different viewpoints (for AS, often Keynesian against classical), and a supported judgement.
- **Diagrams:** label both axes, both curves, the original and new equilibria, and the direction of the shift.
