These questions cover section 14.3 of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026: regional disparities in social and economic development, the concept of core-periphery, and cumulative causation with spread and backwash effects, divergence and convergence. All countries and data in Questions 1 to 9 are fictional. Revise first with the [14.3 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-regional-development-within-countries/) and the [14.3 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-regional-development-within-countries-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Define the term "backwash effect". **[2]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by agglomeration economies and how they contribute to cumulative causation. **[3]**

**3.** The table shows GDP per capita, in US dollars, for the four regions of the fictional country of Dorvia. The national average is 11,000.

| Region | GDP per capita | Life expectancy (years) |
|---|---|---|
| A (capital region) | 19,800 | 78 |
| B | 12,100 | 76 |
| C | 7,700 | 74 |
| D | 5,500 | 75 |

**(a)** Calculate the index (national average = 100) for region D. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the ratio of GDP per capita in region A to region D. **[1]**

**(c)** Using the data, comment on whether economic and social disparities in Dorvia follow the same pattern. **[3]**

**4.** In the fictional country of Esland, GDP per capita in the core rose from 30,000 to 45,000 US dollars between 2000 and 2020, and in the periphery from 6,000 to 10,000 US dollars.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage growth in GDP per capita in each region. **[2]**

**(b)** Using your answers and the data, explain why it could be argued that Esland's regions are both converging and diverging. **[4]**

**5.** Outline two kinds of initial advantage that might start cumulative causation in a region. **[4]**

**6.** Explain how out-migration can cause a peripheral region to decline further. **[4]**

**7.** Suggest four conditions under which spread effects may come to outweigh backwash effects. **[4]**

**8.** Explain the difference between the core and the periphery in a country's space economy. **[3]**

**9.** Explain how the loss of an initial advantage can cause decline in a region of a high-income country. **[3]**

## Section B

**10.** Using examples, explain the process of cumulative causation. **[10]**

**11.** "Regional disparities within countries always widen as countries develop." With reference to models and examples, how far do you agree? **[20]**

**12.** Assess the relative importance of spread effects and backwash effects in explaining regional disparities in one country you have studied. **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** A flow or process that benefits the core at the expense of the periphery [1], such as migration of young, skilled workers or capital from the periphery to the core [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Agglomeration economies are the benefits firms gain from locating close to each other [1], such as shared suppliers, a pool of skilled labour and exchange of ideas [1]. They make the core more attractive to further investment, so growth reinforces itself [1]. **[3]**

**3. (a)** 5,500 / 11,000 x 100 = 50 [1].

**(b)** 19,800 / 5,500 = 3.6 [1].

**(c)** Economic disparity is large: region A's GDP per capita is 3.6 times region D's [1]. Life expectancy varies much less, from 74 to 78 years [1]. The patterns differ: region D has the lowest income but a higher life expectancy than region C, so social and economic disparities do not match exactly [1]. **[5]**

**4. (a)** Core: (45,000 - 30,000) / 30,000 x 100 = 50% [1]. Periphery: (10,000 - 6,000) / 6,000 x 100 = 66.7% [1].

**(b)** The periphery grew faster in percentage terms [1], so the ratio of core to periphery fell from 5.0 to 4.5, showing relative convergence [1]. But the absolute gap widened from 24,000 to 35,000 US dollars [1], showing absolute divergence; the conclusion depends on which measure is used [1]. **[6]**

**5.** A natural resource such as coal or iron ore [1] attracts early industry that creates jobs and linkages [1]. A political function such as being the capital [1] brings government spending, jobs and infrastructure [1]. Also credit port or site advantages, an early start or fertile land, each with its effect. **[4]**

**6.** Young and skilled people leave for the core [1], so the region loses workers and entrepreneurs, and its population ages [1]. Spending and tax revenue fall, so shops, schools and services close [1], which gives further reasons to leave, reinforcing decline [1]. **[4]**

**7.** Rising congestion, land prices or wages in the core push firms out [1]; improved transport links make peripheral sites accessible [1]; government investment or incentives in the periphery [1]; the periphery has its own resources or a growing regional city [1]. **[4]**

**8.** The core has the greatest concentration of population, investment, decision-making and wealth [1]. The periphery has lower incomes and fewer services [1] and depends on the core for capital, markets and jobs [1]. **[3]**

**9.** The advantage that attracted industry, such as coal or a port, declines or becomes uncompetitive [1]. Firms close, causing job losses and less spending [1], so the cumulative causation process works in reverse, with out-migration and falling tax revenue [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Levels-based. Credit: definition of cumulative causation [1]; an initial advantage, with an example [1]; new investment and jobs [1]; in-migration of workers [1]; the multiplier effect [1]; agglomeration economies [1]; higher tax revenue funding infrastructure [1]; further investment so the cycle repeats [1]; backwash effects on the periphery [1]; a named real example such as the South-east of Brazil or another core region [1]. **[10]**

**11.** The real exam marks this essay with levels of response. Indicative creditworthy content:

*Introduction:* define regional disparities [1]; define divergence and convergence [1].

*Agree:* Myrdal's cumulative causation predicts backwash and divergence [1]; Friedmann's stage 2 shows a single dominant core [1]; examples of widening gaps during rapid growth, such as coastal and interior China or the South-east and North-east of Brazil [1].

*Disagree:* Williamson's inverted U suggests inequality falls in later development [1]; Friedmann's stages 3 and 4 show sub-cores and integration [1]; spread effects grow as core diseconomies rise [1]; government regional policy can narrow gaps [1].

*Complications:* relative and absolute measures may disagree [1]; social and economic indicators may disagree [1]; disparities can widen in high-income countries when old industrial regions decline [1].

*Judgement:* a clear view on "always" [1]; justified with models [1] and examples [1]; recognition that outcomes depend on policy [1], measure [1] and stage of development [1]; a conclusion answering the question [1]; awareness that models are generalisations [1]. **[20]**
*Examiner insight:* "Always" invites a counter-example; one well-chosen case of convergence weakens the statement.

**12.** Levels-based. Indicative content:

*Introduction:* name and locate the country, its core and periphery [1]; define spread and backwash [1].

*Backwash:* migration from periphery to core with evidence [1]; capital and investment concentrated in the core [1]; competition from core firms [1]; concentration of services [1].

*Spread:* demand from the core for peripheral products [1]; relocation of firms or growth of sub-cores [1]; remittances [1]; infrastructure linking the regions [1].

*Other factors:* physical constraints such as drought or terrain [1]; government policy [1]; historical initial advantages [1].

*Evaluation:* which effect has dominated in which period [1]; evidence of divergence or convergence [1]; which measure is used [1]; interaction between effects [1]; overall judgement [1]; supported by evidence from the country [1]; a conclusion that answers the question [1]. **[20]**
*Examiner insight:* A time-based answer (backwash dominant in one period, spread in a later one) is a strong way to structure the judgement.
