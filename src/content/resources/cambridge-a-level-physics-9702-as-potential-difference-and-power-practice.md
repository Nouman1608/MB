## Section A

**1.** Define the potential difference across a component. **[2]**

**2.** Show that the volt is equivalent to kg m² s⁻³ A⁻¹. **[2]**

**3.** When 1.5 C of charge passes through a component, 6.0 J of energy is transferred. Calculate the p.d. across the component and the energy transferred by each electron that passes through it. **[2]**

**4.** A lamp is rated 36 W, 12 V. Calculate the current in the lamp and its resistance at normal operation. **[3]**

**5.** Starting from P = VI, show that the power dissipated in a resistor can be written as P = V²/R. **[2]**

**6.** An electron is accelerated from rest through a p.d. of 1.5 kV. Calculate the kinetic energy it gains. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** An immersion heater is rated 3.0 kW, 240 V.

**(a)** Calculate the current in the heater. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the resistance of the heating element. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the charge that flows through the heater in 10 minutes. **[2]**

**(d)** Calculate the energy transferred in 10 minutes. **[1]**

**(e)** The supply voltage falls to 216 V. Assuming the resistance is unchanged, calculate the new power. **[2]**

**8.** Resistors of 15 Ω and 45 Ω are connected to a 12 V supply of negligible internal resistance.

**(a)** The resistors are connected in series. Calculate the power dissipated in each. **[3]**

**(b)** The resistors are now connected in parallel. Calculate the power dissipated in each. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the larger resistor dissipates more power in (a) but less in (b). **[2]**

**9.** An electric motor connected to a 6.0 V supply draws a current of 2.5 A. In 4.0 s it lifts a 2.0 kg mass through a vertical height of 1.5 m at constant speed.

**(a)** Calculate the electrical energy supplied to the motor in 4.0 s. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the gain in gravitational potential energy of the mass. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the efficiency of the motor. **[1]**

**(d)** A student calculates the input power of the motor using P = I²R, with R the resistance of the motor's coil. Explain why this gives the wrong answer. **[2]**

**10.** An extension cable has a total resistance of 0.20 Ω and supplies an appliance that draws 6.0 A.

**(a)** Calculate the power wasted in the cable. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the p.d. across the cable. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why a thicker cable would reduce the power wasted. **[2]**

**11.** A 220 Ω resistor has a power rating of 0.50 W. Calculate the maximum current it can carry and the maximum p.d. that can be applied across it. **[3]**

**12.** A filament lamp is rated 60 W, 230 V. A student predicts that on a 115 V supply its power will be 15 W.

**(a)** Show how the student obtained 15 W, and state the assumption made. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why the actual power on 115 V is greater than 15 W. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** The energy transferred (from electrical energy to other forms) [1] per unit charge passing through the component [1].

**2.** V = W/Q, so 1 V = 1 J C⁻¹ [1]; J = kg m² s⁻² and C = A s, so V = kg m² s⁻² ÷ A s = kg m² s⁻³ A⁻¹ [1].

**3.** V = W/Q = 6.0 ÷ 1.5 = 4.0 V [1]; energy per electron = eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 4.0 = 6.4 × 10⁻¹⁹ J [1].

**4.** I = P/V = 36 ÷ 12 = 3.0 A [1]; R = V/I = 12 ÷ 3.0 [1] = 4.0 Ω [1].

**5.** For a resistor, I = V/R [1]; so P = VI = V × V/R = V²/R [1].

**6.** W = QV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 1500 [1] = 2.4 × 10⁻¹⁶ J [1].

**7. (a)** I = P/V = 3000 ÷ 240 = 12.5 A [1].

**(b)** R = V²/P = 240² ÷ 3000 [1] = 19.2 Ω [1].

**(c)** Q = It = 12.5 × 600 [1] = 7.5 × 10³ C [1].

**(d)** W = Pt = 3000 × 600 = 1.8 × 10⁶ J [1].

**(e)** P = V²/R = 216² ÷ 19.2 [1] = 2.4 × 10³ W (2430 W) [1].

**8. (a)** Total resistance = 60 Ω, so I = 12 ÷ 60 = 0.20 A [1]. 15 Ω: P = 0.20² × 15 = 0.60 W [1]. 45 Ω: P = 0.20² × 45 = 1.8 W [1].

**(b)** p.d. across each = 12 V. 15 Ω: P = 12² ÷ 15 = 9.6 W [1]. 45 Ω: P = 12² ÷ 45 = 3.2 W [1].

**(c)** In series the current is the same in both, so P = I²R is greater for the larger resistance [1]. In parallel the p.d. is the same across both, so P = V²/R is smaller for the larger resistance [1].

**9. (a)** W = VIt [1] = 6.0 × 2.5 × 4.0 = 60 J [1].

**(b)** mgh = 2.0 × 9.81 × 1.5 = 29 J [1].

**(c)** Efficiency = 29.4 ÷ 60 = 0.49 (49%) [1].

**(d)** Not all the energy supplied to a motor is dissipated in its resistance: much is transferred to kinetic and gravitational potential energy (useful work) [1]. P = I²R gives only the heating in the coil; the total input power is VI [1].

**10. (a)** P = I²R = 6.0² × 0.20 [1] = 7.2 W [1].

**(b)** V = IR = 6.0 × 0.20 = 1.2 V [1].

**(c)** A thicker cable has a larger cross-sectional area and so a smaller resistance [1]; for the same current, the power wasted, I²R, is smaller [1].

**11.** I_max = √(P/R) = √(0.50 ÷ 220) [1] = 0.048 A [1]; V_max = √(PR) = √(0.50 × 220) = 10.5 V [1].

**12. (a)** R = V²/P = 230² ÷ 60 = 882 Ω; P = 115² ÷ 882 = 15 W (equivalently, half the voltage gives a quarter of the power) [1]. Assumption: the resistance of the filament stays the same [1].

**(b)** At 115 V the current is smaller, so the filament is at a lower temperature [1]. Its resistance is therefore smaller than at 230 V [1]. Since P = V²/R, a smaller R gives a power greater than 15 W [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining p.d. without "per unit charge".
- Keeping the old current when the voltage changes, instead of recalculating it.
- Forgetting to convert kW to W and minutes to seconds.
- Using the supply voltage in V²/R for one resistor in a series circuit.
- Calculating a motor's input power from I²R.
- Giving the filament-lamp explanation without linking temperature to resistance.
