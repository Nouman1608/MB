## Definitions to learn word for word

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Supply-side policy (4.5.1) | Government action to increase the economy's productive capacity and the efficiency of its markets, shifting the PPC outwards over time |
| Economic growth (4.6.1) | Rising output of goods and services across time, usually measured by the percentage change in real GDP |
| GDP | The total value of goods and services produced in a country in a period |
| Nominal GDP | GDP at the prices of the year it is measured |
| Real GDP | GDP adjusted for inflation, using constant prices |
| GDP per head | GDP divided by population |
| Recession (4.6.3) | A period when real GDP falls; often two consecutive quarters of falling real GDP |

## Supply-side measures at a glance (4.5.2)

| Measure | What the government does | Mechanism | Main drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Education and training | Funds schools, colleges and apprenticeships | Output per worker up; workers more mobile | Slow, costly, skills may not match jobs |
| Labour market reforms | Weakens barriers to hiring and moving; improves job information | Firms hire more readily; unemployed fill vacancies faster | Job insecurity; lower pay at the bottom |
| Lower direct taxes | Cuts income tax and corporation tax | Extra work pays more; firms keep more profit to invest | Lower tax revenue; gains skewed to high earners |
| Deregulation | Removes entry barriers and rules | More competition, lower costs, innovation | Weaker protection of consumers and the environment |
| Incentives to work and invest | Lower benefits; investment grants; tax allowances | More labour and capital supplied | Hurts people who cannot work; grants cost money |
| Privatisation | Sells state-owned firms | Profit motive and competition cut waste | Private monopoly; loss of unprofitable services |

### Method: a chain for any 4-mark explanation

1. Name the measure and what the government does.
2. Say who changes behaviour (workers, firms, consumers).
3. Say what changes in output, productivity or costs.
4. Link to the aim: growth, unemployment, prices, balance of payments or redistribution.

Worked reminder (invented). A firm gets a grant to buy machines, so output per worker rises from 20 to 23 in a week, a rise of (23 − 20) ÷ 20 × 100 = 15%. Capacity has risen, and the PPC can shift out only if the firm also finds buyers.

## Effects on macroeconomic aims (4.5.3)

- Growth: more productive resources shift the PPC outwards.
- Unemployment: better skills and flexible labour markets reduce structural unemployment.
- Prices: lower costs and a larger supply ease price pressure.
- Balance of payments: more competitive exports and fewer imports needed.
- Redistribution: education helps; tax and benefit cuts may hurt.
- Evaluation words: time lag, cost, uncertain response, depends on demand.

Tax-cut revenue check (invented). National income is 2 000 million and the tax rate is 25%, so revenue is 500. The rate falls to 20% and income rises to 2 300, so revenue is 460. Revenue would stay at 500 only if income reached 500 ÷ 0.20 = 2 500.

## Measuring growth (4.6.2)

| Calculation | Formula |
|---|---|
| Real GDP | (nominal GDP ÷ price index) × 100 |
| Growth rate | (new − old) ÷ old × 100 |
| GDP per head | GDP ÷ population |
| Real GDP per head growth | Compare real GDP ÷ population in the two years |

### Method: finding real growth

1. Divide each year's nominal GDP by its price index and multiply by 100.
2. Use the base year's own figure unchanged.
3. Find the percentage change in the two real figures.

Worked reminder (Country M, invented). Nominal GDP is 960 and then 1 008; the price index goes from 100 to 108. Nominal growth is 5.0%. Real GDP in the second year = 1 008 ÷ 108 × 100 = 933.3. Real growth = (933.3 − 960) ÷ 960 × 100 = −2.8%. Output fell even though the nominal figure rose.

Must-know distinction: real GDP growth shows output change; GDP per head growth also needs population. When population grows faster than output, GDP per head falls.

Limits of GDP per head: an average that hides inequality, leaves out unpaid and informal work, ignores pollution, and says nothing about quality of life.

## Recession (4.6.3)

- Causes: falling consumer or investment spending, weaker exports, higher interest rates, tight credit, cuts in government spending, a rise in key input prices.
- Consequences: rising unemployment, failing firms, lower incomes, lower tax revenue and higher benefit spending, slower price rises, more poverty.
- PPC: a recession moves the economy from a point on the curve to a point inside it. The curve stays where it is, because the resources still exist. They are unemployed or underused.
- Recovery moves the economy back towards the PPC. Growth in potential output shifts the curve outwards, which is a separate event.

| Event | PPC picture |
|---|---|
| Recession | Move from a point on the PPC to a point inside it |
| Recovery | Move from inside back towards the PPC |
| Supply-side success | PPC shifts outwards |
| Long recession scarring skills and capital | PPC may shift inwards |

## Must-know distinctions

- Supply-side policy versus demand-side policy: supply-side policy works on capacity and costs over years; fiscal and monetary policy work on spending, usually faster.
- Direct versus indirect taxes: lower direct taxes (on income and profit) are the supply-side measure. Taxes on spending are not.
- Deregulation versus privatisation: deregulation removes rules. Privatisation changes who owns an enterprise.
- Real versus nominal: only real GDP removes the effect of price changes.
- Total versus per head: a larger economy is not always a richer one per person.
- Recession versus slow growth: in a recession real GDP falls. Slow growth is still positive growth.
- Cyclical versus structural unemployment: a recession causes cyclical unemployment, which supply-side measures do little to cure. Training addresses structural unemployment.

## Evaluation checklist for supply-side questions

- Time: how long before output responds?
- Cost: what does it cost the government, and what does it give up?
- Size of response: will people actually change behaviour?
- Who gains and loses: workers, firms, consumers, taxpayers, the poor.
- Demand: is there enough spending to buy the extra output?
- Conclusion: give a judgement that rests on a named condition, for example "this works best when skills shortages are the main problem".

Worked reminder: per head (Country C, invented). Real GDP rises from 1 200 to 1 260 billion (up 5.0%) and population from 60 to 63 million (up 5.0%). GDP per head is 20 000 both years, so there is no gain for the average person even though output grew.

## Quick self-test

1. Define supply-side policy.
2. Name the six supply-side measures in the syllabus.
3. Nominal GDP is 1 500 and the price index is 125. Find real GDP.
4. Real GDP per head rises from 12 000 to 12 360. Find the percentage growth.
5. Real GDP per head is 18 000 and population is 25 million. Find real GDP.
6. Real GDP by quarter is 300, 297 and 294. Is this a recession by the two-quarter rule, and what is the fall from the first quarter?
7. Give two reasons a cut in income tax might not raise output.
8. Give one benefit and one risk of privatisation.
9. A recession is a movement within the PPC. Where does the economy move?
10. Why can real GDP growth of 3% coexist with falling GDP per head?

### Answers

1. Government action to raise productive capacity and market efficiency, so the PPC shifts outwards.
2. Education and training, labour market reforms, lower direct taxes, deregulation, improved incentives to work and invest, privatisation.
3. 1 500 ÷ 125 × 100 = **1 200**.
4. (12 360 − 12 000) ÷ 12 000 × 100 = **3.0%**.
5. 18 000 × 25 million = **450 billion** (450 000 million).
6. Falls of 1.0% and 1.0%, so yes. The fall from the first quarter is (294 − 300) ÷ 300 × 100 = **−2.0%**.
7. Some people may work fewer hours because the same income needs less effort; and weak demand may mean firms do not need extra output.
8. Benefit: competition and the profit motive cut waste. Risk: a private monopoly raises prices.
9. From a point on the curve to a point inside it.
10. Population may be growing faster than 3%, so output per person falls.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using nominal GDP when the question asks for growth in output.
- Forgetting to multiply by 100 when applying a price index.
- Naming a supply-side measure with no mechanism.
- Saying a measure "always" works, with no time lag or cost.
- Describing a recession as a shift of the curve instead of a move inside it.
- Giving a total GDP figure when asked for GDP per head.
- Leaving out the evaluation: who gains, who loses and how long it takes.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is supply-side policy?**
Supply-side policy is government action to raise the economy's productive capacity by making labour, capital and markets more productive and efficient. It is not mainly about boosting spending.

**What are examples of supply-side measures?**
Examples include education and training, labour market reforms, lower direct taxes, deregulation, improving incentives to work and invest, and privatisation.

**What is a recession?**
A recession is a period when real GDP falls. A common rule of thumb is a fall for two consecutive quarters, although the syllabus asks for the meaning rather than a fixed rule.

**What are the limits of GDP per head as a measure?**
GDP per head is an average that hides inequality, leaves out unpaid work and the informal sector, ignores pollution and leisure, and cannot show the quality of goods and services.
