## Section A

**1.** Two cells are modelled as cubes of side 20 μm and 40 μm.

**(a)** Calculate the SA:V of each cell. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain which cell would be better supplied with oxygen by diffusion alone. **[1]**

**2.** State the function of:

**(a)** elastic fibres in the alveoli **[1]**

**(b)** smooth muscle in the bronchioles **[1]**

**3.** Name **two** tissues present in the trachea that are absent from the alveoli, and give the function of each. **[2]**

**4.** Explain how root hair cells are adapted for the uptake of water and mineral ions. **[2]**

**5.** Distinguish between tidal volume and vital capacity. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Describe and explain how air is forced out of the lungs during a cough. **[4]**

**7.** A student used a spirometer at rest and during exercise.

| | Tidal volume / dm³ | Breathing rate / breaths min⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|
| Rest | 0.50 | 12 |
| Exercise | 1.6 | 30 |

**(a)** Calculate the ventilation rate at rest and during exercise. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage increase in ventilation rate. **[1]**

**8.** On a spirometer trace, the line joining the troughs falls from 4.10 dm³ at 0 s to 3.62 dm³ at 90 s.

**(a)** Calculate the rate of oxygen uptake in dm³ min⁻¹. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why the trace falls even though the subject breathes out as much gas as they breathe in. **[2]**

**9.** **(a)** Explain why a bony fish with countercurrent flow over its gills absorbs more oxygen than it would with parallel flow. **[3]**

**(b)** Suggest why a fish dies quickly when out of water, even though air contains more oxygen than water. **[2]**

**10.** During flight, the volume of tracheal fluid at the ends of the tracheoles in an insect's flight muscle decreases. Explain how this happens and why it is an advantage. **[3]**

**11.** Insects living in dry habitats keep their spiracles closed for long periods. Suggest the advantage and the disadvantage of this. **[2]**

**12.** Describe how you could tell a section of trachea from a section of a small bronchiole when viewing prepared slides with a light microscope. **[3]**

**13.** In a drawing of a lung section, an alveolus that is 0.25 mm across in the tissue is drawn 45 mm across. Calculate the magnification of the drawing. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** 20 μm cube: SA = 6 × 20² = 2400 μm², V = 8000 μm³, SA:V = **0.3 : 1** (0.3 μm⁻¹) [1]. 40 μm cube: SA = 9600 μm², V = 64 000 μm³, SA:V = **0.15 : 1** [1].

**(b)** The 20 μm cell: it has the larger SA:V and shorter diffusion distance to its centre, so oxygen reaches all of its cytoplasm faster in relation to its needs [1]. **[3]**

*Examiner insight:* Doubling the side halves the ratio; state the ratio in the same units for both cells so they can be compared.

**2. (a)** Stretch when the alveoli fill and recoil to help push air out [1].

**(b)** Contracts to narrow the airway, controlling the flow of air to the alveoli [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Any two [1 each]: cartilage, which holds the airway open; ciliated epithelium, whose cilia move mucus up away from the lungs; goblet cells, which secrete mucus to trap particles and microorganisms; smooth muscle, which narrows the airway. **[2]**

**4.** The hair-like extension greatly increases the surface area for absorption [1]. It has a thin cell wall and membrane, so the distance for water and ions to cross is short (and many mitochondria supply ATP for active uptake of ions) [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Tidal volume is the volume of air breathed in or out in one normal breath at rest [1]. Vital capacity is the largest volume that can be breathed out after the deepest possible breath in [1]. **[2]**

**6.** The internal intercostal muscles contract, pulling the ribs down and in [1]. The abdominal muscles contract, pushing the diaphragm up [1]. The volume of the thorax decreases quickly [1], so pressure in the lungs rises well above atmospheric pressure and air is forced out [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* A cough is forced expiration; answers that describe only the passive recoil of resting expiration miss the muscle marks.

**7. (a)** Rest: 0.50 × 12 = **6.0 dm³ min⁻¹**. Exercise: 1.6 × 30 = **48 dm³ min⁻¹** [2].

**(b)** (48 − 6.0) ÷ 6.0 × 100 = **700 %** [1]. **[3]**

**8. (a)** Fall = 4.10 − 3.62 = 0.48 dm³ in 1.5 min [1]. Rate = 0.48 ÷ 1.5 = **0.32 dm³ min⁻¹** [1].

**(b)** Carbon dioxide in the exhaled air is absorbed by soda lime [1], so the volume in the chamber falls by the volume of oxygen absorbed into the blood [1]. **[4]**

**9. (a)** Blood and water flow in opposite directions across the lamellae [1]. Blood always meets water with a higher oxygen concentration than itself, so a diffusion gradient is kept along the whole lamella [1]. With parallel flow the two would reach equilibrium partway along and diffusion would stop, so less oxygen would be absorbed [1].

**(b)** Without water to support them, the gill filaments and lamellae stick together [1], so the surface area for gas exchange is greatly reduced (and the surfaces dry out) [1]. **[5]**

**10.** Flight muscle respires anaerobically as well as aerobically, producing lactate, which lowers the water potential of the muscle cells [1]. Water moves out of the tracheole ends into the cells by osmosis [1]. More of the tracheole wall is then in contact with air, so oxygen diffuses to the cells faster, as gases diffuse faster in air than in liquid [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Advantage: less water vapour is lost by evaporation from the tracheal system [1]. Disadvantage: less oxygen enters and carbon dioxide builds up, so the rate of gas exchange (and so activity) is limited [1]. **[2]**

**12.** The trachea has C-shaped rings of cartilage; a small bronchiole has no cartilage [1]. The trachea has ciliated epithelium with many goblet cells; small bronchioles have few or no goblet cells and may lack cilia [1]. The bronchiole wall is mainly smooth muscle and elastic fibres, and its lumen is much narrower [1]. **[3]**

**13.** Magnification = image size ÷ actual size = 45 ÷ 0.25 [1] = **× 180** [1]. **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* Image and actual sizes must be in the same units before dividing.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying alveoli contain cartilage, cilia or goblet cells.
- Describing passive expiration when the question is about forced expiration.
- Forgetting that soda lime removes carbon dioxide from the spirometer.
- Explaining countercurrent flow without the gradient "along the whole length".
- Calling an increase from 6.0 to 48 an increase of 800 %; it is 700 %.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to know the numbers for lung volumes?**
No fixed values are required. You need to read volumes from a trace and use them, for example tidal volume × breathing rate for ventilation rate.

**Why does the fish gill question ask about air?**
It tests 3.1.1(b): the efficiency of an exchange surface depends on its large surface area, which the gill can only keep when supported by water.

**Can an insect ventilate its tracheal system?**
Yes. Larger, active insects use thoracic and abdominal movements to change their body volume and pump air through the tracheae, as well as relying on diffusion.
