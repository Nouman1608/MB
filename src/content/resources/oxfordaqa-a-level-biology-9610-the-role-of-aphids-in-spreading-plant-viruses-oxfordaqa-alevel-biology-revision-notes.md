For explanations, experimental evidence and worked field data, use the [aphids and plant viruses study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-biology-9610-the-role-of-aphids-in-spreading-plant-viruses-oxfordaqa-alevel-biology/); then try the [aphids and plant viruses practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-biology-9610-the-role-of-aphids-in-spreading-plant-viruses-oxfordaqa-alevel-biology-practice/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Plant virus disease | A disease of plants caused by a virus; responsible for losses in crop production and quality worldwide |
| Vector | A living organism that carries a pathogen from one host to another |
| Aphid | A small insect that feeds on phloem sap and acts as a vector of plant viruses |
| Stylets | The aphid's sucking mouthparts, inserted into phloem vessels |
| Phloem sap | Solution of sucrose and other organic substances carried in the phloem, under high pressure |
| Mass flow hypothesis | Explanation of translocation: high hydrostatic pressure at a source drives sap to a sink |
| Winged female | Aphid form produced by some species that migrates to start colonies on new host plants |
| Acellular | Not made of cells; viruses rely on host cells to replicate |

## Plant virus diseases (3.2.9.1)

- Many important plant diseases are caused by viruses.
- They reduce crop production (yield) and quality in all parts of the world.
- Symptoms depend on the disease: yellowing of leaves, leaf distortion, abnormalities of other parts.
- Plants are immobile, so transmission usually involves vectors such as aphids.

**Symptoms to yield:**

| Symptom | Chain |
|---|---|
| Yellowing | Less chlorophyll → less light absorbed → less photosynthesis → less sugar for growth and storage → lower yield |
| Distortion | Less leaf area facing light → less photosynthesis → lower yield |
| Other abnormalities | Stunted or misshapen plants, fruits or storage organs → lower yield and quality |

## Why vectors are needed

- Plants cannot move to contact each other.
- Plant cells have cell walls, so virus particles cannot simply enter.
- Viruses cannot move themselves.
- A vector moves between plants and delivers virus particles directly into the phloem.

## Aphid feeding (3.2.9.2)

1. Stylets pushed into the plant.
2. Stylets reach and puncture a phloem vessel.
3. Sap under high pressure is forced into the aphid's gut.

**Why the pressure:** sucrose loaded at the source lowers water potential in the phloem → water enters by osmosis → high hydrostatic pressure.

## Transmission chain

Infected plant → aphid feeds, virus enters stylets and gut → aphid moves (walks, or flies if winged female) → feeds on healthy plant, virus introduced into phloem → virus carried in phloem sap around the plant → host cells replicate virus → symptoms.

**Winged females:** migrate to new host plants, start colonies, so carry viruses over longer distances, to other parts of a field or other fields.

## Aphids as an experimental tool

- Aphid feeds; aphid anaesthetised; body removed; stylet left in phloem.
- Sap flows out of the stylet: evidence that phloem contents are under pressure (supports mass flow).
- Sap analysed: shows sucrose and other organic substances.
- Comparing samples at different positions shows concentration gradients from source to sink.

## Interpreting field data

- Convert to percentages of plants infected.
- Calculate new infections per week from cumulative totals.
- Look for a time lag between aphid numbers and symptoms (time for the virus to multiply).
- A rise together is a correlation; stronger evidence: test aphids for virus, or compare protected and unprotected plants.

## Exam traps

- "Aphids bite leaves." They insert stylets into phloem.
- "Aphids suck hard." Phloem pressure forces sap in.
- "Aphids feed on xylem." Phloem.
- "Plants infect each other." Vectors are usually needed.
- "Yellowing reduces yield" with no link to chlorophyll and photosynthesis.
- Reading cumulative totals as weekly new cases.
- Calling a correlation proof.

## Quick self-test

1. State two kinds of loss caused by plant virus diseases.
2. Give three types of symptom named in the specification.
3. Explain how yellowing of leaves reduces crop yield.
4. Why do plant viruses usually need a vector?
5. What are stylets?
6. Which tissue do aphids feed from?
7. Why does an aphid not need to suck hard to feed?
8. Explain why phloem sap is under high pressure.
9. What is the role of winged female aphids in the spread of a virus?
10. How can aphid stylets be used to show that phloem contents are under pressure?

### Answers

1. Losses in crop production (yield) and in quality of produce.
2. Yellowing of leaves, leaf distortion, abnormalities of other parts of the plant.
3. Less chlorophyll, so less light absorbed and less photosynthesis; less sugar is available for growth and storage.
4. Plants are immobile, plant cells have cell walls, and viruses cannot move themselves, so something must carry them into the plant.
5. The aphid's sucking mouthparts, inserted into phloem vessels.
6. The phloem.
7. Phloem sap is under high pressure, so it is forced into the aphid's gut once a phloem vessel is punctured.
8. Sucrose loaded at the source lowers the water potential, so water enters by osmosis, raising the hydrostatic pressure.
9. They migrate to new host plants and start colonies, carrying the virus over longer distances.
10. Remove the feeding aphid's body, leaving the stylet in the phloem; sap continues to flow out of the cut stylet.
11. 55 − 25 = **30**.
12. The virus takes time to multiply in the plant before symptoms are visible.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to name particular plant viruses?**
No. The specification describes plant virus diseases in general and their symptoms. Questions supply the details of any named disease.

**How does this section link to mass flow?**
Aphids feed because phloem sap is under high pressure, which is the basis of the mass flow hypothesis, and their stylets are used experimentally to sample phloem sap.
