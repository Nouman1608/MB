## What diffraction is

> **Diffraction** is the spreading of a wave as it passes through a gap or
> around the edge of an obstacle.

Three points sharpen this definition:

- **What does not change.** The wavelength, frequency and speed are the same
after diffraction as before, because the wave stays in the same medium. In a
ripple tank, the spacing of the wavefronts is the same on both sides of the
gap.
- **What does change.** The direction of travel of parts of the wavefront,
and so the region the wave reaches. Because the energy spreads over a wider
region, the amplitude beyond the gap is smaller than before it.
- **Not refraction.** Refraction is a change of direction caused by a change
of speed at a boundary between media, and it changes the wavelength.
Diffraction happens within one medium and changes neither.

All waves diffract: water waves, sound, light, microwaves and every other
electromagnetic wave. Diffraction is a wave property, and showing it is one
way of showing that something behaves as a wave.

## The ripple tank experiment

**Set-up.** A straight bar dipper vibrates on the water surface, producing
plane (straight) wavefronts. Two barriers are placed across the tank with an
adjustable gap between them. A lamp above the tank projects the wave pattern
onto a screen or paper below, where bright lines show the crests. A
stroboscope can make the pattern appear stationary so it can be sketched or
photographed.

**What to vary and what you see:**

| Change | Observation |
|---|---|
| Gap much wider than the wavelength | Most of the wavefront passes straight through; only the ends of the wavefronts curve, near the edges of the gap; a clear "shadow" region behind each barrier |
| Gap narrowed until it is about equal to the wavelength | Wavefronts emerge almost as semicircles, spreading into the region behind the barriers |
| Gap kept fixed, frequency of the dipper decreased | Wavelength increases, so the spreading increases |
| Gap kept fixed, frequency increased | Wavelength decreases, so the spreading decreases |
| Single barrier edge (no gap) | Wavefronts curve round the edge into the region behind it |

The pattern of the whole experiment reduces to one qualitative rule:

> The spreading is greatest when the gap width is **similar to the
> wavelength**; when the gap is much wider than the wavelength, there is
> little spreading.

It is the **ratio** of wavelength to gap width that matters, not either
quantity on its own. Doubling both leaves the pattern unchanged in shape.

**Worked example.** The dipper vibrates at 10 Hz, and the waves travel at
0.20 m s⁻¹. Predict what is seen with gaps of 2.0 cm and 10 cm.

```
lambda = v / f = 0.20 / 10 = 0.020 m = 2.0 cm

gap 2.0 cm: gap similar to lambda -> strong spreading, near-semicircular wavefronts
gap 10 cm:  gap = 5 lambda       -> little spreading, straight wavefronts with curved ends
```

Behind both gaps the wavefronts are still 2.0 cm apart.

A gap can also be made **narrower** than the wavelength. The wave still
spreads widely, but much less energy gets through, so the diffracted waves
are weak. For a clear demonstration, a gap about equal to the wavelength is
best.

## Other demonstrations

**Sound.** Speech has wavelengths around 1 m (for example, a 340 Hz sound in
air has λ = 340 ÷ 340 = 1.0 m). That is comparable to the width of a doorway,
so sound diffracts strongly through it, and you hear someone speaking in the
next room without seeing them.

**Light.** Visible light has wavelengths of about 5 × 10⁻⁷ m. A doorway about
0.9 m wide is nearly two million times wider than this, so light passing
through it shows no noticeable spreading, which is why the room beyond casts
sharp shadows. Diffraction of light *is* observed when it passes through a
very narrow slit (a fraction of a millimetre wide): a laser beam passing
through such a slit spreads out into a pattern on a screen, and the pattern
becomes **wider** as the slit is made **narrower**.

**Microwaves.** Microwaves from school apparatus have wavelengths of a few
centimetres, so gaps between metal plates a few centimetres wide produce
clear diffraction. Moving a receiver in an arc behind the gap shows that the
microwaves reach positions well away from the straight-through direction.

**Comparing waves at one gap.** For the same gap, a longer-wavelength wave
diffracts more. That is why, at a doorway, sound spreads and light does not,
and why lower-pitched sounds spread round obstacles more than higher-pitched
ones.

## Why diffraction matters for the rest of Topic 8

In two-source interference and the diffraction grating (subtopics 8.3 and
8.4), light or microwaves pass through narrow slits. Diffraction at each slit
spreads the waves so that waves from different slits **overlap** and can
superpose. Without diffraction, the beams would pass straight through and no
interference pattern would form.

## Exam technique

- **Explain the meaning of diffraction**: "spreading of a wave as it passes
through a gap or round an obstacle". Avoid "bending", which examiners may
read as refraction.
- **Describe an experiment**: name the apparatus, what is changed, and what
is observed for at least two conditions. State that the wavelength is
unchanged.
- **Explain a difference** (sound round a door but not light): compare each
wavelength with the gap width, giving orders of magnitude.
- **Draw diffracted wavefronts**: keep the spacing equal to the incident
spacing; curve only the ends for a wide gap; draw near-semicircles for a gap
comparable with λ.

## Common mistakes

- Drawing diffracted wavefronts closer together or further apart than the
incident ones.
- Saying diffraction only happens when the gap equals the wavelength; it
always happens, but it is most noticeable then.
- Stating that a wider gap gives more spreading.
- Confusing diffraction with refraction.
- Claiming that light does not diffract.

## Quick revision checklist

- Diffraction: spreading through a gap or round an obstacle
- Wavelength, frequency and speed unchanged; amplitude reduced as energy
spreads
- Ripple tank: wide gap → little spreading; gap ≈ λ → strong spreading
- Longer wavelength for the same gap → more spreading
- Sound diffracts round doorways; light needs very narrow slits
- Diffraction lets waves from separate slits overlap and interfere

## Frequently asked questions

**Does the wavelength change when a wave diffracts?**
No. The wave stays in the same medium, so its speed and frequency, and
therefore its wavelength, are unchanged.

**When is diffraction most noticeable?**
When the gap width is similar to the wavelength of the wave.

**Why can you hear round a corner but not see round it?**
Sound wavelengths are comparable to the size of doorways, so sound diffracts
strongly; light wavelengths are about a million times smaller, so its
diffraction there is negligible.
