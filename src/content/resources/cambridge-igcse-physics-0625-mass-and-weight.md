This guide teaches section 1.3, Mass and weight, of the Cambridge IGCSE Physics 0625 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. Core statements 1 to 4 define mass, weight and gravitational field strength and cover comparing them with a balance. Supplement statement 5, describing weight as the effect of a gravitational field on a mass, is examined only on Papers 2 and 4 and is labelled **Extended only**. The [Motion, forces and energy study guide](/resources/igcse-physics-motion-forces-and-energy/) shows where this section sits in Topic 1; this page goes further into the definitions, the equation and the instruments.

## What the syllabus asks for

| Statement | Tier | Content |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Core | Mass is a measure of the quantity of matter in an object at rest relative to the observer |
| 2 | Core | Weight is a gravitational force on an object that has mass |
| 3 | Core | Gravitational field strength is force per unit mass; recall and use g = W/m; know that g is equivalent to the acceleration of free fall |
| 4 | Core | Weights (and masses) may be compared using a balance |
| 5 | Extended only | Describe, and use the concept of, weight as the effect of a gravitational field on a mass |

## Mass

**Mass** is a measure of the quantity of matter in an object. It is measured in kilograms (kg) and is a scalar. The syllabus wording adds "at rest relative to the observer": the mass you measure is for an object that is not moving relative to you. In practice, at IGCSE, take the point that matters: an object's mass does **not** depend on where it is. A 2.0 kg bag of rice has a mass of 2.0 kg in your kitchen, on a mountain, on the Moon or in a spacecraft.

## Weight

**Weight** is a gravitational force on an object that has mass. As a force it is measured in newtons (N), acts downwards towards the centre of the Earth (or whichever planet or moon the object is on) and is a vector. Because weight depends on how strongly the object is pulled, it **does** change from place to place.

| | Mass | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | quantity of matter | gravitational force |
| Unit | kg | N |
| Scalar or vector | scalar | vector (downwards) |
| Changes with location? | no | yes |
| Measured with | beam balance (comparison) | spring balance (newton meter) |

## Gravitational field strength, g

**Gravitational field strength** is the force per unit mass:

```
g = W / m       g in N/kg,  W in N,  m in kg
W = m g         m = W / g
```

Near the Earth's surface g is about **9.8 N/kg**: each kilogram of mass has a weight of 9.8 N. Other bodies have different values; the Moon's surface value is about 1.6 N/kg and Mars's about 3.7 N/kg.

### Why g is also the acceleration of free fall

Section 1.2 gives the acceleration of free fall near the Earth as about 9.8 m/s². That is the same number as the field strength, 9.8 N/kg, and the syllabus asks you to know the two are equivalent. A freely falling object has only its weight acting on it, so each kilogram is pulled with 9.8 N and speeds up by 9.8 m/s every second. The units agree too: 1 N is the force that gives 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m/s², so 1 N/kg = 1 m/s².

**Worked example 1.** A planetary rover has a mass of 900 kg. Find its weight on the Earth (g = 9.8 N/kg) and on Mars (g = 3.7 N/kg).

```
Earth:  W = m g = 900 × 9.8 = 8820 N  ≈ 8800 N
Mars:   W = m g = 900 × 3.7 = 3330 N  ≈ 3300 N
mass on Mars: still 900 kg
```

**Worked example 2.** On a newly explored moon, a 2.5 kg test mass hangs from a newton meter that reads 4.0 N. Find g there, and the weight of a 70 kg astronaut.

```
g = W / m = 4.0 / 2.5 = 1.6 N/kg
astronaut: W = 70 × 1.6 = 112 N  ≈ 110 N
```

Notice what the question gave you: a force and a mass. Always convert grams to kilograms first: 250 g is 0.250 kg, not 250 kg.

## Measuring and comparing: balances

Statement 4 says weights (and masses) can be **compared using a balance**.

- **Beam balance (two pans).** An unknown object sits in one pan and standard masses are added to the other until the beam is level. The beam balances when the two **weights** are equal. Both pans are in the same gravitational field, so equal weights mean equal **masses**. The method gives the same result on the Moon: everything weighs less, but by the same factor on both sides.
- **Spring balance (newton meter).** The spring stretches in proportion to the force pulling it, so the scale reads **weight** directly, in newtons. Taken to the Moon, the same object gives a smaller reading.
- **Electronic top-pan balance.** It measures the force on its pan but displays the result in grams or kilograms, using Earth's value of g. On the Moon it would show a mass about one sixth of the true value, because the force on it is smaller.

### Finding g in the laboratory

Hang a newton meter from a clamp. Hang masses of 100 g, 200 g, up to 500 g from it in turn, reading the weight each time with your eye level with the pointer. Convert each mass to kilograms and plot weight (y-axis) against mass (x-axis). The points lie on a straight line through the origin, and its **gradient** is g. For example, a gradient of 4.9 N ÷ 0.50 kg = 9.8 N/kg.

## Weight as the effect of a gravitational field (Extended only)

A **gravitational field** is a region in which a mass experiences a force. Weight is the effect of that field on a mass: the field is there whether or not an object is in it, and any mass placed in it is pulled with a force W = mg in the direction of the field.

Using this idea:

- **The field, not the object, sets g.** At a given place every mass experiences the same force per kilogram, so a 1 kg and a 10 kg ball have weights in the ratio 1 : 10 and fall with the same acceleration.
- **g depends on the body and the distance from it.** A body with more mass produces a stronger field at its surface, and the field weakens with distance from the body, so a satellite's weight is less than it was on the launch pad, but not zero.
- **Weight is a vector** pointing along the field, towards the centre of the planet or moon. On opposite sides of the Earth, two people's weights point in opposite directions.

Typical questions: compare the weight of an object on two planets, given g for each; explain why a beam balance gives the same reading on the Moon; explain why a dropped hammer and feather fall together on the airless Moon (same field strength, so the same acceleration for every mass).

## Common mistakes

- Giving weight in kilograms, or mass in newtons.
- Saying mass changes on the Moon. Mass is unchanged; weight is smaller.
- Using grams in g = W/m.
- Writing "gravity" when the question asks for the name of the force: say **weight** or **gravitational force**.
- Saying a beam balance measures weight only. It compares weights, and so compares masses.
- *(Extended)* Saying there is "no gravity" in orbit. The field is weaker but still there.

## Exam technique: command words

- **State** what is meant by mass or weight: use the syllabus definitions, word for word if you can. "Mass is how heavy something is" scores nothing.
- **Define** gravitational field strength: "force per unit mass". Writing only g = W/m usually earns the mark only if the symbols are defined.
- **Calculate**: equation, substitution, answer with unit (N, kg or N/kg).
- **Explain** a balance result: say what is equal at balance (the weights), then why that means the masses are equal (same g on both sides).
- **Describe** *(Extended)*: weight as "the effect of a gravitational field on a mass", then apply W = mg to the situation.
