Revise first with the [ionic bonding study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-chemistry-4ch1-ionic-bonding/) and the [ionic bonding revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-chemistry-4ch1-ionic-bonding-revision-notes/). ## Section A: short questions

**1.** State what is meant by an ion. **[1]**

**2.** Give the charge on the ion formed by each element.

**(a)** potassium **[1]**
**(b)** nitrogen **[1]**
**(c)** bromine **[1]**

**3.** Describe how an oxygen atom forms an oxide ion. Give the electronic configuration of the ion. **[2]**

**4.** Write the formula of each compound.

**(a)** lithium sulfate **[1]**
**(b)** zinc chloride **[1]**
**(c)** iron(III) nitrate **[1]**
**(d)** ammonium nitrate **[1]**

**5.** Give the formula and charge of each ion in lead(II) carbonate. **[2]**

**6.** State what is meant by ionic bonding. **[1]**

## Section B: structured questions

**7.** Sodium reacts with sulfur to form sodium sulfide.

**(a)** Give the electronic configurations of a sodium atom and a sulfur atom. **[1]**
**(b)** Describe, in terms of electron transfer, how sodium sulfide forms. Include the charges on both ions. **[3]**
**(c)** Write the formula of sodium sulfide. **[1]**

**8.** Describe a dot-and-cross diagram for calcium oxide. Show outer electrons only. **[3]**

**9.** The particles Ne, Na⁺, Mg²⁺ and F⁻ all have 10 electrons.

**(a)** Give their shared electronic configuration. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why Mg²⁺ has a charge of 2+ even though it has the same number of electrons as Ne. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why fluorine forms a negative ion but sodium forms a positive ion. **[2]**

**10.** The table gives data for three ionic compounds.

| Compound | Ions | Melting point / °C |
|---|---|---|
| Sodium fluoride | Na⁺, F⁻ | 993 |
| Magnesium oxide | Mg²⁺, O²⁻ | 2852 |
| Potassium chloride | K⁺, Cl⁻ | 770 |

**(a)** Explain why all three compounds have high melting points. **[3]**
**(b)** Suggest why magnesium oxide has a much higher melting point than the other two. **[2]**
**(c)** A student writes: "Magnesium oxide has a high melting point because its covalent bonds are strong." Explain the error. **[1]**

**11.** A student tests solid potassium iodide, molten potassium iodide and potassium iodide solution in a circuit with a bulb.

**(a)** State in which of the three the bulb lights. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the bulb does not light with the solid. **[1]**
**(c)** Another student says the molten compound conducts because "electrons can now flow through it". Explain why this is wrong. **[1]**

**12.** Element X is in Group 2 and element Y is in Group 7.

**(a)** Predict the formula of the compound formed between X and Y. Explain your answer in terms of electrons. **[3]**
**(b)** Describe the arrangement of ions in a solid sample of this compound. **[2]**
**(c)** The compound dissolves in water. State whether the solution conducts electricity and give a reason. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** A charged particle formed when an atom or group of atoms loses or gains electrons [1].

**2. (a)** 1+ [1].
**(b)** 3− [1].
**(c)** 1− [1].

**3.** The oxygen atom (2,6) gains two electrons [1]. The oxide ion is O²⁻ with configuration 2,8 [1].

**4. (a)** Li₂SO₄ [1].
**(b)** ZnCl₂ [1].
**(c)** Fe(NO₃)₃ [1].
**(d)** NH₄NO₃ [1].

**5.** Pb²⁺ [1] and CO₃²⁻ [1].

**6.** The strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions [1].

**7. (a)** Sodium 2,8,1 and sulfur 2,8,6 [1].
**(b)** Each sodium atom loses its one outer electron, forming Na⁺ [1]. A sulfur atom gains two electrons, forming S²⁻ [1]. Two sodium atoms transfer one electron each to one sulfur atom [1].
**(c)** Na₂S [1].
*Marking note:* The ratio mark in (b) needs "two sodium atoms" (or equivalent); "sodium gives sulfur two electrons" from a single atom is wrong.

**8.** Ca shown losing 2 outer electrons (crosses) and O with 6 outer electrons (dots) [1]. Final diagram: [Ca]²⁺ and [O]²⁻, each in brackets with charges [1]. The oxide ion has 8 outer electrons: 6 dots and 2 crosses [1].

**9. (a)** 2,8 [1].
**(b)** Mg²⁺ has 12 protons [1] but only 10 electrons, so it has two more positive charges than negative charges [1].
**(c)** Fluorine has 7 outer electrons and gains 1 to complete its outer shell [1]. Sodium has 1 outer electron and loses it, leaving a full outer shell [1].

**10. (a)** They have giant ionic lattices [1]. There are strong electrostatic attractions between oppositely charged ions [1]. A lot of energy is needed to overcome the many attractions [1].
**(b)** Its ions have higher charges (2+ and 2−) [1], so the electrostatic attractions between them are stronger [1].
**(c)** Magnesium oxide is ionic, not covalent; its melting point depends on attractions between ions [1].

**11. (a)** Molten [1] and solution [1].
**(b)** The ions are held in fixed positions and cannot move [1].
**(c)** It is the ions that move and carry the charge, not electrons [1].

**12. (a)** XY₂ [1]. X loses 2 electrons to form X²⁺ [1]; each Y gains 1 electron to form Y⁻, so two Y⁻ ions balance one X²⁺ [1].
**(b)** A regular, giant three-dimensional lattice [1] of alternating positive and negative ions, each surrounded by ions of the opposite charge [1].
**(c)** It conducts [1], because the ions are free to move and carry charge [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing ion formation as sharing.
- Forgetting the ratio of atoms in electron transfer.
- Missing brackets on compound ions in formulae.
- Using "molecules" or "covalent bonds" for ionic compounds.
- Saying electrons carry the current in molten salts.
