The section is assessed in Paper 1 Geographical Themes, where you answer three 25-mark questions, one from each section; Paper 2 Geographical Skills may also use data on the themes, as in Questions 5 and 6. ## Questions

**1.** Define the term "sustainable development". **[2]**

**2.** Define the term "desertification". **[2]**

**3.** Explain the difference between the natural greenhouse effect and enhanced global warming. **[3]**

**4.** Explain how overgrazing can lead to desertification. **[4]**

**5.** Study the table. It shows soil loss measured on slopes of the same angle in the Tarvel Hills, a fictional area.

| Land use | Soil loss (tonnes per hectare per year) |
|---|---|
| Natural forest | 0.5 |
| Grazed grassland | 4.0 |
| Maize, ploughed up and down the slope | 36.0 |
| Maize, contour ploughed | 14.4 |
| Maize on terraces | 3.6 |

**(a)** Calculate how many times greater soil loss is under maize ploughed up and down the slope than under natural forest. Show your working. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage reduction in soil loss when maize is grown on terraces instead of being ploughed up and down the slope. **[2]**
**(c)** A farmer grows maize on 12 hectares. Calculate how many more tonnes of soil are lost each year if the land is ploughed up and down the slope rather than terraced. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why contour ploughing and terraces reduce soil loss. **[3]**

**6.** A line graph shows dissolved oxygen in the River Sella, a fictional river, at five sites. A paper mill (fictional) discharges waste water at the 0 km point. The readings are: 2 km upstream, 9.2 mg per litre; 0.5 km downstream, 3.1; 2 km downstream, 4.4; 5 km downstream, 6.0; 10 km downstream, 7.9. Fish need at least 5 mg per litre to survive for long.

**(a)** Describe the changes in dissolved oxygen shown by the graph. Use data in your answer. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the mean dissolved oxygen of the four downstream sites, and the range for all five sites. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest two reasons why dissolved oxygen rises again further downstream. **[2]**
**(d)** Suggest how this pollution could affect people living along the river. **[3]**

**7.** Read the source.

> Brennick Minerals (a fictional company) plans a limestone quarry and cement works on Ardmore Ridge, 2 km from the fictional village of Calder Bay. The village has 900 residents, a small tourist trade and a river used for fishing. The plan would create 150 jobs. There would be blasting each weekday, about 80 lorries a day on the village road, and a 60 m chimney for the cement kiln, which would burn coal.

**(a)** Identify two types of pollution that the plan could cause. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how the plan could threaten the environment and people, locally and globally. **[5]**
**(c)** Suggest two ways in which the plan could be managed more sustainably. **[4]**

**8.** Explain why resource conservation is important. Use examples. **[5]**

**9.** Describe how noise and visual pollution from economic activities can affect people. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how economic activities in one country can harm the environment in other countries or globally. **[4]**

**11.** For a named area where economic development is putting the environment at risk, describe the threats and explain how far attempts to manage them have succeeded. **[8]**

**12.** "The benefits of economic development are greater than the environmental risks it causes." To what extent do you agree? Refer to examples. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Development that meets the needs of people today [1] without reducing the ability of future generations to meet their own needs, for example by not using up resources or damaging land [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The future-generations idea earns the second mark; "development that protects the environment" alone is too vague.

**2.** The degradation of land so that it becomes more like a desert [1], in arid or semi-arid areas, caused by human activity and drought [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Desertification is land being damaged, not a desert moving; answers that say "the desert spreads" without the idea of degradation score one mark at most.

**3.** The natural greenhouse effect is when gases such as carbon dioxide and methane trap heat in the atmosphere [1], keeping the Earth warm enough for life [1]. Enhanced global warming is the extra warming caused when human activities, such as burning fossil fuels, add more of these gases [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The key difference is the human cause; an answer that describes only one of the two processes cannot reach full marks.

**4.** Too many animals eat the grass and shrubs faster than they can regrow [1]. The bare soil is exposed to wind and heavy rain, so topsoil is eroded [1]. Hooves trample and compact the soil, so water runs off instead of soaking in [1]. With fewer plants and less fertile soil, the land can no longer support farming and becomes more desert-like [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Build a chain of linked steps from animals to bare soil to erosion to desert-like land; four separate facts with no links score lower.

**5. (a)** 36.0 ÷ 0.5 [1] = 72 times greater [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** (36.0 − 3.6) ÷ 36.0 × 100 [1] = 90% reduction [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Up and down: 36.0 × 12 = 432 tonnes; terraces: 3.6 × 12 = 43.2 tonnes [1]. Difference: 432 − 43.2 = 388.8 tonnes [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Contour ploughing makes furrows along the slope, which trap water instead of channelling it downhill [1]. Terraces break the slope into flat steps, so running water slows down [1]. Slower water has less energy to carry soil, and more water soaks into the ground, so less soil is washed away [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* In part (c) show both totals before subtracting; a final answer with no working gets nothing if it is wrong.

**6. (a)** Dissolved oxygen falls sharply from 9.2 mg per litre upstream to 3.1 mg per litre 0.5 km below the mill, a fall of 6.1 [1]. It then rises steadily with distance downstream, to 7.9 mg per litre at 10 km [1]. It stays below the 5 mg per litre fish need until somewhere between 2 and 5 km downstream, and by 10 km it has still not returned to the upstream level [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Mean: (3.1 + 4.4 + 6.0 + 7.9) ÷ 4 = 5.35 mg per litre [1]. Range: 9.2 − 3.1 = 6.1 mg per litre [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Tributaries bring in clean water that dilutes the waste [1]. Turbulent flow over rapids mixes oxygen from the air into the water, and bacteria finish breaking down the waste, so they use less oxygen [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Fish die near the mill, so people who fish lose food and income [1]. River water may be unsafe to drink or need costly treatment [1]. The polluted river may smell and look unpleasant, reducing its use for washing or recreation [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Describe" with "use data" needs figures with units and a reference to distance; a general statement that oxygen "goes down then up" earns one mark.

**7. (a)** Noise pollution from blasting or lorries [1]; air pollution from the chimney or dust [1]. (Visual pollution from the quarry is also acceptable.) **[2]**
**(b)** Dust from blasting could settle on homes and cause breathing problems for the 900 residents [1]. Eighty lorries a day would add noise, fumes and danger on the village road [1]. Silt or dust washed into the river could harm fish and the fishing that villagers rely on [1]. A large scar on Ardmore Ridge would spoil the view and could reduce the tourist trade [1]. Globally, burning coal in the kiln releases carbon dioxide, adding to enhanced global warming [1]. **[5]**
**(c)** Limit blasting to set hours and build earth banks or plant trees as screens [1], which reduce noise and visual impact for villagers [1]. Use settling ponds to trap silt and fit filters on the chimney [1], which protect the river's fish and reduce dust in the air [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the source's own details, such as the 80 lorries or the fishing river; a generic list of quarry impacts does not reach the top marks.

**8.** Non-renewable resources such as oil and coal are finite, so conserving them makes them last longer for future generations [1]. Renewable resources can still run out if used too fast; for example, overfishing can collapse fish stocks [1]. Using less fuel reduces greenhouse gas emissions and so slows enhanced global warming [1]. Recycling metals means fewer new mines, which reduces visual pollution and habitat loss [1]. Using resources efficiently saves money for households, firms and governments [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Each reason needs an example or a consequence; "so we do not run out" repeated in different words earns one mark.

**9.** Noise from airports, motorways or factories can disturb sleep [1] and cause stress, affecting people's health and work [1]. Visual pollution such as quarries, spoil heaps or pylons spoils the landscape [1], which can lower house prices or put tourists off, reducing local income [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The question asks about both types; an answer on noise alone can reach only half marks.

**10.** Burning fossil fuels in one country releases carbon dioxide that mixes in the global atmosphere [1], adding to enhanced global warming and sea-level rise that affect low-lying coasts everywhere [1]. Sulphur dioxide from power stations can be blown across borders and fall as acid rain, damaging forests and lakes in other countries [1]. A river polluted by industry upstream carries the pollution into a neighbouring country or the sea [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the pathway, such as wind, river or atmosphere; naming a global problem without explaining how it travels earns half the marks.

**11.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point; these are indicative creditworthy points for the Niger Delta, Nigeria. Named area: the Niger Delta, where oil was found at Oloibiri in 1956 [1]. Threat: oil spills from pipelines and wells pollute creeks, mangroves and farmland [1]. Threat to people: at Bodo, two spills that began in 2008 polluted creeks and harmed fishing income [1]. Threat: gas flaring releases carbon dioxide and soot, adding to local air pollution and enhanced global warming [1]. Evidence: UNEP's 2011 assessment found benzene in one community's drinking water about 900 times the WHO guideline [1]. Management: Shell agreed in 2015 to pay £55 million to Bodo after a London court case [1]. Management: the government clean-up agency HYPREP launched the Ogoniland clean-up in 2016, including mangrove restoration [1]. Judgement: success is limited, because clean-up is slow, new spills continue and UNEP estimated recovery could take up to 30 years [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* "How far" needs a judgement on success, backed by evidence; describing management without saying whether it worked keeps an answer in the lower levels.

**12.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point; these are indicative creditworthy points. Define economic development as growth in jobs, incomes and services, and identify the named risks: soil erosion, desertification, enhanced global warming and pollution [1]. Agree: oil and gas have provided most of Nigeria's export earnings, paying for services [1]. Agree: new industries such as a quarry bring jobs to areas with few alternatives [1]. Agree: richer countries can afford cleaner technology and environmental laws [1]. Disagree: in the Niger Delta, spills and flaring harmed fishing, farming and health [1]. Disagree: the costs of repair are high; UNEP estimated about US$1 billion for the first five years in Ogoniland [1]. Disagree: overgrazing and overcultivation contribute to desertification in the Sahel, destroying the land people depend on [1]. Disagree: enhanced global warming harms people who did not benefit from the development that caused it [1]. Evaluation: the balance depends on management, such as terracing, enforced laws and schemes like the Great Green Wall [1]. Judgement: benefits can be greater only when development is managed sustainably; unmanaged, the risks often outweigh them [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* A "to what extent" answer needs both sides with real examples and a final judgement that follows from them, not a list of benefits followed by a list of problems.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "global warming" without saying the extra warming comes from human emissions.
- Defining desertification as a desert spreading, with no idea of land degradation.
- Skipping working in calculations such as Questions 5 and 6, or leaving out units.
- Describing a graph with no figures or distances, as in Question 6(a).
- Ignoring the source's details in an application question like Question 7.
- Answering about the environment only when the question also asks about people.
- Covering only one of two named types, as in Question 9.
- Case study answers with no place names, dates or evidence.
- Writing about management without judging how far it has succeeded.
