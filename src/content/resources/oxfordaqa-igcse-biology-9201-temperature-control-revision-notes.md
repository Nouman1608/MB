Condensed recall for **3.4.4 Temperature control** of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Biology (9201)
specification, Version 5.2, for exams in May/June 2018 onwards. The qualification is untiered.

For full explanations and worked examples, read the [Temperature control study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-9201-temperature-control/).
Then try the [Temperature control practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-9201-temperature-control-practice/).
For the general idea of a control system, see the [Homeostasis revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-9201-homeostasis-revision-notes/).

## The essentials

- Body temperature is **monitored and controlled by the thermoregulatory centre in the brain**.
- The centre has **receptors sensitive to the temperature of the blood** flowing through the brain.
- **Temperature receptors in the skin** send impulses to the centre, giving information about skin temperature.
- The name "hypothalamus" is **not required**.

```
too hot or too cold → receptors (brain and skin) → thermoregulatory centre → effectors → response
```

## Core temperature too high

| Response | Effect |
|---|---|
| Blood vessels supplying the skin capillaries **dilate** | More blood flows through the skin capillaries; more energy is transferred from the skin to the environment |
| Sweat glands release **more sweat** | Sweat **evaporates**, which cools the body |

More water is lost when it is hot, so **more fluid must be taken in** (drink or food).

## Core temperature too low

| Response | Effect |
|---|---|
| Blood vessels supplying the skin capillaries **constrict** | Less blood flows through the skin capillaries; less energy is transferred from the skin to the surroundings |
| Muscles may **shiver** | Contraction needs **respiration**, which transfers some energy to warm the body |

## Comparison

| | Too hot | Too cold |
|---|---|---|
| Skin blood vessels | Dilate | Constrict |
| Blood flow near the surface | Increases | Decreases |
| Sweat | More | Little |
| Shivering | No | May occur |
| Energy transferred to surroundings | More | Less |

## Calculations

```
rate = change ÷ time
fluid shortfall = fluid lost − fluid replaced
```

Worked reminder 1: a player sweats 0.9 dm³ per hour for 2 hours and drinks 0.4 dm³ three times.
Lost = 0.9 × 2 = 1.8 dm³. Drunk = 0.4 × 3 = 1.2 dm³. Shortfall = 1.8 − 1.2 = **0.6 dm³**.

Worked reminder 2: skin temperature of a hand falls from 32.0 °C to 24.0 °C in 10 minutes.
Fall = 8.0 °C. Rate = 8.0 ÷ 10 = **0.8 °C per minute**.

Core temperature stays almost steady while skin temperature changes a lot with the surroundings, which shows the control
system at work.

## Order of events: a quick recap

1. The surroundings change, or the body produces more energy (for example during exercise).
2. Receptors in the brain (blood temperature) and in the skin (skin temperature) detect the change.
3. The thermoregulatory centre receives and processes the information.
4. Effectors respond: sweat glands, skin blood vessels, or muscles.
5. The temperature returns towards normal.

## Hot and cold in one picture

| Question | If too hot | If too cold |
|---|---|---|
| What happens to skin blood vessels? | Dilate | Constrict |
| What happens to the flow of blood through skin capillaries? | More | Less |
| What does this do to the energy transferred to the surroundings? | Increases it | Reduces it |
| What else happens? | More sweat; evaporation cools the body | Muscles may shiver; respiration transfers energy |
| What must be done about water? | Take in more fluid to replace sweat | Nothing extra |

## Reading data

- A table of core and skin temperature at different room temperatures: the core stays almost the same, the skin
  changes a lot. This shows control of the **core**.
- A graph of skin temperature falling in cold water: calculate the rate of fall from the change divided by the time.
- Fluid questions: lost minus replaced gives the shortfall.

## Model answers to learn

- **How does sweating cool the body? (2 marks):** "Sweat glands release more sweat. The sweat evaporates and this cools the body."
- **Why do skin blood vessels dilate when you are hot? (2 marks):** "More blood flows through the skin capillaries, so more energy is transferred from the skin to the environment."
- **Why does a cold person shiver? (2 marks):** "The muscles contract and need respiration, which transfers some energy to warm the body."
- **Why must a person drink more in hot weather? (1 mark):** "More water is lost in sweat, so more fluid must be taken in to balance it."

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Core temperature | Temperature of the inside of the body, such as the blood flowing through the brain |
| Thermoregulatory centre | The centre in the brain that monitors and controls body temperature |
| Dilate | Widen, so more blood flows |
| Constrict | Narrow, so less blood flows |
| Evaporate | Change from liquid to gas, taking energy from the body |
| Shivering | Rapid muscle contraction that needs respiration and transfers energy |
| Capillaries | The smallest blood vessels, supplying the skin |

## Exam traps

- Blood vessels **dilate** or **constrict**; they do not move.
- Sweat cools by **evaporating**, not by being cold.
- Shivering warms the body because of **respiration**, not because the muscles "rub".
- Receptors in the **brain** detect blood temperature; those in the **skin** detect skin temperature.
- A response needs its **effect** (for example, more energy is transferred to the environment).
- Do not forget the **fluid replacement** point.

## Self-test

1. Where is body temperature monitored and controlled?
2. What do the receptors in the thermoregulatory centre detect?
3. What do the receptors in the skin detect?
4. What happens to the blood vessels supplying the skin capillaries if the core temperature is too high?
5. What do the sweat glands do if the core temperature is too high?
6. Why does sweating cool the body?
7. What happens to the skin blood vessels if the core temperature is too low?
8. Why does shivering warm the body?
9. Why must more fluid be taken in when it is hot?
10. A hand's skin temperature falls from 30.0 °C to 21.0 °C in 12 minutes. Calculate the rate of fall.

### Answers

1. By the thermoregulatory centre in the brain.
2. The temperature of the blood flowing through the brain.
3. Skin temperature (the temperature at the surface of the body).
4. They dilate, so more blood flows through the skin capillaries.
5. They release more sweat.
6. The sweat evaporates, which cools the body.
7. They constrict, so less blood flows through the skin capillaries.
8. The muscle contraction needs respiration, which transfers some energy to warm the body.
9. More water is lost in sweat when it is hot.
10. (30.0 − 21.0) ÷ 12 = 9.0 ÷ 12 = **0.75 °C per minute**.
