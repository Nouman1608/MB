## 15.6 Naming and drawing aldehydes and ketones

Aldehydes and ketones share one functional group: C=O, the carbonyl group.

- In an **aldehyde** the carbonyl carbon is at the end of the chain and carries a hydrogen atom. The group is written –CHO. The suffix is **-al**, and the CHO carbon is always C1, so no locant is needed.
- In a **ketone** the carbonyl carbon is bonded to two carbon atoms. The suffix is **-one**, with a locant for the carbonyl carbon (propanone and butanone need none, because there is only one possible position).

Name the longest chain containing the carbonyl carbon, give the carbonyl the lowest number, then add side chains alphabetically.

### Worked example 1: naming

(a) (CH₃)₃CCHO. The chain containing CHO has three carbons: propanal. C2 carries two methyl groups. Answer: **2,2-dimethylpropanal**.

(b) CH₃COCH(CH₃)CH₂CH₃. The longest chain through C=O has five carbons. From the left, C=O is C2; from the right, C4. Take C2. The methyl group is on C3. Answer: **3-methylpentan-2-one**.

(c) A skeletal formula shows a zigzag of four lines. The =O is drawn on the carbon at the first bend, next to one end. That is a five-carbon chain with C=O at C2: **pentan-2-one**.

### Three ways to draw them

Structural formula: CH₃CH₂COCH₃ (butanone). A displayed formula draws every bond:

```
    H   H       H
    |   |       |
H — C — C — C — C — H
    |   |   ‖   |
    H   H   O   H
```

Skeletal formula: draw the carbon zigzag, leave out C and H atoms, and draw the =O on the correct corner. For an aldehyde, the =O sits on the end of a line, and you may write the end group as CHO.

## 15.7 Physical properties

### i No hydrogen bonds between carbonyl molecules

Hydrogen bonding needs a hydrogen atom bonded directly to N, O or F. In aldehydes and ketones every hydrogen is bonded to carbon, so the molecules **cannot hydrogen bond to each other**. They have London forces plus permanent dipole–permanent dipole forces from the polar C=O bond.

### Worked example 2: comparing boiling temperatures

Use these values. All four compounds have Mr close to 58–60.

| Compound | Mr | Boiling temperature / °C |
|---|---|---|
| Butane | 58 | −1 |
| Propanal | 58 | 48 |
| Propanone | 58 | 56 |
| Propan-1-ol | 60 | 97 |

The London forces are similar in size, because the molecules have similar numbers of electrons. Butane has only London forces, so it boils lowest. Propanal and propanone add permanent dipole–dipole attractions from C=O, which need more energy to overcome. Propan-1-ol boils highest because its O–H group lets its molecules form hydrogen bonds with each other; these are the strongest of the intermolecular forces here. No covalent bonds break on boiling.

### ii Hydrogen bonds with water

A carbonyl oxygen has two lone pairs and a δ− charge. It can accept a hydrogen bond from the δ+ hydrogen of a water molecule (O–H····O=C). That is why the small members dissolve well: propanone mixes with water in all proportions. As the hydrocarbon chain grows, more of the molecule cannot hydrogen bond, and breaking water's own hydrogen bonds to fit it in is not repaid, so solubility falls.

## 15.8 i Oxidation: telling aldehydes from ketones

An aldehyde has a hydrogen on the carbonyl carbon, so it is oxidised easily to a carboxylic acid. Ketones lack this hydrogen, so these mild reagents leave them unchanged. The specification lets you write the oxidising agent as [O]:

RCHO + [O] → RCOOH

| Reagent and conditions | Aldehyde | Ketone |
|---|---|---|
| Fehling's or Benedict's solution, warm | Blue solution gives a **red precipitate** (copper(I) oxide) | Stays blue |
| Tollens' reagent (ammoniacal silver nitrate), warm in a water bath | **Silver mirror** forms on the tube (or a grey-black precipitate) | No change |
| Potassium dichromate(VI) with dilute sulfuric acid, warm | **Orange to green** | Stays orange |

What is reduced? In Fehling's and Benedict's, copper(II) is reduced to copper(I). In Tollens' reagent, Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag. With acidified dichromate, orange Cr₂O₇²⁻ ions become green Cr³⁺ ions. Fehling's, Benedict's and Tollens' are alkaline, so the acid formed there is present as its carboxylate ion.

To make Tollens' reagent, add a drop of sodium hydroxide solution to silver nitrate solution, then dilute ammonia dropwise until the brown precipitate just dissolves.

### Worked example 3: how much silver?

1.71 g of heptanal, CH₃(CH₂)₅CHO, reacts completely with Tollens' reagent. Each aldehyde molecule loses two electrons when it is oxidised, so 2 mol Ag form per mole of aldehyde. Use Ar: C 12.0, H 1.0, O 16.0, Ag 107.9.

```
Mr(C₇H₁₄O)  = 114.0
n(heptanal) = 1.71 ÷ 114.0 = 0.0150 mol
n(Ag)       = 2 × 0.0150 = 0.0300 mol
m(Ag)       = 0.0300 × 107.9 = 3.24 g
```

## 15.8 ii Reduction with LiAlH₄

Lithium tetrahydridoaluminate(III), LiAlH₄ (lithium aluminium hydride), in **dry ether (ethoxyethane)** reduces carbonyl compounds to alcohols. The ether must be dry because LiAlH₄ reacts violently with water. Water or dilute acid is added only after the reduction is over. The reducing agent can be written as [H]:

- aldehyde → **primary** alcohol: RCHO + 2[H] → RCH₂OH
- ketone → **secondary** alcohol: RCOR' + 2[H] → RCH(OH)R'

### Worked example 4: reduction and yield

2.15 g of 2,2-dimethylpropanal, (CH₃)₃CCHO, is reduced. Give the product, then the maximum mass, and the yield if 1.76 g is collected.

The product is a primary alcohol, **2,2-dimethylpropan-1-ol**, (CH₃)₃CCH₂OH.

```
(CH₃)₃CCHO + 2[H] → (CH₃)₃CCH₂OH
Mr: C₅H₁₀O = 86.0   C₅H₁₂O = 88.0
n = 2.15 ÷ 86.0 = 0.0250 mol
maximum mass = 0.0250 × 88.0 = 2.20 g
yield = 1.76 ÷ 2.20 × 100 = 80.0%
```

## 15.8 iii Nucleophilic addition of HCN

Hydrogen cyanide adds across C=O to give a **hydroxynitrile**. HCN is a weak acid and barely ionises, so KCN is added to supply cyanide ions, the nucleophile. HCN is extremely toxic, so the reaction needs a fume cupboard.

The C=O bond is polar: C is δ+, O is δ−. The mechanism, drawn with curly arrows, using butanone:

1. Draw a curly arrow that starts at the **lone pair on the C atom of :CN⁻** and ends on the δ+ carbonyl carbon. Another arrow takes the **C=O π bond** pair onto the oxygen.
2. This gives an intermediate with O⁻ on the former carbonyl carbon. A curly arrow goes from a **lone pair on O⁻** to the H of an HCN molecule, and an arrow from the H–C bond to the C of that CN.
3. The product is 2-hydroxy-2-methylbutanenitrile, and CN⁻ is regenerated.

```
      δ+ δ−                   O⁻                       OH
CH₃CH₂–C=O  + :CN⁻  →  CH₃CH₂–C–CH₃  + H–CN  →  CH₃CH₂–C–CH₃  + CN⁻
       |                      |                        |
      CH₃                     CN                       CN
```

Show the dipoles, the lone pair on carbon and the negative charge in your drawing.

### Optical activity as evidence

The carbonyl carbon is trigonal planar: it and its three neighbours sit flat. Cyanide can attack from above or below that plane with **equal probability**. When the new chiral carbon carries four different groups, top and bottom attack each make one enantiomer, so they form 50 : 50: a **racemic mixture**, which does not rotate plane-polarised light. So an optically inactive product supports this mechanism.

### Worked example 5: is the product chiral?

(a) Butanone + HCN → CH₃CH₂C(OH)(CN)CH₃. The central carbon has OH, CN, CH₃ and C₂H₅: four different groups, so it is chiral. The product forms as a racemic mixture, so the sample is **not** optically active.

(b) Pentan-3-one + HCN → (CH₃CH₂)₂C(OH)CN, **2-ethyl-2-hydroxybutanenitrile**. Two of the groups are ethyl, so there is no chiral centre and no optical isomers at all.

Both samples are inactive, for different reasons. Say which applies.

## 15.8 iv The 2,4-DNPH test

2,4-Dinitrophenylhydrazine (2,4-DNPH, Brady's reagent) gives a **yellow-orange precipitate** with any aldehyde or ketone. It shows a carbonyl group is present but not which kind. You do not need the equation.

To identify the compound, filter off the precipitate (the derivative), **recrystallise** it from a minimum of hot solvent, dry it, and measure its melting temperature. Compare with a table of known values. A pure solid melts sharply at its true value; impurities lower the melting temperature and spread it over a range.

### Worked example 6: identifying a carbonyl compound

Use these values for the 2,4-DNPH derivatives.

| Carbonyl compound | Derivative melting temperature / °C |
|---|---|
| Ethanal | 168 |
| Propanal | 156 |
| Propanone | 126 |
| Butanal | 123 |
| Butanone | 115 |

A purified derivative of compound Y melts at 125 °C. That sits between butanal and propanone, so the melting temperature alone does not decide. Y gives no silver mirror with Tollens' reagent, so it is not an aldehyde. Y is **propanone**. Always back a close melting-temperature match with a second test.

## 15.8 v The iodoform test

Warm the compound with iodine solution and sodium hydroxide solution. A **pale yellow precipitate** of triiodomethane (iodoform, CHI₃), with an antiseptic smell, shows a **CH₃CO– group**. Positive carbonyl compounds are methyl ketones (CH₃COR) and ethanal, the only aldehyde with this group.

The rest of the molecule leaves as a carboxylate ion with one fewer carbon atom.

### Worked example 7: equation and mass

3-Methylbutan-2-one, (CH₃)₂CHCOCH₃, has a CH₃CO– group, so it gives a positive test.

```
(CH₃)₂CHCOCH₃ + 3I₂ + 4NaOH → CHI₃ + (CH₃)₂CHCOONa + 3NaI + 3H₂O
```

The organic by-product is sodium 2-methylpropanoate. From 2.58 g of the ketone (Mr 86.0): n = 2.58 ÷ 86.0 = 0.0300 mol, so the maximum mass of CHI₃ (Mr 393.7) is 0.0300 × 393.7 = **11.8 g**.

## Common errors

- Numbering a ketone from the wrong end, giving pentan-4-one instead of pentan-2-one.
- Saying aldehydes hydrogen bond to each other. They hydrogen bond only to water.
- Writing "red precipitate" for a ketone with Fehling's solution, or "silver mirror" with a ketone.
- Using LiAlH₄ in water, or forgetting that a ketone gives a secondary, not primary, alcohol.
- Starting the curly arrow from the negative charge on N rather than the lone pair on the carbon of CN⁻.
- Saying the HCN product is optically active. A racemic mixture is not.
- Claiming 2,4-DNPH tells aldehydes from ketones. It only shows C=O; use Tollens' or Fehling's for that.
- Saying every aldehyde gives iodoform. Only ethanal does.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do you name aldehydes and ketones?**
Aldehydes end in -al and the CHO carbon is always C1, so no locant is needed, as in hexanal. Ketones end in -one with a locant for the carbonyl carbon, as in pentan-2-one; choose the longest chain through the C=O carbon and give it the lowest number.

**Why are small aldehydes and ketones soluble in water?**
The δ− carbonyl oxygen has lone pairs and can accept a hydrogen bond from a water hydrogen. Propanone mixes with water in all proportions, but solubility falls as the hydrocarbon chain gets longer.

**What does LiAlH₄ do to aldehydes and ketones?**
Lithium tetrahydridoaluminate(III) in dry ether reduces an aldehyde to a primary alcohol and a ketone to a secondary alcohol, for example RCHO + 2[H] → RCH₂OH. The ether must be dry because LiAlH₄ reacts violently with water.

**Why is the product of HCN and butanone not optically active?**
The carbonyl carbon is trigonal planar, so cyanide attacks from above and below with equal probability. Both enantiomers form in equal amounts, giving a racemic mixture whose rotations cancel; with pentan-3-one the product has no chiral centre at all.
