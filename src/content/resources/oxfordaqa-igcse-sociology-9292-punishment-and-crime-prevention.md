This guide covers two content statements from **3.2.2.3** in Paper 2 of OxfordAQA International
GCSE Sociology (9292): "**Aims and types of punishment**" and "**Crime prevention: types of policing,
surveillance**". **Zuboff** is among the sociologists the specification names for 3.2.2, and her work
on surveillance belongs here. The [Socialisation study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-sociology-socialisation/)
explains that the social control parts of 3.2.2 were not covered on that page; this guide deals with
the formal end of social control, after [formal and informal social control](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-sociology-9292-formal-and-informal-social-control/).

## The aims of punishment

Societies punish offenders for several reasons, which often overlap or conflict.

| Aim | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Retribution** | The offender deserves to suffer in proportion to the crime | A long sentence for a serious violent offence |
| **Deterrence** | Discouraging crime by making punishment feared: individual deterrence targets the offender; general deterrence warns everyone else | Publicised heavy fines for a common offence |
| **Rehabilitation** | Reforming the offender so they will not reoffend | Education, training or drug treatment in prison |
| **Incapacitation (public protection)** | Removing the offender's ability to offend | Imprisonment; driving bans |
| **Reparation** | The offender repairs the harm done | Compensation; unpaid community work |
| **Denunciation** | Society publicly expresses its disapproval | A court's formal condemnation in sentencing |

Exam questions may ask you to identify an aim from an example, or to explain why aims conflict. For
instance, a short prison sentence may satisfy retribution but do little for rehabilitation.

## Types of punishment

Punishments vary between societies and over time:

- **Fines** and **compensation orders**.
- **Community sentences**, such as unpaid work or supervision.
- **Probation** and **suspended sentences**, where the offender stays in the community under
  conditions.
- **Imprisonment**, from short sentences to life.
- **Restorative justice**, bringing offender and victim together so the offender understands the harm
  and makes amends.
- **Corporal punishment** and **capital punishment**, which remain lawful in some countries and have
  been abolished in many others.

Which punishments a society uses reflects its values, which is why the comparison between societies
is sociologically revealing.

## Perspectives on punishment

**Functionalist.** **Durkheim** argued that punishment has a function for society as a whole: it
expresses collective outrage, reaffirms shared values and reminds everyone where the boundaries of
acceptable behaviour lie. From this view, punishment is less about the offender than about holding
society together.

**Marxist.** Marxists argue that punishment falls most heavily on the working class, while crimes of
the powerful, such as corporate crime, are less often prosecuted or are dealt with by fines rather
than prison. Punishment therefore helps maintain an unequal social order.

**Foucault.** In *Discipline and Punish* (1975), **Michel Foucault** argued that punishment shifted
from public, physical spectacles, such as executions, to a system of **discipline** that controls the
mind through constant observation. He used Jeremy Bentham's design for a **panopticon**, a prison in
which inmates can always be watched without knowing when, as a model: people who might be watched at
any time begin to watch themselves. This idea links punishment to surveillance in wider society.

**Interactionist.** Following **Becker**, interactionists point out that punishment depends on who is
caught and labelled. Police and courts act on stereotypes, so the same offence may lead to a warning
for one person and a prosecution for another. Punishment can also deepen a deviant identity: a prison
sentence may become a master status that makes reoffending more likely.

**Feminist.** Feminists examine how gender shapes punishment. Women who break gender norms may face
**double deviance**, judged both for the offence and for being "unfeminine". Feminists have also
criticised justice systems for treating domestic abuse less seriously than violence in public, which
leaves many women without protection.

## Crime prevention: types of policing

| Type | Main idea | Points for evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| **Policing by consent** | Police authority rests on public co-operation and trust, a principle associated with the creation of London's Metropolitan Police in 1829 | Depends on trust, which may be weaker in some communities |
| **Community or neighbourhood policing** | Officers work in a local area, build relationships and solve local problems | Builds trust and intelligence, but needs resources |
| **Zero-tolerance policing** | Strict enforcement against even minor offences, often linked to the "broken windows" idea of Wilson and Kelling (1982) that visible disorder encourages more serious crime | May reduce disorder, but can damage relations with communities that feel targeted |
| **Intelligence-led and predictive policing** | Using data to target places and people judged at risk | Efficient use of resources, but data may reproduce existing biases |
| **Situational crime prevention** | Reducing opportunities for crime through design: locks, lighting, CCTV (associated with Ronald Clarke) | Can work locally, but crime may be **displaced** to other places or times |

## Surveillance

**Surveillance** means monitoring people's behaviour, movements or communications. Forms include
**CCTV**, automatic number-plate recognition, facial recognition, monitoring of online activity, and
data collected through phones, payment cards and apps.

**Shoshana Zuboff**, in *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism* (2019), argued that technology companies
collect vast amounts of data about users' behaviour and use it to predict and influence that behaviour
for profit. Her argument extends the study of social control beyond the state: private companies now
hold detailed knowledge of people's lives, and that knowledge can shape what people see, buy and do.

Sociological debates about surveillance include:

- **Effectiveness:** does it prevent crime, or mainly help detect it afterwards?
- **Displacement:** does visible surveillance simply move crime elsewhere?
- **Privacy and power:** who watches, who is watched, and who controls the data?
- **Inequality:** some groups and areas are watched more closely than others.

## Worked example 1: a short-answer question

*Explain **one** aim of punishment, using an example. [3 marks]*

> One aim is rehabilitation (1), which tries to reform offenders so that they do not reoffend (1); for
> example, prisoners may be offered education or training so they can find legal work on release (1).

## Worked example 2: planning an extended answer

*Discuss how far surveillance is an effective way of preventing crime. Use examples from your own
society or a society you know about. [12 marks]*

- **For:** CCTV and situational prevention reduce opportunities; Foucault's panopticon effect makes
  people regulate themselves; data helps target resources.
- **Against:** displacement; surveillance may detect more than it prevents; privacy concerns;
  unequal targeting; Zuboff on private power over data.
- **Application:** a named society, for example its use of public CCTV or facial recognition.
- **Judgement:** surveillance can prevent some opportunistic crime, but it raises costs in privacy and
  fairness and does little about the causes of crime.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing **deterrence** with **incapacitation**.
- Treating zero-tolerance policing as simply "strict" without its rationale or criticisms.
- Naming Foucault or Zuboff without their arguments.
- Assuming surveillance only means CCTV.
- Ignoring variation in punishment between societies.
- No example from a named society in an extended answer.
