## Section A: short questions

**1.** What is meant by **informal social control**? **[2]**

**2.** Identify **two** agencies of formal social control. **[2]**

**3.** Identify **one** formal positive sanction and **one** informal negative sanction. **[2]**

**4.** What is meant by a **moral entrepreneur**? **[2]**

**5.** Study **Item A** and answer the questions that follow.

> **Item A** — Field notes from an observation in a school canteen
>
> *Original extract, invented for this practice question.*
>
> "A Year 9 boy pushes into the queue. Two girls behind him roll their eyes and one says loudly,
> 'Some people think the rules don't apply to them.' Others laugh. He looks embarrassed and moves to
> the back. A few minutes later a teacher on duty notices a different pupil throwing food and gives
> him a detention slip."

**(a)** Using Item A, identify **one** example of informal social control. **[1]**

**(b)** Using Item A, explain the difference between the two sanctions described. **[3]**

## Section B: longer questions

**6.** Describe how **peers** act as an agency of informal social control. **[4]**

**7.** Explain the difference between **consensus** and **conflict** views of social control. **[4]**

**8.** Explain how Althusser's ideas help Marxists explain social control. **[4]**

**9.** Explain **two** ways in which women may be subject to greater social control than men,
according to feminists. **[6]**

**10.** Explain how the interactionist view of social control differs from the functionalist view.
**[6]**

**11.** Discuss how far formal social control is more effective than informal social control in
maintaining order. Use examples from your own society or a society you know about. **[12]**

## Answers: Section A

**A1** [2 marks]

Unofficial pressure to follow **unwritten norms** [1], exercised through everyday relationships such
as families, peers or religion [1].

**A2** [2 marks — 1 each]

Any two: government and laws; the police; other enforcement agencies such as customs or regulators;
courts; prisons; probation services.

**A3** [2 marks — 1 each]

Formal positive, e.g. a certificate, promotion or medal [1]; informal negative, e.g. gossip, teasing
or being ignored [1].

**A4** [2 marks]

Becker's term for a person or group that **campaigns to create or enforce a rule** [1], so defining
some behaviour as deviant [1].

**A5 (a)** [1 mark]

The girls' eye-rolling and loud comment, or the laughter, which embarrass the boy into moving [1].

**A5 (b)** [3 marks]

The girls' comment is an **informal negative sanction** from peers enforcing an unwritten norm about
queueing [1]. The detention is a **formal negative sanction** given by a teacher under the school's
written rules [1]. Both discourage rule-breaking, but one relies on embarrassment within a
relationship and the other on official authority [1].

## Answers: Section B

**A6** [4 marks]

Peers are people of a similar age or status [1]. The wish to be accepted leads individuals to follow
the group's norms [1]. Peers use informal sanctions such as approval and popularity for conforming [1]
and teasing or exclusion for breaking group norms [1].

**A7** [4 marks]

The consensus view sees society as based on **shared values** [1], so social control protects those
values and benefits everyone [1]. Conflict views see society as divided by **unequal power** [1], so
social control serves powerful groups such as a ruling class or men [1].

**A8** [4 marks]

Althusser argued that the ruling class keeps control through two kinds of agency [1]. **Repressive
state apparatuses**, such as the police and courts, control by force [1]. **Ideological state
apparatuses**, such as education, media and religion, control by shaping beliefs [1], so that people
accept inequality as normal and rarely challenge capitalism [1].

**A9** [6 marks — up to 3 per way]

Two from, following Heidensohn: **at home** [1] — domestic and childcare responsibilities [1] leave
women less time and freedom to go out [1]; **in public** [1] — fear of violence and concern about
reputation [1] lead women to limit where and when they go out [1]; **at work** [1] — male supervisors
and harassment [1] restrict women's behaviour and opportunities [1]. Also credit control through
dress codes or media judgements of women's appearance [1] [1] [1].

**A10** [6 marks]

Functionalists see social control as reinforcing **shared values** for the good of society [1]; for
Durkheim, punishment marks the boundaries of acceptable behaviour [1]. Interactionists focus on how
rules are **made and applied in interaction** [1]; Becker argued that deviance results from labelling
[1], that rules are created by moral entrepreneurs [1] and that they are enforced **selectively**,
so the same act may be punished in one person and ignored in another [1].

**A11** [12 marks — levels]

| Level | Marks | Descriptor |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | 10–12 | Detailed knowledge; well applied to a named society; balanced discussion with a clear, justified conclusion |
| 3 | 7–9 | Good knowledge; some application; discussion with a conclusion |
| 2 | 4–6 | Some relevant knowledge; limited application or one-sided |
| 1 | 1–3 | Basic, mainly descriptive points |

Indicative content:

- **Formal control as effective:** clear written rules; serious sanctions; deterrence; agencies with
  authority to enforce; Durkheim on punishment marking boundaries.
- **Informal control as effective:** most people conform because of families, peers, religion and
  workplace expectations, not fear of police; informal sanctions operate constantly; ideological
  apparatuses shape beliefs (Althusser); Heidensohn's evidence on women's conformity.
- **Limits:** formal control depends on detection and may be applied selectively (Becker); informal
  control weakens in large, anonymous cities; online spaces challenge both.
- **Application:** a named society, for example the role of community or religious pressure
  alongside policing.
- **Judgement:** for example, informal control does most of the everyday work, with formal control
  as a back-up when it fails.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Putting sanctions in the wrong box of the grid.
- Listing agencies without explaining how they control.
- Naming a sociologist without the argument.
- Confusing socialisation with social control.
- An extended answer with no named society.
