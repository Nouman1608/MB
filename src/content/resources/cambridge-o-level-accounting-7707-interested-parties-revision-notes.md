These notes condense section **6.4 Interested parties** of the Cambridge O Level Accounting 7707 syllabus for 2027 to 2029. The syllabus asks "how accounting information may be used for decision-making" by owners, managers, employees, banks, investors and lenders, suppliers, customers, governments / tax authorities, club members and "other interested parties, e.g. public and environmental bodies". The worked example of one company read by several parties is in the [interested parties study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-interested-parties/); test yourself with the [interested parties practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-interested-parties-practice/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Interested party | Any person or group that uses accounting information to make a decision |
| Internal user | A user inside the organisation with access to detailed records, such as managers |
| External user | A user outside the organisation who relies mainly on the financial statements |
| Security | An asset a lender can claim if a loan is not repaid |
| Credit limit | The most a supplier will allow a customer to owe at one time |
| Conflict of interest | When two parties want different outcomes from the same figures |

## Party, decision, information

| Party | Decision | Look at |
|---|---|---|
| Owners | Continue, expand, invest more, how much to draw | Profit, margins, ROCE, trends |
| Managers (internal) | Prices, cost control, inventory, credit control, planning | Detailed costs, all ratios, budgets |
| Employees | Job security; whether a pay rise is affordable | Profit trend, liquidity |
| Banks | Lend or not; overdraft size; security and interest rate | Liquidity ratios, profit, assets, existing borrowing |
| Investors and lenders | Buy shares or lend; is the return worth the risk | ROCE, profit, dividends, existing debt |
| Suppliers | Give credit; how much; on what terms | Trade payables days, liquidity |
| Customers | Rely on the business for supplies, spares, after-sales service | Profit trend, liquidity, continuity |
| Governments / tax authorities | Tax due; statistics; policy | Profit, revenue, wages |
| Club members | Change subscriptions; judge the committee | Surplus or deficit, accumulated fund, activity results |
| Public and environmental bodies | Effect on jobs and the local environment | Employment, environmental spending, continuity |

## Answer chain

**Party + decision + figure + reason.** "A bank deciding on an overdraft would look at the acid test ratio, because a ratio below 1 : 1 suggests the business may struggle to repay on time."

## One set of figures, read three ways

A company's acid test ratio falls from 1.10 : 1 to 0.60 : 1 while its dividend doubles.

- **A supplier** reads the acid test and its payables days and may cut the credit limit.
- **A bank** reads the same fall as a risk to repayment and may ask for security or refuse an overdraft.
- **A shareholder** welcomes the higher dividend but should note that paying it used cash the company was short of.

The figures are the same; the decision each party makes is different. Name the party, the figure and the decision every time.

## Conflicts of interest

- Shareholders want dividends; lenders want cash kept for repayment.
- Employees want higher pay; owners want the profit.
- Owners want lower taxable profit; tax authorities want the true figure.
- Club members disagree over low subscriptions versus better facilities.
- Managers may want to show good results; owners and lenders need the true position.

## Exam traps

- "Interested in profit" with no decision earns little.
- Suppliers give credit to the business; customers buy from it. Do not swap them.
- Managers are internal and see detailed records; other parties mostly see summarised statements.
- Club members use the income and expenditure account and accumulated fund, not profit.
- "Investors and lenders" covers people considering a future investment or loan, not only existing ones.
- Quote the figure from the question that matters to the named party.
- Some users need information the statements do not show, such as environmental effects.

## Self-test

1. Which ratio would a supplier look at first when deciding whether to give credit, and why?
2. Give two things a bank considers before granting a loan.
3. What decision might employees make, or support, using a company's accounts?
4. Which statement would a club member use to judge whether subscriptions should rise?
5. How does a tax authority use a business's accounts?
6. State one way managers' use of accounting information differs from that of external users.
7. Explain one conflict of interest between shareholders and lenders.
8. Which ratio would an investor use to compare the return from two companies?
9. Why might a customer be interested in a supplier's accounts?
10. What might an environmental body look for in a factory's accounts?
11. A business takes 80 days to pay suppliers who allow 30 days. Which party is most directly concerned?
12. Why might owners and employees read the same falling profit differently?

### Answers

1. Trade payables turnover in days: it shows how long the business takes to pay its suppliers, so a long period warns of slow payment.
2. Any two: liquidity (current and acid test ratios); profit to cover interest; assets available as security; existing loans; the trend in results.
3. Whether jobs are secure, or whether to press for a pay rise the company can afford.
4. The income and expenditure account (surplus or deficit), with the accumulated fund in the statement of financial position.
5. To check the profit on which tax is calculated and the amount due; also for national statistics.
6. Managers are inside the business and use detailed, frequent information to plan and control; external users rely on summarised annual statements.
7. Shareholders may want high dividends paid out now, while lenders want cash kept in the company so that interest and the loan can be repaid.
8. Return on capital employed (ROCE).
9. To judge whether the supplier will continue trading and keep providing goods, spares and after-sales service.
10. Any one: spending on waste disposal, cleaner equipment or environmental fines; whether the factory will continue to operate locally.
11. The suppliers (trade payables), who may refuse further credit.
12. Owners may cut costs, including wages, to protect profit, while employees may argue that the business is still profitable enough to protect jobs and pay.
