[Unseen prose study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-english-literature-9275-unseen-prose/).

## At a glance

- Paper 2a, **Section C**, **Route A only**.
- One question on one unseen prose extract, **30 marks**.
- Last section of a 2 hour 15 minute, open-book, 90-mark paper.
- About **45 minutes**: protect this time.
- Route B takes the non-exam assessment instead.

## What the marks reward

| AO | In this section | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| AO1 | Understanding of the extract's ideas, shown through a clear interpretation that answers the question | A first sentence that states what the writer presents and how it changes |
| AO2 | Analysis of how the writer creates meanings and effects through language, structure and form | Method and effect at a precise moment, in every paragraph |
| AO3 | An informed personal response, with appropriate terminology in organised, accurate writing | Terms used correctly, a developing argument, an alternative reading weighed |

Questions usually ask **how the writer presents** a character, a relationship, a place or a mood, or
how tension is created. Keep that focus in the first line of each paragraph.

## The 45-minute method

1. Read the **question** and underline its focus (1 minute).
2. Read the extract for **sense** (4 minutes).
3. Read again for **method**, annotating only what fits the question (5 minutes).
4. **Plan** an interpretation and three or four points in extract order (5 minutes).
5. **Write** (about 30 minutes), ending with the extract's final lines.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Narrative perspective | Who tells the story and from where |
| First-person narrator | The story is told by "I", a character in it |
| Third-person limited | The narration stays close to one character |
| Free indirect style | The narration takes on a character's own thoughts and phrasing |
| Withholding | Information the writer deliberately keeps back |
| Pace | The speed at which events unfold, shaped by sentence length and detail |
| Pathetic fallacy | Weather or setting reflecting feeling |
| Characterisation | How a writer builds a character through speech, action and description |
| Dialogue | Direct speech, revealing relationships and what is unsaid |
| Atmosphere | The overall mood created by setting and description |
| Turning point | Where the extract shifts in time, mood or understanding |
| In medias res | Opening in the middle of events, so the reader must piece the situation together |
| Foreshadowing | An early hint that prepares for a later moment in the extract |
| Motif | A detail that recurs and gathers meaning each time |
| Sentence variety | Changes in sentence length and shape that control pace and emphasis |
| Ambiguous ending | A close that leaves more than one outcome possible |

## Worked mini-example

The extract below is original, written for these notes; it is not from a published novel.

> *From the top board the pool looked like a postage stamp someone had dropped. Priya curled her
> toes over the edge, where the rough paint gave way to nothing. Below, her coach's voice arrived
> late and small, as if it had travelled a long way to reach her. Behind her, on the ladder, a boy
> coughed. She did not turn round. The clock on the far wall jerked forward a minute, then another.
> She had climbed up here eleven times this month. Eleven times she had climbed back down. She
> lifted her arms.*

| Detail | Method | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| "a postage stamp someone had dropped" | Simile | The pool looks tiny and almost accidental, so the height feels dizzying |
| "gave way to nothing" | Precise sensory detail | The edge becomes a boundary between safety and emptiness |
| "late and small" | Description of sound | The coach's help is distant; she is alone with the decision |
| "a boy coughed" | Detail of other characters | Silent pressure from behind; we sense impatience without being told |
| "jerked forward" | Personification of the clock | Time moves in sudden jolts, matching her nerves |
| "Eleven times" | Repetition, information withheld until late | A history of failure is revealed only now, raising the stakes |
| "She lifted her arms." | Short final sentence, ambiguous ending | We never learn whether she dives, so the tension is held at its peak |

**Annotated model paragraph** (question: how does the writer create tension?):

> The writer creates tension by isolating Priya at the edge and then refusing to tell us what she
> does. **[AO1: interpretation answers the question]** The simile comparing the pool to "a postage
> stamp someone had dropped" shrinks the water to something small and careless, so the reader feels
> the height through her eyes. **[AO2: simile and viewpoint, with effect]** Even help is distant, as
> her coach's voice arrives "late and small", while the boy's cough behind her adds pressure that
> no one names. **[AO2: sound and implied pressure]** Only near the end does the writer reveal that
> she has climbed down "eleven times", and the repetition turns this attempt into a test of
> everything that has gone before. **[AO2 structure: withholding; AO3: developed reading]** The
> final short sentence stops at the moment of decision, so the extract ends with the tension still
> rising, perhaps leaving us to hope she jumps. **[AO2 structure: ending; AO3: personal response]**

## Turning a detail into analysis

| Detail you notice | Weak comment | Stronger comment |
|---|---|---|
| A very short sentence after long ones | "This creates tension." | "The abrupt stop mirrors the character's shock, so the reader is halted at the same moment she is." |
| A repeated phrase | "The writer uses repetition." | "Each repetition sounds less certain, showing the character's confidence draining away." |
| An everyday simile | "This is a simile." | "Comparing a crisis to something ordinary shows a mind trying to keep control." |
| A missing explanation | "We don't know why." | "Withholding the cause makes the reader share the character's uncertainty." |

The stronger comments name the effect **at that moment** and connect it to a character or the reader.

## Every answer should include

- One point about **narrative perspective**.
- One point about **structure**: opening, a shift, or the ending.
- Several points about **language**, each with its effect on the reader.
- A clear **interpretation** that answers the question.

## Paragraph pattern

**Claim → short quotation → method → effect on the reader → link to the extract's development.**

## Exam traps

- Guessing what happens outside the extract.
- Paraphrasing paragraph by paragraph.
- "Short sentences create tension" with no specific explanation.
- Ignoring who tells the story.
- Running short of time because the poetry sections overran.
- Skipping the ending.
- Treating a first-person narrator's view as the whole truth.
- Calling every detail of weather "pathetic fallacy" without showing the link to feeling.
- Writing about the extract as if it were a poem, with line-by-line comments on sound.
- Ignoring the question's focus once a striking detail catches your eye.

## Self-test

1. Which route and which section include unseen prose?
2. How many marks is it worth, and how long should you spend on it?
3. What is free indirect style?
4. Why should you not speculate about the rest of the novel?
5. Name three things every answer should include.
6. Why is timing especially important for this section?
7. Which three assessment objectives apply, and what does AO1 require here?
8. What does "withholding" do to a reader?
9. In the worked extract, why does the writer wait until near the end to reveal "eleven times"?
10. What is an ambiguous ending, and why is it worth a paragraph?
11. Give one sign that a narrator may not be reliable.
12. Improve this comment: "The weather is pathetic fallacy."

**Answers:** 1. Route A, Paper 2a Section C. 2. 30 marks; about 45 minutes. 3. Third-person narration
that takes on a character's own thoughts and phrasing. 4. Marks come from analysing what is printed;
guesses are not supported by the text. 5. Any three of: a point on narrative perspective, a point on
structure, language analysis with effects, a clear interpretation. 6. It is the last section, so
overruns on the poetry sections reduce the time left for it. 7. AO1, AO2 and AO3; AO1 requires a
clear interpretation of the extract's ideas that answers the question. 8. It keeps information back,
so the reader shares a character's uncertainty and reads on to resolve it. 9. Withholding it raises the
stakes at the last moment: the reader learns this attempt follows many failures. 10. An ending that leaves more than
one outcome open; it shapes the reader's final response, so analysing it shows awareness of
structure. 11. For example: a gap between what the narrator says and what the details show, or a
narrator who excuses themselves. 12. For example: "The relentless rain mirrors the pressure the
character cannot escape, so the setting seems to press in on him."
