This guide covers sub-topic C.5, Doppler effect, in theme C (Wave behaviour) of the IB *Physics guide* (first assessment 2025). The nature of the effect, wavefront diagrams, the light-wave relationship Δf/f = Δλ/λ ≈ v/c and spectral-line shifts are for SL and HL. The formulas for sound and other mechanical waves with a moving source or a moving observer are additional higher level (HL only), and are marked below.

The [travelling waves and Doppler effect study guide](/resources/ib-dp-physics-travelling-waves-and-the-doppler-effect/) states each formula with one example. ## The effect in one sentence

The **Doppler effect** is the change in observed frequency (and wavelength) of a wave when there is relative motion between the source and the observer. Approach gives a higher frequency; separation gives a lower one. Only the component of velocity **along the line joining source and observer** matters.

## Wavefront diagrams: where the change comes from

Draw wavefronts as circles, each one emitted one period T after the last.

**Stationary source, stationary observer.** Concentric circles, spaced λ apart; the observer receives f wavefronts per second.

**Moving source.** Each circle is centred on the point where the source was when it emitted it. In one period the wave travels vT, but the source moves usT in the same direction. So, ahead of the source:

λ′ = vT − usT = (v − us)/f

and behind it λ′ = (v + us)/f. The wave speed in the medium is still v, so the observed frequency ahead is

f′ = v/λ′ = f v/(v − us)

That is where the HL formula comes from. The source has changed the **wavelength in the medium**.

**Moving observer.** The source is still, so the circles stay concentric and λ is unchanged. An observer moving towards the source at uo meets wavefronts at a relative speed v + uo, so

f′ = (v + uo)/λ = f(v + uo)/v

The **wavelength is not changed**; the observer simply meets wavefronts more often.

**Exam point:** for sound, a source moving at 20 m s⁻¹ towards you and you moving at 20 m s⁻¹ towards the source do **not** give the same frequency, because the medium (air) provides a frame of reference. For light there is no medium, so only the relative velocity matters, and for v much less than c the two cases agree.

## Doppler effect for light (SL and HL)

Light always travels at c relative to any observer, so the sound formulas do not apply. For relative speeds much smaller than c:

**Δf/f = Δλ/λ ≈ v/c**

where f and λ are the emitted (rest) values and Δf, Δλ are the shifts.

- Source and observer **separating**: λ increases, f decreases: **redshift**.
- Source and observer **approaching**: λ decreases, f increases: **blueshift**.

**Worked example 1 (blueshift).** The hydrogen line with rest wavelength 656.3 nm is observed at 655.0 nm in the spectrum of a star.

- Δλ = 655.0 − 656.3 = −1.3 nm (shorter, so the star is approaching).
- v = c|Δλ|/λ = 3.00 × 10⁸ × 1.3/656.3 = **5.9 × 10⁵ m s⁻¹** towards us.
- v/c ≈ 0.002, so the approximation is valid.

**Worked example 2 (frequency form).** A spacecraft transmits a radio signal at 8.4 GHz while moving directly away from Earth at 15 km s⁻¹.

- Δf = f v/c = 8.4 × 10⁹ × 1.5 × 10⁴ / 3.00 × 10⁸ = 4.2 × 10⁵ Hz.
- Received frequency = 8.4 × 10⁹ − 4.2 × 10⁵ Hz, a decrease of **0.42 MHz**.
- The fractional shift is tiny (5 × 10⁻⁵), yet easily measured, which is why Doppler tracking can find a spacecraft's speed precisely.

## Spectral lines and the motion of stars and galaxies (SL and HL)

Each element absorbs and emits light at a fixed set of wavelengths (theme E). In the spectrum of a star or galaxy, the **pattern** of lines is recognisable, but the whole pattern is shifted.

1. Identify a known line (for example a hydrogen line).
2. Measure its observed wavelength and compare with the laboratory value.
3. The sign of Δλ gives the direction: longer means receding, shorter means approaching.
4. Δλ/λ gives the speed along the line of sight.

Every line in the pattern shifts by the **same fraction** of its own wavelength, which confirms that the shift is due to motion, not to a different element.

**Worked example 3 (orbital motion).** A line with rest wavelength 500.00 nm in a star's spectrum moves regularly between 499.88 nm and 500.12 nm.

- Maximum Δλ = 0.12 nm either side.
- Maximum line-of-sight speed = 3.00 × 10⁸ × 0.12/500.00 = **7.2 × 10⁴ m s⁻¹**.
- The shift alternates between blue and red, so the star is moving alternately towards and away from us: it is **orbiting** another body, such as an unseen companion star.

Light from distant galaxies is redshifted, showing that they are receding.

## HL only: sound and mechanical waves

With v the wave speed relative to the medium, us the source speed and uo the observer speed:

```
Moving source:    f′ = f v/(v ± us)      use −  for approach, +  for recession
Moving observer:  f′ = f (v ± uo)/v      use +  for approach, −  for recession
```

**Sign check:** approaching always gives f′ > f. If your answer is lower for an approaching case, a sign is wrong.

**Worked example 4 (HL, two readings).** A train sounds its horn while passing a stationary listener. The listener hears 560 Hz as it approaches and 480 Hz as it moves away. Use v = 340 m s⁻¹. Find the train's speed and the horn's frequency.

```
Approach:   560 = f × 340/(340 − us)
Recession:  480 = f × 340/(340 + us)
Divide:     560/480 = (340 + us)/(340 − us)
            7(340 − us) = 6(340 + us)
            2380 − 7us = 2040 + 6us
            13us = 340   →   us = 26 m s⁻¹
Then:       f = 560 × (340 − 26.2)/340 = 517 Hz
```

Answer: **us ≈ 26 m s⁻¹, f ≈ 517 Hz**. Dividing the two equations removes f, which is the key step.

**Worked example 5 (HL, moving observer).** A cyclist rides directly away from a stationary siren of 650 Hz at 8.0 m s⁻¹.

- f′ = 650 × (340 − 8.0)/340 = **635 Hz**.
- The wavelength in the air is unchanged at 340/650 = 0.523 m.

## Common mistakes

- Saying the **wave speed** changes. For sound, the source changes the wavelength, the observer changes the rate of meeting wavefronts; the speed in the medium stays v.
- Using the total velocity rather than the component along the line of sight.
- Applying the sound formulas to light.
- Using Δλ/λ ≈ v/c when v is not much smaller than c.
- Taking λ in Δλ/λ as the observed value rather than the rest value (it matters little for small shifts, but use the rest value).
- Mixing up redshift (receding) and blueshift (approaching).
- Including both a moving source and a moving observer in one problem; the guide's problems involve one at a time.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** (wavefront diagrams): say where wavefronts are closer together and what that does to λ and f.
- **Explain** the difference between source and observer motion for sound: the source changes λ; the observer changes the rate of meeting wavefronts.
- **Determine** a speed from a spectrum: show Δλ, the ratio, and state the direction.
- **Calculate** (HL): write the formula with the sign chosen, substitute, then sense-check that approach gives a higher frequency.
