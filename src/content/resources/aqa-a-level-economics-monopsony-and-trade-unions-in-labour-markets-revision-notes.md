## Definitions to recall

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Monopsony | A labour market with a single buyer of labour; in use, a dominant employer with wage-setting power |
| Monopsony power | The ability of an employer to set a wage below the competitive level |
| Trade union | A body of workers that negotiates jointly with employers on pay and conditions |
| Collective bargaining | Negotiation between a union and an employer (or employers) on behalf of members |
| Imperfect information | Workers or employers lack full knowledge of wages, vacancies or productivity |
| Average cost of labour (AC) | The wage per worker; the labour supply curve |
| Marginal cost of labour (MCL) | The addition to the total wage bill from employing one more worker |
| Marginal revenue product (MRP) | The extra revenue from employing one more worker; the labour demand curve |
| Bilateral monopoly | A single seller (a union) facing a single buyer (an employer) |
| Insiders and outsiders | Insiders keep their jobs at the union wage; outsiders are unemployed or priced out |

## Three imperfections in one line each

- **Monopsony power:** one dominant buyer sets the wage instead of taking it.
- **Trade unions:** many sellers act as one, pushing the wage above the competitive level.
- **Imperfect information:** workers do not know better offers elsewhere; employers do not know a candidate's productivity. Search is costly, so wages differ for similar work.

## Monopsony: key rules

1. Employer hires where **MCL = MRP**.
2. It pays the wage on the **supply** curve at that employment level.
3. MCL lies above supply because one wage is paid to all workers, so extra hiring pushes up everyone's pay.
4. Result: wage and employment both **below** the competitive levels.

### Method in steps (diagram)

1. Label axes: wage (vertical), number of workers (horizontal).
2. Draw upward-sloping supply (AC of labour) and a steeper MCL above it.
3. Draw downward-sloping MRP (demand).
4. Mark competitive equilibrium where supply meets MRP: wage Wc, employment Lc.
5. Mark where MCL meets MRP: employment Lm, lower than Lc.
6. Go vertically from Lm to supply and across: the monopsony wage Wm, below Wc.
7. Show the gap between MRP and Wm at Lm.

### Worked reminder: a small invented town

Labour supply in Town B: wage = 4 + 0.5L, with L in hundreds of workers and the wage in pounds per hour. MRP = 16 − 0.5L.

| Step | Working | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Competitive | 4 + 0.5L = 16 − 0.5L, so L = 12 | Wage 4 + 6 = 10; employment 1,200 |
| Total labour cost | (4 + 0.5L) × L = 4L + 0.5L² | Marginal cost = 4 + L |
| Monopsony | 4 + L = 16 − 0.5L, so 1.5L = 12, L = 8 | Employment 800 |
| Wage from supply | 4 + 0.5 × 8 | Wage 8 |
| MRP at 8 | 16 − 0.5 × 8 | 12 |
| Gap | 12 − 8 | 4 pounds per hour |

Monopsony wage is (10 − 8)/10 = 20% below the competitive wage. Employment is (12 − 8)/12 = 33.3% lower. Wage bill: 8 × 8 = 64, against 10 × 12 = 120 (units are hundreds of workers times pounds).

### Evaluation points for monopsony

- A more elastic labour supply narrows the gaps.
- Workers who can move easily or find out about other jobs reduce monopsony power.
- Few labour markets have a single employer; dominant employers and employers acting alike are more common.
- Low wages can raise staff turnover and recruitment costs, a long-run limit on wage cutting.

## Union power: what to recall

| Factor | Stronger union when... |
|---|---|
| Membership density | most workers are members |
| Demand for labour | demand is wage-inelastic |
| Share of labour cost | labour is a small part of total cost |
| Product demand | demand for the product is inelastic |
| Substitutes | few ways to replace the workers |
| Skills | workers are skilled and scarce |
| Economy | unemployment is low and demand is strong |
| Profitability | the firm is profitable and can afford a rise |
| Law and attitudes | the law allows industrial action and public opinion is supportive |

Three things that keep a union's gains in check: employers can replace workers with capital, import or relocate; high wages can cut employment; and a strike costs members income.

## Union in a perfectly competitive market

Wage rises above equilibrium; employment falls along the demand curve; surplus labour appears.

Using Town B's competitive market (wage 10, 1,200 workers), a union wins a wage of 12:

| Step | Working | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Demand at 12 | 16 − 0.5L = 12, so L = 8 | 800 employed |
| Supply at 12 | 4 + 0.5L = 12, so L = 16 | 1,600 willing |
| Surplus | 16 − 8 | 800 unemployed |
| Wage bill | 12 × 8 = 96 against 10 × 12 = 120 | Falls by 24 |
| Elasticity | %ΔW = +20%; %ΔL = −33.3% | −33.3 ÷ 20 = −1.67 |

Because the wage elasticity of demand for labour is greater than 1 in size, the total pay of members falls. If it were inelastic, the pay bill would rise. Either way, jobs are lost and outsiders suffer.

## Union in a monopsony

The union wage replaces the rising supply wage up to the point where workers are willing to work at that wage, so MCL is flat at the union wage over that range. Employment is the smaller of what the firm demands and what workers supply at that wage.

Town B, where the monopsony wage is 8 and employment is 800:

| Union wage | Supply (hundreds) | Demand (hundreds) | Employment | Wage bill |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 9 | 10 | 14 | 10 | 90 |
| 10 | 12 | 12 | 12 | 120 |
| 11 | 14 | 10 | 10 | 110 |
| 12 | 16 | 8 | 8 | 96 |
| 13 | 18 | 6 | 6 | 78 |

- Wage 9 to 10: both wages and employment rise.
- Wage 10 (the competitive wage): employment is maximised at 1,200.
- Wage 11: employment of 1,000 is still above the monopsony level of 800.
- Wage 12: employment returns to the monopsony level, 800.
- Above 12: employment falls below what monopsony alone would give.

Key statement: a union in a monopsony can raise both wages and employment, but only for wage rises up to a limit. The result depends on where the wage is set.

## Must-know distinctions

| Idea A | Idea B |
|---|---|
| Union in a competitive market: raises wage, cuts employment | Union in monopsony: can raise wage and employment |
| Supply curve (AC): the wage the employer must pay | MCL: the extra cost of an extra worker |
| Employment decided at MCL = MRP | Wage read from supply, not from MCL |
| Insiders: members in work | Outsiders: unemployed or priced out |

## Quick self-test

1. Why does MCL lie above the supply curve for a monopsonist?
2. Where does a monopsonist choose employment, and how is the wage found?
3. In Town B, what are the monopsony wage and employment?
4. Name two factors that make a union stronger.
5. A union in a competitive market raises the wage 10%; employment falls 6%. What does this say about the wage elasticity of demand and the total wage bill?
6. In Town B, a union sets the wage at 11. What happens to employment compared with monopsony?
7. State the effect of a union in a monopsony if the wage is set far above the competitive level.
8. How does imperfect information contribute to labour market imperfection?
9. What is a bilateral monopoly?
10. Give one reason why an employer's monopsony power might be limited.

### Answers

1. One wage is paid to all workers, so hiring one more means raising the pay of existing staff as well as paying the new worker.
2. At the employment level where MCL = MRP; the wage is then read off the supply curve at that level.
3. Wage 8 pounds per hour, employment 800 workers.
4. Any two of: high membership density, inelastic demand for labour, inelastic product demand, labour a small share of costs, few substitutes, scarce skills, low unemployment, profitable firm.
5. Elasticity is −6 ÷ 10 = −0.6, which is inelastic, so the total wage bill rises. As a check, 1.10 × 0.94 = 1.034, a 3.4% rise.
6. Employment is 1,000, which is above the monopsony level of 800.
7. Employment falls below the monopsony level, so the union costs jobs.
8. Workers cannot compare wages or vacancies, so they stay in lower-paid jobs; employers can pay less than the value of workers' output without losing them all.
9. A single seller of labour (a union) facing a single buyer (an employer).
10. Easy movement of workers to other employers or areas makes labour supply more elastic.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Drawing MCL below or on the supply curve.
- Taking the monopsony wage from the MRP or MCL curve.
- Describing the union effect in a competitive market without noting the surplus of labour at the union wage.
- Saying unions always cause unemployment, or always raise employment, with no mention of the market type.
- Forgetting to calculate or compare wage elasticity when asked about the wage bill.
- Listing union power factors without explaining the chain of reasoning behind each one.
- Concluding with a repeat of the points rather than a judgement that depends on stated conditions.
- Ignoring outsiders when judging who benefits.
