These questions cover sub-topic (h) Chemical tests (points 2.44 to 2.50) of Topic 2 in the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Chemistry (4CH1) specification, Issue 3 (September 2024). None of these points has a C reference, so every question suits both Paper 1 and Paper 2. Revise first with the [chemical tests study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-chemistry-4ch1-chemical-tests/) or the [chemical tests revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-chemistry-4ch1-chemical-tests-revision-notes/). ## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** **(a)** Describe the test for carbon dioxide and give the positive result. **[2]**
**(b)** Write the balanced equation, with state symbols, for the reaction in the test. **[1]**

**2.** A student warms a solid with sodium hydroxide solution and holds **dry** red litmus paper at the mouth of the tube. The paper does not change colour, although the solid is an ammonium salt.

**(a)** Explain why the paper did not change colour. **[1]**
**(b)** State the result the student should see with the correct method. **[1]**

**3.** Four solids give these flame colours: lilac, orange-red, yellow and blue-green. Name the cation that gives each colour. **[2]**

**4.** **(a)** Explain why a yellow (luminous) Bunsen flame is not used for a flame test. **[1]**
**(b)** A potassium compound contains a trace of a sodium compound. Explain why the flame test may not show that potassium is present. **[1]**

**5.** Write the ionic equation, with state symbols, for the reaction when sodium hydroxide solution is added to a solution containing iron(III) ions. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why the solution is acidified with dilute hydrochloric acid, and not dilute sulfuric acid, before barium chloride solution is added in the test for sulfate ions. **[2]**

### Section B: longer and structured questions

**7.** Each reaction below gives off a gas. For each, name the gas and describe a test, with its positive result, that identifies it.

**(a)** zinc added to dilute sulfuric acid **[2]**
**(b)** ammonium chloride warmed with sodium hydroxide solution **[2]**
**(c)** calcium carbonate added to dilute hydrochloric acid **[2]**

**8.** A solution, X, is tested.

- Sodium hydroxide solution gives a green precipitate. After some time the top of the precipitate turns brown.
- Dilute hydrochloric acid followed by barium chloride solution gives a white precipitate.

**(a)** Identify the cation and the anion in X. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest why the top of the green precipitate turns brown. **[1]**
**(c)** Write the ionic equation for the formation of the white precipitate. **[1]**

**9.** Four solids, P, Q, R and S, are tested. A dash means no visible change.

| Test | P | Q | R | S |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Flame test | red | no clear colour | lilac | no clear colour |
| Solution + sodium hydroxide, then warm | -- | brown precipitate | -- | no precipitate; gas turns damp red litmus blue |
| Solution + dilute nitric acid + silver nitrate | cream precipitate | white precipitate | fizzes; no precipitate | yellow precipitate |
| Solid + dilute hydrochloric acid | -- | -- | fizzes; gas turns limewater milky | -- |

Name the compound P, Q, R and S. **[8]**

**10.** A student adds barium chloride solution to an unknown solution. A white precipitate forms. The student concludes that the solution contains sulfate ions.

**(a)** Explain why this conclusion may not be valid. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe how the test should be carried out, and give the result that would confirm sulfate ions. **[2]**

**11.** A bottle of colourless liquid is labelled "distilled water".

**(a)** Describe a chemical test to show that the liquid contains water, and give the result. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe a physical test to show whether the water is pure, and give the result for pure water. **[2]**
**(c)** The liquid boils at 100.8 °C at normal atmospheric pressure. State and explain what this shows. **[1]**

**12.** Four unlabelled jars contain white solids: sodium chloride, sodium sulfate, sodium carbonate and potassium chloride. Describe tests that would identify each solid. Give the result of each test. **[6]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Bubble the gas through limewater (calcium hydroxide solution) [1]. The limewater turns milky (cloudy) [1].
**(b)** `Ca(OH)₂(aq) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s) + H₂O(l)` [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "The gas puts out a lighted splint" is not a test for carbon dioxide; several gases do this.

**2. (a)** Ammonia must dissolve in water on the paper to form an alkaline solution; dry paper has no water [1].
**(b)** Damp red litmus paper turns blue [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The paper must also be held at the mouth of the tube, not dipped into the liquid, or the sodium hydroxide itself turns it blue.

**3.** Lilac: K⁺; orange-red: Ca²⁺; yellow: Na⁺; blue-green: Cu²⁺. Any two correct [1]; all four correct [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Red is Li⁺, not Ca²⁺. Learn "orange-red" for calcium as a single phrase.

**4. (a)** The yellow flame has its own colour, which would hide the colour from the sample [1].
**(b)** Sodium's strong yellow colour masks the fainter lilac colour of potassium [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* In (a), "it is not hot enough" does not explain the problem; the issue is the flame's own colour.

**5.** `Fe³⁺(aq) + 3OH⁻(aq) → Fe(OH)₃(s)`: correct formulae and balancing [1]; state symbols [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The number of hydroxide ions matches the charge on the iron ion. Fe(OH)₂ is iron(II) hydroxide.

**6.** The acid removes carbonate ions, which would otherwise form a white precipitate with barium ions [1]. Sulfuric acid contains sulfate ions, so it would always give a white precipitate, whatever the sample contained [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Both parts are needed: why acidify at all, and why this particular acid.

**7. (a)** Hydrogen [1]. A lighted splint at the mouth of the tube gives a squeaky pop [1].
**(b)** Ammonia [1]. Damp red litmus paper held in the gas turns blue [1].
**(c)** Carbon dioxide [1]. Bubbled through limewater, it turns the limewater milky [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A test without a result scores zero for that mark. "Glowing splint" for hydrogen is a common slip; glowing is the oxygen test.

**8. (a)** Cation: iron(II), Fe²⁺ [1]. Anion: sulfate, SO₄²⁻ [1].
**(b)** Iron(II) hydroxide at the surface reacts with oxygen in the air and is oxidised to brown iron(III) hydroxide [1].
**(c)** `Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s)` [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Iron" without (II) scores nothing in (a). Report the colour seen immediately: green.

**9.** P: lithium [1] bromide [1]. Q: iron(III) [1] chloride [1]. R: potassium [1] carbonate [1]. S: ammonium [1] iodide [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Cream is bromide and yellow is iodide. For R, the fizzing with nitric acid is the carbonate reacting; the limewater result confirms it. A name such as "lithium bromide" earns both marks for P.

**10. (a)** Carbonate ions (or other anions) also form a white precipitate with barium ions [1]. No acid was added first to remove carbonate ions [1].
**(b)** Add dilute hydrochloric acid to the solution first, then barium chloride solution [1]. A white precipitate (of barium sulfate) confirms sulfate ions [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Nitric acid is also accepted for acidifying here, but sulfuric acid is never accepted.

**11. (a)** Add a few drops of the liquid to anhydrous copper(II) sulfate [1]. It turns from white to blue [1].
**(b)** Heat the liquid and measure the temperature at which it boils with a thermometer [1]. Pure water boils at 100 °C (or melts at 0 °C) [1].
**(c)** The water is not pure: dissolved impurities raise the boiling point above 100 °C [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Anhydrous copper(II) sulfate shows that water is present, not that it is pure; salt solutions turn it blue too.

**12.** One mark per point:
- Flame test on each solid [1]: potassium chloride gives a lilac flame; the other three give a yellow flame [1].
- Add dilute hydrochloric acid to each sodium solid [1]: sodium carbonate fizzes and the gas turns limewater milky [1].
- Add dilute hydrochloric acid and barium chloride solution to solutions of the remaining two [1]: sodium sulfate gives a white precipitate; sodium chloride does not [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Dilute nitric acid and silver nitrate solution, giving a white precipitate with sodium chloride, is an equally valid final test. A logical order saves tests. Each test needs its reagents in full and the result that identifies the solid; "use the halide test" with no reagents or result is not enough.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving a test without its result, or a result without the test.
- Using dry litmus paper for ammonia or chlorine.
- Confusing lithium (red) with calcium (orange-red), or bromide (cream) with iodide (yellow).
- Writing "iron" without (II) or (III).
- Acidifying with the wrong acid.
- Using anhydrous copper(II) sulfate as a test for purity.
