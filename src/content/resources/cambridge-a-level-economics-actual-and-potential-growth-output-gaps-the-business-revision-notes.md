## Key definitions (9.2.1 and 9.2.2)

| Term | Meaning | Diagram clue |
|---|---|---|
| Actual growth | Percentage rise in real GDP | AD shifts right; move towards the PPC |
| Potential growth | Percentage rise in productive capacity | LRAS shifts right; PPC moves out |
| Output gap | Actual output minus potential output | Actual real GDP is left or right of the LRAS line |
| Positive gap | Actual above potential: overheating | Inflation pressure, low unemployment |
| Negative gap | Actual below potential: spare capacity | Falling inflation, high unemployment |

## Formulas

| Item | Formula |
|---|---|
| Growth rate | (new − old) / old × 100 |
| Output gap (%) | (actual − potential) / potential × 100 |
| Multiplier | 1 / (mps + mpt + mpm), or 1 / (1 − mpc) in a closed economy with no tax |
| Change in income | multiplier × change in injection |
| Injection to close a gap | gap in output / multiplier |
| Potential growth (from inputs) | (1 + labour growth) × (1 + productivity growth) − 1 |

mps is the marginal propensity to save, mpt the marginal rate of tax and mpm the marginal propensity to import.

## Method boxes

**Measuring a gap.** Step 1: find actual and potential output. Step 2: subtract (actual minus potential). Step 3: divide by potential. Step 4: state positive or negative and what it means for unemployment and inflation.

**Does a gap close?** Compare actual growth with potential growth. If actual growth is higher, a negative gap narrows or a positive gap widens. If it is lower, the reverse happens.

**Identifying a phase.** Look at the level of output and its growth rate. Growth falling but positive is a downturn. Falling output is recession. The lowest output is the trough. Rising growth after the trough is recovery. Fast growth with output above trend is a boom.

## Worked reminders

**Gap narrowing.** Country M (invented): actual output rises from 640 to 672 (+5.0%), potential from 700 to 724.5 (+3.5%). Gap before: (640 − 700) / 700 = −8.57%. Gap after: (672 − 724.5) / 724.5 = −7.25%. It narrows but does not close.

**Stabiliser strength.** A fall in exports of 30 with mps = 0.15, mpm = 0.10 and mpt = 0.25 gives a multiplier of 1 / 0.50 = 2.0 and a fall in income of 60. If mpt were 0.10, the multiplier would be 1 / 0.35 = 2.86 and the fall would be 85.7. A higher marginal tax rate means a smaller swing.

**Tax revenue.** If income falls by 50 and the marginal tax rate is 0.25, revenue falls by 12.5 without any decision.

**Closing a negative gap.** Actual 1,045, potential 1,100, multiplier 2.2. Gap = −5.0%. Output must rise by 55, so injection = 55 / 2.2 = 25.

**Potential growth from inputs.** Labour force +2.0%, output per worker +1.5%: (1.02 × 1.015) − 1 = 3.53%.

## Chains of reasoning to learn

**Positive output gap.** Aggregate demand above sustainable capacity, then firms use overtime and run short of labour, then wages and costs rise, then firms raise prices, then inflation accelerates. Imports are also pulled in as demand outruns domestic supply. The gap cannot last: it closes through higher prices, tighter policy or a downturn.

**Negative output gap.** Weak demand, then firms cut output and jobs, then unemployment rises and income falls, then spending weakens further through the multiplier. Inflation pressure falls because firms and workers have little pricing power. Lost output is never recovered, and a long gap can cut potential output by reducing investment and skills.

**Higher potential growth.** More or better factors or technology, then lower costs per unit and more capacity, then LRAS shifts right, then higher output can be sustained with lower inflation pressure.

**Stabiliser in a slump.** Income falls, then tax revenue falls and benefit payments rise, then disposable income falls by less than income, then consumption is supported and the fall in aggregate demand is smaller.

## More worked reminders

**Stimulus matched to the gap.** Country S (invented): potential output 1,500, multiplier 2.0, extra government spending 40. If actual output is 1,380 (gap −8.0%), the rise is 80, to 1,460, and the gap becomes −2.67%. If actual output is 1,560 (gap +4.0%), the rise gives 1,640 and a gap of +9.33%. The same policy helps in the first case and overheats the economy in the second.

**Index numbers across the cycle.** Growth of +2%, −1% and −2% from a base of 100 gives 102, 100.98 and 98.96. Output has fallen below the base year, so the economy is in recession. Two further years of +3% give 101.93 and 104.99. Output regains the peak of 102 only in the final year, even though growth was positive in both years.

## The business cycle (9.2.3)

| Phase | Growth and output | Unemployment | Inflation | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Boom or peak | Fast growth, output high | Low | Rising | Positive |
| Downturn | Growth slows or turns negative | Starts to rise | Slowing | Narrowing |
| Recession or trough | Output falls to its lowest | High | Low or falling | Negative |
| Recovery | Growth returns | Falls | Starts to rise | Closing |

**Causes to recall (D C A M S P):**

- **D**emand expectations and confidence.
- **C**redit and interest rates.
- **A**ccelerator effect on investment.
- **M**ultiplier magnifying changes in spending.
- **S**hocks: external demand, commodity prices, technology, natural events, stock cycles.
- **P**olicy mistakes or time lags.

**Automatic stabilisers.** Progressive income tax and benefits linked to unemployment change automatically with income. In a boom they raise tax revenue and cut benefit spending, withdrawing demand. In a slump they do the opposite. They reduce the size of the cycle, not remove it. They are not discretionary policy.

## Policies to promote growth (9.2.4)

| Policy | Targets | Main strength | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Higher government spending or lower taxes | Actual growth | Fast; multiplier | Inflation near capacity; deficit; crowding out |
| Lower interest rates | Actual growth | Quick to implement | Weak if confidence is low; lags |
| Education, training | Potential growth | Raises productivity | Long lags; costly |
| Infrastructure | Both | Raises capacity and demand | Costly; poor projects waste money |
| Tax incentives, R&D support | Potential growth | Raises investment | Uncertain; may favour the rich |
| Competition, deregulation, trade opening | Potential growth | Lower costs | Job losses; winners and losers |

**Evaluation prompts.** Which gap is the economy in? Is the problem demand or supply? How long will the policy take? What does it cost and who pays? Does it worsen inflation, the budget, the current account or the environment? Is the effect temporary or lasting?

**Judging effectiveness:** time lag, size and sign of the gap, cost and crowding out, effect on other objectives, short run against long run, how reliable estimates are.

## Must-know distinctions

- Actual growth moves AD along SRAS. Potential growth moves LRAS.
- A negative gap does not mean negative growth. Output can grow while the gap stays negative.
- Automatic stabilisers happen without a decision. Discretionary policy needs a decision.
- Recession is a fall in output. A downturn is a slowing of growth.
- Demand-side policy is quicker but temporary. Supply-side policy is slower but raises capacity.

## Quick self-test

1. Define potential growth.
2. Actual output is 1,155 and potential output is 1,100. Calculate the output gap.
3. Actual is 1,045 and potential 1,100. The multiplier is 2.2. How big an injection closes the gap?
4. The labour force grows 2.0% and output per worker 1.5%. Calculate potential growth.
5. Why can actual growth exceed potential growth only for a time?
6. Give two automatic stabilisers.
7. Real GDP index 100 rises 3%, falls 1%, then rises 2.5%. What is the final index?
8. mps = 0.1, mpt = 0.3, mpm = 0.1. Investment falls by 15. Calculate the fall in income.
9. Growth was 3.0%, then 1.2%, then 0.4%. Which phase is this?
10. Why is the size of an output gap uncertain?

### Answers

1. The percentage increase in the economy's productive capacity.
2. (1,155 − 1,100) / 1,100 × 100 = +5.0%, a positive gap.
3. Gap in output 55; 55 / 2.2 = 25.
4. 1.02 × 1.015 − 1 = 3.53%.
5. Output cannot stay above capacity: shortages and wage and price pressure build up.
6. Progressive income tax and unemployment benefits.
7. 100 × 1.03 × 0.99 × 1.025 = 104.5.
8. Multiplier = 1 / 0.5 = 2.0; fall = 30.
9. A downturn: growth is positive but slowing.
10. Potential output is estimated, not observed, and is revised.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing the gap by actual output instead of potential.
- Treating a positive gap as only good news, with no mention of inflation.
- Confusing the cycle phases: slowdown, recession and trough are not the same.
- Saying stabilisers need a government decision.
- Using a demand-side policy for a supply problem, or the reverse.
- Listing policies with no judgement of timing, cost or side effects.
- Forgetting that stimulus in a positive-gap economy mainly raises prices.
- Not using the figures when the question gives data.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does a negative output gap mean negative growth?**
No. Output can grow while the gap stays negative, if actual output is still below potential.

**What is the difference between a recession and a downturn?**
A recession is a fall in output. A downturn, or slowdown, is a slowing of growth that may or may not turn negative.

**Is a positive output gap good news?**
Not only. Actual output above potential means low unemployment but also inflation pressure, as resources are used beyond their normal level.

**How do demand-side and supply-side growth policies differ?**
Demand-side policy is quicker but temporary and moves actual output. Supply-side policy is slower but raises productive capacity.
