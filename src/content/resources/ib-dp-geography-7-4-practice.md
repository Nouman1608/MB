## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between a linear and a circular urban metabolism. **[2]**

**2.** Outline one advantage and one disadvantage of a green belt. **[2]**

**3.** State the main aim of a sponge city. **[1]**

**4.** Outline the idea of the 15-minute city. **[2]**

**5.** Define the runoff coefficient. **[1]**

**6.** Suggest two indicators that could show whether a city is becoming more socially sustainable. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** An invented car park of 12,000 m squared is redeveloped. Before, the whole area was asphalt (runoff coefficient 0.95). After, 5,000 m squared is green roof (coefficient 0.35), 4,000 m squared is permeable paving (0.45) and 3,000 m squared remains asphalt. A storm delivers 25 mm of rain.

**(a)** Calculate the runoff before redevelopment. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the runoff after redevelopment. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the percentage reduction in runoff. **[1]**

**(d)** Explain why the reduction may be smaller in a very intense storm. **[2]**

**8.** Invented emission factors: car 0.18 kg carbon dioxide per passenger-km; light rail 0.04 kg per passenger-km. A corridor carries 15,000 commuters each travelling 10 km.

**(a)** Calculate the daily emissions if all travel by car. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the daily emissions if 30% switch to light rail. **[2]**

**(c)** State one assumption that affects the light rail emission factor. **[1]**

**9.** Explain how an urban growth boundary can contribute to sustainability, and outline one criticism of it. **[4]**

**10.** Suggest why a 15-minute city strategy might be harder to apply in a low-density suburb than in a dense city centre. **[3]**

## Section C

**11.** "Technology will be the key to building sustainable cities." To what extent do you agree? **[10]**

**12.** Evaluate the extent to which one or more named cities have become more sustainable. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** A linear metabolism takes in resources, uses them once and discharges waste and emissions [1]; a circular metabolism reuses and recycles resources, such as treating wastewater for reuse and turning organic waste into compost or energy [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Advantage: protects countryside and limits sprawl [1]. Disadvantage: can raise house prices inside the belt or push development to towns beyond it, lengthening commutes [1]. **[2]**

**3.** To absorb, store and reuse rainwater rather than piping it away, reducing flooding and water shortage [1]. **[1]**

**4.** Residents should be able to reach daily needs such as work, shops, school, health care and parks [1] within a 15-minute walk or cycle ride, reducing car use [1]. **[2]**

**5.** The proportion of rainfall that runs off a surface rather than soaking in or evaporating [1]. **[1]**

**6.** Any two, for example: share of households spending more than a set share of income on housing [1]; share of residents in informal housing [1]; share of residents within walking distance of green space [1]; share of journeys made by public transport by low-income groups [1]. Maximum [2]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** 12,000 x 0.025 x 0.95 = **285 m cubed** [1].

**(b)** Green roof: 5,000 x 0.025 x 0.35 = 43.75; permeable paving: 4,000 x 0.025 x 0.45 = 45; asphalt: 3,000 x 0.025 x 0.95 = 71.25 [1]. Total = **160 m cubed** [1].

**(c)** (285 - 160) / 285 x 100 = **43.9%** [1].

**(d)** Green roofs and permeable surfaces can store only a limited amount of water [1]; once they are saturated, or if rain falls faster than water can infiltrate, they behave more like impermeable surfaces and runoff increases [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** 15,000 x 10 x 0.18 = **27,000 kg** [1].

**(b)** Cars: 10,500 x 10 x 0.18 = 18,900 kg; light rail: 4,500 x 10 x 0.04 = 1,800 kg [1]. Total = **20,700 kg** [1].

**(c)** For example, how full the trains are, or how the electricity is generated [1]. **[4]**

**9.** The boundary restricts development beyond a set line [1], encouraging denser building and brownfield reuse inside it, which supports public transport and shorter journeys and protects farmland and habitats outside [1] [1]. Criticism: limiting land supply can push up housing costs, reducing affordability, as critics have argued in Portland, Oregon [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Suburbs are mainly residential, with shops, schools and workplaces far apart [1]. Low density means fewer customers within walking distance, so local services may not reach their threshold [1]. Achieving the goal would require rezoning for mixed use and denser building, which residents may oppose [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Indicative content:

- **Arguments for:** smart-city sensors manage traffic and energy, as in Songdo [1]; renewable energy and efficient buildings cut emissions [1]; sponge-city drainage reduces flooding [1].
- **Limits of technology:** high cost and slow retrofitting in existing cities [1]; flagship cities such as Masdar have been built more slowly and on a smaller scale than promised [1]; privacy concerns and exclusion of people without digital access [1].
- **Non-technological factors:** planning, zoning and governance, as in Curitiba's low-cost bus system linked to land use [1]; social urbanism in Medellin shows access and safety matter [1].
- **Equity and affordability:** technology does not by itself solve housing costs or informal settlements [1].
- **Judgement:** for example, disagree partly: technology is a useful tool, but planning and inclusion decide whether cities become sustainable [1]. **[10]**

**12.** Indicative content:

- **Criteria:** environmental, economic and social indicators, with evidence of change over time [1].
- **Curitiba:** bus rapid transit and zoning shaped growth for decades [1]; metropolitan growth has outrun the plan and car ownership has risen [1].
- **Portland:** growth boundary and light rail support dense neighbourhoods [1]; affordability concerns [1].
- **Rotterdam or sponge cities:** climate adaptation through water storage [1]; costly to scale up [1].
- **Medellin:** social urbanism improved access for hillside neighbourhoods [1].
- **Trade-offs:** green gentrification and affordability can worsen while emissions fall [1].
- **Judgement:** a supported conclusion stating how far, and on which pillars, the named cities have progressed [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* both essays reward evaluation across all three pillars and a clear judgement rather than a list of features.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Forgetting to convert millimetres of rain to metres.
- Reporting the remaining runoff as the reduction.
- Ignoring housing costs when evaluating growth controls.
- Describing sustainable cities with no named examples.
