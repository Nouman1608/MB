## Section A

**1.** Identify **three** stakeholder groups of a manufacturing business. **[3]**

**2.** Define a non-financial factor and give **one** example. **[2]**

**3.** A manager proposes cutting spending on health and safety training. Explain **one** way in which this could affect the workforce. **[3]**

**4.** State **two** ways in which the use of natural resources could be affected by an accounting decision. **[2]**

**5.** A company wants to move its factory to a cheaper site. The saving on rent and running costs would be 90,000 a year. The cost of moving is 270,000. Calculate the payback period in years. **[2]**

## Section B

Larkin Ceramics employs 60 people in a factory in a small town. The directors plan to move production to a cheaper site 200 kilometres away. The move would save 90,000 a year in rent and running costs and would cost 270,000. About 45 of the 60 workers would not move. The factory is the town's largest employer and uses a local clay supply. The new site has a cheaper but less clean energy supply.

**6.** Explain **two** effects of the move on the local community. **[4]**

**7.** Prepare a table of **four** stakeholders, stating how each would be affected by the move. **[8]**

**8.** Larkin could instead stay and spend 6,000 a year more on recycled packaging, which is 0.75% of annual sales of 800,000. Show that this is 0.75% and explain **one** possible non-financial benefit of making the change. **[3]**

**9.** Evaluate whether Larkin Ceramics should move production. Use the information and reach a justified conclusion. **[8]**

---

## Answers

**1.** Any three: owners or shareholders [1]; employees [1]; customers [1]; suppliers [1]; lenders [1]; government or tax authorities [1]; the local community [1].

**2.** A factor that cannot easily be measured in money, or that does not appear in the financial statements [1]. Example: reputation, employee morale, customer satisfaction, safety, or the environment [1].

**3.** Workers may be less aware of how to use machines safely, so accidents could become more likely [1]; injured workers may take time off, reducing output and increasing costs [1]; morale and trust may fall, causing higher staff turnover [1].

**4.** Any two: resources may be used up faster, for example more timber or water [1]; the choice of supplier may affect the sustainability of the resource [1]; the business may save resources by recycling or reducing waste [1]; costs may rise in future as resources become scarcer [1].

**5.** Payback = 270,000 ÷ 90,000 [1] = **3 years** [1].

**6.** Any two effects with development, 2 marks each: job losses, as 45 workers would not move [1], reducing spending power in the town, so shops and services lose trade [1]; the town loses its largest employer [1], so unemployment rises and the town may decline [1]; less traffic and noise from the factory [1], which could improve the local environment [1]; the closure may harm the company's reputation locally [1], reducing goodwill and local sales [1].

**7.** Any four stakeholders, 2 marks each (stakeholder 1, effect with reason 1):

| Stakeholder | Effect |
|---|---|
| Owners | Gain 90,000 a year after a 3-year payback, so higher profits; risk to reputation [2] |
| Employees | 45 lose their jobs; the remaining 15 must move or lose their jobs; uncertainty reduces morale [2] |
| Local community | Lose the town's largest employer and spending in local businesses [2] |
| Local suppliers | The local clay supplier loses a customer or faces higher transport costs [2] |
| Customers | May gain if the savings lead to lower prices, but may lose if deliveries or quality suffer in the transition [2] |
| Government | Loses income tax and may pay unemployment benefit [2] |

**8.** 6,000 ÷ 800,000 × 100 = **0.75%** [1]. Benefit: a better reputation with customers who care about the environment [1], which could attract more sales or reduce the risk of fines [1].

**9.** For moving: the saving of 90,000 a year is large and the payback is 3 years [1]; the owners benefit and the saving could fund growth or safeguard other jobs [1]. Against moving: 45 workers lose their jobs and the town loses its main employer [1]; the company may lose skilled staff and the knowledge they hold, so quality or output could suffer [1]; the cheaper energy is less clean, harming the environment [1]; relocation could harm reputation locally [1]. Alternatives: the company could negotiate with the staff, keep part of the production in the town, retrain staff, or phase the move over a longer period [1].
Judgement: move only if the company can reduce harm. The financial case is strong (payback in 3 years) but the social cost is high, so the best course may be a phased move, support for staff who leave and cleaner energy at the new site. The conclusion must follow the evidence and weigh both sides [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving only financial points in an evaluation (Question 9).
- Listing stakeholders without stating how each is affected (Question 7).
- Writing general statements with no link to the case study (Question 6).
- Giving a conclusion that does not follow from the argument.
- Ignoring figures given in the question, such as the 3-year payback.

## Approaching these questions

Underline the numbers and the groups of people in the case study. For each group, write one effect and one reason. For evaluation, write a financial argument, a social argument, an alternative, and a judgement. Make the judgement specific to the case.
