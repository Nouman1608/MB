Condensed recall for **12.2 Changing identities and cultures** (Unit 5: Human development and
diversity). For the full explanation, read the
[Human development and diversity study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-human-development-diversity-study-guide/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Culture | Language, religion, food, dress, values and practices |
| Identity | How individuals and groups see themselves and are seen by others |
| Cultural diffusion | Spread of cultural traits through migration, trade, media and digital networks |
| Cultural homogenisation | Cultures converge towards a shared, often Western or commercial, pattern |
| Hybridisation / glocalisation | Blending of global and local features (glocalisation: Roland Robertson); for example global brands adapting products to local tastes |
| Cultural resistance and revival | Communities defend or reinvigorate traditions, language or heritage because they feel threatened |
| Multiple identities | Overlapping local, national, ethnic and global identities |

Migration and digital life can intensify multiple identities.

Further terms worth using:

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Diaspora | A population living outside its place of origin that keeps cultural and economic links with it |
| Cultural imperialism | The argument that powerful countries and corporations impose their culture on others |
| McDonaldization | George Ritzer's term for the spread of standardised, efficiency-driven ways of producing and consuming |
| Lingua franca | A shared language used between speakers of different languages, now often English |

## How culture moves

- **Migration and diasporas** carry food, religion, music and language; diaspora communities often
  blend the culture of origin with that of the host society.
- **Media and streaming** distribute film, music and television globally almost instantly.
- **Transnational corporations** spread brands, products and working practices.
- **The internet and social media** let ideas, trends and language spread peer to peer, not only
  from powerful centres outwards.
- **Tourism and education** expose people to other cultures, and study abroad builds networks.

Flows are not only from the West. The **Korean Wave (Hallyu)** shows diffusion from an Asian
economy: K-pop groups such as BTS and Korean series such as *Squid Game* (2021) gained huge global
audiences, helped by streaming platforms and by South Korean government support for cultural
exports as a form of soft power. Bollywood films and Japanese anime are other examples.

## Evidence for each argument

| Homogenisation (erosion) | Hybridisation and new forms |
|---|---|
| Global brands and chains appear in cities worldwide | McDonald's in India sells no beef and offers vegetarian items such as the McAloo Tikki |
| English spreads as the language of business, science and the internet | Global streaming services commission local-language content for local audiences |
| UNESCO considers a large share of the world's roughly 7,000 languages to be endangered | Diaspora communities create new fusion food, music and fashion |
| Young people in many countries share similar consumer tastes | Non-Western cultural exports (Hallyu, Bollywood) reach global audiences |

## Resistance and revival

- **Welsh language revival:** a Welsh-language television channel (S4C, from 1982), the Welsh
  Language Act of 1993, Welsh-medium schooling and a government aim of one million Welsh speakers
  by 2050.
- **Māori language revival in New Zealand:** "language nest" pre-schools (kōhanga reo) from the
  early 1980s, and recognition of Māori as an official language in 1987.
- **Protecting national culture:** quotas for local-language music on radio, as in France, or for
  domestic films in cinemas.

Revival shows that globalisation can provoke a stronger sense of local identity rather than simply
erasing it.

## Two arguments to weigh

1. Globalisation **erodes diversity** (homogenisation).
2. Globalisation **creates new cultural forms** (hybridisation, glocalisation).

The best answers give evidence for each side from a named place, then **reach a judgement** about
which is stronger in that context.

## Who is affected

Media, language and migration do not affect all age groups or social classes in the same way.
Younger people and city dwellers may adopt new influences faster; others may act to protect
traditions.

A second-generation migrant may feel British at school, Pakistani at home and Muslim at the mosque,
and switch identity by context; these **multiple identities** are a normal outcome of global
interaction rather than a sign of confusion. Identity is also shaped by how others see a group,
which can mean discrimination or stereotyping.

## Model paragraph (to what extent)

*"The spread of global brands into India might suggest homogenisation, but McDonald's had to remove
beef and build a largely vegetarian menu to succeed there, so the global brand adapted to local
culture rather than replacing it. At the same time, English-language media are changing the tastes
of young urban Indians faster than those of older rural people. Globalisation has therefore produced
hybrid forms in food while increasing convergence in some youth consumer culture, so the effect is
uneven rather than a simple loss of diversity."*

## Exam traps

- Presenting homogenisation as inevitable and complete.
- Writing about culture in general with no named place.
- Treating a community as having one voice.
- Confusing culture with identity.
- Assuming cultural flows run only from the West to the rest.
- Using glocalisation examples without explaining why the company adapted.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish culture from identity.
2. Define cultural diffusion.
3. What is glocalisation?
4. Why might communities revive traditions?
5. Why is a judgement needed in a 12.2 answer?
6. What is a diaspora?
7. Give one example of cultural diffusion that does not come from the West.
8. Give one piece of evidence for homogenisation.
9. Name two measures used to revive the Welsh language.
10. What does McDonaldization mean?
11. Why might two members of the same family respond differently to global culture?

**Answers:** 1. Culture is the practices and values; identity is how people see themselves and are seen.
2. The spread of cultural traits between places. 3. Blending of global and local features, such as
adapting global brands to local tastes. 4. They feel threatened by change. 5. Evidence supports
both erosion and new forms, so you must decide which is stronger in the context. 6. A population
living outside its place of origin that keeps links with it. 7. For example the Korean Wave (K-pop,
Korean television) or Bollywood. 8. For example the spread of English or of global chains, or
endangered languages. 9. For example S4C, the 1993 Welsh Language Act or Welsh-medium schooling.
10. Ritzer's term for the spread of standardised, efficiency-driven production and consumption.
11. Age, education, place of residence and social networks shape exposure and attitudes.
