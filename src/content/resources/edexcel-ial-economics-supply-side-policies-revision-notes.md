## Core idea (3a)

Supply-side policies aim to raise potential output, shown as a rightward shift of the vertical long-run aggregate supply curve (general price level up the side, real output along the bottom). They work through three routes.

| Route | Meaning | Example policy |
|---|---|---|
| Productivity | More output per worker or per hour | Training, investment, infrastructure |
| Competition | More rivalry, lower costs, more innovation | Deregulation, privatisation with competition, start-up finance |
| Incentives | Greater reward for work, saving and investment | Income tax cuts, welfare changes, investment allowances |

## Sorting the policies (3b and 3c)

| Free market (3b) | Interventionist (3c) |
|---|---|
| Deregulation of product and labour markets | Investment in education, training and skills |
| Privatisation | Tax incentives or subsidies for investment |
| Reduction in taxation | Infrastructure investment |
| Changing the levels of welfare payments | Finance for business start-ups |
| Cutting the costs of bureaucracy for firms | Regional policy |

Rule of thumb: free market policies shrink the state's role; interventionist policies use state money or action to correct a market failure.

## Policy cards

**Deregulation.** Chain: fewer barriers to entry, more firms, more competition, lower costs and prices, higher productivity. Labour version: easier hiring raises labour mobility and lowers the cost of taking on workers. Weakness: less protection for workers, consumers and the environment.

**Privatisation.** Chain: profit motive, cost cutting, lower unit costs, possibly lower prices. Weakness: private monopoly, loss of unprofitable services, short-run job losses. One-off sale revenue is a fiscal gain, not a supply-side effect.

**Tax reduction.** Lower marginal rates raise the reward to work; lower corporation tax raises returns to investment and may attract foreign direct investment. Weakness: small response if labour supply is inelastic, income effects, lost revenue, and benefits go mainly to higher earners.

**Welfare changes.** Lower or tougher-to-claim benefits raise the gain from working. Weakness: inequality, and no effect if there are no vacancies.

**Cutting bureaucracy.** Lower compliance costs, more start-ups. Weakness: some rules protect safety; savings may be small.

**Education, training and skills.** Higher human capital, higher productivity, better occupational mobility. Weakness: long time lag, high cost, skill mismatch.

**Investment incentives.** More capital per worker. Weakness: deadweight, since some firms would have invested anyway, and the policy depends on firms' confidence.

**Infrastructure.** Lower costs, wider markets, supports growth. Weakness: very costly and slow, and poor project choice is government failure.

**Start-up finance.** Eases borrowing constraints for new firms. Weakness: many fail.

**Regional policy.** Reduces regional unemployment and inequality. Weakness: dependency on subsidy and firms may leave when support ends.

## Formulas and methods

| Item | Formula |
|---|---|
| Productivity | Output / number of workers (or hours) |
| Percentage change | (new / old - 1) x 100 |
| Replacement rate | Benefit / net wage from work |
| Tax revenue | Tax rate x taxable base |
| Break-even base for a tax cut | Old revenue / new tax rate |
| Cost per unit of extra investment | Subsidy cost / additional investment caused |
| Compound growth | Starting value x (1 + g) raised to the number of years |

**Method for a tax-cut revenue question.**

1. Revenue before = old rate x old base.
2. Revenue after = new rate x new base.
3. Break-even base = old revenue / new rate.
4. Express the rise in base needed as a percentage.

**Small worked reminders.**

- Output 150 billion cu with 5.0 million workers is 30,000 cu per worker. Output of 156 billion cu gives 31,200 cu, a rise of 4.0%.
- Benefit 90 cu and net wage 150 cu give a replacement rate of 60%. A cut in benefit to 75 cu gives 50%.
- Tax rate 25% on a base of 400 billion cu raises 100 billion cu. At 20%, the base must reach 100 / 0.20 = 500 billion cu, a rise of 25%.
- Compounding: 2% growth for 5 years gives 1.1041; 3% gives 1.1593. Potential output ends 5.0% higher on the faster path.

## Evaluating: the set of criteria

Time lag, cost and opportunity cost, size of the response, equity, certainty, and effect on other objectives. A good judgement picks the criteria that matter in the case given and names the condition on which the answer turns. For example, if unemployment is structural, training suits better than a demand stimulus; if firms face high red tape, deregulation may deliver faster.

## Matching policy to problem

| Problem | Policy that fits | Main limit |
|---|---|---|
| Workers lack skills employers want | Education, training and skills | Slow, costly |
| Weak incentive to take a job | Tax cut, welfare change | Inequality, lost revenue |
| Firms face high compliance costs | Cutting bureaucracy, deregulation | Weaker protection |
| Low investment per worker | Tax incentives, subsidies | Deadweight |
| High costs of moving goods and people | Infrastructure | Cost, long build time |
| New firms cannot borrow | Start-up finance | High failure rate |
| One area has high unemployment | Regional policy | Dependency |
| State firm is inefficient | Privatisation | Monopoly power |

## Method for a policy evaluation answer

1. Define the policy in one line and place it as free market or interventionist.
2. Give a chain of at least three links ending in a shift of LRAS or higher productivity.
3. Add a second line of analysis: a cost, a time lag or a side effect on another objective.
4. Weigh it against a rival policy using two or three criteria.
5. Finish with a judgement tied to a condition, such as how strongly people respond or which kind of unemployment is present.

**Short model paragraph.** Training subsidies raise the skills of workers, so output per worker rises and LRAS shifts right. The effect is slow, because trained workers take years to arrive, and costly, because the budget has to find the money. A tax cut could act faster but it favours higher earners and loses revenue. Which is better depends on whether the main problem is skills or the reward for working.

## Must-know distinctions

- Free market versus interventionist: tax reductions are free market; tax incentives or subsidies for investment are interventionist.
- Supply-side versus demand-side: supply-side policy targets potential output (LRAS); demand-side policy targets aggregate demand.
- Short-run versus long-run effect: a subsidy can raise demand quickly but raises capacity only slowly.
- Output per worker versus output per hour.

## Quick self-test

1. Is a cut in the top income tax rate a free market or an interventionist policy?
2. Is finance for business start-ups a free market or an interventionist policy?
3. Output rises from 150 billion cu to 156 billion cu with 5.0 million workers. Find the percentage rise in output per worker.
4. Benefit is 90 cu and net pay is 150 cu. Find the replacement rate, then find it after benefit falls to 75 cu.
5. Tax at 25% on a base of 400 billion cu is cut to 20%. Find the base that keeps revenue unchanged.
6. State what is meant by deadweight in an investment subsidy scheme.
7. Potential output grows at 2% a year for 5 years, then at 3% for 5 years from the same start. By what percentage is the 3% path higher after 5 years?
8. A privatised firm cuts unit cost from 40 cu to 36 cu. Find the percentage change.
9. Give two weaknesses of spending more on education and training.
10. Describe in words the diagram that shows a successful supply-side policy.

### Answers

1. Free market (reduction in taxation).
2. Interventionist.
3. 156 / 5.0 = 31,200 cu per worker and 150 / 5.0 = 30,000 cu, so a rise of 4.0%.
4. 90 / 150 = 60%; 75 / 150 = 50%.
5. 25% x 400 = 100 billion cu; 100 / 0.20 = 500 billion cu, so the base must rise by 25%.
6. Subsidy paid for investment that firms would have undertaken without it, so it does not add to total investment.
7. 1.03^5 = 1.1593, 1.02^5 = 1.1041; 1.1593 / 1.1041 - 1 = 5.0%.
8. (36 / 40 - 1) x 100 = -10%.
9. Any two: long time lag; high cost and opportunity cost; skills may not match employers' needs; trained workers may leave the area or country.
10. With the price level up the side and real output along the bottom, the vertical LRAS curve shifts right from Y1 to Y2, so potential output rises.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a policy without explaining the chain to higher supply.
- Mixing up free market and interventionist categories.
- Treating a tax cut as only a supply-side measure and ignoring its demand effect.
- Giving a list of strengths or weaknesses with no judgement.
- Ignoring time lags when comparing policies.
- Calculating a percentage change from the wrong base.
- Forgetting that a welfare cut works only if vacancies exist.
- Quoting figures with no source or label; say when a figure is illustrative.
