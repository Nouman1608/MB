## Principles of genetic engineering (6.1.3(f)(i))

**Genetic engineering** is the deliberate transfer of a gene from one organism into another so that the recipient makes the gene's product. This works because the genetic code is universal: a bacterium's ribosomes read a human gene using the same codons. The organism that now carries the foreign gene is **transgenic**, and DNA made by joining sequences from different sources is **recombinant DNA**.

Every project has the same four stages.

1. **Isolate** the gene of interest.
2. **Insert** it into a **vector**, a carrier that can enter a host cell and is copied or expressed there.
3. **Transfer** the recombinant vector into host cells.
4. **Identify** the cells that took it up, and grow them.

### Isolating the gene

- **Restriction enzymes** cut the gene out of the donor's DNA, though it must then be found among many fragments.
- **Reverse transcriptase** can make complementary DNA (cDNA) from mRNA. Cells that make a lot of a protein hold a lot of its mRNA. For example, pancreatic beta cells hold insulin mRNA. The cDNA is a copy of the coding sequence with no introns, which suits a bacterial host that cannot splice them out.
- A **gene machine** can build a DNA sequence from known bases.

### Vectors

A **plasmid** is a small circular loop of DNA in bacteria, separate from the main chromosome, which copies itself and can carry useful genes. Other vectors include **viruses** and, in plants, the Ti plasmid of *Agrobacterium tumefaciens*. A useful plasmid vector has an origin of replication, a restriction site and a **marker gene**.

The inserted gene also needs a **promoter** at its start, so RNA polymerase can begin transcription in the host, and a terminator at its end. Without a promoter that works in the host, the gene is present but not expressed.

## Techniques (6.1.3(f)(ii))

### Restriction enzymes

A **restriction enzyme** (restriction endonuclease) cuts DNA by hydrolysing phosphodiester bonds, but only where it finds its specific **recognition sequence**. The shape of the active site is complementary to that sequence only. EcoRI recognises 5′-GAATTC-3′ and cuts each strand after the G. The sequence reads the same 5′ to 3′ on both strands (palindromic), so the cuts are staggered. This leaves short single-stranded overhangs called **sticky ends**. Some enzymes cut straight across and leave **blunt ends**.

If you cut the gene and the plasmid with the **same** enzyme, the sticky ends are complementary and will pair by hydrogen bonds. That pairing is called **annealing**.

**Worked example 1: fragments from a digest.** A linear piece of DNA is 7,400 base pairs (bp) long. EcoRI cuts it at positions 1,900 and 5,200 bp from the left end.

1. Two cuts in a linear molecule give three pieces.
2. Left piece: 1,900 bp.
3. Middle piece: 5,200 − 1,900 = 3,300 bp.
4. Right piece: 7,400 − 5,200 = 2,200 bp.
5. Check: the three pieces sum to 7,400 bp.

A circular plasmid cut at two sites gives two pieces, not three.

**Worked example 2: how often does a site occur?** A six-base site has 4⁶ = 4,096 possible sequences, so in random DNA it appears about once per 4,096 bp. A 4,700 bp plasmid might have about one, which is why vectors are chosen to have a single useful site.

### DNA ligase

Annealed sticky ends are held only by hydrogen bonds and can separate. **DNA ligase** makes **phosphodiester bonds** where the gene and plasmid backbones meet, closing the nicks permanently.

**Worked example 3: checking the product.** A vector of 4,700 bp is opened with one EcoRI cut and a 1,150 bp gene, released by the same enzyme, is ligated in.

- Recombinant plasmid = 4,700 + 1,150 = 5,850 bp.
- Cutting it again with EcoRI should give 4,700 bp and 1,150 bp pieces, confirming the insert.

### Getting DNA into the host cell

| Method | How it works | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| **Electroporation** | A brief high-voltage pulse creates temporary pores in the cell surface membrane (and wall) so DNA can pass through | Bacteria, yeast, some plant and animal cells |
| **Heat shock with calcium ions** | Chilled cells in calcium chloride take up plasmid DNA when briefly warmed | Bacteria |
| **Agrobacterium** | The Ti plasmid carrying the gene transfers it into plant DNA | Plants |
| **Viral vector** | A modified virus carries the gene into the cell | Animal cells, gene therapy |

Most cells take up no plasmid.

### Identifying transformed cells

A **marker gene** is put in the vector. Two common types are:

- **Antibiotic resistance.** Only cells with the plasmid grow on agar containing the antibiotic.
- **Green fluorescent protein (GFP).** Transformed cells glow under ultraviolet light.

**Insertional inactivation** adds a second check. If the gene is inserted into the middle of one resistance gene, that resistance is lost. Colonies that grew on antibiotic A but fail on antibiotic B carry a recombinant plasmid.

**Worked example 4: counting recombinants.** A vector has one gene giving resistance to spectinomycin and another giving resistance to chloramphenicol. The gene of interest is placed in the middle of the chloramphenicol gene. Treated bacteria are spread on spectinomycin agar and 248 colonies grow. Replica plating copies them onto chloramphenicol agar, where 59 colonies grow.

1. All 248 hold a plasmid, because they resist spectinomycin.
2. The 59 that also resist chloramphenicol have an intact gene, so no insert.
3. Recombinants: 248 − 59 = 189.
4. Percentage: 189 ÷ 248 × 100 = 76.2%.

**Worked example 5: transformation efficiency.** A tube receives 0.05 µg of plasmid. After recovery the cells are in 2.0 cm³ of broth, and 0.2 cm³ is plated, giving 130 colonies.

1. Colonies in the whole tube: 130 × (2.0 ÷ 0.2) = 1,300.
2. Efficiency: 1,300 ÷ 0.05 = 26,000 colonies per µg of DNA.

## Ethical issues (6.1.3(g))

Give both sides and a reasoned judgement: who benefits, who may be harmed, and what is uncertain. Ethical issues for humans are covered under gene therapy below.

### Plants: insect-resistant soya

A gene from the bacterium *Bacillus thuringiensis* codes for a protein toxic to certain insect larvae, and it can be put into soya plants.

| Positive | Negative |
|---|---|
| Less crop lost to pests, so higher yield and income | Non-target insects may be harmed |
| Less insecticide sprayed, which saves money and reduces pollution | Pests may evolve resistance to the toxin through selection |
| Food supply is more secure | Genes could spread to wild relatives through pollen, and long-term effects are uncertain |

**Worked example 6: judging a trial.** In a trial, conventional soya gave a mean yield of 2.9 tonnes per hectare and the modified variety gave 3.4. The modified plots were sprayed 2 times in the season; the conventional plots 6 times.

- Yield increase: (3.4 − 2.9) ÷ 2.9 × 100 = 17.2%.
- Fall in spraying: (6 − 2) ÷ 6 × 100 = 66.7%.
- Judgement: both measures favour the modified crop, but one trial says nothing about wild insects or resistance building up.

### Microorganisms: modified pathogens for research

Researchers alter bacteria and viruses, for example adding a fluorescent protein gene so infection can be followed, or removing a gene to find its role. This improves understanding of disease. The negative case is **biosecurity**: a modified pathogen could escape or be misused, so work must be tightly controlled.

### Animals: pharming

**Pharming** means genetically modified animals that produce pharmaceuticals. A human gene is joined to a promoter active only in mammary gland cells, so the protein is secreted in milk and purified. For example, a goat giving 600 dm³ of milk a year with 3.5 g of the protein in each dm³ gives 600 × 3.5 = 2,100 g, or 2.1 kg. Positive: complex human proteins are made correctly and cheaply. Negative: animal welfare, low success rates in producing transgenic animals, and objections to treating animals as production systems.

### Patenting and technology transfer

A company can patent a modified organism or gene. A patent rewards invention, but it can make seed expensive and give a few companies control of food production. **Technology transfer** asks whether the benefits reach poorer farmers, for example by making modified seed available at low cost. Sharing could reduce hunger, but farmers may become dependent on suppliers, and a variety bred for one region may not suit another.

## Gene therapy (6.1.3(h))

**Gene therapy** treats a genetic disorder by altering the genes in a patient's cells. It works best for disorders caused by a single faulty gene, especially recessive ones, where supplying one working copy can restore the missing protein. Examples include severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) and cystic fibrosis.

### Principles

1. Identify the gene responsible and obtain a normal copy.
2. Put the normal allele into a vector. Common vectors are modified viruses (the viral genes that cause disease are removed) and liposomes (lipid spheres that fuse with the cell membrane).
3. Deliver it to the target cells, either by treating cells outside the body and returning them (**ex vivo**) or by delivering the vector directly (**in vivo**).
4. The cells make the functional protein.

### Somatic and germ line therapy

| Feature | Somatic cell gene therapy | Germ line cell gene therapy |
|---|---|---|
| Cells treated | Body cells, such as bone marrow, lung or retina cells | Gametes, a zygote or early embryo |
| Inherited by children? | No | Yes, because every cell of the person, including their gametes, carries the change |
| Who is affected? | The patient only, who can consent | Future people, who cannot consent |
| Effect | Often temporary, because treated cells die and are replaced, so treatment may need repeating | Potentially permanent in the family line |
| Status | Used in medicine | Heavily restricted, and ethically controversial |

### Potential and problems

- **Potential:** it can treat the cause rather than relieve symptoms.
- **Problems:** the gene may insert at random and disrupt another gene, which can cause cancer; the immune system may attack the vector; the effect may wear off; and cost is high.

**Worked example 7: reading a gene therapy result.** A patient with an enzyme deficiency has blood enzyme activity of 2 units before treatment. After somatic gene therapy it is 11 units at 1 month, 7 units at 6 months and 4 units at 12 months.

- At 1 month: 11 ÷ 2 = 5.5 times the starting activity.
- At 12 months: 4 ÷ 2 = 2 times the starting activity, so still above baseline.
- Interpretation: the treatment worked, but activity falls. Treated cells may die and be replaced by untreated ones, so repeat treatment may be needed.

## Common errors

- Writing that ligase "joins the bases". It forms phosphodiester bonds in the sugar-phosphate backbone.
- Saying a restriction enzyme cuts at any sequence. Its active site fits one recognition sequence.
- Calling cDNA a gene cut from the chromosome. It is made from mRNA, so it has no introns.
- Giving ethics answers with one side only, or with no conclusion.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why can a bacterium make a human protein?**
The genetic code is universal, so a bacterium's ribosomes read the codons of a human gene in the same way as human ribosomes. This is the principle that makes genetic engineering possible.

**What are the four stages of genetic engineering?**
Isolate the gene of interest, insert it into a vector, transfer the recombinant vector into host cells, and identify and grow the cells that took it up.

**Why does an inserted gene need a promoter?**
A promoter at the start of the gene lets RNA polymerase begin transcription in the host cell. Without a promoter that works in the host, the gene is present but not expressed.

**What is the difference between sticky ends and blunt ends?**
Sticky ends are short single-stranded overhangs left when a restriction enzyme such as EcoRI makes staggered cuts in a palindromic recognition sequence. Blunt ends are left by enzymes that cut straight across both strands.
