This guide goes deeper into **3.3.11.1 Preparation** of amines, the first subtopic of section 3.3.11 Amines in the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International A2 content, assessed in Unit 4 (Organic 2 and Physical 2) and in the synoptic Unit 5. The subtopic names three routes. Exam questions rarely ask for one route on its own; they ask you to choose between routes, to balance reduction equations, or to work through a two-step synthesis with yields.

Condense the ideas with the [amine preparation revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-11-1-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [amine preparation practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-11-1-practice/). For all of section 3.3.11, use the [amines study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-amines/).

## What the specification asks for

The subtopic has two statements:

- **Primary aliphatic amines** can be prepared by the reaction of **ammonia with halogenoalkanes** and by the **reduction of nitriles**.
- **Aromatic amines**, prepared by the **reduction of nitro compounds**, are used in the manufacture of **dyes**.

The nucleophilic substitution mechanism of ammonia with halogenoalkanes is listed in 3.3.11.3, and cyanide substitution in 3.3.3.1, so both can appear in a preparation question.

## Route 1: halogenoalkane and ammonia

Heat the halogenoalkane with an **excess of ammonia dissolved in ethanol**, in a sealed tube so the volatile ammonia cannot escape.

`CH₃CH₂CH₂Br + 2NH₃ → CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₂ + NH₄Br`  (propylamine)

Why two ammonia molecules? The first acts as the nucleophile: its lone pair attacks the δ+ carbon and bromide leaves, giving CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₃⁺. The second acts as a base and removes H⁺ from that ion, releasing the free amine.

Each condition has a reason you may be asked for. Ethanol is the solvent because it dissolves both the halogenoalkane and ammonia, whereas water would allow some hydroxide substitution to give an alcohol. The tube is sealed because ammonia is a gas at the reaction temperature and would otherwise escape before it could react.

The weakness of this route is that the primary amine also has a lone pair, so it competes with ammonia for the halogenoalkane. Secondary and tertiary amines and a quaternary ammonium salt also form. A large excess of ammonia makes it more likely that each halogenoalkane molecule meets ammonia, so the primary amine is the main product, but the mixture still needs separating. The carbon chain stays the same length.

## Route 2: reducing a nitrile

A nitrile, R–C≡N, is made from a halogenoalkane by heating under reflux with **potassium cyanide in aqueous ethanol**, then reduced to a primary amine. The specification does not name a reducing agent; hydrogen with a nickel catalyst, or LiAlH₄ in dry ether, are both used. Using [H]:

`R–C≡N + 4[H] → R–CH₂–NH₂`

Two features make this route useful:

- Only the **primary amine** forms, because there is no free amine present while the nitrile is being made.
- The chain is **one carbon longer** than the halogenoalkane, because the C of the cyanide ion becomes part of the chain.

Potassium cyanide is highly toxic, so it must be handled with great care; that is the cost of this route.

### Worked example 1: choosing a route

A chemist wants hexan-1-amine, CH₃(CH₂)₅NH₂. Two starting materials are available: 1-bromohexane and 1-bromopentane. Give a route from each and compare them.

- **From 1-bromohexane (six carbons, same as the target)**: heat with excess ethanolic ammonia in a sealed tube.
  `CH₃(CH₂)₅Br + 2NH₃ → CH₃(CH₂)₅NH₂ + NH₄Br`
- **From 1-bromopentane (five carbons, one short)**: step 1, reflux with KCN in aqueous ethanol to give hexanenitrile, CH₃(CH₂)₄CN; step 2, reduce with H₂ and Ni.
  `CH₃(CH₂)₄CN + 2H₂ → CH₃(CH₂)₅NH₂`
- **Comparison**: the ammonia route is one step but gives a mixture of amines; the nitrile route is two steps and uses toxic KCN but gives only the primary amine.

Count carbons first. If the target has one more carbon than the halogenoalkane, the nitrile route is the only one of the two that works.

### Worked example 2: hydrogen for a nitrile reduction

3.45 g of butanenitrile, CH₃CH₂CH₂CN (Mr 69.0), is reduced to butan-1-amine (Mr 73.0) with hydrogen and a nickel catalyst. Calculate the volume of hydrogen needed at 298 K and 100 kPa, and the maximum mass of amine. (R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹)

1. `CH₃CH₂CH₂CN + 2H₂ → CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂NH₂`
2. n(nitrile) = 3.45 ÷ 69.0 = 0.0500 mol, so n(H₂) = 0.100 mol.
3. V = nRT ÷ p = 0.100 × 8.31 × 298 ÷ 100 000 = 2.48 × 10⁻³ m³ = **2.48 dm³**.
4. Maximum mass of amine = 0.0500 × 73.0 = **3.65 g**.

Note the name change: butanenitrile already counts the C≡N carbon, so it has four carbons and gives butan-1-amine, not pentan-1-amine.

## Route 3: aromatic amines from nitro compounds

An aromatic amine has its nitrogen bonded directly to the benzene ring. It is made in two stages:

1. **Nitration** (section 3.3.10.2) puts an –NO₂ group on the ring.
2. **Reduction**: heat under reflux with **tin and concentrated hydrochloric acid**. The acid protonates the amine as it forms, so the product in the flask is a salt such as C₆H₅NH₃⁺Cl⁻. Adding **sodium hydroxide** releases the free amine: C₆H₅NH₃⁺ + OH⁻ → C₆H₅NH₂ + H₂O.

`C₆H₅NO₂ + 6[H] → C₆H₅NH₂ + 2H₂O`

Each nitro group needs **6[H]**: four to remove the two oxygen atoms as two water molecules and two to form the N–H bonds. The specification links these aromatic amines to the **manufacture of dyes**.

### Worked example 3: identifying a reduction product from Mr

A nitro compound A, C₇H₇NO₂ (Mr 137.0), is made by nitrating methylbenzene. A is reduced to an amine B. Find the Mr of B, give a structure for B and write the equation.

1. Each –NO₂ → –NH₂ removes two O (−32.0) and adds two H (+2.0), a change of −30.0.
2. Mr(B) = 137.0 − 30.0 = **107.0**, formula C₇H₉N.
3. Nitration of methylbenzene gives mainly the 2- and 4-isomers, so B could be **4-methylphenylamine**, CH₃C₆H₄NH₂ (or the 2-isomer).
4. `CH₃C₆H₄NO₂ + 6[H] → CH₃C₆H₄NH₂ + 2H₂O`

The "−30 per nitro group" check is a quick way to confirm an answer, or to count nitro groups from Mr data.

## Comparing the three routes

| Route | Starting material | Steps | Chain length | Purity of product | Main hazard or drawback |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| NH₃ + halogenoalkane | RX | 1 | Unchanged | Mixture of amines | Further substitution |
| Nitrile reduction | RX via RCN | 2 | One carbon longer | Primary amine only | Toxic KCN |
| Nitro reduction | Arene via ArNO₂ | 2 | Unchanged | Aromatic primary amine | Concentrated acids; product must be freed with NaOH |

## Common mistakes

- Using one molecule of ammonia per halogenoalkane in the equation.
- Forgetting that excess ammonia reduces, but does not prevent, further substitution.
- Miscounting carbons in nitrile names; the C≡N carbon is included.
- Writing 2[H] for a nitrile; it needs 4[H] (or 2H₂).
- Writing 2[H] or 4[H] for a nitro group; it needs 6[H] and gives 2H₂O.
- Leaving out the NaOH step, or claiming Sn/HCl gives the free amine directly.
- Calling an amine with a CH₂ between the ring and N "aromatic"; the N must be on a ring carbon.

## Exam technique

- **Suggest a route**: give reagents and conditions for every step and name each intermediate.
- **Explain** why a route gives an impure product: the amine formed is itself a nucleophile because of its lone pair.
- **Compare** routes: chain length, number of steps, purity and hazard.
- **Calculate**: balance the [H] or H₂ first, then use moles; for multistep problems multiply the yields.
