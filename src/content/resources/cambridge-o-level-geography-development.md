This guide goes deeper into **subtopic 3.1 Development** of Cambridge
O Level Geography (2217), 2026 series (Version 3). The [Economic
Development study guide](/resources/o-level-geography-economic-development/)
sets out the whole theme; this page works through the reasoning 3.1
questions reward: using indicators with data, explaining *why*
inequality and sector change happen, and building a transnational
corporation (TNC) case study. Follow it with the [development revision
notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-geography-development-revision-notes/)
and the [development practice
questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-geography-development-practice/).

## What 3.1 covers

- Using a range of **indicators** to assess a country's level of
  development: GNP per capita, literacy, life expectancy and composite
  indices such as the Human Development Index (HDI).
- Identifying and suggesting reasons for **inequalities between and
  within countries**.
- Classifying production into **primary, secondary, tertiary and
  quaternary** sectors, with examples.
- Describing and explaining how the **proportions employed in each
  sector** change with development.
- Describing and explaining **globalisation** and its impacts.
- **Case study**: a transnational corporation and its global links.

## Indicators: what each one tells you

| Indicator | Type | What it shows | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| GNP (or GNI) per capita | Economic | Average income per person, usually in US dollars | An average: hides inequality; ignores the informal economy and subsistence output |
| Literacy rate | Social | Percentage of adults who can read and write | Says little about the quality or level of education |
| Life expectancy | Social | Average number of years a newborn can expect to live | Pulled down strongly by deaths in infancy |
| Infant mortality | Social | Deaths under one year old per 1,000 live births | Data can be poor where births are not registered |
| HDI | Composite | Combines life expectancy, education and income into a value from 0 to 1 | Still national averages; leaves out factors such as political freedom |

The point examiners look for: **no single indicator is enough**. A
country rich from oil exports can have a high GNP per capita but low
literacy among women; a country with modest income can have high life
expectancy because of good primary health care. A composite index
reduces the distortion of any one measure.

## Worked example 1: comparing countries with data

Three illustrative countries:

| Country | GNP per capita (US$) | Life expectancy (years) | Adult literacy (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| P | 45,000 | 82 | 99 |
| Q | 12,000 | 76 | 94 |
| R | 1,500 | 62 | 55 |

A strong comparison: *"P has a GNP per capita 30 times that of R
(45,000 compared with 1,500 US dollars), and its people live on
average 20 years longer (82 compared with 62). Q's income is about a
quarter of P's, yet its life expectancy is only 6 years lower,
suggesting that health care improves faster than income at middle
levels of development."* Note the use of **calculated comparisons**
(times as much, difference in years), not just a list of figures.

## Inequality between and within countries

**Between countries**, reasons include:

- **Physical factors**: landlocked position, extreme climates, frequent
  hazards (drought, tropical storms), few natural resources.
- **Economic factors**: dependence on exporting a few low-value primary
  products whose prices change; debt repayments; little capital for
  investment.
- **Historical and political factors**: the legacy of colonialism,
  conflict and unstable or corrupt government, which discourage
  investment.
- **Social factors**: poor health and education limit productivity.

**Within countries**, wealth usually concentrates in a **core** (often
the capital and main ports) while the **periphery** lags behind. In
Brazil, the south-east around São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro is far
wealthier than the north-east; in China, the eastern coastal provinces
grew faster than the interior. Causes: investment and infrastructure
go where markets and ports already exist, young workers migrate to the
core, and the cycle reinforces itself. Urban–rural gaps are a
within-country inequality too.

## Sectors and employment structure

- **Primary**: extracting raw materials (farming, fishing, mining,
  forestry).
- **Secondary**: manufacturing and processing (car assembly, food
  processing, construction).
- **Tertiary**: services (retail, health, education, transport,
  tourism).
- **Quaternary**: research, information and knowledge work (software
  development, research and development, data services).

**How the proportions change, and why:**

1. *Low development*: most people work in the primary sector, largely
   subsistence farming, because there is little capital or technology.
2. *Industrialising*: mechanisation of farming releases labour; factory
   jobs pay more, so workers move to the secondary sector. Investment,
   often from TNCs, builds factories.
3. *High development*: rising incomes create demand for services;
   manufacturing is automated or moved to lower-cost countries
   (deindustrialisation); tertiary and quaternary employment dominate.

Data for employment structure are often shown on **pie charts,
divided bar graphs or triangular graphs**. On a triangular graph,
check that the three percentages you read add up to 100.

## Globalisation

Globalisation is the increasing **connection and interdependence** of
the world's economies and cultures through trade, investment,
migration and information. Its drivers include:

- **Transport**: container ships and cheaper air freight cut the cost
  of moving goods.
- **Information and communication technology**: the internet allows
  firms to manage operations and serve customers worldwide.
- **TNCs** seeking lower costs and new markets.
- **Trade agreements** and governments encouraging foreign
  investment, for example through tax incentives.

Impacts should be weighed at different scales. Positives: jobs,
investment, transfer of skills and technology, cheaper goods, export
earnings. Negatives: low wages and poor conditions in some factories,
profits leaving the host country, local firms out-competed, job losses
when factories relocate, and environmental damage from production and
transport.

## Worked example 2: building a TNC case study

A TNC case study needs **named places and specific links**. An outline
using Toyota:

- **Headquarters and research**: Toyota City, Aichi Prefecture, Japan.
- **Global production**: car and engine plants in many countries, for
  example a car plant at Burnaston near Derby and an engine plant at
  Deeside in North Wales (UK), and a large plant at Georgetown,
  Kentucky (USA).
- **Why locate abroad**: to be inside major markets, avoid import
  tariffs, reduce transport costs to customers and use skilled local
  workforces.
- **Impacts on a host country**: direct jobs and many more in
  component suppliers (a multiplier effect), training and export
  earnings; but decisions are made in Japan, profits return there, and
  the host is exposed if production is cut.

Whichever TNC you choose, learn a headquarters location, at least two
named overseas sites with their function, the reasons for those
locations, and one positive and one negative impact with detail.

## Common mistakes

- Quoting GNP rather than GNP **per capita** when comparing living
  standards.
- Describing the sector shift without explaining it.
- Answering a globalisation question only with positives.
- A TNC "case study" with no named overseas locations.
- Comparing countries by listing figures for each without a
  comparative statement.

## Exam technique

- **Compare**: use "whereas", "higher than", "twice as much" and
  calculated differences.
- **Suggest reasons**: give several different types of reason
  (physical, economic, political, social), each developed.
- **Explain**: for sector change, give the cause and the effect on
  employment.
- **Evaluate or discuss** globalisation: positives, negatives and a
  judgement about who gains and who loses.
