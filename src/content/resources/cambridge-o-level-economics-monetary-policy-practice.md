## Questions

**1.** Define money supply. **[2]**

**2.** Define monetary policy. **[2]**

**3.** Explain how a rise in the interest rate may reduce the rate of inflation. **[4]**

**4.** A small bakery in Country B has a loan of 40,000. The interest rate rises from 7% to 10%.

**(a)** Calculate the extra interest the bakery pays in a year. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage increase in its yearly interest cost. Round to one decimal place. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain one reason why the bakery may now cut its investment. **[2]**

**5.** In Country C the interest rate on savings is 4% and inflation is 6.5%.

**(a)** Estimate the real interest rate. **[2]**
**(b)** A saver deposits 1,000 for a year. Calculate the buying power of the money at the end of the year, in start-of-year prices, to two decimal places. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain who gains when the real interest rate is negative. **[2]**

**6.** The money supply of Country D grows from 250 billion to 272.5 billion in a year. Real output grows by 4%.

**(a)** Calculate the growth in the money supply. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why this may threaten stable prices. **[3]**

**7.** In Country F banks must keep 8% of deposits as reserves and lend the rest. A new deposit of 2,000 enters the banking system.

**(a)** Calculate the maximum total deposits that can result. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the new lending created. **[1]**
**(c)** The central bank raises the requirement to 12.5%. Calculate the change in maximum total deposits. **[3]**
**(d)** State one assumption behind your answers. **[1]**

**8.** Country E has an exchange rate of 1 E-unit = 4 F-units. It exports a machine priced at 2,400 E-units and imports a good priced at 6,000 F-units. The E-unit then falls to 1 E-unit = 3 F-units.

**(a)** Find the machine's price in F-units at each exchange rate. **[2]**
**(b)** Work out the percentage change in that price. **[2]**
**(c)** Find the imported good's price in E-units at each rate, and the percentage change. **[3]**
**(d)** Demand for the machine has a price elasticity of 1.2. Estimate the percentage change in the quantity sold. **[2]**

**9.** Explain two ways in which a cut in interest rates could reduce unemployment, and one reason why it might not. **[6]**

**10.** A government wants both low inflation and full employment. Discuss whether a rise in the interest rate can achieve both. **[8]**

**11.** Country G (invented) has growth of 0.5%, unemployment of 11% and low inflation. The central bank cuts its rate from 5% to 3%, and the G-unit falls from 1 G-unit = 2.0 H-units to 1.6 H-units. A firm there has a loan of 120,000 and exports a good priced at 5,000 G-units.

**(a)** Calculate the yearly interest saved by the firm. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the export price in H-units at each rate, and the percentage change. **[3]**
**(c)** Evaluate whether these measures will help Country G achieve its macroeconomic aims. **[7]**

## Answers

**1.** The stock of money [1] held in a country at a given moment, such as notes, coins and bank deposits [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* State what is counted and where it is held; the phrase "money in the economy" alone is thin.

**2.** Action on borrowing costs, the quantity of money and the currency's value [1] by the government or central bank to steer the economy towards its macroeconomic aims [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming the measures plus the purpose makes a complete definition; tax and spending changes belong to fiscal policy and gain nothing here.

**3.** Loans become dearer, so households and firms borrow less [1]. Saving pays more, so households spend less [1]. Total spending falls or grows more slowly [1]. Firms find it harder to raise prices, so inflation slows [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers gain credit when each step leads to the next; a bare statement that "inflation falls" skips the explanation the command word asks for.

**4. (a)** Interest at 7% is 40,000 × 0.07 = 2,800 and at 10% is 4,000 [1]. Extra = 4,000 − 2,800 = **1,200** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** (1,200 ÷ 2,800) × 100 [1] = **42.9%** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Borrowing costs more, so fewer projects are expected to earn enough profit [1]. Less money is left after paying interest to fund new equipment [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the old figure as the base for a percentage change; dividing by the new one gives a different answer. Show the working before the rounded result.

**5. (a)** Real rate ≈ 4 − 6.5 [1] = **−2.5%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 1,000 × 1.04 = 1,040 [1]. Divide by the price rise: 1,040 ÷ 1.065 [1] = **976.53** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Borrowers gain [1] because the real value of what they owe falls as prices rise faster than interest accumulates [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Say clearly that the saver is worse off in buying power even though the balance grew; stating only the nominal gain misses the point of real rates.

**6. (a)** (272.5 − 250) ÷ 250 × 100 [1] = **9%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Money supply grows by 9% but output by only 4% [1]. People and firms have more to spend than the economy produces [1]. This can pull prices up, causing inflation [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare the two growth rates in your answer; quoting the 9% on its own does not show why it threatens prices.

**7. (a)** 2,000 ÷ 0.08 [1] = **25,000** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 25,000 − 2,000 = **23,000** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** 2,000 ÷ 0.125 = 16,000 [1]. Change = 16,000 − 25,000 [1] = **−9,000**, a fall of 9,000 [1]. **[3]**
**(d)** All loans are spent and redeposited in the banking system, and no cash is held outside banks [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* State the direction of the change as well as its size; an answer of "9,000" with no sign leaves it unclear whether the money supply rose or fell.

**8. (a)** Before: 2,400 × 4 = **9,600 F-units** [1]. After: 2,400 × 3 = **7,200 F-units** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** (7,200 − 9,600) ÷ 9,600 × 100 [1] = **−25%** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Before: 6,000 ÷ 4 = **1,500 E-units** [1]. After: 6,000 ÷ 3 = **2,000 E-units** [1]. Change = 500 ÷ 1,500 × 100 = **+33.3%** [1]. **[3]**
**(d)** Quantity change ≈ 1.2 × 25 [1] = **30% rise** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Exports are multiplied by the rate and imports divided by it; a different base gives a different percentage in each direction, so explain the base you used.

**9.** First way: cheaper loans lead firms to invest and expand [1], so they need more workers and unemployment falls [1]. Second way: households borrow and spend more [1], sales rise and firms hire more workers [1]. It may not work because business confidence is low [1], so firms do not borrow even at a low rate and do not hire [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Two distinct routes (firms and households) earn more than one route repeated; a limitation needs a reason, not just the word "confidence".

**10.** A higher rate reduces borrowing and spending [1]. Firms find it harder to raise prices, so inflation slows [1]. However, lower sales make firms cut output [1] and employ fewer workers, so unemployment rises [1]. The two aims therefore conflict: success on one tends to harm the other [1]. The effect on prices is weaker if inflation is cost-push, caused by dearer imports or wages, since spending is not the cause [1]. Effects also take months to appear, so a small rise may change little [1]. Judgement: a rate rise is more likely to cut inflation than to deliver full employment, so it cannot achieve both together [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* With a discuss question, set out both sides and finish with a verdict that answers the question asked; a list of points without a verdict leaves the task unfinished.

**11. (a)** 120,000 × 0.05 = 6,000 and 120,000 × 0.03 = 3,600 [1]. Saving = **2,400** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Before: 5,000 × 2.0 = **10,000 H-units** [1]. After: 5,000 × 1.6 = **8,000 H-units** [1]. Change = **−20%** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Cheaper loans encourage firms to invest and households to spend, which raises output and may cut unemployment [1]. A weaker currency makes exports cheaper abroad, so export sales rise if demand is elastic [1]. Both support growth and jobs, and inflation is low, so there is room to loosen policy [1]. However, with low confidence firms may not borrow [1]. A weaker currency makes imported inputs dearer and could raise prices [1]. Higher spending may draw in imports and worsen the current account [1]. Judgement: the measures probably help because unemployment is high and inflation is not a threat, but the gain depends on confidence and on elastic foreign demand [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the data in the question (unemployment, low inflation) to support the judgement; answers gain credit when evaluation is applied to the case, not recited in general.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining monetary policy by tax or government spending.
- Stating that rates "affect the economy" with no chain of steps.
- Using the new value, not the original, as the base for a percentage change.
- Multiplying when the question needs division for imports at an exchange rate.
- Forgetting the sign or direction of a change in deposits or prices.
- Confusing a nominal rate with a real rate.
- Writing a list of evaluation points with no verdict.
- Ignoring the case data when asked to evaluate.
