## 3.7 Supply side policies

**Supply side policy** is government action aimed at increasing the productive capacity of the economy, so that it can produce more goods and services over time. It works on the quantity and quality of resources and on how well markets function. Compare this with fiscal and monetary policy, which mainly change spending in the short run.

The specification's determinants of economic growth (section 3.1) show where supply side policy can act: investment, technology, the size of the workforce, education and training, natural resources and government policies. Common examples:

- Education and training, so workers produce more per hour and can move between jobs.
- Investment in transport and communications, so firms' costs fall.
- Support for research and new technology, such as grants or tax relief for innovation.
- Lower taxes on income or profits, to reward work and investment.
- Reducing unnecessary regulation, to make it cheaper to start and run a business.
- Encouraging competition, so firms cut costs to survive.

### How supply side policy achieves objectives

- **Economic growth:** a higher productive capacity lets output rise without running into shortages.
- **Low unemployment:** training helps unemployed workers with outdated skills to find work, which tackles structural unemployment in particular.
- **Price stability:** if firms' costs fall or productivity rises, output can grow without pushing prices up.
- **Fair distribution of income:** better skills can raise the pay of lower-paid workers, although tax cuts that mostly help high earners can widen gaps.

### Worked example: a training programme (Country A, illustrative)

Country A has 20 million workers and produces £400bn a year. A training programme costs the government £6bn a year and raises output by 2 per cent, with the same workforce.

1. Output per worker now: £400bn ÷ 20 million = £20,000.
2. Extra output: 2% of £400bn = £8bn, so new output is £408bn.
3. New output per worker: £408bn ÷ 20 million = £20,400, a rise of £400 per worker.
4. Suppose the government collects 25% of extra output as tax: 25% × £8bn = £2bn.
5. Net annual cost to the government: £6bn − £2bn = £4bn.
6. Opportunity cost: if a new classroom costs £250,000, the £6bn could have built £6bn ÷ £250,000 = 24,000 classrooms.

The programme adds £8bn to output for a £6bn outlay, but the government must decide whether 24,000 classrooms would give more benefit.

### Evaluating supply side policy

**Benefits:** it raises potential output, which tends to be a lasting effect; it can raise growth and cut unemployment without adding to inflation; it can improve living standards over many years.

**Costs and limits:**

- Time lag: a child educated today enters the workforce years later.
- Opportunity cost: money spent here cannot be used for hospitals, benefits or tax cuts.
- Uncertainty: training may not match the skills firms need.
- Winners and losers: cutting regulation may harm workers or the environment, and tax cuts that favour high earners may widen inequality.
- Tax cuts reduce government revenue unless they stimulate enough extra activity.

A strong evaluation judges the policy against the objective and the time frame. For a country with high structural unemployment and a short horizon, supply side policy alone may be too slow; for long-term growth it may be the most sustainable choice.

## 3.8 Externalities

Markets often work well, but they can fail when prices do not reflect the full costs and benefits to society. An **externality** is a cost or benefit that falls on a third party who is not part of the transaction, and which is not reflected in the market price.

- **Negative externality:** a cost imposed on others. Examples: a factory's air pollution, noise from a night-time venue, traffic congestion.
- **Positive externality:** a benefit to others. Examples: a vaccinated person lowers disease risk for others; an educated worker is more productive and may help colleagues; a homeowner's garden pleases neighbours.

Key relationships:

- Social cost = private cost + external cost.
- Social benefit = private benefit + external benefit.

Because buyers and sellers consider only their own costs and benefits, a market **overproduces** goods with negative externalities and **underproduces** goods with positive externalities, compared with the level that is best for society. This is why governments may intervene.

### Diagram described in words

On a demand and supply diagram for a negative externality, price is on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal. The market equilibrium is where demand meets the supply curve (private cost). A social cost curve lies above it by the amount of the external cost. It meets demand at a lower quantity, so the socially best output is below the market output. For a positive externality, a social benefit curve lies above the demand curve, and the socially best output is above the market output.

## Government policies to correct externalities

### 1. Taxation (negative externalities)

A tax on each unit raises the producer's cost and shifts supply up and left. Ideally the tax equals the external cost.

**Worked example (illustrative market for a chemical product).** Demand is Qd = 2,000 − 25P and supply is Qs = 25P, where P is the price in pounds and Q the weekly quantity. Each unit creates £10 of external cost.

| Price (£) | Quantity demanded | Supply, no tax | Supply, £10 tax |
|---|---|---|---|
| 35 | 1,125 | 875 | 625 |
| 40 | 1,000 | 1,000 | 750 |
| 45 | 875 | 1,125 | 875 |

1. No tax: demand equals supply at £40 and 1,000 units.
2. With a £10 tax, the producer keeps price minus £10, so supply is 25(P − 10). Setting 2,000 − 25P = 25P − 250 gives 50P = 2,250, so P = £45 and Q = 875.
3. Consumers pay £5 more (£45 − £40). Producers receive £35, which is £5 less. The burden is shared equally.
4. Tax revenue: 875 × £10 = £8,750.
5. External cost avoided: (1,000 − 875) × £10 = £1,250.

Evaluation: a tax puts a price on the externality and raises revenue that can fund other things. But putting an exact figure on the harm is difficult, so the rate chosen may be too high or too low. If demand is price inelastic, a tax raises the price a lot but cuts quantity only slightly. For instance, with price elasticity of demand of −0.3, a 10% price rise cuts quantity by only 3%. The tax can also fall hardest on lower-income households, and firms may avoid it or move abroad.

### 2. Subsidies (positive externalities)

A subsidy per unit lowers producers' costs and shifts supply down and right, raising output.

**Worked example (illustrative adult training courses).** Demand is Qd = 1,800 − 20P and supply is Qs = 10P. Each course gives £12 of external benefit.

1. No subsidy: 1,800 − 20P = 10P, so P = £60 and Q = 600.
2. With a £12 subsidy, producers receive P + 12, so supply is 10(P + 12). Setting 1,800 − 20P = 10P + 120 gives 30P = 1,680, so P = £56 and Q = 680.
3. Buyers pay £4 less, and providers receive £68, which is £8 more.
4. Cost to government: 680 × £12 = £8,160.
5. Of this, 600 × £12 = £7,200 went on courses that would have been bought anyway. Only 80 extra courses were gained, with an extra external benefit of 80 × £12 = £960.

Evaluation: subsidies increase output and make the good more affordable. However, they cost taxpayers money, which has an opportunity cost, and much of the subsidy may go to people who would have bought the product anyway. Providers may keep some as profit, and it is hard to know the right size of subsidy.

### 3. State provision

The government provides the good or service directly, often free at the point of use and paid for by taxation. Examples include state schools, vaccination clinics and public parks.

- **Benefits:** access does not depend on ability to pay, so more people use it and the external benefit grows; it can be directed to where need is greatest.
- **Costs:** high cost to taxpayers, with an opportunity cost in other public spending; possible waste or lack of choice; free provision can lead to overuse; the quality and quantity depend on the government's budget.

### 4. Legislation and regulation

Laws and rules ban or limit harmful activity or require beneficial activity. Examples include limits on factory emissions, bans on smoking in public places, rules that require children to attend school and compulsory safety standards.

- **Benefits:** direct and certain; it can set a firm limit where harm is serious.
- **Costs:** needs monitoring and enforcement, which costs money; firms face higher costs that may be passed on in prices; rigid rules may not suit every firm; people may break the law or create a black market.

### 5. Information provision

Governments try to change behaviour by informing people about harms or benefits through campaigns, warning labels or school education.

- **Benefits:** relatively cheap, does not restrict freedom of choice, and can have long-run effects on attitudes.
- **Costs:** results are uncertain and slow, people may ignore the message, and campaigns from firms with large advertising budgets may cancel its effect.

## How to evaluate policies

For any policy, ask:

1. **Effectiveness:** how far does it move output towards the social optimum?
2. **Cost and opportunity cost:** who pays, and what else could the money do?
3. **Side effects:** effects on jobs, prices, inequality, enforcement.
4. **Time:** how quickly does it work?
5. **Judgement:** which policy fits this externality, and is a combination better?

A judgement should be supported by a reason from the context. Often a mix works best, such as a tax with an information campaign.

## Common errors

- Describing an externality as a cost to the buyer or seller. It falls on a third party.
- Saying a tax removes the externality. It reduces output towards the social optimum.
- Forgetting that a subsidy has an opportunity cost.
- Listing benefits and costs without reaching a judgement.
- Confusing supply side policy with demand management. Supply side policy raises productive capacity.
