Subtopic 25.1 of Cambridge International AS & A Level Physics 9702 (2025–2027) covers luminosity, radiant flux intensity and the inverse square law, standard candles, and how they give distances to galaxies. These notes are condensed for recall. For full explanations and worked examples, read the [Standard candles study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-standard-candles/); to apply the ideas, try the [Standard candles practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-standard-candles-practice/).

## Equations

```
F = L / (4πd²)          (recall)
d = √( L / (4πF) )
L = 4πd²F
same L:  F₁/F₂ = (d₂/d₁)²    so    d₂ = d₁ √(F₁/F₂)
```

## L versus F

| | Luminosity L | Radiant flux intensity F |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Total power of radiation emitted by the star | Power received per unit area at the observer |
| Unit | W | W m⁻² |
| Depends on distance? | No | Yes, ∝ 1/d² |
| Measured or known? | Known (standard candle) or calculated | Measured directly |

## Inverse square law: why and when

- All the power L passes through a sphere of area 4πd².
- Doubling d → F ÷ 4; trebling d → F ÷ 9; ten times d → F ÷ 100.
- Assumes: equal emission in all directions; no absorption or scattering on the way; point source.
- Dust absorbs radiation → measured F too small → calculated d **too large**.

## Standard candles

- **Definition:** an astronomical object of known luminosity.
- **Use:** measure F of a standard candle in a galaxy; use known L to calculate d; this gives the distance to the galaxy.
- **Calibration:** L is found from nearby examples at known distance, L = 4πd²F.

| Example | How L is known | Range |
|---|---|---|
| Cepheid variable stars | Period of brightness variation is related to luminosity | Nearby galaxies |
| Type Ia supernovae | Very similar peak luminosity each time | Very distant galaxies |

## Method for distance questions

1. Identify L (given, or found from a calibrating object).
2. Identify the measured F.
3. Rearrange: d = √(L/(4πF)).
4. Square root last; check d is sensible (galaxies: 10²¹ m and beyond).
5. For two objects with the same L, use the ratio d₂ = d₁√(F₁/F₂).

## Quick calculations

- Sun: L = 3.8 × 10²⁶ W, d = 1.5 × 10¹¹ m → F = 1.3 × 10³ W m⁻².
- 100 W source at 5.0 m (radiating equally in all directions): F = 0.32 W m⁻².
- Cepheid calibrated at 1.5 × 10¹⁹ m with F = 3.0 × 10⁻¹¹ W m⁻²; same-period Cepheid with F = 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁷ W m⁻² is at 2.4 × 10²² m.
- 20 % of the radiation absorbed: calculated distance is 1/√0.80 = 1.12 times the true value (12 % too large).

## Ratio method in one line

Two objects of the same luminosity: the one with 100 times less flux is 10 times farther away, because d ∝ 1/√F. Write d₂ = d₁√(F₁/F₂) with the brighter object as object 1, so the square root is greater than 1.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Luminosity | Total power of radiation emitted by a star |
| Radiant flux intensity | Radiant power passing through unit area normal to the direction of travel |
| Inverse square law | F is inversely proportional to the square of the distance from a point source |
| Standard candle | Astronomical object of known luminosity |
| Calibration | Finding the luminosity of a type of standard candle using examples at known distances |
| Cepheid variable | Pulsating star whose period of variation is related to its luminosity |

## Exam traps

- Calling both quantities "brightness".
- 4πd instead of 4πd², or πd² instead of 4πd².
- Forgetting the square root when finding d.
- Defining a standard candle as "a bright star" or "a star used for reference".
- Inverting the ratio so a dimmer standard candle comes out nearer.
- Quoting L in W m⁻² or F in W.
- Stating that dust makes objects seem closer; it makes them seem **farther**.

## Self-test

1. Define luminosity.
2. Define radiant flux intensity and give its unit.
3. State what is meant by a standard candle.
4. Derive F = L/(4πd²) in one sentence.
5. A star's distance doubles. What happens to F?
6. Calculate F at a distance of 2.4 × 10¹⁷ m from a star of luminosity 3.6 × 10²⁷ W.
7. A standard candle of luminosity 1.2 × 10³⁰ W has F = 2.0 × 10⁻¹⁵ W m⁻². Calculate its distance.
8. Two identical standard candles have fluxes in the ratio 16 : 1. What is the ratio of their distances?
9. How are standard candles used to find the distance to a galaxy?
10. How is the luminosity of a type of standard candle established?
11. Interstellar dust absorbs some light. Is the calculated distance too large or too small?
12. Name two types of object used as standard candles.

**Answers:** 1. The total power of radiation emitted by a star. 2. Power of radiation received per unit area (normal to the direction of travel); W m⁻². 3. An astronomical object of known luminosity. 4. At distance d the power L is spread over the surface of a sphere of area 4πd². 5. It falls to a quarter. 6. F = 3.6 × 10²⁷ / (4π × (2.4 × 10¹⁷)²) = 5.0 × 10⁻⁹ W m⁻². 7. d = √(1.2 × 10³⁰ / (4π × 2.0 × 10⁻¹⁵)) = 6.9 × 10²¹ m. 8. 1 : 4 (the brighter one is four times nearer). 9. Identify a standard candle in the galaxy, measure its radiant flux intensity, and use its known luminosity in F = L/(4πd²) to find d. 10. Measure F for examples at known distances and calculate L = 4πd²F. 11. Too large. 12. Cepheid variable stars and Type Ia supernovae.
