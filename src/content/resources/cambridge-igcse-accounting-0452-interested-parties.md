This guide covers section **6.4 Interested parties**. The syllabus asks you to understand "how accounting information may be used for decision-making" by ten parties: owners, managers, employees, banks, investors and lenders, suppliers, customers, governments / tax authorities, club members, and other interested parties, e.g. public and environmental bodies. ## The key idea: decision, then evidence

Every interested party reads the same statements, but each asks a different question. An answer that says a party wants "to see how the business is doing" earns little. A strong answer gives three things:

1. **the party**
2. **the decision** it has to make
3. **the information or ratio** it would use, and why that figure helps

## The ten parties and their decisions

| Party | Typical decision | Information it looks at most |
|---|---|---|
| Owners | Keep, expand or close the business; how much to withdraw | Profit for the year, profit margin, ROCE, capital |
| Managers | Set prices, control costs, order inventory, plan | Gross profit margin, expenses line by line, inventory turnover, receivables days |
| Employees | Whether jobs are secure; whether to ask for a pay rise | Profit trend, cash position, liquidity |
| Banks | Whether to lend or allow an overdraft, and on what terms | Liquidity ratios, profit to cover interest, existing loans, assets as security |
| Investors and lenders | Whether to buy shares (investors) or lend long term (lenders) | Investors: profit, ROCE, dividends. Lenders: ability to pay interest and repay |
| Suppliers | Whether to supply on credit, and how much credit to allow | Current and acid test ratios, trade payables turnover |
| Customers | Whether the business will keep supplying and honour guarantees | Profit and liquidity, signs of survival |
| Governments / tax authorities | How much tax is due; grants; statistics | Profit for the year, revenue |
| Club members | Whether subscriptions are well used; whether to raise them | Surplus or deficit, accumulated fund, receipts and payments |
| Other interested parties, e.g. public and environmental bodies | Effect on local jobs, the community and the environment | Profit and survival; spending on items such as waste disposal where shown |

Two distinctions are often tested.

- **Managers against owners.** Managers run the business day to day and have access to detailed internal records, so they can look at each expense and each product. Owners of a larger business may see only the published statements.
- **Investors against lenders.** An investor buying shares gains if profit and dividends grow, so looks at profitability. A lender receives fixed interest and wants repayment, so looks at liquidity, existing borrowing and security.

## Worked example: one set of figures, five readers

Saltmarsh Ceramics Limited makes and sells tableware. This year's figures:

| Item | This year | Last year |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 520,000 | 505,000 |
| Gross profit | 208,000 | 212,000 |
| Profit for the year | 52,000 | 61,000 |
| Loan interest | 6,000 | 6,000 |
| Capital employed | 400,000 | 390,000 |
| Current assets (inventory) | 96,000 (40,000) | |
| Current liabilities | 48,000 | |
| Trade payables / credit purchases | 30,000 / 292,000 | |
| Dividends paid | 40,000 | |

Ratios: gross profit margin 208,000 ÷ 520,000 × 100 = 40.00%; profit margin 52,000 ÷ 520,000 × 100 = 10.00%; ROCE (52,000 + 6,000) ÷ 400,000 × 100 = 14.50%; current ratio 2.00 : 1; acid test (96,000 − 40,000) : 48,000 = 1.17 : 1; trade payables turnover 30,000 ÷ 292,000 × 365 = 37.50 days. Suppliers allow 30 days.

**A supplier** asked for a larger credit limit. The acid test of 1.17 : 1 suggests Saltmarsh can pay current liabilities without selling inventory, but it takes 37.50 days to pay against 30 allowed. A reasonable decision is to continue supplying on credit, raise the limit only a little, and remind Saltmarsh of the terms.

**The bank** is asked for an overdraft. Profit before interest of 58,000 covers the 6,000 interest many times over, and liquidity is sound. The bank would also look at what assets could act as security.

**An investor** considering buying shares sees ROCE of 14.50% but profit falling by 9,000, about 14.75%, while revenue rose. Dividends of 40,000 took about 77% of the year's profit. The investor would want to know whether the fall will continue before deciding.

**Employees** see a business still profitable and liquid, so jobs appear secure, but falling profit weakens the case for a large pay rise.

**Managers** look below the headline. Revenue rose by 15,000 but gross profit fell by 4,000, so the gross profit margin fell from 41.98% to 40.00%. They would check selling prices and the cost of clay and glazes, and review expenses, before setting next year's prices.

Each reader made a different decision from the same statements.

## Club members

Clubs are non-profit organisations, so members do not want profit; they want their subscriptions used well. The Riverside Rowing Club has 120 members paying $90 a year, so subscriptions are $10,800. The income and expenditure account shows a deficit of $1,440. Spread across members that is $12 each, so members can decide at the annual meeting whether to raise the subscription to about $102, cut costs or hold a fund-raising event. They also look at the accumulated fund to see whether the club's net assets are growing and at the receipts and payments account to see where cash went.

## Governments, tax authorities and other parties

Tax authorities use profit for the year to work out tax due and may check revenue against other records. Governments use business statistics for economic planning and to decide on grants. Public and environmental bodies, such as a local council or an environmental group, are interested in whether a business will keep providing local jobs and whether it can afford its obligations, for example to dispose of waste properly. Financial statements show only what can be measured in money, so these parties often need more information than the statements give.

## When interests conflict

| Parties | Conflict |
|---|---|
| Owners and lenders | Owners may want high drawings or dividends; lenders prefer cash kept in the business |
| Owners and employees | Higher pay reduces profit available to owners |
| Owners and customers | Higher prices raise profit but cost customers more |
| Owners and tax authorities | Lower reported profit means less tax, so tax authorities check that profit is not understated |

In Saltmarsh's case, paying 40,000 of dividends from 52,000 of profit suits shareholders but leaves less cash for the business, which a lender might not welcome.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Explain how a bank and a supplier might each use Saltmarsh's accounting information.* [4 marks]

> A bank deciding whether to grant an overdraft would look at liquidity **(decision and evidence)**. The acid test of 1.17 : 1 suggests Saltmarsh could repay short-term debts without selling inventory **(applied)**. A supplier deciding whether to give credit would look at how quickly Saltmarsh pays **(decision and evidence)**. Its trade payables turnover of 37.50 days is longer than the 30 days allowed, so the supplier might limit the credit offered **(applied)**.

## Common mistakes

- Writing "to see how well the business is doing" without a decision.
- Treating investors and lenders as identical.
- Saying club members want profit: clubs record a surplus or deficit.
- Forgetting customers and public bodies, which are on the 2027-2029 list.
- Naming a ratio without saying why it helps that party.

## Exam technique

- **State** or **Identify** a party: one word or phrase is enough.
- **Explain** a use: party, decision, information, reason.
- **Discuss** or **Evaluate**: show what the information tells the party and what it cannot tell them, then conclude.
- **Advise** a party: give a clear decision, supported by figures from the case.
