Section 4.2, *Age and family life*, closes Paper 2 of Cambridge International AS & A Level Sociology (9699). It turns from relations between partners (4.1) to relations between generations: children, parents and grandparents. The syllabus links it to the key concepts *Power, control and resistance* and *Inequality and opportunity*.

The [Paper 2 family guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-sociology-the-family/) summarises this section. Revise with the [4.2 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-age-and-family-life-revision-notes/) and practise with the [age and family life practice questions](/resources/a-level-cambridge-sociology-age-and-family-life-practice/). This guide includes studies and arguments those questions do not use, so it extends your evidence base.

## What the syllabus lists

- The social construction of childhood, and changes in the role and social position of children in the family.
- The role and social position of grandparents in the family, including cross-cultural comparisons and the impact of changing life expectancy upon the family.
- Social class, gender and ethnicity as factors affecting the experiences of children in the family.
- Changes in the concepts of motherhood and fatherhood.

## Childhood as a social construction

Saying childhood is **socially constructed** means that what children are thought to be, how long childhood lasts and how children should be treated are set by society, not by biology alone. Biological immaturity is universal; its meaning is not.

### Evidence from history

- **Philippe Ariès** (*Centuries of Childhood*, English edition 1962) used paintings and other sources to argue that in medieval Europe the idea of childhood as a separate stage barely existed: once past infancy, children joined adult work and leisure.
- **Edward Shorter** (*The Making of the Modern Family*, 1975) argued that high infant mortality once discouraged deep emotional investment in young children.
- **Linda Pollock** (*Forgotten Children*, 1983) challenged Ariès, using diaries and letters from the sixteenth century onwards to show that parents did see children as different and cared for them. Use this as evaluation: childhood may have changed less sharply than Ariès claimed.
- **Hugh Cunningham** (*The Invention of Childhood*, 2006) traced how ideas of childhood in England changed over a thousand years, emphasising gradual change rather than a single turning point.

### Evidence from comparison

- In many societies children work, care for siblings or contribute to household income from an early age. Samantha Punch's research in rural Bolivia (2001) described children combining school with substantial household and farm work, and negotiating their own space within it.
- Ideas of when childhood ends differ: legal ages of marriage, work, voting and criminal responsibility vary between countries.

### Two images of the child

Chris Jenks (*Childhood*, 1996) contrasted two traditions in Western thought: the **Dionysian** child, naturally wild and needing control, and the **Apollonian** child, naturally innocent and needing protection and nurture. Both shape how families and states treat children.

## Changes in the role and social position of children

**The march of progress view.** Children's position has improved: laws restrict child labour, education is compulsory, child protection is stronger, and the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child (1989) sets out children's rights. Smaller families and lower infant mortality mean parents invest more emotionally and financially in each child. The family has become **child-centred**, and children have more say in decisions, including spending (sometimes called "pester power").

**The conflict view.** Protection is also control. Diana Gittins (*The Family in Question*, 1985) used the term **age patriarchy** to describe adult domination over children, including control of their time, space, bodies and money. Children's dependence is enforced by law and custom, and abuse shows the dangers of adult power.

**The toxic or disappearing childhood debate.** Some writers argue that media, technology and consumer culture are blurring childhood and adulthood or harming children's development; others reply that children are active, competent users of technology. Treat this as a debate, not a settled fact.

## Grandparents and changing life expectancy

### Changing role and position

- **Rising life expectancy** means more people live to become grandparents and great-grandparents, and for longer. Julia Brannen (2003) described the **beanpole family**: many generations, but few members in each because of smaller family sizes.
- **Childcare**: where mothers work, grandparents often provide informal childcare, giving them a renewed practical role.
- **Financial help** may flow down the generations, through gifts, housing or inheritance.
- **The pivot generation**: adults in middle age may care for both their own children and their ageing parents.
- **Dependency**: longer life can mean longer periods of ill health, so older people may need care from their children.

### Cross-cultural comparison

The syllabus asks for comparison between societies.

- In many South Asian and East Asian societies, grandparents traditionally live with a married son's family and hold authority, supported by values of respect for elders and filial duty.
- In many Western societies, grandparents more often live separately and their influence depends on choice and geography.
- Migration, urbanisation and women's employment are changing traditional patterns: grandparents may be left behind in rural areas, or may migrate to help with childcare.

**Evaluation**: the role of grandparents depends on health, income, distance, divorce in the middle generation and cultural expectations, so there is no single "grandparent role".

## Class, gender and ethnicity and children's experiences

### Social class

- **Parenting styles**: Annette Lareau's *Unequal Childhoods* (2003) contrasted middle-class "concerted cultivation" with working-class and poor families' "accomplishment of natural growth".
- **Material resources**: income shapes diet, housing, space to study, access to digital devices and holidays.
- **Responsibilities**: in poorer households children are more likely to care for siblings or contribute to income.

### Gender

- Mayer Hillman and colleagues' *One False Move* (1990) found that boys were allowed more independence than girls, for example to travel unaccompanied.
- Angela McRobbie and Jenny Garber (1976) described girls' **bedroom culture**, with girls' leisure more home-based and supervised than boys'.
- Girls may be expected to do more domestic work and care.

### Ethnicity

- Ghazala Bhatti's *Asian Children at Home and at School* (1999) found that many Asian families placed great weight on family honour (*izzat*) and on parents' expectations, with girls in particular more closely supervised.
- Religion, language and expectations about obedience, extended kin and marriage shape children's experiences, and children of migrants may move between the norms of home and the wider society.

**Evaluation**: these factors interact. A middle-class girl and a working-class boy from the same ethnic group may have very different childhoods, so avoid treating any group as uniform.

## Changing concepts of motherhood and fatherhood

**Motherhood.**
- The ideal of the full-time mother has been challenged by women's paid employment.
- Sharon Hays (*The Cultural Contradictions of Motherhood*, 1996) described an ideology of **intensive mothering**: child-centred, expert-guided, emotionally absorbing and time-consuming, which places heavy demands on working mothers.
- Feminists argue the ideal of the "good mother" makes women feel responsible for any problem in their children's lives.

**Fatherhood.**
- The traditional father was the breadwinner and disciplinarian.
- The "new father" ideal is more involved in daily care and emotional life; Esther Dermott (2008) described "intimate fatherhood" based on emotional closeness rather than time or tasks.
- Critics argue that practice lags behind the ideal: mothers still carry most routine care (link to 4.1).

**Evaluation**: separate the concepts (ideals) from practice, and note variation by class, ethnicity and society, as well as the effect of policy such as paternity leave.

## Worked example: arguing against a view

*Quotation (practice): "Children today have more power in the family than ever before."*

> **Argument 1: age patriarchy.** Gittins (1985) argued that adults still control children's time, space, bodies and money. Children cannot choose where they live, when they go out or what they do with their earnings, and the law enforces their dependence. More say over purchases does not equal power over major decisions.
>
> **Argument 2: inequality between childhoods.** Lareau and Punch show that children's experiences differ by class and society. Many children worldwide work or care for others from an early age, with little say. The claim generalises from affluent, child-centred families.

Why it works: each argument names a perspective or study, explains it and directly challenges "more power than ever".

## Common mistakes

- Using Ariès without Pollock's criticism.
- Describing grandparents without cross-cultural comparison or life expectancy, both named in the syllabus.
- Treating "child-centred" as meaning children hold power.
- Writing about class only, when gender and ethnicity are also named.
- Confusing ideals of parenthood with what parents actually do.
