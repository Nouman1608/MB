Section A of Paper 3 asks for one essay on a set Shakespeare play. It is worth 25 of the paper's 50
marks and, unlike the AS Level papers, is assessed on all five objectives, each 20%: AO1 to AO4 plus
**AO5, the discussion and evaluation of varying opinions and interpretations**. This guide focuses on the
Shakespeare-specific skills and on making AO5 part of the argument. For the paper's format and both
sections together, see the [Paper 3 overview](/resources/a-level-english-literature-paper-3-shakespeare-and-drama/).

## What the section asks of you

On each play you choose an **(a) question**, an essay on the play as a whole, or a **(b) question**,
which prints a passage from the play for close analysis in relation to the whole. The play is not
allowed in the exam room. For 2026 the set plays are *Hamlet* and *The Taming of the Shrew*; for 2027
they are *Henry IV, Part 1* and *The Taming of the Shrew*. Check the list for your examination year.

## Shakespeare's dramatic language

### Verse, prose and rhyme

Shakespeare's characters move between three modes, and the movement is meaningful.

- **Blank verse** (unrhymed iambic pentameter) is the usual voice of high-status characters and serious
  feeling. Look for lines that break the pattern: an extra syllable, a short line, a line shared between
  two speakers.
- **Prose** is often used for comedy, low-status characters, letters, and characters feigning or
  experiencing disorder. A high-status character who drops into prose has changed something: mood,
  company or self-control. Hamlet's shifts between verse and prose, depending on whom he is with and
  what he is performing, are an example.
- **Rhyming couplets** often close scenes or mark a sententious, quotable conclusion.

A **shared line**, where one speaker completes another's pentameter, can suggest intimacy, haste or
conflict; a **short line** can mark a pause the actor must fill.

### Soliloquy and its effect

Soliloquies give the audience access to a character's reasoning, and so shape our judgement. Ask how the
soliloquy positions the audience: as confidant, judge or accomplice? In *Henry IV, Part 1*, Hal's early
soliloquy reveals that his tavern life is a calculated performance, which changes how every later tavern
scene is watched. In *Hamlet*, the soliloquies chart a mind arguing with itself, and their placement
between public scenes contrasts private thought with public action.

### Imagery patterns

Shakespeare builds clusters of imagery across a play. In *Hamlet*, images of disease, rot and poison run
through the language of the Danish court; in *The Taming of the Shrew*, Petruchio describes his treatment
of Katherina in the language of taming a hawk. A passage answer that connects one image to its wider
pattern shows AO1 knowledge of the whole play efficiently.

## Structure and genre

- **Five-act shape**: exposition, complication, crisis, falling action and resolution, though
  Shakespeare often blurs it. Locate a (b) passage in this arc.
- **Parallel and contrasting plots**: the tavern and the court in *Henry IV, Part 1*; Bianca's suitors
  alongside Katherina and Petruchio; the several sons seeking to avenge fathers in *Hamlet*.
- **Frames**: *The Taming of the Shrew* opens with an Induction in which a drunken tinker, Christopher
  Sly, is tricked into believing he is a lord, and the main story is performed for him as a play. This
  frame invites readings of the whole action as performance.
- **Genre**: tragedy, comedy and history set different expectations about endings, and much critical
  debate concerns how far a play fulfils them.

## Context for Shakespeare

Useful contexts are those that explain dramatic choices: the open, daylit stages and minimal scenery that
made language carry setting; the convention that women's roles were played by boy actors; ideas about
kingship, honour, marriage and the family that the plays test. Tie any context to a specific moment.

## Making AO5 work

AO5 asks you to **discuss and evaluate** other interpretations. It does not require a list of critics'
names. Strong sources of alternative readings are:

- **Performance choices**: how different productions might stage a moment, and how that changes its
  meaning.
- **Critical approaches**: for example feminist, historicist or psychoanalytic readings, stated as ways
  of reading the play.
- **Audiences across time**: how a modern audience's response might differ from an early audience's.

The skill is to **weigh** a reading against textual evidence, then judge.

### Annotated model paragraph

A strong AO5 paragraph on Katherina's final speech in *The Taming of the Shrew* might read:

> Katherina's long closing speech on a wife's duty to obey can be played in more than one way, and the
> choice shapes the whole comedy. Performed sincerely, it completes Petruchio's "taming" and makes the
> ending a celebration of order; some modern productions instead stage it with irony, so that her
> fluency itself becomes a sign of independence. The text supports neither reading alone: the speech is
> the longest she gives, which suggests her voice has grown rather than been silenced, yet it is
> delivered in public, at Petruchio's command, to win his wager. The Induction tips the balance for me:
> because the whole story is staged as a play for Sly, the speech reads as a performance of submission
> rather than a statement of belief.

The paragraph sets out two readings, tests them against **structure and dramatic context**, and reaches a
**judgement**: that is AO5 working with AO2 and AO3.

## The (b) passage answer

1. **Locate** the passage in the play's structure in one sentence.
2. **Thesis** that answers the question and names the passage's dramatic function.
3. **Analyse closely**: verse or prose, line breaks and shared lines, imagery, staging implied by the
   language, who is present.
4. **Link outward** to other moments, especially through imagery patterns or parallel scenes.
5. **Fold in AO5**: one or two points where a different staging or reading would change the passage's
   effect, evaluated against the text.

## The (a) essay

Start with a thesis that a reader could dispute. Asked "How far does the play present Hal as a
calculating politician?", a weak opening agrees and lists examples. A stronger one takes a measured
position: "Hal's early soliloquy invites us to see every tavern scene as calculation, but the warmth of
his exchanges with Falstaff suggests a prince who enjoys the role he claims merely to be playing; the
play presents calculation and pleasure as hard to separate." That thesis answers "How far", prepares an
AO5 debate, and gives the essay a direction.

- An arguable thesis that addresses the question's terms, including "How far" if it appears.
- Three or four moments from across the play, including the ending.
- AO5 woven into two or three paragraphs, not saved for the conclusion.
- Precise, brief quotation and reference from memory.

## Common mistakes

- **AO5 as name-dropping**: a critic's name with no evaluation earns little.
- **A detached AO5 paragraph**: alternative readings listed at the end, unconnected to the argument.
- **Ignoring verse**: never commenting on metre, prose shifts or shared lines.
- **Character as person**: speculating about off-stage lives rather than dramatic presentation.
- **Context as history lesson**: Elizabethan background unconnected to a specific moment.
