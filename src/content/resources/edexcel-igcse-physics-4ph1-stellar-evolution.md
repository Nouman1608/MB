This guide covers sub-topic **8(c) Stellar evolution** of the Pearson
Edexcel International GCSE in Physics (4PH1), Issue 4 specification:
statements 8.7 to 8.12P. Statements 8.7 to 8.10 are core; 8.11P (absolute
magnitude) and 8.12P (the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram) are Physics-only
and assessed on Paper 2P. For the topic as a whole, see the
[Astrophysics study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-physics-astrophysics/).
Fusion, the energy source of stars, is explained in
[Fission and fusion](/resources/edexcel-igcse-physics-4ph1-fission-and-fusion/).

## What the specification asks for

- **8.7** understand how stars can be classified according to their colour
- **8.8** know that a star's colour is related to its surface temperature
- **8.9** describe the evolution of stars of similar mass to the Sun through the stages nebula, star (main sequence), red giant, white dwarf
- **8.10** describe the evolution of stars with a mass larger than the Sun
- **8.11P** understand how the brightness of a star at a standard distance can be represented using absolute magnitude
- **8.12P** draw the main components of the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram (HR diagram)

## Colour and surface temperature (8.7, 8.8)

Stars are grouped by colour, and the colour tells you the **surface
temperature**. The sequence from coolest to hottest is:

**red → orange → yellow → white → blue**

- Red stars are the coolest, with surfaces at around 3000 K.
- The Sun is a yellow star with a surface temperature of about 5800 K.
- Blue stars are the hottest, with surfaces above 10 000 K.

*Temperatures are approximate and given only to show the trend.*

The reason is the same as for a piece of metal heated in a flame. It
first glows dull red, then orange, yellow and eventually white as it gets
hotter. A hotter object emits more of its radiation at shorter
wavelengths, so its light shifts from the red end of the spectrum
towards the blue end. Colour is therefore a property of the star's
surface, not of its distance or its size.

## A star like the Sun (8.9)

The specification names four stages. Each change has a cause you should
be able to give.

1. **Nebula.** A cloud of gas (mostly hydrogen) and dust. Gravity pulls the material together; as it collapses, it heats up.
2. **Star (main sequence).** When the core is hot and dense enough, hydrogen nuclei fuse to form helium, releasing energy. The star is **stable** for a very long time because the inward pull of **gravity** is balanced by the outward **pressure** from the energy released by fusion. The Sun is at this stage.
3. **Red giant.** When the hydrogen in the core runs out, the balance is lost. The core contracts and heats up, while the outer layers **expand and cool**, so the star becomes much larger and redder.
4. **White dwarf.** The outer layers drift away into space, leaving the hot, dense core: a white dwarf. It is very small and no longer fuses hydrogen, so it slowly cools and fades.

## A star more massive than the Sun (8.10)

Statement 8.10 is a single general statement and does not list stages.
The accepted sequence for a star much more massive than the Sun is:

- **Nebula → main sequence star.** A more massive star is hotter and brighter on the main sequence and uses up its hydrogen much faster, so its main-sequence lifetime is shorter.
- **Red supergiant.** When the core hydrogen runs out, it swells into a red supergiant, larger than a red giant.
- **Supernova.** The core collapses and the star explodes in a supernova, scattering material into space, where it can form new nebulae.
- **Neutron star or black hole.** What remains of the core becomes an extremely dense neutron star or, for the most massive stars, a black hole, from which not even light can escape.

The key comparison is that **mass decides the fate**: Sun-like stars
end as white dwarfs; much more massive stars end, after a supernova, as
neutron stars or black holes.

## Absolute magnitude (8.11P)

How bright a star looks from Earth (its apparent brightness) depends on
two things: how much light it actually gives out, and how far away it
is. A dim star nearby can look brighter than a powerful star far away.
To compare stars fairly, astronomers imagine every star at the **same
standard distance** (this is 10 parsecs, about 32.6 light-years). The
brightness a star would have at that distance is its **absolute
magnitude**.

Two features of the magnitude scale cause most errors:

- **Smaller numbers mean brighter stars.** A star of absolute magnitude −5 is far brighter than one of +10.
- **The values can be negative.** The very brightest stars have negative absolute magnitudes. The Sun's is about +5.

## The Hertzsprung–Russell diagram (8.12P)

The HR diagram is a graph of stars that shows how brightness and
temperature are linked. To draw its main components:

**Axes**

- **Vertical:** absolute magnitude (or luminosity). Brighter stars at the **top**, so absolute magnitude runs from large positive numbers at the bottom to negative numbers at the top.
- **Horizontal:** surface temperature (or colour). **Hot on the left, cool on the right**: the temperature axis runs backwards compared with an ordinary graph. Colour goes from blue on the left to red on the right.

**Regions**

- **Main sequence:** a diagonal band from the top left (hot, bright, blue stars) to the bottom right (cool, dim, red stars). Most stars, including the Sun (near the middle), lie here.
- **Red giants:** above the main sequence on the right: cool but very bright, because they are so large.
- **Supergiants:** across the top: the brightest stars.
- **White dwarfs:** below the main sequence on the left: hot but dim, because they are so small.

The Sun's life can be traced on the diagram: on the main sequence now,
it will move up and to the right to become a red giant, then down and
to the left to become a white dwarf.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking red stars are the hottest, by analogy with "red hot". Blue stars are hottest.
- Leaving out the cause of a stage change, such as "hydrogen runs out" before red giant.
- Saying the Sun will become a supernova or a black hole.
- Describing absolute magnitude as "how bright the star looks from Earth"; that is apparent brightness.
- Drawing the HR temperature axis increasing to the right.
- Placing white dwarfs at the top or red giants on the left of the diagram.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** (8.9, 8.10): give the stages in order **and** the key event linking each pair.
- **Draw** (8.12P): label both axes with quantities and the direction of increase, then add and label the four regions.
- **Explain** (main-sequence stability): name both forces and say they are balanced.
- **Describe the differences** (8.10 with 8.9): use the word "whereas" to link each pair of stages.
