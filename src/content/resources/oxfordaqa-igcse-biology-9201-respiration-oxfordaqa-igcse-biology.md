## Respiration transfers energy

Respiration is a series of chemical reactions in cells that **transfers energy** from glucose. Energy is not
"made" or "produced": it is transferred from the chemical store in glucose to the cell's processes and, in part,
to the surroundings as heat.

```
aerobic:   glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water
           C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O
```

**Aerobic respiration takes place continuously in both plants and animals**, day and night. **Most of the
reactions take place inside mitochondria**, so cells with a high demand for energy, such as muscle cells, contain
many mitochondria.

**Worked example 1: evidence that seeds release energy** (invented data). Two vacuum flasks each hold the same
mass of pea seeds and a thermometer. The seeds in flask A are germinating. The seeds in flask B were boiled first,
which kills them. Both flasks are washed with disinfectant to kill microorganisms.

| Day | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Flask A temperature (°C) | 20.0 | 21.5 | 23.0 | 24.5 |
| Flask B temperature (°C) | 20.0 | 20.0 | 20.1 | 20.0 |

```
rise in A over 3 days = 24.5 − 20.0 = 4.5 °C   → 1.5 °C per day
rise in B over 3 days = 0.0 °C (within error)
```

Interpretation: living seeds respire, and some of the energy transferred heats the flask. Flask B is the
**control**: it shows the rise is caused by living seeds, not by the flask or the room. The disinfectant makes sure
the heat is from the seeds, not from microorganisms respiring on them.

## Respiration in plants: light and dark

Plants respire all the time, but in light they also photosynthesise. What you measure is the **net** effect.

| Condition | Respiration | Photosynthesis | Net gas exchange of the plant |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dark | Yes | No | Takes in oxygen, gives out carbon dioxide |
| Dim light | Yes | Slow | Little or no net exchange |
| Bright light | Yes | Fast (faster than respiration) | Takes in carbon dioxide, gives out oxygen |

A common error is to say "plants photosynthesise in the day and respire at night". They respire **continuously**;
in bright light, photosynthesis simply hides it.

## What the energy is used for

| Use | Example |
|---|---|
| Building larger molecules from smaller ones | Joining glucose into starch or glycogen; joining amino acids into proteins |
| In animals, muscle contraction | Movement, breathing, the heartbeat |
| In mammals and birds, keeping a steady body temperature in colder surroundings | Respiration releases heat that replaces heat lost to cold surroundings |
| In plants, building sugars, nitrates and other nutrients into amino acids, then into proteins | Growth of new cells |

## The body during exercise

Exercise increases the demand for energy in the muscles. The body reacts with three changes:

1. **Heart rate increases**, increasing blood flow to the muscles.
2. **The rate and depth of breathing increase**.
3. **Glycogen stored in the muscles is converted back to glucose**.

Together these **increase the supply of glucose and oxygen** to the muscles and **increase the rate of removal of
carbon dioxide** from them.

## Anaerobic respiration in muscle

If insufficient oxygen is reaching the muscles, energy is transferred by **anaerobic respiration**:

```
glucose → lactic acid
C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₃H₆O₃
```

- It is the **incomplete breakdown** of glucose, so **much less energy is transferred** than in aerobic
  respiration.
- **Lactic acid builds up**. An **oxygen debt** must be repaid to **oxidise the lactic acid to carbon dioxide and
  water**.
- During long periods of vigorous activity, muscles become **fatigued** and stop contracting efficiently. One
  cause is the build-up of lactic acid. **Blood flowing through the muscles eventually removes the lactic acid.**

**Annotated model answer**

*Question: A runner sprints 400 m. Explain why she continues to breathe deeply and quickly for several minutes after
she stops. [3]*

> During the sprint not enough oxygen reached her muscles, so they respired anaerobically and lactic acid built up
> [1]. This created an oxygen debt [1]. After the race, the extra oxygen taken in by deep, fast breathing is used to
> oxidise the lactic acid to carbon dioxide and water [1].

"To get her breath back" is not an explanation; the mark scheme wants lactic acid and oxygen debt.

## Anaerobic respiration in plants and microorganisms

**Anaerobic respiration in plant cells and in some microorganisms results in the production of ethanol and carbon
dioxide**, not lactic acid:

```
glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide
```

A plant context: when soil is **waterlogged**, the air spaces fill with water and root cells receive little
oxygen. The roots respire anaerobically, which transfers less energy and produces ethanol, so the roots may be
damaged and the plant grows poorly. Yeast, a microorganism, respires in the same way when oxygen is short.

| | Aerobic | Anaerobic (muscle) | Anaerobic (plants, some microorganisms) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oxygen needed? | Yes | No | No |
| Products | Carbon dioxide and water | Lactic acid | Ethanol and carbon dioxide |
| Breakdown of glucose | Complete | Incomplete | Incomplete |
| Energy transferred | Much more | Much less | Much less |

## Required practical: effects of exercise

The required practical is to **investigate the effects of exercise on the human body**. You can be asked to design
the procedure.

**Worked example 2: reading a recovery curve** (invented data). A student's resting pulse is 70 beats per minute.
She steps for 3 minutes, then her pulse is taken every minute.

| Minutes after exercise | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pulse (beats per minute) | 136 | 118 | 101 | 88 | 78 | 72 | 70 |

```
increase caused by exercise = 136 − 70 = 66 beats per minute
percentage increase = 66 ÷ 70 × 100 = 94% (2 s.f.)
recovery time = 6 minutes (first reading back at the resting value)
fall in first minute = 136 − 118 = 18; fall from 5 to 6 minutes = 2
```

The pulse falls fastest just after exercise and more slowly as it nears the resting rate. A fitter person usually
has a shorter recovery time, so the same test can compare people, provided the exercise, the time and the method
of measuring are kept the same.

## Common mistakes

- Saying respiration "makes energy". It transfers energy.
- Saying plants respire only at night.
- Writing that lactic acid is "broken down by breathing". It is oxidised using oxygen, which is the oxygen debt.
- Giving lactic acid as a product in plants or yeast; they make ethanol and carbon dioxide.
- Saying anaerobic respiration releases "no energy". It transfers much less, not none.
- Listing exercise changes without saying they increase the supply of oxygen and glucose and the removal of
  carbon dioxide.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** an exercise change: name the change, then link it to oxygen, glucose or carbon dioxide.
- **Compare** aerobic and anaerobic: paired points on oxygen, products and energy.
- **Describe** a method: independent, dependent and control variables, repeats and a safety point.
- **Use data**: quote values, calculate changes, and explain the shape of the curve.
