## Section A

**1.** Give the meaning of the term "eutrophication". **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between physical water scarcity and economic water scarcity. **[2]**

**3.** The table shows renewable water resources and population for two fictional countries. Use these thresholds: below 1 700 m³ per person per year is water stress; below 1 000 m³ is water scarcity; below 500 m³ is absolute scarcity.

| Country | Renewable water (km³ per year) | Population (millions) |
|---|---|---|
| Ostrel | 42 | 30 |
| Bayan | 9 | 20 |

**(a)** Calculate the renewable water per person in each country. Show your working. (1 km³ = 1 000 000 000 m³) **[2]**

**(b)** Classify each country using the thresholds. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest **two** ways Bayan could increase its water supply, and give one drawback of each. **[4]**

**4.** Explain why transboundary pollution is difficult to manage. **[4]**

**5.** Explain how inadequate waste management can degrade an urban environment. **[4]**

**6.** Outline the idea of the environmental Kuznets curve and give one limitation of it. **[4]**

## Section B

**7.** The table shows annual mean concentrations of fine particulates (PM2.5, micrograms per m³) at four sites in Teravin, a fictional city in an MIC.

| Site | Description | 2015 | 2024 |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | City-centre road junction | 68 | 41 |
| B | Industrial district with a coal-fired plant | 85 | 80 |
| C | Residential suburb | 35 | 22 |
| D | Informal settlement beside an open dump | 72 | 75 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in PM2.5 at site A. **[2]**

**(b)** Describe the changes in PM2.5 shown in the table. **[3]**

**(c)** Suggest reasons for the differences in change between the sites. **[5]**

**8.** Explain how deforestation can lead to the degradation of a rural environment. **[6]**

**9.** Explain the constraints that limit improvements to degraded urban environments in LICs and MICs. **[6]**

**10.** "Population growth is the main cause of the degradation of rural environments." With reference to examples, how far do you agree? **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** The enrichment of water with nutrients such as nitrates and phosphates [1], causing rapid growth of algae that, as they decay, use up dissolved oxygen and kill fish [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Physical scarcity means there is not enough water to meet demand, typical of arid regions [1]; economic scarcity means water exists but people lack the money or infrastructure to obtain it [1]. **[2]**

**3. (a)** Ostrel: 42 000 000 000 ÷ 30 000 000 = 1 400 m³ per person [1]; Bayan: 9 000 000 000 ÷ 20 000 000 = 450 m³ per person [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Ostrel: water stress (between 1 000 and 1 700) [1]; Bayan: absolute scarcity (below 500) [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** Desalination of seawater [1], but it uses large amounts of energy and produces brine waste [1]. Recycling treated wastewater for irrigation or industry [1], but treatment is costly and people may not accept it [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* also credit groundwater pumping (falling water tables) or inter-basin transfer (cost or conflict) with a matching drawback.

**4.** The pollution is produced in one country but harms another [1], so the affected country has no legal power over the polluter [1]. Agreements need cooperation between governments with different priorities and levels of wealth [1]. It is hard to prove the source and share the cost of reducing it [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Uncollected rubbish is dumped in streets, rivers and open land [1], blocking drains so that heavy rain causes flooding [1]. Burning waste releases smoke and toxic fumes, worsening air quality [1]. Leachate from dumps seeps into groundwater and streams, polluting water supplies [1]. **[4]**

**6.** It suggests that some pollution rises as a country industrialises [1] and then falls as incomes rise, because richer societies demand and can afford pollution controls [1]. Limitation: it fits local pollutants such as sulphur dioxide better than carbon dioxide or waste [1], and falls in HICs may come from moving polluting industry abroad [1]. **[4]**

**7. (a)** (41 − 68) ÷ 68 × 100 [1] = −39.7%, a fall of about 40% [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** PM2.5 fell at sites A and C, by 27 and 13 micrograms per m³ [1]. It changed little at the industrial site B (85 to 80), which remains the highest [1]. It rose at site D, from 72 to 75, the only site to get worse [1]. **[3]**

**(c)** At A, cleaner vehicles, traffic controls or public transport may have cut exhaust emissions [1]. C is away from major sources, so falls in city-wide pollution show clearly there [1]. At B, the coal-fired plant is a large point source that may not yet have been fitted with controls [1], because closing or upgrading it is costly and it supplies power and jobs [1]. At D, open burning at the growing dump and household cooking with solid fuel may have increased local smoke [1]. **[5]**

**8.** Removing trees reduces interception, so more rain reaches the ground directly [1]. Without roots to bind it and leaf litter to protect it, soil is eroded by surface run-off [1]. Faster run-off increases flooding downstream [1], and eroded sediment fills river channels and reservoirs [1]. Nutrients stored in the vegetation are lost, so soils become less fertile [1]. Habitat loss reduces biodiversity [1]. **[6]**

**9.** Cities lack the money to build sewerage, drainage and waste collection as fast as they grow [1], and many residents cannot pay for services [1]. Informal settlements may not be recognised by authorities, so services are not provided [1]. Weak enforcement or corruption allows polluting industries to continue [1], because they provide jobs and tax revenue [1]. Lack of monitoring data means problems are not measured or prioritised [1]. **[6]**

**10.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point. The points below are indicative creditworthy content.

*Introduction:* define rural degradation [1] and the population argument, linked to Malthus [1].

*Agree:* population growth shortens fallow periods, so soils lose fertility [1]. More people push farming onto steep slopes and dry margins, increasing erosion [1]. Demand for fuelwood clears woodland around villages [1]. More livestock causes overgrazing on shared land [1]. Pressure on land is greatest where farmers have no alternative incomes [1].

*Disagree:* poor practices, such as ploughing down slopes or irrigation without drainage, cause degradation even at low densities [1]. Commercial deforestation for cattle, soy or plantations is driven by markets, not local population [1]. Insecure land tenure discourages conservation [1]. Government policy, such as cash-crop promotion, can push degradation [1]. Climate variability and drought trigger degradation in semi-arid areas [1]. Boserup argued population pressure can drive innovation [1], supported by the Machakos study, where more people brought less erosion [1].

*Analysis:* population acts through other factors, such as technology, tenure and markets [1]. Its effect depends on whether farmers have capital and secure rights to invest [1].

*Use of examples:* a named area where population pressure contributed to degradation [1]; a named area where markets or policy were the main cause [1].

*Judgement:* a reasoned conclusion on "how far", for example that population growth is a contributing but rarely the main cause [1]. A clear final view [1]. **[20]**

*Examiner insight:* the best answers use Boserup and Machakos to challenge the statement, rather than simply listing other causes.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Forgetting to convert km³ to m³ before dividing.
- Describing table data without values at named sites.
- Ignoring the site descriptions in "suggest" answers.
- Explaining deforestation effects without the process chain.
- Essays that accept the population argument without challenge.
