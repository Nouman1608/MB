These revision notes condense **reading and writing about prose fiction and drama** for IB Diploma
Programme Language A: Literature, at SL and HL. They follow the current IB subject brief (first
assessments for SL and HL 2021) and map to the areas of exploration **Time and space** and
**Intertextuality: connecting texts**. These skills feed every assessed component: Paper 1,
Paper 2, the individual oral and, at HL, the HL essay.

## Key terms: prose fiction

| Term | Meaning | What to say about it |
|---|---|---|
| Narrator | The voice that tells the story | What it knows, hides or judges |
| Focaliser | The character through whose eyes events are seen | How their view limits or colours events |
| First person | "I" narration by a character | Intimacy; bias; possible unreliability |
| Third-person limited | "He/she", close to one mind | Sympathy with one character; gaps elsewhere |
| Third-person omniscient | Moves between minds, may comment | Authority; irony; wide view |
| Free indirect style | Character's thoughts in the narrator's sentence, without "she thought" | Blurs judgement: whose view is this? |
| Unreliable narrator | An account the reader learns to doubt | How the text signals doubt, and when |
| Analepsis | Flashback | What the past explains or reframes |
| Prolepsis | Flash-forward | Suspense, dread, a sense of fate |
| Scene / summary / ellipsis | Real-time telling / compressed telling / skipped time | What the writer lingers on or skips |
| Direct / indirect characterisation | Telling us / showing us through speech and action | What we infer and why |
| Frame narrative | A story told inside another story | Who listens, and how that filters it |
| Pathetic fallacy | Weather or nature mirroring feeling | Why the mirror matters here |

## Key terms: drama

| Term | Meaning | What to say about it |
|---|---|---|
| Stage directions | Instructions for set, movement, tone | They are evidence, not background |
| Soliloquy | A character alone on stage speaks thoughts aloud | Private access for the audience only |
| Monologue | A long speech to other characters (or the audience) | Power, persuasion, loss of control |
| Aside | A brief remark others on stage do not hear | Complicity between character and audience |
| Dramatic irony | The audience knows more than a character | Tension, pity, comedy |
| Subtext | Meaning beneath the spoken words | What the pause or change of subject hides |
| Off-stage action | Events reported, heard or implied, not shown | Why the playwright withholds it |
| Props, set, lighting, sound | Physical elements of performance | Symbol, mood, focus of attention |
| Act / scene break | Structural division | Time jumps, shifts of place, peaks of tension |

## Method in steps: a prose extract

1. **Perspective.** Who narrates? Whose eyes? Mark any slide into free indirect style.
2. **Time.** Put the events in story order, then compare with the order of telling. Mark
   flashbacks, flash-forwards and changes of pace.
3. **Setting.** What does the place or weather do: mirror, contrast or pressure?
4. **Character.** List what is shown rather than told. Choose the most revealing detail.
5. **Structure.** Look hard at the first and last sentences and any short paragraph that stands
   alone.
6. **Thesis.** One sentence saying what the extract does and how.

## Method in steps: a drama extract

1. **Stage picture.** Read every stage direction first. Who is on stage? What can the audience
   see?
2. **Power in dialogue.** Who speaks most, who interrupts, who asks and who answers?
3. **Silence and subtext.** Mark every pause, dash and unfinished line. Say what it hides.
4. **Conventions.** Is there a soliloquy, aside or dramatic irony? What does the audience know that
   a character does not?
5. **Sound, light and objects.** Identify one prop or effect that carries symbolic weight.
6. **Thesis.** Tie staging and dialogue together in one claim about the scene.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Author vs narrator.** Austen writes *Pride and Prejudice* (1813); the ironic narrator opens it
  with "It is a truth universally acknowledged". The irony belongs to the narrative voice, and the
  rest of the novel tests the "truth" it states.
- **Narrator vs focaliser.** In a third-person limited novel, the narrator tells but a character
  sees. Getting this right is the basis of any comment on bias.
- **Story vs telling.** The story is what happened; the telling is the order and speed of the text.
  Retelling the story earns little. Analysing the telling earns credit.
- **Soliloquy vs monologue vs aside.** Alone and thinking; talking at length to others; a quick
  line others do not hear.
- **Dialogue in prose vs dialogue in drama.** In a novel, a tag such as "she said coldly" tells you
  how a line sounds. In a play, the actor and director decide unless a stage direction specifies,
  so the same words can be performed in several ways. Say so when it matters.
- **In-text setting vs context.** Setting is a craft choice inside the work. The **Time and space**
  area of exploration asks about the contexts in which a work was written and is received. Keep the
  two apart in your writing.
- **Direct vs indirect characterisation.** Being told a character is cruel is weaker evidence than
  watching them act cruelly.

## Small worked reminders

**Frame narrative.** Joseph Conrad's *Heart of Darkness* (1899) is told by an unnamed narrator who
reports Marlow's story. You therefore receive Marlow's account second-hand, through a listener on a
boat, and the frame reminds you that every account is shaped by who tells it and who hears it.

**Pace.** *Original text written for Marlbridge:* "The war took four years. The walk from the
station to her door took eleven minutes, and he remembered every one." Four years pass in one short
sentence (summary); eleven minutes get a whole clause and "every one" (scene). The imbalance shows
what matters to the character.

**Off-stage sound.** In Ibsen's *A Doll's House* (1879), Nora's departure is marked by the sound of
a door slamming off stage. The audience is left alone with Torvald and the noise; the play refuses a
final speech.

**Props.** In Glaspell's *Trifles* (1916), a broken birdcage becomes evidence. The men miss its meaning; the
women and the audience do not. A prop shifts the audience's sympathy.

## Linking to the areas of exploration

- **Time and space.** Ask how a work's time and place of writing shape its form. A play written for
  a particular kind of stage, or a novel first read in instalments, carries traces of that context.
  Treat context as evidence for a reading, not a biography paragraph.
- **Intertextuality.** Prose and drama handle the same concerns by different means. A novel can
  state a thought; a play must stage it. Comparing those means gives a Paper 2 essay real
  comparative content. See the [intertextuality revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-literature-intertextuality-revision-notes/)
  and the [readers, writers and texts revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-literature-readers-writers-texts-revision-notes/).

## Paragraph formula

**Claim → embedded evidence → technique → effect → link to the whole work.** One point, fully
developed, beats three techniques named in a list.

## Quick self-test

1. What is the difference between a narrator and a focaliser?
2. Name the technique: "She would not cry. Not here, not in front of him." (third-person novel)
3. A chapter opens: "Years later, she would remember this as the last happy evening." Name the
   technique and one effect.
4. What is the difference between a scene and a summary in prose?
5. Give one reason a writer might use a frame narrative.
6. What is the difference between a soliloquy and an aside?
7. A character says "I'm fine" while the stage direction reads *(she grips the chair)*. What term
   describes the gap, and what should you write about it?
8. Why might a playwright keep a key event off stage?
9. Which area of exploration covers the context in which a work was written and received?
10. In a Paper 2 essay comparing a novel and a play, what makes a stronger comparison than "both
    show grief"?

### Answers

1. The narrator tells the story; the focaliser is the character whose perception shapes what is
   seen. They can differ in third-person limited narration.
2. Free indirect style: the character's thoughts in the narrator's sentence, with no "she thought".
3. Prolepsis (flash-forward). It creates dread or poignancy, since the reader knows the happiness
   will end before the character does.
4. A scene tells events at roughly real-time speed with detail; a summary compresses a long period
   into a few words.
5. Any one: it filters the story through a listener or teller whose reliability you must weigh; it
   creates distance in time; it lets several accounts sit side by side.
6. A soliloquy is spoken by a character alone on stage; an aside is a short remark that other
   characters on stage do not hear.
7. Subtext (or dramatic contrast between words and action). Explain what the gesture reveals that
   the words deny, and how an audience reads the contradiction.
8. Any one: to create suspense; to focus on reactions rather than the event; to keep violence or
   spectacle in the audience's imagination; practical limits of the stage.
9. Time and space.
10. Comparing *methods*: how each form presents the grief (narration of thought vs silence and
    staging), and what each method lets the reader or audience understand.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Retelling the plot instead of analysing the order and pace of the telling.
- Calling a narrator "omniscient" when the text never leaves one character's mind.
- Treating the narrator's opinions as the author's.
- Ignoring stage directions, or quoting them without saying what they show an audience.
- Writing "this creates tension" without naming what on stage or on the page creates it.
- Naming pathetic fallacy for every change in weather without explaining what the link adds.
- Dropping long quotations in without comment instead of embedding a few precise words.
- Writing a context paragraph that never connects to a technique or an effect.
- In comparison, discussing the novel and the play in two separate blocks with no linking point.
