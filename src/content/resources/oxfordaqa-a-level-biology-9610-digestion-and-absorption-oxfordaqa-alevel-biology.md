## Following one molecule at a time

Examiners often ask you to trace a molecule. Use a fixed pattern: **where, enzyme, source, bond, product**.

| Molecule | Where | Enzyme (source) | Bond hydrolysed | Product |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Starch | Mouth, then duodenum | Amylase (salivary glands, then pancreas) | Glycosidic | Maltose |
| Maltose | Ileum epithelium | Maltase, membrane-bound | Glycosidic | Two glucose |
| Sucrose | Ileum epithelium | Sucrase, membrane-bound | Glycosidic | Glucose and fructose |
| Lactose | Ileum epithelium | Lactase, membrane-bound | Glycosidic | Glucose and galactose |
| Triglyceride | Duodenum | Lipase (pancreas) | Ester | Monoglycerides and fatty acids |
| Polypeptide | Stomach, then duodenum | Endopeptidases | Peptide, within the chain | Shorter polypeptides |
| Shorter polypeptide | Duodenum and ileum | Exopeptidases | Peptide, at the ends | Amino acids and dipeptides |
| Dipeptide | Ileum epithelium | Dipeptidase, membrane-bound | Peptide | Two amino acids |

Notice that every carbohydrate and protein pathway ends on the membrane of the ileum epithelium. The final products are released right beside the transport proteins that absorb them, so they are taken up before they can diffuse away or be used by gut bacteria.

## Counting hydrolysis reactions

Every hydrolysis uses one water molecule and breaks one bond.

**Worked example 1: a starch chain.** An unbranched amylose chain contains 600 glucose units.

1. Bonds in the chain = 600 − 1 = 599 glycosidic bonds.
2. If amylase converts the chain entirely to maltose, it makes 600 ÷ 2 = 300 maltose molecules. Each maltose contains one bond, so amylase has broken 599 − 300 = 299 bonds.
3. Maltase then breaks the remaining 300 bonds.
4. Total water molecules used = 299 + 300 = 599, the same as the number of bonds.

**Worked example 2: a triglyceride.** Lipase hydrolyses two of the three ester bonds, giving one monoglyceride and two fatty acids. That uses two water molecules per triglyceride. If a question says complete hydrolysis to glycerol, three water molecules are used.

## Bile salts: why emulsification matters

Bile salts are not enzymes, and they do not break bonds. They emulsify lipids: they split large droplets into many small droplets. Lipase is water-soluble and acts only at the surface of a droplet, so more surface means faster digestion.

**Worked example 3.** A lipid droplet of diameter 1.0 mm is emulsified into droplets of diameter 0.01 mm.

1. Volume scales with diameter cubed: (1.0 ÷ 0.01)³ = 100³ = 10⁶ small droplets form.
2. Surface area of one droplet scales with diameter squared, so each small droplet has 1/100² = 1/10⁴ of the original area.
3. Total area = 10⁶ × 1/10⁴ = 100 times the original.

The mass of lipid is unchanged, but the area available to lipase has risen a hundredfold. Bile salts then help again during absorption, by forming micelles.

## The ileum: three principles

Rather than listing features, sort them by the principle they serve. This structure suits "explain how the ileum is adapted" questions.

- **Large surface area:** folds, villi and microvilli. More membrane means more carrier proteins and more membrane-bound enzymes.
- **Short diffusion distance:** a single layer of epithelial cells covers each villus, with capillaries just beneath.
- **Steep concentration gradient:** blood flowing through the capillaries carries absorbed molecules away; the lacteal removes chylomicrons. This keeps the concentration inside the villus lower than in the lumen.

Two further features support active processes: epithelial cells contain many mitochondria to supply ATP, and their membranes carry many carrier and co-transporter proteins.

## Deciding how a substance is absorbed: using data

Diffusion, active transport and co-transport respond differently to experimental treatments. Learn the predictions, then match the data to them.

| Treatment | Simple or facilitated diffusion | Co-transport with sodium ions |
|---|---|---|
| Respiratory inhibitor (no ATP) | Continues | Falls, because the sodium-potassium pump stops and the sodium gradient runs down |
| Sodium ions removed from the lumen | Continues | Falls sharply |
| Concentration in lumen lower than in cells | Stops (no net uptake) | Continues against the gradient |

**Worked example 4 (illustrative data).** Pieces of ileum were placed in glucose solution under different conditions. Uptake was measured in arbitrary units.

| Condition | Glucose uptake |
|---|---|
| Normal saline, oxygen supplied | 40 |
| Normal saline, respiratory inhibitor added | 9 |
| Sodium-free solution, oxygen supplied | 7 |

- The inhibitor reduces uptake by (40 − 9) ÷ 40 × 100 = 77.5%. Most uptake depends on ATP.
- Removing sodium ions reduces it by (40 − 7) ÷ 40 × 100 = 82.5%. Most uptake depends on sodium ions.
- Together, these support co-transport driven by a sodium gradient that ATP maintains. The small remaining uptake is consistent with facilitated diffusion.

## Lipid absorption, step by step

1. Monoglycerides and fatty acids associate with bile salts to form micelles.
2. Micelles carry these products to the epithelial cell surface and keep them in solution near the membrane.
3. The monoglycerides and fatty acids are released and, being lipid-soluble, diffuse across the phospholipid bilayer.
4. Inside the cell, the endoplasmic reticulum recombines them into triglycerides.
5. Triglycerides are combined with proteins and packaged, via the Golgi apparatus, into chylomicrons.
6. Chylomicrons leave by exocytosis and enter the lacteal, travelling in the lymph before reaching the blood.

Why so many steps? Triglycerides are too large to cross the membrane, and lipid products are insoluble in water, so micelles are needed to ferry them across the watery lumen to the membrane.

## Common mistakes

- Writing "amylase breaks down carbohydrates" without naming starch and maltose.
- Saying disaccharidases are secreted into the lumen. They are bound to the epithelial membrane.
- Calling bile salts enzymes, or saying they digest lipids.
- Saying fatty acids enter by active transport. They diffuse across the membrane.
- Writing that glucose co-transport uses ATP directly. ATP drives the sodium-potassium pump, which creates the sodium gradient.
- Confusing absorption (crossing into cells) with assimilation (becoming part of cells) or egestion with excretion.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a pathway: give the sequence in order, naming enzyme, bond and product.
- **Explain** an adaptation: name the feature, then the principle it serves, then the effect on absorption rate.
- **Use the data** or **analyse**: quote values, calculate percentage changes, and say which process the result supports.
- **Suggest**: apply a principle to an unfamiliar situation, such as a drug that blocks a carrier protein.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why are some digestive enzymes membrane-bound?**
Disaccharidases and dipeptidases are fixed in the epithelial membrane, so the monosaccharides and amino acids they release are produced right beside the transport proteins that absorb them.

**Do I need to know the liver and gall bladder for 9610?**
No. The specification limits the glands to the salivary glands and pancreas. You do need the role of bile salts in lipid digestion and absorption.
