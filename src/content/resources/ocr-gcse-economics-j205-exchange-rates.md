## What section 4.3 asks you to do

Each specification statement matches a command word, so learn the skill as well as the content.

| Specification statement | Command word | What the examiner wants |
|---|---|---|
| How exchange rates are determined through the interaction of supply and demand | Draw and analyse | A labelled diagram, then a logical chain from cause to new rate |
| Currency conversion | Calculate | Correct method, working shown, units and currency symbols |
| Recent and historical exchange rate data | Analyse | Trends, highs and lows, sizes of change, possible reasons |
| The effect of changes in the exchange rate on consumers and producers | Evaluate | Both sides, then a judgement supported by what it depends on |

## The currency market in plain terms

An exchange rate is the price of one currency expressed in another. When the rate is quoted as £1 = €1.15, the pound is the thing being priced, and the euro figure is its price. Like any price in a free market, it is set where the quantity of pounds people want to buy equals the quantity people want to sell.

**Who buys pounds (demand)?** Anyone outside the UK who needs pounds: overseas buyers of UK goods and services (cars, insurance, university places), tourists visiting the UK, foreign savers and investors buying UK assets, and speculators who expect the pound to rise.

**Who sells pounds (supply)?** Anyone in the UK who needs foreign currency: buyers of imports, residents holidaying abroad, savers and firms investing overseas, and speculators who expect the pound to fall.

**Appreciation** is a rise in a currency's value, so £1 buys more foreign currency. **Depreciation** is a fall, so £1 buys less.

## Drawing and analysing the diagram

Build the diagram in this order.

1. Vertical axis: the exchange rate, written as the price of the currency, for example "€ per £".
2. Horizontal axis: "quantity of pounds traded".
3. Demand for pounds (D) slopes down: a lower rate makes UK goods cheaper abroad, so more pounds are wanted.
4. Supply of pounds (S) slopes up: a higher rate makes foreign goods cheaper for UK buyers, so they offer more pounds.
5. Mark the equilibrium rate and quantity where the curves cross.
6. Draw the shift, label the new curve (D1 or S1), mark the new rate and say "appreciates" or "depreciates".

### Worked example 1: when both curves move

Interest rates are a common exam trigger because they shift both curves at once. The table shows the market for pounds in Country A (illustrative, quantities in millions of pounds a day).

| Rate (€ per £) | 1.05 | 1.10 | 1.15 | 1.20 | 1.25 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Demand before | 90 | 80 | 70 | 60 | 50 |
| Supply before | 50 | 60 | 70 | 80 | 90 |
| Demand after | 110 | 100 | 90 | 80 | 70 |
| Supply after | 30 | 40 | 50 | 60 | 70 |

The central bank raises interest rates. Saving in pounds now earns more, so foreign savers buy pounds (demand rises by 20 million at every rate) and UK savers keep more money at home (supply falls by 20 million at every rate).

- Before: equilibrium at €1.15 and 70 million pounds.
- After: equilibrium at €1.25 and 70 million pounds.
- The pound appreciates by (1.25 − 1.15) ÷ 1.15 × 100 = **8.7%**.

The quantity traded is unchanged because the curves moved by equal amounts in opposite directions, but the rate moved further than with one shift. Say which curve moves, which way and why, before stating the new rate.

## Calculating currency conversion

The rule is simple: multiply to go from pounds into the other currency, divide to go back into pounds. Two harder versions appear in questions.

### Worked example 2: buying and selling rates

A bureau sells euros to travellers at £1 = €1.16 and buys them back at £1 = €1.24. A traveller changes £500 before a trip, spends €430 and changes the rest back.

- Euros bought: 500 × 1.16 = **€580**.
- Euros left: 580 − 430 = €150.
- Pounds back: 150 ÷ 1.24 = **£120.97**.

The bureau profits from the gap between its two rates, so the traveller loses on both conversions.

### Worked example 3: the same change, two percentages

The rate moves from £1 = $1.30 to £1 = $1.17.

- Change in the value of the pound: (1.17 − 1.30) ÷ 1.30 × 100 = **−10.0%**.
- Value of $1 in pounds: before 1 ÷ 1.30 = £0.769, after 1 ÷ 1.17 = £0.855.
- Change in the value of the dollar: (0.855 − 0.769) ÷ 0.769 × 100 = **+11.1%**.

A 10% depreciation of the pound is an 11.1% appreciation of the dollar against it. The percentages differ because the base is different. Always state which currency you are describing.

## Analysing exchange rate data

The specification asks for recent and historical data, so questions may show a table or line graph over months or years. A complete analysis does four things: describe the overall trend, pick out the highest and lowest points, calculate a change, and suggest a reason while noting that the data alone does not prove it.

### Worked example 4: one pound, two currencies

Country A's currency against the euro and the dollar (illustrative quarterly averages):

| Quarter | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| € per £ | 1.15 | 1.17 | 1.14 | 1.12 | 1.13 | 1.18 |
| $ per £ | 1.30 | 1.27 | 1.24 | 1.22 | 1.20 | 1.21 |

- Against the euro the pound fluctuated, reaching a low of 1.12 in Quarter 4 (2.6% below Quarter 1), then ended 2.6% higher than it started.
- Against the dollar it fell in every quarter to Quarter 5, then rose slightly; overall it fell by (1.21 − 1.30) ÷ 1.30 = **6.9%**.
- So a Country A firm exporting to the USA became more price competitive there, while one exporting to the euro area became slightly less so by Quarter 6.

So always name the currency the pound is measured against, and the period.

Historical events can illustrate a point: the pound fell sharply after the UK's EU referendum result in June 2016. In the exam, though, work from the data given.

## Effects on consumers and producers

The core chain for a depreciation is: each pound buys less foreign currency, so UK exports become cheaper abroad and imports become dearer in the UK. An appreciation reverses each link. The mnemonic **SPICED** (Strong Pound, Imports Cheap, Exports Dear) helps you avoid getting the direction wrong.

| Group | Depreciation of the pound | Appreciation of the pound |
|---|---|---|
| Exporters | Lower foreign price, sales may rise | Higher foreign price, sales may fall |
| Firms using imported inputs | Costs in pounds rise | Costs in pounds fall |
| UK tourism businesses | More overseas visitors | Fewer overseas visitors |
| Consumers buying imports | Higher prices, lower real income | Lower prices, more choice |
| UK residents holidaying abroad | Holidays cost more | Holidays cost less |
| Workers in export industries | Jobs more secure | Jobs at risk |

### Worked example 5: an exporter with imported parts

Harrowfield Bikes (invented) sells 2,000 bikes a year in the euro area at £400 each. Each bike uses components costing €150 and £180 of other UK costs. The pound depreciates from €1.20 to €1.08 (a 10% fall).

| | At €1.20 | At €1.08 |
|---|---|---|
| Euro price of a £400 bike | €480 | €432 |
| Component cost in pounds | 150 ÷ 1.20 = £125.00 | 150 ÷ 1.08 = £138.89 |
| Profit per bike | 400 − 125 − 180 = £95.00 | 400 − 138.89 − 180 = £81.11 |

- If the firm lets the euro price fall to €432 and sales rise 15% to 2,300, annual profit goes from 2,000 × 95 = £190,000 to 2,300 × 81.11 = about **£186,556**. Profit barely changes, because the components, over 40% of each bike's total cost, are now dearer.
- If instead it keeps the euro price at €480, it receives 480 ÷ 1.08 = £444.44 per bike. Profit per bike is 444.44 − 138.89 − 180 = £125.56, and on 2,000 bikes that is about **£251,111**.

So a depreciation gives the firm a choice: cut the foreign price to win sales, or hold it and widen the margin. Which pays more depends on price elasticity and the share of imported inputs.

For consumers the same change bites: a €3,000 holiday rises from £2,500 to £2,777.78.

## Building an evaluation

For an "evaluate" question, use at least two of these "it depends" factors, applied to the case:

- **Price elasticity of demand**: sales change a lot only if demand is elastic.
- **Import content**: firms using imported materials lose part of the gain from depreciation.
- **Size and duration**: a brief movement may be ignored, and contracts already signed delay the effect.
- **Pricing choice**: firms may hold foreign prices and take a wider margin instead.
- **Rivals' currencies**: if competitors' currencies fell too, there is no advantage.
- **Inflation**: dearer imports can raise prices generally and erode the competitive gain over time.

End with a judgement that names the group, for example: "For an exporter with elastic demand and few imported inputs the depreciation is mainly beneficial, but for consumers who buy many imported goods it is mainly harmful."

## Common mistakes

- Dividing when you should multiply, or giving the answer in the wrong currency.
- Assuming a 10% fall in one currency is a 10% rise in the other.
- Shifting the wrong curve: UK spending on imports shifts the supply of pounds.
- Calling depreciation "good for the economy" without naming the group or the condition.
