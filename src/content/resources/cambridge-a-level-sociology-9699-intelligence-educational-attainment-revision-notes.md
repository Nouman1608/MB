These notes condense section 6.1, *Intelligence and educational attainment*, of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699), assessed on Paper 3 – Education. Three syllabus strands: the difficulties in defining intelligence; IQ tests and how far social factors influence them; and intelligence as an influence on educational attainment.

Full explanations: [intelligence and educational attainment study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-intelligence-educational-attainment/).

## Core argument

Intelligence is hard to define, so tests measure a chosen version of it. Scores are shaped by culture, language, practice, the testing situation and the environment. Measured intelligence predicts attainment, but this does not show that innate ability *causes* the gaps between classes, ethnic groups or genders.

## Defining intelligence: four problems

| Problem | Point | Name to use |
|---|---|---|
| One or many? | General factor versus several intelligences | Spearman (g); Gardner; Sternberg |
| Circularity | Intelligence becomes "what the test measures" | Gould (reification) |
| Cultural variation | Societies value different kinds of intelligent behaviour | Cross-cultural comparison |
| Expanding definitions | Emotional intelligence and others widen the concept | Goleman (1995) |

**So what?** Whoever defines intelligence decides who is labelled able — a narrow definition becomes unequal opportunity.

## Social influences on IQ scores

- **Cultural bias** in items; **language** of the test; **test-wiseness** through practice.
- **Flynn effect:** scores rose too fast for genetic change.
- **Stereotype threat:** Steele and Aronson (1995) — Black American students scored lower on a difficult verbal test when told it measured ability.
- **Heritability depends on environment:** Turkheimer et al. (2003) — among poorer families, environment explained most IQ variation; among affluent families, genes explained more.
- **Flawed evidence:** Kamin (1974) on Burt's twin data.

## Heritability: get it right

- Heritability = share of variation **within one population** linked to genetic differences.
- It changes with the environment; it is not a fixed property of a trait.
- **Lewontin (1970) seed analogy:** within each field differences may be genetic, but the difference between a rich and a poor field is wholly environmental. Within-group heritability says nothing about between-group gaps.

## How schools construct ability

| Idea | Source | Point |
|---|---|---|
| New IQism | Gillborn and Youdell (2000) | Belief in fixed potential drives setting and tiering |
| Ideology of giftedness | Bourdieu and Passeron, *Les Héritiers* (1964) | Cultural advantage credited as natural gift |
| Learning without Limits | Hart et al. (2004) | Teachers rejecting fixed ability show labels are a choice |

## Does intelligence explain attainment?

**For:** IQ correlates with school results; hereditarians (Jensen, Herrnstein and Murray); functionalist meritocracy.

**Against:**
- correlation is not causation — home advantage raises both scores and results;
- Lewontin: individual heritability cannot explain group gaps;
- gender gaps have reversed within a generation in many countries — too fast for genes;
- selection labels shape later attainment;
- Bowles and Gintis: IQ explains little of the link between background and income.

**Judgement:** intelligence may explain some differences between individuals in the same group, but it is a weak explanation of differences between groups.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Intelligence | General capacity to learn, reason and solve problems; definitions are contested |
| IQ | Score on a standardised test, scaled so the population average is 100 |
| g | Proposed general factor underlying performance on many tests |
| Multiple intelligences | Gardner's theory of several distinct abilities |
| Emotional intelligence | Ability to recognise and manage emotions (Goleman, 1995) |
| Reification | Treating an abstract idea as a real thing |
| Heritability | Proportion of variation within a population linked to genetic differences |
| Stereotype threat | Lower performance caused by awareness of a negative group stereotype |
| Flynn effect | Rise in average IQ scores over the 20th century |
| Hereditarian view | Attainment differences mainly reflect inherited ability |
| New IQism | Belief in fixed, measurable pupil potential |
| Ideology of giftedness | Treating cultural advantage as natural talent |

## Exam traps

- Discussing brain function instead of the social uses of intelligence.
- Claiming heritability is fixed or applies to differences between groups.
- Writing "IQ tests are biased" without explaining a mechanism.
- Treating hereditarian claims as settled; present them as contested.
- Ignoring the third strand: how far intelligence *influences attainment*.

## Self-test

1. Why is defining intelligence circular?
2. Give one example of cultural variation in definitions of intelligence.
3. What is stereotype threat, and who demonstrated it?
4. Define heritability accurately.
5. What did Turkheimer and colleagues find?
6. Summarise Lewontin's seed analogy.
7. What is "new IQism"?
8. What do Bourdieu and Passeron mean by the ideology of giftedness?
9. Why does the reversal of gender gaps challenge hereditarian views?
10. Give one reason a correlation between IQ and exam results does not show causation.
11. What did Hart and colleagues argue about ability labels?

### Answers

1. Without an agreed definition, intelligence becomes "whatever the test measures", so the test defines the concept it claims to measure.
2. Some societies stress speed and abstract reasoning; others value practical wisdom, social responsibility or memory for oral tradition.
3. Lower performance caused by awareness of a negative stereotype about one's group; Steele and Aronson (1995).
4. The share of variation in a trait within a particular population that is linked to genetic differences.
5. Environment explained most IQ variation among poorer families; genes explained more among affluent families.
6. Differences within each field may be genetic, but the difference between a rich and a poor field is environmental, so within-group heritability cannot explain between-group gaps.
7. Gillborn and Youdell's term for the belief that pupils have a fixed, measurable potential, which drives setting and tiering.
8. Schools treat the cultural advantages of privileged pupils as natural talent, so class reproduction looks like nature.
9. The gaps changed within a generation, far too fast for genetic change, so social causes must be at work.
10. Home advantages may raise both IQ scores and exam results, and tests and exams reward overlapping learned skills.
11. Ability labels are a choice schools make; teaching that assumes every pupil can improve challenges fixed-ability thinking.
