This guide goes deeper into section **4.5.1 Homeostasis** of the AQA GCSE Biology (8461) specification, for
first teaching from 2016. Section 4.5.1 is short, but it sets up the language used in the whole of Topic 5:
receptors, coordination centres and effectors. Homeostasis and response is assessed on Paper 2. No statement in
4.5.1 is marked Higher tier only.

## The definition, piece by piece

The specification defines homeostasis as **the regulation of the internal conditions of a cell or organism to
maintain optimum conditions for function in response to internal and external changes**. Each phrase earns its
place:

| Phrase | What it means |
|---|---|
| Regulation | Conditions are actively controlled, not left to chance |
| Internal conditions | Conditions inside the body or cell, such as blood glucose concentration, not the weather |
| Of a cell or organism | Homeostasis works at the level of single cells as well as the whole body |
| Optimum conditions for function | The values at which enzymes and cells work best |
| Internal and external changes | Changes can start inside the body (such as exercise warming the muscles) or outside (such as a cold day) |

A full-mark definition in an exam needs **regulation**, **internal conditions** and **optimum conditions**.
"Keeping the body the same" is too vague to score.

## Why optimum conditions matter

Homeostasis **maintains optimal conditions for enzyme action and all cell functions**. Two ideas from earlier
topics explain why.

- **Enzymes** (4.2.2.1) work fastest at an optimum temperature. Too cold and reactions slow; too hot and the
  enzymes denature, so the active site no longer fits the substrate.
- **Osmosis** (4.1.3.2) moves water across cell membranes. If body fluids become too concentrated or too dilute,
  cells lose or gain too much water by osmosis and do not function efficiently.

So the three conditions the specification names are each linked to enzymes or osmosis:

| Condition controlled | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Blood glucose concentration | Cells need a steady supply of glucose for respiration; too much or too little glucose in the blood affects water movement by osmosis |
| Body temperature | Enzymes need to be close to their optimum temperature |
| Water levels | Cells must not lose or gain too much water by osmosis |

## Automatic control systems

The specification says these **automatic control systems** may involve **nervous responses** or **chemical
responses**. "Automatic" means you do not decide consciously to control your blood glucose; it happens without
thought. "Chemical" responses use **hormones**, which you meet in 4.5.3.

| | Nervous response | Chemical (hormonal) response |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | Electrical impulses along neurones | Hormones carried in the blood |
| Speed | Very fast | Slower |
| Duration | Short-lived | Lasts longer |
| Example in homeostasis | Skin temperature receptors sending impulses to the brain | Insulin from the pancreas controlling blood glucose |

## The three parts of every control system

All control systems include:

1. **Receptors**: cells that **detect stimuli**, which are changes in the environment (inside or outside the body).
2. **Coordination centres**, such as the **brain, spinal cord and pancreas**, which **receive and process
   information** from receptors.
3. **Effectors**, which are **muscles or glands**, and which **bring about responses which restore optimum
   levels**.

The word **restore** is the key to the whole section. A control system does not just respond; it responds in the
direction that brings the condition **back** towards its optimum. When the condition is too high, the response
lowers it; when it is too low, the response raises it. In Higher tier content later in the topic (4.5.3), this is
called **negative feedback**.

## Applying the model

The skill examiners test is placing the parts of a real system into the model.

**Worked example 1: a cold day** (body temperature is Biology only content, 4.5.2.4)

| Part | In this system |
|---|---|
| Stimulus | Fall in temperature of the blood and the skin |
| Receptors | Temperature receptors in the skin and in the thermoregulatory centre |
| Coordination centre | Thermoregulatory centre in the brain |
| Effectors | Blood vessels in the skin (constrict); skeletal muscles (shiver); sweat glands (stop producing sweat) |
| Response | Less energy transferred from the skin; more energy released by muscle contraction |
| Result | Body temperature rises back towards the optimum |

**Worked example 2: blood glucose after a meal** (4.5.3.2)

| Part | In this system |
|---|---|
| Stimulus | Rise in blood glucose concentration |
| Coordination centre | Pancreas, which monitors blood glucose and releases insulin |
| Effectors | Liver and muscle cells, which respond to insulin |
| Response | Glucose moves from the blood into cells; excess is converted to glycogen in liver and muscle cells |
| Result | Blood glucose concentration falls back towards normal |

Notice that the pancreas both detects the change and responds by secreting a hormone. The specification lists
it as a coordination centre.

**Annotated model answer**

*Question: Explain why the body must control its internal temperature. [3]*

> Reactions in cells are controlled by enzymes [1]. Enzymes work best at an optimum temperature [1]. If the
> temperature rises too high, the enzymes denature and reactions slow down or stop, so cells cannot function
> properly [1].

Each mark builds a chain: enzymes, optimum, consequence. An answer that says only "so that we do not get too
hot" scores nothing, because it gives no biological reason.

## Changes that disturb homeostasis

Examiners like to give you a scenario and ask which condition changes.

| Scenario | Condition disturbed | Internal or external change? |
|---|---|---|
| Running a race | Body temperature rises from respiration in muscles; water lost in sweat | Internal |
| Sitting in a hot room | Body temperature; water lost in sweat | External |
| Eating a sugary meal | Blood glucose rises | Internal (after digestion and absorption) |
| Drinking a large volume of water | Water level rises; blood more dilute | Internal |
| Missing a meal | Blood glucose falls | Internal |

## Common mistakes

- Defining homeostasis as "keeping the body at the same temperature". Temperature is only one of the conditions,
  and the key word is **regulation**.
- Saying receptors "respond" to a change. Receptors **detect**; effectors **respond**.
- Naming an organ such as the skin as an effector. The effectors are the **muscles or glands**, such as sweat
  glands.
- Forgetting that responses **restore** the optimum, so they act against the change.
- Describing homeostasis as only nervous or only hormonal. It may involve either.

## Exam technique for AQA command words

- **Define** or **State what is meant by**: use the specification's key words.
- **Explain** why a condition is controlled: link it to enzymes or to osmosis.
- **Identify** the parts of a control system: give the receptor, coordination centre and effector by name.
- **Describe** a response: say what the effector does and in which direction the condition changes.
