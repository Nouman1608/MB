## Questions

**1.** Define the term *unique selling point (USP)*. **[2]**

**2.** Identify the type of market segmentation (gender, age, location or income) each business is using. **[3]**
- A bank offers a free account to customers under 18.
- A clothing chain stocks heavy coats only in its northern shops.
- A watchmaker makes a gold-plated range for wealthy customers.

**3.** Dalewood Tea Co. surveyed 320 customers about their favourite type of tea: green 96, black 128, herbal 64, fruit 32.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage who prefer black tea. **[1]**
**(b)** State whether this is primary or secondary, and quantitative or qualitative, research. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain one reason why the results might be inaccurate. **[2]**
**(d)** Analyse how Dalewood could use the findings to decide which teas to promote. **[3]**

**4.** Explain one benefit and one drawback to a new business of using published government statistics about household income. **[4]**

**5.** Calderon Cycles makes 500 touring bicycles a month. Variable cost is 120 per bicycle and fixed costs are 30,000 a month.

**(a)** Calculate the selling price using cost-plus pricing with a 30% mark-up on average cost. **[4]**
**(b)** Calculate the monthly profit if all 500 are sold at this price. **[3]**

**6.** Dalewood Tea Co. is launching a rare, high-quality tea in a country where no rival sells anything similar. Its target market is wealthy adults. Evaluate whether it should use skimming or penetration pricing. **[6]**

**7.** A supermarket sells kettles that cost it 15 each at a price of 12 as a loss-leader. It sells 800.

**(a)** Calculate the total loss on the kettles. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how the supermarket could still gain from this strategy. **[4]**

**8.** Calderon Cycles' e-bike sold these units in each year since launch: year 1: 25; year 2: 140; year 3: 390; year 4: 560; year 5: 555; year 6: 420; year 7: 210.

**(a)** State the product life cycle stage in year 1, year 3 and year 7. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage fall in sales from year 6 to year 7. **[2]**
**(c)** Calderon is considering a cheaper student version as an extension strategy. Evaluate whether this is suitable. **[5]**

**9.** **(a)** Explain one benefit and one risk of Dalewood Tea Co. developing a new product rather than adapting an existing one. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain why a business might broaden and balance its product portfolio. **[3]**

**10.** Dalewood has 20,000 to launch a fruit tea aimed at 18 to 25 year olds, who mostly use social media. A rival already sells similar tea in supermarkets.

**(a)** It plans to spend 50% on influencers, 30% on in-store samples and 20% on posters. Calculate the amount for each. **[2]**
**(b)** Analyse two factors that should influence the choice of promotional mix. **[6]**

**11.** A producer sells a case of jam to a wholesaler for 20. The wholesaler sells it to a retailer for 26, and the retailer sells it to the public for 40.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage mark-up of the wholesaler and of the retailer, to one decimal place where needed. **[3]**
**(b)** Evaluate whether the producer should keep using the wholesaler or sell direct to the public. **[6]**

**12.** Calderon Cycles receives 1,800 online orders a month. 62% come through mobile phones.

**(a)** Calculate the number of mobile orders and computer orders. **[2]**
**(b)** Identify two drawbacks of selling by e-commerce. **[2]**
**(c)** Calderon's touring bicycle is now in maturity and rivals sell it for less. Justify one change to the marketing mix. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** A feature or benefit that makes a product different from rivals' products [1] and gives customers a reason to choose it [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition earns credit for the idea of difference from competitors; a vague "something special" is weaker.

**2.** Age [1]. Location [1]. Income [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Each answer needs one of the named types; naming the product alone does not show the type.

**3. (a)** 128 ÷ 320 × 100 = **40%** [1].
**(b)** Primary [1] and quantitative [1].
**(c)** Any one developed: the sample may not represent all tea drinkers, for example only existing customers were asked [1], so the preferences may not match the whole market and Dalewood could stock the wrong teas [1].
**(d)** Black is the largest group at 40% and fruit the smallest at 10% [1]. Dalewood could focus promotion and stock on black and green, which together are 70% (224 of 320) [1]. The survey does not say why people choose, so a follow-up interview would help before dropping fruit tea [1].
*Examiner insight:* Analysis questions gain credit when a number from the data is used and then linked to a decision; restating the table gains little.

**4.** Benefit: it is cheap or free and quick to obtain [1], so a new business with a small budget can see income levels without paying for surveys [1]. Drawback: the data may be out of date or not specific to the business's customers [1], so it could lead to wrong pricing or targeting decisions [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each point needs a developed consequence for the business; a bare "cheap" is one idea.

**5. (a)** Fixed cost per bicycle = 30,000 ÷ 500 = 60 [1]. Average cost = 120 + 60 = 180 [1]. Mark-up = 180 × 0.30 = 54 [1]. Price = **234** [1].
**(b)** Revenue = 500 × 234 = 117,000 [1]. Total cost = 500 × 120 + 30,000 = 90,000 [1]. Profit = **27,000** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Showing each stage of working lets partial credit be given if one figure goes wrong; a bare final figure risks losing it all.

**6.** For skimming: the product is unique with no rivals [1], and wealthy buyers can pay a high price, which earns a high profit per unit [1]. A high price also fits a rare, high-quality image [1]. Against: rivals may enter once they see high profits [1], and a high price limits the number of buyers. Penetration would win share quickly but earns low profit and may harm the premium image [1]. Judgement: skimming is more suitable because there is no competition and the target market is wealthy, though Dalewood should plan to lower the price when rivals appear [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation questions are rewarded for a reasoned conclusion that refers to the case; a list of pros and cons with no decision stays low.

**7. (a)** Loss per kettle = 15 − 12 = 3 [1]. Total loss = 3 × 800 = **2,400** [1].
**(b)** The low price attracts shoppers into the store [1]. They buy other goods at normal prices [1]. If the profit on those extra sales exceeds 2,400 [1], the strategy pays, and it may also build a reputation for low prices and repeat visits [1].
*Examiner insight:* A strong answer states the condition under which the loss is recovered rather than just saying "it brings in customers".

**8. (a)** Year 1: introduction [1]. Year 3: growth [1]. Year 7: decline [1].
**(b)** (420 − 210) ÷ 420 × 100 [1] = **50%** [1].
**(c)** For: a cheaper version opens a new segment of students [1], and it uses existing design and production, so costs are lower than a new product [1]. Against: it might take sales from the full-price model [1], and students may have little income to spend, with falling sales suggesting the whole market may be shrinking [1]. Judgement: suitable if research shows student demand, because sales are in decline and a new segment could prolong the product's life [1].
*Examiner insight:* Answers are stronger when they link the strategy to the stage in the cycle, as the specification asks for effectiveness and timing.

**9. (a)** Benefit: a new product can open new sales and reach new customers [1], giving the firm an edge over rivals [1]. Risk: development is costly [1] and the product may fail, wasting the investment [1].
**(b)** Broadening spreads risk, as a problem with one product does not harm the whole business [1], and it reaches more segments [1]. Balancing means older products earn cash to fund new ones [1].
*Examiner insight:* Part (a) asks for a benefit and a risk, so two benefits or two risks cannot earn full credit.

**10. (a)** Influencers = 20,000 × 0.50 = **10,000** [1]; samples = **6,000**; posters = **4,000** [1] for all three.
**(b)** Target market: the audience is 18 to 25 and uses social media [1], so influencers reach them directly [1], while posters may miss many of them [1]. Competitors: a rival already sells in supermarkets [1], so in-store samples could win customers at the shelf [1] and help differentiate the product from the rival's tea [1]. (Budget is also acceptable: 20,000 is too small for TV.)
*Examiner insight:* Two factors are asked for, so each should be named and applied; marks are lost when factors are named without reference to the case.

**11. (a)** Wholesaler: (26 − 20) ÷ 20 × 100 = **30%** [1]. Retailer: (40 − 26) ÷ 26 × 100 [1] = **53.8%** [1].
**(b)** Keep wholesaler: it buys in bulk, stores and delivers to many retailers [1], so the producer avoids storage and selling costs [1]. Direct: the producer could keep more of the 40 retail price, or lower the price to customers [1], but it would need delivery, staff and marketing, and may reach fewer outlets [1]. The wholesaler's 6 mark-up is small against these costs [1]. Judgement: keep the wholesaler unless direct sales could be run cheaply, for example online [1].
*Examiner insight:* A recommendation is only credited when it draws on the numbers or the scenario; unsupported opinions score low.

**12. (a)** Mobile = 1,800 × 0.62 = **1,116** [1]. Computer = 1,800 − 1,116 = **684** [1].
**(b)** Any two: delivery costs and delays; security or trust worries; strong competition; customers cannot touch the product [1] [1].
**(c)** Change: lower the price, a competitive or cost-plus adjustment [1]. Rivals sell for less and maturity means sales are flat [1], so a lower price could protect share [1]. It fits a product with a known brand and design that no longer attracts new buyers by novelty [1]. Against: lower margin per bicycle [1], and rivals could cut again [1]. It must be matched by promotion that shows the bicycle's quality, so that the brand image is not harmed [1]. Judgement: justified if volume rises enough to cover the lower margin; otherwise an extension strategy such as an upgraded model would be better [1].
*Examiner insight:* Justify means choosing one change and defending it; the weakest answers list changes to all four Ps.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Stating a method such as skimming without applying it to the business.
- Leaving out units or the working in calculations.
- Writing "cheap" as the only reason for secondary research.
- Not using figures from the stem in analysis or evaluation.
- Mixing up mark-up with profit margin.
- Reading a flat sales year as decline.
- Evaluating without a final judgement.
- Describing e-commerce in general terms only.
