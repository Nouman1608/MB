## Section A

**1.** Explain the difference between behaviourist and nativist explanations of language acquisition. **[4]**

**2.** Explain what the critical period hypothesis proposes, and give **one** reason why evidence for it is hard to obtain. **[3]**

**3.** Distinguish linguistic determinism from linguistic relativity, giving an example or study for each. **[4]**

**4.** Define idiolect, dialect and sociolect. **[3]**

**5.** Explain overt and covert prestige, naming **one** study for each. **[4]**

## Section B

Read the passage below (original, written for this page) and answer Questions 6 to 9.

> *My grandmother's language has no separate word for "cousin". Everyone of my generation is simply "brother" or
> "sister". When I explain to friends at school that I have forty siblings, they laugh. But I do not think of my
> cousins as less close than my brothers; I am not sure I could, even if I tried. At school I speak differently
> again. My friends and I use words our teachers pretend not to understand, and when a new student uses them wrongly,
> everyone notices. I suppose I have one self for my family and another for my friends, and each has its own
> vocabulary.*

**6.** Discuss how far the writer's experience of kinship terms supports the idea that language shapes thought. **[8]**

**7.** Analyse how the passage shows language being used for inclusion and exclusion. **[5]**

**8.** Using the passage, explain how a speaker can belong to more than one speech community. **[4]**

**9.** Write a plan for an essay discussing the most important issues the passage raises about **language and identity**: an introduction sentence, three paragraph points each with a concept or study, and a conclusion. **[10]**

**10.** Evaluate Fodor's language of thought hypothesis as a challenge to linguistic determinism. **[5]**

**11.** Explain what Labov's Martha's Vineyard study suggests about accent and identity. **[3]**

**12.** "There is no such thing as a genderlect." Discuss, referring to **two** researchers. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** **Behaviourism** (Skinner) explains language as learned behaviour [1], acquired through **imitation and reinforcement** from the environment [1]. **Nativism** (Chomsky) holds that humans have an **innate capacity** for language [1], because children acquire complex grammar from limited, imperfect input (**poverty of the stimulus**) [1].

**2.** It proposes that there is a **window, usually up to puberty, when language is acquired most readily** [1], after which full acquisition, especially of grammar, becomes difficult [1]. Evidence is hard to obtain because **it would be unethical to deprive children of language**, so researchers rely on rare cases like Genie in which other factors, such as abuse, complicate conclusions [1].

**3.** **Determinism**: language **determines** thought, so we cannot think what our language cannot express [1]; e.g. Whorf's claims about Hopi time, or Orwell's Newspeak as a fictional illustration [1]. **Relativity**: language **influences** habitual thought and perception [1]; e.g. Kay and Kempton's (1984) blue-green colour study [1].

**4.** **Idiolect**: an individual's unique pattern of language use [1]. **Dialect**: a regional variety with distinctive lexis and grammar [1]. **Sociolect**: a variety linked to a social group, such as class, age or occupation [1].

**5.** **Overt prestige** is status attached to standard forms and widely recognised in society [1], e.g. Labov's New York department store study (1966) [1]. **Covert prestige** is status gained within a group from non-standard forms [1], e.g. Trudgill's Norwich study (1974) [1].

**6.** Support: the writer **does not think of cousins as less close** and is "not sure I could", which suggests the absence of a separate term shapes perception of kinship [1] [1], consistent with **linguistic relativity** [1]. The friends' laughter shows **English speakers categorise relatives differently** [1]. Challenge: the writer **understands the English distinction** perfectly well and explains it, so the language does not **determine** what can be thought [1] [1]. The closeness may also reflect **cultural practice** that the vocabulary expresses rather than causes [1]. Judgement: the passage supports a **weak relativist view, not determinism** [1].

**7.** **Shared in-group vocabulary** ("words our teachers pretend not to understand") marks students as insiders [1] [1] and adults as outsiders [1]. **Exclusion** through misuse: "when a new student uses them wrongly, everyone notices", showing competence in the group's lexis is a test of membership [1] [1].

**8.** A speech community is a group sharing norms of language use [1]. The writer belongs to a **family community** with kinship terms from the grandmother's language [1] and a **peer community** with its own slang [1]; moving between them shows identities are **performed differently in each community** [1].

**9.** **Introduction**: identifies the aspect and a line of argument, e.g. "The passage presents identity as plural, shaped by both inherited and peer language" [1] [1]. **Point 1**: kinship terms and thought, with **linguistic relativity / Kay and Kempton** and evaluation against determinism [1] [1]. **Point 2**: peer slang and inclusion/exclusion, with **covert prestige or speech communities** [1] [1]. **Point 3**: multiple selves and vocabularies, with **accommodation (Giles)** or Labov's identity work [1] [1]. **Conclusion**: a judgement, e.g. language expresses and helps build each identity without fully determining it [1] [1].

**10.** Fodor proposes that thought takes place in **mentalese**, an internal code distinct from any spoken language [1]. This challenges determinism because, if thought is prior to language, **our spoken language cannot set the limits of thought** [1] [1]. Evaluation: it fits evidence that **pre-linguistic infants and people without language** appear to think [1], but mentalese is **hard to test directly**, so it remains a hypothesis rather than proof [1].

**11.** Labov found that islanders who **identified strongly with Martha's Vineyard** used a distinctive local vowel pronunciation more [1] [1], suggesting accent can be an **act of identity**, signalling belonging, not just a product of where one is from [1].

**12.** **Lakoff (1975)** proposed features of "women's language", such as hedges and tag questions, framed as a **deficit** reflecting lower power [1] [1]. **Tannen (1990)** proposed **different but equal** styles, such as "rapport" and "report" talk [1] [1]. Against genderlect: **Cameron (2007)** argues differences are overstated and vary more by context and role than by gender [1] [1]. Judgement: patterns may exist in particular contexts, but a **single gendered variety** is hard to defend; gender is one factor among many in language variation [1] [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating the passage's anecdote as proof of determinism.
- Citing Sapir-Whorf without distinguishing the strong and weak forms.
- Naming studies without explaining what they found.
- Plans with no evaluation or conclusion.
- Presenting genderlect research as fact rather than debate.
