This practice set covers syllabus section **1.2** of IB DP Mathematics: Analysis and Approaches (first
assessment 2021), common to SL and HL: arithmetic sequences and series, sigma notation, applications and
modelling data that is not perfectly arithmetic. Section A is calculator-free, like Paper 1; Section B
expects technology, like Paper 2. Give answers exactly or to 3 significant figures, and money to the
nearest penny.

## Section A

**1.** (calculator-free) An arithmetic sequence has u₁ = 40 and d = −6.

**(a)** Find u₁₅. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the first term that is negative, and state which term it is. **[2]**

**2.** (calculator-free) In an arithmetic sequence, u₃ = 14 and u₉ = 32. Find d, u₁ and S₁₀. **[4]**

**3.** (calculator-free) Evaluate the sum from k = 10 to k = 30 of (2k − 5). **[3]**

**4.** (calculator-free) Find the sum of all the even numbers from 50 to 150 inclusive. **[3]**

**5.** (calculator-free) The sum of the first n terms of a sequence is S_n = 5n − n².

**(a)** Find u₁. **[1]**
**(b)** Show that u_n = 6 − 2n. **[2]**
**(c)** State the common difference. **[1]**

**6.** (calculator-free) The expressions x, 2x + 3 and 5x − 1 are consecutive terms of an arithmetic
sequence. Find x and the common difference. **[3]**

**7.** (calculator-free) An arithmetic sequence has u₁ = 3 and d = 4. Given that S_n = 1275, find n. **[4]**

## Section B

**8.** (calculator allowed) Leila saves £50 in week 1 and increases the amount she saves by £3 each week.

**(a)** Find the amount she saves in week 30. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the total she has saved after 30 weeks. **[2]**
**(c)** Find the least number of weeks for her total savings to reach at least £5000. **[3]**

**9.** (calculator allowed) £3000 is invested at 3.2% simple interest per year.

**(a)** Find the balance after 8 years. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the number of complete years after which the balance first exceeds £4000. **[2]**

**10.** (calculator allowed) A plant's height is measured at the end of five consecutive weeks: 12.0 cm,
14.1 cm, 16.3 cm, 18.2 cm, 20.4 cm.

**(a)** Show that the heights do not form an exact arithmetic sequence. **[1]**
**(b)** Use the first and last measurements to find an approximate common difference. **[1]**
**(c)** Use your model to predict the height at the end of week 12. **[2]**
**(d)** Comment on the reliability of your prediction. **[1]**

**11.** (calculator allowed) Logs are stacked in rows. The bottom row has 30 logs and each row above has one
fewer log than the row below. The top row has 8 logs.

**(a)** Find the number of rows. **[1]**
**(b)** Find the total number of logs. **[2]**

**12.** (calculator allowed) Sequence A is 5, 9, 13, … and sequence B is 200, 194, 188, …

**(a)** Find the least value of n for which the nth term of A is greater than the nth term of B. **[3]**
**(b)** Find the value of n for which the sum of the first n terms of A equals the sum of the first n
terms of B. **[3]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** u₁₅ = 40 + 14(−6) [1] = **−44** [1]
**(b)** 40 − 6(n − 1) < 0 gives n − 1 > 6.67, so n = 8 [1]; u₈ = **−2**, the **8th term** [1]

**2.** u₉ − u₃ = 6d = 18, so **d = 3** [1]; u₁ = 14 − 2(3) = **8** [1]; S₁₀ = 5(16 + 9 × 3) [1] = **215** [1]

**3.** 21 terms [1]; first term 15, last term 55 [1]; sum = (21/2)(15 + 55) = **735** [1]
*Examiner insight:* 30 − 10 = 20 is the number of gaps, not terms; 20 terms gives 700 and loses two marks.

**4.** n = (150 − 50)/2 + 1 = 51 [1]; S = (51/2)(50 + 150) [1] = **5100** [1]

**5. (a)** u₁ = S₁ = 5 − 1 = **4** [1]
**(b)** u_n = S_n − S_{n−1} = 5n − n² − [5(n − 1) − (n − 1)²] [1] = 5n − n² − 5n + 5 + n² − 2n + 1 =
**6 − 2n** [1]
**(c)** **d = −2** [1]

**6.** 2(2x + 3) = x + (5x − 1) [1]; 4x + 6 = 6x − 1, so **x = 3.5** [1]; terms 3.5, 10, 16.5, so
**d = 6.5** [1]

**7.** S_n = (n/2)(6 + 4(n − 1)) = n(2n + 1) [1]; 2n² + n − 1275 = 0 [1];
n = (−1 ± √10 201)/4 = (−1 ± 101)/4 [1]; **n = 25** (reject the negative root) [1]
*Examiner insight:* The discriminant 1 + 8 × 1275 = 10 201 = 101² is a perfect square, which is the signal
that the question was designed for Paper 1.

**8. (a)** 50 + 29 × 3 [1] = **£137** [1]
**(b)** (30/2)(50 + 137) [1] = **£2805** [1]
**(c)** (n/2)(100 + 3(n − 1)) ≥ 5000 [1]; GDC gives n ≥ 43.79 [1]; **44 weeks** (S₄₃ = £4859,
S₄₄ = £5038) [1]

**9. (a)** Interest per year 0.032 × 3000 = £96 [1]; balance 3000 + 8 × 96 = **£3768** [1]
**(b)** 96n > 1000 gives n > 10.4 [1]; **11 years** [1]

**10. (a)** Differences are 2.1, 2.2, 1.9, 2.2, which are not all equal [1]
**(b)** d ≈ (20.4 − 12.0)/4 = **2.1 cm per week** [1]
**(c)** u₁₂ ≈ 12.0 + 11 × 2.1 [1] = **35.1 cm** [1]
**(d)** Week 12 is well beyond the data, so this is extrapolation; growth may slow, so the prediction is
unreliable [1]

**11. (a)** 30 − (n − 1) = 8, so **n = 23 rows** [1]
**(b)** (23/2)(30 + 8) [1] = **437 logs** [1]

**12. (a)** 5 + 4(n − 1) > 200 − 6(n − 1) [1]; 10(n − 1) > 195, so n > 20.5 [1]; **n = 21** [1]
**(b)** S_A = n(2n + 3) and S_B = n(203 − 3n) [1]; 2n + 3 = 203 − 3n (n ≠ 0) [1]; **n = 40** [1]

## Where marks are usually lost

- Counting terms in a range without the +1.
- Rounding a least-n answer down.
- Leaving a quadratic in n unsolved, or keeping its negative root.
- Giving a prediction from data without any comment on reliability.
- Using the GDC without writing u₁ and d first.

## Frequently asked questions

**When should I use (n/2)(u₁ + u_n) instead of the other sum formula?**
Whenever the last term is known or easy to find, such as in a sigma sum or a "sum of multiples" question.

**Can n ever be zero in these questions?**
No. n counts terms, so it is a positive integer; divide by n only after noting n ≠ 0.
