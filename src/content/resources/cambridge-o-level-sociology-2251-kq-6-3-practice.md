## Set A — Dealing with crime

**(a)** Define the terms:
- (i) *deterrence* **[2]**
- (ii) *probation* **[2]**

**(b)** Give **two** aims of punishment other than rehabilitation. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **three** limitations of financial penalties as a punishment. **[6]**

**(d)** Explain **three** ways crime prevention strategies may reduce crime. **[6]**

**(e)** Discuss the effectiveness of capital punishment. **[8]**

**(f)** Evaluate the view that community policing is more effective than military style policing. **[14]**

## Set B — Extra practice

**(g)** Explain **two** ways ostracism may be used to punish deviance. **[4]**

**(j)** Explain **two** limitations of stop and search. **[4]**

**(k)** Explain the difference between retribution and rehabilitation. **[4]**

## Answers — Set A

**(a)(i) [2]** Punishment intended to discourage crime through fear of the consequences (1), either
putting the offender off reoffending (individual) or putting others off by example (general) (1).

**(a)(ii) [2]** A sentence served in the community under the supervision of a probation officer (1),
with conditions the offender must keep, aimed at rehabilitation (1).

**(b) [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: protection of citizens; deterrence; retribution; incapacitation.

**(c) [6]** Three limitations, 2 marks each (point plus development):
- **Unequal impact**: the same fine is a heavy burden for the poor but trivial for the rich.
- **Weak deterrent** for wealthy offenders and corporations, which may treat fines as a business cost.
- **Unpaid fines**: offenders who cannot pay may be imprisoned, punishing poverty.
- **No rehabilitation**: a fine does nothing to address the causes of offending.
- **Effects on family**: the offender's family may suffer the financial loss.

**(d) [6]** Three ways, 2 marks each:
- **Situational prevention** (Clarke): locks, lighting and alarms reduce opportunities.
- **Defensible space** (Newman, 1972): design lets residents watch shared areas.
- **Surveillance**: CCTV increases the risk of being caught, deterring offenders.
- **Social prevention**: youth clubs, education and job schemes tackle causes of crime.
- **Neighbourhood schemes**: residents watching out for each other strengthen informal control.

**(e) [8]** Levels: 7–8 clear, developed, both sides with evaluation; 4–6 some development; 1–3
basic.
- *Arguments for effectiveness:* permanent incapacitation; retribution for the most serious crimes;
  some argue it deters; satisfies some victims' families.
- *Arguments against:* irreversible if the person is innocent; deterrent effect is disputed, since
  many murders are committed in anger or without thought of consequences; may be applied unequally to
  poorer or minority defendants; condemned by human rights organisations; abolished in many countries.
- *Evaluation:* effective at incapacitation and retribution, but doubtful at deterrence, and the risk
  of error weighs heavily; credit a judgement.

**(f) [14]** Levels: 11–14 balanced, developed, evaluated with a conclusion; 7–10 developed but
less balanced; 4–6 limited; 1–3 basic.

*For community policing:*
- Builds trust, so the public report crime and give information.
- Local knowledge helps prevent crime before it happens.
- Officers work with schools, youth groups and community leaders.
- Kinsey, Lea and Young (1986): losing public cooperation pushes police into a vicious circle of
  military-style policing.
- Avoids the tension caused by heavy stop and search (Scarman Report, 1981).

*For military style policing:*
- Can restore order quickly during riots or serious unrest.
- Better equipped to deal with organised and armed crime or terrorism.
- Visible force may deter some offending.

*Evaluation:* military style policing can alienate communities and reduce cooperation; community
policing may be slow against serious organised crime; many forces combine the two depending on the
situation.

*Conclusion:* community policing is more effective for everyday crime prevention, while military style
methods may be needed in emergencies. Credit a reasoned judgement.

## Answers — Set B

**(g) [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each: a community refuses to speak to or associate with an offender, as
in Amish shunning; exclusion from religious or social events; loss of business or employment within the
community; online exclusion or blocking by peers.

**(h) [4]** Two limitations, 2 marks each: no due process, so innocent people may be wrongly named, as
after the 2013 Boston Marathon bombing; punishment may be out of proportion to the offence; risk of
violence and harassment; may interfere with police investigations and fair trials.

**(i) [4]** Two strengths, 2 marks each: avoid labelling first-time or minor offenders with a criminal
record; quick and cheap compared with prosecution; give a warning that may deter future offending;
keep offenders out of prison, where they could learn criminal skills.

**(j) [4]** Two limitations, 2 marks each: may target minority or young people unfairly, damaging
trust; most searches find nothing; can provoke anger and unrest, as before the Brixton disturbances in
1981; inflates recorded crime among targeted groups.

**(k) [4]** Retribution is punishment the offender deserves, in proportion to the harm caused (1),
looking back at the crime (1). Rehabilitation aims to change the offender so they no longer offend (1),
looking forward to their behaviour after punishment (1).

**(l) [8]** Levels as for A(e).
- *Effective:* cheaper than prison; offenders keep jobs and family ties; curfews limit opportunities
  to offend at high-risk times; protect victims; avoid the criminal contacts of prison.
- *Limitations:* depend on effective monitoring; can be breached or removed; do not address causes of
  offending; may be seen as too lenient by victims and the public.
- *Evaluation:* effective at partial incapacitation and protection, less so at rehabilitation unless
  combined with support.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to know which countries use capital punishment?** It helps to give an example, but focus
on judging effectiveness against the aims of punishment.

**Can I use historical examples?** Yes, where they illustrate a named punishment such as exile.

**How do I link 6.3 to 6.2?** Policing strategies such as stop and search shape the crime statistics
explained in 6.2.
