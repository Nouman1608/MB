This guide goes deeper into sub-topic 4.4 of Option D, Geophysical hazards, in IB Diploma Programme Geography (first assessments 2019): future resilience and adaptation. It is examined in Paper 1 if your school teaches this option. The [4.4 revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-geography-geophysical-hazards-future-resilience-and-adaptation-revision-notes/) already list structural and non-structural measures, parametric insurance, the Sendai Framework and the Japan, Indian Ocean and Turkey-Syria cases. This page adds what those notes leave short: why future risk is rising even where hazards are not, how pre-event and post-event management connect over time, how to manage mass movement, and how to build an evaluative answer. Test yourself with the [4.4 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-dp-geography-ib-dp-geography-4-4-practice/); the [Geophysical hazards option study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-geophysical-hazards-study-guide/) gives the overview of all four sub-topics.

## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Explain how geophysical hazard risk may change in future, and why.
- Distinguish pre-event management (reducing risk before an event) from post-event management (response and recovery).
- Apply both to the three hazard types in the option: earthquakes, volcanoes and mass movement.
- Judge how far resilience can be increased, for whom, and at what cost.

## Why risk rises when hazards do not

Earthquake and eruption frequencies do not change on human timescales. Yet recorded disasters and losses have grown. The reason lies on the other side of the risk equation:

- **Exposure grows.** More people and more expensive assets now sit in hazard zones. Many of the fastest-growing cities lie on plate boundaries or volcanic slopes.
- **Densification.** Vertical growth puts more people in each building, so one collapse kills more.
- **Land-use change.** Informal settlement on steep, cut slopes, and deforestation above them, turns stable hillsides into landslide sources.
- **Interconnection.** Global supply chains mean damage in one place disrupts production elsewhere, so economic impacts spread beyond the hazard zone.
- **Better reporting.** Part of the apparent rise is simply that more events are recorded and costed than in the past.

Two trends run in opposite directions. **Economic losses** tend to be highest in high-income countries, because buildings and infrastructure are worth more. **Deaths** tend to be concentrated in low- and middle-income countries, where vulnerability is higher. An answer on future risk should mention both measures and say which one it is judging.

## Pre-event and post-event management as one timeline

Students often list measures without placing them in time. A clearer structure follows the hazard management cycle: mitigation and preparedness come before the event; response and recovery come after it; and lessons from recovery should feed back into the next round of mitigation.

| Stage | Purpose | Earthquake example | Volcano example | Mass movement example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mitigation (pre-event) | Reduce exposure or vulnerability | Zoning, codes, retrofitting | Exclusion zones, lahar channels | Slope drainage, retaining walls, terracing |
| Preparedness (pre-event) | Be ready to act | Drills, early warning | Monitoring, evacuation plans | Rainfall thresholds for alerts |
| Response (post-event) | Save lives | Search and rescue | Evacuation, shelters | Clearing debris, rescue |
| Recovery (post-event) | Restore and improve | Rebuilding to code | Resettlement | Re-engineering or abandoning slopes |

### The Park model

Park's disaster-response model (1991) plots quality of life against time. Quality of life falls sharply at impact, during **disruption**; then **relief** (hours to days) stops the decline; **rehabilitation** (weeks to months) restores basic services; and **reconstruction** (years) rebuilds. Use it to compare places:

- A steeper and deeper fall shows higher vulnerability or weaker preparedness.
- A faster return shows stronger resilience.
- Recovery to a **higher** level than before the event represents "building back better"; recovery to a lower level shows lasting damage.

The model is a generalisation. Real recoveries are uneven: wealthier households often recover faster than poorer ones in the same city, so a single curve hides inequality.

## Managing mass movement

Mass movement is the hazard students prepare least, yet it is often the easiest to manage, because many slopes fail in predictable conditions.

- **Drainage** lowers pore-water pressure, which is the most common trigger of slope failure after heavy rain.
- **Retaining walls, soil nails and rock netting** hold material in place or catch falling rock.
- **Regrading and terracing** reduce the slope angle.
- **Vegetation** binds soil with roots and intercepts rain, although it works slowly and cannot stop deep-seated failures.
- **Rainfall-threshold warnings** issue alerts when rain over a set period passes a level known to trigger slides.
- **Land-use control** keeps housing off the steepest or historically active slopes.

Hong Kong is a well-known example: after deadly landslides in 1972, the government set up a dedicated slope-safety office and a long-term programme of inspecting and upgrading man-made slopes, combined with public landslip warnings during heavy rain. Volcanic lahars sit between hazard types: they are mass movements of volcanic material mixed with water. At Nevado del Ruiz in Colombia in 1985, a lahar buried the town of Armero and killed over 20,000 people, even though the hazard had been mapped beforehand; the failure was in communication and decision-making, not science.

## Worked example: judging resilience over time

A source gives invented figures for two cities hit by earthquakes of similar magnitude.

| | City P | City Q |
|---|---|---|
| Share of buildings meeting codes | High | Low |
| Days until main hospital reopened | 3 | 40 |
| Years until housing stock rebuilt | 4 | 12 |
| Rebuilt housing on the same faulted land? | No, rezoned | Yes |

Reasoning, step by step:

1. **Impact stage.** City P's code compliance limits collapse, so its Park curve falls less steeply.
2. **Relief and rehabilitation.** Hospital reopening in 3 days against 40 shows P restores critical services far faster.
3. **Reconstruction.** P rebuilds in 4 years against 12, and moves housing off faulted ground, lowering future exposure.
4. **Judgement.** P has built back better; Q has recovered slowly and rebuilt its exposure, so its risk for the next event is roughly unchanged.

This structure turns a data table into an evaluation of resilience rather than a description of numbers.

## Technology: promise and limits

- **Monitoring** (seismometers, satellite measurement of ground deformation, gas sensors) improves volcanic forecasting. At Mount Pinatubo in the Philippines in 1991, scientists forecast the major eruption and tens of thousands of people were evacuated beforehand.
- **Earthquake early warning** gives seconds to tens of seconds of notice after a rupture starts. It cannot predict earthquakes; it detects them and races the slower surface waves. Its value depends on automatic actions, such as stopping trains and lifts.
- **GIS hazard mapping** combines hazard, population and building data to target retrofitting.

Each depends on governance: a forecast is only useful if people trust it and can act on it.

## Annotated model answer

*Evaluate post-event management as a way of increasing resilience to earthquakes.* **(sample paragraph)**

"Fast relief, such as search and rescue in the first 72 hours, saves lives that pre-event measures failed to protect **[strength tied to the Park model's relief stage]**. Reconstruction can also lower future risk if land is rezoned and buildings meet codes, as when cities relocate housing off ruptured ground **[shows post-event links back to mitigation]**. However, post-event action cannot prevent the initial collapse that causes most deaths, and rebuilding is slow and costly where governments lack funds **[limitation]**. Post-event management therefore increases resilience most when it is used to build back better, but it is a complement to pre-event mitigation, not a substitute **[judgement]**."

## Common mistakes

- Claiming hazards are becoming more frequent, when the change is mostly in exposure and vulnerability.
- Writing "early warning predicts earthquakes".
- Treating recovery as returning to normal, when normal may have been the source of vulnerability.
- Ignoring mass movement or treating it as identical to earthquakes.
- Using the Park model without saying what the shape of the curve shows.

## Exam technique

- **Evaluate** needs strengths and limitations weighed against criteria, then a judgement.
- **To what extent** needs a position stated early and qualified at the end.
- **Examine** needs the assumptions behind a claim brought out, such as whether technology helps everyone equally.
- **Suggest** needs realistic measures linked to the place in the source.
- Structure longer answers around time (before, during, after) or around hazard type, and keep located examples precise.
