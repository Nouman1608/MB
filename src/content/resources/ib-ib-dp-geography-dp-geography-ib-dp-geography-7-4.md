## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Explain what makes an urban system sustainable and resilient.
- Evaluate strategies for urban sustainability, including transport, housing, energy, water and waste.
- Consider future challenges facing cities and how they might be met.
- Judge how far strategies balance economic, social and environmental goals.

## Urban metabolism: linear and circular cities

The idea of **urban metabolism** treats a city like an organism that takes in resources and gives out wastes. Herbert Girardet contrasted two kinds:

- A **linear metabolism** takes in food, water, energy and materials from a wide hinterland, uses them once, and discharges waste, sewage and emissions. Its ecological footprint is far larger than its area.
- A **circular metabolism** reuses and recycles: wastewater is treated and reused, organic waste becomes compost or biogas, materials from demolished buildings are recycled, and energy comes increasingly from renewable sources.

The concept gives a test for any strategy: does it shorten the loop between input and output, or only treat the output?

## Limiting sprawl

Sprawl increases car dependence, land take and the cost of infrastructure per household. Cities use several tools to contain it:

- **Green belts:** rings of protected open land around a city, such as London's green belt, established in the mid-twentieth century. They protect countryside but can raise house prices inside the belt and push development to towns beyond it, lengthening commutes.
- **Urban growth boundaries:** a line beyond which urban development is restricted, reviewed periodically. Portland, Oregon, has used one since the late 1970s, combined with investment in light rail. Supporters credit it with denser, more walkable neighbourhoods; critics argue it has contributed to rising housing costs.
- **Brownfield-first policies:** prioritising redevelopment of previously used land before greenfield sites.

Each tool trades land protection against housing affordability, a classic sustainability trade-off to evaluate.

## Redesigning for the future

### Sponge cities

China launched a **sponge city** programme in 2015 with a group of pilot cities. The aim is to absorb, store and reuse rainwater rather than piping it away as fast as possible, through permeable paving, green roofs, wetlands, rain gardens and storage tanks. It addresses both urban flooding and water shortage. Limits include high cost, the difficulty of retrofitting dense older districts, and the risk that extreme storms exceed the system's design.

### The 15-minute city

The **15-minute city**, an idea associated with the urbanist Carlos Moreno and promoted in Paris under mayor Anne Hidalgo from around 2020, aims for residents to reach daily needs, such as work, shops, school, health care and parks, within a 15-minute walk or cycle ride. It reduces car use and emissions and supports local businesses. It works most easily in dense, mixed-use cities; in low-density suburbs it would require major changes to land use, and some critics fear it could raise property values in the best-served districts.

### Resilience planning

Sustainability also means coping with shocks. The Rockefeller Foundation's 100 Resilient Cities network, launched in 2013, funded chief resilience officers in member cities to plan for shocks such as floods and heatwaves and for long-term stresses such as housing shortages. Resilience planning asks a city to identify its main threats, the groups most exposed and the systems most likely to fail.

## Putting numbers on sustainability claims

### Worked example 1: runoff from a redeveloped block

An invented city block covers 20,000 m squared. A storm delivers 30 mm of rain (0.03 m). Runoff volume = area x rainfall x runoff coefficient, where the coefficient is the share of rain that runs off.

| Surface | Area (m squared) | Runoff coefficient | Runoff (m cubed) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Before: conventional roofs and paving | 20,000 | 0.9 | 20,000 x 0.03 x 0.9 = 540 |
| After: green roofs | 8,000 | 0.4 | 8,000 x 0.03 x 0.4 = 96 |
| After: permeable paving | 6,000 | 0.5 | 6,000 x 0.03 x 0.5 = 90 |
| After: conventional surfaces | 6,000 | 0.9 | 6,000 x 0.03 x 0.9 = 162 |

After redevelopment, runoff = 96 + 90 + 162 = 348 m cubed, compared with 540 before. The reduction is 540 - 348 = 192 m cubed, or 192 / 540 x 100 = **35.6%**. Less water reaches the drains at once, lowering flood risk downstream. The calculation also shows the limit: a third of the block still drains as before.

### Worked example 2: shifting commuters to public transport

Invented emission factors: a car journey emits 0.17 kg of carbon dioxide per passenger-kilometre; bus rapid transit emits 0.05 kg.

- 10,000 commuters each travel 12 km by car: 10,000 x 12 x 0.17 = 20,400 kg a day.
- If 40% switch to bus rapid transit: cars 6,000 x 12 x 0.17 = 12,240 kg; buses 4,000 x 12 x 0.05 = 2,400 kg; total 14,640 kg.
- Saving = 20,400 - 14,640 = 5,760 kg a day, a **28.2%** reduction.

The result depends on how full the buses are and on how the electricity or fuel is produced, so a fair evaluation states its assumptions.

## Measuring progress

Useful indicators for a sustainable urban system include:

- ecological or carbon footprint per resident;
- share of journeys by walking, cycling and public transport;
- share of households spending more than a set proportion of income on housing;
- share of the population living within walking distance of green space;
- share of waste recycled or composted;
- share of residents in informal housing.

A city can improve on some indicators while worsening on others, for example cutting emissions per person while housing affordability declines. Strong answers name both.

## Annotated model answer

*Evaluate strategies used to make urban areas more sustainable.* **(sample paragraph)**

"Sponge-city design, such as China's pilot programme from 2015, tackles flooding and water shortage together by storing rainwater rather than piping it away, an example of moving towards a circular metabolism **[strategy linked to concept]**. Retrofitting green roofs and permeable paving can cut storm runoff substantially, though not completely **[evidence of effectiveness]**. However, the cost is high and dense older districts are hard to retrofit, while the most extreme storms can still overwhelm the system **[limitations]**. Such strategies are most effective as part of a wider plan that also limits sprawl and protects affordability, since environmental gains alone do not make a city sustainable **[judgement across pillars]**."

## Common mistakes

- Treating sustainability as environmental only, ignoring affordability and inclusion.
- Praising green belts or growth boundaries without mentioning housing costs.
- Assuming flagship technology works at full scale in existing cities.
- Quoting percentage savings without stating the assumptions behind them.
- Ignoring resilience to future shocks.

## Exam technique

- **Evaluate** a strategy with effectiveness, cost, equity, transferability and long-term viability, then a judgement.
- **To what extent** questions on sustainability need a position on whether a city is moving towards or away from it.
- **Suggest** strategies for a city in a resource by linking each to a problem shown.
- Use indicators and calculations to make evaluation precise.
