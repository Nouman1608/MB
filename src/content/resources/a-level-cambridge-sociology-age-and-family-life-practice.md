These questions cover section 4.2 *Age and family life* of the Cambridge International AS & A Level
Sociology (9699) syllabus for 2024–2026: the social construction of childhood and the changing
position of children; the role of grandparents, including cross-cultural comparison and rising
life expectancy; how class, gender and ethnicity affect children's experiences; and changing ideas
of motherhood and fatherhood. Question types follow the Paper 2 pattern the syllabus describes.

---

## Questions

**1.** What is meant by the *social construction of childhood*? Give **one** example. **[4]**

**2.** Explain **two** ways in which the role of grandparents in the family may be changing. **[6]**

**3.** Explain **two** ways in which social class may affect the experiences of children in the
family. **[6]**

**4.** *"Childhood today is a time of greater protection and happiness than ever before."*

- **(a)** Explain the view expressed in the quotation. **[6]**
- **(b)** Using sociological material, give **two** arguments against the view expressed in the
  quotation. **[10]**

**5.** "The concepts of motherhood and fatherhood have changed a great deal in modern societies."
Evaluate this view. **[26]**

## Answers and mark guidance

### 1. Social construction of childhood [4]

- **Meaning (2 marks):** childhood is not a fixed biological stage; what it means to be a child,
  how long childhood lasts and how children are treated are defined by society and vary between
  cultures and over time.
- **Example (2 marks):** Philippe Ariès (*Centuries of Childhood*, English edition 1962) argued
  that in medieval Europe children were treated as "little adults" once past infancy; or the
  contrast between societies where children work from an early age and those where they are
  legally kept in full-time education. One mark for a bare example, two for an explained one.

### 2. Changing role of grandparents [6]

Up to 3 marks per explanation.

- **Rising life expectancy:** more people live long enough to be grandparents and even
  great-grandparents, producing the vertically extended **beanpole family** described by Julia
  Brannen (2003).
- **Childcare:** with more mothers in paid work, grandparents often provide informal, unpaid
  childcare, giving them a renewed practical role.
- **Financial support:** older people may transfer money, housing or inheritance to younger
  generations.
- **Cross-cultural difference:** in many South and East Asian societies grandparents traditionally
  live with married sons and hold authority; migration and urbanisation may be weakening this.
- **Dependency:** some grandparents become dependent, creating a "sandwich" or pivot generation of
  adults caring for both children and parents.

### 3. Social class and children's experiences [6]

Up to 3 marks per way.

- **Parenting style:** Annette Lareau's *Unequal Childhoods* (2003) found middle-class parents in
  the USA practising "concerted cultivation" (organised activities, negotiation with adults) while
  working-class and poor families relied more on "natural growth", with consequences for
  confidence in school.
- **Material resources:** income affects housing, diet, space to study and access to digital
  devices.
- **Child labour and responsibility:** in poorer households children may care for siblings or earn
  money, shortening childhood.
- **Cultural capital:** middle-class children gain knowledge and tastes valued by schools.

### 4(a). Explaining the view [6]

Credit explanation of the **march of progress** view:

- Children are now protected by laws on child labour, compulsory schooling and child protection.
- Lower infant mortality and smaller families mean parents invest more emotionally and financially
  in each child — the family has become **child-centred**.
- Children have rights (for example under the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child, 1989) and
  more say in family decisions.

### 4(b). Arguing against the view [10]

Up to 5 marks per argument.

- **Disappearance or erosion of childhood:** Neil Postman (*The Disappearance of Childhood*, 1982)
  argued that television gave children access to adult information, blurring the boundary between
  childhood and adulthood. Sue Palmer (*Toxic Childhood*, 2006) blamed technology, marketing and
  long working hours for harming children's development.
- **Conflict views of control:** protection is also control; children's time, space and bodies are
  regulated by adults (age patriarchy), so more protection need not mean more happiness.
- **Global inequality:** many children worldwide still experience poverty, child labour, early
  marriage or conflict, so the view generalises from wealthy societies.
- **Inequality within societies:** class, gender and ethnicity produce very different childhoods.

### 5. Essay: changing motherhood and fatherhood [26]

**Indicative content (AO1):**
- *Evidence of change:* women's rising employment has challenged the idea of the full-time mother;
  fathers spend more time on childcare than in earlier generations; the idea of the "new man" and
  involved father; Esther Dermott's *Intimate Fatherhood* (2008) argues fatherhood is increasingly
  defined by emotional closeness rather than breadwinning; lone parenthood and reconstituted
  families diversify parenting roles.
- *Evidence of continuity:* mothers still do most childcare and emotional work; many fathers
  "help" rather than share responsibility; Ann Oakley's research (*The Sociology of Housework*,
  1974) showed housework and childcare as women's work, and feminists argue much has stayed the
  same; intensive mothering ideals may have increased pressure on mothers.

**Application (AO2):** compare societies, for example different paternity leave policies or the
influence of extended families on parenting; apply concepts such as dual burden, emotion work and
the symmetrical family.

**Analysis and evaluation (AO3):**
- Distinguish **ideals** (what people say parenting should be) from **practices** (who actually does
  the work).
- Note that change varies by class, ethnicity and country.
- Conclude with a judgement, for example that the *concepts* have changed more than the *division
  of labour*.

**Level guidance:** top-band answers combine accurate evidence on both mothers and fathers with
sustained evaluation; middle-band answers describe change without weighing it; low-band answers
rely on common-sense assertion.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which studies are most useful for 4.2?** Ariès and Postman for childhood, Brannen for
grandparents and beanpole families, Lareau for class differences, and Dermott and Oakley for
parenting roles.

**Do I need cross-cultural examples?** Yes. The syllabus explicitly asks for cross-cultural
comparison of grandparents' roles, and comparative examples strengthen childhood answers too.

**Is "child-centred" the same as "children have power"?** No. Child-centred families invest more in
children, but adults may still control them closely.
