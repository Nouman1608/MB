This guide covers **Topic 2 Poetry and Unseen Texts**, the second of two
topics in OxfordAQA International GCSE English Literature (9275),
Version 4.2, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. It represents Route A's
Paper 2a content, one of two available routes through the qualification.

## Where this fits in 9275

Topic 1 (Prose and Drama) covers set prose fiction and drama texts
assessed in Paper 1, compulsory for every candidate. Topic 2 then
diverges by route: Route A pairs Paper 1 with Paper 2a (Poetry and
Unseen Texts, covered here), while Route B pairs Paper 1 with Paper 2b
plus a non-exam assessment. This guide covers the shared poetry content
plus Route A's unseen-prose section specifically.

## Syllabus coverage

**OXFORDAQA INTERNATIONAL GCSE ENGLISH LITERATURE (9275) — TOPIC 2 POETRY AND UNSEEN TEXTS**

- 3.2.1 Poetry — study of the OxfordAQA Exams Poetry Anthology *People
and Places*, comprising 20 thematically linked poems allowing candidates
to explore connected ideas and themes from poets and poetry across
different times and contexts
- 3.2.2 Unseen poetry — preparation through exposure to a wide range of
poetry, developing the ability to closely analyse unfamiliar poems and
to analyse and compare key features such as content, theme, structure
and use of language
- 3.2.3 Unseen prose (Route A only) — preparation through exposure to a
wide range of prose texts, developing the ability to closely analyse the
variety of ways writers create meaning and influence the reader

Route B replaces 3.2.3 with a non-exam assessment on a substantial whole
prose fiction text chosen outside the qualification's prescribed set
texts, internally assessed rather than examined; this guide focuses on
Route A's examined unseen-prose alternative.

## How to approach it

The *People and Places* anthology rewards thematic revision over
poem-by-poem isolation: since its 20 poems are deliberately linked by
theme, practise comparing pairs or small groups of poems on a shared
theme (for example, displacement, identity, or a sense of place) rather
than revising each poem as an unconnected unit. Exam questions on
anthology poetry typically ask for comparison, so having two or three
well-prepared comparison pairings ready for common themes is more useful
than shallow familiarity with all 20 poems individually.

Unseen poetry and unseen prose share a core transferable skill: close
analysis of an unfamiliar text under time pressure, without the benefit
of having studied it in advance. Build this skill through timed practice
on genuinely unfamiliar poems and extracts, focusing on what can be
inferred and analysed from the text itself — structure, language choices,
tone and effect — rather than relying on background knowledge of a
poet or novelist that will not be available for a genuinely unseen text.

For unseen prose specifically, practise identifying how a writer creates
meaning through narrative viewpoint, structure and language within a
short extract, since a full unseen text will only ever be given in
excerpt form — the analysis must work from what is actually on the page,
not assumptions about a wider plot.

## Worked example: comparing anthology poems

Two poems from *People and Places* dealing with the theme of leaving a
familiar place might be compared like this:

```
Poem A: uses concrete, sensory detail (smells, sounds) to convey loss
        of a specific remembered place
Poem B: uses more abstract, reflective language to convey the emotional
        aftermath of leaving, with less physical detail

Comparison point: both poems treat leaving as an emotional rupture, but
Poem A roots that emotion in physical memory while Poem B foregrounds
introspection -- a genuine point of contrast to build an answer around,
rather than simply stating both poems are "about leaving."
```

## Common mistakes

Treating the anthology as a list of poems to summarise rather than
compare, when exam questions typically ask for comparison across two or
more poems. Analysing an unseen poem or prose extract by guessing at the
poet's or writer's biography or wider body of work, instead of working
from the text actually provided. Naming a poetic device without
explaining its effect on meaning or the reader. Running out of time on
the anthology question because equal time was not planned across the
paper's sections.

## Quick revision checklist

- Prepare thematic comparison pairings across the *People and Places*
anthology rather than revising poems in isolation.
- Practise unseen poetry and unseen prose analysis under timed
conditions on genuinely unfamiliar texts.
- Build answers from what is explicitly and implicitly present in the
text, not assumed background knowledge.
- For Route A candidates: confirm which route your centre follows, since
Route B substitutes non-exam assessment for the unseen-prose section
covered here.

## Why unseen texts matter to the wider qualification

Unseen analysis is deliberately included alongside studied-text
questions because it tests transferable literary skill rather than
memorised knowledge of set texts and their critical context. A candidate
who can only discuss pre-prepared points about the anthology or set
prose and drama, without being able to apply the same analytical
approach to something genuinely new, has not developed the skill this
component is designed to assess -- which is part of why timed,
genuinely-unseen practice matters more here than in the studied-text
components of Topic 1.

It also reflects the qualification's broader assessment design, which
balances studied and unstudied material so that a candidate's grade
genuinely tracks reading and analytical skill rather than recall alone.
