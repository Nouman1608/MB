> **Original questions written for Marlbridge.** They are not past-paper questions and do not copy the real exam's structure or mark tariffs.

**HL only.** These questions practise Regional study 11 in the Europe option (HL Paper 3). Revise first with the [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-ussr-post-soviet-russia-revision-notes/) and the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-ussr-post-soviet-russia/).

## Questions

**1.** State two features of Soviet agriculture under Khrushchev. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why Khrushchev's Secret Speech was significant. **[4]**

**3.** Read this short extract, constructed for practice.

> "At the factory we meet and talk about the plan, but nothing changes. The directors keep their cars, the shops for officials keep their sausage, and we keep the queue. They call this stability. I call it waiting." (Constructed for practice, in the voice of a Soviet worker in the late 1970s.)

**(a)** Explain what the extract suggests about Soviet society under Brezhnev. **[3]**

**(b)** Analyse one limitation of the extract as evidence about the whole Soviet population. **[3]**

**4.** Explain two reasons why the dissident movement had limited impact in the 1970s. **[4]**

**5.** Analyse the reasons for the failure of the August 1991 coup. **[6]**

**6.** Examine the economic policies of Yeltsin in the 1990s. **[6]**

**7.** "Gorbachev's reforms, rather than long-term economic weakness, brought about the end of the USSR." To what extent do you agree? Plan an answer: state your argument, three themes and a counter-argument. **[15]**

**8.** Compare the political systems of Russia under Yeltsin and under Putin. **[4]**

**9.** Discuss how historians disagree about why the USSR collapsed. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two: the Virgin Lands campaign in Kazakhstan and Siberia; the corn campaign; reliance on state and collective farms; grain imports in 1963; early good harvests followed by poor results. One mark each. **[2]**

**2.** Up to 4. Khrushchev denounced Stalin's crimes and the cult of personality at the 20th Party Congress in 1956, breaking a taboo (1); it led to the release and rehabilitation of many prisoners and a more open culture, the Thaw (1); it shook the authority of communist parties inside and outside the USSR, contributing to unrest in Poland and Hungary in 1956 (1); it divided the party leadership, and some officials resented it, which contributed to opposition to Khrushchev (1). **[4]**

**3. (a)** Up to 3: the plan and meetings seem formal and unproductive (1); officials enjoy privileges not open to ordinary workers, reflecting the nomenklatura (1); "stability" is described as stagnation, matching the idea of an era of stagnation (1). **[3]**
**(b)** Up to 3: it is one worker's view, and others, such as the elite or some rural people, may have felt differently (1); the extract is constructed, but even a real memory might be shaped by later criticism of the regime (1); it does not show gains such as better housing or social security that some valued (1). **[3]**

**4.** Up to 4, two reasons explained. The KGB watched, arrested, exiled or confined dissidents in psychiatric hospitals, as with Sakharov in Gorky in 1980 (2). They were few, intellectual and isolated from most workers and peasants, and censorship limited the spread of their ideas, though samizdat and foreign broadcasts reached some (2). **[4]**

**5.** Up to 6 using a markband approach. The plotters were poorly organised and failed to arrest Yeltsin (1); Yeltsin's defiance in Moscow gave a focus for resistance (1); public support in Moscow and Leningrad was strong (1); parts of the army and KGB hesitated or refused to use force (1); the plotters lacked clear leadership and a plan, and the coup lasted only three days (1); a judgement on the main reason, such as the weakness of the plotters' support (1). **[6]**

**6.** Up to 6: shock therapy from January 1992 freed prices (1); inflation was very high and savings were lost (1); voucher privatization and loans-for-shares of 1995 moved assets to a few oligarchs (1); output fell, poverty rose and life expectancy dropped (1); the 1998 default showed deep weakness (1); a judgement on whether the policies were necessary or badly carried out (1). **[6]**

**7.** Credit a plan with these features, 3 marks each, 15 in all. Understanding of the claim: perestroika, glasnost and the end of party monopoly as reforms that weakened the system (3). Case for Gorbachev: economic disorganisation, rising nationalism after glasnost, refusal to use force in 1989 (3). Case for long-term weakness: stagnation, dependence on oil, military costs, Afghanistan, the nomenklatura (3). Perspectives: historians who stress leadership and those who stress structure or national movements (3). Counter-argument and judgement: for instance that reform exposed problems that already existed, so both mattered, with Gorbachev as trigger (3). **[15]**

**8.** Up to 4. Both have a strong presidency under the 1993 constitution (1); under Yeltsin the political system was more chaotic, with open conflict between president and parliament, a free but oligarch-dominated press and a strong Communist Party (1); under Putin control was more centralised, with the vertical of power and a dominant party (1); a judgement on difference, such as stability against pluralism (1). **[4]**

**9.** Up to 6: one view stresses Gorbachev's decisions (2); another stresses long-term economic failure and the burden of empire (2); explains why they differ, for example the use of insider accounts or archives (1); a reasoned judgement (1). **[6]**
