## What promotion is

**Promotion** is the way a business communicates with customers to inform, persuade and remind them to buy. Its aims include raising awareness, building the brand, encouraging trial, and keeping customers loyal. The **promotional mix** is the combination of methods a business uses.

## The methods

| Method | Description | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Above the line** | Paid-for mass media advertising: television, radio, press, cinema, posters | Wide reach; builds brand image | Expensive; hard to target and to link to sales |
| **Below the line** | Promotions the business controls directly: discounts, coupons, free samples, point-of-sale displays, loyalty cards, competitions, direct mail | Targeted; quick effect; easier to measure | Short-lived; can reduce margins and teach customers to wait for deals |
| **Branding** | Building a recognisable identity through name, logo, packaging and values | Long-term loyalty and premium price | Slow and costly; damaged by one failure |
| **Personal selling** | Staff talk to buyers directly | Best for complex, high-value sales; builds relationships | Costly per contact; depends on staff skill |
| **Internet** | Website, search advertising, email, online display adverts | Targeted; measurable; flexible budgets | Crowded; customers may ignore or block adverts |
| **Social media** | Posts, influencers, communities on social platforms | Low cost per reach; conversation with customers; targeted | Results unpredictable; negative comments spread; needs constant attention |
| **Drip marketing** | A planned series of regular messages (emails, letters, texts) over time to the same customers | Keeps the brand in mind; cheap per contact; builds trust | Can irritate if too frequent; relies on customer data |
| **Viral marketing** | Content so interesting that people share it, spreading like a virus | Very cheap reach; trusted because friends share it | Cannot be controlled or guaranteed; may spread in unintended ways |

Above the line and below the line are the traditional split. Many modern methods (internet and social media) cross the divide.

### Worked example 1: comparing cost per sale

Sunbeam Juice has a promotion budget. It estimates the results of four options.

| Option | Cost | People reached | Purchase rate | Sales | Cost per sale |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Television (above the line) | £70,000 | 2,000,000 | 0.4% | 8,000 | **£8.75** |
| Social media and influencers | £30,000 | 400,000 | 1.5% | 6,000 | **£5.00** |
| In-store sampling and coupons (below the line) | £20,000 | 25,000 | 12% | 3,000 | **£6.67** |
| Drip email series | £6,000 | 50,000 | 2.4% | 1,200 | **£5.00** |

Workings: sales = people × purchase rate (for example 2,000,000 × 0.004 = 8,000); cost per sale = cost ÷ sales (70,000 ÷ 8,000 = 8.75).

**Interpretation.** Television wins the most sales but at the highest cost per sale. Social media and the email series are cheapest per sale, though the email series is small. Sampling converts a high share of those reached, but only 25,000 people are reached. The best mix depends on the objective. If Sunbeam wants **awareness** for a launch, television's reach has value that the cost per sale does not show. If it wants **sales now**, social media and email look better. Television also builds the brand beyond this campaign, which the table does not measure. A sensible promotional mix could combine social media for reach, sampling to encourage trial, and drip emails to bring buyers back.

## Advertising elasticity of demand

**Advertising elasticity of demand (AED)** measures how responsive the quantity demanded is to a change in advertising spending.

AED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in advertising expenditure

- A value **greater than 1** means demand is **advertising elastic**: sales rise by a bigger percentage than spending.
- A value **between 0 and 1** means advertising has some effect, but sales rise by a smaller percentage than spending.
- A value near **0** means advertising has little effect.
- A **negative** value would mean sales fell when spending rose.

### Worked example 2: AED and diminishing returns

Sunbeam raises its advertising in steps. The contribution on each carton is **£6**.

| Step | Spend | Sales | % change in spend | % change in sales | AED | Extra contribution | Extra spend | Net gain |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Start | £40,000 | 80,000 | | | | | | |
| 1 | £50,000 | 88,000 | +25.0% | +10.0% | **0.40** | 8,000 × £6 = £48,000 | £10,000 | **+£38,000** |
| 2 | £60,000 | 91,000 | +20.0% | +3.4% | **0.17** | 3,000 × £6 = £18,000 | £10,000 | **+£8,000** |
| 3 | £70,000 | 92,000 | +16.7% | +1.1% | **0.07** | 1,000 × £6 = £6,000 | £10,000 | **−£4,000** |

Step 1 working: % change in spend = (50,000 − 40,000) ÷ 40,000 × 100 = 25%. % change in sales = (88,000 − 80,000) ÷ 80,000 × 100 = 10%. AED = 10 ÷ 25 = **0.40**.

**Interpretation.** The AED is below 1 at every step, so sales respond less than proportionately. It falls as spending rises, which shows **diminishing returns**: each extra £10,000 buys fewer extra sales. Because the contribution per carton is high, the first two steps still add profit (+£38,000 and +£8,000). The third step loses money (−£4,000), so Sunbeam should stop at £60,000, unless the spending has long-term brand benefits that the calculation does not capture.

**Usefulness and limits.** AED helps a business judge how much to spend and compare media. But sales also change because of price, seasons, rivals and the economy, so the effect of advertising alone is hard to isolate. The effect may come later than the spending (a lag), and a figure from one campaign may not apply to the next.

## Drip and viral marketing

**Drip marketing** works by regular contact. A customer who joins a mailing list might receive a welcome offer on day 1, a recipe on day 7, a reminder on day 30 and a loyalty offer on day 60. Each message costs little, and the sequence builds familiarity.

**Viral marketing** depends on sharing. Suppose a video is seeded with 500 people and each viewer passes it on to a number of others in the next round.

| Round | If each viewer shares with 1.4 people | If each viewer shares with 0.8 people |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 500 | 500 |
| 2 | 700 | 400 |
| 3 | 980 | 320 |
| 4 | 1,372 | 256 |
| 5 | 1,921 | 205 |
| Total | **5,473** | **1,681** |

With a sharing rate above 1, each round is larger than the last and the campaign grows. Below 1, it fades. A viral campaign is therefore a gamble: the content must be shared more than once per viewer on average, and the business cannot control the result.

## Importance of promotion

Promotion supports sales growth, brand image and customer loyalty, and can help to launch new products or revive mature ones. Its cost can be large, so it must be weighed against the extra sales it produces. **Stakeholders:** customers receive information but can be misled by poor advertising; employees benefit from rising sales; shareholders see higher revenue but lower profit if the spending is excessive; the media and agencies earn income; regulators control misleading claims.

## Choosing a promotional mix

To recommend and justify a mix:

1. Set the **objective** (awareness, trial, loyalty).
2. Identify the **target market** and where it spends time.
3. Check the **budget** and compare cost per sale.
4. Consider the **product**: a complex product suits personal selling; an impulse product suits displays and coupons.
5. Combine methods that support each other, such as television for awareness and below the line for trial.
6. State a **risk** and how results will be measured.

## Common mistakes

- Treating above the line and below the line as the same.
- Judging a method only by cost per sale, ignoring brand value and objectives.
- Using absolute changes instead of percentage changes in AED.
- Forgetting that sales may rise for reasons other than advertising.
- Assuming viral marketing is free and certain.

## Exam technique

Name the method, apply it to the product and audience, and give a strength and a limit. In calculations, show the percentage changes before dividing, and interpret the result in terms of profit and the objective.
