This set practises **3.3.11.1 Preparation** of amines from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International A2 content, assessed in Unit 4 and in the synoptic Unit 5. If you get stuck, go back to the [amine preparation study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-11-1/) or the [amine preparation revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-11-1-revision-notes/). Use Ar values H = 1.0, C = 12.0, N = 14.0, O = 16.0 and Br = 79.9.

## Section A: short questions

**1.** Which method converts 1-bromopropane into a primary amine with one more carbon atom, without forming secondary or tertiary amines?

A: heat with excess ethanolic ammonia in a sealed tube

B: reflux with KCN in aqueous ethanol, then reduce with hydrogen and nickel

C: reflux with tin and concentrated hydrochloric acid

D: warm with aqueous sodium hydroxide

**[1]**

**2.** Write an equation for the reaction of 2-bromobutane with excess ammonia to form butan-2-amine. **[1]**

**3.** Explain why the reaction in question 2 gives a mixture of amines even when ammonia is in excess. **[2]**

**4.** Hexanedinitrile, NC–(CH₂)₄–CN, is reduced to hexane-1,6-diamine. State the number of moles of H₂ needed per mole of hexanedinitrile. **[1]**

**5.** Classify each amine as aliphatic or aromatic.

**(a)** C₆H₅CH₂CH₂NH₂ **[1]**

**(b)** CH₃C₆H₄NH₂ (NH₂ on a ring carbon) **[1]**

**(c)** C₆H₁₁NH₂ (cyclohexylamine) **[1]**

## Section B: structured questions

**6.** A chemist wants to make butan-1-amine, CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂NH₂, from 1-chloropropane.

**(a)** Give the reagents and conditions for each of the two steps, and name the intermediate. **[3]**

**(b)** Write an equation for the second step, using H₂. **[1]**

**(c)** State one hazard of this route. **[1]**

**7.** 6.85 g of 1-bromobutane (Mr = 136.9) is converted into pentanenitrile with an 85.0% yield. The pentanenitrile is then reduced to pentan-1-amine (Mr = 87.0) with a 90.0% yield.

**(a)** Write equations for both steps, using [H] for the reduction. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the mass of pentan-1-amine obtained. **[3]**

**8.** 1,4-Dinitrobenzene, C₆H₄(NO₂)₂ (Mr = 168.0), is reduced with tin and concentrated hydrochloric acid, and the mixture is then made alkaline.

**(a)** Write an equation for the reduction, using [H]. **[1]**

**(b)** Give the formula of the organic ion present before alkali is added, and write an equation for its reaction with hydroxide ions. **[2]**

**(c)** 5.04 g of 1,4-dinitrobenzene gives benzene-1,4-diamine (Mr = 108.0) with a 72.0% yield. Calculate the mass of diamine obtained. **[2]**

**9.** An aromatic nitro compound D contains one benzene ring and an ethyl group. D has Mr = 151.0. When D is reduced, the amine formed has Mr = 121.0.

**(a)** Use the change in Mr to show that D has one nitro group. **[1]**

**(b)** Give the molecular formula of the amine and a possible structural formula for it. **[2]**

**10.** Propan-1-amine can be made from 1-bromopropane or from bromoethane. Compare the two routes, giving reagents for each and considering the number of steps, the purity of the product and any hazard. **[4]**

**11.** Outline the mechanism for the reaction of 1-bromopropane with ammonia to form propylamine. Describe each curly arrow and give the intermediate. **[3]**

**12.** A student suggests making phenylamine by heating chlorobenzene with ammonia, by analogy with the halogenoalkane route.

**(a)** Explain why this would not work under normal conditions. **[2]**

**(b)** State the route actually used to make phenylamine from benzene. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. A gives a mixture with the same chain length; C reduces nitro groups; D gives an alcohol.

**2.** CH₃CHBrCH₂CH₃ + 2NH₃ → CH₃CH(NH₂)CH₂CH₃ + NH₄Br [1]

**3.** Butan-2-amine also has a lone pair on nitrogen, so it is a nucleophile [1]; it can attack more 2-bromobutane, giving secondary and tertiary amines (and a quaternary salt) [1]

**4.** 4 mol (2 H₂ per C≡N group) [1]

**5. (a)** Aliphatic (N is on a chain carbon, not on the ring) [1]

**(b)** Aromatic [1]

**(c)** Aliphatic (the ring is not a benzene ring) [1]

**6. (a)** Step 1: KCN in aqueous ethanol, heat under reflux [1]; intermediate butanenitrile, CH₃CH₂CH₂CN [1]; step 2: hydrogen with a nickel catalyst (or LiAlH₄ in dry ether) [1]

**(b)** CH₃CH₂CH₂CN + 2H₂ → CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂NH₂ [1]

**(c)** Potassium cyanide is highly toxic (or hydrogen is flammable) [1]

*Examiner insight:* naming the intermediate "propanenitrile" is the classic slip; the C≡N carbon counts, so three carbons from the chloroalkane plus one gives butanenitrile.

**7. (a)** CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂Br + CN⁻ → CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CN + Br⁻ [1]; CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CN + 4[H] → CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₂NH₂ [1]

**(b)** n(1-bromobutane) = 6.85 ÷ 136.9 = 0.0500 mol [1]; overall yield = 0.850 × 0.900 = 0.765, so n(amine) = 0.0383 mol [1]; mass = 0.0383 × 87.0 = **3.33 g** [1]

**8. (a)** C₆H₄(NO₂)₂ + 12[H] → C₆H₄(NH₂)₂ + 4H₂O [1]

**(b)** ⁺H₃N–C₆H₄–NH₃⁺ (accept as its chloride salt) [1]; ⁺H₃N–C₆H₄–NH₃⁺ + 2OH⁻ → H₂N–C₆H₄–NH₂ + 2H₂O [1]

**(c)** n = 5.04 ÷ 168.0 = 0.0300 mol; n(diamine) = 0.0300 × 0.720 = 0.0216 mol [1]; mass = 0.0216 × 108.0 = **2.33 g** [1]

*Examiner insight:* both amino groups are protonated in the acid, so the ion carries a 2+ charge and needs two hydroxide ions.

**9. (a)** Each –NO₂ → –NH₂ lowers Mr by 30.0; 151.0 − 121.0 = 30.0, so there is one nitro group [1]

**(b)** C₈H₁₁N [1]; for example 4-ethylphenylamine, CH₃CH₂C₆H₄NH₂ (2- or 3-isomer accepted) [1]

**10.** Any four [4]:

- From 1-bromopropane: excess ethanolic ammonia, heated in a sealed tube;
- one step, but a mixture of primary, secondary and tertiary amines forms, so the product must be separated;
- from bromoethane: KCN in aqueous ethanol to give propanenitrile, then reduction with H₂/Ni or LiAlH₄;
- two steps, but only the primary amine forms;
- the nitrile route uses highly toxic potassium cyanide.

**11.** Curly arrow from the lone pair on N of NH₃ to the δ+ carbon bonded to Br, and arrow from the C–Br bond to Br, which leaves as Br⁻ [1]; intermediate CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₃⁺ [1]; a second NH₃ uses its lone pair to remove H⁺ from N⁺, with an arrow from the N–H bond to N, giving propylamine and NH₄⁺ [1]

**12. (a)** In chlorobenzene a lone pair on chlorine overlaps with the delocalised ring, so the C–Cl bond is stronger than in a halogenoalkane [1]; and the electron-rich ring repels the ammonia nucleophile, so substitution does not occur [1]

**(b)** Nitrate benzene (conc. HNO₃ and conc. H₂SO₄), then reduce the nitrobenzene with tin and concentrated HCl, then add NaOH [1]

## Where marks are usually lost

- Miscounting carbons in nitrile names.
- Wrong numbers of [H]: 4 per C≡N, 6 per NO₂.
- One NH₃ in a halogenoalkane equation.
- Forgetting that the Sn/HCl product is an ammonium salt.
- Adding step yields instead of multiplying them.
