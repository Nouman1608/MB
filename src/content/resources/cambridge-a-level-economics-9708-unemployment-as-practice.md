## Section A

**1.** Which person is counted as unemployed?

A: A full-time university student not looking for work

B: A retired teacher

C: A factory worker who lost her job and is applying for new ones

D: A part-time shop assistant who would like more hours

**[1]**

**2.** Which type of unemployment is caused by a fall in aggregate demand?

A: Cyclical

B: Frictional

C: Seasonal

D: Structural

**[1]**

**3.** Define the unemployment rate. **[2]**

**4.** Explain one advantage of the labour force survey over the claimant count. **[2]**

**5.** Explain what is meant by frictional unemployment. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why technological unemployment can become structural unemployment. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Country S has a working-age population of 30 million. 21.6 million people are employed and 1.8 million are unemployed.

**(a)** Calculate the size of the labour force and the unemployment rate. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the number of economically inactive people of working age. **[1]**

**(c)** The claimant count shows 1.2 million. Calculate this as a percentage of the survey figure and give two reasons for the difference. **[3]**

**(d)** An extra 0.4 million people want work but have stopped looking. Calculate the unemployment rate if they were counted as unemployed and part of the labour force. **[2]**

**8.** *Original extract:* "Unemployment in Coast Province rises from about 4% in the summer to about 9% each winter, when hotels and beach businesses close. In Mill Valley, unemployment has stayed near 14% for five years since the cotton mills shut, while engineering firms in the capital struggle to fill vacancies."

**(a)** Identify the type of unemployment in Coast Province and explain your answer. **[2]**

**(b)** Identify the type of unemployment in Mill Valley and explain your answer. **[3]**

**(c)** Explain why a rise in aggregate demand would do little to reduce unemployment in Mill Valley. **[2]**

**9.** Explain two consequences of high unemployment for the government. **[4]**

**10.** Explain why the claimant count can fall even when no extra jobs are created. **[2]**

**11.** Explain how the informal sector can make measured unemployment a poor guide to the labour market in some economies. **[3]**

**12.** "All unemployment is equally harmful." Comment on this statement. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** **C** [1]. She is without work, available and seeking it. D is employed (underemployed).

**2.** **A** [1].

**3.** The number of people **unemployed** [1] expressed as a **percentage of the labour force** (the employed plus the unemployed) [1].

**4.** It uses a **consistent international definition** [1], so it includes jobless people who are not entitled to benefit and allows comparison between countries and over time [1].

**5.** Unemployment while people **move between jobs** [1]; it arises because it takes time to find a suitable vacancy, as job-seekers and employers have **imperfect information** [1].

**6.** Workers replaced by new technology may **lack the skills** needed for the new jobs that technology creates [1], so the mismatch between their skills and available jobs **lasts for a long time**, which is structural unemployment [1].

**7. (a)** Labour force = 21.6 + 1.8 = **23.4 million** [1]. Rate = 1.8 / 23.4 x 100 = **7.7%** [1].

**(b)** Inactive = 30 - 23.4 = **6.6 million** [1].

**(c)** 1.2 / 1.8 x 100 = **66.7%** [1]. Reasons: some jobless people are **not eligible** for benefit, for example because a partner works [1]; some eligible people **do not claim**, for example through lack of information or stigma [1].

**(d)** Unemployed = 2.2 million; labour force = 23.8 million [1]. Rate = 2.2 / 23.8 x 100 = **9.2%** [1].

**8. (a)** **Seasonal** [1]: unemployment rises in a regular pattern each winter when tourism-related demand for labour falls [1].

**(b)** **Structural** [1]: it followed the closure of a declining industry (cotton mills) [1] and has lasted five years while vacancies exist elsewhere for workers with different skills [1].

**(c)** The problem is a **mismatch of skills and location**, not a lack of demand [1]. Extra spending would raise demand for engineers in the capital, but Mill Valley's workers lack those skills or cannot move, so they would remain unemployed [1].

**9.** **Higher spending on benefits** [1]: more people claim unemployment-related support, which has an opportunity cost in other spending such as education [1]. **Lower tax revenue** [1]: the unemployed pay less income tax and spend less, so indirect tax receipts fall, widening the budget deficit [1].

**10.** The claimant count depends on **benefit rules** [1]: if eligibility is tightened, or claimants leave benefit for other reasons such as training schemes, the count falls although the same number lack work [1].

**11.** Many people work in **unregistered jobs**, such as casual trading or farm labour [1]. Surveys and registers may miss them or record them as unemployed [1], so measured unemployment may say little about how many people have some income or how well they are employed [1].

**12.** The statement is too strong [1]. **Frictional** unemployment is usually short and can lead to a better match between workers and jobs, so its harm is small [1]. **Long-term structural** unemployment causes lasting loss of skills, income and confidence, and output is lost for years [1]. Harm also depends on **who is affected** and on the benefit system, so different types and durations are not equally harmful [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing by the working-age population in Question 7.
- Forgetting to add discouraged workers to both the unemployed and the labour force in Question 7(d).
- Naming the type in Question 8 without quoting evidence from the extract.
- Ignoring duration and type when judging harm in Question 12.
