This guide goes deeper into **3.2.3.1 Trends in properties**, the first point of section 3.2.3 Group 7(17), the halogens, in the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification. The point covers the trends in electronegativity and boiling point; "the trend in oxidising ability of the halogens down the group, including displacement reactions of halide ions in aqueous solution"; "the trend in reducing ability of the halide ions, including the reactions of solid sodium halides with concentrated sulfuric acid"; the use of acidified silver nitrate to identify halide ions; and the trend in solubility of the silver halides in ammonia. You must explain why silver nitrate is used, why it is acidified and why ammonia is added. It is International AS content, assessed in Unit 1.

The [halogens study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-group-7-17-the-halogens/) gives an overview of every trend. This page works through the **equations and redox reasoning** in more detail, particularly the sulfuric acid reactions, which carry the most marks. Afterwards, use the [trends in properties revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-2-3-1-revision-notes/) and the [trends in properties practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-2-3-1-practice/).

## One explanation for four trends

Down Group 7 each atom has **one more occupied shell**. So:

- the atomic radius increases
- shielding by inner electrons increases
- the nucleus attracts electrons in or near the outer shell less strongly, even though the nuclear charge rises.

This single idea explains the fall in electronegativity, the fall in oxidising ability and the rise in reducing ability of the halide ions. Only the boiling point trend needs a different explanation.

| Property | Trend down the group | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Electronegativity | decreases | bonding pair further from a more shielded nucleus |
| Boiling point | increases | more electrons per molecule, stronger van der Waals forces |
| Oxidising ability of X₂ | decreases | incoming electron enters a shell further out and more shielded |
| Reducing ability of X⁻ | increases | outer electron of X⁻ further out and more shielded, so lost more easily |

## Boiling points: what is and is not broken

All the halogens are diatomic simple molecules. When they boil, **only the van der Waals forces between molecules** are overcome; the X–X bond is not broken. F₂ has 18 electrons and I₂ has 106, so the van der Waals forces, and the boiling points, increase down the group. Bond enthalpy is irrelevant here, and quoting it loses the mark.

## Oxidising ability: displacement in aqueous solution

A halogen oxidises a halide ion **below** it in the group. The more reactive halogen gains electrons; the halide ion loses them.

| Halogen added | to Cl⁻(aq) | to Br⁻(aq) | to I⁻(aq) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cl₂(aq) | no reaction | orange-yellow solution: Br₂ formed | brown solution: I₂ formed |
| Br₂(aq) | no reaction | no reaction | brown solution: I₂ formed |
| I₂(aq) | no reaction | no reaction | no reaction |

Adding a non-polar solvent such as cyclohexane and shaking makes the product easier to identify: bromine gives an orange organic layer and iodine a violet (purple) one.

### Worked example 1: half-equations and an ionic equation

*Write half-equations and an overall ionic equation for the reaction of bromine water with potassium iodide solution, and identify the oxidising agent.*

```
reduction:   Br₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Br⁻
oxidation:   2I⁻ → I₂ + 2e⁻
overall:     Br₂ + 2I⁻ → 2Br⁻ + I₂
```

Bromine gains electrons, so **bromine is the oxidising agent**. K⁺ is a spectator ion and is left out.

## Reducing ability: solid sodium halides with concentrated sulfuric acid

Every sodium halide first undergoes an **acid–base** reaction, producing misty fumes of the hydrogen halide:

```
NaX(s) + H₂SO₄(l) → NaHSO₄(s) + HX(g)
```

What follows depends on whether the halide ion can reduce sulfur, which is +6 in H₂SO₄.

### Fluoride and chloride: no redox

F⁻ and Cl⁻ are not strong enough reducing agents to reduce sulfuric acid. Only misty fumes of HF or HCl are seen.

### Bromide: sulfur reduced to +4

HBr reduces sulfuric acid to sulfur dioxide, and bromide is oxidised to bromine:

```
half-equation (reduction):   H₂SO₄ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → SO₂ + 2H₂O
redox step:                  2HBr + H₂SO₄ → Br₂ + SO₂ + 2H₂O
overall from the solid:      2NaBr + 3H₂SO₄ → 2NaHSO₄ + Br₂ + SO₂ + 2H₂O
```

Observations: misty fumes (HBr), orange-brown fumes (Br₂), and a colourless, choking gas (SO₂).

### Iodide: sulfur reduced to +4, 0 and −2

Iodide is the strongest reducing agent of the four and reduces sulfur further:

| Product | S oxidation state | Equation from HI | Observation |
|---|---|---|---|
| SO₂ | +4 | 2HI + H₂SO₄ → I₂ + SO₂ + 2H₂O | colourless choking gas |
| S | 0 | 6HI + H₂SO₄ → 3I₂ + S + 4H₂O | yellow solid |
| H₂S | −2 | 8HI + H₂SO₄ → 4I₂ + H₂S + 4H₂O | smell of bad eggs |

Iodine appears as a black solid and purple vapour. Each reduction product has a matching half-equation, for example H₂SO₄ + 6H⁺ + 6e⁻ → S + 4H₂O.

### Worked example 2: balancing one of the iodide reactions

*Write an overall equation for solid sodium iodide with concentrated sulfuric acid forming sodium hydrogensulfate, iodine, sulfur and water.*

```
electrons:   S goes from +6 to 0, gaining 6e⁻; each I⁻ → ½I₂ loses 1e⁻, so 6 I⁻ are needed
redox part:  6I⁻ give 3I₂; 1 H₂SO₄ gives S
sodium:      6 Na⁺ end up in 6 NaHSO₄, which uses 6 more H₂SO₄
water:       balance H and O with 4H₂O
overall:     6NaI + 7H₂SO₄ → 6NaHSO₄ + 3I₂ + S + 4H₂O
```

Check: S 7 = 6 + 1; O 28 = 24 + 4; H 14 = 6 + 8.

## Identifying halide ions with silver nitrate

Add dilute nitric acid, then silver nitrate solution:

```
Ag⁺(aq) + X⁻(aq) → AgX(s)
```

| Halide | Precipitate | Dilute NH₃(aq) | Concentrated NH₃(aq) |
|---|---|---|---|
| F⁻ | none (AgF is soluble) | not applicable | not applicable |
| Cl⁻ | white | dissolves | dissolves |
| Br⁻ | cream | does not dissolve | dissolves |
| I⁻ | yellow | does not dissolve | does not dissolve |

When AgCl dissolves, a soluble complex ion forms:

```
AgCl(s) + 2NH₃(aq) → [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)
```

The three reasons the specification asks for:

- **Why silver nitrate**: it gives precipitates of different colours with Cl⁻, Br⁻ and I⁻.
- **Why acidified**: nitric acid reacts with and removes ions such as carbonate (and hydroxide) that would otherwise form precipitates with Ag⁺ and give a false positive. Hydrochloric acid must not be used, because it adds chloride ions.
- **Why ammonia is added**: the white, cream and yellow colours are hard to tell apart, but the precipitates differ in solubility in ammonia, which decreases from AgCl to AgI.

### Worked example 3: interpreting test results

*Solution R gives a cream precipitate with acidified silver nitrate. The precipitate does not dissolve in dilute ammonia but dissolves in concentrated ammonia. A second portion of R turns orange when chlorine water is added. Identify the anion in R and explain both observations.*

- Cream precipitate, insoluble in dilute but soluble in concentrated ammonia: **AgBr**, so R contains **Br⁻**.
- Chlorine is a stronger oxidising agent than bromine, so it oxidises Br⁻ to orange Br₂: Cl₂ + 2Br⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + Br₂.

## Common mistakes

- Breaking X–X bonds to explain boiling points.
- Writing displacement equations with sodium or potassium ions included.
- Saying iodine displaces bromine.
- Forgetting that the first step with sulfuric acid is acid–base, not redox, for every halide.
- Describing HCl or HBr as "white fumes". They are misty (steamy) fumes.
- Acidifying with hydrochloric acid before a halide test.

## Exam technique

- **"Explain the trend"**: state the trend, then the reason in terms of shells, shielding and attraction.
- **"Give the role of sulfuric acid"**: acid in the first step, oxidising agent in the second.
- **"State observations"**: give colours and physical states (gas, solid, solution), not just product names.
- **"Explain why the silver nitrate is acidified"**: name the interfering ion (carbonate) and what it would do.
