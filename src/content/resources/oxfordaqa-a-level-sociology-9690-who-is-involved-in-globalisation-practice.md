Revise first with the [study guide on who is involved in globalisation](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-sociology-9690-who-is-involved-in-globalisation/) and the [matching revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-sociology-9690-who-is-involved-in-globalisation-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by tied aid. **[2]**

**2.** Identify two UN agencies and state the main area of work of each. **[2]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by a "race to the bottom" between less developed states. **[3]**

**4.** Outline two differences between the role of the International Monetary Fund and the role of UN agencies such as UNICEF. **[4]**

**5.** Outline two reasons why some sociologists argue that the nation state remains powerful in a globalised world. **[4]**

**6.** Study the table, which gives fictional data for three fictional donor countries.

| Donor (fictional) | Gross national income (US$ million) | Aid given (US$ million) |
|---|---|---|
| Velan | 1,500,000 | 4,500 |
| Ostra | 300,000 | 2,700 |
| Tamsk | 800,000 | 2,400 |

**(a)** Calculate aid as a percentage of gross national income for Velan. Show your working. **[2]**
**(b)** Using the table, explain why the largest donor in money terms may not be the most generous donor. **[4]**

### Section B: longer and structured questions

**7.** Read the item.

> The fictional country of Sarnel has created an export processing zone on its coast. A fictional clothing company, Lumeo, has opened a factory there employing 2,000 people. Sarnel's government offered Lumeo ten years without company tax and agreed that workers in the zone may not join trade unions. Lumeo buys its cloth from abroad rather than from Sarnel's own mills. Lumeo's profits are paid to shareholders in its home country.

**(a)** Identify two ways in which Sarnel may benefit from the factory. **[2]**
**(b)** Using the item, analyse how the relationship between Lumeo and Sarnel illustrates the roles of TNCs and less developed states in globalisation. **[6]**

**8.** Explain two ways in which indigenous peoples are involved in globalisation. **[4]**

**9.** Outline three criticisms of structural adjustment programmes. **[6]**

**10.** Discuss whether transnational corporations are now more powerful than nation states. **[8]**

**11.** Assess the view that transnational and UN agencies mainly serve the interests of more developed nation states. **[12]**

**12.** Evaluate the arguments for and against development aid. Use examples from your own society or a society you know about. **[16]**

## Answers

Extended answers (Questions 10, 11 and 12) are shown as indicative creditworthy points. The real exam marks extended responses with levels of response, judging the answer as a whole.

### Section A

**1.** Aid that must be spent on goods or services from the donor country [1], so part of its value returns to the donor's own firms rather than meeting the recipient's needs [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any two, each with a correct area of work: UNICEF and children [1]; UNHCR and refugees [1]; the World Health Organization and health; the International Labour Organization and labour standards; the UN Development Programme and human development. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The IMF and World Bank are part of the UN system in a formal sense but are usually treated separately as lenders; a UN service agency is the safer answer.

**3.** Less developed states compete with each other to attract foreign investment [1] by offering lower taxes, wages or regulations [1], so standards for workers and the environment are pushed down across all of them [1]. **[3]**

**4.** The IMF lends money to countries in financial crisis [1], while UNICEF delivers services and campaigns for children's welfare [1]. The IMF can attach conditions such as spending cuts to its loans, giving it leverage over borrowers [1], while UN agencies depend on voluntary funding and can rarely enforce their targets [1]. **[4]**

**5.** States still control borders, passports and visas [1], so they decide who can enter and work, which global markets cannot override [1]. States still raise taxes and command armies [1], and some, such as China, have used globalisation to increase their power rather than lose it [1]. **[4]**

**6. (a)** 4,500 / 1,500,000 x 100 [1] = 0.3% [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Velan gives the most aid in money terms, US$4,500 million [1], but this is only 0.3% of its income [1]. Ostra gives less money, US$2,700 million, but 0.9% of its income [1], so Ostra is more generous relative to its wealth and is the only one above the UN target of 0.7% [1]. **[4]**

### Section B

**7. (a)** Any two: 2,000 jobs [1]; wages and spending in the local economy [1]; possible skills or experience for workers; export links. **[2]**
**(b)** Sarnel offers ten years without company tax, showing a less developed state competing for investment [1], which supports the "race to the bottom" idea because other states must match such offers [1]. The ban on trade unions shows the state lowering labour standards to attract a TNC [1], so the costs of globalisation fall on workers [1]. Lumeo buys cloth abroad and pays profits to shareholders in its home country [1], so few linkages or gains stay in Sarnel, supporting dependency or Sklair-style views that TNCs serve a transnational capitalist class [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Indigenous land is often targeted for mining, logging, dams or pipelines that supply global markets [1], so indigenous peoples bear many of the costs of economic globalisation [1]. They also organise across borders and use global rights such as UNDRIP's free, prior and informed consent [1], as at Standing Rock in 2016, so they act as participants in globalisation, not only victims [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Conditions required cuts in state spending [1], which reduced health and education budgets in borrowing countries [1]. User fees for public services were introduced [1], which priced the poorest out of clinics and schools [1]. Rapid opening of markets exposed new local industries to foreign competition [1], causing job losses and deepening dependency on raw-material exports [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Indicative content:
- Ohmae and Strange argue power has shifted from states to markets and corporations [1].
- TNCs can move production to wherever costs are lowest, pressuring states [1].
- Sklair: a transnational capitalist class includes globalising politicians as well as executives [1].
- Application: states offering tax holidays and union bans to attract investment [1].
- Counter: states control borders, laws, taxes and armies [1].
- States can regulate or exclude TNCs, as in national data or ownership laws [1].
- Developmental states such as South Korea directed TNC investment to national goals [1].
- Judgement: TNCs outweigh small, indebted states, but not large or strong ones [1].
**[8]**

**11.** Indicative content:
- Voting in the IMF and World Bank is weighted by financial contribution [1].
- So the largest economies have most influence over lending decisions [1].
- Structural adjustment opened markets to Western firms (Washington Consensus) [1].
- Chang: rich states deny poorer ones the protection they once used [1].
- WTO rules favour those with most bargaining power [1].
- Marxist and dependency views see agencies as tools of the core [1].
- Application: own-society or known example of loan conditions or trade rules [1].
- Counter: UN agencies such as WHO delivered global public goods, for example smallpox eradication [1].
- UNDP's Human Development Report broadened development beyond income [1].
- The SDGs were negotiated with less developed states, giving them a voice [1].
- Rising powers such as China now create alternative lenders and influence [1].
- Judgement: lenders largely reflect rich states' interests; UN service agencies less so [1].
**[12]**

**12.** Indicative content:
- Distinguish types: humanitarian, development, bilateral, multilateral, tied [1].
- For: modernisation theory sees aid as capital for take-off [1].
- For: Sachs's "big push" to break the poverty trap [1].
- For: humanitarian aid saves lives in disasters [1].
- For: health aid has supported vaccination and disease control [1].
- Own-society application: a named aid-funded project, such as a school or clinic [1].
- Against (right): Moyo, dependency and corruption [1].
- Aid can weaken governments' accountability to their own taxpayers [1].
- Against (right): Easterly's planners versus searchers [1].
- Against (left): Hayter, aid as imperialism serving donors [1].
- Tied aid returns value to donor firms [1].
- Few donors meet the UN 0.7 per cent target, so the "big push" is rarely tested [1].
- Analysis: emergency aid and long-term budget aid raise different issues [1].
- NGO aid can be more targeted but is accountable to donors [1].
- Own-society application: debate in a known country over loan conditions or aid dependency [1].
- Judgement: aid's value depends on its type, conditions and local control [1].
**[16]**
*Examiner insight:* An aid answer that never names a type of aid cannot reach the top band; the judgement depends on the distinctions.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing the IMF and World Bank with UN service agencies.
- Treating NGOs and grassroots movements as the same thing.
- Leaving an item unused or quoting it without linking it to a concept.
- Comparing donors in money terms only, ignoring share of income.
- Ending an extended answer without a judgement about power.
