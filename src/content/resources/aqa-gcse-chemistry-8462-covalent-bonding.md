This guide covers **4.2.1.4 Covalent bonding** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) specification, for teaching
from September 2016 onwards (version 1.1). It is part of topic 4.2, Bonding, structure, and the properties of
matter, examined on Paper 1 at Foundation and Higher Tier. No statement in it is marked (HT only), and there
is no required practical.

For quick recall use the [covalent bonding revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-covalent-bonding-revision-notes/),

## Why atoms share, and how many bonds each forms

Non-metal atoms need extra electrons to reach the stable arrangement of a noble gas. When two non-metal
atoms bond, neither gives electrons away; instead each provides an electron to a **shared pair**. Each
shared pair counts towards the outer shell of **both** atoms.

The number of bonds an atom usually forms equals the number of electrons it needs to fill its outer shell:

| Atom | Group | Outer electrons | Needs | Usual bonds |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| H | 1 | 1 | 1 (to reach 2) | 1 |
| Cl | 7 | 7 | 1 | 1 |
| O | 6 | 6 | 2 | 2 |
| N | 5 | 5 | 3 | 3 |
| C | 4 | 4 | 4 | 4 |

This table is a checking tool. If your diagram of a molecule gives oxygen three bonds or carbon three, it is
wrong before you look any further.

## Checking a dot and cross diagram

A complete diagram for these eight molecules shows **every outer-shell electron**. Pairs of outer electrons
that are not shared are often called lone pairs. Use three checks.

1. **Total electrons.** Add up the outer electrons of all the atoms. The diagram must show exactly that
   many.
2. **Full shells.** Count every electron in or touching each atom's circle: 2 for hydrogen, 8 for the
   others.
3. **Bonds in the overlap.** Every shared pair sits where the circles overlap, with one dot and one cross.

| Molecule | Total outer electrons | Shared pairs | Lone pairs |
|---|---|---|---|
| H₂ | 2 | 1 | 0 |
| Cl₂ | 14 | 1 | 3 on each Cl |
| O₂ | 12 | 2 (a double bond) | 2 on each O |
| N₂ | 10 | 3 (a triple bond) | 1 on each N |
| HCl | 8 | 1 | 3 on the Cl |
| H₂O | 8 | 2 | 2 on the O |
| NH₃ | 8 | 3 | 1 on the N |
| CH₄ | 8 | 4 | 0 |

### Worked example 1: oxygen, O₂

*Draw a dot and cross diagram for an oxygen molecule, showing outer electrons only.*

```
Each O has 6 outer electrons and needs 2 more  -> 2 shared pairs (double bond)
Total electrons to show: 6 + 6 = 12
In the overlap: two pairs, each one dot + one cross  (4 electrons)
Outside the overlap on each O: two lone pairs        (4 electrons each)
Check: 4 + 4 + 4 = 12 shown; each O counts 4 shared + 4 own = 8
```

A frequent error is drawing a single shared pair and then adding extra non-bonding electrons to make
the numbers look right. Run check 1: a single bond plus six lone electrons each gives 2 + 6 + 6 = 14, which
is too many.

### Worked example 2: methane, CH₄

```
C has 4 outer electrons (crosses); each H has 1 (dots)
Total: 4 + 4 x 1 = 8
Four overlaps, each with one dot and one cross; no lone pairs
Check: C counts 8; each H counts 2
```

## Small molecules, polymers or giant structures?

| Type | What a diagram shows | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Small molecules | Separate groups of a few atoms, each group the same | H₂O, CO₂, NH₃, CH₄, Cl₂ |
| Polymers (very large molecules) | A long chain, or a repeat unit in brackets with *n* | Poly(ethene) |
| Giant covalent structures | A network of atoms joined by bonds in every direction, continuing past the edge | Diamond, graphite, silicon dioxide |

From a formula, small molecules usually have a formula made only of non-metals with small numbers:
for example SO₂, HBr, C₂H₆ and I₂. The giant covalent structures named in the specification are diamond
and silicon dioxide; graphite and graphene appear later in topic 4.2.

## Using lines for bonds

A single line is one shared pair. A double line is two shared pairs, and a triple line is three.

- **Small molecule:** draw every atom and every bond, for example H–Cl or O=O.
- **Polymer repeat unit:** draw the unit's atoms, put square brackets round it with a bond line passing
  through each bracket, and write *n* after the closing bracket. *n* is a large number.
- **Part of a giant structure:** draw a section of the network with bond lines, and leave bonds running off
  the edge to show the structure continues.

```
Part of a silicon dioxide network (2D sketch):

  –O–Si–O–Si–O–
     |    |
     O    O
     |    |
  –O–Si–O–Si–O–
```

Each silicon atom in the real structure bonds to four oxygen atoms and each oxygen to two silicon atoms,
which is why the formula is SiO₂. A flat sketch cannot show all four bonds around silicon in 3D, which is
itself a limitation worth stating.

## Deducing a molecular formula

A **molecular formula** gives the **actual** number of atoms of each element in one molecule. Unlike an
ionic empirical formula, you do **not** simplify it.

### Worked example 3: hydrogen peroxide

*A ball and stick model shows two oxygen atoms bonded to each other, with one hydrogen atom bonded to each
oxygen. Give the molecular formula.*

```
Count: H = 2, O = 2
Molecular formula: H2O2  (written H₂O₂)
Do NOT simplify to HO: that would describe a different number of atoms per molecule
Check bonds: each O has 2 bonds (O–O and O–H); each H has 1
```

When a model has several kinds of atom, write the counts in the order the question uses, or C, then H, then
the others for carbon compounds.

## Limitations of each representation

| Representation | Shows well | Does not show |
|---|---|---|
| Dot and cross | Which electrons are shared and where each came from | The 3D shape of the molecule |
| Displayed formula (lines) | Which atoms are bonded, and single, double or triple bonds | Lone pairs, the 3D shape |
| Ball and stick | The 3D shape and the bond angles | Relative atom sizes; the sticks are not physical rods |
| Space-filling | Relative sizes and overall shape | The individual bonds, which are hidden |

## Exam technique

- **Draw:** use dots for one atom's electrons and crosses for the other's; show outer shells only unless
  told otherwise; overlapping circles are fine.
- **Recognise / Identify:** give the category (small molecule, polymer, giant covalent) and point to the
  diagram feature that told you.
- **Deduce:** show your count of each atom before writing the formula.

## Common mistakes

- Simplifying a molecular formula (writing CH₃ for C₂H₆).
- Drawing nitrogen with a single bond: N₂ needs a triple bond.
- Placing shared pairs outside the overlap, or giving a shared pair two dots.
- Calling silicon dioxide a small molecule because its formula is short.
- Forgetting *n* or the extended bond lines on a polymer repeat unit.
