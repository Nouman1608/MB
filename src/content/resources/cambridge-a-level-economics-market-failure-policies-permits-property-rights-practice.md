## Questions

**1.** Define government failure. **[2]**

**2.** In Country P, three firms each emit 90 tonnes of waste a year. The government caps total emissions at 180 tonnes and gives each firm 60 tradable permits. Each tonne cut costs firm P 8, firm Q 5 and firm R 2. Firm R can cut at most 80 tonnes.

**(a)** Calculate the total cost of meeting the cap if no trading is allowed and each firm cuts the same amount. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the total cost when permits are traded and the saving from trading. **[3]**
**(c)** State the permit price that clears the market. **[1]**

**3.** Explain two reasons why a tradable permit scheme might not achieve an efficient allocation of resources. **[4]**

**4.** Shared pasture in Country S is open to all. Each cow costs 28 to keep for a season. Total milk value for herds of 1 to 6 cows is 50, 90, 120, 140, 150 and 150.

**(a)** Find the herd size under open access. Show your working. **[3]**
**(b)** Find the efficient herd size. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the loss of net value caused by open access. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain how a clearly defined private property right would change the outcome. **[2]**

**5.** A state-owned bus company in Country T faces demand P = 60 - Q (Q in thousands of journeys), fixed cost 300 (thousand) and constant marginal cost 10.

**(a)** Find output, price and profit if the state prices at marginal cost. **[2]**
**(b)** Find output, price and profit if the firm is privatised and maximises profit. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the deadweight loss under the private monopoly. **[2]**
**(d)** State one argument for privatisation despite your results. **[1]**

**6.** In Country U, monthly sales of a snack were 800,000 units. After a health-information campaign costing 360,000, sales were 752,000.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage fall in sales. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the cost per unit of consumption avoided. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain two reasons why information alone may not correct overconsumption. **[2]**

**7.** A school canteen moves fruit to eye level. Daily fruit sales rise from 120 to 156.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage increase. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why this is an example of a nudge. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain two limitations of nudges. **[2]**

**8.** A firm has 5,000 workers. Under opt-in pension enrolment, 35% join. Under automatic enrolment, 80% remain.

**(a)** Calculate the extra members, the percentage increase in members and the percentage-point change. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why the default option changes behaviour. **[2]**

**9.** In Country V, the marginal abatement cost of cutting a pollutant is MAC = 3q and the marginal social benefit is MSB = 150 - 2q, where q is thousand tonnes cut. Net benefit equals total benefit minus total cost, which is 150q - q² - 1.5q².

**(a)** Find the efficient cut and the marginal cost at that point. **[2]**
**(b)** The regulator orders a cut of 40. Calculate the net benefit at 30, at 40, and the welfare loss. **[3]**
**(c)** Identify and explain a likely cause of this government failure. **[2]**

**10.** Explain two causes of government failure, with an example of each. **[6]**

**11.** The government of Country W plans to privatise a state-owned electricity grid that is a natural monopoly. Evaluate whether this is the best way to improve the use of resources. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Government intervention in a market [1] that leads to a misallocation of resources, so the net welfare gain is smaller than it could be or becomes a net welfare loss, or that creates new problems [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* answers that only say "the policy did not work" miss the comparison with a more efficient outcome, which the definition needs.

**2. (a)** Each firm cuts 90 - 60 = 30 tonnes [1]. Cost = 30 x 8 + 30 x 5 + 30 x 2 = 240 + 150 + 60 = **450** [1].
**(b)** Total cut needed is 90. R cuts its maximum, 80, at 2 = 160 [1]. Q cuts the remaining 10 at 5 = 50, and P cuts none [1]. Total 210, saving 450 - 210 = **240** [1].
**(c)** **5**, the marginal cutter Q's cost [1]. R sells 50 permits and P and Q buy 30 and 20. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* show the number cut by each firm so that your method is visible and easy to check.

**3.** Reason one: the regulator sets the cap with imperfect information [1], so it may be too loose or too tight and cost more than the benefit gained [1]. Reason two: free initial allocation favours existing polluters and may deter entry [1], while large firms may hoard permits to block rivals [1]. Other valid reasons: pollution clustering where permits are bought, and costly monitoring. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* the strongest answers tie each weakness to why efficiency is missed. Listing four brief weaknesses earns less than developing two.

**4. (a)** Revenue per cow = 50, 45, 40, 35, 30, 25 [1]. Cows enter while revenue per cow is at least 28 [1]. At 5 cows it is 30, at 6 cows 25, so **5 cows** [1].
**(b)** Extra value from each cow: 50, 40, 30, 20, 10, 0 [1]. Third cow adds 30 > 28, fourth adds 20 < 28, so **3 cows** [1].
**(c)** Net value at 3 cows = 120 - 84 = 36 [1]. At 5 cows = 150 - 140 = 10. Loss = **26** [1].
**(d)** One owner, or a rights system, bears the cost each extra cow imposes on others through lower output per cow [1], so the herd is limited where extra value equals cost [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* separate revenue per cow from extra value. Using one column for both gives one herd size and loses the contrast the question tests.

**5. (a)** MC pricing: 60 - Q = 10, so Q = **50**, P = **10** [1]. Profit = 0 x 50 - 300 = **-300** [1].
**(b)** MR = 60 - 2Q = 10, so Q = **25** [1]. P = 60 - 25 = **35** [1]. Profit = (35 - 10) x 25 - 300 = **325** [1].
**(c)** DWL = 1/2 x (35 - 10) x (50 - 25) [1] = **312.5** [1].
**(d)** For example: the firm has an incentive to cut costs, or the state avoids a subsidy of 300 [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* use marginal revenue, not demand, when finding the profit-maximising output. The triangle needs the gap between price and marginal cost, not the profit.

**6. (a)** Fall = 800,000 - 752,000 = 48,000 [1]. 48,000 / 800,000 = **6%** [1].
**(b)** 360,000 / 48,000 = **7.50** [1].
**(c)** Addicted or present-biased consumers may ignore it [1]. Its effects may fade over time [1]. (Also valid: it does not reduce harm to third parties.) **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* cost per unit avoided divides the cost by the change in units, not by total sales.

**7. (a)** Rise = 36 [1]. 36 / 120 = **30%** [1].
**(b)** It changes the choice architecture, how options are presented [1] while no option is banned and the price is unchanged [1].
**(c)** The effect may be small or temporary [1]. It cannot tackle large external costs on its own [1]. (Also valid: it may be seen as manipulation.) **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* tie "nudge" to the unchanged price and open choice. Describing the move as encouragement alone earns little.

**8. (a)** Members rise from 1,750 to 4,000, extra **2,250** [1]. 2,250 / 1,750 = **128.6%** [1]. Percentage points: 80 - 35 = **45** [1].
**(b)** People tend to stick with the default option because of inertia [1], and leaving needs effort, so most stay [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* percentage change and percentage-point change are different answers. Giving both, labelled, prevents confusion.

**9. (a)** 3q = 150 - 2q, so q = **30** [1]. MAC = **90** [1].
**(b)** Net benefit at 30 = 4,500 - 900 - 1,350 = **2,250** [1]. At 40 = 6,000 - 1,600 - 2,400 = **2,000** [1]. Welfare loss = **250** [1].
**(c)** Imperfect information [1]: the regulator overestimated the marginal social benefit or underestimated abatement costs, so ordered too large a cut [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* the efficient point is where MAC equals MSB. A larger cut adds more to cost than to benefit, so overshooting lowers net benefit.

**10.** Imperfect information: a regulator sets a cap or tax at the wrong level [1], for example ordering a cut whose cost exceeds its benefit [1], which wastes resources [1]. Political pressure: ministers favour visible short-term measures or yield to lobbying [1], for example protecting a vocal industry rather than the most valuable use of resources [1], which misallocates resources [1]. Also accept administrative cost, unintended consequences and regulatory capture. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* name a mechanism for each cause. A list of causes with no example reads as a definition.

**11.** A natural monopoly has high fixed costs, so one firm serving the market is efficient [1]. Privatisation alone leaves the monopoly [1], so the owner can raise price and cut output [1], causing deadweight loss [1]. The profit motive may cut costs [1], but the gain only matters if savings exceed the welfare loss [1]. The sale raises revenue and ends any subsidy [1]. State ownership may bring political interference and weak cost control [1], though it may price nearer marginal cost [1]. Judgement: the answer depends on how far competition or the design of the sale limits market power, and on how large the cost falls are [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* a verdict that turns on a stated condition gains more than a one-sided list.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating costs without showing the cut by each firm.
- Setting the permit price without reference to the marginal cutter.
- Using average revenue instead of marginal revenue to find the efficient number.
- Confusing percentage change with percentage-point change.
- Treating nudges as bans or taxes.
- Treating privatisation as a cure without discussing market structure.
- Calling any bad outcome government failure without a comparison with a more efficient outcome.
- Evaluation that is generic rather than tied to the context.
