These questions cover two bullets of content area 2.1.4, "Validity of contracts": misrepresentation, including the Misrepresentation Act 1967 and the Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act 1945, and mistake.

---

## Section A

**1.** Define a misrepresentation. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why a statement of opinion is not usually a misrepresentation, and when it may be treated as one. **[3]**

**3.** Identify **two** situations in which silence can amount to a misrepresentation. **[2]**

**4.** Explain the effect of section 2(1) of the Misrepresentation Act 1967 on the burden of proof. **[2]**

**5.** State **three** bars to rescission. **[3]**

## Section B

**6.** Priya buys a second-hand van from Tom, a dealer, after Tom says, "It's done 40,000 miles." The real figure is 140,000; Tom's records showed this, but he did not check them. Priya did not inspect the logbook, which she was offered.

Advise Priya on whether she has a claim for misrepresentation and what remedies are available. **[8]**

**7.** Explain the difference between a contract that is void and one that is voidable, and why the difference matters to a third party. **[4]**

**8.** Explain how the Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act 1945 applies to claims for misrepresentation, using **two** decided cases. **[4]**

**9.** Lena agrees to buy a painting from Max. Unknown to both, the painting was destroyed in a fire the night before.

**(a)** Explain the effect of this mistake on the contract. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain how the answer would differ if the painting existed but both parties wrongly believed it was by a famous artist. **[3]**

**10.** A rogue visits Ahmed's shop, claims to be a well-known actor, and pays for a watch with a cheque that later bounces. The rogue sells the watch to Grace, who knows nothing of this.

Advise Ahmed on whether he can recover the watch from Grace. **[5]**

**11.** Explain **two** differences between fraudulent misrepresentation and negligent misrepresentation under section 2(1). **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate whether the law on misrepresentation strikes a fair balance between the parties to a contract. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** An **untrue statement of fact** made by one party to the other [1] before or at the time of the contract, which **induces** the other to enter into it [1].

**2.** An opinion is a statement of belief, not a fact that can be true or false, so an honest opinion is not a misrepresentation (*Bisset v Wilkinson* [1927]) [1]. It may be treated as a statement of fact where the maker has **special knowledge or expertise** [1], as in *Esso Petroleum v Mardon* [1976], where Esso's experienced staff forecast sales [1].

**3.** Any two [1] each: a **half-truth** (*Dimmock v Hallett*); a **change of circumstances** before the contract (*With v O'Flanagan*); a contract of **utmost good faith**, such as insurance; a **fiduciary** relationship.

**4.** The defendant is liable for a misrepresentation **unless they prove** [1] that they had **reasonable grounds to believe, and did believe**, the statement was true up to the time of the contract [1].

**5.** Any three [1] each: **affirmation** (*Long v Lloyd*); **lapse of time** (*Leaf v International Galleries*); **restitution impossible** (*Clarke v Dickson*); **third-party rights** acquired in good faith.

**6.** **Statement of fact**: the mileage is a verifiable fact, not opinion [1]. **Untrue** [1]. **Inducement**: Priya bought on the strength of it [1]; her failure to inspect the logbook does not prevent reliance (*Redgrave v Hurd* (1881)) [1]. **Type**: Tom did not know it was false, so probably not fraud under *Derry v Peek*, but under **s 2(1)** he must prove reasonable grounds for belief; his own records showed the true figure, so, as in *Howard Marine v Ogden* [1978], he cannot [1]. **Remedies**: **rescission** (return the van for her money) if she acts promptly and does not affirm [1]; **damages** under s 2(1) measured as if fraudulent (*Royscot v Rogerson* [1991]) [1]; Tom may argue for a reduction under the **1945 Act** because she did not check the logbook (*Gran Gelato*), though the court may decide that a dealer's statement could reasonably be relied on [1].

**7.** A **void** contract has **no legal effect from the start** [1]; a **voidable** contract is **valid until rescinded** by the innocent party [1]. If goods are sold under a void contract, the buyer gets no title and cannot pass any to a **third party** [1]; under a voidable contract a third party who buys in good faith **before rescission** gets good title [1].

**8.** Under the 1945 Act damages can be **reduced** where the claimant was partly at fault [1]. In *Gran Gelato v Richcliff* [1992] the court accepted that this could apply to a claim under **s 2(1)** where the defendant was also liable in negligence [1]. In *Standard Chartered Bank v Pakistan National Shipping* [2002] the House of Lords held there is **no reduction for fraudulent** misrepresentation [1], because a dishonest defendant cannot rely on the victim's carelessness [1].

**9. (a)** This is a **common mistake as to the existence of the subject matter** (*res extincta*) [1]. As in *Couturier v Hastie* (1856), where the cargo no longer existed [1], the contract is **void**, so Lena owes nothing and can recover any money paid [1].
**(b)** This is a **common mistake as to quality** [1], which is almost never operative: in *Bell v Lever Bros* [1932] and *Leaf v International Galleries* [1950] the contracts stood [1]. Under *Great Peace Shipping* [2002] it is void only if the mistake makes the contract essentially different, so the contract is probably **valid** [1].

**10.** This is a **unilateral mistake as to identity** [1]. Because the dealing was **face to face**, Ahmed is presumed to intend to contract with the person in front of him [1], so, as in *Phillips v Brooks* [1919] and *Lewis v Averay* [1972], the contract is **voidable** for fraudulent misrepresentation, not void [1]. The rogue therefore obtained voidable title and sold to Grace, who bought **in good faith before Ahmed rescinded** [1]. Grace keeps the watch; Ahmed's remedy is against the rogue (*Ingram v Little* [1961] is an exceptional case and unlikely to be followed) [1].

**11.** For each of two differences: difference identified [1] and explained [1].

- **State of mind** [1]: fraud requires knowledge, no belief or recklessness (*Derry v Peek*), while s 2(1) applies to a careless defendant [1].
- **Burden of proof** [1]: in fraud the claimant must prove dishonesty, which is difficult; under s 2(1) the defendant must prove reasonable belief [1].
- **Contributory negligence** [1]: damages may be reduced under s 2(1) (*Gran Gelato*) but never in fraud (*Standard Chartered*) [1].

**12.** Levels-based answer. Credit:

- **AO1**: elements (statement of fact; inducement); types (fraud, s 2(1), common law, innocent); remedies (rescission, damages, s 2(2)); bars; s 3; the 1945 Act.
- **Fair to the claimant**: reversed burden under s 2(1); *Redgrave v Hurd* protects those who do not check; *Royscot* gives generous damages; s 3 prevents unfair exclusion.
- **Fair to the defendant**: opinions and puffs are not actionable (*Bisset*); no reliance if the claimant relied on their own checks (*Attwood v Small*); the 1945 Act can reduce damages; s 2(2) lets the court award damages instead of rescission for innocent misrepresentation.
- **Criticisms**: *Royscot* treats a careless defendant as a fraudster; the rules on silence may let sellers conceal important facts; bars to rescission can leave a claimant with only damages.
- **Evaluation**: reach a conclusion, for example that the law leans towards the misled party, which is justified because the maker of the statement controls its accuracy, though *Royscot* goes further than necessary.

Top-level answers use cases accurately, consider both parties and reach a justified conclusion.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating the mileage or similar facts as opinion.
- Forgetting the reversed burden of proof under s 2(1).
- Applying contributory negligence to fraud.
- Calling a mistake as to quality operative.
- Treating every identity mistake as making the contract void.
