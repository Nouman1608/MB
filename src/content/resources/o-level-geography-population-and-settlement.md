This guide covers **Theme 1 Population and Settlement**, for Cambridge
O Level Geography 2217, 2026 (Version 3), the first of three themes
that together make up the full syllabus. 2217 shares an identical
three-theme, 19-sub-topic structure with Cambridge IGCSE Geography
0460, independently confirmed against 2217's own specification PDF —
useful to know if a student's centre offers both qualifications, since
the underlying content maps directly across the two.
Exams for 2217 are available in the November series only, a scheduling
detail worth confirming with a school early, since it affects how a
whole year's revision calendar should be planned.

## Where this fits in 2217

Population and Settlement is the first of three themes, alongside The
Natural Environment and Economic Development. It is the theme most
directly about people rather than physical processes, and — as with
its IGCSE sibling — every sub-topic is examined with reference to
named, real-world case studies rather than abstract theory alone. This
positions Theme 1 as the syllabus's human-geography anchor, with the
techniques and case-study habits built here carrying forward into how
the other two, more physically-focused themes are also approached.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE O LEVEL GEOGRAPHY 2217 — THEME 1 POPULATION AND
SETTLEMENT**

- 1.1 Population Dynamics — the causes of rapid population growth and
change, including birth and death rates and the concept of natural increase
- 1.2 Migration — the causes and consequences of national and
international migration, both voluntary and forced, examined through
push and pull factors
- 1.3 Population Structure — the causes and consequences of different
and changing population structures, read through population pyramids
and the dependency ratio they help calculate
- 1.4 Population Density and Distribution — the physical and human
factors influencing where people live and at what density, and how the
two categories of factor interact
- 1.5 Settlements (Rural and Urban) and Service Provision — the
factors influencing the sites, growth and functions of settlements,
and the pattern and provision of services within them, connected through
the concept of settlement hierarchy
- 1.6 Urban Settlements — land use patterns within urban areas, and the
problems associated with rapid urban growth, particularly in developing
countries, drawing directly on named city case studies
- 1.7 Urbanisation — the causes and consequences of urbanisation, and
strategies for managing its effects, examined at both national and
city scale

## How to approach it

As with the IGCSE variant of this syllabus, every sub-topic here is
assessed with reference to specific named case studies, so revision
should be organised case-study first: know two or three concrete,
detailed examples per sub-topic — a real city, a real migration flow, a
real country's population structure — rather than only the abstract
definitions, since questions consistently ask candidates to "describe"
or "explain, using examples." A named case study with only vague
detail (a country name and nothing else) earns far less credit than
one supported by specific facts and figures, so building depth into a
small number of case studies is a better investment of revision time
than spreading effort thinly across many. Population pyramids deserve dedicated
practice, both as a stand-alone reading skill and as a bridge to other
sub-topics: a youthful pyramid connects directly to high birth rates in
1.1 and to pressure on services in 1.5, so practise tracing those links
across sub-topics. Being able to read a pyramid's shape, explain the
population dynamics behind it, and connect it forward to a real
consequence (service pressure, migration pattern, dependency ratio) in
a single answer is a genuinely higher-level skill than reading the
pyramid alone. Because 2217 is only examined in the November
series, plan a revision timetable that works backwards from that single
sitting rather than assuming the multiple entry points some other
qualifications offer — there is no equivalent June resit opportunity
to fall back on if preparation runs behind schedule.

## Reading population pyramids fluently

A population pyramid's overall shape reveals a country's stage of development at a glance: a wide
base and narrow top (a "true pyramid" shape) indicates high birth rates and a young population,
typically associated with earlier stages of development; a more rectangular shape with a narrower
base indicates lower birth rates and an ageing population, typically associated with later stages of
development. Beyond overall shape, examine specific bulges or narrowings — a sudden narrowing at a
particular age band can indicate a past event (war, famine, emigration) affecting that specific
cohort, while a bulge can indicate a past baby boom or in-migration of a particular age group.
Reading a pyramid this way — shape first, then specific irregularities and their likely causes —
is what distinguishes strong analysis from a purely descriptive account of "more males than females
in this age group."

## Connecting settlement and service provision

1.5's settlement content and service provision are examined together because they're causally
linked: a settlement's size, function and connectivity (its site and situation) directly determine
what services it can support — a larger, more central settlement typically has a wider range and
higher order of services than a small, isolated one. This relationship, sometimes described through
settlement hierarchy, is worth understanding as cause and effect rather than as two separate lists
of facts to memorise, since exam questions frequently ask candidates to explain *why* a settlement
has (or lacks) a particular service, which requires this causal reasoning rather than description
alone.
