## What 5.6 covers

The syllabus lists: why businesses keep different types of accounting records
rather than using the full double entry system; the advantages and disadvantages
of not maintaining a full set of records; how to prepare opening and closing
statements of affairs; how to calculate profit or loss from changes in capital
over time; how to calculate sales, purchases, gross profit, trade receivables and
trade payables and other figures from incomplete information; how to prepare
statements of profit or loss and statements of financial position from incomplete
records; adjustments as in 5.1; and how to apply mark-up, margin and inventory
turnover to arrive at missing figures.

Questions are set **only on sole traders**, and not on service or manufacturing
businesses.

## Why records are incomplete

Many small traders keep only a bank account, a file of unpaid invoices and a note
of cash takings. Full double entry needs time, skill or an accountant's fee, and the
owner may feel that knowing the bank balance is enough.

The type of record kept depends on the business. A trader who sells only for cash
and buys from a few suppliers may manage with a cash book alone (**single entry**).
One with many credit customers needs at least a record of who owes what. A trader
who wants a bank loan, or who must report profit for tax, has a stronger reason to
keep full double entry records, often with accounting software that completes the
double entry automatically.

| Advantages of not keeping full records | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Cheaper and quicker | Profit is not known until someone reconstructs the figures |
| Needs little bookkeeping knowledge | Errors and theft are harder to detect; there is no trial balance |
| Enough for a very small, cash-based business | Hard to control receivables and payables or set prices |
| | Lenders and tax authorities may not accept the figures |

## Worked example: Joshi Homewares

Leena Joshi runs a homewares stall. She keeps a bank account and lists of amounts
owed.

**Assets and liabilities**

| | 1 January 2028 | 31 December 2028 |
|---|---|---|
| Fixtures (valuation) | 6,000 | 6,700 |
| Inventory | 4,800 | 5,600 |
| Trade receivables | 1,250 | 1,650 |
| Rent prepaid | 300 | 100 |
| Bank | 2,300 | 250 |
| Trade payables | 2,100 | 2,700 |
| Wages accrued | – | 250 |

**Bank summary for 2028**

| Receipts | | Payments | |
|---|---|---|---|
| From credit customers | 18,400 | To suppliers | 39,500 |
| Cash sales banked | 41,600 | Rent | 4,000 |
| Capital introduced | 3,000 | Wages | 6,200 |
| | | General expenses | 1,850 |
| | | Drawings | 12,000 |
| | | New display unit (fixtures) | 1,500 |

### Method 1: profit from the change in capital

```
Statement of affairs              1 Jan 2028     31 Dec 2028
Fixtures                               6,000           6,700
Inventory                              4,800           5,600
Trade receivables                      1,250           1,650
Rent prepaid                             300             100
Bank                                   2,300             250
                                      14,650          14,300
Trade payables                        (2,100)         (2,700)
Wages accrued                              –            (250)
Capital                               12,550          11,350
```

Capital fell by 1,200, but that is not a loss. Leena took out 12,000 and paid in
3,000.

```
Closing capital                         11,350
Less opening capital                   (12,550)
                                        (1,200)
Add drawings                            12,000
Less capital introduced                 (3,000)
Profit for the year                      7,800
```

Drawings are added back because they reduced capital without being a loss;
capital introduced is deducted because it raised capital without being a profit.

### Method 2: rebuilding the statements

```
Credit sales = received 18,400 + closing receivables 1,650 − opening 1,250 = 18,800
Total sales  = 18,800 + cash sales 41,600                                 = 60,400
Purchases    = paid 39,500 + closing payables 2,700 − opening 2,100       = 40,100
Rent         = paid 4,000 + opening prepaid 300 − closing prepaid 100     =  4,200
Wages        = paid 6,200 + closing accrual 250                           =  6,450
Depreciation = 6,000 + 1,500 − 6,700 (revaluation)                        =    800
```

```
Joshi Homewares: Statement of profit or loss for the year ended 31 December 2028
Revenue                                                    60,400
Opening inventory                             4,800
Purchases                                    40,100
                                             44,900
Closing inventory                            (5,600)       39,300
Gross profit                                               21,100
Rent                                          4,200
Wages                                         6,450
General expenses                              1,850
Depreciation of fixtures                        800        13,300
Profit for the year                                         7,800
```

Both methods give **7,800**. Method 1 is quicker, but only method 2 shows revenue,
gross profit and each expense, so Leena can see her margin (21,100 ÷ 60,400
= 34.93%) and which costs are rising. The closing capital section reads: opening
12,550 + capital introduced 3,000 + profit 7,800 − drawings 12,000 = 11,350.

## Finding missing figures with ratios

The syllabus formulas are: **mark-up** = gross profit ÷ cost of sales × 100;
**gross profit margin** = gross profit ÷ revenue × 100; **rate of inventory turnover** = cost of
sales ÷ average inventory.

**Mark-up to margin.** A mark-up of 50% means cost 100, profit 50, selling price
150, so the margin is 50 ÷ 150 = 33⅓%. A margin of 20% means selling price 100,
profit 20, cost 80, so the mark-up is 20 ÷ 80 = 25%.

**Worked example.** Ravi Sood's revenue was 72,000 and he uses a mark-up of 50%.
His rate of inventory turnover is 8 times and opening inventory was 5,400.

```
Cost of sales        72,000 × 100/150          = 48,000
Gross profit         72,000 − 48,000           = 24,000
Average inventory    48,000 ÷ 8                =  6,000
Closing inventory    (6,000 × 2) − 5,400       =  6,600
Purchases            48,000 + 6,600 − 5,400    = 49,200
```

Each step uses one formula rearranged. Writing the formula before the numbers
earns method marks if an earlier figure is wrong.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the change in capital as the profit, without adjusting for drawings and
  capital introduced.
- Adding capital introduced instead of deducting it.
- Using cash received from customers as sales.
- Leaving prepayments and accruals out of the statements of affairs.
- Applying a mark-up percentage to revenue, or a margin to cost of sales.
- Forgetting cash sales when calculating total revenue.

## Exam technique

- **Prepare** statements of affairs in a clear two-column layout with a heading and
  date.
- **Calculate** missing figures with a short labelled working, or a reconstructed
  account if you prefer.
- **Explain** advantages and disadvantages for the trader in the question, for
  example "Leena cannot see that wages have risen without full records".
