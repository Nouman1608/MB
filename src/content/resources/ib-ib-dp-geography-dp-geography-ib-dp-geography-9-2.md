The [9.2 revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-consequences-of-global-climate-change-revision-notes/) already summarise the physical processes behind sea-level rise, rainfall change and glacier retreat, and compare Bangladesh with the Netherlands. Practise with the [9.2 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-dp-geography-ib-dp-geography-9-2-practice/); the [Global Climate study guide](/resources/ib-dp-geography-global-climate/) covers the whole unit.

## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Explain how global climate change affects physical systems: the hydrological cycle, the cryosphere, the oceans and the biosphere.
- Explain how those physical changes affect people and places: food, water, health, settlement, migration and economies.
- Show that consequences vary between places and between groups of people, and that some places may gain in some respects.

## Organising consequences: two useful splits

**Sudden-onset and slow-onset.** Some consequences arrive as events: heatwaves, floods, intense storms and wildfires. Others build gradually: sea-level rise, glacier retreat, ocean acidification, desertification and the shift of species ranges. Slow-onset change is easy to under-rate because no single day looks like a disaster, yet it can make a place permanently harder to live in. Strong answers include both kinds.

**Direct and cascading.** A direct consequence follows immediately from the physical change, such as crop failure during a drought. A cascading consequence travels onwards through connected systems: crop failure raises food prices, which pushes poorer households to sell livestock or withdraw children from school, which reduces their ability to cope with the next drought. Tracing one cascade in three or four linked steps is far more convincing than listing ten unconnected impacts.

## Relative sea-level rise

Global sea level rises through thermal expansion and the melting of land ice. What matters for a particular coast is **relative sea-level change**: the change in sea level compared with the land. If the land is sinking, relative rise is faster than the global average.

Land can sink because of natural sediment compaction in deltas, or because people pump groundwater or extract gas from beneath it. Jakarta in Indonesia is a widely studied case: heavy groundwater pumping has made parts of the city subside, worsening coastal flooding. The Indonesian government announced in 2019 that it would move the capital to a new city on Borneo, citing Jakarta's congestion and flooding among its reasons. A coast where the land is rising, such as parts of Scandinavia still rebounding after the last ice sheets melted, experiences slower relative rise.

### Worked example: rates that add up

Rates are invented for practice.

```
Delta city:   global sea-level rise    3.5 mm per year
              local subsidence        10.0 mm per year
              relative rise           13.5 mm per year
              over 30 years           13.5 x 30 = 405 mm, about 0.4 m

Rising coast: global sea-level rise    3.5 mm per year
              local land uplift        -2.0 mm per year
              relative rise            1.5 mm per year
              over 30 years            1.5 x 30 = 45 mm
```

The same global change produces a relative rise nine times faster in the delta city (13.5 / 1.5 = 9). The lesson to write: a coastline's exposure depends on local processes as well as global ones, and in subsiding cities human groundwater use can matter more than climate change in the short term.

## Consequences by system

| System | Physical change | Consequences for people |
|---|---|---|
| Hydrological cycle | More intense downpours; longer dry spells in some regions; earlier snowmelt | Flash floods; drought; reservoirs filling at the wrong time of year for farmers |
| Cryosphere | Glacier retreat; thinning sea ice; thawing permafrost | Less reliable dry-season river flow; Arctic coastal erosion; damage to buildings and pipelines |
| Oceans | Warming; acidification; changing currents | Coral bleaching; shellfish harmed; fish stocks moving to cooler water |
| Biosphere | Species moving polewards and uphill; changed timing of seasons | Pests and disease vectors reaching new areas; mismatches between pollinators and flowering |

## Examples that show uneven consequences

- **Arctic coasts.** Shishmaref, an Iñupiat village on a barrier island in Alaska, voted in 2016 to relocate because thinner sea ice and thawing ground leave the island exposed to storm erosion. The move depends on funding that is hard to secure, showing how slow-onset change becomes a question of who pays.
- **South Asian heat.** A severe heatwave in 2015 killed thousands of people across India and Pakistan, where Karachi was badly affected. Outdoor workers, older people and residents of crowded housing without cooling faced the greatest risk.
- **Shifting fish stocks.** As North Atlantic waters warmed, mackerel became more abundant further north and west, into Icelandic and Faroese waters. From around 2010 this led to a long dispute over catch shares with the European Union and Norway, sometimes called the "mackerel war": a physical consequence turned into a political one.
- **Andean water.** Retreating glaciers in the tropical Andes first increase meltwater, then reduce it as the ice shrinks. Farmers and cities that rely on dry-season river flow lose a natural water store.

## Winners and losers

Answers that present every consequence as negative everywhere lose analytical credit. At high latitudes, longer growing seasons may allow new crops; shrinking Arctic sea ice may open shipping routes; higher carbon dioxide can raise the growth of some plants where water and nutrients allow. These gains are usually limited and come with new risks, and they tend to fall in wealthier regions, while the heaviest losses fall on places that emitted least. Making this point, with an example, is an easy way to show balance.

## Annotated model answer

*Examine the consequences of glacier retreat for people living downstream.* **(sample paragraph)**

"In the short term, faster melting increases river flow from glaciers in ranges such as the tropical Andes, which can temporarily raise water supply and also flood risk from glacial lakes **[short-term effect, including a risk]**. As glaciers shrink further, however, meltwater declines, so rivers carry less water in the dry season, when farmers and cities depend on it most **[long-term reversal: shows time scale]**. The consequences are uneven: large cities can invest in reservoirs and pipelines, while small farmers with no storage lose irrigation water **[different groups, different capacity]**. Glacier retreat therefore changes the timing and reliability of water rather than only its total amount, and its effects depend on people's ability to store water **[examined conclusion]**."

## Common mistakes

- Listing consequences with no causal link back to a physical process.
- Using global sea-level figures for a coast where local subsidence or uplift dominates.
- Forgetting slow-onset consequences, or treating them as less serious because they are gradual.
- Describing all consequences as negative everywhere, with no consideration of uneven gains and losses.
- Giving death tolls or costs you cannot support. If unsure, say "thousands" or "estimates vary".

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a map or graph of projected change by giving the pattern, the extremes with values, and any anomaly.
- **Explain** a consequence as a chain: physical change, effect on a system, effect on people.
- **Examine** rewards considering time scale (short-term and long-term), spatial scale (local to global) and different groups.
- **To what extent** questions on whether climate change is the main cause of a problem need you to weigh other causes, such as groundwater pumping or poor land management, before reaching a judgement.
- Keep your case studies located and dated, and know at least one consequence that crosses a border.
