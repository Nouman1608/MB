## Section A

**1.** Define the term "saprolite". **[2]**

**2.** Outline how hydrolysis weathers granite. **[3]**

**3.** The table shows joint spacing and the depth to the weathering front at five boreholes in granite in the fictional Sena Hills, a humid tropical area.

| Borehole | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mean joint spacing (m) | 0.3 | 0.5 | 1.0 | 2.0 | 4.0 |
| Depth to weathering front (m) | 38 | 30 | 18 | 9 | 3 |

**(a)** Describe the relationship shown in the table. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain the relationship. **[3]**

**(c)** Suggest which borehole site is most likely to become a bornhardt if the saprolite is later stripped away, and give a reason. **[2]**

**4.** Assume that the rate of chemical weathering doubles for every 10 °C rise in temperature.

**(a)** Calculate how many times faster weathering would be at a site with a mean temperature of 28 °C than at a site with a mean temperature of 8 °C. Show your working. **[2]**

**(b)** Give **two** reasons, other than temperature, why weathering profiles are deep in humid tropical areas. **[2]**

**5.** Explain how sheeting joints help bornhardts keep their dome shape. **[3]**

**6.** Explain the formation of cockpit karst. **[4]**

## Section B

**7.** Describe and explain the features of a deep weathering profile in granite. **[6]**

**8.** Explain why the same type of landform, such as an inselberg or a tor, may be produced by more than one process. **[5]**

**9.** Explain the conditions in which tower karst rather than cone karst is likely to develop. **[4]**

**10.** "Granite and limestone landforms in the tropics are mainly the product of past rather than present climates." How far do you agree? **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** Chemically rotted rock that remains in place [1], keeping the original rock structure but weak enough to be dug easily [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Water containing hydrogen ions enters granite along joints [1]. The hydrogen ions replace potassium in feldspar, which breaks down to kaolinite clay [1], releasing silica and potassium in solution, while quartz survives as sand grains [1]. **[3]**

**3. (a)** As joint spacing increases, depth to the weathering front decreases (an inverse relationship) [1], from 38 m where joints are 0.3 m apart to 3 m where they are 4.0 m apart [1].

**(b)** Joints let water into the rock [1]. Closely spaced joints give a larger surface area for hydrolysis and allow water to penetrate deeper [1]. Widely jointed rock has few routes for water, so it stays fresh [1].

**(c)** Borehole 5 [1], because the granite is massive with joints 4.0 m apart, so it has resisted weathering and would stand above the surrounding stripped surface as a dome [1]. **[7]**

**4. (a)** Difference = 28 − 8 = 20 °C, which is two doublings [1]. 2 × 2 = 4 times faster [1].

**(b)** Heavy rainfall keeps the rock permanently moist [1]; long periods without glaciation have allowed weathering to continue for millions of years, or organic acids from rapid decay increase weathering [1]. **[4]**

**5.** As overlying rock is removed, the granite expands because pressure is released [1]. Curved joints form parallel to the surface (sheeting joints) [1]. Slabs peel off along them (exfoliation), so the rock surface stays rounded and the dome shape is kept [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Rainwater charged with soil carbon dioxide dissolves limestone by carbonation [1]. Solution is concentrated where joints intersect, enlarging them into enclosed hollows (dolines) [1]. Hollows deepen and widen, merging into star-shaped cockpits [1]. The rock between them is left as rounded residual hills and ridges, with drainage underground, as in Cockpit Country, Jamaica [1]. **[4]**

**7.** A leached, iron-rich soil at the surface [1]. Below it, saprolite: rotted rock that keeps the original structure, produced by hydrolysis of feldspar to kaolinite [1]. A zone of corestones, rounded blocks of fresh rock surrounded by saprolite, where joints are widely spaced [1]. Corestones are rounded because weathering attacks corners and edges from several sides [1]. A sharp weathering front separates rotted rock from fresh bedrock [1]. The front is irregular because it reaches deeper where joints are close together [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Equifinality means different processes can produce similar forms [1]. Tors may result from deep weathering then stripping (Linton) [1] or from frost shattering under periglacial conditions (Palmer and Nielson) [1]. Inselbergs may form by etching of massive rock [1] or by scarp retreat across a plain, leaving remnants [1]. **[5]**

**9.** Tower karst develops where depressions are lowered to the water table [1], which happens where uplift is slow or the limestone is thin [1]. Rivers can then spread alluvium across a flat floor [1], and acidic swamp water undercuts the foot of each hill, steepening the sides [1]. **[4]**

**10.** The real exam marks this essay with levels of response, not point by point. The points below are indicative creditworthy content.

*Introduction:* defines past climates as earlier conditions, possibly warmer, wetter or drier than today [1], and present climate as today's processes [1].

*Agree:* deep weathering profiles may take millions of years to form [1], so they record long periods of past weathering [1]. Tors and inselbergs need stripping, often triggered by a change of climate or base level [1]. Some granite domes stand in deserts today, where deep weathering is not happening [1]. Parts of the tower karst of southern China are thought to be partly inherited from past climates [1]. Similar landforms outside the tropics, such as Dartmoor tors, show forms can be relict [1].

*Disagree:* hydrolysis is active today in hot, wet conditions [1], and the weathering front is still advancing [1]. Carbonation is rapid today because of high soil carbon dioxide and rainfall [1]. Cockpits and foot caves are still being enlarged by present solution [1]. Exfoliation along sheeting joints continues on bornhardts [1].

*Interaction:* most landforms combine inherited form with present processes [1]. Equifinality makes it hard to tell which climate produced a landform [1]. Tectonic uplift and rock structure also control landform development, not climate alone [1].

*Use of examples:* named examples such as the Matobo Hills, Guilin or Cockpit Country [1].

*Judgement:* large landforms (inselbergs, tower karst) are mainly inherited over long timescales [1]; smaller features and weathering processes are mainly present-day [1]; a reasoned final view [1]. **[20]**

*Examiner insight:* the best essays vary their judgement by landform and timescale and use evidence such as domes in deserts or relict tors.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a table without quoting values at both ends.
- Misapplying the doubling rule: a 20 °C rise is two doublings, so ×4, not ×2 or ×20.
- Explaining granite landforms without mentioning joints.
- Writing that quartz is weathered by hydrolysis.
- Essays that describe landforms but never discuss climate change.
