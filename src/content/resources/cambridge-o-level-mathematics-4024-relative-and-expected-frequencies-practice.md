These questions cover section 8.2, Relative and expected frequencies, of the Cambridge O Level Mathematics (Syllabus D) 4024 syllabus for 2025, 2026 and 2027. The ideas are explained in the [relative and expected frequencies study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-mathematics-4024-relative-and-expected-frequencies/), and the [probability revision notes](/resources/o-level-mathematics-probability-revision-notes/) summarise them. Give probabilities as fractions or decimals, and give whole-number answers where the context needs them.

## Section A

**1.** A spinner is spun 200 times and lands on red 54 times.

**(a)** Find the relative frequency of red.

**(b)** How many reds do you expect in 800 spins, using your answer to (a)? **[3]**

**2.** The probability that an item from a factory is defective is 0.04. How many defective items do you expect in a batch of 1500? **[2]**

**3.** Drawing pins were dropped in two experiments. In the first, 28 of 80 landed point up. In the second, 57 of 120 landed point up. Find the best estimate of the probability that a pin lands point up. **[3]**

**4.** A fair dice is rolled 60 times. How many times do you expect a score of 6? **[2]**

**5.** In a survey of 80 students, 18 say they walk to school. Estimate how many of the 640 students in the school walk. **[3]**

## Section B

**6.** A coin is flipped 600 times and lands heads 330 times.

**(a)** Find the relative frequency of heads.

**(b)** How many heads would you expect from a fair coin?

**(c)** Does the result prove the coin is biased? Give a reason. **[4]**

**7.** A bag holds 60 counters, some black and some white. A counter is drawn at random and replaced, and this is done 40 times. A black counter is drawn 14 times. Estimate how many black counters are in the bag. **[3]**

**8.** A pupil flips a coin 20 times and gets 7 heads. Another pupil flips the same coin 2000 times and gets 1030 heads. Whose result gives the better estimate of the probability of heads? Give a reason. **[2]**

**9.** A spinner has three sections A, B and C. P(A) = 0.5, P(B) = 0.3 and P(C) = x. The spinner is spun 500 times. Find x, and the expected number of times C comes up. **[3]**

**10.** A teacher wants a random sample of 10 students from a class of 30. She chooses the first 10 names on the register. Explain whether this gives a random sample. **[2]**

**11.** A quality check on 250 items finds 7 faulty ones. Estimate the probability that an item is faulty, and how many faulty items to expect in a run of 12 000. **[3]**

**12.** The probability of rain on a day in a town is 0.35. In a period of 40 days, how many rainy days and how many dry days do you expect? **[3]**

**13.** A dice is rolled 300 times. A score of 5 comes up 68 times.

**(a)** How many fives would you expect if the dice were fair?

**(b)** Find the relative frequency of a five.

**(c)** Suggest what the pupil should do next to decide if the dice is biased. **[3]**

**14.** A spinner with three colours is spun 100 times. It lands on red 25 times, blue 40 times and green 35 times.

**(a)** Write down the relative frequency of each colour.

**(b)** Use your answers to estimate how many times each colour will come up in 500 spins. **[3]**

**15.** From a sample of 20 eggs, 3 are cracked. Estimate the number of cracked eggs in a delivery of 2000 eggs, and say why the estimate may not be reliable. **[3]**

**16.** A fair coin lands heads three times in a row. What is the probability that the next flip is heads? Explain your answer. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** 54 ÷ 200 = **0.27** [1]. **(b)** 0.27 × 800 [1] = **216** [1].

**2.** 0.04 × 1500 [1] = **60** [1].

**3.** Combined successes = 28 + 57 = 85 and trials = 80 + 120 = 200 [1]. 85/200 [1] = **0.425** [1].

**4.** P(6) = 1/6 [1]. 1/6 × 60 = **10** [1].

**5.** Proportion = 18/80 = 0.225 [1]. 0.225 × 640 [1] = **144 students** [1].

**6. (a)** 330 ÷ 600 = **0.55** [1]. **(b)** **300** [1]. **(c)** **No**, not proof [1]: the result is 30 more than expected, which could be due to chance, and more flips would give stronger evidence [1].

**7.** Relative frequency of black = 14/40 = 0.35 [1]. Expected black = 0.35 × 60 [1] = **21** [1].

**8.** The second pupil's result, because it is based on many more trials [1], and relative frequency becomes more reliable as the number of trials grows, so 1030/2000 = 0.515 is a better estimate [1].

**9.** 0.5 + 0.3 + x = 1 [1], so **x = 0.2** [1]. Expected = 0.2 × 500 = **100** [1].

**10.** **Not random** [1]. The students not on the first part of the register had no chance of being chosen, and names might be listed in an order that affects the sample, so not every student has an equal chance [1].

**11.** Estimated probability = 7/250 = **0.028** [1]. Expected faulty = 0.028 × 12 000 [1] = **336** [1].

**12.** Rainy days: 0.35 × 40 = **14** [1][1]. Dry days: 40 − 14 = **26** [1].

**13. (a)** 300 × 1/6 = **50** [1]. **(b)** 68/300 = **0.227** [1]. **(c)** Roll the dice many more times and compare the relative frequencies with 1/6 [1].

**14. (a)** Red **0.25**, blue **0.4**, green **0.35** [1]. **(b)** Red 0.25 × 500 = **125**, blue 0.4 × 500 = **200**, green 0.35 × 500 = **175** [1][1].

**15.** Proportion cracked = 3/20 = 0.15 [1]. Expected = 0.15 × 2000 = **300** [1]. The sample is small, so the proportion may differ a lot from the true one [1].

**16.** The probability is **1/2** [1]. Each flip is independent of earlier flips, so previous results do not change the chance [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing by the wrong total when finding a relative frequency.
- Averaging two relative frequencies instead of combining the data.
- Treating an expected frequency as a guarantee.
- Claiming that a small number of trials proves a coin or dice is biased.
- Forgetting that the probabilities of all outcomes add up to 1.
