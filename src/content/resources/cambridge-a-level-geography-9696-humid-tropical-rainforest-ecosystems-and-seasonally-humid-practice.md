## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between primary succession and secondary succession. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why many rainforest trees have drip-tip leaves and buttress roots. **[2]**

**3.** Explain why the litter store is small in **both** rainforest and savanna nutrient cycles, but for different reasons. **[3]**

**4.** The table shows energy available at each trophic level in a fictional savanna food chain.

| Trophic level | Energy (kJ per m² per year) |
|---|---|
| Producers (NPP) | 18 000 |
| Primary consumers | 1 620 |
| Secondary consumers | 140 |
| Tertiary consumers | 12 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of energy transferred from producers to primary consumers. Show your working. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage transferred from secondary to tertiary consumers. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why the energy available falls at each trophic level. **[3]**

**5.** The table shows soil properties at four positions on a slope in the fictional Ruva savanna.

| Slope position | Soil colour | Clay content (%) | Depth to water table in wet season (m) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hilltop | Red | 18 | More than 5 |
| Upper slope | Red-brown | 22 | 3 |
| Lower slope | Brown | 35 | 1 |
| Valley floor | Dark grey | 55 | 0.1 |

**(a)** Describe how the soil changes down the slope. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain these changes. **[3]**

**6.** Explain what is meant by cation exchange capacity and why it is low in many rainforest soils. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** Compare the vegetation characteristics of rainforest and savanna ecosystems. **[6]**

**8.** Explain the processes that form tropical red and brown earths. **[5]**

**9.** Explain how a Gersmehl diagram for a rainforest plot would change after the forest is cleared and the land is cropped for several years. **[4]**

**10.** "Savanna is best described as a plagioclimax." How far do you agree? **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** Primary succession begins on a surface with no soil, such as new lava [1]. Secondary succession begins where vegetation has been removed but soil remains, such as abandoned farmland [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Drip tips shed rainwater quickly, which discourages fungi and algae on the leaf surface [1]. Buttress roots support tall trees whose roots are shallow, spreading near the surface where nutrients are released [1]. **[2]**

**3.** In the rainforest, high temperatures and moisture allow decomposers to break down litter within weeks to months [1]. In the savanna, dry-season fires burn the litter [1], and termites remove much of it [1]. **[3]**

**4. (a)** 1 620 ÷ 18 000 × 100 [1] = 9.0% [1].

**(b)** 12 ÷ 140 × 100 = 8.6% [1].

**(c)** Organisms use most of the energy they take in for respiration, losing it as heat [1]. Some material is not eaten, such as roots and wood [1], and some eaten material is not digested and is lost in waste [1]. **[6]**

**5. (a)** Colour changes from red on the hilltop to dark grey on the valley floor [1]. Clay content increases from 18% to 55% [1]. The wet-season water table rises from more than 5 m below the surface to 0.1 m [1].

**(b)** Upper slopes are well drained, so iron is oxidised, giving red colours [1]. Fine particles and dissolved bases are washed downslope by throughflow and surface runoff, so clay accumulates lower down [1]. Valley floors are waterlogged in the wet season, so iron is reduced and organic matter builds up, giving dark grey colours [1]. **[6]**

**6.** Cation exchange capacity is the ability of soil particles, mainly clay and humus, to hold nutrient ions such as calcium and potassium [1]. Rainforest soils are dominated by kaolinite and iron and aluminium oxides, which hold few ions [1]. Humus is decomposed so fast that little accumulates, so few nutrients are held against leaching [1]. **[3]**

**7.** Rainforest has a dense, layered structure with emergents over 40 m, whereas savanna is mainly grass with scattered trees [1]. Rainforest trees are evergreen, whereas many savanna trees are deciduous in the dry season [1]. Rainforest trees have shallow roots and buttresses, whereas savanna trees have deep tap roots to reach groundwater [1]. Rainforest leaves are large with drip tips, whereas savanna leaves are small, waxy or thorny to reduce water loss [1]. Savanna trees have thick, fire-resistant bark, whereas rainforest bark is thin [1]. Both have high productivity, but rainforest biodiversity is far greater [1]. **[6]**

**8.** In the wet season, rain percolates downwards, leaching soluble bases and moving iron down the profile [1]. Intense chemical weathering produces iron oxides that give red colours [1]. In the dry season, evaporation draws water and minerals upwards by capillary rise [1]. Repeated wetting and drying concentrates iron into concretions or a laterite crust [1]. Leaching is seasonal, so the soil is shallower and less leached than an oxisol, and browner where organic matter is higher [1]. **[5]**

**9.** The biomass store shrinks sharply, because trees are removed [1]. The litter store becomes very small, because little plant material falls [1]. The soil store rises briefly from ash after burning, then falls as crops take up nutrients and rain leaches them [1]. Outputs by leaching and runoff become much larger, and the cycle is no longer closed [1]. **[4]**

**10.** The real exam marks this essay with levels of response, not point by point. The points below are indicative creditworthy content.

*Introduction:* defines a plagioclimax as a community held by human activity [1], and contrasts it with a climatic climax and an edaphic subclimax [1].

*Agree:* people have burned savanna for thousands of years to improve grazing and hunting [1]. Fire kills tree seedlings but grasses regrow from roots [1]. Plots protected from fire often become woodland [1]. Grazing by livestock, and browsing by wildlife, prevent tree regeneration [1]. Many savanna plants are pyrophytes, showing long exposure to fire [1].

*Disagree:* the wet-dry climate limits tree growth because there is too little water for closed forest in the dry season [1]. In drier savannas, trees do not invade even when fire is excluded [1]. Lightning fires occur naturally, so some savanna is a fire subclimax rather than a plagioclimax [1]. Infertile or seasonally waterlogged soils favour grass, an edaphic subclimax [1]. The catena shows vegetation changing with drainage on a single slope [1]. Savanna existed before humans in some areas [1].

*Interaction:* climate, soils, fire and grazing act together [1]. Human burning may extend savanna into areas that would otherwise be woodland [1]. Large wild herbivores such as elephants also open woodland by pushing over and stripping trees, a natural disturbance [1].

*Use of examples:* reference to named savanna regions or fire experiments [1].

*Judgement:* in wetter savannas the plagioclimax view is strongest [1]; in drier savannas the climatic climax view is stronger [1]; a clear final view on "best described" [1]. **[20]**

*Examiner insight:* the best answers make their judgement depend on rainfall and use evidence such as fire-exclusion plots.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating transfer percentages with the numbers the wrong way round.
- Describing a catena without quoting values from the table.
- Saying rainforest soils are fertile.
- Comparisons that describe one ecosystem and then the other.
- Essays that define the terms but never weigh the evidence.
