This guide focuses on one subtopic: **3.3.5 Refraction and total internal reflection**, statements a to h of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Physics (9203) specification, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. The whole subtopic is marked **P** in the specification, so it is assessed in the full Physics award (9203) and not in Combined Science. It contains one of the five required practicals: *investigate the refraction of light by different substances*.

The [reflection, refraction and total internal reflection guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-reflection-refraction-refractive-index-and-total/) gives an overview of the unit. This page goes further on the parts students most often find hard: light **leaving** a block, deciding between refraction and total internal reflection, analysing practical data, and optical fibres. Afterwards, use the [refraction revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-refraction-and-total-internal-reflection-oxfordaqa-revision-notes/) and the [refraction practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-refraction-and-total-internal-reflection-oxfordaqa-practice/).

## The statements at a glance

| Statement | Content |
|---|---|
| a | Wave speed depends on the medium; a change of speed changes direction unless the wave travels along the normal. This is refraction |
| b | Light entering a denser medium bends towards the normal; entering a less dense medium it bends away. Explain with wavefront diagrams |
| c | A prism can disperse light into a spectrum |
| d | Refractive index defined using wave speed |
| e | n = sin i / sin r; required practical |
| f | n = 1 / sin c (recall of critical angle values is not required) |
| g | Total internal reflection: a special case when the angle of incidence in the denser medium exceeds the critical angle |
| h | Visible light and infrared travel along optical fibres by total internal reflection; applications in medicine and communications |

## Refraction is about speed

The key idea in statement a is that refraction is caused by a **change in speed**. Direction changes only as a consequence. A ray that meets a boundary along the normal changes speed but not direction. In a wavefront diagram, a slanted wavefront reaches the boundary one end first. That end slows down in the denser medium while the rest is still moving quickly, so the wavefront swings round and the ray turns towards the normal. Frequency is fixed by the source and does not change, so in the slower medium the wavelength is shorter and the wavefronts are drawn closer together. Leaving the denser medium, the process reverses: the wave speeds up, the wavelength grows and the ray bends away from the normal.

In this subtopic "denser" means optically denser: the medium in which light travels more slowly, with the higher refractive index.

## Refractive index two ways

Statement d defines refractive index from wave speeds:

> refractive index, n = speed of light in a vacuum ÷ speed of light in the medium

Statement e links it to angles, for light passing from air (treated as a vacuum) into the medium:

> n = sin i ÷ sin r

where *i* is the angle in air and *r* the angle in the medium, both measured from the normal. Because light is slowest in the medium, n is always greater than 1 and has no unit.

**Light coming out.** For a ray leaving a medium into air, swap the roles: the angle in air is still the larger one, so sin(angle in air) = n × sin(angle in the medium). Writing the formula with the angle in air on top, whichever direction the light travels, avoids the most common error in this topic.

## Worked example 1: in and out of a block

A ray in air strikes a glass block of refractive index 1.52 with an angle of incidence of 55°.

```
Entering:   sin r = sin 55° / 1.52 = 0.8192 / 1.52 = 0.5389
            r = 32.6°   (smaller angle: bent towards the normal)
```

Inside a different glass of the same refractive index, a ray meets the far surface at 25° to the normal.

```
Leaving:    sin(angle in air) = 1.52 × sin 25° = 1.52 × 0.4226 = 0.6424
            angle in air = 40.0°   (larger angle: bent away from the normal)
```

## Critical angle and total internal reflection

When light travels from glass towards air, the angle in air is always larger than the angle in the glass. As the angle in the glass increases, the refracted ray in air gets closer to the surface. At the **critical angle**, *c*, the refracted ray travels along the surface (angle in air 90°). Putting sin 90° = 1 into the exit form gives statement f:

> n = 1 ÷ sin c

For any angle of incidence inside the denser medium **greater** than *c*, no refraction is possible and all the light is reflected back inside. This is **total internal reflection** (statement g). Two conditions must both hold:

1. The light is in the denser medium, travelling towards a less dense one.
2. The angle of incidence is greater than the critical angle.

Light going from air into glass can never be totally internally reflected, whatever the angle.

## Worked example 2: refraction or total internal reflection?

A transparent plastic has a refractive index of 1.60. Light inside it meets the plastic–air surface at (i) 35° and (ii) 45°. Decide what happens in each case.

```
Critical angle:  sin c = 1 / 1.60 = 0.625,  c = 38.7°
(i)  35° < 38.7°: the light refracts out.
     sin(angle in air) = 1.60 × sin 35° = 1.60 × 0.5736 = 0.9177
     angle in air = 66.6°  (a weak partial reflection also occurs)
(ii) 45° > 38.7°: total internal reflection.
     The ray reflects inside with angle of reflection 45°.
```

A quick check for any exit calculation: if n × sin(angle inside) comes out greater than 1, there is no angle in air, so the light must be totally internally reflected.

## Dispersion by a prism

White light is a mixture of colours. In glass, each colour travels at a slightly different speed, so each has a slightly different refractive index and refracts by a different amount. A triangular prism refracts the light at both faces, so the colours spread out into a spectrum: red is deviated least, violet most. The order red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet is worth knowing for diagram questions. A rectangular block does not produce a spectrum, because the second face bends each colour back by the amount the first face bent it, so the colours emerge parallel.

## The required practical: analysing results

In the refraction practical you shine a ray into a block (or a transparent container of liquid), mark the incident and emergent rays, join them to draw the ray inside, and measure *i* and *r* from a normal drawn with a protractor. Repeating with several values of *i* lets you test the relationship. These are example results for one transparent material:

| i (°) | 20 | 30 | 40 | 50 | 60 | 70 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| r (°) | 13 | 20 | 26 | 31 | 36 | 39 |
| sin i ÷ sin r | 1.52 | 1.46 | 1.47 | 1.49 | 1.47 | 1.49 |

The mean of the last row is 1.48. A better method is to plot sin *i* (vertical axis) against sin *r* (horizontal axis): the points lie close to a straight line through the origin, and its gradient, about 1.48, is the refractive index. The scatter in the ratio at small angles comes from reading angles to the nearest degree: an error of 1° matters more when *r* is only 13°. Using a narrow ray, a sharp pencil and larger angles reduces this uncertainty. Comparing different substances with the same method shows which refracts light most.

## Optical fibres (statement h)

An optical fibre is a thin, flexible strand of glass or plastic. Light, or infrared, entering one end strikes the inside surface at an angle greater than the critical angle, so it is totally internally reflected again and again and travels along the fibre even when it bends.

- **Medicine**: an endoscope carries light down one bundle of fibres into the body and returns an image up another, so doctors can see inside the body through a small opening, or through the mouth, without major surgery.
- **Communications**: pulses of light or infrared carry telephone, television and internet signals. Fibres carry much more information than copper cables of similar size, the signal loses little energy over long distances, and the signals are not affected by electrical interference.

## Common mistakes

- Saying light bends "because it is denser" without mentioning the change in speed.
- Putting the angle inside the glass on top of the fraction when light leaves a block.
- Thinking total internal reflection can happen as light enters glass from air.
- Writing that the frequency changes on refraction. Only speed and wavelength change.
- Using "equal to the critical angle" as the condition for total internal reflection; the angle must be greater.
- Calling a spectrum the result of reflection.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** refraction: speed change first, then the direction change, then towards or away from the normal.
- **Calculate**: write the equation, substitute with units where relevant, and give angles to about three significant figures. Refractive index has no unit.
- **Describe** an application of optical fibres: name the use, then a benefit linked to it.
- **Suggest** improvements to the practical: refer to the size of the angles, repeat readings and a graph method.
