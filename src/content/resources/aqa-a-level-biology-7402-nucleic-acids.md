## What the specification says

**3.1.5.1** DNA and RNA are important information-carrying molecules. In all living cells, DNA holds genetic information and RNA transfers genetic information from DNA to the ribosomes. Ribosomes are formed from RNA and proteins. Both are polymers of nucleotides, each made of a pentose, a nitrogen-containing organic base and a phosphate group. A condensation reaction between two nucleotides forms a phosphodiester bond. DNA is a double helix of two polynucleotide chains held together by hydrogen bonds between specific complementary base pairs. An RNA molecule is a relatively short polynucleotide chain. You should appreciate that the relative simplicity of DNA led many scientists to doubt that it carried the genetic code.

**3.1.5.2** Semi-conservative replication ensures genetic continuity between generations of cells. You need the roles of DNA helicase and DNA polymerase, and to evaluate the work of scientists in validating the Watson–Crick model of DNA replication.

## DNA and RNA side by side

| Feature | DNA | RNA |
|---|---|---|
| Pentose sugar | Deoxyribose | Ribose |
| Bases | Adenine, cytosine, guanine, thymine | Adenine, cytosine, guanine, uracil |
| Number of polynucleotide chains | Two, forming a double helix | One |
| Length | Very long | Relatively short |
| Held together by | Hydrogen bonds between complementary bases | (single chain) |
| Job in cells | Holds genetic information | Transfers genetic information from DNA to ribosomes; also forms part of ribosomes |

Two exam points hide in this table. First, **ribosomes contain RNA**, so RNA is structural as well as a messenger. Second, "relatively short" is the specification's phrase for RNA: use it when comparing.

## The structure that makes DNA stable but copyable

Each strand has a **sugar-phosphate backbone**: deoxyribose and phosphate joined by phosphodiester bonds, with a base attached to each sugar. The bases point inward and pair across the helix.

- **Stable:** the phosphodiester bonds in the backbone are strong covalent bonds, and the bases are tucked inside the helix. Many hydrogen bonds between base pairs hold the two strands together, so the molecule does not fall apart in normal conditions.
- **Copyable:** each hydrogen bond is weak, so the strands can be separated by an enzyme without breaking the backbone. Each exposed strand then acts as a template.
- **Accurate:** pairing is specific. Adenine pairs only with thymine, and cytosine only with guanine, so the sequence of one strand fixes the sequence of the other.

You may be told in a question that a C–G pair is held by three hydrogen bonds and an A–T pair by two. If so, a DNA molecule with a higher proportion of C–G pairs needs more energy to separate its strands. Use the data you are given rather than assuming it.

## Worked example 1: complementary sequences

A short stretch of one DNA strand reads: **T A C G G A T C**.

1. **Complementary DNA strand:** swap each base for its partner: A for T, T for A, G for C, C for G. Answer: **A T G C C T A G**.
2. **Complementary RNA sequence** (as made when information is transferred from DNA): RNA has uracil instead of thymine, so wherever the DNA strand has A, the RNA has U. Answer: **A U G C C U A G**.
3. **Base count check:** the original strand has 2 T, 2 A, 2 C and 2 G. Its DNA partner must have 2 A, 2 T, 2 G and 2 C, which it does.

The most common error is writing T in an RNA sequence. RNA never contains thymine.

## Why a four-base code can carry so much information

Many early scientists doubted that DNA could carry genetic information because it is chemically simple: a repeating backbone and only four kinds of base, while proteins are built from twenty different amino acids. That simplicity seemed too limited to store the instructions for a whole organism.

The answer lies in **sequence**. The number of possible sequences of length n is 4ⁿ.

| Length (bases) | Possible sequences |
|---|---|
| 1 | 4 |
| 3 | 4³ = 64 |
| 10 | 4¹⁰ = 1,048,576 |
| 20 | 4²⁰ ≈ 1.1 × 10¹² |

Even short stretches can take an enormous number of forms, and real DNA molecules are millions of bases long. Information is carried by the order of the bases, not by the variety of the chemicals themselves.

## DNA replication: who does what

| Step | What happens | Done by |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The double helix unwinds | DNA helicase |
| 2 | Hydrogen bonds between complementary bases break, separating the strands | DNA helicase |
| 3 | Free DNA nucleotides are attracted to exposed bases on each template strand | Complementary base pairing (no enzyme named) |
| 4 | Hydrogen bonds form between the new nucleotides and the template bases | Base pairing |
| 5 | Adjacent new nucleotides are joined by condensation, forming phosphodiester bonds | DNA polymerase |
| 6 | Each new molecule has one original strand and one new strand | Result: semi-conservative |

Keep the enzymes' jobs apart. **Helicase** deals with hydrogen bonds between strands. **DNA polymerase** catalyses condensation reactions between neighbouring nucleotides on the same new strand. It does not form hydrogen bonds and it does not break the helix apart.

Because each new molecule keeps one original strand as a template, the base sequence is copied faithfully. That is what the specification means by **genetic continuity between generations of cells**: daughter cells produced by division (3.2.2) receive the same genetic information.

## Worked example 2: predicting bands the other way round

The classic experiment grew bacteria on heavy nitrogen (¹⁵N) and moved them to light nitrogen (¹⁴N). Suppose instead that bacteria grown for many generations on **¹⁴N** (light DNA) are moved to **¹⁵N** and allowed to replicate. DNA is then separated by density in a centrifuge tube.

| Model | After 1 generation | After 2 generations |
|---|---|---|
| Semi-conservative | All intermediate (one ¹⁴N strand, one ¹⁵N strand) | Half intermediate, half heavy |
| Conservative | Half light (original molecules), half heavy | Quarter light, three-quarters heavy |
| Dispersive | All intermediate | All in one band between intermediate and heavy |

How to evaluate the models from real results:

- An all-intermediate band after one generation rules out the **conservative** model, which predicts a light and a heavy band.
- After two generations, separate intermediate and heavy bands rule out the **dispersive** model, which predicts one band.
- Only the semi-conservative model fits both results, which supports the Watson–Crick prediction.

The logic is the same as in the original experiment, and examiners like reversed or altered versions because they test understanding rather than memory.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** replication in order and name each enzyme with its exact job.
- **Explain** semi-conservative replication by saying what each new molecule contains.
- **Compare** DNA and RNA using matched pairs: "DNA contains deoxyribose, whereas RNA contains ribose".
- **Evaluate** the evidence by saying which result rules out which model.
- **Calculate** with base pairing: use complementary pairing within one double-stranded molecule only.

## Common mistakes

- Writing thymine in RNA, or uracil in DNA.
- Saying DNA polymerase forms hydrogen bonds or joins the two strands together.
- Saying helicase "unzips by breaking phosphodiester bonds". It breaks hydrogen bonds between bases.
- Describing RNA as double-stranded, or forgetting that ribosomes contain RNA.
- Claiming one result proves semi-conservative replication; results rule models out.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between DNA and RNA nucleotides?**
A DNA nucleotide contains deoxyribose and one of adenine, cytosine, guanine or thymine. An RNA nucleotide contains ribose and one of adenine, cytosine, guanine or uracil.

**What does DNA polymerase do in replication?**
It catalyses the condensation reactions that join adjacent nucleotides, forming phosphodiester bonds in the new strand.

**Why did scientists doubt DNA was the genetic material?**
DNA seemed too simple, with a repeating backbone and only four bases, compared with proteins made of twenty amino acids.

**What does semi-conservative replication mean?**
Each new DNA molecule contains one strand from the original molecule and one newly made strand.
