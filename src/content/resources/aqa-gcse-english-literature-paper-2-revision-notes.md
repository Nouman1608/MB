## Paper 2 at a glance

Closed book, **60% of the GCSE** (versus Paper 1's 40%) — allocate revision time accordingly.

| Section | Focus | Marks |
|---|---|---|
| A: Modern Texts | One post-1914 prose or drama text, studied in full | 34 (30 + 4 for AO4 accuracy) |
| B: Poetry | One 15-poem anthology cluster, studied in full | 30 |
| C: Unseen Poetry | Two poems never seen before the exam | 32 (24 + 8) |

## Section A: modern prose/drama

Centre-chosen from 12 texts (e.g. *An Inspector Calls*, *Lord of the Flies*, *Animal Farm*). Know the
world the text was written into, not just its plot — social and historical context matters more here
than for Paper 1's older texts.

## Section B: poetry cluster

One cluster of 15 poems from *Poems Past and Present* (Love and Relationships; Power and Conflict;
Worlds and Lives), spanning 1789 to the present. You write about a **named poem plus a second poem of
your own choosing** from the same cluster — build genuine comparative fluency across all 15, not deep
knowledge of just two or three favourites.

## Section C: unseen poetry

The one part of the whole GCSE that cannot be pre-learned. The only real preparation is regular, timed
practice analysing unfamiliar poems, building the habit of spotting structure, form and language
shifts within a single reading under time pressure.

## Worked example: comparing poems by structure, not just theme

A weak Section B answer compares two poems only by theme ("both are about loss"). A strong answer adds
structure and form:

```
Theme:        both poems address loss
Structure:    Poem A uses a regular rhyme scheme that breaks down in
              the final stanza, formally enacting the loss of order
              the speaker feels
Poem B:       uses enjambment throughout to create a restless,
              unresolved feeling, contrasting with Poem A's more
              controlled form
Comparison:   both poems use FORM to reinforce their theme, but in
              opposite ways -- one through breakdown, one through
              persistent lack of resolution
```

AQA's assessment objectives credit understanding and response to the text (AO1), analysis of the
writer's methods — language, **form and structure** (AO2) — and relevant context (AO3). Marking is
holistic rather than built from equally-weighted slices, but AO2's higher bands are only reached by
comparing form and structure as well as theme — **theme alone caps marks in the middle bands**.

## Section A: character, theme and context together

A strong Section A answer integrates four elements rather than treating them as separate paragraphs:
what a character does or says, the theme this connects to, the historical/social context that shapes
why it matters, and the specific method (language, structure, form) the writer uses to convey it.
Examiners consistently note that the strongest answers move fluidly between these four elements within
a single paragraph, rather than working through them as a checklist in a fixed order.

## Building the poetry comparison

Because Section B requires comparing a named poem with a second poem of your own choosing, prepare a
small set of "pairing plans" in advance — two or three poems from the cluster you could confidently
compare with any other poem on a shared theme (such as loss, power, or identity), rather than deciding
your second poem cold in the exam. This preparation converts what feels like an open-ended choice under
pressure into a rehearsed, confident decision.

## Exam traps

- Retelling the plot in Section A instead of analysing it — summary without analysis caps marks.
- Comparing poems by theme only, ignoring form and structure.
- Under-timing Section C because it feels the least prepared-for — it carries 32 marks, close to
  Section A's 34 and Section B's 30, so running short on it is one of the most avoidable ways to lose marks.
- Forgetting the 60/40 split with Paper 1 and under-revising Paper 2 relative to its actual weight.

## Unseen poetry: a repeatable approach

Rather than approaching each unseen poem cold, use a consistent first-read routine: note the poem's
apparent subject and tone on a first read; identify its structural shape (regular or irregular stanzas,
rhyme, line length) on a second pass; then look for two or three specific language choices that support
or complicate the initial impression of tone. Comparing two unseen poems using this same routine for
both makes the comparison itself more systematic, since you are comparing like-for-like observations
rather than whatever happened to stand out first in each poem.

## Self-test

1. What proportion of the GCSE does Paper 2 carry, versus Paper 1?
2. In Section B, how many poems must you be prepared to write about?
3. Why can't Section C be prepared for with memorised content?
4. Besides theme, which two things must a strong Section B comparison also address to reach the top mark bands?
5. What is the most common way marks are lost in Section A?

A final practical habit: after finishing Section C, briefly check the time remaining against what is
left for reviewing Section A and B answers — since Section C is the paper's most time-pressured part
for most students, protecting a short review window at the very end, rather than using every remaining
minute on the unseen poems, helps catch avoidable errors elsewhere on the paper.

**Answers:** 1. 60% (Paper 1 carries 40%). 2. A named poem plus a second poem of your own choosing —
effectively any of the 15 poems in your studied cluster. 3. Because the poems are unseen before the
exam, so no quotation bank or memorised analysis can prepare for the specific poems that appear —
only general analytical skill built through wide practice. 4. Form and structure — the writer's methods, credited under AO2. 5.
Summarising the plot instead of analysing character, theme, context and writer's method.
