## Effective demand

**Demand** in economics is always **effective demand**: the quantity consumers are willing **and able** to buy at each price over a given period. Two conditions must hold together. A family that wants a car but has no income or credit to pay for it adds nothing to demand. A wealthy buyer with no interest in the car adds nothing either.

This matters in applied questions. A government report might say a country "needs" far more clinics. Need is not effective demand: unless patients, insurers or the state can pay, the need does not appear on a demand curve, and private firms have no price signal to respond to. Spotting the difference between need, want and effective demand is a common source of analysis marks in data-response questions.

Demand is also a **flow** over time. "Demand for bread is 4,000 loaves" is incomplete; "4,000 loaves a week at 2 cu each" is a point on a demand curve.

## Why the curves slope as they do

A **demand curve** shows the quantity demanded at each price, other things being equal. It normally slopes **downwards**: at a lower price the good is more affordable from a given income, and it becomes cheaper compared with alternatives, so consumers buy more of it and new buyers enter the market.

A **supply curve** shows the quantity firms are willing and able to sell at each price, other things being equal. It normally slopes **upwards**: a higher price makes production more profitable, lets firms cover the higher cost of producing extra units, and draws resources and firms into the market.

The phrase **other things being equal** (ceteris paribus) is the key to 2.1.7. Each curve isolates the effect of the good's own price. Everything else is held constant, and when something else changes, the curve itself moves.

## Individual and market curves

Market demand is the **horizontal sum** of individual demands: at each price, add the quantities. Market supply is built the same way from each firm's supply.

### Worked example 1: a kink in market demand

An invented city has two groups of bus passengers. Price P is in cu per ticket and quantity is thousands of tickets a week.

```
Students:  Qd = 50 - 10P   (they buy none at a price of 5 or more)
Adults:    Qd = 80 - 10P   (they buy none at a price of 8 or more)

Price 2:  students 30 + adults 60 = 90
Price 4:  students 10 + adults 40 = 50
Price 6:  students  0 + adults 20 = 20
```

Below a price of 5, market demand is Qd = 130 - 20P. Above 5, only adults buy, so market demand is Qd = 80 - 10P. The market curve has a **kink** at a price of 5, where students leave the market. Adding the two equations at every price would wrongly give a negative student quantity at a price of 6.

On the supply side, suppose 40 identical bus firms each supply Qs = 3P - 6 (they supply nothing at a price of 2 or less). Market supply is 40 times each firm's quantity: Qs = 120P - 240. At a price of 4, each firm offers 6 thousand tickets and the market offers 240 thousand. If 10 more firms enter, market supply becomes 150P - 300, which is more at every price above 2: an increase in the number of firms shifts market supply to the right.

## Determinants and shifts

| Determinant of demand (2.1.3) | Change that shifts D to the right (2.1.5) |
|---|---|
| Income | Higher income for a normal good; lower income for an inferior good |
| Price of a substitute | Rise in its price |
| Price of a complement | Fall in its price |
| Tastes, fashion, advertising | Favourable change |
| Size and age structure of population | More consumers in the relevant group |
| Expectations of future prices | Prices expected to rise |
| Credit availability and interest rates | Cheaper, easier credit for goods bought on loan |

| Determinant of supply (2.1.4) | Change that shifts S to the right (2.1.6) |
|---|---|
| Costs of production (wages, materials, energy) | Lower costs |
| Technology and productivity | Improvement |
| Indirect taxes and subsidies | Lower tax or new subsidy |
| Number of firms | Entry of new firms |
| Prices of other goods the firm could make | Fall in their price, so resources switch to this good |
| Natural conditions (farm goods) | Good harvest weather |
| Producers' expectations | Expected future conditions that encourage selling more now |

Each change in the opposite direction shifts the curve to the left.

### Two ways to read a shift

A shift can be read horizontally or vertically. Take demand for an invented brand of trainers, Qd = 120 - 4P, which becomes Qd = 140 - 4P after a successful advertising campaign.

```
Horizontal reading (at a price of 15):
  before: 120 - 60 = 60     after: 140 - 60 = 80
  20 more pairs are demanded at the same price.

Vertical reading (for 60 pairs):
  before: 60 = 120 - 4P -> P = 15
  after:  60 = 140 - 4P -> P = 20
  consumers will now pay 5 cu more for the same quantity.
```

Both describe the same rightward shift. The vertical reading is useful later, when an indirect tax shifts supply upwards by the amount of the tax.

## Shift or movement

| Cause | Effect on the curve | Words to use |
|---|---|---|
| Change in the good's own price | Movement along the existing curve | Extension or contraction of quantity demanded (or supplied) |
| Change in any other determinant | Shift of the whole curve | Increase or decrease in demand (or supply) |

A chain of events often contains both. Suppose the cost of rubber rises. Supply of tyres shifts **left** (a cost determinant). The resulting higher price of tyres causes a **contraction** in quantity demanded, a movement along the demand curve. The demand curve for tyres does not shift, because nothing on the demand side has changed. Then, because tyres and cars are complements, the higher price of tyres can shift the **demand for cars** left. Keep each market and each curve separate.

## Annotated model answer

**Question:** Explain the difference between a decrease in demand and a contraction in demand. **[4]**

> A **decrease in demand** is a **leftward shift of the whole demand curve**, so that less is demanded at every price *(1: definition)*. It is caused by a change in a determinant other than the good's own price, for example a fall in income for a normal good *(1: cause, with example)*. A **contraction in demand** is a **movement up along an existing demand curve**, showing a fall in quantity demanded *(1: definition)*. It is caused only by a rise in the good's own price, with all other determinants unchanged *(1: cause)*.

The answer pairs each term with its diagram feature and its cause. "Demand falls when price rises" would earn little, because it does not separate the two ideas.

## Common mistakes

- Writing "demand rises" after a fall in the good's own price. That is an extension in quantity demanded.
- Shifting the demand curve after a cost change. Costs are a supply determinant.
- Adding individual demand equations without checking that each quantity is positive, which misses the kink.
- Forgetting "able to pay" in a definition of effective demand.
- Describing a leftward supply shift as "supply goes up" because the curve moves upwards on the diagram. Use left and right, or decrease and increase.
- Ignoring the time period, for example giving a quantity without "per week" or "per year".

## Exam technique

- **Define** questions on demand or supply want "willing and able", "at each price" and "over a period of time".
- **Explain** questions about a change want three steps: the determinant that changed, the direction of the shift, and the reason consumers or firms respond that way.
- **Analyse** questions usually need a diagram. Label the axes Price and Quantity, label the original and new curves (for example D1 and D2), and show the direction with an arrow.
- In Paper 1, a common trap pairs a correct direction with the wrong curve. Decide which curve moves before you look at the options.
- In the data response, quote the cause from the extract (for example "a 12% rise in average wages") and say which determinant it affects.
