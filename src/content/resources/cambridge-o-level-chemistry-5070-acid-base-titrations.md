Section 12.2 of the Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 syllabus (2026–2028) has two learning outcomes. You must be able to describe an acid–base titration, including the use of a **burette**, a **volumetric pipette** and a **suitable indicator**, and describe how to identify the **end-point** of a titration using an indicator. Titrations are also listed among the experimental contexts for the practical paper and the Alternative to Practical, so expect to read burette scales, complete results tables and judge technique as well as describe the method.

The broader [Practical Techniques guide](/resources/practical-techniques-titrations-chromatography-separation/) gives the outline method in five steps. This guide adds the technique details that turn a rough description into a full-mark answer, and shows how to process a set of titration results.

## What a titration does

A titration finds the volume of one solution that reacts exactly with a measured volume of another. In an acid–base titration, one solution is an acid and the other an alkali. The **end-point** is the point at which the indicator changes colour, showing that the acid and alkali have exactly neutralised each other.

Titration has two uses at O Level: finding an unknown concentration (the calculation belongs to the mole topic) and preparing a soluble salt from an acid and an alkali, described in [Acids, Bases and Salts](/resources/acids-bases-and-salts/).

## The apparatus and why each item is used

| Item | Job in the titration | Why this item and not another |
|---|---|---|
| Volumetric pipette with pipette filler | delivers a fixed volume (often 25.0 cm³) of one solution into the flask | one precise volume; the filler means no solution is drawn up by mouth |
| Burette (50 cm³) | adds the other solution and measures the volume added | adds drops near the end-point; reads to 0.05 cm³ |
| Conical flask | holds the pipetted solution | can be swirled without spilling |
| White tile | under the flask | makes the colour change easier to see |
| Indicator | shows the end-point | changes colour sharply at neutralisation |
| Small funnel | fills the burette | removed before reading, as drips from it would change the reading |

## A full method, with technique points

1. **Rinse the pipette** with a little of the alkali it will measure, then fill it using a pipette filler until the bottom of the meniscus sits on the line. Rinsing with the solution, not water, stops the solution being diluted by drops left inside.
2. **Run the alkali into a clean conical flask.** The flask may be wet with distilled water: added water does not change the amount of alkali in the flask.
3. **Add two or three drops of a suitable indicator.** Too much indicator can blur the colour change.
4. **Rinse the burette** with a little of the acid, then fill it using a small funnel. Remove the funnel and run a little acid out so that the tip below the tap is full and has no air bubble.
5. **Record the initial burette reading**, at eye level, from the bottom of the meniscus, to the nearest 0.05 cm³.
6. **Add acid while swirling the flask** on a white tile. Near the end-point, where the colour starts to change and then fades on swirling, add the acid **drop by drop**.
7. **Stop at the first permanent colour change.** Record the final burette reading.
8. **Repeat.** The first run is a rough titration, done quickly to find the approximate end-point. Then carry out accurate titrations until two or more results are **concordant**, usually meaning within 0.10 cm³ of each other.

## Choosing a suitable indicator

| Indicator | Colour in acid | Colour in alkali | Change seen when acid is added from the burette to alkali |
|---|---|---|---|
| Methyl orange | red | yellow | yellow → orange (the end-point colour) |
| Thymolphthalein | colourless | blue | blue → colourless |

Both indicators change colour over a narrow range of pH, so one drop of solution at the end-point produces a sudden, clear change. **Universal indicator is not suitable** for a titration: it changes through many colours gradually as pH changes, so there is no single, sharp end-point.

**Identifying the end-point** (outcome 2): the end-point is reached when one drop of solution from the burette makes the indicator change colour permanently. With methyl orange and acid in the burette, the yellow solution turns orange; one more drop would turn it red, which means the end-point has been overshot.

## Worked example 1: processing titration results

*25.0 cm³ of sodium hydroxide solution was titrated with dilute hydrochloric acid using thymolphthalein. Complete the table and find the mean titre.*

| | Rough | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Final reading / cm³ | 23.60 | 46.15 | 22.95 | 45.90 |
| Initial reading / cm³ | 0.00 | 23.60 | 0.10 | 22.95 |
| Titre / cm³ | 23.60 | 22.55 | 22.85 | 22.95 |

**Step 1.** Titre = final reading − initial reading for each column, as shown in the bottom row.

**Step 2.** Ignore the rough titre: it was done quickly and overshoots the end-point.

**Step 3.** Find the concordant titres, within 0.10 cm³ of each other: 22.85 and 22.95 cm³. Titration 1 (22.55 cm³) is not concordant and is left out.

**Step 4.** Mean titre = (22.85 + 22.95) ÷ 2 = **22.90 cm³**. Show which results you used, so the examiner can follow the choice.

## Worked example 2: describing the method in an exam answer

*Describe how to find the volume of dilute sulfuric acid needed to neutralise 25.0 cm³ of aqueous potassium hydroxide. [5]*

**Model answer.** Use a volumetric pipette (with a filler) to put 25.0 cm³ of potassium hydroxide into a conical flask [1]. Add a few drops of methyl orange [1]. Fill a burette with the sulfuric acid and record the initial reading [1]. Add the acid while swirling, then dropwise near the end-point, until the indicator turns from yellow to orange [1]. Record the final reading, then repeat until concordant results are obtained [1].

*Why it scores:* each mark is a named piece of apparatus or a named observation. "Add acid until it is neutral" would not score the end-point mark; the colour change must be stated.

## Linking to the mole topic

Once the mean titre is known, the unknown concentration is found with moles = concentration × volume (volume in dm³) and the mole ratio from the balanced equation. That method is practised in [The Mole and the Avogadro Constant](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-the-mole-and-the-avogadro-constant/). In section 12.2 the marks are for the practical method and the end-point.

## Common mistakes

- **Rinsing the burette or pipette with water and using it at once.** The water left inside dilutes the solution. Rinse with the solution it will hold.
- **Reading the top of the meniscus**, or reading from above or below eye level.
- **Leaving the funnel in the burette**, or leaving an air bubble below the tap; both make the titre wrong.
- **Including the rough titre in the mean.**
- **Writing "until it changes colour"** without naming the colours.
- **Recording burette readings to one decimal place.** Use two, ending in 0 or 5.
- **Choosing universal indicator.**

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a titration: give apparatus, volumes and the colour change in order. A numbered list is clear and acceptable.
- **Explain** a technique point: say what would go wrong without it ("so that the acid is not diluted by water left in the burette").
- **Suggest** an improvement: common credited answers include reading at eye level, adding dropwise near the end-point, using a white tile, and repeating to obtain concordant results.
- In results tables, give every reading to two decimal places with the unit in the heading, as the syllabus expects data recorded "to an appropriate degree of precision and using appropriate units".
