Condensed notes on the development of international human rights law. For full explanation and an
annotated plan, use the [Human Rights in international law study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-7162-human-rights-in-international-law/).

## The story in one line

**War and atrocity** → **Nuremberg** → **UN Charter 1945** → **UDHR 1948** (standards, not binding) →
**Council of Europe 1949** → **ECHR signed 1950, in force 1953** (binding, with a court) → **Covenants
1966** → **Protocol 11, 1998** (single full-time Court).

## The Second World War and its aftermath

- Atrocities carried out by states under their own laws: domestic legality did not ensure justice.
- **Four freedoms** (Roosevelt, 1941): speech and expression, worship, from want, from fear.
- **Nuremberg trials (1945 to 1946)**: International Military Tribunal; **crimes against humanity**;
  individuals responsible under international law; obeying orders not a complete defence.
- Aim: international institutions to keep peace and protect rights above the state.

## The United Nations and the UDHR

| Point | Detail |
|---|---|
| UN Charter | Signed San Francisco, June 1945; UN in existence October 1945; purposes include promoting respect for human rights without distinction as to race, sex, language or religion |
| Drafting body | UN Commission on Human Rights, chaired by Eleanor Roosevelt; contributors included René Cassin, John Humphrey, Peng Chun Chang and Charles Malik |
| Adoption | General Assembly, Paris, 10 December 1948: 48 for, none against, eight abstentions |
| Content | 30 Articles; civil and political plus economic and social rights; preamble on "inherent dignity" |
| Status | General Assembly resolution, not a treaty; influential, parts argued to be customary law |
| Follow-up | ICCPR and ICESCR, 1966; with the UDHR, the "International Bill of Human Rights"; monitored by expert committees, no binding court |

## The Council of Europe and the ECHR

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 5 May 1949 | Treaty of London creates the Council of Europe; ten founding states including the UK; seat in Strasbourg |
| 4 November 1950 | ECHR signed in Rome; Sir David Maxwell Fyfe prominent in drafting |
| March 1951 | UK is the first state to ratify |
| 3 September 1953 | ECHR in force after ten ratifications |
| 1954 | European Commission of Human Rights set up |
| 1959 | European Court of Human Rights set up |
| 1966 | UK accepts individual petition and the Court's jurisdiction |
| 1998 | Protocol 11: Commission abolished; single full-time Court; individual petition compulsory |
| 2022 | Russia expelled from the Council of Europe |

- **Committee of Ministers** supervises execution of judgments.
- **Not the EU**: the EU has the Court of Justice in Luxembourg and its own Charter of Fundamental
  Rights. The UK left the EU in 2020 but remains in the Council of Europe and the Convention.

## Comparison

| | UDHR | Covenants | ECHR |
|---|---|---|---|
| Binding? | No | Yes | Yes |
| Enforcement | None directly | Reports to expert committees | Court with binding judgments |
| Scope | All categories | Civil and political; economic, social and cultural | Civil and political |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Crimes against humanity | Serious crimes against civilians, prosecuted at Nuremberg under international law |
| General Assembly resolution | A statement adopted by UN members; not binding like a treaty |
| Treaty | A binding agreement between states |
| Ratification | A state's formal consent to be bound by a treaty |
| Individual petition | The right of a person to bring a complaint to the Strasbourg institutions |
| Protocol | An additional treaty adding rights or changing procedures under the Convention |
| Committee of Ministers | Council of Europe body supervising execution of judgments |

## Exam traps

- Confusing the Council of Europe with the EU, or Strasbourg with Luxembourg.
- Calling the UDHR a treaty.
- Mixing up 1950 (signed) and 1953 (in force).
- Saying UK citizens could petition Strasbourg from 1953; the UK accepted individual petition in 1966.
- Forgetting that the Convention covers civil and political rights, while the UDHR also covers economic
  and social rights.
- Saying Brexit took the UK out of the Convention.

## Self-test

1. Why did the Second World War change attitudes to how states treat their own citizens?
2. What principle of responsibility did the Nuremberg trials establish?
3. When was the UN Charter signed, and what does it say about human rights?
4. Who chaired the UN Commission on Human Rights during the drafting of the UDHR?
5. When and where was the UDHR adopted, and what was the vote?
6. Is the UDHR binding? Explain.
7. Name the two UN Covenants of 1966.
8. When and by which treaty was the Council of Europe created?
9. When was the ECHR signed and when did it come into force?
10. When did the UK accept the right of individual petition?
11. What did Protocol 11 change?
12. Give two differences between the Council of Europe and the European Union.

**Answers:** 1. States had committed atrocities under their own laws, showing domestic law could not be
relied on to protect rights. 2. Individuals can be criminally responsible under international law,
including for crimes against humanity. 3. June 1945; it lists promoting respect for human rights and
fundamental freedoms for all among the UN's purposes. 4. Eleanor Roosevelt. 5. 10 December 1948 in
Paris; 48 for, none against, eight abstentions. 6. No; it is a General Assembly resolution, though
influential and partly argued to reflect customary law. 7. The International Covenant on Civil and
Political Rights and the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights. 8. 5 May 1949, by
the Treaty of London. 9. Signed 4 November 1950 in Rome; in force 3 September 1953. 10. 1966. 11. It
abolished the Commission, created a single full-time Court and made individual petition compulsory. 12.
Any two of: different organisations with different membership; different courts (Strasbourg and
Luxembourg); the UK has left the EU but remains in the Council of Europe; the EU has its own Charter of
Fundamental Rights.
