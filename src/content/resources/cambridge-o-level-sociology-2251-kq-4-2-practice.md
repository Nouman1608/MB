These questions practise key question 4.2 of Cambridge O Level Sociology (2251), *What is the role of
the family for the individual and society?* Set A follows the six-part (a)–(f) structure the syllabus
describes for each Paper 2 question; Set B gives extra practice on individual concepts and on the
debate about whether family life is positive or negative.

---

## Set A — Perspectives on the family

**(a)** Define the terms:
- (i) *warm bath theory* **[2]**
- (ii) *segregated conjugal roles* **[2]**

**(b)** Give **two** functions of the family identified by Murdock. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **three** ways the family may benefit capitalism, according to Marxists. **[6]**

**(d)** Explain **three** ways the family may benefit men more than women, according to feminists. **[6]**

**(e)** Discuss the loss of functions debate. **[8]**

**(f)** Evaluate the view that the family benefits all of its members equally. **[14]**

## Set B — Extra practice

**(g)** Distinguish between the dual burden and the triple shift. **[4]**

**(h)** Explain **two** ways gendered socialisation in the family may prepare girls for domestic
roles. **[4]**

**(i)** Explain **two** criticisms of the functionalist view of the family. **[4]**

**(j)** Explain **two** ways the family may reproduce class inequality. **[4]**

**(k)** Explain **two** reasons why domestic violence may be under-reported. **[4]**

**(l)** Discuss whether the experience of family life is positive or negative for children. **[8]**

## Answers — Set A

**(a)(i) [2]** Parsons's idea that the family stabilises adult personalities by giving them a place
to relax and recover from the stresses of work (1), which keeps them productive and society stable
(1).

**(a)(ii) [2]** Conjugal roles where husband and wife have separate tasks, responsibilities and
leisure (1), such as the husband as breadwinner and the wife responsible for the home (1) (Bott,
1957).

**(b) [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: sexual; reproductive; economic; educational.

**(c) [6]** Three ways, 2 marks each (1 for the point, 1 for development):
- **Unpaid domestic labour** maintains today's workers and raises the next generation at no cost to
  employers (Benston, 1969).
- **Unit of consumption**: families spend wages on goods and services, creating profit; advertising
  targets children, who pressure parents to buy.
- **Inheritance** keeps private property in the same families, reproducing class (Engels, 1884).
- **Ideological control**: children learn to obey authority, preparing them to accept hierarchy at
  work.
- **Illusion of a private life**: the family offers workers a refuge from the pressures of work, which
  helps them keep going without challenging the system (Zaretsky, 1976).

**(d) [6]** Three ways, 2 marks each:
- **Domestic division of labour**: women do most housework and childcare even when in paid work
  (Oakley, 1974).
- **Dual burden or triple shift**: women combine paid work, domestic work and emotion work.
- **Decision-making**: men take the important decisions about money and moving house (Edgell, 1980).
- **Domestic violence**: men's violence against women reflects and maintains patriarchal control
  (Dobash and Dobash, 1979).
- **Gendered socialisation**: girls are channelled towards caring roles, reproducing inequality.

**(e) [8]** Levels: 7–8 clear, developed, both sides with evaluation; 4–6 some development; 1–3
basic, list-like.
- **Parsons:** industrialisation brought **structural differentiation**: factories took over
  production, schools education, hospitals health care.
- The family keeps two core functions, primary socialisation and stabilising adults, and becomes more
  specialised.
- **Fletcher (1966):** functions have grown in importance; parents supervise health and schooling
  more closely; the welfare state supports rather than replaces the family.
- Credit examples such as parents helping with homework or home care for elderly relatives.
- **Evaluation:** in societies where the family is still a unit of production, such as family farms
  or businesses, the loss of functions is less clear; the debate is mainly about industrial societies.

**(f) [14]** Levels: 11–14 balanced, developed, evaluated with a conclusion; 7–10 developed but
less balanced; 4–6 limited; 1–3 basic.

*For the view:*
- Functionalists: Murdock's four functions benefit all members and society.
- Parsons: children gain primary socialisation; adults gain emotional stability.
- Joint conjugal roles and dual-earner couples suggest more equal sharing (Young and Willmott's
  symmetrical family, 1973).
- Families provide care for sick and elderly members.

*Against the view:*
- Feminists: unequal division of labour (Oakley, 1974); triple shift; men's control of important
  decisions (Edgell, 1980).
- Domestic violence and abuse show the family can harm some members (Dobash and Dobash, 1979).
- Marxists: the main beneficiary is capitalism, not members.
- Children may be controlled or harmed; experience varies by age.
- Leach (1967): the isolated nuclear family can become a source of discontent.

*Conclusion:* the family benefits many members, but rarely equally; gender and age shape who gains
most. Credit a reasoned judgement either way.

## Answers — Set B

**(g) [4]** Dual burden: a woman doing paid work and most domestic work (2). Triple shift: paid work,
domestic work and **emotion work**, managing family members' feelings (Duncombe and Marsden) (2).

**(h) [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each: **canalisation**, giving girls toys such as dolls and kitchen sets
that point towards caring roles (Oakley); **manipulation**, praising girls for being helpful and tidy
and discouraging rough play; girls given more household chores than brothers; mothers acting as role
models for domestic work.

**(i) [4]** Two criticisms, 2 marks each: idealised, ignoring conflict and abuse; based on the
middle-class nuclear family and ignores diversity; assumes all members share the same interests,
ignoring gender inequality (feminists); ignores the way the family serves capitalism (Marxists).

**(j) [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each: inheritance of wealth and property keeps advantage in the same
families; middle-class parents pass on cultural capital that helps children succeed at school; wealthy
families can pay for private education and tuition; family contacts help children into well-paid
jobs.

**(k) [4]** Two reasons, 2 marks each: victims fear further violence or losing their home; shame or
stigma; financial dependence on the abuser; abuse seen as a private family matter; fear that police
will not take it seriously; children or cultural pressure to keep the family together.

**(l) [8]** Levels as for A(e).
- *Positive:* primary socialisation; love, security and care; child-centred families give more time
  and resources to children; parents support education.
- *Negative:* child abuse and neglect; witnessing domestic violence; strict control and conflict
  between generations; poverty in some families limits life chances.
- *Evaluation:* functionalists stress the positive; feminists and critics such as Leach (1967) stress
  the dark side; experience depends on family circumstances, class and culture.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need dates for every study?** No. Accurate names and findings matter more, but a date shows
precise knowledge.

**Can I use a Marxist feminist point in a Marxist answer?** Yes, if you explain how it benefits
capitalism, as with Benston's argument about unpaid domestic labour.

**How many perspectives should a (f) answer use?** At least two, set against each other, with a
conclusion that answers the question.
