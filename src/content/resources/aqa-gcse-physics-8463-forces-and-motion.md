## Describing motion

**Distance** is how far an object moves; it is a scalar. **Displacement** is the straight-line distance
from start to finish **with its direction**; it is a vector. A runner who completes one 400 m lap has
travelled 400 m but has a displacement of zero.

**Speed** is a scalar; **velocity** is speed in a given direction. **Higher tier only:** an object moving in a
circle at constant speed has a **changing velocity**, because its direction is always changing. A car going
round a roundabout at a steady 8 m/s is therefore accelerating.

Recall typical speeds: walking about 1.5 m/s, running about 3 m/s, cycling about 6 m/s, and sound in air
about 330 m/s. Real speeds vary with age, terrain, fitness and distance travelled. A car at the UK motorway
limit of 70 mph travels at about 31 m/s.

```
distance travelled = speed × time      s = v t       (recall; constant speed)
average speed = total distance ÷ total time
```

### Distance–time graphs

- Gradient = speed. A horizontal line means the object is stationary.
- A steeper line means a greater speed; a curve means the speed is changing.
- **Higher tier only:** for an accelerating object, draw a **tangent** to the curve at the time required and
  find its gradient. If a tangent at t = 4.0 s passes through (2.0 s, 0 m) and (6.0 s, 24 m), the speed at
  4.0 s is 24 ÷ 4.0 = **6.0 m/s**.

### Acceleration and velocity–time graphs

```
acceleration = change in velocity ÷ time taken      a = Δv / t      (recall)
```

An object that slows down is **decelerating** (negative acceleration).

On a velocity–time graph, the **gradient = acceleration**. **Higher tier only:** the **area under the graph
= distance travelled** (or displacement); count squares where the line is curved.

**Worked example 1.** A train accelerates uniformly from rest to 20 m/s in 40 s, travels at 20 m/s for 60 s,
then decelerates uniformly to rest in 20 s.

```
acceleration    = 20 / 40 = 0.50 m/s²
deceleration    = 20 / 20 = 1.0 m/s²
Higher tier only, distance = area:
  ½ × 40 × 20 + 60 × 20 + ½ × 20 × 20 = 400 + 1200 + 200 = 1800 m
```

You should be able to estimate everyday accelerations. A car reaching 27 m/s from rest in about 10 s has
an acceleration of roughly 3 m/s².

### Uniform acceleration

```
v² − u² = 2 a s      (equation sheet)
```

Near the Earth's surface, any object falling freely accelerates at about **9.8 m/s²**.

**Worked example 2.** A ball is dropped from rest from a height of 1.8 m. Find its speed as it reaches the
ground, ignoring air resistance.

```
v² − 0² = 2 × 9.8 × 1.8 = 35.28
v = √35.28 = 5.9 m/s
```

### Terminal velocity (physics only graphs)

An object falling through a fluid first accelerates because of its weight. As its speed increases, the
drag (air resistance) increases. The resultant force falls, so the acceleration falls. Eventually the drag
equals the weight, the resultant force is zero and the object moves at its **terminal velocity**.

**Interpreting a skydiver's velocity–time graph:**

| Section of graph | Shape | Forces |
|---|---|---|
| Just after jumping | Steep, straight-looking start (gradient about 9.8 m/s²) | Weight much bigger than air resistance |
| Speeding up | Curve, gradient decreasing | Air resistance growing; resultant force falling |
| First terminal velocity | Horizontal line | Air resistance = weight |
| Parachute opens | Sudden fall in velocity (steep negative gradient) | Air resistance much bigger than weight; resultant force upwards |
| Second terminal velocity | Horizontal at a lower velocity | Air resistance = weight again, at a lower speed because the area is larger |

## Newton's laws

**First law.** If the resultant force on an object is zero, a stationary object stays stationary and a moving
object continues at the same speed in the same direction. So a vehicle at a steady speed has its resistive
forces balancing its driving force. The velocity changes only if there is a resultant force.
**Higher tier only:** the tendency of objects to continue in their state of rest or uniform motion is
**inertia**.

**Second law.** The acceleration of an object is proportional to the resultant force on it and inversely
proportional to its mass.

```
resultant force = mass × acceleration      F = m a      (recall)
```

**Worked example 3.** A 950 kg car has a driving force of 2600 N and resistive forces of 700 N.

```
resultant force = 2600 − 700 = 1900 N
a = F / m = 1900 / 950 = 2.0 m/s²
```

**Higher tier only: inertial mass** is a measure of how difficult it is to change an object's velocity. It is
defined as the ratio **force ÷ acceleration**. A resultant force of 600 N that gives a boat an acceleration of
0.40 m/s² shows an inertial mass of 600 / 0.40 = **1500 kg**.

**Required practical 7: analysing the results.** Plotting acceleration against force (at constant mass)
gives a straight line through the origin: a ∝ F. Plotting acceleration against mass (at constant force)
gives a curve: doubling the mass halves the acceleration. When you vary the force, keep the total mass of
the system constant by moving masses from the trolley to the hanger, because the hanging masses are being
accelerated too.

**Third law.** Whenever two objects interact, the forces they exert on each other are equal and opposite.
In an equilibrium situation, such as a person standing still on the floor, the person pushes down on the
floor and the floor pushes up on the person with an equal force. The person's weight is balanced by the
floor's push, but the third-law partner of the weight is the person's gravitational pull on the Earth.

## Forces and braking

```
stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance
```

- **Thinking distance** is travelled during the driver's reaction time, at constant speed, so it is
  **proportional to speed**. Reaction times vary from about 0.2 s to 0.9 s; tiredness, drugs, alcohol and
  distractions make them longer.
- **Braking distance** increases with speed, with wet or icy roads, and with worn brakes or tyres. For a
  given braking force, v² = 2as shows that braking distance is **proportional to speed squared**.

### Estimating over a range of speeds (worked example 4)

Take a reaction time of 0.7 s and a deceleration of 7 m/s².

| Speed (m/s) | Thinking distance (m) | Braking distance (m) | Stopping distance (m) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | 0.7 × 10 = 7 | 10² ÷ (2 × 7) ≈ 7 | ≈ 14 |
| 20 | 14 | 20² ÷ 14 ≈ 29 | ≈ 43 |
| 30 | 21 | 30² ÷ 14 ≈ 64 | ≈ 85 |

Tripling the speed triples the thinking distance but makes the braking distance nine times larger. On a
graph of stopping distance against speed, this shows as a curve that gets steeper.

### Measuring reaction time

A partner drops a ruler between your finger and thumb, and you catch it. The distance it falls gives your
reaction time. If the ruler falls 0.20 m:

```
v² = 2 × 9.8 × 0.20 = 3.92   →   v = 1.98 m/s
t = Δv / a = 1.98 / 9.8 = 0.20 s
```

### Braking and large decelerations

When the brakes are applied, work done by friction between the brakes and the wheel reduces the vehicle's
kinetic energy, and the brakes' temperature rises. The greater the speed, the greater the braking force
needed to stop in a certain distance. A greater braking force gives a greater deceleration, and large
decelerations can make the brakes overheat or the driver lose control.

**Higher tier only, worked example 5.** Estimate the braking force on a 1500 kg car that stops from 25 m/s
in 50 m.

```
0 − 25² = 2 × a × 50   →   a = −625 / 100 = −6.25 m/s²
F = m a = 1500 × 6.25 ≈ 9400 N
```

## Common mistakes

- Reading a speed from a velocity–time graph's gradient (that is acceleration).
- Forgetting the square root in v² − u² = 2as.
- Saying an object at terminal velocity has no forces on it. The forces are balanced.
- Using the driving force in F = ma instead of the resultant force.
- Saying braking distance doubles when speed doubles. It quadruples, for the same braking force.
- Pairing weight with the normal contact force as a third-law pair.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** changes in motion by naming the forces, comparing their sizes, then stating the resultant
  force and its effect.
- **Estimate**: use sensible rounded values and say they are estimates; use the symbol ~ for an approximate
  value.
- **Interpret a graph**: quote values with units and say whether the gradient is increasing, decreasing or
  constant.
