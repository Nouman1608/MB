## 10.4.1 Accounting data and strategy

Three uses:

1. **Where are we now?** Strengths and weaknesses from profitability, liquidity, gearing.
2. **What can we afford?** Retained profit, cash and borrowing capacity limit strategy and decide how it is financed.
3. **Is it working?** Results against targets decide whether to continue, change or stop.

**Annual report contents (typical):** audited financial statements with notes; chair's and directors' statements on performance and strategy; auditor's report; governance information; wider information such as environmental impact.

| User | Uses it for | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| Managers | Progress against targets | Little on competitors |
| Shareholders | Profit, dividends, plans | Directors' statements present the company favourably |
| Lenders | Gearing, debts, cash | Out of date when published |
| Employees | Signs of job security | Totals hide individual sites |
| Competitors | Margins, growth | Key details withheld |

## Performance over time and against competitors

- One year can mislead; look for a **trend** over three or more years.
- Compare with a rival in the same industry, ideally with the same year end.
- Ask what the trend implies for strategy: rebuild margins, cut borrowing, invest, or slow growth.

## Debt or equity: effects on ratios

| Ratio | Financed by loan | Financed by share issue |
|---|---|---|
| ROCE | Same as share issue (uses profit before interest; same capital employed) | Same as loan |
| Gearing | Rises | Falls |
| Interest | Rises | Unchanged |
| Profit for the year | Lower (more interest) | Higher |
| EPS | Usually higher if the project earns more than the interest rate | Diluted by extra shares |
| Control | Kept by existing owners | Diluted |

**Rule:** debt raises EPS when the project's return exceeds the interest rate, but adds risk if profits fall.

**Worked reminder: Elmstead plc.** Needs 2,000; project adds 300 to profit from operations (15% return). Loan at 5% costs 100 a year. Return 15% > cost 5%, so a loan raises EPS more than a share issue would, but raises gearing.

## Dividend strategy: effects on ratios

| Change | Dividend cover | Dividend yield | Retained profit and cash | Later effects |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Raise dividend | Falls | Rises (at same price) | Lower | Liquidity weaker; may need more borrowing (gearing up) |
| Cut dividend | Rises | Falls | Higher | Funds for investment; share price may fall |

## Growth: effects on ratios

| Growth route | Short-term effects |
|---|---|
| Organic, new branches | Margins and ROCE dip; inventory and receivables rise; liquidity weakens |
| Debt-financed takeover | Gearing up; ROCE may fall if price paid is high |
| Share-financed takeover | Gearing down; EPS may fall |
| Fast credit sales growth | Receivables days up; cash pressure; overtrading risk |

## Other strategies: effects on ratios

| Strategy | Effects |
|---|---|
| Sale and leaseback | Cash and liquidity up; rent lowers profit margin; if loans are repaid, gearing falls |
| Cost cutting | Margins and ROCE up, unless revenue falls |
| Price cuts | Gross margin down; inventory turnover may rise |
| JIT | Inventory turnover up; less cash tied up in stock |
| Longer customer credit | Revenue may rise; receivables days up; cash down |

## Limitations of published accounts and ratios

- Historical, not forward-looking.
- Window dressing at the year end.
- Estimates in inventory and depreciation.
- Different accounting policies, year ends and business models.
- Non-financial factors missing.
- Inflation distorts comparisons over time.
- Ratios show what changed, not why.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Annual report | Yearly document with audited statements and directors' commentary |
| Window dressing | Making year-end figures look better than normal |
| Dilution | Each share's share of profit and control falls when new shares are issued |
| Earnings per share | Profit for the year ÷ number of shares |
| Overtrading | Growing faster than working capital can support |

## Exam traps

- Saying a loan lowers ROCE when profit from operations and capital employed are the same as with shares.
- Forgetting EPS dilution with a share issue.
- Treating a dividend rise as good news with no cost.
- Giving a limitation of ratios without linking it to the case.
- Ignoring the condition in a debt or equity judgement: stability of profit and interest rates.

## Self-test

1. Give three ways accounting data helps strategy.
2. Name three typical contents of an annual report.
3. Give one caution for shareholders using the annual report.
4. Why is ROCE the same whether an expansion is financed by loan or shares?
5. When does debt finance raise EPS?
6. Profit for the year 900; 6,000 thousand shares. Calculate EPS.
7. What happens to dividend cover when the dividend is raised?
8. Dividend per share 9 cents; price $1.50. Calculate dividend yield.
9. Give one short-term effect of organic growth on ratios.
10. How does sale and leaseback affect liquidity?
11. What is window dressing?
12. Give two limitations of ratio analysis.

### Answers

1. Shows the current position; shows what can be afforded; checks whether the strategy is working.
2. Any three: audited financial statements and notes; chair's or directors' statements; auditor's report; governance information; environmental or wider information.
3. Directors' statements are written to present the company favourably (or: the data is historical).
4. ROCE uses profit from operations (before interest), and capital employed rises by the same amount either way.
5. When the project's return is higher than the interest rate on the loan.
6. 900 ÷ 6,000 = **$0.15** (15 cents).
7. It falls.
8. 0.09 ÷ 1.50 × 100 = **6%**.
9. Margins and ROCE fall at first (or liquidity weakens as inventory and receivables rise).
10. Cash rises, so liquidity improves.
11. Making year-end figures look better than usual, for example by collecting debts just before the year end.
12. Any two: historical; window dressing; estimates; different accounting policies; non-financial factors ignored; inflation.
