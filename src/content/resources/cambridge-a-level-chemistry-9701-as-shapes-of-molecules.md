This guide covers subtopic **3.5, Shapes of molecules**, from Topic 3, Chemical bonding, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is AS Level content. The [Chemical Bonding: Shapes and Intermolecular Forces](/resources/as-chemical-bonding-shapes-and-intermolecular-forces/) guide lists the seven named shapes; this page gives you a reliable method for **predicting** the shape of an unfamiliar molecule or ion, which is the second learning outcome and the one where most marks are lost. Condensed notes are in the [shapes of molecules revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-as-shapes-of-molecules-revision-notes/), and you can practise with the [shapes of molecules practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-as-shapes-of-molecules-practice/).

## Syllabus coverage

Candidates should be able to:

1. state and explain the shapes of, and bond angles in, molecules by using VSEPR theory, including as simple examples BF₃ (trigonal planar, 120°), CO₂ (linear, 180°), CH₄ (tetrahedral, 109.5°), NH₃ (pyramidal, 107°), H₂O (non-linear, 104.5°), SF₆ (octahedral, 90°) and PF₅ (trigonal bipyramidal, 120° and 90°)
2. predict the shapes of, and bond angles in, molecules and ions analogous to those specified in outcome 1

"Analogous" means a species with the **same number of bonding regions and lone pairs** around the central atom as one of the seven named examples. Learn the seven, then learn to count.

## VSEPR theory in three statements

1. Electron pairs in the outer shell of the central atom **repel** each other.
2. They arrange themselves **as far apart as possible** to minimise repulsion.
3. **Lone pairs repel more strongly than bonding pairs**: lone pair–lone pair > lone pair–bonding pair > bonding pair–bonding pair. Each lone pair that replaces a bonding pair closes the remaining bond angles by roughly 2 to 3°.

Lone pairs repel more because they are held by only one nucleus, so their electron density is closer to the central atom and spread more widely.

A **double or triple bond counts as one region** of electron density for shape purposes, because all its electrons lie between the same two atoms.

## A counting method that works

For a central atom bonded to H atoms or halogen atoms by single bonds:

    lone pairs on central atom = (outer electrons of central atom − number of bonds ± charge) ÷ 2

Add one electron for each negative charge; subtract one for each positive charge.

For central atoms with double bonds to oxygen, count the electrons the central atom uses: one per single bond, two per double bond. Whatever is left, divided by two, is the number of lone pairs.

Then:

    total regions = bonded atoms + lone pairs

The total gives the **arrangement of electron pairs**; the lone pairs decide the **name of the shape**, which describes only the positions of the atoms.

## The seven named shapes

| Regions | Lone pairs | Shape | Angle | Named example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 0 | linear | 180° | CO₂ |
| 3 | 0 | trigonal planar | 120° | BF₃ |
| 4 | 0 | tetrahedral | 109.5° | CH₄ |
| 4 | 1 | pyramidal | 107° | NH₃ |
| 4 | 2 | non-linear | 104.5° | H₂O |
| 5 | 0 | trigonal bipyramidal | 120° and 90° | PF₅ |
| 6 | 0 | octahedral | 90° | SF₆ |

Notice that NH₃ and H₂O both have four regions arranged roughly tetrahedrally. Their shapes have different names because the lone pairs are not counted as part of the shape.

For PF₅, three fluorine atoms lie in a triangle around phosphorus (the equatorial positions, 120° apart) and two lie above and below (the axial positions, 90° to the triangle). You must give **both** angles.

## Worked examples: predicting analogous shapes

**Example 1: the oxonium ion, H₃O⁺.**
Oxygen has 6 outer electrons; 3 bonds; charge +1.
Lone pairs = (6 − 3 − 1) ÷ 2 = 1. Regions = 3 + 1 = 4.
Analogous to NH₃: **pyramidal, about 107°**.

**Example 2: the amide ion, NH₂⁻.**
Nitrogen has 5 outer electrons; 2 bonds; charge −1.
Lone pairs = (5 − 2 + 1) ÷ 2 = 2. Regions = 2 + 2 = 4.
Analogous to H₂O: **non-linear, about 104.5°**.

**Example 3: the hexafluorophosphate ion, PF₆⁻.**
Phosphorus has 5 outer electrons; 6 bonds; charge −1.
Lone pairs = (5 − 6 + 1) ÷ 2 = 0. Regions = 6.
Analogous to SF₆: **octahedral, 90°**.

**Example 4: the tetrachlorophosphonium ion, PCl₄⁺.**
Lone pairs = (5 − 4 − 1) ÷ 2 = 0. Regions = 4.
Analogous to CH₄: **tetrahedral, 109.5°**.

**Example 5: sulfur dioxide, SO₂.**
Sulfur has 6 outer electrons and forms two S=O double bonds, using 4. Remaining 2 electrons = 1 lone pair. Regions = 2 bonded atoms + 1 lone pair = 3.
Three regions with one lone pair: **non-linear (bent)**, with the angle **slightly less than 120°**, because the lone pair pushes the two S=O regions closer together.

**Example 6: the carbonate ion, CO₃²⁻, and the nitrate ion, NO₃⁻.**
In each, the central atom is bonded to three oxygen atoms and has no lone pair (the negative charges sit on oxygen atoms). Three regions, no lone pairs: analogous to BF₃, **trigonal planar, 120°**.

## Annotated model answer

**Question.** Predict the shape of the PH₄⁺ ion and its bond angle. Explain your answer. [3]

- Phosphorus has four bonding pairs and no lone pairs in PH₄⁺. *(one mark: correct count)*
- The four pairs repel equally and get as far apart as possible. *(one mark: VSEPR reasoning)*
- So the ion is tetrahedral with H–P–H angles of 109.5°. *(one mark: shape and angle)*

An answer that gives "tetrahedral, 109.5°" alone typically earns only the final mark, because an "explain" question needs the electron-pair reasoning.

## Explaining why the angle changes

**Question.** Explain why the bond angle in NH₃ (107°) is smaller than in CH₄ (109.5°). [2]

Both have four electron pairs around the central atom. In NH₃, one pair is a lone pair, which repels the bonding pairs more strongly than a bonding pair does, pushing the N–H bonds closer together.

The same reasoning extends to H₂O: two lone pairs give even more repulsion, so the angle falls to 104.5°.

## Drawing shapes in the exam

- Use **wedges** (towards you) and **dashed lines** (away from you) for three-dimensional shapes such as tetrahedral and pyramidal.
- **Show lone pairs** on the central atom when the question asks for shape and explanation.
- **Label one bond angle** with its value.
- For trigonal bipyramidal, mark one 120° angle in the triangle and one 90° angle between an axial and an equatorial bond.

## How shape links to the rest of Topic 3

- Shape decides whether a molecule with polar bonds is **polar overall**: in CO₂ and BF₃ the bond dipoles cancel; in NH₃ and H₂O they do not. This is the job of subtopic 3.6.
- Tetrahedral, trigonal planar and linear carbon atoms correspond to **sp³, sp² and sp** hybridisation from subtopic 3.4.

## Common mistakes

- Calling NH₃ "tetrahedral". That is the arrangement of its electron pairs; the shape of the atoms is pyramidal. Use the syllabus names, such as "non-linear" for H₂O.
- Counting a double bond as two regions in CO₂ or SO₂.
- Forgetting to adjust for the charge on an ion.
- Giving only 120° (or only 90°) for a trigonal bipyramidal molecule.
- Saying lone pairs "take up more room" without saying they **repel more**.
- Using the shape of a different ion with the same formula pattern without counting, for example assuming every AX₃ species is trigonal planar.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is NH₃ not trigonal planar like BF₃?**
Nitrogen has a lone pair as well as three bonding pairs, so there are four regions. Boron in BF₃ has only three bonding pairs and no lone pair.

**Do I need to know shapes with lone pairs around a five- or six-pair central atom?**
The named examples with five and six pairs have no lone pairs. Analogous species, such as PCl₅ and PF₆⁻, follow the same pattern.
