## Questions

**1.** Sales in Country M's tile market (£ million) are: firm A 72, B 55, C 41, D 33, E 19, and many small firms 80 in total.

**(a)** Work out the concentration ratio for the three largest firms. **[2]**
**(b)** Firms A and D merge. Work out the new ratio for the three largest firms. **[2]**

**2.** Explain **two** ways in which oligopolistic markets can differ from one another. **[4]**

**3.** A firm sells 500 units a week at £18. Above this price its demand is P = 28 − 0.02Q. Below it, demand is P = 38 − 0.04Q.

**(a)** Calculate marginal revenue at 500 units on each part of the demand curve. **[2]**
**(b)** The firm's marginal cost rises from £3 to £7. Explain the effect on price and output. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage change in revenue if the firm raises price to £20. **[3]**
**(d)** State one limitation of the model. **[1]**

**4.** **(a)** Distinguish between tacit and overt collusion. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain **two** reasons why a cartel may break down. **[4]**

**5.** Two firms with equal output form a cartel. Market demand is P = 150 − 3Q (Q in thousand units, P in £). Each firm's marginal cost is £30.

**(a)** Calculate the cartel's output and price. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate joint profit and profit per firm, in £ thousand. **[2]**
**(c)** Firm X secretly produces 13 thousand units while Y produces 10 thousand. Show that X gains and that joint profit falls. **[3]**

**6.** Firms R and S each choose Hold or Cut price. Profits in £m (R first, S second): both Hold 15, 15; R cuts and S holds 22, 5; R holds and S cuts 5, 22; both cut 10, 10.

**(a)** Explain what is likely to happen if each firm acts independently. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why the best joint outcome is hard to sustain. **[3]**

**7.** A firm sells 4.0 million units and earns a margin of £3.60 a unit. An advertising campaign costs £1.8 million.

**(a)** Calculate the extra units needed to break even. **[2]**
**(b)** Express this as a percentage of current sales. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the net gain or loss if sales rise by 15%. **[2]**
**(d)** State one way advertising might disadvantage consumers. **[1]**

**8.** A firm with average cost £16 normally sells 80 thousand units at £22. In a one-year price war it sells 100 thousand units at £14. After a rival leaves it sells 110 thousand at £23.

**(a)** Calculate profit in a normal year, in the war year and after the rival leaves. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate how many years it takes to recover the sacrifice. **[2]**
**(c)** Distinguish predatory pricing from limit pricing. **[2]**

**9.** **(a)** Classify each as an innocent or strategic barrier: a high capital cost of plant; exclusive contracts with retailers; spare capacity kept to threaten price cuts. **[3]**
**(b)** A firm with revenue £250 million spends £20 million on R&D. Calculate R&D as a percentage of revenue. **[1]**
**(c)** A project has a 0.35 chance of success, worth £90 million, and costs £20 million. Calculate the expected net gain. **[2]**
**(d)** State why R&D matters in oligopoly. **[1]**

**10.** Demand is P = 120 − 4Q (Q in thousand units, P in £) and marginal cost is £24.

**(a)** Calculate output, price and profit for a profit-maximising firm. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate output, price and profit for a revenue maximiser. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest one reason managers might pursue revenue. **[1]**

**11.** Evaluate whether oligopoly is likely to benefit consumers. **[12]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Largest three: 72 + 55 + 41 = 168 [1]. Total sales = 72 + 55 + 41 + 33 + 19 + 80 = 300, so ratio = 168 / 300 = **56%** [1].
**(b)** Merged firm 72 + 33 = 105, then 105 + 55 + 41 = 201 [1]. Ratio = 201 / 300 = **67%** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers gain credit by showing the total and the sum of the top firms before dividing. Bare percentages leave no working to credit.

**2.** Number of firms or concentration: some have two or three firms, others several [1]; this changes how easily they can coordinate [1]. Ease of entry: some have high barriers, others lower [1]; this changes the threat from new rivals and firms' pricing freedom [1]. Product differentiation is also acceptable. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Two named dimensions are needed, each with its consequence. Stating the dimension alone leaves the examiner little to credit.

**3. (a)** Above: MR = 28 − 0.04 × 500 = **8** [1]. Below: MR = 38 − 0.08 × 500 = **−2** [1].
**(b)** MC of £3 and £7 are both between −2 and 8 [1], so output stays 500 and price £18 [1].
**(c)** At £20, Q = (28 − 20) / 0.02 = 400 [1]. Revenue = 20 × 400 = 8,000, compared with 18 × 500 = 9,000 [1]. Change = −1,000 / 9,000 = **−11.1%** [1].
**(d)** For example, it gives no reason for the original kink price [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the demand curve above the kink for a price rise and show Q from it. Using the lower curve is a common slip.

**4. (a)** Tacit collusion is coordination without a formal agreement, for example following a leader [1]. Overt collusion is an open or formal agreement, as in a cartel [1].
**(b)** Each firm gains by cheating on the agreed output while others comply [1], so agreements are hard to police [1]. Differing costs or demand make a common price or quota hard to agree [1], and entrants or non-members can undercut the cartel price [1]. Legal penalties are also acceptable. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Each reason needs its link to breakdown, not just a label. "Cheating" alone is incomplete.

**5. (a)** MR = 150 − 6Q [1]. Set equal to 30: Q = **20 thousand** [1]. P = 150 − 60 = **£90** [1].
**(b)** Joint profit = (90 − 30) × 20 = **£1,200 thousand** [1]; each firm **£600 thousand** [1].
**(c)** Q = 23, P = 150 − 69 = £81 [1]. X earns 51 × 13 = 663, above 600 [1]. Y earns 51 × 10 = 510; joint profit 1,173, below 1,200 [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the whole market output to find the new price. Using only X's output gives the wrong price.

**6. (a)** If S holds, R gains by cutting (22 against 15) [1]. If S cuts, R still does better by cutting (10 against 5) [1]. The same is true for S, so both cut and each earns 10 [1].
**(b)** Both holding gives 15 each, total 30, against 20 when both cut [1]. Each firm gains from cheating (22 against 15) [1]. Neither can be sure the other will hold, so there is uncertainty and temptation [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Explain the reasoning for each rival choice; stating "they will both cut" with no comparison reads as assertion.

**7. (a)** Extra units = cost / margin = 1.8 / 3.6 [1] = **0.5 million** [1].
**(b)** 0.5 / 4.0 = **12.5%** [1].
**(c)** Extra units = 0.15 × 4.0 = 0.6 million; contribution = 0.6 × 3.6 = 2.16 [1]. Net = 2.16 − 1.8 = **+£0.36 million** [1].
**(d)** For example, costs passed on in higher prices [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* State the unit (million units or £ million). Mixing units can make a correct method look wrong.

**8. (a)** Normal: 80 × (22 − 16) = **£480 thousand** [1]. War: 100 × (14 − 16) = **−£200 thousand** [1]. After: 110 × (23 − 16) = **£770 thousand** [1].
**(b)** Sacrifice = 480 + 200 = 680 [1]. Gain per year = 770 − 480 = 290, so payback = 680 / 290 = **2.3 years** [1].
**(c)** Predatory pricing sets price below cost to remove a rival [1]. Limit pricing keeps price above cost but low enough that entry is unprofitable [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare the war year with a normal year, not with zero. The sacrifice is the profit given up.

**9. (a)** Capital cost: innocent [1]; exclusive contracts: strategic [1]; spare capacity: strategic [1].
**(b)** 20 / 250 × 100 = **8%** [1].
**(c)** Expected value = 0.35 × 90 = 31.5 [1]; net = 31.5 − 20 = **£11.5 million** [1].
**(d)** It allows competition without price cuts and can create a lead or a barrier [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Give the reason a barrier is strategic (chosen by incumbents), not just the label.

**10. (a)** MR = 120 − 8Q = 24, so Q = **12** [1]; P = 120 − 48 = **£72** [1]; profit = 48 × 12 = **£576 thousand** [1].
**(b)** MR = 0, so Q = **15** and P = **£60** [1]; profit = 36 × 15 = **£540 thousand** [1].
**(c)** For example, managers' pay may be linked to sales [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Set MR to zero for revenue maximisation, not to MC. Check the answer against the task's objective.

**11.** Gains for consumers: scale economies can feed through into lower prices [1]; large profits can pay for R&D and new products [1]; rivalry through quality gives choice [1]; prices may be stable [1]. Costs: collusion can raise price and cut output towards the monopoly level [1]. Barriers protect profit [1]. Advertising may be wasteful and raise price [1]. Less pressure to cut costs [1]. Evaluation: the effect depends on entry conditions and contestability [1]; whether firms collude or compete [1]; whether spending is real innovation [1]; and the time period, because gains from R&D take time. A supported judgement for a stated market [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers tend to do better when they compare competitive and collusive cases and reach a conclusion tied to specific conditions, rather than listing both sides.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Skipping the working for concentration ratios.
- Using the wrong demand segment in kinked demand questions.
- Setting MR = MC with the demand slope instead of the MR slope.
- Giving cartel profit without dividing by the number of firms.
- Comparing the price war with zero instead of with normal profit.
- Describing barriers without saying who creates them.
- Listing advantages and disadvantages with no judgement.
- Leaving out units.
