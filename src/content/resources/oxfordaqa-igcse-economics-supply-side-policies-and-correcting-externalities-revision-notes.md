## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Supply-side policy | Action to raise the economy's capacity to produce and cut costs, shifting long-run supply right |
| Direct tax | Tax charged on the earnings or profit of the person or firm paying it |
| Privatisation | Sale of a government-run firm to private owners |
| Deregulation | Removal or loosening of rules that limit entry or behaviour |
| Externality | A side effect, good or bad, that reaches someone who is neither buyer nor seller |
| Social cost | Private cost with external cost added |
| Social benefit | Private benefit with external benefit added |

## The four supply-side policies at a glance

| Policy | Mechanism | Main advantage | Main disadvantage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Education and training | Skills raise productivity | Growth, fewer structural job losses | Costly, slow, may not match employers' needs |
| Lower direct taxes | Bigger reward for working | Work incentive, may attract skilled labour | Lost revenue, helps high earners most |
| Lower taxes on business profits | Firms keep more, invest more | Investment, enterprise | Lost revenue, profits may not be invested |
| Privatisation and deregulation | Competition and profit motive | Efficiency, lower prices, lower subsidies | Monopoly power, job losses, weaker standards |

## Chains of reasoning to learn

- **Training:** more spending on skills → output per worker up → unit costs down → capacity and exports up → growth and lower inflation pressure.
- **Lower income tax:** more take-home pay per hour → more willing to work or take a job → bigger labour supply → higher output and fewer people out of work.
- **Lower profit tax:** more retained profit → more investment → more capital per worker → higher productivity and capacity.
- **Deregulation:** fewer barriers → more firms → competition → lower prices and better service.
- **Privatisation:** profit motive → cost cutting → lower costs; but profit may be put above poorer customers.

## Linking to objectives

| Objective | Likely effect | Possible problem |
|---|---|---|
| Growth | Capacity rises | Slow to appear |
| Employment | Better skills and incentives | Privatisation may cut jobs |
| Price stability | Lower unit costs | Tax cuts may raise demand |
| Balance of payments | Cheaper, better exports | Needs time |
| Inequality | Training can raise low earnings | Tax cuts favour high earners |

## Method in steps: a supply-side evaluation answer

1. Name the policy and say what it changes (skills, incentives, costs or competition).
2. Give the chain: policy, change, effect on output or costs, effect on the objective.
3. Give one disadvantage: cost, time lag, fairness or conflict with another objective.
4. Say what the result depends on, such as how people respond or the size of the cut.
5. Reach a judgement. Name the policy and the objective it suits best.

## Worked reminders

**Income tax cut.** Pay 2,200; rate falls from 25% to 20%.

- Before: `2,200 × 0.75 = 1,650`. After: `2,200 × 0.80 = 1,760`.
- Gain 110, or `110 / 1,650 × 100 ≈ 6.7%`.

**Profit tax cut.** Profit 120,000; rate falls from 20% to 16%.

- Tax before: `120,000 × 0.20 = 24,000`. After: `120,000 × 0.16 = 19,200`.
- Retained: 96,000 rises to 100,800, an increase of 4,800.

**Revenue effect.** 500 firms each make 40,000 profit. A 20% rate gives `500 × 40,000 × 0.20 = 4,000,000`. At 15% on the same profits: `500 × 40,000 × 0.15 = 3,000,000`. Revenue falls by 1,000,000 unless profits rise.

**Productivity.** Output per worker rises from 25 to 28: `(28 - 25) / 25 × 100 = 12%`.

**Potential output.** Rises from 500 to 525: `(525 - 500) / 500 × 100 = 5%`.

**Training bill.** 20 trainees at 1,500 each: `20 × 1,500 = 30,000`.

## Externalities: policy toolkit

| Tool | Used for | How it works | Drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Indirect tax | Negative | Raises cost, supply shifts left, price up, quantity down | Hard to size; falls on poor consumers |
| Subsidy | Positive | Lowers cost, supply shifts right, price down, quantity up | Cost to taxpayers |
| Ban, limit or standard | Negative | Sets or forbids the activity | Enforcement; black markets |
| Fine | Negative | Penalises breaking a rule | Must be caught and large enough |
| Direct provision | Positive | State supplies the good | Costly; may be inefficient |
| Information campaign | Both | Changes demand through awareness | Slow; uncertain effect |

**Tax and subsidy sums.**

- Tax of 12 per unit on 250 units: `12 × 250 = 3,000` revenue.
- Subsidy of 5 per unit on 800 units: `5 × 800 = 4,000` cost.
- Private cost 14 plus external cost 5 gives social cost `14 + 5 = 19`.

**Worked market reminder (tax).** Demand is `Qd = 90 - 3P` and supply is `Qs = 3P - 30`.

- Equilibrium: `90 - 3P = 3P - 30`, so `P = 20` and `Q = 30`.
- A tax of 6 per unit: `Qs = 3(P - 6) - 30 = 3P - 48`.
- New equilibrium: `90 - 3P = 3P - 48`, so `P = 23` and `Q = 21`.
- Buyers pay 3 more. Sellers keep `23 - 6 = 17`, which is 3 less. Revenue is `6 × 21 = 126`.
- Check the shifts in words: supply moved left along an unchanged demand curve, so price rose and quantity fell.

**Elasticity link.** A tax is more effective at cutting output where demand is elastic. If PED is -0.25, a 20% price rise cuts quantity by `-0.25 × 20 = -5%` only.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Tax and cap:** both can reach the same quantity, but a tax gives the government the revenue and a cap leaves it with the firms.
- **Tax and subsidy shifts:** both move the supply curve. A tax shifts it left, a subsidy shifts it right. Demand does not move.
- **Direct and indirect taxes:** direct taxes (income, profit) are supply-side tools; indirect taxes (on goods) are the usual tool for a negative externality.
- **Efficiency and fairness:** a policy can raise output but widen inequality.

## Quick self-test

1. State the effect of supply-side policies on long-run supply in words.
2. Pay of 2,000 is taxed at 25%. The rate falls to 20%. Find take-home pay before and after.
3. Give two advantages and two disadvantages of lower taxes on business profits.
4. Explain why training takes years to raise growth.
5. A government sells a loss-making state firm. State one benefit and one risk.
6. Name two ways deregulation can lower prices.
7. A good has private cost 30 and external cost 12. Find social cost and the external cost as a percentage of private cost.
8. A tax of 8 per unit is placed on 400 units. Find revenue.
9. Name two policies for a positive externality.
10. Why might a cap on pollution be harder to enforce than a tax?

### Answers

1. Long-run supply shifts right, so capacity rises.
2. Before: `2,000 × 0.75 = 1,500`. After: `2,000 × 0.80 = 1,600`. Gain 100.
3. Advantages: more investment; more enterprise or inward investment. Disadvantages: lost revenue; profits may not be invested; helps owners.
4. People must be trained, then join or return to the workforce, and firms must put the skills to use.
5. Benefit: the end of subsidies and likely efficiency gains. Risk: job losses or a private monopoly raising prices.
6. More firms can enter and compete; lower costs can be passed on.
7. `30 + 12 = 42`. `12 / 30 × 100 = 40%`.
8. `8 × 400 = 3,200`.
9. Subsidy; direct provision; information campaign.
10. It needs inspection and punishment of breaches, and firms have a reason to hide them.

## Evaluation prompts to keep ready

- **How big?** A small tax cut may change behaviour very little.
- **How fast?** Training and investment take years; a ban can act at once.
- **Who gains and loses?** Compare owners, workers, consumers and the government.
- **Who pays?** Think of taxpayers, households on low incomes and firms.
- **What else changes?** A policy for one objective may cut across another, so name the conflict.
- **What does it depend on?** Elasticity of demand, willingness of firms to invest, and enforcement.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "supply-side policies increase demand" instead of capacity and costs.
- Stating a benefit with no chain from the policy to the objective.
- Forgetting the time lag when a question asks about a short-run problem.
- Saying a tax cut raises revenue with no condition.
- Shifting demand instead of supply in a tax or subsidy answer.
- Giving only advantages when the question says "evaluate" or "discuss".
- Calling a cost to the buyer or seller an externality.
- Leaving out units or the sign in a calculation.
- Failing to use the context, such as the named country or good.
