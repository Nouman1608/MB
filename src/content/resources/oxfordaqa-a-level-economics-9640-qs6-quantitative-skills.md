This guide covers quantitative skill **QS6** in section 3.5 of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Economics (9640) specification: "calculate cost, revenue and profit, including average and totals (for A2 assessments, students will also need to calculate marginal values)". Quantitative skills can be tested in any unit, so QS6 turns up in AS questions on production and costs and in A2 questions on market structures. Every firm and figure below is invented.

The formulas themselves are set out in the broader [cost, revenue, profit and elasticity guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-economics-cost-revenue-and-profit-calculations-and-elasticity/). This page goes further: it shows how to work backwards from averages, how to fill an incomplete cost table, how to build totals from marginal values and why the option with the highest revenue is not always the most profitable. Condensed recall is in the [QS6 revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-economics-9640-qs6-quantitative-skills-revision-notes/), and the [QS6 practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-economics-9640-qs6-quantitative-skills-practice/) test it. ## What QS6 asks of you

| Values | Assessed at | Typical task |
|---|---|---|
| Totals: total cost, total revenue, profit | AS and A2 | Find profit from price, output and costs |
| Averages: average fixed, variable and total cost; average revenue | AS and A2 | Find cost per unit, or work back from it to a total |
| Marginal values: marginal cost, marginal revenue | A2 only | Find the change in a total for one more unit and use it to locate the profit-maximising output |

The economics behind these calculations is in the content on costs, revenue and profit at AS (section 3.1.3) and on the theory of the firm at A2. QS6 is the skill of doing the arithmetic accurately and then saying what the number shows.

## Three ideas that make every calculation easier

**1. Every average hides a total.** An average is a total divided by output, so multiplying by output gets the total back:

```
TC = AC x Q        VC = AVC x Q        FC = AFC x Q
TR = AR x Q        profit = (AR - AC) x Q
```

The last line is the one candidates forget. Profit per unit is AR minus AC, and total profit is profit per unit multiplied by output. On a cost and revenue diagram it is the rectangle between the AR and AC curves, as wide as the output.

**2. Fixed cost never changes in the short run, so AFC must keep falling.** If AFC is 6 at 100 units, FC is 600, so AFC at 200 units is 3. When a question gives fixed cost at one output, you know it at every output.

**3. Marginal values join up the totals (A2).** Total cost at any output is fixed cost plus the marginal cost of every unit up to that output. Total revenue is the sum of the marginal revenues. When output rises in steps of more than one unit, divide the change in the total by the change in output:

```
MC per unit = change in TC / change in Q
MR per unit = change in TR / change in Q
```

## Worked example 1: completing a cost table

A print shop has fixed costs of 600 a week. Output is measured in orders. The question gives one fact at each output and asks you to complete the table.

| Orders | Given | TC | VC | AFC | AVC | AC |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 100 | AC = 15.00 | 1,500 | 900 | 6.00 | 9.00 | 15.00 |
| 150 | AVC = 9.20 | 1,980 | 1,380 | 4.00 | 9.20 | 13.20 |
| 200 | TC = 2,600 | 2,600 | 2,000 | 3.00 | 10.00 | 13.00 |
| 250 | AC = 13.60 | 3,400 | 2,800 | 2.40 | 11.20 | 13.60 |

Working at each row:

- **100 orders:** TC = 15.00 x 100 = 1,500. VC = 1,500 - 600 = 900. AVC = 900 / 100 = 9.00.
- **150 orders:** VC = 9.20 x 150 = 1,380. TC = 1,380 + 600 = 1,980. AC = 1,980 / 150 = 13.20.
- **200 orders:** VC = 2,600 - 600 = 2,000. AC = 2,600 / 200 = 13.00.
- **250 orders:** TC = 13.60 x 250 = 3,400. VC = 2,800, AVC = 11.20.

Check each row with AC = AFC + AVC: at 150 orders, 4.00 + 9.20 = 13.20.

**A2 extension.** Marginal cost per order over each step is (1,980 - 1,500) / 50 = 9.60, then 620 / 50 = 12.40, then 800 / 50 = 16.00. While MC is below AC (12.40 against 13.20), AC falls. Once MC is above AC (16.00 against 13.00), AC rises. Lowest AC in the table is at 200 orders.

## Worked example 2 (A2): building totals from marginal values

A cake stall has fixed costs of 50 a day. The marginal cost of each extra tray of cakes and the price needed to sell each quantity are given.

| Trays | Price (AR) | TR | MR | MC | TC | Profit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 55 | 55 | 55 | 20 | 70 | -15 |
| 2 | 50 | 100 | 45 | 16 | 86 | 14 |
| 3 | 45 | 135 | 35 | 18 | 104 | 31 |
| 4 | 40 | 160 | 25 | 24 | 128 | 32 |
| 5 | 35 | 175 | 15 | 32 | 160 | 15 |
| 6 | 30 | 180 | 5 | 42 | 202 | -22 |

TC is built by adding each MC to the previous total, starting from fixed cost: 50 + 20 = 70, then 70 + 16 = 86, and so on. MR is the change in TR: 100 - 55 = 45.

Profit is highest at 4 trays (32). The marginal values explain why. The fourth tray adds 25 to revenue and 24 to cost, so it adds 1 to profit. The fifth adds 15 to revenue but 32 to cost, so it cuts profit by 17. Producing every unit for which MR is at least MC, and stopping there, is the profit-maximising rule in marginal form. Check with the averages: at 4 trays AC = 128 / 4 = 32, so profit = (40 - 32) x 4 = 32.

## Worked example 3: higher revenue is not higher profit

A firm has fixed costs of 2,700 a week and a constant AVC of 6.50. It is choosing between two prices.

| | Option A | Option B |
|---|---|---|
| Price | 12 | 14 |
| Weekly sales | 900 | 700 |
| TR | 10,800 | 9,800 |
| TC = 2,700 + 6.50 x Q | 8,550 | 7,250 |
| AC | 9.50 | 10.36 |
| Profit | 2,250 | 2,550 |

Option B earns 1,000 less revenue but 300 more profit, because the 200 units it no longer sells each cost 6.50 to make (1,300 saved). AC is higher under B because fixed cost is spread over fewer units, yet profit per unit (14 - 10.36 = 3.64) more than makes up for the lower output. A one-line answer that chooses A "because it earns more" misses the cost side entirely.

## Normal profit in a calculation

Economists treat **normal profit**, the minimum return that keeps the entrepreneur in the industry, as a cost. If a question includes it in total cost, then TR = TC means the firm earns exactly normal profit, not that it is failing. Any surplus is **supernormal profit**. If a café owner could earn 28,000 a year elsewhere, revenue of 150,000 and other costs of 115,000 leave 35,000 of accounting profit, but only 7,000 of supernormal profit once the 28,000 is counted. Read the question to see whether its cost figures include normal profit.

## Common mistakes

- Dividing total profit by output and calling it the profit: that is profit per unit.
- Recalculating fixed cost at each output instead of treating it as constant.
- Writing MC as TC divided by output (that is AC).
- Forgetting to divide by the change in output when output rises in steps of 10 or 50.
- Building TC from MC but leaving out fixed cost.
- Choosing an option because revenue is higher, without checking cost.
- Rounding AC early, then multiplying the rounded figure by a large output.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate:** set out the formula, substitute, and give the answer with its unit (per unit, per week). A correct method can earn credit even if the arithmetic slips.
- **Explain:** after the number, add one sentence that links it to the economics: AC falls because MC is below it; profit rises because the cost saved exceeds the revenue lost.
- **Analyse or evaluate:** use your figures as evidence, then question them. Is AVC really constant? Will rivals react to a price rise? Is the data for one week typical?
- Check totals against averages: profit from TR - TC should equal (AR - AC) x Q.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need marginal values in AS papers?**
The specification states that marginal values are needed for A2 assessments. Totals and averages are needed at both stages.

**How do I find fixed cost if the question gives only averages?**
Multiply average fixed cost by output, or subtract total variable cost from total cost. Either gives the same figure at every output in the short run.

**Why does profit peak where MR stops exceeding MC?**
Each unit with MR above MC adds to profit; the first unit with MC above MR reduces it, so profit is highest just before that unit.
