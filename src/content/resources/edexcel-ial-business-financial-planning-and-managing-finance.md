## 1. Sales, revenue and costs

**Sales volume** is the number of units sold. **Sales revenue** = selling price × sales volume.

**Fixed costs** do not change with output in the short run (rent, insurance). **Variable costs** change with output (materials). **Total costs** = fixed costs + total variable costs. **Average cost** = total costs ÷ output.

**Worked example: Oxmoor Candles.** Selling price 8.50, variable cost 3.50 a candle, fixed costs 9,000 a month.

```
At 3,000 candles:
Sales revenue  = 8.50 x 3,000 = 25,500
Variable costs = 3.50 x 3,000 = 10,500
Total costs    = 9,000 + 10,500 = 19,500
Average cost   = 19,500 / 3,000 = 6.50

At 4,000 candles:
Total costs  = 9,000 + 14,000 = 23,000
Average cost = 23,000 / 4,000 = 5.75
```

Average cost falls as output rises because the same fixed costs are spread over more units.

**Ways of improving sales volumes and sales revenues** include: better promotion; improving the product; widening distribution (online sales, new outlets); and changing price. A price cut may lift volume but lowers revenue per unit, so total sales revenue can rise or fall. A price rise does the reverse. Judge each in context.

## 2. Sales forecasting

A **sales forecast** is an estimate of future sales. **Purposes**: it sets the base for the budget and cash-flow forecast, guides production and inventory, helps decide staffing, and supports a loan request.

**Factors affecting forecasts**

- **Consumer trends**: changing tastes, fashions and habits.
- **Economic variables**: income levels, interest rates, exchange rates, unemployment.
- **Actions of competitors**: price cuts, launches, promotions.

**Difficulties**: the future is uncertain; a new business has no sales history; trends change quickly; and competitors react unpredictably. The further ahead the forecast, the weaker it is. An overestimate leaves the business with surplus inventory and cash tied up; an underestimate loses sales.

## 3. Break-even

**Contribution** (per unit) = selling price − variable cost per unit. Each unit sold contributes this amount towards fixed costs, then profit.

**Break-even point** is where total fixed costs + total variable costs = total revenue, so profit is zero. Using contribution:

```
Break-even output = total fixed costs / contribution per unit
Margin of safety  = actual (or budgeted) output - break-even output
```

The margin of safety is how far sales can fall before losses begin. Many texts also give it as a percentage of actual output; that is the standard form, not given in the specification.

**Oxmoor Candles again.** Contribution = 8.50 − 3.50 = 5.00. Break-even = 9,000 ÷ 5.00 = **1,800 candles**. At 3,000 candles the margin of safety is 3,000 − 1,800 = **1,200 candles** (40% of actual output).

Check: at 1,800 candles, revenue = 15,300 and total costs = 9,000 + 6,300 = 15,300.

A price rise to 9.50 lifts contribution to 6.00 and cuts break-even to 9,000 ÷ 6.00 = 1,500 candles. If fixed costs rise to 10,500, break-even rises to 2,100 candles.

**Interpreting a break-even chart.** The chart has output on the horizontal axis and money on the vertical axis.

```
Line               Starts at          Slope
Fixed costs        9,000 at 0 units   flat
Total costs        9,000 at 0 units   rises by 3.50 a unit
Sales revenue      0 at 0 units       rises by 8.50 a unit
```

Where the revenue line crosses the total cost line is break-even (1,800 candles). Below it the gap between the lines is a loss; above it the gap is profit. The margin of safety is the horizontal distance from break-even to current output. A steeper revenue line means a higher price; a higher total cost line means higher fixed or variable costs.

**Limitations of break-even analysis**: it assumes all output is sold, a constant selling price and constant variable cost per unit (no bulk discounts), and fixed costs that stay fixed; it suits a single product; it depends on forecasts and costs that may be wrong; and it ignores cash timing and competitors' reactions.

## 4. Cash flow

A **cash-flow forecast** shows expected cash inflows and outflows, month by month, and the resulting balance.

```
Net cash flow   = cash inflows - cash outflows
Closing balance = opening balance + net cash flow
```

The closing balance becomes next month's opening balance.

**Worked example: Marsden Cycle Works.** Opening balance 3,000.

| Month | Opening | Inflows | Outflows | Net | Closing |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3,000 | 14,000 | 15,500 | −1,500 | 1,500 |
| 2 | 1,500 | 12,000 | 16,000 | −4,000 | −2,500 |
| 3 | −2,500 | 18,000 | 17,000 | 1,000 | −1,500 |
| 4 | −1,500 | 20,000 | 16,500 | 3,500 | 2,000 |

**Interpretation**: the balance is negative in months 2 and 3, so Marsden needs an overdraft or another short-term source of finance for those months. By month 4 it recovers. Inflows total 64,000 and outflows 65,000, so the four months are a net outflow of 1,000.

**Use**: it warns of shortages in advance, supports loan or overdraft requests, and helps time purchases. **Limitations**: it rests on forecast sales (see section 2); late-paying customers or unexpected costs upset it; it must be updated; and a positive balance does not prove profit.

## 5. Budgets

A **budget** is a financial plan for a future period, set for a department or the whole business. **Purposes**: to set targets, control spending, allocate resources, coordinate departments, motivate managers and allow later comparison with actual results.

**Types of budget**

- **Based on historical figures**: this period's budget starts from last period's actual figures, adjusted for expected changes. It is quick, but carries over past inefficiency.
- **Zero based**: every budget starts at zero and each item of spending must be justified. It challenges waste but takes more time.

**Variance analysis** compares budgeted and actual figures. Variance = actual − budget. A **favourable** variance raises profit (higher revenue or lower cost); an **adverse** variance lowers it.

**Worked example: Ashgrove Bakehouse, one month.**

| Item | Budget | Actual | Variance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sales revenue | 40,000 | 36,500 | −3,500 adverse |
| Materials | 14,000 | 15,400 | +1,400 adverse |
| Wages | 9,000 | 8,700 | −300 favourable |
| Marketing | 3,000 | 3,000 | 0 |
| Profit | 14,000 | 9,400 | −4,600 adverse |

The profit variance equals −3,500 − 1,400 + 300 = −4,600. Revenue fell 8.75% below budget and materials ran 10% over, so investigate both.

**Difficulties of budgeting**: budgets rely on forecasts; they take time; managers may bid for padded budgets; fixed budgets are slow to change when conditions shift; and missing a target can demotivate staff.

## 6. Profit

The **statement of comprehensive income** (profit and loss account) in the specification's layout:

```
revenue
- cost of sales          = gross profit
- other operating expenses = operating profit
- interest               = profit for the year (net profit)
```

**Worked example: Hallam Vale Textiles.** Revenue 480,000; cost of sales 288,000; other operating expenses 138,000; interest 9,000.

```
Gross profit            = 480,000 - 288,000 = 192,000
Operating profit        = 192,000 - 138,000 =  54,000
Profit for the year     =  54,000 -   9,000 =  45,000

Gross profit margin     = 192,000 / 480,000 x 100 = 40%
Operating profit margin =  54,000 / 480,000 x 100 = 11.25%
Profit for the year margin = 45,000 / 480,000 x 100 = 9.4%
```

Each margin is the profit measure ÷ revenue × 100. Compare margins with earlier years and rivals.

**Ways to increase profits**: raise revenue (price, volume) or cut costs. **Ways to improve profitability** (margins): a 5% price rise with volume held lifts revenue to 504,000 and gross profit margin from 40% to 42.9% in this example; negotiating cheaper materials (a 2% fall in cost of sales raises gross profit to 197,760); cutting other operating expenses; and cutting interest by repaying debt. Raising volume alone raises profit but not necessarily the margins.

## 7. Liquidity

**Profit is not cash.** Profit is revenue minus costs over a period, counted when earned or incurred. Cash is money actually received and paid. A credit sale of 12,000 at a cost of 7,500 adds 4,500 to profit now, but no cash arrives until the customer pays.

The **statement of financial position** (balance sheet) lists non-current assets, current assets (inventory, trade receivables, cash), current liabilities (trade payables, overdraft) and non-current liabilities.

```
Current ratio   = current assets / current liabilities
Acid test ratio = (current assets - inventory) / current liabilities
Working capital = current assets - current liabilities
```

**Worked example: Drystone Wholesale.** Inventory 36,000; trade receivables 24,000; cash 6,000; trade payables 28,000; overdraft 14,000.

```
Current assets      = 66,000   Current liabilities = 42,000
Current ratio       = 66,000 / 42,000 = 1.57
Acid test ratio     = 30,000 / 42,000 = 0.71
Working capital     = 24,000
```

The acid test is below 1: without selling inventory, Drystone cannot cover all its short-term debts.

**Ways to improve liquidity**

- **Assets**: sell surplus non-current assets for cash.
- **Supplier credit terms**: negotiate longer payment periods so cash leaves later.
- **Factoring**: sell trade receivables to a finance firm for immediate cash, at a fee.
- **Inventory (just in time)**: hold less inventory, releasing cash. If Drystone cut inventory to 20,000 and held the 16,000 as cash, the current ratio stays at 1.57 but the acid test rises to 46,000 ÷ 42,000 = **1.10**.

**Working capital** is the money available for day-to-day running. Cash matters because wages, suppliers and tax must be paid on time. Too little risks failure; too much idle cash earns nothing.

## 8. Business failure

**Internal causes**: poor management of cash flow; overestimation of sales; **overtrading** (expanding output faster than the cash to fund it); poor inventory control; poor marketing; poor quality.

**External causes**: market conditions; competition; the economy; exchange rates; interest rates; government regulations; supplier problems; natural phenomena.

Link causes to figures: a business that overestimated sales may hold unsold inventory, pay for it on credit and run out of cash.

## Common errors

- Dividing fixed costs by selling price rather than contribution.
- Giving break-even in money when units are asked for, and the reverse.
- Calling a negative closing balance a loss; it is a cash shortfall.
- Writing variances without the words favourable or adverse.
- Forgetting to remove inventory in the acid test.
- Saying a high current ratio is always good; idle cash and slow-moving inventory can inflate it.
- Listing causes of failure without linking them to the business in the source.
