# Key concepts in unit tasks

Examples are written in English. Do any task that asks you to produce language in the language your school course uses, and ask your school teacher to check it. MYP has no prescribed content list: schools design their own units, so ask your teacher which concepts and contexts your units use.

Course hub: [/boards/ib/ib-myp/myp-language-acquisition/](/boards/ib/ib-myp/myp-language-acquisition/). For the main explanation of the concepts, see the reviewed [key and related concepts study guide](/resources/ib-myp-language-acquisition-key-and-related-concepts/). This page is different: it shows how to *use* a key concept to read and plan a task.

## What the brief says

The IB brief names four key concepts for language acquisition: **communication, connections, creativity and culture**. Key concepts broadly frame the whole MYP curriculum. You explore them through the six global contexts: identities and relationships; orientation in space and time; personal and cultural expression; scientific and technical innovation; globalization and sustainability; fairness and development. The curriculum explores factual, conceptual and debatable questions.

## One task, four readings

Take a single task (original, for Marlbridge): *write a message to a new classmate who has just joined your school.* Read it through each key concept in turn.

| Key concept | The question it asks you | What changes in your message |
|---|---|---|
| Communication | Who is the audience, what is the purpose and what is the mode? | A friendly written message, short, with a clear welcome and one practical piece of information |
| Connections | What links can I draw to what the reader already knows? | Mention something you both share, such as the same lessons or a place near school |
| Creativity | How can I use the language I have in a fresh way? | An unexpected opening line instead of a stock greeting |
| Culture | What does this community expect in a welcome? | Choose a form of address and level of politeness that fits the community |

The same task is not four tasks. The concept is a *lens* that tells you what to check.

## Worked example: choosing the lens for a speaking task

A speaking task asks you to describe a festival you enjoy to a visitor who has never heard of it.

1. **Name the lens.** Culture is the strongest: the visitor needs the festival's meaning, not only its date.
2. **Add a second lens.** Communication: the visitor is a stranger, so use clear, polite language and avoid private jokes.
3. **Plan three moves.** Say what it is; explain why people keep doing it; invite a question.
4. **Check against the brief.** Criterion C asks you to interact on topics of personal, local and global interest and significance. Your festival is local and personal; one comparison with another place makes it wider.

## Worked example: writing a statement of inquiry from a task

Task: a unit on the school day in different places. Key concept: **culture**. Related concept: **conventions**. Context: **identities and relationships**. A possible statement: "Cultural expectations about the school day shape how students relate to each other and to their teachers." Check it is a general claim (not a question), that it names a key concept, uses a related concept and points to a context.

## Common errors

- Treating a concept as a topic: "food" is a topic, "culture" is a concept.
- Using only one concept for every task.
- Describing facts about a culture without explaining how it shapes language.

## Check your understanding

1. Name the four key concepts the brief gives for language acquisition. *Communication, connections, creativity, culture.*
2. Which concept asks you to think about audience, purpose and mode? *Communication.*
3. Why is "food" not a key concept? *It is a topic; a concept such as culture applies across many topics.*
