**Key question 3 of Core Content Option B in Cambridge O Level History 2147 asks how far Hitler's foreign policy was
to blame for the outbreak of war in Europe in 1939.** The wording invites a weighing exercise: Hitler's aims and
actions on one side, and on the other the Treaty of Versailles, the failures of the League, appeasement and the
Nazi–Soviet Pact, all of which are named focus points. The question was the Paper 2 prescribed topic for November 2025
and remains on Paper 1 throughout 2024–2026.

For condensed recall, use the [Hitler's foreign policy revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-world-history-2147-hitlers-foreign-policy-revision-notes/);
for exam questions with mark schemes, use the
[Hitler's foreign policy practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-world-history-2147-hitlers-foreign-policy-practice/).
The [O Level twentieth century study guide](/resources/o-level-world-history-twentieth-century-1919-1989/) places
this question in Option B.

## What the specification sets

Focus points:

1. What were the long-term consequences of the Treaty of Versailles?
2. What were the consequences of the failures of the League of Nations in the 1930s?
3. Was the policy of appeasement justified?
4. How important was the Nazi–Soviet Pact?
5. Why did Britain and France declare war on Germany in September 1939?

Specified content: Hitler's foreign policy aims; rearmament; the Saar; remilitarisation of the Rhineland; the
Rome–Berlin Axis and the Anti-Comintern Pact; German and Italian involvement in the Spanish Civil War; Anschluss with
Austria; the crisis over Czechoslovakia and the Munich Agreement; the Polish crisis and the outbreak of war.

## Hitler's aims

Hitler set out his ideas in *Mein Kampf* (written in the mid-1920s). Four aims recur:

1. **Overturn the Treaty of Versailles**: rearm, recover lost land and end reparations.
2. **Unite all German-speaking people** in one Reich, including Austria and the Sudetenland.
3. **Win Lebensraum** ("living space") in Eastern Europe, especially at the expense of Poland and the USSR.
4. **Destroy communism**, which he linked to his racial ideas.

The first two aims had some sympathy abroad, because many people in Britain thought Versailles unfair. The third could
only be achieved by war. Historians disagree about how far Hitler followed a plan. A. J. P. Taylor, in *The Origins of
the Second World War* (1961), argued that Hitler was an opportunist who exploited the mistakes of others; most
historians since have given more weight to his long-term aims, while accepting that he seized chances as they came.

## The steps to war, 1933–39

| Date | Action | Reaction | What it taught Hitler |
|---|---|---|---|
| October 1933 | Left the Disarmament Conference and the League | Little | International bodies could be ignored |
| January 1934 | Non-aggression pact with Poland | Surprise in France | He could split France's eastern allies |
| July 1934 | Austrian Nazis murdered Chancellor Dollfuss | Mussolini moved troops to the Brenner Pass; Hitler backed off | He needed Italy's friendship before taking Austria |
| January 1935 | Saar plebiscite: about 90% voted to rejoin Germany | Accepted as legal | Self-determination could serve German aims |
| March 1935 | Conscription and an air force announced openly | Stresa Front (April) protested | Protest was not action |
| June 1935 | Anglo-German Naval Agreement: navy up to 35% of Britain's | France angered | Britain would bargain over Versailles |
| March 1936 | Troops into the demilitarised Rhineland | Britain and France did not act | Risk-taking paid |
| 1936–39 | Condor Legion and Italian troops aid Franco in Spain; bombing of Guernica (1937) | Non-Intervention Committee ignored | Tested weapons and tactics; drew Italy closer |
| 1936–37 | Rome–Berlin Axis; Anti-Comintern Pact with Japan, joined by Italy | Little | Hitler had allies |
| March 1938 | Anschluss with Austria | Protest only | Union with Austria was possible without war |
| September 1938 | Munich Agreement gives Germany the Sudetenland | Chamberlain claims "peace for our time" | Britain and France would give way |
| March 1939 | Occupation of Bohemia and Moravia; Memel taken | Britain and France guarantee Poland | The limit of appeasement had been reached |
| August 1939 | Nazi–Soviet Pact | Britain confirms its guarantee to Poland | War on one front only |
| 1 September 1939 | Invasion of Poland | Britain and France declare war on 3 September | |

## Long-term consequences of Versailles

Versailles gave Hitler grievances to exploit and a ready audience. The disarmament clauses, the Rhineland, the Saar,
the ban on Anschluss and the Polish Corridor were each the subject of a step in the table above. It also left Germany
surrounded by weaker states, and Germany's potential economic power intact. The Treaty therefore helps explain why
Hitler's early demands seemed reasonable to some observers, but it did not decide that he would go beyond them.

## Consequences of the League's failures

Manchuria and Abyssinia showed that collective security would not stop a determined great power. Abyssinia pushed
Mussolini towards Hitler and dropped the barrier he had placed in Austria's way in 1934. While the powers were
focused on Abyssinia, Hitler remilitarised the Rhineland. After 1936 the League was not used in any of the crises over
Austria, Czechoslovakia or Poland.

## Was appeasement justified?

| Justified | Not justified |
|---|---|
| Britain was not ready to fight; rearmament, especially of the air force, needed time | It allowed Germany to grow stronger faster than Britain and France |
| Public opinion feared another war on the scale of 1914–18 and feared bombing | It encouraged Hitler to believe Britain and France would always back down |
| Many saw Versailles as unfair, so German demands seemed partly reasonable | Czechoslovakia, with strong frontier defences, its army and the Škoda arms works, was abandoned |
| The Dominions were reluctant to support a war over Central Europe | It excluded the USSR from Munich, damaging any chance of an alliance |
| Communism was seen by some as a greater danger | Hitler's own words in *Mein Kampf* showed aims that could not be appeased |

A balanced view: appeasement was understandable in 1936–38, and Munich bought time for British rearmament, but it was
based on a false belief that Hitler's aims were limited. March 1939 proved that belief wrong.

## How important was the Nazi–Soviet Pact?

Signed on 23 August 1939 by the foreign ministers Ribbentrop and Molotov, it promised non-aggression and contained a
secret protocol dividing Poland and placing the Baltic states and part of Romania in the Soviet sphere.

- **For Hitler**, it removed the danger of a two-front war and meant he could attack Poland expecting Britain and
  France to back down again.
- **For Stalin**, it gained time to prepare and territory as a buffer. He distrusted Britain and France after Munich,
  and their negotiators in Moscow in August 1939 had little authority.
- **Importance:** it made the invasion of Poland possible within a week. It did not cause Hitler's aims, but it removed
  the last obstacle to acting on them.

## Why did Britain and France declare war?

After March 1939, Hitler had broken a signed agreement and taken non-German territory, so his claim to want only
German lands was disproved. Britain and France had guaranteed Poland, and to abandon it would leave no limit on German
expansion. Britain was now better rearmed, especially with modern fighters and radar under development, and public
opinion had hardened. When Germany invaded Poland on 1 September 1939 and ignored an ultimatum to withdraw, Britain
declared war on 3 September, followed by France the same day.

## Annotated model answer: a (b) question

*Why did Britain change its policy towards Germany in 1939? Explain your answer.*

> Britain changed its policy because Hitler's occupation of Bohemia and Moravia in March 1939 showed that his aims
> were not limited to German-speaking people. In September 1938 he had declared that the Sudetenland was his last
> territorial demand in Europe; six months later he took Prague, so Chamberlain could no longer trust any agreement with him. **[Reason with specific
> evidence and a link to the change.]** A second reason was rearmament. By 1939 Britain had built up its air defences,
> so its leaders felt more able to risk war than in 1938. **[Second reason, explained.]** This led to the guarantee to
> Poland on 31 March 1939. **[Outcome linked.]**

## Common mistakes

- Treating appeasement as cowardice without explaining the reasons for it.
- Saying Britain went to war over Czechoslovakia.
- Forgetting the Saar and the Spanish Civil War, which are named in the content.
- Writing a narrative of events in a "how far" answer without weighing other factors against Hitler.

## Exam technique for 2147

- **Describe**: a single event, such as the Anschluss, in precise steps.
- **Explain**: why a decision was made, such as Chamberlain's at Munich.
- **How far**: weigh Hitler against Versailles, the League, appeasement and the Pact, and reach a judgement.
- **Sources**: many Paper 2 sources on this topic are British cartoons of 1936–39. Decide whether the cartoonist
  supports or attacks appeasement.
