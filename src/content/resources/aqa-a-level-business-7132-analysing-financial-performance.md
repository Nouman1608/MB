## Budgets and variance analysis

A **budget** is a financial plan for a future period, setting expected income and spending. Budgets are set for the business as a whole or for departments, often by agreeing figures between managers and senior staff, using past data, sales forecasts and expected cost changes.

**Value of budgeting:**

- sets targets and gives direction
- controls spending: managers know their limits
- helps coordinate departments
- allows **variance analysis** to spot problems early
- helps to allocate resources and secure finance
- can motivate staff when targets are agreed with them.

**Limits:** budgets can be inaccurate if forecasts are poor; setting them takes time; they can be too rigid when conditions change; managers may inflate budgets or spend everything to avoid a smaller budget next year; and a focus on hitting the budget can harm quality or long-term investment.

### Variances

A **variance** is the difference between the budgeted figure and the actual figure.

- A **favourable variance** increases profit: revenue higher than budget or costs lower than budget.
- An **adverse variance** reduces profit: revenue lower than budget or costs higher than budget.

*Worked example.* Tamsin Tiles budgets and actual results for a quarter:

| Item | Budget | Actual | Variance | Type |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | £500,000 | £470,000 | £30,000 | Adverse |
| Materials | £200,000 | £215,000 | £15,000 | Adverse |
| Labour | £120,000 | £114,000 | £6,000 | Favourable |
| Overheads | £80,000 | £80,000 | £0 | None |
| **Profit** | **£100,000** | **£61,000** | **£39,000** | **Adverse** |

Budgeted profit = 500,000 − 200,000 − 120,000 − 80,000 = £100,000. Actual profit = 470,000 − 215,000 − 114,000 − 80,000 = £61,000. The profit variance is 39% below budget (39,000 ÷ 100,000). Variances should be investigated: sales may have fallen because of a rival's price cut or a weak market, materials may have risen in price, or waste may have increased. The favourable labour variance may be due to lower output, not savings; if sales fell then fewer hours were needed. Managers should find the cause before deciding on action.

## Cash flow forecasts

A **cash flow forecast** predicts cash receipts and payments over future months, showing the expected **closing balance** each month. It is built from sales forecasts, payment terms, costs and planned purchases.

| | January | February | March |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opening balance | £10,000 | £4,000 | −£3,000 |
| Receipts | £30,000 | £28,000 | £45,000 |
| Payments | £36,000 | £35,000 | £30,000 |
| Net cash flow | −£6,000 | −£7,000 | +£15,000 |
| **Closing balance** | **£4,000** | **−£3,000** | **£12,000** |

Opening balance for each month equals the previous month's closing balance. Closing = opening + receipts − payments. The forecast shows a shortfall of £3,000 in February, so the business should arrange an overdraft, delay a payment, collect money from customers earlier or ask suppliers for longer credit.

**Value:** it warns of problems before they arise, supports loan applications and helps timing of big purchases. **Limits:** it is only as accurate as its assumptions; sales and customer payment times are uncertain; unexpected events (a bad debt, a price rise) change the picture; and it needs regular updating.

## Break-even analysis

**Break-even** is the level of output at which total revenue equals total cost, so there is neither profit nor loss.

### Key formulas

- **Contribution per unit** = selling price − variable cost per unit
- **Total contribution** = contribution per unit × output
- **Profit** = total contribution − fixed costs
- **Break-even output** = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit
- **Margin of safety** = actual (or budgeted) output − break-even output
- Margin of safety as a percentage = margin of safety ÷ actual output × 100

### Worked example

Perrin Pots sells ceramic planters at £40. Variable cost is £22 per unit. Fixed costs are £90,000 a year.

- Contribution per unit = 40 − 22 = **£18**.
- Break-even output = 90,000 ÷ 18 = **5,000 units**; break-even revenue = 5,000 × £40 = **£200,000**.
- If actual sales are 6,500 units: margin of safety = 6,500 − 5,000 = **1,500 units**, which is 1,500 ÷ 6,500 = **23.1%** of sales.
- Total contribution = 6,500 × 18 = £117,000; profit = 117,000 − 90,000 = **£27,000**.
- Target profit of £36,000 needs output of (90,000 + 36,000) ÷ 18 = **7,000 units**.

### The break-even chart

The chart has output on the horizontal axis and money (£) on the vertical axis. It has three lines:

- **Fixed costs**: a horizontal line at £90,000.
- **Total costs**: starts at £90,000 on the vertical axis and rises by £22 per unit.
- **Total revenue**: starts at zero and rises by £40 per unit.

Revenue crosses total cost at 5,000 units and £200,000: the break-even point. To the left, total costs are above revenue (a loss); to the right, revenue is above costs (a profit). The margin of safety is the horizontal distance between actual output and the break-even output. The vertical gap between the revenue and total cost lines at any output shows profit or loss.

### Effects of changes

| Change | Effect on chart | Effect on break-even |
|---|---|---|
| Price rises to £44 | Revenue line steeper | Contribution £22; break-even 90,000 ÷ 22 = **4,091 units** (lower) |
| Price falls to £36 | Revenue line flatter | Contribution £14; break-even 90,000 ÷ 14 = **6,429 units** (higher) |
| Variable cost per unit rises to £24 | Total cost line steeper | Contribution £16; break-even 90,000 ÷ 16 = **5,625 units** (higher) |
| Fixed costs rise to £108,000 | Fixed and total cost lines shift up | Break-even 108,000 ÷ 18 = **6,000 units** (higher) |
| Output rises | Move right along the chart | Margin of safety grows (if above break-even) |

### Value and limits

**Value:** simple; shows risk (how far sales can fall before losses); helps set prices and output targets; useful in loan applications and when planning a new product. **Limits:** it assumes all output is sold and that costs and prices stay constant (in reality discounts and bulk buying change them); it assumes a single product (or fixed mix); fixed costs may change at higher output; and it ignores demand: it does not say whether 5,000 units will actually sell.

## Analysing profitability

Profit margins show profit as a percentage of revenue:

- **Gross profit margin** = gross profit ÷ revenue × 100
- **Operating profit margin** (profit from operations) = operating profit ÷ revenue × 100
- **Profit for the year margin** = profit for the year ÷ revenue × 100

*Worked example.* Hallam Home Goods:

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | £1,600,000 | £1,800,000 |
| Gross profit | £704,000 | £720,000 |
| Operating profit | £160,000 | £180,000 |
| Profit for the year | n/a | £120,000 |

- Gross margin: Year 1 = 704 ÷ 1,600 = **44%**; Year 2 = 720 ÷ 1,800 = **40%**.
- Operating margin: Year 1 = 160 ÷ 1,600 = **10%**; Year 2 = 180 ÷ 1,800 = **10%**.
- Profit for the year margin, Year 2 = 120 ÷ 1,800 = **6.7%**.

**Interpretation.** Revenue grew 12.5%, but the gross margin fell four percentage points, which suggests higher purchase costs or price cuts to win sales. The operating margin held steady, so overheads must have fallen as a percentage of revenue: Year 1 operating expenses were 704,000 − 160,000 = £544,000, or 34% of revenue; Year 2 they were 720,000 − 180,000 = £540,000, or 30%. Hallam controlled overheads while its direct costs rose in proportion. It should find out why the gross margin has fallen and whether the lower price is a deliberate strategy. Compare margins with earlier years, targets and competitors, and bear in mind differences in business type: a supermarket has a low margin and high volume; a jeweller a high margin and low volume.

## Timings of cash inflows and outflows

- **Receivables** (trade receivables, debtors) are amounts owed to the business by customers who have bought on credit.
- **Payables** (trade payables, creditors) are amounts the business owes to suppliers for goods bought on credit.

Cash flow problems often come from timing. If Hallam sells goods worth £60,000 on 60 days' credit but must pay suppliers within 30 days, it must fund a 30-day gap from its own cash or an overdraft. The longer customers take to pay (receivables rise), the weaker the cash position; the longer the business can delay paying suppliers (payables rise), the better the cash position, though stretching supplier credit risks damaging relationships. Seasonal sales, large one-off payments (tax, equipment) and payment terms all affect timing. A forecast makes these gaps visible.

## Using data in financial decisions and planning

Financial data helps managers decide whether to accept an order (using contribution), set prices, plan finance, judge a manager's performance (variances), and choose between projects. Its limits are that it is historical, may be inaccurate, can hide non-financial factors such as staff morale and customer satisfaction, and must be interpreted alongside market conditions and competitors' performance. Technology (spreadsheets, accounting software) makes calculation and updating faster but cannot improve poor assumptions.

## Technique for exam questions

- For calculations, set out formula, working and units.
- For variances, state whether adverse or favourable and what it does to profit.
- For break-even, label axes and lines on any diagram; say what changes shift or rotate lines.
- Interpret every result in the context of the business, and assess the reliability of the data.

## Common mistakes

- Calling a higher cost than budget a favourable variance.
- Forgetting to add fixed costs to the total cost line on a break-even chart.
- Using total contribution instead of contribution per unit in the break-even formula.
- Treating margin of safety as a percentage when the question asks for units, or the reverse.
- Mixing up the three profit margins.
- Ignoring the timing of receipts when forecasting cash.
