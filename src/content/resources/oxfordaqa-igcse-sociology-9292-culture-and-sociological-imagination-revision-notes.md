**These notes cover 3.2.2.1 Individuals and society** in OxfordAQA International GCSE Sociology
(9292): "Culture: norms and values; roles; status; stereotypes; subcultures, including ethnic groups
in multicultural societies" and "The sociological imagination (Mills); connecting personal troubles
and public issues." They condense the
[culture and sociological imagination study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-sociology-9292-culture-and-sociological-imagination/)
and sit before the material in the [Socialisation revision notes](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-sociology-socialisation-revision-notes/).

## Key terms

| Term | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Culture | The shared, learned way of life of a group or society | Language, customs, beliefs, food |
| Values | Broad shared beliefs about what is good or important | Respect for elders; education |
| Norms | Specific expected rules of behaviour | Removing shoes indoors; queueing |
| Status | A position in a group or society | Teacher, daughter, chief |
| Ascribed status | Given at birth | Sex, caste, a royal title |
| Achieved status | Earned through effort | Doctor, graduate, champion |
| Role | The behaviour expected of someone with a status | A teacher being fair and prepared |
| Role conflict | Clashing demands of two roles | Working parent with an ill child |
| Stereotype | An oversimplified image applied to a whole group | "Teenagers are irresponsible" |
| Subculture | A group with some distinct norms and values within a wider culture | Youth styles; religious communities |
| Multicultural society | A society in which several ethnic groups live side by side | Communities keeping distinct languages and festivals |
| Hybrid identity | An identity combining elements of more than one culture | Young people mixing heritage and mainstream culture |
| Sociological imagination | Connecting individual lives to wider society and history (Mills) | Seeing unemployment as structural |
| Personal trouble | A problem within one person's life and surroundings | One person losing a job |
| Public issue | A problem affecting many, rooted in social structure | Mass unemployment |

## Culture in four lines

- Culture is **learned** through socialisation, not inherited.
- It **varies** between societies and **changes** over time.
- **Values** are general; **norms** put them into practice in specific situations.
- One value can produce **different norms** in different societies.

## Status and role

- Status = **position**; role = **expected behaviour**.
- Ascribed statuses can limit opportunities (caste, sex); achieved statuses reflect effort, though
  access to them is unequal.
- Everyone holds **several statuses** at once, which is why role conflict happens.

## Stereotypes and subcultures

- Stereotypes are **learned**, often through the media, and shape **treatment**: labelling in
  schools, othering, policing.
- Subcultures **share much** of the mainstream culture; they differ in **some** norms and values.
- Ethnic groups in multicultural societies may keep distinct languages, religions, foods and family
  norms while sharing school and work with others; young people may form hybrid identities.

## Mills and the sociological imagination

- **C. Wright Mills**, *The Sociological Imagination* (1959).
- People often see their problems as private; Mills asked sociologists to link **biography** and
  **history**.
- **Personal troubles** are explained by individual circumstances; **public issues** arise from the
  structure of society.
- Classic example: one unemployed person may be a personal trouble; millions unemployed is a public
  issue that needs a social explanation and collective response.

## Using the idea in other topics

| Topic | Trouble view | Issue view |
|---|---|---|
| Education (3.1.2) | A pupil "didn't try" | Material deprivation, labelling, cost of schooling |
| Families (3.1.1) | A couple "didn't get on" | Divorce laws, women's paid work, changing values |
| Crime (3.2.2.4) | "Bad choices" | Unemployment, policing patterns, stereotyping |
| Inequality (3.2.1) | "Lack of talent" | Class, caste, gender barriers |

## Model sentence

"Mills would argue that rising youth unemployment in [a named society] is a public issue rather than
a collection of personal troubles, because so many young people are affected at once that the cause
must lie in the economy rather than in individual failings."

## Exam traps

- Using "norm" and "value" interchangeably.
- Defining status as wealth or popularity.
- Saying a role is a position (that is status).
- Presenting subcultures as entirely separate from mainstream culture.
- Naming Mills without the troubles/issues distinction.
- Treating every problem as either wholly personal or wholly social: Mills links the two.
- No example from a named society in an extended answer that asks for one.

## Self-test

1. Define culture, and explain why sociologists say it is learned.
2. What is the difference between values and norms?
3. Give an example of one value producing different norms in two societies.
4. What is the difference between ascribed and achieved status?
5. What is a role?
6. Give an example of role conflict.
7. Define a stereotype and explain one way stereotypes are spread.
8. What is a subculture?
9. How might members of a minority ethnic group in a multicultural society maintain a distinct
   culture?
10. Who introduced the sociological imagination, and in which year was his book published?
11. Explain the difference between a personal trouble and a public issue.
12. Apply the sociological imagination to a student leaving school early.

**Answers:** 1. The shared, learned way of life of a group; it is learned because it varies between
societies and changes over time, so it cannot be inherited biologically. 2. Values are broad beliefs
about what is good or important; norms are specific rules of behaviour that put values into practice.
3. For example, respect for elders shown by standing when they enter in one society and by a special
form of address in another. 4. Ascribed status is given at birth; achieved status is earned through
effort. 5. The behaviour expected of someone with a particular status. 6. For example, a working
parent whose child is ill on the day of an important meeting. 7. An oversimplified image applied to
every member of a group; through the media, or through family and peer socialisation. 8. A group
within a wider society sharing some distinct norms and values while sharing much of the mainstream
culture. 9. Through language, religion, food, festivals and family norms passed on at home and in the
community. 10. C. Wright Mills; 1959. 11. A personal trouble affects an individual and is explained by
their circumstances; a public issue affects many and arises from the structure of society.
12. Instead of blaming motivation, ask whether poverty, the need to earn, distance to school or gender
norms affect many students in the same way, which would make early leaving a public issue.
