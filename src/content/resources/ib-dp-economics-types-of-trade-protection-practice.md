## Questions

**1.** Define a tariff. **[2]**

**2.** Sketch and label a diagram to show the effect of a tariff in a market where the country imports the good. **[4]**

**3.** Norland imports tyres. The world price is 40 currency units. The domestic demand and supply data are:

| Price | Quantity demanded | Quantity supplied |
|---|---|---|
| 40 | 100 | 40 |
| 50 | 80 | 60 |

Norland now imposes a tariff of 10 per tyre.

**(a)** State domestic production before and after the tariff. **[2]**
**(b)** State the quantity of imports after the tariff. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why consumption of tyres falls. **[2]**

**4.** (HL only, calculator allowed) Zandria imports bicycles at a world price of 40. Demand is Qd = 200 − 2P and supply is Qs = 3P − 50. The government imposes a tariff of 6 per bicycle.

**(a)** Calculate the quantity of imports before and after the tariff. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate government revenue. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the loss of consumer surplus. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the gain in producer surplus. **[1]**
**(e)** Calculate the total welfare loss. **[2]**

**5.** Explain two ways in which an import quota differs from a tariff that produces the same domestic price. **[4]**

**6.** (HL only, calculator allowed) Kelvar imports cotton at a world price of 24. Demand is Qd = 150 − 3P and supply is Qs = 2P − 20. The government sets an import quota of 20.

**(a)** Calculate the domestic price with the quota. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the quota rent. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the welfare loss to Kelvar, assuming the rent is kept inside the country. **[2]**

**7.** (calculator-free) Explain, using a diagram, the effect of a per-unit subsidy to domestic producers in a country that imports a good at the world price. **[5]**

**8.** (HL only, calculator allowed) Meridia exports tea at a world price of 44. Demand is Qd = 160 − 3P and supply is Qs = 2P − 30. The government pays an export subsidy of 6 per unit.

**(a)** Calculate exports before and after the subsidy. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate what the subsidy costs the government. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the loss of consumer surplus. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the gain in producer surplus. **[1]**
**(e)** Calculate the net change in welfare. **[1]**

**9.** Explain two ways in which a product standard applied only to imports can reduce the quantity of imports. **[4]**

**10.** Discuss whether a government that wants to expand a domestic steel industry should use a tariff or a production subsidy. **[10]**

**11.** The fictional country of Halvern requires all imported toys to be tested at one domestic laboratory. Each tested toy costs importers a fee, and testing takes several weeks. Domestic toy makers test their own products. To what extent is this rule a genuine safety measure and not protection? **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** A tariff is a tax on imported goods [1]. It raises the price of the import in the domestic market [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Aim to name the tool by its mechanism, a tax levied on imports; "a barrier to trade" is too wide to define it.

**2.** Axes are price and quantity, with domestic demand D and domestic supply S labelled [1]. A horizontal world price line Pw sits below the point where D meets S, and a second line Pw + t above it [1]. Four quantities are marked on the horizontal axis: Qs and Qd under free trade, and Qs and Qd with the tariff [1]. The revenue rectangle is shaded between the two new quantities and the price gap, with the two welfare-loss triangles labelled [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A diagram that omits the world price line or the quantities cannot show the effect on imports, however neat it looks.

**3. (a)** Before: 40 [1]. After: 60 [1].
**(b)** At 50, imports = 80 − 60 = **20 [1]**.
**(c)** The tariff raises the domestic price from 40 to 50 [1]. A higher price moves consumers up the demand curve, so quantity demanded falls from 100 to 80 [1].
*Examiner insight:* "State" asks for a bare value, so spend no time on reasons in part (a); the reasoning belongs in part (c), where the command term is "explain".

**4. (a)** Before: Qd = 200 − 80 = 120 and Qs = 120 − 50 = 70, so imports are **50 [1]**. After, at 46: Qd = 108 and Qs = 88, so imports are **20 [1]**.
**(b)** 6 × 20 = **120 [1]**.
**(c)** ½ × (120 + 108) × 6 [1] = **684** [1].
**(d)** ½ × (70 + 88) × 6 = **474 [1]**.
**(e)** ½ × 6 × (88 − 70) = 54 and ½ × 6 × (120 − 108) = 36 [1]. Total = 54 + 36 = **90 [1]**. Check: 474 + 120 + 90 = 684.
*Examiner insight:* Part (e) can be found as consumer loss minus producer gain minus revenue, but showing the two triangles protects the method mark if one earlier figure is wrong.

**5.** First, revenue: a tariff pays area d to the government [1], whereas a quota creates quota rent, collected by licence holders and not necessarily by the government [1]. Second, adjustment: a tariff fixes the price gap and lets the quantity of imports respond to demand [1], while a quota sets the quantity and lets the price respond, so a rise in demand pushes the price further up [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Pair each difference with its consequence for price or quantity; two bare labels give you little to build on.

**6. (a)** Set Qd − Qs = 20: 150 − 3P − (2P − 20) = 20 [1]. So 170 − 5P = 20 and P = **30 [1]**.
**(b)** At Pw = 24 the price gap is 30 − 24 = 6 [1]. Rent = 6 × 20 = **120 [1]**.
**(c)** At 24: Qd = 78 and Qs = 28. At 30: Qd = 60 and Qs = 40. Triangles: ½ × 6 × (40 − 28) = 36 and ½ × 6 × (78 − 60) = 54 [1]. Total = **90 [1]**.
*Examiner insight:* The phrase "kept inside the country" tells you not to count the rent as a loss; read such conditions before you start the calculation.

**7.** The subsidy lowers producers' costs, so S shifts down by the subsidy, equal to a shift to the right [1]. Consumers can still buy at the world price, so the price they pay is unchanged [1]. Producers receive Pw plus the subsidy, so domestic output rises along the old supply curve [1]. Quantity demanded is unchanged, so imports fall by the rise in domestic output [1]. The government pays the subsidy times the new output, which is the cost to taxpayers [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Saying the consumer price is unchanged separates a good answer from one that treats a subsidy like a tariff.

**8. (a)** Before, at 44: Qd = 28 and Qs = 58, so exports are **30 [1]**. After, at 50: Qd = 10 and Qs = 70, so exports are **60 [1]**.
**(b)** 6 × 60 = **360 [1]**.
**(c)** ½ × (28 + 10) × 6 [1] = **114** [1].
**(d)** ½ × (58 + 70) × 6 = **384 [1]**.
**(e)** 384 − 114 − 360 = **−90 [1]**.
*Examiner insight:* The domestic price rises to Pw plus the subsidy; using Pw alone for the consumer loss is the usual error and loses the accuracy marks.

**9.** First, extra cost: a testing or labelling requirement adds a cost per unit [1], which raises the price of imports and so reduces the quantity bought [1]. Second, delay and uncertainty: slow inspections or the risk that goods are rejected make importers bring in less [1], so supply of imports falls [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "It is a barrier" is not an explanation; credit comes from a mechanism that leads to fewer imports.

**10.** Model plan with indicative content. The guide says papers use an analytic markscheme together with markbands, so treat this as indicative content and not a tally.
- Both tools raise domestic output: the tariff by raising the price producers get, the subsidy by paying them more per unit [1].
- Tariff: consumers pay a higher price and buy less, a cost for buyers, including firms that use steel [1].
- Subsidy: the consumer price is unchanged, so buyers are not harmed [1].
- Tariff: raises revenue, which can fund other spending [1].
- Subsidy: costs taxpayers money, and the burden depends on how large the budget deficit is [1].
- Both cause welfare loss from output that costs more than imports, shown by the production triangle [1].
- Diagrams for each tool used to support the points [1].
- Tariff may provoke other countries to respond with their own tariffs, harming exporters [1].
- Subsidy may be hard to end once firms depend on it [1].
- A judgment, such as the subsidy being less harmful to consumers but dearer to the budget, with a criterion given [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Explaining only the tariff leaves the question half answered; the task is to weigh the two tools against each other.

**11.** A model plan.
- The laboratory fee adds a cost per unit that only imports pay, so import prices rise [1].
- Weeks of delay reduce how much can be sold and when [1].
- The fee is a compliance cost and not government revenue, so resources are used up [1].
- Domestic toy makers avoid the fee and delay, so they gain a price and timing advantage [1].
- Consumers face higher prices and less choice [1].
- Testing could have a real purpose: unsafe toys harm children, and testing before sale may help prevent that [1].
- Evidence of protection: the safety test applies only to imports, since local makers test themselves [1].
- Judgment: the rule may have a safety motive, but different treatment suggests it also shelters domestic firms; a rule that tested every toy to the same standard would be more convincing [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* With "to what extent", commit to a view and back it with details from the case, rather than listing effects.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Drawing the diagram without the world price line or without the new quantities.
- Using free-trade imports to calculate tariff revenue.
- Taking trapezoid widths from the wrong curve. Use the old and new quantities on the same curve.
- Treating a quota as if the government always collects the rent.
- Saying a producer subsidy raises the consumer price in an import-competing market.
- Using Pw instead of Pw plus the subsidy when finding the effect of an export subsidy on the home market.
- Writing "calculate" answers as a single number with no working.
- Describing the standard as bad without considering its safety purpose, or good without noticing who it treats differently.
