## Why the closing inventory figure matters

Closing inventory appears twice in the year-end statements:

- In the **statement of profit or loss** it is deducted from opening
inventory plus net purchases to find **cost of sales**.
- In the **statement of financial position** it is a **current asset**.

So any change in its value moves gross profit, profit for the year, capital
(equity) and current assets by the same amount. Next year it becomes the
opening inventory, so an error also carries forward.

## Cost and net realisable value

**Cost** is what the business paid to buy the goods and bring them to their
present location and condition: the purchase price less trade discount, plus
costs such as carriage inwards. It does not include selling costs.

**Net realisable value (NRV)** is the amount the goods are expected to be
sold for, **less** any further costs to complete, repair or sell them.

The rule: **value inventory at the lower of cost and net realisable value.**
This applies the **prudence** concept. If goods will sell for less than
they cost, the loss is recognised now, when it becomes known. If goods will
sell for more than cost, the profit is not recognised until they are sold
(the **realisation** concept), so they stay at cost.

The comparison is made **item by item**, or for each group of identical
items, never on totals. Comparing totals lets the profit expected on some
lines hide the losses on others.

## Worked example 1: item-by-item valuation

Kerry's hardware store counts its inventory at 31 December 2027.

| Line | Units | Cost per unit | NRV information |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paint (tins) | 120 | $9.50 + $0.50 carriage inwards | Sells for $14 |
| Garden hoses | 45 | $22 | Sells for $25; special delivery cost of $4 per hose to sell them |
| Ladders (damaged) | 8 | $65 | Normally $90; after repairs costing $15 each, can be sold for $70 |
| Power drills | 15 | $48 | Replaced by a new model; price cut to $45 |

```
Line           Cost/unit   NRV/unit           Lower   Units   Value $
Paint            10.00      14.00              10.00    120     1,200
Garden hoses     22.00      25 − 4 = 21.00     21.00     45       945
Ladders          65.00      70 − 15 = 55.00    55.00      8       440
Power drills     48.00      45.00              45.00     15       675
                                                              -------
Inventory value                                                 3,260
                                                              =======
```

At cost the inventory would be $1,200 + $990 + $520 + $720 = $3,430. The
correct valuation, $3,260, is $170 lower, so gross profit is $170 lower than
if every line were left at cost.

Three points the example teaches:

- Carriage inwards is part of **cost**.
- Selling and repair costs reduce **NRV**, not cost.
- The paint stays at cost: its expected profit is not recognised early.

## Worked example 2: an inventory count after the year end

A business may not be able to count goods on the last day of the year, so it may
count a few days later and adjust back to the year-end date. The rule is to
ask, for each movement, **"was this item in inventory at the year end?"**

Rafiq's year ends on 31 December 2027. His inventory was counted on 5
January 2028 and valued at cost, $18,640. Between 1 and 5 January 2028:

1. Goods were sold for $2,400. Rafiq adds a mark-up of 50% on cost.
2. Goods costing $1,150 were received from suppliers.
3. A customer returned goods sold for $300.
4. Goods costing $180 were returned to a supplier.

```
                                                             $
Inventory at cost on 5 January 2028                     18,640
Add  Goods sold 1–5 January, at cost ($2,400 × 100/150)  1,600
Less Goods received 1–5 January                         (1,150)
Less Sales returns 1–5 January, at cost ($300 × 100/150)  (200)
Add  Goods returned to supplier 1–5 January                180
                                                        ------
Inventory at cost on 31 December 2027                   19,070
                                                        ======
```

- Goods sold in January **were** in inventory on 31 December: add back, at
**cost**, not selling price.
- Goods received in January **were not** there on 31 December: deduct.
- Goods returned by a customer in January were with the customer on 31
December: deduct, at cost.
- Goods sent back to a supplier in January were still held on 31 December:
add.

Converting selling price to cost: with a mark-up of 50%, selling price is
150% of cost, so cost = selling price × 100/150. With a margin of 25%, cost
is 75% of selling price.

## The effect of an incorrect valuation

Suppose Rafiq's 2027 figures are: revenue $80,000; opening inventory
$6,000; purchases $50,000; correct closing inventory $7,400. A counting
error records closing inventory as $6,500, **undervalued by $900**.

```
                              Correct      As recorded
                                 $              $
Revenue                       80,000         80,000
Opening inventory              6,000          6,000
Purchases                     50,000         50,000
Closing inventory             (7,400)        (6,500)
Cost of sales                 48,600         49,500
Gross profit                  31,400         30,500
```

| Figure | 2027 (closing inventory undervalued) | 2028 (opening inventory undervalued) |
|---|---|---|
| Cost of sales | Overstated by $900 | Understated by $900 |
| Gross profit | Understated by $900 | Overstated by $900 |
| Profit for the year | Understated by $900 | Overstated by $900 |
| Equity at year end | Understated by $900 | Correct, if 2028 closing inventory is right |
| Current assets (inventory) | Understated by $900 | Correct, if 2028 closing inventory is right |

An **overvaluation** has the opposite effects. Over the two years total
profit is unchanged: the error shifts profit from one year to the next.
That is why a deliberate overvaluation is a way of making one year look
better at the expense of the next.

## Common mistakes

- Comparing total cost with total NRV instead of item by item.
- Deducting selling costs from cost, or adding carriage inwards to NRV.
- Adding back goods sold after the year end at **selling price**.
- Confusing mark-up (on cost) with margin (on selling price).
- Saying an inventory error affects "profit" without giving gross profit,
profit for the year, equity and current assets separately.
- Forgetting the reverse effect in the following year.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate**: set out a table with cost, NRV and the lower value for each
line, then total.
- **Explain** the rule using concepts: prudence for losses, realisation for
gains.
- **State the effect** of an error: give the direction and amount for each
figure the question names, and for each year if two are asked.
- In a count-adjustment question, write one line per movement with a plus
or minus and a short label, so method marks are clear.
