## What 2.3.1 is really asking

The heading says "to a business **and** workers". Examiners can ask about either side, and the best answers keep the two apart:

- **To the business**, training is a cost now in return for better performance later: fewer errors, higher productivity, safer working and staff who can cover for each other.
- **To the worker**, training is an investment in their own skills: confidence in the job, a qualification or record of learning, and a better chance of promotion or higher pay.

Paper 1 is set in a small business (up to 49 employees) and Paper 2 in a large business (more than 250 employees). A small business may rely on the owner coaching new staff on the job; a large business is more likely to have a training department and planned off-the-job programmes. Use the size of the business in the question when you judge which type fits.

## The three types in more depth

### Induction

Induction is the first training a new employee receives. Its main purpose is to help the new starter settle in and become useful quickly. A typical programme covers:

- the layout of the site, emergency exits and fire procedures;
- the rules of the business, such as hours, breaks, dress code and how to report absence;
- the people: who the line manager is and who to ask for help;
- an introduction to the products, customers and values of the business.

Why it matters: a new starter who is confused or feels ignored in the first weeks is more likely to leave, and the business then pays to recruit and select all over again. Induction is also usually where basic safety instruction happens, so it links directly to the health and safety point.

### On-the-job training

The employee learns at the workplace while doing the job. Standard methods include:

- **demonstration**, where an experienced colleague shows how a task is done;
- **shadowing**, where the trainee follows a colleague through their day;
- **coaching**, where a supervisor watches the trainee work and gives feedback;
- **mentoring**, where a more senior employee gives longer-term advice.

Main purpose: to teach the specific skills of this job, in this workplace, with little disruption to output. Its weaknesses come from the same feature: the trainer is also trying to do their own work, so their output falls, and any bad habits are passed straight on.

### Off-the-job training

The employee learns away from their normal work, for example at a college, on a course run by a training provider, at a conference or in a training room on site. Main purpose: to teach skills that are hard to learn while working, such as a recognised qualification, a new software system or specialist technical knowledge.

Classification test: ask "is the person learning **while doing their normal job**?" If yes, it is on-the-job. A two-day course in a training room in the same building is still off-the-job, because the trainee has stopped doing their job to learn.

## Matching the type to the purpose

| Situation | Most suitable type | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| A new cashier starts on Monday | Induction, then on-the-job | Needs rules and safety first, then till skills best learnt at the till |
| A delivery firm buys vans with new tracking software | Off-the-job, then on-the-job | Software needs focused teaching; practice then happens on real rounds |
| An accountant needs a professional qualification | Off-the-job | Requires college study and exams |
| A kitchen assistant must learn a new dish | On-the-job | Quick demonstration in the real kitchen |

Many businesses combine types. A judgement that says "both, in this order, because..." is often stronger than choosing one.

## Training and health and safety compliance

The specification asks for "compliance with health and safety laws" but names no particular law, so you need the principle rather than legal detail. Businesses must follow the health and safety laws of the country where they operate, and training is the main way they make sure employees know how to work safely.

Training that supports compliance often covers:

- safe lifting and handling of heavy items;
- use of protective clothing and equipment;
- safe use of machinery, tools and chemicals;
- fire and evacuation procedures;
- how to report accidents and hazards.

Two points that lift an answer:

1. **Refresher training.** One session at induction is not enough when equipment, methods or rules change, so compliance needs repeated training.
2. **Records.** Keeping a record of who was trained, and when, lets a business show that it took its duty seriously if an accident happens.

The consequences of poor compliance are injuries to workers, time off work, possible fines or legal action, and damage to reputation that can affect recruitment and sales.

## Benefits and limitations, developed

| | Benefits | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| Business | Fewer mistakes and less waste; higher productivity; better customer service; a flexible workforce that can cover absence; safer working | Direct cost of fees and materials; lost output while staff train; benefits take time to appear; trained staff may be recruited by competitors |
| Worker | New and transferable skills; greater confidence; qualifications; better chance of promotion and higher pay; safer working | Time away from usual tasks or family; extra pressure to pass assessments; training may not suit how the person learns |

A limitation that students often miss is that the business pays for the training but the worker owns the skills. A business can reduce this risk by offering promotion routes, so trained staff have a reason to stay.

### Worked example 1: comparing two types

Ashdown Opticians must train 3 new assistants in fitting glasses.

- **Off-the-job option**: an external course costing 620 per person, lasting 3 days. Agency cover is needed for each day an assistant is away, at 140 a day.
- **On-the-job option**: a senior optician coaches each assistant for 2 hours a day over 8 working days. Each hour of coaching costs the business an estimated 35 in lost appointments.

Off-the-job cost: fees 3 × 620 = 1,860; cover 3 × 3 = 9 days × 140 = 1,260; total **3,120**.

On-the-job cost: 3 × 2 × 8 = 48 hours × 35 = **1,680**.

On-the-job is 3,120 − 1,680 = **1,440** cheaper. That is not the end of the answer. The external course gives a recognised qualification and expert teaching, which may matter for a skill where mistakes affect customers' eyesight. On-the-job training teaches the shop's own methods but depends on how good the senior optician is as a teacher, and takes her away from customers for 48 hours.

### Worked example 2: the value of induction

Brackley Hotels hires 60 seasonal staff a year. With no structured induction, 15 leave in their first month, which is 15 ÷ 60 × 100 = 25%. Each early leaver costs about 900 to replace.

The hotel introduces an induction programme costing 120 per new starter. Early leavers fall to 6 (10%).

- Cost of induction: 60 × 120 = **7,200**.
- Saving on replacements: (15 − 6) × 900 = **8,100**.
- Net gain: 8,100 − 7,200 = **900**.

The money saving is small, so the stronger argument for induction is what the figures leave out: safer working from the first day, more consistent service to guests and less pressure on the existing staff who were covering for the people who left.

## Exam technique for training questions

The command word taxonomy in the specification tells you what each question wants.

- **Define** (for example induction training): a precise meaning with no example needed.
- **State**: pick the answer out of the case, in no more than a sentence.
- **Outline**: two linked points, placed in the context of the question.
- **Explain**: a statement with two further expansion points, with no context. "Training gives workers new skills (statement), so they make fewer errors (expansion), which raises their confidence and chances of promotion (expansion)."
- **Analyse**: an extended chain of reasoning about the business in the case.
- **Justify**: recommend one of two options, such as on-the-job or off-the-job training, using the information given.
- **Evaluate**: weigh benefits against limitations and reach a supported conclusion.

### Annotated model answer

*Justify whether Ashdown Opticians should use on-the-job or off-the-job training.*

> On-the-job training costs 1,440 less, which matters to a small business, and assistants learn the exact methods used in this shop. However, it takes the senior optician away from 48 hours of appointments, and if she is a poor trainer, errors could harm customers' trust. The off-the-job course costs more but gives a recognised qualification and expert teaching. I recommend the off-the-job course, because fitting glasses affects customers' health and reputation is vital to a local optician, so the extra 1,440 is a small price for reliable skills.

Why it works: it uses the figures, names a drawback of each option, chooses one, and gives a deciding reason tied to the case.

## Common mistakes

- Calling any training that happens on the premises "on-the-job". Ask whether the person is doing their normal job while learning.
- Describing induction as general training for all staff. It is for new starters.
- Writing "training motivates workers" with no chain. Say how: new skills, recognition, progression.
- Treating health and safety training as optional. It is about meeting a legal duty.
- Counting only the fee as the cost of training and forgetting lost output or cover.
