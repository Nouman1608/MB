## Culture and socialisation

- **Culture** — the whole way of life of a group: norms, values, roles, beliefs, language.
- **Norms** — expected behaviours in a given situation. **Values** — beliefs about what is important.
- **Roles** — behaviours attached to a position. **Status** — the position itself.
- **Ascribed status** is fixed at birth; **achieved status** is earned.

**Primary socialisation** occurs in the family in early childhood; **secondary socialisation** through school, peers, media, religion and workplace.

**Agencies and their methods:** family (imitation, sanctions), education (formal curriculum and the **hidden curriculum**), peer group (peer pressure), media (role models and representation), religion, workplace.

**Sanctions** are positive or negative, formal or informal. Informal sanctions — a look, exclusion from a group — do most of the work of everyday social control.

**Nature versus nurture:** the sociological evidence is **cross-cultural variation** in norms and gender roles, and **feral children** cases where individuals raised without human contact fail to develop language or normal social behaviour. Both show that behaviour assumed to be natural is in fact learnt.

**Subculture** — a group with distinct norms within a wider society. **Counter-culture** — one actively opposed to the mainstream.

## Identity

Shaped by **gender, ethnicity, social class, age, nationality and religion** — each transmitted through the agencies of socialisation.

- **Gender identity** is socialised through toys, clothing, language, expectations and media representation. The **sex/gender distinction** matters: sex is biological, gender is socially constructed, which is why gender roles vary between cultures and over time.
- **Ethnic identity** involves shared culture, language, religion and history. **Hybrid identity** results from combining elements of two or more cultures — increasingly common with globalisation.

## Research methods

**Primary** data is collected first-hand; **secondary** already exists. **Quantitative** is numerical; **qualitative** is descriptive.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Reliability** | Repeating the study gives the same result |
| **Validity** | It gives a true picture |
| **Representativeness** | The sample reflects the wider population |

**Quantitative methods are strong on reliability, weak on validity; qualitative methods are the reverse.** That trade-off answers most methods questions on its own.

| Method | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|
| **Questionnaire** | Cheap, large samples, reliable | Low response rate, low validity |
| **Structured interview** | Reliable, comparable | Interviewer bias, little depth |
| **Unstructured interview** | Valid, detailed | Slow, unreliable, small samples |
| **Participant observation** | Very valid, reveals real behaviour | Hawthorne effect, going native, ethical issues |
| **Official statistics** | Cheap, large-scale | Socially constructed by the state |

**Sampling:** random, systematic, stratified, quota, snowball, opportunity. A **pilot study** trials the method first so problems can be corrected.

**Ethics:** informed consent, confidentiality, protection from harm, no deception, right to withdraw.

## Social inequality

**Class:** life chances — the opportunities for health, education, housing and income — are predicted by class more strongly than by any other single factor.

- **Social mobility** — **intergenerational** compares a person with their parents; **intragenerational** compares stages of their own career.
- **Open** societies allow movement by merit; **closed** societies fix status at birth.
- **Absolute poverty** is lacking the necessities for survival; **relative poverty** is falling below the standard considered acceptable in that society — which is why relative poverty exists in wealthy countries.
- **Cycle of deprivation:** poor housing and diet → poor health and education → low-paid work → poverty reproduced in the next generation.

**Gender:** the **glass ceiling**, horizontal and vertical segregation, the pay gap. Causes include socialisation, caring responsibilities, discrimination and career interruption. Changes include equal-pay legislation and girls' educational success.

**Ethnicity:** discrimination in employment and housing, institutional racism, and media representation. **Ethnic and class disadvantage overlap heavily**, and much of the apparent ethnic effect narrows once class is controlled for — a qualification that shows judgement.

## Exam traps

- Confusing reliability with validity.
- Confusing absolute with relative poverty.
- Confusing ascribed with achieved status.
- Confusing sex with gender.
- Evaluating a method in the abstract rather than for the specific topic.
- Giving one side only when evaluation needs both plus a judgement.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish norms from values.
2. What two kinds of evidence support the nurture side of the debate?
3. Distinguish sex from gender, and say why the distinction matters.
4. Distinguish reliability from validity, and say which methods favour each.
5. Explain the cycle of deprivation.

**Answers:** 1. Norms are expected behaviours in particular situations; values are beliefs about what is important and worth pursuing. 2. Cross-cultural variation in norms and gender roles, and feral children cases where individuals raised without human contact fail to develop language or normal social behaviour. 3. Sex is biological and gender is socially constructed; the distinction explains why gender roles differ between cultures and change over time, which biology alone could not account for. 4. Reliability is consistency on repetition, favoured by quantitative methods; validity is giving a true picture, favoured by qualitative methods. 5. Poor housing and diet produce poor health and educational underachievement, leading to low-paid insecure work, which reproduces the same conditions in the next generation.
