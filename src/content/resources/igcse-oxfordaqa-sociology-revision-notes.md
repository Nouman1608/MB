Paper 1 has three sections: **Families** (3.1.1, 22 marks), **Education**
(3.1.2, 22 marks) and **Research methods** (3.1.3, 16 marks — shared
with Paper 2).

## Families (3.1.1)

**Family types:** nuclear, extended, single-parent, reconstituted (step-),
"living apart together" couples, child-free couples, "beanpole" families
(many generations, few members each). **Therborn** identified different
family systems across world regions, showing family form is not
universal. Marriage types include arranged and love marriages,
monogamy and (in some societies) polygamy.

**Perspectives (named sociologists: Engels, Oakley, Parsons, Smart,
Stacey, Therborn):**

| Perspective | Core claim |
|---|---|
| **Functionalism** (Parsons) | Family performs positive functions — primary socialisation, "stabilisation of adult personalities" |
| **Marxism** (Engels) | Family serves capitalism — reproduces labour power, transmits property, a form of social control |
| **Feminism** (Oakley) | Family positions women unequally, especially around domestic labour and childcare |

**Social change:** industrialisation and urbanisation reshaped family
form; demographic change (rising life expectancy, falling birth rates)
has produced more "beanpole" families and a "pivot generation" caring
for children and ageing parents simultaneously. **Smart's** work on
individualisation and **Stacey's** work on diverse, "negotiated" family
forms both describe growing family diversity.

**Gender, age and family life:** unequal division of **domestic labour**
persists even in dual-earner households; children's status has shifted
from economic contributors to long-term financial dependants; **"boomerang"
adult children** return home for economic reasons; older people's roles
range from providing childcare to needing care themselves.

## Education (3.1.2)

**Official (formal) curriculum** — subjects formally taught (maths,
science, history). **Hidden curriculum** — attitudes and behaviours
taught informally through school organisation and routine (punctuality,
respect for authority, competitiveness).

**Perspectives (named sociologists: Becker, Bourdieu, Davis and Moore,
Freire, Illich, Samson):**

| Perspective | Core claim |
|---|---|
| **Functionalism** (Davis and Moore) | Education allocates roles by ability and socialises shared values — "role allocation" |
| **Marxism** (Bourdieu) | Education reproduces class inequality; better-off pupils have more **cultural capital**, which schools reward |
| **Radical/critical** (Freire, Illich) | Traditional schooling can suppress critical thought rather than develop it |

**Relationships and processes in school: labelling and the
self-fulfilling prophecy** — a teacher attaches a label (e.g. "bright,"
"disruptive"), often on limited evidence (**Becker**'s work on
labelling); this shapes teacher expectations and how the pupil is
treated; the pupil internalises and acts out the label, making the
original expectation come true. Pupil subcultures can form in response,
some pro-school and some anti-school. Racism and sexism in the classroom
can operate through the same labelling process.

**Educational attainment:** differences by **social class** (cultural
capital, resources for study), **gender** (subject-choice patterns,
gendered expectations) and **ethnicity** (differing teacher expectations,
stereotyping); **credentialism** — qualifications used as a filter even
where the content is not directly job-relevant, which raises measurement
issues when comparing attainment across groups.

## Research methods (3.1.3 — shared with Paper 2)

**Primary** data is collected first-hand; **secondary** already exists.
**Quantitative** is numerical; **qualitative** is descriptive.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Reliability** | Repeating the study gives the same result |
| **Validity** | It gives a true picture |
| **Representativeness** | The sample reflects the wider population |

**Quantitative methods are strong on reliability, weak on validity;
qualitative methods are the reverse.** That trade-off answers most
methods questions on its own.

| Method | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|
| **Questionnaire** | Cheap, large samples, reliable | Low response rate, low validity |
| **Structured interview** | Reliable, comparable | Interviewer bias, little depth |
| **Unstructured interview** | Valid, detailed | Slow, unreliable, small samples |
| **Participant observation** | Very valid, reveals real behaviour | Hawthorne effect, going native, ethical issues |
| **Official statistics** | Cheap, large-scale | Socially constructed by the state |

**Sampling:** random, systematic, stratified, quota, snowball,
opportunity. A **pilot study** trials the method first so problems can
be corrected.

**Ethics:** informed consent, confidentiality, protection from harm, no
deception, right to withdraw. Research involving children additionally
requires parental or guardian consent alongside the child's own
agreement.

## Exam traps

- Naming a family type, perspective or factor in achievement without
  explaining it.
- Describing a perspective on families or education without a clearly
  stated point of difference from another perspective.
- Confusing the official/formal curriculum with the hidden curriculum.
- Confusing reliability with validity.
- Evaluating a method in the abstract rather than for the specific
  topic being researched.
- Giving one side only when evaluation needs both plus a judgement.

## Self-test

1. Name **two** family types other than the nuclear family.
2. State the functionalist and Marxist views of the family's role in
   society, and one difference between them.
3. Distinguish the official curriculum from the hidden curriculum.
4. What does labelling in school lead to, and by what process?
5. Distinguish reliability from validity, and say which methods favour
   each.

**Answers:** 1. Any two of: extended, single-parent, reconstituted,
"living apart together," child-free, "beanpole" families. 2.
Functionalism: the family performs positive functions such as primary
socialisation and stabilising adult personalities. Marxism: the family
serves capitalism's interests, reproducing labour power and transmitting
property. The key difference is that functionalism sees the family as
broadly beneficial for society as a whole, while Marxism sees it as
serving one class's interests. 3. The official curriculum is what
schools formally set out to teach (subjects and content); the hidden
curriculum is the attitudes and behaviours taught informally through
the way the school is organised and run. 4. Labelling can lead to a
self-fulfilling prophecy, in which a teacher's expectation of a pupil
(based on a label) shapes how the pupil is treated, which the pupil then
internalises and acts out, making the original expectation come true. 5.
Reliability is consistency on repetition, favoured by quantitative
methods; validity is giving a true picture, favoured by qualitative
methods.
