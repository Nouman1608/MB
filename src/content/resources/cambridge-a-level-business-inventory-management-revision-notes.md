## 4.2.1 Managing inventory

### Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Inventory | Materials and goods the business is holding at a point in time |
| Raw materials | Inputs bought in, not yet used |
| Work in progress | Part-finished goods still in production |
| Finished products | Completed goods awaiting sale or delivery |
| Lead time | Gap between placing an order and receiving it |
| Buffer inventory | Minimum safety level the business plans not to go below |
| Re-order level | Inventory level that triggers a new order |
| Stock-out | Running out of inventory, so production or sales stop |
| Supply chain | All the stages and firms from raw material to final customer |
| Supply Chain Management (SCM) | Coordinating the flow of materials, information and payments along that chain |

### Why inventory is held

- Raw materials: keep production going, allow bulk buying.
- Work in progress: unavoidable when production takes several stages.
- Finished products: meet orders at once, cope with seasonal or sudden demand.

### Costs and benefits of holding inventory

| Benefits | Costs |
|---|---|
| Customers served at once | Cash tied up (opportunity cost, pressure on cash flow) |
| Production continues if supply fails | Storage, insurance, security, refrigeration |
| Bulk-buying discounts possible | Damage, theft, going out of date, obsolescence |
| Demand surges can be met | Handling and record-keeping time |

Link to cash: every unit in the store is money that has been paid out but not yet earned back.

### Formulas (standard forms; the syllabus names the terms but gives no formula)

| Quantity | Formula |
|---|---|
| Re-order level | (usage per day x lead time in days) + buffer inventory |
| Maximum inventory | buffer inventory + order quantity (when delivery arrives as inventory reaches the buffer) |
| Days from maximum to re-order level | (maximum - re-order level) / usage per day |
| Length of one cycle | days to re-order level + lead time |

### Method in steps: re-order level questions

1. Find usage per day (convert from week or month if needed).
2. Multiply by the lead time to get usage while waiting for delivery.
3. Add the buffer inventory.
4. State the unit and show the working.

### Worked reminder

A bakery supplier, Pentlow Mill, delivers flour to a cafe that uses 25 kg a day. The lead time is 4 days, buffer inventory is 60 kg and each order is 300 kg.

- Re-order level = (25 x 4) + 60 = **160 kg**.
- Maximum inventory = 60 + 300 = **360 kg**.
- Days from 360 to 160 = 200 / 25 = **8 days**; with the 4-day lead time, one cycle lasts **12 days**.

### Reading an inventory control chart

| Feature on the chart | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Downward slope | Rate of usage (steeper = faster) |
| Vertical rise | Delivery; the height is the order quantity |
| Time from order to rise | Lead time |
| Horizontal line, low | Buffer inventory |
| Horizontal line, middle | Re-order level |
| Horizontal line, top | Maximum inventory |
| Line dips below buffer | Buffer is being used; delivery late or demand higher |
| Line reaches zero | Stock-out |

If a chart shows a rise that arrives while the line is still well above the buffer, the supplier delivered early or the order was placed too soon.

### What changes the re-order level

| Change | Effect on re-order level |
|---|---|
| Higher daily usage | Higher |
| Longer lead time | Higher |
| Bigger buffer | Higher |
| Lower usage, shorter lead time | Lower (but the buffer is still added) |

Order quantity does not alter the re-order level. It changes the maximum level and how often orders are placed.

### Why SCM matters

- Reliable, well-informed suppliers allow less inventory to be held safely.
- Shorter and more predictable lead times reduce the buffer needed.
- Close relationships with suppliers help with quality, costs and speed of delivery.
- Faster, more dependable delivery to customers can be a competitive advantage.
- Weaknesses anywhere in the chain pass to the business, so managing them reduces risk.
- Limits: it takes time and money, and it relies on suppliers cooperating.

## 4.2.2 Just in Time (JIT)

### JIT and JIC compared

| | JIT | JIC |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Hold minimum inventory, cut costs and waste | Avoid ever running out |
| Buffer | Very small or none | Large |
| Orders | Small and frequent | Larger and less frequent |
| Depends on | Reliable suppliers, accurate forecasts | Space and cash to hold inventory |
| Weakness | Stoppage if a delivery fails | High holding costs, obsolescence |

### Impact of adopting JIT

| Positive | Negative |
|---|---|
| Lower storage and holding costs | Production stops if a supplier is late |
| Less cash tied up | Bulk discounts may be lost |
| Less waste and damage | More orders, so higher ordering costs |
| Quality faults show up quickly | Hard to meet sudden surges in demand |
| Better response to changing wants | Staff and suppliers need training and commitment |

### When JIT is more likely to suit a business

- Demand is steady and forecasts are reliable.
- Suppliers are dependable and close.
- Materials are bulky, perishable or expensive to store.
- Staff can work flexibly.

JIC is more likely to suit a business with uncertain supply, long delivery times or sharp seasonal peaks.

### Method in steps: a JIT or JIC judgement

1. State what the business is like: stable or uncertain demand, reliable or unreliable suppliers, space and cash available.
2. Give the case for the approach, with a cost or benefit taken from the data.
3. Give the case against, again from the data, such as a lost discount or the risk of a stoppage.
4. Reach a decision and say what it depends on, for example how dependable the main supplier proves to be.

### Worked reminder: reading a late delivery

Suppose the cafe above has a chart reading 360 kg at day 0 and 160 kg at day 8, when the order is placed. Usage is 25 kg a day. On time, the delivery arrives on day 12 with 60 kg left. If it arrives 2 days late, the cafe has 60 - (25 x 2) = **10 kg** left on day 14. The buffer has almost gone but no stock-out has happened. A delay of 3 days would cause one: 60 / 25 = 2.4 days of cover remain after day 12.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Buffer inventory** is a minimum level; **re-order level** is a trigger level. The buffer is a part of the re-order level.
- **Lead time** is time; **order quantity** is amount.
- **Raw materials** go in; **work in progress** is half-way; **finished products** go out.
- **JIT** is a method of minimising inventory; **JIC** is a method of protecting against shortage.
- **Holding cost** is the cost of keeping items; **ordering cost** is the cost of placing and receiving orders. JIT lowers the first and raises the second.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the three types of inventory.
2. Define lead time.
3. A firm uses 18 units a day, the lead time is 6 days and the buffer inventory is 50 units. Calculate the re-order level.
4. A re-order level is 500 units, the lead time is 6 days and the buffer inventory is 140 units. Calculate daily usage.
5. A firm has a buffer of 90 units and orders 450 units at a time. Calculate the maximum inventory level, assuming delivery arrives as inventory reaches the buffer.
6. State two costs of holding inventory.
7. On a chart, what does a steeper falling line show?
8. State one reason a business might hold finished products.
9. Give one reason why JIT could fail for a business.
10. Explain one way good supply chain management could reduce the buffer inventory a business holds.

### Answers

1. Raw materials, work in progress, finished products.
2. The time between placing an order and receiving it.
3. (18 x 6) + 50 = 108 + 50 = **158 units**.
4. Usage in the lead time = 500 - 140 = 360, so daily usage = 360 / 6 = **60 units**.
5. 90 + 450 = **540 units**.
6. Any two from: storage space, insurance and security, cash tied up, damage, theft, obsolescence, handling costs.
7. A faster rate of usage.
8. To meet orders at once, so sales are not lost, or to cope with seasonal demand.
9. A supplier delivers late and, with no buffer, production stops.
10. Reliable suppliers with short, predictable lead times mean less cover is needed against late deliveries, so a smaller buffer is enough.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Omitting the buffer inventory from the re-order level.
- Using the wrong time unit, such as weekly usage with a lead time in days.
- Writing "no inventory" for JIT instead of minimum inventory.
- Treating every cost of holding inventory as a storage cost, and forgetting cash tied up.
- Giving only advantages or only disadvantages of JIT in an evaluative answer.
- Writing general points about JIT with no link to the business in the case.
- Misreading a chart: calling the vertical rise usage, or ignoring the lead time gap.
- Defining supply chain management as just buying from suppliers, rather than coordinating the whole flow.
