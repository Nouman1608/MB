This guide covers section **1.1 The purpose of accounting** of the Cambridge O Level Accounting 7707 syllabus for examination in 2027, 2028 and 2029. The syllabus asks you to understand "the difference between book-keeping and accounting", "the purposes of measuring business profit and loss" and "the role of accounting in providing information for monitoring progress and decision-making". The examples here follow one invented business, Juniper Print Studio, owned by Kamal Ansari. Amounts are in dollars.

## 1. Book-keeping and accounting

**Book-keeping** is the detailed, day-to-day recording of a business's financial transactions. It includes entering source documents in the books of prime entry, posting entries to the ledger accounts and balancing those accounts.

**Accounting** uses the records that book-keeping produces. It includes preparing the financial statements, analysing and interpreting the results, and communicating them to the people who need them, so that they can make decisions.

Book-keeping is the foundation and accounting is what is built on it. Without accurate book-keeping, the statements would be wrong; without accounting, the records would be a pile of figures that nobody could use.

### Sorting tasks

| Task at Juniper Print Studio | Book-keeping or accounting? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Entering a sales invoice for printed leaflets in the sales journal | Book-keeping | Routine recording of a transaction |
| Posting the cash book entries to the ledger | Book-keeping | Recording in the ledger |
| Balancing the trade receivables accounts at the month end | Book-keeping | Part of keeping the ledger |
| Preparing the statement of profit or loss for the year | Accounting | Summarising the records into a statement |
| Comparing this year's profit with last year's | Accounting | Analysing results |
| Advising Kamal whether he can afford a new printing machine | Accounting | Using information for a decision |

A useful test: if the task **records** what happened, it is book-keeping; if it **summarises, explains or uses** what happened, it is accounting.

A small business may have one person who does both. In a larger business, book-keepers record transactions while accountants prepare statements and give advice. The distinction is about the activity, not the job title.

## 2. The purposes of measuring profit and loss

Profit for a period is the amount by which revenue exceeds expenses; a loss is the amount by which expenses exceed revenue. Measuring it serves several purposes.

1. **To judge how well the business has performed.** Profit is the main measure of whether the business is achieving its aim of making money for the owner.
2. **To decide how much the owner can take out.** Drawings above profit reduce the owner's capital in the business. In a limited company, profit limits the dividends that can be paid.
3. **To compare with earlier periods and other businesses**, showing whether results are improving.
4. **To work out the tax** that may be due on the profit.
5. **To support applications for loans.** Lenders want evidence that the business earns enough to pay interest and repay.
6. **To share profit fairly** between partners, according to their agreement.
7. **To plan ahead.** Knowing how much profit was made helps the owner decide whether to expand, carry on as now or cut back.

Measuring a **loss** is just as important. It warns the owner that action is needed, such as raising prices, cutting expenses or, if losses continue, considering closure.

### Worked example: profit and drawings

Juniper Print Studio's results for the year:

| | $ |
|---|---|
| Revenue | 84,000 |
| Cost of sales (paper, ink) | 30,000 |
| Expenses (rent, wages, electricity, depreciation) | 38,500 |
| Profit for the year | 84,000 − 30,000 − 38,500 = **15,500** |

During the year Kamal took drawings of 18,000. His capital therefore changes by 15,500 − 18,000 = **−2,500**. Measuring profit shows Kamal that he took 2,500 more than the business earned. If he continues, the business's capital will keep shrinking, and with it the resources the business needs. The decision this information supports: reduce drawings to no more than profit, or find ways to raise profit.

## 3. Accounting information for monitoring progress

**Monitoring progress** means checking regularly how the business is doing, so problems are spotted early. Accounting information makes this possible by allowing comparisons:

- **with earlier periods**: is revenue or profit rising or falling?
- **with plans or targets** the owner has set
- **with similar businesses**, where their figures are available

### Worked example: three years at Juniper

| | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 70,000 | 78,000 | 84,000 |
| Profit for the year | 14,000 | 14,820 | 15,500 |
| Profit as % of revenue | 20.00% | 19.00% | 18.45% |

Revenue and profit have both risen every year, which looks like progress. But profit as a percentage of revenue has fallen from 20.00% to 18.45%. For each $100 of sales, Kamal keeps less profit than before, so his costs are rising faster than his sales. Without the accounting records he would see a busy shop and assume all was well; monitoring tells him to investigate his costs. (Percentages such as this are developed further in Topic 6.)

## 4. Accounting information for decision-making

Decisions are about the future, but good decisions rely on accurate information about the past and present. Examples at Juniper:

| Decision | Accounting information used |
|---|---|
| Should prices be raised? | Cost of paper and ink compared with revenue; profit trend |
| Can the business afford a new machine for 12,000? | Bank balance, profit, existing loans |
| Should a customer be allowed credit? | How quickly that customer has paid in the past (trade receivables records) |
| Should a part-time assistant be hired? | Wages compared with the extra revenue expected |
| Should the business borrow? | Profit available to pay interest; assets that could be offered as security |

Accounting information is also used by people outside the business, such as banks, suppliers and tax authorities. Topic 6 covers these interested parties in detail.

For information to help decisions it must be **accurate**, **up to date** and **understandable**. A statement prepared months late, or full of errors, can lead to a poor decision.

## Common mistakes

- Defining accounting as "recording transactions". That is book-keeping.
- Writing that book-keeping and accounting are done by different people. The difference is the activity.
- Giving only one purpose of measuring profit when two or more are asked for.
- Saying profit equals the cash in the bank. Profit is revenue less expenses; cash can differ for many reasons.
- Describing monitoring without saying what the figures are compared with.

## Exam technique

- **Define**: give a precise meaning, for example "book-keeping is the detailed recording of financial transactions".
- **State** or **Give**: short points, one per mark.
- **Explain**: the point plus why, for example "profit is measured to decide drawings, because drawings above profit reduce capital".
- **Explain the difference between** two terms: define both and show the contrast in one linked sentence.
- **Advise**: use the figures in the question and give a clear recommendation.
