These questions cover section 1.3, *Opportunity cost*, of the Cambridge O Level Economics 2281 syllabus for examination in 2026: its definition, examples in different contexts, and its influence on the decisions of consumers, workers, producers and governments. All people, firms and figures are invented. Revise first with the [study guide on opportunity cost](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-opportunity-cost/) and the [revision notes on opportunity cost](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-opportunity-cost-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** What is meant by opportunity cost? **[1]**

A: The money price of a good

B: The total of all alternatives given up

C: The next best alternative forgone

D: The cost of producing one more unit

**2.** Hana has 20 cu. She would most like a book, then a cinema ticket, then a T-shirt; each costs 20 cu. She buys the book. What is her opportunity cost? **[1]**

A: The book

B: The cinema ticket

C: The cinema ticket and the T-shirt

D: 20 cu

**3.** A farm can produce either 900 kg of tomatoes or 300 kg of peppers on the same land. What is the opportunity cost of 1 kg of peppers? **[1]**

A: 1/3 kg of tomatoes

B: 3 kg of tomatoes

C: 300 kg of tomatoes

D: 600 kg of tomatoes

**4.** Explain why the use of a free good has no opportunity cost. **[2]**

**5.** Give **one** example of opportunity cost for a worker and **one** for a producer. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why opportunity cost is not always the same as the money price of a good. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**7.** A clothing factory can use a production line to make either 1,200 shirts or 400 coats a week.

**(a)** Calculate the opportunity cost of one coat. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the opportunity cost of one shirt. **[1]**

**(c)** The factory makes coats. The profit is 8 cu per coat and 2 cu per shirt. Explain, using figures, whether the factory should switch to shirts. **[3]**

**8.** Tomas is 16. He can stay at school for two more years or take a job paying 700 cu a month.

**(a)** State the opportunity cost of staying at school. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain how a rise in the wage offered to 900 cu a month might affect Tomas's decision. **[3]**

**9.** A government has 6 billion cu to spend and must choose between projects it ranks in this order: a new hospital (1st), a motorway (2nd), a sports stadium (3rd). Each costs 6 billion cu.

**(a)** Identify the opportunity cost of building the hospital. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why the opportunity cost is not the motorway and the stadium together. **[2]**

**10.** Explain how opportunity cost influences the decisions of consumers when the price of a good they usually buy rises. **[3]**

**11.** Aiko is offered two jobs. Job X pays 1,400 cu a month for 45 hours a week. Job Y pays 1,250 cu a month for 36 hours a week. She takes job X.

**(a)** State the opportunity cost of her choice. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain **one** reason why another worker might choose job Y. **[2]**

**12.** A government decides to borrow to build a new airport instead of raising taxes. Discuss whether borrowing means the airport has no opportunity cost. **[5]**

## Answers

**1.** C [1]. **[1]**

**2.** B [1]. **[1]**

**3.** B: 900 ÷ 300 = 3 kg of tomatoes [1]. **[1]**

*Examiner insight:* option A is the opportunity cost of 1 kg of tomatoes. Write the division with units to avoid inverting it.

**4.** A free good is not scarce and uses no scarce resources [1], so using it does not mean giving up any alternative [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Worker: working overtime means giving up leisure time, or taking one job means giving up the next best job [1]. Producer: using a machine to make one product means giving up the output of another product [1]. **[2]**

**6.** The price is what is paid in money, while opportunity cost is the next best alternative given up [1]; for example, a free concert ticket has no price but the evening could have been spent working for wages, so the time still has an opportunity cost [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** 1,200 ÷ 400 = **3 shirts** [1].

**(b)** 400 ÷ 1,200 = **1/3 of a coat** [1].

**(c)** Coats: 400 × 8 = 3,200 cu profit a week; shirts: 1,200 × 2 = 2,400 cu [1]. The opportunity cost of making coats is the 2,400 cu profit from shirts, which is less than the 3,200 cu from coats [1], so the factory should not switch; it would give up 800 cu of profit a week [1]. **[5]**

**8. (a)** The wages (700 cu a month) and work experience he would have gained from the job [1].

**(b)** The opportunity cost of staying at school rises from 700 cu to 900 cu a month [1], so staying at school becomes a more costly choice [1]. Tomas may leave school to take the job, unless he expects the qualifications to bring higher wages later that are worth more than the earnings he gives up [1]. **[4]**

**9. (a)** The motorway [1].

**(b)** The government could only have built one project with the 6 billion cu [1], so only the next best alternative, the motorway, is given up; the stadium would not have been built either way [1]. **[3]**

**10.** When the price rises, buying the same amount means giving up more of other goods [1], so the opportunity cost of each unit rises [1]; consumers with a limited income may buy less of the good and more of others [1]. **[3]**

**11. (a)** Job Y: 1,250 cu a month and 9 extra hours of free time a week [1].

**(b)** A worker who values leisure, family time or study highly [1] may decide that 9 extra hours a week are worth more than the extra 150 cu a month [1]. **[3]**

**12.** Borrowing means taxes do not need to rise now, so households keep their income for other goods today [1]. But the resources used to build the airport, such as workers, land and materials, could have been used for other projects, so an opportunity cost still exists now [1]. The loan must also be repaid with interest [1], so future governments will have less to spend on services such as health or education [1]. Conclusion: borrowing changes who bears the opportunity cost and when, but does not remove it [1]. **[5]**

*Examiner insight:* the strongest answers separate the opportunity cost of the resources (always present) from the question of who pays and when.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming more than one alternative as the opportunity cost.
- Inverting ratios in calculations, or leaving out the units of the good given up.
- Giving a money figure without saying what it represents.
- Assuming anything provided at no charge has no opportunity cost.
- Writing a "discuss" answer with no conclusion.
