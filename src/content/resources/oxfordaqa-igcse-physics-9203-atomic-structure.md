This guide covers one subtopic in depth: **3.7.1 Atomic structure**, statements a to f of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Physics (9203) specification, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. The qualification is untiered. Everything in the rest of Topic 7, from radioactive decay to fission, relies on being able to read an atomic symbol quickly and accurately, so this page gives extended practice in counting particles as well as a fuller account of the alpha scattering evidence.

The [atomic structure, isotopes and types of ionising radiation guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-atomic-structure-isotopes-and-types-of-ionising/) covers this alongside radiation. Afterwards, use the [atomic structure revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-atomic-structure-revision-notes/) and the [atomic structure practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-atomic-structure-practice/).

## What the specification asks for

| Statement | Content |
|---|---|
| a | Atoms have a radius of about 10⁻¹⁰ m; a small, central, positive nucleus of protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons; the nucleus is much smaller than the atom and holds almost all the mass |
| b | The scattering of alpha particles by thin metal foil is evidence for the distribution of mass in the atom |
| c | Relative masses and charges of the proton (1, +1), neutron (1, 0) and electron (very small, −1) |
| d | In an atom, the number of electrons equals the number of protons, so there is no overall charge |
| e | Electrons are arranged at various distances from the nucleus; atoms can lose or gain outer electrons to form ions |
| f | Atoms of an element have the same number of protons but isotopes have different numbers of neutrons; atomic (proton) number and mass number; nuclide notation |

## How small, and how empty? (statement a)

An atom has a radius of about 10⁻¹⁰ m. A nucleus is far smaller still, with a radius of around 10⁻¹⁵ m, so the atom is roughly 100 000 times wider than its nucleus. If a nucleus were the size of a marble of radius 5 mm, the atom would have a radius of 5 mm × 100 000 = 500 m. Almost all the mass is packed into that tiny nucleus, while the electrons occupy the space around it. So the **atom** is mostly empty space, but the **nucleus** is extremely dense. Keep the two words apart in your answers.

## The alpha scattering evidence (statement b)

In the early twentieth century, Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden, working with Ernest Rutherford, fired alpha particles at a very thin sheet of gold foil and recorded where they went. Before this, the accepted picture (J. J. Thomson's "plum pudding" model) spread positive charge and mass evenly through the atom, with electrons dotted through it. That model predicted the alpha particles would all pass through with only tiny deflections. The results told a different story, and Rutherford explained them in 1911 with the nuclear model.

| Observation | What it shows |
|---|---|
| Most alpha particles passed straight through | Most of the atom is empty space |
| A small number were deflected through small angles | They passed near a concentrated positive charge and were repelled |
| A very few bounced back through large angles | The positive charge and almost all the mass are concentrated in a tiny nucleus |

**Why the bounce-back shows where the mass is.** An alpha particle is fast and fairly massive. For one to be turned right round, it must strike something much more massive than itself. If the mass were spread through the atom, nothing in its path would be heavy enough. That is why the specification describes this experiment as evidence for the **distribution of mass**, not only of charge.

**Why the foil must be thin.** In thin foil, each alpha particle meets only a few layers of atoms, so a large deflection can be traced to a single close encounter with one nucleus.

## Particles in the atom (statements c and d)

| Particle | Where | Relative mass | Relative charge |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proton | Nucleus | 1 | +1 |
| Neutron | Nucleus | 1 | 0 |
| Electron | Around the nucleus | Very small | −1 |

An atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons, so the charges cancel and the atom is neutral. The nucleus as a whole is positive, because it contains protons and neutral neutrons only.

## Electrons and ions (statement e)

Electrons are arranged at different distances from the nucleus. The outer ones are the easiest to remove. An atom that **loses** outer electrons has more protons than electrons and becomes a **positive ion**; an atom that **gains** electrons becomes a **negative ion**. The nucleus is untouched, so the element does not change.

> charge of ion = number of protons − number of electrons

## Isotopes and notation (statement f)

An element is defined by its number of protons, the **atomic number** (proton number), Z. The total number of protons and neutrons is the **mass number**, A. They are written with the mass number above and the atomic number below the symbol, for example ³⁵₁₇Cl.

> neutrons = A − Z

**Isotopes** are atoms of the same element (same Z) with different numbers of neutrons (different A). They have the same number of electrons when neutral, so they behave the same chemically, but their nuclei can differ in stability.

## Worked example 1: two isotopes of chlorine

```
³⁵₁₇Cl:  protons 17, neutrons 35 − 17 = 18, electrons 17
³⁷₁₇Cl:  protons 17, neutrons 37 − 17 = 20, electrons 17
```

Both have 17 protons, so both are chlorine. They differ by two neutrons, so they are isotopes.

## Worked example 2: ions

```
Magnesium ²⁴₁₂Mg loses 2 electrons:
  protons 12, electrons 12 − 2 = 10, charge = 12 − 10 = +2
Oxygen ¹⁶₈O gains 2 electrons:
  protons 8, electrons 8 + 2 = 10, charge = 8 − 10 = −2
```

The two ions have the same number of electrons but different charges, because their numbers of protons differ.

## Worked example 3: uranium

Uranium-235 and uranium-238 are written ²³⁵₉₂U and ²³⁸₉₂U. Each has 92 protons. Uranium-235 has 235 − 92 = 143 neutrons, and uranium-238 has 238 − 92 = 146 neutrons. The name "uranium-235" gives only the mass number; the atomic number comes from the element.

## Common mistakes

- Saying the nucleus is mostly empty space. The atom is.
- Taking the bottom number as the number of neutrons.
- Writing the charge of an ion the wrong way round. A negative ion has extra electrons.
- Saying an ion has a different number of protons. Only electrons change.
- Describing isotopes as "different elements". They are the same element.
- Giving the electron's mass as zero. It is very small, not zero.
- Listing alpha scattering observations without the conclusion each one supports.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** the nuclear model: size of atom, small positive nucleus, protons and neutrons in it, electrons around, mass in the nucleus.
- **Explain** the scattering evidence: match each observation to its conclusion, one pair at a time.
- **Calculate** particle numbers: write protons, neutrons and electrons separately, showing A − Z.
- **Compare** isotopes: same protons, different neutrons, same element.
