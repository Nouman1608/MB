This set covers section 10.3 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699) Paper 4: violent
media and violent behaviour, the media and crime, positive effects, and the effects of
sensationalism and stereotyping. Section A tests concepts; Section B asks for developed
explanations and essays.

## Section A

**1.** Define the term *desensitisation*. **[2]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by *catharsis* in debates about violent media. **[2]**

**3.** Explain **one** methodological weakness of Belson's (1978) study of television violence.
**[3]**

**4.** Explain what Felson meant by the *dramatic fallacy*. **[2]**

**5.** Identify **three** news values for crime described by Jewkes (2004). **[3]**

**6.** Outline the stages of the deviance amplification spiral. **[4]**

**7.** Explain **one** way in which the media might have a positive impact on behaviour. **[3]**

**8.** Explain what is meant by *stereotype threat*. **[3]**

## Section B

**9.** Explain **two** reasons why it is difficult to prove that violent media cause violent
behaviour. **[8]**

**10.** Explain **two** ways in which media sensationalism and stereotyping may affect behaviour.
**[8]**

**11.** "Violent media leads to violent behaviour." Evaluate this view. **[35]**

**12.** "The media's main impact on crime is through deviance amplification and moral panics."
Evaluate this view. **[35]**

## Answers

**1.** A reduced emotional response to violence [1] resulting from repeated exposure, so violence
comes to seem normal or acceptable [1]. **[2]**

**2.** The idea that watching violence releases aggressive feelings [1], so violent media may reduce
rather than increase violent behaviour, as Feshbach and Singer (1971) suggested [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Identify [1], explain [1], consequence [1]: it shows correlation, not causation [1]; boys
who were already aggressive may have chosen to watch more violent programmes [1], so the direction
of cause cannot be established [1]. Also creditable: reliance on boys' recall and honesty in
reporting their own violence. **[3]**

**4.** The belief that crime is mostly dramatic and violent [1], when in reality most crime is
minor and property-related, a belief encouraged by media coverage [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Any three: threshold; predictability; simplification; individualism; risk; sex; celebrity;
proximity; violence; spectacle; children [1 each]. **[3]**

**6.** Deviance is reported in exaggerated terms [1]; the public become concerned and the group is
labelled [1]; police and authorities tighten control [1]; the group becomes more isolated and its
deviance increases, which leads to more reporting [1]. **[4]**

**7.** Identify [1], explain [1], example [1]: public health campaigns [1] aim to change behaviour
by informing people of risks [1], for example campaigns against drink-driving or smoking [1].
Also creditable: educational programmes such as *Sesame Street*; charity appeals such as Live Aid
(1985); mobilisation such as #BlackLivesMatter. **[3]**

**8.** When people are reminded of a negative stereotype about their group [1] they may perform
worse, for example on tests [1], as Steele and Aronson (1995) found, showing that stereotypes can
affect behaviour directly [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Up to 4 marks per reason: identify [1], explain [1], develop with a study [1], evaluate [1].
- **Direction of cause** [1]: aggressive people may choose violent media rather than being made
  aggressive by it [1]; Belson's (1978) survey cannot separate the two [1]; so correlations do not
  prove effects [1].
- **Artificial research settings** [1]: laboratory experiments measure aggression against toys
  soon after viewing [1], as in Bandura's studies [1]; this may not reflect real violence or
  long-term behaviour [1].
- Also creditable: other variables such as family, peers and poverty; defining "violence"; natural
  experiments such as St Helena finding no effect. **[8]**

**10.** Up to 4 marks per way: identify [1], explain [1], develop [1], example [1].
- **Self-fulfilling prophecy** [1]: groups stereotyped as deviant are treated with suspicion by
  police, employers or teachers [1]; they may come to act in line with the label [1]; this links to
  deviance amplification [1].
- **Body image** [1]: repeated idealised images affect how people see their own bodies [1];
  Becker's research in Fiji found increased concern with weight among adolescent girls after
  television arrived [1]; this suggests long-term cultural effects [1].
- Also creditable: fear of crime raised by sensational reporting; prejudice towards whole groups;
  stereotype threat. **[8]**

**11.** Marked in levels. The top level weighs studies for and against, evaluates their methods and
considers mechanisms other than imitation. Indicative content:
- **For the view:** Belson (1978); Bandura's experiments; desensitisation; concern about
  interactive video games; copycat incidents.
- **Against the view:** Feshbach and Singer (1971) on catharsis; Hagell and Newburn (1994); the St
  Helena natural experiment; Gauntlett's critique of effects research; active audiences (10.2).
- **Methodological evaluation:** correlation and direction of cause; artificiality; time scale;
  definition of violence; other variables.
- **Sociological context:** moral panics about violent media, such as "video nasties" and the Video
  Recordings Act 1984; Barker (1984) argued the campaign had little evidence.
- **Judgement:** for example, violent media may affect some vulnerable individuals in some contexts,
  but a general causal link is not supported. **[35]**

**12.** Marked in levels. The top level explains amplification and moral panics with studies, then
weighs them against other media impacts on crime. Indicative content:
- **For the view:** Wilkins' deviance amplification spiral; Young's *The Drugtakers* (1971);
  moral panics shaping policing and law (Cohen; Hall et al., 1978); Goode and Ben-Yehuda's
  disproportionality.
- **Other impacts:** distorted picture of crime (Felson's dramatic fallacy; Jewkes's news values);
  fear of crime (Gerbner); copycat crime; glamorisation; relative deprivation (left realism);
  positive impacts such as appeals that help solve crimes.
- **Evaluation:** amplification is hard to measure and assumes a passive public; McRobbie and
  Thornton (1995) suggest folk devils can answer back; impacts vary by crime type.
- **Judgement:** for example, amplification and moral panics explain the media's effect on
  *reactions* to crime, while distorted reporting and relative deprivation explain more about fear
  and motivation. **[35]**
