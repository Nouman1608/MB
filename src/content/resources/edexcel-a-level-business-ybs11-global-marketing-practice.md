Each question uses one of the specification's command words at its published International A Level tariff: **define** (2), **calculate** (4), **explain** (4), **analyse** (6), **discuss** (8) and **assess** (12). All countries, businesses and figures are invented.

## Sources

> **Source 1: Brightwave Snacks**
>
> Brightwave sells crisps in 12 countries under one global brand. A single
> standard campaign for all countries costs $1.8m. Adapting the campaign
> for each country would cost $0.35m per country. In Country K, Brightwave
> sells a pack for an ex-factory price of $1.20 plus $0.10 freight. Country K
> charges a 15% tariff on that total. The distributor adds 20% to the
> landed cost and the retailer adds 35%. A local rival's pack costs $2.20.
> A flavour adapted for Country K is expected to raise sales by 10% on
> current revenue of $6m, with a contribution margin of 35%, at an extra
> cost of $120,000.
>
> **Source 2: Terra Outdoor**
>
> Terra sells specialist hiking gear online to 25,000 customers in 40
> countries, spending $120 on average. Customers are loyal and care about
> product quality and expert advice. Terra uses the same English-language
> website everywhere. A large sports retailer has just entered the market
> and is cutting prices by 10%. Terra expects to lose 20% of its customers
> unless it cuts its own prices by 10%.

## Section A

Questions 1 to 6 refer to Source 1.

**1.** Define the term "glocalisation". **[2]**

**2.** Calculate the extra cost of adapting the campaign in all 12 countries instead of using a single standard campaign. **[4]**

**3.** Calculate the retail price of Brightwave's pack in Country K, to the nearest cent, and its difference from the local rival's price. **[4]**

**4.** Calculate the net gain from the adapted flavour in Country K. **[4]**

**5.** Explain **one** reason why Brightwave might adapt its product for Country K. **[4]**

**6.** Analyse the problems for Brightwave of using a polycentric approach in all 12 countries. **[6]**

## Section B

Questions 7 to 12 refer to Source 2.

**7.** Define the term "niche market". **[2]**

**8.** Calculate Terra's current annual revenue and its revenue if it loses 20% of customers. **[4]**

**9.** Calculate Terra's revenue if it cuts prices by 10% and keeps all its customers, and the difference from your answer to question 8. **[4]**

**10.** Explain **one** way in which language could affect Terra's global marketing. **[4]**

**11.** Discuss whether Terra should cut its prices to match the new competitor. **[8]**

**12.** Assess whether Brightwave should standardise or adapt its marketing across its 12 countries. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** A strategy that combines a global brand and approach [1] with adaptation of some elements to suit local markets [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Adapted campaigns = 12 x $0.35m [1] = $4.2m [1]. Extra cost = 4.2 - 1.8 [1] = **$2.4m** [1]. **[4]**

**3.** Freight-inclusive cost = 1.20 + 0.10 = $1.30; tariff = 15% x 1.30 = $0.195; landed cost = **$1.495** [1]. Distributor: 1.495 x 1.20 = **$1.794** [1]. Retailer: 1.794 x 1.35 = $2.4219, so **$2.42** [1]. Difference = 2.42 - 2.20 = **$0.22 dearer** (about 10%) [1]. **[4]**

**4.** Extra revenue = 10% x $6m = $600,000 [1]. Extra contribution = 35% x 600,000 = **$210,000** [1]. Net gain = 210,000 - 120,000 [1] = **$90,000** [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Tastes differ between countries, so a flavour that sells well at home may not suit Country K [1]. Adapting the flavour should raise sales by 10%, adding $210,000 of contribution [1], which is more than the $120,000 cost [1], so adaptation improves profit and helps Brightwave compete with local snacks [1]. **[4]**

**6.** A polycentric approach treats each country separately, so Brightwave would need 12 different campaigns, which would cost $4.2m compared with $1.8m for a standard one [1]. It loses economies of scale in design, advertising and packaging [1]. Different messages in different countries may weaken the global brand identity, so customers who travel would see an inconsistent brand [1]. Managing 12 separate strategies needs more local staff and co-ordination [1], and if local managers make mistakes, there is less central control [1]. The extra cost is justified only where the local market is large enough, such as Country K, with its $6m revenue [1]. **[6]**

**7.** A small, specialised segment of a larger market [1] with distinct needs that mainstream products do not meet [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Current revenue = 25,000 x $120 [1] = **$3.0m** [1]. After losing 20%: 3.0m x 0.80 [1] = **$2.4m** [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Revenue after the cut = 25,000 x (120 x 0.90) = 25,000 x $108 [1] = **$2.7m** [1]. Difference = 2.7 - 2.4 [1] = **$0.3m higher** than losing the customers [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Terra uses the same English-language website everywhere [1], so customers who do not read English well may not understand product details or trust the website [1]. Mistranslated or poorly adapted text can create unintended meanings and damage the brand [1], while translating the site into key languages could raise sales in non-English-speaking countries but costs money [1]. **[4]**

**11.** **For cutting prices:** it keeps all customers and revenue of $2.7m, which is $0.3m more than losing 20% of customers [1]; it keeps volume, so fixed costs per sale stay low and Terra's online reach is preserved [1]; the gap between Terra and the large retailer is smaller [1].
**Against:** revenue still falls by 10% compared with the current $3.0m, and since costs probably stay the same, profit falls by more than 10% [1]; Terra's customers are loyal and care about quality and advice, so many might stay without a price cut [1]; a price war with a large retailer is hard to win [1].
**Judgement:** a smaller cut, or a stress on expert advice and quality, is probably better than a full 10% cut, because the customers value quality as well as price [1]. Terra should test the response of a few customer groups first; the decision depends on how much of the 20% would really leave and on Terra's margin [1]. **[8]**

**12.** **For standardising:** a single campaign costs $1.8m, against $4.2m adapted, a saving of $2.4m [1]; a consistent brand builds recognition, particularly for travellers [1]; snack tastes may not differ greatly in some markets [1]; a standard approach is simpler to manage [1].
**For adapting:** the Country K flavour adds a net $90,000 and shows adaptation can pay in a large market [1]; local pricing matters, as Brightwave's retail price in Country K is above the local rival's $2.20 [1]; cultural and language differences can lead to mistakes [1]; competitors with local products may win customers with better fit [1].
**Judgement:** Brightwave should use glocalisation: keep the global brand and core product, but adapt the flavour and campaign in the larger markets where the extra contribution exceeds the extra cost, as in Country K, and standardise in the smaller ones [1]. A blanket adaptation across all 12 countries is likely to cost more than it earns [1]. The decision depends on market size and how different tastes are in each country, which the source gives only for Country K, so Brightwave should research the others [1]. It must also keep pricing under review, since tariffs and distributor margins make its product dearer than local rivals [1]. **[12]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Forgetting that the tariff is charged on the ex-factory price plus freight, not on the ex-factory price alone.
- Adding the margins together instead of applying them one after the other.
- Leaving the 20% of lost customers out of the revenue comparison.
- Describing a polycentric approach without referring to the cost figures.
- Concluding without a judgement tied to market size.
