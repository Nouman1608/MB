## 2.11.1 Defining the mixed economic system

A **mixed economic system** combines private-sector choices, guided by prices, with public-sector choices made by the government. Private firms and households decide most things, while the government supplies some goods itself, taxes, subsidises, regulates and sets some prices.

Why mix? A pure market can fail: it under-provides public and merit goods, over-provides demerit goods and ignores external costs. Governments therefore use the market for most decisions and step in where it fails. Countries differ in how far they intervene, so "mixed" is a matter of degree.

## Maximum prices (price ceilings)

A **maximum price** is a legal cap: sellers may not charge more than it. It has an effect only if it sits **below** the equilibrium price.

**Worked example (rented homes, Country P, illustrative).** Demand is Qd = 600 − 20P and supply is Qs = 100 + 30P, with P the monthly rent in hundreds and Q in homes.

1. Equilibrium: 600 − 20P = 100 + 30P, so 500 = 50P, P = 10 and Q = 400.
2. The government sets a maximum rent of 6. Then Qd = 600 − 120 = 480 and Qs = 100 + 180 = 280.
3. **Shortage = 480 − 280 = 200 homes.** Only 280 are supplied, so the quantity traded is set by supply.
4. Tenants who find a home gain: they pay 6 instead of 10. The 200 who cannot find one lose.
5. Demand for 280 homes would support a rent of 600 − 20P = 280, so P = 16. A black market could appear at that level.

```

10 |  ........ equilibrium E (400 homes)

   |       280 supplied      480 demanded
   +------------------------------------ Homes
```

Diagram in words: the maximum price line is horizontal below the intersection of demand and supply. It cuts supply at 280 and demand at 480, and the horizontal gap between them is the shortage.

Consequences: rationing by queues or favouritism, falling quality, and less incentive to supply. Gains: lower prices for those who get the good. Effectiveness depends on enforcement and on how elastic supply is.

## Minimum prices (price floors)

A **minimum price** is a legal floor: sellers may not charge less than it. It has an effect only if it sits **above** equilibrium. Governments use it to support producers' incomes or to discourage consumption.

**Worked example (tea leaves, Country Q, illustrative).** Qd = 500 − 10P and Qs = 50 + 15P.

1. Equilibrium: 450 = 25P, so P = 18 and Q = 320. Producers' revenue is 18 × 320 = 5,760.
2. A minimum price of 22 gives Qd = 280 and Qs = 380.
3. **Surplus = 380 − 280 = 100.**
4. If the government buys the surplus, it spends 22 × 100 = 2,200. Producers then sell 380 and receive 22 × 380 = 8,360.
5. If it does not buy, the 100 unsold units go to waste or are sold illegally below 22. Sellers who do trade earn 22 × 280 = 6,160, which is above 5,760 only because demand is inelastic here.

Diagram in words: the minimum price line is horizontal above equilibrium. It cuts demand at 280 and supply at 380, and the gap is the surplus.

Evaluation: producers who sell gain, consumers pay more and buy less, taxpayers fund any purchases, and resources go into over-production.

## Maximum and minimum prices in the labour market

Here the price is the wage, demand is firms' demand for workers and supply is workers' willingness to work. A **minimum wage** is a price floor.

**Worked example (Country R, illustrative).** Labour demand Ld = 900 − 30W, labour supply Ls = 150 + 20W, with W the hourly wage and L in hundreds of workers.

1. Equilibrium: 750 = 50W, so W = 15 and L = 450.
2. A minimum wage of 18 gives Ld = 360 and Ls = 510.
3. **Unemployment = 510 − 360 = 150** (hundreds), meaning 15,000 workers want a job at 18 and cannot get one.
4. Total pay to employed workers falls from 15 × 450 = 6,750 to 18 × 360 = 6,480, because the fall in jobs outweighs the rise in pay.

Evaluation: workers who keep their jobs gain, but some lose work. The effect depends on how elastic labour demand is. Where firms have few substitutes for workers, the fall in jobs is small. A **maximum wage** would sit below equilibrium and create a shortage of labour, but it is rare.

## Maximum and minimum prices in the foreign exchange market

The "price" is the exchange rate: how many units of one currency buy a unit of another. Demand for a currency comes from foreigners buying that country's exports and assets. Supply comes from residents buying imports and assets abroad.

**Worked example (the dinar, Country S, illustrative).** Qd = 700 − 50P and Qs = 100 + 100P, where P is the foreign-currency price of one dinar and Q is millions of dinars.

1. Equilibrium: 600 = 150P, so P = 4 and Q = 500.
2. **Maximum exchange rate of 3** (to keep exports cheap): Qd = 550 and Qs = 400, so there is excess demand of 150 million dinars. The central bank must supply the missing dinars, or ration, or accept a black market. Selling 150 million dinars at 3 brings in 450 million foreign units, but creating that many dinars may fuel inflation.
3. **Minimum exchange rate of 5** (to keep import prices low): Qd = 450 and Qs = 600, so there is excess supply of 150 million dinars. The central bank must buy them, spending 5 × 150 = 750 million foreign units of reserves.

Evaluation: holding a rate above equilibrium needs enough foreign reserves and cannot last if they run out. A lower rate helps exporters but makes imports dearer; a higher rate does the reverse.

## Indirect taxation

An **indirect tax** is charged on spending, such as a sales tax or excise duty, and is collected by sellers. A **specific tax** is a fixed sum per unit. Costs rise by the tax, so the supply curve moves **up by that sum**. The vertical distance between the two supply curves is therefore the tax.

**Worked example (packaged juice, Country T, illustrative).** Qd = 360 − 10P and Qs = −120 + 20P.

1. Equilibrium: 480 = 30P, so P = 16 and Q = 200.
2. A tax of 6 per unit: sellers now receive P − 6, so Qs = −120 + 20(P − 6) = −240 + 20P.
3. New equilibrium: 600 = 30P, so **P = 20 and Q = 160**.
4. Consumers pay 4 more (20 − 16). Sellers receive 20 − 6 = 14, which is 2 less than before. The tax of 6 splits as 4 paid by consumers and 2 by producers.
5. Government revenue = 6 × 160 = 960, of which consumers bear 4 × 160 = 640 and producers 2 × 160 = 320.

Diagram in words: supply shifts up by 6. The tax area is a rectangle with height 6 and width 160, divided at the old price into a consumer part and a producer part.

Who bears more? The side with the **more inelastic** curve. At the original equilibrium, price elasticity of demand is 10 × 16 / 200 = 0.8 (in size) and price elasticity of supply is 20 × 16 / 200 = 1.6. Demand is less elastic, so consumers bear the larger share.

Uses: raising revenue and discouraging demerit goods. Limits: with inelastic demand a tax cuts consumption only slightly, can be regressive, and may encourage smuggling.

## Subsidies

A **subsidy** is a payment by the government to producers (or consumers) that lowers costs. The supply curve moves **down by the subsidy**.

**Worked example (solar water heaters, Country U, illustrative).** Qd = 450 − 10P and Qs = −150 + 20P.

1. Equilibrium: 600 = 30P, so P = 20 and Q = 250.
2. A subsidy of 6 per unit: sellers receive P + 6, so Qs = −150 + 20(P + 6) = −30 + 20P.
3. New equilibrium: 480 = 30P, so **P = 16 and Q = 290**.
4. Consumers pay 4 less. Sellers receive 16 + 6 = 22, which is 2 more than before.
5. Cost to government = 6 × 290 = 1,740.

Uses: encouraging merit goods and goods with external benefits, and supporting infant industries. Limits: the cost falls on taxpayers, producers may become inefficient, and removal can be politically hard.

## Regulation, privatisation, nationalisation and direct provision

**Regulation** means rules backed by law, such as pollution limits, safety standards, age limits on sales and controls on monopoly pricing. It targets the problem directly, but monitoring costs money and compliance raises firms' costs.

**Privatisation** is the sale of state-owned assets to the private sector. **Nationalisation** is the transfer of private firms into public ownership.

| | Privatisation | Nationalisation |
|---|---|---|
| Likely gains | Profit motive, efficiency, sale proceeds for the government | Control of natural monopolies, protection of essential services and jobs |
| Likely losses | Loss of social aims, possible private monopoly, job losses | Weak incentives, taxpayer cost of losses, political interference |

A small calculation: if a state firm is sold for 450 and it was costing the government 30 a year in losses, the sale equals 15 years of losses (450 / 30), but the government also gives up any future profits and control.

**Direct provision** means the government produces and supplies goods itself, often free at the point of use, such as schools, clinics and roads. It overcomes under-provision of public and merit goods. Its cost is borne by taxpayers and it faces **opportunity cost**.

**Worked example (illustrative).** A budget of 300 can build bridges costing 60 each or flood barriers costing 40 each.
- The maximum is 300 / 60 = 5 bridges or 300 / 40 = 7 barriers.
- Building 3 bridges costs 180, leaving 120, which pays for 120 / 40 = 3 barriers.
- The opportunity cost of one more bridge is 60 / 40 = 1.5 barriers.

## Judging effectiveness

| Problem in a market system | Possible intervention | Strength | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Demerit good over-consumed | Indirect tax | Raises price, raises revenue | Weak if demand is inelastic |
| Merit good under-consumed | Subsidy or direct provision | Lowers price or removes it | Cost to taxpayers |
| Unaffordable essentials | Maximum price | Quick relief | Shortages, black markets |
| Low farm or labour incomes | Minimum price or wage | Raises earnings | Surplus or unemployment |
| Public goods missing | Direct provision | Fills the gap | Cost, possible waste |
| Harmful external costs | Regulation | Direct limit | Enforcement cost |

To evaluate, weigh intended benefits against side effects, cost, time and who gains and loses. Intervention can also fail through poor information or political aims, which creates **government failure**. In your conclusion, make a judgement that depends on the context, such as how elastic demand is or how well a rule can be enforced.

## Common errors

- Setting a ceiling above equilibrium or a floor below it: the control then changes nothing.
- Reading a shortage or surplus from the equilibrium quantity instead of from the gap between demand and supply at the controlled price.
- Shifting supply the wrong way for a tax or subsidy.
- Forgetting that the consumer's price and the producer's receipt differ once a tax or subsidy exists.
- Describing intervention without a drawback, or ending with no judgement.
