## 1. Employment, unemployment and the labour force

The working-age population divides into three groups:

- **Employed:** people in paid work, full-time or part-time, including the self-employed.
- **Unemployed:** people without a job who are willing and able to work and are actively looking for work.
- **Economically inactive:** people neither in work nor looking for it, such as full-time students, retired people, those caring for family and many long-term sick.

The **labour force** (economically active population) is the employed plus the unemployed.

**Unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ labour force × 100**

**Worked example 1 (invented Country S).**

| Group | Millions |
|---|---|
| Population aged 16 and over | 52.0 |
| Employed | 32.4 |
| Unemployed | 1.6 |
| Labour force | 32.4 + 1.6 = **34.0** |
| Economically inactive | 52.0 − 34.0 = **18.0** |

- Unemployment rate = 1.6 ÷ 34.0 × 100 = **4.71%**.
- Share of the population aged 16 and over in the labour force = 34.0 ÷ 52.0 = **65.4%**.

The denominator is the labour force, not the whole population. Dividing 1.6 by 52.0 would give 3.1%, which understates unemployment.

## 2. Full employment as a policy objective

**Full employment** does not mean zero unemployment. It is the situation in which everyone who wants a job at the going wage can find one, apart from those briefly between jobs. Some frictional unemployment always exists because people change jobs, leave education and move house, and it takes time to match workers to vacancies.

Governments aim for high and stable employment because unemployment wastes resources, lowers incomes and costs the public finances. In practice, policymakers judge "full employment" by whether unemployment is low enough that further falls would cause rapidly rising wages and prices.

## 3. Measuring unemployment

| | Labour Force Survey (LFS) | Claimant count |
|---|---|---|
| Method | Survey of a sample of households | Count of people claiming unemployment-related benefits |
| Definition | International (ILO) definition: without a job, actively sought work in the past four weeks and able to start within two weeks, or waiting to start a job already found | Depends on the benefit rules in force |
| Strengths | Comparable across countries and over time; counts unemployed people whether or not they claim | Quick and cheap to collect; available for small local areas; no sampling error |
| Weaknesses | Sampling error; relies on accurate answers; slower to collect | Misses people not entitled to or not claiming benefits; changes when rules change |

Typically the LFS figure is **higher** than the claimant count, because many unemployed people do not claim, for example those with a working partner, savings, or who are young and living with parents. In Country S, the LFS gives 1.6 million unemployed while 1.1 million people claim benefits. The 0.5 million difference shows that the two measures count different groups; neither is simply "wrong".

Both measures miss **hidden unemployment** (people who would like a job but have given up looking, so count as inactive) and **underemployment** (part-time workers who want more hours, or workers in jobs below their skill level).

## 4. Causes of unemployment

| Type | Cause | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Frictional | Time taken to move between jobs and match workers to vacancies | A graduate searching for a first job |
| Structural | Mismatch between workers' skills or location and the jobs available, often after an industry declines or technology changes | Workers from a closed shipyard whose skills are not wanted locally |
| Cyclical (demand-deficient) | Fall in aggregate demand in a downturn or recession | Retail staff laid off as spending falls |
| Seasonal | Predictable changes in demand during the year | Beach café staff in winter |

**Evaluating causes.**

- **Cyclical** unemployment can be reduced by raising aggregate demand, but only if the problem is lack of demand; it may persist if confidence stays low.
- **Structural** unemployment needs retraining, help with moving, or new industries, which take time and money; it does not respond quickly to more demand.
- **Frictional** unemployment can be shortened by better information on vacancies, but it cannot be eliminated and is partly a sign of a healthy, mobile labour market.
- Types overlap: cyclical unemployment can become structural if workers lose skills while out of work for a long time.

## 5. Consequences of unemployment

**Worked example 2 (Country S, a recession).** Cyclical unemployment rises by 0.6 million.

- Each worker produced £55,000 of output a year, so lost output = 0.6 m × £55,000 = **£33 bn** a year.
- Each unemployed worker pays £6,000 less in tax and receives £5,000 in benefits, so the cost to the public finances = 0.6 m × £11,000 = **£6.6 bn** a year.

| Who | Consequences |
|---|---|
| Individuals | Lost income, lower living standards, loss of skills, poorer health and wellbeing, lower future earnings |
| Firms | Lower demand for their products; but easier recruitment and less pressure on wages |
| Government | Less tax revenue, more benefit spending, a larger budget deficit |
| Economy | Lost output (inside the PPC), lower growth, wider inequality, regional decline |

**Evaluation.** The cost depends on the **duration** (long-term unemployment is far more damaging than short spells), the **type** (frictional unemployment has a much smaller cost), and **where** it is concentrated (regional unemployment can lead to lasting decline).

## 6. Effects of full employment

| Benefits | Risks |
|---|---|
| Output close to potential, so resources are not wasted | Wage pressure as firms compete for scarce workers, which can feed inflation |
| Higher incomes and tax revenue, lower benefit spending | Skill shortages that hold back growth |
| Lower poverty and inequality | Rising imports as incomes grow, worsening the current account |
| Better wellbeing and social cohesion | Less incentive for firms to train if they can poach staff, although tight markets can also push firms to invest in labour-saving technology |

The overall effect depends on whether productive capacity is growing: if it is, full employment can be maintained without inflation.

## Annotated model answer

*Question:* "Explain why the Labour Force Survey measure of unemployment is usually higher than the claimant count."

> The LFS counts everyone without a job who has looked for work in the past four weeks and can start within two weeks, whether or not they receive benefits *[definition]*. The claimant count includes only people receiving unemployment-related benefits *[contrast]*. Many unemployed people are not entitled to benefits or choose not to claim, for example because a partner works *[reason]*. These people appear in the LFS but not in the claimant count, so the LFS figure is higher, as in Country S where it is 1.6 million against 1.1 million *[conclusion with data]*.

## Common mistakes

- Dividing the number unemployed by the whole population instead of the labour force.
- Counting students or retired people as unemployed.
- Defining full employment as zero unemployment.
- Giving cyclical remedies for structural unemployment.
- Treating every consequence of unemployment as equally serious, whatever its length or type.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate:** show the labour force first, then the rate to two decimal places.
- **Explain:** name the type of unemployment, its cause and its effect, applied to the context.
- **Evaluate:** distinguish types and durations, consider who bears the cost, and judge which policy fits the cause.
