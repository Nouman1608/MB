These questions cover Unit S1, section 1: the basic ideas of mathematical modelling as applied in probability and statistics. All data are invented for practice. No significance tests are needed: comparisons are made by inspecting predictions and observations.

## Section A

**1.** Explain what is meant by a mathematical model in statistics. **[2]**

**2.** Give two reasons why mathematical models are used in probability and statistics. **[2]**

**3.** State two limitations of using a mathematical model. **[2]**

**4.** A die is modelled as fair and rolled 240 times. State the expected frequency of each score, and the expected number of odd scores. **[2]**

## Section B

**5.** A transport planner wants to model the number of passengers who miss a particular train each day. Describe, in order, the stages of the modelling process she should follow. **[5]**

**6.** A spinner has five sectors labelled 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5. A student models the score X with P(X = x) = x/15 for x = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5. The spinner is spun 300 times.

**(a)** Find the expected frequency of each score under the model. **[2]**

The observed frequencies of scores 1 to 5 are 24, 37, 58, 85 and 96.

**(b)** Comment on how well the model fits these frequencies. **[2]**

**(c)** Find the mean score predicted by the model and the observed mean score, and comment. **[3]**

**7.** A call centre models its calls as follows: each call is answered within one minute with probability 0.9, independently of all other calls. It receives 50 calls each hour.

**(a)** Find the number of calls per hour the model predicts will be answered within one minute. **[1]**

Over eight hours, the numbers of calls answered within one minute were:

```
46  44  47  45  31  46  45  44
```

**(b)** Compare these data with the model's prediction. **[3]**

**(c)** State which assumption of the model is most open to doubt, giving a reason in context. **[2]**

**(d)** Suggest a refinement to the model. **[2]**

**8.** A student models the time she takes to walk to school, T minutes, by a Normal distribution with mean 12 and standard deviation 1.5.

**(a)** Give one reason why a Normal model may be suitable and one reason why it may not be. **[2]**

**(b)** A Normal distribution gives a non-zero probability to negative values. Explain why this is not a practical concern for this model. **[2]**

**9.** A hospital models the day of the week on which babies are born as equally likely to be any of the seven days. Over a period, 140 births were recorded:

| Day | Mon | Tue | Wed | Thu | Fri | Sat | Sun |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Births | 22 | 23 | 21 | 22 | 24 | 15 | 13 |

**(a)** Find the expected frequency for each day under the model. **[1]**

**(b)** Compare the observed frequencies with the model. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest a refined model, and explain how it should be tested. **[3]**

**10.** There is no theoretical reason to choose a particular probability that a drawing pin lands point up when dropped. Explain how a probability model for this could be built and then tested. **[4]**

**11.** For each model, state whether it is deterministic or a probability model, giving a reason.

**(a)** The cost of a taxi journey is £3 plus £1.50 per kilometre. **[1]**

**(b)** Each light bulb in a box fails within a year with probability 0.04. **[1]**

**12.** A student estimates new probabilities for the spinner in Question 6 from the 300 observed spins, and then says: "My refined model fits the 300 spins perfectly, so it is correct." Criticise this claim. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** A simplified description of a real-world situation, expressed mathematically (for example as a probability distribution) [1], that can be used to make predictions about that situation [1].

**2.** Any two from: they are quick or cheap compared with collecting complete data; they simplify a complex situation; they allow predictions to be made; parameters can be varied to explore the effect of changes [1] [1].

**3.** Any two from: simplifying assumptions may leave out important factors; the model only applies where its assumptions hold; predictions outside the range of data used to test it may be unreliable [1] [1].

**4.** Each score: 240 × 1/6 = **40** [1]. Odd scores: 240 × 1/2 = **120** [1].

**5.** Recognise the real problem and the random quantity (passengers missing the train each day) [1]. Make simplifying assumptions and devise a model [1]. Use the model to predict, for example the expected number missing each day [1]. Collect data on how many actually miss the train, and compare them with the predictions [1]. Refine the model (change an assumption or parameter) if the fit is poor, and repeat the comparison [1].

**6. (a)** Expected frequencies = 300 × x/15 = 20x [1]: **20, 40, 60, 80, 100** [1].

**(b)** Every observed frequency is within 5 of its expected value (24 against 20, 37 against 40, 58 against 60, 85 against 80, 96 against 100) and the increasing pattern matches [1], so the model appears to fit well [1].

**(c)** Model: E(X) = (1 + 4 + 9 + 16 + 25)/15 = 55/15 = **3.67** (3 s.f.) [1]. Observed: (24 + 74 + 174 + 340 + 480)/300 = 1092/300 = **3.64** [1]. These are very close, which supports the model [1].

**7. (a)** 50 × 0.9 = **45** [1].

**(b)** Seven of the eight hours give values between 44 and 47, close to 45 [1]. One hour gives 31, far below the prediction [1]. The mean is 348/8 = 43.5, pulled below 45 by that one hour [1].

**(c)** Independence [1]: if the centre is short of staff or a fault occurs in one hour, many calls are delayed together, as in the hour with 31 [1].

**(d)** For example, let the probability of answering within one minute depend on the hour or on the number of staff on duty [1], then compare the refined model's predictions with data from further hours [1].

**8. (a)** Suitable: walking times are likely to cluster symmetrically around a typical value [1]. Not suitable: delays (such as waiting at a crossing) may make the distribution skewed, with occasional long times [1].

**(b)** Zero is 12 ÷ 1.5 = 8 standard deviations below the mean [1], so the probability the model gives to negative times is negligible [1].

**9. (a)** 140 ÷ 7 = **20** each [1].

**(b)** Weekdays are all above 20 (21 to 24) [1], while Saturday (15) and Sunday (13) are well below, so the equally-likely model does not fit well [1].

**(c)** For example, give weekdays one probability and weekend days a smaller one, estimated from these data: weekdays 112/140 shared among five days and weekends 28/140 shared between two days [1] [1]. The refined model should then be tested against a new set of births [1].

**10.** Drop the pin a large number of times and record the relative frequency of "point up" [1]; use this as the model's probability [1]. Then use the model to predict results for a new series of drops [1], and compare the predictions with the new observations, refining the probability if they disagree [1].

**11. (a)** Deterministic: each distance gives one exact cost, with no randomness [1].

**(b)** Probability model: it gives a chance of failure, so the number of failures varies [1].

**12.** The refined probabilities were estimated from those same 300 spins, so it is bound to fit them [1]. It must be tested on new data before it can be judged a good model [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Omitting the comparison or refinement stage from a description of the modelling cycle.
- Comparing only means: in Question 7 the evidence is the single low hour, not the mean.
- Suggesting a refinement that does not say what changes.
- Writing comments without numbers or without context.
- Treating a refined model as proven because it fits the data it was built from.
