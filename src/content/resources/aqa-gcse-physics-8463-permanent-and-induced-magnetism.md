## Poles and the forces between them

The **poles** are the places on a magnet where the magnetic forces are **strongest**. A freely suspended bar
magnet turns until one end points roughly north; that end is called the **north-seeking pole** (north pole) and
the other the **south-seeking pole** (south pole).

When two magnets are close:

- **like poles repel** (N and N, or S and S)
- **unlike poles attract** (N and S).

The force between magnets is a **non-contact force**: it acts across a gap, through air and through many other
materials such as paper or plastic. It gets weaker as the distance between the magnets increases.

## Permanent and induced magnets

A **permanent magnet** produces its **own** magnetic field all the time. A bar magnet made of steel is an
example.

An **induced magnet** is a material that **becomes a magnet when it is placed in a magnetic field**. A steel
paper clip near a bar magnet is one. The end of the clip nearest the magnet's N pole becomes an S pole, so the
clip is pulled towards the magnet. That is why **induced magnetism always causes a force of attraction**: the
induced pole nearest the magnet is always the opposite kind.

When it is removed from the field, an induced magnet **loses most or all of its magnetism quickly**. Soft iron
loses its magnetism almost at once, which is why it is used where magnetism must be switched off, such as the
core of an electromagnet.

### A chain of clips

Hang one paper clip from the N pole of a bar magnet, then hang a second clip from the first. The second clip is
held because the first clip has become an induced magnet. Pull the top clip away from the magnet and the chain
falls apart: the induced magnetism disappears once the clips leave the field.

### Testing whether a bar is a magnet

You have two identical-looking metal bars. One is a permanent magnet; the other is unmagnetised iron. How can
you tell which is which using only a known magnet?

- **Attraction proves nothing**: a magnet attracts an unmagnetised iron bar (induced magnetism) and also
  attracts the opposite pole of another magnet.
- **Repulsion is the only proof.** If one end of a bar is **repelled** by a pole of the known magnet, that bar
  must be a permanent magnet, because an induced magnet is only ever attracted.

This "repulsion is the test" idea is one of the most common reasoning questions on 4.7.1.

## Magnetic materials

The **magnetic materials** named in the specification are **iron, steel, cobalt and nickel**. They are attracted
by a magnet and can be made into induced magnets. Most other metals, such as copper, aluminium and brass, are not
magnetic. A question may describe a sorting machine or a can-sorting magnet: a steel can is attracted; an
aluminium can is not.

## Magnetic fields

A **magnetic field** is the **region around a magnet where a force acts on another magnet or on a magnetic
material**.

- The **strength** of the field depends on the **distance from the magnet**: it is strongest close to the magnet.
- The field is **strongest at the poles**.
- The **direction** of the field at any point is the direction of the force that would act on a **north pole**
  placed there.
- So field lines go **from the north (seeking) pole to the south (seeking) pole** of a magnet.

### Drawing the field of a bar magnet

A correct diagram has all of these features:

1. Lines leave the N pole and enter the S pole, with **arrows pointing N to S** outside the magnet.
2. Lines are **closest together at the poles** (strongest field) and spread out with distance (weaker field).
3. **Lines never cross or touch.** At any point the field has only one direction.
4. Lines are smooth curves that start and finish on the magnet; they do not stop in mid-air.

A single line with an arrow shows direction; the **spacing** of several lines shows strength. Examiners look for
both.

### Plotting a field with a compass

A plotting compass is a tiny bar magnet that is free to turn. Its needle lines up with the field, with its N end
pointing along the field direction.

1. Place the magnet on paper and draw round it.
2. Put the compass close to the N pole. Mark a dot at the point the needle's N end points to.
3. Move the compass so the **tail** of the needle is over that dot, and mark the new point the needle shows.
4. Repeat until you reach the S pole. Join the dots with a smooth line and add an arrow from N to S.
5. Start again from different points around the N pole to build up the pattern.

Iron filings sprinkled on paper over a magnet show the **shape** of the pattern quickly, but they do not show the
**direction**. Only the compass method gives arrows.

## The Earth's magnetic field

A **magnetic compass contains a small bar magnet**. Wherever you take it, away from other magnets, the needle
settles pointing in a particular direction, roughly north–south. A magnet only lines up like this when it is in a
magnetic field, so this is **evidence that the Earth has a magnetic field**. The field is like that of a bar magnet
inside the Earth, which is evidence that the **Earth's core must be magnetic**.

A useful consequence: the N pole of a compass needle points towards the Earth's geographic north. Unlike poles
attract, so the magnetic pole near the geographic North Pole must behave as a **south** magnetic pole. Field lines
around the Earth therefore run from the southern hemisphere to the northern hemisphere.

## Worked examples

**Worked example 1.** A student holds an iron nail near the S pole of a bar magnet. Explain why the nail is
attracted, and what happens when the magnet is taken away.

> The nail becomes an induced magnet because it is in the magnet's field. The end nearest the S pole becomes an N
> pole, and unlike poles attract. When the magnet is removed, the nail loses most or all of its magnetism quickly,
> so it no longer attracts other iron objects.

**Worked example 2.** Two bar magnets are laid end to end with their N poles facing, a few centimetres apart.
Describe the force between them, and how it changes if one magnet is turned round.

> Like poles face each other, so the magnets repel. If one magnet is turned round, an N pole faces an S pole, so they
> attract. In both cases the force gets stronger as the gap is made smaller, because the field is stronger close to
> the poles.

## Common mistakes

- Saying a magnet can repel an unmagnetised piece of iron. Induced magnetism always attracts.
- Using attraction as proof that an object is a magnet.
- Listing copper or aluminium as magnetic materials.
- Drawing field lines from S to N, or letting them cross.
- Drawing evenly spaced lines all round the magnet, which hides that the field is strongest at the poles.
- Saying iron filings show the field direction.
- Describing the compass as evidence of a magnetic field "at the North Pole" only. It works everywhere, which is
  what shows the whole Earth has a field.

## Exam technique

**State** or **name** needs a single fact, such as one magnetic material. **Describe** a field pattern by naming the
shape, the direction (N to S) and where it is strongest. **Explain** needs a cause: "the paper clip is attracted
**because** it becomes an induced magnet with an opposite pole nearest the magnet". In a **draw** question, check
arrows, spacing and that no lines cross before moving on. When a question asks you to **suggest** how to identify a
magnet, make repulsion the key step and say why attraction alone is not enough.
