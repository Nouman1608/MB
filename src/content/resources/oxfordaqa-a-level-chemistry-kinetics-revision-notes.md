## Definitions to learn word for word

| Term | What to write |
|---|---|
| Activation energy, Ea (3.1.6.1) | The minimum energy that particles must have when they collide for a reaction to occur |
| Rate of reaction (3.1.6.3) | The change in concentration (or amount) of a reactant or product per unit time |
| Catalyst (3.1.6.5) | A substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction without being changed in chemical composition or amount |
| How a catalyst works (3.1.6.5) | It provides an alternative reaction route of lower activation energy |
| Successful collision | A collision between particles with energy at or above Ea, which leads to a reaction |

## Rate: meaning and units

| What you measure | Mean rate | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Concentration of a reactant or product | change in concentration ÷ time | mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ |
| Volume of gas formed | change in volume ÷ time | cm³ s⁻¹ |
| Amount of a substance | change in amount ÷ time | mol s⁻¹ |
| Mass lost as a gas escapes | change in mass ÷ time | g s⁻¹ |

- Rate is quoted as a positive number whether a reactant falls or a product rises.
- If the equation ratio is 1 : 1, a reactant is used up at the same rate as the product forms.
- Rate is highest at the start and falls as reactants are used up.

**Worked reminder.** The concentration of a product rises from 0 to 0.036 mol dm⁻³ in 120 s.
Mean rate = 0.036 ÷ 120 = **3.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹**.

## 3.1.6.1 Collision theory

> **Method: a three-link chain for any rate question**
> - Link A: do particles now meet **more or less often**?
> - Link B: is a **bigger or smaller fraction** of those meetings carrying at least Ea?
> - Link C: finish with the effect on **successful collisions per second**, then on rate.

Why most collisions fail: in a typical mixture only a tiny minority of particles carry Ea or more. A
collision between two lower-energy particles cannot break the necessary bonds, so the pair rebounds with no
change.

## 3.1.6.2 Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution: drawing rules

| Feature | What to show | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Start | Line leaves (0, 0) | Zero-energy molecules do not exist |
| Peak | One maximum | It marks the most probable energy |
| Shape | Lopsided, long right-hand side | The tail pulls the mean energy above the peak energy |
| Tail | Flattens towards the energy axis, never reaching it | No upper energy limit |
| Area under line | Fixed for a given sample | It represents every molecule present |
| Area beyond Ea | Small sliver in the tail | Molecules able to react on collision |

> **Method: comparing a cool and a warm sample on shared axes**
> - Cool curve: taller, narrower, peak nearer the origin.
> - Warm curve: shorter, broader, peak displaced to higher energy.
> - One crossing point; to the right of it the warm curve is on top, so place Ea there.
> - Enclosed areas match, because no molecules are gained or lost.

**Reading areas from a grid.** Proportion with E ≥ Ea = (squares beyond Ea ÷ total squares) × 100. Compare
two curves or two Ea lines by dividing one count by the other.

## 3.1.6.3 Temperature

- Warming raises the average kinetic energy of the molecules.
- Big effect: the distribution moves to higher energy, so the **fraction at or above Ea multiplies**.
- Small effect: faster molecules meet slightly more often.
- Why "small rise, big effect": Ea sits in the thin tail, and a modest sideways shift of the curve swells
  that thin sliver many times over.
- For a reaction with Ea near 50 kJ mol⁻¹, a 10 K rise near room temperature roughly doubles the rate, while
  collision frequency rises by only about 2 %.

## 3.1.6.4 Concentration and pressure

- Concentrated solution: each cm³ holds more reactant particles, so they meet **more often**; with the same
  fraction succeeding, the number of successes per second, and so the rate, increases.
- Compressed gas (same temperature): the molecules are packed into less space, so they meet **more often**
  and the rate increases.
- From pV = nRT: n/V = p/(RT), so at fixed T, doubling p doubles the amount of gas per unit volume.
- Neither change moves the Maxwell–Boltzmann curve or changes the **proportion** of collisions with
  E ≥ Ea.

## 3.1.6.5 Catalysts on a distribution curve

> **Method: catalyst sketch for a gas reaction at a fixed temperature**
> - A single distribution line only, since temperature (and so the spread of energies) is unchanged.
> - Two energy markers in the tail: the original Ea, and Ea(cat) at a lower energy.
> - Shade the band between them: molecules that only the new pathway lets react.
> - Words to include: different pathway, smaller energy barrier, bigger fraction able to react, more
>   successful collisions each second.

- The catalyst is left with the same mass and chemical composition at the end.
- On an enthalpy profile, the catalysed hump is lower; reactant and product levels, and so ΔH, do not
  change.
- A catalyst does not change the position of equilibrium (3.1.7); see
  [equilibria revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-equilibria-revision-notes/).

## Must-know distinctions

- **Hotter:** meetings slightly more frequent; fraction with E ≥ Ea much bigger; curve flattens and moves
  right; barrier unchanged.
- **More concentrated, or gas compressed:** meetings more frequent; fraction unchanged; curve unchanged;
  barrier unchanged.
- **Catalyst added:** meeting frequency unchanged; fraction bigger because the barrier is lower on a new
  pathway; curve unchanged, only the Ea marker moves.
- **Peak vs average:** the peak gives the commonest energy; the long tail lifts the average above it.
- **Frequency vs proportion:** concentration and pressure change how often particles collide; temperature
  and catalysts mainly change what fraction of collisions succeed.
- **Curve vs Ea line:** temperature moves the curve; a catalyst moves the Ea line.

## Quick self-test

1. Define activation energy.
2. Why do most collisions in a reaction mixture not lead to a reaction?
3. On a Maxwell–Boltzmann sketch, what does the area beyond the Ea line stand for?
4. Why is the mean energy greater than the most probable energy?
5. A reactant concentration falls from 0.64 mol dm⁻³ to 0.40 mol dm⁻³ in 80 s. Calculate the mean rate.
6. A gas syringe collects 66 cm³ of gas in the first 40 s. Calculate the mean rate.
7. A curve has a total area of 2400 squares, with 30 squares beyond Ea. What percentage of molecules have
   E ≥ Ea?
8. Beyond Ea there are 5 squares; beyond Ea(cat) there are 40. By what factor has the catalyst increased the
   proportion of molecules that can react?
9. A catalyst lowers Ea from 120 kJ mol⁻¹ to 75 kJ mol⁻¹. By how much has the barrier fallen?
10. Does doubling the pressure of a gas change the proportion of collisions with E ≥ Ea?
11. When a reaction mixture is warmed, which effect contributes most to the rise in rate?
12. Name two things about a catalyst that are the same at the end of a reaction as at the start.

### Answers

1. The minimum energy that particles must have when they collide for a reaction to occur.
2. Most particles have less energy than the activation energy.
3. The molecules with enough energy to react if they collide.
4. The curve is not symmetrical; the long high-energy tail pulls the mean to the right of the peak.
5. (0.64 − 0.40) ÷ 80 = 0.24 ÷ 80 = **3.0 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹**.
6. 66 ÷ 40 = **1.65 cm³ s⁻¹**.
7. 30 ÷ 2400 × 100 = **1.25 %**.
8. 40 ÷ 5 = **8 times**.
9. 120 − 75 = **45 kJ mol⁻¹**.
10. No. It increases collision frequency only.
11. The larger proportion of molecules with energy at or above Ea.
12. Its chemical composition and its amount (mass).

## Where marks are usually lost

- An Ea definition without "minimum".
- A warm curve drawn taller than the cool one, or with more area beneath it.
- Lines that begin part-way up the y-axis or land on the energy axis.
- Temperature explained by collision frequency alone.
- Concentration or pressure credited with raising particle energy.
- A catalyst said to "supply energy" or to shift the distribution.
- "Lower activation energy" with no mention of a different pathway.
- Rate units missing the "per second" part.
- Calling the peak the mean energy.
- Stopping before the final link: successful collisions per second.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the units of rate of reaction?**
With concentration in mol dm⁻³ and time in seconds, rate is in mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹. Rate is always quoted as a positive number, whether a reactant is falling or a product is rising.

**Is the peak of a Maxwell–Boltzmann curve the mean energy?**
No. The peak shows the most probable energy. The long high-energy tail pulls the mean energy above the most probable value.

**How does a hotter Maxwell–Boltzmann curve differ from a cooler one?**
At a higher temperature the peak moves to the right and becomes lower, and the curve is broader. The total area under the curve stays the same because the number of molecules is unchanged.

**How do you show the effect of a catalyst on a Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution?**
Keep a single curve, because the temperature has not changed, and add a second, lower Ea line nearer the peak. The extra area between the two lines represents molecules that can now react by the catalysed route.
