Topic 5 belongs to Unit 1: Structure, Bonding and Introduction to Organic Chemistry, an IAS unit. Data for this set: H = 1.0, C = 12.0, N = 14.0, O = 16.0, Cl = 35.5, Ca = 40.1. Molar volume of a gas = 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹ at room temperature and pressure.

## Questions

**1.** This question is about the structure of alkenes.

**(a)** State the general formula of open-chain alkenes with one C=C bond. **[1]**
**(b)** State the general formula of cycloalkenes with one ring and one C=C bond. **[1]**
**(c)** Describe how the σ bond and the π bond in a C=C double bond are formed. **[2]**

**2.** Ethene reacts with hydrogen bromide at room temperature.

**(a)** Describe, with the help of a labelled sketch, the mechanism of this reaction. Show curly arrows, dipoles, charges and the intermediate. **[4]**
**(b)** Give the name of the product. **[1]**

**3.** 2-chlorobut-2-ene has the structure CH₃CCl=CHCH₃.

**(a)** Explain why 2-chlorobut-2-ene shows geometric isomerism. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the cis–trans naming system is not suitable for this compound. **[1]**
**(c)** Describe the Z isomer and explain how you assigned it. **[2]**

**4.** 1.75 g of an unbranched, open-chain alkene X reacts completely with 600 cm³ of hydrogen at room temperature and pressure. One molecule of X contains one C=C bond.

**(a)** Calculate the molar mass of X and deduce its molecular formula. **[3]**
**(b)** X exists as E and Z isomers. Give the name of X. **[1]**
**(c)** State the catalyst for this reaction and name the organic product. **[2]**

**5.** Cyclohexene, C₆H₁₀, is reacted separately with four reagents. For each, give the information asked for.

**(a)** Hydrogen bromide: the name of the organic product. **[1]**
**(b)** Steam: the conditions and the name of the organic product. **[2]**
**(c)** Acidified potassium manganate(VII): the observation and the name of the organic product. **[2]**
**(d)** Hydrogen: the catalyst and the name of the organic product. **[2]**

**6.** Ethene is bubbled into an aqueous solution containing bromine and a high concentration of sodium chloride. The organic products include 1,2-dibromoethane and 1-bromo-2-chloroethane, but no 1,2-dichloroethane.

**(a)** Explain how the formation of 1-bromo-2-chloroethane supports a two-step mechanism with a carbocation intermediate. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why no 1,2-dichloroethane forms. **[1]**
**(c)** Suggest the name of one other organic product, formed by reaction with water. **[1]**

**7.** 2-ethylbut-1-ene, CH₂=C(CH₂CH₃)₂, reacts with hydrogen bromide to give a major and a minor product.

**(a)** Using curly arrows, show how the major product forms. **[4]**
**(b)** Give the name of this product. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why this product is formed in much larger amounts than the other possible product. **[2]**

**8.** Describe a simple chemical test to distinguish cyclohexane from cyclohexene. Give the reagent and the result for each compound. **[3]**

**9.** This question is about addition polymers.

**(a)** Draw the repeat unit of the polymer formed from propenenitrile, CH₂=CHCN, and explain why it is described as an addition polymer. **[2]**
**(b)** A polymer has the repeat unit –[CH₂–C(CH₃)₂]ₙ–. Give the structure of its monomer. **[1]**
**(c)** A chain of poly(propenenitrile) has Mr 2.12 × 10⁵. Calculate the number of monomer units in the chain. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why poly(alkenes) persist for a long time in landfill. **[2]**

**10.** A waste-to-energy plant burns 2.50 kg of poly(chloroethene), PVC, repeat unit –[CH₂–CHCl]–. Assume all the chlorine is released as hydrogen chloride.

**(a)** Calculate the mass of hydrogen chloride formed. **[2]**
**(b)** The exhaust gas passes through a scrubber containing calcium hydroxide. Write an equation for the reaction and calculate the minimum mass of calcium hydroxide needed. **[3]**
**(c)** Give one advantage of incinerating polymer waste, other than reducing landfill. **[1]**
**(d)** Give one benefit and one limitation of replacing PVC packaging with a biodegradable polymer. **[2]**

**11.** A student makes cyclohexene by heating 6.00 g of cyclohexanol, C₆H₁₁OH, with a phosphoric acid catalyst. She collects 3.69 g of pure cyclohexene.

**(a)** Give a balanced equation for the dehydration. **[1]**
**(b)** Determine the percentage yield of cyclohexene. **[3]**
**(c)** Suggest one practical reason why the yield is less than 100%. **[1]**
**(d)** A drop of the product is shaken with bromine water. State what is seen. **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** CₙH₂ₙ [1]
**(b)** CₙH₂ₙ₋₂ [1]
**(c)** σ bond: head-on overlap of orbitals along the axis between the two carbon nuclei [1]; π bond: sideways overlap of p orbitals, giving electron density above and below the plane of the molecule [1].
*Examiner insight:* For each bond give the type of overlap and where the electron density lies; "above and below the plane" is the key π detail.

**2. (a)** HBr shown with Hδ+ and Brδ− [1]; curly arrow from the C=C to the H atom [1]; curly arrow from the H–Br bond onto Br, giving Br⁻ and the carbocation CH₃CH₂⁺ [1]; Br⁻ uses a lone pair to bond to C⁺, shown by a third curly arrow [1].
**(b)** **Bromoethane** [1]
*Examiner insight:* The specification expects arrows to start from a bond or a lone pair, so an arrow drawn from the Br⁻ charge or from a carbon atom is unlikely to be credited.

**3. (a)** Rotation about the C=C is restricted because twisting would break the π bond overlap [1]; carbon 2 holds Cl and CH₃ while carbon 3 holds CH₃ and H, so both have two unlike groups [1].
**(b)** Both carbons carry a CH₃ group but the other groups differ, so "cis" could refer to the methyl groups or to a different pair; the name is ambiguous [1].
**(c)** Priorities: Cl over CH₃ on carbon 2 (atomic number 17 vs 6), and CH₃ over H on carbon 3 [1]; the **Z isomer** has Cl and the carbon-3 CH₃ on the same side, which places the two methyl groups on opposite sides [1].
*Examiner insight:* State the atomic-number comparison for each carbon; a correct letter with no stated priorities leaves the explanation incomplete.

**4. (a)** n(H₂) = 0.600 ÷ 24.0 = 0.0250 mol, so n(X) = 0.0250 mol [1]; M = 1.75 ÷ 0.0250 = 70.0 g mol⁻¹ [1]; CₙH₂ₙ: 14n = 70.0 so n = 5, **C₅H₁₀** [1].
**(b)** **Pent-2-ene** (pent-1-ene has two H atoms on carbon 1 and cannot show E–Z isomerism) [1].
**(c)** Nickel [1]; **pentane** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Convert cm³ to dm³ before dividing by 24.0, and show each step of the working.

**5. (a)** **Bromocyclohexane** [1]
**(b)** Phosphoric acid catalyst with high temperature and pressure [1]; **cyclohexanol** [1].
**(c)** Purple solution turns colourless [1]; **cyclohexane-1,2-diol** [1].
**(d)** Nickel catalyst (with heat) [1]; **cyclohexane** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Give the starting colour as well as the final one; "turns colourless" alone does not describe a change.

**6. (a)** Bromine is polarised and the δ+ bromine adds first, forming the carbocation BrCH₂CH₂⁺ [1]; the carbocation can then be attacked by any nucleophile present [1]; attack by Cl⁻ gives BrCH₂CH₂Cl, which could not form if both bromine atoms added in one step [1].
**(b)** Cl⁻ is a nucleophile, not an electrophile, so it cannot make the first attack on the electron-rich C=C; every product must contain at least one bromine [1].
**(c)** **2-bromoethanol** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Link each product to a step of the mechanism; a bare list of products explains nothing.

**7. (a)** HBr drawn with Hδ+–Brδ−; arrow from the C=C to H [1]; arrow from the H–Br bond onto Br [1]; tertiary carbocation CH₃C⁺(CH₂CH₃)₂ with Br⁻ shown with a lone pair [1]; arrow from the lone pair on Br⁻ to C⁺ [1].
**(b)** **3-bromo-3-methylpentane** [1]
**(c)** H adding to the CH₂ end gives a tertiary carbocation, while H adding to the other carbon gives a primary carbocation [1]; the tertiary carbocation is more stable because three alkyl groups release electron density towards C⁺, so it forms faster and its product dominates [1].
*Examiner insight:* A tidy mechanism through the primary carbocation still leads to the wrong product, so check which carbocation you drew.

**8.** Reagent: bromine water [1]; cyclohexene: orange-brown to colourless [1]; cyclohexane: stays orange-brown (no visible change) [1].
*Examiner insight:* Give a separate result for each compound; one observation covers only half of a two-compound test.

**9. (a)** –[CH₂–CH(CN)]ₙ– with bonds through the brackets [1]; the C=C bonds open and monomers join with no other product formed [1].
**(b)** **CH₂=C(CH₃)₂** (2-methylpropene) [1]
**(c)** Mr(C₃H₃N) = 36.0 + 3.0 + 14.0 = 53.0 [1]; 2.12 × 10⁵ ÷ 53.0 = **4000** [1].
**(d)** The chains are saturated and held together by strong C–C and C–H bonds [1]; so they are unreactive and microorganisms cannot break them down [1].
*Examiner insight:* A repeat unit with a remaining C=C, or without extension bonds, is a common way to lose the structure mark.

**10. (a)** Mr(C₂H₃Cl) = 24.0 + 3.0 + 35.5 = 62.5, so n(HCl) = 2500 ÷ 62.5 = 40.0 mol [1]; mass = 40.0 × 36.5 = **1460 g (1.46 kg)** [1].
**(b)** Ca(OH)₂ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + 2H₂O [1]; n(Ca(OH)₂) = 40.0 ÷ 2 = 20.0 mol [1]; Mr = 74.1, mass = 20.0 × 74.1 = **1480 g (1.48 kg)** [1].
**(c)** The energy released can generate electricity or heat [1].
**(d)** Benefit: it is broken down by microorganisms, so less long-lived waste [1]; limitation: it may need composting conditions to degrade, or may cost more to produce [1].
*Examiner insight:* Use the 1 : 2 ratio from your equation; forgetting to halve the moles of HCl doubles the mass of calcium hydroxide.

**11. (a)** C₆H₁₁OH → C₆H₁₀ + H₂O [1]
**(b)** n(cyclohexanol) = 6.00 ÷ 100.0 = 0.0600 mol [1]; theoretical mass of cyclohexene = 0.0600 × 82.0 = 4.92 g [1]; yield = 3.69 ÷ 4.92 × 100 = **75.0%** [1].
**(c)** Some product is lost during transfer, separation or distillation (or the reaction is incomplete) [1].
**(d)** Orange-brown bromine water turns colourless [1].
*Examiner insight:* Find the theoretical yield via moles first; dividing 3.69 by 6.00 directly gives a meaningless figure.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using CₙH₂ₙ for a cycloalkene.
- Describing π overlap without saying where the electron density lies.
- Starting curly arrows from atoms or charges instead of from the C=C, a bond or a lone pair.
- Missing the δ+/δ− labels on HBr or Br₂, or the charge on the carbocation.
- Choosing the primary carbocation when a more stable one is available.
- Assigning E or Z without stating the atomic-number priorities on each carbon.
- Giving the conditions for hydration without the acid catalyst.
- Leaving a C=C in a polymer repeat unit.
- Forgetting mole ratios from the equation in scrubbing and yield calculations.
- Describing only the final colour in a colour-change test.
