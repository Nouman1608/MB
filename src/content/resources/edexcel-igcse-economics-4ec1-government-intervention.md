## What the specification asks for

| Point | Wording |
|---|---|
| a | Government policy to deal with externalities: taxation, subsidies, fines, regulation, pollution permits |
| b | Advantages and disadvantages of each government policy |
| c | Government regulation of competition to promote competition, limit monopoly power, protect consumer interests, and control mergers and takeovers |
| d | Government intervention in the labour market: reasons for a minimum wage; its advantages and disadvantages; diagrams to show the impact of introducing and increasing a minimum wage |

## Policies for externalities (points a and b)

Recall from [externalities](/resources/edexcel-igcse-economics-4ec1-externalities/): goods with external costs are over-produced, and goods with external benefits are under-consumed. Each policy tries to move output towards the level that is best for society.

### Taxation

An indirect tax on a good with external costs raises firms' costs, so supply falls, the price rises and less is bought.

*Invented case.* A tax of 0.40 is placed on each single-use plastic bottle. Before the tax, 50 million bottles a year are sold at 1.00. After the tax, 42 million are sold at 1.30.

```
Fall in sales      = (42 − 50) ÷ 50 × 100 = −16%
Tax revenue        = 0.40 × 42 million = 16.8 million
Price paid by buyers rose 0.30; sellers keep 1.30 − 0.40 = 0.90, so they bear 0.10
```

- **Advantages:** the polluter (and buyer) pays; revenue can fund clean-up or recycling; firms have an incentive to switch to cleaner products.
- **Disadvantages:** the right tax is hard to measure; if demand is price inelastic, sales and pollution fall only a little; taxes on everyday goods can take a larger share of poorer households' income.

### Subsidies

A subsidy is a payment from the government to producers (or consumers) that lowers costs, so supply rises, the price falls and more is bought. It suits goods with external benefits, such as vaccinations, or cleaner alternatives to polluting goods.

*Invented case.* The government pays clinics 8 per flu vaccination, and the price falls from 20 to 14. Uptake rises from 300 000 to 380 000.

```
Cost to government = 8 × 380 000 = 3.04 million
Increase in uptake = (380 000 − 300 000) ÷ 300 000 × 100 = 26.7%
```

- **Advantages:** raises consumption of goods with external benefits; lowers prices; can speed up the switch to cleaner technology.
- **Disadvantages:** costs taxpayers, with an opportunity cost; firms may become dependent on it; part of the subsidy may go into profit rather than lower prices.

### Fines

A fine is a penalty for breaking a rule, such as dumping waste illegally.

- **Advantages:** clear and easy to understand; punishes the polluter; raises some revenue.
- **Disadvantages:** firms must be caught, so monitoring is needed; harm has already happened by the time a fine is paid; a fine that is small relative to the money saved will not deter.

*Invented case.* A firm saves 50 000 a year by not treating waste. Inspectors catch 1 in 4 offenders. A fine of 120 000 has an expected cost of 120 000 ÷ 4 = 30 000, less than the saving, so the firm may keep dumping. The fine would need to exceed 200 000, or inspections increase, to deter it.

### Regulation

Regulation is a set of rules that limits or bans harmful activities: emission limits for vehicles, bans on dangerous chemicals, rules on noise at night.

- **Advantages:** certain outcome for very harmful activities; simple to understand; quick to apply.
- **Disadvantages:** needs inspectors to enforce; compliance raises costs for firms; once firms meet the limit, they have no incentive to cut pollution further.

### Pollution permits

The government sets a total limit (cap) on pollution and issues permits that allow a set amount each. Firms that cut pollution cheaply can sell spare permits to firms that find cutting expensive.

*Invented case.* Three factories each emit 100 tonnes. The government issues 240 one-tonne permits, 80 to each. In year 2 it issues only 210.

```
Year 1 pollution cut = 300 − 240 = 60 tonnes (20%)
Year 2 pollution cut = 300 − 210 = 90 tonnes (30%)
```

- **Advantages:** the total amount of pollution is capped with certainty; pollution is cut where it is cheapest; reducing the number of permits over time tightens the limit; cleaner firms earn revenue.
- **Disadvantages:** setting the right cap is difficult; monitoring each firm's emissions is costly; if too many permits are issued, the price is low and pollution barely falls; large firms may buy permits rather than clean up.

### Choosing between policies

| Situation | Policy often suited | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Extremely dangerous substance | Regulation (ban) | Certain result; no level of use is acceptable |
| Many small sources, such as single-use packaging | Taxation | Easy to collect at the point of sale; changes behaviour widely |
| A good with external benefits, such as vaccination | Subsidy | Lowers the price and raises uptake |
| A few large emitters | Pollution permits | Total cap is certain; cuts happen where cheapest |
| Illegal one-off dumping | Fines | Punishes rule-breaking |

## Regulating competition (point c)

| Aim | What the government does | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Promote competition | Remove barriers to entry, such as unnecessary licences; support new firms | More rivals hold prices down and widen choice |
| Limit monopoly power | Investigate dominant firms; ban price fixing and cartels; cap prices of firms with no rivals | Stop firms using market power to raise prices or cut quality |
| Protect consumer interests | Laws on product safety, honest advertising, accurate labelling and refunds | Consumers often know less than sellers about products |
| Control mergers and takeovers | Investigate mergers that would create a large market share; block them or set conditions | Prevent firms gaining enough power to raise prices |

*Invented case.* Two supermarket chains with 22% and 19% of a national market plan to merge. Combined share = 22 + 19 = **41%**. A regulator might allow the merger only if the new firm sells some stores to rivals, so that competition remains in each town.

**Evaluation:** competition rules cost money to run; investigations can take years; blocking a merger may prevent economies of scale that would have lowered costs.

## The minimum wage (point d)

A minimum wage is the lowest wage per hour that employers are legally allowed to pay.

**Reasons:** to reduce poverty among low-paid workers; to prevent exploitation by employers with strong bargaining power; to make work more rewarding than relying on benefits; to reduce income inequality.

### Diagrams

**Introducing a minimum wage:** on the labour market diagram, draw a horizontal line labelled "minimum wage" **above** the equilibrium wage. Read off the quantity demanded (Qd) and quantity supplied (Qs) at that wage. Employment falls from Qe to Qd, and the gap Qs − Qd is a surplus of labour (unemployment). A minimum wage set **below** equilibrium has no effect.

**Increasing a minimum wage:** draw a second, higher line. Quantity demanded falls further and quantity supplied rises, so the surplus of labour widens.

*Invented schedule: care workers in Region P, wage per hour.*

| Wage | Demanded | Supplied |
|---|---|---|
| 9 | 8 000 | 6 000 |
| 10 | 7 500 | 7 500 |
| 11 | 7 000 | 9 000 |
| 12 | 6 500 | 10 500 |

- Equilibrium: wage 10, employment 7 500.
- Minimum wage of 11: employment 7 000 (500 fewer jobs); surplus 9 000 − 7 000 = **2 000**.
- Raised to 12: employment 6 500; surplus 10 500 − 6 500 = **4 000**.

### Advantages and disadvantages

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Higher pay for low-paid workers who keep their jobs | Unemployment if set well above equilibrium |
| Less poverty and inequality | Higher costs for firms, leading to higher prices or lower profit |
| Higher pay may raise motivation and productivity | Firms may replace workers with machines |
| Workers spend more, supporting demand | Some work may move into the informal economy |

**Evaluation:** the effect depends on how far above equilibrium the wage is set, how easily firms can replace workers, and whether productivity rises with pay.

## Common mistakes

- Using a subsidy for a good with external costs, or a tax to encourage vaccinations.
- Forgetting that fines and regulation need enforcement.
- Drawing a minimum wage below equilibrium and claiming unemployment.
- Saying permits always cut pollution; it depends on the cap.
- Listing aims of competition policy with no example of what the government does.
