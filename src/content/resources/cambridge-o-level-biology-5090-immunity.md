## Antigens and antibodies

An **antigen** is a molecule, usually on the surface of a pathogen, that the body recognises as foreign. **Each pathogen has its own antigens, which have specific shapes.**

An **antibody** is a **protein** made by lymphocytes (a type of white blood cell) in response to an antigen. The shape of an antibody has a binding site that is **complementary** to the shape of one antigen. **Specific antibodies have complementary shapes which fit specific antigens**, like a lock and key. An antibody made against one pathogen therefore does not fit the antigens on a different pathogen.

### What antibodies do

Antibodies bind to antigens on the pathogen. This leads to:

- **direct destruction** of the pathogen, for example by clumping pathogens together or breaking them open, or
- **marking** the pathogen for destruction by **phagocytes**, which engulf and digest it.

## Active immunity

**Active immunity is defence against a pathogen by antibody production in the body.** The person's own lymphocytes make the antibodies, so the person plays an active part.

Active immunity is gained in two ways:

1. **After an infection** by the pathogen: the person catches the disease, and the lymphocytes produce antibodies and memory cells.
2. **By vaccination**: the person is given a harmless form of the pathogen or its antigens.

### What happens in an infection

1. Pathogens enter the body. Their antigens are detected.
2. Lymphocytes that fit the antigen divide and make **specific antibodies**.
3. The antibodies destroy the pathogens, or mark them for the phagocytes to destroy.
4. Some lymphocytes remain as **memory cells**.
5. If the same pathogen enters again, the memory cells respond **faster and make more antibodies**, and so the pathogen is destroyed before the person becomes ill. This is **long-term immunity**.

The first time a person meets a pathogen, it takes several days to produce enough antibodies, and during that time the person is ill. This is why vaccination is useful: it allows the memory cells to form without the illness.

## Vaccination

**Vaccination outlines (the syllabus steps):**

1. **Weakened (or dead) pathogens, or their antigens, are given** to the person, usually by injection. They cannot cause the disease, or cause only a very mild form.
2. The **antigens stimulate an immune response by lymphocytes**, which produce antibodies.
3. **Memory cells are produced**, which give **long-term immunity**.

If the person is later infected by the real pathogen, the memory cells quickly make large numbers of antibodies, so the person does not become ill, or has a mild illness.

### Antibody levels after vaccination

The body responds more strongly and faster the second time. *Illustrative figures:*

| | First exposure | Second exposure |
|---|---|---|
| Time to reach the peak | 10 days | 3 days |
| Peak antibody level (arbitrary units) | 10 | 100 |

1. The second response peaks **7 days sooner** (10 - 3).
2. The peak is **10 times higher** (100 divided by 10).
3. **Explanation**: memory cells recognise the antigen immediately and divide quickly, producing a large number of lymphocytes making the correct antibody.

### The role of vaccination in controlling the spread of disease

If most of a population is vaccinated, the pathogen has few people it can infect, so it spreads poorly, and even unvaccinated people are protected because they are unlikely to meet an infected person. This is often called **herd immunity**. High vaccination rates have greatly reduced diseases such as polio and measles, and have removed smallpox from the world.

*Worked example.* In a town of 10 000 people, 9200 have been vaccinated against a disease. The number who are not vaccinated is 10 000 - 9200 = 800, which is 800 divided by 10 000 x 100 = **8 per cent**. Because only 8 per cent are susceptible, an infected person is unlikely to meet others who can catch the disease, so an outbreak spreads slowly or dies out.

If the proportion vaccinated falls, more people can catch the disease, and outbreaks can happen again.

## Passive immunity

**Passive immunity is a short-term defence against a pathogen by antibodies acquired from another individual.** The person does not make the antibodies. The syllabus limits this to two examples:

- **across the placenta**: antibodies from the mother's blood pass into the baby's blood before birth
- **in breast milk**: antibodies are passed in the milk, especially the first milk (colostrum)

Passive immunity is **short term**. The antibodies are broken down after some weeks or months, and the baby's body has not made them, so **memory cells are not produced**. When the antibodies have gone, the protection has gone.

### Importance of breast-feeding

Breast milk gives the baby antibodies against the pathogens that the mother has met. A newborn baby's immune system is not fully developed, so these antibodies protect the baby during the first months, especially in the gut and airways. Breast-feeding is therefore important for the development of passive immunity in infants, as well as supplying nutrients.

## Active and passive immunity compared

| Feature | Active immunity | Passive immunity |
|---|---|---|
| Who makes the antibodies? | The person's own lymphocytes | Another individual (for example the mother) |
| How gained | Infection or vaccination | Across the placenta or in breast milk |
| Memory cells produced? | **Yes** | **No** |
| Speed of protection | Slow at first (days) | Immediate |
| Length of protection | Long term | Short term |

## HIV and the immune system

HIV infects and destroys **lymphocytes**. Over time, the number of lymphocytes **decreases**, and the body has a **reduced ability to produce antibodies**. This **weakens the immune system**, so the person is more likely to get infections that a healthy immune system would deal with. When the immune system has become very weak, the person is said to have AIDS (see subtopic 12.1).

## Vaccination, infection and antibiotics: avoiding confusion

Students often mix up three ideas that all protect people from disease. A clear table helps.

| | Vaccination | Antibiotic treatment | Passive immunity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Prevent a disease | Cure a bacterial infection | Give short-term protection |
| What is given | Weakened pathogen or antigens | A drug that kills bacteria | Antibodies from another person |
| Works on viruses? | Yes (vaccines exist for many) | No | Yes, if the antibodies are specific |
| Memory cells made? | Yes | No | No |
| Length of effect | Long term | Until the drug is used up | Weeks or months |

### Why some diseases need repeated vaccination

Some pathogens, such as the influenza virus, change their antigens often. Antibodies and memory cells that fit last year's antigens may not fit this year's. New vaccines containing the current antigens must be made. This is a good example of the specific shape of antigens, because a small change in the shape can stop an antibody from fitting.

### Why vaccines are given before infection

Vaccination works by preparing the body in advance. After an infection has begun, it is too late for the first, slow response to prevent illness. A vaccinated person has memory cells ready, so the second-exposure type of response happens at the first real infection.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that antibodies are produced by phagocytes. Lymphocytes make antibodies.
- Saying that antibodies "kill" the pathogen in every case. They may destroy it directly or mark it for phagocytes.
- Saying that a vaccine contains the live, dangerous pathogen. It contains a weakened pathogen or its antigens.
- Saying that vaccination gives passive immunity. It gives active immunity.
- Saying that memory cells are made in passive immunity.
- Writing "antibodies fit any antigen". They are specific.
- Saying that HIV kills all white blood cells. It reduces lymphocytes.
- Describing antibiotics and vaccines as the same thing.

## Command words

- **Describe**: define active immunity and antibodies.
- **State**: give the specific shape fact or the lack of memory cells in passive immunity.
- **Outline**: give the three stages of vaccination.
- **Explain**: give the reason for the role of vaccination and breast-feeding.
