## Questions

**1.** Describe what is meant by:

**(a)** a pathogen **[1]**
**(b)** a transmissible disease. **[1]**

**2.** Which of these is one of the body's barriers to the entry of pathogens?

- A antibodies in the blood
- B phagocytes in the lungs
- C acid in the stomach
- D memory cells in the blood **[1]**

**3.** Malaria is common in a low-lying area with many ponds and flooded fields.

**(a)** Explain why the mosquito is described as a vector. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain how a person catches malaria from someone who already has the disease. **[3]**
**(c)** The local council plans to fill in small ponds, spray a thin film of oil on larger ponds, and give out insecticide-treated bed nets. Explain how each of these three measures reduces malaria, with reference to the mosquito's life cycle. **[4]**

**4.**

**(a)** State the type of pathogen that HIV is. **[1]**
**(b)** Describe two ways in which HIV is transmitted. **[2]**
**(c)** Outline how HIV affects the immune system, and explain why this may lead to AIDS. **[3]**
**(d)** Suggest why a doctor does not prescribe antibiotics to treat HIV infection. **[1]**

**5.** A flood breaks a town's sewage pipes and stops its water treatment. Within a week, many people have cholera.

**(a)** Suggest why the flood led to an outbreak of cholera. **[3]**
**(b)** A patient's blood is tested. Explain why the concentration of ions in the blood is low. **[2]**

**6.** An adult volunteer's reaction time was measured three times in a ruler-drop test before and after drinking alcohol, under supervision.

| Trial | Before alcohol / s | After alcohol / s |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.24 | 0.31 |
| 2 | 0.26 | 0.35 |
| 3 | 0.25 | 0.33 |

**(a)** Calculate the mean reaction time before and after alcohol. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage increase in mean reaction time. **[2]**
**(c)** Use the results to explain why drinking alcohol before driving is dangerous. **[1]**
**(d)** State two other effects of excessive alcohol consumption. **[2]**

**7.** A study recorded the mean birth weight of babies. Mothers who did not smoke had babies with a mean birth weight of 3.40 kg. Mothers who smoked during pregnancy had babies with a mean birth weight of 3.15 kg.

**(a)** Calculate the difference as a percentage of the non-smokers' mean, to one decimal place. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how carbon monoxide in tobacco smoke could reduce birth weight. **[2]**
**(c)** Name the component of tobacco smoke that is addictive and the component that contains carcinogens. **[2]**

**8.** A hospital tested bacteria from infected wounds. In the first year, 18 of 240 samples were resistant to a commonly used antibiotic. After several years of heavy use of that antibiotic, 150 of 200 samples were resistant.

**(a)** Describe what is meant by a drug. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage of resistant samples in each test. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain the increase in resistant samples. **[3]**
**(d)** State how the development of resistant bacteria such as MRSA can be minimised. **[1]**

**9.**

**(a)** Explain why an antibody against the measles pathogen does not protect against the polio pathogen. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe two ways in which antibodies lead to the destruction of a pathogen. **[2]**

**10.** A school has 4000 pupils. 95% have been vaccinated against a transmissible disease.

**(a)** Calculate how many pupils have not been vaccinated. **[1]**
**(b)** Outline how vaccination gives a pupil long-term immunity. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why the unvaccinated pupils are unlikely to catch the disease. **[2]**

**11.**

**(a)** Give the two ways in which a baby can acquire antibodies from its mother. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain the importance of breast-feeding for a newborn baby. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the protection from these antibodies lasts only a few months. **[2]**

**12.** A woman had chickenpox as a child. Years later she has a baby.

**(a)** Compare the immunity the woman has against chickenpox with the immunity her newborn baby has against it. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain the role of vaccination in controlling the spread of transmissible diseases, and suggest why clean water and hygiene are still needed to control cholera. **[4]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** A disease-causing organism [1].
**(b)** A disease in which the pathogen can be passed from one host to another [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "A germ" or "something that makes you ill" is too vague; the word organism is needed in (a).

**2.** **C** [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* A, B and D act after a pathogen has entered, so none of them is a barrier.

**3. (a)** It carries the pathogen from one person (host) to another [1].
**(b)** A female mosquito bites the infected person and takes in blood containing the parasite [1]. The parasite develops in the mosquito [1]. When the mosquito bites another person, the parasite passes into that person's blood [1].
**(c)** Filling in ponds removes the still water where eggs are laid and larvae develop [1]. Oil stops larvae and pupae reaching air at the surface, so they die [1]. Bed nets stop adult mosquitoes biting people at night [1], and the insecticide kills adults that land on them [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* In (c) each measure must be tied to the life-cycle stage it targets; restating the measures gains nothing.

**4. (a)** A virus [1].
**(b)** Any two [2]: unprotected sexual intercourse; sharing needles; transfusion of infected blood; mother to baby across the placenta, at birth or in breast milk.
**(c)** HIV decreases the number of lymphocytes [1], so the ability to produce antibodies falls [1]. The immune system is weakened, so other pathogens cause infections the body cannot fight, which is AIDS [1].
**(d)** Antibiotics do not affect viruses [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* "Through blood" alone is too vague for (b); name the situation, such as shared needles or a transfusion.

**5. (a)** Faeces containing cholera bacteria entered the water from the broken pipes [1]. The water was not treated, so the bacteria were not killed [1]. People drank the water or used it to prepare food [1].
**(b)** The toxin causes chloride ions to be secreted into the small intestine [1], and ions are lost from the body in the diarrhoea [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* In (a), "dirty water" earns nothing on its own; say how the bacteria got into the water and why they survived.

**6. (a)** Before: (0.24 + 0.26 + 0.25) ÷ 3 = **0.25 s** [1]. After: (0.31 + 0.35 + 0.33) ÷ 3 = **0.33 s** [1].
**(b)** (0.33 − 0.25) ÷ 0.25 × 100 [1] = **32%** [1].
**(c)** The reaction time is longer, so a driver takes longer to brake or steer in an emergency [1].
**(d)** Any two [2]: acts as a depressant; reduced self-control; damage to the liver; social implications such as violence or family breakdown. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Allow error carried forward in (b) from wrong means in (a), but units (s) are expected on the means.

**7. (a)** 3.40 − 3.15 = 0.25 kg [1]; 0.25 ÷ 3.40 × 100 = **7.4%** [1].
**(b)** Carbon monoxide combines with haemoglobin, so the mother's blood carries less oxygen [1]. Less oxygen reaches the fetus across the placenta, so it grows less [1].
**(c)** Addictive: nicotine [1]. Carcinogens: tar [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* "Carbon monoxide is poisonous" is not enough in (b); the mark needs haemoglobin and the drop in oxygen carried.

**8. (a)** Any substance taken into the body that modifies or affects chemical reactions in the body [1].
**(b)** 18 ÷ 240 × 100 = **7.5%** [1]; 150 ÷ 200 × 100 = **75%** [1].
**(c)** Some bacteria were already resistant [1]. The antibiotic killed the non-resistant bacteria, but the resistant ones survived [1]. The survivors reproduced, so resistant bacteria became more common [1].
**(d)** Use antibiotics only when essential [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers saying the bacteria "got used to" or "became immune to" the antibiotic do not gain the survival marks in (c).

**9. (a)** Each pathogen has its own antigens with a specific shape [1]. The antibody has a complementary shape that fits only measles antigens, so it cannot bind to polio antigens [1].
**(b)** Antibodies bind and directly destroy the pathogen [1]; or they mark it for destruction by phagocytes [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Same shape" instead of "complementary shape" usually loses the second mark in (a).

**10. (a)** 4000 × 0.05 = **200 pupils** [1].
**(b)** Weakened pathogens or their antigens are given [1]. The antigens stimulate lymphocytes to produce antibodies [1]. Memory cells are produced, giving long-term immunity [1].
**(c)** Most pupils are immune, so few become infected or pass the pathogen on [1]. The pathogen has very few hosts to spread through, so it is unlikely to reach an unvaccinated pupil [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b), memory cells are the link to "long-term"; an answer that stops at antibodies explains only the first response.

**11. (a)** Across the placenta [1]; in breast milk [1].
**(b)** A newborn's own immune system is not yet producing many antibodies [1]. Antibodies in breast milk protect it from pathogens the mother has met, while it is most vulnerable [1].
**(c)** This is passive immunity, so no memory cells are produced [1]. The antibodies are broken down and the baby cannot make more of them [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Only the two routes in the syllabus are credited in (a); vaccination of the baby is active, not passive.

**12. (a)** The woman has active immunity; the baby has passive immunity [1]. The woman's own lymphocytes made her antibodies; the baby's antibodies came from her, across the placenta or in milk [1]. The woman has memory cells; the baby has none [1]. Her immunity is long-term; the baby's is short-term [1].
**(b)** Vaccinated people do not become ill and do not pass the pathogen on [1]. If enough of the population is vaccinated, the pathogen cannot spread, which is how a disease can be controlled [1]. Cholera is spread by bacteria in contaminated water [1], so a clean water supply, sewage treatment and hand washing stop the bacteria reaching people whether or not they are immune [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* In a "compare" question each mark needs both sides stated; "the woman has memory cells" alone does not complete the comparison.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming the mosquito as the cause of malaria rather than the vector.
- Giving control measures without linking them to a stage of the mosquito life cycle.
- Vague transmission routes such as "blood" or "dirty water".
- Leaving chloride ions or osmosis out of a cholera explanation.
- Missing units on means, or wrong rounding.
- Saying bacteria "become immune" instead of describing survival of resistant bacteria.
- Writing that antibiotics treat viral diseases such as HIV.
- Leaving memory cells out of an explanation of long-term immunity.
- Giving memory cells to passive immunity.
- One-sided answers to "compare" questions.
