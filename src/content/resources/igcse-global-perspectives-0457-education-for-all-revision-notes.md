These notes condense the [Education for all study guide](/resources/igcse-global-perspectives-0457-education-for-all/). Read that first if anything here is unfamiliar.

They cover the topic **Education for all** from the Cambridge IGCSE Global Perspectives (0457) syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027, Version 2 (published April 2025): the topic list in section 3, the assessment objectives in section 2 and the component details in section 4. The topic list is for all components, so this topic can appear in Component 1 Written Exam or be chosen for Component 2 Individual Report or Component 3 Team Project. **Knowledge of the topic is not assessed**; these notes show how to use it to practise the assessed skills. Test yourself afterwards with the [practice questions](/resources/igcse-global-perspectives-0457-education-for-all-practice/), and track progress on the [0457 checklist](/checklists/cambridge/igcse/global-perspectives/) and the [Global Perspectives hub](/boards/cambridge/igcse/global-perspectives/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning (syllabus wording where printed) |
|---|---|
| Issue | "An important subject or problem for discussion" |
| Perspective | "A viewpoint on an issue based on evidence and reasoning" |
| Research | "The systematic study of a global issue to find evidence and develop understanding" |
| Evidence | "Information about a global issue that helps to develop understanding or prove that something is true or false" |
| Argument | "A series of statements containing reasons and evidence which support a claim about a global issue" |
| Fact | Can be checked as true or false |
| Opinion | A personal view that cannot be proved |
| Claim | A statement put forward as true that needs support |
| Value | A belief about what matters or what is right ("every child deserves school") |
| Prediction | A statement about what will happen |
| Generalisation | A broad statement drawn from limited cases |
| Bias | A one-sided presentation of an issue |
| Vested interest | The source gains if its view is accepted |
| Course of action | A proposed step to change, improve or resolve an issue |

The last eight before "course of action" are the terms the syllabus says candidates "will benefit from an understanding of" for Component 1.

## The skills, mapped to this topic

| Assessment objective (syllabus name) | What it looks like with Education for all |
|---|---|
| AO1 Research, analysis and evaluation | Find education issues; trace causes and consequences; test the evidence in sources; judge courses of action |
| AO2 Reflection | Explain why a parent, teacher, minister or charity sees an issue differently, with empathy; justify your own view with evidence |
| AO3 Communication and collaboration | Present arguments clearly with citations; contribute to a Team Project on a local education issue |

The syllabus weights AO1 at 100% of Component 1. AO2 and AO3 carry marks only in Components 2 and 3.

## Real-world reference points at a glance

| What | When | What it says or did | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Universal Declaration of Human Rights, Article 26 | 1948 | Everyone has the right to education; education free at least in the elementary and fundamental stages; elementary education compulsory | Shows an agreed principle, not whether it is met in practice |
| World Conference on Education for All, Jomtien, Thailand | 1990 | International meeting that launched the Education for All movement | Useful background; a conference is a commitment, not an outcome |
| Sustainable Development Goal 4 | 2015 | "Ensure inclusive and equitable quality education and promote lifelong learning opportunities for all" | Adds "quality", so progress must be judged by learning, not just attendance |
| Pakistan Constitution, Article 25-A | 2010 (Eighteenth Amendment) | The state must provide free and compulsory education to all children aged five to sixteen | A national legal right; you need data to judge how far it is delivered |
| Malala Yousafzai and Kailash Satyarthi | 2014 | Shared the Nobel Peace Prize, recognised for work on children's rights including the right to education | Personal stories bring attention and testimony, but are not statistical evidence |

Check up-to-date figures, such as numbers of children out of school, from the organisation that collects them, and note the year. Old data weakens an argument about the present.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Access vs quality.** Being enrolled in school is not the same as learning. A source that shows more children in school does not prove better education.
- **Enrolment vs attendance vs completion.** Enrolled means registered; attending means present; completing means finishing a stage. A rise in one does not prove a rise in the others.
- **Percent vs percentage points.** A rise from 60% to 75% is 15 percentage points, but a 25% relative increase.
- **Promise vs outcome.** A law or goal shows intent. Outcomes need data.
- **Perspective vs opinion.** A perspective has evidence and reasoning behind it; an opinion may not.
- **Prediction vs generalisation.** A prediction is about the future ("will"); a generalisation stretches a few cases to all ("all", "every", "always").
- **Cause vs consequence.** Cost is a cause of children leaving school; fewer job choices is a consequence. Keep them in separate paragraphs and link them.
- **Local vs global issue.** The Team Project needs a local issue. The Individual Report needs a global issue with local and/or national and global perspectives.

## Mini essay plan: an "assess actions" extended response

Component 1 includes an extended response in which candidates "assess actions in response to the global issue and explain their judgements with reasons and evidence". A typical task on this topic might ask you to assess two actions a government could take to help more children finish secondary school, such as **free school transport** and **scholarships for pupils from low-income families**.

1. **Opening judgement (2–3 sentences).** State which action you think is more effective and the main reason. Name the cause each action targets.
2. **Action 1: free transport.** Effect: removes the distance and travel-cost barrier. Evidence: quote the source data on distance or travel cost. Limitation: does not help if the school is full or if families need the child's income. Sustainability: running costs every year.
3. **Action 2: scholarships.** Effect: reduces cost for the families who most need help. Evidence: from the sources. Limitation: needs fair selection; some children who need it may be missed; does not create school places.
4. **Compare using criteria.** Which reaches more children? Which tackles the cause the sources show is biggest? Which is cheaper per child? Which lasts?
5. **Weigh the evidence.** Is it from a source with a vested interest? Is the sample large and recent?
6. **Final judgement.** Answer the question directly. A judgement that one action works best combined with another is fine if you explain why.

## Quick self-test

1. What does the syllabus say about knowledge of content in 0457?
2. Give the syllabus definition of "perspective".
3. Is "Every child deserves to go to school" a fact, a value or a prediction?
4. A source says attendance rose from 50% to 60%. State the rise in percentage points and in relative terms.
5. Why is a law making school free weak evidence that all children attend?
6. Name two possible causes of children leaving school early.
7. Name one consequence of children leaving school early at a local level.
8. A charity running a school-meals scheme reports that the scheme works. What should you note about the source?
9. "All parents in the north want private schools," based on interviews with five parents. Which term describes this?
10. Why is "What is SDG 4?" a weak Individual Report question?
11. What must a Team Project issue be, according to the syllabus?
12. Which of AO1, AO2 and AO3 carry no marks in Component 1?

### Answers

1. It states that knowledge of content is not assessed; the topics are a context for developing skills.
2. "A viewpoint on an issue based on evidence and reasoning."
3. A value: it states what the speaker believes is right.
4. 10 percentage points; a 20% relative increase (10 ÷ 50).
5. It shows what the government intends, not what happens. Other costs, distance or a lack of places may still keep children out. You need attendance data.
6. Any two: cost (fees, uniforms, transport, lost family income); distance or unsafe journeys; too few schools or teachers; conflict or disaster; social expectations; buildings or teaching not suited to children with disabilities.
7. For example, fewer trained local people such as teachers or health workers.
8. It may have a vested interest in showing success, for example to keep its funding; check whether its evidence is independent.
9. A generalisation (from a very small sample).
10. It asks for description, not judgement, and does not divide opinion.
11. A local issue, chosen by the team from a topic on the topic list.
12. Both AO2 and AO3 carry 0% of Component 1; only AO1 is assessed there.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Bringing in remembered facts about education instead of using the exam sources, which the syllabus says are the basis of the questions.
- Calling a source "biased" without naming the interest it serves or quoting its language.
- Treating a percentage-point rise as a percentage rise, or the other way round.
- Accepting enrolment figures as proof that children are learning.
- Describing a perspective without explaining why that person or group holds it.
- Listing causes and consequences without linking them into a chain of reasoning.
- Assessing an action without saying which cause it targets, who pays, or whether it lasts.
- Ending an extended answer with a summary instead of a clear judgement.
- In the Individual Report, choosing a question that does not divide opinion or lacks a global dimension.
- In the Team Project, choosing an action whose success the team never planned to measure.
