## Section A

**1.** Define the decay constant of a radioactive isotope. **[1]**

**2.** Radioactive decay is described as random and spontaneous. Explain what is meant by each term. **[2]**

**3.** An isotope has a half-life of 14.3 days. Calculate its decay constant in s⁻¹. **[2]**

**4.** A sample of the isotope in Question 3 has an activity of 2.4 × 10⁶ Bq. Calculate the number of undecayed nuclei in the sample. **[1]**

**5.** An isotope has a half-life of 6.0 hours. Calculate the fraction of the original nuclei that remain undecayed after 20 hours. **[2]**

**6.** A student records the count from a long-lived source in ten consecutive 30 s intervals. The readings range from 141 to 178. Explain how these readings provide evidence for the random nature of radioactive decay. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** The corrected count rate C from a source is measured at intervals.

| t / min | 0 | 20 | 40 | 60 | 80 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| C / min⁻¹ | 1200 | 756 | 476 | 300 | 189 |

**(a)** Explain why a graph of ln C against t should be a straight line. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate ln C for t = 0 and t = 80 min, and use these values to determine the decay constant. **[2]**

**(c)** Determine the half-life of the source. **[1]**

**8.** A sample contains 2.0 mg of an isotope of molar mass 90 g mol⁻¹. The half-life of the isotope is 28.8 years.

**(a)** Calculate the number of nuclei in the sample. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the decay constant in s⁻¹. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the activity of the sample. **[1]**

**(d)** Explain why the activity of this sample will be almost the same one week later. **[1]**

**9.** A hospital needs a tracer with an activity of 400 MBq at the time of injection. The tracer has a half-life of 6.0 hours and is prepared 3.0 hours before injection. Calculate the activity it must have when prepared. **[3]**

**10.** A sample initially contains 2.0 × 10¹² undecayed nuclei of an isotope with half-life 45 minutes.

**(a)** Calculate the decay constant in s⁻¹. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the initial activity. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the number of nuclei that decay in the first hour. **[2]**

**11.** **(a)** Sketch a graph showing how the number N of undecayed nuclei in a sample varies with time t, starting from N₀. Mark at least two half-lives on your time axis. **[3]**

**(b)** On the same axes, sketch the curve for a second isotope with the same N₀ but half the half-life. **[1]**

**12.** A student suggests that heating a radioactive source in an oven would increase its activity, "because the nuclei would have more energy". Explain why the student is wrong. **[2]**

**13.** Two samples, X and Y, start with the same number of undecayed nuclei. X has a half-life of 2.0 h and Y has a half-life of 6.0 h.

**(a)** State the ratio (initial activity of X) ÷ (initial activity of Y). **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the time at which the two samples have equal activity. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** The probability that an individual nucleus will decay per unit time [1].

**2.** Random: it is impossible to predict when a particular nucleus will decay, or which nucleus will decay next [1]. Spontaneous: the decay is not affected by external factors such as temperature or pressure [1].

**3.** t½ = 14.3 × 24 × 3600 = 1.24 × 10⁶ s [1]; λ = 0.693 ÷ 1.24 × 10⁶ = 5.6 × 10⁻⁷ s⁻¹ [1].

**4.** N = A/λ = 2.4 × 10⁶ ÷ 5.6 × 10⁻⁷ = 4.3 × 10¹² [1].

**5.** λ = 0.693 ÷ 6.0 = 0.1155 h⁻¹ [1]; N/N₀ = e^(−0.1155 × 20) = 0.099 [1].

**6.** The counts in equal time intervals vary unpredictably about a mean value [1]; the activity of a long-lived source is effectively constant during the experiment, so the variation must come from the decay process being random [1].

**7. (a)** C = C₀e^(−λt), so ln C = ln C₀ − λt, which has the form y = c + mx [1].

**(b)** ln 1200 = 7.090 and ln 189 = 5.242 [1]; λ = −gradient = (7.090 − 5.242) ÷ 80 = 0.0231 min⁻¹ [1].

**(c)** t½ = 0.693 ÷ 0.0231 = 30 min [1].

**8. (a)** N = (2.0 × 10⁻³ ÷ 90) × 6.02 × 10²³ = 1.3 × 10¹⁹ [1].

**(b)** t½ = 28.8 × 3.15 × 10⁷ = 9.07 × 10⁸ s; λ = 0.693 ÷ 9.07 × 10⁸ = 7.6 × 10⁻¹⁰ s⁻¹ [1].

**(c)** A = λN = 7.6 × 10⁻¹⁰ × 1.34 × 10¹⁹ = 1.0 × 10¹⁰ Bq [1].

**(d)** One week is a tiny fraction of the half-life of 28.8 years, so the number of undecayed nuclei, and hence A = λN, hardly changes [1].

**9.** λ = 0.693 ÷ 6.0 = 0.1155 h⁻¹ [1]; 400 = A₀e^(−0.1155 × 3.0) [1]; A₀ = 400 × e^(0.3465) = 570 MBq (566 MBq) [1].

**10. (a)** λ = 0.693 ÷ (45 × 60) = 2.57 × 10⁻⁴ s⁻¹ [1].

**(b)** A₀ = λN₀ = 2.57 × 10⁻⁴ × 2.0 × 10¹² = 5.1 × 10⁸ Bq [1].

**(c)** N after 3600 s = 2.0 × 10¹² × e^(−2.57 × 10⁻⁴ × 3600) = 7.9 × 10¹¹ [1]; number decayed = 2.0 × 10¹² − 7.9 × 10¹¹ = 1.2 × 10¹² [1].

**11. (a)** Curve starting at N₀ and decreasing with decreasing gradient [1]; N₀/2 at t½ and N₀/4 at 2t½, at equal time intervals [1]; curve approaches zero without reaching it [1].

**(b)** A curve from the same N₀ that falls more steeply, reaching N₀/2 at half the original half-life, and lying below the first curve at all later times [1].

**12.** Radioactive decay is spontaneous: it is not affected by external conditions such as temperature [1]. Heating changes the energy of the atoms and molecules (their kinetic energy and electrons), not the nuclei, so the decay constant and the activity are unchanged [1].

**13. (a)** A = λN and λ ∝ 1/t½, so the ratio is 6.0 ÷ 2.0 = 3 [1].

**(b)** λ_Y = 0.693 ÷ 6.0 = 0.1155 h⁻¹ and λ_X = 3λ_Y [1]. Equal activity: 3λ_Y N₀e^(−3λ_Y t) = λ_Y N₀e^(−λ_Y t), so e^(2λ_Y t) = 3 [1]; t = ln 3 ÷ (2 × 0.1155) = 4.8 h [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Definitions of half-life or decay constant that miss "undecayed nuclei" or "probability per unit time".
- Mixing time units: using a half-life in days or years with activity in becquerels.
- Forgetting to subtract background before calculating ratios or plotting.
- Using log₁₀ where ln is needed, or making a sign error with the gradient.
- Sketching decay curves that reach zero, or with unequal half-life intervals.
- Explaining randomness with "it is unpredictable" without reference to which nucleus decays or when.
