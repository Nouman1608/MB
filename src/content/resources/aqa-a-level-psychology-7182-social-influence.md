Version 1.4 names only two types of conformity. Identification may appear in older textbooks but is not specification content, so use internalisation and compliance in answers.

## Conformity

**Compliance** is going along with the group in public while privately disagreeing; it stops when the group is absent. **Internalisation** is accepting the group's view as your own, publicly and privately, so it lasts without the group.

Deutsch and Gerard (1955) proposed two explanations:

- **Normative social influence (NSI)**: conforming to be liked and accepted, and to avoid rejection. It is emotional rather than cognitive and usually leads to compliance. It is most likely with strangers when you fear looking foolish, or with people whose approval you value.
- **Informational social influence (ISI)**: conforming because you believe others are more likely to be right. It is cognitive and usually leads to internalisation. It is most likely when the situation is ambiguous or new, or others are seen as experts.

**Evaluation.** Asch's post-study interviews support both: some participants said they doubted their own judgement (ISI), while others said they knew the answer was wrong but did not want to stand out (NSI). However, the two processes often operate together, so in real situations it is hard to say which one caused a particular act of conformity.

## Asch's variables

In Asch's line-judgement studies, a naïve participant judged which of three comparison lines matched a standard line, answering after confederates who gave the same wrong answer on critical trials. The variations isolated three variables.

| Variable | Finding | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Group size | With one confederate, conformity was very low; with three it rose to about 31.8%; adding more made little further difference | A majority of three is enough to create strong normative pressure |
| Unanimity | One dissenting confederate who gave the right answer cut conformity to about 5.5%; a dissenter who gave a different wrong answer also reduced it | Breaking the group's unanimity frees the participant to act independently |
| Task difficulty | Making the lines more similar in length increased conformity | When the answer is unclear, others seem a better guide: ISI |

**Evaluation of Asch.**
- Strength: high control of variables, so cause and effect between each variable and conformity can be established.
- Limitation: the task was artificial and trivial, so it may not reflect conformity where real consequences follow, which lowers external validity.
- Limitation: Perrin and Spencer (1980) repeated the study with UK engineering students and found far less conformity, suggesting the original findings reflected 1950s American culture ("a child of its time").
- Limitation: participants were deceived about the confederates and may have felt real stress, raising ethical issues.

## Obedience

### Situational explanations

**Agentic state.** Milgram proposed that people obey when they see themselves as an agent carrying out another person's wishes rather than as autonomous individuals responsible for their own actions. The move from autonomy to agency is the **agentic shift**. Participants who showed visible stress yet continued were, on this view, experiencing **moral strain** but felt unable to stop. **Binding factors**, such as not wanting to seem rude or blaming the victim, kept them in the agentic state.

**Legitimacy of authority.** Societies are organised hierarchically, and people accept that those higher up have the right to give orders. We learn this from childhood with parents and teachers. When authority is used destructively, the same acceptance can lead to harm.

### Milgram's situational variables

In Milgram's baseline study (1963), 65% of 40 male participants continued to the maximum 450 V, and all went to at least 300 V.

| Variation | Obedience to 450 V |
|---|---|
| Learner in the same room (proximity) | 40% |
| Participant forced the learner's hand onto a shock plate (touch proximity) | 30% |
| Experimenter gave orders by telephone | 20.5% |
| Moved from Yale University to a run-down office in Bridgeport (location) | 47.5% |
| Experimenter replaced by an "ordinary member of the public" (no lab coat, the uniform condition) | 20% |

Outside Milgram's work, **Bickman (1974)** found people in a New York street were more likely to obey a stranger dressed as a security guard than one in a milkman's uniform or ordinary clothes.

**Evaluation.**
- Support: Hofling et al. (1966) found 21 of 22 nurses were prepared to give a dangerous drug dose on a telephoned order from an unknown doctor, suggesting obedience to legitimate authority in a real setting.
- Challenge: Rank and Jacobson (1977) found most nurses refused a similar order when they knew the drug and could consult colleagues, so situational detail matters.
- Validity: Orne and Holland (1968) argued participants may not have believed the shocks were real. Sheridan and King (1972), using real shocks to a puppy, still found high obedience, which supports Milgram.
- Ethics: deception and distress, with the right to withdraw made difficult by the prods.

## The dispositional explanation

**Adorno et al. (1950)** proposed that obedience reflects an **Authoritarian Personality**, developed through a harsh, strict upbringing with conditional love. The child's hostility to the parents is displaced onto weaker groups, while deference to authority is learned. The **F-scale** measures traits such as respect for authority, rigid moral views and contempt for those of lower status.

**Evaluation.** Elms and Milgram (1966) found that fully obedient participants in Milgram's studies scored higher on the F-scale than defiant ones, which supports a link. However, the link is correlational: a third factor, such as level of education, could explain both. The F-scale also measures right-wing attitudes only, so it cannot account for obedience to authorities of other political views.

## Resistance to social influence

**Social support.** The presence of others who resist shows that resistance is possible and shares the risk. In Asch's studies a dissenter reduced conformity; in Milgram's study, when two confederate "teachers" refused to continue, obedience fell to 10%.

**Locus of control** (Rotter, 1966). People with an **internal** locus believe outcomes depend on their own actions; those with an **external** locus believe outcomes depend on luck or powerful others. Internals are more likely to resist pressure because they take personal responsibility and rely less on others' approval. Holland (1967) found 37% of internals refused to continue to the highest shock level in a Milgram-type procedure, compared with 23% of externals.

## Minority influence

A minority can change a majority's view, usually through internalisation.

- **Consistency**: the minority keeps the same view over time and among its members. Moscovici et al. (1969) found that a consistent minority who called blue slides "green" on every trial produced green answers on 8.42% of trials, against 1.25% for an inconsistent minority.
- **Commitment**: personal sacrifice signals that the minority believes its view (the augmentation principle), so others pay attention.
- **Flexibility**: Nemeth argued that rigid repetition can seem dogmatic; a minority that compromises appropriately while keeping its core position is more persuasive.

**Evaluation.** Moscovici's task, naming slide colours, is artificial, and the effect was small in absolute terms. Real minorities, such as campaigners, face hostility and power differences that the laboratory cannot reproduce.

## Applied model answer

*Original scenario.* At a school council meeting, eleven members vote to keep the canteen menu. Priya, a new member, privately thinks the menu should change but votes with the others. Later, Sam argues every week, with evidence, for a healthier menu, and slowly others agree. *Using your knowledge of social influence, explain Priya's behaviour and Sam's success.*

> Priya shows compliance: she agrees publicly but not privately. This is likely to be normative social influence, because she is new and wants to be accepted by a unanimous group of eleven, larger than the three people Asch found was enough for strong pressure. Sam is a minority who persuades others by being consistent, arguing the same position every week. Using evidence and continuing to argue despite opposition shows commitment, so others think his view through more deeply, which can lead them to internalise it.

Each sentence links a concept to a detail of the scenario, which is what application marks reward.

## Common mistakes

- Using identification, which is not named in Version 1.4, in place of internalisation or compliance.
- Mixing up NSI (to be liked) and ISI (to be right).
- Describing Milgram's procedure without the variation findings when a question asks about situational variables.
- Treating the Authoritarian Personality as situational; it is dispositional.
- Saying an external locus of control helps people resist; the reverse is true.
- Listing consistency, commitment and flexibility without explaining why each persuades.

## Exam technique

- **Outline** and **describe**: accurate content, no evaluation needed.
- **Explain**: give the reason or mechanism, often linking a variable to an outcome.
- **Apply** questions ("Using your knowledge of..."): quote or refer to the scenario in every point.
- **Evaluate** and **discuss**: make a point, support it with evidence, then say what it means for the theory, and finish with a judgement.
