## Section A: knowledge and concepts

**1.** Explain why the silence of a victim can be a deliberate method in crime writing. **[2]**

**2.** Explain how the form of the dramatic monologue affects the presentation of victims in
Browning's poems. **[3]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by the "body as evidence" and why it can reduce a reader's pity. **[2]**

**4.** For one set text, identify a character who is both a wrongdoer and a victim and explain how
this complicates the reader's response. **[4]**

**5.** The specification refers to crime stories creating "repugnance". Explain how a writer can
create repugnance through the presentation of suffering. **[2]**

## Section B: an original extract and essay skills

**Extract (original).** The narrator is a police officer called to a house in a small town.

> The kitchen was clean. That was the first thing, and I have never forgotten it. Someone had wiped
> the table and folded the cloth over the tap. On the fridge, held by a magnet shaped like a lemon,
> was a list in blue biro: *bread, stamps, ring Mum*. Mrs Allen was in the next room. My sergeant
> went in first and came out again without speaking, and I understood that I was not to look, and I
> looked anyway. Afterwards, in the car, he asked me what I had noticed, and I told him about the
> list, and he said that was not evidence, and I said I knew.

**6.** Analyse how the writer presents the victim without describing her body. **[6]**

**7.** Explain how the narrator's voice shapes the reader's response to the crime. **[4]**

**8.** Explain the significance of the final exchange between the sergeant and the narrator. **[3]**

**9.** A student wants to link this extract to the way victims are reduced to evidence in Poe's
Dupin stories. Explain one similarity and one difference. **[2]**

**10.** Plan an essay for the question: "In crime writing, the victim matters less than the
criminal." Use one set text and list five elements of your plan. **[5]**

**11.** Read this student paragraph on *Macbeth*: "Lots of people die in *Macbeth*. Duncan, Banquo
and Lady Macduff are killed. This is very sad and shows Macbeth is evil." Explain two weaknesses and
write an improved topic sentence. **[4]**

**12.** For one set text, name three moments you would learn for an essay on victims and suffering,
from different parts of the text, and state what each would show. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** The victim's silence focuses attention on the criminal or detective [1], and the gap invites
the reader to notice who is not heard, so the text can comment on power or indifference [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Only the speaker, often the criminal, has a voice [1], so the victim's experience must be
inferred from what the speaker reveals or hides [1]. The reader becomes a judge reading against the
speaker, as with the Duke in "My Last Duchess" [1]. **[3]**

**3.** The victim's injuries or remains become clues to be read [1], so the reader's attention shifts
from the person's suffering to the puzzle, which can turn pity into curiosity [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Character identified with accurate reference [1]; how they are a wrongdoer [1] and a victim
[1]; effect on response explained [1]. For example: Nancy in *Oliver Twist* [1] helps kidnap Oliver
back to Fagin [1] but is murdered by Sikes after trying to save him [1], so the reader's judgement
of her criminal past gives way to pity [1]. Credit Mrs Ferrars, Lady Macbeth, or the Mariner. **[4]**

**5.** By describing suffering in physical or sensory detail, or by showing a criminal's coldness
towards it [1], so the reader recoils from the act and from the person who commits it [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Any six, [1] each:

- The opening focus on the clean kitchen displaces attention from the body to the scene.
- "That was the first thing, and I have never forgotten it" shows lasting shock through an
  unexpected detail.
- The wiped table and folded cloth suggest either the victim's ordinary care or a killer's
  unsettling tidiness.
- The shopping list ("bread, stamps, ring Mum") gives the victim an ordinary life and plans that will
  not happen.
- The lemon-shaped magnet is a homely detail that makes the loss domestic and real.
- "Mrs Allen was in the next room" is a flat, restrained statement that withholds the horror.
- The sergeant's silent return conveys what is in the room without description.
- "I looked anyway" admits what was seen while still refusing to describe it, so the reader must
  imagine.

**7.** Any four, [1] each: the retrospective first person ("I have never forgotten it") shows the
crime's long effect on a witness; the plain, unemotional sentences create restraint that makes the
emotion stronger; the narrator notices human details rather than clues, guiding the reader to see a
person, not a case; the admission of disobedience ("I looked anyway") makes the narrator honest and
vulnerable; the voice positions the reader as another witness. **[4]**

**8.** Any three, [1] each: the sergeant's question tests the narrator as a detective; his answer
that the list "was not evidence" states the investigator's view of victims as material for
solving; the narrator's "I said I knew" accepts the rule but keeps the human detail as what matters;
the exchange states the tension between justice and compassion in crime writing. **[3]**

**9.** Similarity [1]: in both, an investigator approaches the scene as a source of evidence. Difference
[1]: Dupin turns injuries into clues for reasoning, while this narrator keeps the human detail and
rejects the purely evidential view. **[2]**

**10.** Any five, [1] each: a one-sentence judgement on the view; a moment where the victim is
silenced or reduced to evidence (support); a moment where suffering is given weight (challenge); the
method that controls attention, such as point of view or onstage presentation; the aftermath of the
crime; a relevant context linked to a specific choice; a counter-reading that silence is a deliberate
gap; a conclusion on the reader's response. **[5]**

**11.** Weakness one [1]: it lists victims without analysing how the play presents them. Weakness two
[1]: "very sad" and "evil" are vague responses and moral labels, not arguments about significance.
Improved topic sentence [2] for an argued, precise claim, [1] for a partial improvement. Example:
"The shift from Duncan's offstage murder to the killing of Macduff's son in front of the audience
marks Macbeth's crimes changing from ambition to cruelty, and forces the audience to witness the
suffering it earlier only imagined." **[4]**

**12.** Three moments from different parts of the text, each with a stated purpose, [1] each. For
example, on Stevenson: the trampled child at the start of *Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde* (Hyde's cruelty
first seen through a witness); the murder of Sir Danvers Carew (violence against respectability);
Fettes recognising the body in "The Body Snatcher" (the victim as a person, not a specimen). **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Listing victims instead of analysing how they are presented.
- Writing only about criminals and detectives.
- Moral labels in place of analysis of methods and response.
- Forgetting the aftermath of crime and those who suffer beyond the victim.
