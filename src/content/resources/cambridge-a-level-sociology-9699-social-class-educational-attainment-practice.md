These questions cover section 6.2, *Social class and educational attainment*, of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Sociology (9699) syllabus for 2024–2026, assessed on Paper 3 – Education. All schools, people, programmes and data in the items are fictional and invented for practice.

## Section A – Short-answer and explanation questions

**1.** What is meant by "habitus"? **[2]**

**2.** Identify two weaknesses of using free school meal eligibility as a measure of social class. **[2]**

**3.** Distinguish between cultural deprivation and cultural capital explanations of class differences in attainment. **[4]**

**4.** Explain how opportunity cost may affect working-class pupils' decisions about staying in education. **[4]**

**5.** Read the item. The data are invented.

> In the fictional district of Brennock, a pilot scheme paid families on low incomes a weekly allowance if their 16-year-olds stayed in full-time education. Before the scheme, 180 of 400 eligible young people stayed on. In the first year of the scheme, 252 of 400 stayed on.

**(a)** Calculate the staying-on rate before and during the scheme, and the change in percentage points. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain one reason the scheme might have raised staying-on rates and one limitation of judging it from these figures. **[4]**

**6.** Explain two ways in which parents' cultural capital may help their children's attainment, other than through speech. **[6]**

**7.** Read the item.

> At Fenwick High, a fictional school, Year 8 pupils are placed in sets after one test. In the lowest set, lessons focus on worksheets and copying. Several boys in the set call the top-set pupils "boffins" and spend lessons joking. One girl in the same set quietly completes every worksheet but says she "just wants to get through it".

Using the item and sociological material, explain two ways in which in-school factors may affect attainment. **[6]**

**8.** Explain two criticisms of compensatory education programmes. **[6]**

## Section B – Arguments against a view and essays

**9.** "Working-class pupils under-achieve because their parents do not value education."

Using sociological material, give two arguments against this view. **[8]**

**10.** "Labelling by teachers is the main reason for social class differences in attainment."

Using sociological material, give two arguments against this view. **[8]**

**11.** Evaluate the view that factors inside the school are more important than factors outside it in explaining social class differences in attainment. **[26]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that compensatory education can significantly reduce social class differences in attainment. **[26]**

## Answers

**1.** A set of dispositions, tastes and ways of acting learned in early life (Bourdieu) [1], which make some pupils feel at ease in school and others feel that education is not for them [1]. **[2]**

**2.** It divides pupils into two groups only, hiding differences within the non-eligible majority [1]; eligibility rules change over time and between places, and some eligible families do not claim [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Cultural deprivation explanations say some working-class homes lack the attitudes, values or language schools need [1], so the cause lies in the family, which critics say blames the victim [1]. Cultural capital explanations say schools reward middle-class culture as if it were ability [1], so the cause lies in the match between home culture and school culture, not in a deficit (Bourdieu) [1]. **[4]**

**4.** Staying in education means giving up wages that could be earned [1]. For a low-income family, this lost income may be needed for household costs [1], so a pupil with good grades may leave to work [1]. Poverty therefore shapes the choice to continue, not just attainment, linking to Boudon's secondary effects [1]. **[4]**

**5. (a)** Before: 180 ÷ 400 × 100 = 45% [1]. During: 252 ÷ 400 × 100 = 63% [1]. Change: 63 − 45 = 18 percentage points [1].
**(b)** Reason: the allowance reduces the opportunity cost of staying on, a material barrier [1], so families can afford to keep young people in education [1]. Limitation: one year in one district may reflect other changes, such as a local fall in jobs [1], and staying on does not show whether attainment rose [1]. **[7]**

**6.** Way 1: knowledge of the system [1]. Reay (1998) found middle-class mothers used their educational knowledge and confidence to challenge teachers and secure support [1], so their children received extra help that working-class children, whose mothers cared but felt less able to intervene, did not [1].
Way 2: organised activities [1]. Lareau (2003) found middle-class parents practised concerted cultivation through clubs, lessons and reasoning with adults [1], building skills and confidence that match school expectations [1]. **[6]**

**7.** Way 1: setting shapes knowledge [1]. The lowest set receives worksheets and copying rather than challenging work, so pupils meet less of what exams reward [1]; Keddie found lower streams received different knowledge, which limits attainment regardless of ability [1].
Way 2: pupil responses to labels [1]. The boys mocking "boffins" form an anti-school subculture like Hargreaves's low-stream pupils, gaining status by rejecting school [1]; the girl's "getting through it" resembles Woods's ritualism or compliance, so even quiet pupils may not engage fully [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Criticism 1: the deficit assumption [1]. Many programmes assume working-class families lack the right culture [1], which critics such as Keddie see as blaming the victim and ignoring how schools favour middle-class culture [1].
Criticism 2: they treat symptoms [1]. Programmes are often small and short [1], while the inequality of income and housing that creates disadvantage remains, so Marxists argue education cannot compensate for society [1]. **[6]**

**9.** Argument 1: parents may value education but lack resources to act on it [1]. Reay (1998) found working-class mothers cared deeply but had less confidence, time and educational knowledge [1]. Long working hours and opportunity costs also limit what families can do [1]. So the gap reflects cultural capital and material conditions, not values [1].
Argument 2: in-school factors cause under-achievement regardless of parents [1]. Ball (1981) found pupils from manual backgrounds less likely than similar-ability peers to reach top bands [1], and lower bands met lower expectations [1]. The cause then lies in the school's decisions [1]. **[8]**

**10.** Argument 1: material factors create gaps before school labels operate [1]. Poverty, poor housing and the costs of schooling lower attainment from early childhood [1]. Feinstein's cohort evidence suggests class differences in development appear early, even if the size of the effect is debated [1]. So labelling may widen gaps but does not create them [1].
Argument 2: pupils do not simply accept labels [1]. Woods (1979) identified many adaptations to school, from compliance to rebellion [1], and some pupils work harder to disprove a label [1]. Labelling theory is too deterministic to be the main explanation [1]. **[8]**

**11.** Marked by levels of response; this is indicative content.

- **Knowledge (AO1):** internal factors — labelling (Becker, Rist), setting (Ball), triage (Gillborn and Youdell), subcultures (Hargreaves, Lacey, Woods, Willis); external factors — material deprivation and opportunity cost, cultural deprivation (Douglas, Sugarman), speech codes (Bernstein), cultural capital (Bourdieu, Reay, Lareau, Sullivan).
- **Application (AO2):** setting by early tests; school choice by house price; post-16 allowances.
- **Analysis and evaluation (AO3):** labels follow class lines because of external factors; early gaps before school (Feinstein, and the regression to the mean debate); ethnographies are small; factors interact, for example money buys cultural capital.
- **Judgement:** for example, external factors create the starting gap and internal factors widen or narrow it.

**Level guidance:** top answers show interaction between factors and reach a judgement that follows from the evidence; middle answers describe internal and external factors in turn; low answers list studies without linking them to the question.

**12.** Marked by levels of response; this is indicative content.

- **For:** Perry Preschool's long-term outcomes; Education Maintenance Allowance targeting opportunity cost; conditional cash transfers such as Progresa and Bolsa Família raising attendance; Pupil Premium funding targeted support.
- **Against:** Head Start's early gains often faded; deficit assumptions blame families; programmes are small relative to wider inequality; Marxist view that education cannot compensate for society.
- **Evaluation:** distinguish attendance from attainment; short-term test gains from long-term life outcomes; material-targeted from culture-targeted programmes; evidence varies by country.
- **Judgement:** for example, well-targeted long-term programmes can narrow gaps, but not "significantly" without wider economic change.

**Level guidance:** top answers judge "significantly" using evidence of what different programmes achieved; middle answers describe programmes; low answers assert success or failure.
