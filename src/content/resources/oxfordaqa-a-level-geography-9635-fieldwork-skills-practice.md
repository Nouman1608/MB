These questions cover section 3.5.2 Fieldwork skills of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Geography (9635) specification, Version 3.2 (3.5.2.1 to 3.5.2.5). The specification assesses fieldwork in Unit 5: Geographical and Fieldwork skills through unfamiliar fieldwork questions and familiar fieldwork questions about your own investigation. Revise first with the [fieldwork skills study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-geography-9635-fieldwork-skills/) and the [fieldwork skills revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-geography-9635-fieldwork-skills-revision-notes/).

All places, people and data below are fictional, except where a question asks about your own fieldwork.

## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Distinguish between a hypothesis and a research question. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between accuracy and precision in fieldwork measurement. **[2]**

**3.** Students will sample 120 quadrats across a dune system. The embryo dune zone covers 15% of the area, the yellow dune zone 35% and the grey dune zone 50%. Calculate a stratified sample for each zone. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why background research from secondary sources should be carried out before primary data collection. **[3]**

**5.** Students timed a float over a 10 m stretch of the fictional Allen Beck three times: 22 s, 18 s and 25 s.

**(a)** Calculate the velocity for each trial and the mean velocity. Show your working. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest **two** ways the students could improve the reliability or accuracy of their velocity data. **[4]**

**6.** Outline **one** ethical issue in fieldwork other than obtaining consent. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**7.** Students plan to test the hypothesis: "Environmental quality improves with distance from the centre of Brenton."

**(a)** Suggest suitable primary data and a sampling strategy for this investigation. **[4]**
**(b)** Suggest and justify **one** way of presenting the data. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest and justify **one** statistical technique to analyse the data. **[2]**

**8.** In the same investigation, students interviewed residents. Two responses were:

> "Since the bypass opened, the lorries have gone and the street feels calmer."
> "There's nowhere to sit and the park is full of litter."

Explain how the students could analyse interview responses like these. **[4]**

**9.** Another group tested a similar hypothesis with 12 sites and obtained a Spearman's rank value of rs = 0.41. The two-tailed critical value for n = 12 at the 0.05 level is 0.587.

**(a)** Interpret this result. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest **three** reasons why the hypothesis might still be correct despite this result. **[3]**

**10.** With reference to your own fieldwork investigation, explain how you justified your hypothesis or research question and your choice of location. **[6]**

**11.** With reference to your own fieldwork investigation, assess the reliability of your conclusions. **[9]**

**12.** "Planning is the most important stage of a fieldwork enquiry." To what extent do you agree with this view? **[9]**

## Answers

Questions 10 to 12 are extended responses. The real exam marks these with levels of response, not point by point; the [1] points show the kind of content that earns credit. For familiar fieldwork questions, the content must come from your own investigation.

**1.** A hypothesis is a testable prediction, often directional, based on theory [1]. A research question is an open question that guides investigation without predicting an outcome, often used for qualitative enquiries [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Accuracy is how close a measurement is to the true value [1]. Precision is how finely it is measured, for example to the nearest millimetre rather than centimetre; a precise reading can still be inaccurate if the instrument is faulty [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Embryo: 120 x 0.15 = 18; yellow: 120 x 0.35 = 42 [1]; grey: 120 x 0.50 = 60, total 120 [1]. **[2]**

**4.** It provides the theoretical background needed to justify a hypothesis [1]. Maps and existing data help choose suitable, safe sites and methods [1]. It gives context for comparing and explaining primary results later [1]. **[3]**

**5. (a)** Velocities: 10 / 22 = 0.45 m/s, 10 / 18 = 0.56 m/s, 10 / 25 = 0.40 m/s [1]. Mean = (0.45 + 0.56 + 0.40) / 3 = 0.47 m/s [1].
**(b)** Repeat the timing more times, such as five, and discard anomalies before averaging [1], which reduces the effect of a single unusual trial [1]. Use a flow meter instead of a float [1], because a float is affected by wind and surface currents and may catch on vegetation [1]. **[6]**

**6.** For example, damage to the environment [1], such as trampling dune vegetation or disturbing wildlife, which should be minimised by keeping to paths [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* the impact of representing data, such as labelling a neighbourhood as deprived, earns equal credit.

**7. (a)** Use an environmental quality survey with scored criteria such as litter, noise and building condition [1], so each site has a comparable numerical score [1]. Use systematic sampling along transects, for example every 200 m outwards from the centre [1], to give even coverage of the distance gradient being tested [1].
**(b)** A scatter graph of environmental quality score against distance [1], because it shows whether the two continuous variables are related and highlights anomalies [1].
**(c)** Spearman's rank correlation [1], because it tests the strength and significance of the relationship between two sets of paired data [1]. **[8]**

**8.** Code each response by theme, for example "traffic", "litter", "facilities" [1], and by whether it is positive or negative [1]. Count how often each theme occurs to compare areas or groups [1]. Use short quotations to illustrate the range of views, noting that a small number of interviews may not be representative [1]. **[4]**

**9. (a)** 0.41 is below the critical value of 0.587 [1], so the null hypothesis cannot be rejected at the 0.05 level; the relationship could have arisen by chance [1].
**(b)** Twelve sites is a small sample, so a real relationship may not reach significance [1]. Local anomalies, such as a park near the centre, may weaken the overall trend [1]. The scoring of environmental quality is subjective and may vary between surveyors [1]. **[5]**

**10.** *Indicative content.* State the hypothesis precisely [1]. Link it to a named theory or concept, for example bid-rent theory or longshore drift [1]. Explain how secondary sources such as maps or previous studies supported the prediction [1]. Explain why the location was suitable for testing it, such as a clear gradient or drift direction [1]. Refer to accessibility and safety as reasons for the choice [1]. Note the scale: the area was small enough to sample adequately in the time available [1]. **[6]**

**11.** *Indicative content (levels marking applies).* State the conclusion reached, with evidence [1]. Sample size: whether there were enough sites or respondents [1]. Sampling method: whether it was representative [1]. Timing: whether data were collected at typical times or only once [1]. Equipment accuracy and human error in measurement [1]. Statistical result: significance level reached [1]. Anomalies and how they were treated [1]. Consistency with secondary data or previous studies [1]. Judgement on how far the conclusion can be trusted, with a specific improvement [1]. **[9]**

**12.** *Indicative content (levels marking applies).* Planning sets the aim, a testable hypothesis and the methods, so errors here affect every later stage [1]. A clear hypothesis determines which data and statistical tests are needed [1]. Secondary research and site selection shape the validity of results [1]. Risk assessment protects participants and allows data collection to happen at all [1]. However, sampling and data collection determine reliability, however well planned [1]. Presentation and analysis turn data into evidence; poor analysis can waste good data [1]. Evaluation identifies weaknesses and allows the enquiry to be improved or taken forward [1]. The stages are interdependent: evaluation often leads back to planning [1]. Judgement: planning is the foundation, but every stage contributes, so it is the most important only in the sense that later stages depend on it [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Familiar fieldwork answers that describe a generic investigation rather than your own.
- Unfamiliar answers that ignore details of the scenario given.
- Naming a method without explaining why it suits the question.
- Calculations without units or working.
- Treating a non-significant result as a failure rather than a finding to explain.
