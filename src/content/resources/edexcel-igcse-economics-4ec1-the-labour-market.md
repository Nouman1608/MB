## What the specification asks for

| Point | Wording |
|---|---|
| a | Factors affecting the demand for labour: demand for the final product (derived demand); availability of substitutes, including machines; productivity of workforce |
| b | Factors affecting the supply of labour: population size; migration; age distribution of population; retirement age; school-leaving age; female participation; skills and qualifications; ability to move geographic locations or to different types of employment |
| c | Importance of the quantity and quality of labour to business |
| d | Impact of education and training on human capital and quality of labour |
| e | Labour market diagrams showing supply of labour, demand for labour, equilibrium wage and quantity of labour (employment), and the effect of shifts in demand and supply |
| f | Trade union involvement: impact of trade union activity to improve working conditions and increase wages |

## Demand for labour (point a)

The demand for labour is the number of workers firms are willing and able to employ at each wage rate. It slopes downwards: at a lower wage, firms want to hire more.

### Derived demand

Labour is not wanted for itself but for what it produces. A **derived demand** comes from the demand for the final product.

*Chain:* a housing boom raises demand for new homes → building firms need to produce more → they hire more bricklayers and electricians → demand for construction labour shifts right. If the boom ends, the chain runs the other way.

### Substitutes, including machines

If machines or other inputs can do the same job more cheaply, firms substitute them for workers, and demand for that labour shifts left. *Example:* self-service checkouts reduce the demand for supermarket cashiers. If machines become dearer, or cannot do the work, demand for labour holds up.

### Productivity of the workforce

More productive workers produce more output for each hour paid, so each worker adds more to revenue. Firms are willing to employ more of them at any given wage, so demand shifts right.

## Supply of labour (point b)

The supply of labour is the number of people willing and able to work at each wage rate. It slopes upwards: a higher wage attracts more people into a job.

| Factor | Example of change | Effect on supply |
|---|---|---|
| Population size | Population grows | Increases |
| Migration | Workers emigrate to higher-wage countries | Decreases at home |
| Age distribution | A larger share of people over retirement age | Decreases |
| Retirement age | Raised from 62 to 65 | Increases |
| School-leaving age | Raised from 16 to 18 | Decreases in the short run |
| Female participation | Better childcare lets more women work | Increases |
| Skills and qualifications | More people qualify as accountants | Increases for accountancy jobs |
| Ability to move location or type of work | Cheaper housing near jobs; retraining schemes | Increases where workers are needed |

The last factor is about **mobility**. Geographical mobility is the ability to move to where jobs are; occupational mobility is the ability to move into a different type of work. Poor mobility means vacancies stay unfilled in one place or job while unemployment persists in another.

## Quantity and quality of labour for business (points c and d)

- **Quantity:** a firm needs enough workers, or hours, to meet orders. A shortage means lost sales, overtime costs or higher wages to attract staff.
- **Quality:** skilled, healthy, motivated workers produce more and make fewer errors, lowering unit costs and raising product quality.

**Human capital** is the knowledge, skills and experience of the workforce. **Education** builds general skills (literacy, numeracy, problem-solving); **training** builds job-specific skills. Both raise the quality of labour.

*Invented example.* A hotel pays receptionists 15 an hour. Untrained staff check in 6 guests an hour; after a training course, 8.

```
Labour cost per check-in before = 15 ÷ 6 = 2.50
Labour cost per check-in after  = 15 ÷ 8 = 1.875 ≈ 1.88
```

Evaluation: the gain depends on the cost of the course and whether trained staff stay.

## Labour market diagrams (point e)

**How to draw:** wage rate on the vertical axis; quantity of labour (employment) on the horizontal axis; downward-sloping D (demand for labour); upward-sloping S (supply of labour); equilibrium wage W and quantity Q where they cross.

*Invented schedule: delivery drivers in City H, wage per hour.*

| Wage | Drivers demanded | Drivers supplied |
|---|---|---|
| 9 | 2 600 | 1 400 |
| 10 | 2 400 | 1 800 |
| 11 | 2 200 | 2 200 |
| 12 | 2 000 | 2 600 |
| 13 | 1 800 | 3 000 |

Equilibrium wage = **11**; employment = **2 200**. At a wage of 9 there is a shortage of 2 600 − 1 400 = 1 200 drivers, so firms raise wages until the market clears.

### Shifts

**Demand increases.** Online shopping grows, so derived demand rises by 400 drivers at every wage.

| Wage | New drivers demanded | Drivers supplied | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| 11 | 2 600 | 2 200 | Shortage of 400 |
| 12 | 2 400 | 2 600 | Surplus of 200 |

At 11 there is now a shortage, so the wage rises; at 12 there is a surplus, so the new equilibrium lies between 11 and 12, with employment between 2 200 and 2 600. Questions usually ask for the direction: **wage rises, employment rises**.

**Supply decreases** (a new rule raises the minimum age for drivers). S shifts left: **wage rises, employment falls**.

| Shift | Wage | Employment |
|---|---|---|
| D right | Up | Up |
| D left | Down | Down |
| S right | Down | Up |
| S left | Up | Down |

**Movement versus shift:** a change in the wage moves along the curves; a change in any factor from points a or b shifts a curve. Label new curves D1 and S1, and mark W1 and Q1.

## Trade unions (point f)

A **trade union** is an organisation of workers that bargains with employers on their members' behalf. Collective bargaining gives workers more power than negotiating alone.

How unions seek better **working conditions**: safer equipment, limits on hours, paid holidays, protection from unfair treatment.

How unions seek **higher wages**: negotiating pay rises, backed by the threat of industrial action such as strikes or refusing overtime.

### Using the City H schedule

If a union negotiates a wage of 13 for all drivers, firms demand only 1 800 drivers, compared with 2 200 at the equilibrium. Those who stay employed gain 2 an hour, but 400 fewer drivers are employed, while 3 000 would like to work at that wage.

### Evaluating union activity

- **For workers:** higher pay and safer conditions; a voice in decisions.
- **Against:** if the wage is pushed above equilibrium, fewer jobs; strikes cost workers lost pay.
- **For businesses:** one negotiation instead of many; better conditions can raise motivation and productivity.
- **Against:** higher costs and disruption from industrial action.
- **Depends on:** whether productivity rises with pay, and whether the firm can pass costs on in higher prices.

## Common mistakes

- Shifting a curve when only the wage changes.
- Mixing up the axes: wage is vertical, employment horizontal.
- Saying a raised school-leaving age increases labour supply in the short run. It reduces it.
- Treating derived demand as "demand for workers' wages".
- Claiming unions always cause unemployment; it depends where the wage is set and on productivity.

## Exam technique

- **Draw:** label axes, curves, original and new equilibria.
- **Explain:** factor → curve shift → effect on wage and employment.
- **Calculate:** shortages and surpluses from schedules; percentage changes in wages.
- **Evaluate:** weigh gains for those employed against jobs lost, and name the deciding factor.
