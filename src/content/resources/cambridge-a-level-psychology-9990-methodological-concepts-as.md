**This guide covers the "Methodological concepts" heading of section 3.1.2 of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Psychology (9990) syllabus.** These are the ideas used to design and judge any study, whatever its method: aims and hypotheses, variables, controls, types of data, sampling, ethics, validity, reliability and replicability, and data analysis. Most syllabus statements ask you to describe a concept, evaluate studies with it and apply it to a new situation, so each section below ends with a short application. The six methods themselves are covered in the [research methods guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-psychology-9990-research-methods-as/), and the [Paper 2 Research Methods overview](/resources/a-level-cambridge-psychology-research-methods/) explains how the paper is set.

Condense this guide with the [methodological concepts revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-psychology-9990-methodological-concepts-as-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [methodological concepts practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-psychology-9990-methodological-concepts-as-practice/).

## Aims and hypotheses

An **aim** is a statement of what the study intends to investigate, for example "to investigate whether doodling affects recall of a telephone message". A **hypothesis** is a testable prediction.

- The **null hypothesis** states there will be no difference (or no relationship), for example "There will be no difference in the number of names recalled by participants who doodle and those who do not."
- The **alternative hypothesis** predicts a difference or relationship. A **directional (one-tailed)** version states the direction: "Participants who doodle will recall more names than those who do not." A **non-directional (two-tailed)** version does not: "There will be a difference in the number of names recalled…"

Use a directional hypothesis when previous research suggests which way the result will go. A good hypothesis includes both operationalised variables.

## Variables and operationalisation

The **independent variable (IV)** is the one the researcher changes; the **dependent variable (DV)** is the one measured. An **operational definition** states exactly how a variable is manipulated or measured, so that another researcher could repeat it. "Stress" is not operationalised; "score on a 10-item stress questionnaire, 0 to 40" is.

Dependent variables can be measured in several ways: counts (number of words recalled), time (seconds to respond), scores on a scale, ratings by observers or physiological measures such as heart rate.

*Application*: in a study of whether caffeine improves concentration, the IV could be "drinking 200 ml of coffee or 200 ml of decaffeinated coffee 30 minutes before the test", and the DV "number of target letters crossed out in a two-minute letter-search task".

## Controlling variables

A **control** is any step that keeps a variable other than the IV constant. **Standardisation** means every participant experiences the same procedure: the same instructions, materials, timing and environment.

- **Uncontrolled variables** are any variables other than the IV that could affect the DV.
- **Participant variables** are differences between people, such as age, ability or mood.
- **Situational variables** are features of the setting, such as noise, temperature or time of day.

If an uncontrolled variable changes systematically with the IV, the results are confounded, and you cannot tell what caused the change in the DV.

*Application*: in the caffeine study, test everyone at the same time of day (situational) and ask participants not to drink caffeine beforehand (participant).

## Types of data

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Quantitative | Numerical | Number of shocks given; reaction time |
| Qualitative | Descriptive, in words or images | Interview comments about how a participant felt |
| Objective | Not affected by the researcher's or participant's opinion | MRI measurements; EEG readings |
| Subjective | Depends on personal interpretation or judgement | Self-rated mood; an observer's impression |

Quantitative data are easy to compare and analyse but may lose detail; qualitative data are rich but harder to compare and more open to interpretation. Objective data improve validity; subjective data may be the only way to measure experiences such as pain or dream content.

## Sampling

The **population** is the group the researcher wants to draw conclusions about; the **sample** is the people who actually take part.

- **Opportunity sampling**: whoever is available and willing at the time. Quick and cheap, but likely to be unrepresentative.
- **Random sampling**: every member of the population has an equal chance of being chosen, for example by drawing names from a full list. More likely to be representative, but needs a complete list and chosen people may refuse.
- **Volunteer (self-selecting) sampling**: participants respond to an advertisement. Easy to reach large numbers, but volunteers may be more motivated or curious than others. Milgram's newspaper advertisement is an example.

**Generalisation** is applying findings beyond the sample. The more representative the sample, the more confidently findings can be generalised.

## Ethics

For **human participants** the syllabus lists: minimising harm (and maximising benefit), valid consent including informed consent, the right to withdraw, lack of deception, confidentiality, privacy and debriefing. Children need consent from a parent or guardian, and their own agreement where possible.

For **animals** it lists: minimising harm (and maximising benefit), replacement (using alternatives where possible), species (choosing a species suited to the question and least likely to suffer), numbers (the smallest number needed), and procedures, covering pain, suffering and distress; housing; and reward, deprivation and aversive stimuli.

*Application*: "Evaluate the study based on ethical guidelines" needs a guideline named, applied to a specific feature of the study, and, ideally, the researcher's justification.

## Validity

**Validity** is whether a study measures what it claims to measure. **Ecological validity** is how far findings apply to real-life settings. Studies are evaluated for validity in terms of:

- **Subjectivity and objectivity**: subjective measures can be biased by interpretation.
- **Demand characteristics**: cues that tell participants what the study is about, so they change their behaviour, for example trying to help the researcher.
- **Generalisability**: whether the sample allows conclusions about the wider population.

## Reliability and replicability

**Reliability** is consistency. **Inter-rater** and **inter-observer reliability** check that two or more researchers scoring or observing the same behaviour agree. **Test-retest reliability** checks that the same test gives similar results when the same people take it again later. **Replicability** is whether a study can be repeated exactly, which depends on a standardised, fully described procedure. In a plan, you show replicability by giving enough detail (timings, wording, materials) for someone else to repeat the study.

## Data analysis

You need to **present and interpret data in tables** and recognise and find measures of central tendency and spread. The syllabus states that candidates will not be required to carry out calculations.

*Worked example with invented data*: scores out of 10 on a memory test.

| Condition | Scores (in order) |
|---|---|
| Doodling | 4, 5, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 |
| Control | 3, 3, 4, 4, 4, 5, 6 |

- **Mode** (most frequent score): doodling 5; control 4.
- **Median** (middle score when in order): doodling 6; control 4.
- **Mean** (total divided by number of scores): doodling about 6.3; control about 4.1. You need to recognise and interpret the mean, not calculate it.
- **Range** (highest minus lowest): doodling 9 − 4 = 5; control 6 − 3 = 3. The doodling scores are more spread out.
- **Standard deviation** shows how spread scores are around the mean; a larger standard deviation means scores vary more.

Graphs: a **bar chart** shows separate categories, such as mean scores for two conditions, with gaps between bars; a **histogram** shows the frequency of continuous data in intervals, with no gaps; a **scatter graph** shows the relationship between two co-variables.

## Common mistakes

- Writing a hypothesis without operationalised variables.
- Confusing validity with reliability.
- Calling random allocation "random sampling".
- Using a bar chart for continuous data, or a histogram for categories.
- Naming an ethical guideline without saying how the study breaks or meets it.
