These notes condense section 3.3, Workers, of the Cambridge IGCSE Economics 0455 syllabus for examination in 2026: choice of occupation, wage determination, differences in earnings (with labour-market diagrams) and division of labour. All figures are invented. For explanations and worked examples read the [workers study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-workers/), then practise with the [workers practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-workers-practice/). The combined [workers, wages and trade unions revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-workers-wages-and-trade-unions-revision-notes/) also cover section 3.4.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Wage factors | Money rewards: basic pay, overtime, bonus, commission |
| Non-wage factors | Other features of a job: hours, security, promotion, training, conditions, fringe benefits, satisfaction |
| Fringe benefits | Extras beyond pay, such as a pension, health insurance or a company car |
| Derived demand | Demand for labour comes from demand for the product it makes |
| Supply of labour | Number of workers willing and able to work in a job at each wage |
| Minimum wage | A legal lowest wage per hour that employers must pay |
| Discrimination | Treating workers differently because of gender, race or another feature unrelated to their ability |
| Specialisation | Concentrating on a particular product or task |
| Division of labour | Splitting production into separate tasks done by different workers |

## 3.3.1 Choosing an occupation

- Compare the **whole package**: wage factors plus non-wage factors.
- Turn money items into a common measure, such as pay after travel costs or pay per hour.
- Non-wage factors explain why people accept lower-paid jobs: security, shorter hours, satisfaction, location.

Worked reminder: Job X pays 2,000 a month for 160 hours; Job Y pays 2,100 for 180 hours.

```
X: 2,000 / 160 = 12.50 an hour
Y: 2,100 / 180 = 11.67 an hour
```

Y pays more per month but less per hour.

## 3.3.2 Wage determination

**Three influences named in the syllabus:** demand and supply; relative bargaining power; government policy, including the minimum wage.

| Shifts demand for labour right | Shifts supply of labour right |
|---|---|
| Higher demand for the product | Shorter or cheaper training |
| Higher productivity of workers | Better non-wage factors |
| Capital becomes more expensive | More workers (population growth, immigration) |
| | Lower wages in other jobs |

**Effects to learn:**

| Change | Wage | Employment |
|---|---|---|
| Demand for labour rises | Up | Up |
| Demand for labour falls | Down | Down |
| Supply of labour rises | Down | Up |
| Supply of labour falls | Up | Down |
| Minimum wage above equilibrium | Up for those employed | Down; surplus of workers |
| Minimum wage below equilibrium | No effect | No effect |

**Bargaining power** is stronger when workers are organised, skills are scarce, the employer is profitable and unemployment is low.

## Labour-market diagram checklist

- Vertical axis: wage rate. Horizontal axis: quantity of labour.
- D slopes down; S slopes up; label equilibrium W and Q.
- New curve labelled D1 or S1; new W1 and Q1 marked.
- Arrows show direction of change.
- A minimum wage is a horizontal line above W; show the gap between quantity supplied and quantity demanded.

## 3.3.3 Differences in earnings

| Comparison | Key causes |
|---|---|
| Skilled / unskilled | Low supply and high demand for skills; stronger bargaining |
| Primary / secondary / tertiary | Productivity and value of output; numbers seeking work in each sector |
| Male / female | Discrimination; different occupations and hours; equal pay laws |
| Private / public | Government sets public pay within budgets; private pay follows profit and the market |

Worked reminder: skilled pay 30 an hour, unskilled 12.

```
Gap = 18 an hour
Skilled earns 30 / 12 = 2.5 times as much
Unskilled earns 12 / 30 x 100 = 40% of skilled pay
```

## 3.3.4 Division of labour

| | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Workers | Skill at one task, higher earnings possible | Boredom; narrow skills; job loss risk |
| Firms | Higher productivity, lower unit cost, less training | Stoppage at one stage halts all; errors from boredom |
| Economy | More output, higher living standards, more trade | Structural unemployment if an industry declines; dependence on few industries |

Worked reminder: 5 workers make 60 units alone; divided into tasks they make 90.

```
Output per worker: 12 rises to 18, a 50% rise in productivity
```

## Exam traps

- **Derived demand.** Demand for workers falls when demand for the product falls, even if the workers are just as skilled.
- **Movement versus shift.** A wage change alone is a movement along the curves.
- **Minimum wage below equilibrium** has no effect; say so.
- **Gaps are not proof.** A male–female earnings gap may come from different jobs and hours as well as discrimination.
- **Name the group.** Division of labour questions often ask for workers, firms *or* the economy; answer for the one named.
- **Per hour versus per month.** Compare jobs on the same basis.

## Self-test

1. Give two non-wage factors that affect choice of occupation.
2. Why is demand for labour a derived demand?
3. State the effect on wage and employment of a fall in the supply of nurses.
4. A minimum wage of 8 is set when the market wage is 9. What is the effect?
5. Give two reasons why surgeons earn more than porters.
6. Name two influences on wage determination given in the syllabus.
7. What does a labour-market diagram show on each axis?
8. Job P pays 1,800 for 150 hours a month. Calculate the hourly rate.
9. Give one disadvantage of division of labour for workers.
10. Give one advantage of division of labour for the economy.
11. Suggest one reason why public-sector pay may be lower than private-sector pay.
12. What happens to the wage of computer programmers if demand for software rises?

### Answers

1. Any two: hours, holidays, job security, promotion prospects, training, working conditions, fringe benefits, job satisfaction, location.
2. Firms employ workers because consumers demand the goods and services the workers produce.
3. Wage rises; employment falls.
4. No effect, because the minimum is below the market wage.
5. Any two: long, costly training limits supply; high value of output keeps demand high; stronger bargaining power.
6. Any two: demand and supply; relative bargaining power; government policy (including minimum wage).
7. Vertical: wage rate. Horizontal: quantity of labour.
8. 1,800 / 150 = 12.
9. Boredom from repetition, or narrow skills that make changing jobs harder.
10. Higher total output and living standards, or more goods available to trade.
11. The government limits pay to control its spending.
12. Demand for programmers rises (derived demand), so their wage and employment rise.
