## The one equation

E = tensile stress / tensile strain = FL/(AΔL)

| Symbol | Quantity | Unit | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| F | tension (force) | N | mass given? multiply by g |
| L | original length | m | not the stretched length |
| A | cross-sectional area | m² | A = πd²/4 from the diameter |
| ΔL | extension | m | given in mm? multiply by 10⁻³ |
| E | Young modulus | Pa | often quoted in GPa |

Rearranged forms: ΔL = FL/(EA); F = EAΔL/L; A = FL/(EΔL).

Link to the spring constant of a wire: **k = EA/L**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Tensile stress | Tension per unit cross-sectional area, F/A (Pa) |
| Tensile strain | Extension per unit original length, ΔL/L (no unit) |
| Young modulus | Stress/strain within the limit of proportionality; a property of the material |
| Limit of proportionality | Point beyond which stress is no longer proportional to strain |
| Stiffness (k) | Force per unit extension of one object; depends on its dimensions |
| Reference wire | Unloaded wire hung beside the test wire to cancel sagging of the support and temperature changes |

## Unit conversions that cost marks

| Given | Convert to |
|---|---|
| 1 mm | 10⁻³ m |
| 1 mm² | 10⁻⁶ m² |
| 1 MPa | 10⁶ Pa |
| 1 GPa | 10⁹ Pa |
| 0.20% strain | 2.0 × 10⁻³ |

## Proportional reasoning

For a wire under tension F: ΔL ∝ FL/(Ed²).

| Change (all else fixed) | Effect on extension |
|---|---|
| Double the length | × 2 |
| Double the diameter | × 1/4 |
| Halve the diameter | × 4 |
| Material with twice the E | × 1/2 |
| Double the force (within limit) | × 2 |

For a given force and area, the stress and the strain do not depend on length: a longer wire has the same strain and so a proportionally larger extension.

## Reading graphs

| Axes (vertical against horizontal) | Gradient | E from gradient |
|---|---|---|
| Stress against strain | E | E = gradient |
| Force against extension | EA/L | E = gradient × L/A |
| Extension against force | L/(EA) | E = L/(A × gradient) |

Use only the straight part, and two points far apart.

## Required practical 2 in six lines

1. Long, thin wire clamped at the top; marker on the wire; fixed scale or vernier against a reference wire.
2. L from clamp to marker with a metre rule or tape.
3. Diameter with a micrometer at several points, in two perpendicular directions; take the mean.
4. Add loads in equal steps; record the scale reading each time; extension = reading − initial reading.
5. Unload in steps; readings should return, showing the elastic limit was not passed.
6. Plot a graph and use the gradient as in the table above.

**Uncertainty.** %A = 2 × %d. Usually %d is the biggest term, so %E is dominated by the area. Combine by adding percentage uncertainties: %E = %L + %A + %gradient.

## Exam traps

- A force-extension gradient is k, not E.
- Radius and diameter confused, or d not squared.
- Strain written with a unit, or as a percentage substituted directly.
- A point on the curved part used for the gradient.
- "A thicker wire has a bigger Young modulus": no, it has a bigger k.
- Forgetting that a hanging mass gives F = mg.
- Writing E to more significant figures than the data justify.

## Quick self-test

1. Define the Young modulus.
2. Explain why the Young modulus has the same unit as stress.
3. A wire has diameter 0.70 mm. Find its cross-sectional area in m².
4. A material reaches a stress of 240 MPa at a strain of 1.2 × 10⁻³, within its limit of proportionality. Find E.
5. A wire of length 1.5 m and area 2.0 × 10⁻⁷ m² has E = 1.2 × 10¹¹ Pa. Find its extension under 50 N.
6. Wire Y is the same material as wire X, with twice the length and twice the diameter. The same force acts on each. Give the ratio of extensions Y : X.
7. A wire has E = 2.0 × 10¹¹ Pa, A = 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ m² and L = 2.0 m. Find its spring constant.
8. A graph of extension against force for a wire of length 1.8 m and area 8.0 × 10⁻⁸ m² has gradient 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ m N⁻¹. Find E.
9. A stress of 84 MPa produces a strain of 0.12%. Find E.
10. A diameter is measured as 0.25 ± 0.01 mm. Find the percentage uncertainty in the area.
11. State the purpose of a reference wire in the Young modulus experiment.
12. Why are the readings also taken while the load is removed?

### Answers

1. The ratio of tensile stress to tensile strain, for a material within its limit of proportionality.
2. Strain is a ratio of two lengths and has no unit, so stress ÷ strain has the unit of stress, Pa.
3. A = π × (0.35 × 10⁻³)² = 3.85 × 10⁻⁷ m².
4. E = 240 × 10⁶ / 1.2 × 10⁻³ = 2.0 × 10¹¹ Pa.
5. ΔL = FL/(EA) = 50 × 1.5 / (1.2 × 10¹¹ × 2.0 × 10⁻⁷) = 3.1 × 10⁻³ m.
6. Length × 2, area × 4, so extension × 2/4: ratio 1 : 2.
7. k = EA/L = 2.0 × 10¹¹ × 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ / 2.0 = 1.0 × 10⁴ N m⁻¹.
8. E = L/(A × gradient) = 1.8 / (8.0 × 10⁻⁸ × 2.5 × 10⁻⁴) = 9.0 × 10¹⁰ Pa.
9. Strain = 1.2 × 10⁻³, so E = 84 × 10⁶ / 1.2 × 10⁻³ = 7.0 × 10¹⁰ Pa.
10. 0.01/0.25 = 4%, so area 8%.
11. Any sagging of the support or thermal expansion affects both wires equally, so the scale reading between them changes only because of the load.
12. If the readings return to their original values, the wire has not passed its elastic limit, so the data come from the region where E applies.
