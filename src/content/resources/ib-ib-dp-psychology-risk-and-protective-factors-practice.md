> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge** on risk and protective factors in the Health
> and well-being context (IB DP Psychology, first assessment 2027). They are not past-paper questions.
> Question 13 uses the Paper 1 Section C tariff of 15 marks; the rest use practice tariffs. The survey in
> Question 9 is invented, with invented figures.

Related: [Risk and protective factors study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-psychology-risk-and-protective-factors/),
[revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-psychology-risk-and-protective-factors-revision-notes/) and the

## Section A

**1.** Define "protective factor". **[2]**

**2.** Give one example of a risk factor and one example of a protective factor for stress-related illness. **[2]**

**3.** Distinguish between the behavioural and physiological pathways from a risk factor to illness. **[2]**

**4.** State what is meant by a graded relationship. **[1]**

**5.** Outline the buffering hypothesis. **[2]**

**6.** State why Berkman and Syme (1979) is described as a prospective study. **[1]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain why Cohen, Tyrrell and Smith (1991) gave some participants salt-water drops and kept all participants in quarantine. **[3]**

**8.** Explain two limitations of Felitti et al. (1998) as evidence that childhood adversity causes adult illness. **[4]**

**9.** Invented survey: 500 office workers rate their job stress (high or low) and whether they have someone they could rely on in a crisis (yes or no). Over the next year, the number who take at least five days of sick leave is recorded.

| Group | Workers | Five or more sick days |
|---|---|---|
| Low stress, support | 150 | 15 |
| Low stress, no support | 100 | 12 |
| High stress, support | 150 | 24 |
| High stress, no support | 100 | 40 |

(a) Calculate the percentage with five or more sick days in each group.
(b) Explain whether the results fit the buffering hypothesis.
(c) Give one reason the survey cannot show that support causes better health. **[7]**

**10.** Outline one ethical consideration in research that deliberately exposes participants to a cold virus, and how it can be addressed. **[2]**

**11.** Explain how Cohen et al. (1997) adds to the evidence from Berkman and Syme (1979). **[3]**

**12.** Explain why socio-economic status is a possible confounding variable in research on adverse childhood experiences. **[2]**

**13.** Evaluate the role of stress in one health problem, with reference to the concept of causality. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** A characteristic or experience that reduces the likelihood of a health problem **[1]** or reduces the harmful effect of a risk factor **[1]**.

**2.** Risk factor, for example chronic work stress or adverse childhood experiences **[1]**; protective factor, for example social support or a diverse social network **[1]**.

**3.** The behavioural pathway works through changes in behaviour, such as smoking or poor sleep under stress **[1]**; the physiological pathway works directly through body systems, such as cortisol suppressing immune function **[1]**.

**4.** The greater the level of a factor, the greater the effect on the outcome **[1]**.

**5.** Social support protects health mainly when stress is high **[1]**, for example by changing how a stressor is appraised or helping a person cope **[1]**.

**6.** Social ties were measured first and deaths were recorded over the following nine years **[1]**.

**7.** The salt-water group was a control showing how many people developed cold symptoms without the virus **[1]**. Quarantine kept exposure to other infections the same for everyone **[1]**, so differences in illness could be linked to stress rather than to different contact with viruses **[1]**.

**8.** Two limitations, two marks each:
- The data are correlational: another variable, such as poverty, could lead to both childhood adversity and adult illness, so cause cannot be inferred **[2]**.
- Childhood experiences were recalled by adults, so recall may be inaccurate or affected by current health and mood **[2]**.
- Also creditable for two marks: the sample came from insured patients in one American city, limiting generalisation.

**9.** (a) Low stress, support: 15 / 150 = 10% **[1]**; low stress, no support: 12 / 100 = 12% **[1]**; high stress, support: 24 / 150 = 16% **[1]**; high stress, no support: 40 / 100 = 40% **[1]**.
(b) Yes: support makes little difference under low stress (10% compared with 12%) **[1]**, but a large difference under high stress (16% compared with 40%), which is the pattern the buffering hypothesis predicts **[1]**.
(c) It is correlational: support was not manipulated, so workers with support may differ in other ways, such as income or personality, that affect sick leave **[1]**.

**10.** Participants are deliberately made ill, which risks harm **[1]**; this can be addressed through full informed consent, using a mild virus, medical supervision during quarantine and the right to withdraw **[1]**.

**11.** Berkman and Syme showed that people with fewer social ties had higher mortality, but exposure to illness was not controlled **[1]**. Cohen et al. measured network diversity and then gave all participants the same viral exposure **[1]**, so the finding that diverse networks were linked to lower susceptibility cannot be explained by differences in exposure, which strengthens the conclusion that social ties are protective **[1]**.

**12.** Low income is linked to higher rates of household stress and adversity in childhood **[1]** and also to poorer adult health through access to care, housing and diet, so it could explain the link between ACEs and illness **[1]**.

**13.** Marked with the Paper 1 Section C markbands (15 marks). The top band, 13 to 15 marks, needs the demands of the question addressed; relevant knowledge and understanding fully explained; well-developed critical analysis; links to the concept of causality included throughout and fully explained; a reasoned, clearly stated conclusion; and accurate, precise terminology. Content that can be credited:
- **Knowledge:** names a health problem, such as stress-related illness; defines stress, risk and protective factors; behavioural and physiological pathways; graded relationships; the buffering hypothesis.
- **Research as evidence:** two or three of Felitti et al. (1998), Cohen, Tyrrell and Smith (1991), Berkman and Syme (1979) and Cohen et al. (1997). Details of studies are not assessed, so credit what the evidence shows.
- **Critical analysis:** correlation and confounding; prospective versus retrospective designs; controlled exposure; ethics; culture.
- **Causality:** what each design can and cannot show about cause and effect, and whether the pathway is behavioural, physiological or unknown.
- **Conclusion:** a judgement on how strong the evidence is that stress plays a causal role in the health problem.

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 7:** explaining the salt water but not the quarantine.
- **Question 9(b):** saying support "always helps" without comparing the two stress levels.
- **Question 12:** naming socio-economic status without explaining how it links to both variables.
- **Question 13:** listing factors with no named health problem and no research, or never returning to causality.
