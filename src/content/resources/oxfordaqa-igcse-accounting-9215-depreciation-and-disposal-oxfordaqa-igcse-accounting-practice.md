## Section A

**1.** State **two** causes of depreciation and explain **one** of them. **[3]**

**2.** Explain why depreciation is described as a non-cash expense. **[2]**

**3.** Which entry records the annual depreciation charge?

A: Debit provision for depreciation, credit depreciation

B: Debit depreciation, credit provision for depreciation

C: Debit asset account, credit depreciation

D: Debit depreciation, credit bank

**[1]**

**4.** A delivery bicycle costs 3,800. It is expected to last 5 years and then be sold for 300. Calculate the annual straight line depreciation charge. **[2]**

**5.** A machine costs 50,000 and is depreciated at 20% a year by the reducing balance method. Calculate the charge for each of Years 1, 2 and 3. **[3]**

**6.** Explain why a profit on disposal of a non-current asset is not included in revenue. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Ashby Lettings buys office furniture for 12,000. It expects to use it for 5 years and then sell it for 2,000.

**(a)** Calculate the straight line charge and the net book value at the end of Year 2. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the charges for Years 1 and 2 if reducing balance at 30% were used instead. **[2]**

**(c)** Evaluate which method is more suitable for the furniture. **[3]**

**8.** Pryor Engineering bought a machine on 1 April Year 1 for 18,000. It charges straight line depreciation at 20% of cost a year on a time basis. Its year ends on 31 December. The machine was sold on 31 August Year 3 for 9,000, received by bank transfer.

**(a)** Calculate the provision for depreciation on the machine at the date of sale. **[3]**

**(b)** Prepare the disposal account. **[4]**

**9.** Quayside Couriers depreciates vehicles at 20% a year on the cost of vehicles held at the year end, with a full year's charge in the year of purchase and none in the year of sale. Its year ends on 31 December.

At 1 January Year 4: vehicles at cost 80,000; provision for depreciation 31,000.

During Year 4 a van bought on 1 January Year 1 for 20,000 was sold for 7,000 by bank transfer, and a new van was bought for 26,000 by bank transfer.

**(a)** Prepare the vehicles account for Year 4. **[3]**

**(b)** Prepare the provision for depreciation account for Year 4. **[4]**

**(c)** Prepare the disposal of vehicles account. **[3]**

**(d)** Show the statement of financial position extract for vehicles at 31 December Year 4. **[2]**

**10.** A trader's draft profit is 30,000. Depreciation of 4,500 on equipment has been omitted.

**(a)** Calculate the corrected profit. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain the effect of the omission on the statement of financial position. **[2]**

**11.** The owner of a shop says: "Because we charge depreciation every year, we will have the cash to replace our equipment when it wears out." Assess this statement. **[3]**

**12.** A computer bought for 4,000 is depreciated at 40% reducing balance, with a full year in the year of purchase and none in the year of sale. It is sold early in Year 3 for 1,500. Calculate the profit or loss on disposal, showing your workings. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two of: wear and tear; obsolescence; passage of time [1] [1]. Explanation, for example: obsolescence means newer models make the asset out of date, so its usefulness to the business falls even though it still works [1].

**2.** The charge reduces profit in the income statement [1], but no cash leaves the business when it is recorded; the cash was paid when the asset was bought [1].

**3.** B [1].

**4.** (3,800 − 300) ÷ 5 [1] = **700 a year** [1].

**5.** Year 1: 20% × 50,000 = **10,000** [1]. Year 2: 20% × 40,000 = **8,000** [1]. Year 3: 20% × 32,000 = **6,400** [1].

**6.** Revenue is income from the business's normal trading [1]. Selling a non-current asset is not normal trading, so the profit is shown separately as other income; it arises because depreciation charged was more than the asset's actual fall in value [1].

**7. (a)** (12,000 − 2,000) ÷ 5 = **2,000** a year [1]. NBV end of Year 2 = 12,000 − 4,000 = **8,000** [1].

**(b)** Year 1: 30% × 12,000 = **3,600** [1]. Year 2: 30% × 8,400 = **2,520** [1].

**(c)** Furniture is used fairly evenly and gives a similar benefit every year [1], so straight line matches the cost to the benefit better [1]. Reducing balance would charge far more in early years (6,120 against 4,000 over two years), reducing early profits for no good reason, so straight line is more suitable [1]. **[7]**

**8. (a)** Annual charge 20% × 18,000 = 3,600. Year 1, 9 months: 2,700 [1]. Year 2: 3,600; Year 3, 8 months: 2,400 [1]. Provision = **8,700** [1].

**(b)** Disposal of machine account

**[7]**

**(b)** Provision on van sold: 3 years × 4,000 = 12,000 [1]. Year 4 charge: 20% × 86,000 = 17,200 [1].

**(d)** Vehicles: cost **86,000** less provision **36,200** [1] = net book value **49,800** [1]. **[12]**

**10. (a)** 30,000 − 4,500 = **25,500** [1].

**(b)** The provision for depreciation is understated by 4,500 [1], so the net book value of equipment, total assets and capital (through profit) are all overstated by 4,500 [1]. **[3]**

**11.** The statement is wrong in general: depreciation is a book entry that moves no cash [1]. It keeps profit lower, which may reduce the amount the owner withdraws, but no money is set aside in the bank by the entry [1]. Cash for replacement exists only if the business deliberately saves or borrows it, and replacement may also cost more than the original asset [1].

**12.** Year 1: 40% × 4,000 = 1,600, NBV 2,400 [1]. Year 2: 40% × 2,400 = 960, NBV 1,440 [1]. Proceeds 1,500 − NBV 1,440 [1] = **profit on disposal of 60** [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the whole provision balance instead of the depreciation on the asset sold.
- Ignoring the stated part-year policy.
- Applying a reducing balance rate to cost after Year 1.
- Showing a profit on disposal on the credit side of the disposal account.
- Explaining depreciation as cash put aside.
