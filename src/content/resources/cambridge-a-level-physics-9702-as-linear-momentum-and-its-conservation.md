## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE INTERNATIONAL AS & A LEVEL PHYSICS 9702 — AS Level, Topic 3.3**

Candidates should be able to:

1. state the principle of conservation of momentum;
2. apply the principle of conservation of momentum to solve simple problems, including elastic and inelastic interactions between objects in both one and two dimensions (knowledge of the concept of coefficient of restitution is not required);
3. recall that, for an elastic collision, total kinetic energy is conserved and the relative speed of approach is equal to the relative speed of separation;
4. understand that, while momentum of a system is always conserved in interactions between objects, some change in kinetic energy may take place.

## The principle

**The total momentum of a system of interacting objects remains constant, provided no resultant external force acts on the system.**

The condition is essential. During a collision the objects exert large forces on each other, but these are **internal** forces in third-law pairs: equal, opposite, and acting for the same time, so the momentum one object gains is exactly the momentum the other loses. External forces such as friction or air resistance usually act too, but the collision is so brief that their effect on momentum is negligible.

## Method for one-dimensional problems

1. Choose a positive direction and give every velocity a sign.
2. Write total momentum before = total momentum after.
3. Solve for the unknown; a negative answer means the object moves in the negative direction.
4. If asked, compare total kinetic energy before and after.

### Worked example 1: bodies moving towards each other

A 3.0 kg trolley moving right at 4.0 m s⁻¹ meets a 2.0 kg trolley moving left at 5.0 m s⁻¹. They stick together. Take right as positive.

```
before: (3.0)(+4.0) + (2.0)(-5.0) = 12.0 - 10.0 = +2.0 kg m s^-1
after:  (3.0 + 2.0) v = 2.0      ->  v = +0.40 m s^-1  (to the right)

KE before = 1/2(3.0)(4.0^2) + 1/2(2.0)(5.0^2) = 24 + 25 = 49 J
KE after  = 1/2(5.0)(0.40^2) = 0.40 J
```

Momentum is conserved; 48.6 J of kinetic energy is transferred to other forms (internal energy, sound, deformation). Forgetting the minus sign on the 2.0 kg trolley gives 22 kg m s⁻¹ before and a wrong v of 4.4 m s⁻¹.

## Elastic and inelastic collisions

| | Elastic | Inelastic |
|---|---|---|
| Total momentum | Conserved | Conserved |
| Total kinetic energy | Conserved | Not conserved (some transferred to other forms) |
| Relative speed of separation | Equal to relative speed of approach | Less than relative speed of approach |
| Example | Collisions between gas molecules (ideally) | Most real collisions; objects that stick together |

When objects stick together the collision is called **perfectly inelastic**: they separate at zero relative speed, and the loss of kinetic energy is the largest that momentum conservation allows.

### Worked example 2: an elastic collision using relative speeds

A 0.40 kg ball moving at 6.0 m s⁻¹ collides elastically head-on with a stationary 0.20 kg ball. Find both final velocities.

Two unknowns need two equations. Using kinetic energy directly gives squared terms; the relative-speed rule is linear and much quicker.

```
momentum:        (0.40)(6.0) = 0.40 v1 + 0.20 v2   ->  2.4 = 0.40 v1 + 0.20 v2
relative speeds: approach = 6.0 - 0 = 6.0,  so  v2 - v1 = 6.0   ->  v2 = v1 + 6.0

substitute:  2.4 = 0.40 v1 + 0.20(v1 + 6.0) = 0.60 v1 + 1.2
             v1 = 2.0 m s^-1,   v2 = 8.0 m s^-1   (both forwards)

check KE:  before 1/2(0.40)(6.0^2) = 7.2 J
           after  1/2(0.40)(2.0^2) + 1/2(0.20)(8.0^2) = 0.8 + 6.4 = 7.2 J
```

The check confirms the collision is elastic, as stated. In exams, the relative-speed rule may be used only when the question says the collision is elastic, or once you have shown it is.

## Explosions and objects pushed apart

If a system starts at rest, its total momentum is zero, and it stays zero after an explosion or recoil.

### Worked example 3: trolleys sprung apart

Two trolleys of mass 0.80 kg and 1.20 kg are held together with a compressed spring between them, at rest. When released, the 0.80 kg trolley moves left at 0.60 m s⁻¹.

```
0 = (0.80)(-0.60) + (1.20) v   ->  v = +0.40 m s^-1  (to the right)

KE after = 1/2(0.80)(0.60^2) + 1/2(1.20)(0.40^2) = 0.144 + 0.096 = 0.240 J
```

Kinetic energy has **increased** from zero: the 0.240 J came from the elastic potential energy of the spring. Momentum is conserved; kinetic energy is not, in either direction. The lighter trolley moves faster and receives the larger share of the kinetic energy.

## Two-dimensional collisions

Momentum is a vector, so it is conserved **separately in each of two perpendicular directions**. Resolve every momentum into components, write a conservation equation for each direction, and solve.

### Worked example 4: a glancing collision between equal masses

A puck A of mass 0.50 kg moving at 4.0 m s⁻¹ along the x-axis hits a stationary puck B of equal mass. Afterwards A moves at 2.0 m s⁻¹ at 60° to the x-axis. Find the velocity of B.

```
x:  (0.50)(4.0) = (0.50)(2.0 cos 60) + (0.50) v_Bx   ->  2.0 = 0.50 + 0.50 v_Bx  ->  v_Bx = 3.0 m s^-1
y:  0 = (0.50)(2.0 sin 60) + (0.50) v_By             ->  v_By = -1.73 m s^-1

v_B = sqrt(3.0^2 + 1.73^2) = 3.46 m s^-1
angle below the x-axis = tan^-1(1.73 / 3.0) = 30 degrees
```

The pucks separate at 60° + 30° = 90°. Checking kinetic energy: before ½(0.50)(4.0²) = 4.0 J; after ½(0.50)(2.0²) + ½(0.50)(3.46²) = 1.0 + 3.0 = 4.0 J. So this collision is elastic. For equal masses with one initially at rest, an elastic glancing collision always sends them off at right angles, a useful check.

## Common mistakes

- **Ignoring signs** for objects moving in opposite directions.
- **Assuming kinetic energy is conserved** without being told the collision is elastic.
- **Stating the principle without the condition** "no resultant external force".
- **Adding speeds rather than momentum components** in two dimensions.
- **Applying the relative-speed rule to an inelastic collision.**
- **Saying momentum is "lost" in an inelastic collision.** Kinetic energy is lost from the objects; momentum never is.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **State** the principle: full wording, including the condition on external force.
- **Calculate** a velocity: show the momentum equation with signs before substituting.
- **Show that** a collision is inelastic: calculate the total kinetic energy before and after and compare them.
- **Explain** why momentum is conserved while kinetic energy is not: internal forces are third-law pairs acting for equal times, so momentum changes cancel; kinetic energy can be transferred to internal energy, sound or deformation.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is momentum conserved in an inelastic collision?**
Yes. Momentum is conserved in every collision with no resultant external force. Only kinetic energy is not conserved.

**How can I tell if a collision is elastic?**
Compare total kinetic energy before and after, or check whether the relative speed of separation equals the relative speed of approach.

**Do I need the coefficient of restitution for Cambridge 9702?**
No. The syllabus states that knowledge of the coefficient of restitution is not required.
