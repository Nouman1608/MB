These notes condense section 2.7 (price elasticity of demand) of the Cambridge IGCSE Economics 0455 syllabus for examination in 2026. All figures are invented. Worked explanations are in the [PED study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-price-elasticity-of-demand/); test yourself with the [PED practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-price-elasticity-of-demand-practice/).

## 2.7.1 and 2.7.2 Definition and calculation

- **PED:** the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in price.
- **Formula:** PED = % change in quantity demanded / % change in price.
- **Percentage change** = (new - old) / old x 100.
- PED is negative; judge it by its size.

| Size | Type | Diagram |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Perfectly inelastic | Vertical line |
| 0 to 1 | Inelastic | Steep curve |
| 1 | Unitary | Curve bending towards the origin |
| Above 1 | Elastic | Shallow curve |
| Infinite | Perfectly elastic | Horizontal line |

### Rearranged formulae

```
% change in Qd = PED x % change in P
% change in P needed = % change in Qd wanted / PED
```

Examples:

```
PED = -0.8, price rises 15%: Qd changes by -0.8 x 15 = -12%
PED = -2, firm wants sales up 10%: price change = 10 / -2 = -5%
Price 6 -> 4.80 (-20%), sales 300 -> 330 (+10%): PED = 10 / -20 = -0.5
```

- Slope is not elasticity: on a straight-line demand curve PED is higher at high prices and lower at low prices.

### Method for a full-mark calculation

1. Write the formula with quantity on top.
2. Work out each percentage change from the original values, keeping the sign.
3. Divide and round sensibly (usually to two decimal places).
4. State elastic, inelastic or unitary, and give the reason: compare the two percentage changes.
5. If asked, say what happens to revenue and why.

## 2.7.3 Determinants

| Factor | More elastic | More inelastic |
|---|---|---|
| Substitutes | Many, close | Few, distant |
| Necessity | Luxury | Necessity |
| Share of income | Large | Small |
| Time | Long period | Short period |
| Definition | One brand | Whole category |
| Habit | Impulse purchase | Habit-forming |

Always link the determinant to the product: "Few substitutes for insulin, so patients cannot switch, so demand is inelastic."

## 2.7.4 PED and revenue

| Demand | Price up | Price down |
|---|---|---|
| Elastic | Revenue down | Revenue up |
| Inelastic | Revenue up | Revenue down |
| Unitary | No change | No change |

**Rectangles for a price cut from P1 to P2:**

```
Revenue lost = (P1 - P2) x Q1
Revenue gained = P2 x (Q2 - Q1)
```

Example: price 8 -> 6; sales 50 -> 80.

```
Lost = 2 x 50 = 100; gained = 6 x 30 = 180; net +80
Revenue 400 -> 480; PED = +60% / -25% = -2.4 (elastic)
```

## 2.7.5 Significance

| Decision maker | Use of PED |
|---|---|
| Consumers | Inelastic necessities take more of the budget after a price rise; elastic goods can be swapped for substitutes |
| Producers | Raise price if demand is inelastic, cut price if elastic, to raise revenue; pass on cost rises more easily with inelastic demand; charge lower prices to groups with elastic demand |
| Government | Tax inelastic products for revenue; taxes on harmful products with inelastic demand cut consumption only a little; subsidies work best where demand is elastic |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| PED | Responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in price |
| Elastic demand | PED greater than 1 in size |
| Inelastic demand | PED between 0 and 1 in size |
| Unitary elasticity | PED equal to 1 in size |
| Total spending (revenue) | Price x quantity |
| Substitute | A product that can be used instead |

## Exam traps

- Price on top of the formula.
- Using the new figure as the base.
- Calling PED of -1.5 inelastic because of the minus sign.
- Treating a steep curve as perfectly inelastic.
- Stating "revenue rises" without saying whether demand is elastic or inelastic.
- Listing determinants without applying them.
- In tax questions, forgetting that inelastic demand gives more revenue but less change in consumption.

## Quick self-test

1. Define PED.
2. Price rises from 20 to 25 and quantity falls from 400 to 360. Calculate PED.
3. Is the demand in question 2 elastic or inelastic?
4. PED is -1.2. Price is cut by 10%. By what percentage does quantity demanded change?
5. A firm with PED of -0.25 wants to raise sales by 5%. What price change is needed?
6. What shape is a perfectly inelastic demand curve?
7. Give two determinants that make demand elastic.
8. Demand is inelastic. What happens to revenue when price rises?
9. Price falls from 10 to 9 and sales rise from 200 to 240. Calculate revenue lost and gained.
10. Why might a government prefer to tax products with inelastic demand?
11. Why might a tax on a harmful product with inelastic demand fail to cut consumption much?
12. Why does PED differ along a straight-line demand curve?

### Answers

1. The responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in price.
2. -10% / +25% = -0.4.
3. Inelastic (size less than 1).
4. -1.2 x -10 = +12%.
5. 5 / -0.25 = -20%.
6. A vertical line.
7. Any two: many close substitutes, luxury, large share of income, long time period, narrowly defined product.
8. Revenue rises.
9. Lost = 1 x 200 = 200; gained = 9 x 40 = 360; revenue rises by 160 (2,000 to 2,160).
10. Sales fall little, so the tax raises more revenue.
11. Buyers respond little to the higher price, so quantity falls only slightly.
12. The same price change is a smaller percentage at high prices and the same quantity change a larger percentage at low quantities.
