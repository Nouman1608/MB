(7404/7405), version 1.1, 1 December 2015, for AS and A-level exams June 2016 onwards: 3.2.1.1
Classification and 3.2.1.2 Physical properties of Period 3 elements. All of it is AS content, so nothing
here is A-level only. The topic is examined on AS Paper 1 and A-level Paper 1, and A-level Paper 3 can test
any content.

## Questions

**1.** Which element is in the d block of the Periodic Table?

- A gallium (Z = 31)
- B zinc (Z = 30)
- C calcium (Z = 20)
- D helium (Z = 2)

**[1]**

**2.** State the property of an atom that determines the position of its element in the Periodic Table. **[1]**

**3.** An atom of element E has the electron configuration 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d¹⁰ 4s² 4p².

**(a)** State the block of the Periodic Table that contains E. **[1]**
**(b)** State the period and the group that contain E. **[2]**

**4.** For each element, state its block and explain your answer.

**(a)** helium, which is placed in Group 0 **[2]**
**(b)** the element with proton number 24 **[2]**

**5.** Describe and explain the trend in atomic radius of the elements from sodium to chlorine. **[4]**

**6.** Explain why the first ionisation energy of chlorine is greater than that of silicon. **[3]**

**7.** **(a)** Write an equation, with state symbols, for the first ionisation energy of aluminium. **[1]**
**(b)** State which element in Period 3 has the highest first ionisation energy. **[1]**

**8.** Explain why magnesium has a higher melting point than sodium. **[3]**

**9.** Explain why sulfur has a higher melting point than chlorine. **[3]**

**10.** Explain why the melting points of silicon, phosphorus and argon decrease in the order
Si > P > Ar. Refer to the structure of each element. **[6]**

**11.** X, Y and Z are elements in Period 3.

- X forms 3+ ions and conducts electricity when solid.
- Y has the highest melting point in Period 3.
- Z exists as diatomic molecules.

**(a)** Identify X, Y and Z. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why Y has a higher melting point than X. **[2]**
**(c)** State the block that contains all three elements. **[1]**

**12.** A student writes: "Across Period 3, melting point increases because atomic radius decreases and
nuclear charge increases."

Evaluate this statement. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. Zinc's last electron in energy order goes into 3d (3d¹⁰ 4s²). Gallium is p block (4p¹),
calcium is s block and helium is s block. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Gallium is the tempting wrong answer: 3d¹⁰ appears in its configuration, but its last electron goes into 4p.

**2.** Its **proton number** (atomic number) [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* "Mass number" or "relative atomic mass" gets no credit; the table is ordered by proton
number.

**3. (a)** **p block**, because the last electron is added to the 4p sub-shell [1].
**(b)** **Period 4** [1]; **Group 4 (14)**, since it has four outer electrons, 4s² 4p² [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b) period and group are separate marks; giving "Group 2" from the 4s² alone loses
the group mark.

**4. (a)** **s block** [1]; its configuration is 1s², so its highest-energy electron is in an s sub-shell [1].
**(b)** **d block** [1]; the configuration is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d⁵ 4s¹ (chromium), and the element sits in
the block where the 3d sub-shell is being filled [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The block alone is worth only one mark in each part; the second mark needs the
sub-shell reason, not a restatement of the answer.

**5.** Atomic radius **decreases** from Na to Cl [1]. Nuclear charge increases, because each element has one
more proton [1]. The outer electrons are in the same (third) shell, so shielding is similar [1]. The
outer electrons are attracted more strongly and pulled closer to the nucleus [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "More shells" or "more shielding" contradicts the same-shell point and can cost the
shielding mark.

**6.** Chlorine has more protons (17 against 14), so a greater nuclear charge [1]. In both atoms the outer
electron is removed from the third shell, so shielding is similar [1]. Chlorine's atomic radius is smaller,
so its outer electron is attracted more strongly and more energy is needed to remove it [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Each mark is for a separate factor; a long answer about nuclear charge alone scores
one mark at most.

**7. (a)** **Al(g) → Al⁺(g) + e⁻** [1].
**(b)** **Argon** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The equation mark needs (g) on the aluminium atom and the ion; Al(s) on the left, or
no state symbols, does not score.

**8.** Both are giant metallic lattices with metallic bonding [1]. Magnesium forms Mg²⁺ ions, which have a
greater charge (and are smaller) than Na⁺ ions, and each Mg atom provides two delocalised electrons
compared with one for Na [1]. So the attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons is
stronger in magnesium, and more energy is needed to overcome it [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers that mention "ionic bonds" or "intermolecular forces" in a metal lose the
structure mark, even if the rest is right.

**9.** Both are simple molecular, made of S₈ and Cl₂ molecules [1]. S₈ has more electrons (128) than Cl₂
(34), so the van der Waals forces between S₈ molecules are stronger [1]. More energy is needed to overcome
these forces, so sulfur has the higher melting point [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Saying that S–S or Cl–Cl covalent bonds break on melting does not earn the second mark here,
because it shows the wrong idea of what is overcome.

**10.** Silicon is macromolecular (giant covalent) [1]. To melt it, many strong covalent bonds must be
broken, which needs a lot of energy, so its melting point is very high [1]. Phosphorus is simple molecular,
made of P₄ molecules [1]. When it melts only the weak van der Waals forces between molecules are overcome;
the P–P covalent bonds are not broken [1]. Argon exists as single atoms, each with 18 electrons, compared
with 60 electrons in a P₄ molecule [1]. So the van der Waals forces between argon atoms are weaker than
those between P₄ molecules, less energy is needed to overcome them, and argon has the lowest melting point
[1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* In a six-mark explanation each element needs its structure named and what is broken or
overcome; leaving argon out caps the answer well below full marks.

**11. (a)** X is **aluminium** [1]; Y is **silicon** [1]; Z is **chlorine** [1].
**(b)** Silicon is macromolecular, so melting means breaking many strong covalent bonds [1]; this needs
more energy than overcoming the metallic bonding between Al³⁺ ions and delocalised electrons in aluminium
[1].
**(c)** **p block** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* In (a) each identification is marked on its own, so a wrong X does not lose the marks
for a correct Y and Z.

**12.** Award one mark for each of these points.

- Melting point does increase from Na to Al [1], but the main reason is stronger metallic bonding: higher
  ion charge and more delocalised electrons per atom, not radius alone [1].
- Silicon has the highest melting point because it is macromolecular and covalent bonds must be broken [1];
  this is a change of structure, not a radius effect.
- From P onwards the melting point is much lower even though radius keeps falling and nuclear charge keeps
  rising [1], because P, S and Cl are simple molecular and only van der Waals forces between molecules are
  overcome [1].
- Conclusion: the statement is incorrect for the period as a whole; melting point depends on structure and
  bonding, and peaks at silicon rather than rising throughout [1].

**[6]**
*Examiner insight:* "Evaluate" needs a clear judgement at the end; listing correct facts without saying
whether the statement holds usually loses the final mark.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Explaining Period 3 trends with "more shells" or "more shielding".
- Giving nuclear charge as the only factor for radius or ionisation energy and leaving out similar shielding.
- Saying covalent bonds break when P₄, S₈ or Cl₂ melts.
- Describing silicon as having "strong intermolecular forces"; it has covalent bonds throughout.
- Writing metallic bonding as attraction between atoms, or between ions and ions.
- Comparing van der Waals forces by size of molecule without linking to the number of electrons.
- Classifying helium as p block or gallium as d block.
- Missing state symbols in ionisation energy equations.
- Ending an "evaluate" answer without a judgement.
