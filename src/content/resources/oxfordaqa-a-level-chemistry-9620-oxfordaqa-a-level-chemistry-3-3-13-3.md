This guide goes deeper into **3.3.13.3 Action of anticancer drugs**, the last subtopic of section 3.3.13 Amino acids and proteins in the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International A-level only content, assessed in Unit 4 (Organic 2 and Physical 2) and available again in the synoptic Unit 5. The subtopic is short, but questions on it are almost always synoptic: they pull in complex ions, co-ordinate bonding, cis–trans isomerism and amount-of-substance calculations, then finish with an extended answer about benefits and risks.

## What the specification asks for

The subtopic makes three statements and sets two skills:

- the Pt(II) complex cisplatin is used as an anticancer drug
- cisplatin prevents DNA replication in cancer cells by a ligand replacement reaction with DNA, in which a bond is formed between platinum and a nitrogen atom
- appreciate that society needs to assess the balance between the benefits and the adverse effects of drugs, such as cisplatin.

You should be able to **explain why cisplatin prevents DNA replication** and **explain why such drugs can have adverse effects**. Section 3.2.5.3 adds a linked statement: square planar complexes can show cis–trans isomerism, and cisplatin is the cis isomer.

A note on wording: the specification places the nitrogen atom "on a protein". In the DNA itself, the nitrogen that bonds to platinum is on a base, usually guanine. In an answer, "a nitrogen atom on DNA (on a guanine base)" uses the lone pair correctly and matches the specification's description of a reaction with DNA.

## The structure of cisplatin

Cisplatin is [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂]. Four ligands surround one platinum ion:

| Feature | Detail | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Metal ion | Platinum in the +2 oxidation state | Pt(II) forms square planar complexes |
| Ligands | Two NH₃ molecules and two Cl⁻ ions | Each donates a lone pair to form a co-ordinate bond |
| Co-ordination number | 4 | Four co-ordinate bonds to platinum |
| Shape | Square planar, bond angles 90° | Makes cis and trans arrangements possible |
| Overall charge | 0 | +2 from Pt and 2 × (−1) from Cl⁻ cancel; NH₃ is neutral |
| Isomer | Cis: the two Cl⁻ ligands are next to each other, at 90° | Lets platinum bind to two nearby sites on DNA |

### Worked example 1: oxidation state and charge

**Question.** Show that platinum is in the +2 oxidation state in cisplatin, and explain why the complex has no overall charge.

**Method.** Let the oxidation state of Pt be x. Each NH₃ ligand is a neutral molecule (0); each chloride ligand is −1. The complex is neutral, so x + 2(0) + 2(−1) = 0, which gives x = **+2**. The two negative chloride ligands balance the 2+ charge on the metal, so the complex is a neutral molecule rather than an ion.

**Comment.** Questions often hide this step inside a longer item, for example by asking for the charge of the platinum species after both chloride ligands have been replaced by neutral nitrogen donors. The same arithmetic gives 2+, because the oxidation state of platinum does not change.

### Cis and trans isomers

In a square planar complex [MA₂B₂], the two B ligands can sit next to each other (cis, 90° apart) or opposite each other (trans, 180° apart). These are stereoisomers: same atoms, same bonds, different arrangement in space. A tetrahedral complex [MA₂B₂] has no such isomers, because every position in a tetrahedron is next to every other position.

Only the cis isomer of [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂] is used as the drug. The trans isomer, sometimes called transplatin, has the same formula but its chloride ligands point in opposite directions.

## How cisplatin stops DNA replication

Learn this as a chain of four linked steps. Each step is a separate marking point in a typical "explain" question.

1. **Ligand replacement.** A chloride ligand leaves platinum and is replaced by a nitrogen atom on DNA. The NH₃ ligands stay bonded.
2. **Co-ordinate bond.** The nitrogen atom has a lone pair of electrons. It donates this pair to platinum, forming a co-ordinate (dative covalent) bond, N→Pt.
3. **Two sites, close together.** Because the two chloride ligands are cis, both can be replaced, so one platinum binds to two nitrogen atoms that are close together on the DNA. This links two nearby parts of the strand.
4. **Distorted DNA cannot be copied.** The bonding distorts the shape of the DNA, so it cannot be replicated normally. Without replication the cancer cell cannot divide, so the tumour stops growing.

The co-ordination number stays at 4 throughout: two Cl⁻ ligands out, two nitrogen donors in. Platinum stays in the +2 oxidation state, so this is a substitution, not a redox reaction.

### Writing an equation

Using G–N: to represent a guanine nitrogen with its lone pair, the overall replacement can be summarised as:

```
[Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂]  +  2 G–N:   →   [Pt(NH₃)₂(N–G)₂]²⁺  +  2Cl⁻
```

Check the charges: the left is neutral; on the right the platinum unit is 2+ (Pt²⁺ with four neutral donors) and two chloride ions are 2−. Charge balances. A common error is to keep the chloride ligands on platinum, or to show the bond forming through chlorine.

### Worked example 2: annotated model answer

**Question.** Explain how cisplatin prevents the replication of DNA in cancer cells. (4 marks)

**Model answer.**

"The chloride ligands in cisplatin are replaced by nitrogen atoms on the DNA in a ligand replacement reaction ✔. Each nitrogen uses its lone pair to form a co-ordinate bond to platinum ✔. Because the two chloride ligands are cis, platinum bonds to two nitrogen atoms close together on the DNA ✔. This distorts the DNA so that it cannot be copied, and the cancer cell cannot divide ✔."

**Why it scores.** It names the reaction type, identifies which ligands leave, describes the bond correctly, uses the cis arrangement, and ends with the consequence for the cell. "Cisplatin attacks DNA" earns nothing: it names no bond and no mechanism.

## Why cisplatin has adverse effects

The specification asks you to explain why such drugs *can* have adverse effects. The chemistry gives the reason directly:

- Ligand replacement with DNA does not depend on whether the cell is cancerous. Cisplatin binds to the DNA of **healthy cells** as well.
- It therefore stops healthy cells from dividing too. Tissues that depend on regular cell division are affected.
- Known side effects of cisplatin include **kidney damage** and **hearing loss**, and patients often feel very sick.

So the adverse effects are not a separate property of the drug; they follow from the same reaction that makes it work. That is the key idea examiners look for.

### Ways the harm is reduced

Answers that weigh benefit and harm earn more credit when they say how harm can be limited. Points that follow from the chemistry:

- give the drug at the lowest dose that is effective
- give it in cycles, so healthy tissue has time to recover between doses
- use it alongside other treatments so that a smaller dose is needed
- monitor patients, for example their kidney function and hearing, and stop or change treatment if damage appears.

## Weighing benefits and adverse effects

"Appreciate that society needs to assess the balance" is assessed by extended-response questions with command words such as **discuss** or **evaluate**. A strong answer gives both sides and reaches a judgement.

| Benefits | Adverse effects and costs |
|---|---|
| Stops cancer cells dividing, so tumours can shrink | Also stops healthy cells dividing |
| Can extend life or cure some cancers | Side effects such as kidney damage and hearing loss |
| Can be combined with other treatments | Unpleasant for the patient, may need further treatment |
| | Platinum is a rare, expensive metal |

**Who decides?** Doctors, regulators and patients each have a role. A drug is approved when trials show that the benefit outweighs the risk for a particular use. The patient, once informed of the likely effects, gives consent. The balance can differ between patients: for a life-threatening cancer, serious side effects may be judged acceptable.

### Worked example 3: structuring an evaluation

**Question.** Evaluate the use of cisplatin to treat cancer. (6 marks)

**Plan.**

1. **How it works**: ligand replacement, N lone pair, co-ordinate bond to Pt, DNA cannot replicate, cancer cells cannot divide.
2. **Why it causes harm**: binds to the DNA of healthy cells too; side effects such as kidney damage and hearing loss.
3. **How harm is limited**: lowest effective dose, cycles of treatment, monitoring.
4. **Judgement**: for many patients the chance of shrinking or removing a tumour outweighs the side effects, provided they are monitored and have given informed consent.

**Comment.** Examiners reward the link between chemistry and consequence. A list of side effects with no mention of *why* they happen stays in the lower band.

## Common mistakes

- Saying platinum bonds to DNA "through chlorine". The chloride ligands leave.
- Calling the bond ionic or a hydrogen bond. It is a co-ordinate bond from a nitrogen lone pair.
- Describing cisplatin as tetrahedral, or as an octahedral complex with water ligands.
- Forgetting the consequence: "it binds to DNA" without "so DNA cannot replicate and the cell cannot divide".
- Explaining side effects as "the drug is toxic" without saying it binds to the DNA of healthy cells.
- Saying the trans isomer is the drug, or that cis and trans isomers are structural isomers.

## Exam technique for OxfordAQA command words

- **State** or **Give**: a short fact, such as "square planar" or "+2".
- **Explain**: a linked chain, as in the four steps above. Each step needs its reason.
- **Suggest**: apply the same ideas to an unfamiliar complex, for example one with only one chloride ligand, and give a reason based on the number or position of leaving groups.
- **Deduce**: work out a charge, oxidation state or formula from information given.
- **Evaluate** or **Discuss**: give benefits and adverse effects, explain each with chemistry, and end with a supported judgement.
