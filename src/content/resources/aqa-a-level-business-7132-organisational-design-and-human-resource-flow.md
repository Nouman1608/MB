## Organisational structure

An **organisational structure** shows how a business is arranged: who is responsible for what, who reports to whom, and how work is divided and coordinated. Structure is usually shown on an organisation chart.

### Models of organisational structure

| Structure | How it is organised | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Functional** | By activity: marketing, operations, finance, HR | Clear specialisation and expertise; efficient use of skills; clear career paths in each function | Departments may pursue their own aims (silo thinking); slow coordination across functions; harder to see each product's performance |
| **Product-based** | By product or product group: each division has its own functions | Focus on each product and its customers; managers accountable for results; quick response to product-specific needs | Duplication of functions (each division has its own marketing, for example); higher costs; competition between divisions |
| **Regional (geographic)** | By area or country | Local knowledge; quick local decisions; suits differences in culture and law | Duplicated roles; inconsistent brand or standards; difficult central control |
| **Matrix** | Staff belong to a function and also to a project or product team, so report to two managers | Flexible use of specialists; strong cross-functional teamwork; good for projects | Confusion over authority and priorities; conflict between managers; more meetings; slower decisions |

Choosing among them depends on what the business sells, how many products and markets it has, its size, and how much it needs to coordinate. A firm with one product in one country is likely to be functional; a firm selling many different products may be product-based; a global firm may use regional divisions; a consultancy running many projects may use a matrix.

## Influences on organisational design

Designing the structure involves choices about the following concepts.

- **Authority**: the right to make decisions and give instructions. Responsibility is the duty to answer for results. Authority should match responsibility.
- **Hierarchy (levels)**: the number of layers of management between top and bottom, and the **chain of command** from the top downwards. A **tall** structure has many levels; a **flat** one has few.
- **Span of control**: the number of subordinates a manager directly supervises.
- **Delegation**: passing authority for a task or decision to a subordinate, who becomes accountable for the result while the manager remains ultimately accountable.
- **Centralisation**: decisions made at the top. **Decentralisation**: decisions pushed down to lower levels or local units.

### Span and hierarchy: worked example

Hartwell Packaging has 500 operatives. Compare a wide span of 10 with a narrow span of 5.

| | Span of 10 | Span of 5 |
|---|---|---|
| Supervisors needed | 500 ÷ 10 = 50 | 500 ÷ 5 = 100 |
| Managers above them | 50 ÷ 10 = 5 | 100 ÷ 5 = 20, then 20 ÷ 5 = 4 |
| Director | 1 | 1 |
| Management posts in total | 50 + 5 + 1 = **56** | 100 + 20 + 4 + 1 = **125** |
| Levels (including operatives) | 4 | 5 |

If each management post costs £40,000 a year: span of 10 costs 56 × £40,000 = **£2,240,000**; span of 5 costs 125 × £40,000 = **£5,000,000**. The wider span saves **£2,760,000** and gives a shorter chain of command, so communication is faster. But with a span of 10, each supervisor has less time for each worker, so the span suits experienced staff doing routine work. A narrow span suits complex or risky work needing close supervision.

| | Tall (narrow span) | Flat (wide span) |
|---|---|---|
| Communication | Slow, can be distorted through many layers | Faster and clearer |
| Control | Close supervision | Less supervision, so more staff autonomy |
| Promotion | Many steps | Few steps, so less motivating for some |
| Cost | High management cost | Lower management cost |
| Suits | Complex, risky work, inexperienced staff | Experienced, skilled staff; need for speed |

### Centralisation and decentralisation

| | Centralised | Decentralised |
|---|---|---|
| Decisions | By senior managers | By local or lower-level managers |
| Advantages | Consistent decisions and brand; clear control; economies of scale; senior managers have the full picture | Faster, better-informed local decisions; motivation of staff given responsibility; develops future managers; frees senior managers for strategy |
| Disadvantages | Slow; senior managers overloaded; local staff demotivated and local needs ignored | Inconsistency; harder to control; risk of poor decisions by less experienced managers; duplication of effort |

### Delegation

Delegation spreads workload, develops staff and speeds decisions. It requires trust, clear instructions, training and the right authority to carry out the task. It fails when managers delegate without authority, or hold onto tasks because they do not trust staff.

### Influences on the choice of design

- **Size and growth** of the business.
- **Type of product and market**: variety of products, geographic spread.
- **Nature of the work**: routine or complex; need for supervision.
- **Skills and attitudes of the workforce**: experienced staff can work with wide spans and delegation.
- **Management style and culture**: autocratic leaders centralise; democratic ones delegate.
- **Technology**: information systems and communications allow flatter, more flexible structures and remote teams.
- **Rate of change** and need for flexibility.
- **Cost** of management layers.
- **External environment**: law, customers, competitors.

## The value of changing organisational design

Businesses change structure because the old one no longer fits: growth, new products or markets, new technology, falling performance or a need to cut costs.

**Benefits:** faster decisions; lower costs (fewer layers); better communication; closer to customers; better use of skills; more motivated staff; greater flexibility and competitiveness.

**Costs and difficulties:** disruption and uncertainty; resistance from managers who lose status; redundancy and retraining costs; loss of experience; confusion about authority during the transition; the benefits may take time. Delayering (removing levels) saves money but overloads remaining managers with wider spans. A change should be linked to clear objectives, communicated openly and monitored.

## Managing the human resource flow

The **human resource flow** is the movement of people into, through and out of the business. Managing it ensures the business has the right people, in the right numbers, with the right skills, at the right time, helping meet HR objectives such as skills, cost control and engagement.

### The HR plan

A **human resource plan** compares the workforce needed with the workforce available, and sets out how to close any gap.

- **Demand for labour** depends on forecast sales and output, productivity, planned technology and business strategy.
- **Supply of labour** depends on the current workforce minus expected leavers (resignations, retirements) plus any internal moves, and on the external labour market.

*Worked example.* Kestrel Services expects to need 240 employees next year. It has 250 now, and expects labour turnover of 12%.

- Expected leavers = 12% × 250 = **30**.
- Expected supply without recruitment = 250 − 30 = **220**.
- Shortfall = 240 − 220 = **20** employees.

HR can close the gap by recruiting 20 (externally or by promotion), by retraining or redeploying staff from areas with surplus, by raising productivity, or by using overtime and temporary staff. If demand were only 200, there would be a surplus of 20 (220 − 200), which would be handled through natural wastage (not replacing leavers), redeployment or, as a last resort, redundancy.

### Recruitment

Recruitment finds and attracts suitable candidates, using internal or external routes.

| | Internal | External |
|---|---|---|
| Advantages | Cheaper and quicker; known quantity; motivates staff by showing promotion chances; shorter induction | Wider choice; new ideas and skills; no vacancy created elsewhere |
| Disadvantages | Limited choice; creates another vacancy; may cause resentment among unsuccessful candidates; no fresh ideas | Costly and slow; risk of poor fit; longer induction; may demotivate existing staff |

Selection (interviews, tests, references) then chooses the best candidate. Technology, such as online job boards, applicant tracking and video interviews, can speed up the process but may introduce bias.

### Training

Training ensures staff have the skills the business needs, now and as it changes. It may be induction, on-the-job, off-the-job or online. It links to retention and promotion. (See the [HR objectives guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-setting-human-resource-objectives/) for the costs and benefits.)

### Redeployment

**Redeployment** moves employees to different jobs, departments or locations within the business, often when their present role is no longer needed. Benefits: retains skills and knowledge; avoids redundancy costs; saves recruitment costs; maintains morale. Difficulties: employees may need retraining; they may resist a different job or relocation; it may not be possible if skills do not match.

### Redundancy

**Redundancy** happens when a job is no longer needed and the employee is dismissed, usually with payment. Reasons: falling demand, automation, closure of a site, or restructuring.

- **Benefits:** reduces costs; matches workforce to demand; can be needed for survival.
- **Costs and difficulties:** redundancy payments; loss of skills and experience; falling morale and loyalty among those who remain; damage to reputation as an employer; legal procedures; risk of losing the best staff who find jobs easily; later recruitment costs if demand returns.

*Worked example.* Kestrel decides to make 15 staff redundant. Redundancy and related costs are £8,000 per person and each post cost £28,000 a year in wages and on-costs.

- One-off cost = 15 × £8,000 = **£120,000**.
- Annual saving = 15 × £28,000 = **£420,000**.
- Payback = £120,000 ÷ £420,000 = 0.29 years, or about **3.4 months**.

The financial case looks strong, but it ignores the lost output from fewer staff, the effect on remaining employees, and the cost of rehiring if demand recovers. Alternatives are a recruitment freeze, voluntary redundancy, reduced hours or redeployment.

### Linking the flow to HR objectives

| HR objective | Role of flow decisions |
|---|---|
| Right number, skills and location | HR plan, recruitment, redeployment, redundancy |
| Talent development | Internal promotion, training |
| Engagement | Redeployment before redundancy; fair, clear processes |
| Diversity | Fair, wide recruitment |
| Cost control | Natural wastage, redundancy, avoiding overstaffing |

## Technique for exam questions

- For structure questions, link the structure to features of the business: products, markets, size and workforce.
- For span and hierarchy, show calculations where figures are given.
- For HR flow, start from the plan: is there a shortage or surplus? Then choose the method suited to it.
- Evaluate by weighing cost, speed, morale and long-term effects.

## Common mistakes

- Describing a structure without saying when it suits a business.
- Mixing up delegation (passing authority) with decentralisation (a structure-wide approach).
- Saying that a flat structure is always better.
- Treating redundancy as the only response to a surplus.
- Ignoring morale and reputation effects of redundancy.
- Forgetting that recruitment from inside creates another vacancy.
