These notes condense section **1.6 Classification of goods and services** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Economics 9708 syllabus (examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028). It is AS content in Topic 1. For full explanations and worked examples, use the [classification of goods study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-9708-classification-of-goods-and-services-cambridge-alevel-economics/); for exam-style questions, use the [classification of goods practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-9708-classification-of-goods-and-services-cambridge-alevel-economics-practice/).

## The four outcomes in one line each

- **1.6.1** Free goods have no opportunity cost; private (economic) goods are scarce, excludable and rival.
- **1.6.2** Public goods are non-excludable and non-rival, so free riding stops the market providing them.
- **1.6.3** Merit goods are under-consumed as a result of imperfect information.
- **1.6.4** Demerit goods are over-consumed as a result of imperfect information.

## Sorting test

Ask the questions in this order.

1. **Is it scarce?** No: free good. Yes: go on.
2. **Can non-payers be excluded, and does one person's use reduce what is left for others?** Excludable and rival: private good. Non-excludable and non-rival: public good. One property only: not a public good under the 9708 definition; say which property fails.
3. **Separately, do consumers misjudge its value to themselves?** Underestimate the benefit: merit good. Underestimate the harm: demerit good. Neither: no information problem.

Step 3 is a separate test from steps 1 and 2. A good can be a private good and a merit good at once (a vaccination, a university course).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning to learn |
|---|---|
| Free good | A good with no opportunity cost, because using it does not mean giving up anything else |
| Private good (economic good) | A scarce good with an opportunity cost, which is excludable and rival |
| Excludable | Non-payers can be prevented from consuming |
| Rival | One person's consumption reduces the amount available to others |
| Public good | A good that is non-excludable and non-rival |
| Free rider | Someone who benefits from a good without paying for it |
| Free-rider problem | Non-excludability means people wait for others to pay, so too little or no revenue is raised and the market does not supply the good |
| Merit good | A good that is under-consumed as a result of imperfect information in the market |
| Demerit good | A good that is over-consumed as a result of imperfect information in the market |
| Imperfect information | Consumers lack the knowledge to judge accurately the benefit or harm a good will bring them |

## Recall points

**Free versus economic depends on context.** Rain falling on a field is free; rainwater collected, filtered and piped to homes is an economic good because resources were used.

**"No price" is not the same as "free".** A good supplied at zero price by the state, such as a library loan, still uses resources and has an opportunity cost.

**Why both public-good properties matter.**

- Non-excludability causes free riding, so firms cannot raise revenue.
- Non-rivalry means an extra user costs nothing, so excluding anyone would waste benefit.

**Typical public goods:** national defence, street lighting, flood defences, lighthouses. Use one that fits the context of the question.

**Borderline goods.** A busy road becomes rival through congestion; a toll makes it excludable. A locked subscription channel is non-rival but excludable. Neither is a pure public good.

**Merit goods: why information fails.** Benefits come in the future (pension saving), are invisible when things go right (a vaccination), or are hard to value (education). Consumers underestimate them and buy too little.

**Demerit goods: why information fails.** Harm is delayed (smoking), builds up slowly (sugar), or is played down by habit or advertising (gambling). Consumers underestimate it and buy too much.

**Individual-level test (invented figures).** Price 40 cu. Perceived benefit 25 cu; fully informed benefit 60 cu. The buyer does not buy, although an informed buyer would. That missing purchase is under-consumption. Reverse the numbers for a demerit good.

**Link forward.** The non-provision of public goods and the mis-consumption of merit and demerit goods are the reasons for intervention listed in section 3.1. Methods such as taxes, subsidies, direct provision and information come in section 3.2.

## Exam traps

- Defining a public good by who provides it. A state school place is a private good provided by the state.
- Writing "non-rival" when you mean "non-excludable". Excludability is about payment; rivalry is about using up.
- Giving only one property of a public good in a definition.
- Explaining merit goods through external benefits. At AS the definition rests on imperfect information.
- Saying a merit good is "not provided" by the market. It is provided, but under-consumed.
- Calling any zero-price item a free good.
- Treating classification as permanent. Scarcity and excludability can change with technology and context.

## Self-test

1. Define a free good.
2. Give the two features that make a private good different from a public good.
3. State what "non-rival" means.
4. Explain why non-excludability leads to free riding.
5. Why might a fully informed consumer buy more of a merit good than an actual consumer does?
6. A town council supplies swimming lessons at zero price. Is this a free good? Explain.
7. Classify a toll motorway with light traffic using the two public-good properties.
8. Give the syllabus definition of a demerit good.
9. Can a good be both a private good and a merit good? Give an example.
10. A smoker values a packet of cigarettes at 9 cu, the price is 7 cu, and a fully informed smoker would value it at 2 cu. What does this show?
11. Which section of the syllabus lists the reasons for government intervention linked to these goods?
12. Why does non-rivalry mean that charging a price can waste benefit?

**Answers**

1. A good with no opportunity cost, because it is so plentiful that using it does not mean giving up anything else.
2. A private good is excludable and rival; a public good is non-excludable and non-rival.
3. One person's consumption does not reduce the amount available to others.
4. Non-payers cannot be stopped from benefiting, so each person has an incentive to let others pay; too little revenue is raised for a firm to cover its costs.
5. Because the actual consumer underestimates the benefit to themselves as a result of imperfect information.
6. No. The lessons use resources (instructors, pool, water), so they have an opportunity cost. They are an economic good supplied at zero price.
7. It is excludable (the toll keeps out non-payers) and, while traffic is light, close to non-rival. Because it is excludable, it is not a public good.
8. A good that is over-consumed as a result of imperfect information in the market.
9. Yes. A vaccination is scarce, excludable and rival, and it is under-consumed because people underestimate its benefit.
10. The smoker buys (9 cu > 7 cu) though an informed buyer would not (2 cu < 7 cu). This is over-consumption caused by imperfect information: a demerit good.
11. Section 3.1, Reasons for government intervention in markets.
12. An extra user adds no cost, so anyone kept out by the price loses a benefit that could have been enjoyed at no cost to anyone else.
