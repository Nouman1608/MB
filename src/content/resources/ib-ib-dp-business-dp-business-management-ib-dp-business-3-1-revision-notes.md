## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Finance | The funds a business needs, and the function that raises, manages and records them |
| Capital expenditure | Spending on non-current assets used for more than one year, or on improving them |
| Revenue expenditure | Spending on day-to-day running, with benefit used up within the year |
| Non-current asset | An asset kept and used for more than a year, such as machinery or buildings |
| Statement of profit or loss | Shows revenue, expenses and profit for a period; revenue expenditure appears here |
| Statement of financial position | Shows assets, liabilities and equity at a date; capital expenditure appears here as assets |

## The role of finance

**Why businesses need funds:**

* start-up: buying assets before revenue arrives
* day-to-day operation: wages, suppliers, bills
* replacement of worn-out assets
* expansion: new sites, products or markets
* survival through shocks such as falling sales

**Jobs of the finance function:**

* raising funds (links to section 3.2)
* managing cash (section 3.7)
* recording transactions and preparing final accounts (section 3.4)
* analysing results for decisions (sections 3.5 and 3.8)
* controlling spending against plans

**Applying it:** a start-up's main need is funds for assets and survival; a growing firm's is expansion without a cash shortage; a struggling firm's is meeting debts while it recovers.

## Capital versus revenue: the test

Ask: does this spending create or improve an asset that will serve the business for more than a year?

* Yes: capital expenditure.
* No, it keeps the business running this year: revenue expenditure.

## Borderline items

| Item | Type |
|---|---|
| Purchase of machinery, vehicles, buildings | Capital |
| Delivery and installation of new equipment | Capital |
| Extension or major upgrade that adds value or life | Capital |
| Software licence bought outright for several years | Capital |
| Wages, rent, utilities, advertising | Revenue |
| Raw materials and stock for resale | Revenue |
| Repairs that restore an asset to working order | Revenue |
| Routine maintenance, repainting, new tyres | Revenue |
| Staff training, even on new equipment | Revenue |
| Monthly or annual software subscriptions | Revenue |

## Why classification matters

* **Accounts:** revenue expenditure reduces this year's profit in full; capital expenditure is shown as a non-current asset and spread over its life (depreciation, HL only in section 3.4).
* **Misclassification:** capital recorded as revenue understates profit and assets; revenue recorded as capital overstates them.
* **Funding:** revenue expenditure is paid from trading cash; large capital expenditure may need longer-term finance.

## Worked mini-example

Pennyrigg Bakery (invented) spends: new oven 18,000; oven installation 1,500; flour and butter 9,400; staff wages 32,000; oven repair after a breakdown 600; shop sign that will last for years 2,500.

* Capital: 18,000 + 1,500 + 2,500 = **22,000**.
* Revenue: 9,400 + 32,000 + 600 = **42,000**.
* If the 18,000 oven were recorded as revenue expenditure, the year's profit would be understated by 18,000 (before depreciation).

## Exam traps

* Installation costs counted as revenue; they are part of the asset.
* Training on new equipment counted as capital; it is revenue.
* Every repair counted as capital; only improvements count.
* Saying capital expenditure never affects profit; it does, through depreciation over time.
* Explaining the role of finance without linking it to the business's situation.

## Quick self-test

1. Define capital expenditure.
2. Define revenue expenditure.
3. Classify: (i) a new delivery van, (ii) fuel for the van, (iii) insurance for the van for the year.
4. Is the cost of installing a new machine capital or revenue? Why?
5. Is training staff on a new machine capital or revenue? Why?
6. Give three reasons a business needs finance.
7. State two jobs of the finance function.
8. Where does capital expenditure appear in the final accounts?
9. What happens to profit if capital expenditure is recorded as revenue expenditure?
10. A firm spends 50,000 on a building extension, 4,000 on repainting and 12,000 on wages. Calculate capital and revenue expenditure.
11. Why is capital expenditure often funded differently from revenue expenditure?
12. Give one way the role of finance differs for a start-up and for a mature business.

### Answers

1. Spending on non-current assets used for more than one year, or on improving them.
2. Spending on day-to-day running whose benefit is used up within the year.
3. (i) Capital, (ii) revenue, (iii) revenue.
4. Capital, because it is needed to bring the asset into use and so forms part of its cost.
5. Revenue, because it develops staff skills and does not add to the asset.
6. Any three: start-up, day-to-day operation, replacing assets, expansion, surviving shocks.
7. Any two: raising funds, managing cash, recording transactions, analysing results, controlling spending.
8. In the statement of financial position, as a non-current asset.
9. Profit for that year is understated, and assets are understated.
10. Capital 50,000; revenue 4,000 + 12,000 = 16,000.
11. It is large and irregular, and the asset lasts for years, so longer-term finance matches it better than trading cash.
12. A start-up needs finance mainly to buy assets and survive until sales build; a mature business more often needs it to expand or replace assets.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a repair capital or revenue expenditure?**
A repair that only restores an asset to working order is revenue expenditure. Spending that improves an asset or extends its life is capital expenditure.

**Why does it matter whether spending is capital or revenue?**
It changes reported profit and assets in the final accounts, and it affects which source of finance is suitable.
