Condensed recall for **1.2 Flooding and flood mitigation** (IB DP Geography, Option A: Freshwater). Fuller explanation is in the [Freshwater study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-freshwater-study-guide/).

## Causes of flooding

| Physical | Human contributors |
|---|---|
| Intense or prolonged rainfall | Deforestation |
| Snowmelt | Urbanisation (impermeable surfaces, drains) |
| Saturated ground | Drainage of wetlands |
| Storm surges at estuaries | Building on floodplains |
| | Poorly maintained defences |

Link causes to the hydrograph: more quick flow gives a shorter lag time and higher peak.

### How each human factor works

- **Deforestation** removes interception and reduces infiltration through root channels, so more rain becomes overland flow; exposed soil also washes into channels and raises the bed, reducing channel capacity.
- **Urbanisation** replaces soil with impermeable surfaces and adds drains that speed water to the river.
- **Wetland drainage** removes natural storage that would hold back flood water and release it slowly.
- **Building on floodplains** does not change the river's behaviour so much as increase **exposure**: more people and property sit in the path of floods.

A **flash flood** rises within hours of intense rain, often in small, steep or urban catchments; a **riverine flood** builds over days in large basins after prolonged rain or snowmelt.

## Flood frequency

The **recurrence interval** estimates how often a flood of a given size is expected on average:

**Recurrence interval = (n + 1) / m**, where n is the number of years of record and m is the rank of the flood (largest = 1).

Worked check: with 49 years of records, the largest flood has a recurrence interval of (49 + 1) / 1 = 50 years, meaning a 1 in 50 (2 per cent) chance in any year. A "100-year flood" has a 1 per cent chance every year: two can happen in consecutive years. Short records and changing land use or climate make these estimates less reliable.

## Impacts

- **Primary:** immediate damage, deaths and injuries.
- **Secondary:** disease, disrupted services, lost livelihoods.
- **Long-term:** insurance costs, mental health effects, migration.
- **Benefits:** floodplain soils can be renewed by deposited silt.

Impacts depend on **vulnerability** as well as flood size: poorer households with fragile housing, no insurance and few savings recover more slowly, and floods in lower-income countries tend to cause more deaths, while floods in higher-income countries tend to cause higher economic losses.

## Mitigation types

| Type | Examples | Strengths | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hard engineering | Dams, embankments and levees, channel straightening, flood walls, storage reservoirs | Effective against a design flood | Costly; can displace risk downstream |
| Soft engineering | Afforestation, wetland and floodplain restoration, washlands, flood warnings, land-use zoning, natural flood management in the upper catchment | Works with natural processes; often cheaper | May be slower; needs land and cooperation |
| Adaptation and preparedness | Flood-proofing buildings, community plans, insurance, evacuation routes | Reduces vulnerability | Does not stop the flood itself |

Modern strategies often combine them in **integrated catchment management**.

**Levee effect:** strong defences can encourage more building behind them, so when a flood exceeds the design standard the damage is greater than if no one had built there.

## Case studies

### Pakistan, 2022

An exceptionally intense monsoon, with rainfall far above normal in Sindh and Balochistan, combined with high glacier melt in the north, flooded a large share of the country; widely reported government estimates put it at roughly a third at the peak. More than 1,700 people died and millions were displaced. Low-lying, flat land drained slowly, so water stood for months, destroying crops and spreading water-borne disease. The disaster showed how poverty, weak drainage and settlement on floodplains magnify a physical event, and it became central to international debate about loss and damage from climate change.

### Room for the River, the Netherlands

After high river levels in the 1990s forced large evacuations, the Dutch government shifted from simply raising dykes to giving rivers more space. The programme lowered floodplains, moved dykes back, created side channels and removed obstacles at many sites along the Rhine and its branches. Evaluation: it reduces flood levels while creating habitat and recreation space, but it required buying land and relocating some farms, which needed negotiation and compensation.

### Slowing the Flow, Pickering, England

In a small North Yorkshire town with a history of flash floods, a conventional flood wall was judged too costly. Instead, partners built leaky woody dams, planted trees and created a bund to store water in the upper catchment. The town was not flooded during heavy rain at the end of 2015, which supporters credit to the scheme. Evaluation: low cost and good for wildlife, but natural flood management works best for small catchments and moderate events and is hard to measure precisely.

## Evaluating a strategy

Compare economic, social, environmental and political dimensions: who pays, who is protected, who is displaced, what happens to habitats, and who decides. Use a flood event you have studied; if you cannot recall exact figures, describe the pattern of impacts and responses rather than guessing numbers.

## Model paragraph

*Compare the effectiveness of hard and soft approaches to flood mitigation.* "Hard engineering, such as raised levees, gives a predictable standard of protection to a specific town, but can pass faster water downstream and encourage building behind the defences. Soft approaches, such as Room for the River, lower flood levels by giving water space, and bring environmental benefits, but they need large areas of land and political agreement. Both are most effective when combined: soft measures reduce peaks across the basin, while hard defences protect the highest-value assets."

## Exam traps

- Treating all floods as purely natural: examiners reward discussion of human contribution.
- Listing strategies without saying who gains and who loses.
- Calling hard engineering always bad and soft always good: evaluate in context.
- Using vague or invented figures for a case study.
- Saying a 100-year flood happens once a century: it is a 1 per cent chance each year.
- Forgetting that floodplain building raises exposure even if it does not change the hydrograph.
