## Values behind conservation

Arguments for conserving biodiversity rest on two kinds of value:

- **Instrumental (use) value**: biodiversity matters because of what it does for people. This includes direct use (food, timber, medicines, crop genes), indirect use (ecosystem services such as pollination, flood control and carbon storage) and economic value from tourism.
- **Intrinsic value**: species and ecosystems matter in themselves, whatever their use to humans.

A further group of values is often called **non-use value**: people value knowing a species exists (existence value) or keeping it for future generations (bequest value).

These map onto environmental value systems from Topic 1. A technocentric view tends to stress instrumental value and argue for conservation where it pays. An ecocentric view stresses intrinsic value and argues for protection even where there is no economic return. Strong answers name the value system behind an argument rather than just listing reasons.

## Who conserves: organisations and agreements

| Type | Examples | Typical strengths | Typical limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Governmental and intergovernmental | National park agencies; the UN Environment Programme (UNEP) | Can make and enforce laws; large budgets; negotiate treaties | Slow; influenced by economic and political interests |
| Non-governmental organisations (NGOs) | WWF, Greenpeace | Act quickly; use media campaigns; raise funds from the public; often work with local communities | No legal power; depend on donations; may pursue one issue |
| Mixed membership | IUCN, whose members include both states and NGOs | Scientific authority; publishes the Red List | Advises rather than enforces |

International agreements set the framework:

- **CITES** (agreed in 1973) controls international trade in listed species through permits.
- The **Convention on Biological Diversity** (opened for signature at the 1992 Rio Earth Summit) commits parties to conserve biodiversity, use it sustainably and share benefits from genetic resources fairly.
- The **Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework** (2022) includes a target to conserve 30% of land and 30% of sea areas by 2030, often called "30 by 30".

Agreements only work through national action, funding and enforcement. A target such as 30 by 30 can also be met with poorly managed "paper parks" that protect little in practice.

## Island biogeography and reserve design

The theory of **island biogeography**, developed by Robert MacArthur and E. O. Wilson in 1967, explains the number of species on an island as a balance between **immigration** of new species and **extinction** of existing ones.

- **Larger islands** hold more species: they have larger populations (lower extinction rates) and more habitat types.
- **Nearer islands** hold more species: colonists arrive more often from the mainland (higher immigration).

Conservationists apply the theory to reserves, which are "islands" of habitat in a sea of farmland or cities. It explains why large reserves, reserves close to other habitat and reserves linked by corridors keep more species.

### Worked example: shape and core area

Two proposed reserves have the same total area of about 28.3 km². Reserve C is a circle of radius 3 km. Reserve S is a square of the same area. Edge effects reach 0.5 km inside each boundary.

```
Reserve C (circle)
total area = π × 3²   = 28.27 km²
core radius = 3 − 0.5 = 2.5 km
core area  = π × 2.5² = 19.63 km²
core share = 19.63 ÷ 28.27 × 100 = 69.4 %

Reserve S (square of equal area)
side       = √28.27  = 5.32 km
core side  = 5.32 − 2 × 0.5 = 4.32 km
core area  = 4.32²   = 18.64 km²   (from unrounded side)
core share = 18.64 ÷ 28.27 × 100 = 65.9 %
```

**Interpretation.** The circle has the shortest boundary for its area, so it keeps the largest core. Real reserves rarely follow ideal shapes: boundaries follow land ownership, rivers and the location of the habitat itself. Use the calculation to support a principle, then acknowledge practical limits.

## Land sharing and land sparing

Conservation has to coexist with food production. Two strategies are debated:

- **Land sparing**: farm a smaller area intensively for high yields, and set aside the land saved as protected habitat.
- **Land sharing**: farm a larger area in a wildlife-friendly way, with lower yields but more habitat within farmland (hedgerows, field margins, agroforestry, less pesticide).

Land sparing can protect species that need large areas of undisturbed habitat, but intensive farming may pollute surrounding habitats, and higher yields can encourage more clearance if profits rise. Land sharing supports species that live alongside people and maintains ecosystem services on farms, but it needs more land for the same food. Many studies suggest the best mix depends on the species and region concerned, so a justified answer weighs both.

## Choosing priorities

Money for conservation is limited, so organisations must choose.

- **Biodiversity hotspots** are regions with very high numbers of endemic species that have already lost much of their original habitat. Prioritising them protects many unique species per unit of spending.
- The **EDGE of Existence** programme run by the Zoological Society of London prioritises species that are **E**volutionarily **D**istinct and **G**lobally **E**ndangered: species with few close relatives, whose loss would remove a large share of unique evolutionary history.
- **Community-based conservation** puts local people at the centre, through shared management, income from tourism or sustainable harvesting. It recognises that conservation fails if local people bear the costs and gain nothing.
- **UNESCO biosphere reserves** combine a strictly protected **core**, a **buffer zone** for research and low-impact use, and a **transition zone** where people live and work sustainably.

## Regeneration and rewilding in practice

**Regeneration** (ecological restoration) returns a degraded ecosystem towards an earlier state: replanting native trees, re-wetting drained peatland, removing invasive species or reconnecting a river to its floodplain. It speeds up the succession studied in Topic 2.

**Rewilding** aims to restore self-sustaining natural processes, often by reintroducing missing species and then reducing human management. The reintroduction of grey wolves to Yellowstone National Park in the United States in 1995 is the best-known example. Researchers have linked wolf return to changes in elk behaviour and numbers and to the recovery of some riverside vegetation, although the size of this trophic cascade is still debated.

### How to evaluate a regeneration project

Use these headings for any named project:

1. **Aim**: which ecosystem, species or process is being restored?
2. **Evidence of success**: species returning, vegetation cover, water quality, ecosystem services.
3. **Time scale**: restoration takes decades; early results can mislead.
4. **Costs and conflicts**: who pays, and who loses (for example, farmers worried about predators)?
5. **Baseline**: which earlier state is the target, and who chose it?
6. **Judgement**: is it worth replicating elsewhere, and under what conditions?

## Model answer: distinguish

**Question.** Distinguish between the roles of governmental organisations and NGOs in conservation. **[3]**

**Answer.** Governmental organisations can pass and enforce laws, such as designating national parks, and fund large programmes from taxation [1]. NGOs have no legal power, so they influence policy through media campaigns, public pressure and fundraising, and can act quickly and locally [1]. Governments therefore protect biodiversity mainly through regulation, whereas NGOs work mainly through persuasion and direct projects [1].

## Common mistakes

- Treating CITES as a ban on all trade. It controls trade through permits; only the most threatened listed species face a near-total ban on commercial trade.
- Saying "large islands have more species because they are bigger" without the mechanism (lower extinction, more habitats).
- Equating rewilding with any tree planting. Rewilding focuses on restoring processes and reducing management.
- Arguing for land sparing or land sharing without saying which species or region the argument applies to.
- Giving an organisation's name with no strength or limitation of its approach.

## Exam technique

- **Justify**: give the reason for a choice, such as why a reserve should be circular, with evidence.
- **Compare and contrast**: write about both approaches in each sentence, using "whereas" or "both".
- **Evaluate**: use the six headings above, then judge.
- **Discuss**: in essays on conservation values, show at least two value systems and reach a conclusion.
