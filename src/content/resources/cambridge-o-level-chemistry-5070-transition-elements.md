This guide covers subtopic **8.4 Transition elements** of Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 (2026–2028 series). The overview in [Periodic Table: Groups and Trends](/resources/periodic-table-groups-and-trends/) lists the properties; this guide gives evidence for each one, shows how to work out oxidation numbers in transition-metal compounds, and links the properties to the places they are tested elsewhere in the syllabus.

## What the syllabus asks

There is one outcome with five parts. Describe the transition elements as metals that:

- (a) have **high densities**
- (b) have **high melting points**
- (c) have **variable oxidation numbers**
- (d) form **coloured compounds**
- (e) often act as **catalysts**, as elements and in compounds

5070 has no tiers, so all five are required. Questions usually ask you to state two or three of these, to compare a transition element with a Group I metal, or to use one property (often colour or oxidation number) to identify a transition element from data.

## Where the transition elements are

The transition elements form the central block of the Periodic Table, between Group II and Group III. The first row lies in Period 4, from scandium to zinc, and includes the metals you meet most often in this syllabus: **chromium, manganese, iron, nickel and copper**, with vanadium appearing through vanadium(V) oxide. They are all metals: shiny, good conductors of heat and electricity, malleable and ductile.

## (a) and (b): high density and high melting point

The clearest way to show these properties is a comparison with Group I.

| Metal | Type | Melting point / °C | Density / g cm⁻³ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sodium | Group I | 98 | 0.97 |
| Potassium | Group I | 63 | 0.86 |
| Iron | Transition | 1538 | 7.87 |
| Nickel | Transition | 1455 | 8.91 |
| Copper | Transition | 1085 | 8.96 |

The transition metals melt at temperatures more than ten times higher on the Celsius scale and are roughly eight to ten times as dense. Their atoms are packed closely in a giant metallic lattice with strong attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons, so a great deal of energy is needed to melt them. A useful exam phrase is "high density and high melting point **compared with Group I metals**": the comparison makes the description precise.

## (c): variable oxidation numbers

A Group I metal always has oxidation number +1 in its compounds and a Group II metal always +2. A transition element can have **more than one** oxidation number, so it forms more than one ion or more than one series of compounds. That is why their names carry **Roman numerals** (subtopic 6.4).

| Element | Compound | Oxidation number of the metal |
|---|---|---|
| Iron | iron(II) sulfate, FeSO₄ | +2 |
| Iron | iron(III) oxide, Fe₂O₃ | +3 |
| Copper | copper(I) oxide, Cu₂O | +1 |
| Copper | copper(II) oxide, CuO | +2 |
| Manganese | manganese(IV) oxide, MnO₂ | +4 |
| Manganese | potassium manganate(VII), KMnO₄ | +7 |
| Vanadium | vanadium(V) oxide, V₂O₅ | +5 |

### Worked example: finding an oxidation number

*Deduce the oxidation number of chromium in Cr₂O₃ and name the compound.*

Use the rules from subtopic 6.4: oxygen in a compound is −2, and the oxidation numbers in a compound add up to zero.

- Three oxygens contribute 3 × (−2) = −6.
- Two chromium atoms must contribute +6, so each is **+3**.
- Name: **chromium(III) oxide**.

For an ion, the total equals the charge. In the manganate(VII) ion, MnO₄⁻: four oxygens give −8, the total must be −1, so manganese is +7. The Roman numeral in the name is the oxidation number you have just calculated, which is a quick check on your answer.

## (d): coloured compounds

Compounds of Group I and Group II metals are white, and their solutions colourless. Transition-element compounds are typically **coloured**, and the colour often depends on the oxidation number.

| Compound or ion | Colour |
|---|---|
| Hydrated copper(II) sulfate | Blue crystals |
| Iron(II) ions in solution | Pale green |
| Iron(III) ions in solution | Yellow-brown |
| Potassium manganate(VII) solution | Purple |
| Copper(II) oxide | Black |

These colours are used in chemical analysis (Topic 12). With aqueous sodium hydroxide, copper(II) ions give a **light blue** precipitate, iron(II) a **green** precipitate, iron(III) a **red-brown** precipitate and chromium(III) a **green** precipitate. Compare the aluminium, calcium and zinc tests, which all give **white** precipitates. Colour is therefore strong evidence that an unknown compound contains a transition element. Iron(II) and iron(III) giving different colours also shows variable oxidation numbers at work. See [Identification of Ions and Gases](/resources/identification-of-ions-and-gases/) for the full test tables.

Zinc sits at the end of the first row but forms only Zn²⁺ and white compounds, so it is a poor choice of example for colour or variable oxidation number.

## (e): catalysts, as elements and in compounds

The syllabus wording distinguishes the **element** acting as a catalyst from a **compound** of the element acting as one. Use examples you already know from 5070:

| Process | Catalyst | Element or compound? |
|---|---|---|
| Haber process: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ | Iron | Element |
| Addition of hydrogen to alkenes | Nickel | Element |
| Contact process: 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃ | Vanadium(V) oxide | Compound |

A catalyst increases the rate of a reaction by lowering the activation energy and is not used up. In industry, this means the same catalyst works for a long time, and reactions reach a useful rate at a lower temperature, saving energy.

## Worked example: identifying a transition element from data

*The table describes three metals. Identify which is a transition element and give two pieces of evidence.*

| Metal | Melting point / °C | Density / g cm⁻³ | Colour of its chloride |
|---|---|---|---|
| P | 98 | 0.97 | White |
| Q | 1538 | 7.87 | Yellow-brown and pale green forms |
| R | 650 | 1.74 | White |

**Answer:** Q. It has a high melting point and high density, and it forms coloured chlorides. Two differently coloured chlorides also suggest two oxidation numbers. (P is sodium and R is magnesium, but you do not need to name them.) Use the data given: quote the property, not just the letter.

## Common mistakes

- **"Transition metals are dense and strong."** Strength is not one of the five properties; give density and melting point.
- **"Transition metals are colourful."** It is their **compounds** that are coloured; most of the metals themselves are silvery-grey (copper is the exception you know).
- **"They are catalysts."** The syllabus says they *often* act as catalysts; and you should be ready to say whether the catalyst is the element or a compound.
- **Reading the Roman numeral as a number of atoms.** Iron(III) oxide is Fe₂O₃, not FeO₃.
- **Using zinc as an example** of a coloured compound or variable oxidation number.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** the transition elements: list properties precisely; one property per mark.
- **Compare** with Group I: write comparatives ("higher melting point than sodium").
- **Deduce** an oxidation number: show the arithmetic with oxygen at −2.
- **Identify** from data: name the letter and quote the evidence from the table.
