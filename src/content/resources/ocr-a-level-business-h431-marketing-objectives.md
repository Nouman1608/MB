## What a marketing objective is

A **marketing objective** is a target for the marketing function to achieve in a stated period. It says what marketing must deliver, not how. The "how" is the marketing strategy and the marketing mix.

Common marketing objectives include:

- **Sales volume or sales value:** sell 90,000 units, or reach £2.4 million of revenue.
- **Market share:** hold 7.5% of the market by value.
- **Market growth or market penetration:** persuade more people in the target group to buy.
- **Brand awareness:** raise the share of target customers who recognise the brand from 25% to 35%.
- **Customer loyalty and retention:** keep 80% of customers from one year to the next.
- **Entering a new market or segment:** launch in a new region or to a new age group.
- **Launching a product:** introduce a new product by a set date.
- **Contribution to profit:** raise the profit margin on a product line, not just its sales.

A well-written objective is **SMART**: specific, measurable, achievable, realistic and time-bound. "Increase sales" is a wish. "Raise online sales revenue from £420,000 to £500,000 by the end of next year" can be checked.

Marketing objectives are not the same as the business's corporate objectives. Corporate objectives, such as profit or survival, belong to the whole business. Marketing objectives are the part of that plan which marketing is responsible for.

## From corporate aim to marketing target

Objectives work as a chain. Each level supports the one above.

1. **Corporate objective:** what the whole business wants, such as a higher profit.
2. **Marketing objective:** the customer and market result needed to achieve it.
3. **Marketing strategy and mix:** the choices about product, price, place and promotion that deliver it.
4. **Targets for teams:** budgets and measures for sales staff, the website and advertising.

### Worked example 1: turning a corporate aim into a market share target

**Case.** Harbrook Bakery is a local bakery chain. All figures are invented. The market for bakery goods in its region is worth £8,000,000 a year and is growing by 4% a year. Harbrook has a 5% share, so its sales are £400,000. The owners' corporate objective is to double profit within two years. Marketing analysis shows that profit will double only if sales reach about £650,000.

**Step 1: forecast the market.** Market value in two years = £8,000,000 × 1.04 × 1.04 = **£8,652,800**.

**Step 2: find the share needed.** £650,000 ÷ £8,652,800 = 7.5% (to one decimal place). A target of **7.5% market share** is therefore consistent with the corporate objective.

**Step 3: state the target.** At 7.5%, sales would be 7.5% × £8,652,800 = **£648,960**.

**Step 4: measure the task.** Growth required = (£648,960 − £400,000) ÷ £400,000 × 100 = **62.24%** over two years. The market grows by about 8.2% over the same period (1.04 squared is 1.0816), so most of the increase must come from winning share, not from the market's growth.

**What this shows.** The marketing objective, "raise market share from 5% to 7.5% in two years", is specific, measurable and time-bound. It also tells managers how big the challenge is. Winning 2.5 percentage points of share from competitors is demanding, so the objective may need to be judged against the budget and the competitors' likely response. This is the sense in which objectives are **important in achieving business objectives**: if the share target is missed, the profit target is likely to be missed too.

## Why clarity matters

The specification asks you to explain the need for clear objectives. Clear objectives:

- **give direction:** every manager knows what marketing is trying to achieve;
- **allow measurement and control:** results can be compared with targets and corrective action taken (for example a variance of £30,000 below target in quarter 2);
- **guide the use of resources:** a business that wants awareness spends differently from one that wants repeat purchase;
- **keep the mix consistent:** a premium image is damaged by heavy discounting, so the price must follow the objective;
- **motivate staff:** a clear, achievable target gives a team something to aim at, especially when linked to rewards;
- **inform stakeholders:** lenders, shareholders and suppliers can judge the plan.

Vague objectives do the opposite. Teams pull in different directions, resources are wasted and success cannot be shown.

## Usefulness and limits

Evaluating usefulness means weighing these benefits against the weaknesses.

| Stakeholder | How objectives are useful | Possible problem |
|---|---|---|
| Owners and shareholders | Show how marketing supports profit and growth | Ambitious targets may encourage risky spending |
| Marketing managers | Direction, a budget case and a way to judge campaigns | Targets can be missed for reasons outside their control |
| Sales and marketing staff | Clear goals; may link to bonuses | Pressure; short-term focus on the number measured |
| Suppliers | Advance warning of volumes to order | Plans change, leaving stock unsold |
| Customers | Objectives such as loyalty can improve service | A sales target can lead to pushy selling |
| Finance and operations | Forecasts for cash flow and capacity | Over-optimistic sales objectives cause shortages |

General limits of marketing objectives:

- They rest on forecasts, which can be wrong.
- Fixed targets can become out of date if the market changes quickly, such as a new competitor or a recession.
- Measuring one target (such as volume) can damage another (such as margin or reputation).
- Poor information leads to objectives that are not realistic.

A balanced evaluation usually concludes that objectives are very useful if they are reviewed regularly and tied to a realistic budget.

## Conflicts between objectives

A business often has several marketing objectives at once, and they can conflict.

### Worked example 2: sales volume against margin

Harbrook sells a loaf box for **£5.00**. The variable cost is **£3.50**, so the contribution is £1.50 per box and the margin is £1.50 ÷ £5.00 = **30%**. Current sales are 40,000 boxes a year, giving a total contribution of 40,000 × £1.50 = **£60,000**.

The marketing team proposes a promotion, cutting the price to **£4.50**, to meet a "gain share" objective.

1. New contribution per box = £4.50 − £3.50 = **£1.00**.
2. New margin = £1.00 ÷ £4.50 = **22.2%**.
3. To earn the same £60,000 total contribution, sales must reach £60,000 ÷ £1.00 = **60,000 boxes**.
4. That is 20,000 more boxes, an increase of **50%**.

The two objectives, "increase market share" and "protect profit margin", pull against each other. A price cut will probably raise volume, but it must raise it by half just to stand still on profit. Whether the cut is sensible depends on how responsive customers are to price and on whether competitors copy it. A manager has to decide which objective has priority. Usually this is settled by looking back at the corporate objective: if the business is aiming at survival and cash, margin may matter more; if it is fighting for a market while it is growing, share may matter more.

## Recommending objectives

The final skill is to **recommend and justify** objectives for a given business. Look at its situation and match the objective to it.

| Situation | Suitable marketing objective | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| New business or product launch | Brand awareness; trial; first-year sales | Nobody knows the product yet |
| Fast-growing market | Market share and growth | Winning customers now is easier than later |
| Mature or saturated market | Customer retention; margin; defending share | New customers are costly to win |
| Declining sales | Reposition or enter a new segment | Existing approach no longer works |
| Cash shortage | Margin and short-term sales | Survival needs cash |
| Strong competition | Differentiation and loyalty | Avoids a price war |

A strong answer proposes two or three linked objectives with numbers and a time period, explains why they fit the business's position, and says what could prevent them being met. For example, for Harbrook: "Raise market share from 5% to 7.5% by the end of year 2 and keep customer retention at 75% or above. Share fits a growing market; retention protects margin because existing customers cost less to serve. The risk is a competitor's price cuts, so Harbrook should review progress every quarter."

## Common mistakes

- Giving the corporate objective (such as profit) and calling it a marketing objective.
- Writing objectives without numbers or dates.
- Listing objectives without saying why they suit the business in the case.
- Treating all objectives as compatible. Growth in share often costs margin.
- Evaluating "usefulness" with a list of advantages only. Add limits and a judgement.

## Exam technique

For "explain", define the term and add one linked point. For "evaluate", give benefits, limits and a conclusion about the importance of the objective to this business. For "recommend and justify", name the objective, give a target and a time scale, and tie the reason to the evidence in the case.
