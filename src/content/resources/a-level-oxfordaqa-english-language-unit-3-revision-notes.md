Unit 3 (Language variation) of OxfordAQA International A-level English Language (9670) is a two-hour,
50-mark exam worth 30% of the International A-level. **Section A, Learning language** (25 marks) gives you
data on early child language acquisition or later teenage language development. **Section B,
International English** (25 marks) sets out an issue about world English or English as a lingua franca for
discussion. In each section you answer one question from a choice of two. For the full explanation, use
the [Unit 3 study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-english-language-unit-3-language-variation-study-guide/).

## Unit 3 at a glance

| Detail | Section A: Learning language (3.3.1) | Section B: International English (3.3.2) |
|---|---|---|
| Marks | 25 | 25 |
| Choice | One question from two | One question from two |
| Stimulus | Data: speech, writing or multimodal texts by native or EAL speakers | An issue for discussion |
| Scope | Early acquisition in young children and later development in teenagers | Established world English and English as a global lingua franca |

The specification says students must show, across the unit: knowledge of the topic area **including
different theories and approaches**; **data analysis** skills; and skills in **academic argument and
evaluation of ideas**.

## Assessment objectives

| AO | Wording | Unit 3 share of the International A-level |
|---|---|---|
| AO1 | Demonstrate understanding of methods, terminology, concepts and issues in English language | 15% |
| AO2 | Analyse how meanings are shaped in a range of texts and data | 9% |
| AO3 | Demonstrate ability to use English in different ways | 6% |

AO1 is the largest objective in Unit 3: accurate concepts, terminology and theory matter most.

## Section A: Learning language — core knowledge

### Stages of early spoken development

| Stage | Typical features |
|---|---|
| Pre-verbal | Cooing, babbling (repeated consonant–vowel syllables) |
| Holophrastic | Single words carrying whole meanings ("milk" = "I want milk") |
| Two-word | Simple combinations with emerging word order ("daddy gone") |
| Telegraphic | Content words with function words and inflections often missing ("want go park") |
| Post-telegraphic | Function words, inflections and more complex clauses emerge |

### Common features to name in data

| Feature | Example |
|---|---|
| Overextension | Calling every four-legged animal "dog" |
| Underextension | Using "car" only for the family car |
| Virtuous error (overgeneralisation) | "I runned", "two mouses": a rule applied too widely |
| Deletion / substitution (phonology) | "nana" for "banana"; "wabbit" for "rabbit" |
| Child-directed speech | Adults' simplified, repetitive, high-pitched speech with questions and recasts |

### Theories and approaches

| Approach | Key idea | Evaluation point |
|---|---|---|
| Behaviourist (Skinner) | Learning through imitation and reinforcement | Does not explain virtuous errors children have never heard |
| Nativist (Chomsky) | An innate capacity for language (a "language acquisition device") | Hard to test; underplays interaction |
| Cognitive (Piaget) | Language follows cognitive development | Explains some timing but not all |
| Interactionist (Bruner; Vygotsky) | Interaction and adult support (scaffolding) drive development | Supported by caregiver-talk data; variation across cultures |

### Later development (teenagers)

Writing and speech in adolescence: growing control of complex sentences and cohesion, wider formal
vocabulary, register shifts between peer and academic contexts, and features of teenage peer talk. For
EAL learners, data may show transfer from a first language and developing control of English
inflections.

## Section B: International English — core knowledge

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| World Englishes | Established varieties such as Indian, Nigerian or Singapore English, with their own norms |
| English as a lingua franca | English used between speakers with different first languages |
| Kachru's circles | Inner (e.g. UK, USA), Outer (e.g. India, Nigeria), Expanding (e.g. China, Brazil) |
| Pidgin and creole | A pidgin is a contact language with no native speakers; a creole develops when it becomes a first language |
| Codeswitching | Moving between languages or varieties within a conversation |
| Standard and prestige | Debates about which norms (if any) should be taught and tested |

### Typical issues set for discussion

- Should international learners aim for British or American norms, or for intelligibility?
- Is the global spread of English a threat to other languages?
- Who "owns" English when most users are non-native speakers?
- Are new varieties "errors" or legitimate developments?

## Worked example: analysing a child's utterance (Section A)

Data: a child aged about 2 years 6 months says, "Daddy goed work. Him coming back?"

- **Grammar:** "goed" is a virtuous error: the regular past-tense *-ed* rule overgeneralised to an
  irregular verb, which suggests the child has internalised the rule rather than imitated it.
- **Telegraphic features:** omission of the preposition *to* and the auxiliary *is*.
- **Pronoun:** "Him" in subject position shows developing case marking.
- **Pragmatics:** a question formed with rising intonation instead of inversion.
- **Theory link:** the virtuous error challenges a purely behaviourist account and is often cited in
  support of rule-learning (nativist) views; an interactionist would ask how the caregiver replies.

## Writing a Section B answer

1. Define the terms in the issue.
2. Set out at least two positions, with named approaches or researchers where you know them.
3. Use examples of real varieties or situations.
4. Evaluate: which argument is stronger, and in what contexts?
5. Conclude with a judgement linked to the question.

## Common mistakes / exam traps

- **Labelling without explaining**: naming a "virtuous error" without saying what rule it shows.
- **Treating theories as right or wrong**: evaluate what each explains and what it misses.
- **Ignoring the data**: Section A answers must analyse the data provided as their starting point.
- **Assuming deficit**: describing world Englishes or EAL data as "mistakes" without considering norms.
- **No argument in Section B**: the question sets out an issue; take a reasoned position.
- **Forgetting teenagers**: Section A covers later development, not only early childhood.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does Unit 3 Section A only test child language?**
No. The specification says it covers early language acquisition in young children and later language
development in teenagers, using data from native speakers or EAL speakers.

**What counts as "international English" in 9670?**
The specification uses it as a label for both established world Englishes and the more recent global
expansion of English as a lingua franca.

**Do I need to quote researchers by name?**
The specification asks for knowledge of different theories and approaches. Naming them accurately helps
AO1, but explaining and evaluating the ideas matters more than the names.

**How is Unit 3 related to the AS course?**
Section B builds on the skills developed in Section B of Unit 2 (Language and society).
