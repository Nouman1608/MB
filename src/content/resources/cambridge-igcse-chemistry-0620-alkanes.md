This guide covers subtopic **11.4 Alkanes** of Topic 11, Organic chemistry, in the Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620 syllabus for 2026–2028. Outcomes 1 and 2 are **Core**; outcomes 3 and 4 are **Supplement**, examined only on the Extended papers, and are labelled *(Extended)* below. For quick recall use the [alkanes revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-alkanes-revision-notes/), and practise with the [alkanes practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-alkanes-practice/). Alkanes are the main components of petroleum, covered in the [fuels study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-fuels/).

## What 11.4 asks you to do

- **Core 1:** state that the bonding in alkanes is **single covalent** and that alkanes are **saturated hydrocarbons**.
- **Core 2:** describe alkanes as **generally unreactive**, except for **combustion** and **substitution by chlorine**.
- **Supplement 3:** state that in a **substitution reaction** one atom or group of atoms is replaced by another atom or group of atoms.
- **Supplement 4:** describe the substitution of alkanes by chlorine as a **photochemical** reaction, with **ultraviolet light** providing the **activation energy, Ea**, and draw the structural or displayed formulae of the products, **limited to monosubstitution**.

From 11.1 and 11.2 you also need the general formula **CₙH₂ₙ₊₂**, and the names and displayed formulae of methane and ethane (Core) and of unbranched alkanes up to four carbons (Extended).

## Structure: single bonds and saturation

Every carbon atom in an alkane forms **four single covalent bonds**, to other carbon atoms or to hydrogen atoms. Because all carbon–carbon bonds are single, alkanes are **saturated**: no more atoms can be added to the molecule without first removing some.

| Alkane | Molecular formula | Structural formula |
|---|---|---|
| methane | CH₄ | CH₄ |
| ethane | C₂H₆ | CH₃CH₃ |
| propane | C₃H₈ | CH₃CH₂CH₃ |
| butane | C₄H₁₀ | CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃ |

Check any formula against CₙH₂ₙ₊₂: for n = 3, 2n + 2 = 8, giving C₃H₈.

A displayed formula shows every atom and every bond. For ethane:

```
    H   H
    |   |
H — C — C — H
    |   |
    H   H
```

## Why alkanes are unreactive

Alkanes have **no double bond** and no reactive functional group. The C–C and C–H single bonds are strong, and there is nowhere for another molecule to add on. That is why alkanes do not decolourise aqueous bromine at room temperature, which is the basis of the test in 11.5 that distinguishes them from alkenes. The two reactions the syllabus names are exceptions that need a large energy input to start: a flame for combustion, ultraviolet light for substitution.

## Combustion

Alkanes burn in a plentiful supply of oxygen to form **carbon dioxide and water**, releasing a large amount of energy. This is why the alkane fractions from petroleum are used as fuels.

```
methane:  CH₄  + 2O₂ → CO₂  + 2H₂O
propane:  C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O
```

### Worked example 1: balancing the combustion of butane

1. Write the formulae: C₄H₁₀ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O.
2. Balance carbon: 4 C on the left, so **4CO₂**.
3. Balance hydrogen: 10 H on the left, so **5H₂O**.
4. Count oxygen on the right: (4 × 2) + 5 = 13 atoms, so 6½ O₂.
5. Remove the fraction by doubling everything:

```
2C₄H₁₀ + 13O₂ → 8CO₂ + 10H₂O
```

Final check: C 8 = 8; H 20 = 20; O 26 = 16 + 10. Always balance C, then H, then O last, because oxygen appears in two products.

If the oxygen supply is limited, **incomplete combustion** produces carbon monoxide and soot (carbon) instead of carbon dioxide; this links to the air-pollution content of Topic 10. Unless a question mentions limited oxygen, write complete combustion.

## Substitution by chlorine *(Extended)*

In a **substitution reaction**, one atom or group of atoms is **replaced** by another atom or group of atoms. With chlorine, one hydrogen atom on the alkane is replaced by one chlorine atom, and the hydrogen atom that leaves joins the other chlorine atom as **hydrogen chloride**.

```
CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl
methane + chlorine → chloromethane + hydrogen chloride
```

The reaction is **photochemical**: it needs **ultraviolet (UV) light**, which supplies the **activation energy** needed to start it. Methane and chlorine kept in the dark do not react.

Notice the contrast with the alkenes in 11.5. Alkenes undergo **addition**, giving a single product; substitution always gives **two** products, the substituted alkane and hydrogen chloride.

### Drawing the product: monosubstitution

The syllabus limits you to **monosubstitution**, meaning exactly **one** hydrogen atom is replaced. Chloromethane:

```
    H
    |
H — C — Cl
    |
    H
```

Ethane gives chloroethane, CH₃CH₂Cl. All six hydrogen atoms in ethane are equivalent, so there is only one possible monosubstituted product:

```
C₂H₆ + Cl₂ → C₂H₅Cl + HCl
```

### Worked example 2: monosubstitution of propane

*Question: Draw the structural formulae of the two organic products that could form when one hydrogen atom in propane is replaced by chlorine.* [2]

Propane, CH₃CH₂CH₃, has hydrogen atoms in two different positions: on an end carbon or on the middle carbon.

- Replacing an end hydrogen gives **CH₂ClCH₂CH₃** (1-chloropropane).
- Replacing a middle hydrogen gives **CH₃CHClCH₃** (2-chloropropane).

Both have the molecular formula C₃H₇Cl; they are structural isomers. Writing CH₃CH₂CH₂Cl is the same molecule as CH₂ClCH₂CH₃, read from the other end, so it is not a third product. The names are given for clarity: the syllabus outcome asks you to draw the formulae.

## Annotated model answer

**Question:** Methane reacts with chlorine. State the condition needed and explain why it is needed. Write an equation for the reaction. [3] *(Extended)*

**Answer:** Ultraviolet light is needed [1]. It provides the activation energy for the reaction to start [1]. CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl [1].

*Why it scores:* the condition is specific ("ultraviolet light", not just "light" or "heat"), the explanation uses the term **activation energy**, and the equation shows **monosubstitution** with HCl as the second product.

## Common mistakes

- **Calling alkanes unsaturated**, or confusing "saturated" with "contains only hydrogen and carbon". Saturated means all C–C bonds are single.
- **Saying alkanes are completely unreactive.** The syllabus wording is *generally* unreactive, with two exceptions.
- **Forgetting HCl** in a substitution equation, or writing H₂ as a product.
- **Writing an addition product** such as CH₄Cl₂ for methane and chlorine. Carbon cannot form five bonds; one hydrogen must leave.
- **Replacing more than one hydrogen** when the question asks for the monosubstituted product.
- **Giving "light" or "heat" as the condition** instead of ultraviolet light.
- **Unbalanced oxygen** in combustion equations, especially for alkanes with an even number of carbons, which need doubling.

## Exam technique

- **State:** short and exact – "single covalent bonds", "saturated", "ultraviolet light".
- **Describe** (properties): "generally unreactive; they undergo combustion and substitution with chlorine". Name both exceptions.
- **Describe** (substitution) *(Extended)*: give the reagent, the condition (UV light), the products and the meaning of substitution.
- **Draw:** in a displayed formula, every bond must be shown, including each C–H; in a structural formula such as CH₃CH₂Cl, the arrangement must be unambiguous.
- **Explain:** link structure to behaviour – no C=C bond, so nothing can add; UV supplies Ea, so the reaction starts.
