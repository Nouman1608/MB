These questions cover Topic 14, Economic transition (sections 14.1 to 14.4), of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026. The topic is one of four options in Paper 4 Advanced Human Geography Options, where each option has a 10-mark structured question and a choice of two 20-mark essays. All countries, regions, firms and data below are fictional unless a question asks you to use your own case study.

## Questions

**1.** Define the term "quaternary sector". **[2]**

**2.** Explain the difference between outsourcing and offshoring. **[3]**

**3.** The table shows employment by sector in Velmara, a fictional country (% of workforce).

| Year | Primary | Secondary | Tertiary | Quaternary |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1990 | 58 | 18 | 21 | 3 |
| 2005 | 41 | 27 | 28 | 4 |
| 2020 | 24 | 29 | 39 | 8 |

**(a)** Describe the changes in Velmara's employment structure between 1990 and 2020. **[3]**
**(b)** Suggest reasons for these changes. **[4]**

**4.** The table shows indicators for four fictional countries.

| Country | GNI per capita (PPP, US$) | HDI | Gini coefficient | Population in multidimensional poverty (%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Arvenia | 42,000 | 0.79 | 0.48 | 6 |
| Bostrel | 18,500 | 0.81 | 0.29 | 2 |
| Calmora | 6,200 | 0.58 | 0.41 | 31 |
| Dunesse | 2,900 | 0.49 | 0.36 | 52 |

**(a)** Identify the country that ranks higher on HDI than on GNI per capita. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate how many times greater Arvenia's GNI per capita is than Dunesse's. Show your working. **[2]**
**(c)** Using the table, explain why GNI per capita alone may give a misleading picture of wellbeing. **[4]**

**5.** Explain two backwash effects that may affect a peripheral region. **[4]**

**6.** The table shows GDP per capita (US$) in two regions of Dravonia, a fictional country.

| Year | Kessa Valley (core) | Northern Uplands (periphery) |
|---|---|---|
| 2000 | 12,400 | 3,100 |
| 2010 | 19,800 | 4,500 |
| 2020 | 26,600 | 7,000 |

**(a)** Calculate the ratio of core to periphery GDP per capita for each year, to one decimal place. **[3]**
**(b)** Using your answers and the table, state whether the regions diverged or converged. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest two reasons for the change between 2010 and 2020. **[4]**

**7.** Explain the factors that affect the spatial structure of transnational corporations (TNCs). **[6]**

**8.** Read the source.

> Orrin Apparel (a fictional TNC) has its headquarters and design studio in a high-income country. It owns no garment factories. Instead it places orders with 64 contract factories in three lower-income countries in Asia, where wages are a small fraction of those at headquarters. A regional office in a large Asian port city manages quality control and shipping. Last year it moved 15 orders from one supplier country, where the minimum wage had risen, to another.

**(a)** Explain how the source illustrates the new international division of labour (NIDL). **[4]**
**(b)** Suggest two impacts of Orrin Apparel's operations on a supplier country. **[6]**

**9.** Explain two factors that helped the emergence of newly industrialised countries (NICs). **[4]**

**10.** With reference to one country you have studied, assess the difficulties faced in trying to reduce regional disparities. **[20]**

**11.** "TNCs benefit their home countries more than the countries where their goods are made." With reference to one TNC you have studied, how far do you agree? **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** Economic activity based on knowledge and information [1], such as research and development, ICT or consultancy [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition earns the second mark only with an example that is clearly quaternary; "banking" alone is often read as tertiary.

**2.** Outsourcing is contracting work to another firm [1]. Offshoring is moving work to another country [1]. They can happen separately: a firm can offshore to its own subsidiary abroad without outsourcing [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Explain the difference" needs a contrast; two separate definitions with no linking point usually stop at 2.

**3. (a)** Primary employment fell from 58% to 24%, a fall of 34 percentage points [1]. Tertiary rose from 21% to 39% and became the largest sector [1]. Secondary rose from 18% to 29%, mostly by 2005, then levelled off (only 2 points after 2005); quaternary rose from 3% to 8% [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Mechanisation of farming reduced the labour needed on the land [1]. Manufacturing investment, perhaps FDI, created factory jobs that absorbed rural migrants [1]. Rising incomes increased demand for services such as retail and health [1]. Better education and ICT allowed knowledge-based quaternary jobs to grow [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* In "describe" answers, quote the data with units and dates; in "suggest", link each reason to a specific change in the table.

**4. (a)** Bostrel [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 42,000 ÷ 2,900 [1] = 14.5 times [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Arvenia has the highest GNI per capita but a lower HDI than Bostrel [1]. Arvenia's Gini coefficient (0.48) is the highest, so its income is shared unequally [1]. Arvenia has 6% in multidimensional poverty against Bostrel's 2%, despite GNI per capita about 2.3 times higher [1]. GNI per capita is an average, so it hides distribution and says nothing directly about health or education [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Using the table" means each point must quote a figure; a general critique of GNI with no data rarely gains more than 1.

**5.** Out-migration of young and skilled workers to the core [1] leaves the periphery with an ageing workforce and fewer entrepreneurs [1]. Capital flows to the core, where returns are higher [1], so peripheral firms struggle to raise investment and services decline [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each effect needs a consequence for the periphery; naming "brain drain" alone gives only half the credit.

**6. (a)** 2000: 12,400 ÷ 3,100 = 4.0 [1]; 2010: 19,800 ÷ 4,500 = 4.4 [1]; 2020: 26,600 ÷ 7,000 = 3.8 [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** The regions diverged between 2000 and 2010, as the ratio rose from 4.0 to 4.4 [1]. They converged in relative terms between 2010 and 2020 (ratio fell to 3.8), although the absolute gap widened from 15,300 to 19,600 US dollars [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Rising land and labour costs or congestion in Kessa Valley may have pushed firms to the periphery (spread effects) [1], so new jobs raised incomes there [1]. Government regional policy, such as new transport links or tax incentives, may have attracted investment to the Northern Uplands [1], increasing growth there faster than in the core [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers notice that a falling ratio and a widening dollar gap can both be true, and say which one they mean by "convergence".

**7.** Lower labour and land costs pull routine production to lower-income countries [1]. Large markets attract production or sales offices inside them to avoid tariffs and transport costs [1]. Tax incentives and export processing zones offered by governments attract investment [1]. Containerisation and ICT allow a headquarters to coordinate distant sites [1]. Headquarters and research stay where skilled labour and finance are concentrated, usually in the home country [1]. This creates a hierarchy: headquarters, regional offices in world cities, and production sites in lower-cost countries [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* The question asks about spatial structure, so the top marks go to answers that say where each function locates and why.

**8. (a)** Design and control stay at headquarters in a high-income country [1]. Labour-intensive garment assembly is done in lower-income countries [1]. The location of production is driven by low wages, shown by the large wage gap [1]. Orders moved when the minimum wage rose, showing that production chases low labour costs [1]. **[4]**
**(b)** Employment: contract factories create jobs [1], often for young women moving from rural areas [1], and wages can be higher than in farming [1]. Instability: the firm owns no factories, so it can move orders quickly [1], as it did when wages rose [1], leaving workers exposed to sudden job losses [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* "Suggest" answers must use the source: the fact that Orrin owns no factories is the key detail for judging how secure the jobs are.

**9.** Export-oriented industrialisation directed by the state [1], for example South Korea's five-year plans and support for chaebol [1]. Heavy investment in education [1] produced a skilled, productive workforce that attracted investment [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming a real NIC and one specific policy turns a general factor into a developed explanation.

**10.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point. These indicative points show what a top-level answer could include.

*Introduction:* defines regional disparity and core-periphery [1]; names a country and policy, e.g. China's Western Development strategy, proposed in 1999 [1].

*Scale of the problem:* coastal Special Economic Zones from 1980 gave the east an initial advantage [1]; the western share of national output fell from about 20.9% (1990) to 17.1% (2000) [1].

*Physical difficulties:* mountainous and high-altitude terrain raises construction costs [1], shown by the Qinghai-Tibet Railway crossing the Tanggula Pass at 5,072 m [1]; distance from ports raises transport costs for western firms [1].

*Economic difficulties:* cumulative causation continues to favour the core [1]; young workers migrate to coastal cities (backwash) [1]; foreign investment in some western provinces stayed weak [1]; better links can let coastal firms out-compete western ones [1].

*Political and social difficulties:* in Tibet and Xinjiang critics argue infrastructure brings Han in-migration and resource extraction [1]; the benefits may not reach local groups [1].

*Evidence of partial success:* growth of Chongqing and Chengdu [1]; gas and electricity transfer projects link western resources to eastern demand [1].

*Evaluation:* Guizhou still had the fourth-lowest provincial GDP per capita in 2020 [1]; relative gaps are harder to close than absolute poverty [1]; judges which difficulty is greatest, e.g. backwash over terrain [1]; distinguishes viewpoints of central government and local critics [1]; a supported conclusion that answers "assess" [1]. **[20]**
*Examiner insight:* The higher levels need a ranked judgement of the difficulties, not a list of projects with dates.

**11.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point. These indicative points show what a top-level answer could include.

*Introduction:* defines TNC and home and host country [1]; names a TNC, e.g. Nike [1]; states a line of argument [1].

*Benefits to the home country:* headquarters near Beaverton, Oregon, employs about 10,500 people in management, design and marketing [1]; high-value functions and profits stay with the brand [1]; research and design jobs are skilled and well paid [1].

*Benefits to host countries:* Vietnam, Indonesia and China made about 51%, 28% and 17% of Nike Brand footwear in fiscal 2025 [1], so the work supports large numbers of factory jobs [1]; export earnings and skills transfer [1]; some FDI, e.g. the owned Air-Sole plant in Dong Nai Province, Vietnam [1].

*Costs to host countries:* low wages and criticism of factory conditions in the 1990s [1]; contracts can move as costs rise [1]; most value added leaves the country [1].

*Wider effects on the home country:* loss of manufacturing jobs as production moved abroad [1]; consumers gain from lower prices [1].

*Evaluation:* the NIDL explains the split of functions [1]; benefits differ by group: shareholders, designers, factory workers [1]; host countries such as South Korea and Taiwan later moved up the value chain [1]; judgement on the balance of benefits [1]; a supported conclusion [1]. **[20]**
*Examiner insight:* Case-study detail earns most credit when it is used to weigh home against host benefits, not when it is given as a company history.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Quoting data without units, years or percentage points.
- Calculating a ratio the wrong way round, or not showing working.
- Treating a falling ratio as proof that a gap in dollars has narrowed.
- Writing about a TNC's products and history instead of where its functions are and why.
- Assuming a TNC owns the factories that make its goods.
- Listing regional policy projects with no judgement on whether disparities fell.
- Ignoring the source in "suggest" questions and writing a learned answer instead.
- Ending an essay without a conclusion that answers "how far" or "assess".
