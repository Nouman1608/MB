This guide covers subtopic **9.1 Properties of metals** of Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 (2026–2028 series). The overview in [Metal Properties and Reactivity](/resources/metal-properties-and-reactivity/) treats this alongside the reactivity series; here the focus is on the comparison with non-metals, the structural reasons behind each physical property, and writing correct products and equations for the three types of chemical reaction the syllabus names.

## What the syllabus asks

1. **Compare** the general physical properties of metals and non-metals, including thermal conductivity, electrical conductivity, malleability and ductility, and melting points and boiling points.
2. **Describe** the general chemical properties of metals, limited to their reactions with dilute acids, cold water and steam, and oxygen.

Subtopic 9.4 asks you to rank metals and explain the order using the reactivity series. Here the emphasis is on what the reactions are, what forms and what you observe.

## Physical properties: the comparison

| Property | Metals (general) | Non-metals (general) |
|---|---|---|
| Thermal conductivity | Good | Poor (insulators) |
| Electrical conductivity | Good | Poor (except graphite) |
| Malleability and ductility | Malleable and ductile | Brittle when solid |
| Melting and boiling points | Usually high | Usually low (except giant covalent: diamond, graphite, silicon) |
| Appearance | Shiny when freshly cut or polished | Usually dull |

"Compare" means the answer must mention **both** groups. "Metals are good conductors" is a description; "metals are good conductors of electricity but most non-metals are poor conductors" is a comparison.

**Malleable** means the material can be hammered or pressed into shape without breaking. **Ductile** means it can be drawn out into wires. A solid non-metal such as sulfur shatters or crumbles instead.

## Why metals behave like this

These properties come from **metallic bonding** (subtopic 2.7): a giant lattice of positive ions held together by electrostatic attraction to a "sea" of delocalised electrons.

- **Electrical conductivity:** the delocalised electrons move through the lattice when a voltage is applied, carrying charge. This happens in the solid and in the liquid.
- **Thermal conductivity:** the mobile delocalised electrons transfer energy quickly through the metal.
- **Malleability and ductility:** the ions are arranged in layers. When a force is applied, the layers slide over each other, but the delocalised electrons keep attracting the ions in their new positions, so the metal changes shape instead of breaking.
- **High melting and boiling points:** the attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons is strong and acts throughout the giant lattice, so a lot of energy is needed to separate the particles.

Most non-metals are simple molecular substances (sulfur, iodine, chlorine, oxygen). They have no delocalised electrons, so they do not conduct; and only weak forces act between their molecules, so they melt and boil at low temperatures.

### The exceptions examiners use

- **Mercury** is a metal but a liquid at r.t.p. (melting point −39 °C).
- **Sodium and potassium** are metals with low melting points (98 °C and 63 °C).
- **Graphite** is a non-metal that conducts electricity.
- **Diamond** is a non-metal with a very high melting point, because it is a giant covalent structure.

The syllabus says "general" properties, so the general rule is what you state; the exceptions matter when a question gives data and asks you to classify an element.

### Worked example: classifying from data

*Element A melts at 1085 °C, conducts electricity as a solid and can be drawn into wire. Element B melts at 115 °C, does not conduct and crumbles when hit. Element C conducts electricity, but flakes apart instead of bending when pressed and does not melt below 3000 °C. Classify each and justify.*

- **A is a metal:** high melting point, conducts, ductile. (It is copper.)
- **B is a non-metal:** low melting point, does not conduct, brittle. (It is sulfur.)
- **C is a non-metal:** conduction alone is not enough evidence. It is not malleable, and a non-metal with a giant covalent structure can have a very high melting point and still conduct. (It is graphite.)

The key skill is to weigh **all** the evidence rather than classify from conductivity alone.

## Chemical properties: three reactions

### 1. With dilute acids

```
metal + dilute acid → salt + hydrogen
Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂
Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂
Fe + 2HCl → FeCl₂ + H₂
```

**Observations:** effervescence (fizzing); the metal gets smaller and may disappear; the mixture gets warm. Test the gas with a lighted splint: hydrogen burns with a squeaky "pop". The salt is named from the acid: hydrochloric gives chlorides, sulfuric gives sulfates. Copper, silver and gold do not react with dilute acids.

### 2. With cold water and with steam

```
cold water: metal + water → metal hydroxide + hydrogen
            Ca + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + H₂
steam:      metal + steam → metal oxide + hydrogen
            Mg + H₂O → MgO + H₂
```

The most reactive metals (potassium, sodium, calcium) react with cold water to form the **hydroxide**. Magnesium reacts only very slowly with cold water but quickly when heated in **steam**, forming the **oxide**. Zinc and iron also react with steam when heated, forming their oxides and hydrogen. Copper does not react with water or steam.

**Rule worth memorising:** cold water gives a hydroxide; steam gives an oxide. A very common error is to write magnesium hydroxide as the product with steam.

### 3. With oxygen

```
metal + oxygen → metal oxide
2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO
2Cu + O₂ → 2CuO
```

| Metal heated in air | Observation | Product |
|---|---|---|
| Magnesium | Burns with a bright white flame | White magnesium oxide powder |
| Iron (wool) | Glows and gives off sparks | Iron oxide |
| Copper | Does not burn; surface turns black | Black copper(II) oxide |
| Gold | No change | None |

Metal oxides are **basic**: they react with acids to form a salt and water (subtopic 7.2). This is a chemical property that separates metals from non-metals, whose oxides are generally acidic.

### Worked example: predicting products

*Predict the products of (a) calcium with dilute hydrochloric acid; (b) zinc heated in steam; (c) sodium with cold water.*

- (a) Calcium chloride and hydrogen: Ca + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂.
- (b) Zinc oxide and hydrogen: Zn + H₂O → ZnO + H₂ (steam gives the oxide).
- (c) Sodium hydroxide and hydrogen: 2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂ (cold water gives the hydroxide).

## Common mistakes

- **Describing only metals** when the question says compare.
- **"Metals conduct because electrons move" without "delocalised".** The word matters in 5070 answers.
- **Writing "metal + acid → salt + water".** That is the reaction of a base; metals give hydrogen.
- **Hydroxide with steam, oxide with cold water.** It is the other way round.
- **Saying copper reacts with dilute acid.** It does not.
- **Classifying by conductivity alone**: graphite is the trap.

## Exam technique

- **Compare**: write a sentence with "but" or "whereas" that names both metals and non-metals.
- **Describe** a reaction: give the products **and** at least one observation.
- **Explain** a physical property: name the particle responsible (delocalised electrons, layers of ions).
- Equations: check the formula of the salt from the ion charges before balancing.
