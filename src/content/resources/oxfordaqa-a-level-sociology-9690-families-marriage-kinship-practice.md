## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define what is meant by a **horizontally extended family**. **[2]**

**2.** Define what is meant by a **matrilineal** family system. **[2]**

**3.** Outline **one** difference between a family and a household. **[2]**

**4.** Outline what is meant by **serial monogamy**. **[2]**

**5.** Identify and briefly explain **two** reasons why a single-parent family may be formed. **[4]**

**6.** Identify and briefly explain **two** ways in which communes differ from nuclear families. **[4]**

**7.** Identify and briefly explain **two** of the dimensions Therborn uses to compare family systems.
**[4]**

### Section B: longer questions

**8.** Explain **two** reasons why people may live alone rather than in a family. **[8]**

**9.** Explain **two** ways in which patriarchal families differ from matriarchal or matrilineal
families. **[8]**

**10.** Explain **two** problems with the claim that the nuclear family is universal. **[8]**

**11.** Using examples from a society you know, evaluate the view that arranged marriages are being
replaced by love marriages. **[20]**

**12.** Evaluate the usefulness of Therborn's idea of family systems for understanding family life
in different societies. **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** A family extended within the same generation [1], for example brothers living together with
their wives and children, or cousins sharing a household [1]. **[2]**

**2.** A system in which descent and inheritance are traced through the female line [1], for example
property passing from mother to daughter, as among the Khasi of north-east India [1]. **[2]**

**3.** A family is a group linked by kinship (blood, marriage or adoption) [1], whereas a household is
people sharing accommodation and daily life, who need not be related, such as flatmates [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Having a sequence of marriages, but only one spouse at any one time [1], with earlier
marriages ended by divorce or death before remarriage [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Two reasons, 2 marks each (identify [1], explain [1]):
- Divorce or separation [1]: one parent, often the mother, keeps day-to-day care of the children [1].
- Death of a partner [1]: widowhood leaves the surviving parent raising children alone [1].
- Birth outside a couple [1]: a mother may choose, or be left, to raise a child without a partner [1].
- Migration of one parent for work [1]: the remaining parent may function as a lone parent for long
  periods [1].
**[4]**

**6.** Two ways, 2 marks each:
- Shared child-rearing [1]: in communes such as the kibbutz, children were cared for collectively
  in children's houses rather than only by parents [1].
- Shared property [1]: income and resources belong to the group, not to a couple's private
  household [1].
- Size and membership [1]: membership is based on choice and shared beliefs, not only kinship [1].
**[4]**

**7.** Two dimensions, 2 marks each:
- Patriarchy [1]: the power of fathers and husbands over wives and children, which Therborn found
  declined at different speeds in different regions [1].
- Marriage [1]: who marries, at what age, and whether marriage can be ended, which varies between
  systems [1].
- Fertility [1]: the number of children born, which fell in most systems across the twentieth
  century [1].
**[4]**

**8.** Two reasons, 4 marks each (identify [1], explain [1], develop [1], evidence or example [1]):
- **Choice and individualisation** [1]: rising incomes allow people to afford their own homes [1],
  and individualisation means people prioritise independence and personal development [1];
  Klinenberg's *Going Solo* (2012) found many solo dwellers chose it and remained socially active [1].
- **Life-course events** [1]: divorce, separation or widowhood can leave adults alone [1]; longer
  life expectancy means more older people, especially women, outlive partners [1]; for example,
  widowed older women make up a large share of one-person households in ageing societies [1].
- Credit: delayed marriage, migration for work or study, housing policy.
**[8]**

**9.** Two ways, 4 marks each:
- **Authority** [1]: in patriarchal families men, often the eldest male, make major decisions [1];
  in matriarchal families women hold the main authority [1], though in many matrilineal societies
  authority still lies with a mother's brother, so the contrast is less sharp than it seems [1].
- **Descent and residence** [1]: patriarchal families are usually patrilineal and patrilocal, with
  wives joining the husband's household [1]; matrilineal systems pass property through women and
  may keep daughters in the maternal home [1], as with the Mosuo "walking marriage", where partners
  live apart in their mothers' households [1].
**[8]**

**10.** Two problems, 4 marks each:
- **Communes and collective child-rearing** [1]: Murdock (1949) claimed the nuclear family was
  universal [1], but in kibbutzim children were raised largely in children's houses [1], showing that
  its functions can be performed by other institutions [1].
- **Cultural variation** [1]: matrilineal systems such as the Mosuo separate partners' households [1],
  so the co-resident couple with children is not found everywhere [1]; Therborn's comparative work
  also shows multiple family systems rather than one model [1].
- Credit: rise of single-parent families and living alone; extended family dominance in many
  societies.
**[8]**

**11.** Levels of response:
- 17–20: detailed, accurate knowledge; sustained application to a named society; balanced evaluation
  with a justified conclusion.
- 13–16: good knowledge and application; some evaluation.
- 9–12: reasonable knowledge; limited application or evaluation.
- 5–8: basic, mainly descriptive.
- 1–4: fragments or little relevance.

Indicative content. *For the view:* individualisation means people value personal choice in
partners; urbanisation and migration weaken parental control; women's education and employment
give young women more independence; global media spread ideals of romantic love; dating apps
allow partner choice beyond the family. *Against the view:* arranged marriage remains widespread
in parts of South Asia and the Middle East and among diaspora communities; many arranged marriages
now include consent, veto and meeting before marriage, so the form adapts rather than disappears;
"love" marriages are still filtered by class, caste, religion and ethnicity; families often stay
involved in approving love matches. *Application:* reward accurate examples of marriage practice
from a named society. *Conclusion:* reward a supported judgement, for example that a hybrid
"semi-arranged" pattern is growing rather than one type replacing the other. **[20]**

**12.** Levels of response as for Question 11.

Indicative content. *Strengths:* Therborn compares family systems across all world regions,
avoiding ethnocentric focus on Western families; his dimensions (patriarchy, marriage, fertility)
let students compare their own society systematically; he shows how religion, law and colonial
history shape families; he documents the uneven decline of patriarchy, linking families to power;
he challenges both Murdock's universal nuclear family and the idea that all families converge on
the Western model. *Limitations:* broad regional systems can hide large differences within a
region, for instance between urban and rural families or social classes; a macro-level approach says
little about how people experience family life, which interpretivists and Smart's idea of personal
life stress; systems change, so classifications may date; feminists may argue declining formal
patriarchy does not mean equality in domestic labour. *Conclusion:* reward a supported judgement,
for example that Therborn is very useful for comparison but needs micro-level studies alongside.
**[20]**
