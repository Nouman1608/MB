## Two groups of error

A trial balance lists every ledger balance in a debit column and a credit column. If the totals agree, the arithmetic of double entry holds. It does not show that every entry is right. The specification does not name error types, so the usual names below are labels only. In an answer, describe the error in words as well.

**Errors that do not affect the balancing.** A debit and a credit of the same amount are still posted, so the totals still agree.

| Error | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Omission | A transaction is left out completely | A credit sale of 260 to M. Joshi is never recorded |
| Commission | Right type of account, wrong account | A receipt of 340 is credited to S. Okonkwo instead of S. Okoro |
| Principle | Item put in the wrong class of account | A delivery van costing 9,500 is debited to motor expenses |
| Original entry | Wrong figure used in both entries | An invoice for 1,260 is recorded as 1,620 in both accounts |
| Compensating | Two errors of equal size on opposite sides cancel | Wages overcast by 400 and rent received overcast by 400 |
| Complete reversal | Correct accounts, each on the wrong side | A receipt of 520 debited to the customer and credited to bank |

**Errors that do affect the balancing.** The two sides of the trial balance no longer agree.

- A single-sided entry: only the debit or only the credit is posted.
- Different amounts on the two sides: debit 380, credit 308.
- Both entries on the same side: two debits, or two credits.
- An addition error (casting error) in an account, or a balance carried down wrongly.
- A balance copied to the trial balance wrongly or on the wrong side.

**Method in steps for any error.** Work out what was done. Work out what should have been done. Then write the entry that fixes the difference.

## The suspense account

A suspense account is a temporary ledger account. If the trial balance does not agree, the difference goes into it so the totals match for now. The aim is to find the errors, correct them and clear the account to nil. A trial balance with a higher credit total gets the difference as a **debit** in suspense. A higher debit total gets a **credit**.

**Worked example.** Kelsby Bakery's trial balance at 30 June 2026 is below.

| Account | Dr | Cr |
|---|---:|---:|
| Premises | 52,000 | |
| Ovens and equipment | 6,800 | |
| Inventory at 1 July 2025 | 8,400 | |
| Purchases | 41,200 | |
| Rent | 308 | |
| Wages | 12,600 | |
| Trade receivables | 6,900 | |
| Bank | 4,120 | |
| Drawings | 9,000 | |
| Sales | | 74,000 |
| Trade payables | | 6,200 |
| Loan | | 15,000 |
| Capital | | 45,750 |
| Discount received | | 710 |
| **Totals** | **141,328** | **141,660** |

The credit side is 332 higher, so 332 is debited to a suspense account. The totals then both read 141,660. These errors are found.

1. A credit sale of 450 to Ellis Ltd was credited to sales. The debit in Ellis's account was never posted.
2. Rent of 380 was paid by bank. The bank entry is right, but the rent account was debited with 308.
3. Discount received of 95 was posted to the debit side of the discount received account.
4. A computer costing 1,200 was debited to purchases.

Errors 1 to 3 affect the trial balance. Error 4 does not: it is an error of principle.

Error 1 is a single-sided entry. Debit Trade receivables (Ellis Ltd) 450 and credit Suspense 450. Error 2 is a different-amounts error. Debit Rent 72 and credit Suspense 72. Error 3 puts a credit item on the debit side, so the account is wrong by twice 95. Debit Suspense 190 and credit Discount received 190. For error 4, debit Office equipment 1,200 and credit Purchases 1,200. Suspense is not involved.

| Suspense account | | | |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Difference per trial balance | 332 | Trade receivables (Ellis Ltd) | 450 |
| Discount received | 190 | Rent | 72 |
| | **522** | | **522** |

The account clears to nil, which suggests all the errors have been found. A suspense account that does not clear tells you at least one error is still hidden.

## Why reconcile

A bank statement is the bank's own record of the account. The business's cash book (bank column) is the business's record of the same account. The two should tell the same story, yet at a given date the balances usually differ. A bank reconciliation statement explains the difference.

Its purposes are to:

- check the accuracy of both records and find mistakes made by the business or the bank;
- spot entries on the statement that are missing from the cash book, such as charges;
- detect fraud or unauthorised payments;
- show the true bank balance the business can rely on, once timing differences are understood.

## The process

Compare the cash book with the statement line by line, ticking items that appear in both. Then sort what is left.

| Item left over | Cause | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| On the statement, not the cash book | Credit transfers, bank interest, bank charges, standing orders, direct debits, dishonoured cheques | Update the cash book |
| In the cash book wrongly | Error by the business | Correct the cash book |
| In the cash book, not the statement | Unpresented cheques, uncredited lodgements (banked but not yet shown) | Put on the reconciliation statement |
| On the statement wrongly | Bank error | Put on the reconciliation statement and tell the bank |

Update the cash book first. Only the timing items, and any bank error, are left for the reconciliation statement. A statement shows a **credit** balance when the bank holds money for you, because it is a liability of the bank. Your cash book shows the same position as a **debit**.

## Worked example: Halvorn Cycles

At 31 October 2026 the cash book (bank column) of Halvorn Cycles shows a debit balance of 3,640. Comparing it with the bank statement gives these differences.

- Credit transfer from a customer, 700, not in the cash book.
- Bank interest received, 12, not in the cash book.
- Bank charges, 45, not in the cash book.
- Direct debit for insurance, 120, not in the cash book.
- Standing order for a loan repayment, 300, not in the cash book.
- A customer's cheque for 230 was dishonoured and is on the statement only.
- A cheque to a supplier for 450 was entered in the cash book as 540.
- Cheques of 380 and 215 to suppliers have not been presented.
- A lodgement of 1,100 made on 31 October is not yet on the statement.

**Step 1: update the cash book.** The error understated the balance by 90, so it is added back.

| Cash book (bank) | | | |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Balance b/d | 3,640 | Bank charges | 45 |
| Credit transfer | 700 | Direct debit | 120 |
| Bank interest | 12 | Standing order | 300 |
| Correction: cheque error | 90 | Dishonoured cheque | 230 |
| | | Balance c/d | 3,747 |
| | **4,442** | | **4,442** |

**Step 2: prepare the bank reconciliation statement.**

| Bank reconciliation statement at 31 October 2026 | |
|---|---:|
| Balance per updated cash book | 3,747 |
| Add: unpresented cheques (380 + 215) | 595 |
| | 4,342 |
| Less: uncredited lodgement | 1,100 |
| **Balance per bank statement** | **3,242** |

The reverse route gives the same result: 3,242 + 1,100 − 595 = 3,747. Give the statement a heading, a date and a labelled final line.

## Common errors

- Putting the suspense difference on the wrong side. Check which total is higher, then put the difference on the side that is lower.
- Posting a one-sided correction to suspense in the wrong direction. Say whether suspense was debited or credited when the error was made, then reverse it.
- Journalising an error of principle without clearing the original wrong account.
- Treating a compensating error as if it needs suspense.
- Using the single amount to correct a complete reversal, when double is needed.
- Adjusting the cash book for unpresented cheques. They belong on the reconciliation only.
- Adding a dishonoured cheque to the cash book. It reduces the balance.
- Starting the reconciliation from the unadjusted cash book balance.
