Every question here tests section **3.3.4 Alkenes** in the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level
Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3, for International AS and A-level exams May/June 2020
onwards, across 3.3.4.1 to 3.3.4.4: bonding and reactivity, addition reactions, addition polymers and
epoxyethane. No part is International A-level only. Alkene questions belong to Unit 2 (Organic 1 and
Physical 1), and the synoptic Unit 5 can include them.

## Questions

**1.** Which alkene gives only one organic product when it reacts with hydrogen bromide?

- A hept-1-ene
- B 2-methylpent-2-ene
- C 3,4-dimethylhex-3-ene
- D hept-2-ene

**[1]**

**2.** Two liquid hydrocarbons, X and Y, both have the molecular formula C₇H₁₄. X decolourises bromine
water. Y does not.

**(a)** State what the result shows about the structure of X. **[1]**
**(b)** Suggest a name for Y. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain, in terms of bonding, why X reacts with bromine. **[2]**

**3.** Cold, concentrated sulfuric acid is added to non-1-ene, CH₃(CH₂)₆CH=CH₂.

**(a)** Which species acts as the electrophile? **[1]**
**(b)** Using curly arrows, show how the major addition product forms. **[4]**

**4.** 2-methylhex-2-ene, (CH₃)₂C=CHCH₂CH₂CH₃, reacts with hydrogen bromide.

**(a)** Draw the two carbocations that can form and classify each one. **[2]**
**(b)** Give the names of the main product and of the product formed in smaller amount. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why one product is formed in a much larger amount. **[2]**

**5.** A 2.00 g mixture of cyclohexane and cyclopentene, C₅H₈, reacts with exactly 25.0 cm³ of a
0.400 mol dm⁻³ solution of bromine in an inert organic solvent. Calculate the percentage by mass of
cyclopentene in the mixture. **[4]**

**6.** This question is about addition polymers.

**(a)** Draw the repeating unit of the polymer made from 2-methylpropenenitrile, CH₂=C(CH₃)CN, and name
the polymer. **[2]**
**(b)** Part of another polymer is –CH₂–CH(OCOCH₃)–CH₂–CH(OCOCH₃)–. Give the repeating unit and name the
monomer. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the polymer in part (a) does not decolourise bromine water. **[2]**

**7.** Two samples of poly(propene) have average Mr values of 8.4 × 10⁴ and 3.4 × 10⁵.

**(a)** Predict which sample softens at the higher temperature. Explain your answer. **[3]**
**(b)** An inflatable paddling pool is made from PVC containing a plasticiser. Explain how the plasticiser
makes the PVC flexible. **[2]**
**(c)** Give one typical use of PVC without a plasticiser. **[1]**

**8.** Epoxyethane is made from ethene.

**(a)** Write an equation for the reaction. **[1]**
**(b)** Name the catalyst. **[1]**
**(c)** State the percentage atom economy and give a reason. **[1]**
**(d)** State two hazards associated with this process. **[2]**
**(e)** Suggest why the temperature of the reactor must be controlled carefully. **[1]**

**9.** Epoxyethane reacts with methanol.

**(a)** Explain why epoxyethane is so reactive. **[2]**
**(b)** Write an equation for the reaction and name the organic product. **[2]**
**(c)** Outline the mechanism for the reaction. **[3]**

**10.** A non-ionic surfactant is made from dodecan-1-ol, C₁₂H₂₅OH, and epoxyethane. A sample has
Mr = 538.0.

**(a)** Write a general equation for the formation of the surfactant from one molecule of dodecan-1-ol
and n molecules of epoxyethane. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate n. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest why this molecule can act as a surfactant. **[2]**
**(d)** Give one economic reason why such surfactants matter and one environmental concern about them.
**[2]**

**11.** A plant converts 7.00 tonnes of ethene into epoxyethane with an 80.0% yield. All the epoxyethane
then reacts with water.

**(a)** Calculate the mass of epoxyethane formed. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the mass of ethane-1,2-diol formed, assuming full conversion. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why a large excess of water is used. **[1]**
**(d)** Give two uses of ethane-1,2-diol. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** **C** [1]. It is symmetrical, so both possible carbocations are identical. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Check the groups on each carbon of the double bond, not the whole molecule; B and D
look balanced but their two C=C carbons carry different groups.

**2. (a)** X contains a **C=C double bond** (it is unsaturated) [1]
**(b)** A cycloalkane, e.g. **methylcyclohexane** or **cycloheptane** [1]
**(c)** The C=C bond is a centre of high electron density [1]. It induces a dipole in Br₂, and the δ+ Br
accepts a pair of electrons from the double bond (acts as an electrophile) [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b) a straight-chain alkane cannot be right, because C₇H₁₄ does not fit CₙH₂ₙ₊₂;
check any suggested formula against the one given.

**3. (a)** A hydrogen atom of H₂SO₄ (Hδ+) [1]
**(b)** Curly arrow from the double bond to an H atom in H₂SO₄, plus an arrow moving the O–H bonding pair
onto O [1]; secondary
carbocation **CH₃(CH₂)₆C⁺HCH₃** [1]; the hydrogensulfate ion's negative oxygen uses a lone pair to bond to C⁺ (arrow
shown) [1]; product
**CH₃(CH₂)₆CH(OSO₂OH)CH₃** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Because the major product is asked for, the positive charge must be on C2; a
mechanism through the primary carbocation answers a different question.

**4. (a)** **(CH₃)₂C⁺CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₃**, tertiary [1]; **(CH₃)₂CHC⁺HCH₂CH₂CH₃**, secondary [1]
**(b)** Major: **2-bromo-2-methylhexane** [1]. Minor: **3-bromo-2-methylhexane** [1]
**(c)** The tertiary carbocation has three electron-releasing alkyl groups that spread the positive
charge, so it is more stable than the secondary one [1]. It is formed faster, so most of the HBr adds by
this route [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* An answer about the stability of the products, not the carbocations, misses the
point; name the intermediates in your explanation.

**5.** n(Br₂) = 0.0250 × 0.400 = 0.0100 mol [1]. Only cyclopentene reacts (1 : 1), so n(C₅H₈) =
0.0100 mol [1]. Mr(C₅H₈) = 68.0, mass = 0.680 g [1]. Percentage = 0.680 ÷ 2.00 × 100 = **34.0%** [1].
**[4]**
*Examiner insight:* State that cyclohexane does not react; otherwise the 1 : 1 link is unexplained.

**6. (a)** Repeating unit **–[CH₂–C(CH₃)(CN)]ₙ–**, single C–C, both CH₃ and CN on the same carbon,
extension bonds and n [1]; **poly(2-methylpropenenitrile)** [1]
**(b)** Repeating unit **–[CH₂–CH(OCOCH₃)]ₙ–** [1]; monomer **ethenyl ethanoate**, CH₂=CHOCOCH₃ [1]
**(c)** The polymer is saturated: there is no C=C bond left, so there is no centre of high electron
density for bromine to attack [1]. Its backbone is made of strong C–C and C–H bonds of low polarity [1].
**[6]**
*Examiner insight:* In (a) the two substituents must stay on the same carbon; splitting them across the
two main-chain carbons gives a different polymer.

**7. (a)** The sample with Mr **3.4 × 10⁵** [1]. Its chains are longer, with more electrons and more
contact between neighbouring chains [1], so the total induced dipole–dipole (van der Waals) attraction
is larger and more energy is needed to let the chains move apart [1].
**(b)** Plasticiser molecules sit between the chains and push them further apart [1]. With the chains spaced out,
the attraction between them is weaker and they can move over one another [1].
**(c)** Any one: **water or sewage pipes**, **doors**, **bank cards** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* In (a) avoid saying covalent bonds break on softening; the change is about forces
between chains, and mixing the two up contradicts the answer.

**8. (a)** **2CH₂=CH₂ + O₂ → 2C₂H₄O** [1]
**(b)** **Silver** [1]
**(c)** **100%**, because epoxyethane is the only product [1]
**(d)** Any two: epoxyethane is extremely flammable; it forms explosive mixtures with air; it is toxic;
it is a carcinogen [1] [1]
**(e)** The competing complete combustion of ethene is far more exothermic, so the temperature could rise
out of control (and yield would fall) [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* "Dangerous chemicals" alone is too vague for (d); each hazard needs to name a
specific property.

**9. (a)** The ring bond angles are about 60°, far below 109.5°, so the ring is strained and the C–O bonds
are weak [1]. The C–O bonds are polar, so the ring carbons are δ+ and attract nucleophiles [1].
**(b)** **C₂H₄O + CH₃OH → CH₃OCH₂CH₂OH** [1]; **2-methoxyethanol** [1]
**(c)** Arrow from a lone pair on the methanol oxygen to a ring carbon [1]; arrow from that C–O ring bond
to the ring oxygen, giving CH₃O⁺(H)CH₂CH₂O⁻ [1]; proton transferred from O⁺–H to O⁻ to give the product
[1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* The attacking species is the nucleophile, so the first arrow starts at methanol's lone
pair; reversing it, as if the ring attacked methanol, loses the mechanism.

**10. (a)** **C₁₂H₂₅OH + nC₂H₄O → C₁₂H₂₅(OCH₂CH₂)ₙOH** [1]
**(b)** Mr(C₁₂H₂₅OH) = 186.0 [1]; n = (538.0 − 186.0) ÷ 44.0 = **8** [1]
**(c)** The C₁₂H₂₅ chain is non-polar and mixes with grease or oil [1]; the ether chain ending in OH is
polar and is attracted to water [1]
**(d)** Economic: used on a very large scale in detergents and cleaning products [1]. Environmental: can
harm aquatic life if it reaches waterways, so it needs to break down readily [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b) the whole Mr of each epoxyethane unit adds on, because the reaction is
addition; subtracting water as if it were a condensation gives the wrong n.

**11. (a)** n(C₂H₄) = 7.00 × 10⁶ ÷ 28.0 = 2.50 × 10⁵ mol [1]. Theoretical mass of C₂H₄O = 2.50 × 10⁵ ×
44.0 = 1.10 × 10⁷ g = 11.0 tonnes [1]. At 80.0%: **8.80 tonnes** [1]
**(b)** n(C₂H₄O) = 8.80 × 10⁶ ÷ 44.0 = 2.00 × 10⁵ mol [1]; mass of C₂H₆O₂ = 2.00 × 10⁵ × 62.0 =
1.24 × 10⁷ g = **12.4 tonnes** [1]
**(c)** The diol still has OH groups and could react with more epoxyethane; excess water limits this [1]
**(d)** Any two: **antifreeze**, **engine coolant**, **making polyester (PET)** [1] [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Convert tonnes to grams (× 10⁶) before dividing by Mr, or work in tonnes throughout;
mixing units gives answers a million times out.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Opening arrow begun at an alkene carbon rather than at the double bond itself.
- Partial charges missing from HBr, or Br₂ shown without its induced dipole.
- Hydrogensulfate shown bonding to carbon through sulfur.
- Major product explained by product stability rather than carbocation stability.
- Repeating unit with a C=C, missing extension bonds, or substituents moved to the wrong carbon.
- Polymer unreactivity blamed on intermolecular forces.
- Epoxyethane mechanism missing the final proton transfer.
- Unit slips between tonnes and grams in industrial calculations.
