This guide covers section 3.17, Interpretation, analysis and communication of accounting information, of the AQA A-level Accounting (7127) specification (version 1.1, October 2026, for A-level exams from June 2019 onwards). The qualification is not tiered. The specification lists section 17 in the content assessed by both Paper 1 and Paper 2, each a 3-hour written exam, and you must use a calculator in the exam.

The twelve 3.8 ratios, the cash versus profit reconciliation and the general limitations of ratios are taught in the [3.8 study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-accounting-analysis-and-evaluation-of-financial-information/) and are not repeated here. Use it with the [revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-accounting-interpretation-analysis-and-communication-of-accounting-info-revision-notes/) and the [practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-accounting-interpretation-analysis-and-communication-of-accounting-info-practice/). See also the [AQA A-level Accounting hub](/boards/aqa/a-level/accounting/), the [7127 checklist](/checklists/aqa/a-level/accounting/) and the [free diagnostics](/diagnostics/). All businesses and figures are invented.

The specification's appendix says you must know these formulae.

| Ratio | Formula | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Earnings per share (EPS) | Earnings in pence (profit after tax in pence) / Number of issued ordinary shares | pence |
| Price earnings (P/E) | Current market price / Earnings per share | times |
| Dividend yield | Dividend per share / Market price per share × 100 | % |
| Dividend cover | Profit after interest and tax / Ordinary share dividends paid | times |
| Interest cover | Profit before interest and tax / Interest payable | times |

Keep EPS and the share price both in pence. Dividend per share = ordinary dividends paid / number of issued ordinary shares.

### Worked example: Kestrel Foods plc

Year ended 31 March 2026:

| Item | £ |
|---|---:|
| Profit from operations (profit before interest and tax) | 1,250,000 |
| Finance costs (interest payable) | (150,000) |
| Profit for the year before tax | 1,100,000 |
| Tax | (275,000) |
| Profit for the year after tax | 825,000 |

Kestrel has 5,000,000 issued ordinary shares of £1, paid ordinary dividends of £330,000, and its year-end share price was 231p.

```
EPS            = 82,500,000p / 5,000,000      = 16.50p
Dividend/share = 33,000,000p / 5,000,000      = 6.60p
P/E            = 231 / 16.50                  = 14.00 times
Dividend yield = 6.60 / 231 × 100             = 2.86%
Dividend cover = 825,000 / 330,000            = 2.50 times
Interest cover = 1,250,000 / 150,000          = 8.33 times
```

### What each one tells you

- **EPS** is the profit earned for each ordinary share. A rising EPS over time is a sign of growth. Do not compare EPS between two companies: the number and nominal value of shares differ, so a higher EPS does not mean a better company.
- **P/E** shows how many years of current earnings the price represents. A higher P/E usually means investors expect growth or see low risk; a low one may signal expected trouble.
- **Dividend yield** is the cash return on the current price. An income investor compares it with interest on savings. A high yield can also come from a falling share price.
- **Dividend cover** shows how safe the dividend is. Cover of 2.50 times means profit after tax could fall by more than half before the dividend could not be paid from this year's profit.
- **Interest cover** shows how easily operating profit meets interest. A fall means more borrowing, higher rates or weaker profit, and more risk for lenders.

## Evaluating performance internally and externally

The focus is profitability, liquidity, efficiency and capital structure.

- **Internal, across periods (trend analysis):** this year against last year, a budget or a target. Policies are usually unchanged, so the comparison is fair.
- **External (inter-firm comparison):** against a competitor or industry average, showing whether a result is good for the sector.

### Worked example: trend and competitor

Kestrel's 2025 figures: profit after tax £760,000, dividends £300,000, interest payable £160,000, profit before interest and tax £1,100,000, share price 200p, same 5,000,000 shares. Harrier Foods plc is a competitor with 3,000,000 shares: profit before interest and tax £1,080,000, interest £300,000, profit after tax £630,000, dividends £450,000, share price 300p. Capital gearing (a 3.8 ratio) is given.

| Ratio | Kestrel 2025 | Kestrel 2026 | Harrier 2026 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| EPS | 15.20p | 16.50p | 21.00p |
| Dividend per share | 6.00p | 6.60p | 15.00p |
| P/E | 13.16 | 14.00 | 14.29 |
| Dividend yield | 3.00% | 2.86% | 5.00% |
| Dividend cover | 2.53 | 2.50 | 1.40 |
| Interest cover | 6.88 | 8.33 | 3.60 |
| Capital gearing (given) | 22% | 18% | 48% |

**Trend.** Kestrel's EPS rose by 1.30p, and interest cover improved because profit rose while interest fell. The share price rose faster than EPS, so P/E went up: the market is more confident.

**Against Harrier.** Harrier's higher EPS proves nothing, because it has fewer shares. Harrier pays out about 71% of its profit, which gives a 5.00% yield, but cover of 1.40 means a modest fall in profit could force a dividend cut. Its gearing of 48% and interest cover of 3.60 add risk. Kestrel suits a growth investor wanting safety; Harrier suits one wanting income now.

**Limits of comparing with a competitor.** Different accounting policies, year ends, sizes, product mixes and financing weaken the comparison. Share prices move daily, so P/E and yield depend on the date chosen.

### Management information is interpreted the same way

Section 3.17 covers management information too. Kestrel's bakery division reports budget against actual for a quarter:

| £ | Budget | Actual | % of revenue (budget / actual) |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Revenue | 800,000 | 760,000 | |
| Materials | 320,000 | 296,400 | 40.0% / 39.0% |
| Labour | 200,000 | 212,000 | 25.0% / 27.9% |
| Overheads | 150,000 | 150,000 | |
| Profit | 130,000 | 101,600 | 16.25% / 13.37% |

Revenue fell 5%. Materials fell roughly in line with output. Labour rose by £12,000 although less was produced, pointing to overtime or idle time, so investigate labour scheduling. For the techniques behind such reports, see [budgeting](/resources/aqa-a-level-accounting-budgeting/) and [standard costing and variance analysis](/resources/aqa-a-level-accounting-standard-costing-and-variance-analysis/).

## Cash and profit: company transactions

3.17 repeats the 3.8 outcome on cash versus profit. For a company, add these transactions, each treated separately from Kestrel's position above:

| Transaction | Profit | Cash | Investors' ratios |
|---|---|---|---|
| Issue 1,000,000 shares for cash at £2.20 | No effect | Up £2,200,000 | EPS falls to 13.75p on the same profit; gearing falls |
| Pay a further dividend of £200,000 | No effect (shown in the statement of changes in equity) | Down £200,000 | Total dividends rise, so dividend per share and yield rise and cover falls |
| Bonus issue of 1 share for every 5 held | No effect | No effect | EPS falls to 13.75p; share price would be expected to fall |
| Issue £1,000,000 of 6% debentures | Interest of £60,000 a year reduces profit before tax | Up £1,000,000 | Interest cover falls to 5.95 times |

The [limited company accounts guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-accounting-limited-company-accounts/) shows where each item appears in the statements.

## Stakeholders and communication

The specification lists these stakeholders. Link each to the measures it cares about.

| Stakeholder | Type | Main interest | Measures it looks at |
|---|---|---|---|
| Employees | Internal | Job security, pay | Profit trend, liquidity |
| Management | Internal | Running and controlling the business | All ratios, budgets, variances |
| Owners/shareholders | Internal | Return and risk | EPS, P/E, dividend yield and cover, ROCE |
| Customers | External | Continued supply, after-sales service | Liquidity, survival |
| Suppliers | External | Being paid on time | Liquidity ratios, trade payable days |
| Government | External | Tax, employment, compliance | Profit, number of employees |
| Lenders | External | Repayment and interest | Interest cover, gearing, liquidity |
| Local community | External | Jobs, local spending, environment | Profit trend, expansion or closure plans |

**Effective communication.** The specification's skills annex asks you to present information numerically, graphically and in written form so non-accountants can use it. Lead with the conclusion, explain ratios in plain words, chart trends, give only the figures that reader needs, and state the limitations.

**Value of published accounts.** They follow a standard format (IAS1), so years and companies can be compared, and they reach external stakeholders who cannot see internal records. But they are historic, summarised, produced after the year end and say little about non-financial performance.

## Systems of recording data

**Features and main applications of accounting software:** automatic posting of both sides of each entry; invoicing and sales and purchase ledgers; aged lists of receivables and payables; bank reconciliation; payroll; inventory records; instant financial statements, budgets and reports.

| Computerised versus manual | |
|---|---|
| Advantages | Faster processing; arithmetic is always correct; records are up to date; reports produced instantly; one entry updates every ledger; backups possible |
| Disadvantages | Cost of software, hardware and training; errors of principle and wrong input still occur; risk of hacking, viruses and data loss; system failure stops work; staff may resist change |

| Single entry versus double entry | Single entry (one side recorded, often just a cash book) | Double entry (both sides recorded) |
|---|---|---|
| Advantages | Simple, cheap, quick; enough for a very small trader | Complete record; trial balance checks arithmetic; financial statements easy to prepare; errors and fraud easier to find |
| Disadvantages | No trial balance; statements must be reconstructed; errors and fraud hard to detect; no full record of assets and liabilities | Needs time, skill and cost |

The [double entry model guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-accounting-double-entry-model/) revises the full system.

## Critical assessment of recommendations

Work in this order: the financial case, the impact on stakeholders, the local and national economy and the environment, then a judgement.

### Worked example: automating a production line

Kestrel's directors recommend replacing a manual packing line with an automated one. It costs £1,800,000, with a 10-year life and no residual value. Wages fall by £520,000 a year; extra maintenance and energy cost £70,000 a year. Thirty jobs go, with redundancy costs of £240,000.

```
Net annual cash saving = 520,000 - 70,000     = £450,000
Depreciation           = 1,800,000 / 10       = £180,000
Rise in profit from operations = 450,000 - 180,000 = £270,000
Outlay recovered in (1,800,000 + 240,000) / 450,000 = 4.53 years
```

- **Shareholders:** profit and EPS rise after the redundancy year.
- **Employees:** thirty jobs lost; remaining staff need training.
- **Lenders:** if the line is financed by a loan, interest cover falls.
- **Local economy:** thirty fewer wages spent in local shops; local suppliers may lose orders.
- **National economy:** higher productivity and competitiveness, but more people claiming benefits; the machine may be imported.
- **Environment:** higher electricity use, possibly offset by less packaging waste.

**Judgement.** The financial case is sound: the outlay is recovered within half the line's life. Harm could be reduced by redeploying staff. The answer depends on how reliable the savings estimate is. For appraisal methods, see [capital investment appraisal](/resources/aqa-a-level-accounting-capital-investment-appraisal/).

## Common errors

- Working EPS in pounds and then dividing a share price in pence by it.
- Comparing EPS between companies with different numbers of shares.
- Using profit before tax for dividend cover, or profit after interest for interest cover.
- Saying a high dividend yield is always good, without checking cover.
- Assessing a recommendation only on the figures, or only on non-financial points, with no judgement.
