## Confirm your two options first

Paper 2 pairs one Section A society-in-change option (Germany 1919–49,
Russia and the USSR 1914–53, or the USA 1920–68) with one Section B
thematic study (Technology and change, or Medicine and change, both
c800–present). Confirm both before revising further — generic history
revision material found elsewhere will not necessarily match either
choice.

## Section A: build a turning-point timeline, not a fact list

A society-in-change answer is judged on whether it explains **why**
change happened and **how significant** it was, not on how many events
it lists. Build a timeline of three or four genuine turning points —
a change of regime, a war, an economic crisis — and for each, prepare:

- What changed (political, social or economic).
- Why it changed at that specific moment.
- How the change connects to the turning point before and after it.

A response that treats the period as a connected chain of cause and
consequence outperforms one that lists isolated facts in date order.

## Section B: sample the timeline, don't cover it evenly

Spanning c800 to the present, no thematic study can be revised event by
event. Instead, prepare a **small set of well-evidenced examples spread
across different eras** — for the theme actually studied, aim for at
least one clear example from an early period, one from a middle period,
and one modern example. This spread lets you answer questions about
the pace of change (did it speed up, slow down, or stay steady?) with
real evidence rather than only recent examples.

## The question types within each section

Each section builds from short recall to a single extended-response
question, not "how far" phrasing throughout. Expect: a "give three
changes/developments" recall item, an "explain why one turning point/
factor..." item, an "explain two reasons why..." item, and finally
one extended-response question per section that does use "how far"
(or a similarly weighed-judgement wording such as an inverted-commas
statement to agree or disagree with) — it is only this final question
that signals a **weighed judgement across multiple factors**, not a
single-factor narrative. For that extended-response question on a
named factor (e.g. war, or the role of an individual), structure the
answer as:

1. Explain the named factor's specific contribution, with an example.
2. Weigh it against at least one other cause of change drawn from your
studied content -- the specification does not name a fixed set of
factors to choose from.
3. Reach a supported judgement on how far the named factor explains the
change overall.

## Change is not the same as progress

A recurring trap, especially in the technology and medicine themes, is
assuming change always means improvement. The specification expects
recognition that change can be uneven — periods of rapid breakthrough
followed by long stretches of slow progress, or setbacks that reversed
earlier gains. Naming a setback or a slow period, where relevant, shows
a more sophisticated grasp of "change" than a purely celebratory
narrative of progress.

## A new qualification — treat OxfordAQA's own materials as primary

Because this specification is new (first teaching September 2026, first
exams May/June 2028), generic revision guides written for other boards'
similar historical periods may not match this specification's exact
question style or content emphasis. Prioritise OxfordAQA's own specimen
assessment materials over adapted resources from other exam boards.

## Balancing revision time between Papers 1 and 2

Because Paper 1 (International Relations Depth Studies) and Paper 2
(Studies in Change) carry identical timing and weighting — both 1 hour
45 minutes, both worth 60 marks and 50% of the GCSE — revision time
should be split evenly between them rather than skewed toward whichever
paper's content feels more familiar or was taught more recently. A
candidate who has thoroughly revised Paper 1 but only lightly reviewed
Paper 2 has not actually balanced their overall GCSE preparation, even
if their confidence feels balanced.

## Exam traps

- Listing events in a Section A answer without explaining why each
turning point happened or how it connects to the next.
- Trying to cover the entire c800–present timeframe evenly in Section B
rather than using well-chosen, spread-out examples.
- Treating "how far" questions as an invitation to discuss only one
factor.
- Assuming all change in the technology or medicine themes represents
straightforward progress.
- Revising from another board's materials as if question style will
transfer directly to this new specification.

## Comparing Section A and Section B revision styles

It helps to name explicitly how differently these two sections should
be revised, since applying Section A's chronological-timeline approach
to Section B's much longer timeframe (or vice versa) wastes preparation
time: Section A rewards a dense, connected narrative across a few
decades; Section B rewards a sparse, well-chosen sample of moments
across many centuries. Treating both sections with the same revision
method is a subtle but avoidable inefficiency.

## Self-test

1. What two things does a strong Section A answer explain, beyond just
what changed?
2. Why should Section B revision use a small, spread-out set of
examples rather than attempting even coverage?
3. What does a "how far" question require, structurally?
4. Why is "change" not the same as "progress" in this paper's themes?
5. Why should OxfordAQA's own materials be prioritised over other
boards' revision guides for this qualification?

**Answers:** 1. Why the change happened at that point, and how
significant or connected it was to surrounding turning points, not
just a description of what changed. 2. Because the c800–present
timeframe is too broad to cover evenly; a small set of examples spread
across different eras supports questions about pace and significance of
change with real evidence. 3. Explaining a named factor's specific
contribution, weighing it against at least one other factor, then
reaching a supported judgement on how far it explains the change
overall. 4. Because change can include setbacks or slow periods, not
only improvement — the specification expects this more sophisticated
view, particularly in the technology and medicine themes. 5. Because
this is a brand-new qualification with no live past papers yet (first
examined May/June 2028), so its own specimen materials are the safest guide to actual question style and
content emphasis.

## Frequently asked questions

**How is Paper 2 of OxfordAQA 9245 structured?**
Paper 2, Studies in change (1 hour 45 minutes, 60 marks, 50%), has Section A on one society in change and Section B on one thematic study; each section has four questions worth 3, 6, 9 and 12 marks.

**Which Section A options are there?**
Life in Germany, 1919–1949; Life in Russia and the USSR, 1914–1953; and Life in the USA, 1920–1968.

**How should a thematic study spanning c800 to today be revised?**
With a small set of well-evidenced examples spread across the periods, rather than trying to cover every event.
