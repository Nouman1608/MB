This guide covers subtopic **13.2 Urinary system** of Cambridge O Level Biology 5090, 2026-2028 series. The syllabus asks you to identify the kidneys, ureters, bladder and urethra on diagrams and state what each does; to explain the need for excretion in terms of the toxicity of urea; to outline the structure of a nephron and its blood vessels (Bowman's capsule, glomerulus, tubules, loop of Henle and collecting duct); to outline how a nephron works (filtration, reabsorption and the formation of urine); to describe the liver's role in assimilating amino acids into proteins; and to describe deamination as the process that forms urea.

The [Excretion topic guide](/resources/o-level-biology-5090-excretion/) covers the whole topic. This page goes deeper on the urinary system, with the logic of each step and some worked examples. Use the [13.2 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-biology-5090-urinary-system-revision-notes/) for quick recall and the [13.2 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-biology-5090-urinary-system-practice/) to test yourself.

## Why the body must excrete

**Excretion** is the removal of toxic materials and the waste products of metabolism from organisms. Cells carry out thousands of chemical reactions, and some of them make substances that the body cannot use and that do harm if they build up. Two examples at this level are carbon dioxide, which is made in respiration and removed by the lungs, and **urea**, which is made in the liver and removed by the kidneys.

The syllabus limits the explanation of the need for excretion to the **toxicity of urea**. If urea were not removed, its concentration in the blood and tissue fluid would rise, and it would interfere with the normal working of cells. Excretion therefore keeps the internal environment safe for cells to work in. This is different from egestion, which is the removal of undigested food from the gut. That material has never been part of the cells, so it is not a waste product of metabolism.

## The organs of the urinary system

Urine is made in the kidneys and leaves the body by a short route. Learn the organs in order.

| Organ | Number | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Kidney | 2 | Removes urea and excess salts and water from the blood as urine |
| Ureter | 2 | A tube that carries urine from each kidney to the bladder |
| Bladder | 1 | A muscular bag that stores urine until it is released |
| Urethra | 1 | A tube that carries urine from the bladder out of the body |

The syllabus asks you to describe the function of the kidney simply, as removing urea and excess salts and water from the blood as urine. Do not add more than this unless the question asks you to describe the nephron.

On a diagram the kidneys sit on either side of the backbone, towards the back of the abdomen. Each has a ureter leaving it. The two ureters run down to the bladder, which sits low in the abdomen. A single urethra leaves the bottom of the bladder. A blood vessel carrying blood to each kidney (the renal artery) and one carrying blood away (the renal vein) are also drawn on many diagrams. Blood entering the kidney contains urea. Blood leaving it has far less.

## The liver: where urea comes from

The kidney removes urea, but the liver makes it. This part of the specification links two organs, so be clear about who does what.

### Assimilation of amino acids

After a meal, protein has been digested to **amino acids**, which are absorbed into the blood and carried to the liver by the hepatic portal vein. The liver controls what happens next. One role is **assimilation**: the liver takes some of the amino acids and builds them into **proteins**, including the plasma proteins found in blood. Other amino acids travel on to body cells, which use them to make their own proteins for growth and repair.

### Deamination

The body cannot store amino acids. Any that are in excess of what is needed are broken down in the liver. In **deamination**, the **nitrogen-containing part** of the amino acid is removed. This leaves a part that contains carbon, hydrogen and oxygen only, which can be used in respiration to release energy or converted to carbohydrate or fat. The nitrogen-containing part is changed into **urea**.

Urea is released into the blood and carried to the kidneys. The sequence to remember is:

1. Excess amino acids reach the liver.
2. Deamination removes the nitrogen-containing part.
3. The nitrogen-containing part is made into urea.
4. Urea enters the blood and is carried to the kidneys.
5. The kidneys remove urea in urine.

A common mistake is to say that the kidney makes urea, or that deamination removes the whole amino acid. Only the nitrogen-containing part is removed.

## The structure of a nephron

Each kidney contains about a million tiny tubes called **nephrons**. A nephron, with its blood vessels, is the unit that makes urine. The syllabus limits the parts to five.

| Part | Description |
|---|---|
| Bowman's capsule | A cup-shaped structure at the start of the nephron that surrounds the glomerulus |
| Glomerulus | A tight knot of blood capillaries inside the Bowman's capsule |
| Tubules | The coiled tubes that follow the capsule, where reabsorption takes place |
| Loop of Henle | A hairpin-shaped section of the tubule that dips down and comes back up |
| Collecting duct | A tube that receives liquid from several nephrons and leads to the ureter |

The blood vessels matter too. Blood arrives at the glomerulus in a wide vessel and leaves in a narrower one, which keeps the pressure in the glomerulus high. After the glomerulus, the blood vessel forms a network of capillaries around the tubules. This is the route by which useful substances are returned to the blood.

Keep the distinction between the **capsule** (a cup) and the **glomerulus** (the capillaries inside it) clear. Many answers lose marks by swapping them.

## The function of a nephron

Urine is made in two main stages: filtration and reabsorption. The syllabus says that details of these processes are not required, so you need the outline and the correct vocabulary.

### Filtration at the glomerulus

Because the pressure in the glomerulus is high, liquid is forced out of the blood through the capillary walls and into the Bowman's capsule. Small molecules pass through. This filtrate contains **water, glucose, urea and ions**. Large molecules such as proteins and blood cells are too big to pass through and stay in the blood.

Filtration is not selective about what is useful. Glucose is filtered along with urea, because both are small. This is why the next stage is needed.

### Reabsorption in the tubules

As the filtrate flows along the tubules, the useful substances are taken back into the blood in the surrounding capillaries.

- **All of the glucose** is reabsorbed.
- **Some of the ions** are reabsorbed, depending on what the body needs.
- **Most of the water** is reabsorbed.

Urea is not all reabsorbed, so most of it stays in the tubule. The loop of Henle takes part in the reabsorption of water and ions.

### Formation of urine

What is left in the tubule passes into the collecting duct and then to the ureter. This liquid is **urine**. It contains **urea, excess water and excess ions**. The word "excess" matters. The kidney does not remove all the water and salts, only the amount over what the body needs. If you drink a lot of water, the kidneys produce a large volume of dilute urine. If you are dehydrated, they produce a small volume of concentrated urine.

## Worked example: comparing blood, filtrate and urine

*A healthy person's blood plasma, the filtrate in the Bowman's capsule and urine are tested for four substances. The table shows whether each is present.*

| Substance | Blood plasma | Filtrate | Urine |
|---|---|---|---|
| Protein | Yes | No | No |
| Glucose | Yes | Yes | No |
| Urea | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Ions | Yes | Yes | Yes |

**Why is protein absent from the filtrate?** Protein molecules are too large to pass through the walls of the capillaries in the glomerulus, so they stay in the blood.

**Why is glucose in the filtrate but not in urine?** Glucose is small enough to be filtered, but all of it is reabsorbed into the blood in the tubules.

**What would it mean if glucose were found in urine?** The tubules have not reabsorbed all the glucose, for example because the concentration in the blood is too high. This link is used in the diagnosis of diabetes in the later topic on blood glucose control.

## Worked example: how much water is reabsorbed?

*Suppose 180 dm3 of liquid is filtered from the blood in a day and 1.5 dm3 of urine is produced. What percentage of the water is reabsorbed?*

1. Volume reabsorbed = 180 - 1.5 = 178.5 dm3.
2. Percentage = 178.5 divided by 180 x 100 = 99.2 per cent (to one decimal place).

This shows what "most of the water" means in practice. Nearly all of the water that is filtered goes back into the blood. These figures are used to practise the arithmetic. You are not asked to remember them.

## Putting it together: the path of urea

Follow urea from where it is made to where it leaves the body.

- Protein is eaten and digested to amino acids.
- Amino acids are absorbed and carried to the liver.
- Excess amino acids are deaminated and the nitrogen-containing part becomes urea.
- Urea is carried in the blood to the kidneys.
- It is filtered at the glomerulus into the Bowman's capsule.
- It passes along the tubule and collecting duct and is not fully reabsorbed.
- Urine travels down the ureter to the bladder, where it is stored.
- Urine leaves the body through the urethra.

Being able to tell this story in order is a good way to hold the topic together, and it is the basis of many structured questions.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that the kidney makes urea. The liver does.
- Saying that the bladder makes urine. It only stores it.
- Mixing up the ureter (kidney to bladder) and the urethra (bladder to outside).
- Saying that all the water is removed. Only the excess is lost.
- Writing that glucose is not filtered. It is filtered, then reabsorbed.
- Saying that filtration is selective. It depends on size.
- Writing that deamination removes the whole amino acid.

## Command words

- **Identify**: name a part on a diagram.
- **State**: give the function or fact without explanation.
- **Outline**: give the main points of the structure or process briefly.
- **Explain**: give a reason, for example why urea must be removed.
- **Describe**: give the stages in order.
