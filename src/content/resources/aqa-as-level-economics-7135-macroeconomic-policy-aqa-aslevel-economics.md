This guide covers **3.2.4 Macroeconomic policy** in AQA AS Economics (7135), specification version 1.3, the final sub-topic of Paper 2. At AS it has three parts: monetary policy, fiscal policy and supply-side policies. The AS content sets two limits worth knowing: you do not need to know how interest rates and the exchange rate are determined, only how changes in them affect the economy, and you are not required to know the arguments for and against balancing the budget or limiting the national debt. All countries and figures below are invented unless stated.

## 3.2.4.1 Monetary policy

Monetary policy involves the central bank taking action to influence interest rates, the supply of money and credit, and the exchange rate. In the UK the government sets the objectives and the Bank of England's **Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)** uses **bank rate** to try to achieve them. The central objective is the government's target rate of inflation, set at 2% for CPI inflation, alongside support for the government's wider economic aims.

**How a rise in bank rate works.**

```
Bank rate rises
 -> commercial banks raise loan and mortgage rates
 -> borrowing is dearer and saving more rewarding
 -> consumption and investment fall (C and I)
 -> the exchange rate tends to rise as saving in the UK becomes more attractive
 -> exports dearer, imports cheaper, so net exports fall
 -> AD shifts left, demand-pull pressure eases, inflation falls back towards target
```

The lags matter: the full effect on inflation takes a considerable time, so the MPC sets bank rate according to **forecasts** of inflation rather than today's figure.

**Factors the MPC considers.** Examples include the current and forecast rate of inflation; the growth of real GDP and the size of the output gap; wage growth and unemployment; house prices and consumer borrowing; the exchange rate and import prices; and world commodity prices and economic conditions abroad.

**Exchange rate effects on the objectives.** A **fall** in the exchange rate makes exports cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home:

| Objective | Likely effect of a depreciation |
|---|---|
| Growth | Net exports rise, so AD and real output rise |
| Unemployment | Jobs in exporting and import-competing firms increase |
| Inflation | Import prices rise and AD increases, so inflation tends to rise |
| Current account | Improves if export and import volumes respond enough to the price changes |

The final row connects to price elasticity, as the specification notes: the more price elastic the demand for exports and imports, the more the current account improves.

## 3.2.4.2 Fiscal policy

Fiscal policy is the manipulation of **government spending, taxation and the budget balance**. It has **macroeconomic functions** (influencing AD, growth, unemployment and inflation) and **microeconomic functions** (correcting market failures, changing incentives and redistributing income).

**Influencing AD.** Higher spending or lower taxes raise AD, and the government may deliberately run a **budget deficit** (spending above revenue) to do so. Lower spending or higher taxes reduce AD, and a deliberate **budget surplus** withdraws demand.

**Influencing AS.** Spending on education, training and infrastructure, and tax changes that alter incentives to work and invest, can shift LRAS to the right over time.

**The pattern of economic activity.** Spending and tax choices change what is produced and who benefits: higher fuel duty shifts spending away from car travel; spending on housebuilding raises activity in construction; a region that receives transport investment can grow faster than others.

**Main UK taxes and spending.** Taxes include income tax, National Insurance contributions, VAT, corporation tax, council tax and excise duties on fuel, alcohol and tobacco. Main categories of spending include health, education, welfare (social protection) and defence.

**Direct and indirect taxes.** A **direct tax** is levied on income or wealth and paid by the person or firm on whom it falls (income tax, corporation tax). An **indirect tax** is levied on spending (VAT, excise duties).

### Worked example: classifying a tax

Country U's income tax: no tax on the first 10,000; 20% on income from 10,000 to 40,000; 40% on income above 40,000.

```
Income 20,000: tax = 0.20 x 10,000 = 2,000        average rate 10.0%
Income 60,000: tax = 0.20 x 30,000 + 0.40 x 20,000
                   = 6,000 + 8,000 = 14,000      average rate 23.3%
```

The **average rate rises with income**, so the tax is **progressive**. A **proportional** tax takes the same percentage at every income; a **regressive** tax takes a smaller percentage as income rises. A sales tax can be regressive: if a household on 15,000 pays 1,500 in VAT (10% of income) and one on 60,000 pays 3,000 (5%), the burden falls more heavily on the lower income.

**Budget balance and national debt.** The budget balance is a flow for one year; the national debt is the stock of accumulated government borrowing. A deficit of 50 bn added to a debt of 1,200 bn raises the debt to 1,250 bn; a surplus would reduce it.

## 3.2.4.3 Supply-side policies

**Supply-side policies** are government measures designed to raise the economy's productive potential. **Supply-side improvements** are the resulting increases in capacity, and the specification stresses that improvements often come from the **private sector** independently of government, through productivity gains, innovation and investment.

**Examples named in the specification:** government spending on education and training; cuts in income tax and corporation tax; welfare reform; industrial policy.

| Policy | Intended route to higher potential output |
|---|---|
| Education and training | More skilled, more productive workers; lower structural unemployment |
| Income tax cuts | Higher reward for working, so more people work or work longer hours |
| Corporation tax cuts | Higher post-tax profit, so more investment in capital |
| Welfare reform | Stronger incentive to move from benefits into work |
| Industrial policy | Support for particular sectors, research or infrastructure |

**Effects.** By shifting LRAS right, successful supply-side policies can lower unemployment (especially structural), ease the rate of increase in prices (more capacity relieves cost pressure), and improve the current account (higher productivity makes exports more competitive). Their weaknesses are time lags, uncertain effects on behaviour, and costs to the government.

## Annotated model paragraph

*Question style: assess whether a cut in bank rate is likely to increase real output.*

> A lower bank rate reduces the cost of borrowing, so households spend more on credit and firms invest more, and the exchange rate tends to fall, raising net exports. AD shifts right. If there is a negative output gap, real output rises with little inflation. However, if confidence is low, households may save rather than borrow, so the rise in AD is small, and if the economy is close to capacity the main effect is on prices. A rate cut is therefore most effective in a mild downturn when firms and households are willing to borrow.

Why it works: a full transmission chain, a condition on spare capacity, a condition on confidence and a judgement tied to circumstances.

## Common mistakes

- Saying the government sets bank rate; the MPC does, to meet the government's target.
- Explaining how interest rates are determined in money markets, which AS does not require.
- Confusing the budget deficit (flow) with the national debt (stock).
- Calling VAT progressive because richer people pay more in total; check the percentage of income.
- Treating any growth as a supply-side improvement.
- Spending time on the pros and cons of balancing the budget, which the AS specification excludes.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** questions on policy need the transmission chain, ending with the effect on a named objective.
- **Evaluate** questions reward conditions: the output gap, confidence, time lags, elasticity of export and import demand, and the cost to the government.
- When the data show a conflict, say which objective the policy helps and which it may harm.
