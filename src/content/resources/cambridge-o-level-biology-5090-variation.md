This guide covers subtopic **17.1 Variation** of Cambridge O Level Biology 5090, 2026-2028 series. The syllabus asks you to describe variation as differences between individuals of the same species; to understand continuous variation (a range of phenotypes between two extremes, including body length and body mass) and discontinuous variation (a limited number of phenotypes with no intermediates, including ABO blood groups and seed shape and colour in peas); to understand that discontinuous variation is usually caused by genes only and continuous variation by genes and the environment; and to investigate and describe examples of each.

The [Inheritance topic guide](/resources/o-level-biology-5090-inheritance/) covers the whole topic. This page goes deeper on variation, with the graph types and a worked data example. Use the [17.1 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-biology-5090-variation-practice/) to test yourself.

## What is variation?

**Variation is the differences between individuals of the same species.** No two people are exactly alike, apart from identical twins, and no two pea plants in a field are exactly the same. These differences are in the **phenotype**, the observable features of an organism, such as height, eye colour or blood group.

Variation matters because it is the raw material for natural selection (see the selection subtopic). If all the individuals in a population were identical, none would be better adapted than the others.

## Two types of variation

### Continuous variation

**Continuous variation results in a range of phenotypes between two extremes.** There are many possible values in between, and the values form a smooth range, with no clear groups. Examples:

- body length and body height;
- body mass;
- length of leaves from the same type of tree;
- skin colour.

Measurements for continuous variation are numbers, such as 152 cm, 153.4 cm and so on. When plotted as a graph, most individuals have a value near the middle of the range and few have extreme values, which gives a bell-shaped curve.

### Discontinuous variation

**Discontinuous variation results in a limited number of phenotypes with no intermediates.** Each individual falls into one clear category.

- **ABO blood groups** in humans: A, B, AB or O. A person cannot be between A and B.
- **Seed shape in peas**: round or wrinkled.
- **Seed colour in peas**: yellow or green.
- The ability to roll the tongue (a person either can or cannot).

For discontinuous variation, the data are in categories, so the results are best shown as a **bar chart** with gaps between the bars. For continuous variation, the results are best shown as a **histogram** or a line graph, because the values are numerical and run on from each other.

| Feature | Continuous variation | Discontinuous variation |
|---|---|---|
| Phenotypes | A full range between two extremes | A few distinct categories |
| Intermediates | Yes | No |
| Examples | Height, body mass | Blood group, pea shape and colour |
| Cause | Genes and the environment | Usually genes only |
| Typical graph | Histogram or bell-shaped curve | Bar chart |

## Causes of variation

### Genes

Many differences between individuals are inherited from their parents. The genes determine, for example, a person's blood group. The blood group cannot be changed by the environment, so ABO blood groups are an example of variation caused by **genes only**.

### The environment

The environment affects how an organism develops. For example, a plant given plenty of light and water grows taller than a genetically identical plant in shade or poor soil. A person's body mass is affected by diet and exercise as well as by genes.

### Why continuous variation depends on both

**Continuous variation is caused by genes and the environment.** Height is a good example. Genes control the potential height, but diet, health and nutrition during growth affect how much of the potential is reached. This means that a graph of height in a population shows a wide range of values.

**Discontinuous variation is usually caused by genes only.** The environment has little or no effect on the category the individual is in. This is why seed shape in peas does not change if the plant is watered more, and a person's blood group does not change with diet.

### Worked example: separating genes from environment

*Two groups of genetically identical bean plants (cuttings from one parent) were grown with different amounts of light. After four weeks the mean heights were 18 cm in the shade and 30 cm in full light.*

1. The plants are genetically identical, so the difference is due to the environment (light).
2. The difference is 30 - 18 = 12 cm.
3. This is 12 divided by 18 x 100 = 66.7 per cent greater than the shade plants.

This shows that height, a continuous feature, is influenced by the environment. For a discontinuous feature, such as seed colour in these plants, the same change in light would not change the seed colour.

## Investigating variation

The syllabus asks you to investigate and describe examples of both types. A typical investigation is as follows.

### Continuous variation: length of leaves

1. Collect a large sample of leaves from the same species, for example 50 leaves from one bush or from different plants of the same species.
2. Measure the length of each leaf with a ruler, to the nearest millimetre.
3. Group the lengths into classes, such as 40 to 49 mm, 50 to 59 mm and so on.
4. Count the number in each class (the frequency).
5. Draw a histogram or a frequency graph.

A sample of 50 leaves is better than 5, because a larger sample gives a more reliable picture of the whole population. It also helps to choose the leaves at random, not just the biggest ones.

### Worked example: frequency data

| Leaf length class (mm) | Number of leaves |
|---|---|
| 40 to 49 | 3 |
| 50 to 59 | 9 |
| 60 to 69 | 18 |
| 70 to 79 | 14 |
| 80 to 89 | 6 |

1. Total number of leaves = 3 + 9 + 18 + 14 + 6 = 50.
2. The most common class (the modal class) is 60 to 69 mm, with 18 leaves.
3. The percentage in the modal class = 18 divided by 50 x 100 = 36 per cent.
4. The graph rises to a peak in the middle and falls on both sides. This shape is typical of continuous variation.

### Discontinuous variation: tongue rolling or blood groups

1. Ask each person in a class whether they can roll their tongue, or use data on blood groups.
2. Count the number in each category.
3. Draw a bar chart, with gaps between the bars.

The results fall in clear groups with no intermediates. For example, in a class of 30 students, 21 may be able to roll the tongue and 9 may not, so 70 per cent can (21 divided by 30 x 100).

## Worked example: classify each feature

*Decide whether each feature shows continuous or discontinuous variation and say what causes it.*

| Feature | Type | Cause |
|---|---|---|
| Hand span | Continuous (any value in a range) | Genes and environment (nutrition, growth) |
| Seed colour in peas (yellow or green) | Discontinuous (two categories) | Genes only |
| Mass of apples on one tree | Continuous | Genes and environment (sunlight, water) |
| ABO blood group | Discontinuous (four categories) | Genes only |
| Ability to roll the tongue | Discontinuous (can or cannot) | Mainly genes |

When classifying, ask two questions. Can I put the individual into a clear category with no in-between? If so, it is discontinuous. Can I measure it as a number that could be anywhere in a range? If so, it is continuous.

## Where variation comes from

At this level, remember three ideas, which are explained in the inheritance subtopic. First, individuals inherit different combinations of genes from their parents, so sexual reproduction produces variation. Second, a change in a gene (a mutation) can produce a new feature. Third, the environment affects how some features develop. The first two are genetic and are passed on. The third is not passed on, because a change caused only by the environment, such as a tall plant that grew tall in good soil, is not inherited by its offspring.

## Presenting and interpreting variation data

| Step | What to do |
|---|---|
| Sample | Use a large, random sample from one species |
| Measure | Use the same method for every individual |
| Group | Put continuous data into equal-sized classes |
| Display | Histogram for continuous data; bar chart for categories |
| Describe | Name the range, the most common class and the shape |
| Conclude | Link the pattern to genes and the environment |

## Common mistakes

- Writing "variation is differences between species". It is between individuals of the same species.
- Saying that continuous variation is caused only by the environment. It is caused by genes and the environment.
- Saying that discontinuous variation has intermediate forms.
- Drawing a bar chart with gaps for continuous data (use a histogram), or a smooth line for categories.
- Using a small sample and then making conclusions about a whole population.
- Giving height as an example of discontinuous variation.
- Forgetting to give an example when asked. Always name one: height or body mass for continuous, blood group for discontinuous.

## Command words

- **Describe**: define variation or the shape of a graph.
- **Understand/Explain**: link each type to genes and the environment.
- **Investigate**: plan the measurement, sample size and display of results.
- **Identify**: decide from data whether variation is continuous or discontinuous.
