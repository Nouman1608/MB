This page is a guide to OCR GCSE (9-1) Mathematics (J560) past papers from June 2024 to June 2026. For each series you get the grade thresholds, copied from OCR's grade-boundary documents, and for June 2025 and June 2024 a short account of what the examiners saw, written by us from OCR's examiner reports. We do not host any papers. Every paper, mark scheme and report is on OCR's own site or its teacher platform.

## Where to get the official papers

- [OCR GCSE Mathematics (J560) qualification page](https://www.ocr.org.uk/qualifications/gcse/mathematics-j560-from-2015/)
- [OCR GCSE Mathematics assessment page, where examiner reports are listed](https://www.ocr.org.uk/qualifications/gcse/mathematics-j560-from-2015/assessment/)
- [OCR past paper finder](https://www.ocr.org.uk/qualifications/past-paper-finder/)
- [OCR grade boundaries page](https://www.ocr.org.uk/administration/grade-boundaries/)

OCR publishes question papers and mark schemes through its past paper finder, and grade boundaries on the page above. Examiner reports for each series are listed on the assessment page for the qualification. The full question papers and mark schemes that the reports refer to are held on Teach Cambridge, OCR's teacher site, so ask your teacher or exams officer if you cannot open them.

Use the three documents together. Sit the paper first, under timed conditions. Mark it strictly with the mark scheme, which tells you what each mark needs. Then read the examiner report for that paper: it tells you why answers that looked right still lost marks. Our notes for each series below are a quick way into that report.

Check which specification a paper was set on. J560 is a linear qualification first assessed in 2017 and still current. It is tiered. Foundation tier candidates sit Papers 1, 2 and 3 (J560/01 to J560/03), and Higher tier candidates sit Papers 4, 5 and 6 (J560/04 to J560/06). Every paper is 1 hour 30 minutes and 100 marks, so each tier totals 300 marks. Papers 1 and 3 (Foundation) and Papers 4 and 6 (Higher) allow a calculator. Paper 2 (Foundation) and Paper 5 (Higher) are non-calculator. The topics apply across both tiers and may appear on any paper.

## June 2025

The June 2025 reports describe candidates who attempted most of each paper and had time to finish. Across all six papers, the examiners return to the same points: show clear, ordered working, use the calculator well on calculator papers, give answers in the form asked for, and write explanations that are short and precise.

**Where marks were lost – J560/01 (Foundation, calculator)**

- Calculator use: many candidates worked by hand on a calculator paper, which led to arithmetic errors.
- Units and applications: converting between metric units caused problems, and the harder topics were equations of straight lines, trigonometry, population density, inverse proportion, bearings and recognising types of graph.
- Presentation and reading: some answers were almost unreadable, some candidates did not read the question carefully, diagrams drawn in pen could not be altered, and the formulae sheet was not used.

**Where marks were lost – J560/02 (Foundation, non-calculator)**

- Working: numbers and calculations were scattered across the page, and too many candidates did not respond to problem-solving questions or did not show enough working in them.
- Geometry: the area of a triangle was found without halving, the properties of quadrilaterals and their rotational symmetry were not known, and shapes were not always notated correctly.
- Number and algebra: division by decimals, negative indices and solving simultaneous equations from a graph were weaker. Converting capacity and time, and metric units such as millilitres and litres, remained a weakness.
- Stronger areas: number properties, bar charts, converting between decimals, percentages and fractions, expanding single brackets and putting probabilities on a tree diagram.

**Where marks were lost – J560/03 (Foundation, calculator)**

- Reading and vocabulary: key information in a question was overlooked, geometric terms (especially those for the circle) were not known, and problems set in context were harder than straight arithmetic.
- Method: candidates who did not know a standard method fell back on trial and improvement, used non-calculator methods for percentages and did not use the fraction function of the calculator.
- Constructions and drawing: freehand drawing, inaccurate constructions and unfamiliarity with construction techniques.
- Layout: working was random, several methods were shown without saying which was the final one, and substitutions into formulae were not clear.

**Where marks were lost – J560/04 (Higher, calculator)**

- Calculator judgement: some candidates used a calculator for questions that were designed to be simpler without one, which made the values harder. Others gave the full calculator display when a stated number of significant figures was asked for.
- Algebra: brackets were left out so the order of operations was wrong, algebra was not set out in a logical flow, and signs were changed by crossing out or over-writing. Candidates could not always form an equation for proportionality and left the proportionality symbol in the expression.
- Accuracy: truncated or heavily rounded values were carried into later steps, and guesswork by trial scored nothing unless the trials were shown.
- Instructions: a correct attempt was sometimes crossed out for a wrong one, and in "show that" questions a fact was stated where a calculation was needed.

**Where marks were lost – J560/05 (Higher, non-calculator)**

- Less familiar topics: sectors in a problem-solving setting, the cosine rule for a side in a non-right-angled triangle, proving two triangles congruent, and rearranging a formula where the subject appears twice.
- Congruence proofs: some relied only on annotated diagrams where a short, well-structured written argument was needed.
- Working: fractions and decimals led to frequent computational errors, and candidates who used trial and improvement or presented several methods with no final choice lost marks.
- Stronger areas: multiplying directed numbers, dividing decimals, inequalities on a number line, two-way tables, proportion, histograms, recurring decimals as fractions and simplifying algebraic fractions.

**Where marks were lost – J560/06 (Higher, calculator)**

- Written responses: candidates of all abilities struggled with questions that asked for a comment or explanation, and some who scored over 90 marks lost theirs there.
- Layout: multi-step solutions were scattered across the page, and candidates switched between methods without crossing anything out.
- Errors: formulae were not used correctly even when given, values were rounded too soon, some candidates avoided the calculator, and there was limited confidence with algebra.
- Mark allocation: candidates should link the marks available and the question's position in the paper to the likely method, since early questions do not need advanced methods.

## June 2024

The June 2024 reports describe well-prepared candidates on the calculator papers, with a clear rise in the Higher Paper 6 marks. Weaker spots were units, negative numbers, vectors, geometry and explanations. Presentation and calculator use come up again and again.

**Where marks were lost – J560/01 (Foundation, calculator)**

- Calculator use: candidates used a calculator well to convert negative indices and standard form, but many still did percentages by hand and used repeated addition or subtraction in place of division, and some did not use a calculator at all.
- Accuracy: early rounding gave inaccurate answers, and answers were not checked to see whether they made sense in the context.
- Presentation and recall: working was scattered, key terms such as median and range were not always known, and the formulae sheet was not used. There was no sign that time was a problem.

**Where marks were lost – J560/02 (Foundation, non-calculator)**

- Number: negative numbers in basic arithmetic were poorly handled, and percentages, decimals and fractions were not linked well. Finding a percentage of an amount, in particular in compound interest, was weak.
- Later questions: quadratic equations, area and perimeter and compound interest were hard to access.
- Units: converting between units (millilitres and litres, for example) and understanding the units of a quantity such as acceleration were weak. Fractions of time and substituting into formulae were also poor, and kinematic formula knowledge was notably weak.
- Vectors: vector knowledge remained very weak, including writing column vectors from drawn vectors.
- Equipment and presentation: some candidates did not use compasses or a 30 cm ruler, and a few digits were written unclearly. The response to "show your working" questions was improving.

**Where marks were lost – J560/03 (Foundation, calculator)**

- Comparing and converting: candidates were confident changing a fraction to a decimal or percentage, but some compared decimals wrongly, and many converted fractions to rounded decimals and lost accuracy.
- Method and drawing: long lists of repeated additions were used instead of division on a calculator, and a number of candidates plotted points for a curve but did not draw it, or joined them with a ruler.
- Reasoning: most did not understand the process of a geometric proof, could not apply algebra to a two-dimensional problem, and gave poorly expressed reasons. The word assumption was not understood by many, and some key vocabulary was unfamiliar.
- Layout: disorganised working with no clear choice of method.

**Where marks were lost – J560/04 (Higher, calculator)**

- Algebra: expressions and equations were set up but then manipulated with errors, especially brackets for the order of operations and expanding brackets.
- Geometry: right-angled triangles were a problem, with the cosine rule chosen where basic trigonometry was enough, and the area formula with sine used when no angle was given. Applying the angle properties of circles to problems, and terms such as alternate angles and the alternate segment theorem, were uncertain.
- Number: calculations were spoilt by early rounding or truncation, percentage multipliers were not well known, and factorising quadratics and completing the square were weaker.

**Where marks were lost – J560/05 (Higher, non-calculator)**

- Less secure topics: calculations with fractions, inverse proportion, the link between linear and volume scale factors of similar shapes, simplifying expressions with fractional coefficients, cube roots of expressions, and finding where a line and a curve intersect.
- Working: scattered working, a choice of methods with no clear final answer, and trial and improvement on multi-mark questions.
- Communication: most candidates now wrote concise explanations with correct terms, and those who annotated diagrams and highlighted key words did better.
- Stronger areas: correlation, outliers, a line of best fit, estimation and density, perimeter problems, vector notation and expanding three brackets.

**Where marks were lost – J560/06 (Higher, calculator)**

- Written explanations: most of the comment and explanation questions came in the first half, and candidates of all abilities struggled to express themselves clearly.
- Layout: a multi-step problem was often tackled by several methods in turn, none of which reached an answer, and with nothing crossed out.
- Mark allocation: candidates would benefit from linking the marks and the position of a question to the likely method, for example not reaching for the sine or cosine rule in a high-mark question near the start of the paper.
- Preparation: familiar questions were answered with great success, and the proportion scoring 80 or more was nearly double that of 2023.

## What examiners keep saying

Across the two series with reports, the same points come up on all six papers.

- Set out your working in order. Marks are hard to award when numbers are scattered across the page or several methods are shown with no final choice. Cross out what you abandon.
- Use your calculator on calculator papers (percentages, division, fractions, powers), but do not force it into a question that is simpler without it.
- Avoid early rounding and truncation. Keep full values until the last step.
- Know units and conversions, including capacity and time, and use brackets to show the order of operations.
- Read the question, give the answer in the form asked for, and note the number of marks and the position of the question in the paper.
- Practise short, precise written explanations and know the vocabulary, including geometric terms and the word assumption.
- Use the formulae sheet.

## Frequently asked questions

**Where can I find OCR GCSE Mathematics (J560) past papers?**
OCR publishes question papers and mark schemes through its past paper finder. Grade boundaries are on OCR's grade boundaries page, and examiner reports are listed on the assessment page for the qualification.

**Which J560 papers should I sit?**
The three papers for your tier: Papers 1, 2 and 3 for Foundation, or Papers 4, 5 and 6 for Higher. Each is 100 marks and 1 hour 30 minutes, and Papers 2 and 5 are non-calculator.

**How are the grade thresholds used?**
The overall grade comes from adding the three paper marks of your tier, out of 300, and comparing the total with that series' boundaries. The paper rows are shown as a guide to how each paper performed.

**What mistakes do J560 examiners report most often?**
Disorganised working, early rounding, not using the calculator well, weak unit conversions, missing brackets, and explanations that are vague or imprecise.

**Can grade thresholds tell me what mark I need next time?**
No. Thresholds change every series and should only be used as a rough guide for the series they belong to, never to predict future ones.
