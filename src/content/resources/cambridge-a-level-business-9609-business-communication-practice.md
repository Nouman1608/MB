All businesses are invented. Money is in dollars.

## Section A

**1.** Define *two-way communication*. **[2]**

**2.** Identify **two** methods of communication and give **one** weakness of each. **[4]**

**3.** Explain the difference between vertical and horizontal communication, using an example of each. **[4]**

**4.** Explain why a business might use a visual method to communicate monthly sales figures to its sales team. **[3]**

**5.** Explain **one** problem associated with upward vertical communication. **[2]**

**6.** Redfearn Hotels has 220 staff. Every manager sends a weekly email update to all staff. Each update takes an average of 4 minutes to read, and there are 9 managers. Staff are paid an average of $15 an hour.

**(a)** Calculate the weekly cost of staff time spent reading the updates. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain **one** way the hotel could reduce this cost without losing important information. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Marlow Vale Engineering is moving its main factory to a new site 25 kilometres away in nine months. The 140 production staff have heard about the move only through rumours in the canteen. Morale has fallen and three skilled machinists have left.

**(a)** Analyse **two** reasons why communication about the move is essential for Marlow Vale. **[6]**

**(b)** Advise Marlow Vale's managers on how to communicate the move from now on. **[8]**

**8.** Brightwater Software has development teams in two countries with a seven-hour time difference. Each team works to its own targets and they communicate mainly by email. Bugs are often fixed twice, once by each team.

**(a)** Identify **two** barriers to communication in this case. **[2]**

**(b)** Analyse how these barriers affect the efficiency of Brightwater. **[6]**

**9.** Hollinghurst Retail has six levels of hierarchy between the managing director and shop-floor staff. A new pricing policy announced by the managing director was applied incorrectly in 40% of stores.

**(a)** Using the communication process, explain **two** points at which the message could have gone wrong. **[4]**

**(b)** Evaluate whether delayering is the best way to improve communication at Hollinghurst. **[10]**

**10.** Evaluate the view that managers should try to stop informal communication in a business. **[12]**

**11.** Callow Kitchens fits 600 kitchens a year. 6% of jobs need a return visit because fitters misunderstood the customer's design, each costing $250. A new process, in which a designer meets the fitter on site for 30 minutes before each job, would cut return visits to 2%. Designers are paid $28 an hour.

**(a)** Calculate the net annual saving from the new process. **[4]**

**(b)** Assess whether the new process is worthwhile for Callow Kitchens. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Communication in which the receiver responds to the sender [1], so the sender can check understanding and both can discuss the message [1].

**2.** Any two methods, each with a matching weakness, for example: spoken [1], no permanent record unless notes are taken [1]; written [1], slow and gives no immediate feedback [1]. Electronic: overload or impersonal; visual: may oversimplify or need explanation. **[4]**

**3.** Vertical communication passes up or down the hierarchy [1], for example a production manager giving targets to supervisors [1]. Horizontal communication passes between people at the same level [1], for example the marketing manager telling the operations manager about a planned promotion [1]. **[4]**

**4.** A chart or dashboard makes trends and comparisons easy to see at a glance [1], so salespeople can quickly spot which products or regions are behind target [1] and act on it sooner than if they had to read tables of figures [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Filtering: staff may report only good news to avoid blame [1], so senior managers make decisions on an over-optimistic picture and problems grow [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** Time per member of staff = 9 × 4 = 36 minutes = 0.6 hours [1]. Total = 220 × 0.6 = 132 hours [1]. Cost = 132 × 15 = **$1,980** a week [1].

**(b)** Combine the nine updates into one weekly bulletin with headings [1], so staff read only the sections relevant to their department, cutting reading time while keeping the information in one record [1]. **[5]**

**7. (a)** Reason 1: the move is a major change that affects staff jobs and travel [1]. Without clear information, uncertainty fills with rumour [1], which has already cut morale and caused three skilled machinists to leave, raising recruitment costs [1]. Reason 2: production must continue during the move, so departments need to coordinate timing [1]. Poor communication could cause lost output or missed orders [1], damaging customer relationships during a risky period [1].

**(b)** Hold face-to-face meetings for all 140 staff, led by a senior manager [1], because the news is sensitive and staff need to ask questions about travel and jobs [1]. Follow up with a written summary of dates and support, such as transport help [1], so staff have an accurate record and rumours lose force [1]. Set up two-way channels, such as a question box or regular team briefings [1], so managers learn staff concerns before more skilled workers leave [1]. Judgement: the priority is the face-to-face meeting soon, because rumours are already causing labour turnover [1]; success depends on managers being honest, including about any job losses, or trust will not return [1]. **[14]**

**8. (a)** Any two: distance and time zones [1]; reliance on a one-way, slow method (email) [1]; departments with separate targets so no horizontal coordination; overload of emails.

**(b)** The seven-hour gap means a question sent by email may wait a whole working day for a reply [1], so problems take longer to resolve and projects slow down [1]. Because each team works to its own targets, neither has a reason to share what it is fixing [1], so bugs are fixed twice and developer time is wasted, raising costs [1]. Duplicated work also means fewer new features are produced from the same wage bill [1], so Brightwater may fall behind rivals in launching updates [1]. **[8]**

**9. (a)** Message: the policy may have been written with unclear terms, so managers interpreted it differently [1] [1]. Channel: passing through six levels, each manager summarised the policy and details were lost or changed [1], and there was no feedback check to confirm understanding before stores applied it [1].

**(b)** For delayering: fewer levels means less distortion and faster messages [1], so a policy reaches stores closer to its original form [1]. It also cuts management salary costs [1]. Against: the remaining managers have wider spans of control [1] and may have less time to explain changes to each store [1]. Delayering is costly and disruptive, with redundancy payments and possible loss of morale [1]. Other remedies may solve the problem more cheaply: sending the policy directly to every store manager electronically with a short video explanation and a required confirmation [1]. Judgement: delayering addresses a cause but is a large structural change for one communication failure [1]; a direct briefing with feedback is the better first step, and delayering is justified only if distortion is repeated across many messages [1] [1]. **[14]**

**10.** Informal communication is communication outside official channels, such as the grapevine [1]. Case for stopping it: rumours can spread inaccurate information [1], for example about job losses, which lowers morale and raises labour turnover [1]; it may bypass managers and undermine their authority [1]. Case against: it is fast and builds relationships between staff [1], which supports teamwork and motivation [1]; it tells managers what staff really think [1], an early warning of problems that formal upward channels may filter [1]. It is also practically impossible to stop, and trying to do so may damage trust [1]. Judgement: managers should not try to stop it but should reduce harmful rumour by giving early, honest formal communication [1]; how far informal communication helps or harms depends on the quality of formal communication and the level of trust in the business [1] [1]. **[12]**

**11. (a)** Return visits now: 600 × 0.06 = 36, cost 36 × 250 = $9,000 [1]. After: 600 × 0.02 = 12, cost $3,000, so gross saving $6,000 [1]. Designer time: 600 × 0.5 = 300 hours × 28 = $8,400 [1]. Net saving = 6,000 − 8,400 = **−$2,400**, a net cost of $2,400 a year [1].

**(b)** On the figures alone the process is not worthwhile: it costs $2,400 a year more than it saves [1]. However, the figures exclude the effect of return visits on customers [1]: a misfitted kitchen damages reputation and may lose future sales and recommendations [1]. Fewer misunderstandings may also improve fitter morale [1]. The meeting could be shortened or used only on complex designs, which might turn the loss into a saving [1]. Judgement: worthwhile only if the reputation gain is significant or if it is targeted at complex jobs [1]. **[10]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving a method when the question asks about a channel, or the reverse.
- Listing barriers without linking them to costs, delays or errors in the case.
- In calculation questions, forgetting to convert minutes into hours.
- Recommending a remedy that does not fit the barrier.
- Ending an evaluate, assess or advise answer without a clear judgement.
