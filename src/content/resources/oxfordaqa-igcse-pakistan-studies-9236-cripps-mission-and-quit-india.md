Key development 3.1.1.4 of the 9236 specification, Road to independence (1939-47), begins with "the
Second World War and its effects on the Indian subcontinent, including the 'Day of Deliverance'" and
"the Quit India movement – aims and impact; the 1942 Cripps mission". This guide covers those
points, from the outbreak of war in 1939 to the suppression of Quit India. It belongs to the
compulsory Pakistan movement topic in Paper 1 Section A. The overall sequence from 1906 to 1947 is
in the [History and Culture of Pakistan study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-pakistan-studies-history-and-culture/).

## How the war began for India

On 3 September 1939 the Viceroy, **Lord Linlithgow**, declared that India was at war with Germany.
He did so without consulting Indian political leaders. Congress said it would support the war only
if Britain promised independence and gave Indians real power at once. When Britain refused,
the **Congress provincial ministries resigned** in October and November 1939.

## The Day of Deliverance (22 December 1939)

Muhammad Ali Jinnah asked Muslims to observe **22 December 1939** as a "Day of Deliverance" from
Congress rule, with prayers and meetings across India. The day showed:

- how bitter Muslim opinion had become after Congress rule in the provinces from 1937 to 1939;
- that the Muslim League now claimed to speak for Muslims nationally;
- that the League was ready to take a position different from Congress during the war.

## The war changes the balance of power

The war made Britain need Indian soldiers, supplies and political calm. Several effects followed.

- **The League's position strengthened.** With Congress out of office and opposed to the war effort,
  the British valued the co-operation of the League and other groups.
- **The August Offer (1940).** Linlithgow offered to expand his Executive Council with more Indian
  members, to set up a body after the war to frame a new constitution, and promised that no
  constitution would be adopted without the agreement of large minorities. The last point gave the
  League, in effect, a veto over any settlement it rejected.
- **The Lahore Resolution (March 1940).** Meeting in Lahore, the League demanded independent states
  in the Muslim-majority areas of the north-west and east. The wartime situation gave the demand
  room to grow.
- **Economic and military strain.** The Indian Army expanded to more than two million men; prices
  rose; and in 1943 famine in Bengal killed very large numbers of people.

## The Cripps Mission (March 1942)

In early 1942 Japan captured Singapore and advanced through Burma towards India's eastern border.
Britain needed Indian support urgently, and the United States pressed for a political settlement.
The War Cabinet sent **Sir Stafford Cripps**, a Labour minister known to sympathise with Indian
demands, with new proposals.

**What Cripps offered:**

- **dominion status** for India after the war, with the right to leave the Commonwealth;
- a **constitution-making body** after the war, elected by the provincial assemblies, with members
  from the princely states;
- the right of any **province to stay out** of the new Indian Union and make its own arrangements;
- during the war, Britain would keep control of **defence**, while Indian leaders would join the
  Viceroy's Council.

**Why Congress rejected it:** it wanted a national government with real power immediately,
including over defence; and it objected to the provincial opt-out, which could break up India.
Gandhi is often quoted as calling the offer a "post-dated cheque".

**Why the League rejected it:** the opt-out came closer than any earlier British offer to accepting
the idea of Pakistan, but it did not concede Pakistan clearly, and the method of deciding whether a
province stayed out could go against Muslims where their majority was narrow.

**Importance:** although it failed, the mission was the first official British plan to allow a
province to stay out of an Indian Union. Muslims could argue afterwards that the principle behind
Pakistan had been accepted.

## The Quit India movement (August 1942)

After Cripps left, Gandhi concluded that Britain would not hand over power during the war. On
**8 August 1942** the All-India Congress Committee, meeting in Bombay, passed the Quit India
resolution, demanding that Britain leave India at once. Gandhi urged Indians to "do or die".

**Aims:** an immediate end to British rule; an Indian government that would decide how India took
part in the war; mass non-violent struggle if Britain refused.

**What happened:** the government arrested Gandhi and the Congress leaders the next morning. Without
leaders, the movement turned violent in many areas: railways, telegraph lines and police stations
were attacked. The British used troops and mass arrests, and tens of thousands were imprisoned.
Within months the movement had been crushed.

**The League's response:** the League refused to take part. It argued that Congress wanted to
inherit power over the whole of India and that Muslims would be dominated; League speakers answered
"Quit India" with the slogan "Divide and Quit".

## Impact of Quit India

- **Congress was sidelined.** Its leaders spent most of the rest of the war in prison, and it could
  not negotiate.
- **The League grew.** With its rival absent, the League organised freely, gained members and formed
  provincial ministries in several Muslim-majority provinces during the war.
- **British attitudes shifted.** The League's refusal to join a rising during wartime contrasted
  with Congress's challenge, and British officials took the League's claims more seriously.
- **Independence came closer.** The scale of the rising showed that Britain could not rule India
  indefinitely against Congress opposition once the war was over.

## Linking forward to 1944-47

The events of 1939-42 set up the final stage of the Pakistan movement.

- **Gandhi's release (1944).** Gandhi was released from prison in 1944 and met Jinnah for talks in
  September that year. The talks failed, but Gandhi's willingness to negotiate with Jinnah directly
  showed how far the League's standing had risen.
- **The Simla Conference (1945).** The new Viceroy, Lord Wavell, called Indian leaders to Simla to
  form a new Executive Council. It broke down when Jinnah insisted that the League alone should
  nominate the Muslim members, a claim Congress rejected.
- **The 1945-46 elections.** The League's strong performance in the Muslim seats tested its claim to
  speak for Muslims, which it had built during the war years.

When you explain the importance of Quit India or Cripps, a sentence linking them to these later
events shows the long-term significance examiners reward.

## Worked example

**Question:** Explain why the Muslim League rejected the Cripps proposals. **[4]**

```
Point 1   the proposals did not clearly accept Pakistan
Explain   provinces could stay out of the Union, but there was no
          promise of a separate Muslim state, which the League had
          demanded in the Lahore Resolution of 1940

Point 2   the opt-out method could work against Muslims
Explain   in provinces where the Muslim majority was narrow, a vote
          in the assembly might keep the province in an India
          dominated by Congress
```

## Common mistakes

- **Confusing the Cripps Mission (1942) with the Cabinet Mission (1946).** Cripps was a member of
  both, which adds to the confusion.
- **Saying the League supported Quit India.** It refused.
- **Dating the Day of Deliverance to 1940.** It was 22 December 1939.
- **Describing Quit India as entirely peaceful.** It began as a non-violent call but led to widespread
  violence after the arrests.

## Exam technique

For "importance" questions, explain the effect on the League's position, not only the events. For
9-mark comparisons, give each event its own developed paragraph and then a judgement that compares
them directly.
