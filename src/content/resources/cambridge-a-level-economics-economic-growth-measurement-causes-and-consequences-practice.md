## Questions

**1.** Define economic growth. **[2]**

**2.** Country L is an invented economy. Real output rose from 810 cu bn in Year 1 to 842.4 cu bn in Year 2. Find the percentage growth in output. **[2]**

**3.** Country M has a price index of 100 in Year 1, the base year. Nominal GDP was 1,800 cu bn in Year 1 and 2,019.6 cu bn in Year 2. The price index in Year 2 was 110.

**(a)** Find real GDP for Year 2. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the percentage change in real output. **[2]**
**(c)** Find the percentage change in nominal GDP. **[1]**

**4.** Using your answers to question 3, explain why the growth of nominal GDP gives a misleading picture of Country M's economic growth. **[3]**

**5.** Country N has real GDP of 3,600 cu bn in Year 1 and 3,708 cu bn in Year 2. Its population is 24.00 million in Year 1 and 24.72 million in Year 2.

**(a)** Calculate the growth rate of real GDP. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate real GDP per person, in cu thousand, in each year. **[2]**
**(c)** Comment on what the figures show about output per person. **[2]**

**6.** Country O has real GDP of 800 cu bn in Year 1. Real GDP then grows by 6% in Year 2, falls by 3% in Year 3 and grows by 4% in Year 4.

**(a)** Calculate real GDP in each of Years 2, 3 and 4, to 2 decimal places. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the total percentage growth between Year 1 and Year 4, to 2 decimal places. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the answer to (b) is not the sum of the three annual rates. **[1]**

**7.** Country P has real GDP of 280 cu bn and grows by 5% in a year. Country Q has real GDP of 2,000 cu bn and grows by 1.5% in the same year.

**(a)** Calculate the increase in real GDP in each country. **[2]**
**(b)** Comment on which country has performed better. **[2]**

**8.** The government of an economy with spare capacity increases spending on road building. Using AD/AS analysis, explain how this may cause economic growth in the short run, and why the effect on the price level depends on how close the economy is to full capacity. **[5]**

**9.** Explain how investment in education and in new technology can raise an economy's long-run rate of economic growth. **[6]**

**10.** The table shows figures for Country R. Real GDP is in constant prices, and the price index is 100 in Year 1.

| Year | Nominal GDP (cu bn) | Price index |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1,000.00 | 100 |
| 2 | 1,081.60 | 104 |
| 3 | 1,122.70 | 109 |
| 4 | 1,204.00 | 112 |
| 5 | 1,319.24 | 118 |

**(a)** Calculate real GDP for each of Years 2 to 5. **[4]**
**(b)** Calculate the growth rate of real GDP in Year 3, to 1 decimal place, and say what it shows. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the growth rate of nominal GDP in Year 3, to 1 decimal place, and explain why it differs from your answer to (b). **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the total growth in real GDP between Year 1 and Year 5. **[2]**

**11.** Explain two reasons why a rise in real GDP may not match the change in a country's living standards. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate whether economic growth always improves the welfare of a country's citizens. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** A rise in the volume of goods and services that an economy produces [1], measured over time by the percentage change in real GDP [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Credit for this kind of definition depends on naming real output and a time period. Writing only "more money" or "higher GDP" leaves out price adjustment.

**2.** Change = 842.4 − 810 = 32.4 [1]. Growth = 32.4 / 810 × 100 = **4.0%** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Dividing by the later year instead of the earlier one gives a different answer. State the base year in your working.

**3.** **(a)** Real GDP = 2,019.6 / 110 × 100 [1] = **1,836.0 cu bn** [1].
**(b)** Growth = (1,836 − 1,800) / 1,800 × 100 [1] = **2.0%** [1].
**(c)** Nominal growth = (2,019.6 − 1,800) / 1,800 × 100 = **12.2%** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Showing the deflating step is the safest route. A growth rate found from nominal figures does not answer a question about real growth.

**4.** Nominal GDP is measured at current prices, so it rises when prices rise as well as when output rises [1]. The price index rose from 100 to 110, so prices rose by 10% [1]. Nominal GDP grew by 12.2%, but real GDP grew by only 2.0%, so most of the apparent growth was higher prices and not more output [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the figures from the previous part. A general statement that nominal GDP "includes inflation" is stronger when backed by the numbers.

**5.** **(a)** Growth = 108 / 3,600 × 100 = **3.0%** [1].
**(b)** Year 1: 3,600 / 24.00 = **150.0** cu thousand [1]. Year 2: 3,708 / 24.72 = **150.0** cu thousand [1].
**(c)** Real GDP per person did not change (0% growth) [1], although total real GDP grew by 3.0%, because the population also grew by 3.0% [1]. On this measure living standards did not rise. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* A comment should compare the two growth rates. Saying only "per capita is 150" does not explain why it is different from the total growth rate.

**6.** **(a)** Year 2: 800 × 1.06 = **848.00** [1]. Year 3: 848 × 0.97 = **822.56** [1]. Year 4: 822.56 × 1.04 = **855.46** [1].
**(b)** Total change = 855.4624 − 800 = 55.4624 [1], so growth = 55.4624 / 800 × 100 = **6.93%** [1].
**(c)** Each year's percentage applies to a different starting level, so the rates compound and do not add (the sum would be 7%) [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers that keep the unrounded value until the last step avoid small rounding differences in the final percentage.

**7.** **(a)** P: 280 × 0.05 = **14 cu bn** [1]. Q: 2,000 × 0.015 = **30 cu bn** [1].
**(b)** P has the faster growth rate (5% against 1.5%) [1], but Q adds more output in absolute terms (30 against 14), because it starts from a much larger base [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A reasoned comment names both the percentage and the absolute change. Picking one country without stating the measure used is less convincing.

**8.** Government spending (G) is a component of AD [1], so more spending shifts the AD curve to the right [1]. With spare capacity, the SRAS curve is relatively flat, so firms can raise output [1] and the price level rises little [1]. Closer to full capacity, SRAS is steeper, so the same rise in AD raises real GDP by less and raises the price level by more [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers say which curve shifts and where, then describe both equilibrium changes. Stating that growth "happens" without the AD shift is incomplete.

**9.** Education raises the skills and productivity of workers [1], so each worker produces more in the same time [1]. New technology allows more output from the same inputs [1]. Both increase the economy's productive capacity, shifting LRAS to the right or the PPC outward [1]. Spending on these also adds to AD in the short run [1]. Because capacity itself rises, growth can be sustained and is not limited by spare capacity running out [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Each cause needs a link to output. Noting the difference between a rise in output per worker and a rise in total output adds depth.

**10.** **(a)** Year 2: 1,081.60 / 104 × 100 = **1,040** [1]. Year 3: 1,122.70 / 109 × 100 = **1,030** [1]. Year 4: 1,204.00 / 112 × 100 = **1,075** [1]. Year 5: 1,319.24 / 118 × 100 = **1,118** [1].
**(b)** (1,030 − 1,040) / 1,040 × 100 = **−1.0%** [1]. Real output fell (negative growth) [1].
**(c)** (1,122.70 − 1,081.60) / 1,081.60 × 100 = **3.8%** [1]. Nominal GDP rose because prices rose by about 4.8% (index 104 to 109), more than offsetting the fall in real output [1].
**(d)** (1,118 − 1,000) / 1,000 × 100 = **11.8%** [1] in real terms over the four years, which is not the sum of the annual rates [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep a working table for the real figures. Using them for later parts avoids repeating the deflating step.

**11.** Reason 1: GDP excludes much unpaid and informal output [1], so measured growth may be a poor guide to the change in what people actually have [1]. Reason 2: GDP is an average and says nothing about distribution [1], so if the gains go to a few households, most people's living standards may not rise [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each reason needs a developed link to living standards. A list of limitations without explanation tends to earn less.

**12.** Benefit: higher real GDP means more goods and services and higher average real incomes [1]. More output raises employment and tax revenue [1], which can fund health and education [1]. Costs: if demand-led growth is near full capacity it may cause inflation [1]; growth can raise resource use and pollution [1]; the gains may be unequal, so inequality may widen [1]. Evaluation: the answer depends on the source of growth, since growth from higher productivity is less likely to raise prices [1]; on distribution, which decides who gains [1]; and on time and starting point, since a low-income economy may gain more from extra output and environmental costs may appear later [1]. Conclusion: growth usually improves welfare, but not always, and the outcome depends on how the growth is generated and shared [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* A conclusion that answers "always" directly, with a stated condition, tends to improve an evaluation. A list of pros and cons with no judgement usually does not.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating growth from nominal GDP when the question asks about real growth.
- Dividing by the later year instead of the earlier year.
- Adding annual growth rates over several years instead of compounding.
- Quoting a per capita figure with no comparison to total growth.
- Giving a number with no unit or percentage sign, or the wrong rounding.
- Describing an AD shift without saying whether there is spare capacity.
- Listing causes of long-run growth without linking them to LRAS or the PPC.
- Listing consequences without any judgement about who gains or when.
