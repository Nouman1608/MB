## Catalysts: the two-part definition

The syllabus definition has two parts, and each is worth a mark:

1. A catalyst **increases the rate** of a chemical reaction.
2. It is **not changed** by the reaction.

Because it is not changed, the same catalyst molecule can be used again and again. A small amount of catalyst can therefore speed up the reaction of a large amount of reactant.

## Why living organisms need enzymes

Cells carry out thousands of chemical reactions. Together these are called **metabolism**. At body temperature most of these reactions would happen far too slowly to keep the organism alive. Raising the temperature enough to speed them up would damage cells. Enzymes solve the problem: they speed up reactions at the temperature of the organism.

The syllabus says enzymes are "involved in all metabolic reactions". Examples you meet in other 5090 topics include:

| Process (topic) | Enzyme-controlled reaction |
|---|---|
| Digestion (Human nutrition) | Amylase breaks down starch to maltose; maltase breaks down maltose to glucose; protease breaks down protein to amino acids; lipase breaks down lipids to fatty acids and glycerol |
| Respiration | Each step that releases energy from glucose is catalysed by an enzyme |
| Photosynthesis | The reactions that make glucose in chloroplasts are catalysed by enzymes |

Enzymes are **proteins**. A protein is a chain of amino acids folded into a particular three-dimensional shape, and that shape includes the active site. This is why anything that changes the shape of the protein changes how well the enzyme works.

## Enzyme action step by step

Learn the steps in order, using the four syllabus terms:

1. **Substrate** molecules and enzyme molecules move about and collide.
2. A substrate molecule enters the **active site**, a region of the enzyme with a shape complementary to the substrate.
3. The substrate binds, forming an **enzyme-substrate complex**.
4. The reaction takes place while the substrate is held in the active site.
5. The **product** (or products) leave the active site.
6. The enzyme is unchanged and its active site is free to bind another substrate molecule.

### Breaking down and building up

Enzymes catalyse two kinds of reaction:

- **Breakdown**: one large substrate is split into smaller products. Example: amylase breaks starch into maltose.
- **Building up (synthesis)**: two or more smaller substrate molecules are joined into one larger product. The active site holds them together so they react.

In both cases the enzyme leaves unchanged.

## Specificity and the lock-and-key hypothesis

Each enzyme catalyses only one reaction, or a small group of very similar reactions. This is **specificity**.

The lock-and-key hypothesis explains it:

- The active site (the lock) has a particular shape.
- Only a substrate (the key) with a **complementary** shape fits into it.
- Other molecules have different shapes, do not fit, and so no enzyme-substrate complex forms and no reaction occurs.

**Applying it.** Amylase digests starch but not protein. The protein molecule does not have a shape complementary to amylase's active site, so it cannot bind. This is why the digestive system needs several different enzymes: amylase and maltase for carbohydrates, protease for proteins and lipase for lipids.

**Naming enzymes.** Many enzyme names end in "-ase", and the first part often names the substrate: **malt**ase acts on **malt**ose, **lip**ase on **lip**ids. This can help you work out an enzyme's substrate in an unfamiliar question.

## Annotated model answer

*Explain how an enzyme catalyses the breakdown of its substrate. [4]*

> The substrate fits into the enzyme's active site, which has a shape complementary to the substrate *(1: active site and complementary shape)*. This forms an enzyme-substrate complex *(1: named complex)*. The substrate is broken down into products while in the active site *(1: reaction in the active site)*, and the products are released, leaving the enzyme unchanged so it can be used again *(1: products released, enzyme unchanged)*.

What makes it a full-mark answer: every syllabus term is used in the correct order, and the final sentence links back to the definition of a catalyst.

**Common weaker versions and why they lose marks:**

- "The substrate has the same shape as the active site." Shapes are **complementary**, not identical.
- "The enzyme reacts with the substrate and is used up." Enzymes are not used up.
- "The enzyme kills the substrate." Enzymes are not living and do not kill; they catalyse reactions.
- "The active site matches the product." It fits the substrate.

## Worked example: an unfamiliar enzyme

*Sucrase is an enzyme that breaks down the sugar sucrose. A student adds sucrase to a solution of sucrose and to a solution of starch. Suggest and explain the results.*

> **Sucrose solution:** sucrose is broken down, because its shape is complementary to the active site of sucrase, so enzyme-substrate complexes form and products are released.
>
> **Starch solution:** no reaction, because starch has a different shape that does not fit the active site of sucrase, so no enzyme-substrate complex forms.

The enzyme's name gives away its substrate, and the explanation in both parts comes from shape and fit. Notice that the answer does not need any knowledge of sucrase beyond the question: "suggest" questions test whether you can apply the lock-and-key model to an enzyme you have not studied.

## Interpreting a diagram of enzyme action

Diagrams in questions usually show four stages from left to right: enzyme and substrate separate; substrate in the active site; products formed in the active site; products leaving. You may be asked to label the active site, the enzyme-substrate complex and the products, or to explain why a second molecule shown with a different shape does not react. For the second type, the answer always comes back to shape: it is not complementary to the active site, so it cannot bind.

## Common mistakes

- Giving only one part of the catalyst definition.
- Describing enzymes as living or as being used up.
- Saying "same shape" instead of "complementary shape".
- Leaving out the enzyme-substrate complex in an "explain enzyme action" answer.
- Stating that an enzyme works on any molecule of the same type, such as "any carbohydrate".
- Bringing temperature and pH into a 5.1 question when it asks only about the mechanism.

## Command words

- **Describe**: give the definition or the sequence of steps.
- **Explain**: say why, for example why amylase cannot digest protein, using shape and fit.
- **State**: a brief answer, such as naming the product of maltase.
- **Suggest**: apply the model to an unfamiliar enzyme or substrate.
