## Structure, bonding and reactivity (3.3.4.1)

Alkenes are **unsaturated** hydrocarbons: they contain only carbon and hydrogen, and they have at least
one carbon–carbon double bond. Ethene, CH₂=CH₂, is the simplest. A non-cyclic alkene with one double bond
has the general formula CₙH₂ₙ.

A double bond has two shared pairs of electrons between the same two carbon atoms. That makes the C=C
bond a **centre of high electron density**. Species that are short of electrons are attracted to it.
These are **electrophiles**: electron pair acceptors, such as H⁺ or the δ+ end of a polar molecule.

This explains the typical reaction of alkenes. An electrophile attacks the double bond, one of the two
bonds breaks, and two new single bonds form. The result is **addition**: two species add across the
double bond and the product is saturated.

## Electrophilic addition (3.3.4.2)

Every mechanism in this section follows the same two steps.

1. The electrophile accepts a pair of electrons from the C=C bond. A bond in the electrophile breaks
   heterolytically, and a **carbocation** (an ion with a positive carbon) forms with a negative ion.
2. The negative ion uses a lone pair to bond to the positive carbon.

Curly arrows show the movement of a pair of electrons. Each arrow starts at a bond or a lone pair and ends
at the atom (or bond position) the electrons move to.

### With hydrogen bromide

H–Br is polar: Hδ+–Brδ−. The δ+ hydrogen is the electrophile. The reaction happens at room temperature.

```
Step 1   H₂C=CH₂  +  Hδ+–Brδ−   →   H₃C–CH₂⁺  +  Br⁻
         arrow 1: from the C=C bond to the H of H–Br
         arrow 2: from the H–Br bond to the Br atom

Step 2   H₃C–CH₂⁺  +  :Br⁻   →   H₃C–CH₂Br   (bromoethane)
         arrow 3: from a lone pair on Br⁻ to the positive carbon
```

### With bromine, and the test for unsaturation

Br₂ is not polar. As a bromine molecule approaches the high electron density of the double bond, the
electrons in Br–Br are repelled away from the nearer atom. This **induces a dipole**: the nearer Br atom
becomes δ+ and acts as the electrophile.

```
Step 1   H₂C=CH₂  +  Brδ+–Brδ−   →   BrH₂C–CH₂⁺  +  Br⁻
         arrow 1: from the C=C bond to the δ+ Br
         arrow 2: from the Br–Br bond to the δ− Br

Step 2   BrH₂C–CH₂⁺  +  :Br⁻   →   BrH₂C–CH₂Br   (1,2-dibromoethane)
```

**Test for unsaturation.** Shake the compound with bromine water at room temperature. An alkene
decolourises it: **orange to colourless**, because the bromine is used up in addition. A saturated
compound such as an alkane leaves the solution orange.

### With sulfuric acid

Cold, concentrated sulfuric acid adds across the double bond. One H of H₂SO₄ is the electrophile.

```
Step 1   H₂C=CH₂  +  H–OSO₂OH   →   H₃C–CH₂⁺  +  ⁻OSO₂OH
         arrow 1: from the C=C bond to an H of H₂SO₄
         arrow 2: from that O–H bond to the O atom

Step 2   H₃C–CH₂⁺  +  ⁻OSO₂OH   →   CH₃CH₂OSO₂OH   (ethyl hydrogensulfate)
         arrow 3: from a lone pair on the negative O to the positive carbon
```

The negative O of the hydrogensulfate ion, not the S or an OH, bonds to the carbon. Making alcohols by
hydrating alkenes is taught in the [alcohols study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-alcohols/).

### Major and minor products (unsymmetrical alkenes)

An **unsymmetrical** alkene has different groups on the two carbons of the double bond, for example
propene, CH₃CH=CH₂. With HBr or H₂SO₄, the electrophile can add to either carbon, so two carbocations are
possible and two products form.

Carbocations are classified by how many alkyl groups are bonded to the positive carbon:

| Type | Alkyl groups on C⁺ | Example | Stability |
|---|---|---|---|
| primary | 1 | CH₃CH₂CH₂⁺ | least stable |
| secondary | 2 | CH₃CH⁺CH₃ | more stable |
| tertiary | 3 | (CH₃)₃C⁺ | most stable |

Alkyl groups release electron density towards the positive carbon. This spreads out (reduces) the
positive charge and stabilises the ion. More alkyl groups give more stability. The more stable
carbocation forms more readily, so most of the product forms through it. That product is the **major
product**; the other is the **minor product**.

**Worked example 1.** Propene reacts with hydrogen bromide. Name the major and minor products and explain
which is major.

- If H adds to the end carbon (C1), the positive charge is on C2: CH₃CH⁺CH₃, a **secondary** carbocation.
  Br⁻ then bonds to C2, giving **2-bromopropane**.
- If H adds to C2, the positive charge is on C1: CH₃CH₂CH₂⁺, a **primary** carbocation. Br⁻ bonds to
  C1, giving **1-bromopropane**.
- The secondary carbocation has two electron-releasing alkyl groups, so it is more stable than the
  primary carbocation and forms more readily.
- Major product: **2-bromopropane**. Minor product: **1-bromopropane**.

Bromine does not give major and minor products. Both atoms that add are Br, so either route leads to the
same compound (1,2-dibromopropane from propene). A symmetrical alkene such as but-2-ene also gives one
product with HBr, because both carbocations are identical.

## Addition polymers (3.3.4.3)

Alkenes and substituted alkenes (alkenes with another atom or group in place of an H, such as
chloroethene, CH₂=CHCl) join together in **addition polymerisation**. The double bonds open and the
monomers bond into a long saturated chain. Nothing else is produced.

### Repeating units, monomers and names

The **repeating unit** is the two-carbon unit that came from one monomer. To draw it, open the double
bond, keep every substituent on the same carbon, and draw extension bonds from both carbons. Put
brackets round it and an n outside.

```
monomer               repeating unit

  H   Cl                  H   Cl
  |   |                   |   |
  C = C              —(— C — C —)—n
  |   |                   |   |
  H   H                   H   H
```

To go **back** from a section of chain, find the two-carbon pattern that repeats, then put the double
bond back between those two carbons to get the monomer.

**IUPAC naming:** write poly, then the monomer's name in brackets. Ethene gives poly(ethene); propene
gives poly(propene); chloroethene gives poly(chloroethene); phenylethene gives poly(phenylethene).

**Worked example 2.** A section of an addition polymer is –CH₂–C(CH₃)₂–CH₂–C(CH₃)₂–CH₂–C(CH₃)₂–. Give
the repeating unit, the monomer and the polymer's name.

- The repeating two-carbon unit is **–CH₂–C(CH₃)₂–**.
- Put the double bond back: monomer **CH₂=C(CH₃)₂**, which is **2-methylpropene**.
- Polymer name: **poly(2-methylpropene)**.

**Worked example 3.** A sample of poly(chloroethene) has an average Mr of 1.25 × 10⁵. How many monomer
units are in an average chain? (Ar: H = 1.0, C = 12.0, Cl = 35.5)

- Mr of C₂H₃Cl = 2(12.0) + 3(1.0) + 35.5 = 62.5. The repeating unit has the same formula as the monomer.
- Number of units = 1.25 × 10⁵ / 62.5 = **2000**.

### Why addition polymers are unreactive

The polymer chain is saturated. It contains only strong, non-polar (or almost non-polar) C–C and C–H
bonds, and there is no double bond left for electrophiles to attack. So polyalkenes are unreactive.

### Intermolecular forces in polyalkenes

The chains in poly(ethene) and poly(propene) are non-polar, so the forces between them are **van der
Waals forces**. Each chain is very long and has a very large number of electrons, and neighbouring
chains are in contact along their length. So, although each interaction is weak, the total attraction
between chains is large. That is why these polymers are solids. In poly(chloroethene) the C–Cl bond is
polar, so there are also **permanent dipole–dipole forces** between the chains.

### PVC and plasticisers

Poly(chloroethene) is commonly known as **PVC**. Unplasticised PVC is rigid; typical uses include window
frames, drainpipes and guttering. A **plasticiser** is a small molecule mixed into the polymer. Its
molecules get between the chains and push them further apart. This weakens the intermolecular forces
between chains, so the chains slide past each other more easily and the PVC becomes flexible.
Plasticised PVC is used, for example, for electrical cable insulation and flooring.

### How understanding developed over time

The specification asks you to appreciate that knowledge of polymer production and properties has
developed over time. One example: early poly(ethene) was made at very high pressure, which gives
branched chains. Later, catalysts allowed poly(ethene) to be made at lower pressure with much less
branching. Less-branched chains pack more closely, so the forces between them are stronger and the
material is denser and stiffer. Learning how to modify PVC with plasticisers is another example: the same
polymer can be given very different properties.

## Common errors

- Starting the first curly arrow from a carbon atom instead of from the double bond.
- Leaving out the δ+/δ− on H–Br, or the induced dipole on Br₂.
- Drawing the second arrow from the negative ion's charge sign rather than from a lone pair.
- Calling the more stable carbocation "more reactive", or saying alkyl groups "attract" electrons.
- Claiming that bromine gives major and minor products with propene.
- Leaving the double bond in a repeating unit, or forgetting the extension bonds and n.
- Putting a substituent on the wrong carbon, so the repeating unit becomes three carbons long.
- Saying polyalkenes are unreactive because "the bonds are weak".
