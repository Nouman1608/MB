## Questions

**1.** State the unit, and its symbol, for each of: current, potential difference and resistance. **[3]**

**2.** A resistor of resistance 330 Ω has a p.d. of 4.5 V across it. Calculate the current through it in milliamperes. **[3]**

**3.** An ammeter reads 0.28 A and a voltmeter across the same component reads 7.0 V. The component has constant resistance.

**(a)** Calculate its resistance. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the p.d. needed to make a current of 0.12 A flow through it. **[2]**

**4.** **(a)** State what is meant by potential difference. **[1]**
**(b)** What does a p.d. of 9 V across a component tell you about the energy transferred? **[1]**
**(c)** A 6.0 V supply is connected across a 20 Ω resistor. The resistor is then replaced by a 40 Ω resistor. Calculate the current in each case and describe the change. **[3]**

**5.** Describe how you would investigate the effect of the length of a wire on its resistance. Include the circuit and how you would obtain reliable results. **[5]**

**6.** In an investigation, the current through each length of one wire was kept at 0.60 A. The p.d. across the wire was recorded.

| Length (m) | 0.30 | 0.60 | 0.90 |
|---|---|---|---|
| P.d. (V) | 1.44 | 2.88 | 4.32 |

**(a)** Calculate the resistance for each length. **[3]**
**(b)** State the relationship between resistance and length shown by the data. **[1]**
**(c)** Predict the resistance of 1.20 m of the same wire at the same temperature. **[1]**

**7.** The table shows readings for a filament lamp.

| P.d. (V) | 2.0 | 4.0 | 6.0 | 8.0 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Current (A) | 0.30 | 0.50 | 0.64 | 0.75 |

**(a)** Calculate the resistance at 2.0 V and at 8.0 V. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe how the resistance changes and explain why. **[3]**

**8.** Describe the I-V graph of a diode and state what it shows about how the diode works. **[4]**

**9.** **(a)** An NTC thermistor is connected across a 3.0 V supply. Its resistance is 1800 Ω at 25 °C and 300 Ω at 75 °C. Calculate the current in mA at each temperature. **[3]**
**(b)** An LDR is in the same type of circuit. State how its resistance changes when the light becomes brighter, and what happens to the ammeter reading. **[2]**

**10.** Two components, X and Y, are tested. The table gives their readings for both directions of current.

| P.d. (V) | -6.0 | -3.0 | 3.0 | 6.0 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Current through X (A) | -0.12 | -0.06 | 0.06 | 0.12 |
| Current through Y (A) | -0.15 | -0.10 | 0.10 | 0.15 |

**(a)** State which component is linear and give a reason from the data. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the resistance of X. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the resistance of Y at 3.0 V and at 6.0 V. **[2]**
**(d)** State what the results show about Y and name a component that could be Y. **[1]**

**11.** A student investigates an NTC thermistor.

**(a)** Describe a circuit and method for finding the thermistor's resistance at different temperatures. **[3]**
**(b)** The student keeps the p.d. at 4.0 V. The current is 2.0 mA at 10 °C and 20.0 mA at 70 °C. Calculate the resistance at each temperature. **[2]**
**(c)** State the pattern shown. **[1]**
**(d)** The student says: "This proves the thermistor does not obey V = IR." Evaluate this claim. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Current: ampere, A [1]. Potential difference: volt, V [1]. Resistance: ohm, Ω [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Give both the name and the symbol, and use the Greek letter omega for the ohm. Writing only a symbol or only a name risks losing the credit for that quantity.

**2.** I = V ÷ R [1]. I = 4.5 ÷ 330 [1]. I = 0.0136 A = **13.6 mA** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Write the equation and the substitution as separate lines. A wrong unit, such as leaving the answer as 0.0136 when milliamperes were asked for, can cost the final mark.

**3. (a)** R = V ÷ I = 7.0 ÷ 0.28 [1] = **25 Ω** [1].
**(b)** V = I × R = 0.12 × 25 [1] = **3.0 V** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Use your answer to part (a) in part (b), and set out each substitution on its own line so your method is easy to follow.

**4. (a)** The energy transferred per unit charge (per coulomb) as charge moves between two points [1].
**(b)** Each coulomb of charge transfers **9 J** of energy [1].
**(c)** 20 Ω: I = 6.0 ÷ 20 = **0.30 A** [1]. 40 Ω: I = 6.0 ÷ 40 = **0.15 A** [1]. Doubling the resistance halves the current (same p.d.) [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Definitions of p.d. should mention energy and charge. "Voltage pushes the current" is too vague to describe it.

**5.** Connect the wire in series with a power supply, an ammeter and a switch [1]. Connect a voltmeter across the length of wire being tested [1]. Use a metre rule and a crocodile clip to change the length tested, and record the length [1]. Calculate R = V ÷ I for each length, repeat and take a mean [1]. Use a small current and switch off between readings so the wire stays at constant temperature [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Method questions are easiest to answer in a logical order: circuit, what you change, what you measure, calculation, control. Naming a control variable and a reason is a typical way to earn the last point.

**6. (a)** R = V ÷ I: 1.44 ÷ 0.60 = **2.4 Ω** [1]; 2.88 ÷ 0.60 = **4.8 Ω** [1]; 4.32 ÷ 0.60 = **7.2 Ω** [1].
**(b)** Resistance is directly proportional to length [1].
**(c)** 4 × 2.4 = **9.6 Ω** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* "Directly proportional" is more complete than "as length goes up, resistance goes up", because it describes the pattern in the numbers. Show the division for each length.

**7. (a)** 2.0 ÷ 0.30 = **6.7 Ω** [1]; 8.0 ÷ 0.75 = **10.7 Ω** [1].
**(b)** Resistance increases as the p.d. and current increase [1]. The filament gets hotter [1]. Its ions vibrate more, so electrons collide with them more often [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Explanations earn more when they name the cause (temperature rise) and the effect on charge flow (more collisions). Stating only "it heats up" is incomplete.

**8.** In the forward direction there is almost no current until a small p.d. is reached [1]. Above that p.d. the current rises steeply [1]. In the reverse direction the current is almost zero [1]. A diode lets current flow in one direction only [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A sketch or description must show both directions. A graph that is symmetrical about the origin is a typical error that loses the reverse-direction point.

**9. (a)** I = V ÷ R. At 25 °C: 3.0 ÷ 1800 [1] = 0.00167 A = **1.67 mA** [1]. At 75 °C: 3.0 ÷ 300 = 0.010 A = **10 mA** [1].
**(b)** Resistance decreases [1]. The ammeter reading increases [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* State the direction of the change in both the resistance and the current for part (b). Answering only one of them may leave a mark unclaimed.

**10. (a)** X is linear [1]. Its current is proportional to the p.d. (it doubles when p.d. doubles), and the ratio V ÷ I is the same each time [1].
**(b)** R = 6.0 ÷ 0.12 = **50 Ω** [1].
**(c)** At 3.0 V: 3.0 ÷ 0.10 = **30 Ω**; at 6.0 V: 6.0 ÷ 0.15 = **40 Ω** [2].
**(d)** Y is non-linear: its resistance increases as p.d. increases, like a filament lamp [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote figures from the table to support the conclusion. "It is a straight line" cannot be seen from four readings, whereas the constant ratio V ÷ I is evidence.

**11. (a)** Connect the thermistor in series with a power supply and ammeter, with a voltmeter across the thermistor [1]. Place the thermistor in a water bath and read the temperature with a thermometer [1]. At each temperature read V and I, calculate R = V ÷ I, and repeat as the temperature changes [1].
**(b)** 10 °C: R = 4.0 ÷ 0.0020 = **2000 Ω** [1]. 70 °C: R = 4.0 ÷ 0.0200 = **200 Ω** [1].
**(c)** Resistance decreases as the temperature increases [1].
**(d)** The claim is not supported [1]. The p.d. was constant, and the resistance changed because the temperature changed. V = IR still holds at each temperature, and to test linearity you need an I-V graph at constant temperature [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* In an evaluation, say what the data can show and what it cannot. Pointing out that temperature, not p.d., was varied is the key idea.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving mA or kΩ unconverted before substituting.
- Missing or incorrect units, including A written for resistance.
- Writing the answer with no working when the question says "calculate".
- Confusing the thermistor (temperature) with the LDR (light).
- Using the gradient of a curve as the resistance.
- Drawing a symmetrical diode graph.
- Not naming the cause when explaining a lamp's changing resistance: temperature rises, ions vibrate more, more collisions.
- Method answers with no mention of what is kept constant or how readings are repeated.
- Claiming that V = IR does not apply to non-linear components. It applies at every point.
