This guide covers one subtopic in depth: **3.7.3 Nuclear fission**, statements a to e of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Physics (9203) specification, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. Statement b, which names the two fissionable substances, is marked **P** (full Physics award only). The specification says you should be able to **sketch or complete a labelled diagram to illustrate how a chain reaction may occur**, and that you should be aware that **most nuclear reactors use uranium-235**. This page sets out the fission process step by step, shows how to draw and count a chain reaction, and explains how a reactor keeps it under control.

The [half-life, contamination, fission and fusion guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-half-life-contamination-nuclear-fission-and-fusion/) treats fission alongside fusion and half-life. Afterwards, use the [fission revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-nuclear-fission-revision-notes/) and the [fission practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-nuclear-fission-practice/).

## What the specification asks for

| Statement | Content |
|---|---|
| a | Fission is the splitting of a large, unstable nucleus and the release of energy |
| b (P) | Two fissionable substances in common use in reactors: uranium-235 and plutonium-239; most reactors use uranium-235 |
| c | The nucleus must first absorb a neutron; it splits into two smaller nuclei, releasing two or three neutrons and energy; far more energy than a chemical reaction of similar mass |
| d | A chain reaction occurs when fission neutrons cause further fission; control rods absorb neutrons so that on average only one neutron per fission causes another |
| e | Fission produces radioactive waste that may stay dangerous for a long time; its disposal must be managed carefully and influences the use of nuclear power |

## The fission process, step by step (statements a and c)

1. A **neutron** is absorbed by a uranium-235 (or plutonium-239) nucleus.
2. The nucleus becomes **unstable**.
3. It **splits** into two smaller nuclei, of roughly similar size (the fission products).
4. **Two or three neutrons** are released.
5. **Energy** is released, carried mainly as the kinetic energy of the fast-moving products, together with gamma radiation.

The order matters in answers: the neutron is absorbed first, then the nucleus splits. Saying "a neutron hits the nucleus and splits it" misses the absorption step, which is what makes the nucleus unstable. Fission produced this way is sometimes called **induced** fission, because it needs the absorbed neutron to start it.

**One example.** One possible fission of uranium-235 produces barium and krypton:

```
²³⁵₉₂U + ¹₀n  →  ¹⁴¹₅₆Ba + ⁹²₃₆Kr + 3 ¹₀n + energy
Mass numbers:   235 + 1 = 236     141 + 92 + 3 = 236
Atomic numbers: 92 + 0 = 92       56 + 36 + 0 = 92
```

Other splits are possible, with different products and two neutrons instead of three. You do not need to learn these equations, but you should recognise that the products are two **smaller** nuclei plus **two or three** neutrons, and that the numbers still balance.

**How much energy?** The energy released by the fission of a given mass of uranium-235 is far greater than that released by burning a similar mass of a fuel such as coal: millions of times greater. This is why a reactor needs only a small mass of fuel compared with a fossil-fuel power station of the same output.

## Chain reactions (statement d)

Each fission releases two or three neutrons. If these neutrons are absorbed by other uranium-235 nuclei, those nuclei split too, releasing more neutrons. This is a **chain reaction**.

**Counting an uncontrolled chain.** Suppose every fission releases 3 neutrons and every neutron causes another fission.

```
Generation 1: 1 fission  → 3 neutrons
Generation 2: 3 fissions → 9 neutrons
Generation 3: 9 fissions → 27 neutrons
Generation 4: 27 fissions
```

The number of fissions triples each generation, so the rate of energy release grows extremely quickly. If each fission caused only 2 more, the pattern would be 1, 2, 4, 8, ... which still grows rapidly.

**Drawing a chain reaction diagram.** Draw one neutron arrow reaching a large nucleus labelled uranium-235. From it draw two smaller circles (fission products) and two or three neutron arrows. Each neutron arrow then reaches another uranium-235 nucleus, which splits in the same way. Label at least: neutron, uranium-235 nucleus, fission products (smaller nuclei), released neutrons and energy. Draw at least two generations so the branching is clear.

## Controlling the reaction (statement d)

In a nuclear reactor the chain reaction must stay **steady**. **Control rods**, made of a material that absorbs neutrons, are placed between the fuel rods. They absorb enough of the fission neutrons that, on average, **only one neutron from each fission goes on to cause another fission**. The rate of fission, and so the rate of energy transfer, then stays constant.

- Lowering the control rods further into the reactor absorbs more neutrons, so the rate of fission falls.
- Raising them absorbs fewer neutrons, so the rate rises.
- Fully lowering them can shut the reactor down.

If each fission releases 3 neutrons, then to keep the chain steady, on average 2 of every 3 must be absorbed or lost and only 1 must cause a new fission.

**From fission to electricity.** The energy released heats a coolant, which is used to turn water into steam. The steam turns turbines, which drive generators, as in any thermal power station.

## Nuclear waste (statement e)

The fission products and used fuel are **radioactive**. Some of the isotopes have long half-lives, so the waste stays dangerous for a very long time. It must be stored securely, shielded, cooled where necessary, and kept out of the environment and away from people, often for many years. The difficulty and cost of managing this waste safely is one of the factors that decides whether a country uses nuclear power to generate electricity.

A balanced evaluation of nuclear power includes points on both sides: it releases no carbon dioxide from burning fuel during operation and transfers a large amount of energy from a small mass of fuel; but it produces long-lived radioactive waste, there is a risk of accidents releasing radioactive material, and power stations are expensive to build and to dismantle at the end of their life.

## Common mistakes

- Saying the neutron "breaks" the nucleus without being absorbed first.
- Saying fission produces "two or three nuclei". It produces two smaller nuclei and two or three **neutrons**.
- Saying control rods "slow down" neutrons. They **absorb** them.
- Thinking a chain reaction needs every neutron to cause fission. A steady reaction needs, on average, only one.
- Confusing fission (splitting large nuclei) with fusion (joining light nuclei).
- Saying nuclear waste is dangerous "because it is hot". The hazard is mainly its radioactivity.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** fission: absorb neutron, unstable, split into two smaller nuclei, two or three neutrons released, energy released.
- **Explain** a chain reaction: the neutrons released go on to cause further fissions.
- **Explain** the role of control rods: they absorb neutrons, so that on average one neutron per fission causes another.
- **Evaluate** nuclear power: give advantages and disadvantages, then a judgement linked to the context in the question.
