This guide covers subtopic **9.3, Chemical periodicity of other elements**, from Topic 9, The Periodic Table: chemical periodicity, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is AS Level content.

Subtopic 9.3 is an application skill. It contains no new facts to learn: it asks you to use the Period 3 patterns from 9.1 and 9.2, the ionisation energy ideas from Topic 2 and the Group 2 trends from Topic 10 on elements you have not studied. The [Periodicity Across Period 3](/resources/as-periodicity-across-period-3/) guide includes one short deduction. This page sets out a method for both kinds of 9.3 question and works through several more examples. Condensed notes are in the [9.3 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-as-chemical-periodicity-of-other-elements-revision-notes/), and the [9.3 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-as-chemical-periodicity-of-other-elements-practice/) give more.

## What 9.3 asks you to do

1. **Predict** the characteristic properties of an element in a given group by using knowledge of chemical periodicity.
2. **Deduce** the nature, possible position in the Periodic Table and identity of unknown elements from given information about physical and chemical properties.

The first is "forwards": you are told the group and you predict. The second is "backwards": you are given data and you work out the group, the period, and possibly the element.

## The tools you bring from Period 3

**Group number gives the outer electrons, and so the formulas.** The highest oxidation number is the number of outer-shell electrons. That fixes the formula of the highest oxide and chloride.

| Group | Outer electrons | Typical highest oxide | Typical highest chloride |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | M₂O | MCl |
| 2 | 2 | MO | MCl₂ |
| 13 | 3 | M₂O₃ | MCl₃ |
| 14 | 4 | XO₂ | XCl₄ |
| 15 | 5 | X₄O₁₀ (+5) | XCl₅ (+5) |
| 16 | 6 | XO₃ (+6), also XO₂ (+4) | not required |

**Position across the period gives the bonding.** Elements on the left are metals with low electronegativity. They form ionic oxides, which are basic, and ionic chlorides, which dissolve to give roughly neutral solutions. Elements on the right are non-metals with high electronegativity. They form covalent oxides, which are acidic, and covalent chlorides, which are hydrolysed by water to give acidic solutions and fumes of HCl. The boundary region (aluminium in Period 3) gives amphoteric oxides.

**Position down a group adjusts the picture.** Down a group, atomic radius increases and ionisation energy decreases, so metallic character increases. Group 2 shows this: the elements react more vigorously with water further down, and the hydroxides become more soluble, giving more alkaline solutions.

**Ionisation energies give the group.** In a set of successive ionisation energies, the large jump comes after the last outer-shell electron has been removed. A large jump between the third and fourth ionisation energies means three outer electrons: Group 13.

## Predicting: the method

For an element in a stated group and period:

1. Write its number of outer electrons and highest oxidation number.
2. Write the formulas of its oxide and chloride.
3. Decide metal or non-metal from its position across the period, and adjust for its position down the group.
4. Predict the bonding, then the behaviour of the oxide and chloride with water, acid and alkali.
5. Predict physical properties (melting point, conductivity) from the structure.

**Worked example 1: potassium (Group 1, Period 4).**

- One outer electron, oxidation number +1: oxide K₂O, chloride KCl.
- A Group 1 metal further down than sodium, so more reactive than sodium.
- K₂O is ionic and basic: K₂O + H₂O → 2KOH, a strongly alkaline solution.
- KCl is ionic and dissolves without reaction, giving a neutral solution.
- With water: 2K + 2H₂O → 2KOH + H₂, more vigorous than the sodium reaction.
- A giant metallic lattice that conducts electricity, with one delocalised electron per atom.

**Worked example 2: germanium (Group 14, Period 4).**

- Four outer electrons, highest oxidation number +4: oxide GeO₂, chloride GeCl₄.
- By analogy with silicon, GeCl₄ is predicted to be a covalent, simple molecular liquid that is hydrolysed by water: GeCl₄ + 2H₂O → GeO₂ + 4HCl, giving an acidic solution and fumes of HCl.
- GeO₂ is predicted to be a high-melting solid, like SiO₂. Because germanium is further down the group, it has more metallic character than silicon, so its oxide is expected to be less acidic than SiO₂.

**Worked example 3: selenium (Group 16, Period 4).**

- Six outer electrons: oxides SeO₂ (+4) and SeO₃ (+6), by analogy with sulfur.
- A non-metal on the right of its period, so its oxides are covalent and acidic: SeO₂ + H₂O → H₂SeO₃, an acidic solution.
- The oxides react with sodium hydroxide, for example SeO₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂SeO₃ + H₂O.

Notice how each prediction is justified by a named analogy ("like sulfur", "like silicon") and a trend ("further down the group, more metallic"). The justification earns credit as well as the prediction.

## Deducing: the method

Work through the evidence in this order and write down what each piece tells you:

1. **Formulas of compounds** → oxidation number → group.
2. **Successive ionisation energies** → number of outer electrons → group.
3. **Oxide behaviour** (pH in water; reaction with acid and/or NaOH) → metal, non-metal or boundary.
4. **Chloride behaviour** (dissolves or hydrolyses; pH; fumes) → ionic or covalent.
5. **Physical data** (melting point, conductivity) → structure.
6. **Reactivity compared with a known element** → period (higher or lower in the group).

Then state the group, the likely period, and an identity consistent with all the data. If two elements fit, say so.

**Worked example 4.** Element E has the following properties:

- Its chloride has the formula ECl₂. It is a white solid with a high melting point that dissolves in water to give a neutral solution.
- Its oxide reacts with water to give a solution of pH about 12.
- E reacts steadily with cold water, giving hydrogen, faster than magnesium does.

*Deduce the group, and suggest the identity, of E.*

- ECl₂ → oxidation number +2 → **Group 2**.
- High melting point, neutral solution → **ionic** chloride → a metal.
- Basic oxide giving pH about 12 → more alkaline than MgO in water, so the hydroxide is more soluble than Mg(OH)₂: further down Group 2.
- Reacts with cold water faster than magnesium → further down Group 2.
- E is a Group 2 metal below magnesium: **calcium** is the most likely identity. Equation: Ca + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + H₂.

**Worked example 5.** Element Z has the following properties:

- The first four ionisation energies show a large jump between the third and the fourth.
- Its oxide, Z₂O₃, is insoluble in water but dissolves in both dilute hydrochloric acid and aqueous sodium hydroxide.
- Its chloride, ZCl₃, dissolves in water to give a solution of pH about 3.

*Deduce the nature, group and possible identity of Z.*

- Jump after the third ionisation energy → three outer electrons → **Group 13**.
- Z₂O₃ dissolves in acid and in NaOH → **amphoteric** → Z lies at the metal/non-metal boundary.
- ZCl₃ gives an acidic solution → small, highly charged Z³⁺ ion polarising water, as for Al³⁺.
- Z is a metal with amphoteric oxide in Group 13: **aluminium** fits all the data.

## Common mistakes

- **Predicting without a reason.** Always name the analogy or trend.
- **Using the period number for the number of outer electrons.** It is the group that gives the outer electrons; the period gives the number of occupied shells.
- **Ignoring "down the group" effects.** An element below silicon is not identical to silicon.
- **Reading the ionisation energy jump wrongly.** If the jump is between the nth and (n+1)th values, there are n outer electrons.
- **Giving one identity when the data cannot separate two.** Say "Group 2, below magnesium, for example calcium".
- **Contradicting one piece of evidence.** Check your answer against every statement in the question.

## Exam technique: command words in 9.3

- **Predict**: give the property and justify it from the group or period trend.
- **Deduce**: give a conclusion that follows from the data, showing each inference.
- **Suggest**: an answer consistent with the information, where more than one may be acceptable.
- **Identify**: name the element (or compound) with a brief reason.
