## Section A -- short answers and scenarios

**1.** Identify the **four** types of sentence available for adult offenders. **[4]**

**2.** Distinguish an absolute discharge from a conditional discharge. **[2]**

**3.** State **three** requirements that may be included in a community order. **[3]**

**4.** Explain the effect of a guilty plea on sentence. **[3]**

**5.** Tom, 15, pleads guilty to criminal damage, his first offence. Identify the sentence he is most likely to receive and describe how it works. **[4]**

**6.** Una, 45, is convicted after trial of burglary of a dwelling at night while the occupants were asleep. She has several previous convictions for burglary. Identify **two** aggravating factors and explain their effect on sentence. **[4]**

**7.** Vik, 23, steals £60 from a shop till where he works part-time, pleading guilty at the first opportunity. He is a heroin user with no previous convictions. Advise on a suitable sentence and explain which aims of sentencing it would serve. **[6]**

## Section B -- short answer and essay

**8.** State the five purposes of sentencing adult offenders in the Sentencing Code. **[5]**

**9.** Explain the role of parents in the sentencing of young offenders. **[5]**

**10.** Describe the custodial sentences available for young offenders. **[6]**

**11.** Explain how deterrence operates as an aim of sentencing, and identify **one** weakness of it. **[4]**

**12.** "The youth justice system rightly places rehabilitation above punishment." Evaluate this statement. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Custodial sentences [1]; community sentences [1]; fines [1]; discharges [1].

**2.** An absolute discharge imposes no penalty at all, because the court considers the offender barely blameworthy [1]. A conditional discharge imposes no penalty unless the offender commits a further offence within a set period of up to three years, when they can be sentenced again for the original offence [1].

**3.** Any three, 1 mark each: unpaid work [1]; curfew [1]; exclusion [1]; rehabilitation activity; drug rehabilitation; alcohol treatment; mental health treatment; prohibited activity.

**4.** A guilty plea reduces the sentence because it saves court time and spares witnesses [1]. A plea at the first opportunity earns a reduction of up to one-third [1]; a later plea earns less, reducing to about one-tenth on the first day of trial [1].

**5.** Tom is likely to receive a **referral order**, which is available for most first-time offenders who plead guilty [1]. He is referred to a youth offender panel [1], which agrees a contract with him lasting between three and twelve months [1]. The contract may include reparation to the victim and activities to address his behaviour; the conviction is spent on completion [1].

**6.** Previous convictions are a statutory aggravating factor, especially where they are relevant and recent [1]; repeat burglaries also risk a minimum sentence for a third domestic burglary [1]. Burgling an occupied home at night increases the harm, including psychological harm to the victims [1]. Both factors move the sentence up within, or above, the guideline category range, making custody very likely; as she was convicted after trial, she receives no plea reduction [1].

**7.** The offence involves a breach of trust, which increases culpability, but the amount is small [1]. Mitigation includes no previous convictions [1], and his first-opportunity plea earns up to a one-third reduction [1]. A community order with a drug rehabilitation requirement is suitable, perhaps with unpaid work or a curfew as the punitive element [1]. The drug requirement serves **reform and rehabilitation**, tackling the cause of offending [1], while unpaid work serves **punishment** and **reparation** to the community [1].

**8.** Punishment of offenders [1]; reduction of crime, including by deterrence [1]; reform and rehabilitation of offenders [1]; protection of the public [1]; reparation by offenders to persons affected by their offences [1].

**9.** Parents of offenders under 16 must normally attend court [1]. For an offender under 16, the court must normally order the parent to pay any fine, costs or compensation, and it may do so for 16–17 year olds [1]. A **parenting order** under the Crime and Disorder Act 1998 can require the parent to attend counselling or guidance sessions [1] and to comply with other requirements, such as ensuring the child attends school [1]. A parent may also be **bound over** to take proper care of and exercise proper control over the child [1].

**10.** A **detention and training order** is available for offenders aged 12–17 [1] and lasts between four months and two years, with half normally in custody and half under supervision [1]; for under-15s it requires the offender to be persistent [1]. **Long-term detention** is available for grave crimes that would carry at least fourteen years for an adult [1], where a detention and training order would be too short [1]. **Detention at His Majesty's pleasure** is mandatory for murder committed under 18, with a minimum term [1].

**11.** **Individual deterrence** aims to discourage the offender from reoffending by the experience or threat of punishment, as with a suspended sentence [1]. **General deterrence** aims to discourage others by exemplary sentences [1], as in *R v Blackshaw* (2011), where four-year terms were upheld for inciting riots on Facebook [1]. Weakness: it assumes offenders weigh consequences rationally, which impulsive offenders or those with addictions often do not [1].

**12.** **For the statement**: the principal aim of the youth justice system is to prevent offending (s37 Crime and Disorder Act 1998), and courts must have regard to the child's welfare (Children and Young Persons Act 1933) [1]. Children are still developing and have a greater capacity for change, so rehabilitation is more likely to succeed [1]. Referral orders and youth rehabilitation orders focus on addressing behaviour and reparation, and spent convictions avoid long-term harm to education and employment [1]. Custody as a last resort reflects concern that it can entrench criminal behaviour and links with older offenders [1]. **Against**: victims and the public may feel serious offending by young people is not adequately punished [1]; detention and training orders and long-term detention show punishment and public protection still matter for serious crimes [1]; a sharp change of approach at 18 may not match an individual's actual maturity [1]. **Balance**: the system does not abandon punishment; it weights aims differently, as with unpaid work for 16–17 year olds [1], and parental responsibility orders tackle causes of offending in the home [1]. **Conclusion**: the priority on rehabilitation is justified, provided custody remains available for the most serious cases [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Recommending a sentence without referring to aggravating and mitigating factors.
- Forgetting the guilty plea reduction, or awarding it after conviction at trial.
- Writing about parenting orders without saying what they require.
- Evaluating youth sentencing without the statutory aims.
