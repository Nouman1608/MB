The focused study **Political and economic transitions** is one of the five focused studies in IB DP History (first assessment 2028). It has two examples, and both must be studied: **the Meiji Restoration (1853 to 1894)** and **the Russian Federation (1985 to 1999)**. Three inquiry questions run through both. Paper 1 assesses the study through sources, so this guide gives the context and shows how to use it. It is the same for SL and HL.

## How paper 1 uses this study

Paper 1 (1 hour 15 minutes, 24 marks) gives three sources (A, B and C) and one inquiry question. You explain how the content of sources A and B can be used (6 marks), analyse how the context of source C shapes its use (6 marks) and examine how the perspectives in all the sources can be used (12 marks). The inquiry questions and grounding content provide the context you need.

## Inquiry question 1: What caused the transition to take place?

### The Meiji Restoration

The grounding content names the decline of the Tokugawa Shogunate, stagnation and demands for rapid modernisation, and the influence of the United States and European countries.

From 1603 the Tokugawa shoguns ruled Japan in the name of the emperor, in a society ranked into samurai, peasants, artisans and merchants, and with limited foreign contact. By the nineteenth century the system was under strain. The samurai class was costly and many were poor, rice taxes were difficult to raise, peasant uprisings were frequent, and merchants gained wealth without political power. In 1853 an American naval squadron under Commodore Matthew Perry arrived in Edo Bay and demanded that Japan open to trade. In 1854 the Convention of Kanagawa opened two ports, and in 1858 treaties signed under pressure gave Western powers trading rights and extraterritorial privileges ("unequal treaties"). The shogunate looked weak, and this fuelled demands to "honour the emperor and expel the barbarians". Activists in domains such as Satsuma and Chōshū turned against the shogun. After a brief civil war (the Boshin War, 1868 to 1869), the shogunate ended and imperial rule was "restored" in the name of the young Emperor Mutsuhito, known as Meiji.

### The Russian Federation

The grounding content names the role of the Communist Party, the impact of stagnation and failed economic reform, and challenges to communism in Eastern Europe.

By the 1980s the Soviet Union's centrally planned economy had slowed. Growth was low, consumer goods were scarce, and the system was costly to sustain, including a heavy military burden and the war in Afghanistan. The Communist Party of the Soviet Union held a monopoly of power. When Mikhail Gorbachev became General Secretary in March 1985, he launched reforms: *perestroika* (restructuring), *glasnost* (openness) and *uskorenie* (acceleration). These did not fix the economy and loosened the party's control over politics. In 1989 communist regimes in Eastern Europe fell, and the Soviet government did not intervene. This encouraged nationalist movements in the Soviet republics. Whether the Soviet collapse was caused mostly by economic failure, by Gorbachev's decisions, by nationalism or by wider pressures remains debated.

## Inquiry question 2: How was the transition achieved?

### The Meiji Restoration

The grounding content names the roles of the Meiji oligarchy and Emperor Mutsuhito; land reform, industrialisation and trade; and the Constitution of 1889.

A small group of young leaders from the former samurai class, often called the Meiji oligarchs (for example Ōkubo Toshimichi, Kido Takayoshi, Itō Hirobumi and Yamagata Aritomo), ran the government in the emperor's name. In 1868 the Charter Oath promised that deliberative assemblies would be set up and that knowledge would be sought from around the world. In 1871 the domains were abolished and replaced by prefectures, which centralised power. The old class system was dismantled, and samurai privileges were reduced. A new army based on conscription was created in 1873. The land tax reform of 1873 gave land titles and replaced rice payments with a cash tax, providing a steady source of government revenue.

The government built railways, telegraph lines and model factories (for example, a state silk-reeling mill at Tomioka in 1872) and sent missions abroad, such as the Iwakura Mission of 1871 to 1873, to study Western institutions. Many factories were later sold to private business groups. Trade expanded, and revision of the unequal treaties became a major national goal. The Constitution of 1889 created a Diet (parliament) with an elected lower house, but gave the emperor wide powers, and the vote was limited to a small group of wealthy men. Historians debate how much the constitution was a step towards democracy and how much it was a way to strengthen state power.

### The Russian Federation

The grounding content names the roles of Mikhail Gorbachev and Boris Yeltsin, the Constitution of 1993, and privatisation, market liberalisation and tax reforms.

Gorbachev's reforms led to multi-candidate elections in 1989 and 1990 and to the end of the party's constitutional monopoly. Boris Yeltsin, a former party official turned reformer, was elected president of the Russian republic in June 1991. After the failed hardliners' coup in August 1991, the Soviet Union's authority collapsed. In December 1991 the leaders of Russia, Ukraine and Belarus declared the USSR dissolved, and Gorbachev resigned on 25 December.

In January 1992 Yeltsin's government freed most prices ("shock therapy"), and prices rose sharply. Mass privatisation followed, with vouchers distributed to citizens, and later "loans for shares" deals gave a small group of businessmen control of major assets. Tax reforms and a market-based commercial law were introduced. The Constitution of 1993, approved by a referendum in December, created a strong presidency. Evaluating the transition requires weighing the speed of liberalisation against its social costs.

## Inquiry question 3: What challenges did society face after the transition?

### The Meiji Restoration

The grounding content names peasant unrest, the Satsuma Rebellion, and foreign relations and imperialism.

The land tax and conscription caused resentment, and peasant uprisings occurred in the 1870s. Many samurai lost their stipends and their status. In 1877 Saigō Takamori, a former leading figure of the Restoration, led the Satsuma Rebellion against the government. The new conscript army defeated it, which showed that the state's military monopoly was established. In foreign affairs, the government sought treaty revision and also pursued influence in East Asia. In 1876 Japan forced Korea to sign a treaty opening ports, and it fought a war with China in 1894 to 1895, which began the period of Japanese imperial expansion.

### The Russian Federation

The grounding content names the Soviet coup attempt (1991) and the constitutional crisis (September to October 1993), labour strikes, protests and organised crime, and the First Chechen War (1994 to 1996).

In August 1991 hardliners in the party and security services tried to remove Gorbachev. The coup collapsed within days, with Yeltsin as a symbol of resistance in Moscow. In September 1993 Yeltsin dissolved the parliament, which opposed his reforms, and the parliament's leaders, including Aleksandr Rutskoy and Ruslan Khasbulatov, resisted from the White House building. In early October the army shelled the building and the leaders were arrested. Economic hardship was severe: prices rose quickly, wages and pensions were often delayed, and strikes and protests, including miners' strikes, occurred. Organised crime and corruption grew as state controls weakened. In December 1994 Yeltsin sent troops into Chechnya, which had declared independence, and the First Chechen War ended in 1996 with a ceasefire and Russian withdrawal.

## Using sources

- For Japan, sources include Perry's expedition records, the Charter Oath, Iwakura Mission reports, woodblock prints and the Constitution. A Japanese official, a peasant protest petition and a Western diplomat give different perspectives.
- For Russia, sources include speeches by Gorbachev and Yeltsin, newspaper reports, polling data, cartoons and later academic assessments.
- In both cases ask who is writing, when, and for whom. A government statement tends to justify policy, while a protest petition shows costs.

## Linking to the concepts

- **Cause and consequence:** actors (the oligarchs, Gorbachev, Yeltsin) and conditions (foreign pressure, economic stagnation).
- **Continuity and change:** rapid political change, with continuity in elites and some institutions.
- **Perspectives:** winners and losers of reform.
- **Significance:** why these two transitions matter for the history of modernisation and of the end of the Cold War.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the Restoration as a restoration of real emperor power with no oligarchs.
- Saying that Yeltsin led the USSR (he led Russia; Gorbachev led the USSR).
- Confusing the 1991 coup attempt with the 1993 crisis.
- Describing events instead of using sources.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why compare Japan and Russia?** Both undertook state-led transformations under pressure, and the study tests how sources show their causes and costs.

**Do I need figures?** Approximate figures help, but the paper tests how you use sources.
