All businesses and figures are invented.

## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Define the term *barrier to communication*. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between vertical and horizontal communication. **[2]**

**3.** State whether each of these is formal or informal communication: (i) minutes of a board meeting, (ii) a chat between two cashiers during a break, (iii) an annual appraisal interview. **[3]**

**4.** Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of using a messaging app for communication with delivery drivers. **[4]**

**5.** Rushbrook Builders (invented) employs 180 staff. 45 work on remote sites without reliable internet. Calculate the percentage of staff who may not receive messages sent only by email. **[1]**

**6.** Explain why two-way communication may be better than one-way communication when introducing new equipment. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

Questions 7 to 10 use the case below.

Marrowgate Software is an invented company with 90 developers: 50 in its home country and 40 in a second country eight hours ahead. Managers announce project changes in a long monthly email written in technical language. Developers in the second country often start work on old instructions. Rumours about job cuts at the second site spread through a staff chat group after a manager mentioned "consolidation" in a meeting. Staff turnover at the second site is rising.

**7.** Identify two barriers to communication at Marrowgate. **[2]**

**8.** Explain how the time difference affects communication between the two sites. **[2]**

**9.** Analyse the effects of the rumour spread through the staff chat group on Marrowgate. **[6]**

**10.** Recommend how Marrowgate should communicate project changes to its developers in future. **[8]**

**11.** Saltmarsh Bank (invented) must tell 30 branch staff that their branch will close in six months and that staff will be offered roles in other branches or redundancy. Evaluate two methods the bank could use to communicate this news. **[8]**

**12.** Explain two reasons why an organisation with many levels of hierarchy may suffer from poor upward communication. **[4]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** Anything that stops a message being sent, received or understood as intended [1], for example jargon, noise or information overload [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Vertical communication moves up or down the hierarchy, such as between a manager and a subordinate [1]; horizontal communication moves between people at the same level, such as between two department heads [1]. **[2]**

**3.** (i) Formal [1]. (ii) Informal [1]. (iii) Formal [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Advantage: messages reach drivers instantly while they are on the road [1], so route changes or customer updates can be acted on quickly [1]. Disadvantage: drivers may read messages while driving or miss them [1], which raises safety risks or leads to missed instructions [1]. **[4]**

**5.** 45 ÷ 180 × 100 = **25%** [1] **[1]**

**6.** Two-way communication lets staff ask questions and lets managers check understanding [1], which reduces mistakes and accidents when staff are learning to use unfamiliar equipment [1]. **[2]**

### Section B

**7.** Any two: technical language or jargon [1]; time zone difference [1]; information overload from a long monthly email [1]; one-way communication with no feedback [1]. Maximum two. **[2]**

**8.** With the second site eight hours ahead, a change made during the home site's working day usually arrives after the second site has finished work [1], so the change is read late and developers work on old instructions, causing wasted work [1]. **[2]**

**9.** The rumour is informal communication spreading faster than any formal message [1]. Staff at the second site fear job cuts, which lowers morale and motivation [1]. Rising staff turnover suggests skilled developers are leaving, raising recruitment and training costs [1]. Remaining staff may distrust future formal messages, making later changes harder to introduce [1]. The cause was an unclear word ("consolidation") with no explanation, so a formal clarification was missing [1]. Overall, a communication failure has become a cost and a retention problem [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Link each effect to evidence in the case, such as the rising turnover, rather than listing general effects of rumours.

**10.** Criteria from the case: messages must reach both sites quickly, be understood by all developers and allow questions [1]. Option 1: short written change notes issued when each change happens, in plain language, rather than a long monthly email [1]; this reduces overload and delay but is still one-way [1]. Option 2: a short video meeting in the overlapping hour of the two working days [1]; it allows questions and confirmation, but the eight-hour gap means some staff must start early or stay late [1]. Informal channels such as team chat can be used to check understanding but not to announce changes [1]. Recommendation: plain-language change notes plus a brief live meeting for major changes, with a rule that the second site confirms receipt before work starts [1]; this works only if managers keep notes short and the overlap hour is protected [1]. **[8]**

**11.** Method 1, face-to-face meeting with all 30 staff: shows respect and allows questions on sensitive news [1]; staff hear the same message at the same time, reducing rumours [1]; but strong emotions may make detail hard to absorb [1]. Method 2, a formal letter to each employee: provides a record of the options and dates [1], useful for legal and planning purposes [1]; but on its own it is impersonal and gives no chance to ask questions [1]. Judgement: the meeting should come first, followed by a letter on the same day setting out the options [1], because the news is sensitive and also needs a record; the order matters, since staff learning of closure by letter first may lose trust [1]. **[8]**

**12.** Reason 1: messages pass through many managers before reaching the top [1], so they are delayed, filtered or softened at each level [1]. Reason 2: junior staff are far from senior managers in status [1], so they may fear speaking up about problems, and managers do not hear them [1]. **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

* Naming barriers without explaining their effect on the business in the case.
* Recommending a method without criteria taken from the stem.
* Ignoring the need for a record when the news is legal or sensitive.
* Treating informal communication only as harmful.
* Giving two methods but no comparison or order of use.
