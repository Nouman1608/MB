Knowledge tells you what writers can do. Skills turn that knowledge into text. The Paper 2 Writing section of the Cambridge International AS & A Level English Language (9093) syllabus lists seven skills and techniques for writers. This guide takes them one by one, shows where they are tested and models the hardest ones: structuring a longer piece, organising it for effect and reflecting on your own writing. For the paper as a whole, read the [Paper 2 Writing study guide](/resources/a-level-english-language-paper-2-writing/) first. For the knowledge that supports these skills, see the [Paper 2 knowledge and understanding guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-english-language-9093-paper-2-knowledge-and-understanding/).

## Where the skills are tested

| Part of Paper 2 | Task | Marks | Skills most used |
|---|---|---|---|
| Question 1(a) | a short text of no more than 400 words for a form, purpose and audience | 15 | writing to a brief; paragraphs; language features; accuracy |
| Question 1(b) | a reflective commentary on how the text fulfils the brief | 10 | reflecting and evaluating |
| Section B | one extended piece of 600 to 900 words: imaginative/descriptive, discursive/argumentative, or review/critical | 25 | structure; organising for effect; paragraphs; language features; accuracy |

Paper 2 lasts 2 hours and is worth 50 marks. AO2 is about 80% of the paper and AO3 about 20%. Dictionaries may not be used.

## The seven skills

The syllabus says candidates should be prepared to demonstrate the following.

1. writing for a specified audience and purpose, to fulfil the brief provided;
2. producing an appropriate structure for longer pieces of writing;
3. organising writing to achieve specific effects;
4. structuring paragraphs;
5. using a range of appropriate linguistic elements and literary features;
6. expressing ideas accurately and clearly at both sentence and word level;
7. reflecting upon and evaluating the qualities of their own writing, including aspects relating to its purpose, form and audience.

### Skill 1: fulfil the brief

The brief gives you a **form**, a **purpose** and an **audience**. Underline all three before you plan. Everything you write must serve them.

| Brief says | Your decisions |
|---|---|
| "an article for a school magazine to persuade students" | a register that suits students, direct address, persuasive devices, subheading or title |
| "a review of a restaurant for a local newspaper" | judgement, evaluative vocabulary, balanced detail, verdict |
| "a short story opening for young readers" | accessible vocabulary, vivid detail, a hook |

Check the brief again halfway through writing and at the end.

### Skill 2: produce an appropriate structure for longer pieces

The syllabus gives three examples.

- **Imaginative writing:** Freytag's Pyramid, which moves from exposition through rising action to a climax, then falling action and resolution.
- **Discursive writing:** dialectical structure, which puts a view, a counter-view and a resolution.
- **Review writing:** introductory, summary and evaluative sections.

| Category | Typical structure | Plan |
|---|---|---|
| Imaginative/descriptive | exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, resolution; or a descriptive journey through a place | decide where the turning point falls |
| Discursive/argumentative | thesis, counter-argument, response, synthesis | order the points from strongest to most subtle |
| Review/critical | introduce the subject, summarise it, evaluate strengths and weaknesses, give a verdict | decide the verdict first, then arrange the evidence |

A plan of 600 to 900 words needs about five to seven paragraphs. Decide what each paragraph does before you write.

### Skill 3: organise for specific effects

The syllabus examples are: withholding key information in imaginative writing, juxtaposing counterarguments in discursive writing, and evidentiary logic in critical writing.

- **Withholding information.** Delay a key fact to build tension or surprise. Example: an opening that describes a family carefully avoiding an envelope on the mantelpiece, with its contents revealed only at the end.
- **Juxtaposing counterarguments.** Place opposing arguments side by side so that the reader feels the contrast, then resolve it.
- **Evidentiary logic.** In a review or critical piece, build the verdict step by step: claim, evidence, evaluation.

Ask yourself: what do I want the reader to feel or think at this point, and what order will produce it?

### Skill 4: structure paragraphs

The syllabus examples are topic sentences, connectives, internal coherence and discourse markers.

A reliable pattern is: **topic sentence** (the point), **development** (evidence, example or detail), **comment** (what it shows), **link** (to the next paragraph or the brief).

| Tool | Examples |
|---|---|
| Topic sentences | "The strongest argument for a later start concerns sleep." |
| Connectives | however, therefore, in addition, by contrast, meanwhile |
| Discourse markers | to begin with, as a result, in other words, on balance |
| Coherence | each sentence builds on the one before; pronouns refer clearly |

In imaginative writing, paragraphs can be shorter and more varied, but each should still have a purpose.

### Skill 5: use linguistic elements and literary features

The syllabus examples link features to categories: **imagery in descriptive writing**, **rhetorical devices in argumentative writing** and **evaluative lexis in critical writing**.

| Category | Features to use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Descriptive | imagery, sensory detail, precise verbs, varied sentence length | "rain stitched the window to the night" |
| Argumentative | rhetorical question, rule of three, direct address, modality | "If not now, when? If not us, who?" |
| Critical | evaluative adjectives, comparatives, hedging | "a competent but uninspired debut" |

Use features **for a reason**. A device added to decorate the text, with no purpose, weakens it.

### Skill 6: express ideas accurately and clearly

This skill covers **sentence level** (grammar, punctuation, sentence structure) and **word level** (spelling, vocabulary choice). Common problems:

| Problem | Example | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Comma splice | "The park is large, it has a lake." | "The park is large, and it has a lake." or use a full stop |
| Subject-verb agreement | "Each of the pupils have a locker." | "Each of the pupils has a locker." |
| Tense shift | "She opened the door and sees a light." | "She opened the door and saw a light." |
| Apostrophes | "the childrens' games" | "the children's games" |
| Vague words | "a lot of good things" | "dozens of well-run workshops" |
| Wrong register | "The council's plan is rubbish." (in a formal letter) | "The council's plan is inadequate." |

Leave time to proofread. Read the text aloud in your head; mistakes in rhythm often reveal mistakes in grammar.

### Skill 7: reflect upon and evaluate your own writing

Question 1(b) asks you to explain how your linguistic choices contributed to fulfilling the brief, focusing on choices of **form, structure and language**, and analysing how they relate to **audience** and shape **meaning**.

A strong commentary:

1. names a **choice**;
2. gives a short **example** from your own text;
3. explains the **effect** on the reader;
4. links it to **audience, purpose or form**.

*Sentence frames:* "I chose ... because ...", "By using ... I aimed to ...", "The shift from ... to ... shows ...", "This suits the audience because ...".

## Worked example: a dialectical paragraph

**Brief (Section B, discursive).** *Write the argument section of an article for a school newsletter on the question: should the school day start later?*

> Supporters of a later start have a simple argument: teenagers are often at their most alert later in the morning, so a nine o'clock start would improve attention in lessons. If this is true, the gain could be large, because the first period is currently the one in which many pupils struggle most. Yet the argument is not as simple as it sounds. A later start means a later finish, which would collide with sports practice, part-time jobs and bus timetables. On balance, the benefit is real but depends on whether the practical costs can be managed. A trial, perhaps for one term, would be the sensible way to find out.

How the paragraph works:

| Feature | Where |
|---|---|
| Topic sentence | "Supporters of a later start have a simple argument" |
| Counterargument juxtaposed | "Yet the argument is not as simple as it sounds" |
| Connectives and discourse markers | "so", "because", "Yet", "On balance" |
| Modality | "could", "often", "perhaps" |
| Resolution | the proposal of a trial |

## Worked example: withholding information

> The envelope had been on the mantelpiece for three days. Nobody touched it. Nobody mentioned it. Mum dusted around it as though it were a sleeping animal, and Dad found reasons to be in the garden.

What is *in* the envelope is withheld. The detail of how the family behaves does the work: personification ("sleeping animal"), short sentences and repetition build suspense. The reveal is delayed until the end of the piece, so the structure itself creates the effect.

## Common mistakes

- **Ignoring the brief** by writing a good text in the wrong form or for the wrong audience.
- **A shapeless extended piece** with no plan, so the ending feels accidental.
- **Narrating a plot** in imaginative writing instead of controlling pace and effect.
- **A list of points** in discursive writing, with no counterargument or resolution.
- **Over-written description**, with an image in every line and no focus.
- **Careless accuracy**: comma splices, tense shifts and spelling errors in simple words.
- **A commentary that lists features** without effects or links to the audience.
- **No time left for Question 1(b)**, which is worth 10 marks.

## Exam technique

- Spend about five minutes planning each task, noting form, purpose, audience and structure.
- Keep Question 1(a) within 400 words and Section B within 600 to 900 words.
- Reserve about 15 minutes for the commentary and some minutes for proofreading.
- In Section B, choose the category that suits your strengths and for which you already know a structure.
- Practise by writing short sections to a time limit and then reflecting on them.
