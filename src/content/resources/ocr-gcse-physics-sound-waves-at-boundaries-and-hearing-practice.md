These questions cover statements P5.1f to P5.1i of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Gateway Science Physics A (J249) specification, Version 5.0 (July 2026): sound at boundaries, interfaces, conversions between sound and vibrations, and the limits of hearing. Every question is **Higher Tier only**. Use v = f λ throughout, and take the speed of sound in air as 340 m/s unless the question gives another value. Read the [study guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-physics-sound-waves-at-boundaries-and-hearing/) and [revision notes](/resources/ocr-gcse-physics-sound-waves-at-boundaries-and-hearing-revision-notes/) first if you need to. Course pages: the [OCR GCSE Physics hub](/boards/ocr/gcse/physics/) and the [checklist](/checklists/ocr/gcse/physics/).

## Questions

**1.** (Higher Tier only) A sound wave travels from air into steel.

**(a)** State which quantity of the wave does not change. **[1]**
**(b)** State what happens to the wavelength, and give the reason. **[2]**

**2.** (Higher Tier only) A note of frequency 275 Hz travels in air. It then passes into a liquid where the speed of sound is 1400 m/s. Calculate the wavelength of the note in the air and in the liquid. Give each answer to 2 significant figures. **[4]**

**3.** (Higher Tier only) A note of frequency 1750 Hz has a wavelength of 3.2 m as it travels along a metal bar. Calculate the speed of sound in the metal, then calculate the wavelength of the same note when it leaves the bar and travels in air. **[4]**

**4.** (Higher Tier only) A pulse of ultrasound meets the boundary between two different tissues. Describe what can happen to the pulse at the boundary. **[3]**

**5.** (Higher Tier only) Before an ultrasound scan, a gel is spread on the skin. Explain why. **[3]**

**6.** (Higher Tier only) A survey vessel fires a downward pulse of sound into the sea, where sound travels at 1500 m/s.

**(a)** The echo from the seabed is detected 0.52 s after firing. Find the depth of the seabed. **[2]**
**(b)** An earlier echo, from a shoal of fish, is detected 0.30 s after firing. Find the depth of the shoal. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate how far the shoal is above the seabed. **[1]**

**7.** (Higher Tier only) Describe the path of a sound wave through the ear, from the air outside to the signal in the nerve. Say what form the disturbance takes at each stage. **[4]**

**8.** (Higher Tier only) A student strikes a tuning fork and holds the base against a wooden table.

**(a)** Give two examples of devices or objects, other than the table, where a sound wave and a vibration of a solid are converted into one another. For each, say which way the conversion goes. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the sound is louder when the fork touches the table. **[2]**

**9.** (Higher Tier only) Explain why the ear can only respond to sound over a limited range of frequencies. **[4]**

**10.** (Higher Tier only) A hammer strikes a steel rail. A listener stands 850 m away with an ear pressed to the rail and hears the sound twice: once through the steel and once through the air. The speed of sound is 5100 m/s in steel and 340 m/s in air.

**(a)** Calculate the time for the sound to reach the listener through the air. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the time for the sound to reach the listener through the steel. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the time interval between the two sounds. **[1]**
**(d)** The sound in the steel has frequency 1700 Hz. Calculate its wavelength in the steel. **[1]**

**11.** (Higher Tier only) A hearing survey records the highest frequency each person can hear.

| Person | Age (years) | Highest frequency heard (kHz) |
|---|---|---|
| A | 25 | 19.5 |
| B | 45 | 16.0 |
| C | 65 | 11.0 |

**(a)** Calculate the wavelength in air of the highest frequency heard by person C. Give your answer in metres to 2 significant figures. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe the pattern in the data. **[1]**
**(c)** An alarm emits a steady tone of 15 kHz. State which people could hear it and explain your answer. **[2]**
**(d)** Suggest two reasons why the highest audible frequency falls with age. **[2]**
**(e)** A bat produces ultrasound of frequency 85 kHz. Calculate its wavelength in air in millimetres, and explain why none of the three people can hear it. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** The frequency [1].
**(b)** The wavelength increases [1], because the speed is greater in steel and the frequency is fixed, so λ = v / f is larger [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Say that the frequency of the wave is unchanged, not the speed or wavelength. Give a reason with the wavelength, not just the direction of change.

**2.** Air: λ = v / f = 340 / 275 [1] = 1.236 m, so **1.2 m** [1].
Liquid: the frequency is still 275 Hz [1], so λ = 1400 / 275 = 5.09 m, so **5.1 m** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the substitution before the answer. If the frequency were wrongly changed in the liquid, the working would show where the error began, which is why a written line of working matters.

**3.** Speed: v = f λ = 1750 × 3.2 [1] = **5600 m/s** [1].
In air the frequency is still 1750 Hz [1], so λ = 340 / 1750 = **0.19 m** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* This is the same equation rearranged in two directions. State that the frequency is unchanged, because it shows the idea behind the method as well as the arithmetic.

**4.** Some of the pulse is reflected back from the boundary [1]. Some is transmitted into the second tissue, where its speed and wavelength change [1]. Some is absorbed, transferring energy to the thermal store of the tissue [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Name all three effects and say what happens in each. Three separate points are needed, so "it bounces and goes through" alone leaves out one idea.

**5.** Without gel there is a layer of air between the probe and the skin [1]. Air and skin are very different materials, so almost all of the ultrasound would be reflected at this boundary [1]. The gel fills the gap, so more ultrasound is transmitted into the body [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the gel to the air gap and to reflection at the interface. "To make it work better" or "to keep the probe clean" does not address the physics.

**6. (a)** Distance there and back = 1500 × 0.52 [1] = 780 m, so depth = 780 / 2 = **390 m** [1].
**(b)** 1500 × 0.30 = 450 m there and back [1], so depth = **225 m** [1].
**(c)** 390 − 225 = **165 m** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Forgetting to halve is the most common slip, and it gives 780 m and 450 m. Writing "there and back" beside the first calculation shows you know why.

**7.** Sound waves in air are collected by the outer ear and travel along the ear canal to the eardrum [1]. The wave makes the eardrum vibrate, so the disturbance is now a vibration of a solid membrane [1]. The ossicles (three small bones) pass the vibration across the middle ear to the cochlea [1]. In the fluid-filled cochlea the vibration becomes a wave in liquid, and hair cells convert it into electrical signals that the auditory nerve carries to the brain [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A list of parts earns little unless each is paired with the change of form. Say what is vibrating at each stage.

**8. (a)** Loudspeaker cone: vibrating solid produces a sound wave in the air [1]. Microphone diaphragm (or eardrum): sound wave in the air makes a solid vibrate [1]. Any two correct examples with the correct direction are acceptable.
**(b)** The fork vibrates the table, which has a large surface [1]. The large surface pushes on much more air, so a bigger sound wave is produced [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* An example without a direction of conversion is incomplete, because the specification asks for the process, not only the object.

**9.** The parts of the ear that vibrate (the eardrum, ossicles and the structures in the cochlea) have mass and stiffness [1]. At very high frequencies the parts cannot change direction quickly enough to keep in step with the sound [1]. At very low frequencies the response is weak and the pressure changes are too slow to produce a useful vibration [1]. Outside the range the vibration is too small to stimulate the hair cells, so no signal reaches the brain [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Mention a physical property of the parts, such as mass, stiffness or inertia. Answers that only repeat "the ear cannot hear it" do not explain anything.

**10. (a)** t = distance / speed = 850 / 340 [1] = **2.5 s** [1].
**(b)** t = 850 / 5100 [1] = **0.17 s** (0.167 s) [1].
**(c)** 2.5 − 0.167 = **2.3 s** [1].
**(d)** λ = 5100 / 1700 = **3.0 m** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Carry the unrounded steel time (0.1667 s) into the subtraction. Rounding it to 0.2 s first would give 2.3 s here by luck, but the same habit gives wrong answers for other numbers.

**11. (a)** 11.0 kHz = 11 000 Hz [1]. λ = 340 / 11 000 = **0.031 m** [1].
**(b)** The older the person, the lower the highest frequency they can hear [1].
**(c)** Persons A and B [1], because their upper limits (19.5 kHz and 16.0 kHz) are above 15 kHz, but person C's limit of 11.0 kHz is below it [1].
**(d)** Any two from: hair cells in the cochlea are damaged and are not replaced [1]; long-term exposure to loud noise wears the ear [1].
**(e)** 85 kHz = 85 000 Hz, λ = 340 / 85 000 = 0.004 m = **4.0 mm** [1]. It is above the upper limit of all three people (the highest is 19.5 kHz), so it is ultrasound to them [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* In part (c) you need both the comparison and the conclusion for each person. Quoting only the values from the table without comparing them to 15 kHz is a statement of the data, not an explanation.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Changing the frequency when the sound crosses a boundary. It is the speed and wavelength that change.
- Leaving frequencies in kHz or MHz when substituting into v = f λ.
- Giving the round-trip distance as the depth in echo questions.
- Rounding too early in multi-step calculations, then subtracting two rounded values.
- Saying absorbed energy "disappears" instead of passing to the thermal store of the material.
- Writing "the sound goes through" for transmission without saying that speed and wavelength change.
- Listing the parts of the ear in order without describing the form of the disturbance at each stage.
- Giving no physical reason (mass, stiffness, inertia) for the limited hearing range.
