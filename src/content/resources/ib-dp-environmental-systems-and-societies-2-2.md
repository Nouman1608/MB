Subtopic 2.2 Energy and biomass in ecosystems is part of Topic 2 Ecology in IB Diploma Programme
Environmental Systems and Societies (ESS), first assessment 2026. The IB's public subject brief names
Topic 2 but not its subtopics, so this title follows the course's provisional checklist; confirm it
in your school's copy of the ESS guide.

The [ecosystems, energy and biogeochemical cycles study guide](/resources/ib-dp-ess-ecosystems-energy-biogeochemical-cycles/)
defines GPP, NPP, GSP and NSP, gives the ecological efficiency formula and describes the three kinds
of pyramid. This guide goes deeper: photosynthesis and respiration as the transformations that drive
every ecosystem, the energy budget of a single consumer, efficiencies across several trophic levels,
unit conversions, what trophic position means for feeding people, and how to calculate and explain
biomagnification.

## What 2.2 asks you to do

- Explain photosynthesis and respiration as energy transformations.
- Apply the first and second laws of thermodynamics to ecosystems.
- Construct and interpret food chains, food webs and pyramids.
- Calculate productivity values and efficiencies, with correct units.
- Explain the consequences of energy loss for food-chain length and human food production.
- Explain bioaccumulation and biomagnification.

## The two key transformations

**Photosynthesis** converts light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose:

`6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂` (using light energy, in chlorophyll)

Inputs: carbon dioxide, water, light. Outputs: glucose (stored energy and matter), oxygen. It is a
transformation of energy (light to chemical) and of matter (inorganic to organic).

**Respiration** releases the chemical energy in glucose for use by the organism:

`C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O` (energy released, much of it as heat)

Every organism respires all the time, including plants. Respiration is the main reason energy is
"lost" from each trophic level.

### The laws of thermodynamics in ecosystems

- **First law:** energy is neither created nor destroyed, only transformed. So every energy budget
  must balance: what enters a trophic level equals what is stored plus what leaves.
- **Second law:** every transformation increases entropy, so some energy is degraded to heat that
  cannot be used for work. This is why energy flows one way and must be replaced continuously by
  sunlight.

## Food chains, food webs and trophic levels

A **food chain** shows one pathway of feeding; a **food web** shows interlinked chains. Omnivores
occupy more than one trophic level; a fox eating berries is a primary consumer, eating rabbits a
secondary consumer. **Decomposers** (bacteria, fungi) and **detritivores** (earthworms, woodlice)
take energy from dead matter at every level, so their arrows come from all levels.

## The energy budget of a consumer

For an individual animal or a whole consumer level:

```
Food ingested = egested (faeces) + assimilated
Assimilated   = respiration + new biomass (growth and reproduction) + excretion
```

Herbivores egest a large share of what they eat because cellulose is hard to digest. Endotherms
(birds, mammals) respire a large share of what they assimilate to keep warm, so they convert less
into new biomass than ectotherms such as insects or fish.

## Efficiencies across several levels

**Worked example 1 (fictional woodland data, kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹).**

| Level | Value |
|---|---|
| Producers' NPP | 12,000 |
| Caterpillars: ingested | 2,400 |
| Caterpillars: egested | 1,200 |
| Caterpillars: respired | 840 |
| Insect-eating birds: new biomass | 18 |

```
Caterpillar GSP (assimilated) = 2400 − 1200 = 1200
Caterpillar NSP = 1200 − 840 = 360

Producers → caterpillars:  360 / 12000 × 100 = 3.0 %
Caterpillars → birds:      18 / 360 × 100   = 5.0 %
Producers → birds overall: 18 / 12000 × 100 = 0.15 %
```

Only 0.15% of producer NPP becomes bird biomass. These values are below the rough 10% guide figure,
which is common in real ecosystems. The birds' low efficiency reflects the heat lost as endotherms.

### Unit conversions

Productivity is per unit area per unit time, so check both before comparing sites. 1 hectare =
10,000 m².

```
850 g m⁻² yr⁻¹ × 10000 m² ha⁻¹ = 8 500 000 g ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹
                              = 8500 kg ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹
                              = 8.5 t ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹
```

## Estimating NPP from biomass change

NPP can be estimated by harvesting plant material from sample plots at the start and end of a period
and comparing dry mass.

**Worked example 2 (fictional data).** Grass in 1 m² plots has a mean dry mass of 180 g in April and
430 g in August (about 4 months, or one-third of a year).

```
Increase in dry biomass = 430 − 180 = 250 g m⁻²
NPP ≈ 250 g m⁻² ÷ (4/12) yr = 750 g m⁻² yr⁻¹
```

This underestimates NPP, because biomass eaten by herbivores or lost as dead leaves and roots during
the period is not counted, and below-ground growth is hard to sample.

## Trophic position and feeding people

Because energy is lost at every step, eating lower in the food chain supports more people per area.

**Worked example 3 (assumed values).** A hectare of grain supplies 30,000 MJ of food energy a year. If
the grain is fed to cattle with a 10% conversion efficiency, the beef supplies 3,000 MJ. Assume a
person needs about 10 MJ per day, so 3,650 MJ a year.

```
People fed by grain directly = 30000 / 3650 = 8.2 people
People fed by beef           = 3000 / 3650  = 0.82 people
```

The same land feeds about ten times as many people on the grain. Real decisions are more complex:
grazing animals can use land unsuitable for crops, and meat and dairy supply protein and nutrients
that matter for health.

## Bioaccumulation and biomagnification

- **Bioaccumulation:** a persistent substance builds up in an individual organism over its lifetime,
  because it is taken in faster than it is broken down or excreted.
- **Biomagnification:** the concentration of the substance increases at each higher trophic level,
  because each consumer eats many organisms from the level below and retains the substance.

Substances that biomagnify are persistent, fat-soluble and not easily excreted: DDT, PCBs and methyl
mercury are standard examples.

**Worked example 4 (fictional concentrations, ppm).**

| Water | Zooplankton | Small fish | Large fish | Fish-eating bird |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.00005 | 0.04 | 0.5 | 2.0 | 25 |

```
Factor, small fish → bird = 25 / 0.5    = 50
Factor, water → bird      = 25 / 0.00005 = 500 000
```

The top predator carries a concentration half a million times that of the water, which is why the
effects of DDT showed first in birds of prey, through thin eggshells and breeding failure.

## Constructing pyramids from data

Draw bars centred on a vertical axis, with widths **to scale**, producers at the bottom and each bar
labelled with its trophic level and value. If values span a huge range, state that the scale is not
linear. A pyramid of productivity always narrows upward; if your data give an inverted productivity
pyramid, recheck the units or the calculations.

## Common mistakes

- Saying energy is "used up". It is transformed, mainly to heat.
- Leaving respiration out of a producer's budget.
- Dividing by the wrong level when calculating efficiency: always divide by the level below.
- Comparing productivity values with different area or time units.
- Using bioaccumulation and biomagnification interchangeably.

## Exam technique

"Calculate" questions need the formula, the substitution and units. "Explain" energy-loss questions
need the three routes: not eaten, not digested (egested), respired as heat. "Evaluate" diet or land
use claims need the efficiency argument and at least one limitation.
