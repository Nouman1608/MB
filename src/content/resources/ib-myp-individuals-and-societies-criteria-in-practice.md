This guide works through what genuinely top-band evidence looks like
for each of IB Middle Years Programme Individuals and Societies's four
assessment criteria, applied to real coursework tasks. The [assessment
revision
notes](/resources/ib-myp-individuals-and-societies-assessment-revision-notes/)
list the four criteria and their scoring structure -- this guide goes
further, since knowing a criterion's name is a different skill from
producing coursework that satisfies its top achievement-level
descriptors.

## Where this fits in the subject

"Individuals and societies" spans multiple disciplines -- history,
geography, economics, and others depending on how a school structures
the course -- and the four criteria are deliberately written broadly
enough to apply across all of them. A genuinely investigative geography
fieldwork task and a genuinely investigative historical
source-evaluation task can both generate strong Criterion B evidence
despite using very different specific methods, provided each
demonstrates the same underlying systematic, source-critical approach
the criterion actually assesses.

## Criterion A: Knowing and understanding

Top-band evidence develops factual and conceptual knowledge about
individuals and societies and applies it accurately to unfamiliar
contexts, using discipline-appropriate terminology precisely rather
than approximately. Asked to explain why a historical trade route
developed where it did, top-band evidence does not just state relevant
facts (climate, terrain, resources) -- it connects those facts using an
appropriate conceptual framework, such as supply and demand or
geographic accessibility, to build a reasoned explanation, applying the
same conceptual knowledge a student would use to analyse an unfamiliar
trade route they had not specifically studied.

## Criterion B: Investigating

Top-band evidence develops a systematic research question and process,
working both independently and collaboratively, and evaluates the
reliability and relevance of a range of sources rather than accepting
the first sources found without scrutiny. A lower-band investigation
into a local environmental issue might use only one or two easily
found sources without comment on their reliability. Top-band Criterion
B evidence poses a clear, specific research question, consults a
genuine range of source types -- a government report, a news article, a
firsthand community account -- and explicitly evaluates each source's
reliability and relevance to the specific research question before
drawing conclusions from them.

## Criterion C: Communicating

Top-band evidence organises, documents and communicates learning
clearly using a format and structure appropriate to the task and
discipline, correctly referencing sources and using subject-specific
terminology accurately throughout. Two students researching the same
historical event illustrate the distinction well: one presents a
chronological list of facts with no analytical structure and no source
referencing; the other structures the response around a clear
argument, integrates evidence from named, properly referenced sources
at the points that support each part of the argument, and uses
period-appropriate historical terminology consistently. Only the
second demonstrates top-band communication, since the criterion
assesses how the learning is organised and documented, not simply
whether the underlying facts are accurate.

## Criterion D: Thinking critically

Top-band evidence uses critical-thinking skills to analyse and
evaluate the investigation's own process and findings, considering
different perspectives, and drawing well-substantiated conclusions
rather than accepting a single source's account or a single
perspective as the whole picture. Asked to evaluate the causes of a
historical event, top-band Criterion D evidence explicitly considers
more than one plausible perspective or interpretation -- how the event
is represented differently by two different historical accounts, for
example -- evaluates the strengths and weaknesses of each, and reaches
a substantiated conclusion about which interpretation the available
evidence best supports, rather than presenting one interpretation as
simply correct without comparison.

## Connecting the four criteria to a single coursework unit

A well-designed unit -- for example, a local-issue investigation
combining history and geography -- naturally generates evidence for
all four criteria in one connected piece of work: the conceptual
knowledge needed to frame the issue (Criterion A), the process of
researching and evaluating sources about it (Criterion B), the
structured, properly referenced write-up of findings (Criterion C),
and a final critical evaluation weighing different perspectives on the
issue (Criterion D). Revising each criterion as an entirely separate
skill, disconnected from how a real investigation actually unfolds,
risks missing how naturally the four build on each other within a
single piece of coursework -- strong Criterion D evaluation, for
instance, is only possible once Criterion B's source evaluation has
already surfaced more than one perspective to weigh.

## How to approach it

Practise applying conceptual frameworks to unfamiliar examples, not
just the specific cases studied in class, since Criterion A rewards
transferable understanding over memorised recall. Build the habit of
explicitly stating why each source used is reliable or limited for the
specific research question being asked, rather than treating source
use and source evaluation as separate steps. Structure written work
around a clear argument with properly referenced evidence rather than
a chronological or descriptive account, and check that terminology is
used precisely and consistently throughout. Deliberately seek out a
second perspective or interpretation before writing a critical
evaluation, since Criterion D specifically rewards weighing more than
one view rather than developing a single interpretation in isolation.

## Common mistakes

Stating facts accurately without connecting them to a conceptual framework, which limits Criterion A
evidence to recall rather than genuine application. Using sources without evaluating their reliability
or relevance to the specific question being investigated, which is the most common reason Criterion B
evidence falls short even when the sources themselves are reasonable choices. Presenting research
findings as an unstructured list of facts rather than an organised, referenced argument, which limits
Criterion C regardless of how accurate the underlying content is. Treating a single source's account or
a single interpretation as settled fact rather than one perspective among several, which is the most
common reason Criterion D evidence fails to reach top band even when the surrounding research is
otherwise strong.
