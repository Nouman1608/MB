**Syllabus:** Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027, **A Level**
subtopic 26.1. Orders are limited to 0, 1 and 2 for each reactant.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition to recall |
|---|---|
| Rate equation | rate = k[A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ, determined experimentally |
| Order (with respect to A) | the power m to which [A] is raised in the rate equation |
| Overall order | m + n |
| Rate constant, k | proportionality constant in the rate equation; depends on temperature only |
| Half-life, t½ | time taken for a reactant's concentration to halve |
| Rate-determining step | slowest step of a mechanism |
| Intermediate | formed in one step, used in a later one; absent from the overall equation |
| Catalyst (in a mechanism) | used in one step, regenerated in a later one |

## What a change in concentration does

| Order | [A] × 2 | [A] × 3 | [A] × ½ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | rate × 1 | rate × 1 | rate × 1 |
| 1 | rate × 2 | rate × 3 | rate × ½ |
| 2 | rate × 4 | rate × 9 | rate × ¼ |

If two concentrations change together, multiply their effects: doubling both [A] (first
order) and [B] (second order) multiplies the rate by 2 × 4 = 8.

## Graph shapes

| Graph | Zero order | First order | Second order |
|---|---|---|---|
| [A] against time | straight line, negative gradient | curve, constant half-life | curve, half-lives increase |
| rate against [A] | horizontal line | straight line through origin | upward curve through origin |

Rate at any time = gradient of the tangent to the concentration–time curve. Initial rate =
gradient of the tangent at t = 0.

## Initial-rates method

1. Choose two experiments in which only one concentration changes.
2. Find the factor change in concentration and in rate.
3. Deduce the order; repeat for each reactant.
4. Write the rate equation; substitute one experiment to find k; add units.

*Invented data:*

| Exp. | [R] | [S] | rate / mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.010 | 0.020 | 3.0 × 10⁻⁵ |
| 2 | 0.020 | 0.020 | 1.2 × 10⁻⁴ |
| 3 | 0.010 | 0.060 | 9.0 × 10⁻⁵ |

    1 → 2: [R] × 2, rate × 4 → second order in R
    1 → 3: [S] × 3, rate × 3 → first order in S
    rate = k[R]²[S];  k = 3.0 × 10⁻⁵ ÷ (0.010² × 0.020) = 15 dm⁶ mol⁻² s⁻¹

## Half-life

- First order: t½ is **constant**, whatever the starting concentration.
- k = 0.693 ÷ t½ (first order only); units s⁻¹ if t½ in s.
- After n half-lives, fraction remaining = (½)ⁿ.

*Example:* t½ = 45.0 min = 2700 s → k = 0.693 ÷ 2700 = 2.57 × 10⁻⁴ s⁻¹. After 3.00 h
(4 half-lives), 1/16 remains.

## Units of k

    zero:   mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹       first:  s⁻¹
    second: dm³ mol⁻¹ s⁻¹      third:  dm⁶ mol⁻² s⁻¹

Method: k = rate ÷ (concentration terms); cancel units.

## Mechanisms and the rate-determining step

- Species in the rate equation appear in the slow step or in fast steps **before** it.
- Species that are zero order react only **after** the slow step.
- The orders tell you how many of each particle are involved up to and including the slow step.
- The steps must add up to the overall equation.

**Example (well-established):** hydrolysis of 2-bromo-2-methylpropane,
(CH₃)₃CBr + OH⁻ → (CH₃)₃COH + Br⁻, rate = k[(CH₃)₃CBr].

    slow:  (CH₃)₃CBr → (CH₃)₃C⁺ + Br⁻
    fast:  (CH₃)₃C⁺ + OH⁻ → (CH₃)₃COH

OH⁻ is zero order (after the slow step); (CH₃)₃C⁺ is the intermediate.

**Identifying species in a given mechanism:**

- intermediate: produced, then consumed;
- catalyst: consumed, then regenerated; can appear in the rate equation but not in the overall
  equation.

## Temperature

- Higher temperature → **larger k** → faster rate at the same concentrations.
- Reason: a greater proportion of molecules have energy ≥ Eₐ, so a greater proportion of
  collisions are successful.
- Orders do not change with temperature.

## Exam traps

- Never take orders from stoichiometric coefficients.
- Use k = 0.693 ÷ t½ only for first order.
- Convert minutes to seconds before calculating k in s⁻¹.
- Give units for k every time.
- Zero-order reactants still react; they are just not in the rate-determining step.
- An intermediate is not the same as a transition state, and not a catalyst.
- State the pair of experiments used in every order deduction.

## Self-test

1. Define order of reaction with respect to a reactant.
2. What is the shape of a concentration–time graph for a zero-order reactant?
3. What test on a concentration–time graph shows first order?
4. The rate equation is rate = k[A]²[B]. What is the overall order, and what happens to the
   rate if [A] is halved and [B] doubled?
5. Give the units of k for a second-order reaction.
6. A first-order reaction has t½ = 120 s. Calculate k.
7. For rate = k[A][B] with k = 0.25 dm³ mol⁻¹ s⁻¹, calculate the rate when [A] = 0.040 and
   [B] = 0.10 mol dm⁻³.
8. What fraction of a first-order reactant remains after five half-lives?
9. In a mechanism, step 1 (slow) is P + Q → PQ and step 2 (fast) is PQ + Q → PQ₂. Write the
   rate equation and name the intermediate.
10. Why can a catalyst appear in a rate equation?
11. Explain how a temperature rise affects k.
12. What is the rate when a second-order reactant A has [A] = 0.030 mol dm⁻³ and
    k = 0.50 dm³ mol⁻¹ s⁻¹?

**Answers**

1. The power to which the concentration of that reactant is raised in the rate equation.
2. A straight line with a negative gradient.
3. Successive half-lives are constant.
4. Order 3; rate × (½)² × 2 = **× ½**.
5. dm³ mol⁻¹ s⁻¹.
6. 0.693 ÷ 120 = **5.78 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹**.
7. 0.25 × 0.040 × 0.10 = **1.0 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹**.
8. (½)⁵ = **1/32**.
9. rate = k[P][Q]; intermediate PQ.
10. It takes part in (or before) the rate-determining step, even though it is regenerated and so
    does not appear in the overall equation.
11. k increases, because a greater proportion of molecules have energy at least equal to the
    activation energy.
12. 0.50 × 0.030² = **4.5 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹**.
