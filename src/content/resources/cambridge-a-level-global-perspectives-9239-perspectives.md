This guide covers the "Perspectives" section of Component 4, the Cambridge Research Report, in the Cambridge International AS & A Level Global Perspectives & Research (9239) syllabus for 2026-2028. The syllabus says: "A candidate's research question should lead to the discussion of different perspectives." The matching assessment points are "build and compare contrasting perspectives (AO1)" and "reflect on how alternative perspectives have influenced their argument (AO2)". For the component as a whole, read the [Cambridge Research Report study guide](/resources/a-level-global-perspectives-9239-component-4-cambridge-research-report/) first. This page goes further into building, comparing and judging perspectives.

## What the syllabus means by a perspective

The syllabus defines it precisely: "A perspective is a coherent world view which is a response to an issue. A perspective is made up of argument, evidence and assumptions, and may be influenced by a particular context."

| Part | What it is | Question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| **Argument** | the reasons that lead to a conclusion | What is being claimed, and why? |
| **Evidence** | the facts, data and examples used | What supports the claim? |
| **Assumptions** | "unstated reasons" that lead people to value different kinds of evidence and reach different conclusions | What must be accepted for the argument to work? |
| **Context** | the setting that shapes the view: place, time, culture, interests | Who holds this view, and in what situation? |

A perspective is **not** the same as a single source, a single person's opinion or a list of facts. It is a **whole position**, with its own reasoning. Several sources may belong to one perspective, and one source may contain more than one.

## The syllabus's own example

The syllabus uses industrialisation.

- One side argues **for**: "industrial production can manufacture goods more efficiently". Its evidence may be "quantitative evidence of rates of production and profitability".
- The other argues **against**, "because of the pollution it generates". Its evidence may be "observational evidence of its impact on the natural environment".
- Their assumptions differ: they are "unstated reasons which lead them to value different kinds of evidence and come to different conclusions".

The syllabus concludes: "A judgement between them can, however, be reached by the careful and sympathetic evaluation of the differing yet well-established views."

Two ideas in that sentence deserve attention. **Sympathetic** means you present each perspective in the strongest form its supporters would recognise. **Well-established** means you choose perspectives that serious people hold and that have real evidence, not a weak version created to be defeated.

## The question must lead to perspectives

The syllabus says "it should be clear from the research question that it will be possible to analyse and evaluate different perspectives". It rejects "Industrialisation" (not a question) and "Has industrialisation been environmentally damaging?" (one-sided), and accepts "Is industrialisation a good thing?". If you find it hard to name two contrasting perspectives in your question, rewrite the question first.

## Step by step: build a perspective

1. **State the position** in one sentence: "Cities should ban private cars from their centres."
2. **List the arguments** in two or three reasons.
3. **Find the evidence** that supports each reason.
4. **Identify the assumptions**: what is taken for granted?
5. **Note the context**: who holds the view, and where?

## Worked example (fictional)

*Research question: Should cities ban private cars from their centres?*

| | Perspective A: ban | Perspective B: do not ban |
|---|---|---|
| **Position** | A ban makes cities healthier and more pleasant | A ban harms businesses and limits access |
| **Arguments** | Cleaner air; safer streets; space for people | Shoppers and workers need access; deliveries need roads; some people cannot use public transport |
| **Evidence** | air-quality readings before and after ban; accident statistics; surveys of residents | retail sales data; surveys of shop owners; reports from disability groups |
| **Assumptions** | health and environment outweigh convenience; good public transport exists or can be built | economic activity depends on easy car access; alternatives are inadequate |
| **Context** | often held by city planners and public health researchers | often held by shop owners and some disability campaigners |

### Comparing them

Compare along the same lines for each perspective.

| Feature | Comparison |
|---|---|
| Agreement | both want a successful city centre |
| Disagreement | they weigh health and convenience differently |
| Type of evidence | A relies on health and safety data; B on retail and accessibility data |
| Assumptions | A assumes alternatives are available; B assumes they are not, so the quality of public transport is a key point |
| Strength | A has strong evidence on air quality; B has strong evidence on the needs of people with limited mobility |
| Weakness | A may under-estimate hardship for some groups; B may over-estimate the effect on trade |

### Reaching a judgement

A judgement weighs the perspectives, and says which is more convincing and why:

> "Perspective A is more convincing for the central streets of large cities with good public transport, because the evidence on air quality and safety is strong and measured over time. Perspective B has force where alternatives are poor, so a partial ban with exceptions for access and deliveries is more defensible than a total ban."

Notice that the judgement is **qualified**: it says when and where each view is stronger.

### Writing the comparison

A paragraph that compares perspectives usually moves through four steps: say what both share, show where they differ, explain why they differ (usually an assumption or a type of evidence), and say what that means for the question.

> "Both perspectives want a successful city centre, but they disagree about what success depends on. Supporters of a ban point to air-quality readings that improved after cars were removed, and treat health as the first priority. Opponents point to retail data and to the needs of people with limited mobility, and assume that alternatives to the car are not yet good enough. The disagreement therefore rests less on the facts about air quality, which are not seriously disputed, than on an assumption about public transport. This suggests that the strength of the alternatives is the key question for the final judgement."

Notice what this paragraph does: it names the evidence for each side, finds the assumption underneath the disagreement and carries the point forward. It does not simply say that "there are two views".

### Choosing how many perspectives

Two well-developed contrasting perspectives are better than four thin ones. A third perspective is worth adding only when it is genuinely different, for example a position that says the question depends on local conditions. With 5000 words available, aim for depth: each perspective should have enough space for argument, evidence and assumptions.

## Reflecting on how perspectives influenced your argument (AO2)

The AO2 criterion asks you to reflect on "how alternative perspectives have influenced their argument". It is not enough to say "I considered both sides". Say:

1. **Where you started**: your initial view, as in the proposal.
2. **What changed**: which perspective or piece of evidence moved your view, and how.
3. **What stayed**: the part of your view that the alternative did not change, and why.
4. **How it shaped the argument**: for example, you added the exception for access because of Perspective B.

Your research log is a good source for this reflection, because it records the changes as they happened.

## Common mistakes

- **Listing views** ("some say ... others say ...") without argument, evidence and assumptions.
- **Making one perspective a straw man**, a weak version that is easy to defeat.
- **Treating each source as a perspective.** A perspective is a coherent position, possibly supported by many sources.
- **Ignoring assumptions**, which are the hidden reasons for disagreement.
- **Comparing separately but never together**: the two perspectives should meet in a comparison and in a judgement.
- **Reaching a judgement that simply repeats the candidate's earlier opinion**, without any sign that the other perspective was taken seriously.
- **A third or fourth perspective added at the end** with no development.
- **A question that is loaded**, so there is no real second perspective.

## Checklist for your perspectives section

- Each perspective is stated in one clear sentence.
- Argument, evidence and assumptions are all identified for each.
- Context is mentioned where it affects the view.
- The perspectives are compared on the same features.
- A judgement is reached, with reasons and limits.
- Reflection shows what changed in your thinking.
