to practise, use the

## The argument in one paragraph

Pluralists see media content as a mirror of audience demand and social diversity. Marxists see it
as shaped by class power, but disagree about how: owners' direct manipulation, economic pressure
(political economy) or hegemony through journalists' shared assumptions. News selection depends on
news values, gatekeepers, sources and commercial pressure; censorship operates through states,
laws, regulators, advertisers and journalists themselves. Postmodernists argue that fragmentation
and hyperreality make any single source of control less meaningful. The media influence politics
mainly by setting agendas and framing issues.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Pluralism** | Many competing outlets reflect audience choice and social diversity |
| **Consumer sovereignty** | Audiences' choices determine what is produced |
| **Mass manipulation** | Owners deliberately use media to spread ruling-class ideas |
| **Political economy approach** | Economic structure and profit pressure narrow content (Golding and Murdock) |
| **Hegemony** | Dominance of ruling-class ideas through consent (Gramsci) |
| **News values** | Criteria that make an event newsworthy (Galtung and Ruge 1965; Harcup and O'Neill 2001, 2017) |
| **Gatekeeping** | Deciding which stories are published (White, 1950) |
| **Primary definers** | Powerful sources whose framing sets the terms of coverage (Hall et al., 1978) |
| **Churnalism** | Recycling press releases and agency copy (Davies, 2008) |
| **Framing** | Selecting and emphasising aspects of an issue to suggest a cause and remedy (Entman, 1993) |
| **Agenda setting** | Influencing what people think about |
| **Bandwagon / underdog effect** | Polls boosting the leading / trailing side |
| **Hyperreality** | Media images seeming more real than reality (Baudrillard) |
| **Self-censorship** | Journalists avoiding content they expect to cause trouble |

## Theories compared

| Theory | Who shapes content? | Evidence | Criticism |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pluralism** | Audiences, professional journalists | Market competition; public-service broadcasting (Reith: "inform, educate and entertain") | Ownership concentrated; choice limited to what is offered |
| **Manipulative Marxism** | Owners | Harold Evans leaves *The Times* (1982) after disputes with Murdoch | Little evidence of direct orders in most newsrooms |
| **Political economy** | Market pressures | Dependence on advertising, cost-cutting (Golding and Murdock) | Underplays journalists' agency |
| **Hegemonic Marxism** | Journalists' shared assumptions | Glasgow University Media Group, *Bad News* (1976) and later studies | Hard to prove what is taken for granted |
| **Postmodernism** | No single source; fragmented audiences | Baudrillard, *The Gulf War Did Not Take Place* (1991) | Ignores economic power |

## Influences on news

- News values (Galtung and Ruge; Harcup and O'Neill add celebrity, entertainment, shareability).
- Gatekeepers: editors' judgement (White's "Mr Gates").
- Sources: primary definers such as police and politicians.
- Time and budget: churnalism.
- Framing: what is emphasised and what is left out.
- Owners, advertisers and practical constraints.

## Censorship

- State: blocking, banning.
- Legal: defamation, privacy, official secrets.
- Voluntary: the UK DSMA-Notice system.
- Regulatory: broadcast impartiality rules.
- Commercial: advertiser pressure.
- Self-censorship: anticipating reactions.

## Media and the political process

- **Agenda setting:** what to think about.
- **Framing:** how to think about it.
- **Opinion polls:** bandwagon and underdog effects; "horse race" coverage.
- **News reporting:** partisan press; *The Sun*'s "It's The Sun Wot Won It" (1992), a claim
  researchers dispute.
- **Limits:** selective exposure; strongest effects on undecided voters and issue salience.

## Essay plan in five steps

*"Media content is shaped mainly by owners." Evaluate.*

1. Define the view as manipulative Marxism; give the Evans and Murdoch example.
2. Political economy: market pressure narrows content without orders (Golding and Murdock).
3. Hegemony: journalists' shared assumptions (Glasgow University Media Group; primary definers).
4. Pluralism: audience choice, professional norms, public service; postmodern fragmentation.
5. Judge: owners matter in particular cases; routine, commercial and cultural pressures explain
   most content.

## Exam traps

- Three Marxist approaches, not one: manipulation, political economy, hegemony.
- Agenda setting ≠ framing.
- "Postmodernist contribution" means what the theory adds; say what it explains and what it misses.
- Censorship includes self-censorship and commercial pressure, not only states.
- Do not claim the media decide elections; say how influence might work and evaluate it.

## Self-test

1. State two claims on which pluralism rests.
2. What was Reith's aim for public-service broadcasting?
3. How does the political economy approach differ from the manipulative approach?
4. Define hegemony.
5. Name one Glasgow University Media Group study.
6. What did White's "Mr Gates" study show?
7. What are primary definers?
8. What is churnalism?
9. Distinguish agenda setting from framing.
10. Give two types of censorship other than state censorship.
11. What is the bandwagon effect?
12. Give one strength and one weakness of the postmodernist view of media content.

### Answers

1. Any two: consumer sovereignty; diversity of outlets; professional journalism; regulation and
   public service.
2. To "inform, educate and entertain".
3. It explains narrow content through economic pressures such as advertising and cost-cutting,
   not owners' deliberate intervention.
4. The dominance of ruling-class ideas through consent rather than force (Gramsci).
5. *Bad News* (1976), *More Bad News* (1980) or *Really Bad News* (1982).
6. That an editor rejected wire stories partly on personal judgement, so gatekeeping is selective.
7. Powerful, accredited sources such as police and politicians whose framing sets the terms of
   coverage (Hall et al., 1978).
8. Journalism that recycles press releases and agency copy instead of investigating (Davies, 2008).
9. Agenda setting influences which issues people think about; framing influences how they
   understand an issue's cause and remedy.
10. Any two: legal limits; voluntary systems such as DSMA-Notices; regulation; commercial pressure;
    self-censorship.
11. Published polls boosting support for the side shown to be winning.
12. Strength: explains fragmented audiences and the power of images. Weakness: underplays the
    economic power of media corporations.
