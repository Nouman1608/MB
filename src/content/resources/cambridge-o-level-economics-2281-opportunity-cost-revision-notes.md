These are condensed notes for section 1.3, *Opportunity cost* (1.3.1 definition; 1.3.2 influence on decision making), of the Cambridge O Level Economics 2281 syllabus for examination in 2026. For full explanations and worked examples, use the [study guide on opportunity cost](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-opportunity-cost/). Then try the [practice questions on opportunity cost](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-opportunity-cost-practice/). All examples are invented.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition to learn |
|---|---|
| Opportunity cost | The next best alternative forgone when a choice is made |
| Next best alternative | The option that would have been chosen if the actual choice were not available |
| Forgone | Given up |
| Money cost | The price paid; not the same as opportunity cost |
| Free good | A good with zero opportunity cost |

## Why it exists

Finite resources + unlimited wants → choice → using a resource one way means it cannot be used another way → every choice has an opportunity cost (except using a free good).

## Spotting the opportunity cost: three checks

1. List the options the decision maker had.
2. Rank them, using the information given.
3. The opportunity cost is the **second-ranked** option only.

**Worked reminder.** A town council (invented) can spend 5 million cu on one of: a sports centre (chosen), a library, a new bus route, a park. If the council ranked the library second, the opportunity cost is **the library**, not the library, bus route and park together.

## Calculating opportunity cost in goods

Opportunity cost of 1 unit of X = amount of Y given up ÷ amount of X gained.

**Worked reminder.** A factory can make 600 shirts or 150 jackets a week.

- OC of 1 jacket = 600 ÷ 150 = **4 shirts**.
- OC of 1 shirt = 150 ÷ 600 = **0.25 jackets**.
- Check: 4 × 0.25 = 1.

**Second reminder.** A country moves from 80 units of food and 30 units of clothing to 60 units of food and 40 units of clothing. 20 food are given up for 10 clothing, so OC of 1 unit of clothing = 20 ÷ 10 = **2 units of food**.

## Examples by decision maker

| Decision maker | Choice | Opportunity cost |
|---|---|---|
| Consumer | Buys a bicycle for 300 cu | The next best use of 300 cu, such as a phone |
| Worker | Works overtime on Sunday | Leisure or family time that day |
| Student | Goes to university | Wages from the job they would have taken |
| Producer | Uses land to grow coffee | The tea (or other crop) that land could have grown |
| Government | Funds new roads | The next best project, such as more nurses |

## How opportunity cost changes decisions

- **Consumers:** if a good's price rises, buying it means giving up more of other goods, so they buy less.
- **Workers:** a higher wage in another job raises the opportunity cost of staying where they are, so some switch jobs; higher wages for school leavers raise the opportunity cost of staying in education.
- **Producers:** if another product becomes more profitable, the opportunity cost of making the current one rises, so firms switch resources.
- **Governments:** rank projects by benefit; the higher the value of what is given up, the stronger the case needed for a project. Borrowing moves the opportunity cost to the future.

**Chain to write.** Wages for unskilled jobs rise → the earnings forgone by studying rise → the opportunity cost of education rises → some students leave education earlier.

## Must-know distinctions

- Opportunity cost (what is given up) versus money cost (what is paid).
- The next best alternative versus all alternatives.
- Zero price versus zero opportunity cost: a free service paid for by taxes still has an opportunity cost.
- Opportunity cost of X in terms of Y versus Y in terms of X: they are reciprocals.

## Exam traps

- Listing every alternative.
- Writing a money figure when the question asks for the alternative given up.
- Claiming government services provided at no charge have no opportunity cost.
- Inverting the ratio in a calculation.
- Forgetting time as a resource: a worker's choice of hours has an opportunity cost in leisure.
- In a "discuss" answer about a government choice, not saying who loses out.

## Quick self-test

1. Define opportunity cost.
2. Why does every choice involving scarce resources have an opportunity cost?
3. What is the opportunity cost of using a free good?
4. Mina can study, go to the gym or watch a film tonight. She studies, and would otherwise have gone to the gym. What is her opportunity cost?
5. A machine can make 300 cups or 100 plates an hour. What is the opportunity cost of one plate?
6. Using question 5, what is the opportunity cost of one cup?
7. Give an example of opportunity cost for a government.
8. Explain why a rise in wages for school leavers might cause more students to leave school.
9. Why is opportunity cost not the same as the price of a good?
10. A firm switches its factory from making fans to making heaters. What is the opportunity cost of the heaters?
11. Why might two people facing the same choice have different opportunity costs?
12. Explain how a government borrowing money to fund a hospital still faces an opportunity cost.

### Answers

1. The next best alternative forgone when a choice is made.
2. Resources are finite, so using them in one way means they cannot be used in another.
3. Zero.
4. The gym session.
5. 300 ÷ 100 = 3 cups.
6. 100 ÷ 300 = 1/3 of a plate.
7. Spending on defence means a school (or other next best project) is not built.
8. The wages given up by staying at school rise, so the opportunity cost of education rises and some choose to work.
9. The price is what is paid; opportunity cost is the next best thing given up, which may be a different good or time.
10. The fans that the factory could have made.
11. They rank their alternatives differently, so the next best alternative differs.
12. The loan must be repaid with interest later, so future spending on other projects is reduced.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can opportunity cost be time rather than goods?**
Yes. Time is a scarce resource, so the opportunity cost of spending an evening working may be the leisure or study time given up.

**How do I find opportunity cost from figures?**
Divide the amount of the good given up by the amount of the good gained, and give the answer in units of the good given up.
