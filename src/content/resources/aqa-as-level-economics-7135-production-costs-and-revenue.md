| 3.1.3.5 | Average revenue, total revenue and profit | Difference between AR and TR; why AR is the firm's demand curve; profit = TR - TC; calculate them from data |

**Not required at AS.** The specification says the short-run average cost curve is "likely to be U-shaped" but a formal link with the law of diminishing returns is not expected. Marginal cost, marginal revenue and technological change appear only in the A-level content. So at AS you find the most profitable output by comparing profit at each output, not by setting MC equal to MR.

## 3.1.3.1 Productivity

Production is the process that turns the services of factors such as labour and capital into final output. **Labour productivity** is output per worker (or per worker-hour).

```
A bakery: 8 workers bake 400 loaves an hour  -> 50 loaves per worker
New ovens: 8 workers bake 480 loaves an hour -> 60 loaves per worker
Productivity rise = 10 / 50 = 20%
```

Productivity matters because, with the same wage, more output per worker means a lower labour cost per unit. In a data question, check whether output grew because productivity rose or simply because more workers were hired; only the first is a productivity gain.

## 3.1.3.2 Specialisation, the division of labour and exchange

**Specialisation** means concentrating on a narrow range of tasks or products. The **division of labour** is specialisation by workers within a production process.

Benefits to learn:

- Workers become quicker and more skilled through repetition.
- Time is not lost moving between tasks.
- Workers can be matched to the tasks they do best.
- Specialised machinery becomes worth buying because each task is done in volume.

Drawbacks to use in evaluation: repetitive work can lower motivation, a stoppage at one stage halts the whole process, and narrowly skilled workers may struggle to find new jobs if demand changes.

**Why exchange matters.** A specialist produces far more of one thing than they need and none of most things they want, so they must trade. Under barter, trade requires a **double coincidence of wants**: each party must want what the other offers. Money solves this by acting as a **medium of exchange**, accepted by everyone in payment, so a baker can sell bread for money and spend it on anything. Without an efficient means of exchange, the gains from specialisation would be lost in the time and effort of finding trading partners.

## 3.1.3.3 Costs of production

- **Short run:** at least one factor of production is fixed. **Long run:** all factors can be varied.
- **Fixed costs** do not change with output in the short run (rent, insurance, loan interest). **Variable costs** change with output (ingredients, energy used in production, hourly wages).
- **Total cost** = total fixed cost + total variable cost. **Average cost** = total cost / output.

### Worked example: one bakery's cost and revenue table

Output is in hundreds of loaves a day; costs and revenue are in pounds. Fixed cost is 120 a day.

| Output | TFC | TVC | TC | AFC | AVC | ATC | Price (AR) | TR | Profit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 120 | 0 | 120 | -- | -- | -- | -- | 0 | -120 |
| 1 | 120 | 60 | 180 | 120 | 60 | 180 | 150 | 150 | -30 |
| 2 | 120 | 100 | 220 | 60 | 50 | 110 | 140 | 280 | 60 |
| 3 | 120 | 150 | 270 | 40 | 50 | 90 | 130 | 390 | 120 |
| 4 | 120 | 220 | 340 | 30 | 55 | 85 | 120 | 480 | 140 |
| 5 | 120 | 320 | 440 | 24 | 64 | 88 | 110 | 550 | 110 |
| 6 | 120 | 460 | 580 | 20 | 76.67 | 96.67 | 100 | 600 | 20 |

What the table shows:

- **AFC falls continuously** because the same 120 is spread over more output.
- **ATC is U-shaped**: it falls to 85 at an output of 4, then rises. At AS you describe this shape; you are not asked to prove it from diminishing returns.
- **AR equals price.** Each hundred loaves sells for the price on the demand curve, so the AR column is the firm's demand curve. To sell more, the bakery must accept a lower price.
- **Profit is greatest at an output of 4** (480 - 340 = 140). At an output of 6, revenue is highest (600) but profit is only 20: maximising revenue and maximising profit are different objectives.
- At an output of 1, TR (150) is below TC (180), a loss of 30, even though revenue covers the variable cost of 60.

## 3.1.3.4 Economies and diseconomies of scale

**Internal economies of scale** arise from the growth of the firm itself and lower its long-run average cost.

| Type | How unit cost falls |
|---|---|
| Technical | Larger, more efficient machines; a delivery lorry twice the size costs less than twice as much to run |
| Purchasing | Bulk buying earns discounts on inputs |
| Managerial | Specialist managers can be employed for finance, marketing and personnel |
| Financial | Large firms borrow at lower interest rates because lenders see less risk |
| Marketing | An advertising campaign is spread over more units sold |
| Risk-bearing | A range of products and markets protects the firm when one market falls |

**External economies of scale** arise from the growth of the industry, often in one area, and lower costs for every firm in it: a pool of skilled local labour, specialist suppliers nearby, training courses at local colleges and shared infrastructure.

**Diseconomies of scale** raise long-run average cost as a firm becomes very large: communication becomes slower through many layers of management, coordination of many sites becomes harder, workers can feel remote from decisions and lose motivation, and managers find it harder to control costs.

**The LRAC curve.** Its shape is set by these forces. It falls while economies of scale outweigh diseconomies, can be flat across a range of outputs, and rises once diseconomies dominate. The specification also asks you to be able to use an LRAC curve to show the benefits of economies of scale that may come from monopoly, which links this part to 3.1.4.

## Annotated model paragraph

*Question style: explain one internal economy of scale that a supermarket chain may gain as it opens more stores.*

> A larger chain can gain purchasing economies of scale. Because it buys far more stock than a single shop, it can negotiate bulk discounts from food producers, which lowers the cost of each item it sells. As this cost is spread across every store, the chain's long-run average cost falls as it expands, which may allow it to cut prices or earn a higher profit margin.

Why it works: it names the type, explains why size gives the advantage, and ends with the effect on long-run average cost and a consequence for the firm.

## Common mistakes

- Calling wages a fixed cost in every case. Salaried staff on contracts may be fixed in the short run; hourly or overtime pay is variable.
- Dividing total fixed cost by output and calling it average cost. That is AFC; ATC includes variable cost too.
- Confusing internal economies (from the firm's growth) with external economies (from the industry's growth).
- Writing that a larger firm always has lower costs. Diseconomies can raise LRAC.
- Assuming the output with the highest revenue gives the highest profit.
- Using MC and MR in an AS answer when the data give only totals and averages; calculate profit at each output instead.

## Exam technique for this sub-topic

- **Calculate** items in Section A often ask for one cell of a cost table. Write the formula first, then substitute, so a slip still shows correct method.
- **Explain** questions on economies of scale need the type, the reason linked to size, and the effect on average cost.
- **Using the data** questions often give total cost and output for two firm sizes. Convert to average cost before comparing, because total cost always rises with output.
- In evaluative answers, a good condition is whether the firm has reached the size where diseconomies begin, or whether its market is large enough to sell the extra output.
