## Questions

**1.** State two factors that directly change the size of a country's population. **[2]**

**2.** In a year, Country R (illustrative) had an opening population of 25,000,000, with 400,000 births, 275,000 deaths, 30,000 immigrants and 105,000 emigrants.

**(a)** Find the birth rate and the death rate, each per 1,000 people. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate net migration. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the population growth rate as a percentage and the closing population. **[3]**

**3.** Explain two reasons why the birth rate in a country may fall as the country develops. **[4]**

**4.** Explain why a country with excellent healthcare may have a higher death rate than a poorer country. **[3]**

**5.** Explain one push factor and one pull factor that may lead workers to move from one country to another. **[4]**

**6.** The table shows Country T (illustrative).

| Population (millions) | 4 | 8 | 12 | 16 | 20 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Total output (billions) | 60 | 168 | 276 | 352 | 380 |

**(a)** Work out output per head for every population size. **[2]**
**(b)** Identify the optimum population. **[1]**
**(c)** Using the extra output from the last 4 million people, explain why 20 million is over-populated. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain how new technology could change the optimum. **[2]**

**7.** Country S (illustrative) has this age and gender structure, in % of its total population.

| Age group | Males | Females |
|---|---|---|
| 0-14 | 11 | 10 |
| 15-39 | 19 | 18 |
| 40-64 | 15 | 16 |
| 65+ | 4 | 7 |

**(a)** Dependants per 100 people of working age is a reading aid, not a syllabus term. Calculate the number of dependants (aged 0-14 and 65 and over) per 100 people aged 15-64. **[3]**
**(b)** Describe one feature of the gender pattern and suggest a reason for it. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain two effects of this age structure on the government. **[4]**

**8.** Real GDP in Country Y (illustrative) rises by 4.2% in a year and its population by 2.8%.

**(a)** Find by how many per cent real output per person changes. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain one reason why this figure does not prove that everyone is better off. **[2]**

**9.** In Country Z (illustrative) many men aged 20 to 40 have moved abroad for work, so women outnumber men at those ages. Explain two effects on the country. **[4]**

**10.** Evaluate whether a falling population is a problem for a country. **[8]**

**11.** Country N (illustrative) has a wide-based pyramid and a population growing at 2.5% a year. Discuss the economic effects of this structure and conclude whether the effects are mainly beneficial or harmful. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two of births (the birth rate), deaths (the death rate) or net migration (immigration or emigration), [1] + [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming "factors" such as education or healthcare is an indirect cause, so a direct answer needs the births, deaths or migration route that changes the number of people.

**2. (a)** Birth rate = 400,000 ÷ 25,000,000 × 1,000 = **16 per 1,000** [1]. Death rate = 275,000 ÷ 25,000,000 × 1,000 = **11 per 1,000** [1].
**(b)** Net migration = 30,000 − 105,000 = **−75,000** (net emigration) [1].
**(c)** Natural change = 400,000 − 275,000 = 125,000, total change = 125,000 − 75,000 = 50,000 [1]. Growth = 50,000 ÷ 25,000,000 × 100 = **0.2%** [1]. Closing population = 25,000,000 + 50,000 = **25,050,000** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show each subtraction and keep the minus sign on net migration. Visible working lets a marker see which step went wrong, whereas a bare wrong answer shows nothing.

**3.** First reason, such as more education for girls and women [1], which raises the opportunity cost of childcare so women have fewer children and have them later [1]. Second reason, such as lower infant mortality [1], which means families no longer need many children to be sure some survive [1]. Accept contraception, urbanisation, state pensions. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each reason is worth more when it carries a link ("so", "which means") to the birth rate. A list of factors with no reasoning usually gains little.

**4.** Healthcare is only one influence on the death rate; age structure matters too [1]. A rich country may have a large share of elderly people [1], and more elderly people die each year, so deaths per 1,000 can be higher than in a poorer country with a young population [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The question asks for "why", so state the age-structure link explicitly. Saying only "old people die" with no comparison to the poorer country is incomplete.

**5.** Push factor, such as high unemployment or low wages at home [1], which means workers expect to do better by leaving [1]. Pull factor, such as higher wages or good jobs in the destination [1], which attracts workers from countries with fewer opportunities [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Place the push factor in the home country and the pull factor in the destination. Mixing the two, or giving two push factors, loses the second pair of marks.

**6. (a)** Output per head = total output ÷ population: 60 ÷ 4 = 15; 168 ÷ 8 = 21; 276 ÷ 12 = 23 [1]; 352 ÷ 16 = 22; 380 ÷ 20 = 19 [1].
**(b)** Optimum = **12 million** (highest output per head, 23) [1].
**(c)** The last 4 million people add 380 − 352 = 28 billion [1], which is 7 per person, far below the average of 19, so average output per head falls [1].
**(d)** Technology raises output per worker [1], so each population size produces more and the peak of output per head shifts to a larger population [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Define the optimum as highest output per head. Picking the population with the highest total output (20 million) is a common wrong answer and cannot gain the mark.

**7. (a)** Aged 0-14 = 11 + 10 = 21% and aged 65+ = 4 + 7 = 11% [1]. Aged 15-64 = 100 − 21 − 11 = 68% [1]. Dependants per 100 = 32 ÷ 68 × 100 = **47.1** [1].
**(b)** Women outnumber men aged 65 and over (7 against 4) [1], probably because women have longer life expectancy [1].
**(c)** Government spending on pensions and healthcare rises because 11% are aged 65 and over [1], and the 68% of working age pay the taxes that fund this, so each worker may face a heavier burden [1]. Second effect, such as fewer children needing schools, so spending on education could fall or be redirected [1], or labour shortages that may lead the government to raise the retirement age or allow more immigration [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Show how the dependants are grouped before dividing. In part (c), link each effect to the government budget or labour market, not just to "more old people".

**8. (a)** Real GDP per head growth = 1.042 ÷ 1.028 − 1 [1] = 0.01362 [1] = **1.36%** (accept 1.4%) [1].
**(b)** GDP per head is an average [1], so it can rise while the gains go to a small group, with others no better off [1]. Accept: it ignores health, working hours or the environment. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* A quick estimate of 4.2 − 2.8 = 1.4 shows the idea. Dividing one growth rate by the other would answer a different question, so state which method you use.

**9.** Fewer working-age men reduces the labour force and may cause shortages in some jobs, cutting output [1], although the labour left behind may be paid more [1]. Families may receive money sent home by the workers abroad [1], which raises spending and living standards in those households [1]. Accept: fewer future births if women lack partners; social strain. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A two-way answer with one cost and one benefit is acceptable when the question asks for effects. Both effects must link to the gender imbalance, not to emigration in general.

**10.** Costs: fewer workers and taxpayers [1], so pensions and healthcare for older people are harder to pay for [1]; weaker demand for housing and goods, so firms may close or cut investment [1]. Benefits: less pressure on resources and the environment [1], and if the country was over-populated, output per head may rise [1]. Evaluation: the effect depends on the starting position relative to the optimum [1], on the speed of the fall, which gives time to adapt [1], and on whether productivity or immigration can offset it [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Finish with a conclusion tied to a named condition, such as whether the country sits above or below its optimum. Costs and benefits listed with no conclusion stay at description.

**11.** Benefits: a large future workforce [1], a growing market for firms [1], and a low number of pensioners so lower pension costs [1]. Harms: high spending on schools and child health [1], and pressure on housing and food [1]. Unemployment if jobs grow more slowly than the labour force [1]. Judgement: the effect depends on whether the country has enough jobs, capital and skills for the young [1], and whether it is below or above the optimum, so the effect is beneficial in the long run if investment keeps pace and harmful if it does not [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Separate short-run costs (schools, food) from long-run gains (workers). Making a distinction over time is one of the clearest ways to show evaluation.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating rates per person, or not per 1,000, and dropping the minus sign on net migration.
- Forgetting migration when finding total population growth.
- Defining optimum population as the largest or most productive total population.
- Saying a high death rate means poor healthcare with no reference to age structure.
- Describing a pyramid without reading the base, middle and top.
- Working out dependants without grouping both the young and the old.
- Listing effects of population change with no chain of reasoning to taxes, spending, jobs or living standards.
- Giving one-sided answers when the question says "evaluate" or "discuss".
