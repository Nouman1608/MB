Subtopic 1.3 Sustainability completes the Foundation topic of IB Diploma Programme Environmental
Systems and Societies (ESS), first assessment 2026. Sustainability is the third of the key concepts that
the subject brief says run through the whole course. The
[perspectives, systems and sustainability study guide](/resources/ib-dp-ess-foundation-perspectives-systems-sustainability/)
defines natural capital, ecosystem services and footprints and works a timber harvest. This guide
goes further: how an ecological footprint is built from its components, how carbon and water
footprints are calculated and interpreted, how global frameworks such as planetary boundaries, the
doughnut and the Sustainable Development Goals are used, how environmental justice fits in, and how
an environmental impact assessment works stage by stage.

The brief does not print learning outcomes for 1.3, so the scope below follows the usual teaching of
this subtopic; check it against your school's copy of the ESS guide.

## What 1.3 asks you to do

- Define sustainability and sustainable development; apply the environmental, social and economic
  pillars.
- Use natural capital and natural income to judge whether a use is sustainable.
- Classify ecosystem services.
- Calculate and interpret ecological, carbon and water footprints and compare them with biocapacity.
- Apply planetary boundaries, doughnut economics and the circular economy.
- Explain environmental justice and outline the stages and limits of an environmental impact
  assessment.

## Sustainability and sustainable development

**Sustainability** is the use and management of resources that allows full natural replacement and
the long-term survival of the system. **Sustainable development**, the term popularised by the
Brundtland Report (1987), is development that meets the needs of the present without compromising
the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. The difference is emphasis: sustainability
is a property of a system; sustainable development is a direction of change for societies.

A use is sustainable only if all three pillars hold. A palm oil plantation may be economically
profitable, but if it clears forest (environmental pillar) and displaces communities without consent
(social pillar), it fails the test.

## Ecological footprint: building it from components

An **ecological footprint (EF)** is the area of biologically productive land and water needed to
supply a population's resources and absorb its wastes, measured in global hectares (gha). It is
usually built from components:

- **carbon**: forest area needed to absorb carbon dioxide emissions not taken up by the oceans
- **cropland**: food, animal feed, fibre and oil crops
- **grazing land**: meat, dairy, leather and wool
- **forest products**: timber, paper and fuelwood
- **fishing grounds**: fish and seafood
- **built-up land**: land covered by buildings, roads and infrastructure.

**Worked example 1 (fictional data).** Country R has 8 million people.

| Component | gha per person |
|---|---|
| Carbon | 2.6 |
| Cropland | 0.9 |
| Grazing land | 0.3 |
| Forest products | 0.4 |
| Fishing grounds | 0.1 |
| Built-up land | 0.1 |

Biocapacity is 1.6 gha per person.

```
EF per person = 2.6 + 0.9 + 0.3 + 0.4 + 0.1 + 0.1 = 4.4 gha
Total EF      = 4.4 × 8 million = 35.2 million gha
Deficit       = 4.4 − 1.6 = 2.8 gha per person
EF / biocapacity = 4.4 / 1.6 = 2.75
Carbon share  = 2.6 / 4.4 × 100 = 59.1 % (3 s.f.)
```

If Country R halved its carbon component (to 1.3 gha), its EF would fall to 3.1 gha per person and
the ratio to 3.1 / 1.6 = 1.94 (3 s.f.). It would still be in deficit, which shows that energy
policy alone cannot close the gap.

**Interpreting EF.** Strengths: one easily understood number; allows comparison between countries
and over time. Limitations: it averages over a whole population; it omits some impacts, such as
toxic pollution and biodiversity loss; results depend on assumptions about yields; and a country can
reduce its domestic footprint by importing goods, shifting the impact elsewhere.

## Carbon and water footprints

A **carbon footprint** is the total greenhouse gas emissions caused directly and indirectly by a
person, product, organisation or country, in tonnes of CO₂ equivalent (t CO₂e).

**Worked example 2 (invented emission factors).** A household drives 8,000 km a year (0.17 kg CO₂e
per km), uses 2,500 kWh of electricity (0.20 kg CO₂e per kWh) and takes one return flight
(1,100 kg CO₂e).

```
Driving     = 8000 × 0.17 = 1360 kg
Electricity = 2500 × 0.20 = 500 kg
Flight      = 1100 kg
Total       = 1360 + 500 + 1100 = 2960 kg = 2.96 t CO2e
Driving share = 1360 / 2960 × 100 = 45.9 % (3 s.f.)
```

The largest single lever is driving. Note what is missing: food, goods and services bought by the
household, which often add a large indirect share.

A **water footprint** is the total volume of fresh water used to produce the goods and services
consumed. It is often split into:

- **green water**: rainwater stored in soil and used by crops
- **blue water**: surface or groundwater withdrawn, for example for irrigation
- **grey water**: the volume needed to dilute pollutants to acceptable standards.

The split matters for sustainability: blue water taken from a stressed river has a greater impact
than green water from rain-fed fields.

## Global frameworks

### Planetary boundaries

The planetary boundaries framework (Rockström and colleagues, 2009) defines nine Earth-system
processes with a "safe operating space" for humanity. A 2023 update led by Katherine Richardson
assessed six of the nine boundaries as already crossed. Use the framework to explain why local
problems, such as fertiliser run-off, add up to global risk.

### Doughnut economics

Kate Raworth's doughnut places a **social foundation** (enough food, water, health, education,
housing and political voice) inside an **ecological ceiling** (the planetary boundaries). Shortfalls
fall inside the hole; overshoot falls outside the ring. In 2020 Amsterdam adopted a city strategy
based on the doughnut model, an example of the model being used to guide local policy.

### The Sustainable Development Goals

The UN adopted 17 Sustainable Development Goals in 2015, with targets for 2030. They combine
environmental goals (climate action, life on land, life below water) with social and economic ones
(no poverty, zero hunger, decent work). Critics note possible conflicts between goals, such as
economic growth and climate action.

### Circular economy

A circular economy designs out waste and pollution, keeps products and materials in use for as long
as possible, and regenerates natural systems. In practice this means repair, reuse, refurbishment
and remanufacture before recycling, with recycling as a later loop, not the first.

## Environmental justice

**Environmental justice** is the fair distribution of environmental benefits and harms, and fair
participation in decisions. Pollution, flood risk and heat stress often fall most heavily on
low-income communities, while those with the largest footprints may feel the least impact. It links
the social pillar of sustainability to the perspectives you met in 1.1.

## Environmental impact assessment, stage by stage

1. **Screening:** decide whether the project needs a full EIA.
2. **Scoping:** decide which impacts and alternatives to study.
3. **Baseline study:** record the current environment (species, water quality, air quality, land use,
   communities).
4. **Prediction:** estimate the size and significance of impacts.
5. **Mitigation:** design measures to avoid, reduce or compensate for impacts.
6. **Report and consultation:** publish findings with a non-technical summary; hear from the public.
7. **Decision:** approve, approve with conditions, or refuse.
8. **Monitoring:** check impacts after construction and enforce conditions.

**Evaluating EIAs.** They make impacts visible before decisions are taken and give communities a
voice. But baselines may be too short to capture seasonal or yearly variation; predictions are
uncertain; the developer often pays for the report; indirect and cumulative impacts are hard to
assess; and decision-makers may still give economic benefits more weight.

## Common mistakes

- Treating sustainability and sustainable development as identical terms.
- Giving a footprint with no unit, or comparing a total EF with a per-person biocapacity.
- Saying a low domestic footprint proves a country is sustainable, ignoring imports.
- Listing SDGs without explaining a link or a conflict.
- Describing EIA stages without evaluating the process.

## Exam technique

"Calculate" needs working and units. "Explain" a framework needs what it measures and how it guides
decisions. "Evaluate" an indicator such as EF needs strengths, limitations and a conclusion about its
usefulness. In Paper 1, connect the case study's data to a framework: a country in deficit, a city
overshooting the ceiling, a project lacking a baseline.
