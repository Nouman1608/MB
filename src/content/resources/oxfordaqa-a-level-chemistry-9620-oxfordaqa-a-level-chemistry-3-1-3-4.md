This guide goes deeper into one point of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3: **3.1.3.4 Bonding and physical properties**. It is International AS content, examined in Unit 1, and Unit 2 questions may draw on bonding principles. The specification names four types of crystal structure (ionic, metallic, macromolecular or giant covalent, and molecular) and six example crystals: diamond, graphite, ice, iodine, magnesium and sodium chloride. You must be able to:

- relate the melting point and conductivity of materials to the type of structure and the bonding present;
- explain the energy changes associated with changes of state;
- draw diagrams to represent these structures involving specified numbers of particles.

The broader [bonding and structure guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-bonding-and-structure/) introduces ionic, covalent and metallic bonding. This page concentrates on reasoning from structure to property. Afterwards, use the [bonding and physical properties revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-3-4-revision-notes/) and the [bonding and physical properties practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-3-4-practice/).

## The one question behind every property

Every melting-point or conductivity answer comes back to one question: **what particles are present, and what holds them in place?**

| Crystal type | Particles in the lattice | What holds them | What must be overcome on melting |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ionic (sodium chloride) | Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions | Electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions, in all directions | Many strong ionic attractions |
| Metallic (magnesium) | Mg²⁺ ions and delocalised electrons | Attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons | Strong metallic bonding (enough to let ions move past each other) |
| Macromolecular (diamond, graphite) | Carbon atoms | Covalent bonds throughout the whole structure | Many strong covalent bonds |
| Molecular (iodine, ice) | I₂ or H₂O molecules | Covalent bonds inside each molecule; intermolecular forces between molecules | Only the weak forces **between** molecules |

The last column is where most marks are won or lost. In a molecular crystal, melting and boiling never break the covalent bonds inside the molecules. Iodine vapour is still made of I₂ molecules, and steam is still H₂O.

## Melting point

Approximate melting points of the six named crystals:

| Substance | Type | Melting point |
|---|---|---|
| Diamond | Macromolecular | Very high (above 3500 °C) |
| Graphite | Macromolecular | Very high (above 3500 °C; it sublimes) |
| Sodium chloride | Ionic | 801 °C |
| Magnesium | Metallic | 650 °C |
| Iodine | Molecular | 114 °C |
| Ice | Molecular | 0 °C |

The order follows the strength and number of the interactions that must be overcome.

- **Macromolecular**: every atom is held by covalent bonds, so melting means breaking a huge number of strong bonds.
- **Ionic**: a giant lattice of strong electrostatic attractions. Smaller ions and higher charges give stronger attraction.
- **Metallic**: a giant lattice. Mg²⁺ has a 2+ charge and each atom gives two electrons to the delocalised sea, so the attraction is stronger than in a Group 1 metal.
- **Molecular**: only intermolecular forces hold the molecules together. Iodine relies on induced dipole–dipole (van der Waals) forces between large I₂ molecules with many electrons. Ice relies on hydrogen bonds. Both are much weaker than ionic, metallic or covalent bonding.

Ice melting below iodine does not mean hydrogen bonds are weaker than van der Waals forces in general. I₂ molecules have far more electrons than H₂O molecules, so their induced dipole–dipole forces are unusually strong. Compare like with like before drawing conclusions about force types.

## Electrical conductivity

To conduct, a substance needs **charged particles that are free to move**.

| Substance | Solid | Liquid (molten) | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Magnesium | Conducts | Conducts | Delocalised electrons move through the structure |
| Graphite | Conducts | — | One delocalised electron per carbon atom moves along the layers |
| Sodium chloride | Does not conduct | Conducts (also in aqueous solution) | Ions are fixed in the solid lattice but free to move when molten or dissolved |
| Diamond | Does not conduct | — | All four outer electrons of each carbon are in localised covalent bonds |
| Iodine and ice | Do not conduct | Do not conduct | Neutral molecules; no free ions or delocalised electrons |

Two precise points earn credit:

1. For ionic compounds the carriers are **ions**, not electrons. Writing "molten sodium chloride conducts because electrons can move" loses the mark.
2. Graphite conducts because of **delocalised electrons**. Its layers sliding over each other explains softness, not conductivity.

## Energy changes associated with changes of state

Melting and boiling are **endothermic**: energy must be supplied to overcome the forces holding particles together. Freezing and condensing are **exothermic**: the same forces form again and energy is released.

When a solid is heated steadily, its temperature rises until it reaches the melting point and then **stays constant** while it melts. The energy supplied is being used to overcome forces between particles, not to increase their kinetic energy. The same happens at the boiling point.

### Worked example 1: why boiling needs more energy than melting

The enthalpy change to melt ice at 0 °C is +6.01 kJ mol⁻¹; to boil water at 100 °C it is +40.7 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the energy to melt 54.0 g of ice and to boil 54.0 g of water, and explain the difference.

```
n(H₂O) = 54.0 ÷ 18.0 = 3.00 mol
melting: 3.00 × 6.01 = 18.0 kJ
boiling: 3.00 × 40.7 = 122 kJ
```

Boiling needs about seven times more energy. On melting, only some hydrogen bonds break: molecules in liquid water are still close together and still hydrogen bonded, but now able to move past one another. On boiling, the molecules separate completely, so essentially all the remaining hydrogen bonds must be overcome.

## The six named structures and how to draw them

Questions often say "draw a diagram ... showing **n** particles". The examiner checks the arrangement, the labels and that the number matches.

- **Sodium chloride**: alternating Na⁺ and Cl⁻ in a cubic lattice. A 2 × 2 × 2 cube shows 8 ions (4 Na⁺, 4 Cl⁻). In the full lattice each ion has 6 nearest neighbours of opposite charge.
- **Magnesium**: a regular lattice of Mg²⁺ ions surrounded by delocalised electrons. Because each atom supplies two electrons, a diagram with 6 Mg²⁺ ions should show 12 electrons if electrons are drawn individually.
- **Diamond**: each carbon bonded to four others tetrahedrally. A five-atom diagram shows one central carbon and four bonded carbons at the corners of a tetrahedron.
- **Graphite**: flat layers of hexagonal rings, each carbon bonded to three others, with weak forces between layers. Show at least two layers, clearly separated.
- **Iodine**: a regular lattice of I₂ molecules. Draw each molecule as two touching atoms joined by a covalent bond, with gaps between molecules to show weak intermolecular forces.
- **Ice**: each H₂O molecule hydrogen bonded to four neighbours in an open, tetrahedral arrangement. Draw hydrogen bonds as dashed lines from a δ+ hydrogen to a lone pair on a neighbouring oxygen.

### Worked example 2: deducing structure from data

Solid P melts at 1710 °C, does not conduct as a solid or when molten, and is insoluble in water. Solid Q melts at 186 °C and does not conduct in any state. Identify each type of structure.

**Annotated answer.** P has a very high melting point, so a very large number of strong bonds must be broken: it is macromolecular (giant covalent) [the reason ties the property to bonding]. It is not ionic, because an ionic compound would conduct when molten [eliminating the alternative strengthens the answer]. Q has a low melting point, so only weak intermolecular forces are overcome: it is molecular [naming what is overcome, not "weak bonds"].

## Common mistakes

- Saying that covalent bonds break when iodine or ice melts.
- Writing "ionic bonds are strong" without saying that **many** attractions act in **all directions** in a giant lattice.
- Giving electrons as the charge carriers in molten sodium chloride.
- Explaining graphite's conductivity by layers sliding.
- Drawing a metal with no delocalised electrons, or with ions labelled as atoms.
- Calling diamond and graphite "molecules": they are macromolecular, with no separate molecules.
- Stating that the temperature rises during melting.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** questions on melting points need three links: the structure type, the force or bond overcome, and its relative strength (with "more energy needed").
- **Compare** questions need a statement about both substances, not just the one with the higher value.
- **Draw** questions: label the particles, show the required number and include a key if you use symbols.
- **Suggest** questions use unfamiliar substances: classify them from the data first, then reason as for the named examples.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does graphite conduct electricity but diamond does not?**
Each carbon in graphite forms three covalent bonds, leaving one delocalised electron per atom that can move along the layers. In diamond all four outer electrons of each carbon are in localised covalent bonds, so there are no mobile charge carriers.

**Why does the temperature stay constant while a solid melts?**
The energy supplied is used to overcome the forces between particles rather than to increase their kinetic energy. Once all the solid has melted, the temperature rises again.

**Does sodium chloride conduct as a solid?**
No. The ions are held in fixed positions in the lattice. Sodium chloride conducts when molten or dissolved in water, because the ions are then free to move.
