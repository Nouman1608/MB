This guide covers subtopic **34.2, Phenylamine and azo compounds**, from Topic 34, Nitrogen
compounds, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is A Level
content. The broader [Amines: Aliphatic and Aromatic study guide](/resources/a-amines-aliphatic-and-aromatic/)
summarises each reaction; this page follows the chemistry step by step, from benzene to a finished
dye, and shows how to work backwards from an unfamiliar azo compound.

## What the syllabus asks for

Subtopic 34.2 has four learning outcomes:

1. describe the preparation of phenylamine "via the nitration of benzene to form nitrobenzene
   followed by reduction with hot Sn/concentrated HCl followed by NaOH(aq)"
2. describe (a) the reaction of phenylamine with Br₂(aq) at room temperature, and (b) its reaction
   with HNO₂, or NaNO₂ and dilute acid, below 10 °C to give the diazonium salt, and the further
   warming of that salt with water to give phenol
3. describe and explain the relative basicities of aqueous ammonia, ethylamine and phenylamine
4. recall that benzenediazonium chloride couples with phenol in NaOH(aq) to form an azo compound;
   identify the azo group; state that azo compounds are often used as dyes; and recall that other
   azo dyes can be formed by a similar route

## From benzene to phenylamine

**Step 1: nitration.** Benzene is heated with a mixture of concentrated HNO₃ and concentrated H₂SO₄
at a temperature between 25 °C and 60 °C (from subtopic 30.1). The electrophile is NO₂⁺.

    C₆H₆ + HNO₃ → C₆H₅NO₂ + H₂O

Keeping the temperature in this range limits further nitration of the ring.

**Step 2: reduction with hot tin and concentrated hydrochloric acid.** Nitrobenzene is heated under
reflux with tin and concentrated HCl. Using [H] for the reducing agent:

    C₆H₅NO₂ + 6[H] → C₆H₅NH₂ + 2H₂O

Because the mixture contains excess concentrated acid, the phenylamine formed is protonated and is
actually present as the **phenylammonium ion**, C₆H₅NH₃⁺.

**Step 3: NaOH(aq).** Adding aqueous sodium hydroxide removes the proton and releases free
phenylamine:

    C₆H₅NH₃⁺ + OH⁻ → C₆H₅NH₂ + H₂O

Questions often ask why the alkali is needed. The answer is that the reduction step leaves the
product as a salt, and the salt is converted to the amine by a stronger base.

## Phenylamine with bromine water

At room temperature, with no catalyst, phenylamine decolourises orange bromine water and gives a
**white precipitate** of 2,4,6-tribromophenylamine:

    C₆H₅NH₂ + 3Br₂ → C₆H₂Br₃NH₂ + 3HBr

The explanation mirrors phenol. A lone pair on nitrogen overlaps with the π system of the ring and is
partly delocalised into it. This raises the electron density of the ring, so it is **activated**:
it can polarise Br₂ on its own, no halogen carrier is needed, and three hydrogens are substituted.
The –NH₂ group directs to the **2-, 4- and 6-positions**. Benzene, by contrast, needs a halogen
carrier and takes one bromine atom.

## Diazotisation, and phenol from the diazonium salt

**Making nitrous acid.** HNO₂ is unstable, so it is made in the flask from sodium nitrite and dilute
hydrochloric acid:

    NaNO₂ + HCl → HNO₂ + NaCl

**Diazotisation, below 10 °C:**

    C₆H₅NH₂ + HNO₂ + HCl → C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻ + 2H₂O

The product is **benzenediazonium chloride**, containing the ion C₆H₅–N≡N⁺. The low temperature
matters because the diazonium salt decomposes on warming. An ice bath keeps it intact for the next
step.

**Warming with water gives phenol:**

    C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻ + H₂O → C₆H₅OH + N₂ + HCl

You would see bubbles of nitrogen. Notice that the same instability that forces the cold conditions
is used deliberately here.

## Relative basicity: ethylamine > ammonia > phenylamine

All three accept a proton using the lone pair on nitrogen. The difference is how available that lone
pair is.

- **Ethylamine:** the ethyl group is electron-donating, so electron density on nitrogen increases
  and the lone pair is more available. Strongest base of the three.
- **Ammonia:** no donating or delocalising group. Intermediate.
- **Phenylamine:** the lone pair is partly delocalised into the benzene ring, so it is less
  available to accept H⁺. Weakest base of the three.

Writing all three explanations, with the comparison word in each, is what earns full credit.

## Azo compounds

**Coupling.** Benzenediazonium chloride is added to phenol dissolved in **NaOH(aq)**, kept cold. A
yellow-orange azo compound forms:

    C₆H₅N₂⁺ + C₆H₅O⁻ → C₆H₅N=NC₆H₄OH

- The diazonium ion is a weak electrophile, so it reacts only with a strongly activated ring. In
  alkali, phenol is present as the phenoxide ion, which is even more activated than phenol itself.
- Substitution happens at the **4-position** of the phenol ring (opposite the –OH).
- The product is 4-hydroxyazobenzene (also named 4-(phenyldiazenyl)phenol).

**The azo group** is **–N=N–**, linking two aromatic rings. Identify it by the nitrogen–nitrogen
double bond, not by the ring or the –OH.

**Why azo compounds are dyes.** The –N=N– group joins the two delocalised ring systems into one
extended delocalised system. Such a system absorbs light in the visible region, so the compound is
coloured. Azo compounds are often used as dyes.

**Other azo dyes.** Using a different aromatic amine, or a different coupling partner such as a
substituted phenol or naphthol, gives a different azo dye by the same two steps: diazotisation below
10 °C, then coupling in alkaline solution.

## Worked example 1: working backwards from an azo dye

**Question.** An azo dye has the structure CH₃C₆H₄–N=N–C₆H₄OH, with the methyl group at the
4-position of its ring. Identify the two starting materials and give the conditions for each step.

**Answer.** Split the molecule at the N=N bond.

- The ring without –OH came from the **diazonium salt**, so the amine was
  **4-methylphenylamine**, CH₃C₆H₄NH₂.
- The ring with –OH, joined at its 4-position, came from **phenol**.
- Step 1: 4-methylphenylamine with NaNO₂ and dilute HCl, **below 10 °C**.
- Step 2: add the diazonium salt solution to phenol in **NaOH(aq)**, kept cold.

## Worked example 2: a three-step route from nitrobenzene

**Question.** Give a route from nitrobenzene to phenol, naming each intermediate. **[4]**

**Model answer.**

1. Heat with **tin and concentrated HCl**, then add **NaOH(aq)**, to give **phenylamine**. **[1]**
2. Add **NaNO₂ and dilute HCl** **[1]**, **below 10 °C**, to give **benzenediazonium chloride** **[1]**.
3. **Warm** the solution with water: phenol forms and nitrogen is evolved. **[1]**

*Why answers lose marks:* leaving out NaOH(aq) in step 1, or giving "below 10 °C" for step 3, where
warming is required.

## Common mistakes

- Writing "dilute HCl" for the tin reduction; the syllabus says **concentrated**.
- Forgetting that the reduction gives the phenylammonium salt, so NaOH(aq) is needed.
- Giving a catalyst for bromination of phenylamine. None is needed.
- Writing **–N≡N–** as the azo group. In the dye the link is a **double** bond; the triple bond is
  in the diazonium ion.
- Coupling in acid. The phenol must be in **NaOH(aq)**.
- Explaining basicity of phenylamine as "the ring is electron-withdrawing" without mentioning the
  delocalised lone pair.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Describe** the preparation: name all three stages, with Sn, concentrated HCl, heat, then NaOH(aq).
- **Describe** a reaction with Br₂(aq): give the observation, product and equation.
- **Explain** the basicity order: one reason per compound, tied to lone-pair availability.
- **Identify** the azo group: draw or name –N=N–.
- **State** why azo compounds are coloured or useful: extended delocalisation absorbs visible light;
  used as dyes.
