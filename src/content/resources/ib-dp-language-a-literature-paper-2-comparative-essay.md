This study guide teaches the Paper 2 comparative essay for IB Diploma Programme Language A: Literature,
at SL and HL. It draws on two of the course's three areas of exploration, **Intertextuality: connecting
texts** and **Time and space**, because Paper 2 asks you to connect two literary works and to read each
in its context. It follows the current IB subject brief (first assessments for SL and HL 2021). The task
is the same at both levels; only its weighting differs.

and the [Paper 2 practice questions](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-literature-paper-2-comparative-essay-practice/).

## What Paper 2 is

The subject brief describes Paper 2 as a **comparative essay based on two literary works**, written in
response to **a choice of one out of four questions**.

| Fact from the subject brief | SL | HL |
|---|---|---|
| Time | 1.75 hours (1 hour 45 minutes) | 1.75 hours (1 hour 45 minutes) |
| Weighting of final grade | 35% | 25% |
| Basis of the essay | two literary works | two literary works |

You choose the question and the two works, so have more than two works ready. And two good separate
discussions do not do a comparative task. Prepare to write from memory; your teacher will confirm the exam rules
and which of your studied works you may use for Paper 2.

The worked examples on this page use two public-domain works: Robert Louis Stevenson, *Strange Case of
Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde* (novella, 1886), and Oscar Wilde, *The Picture of Dorian Gray* (novel, magazine
version 1890, book edition 1891). Both show a respectable London gentleman living a hidden second life.
Swap in your own works; the method is the same.

## Answering the question set

### Sort the question

A practical way to read the four questions (this is a study habit, not an IB classification) is to ask
what each one is mainly about:

- **A human concern**: guilt, ambition, secrecy, freedom, family.
- **A feature of craft**: setting, narrative voice, a minor character, an ending, a symbol.
- **Readers and contexts**: how a work reflects or challenges its time, or how readers respond.

Many questions mix two. Craft questions suit well-prepared works, because analysis of method is the
answer.

### Unpack it in three steps

Take this **original question written for Marlbridge**: *"How, and to what effect, do two works you have
studied use a particular place to reveal what characters try to hide?"*

1. **Key terms**: "a particular place", "reveal", "try to hide". Every paragraph must touch at least one.
2. **Double demand**: "how" (methods) *and* "to what effect" (what the methods do to our understanding).
   An essay that only lists places answers half the question.
3. **Fit test**: name one precise moment from each work before you commit. Stevenson: the windowless
   building with one door on a London by-street, which turns out to be the back of Jekyll's house.
   Wilde: the old schoolroom at the top of Dorian's house, where he locks the portrait under a purple
   satin coverlet. If you can find three comparable moments like this, the pair fits.

### Choose the pair, not the favourite

Sketch a quick grid: works down the side, the question's key terms across the top, one moment in each
cell. The pair with the fullest cells wins, even if it is not your favourite pair.

## Building the thesis

A Paper 2 thesis does four things: it answers the question in the question's own terms, names both
works, states what they share, and states how they differ in method or effect.

- *Weak:* "Both novels are about hidden lives in Victorian London." (topic, no claim, no question terms)
- *Better:* "Both writers use a locked room to show what their characters hide." (claim, no difference)
- *Strong:* "Stevenson and Wilde both use a locked room to hold the self their gentlemen cannot show in
  public, but Stevenson's room has a door onto the street, so the hidden self escapes and harms others,
  while Wilde seals his room at the top of the house, so the hidden self becomes a record that Dorian
  must finally face alone."

Test your thesis with four checks. Does it use the question's key terms? Does it contain a "but",
"while" or "whereas"? Does it say *to what effect*? Could a reasonable reader disagree with it? If the
answer to any check is no, rewrite before you start the introduction.

## The introduction and the final paragraph

**Introduction (4–5 sentences):** say what the key term means for this essay ("place" here means a
building or room the writer describes closely); identify each work with its author, form and date;
give the thesis; preview the points in order. No biography, no plot summary.

**Final paragraph:** answer the question directly in one sentence, then return to the most significant
*difference* and say what it shows about the two works together. Do not add new evidence, and do not
simply repeat the introduction.

## Integrated comparison

Integration means each body paragraph works on **one point** and uses **both works** to develop it. The
comparison sits inside the paragraph, not in a separate section at the end.

### Three levels of integration

1. **Alternating**: a sentence on Work A, then a sentence on Work B. Better than blocks, but it can read
   like a list.
2. **Hinged**: a comparative topic sentence, evidence from each work, then a sentence that states what
   the difference shows.
3. **Analytical**: one technique examined across both works at once, such as how each writer uses a
   door, a mirror or a narrator to control what the reader sees.

Aim for level 2 in every paragraph and level 3 where you can.

### Model paragraph (original writing for Marlbridge)

> Both writers hide the secret self inside a house, but Stevenson lets the hiding place face the street
> while Wilde locks it deep inside a private home. Stevenson's opening chapter lingers on "a certain
> sinister block of building" with no window and a single door; only later do we learn it is the back of
> Jekyll's respectable house, so the monstrous and the respectable share one structure. Wilde, by
> contrast, has Dorian lock the portrait in the old schoolroom at the top of his house, under a purple
> satin coverlet. The choice of a childhood room is pointed: the record of Dorian's corruption is stored where
> his innocence once was. The difference changes where the danger falls. Stevenson's door opens onto a
> London by-street, and the first story we hear is of Hyde, who "trampled calmly" over a child; the hidden
> self spills into the city. Wilde's room turns the damage inward, onto the canvas, which by the end
> "had been like conscience to him". Both houses show a divided life, but Stevenson's threatens others
> first, while Wilde's threatens its owner.

The topic sentence already compares, each work gets precise evidence, and the connectives introduce
analysis rather than a switch of text.

## Remembered quotation

Plan to write from memory (your teacher will confirm the rules), so evidence has to be learned. Short, exact quotations are worth more than
long, shaky ones.

### Build a quotation bank by concern

Group quotations under the concerns and features you expect questions on, so that one cluster serves
several questions.

| Cluster | *Jekyll and Hyde* | *The Picture of Dorian Gray* | Method to name |
|---|---|---|---|
| The divided self | "man is not truly one, but truly two" | "the most magical of mirrors" | aphorism; symbol |
| Release of the hidden self | "My devil had been long caged" | "The only way to get rid of a temptation is to yield to it" | metaphor; epigram |
| Place and concealment | "a certain sinister block of building" | the old schoolroom, the purple satin coverlet (reference) | setting as symbol |
| Violence against the innocent | "trampled calmly" | Basil killed in the room with the portrait (reference) | understatement; placement |
| Endings | "I bring the life of that unhappy Henry Jekyll to an end" | "withered, wrinkled, and loathsome of visage" | first-person confession; reversal |

### How to learn them

1. Keep each quotation between three and ten words where you can.
2. Learn **where** it comes from (whose voice, which chapter or stage of the plot) and **one technique**
   in it. A quotation without a comment is decoration.
3. Test by retrieval: cover the bank, write the cluster from memory, check every word, repeat after a
   few days.
4. If you are unsure of the exact words on the day, **paraphrase precisely** without quotation marks.
   "Near the end, Dorian decides that the portrait is the last piece of evidence against him" is sound
   evidence. A misquotation in quotation marks is not.

### Embed, don't drop

- *Dropped:* Jekyll is divided. "man is not truly one, but truly two". This shows duality.
- *Embedded:* Jekyll's statement turns his experiment into a general law, claiming that "man is not
  truly one, but truly two", so his private failure is presented as everyone's condition.

The embedded version fits the grammar of the sentence, says who speaks, and analyses the effect.

## Time and space in the comparison

Context earns its place when it **explains a difference** between the works. Two examples:

- Stevenson's story reaches us through a lawyer, Utterson, and through documents: Lanyon's narrative
  and Jekyll's full statement. For a novella of the 1880s about a professional class that valued
  reputation, the form is itself an investigation, and the truth arrives as written evidence.
- Wilde's novel was attacked by some reviewers as immoral when it first appeared in a magazine in 1890.
  The 1891 book added a Preface of short statements ending "All art is quite useless". Set beside
  Stevenson's investigation, this shows two different answers to readers of the same period: Stevenson
  gives them a case to solve, Wilde refuses to hand them a moral.

A readers-today point also counts: a modern reader may see Jekyll's two selves less as good and evil
than as a public role and a private life. One such sentence, tied to a method, is enough.

## Common errors

- **Answering a prepared question.** A memorised essay on "duality" bent to fit a question about place.
- **One key term dropped.** Writing about "hide" and forgetting "reveal", or "how" without "effect".
- **Uneven evidence.** Exact quotations from one work, vague summary of the other.
- **Comparison only at the joins.** "Similarly" at the start of the paragraph, then two separate
  discussions.
- **Misquoting.** Changing words, or merging two quotations into one. Paraphrase instead.
- **Context as decoration.** Dates and biography that explain nothing about method.
- **A summary conclusion.** Repeating points instead of answering the question.
