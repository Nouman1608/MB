This guide covers subtopic **1.1 Business activity** of the Cambridge IGCSE Business 0264 syllabus for examination in 2027, 2028 and 2029. The broader [Business activity and economic sectors study guide](/resources/igcse-business-0264-business-activity-and-economic-sectors/) introduces 1.1 alongside 1.2; this page goes deeper into 1.1 alone: how to classify factors of production in awkward cases, why businesses change their mix of factors, why added value is not profit, how to judge different ways of raising added value, and how to use opportunity cost to support a decision. For quick recall use the [Business activity revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-business-0264-business-activity-revision-notes/), and to test yourself the [Business activity practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-business-0264-business-activity-practice/). All businesses and figures on this page are invented for teaching.

## What 1.1 covers

Section 1.1.1, "The nature of business activity", lists three content points:

- factors of production: land, labour, capital and enterprise
- concept of adding value and how added value can be increased
- concept of opportunity cost

The syllabus also says that candidates must apply their understanding to a variety of business situations and make simple decisions based on analysis and evaluation of the information provided. So the skill is not reciting definitions but using them on the business in front of you.

## Why business activity exists

People have far more wants than can be met, while the resources to produce goods and services are limited. Business activity is the process of combining those limited resources to make products that customers will pay for. Two questions follow, and they are the rest of this subtopic: which resources does a business use (factors of production), and how does it create something worth more than what it put in (added value)? Because resources are limited, every use of them also means giving something else up (opportunity cost).

## Factors of production in depth

### Classifying awkward cases

Most marks are lost not on the definitions but on applying them. Use the test "what kind of resource is this, in this business?"

| Item in a case | Factor | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| A plot of land with mineral deposits | Land | A natural resource |
| A delivery van owned by a bakery | Capital | Man-made, used to produce the service of delivery |
| The van driver's skill and time | Labour | Human effort |
| A website that takes customer orders | Capital | A man-made tool used in production |
| An owner who invests savings, hires staff and also serves customers | Enterprise and labour | Taking the risk and organising is enterprise; serving customers is labour |
| Wages, rent or interest | None by itself | These are payments for factors, not factors |

The last two rows matter most. One person can supply two factors. And a cost in the accounts is not a factor; it is the price paid for one.

### Quantity and quality of factors

Output depends on the **quality** of factors as well as the quantity. Trained labour, modern capital and fertile land produce more, or better, output from the same amount of input. When a question asks how a business could produce more without hiring more staff, improving the quality of labour (training) or capital (newer machinery) is a valid answer.

### Changing the mix of factors

Businesses can often swap one factor for another. A supermarket that installs self-service tills uses more capital and less labour. Reasons a business might do this, and the drawbacks it must weigh:

| Possible benefit of more capital, less labour | Possible drawback |
|---|---|
| Lower wage costs over time | High cost of buying the equipment |
| Faster, more consistent output | Breakdowns can stop production |
| Fewer errors on repetitive tasks | Customers may value personal service |
| Can operate for longer hours | Remaining staff may need training; job losses can harm motivation and reputation |

Labour-intensive businesses (such as hair salons or private tutoring) find it harder to substitute capital, because the product is the worker's skill.

## Added value in depth

Added value is the difference between the selling price of a product and the cost of the bought-in materials or inputs used to make it. It measures what the business itself has contributed.

### Added value is not profit

Out of its added value, a business must still pay for its other factors: wages for labour, rent for premises, interest on loans, and so on. Only what is left is profit.

**Worked example.** Tamsin's Juice Bar (a fictional business) sells smoothies at 4.20 each. Bought-in fruit, yoghurt and cups cost 1.55 per smoothie. It sells 3,000 a month. Wages are 3,800, rent 1,900 and other overheads 600 a month.

```
Added value per smoothie = 4.20 − 1.55 = 2.65
Total added value        = 2.65 × 3,000 = 7,950
Other costs              = 3,800 + 1,900 + 600 = 6,300
Profit                   = 7,950 − 6,300 = 1,650
```

*Annotation.* A business can add a lot of value and still make a loss if its wages and rent are high. Equally, rising added value does not guarantee rising profit.

### Ways businesses add value

Customers pay more than the cost of the inputs because the business makes the product more useful or desirable. Common sources of added value:

- **Branding**: a recognised name that customers trust or want to be seen with
- **Quality and design**: better materials or a more attractive product
- **Convenience**: the product is available where and when customers want it, such as ready-made meals
- **Service**: advice, delivery, after-sales care or personalisation
- **Unique features**: something competitors do not offer
- **Packaging and presentation**: turning a basic item into a gift

### Judging a change: does total added value rise?

A higher price raises added value per unit, but may reduce sales. A full answer checks the **total**.

Tamsin considers rebranding with locally sourced fruit. Input cost rises to 1.85 and price to 4.90, but she expects sales to fall to 2,700 a month.

```
New added value per smoothie = 4.90 − 1.85 = 3.05
New total added value        = 3.05 × 2,700 = 8,235
Change                       = 8,235 − 7,950 = +285
Profit (other costs unchanged) = 8,235 − 6,300 = 1,935
```

The change looks worthwhile, but it rests on the sales forecast. If sales fell to 2,500, total added value would be 3.05 × 2,500 = 7,625, below the current 7,950. A strong answer states the assumption and says what would make the decision wrong.

### Negative added value

If a product is sold for less than its bought-in inputs, added value is negative. A clearance sale of smoothies at 1.20 when inputs cost 1.55 gives −0.35 each. A business might still do this to clear stock that would otherwise be wasted, since the inputs have already been paid for.

## Opportunity cost in depth

Opportunity cost is the benefit of the next best alternative given up when a choice is made. It exists because resources (money, land, time, workers) are limited.

### Applying it beyond money

- **Land**: a field used to grow one crop cannot be used for another, or rented out.
- **Time**: hours an owner spends on paperwork are hours not spent winning customers.
- **Labour**: staff moved to a new product are no longer making the old one.
- **For individuals**: a person who leaves a salaried job to start a business gives up that salary. This is part of the risk of enterprise.
- **For governments**: money spent on a new road cannot be spent on schools or hospitals.

### Worked example: using opportunity cost to support a decision

Brennock Farm (fictional) has a 20-hectare field. Options for next year, with expected profit:

| Option | Expected profit a year | Other information |
|---|---|---|
| Grow barley | 9,000 | Depends on weather and grain prices |
| Rent to a solar panel company | 11,500 | Fixed payment; contract for many years |
| Graze sheep | 7,200 | Uses existing farm workers |

If the farmer chooses the solar contract, the opportunity cost is the barley profit of 9,000 a year (the next best alternative), not 9,000 + 7,200.

*Annotation of a justified recommendation.* "I recommend renting the field for solar panels. It earns 11,500, which is 2,500 more than the opportunity cost of 9,000 from barley. The rent is fixed, so the income is also less risky than barley, which depends on the weather. However, a long contract means the farm gives up the use of the field for many years, so if grain prices rose sharply the opportunity cost would grow. On balance the higher and more certain income outweighs this." This answer uses the figures, names the opportunity cost correctly, considers a drawback specific to this case, and reaches a decision with a reason.

## Common mistakes

- **Naming a payment as a factor**: "wages" instead of labour.
- **Treating "capital" as money** in a factors question.
- **Forgetting that one person can provide both labour and enterprise.**
- **Calling added value "profit"** or subtracting wages and rent when calculating it.
- **Judging a price rise on added value per unit only**, without checking total added value at the new sales level.
- **Adding up all rejected options** as the opportunity cost.
- **Giving the cost of the chosen option** as the opportunity cost.

## Exam technique for the command words

The syllabus lists these command words: calculate, consider, define, explain, identify, justify, outline and state.

- **Define** (added value, opportunity cost): give the precise meaning in one sentence.
- **Identify** (a factor of production): name it; one word is enough.
- **Calculate** (added value): show the formula, the figures chosen and the unit (per item or per month).
- **Outline** (ways to increase added value): set out the main points briefly.
- **Explain**: give the point, then say why or how it affects this business, using its data.
- **Consider**: review the information given and respond to it, weighing more than one side.
- **Justify** (a recommendation): support your choice with evidence from the case, including what is given up.
