## Questions

### Section A

**1.** Define opportunity cost. **[1]**

**2.** State the three key economic decisions every economy must make. **[3]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by the assumption of ceteris paribus. **[2]**

**4.** A car manufacturer uses a robotic welding line, a factory site, engineers and the risk-taking of its founder.
Identify the factor of production that each of these represents. **[4]**

**5.** Explain why a positive statement is not necessarily a true statement. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why clean rivers are an example of a scarce resource. **[2]**

### Section B

**7.** Read the original extract.

*"Northbay's council spent 4 million on a new cycle lane network. In the first year, cycle journeys rose by 18% and
recorded accidents involving cyclists fell. Shop owners on the main road say that removing parking spaces was a
mistake and that the council should have consulted them more. Some residents argue that the money would have been
better spent on repairing roads."*

**(a)** Identify **one** positive statement in the extract and explain why it is positive. **[2]**

**(b)** Identify **one** normative statement in the extract and explain why it is normative. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain how value judgements could lead two people who accept the same data to disagree about the cycle
lanes. **[3]**

**8.** A furniture workshop can produce either 50 desks or 200 chairs a week, or any combination on a straight line
between these.

**(a)** Calculate the opportunity cost of one desk in terms of chairs. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the opportunity cost of increasing desk output from 20 to 30. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain what the straight-line boundary tells you about the workshop's resources. **[2]**

**9.** Using a production possibility diagram, explain the effect of a temporary closure of factories during a
power shortage. **[3]**

**10.** Using a production possibility diagram, explain how a new technology that raises output of computers, but
not of food, affects an economy's production possibilities. **[4]**

**11.** Explain why all points on a production possibility boundary are productively efficient but not all are
allocatively efficient. **[4]**

**12.** Northbay's council has 4 million to spend and is choosing between more cycle lanes and repairing roads.
Assess how far economic analysis alone can decide which option is best. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** The **next best alternative forgone** when a choice is made [1].

**2.** **What** to produce [1]; **how** to produce it [1]; **who** is to benefit from the goods and services produced
[1]. **[3]**

**3.** Ceteris paribus means "**all other things being equal**" [1]: economists assume other influences stay the same
so that the effect of one change can be isolated [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Robotic welding line: **capital** [1]. Factory site: **land** [1]. Engineers: **labour** [1]. Founder's
risk-taking: **enterprise** [1]. **[4]**

**5.** A positive statement is one that **can be tested against evidence** [1]; the evidence may show it to be false,
but it remains positive because it can be checked [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Clean rivers are **limited** relative to the demands placed on them for drinking water, farming, industry and
leisure [1]. Using a river to carry away waste reduces its availability for other uses, so there is an
**opportunity cost** [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** For example, "cycle journeys rose by 18%" [1], which is positive because it can be **checked against
traffic count data** [1]. (Also accept: "recorded accidents involving cyclists fell"; "the council spent 4 million".)

**(b)** For example, "the council should have consulted them more" [1], which is normative because "should" expresses
a **value judgement** that evidence cannot settle [1]. (Also accept: "removing parking spaces was a mistake"; "the
money would have been better spent on repairing roads".)

**(c)** Both may accept that journeys rose by 18% and accidents fell (positive evidence) [1]. One may place more value
on **health, safety and cleaner air** [1], while the other places more value on **shop owners' trade or the
condition of roads** [1]; so they reach different conclusions from the same facts.

**8. (a)** 200 ÷ 50 = **4 chairs** per desk [1].

**(b)** 10 extra desks × 4 = **40 chairs** [1].

**(c)** The opportunity cost is **constant** [1], which means the workshop's resources are **equally suited** to
making desks and chairs [1].

**9.** Diagram with two goods on the axes and a production possibility boundary [1]. Output moves from a point on the
boundary to a point **inside** it [1], because factories and workers are idle; the **boundary does not shift**,
since productive capacity is unchanged and output can return once power is restored [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Diagram with computers on one axis and food on the other [1]. The boundary **pivots outwards along the computer
axis**; the food intercept stays the same [1]. The economy can make more computers for any given amount of food [1],
and because fewer resources are needed for a given computer output, some can move to food, so more of both may be
produced [1]. **[4]**

**11.** On the boundary all resources are fully and efficiently used [1], so more of one good can be produced only by
producing less of the other: this is productive efficiency [1]. Allocative efficiency requires producing the
**combination of goods that society most wants** [1]; many boundary points have the wrong mix, for example producing
almost no food when people need it [1]. **[4]**

**12.** *What economic analysis can do:* estimate the costs and benefits of each option, such as journey times,
accidents avoided and vehicle repair costs [1]; identify who gains and who loses [1]; measure the opportunity cost of
each choice [1]; use evidence from the first year of cycle lanes to predict future effects [1]. *Limits:* future
effects are uncertain, because ceteris paribus rarely holds [1]; some benefits, such as health or a pleasant
street, are hard to value [1]; the final choice depends on **value judgements**, such as how much weight to give
cyclists compared with drivers and shop owners [1]. *Judgement:* economic analysis can narrow the choice and show
the trade-offs, but it cannot alone decide which is best, because that depends on normative judgements [2]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Quoting a statement without giving a reason for classifying it.
- Treating money or a loan as capital.
- Giving all alternatives as the opportunity cost.
- Dividing the wrong way round in opportunity cost calculations.
- Showing a temporary closure as an inward shift of the boundary.
- Ending an "assess" question without a judgement.

## Frequently asked questions

**How should I answer "identify a normative statement" questions?**
Quote the exact words from the extract, then give the reason, usually a value word such as "should", "mistake" or
"better", that shows evidence alone cannot settle it.

**Does AQA AS Economics require the same 3.1.1 content as the A-level?**
Yes. The five parts of 3.1.1 are worded the same in the AS (7135) and A-level (7136) specifications.
