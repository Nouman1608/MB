## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between a cost centre and a cost unit, giving an example of each for a hotel. **[2]**

**2.** Fordell Garage repairs a van (Job 88). Parts cost 340 and a mechanic works 6 hours at 28.00 an hour. Overheads are absorbed at 20.00 per direct labour hour. The garage adds a mark-up of 25%. Calculate the price of the job. **[3]**

**3.** A department's OAR is 14.00 per machine hour, based on 5,000 budgeted hours. It actually worked 5,300 hours and spent 72,800 on overheads. Calculate the under- or over-absorption and state its effect on profit. **[3]**

**4.** A product sells for 80.00 with a variable cost of 50.00. Fixed costs are 117,000. Calculate the break-even point in units, the break-even revenue and the C/S ratio. **[3]**

**5.** Explain why a marginal costing profit statement never needs an adjustment for under- or over-absorbed overheads. **[2]**

**6.** State **two** limitations of break-even analysis. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Lowther Plastics has two production departments, Moulding and Finishing, and one service department, Stores.

| Overhead | Total | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Rent | 24,000 | Floor area: Moulding 3,000 m², Finishing 2,000 m², Stores 1,000 m² |
| Machine depreciation | 18,000 | Machine value: Moulding 60,000, Finishing 25,000, Stores 5,000 |
| Indirect labour | 29,500 | Allocated: Moulding 9,500, Finishing 14,000, Stores 6,000 |

Stores costs are reapportioned 70% to Moulding and 30% to Finishing, in line with requisitions. Moulding is machine-intensive, with 10,300 budgeted machine hours; Finishing is labour-intensive, with 6,060 budgeted direct labour hours.

**(a)** Prepare an overhead analysis showing the total overheads of each production department. **[6]**
**(b)** Calculate an appropriate OAR for each production department. **[2]**
**(c)** A job needs 3 machine hours in Moulding and 2 labour hours in Finishing. Calculate the overheads absorbed by the job. **[2]**

**8.** Tarnside Bags sells bags at 40.00. Variable production cost is 16.00 a bag. Fixed production overheads are 60,000 a month, absorbed on normal output of 5,000 bags. Fixed administration costs are 25,000 a month. In March it made 5,000 bags and sold 4,400. There was no opening inventory.

**(a)** Prepare a profit statement for March using absorption costing. **[4]**
**(b)** Prepare a profit statement for March using marginal costing. **[4]**
**(c)** Prepare a statement reconciling the two profits. **[3]**

**9.** Selwood Lamps sells a lamp at 50.00 with variable cost 32.00. Fixed costs are 162,000 a year and budgeted sales are 12,000 lamps.

**(a)** Calculate the break-even point in units and the margin of safety as a percentage of budgeted sales. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the sales needed for a profit of 72,000. **[2]**
**(c)** The manager proposes raising the price to 54.00, expecting sales to fall to 10,500 lamps. Calculate the profit and break-even point under the proposal. **[3]**
**(d)** Evaluate the proposal. **[4]**

**10.** Ashby Valves makes 20,000 units a year of component K. Its cost per unit is direct materials 6.20, direct labour 4.80, variable overhead 1.50 and fixed overhead absorbed 3.50. Of the fixed overhead, 1.00 a unit is a supervisor's salary that would be saved if K were bought in; the rest would continue. A supplier offers K at 13.20 a unit.

**(a)** Calculate the annual financial effect of buying K instead of making it. **[4]**
**(b)** Advise Ashby whether to accept the supplier's offer. **[5]**

**11.** Ottery Mills can make 50,000 units a year and sells 44,000 at 18.00 each. Variable cost is 11.00 a unit and fixed costs are 210,000. A new customer offers to buy 9,000 units at 14.00, with extra delivery costs of 4,500 in total. The order must be accepted in full or not at all.

**(a)** Calculate the effect of the order on annual profit. **[4]**
**(b)** Discuss **two** non-financial factors Ottery should consider. **[4]**

**12.** Brookmere Hotel's spa has revenue of 96,000, variable costs of 58,000 and apportioned fixed costs of 46,000, giving a loss of 8,000. If the spa closed, 21,000 of its fixed costs would be saved.

**(a)** Calculate the effect on the hotel's profit of closing the spa. **[3]**
**(b)** A local business offers to rent the spa space for 20,000 a year if it closes. Recalculate the effect of closure. **[2]**
**(c)** Advise the hotel, including non-financial factors. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** A cost centre is a location, function or item of equipment for which costs are collected, such as the hotel kitchen [1]. A cost unit is the unit of output to which costs are charged, such as one room-night [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Cost = 340 + (6 × 28.00) 168 + (6 × 20.00) 120 = 628 [1]. Mark-up 628 × 25% = 157 [1]. Price **785** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Labour hours drive both the direct labour cost and the overheads absorbed here.

**3.** Absorbed = 5,300 × 14.00 = 74,200 [1]. Actual is 72,800, so overheads are **over-absorbed by 1,400** [1]. The 1,400 is deducted from cost of sales, increasing profit [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Absorbed overhead uses actual hours; the 5,000 budgeted hours were only for setting the rate.

**4.** Contribution 30.00; break-even = 117,000 ÷ 30 = **3,900 units** [1]. Revenue 3,900 × 80 = **312,000** [1]. C/S ratio 30 ÷ 80 = **37.5%** [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Marginal costing does not absorb fixed overheads into units [1]; the actual fixed overhead is charged in full as a period cost, so no difference between absorbed and actual can arise [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Any two: it assumes the selling price per unit is constant [1]; it assumes fixed costs stay fixed at all levels of output, or that variable cost per unit is constant, or that all output is sold, or a single product [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)**

| | Total | Moulding | Finishing | Stores |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rent (3:2:1) | 24,000 | 12,000 | 8,000 | 4,000 |
| Machine depreciation (60:25:5) | 18,000 | 12,000 | 5,000 | 1,000 |
| Indirect labour | 29,500 | 9,500 | 14,000 | 6,000 |
| Subtotal | 71,500 | 33,500 | 27,000 | 11,000 |
| Stores (70:30) | | 7,700 | 3,300 | (11,000) |
| **Total** | **71,500** | **41,200** | **30,300** | |

Rent apportioned [1]; depreciation apportioned [1]; indirect labour allocated [1]; subtotals [1]; stores reapportioned [1]; department totals [1].
**(b)** Moulding 41,200 ÷ 10,300 = **4.00 per machine hour** [1]. Finishing 30,300 ÷ 6,060 = **5.00 per direct labour hour** [1].
**(c)** (3 × 4.00) + (2 × 5.00) [1] = **22.00** [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the basis each department's activity suggests; using labour hours for Moulding would not earn the (b) marks.

**8. (a)** Fixed overhead per bag = 60,000 ÷ 5,000 = 12.00, so full production cost 28.00 [1]. Sales 4,400 × 40 = 176,000 [1]; cost of sales 4,400 × 28 = 123,200, gross profit 52,800 [1]; less administration 25,000, profit **27,800** [1].
**(b)** Sales 176,000; variable cost of sales 4,400 × 16 = 70,400 [1]; contribution **105,600** [1]; fixed costs 60,000 + 25,000 = 85,000 [1]; profit **20,600** [1].
**(c)** Marginal costing profit 20,600 [1]; add fixed overhead in closing inventory 600 × 12 = 7,200 [1]; absorption costing profit **27,800** [1]. **[11]**
*Examiner insight:* Output equals normal output, so there is no under- or over-absorption to adjust.

**9. (a)** Contribution 18.00; break-even = 162,000 ÷ 18 = **9,000 lamps** [1]. Margin of safety 12,000 − 9,000 = 3,000 [1], which is **25%** [1].
**(b)** (162,000 + 72,000) ÷ 18 [1] = **13,000 lamps** [1].
**(c)** New contribution 22.00; profit = 10,500 × 22 − 162,000 = **69,000** [1], compared with 54,000 now [1]. Break-even 162,000 ÷ 22 = 7,363.6, so **7,364 lamps** [1].
**(d)** For: profit rises by 15,000 and the break-even point falls, so the business is less risky [1]; the margin of safety rises to 3,136 lamps [1]. Against: the forecast fall to 10,500 is an estimate; if sales fell below 9,819, profit would be lower than now [1]. Judgement: adopt if market research supports the sales forecast and competitors' prices allow it [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* A sensitivity figure, such as the sales needed to match current profit (54,000 + 162,000) ÷ 22 = 9,818, makes an evaluation stronger.

**10. (a)** Relevant cost of making = 6.20 + 4.80 + 1.50 + 1.00 = 13.50 [1]; the other 2.50 of fixed overhead continues either way and is not relevant [1]. Buying costs 13.20, saving 0.30 a unit [1]. Annual saving 0.30 × 20,000 = **6,000** [1].
**(b)** Financially, buying saves 6,000 a year [1]. But the saving is small, about 2% of the cost of making [1]. Ashby would depend on the supplier's reliability and quality for a component it needs [1], could lose in-house skills, and the supplier may raise its price later [1]. Advice: buy only if the supplier can guarantee quality and delivery, perhaps through a long-term contract; otherwise keep making K [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Comparing the full cost of 16.00 with 13.20 overstates the saving; unavoidable fixed costs are not relevant.

**11. (a)** Order contribution = 9,000 × (14.00 − 11.00) − 4,500 = 22,500 [1]. Spare capacity is 6,000 units, so 3,000 normal sales are lost [1]. Lost contribution 3,000 × 7.00 = 21,000 [1]. Profit rises by **1,500** [1].
**(b)** Existing customers paying 18.00 may learn of the 14.00 price [1] and demand a cut, which would cost far more than the 1,500 gained [1]. Customers who lose 3,000 units of supply may move to a competitor [1] and not return, reducing future contribution [1]. The new customer's reliability and the chance of repeat orders are also creditworthy factors. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* At full capacity a special order must cover the contribution it displaces, not just its own variable cost.

**12. (a)** Contribution lost 96,000 − 58,000 = 38,000 [1]; fixed costs saved 21,000 [1]; profit falls by **17,000** [1].
**(b)** Savings 21,000 + rent 20,000 = 41,000, against 38,000 lost [1]: profit rises by **3,000** [1].
**(c)** The rental makes closure slightly better financially, by 3,000 [1]. But guests may choose the hotel because of its spa, so room and restaurant bookings could fall by more than 3,000 [1]; spa staff would lose their jobs, affecting morale and reputation [1]. Advice: keep the spa unless the hotel is confident that room demand does not depend on it [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating the OAR from actual instead of budgeted figures.
- Leaving out the under- or over-absorption adjustment when output differs from normal.
- Using full cost, including unavoidable fixed overheads, in make-or-buy or closure decisions.
- Forgetting the contribution lost on normal sales when a special order exceeds spare capacity.
- Rounding break-even units down.
- Giving non-financial factors as single words with no effect explained.
