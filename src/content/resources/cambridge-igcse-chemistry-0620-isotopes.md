## The definition

Isotopes are **different atoms of the same element that have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons**.

Because the number of protons is the same, the proton number is the same, so the atoms are the same element. Because the number of neutrons differs, the mass number differs. In an exam, write both halves: "same number of protons" **and** "different numbers of neutrons".

Hydrogen shows the idea clearly. It has three isotopes:

| Isotope | Symbol | Protons | Neutrons | Electrons |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hydrogen-1 | ¹₁H | 1 | 0 | 1 |
| Hydrogen-2 (deuterium) | ²₁H | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| Hydrogen-3 (tritium) | ³₁H | 1 | 2 | 1 |

Hydrogen-1 is the only atom with no neutrons at all.

## Reading symbols for atoms and ions

In a symbol such as ³⁹₁₉K:

- the **top** number, A, is the mass number (protons + neutrons)
- the **bottom** number, Z, is the proton number
- a **charge** at the top right shows an ion.

The name of an isotope is often written with its mass number, such as potassium-39 or K-39.

### Worked example 1: using symbols

| Symbol | Protons | Neutrons | Electrons | Atom or ion? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ³⁹₁₉K | 19 | 20 | 19 | Atom |
| ⁴¹₁₉K | 19 | 22 | 19 | Atom |
| ³⁹₁₉K⁺ | 19 | 20 | 18 | Positive ion |
| ⁸¹₃₅Br⁻ | 35 | 46 | 36 | Negative ion |

The bromine example uses an element beyond proton number 20. You can still count its particles from the symbol, even though you do not need to write its electronic configuration.

### Worked example 2: isotopes or not?

| Pair | Same protons? | Same neutrons? | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| ³⁹₁₉K and ⁴¹₁₉K | Yes (19) | No (20 and 22) | Isotopes |
| ³⁹₁₉K and ³⁹₁₉K⁺ | Yes | Yes | Not isotopes: an atom and its ion differ only in electrons |
| ¹⁴₆C and ¹⁴₇N | No (6 and 7) | No (8 and 7) | Not isotopes: different elements with the same mass number |
| ⁶₃Li and ⁷₃Li | Yes (3) | No (3 and 4) | Isotopes |

The quick test: same **bottom** number and different **top** number means isotopes. Charges are irrelevant to the test, so an ion such as ³⁷₁₇Cl⁻ is an ion of an isotope, chlorine-37.

## Same chemical properties (Extended only)

Isotopes of an element have the **same number of electrons** (in their atoms), so they have the **same electronic configuration**. Chemical reactions involve the electrons, especially those in the outer shell, so isotopes react in the same way.

For example, lithium-6 and lithium-7 both have three electrons, arranged 2,1. Both react with water to form lithium hydroxide and hydrogen, in the same way.

A full-mark answer has a chain of three links:

1. same number of electrons
2. so the same electronic configuration (same outer-shell electrons)
3. so the same chemical properties.

Answers that stop at "same number of protons" miss the point: the protons explain why they are the same element, not why they react the same way.

## Relative atomic mass (Extended only)

The relative atomic mass, Aᵣ, of an element is the **average** mass of its atoms, taking into account how common each isotope is (its abundance).

Aᵣ = (sum of mass × abundance for each isotope) ÷ (total abundance)

When abundances are percentages, the total abundance is 100. When they are numbers of atoms, divide by the total number of atoms.

### Worked example 3: lithium, two isotopes

Lithium is approximately 7.5% lithium-6 and 92.5% lithium-7.

```
Aᵣ = (6 × 7.5 + 7 × 92.5) ÷ 100
   = (45 + 647.5) ÷ 100
   = 692.5 ÷ 100
   = 6.925 = 6.9 (1 d.p.)
```

Check: the answer is between 6 and 7 and much closer to 7, because lithium-7 is far more common.

### Worked example 4: neon, three isotopes

Neon is approximately 90.5% neon-20, 0.3% neon-21 and 9.2% neon-22.

```
check: 90.5 + 0.3 + 9.2 = 100.0
Aᵣ = (20 × 90.5 + 21 × 0.3 + 22 × 9.2) ÷ 100
   = (1810 + 6.3 + 202.4) ÷ 100
   = 2018.7 ÷ 100
   = 20.187 = 20.2 (1 d.p.)
```

### Worked example 5: abundance as numbers of atoms

In a sample of 1000 silicon atoms, 922 are silicon-28, 47 are silicon-29 and 31 are silicon-30.

```
total atoms = 922 + 47 + 31 = 1000
Aᵣ = (28 × 922 + 29 × 47 + 30 × 31) ÷ 1000
   = (25 816 + 1363 + 930) ÷ 1000
   = 28 109 ÷ 1000
   = 28.109 = 28.1 (1 d.p.)
```

If the data were given as a ratio, for example "three atoms of isotope A for every one atom of isotope B", treat the ratio numbers as abundances and divide by their total, 3 + 1 = 4.

### Worked example 6: two isotopes in almost equal amounts

Bromine is approximately 50.7% bromine-79 and 49.3% bromine-81.

```
Aᵣ = (79 × 50.7 + 81 × 49.3) ÷ 100
   = (4005.3 + 3993.3) ÷ 100
   = 7998.6 ÷ 100
   = 79.986 = 80.0 (1 d.p.)
```

Check: with nearly equal amounts, Aᵣ should be close to the midpoint of 79 and 81, which is 80. It is.

## Common mistakes

- Defining isotopes as "atoms with different mass numbers" without saying "same element" or "same number of protons".
- Writing "different number of electrons". Isotopic atoms have the same number of electrons.
- Treating an atom and its ion as isotopes.
- Mixing up the top and bottom numbers in a symbol.
- Taking a simple average of the isotope masses and ignoring abundance.
- Dividing by 100 when the abundances are numbers of atoms that do not total 100.
- Explaining identical chemical properties by "same number of protons".

## Exam technique

- **Define**: use the full wording: different atoms of the same element, same number of protons, different numbers of neutrons.
- **State** the reason for identical chemistry: electrons → configuration → chemical properties.
- **Calculate**: write the expression with every isotope, show the total, divide, then round to the precision asked. Check that your answer lies between the lightest and heaviest isotope masses and nearer the most abundant.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is the relative atomic mass of an element often not a whole number?**
It is an average of the masses of its isotopes, weighted by abundance, so it usually falls between whole numbers.

**Do isotopes have the same number of electrons?**
Yes, as neutral atoms. They have the same number of protons, and in an atom electrons equal protons.
