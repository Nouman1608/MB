**Insurance** is an arrangement in which a business or person pays a regular sum of money, called a **premium**, to an insurance company in return for a promise to pay money if a specified loss happens. Sub-topic 5.3 of Cambridge O Level Commerce (7100) has three parts: the **purposes of insurance** (5.3.1), the **types of insurance risk** (5.3.2) and **how enterprises make use of insurance** (5.3.3).

Key words: the **insurer** is the company that provides the cover; the **insured** (policyholder) is the person or business covered; the **premium** is the payment; a **claim** is a request for payment after a loss.

## 5.3.1 Purposes of insurance

| Purpose | Explanation | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Risk reduction** | Insurance spreads the cost of a possible loss so that no single business bears all of it. Insurers also encourage safety measures | A discount for fitting a fire alarm |
| **Compensation** | The insurer pays the insured for the loss, so the business can recover | Payment to repair a warehouse after a fire |
| **Financial protection** | A large unexpected loss does not destroy the business | A shop survives a burglary because the stolen stock is paid for |
| **Business confidence** | The owner can run the business and take sensible risks, knowing that disasters are covered; lenders and customers also feel safer | A bank is more willing to lend to an insured business |
| **Investment** | Some policies, such as life policies, build up a sum of money that is paid out later, and insurers invest premiums | A policy that pays a lump sum on retirement |

## 5.3.2 Types of insurance risk

| Insurance | What it covers | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Consequential loss** | Loss of profit and extra costs after an event such as a fire shuts the business | A bakery loses a month's income while the oven is repaired |
| **Employer's liability** | Compensation to employees injured or made ill because of their work | A worker injured by a faulty machine |
| **Fire** | Damage to buildings, stock and equipment by fire | A fire destroys a warehouse |
| **Motor** | Damage to vehicles and injury or damage caused to others by them | A delivery van crash |
| **Premises** | Damage to buildings and their contents, for example from storm or flood | A flood damages a shop |
| **Public liability** | Compensation to members of the public injured or whose property is damaged because of the business | A customer slips on a wet floor |
| **Theft** | Loss of goods or money by theft or burglary | Stolen stock from a shop |

### Which cover for which risk?

| Situation | Insurance |
|---|---|
| A customer is hurt in a shop's showroom | Public liability |
| A warehouse is damaged by fire | Fire |
| A factory worker is injured at a machine | Employer's liability |
| Profits are lost while a restaurant is closed for repairs | Consequential loss |
| A delivery van is stolen | Motor (and theft) |
| Stock is stolen after a break-in | Theft |

Some risks cannot be insured, such as normal business losses from poor decisions or falling demand. Insurance works only for chance events, not for events certain to happen.

## 5.3.3 How enterprises make use of insurance

### Pooling of risk

**Pooling** means that many people or businesses pay premiums into a common fund. Only a few will suffer a loss in any year, and claims are paid from the fund.

*Worked example.* An insurer covers 1,000 shops. Each pays a $300 premium.

1. Total premiums = 1,000 × 300 = **$300,000**.
2. In a year the insurer expects 12 shops to make claims averaging $20,000.
3. Expected claims = 12 × 20,000 = **$240,000**.
4. Left for running costs and profit = 300,000 − 240,000 = **$60,000**.

No single shop could afford a $20,000 loss without help, but by sharing the cost the 1,000 shops each pay only $300. The law of large numbers helps the insurer predict how many claims will occur.

### Calculation of premiums

Premiums depend on the **risk**: the more likely the loss and the higher the value, the higher the premium. Factors include the value of the property, the type of business, the location, the claims history and safety measures.

A simple method is **premium = sum insured × rate**.

*Worked example.* A shop's stock is valued at $80,000. The fire insurance rate is 0.5%.

Premium = 0.5% × 80,000 = $400 per year.

The insurer then adjusts the premium:

- **No-claims discount** for years without claims. If the shop gets a 20% discount, the discount is 20% × 400 = $80, so it pays $320.
- **Loading** (extra charge) for higher risk. If the shop is in a flood zone and a 10% loading is added to the $400, the premium becomes $440.
- **Excess**: an amount the insured pays on each claim. With a $100 excess, a $1,500 loss gets $1,400 from the insurer.

### Insurance documents

| Document | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Proposal form** | The form completed by the person wanting insurance, giving full details of the risk; the insurer uses it to decide whether to accept and what premium to charge. The answers must be honest |
| **Cover note** | A temporary document that proves the insured is covered until the full policy is issued |
| **Policy** | The formal contract that sets out what is covered, the premium, the sum insured, exclusions and conditions |

The usual order is: proposal form, then cover note, then policy. If the proposal form contains false information, the insurer may refuse to pay a claim.

### Role of an insurance broker

A **broker** is an independent agent who arranges insurance between the customer and an insurer. A broker:

- compares policies from many insurers to find suitable cover at a fair price;
- gives expert advice on the type and amount of cover needed;
- helps complete forms and make claims;
- is usually paid commission by the insurer.

Benefits: saves the customer time and gives access to many insurers. Drawback: the broker's commission may add to cost, and the broker may favour insurers that pay more.

## Worked example: insuring a new café

*Invented example.* Maya opens a café with a rented building, a kitchen, three employees and a delivery scooter.

| Risk | Cover Maya should consider | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Fire in the kitchen | Fire insurance | Pays for repairs and replacement equipment |
| Customers slipping on wet floors | Public liability | Pays compensation to the public |
| A kitchen worker burning an arm | Employer's liability | Pays compensation to employees |
| Break-in at night | Theft | Replaces stolen equipment and cash |
| Closure for repairs after a flood | Premises and consequential loss | Repairs the building and replaces lost profit |
| Scooter accident | Motor | Covers damage and injury to others |

Maya fills in a **proposal form** with honest details about the building, the fire alarms and the number of staff. The insurer gives a **cover note** straight away so she is protected from the first day. The full **policy** arrives a week later. If she cannot decide between insurers, a **broker** can compare them for her and may find a cheaper package.

Honesty matters. If Maya hides the fact that the kitchen has no fire alarm, the insurer may refuse a later claim, so all of the premiums paid would be wasted.

## Annotated model answer

**Question:** A furniture shop has been closed for two months after a fire. Explain **two** types of insurance that would help the owner. [4]

> Fire insurance would pay for the repair of the building and the cost of replacing damaged furniture [1], so the owner can reopen without paying for all the damage [1]. Consequential loss insurance would pay for the profit lost while the shop was closed and extra costs such as renting temporary premises [1], so the owner can pay wages and bills during the closure [1].

**Why it scores 4:** each type is named correctly and linked to the shop's situation.

## Common mistakes

- Saying insurance **prevents** a loss. It pays after the loss.
- Confusing employer's liability (employees) with public liability (public).
- Saying a cover note is the full policy.
- Believing every risk can be insured.
- Forgetting to subtract the discount, or adding it.
- Calling the broker an employee of the insurer. A broker is an independent agent.

## Exam technique

- **Name** the type, then link it to the scenario.
- **Show the formula** in premium sums, then the answer in dollars.
- **Order** the documents proposal form, cover note, policy.
