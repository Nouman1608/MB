Human relationships is the third of the four contexts in the IB Psychology course for first assessment 2027. It is studied by every SL and HL student. A **context** is the real-world setting in which you apply the content (the biological, cognitive and sociocultural approaches and research methodology) and the six concepts (bias, causality, change, measurement, perspective and responsibility). This page maps the whole context. Two detailed pages go deeper: **group behaviour** and **interpersonal relationships**. Each has its own study guide, revision notes and practice set.

Related: [group behaviour study guide](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-human-relationships-group-behaviour/) and [interpersonal relationships study guide](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-human-relationships-interpersonal-relationships/).

## What the guide says the context is about

The guide opens with a simple claim: people are social and interdependent. We influence others and are influenced by them in friendships, romantic partnerships, family relationships and work relationships. It then splits the context into two aspects.

**Group behaviour.** Here the guide names a set of ideas that explain how a person becomes part of a group and how groups shape behaviour.

- **Social learning and acculturation** explain how a person gradually becomes part of a group by learning acceptable behaviours and cultural norms.
- **Social identity theory** explains why belonging matters so much and why people favour their own group over others.
- **Cultural dimensions** describe cross-cultural variation in behaviour.
- **Conformity** and **compliance techniques** are specific types of social influence.

**Interpersonal relationships.** Here the guide asks how relationships develop and what influences them. It lists purely cognitive models, genetic factors, chemical messengers and communication or language. The practical payoff is stated directly: if we understand how relationships develop and what they depend on, we can design strategies for improving them.

The guide's sub-headings ("Group behaviour" and "Interpersonal relationships") are for convenience. They are not used to form examination questions. A question will therefore draw on the content terms and the concepts, and will name a context, rather than saying "this is a group behaviour question".

## The areas of study you must cover

For group behaviour, the guide lists six areas with learning objectives.

| Area of study | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| Acculturation | Describe different acculturation strategies and their effect on behaviour and mental health |
| Compliance techniques | Apply one or more compliance techniques to change group behaviour |
| Conformity | Explain the role of conformity in understanding group behaviour |
| Cultural dimensions | Explain the role of one or more cultural dimensions in understanding group behaviour |
| Social identity theory | Apply the theory to explain and change group behaviour |
| Social learning | Apply social learning theory to change group behaviour |

For interpersonal relationships, the guide says to study **one or more of social relationships or interpersonal conflict and aggression**. That choice is yours (or your teacher's). The four areas are:

| Area of study | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| Chemical messengers | Explain the role of one or more chemical messengers in interpersonal relationships |
| Cognitive explanations | Give one or more cognitive explanations for interpersonal relationships |
| Communication and language | Explain the role of communication in interpersonal relationships |
| Strategies for improving relationships | Describe one or more strategies for improving interpersonal relationships |

Notice the verbs. "Apply", "explain" and "the role of" all ask for more than a definition. You are expected to say how an idea works and what it does to behaviour.

## How the context connects to the content terms

The context is where the content terms from the sociocultural approach are used. Social identity theory, conformity, compliance techniques, cultural dimensions, models of acculturation, enculturation and social learning theory all appear in the guide's list of sociocultural terms. The biological approach provides chemical messengers and genetic inheritance, which feed the interpersonal strand. The cognitive approach provides cognitive biases and schema theory, which are useful for cognitive explanations of how we judge other people. Revise the context and the content together: the same piece of evidence often serves both.

The HL extensions also reach into this context. The guide's motivation table lists the role of motivation in influencing group behaviours, motivational strategies to change individual behaviour and the role of motivators in employee satisfaction. Its technology table lists the impact of technology on interpersonal relationships, the role of social media in group behaviour and the use of technology to assist acculturation into a new society. HL students meet these in Paper 3; SL students can still use them as examples of change and perspective.

## The class practical: a survey or questionnaire

Every context has a class practical, and in this one the method is a **survey or questionnaire**. The guide offers two suggestions: the love quiz of Hazan and Shaver (1987) and the cross-cultural attraction study of Buss (1989). These are examples only; your teacher may choose another survey as long as it relates to human relationships.

Paper 2 Section A asks four compulsory questions about the class practicals, and the guide stresses that the focus is on the research method itself, not on the context in which you ran it. So for this context, be ready to explain how a survey works, why closed and open questions produce different data, how to sample, which biases threaten a self-report survey (social desirability and response bias) and what ethical steps you took. The class practicals study guide on this site covers the method in more detail.

## How the context is assessed

The guide describes the papers as follows. Treat these as the official structure and do not rely on remembered patterns from earlier courses.

- **Paper 1** integrates concepts, content and contexts and is worth 35 marks.
  - **Section A** has two compulsory short-answer questions on content from two of the three approaches (8 marks). The guide says a context will not be specified here, because content can be taught in any context.
  - **Section B** has two compulsory questions that ask you to apply your knowledge of content to an unseen situation, each based on one of the four contexts (12 marks). A human relationships scenario might describe a new student joining a school, a team at work or a couple in conflict.
  - **Section C** gives two concept-based extended response questions, each from a different context, and you answer one (15 marks). The focus is conceptual understanding and critical thinking, and knowledge of the details of specific studies is not assessed.
- **Paper 2** applies concepts and content to research contexts and is also worth 35 marks. Section A is four compulsory questions on the class practicals. Section B asks you to evaluate an unseen research study with regard to two or more concepts. The study aligns with one of the four contexts, and could be a human relationships study.
- **Paper 3** is HL only and uses the HL extensions (culture, motivation, technology) in source-based questions, so a human relationships example is possible there too.

The guide also gives the weightings of each paper and the internal assessment, which are the same for every context. Check the assessment section of the guide or your teacher's materials for the full table.

## How to approach the extended response (Paper 1 Section C)

The 15-mark markband rewards five things: understanding of the demands of the question, explained knowledge of content and concepts, critical analysis, links between concepts and a reasoned conclusion. The top band expects links between concepts throughout the response, precise terminology and a clearly stated, consistent conclusion.

A reliable plan for a human relationships question is as follows.

1. **Decode the question.** Underline the command word (for example "evaluate" or "discuss") and the named concepts. Note which of group behaviour or interpersonal relationships it leans towards.
2. **Choose three to four concepts.** The guide recommends three to four relevant concepts for a context. If the question names two concepts, use those and add one or two more only where they sharpen the argument.
3. **Pick evidence that does work.** Choose content terms and examples that let you make a claim, not just describe. You do not need exact statistics, because details of studies are not assessed in this section. You do need to say what the evidence shows and how strong it is.
4. **Argue in paragraphs, each with a claim, evidence, evaluation and concept link.** For example: "Conformity can change group behaviour (claim). Asch-type experiments show people go along with a clear majority even when it is wrong (evidence). The task was artificial and the sample narrow, so ecological validity is limited (evaluation). This is a measurement problem, because a line-judging task may not measure conformity in real friendship groups (concept link)."
5. **Finish with a conclusion that weighs the evidence.** A conclusion that only repeats the points will not reach the top band. Say which factor, explanation or strategy is most convincing, and under which conditions.

### Worked example: a concept-based plan

Suppose the question is: **"Evaluate the use of psychological research to change behaviour in groups. Refer to at least two concepts."** (written for practice, not a past question.)

- **Concept 1, change.** Strategies: social identity theory applied through shared goals (superordinate goals reduce intergroup hostility), compliance techniques (foot-in-the-door can raise participation) and social learning (role models). Evaluate: does change last, and is it ethical to use techniques that work partly outside awareness?
- **Concept 2, causality.** Experiments on conformity and compliance support cause and effect, but groups in real life involve many interacting variables, so field evidence is correlational. Bidirectional ambiguity matters: do people join a group because of shared attitudes, or does the group shape the attitudes?
- **Concept 3, responsibility.** Compliance techniques can be used to manipulate people. Researchers who show that a technique works have a duty to explain the risks. Stigma is a risk for groups studied in acculturation research.
- **Concept 4, perspective or bias.** Most conformity and attraction research has used Western student samples. An emic approach could give a more valid view of how a group works in a specific culture.
- **Conclusion.** For example: research gives strong evidence that group behaviour can be changed, with the most convincing strategies being those that change identity and goals rather than only instructions, but the evidence is weaker on long-term change outside controlled settings.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the two aspects as separate subjects. Many real questions need both (for example how culture and chemical messengers combine to shape attraction).
- Writing about "relationships" in general terms without naming a content term or area of study.
- Retelling a famous study at length in Section C, where study detail is not assessed, instead of using it as evidence in an argument.
- Using only one concept, or listing concepts without linking them to the argument.
- Ignoring the survey practical when preparing for Paper 2.
- Forgetting evaluation: sampling, culture, ethics, ecological validity and the direction of causality.

## Revision checklist

- Can you define each of the ten content terms for this context and give an original example?
- Can you give one strength and one limitation for conformity, compliance, social identity theory and social learning?
- Can you describe at least two acculturation strategies and say how each can affect mental health?
- Can you explain one chemical messenger, one cognitive explanation, the role of communication and one improvement strategy?
- Can you describe the survey method with its sampling, question design and ethical steps?
- Have you planned three Section C responses with three to four concepts each?
