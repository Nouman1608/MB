This guide covers subtopic **8.5 Noble gases** in Topic 8, The Periodic Table, of Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620, 2026–2028 series. The [Periodic Table groups and trends study guide](/resources/periodic-table-groups-and-trends/) gives a one-paragraph summary. This page unpacks the single syllabus statement into the separate ideas examiners credit, links the noble-gas configuration to ionic and covalent bonding, and shows how to handle data questions about the group. Afterwards, use the [noble gases revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-noble-gases-revision-notes/) and the [noble gases practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-noble-gases-practice/).

## What the syllabus asks for

This subtopic is **Core only**. The whole requirement is one statement:

> Describe the Group VIII noble gases as unreactive, monatomic gases and explain this in terms of electronic configuration.

It contains three descriptions (**unreactive**, **monatomic**, **gases**) and one explanation (**electronic configuration**). Questions often split these into separate marks, so learn each one.

Two related statements from Topic 2 are also needed here: Group VIII noble gases have a **full outer electron shell** (2.2), and covalent bonds and ions form so that atoms reach **noble gas electronic configurations** (2.4 and 2.5).

## The members of the group

The noble gases are helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon and radon. They are in the final column of the Periodic Table, which the syllabus calls **Group VIII**. (Other books may call it Group 0 or Group 18; in a 0620 answer, use Group VIII.)

Within proton numbers 1 to 20 you need three configurations:

| Noble gas | Symbol | Proton number | Electronic configuration | Period |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Helium | He | 2 | 2 | 1 |
| Neon | Ne | 10 | 2,8 | 2 |
| Argon | Ar | 18 | 2,8,8 | 3 |

Each one has a **full outer shell**. Helium is the exception to "eight outer electrons": its only shell is the first, which is full with two.

## Unreactive: the explanation

Atoms of other elements react by losing, gaining or sharing electrons. They do this in a way that gives them a full outer shell, the same arrangement as a noble gas. A noble gas atom **already** has a full outer shell, so it has no tendency to lose, gain or share electrons. That is why the noble gases are unreactive.

A full-mark explanation links the two parts:

> Noble gases have a full outer shell of electrons **[1]**, so they do not need to lose, gain or share electrons **[1]**.

"They are stable" on its own does not earn the explanation mark, because it does not mention electrons.

## Monatomic: what it means and why

**Monatomic** means the element exists as **single, separate atoms**, not as molecules. Compare the other gaseous elements:

| Element | Formula of the gas | Particles |
|---|---|---|
| Hydrogen | H₂ | Diatomic molecules |
| Nitrogen | N₂ | Diatomic molecules |
| Oxygen | O₂ | Diatomic molecules |
| Chlorine | Cl₂ | Diatomic molecules |
| Neon | Ne | Single atoms |
| Argon | Ar | Single atoms |

Chlorine atoms (2,8,7) each need one more electron, so two of them share a pair and each reaches the argon configuration: that is why chlorine is Cl₂. Argon atoms (2,8,8) need nothing, so they do not bond to each other: argon is Ar. The **same** electronic-configuration argument therefore explains both "unreactive" and "monatomic".

When you draw particles of a noble gas, draw separate single circles spread far apart and randomly arranged, as for any gas. Do not join them into pairs.

## Gases at room temperature

Because the atoms are not bonded to each other, only very weak forces act between them. Little energy is needed to separate them, so the noble gases have very low boiling points and are all gases at room temperature and pressure.

## The noble-gas configuration in bonding

Much of Topic 2 rests on this subtopic. Atoms form ions or covalent bonds so that they end up with a noble-gas configuration:

| Particle | Configuration | Same as |
|---|---|---|
| Na⁺ | 2,8 | Neon |
| Mg²⁺ | 2,8 | Neon |
| O²⁻ | 2,8 | Neon |
| Cl⁻ | 2,8,8 | Argon |
| K⁺ | 2,8,8 | Argon |
| Each H atom in H₂ | 2 (shared pair) | Helium |
| Each Cl atom in Cl₂ | 8 in outer shell (including shared pair) | Argon |

This is why noble gases are often used as a reference point: "the sodium ion has the same electronic configuration as neon". Be careful with the reverse: a particle with the configuration 2,8,8 and a charge is an ion (such as Cl⁻ or K⁺), not an argon atom; name the particle from its charge as well as its configuration.

## Noble gases in the air

Clean, dry air is approximately 78% nitrogen and 21% oxygen, with the remainder a mixture of noble gases and carbon dioxide (10.3). Their unreactivity means they remain in the air as uncombined, single atoms.

## Data questions about the group

The Extended outcome in 8.1 (identify trends in groups, given information) can be tested with noble-gas data. Real boiling points:

| Noble gas | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|
| Helium | −269 |
| Neon | −246 |
| Argon | −186 |
| Krypton | −153 |
| Xenon | −108 |
| Radon | ? |

**Trend:** boiling point increases down the group. **Prediction for radon:** above −108 °C, but radon is still a gas at room temperature (the actual value is about −62 °C). State the direction, quote the values, then predict in the same direction.

## Annotated model answer

*Describe the Group VIII noble gases and explain their properties in terms of electronic configuration.* **[4]**

> The noble gases are unreactive **[1]**, monatomic gases **[1]**. Each atom has a full outer shell of electrons **[1]**, so it has no tendency to lose, gain or share electrons, and so does not react or bond with other atoms **[1]**.

The first two marks are descriptions; the last two are the explanation. An answer that stops at "they have full outer shells" gives the cause but not the consequence, and usually drops the final mark.

## Common mistakes

- **"All noble gases have eight outer electrons."** Helium has two.
- **"They are unreactive because they are stable"** without mentioning electrons.
- **Writing Ar₂ or Ne₂**, or drawing pairs of atoms. Noble gases are monatomic.
- **"They are unreactive because they are gases."** Chlorine is a gas and is very reactive; the reason is the full outer shell.
- **Calling noble gas particles molecules.** They are atoms.
- **Saying noble gases form ions.** With a full outer shell they have no tendency to lose or gain electrons.
- **Using "Group 0"** when the syllabus uses Group VIII. Either is understood, but the syllabus term is safest.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Describe**: unreactive, monatomic, gases. Three separate points.
- **Explain**: full outer shell, so no tendency to lose, gain or share electrons.
- **State** the configuration: give it in the 2,8,8 format for argon.
- **Deduce** from a diagram: count shells and outer electrons; a full outer shell means Group VIII.
- **Predict** from data (Extended): state the trend, use the values, and keep the prediction consistent with the trend.
