We are not born knowing how to behave: identity is learned through socialisation and shaped by the
groups we belong to. Key questions 2.1 (*How do we learn our identity?*) and 2.3 (*What influences
our social identity?*) of Cambridge O Level Sociology (2251) cover how society is socially
constructed, the nature–nurture debate, the agencies and processes of socialisation, competing
theories of socialisation, and the influence of age, gender, ethnicity, class, the digital world,
globalisation and consumer choice on identity.

Key question 2.2 (social control) is summarised in the
[O Level social control and identity revision notes](/resources/o-level-sociology-social-control-and-identity-revision-notes/).

  identity.

## What does "society is socially constructed" mean?

Social life feels natural, but it is made by people and learned. Its building blocks:

| Concept | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Culture** | The shared way of life of a group | Language, food, dress, festivals |
| **Values** | Beliefs about what is important | Respect for elders; education |
| **Norms** | Rules of behaviour based on values | Removing shoes before entering a home |
| **Customs** | Long-established traditional practices | Wedding rituals such as *mehndi* |
| **Roles** | Expected behaviour of a position | Prefect, elder sibling, shopkeeper |
| **Status** | Social position: **ascribed** at birth or **achieved** by effort | Caste or royal birth (ascribed); doctor (achieved) |
| **Stereotypes** | Fixed, oversimplified images of groups | "Boys don't cry" |

These shape identity because people judge themselves by the roles and statuses they hold and by
how others respond to them.

## Is behaviour caused by nature or nurture?

**Nature** says behaviour is inborn; **nurture** says it is learned. Sociologists stress nurture:

- **Isolated children:** Oxana Malaya, found in Ukraine in 1991 after years living largely among dogs,
  showed dog-like behaviour and limited speech — suggesting human behaviour depends on socialisation.
- **Relativity of culture:** what is polite, beautiful or moral varies between societies and over
  time, which would not happen if behaviour were fixed by biology.

## What are primary and secondary socialisation?

**Primary socialisation** happens in early childhood, mainly in the **family**. **Secondary
socialisation** continues throughout life through **education**, the **peer group**, the **media**,
**religion** and the **workplace**.

| Agency | Process (2.1.4) | How effective? |
|---|---|---|
| Family | **Canalisation** — channelling children towards "appropriate" toys and activities; **role modelling** | Very powerful in early years; less so in adolescence |
| Education | **Hidden curriculum** — punctuality, obedience, competition | Reaches almost all children for many years |
| Peer group | **Peer pressure** — acceptance or rejection | Strong in teenage years |
| Media | **Media representations** of gender, success, beauty | Constant exposure; audiences may resist |
| Religion | **Religious teachings** on morality and family | Strong in religious communities |
| Workplace | **Workplace training** — induction, codes of conduct | Shapes adult occupational identity |

## How do sociological theories explain socialisation?

- **Consensus (functionalism):** socialisation passes on shared values, creating **value consensus**
  and **social cohesion**. Problems arise from **inadequate socialisation**, for example when
  families fail to teach norms.
- **Conflict (Marxism):** socialisation teaches **capitalist ideology** — accepting hierarchy and
  inequality as normal — and **reproduces social class**. Samuel Bowles and Herbert Gintis
  (*Schooling in Capitalist America*, 1976) argued the hidden curriculum prepares obedient workers.
- **Conflict (feminism):** **gender role socialisation** teaches masculinity and femininity in ways
  that keep women subordinate. Sue Sharpe's *Just Like a Girl* (1976) found working-class girls in
  London prioritised marriage and children; when she repeated the study in the 1990s, girls'
  priorities had shifted towards jobs and careers.

## What influences social identity?

**Age** — life stages are defined differently across societies; in many communities, adulthood is
marked by marriage or earning rather than a fixed age.

**Gender** — expectations of men and women are learned and vary; they are changing as more women
gain education and paid work.

**Ethnicity** — shared language, religion, history and customs; ethnic identity can be strengthened
in minority communities or among migrants abroad.

**Social class** — occupation, income and education shape lifestyle, values and opportunities.

**The digital self (2.3.2)** — online profiles let people present chosen identities and join virtual
communities. Positives: expression, connection with distant relatives, support networks. Negatives:
cyberbullying, pressure to look perfect, privacy risks.

**Globalisation (2.3.3)** — global media and brands may **homogenise** identities; mixing cultures
creates **hybrid identities**, such as young people of South Asian heritage in the UK or Gulf states
combining traditions; some groups respond with **cultural defence**, reinforcing language, dress or
religion. Cultural diversity and **multiculturalism** can enrich identity but may also produce
tension; **global culture** can connect people while weakening local traditions.

**Postmodernism (2.3.4)** — identities are chosen, not given: people "pick and mix" through
consumption, lifestyle and digital identities. Critics reply that money, family expectations,
religion and class still limit choice.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which agency is most important?** The family is usually seen as the most important for primary
socialisation, but its influence competes with peers and media as children grow up.

**What is the difference between canalisation and role modelling?** Canalisation directs children
towards certain toys or activities; role modelling is learning by copying others' behaviour.

**Do I need sociologists' names?** Not always, but accurate examples such as Bowles and Gintis or
Sharpe strengthen explanations of theories.
