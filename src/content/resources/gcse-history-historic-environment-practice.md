## Where this question sits

The historic environment is examined by **one question** — the fourth and final question of
Section B (British depth studies) on Paper 2 — worth 16 marks (including 4 for spelling,
punctuation and grammar). Section B has four compulsory questions in total: Q1 (8 marks) evaluates
a written or visual interpretation and is where "how far do you agree" judgement phrasing belongs;
Q2 (8 marks) explains causation, change, continuity or consequence; Q3 (8 marks) is a narrative
account; and Q4 — the one this page practises — is an essay linked to your specified site, drawing
on cause, consequence, change and continuity. It is not a lower-tariff describe-then-explain pair,
and it is not itself a "how far do you agree" interpretation question.

## Practice questions

**1.** Explain how the evidence at your specified site reveals continuity and change during the
period covered by your depth study. **[16]**

**2.** Using your knowledge of your specified site and your understanding of the wider historical
period, explain what the site reveals about the causes or consequences of a key development in
your depth study. **[16]**

**3.** "A historic site tells us more about the people who used it than about the events connected
with it." Using your specified site, explain how far the evidence supports this view. **[16]**

---

## Answers (worked approach, since your specific site and depth study vary)

**1.** Build the answer around the Feature → Context → Link → Significance structure applied
across the whole essay rather than a single feature: identify two or three specific, physical
features of the site [4], set each against what was happening in the wider historical period [4],
explicitly link each feature to a specific instance of continuity or change — does the feature show
something staying the same, or something changing, and why? [4], and close with a substantiated
judgement about the overall balance of continuity against change the site demonstrates [4]. Marks
are lost by describing features and period context in separate, unconnected paragraphs rather than
making the continuity/change link explicit throughout.

**2.** As with Q1, ground the causation or consequence argument in specific, named features of the
site [4], explain the wider historical development those features connect to [4], make the causal
or consequential link explicit rather than implied [4], and reach a judgement on how strongly the
site's evidence supports that link [4]. The strongest answers select features that genuinely bear
on causation or consequence, rather than generic site description that never engages with *why*
something happened or *what resulted* from it.

**3.** A top-band answer takes a clear position (agree, partly agree, or disagree) early [4],
supports it with specific evidence from the specified site about the people who used it — layout,
artefacts, inscriptions, domestic features — weighed directly against evidence connected to specific
events at the site [8], and closes with a substantiated judgement about which category of evidence
the site more strongly reveals [4]. A response that only describes site features and events without
directly weighing "people" evidence against "events" evidence as the question demands will not
reach the top band, however detailed the description.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a site feature without connecting it to continuity, change, cause or consequence.
- Treating the historic environment essay as disconnected from the rest of the depth study it's
  drawn from.
- Answering as if this were a "how far do you agree" interpretation question (that's Q1 of the
  section, not this one) rather than an essay built from your own knowledge of the site.
- Using out-of-date knowledge of which site is specified — AQA changes it every year, not every
  two years; always confirm the current one for your exact depth study and examination series.
- Listing site features without explicitly weighing one category of evidence against another where
  the question demands a judgement between them.

## Why the historic environment essay is examined this way

The historic environment essay exists to test a genuinely different historical skill from the rest
of the depth study: reading physical, material evidence (a building's layout, an artefact's
placement, a site's location) and connecting it back to historical knowledge, rather than working
from written sources alone. This is why a strong answer always starts from specific, concrete
features of the site rather than describing the site as a whole in general terms — the skill being
tested is moving convincingly from concrete physical detail to a broader historical conclusion,
which is a different demand from the purely textual source analysis and interpretation evaluation
tested elsewhere in the qualification (including Q1 of this same section).

## Preparing without knowing the exact exam site in advance

Because the specific historic site changes every examination series and the questions above are
deliberately written to be site-agnostic, the best preparation is practising the Feature → Context →
Link → Significance method against your own actual specified site repeatedly, until the structure
itself becomes automatic regardless of which specific angle a real exam question asks about.
Building a short bank of five or six well-known features of your specified site, each with a
prepared Context/Link/Significance note, gives you material ready to adapt to whatever the exam
actually asks.
