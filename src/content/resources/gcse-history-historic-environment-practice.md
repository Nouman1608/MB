## Section A

**1.** Choose one feature of your specified historic site (a building, a layout, an artefact, or its location). Describe the feature in your own words. **[4]**

Section A moves from description (Q1) to explanation (Q2), so practise both in that order before attempting Section B's judgement question.

**2.** Explain what this feature reveals about the wider historical period your depth study covers. **[8]**

The jump from 4 marks (Q1) to 8 marks (Q2) reflects the extra demand of explanation over description — plan roughly twice the writing time on Q2 relative to Q1.

## Section B

**3.** "The most important thing a historic site can show us is how ordinary people lived." How far would you agree, using your specified site as evidence? **[16]**

**4.** (Alternative Section B question) "A historic site's value lies mainly in what it tells us about the people who built or used it, rather than the events that took place there." How far would you agree, using your specified site as evidence? **[16]**

---

## Answers (worked approach, since Section A depends on your specified site)

**1.** A strong answer describes the feature with **specific physical detail** (size, materials, layout, decoration, location relative to the rest of the site) rather than a vague general impression. Precision here sets up the explanation in Question 2 — a vague description makes the following link to historical context far harder to establish convincingly.

**2.** Use the Feature → Context → Link → Significance structure: state the feature [1], explain what was happening in the wider historical period [2], explicitly connect the two — does the feature *reflect*, *result from*, or *enable* something about that period? [3], and close with why this connection matters to understanding the period [2]. Marks are lost by describing the feature and the period separately without making the link explicit — the word "because" or "which shows" used deliberately at the Link stage is a simple habit that keeps this connection visible to an examiner.

**3.** A top-band answer takes a clear position (agree, partly agree, or disagree) [2] and supports it with **specific evidence from the specified site** — features that do (or don't) reveal information about ordinary people's lives, contrasted with what the site reveals about other things (power, religion, defence, trade) [8]. The strongest answers weigh the site's evidence about ordinary life against its evidence about other historical themes, reaching a **substantiated judgement** rather than simply listing site features [6].

**4.** As with Q3, a top-band answer takes a clear position [2] and weighs evidence about the site's builders and users against evidence about the specific events associated with the site [8], reaching a substantiated judgement about which category of evidence the site more strongly reveals [6]. A response that only describes the site's features and events without directly weighing "people" against "events" as the question demands will not reach the top band, however detailed the description.

---

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a site feature without connecting it to the historical period.
- Treating the Historic Environment element as disconnected from the rest of the depth study it's drawn from.
- Judgement questions answered as description only, with no clear "how far do you agree" position taken and sustained.
- Using out-of-date knowledge of which sites are specified for the current examination series.
- On Q4-style questions, listing site features without explicitly weighing "people" evidence against "events" evidence as the question requires.

## Why the Historic Environment study is examined this way

The Historic Environment study exists to test a genuinely different historical skill from the rest
of the depth study: reading physical, material evidence (a building's layout, an artefact's
placement, a site's location) and connecting it back to historical knowledge, rather than working
from written sources alone. This is why Section A always starts with a specific feature rather than
the site as a whole — the skill being tested is moving convincingly from a concrete physical detail
to a broader historical conclusion, which is a different demand from purely textual source analysis
elsewhere in the qualification.

## Preparing without knowing the exact exam site in advance

Because the specific historic site changes across examination series and the questions above are
deliberately written to be site-agnostic, the best preparation is practising the Feature → Context →
Link → Significance method against your own actual specified site repeatedly, until the structure
itself becomes automatic regardless of which specific feature a real exam question asks about.
Building a short bank of five or six well-known features of your specified site, each with a
prepared Context/Link/Significance note, gives you material ready to adapt to whatever the exam
actually asks.
