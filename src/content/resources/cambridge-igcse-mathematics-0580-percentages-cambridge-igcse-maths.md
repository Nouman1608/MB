This guide covers subtopic **1.13 Percentages** in Topic 1 Number of the Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics 0580 syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027. It is examined on every paper, often inside money problems. Most of it is common to both tiers; two parts are **Extended only**.

| Statement | Content | Tier |
|---|---|---|
| 1.13.1 | Calculate a given percentage of a quantity | Core and Extended |
| 1.13.2 | Express one quantity as a percentage of another | Core and Extended |
| 1.13.3 | Calculate percentage increase or decrease | Core and Extended |
| 1.13.4 | Calculate with simple and compound interest (formulas are not given) | Core and Extended |
| 1.13.4 | Problems that include repeated percentage change | Extended only |
| 1.13.5 | Calculate using reverse percentages, e.g. find the cost price given the selling price and the percentage profit | Extended only |

## The multiplier: one idea for every question

A percentage is a number of hundredths, so r% of a quantity is the quantity × r/100. Writing a change as a single **multiplier** makes every later step one multiplication or division:

| Change | Multiplier |
|---|---|
| find 35% | × 0.35 |
| increase by 35% | × 1.35 |
| decrease by 35% | × 0.65 |
| find 115% (a percentage over 100%) | × 1.15 |

The original amount is always 100%. An increase of 35% leaves you with 135% of the original; a decrease of 35% leaves 65%.

## Core and Extended

### Worked example 1: one quantity as a percentage of another

(a) Express 45 minutes as a percentage of 2 hours.
(b) A town's population grew from 120 thousand to 138 thousand. Express the new population as a percentage of the old one.

```
(a) Same units first: 2 hours = 120 minutes
    45 ÷ 120 × 100 = 37.5%

(b) 138 ÷ 120 × 100 = 115%
```

In (b) the answer is over 100% because the new value is larger than the original. The percentage **increase** is 115 − 100 = 15%.

### Worked example 2: profit and loss

A trader buys a bicycle for $250 and sells it for $215. Find the loss as an amount and as a percentage.

```
Loss = 250 - 215 = $35
Percentage loss = 35 ÷ 250 × 100 = 14%
```

Percentage profit or loss is always worked out on the **cost price** (the original amount), never on the selling price. Dividing by 215 here gives 16.3%, which is wrong.

### Worked example 3: simple interest

(a) Find the simple interest on $3200 invested for 4 years at 2.5% per year.
(b) $1800 is invested at 3% per year simple interest. How many years does it take to earn $378 interest?

```
(a) Interest each year = 3200 × 0.025 = $80
    For 4 years: 80 × 4 = $320  (total value $3520)

(b) Interest each year = 1800 × 0.03 = $54
    378 ÷ 54 = 7 years
```

The formula is not given, so learn it in words: simple interest = principal × rate × number of years, with the rate as a decimal. The interest is the same every year because it is always calculated on the original amount.

### Worked example 4: compound interest

$5000 is invested at 3.2% per year compound interest. Find (a) the value after 3 years, (b) the interest earned, (c) how much more this is than simple interest at the same rate.

```
(a) multiplier = 1.032
    5000 × 1.032^3 = 5495.52...  =  $5495.52

(b) 5495.52 - 5000 = $495.52

(c) simple interest = 5000 × 0.032 × 3 = $480
    495.52 - 480 = $15.52 more
```

With compound interest, each year's interest is added before the next year's is calculated, so the total grows faster than with simple interest. Read the question: "value" or "amount" means the total, "interest" means only the growth.

## Extended only

### Worked example 5: repeated percentage change

A price is increased by 15% and later decreased by 15%. Find the overall percentage change.

```
overall multiplier = 1.15 × 0.85 = 0.9775
0.9775 = 97.75% of the original
overall change = 100 - 97.75 = 2.25% decrease
```

The two changes do not cancel, because the 15% decrease is taken from a larger amount. Combine successive changes by multiplying the multipliers, never by adding the percentages.

### Worked example 6: reverse percentages

(a) In a sale, a jacket costs $63.75 after a 15% discount. Find the original price.
(b) A shop sells a lamp for $89.60, making a 28% profit. Find the cost price.

```
(a) sale price = original × 0.85
    original = 63.75 ÷ 0.85 = $75

(b) selling price = cost price × 1.28
    cost price = 89.60 ÷ 1.28 = $70
```

Write the relationship first ("sale price = original × 0.85"), then undo it by dividing. Taking 15% of $63.75 and adding it on gives $73.31, because the 15% belongs to the original price, not the sale price.

Reverse percentages also appear with deposits. If a 20% deposit on a car is $3400, the full price is 3400 ÷ 0.2 = $17 000.

## Earnings

Earnings questions combine percentages with pay. For example, a salary of $28 500 rises by 4%: the new salary is 28 500 × 1.04 = $29 640. A salesperson paid 3% commission on sales of $46 000 earns 46 000 × 0.03 = $1380 commission.

## Common mistakes

- Dividing by the new value instead of the original in a percentage change or a percentage profit.
- Using 0.35 as the multiplier for a 35% increase. That finds 35% of the amount, not the increased amount.
- Adding percentages for successive changes, so +15% then −15% is wrongly called "no change" (Extended).
- Finding a percentage of the new amount in a reverse percentage question (Extended).
- Calculating simple interest year by year on the growing total, which is compound interest.
- Giving "interest" when the question asked for the total value, or the reverse.
- Rounding money to 1 decimal place: money answers need 2 decimal places, such as $495.52.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Calculate**: write the multiplier you are using, such as "× 1.032³", so that the method mark is clear.
- **Show (that)** a price is a given value: work from the information to the given value. In reverse percentage questions, do not start from the answer and check it.
- **Explain** why two changes do not cancel: refer to the different base amounts, or show the combined multiplier.
- **Give your answer to the nearest cent / dollar**: round only the final answer.
