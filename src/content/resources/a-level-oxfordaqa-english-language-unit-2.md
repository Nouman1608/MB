This guide covers **Unit 2 Language and Society**, the second of two
units forming the International AS in OxfordAQA International AS and
A-level English Language (9670). Together with Unit 1, it makes up
the International AS, contributing 50% of the AS marks (20% of the
full A-level). It is assessed by a single 2-hour written exam worth
50 marks.

## Where this fits in 9670

Unit 1 examines how audience, purpose, genre and mode shape a text.
Unit 2 shifts focus to the social dimension of language -- how people
use it to express identity, build relationships, mark group membership,
claim power and status, or play and entertain. As in Unit 1, Section A
provides the analytical groundwork that directly feeds Section B's
writing task, so the two sections should be revised as a connected
pair, not independently.

## Syllabus coverage

**OXFORDAQA INTERNATIONAL AS AND A-LEVEL ENGLISH LANGUAGE (9670) —
UNIT 2 LANGUAGE AND SOCIETY**

- 3.2.1 Section A: Language and social groups – texts — one
compulsory text analysis task on spoken, written or multimodal
material illustrating the social dimensions of language use: how
language expresses identity, constructs and maintains relationships,
marks group membership, claims power and status, and creates play and
entertainment. Source material is typically drawn from power-laden
contexts (such as interviews), persuasive or exhortative texts, texts
built around shared interests or group affiliations, and playful or
performative language
- 3.2.2 Section B: Language and social groups – writing — one
compulsory academic essay, connected directly to the data analysed in
Section A, focused on the skills of academic argument in written
language about an aspect of language and social group behaviour

## How to approach it

Section A rewards being able to name and use the specification's own
categories -- group rapport and shared perspectives, assertions of
power and status, expressions of identity and relationship, and
creative play and performance -- rather than describing a text in
general terms. Because Section B's essay must connect directly to
whatever was analysed in Section A, practising the transition from
close text analysis to a structured academic argument is the single
most valuable exam-technique skill for this unit: read for evidence in
Section A with an eye to what argument it could support in Section B,
rather than treating the two sections as unrelated tasks. Since the
source texts can be spoken, written or multimodal, revision should
include a genuine range of text types -- an interview transcript, a
piece of persuasive writing, an example of in-group banter or
wordplay -- rather than only written prose, so that unfamiliar text
types in the exam do not come as a surprise.

## Common mistakes

Writing a Section B essay that restates the Section A analysis rather
than building an academic argument that uses it as evidence. Describing
what a text says without identifying the specific language features
(word choice, structure, register, pragmatic implication) that create
its social effect. Treating "power and status" and "identity" as
interchangeable categories, when the specification separates them as
distinct social functions of language that a text may draw on
individually or together. Neglecting spoken and multimodal data in
revision, when the specification explicitly draws Section A material
from all three modes.

## Worked example

A Section A text is a transcript of a job interview. What kind of
language features would a strong answer identify?

```
POWER AND STATUS: the interviewer's use of closed questions and
    topic control; formal register maintained by both speakers;
    politeness strategies used by the interviewee to mitigate
    disagreement or hedge uncertain answers.

IDENTITY AND RELATIONSHIP: how the interviewee's word choices
    construct a professional persona; use of first-person narrative
    to build credibility and rapport.

LINKING TO SECTION B: these observations about power, status and
    self-presentation in institutional talk could then support a
    Section B essay on how language reflects or reinforces power
    relationships in social interaction.
```

A weak answer would describe the interview's content (what job, what
questions) without naming the specific language features doing the
social work the specification asks students to identify.

## Quick revision checklist

- Practise identifying language features linked to each of the four
social functions: group rapport, power and status, identity and
relationship, and play and performance.
- Analyse a range of text types for Section A -- spoken transcripts,
persuasive writing, and multimodal or informal material -- not only
formal written prose.
- Practise moving directly from a Section A analysis into a Section B
academic essay that uses that analysis as evidence.
- Learn precise terminology for social language functions rather than
general descriptive language.
- Review how Section B differs from Section A in Unit 1's Directed
Writing task -- Unit 2's Section B is an academic essay, not a
transformed or creative writing piece.

## Assessment at a glance

Unit 2 is a 2-hour written exam worth 50 marks, identical in structure
and weighting to Unit 1 -- both contribute 20% each to the full
International A-level and 50% each to the International AS. Across
both units of the AS, AO3 (the ability to use English in different
ways) is weighted more heavily in Unit 1's writing-focused paper,
while AO1 (understanding of methods, terminology, concepts and
issues) carries more weight in Unit 2 -- a useful signal that Unit 2
rewards precise use of linguistic terminology at least as much as
fluent writing style.
