This guide covers **3.2.2 How the macroeconomy works: the circular flow of income, aggregate demand/aggregate supply analysis, and related concepts** in AQA AS Economics (7135), specification version 1.3. It is the theory core of Paper 2: almost every later answer on growth, unemployment, inflation and policy uses the AD/AS model built here. Two AS rules differ from the A-level and change how you calculate: you do **not** calculate the multiplier from the marginal propensity to consume, and you are **not** asked to calculate the accelerator. All countries and figures below are invented.

## 3.2.2.1 The circular flow of income

**National income** measures the value of the economy's output over a year. **Nominal income** is measured at current prices; **real income** is adjusted for changes in the price level, which is why real national income is used as an indicator of economic performance.

In the circular flow, households supply factor services to firms and receive incomes, which they spend on firms' output. Because every pound spent is a pound of someone's income, and every pound of output is sold to someone:

```
national income = national output = national expenditure
```

**Withdrawals** (leakages) leave the flow: saving (S), taxation (T) and imports (M). **Injections** enter it: investment (I), government spending (G) and exports (X).

- If injections exceed withdrawals, spending in the flow rises and national income increases.
- If withdrawals exceed injections, national income falls.
- **Equilibrium national income** is reached when injections equal withdrawals.

**Full employment income** is the level of national income at which the economy's resources are fully employed. Equilibrium and full employment income need not be the same: the flow can settle at a level of income with many workers unemployed.

## 3.2.2.2 AD/AS analysis

- A change in the **price level** is a movement along AD and along AS.
- A change in any component of AD at a given price level **shifts** AD; a change in production costs **shifts** SRAS; a change in the economy's productive capacity shifts **LRAS**.
- **Underlying economic growth** is a rightward shift of LRAS.

**Shocks, including global ones.**

| Shock | Example | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Negative demand-side | Recession in the main export markets cuts exports | AD left: real output falls, price level falls or rises more slowly, cyclical unemployment rises |
| Positive demand-side | Rise in consumer confidence | AD right: output and price level rise |
| Negative supply-side | World energy prices jump | SRAS left: price level rises and output falls together |
| Positive supply-side | Fall in world commodity prices | SRAS right: lower price level, higher output |

Use the diagram the specification names: an AD/AS diagram can show changes in the price level, **demand-deficient (cyclical) unemployment** (output below normal capacity because AD is too low) and economic growth.

## 3.2.2.3 What determines aggregate demand

AD = C + I + G + (X - M).

| Component | Main determinants |
|---|---|
| Consumption | Disposable income, interest rates, wealth (house and share prices), confidence, availability of credit |
| Investment | Expected demand, interest rates, business confidence, technology, government incentives |
| Government spending | Government's policy choices and the state of the economy |
| Net exports | Exchange rate, incomes abroad, relative inflation and quality |

**The basic accelerator.** Investment in new capital depends on the **rate of change** of demand for output, not its level. If sales grow quickly, firms need more machines, so investment rises strongly. If sales growth slows, even though sales are still rising, firms need fewer extra machines, so investment can fall. This is why investment is the most volatile component of AD. AS does not ask you to calculate it.

**Saving.** Determinants include income, interest rates, confidence about future jobs and incomes, and wealth. **Saving and investment are different**: saving is income not spent on consumption, a withdrawal; investment is spending on capital goods, an injection. A rise in saving is not the same as a rise in investment.

## 3.2.2.4 The multiplier at AS

An initial change in spending raises someone's income; part of that income is spent again, becoming another person's income, and so on. Each round is smaller because some income is withdrawn through saving, taxes and imports. The final change in national income is therefore larger than the initial injection. The same process works for a town or region (a local multiplier) as for the nation.

**The AS calculation.** The specification states that AS students will not be required to calculate the multiplier using the MPC or the propensities to withdraw, but should be able to calculate it from the initial change in injections and the resulting change in national income:

```
multiplier = change in national income / initial change in injections
```

### Worked example

Country S's government spends an extra 40 bn on rail building. National income later rises by 100 bn.

```
multiplier = 100 / 40 = 2.5
```

Working backwards: if the multiplier is still 2.5 and the government wants national income to rise by 150 bn, it needs an initial injection of 150 / 2.5 = 60 bn. A fall in exports of 12 bn would, with the same multiplier, reduce national income by 12 x 2.5 = 30 bn.

**Evaluating the multiplier.** A large multiplier makes changes in AD powerful. Its effect on real output depends on spare capacity: near full capacity, more of the extra spending raises prices rather than output. The full effect takes time to work through.

## 3.2.2.5 Short-run aggregate supply

The price level and production costs are the main determinants of SRAS. A rise in money wage rates, raw material prices or business taxation shifts SRAS left; a rise in productivity shifts it right. Wages rising 3% with productivity also rising 3% leaves unit labour cost unchanged, so SRAS need not shift.

## 3.2.2.6 Long-run aggregate supply

LRAS is assumed to be vertical at the **normal capacity level of output**. Its fundamental determinants include **technology, productivity, attitudes, enterprise, factor mobility and economic incentives**. Better training, new technology or more enterprise raise capacity and shift LRAS right; the economy can then produce more without raising the price level.

## Annotated model paragraph

*Question style: explain how a fall in business confidence might affect the economy.*

> Lower confidence makes firms expect lower future sales, so they postpone investment in new capital. Because investment is a component of AD, AD shifts to the left. Through the multiplier process, the fall in investment reduces incomes in capital goods industries, whose workers then spend less, so national income falls by more than the initial cut. On an AD/AS diagram, real output falls below the normal capacity level and the price level falls, or rises more slowly, so demand-deficient unemployment rises.

Why it works: a determinant of investment, the AD shift, the multiplier explained as rounds of spending, and the outcome for output, prices and unemployment on the AD/AS diagram.

## Common mistakes

- Calculating the multiplier as 1/(1 - MPC) in an AS answer where the data give the change in income and the injection; use the data.
- Writing that saving is an injection, or that saving and investment are the same.
- Showing a rise in the price level as a shift of AD.
- Treating any rise in real GDP as a shift of LRAS; growth from higher AD is a movement towards capacity.
- Using the accelerator to mean "investment rises when the economy grows" without the point about the rate of change.
- Drawing an upward-sloping LRAS; at AS it is vertical.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** questions in this sub-topic reward a full chain from cause to the AD/AS outcome.
- **Using the data** means taking the injection and income change from the extract to compute the multiplier.
- In evaluation, the most useful conditions are spare capacity, the size of the multiplier, confidence, and whether the shock comes from demand or supply.
