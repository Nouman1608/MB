Condensed notes on the two vitiating factors in the specification: misrepresentation and economic
duress. For full explanation and a worked scenario, use the
[vitiating factors study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-7162-vitiating-factors/).

## The answer route in one line

**Misrepresentation**: statement → of fact → made before or at contracting → induced the contract →
type → rescission (any bars?) and damages.
**Economic duress**: pressure → illegitimate → no practical choice → significant cause → rescission
(affirmed?).

Both make the contract **voidable**, not void: it stands until the innocent party sets it aside.

## Misrepresentation: elements

| Element | Rule | Case |
|---|---|---|
| Statement | Silence is not enough, except half-truths, changed circumstances and utmost good faith contracts | Dimmock v Hallett (1866); With v O'Flanagan (1936) |
| Fact, not opinion | Honest opinion where neither party has better knowledge is not fact | Bisset v Wilkinson (1927): 2,000 sheep |
| Opinion implying facts | Where the maker has superior knowledge | Smith v Land and House Property (1884): "most desirable" tenant in arrears |
| Fact, not intention | Unless the maker does not hold the stated intention | Edgington v Fitzmaurice (1885): "state of a man's mind" |
| Inducement | Must play a real part, need not be the only cause | JEB Fasteners v Marks Bloom (1983) |
| No inducement | Claimant relied on own investigation | Attwood v Small (1838) |
| Still induced | Even if the claimant could have found out the truth | Redgrave v Hurd (1881) |

## Types and damages

| Type | Test | Damages | Measure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fraudulent | Knowingly, without belief, or reckless (Derry v Peek, 1889) | Deceit | All direct losses, foreseeable or not (Doyle v Olby, 1969) |
| Negligent | MA 1967 s2(1): defendant must prove reasonable grounds and actual belief | Statutory | As if fraudulent (Royscot Trust v Rogerson, 1991) |
| Innocent | Reasonable grounds for belief | Discretionary, s2(2) | Damages in place of rescission |

Howard Marine v Ogden (1978): capacity figure given from memory when correct figures were in the
company's own documents; no reasonable grounds, so liable under s2(1).

## Rescission and its bars

Rescission sets the contract aside and restores the parties. It is **equitable and discretionary**.

| Bar | Case |
|---|---|
| Affirmation | Long v Lloyd (1958): continued use of lorry after discovering faults |
| Lapse of time | Leaf v International Galleries (1950): five years after buying the "Constable" |
| Third-party rights | Car and Universal Finance v Caldwell (1965): rescission effective by telling police and AA before resale |
| Restitution impossible | Parties cannot be restored to their original positions |

Under s2(2) the court may award damages instead for a non-fraudulent misrepresentation." loading="lazy">

## Economic duress

**Definition** (DSND Subsea v Petroleum Geo-Services, 2000):

- pressure with the practical effect of **compulsion** or **no practical choice**;
- the pressure is **illegitimate**;
- it is a **significant cause** of entering the contract.

**Evidence of no choice** (Pao On v Lau Yiu Long, 1980): protest; any alternative course; independent
advice; steps taken to avoid the contract afterwards.

| Case | Point |
|---|---|
| Barton v Armstrong (1976) | Origin: duress to the person by threats to kill |
| Atlas Express v Kafco (1989) | Threat not to deliver unless paid more; no alternative carrier; duress |
| B and S Contracts v Victor Green (1984) | Threat not to build exhibition stands unless paid extra; duress |
| The Atlantic Baron (1979) | Duress existed, but eight months' delay before protesting was affirmation |
| PIA v Times Travel (2021) | Lawful act duress only in very narrow circumstances; not made out |
| Williams v Roffey (1991) | Practical benefit can be consideration provided there is no duress |

**Remedy**: rescission and return of money paid; bars apply, especially affirmation. No damages for
duress itself.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Vitiating factor | A flaw in the making of a contract that makes it voidable |
| Voidable | Valid until the innocent party chooses to set it aside |
| Representation | A statement of fact made before or at the time of contracting that induces it |
| Inducement | The statement played a real part in the decision to contract |
| Rescission | Setting the contract aside and restoring the parties to their original positions |
| Affirmation | Conduct showing the innocent party chooses to continue the contract |
| Illegitimate pressure | Pressure the law does not accept as normal commercial bargaining |

## Exam traps

- Calling a puff or an honest opinion a misrepresentation without discussing Bisset v Wilkinson.
- Forgetting that a true statement can become a misrepresentation if circumstances change before the
  contract (With v O'Flanagan).
- Putting the burden of proof on the claimant under s2(1); it is on the defendant.
- Ignoring the bars: always apply affirmation, delay and third-party rights to the facts.
- Treating hard bargaining as duress; the question is whether the pressure was illegitimate and left no
  practical choice.
- Offering damages as the remedy for economic duress.

## Self-test

1. Is a contract affected by misrepresentation void or voidable?
2. Give two situations in which silence can be a misrepresentation.
3. Why was the statement in Bisset v Wilkinson not a misrepresentation?
4. How did Smith v Land and House Property differ?
5. What did Bowen LJ say in Edgington v Fitzmaurice?
6. Why did the claim fail in Attwood v Small?
7. What is the test for fraudulent misrepresentation?
8. Who bears the burden of proof under s2(1) of the Misrepresentation Act 1967?
9. What did Royscot Trust v Rogerson decide?
10. Name the four bars to rescission.
11. State the three elements of economic duress from DSND Subsea.
12. Why did the claimants lose in The Atlantic Baron?

**Answers:** 1. Voidable. 2. Any two of: half-truths; a statement that becomes untrue before the contract;
contracts of the utmost good faith. 3. It was an honest opinion; neither party knew whether the land
could carry 2,000 sheep. 4. The seller had superior knowledge, so describing the tenant as "most
desirable" implied facts that were untrue. 5. "The state of a man's mind is as much a fact as the state
of his digestion." 6. The buyers relied on their own investigation, so they were not induced. 7. Made
knowingly, without belief in its truth, or recklessly careless whether it was true or false (Derry v
Peek). 8. The defendant, who must prove reasonable grounds for belief. 9. Damages under s2(1) are assessed
as if the misrepresentation were fraudulent. 10. Affirmation, lapse of time, third-party rights and
impossibility of restitution. 11. Pressure with the practical effect of compulsion or no practical
choice, which is illegitimate, and which is a significant cause of entering the contract. 12. They
affirmed the contract by waiting eight months after the pressure ended before protesting.
