## Questions

**1.** Define the term investment, as used in business. **[2]**

**2.** State **two** limitations of using a decision tree to make a business decision. **[2]**

**3.** Fendrake Opticians plans to spend £84,000 on eye-scanning equipment. The expected net cash flows are £30,000 in year 1, £36,000 in year 2, £27,000 in year 3 and £24,000 in year 4. Work out how long the equipment takes to pay back, giving your answer in years and months. **[3]**

**4.** Marchcourt Cinemas is considering a £150,000 refit of one screen. Total net cash inflows over the four-year life of the refit are forecast at £204,000. Marchcourt's investment criteria require an average rate of return of at least 10%. Calculate the ARR and state whether the refit meets this criterion. **[4]**

**5.** Tavistrel Boatyard could buy a hull-cleaning machine for £50,000. Net cash flows are forecast at £18,000, £22,000 and £20,000 in years 1 to 3. The discount factors at 10% are 0.909, 0.826 and 0.751. Calculate the net present value and state what it shows. **[4]**

**6.** Orlanby Events must choose a venue for a summer food fair.

- **Indoor hall**: costs £12,000. There is a 0.7 chance of high attendance, earning £40,000. Low attendance would earn £15,000.
- **Outdoor park**: costs £5,000. Good weather has a 0.6 chance and would earn £30,000. Poor weather has a 0.4 chance and would earn £8,000.

Calculate the missing probability, the expected value and net gain of each option, and state which venue the decision tree recommends. **[6]**

**7.** Venmore Construction is thinking about buying a fleet of electric diggers. Explain how a fall in business confidence could affect this decision. **[3]**

**8.** Durlston Ferries is planning a new route to an island that has never had a ferry service. Explain the difference between risk and uncertainty in this decision. **[4]**

**9.** Ombersley Furniture is deciding whether to replace its skilled hand-finishing team with an automated finishing line. Explain **two** non-financial factors it should consider. **[4]**

**10.** Prenthorpe Pharmacy is deciding whether to open a second shop in a nearby town. Analyse the value of using a decision tree to help make this decision. **[6]**

**11.** Yarlside Bakeries supplies bread to local shops. It has £190,000 of retained profit and must choose **one** of two projects. Its investment criteria are payback within 3 years and a positive NPV at a 6% discount rate.

- **Project X**: an automated bread line costing £300,000. It would let Yarlside bid for an own-label contract with a supermarket chain; talks have started but nothing is signed. The line would replace 14 of Yarlside's bakers, and the firm's brand is built on "hand-made" bread.
- **Project Y**: a fleet of refrigerated vans costing £180,000 to deliver to a hotel group under a three-year contract that has already been signed.

| Year | Project X net cash flow (£) | Project Y net cash flow (£) | Discount factor (6%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 110,000 | 70,000 | 0.943 |
| 2 | 120,000 | 70,000 | 0.890 |
| 3 | 105,000 | 60,000 | 0.840 |
| 4 | 90,000 | 40,000 | 0.792 |
| 5 | 80,000 | -- | 0.747 |

Project X has a payback period of 2 years 8 months, an ARR of 13.7% and an NPV of £129,770. Project Y has a payback period of 2 years 8 months and an ARR of 8.3%. A recent survey of local firms shows business confidence falling.

**(a)** Calculate the net present value of Project Y. **[4]**
**(b)** Evaluate which project Yarlside Bakeries should choose. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** Spending money now on assets such as equipment, buildings or technology [1], in the expectation of earning greater returns in the future [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Credit for the second point depends on mentioning a future return; "buying assets" alone describes a purchase, not an investment.

**2.** Any two: the probabilities are estimates that may be wrong [1]; the method ignores qualitative factors such as staff morale or brand image [1]; the method ignores the timing of returns; managers may bias the forecast outcomes. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Probabilities may be wrong" and "the numbers are guesses" are one point, not two.

**3.** Cumulative cash flow: −54,000 after year 1, −18,000 after year 2 [1]. Still needed at the start of year 3 = £18,000; months = (18,000 ÷ 27,000) × 12 = 8 [1]. Payback = **2 years 8 months** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the cumulative figures so the method is visible even if the months slip.

**4.** Total profit = 204,000 − 150,000 = £54,000 [1]. Average annual profit = 54,000 ÷ 4 = £13,500 [1]. ARR = (13,500 ÷ 150,000) × 100 = **9%** [1]. This is below 10%, so the refit **does not meet** the criterion [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Dividing total inflows (£204,000) by the cost is a frequent slip; ARR uses profit after the cost is taken off.

**5.** Present values: 18,000 × 0.909 = 16,362; 22,000 × 0.826 = 18,172; 20,000 × 0.751 = 15,020 [1]. Total present value = £49,554 [1]. NPV = 49,554 − 50,000 = **−£446** [1]. The negative NPV shows the machine earns slightly less than a 10% return, so on financial grounds it is not worthwhile [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep the minus sign; a bare "£446" reverses the meaning and the interpretation that follows.

**6.** Probability of low attendance = 1 − 0.7 = **0.3** [1]. Indoor EV = (0.7 × 40,000) + (0.3 × 15,000) = **£32,500** [1]; net gain = 32,500 − 12,000 = **£20,500** [1]. Outdoor EV = (0.6 × 30,000) + (0.4 × 8,000) = **£21,200** [1]; net gain = 21,200 − 5,000 = **£16,200** [1]. The tree recommends the **indoor hall**, which has the higher net gain [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Base the recommendation on net gain, not expected value; here both agree, but they often do not.

**7.** Lower business confidence means Venmore's managers expect weaker demand for building work [1]. They may doubt that the forecast cash flows from the diggers will happen [1], so they may delay the purchase or keep their current machines, even if the appraisal figures look positive [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Linking confidence to doubt about forecast cash flows turns a definition into an explanation.

**8.** Risk applies where outcomes can be identified and given probabilities [1], such as fuel price changes, which Durlston can estimate from its other routes [1]. Uncertainty applies where outcomes or their chances cannot be measured [1]; with no past ferry service, Durlston cannot reliably estimate how many islanders and visitors will travel [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Apply each definition to Durlston; two textbook definitions alone reach only half the marks.

**9.** Staff: the line would end the jobs of skilled finishers [1], which could lower morale and loyalty among the remaining workforce and harm Ombersley's local reputation [1]. Brand and quality: customers may value hand-finished furniture [1], so automation could weaken the brand's appeal even if unit costs fall [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Factors must be non-financial; "it saves wages" is a financial point and gains no credit here.

**10.** A decision tree would make Prenthorpe set out every option, including not opening the shop [1], and assign probabilities to outcomes such as strong or weak local demand [1]. This gives a net gain for each option, so the owner has a numerical basis to compare the cost of the second shop with the return [1]. The tree can be amended, for example if rent rises, to see whether the decision switches [1]. However, the probabilities for a town where Prenthorpe has never traded are largely guesses [1], so the tree's value depends on how good the local research is, and it should be used alongside judgement about competitors and staffing [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* One point developed through its consequences earns more analysis credit than a list of features.

**11. (a)** Present values: 70,000 × 0.943 = 66,010; 70,000 × 0.890 = 62,300 [1]; 60,000 × 0.840 = 50,400; 40,000 × 0.792 = 31,680 [1]. Total present value = £210,390 [1]. NPV = 210,390 − 180,000 = **£30,390** [1].
**(b)** Both projects meet both criteria: each pays back in 2 years 8 months, inside the 3-year target, and both have positive NPVs [1]. Project X's NPV of £129,770 is more than four times Project Y's £30,390 [1], and its ARR of 13.7% is well above Y's 8.3% [1], so on the numbers X is clearly stronger. However, X costs £300,000 and Yarlside has only £190,000, so it must borrow at least £110,000 [1], adding interest costs and risk while confidence is falling [1]. X's cash flows depend on a supermarket contract that is not signed [1]; this is uncertainty, so the NPV may be far too high [1]. Y's income rests on a contract already signed, so its forecasts are more reliable [1]. X would also replace 14 bakers, which could damage morale and the "hand-made" brand that existing shop customers value [1]. Choosing one project has an opportunity cost: picking Y gives up X's higher potential return, while picking X could tie up borrowing capacity Yarlside may need later [1]. Judgement: Yarlside should choose **Project Y** now, because it meets both criteria, can be funded from retained profit and carries far less uncertainty [1]. It could revisit X once a signed supermarket contract makes the forecasts dependable [1].
*Examiner insight:* Strong evaluations use the calculated NPV and the case facts to reach a supported decision; either project can be justified with clear reasoning.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Comparing expected values instead of net gains when options cost different amounts.
- Forgetting to complete a missing probability before calculating an expected value.
- Converting part-years to months wrongly, such as writing 0.67 years as 6.7 months.
- Using total inflows rather than profit in the ARR calculation.
- Dropping the minus sign on a negative NPV.
- Defining risk or uncertainty without applying it to the firm in the question.
- Listing financial points when the question asks for non-financial factors.
- Recommending the project with the highest NPV without weighing funding, uncertainty and opportunity cost.
- Finishing an evaluation with no firm recommendation backed by the case facts.
