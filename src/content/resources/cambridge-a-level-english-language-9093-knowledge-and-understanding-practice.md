## Section A

**1.** Identify **three** conventions of an editorial and explain what each one does for the reader. **[6]**

**2.** Explain the difference between tense and aspect, using the verb phrases "she walked", "she was walking" and "she had walked". **[4]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by the "implied reader" of a text, and identify the implied reader of this sentence: *"If your weekends are spent ferrying children between football and swimming, this car was built for you."* **[3]**

**4.** Name the morphological process in each word and explain the effect each might have in an advertisement: (a) "snackable", (b) "brunch", (c) "skincare". **[6]**

**5.** Explain the difference between audience in the **design** of a text and audience in its **reception**. **[4]**

**6.** Identify the purposes of a museum's online guide that describes its exhibits and ends with ticket prices. Explain how the purposes relate to each other. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** Read the opening of a review (original passage):

> *Let's be honest: nobody walks into a three-hour opera about tax reform expecting to cry. I did. Twice. The
> composer has taken the driest subject imaginable and found, buried in it, a story about fairness that will stay
> with you long after the final curtain.*

Analyse how the writer uses **two** conventions of the review form and **two** sentence-level features to engage the reader. **[8]**

**8.** Read this extract from a public notice (original passage):

> *Visitors must keep to marked paths. Dogs should be kept on a lead at all times. Cycling may be permitted on
> designated routes during winter months.*

Analyse how modality is used to signal different levels of obligation, and explain why this suits the form and audience. **[6]**

**9.** A travel writer begins a piece: *"I am standing on a rooftop in a city that never seems to sleep, and below me
the traffic is already roaring."* Analyse the effect of the present tense and the progressive aspect in this opening. **[4]**

**10.** Read this extract from a podcast script (original passage):

> *So here's the question we're asking today. Not "can we fix it?" We can. Not "should we fix it?" We should. The
> question is: will we?*

Analyse how phonology, rhetorical devices and features of spoken address make this suitable for a listening audience. **[8]**

**11.** The same sentence, *"The bridge will close for six months"*, appears in a council leaflet, a local business owner's blog and a news story. Discuss how genre, purpose and context could change its meaning in each. **[6]**

**12.** A charity text about clean water was written for adult donors in 2010 and is now used in a school lesson. Discuss how the change in context of reception might change the way the text is understood. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Any three conventions, each with a function. **Institutional "we"** [1] presents the view as the publication's collective, considered position rather than one person's opinion [1]. **A clear stance from the opening** [1] tells the reader at once what the piece argues, so the evidence that follows is read as support [1]. **Evaluative lexis** such as "reckless" or "overdue" [1] steers the reader's judgement as well as describing events [1]. A closing call to action or judgement, which leaves the reader with the publication's verdict, can be credited in place of any of these.

**2.** **Tense** places an action in time: "walked" is past tense [1]. **Aspect** shows how the action unfolds [1]. "She was walking" uses the **progressive aspect**, presenting the action as ongoing at a past moment, often as a backdrop to another event [1]. "She had walked" uses the **perfect aspect**, presenting the action as completed before a later past point [1].

**3.** The implied reader is **the reader a text constructs and assumes**, revealed by its address, references and assumptions [1]. Here the implied reader is **a parent** [1] **with a busy family routine who drives children to activities at weekends**, shown by "ferrying children between football and swimming" and the direct "you" [1].

**4. (a)** "Snackable" uses **affixation**, the suffix "-able" added to "snack" [1]; it creates an adjective that sounds new and makes the product seem easy to eat at any time [1]. **(b)** "Brunch" is a **blend** of "breakfast" and "lunch" [1]; it names a relaxed, sociable meal in one compact word, suiting a lifestyle advertisement [1]. **(c)** "Skincare" is a **compound** of "skin" and "care" [1]; it presents a product category as an established routine the reader should already have [1].

**5.** **Design** concerns the writer's choices made with an intended audience in mind [1], such as register, assumed knowledge and forms of address [1]. **Reception** concerns how actual readers interpret the text [1], which may differ from the writer's intention because readers differ in age, culture, period or reason for reading [1].

**6.** The guide **informs** (describes exhibits) [1] and **persuades** (encourages a visit, shown by ending with ticket prices) [1]. The informative purpose **serves the persuasive one**: the description is selected to make the exhibits appealing so that the reader acts on the final practical information [1].

**7.** Review conventions (any two; credit others that fit). **A verdict delivered early and personally** ("I did. Twice.") [1] gives the evaluation the authority of first-hand experience [1]. **Context for the work under review** ("three-hour opera about tax reform") [1] lets a reader who has not seen it understand what is being judged [1]. Alternatively, evaluative language such as "driest subject imaginable" and "will stay with you" both judges and recommends. Sentence-level features (any two; credit others that fit). **Minor sentences** "I did." and "Twice." [1] are short and emphatic after the long opening sentence, creating surprise and undercutting the expectation it sets up [1]. The **inclusive imperative "Let's be honest"** [1] creates a conversational tenor, as if reviewer and reader share a view [1]. Alternatively, end-focus places "a story about fairness" at the climax of the final sentence, stressing the work's real subject.

**8.** "**Must**" expresses **the highest obligation**, a rule with no exceptions [1], suiting a protective requirement that applies to everyone [1]. "**Should**" expresses **strong advice rather than an absolute rule** [1], softening the instruction for dog owners [1]. "**May be permitted**" expresses **possibility and conditional permission** [1]. The graded modality suits a **public notice for a general audience**, which must make clear which actions are rules and which are allowances, without needing explanation [1].

**9.** The **present tense** ("I am standing") places the reader in the moment with the writer, creating immediacy [1] [1]. The **progressive aspect** ("am standing", "is already roaring") presents the scene as ongoing and unfinished, so the city feels alive and in motion around the observer [1] [1].

**10.** Phonology: **the repeated "w" sounds** in "we", "will we" and **the rhyming emphasis** of "fix it... fix it" make the pattern memorable to a listener [1] [1]. Rhetorical devices: **a pattern of three questions** with two answered and dismissed builds towards the real question [1] [1]; **antithesis** between "can", "should" and "will" separates ability, duty and action [1]. Spoken address: the **discourse marker "So"** and **signposting "here's the question"** orient a listener who cannot reread [1] [1]; **inclusive "we"** draws the audience into the shared problem [1].

**11.** In the **council leaflet**, the purpose is to inform residents and the sentence is a neutral, authoritative statement of fact, likely followed by practical advice [1] [1]. In the **business owner's blog**, the context is personal and economic, so the same sentence becomes a complaint or warning about lost trade, probably framed with first-person evaluation [1] [1]. In the **news story**, the sentence becomes a reported event, attributed to a source and possibly set against others' reactions, so its meaning depends on the angle the reporter chooses [1] [1].

**12.** The original audience were **adult donors**, so the text was designed to persuade them to give [1]; in a lesson its **reception changes**: pupils may read it as an example of persuasive technique to analyse rather than as a request to act [1]. The **time gap** may also change meaning: figures or situations described may now be out of date, so readers may read it as a historical document [1], and pupils may not share the assumed knowledge of adult donors, changing how references are understood [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming conventions or features without explaining what each does for the reader.
- Treating modal verbs as interchangeable instead of distinguishing degrees of obligation.
- Calling the progressive aspect a "tense".
- Giving a single purpose for a text that clearly combines several.
- Discussing context as background facts rather than as a factor that changes meaning.
