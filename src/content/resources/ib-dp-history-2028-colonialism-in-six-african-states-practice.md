> **HL only. These are original questions written for Marlbridge** to practise Regional study 8 of Africa and the Middle East. They are not reproduced exam questions, and the real Paper 3 has essay questions only and requires one prescribed country. Use the country you have studied. Source extracts are constructed for practice. See the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-colonialism-in-six-african-states/) and [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-colonialism-in-six-african-states-revision-notes/).

## Section A: short answers

**1.** Distinguish between direct rule and indirect rule, giving one country as an example of each. **[4]**

**2.** Explain one reason why forced labour was important to the colonial economy of Mozambique or Côte d'Ivoire. **[3]**

**3.** Identify two ways in which women were affected by colonial rule in one prescribed country. **[2]**

## Section B: source and argument

*Constructed for practice, not a real source:* "The district officer had a hundred thousand people to govern and three clerks. He could not rule without the chief, and the chief knew it." (a modern historian's summary of indirect rule)

**4.** Explain the argument made in this extract. **[3]**

**5.** Explain one way in which the extract may overstate the power of chiefs under colonial rule. **[3]**

**6.** Examine the reasons why the Mau Mau rising in Kenya or an armed rising in another prescribed country was unable to defeat colonial rule by force. **[6]**

## Section C: essay questions

**7.** To what extent did colonial economic policies change the lives of Africans in one prescribed country? **[15]**

**8.** Evaluate the view that political and legal action was more effective than armed rebellion in challenging colonial rule in one prescribed country. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Direct rule: one mark for definition (European officials govern and administer, with appointed local agents as assistants), one for example (Côte d'Ivoire, or Mozambique under the Portuguese). Indirect rule: one mark for definition (governing through existing or recognised African rulers under European supervision), one for example (Nigeria under Lugard, or Uganda through Buganda). **[4]**

**2.** One mark for the reason (colonial governments and companies needed cheap labour for plantations, railways, roads and mines); one for explanation (taxes were not enough, so recruitment or compulsion was used, as with *chibalo* in Mozambique or labour for European plantations in Côte d'Ivoire); one for effect or example (cotton or sugar output rose, people fled across borders, and reforms followed in 1946 in French territories). **[3]**

**3.** One mark each for two of: heavier farm work when men migrated; loss of rights where colonial law favoured male household heads; taxes and market regulation; opportunities and limits in mission education; protest, as in the Aba Women's War of 1929; participation in Mau Mau. **[2]**

**4.** One mark for the claim that a small colonial staff relied on chiefs; one for the idea that African intermediaries held real bargaining power; one for the implication that indirect rule was a practical necessity as well as a policy. **[3]**

**5.** One mark for a limitation, up to two for development. For example: chiefs often held power only because the colonial state recognised them, as warrant chiefs in eastern Nigeria were created by the British and resented; colonial officers could remove chiefs; the chief's authority was weakened if he was seen as serving the colonisers (Aba 1929). **[3]**

**6.** Up to 6 marks. Credit reasons for failure, with examples: the superior weapons, air power and numbers of colonial forces; the detention of leaders and mass arrests (Kenyatta, villagisation, detention camps); lack of support among all groups, as loyalist Africans (including many Kikuyu) fought against Mau Mau; lack of foreign support and sanctuary; and the brutal methods used. Credit counterpoints: military defeat was followed by political reform, and the cost of the Emergency, including the Hola scandal in 1959, pressed Britain to negotiate. Level guide: 1 to 2 for description, 3 to 4 for partial examination, 5 to 6 for balanced examination with a clear conclusion. **[6]**

**7.** Marking points, up to 15 in total:
- up to 3 for understanding "change" and criteria (land, labour, income, trade, family life);
- up to 4 for evidence of change in the chosen country (for example Senegal's groundnuts and Dakar, Kenya's land alienation, Uganda's cotton, Mozambique's forced cotton and migrant labour);
- up to 4 for continuity and limits (subsistence farming, local trade, chiefly authority, women's farm work, the persistence of customary law);
- up to 4 for evaluation of perspectives (colonial development, nationalist exploitation, and recent mixed accounts) and a judgement on the extent of change. **[15]**

**8.** Marking points, up to 15 in total:
- up to 3 for understanding "effective" and choosing criteria (concessions won, independence, cost, unity);
- up to 4 for the effects of political and legal action (parties, unions, petitions, elections, negotiations; for example the RDA, NCNC, KAU or FRELIMO's earlier political phase);
- up to 4 for the effects of armed rebellion (Mau Mau, FRELIMO's war from 1964, other risings) and their limits;
- up to 4 for evaluation and judgement, for example that the two often worked together because violence increased the cost of colonial rule while political groups negotiated terms. **[15]**

## Model paragraph and feedback

Here is a model paragraph for Question 7, using Senegal, followed by points on how to improve a draft.

*Model paragraph:* "Colonial economic policy in Senegal reshaped agriculture around the groundnut. Peasants, encouraged by the Mouride brotherhood and by taxes that demanded cash, grew groundnuts for export, and the Dakar-Niger railway carried the harvest to the coast. This changed the economy, the use of land and the growth of towns. However, many features of life continued: family farming, Islamic leadership and local trade. This essay argues that the policy changed what people grew and how they were linked to world markets more than it changed the structures of village life, and that the gains were uneven because prices fell in the 1930s."

*Feedback points:*

- Choose one prescribed country and keep to it, because the exam requires it.
- Use at least one named policy, crop or date in each paragraph.
- Weigh change against continuity in every answer about impact.
- Do not describe independence unless the question asks.
