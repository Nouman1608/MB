## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Export | A good or service sold to buyers in another country |
| Import | A good or service bought from sellers in another country |
| World price (Pw) | The price at which the good trades internationally, shown as a horizontal line |
| Foreign exchange earnings | Foreign currency received from exports |
| Absolute advantage (HL only) | Producing a good with fewer resources, or more output from the same resources, than another country |
| Opportunity cost (HL only) | The next best alternative given up |
| Comparative advantage (HL only) | A lower opportunity cost of a good than a trading partner has |

## The nine benefits at a glance

| Benefit | One-line chain |
|---|---|
| Increased competition | Foreign rivals force home firms to improve or lose sales |
| Lower prices | More supply and rivalry reduce prices, so consumers gain |
| Greater choice | Consumers and firms can pick from more varieties and inputs |
| Acquisition of resources | Imports bring in materials, energy or technology the country lacks |
| More foreign exchange earnings | Exports earn foreign currency to pay for imports |
| Access to larger markets | Sales are not capped by the home population |
| Economies of scale | Higher output spreads fixed costs, so average cost falls |
| More efficient resource allocation | Resources shift to what the country does best |
| More efficient production | Competition and specialisation cut waste |

For each benefit in an answer, give the mechanism and name who gains. Add a limit if the command term asks you to go beyond analysis.

## Free-trade diagrams

Both diagrams show price up the side, quantity along the bottom, domestic D and S, the no-trade equilibrium and a horizontal line at Pw.

| | World price above domestic price | World price below domestic price |
|---|---|---|
| Result | Country exports | Country imports |
| Quantity traded | Qs − Qd (at Pw) | Qd − Qs (at Pw) |
| Consumers | Pay more, buy less: surplus falls | Pay less, buy more: surplus rises |
| Producers | Receive more, sell more: surplus rises | Receive less, sell less: surplus falls |
| Net effect | Gain, because producers gain more than consumers lose | Gain, because consumers gain more than producers lose |

### Method in steps (HL only calculation)

1. Find the no-trade equilibrium by setting Qd = Qs, to see which case applies.
2. Substitute Pw into the demand and supply equations to find Qd and Qs.
3. Subtract: Qs − Qd for exports, Qd − Qs for imports.
4. Export revenue = Pw × exports. Import expenditure = Pw × imports.

Worked reminder with a fictional market. Qd = 100 − 2P and Qs = 3P − 20. Setting them equal: 120 = 5P, so P = 24 and Q = 52. At Pw = 30, Qd = 40 and Qs = 70, so exports are 30 and revenue is 30 × 30 = 900. At Pw = 16, Qd = 68 and Qs = 28, so imports are 40 and expenditure is 16 × 40 = 640.

## Comparative advantage (HL only)

### Method in steps

1. Write each country's maximum output of each good, or the resource cost of one unit.
2. Opportunity cost of X in terms of Y = Y given up ÷ X gained. Find it for both goods and both countries.
3. The lower opportunity cost for a good gives the comparative advantage in that good.
4. Each country specialises in the good where its opportunity cost is lower.
5. Mutually beneficial terms of trade lie strictly between the two countries' opportunity costs.

Reminder with fictional data. Country J makes 12 shirts or 4 machines. Country K makes 10 shirts or 5 machines.

- Opportunity cost of one machine: J gives up 12 ÷ 4 = 3 shirts, K gives up 10 ÷ 5 = 2 shirts. K has the comparative advantage in machines.
- Opportunity cost of one shirt: J gives up 4 ÷ 12 = ⅓ machine, K gives up 5 ÷ 10 = ½ machine. J has the comparative advantage in shirts.
- Any price between 2 and 3 shirts per machine benefits both.

### The linear PPC diagram

- Each PPC is a straight line, so the opportunity cost is constant.
- The line with the shallower slope for a good shows the lower opportunity cost of that good.
- Different slopes are what create gains. With identical slopes there is nothing to gain.
- After specialisation and trade, each country can consume at a point outside its own PPC.
- Label both axes, both intercepts and the pre-trade and post-trade consumption points.

### Sources of comparative advantage

Factor endowments, technology, skills, infrastructure and institutions, and past investment. These can change over time.

### Limitations of the theory (HL only)

| Limitation | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Constant opportunity cost assumed | Real opportunity costs often rise with output |
| No transport or trade costs | These can wipe out a small gain |
| Resources assumed fully employed and mobile | Displaced workers may be unemployed for long |
| Over-specialisation | Dependence on one export brings price risk |
| Perfect competition assumed | Large firms and scale effects change outcomes |
| Gains uneven | Some groups lose even if the country gains |
| Non-economic costs left out | Environmental damage is not counted |

The guide sets this at evaluation depth. Finish with a judgement: the theory explains why trade can raise output, but its assumptions mean real gains are smaller and less evenly spread.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Absolute vs comparative advantage:** output or resource use, against opportunity cost.
- **Exports vs imports on the diagram:** Qs − Qd against Qd − Qs.
- **Revenue vs expenditure:** exports earn revenue, imports cost expenditure, both at Pw.
- **Movement along a curve vs a shift:** free trade changes the price faced, so quantities move along D and S. The curves themselves do not shift.
- **Gain overall vs gain for everyone:** trade can raise total surplus while some groups lose.

## Quick self-test

1. Outline how access to larger markets benefits a firm.
2. Fictional firm: fixed costs 90 000, variable cost 3 per unit. Calculate average cost at 10 000 units and at 30 000 units.
3. (HL only) With Qd = 100 − 2P and Qs = 3P − 20, state the no-trade price and quantity.
4. (HL only) Using the same market, find exports and export revenue at a world price of 30.
5. (HL only) Using the same market, find imports and import expenditure at a world price of 16.
6. When the world price is below the domestic price, which group in the home country loses?
7. (HL only) Country J makes 12 shirts or 4 machines. Country K makes 10 shirts or 5 machines. Find the opportunity cost of a machine in each country.
8. (HL only) Which country has the comparative advantage in machines, and which in shirts?
9. (HL only) Give the range of terms of trade (shirts per machine) at which both countries gain.
10. (HL only) State two limitations of the theory of comparative advantage.

### Answers

1. Sales are not limited by the home population, so output can rise, fixed costs are spread and profit can grow.
2. (90 000 + 3 × 10 000) ÷ 10 000 = **12**. (90 000 + 3 × 30 000) ÷ 30 000 = **6**.
3. 120 = 5P, so **P = 24, Q = 52**.
4. At 30: Qd = 40, Qs = 70, so exports = **30**. Revenue = 30 × 30 = **900**.
5. At 16: Qd = 68, Qs = 28, so imports = **40**. Expenditure = 16 × 40 = **640**.
6. Domestic producers, who receive a lower price and sell less.
7. J: 12 ÷ 4 = **3 shirts**. K: 10 ÷ 5 = **2 shirts**.
8. K in machines (2 < 3). J in shirts (⅓ machine < ½ machine).
9. **Between 2 and 3** shirts per machine.
10. For example: constant opportunity cost is assumed, and transport costs are ignored.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the domestic equilibrium price instead of Pw when finding revenue or expenditure.
- Subtracting quantities the wrong way round and giving a negative export figure.
- Dividing the wrong way when finding opportunity cost.
- Arguing that a country that is better at both goods gains nothing from trade.
- Drawing the world price as a curve, or leaving the axes, curves or Pw unlabelled.
- Listing benefits with no mechanism, so the answer stays at description when the command term asks for explanation.
- Giving only gains and no losers when asked to evaluate or discuss.
- Listing limitations of the theory without saying how serious each one is, which leaves the answer without a judgement.
- Showing only a final number in a calculation. The guide's meaning of calculate requires the stages of working.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the benefits of international trade in IB DP Economics?**
Sub-topic 4.1 of the IB DP Economics guide lists nine: increased competition, lower prices, greater choice, acquisition of resources, more foreign exchange earnings, access to larger markets, economies of scale, more efficient resource allocation and more efficient production.

**What is the difference between absolute and comparative advantage?**
Absolute advantage compares output: a country makes a good with fewer resources than its partner. Comparative advantage compares opportunity cost: a country makes a good at a lower opportunity cost than its partner.

**Does a country that is better at producing everything still gain from trade?**
Yes. Even with an absolute advantage in both goods, it has a comparative advantage in the good with the lower opportunity cost. Specialising in that good and trading lets both countries consume beyond their own PPC.

**Is comparative advantage HL only in IB Economics?**
Yes. Absolute and comparative advantage, the related calculations and the limitations of the theory are HL only, while the benefits of trade and the free trade diagrams are for SL and HL.
