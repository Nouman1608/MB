## What the specification asks for

Students should be able to "describe the relationship between health and disease and the interactions between different types of disease." The content then states:

- "Health is the state of physical and mental well-being."
- Diseases, communicable and non-communicable, are major causes of ill health; "other factors including diet, stress and life situations may have a profound effect on both physical and mental health."
- Four named interactions between different types of disease.
- Data skills: translate disease incidence information between graphical and numerical forms; construct and interpret frequency tables and diagrams, bar charts and histograms; use a scatter diagram to identify a correlation (MS 2c, 2g, 4a).
- "Students should understand the principles of sampling as applied to scientific data, including epidemiological data" (MS 2d).

## Health and disease are not opposites

Because health means **physical and mental well-being**, three situations are possible:

- a person with a disease is in ill health;
- a person can be free of disease yet in poor health, for example through chronic stress, poor diet or difficult life situations such as poverty or poor housing;
- a person with a well-controlled condition can still enjoy a good state of well-being.

So the **relationship** is: disease is one major cause of ill health, but not the only one. That sentence answers many "describe the relationship" questions.

Diet, stress and life situations act on both kinds of health. A poor diet can cause deficiency or obesity (physical) and affect mood (mental). Long-term stress affects sleep and mental health and can make people less able to cope with illness. Life situations, such as income, where you live and access to healthcare, change what people can eat, how they live and how soon illness is treated.

## How different types of disease interact

The specification lists four interactions. For each, learn a cause-and-effect chain, because "explain" questions reward the links.

| Interaction | Chain to write |
|---|---|
| Defects in the immune system make infectious diseases more likely | Fewer working white blood cells → fewer pathogens destroyed → pathogens reproduce → infection develops |
| Viruses living in cells can trigger cancers | Virus lives inside body cells → changes the cells → cells divide uncontrollably → tumour |
| Immune reactions first caused by a pathogen can trigger allergies | Immune system responds to a pathogen → later over-reacts to harmless substances → skin rashes or asthma |
| Severe physical ill health can lead to mental illness | Long-term pain or disability → loss of work, activities or independence → depression |

Questions often present an unfamiliar case. If a patient on drugs that suppress the immune system keeps catching infections, recognise the first interaction and apply its chain to that patient.

## Handling disease data

### Incidence and rates

**Incidence** is the number of new cases in a population in a given time. To compare places of different sizes, convert to a rate, and be ready to convert back.

**Worked example 1: from a graph to a number.** A bar chart shows an incidence of 12 cases per 1000 children per year for a disease. A town has 25 000 children. How many new cases should the town expect in a year?

```
Number of groups of 1000 = 25 000 / 1000 = 25
Expected cases = 25 x 12 = 300
```

This is "translating between graphical and numerical forms": reading a value from a chart, then using it in a calculation.

### Building a frequency table

**Worked example 2.** A clinic records the ages of 20 patients newly diagnosed with asthma (invented data):

4, 7, 9, 12, 15, 15, 18, 22, 25, 31, 34, 38, 41, 45, 52, 58, 63, 67, 71, 76

| Age group (years) | Values in the group | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to under 20 | 4, 7, 9, 12, 15, 15, 18 | 7 |
| 20 to under 40 | 22, 25, 31, 34, 38 | 5 |
| 40 to under 60 | 41, 45, 52, 58 | 4 |
| 60 to under 80 | 63, 67, 71, 76 | 4 |
| Total | | 20 |

Check that the frequencies add up to the number of values. Age is **continuous**, so the data are drawn as a **histogram**: bars touching, age on the x-axis, frequency on the y-axis. The classes here are equal (20 years each), so bar height can equal frequency. If the categories were separate, such as different countries or types of disease, a **bar chart** with gaps would be correct.

### Interpreting a scatter diagram

A scatter diagram plots two variables measured for each person or place. Describe it in three steps:

1. **Direction**: positive (both increase together), negative (one rises as the other falls) or none.
2. **Strength**: strong if the points lie close to a line, weak if widely spread.
3. **Meaning**: a correlation shows the variables are linked; it does not show that one causes the other.

### Principles of sampling

Epidemiology studies patterns of disease in whole populations, but scientists usually measure a **sample**. To trust a conclusion, ask:

- **Is it large enough?** Small samples are easily distorted by a few unusual individuals.
- **Is it representative?** It should match the population in age, sex and background.
- **Was it chosen at random?** Researchers choosing people themselves may bias the sample.
- **Has it been repeated?** Other studies finding the same pattern increase confidence.

**Worked example 3: evaluating a sample.** A researcher wants to know how common anxiety is among teenagers in a country. She surveys 40 students at one school on the day before exams.

- 40 people is a small sample.
- One school is not representative of all teenagers.
- The day before exams is likely to give unusually high anxiety scores, a biased result.
- Improvement: a large, randomly selected sample from many schools, surveyed at different times of year.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** the relationship between health and disease: define health, then say disease is a major cause of ill health but other factors matter too.
- **Explain** an interaction: give the full chain, not just the pair of diseases.
- **Calculate** a rate or expected number: show the method and units, such as "per 1000 per year".
- **Construct** a chart: choose bar chart or histogram, label axes with units, use a sensible scale.
- **Evaluate** a study: comment on size, representativeness, randomness and repetition, using the details given.

## Common errors

- Defining health as "not being ill" or leaving out mental well-being.
- Naming two diseases that interact without explaining how.
- Drawing a bar chart with gaps for continuous data such as age.
- Comparing numbers of cases in places of different sizes instead of rates.
- Saying a correlation proves cause.
- Calling any small sample "unfair" without saying why it matters.
