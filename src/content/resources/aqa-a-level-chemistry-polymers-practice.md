These questions cover section **3.3.12 Polymers** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification
(7404/7405), version 1.1, 1 December 2015, for AS and A-level exams June 2016 onwards: 3.3.12.1
Condensation polymers and 3.3.12.2 Biodegradability and disposal of polymers. The whole section is
**A-level only**. It is examined on A-level Paper 2, and A-level Paper 3 can test any content.

Learn the ideas first in the [polymers study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-polymers/) and the
[polymers revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-polymers-revision-notes/). The course hub is
[AQA A-Level Chemistry](/boards/aqa/a-level/chemistry/), the
[printable checklist](/checklists/aqa/a-level/chemistry/) lists every specification point, and the
[free 10-minute diagnostics](/diagnostics/) show where your gaps are. Addition polymer questions are in
the [alkenes practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-alkenes-practice/). Questions 7, 9, 10
and 12 need a calculator. Use Ar: H = 1.0, C = 12.0, N = 14.0, O = 16.0, Cl = 35.5. In formulas, C₆H₄ is
a benzene ring joined at positions 1 and 4.

## Questions

**1.** Which pair of compounds reacts to form a polyamide?

- A ethane-1,2-diol and butanedioic acid
- B propene and chloroethene
- C butane-1,4-diamine and hexanedioic acid
- D ethane-1,2-diol and ethene

**[1]**

**2.** Hexanedioic acid reacts with ethane-1,2-diol to form a condensation polymer.

**(a)** Name the type of linkage formed between the monomers. **[1]**
**(b)** Name the other product of the reaction. **[1]**

**3.** Draw the repeating unit of the polymer formed from benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid and
propane-1,3-diol. **[2]**

**4.** A section of a polymer chain is
–CO–(CH₂)₂–CO–NH–C₆H₄–NH–CO–(CH₂)₂–CO–NH–C₆H₄–NH–

**(a)** Draw the repeating unit. **[1]**
**(b)** Give the structures and names of the two monomers. **[2]**
**(c)** State the type of condensation polymer. **[1]**

**5.** A section of a polypeptide chain is
–NH–CH₂–CO–NH–CH(CH₃)–CO–NH–CH₂–CO–NH–CH(CH₃)–CO–

**(a)** Draw the structures of the two amino acids used to make it. **[2]**
**(b)** State the other product formed as the chain is made. **[1]**

**6.** Explain, in terms of intermolecular forces, why nylon 6,6 has a higher melting point than
poly(ethene) with chains of similar length. **[3]**

**7.** This question is about Kevlar.

**(a)** Describe how a hydrogen bond forms between two neighbouring Kevlar chains. **[2]**
**(b)** The repeating unit of Kevlar is –[CO–C₆H₄–CO–NH–C₆H₄–NH]–. A sample of Kevlar has an average Mr of
1.19 × 10⁵. Calculate the average number of repeating units per chain. **[2]**
**(c)** State one typical use of Kevlar. **[1]**

**8.** This question is about the breakdown of polymers.

**(a)** The polyester from Question 3 is heated with aqueous sodium hydroxide. Name the two organic
products. **[2]**
**(b)** Nylon 6,6 is heated with dilute hydrochloric acid. Name the two organic products. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why poly(ethene) is not broken down by heating with aqueous sodium hydroxide. **[2]**
**(d)** State which of the polymers in (a), (b) and (c) is non-biodegradable. **[1]**

**9.** In a school laboratory, nylon 6,6 is made from hexanedioyl dichloride, ClCO(CH₂)₄COCl, and
hexane-1,6-diamine.

**(a)** Write an equation for the formation of nylon 6,6 by this route. Show the repeating unit. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage atom economy of this route, taking two molecules of by-product per
repeating unit. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest why the diacyl dichloride is used instead of hexanedioic acid. **[1]**

**10.** Terylene is made from benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid and ethane-1,2-diol.

**(a)** Calculate the Mr of the repeating unit of Terylene. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the mass of water formed when 9.60 g of Terylene is made. Assume two water molecules
form per repeating unit. **[2]**

**11.** A town collects mixed plastic waste containing poly(propene), PVC and nylon 6,6. Evaluate
landfill, incineration and mechanical recycling as ways of disposing of this waste. **[7]**

**12.** A section of polymer Q is
–CO–(CH₂)₃–CO–NH–(CH₂)₅–NH–CO–(CH₂)₃–CO–NH–(CH₂)₅–NH–

**(a)** Name the two monomers used to make Q. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the Mr of the repeating unit of Q. Show your working. **[2]**
**(c)** Name the strongest type of force between chains of Q and explain how it arises. **[2]**
**(d)** Q is heated with excess aqueous sodium hydroxide. Name the two organic products. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** **C** [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* A diamine plus a dicarboxylic acid gives amide links; A gives a polyester and B and D
are alkenes or a diol that cannot form amide links.

**2. (a)** **Ester** [1]
**(b)** **Water** [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Hydrogen and oxygen" or "OH" is not accepted for (b); the product must be named
as water or written as H₂O.

**3.** Ester links correct at both ends with one residue of each monomer [1]; extension bonds and n
shown: **–[CO–C₆H₄–CO–O–CH₂CH₂CH₂–O]ₙ–** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A unit that keeps an –OH or –H at either end, or contains two diol residues, loses
the first mark.

**4. (a)** **–[CO–(CH₂)₂–CO–NH–C₆H₄–NH]–** with extension bonds [1]
**(b)** **HOOC–(CH₂)₂–COOH, butanedioic acid** [1]; **H₂N–C₆H₄–NH₂, benzene-1,4-diamine** [1]
**(c)** **Polyamide** [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b) a correct structure with the wrong name, or the right name for the wrong
ring positions, is not given the mark; check both.

**5. (a)** **H₂N–CH₂–COOH** [1] and **H₂N–CH(CH₃)–COOH** [1]
**(b)** **Water** [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Drawing the residues, –NH–CH₂–CO–, instead of the full amino acids does not score;
add H to N and OH to C.

**6.** Both have van der Waals forces between chains [1]. Nylon 6,6 also forms hydrogen bonds between the
N–H of one chain and a lone pair on the C=O oxygen of another [1]. Hydrogen bonds are stronger, so more
energy is needed to separate the chains of nylon 6,6 [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Any mention of breaking covalent bonds in the chain on melting contradicts the
answer and can cost the final mark.

**7. (a)** The N–H bond is polar, so the H is δ+ [1]; it is attracted to a lone pair on the δ− oxygen of
a C=O group on the next chain [1].
**(b)** Mr(C₁₄H₁₀N₂O₂) = 14(12.0) + 10(1.0) + 2(14.0) + 2(16.0) = 238.0 [1]; 1.19 × 10⁵ / 238.0 =
**500** [1].
**(c)** Bulletproof vests or body armour (or ropes) [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* In (a) a hydrogen bond drawn between two N–H groups, or between H and the N of
another chain, does not score.

**8. (a)** **Sodium benzene-1,4-dicarboxylate** [1]; **propane-1,3-diol** [1]
**(b)** **Hexanedioic acid** [1]; **hexane-1,6-diammonium chloride** (the salt of the diamine) [1]
**(c)** Poly(ethene) has only C–C and C–H bonds, which are non-polar [1], so there is no δ+ carbon for
the hydroxide ion (nucleophile) to attack [1].
**(d)** **Poly(ethene)** [1] **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b) the free diamine is not accepted, because in acid the amine groups are
protonated; in (a) the free acid is not accepted in alkali.

**9. (a)** n ClCO(CH₂)₄COCl + n H₂N(CH₂)₆NH₂ → **–[CO–(CH₂)₄–CO–NH–(CH₂)₆–NH]ₙ–** [1] **+ 2n HCl** [1]
**(b)** Mr: ClCO(CH₂)₄COCl = 183.0, diamine = 116.0, repeating unit = 226.0 [1]; atom economy =
226.0 / (183.0 + 116.0) × 100 = **75.6%** [1]
**(c)** The acyl chloride is more reactive, so the reaction is fast at room temperature [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Allow error carried forward in (b) from a wrong Mr, but a by-product of water in
(a) loses that mark because the reactant is an acyl chloride.

**10. (a)** C₁₀H₈O₄: **192.0** [1]
**(b)** Repeating units = 9.60 / 192.0 = 0.0500 mol, so water = 0.100 mol [1]; mass of water =
0.100 × 18.0 = **1.80 g** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Using one water per repeating unit halves the answer; a wrong Mr from (a) can
usually be carried forward, but a wrong mole ratio cannot.

**11.** Landfill: simple and needs no sorting of the mixed waste [1]; but uses land, and poly(propene)
and PVC are non-biodegradable so persist for a very long time [1].
Incineration: reduces the volume and the heat can generate electricity [1]; but releases CO₂, a
greenhouse gas [1], and burning PVC gives toxic HCl, which must be removed from the gases [1].
Mechanical recycling: saves crude oil and energy and reduces landfill [1]; but the three polymers must be
collected, sorted by type and cleaned, which costs money and energy, and quality falls each cycle [1].
**[7]**
*Examiner insight:* An evaluation needs an advantage and a disadvantage for each method; a list of only
problems, or "it is bad for the environment" with no chemistry, limits the credit.

**12. (a)** **Pentanedioic acid** [1]; **pentane-1,5-diamine** [1]
**(b)** Repeating unit C₁₀H₁₈N₂O₂ [1]; Mr = 10(12.0) + 18(1.0) + 2(14.0) + 2(16.0) = **198.0** [1]
(check: 132.0 + 102.0 − 2 × 18.0 = 198.0)
**(c)** **Hydrogen bonds** [1], between the δ+ H of N–H on one chain and a lone pair on the C=O oxygen of
another chain [1].
**(d)** **Sodium pentanedioate** [1]; **pentane-1,5-diamine** [1] **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* In (a) the number of carbons in each residue must be counted carefully: the acid
residue –CO–(CH₂)₃–CO– has five carbons, not three.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Repeating units with only one monomer residue when two monomers are used.
- Ends of a repeating unit drawn with –OH or –H instead of extension bonds.
- Writing –CO–NH₂– or –CO–OH– as a linkage.
- Showing water as the by-product when the reactant is an acyl chloride.
- Hydrogen bonds drawn between two N–H groups instead of N–H and C=O.
- Saying polyesters form hydrogen bonds between chains.
- Free amine given as a product of acid hydrolysis, or free acid from alkaline hydrolysis.
- Explaining polyalkene inertness with "weak bonds" rather than non-polar bonds and no δ+ carbon.
- Disposal answers without a disadvantage for each method.
- Miscounting carbons when naming monomers from a chain section.
