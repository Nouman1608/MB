## Answers

**1.** Pentanoic acid [1].

**2.** LiAlH₄ (lithium tetrahydridoaluminate) [1].

**3.** Orange to green [1].

**4.** CH₃COOH + NaHCO₃ → CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂ [1].

**5.** Redox [1]; magnesium propanoate and hydrogen [1].

**6. (a)** Acidified potassium dichromate(VI) (or acidified KMnO₄) [1]; heat under reflux [1].

**(b)** CH₃CH₂CH₂CN + NaOH + H₂O → CH₃CH₂CH₂COONa + NH₃ [1]; CH₃CH₂CH₂COONa + HCl →
CH₃CH₂CH₂COOH + NaCl [1].

**(c)** Methanol [1].

**7. (a)** 2CH₃CH₂CH₂COOH + Mg → (CH₃CH₂CH₂COO)₂Mg + H₂ [1].

**(b)** Mr = 4(12.0) + 8(1.0) + 2(16.0) = 88.0, so n(acid) = 2.20 / 88.0 = 0.0250 mol; n(H₂) = 0.0125 mol
[1]. V = 0.0125 × 24.0 = 0.300 dm³ (300 cm³) [1].

**8. (a)** CH₃CH₂COOH + NaOH → CH₃CH₂COONa + H₂O [1].

**(b)** n(NaOH) = 0.0200 × 0.100 = 0.00200 mol = n(acid) [1]; concentration = 0.00200 / 0.0250 =
0.0800 mol dm⁻³ [1].

**9.** Add sodium carbonate solution to each: only propanoic acid gives effervescence (CO₂) [1]; the other
two show no change [1]. Add a small piece of sodium to the two remaining liquids: propan-1-ol gives
effervescence (H₂) [1]; propanal gives no effervescence [1].

**10. (a)** Concentrated sulfuric acid [1]; the reaction is reversible (reaches equilibrium), so it does not
go to completion [1].

**(b)** CH₃CH₂COOH + CH₃CH₂CH₂OH ⇌ CH₃CH₂COOCH₂CH₂CH₃ + H₂O [1]; propyl propanoate [1].

**11.** Step 1: heat 1-bromopropane with KCN in ethanol [1], giving butanenitrile, CH₃CH₂CH₂CN [1].
Step 2: heat under reflux with dilute acid (or with dilute alkali followed by acidification) to hydrolyse
the nitrile to butanoic acid [1].

**12. (a)** 2-methylpropanoic acid, (CH₃)₂CHCOOH [1].

**(b)** (CH₃)₂CHCOOH + 4[H] → (CH₃)₂CHCH₂OH + H₂O [1].

**13.** Propan-2-ol is a secondary alcohol, so it is oxidised only as far as a ketone, which is not
oxidised further [1]; the product is propanone [1].

**14. (a)** Propanoic acid, CH₃CH₂COOH: the only carboxylic acid with this formula, shown by the CO₂
effervescence [1].

**(b)** Ethyl propanoate [1], CH₃CH₂COOCH₂CH₃ [1].

**(c)** Propan-1-ol [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "distil" instead of "reflux" for oxidation to a carboxylic acid.
- Leaving out the acidification step after alkaline hydrolysis.
- Using the 1 : 1 ratio for sodium carbonate or for metals, where 2 mol of acid give 1 mol of gas.
- Writing the magnesium salt with one carboxylate ion.
- Naming NaBH₄ for the reduction of a carboxylic acid.
- Testing with sodium first: acids and alcohols both fizz with sodium, so it cannot separate them.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I identify an unknown CₙH₂ₙO₂ compound?**
CₙH₂ₙO₂ is either a carboxylic acid or an ester. Effervescence with sodium hydrogencarbonate shows the
acid; an ester gives no gas, but heating it with dilute alkali and then acidifying gives a carboxylic acid
and an alcohol that can be identified separately.

**What observation proves a compound is a carboxylic acid?**
Effervescence with sodium carbonate or sodium hydrogencarbonate. The gas, CO₂, turns limewater milky.
Alcohols and aldehydes give no gas with carbonates.

**Why is the alkaline route to a carboxylic acid followed by acidification?**
In alkali the acid is deprotonated as it forms, so the product is the carboxylate salt. A strong acid
such as HCl then supplies H⁺ to give the free carboxylic acid.
