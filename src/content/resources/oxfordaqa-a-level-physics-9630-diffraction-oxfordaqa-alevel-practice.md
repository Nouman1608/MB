These questions cover section **3.5.7 Diffraction** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630) specification: single-slit patterns with monochromatic and white light, the qualitative width of the central maximum, the plane transmission grating at normal incidence and the "derivation of d sin θ = nλ", and applications of gratings. Marks are shown for each part. All data are invented for practice, and all gratings are used at normal incidence. Take white light as 400 nm to 700 nm.

Revise first with the [diffraction study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-diffraction-oxfordaqa-alevel/) and the [diffraction revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-diffraction-oxfordaqa-alevel-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** State what is meant by diffraction. **[1]**

**2.** White light passes through a single narrow slit. Which colour appears on the outer edge of each side maximum?

A: violet

B: blue

C: green

D: red **[1]**

**3.** Light of wavelength 589 nm falls on a grating with 450 lines per mm. Calculate the angle of the first-order maximum. **[2]**

**4.** A grating has a spacing of 1.25 × 10⁻⁶ m and is lit with light of wavelength 532 nm. Calculate the total number of maxima produced. **[2]**

**5.** A grating is replaced by one with more lines per millimetre. For the same light, the first-order angle

A: decreases

B: stays the same

C: increases

D: becomes zero **[1]**

**6.** Explain why you can hear someone talking in the next room through an open doorway 0.9 m wide but cannot see them unless you are in line with the door. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Light from a lamp falls on a grating with 500 lines per mm. Two bright second-order lines are seen at 29.1° and 41.0° from the straight-through direction.

**(a)** Calculate the grating spacing. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the two wavelengths. **[3]**

**(c)** Determine whether a third-order maximum of the longer wavelength can be observed. **[2]**

**8.** A laser beam is shone through a single narrow slit onto a screen.

**(a)** Describe the pattern on the screen. **[2]**

**(b)** Describe and explain how the pattern changes when the slit is made narrower. **[2]**

**(c)** The red laser is replaced by a blue laser and the slit is unchanged. State how the width of the central maximum changes. **[1]**

**9.** Derive the equation d sin θ = nλ for a plane transmission grating at normal incidence. You may refer to a sketch in your answer. **[4]**

**10.** White light falls on a grating with 600 lines per mm.

**(a)** Calculate the angles at which the first-order spectrum begins and ends. **[2]**

**(b)** Show that the second-order and third-order spectra overlap. **[2]**

**(c)** State how many complete spectra are produced on each side of the centre. **[1]**

**11.** A student uses a laser of wavelength 633 nm and a grating of unknown spacing. She measures the angle between the left and right first-order maxima as 44.6°.

**(a)** Calculate the number of lines per mm on the grating. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why measuring between the left and right maxima is better than measuring one angle. **[1]**

**12.**

**(a)** Explain why a diffraction grating, rather than a double slit, is used to measure the wavelengths in a line spectrum. **[2]**

**(b)** Give two applications of diffraction gratings. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** The spreading out of a wave as it passes through a gap or around an obstacle [1].

**2.** **D** [1]. Longer wavelengths are diffracted through larger angles.

**3.** d = 1/(450 × 10³) = 2.22 × 10⁻⁶ m [1]; sin θ = 589 × 10⁻⁹/2.22 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.265, so θ = **15.4°** [1]

**4.** d/λ = 1.25 × 10⁻⁶/532 × 10⁻⁹ = 2.35, so the highest order is 2 [1]; maxima at n = 0, ±1, ±2 gives **5 maxima** [1]

**5.** **C** [1]. More lines per mm means smaller d, so sin θ = λ/d is larger.

**6.** Sound has a wavelength similar to the width of the doorway, so it diffracts strongly and spreads into the room [1]; light has a wavelength about a million times smaller than the gap, so it passes through with almost no spreading [1].

**7.**

**(a)** d = 1/(500 × 10³) = **2.0 × 10⁻⁶ m** [1]

**(b)** λ = d sin θ/n, with n = 2 [1]; λ₁ = 2.0 × 10⁻⁶ × sin 29.1°/2 = **486 nm** [1]; λ₂ = 2.0 × 10⁻⁶ × sin 41.0°/2 = **656 nm** [1]

**(c)** sin θ = 3 × 656 × 10⁻⁹/2.0 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.984 [1]; this is less than 1, so the maximum exists, at **79.7°** [1]

**8.**

**(a)** A bright central maximum, about twice as wide as the others [1], with much dimmer side maxima separated by dark minima on each side [1].

**(b)** The central maximum, and the whole pattern, becomes **wider** (and dimmer) [1], because diffraction is greater when the slit width is closer to the wavelength [1].

**(c)** It becomes **narrower** [1].

**9.** At normal incidence the waves leave all the slits in phase [1]. For waves leaving adjacent slits, a distance d apart, at angle θ to the normal, the path difference is d sin θ [1] (the side of a right-angled triangle with hypotenuse d). For a maximum, waves from adjacent slits must arrive in phase [1], so the path difference is a whole number of wavelengths: d sin θ = nλ, and then waves from all the slits reinforce [1].

**10.**

**(a)** d = 1.667 × 10⁻⁶ m; violet: sin θ = 400 × 10⁻⁹/1.667 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.240, θ = **13.9°** [1]; red: sin θ = 0.420, θ = **24.8°** [1]

**(b)** Second-order red: sin θ = 0.840, θ = 57.1° [1]; third-order violet: sin θ = 0.720, θ = 46.1°, which is smaller than 57.1°, so the spectra overlap [1].

**(c)** **Two** [1]. For red, d/λ = 2.38, so a complete third-order spectrum cannot form.

**11.**

**(a)** θ = 44.6°/2 = 22.3° [1]; d = 633 × 10⁻⁹/sin 22.3° = 1.67 × 10⁻⁶ m [1]; N = 1/d = 6.0 × 10⁵ per metre = **600 lines per mm** [1]

**(b)** It removes the error from not knowing the exact straight-through position, and halving the larger angle reduces the percentage uncertainty [1].

**12.**

**(a)** A grating gives much sharper, brighter maxima [1], at larger angles, so the angles and therefore the wavelengths can be measured far more precisely [1].

**(b)** Measuring the wavelength of light [1]; identifying elements from their line spectra, in the laboratory or in starlight [1]. Also accept X-ray diffraction to study the arrangement of atoms in crystals.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using lines per mm directly as d (Questions 3, 7 and 10).
- Rounding d/λ up or forgetting to count both sides and the zero order (Question 4).
- Saying a narrower slit gives a narrower pattern (Question 8).
- Missing "normal incidence" or "adjacent slits" in the derivation (Question 9).
- Using the full 44.6° as θ (Question 11).
- Saying light "does not diffract" through a doorway; it does, but the spreading is negligible (Question 6).
