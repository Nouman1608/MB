## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE INTERNATIONAL AS & A LEVEL PHYSICS 9702 — AS Level, Topic 3.1**

Candidates should be able to:

1. understand that mass is the property of an object that resists change in motion;
2. recall F = ma and solve problems using it, understanding that acceleration and resultant force are always in the same direction;
3. define and use linear momentum as the product of mass and velocity;
4. define and use force as rate of change of momentum;
5. state and apply each of Newton's laws of motion;
6. describe and use the concept of weight as the effect of a gravitational field on a mass and recall that the weight of an object is equal to the product of its mass and the acceleration of free fall.

## Mass resists change in motion

Mass is not "the amount of stuff" in this syllabus: it is the property that **resists change in motion** (inertia). A loaded supermarket trolley is harder to start, stop and steer than an empty one, even on a perfectly smooth floor where its weight plays no part. That is why the same resultant force gives a smaller acceleration to a larger mass.

## F = ma: resultant force and direction

The F in F = ma is the **resultant** force, the vector sum of every force on the object. Two consequences:

- **Acceleration is always in the direction of the resultant force, not necessarily the direction of motion.** A car moving forwards while braking has a backward resultant force and a backward acceleration.
- **Zero resultant force means zero acceleration**, so the object is at rest or moving at constant velocity.

### Method for any F = ma problem

1. Choose the object (or system) and draw a free-body diagram showing only forces **on** it.
2. Choose a positive direction, usually the direction of acceleration.
3. Write resultant force = (forces in the positive direction) − (forces in the negative direction).
4. Set the resultant equal to ma and solve.

### Worked example 1: a person in a lift

A 65 kg person stands on bathroom scales in a lift. The scales read the normal contact force R. Take g = 9.81 m s⁻².

```
accelerating upwards at 1.2 m s^-2:   R - mg = ma
   R = m(g + a) = 65 x (9.81 + 1.2) = 716 N
moving at constant velocity:          R = mg = 65 x 9.81 = 638 N
moving up but slowing at 1.2 m s^-2:  mg - R = ma   (acceleration is downwards)
   R = m(g - a) = 65 x (9.81 - 1.2) = 560 N
```

The person's weight is 638 N throughout; only the contact force changes. The third case shows the direction rule: moving up but accelerating down.

### Worked example 2: connected bodies

A car of mass 1100 kg tows a trailer of mass 400 kg. The resultant forward force on the car-and-trailer system is 3000 N. Find the acceleration and the tension in the tow bar.

```
whole system:   a = F / m = 3000 / (1100 + 400) = 2.0 m s^-2
trailer alone:  T = m_trailer x a = 400 x 2.0 = 800 N
```

Treating the system as one object hides the tow-bar tension, because it is an internal force; treating the trailer alone reveals it.

## Linear momentum and force as rate of change of momentum

**Linear momentum** p = mv. It is a vector, with the direction of the velocity, and its unit is kg m s⁻¹ (equivalent to N s).

Newton's second law in its general form: **the resultant force on an object is equal to its rate of change of momentum**:

```
F = delta p / delta t
```

When mass is constant, Δp/Δt = m Δv/Δt = ma, so F = ma is a special case.

### Worked example 3: braking car

A 1200 kg car slows uniformly from 20 m s⁻¹ to rest in 5.0 s.

```
delta p = 0 - (1200 x 20) = -24 000 kg m s^-1
F = delta p / delta t = -24 000 / 5.0 = -4800 N
```

The negative sign shows the force is opposite to the motion.

### Worked example 4: a steady stream of mass

Water leaves a hose at 12 m s⁻¹ and hits a wall at a rate of 1.5 kg every second, stopping dead. The mass of the water is not a single fixed object, so use the momentum form:

```
momentum destroyed each second = (delta m / delta t) x v = 1.5 x 12 = 18 kg m s^-2
force of wall on water = 18 N (backwards); by the third law, water pushes the wall with 18 N
```

The same reasoning gives the force needed to keep a conveyor belt moving at 0.80 m s⁻¹ while sand drops onto it at 25 kg s⁻¹: each second, 25 kg of sand gains 0.80 m s⁻¹, so F = 25 × 0.80 = 20 N.

## Newton's three laws, stated precisely

1. **First law:** an object remains at rest or moves with constant velocity unless acted on by a resultant force.
2. **Second law:** the resultant force on an object is proportional to (and, in SI units, equal to) its rate of change of momentum, and is in the same direction.
3. **Third law:** if body A exerts a force on body B, then body B exerts a force on body A that is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.

### Identifying third-law pairs

A third-law pair acts on **two different bodies**, is of the **same type** of force, and has equal magnitude and opposite direction. For a book on a table:

| Force | Its third-law partner |
|---|---|
| Earth's gravitational pull on the book (the book's weight) | The book's gravitational pull on the Earth |
| The table's upward contact force on the book | The book's downward contact force on the table |

The book's weight and the table's contact force on the book are equal and opposite only because the book is in equilibrium. They act on the same body and are different types, so they are not a third-law pair.

## Weight

Weight is the **effect of a gravitational field on a mass**: the gravitational force on it, W = mg, where g is the acceleration of free fall. On Earth g = 9.81 m s⁻² (given on the data sheet). A 70 kg astronaut has weight 687 N on Earth; on the Moon, where g is about 1.6 m s⁻², the weight is about 110 N, but the mass, and so the resistance to change in motion, is unchanged.

## Common mistakes

- **Using one force instead of the resultant in F = ma.**
- **Assuming acceleration is in the direction of motion.**
- **Naming weight and normal contact force as a third-law pair.**
- **Forgetting that momentum is a vector**, so a change of direction changes momentum.
- **Stating the first law as "an object at rest stays at rest"** without the constant-velocity part.
- **Writing weight in kilograms.** Weight is a force, in newtons.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **State** a law: use the precise wording; "every action has an equal and opposite reaction" earns little.
- **Define** momentum: "the product of mass and velocity", not "mass times speed".
- **Explain** a motion: name the forces, state whether the resultant is zero, and link it to the acceleration through the second law.
- **Calculate**: show a free-body equation (resultant force = ma) before substituting.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is F = ma always true?**
It applies when mass is constant. When mass flows, use force = rate of change of momentum.

**Why are weight and the normal contact force not a third-law pair?**
They act on the same object and are different types of force. Third-law pairs act on different objects and are the same type.

**What is the difference between mass and weight?**
Mass resists change in motion and is the same everywhere; weight is the gravitational force on the mass, W = mg, and depends on the field.
