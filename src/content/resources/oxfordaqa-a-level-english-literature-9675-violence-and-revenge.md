Among the aspects of tragedy the 9675 specification suggests for Unit 1 is "the significance of
violence and revenge". This guide applies that aspect to the Section B essay on later dramatic
tragedies. It builds on the [Unit 1 Section B study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-english-literature-9675-unit-1-section-b/),
which explains the essay task and how later tragedy shifts from public to domestic suffering. Here
the focus is narrower: what violence looks like in these plays, why so much of it happens offstage,
and what has happened to revenge.

## Why this aspect needs rethinking for later plays

In Elizabethan and Jacobean revenge tragedy, violence is public and spectacular, and revenge is a
plot engine: a wrong demands repayment, and the final act fills the stage with bodies. The Section B
plays keep the violence but change its form in three ways.

- **Violence moves offstage or into the past.** Several decisive deaths are heard, reported or
  remembered rather than shown. The audience must imagine them, and the imagined act often weighs
  more than a staged one.
- **Violence turns inward.** Characters destroy themselves, or are destroyed by pressures (money,
  class, marriage, empire) that have no single agent to blame.
- **Revenge shrinks or disappears.** It survives as spite, humiliation and small acts of power,
  rather than as a sacred duty. In some plays the absence of any avenger is itself significant; the
  specification reminds you that "the absence of an 'aspect' can be as significant as its presence".

## Play by play

**Death of a Salesman.** The only fight on stage is a game: Willy's brother Ben spars with the
teenage Biff, trips him and tells him never to fight fair with a stranger. The lesson, offered as
fatherly wisdom, sums up the play's view of the American struggle for success as a hidden kind of
violence. The real violence is turned inward. The rubber tube behind the heater warns early that
Willy is planning to kill himself, and his death in the car happens offstage, marked by sound. Willy
also reads Biff's failure as revenge, accusing him of acting out of spite; the play suggests the
grievance is real, since Biff's discovery of Willy's affair in Boston ended his belief in his
father. The firing scene with Howard shows an economic violence with no blow struck, summed up in
Willy's protest that a man is not "a piece of fruit".

**A Streetcar Named Desire.** Violence is shown and threatened throughout. On poker night Stanley
throws the radio out of the window and hits Stella; she returns to him the same night.
Stanley's later campaign against Blanche works as revenge: he has overheard her describe him as
animal-like, and he answers by exposing her past to Mitch and giving her a bus ticket home as a
birthday present. The rape at the climax happens behind the closing scene, with Stanley's line that
they have had this date from the beginning presenting the violence as fated. Past violence matters
too: the suicide of Blanche's young husband, after her public cruelty to him, is the event she
cannot escape, and her own view that deliberate cruelty is unforgivable judges her as well as
Stanley.

**Hedda Gabler.** General Gabler's pistols are both a prop and a sign of the inheritance Hedda
cannot use as a woman. She fires at Judge Brack in the garden as a joke, which tells the audience
the pistols are loaded and that Hedda is dangerous before she does anything serious. Her worst act
uses fire rather than a gun: she burns Lovborg's manuscript, the book he wrote with Thea, imagining
it as their child. Both deaths happen offstage. Lovborg's, reported by Brack, is sordid rather than
the beautiful end Hedda wanted; Hedda's own shot, from the inner room, cuts off Tesman and Thea's
work on the manuscript. Revenge appears as old resentment of Thea, dating back to school, and
Brack's discovery of the pistol gives him the power to blackmail Hedda, which she ends by killing
herself.

**Waiting for Godot.** Violence is constant but unexplained. Estragon is beaten each night by people
the audience never sees, for reasons no one gives; Pozzo drives Lucky with a whip and a rope;
Vladimir and Estragon twice consider hanging themselves from the tree. No one avenges anything,
and no wrong is ever named. The suffering is real but has no plot, which is why the play's
violence can be read as part of the human condition rather than as anyone's crime.

**Translations.** The violence of empire is mostly quiet: renaming places on the Ordnance Survey map
is presented as damage to a language and memory. It becomes physical when Lieutenant Yolland
disappears after the dance, and Captain Lancey threatens to shoot the livestock and then evict
families and level their homes if Yolland is not found. The attack is implied, never shown, and
local resistance appears in smaller acts, from moving the surveyors' poles to the burning of the
army camp. Hugh's memory of marching off to the 1798 rising, and turning back for home, sets
heroic violence in the past and questions it.

## Building an essay on violence and revenge

1. **Separate the forms.** Physical violence, verbal or psychological violence, institutional or
   economic violence, and violence against the self. Most plays use more than one.
2. **Ask where it happens.** On stage, offstage, in the past or only threatened. Then ask why the
   playwright made that choice and what it does to an audience.
3. **Ask what has replaced revenge.** Spite, humiliation, blackmail, sabotage, or nothing at all.
4. **Tie it to the tragic journey.** Does violence cause the fall, express it or follow from it?
5. **End on the audience.** Shock, pity, unease, complicity: the specification's "pity and fear"
   may be redirected towards social or psychological causes.

## Worked example on an original scene

The scene below is **original**, written for this guide in the style of a modern domestic tragedy.

> *A kitchen at night. DENNIS, fifty, in his work coat, holds a letter. His daughter LIZ, twenty,
> stands by the door with her suitcase.*
> **DENNIS:** They let me go. Thirty years, and a letter.
> **LIZ:** Dad, I'm sorry.
> **DENNIS:** Don't be sorry. Be here. *(He tears the letter slowly into strips and lays them on
> the table in a row.)* There. Now it's tidy.
> **LIZ:** I'll miss the train.
> **DENNIS:** *(not looking up)* Go on, then. Everybody goes.

**Model paragraph:**

> Violence in the scene is displaced from people onto paper, which shows how the play turns economic
> injury into private damage. The firm that dismissed Dennis is never seen; its blow arrives as "a
> letter", and the bare contrast with "Thirty years" measures a working life against a single page.
> The stage direction makes him tear the letter "slowly into strips" and lay them "in a row": the
> controlled movement is more disturbing than an outburst, suggesting anger that has nowhere to go
> and is turned into a grim order. "Now it's tidy" is ironic, since nothing in his life is. His
> treatment of Liz carries the closest thing to revenge, a quiet guilt laid on the one person who
> can be hurt, so that "Everybody goes" punishes her for the firm's decision. The audience's pity is
> unsettled by unease, because the injury he suffers is passed on rather than avenged.

The paragraph identifies the form of violence (economic, then emotional), where it happens
(offstage, through an object), what replaces revenge (blame passed to a daughter) and the effect on
the audience.

## Common mistakes

- **Counting violent incidents.** A list of attacks is not an argument about significance.
- **Ignoring offstage events.** What is heard, reported or remembered is still authorial method.
- **Assuming revenge must be present.** Its absence, in *Godot* especially, is analysable.
- **Moral summaries.** "Stanley is a bad man" earns little; analyse how the play stages his power.
- **Losing the tragic frame.** Always connect violence back to the protagonist's fall and the audience.

## Exam technique

Section B questions are essays with no printed extract, so your evidence comes from memory across
the play. For this aspect, prepare one moment of staged violence, one offstage or past act, and one
moment where revenge, spite or blackmail operates, each with a short quotation or precise reference
to a stage direction. If the question offers a view, for example that violence in the play is
"senseless", test it against these three moments rather than agreeing at once.
