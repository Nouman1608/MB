## P5.3a: what happens when a wave meets a substance (Higher Tier only)

When an electromagnetic wave reaches a boundary or travels through a material, four things can happen:

- **Absorbed:** the wave's energy is transferred to the material, usually warming it.
- **Transmitted:** the wave passes through.
- **Reflected:** the wave bounces off the surface.
- **Refracted:** the wave changes direction as it crosses into a new substance.

Often several happen at once. The mix depends on the substance and on the **wavelength** of the wave. The same material can treat different parts of the spectrum in very different ways.

Examples you can use:

- Clear window glass transmits visible light but absorbs much of the ultraviolet.
- Water in food absorbs microwaves, which is why they heat it. Many plastics and ceramics transmit them.
- Metal surfaces reflect radio waves and microwaves well.
- Your skin absorbs infrared, which you feel as warmth.

A good answer names the substance, names the type of wave and says what happens. "Glass blocks light" is too vague, because glass transmits visible light.

## P5.3b: wave velocity and refraction (Higher Tier only)

Electromagnetic waves travel fastest in a vacuum (about 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s, and almost as fast in air). In denser transparent materials such as glass, perspex or water they travel more slowly. Changing speed is the cause of **refraction**.

When a wave crosses into a new substance:

- The **frequency** stays the same, because it is set by the source.
- The **speed** changes.
- Since wave speed = frequency × wavelength (v = f λ), the **wavelength** changes in proportion to the speed.

So when light slows down in glass, its wavelength gets shorter.

### Why the direction changes

Think of wavefronts. If a wave meets a boundary at an angle, one end of each wavefront reaches the new substance first and slows down while the other end is still travelling fast. The wavefront swings round, and the direction of travel bends.

- Entering a substance where the wave is **slower** (air to glass): the ray bends **towards** the normal.
- Entering a substance where the wave is **faster** (glass to air): the ray bends **away from** the normal.
- Along the normal (angle of incidence 0°): the speed changes but there is no bend.

The **normal** is a line at 90° to the surface at the point where the ray hits. All angles are measured from the normal, never from the surface.

### Worked example 1: wavelength in glass

Red light has frequency 4.8 × 10¹⁴ Hz. Its speed in air is 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s. In a block of glass its speed is 2.0 × 10⁸ m/s. Find its wavelength in air and in the glass.

```
In air:    λ = v ÷ f = 3.00 × 10⁸ ÷ 4.8 × 10¹⁴
             = 6.25 × 10⁻⁷ m  = 625 nm
In glass:  λ = 2.0 × 10⁸ ÷ 4.8 × 10¹⁴
             = 4.17 × 10⁻⁷ m  = 417 nm
```

The frequency is unchanged at 4.8 × 10¹⁴ Hz. The wavelength fell to two-thirds of its value because the speed fell to two-thirds.

### Worked example 2: finding a speed

Blue light has frequency 6.5 × 10¹⁴ Hz. In air its wavelength is 462 nm. In water its wavelength is 346 nm. Find its speed in water.

```
v = f × λ = 6.5 × 10¹⁴ × 346 × 10⁻⁹
  = 2.25 × 10⁸ m/s
```

Check: in air, 6.5 × 10¹⁴ × 462 × 10⁻⁹ = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s, so light is slower in water, as expected.

### Worked example 3: a radio-type calculation

A microwave oven uses waves of frequency 2.45 × 10⁹ Hz. Find the wavelength in air.

```
λ = 3.00 × 10⁸ ÷ 2.45 × 10⁹ = 0.122 m
```

The same equation works across the whole electromagnetic spectrum. Always convert nanometres to metres (× 10⁻⁹) before you use it.

## P5.3d: ray diagrams for reflection and refraction

You need to **construct** two-dimensional ray diagrams. They are qualitative: no equations are needed, but the geometry must be right.

### Reflection at a plane mirror

The law of reflection says the **angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection**, each one taken from the normal.

Steps:

1. Draw the mirror as a straight line.
2. Draw the normal at the point of incidence, at 90° to the mirror (dashed).
3. Draw the incident ray with an arrow, hitting that point.
4. Draw the reflected ray on the other side of the normal at the same angle.
5. Label the two angles.

If a ray hits the mirror at 35° to the normal, it leaves at 35° to the normal. That is 55° to the mirror surface, which is the commonest slip when you measure from the wrong line.

### Refraction at a boundary and through a block

Steps for a ray going from air into a rectangular glass block:

1. Draw the boundary and the normal at the entry point.
2. Draw the incident ray in the air.
3. Inside the glass, draw the ray **bending towards the normal**, so the angle is smaller than in the air.
4. At the exit face draw a new normal. The ray **bends away from the normal** on leaving.
5. The emergent ray is **parallel** to the incident ray but **shifted sideways**.

Reasons: the two bends are equal and opposite because the two faces are parallel. A ray that hits at 0° goes straight through, with no sideways shift.

A faint reflected ray also appears at each surface. Add one if the question asks about reflection as well as refraction.

## P5.3c: convex and concave lenses

A lens refracts light at both surfaces. Its shape decides what it does to a beam. Qualitative work only is required.

| | Convex (converging) lens | Concave (diverging) lens |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | Thicker in the middle | Thinner in the middle |
| Parallel rays | Bend inwards and meet at the **principal focus** | Spread outwards, as if coming from a point on the incoming side |
| Image | Real or virtual, depending on object position | Always virtual, upright and smaller |

**Similarities:** both refract rays at the two surfaces; both have a principal focus on each side of the lens; a ray through the centre passes straight on.

**Differences:** a convex lens brings rays together and a concave lens spreads them out. A convex lens can form a real image on a screen; a concave lens cannot.

### Drawing rays through a convex lens

Draw the lens as a vertical line with outward arrows (for convex) or inward arrows (for concave). Mark the principal focus F on both sides. Use two or three of these rays from the top of the object:

1. A ray **parallel to the axis** bends through F on the far side.
2. A ray **through the centre** of the lens carries straight on.
3. A ray **through F** on the object side emerges parallel to the axis.

Where the rays cross is the top of the **image**. Drop a line to the axis to draw the whole image.

| Object position (convex lens) | Image |
|---|---|
| Beyond twice the focal length (2F) | Real, inverted, smaller, between F and 2F |
| Between F and 2F | Real, inverted, larger, beyond 2F |
| Closer than F | Virtual, upright, larger (a magnifying glass) |

For a **concave** lens the parallel ray spreads out as if it came from F on the object side; trace it backwards with a dashed line. The image is on the same side as the object.

### Using lenses to correct vision

- **Short sight:** the eye focuses distant objects in front of the retina. A concave lens spreads the rays slightly first, so the image forms on the retina.
- **Long sight:** the eye focuses near objects behind the retina. A convex lens converges the rays slightly first, so the image forms on the retina.

The specification's practical suggestions include investigating how a convex lens image of a bulb changes with distance. Your table above predicts the results.

## P5.3e: colour

White light is a mixture of all visible colours. Colour depends on frequency. The primary colours of light are **red, green and blue**. Adding two gives a secondary colour: red + green = yellow, green + blue = cyan, red + blue = magenta.

### Coloured filters

A filter does not add colour. It **transmits** its own colour and **absorbs** the rest.

- A red filter in white light transmits red only.
- A magenta filter transmits red and blue and absorbs green.
- Two filters in series transmit only what both transmit. Red then green leaves nothing, so you see black.

### Coloured objects

An opaque object appears the colour of the light it **reflects** after absorbing the rest.

- A red book in white light reflects red and absorbs other colours.
- In green light, the same book has no red to reflect. It absorbs the green and looks black.
- White objects reflect all visible colours. Black objects absorb nearly all of them.

### Specular reflection and scattering

- **Specular reflection** happens at a smooth surface such as a mirror. Parallel rays stay parallel, so you see an image.
- **Scattering** (diffuse reflection) happens at a rough surface. The normal changes from point to point, so reflected rays leave in many directions. This is why you can read a page from any angle.

Ordinary coloured objects scatter their colour. A glossy surface also gives a specular highlight in the colour of the light source.

## Common errors

- Taking an angle from the surface line when it should be taken from the normal.
- Saying light "speeds up" when it enters glass. It slows down.
- Writing that frequency changes in refraction. The wavelength and speed change.
- Saying a filter "adds" colour, which is the misconception the specification itself warns about.
- Drawing a concave lens ray that actually meets at a point. The rays only appear to come from a point.
- Forgetting to convert nm to m in a calculation.
