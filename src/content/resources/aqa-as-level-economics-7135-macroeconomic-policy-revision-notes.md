These notes condense **3.2.4 Macroeconomic policy** of AQA AS Economics (7135), specification version 1.3, parts 3.2.4.1 to 3.2.4.3. The [3.2.4 study guide](/resources/aqa-as-level-economics-7135-macroeconomic-policy/) sets out each transmission chain and a tax classification example; the [3.2.4 practice questions](/resources/aqa-as-level-economics-7135-macroeconomic-policy-practice/) test them. Figures are invented, except the UK's 2% CPI inflation target.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Monetary policy | Central bank action on interest rates, money and credit, and the exchange rate |
| Bank rate | The Bank of England's main interest rate, set by the MPC |
| Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) | Bank of England committee that sets bank rate to meet the government's inflation target |
| Fiscal policy | Manipulation of government spending, taxation and the budget balance |
| Budget deficit | Government spending exceeds revenue in a year |
| Budget surplus | Revenue exceeds government spending in a year |
| National debt | Total accumulated government borrowing outstanding |
| Direct tax | Tax on income or wealth, such as income tax |
| Indirect tax | Tax on spending, such as VAT |
| Progressive tax | Average rate rises as income rises |
| Proportional tax | Same average rate at every income |
| Regressive tax | Average rate falls as income rises |
| Supply-side policy | Government measure to raise productive potential |
| Supply-side improvement | An actual rise in productive potential, from government or private action |

## 3.2.4.1 Monetary policy

- Government sets the objectives; the MPC uses bank rate. UK target: 2% CPI inflation.
- **Rate rise chain**: loans dearer, saving more attractive, C and I fall, exchange rate tends to rise, net exports fall, AD left, inflation eases.
- **Rate cut chain**: the reverse, raising AD.
- MPC considers: inflation forecasts, GDP growth and the output gap, wages and unemployment, house prices and credit, the exchange rate, commodity prices, the world economy.
- Policy works with long time lags, so decisions rest on forecasts.
- **Depreciation**: exports cheaper, imports dearer, so AD rises, jobs rise, inflation tends to rise, the current account may improve (more so with price elastic demand for exports and imports).
- AS does not require how interest rates or exchange rates are determined.

## 3.2.4.2 Fiscal policy

- Macro functions: AD, growth, jobs, inflation. Micro functions: market failures, incentives, redistribution.
- AD: more spending or lower taxes raises AD; deliberate deficits or surpluses manage demand.
- AS: spending on education, training and infrastructure; incentive-changing taxes.
- Pattern of activity: which goods, sectors and regions grow.
- Main UK taxes: income tax, National Insurance, VAT, corporation tax, council tax, excise duties. Main spending: health, education, welfare, defence.
- Deficit adds to debt; surplus reduces it.

```
Average tax rate = tax paid / income x 100
Debt next year = debt this year + deficit (or - surplus)
Example: debt 900, deficit 45 -> debt 945
```

## 3.2.4.3 Supply-side policies

- Examples in the specification: education and training; cuts in income tax and corporation tax; welfare reform; industrial policy.
- Aim: raise potential output and the trend growth rate, shifting LRAS right.
- Effects: lower (especially structural) unemployment, slower price rises, better current account through competitiveness.
- Many improvements come from the private sector: productivity, innovation, investment.
- Limits: long time lags, uncertain responses to incentives, costs to the budget.

## Exam traps

- "The government raised bank rate." The MPC sets it.
- Stating that a rate rise raises exports.
- Calling VAT progressive because the rich pay more in total.
- Treating the national debt and the deficit as the same.
- Saying supply-side policies work quickly.
- Writing at length on whether the budget should be balanced; AS does not require the arguments.
- Ignoring the conflict a policy may cause between objectives.

## Self-test

1. Who sets the UK inflation target and who sets bank rate?
2. Give the chain from a cut in bank rate to AD.
3. Name three factors the MPC considers.
4. Explain how a depreciation could raise inflation.
5. Distinguish between a direct and an indirect tax, with an example of each.
6. Income 25,000 pays tax of 2,500; income 80,000 pays 16,000. Is the tax progressive?
7. Debt is 1,400 and the budget surplus is 20. What is next year's debt?
8. Give one microeconomic function of fiscal policy.
9. Explain how fiscal policy can influence aggregate supply.
10. Distinguish between a supply-side policy and a supply-side improvement.
11. Name four supply-side policies listed in the specification.
12. Explain one reason why supply-side policies may not reduce unemployment quickly.

### Answers

1. The government sets the target; the MPC of the Bank of England sets bank rate.
2. Borrowing becomes cheaper and saving less rewarding, so C and I rise; the exchange rate tends to fall, raising net exports; AD shifts right.
3. Any three: inflation forecasts, GDP growth, the output gap, wage growth, unemployment, house prices, credit, the exchange rate, commodity prices, world economic conditions.
4. Import prices rise, raising costs and the price of imported goods; higher net exports also raise AD.
5. Direct: on income or wealth, such as income tax. Indirect: on spending, such as VAT.
6. Yes: average rates are 10% and 20%.
7. 1,380.
8. Correcting a market failure, such as a tax on a demerit good; or redistributing income.
9. Spending on education, training or infrastructure, or tax changes that strengthen incentives, can raise productive capacity.
10. A policy is the government measure; an improvement is the actual rise in capacity, which may come from the private sector.
11. Education and training spending; cuts in income and corporation tax; welfare reform; industrial policy.
12. Training takes years to raise skills, so structural unemployment falls slowly.
