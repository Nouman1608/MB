## Key words (S9)

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Experiment | An activity with an unpredictable result |
| Trial | One go at the experiment |
| Outcome | One possible result |
| Event | An outcome, or group of outcomes, you are interested in |
| Fair or unbiased | All outcomes equally likely |
| Biased | Some outcomes favoured |
| At random | Each possible choice equally likely |
| Population | The whole group being studied |
| Sample | The part of the population you actually observe |

## The probability scale (S9)

| Value | Word |
|---|---|
| 0 | impossible |
| between 0 and 0.5 | unlikely |
| 0.5 (1/2, 50%) | evens, even chance |
| between 0.5 and 1 | likely |
| 1 (100%) | certain |

- Probabilities are fractions, decimals or percentages, never ratios.
- Never below 0 and never above 1.
- Convert freely: 4/25 = 0.16 = 16%.
- To mark an event on a scale, work out the number first, then place it.

## Formulas (S10)

| Name | Formula | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| Theoretical probability | favourable outcomes / total outcomes | outcomes equally likely |
| Relative frequency | times the event happened / total trials | from an experiment or survey |
| Expected frequency | probability × number of trials | to predict a count |

### Method: theoretical probability

1. Check the outcomes are equally likely (look for "fair", "at random").
2. Count the favourable outcomes.
3. Count all the outcomes.
4. Divide, and simplify if asked.

### Method: relative frequency

1. Find the total number of trials. This is often the sum of a frequency column.
2. Divide the frequency of the event by that total.
3. State it as an estimate of the probability.

### Method: expected frequency

1. Write the probability (exact if possible).
2. Multiply by the number of trials.
3. Give a sensible answer. A non-whole number is allowed unless the question asks for a whole number of items.

### Small reminders

- A fair spinner has 8 equal sectors, 3 shaded. P(shaded) = 3/8. In 240 spins, expected shaded = 3/8 × 240 = 90.
- A pin lands point up 74 times in 200 drops. Relative frequency = 0.37. In 1500 drops, expected = 0.37 × 1500 = 555.
- A sample survey has 18 yes answers out of 60. Estimated proportion = 18/60 = 0.3. For a population of 400, expect 0.3 × 400 = 120.
- Working backwards: expected frequency 45 in 150 trials gives an estimated probability 45/150 = 0.3.
- A bag with 12 counters and P(blue) = 1/4 has 1/4 × 12 = 3 blue counters.

## Comparing experiment and theory (S11)

1. Work out the theoretical probability from the set-up.
2. Work out the relative frequency from the data.
3. Compare them, using the expected frequency if that helps: observed count against expected count.
4. Comment on the number of trials. A small number means a gap could easily be chance.
5. Suggest more trials before deciding.

Example: a die rolled 120 times gives 27 sixes. Expected 120 × 1/6 = 20. Relative frequency 27/120 = 0.225 against theoretical 1/6 ≈ 0.167. The gap is 7 sixes, but 120 rolls is a small sample, so it does not prove bias.

## Repeating an experiment (S12)

- Repeated experiments usually give different results.
- The differences are due to chance, not mistakes.
- A result that differs from the expected frequency does not mean the model is wrong.
- Past results do not change the probability of the next trial. After a run of misses, the next trial still has the same probability.

## Sample size (S13)

- More trials generally give a relative frequency closer to the true probability.
- "Generally" matters: a larger sample makes a better estimate likely, not certain.
- To estimate a population characteristic, use the larger sample if both are fairly chosen.
- A large sample taken in a biased way is still a poor sample.
- For a population estimate: sample proportion × population size.

## Worked layouts to copy

### From a frequency table

A box of 25 raffle-style tokens is sampled. Colours drawn (with replacement): green 14, yellow 9, red 7.

```
Total trials = 14 + 9 + 7 = 30
Relative frequency of red = 7/30 ≈ 0.233
Expected reds in 300 draws = 7/30 × 300 = 70
```

Write the total first. Most errors here come from using the wrong denominator.

### Choosing the model

```
Fair die, P(multiple of 3) = 2/6 = 1/3      theoretical
Biased die, 15 threes in 60 rolls: 15/60 = 0.25   relative frequency
```

If the question says the die is biased, or gives you results, use the data. If it says fair, use the equally likely model.

### Finding how many from a probability

```
P(winning) = 0.15, 40 tickets
Winning tickets = 0.15 × 40 = 6
```

The answer to a "how many" question about a fixed set (a bag, a pack) should be a whole number. If you get 6.4 you have the wrong probability or the wrong number.

## Wording for written answers (S11 to S13)

Use short, specific sentences that refer to the numbers.

- "The relative frequency, 0.225, is close to the theoretical probability, 0.167, but the sample is small."
- "The observed number, 27, is above the expected number, 20, which could be due to chance."
- "More trials would give a better estimate."
- "Different samples will usually give different results."
- "The larger sample gives the better estimate, provided it is chosen fairly."

Avoid vague phrases such as "it is not fair" with no numbers, or "it is wrong" when you mean "it is different".

## Quick checks before you move on

- Is the answer between 0 and 1 (or 0% and 100%)?
- Does an expected frequency come out smaller than the number of trials?
- Did you use the total number of trials, not just one frequency, as the denominator?
- Does your wording say "estimate" when the probability came from data?
- If asked to compare, did you give both numbers and mention sample size?

## Must-know distinctions

| Idea A | Idea B |
|---|---|
| Theoretical probability: from the model | Relative frequency: from data |
| Expected frequency: a prediction | Observed frequency: what actually happened |
| Probability of one trial | Frequency over many trials |
| Sample: the part you observe | Population: the whole group |

## Quick self-test

1. A bag has 3 red counters and 7 blue counters, picked at random. State P(red) as a fraction, a decimal and a percentage.
2. Which probability word describes an event with probability 0.5?
3. A fair spinner has 5 equal sectors numbered 1 to 5. It is spun 400 times. How many 4s do you expect?
4. A coin is flipped 80 times and lands heads 46 times. Write the relative frequency of heads as a decimal.
5. A factory tests 500 items and finds 35 faulty. Estimate the probability that an item is faulty.
6. Using your answer to question 5, how many faulty items do you expect in 2000 items?
7. Can a probability be 1.2? Give a reason.
8. A fair die is rolled 90 times. How many even numbers do you expect?
9. A survey of 10 people and a survey of 400 people give different estimates of the same proportion. Which should you trust more, and why?
10. After 5 tails in a row, is heads more likely on the next flip of a fair coin?

### Answers

1. 3/10 = 0.3 = 30%.
2. Evens (an even chance).
3. P(4) = 1/5, so 1/5 × 400 = 80.
4. 46/80 = 0.575.
5. 35/500 = 0.07.
6. 0.07 × 2000 = 140.
7. No. A probability cannot be more than 1.
8. P(even) = 3/6 = 1/2, so 1/2 × 90 = 45.
9. The 400-person survey, because larger samples generally give better estimates (assuming both samples are chosen fairly).
10. No. The coin has no memory, so the probability is still 1/2.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving a probability as a ratio, or writing "3 in 10" when a fraction, decimal or percentage is asked for.
- Dividing by the wrong total, such as favourable over unfavourable.
- Using theoretical probability when the question gives experimental data, or the reverse.
- Forgetting to add up the frequencies to get the total number of trials.
- Multiplying the probability by the wrong number of trials.
- Rounding a fraction early and carrying an inaccurate decimal forward.
- Claiming a die or coin is biased from a small experiment, or claiming it is fair because the results are not identical to the expected values.
- Not mentioning sample size when a question asks which estimate is better.
- Writing "it will happen 90 times" when the question is about an expected frequency.
