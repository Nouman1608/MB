**This page is for HL only.** Regional study 8, European totalitarianism (1918 to 1941), is one of twelve studies in the Europe option examined on HL Paper 3, where you write two essays from different regional studies. The guide has six inquiry topics, which come in three pairs: Mussolini's rise and consolidation (1918 to 1926) and his domestic impact (to 1940); the failure of Weimar Germany and Hitler's rise and consolidation (1918 to 1934) and his domestic impact (to 1939); Stalin's rise and consolidation (to 1941) and his domestic impact (to 1941). Questions may be about one leader or compare them, so learn all three.

## Mussolini's rise and consolidation (1918 to 1926)

*Weakness of the liberal state.* Italy emerged from the First World War with heavy losses, debt and inflation. Many Italians felt the peace treaties had cheated them, the "mutilated victory". Strikes and land seizures in 1919 and 1920 (the "two red years") frightened the middle classes and landowners. Governments were unstable coalitions, and the parliamentary system seemed unable to cope. The Catholic and Socialist parties did not cooperate.

*Fascist growth.* Mussolini founded the Fascist movement in 1919. Squads (*squadristi*) attacked socialists and unions, often with the tolerance of police and local elites. The party won 35 seats in the 1921 election and became the National Fascist Party.

*March on Rome (October 1922).* Fascists threatened to march on Rome. The government asked the king to declare martial law, but **Victor Emmanuel III** refused, and instead appointed Mussolini prime minister on 30 October. The "March" was more bluff than conquest; the real decision lay with the king and conservative elites, who thought Mussolini could be controlled.

*Acerbo Law and Matteotti.* The **Acerbo Law** (1923) gave two-thirds of seats to the party winning at least a quarter of votes. In the 1924 election, fascists and allies won a big majority amid violence and fraud. The socialist deputy **Giacomo Matteotti** denounced this and was murdered in June 1924. The opposition's boycott of parliament (the Aventine Secession) failed. After a brief crisis Mussolini announced in January 1925 that he took responsibility.

*Consolidation (1924 to 1926).* Laws restricted the press, gave prefects wide power and, by 1926, banned other parties, set up a special tribunal and secret police (OVRA), and let the government rule by decree. Mussolini was now *Duce*, though the king and Church remained.

## Domestic impact of Mussolini's Italy (to 1940)

*Economic policies and the corporate state.* The regime proclaimed a corporate state, where employers and workers were organised in state-supervised bodies; in practice strikes were banned and workers lost out. Mussolini launched "battles", such as the Battle for Grain, the revaluation of the lira ("Quota 90") and drainage of the Pontine Marshes. The Depression led to state rescue of banks and firms through the IRI (1933) and an aim of self-sufficiency. Results were mixed: wages fell, and the south stayed poor.

*Social policies.* The **Lateran Pacts** (1929) with the Pope gave the Church favour and the Vatican independence, winning Catholic support. Leisure organisations (Dopolavoro) and youth groups (Balilla) aimed to shape Italians. Women were encouraged to have children, and men were favoured in jobs. Antisemitic racial laws were introduced in 1938.

*Propaganda and cult.* The press, film and radio promoted the Duce as a strong leader; slogans claimed "Mussolini is always right".

*Repression, resistance and totalitarian control.* Opponents were jailed or sent into internal exile, as the communist thinker Gramsci was. Opposition was scattered and largely in exile. Yet the monarchy, army, Church and industry kept some independence, so many historians call Italy only partly totalitarian.

## Failure of Weimar Germany and Hitler's rise (1918 to 1934)

*Challenges (1918 to 1929).* The republic was born in defeat and revolution. The Versailles Treaty was resented. The constitution of 1919 used proportional representation, which led to many small parties, and Article 48 allowed emergency rule. Threats came from the left (the Spartacist uprising, 1919) and the right (the Kapp Putsch, 1920, and Hitler's Munich Putsch, 1923). Political murders and the occupation of the Ruhr in 1923 led to **hyperinflation**, which wiped out savings and left lasting distrust. Many judges, officials and officers did not support the republic.

*The "Golden Era" under Stresemann (1924 to 1929).* The new Rentenmark ended inflation (1923). **Gustav Stresemann** worked for reconciliation: the Dawes Plan (1924) brought American loans, the Locarno Treaties (1925) and League entry (1926) restored Germany's standing. Culture flourished. Yet recovery depended on foreign loans, extremist parties persisted, and Stresemann died in 1929.

*Great Depression and crisis (1929 to 1933).* After the Wall Street Crash, loans were recalled and unemployment rose to about six million. Chancellor Brüning ruled by emergency decree. Nazi support rose from 2.6 per cent in 1928 to 18.3 per cent in 1930 and 37.3 per cent in July 1932. Voters feared communism, wanted jobs and responded to Nazi propaganda and organisation. Conservative politicians, such as von Papen, believed they could control Hitler; **Hindenburg** appointed him chancellor on 30 January 1933.

*Consolidation.* After the **Reichstag fire** (27 February 1933), an emergency decree suspended civil rights. The **Enabling Act** (23 March 1933) gave Hitler power to make laws without the Reichstag, passed with the Centre Party's support while communist deputies were arrested or barred. Trade unions were banned in May and parties in July. In the **Night of the Long Knives** (30 June to 2 July 1934) Hitler had Röhm and other SA leaders, and critics such as Schleicher, killed, winning the army's backing. When Hindenburg died in August, Hitler became Führer, and the army swore a personal **oath** to him. Popular support mattered too: many Germans welcomed order, falling unemployment and national pride.

## Domestic impact of Hitler's Germany (to 1939)

*Economic policies.* Schacht's **New Plan** (1934) controlled trade and imports and supported rearmament. The **Four-Year Plan** (1936), under Göring, aimed at self-sufficiency for war. Unemployment fell, helped by rearmament, public works and the removal of women and Jews from the statistics. Real wages grew slowly and consumers had few goods.

*Social policies.* The ideal of the *Volksgemeinschaft*, a "people's community", offered belonging to those defined as racially acceptable. Organisations such as Hitler Youth and Strength Through Joy shaped leisure and education. Women were expected to be mothers. The state persecuted Jews, Roma, disabled people and others; the Nuremberg Laws (1935) stripped Jews of citizenship, and Kristallnacht (November 1938) saw mass violence.

*Propaganda and cult.* Goebbels used radio, film, rallies and the Berlin Olympics (1936) to promote Hitler as saviour.

*Repression, resistance and control.* The Gestapo, SS and concentration camps terrorised opponents. Churches and some youth groups offered limited resistance. Historians debate how far control came from terror and how far from consent. Many Germans informed on neighbours, and others simply conformed.

## Stalin's rise and consolidation (to 1941)

*Position and opponents.* Stalin became General Secretary in 1922, a post that let him appoint officials who would back him. Lenin's last writings were critical of him. In the struggle after Lenin's death (1924), Stalin sided with Bukharin against Trotsky, Zinoviev and Kamenev, then turned on Bukharin and the "Right". His rivals made errors: **Trotsky** was a brilliant but arrogant outsider with little party machine; others underestimated Stalin. His slogan of "Socialism in One Country" appealed to party members.

*Consolidation.* By 1929 Stalin dominated. The **Ryutin Platform** (1932), calling for his removal, was met with arrests, and Stalin wanted harsher punishment. The murder of **Kirov** in December 1934 gave a pretext for mass arrests.

*The purges and Great Terror.* Show trials (1936 to 1938) condemned old Bolsheviks including Zinoviev, Kamenev and Bukharin. In 1937 the army leadership was purged. Over the whole Great Terror, hundreds of thousands were shot and more were sent to the Gulag. It removed rivals, but also damaged the army and the economy.

## Domestic impact of Stalin's USSR (to 1941)

*Economic policies.* **Collectivization** (from 1929) forced peasants into collective farms and attacked the "kulaks". Resistance, such as killing livestock, was fierce. The **Five-Year Plans** (from 1928) built heavy industry at great speed, with new cities such as Magnitogorsk, but with poor quality, harsh discipline and shortages. The famine of 1932 to 1933 killed millions, notably in Ukraine.

*Social policies.* Education and literacy expanded, and women entered work, but in the 1930s abortion was restricted and the family encouraged. Religion was attacked and many churches closed.

*Propaganda and cult.* Socialist realism in art, the cult of Stalin as leader and teacher and a rewritten history glorified the regime.

*Repression, resistance and control.* The NKVD ran a vast system of surveillance and camps. Open resistance was hard, but passive resistance and survival strategies were widespread. Historians such as Arendt and Friedrich and Brzezinski used "totalitarian" models; revisionists such as Getty stressed chaos and local initiative.

## Thinking about "totalitarianism"

A useful evaluation is to compare three features: ideology, a single leader and party, and terror and control of society. Italy had a weaker grip because of the monarchy and Church; Germany combined mass consent with violence; the USSR had the most extensive terror. Historians also debate whether the dictators planned their policies (the intentionalist view) or whether chaotic rivalry drove them (the structuralist view), most notably for the Nazi regime.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need all three leaders?**
Learn all three. Questions can be on one or can compare two.

**Is "totalitarian" the right word?**
It is a debated term. Define it and test each regime against it.
