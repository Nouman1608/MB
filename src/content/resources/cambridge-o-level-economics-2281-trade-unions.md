This guide covers section 3.4 **Trade unions** of the Cambridge O Level Economics 2281 syllabus for examination in 2026. The syllabus lists three parts: the definition of a trade union; the role of trade unions in the economy, "including engaging in collective bargaining on wages, working hours and working conditions; protecting employment; and influencing government policy"; and the advantages and disadvantages of trade union activity, including "factors influencing the strength of trade unions" and the viewpoints of "workers, firms and the government".

## 3.4.1 Definition

A **trade union** is an organisation of workers formed to protect and promote the interests of its members, especially their pay, working conditions and job security. It gains influence by representing many workers at once: an employer can ignore one worker's request, but not easily that of most of its workforce acting together.

## 3.4.2 The role of trade unions

### Collective bargaining

**Collective bargaining** is negotiation between a union, on behalf of all its members, and an employer (or a group of employers) over pay and conditions. It covers the three areas the syllabus names:

- **Wages**: the basic rate, overtime rates, bonuses and annual increases.
- **Working hours**: the length of the working week, shift patterns, breaks and holidays.
- **Working conditions**: health and safety, equipment, training, sick pay and contracts.

### Worked example 1: a pay negotiation

The union at an invented bus company represents 400 drivers earning $600 a week. Prices are rising by 4% a year.

1. **Claim.** The union asks for an 8% rise, pointing to rising prices and the firm's profits.
2. **Offer.** The company offers 3%, pointing to rising fuel costs.
3. **Negotiation.** Both sides meet. The union lowers its claim; the company asks for changes to shift patterns in return for more pay.
4. **Settlement.** They agree a 5% rise plus new rotas that let the company run more buses with the same drivers.

Was this a good result for drivers? The money wage rises to 600 × 1.05 = $630. But prices rose by 4%, so the **real** rise is about 1.05 ÷ 1.04 = 1.0096, roughly 1%. The company's 3% offer would have been a real cut of about 1%, since 1.03 ÷ 1.04 = 0.990. The union turned a fall in living standards into a small gain.

### Industrial action

If bargaining fails, a union may ask members to take **industrial action** to put pressure on the employer.

| Action | What workers do | Effect on the firm |
|---|---|---|
| Overtime ban | Refuse to work beyond normal hours | Output falls where the firm relies on overtime |
| Work-to-rule | Follow every rule exactly, slowing work | Output falls although workers are still paid |
| Go-slow | Work deliberately slowly | Output and quality fall |
| Strike | Stop working altogether | Output stops; workers lose pay |

The threat of action often matters more than action itself: an employer that expects a costly strike may improve its offer to avoid one.

### Protecting employment

Unions try to keep members in work and to protect them at work. They argue against redundancies or for fair redundancy pay and retraining, represent members facing discipline or unfair dismissal, and press for safe workplaces. They may accept new technology in return for promises that no one will be made redundant.

### Influencing government policy

Unions lobby governments on matters affecting workers, such as minimum wages, health and safety law, maximum working hours, employment rights, pensions and training. They may also campaign on wider issues such as education and taxation.

## 3.4.3 Advantages and disadvantages

### Worked example 2: does a strike pay?

Return to the bus company. Suppose the drivers had rejected the 3% offer and struck for 3 weeks, then settled at 5%.

1. Pay lost per driver: 3 × $600 = $1,800.
2. Extra pay won compared with accepting the offer: 5% − 3% = 2% of $600 = $12 a week.
3. Weeks needed to recover the lost pay: 1,800 ÷ 12 = 150 weeks, nearly three years.

On these figures the strike costs each driver more than it gains for a long time. Unions know this, which is why strikes are usually a last resort and why the threat of one is often enough.

### Worked example 3: a productivity agreement

A factory pays 100 workers $500 a week, and they make 10,000 units: a labour cost of 50,000 ÷ 10,000 = $5 per unit. The union agrees a 5% rise in return for new working methods that raise output by 5% to 10,500 units. New wage bill: $52,500. Labour cost per unit: 52,500 ÷ 10,500 = $5. Workers earn more, and the firm's costs per unit do not rise. Agreements like this show that union activity need not harm firms.

### The three viewpoints

**Workers**
- Advantages: higher pay than individuals could win alone; safer conditions; support when facing dismissal; a say in changes at work.
- Disadvantages: membership fees; lost pay during strikes; a large pay rise may lead the firm to employ fewer workers; members must follow decisions they may disagree with.

**Firms**
- Advantages: one body to negotiate with instead of every worker; a channel for explaining changes; workers who feel represented may be more motivated and less likely to leave; productivity agreements.
- Disadvantages: higher wage costs and lower profit; lost output during industrial action; resistance to new methods or job cuts; less flexibility.

**Government**
- Advantages: unions can be consulted on policy; orderly bargaining can prevent unrest; fair treatment of workers may reduce inequality.
- Disadvantages: strikes reduce output and tax revenue; large pay rises may add to inflation; disruption to essential public services; higher unemployment if pay rises cost jobs.

## Factors influencing union strength

Apply the factors to compare two invented unions.

| Factor | Union P: airline pilots | Union Q: fruit pickers |
|---|---|---|
| Share of the workforce in the union | High | Low |
| Skills and ease of replacement | Highly trained, hard to replace quickly | Little training, easy to replace |
| Effect of action on the employer | Flights stop, losses mount quickly | Other workers can be hired |
| Employer's ability to pay | Airline can raise fares if demand is strong | Farms face low, unstable prices |
| Funds to support members during action | Large | Small |
| Labour market | Shortage of pilots | Many people seeking seasonal work |

Union P is far stronger. Other factors to use: the state of the economy (unions are stronger when unemployment is low), the law on strikes, and public support for the workers' case.

## Common mistakes

- Describing unions only as organisers of strikes. Collective bargaining is their main activity.
- Giving advantages from one viewpoint when the question asks for workers, firms and government.
- Treating a money pay rise as a gain without comparing it with inflation.
- Assuming a higher wage always cuts jobs. A productivity agreement or a profitable firm may avoid this.
- Listing strength factors without explaining why each one adds to or reduces bargaining power.

## Exam technique for 3.4

- **Define** a trade union by its purpose and membership.
- **Describe** the role using the three areas the syllabus names.
- **Explain** an advantage or disadvantage by stating whose viewpoint it is and how the effect comes about.
- **Analyse** union strength by linking each factor to how easily the employer can resist the union.
- **Discuss** whether unions benefit an economy: weigh higher pay and better conditions against costs, lost output and possible job losses, and judge what the outcome depends on (productivity, the firm's profits, the state of the economy).
