Section A checks precise knowledge of the French Revolution and Napoleon, unification and post-war Eastern Europe. Section B builds explanation and then full essays: allow about 50 minutes each for questions 11 and 12.

---

## Section A

**1.** Identify **three** long-term causes of the French Revolution. **[3]**

**2.** State **two** reasons why the Civil Constitution of the Clergy divided France. **[2]**

**3.** Identify **three** measures taken by the Committee of Public Safety in 1793-94. **[3]**

**4.** Outline **two** ways in which Cavour gained foreign support for Piedmont. **[2]**

**5.** State **two** results of the Austro-Prussian War of 1866. **[2]**

**6.** Outline **two** reasons for the Warsaw Pact invasion of Czechoslovakia in 1968. **[2]**

---

## Section B

**7.** **(a)** Explain why the monarchy was overthrown in 1792. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain **two** reasons for the fall of Robespierre in 1794. **[4]**

**8.** **(a)** Explain how Napoleon consolidated his power between 1799 and 1804. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain **two** reasons for the failure of Napoleon's empire after 1808. **[4]**

**9.** **(a)** Explain the role of Garibaldi in the unification of Italy. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain how Bismarck isolated France before 1870. **[3]**

**10.** Explain why Solidarity was able to challenge the communist government in Poland in 1980-81. **[4]**

**11.** "Napoleon preserved the gains of the French Revolution." To what extent do you agree with this statement? **[15]**

**12.** Evaluate the reasons for the collapse of communist rule in Central and Eastern Europe in 1989. **[15]**

---

## Answers

**1.** Any three, 1 mark each: royal debt, worsened by the cost of the American War of Independence; an unfair tax system with exemptions for the privileged orders; the influence of Enlightenment ideas on rights and sovereignty; the frustration of the bourgeoisie at their exclusion from privilege and power; peasant burdens of feudal dues, tithes and taxes; the failure of earlier reform attempts. **[3]**

**2.** Any two, 1 mark each: it made the clergy elected and paid by the state, which many Catholics saw as an attack on the Church; the pope condemned it; priests had to swear an oath to the constitution, and around half refused; it turned many peasants, especially in the west, against the Revolution and pushed the king towards the Flight to Varennes. **[2]**

**3.** Any three, 1 mark each: the *levée en masse* (August 1793); the Law of Suspects (September 1793); the Maximum on prices and wages; the Revolutionary Tribunal and executions; representatives on mission sent to the provinces and armies; the Law of 22 Prairial (June 1794); de-Christianization in some areas. **[3]**

**4.** Any two, 1 mark each: Piedmont joined Britain and France in the Crimean War (1855), gaining a seat at the peace congress; he raised the Italian question at the Congress of Paris (1856); he made the secret Plombières agreement (1858) with Napoleon III, promising Nice and Savoy for French help against Austria; he later secured British approval for annexations in 1860. **[2]**

**5.** Any two, 1 mark each: Austria was excluded from German affairs; the German Confederation was dissolved and the North German Confederation formed under Prussia (1867); Prussia annexed Hanover, Hesse-Kassel, Nassau and Frankfurt; Italy gained Venetia; Bismarck's lenient terms to Austria avoided lasting hostility. **[2]**

**6.** Any two, 1 mark each: Dubček's reforms ended censorship and allowed free discussion, which Moscow feared would undermine the party's leading role; there were fears that Czechoslovakia might leave the Warsaw Pact or that reform would spread to other states; leaders in East Germany and Poland pressed for action; Brezhnev asserted the right to defend socialism in the bloc (the Brezhnev Doctrine). **[2]**

**7.** **(a)** Up to 4 marks: the Flight to Varennes (June 1791) showed Louis XVI opposed the Revolution (1); war with Austria and Prussia from April 1792 raised fears of foreign invasion and royal treachery (1); the Brunswick Manifesto threatened Paris if the royal family were harmed, which inflamed radicals (1); the *sans-culottes* and the Paris sections stormed the Tuileries on 10 August 1792, and the Convention abolished the monarchy in September (1). **[4]**
**(b)** 2 marks for each developed reason: military victories, including Fleurus (June 1794), removed the justification for the Terror; the Law of 22 Prairial and the "Great Terror" made deputies fear for their own lives; Robespierre's speech of 8 Thermidor threatening unnamed enemies led colleagues to strike first; divisions within the Committees of Public Safety and General Security left him isolated. **[4]**

**8.** **(a)** Up to 4 marks: the Brumaire coup (November 1799) and the Constitution of the Year VIII made him First Consul with dominant power (1); plebiscites gave an appearance of popular approval (1); the Concordat (1801) ended the conflict with the Church and won Catholic support (1); prefects, the police under Fouché and censorship controlled the country, and he became Consul for life (1802) and emperor (1804) (1). **[4]**
**(b)** 2 marks for each developed reason: the Peninsular War (1808-14) drained troops and money in a guerrilla conflict supported by Britain; the Continental System damaged European economies and provoked resentment, contributing to the break with Russia; the invasion of Russia (1812) destroyed the Grande Armée; the coalition of 1813 brought together Russia, Prussia, Austria and Britain, defeating him at Leipzig. **[4]**

**9.** **(a)** Up to 4 marks: Garibaldi's Thousand landed in Sicily (May 1860) and conquered it with local support (1); he crossed to the mainland and took Naples, collapsing the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies (1); his advance alarmed Cavour, who sent Piedmontese troops through the Papal States to stop him reaching Rome (1); Garibaldi handed his conquests to Victor Emmanuel II at Teano, allowing the Kingdom of Italy to be proclaimed in 1861 (1). **[4]**
**(b)** 1 mark each: he kept Russia friendly, partly through his support during the Polish revolt of 1863; he used Napoleon III's demands for compensation after 1866 to persuade the south German states to sign military alliances with Prussia; he exploited the Hohenzollern candidature and edited the Ems Telegram (July 1870) so that France declared war and appeared the aggressor. **[3]**

**10.** Up to 4 marks: economic crisis, debt and rising food prices produced mass strikes in August 1980, centred on the Gdańsk shipyard (1); Lech Wałęsa's leadership and the union's discipline made it hard to suppress (1); the Catholic Church, strengthened by John Paul II's visit in 1979, gave moral support (1); Solidarity grew to millions of members and won the Gdańsk Agreement recognizing independent unions, until martial law (December 1981) suppressed it (1). **[4]**

**11.** Marked holistically using the 15-mark markbands; the indicative content is a guide, not a checklist. For: the Civil Code preserved legal equality, property rights and the abolition of feudalism; careers open to talent; the Concordat accepted the loss of Church lands; centralized administration and the meritocratic state continued revolutionary reforms. Against: political liberty was destroyed through censorship, police and plebiscites; a new hereditary empire and nobility contradicted equality; the Civil Code reduced women's rights; slavery was restored in the colonies (1802). A strong answer identifies which "gains" are at issue (social, legal, political), judges each, and concludes, for example, that Napoleon preserved the social and legal gains while abandoning political liberty. **[15]**

**12.** Marked holistically using the 15-mark markbands; the indicative content is a guide, not a checklist. Reasons: Gorbachev's reforms and abandonment of the Brezhnev Doctrine, so Soviet troops would not intervene; economic stagnation and Western debt in Poland and Hungary; popular movements such as Solidarity, the Church and civic groups; reform communists in Hungary who opened the border with Austria; the East German exodus and mass demonstrations in Leipzig and Berlin; the domino effect as each revolution encouraged the next; the role of media and Western contacts. A strong answer ranks the factors, distinguishes necessary from sufficient causes (Soviet non-intervention was necessary; popular action made collapse happen), and notes differences between countries such as the violent end in Romania. **[15]**

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I use Russia in a History of Europe essay?**
Yes, where your sections include imperial Russia or the Soviet Union; Russia is part of this regional option.

**Is it acceptable to compare Italy and Germany in one essay?**
Only if the question asks for comparison. Otherwise focus on the country named.
