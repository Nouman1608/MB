This guide covers section 3.8, Analysis and evaluation of financial information, of the AQA A-level Accounting (7127) specification (version 1.0, 22 December 2016, for A-level exams from June 2019 onwards). The qualification is not tiered. The specification lists section 8 in the content assessed by both Paper 1 and Paper 2, each a 3-hour written exam, and you must use a calculator in the exam.

Use it with the [revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-accounting-analysis-and-evaluation-of-financial-information-revision-notes/) and the [practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-accounting-analysis-and-evaluation-of-financial-information-practice/). The ratios are worked from statements like those in [preparation of financial statements of sole traders](/resources/aqa-a-level-accounting-preparation-of-financial-statements-of-sole-traders/), and the users who read them are introduced in [the role of the accountant](/resources/aqa-a-level-accounting-role-of-the-accountant/). See also the [AQA A-level Accounting hub](/boards/aqa/a-level/accounting/), the [7127 checklist](/checklists/aqa/a-level/accounting/) and the [free diagnostics](/diagnostics/). All businesses and figures are invented.

## What section 3.8 covers

| Specification content (3.8) | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| Calculation and interpretation of financial measures and ratios | Calculate and interpret gross profit margin %, markup %, rate of inventory turnover (times and days), profit in relation to revenue %, expenses in relation to revenue %, return on capital employed %, current ratio, liquid capital ratio, trade receivable days, trade payable days and capital gearing |
| Appraising business performance using financial statements and ratios | Judge profitability, liquidity, efficiency and capital structure |
| The difference between cash and profits | Explain why they differ and the effect of transactions on profitability and liquidity |
| The limitations of financial statements and ratio analysis | Cover both financial and non-financial factors |

The specification's appendix of accounting ratios says you will be required to know the formulae, so learn them. Section 3.17 later adds investors' ratios for limited companies; they are not part of 3.8.

## The formulae

These follow the wording of the specification's appendix.

| Group | Ratio | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| Profitability | Gross profit margin | Gross profit / Revenue × 100 |
| Profitability | Markup | Gross profit / Cost of sales × 100 |
| Profitability | Profit in relation to revenue | Profit for year (before tax) / Revenue × 100 |
| Profitability | Expenses in relation to revenue | Expenses / Revenue × 100 |
| Profitability | ROCE (sole trader) | Profit before interest / Capital employed × 100, where capital employed = capital + non-current liabilities |
| Profitability | ROCE (limited company) | Profit from operations / Capital employed × 100, where capital employed = equity + non-current liabilities |
| Liquidity | Current ratio | Current assets / Current liabilities, expressed as x:1 |
| Liquidity | Liquid capital (acid test) ratio | (Current assets - inventory) / Current liabilities, expressed as x:1 |
| Efficiency | Rate of inventory turnover | Cost of sales / Average inventory |
| Efficiency | Rate of inventory turnover (days) | Average inventory / Cost of sales × 365 |
| Efficiency | Trade receivable days | Trade receivables / Credit sales × 365 |
| Efficiency | Trade payable days | Trade payables / Credit purchases × 365 |
| Capital structure | Capital gearing | Non-current liabilities / (Issued share capital + Reserves + Non-current liabilities) × 100 |

Average inventory is normally (opening + closing) / 2. For ROCE, the specification allows either opening or closing capital (or equity); say which one you used. The gearing formula is written for a company. For a sole trader, capital takes the place of share capital plus reserves, so the denominator is the same capital employed figure used for ROCE.

## Worked example: Ashby Garden Supplies

Ashby Garden Supplies is a sole trader. Extracts for the year ended 31 December 2025:

| Income statement | £ |
|---|---:|
| Revenue (credit sales £360,000; cash sales £120,000) | 480,000 |
| Opening inventory | 32,000 |
| Purchases (all on credit) | 308,000 |
| Closing inventory | 40,000 |
| Cost of sales | 300,000 |
| Gross profit | 180,000 |
| Expenses (include loan interest £4,000 and depreciation £12,000) | 132,000 |
| Profit for the year | 48,000 |

| Statement of financial position at 31 December 2025 | £ |
|---|---:|
| Non-current assets | 220,000 |
| Inventory 40,000; trade receivables 36,000; bank 4,000 | 80,000 current assets |
| Trade payables 38,000; other payables 2,000 | 40,000 current liabilities |
| Non-current liability: bank loan | 50,000 |
| Capital (opening 196,000 + profit 48,000 - drawings 34,000) | 210,000 |

Check: 220,000 + 80,000 - 40,000 - 50,000 = 210,000, so the statement balances.

### Profitability

```
Gross profit margin = 180,000 / 480,000 × 100 = 37.50%
Markup              = 180,000 / 300,000 × 100 = 60.00%
Profit to revenue   =  48,000 / 480,000 × 100 = 10.00%
Expenses to revenue = 132,000 / 480,000 × 100 = 27.50%
ROCE = (48,000 + 4,000) / (210,000 + 50,000) × 100
     = 52,000 / 260,000 × 100 = 20.00%  (closing capital)
```

Interest is added back because ROCE measures the return on all long-term funds, including the loan. Notice that gross margin minus expenses to revenue gives profit to revenue: 37.5 - 27.5 = 10.

### Liquidity

```
Current ratio       = 80,000 / 40,000 = 2.00:1
Liquid capital ratio = (80,000 - 40,000) / 40,000 = 1.00:1
```

### Efficiency

```
Average inventory = (32,000 + 40,000) / 2 = 36,000
Inventory turnover = 300,000 / 36,000 = 8.33 times
Inventory days     = 36,000 / 300,000 × 365 = 43.8 days
Receivable days    = 36,000 / 360,000 × 365 = 36.5 days
Payable days       = 38,000 / 308,000 × 365 = 45.0 days
```

Use credit sales, not total revenue, for receivable days: cash sales never create a receivable.

### Capital structure

```
Capital gearing = 50,000 / (210,000 + 50,000) × 100 = 19.23%
```

## Appraising performance

A ratio means little on its own. The specification asks you to appraise **profitability, liquidity, efficiency and capital structure**, so compare with the previous year (or a competitor) and explain causes. Ashby's 2024 ratios, from the same accounting policies:

| Ratio | 2024 | 2025 |
|---|---:|---:|
| Gross profit margin | 40.00% | 37.50% |
| Markup | 66.67% | 60.00% |
| Profit to revenue | 12.00% | 10.00% |
| Expenses to revenue | 28.00% | 27.50% |
| ROCE | 23.58% | 20.00% |
| Current ratio | 2.19:1 | 2.00:1 |
| Liquid capital ratio | 1.32:1 | 1.00:1 |
| Inventory days | 40.6 | 43.8 |
| Receivable days | 30.0 | 36.5 |
| Payable days | 46.6 | 45.0 |
| Capital gearing | 20.33% | 19.23% |

Revenue rose from £450,000 to £480,000 (6.67%), but the appraisal reads like this:

- **Profitability.** Gross margin fell 2.5 percentage points. Possible causes: price cuts to win the extra sales, or dearer purchases not passed on. Expenses were controlled (27.5% against 28.0%), so the fall in profit to revenue comes from trading, not overheads. ROCE fell to 20.00%. That is still well above the 8% Ashby pays on its loan (£4,000 on £50,000), but the trend is down.
- **Liquidity.** Both ratios are still at or above 1:1, but the liquid ratio dropped by about a quarter. Bank is only £4,000. Ashby can meet current debts only if customers pay on time.
- **Efficiency.** Inventory is held about three days longer and customers take six and a half days longer to pay. Both tie up cash. Ashby pays suppliers in about 45 days, so it still collects before it pays, but the gap has narrowed sharply.
- **Capital structure.** Gearing below 20% is low. The loan is not a threat, and Ashby has room to borrow if it needs to.

A sound conclusion: sales growth has been bought with lower margins and looser credit control, and the owner should tighten collection before liquidity becomes a problem.

## Cash and profit are different

Profit is income earned less expenses incurred, measured under the accruals concept. Cash is money actually received and paid. They differ because:

- credit sales and credit purchases are counted before the cash moves;
- depreciation reduces profit but involves no cash;
- buying inventory uses cash but only cost of sales reaches the income statement;
- buying non-current assets uses cash but only depreciation hits profit;
- loans received or repaid, capital introduced and drawings move cash but are not income or expenses.

### Worked example: why Ashby's bank fell

Ashby's bank went from £19,000 to £4,000 despite a profit of £48,000. During 2025 it bought equipment for £30,000. Opening trade receivables were £30,000 and opening trade payables £35,000.

```
Profit for the year                       48,000
Add depreciation (not a cash expense)     12,000
Less increase in inventory (40 - 32)      (8,000)
Less increase in receivables (36 - 30)    (6,000)
Add increase in trade payables (38 - 35)   3,000
Less drawings                            (34,000)
Less equipment bought                    (30,000)
Change in bank                           (15,000)
```

19,000 - 15,000 = £4,000, which agrees with the statement of financial position.

### Effect of transactions on profitability and liquidity

Start from Ashby's current assets £80,000 (inventory £40,000) and current liabilities £40,000. Treat each transaction separately.

| Transaction | Profit | Current ratio | Liquid ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pay a trade payable £10,000 from the bank | No effect | 70/30 = 2.33:1 (rises) | 30/30 = 1.00:1 (no change) |
| Buy inventory £6,000 on credit | No effect | 86/46 = 1.87:1 (falls) | 40/46 = 0.87:1 (falls) |
| Sell goods costing £3,000 for £5,000 cash | +£2,000 | 82/40 = 2.05:1 (rises) | 45/40 = 1.12:1 (rises) |
| Charge depreciation | Falls | No effect | No effect |
| Buy a non-current asset for cash | No immediate effect | Falls | Falls |
| Owner takes cash drawings | No effect | Falls | Falls |

The first row is a useful trap: cash falls, yet the current ratio improves, because a ratio above 1:1 rises when equal amounts leave both sides.

## Limitations of financial statements and ratio analysis

The specification says limitations include both financial and non-financial factors.

**Financial limitations**

- Statements are historic. They show last year, not next year.
- Historic cost ignores inflation, so asset values and ROCE can mislead.
- Different policies (depreciation method, provision for doubtful debts) make comparisons between businesses unreliable.
- A year-end figure may not be typical. Seasonal trade, or paying suppliers just before the year end, changes the liquidity ratios.
- Some figures are estimates, such as depreciation and doubtful debts.
- Ratios need a benchmark. Without last year or a competitor, a ratio cannot be judged.
- Information may be missing. For example, credit sales may not be given separately.

**Non-financial limitations**

- Statements do not show staff skill, morale or turnover.
- Customer satisfaction, reputation and brand strength are not measured.
- They say nothing about market conditions, competitors or new technology.
- Product quality and environmental or social impact are left out.
- The location and management quality of the business are not recorded.

When you evaluate, use these to qualify your conclusion, not as a stand-alone list.

## Common errors

- Using total revenue instead of credit sales for receivable days, or total purchases when some were for cash.
- Using closing inventory when average inventory is required for turnover.
- Mixing up markup (on cost of sales) and margin (on revenue).
- Forgetting to add back interest in sole-trader ROCE.
- Writing a current ratio as a percentage instead of x:1.
- Saying a falling ratio is "bad" with no cause and no comparison.
- Treating a high current ratio as always good, when it may mean idle inventory or slow collection.
- Saying depreciation is a cash outflow.
