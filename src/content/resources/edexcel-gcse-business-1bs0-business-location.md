This guide covers content item 1.4.2, Business location, in Topic 1.4 (Making the business effective) of the Pearson Edexcel Level 1/Level 2 GCSE (9-1) in Business (1BS0) specification, Issue 2, July 2022. It belongs to Theme 1, Investigating small business, examined in Paper 1. The broader [guide to limited liability and business location](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-limited-liability-and-business-location/) defines each factor. This page shows how to weigh the factors against each other for a particular business, which is what Analyse, Justify and Evaluate questions test. All businesses and figures are invented.

## What 1.4.2 contains

The specification lists three groups of factors influencing business location.

| Factor group | Wording in the specification |
|---|---|
| Proximity | Proximity to market, labour, materials and competitors |
| Activity | Nature of the business activity |
| The internet | The impact of the internet on location decisions: e-commerce and/or fixed premises |

"Proximity" simply means nearness. The words "and/or" in the third line matter: a business might trade only online, only from premises, or from both.

## Why location matters so much to a start-up

A location decision is hard to reverse. A lease may run for several years, and fitting out premises costs money that is lost if the business moves. Location also affects revenue and costs at the same time:

- **Revenue:** how many customers can reach the business, or how quickly goods can reach customers.
- **Costs:** rent, business rates, wages and the cost of transporting materials and finished goods.

A good answer therefore rarely says one site is simply "better". It says which site suits the business, because of the balance between expected revenue and expected costs.

## The four proximity factors in more depth

### Proximity to market

The market is the customers. Being close matters most when customers must visit (a hairdresser, a café) or when the product is perishable or costly to deliver (fresh flowers, ready-mixed concrete). Sites with heavy footfall usually charge higher rent, so the owner must judge whether the extra customers outweigh the extra cost.

### Proximity to labour

Labour means workers with the right skills. A business that needs specialist staff, such as software developers or qualified mechanics, may need to locate where those people live. Where many businesses compete for the same workers, wages may be higher. Where skilled labour is scarce, the business may struggle to recruit at all.

### Proximity to materials

Materials are the inputs used to make the product. Being near them matters when inputs are heavy, bulky or perishable, because transport is costly and quality may suffer on a long journey. A business that turns heavy inputs into a lighter product saves most by locating near the inputs.

### Proximity to competitors

This factor works in both directions.

- **Near competitors:** customers who come to an area to compare choices, such as a street of restaurants or an area known for antiques, may also visit your business. Competitors may already have proved there is demand.
- **Away from competitors:** the business may be the only choice for local customers, but it must attract them on its own.

Which effect is stronger depends on the type of product. Shoppers like to compare clothes or furniture, so clustering can help. People rarely compare launderettes, so a second launderette next door mainly splits the trade.

## The nature of the business activity

This is the factor that decides how much weight to give the other four. A useful way to apply it is to ask: "What does this business do, and who needs to come to whom?"

| Type of activity | Example | Dominant factor | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Customers visit | Nail salon | Market | Revenue depends on passing trade and easy access |
| Business visits customers | Mobile window cleaner | Market (spread of customers), not premises | Needs a base near the area served, but no shop front |
| Processes heavy inputs | Sawmill | Materials | Logs are bulky and expensive to move |
| Relies on scarce skills | Video-game studio | Labour | Must recruit specialist staff |
| Sells mainly online | Phone-case seller | Cost of premises and delivery links | Customers never visit |

### Worked example: comparing two sites for a pet-grooming salon

Kerry is choosing a site for Paws and Suds, a dog-grooming salon. Customers bring their dogs in, so the market factor matters most, but costs differ.

| | High-street unit | Retail park unit |
|---|---|---|
| Annual rent | £18,000 | £12,600 |
| Forecast grooms a year | 3,000 | 2,400 |
| Average price per groom | £35 | £35 |

1. High-street revenue = 3,000 × £35 = £105,000
2. Retail park revenue = 2,400 × £35 = £84,000
3. Extra revenue on the high street = £105,000 − £84,000 = £21,000
4. Extra rent on the high street = £18,000 − £12,600 = £5,400
5. Rent as a percentage of revenue: high street £18,000 ÷ £105,000 × 100 = 17.1%; retail park £12,600 ÷ £84,000 × 100 = 15.0%

The high street costs £5,400 more in rent but is forecast to bring £21,000 more revenue. If the extra grooms add little to other costs, the high street looks the better choice. However, the retail park has free parking, which matters to owners arriving with large dogs. If parking problems put customers off, the high-street forecast of 3,000 grooms may not be reached. A strong answer uses the numbers and then questions the forecast.

## The internet: e-commerce and/or fixed premises

E-commerce is buying and selling online. Its effect on location depends on the business.

**When e-commerce weakens the importance of location**

- A business selling goods that can be posted can reach customers nationally, so it can choose a cheaper site away from expensive town centres.
- A business run entirely online may operate from home or a low-cost unit, which lowers fixed costs for a start-up.

**When fixed premises are still needed**

- Services delivered in person, such as hairdressing or car repairs, need a site customers can reach.
- Goods must be stored, packed and dispatched, so an online seller still needs space, ideally near good transport links.
- Some customers want to see, touch or try products before buying.

**Using both.** Many small businesses combine a shop with a website. The shop provides trust and a place to collect or return goods; the website extends the market beyond the local area. This "and/or" choice is what the specification asks you to discuss.

## Annotated model answer

**Question (Outline style):** Outline **one** reason why Brackley Logs, which turns timber into firewood, might locate close to forests. [2]

> Timber is heavy and bulky, so transporting it a long way is expensive *(reason linked to materials)*. Locating near the forests keeps Brackley Logs' transport costs low, which allows it to price its firewood competitively *(consequence for this business)*.

Two linked points, both about this business: that is what Outline requires.

## Common mistakes

- Writing that a business should always locate near customers. Only some activities depend on footfall.
- Treating proximity to competitors as always bad.
- Saying the internet means a business needs no premises at all.
- Comparing sites using costs only, ignoring revenue.
- Ignoring the nature of the activity, which is a factor in its own right.

## Exam technique

- **State** questions take the factor from the source; one short sentence is enough.
- **Outline:** two linked points about the named business.
- **Analyse:** chains of reasoning using the facts given, such as rents, footfall or delivery needs.
- **Justify:** pick one of two sites or approaches, compare both using the data, and finish with what the choice depends on, for example whether the sales forecast is reliable.
