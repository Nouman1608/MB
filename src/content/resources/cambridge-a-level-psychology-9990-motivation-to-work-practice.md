**This practice set covers topic 4.1, Motivation to work, in Specialist Option 4 of Cambridge International A Level Psychology 9990 (syllabus for 2024 to 2026).** Section A uses Paper 3 style short answers and a structured essay. Section B uses Paper 4 style scenarios with research methods. All organisations, employees and data are fictional.

## Section A: Paper 3 style questions

**1.** Outline **two** levels of Maslow's hierarchy of needs, with a workplace example of each. **[4]**

**2.** Explain the difference between profit-sharing and performance-related pay. **[2]**

**3.** Outline the three needs proposed by self-determination theory. **[3]**

**4.** Explain **two** principles of goal-setting theory. **[4]**

**5.** Cash rewards are widely used in organisations.

**(a)** Describe study 1 of Landry et al. (2019). **[6]**

**(b)** Evaluate study 1 of Landry et al. (2019), including a discussion of generalisations from findings. **[10]**

## Section B: Paper 4 style questions

**6.** A manager tells her team: "Try to do better this quarter."

**(a)** Rewrite this as a SMART goal for a sales team. **[2]**

**(b)** Using goal-setting theory, explain why your goal should improve performance more than the original. **[2]**

**7.** Kofi is a nurse who is well paid and has a secure contract, but he says he feels lonely at work and no one notices what he does.

**(a)** Using Maslow's hierarchy, identify **two** needs that are not being met. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest **two** intrinsic motivators the hospital could use. **[2]**

**8.** A company plans to give staff a bonus. A psychologist advises using self-determination theory.

**(a)** Suggest how the company should present the bonus, using Landry et al. (2019). **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why the way it is presented could affect performance. **[3]**

**9.** A psychologist wants to measure need for achievement in 60 managers.

**(a)** Suggest **one** closed question she could include in a questionnaire. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain **one** reason why a questionnaire might not give a valid measure of need for achievement. **[2]**

**10.** In a study (fictional data), employees rated expectancy, instrumentality and valence each from 0 to 1. Employee A scored 0.9, 0.8 and 0.5. Employee B scored 0.9, 0.0 and 1.0.

**(a)** Calculate the motivation score for each employee using V × I × E. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why Employee B's motivation is so low, despite valuing the reward highly. **[2]**

**11.** Compare need theories and cognitive theories of motivation. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Two marks per level: the level named and explained (1) and a workplace example (1). For example: safety, the need for security and protection (1), met by a permanent contract or safe equipment (1); esteem, the need for respect and status (1), met by recognition or promotion (1).

**2.** Up to 2 marks. Profit-sharing gives employees a share of the company's overall profits (1), whereas performance-related pay links each individual's pay to their own measured performance (1).

**3.** One mark each. Competence: the need to feel effective and capable (1). Autonomy: the need to feel in control of one's own actions (1). Relatedness: the need to feel connected to others (1).

**4.** Two marks per principle. Clarity: goals should be specific and measurable (1), so the person knows exactly what to aim for and can direct effort (1). Feedback: progress should be reported (1), so effort can be adjusted to reach the goal (1). Credit challenge, commitment or task complexity with explanation.

**5.**

**(a)** Up to 6 marks. Aim: to test whether the functional meaning of a cash reward affects motivation and performance, using self-determination theory (1). 123 French-speaking students at a Canadian university were randomly allocated to two conditions (1). All were offered a $10 coffee-shop gift card (1), presented either as a token of appreciation (autonomy-supportive) or as a means of reinforcing performance standards (controlling) (1). They completed motivation questionnaires and then had two minutes to solve 25 anagrams (1). The autonomy-supportive group reported more need satisfaction and intrinsic motivation and performed better; in the controlling group, need frustration predicted extrinsic motivation, which did not predict performance (1).

**(b)** Level-based: 9-10 marks for a detailed, balanced evaluation including the named issue, with a conclusion. Indicative content:

- *Generalisations from findings*: students on one course at one Canadian university are not representative of employees; a $10 card and two-minute anagram task are unlike salaries and real jobs, so conclusions about workplace pay must be cautious; the effect of framing might be larger or smaller with real money and long-term employment.
- *Strengths*: random allocation and an identical reward isolate framing as the cause; standardised task.
- *Self-report*: need satisfaction and motivation were measured by questionnaire, open to social desirability.
- *Application*: managers can frame rewards as appreciation at little cost.
- *Reductionism*: one framing manipulation cannot capture all influences on motivation.
- *Conclusion*: a well-controlled demonstration of the importance of reward meaning, needing workplace replication.

**6.**

**(a)** Up to 2 marks. One mark for a goal that is specific and measurable, one for being time-bound and achievable, e.g. "Increase monthly sales by 10% compared with last quarter by 31 March" (2).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. A specific, challenging goal directs effort towards a clear target (1) and allows feedback on progress, whereas "do better" is vague, so people may not try as hard (1).

**7.**

**(a)** One mark each: love and belonging, because he feels lonely (1); esteem, because no one notices his work (1).

**(b)** One mark each: recognition such as praise from managers or an award (1); a sense of belonging through team activities or mentoring (1).

**8.**

**(a)** Up to 2 marks. Present the bonus as a token of appreciation for staff contributions (1), not as a way to enforce performance standards (1).

**(b)** Up to 3 marks. An appreciative framing supports the needs for competence, autonomy and relatedness (1), which increases intrinsic motivation (1); Landry et al. found intrinsic motivation was linked to better performance, whereas a controlling framing led to extrinsic motivation, which did not predict performance (1).

**9.**

**(a)** One mark for a closed question measuring achievement, e.g. "I prefer tasks that are challenging rather than easy. Agree/Disagree" (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. Managers may give socially desirable answers, as high achievement is valued in their role (1), so scores may overestimate their real need for achievement (1).

**10.**

**(a)** One mark each. A: 0.9 × 0.8 × 0.5 = 0.36 (1). B: 0.9 × 0.0 × 1.0 = 0 (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. Employee B's instrumentality is zero: they do not believe good performance will lead to the reward (1). Because the terms multiply, a zero on any one makes motivation zero (1).

**11.** Up to 4 marks. Similarity: both try to explain why people put effort into work (1). Difference: need theories focus on content, what motivates, such as Maslow's five needs (1), whereas cognitive theories focus on process, how people judge goals and rewards, as in VIE theory (1). Need theories are more deterministic, while cognitive theories treat the worker as actively choosing (1).

## Where marks are usually lost

- In question 1, naming a level without a workplace example.
- In question 5(a), stating that one group received more money.
- In question 10(a), adding the values instead of multiplying.
