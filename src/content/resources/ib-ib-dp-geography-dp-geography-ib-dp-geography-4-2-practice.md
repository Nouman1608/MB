## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between a primary and a secondary hazard. **[2]**

**2.** Give two secondary hazards that can follow an earthquake. **[2]**

**3.** A region averages 3 earthquakes of magnitude 6 or more per decade. Using the tenfold rule, estimate the number of magnitude 5 or more per decade. **[1]**

**4.** Outline why the area affected by ash fall is usually elongated. **[2]**

**5.** Define fatalism as a response to hazards. **[1]**

## Section B

**6.** The table shows invented risk data for a city.

| Scenario | Annual probability | Loss if it happens (million) |
|---|---|---|
| Moderate earthquake | 1 in 25 | 50 |
| Major earthquake | 1 in 250 | 2,000 |

**(a)** Calculate the expected annual loss for each scenario and the total. **[3]**

**(b)** A building code reduces the loss from a major earthquake by 40 per cent. Calculate the new total expected annual loss. **[2]**

**(c)** Outline one limitation of expected annual loss as a measure of risk. **[1]**

**7.** Explain why two districts at the same distance from an earthquake's epicentre may experience very different shaking. **[4]**

**8.** Explain why rare, large earthquakes matter more for planning than frequent, small ones. **[3]**

**9.** Explain two factors that influence how people perceive the risk from a nearby volcano. **[4]**

## Section C

**10.** With reference to examples, examine why secondary hazards can cause greater loss of life than primary hazards. **[10]**

**11.** "Human factors are more important than physical factors in determining geophysical hazard risk." Discuss this statement. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** A primary hazard is caused directly by the geophysical event, such as ground shaking or a pyroclastic flow [1]; a secondary hazard is triggered by the event, such as a landslide or tsunami following an earthquake [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any two [1] each: landslides; liquefaction; tsunamis; fires; dam failure. **[2]**

**3.** About 3 x 10 = **30** [1]. **[1]**

**4.** Ash is carried by the wind after being ejected into the atmosphere [1], so it is deposited mostly downwind of the volcano, stretching the affected area in the direction of the prevailing wind [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Accepting hazard losses as inevitable, so little or no action is taken to prepare or reduce risk [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** Moderate: 0.04 x 50 = **2.0 million** [1]. Major: 0.004 x 2,000 = **8.0 million** [1]. Total = **10.0 million** [1].

**(b)** New major-event loss = 2,000 x 0.6 = 1,200 million, so expected loss = 0.004 x 1,200 = 4.8 million [1]. New total = 2.0 + 4.8 = **6.8 million** [1].

**(c)** Any one [1]: probabilities of rare events are uncertain; loss of life, culture and wellbeing is hard to value in money; it averages losses that in reality arrive suddenly and unevenly. **[6]**

**7.** A district built on bedrock transmits seismic waves with little amplification [1]. A district on soft sediments, such as a former lake bed, slows and amplifies the waves, so shaking is stronger and lasts longer [1], as in Mexico City in 1985 [1]. Saturated sandy ground may also liquefy, and resonance between sediment and buildings can magnify damage [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Large earthquakes are much rarer but each unit of magnitude releases about 32 times more energy [1], so they account for most of the energy released and most of the damage [1]. Planning only for frequent, small events would leave buildings and people unprotected against the events that cause most losses [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Experience: people who have lived through an eruption, or whose families remember one, tend to take the risk more seriously [1], while a long gap since the last eruption lets memory fade [1]. Economic dependence: farmers relying on fertile volcanic soils may accept the risk because leaving would cost their livelihood [1], so perceived risk is weighed against income [1]. (Accept: trust in authorities, education, culture or religion.) **[4]**

**10.** Indicative content:

- **Why secondary hazards are deadly:** they can travel far beyond the primary footprint [1]; they arrive after the primary event, when people think the danger has passed [1].
- **Examples:** lahars from the 1985 eruption of Nevado del Ruiz buried Armero, far from the summit [1]; tsunamis after large subduction earthquakes, such as the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami, killed people across many countries hours after the shaking [1]; earthquake-triggered landslides in mountain regions bury villages and block rescue routes [1]; fires after earthquakes in cities where gas lines break [1].
- **Counter-points:** in many earthquakes, building collapse from primary shaking causes most deaths [1]; pyroclastic flows close to the vent are the deadliest volcanic hazard for those within reach [1].
- **Examination:** the balance depends on the setting, warning systems and where people live [1].
- **Conclusion:** secondary hazards often cause the greatest loss of life because their reach and timing catch people unprepared, but this is not universal [1]. **[10]**

**11.** Indicative content:

- **Physical factors:** magnitude and type of hazard set the potential scale [1]; frequency and predictability affect warning [1]; ground conditions amplify shaking, as in Mexico City in 1985 [1]; depth of focus and distance matter [1].
- **Human factors:** exposure from population growth and urbanisation in hazard zones [1]; vulnerability from building quality, poverty and governance [1]; perception and preparedness [1].
- **Interaction:** human choices determine exposure to physical factors, for example building on soft sediments [1].
- **Evidence:** similar-magnitude earthquakes cause very different losses depending on building codes and wealth [1].
- **Conclusion:** physical factors set the hazard, but human factors usually decide whether it becomes a disaster, so the statement is largely true [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* Show how physical and human factors combine. A judgement that human factors decide outcomes while physical factors set potential scale is more convincing than choosing one side only.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Adding probabilities instead of multiplying them by losses.
- Forgetting to recalculate the total after changing one scenario.
- Explaining shaking differences with distance only.
- Treating perception as ignorance without economic context.
