This guide goes deeper into section **3.2.1 Photosynthesis** of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Biology (9201)
specification, Version 5.2, for exams in May/June 2018 onwards. The qualification is untiered, and content from
any part of the specification can be assessed on either paper. Section 3.2.1 is shared with Combined Science, and
it contains a **required practical**: investigate how variables affect the rate of photosynthesis.

The existing [Photosynthesis, limiting factors and transport in plants study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-photosynthesis-limiting-factors-and-transport-in-plants/)
covers 3.2.1 and 3.2.2 together. This page concentrates on 3.2.1 and adds new examples: interacting factors over
a day, planning the practical, and following glucose through the plant. Afterwards, use the
[Photosynthesis revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-9201-photosynthesis-oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-revision-notes/)

## What 3.2.1 asks you to know

| Statement | Content |
|---|---|
| a | Word and balanced symbol equations, with light as the energy source |
| b | Light absorbed by chlorophyll in chloroplasts (in some plant cells and in algae); carbon dioxide from the air and water from the soil converted into glucose; oxygen released as a by-product |
| c | Rate limited by low temperature, shortage of carbon dioxide or shortage of light; the factors interact; the economics of enhancing temperature, carbon dioxide and light in greenhouses |
| d | Glucose used for respiration, or converted to starch, fat or oil, cellulose or proteins |
| e | Plants also use nitrate ions from the soil to make proteins |

## The equations as a record of atoms

```
                 light
carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen
                 light
6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂
```

Each raw material has a source the specification names: **carbon dioxide from the air**, entering the leaf, and
**water from the soil**, absorbed by the roots. Light is not a reactant; it is the energy source, which is why it
is written over the arrow. Photosynthesis transfers energy from the environment into glucose, so the products
store more chemical energy than the reactants.

## Interacting limiting factors

The specification says the factors **interact** and any one of them may limit the rate. The factor that is
in shortest supply sets the rate. Improving any other factor makes no difference until the limiting factor is
improved.

Temperature behaves differently from light and carbon dioxide. Photosynthesis is controlled by enzymes, so at
**low temperature** the reactions are slow. Raising the temperature increases the rate up to an optimum. Well
above that, the enzymes are damaged and the rate falls. Light and carbon dioxide do not show this fall: their
graphs rise and then level off.

**Worked example 1: a day in a closed greenhouse** (invented data). The vents are closed all day.

| Time | 06:00 | 09:00 | 12:00 | 15:00 | 18:00 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Light (% of maximum) | 10 | 60 | 100 | 70 | 15 |
| CO₂ (ppm) | 600 | 420 | 250 | 230 | 300 |
| Temperature (°C) | 14 | 20 | 26 | 25 | 19 |

Reasoning, one time at a time:

- **06:00**: light is very low and the air is cool. Carbon dioxide is plentiful because respiration overnight
  added it. Light is the most likely limiting factor.
- **12:00**: light is at its maximum and the temperature is warm. Carbon dioxide has fallen from 600 to 250 ppm
  because photosynthesis has used it up. Carbon dioxide is now the most likely limiting factor.
- **18:00**: light is falling again, so light limits once more, and carbon dioxide starts to build up because
  respiration now releases more than photosynthesis removes.

The lesson for a grower: one factor does not limit all day. Adding carbon dioxide at midday would help;
adding it at dawn would be wasted money.

## Greenhouse economics

Growers can enhance **temperature** (heaters), **carbon dioxide concentration** (burning fuel such as paraffin
or releasing bottled gas) and **light intensity** (lamps). A paraffin heater raises both temperature and carbon
dioxide at once, because burning the fuel releases heat and carbon dioxide.

To decide whether a change is worth paying for, answer three questions:

1. Is this factor actually limiting at the time it would be supplied?
2. How much extra crop will the change produce?
3. Is the value of the extra crop greater than the extra cost?

**Worked example 2** (invented figures). A grower adds lamps for four extra hours each night. The lamps cost
£15 per night to run. The extra growth is worth £22 per night in October but only £9 per night in June, when
daylight is long.

```
October: 22 − 15 = +£7 per night  → worthwhile
June:    9 − 15  = −£6 per night  → not worthwhile
```

In June, natural light already supplies most of what the plants can use, so light is rarely limiting and extra
lamps add little growth.

## What happens to the glucose

The glucose produced may be used as a source of chemical energy or converted into larger molecules for storage
and use later.

| Use | Why the plant does it |
|---|---|
| Respiration | Transfers energy for the plant's life processes |
| Insoluble starch for storage | Starch does not dissolve, so it can be stored in large amounts |
| Fat or oil for storage | An energy-rich store, for example in many seeds |
| Cellulose | Strengthens the cell wall |
| Proteins | For growth; this also needs nitrate ions absorbed from the soil |

Glucose contains carbon, hydrogen and oxygen only. Proteins also contain nitrogen, which the plant gets from
**nitrate ions** absorbed by the roots. This is why a plant in nitrate-poor soil grows poorly even in bright
light.

## Planning the required practical

The required practical is to **investigate how variables affect the rate of photosynthesis**. The specification
expects you to be able to design the procedure and control variables.

| Variable to change | How to change it | What to keep the same |
|---|---|---|
| Light intensity | Move a lamp to set distances (check with a light meter) | Temperature, carbon dioxide, same plant |
| Carbon dioxide concentration | Use different concentrations of sodium hydrogencarbonate solution | Light, temperature, same plant |
| Temperature | Water bath at set temperatures | Light, carbon dioxide, same plant |

Measure the rate by collecting the oxygen given off by a water plant, for example in a gas syringe or an upturned
measuring cylinder, over a fixed time.

**Worked example 3: rate from a gas syringe.** Readings at one temperature:

| Time (min) | 0 | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Volume of oxygen (cm³) | 0.0 | 0.6 | 1.2 | 1.8 | 2.4 |

```
rate = 2.4 cm³ ÷ 8 min = 0.30 cm³ per minute
```

The volume rises by the same amount every 2 minutes, so the rate is constant over this time.

## Common mistakes

- Treating light as a reactant in the equation.
- Saying chlorophyll is in all plant cells; it is in chloroplasts in **some** plant cells and in algae.
- Naming the same limiting factor for every point on a graph or every time of day.
- Saying high temperature "speeds up photosynthesis" without an optimum; enzymes are damaged at high temperature.
- Forgetting nitrate ions when explaining protein production.
- Recommending a greenhouse change without checking whether the factor is limiting.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** a limiting factor: name the factor, say why it limits here, and say what would happen if it were
  increased.
- **Evaluate** a greenhouse change: compare the value of the extra yield with the cost, and check timing.
- **Describe** a method: state what you change, measure and keep the same, and how you repeat it.
- **Calculate** a rate: volume ÷ time, with units such as cm³ per minute.
