Section 3.1 of the Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 syllabus (2026–2028) has seven outcomes. You must state the formulae of the elements and compounds named in the subject content; define **molecular formula** and **empirical formula**; deduce formulae from models and diagrams and from the charges on ions; "construct word equations, symbol equations and ionic equations to show how reactants form products, including state symbols"; and deduce a symbol equation with state symbols "given relevant information". All seven are required for every 5070 candidate. Calculating empirical formulae from masses belongs to section 3.3.

The [Formulae, Equations and the Mole guide](/resources/formulae-equations-and-the-mole/) introduces these skills briefly. This guide goes further on the parts that cost marks: compound ions and brackets, the difference between molecular and empirical formulae, ionic equations, and turning a description into a balanced equation.

## Formulae of elements

- **Metals** and **noble gases** are written as single atoms: Na, Fe, Cu, He, Ar.
- **Giant covalent elements** are written as single atoms: C (diamond or graphite), Si.
- Seven common elements exist as **diatomic molecules**: H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂.

Whenever one of the diatomic elements appears as an element in an equation, it must have the subscript 2. "H + Cl → HCl" is wrong; hydrogen gas is H₂.

## Ions you must know

| Positive ions | | Negative ions | |
|---|---|---|---|
| hydrogen | H⁺ | chloride | Cl⁻ |
| sodium, potassium, lithium | Na⁺, K⁺, Li⁺ | bromide, iodide | Br⁻, I⁻ |
| silver | Ag⁺ | hydroxide | OH⁻ |
| ammonium | NH₄⁺ | nitrate | NO₃⁻ |
| magnesium, calcium, barium | Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺, Ba²⁺ | oxide | O²⁻ |
| copper(II), zinc, lead(II) | Cu²⁺, Zn²⁺, Pb²⁺ | sulfide | S²⁻ |
| iron(II) | Fe²⁺ | sulfate | SO₄²⁻ |
| iron(III), aluminium | Fe³⁺, Al³⁺ | carbonate | CO₃²⁻ |

A Roman numeral in a name gives the charge on the metal ion: iron(II) is Fe²⁺ and iron(III) is Fe³⁺.

## Deducing ionic formulae from charges

The total positive charge must equal the total negative charge. When more than one **compound ion** (an ion made of several atoms, such as OH⁻ or SO₄²⁻) is needed, put it in **brackets**.

**Worked example 1.** Write the formulae of calcium hydroxide, ammonium sulfate and iron(III) nitrate.

| Compound | Ions | Balancing | Formula |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calcium hydroxide | Ca²⁺, OH⁻ | one 2+ needs two 1− | Ca(OH)₂ |
| Ammonium sulfate | NH₄⁺, SO₄²⁻ | two 1+ balance one 2− | (NH₄)₂SO₄ |
| Iron(III) nitrate | Fe³⁺, NO₃⁻ | one 3+ needs three 1− | Fe(NO₃)₃ |

Without brackets, CaOH₂ would mean one oxygen and two hydrogens: a different (and impossible) substance. Brackets are not needed when there is only one compound ion: sodium hydroxide is NaOH, and calcium carbonate is CaCO₃.

## Molecular and empirical formulae

- **Molecular formula**: the number and type of different atoms in one molecule.
- **Empirical formula**: the simplest whole number ratio of the different atoms or ions in a compound.

To get an empirical formula from a molecular formula, divide every subscript by the largest number that divides them all.

| Compound | Molecular formula | Empirical formula |
|---|---|---|
| Ethene | C₂H₄ | CH₂ |
| Butane | C₄H₁₀ | C₂H₅ |
| Hydrogen peroxide | H₂O₂ | HO |
| Glucose | C₆H₁₂O₆ | CH₂O |
| Water | H₂O | H₂O |
| Methanol | CH₄O (CH₃OH) | CH₄O |

Two points that are often tested:

- The molecular and empirical formula can be the **same** (water, methanol).
- **Ionic compounds** and **giant covalent** substances have only an empirical formula. They contain no molecules, so the formula is the simplest ratio of ions or atoms: MgO, SiO₂.

## Deducing a formula from a model or diagram

Count the atoms of each element. For a **molecule**, give every atom (do not simplify). For a **lattice**, give the simplest ratio.

**Worked example 2.** A ball-and-stick model shows a molecule with three carbon atoms in a chain and eight hydrogen atoms. A second diagram shows part of an ionic lattice in which there are twice as many sodium ions as sulfide ions.

*Answer.* The molecule is **C₃H₈** (all atoms counted). The lattice is **Na₂S** (ratio 2 : 1).

## Writing equations

### Word and symbol equations

A word equation names reactants and products. A **symbol equation** uses formulae and is **balanced**: the same number of each kind of atom on both sides. Balance by changing the **numbers in front** of formulae, never the subscripts.

### State symbols

(s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) aqueous (dissolved in water).

Key judgements: water as a liquid is (l), not (aq); a precipitate is (s); a gas given off is (g); an acid or a dissolved salt is (aq).

### Ionic equations

An ionic equation shows only the particles that **change** in a reaction. Ions that are present but unchanged, **spectator ions**, are left out.

Method:

1. Write the balanced equation with state symbols.
2. Split every **aqueous ionic** substance into its ions. Leave solids, liquids, gases and covalent molecules as they are.
3. Cross out ions that appear unchanged on both sides.
4. Check that atoms and charges both balance.

**Worked example 3 (precipitation).** Aqueous barium chloride reacts with aqueous sodium sulfate.

BaCl₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2NaCl(aq)

Split: Ba²⁺(aq) + 2Cl⁻(aq) + 2Na⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2Na⁺(aq) + 2Cl⁻(aq)

Cancel Na⁺ and Cl⁻: **Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s)**

**Worked example 4 (displacement).** Magnesium reacts with aqueous copper(II) sulfate.

Mg(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → MgSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)

Sulfate is a spectator: **Mg(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + Cu(s)**. Charge check: 2+ on each side.

## Deducing equations from information

Questions often describe a reaction in words. Pick out every substance and its state, write the formulae, then balance.

**Worked example 5.** When lithium is added to water, it fizzes, producing hydrogen gas and a solution of lithium hydroxide. Write the equation with state symbols.

*Answer.*
- Reactants: lithium, Li(s); water, H₂O(l).
- Products: lithium hydroxide in solution, LiOH(aq); hydrogen gas, H₂(g).
- Unbalanced: Li + H₂O → LiOH + H₂. Hydrogen atoms: 2 on the left, 3 on the right.
- Balanced: **2Li(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2LiOH(aq) + H₂(g)**.

## Common mistakes

- Writing Cl, O or H instead of Cl₂, O₂ or H₂ for the elements.
- Leaving out brackets: CaOH₂ for Ca(OH)₂, NH₄₂SO₄ for (NH₄)₂SO₄.
- Simplifying a molecular formula when the question asks for the molecular formula.
- Changing a subscript to balance an equation.
- Writing H₂O(aq).
- In ionic equations, splitting a solid precipitate or a metal into ions.

## Exam technique

- **State** a formula: one formula, correct case (Co is cobalt, CO is carbon monoxide).
- **Define**: use the syllabus wording for molecular and empirical formula.
- **Deduce**: show the charges or the counting.
- **Construct** an equation: include state symbols whenever the question mentions them, and check both atoms and charges.
