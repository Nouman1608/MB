## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Market structure | The way a market is set up: how many sellers, how alike their goods are, how open it is to newcomers |
| Product differentiation | Features that make one seller's good stand apart from rivals' (brand, quality, location, service) |
| Barrier to entry | Any obstacle that stops newcomers joining the market easily |
| Price taker | A seller too small to influence the market price, so it accepts whatever price the market sets |
| Total revenue | Price × quantity |
| Profit | Total revenue − total costs |
| Supernormal profit | Profit above the level needed to keep a firm in the market |
| Normal profit | The minimum profit needed to keep the firm in the market |
| Market share | Firm's sales ÷ total market sales × 100 |
| Survival, growth, market share | Non-profit objectives named in the specification |

## 3.1.4.1 Market structures: the three distinguishing features

1. How many firms compete
2. How different the products are
3. How easily new firms can enter

**Rule of thumb:** many firms + identical product + free entry = most competitive; one firm + blocked entry = least competitive; most real markets lie between.

| Market (illustrative) | Firms | Product | Entry | Closest to |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Street stalls selling the same fruit | Many | Identical | Easy | Highly competitive |
| Cafes in a city centre | Dozens | Differentiated | Fairly easy | Many sellers, differentiated |
| Domestic flights on one route | 2 or 3 | Similar | Costly | A few large firms |

**Why it matters for behaviour:** structure decides whether a firm takes the price, competes on quality and branding, or watches rivals' reactions.

## 3.1.4.2 Objectives of firms

- Profit is an important objective for most firms.
- Other objectives: survival, growth, increasing market share.
- Objectives affect behaviour. A detailed knowledge of each objective is not required.

| Objective | Likely behaviour |
|---|---|
| Profit | Cut costs, set prices to earn more, close weak lines |
| Survival | Cut price or costs, delay investment, accept short-run loss |
| Growth | Accept lower profit now to expand |
| Market share | Price cuts and promotion to win customers |

**Method in steps: comparing two plans.**
1. Revenue = P × Q for each plan.
2. Total cost = fixed + (variable cost per unit × Q).
3. Profit = TR − TC.
4. Market share = firm's Q ÷ market Q × 100.
5. Compare changes in percent; for share, use percentage points.
6. Link the plan to the objective that would favour it.

**Worked reminder.** A small print shop (invented). Plan A: price £10, 3,000 jobs, fixed costs £9,000, variable £4 per job. Plan B: price £8.50, 4,500 jobs.
- A: TR = 30,000; TC = 9,000 + 12,000 = 21,000; profit **£9,000**.
- B: TR = 38,250; TC = 9,000 + 18,000 = 27,000; profit **£11,250**.
- Market of 30,000 jobs: share 10% to 15%, a rise of **5 percentage points**.

Here the growth plan also raises profit, because the extra sales add £4.50 each above variable cost: (8.50 − 4) × 4,500 = 20,250 against (10 − 4) × 3,000 = 18,000, a difference of 2,250. Not every growth plan sacrifices profit; check the numbers.

**Break-even** quantity = fixed costs ÷ (price − variable cost per unit). Print shop at Plan A: 9,000 ÷ 6 = **1,500 jobs**. Margin of safety: (3,000 − 1,500) ÷ 3,000 = 50%.

**Survival logic (short run):** keep producing if price is above variable cost per unit, because each unit adds towards fixed costs. Stop if price is below variable cost. Long run: revenue must cover all costs.

## 3.1.4.3 Competitive markets

**Main characteristics of perfect competition:**
- many buyers and sellers, none able to influence price
- identical products
- no barriers to entry or exit
- good information on prices

**Price:** set by the interaction of market demand and supply. Each firm is a price taker.

**Method in steps: finding equilibrium from equations.**
1. Put the demand and supply equations equal to each other.
2. Rearrange to find P.
3. Put P back into both equations; they must give the same Q.

Worked reminder: Qd = 500 − 10P and Qs = 20P − 100 give 600 = 30P, so **P = 20**, Q = 300. After a demand rise to Qd = 560 − 10P: 660 = 30P, **P = 22**, Q = 340.

Entry reminder: if typical average cost is £20 a unit, the price of £22 gives £2 of supernormal profit on each of 340 units, £680 in total. Entry shifts supply to Qs = 20P − 40: 560 − 10P = 20P − 40, so 600 = 30P, **P = 20**, Q = 360. Price falls by (20 − 22) ÷ 22 × 100 = −9.09% and quantity rises by (360 − 340) ÷ 340 × 100 = +5.88%. Supernormal profit is now zero.

**Why profits are likely lower than in a market dominated by a few large firms** (chain of reasoning):
1. No barriers.
2. Supernormal profit attracts entry.
3. Supply increases.
4. Price falls.
5. Supernormal profit disappears, leaving normal profit.

Where a few firms dominate and barriers are high, entry cannot compete the profit away, and with few rivals there is less pressure to cut price.

**Evaluation points (use two or three):**
- Short run: a competitive market can earn supernormal profit before entry occurs.
- Large firms may have lower costs from economies of scale, so profit can be high even at a lower price.
- A price war between the few firms can reduce profit.
- The threat of new entry can restrain large firms' prices.
- How quickly entry happens depends on how high the barriers are in practice.

**Factors affecting competitiveness:** number of firms; barriers to entry (high start-up cost, legal protection, brand loyalty, cost advantages of large existing firms); degree of product differentiation; how well informed buyers are.

## Must-know distinctions

- Profit versus revenue: a plan can raise revenue and reduce profit.
- Percent change versus percentage-point change.
- Price taker versus price setter.
- Short-run versus long-run profit in competitive markets.
- Supernormal versus normal profit.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the three features used to tell market structures apart.
2. Give the formula for profit.
3. A firm sells 400 items at £25 with fixed costs of £4,000 and variable costs of £10 per item. Find profit.
4. Its market sells 5,000 items. A growth plan raises its sales to 600. Find the change in market share in percentage points.
5. State two other objectives of firms besides profit.
6. Why is a farm in a perfectly competitive market a price taker?
7. Solve Qd = 800 − 20P and Qs = 30P − 200 for the equilibrium price and quantity.
8. Give the five-step reason profits tend to be lower in a market with no barriers to entry.
9. Give two factors that affect how competitive a market is.
10. Give one reason a competitive market might not earn smaller profits than a market run by a few big firms.

### Answers

1. How many firms compete, how different the products are, how easily new firms can enter.
2. Profit = total revenue − total costs.
3. TR = 400 × 25 = £10,000; TC = 4,000 + 4,000 = £8,000; profit **£2,000**.
4. Before 400 ÷ 5,000 = 8%; after 600 ÷ 5,000 = 12%; a rise of **4 percentage points**.
5. Any two of: survival, growth, increasing market share.
6. It is too small to affect the market price and sells an identical product, so charging more loses all its buyers.
7. 800 − 20P = 30P − 200, so 1,000 = 50P, **P = 20**; Q = 800 − 400 = **400**.
8. No barriers, supernormal profit attracts entry, supply rises, price falls, supernormal profit disappears.
9. Any two of: number of firms, barriers to entry, product differentiation, information.
10. In the short run, supernormal profit can exist before entry; or large firms' economies of scale may leave them with lower costs; or a price war may cut the few firms' profit.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying a firm in perfect competition "chooses" its price.
- Quoting a market share change in percent instead of percentage points.
- Treating survival, growth and profit as identical objectives.
- Computing profit from revenue alone, forgetting fixed costs.
- Listing characteristics without linking them to price, profit or behaviour.
- Stating that competitive markets earn no profit, without distinguishing short and long run.
- Asserting that big firms always earn more profit, with no evaluation.
- Using real-world statistics you cannot support.
