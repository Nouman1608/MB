## Water: one gradient from soil to air

Water moves through a plant because water potential falls from the soil to the air. No energy from ATP is used to move water through the xylem; the energy for evaporation comes from the Sun.

### Worked example 1: following the gradient

The values below are invented for practice but have the right pattern.

| Location | Water potential / kPa |
|---|---|
| Soil water | −30 |
| Root hair cell | −250 |
| Root xylem | −500 |
| Leaf xylem | −800 |
| Mesophyll cell wall | −1 200 |
| Air in leaf spaces | −7 000 |
| Atmosphere (dry day) | −95 000 |

Each value is lower (more negative) than the one before, so water moves along the whole chain. The largest drop is between the leaf and the atmosphere. If the air becomes humid, its water potential rises towards zero, that last gradient shrinks, and transpiration slows, which then reduces the pull on the whole column.

## Soil to xylem

**Some mineral ions and organic compounds are transported dissolved in water.** That single statement (7.2.1) explains why the xylem carries ions such as nitrate and magnesium, and why the phloem carries sucrose and amino acids: in both, the solvent is water.

### The apoplast pathway

- Water travels through the **cellulose cell walls** and the spaces between cells.
- Cellulose microfibrils are separated by water-filled spaces; water is attracted to the cellulose by hydrogen bonds. As water ahead moves on, cohesion drags the water behind it, so the apoplast offers little resistance.
- In the xylem, walls are thickened with **lignin**, which keeps the vessels open; water enters and leaves through pits.

### The symplast pathway

- Water crosses a cell surface membrane into a root hair cell by osmosis, then moves from cytoplasm to cytoplasm through **plasmodesmata**, down a water potential gradient.
- Because it crosses a membrane at the start, the plant can select which ions enter.

### The endodermis: where the routes meet

The **Casparian strip** is a band of **suberin** in the radial and end walls of endodermal cells. Suberin is waterproof, so water in the apoplast cannot pass through the wall at that point. It must cross the endodermal cell surface membrane into the cytoplasm, joining the symplast.

Why this matters: every ion reaching the xylem has passed through a partially permeable membrane with specific transport proteins, so toxic ions can be kept out and wanted ions taken in. A common exam slip is to say the strip is made of lignin; the 9700 syllabus names **suberin**.

## Transpiration

**Transpiration** is the evaporation of water from the internal surfaces of leaves, the walls of the mesophyll cells, followed by the diffusion of water vapour from the air spaces through the stomata to the atmosphere.

Two separate processes are named, and each needs its own gradient:

1. **Evaporation**: liquid water in the mesophyll cell walls becomes vapour in the air spaces, which stay close to saturated.
2. **Diffusion**: water vapour moves from the air spaces (high water vapour concentration) out through open stomata to the drier air outside.

Anything that steepens the water vapour gradient (dry or moving air) or speeds evaporation (higher temperature) increases transpiration. Closing the stomata stops most of the diffusion.

## Up the xylem: cohesion-tension

### Hydrogen bonding is the key

Water molecules are polar. The slightly negative oxygen of one molecule is attracted to the slightly positive hydrogen of another, forming a **hydrogen bond**. This gives two effects:

- **Cohesion**: water molecules hydrogen bond to each other, so a column of water behaves like a continuous thread that can be pulled without breaking.
- **Adhesion**: water molecules hydrogen bond to **cellulose** in the walls, which helps hold the column in place and draws water up narrow spaces.

### The mechanism as a chain of causes

1. Water evaporates from mesophyll cell walls. Water moves into those walls from neighbouring cells and from xylem in the leaf veins.
2. Removing water from the top of the xylem lowers the pressure there; the column is under **tension** (negative pressure).
3. Because of cohesion, the whole column is pulled upwards as a unit: the **transpiration pull**.
4. Adhesion to cellulose supports the column and resists it falling back.
5. Lignin stops the vessel walls being pulled inwards and collapsing.

### Evidence you may be asked to interpret

- Trunks and stems become measurably narrower in the middle of a sunny day, when transpiration is fastest, because the tension pulls the xylem walls slightly inwards.
- If a xylem vessel is cut, air is drawn **in**, not water pushed out, showing the contents are under tension.

**Worked example 2.** A dendrometer records a tree trunk diameter of 312.40 mm at 06:00 and 312.16 mm at 14:00. Calculate the percentage decrease and explain it.

```
decrease = 312.40 − 312.16 = 0.24 mm
percentage = 0.24 ÷ 312.40 × 100 = 0.077 %
```

The change is small but consistent with the xylem being under greater tension during the afternoon, when transpiration is fastest.

## Xerophytes: annotated drawings

A **label** names a feature; an **annotation** explains how it reduces water loss. Every annotation should mention the **water vapour gradient**, the **distance for diffusion** or the **surface area** for evaporation.

Example annotations for a rolled leaf with sunken stomata:

- "Leaf rolled with stomata on the inner surface: water vapour is trapped inside the roll, so the water vapour gradient between the air spaces and the outside air is reduced."
- "Stomata in pits: still, humid air collects in each pit, reducing diffusion of water vapour out."
- "Hairs inside the roll: trap a layer of still, moist air, increasing the diffusion distance."
- "Thick waxy cuticle on the outer surface: reduces evaporation directly through the epidermis."

Draw a plan diagram first (tissue outlines, correct proportions), then add these annotations with ruled lines.

## Phloem: sources, sinks and loading

**Assimilates** dissolved in water, such as **sucrose and amino acids**, move in phloem sieve tubes from **sources** to **sinks**. Transport can be in either direction in the stem, depending on where sources and sinks are at that time.

### Loading at the source

1. Proton pumps in the companion cell membrane hydrolyse ATP to move **H⁺ out** of the cell. This is active transport and sets up an H⁺ concentration gradient (and an electrical gradient) across the membrane.
2. H⁺ diffuse back in through **cotransporter proteins**. Each cotransporter will only move H⁺ if sucrose binds at the same time, so sucrose is carried in **against its own concentration gradient**.
3. Sucrose builds up in the companion cell and passes into the sieve tube element through plasmodesmata.

Sucrose is not pumped directly; the energy from ATP is used to make the proton gradient, and that gradient drives sucrose entry. This is often called indirect or secondary active transport.

### Mass flow

1. A high sucrose concentration lowers the water potential of the sap at the source.
2. Water enters the sieve tube by osmosis, mostly from nearby xylem, raising the **hydrostatic pressure**.
3. At a sink, sucrose leaves the sieve tube and is used in respiration or converted to starch, which keeps its concentration low in the sink cells.
4. Water follows by osmosis, so hydrostatic pressure at the sink is lower.
5. Sap flows by **mass flow** down the hydrostatic pressure gradient from source to sink, carrying all its solutes with it.

**Worked example 3.** An aphid's mouthparts are cut while it is feeding from a sieve tube, and sap continues to exude from the cut stylet. Explain what this shows. The sap in sieve tubes is under positive pressure, which is what drives it out, and analysis of the exudate shows it is rich in sucrose. Both support mass flow down a hydrostatic pressure gradient.

## Common mistakes

- Saying water is "pumped" up the xylem or that transpiration "uses ATP".
- Saying water evaporates "from the stomata".
- Naming lignin instead of suberin for the Casparian strip.
- Explaining cohesion without "hydrogen bonds", or linking adhesion to lignin rather than cellulose.
- Writing "sucrose is actively pumped into the companion cell".
- Using "concentration gradient" in place of "hydrostatic pressure gradient" for mass flow.
- Annotations that only name a feature, such as "sunken stomata", with no explanation.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **State** (7.2.1, 7.2.6): one accurate sentence; no explanation needed.
- **Describe** (7.2.2): give the route in order, naming cellulose, plasmodesmata, endodermis, Casparian strip and suberin.
- **Explain** (7.2.3, 7.2.4, 7.2.7, 7.2.8): build a chain of causes; each link usually carries a mark.
- **Annotated drawing** (7.2.5): label plus explanation for each feature.
