These notes cover the **speech**, one of the five forms the syllabus names for Paper 2 Section A: candidates "use, develop and evaluate the information in the text(s) to create a discursive/argumentative/persuasive speech, email, report, letter or article". The general method is in the [directed writing revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-english-language-1123-directed-writing-revision-notes/); the full explanation and a model speech are in the [writing a speech study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-english-language-1123-directed-writing-speech/).

## Task facts

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| Marks | 25: writing 15 (W1–W5), reading 10 (R3, R5) |
| Length | about 250–350 words |
| Sources | one or two texts, about 400–450 words in total |
| Form objectives | W2 (organise for deliberate effect), W4 (register appropriate to context) |
| Purposes | discursive, argumentative or persuasive |

## The speech in one sentence

Writing designed to be **heard once** by a **named audience** on a **particular occasion**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Direct address | speaking to the audience as "you" |
| Inclusive pronoun | "we", "us", "our", which places speaker and audience on the same side |
| Signposting | phrases that show the structure: "First...", "My second point...", "Finally..." |
| Rhetorical question | a question asked for effect, not for an answer |
| Pattern of three | three items in a list, for rhythm and emphasis |
| Anecdote | a brief personal story that makes a point concrete |
| Recap | a short summary of the main points before the close |
| Call to action | a closing request that the audience do something |
| Register | the level of formality and the tone chosen for the audience |
| Counter-argument | the opposing view, presented fairly and then answered |

## Spoken features toolkit

- Greeting that fits the occasion
- Hook: question, image, surprising source fact or anecdote
- Clear position early
- One point per section, each signposted
- Short sentence after a long one, for emphasis
- Two or three rhetorical devices, placed with purpose
- Fair counter-argument, then a reply
- Recap and call to action, or a return to the hook
- "Thank you for listening."

## Audience and register ladder

| Audience | Register |
|---|---|
| classmates at assembly | lively, inclusive, humorous where it fits; no slang |
| parents | respectful, clear, reasoned |
| community meeting | formal, warm, aware of different views |
| competition judges | confident, structured, polished |

## Structure plan (about 300 words)

| Part | Words | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | 40–50 | greeting, hook, position |
| Section 1 | 50–70 | strongest point: use + develop |
| Section 2 | 50–70 | second point: use + evaluate |
| Counter-argument | 40–60 | fair summary of the other side + reply |
| Close | 30–40 | recap + call to action |

## Reworking a source point

1. State the source point briefly in your own words.
2. Apply it to this audience ("for those of us who...").
3. Question its evidence, bias or completeness.
4. Turn it into spoken rhythm: a question, a short sentence, a direct appeal.

## Weak to strong

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "In conclusion, sport is important." | "So keep Friday sport: for our fitness, our focus and our sanity." |
| "Text 2 says exercise improves mood." | "Think about how you feel after a walk in the fresh air. Now imagine Year 11 without it." |
| "Dear parents and teachers," | "Good evening, and thank you for coming." |
| "Isn't it? Don't you agree? Why not?" | One well-placed question before the counter-argument |

## Exam traps

- Forgetting the occasion named in the task, such as a meeting or assembly.

- An essay with "Good morning" added.
- Rhetorical questions in every paragraph.
- Slang or text-speak.
- Speaking to the wrong audience.
- Letter openings or sign-offs.
- No counter-argument.
- Lifting long phrases from the sources.
- Running far past 350 words.

## Self-test

1. Which two writing objectives depend most on the form?
2. Why does a speech need signposting more than an article?
3. Give an inclusive pronoun and explain its effect.
4. Why should rhetorical devices be limited?
5. Give a suitable opening for a speech to parents at a school meeting.
6. How should a speech end?
7. What is the difference between a persuasive and a discursive speech?
8. Why include a counter-argument in a persuasive speech?
9. Name two letter features that should not appear in a speech.
10. Rework this source point for a speech to classmates: "The canteen plans to raise prices by ten per cent."
11. How long should the speech be?
12. Which objective covers accuracy, and why does it still matter in a speech?

**Answers**

1. W2 (organise and structure for deliberate effect) and W4 (register appropriate to context).
2. Listeners hear it once and cannot re-read, so they need to be told where the argument is going.
3. "We": it puts the speaker and audience on the same side.
4. Overuse sounds forced and distracts from the argument; control earns credit.
5. For example: "Good evening, and thank you for coming tonight."
6. With a recap and a call to action, or a line that returns to the opening hook, and "Thank you for listening".
7. A persuasive speech takes a side and aims to move the audience; a discursive speech weighs both sides before recommending.
8. Answering the strongest opposing point makes the speaker more credible.
9. "Dear..." and "Yours sincerely" (or "Yours faithfully").
10. For example: "From next term, your lunch will cost ten per cent more. For some of us, that is the difference between a meal and an empty stomach."
11. About 250–350 words.
12. W5, accurate spelling, punctuation and grammar; the speech is still marked as a written answer.
