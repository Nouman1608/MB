This guide goes deeper on point **3.1.9.2 Commercial applications of electrochemical cells** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. The specification states that cells "can be used as a commercial source of electrical energy", gives the simplified electrode reactions in a lithium cell, says cells "can be non-rechargeable (irreversible), rechargeable or fuel cells", names "the electrode reactions in an alkaline hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell" and "the benefits and risks to society associated with using these cells". Students should be able to "use given electrode data to deduce the reactions occurring in non-rechargeable and rechargeable cells", "deduce the EMF of a cell" and "explain how the electrode reactions can be used to generate an electric current". It is International A2 content (**International A-level only**), assessed in Unit 3.

The [electrode potentials study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-electrode-potentials-and-electrochemical-cells/) explains the lithium cell, a vanadium cell and the alkaline fuel cell. The key skill here is handling **unfamiliar** cells from given data, so this page works through several more. ## A method for any cell

Questions give two half-equations with E⦵ values, usually involving solids, OH⁻ or H₂SO₄, and ask what happens when the cell is used (discharges) or recharged.

**Discharge (the cell supplies current):**

1. The half-equation with the **more positive** E⦵ runs **forwards** (reduction) at the **positive electrode**.
2. The half-equation with the **less positive** E⦵ runs **backwards** (oxidation) at the **negative electrode**.
3. Balance the electrons and add; cancel species that appear on both sides (often OH⁻, H⁺ or H₂O).
4. **EMF = E⦵(positive) − E⦵(negative)**.

**Recharging (an external supply forces current the other way):** both half-reactions are **reversed**. Reduction now happens at the electrode that was negative.

**How a current is generated.** Oxidation at the negative electrode releases electrons. They flow through the external circuit, doing work in the device, to the positive electrode, where reduction uses them up. Ions move through the electrolyte between the electrodes to balance the charge.

## Rechargeable cells

### Worked example 1: the lead–acid cell

Data:

```
PbO₂(s) + 3H⁺(aq) + HSO₄⁻(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ PbSO₄(s) + 2H₂O(l)     +1.69 V
PbSO₄(s) + H⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ Pb(s) + HSO₄⁻(aq)                   −0.36 V
```

1. Positive electrode (reduction): PbO₂ + 3H⁺ + HSO₄⁻ + 2e⁻ → PbSO₄ + 2H₂O
2. Negative electrode (oxidation): Pb + HSO₄⁻ → PbSO₄ + H⁺ + 2e⁻
3. Overall discharge (2e⁻ each, so add directly; one H⁺ cancels):

```
Pb + PbO₂ + 2H⁺ + 2HSO₄⁻ → 2PbSO₄ + 2H₂O
```

4. EMF = 1.69 − (−0.36) = **+2.05 V**. Six cells in series give 6 × 2.05 = 12.3 V, which is why such batteries are described as 12-volt.

**Oxidation states:** lead goes from 0 (Pb) and +4 (PbO₂) to +2 (PbSO₄) at both electrodes.

**Why it can be recharged:** the product, PbSO₄, is an insoluble solid that stays on both electrodes, so a current in the reverse direction can convert it back into Pb and PbO₂. During discharge, sulfuric acid is used up and water formed, so the electrolyte becomes more dilute.

### Worked example 2: the nickel–cadmium cell

Data:

```
NiO(OH)(s) + H₂O(l) + e⁻ ⇌ Ni(OH)₂(s) + OH⁻(aq)      +0.52 V
Cd(OH)₂(s) + 2e⁻ ⇌ Cd(s) + 2OH⁻(aq)                  −0.88 V
```

1. Positive: NiO(OH) + H₂O + e⁻ → Ni(OH)₂ + OH⁻ (×2 to match 2e⁻)
2. Negative: Cd + 2OH⁻ → Cd(OH)₂ + 2e⁻
3. Overall: **Cd + 2NiO(OH) + 2H₂O → Cd(OH)₂ + 2Ni(OH)₂** (the 2OH⁻ cancel)
4. EMF = 0.52 − (−0.88) = **+1.40 V**

Nickel is reduced from +3 to +2; cadmium is oxidised from 0 to +2. When charging, Cd(OH)₂ + 2e⁻ → Cd + 2OH⁻ occurs at the electrode that was negative.

## Non-rechargeable cells

### Worked example 3: a zinc–silver oxide button cell

Data:

```
Ag₂O(s) + H₂O(l) + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2Ag(s) + 2OH⁻(aq)     +0.34 V
ZnO(s) + H₂O(l) + 2e⁻ ⇌ Zn(s) + 2OH⁻(aq)       −1.26 V
```

- Positive: Ag₂O + H₂O + 2e⁻ → 2Ag + 2OH⁻
- Negative: Zn + 2OH⁻ → ZnO + H₂O + 2e⁻
- Overall: **Zn + Ag₂O → ZnO + 2Ag** (H₂O and OH⁻ cancel)
- EMF = 0.34 − (−1.26) = **+1.60 V**

Such cells are sold as non-rechargeable: in practice the reactions cannot be reversed reliably by applying a current, so the cell is discarded when the zinc is used up.

## The lithium cell

The specification gives the simplified electrode reactions:

```
Positive electrode:  Li⁺ + CoO₂ + e⁻ → Li⁺[CoO₂]⁻
Negative electrode:  Li → Li⁺ + e⁻
```

### Worked example 4: EMF of a lithium cell

Given E⦵(Li⁺/Li) = −3.04 V and E⦵ for the positive electrode = +0.56 V:

```
EMF = 0.56 − (−3.04) = +3.60 V
```

Cobalt is reduced from +4 in CoO₂ to +3 in Li⁺[CoO₂]⁻. The very negative E⦵ of lithium is what gives these cells their large EMF; lithium's low mass also keeps them light, which suits phones, tablets and laptops. Recharging reverses both reactions.

## Fuel cells

A fuel cell is supplied continuously with fuel and oxygen. It generates a current and does **not** need to be electrically recharged.

### Worked example 5: alkaline and acidic hydrogen–oxygen cells

**Alkaline electrolyte** (named in the specification):

```
O₂(g) + 2H₂O(l) + 4e⁻ ⇌ 4OH⁻(aq)          +0.40 V
2H₂O(l) + 2e⁻ ⇌ H₂(g) + 2OH⁻(aq)          −0.83 V
```

- Negative: H₂ + 2OH⁻ → 2H₂O + 2e⁻ (×2)
- Positive: O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻
- Overall: **2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O**; EMF = 0.40 − (−0.83) = **+1.23 V**

**Acidic electrolyte** (an unfamiliar example of the same skill):

```
O₂(g) + 4H⁺(aq) + 4e⁻ ⇌ 2H₂O(l)           +1.23 V
2H⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ H₂(g)                      0.00 V
```

- Negative: H₂ → 2H⁺ + 2e⁻; positive: O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O
- Overall: **2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O**; EMF = **+1.23 V**

The overall reaction and the EMF are the same; only the ion carrying charge through the electrolyte differs (OH⁻ or H⁺).

## Benefits and risks to society

| Cell type | Benefits | Risks |
|---|---|---|
| Rechargeable (lead–acid, Ni–Cd, lithium) | Reused many times, so fewer cells made and thrown away; portable power for vehicles and electronics | Lead and cadmium compounds are toxic if cells are dumped; lithium cells can overheat or catch fire if damaged or overcharged; charging uses electricity, which may come from fossil fuels |
| Non-rechargeable | Cheap, convenient, long shelf life | Discarded after one use, wasting metals; toxic or corrosive contents can leak in landfill |
| Hydrogen fuel cell | Only water is produced where it is used; no recharging time | Hydrogen is often made using fossil fuels or electricity; it is flammable, explosive in air, and hard to store and transport |

Collecting and recycling cells reduces the waste of metals and the release of toxic compounds.

## Common mistakes

- Running the more positive half-equation backwards during discharge.
- Forgetting to reverse **both** half-equations for recharging.
- Leaving OH⁻, H⁺ or H₂O uncancelled in the overall equation.
- Multiplying E⦵ when doubling a half-equation.
- Saying a fuel cell stores energy or needs recharging.
- Giving vague benefits ("better for the environment") without saying why.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Deduce** the reactions: state which half-equation is reduced and why (more positive E⦵), then write both electrode equations and the overall equation.
- **Deduce the EMF**: E(positive) − E(negative), sign and unit.
- **Explain how a current is generated**: oxidation releases electrons at the negative electrode → electrons flow through the external circuit → reduction at the positive electrode; ions move in the electrolyte.
- **Evaluate** or **discuss** benefits and risks: give at least one specific point on each side and a conclusion.
