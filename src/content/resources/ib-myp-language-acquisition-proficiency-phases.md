This guide covers **proficiency phases and the criteria structure**
for IB Middle Years Programme Language Acquisition, from 2020. The
[full syllabus
guide](/resources/ib-myp-language-acquisition-syllabus-guide/)
confirms all four assessment criteria carry equal weight (32 marks
each), alongside the [Criteria in
Practice](/resources/ib-myp-language-acquisition-criteria-in-practice/)
guide already on the site.

## Where this fits in the subject

Unlike most subjects assessed by year level alone, MYP Language
Acquisition is formally examined at one of three proficiency phases --
emergent, capable, or proficient -- reflecting actual progress in the
target language. A student assessed as emergent is not expected to
show the same vocabulary range, structural complexity or spontaneous
fluency as one assessed as proficient. Confirming your own assessment
phase with your teacher is the single most important first step in
revision, since practice material pitched at the wrong phase either
underprepares you or wastes study time on unnecessary complexity.

## The four criteria are independently weighted

Criterion A, Listening (32 marks), covers interpreting and
constructing meaning from spoken multimodal text. Criterion B,
Reading (32 marks), covers constructing meaning from written, spatial
and visual text. Criterion C, Speaking (32 marks), covers interacting
on topics of personal, local and global interest. Criterion D, Writing
(32 marks), covers using language suitable to audience and purpose
across registers. Because listening, reading and writing are assessed
together in one on-screen paper while speaking is assessed separately
as an internally marked, IB-moderated interactive task, strong written
performance does not substitute for oral preparation -- all four are
independently weighted at an equal 32 marks each.

## Worked example: matching preparation to phase

A student assessed as "capable" is preparing a writing response. At
the emergent phase, the expectation is simple sentences, basic
vocabulary and formulaic expressions. At the capable phase, the
expectation is a range of sentence structures, more precise
vocabulary, and awareness of register for a given audience and
purpose. At the proficient phase, the expectation is complex, nuanced
structures, idiomatic language, and sophisticated audience awareness.
The consequence for this capable-phase student is that they should
practise varied sentence structure and basic register awareness -- not
emergent-level formulaic phrases, and not proficient-level idiom that
risks errors beyond their genuinely secure range. Practising at your
confirmed phase, rather than either under- or over-reaching, is more
productive than generic "harder is always better" preparation.

## Building a separate speaking-practice routine

Because speaking is internally assessed and externally moderated as
its own interactive task, build a genuinely separate revision routine
for oral fluency -- rehearsing spontaneous, interactive conversation
under time pressure, not prepared monologues -- rather than assuming
comprehensive written revision automatically prepares you for it too.

## Compulsory status and its exception

MYP Language Acquisition is compulsory in every year of the programme,
with one specific exception: bilingual students pursuing courses of
study in multiple languages within the MYP Language and Literature
subject group instead. Confirming which pathway applies to you --
Language Acquisition versus a bilingual Language and Literature route
-- is a basic but important check before assuming this subject's
proficiency-phase structure applies to your own course. Students who
demonstrate additional proficiency in a second language or their
mother tongue beyond the language acquisition course itself can also
earn the separate MYP bilingual certificate, a genuinely separate
achievement track that sits alongside, not instead of, the standard
MYP certificate route through this subject.

## Connecting to the DP pathway

MYP language acquisition examination tasks are explicitly aligned with
the skills needed for high achievement in both DP Language Acquisition
and DP Studies in Language and Literature courses. Strong
criterion-by-criterion habits built at MYP level -- particularly
writing for a specified audience and purpose (Criterion D), and
confident, unscripted speaking (Criterion C) -- transfer directly into
whichever DP language pathway a student later chooses, making this
phase-and-criteria structure worth understanding well beyond the
immediate MYP assessment itself.

## How to approach it

Confirm your assessed proficiency phase with your teacher before
selecting practice material, rather than assuming year group
determines it. Practise listening, reading and writing together, since
they share one on-screen paper, but treat speaking as a genuinely
separate revision strand requiring its own dedicated, spontaneous
practice routine. Remember all four criteria are equally weighted, so
no single skill -- particularly writing, which is easy to over-invest
revision time in relative to the others -- should dominate revision
time at the expense of the rest.

## Common mistakes

Assuming year group determines assessment level, when proficiency phase (confirmed with your teacher)
is what actually matters -- two students in the same class can genuinely be assessed at different
phases. Treating all four criteria as a single combined skill, when they are independently marked and
weighted equally, which can lead to strong writing masking a genuine gap in speaking preparation.
Preparing only written and receptive skills and neglecting dedicated interactive speaking practice,
since Criterion C's spontaneous, unscripted demand is not something reading and writing revision
prepares for. Comparing a proficient-phase peer's more advanced language use against your own
capable- or emergent-phase work and concluding your own preparation is falling short, when the two are
simply being assessed against different descriptors entirely -- what matters for revision is progress against your own phase's expectations, not a direct comparison against a classmate's output.
