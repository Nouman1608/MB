## Questions

**1.** State two reasons why economists cannot easily run controlled experiments on a whole economy. **[2]**

**2.** A café raises the price of hot chocolate by 10%. Sales fall by 8% that week. The same week had unusually heavy rain that kept people at home. Explain, using ceteris paribus, why the 8% fall cannot be put down to the price rise alone. **[4]**

**3.**

**(a)** Classify each statement as positive or normative. **[4]**
(i) "Unemployment in Country A fell from 9% to 7% over two years."
(ii) "Rents in Town G should be capped."
(iii) "A 5% rise in bus fares caused passenger numbers to fall by 3%."
(iv) "The gap between rich and poor in Country A is unfair."

**(b)** Two economists agree that a higher fuel tax would cut fuel use by 6%. They disagree on whether it should be introduced. Explain why. **[3]**

**4.** A town council pays for a public park that is free to enter.

**(a)** Explain why the park is an economic good, not a free good. **[3]**
**(b)** Give one example of a free good and explain why it fits the definition. **[2]**

**5.** A carpenter works 40 hours a week making chairs and tables. A chair takes 5 hours and a table takes 8 hours.

**(a)** Calculate the maximum number of chairs and of tables that can be made in a week. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the opportunity cost of one table in chairs and explain why it is constant. **[3]**
**(c)** A new tool cuts the time for a chair to 4 hours. Describe the change to the frontier and recalculate the opportunity cost of a table. **[3]**

**6.** Country E's production possibility frontier is shown (units in thousands).

| Capital goods | 0 | 8 | 16 | 24 | 32 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Consumer goods | 90 | 84 | 72 | 52 | 0 |

**(a)** Calculate the marginal opportunity cost of capital goods when output rises from 16 to 24. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why marginal opportunity cost rises along this frontier. **[3]**
**(c)** Country E produces 16 capital goods and 60 consumer goods. Explain what this shows and calculate the percentage by which consumer goods could rise with no fall in capital goods. **[3]**
**(d)** Explain whether 24 capital goods and 60 consumer goods is attainable. **[1]**

**7.** For each event, state whether it is a movement along a PPF or a shift, and in which direction. **[4]**
(i) A government moves workers from farming to building new factories.
(ii) A training programme raises the skills of workers in every industry.
(iii) A flood destroys much of a country's farmland and factories.
(iv) Changes in tastes lead firms to make more consumer goods and fewer capital goods.

**8.** Country H has 400 workers who produce 20,000 units a year. After firms buy new machinery, the same workers produce 24,000 units.

**(a)** Calculate output per worker before and after, and the percentage rise. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how the production of capital goods can shift a country's PPF. **[4]**
**(c)** Evaluate whether an economy should always devote more resources to capital goods. **[4]**

**9.** A garment workshop employs 5 workers, each paid 90 a day. Working alone, each makes 12 garments a day. After tasks are divided into cutting, sewing and finishing, the workshop makes 75 garments a day.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage rise in output per worker. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the labour cost per garment before and after. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain two disadvantages of the division of labour. **[4]**

**10.**

**(a)** Explain how money as a medium of exchange supports specialisation. **[3]**
**(b)** A saver holds 2,000 units in cash for four years while inflation is 4% a year. Calculate the purchasing power at the end, in today's units, and explain what this shows about money. **[3]**
**(c)** An importer must pay 80,000 foreign currency units in three months. A bank offers a forward rate of 4.0 foreign units per home unit. Calculate the home-currency cost at this rate and at a spot rate of 3.2 in three months, and explain the benefit of the forward contract. **[3]**

**11.**

**(a)** Distinguish between a command economy and a free market economy. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain two roles of the state in a mixed economy. **[3]**
**(c)** Evaluate whether a mixed economy is better at resource allocation than a free market economy. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two: people change behaviour when they know they are being studied [1]; other factors cannot be held constant in a real economy [1]; experiments on livelihoods raise ethical objections. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Two separate reasons earn more than one reason restated, and each should refer to economies or people, not to science in general.

**2.** Ceteris paribus means all other factors are held constant [1]. Here the rain also changed [1], so part of the 8% fall may be due to fewer customers [1]. To isolate the price effect, compare weeks with similar weather [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Applying the idea to the café, naming the rain, tends to do better than a general definition.

**3. (a)** (i) positive [1]; (ii) normative [1]; (iii) positive [1]; (iv) normative [1]. **[4]**
**(b)** The prediction of a 6% fall is positive and can be tested [1]. They differ on value judgements, such as how to weigh environmental gains against the cost to households [1]. Evidence cannot settle a value judgement, so policy needs both [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A number does not make a statement positive. "Should" and "unfair" signal value judgements.

**4. (a)** Building and running the park uses land, labour and capital [1]. These could have been used elsewhere, for example for housing [1], so there is an opportunity cost, paid through taxes rather than a price [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Sunlight or air in a remote area [1]. One more person's use requires nothing to be given up, and there is enough to meet all wants at zero price [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Free to the user" is not "free good". The test is opportunity cost.

**5. (a)** Chairs 40/5 = 8 [1]; tables 40/8 = 5 [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 8/5 = 1.6 chairs per table [1]. It is constant because every hour makes the same quantity of either good [1], so the frontier is a straight line with a fixed slope [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Maximum chairs rise to 40/4 = 10 [1]; tables stay at 5, so the frontier pivots outward on the chair axis [1]; the opportunity cost of a table rises to 8/4 = 2 chairs [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* State opportunity cost in the good given up, with units.

**6. (a)** Consumer goods lost = 72 − 52 = 20 [1]; 20/8 = 2.5 consumer goods per capital good [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Resources are not equally suited to both goods [1]. The resources best at capital goods move first [1]. Later moves take resources better suited to consumer goods, so more is lost per extra capital good [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** The point is inside the frontier, so resources are unemployed or misallocated [1]. The maximum at 16 capital goods is 72 [1]. (72 − 60)/60 = 20% [1]. **[3]**
**(d)** The maximum consumer goods at 24 capital goods is 52, which is below 60, so the point is unobtainable [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the subtraction and the division. The divisor is the gain, not the loss.

**7.** (i) Movement towards capital goods [1]; (ii) outward shift [1]; (iii) inward shift [1]; (iv) movement towards consumer goods [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Give the reason as well as the label: reallocation is a movement, a change in potential is a shift.

**8. (a)** Before 20,000/400 = 50; after 24,000/400 = 60 [1]; rise 10/50 = 20% [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Capital goods raise productivity [1]. The same resources produce more [1]. Potential output of both goods rises [1], so the PPF shifts outward, which is economic growth [1]. **[4]**
**(c)** More capital goods mean fewer consumer goods now, which is the opportunity cost [1]. The gain is higher future output [1]. Whether it is worth it depends on the quality of investment and the value placed on present against future consumption [1]. Judgement: not always, as it depends on how urgent current needs are [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation needs a conclusion that depends on stated conditions, not a repeat of the explanation.

**9. (a)** Before 12; after 75/5 = 15 [1]; (15 − 12)/12 [1] = 25% [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Wage bill 5 × 90 = 450 [1]; before 450/60 = 7.50 [1]; after 450/75 = 6.00 [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Repetitive work lowers motivation [1], raising absence and turnover [1]. The process depends on every stage [1], so one absence or breakdown can stop the line [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Total garments before is 5 × 12 = 60. Check the denominator.

**10. (a)** Barter needs a double coincidence of wants [1]. Money is accepted for any good, so a worker can sell output for money [1] and buy what they need, making narrow specialisation workable [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** 2,000/1.04⁴ [1] = 1,709.61 [1]. Inflation has cut purchasing power by 290.39, so money is a weaker store of value [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Forward cost: 80,000/4.0 = 20,000 [1]. At the spot rate: 80,000/3.2 = 25,000, so the contract avoids an extra 5,000 [1] and gives certainty for planning [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Link each function of money to the question. A bare list explains little.

**11. (a)** In a command economy, the state owns resources and decides what is produced through planning [1]. In a free market, private owners and consumers decide through the price mechanism [1]. The contrast is who decides, and what coordinates decisions [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Two roles, such as providing public goods [1] and regulating externalities [1]. One is explained: free riding stops private firms charging for public goods [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** A free market may underprovide public goods, ignore external costs and leave inequality [1]. In a mixed economy, the state can respond through provision, regulation and redistribution [1]. But the state may lack information, impose costs and weaken incentives [1]. Too much intervention can harm efficiency [1]. The answer also depends on the weight given to equity against efficiency, a value judgement [1]. Judgement: a mixed economy is usually better, provided intervention is targeted at identified failures [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Tie each evaluative point to a named problem and finish with a supported judgement.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "ceteris paribus" without saying which other factor changed in the question.
- Marking a statement positive because it has a number, ignoring a "should".
- Calling a service "free" when it is paid for by taxes.
- Dividing by the loss, not the gain, when calculating marginal opportunity cost.
- Treating a point inside the PPF as unobtainable.
- Confusing movements with shifts.
- Listing functions of money, advantages or roles of the state without explaining them.
- Evaluating without a conclusion.
