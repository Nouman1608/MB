This guide covers section 3.3.1.2, Graphical skills, within Section A (Geographical skills) of Unit 3 of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Geography (9230) specification, Version 4.3. The specification says that students should be able to:

- **select and construct** appropriate graphs and charts, using appropriate scales: line charts, bar charts, pie charts, pictograms, histograms with equal class intervals, divided bars, scattergraphs and population pyramids;
- **suggest** an appropriate form of graphical representation for the data provided;
- **complete** a variety of graphs and maps: choropleth, isoline, dot maps, desire lines, proportional symbols and flow lines;
- use and understand **gradient, contour and value on isoline maps**;
- **plot** information on graphs when axes and scales are provided;
- **interpret and extract** information from different maps, graphs and charts, including population pyramids, choropleth maps, flow-line maps and dispersion graphs.

Graphical skills can be tested in all three written exams. Paper 3 (1 hour 15 minutes, 60 marks) has the most skills content. For the whole skills theme, see the [geographical and fieldwork skills study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-geography-9230-geographical-and-fieldwork-skills/). This page goes deeper on graphs, with worked examples. All the numbers have been calculated and checked.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Axis | The horizontal (x) or vertical (y) line of a graph, with a scale |
| Scale | The equal steps used to mark values on an axis |
| Class interval | A group of values in a histogram, such as 0 to under 10 |
| Choropleth map | A map shaded in different tones by area according to a value |
| Isoline | A line joining places of equal value (such as contours or isotherms) |
| Desire line | A straight line showing movement between an origin and a destination |
| Flow line | A line whose width is proportional to the amount moving |
| Proportional symbol | A symbol whose size shows the value |
| Dispersion graph | A graph showing how individual values are spread along a scale |
| Gradient | How steep a slope is: vertical change divided by horizontal distance |

## Choosing the right graph

| Data | Best graph |
|---|---|
| Change over time (continuous), such as temperature through a year | Line graph |
| Comparing separate categories, such as rainfall in five towns | Bar chart |
| Parts of a whole, such as land use as a share of the total | Pie chart or divided bar |
| Frequency in continuous classes, such as pebble sizes | Histogram (equal class intervals, bars touching) |
| Showing a count with a symbol, such as one symbol for 100 vehicles | Pictogram |
| Relationship between two variables, such as distance and land value | Scattergraph |
| Age and sex structure | Population pyramid |
| The spread of individual values | Dispersion graph |

When asked to **suggest** a graph, name it and give a reason linked to the data: for example, "a scattergraph, because there are two continuous variables and I want to see whether there is a relationship."

## Constructing graphs: general rules

1. **Title** that says what the graph shows.
2. **Axes** labelled with the variable and unit.
3. **Scale** with equal steps, using most of the space, starting at zero for bar charts and histograms.
4. **Plot accurately**, using a sharp pencil.
5. **Key** if more than one data set.
6. **Line graph:** points joined with straight lines or a smooth curve, as appropriate. **Scattergraph:** points not joined; a line of best fit may be added.

### Pie chart: calculating the angles

A survey of 120 land plots finds: residential 48, commercial 30, industrial 24, open space 18.

- Each plot represents 360 ÷ 120 = **3 degrees**.
- Residential: 48 × 3 = **144 degrees** (40%).
- Commercial: 30 × 3 = **90 degrees** (25%).
- Industrial: 24 × 3 = **72 degrees** (20%).
- Open space: 18 × 3 = **54 degrees** (15%).
- Check: 144 + 90 + 72 + 54 = 360. The percentages add to 100.

Draw a circle, mark the start line, and measure each angle with a protractor, working clockwise.

### Divided bar

For the same data on a bar 10 cm long, each 10% is 1 cm: residential 4.0 cm, commercial 2.5 cm, industrial 2.0 cm and open space 1.5 cm (total 10.0 cm). Label each section.

### Histogram

A histogram shows continuous data in classes of equal width. The bars touch, the horizontal axis is a continuous scale and the vertical axis is frequency. Unlike a bar chart, the order of classes cannot change.

## Maps that show data

| Map | How it works | Main points |
|---|---|---|
| Choropleth | Areas shaded by value class, usually light for low and dark for high | Use a single colour that gets darker; include a key; best for rates or density (not totals) |
| Isoline | Lines join places with equal value | Lines never cross or touch; the closer the lines, the faster the change; label the values |
| Dot map | One dot represents a fixed number (such as 1 000 people) | Shows distribution and clustering; the dot value must be in the key |
| Desire line | Straight lines between origin and destination | Shows the pattern of movement, not the exact route |
| Proportional symbol | Circles or other symbols with area (or height) proportional to the value | Larger symbol means a larger value |
| Flow line | Lines whose width is proportional to the amount moving | Wider line means more movement along the route |

### Completing a choropleth map

Suppose population density classes are: under 50, 50 to 99, 100 to 199, 200 and over. A district with 130 people per km² falls in the 100 to 199 class and is shaded with the third tone. Always check the key and the class boundaries.

## Gradient and contours

**Gradient = vertical change ÷ horizontal distance** (use the same units).

Example: Point A is at 100 m and point B at 160 m on a map. They are 1.5 km (1 500 m) apart.

- Vertical change = 160 - 100 = 60 m.
- Gradient = 60 ÷ 1 500, which is 1 in 25 (or 4%).

On a map, **steep slopes** have contour lines close together, and **gentle slopes** have them far apart.

## Value on isoline maps

You can estimate the value at a point between two isolines by proportion.

Example: An isotherm map has a 20 degree line and a 25 degree line. Point X is 3 cm from the 20 degree line and 2 cm from the 25 degree line. The total gap is 5 cm, and the temperature rises 5 degrees across it.

- Value at X = 20 + (3 ÷ 5) × 5 = **23 degrees**.

## Plotting and reading from given axes

When the question provides axes and scales, plot to the nearest small square or half-square. Use a cross or dot, check each value against the table and join points only if the question asks.

## Interpreting population pyramids

A population pyramid shows the percentage (or number) of males on one side and females on the other, by age group.

| Shape | What it suggests |
|---|---|
| Wide base, narrow top (a true pyramid) | High birth rate, high death rate, low life expectancy; typical of an LIC |
| Straighter sides, wide middle | Low birth rate and death rate; typical of an HIC |
| Narrow base, wide top | Very low birth rate and an ageing population |
| Bulges or notches | Past events, such as war, migration or a baby boom |

### Worked example: dependency ratio

Suppose 38% of a country's population are aged under 15, 58% are aged 15 to 64, and 4% are 65 or over.

- Dependants = 38 + 4 = 42%.
- Dependency ratio = 42 ÷ 58 = 0.724, or about **72 dependants per 100 people of working age**.

A high ratio means that the working population has a heavier burden of supporting the young and old.

## Interpreting other graphs and maps

- **Choropleth:** describe the pattern (for example highest in the north-east), quote the classes and give an exception.
- **Flow-line map:** identify the widest lines (the largest flows) and the origin and destination.
- **Dispersion graph:** each point or mark shows a single value on a line; look for clusters, gaps, the range and any outlier. A tight cluster shows little variation.
- **Scattergraph:** describe direction, strength and anomalies.

## Worked example: a 3-mark answer

**Question:** The table shows percentages of workers in three sectors in a country. A pie chart is to be drawn. Calculate the angles. Primary 15%, secondary 35%, tertiary 50%. [3 marks]

**Model answer:** "360 degrees represents 100%, so 1% is 3.6 degrees. Primary: 15 × 3.6 = 54 degrees. Secondary: 35 × 3.6 = 126 degrees. Tertiary: 50 × 3.6 = 180 degrees. Check: 54 + 126 + 180 = 360."

## Plotting a line graph: a short routine

1. Draw the axes with a ruler. Put the independent variable (such as month or distance) on the horizontal axis and the measured variable on the vertical axis.
2. Choose a scale so that the largest value fits in at least half of the space. If the highest value is 38, a scale of 5 units per large square works well, up to 40.
3. Mark each point with a small cross.
4. Join the points with straight lines if the data are continuous through time, or draw a smooth curve if the question asks for one.
5. Label the axes with units, add a title, and check each plotted value against the table.

For a temperature and rainfall graph (climate graph), temperature is plotted as a line and rainfall as bars, with two vertical axes. Name both scales.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting a bar chart axis above zero**, which exaggerates differences.
- **Using uneven scale steps** (for example 0, 5, 10, 20).
- **Leaving out units or labels** on axes.
- **Joining the points on a scattergraph** like a line graph.
- **Using a choropleth map for totals**, which makes big areas look more important.
- **Crossing isolines.**
- **Not checking that pie chart angles add to 360 degrees.**
