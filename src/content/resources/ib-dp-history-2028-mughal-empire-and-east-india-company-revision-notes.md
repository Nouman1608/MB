These revision notes are for **higher level (HL) only**. The Mughal Empire and the British East India Company (1526 to 1858) is a regional study examined on Paper 3, which standard level students do not sit. They condense the IB *History guide*, first assessment 2028. ## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Mansabdari | System of numbered ranks fixing an official's pay and military duty |
| Jagir | Assignment of land revenue given to a mansabdar in place of salary |
| Jizya | Tax on non-Muslims; abolished by Akbar (1564), reimposed by Aurangzeb (1679) |
| Sulh-i kul | "Peace with all"; Akbar's principle of tolerance |
| Diwani | The right to collect revenue; granted to the Company in 1765 |
| Subsidiary alliance | Treaty in which a ruler accepted a Company force and paid for it |
| Doctrine of lapse | Dalhousie's policy of annexing a state if its ruler died with no natural heir |
| Sepoy | An Indian soldier in the Company's army |
| Bhakti | Devotional movement stressing a personal bond with God |
| Sufism | Mystical tradition within Islam, with orders such as the Chishti |

## Rise of Mughal power in brief

- **Panipat I:** Babur's guns and flanking tactics beat a larger army; Lodi nobles were divided.
- **Humayun:** lost to Sher Shah; exile in Persia; returned in 1555.
- **Suri interval (1540 to 1555):** silver coin, surveyed revenue, roads; a model for Akbar.
- **Panipat II:** Akbar, with Bairam Khan as regent, beats Hemu.

## Consolidation: causes of success

1. Conquest and then alliance, especially with the Rajputs.
2. Mansabdari system linking nobles to the throne.
3. Fair taxation and good survey records under Akbar's ministers such as Todar Mal.
4. Religious policy of tolerance under Akbar.
5. Strong rulers after him, each with a different emphasis: Shah Jahan on show and buildings, Aurangzeb on law and expansion.

## Challenges: three layers

- **Court politics:** Nur Jahan's junta under Jahangir; war of succession between Shah Jahan's sons.
- **Provincial:** revolts by Rajputs, Jats, Sikhs and Satnamis in Aurangzeb's reign.
- **Regional power:** the Marathas under Shivaji (crowned 1674), then the Peshwas; the Deccan campaigns that drained the treasury.

## Impact (E-C-W-S)

- **Economic:** land revenue, silver inflow, cotton and silk exports, growing cities.
- **Cultural:** Persian court culture, miniature painting, the Taj Mahal, Fatehpur Sikri.
- **Women:** Nur Jahan and Jahanara Begum had influence; most women lived under strict seclusion; sati continued.
- **Syncretism:** Bhakti poets (Kabir, Mirabai), Sufi orders, and the rise of Sikhism.

## From Mughal decline to Company rule

| Step | What happened | Why it mattered |
|---|---|---|
| Plassey, 1757 | Clive defeats Siraj-ud-Daula after Mir Jafar defects | Company gains a puppet ruler in Bengal |
| Buxar, 1764 | Company beats three allies | Shows military superiority |
| Diwani, 1765 | Revenue of Bengal, Bihar, Odisha | Finances an army from Indian taxes |
| Wellesley | Subsidiary alliances | Rulers lose independence |
| Dalhousie | Doctrine of lapse, Punjab, Awadh | Fear and anger among rulers and nobles |

## Governors-General: one line each

- **Wellesley (1798 to 1805):** aggressive expansion; Tipu defeated 1799; Delhi taken 1803.
- **Bentinck (1828 to 1835):** sati abolished 1829; English education and economy measures.
- **Dalhousie (1848 to 1856):** lapse, annexations, railways, telegraph.

## 1857: causes and results

- **Causes:** the cartridge rumour was the trigger; underlying causes were annexations, land revenue demands, loss of status for rulers and sepoys, and fear for religion.
- **Course:** Meerut, Delhi, Kanpur, Lucknow, Jhansi; Delhi retaken in September 1857.
- **Results:** end of the Company and of the Mughal line; direct Crown rule; tolerance promised in the 1858 proclamation; army reorganised.

## Perspectives

- "First War of Independence" (Savarkar, 1909) against "sepoy mutiny" and views that stress local grievances.
- Mughal decline as a crisis (Irfan Habib's agrarian crisis; the jagirdari problem) against the idea of regional growth (C. A. Bayly).
- Aurangzeb as bigot against Aurangzeb as political pragmatist (Audrey Truschke).

## Worked example: a short plan

**Question:** "To what extent did the policy of conciliation explain Mughal success under Akbar?"

1. Define success: stable control over most of northern India.
2. Conciliation: Rajput marriages, abolition of the jizya, sulh-i kul.
3. Other factors: army and artillery, mansabdari, revenue reform.
4. Weigh: conciliation brought loyalty, but conquest and administration built the state.
5. Judgement: conciliation was necessary for stability but not sufficient on its own.

## Common mistakes

- Dating the end of the Mughals to 1707 instead of 1858.
- Confusing the three battles of Panipat.
- Treating 1857 as only a sepoy mutiny.
- Omitting the Company's use of Indian revenue and soldiers.

## Self-test

1. Who won the First Battle of Panipat, and what gave him the advantage?
2. What was a jagir?
3. Name Shah Jahan's four sons who fought the war of succession.
4. What did the Company gain in 1765?
5. Name one reform of Bentinck.
6. Which policy of Dalhousie used the death of a ruler without a natural heir?
7. What did the Act of 1858 do?

**Answers:** 1. Babur; artillery, matchlocks and flanking tactics. 2. A grant of land revenue in place of salary. 3. Dara Shikoh, Shuja, Murad and Aurangzeb. 4. The diwani of Bengal, Bihar and Odisha. 5. Abolition of sati (1829), or English education. 6. The doctrine of lapse. 7. Ended Company rule and transferred India to the Crown.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is the Company part of the Mughal story or a separate story?**
It is one story: the Company grew in the space left by Mughal decline. Good essays link the two.

**How many battles must I know?**
Know the two battles of Panipat, Plassey and Buxar in detail, and Third Panipat as a turning point.
