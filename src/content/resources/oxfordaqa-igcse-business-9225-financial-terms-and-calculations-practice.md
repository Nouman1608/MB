All businesses and figures are invented. Section B questions refer to the case study about Juno Juice.

> **Case study: Juno Juice**
>
> Juno Juice sells bottles of fresh juice at $4.00 each. Its fixed costs
> are $15,000 a month and its variable costs are $1.50 per bottle. The
> owner has prepared the following table, which would be shown as a
> break-even chart.
>
> | Bottles sold | Revenue | Total costs |
> |---|---|---|
> | 0 | $0 | $15,000 |
> | 2,000 | $8,000 | $18,000 |
> | 4,000 | $16,000 | $21,000 |
> | 6,000 | $24,000 | $24,000 |
> | 8,000 | $32,000 | $27,000 |
> | 10,000 | $40,000 | $30,000 |
>
> Juno expects to sell 8,500 bottles a month. It is also considering
> buying one of two investments. Investment 1 is a new juicing machine
> costing $36,000, which would bring extra net cash flows of $9,000 in
> year 1, $12,000 in year 2 and $15,000 in year 3. Investment 2 is a
> delivery van costing $30,000, which would bring $7,500 a year.

## Section A

**1.** Which **one** of the following is a variable cost for Juno Juice? **[1]**
- A. Rent of the shop
- B. Fruit used in the juice
- C. Insurance
- D. The manager's salary

**2.** Which **one** of the following is the correct formula for profit? **[1]**
- A. Revenue + total costs
- B. Total costs - revenue
- C. Revenue - total costs
- D. Fixed costs - variable costs

**3.** Define the term "break-even output". **[2]**

**4.** State **two** examples of investment projects that businesses make. **[2]**

**5.** Explain **one** limitation of break-even analysis. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Using the table, state Juno's break-even output and the revenue at that point. **[2]**

**7.** Calculate Juno's total costs when it sells 5,000 bottles. **[2]**

**8.** Calculate the margin of safety if Juno sells the expected 8,500 bottles a month. **[2]**

**9.** Calculate the profit Juno would make by selling 8,500 bottles. **[3]**

**10.** Calculate the payback period for Investment 1 and for Investment 2. **[4]**

**11.** Analyse **two** ways in which a rise in Juno's fixed costs would affect the business. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate whether Juno should choose Investment 1 or Investment 2, using your answer to Question 10. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. **[1]**

**2.** **C** [1]. **[1]**

**3.** The level of output at which revenue is equal to total costs [1], so the business makes neither a profit nor a loss [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Any two: new machinery [1]; buildings [1]; vehicles [1]. **[2]**

**5.** It assumes that all the output is sold [1], which may not happen if demand falls or competitors cut prices [1]. Accept: it assumes that prices and costs stay constant [1], whereas discounts and bulk buying change them [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Break-even output = **6,000 bottles** [1]; revenue at that point = **$24,000** [1]. **[2]**

**7.** Variable costs = 5,000 x $1.50 = $7,500 [1]; total costs = $15,000 + $7,500 = **$22,500** [1]. **[2]**

**8.** 8,500 - 6,000 [1] = **2,500 bottles** [1]. **[2]**

**9.** Revenue = 8,500 x $4 = $34,000 [1]; total costs = $15,000 + (8,500 x $1.50 = $12,750) = $27,750 [1]; profit = $34,000 - $27,750 = **$6,250** [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Investment 1: cumulative cash flow = $9,000 + $12,000 + $15,000 = $36,000 after 3 years [1], so payback = **3 years** [1]. Investment 2: $30,000 / $7,500 [1] = **4 years** [1]. **[4]**

**11.** **Higher break-even:** with higher fixed costs, Juno must sell more bottles before it makes a profit [1]; the break-even output rises above 6,000 bottles [1].
**Lower margin of safety and profit:** at 8,500 bottles, the margin of safety is smaller, so there is more risk if sales fall [1], and each extra $1,000 of fixed costs reduces monthly profit by $1,000 [1]. **[4]**

**12.** **For Investment 1:** payback is 3 years, a year faster than Investment 2 [1]; the machine would bring $36,000 in 3 years [1]; a new machine may also raise output or lower variable costs [1].
**For Investment 2:** the van costs $6,000 less [1]; the cash flows are steady at $7,500 a year, so they are easier to predict [1]; it may help to deliver to customers and sell more [1].
**Limits:** payback ignores profit after the payback date, and the cash flows are only forecasts [1].
**Judgement:** Investment 1 is probably better because it pays back sooner, but Juno should check whether it needs the extra capacity and how much cash it can afford to put in [1]; if Juno wants to reach more customers, then the van might still be worth buying later [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using total costs for the break-even output when the question asks for output.
- Forgetting to add fixed costs to variable costs.
- Defining margin of safety as profit rather than as a number of bottles.
- Calculating payback for Investment 1 as the cost divided by the average cash flow.
- Writing a conclusion without a reason based on the payback figures.
