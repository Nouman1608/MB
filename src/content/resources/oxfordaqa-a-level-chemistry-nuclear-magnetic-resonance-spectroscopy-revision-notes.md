## 3.3.15.1 in ten lines

1. Analytical techniques are used **together** to confirm the structure of a new compound.
2. NMR **locates ¹³C or ¹H atoms** within a molecule.
3. **¹³C spectra are simpler** than ¹H spectra.
4. Chemical shift is recorded on the **δ scale** (ppm).
5. Chemical shift **depends on the molecular environment**.
6. An **integrated** ¹H spectrum shows the ratio of ¹H atoms across environments.
7. ¹H samples are dissolved in **deuterated solvents or CCl₄**.
8. **TMS** is the standard, at δ = 0.
9. You suggest structures from ¹H and ¹³C spectra plus **data-booklet chemical shifts**.
10. The **n+1 rule** covers doublets, triplets and quartets in aliphatic compounds only.

## Terms to define precisely

| Term | Definition to write |
|---|---|
| Environment | The surroundings of a nucleus; nuclei in the same environment give one signal |
| Equivalent | Made identical by the symmetry of the molecule |
| Chemical shift, δ | Signal position in ppm, relative to TMS at 0 |
| Integration | Relative signal area in a ¹H spectrum, proportional to the number of H |
| Splitting | A ¹H signal divided into n + 1 peaks by n non-equivalent H on adjacent carbons |
| Deuterated solvent | A solvent with ¹H replaced by ²H (D), e.g. CDCl₃ |

## Shift ranges (approximate)

Use the Chemistry data booklet in the exam. These rounded ranges are written for these notes.

| ¹H near… | δ / ppm | ¹³C type | δ / ppm |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alkyl only | 0.7–1.7 | Alkyl | 5–45 |
| C=O | 2.0–3.0 | Next to C=O | 20–55 |
| Cl or Br on the same C | 3.0–4.5 | Bonded to Cl or Br | 30–75 |
| O on the same C | 3.1–4.3 | Bonded to O | 50–90 |
| C=C (H on the double bond) | 4.5–6.0 | C=C | 100–150 |
| Aldehyde CHO | 9.0–10.0 | C=O, acid or ester | 160–185 |
| Acid COOH | 10.0–12.0 | C=O, aldehyde or ketone | 190–220 |

The trend: an electronegative atom or a C=O group close to the nucleus means a **larger δ**. Two
electronegative atoms on the same carbon push the signal further still.

## TMS in four points

Tetramethylsilane, Si(CH₃)₄:

- **one intense signal**: 12 equivalent H and 4 equivalent C;
- signal at the **low-δ end**, clear of nearly all organic signals;
- **inert**: no reaction with the sample;
- **volatile**: evaporates off, so the sample can be recovered.

## Solvent choice

| Solvent | Suitable for ¹H NMR? | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| CDCl₃ | Yes | D (²H) gives no ¹H signal |
| CCl₄ | Yes | Contains no H |
| CHCl₃ | No | Its ¹H gives a huge signal that swamps the sample's |

Deuterium still has a nucleus, but it absorbs at a completely different frequency from ¹H, so nothing
from a CDCl₃ molecule appears in the ¹H spectrum.

## ¹³C spectra

- Lines = **carbon environments**. Look for symmetry first.
- δ says what kind of carbon each one is.
- No splitting and a wide δ range (about 0–220 ppm), so one clean line per environment. That is why
  ¹³C spectra are simpler.

**Worked reminder.** Butanedione, CH₃COCOCH₃, is symmetrical: two equivalent C=O carbons (190–220) and two
equivalent CH₃ carbons. Answer: **2 lines**.

## ¹H spectra: five-step method

1. **Count signals**: that is the number of H environments.
2. **Integration**: divide trace heights by the smallest, make whole numbers, scale to the formula.
3. **Shift**: match each δ to the table to locate the group.
4. **Splitting**: n + 1 peaks means n H on the adjacent carbons.
5. **Assemble and check**: draw the structure and tick off every signal, ratio and splitting.

**Worked reminder.** 1,2-dimethoxyethane, CH₃OCH₂CH₂OCH₃ (C₄H₁₀O₂), gives two singlets with trace
heights 21 mm and 14 mm. Ratio 21 : 14 = 3 : 2. The formula has 10 H, so scale by 2: **6 H and 4 H**.
The two CH₂ groups are next to each other, but they are **equivalent**, so they do not split each other
and give a singlet.

## The n+1 rule

| H on adjacent carbons | Pattern | Fragment it suggests |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | singlet | next to O, C=O, or a C with no H |
| 1 | doublet | a CH next door |
| 2 | triplet | a CH₂ next door |
| 3 | quartet | a CH₃ next door |

Fragments worth recognising on sight:

- **2H quartet + 3H triplet**: an ethyl group, CH₃CH₂–.
- **1H quartet + 3H doublet**: CH₃CH< with no other H neighbours.
- **1H triplet + 2H doublet**: –CH–CH₂– where neither carbon has other H neighbours.
- **2H triplet + 2H triplet**: –CH₂CH₂– with different groups at each end.
- **9H singlet**: (CH₃)₃C–.

O–H protons usually appear as singlets in the spectra you meet, and their δ varies from sample to sample.

## Combining techniques

| Data | Tells you |
|---|---|
| Molecular ion (mass spectrum) | Mr; a precise mass fixes the formula |
| IR absorptions | Bonds and functional groups |
| ¹³C lines and shifts | Number and type of carbon environments |
| ¹H signals, integration, splitting | H environments, how many H in each, neighbours |

Work in that order: formula, functional group, carbon skeleton, then H detail.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Shift vs splitting**: δ reflects nearby atoms (O, Cl, C=O); splitting counts H on the carbons next door.
- **Ratio vs number**: integration gives a ratio until you scale it to the formula.
- **¹³C vs ¹H**: ¹³C shows carbon environments with no splitting; ¹H gives integration and splitting.
- **Ester vs acid**: an ester has a ¹³C line at 50–90 (C–O) and no ¹H signal at 10–12.
- **CH₃ next to O vs CH₃ next to C=O**: both singlets; only δ tells them apart.

## Quick self-test

1. How many ¹³C lines does butanedione give, and how many ¹H signals?
2. How many ¹³C lines does 3-methylpentane, CH₃CH₂CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₃, give?
3. Integration trace heights are 8 mm, 12 mm and 20 mm. Give the simplest ratio.
4. A signal is a doublet. How many H are on the adjacent carbons?
5. Why can CCl₄ be used as a ¹H NMR solvent?
6. Which property of TMS stops its signal overlapping the sample's?
7. A ¹³C line appears at δ 172. What type of carbon does it suggest?
8. Predict the ¹H spectrum of 2,2-dichloropropane, CH₃CCl₂CH₃.
9. Two 2H triplets appear in one spectrum. What fragment does this suggest?
10. A ¹H signal appears at δ 3.5. Suggest what the H atoms are near.
11. Give two features that make a ¹³C spectrum easier to interpret than a ¹H spectrum.

### Answers

1. **2 lines** (C=O and CH₃); **1 signal**, a singlet, because all 6 H are equivalent.
2. **4** (the two end CH₃, the two CH₂, the CH, and the branch CH₃).
3. Divide by 4: **2 : 3 : 5**.
4. **1**.
5. It contains **no hydrogen**, so it gives no ¹H signal.
6. It absorbs **upfield of nearly all organic H**, so its signal stays clear of theirs.
7. A **C=O in a carboxylic acid or ester** (160–185).
8. **One singlet** (6H): the two CH₃ groups are equivalent and the central carbon has no H.
9. **–CH₂CH₂–** with a different group at each end.
10. An **O** (or a Cl or Br) on the same carbon.
11. **No splitting**, so one line per environment; and a **wider δ range**, so lines rarely overlap.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Missing symmetry, e.g. counting two lines for the equivalent C=O carbons in butanedione.
- Using n+1 on the H in the group itself instead of on the **adjacent** carbons.
- Splitting H on a carbon that is next to O or C=O, which should be a singlet.
- Giving equivalent neighbours a splitting: equivalent H **do not** split each other.
- Quoting the simplest integration ratio as the answer when the formula needs it scaled.
- Calling deuterated solvents "unreactive" instead of saying they give **no ¹H signal**.
- Listing "signal at zero" as a reason TMS is chosen.
- A structure that fits the splitting but puts a group on the wrong side of an O atom, against the δ data.
- Not checking the final structure against the molecular formula.
