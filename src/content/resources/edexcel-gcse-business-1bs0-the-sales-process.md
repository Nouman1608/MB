This guide covers content item 2.3.4 The sales process, the last item in Topic 2.3 Making operational decisions of the Pearson Edexcel Level 1/Level 2 GCSE (9-1) in Business (1BS0) specification, Issue 2, July 2022, assessed in Theme 2, Building a business (1BS0/02). The combined page on [quality and the sales process](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-quality-and-the-sales-process/) lists each part; this guide shows how each part works in different kinds of business and how to measure whether customer service is paying off. All businesses and figures are invented.

## What 2.3.4 contains

The specification lists:

- **The sales process:** product knowledge, speed and efficiency of service, customer engagement, responses to customer feedback, post-sales service.
- **The importance to businesses of providing good customer service.**

These five parts are best thought of as a journey: knowing the product before the customer arrives, serving them efficiently, engaging with them during the sale, listening to what they say, and supporting them afterwards. Each part is a chance to win or lose a customer.

## 1. Product knowledge

Staff who understand what they sell can answer questions, match products to customers' needs and explain why one option is better than another. This matters most for expensive, technical or unfamiliar products, such as laptops, bicycles, mortgages or skincare for sensitive skin.

Good product knowledge leads to: more sales completed, fewer unsuitable purchases and returns, and the confidence to sell add-ons that genuinely help (a case for a new phone, for example). Poor knowledge makes customers doubt the business and research elsewhere, often buying online instead.

### Worked example 1: does product knowledge training pay?

Avonside Electricals receives 2,000 customer enquiries a year about home appliances. At present 18% become sales. After a £3,500 training course on the main ranges, the manager expects 24%. The average sale is £320, with a contribution of 25%.

1. Sales now = 2,000 × 18% = 360; after training = 2,000 × 24% = 480
2. Extra sales = 120
3. Contribution per sale = £320 × 25% = £80
4. Extra contribution = 120 × £80 = £9,600
5. Net gain = £9,600 − £3,500 = **£6,100**

The 24% is an expectation. Even if conversion rose only to 21%, the extra 60 sales would bring £4,800, still more than the course cost.

## 2. Speed and efficiency of service

Customers value their time. Speed means serving them quickly; efficiency means doing it accurately, without errors or repeated effort. Examples: short queues, quick replies to online messages, fast delivery, orders that arrive correct first time.

Speed without efficiency backfires: a café that serves quickly but gets one order in ten wrong will waste food and annoy customers. Technology often helps, through self-service tills, online ordering, booking apps and tracking for deliveries.

## 3. Customer engagement

Customer engagement means building a relationship with customers through the way the business interacts with them: greeting them, showing interest in their needs, personalising service and keeping in touch. Online, it includes replying to comments, personalised emails and loyalty schemes. Engaged customers feel valued, are more likely to buy again and often promote the business to friends.

## 4. Responses to customer feedback

Feedback comes from surveys, reviews, comments on social media, complaints and conversations with staff. The specification's wording is **responses**: collecting feedback is not enough. A business must act on it and, ideally, tell customers what it changed.

Good responses: replying politely to online reviews, including bad ones; fixing the problem raised; using patterns in feedback to change products or processes. A complaint handled well can turn an unhappy customer into a loyal one, because it shows the business cares.

### Worked example 2: reading feedback data at Cobblers' Row Café

The café asks customers to rate their visit out of 5.

| Month | Responses | Rated 4 or 5 | Percentage rated 4 or 5 | Main complaint |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| May | 240 | 168 | 168 ÷ 240 × 100 = 70% | Slow service at lunch |
| June | 300 | 246 | 246 ÷ 300 × 100 = **82%** | Few vegan options |

After May, the owner added a second till at lunchtime. Satisfaction rose by 12 percentage points and the main complaint changed. That is a response to feedback working: the next step is to act on the vegan comments.

## 5. Post-sales service

Post-sales service is the support a customer receives after buying: delivery and installation, guarantees and warranties, repairs, help lines, easy returns and follow-up contact. It matters most for expensive or complex products, where customers worry about what happens if something goes wrong.

Post-sales service can also earn money. Brookmere Boilers sells an annual service plan for £96; it costs £60 to provide. If 400 customers buy it, the plan earns 400 × (£96 − £60) = **£14,400** a year in contribution and keeps customers in contact with Brookmere when they eventually need a new boiler.

## Why good customer service matters

The second bullet of 2.3.4 asks for the importance to businesses of providing good customer service. Build answers around these effects:

| Effect | How it helps the business |
|---|---|
| Repeat custom and loyalty | Returning customers cost less to win than new ones and buy regularly |
| Word of mouth and reviews | Satisfied customers recommend the business, bringing new customers cheaply |
| Competitive advantage | Service can set a business apart when products and prices are similar to rivals' |
| Premium pricing | Customers will pay more for excellent service |
| Lower costs | Fewer complaints, returns and mistakes to deal with |

Poor customer service reverses each one: lost customers, bad reviews that spread quickly online and extra costs from complaints.

Customer service is especially important for small businesses competing with large ones, which may have lower prices. A local hardware shop cannot match a national chain on price, but it can know its customers by name, give expert advice and order specific items.

## Linking the parts together

Examiners reward answers that show the parts affect each other. Product knowledge makes service faster (staff do not need to look things up). Feedback reveals where speed or knowledge is weak. Good post-sales service generates positive feedback and repeat sales. The overall aim of the sales process is a satisfied customer who comes back.

## Common mistakes

- Listing the five parts without explaining how each affects sales or customers.
- Treating speed as the only part that matters.
- Writing "collect feedback" when the specification says respond to it.
- Forgetting post-sales service in answers about expensive or technical products.
- Saying good service is important "because it is good" without naming a business benefit such as repeat sales or reputation.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate:** conversion rates, percentage satisfied, average waiting times, or the contribution from extra sales.
- **Outline:** two linked points in context, such as how faster service could help Cobblers' Row at lunchtime.
- **Explain:** a general statement with two expansions, for example why post-sales service builds loyalty.
- **Analyse:** take one part of the sales process and follow its effect on customers, sales and costs.
- **Justify:** choose between two service improvements using the figures.
- **Evaluate:** weigh the cost of better service against its benefits and conclude on what the result depends.
