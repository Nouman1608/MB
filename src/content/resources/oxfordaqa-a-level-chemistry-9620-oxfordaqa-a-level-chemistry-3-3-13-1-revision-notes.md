It is International A2 content, examined in Unit 4 and able to reappear in Unit 5. ## The subtopic in four lines

1. An amino acid, H₂N–CH(R)–COOH, has an acidic –COOH group and a basic –NH₂ group.
2. An internal proton transfer gives a zwitterion, ⁺H₃N–CH(R)–COO⁻, overall charge zero.
3. In acid: ⁺H₃N–CH(R)–COOH (charge +1). In alkali: H₂N–CH(R)–COO⁻ (charge −1).
4. Extra –COOH or –NH₂ groups in R react in the same way and change the charge.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning to learn |
|---|---|
| Amino acid | Compound with –NH₂ and –COOH groups; here both on the same carbon |
| Side chain (R) | The group that makes each amino acid different |
| Amphoteric behaviour | Reacting as both an acid and a base |
| Zwitterion | Species with a positive and a negative charge on different atoms, overall neutral |
| Protonation | Gain of H⁺ (–NH₂ → –NH₃⁺; –COO⁻ → –COOH) |
| Deprotonation | Loss of H⁺ (–COOH → –COO⁻; –NH₃⁺ → –NH₂) |
| Carboxylate ion | –COO⁻ |
| Substituted ammonium group | –NH₃⁺ |

## The three forms

| Form | –COOH end | –NH₂ end | Overall charge |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ion in acid solution | –COOH | –NH₃⁺ | +1 |
| Zwitterion | –COO⁻ | –NH₃⁺ | 0 |
| Ion in alkaline solution | –COO⁻ | –NH₂ | −1 |

## Method: any amino acid, any condition

1. List every –COOH and every –NH₂ in the molecule, including the side chain.
2. Acid: change every –NH₂ to –NH₃⁺; leave every –COOH.
3. Alkali: change every –COOH to –COO⁻; leave every –NH₂.
4. Zwitterion: move one H⁺ from the main –COOH to the main –NH₂.
5. Add up the charges.
6. Leave side-chain –OH, –SH, –CH₃ and rings unchanged.

## Examples to know the pattern from

| Amino acid | In excess acid (charge) | In excess alkali (charge) |
|---|---|---|
| Glycine, H₂N–CH₂–COOH | ⁺H₃N–CH₂–COOH (+1) | H₂N–CH₂–COO⁻ (−1) |
| Alanine, H₂N–CH(CH₃)–COOH | ⁺H₃N–CH(CH₃)–COOH (+1) | H₂N–CH(CH₃)–COO⁻ (−1) |
| Serine, R = –CH₂OH | ⁺H₃N–CH(CH₂OH)–COOH (+1) | H₂N–CH(CH₂OH)–COO⁻ (−1) |
| Aspartic acid, R = –CH₂COOH | +1 (both –COOH kept) | −2 (both –COO⁻) |
| Lysine, R = –(CH₂)₄NH₂ | +2 (both –NH₃⁺) | −1 (both –NH₂) |

## Equations

- With HCl: H₂N–CH(R)–COOH + HCl → ⁺H₃N–CH(R)–COOH Cl⁻.
- With NaOH: H₂N–CH(R)–COOH + NaOH → H₂N–CH(R)–COO⁻ Na⁺ + H₂O.
- Moles of NaOH = moles of amino acid × number of –COOH groups.
- Moles of HCl = moles of amino acid × number of –NH₂ groups.

## Stepwise changes with pH

Start in excess acid and add alkali a little at a time:

| Stage | Species (glycine) | Charge | What changed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Excess acid | ⁺H₃N–CH₂–COOH | +1 | Starting cation |
| After the first OH⁻ per molecule | ⁺H₃N–CH₂–COO⁻ | 0 | –COOH lost H⁺ |
| After the second OH⁻ per molecule | H₂N–CH₂–COO⁻ | −1 | –NH₃⁺ lost H⁺ |

- The –COOH group loses its proton first because it is the more acidic group.
- One mole of the cation needs two moles of OH⁻ to reach the anion.
- Adding acid to the anion reverses the steps: –NH₂ is protonated first, then –COO⁻.

## Drawing checklist

- Every –NH₃⁺ shows three H atoms on N and a + on N.
- Every –COO⁻ shows the − on a single-bonded O, with C=O kept.
- The side chain is copied exactly unless it contains –COOH or –NH₂.
- The α-carbon (the one carrying R) keeps its H atom in every form.
- If asked for the charge, add the charges you have drawn rather than guessing.

## Why zwitterions matter

- Solid amino acids exist as zwitterions.
- Ionic attractions between zwitterions give high melting points (often with decomposition).
- They dissolve well in water, a polar solvent, and poorly in non-polar solvents.
- This is evidence that the solid is ionic rather than made of neutral molecules.

## Exam traps

- –NH₃⁺ drawn in alkali, or –COO⁻ in acid.
- Charge on the zwitterion given as +1 or −1.
- Side-chain –COOH or –NH₂ ignored.
- Side-chain –OH deprotonated.
- Charges placed on C instead of N or O.
- –NH₄⁺ written for a protonated amino group.
- Forgetting H₂O as a product with NaOH.

## Self-test

Answer without looking, then check below.

1. Which group in an amino acid is acidic?
2. Which group is basic, and what feature makes it basic?
3. Define zwitterion.
4. Draw glycine as a zwitterion.
5. Draw the ion of alanine in excess hydrochloric acid.
6. Draw the ion of alanine in excess sodium hydroxide.
7. Write an equation for glycine with sodium hydroxide.
8. Give the charge of aspartic acid in excess alkali.
9. Give the charge of lysine in excess acid.
10. Does the –OH in serine react with dilute sodium hydroxide?
11. Why do amino acids have high melting points?
12. How many moles of NaOH react with 0.0050 mol of aspartic acid?

### Answers

1. The carboxyl group, –COOH.
2. The amino group, –NH₂; the lone pair on nitrogen accepts H⁺.
3. A species with both a positive and a negative charge on different atoms, with no overall charge.
4. ⁺H₃N–CH₂–COO⁻.
5. ⁺H₃N–CH(CH₃)–COOH.
6. H₂N–CH(CH₃)–COO⁻.
7. H₂N–CH₂–COOH + NaOH → H₂N–CH₂–COO⁻Na⁺ + H₂O.
8. −2.
9. +2.
10. No; it is an alcohol group and is not deprotonated under these conditions.
11. The solid is made of zwitterions held by strong ionic attractions.
12. 0.010 mol (two –COOH groups per molecule).
