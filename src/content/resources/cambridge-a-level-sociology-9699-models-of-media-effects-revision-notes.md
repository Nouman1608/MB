Level Sociology (9699) Paper 4. Full explanations are in the
[models of media effects study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-models-of-media-effects/);
exam-style questions are in the
[models of media effects practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-models-of-media-effects-practice/).

## The argument in one paragraph

Models of media effects differ on two dimensions: how active the audience is and how direct and
fast the effect is. The hypodermic syringe model sees a passive audience and immediate effects;
indirect models place audiences in social groups (two-step flow, normative), make them active
choosers (uses and gratifications) or stress slow, cumulative influence (cultural effects).
Evidence favours indirect models, but cultural effects research warns that active audiences can
still be shaped over time.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Hypodermic syringe model** | Media inject messages into a passive mass audience with immediate effects |
| **Two-step flow** | Media → opinion leaders → others (Lazarsfeld et al., 1944; Katz and Lazarsfeld, 1955) |
| **Opinion leader** | Person who pays close attention to media and influences others |
| **Multi-step flow** | Influence passes through several layers of people |
| **Uses and gratifications** | Audiences actively use media to satisfy needs (McQuail, Blumler and Brown, 1972) |
| **Normative model** | Media influence depends on group norms; messages that fit are accepted, others rejected |
| **Selective exposure, perception, retention** | Choosing, interpreting and remembering media in line with existing views (Klapper, 1960) |
| **Reinforcement** | Media mainly strengthen existing attitudes |
| **Cultural effects model** | Repeated messages slowly shape "common sense" |
| **Cultivation** | Long-term shaping of perceptions by heavy viewing (Gerbner) |
| **Mean world syndrome** | Heavy viewers see the world as more dangerous |
| **Mainstreaming / resonance** | Heavy viewers converge in outlook / effects are stronger where media match experience |
| **Preferred, negotiated, oppositional readings** | Ways of decoding a text (Hall, 1973; Morley, 1980) |

## The models at a glance

| Model | Audience | Key evidence | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hypodermic syringe | Passive | Bandura's experiments; propaganda fears | Ignores interpretation and context |
| Two-step flow | Social groups | *The People's Choice* (1944), 1940 US election | Influencers blur the two steps |
| Uses and gratifications | Active | Four needs: diversion, relationships, identity, surveillance | Media may create the needs they meet |
| Normative | Group norms | Variation in effects between groups | Hard to separate media from other influences |
| Cultural effects | Exposed over time | Gerbner; Philo, *Seeing and Believing* (1990) | Correlation, not proof of cause |

## Key evidence in brief

- **Cantril (1940), *The Invasion from Mars*:** reactions to Welles's 1938 *War of the Worlds*
  broadcast varied with education and checking other sources — even the classic direct-effects
  case shows variation.
- **Lazarsfeld, Berelson and Gaudet (1944):** personal contact more influential than media in
  voting decisions.
- **McQuail, Blumler and Brown (1972):** diversion, personal relationships, personal identity,
  surveillance.
- **Klapper (1960):** selective exposure, perception and retention; media mainly reinforce.
- **Gerbner:** heavy TV viewers see a "mean world"; mainstreaming and resonance.
- **Philo (1990):** groups recreated TV news images of violent picketing in the 1984–85 miners'
  strike; those with direct experience often rejected the media version.
- **Hall (1973); Morley (1980):** dominant, negotiated and oppositional readings.

## Strengths and limitations: the debate

- **Direct effects** are simple to test in laboratories but artificial and short-term.
- **Two-step flow** recognises social groups but simplifies networks.
- **Uses and gratifications** respects audience agency but may ignore media power over needs.
- **Normative** explains group differences but is hard to measure.
- **Cultural effects** explains long-term ideology but struggles to prove causation.
- **Methods matter:** experiment, panel survey, interview, content analysis, audience ethnography.

## How much influence?

- **Strong:** long-term cultivation; news shaping memory of events; imitation in experiments.
- **Limited:** selective exposure; personal influence; active readings.
- **Balanced view:** short-term direct effects are weak; long-term, cumulative effects on attitudes
  are more plausible, especially where people lack other sources.

## Exam traps

- Compare models; do not just describe them in sequence.
- The syllabus names five models; prepare the normative and cultural effects models as well.
- Bandura was a laboratory study of children; evaluate before using it as proof.
- Cultural effects are slow and cumulative; do not confuse them with direct effects.
- Link each model to the method that supports it.

## Self-test

1. What does the hypodermic syringe model assume about the audience?
2. What did Cantril (1940) find about reactions to the *War of the Worlds* broadcast?
3. Which study first proposed the two-step flow, and what election did it examine?
4. What is an opinion leader?
5. Name the four uses identified by McQuail, Blumler and Brown (1972).
6. Give one limitation of uses and gratifications.
7. What does the normative model claim?
8. Name Klapper's three selective processes.
9. What is mean world syndrome?
10. What did Philo's *Seeing and Believing* (1990) show?
11. Name the three readings in Hall's encoding/decoding model.
12. Why is it hard to prove cultural effects?

### Answers

1. That it is passive, made up of isolated individuals who respond in similar ways.
2. Reactions varied: listeners with more education or who checked other sources were less likely
   to be frightened.
3. *The People's Choice* (Lazarsfeld, Berelson and Gaudet, 1944), on the 1940 US presidential
   election.
4. A person who pays close attention to media and passes on and interprets its messages for others.
5. Diversion, personal relationships, personal identity, surveillance.
6. Any one: assumes people know their needs; media may create needs; underplays media owners' power.
7. Media influence depends on group norms; messages that fit are accepted, and media may set norms
   where other socialising agencies are weak.
8. Selective exposure, selective perception, selective retention.
9. Heavy viewers' belief that the world is more dangerous than it is (Gerbner).
10. Many people reproduced the TV picture of violent picketing in the miners' strike, but those with
    direct experience often rejected it.
11. Dominant (preferred), negotiated, oppositional.
12. Effects build slowly over years and are hard to separate from other influences; surveys show
    correlation rather than cause.
