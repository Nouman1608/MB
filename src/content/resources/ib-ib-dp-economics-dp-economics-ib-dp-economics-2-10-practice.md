Places and figures are invented, and the [1] points in the answers are a revision aid, not IB marks. Revise first with the [2.10 study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-economics-dp-economics-ib-dp-economics-2-10/) or the [2.10 revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-economics-dp-economics-ib-dp-economics-2-10-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Define *asymmetric information*. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between adverse selection and moral hazard. **[2]**

**3.** For each case, state whether it shows adverse selection or moral hazard. **[3]**

- A firm with a fixed-fee cleaning contract cleans less thoroughly once the contract is signed.
- People who expect to make large dental claims are the most eager to buy dental cover.
- A driver with full insurance leaves a car unlocked.

**4.** Define *screening* and give one example. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why a signal must be costly for low-quality sellers to copy. **[2]**

**6.** State **two** government responses to asymmetric information. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** In the fictional country of Fennick, an insurer offers travel cover to 200 people. 150 are low risk with an expected claim of 60 fennies; 50 are high risk with an expected claim of 300 fennies. The insurer cannot tell them apart and charges the average expected claim.

**(a)** Calculate the premium. **[2]**

**(b)** Low-risk travellers will pay at most 90 fennies for cover. Explain what happens to the market. **[3]**

**8.** A Fennick firm rents out power tools. With careful use, the chance of a tool breaking on a hire is 5%; with careless use it is 15%. A repair costs 800 fennies. Careful use takes effort a renter values at 20 fennies.

**(a)** Explain why a renter with no liability for damage uses tools carelessly. **[2]**

**(b)** The firm makes renters pay the first 300 fennies of any repair. Calculate the renter's expected cost of careless and of careful use, and state which is chosen. **[3]**

**9.** Explain how a professional qualification can reduce adverse selection in the labour market. **[3]**

**10.** Explain how a government-run inspection and rating scheme for private care homes could reduce market failure. **[3]**

**11.** Explain **one** advantage and **one** disadvantage of making health insurance compulsory. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate the effectiveness of government regulation as a response to asymmetric information. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** A situation where one party to a transaction [1] has more relevant information than the other party [1].

**2.** Adverse selection arises before a transaction, when one side hides characteristics such as quality or risk [1]. Moral hazard arises after a transaction, when one side changes its actions because it no longer bears the full cost [1].

**3.** Cleaning firm: moral hazard [1]. Dental cover buyers: adverse selection [1]. Driver leaving car unlocked: moral hazard [1].

**4.** The less-informed party designs a test or choice that makes the better-informed party reveal information [1], for example an insurer's medical questionnaire or an employer's probation period [1].

**5.** If low-quality sellers could copy it cheaply, both types would send it [1], so it would no longer separate high quality from low quality and buyers would learn nothing from it [1].

**6.** Any two: legislation such as disclosure laws [1]; regulation such as licensing or minimum standards [1]; provision of information such as published ratings; compulsory insurance.

**7. (a)** (150 × 60 + 50 × 300) / 200 = (9,000 + 15,000) / 200 [1] = **120 fennies** [1].

**(b)** 90 is below 120, so low-risk travellers do not buy [1]. Only high-risk travellers remain, so the insurer's average cost rises to 300 and it raises the premium [1]. Low-risk travellers who would gain from cover at a fair price are left uninsured, so trades that benefit both sides are lost: adverse selection causes market failure [1].

**8. (a)** Damage costs the renter nothing, so careful use brings no saving [1], while it costs 20 fennies of effort, so the rational choice is careless use; the firm cannot observe how tools are used [1].

**(b)** Careless: 0.15 × 300 = **45 fennies** [1]. Careful: 20 + 0.05 × 300 = **35 fennies** [1]. Careful use is cheaper, so the renter chooses it; the excess reduces moral hazard [1].

**9.** Employers cannot observe a job applicant's ability before hiring [1]. A qualification that is easier for high-ability people to obtain acts as a signal of ability [1], so employers can offer higher pay to qualified applicants and able workers are not driven out by an average wage [1].

**10.** Families choosing a care home cannot easily judge quality before moving a relative in, so poor homes can charge the same as good ones [1]. Published ratings narrow the information gap [1], so demand shifts towards better homes and poor homes face pressure to improve, moving the market closer to an efficient allocation [1].

**11.** Advantage: low-risk people cannot leave the pool, so the premium does not spiral upwards and the market does not unravel [1]; more people are covered [1]. Disadvantage: low-risk people pay more than their expected cost, subsidising high-risk people [1], which some see as unfair and which may weaken incentives to stay healthy (moral hazard) [1].

**12.** Regulation can set enforceable standards across a whole market, for example licensing doctors so patients need not judge competence [1]. Disclosure laws give buyers information sellers would otherwise hide, reducing adverse selection [1]. Compulsory insurance prevents low-risk people leaving the pool [1]. However, enforcement and inspection are costly [1], and rules may lag behind new products or be avoided through complex fine print [1]. Licensing can restrict entry and raise prices [1]. Regulation does little against moral hazard, which depends on hidden actions after the deal; private contract design such as an excess may work better there [1]. Judgement: regulation is most effective where quality can be checked at reasonable cost and the harm from poor information is serious, as in medicine, but less effective where behaviour after the deal cannot be observed [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Mixing up the timing of adverse selection and moral hazard.
- Calculating the pooled premium with unweighted averages.
- Explaining a response without naming the problem it addresses.
- Ignoring the equity effect of compulsory insurance.
