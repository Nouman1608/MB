This guide covers section 2.03, Percentages, of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Mathematics J560 specification, Version 2.1 (August 2026), for first assessment in 2017. Every statement in 2.03 is in the first two columns of the specification, so all of it is content for **both tiers**. Repeated percentage change, such as compound interest and depreciation over several years, belongs to section 5.03 and is not covered here.

The [percentages and ordering guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-mathematics-percentages-and-ordering-fractions-decimals-and/) introduces each method with one example. This page goes further on 2.03 alone: percentages below 1% and above 100%, building awkward percentages by hand, reading a multiplier back as a percentage change, original value problems in several contexts, and simple interest worked backwards. ## What the specification asks

| Ref | Statement | Column |
|---|---|---|
| 2.03a | Convert between fractions, decimals and percentages (for example 1 1/2 = 150%) | First (both tiers) |
| 2.03b | Understand percentage is "number of parts per hundred". Calculate a percentage of a quantity, and express one quantity as a percentage of another, with or without a calculator | First (both tiers) |
| 2.03c | Increase or decrease a quantity by a simple percentage, including simple decimal or fractional multipliers. Apply this to simple original value problems and simple interest | First (both tiers) |
| 2.03c | Express percentage change as a decimal or fractional multiplier. Apply this to percentage change problems (including original value problems) | Second (both tiers) |

## Parts per hundred, including the extremes

Percent means "per hundred", so any percentage is a fraction with denominator 100. That makes the unusual cases straightforward.

| Percentage | Fraction | Decimal |
|---|---|---|
| 150% | 150/100 = 3/2 = 1 1/2 | 1.5 |
| 250% | 5/2 | 2.5 |
| 12.5% | 12.5/100 = 1/8 | 0.125 |
| 0.5% | 0.5/100 = 1/200 | 0.005 |
| 33 1/3% | 1/3 | 0.333... |

A percentage above 100% is more than the whole: 250% of 48 is 2.5 × 48 = 120. A percentage below 1% is less than a hundredth: 0.5% of 3 000 is 0.005 × 3 000 = 15.

## Percentages of a quantity by hand

Build any percentage from 50%, 10%, 5%, 1% and their halves.

**Worked example 1.** Find 17.5% of £64 without a calculator.

```
10%   of 64 = 6.40
5%    of 64 = 3.20    (half of 10%)
2.5%  of 64 = 1.60    (half of 5%)
17.5% of 64 = 6.40 + 3.20 + 1.60 = £11.20
```

Subtraction can be quicker: 95% is 100% − 5%, so 95% of £64 is 64 − 3.20 = £60.80.

**With a calculator** use the decimal multiplier: 23% of 1 460 is 0.23 × 1 460 = **335.8**.

## One quantity as a percentage of another

Write a fraction with the reference quantity (the "whole") on the bottom, in the same units, then multiply by 100.

**Worked example 2.**

```
A class of 60 sells 78 tickets. Express the sales as a percentage of the target of 60.
78/60 × 100 = 130%

45 cm as a percentage of 1.2 m:
1.2 m = 120 cm,  45/120 × 100 = 37.5%
```

A result above 100% is not an error when the part exceeds the reference value.

## Multipliers

A multiplier changes a quantity in one step. For an increase of r%, multiply by (100 + r)/100; for a decrease, by (100 − r)/100. The specification's example adds 10% to £2.50 "by either finding 10% and adding, or by multiplying by 1.1 or 110/100": both give £2.75.

| Change | Decimal multiplier | Fractional multiplier |
|---|---|---|
| Increase by 4% | 1.04 | 104/100 = 26/25 |
| Decrease by 15% | 0.85 | 85/100 = 17/20 |
| Increase by 1/8 | 1.125 | 9/8 |
| Decrease by 2/5 | 0.6 | 3/5 |

Fractional multipliers are often easier without a calculator: increasing 72 by 1/8 is 72 × 9/8 = 9 × 9 = **81**.

### Reading a multiplier as a percentage change

The second-column statement asks you to "express percentage change as a decimal or fractional multiplier". Work backwards: new ÷ original gives the multiplier, and the multiplier tells you the change.

**Worked example 3.** A price falls from £240 to £204. Find the percentage change.

```
multiplier = 204 ÷ 240 = 0.85
0.85 = 85% of the original, so the price has fallen by 15%
```

A multiplier of 1.3 is a 30% increase; 0.92 is an 8% decrease; 2 is a 100% increase.

## Original value problems

If you know the value **after** a change, divide by the multiplier.

```
original × multiplier = new value
original = new value ÷ multiplier
```

**Worked example 4.** Three contexts.

```
Sale: after a 15% discount a jacket costs £68.
      68 ÷ 0.85 = £80

VAT:  a bill including 20% VAT is £150.
      150 ÷ 1.2 = £125 before VAT

Pay:  after a 4% rise a salary is £27 040.
      27 040 ÷ 1.04 = £26 000
```

The specification's simple version, the original price of an item costing £10 after a 50% discount, works the same way: 10 ÷ 0.5 = £20.

**Check by going forwards.** £80 × 0.85 = £68. Never find 15% of £68 and add it back: that gives £78.20, because 15% of £68 is a smaller amount than 15% of £80.

## Simple interest, including working backwards

Simple interest is the same amount every year, based on the original sum.

```
interest = principal × rate/100 × number of years
```

**Worked example 5.**

```
Find the time: £2 000 at 3.5% a year earns £350.
               interest per year = 2 000 × 0.035 = £70
               years = 350 ÷ 70 = 5

Find the principal: £84 is earned in 3 years at 4% a year.
               interest per year = 84 ÷ 3 = £28
               £28 is 4% of the principal, so 1% is £7
               principal = 7 × 100 = £700
```

Rearranging the formula gives the same results, but the unitary steps are easier to show on a non-calculator paper.

## Common mistakes

- Using 0.15 as the multiplier for a 15% decrease; that finds the discount, not the sale price.
- Writing 0.5% as 0.5 or 0.05.
- Putting the wrong quantity on the bottom in "as a percentage of".
- Calculating a percentage change against the new value instead of the original.
- Adding the percentage back on in an original value problem.
- Using simple interest when the question describes interest added to a growing balance (that is compound interest, in 5.03).
- Forgetting to state "increase" or "decrease" with a percentage change.

## Exam technique

- **Write the multiplier** you use. It shows your method even if the arithmetic slips.
- **"Show that"** a sale price or original price is correct: write the calculation and a forward check.
- **Money**: give answers to the nearest penny, with two decimal places.
- **Non-calculator percentages**: show each building block (10%, 5%, 1%) on its own line.
