## Syllabus content (1.1.1 The nature of business activity)

- Factors of production: land, labour, capital and enterprise
- Concept of adding value and how added value can be increased
- Concept of opportunity cost

## The big picture

Wants are unlimited; resources are limited. Business activity combines limited resources (factors of production) to make goods and services worth more than the inputs (added value). Every use of limited resources means giving up another use (opportunity cost).

## Factors of production: classification rules

| Rule | Example |
|---|---|
| Natural resources and sites are land | Forest, river water, mineral deposits, a factory site |
| Human effort is labour | Nurse, coder, driver, cleaner |
| Man-made aids to production are capital | Machines, vehicles, tools, buildings, a website |
| Risk-taking and organising is enterprise | The person who starts the business and bears the risk |
| Payments are not factors | Wages pay for labour; rent pays for land or premises |
| One person can supply two factors | An owner who also serves customers: enterprise and labour |

**Quality matters:** trained labour and modern capital raise output from the same quantity of factors.

**Changing the mix:** replacing labour with capital (for example self-service tills).

| For | Against |
|---|---|
| Lower wage costs over time | High purchase cost |
| Faster, more consistent output | Breakdowns halt production |
| Longer operating hours | Loss of personal service; job losses may hurt morale and reputation |

## Added value

**Definition:** the difference between the selling price of a product and the cost of the bought-in materials or inputs used to make it.

```
added value per unit = selling price − cost of bought-in inputs per unit
total added value    = added value per unit × units sold
profit               = total added value − other costs (wages, rent, overheads)
```

**Added value ≠ profit.** Worked check (juice bar): price 4.20, inputs 1.55, 3,000 sold → added value 7,950; other costs 6,300 → profit 1,650.

**Negative added value:** price below input cost (for example clearance of stock that would be wasted).

### Ways to increase added value

| Route | Methods | Risk to mention |
|---|---|---|
| Raise price customers will pay | Branding, quality, design, convenience, service, unique features, packaging | Sales may fall; extra costs of the improvement |
| Cut bought-in input cost | Cheaper or bulk suppliers, less waste | Quality may fall; supply less reliable |

**Always check the total.** Price 4.20 → 4.90 and inputs 1.55 → 1.85 with sales 3,000 → 2,700: total added value 7,950 → 8,235 (up 285). If sales fell to 2,500 instead: 7,625 (down).

## Opportunity cost

**Definition:** the benefit of the next best alternative given up when a choice is made.

- Only the **next best** alternative counts.
- Can be money, output, time or land use.
- Applies to businesses, individuals and governments.
- Entrepreneurs face it: leaving a salaried job gives up the salary.

**Method:** list options → note the chosen one → the highest-value option left is the opportunity cost → compare the gain with what is given up.

Field example: solar rent 11,500 chosen; barley 9,000; sheep 7,200 → opportunity cost 9,000.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Factors of production | Resources used to produce goods and services: land, labour, capital, enterprise |
| Land | Natural resources, including the site used |
| Labour | Human effort, physical and mental |
| Capital | Man-made resources used to produce other goods and services |
| Enterprise | Taking risks and organising the other factors |
| Added value | Selling price minus cost of bought-in inputs |
| Opportunity cost | Benefit of the next best alternative given up |
| Labour-intensive | Uses a high proportion of labour compared with capital |
| Capital-intensive | Uses a high proportion of capital compared with labour |

## Exam traps

- Writing "money" for capital, or "wages" for labour.
- Subtracting wages or rent when calculating added value.
- Concluding a price rise is good from per-unit added value alone.
- Adding all rejected options together as the opportunity cost.
- Giving the price of the chosen option as its opportunity cost.
- Generic answers with no reference to the business in the case.
- A recommendation with no reason, or one that ignores what is given up.

## Self-test

1. Name the four factors of production.
2. Which factor is a delivery van?
3. An owner invests her savings and also works in the shop. Which factors does she supply?
4. Why is "wages" not a correct answer when asked to identify a factor?
5. Define added value.
6. A product sells for 45.00 and its bought-in inputs cost 27.50. Calculate the added value per unit.
7. Why is added value not the same as profit?
8. Give two ways a business can raise the price customers are willing to pay.
9. Price rises and sales fall. What must you check before saying added value has increased?
10. Define opportunity cost.
11. Options: 2,600, 3,100 and 1,900 profit. The 3,100 option is chosen. What is the opportunity cost?
12. Give one example of an opportunity cost that is not measured in money.

**Answers:** 1. Land, labour, capital, enterprise. 2. Capital. 3. Enterprise and labour. 4. Wages are a payment for labour, not a factor. 5. The difference between the selling price of a product and the cost of the bought-in materials or inputs used to make it. 6. 45.00 − 27.50 = 17.50. 7. Wages, rent and other costs must still be paid out of added value. 8. Any two: branding, better quality or design, convenience, better service, unique features, packaging. 9. That total added value (per-unit added value × the new sales volume) is higher. 10. The benefit of the next best alternative given up when a choice is made. 11. 2,600. 12. For example, the owner's time spent on paperwork instead of selling, or a field used for one crop instead of another.
