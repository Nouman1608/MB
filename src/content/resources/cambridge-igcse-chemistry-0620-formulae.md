This guide covers subtopic **3.1 Formulae** in Topic 3, Stoichiometry, for Cambridge IGCSE
Chemistry 0620 (2026–2028 series). The topic overview in
[Formulae, Equations and the Mole](/resources/formulae-equations-and-the-mole/) covers the
outline; this page adds compound ions, a reliable method for ionic equations and practice
at turning a written description into a balanced equation.

## What the syllabus asks for

**Core**

- State the formulae of the elements and compounds named in the subject content.
- Define the **molecular formula** of a compound as the number and type of different atoms
  in one molecule.
- Deduce the formula of a simple compound from the relative numbers of atoms in a model or
  diagram.
- Construct **word equations** and **symbol equations**, including **state symbols**.

**Supplement** (Extended candidates only)

- Define the **empirical formula** as the simplest whole number ratio of the different atoms
  or ions in a compound.
- Deduce the formula of an **ionic compound** from a model or diagram, or **from the charges
  on the ions**.
- Construct symbol equations with state symbols, **including ionic equations**.
- **Deduce** the symbol equation with state symbols for a reaction, given relevant
  information.

## Formulae of elements

- **Metals, carbon and the noble gases** are written as single symbols: Na, Fe, C, He, Ar.
- **Seven common elements exist as diatomic molecules**: H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂. In an
  equation, chlorine gas is always Cl₂, never Cl.

## Molecular and empirical formulae

The **molecular formula** counts every atom in one molecule. The **empirical formula**
(Supplement) reduces this to the simplest whole number ratio.

| Compound | Molecular formula | Empirical formula |
|---|---|---|
| Ethene | C₂H₄ | CH₂ |
| Butane | C₄H₁₀ | C₂H₅ |
| Hydrogen peroxide | H₂O₂ | HO |
| Glucose | C₆H₁₂O₆ | CH₂O |
| Water | H₂O | H₂O (already simplest) |

Ionic compounds and giant covalent substances such as SiO₂ have no molecules, so the
formula written for them is always the empirical formula. Calculating empirical formulae
from masses belongs to subtopic 3.3.

## Deducing a formula from a model

Count the atoms of each element and write them in the formula. A ball-and-stick model of
ethanol with two carbon, six hydrogen and one oxygen atom gives the molecular formula
**C₂H₆O**; it is often written C₂H₅OH to show the –OH group, which contains the same atoms.
For a diagram of part of an ionic lattice (Supplement), count the **ratio** of ions: if
every Ca²⁺ is matched by two F⁻, the formula is **CaF₂**.

## Ionic formulae from charges (Supplement)

The total positive charge must equal the total negative charge.

**Compound ions to learn**

| Ion | Formula | Charge |
|---|---|---|
| Ammonium | NH₄⁺ | 1+ |
| Hydroxide | OH⁻ | 1− |
| Nitrate | NO₃⁻ | 1− |
| Carbonate | CO₃²⁻ | 2− |
| Sulfate | SO₄²⁻ | 2− |

**Metals with more than one ion** carry a Roman numeral: iron(II) is Fe²⁺, iron(III) is
Fe³⁺, copper(II) is Cu²⁺.

**Worked example 1: iron(III) sulfate**

1. Ions: Fe³⁺ and SO₄²⁻.
2. Lowest common multiple of 3 and 2 is 6.
3. Two Fe³⁺ give 6+; three SO₄²⁻ give 6−.
4. Formula: **Fe₂(SO₄)₃**. Brackets are needed because there is more than one compound
   ion; the subscript 3 multiplies everything inside.

Other results using the same method: ammonium sulfate **(NH₄)₂SO₄**; copper(II) hydroxide
**Cu(OH)₂**; sodium carbonate **Na₂CO₃**; aluminium nitrate **Al(NO₃)₃**. A single compound
ion needs no brackets: NaOH, not Na(OH).

## Word and symbol equations with state symbols

| Symbol | Meaning | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| (s) | solid | metals, precipitates, insoluble solids |
| (l) | liquid | water, molten compounds, bromine |
| (g) | gas | H₂, O₂, CO₂, Cl₂ |
| (aq) | aqueous — dissolved in water | acids, alkalis, soluble salts in solution |

**Example:** zinc reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid to give zinc chloride solution and
hydrogen.

```
word:    zinc + hydrochloric acid → zinc chloride + hydrogen
symbol:  Zn(s) + 2HCl(aq) → ZnCl2(aq) + H2(g)
```

Write correct formulae first, then balance by changing the numbers **in front** of formulae
only. Water itself is (l), not (aq).

## Ionic equations (Supplement)

An ionic equation shows only the particles that change. Ions that are in the same state
on both sides are **spectator ions** and are left out.

**Method**

1. Write the balanced symbol equation with state symbols.
2. Split every **aqueous ionic compound** into its ions. Leave solids, liquids, gases and
   molecules as formulae.
3. Cross out ions that appear unchanged on both sides.
4. Check that atoms **and charges** balance.

**Worked example 2: precipitation of barium sulfate**

```
BaCl2(aq) + Na2SO4(aq) → BaSO4(s) + 2NaCl(aq)

Ba²⁺(aq) + 2Cl⁻(aq) + 2Na⁺(aq) + SO4²⁻(aq) → BaSO4(s) + 2Na⁺(aq) + 2Cl⁻(aq)

spectators: Na⁺ and Cl⁻

Ba²⁺(aq) + SO4²⁻(aq) → BaSO4(s)
charge check: (2+) + (2−) = 0 on the left; 0 on the right ✓
```

**More ionic equations worth knowing**

- Displacement: Mg(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + Cu(s) (sulfate ions are spectators).
- Acid with a solid carbonate: CaCO₃(s) + 2H⁺(aq) → Ca²⁺(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g). The
  carbonate stays as a formula because it is a solid.
- Precipitation of a hydroxide: Cu²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Cu(OH)₂(s).

## Deducing an equation from information (Supplement)

The question gives you the reactants, products and conditions in words; you supply formulae,
balancing and states.

**Worked example 3.** *When solid potassium nitrate is heated it decomposes to solid potassium
nitrite, KNO₂, and oxygen gas.*

```
KNO3(s) → KNO2(s) + O2(g)      oxygen unbalanced: 3 left, 4 right
2KNO3(s) → 2KNO2(s) + O2(g)    K 2, N 2, O 6 on each side ✓
```

Unfamiliar formulae are always given; your job is the arithmetic and the states.

## Common mistakes

- **Changing a subscript to balance**, e.g. turning H₂O into H₂O₂.
- **Single atoms for diatomic gases**: writing Cl or O in an equation.
- **Missing brackets**: Fe₂SO₄₃ or NH₄₂SO₄.
- **(aq) on water**, or (aq) on a precipitate.
- **Splitting a solid into ions** in an ionic equation.
- **Unbalanced charges** in an ionic equation even though the atoms balance.
- **Empirical formula that is not a whole number ratio**, such as CH₂.₅ instead of C₂H₅.

## Exam technique: command words

- **State** the formula: one correct formula, with correct capitals and subscripts (Co is
  cobalt; CO is carbon monoxide).
- **Define**: use the syllabus definitions exactly — "number and type of different atoms in
  one molecule"; "simplest whole number ratio".
- **Construct** an equation: formulae, balancing and, where asked, state symbols — often a
  separate mark for each.
- **Deduce**: show the reasoning (charges, LCM, or atom counts) so method marks are
  available.

## Quick checklist

- Diatomic elements and common compound formulae
- Molecular formula definition; *(Supplement)* empirical formula definition
- *(Supplement)* ionic formulae with the five compound ions and Roman numerals
- Word and symbol equations with state symbols
- *(Supplement)* ionic equations and equations deduced from information
