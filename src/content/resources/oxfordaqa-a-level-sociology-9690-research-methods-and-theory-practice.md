These questions cover research methods and theory (3.1.3, especially 3.1.3.5) as assessed in **Unit
2 Section B** of OxfordAQA International AS/A level Sociology (9690). The specification sets one
20-mark extended response question there. Section A below gives short questions that build the
knowledge an extended answer needs; Section B gives longer questions and full 20-mark essays.
Tariffs other than 20 are Marlbridge practice tariffs.

---

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define what is meant by **value freedom**. **[2]**

**2.** Outline what Burawoy meant by **public sociology**. **[2]**

**3.** Outline the difference between **structure** and **agency**. **[2]**

**4.** Define what Merton meant by a **latent function**. **[2]**

**5.** Identify and briefly explain **two** differences between positivist and interpretivist
approaches to research. **[4]**

**6.** Identify and briefly explain **two** features of postmodernity. **[4]**

**7.** Identify and briefly explain **two** ways neo-Marxists differ from traditional Marxists. **[4]**

### Section B: longer questions

**8.** Explain **two** arguments for and **two** arguments against sociologists acting as activists.
**[8]**

**9.** Explain how symbolic interactionism influences a sociologist's choice of research methods.
**[8]**

**10.** Evaluate the view that sociology can and should be value free. **[20]**

**11.** Evaluate the usefulness of feminist theories for understanding society today. **[20]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that the choice of research method depends mainly on a sociologist's view
of structure and agency. **[20]**

---

## Answers

**1.** Research that is not influenced by the researcher's personal, political or moral values [1],
so that findings are objective and could be checked by others [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Sociology that engages directly with publics outside the university [1], such as communities,
social movements or the media, in dialogue about social issues [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Structure refers to the social forces and institutions that shape and constrain behaviour [1];
agency is individuals' capacity to interpret situations and act by choice [1]. **[2]**

**4.** An unintended and often unrecognised consequence of a social institution or practice [1], for
example schools providing a childcare function that allows parents to work [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Two differences, 2 marks each (identify [1], explain [1]):
- View of society [1]: positivists see society as an objective structure of social facts;
  interpretivists see it as built from meanings [1].
- Data [1]: positivists prefer quantitative data for reliability; interpretivists prefer qualitative
  data for validity [1].
- Aim [1]: positivists seek causes and patterns; interpretivists seek understanding (*verstehen*) [1].
**[4]**

**6.** Two features, 2 marks each:
- Media saturation [1]: images and information dominate, producing hyperreality (Baudrillard) [1].
- Fragmentation of identity [1]: people construct identities from global cultural choices rather
  than fixed class or community [1].
- Loss of faith in metanarratives [1]: grand theories and ideologies lose credibility (Lyotard) [1].
- Globalisation and consumption [1]: consumer culture and global flows replace production as the
  centre of social life [1].
**[4]**

**7.** Two differences, 2 marks each:
- Role of ideas [1]: neo-Marxists such as Gramsci give ideas and culture more independent importance
  than economic determinism allows [1].
- Consent and resistance [1]: hegemony must be won, so there is room for resistance and
  counter-hegemony [1].
- Culture industry [1]: the Frankfurt School analysed mass media and culture as a new form of control [1].
**[4]**

**8.** Two arguments for and two against, 2 marks each:
- For: exposes injustice [1]; research on poverty, racism or gender inequality can inform campaigns
  and policy, as Mills's sociological imagination encourages [1].
- For: benefits participants [1]; Burawoy's public sociology and feminist research aim to give
  voice to marginalised groups [1].
- Against: bias [1]; commitment may influence what is studied and found, reducing validity and
  objectivity [1].
- Against: credibility [1]; policymakers and the public may dismiss research seen as campaigning [1].
**[8]**

**9.** 8 marks, point-marked:
- Symbolic interactionists see society as created through interaction and shared meanings [1].
- Blumer: people act towards things on the basis of the meanings they hold [1].
- So researchers must understand meanings from the participants' viewpoint [1].
- This favours qualitative methods such as participant observation [1], which observe behaviour in
  natural settings and allow empathy [1].
- Unstructured interviews allow respondents to explain meanings in their own words [1].
- Interactionists reject imposing the researcher's categories, as closed questionnaires do [1].
- They may see official statistics as social constructs to be studied, not facts (Atkinson) [1].
**[8]**

**10.** Levels of response:
- 17–20: detailed knowledge of several positions; sustained analysis and evaluation; relevant
  research examples; justified conclusion.
- 13–16: good knowledge; clear evaluation of at least two positions.
- 9–12: reasonable knowledge; limited evaluation.
- 5–8: basic, descriptive.
- 1–4: fragments.

Indicative content. *For:* positivists (Comte, Durkheim) argue sociology can be a science with
objective methods; value freedom gives research credibility for policymakers; reliable,
standardised methods allow findings to be checked. *Partly:* Weber argues values guide topic choice
but research can still be objective in method. *Against:* Gouldner calls value freedom a myth;
Becker argues sociologists inevitably take sides; feminists show "neutral" research reflected male
assumptions (Oakley's critique of detached interviewing); funding bodies shape research agendas;
operationalising concepts involves value judgements; Burawoy argues sociology should engage
publics; postmodernists question objective truth. *Conclusion:* supported judgement, for example
that complete value freedom is impossible but transparency and rigorous method protect
objectivity. **[20]**

**11.** Levels of response as for Question 10.

Indicative content. *Strengths:* expose gender inequality in families, work and the state;
uncovered hidden issues such as domestic violence and emotion work; intersectionality (Crenshaw)
shows how gender combines with race and class; feminist methods brought reflexivity and
participant-centred research; international relevance, for example women's rights as part of
development. *Limitations:* feminisms disagree (liberal, Marxist, radical, black); radical
feminism may present all men as oppressors and all families as oppressive; may underplay change
such as girls' educational success; postmodernists question the category "women"; functionalists
see gender roles as complementary. *Conclusion:* supported judgement. **[20]**

**12.** Levels of response as for Question 10.

Indicative content. *For:* structural theories (functionalism, Marxism) see society as external and
measurable, favouring surveys and official statistics (Durkheim); action theories
(interactionism) stress agency and meaning, favouring participant observation and unstructured
interviews; Giddens' structuration may justify mixed methods. *Against:* practical factors (time,
funding, access, researcher characteristics) often decide; ethics, such as research on vulnerable
groups, rule out some methods; the topic itself matters, as sensitive topics need anonymity or
trust; many researchers use triangulation regardless of theory; feminists stress values and
power in the research relationship rather than structure and agency. *Conclusion:* supported
judgement, for example that theory frames choice but practical and ethical factors usually decide
the final design. **[20]**
