## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define what is meant by **ascribed status**. **[2]**

**2.** Define what is meant by **mores**. **[2]**

**3.** Outline what is meant by **role conflict**. **[2]**

**4.** Outline the difference between **ethnocentrism** and **cultural relativism**. **[2]**

**5.** Identify and briefly explain **two** ways in which norms vary between societies or over time.
**[4]**

**6.** Identify and briefly explain **two** differences between material and non-material culture.
**[4]**

**7.** Identify and briefly explain **two** ways in which stereotypes can become self-fulfilling.
**[4]**

### Section B: longer questions

**8.** Explain **two** ways in which status may be changing from ascribed to achieved in modern
societies. **[8]**

**9.** Using **one** example of a personal trouble, explain how Mills's idea of the sociological
imagination would connect it to a public issue. **[8]**

**10.** Explain **two** challenges that ethnic subcultures may face in multicultural societies. **[8]**

**11.** Using examples from a society you know, evaluate the view that society is held together by
shared norms and values. **[20]**

**12.** Evaluate the usefulness of Mills's sociological imagination for understanding the
relationship between individuals and society. **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** A social position fixed at birth or beyond a person's control [1], such as sex, age, caste or
royal birth [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Norms with strong moral significance [1], whose breach brings strong disapproval or outrage
and which may be backed by law (Sumner) [1]. **[2]**

**3.** A situation where the expectations of two roles a person holds are incompatible [1], for
example a parent whose employer expects overtime on the evening of a child's school event [1].
**[2]**

**4.** Ethnocentrism is judging other cultures by the standards of one's own, often seeing one's own
as superior [1]; cultural relativism is understanding a practice within its own cultural context [1].
**[2]**

**5.** Two ways, 2 marks each (identify [1], explain [1]):
- Dress [1]: what counts as modest or acceptable clothing differs between societies and religions [1].
- Greetings and manners [1]: bowing, handshakes or kissing on the cheek are the norm in different
  places [1].
- Change over time [1]: smoking in enclosed public places was once normal in many societies and is
  now banned or disapproved of [1].
- Gender norms [1]: expectations of women's paid employment have shifted in many societies [1].
**[4]**

**6.** Two differences, 2 marks each:
- Form [1]: material culture consists of physical objects such as tools, clothing and buildings;
  non-material culture consists of ideas such as values, beliefs and language [1].
- Change [1]: material culture can change rapidly with new technology, while values may change
  more slowly, creating tension between them [1].
- Visibility [1]: material culture can be observed directly; non-material culture must be inferred
  from behaviour or speech [1].
**[4]**

**7.** Two ways, 2 marks each:
- Internalisation [1]: members of a stereotyped group may come to believe the image and act on it,
  for instance lowering their aspirations [1].
- Institutional treatment [1]: teachers, employers or police who act on stereotypes may restrict
  opportunities, producing the outcome the stereotype predicted [1].
- Media amplification [1]: repeated representations shape how others react to a group, reinforcing
  the behaviour expected [1].
**[4]**

**8.** Two ways, 4 marks each (identify [1], explain [1], develop [1], evidence or example [1]):
- **Education and meritocracy** [1]: modern economies allocate jobs by qualifications [1], so
  occupational status depends more on achievement than family background [1]; for example,
  competitive public examinations select civil servants in many states [1].
- **Legal equality** [1]: laws against discrimination by sex, ethnicity or caste [1] reduce the
  formal power of ascribed characteristics [1], although sociologists show ascription still shapes
  outcomes informally, for example through family wealth and networks [1].
- Credit: urbanisation and anonymity; decline of hereditary positions; social mobility.
**[8]**

**9.** 4 marks for describing the trouble and Mills's concepts, 4 for linking to a public issue:
- Identifies a personal trouble [1], such as a young adult unable to afford to leave the parental
  home [1].
- Explains that Mills defines troubles as private matters within a person's milieu [1], often
  blamed on individual failings [1].
- Links to a public issue [1]: when many young adults are in the same position it reflects housing
  markets, wages and policy [1].
- Explains the sociological imagination as connecting biography and history [1], so the solution
  lies in structural change, not only personal effort [1].
**[8]**

**10.** Two challenges, 4 marks each:
- **Discrimination and stereotyping** [1]: minority groups may be stereotyped [1], limiting access to
  jobs, housing or fair treatment [1], which can push communities to rely more on their own
  networks [1].
- **Generational change** [1]: children socialised in schools and peer groups of the wider society
  [1] may adopt mainstream or hybrid norms [1], creating conflict with parents over language,
  religion or marriage [1].
- Credit: pressure to assimilate; loss of community language; tension over religious practices in
  public institutions.
**[8]**

**11.** Levels of response:
- 17–20: detailed knowledge of several perspectives; sustained application to a named society;
  balanced evaluation; justified conclusion.
- 13–16: good knowledge, some evaluation, some application.
- 9–12: reasonable knowledge; limited evaluation.
- 5–8: basic, descriptive.
- 1–4: fragments.

Indicative content. *For:* functionalists (Durkheim's collective conscience; Parsons on value
consensus) argue shared values create order; socialisation passes on norms; national rituals and
civic values unite diverse groups. *Against:* Marxists argue apparent consensus is ruling-class
ideology, and order rests on economic power and coercion; feminists argue values reflect patriarchy;
multicultural societies contain subcultures with different norms; interactionists see norms as
negotiated and changing; formal social control (law, police) also maintains order, so shared values
are not enough. *Application:* reward examples from a named society, such as national language
policy or tensions between subcultures. *Conclusion:* supported judgement, for example that a thin
layer of shared civic norms coexists with diversity and with coercion. **[20]**

**12.** Levels of response as for Question 11.

Indicative content. *Strengths:* shows that private problems such as unemployment, debt or divorce
have structural causes; challenges individualistic explanations; encourages comparison across
history and societies; underpins public sociology (Burawoy) and links sociology to policy;
relevant to a globalised world where distant forces shape local lives. *Limitations:* a way of
thinking rather than a testable theory; can be read as structurally determinist, underplaying
agency (interactionists, Giddens' structuration); Mills's own focus was mid-twentieth-century
America; postmodernists question grand structural explanations. *Conclusion:* reward a supported
judgement, for example that it is an essential starting point but needs specific theories and
evidence to explain any one issue. **[20]**
