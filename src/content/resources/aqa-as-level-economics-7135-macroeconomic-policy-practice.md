These questions cover **3.2.4 Macroeconomic policy** in AQA AS Economics (7135), specification version 1.3, parts 3.2.4.1 to 3.2.4.3. Country data are invented; the UK's 2% CPI inflation target is real. Revise first with the [3.2.4 study guide](/resources/aqa-as-level-economics-7135-macroeconomic-policy/) and the [3.2.4 revision notes](/resources/aqa-as-level-economics-7135-macroeconomic-policy-revision-notes/).

## Section A: short questions

**1.** Name the body that sets bank rate in the UK and state the main objective it uses bank rate to achieve. **[2]**

**2.** Which one of the following is an indirect tax? **[1]**

A: Income tax

B: Corporation tax

C: Value added tax

D: National Insurance contributions

**3.** Under a tax, a person earning 15,000 pays 1,800, a person earning 45,000 pays 5,400 and a person earning 90,000 pays 10,800. Classify the tax, showing your working. **[2]**

**4.** A government's debt is 2,000 bn. It runs deficits of 80 bn and 60 bn in the next two years and then a surplus of 10 bn. Calculate the national debt at the end of the three years. **[1]**

**5.** Distinguish between a supply-side policy and a supply-side improvement. **[2]**

**6.** Explain how a fall in the exchange rate may raise the rate of inflation. **[2]**

## Section B: structured questions

**7.** Explain how a rise in bank rate is likely to reduce the rate of inflation. **[6]**

**8.** A government cuts the basic rate of income tax and raises the rate of VAT so that total tax revenue is unchanged.

**(a)** Explain the likely effect on how the tax burden is shared between households on low and high incomes. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain one way the change might affect incentives to work. **[2]**

**9.** Explain two ways in which fiscal policy can be used to influence aggregate supply. **[4]**

**10.** Country X's government spends 420 bn and receives 390 bn in revenue in a year. Its national debt at the start of the year is 900 bn and its GDP is 1,500 bn.

**(a)** Calculate the budget balance as a percentage of GDP. **[2]**

**(b)** GDP grows to 1,560 bn by the end of the year. Calculate the debt as a percentage of GDP at the start and end of the year. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why a government might deliberately run a budget deficit in a recession. **[2]**

**11.** Explain how a cut in corporation tax might improve a country's current account balance in the long run. **[4]**

**12.** Inflation is 6%, well above the 2% target, mainly because of a rise in world energy prices. Real GDP is growing by only 0.5% a year. Evaluate whether the Monetary Policy Committee should raise bank rate. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** The Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) of the Bank of England [1]. Its main objective is the government's target for inflation, 2% for CPI inflation [1]. *Total: 2.*

**2.** **C** [1]. VAT is levied on spending; the others are taxes on income or profit. *Total: 1.*

**3.** Average rates: 1,800 / 15,000 = 12%; 5,400 / 45,000 = 12%; 10,800 / 90,000 = 12% [1]. The average rate is the same at every income, so the tax is **proportional** [1]. *Total: 2.*

**4.** 2,000 + 80 + 60 - 10 = **2,130 bn** [1]. *Total: 1.*

**5.** A supply-side policy is a government measure intended to raise productive potential, such as spending on training [1]. A supply-side improvement is an actual increase in productive potential, which may also come from private-sector innovation or investment [1]. *Total: 2.*

**6.** A lower exchange rate makes imports dearer, raising the price of imported goods and of imported materials used by firms [1]. Exports become cheaper, so net exports and AD rise, adding demand-pull pressure [1]. *Total: 2.*

**7.** The MPC raises bank rate, and commercial banks raise their interest rates on loans and mortgages [1]. Borrowing becomes more expensive and saving more rewarding, so households cut spending on credit and mortgage holders have less disposable income [1]. Firms face higher costs of finance and cut investment [1]. Higher UK rates attract saving from abroad, so the exchange rate tends to rise, making exports dearer and imports cheaper [1]. Consumption, investment and net exports fall, so AD shifts left [1]. Demand-pull pressure eases and cheaper imports lower costs, so inflation falls, after a time lag [1]. *Total: 6.*

**8. (a)** Income tax is progressive, so a cut gives the largest gains to higher earners [1]. VAT tends to be regressive, because lower-income households spend a larger share of their income [1]. Shifting revenue from income tax to VAT therefore moves more of the tax burden onto low-income households [1].

**(b)** A lower income tax rate raises take-home pay for each extra hour worked [1], which may encourage people to work longer hours or to move from benefits into work [1]. *Total: 5.*

**9.** Spending on education and training raises workers' skills and productivity [1], so the economy's productive capacity grows and LRAS shifts right [1]. Cutting taxes on income or profits raises the reward for working and investing [1], which can increase the labour supply and the capital stock, raising potential output [1]. Other acceptable answers: infrastructure spending; welfare reform. *Total: 4.*

**10. (a)** Balance = 390 - 420 = -30 bn, a deficit [1]. -30 / 1,500 = **-2.0% of GDP** [1].

**(b)** Start: 900 / 1,500 = **60.0%** [1]. End: debt = 930 bn, so 930 / 1,560 = **59.6%** [1].

**(c)** In a recession AD is too low and unemployment is rising [1]. Spending more than it collects in taxes injects extra demand into the circular flow, raising output and jobs through the multiplier [1]. *Total: 6.*

**11.** Lower corporation tax raises firms' post-tax profit, so they have more funds and more incentive to invest [1]. More investment in new machinery and technology raises productivity [1]. Lower unit costs let domestic firms cut prices or improve quality, so exports become more competitive and imports less attractive [1]. Export volumes rise relative to imports, improving the trade balance and the current account, though only over a long period [1]. *Total: 4.*

**12.** Raising bank rate would cut C and I and strengthen the exchange rate, reducing AD [1], and the stronger currency would lower import prices, including the price of imported energy [1]. Acting firmly may stop expectations of high inflation feeding into wage claims, which would otherwise make inflation persist [1]. However, this inflation is cost-push from a world energy shock, which bank rate cannot reverse [1]. With growth of only 0.5%, higher rates could tip the economy into recession, raising unemployment [1]. If the energy price rise is one-off, inflation will fall back as the rise drops out of the annual rate, without action [1]. Time lags mean a rise now would bite when inflation may already be falling [1]. Judgement: the MPC should raise bank rate only modestly, or hold it, unless there is evidence of rising wage growth or inflation expectations; the risk to output is high and the cause lies outside its control [1]. *Total: 8.*

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying the government or the Chancellor sets bank rate.
- Classifying a tax from the total paid rather than the average rate.
- Using the old GDP in a debt ratio after growth.
- Giving a rate rise chain that stops at "spending falls" without AD and the price level.
- Recommending a rate rise for cost-push inflation with no mention of the risk to growth.
