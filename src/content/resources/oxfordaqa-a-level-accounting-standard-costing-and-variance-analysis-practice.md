## Questions

**1.** Define the terms *standard cost* and *variance analysis*. **[2]**

**2.** Explain **two** advantages to a manufacturer of operating a standard costing system. **[4]**

**3.** Ardwinton Sails uses a standard of 14 metres of sailcloth per sail at 7.50 per metre. In April it
made 260 sails and used 3,710 metres, costing 26,712. Calculate both materials variances (price and
usage), labelling each favourable or adverse. **[4]**

**4.** Pentreath Bakehouse sets a labour standard of 0.2 hours per tray of rolls at 13.50 per hour. Last
month it produced 9,400 trays. Bakers worked and were paid for 1,950 hours, costing 25,935.

**(a)** Calculate the labour variances for rate and for efficiency. **[4]**
**(b)** Suggest **one** possible cause of the labour efficiency variance. **[2]**

**5.** Corsley Scooters budgeted to sell 900 scooters at 240 each. The standard variable cost is 150 per
scooter. It actually sold 840 scooters at 255 each.

**(a)** Calculate the sales price variance and the sales volume variance. **[4]**
**(b)** Comment on whether the price rise benefited the business. **[2]**

**6.** Drumcliffe Packaging has a labour standard of 0.75 hours per carton run at 15.00 per hour. It completed
2,000 runs, and staff worked and were paid for 1,460 hours. The total labour variance was 495 adverse.

**(a)** Calculate the labour efficiency variance. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the labour rate variance. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the actual rate paid per hour. **[2]**

**7.** Morvah Upholstery replaced several experienced upholsterers with trainees on a lower hourly rate.
The variances for the month were: labour rate 2,880 F, labour efficiency 3,520 A, materials usage 1,140 A.
Explain how these variances may be linked, and assess whether the staffing change benefited the business
this month. **[6]**

**8.** Hindlip Flasks makes insulated steel flasks. Standard cost per flask: steel 0.8 kg at 5.00 per kg;
labour 0.25 hours at 18.00 per hour. The budget was for 10,000 flasks. Actual output was 10,400 flasks,
using 8,480 kg of steel costing 41,552 and 2,540 labour hours costing 47,244.

**(a)** Calculate the four materials and labour variances. **[4]**
**(b)** Prepare a statement reconciling the budgeted cost of materials and labour with the actual cost. **[4]**

**9.** Glenarvon Kayaks makes one sea kayak. Its standard cost card per kayak is: polymer 18 kg at 4.00 per
kg; labour 6 hours at 15.00 per hour. The standard selling price is 380. The budget was to make and sell
500 kayaks, with fixed overheads of 70,000.

Actual results: 520 kayaks were made and sold at 370 each. Polymer used was 9,520 kg, costing 39,032. Labour
was 3,050 hours, costing 46,970. Fixed overheads were 70,000.

**(a)** Work out the profit in the budget and the profit actually made. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the six sales, materials and labour variances. **[6]**
**(c)** Prepare a statement reconciling the budgeted profit with the actual profit. **[4]**

**10.** The directors of Tansley Pumps plan to pay the purchasing manager a bonus equal to 10% of any
favourable materials price variance, and the production supervisor a bonus equal to 10% of any favourable
labour efficiency variance. Evaluate this proposal. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** A standard cost is the planned cost of making one unit, decided before production begins from
expected quantities and prices [1]. Variance analysis compares actual results with standards, measures the
differences and explains their causes so managers can act [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Expected cost" on its own is too vague; say the cost is set in advance and per unit.

**2.** Responsibility: each variance is traced to one manager [1], such as the buyer for material prices,
who can then be held to account [1]. Management by exception: only significant
variances need investigating [1], which saves senior managers' time for the problems that matter [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Explain" needs each advantage developed into a consequence for the business, not a bare list.

**3.** Actual price = 26,712 ÷ 3,710 = 7.20 per metre. Price variance = (7.50 − 7.20) × 3,710 [1]
= **1,113 F** [1]. Standard quantity = 260 × 14 = 3,640 m; usage variance = (3,640 − 3,710) × 7.50 [1]
= **525 A** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the formula with numbers substituted, so that a slip in arithmetic still leaves a
correct method visible.

**4. (a)** Actual rate = 25,935 ÷ 1,950 = 13.30. Rate = (13.50 − 13.30) × 1,950 [1] = **390 F** [1].
Standard hours = 9,400 × 0.2 = 1,880; efficiency = (1,880 − 1,950) × 13.50 [1] = **945 A** [1].
**(b)** Bakers took 70 hours more than standard [1]; this could be because newer, lower-paid bakers were
less practised, which also fits the favourable rate variance [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Tie the cause to the data (extra hours, lower rate), not a stock phrase.

**5. (a)** Sales price = (255 − 240) × 840 [1] = **12,600 F** [1]. Standard contribution = 240 − 150 = 90;
sales volume = (840 − 900) × 90 [1] = **5,400 A** [1].
**(b)** The two sales variances net to 12,600 − 5,400 = 7,200 F [1], so the rise added to profit despite
the 60 lost sales, though the lost customers may matter in future periods [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Using the 240 selling price, rather than the 90 contribution, for the volume variance
is a frequent error and overstates the loss.

**6. (a)** Standard hours = 2,000 × 0.75 = 1,500. Efficiency = (1,500 − 1,460) × 15.00 [1] = **600 F** [1].
**(b)** Rate = total − efficiency = 495 A − 600 F = **1,095 A** [1].
**(c)** 1,095 ÷ 1,460 = 0.75 more per hour than standard [1], so actual rate = 15.00 + 0.75 = **15.75** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Treat adverse figures as negative when combining them; adding 495 and 600 gives a
wrong rate variance.

**7.** Trainees are paid less, giving the favourable rate variance of 2,880 [1]. They work more slowly, so
more hours were needed than standard: the adverse efficiency variance [1]. Inexperience also leads to
mistakes and wasted fabric, explaining the adverse materials usage [1]. Net effect: 2,880 − 3,520 − 1,140
= **1,780 A** [1], so this month the change reduced profit [1]. But trainees should speed up and waste less with
experience, so the change may pay off later if the trend improves [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* An "assess" question needs a judgement backed by the net figure, and a point that
looks beyond the current month.

**8. (a)** Actual price = 41,552 ÷ 8,480 = 4.90; price = (5.00 − 4.90) × 8,480 = **848 F** [1].
Usage = (8,320 − 8,480) × 5.00 = **800 A** [1]. Actual rate = 47,244 ÷ 2,540 = 18.60;
rate = (18.00 − 18.60) × 2,540 = **1,524 A** [1]. Efficiency = (2,600 − 2,540) × 18.00 = **1,080 F** [1].

**(b)**

| Budgeted cost (10,000 × 8.50) | | 85,000 |
| Add: extra output of 400 flasks × 8.50 | | 3,400 |

Volume adjustment of 3,400 [1]; standard cost of actual output 88,400 [1]; variances added if adverse and
deducted if favourable, net 396 A [1]; actual cost 88,796 [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* From 85,000, the four variances alone will not reach 88,796; show the output change first.

**9. (a)** Standard contribution = 380 − 72 − 90 = 218. Budgeted profit = 500 × 218 − 70,000 = **39,000** [1].
Revenue = 520 × 370 = 192,400; actual profit = 192,400 − 39,032 − 46,970 − 70,000 = **36,398** [1].
**(b)** Working: actual polymer price 39,032 ÷ 9,520 = 4.10; standard polymer 520 × 18 = 9,360 kg; actual rate 46,970 ÷ 3,050 = 15.40; standard hours 520 × 6 = 3,120.
Sales price = (370 − 380) × 520 = **5,200 A** [1]. Sales volume = (520 − 500) × 218 = **4,360 F** [1].
Materials price = (4.00 − 4.10) × 9,520 = **952 A** [1]. Materials usage = (9,360 − 9,520) × 4.00
= **640 A** [1]. Labour rate = (15.00 − 15.40) × 3,050 = **1,220 A** [1]. Labour efficiency
= (3,120 − 3,050) × 15.00 = **1,050 F** [1].
**(c)**

| Glenarvon Kayaks: reconciliation of budgeted and actual profit | F | A | Dollars |
|---|---|---|---|
| Budgeted profit | | | 39,000 |
| Sales price | | 5,200 | |
| Sales volume | 4,360 | | |
| Materials price | | 952 | |

| Labour rate | | 1,220 | |
| Labour efficiency | 1,050 | | |
| Totals | 5,410 | 8,012 | 2,602 A |
| **Actual profit** | | | **36,398** |

Starts from budgeted profit [1]; favourable variances added [1]; adverse variances deducted [1]; ends on
actual profit 36,398 [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* Work out actual profit separately first; if your statement does not reach it, a
variance has the wrong sign or value.

**10.** For: each manager gets a clear, measurable target [1], which may push the buyer to negotiate
harder [1] and the supervisor to cut idle time [1]. Both variances are within
the control of the person being rewarded, so the scheme matches standard costing's aim of responsibility
[1].

Against: the buyer could earn a bonus by buying cheaper, lower-grade material [1], which raises materials
usage and slows labour, creating adverse variances that cost more than the saving [1]. The supervisor
might rush work to save hours, increasing waste or rejects and damaging quality [1], and sales volume
could fall if customers receive faulty pumps [1]. Out-of-date or slack standards could earn bonuses for no real
improvement [1]. Rewarding favourable variances with no penalty for
knock-on effects encourages each manager to protect their own figure [1].

Judgement: the proposal should not go ahead in this form [1]. A better scheme would base bonuses on the
net total of related variances or on overall profit, with quality checks and regularly updated standards
[1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* Extended writing needs both sides, specific variance links and a supported conclusion.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Basing standard kilograms or standard hours on the budget's output level.
- Putting actual prices or pay rates into the usage or efficiency formula.
- Leaving variances unlabelled, or calling a cost saving adverse.
- Valuing sales volume at selling price instead of standard contribution.
- Adding adverse variances to budgeted profit.
- Leaving out the volume adjustment in a cost reconciliation.
- Evaluations with no final judgement.
