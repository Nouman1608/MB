## Questions

**1.** Define profit maximisation and state the condition that identifies the profit-maximising output. **[3]**

**2.** Use the Firm Beta table.

**(a)** Calculate profit at an output of 5 thousand units, showing your working. **[2]**
**(b)** State the profit-maximising output and the profit earned there. **[2]**

**3.** Firm Beta wants to maximise sales revenue but its shareholders insist on a profit of at least £50 thousand.

**(a)** State the output chosen and the total revenue it earns. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why Firm Beta could not sustain unconstrained revenue maximisation. **[2]**

**4.** Firm Beta aims to maximise sales volume while earning at least normal profit. State the output and price, and justify why a larger output is ruled out. Use whole thousands of units. **[4]**

**5.** A firm has capital of £4.0 million and makes a profit of £0.9 million. It retains 70% of profit to invest.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage growth in its capital. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain one factor that could limit this growth strategy. **[2]**

**6.** Firm Beta is run by an owner who satisfices, accepting any profit of at least £75 thousand.

**(a)** State the outputs that meet this target. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the owner may choose the lowest of them. **[2]**

**7.** Explain what is meant by the principal-agent problem. **[5]**

**8.** A manager is paid a bonus of 2% of profit. Two plans give these profits over four years (£ million): plan A 5.0, 4.5, 3.5, 3.0; plan B 4.0, 4.5, 5.0, 5.5.

**(a)** Which plan does the manager choose if the bonus uses only year-one profit? Give the bonus in each case. **[2]**
**(b)** State how much more total profit plan B earns over four years. **[1]**
**(c)** Find the bonus under each plan if it is based on the four-year average profit. **[2]**
**(d)** Evaluate one limitation of using the four-year average. **[2]**

**9.** A firm switches to recycled packaging, adding £45,000 a year to costs. Each unit sold earns a contribution of £5. Current annual profit is £600,000.

**(a)** Calculate the extra units that must be sold to cover the cost. **[2]**
**(b)** Express the cost as a percentage of current profit. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain one way this CSR action might not reduce long-run profit. **[2]**

**10.** Firm Beta is taken over by a social enterprise that sets the lowest price possible while covering all costs. Use whole thousands of units.

**(a)** State the output and price, and compare them with the profit-maximising price. **[3]**
**(b)** Evaluate one cost to the enterprise of this objective. **[3]**

**11.** Evaluate the view that profit maximisation is the most relevant objective for businesses. **[10]**

**12.** A family-owned joinery workshop and a large listed construction company operate in the same region. Evaluate the factors that might explain why their objectives differ. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Profit maximisation is choosing the output that gives the greatest gap between total revenue and total cost [1]. The rule is MR = MC [1], reached with MC rising through MR from underneath [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Making the most money" alone is vague, so a definition earns more when the MR = MC rule is written out.

**2. (a)** Profit = TR − TC [1] = 300 − 220 = **£80 thousand [1]**. **[2]**
**(b)** The highest profit in the table is at an output of **4 thousand units [1]**, where profit is **£88 thousand [1]**. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the subtraction, and keep the unit (£ thousand) with the final figure.

**3. (a)** Outputs with profit of at least 50 are 2 to 6, and the highest revenue among them is at **6 thousand units [1]**, with **TR = £336 thousand [1]**. **[2]**
**(b)** Revenue peaks at 10 thousand units (£400 thousand) [1], but TC is £600 thousand, a loss of £200 thousand that cannot be sustained [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote values from the schedule in the explanation; a vague remark about "too many costs" is weaker.

**4.** Profit must be at least zero [1]. At 7 thousand units profit is £16 thousand, so this is affordable [1], and the price is 80 − 28 = **£52 [1]**. At 8 thousand units profit is −£40 thousand, so the firm cannot cover its costs [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers earn credit for identifying the constraint (costs covered) as well as the output.

**5. (a)** Retained profit = 0.7 × 0.9 = £0.63 million [1]. Growth = 0.63 ÷ 4.0 [1] = **15.75% [1]**. **[3]**
**(b)** Shareholders may want dividends, so retention is limited [1], and lower payouts or poor profit may raise the risk of a takeover [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the constraint and say how it restricts growth. A bare mention such as "finance" does not explain anything.

**6. (a)** Profits of 80, 88 and 80 meet the target at outputs of **3, 4 and 5 thousand units [1]**. Output 2 (56) and output 6 (56) fall short [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The owner needs no more than a satisfactory profit [1], so may prefer the smallest output of 3 thousand units because it needs fewer staff and less effort for similar profit [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Tie the answer to the idea that a satisficer stops searching once the target is met.

**7.** A principal hires an agent to act on their behalf, such as shareholders and managers [1]. The two have different objectives, for example profit for shareholders but pay or status for managers [1]. The agent knows more than the principal, which is asymmetric information [1]. Monitoring is costly and imperfect, so the agent can act in their own interest [1]. This may lead to lower profit, wasteful spending or poor risk choices [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Explain why the problem arises (divergent aims and unequal information), not only that managers "do not always do what they are told".

**8. (a)** Year one: plan A gives 2% × 5.0 = £100,000 and plan B gives 2% × 4.0 = £80,000 [1], so the manager chooses **plan A [1]**. **[2]**
**(b)** Plan A totals 16.0 and plan B totals 19.0, so B earns **£3.0 million more [1]**. **[1]**
**(c)** Averages are 4.0 for A and 4.75 for B [1]. Bonuses are **£80,000 for A and £95,000 for B [1]**. **[2]**
**(d)** One limitation: the manager still has little reason to care about profit beyond year four [1], or the manager may delay costs to inflate the profit in the averaged years [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A limitation earns more when the reason is developed, so say what the manager can do and why it hurts shareholders.

**9. (a)** Units = 45,000 ÷ 5 [1] = **9,000 units [1]**. **[2]**
**(b)** 45,000 ÷ 600,000 × 100 [1] = **7.5% [1]**. **[2]**
**(c)** Customers may favour the firm and buy more, raising sales [1], or the brand may be protected against bad publicity, preserving future profit [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* An unsupported "CSR is good for the brand" is thin. Link the action to a mechanism that affects revenue or costs.

**10. (a)** Break-even (profit at least zero) allows up to **7 thousand units [1]** at a price of **£52 [1]**, compared with £64 at the profit-maximising output of 4 thousand units [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Profit falls from £88 thousand to £16 thousand [1], leaving far less to reinvest or to cover shocks [1]. The judgement depends on whether the benefit to buyers outweighs the lost funds, and on whether donors or grants can fill the gap [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A one-sided list of costs leaves the question unanswered, so reach a short judgement.

**11.** In favour: profit pays owners and funds investment [1]; survival in competitive markets needs profit [1]; it is clear and measurable, and a usable benchmark [1]. Against: firms rarely know MC and MR exactly [1]; ownership and control are separate in large firms, so managers pursue other aims [1]; stakeholders and CSR matter [1]; short-run maximum may harm long-run profit [1]. Application to a firm in the question [1]. Judgement: relevant as a long-run constraint and benchmark, less so as a full description for large or social firms [1], justified by a stated factor such as ownership or competition [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Both sides plus a conclusion that names what the answer depends on tend to read as evaluation, whereas two lists with no verdict read as description.

**12.** Ownership: the owner of the workshop controls decisions and may satisfice or value lifestyle and local standing [1]; the listed company faces shareholders and may prioritise profit and share value [1]. Control: the large company has a principal-agent problem, so managers may pursue revenue, growth or utility [1], and incentives matter [1]. Competition: strong competition pushes both toward profit and efficiency [1]. Finance: the workshop has limited funds, so survival may dominate [1]; the larger firm can fund growth [1]. Stakeholders: a visible large firm faces more pressure for CSR [1]. Judgement: ownership and control are probably the most important, because they decide whose aims count [1], though competition can override the preferences of either [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare the two firms directly, point by point, rather than describing each separately.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Stating the objective but not the rule or the figures that show it.
- Mixing up revenue maximisation (MR = 0) with profit maximisation (MR = MC).
- Forgetting the profit floor when asked about sales volume.
- Omitting units such as £ thousand or percentage signs.
- Defining the principal-agent problem without the information gap.
- Listing solutions with no cost or side effect.
- Treating CSR as only a cost.
- Ending 10-mark answers without a judgement and a reason for it.
