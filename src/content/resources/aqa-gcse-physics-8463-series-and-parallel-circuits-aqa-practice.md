## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Give **two** differences between a series circuit and a parallel circuit. **[2]**

**2.** Resistors of 33 Ω, 27 Ω and 40 Ω are connected in series. Calculate the total resistance. **[1]**

**3.** Two lamps are in parallel across a 3.0 V cell. State the pd across each lamp. **[1]**

**4.** In a parallel circuit, the current from the cell is 0.90 A. One branch carries 0.65 A. Calculate the current in the other branch. **[1]**

**5.** A 5.0 Ω resistor and a 15 Ω resistor are joined in parallel. A student says their total resistance is 20 Ω. Explain why the student is wrong. **[2]**

**6.** A 4.5 V battery is connected to a 50 Ω resistor and a 100 Ω resistor in series. Calculate the current in the circuit. **[3]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** A 24 V supply is connected in series with three resistors: 20 Ω, 30 Ω and 50 Ω.

**(a)** Calculate the current. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the pd across each resistor. **[3]**
**(c)** Show that your answers to (b) are consistent with the supply pd. **[1]**

**8.** A lamp is connected in series with a 25 Ω resistor and a 9.0 V battery. A voltmeter across the resistor reads 5.0 V.

**(a)** Calculate the current through the lamp. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the resistance of the lamp. **[3]**

**9.** A 12 V supply is connected to three parallel branches with resistances of 6.0 Ω, 12 Ω and 24 Ω.

**(a)** Calculate the current in each branch. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the total current from the supply. **[1]**
**(c)** Use your answer to (b) to find the equivalent resistance of the network, and comment on its value. **[2]**

**10.** A 10 Ω resistor is in series with two identical lamps that are connected in parallel with each other. The supply is 6.0 V and the current from the supply is 0.40 A.

**(a)** Calculate the pd across the 10 Ω resistor. **[2]**
**(b)** State the pd across each lamp. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the current through one lamp and the resistance of one lamp. **[3]**

**11.** A light sensor uses an LDR in series with a 10 kΩ resistor and a 5.0 V supply. In daylight the LDR's resistance is 2.5 kΩ; at night it is 40 kΩ.

**(a)** Calculate the pd across the LDR in daylight. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the pd across the LDR at night. **[2]**
**(c)** A security light must switch on at night when its input pd rises above 3.0 V. State whether the input should be connected across the LDR or across the fixed resistor, and explain your choice. **[2]**

**12.** A student builds a circuit with two lamps in parallel. She then adds a third identical lamp in parallel.

**(a)** Explain what happens to the total resistance of the circuit. **[2]**
**(b)** State what happens to the current from the cell and to the brightness of the first two lamps. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two matched pairs, for example: in series the current is the same through each component, but in parallel the total current is the sum of the branch currents [1]; in series the supply pd is shared between components, but in parallel each branch has the same pd [1]. Also accept: in series the total resistance is the sum of the resistances, while in parallel it is less than the smallest resistance.

**2.** 33 + 27 + 40 = **100 Ω** [1]

**3.** **3.0 V** [1]

**4.** 0.90 − 0.65 = **0.25 A** [1]

**5.** Adding the values is the rule for series, not parallel [1]; in parallel the total resistance is less than the smallest individual resistance, so it must be below 5.0 Ω [1]

**6.** R_total = 50 + 100 = 150 Ω [1]; I = V ÷ R = 4.5 ÷ 150 [1]; I = **0.030 A** (30 mA) [1]

**7. (a)** R_total = 20 + 30 + 50 = 100 Ω [1]; I = 24 ÷ 100 [1]; I = **0.24 A** [1]
**(b)** 0.24 × 20 = **4.8 V** [1]; 0.24 × 30 = **7.2 V** [1]; 0.24 × 50 = **12 V** [1]
**(c)** 4.8 + 7.2 + 12 = 24 V, which equals the supply pd [1]
*Examiner insight:* An incorrect current in (a) carried correctly into (b) still earns the (b) marks.

**8. (a)** I = V ÷ R = 5.0 ÷ 25 [1]; I = **0.20 A** (the same through the lamp, as it is in series) [1]
**(b)** pd across lamp = 9.0 − 5.0 = 4.0 V [1]; R = 4.0 ÷ 0.20 [1]; R = **20 Ω** [1]

**9. (a)** 12 ÷ 6.0 = **2.0 A** [1]; 12 ÷ 12 = **1.0 A** [1]; 12 ÷ 24 = **0.50 A** [1]
**(b)** 2.0 + 1.0 + 0.50 = **3.5 A** [1]
**(c)** R = 12 ÷ 3.5 = **3.4 Ω** (2 s.f.) [1]; this is less than 6.0 Ω, the smallest branch resistance, as expected for a parallel network [1]

**10. (a)** V = I R = 0.40 × 10 [1]; V = **4.0 V** [1]
**(b)** 6.0 − 4.0 = **2.0 V** [1]
**(c)** Identical lamps share the current equally: 0.40 ÷ 2 = **0.20 A** [1]; R = 2.0 ÷ 0.20 [1]; R = **10 Ω** [1]

**11. (a)** R_total = 10 000 + 2500 = 12 500 Ω [1]; I = 5.0 ÷ 12 500 = 0.00040 A [1]; V_LDR = 0.00040 × 2500 = **1.0 V** [1]
**(b)** R_total = 50 000 Ω, so I = 5.0 ÷ 50 000 = 0.00010 A [1]; V_LDR = 0.00010 × 40 000 = **4.0 V** [1]
**(c)** Across the LDR [1]; its pd rises from 1.0 V to 4.0 V at night, passing 3.0 V, while the pd across the fixed resistor falls to 1.0 V [1]

**12. (a)** The total resistance decreases [1]; because the third lamp adds another path for the charge, so more current flows for the same pd [1]
**(b)** The current from the cell increases [1]; the first two lamps stay the same brightness, because each still has the full cell pd across it [1]
*Examiner insight:* The brightness mark assumes a cell whose pd does not change; the key physics is that each parallel branch has the same pd.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Applying the series addition rule to resistors in parallel.
- Using the supply pd instead of the component's own pd in V = IR.
- Forgetting that identical parallel lamps share the branch current equally.
- Converting kΩ incorrectly in sensor circuits.
- Choosing the output across the wrong component in a switching circuit.
