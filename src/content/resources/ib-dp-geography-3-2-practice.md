## Section B

**7.** The table shows invented stake measurements on a glacier, in metres of water equivalent.

| Stake | Winter accumulation | Summer ablation |
|---|---|---|
| A (highest) | 2.0 | 0.8 |
| B | 1.5 | 1.5 |
| C | 1.1 | 2.3 |
| D (lowest) | 0.6 | 3.3 |

**(a)** Calculate the net balance at each stake. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the mean net balance and state what it suggests about the glacier. **[2]**

**(c)** Identify the stake that lies on the equilibrium line, and explain how the line would move if summers became warmer. **[2]**

## Section C

**11.** Explain the formation of two landforms found in periglacial environments. **[8]**

**12.** "Present-day processes cannot fully explain the landscapes of extreme environments." Discuss this statement. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Melting of ice under pressure on the upstream side of an obstacle and refreezing on the downstream side, helping the glacier move over its bed [1]. **[1]**

**2.** Extending flow occurs where the bed steepens, so ice speeds up, thins and cracks into crevasses [1]; compressing flow occurs where the bed flattens, so ice slows and thickens, increasing erosion [1]. **[2]**

**3.** The direction in which the ice moved across the rock [1]. **[1]**

**4.** About **8 times** (2 cubed) [1]. **[1]**

**5.** Uneven collapse of the ground surface, forming hollows, hummocks and lakes, caused by the thawing of ice-rich permafrost [1]. **[1]**

**6.** The idea that different processes can produce similar landforms [1]. **[1]**

**7. (a)** A: +1.2; B: 0.0; C: -1.2; D: -2.7 [2] (all four correct for 2 marks; three correct for 1 mark).

**(b)** Mean = (1.2 + 0 - 1.2 - 2.7) / 4 = -2.7 / 4 = **-0.675 m** (accept -0.68) [1]. The glacier is losing mass overall, so it is thinning and its snout is likely to retreat [1].

**(c)** Stake B, where net balance is zero [1]. Warmer summers increase ablation, so the equilibrium line would move up the glacier, shrinking the accumulation zone [1]. **[6]**

**8.** As ice moves over a resistant rock outcrop, pressure on the upstream side causes some melting [1], and debris in the base of the ice abrades and smooths that side [1]. On the downstream side pressure falls, meltwater refreezes onto the rock and the ice plucks away loosened blocks [1], leaving a smooth, gently sloping upstream face and a steep, jagged downstream face [1]. **[4]**

**9.** In very cold winters, frozen ground contracts and cracks [1]. In spring, meltwater fills the cracks and freezes, forming thin veins of ice [1]. Each winter the crack reopens along the same line and more ice is added, so the ice grows into a wedge [1]. Cracks join into a network, outlining polygons on flat ground [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Rain, though rare, often falls in short, intense storms [1]. There is little vegetation or soil to intercept or absorb it, so runoff is rapid and high-energy [1], producing flash floods and sheet floods that carry large loads and erode wadis and mountain fronts [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Any two landforms, up to 4 marks each:

- **Pingo:** a lake drains or fills with sediment [1]; permafrost advances into the saturated ground beneath [1]; water is squeezed upward and freezes into an ice core [1]; the ground above is pushed up into a dome, which may later collapse into a crater if the core melts [1].
- **Patterned ground:** repeated freezing and thawing of the active layer [1]; frost heave lifts stones towards the surface [1]; larger stones move outward from finer, frost-susceptible material [1]; on flat ground they form circles or polygons, and on slopes they are drawn into stripes [1].
- **Solifluction lobe:** in summer the active layer thaws [1]; meltwater cannot drain through the frozen ground below, so the soil becomes saturated [1]; saturated soil flows slowly downhill [1]; it builds tongue-shaped lobes with steep fronts [1].
- **Ice-wedge polygons:** as in question 9. **[8]**

**12.** Indicative content:

- **For the statement:** many desert landforms, such as large wadis and lake basins, were shaped under wetter past climates [1]; glacial troughs and corries in areas without glaciers today were formed during past glaciations [1]; erratics, drumlins and striations record ice sheets that have melted [1].
- **Against:** many features are actively forming now: dunes, ventifacts and alluvial fans [1]; freeze-thaw, solifluction and patterned ground operate today in periglacial zones [1]; glaciers in high mountains still erode and deposit [1].
- **Complications:** equifinality means similar landforms may have different origins, so it can be hard to know whether a feature is inherited or active [1]; climate change is now creating new processes and landforms, such as thermokarst [1].
- **Examination:** most landscapes are palimpsests, with present processes modifying older forms [1].
- **Conclusion:** the statement is largely true for large-scale relief, which is often inherited, but less true for small-scale features, which reflect present processes [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* Scale is a useful way to structure a judgement: large landforms take a long time to form, so they are more likely to be inherited.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Reading net balance signs the wrong way round.
- Drawing or describing a roche moutonnee with the plucked face up-ice.
- Treating pingos and ice wedges as glacial rather than periglacial.
- Ignoring past climates when explaining desert landscapes.
