## Questions

**1.** Define the term globalisation and identify **one** way a UK business could compete internationally. **[3]**

**2.** Explain **one** benefit and **one** drawback of globalisation for a UK business that makes furniture. **[4]**

**3.** Halsewick Lighting is a UK business that makes designer lamps and sells most of them to shops in other countries. The pound becomes stronger. Analyse how this could affect Halsewick's sales and profit. **[4]**

**4.** Aldercombe Foods imports its spices. The yearly import bill is £180,000 and operating profit is £54,000. A weaker pound raises the import bill by 8%. Prices and volumes stay the same. Calculate the extra cost, the new operating profit and the percentage fall in operating profit (to 1 decimal place). **[4]**

**5.** Treverton Bakehouse has 11 part-time staff, each working 16 hours a week for 52 weeks a year, all paid the legal minimum. The legal minimum rises by 45p an hour. **(a)** Calculate the extra yearly wage cost. **[3]** **(b)** Explain **one** way the owner could respond. **[2]**

**6.** Explain **two** ways the Equality Act (2010) could affect how Treverton Bakehouse recruits staff. **[4]**

**7.** Analyse the benefits to Davenmoor Pallets, a warehouse business, of providing a safe working environment under the Health and Safety at Work Act (1974). **[6]**

**8.** Thornsett Home Goods sold a set of towels as "100% organic cotton" in its adverts. A test shows that they are a cotton and polyester mix. Explain **two** consequences for the business of failing to follow consumer law on trade descriptions. **[4]**

**9.** Orrinshaw Cycles is a UK business that makes bicycles. It sells 1,500 bikes a year abroad at £420 each and buys gears from overseas for £150,000 a year. The pound is expected to strengthen. Overseas sales are expected to fall by 10%, while the gear bill is expected to fall by 6%. A competitor abroad offers cheaper bikes of similar design. Evaluate whether Orrinshaw should keep selling abroad. **[9]**

**10.** Treverton Bakehouse says that employment law, health and safety law and consumer law cost it too much. Evaluate whether the costs of legislation outweigh the benefits for a small business. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Globalisation is the growing connection of the world's economies and markets [1], so goods, services and money cross borders more easily [1]. One way to compete: offering better designs, higher quality products or lower prices [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition earns more credit when it says what is connected and what moves. A bare "selling abroad" is too narrow.

**2.** Benefit: access to a bigger market through exports, which can raise sales [1]. This could let the firm make more and spread its costs over more units [1]. Drawback: overseas furniture makers can sell in the UK and take customers [1]. This could force the firm to cut prices or lose sales [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each "explain" point usually needs a reason attached to it. Naming a benefit and a drawback with no consequence leaves marks unclaimed.

**3.** A stronger pound makes UK lamps dearer for overseas buyers [1]. Halsewick's sales may fall because shops buy from rivals [1]. If it cuts prices to keep sales, each lamp earns less profit [1]. As most sales are abroad, profit is likely to fall [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the detail in the stem. Saying that most sales are abroad lets you reach a conclusion about this business rather than any exporter.

**4.** Extra cost = 8% of £180,000 = **£14,400** [1]. New operating profit = £54,000 - £14,400 = **£39,600** [1]. Fall = £14,400 / £54,000 = 0.2667 [1]. Percentage fall = **26.7%** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Show each stage. Work is easiest to follow when you state the base figure for the percentage, here the original profit.

**5.** **(a)** Hours = 11 x 16 x 52 = 9,152 [1]. Extra cost = 9,152 x £0.45 [1] = **£4,118.40** a year [1]. **(b)** The owner could raise prices [1], which covers the cost but may reduce customers [1]. Accept other responses with a linked effect, such as cutting hours. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* A response in (b) needs its consequence. The idea alone is not developed enough.

**6.** The Act makes unfair treatment because of characteristics such as age, sex, race, disability or religion unlawful [1]. Way 1: the business must choose staff on skills and experience, so it may need clear selection criteria [1]. Way 2: managers may need training in fair interviewing [1], which costs time and money [1]. Accept a workplace adaptation for a disabled applicant. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The specification asks for the effect on the business, so link the law to recruitment and training and not just describe it.

**7.** Fewer accidents mean less absence, so the firm pays less for cover and lost output [1][1]. Staff who feel safe and valued are more motivated and productive [1][1]. A good safety record improves reputation, which helps recruit staff [1]. The firm avoids compensation claims and legal costs [1]. Maximum 6. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* In an "analyse" answer, each benefit scores best when followed by a consequence for the business, not left as a list.

**8.** Consequence 1: the firm may have to refund customers or pay compensation [1], which cuts profit [1]. Consequence 2: customers lose trust in the brand [1], so future sales may fall [1]. Accept legal action or penalties with a linked effect. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming a consequence earns less than showing what it does to the business, so extend each point once.

**9.** Revenue lost: 10% of 1,500 = 150 bikes, and 150 x £420 = £63,000 [1]. Gear saving: 6% of £150,000 = £9,000 [1]. The saving is much smaller than the lost revenue, so exports look weaker, though some production costs also fall with fewer bikes [1]. For keeping: overseas sales are still 1,350 bikes, worth £567,000 [1]. Ending exports would lose that income, and the UK market may not replace it [1]. Design quality could support the price [1]. Against: a cheaper rival abroad adds pressure [1]. Judgement: keep selling abroad, but seek a lower-cost source of gears and review the price [1], since the exports still bring in £567,000 and the gear saving is small [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* An evaluation needs both sides and a decision. Making the judgement depend on the figures in the case stands up better than a general opinion.

**10.** Costs: higher wage bill, training time, equipment, and checks on products [1][1]. These are a larger share of a small firm's budget [1]. Benefits: fair recruitment, a safer workplace and honest selling improve morale, reputation and customer trust [1][1]. Compliance also avoids penalties, compensation and lost sales [1], which could close a small firm [1]. The balance depends on the size of the cost and the likelihood of failure [1]. Judgement: the benefits outweigh the costs over time, because the cost of breaking the law is larger and less certain than the cost of following it [1]. Accept a different reasoned conclusion, for example for a very low-margin firm. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Set costs against benefits directly instead of writing two separate lists. A final judgement that refers back to the firm's size is stronger.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing that a stronger pound is always good or always bad.
- Converting currencies, which the specification says you will not be asked to do.
- Naming a law but leaving out its effect on the business.
- Ignoring the figures or details given in the case study.
- Giving costs of legislation with no benefits, or the reverse.
- Writing a list for an evaluate question with no judgement.
- Leaving out the base figure when calculating a percentage change.
- Confusing importers with exporters.
