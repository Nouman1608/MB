This guide covers quantitative skill **QS8** in section 3.5 of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Economics (9640) specification: "make calculations of elasticity and interpret the result". The elasticities themselves (price, income and cross elasticity of demand, and price elasticity of supply) are taught in the AS content on how markets work, but QS8 can be tested in any unit, for example when a firm chooses a price or a government chooses a tax. Every firm and figure below is invented.

Calculating an elasticity from two data points, and the link between PED and total revenue, are covered in the broader [cost, revenue, profit and elasticity guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-economics-cost-revenue-and-profit-calculations-and-elasticity/) and the [demand and elasticities guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-economics-demand-and-the-elasticities-of-demand/). This page works the other way round: using a known elasticity to forecast, finding the price change needed to hit a target, and interpreting results when the data are messy. ## The two halves of QS8

| Half | What you do | Typical wording |
|---|---|---|
| Calculate | Find an elasticity from data, or use a given elasticity to find a percentage change | "Calculate the price elasticity of demand"; "Use the data to estimate the change in sales" |
| Interpret | Say what the sign and size mean for buyers, sellers or government, and how reliable the figure is | "Explain what the value shows"; "Assess whether the firm should raise its price" |

Half the marks often go on interpretation, so a correct number with no comment rarely earns everything available.

## Rearranging the formula

Every elasticity is a ratio of two percentage changes. If you know any two of the three terms, you can find the third.

```
elasticity = %change in quantity / %change in cause
%change in quantity = elasticity x %change in cause
%change in cause    = %change in quantity / elasticity
```

The "cause" is the good's own price for PED and PES, income for YED, and the price of the other good for XED. Keep the sign of the elasticity throughout: it carries the direction of the change.

## Worked example 1: forecasting sales and revenue with PED

A bus operator carries 20,000 passengers a week at a fare of 2.40. It estimates PED at -1.4 and is considering a 15% fare cut.

1. %change in passengers = -1.4 x -15 = **+21%**, so passengers rise to 24,200.
2. New fare = 2.40 x 0.85 = 2.04.
3. Revenue before = 2.40 x 20,000 = 48,000; after = 2.04 x 24,200 = 49,368.
4. Revenue rises by 2.85%. A shortcut gives the same result: 0.85 x 1.21 = 1.0285.

**Interpretation.** Demand is price elastic, so the cut raises revenue. But the gain is small, and 4,200 more passengers may need extra buses, so profit could fall. The forecast also assumes the PED estimate still holds at the new fare and that rival operators do not respond.

## Worked example 2: the price change needed to reach a target

A city council wants to cut car-park use by 12% to reduce congestion. It estimates PED for parking at -0.4.

%change in price = -12 / -0.4 = **+30%**.

**Interpretation.** Because demand is inelastic, a large price rise is needed for a modest fall in use, and the council's parking revenue rises sharply (1.30 x 0.88 = 1.144, an increase of 14.4%). This is the same reasoning that explains why taxes on goods with inelastic demand raise revenue but change behaviour little. If drivers have few alternatives, such as poor public transport, PED may be even lower than estimated, and the target will be missed.

## Worked example 3: forecasting with YED

Average incomes in Country G are forecast to rise by 4% next year. A retailer has these estimates:

| Product | YED | Forecast change in demand | Type of good |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gym membership | +2.2 | 2.2 x 4 = +8.8% | Normal, income elastic |
| Bread | +0.3 | 0.3 x 4 = +1.2% | Normal, income inelastic |
| Long-distance coach travel | -0.5 | -0.5 x 4 = -2.0% | Inferior |

**Interpretation.** In a period of rising incomes, sellers of income-elastic goods expect fast growth and may need to expand capacity; sellers of inferior goods expect falling demand. In a recession the pattern reverses. A YED estimate is an average over past years and may change as tastes change.

## Worked example 4: rivals and complements with XED

A café estimates XED between its coffee and the coffee of a chain across the road at +1.6. The chain cuts its price by 10%. The café's sales change by 1.6 x -10 = **-16%**. The positive sign shows close substitutes, and the size shows the café is very exposed to its rival's pricing.

A games console maker cuts the console price by 20%. XED between games and the console price is -0.75, so demand for games rises by -0.75 x -20 = **+15%**. The negative sign shows complements. This is why a console maker may accept a low price on the console itself.

## Worked example 5: PES over time

The world price of avocados rises by 25%. Growers' short-run PES is 0.2, so quantity supplied rises by only 0.2 x 25 = 5%: new trees take years to bear fruit. Their long-run PES is 1.6, so once new orchards mature quantity supplied rises by 40%, which in turn tends to push the price back down. Always state the time period when you interpret a PES value.

## Interpreting results when other things change

Elasticity is measured holding other things equal, but real data rarely do. Suppose the price of a snack rises by 10% and sales rise by 5%. That does not show a positive PED. If incomes rose by 6% in the same period and YED is +1.5, income alone would have raised sales by about 9%. The price rise therefore cut sales by roughly 9 - 5 = 4 percentage points relative to what would otherwise have happened, which suggests a PED of roughly -0.4. Treat this as a rough estimate: adding percentage effects in this way is only an approximation. The general lesson is that an elasticity calculated from two observations reflects every change between them, not only the one in the formula.

## Reading the sign and size

| Elasticity | Sign | Size |
|---|---|---|
| PED | Negative for a normal demand curve | Above 1 (ignoring sign): elastic; below 1: inelastic |
| YED | Positive: normal good; negative: inferior good | Above 1: income elastic |
| XED | Positive: substitutes; negative: complements | Larger value: closer relationship |
| PES | Positive for a normal supply curve | Above 1: elastic; below 1: inelastic |

## Common mistakes

- Multiplying when you should divide in a rearranged formula, or the other way round.
- Losing a minus sign, so a price cut is forecast to reduce sales.
- Calling a YED of +0.3 "inferior" because it is small; only a negative YED shows an inferior good.
- Assuming a revenue rise means a profit rise.
- Treating an elasticity estimate as exact or permanent.
- Calculating PED from data in which income or rivals' prices also changed, without comment.
- Forgetting to state the time period when interpreting PES.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate:** write the rearranged formula, substitute with signs, and give the answer as a percentage with its direction.
- **Interpret:** answer three questions in turn: what does the sign show, what does the size show, and so what for the firm or government in the question?
- **Evaluate:** question the estimate. Was it measured over a small price change? Does it apply to this market now? Will rivals react? What happens in the long run?

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I use an elasticity to forecast a large price change?**
You can, but the forecast is less reliable, because elasticity usually changes along a demand curve. Say so in your answer.

**What does a PED of zero mean?**
Quantity demanded does not change at all when price changes: demand is perfectly inelastic.

**Why do I need the time period for PES?**
Supply is usually more elastic in the long run, when firms can add capacity, so the same price rise brings a larger response over time.
