## The Doppler equations and their signs

For a source moving along the line of sight at speed v much less than c:

```
Δf/f = v/c          z = Δλ/λ = −v/c
```

In these equations **v is positive when the source approaches**. An approaching source has its frequency raised (Δf positive) and wavelength shortened (Δλ negative, a **blue shift**). A receding source has negative v, so z is positive: a **red shift**. In practice you calculate the size of the shift and state the direction in words: recession speed = zc.

The same equations apply at radio frequencies, where astronomers usually work in frequency.

**Worked example 1: a radio line.** A radio emission line has a rest frequency of 1420 MHz. In a galaxy it is received at 1415 MHz.

```
Δf/f = (1415 − 1420)/1420 = −3.5 × 10⁻³
v = cΔf/f = −1.1 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹  →  receding at 1.1 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹
```

Galaxies near us can show blue shifts because their own motion through space outweighs the expansion; on large scales almost all galaxies are red-shifted.

## Binary stars in the plane of orbit

When we view a binary system edge-on, each star moves alternately towards and away from us. Its spectral lines oscillate about the rest wavelength with the orbital period. If both stars are bright, each line splits into two components that move apart and back together: one star is approaching while the other recedes, and the lines merge when both move across our line of sight.

From the data: the **maximum shift** gives the orbital speed (v = cΔλ_max/λ); the **time for one cycle** of the shift gives the period T; for a circular orbit **r = vT/2π**.

## Hubble's law and the age of the Universe

Plotting recession speed against distance for galaxies gives a straight line through the origin: **v = Hd**. The simple interpretation is that the Universe is expanding: the further apart two galaxies are, the faster they separate.

**Worked example 2: H from data.** Four galaxies (invented data) have distances 50, 120, 200 and 300 Mpc and recession speeds 3400, 8300, 13 900 and 20 600 km s⁻¹. A best-fit line through the origin has gradient H ≈ 69 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹.

If H has always had the same value, everything was together a time 1/H ago:

```
H = 69 × 10³ m s⁻¹/3.08 × 10²² m = 2.24 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹
age ≈ 1/H = 4.46 × 10¹⁷ s = 1.4 × 10¹⁰ years
```

The assumption that H is constant is the weak point: if expansion has changed rate, 1/H is only an estimate.

## The Big Bang (qualitative)

The Big Bang theory states that the Universe began in an extremely hot, dense state and has been expanding and cooling ever since. Two pieces of evidence are required:

- **Cosmological microwave background radiation**: black-body radiation at about 2.7 K reaching us almost uniformly from every direction. It is radiation from the hot early Universe, stretched to microwave wavelengths by expansion. By Wien's law its peak is at 2.9 × 10⁻³/2.7 ≈ 1.1 × 10⁻³ m, in the microwave region.
- **Relative abundance of hydrogen and helium**: in the early Universe, fusion could only take place for a short time before expansion cooled it too much. Theory predicts roughly three parts hydrogen to one part helium by mass, which matches observation.

## Quasars

Quasars were **discovered as bright radio sources**. They show **very large optical red shifts**, so by Hubble's law they are extremely distant; they are the most distant measurable objects. Because they are still detectable at such distances, their power output must be enormous. The accepted explanation is that they form from **active supermassive black holes**: matter falling towards the black hole radiates intensely.

**Worked example 3: quasar power.** A quasar has z = 0.15 and the intensity at Earth is 1.5 × 10⁻¹³ W m⁻². Take H = 70 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹.

```
v = zc = 4.5 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹ = 4.5 × 10⁴ km s⁻¹
d = v/H = 4.5 × 10⁴/70 = 643 Mpc = 1.98 × 10²⁵ m
P = 4πd²I = 4π × (1.98 × 10²⁵)² × 1.5 × 10⁻¹³ = 7.4 × 10³⁸ W
P/P_sun ≈ 1.9 × 10¹²
```

The equation z = −v/c is valid only for v ≪ c; at z = 0.15 it is still a reasonable estimate, but for the largest red shifts it is not.

## Exoplanets

**Direct detection is difficult** because a planet only reflects light and is extremely faint compared with its star, and because the angular separation between planet and star is tiny, so the planet is lost in the star's glare.

**Radial velocity method.** Star and planet orbit their common centre of mass, so the star moves slightly towards and away from us. Its spectral lines show a small periodic Doppler shift with the planet's orbital period.

**Worked example 4.** A line at 656.3 nm shifts by ±4.4 × 10⁻⁴ nm. The star's speed is v = 3.00 × 10⁸ × 4.4 × 10⁻⁴/656.3 = 2.0 × 10² m s⁻¹. Shifts this small need very stable, high-resolution spectrometers.

**Transit method.** When the planet crosses in front of the star, it blocks a fraction of the star's disc. The **typical light curve** is flat, falls to a lower flat level while the whole planet is in front of the disc, rises back to the original level, and repeats once per orbit. The fractional dip is about (r_planet/r_star)², so a 1.0% dip for a star of radius 6.96 × 10⁸ m gives r_planet ≈ 6.96 × 10⁸ × √0.010 = 7.0 × 10⁷ m. The orbit must be nearly edge-on for a transit to be seen.

## Common mistakes

- Forgetting the minus sign convention, or saying a receding galaxy has a negative z.
- Mixing km s⁻¹ with m s⁻¹ in v = Hd.
- Leaving H in km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹ when calculating 1/H.
- Describing the microwave background as "left-over heat" without saying it is black-body radiation stretched by expansion.
- Forgetting to square-root the transit dip.
- Saying exoplanets are hard to see "because they are far away" without the faintness and angular separation points.

## Exam technique for AQA command words

- **Calculate** (Doppler): compute the size of the shift, then state "towards" or "away" from its sign.
- **Explain** (Big Bang evidence): name the evidence and say how the Big Bang accounts for it.
- **Describe** (detection method): name what is measured, what changes and why.
- **Sketch** (transit light curve): flat, dip with flat bottom, recovery, repetition, axes labelled intensity and time.
