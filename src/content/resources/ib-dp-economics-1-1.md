This guide goes deeper on one sub-topic of the IB Diploma Programme *Economics guide* (first assessment 2022): **1.1 What is economics?**, the first half of Unit 1, Introduction to economics. It is SL and HL content. The wider [guide to Unit 1](/resources/ib-dp-economics-why-economics-and-how-economists-think/) introduces every idea in the unit; this page adds drawing technique, harder calculations and the reasoning chains examiners reward. Consolidate with the [1.1 revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-economics-1-1-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [1.1 practice questions](/resources/ib-dp-economics-1-1-practice/).

All countries, firms and figures below are invented. The main example is Marnova, whose currency is the marn.

## What 1.1 covers

Sub-topic 1.1 has four strands:

| Strand | What you should be able to do |
|---|---|
| Economics as a social science | Explain why economics studies human behaviour, separate microeconomics from macroeconomics, and use the central concepts |
| The problem of choice | Explain scarcity, the factors of production, opportunity cost, free and economic goods, the basic economic questions and how economic systems answer them |
| The production possibilities curve (PPC) | Draw and explain the model, including efficiency, unemployment, opportunity cost and growth |
| The circular flow of income | Draw and explain the model with leakages and injections |

The two diagrams for this sub-topic are the PPC and the circular flow. Both reappear across the course, so precision here pays off later.

## Scarcity: the starting point

**Scarcity** is the gap between unlimited wants and limited resources. It does not mean poverty: a rich country still cannot have everything at once, because its labour, land and capital are finite. Scarcity forces **choice**, and every choice has an **opportunity cost**.

### Classifying factors of production correctly

Exam questions often test whether you can classify a resource in context. The test is what the resource does in production, not what it is called.

| Resource | Factor | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A fishing ground in Marnova's coastal waters | Land | A natural resource, not made by people |
| The skills of a trained nurse | Labour | Human effort, including skill gained through education (human capital) |
| A delivery van owned by a bakery | Capital | A made good used to produce other goods and services |
| The same model of van owned by a family for weekend trips | Not a factor of production | It is a consumer good, used for final enjoyment |
| A founder who raises funds and takes the risk of launching a firm | Entrepreneurship | Organises the other factors and bears risk |
| Money in a firm's bank account | Not capital in this sense | Money buys capital goods but produces nothing itself |

Each factor earns a payment: land earns **rent**, labour earns **wages**, capital earns **interest** and entrepreneurship earns **profit**. These payments are the income flow in the circular flow model below.

### Free goods and economic goods

A **free good** has zero opportunity cost: using it leaves no less for anyone else. An **economic good** uses scarce resources. The line between them can move. Clean air in a remote area is close to a free good; in a city where pollution limits are tight, clean air becomes scarce and every extra unit of pollution allowed has an opportunity cost in health. That shift is a useful link to sustainability.

## Opportunity cost for each decision-maker

Opportunity cost applies to consumers, firms and governments. It is the **single next best alternative** forgone, measured in money or in goods.

**Worked example: a government choice.** Marnova's government has 300 million marns of capital spending left this year. It ranks three projects: a hospital wing first, a teacher-training college second and a coastal road third. It funds the hospital wing.

- The opportunity cost is the college, the second-ranked option.
- It is not "the college and the road": the government could only ever have funded one.
- If the ranking changed, so that the road became second, the opportunity cost would be the road even though the money spent is the same. Opportunity cost depends on the decision-maker's ranking of alternatives, not on the price.

## The production possibilities curve in depth

### Building the curve from data

Marnova can produce solar panels (thousands) and rice (thousand tonnes). With all resources fully and efficiently employed, the maximum combinations are:

| Point | Solar panels | Rice | Rice given up per extra 1,000 panels |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 0 | 200 | |
| B | 20 | 190 | (200 - 190) / 20 = 0.50 |
| C | 40 | 165 | (190 - 165) / 20 = 1.25 |
| D | 60 | 125 | (165 - 125) / 20 = 2.00 |
| E | 80 | 70 | (125 - 70) / 20 = 2.75 |
| F | 100 | 0 | (70 - 0) / 20 = 3.50 |

The opportunity cost of panels rises at every step, so the curve is **concave to the origin** (bowed outwards). The reason is that resources are specialised: the first workers and land moved out of rice are those least suited to farming, while the last are expert farmers on the best paddy land.

Reading the model:

- **Moving from C to E** gains 40,000 panels and loses 165 - 70 = 95 thousand tonnes of rice, an average of 95 / 40 = 2.375 thousand tonnes per thousand panels.
- **Point (60, 100)** lies inside the curve, because point D shows 125 is possible with 60 panels. Marnova is wasting 125 - 100 = 25 thousand tonnes of rice, through unemployment or inefficient use of resources.
- **Point (40, 180)** lies outside the curve, because point C shows only 165 is possible. It is unattainable with current resources and technology.

### Drawing it well

1. Label both axes with the good and its units.
2. Draw the curve meeting both axes; do not leave it floating.
3. Mark at least one point on the curve, one inside and one outside, each with a letter.
4. Use arrows for any movement or shift, and label the new curve PPC2.

### Shifts, movements and pivots

| Change | Effect on the diagram | Example in Marnova |
|---|---|---|
| Unemployed workers find jobs | Movement from inside towards the curve (actual growth) | Recovery after a slump |
| More or better resources, or better technology in both industries | Outward shift of the whole curve | Wider school enrolment raises skills everywhere |
| Better technology in one industry only | Pivot outwards along that axis | Cheaper photovoltaic cells raise only maximum panel output |
| Loss of resources | Inward shift | A cyclone destroys farmland and factories |

### Choosing between capital goods and consumer goods

A PPC with **capital goods** on one axis and **consumer goods** on the other shows a choice about the future. Suppose two invented economies, Marnova and Ostrel, start on identical curves. Marnova chooses a point with more capital goods and fewer consumer goods today; Ostrel chooses the opposite. Marnova adds more machinery and infrastructure, so its curve shifts out further in later years. The opportunity cost of that faster growth is lower consumption now. This is a good way to show that the PPC links scarcity, choice and opportunity cost to economic growth and to sustainability.

## The circular flow in depth

The model shows two kinds of flow running in opposite directions: **real flows** (factors of production and goods and services) and **money flows** (factor incomes and spending). In the simplest version, households sell factor services to firms for rent, wages, interest and profit, and spend that income on firms' output.

Three sectors add a leakage and an injection each:

| Sector | Leakage | Injection |
|---|---|---|
| Financial sector | Saving (S) | Investment (I) |
| Government | Taxes (T) | Government spending (G) |
| Foreign sector | Imports (M) | Exports (X) |

**Worked example.** Marnova's annual figures, in billions of marns, are S = 45, T = 120, M = 85, I = 60, G = 110 and X = 95.

```
Leakages   = 45 + 120 + 85 = 250
Injections = 60 + 110 + 95 = 265
Injections exceed leakages by 15, so the flow of income tends to grow.
```

If the government then cuts spending to 85, injections fall to 60 + 85 + 95 = 240, which is 10 below leakages, so the flow of income tends to shrink. Note that S does not have to equal I, nor T equal G: only the totals of leakages and injections matter for whether income is stable.

## Common mistakes

- Calling money "capital". In economics, capital means made goods used in production.
- Adding up every alternative to get opportunity cost.
- Drawing a straight PPC and then claiming increasing opportunity cost.
- Showing actual growth as a shift of the curve. It is a movement from inside towards the curve.
- Mixing real and money flows on the circular flow, or drawing arrows in only one direction.

## Exam technique

- **Define** questions need a precise one-sentence meaning. "Opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative forgone when a choice is made" earns full credit; "what you give up" does not.
- **Explain** questions need a chain of reasoning: cause, mechanism, effect. With a PPC question, refer to the labelled points as you go ("the move from C to E…").
- **Draw** means an accurate, fully labelled diagram. Paper 1 answers that use a diagram should integrate it into the written explanation.
- **Calculate** questions need working shown and units stated, for example "2.375 thousand tonnes of rice per thousand panels".
- For **discuss** or **evaluate** questions that start from 1.1 ideas, such as whether a free market answers the basic economic questions well, weigh both sides and reach a judgement that depends on stated conditions.
