This guide covers section **3.1.1.2 Types of business ownership** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Business specification (9725), Version 1.1, for International AS exams May/June 2027 onwards. It is International AS content in section 3.1 Business and markets, assessed in Unit 1. Students completing the older 9625 specification should revise from that document, because 9725 changes the list of ownership forms: it includes **partnerships** and does not list co-operatives.

The specification's content falls under three headings:

- **Forms of business**: the meaning of primary, secondary and tertiary sectors; reasons for choosing and changing forms; advantages and disadvantages of sole traders, partnerships, private limited companies (Ltd), public limited companies (plc) and social enterprises; the meaning and significance of being a private sector or public sector organisation; issues when comparing forms (control, objectives, sources of finance, distribution of profits, unlimited and limited liability); advantages and disadvantages of operating as a family business and as a small business.
- **Shareholders**: reasons to be a shareholder; the meaning of share capital (equity); influences on share price and the significance of share price changes; the meaning and significance of dividends, dividends per share, dividend yield and market capitalisation, including calculation.
- **Business and stakeholders**: internal stakeholders (owners/shareholders, employees); external stakeholders (customers, suppliers, creditors, community, government); the impact of business activity on stakeholders.

## Primary, secondary and tertiary sectors

- **Primary sector**: extracting or growing natural resources, such as farming, fishing, mining and forestry.
- **Secondary sector**: turning materials into goods, such as manufacturing, processing and construction.
- **Tertiary sector**: providing services, such as retail, banking, transport, tourism and education.

A single business can operate in more than one sector: a tea company that grows, processes and sells its tea works in all three. In many economies the share of output and employment in the tertiary sector rises as incomes grow.

## Private sector and public sector

**Private sector** organisations are owned by individuals or shareholders, and most aim to make a profit. **Public sector** organisations are owned or controlled by government and usually aim to provide a service, such as healthcare, education or roads, often free or below cost.

The distinction matters because it shapes objectives, finance and accountability. A public sector hospital is funded mainly by taxes and judged on care and value for money; a private hospital must cover its costs and reward its owners. A public limited company is in the **private** sector, despite the word "public" in its name.

## Comparing the forms of business

| Form | Control | Liability | Main finance | Profits go to |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sole trader | The owner alone | Unlimited | Owner's savings, loans, retained profit | The owner |
| Partnership | Shared between partners | Usually unlimited | Partners' capital, loans | Partners, as agreed between them |
| Private limited company | Shareholders, often a small group; directors run it | Limited | Private sale of shares, loans | Shareholders, as dividends |
| Public limited company | Many shareholders; directors run it | Limited | Shares offered to the public, loans | Shareholders, as dividends |
| Social enterprise | Depends on legal form; the social aim leads | Usually limited if a company | Trading, grants, social investors | Mainly reinvested in the social aim |

### Advantages and disadvantages in brief

- **Sole trader.** Easy and cheap to set up, full control, all profit kept, privacy. But unlimited liability, limited finance, long hours and the business depends on one person.
- **Partnership.** More capital and a wider range of skills than a sole trader, shared workload and decisions. But partners are usually jointly responsible for debts with unlimited liability, disagreements can be damaging, and profits must be shared. A written partnership agreement setting out capital, profit shares and how disputes are settled reduces the risk.
- **Ltd.** Limited liability, more finance from selling shares, continuity if an owner leaves. But accounts must be published, setting up costs more, and shares cannot be offered to the public.
- **plc.** Can raise large sums by selling shares to the public; shares are easy to buy and sell; high profile. But flotation is expensive, the original owners may lose control, the company can be taken over, and investors may press for short-term results.
- **Social enterprise.** Its social aim attracts customers, staff and grants. But investors seeking high returns may avoid it, and balancing social and financial aims can be difficult.

### Reasons for choosing and changing form

The choice weighs the **issues** the specification lists: how much **control** the owners want to keep, their **objectives**, the **finance** needed, how **profits** will be shared, and the **liability** risk. A business changes form when these change: a sole trader takes on a partner to gain skills and capital; a partnership becomes a Ltd to obtain limited liability as debts grow; a Ltd becomes a plc to fund major expansion.

## Family businesses and small businesses

### Family business

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Strong commitment and trust between owners | Family disagreements can spill into business decisions |
| Long-term view, not driven by short-term returns | Jobs may go to relatives rather than the best candidates |
| Shared values and a clear identity | Succession problems when the founder retires |
| Can make decisions quickly | May resist outside finance to keep control, limiting growth |

**Worked example: Okonjo & Sons Ltd.** A furniture maker owned by a founder and his three children. The founder wants to retire. Two children want to expand into online sales; the third wants to keep the business small. An outside investor offers capital for 40% of the shares. The family's long-term commitment is a strength, but the disagreement over objectives and the question of who succeeds the founder are typical family business problems. Selling 40% would fund growth but bring an outsider into decisions, so the family must decide how much control it values compared with the chance to grow.

### Small business

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Close contact with customers; personal service | Higher unit costs: fewer economies of scale |
| Flexible and quick to adapt | Harder to raise finance |
| Low overheads | Vulnerable if one customer or one person is lost |
| Can serve niche markets large firms ignore | Limited specialist skills |

## Shareholders

**Share capital (equity)** is the money invested in a company by its shareholders in return for shares. It does not have to be repaid and carries no fixed interest.

**Reasons to be a shareholder:** to receive dividends; to gain if the share price rises; to have a vote; to support a business whose aims the investor shares.

**Influences on share price:** results and forecasts; dividend announcements; takeover bids or rumours; news about the industry; interest rates and the wider economy; investor confidence. **Significance of changes:** a rising price increases shareholders' wealth and makes it easier to raise new capital; a falling price can make a takeover cheaper, put pressure on directors and worry lenders. Trading in existing shares does not change the company's own cash.

### The calculations

```
Market capitalisation = number of shares issued x current share price
Dividend per share    = total dividends paid / number of shares issued
Dividend yield (%)    = dividend per share / current share price x 100
```

**Worked example.** Orbis Telecom plc has 60 million shares at £1.45 and pays total dividends of £4.2 million.

- Market capitalisation = 60m × £1.45 = **£87 million**.
- Dividend per share = £4.2m ÷ 60m = **7p**.
- Dividend yield = 7p ÷ 145p × 100 = **4.8%**.

If the share price falls to £1.20 with the same dividend, the yield rises to 7 ÷ 120 × 100 = **5.8%** and market capitalisation falls to **£72 million**. The higher yield reflects the lower price, not a bigger payout. An investor would ask why the price fell and whether the dividend is safe.

## Stakeholders

| Internal | External |
|---|---|
| Owners/shareholders | Customers |
| Employees | Suppliers |
| | Creditors |
| | Community |
| | Government |

**Impact of business activity.** A business decision usually helps some stakeholders and harms others. For example, if a mine (primary sector) expands, shareholders may gain higher profit, employees gain jobs and overtime, the government gains tax revenue and suppliers gain orders, but the local community may suffer dust, noise and heavier traffic, and creditors take on more risk if the expansion is financed by loans. An analysis answer identifies the groups most affected, explains why, and considers how the business might reduce the conflict.

## Exam technique

- **Short answers**: precise meanings, for example "dividend yield is dividend per share as a percentage of the share price".
- **Calculations**: write the formula, convert pounds and pence consistently, and give the unit.
- **Extended responses**: compare forms using the specification's issues, apply them to the business in the case and reach a judgement.

## Common mistakes

- Placing a plc in the public sector.
- Forgetting that partners usually have unlimited liability.
- Mixing pounds and pence in dividend yield.
- Treating a family business as automatically a small business, or the reverse.
- Listing stakeholders without explaining the impact on them.
