Every question below targets **1.2.2 Use of apparatus and techniques** in OCR A Level Chemistry A (H432), Version 3.1 (May 2026). The questions ask the things written papers ask about practical work: which apparatus to choose, why a step matters, and how a fault changes the result. All data are invented for these questions.

## Questions

**1.** A student records a titre of 23.40 cm³ from a burette graduated every 0.10 cm³.

**(a)** State the uncertainty in the titre. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage uncertainty in the titre. **[1]**

**2.** 0.250 g of a solid is weighed by difference on a two-decimal-place balance.

**(a)** Describe how a mass is measured "by difference". **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage uncertainty in the mass. **[1]**

**(c)** Suggest one change that would reduce this percentage uncertainty, and give the new value. **[1]**

**3.** In an enthalpy experiment the temperature rises from 21.0 °C to 25.5 °C. The thermometer is graduated every 1 °C.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage uncertainty in the temperature change. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest two ways to reduce this percentage uncertainty. **[2]**

**4.** Choose a water bath, an electric heater or a sand bath for each task and give a reason.

**(a)** Warming a test-tube of Tollens' reagent with an unknown organic compound. **[1]**

**(b)** Heating a reaction mixture of ethanol and acid under reflux for one hour. **[1]**

**(c)** Holding a solid mixture evenly at about 150 °C. **[1]**

**5.** A student uses a pH meter to measure the pH of several acid solutions.

**(a)** Describe how the meter should be calibrated. **[2]**

**(b)** State why the probe is rinsed with distilled water between solutions. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why a pH probe on a data logger is better than a pH chart for plotting a pH curve during a titration. **[1]**

**6.** A student finds the concentration of a sodium hydroxide solution. 25.0 cm³ of the sodium hydroxide is pipetted into a conical flask and titrated with standard hydrochloric acid from a burette. For each fault, state the effect on the titre and on the calculated concentration of sodium hydroxide.

**(a)** The burette was rinsed with distilled water but not with the acid. **[2]**

**(b)** The pipette was rinsed with distilled water but not with the sodium hydroxide. **[2]**

**(c)** The end point was overshot in every titration. **[2]**

**7.** A student makes a standard solution in a 250.0 cm³ volumetric flask.

**(a)** The beaker used to dissolve the solid is not rinsed into the flask. Explain the effect on the concentration. **[2]**

**(b)** The student fills the flask until the top of the meniscus is on the line. Explain the effect on the concentration. **[2]**

**8.** A crude organic product dissolved in dichloromethane (density 1.33 g cm⁻³) is shaken with aqueous sodium hydrogencarbonate (density about 1.0 g cm⁻³) in a separating funnel.

**(a)** State which layer contains the organic product. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why the tap must be opened frequently while shaking. **[1]**

**(c)** Describe how the organic layer is collected. **[1]**

**9.** During a recrystallisation a student must filter twice.

**(a)** The hot solution contains insoluble impurities that must be removed before it cools. State the type of filtration and give a reason. **[2]**

**(b)** The crystals that form on cooling must be collected. State the type of filtration and give a reason. **[2]**

**10.** Take the melting point of pure compound Z as 133 °C. Sample A melts from 126 °C to 131 °C. Sample B melts from 132 °C to 133 °C.

**(a)** Identify the purer sample and justify your answer. **[1]**

**(b)** Sample A was later found to be pure but damp. Explain its result. **[1]**

**11.** A student sets up a cell from a Zn²⁺/Zn half-cell and a Cu²⁺/Cu half-cell.

**(a)** The student connects the two solutions with a copper wire instead of a salt bridge. Explain why this is wrong. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why a high-resistance voltmeter is used. **[1]**

**(c)** Suggest why the electrodes are cleaned with emery paper first. **[1]**

**12.** Magnesium ribbon reacts with excess hydrochloric acid, and the hydrogen is collected in a gas syringe.

| Time / s | 0 | 20 | 40 | 60 | 80 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Volume / cm³ | 0 | 18 | 31 | 40 | 46 |

**(a)** Name the type of rate method used here. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the mean rate over the first 20 s and over the last 20 s, with units. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the rate decreases. **[1]**

**(d)** Name a second type of rate method that 1.2.2(l) requires, and give an example. **[1]**

**13.** A student oxidises propan-1-ol by heating it with acidified potassium dichromate(VI). Propan-1-ol is flammable; potassium dichromate(VI) is toxic. For each substance, state the risk and a control measure matched to it. **[4]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** ±0.10 cm³ (two readings of ±0.05 cm³) [1]

**(b)** 0.10 ÷ 23.40 × 100 = **0.43%** [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* Quoting ±0.05 cm³ for a titre ignores the second reading, and the percentage then comes out half the correct value.

**2. (a)** Weigh the container with the solid, transfer the solid, then reweigh the empty container; the mass is the difference [1]

**(b)** 0.01 ÷ 0.250 × 100 = **4.0%** [1]

**(c)** Use a three-decimal-place balance: 0.001 ÷ 0.250 × 100 = **0.40%** (or weigh a larger mass, with the new value calculated) [1] **[3]**

*Examiner insight:* Weighing by difference means any solid left in the container is not counted, which is why it is used.

**3. (a)** Temperature change = 4.5 °C [1]; uncertainty ±1.0 °C, so 1.0 ÷ 4.5 × 100 = **22%** [1]

**(b)** Use a thermometer with finer graduations, such as 0.1 °C [1]; use larger amounts or more concentrated reagents so the temperature change is larger [1] **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* "Repeat the experiment" improves reliability but does not reduce the uncertainty of each reading.

**4. (a)** Water bath: gentle, even warming below 100 °C is enough [1]

**(b)** Electric heater (heating mantle): the mixture is flammable and must boil, so no flame [1]

**(c)** Sand bath: even heating above the 100 °C limit of a water bath [1] **[3]**

**5. (a)** Place the probe in a buffer solution of known pH and adjust the reading to that value [1]; repeat with a second buffer of different pH [1]

**(b)** To stop traces of one solution changing the pH of the next [1]

**(c)** It records pH continuously and precisely (to about 0.01) as titrant is added, whereas a chart gives only rough values about one unit apart [1] **[4]**

**6. (a)** Acid diluted, so the titre is larger [1]; calculated concentration of NaOH too high [1]

**(b)** Less NaOH is delivered, so the titre is smaller [1]; calculated concentration too low [1]

**(c)** Titre larger [1]; calculated concentration too high [1] **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* Here the unknown is in the flask, not the burette, so a larger titre gives a *higher* answer. Work out c(NaOH) = c(HCl) × titre ÷ 25.0 before deciding the direction.

**7. (a)** Some solute stays in the beaker, so fewer moles reach the flask [1]; the concentration is lower than calculated [1]

**(b)** The bottom of the meniscus lies below the line, so the volume is less than 250.0 cm³ [1]; the concentration is higher than calculated [1] **[4]**

**8. (a)** The lower layer, because dichloromethane is denser than the aqueous layer [1]

**(b)** Carbon dioxide forms as hydrogencarbonate reacts with acidic impurities, and the pressure must be released [1]

**(c)** Remove the stopper and run the lower layer out through the tap into a flask [1] **[3]**

**9. (a)** Gravity filtration through fluted filter paper (in a warmed funnel) [1]; it is fast, so the product does not crystallise in the funnel, and the filtrate is kept [1]

**(b)** Filtration under reduced pressure with a Büchner funnel [1]; it is quick and leaves the crystals drier [1] **[4]**

**10. (a)** Sample B: it melts over a narrow range at the value for pure Z, while A melts lower and over 5 °C [1]

**(b)** Water acts as an impurity, lowering the melting point and widening the range [1] **[2]**

**11. (a)** A wire would set up its own electrode reactions; the salt bridge must let ions move between the solutions to complete the circuit [1]

**(b)** It draws almost no current, so it measures the maximum voltage, the cell potential [1]

**(c)** To remove oxide or dirt so the metal itself is in contact with the solution [1] **[3]**

**12. (a)** Continuous monitoring [1]

**(b)** First 20 s: 18 ÷ 20 = **0.90 cm³ s⁻¹** [1]; last 20 s: (46 − 40) ÷ 20 = **0.30 cm³ s⁻¹** [1]

**(c)** The acid concentration falls as it is used up, so there are fewer successful collisions per second [1]

**(d)** An initial rate method, such as a clock reaction [1] **[5]**

**13.** Propan-1-ol vapour could catch fire [1]; heat with an electric heater or water bath, with no naked flames [1]. Dichromate is harmful if it contacts skin or is swallowed [1]; wear gloves and eye protection and avoid spills on skin [1] **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* Each control must match its hazard. Goggles do nothing about flammability.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using ±0.05 cm³ instead of ±0.10 cm³ for a titre.
- Writing "inaccurate" instead of stating whether a result is too high or too low.
- Assuming a larger titre always gives a lower concentration, whatever is in the burette.
- Suggesting repeats as a way to reduce the uncertainty of a single reading.
- Naming fluted filter paper for collecting crystals.
- Giving a generic safety measure with no link to the named hazard.
