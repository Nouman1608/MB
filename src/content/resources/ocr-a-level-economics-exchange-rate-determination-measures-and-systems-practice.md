These questions cover the first part of the OCR *A Level Economics (H460)* topic **Exchange rates** (specification Version 3.0, June 2026, for first assessment in 2021). Country X uses the tala and Country Y uses the dirn. All figures are invented. Revise with the [study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-economics-exchange-rate-determination-measures-and-systems/) and [revision notes](/resources/ocr-a-level-economics-exchange-rate-determination-measures-and-systems-revision-notes/), and use the [OCR Economics hub](/boards/ocr/a-level/economics/) and [checklist](/checklists/ocr/a-level/economics/).

## Questions

**1.** The rate moves from 8.00 to 10.00 tala per dirn.

**(a)** Distinguish between a depreciation and a devaluation. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in the value of the tala in dirn. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the price in tala of a 60-dirn good before and after the move. **[2]**

**2.** Country X trades with three partners. Trade values are 90, 70 and 40. The tala's bilateral indices (base year 100) against them are 108, 96 and 85.

**(a)** Calculate the trade weights. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the effective exchange rate index. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain one reason why the effective rate is more useful than a single bilateral rate. **[2]**

**3.** In the base year the tala is worth 0.125 dirn and price levels in X and Y are both 100. Later the nominal rate falls by 5%, the price level in X is 104 and in Y is 100.

**(a)** Calculate the real exchange rate index (base year = 100). **[3]**
**(b)** Interpret your answer. **[2]**

**4.** Country X's nominal effective rate index and its relative price index (X compared with partners) are:

| Year | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nominal index | 100 | 103 | 108 | 112 | 104 | 101 |
| Relative prices | 100 | 102 | 104 | 107 | 108 | 109 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in the nominal index from Year 4 to Year 5. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe two features of the nominal trend. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the real index in Year 4 (nominal index × relative prices ÷ 100) and explain why it differs from the nominal index. **[2]**

**5.** The market for tala is Qd = 1500 − 8000p and Qs = 8000p − 500 (millions of tala per month, p in dirn per tala), under a floating system.

**(a)** Find the equilibrium rate and quantity. **[2]**
**(b)** Foreign investors withdraw, so demand falls by 160 at every price. Find the new rate and the percentage change. **[3]**
**(c)** Describe the diagram that shows this. **[2]**

**6.** Use the market in question 5, but suppose the central bank holds a peg at 0.125 dirn per tala when demand falls by 160.

**(a)** Calculate the excess at the peg. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the reserves used, in dirn. **[1]**
**(c)** The bank holds 90 dirn of reserves. How many whole months can it keep doing this? **[1]**
**(d)** Explain one other way to defend the peg and one cost. **[2]**

**7.** Evaluate the view that differences in interest rates are the main cause of changes in a floating exchange rate. **[8]**

**8.** A firm in Country X imports an input costing 80 dirn per batch. Total batch cost is 2,000 tala when the rate is 8.00 tala per dirn. The rate rises to 10.00.

**(a)** Calculate the new input cost, the percentage rise in it, and the percentage rise in total batch cost. **[3]**
**(b)** Evaluate the consequences of the depreciation for the wider economy. **[6]**

**9.** **(a)** Explain what is meant by a hybrid exchange rate system. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe one type of hybrid system. **[2]**
**(c)** Name one country that has been classified as having a managed arrangement. **[1]**

**10.** A small open economy imports most of its food and fuel. Evaluate whether a fixed or a floating system suits it better. **[10]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Depreciation is a fall in the market value of a floating currency [1]. Devaluation is a deliberate lowering of a fixed or pegged rate by the authorities [1].
**(b)** Dirn per tala falls from 1 ÷ 8 = 0.125 to 1 ÷ 10 = 0.100 [1]. Change = (0.100 − 0.125) ÷ 0.125 = **−20%** [1].
**(c)** Before: 60 × 8 = **480 tala** [1]. After: 60 × 10 = **600 tala** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Part (b) is easy to answer as +25% by measuring the dirn. State the currency you are measuring.

**2. (a)** Total 200, so weights are **0.45, 0.35 and 0.20** [1].
**(b)** 0.45 × 108 = 48.6, 0.35 × 96 = 33.6, 0.20 × 85 = 17.0 [1]. Sum = **99.2** [1].
**(c)** It weights each partner by its importance in trade [1], so it shows the overall movement when the tala rises against one partner and falls against another [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Show each weight times index before summing so your method is clear. Context-specific reasons score better than the statement "it is more accurate".

**3. (a)** New nominal rate = 0.125 × 0.95 = 0.11875 [1]. Real rate = 0.11875 × 104 ÷ 100 = 0.1235 [1]. Index = 0.1235 ÷ 0.125 × 100 = **98.8** [1].
**(b)** There was a real depreciation of 1.2% [1], much smaller than the 5% nominal fall because prices rose faster in X than in Y [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* A good interpretation says what the number means for competitiveness, not only whether it is above or below 100.

**4. (a)** (104 − 112) ÷ 112 [1] = **−7.1%** [1]
**(b)** Rose from 100 to a peak of 112 in Year 4 [1]. Then fell in two successive years to 101, so the movement is volatile and ends only 1% above the start [1].
**(c)** Real index = 112 × 107 ÷ 100 = **119.8** [1]. It is higher because prices in X rose 7% relative to partners, so the appreciation is larger in real terms [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote figures when describing a trend. Phrases such as "it went up then down" tend to gain little.

**5. (a)** 1500 − 8000p = 8000p − 500, so 2000 = 16000p and p = **0.125** [1]. Q = 1500 − 1000 = **500** [1].
**(b)** New demand is 1340 − 8000p [1]. 1340 − 8000p = 8000p − 500, so p = 1840 ÷ 16000 = **0.115** [1]. Change = (0.115 − 0.125) ÷ 0.125 = **−8%** [1].
**(c)** Axes: dirn per tala (vertical) and quantity of tala (horizontal), with D, S and the original equilibrium (0.125, 500) [1]. D shifts left to D1, giving a new equilibrium at a lower rate and quantity (0.115, 420) [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* A shift in demand is shown by moving the curve, not by moving along it. Label both equilibria.

**6. (a)** At 0.125: Qd = 1340 − 1000 = 340 and Qs = 8000 × 0.125 − 500 = 500 [1]. Excess supply of **160** (million tala) [1].
**(b)** The bank buys 160 tala: 160 × 0.125 = **20 dirn** [1].
**(c)** 90 ÷ 20 = 4.5, so **4 whole months** [1].
**(d)** Raise interest rates to attract capital and increase demand for tala [1]. Cost: higher borrowing costs slow growth and investment [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A cost must be attached to the policy. Naming the tool alone is description.

**7.** Interest rates are an important cause:
- A higher rate relative to other countries raises returns on the currency's assets [1], so "hot money" flows in and demand shifts right, causing appreciation [1].
- The effect is strong in the short run because financial flows are large and fast [1].

Limits and other causes:
- Real returns matter: if inflation is higher, the nominal gain is eroded [1].
- Relative inflation and trade flows govern the longer-run rate [1].
- Speculation, confidence and investment decisions can swamp interest rate effects [1].
- The effect depends on whether the change is expected to last [1].

Judgement: interest rates are likely the main short-run cause but not the sole or the long-run cause [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* A conclusion that separates short run from long run, using the evidence given, shows evaluation. A list of causes alone does not.

**8. (a)** New input cost = 80 × 10 = **800 tala** [1], up from 640, a rise of 160 ÷ 640 = **25%** [1]. Total batch cost = 2,000 − 640 + 800 = 2,160, a rise of **8.0%** [1].
**(b)** For the wider economy:
- Exports become cheaper abroad and imports dearer, which may raise net exports [1], although the effect depends on elasticities [1].
- Imported inflation rises as input and consumer prices increase [1].
- Aggregate demand rises through net exports but short-run aggregate supply falls as costs rise [1].
- Foreign-currency debts rise in tala terms [1].
- Judgement: the net effect depends on how open the economy is, spare capacity and how long the change lasts [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Weigh the costs and benefits against each other. Stating that a depreciation "helps exporters" without limits stays at description.

**9. (a)** A system combining features of fixed and floating rates [1]: the market sets the rate in the main, but the authorities intervene or guide it [1].
**(b)** A managed float: the rate moves with demand and supply [1], but the central bank buys or sells to smooth or steer movements [1]. Band or crawling peg answers also earn credit.
**(c)** For example **Singapore or China**, which the International Monetary Fund's 2023 report classed as a crawl-like arrangement and an other managed arrangement respectively [1]. Check the latest classification. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* A hybrid example needs a statement of how it combines fixed and floating features, not just a country name.

**10.** For fixed:
- A peg reduces uncertainty for importers of essential goods [1].
- It stops a depreciation raising food and fuel prices [1].
- It can import credibility on inflation [1].

Against fixed:
- It needs large reserves, and defending the peg is costly [1].
- Interest rates are tied to the anchor [1].
- The rate may become misaligned [1].

For floating:
- It adjusts automatically and gives policy freedom [1], but a fall would raise import prices sharply, since demand for food and fuel is inelastic [1].

Judgement: with high import dependence and inelastic demand, stability is likely to matter most, so a peg or tightly managed rate is better if reserves and credibility are sufficient [1]. Otherwise a managed float is a compromise [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Apply the context. Answers that use the stated imports of food and fuel tend to gain more than general lists of merits.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Taking a percentage change in the wrong currency.
- Missing units, or reporting a rate without saying which currency is the price.
- Unlabelled diagrams or a single equilibrium shown.
- Treating nominal and real rates as the same.
- Describing intervention without saying what it costs.
- Writing about depreciation helping trade without noting elasticities and time lags.
- Offering a list of merits and drawbacks with no judgement.
- Using a hybrid example without explaining what is managed.
