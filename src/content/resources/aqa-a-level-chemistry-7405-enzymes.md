## Enzymes are protein catalysts

An enzyme is a protein, a polypeptide chain folded into a specific three-dimensional shape (primary, secondary and tertiary structure). It acts as a **biological catalyst**: it speeds up a reaction in a living system without being used up. Like any catalyst, an enzyme provides an alternative reaction pathway with a **lower activation energy**, so more molecules have enough energy to react at body temperature.

Enzymes are usually very efficient and work under mild conditions of temperature and pH, and they are specific to a particular reaction.

## The active site

The enzyme has a region, the **active site**, where the reaction takes place. It is a pocket or groove on the surface, formed by the way the chain folds. The side chains of the amino acids that line the pocket are arranged to attract specific parts of the substrate molecule.

The **substrate** is the molecule that the enzyme acts on. The steps are:

1. The substrate fits into the active site and binds, forming an **enzyme–substrate complex**.
2. Bonds in the substrate are broken or made, helped by the groups in the active site.
3. The **products** leave the active site.
4. The enzyme is unchanged and can bind another substrate molecule.

The binding uses interactions such as hydrogen bonds, ionic attractions and London forces between the side chains of the active site and the substrate. Because the active site has a precise shape and arrangement of groups, a substrate has to fit it closely, so the enzyme is specific.

## Stereospecificity

Many biological molecules are **chiral** and exist as two enantiomers that are non-superimposable mirror images. The active site is itself chiral, because the protein is built from chiral amino acids and folds into a specific 3D shape. It is therefore **stereospecific**: it can bond to only one enantiomer of a substrate.

### Why only one enantiomer fits

Think of a hand and a glove. A right hand fits a right-handed glove but not a left-handed one, even though the two are the same shape in a mirror image.

- The substrate has a chiral centre with four different groups arranged in a particular order in space.
- The active site has binding groups arranged in a matching pattern to attract these four groups.
- The mirror-image enantiomer has the same groups but arranged in the opposite way in space, so they cannot all line up with the binding groups at the same time.
- Only one enantiomer is bound strongly enough and in the right orientation for the reaction.

The same logic applies to a **drug** that binds to a receptor or enzyme. One enantiomer of a chiral drug may fit the active site and be active, while the other may not fit and be inactive or may have a different effect.

## Enzyme inhibitors as drugs

An **enzyme inhibitor** is a molecule that slows or stops an enzyme. Many drugs work in this way.

- The drug molecule has a shape and groups similar to the substrate, so it can bind to the active site.
- When the drug is in the active site, the **substrate cannot bind**, because the site is blocked.
- The reaction catalysed by the enzyme is slowed or stopped.

This principle is used to design drugs that stop an enzyme needed by a disease-causing organism or process. Some antibiotics, for example, block an enzyme that bacteria need to build their cell walls. Because the active site is stereospecific, a drug must have the correct three-dimensional arrangement to fit, and often only one enantiomer of a chiral drug does so.

### Computer-aided design

Chemists use **computers** to model the three-dimensional structure of the active site and of candidate molecules, and to predict how well they will fit and bind. Advantages include:

- a large number of possible molecules can be tested on the computer without making them, saving time and cost,
- the best candidates can be selected for synthesis and laboratory testing,
- the design can be adjusted to improve the fit,
- fewer animal and human tests may be needed in the early stages.

## Worked example 1: why one enantiomer reacts

**Question.** An enzyme catalyses a reaction of one enantiomer of lactic acid but has no effect on the other. Explain this.

The active site of the enzyme is stereospecific. Its binding groups are arranged to match the arrangement of the four different groups around the chiral carbon of the first enantiomer. The other enantiomer has the same groups in the mirror-image arrangement, so they cannot all bond to the active site at the same time. It does not bind properly, and no reaction takes place.

## Worked example 2: how an inhibitor works

**Question.** A drug molecule resembles the substrate of an enzyme. Explain how it reduces the rate of the reaction.

The drug has a similar shape and similar groups to the substrate, so it binds in the active site. This blocks the active site, so substrate molecules cannot bind. The enzyme–substrate complex forms less often, so fewer products form per second and the rate falls.

## Worked example 3: a racemic mixture and an enzyme

10.0 g of a racemic mixture of an amino acid (Mᵣ = 89.0) is treated with an enzyme that reacts with only one enantiomer. What is the maximum mass of enantiomer that can react?

```
A racemic mixture contains equal amounts of the two enantiomers.
mass of each = 10.0 ÷ 2 = 5.00 g
moles of the reacting enantiomer = 5.00 ÷ 89.0 = 0.0562 mol
```

At most **5.00 g** (50 %) can react. The other 5.00 g is not touched by the enzyme. This shows how enzymes can be used to separate enantiomers.

## Worked example 4: a catalytic cycle calculation

An enzyme can convert 1.0 × 10³ molecules of substrate per second at each active site. Calculate the amount of substrate, in mol, converted in 60 s by 2.0 × 10⁻⁹ mol of enzyme with one active site per molecule.

```
n(substrate per second) = 2.0 × 10⁻⁹ × 1.0 × 10³ = 2.0 × 10⁻⁶ mol s⁻¹
n(in 60 s)              = 2.0 × 10⁻⁶ × 60 = 1.2 × 10⁻⁴ mol
```

The enzyme is used again and again, which is why so little is needed.

## Linking the structure to the function

| Feature of the protein | Importance for the enzyme |
|---|---|
| Primary structure | The sequence of amino acids decides which side chains line the active site |
| Secondary structure | The helices and sheets form the framework |
| Tertiary structure (hydrogen bonds, S–S bonds) | Holds the active site in exactly the right shape |
| Chiral amino acids | Make the active site chiral, so it is stereospecific |

A change in the shape of the protein (for example, if it is heated too much) changes the shape of the active site, and the enzyme stops working.

## Common mistakes

- Saying the enzyme is "used up" in the reaction.
- Describing the active site as a place where the enzyme "dissolves" the substrate.
- Saying the enantiomer "does not fit because it is the wrong size". It has the same size and groups, but they are arranged differently in space.
- Writing that the inhibitor "destroys" the enzyme. It blocks the active site.
- Forgetting that the active site is chiral because of the amino acids.
- Saying that computers "make" drugs. They help to model and select candidates.
- Confusing the substrate (acted on) with the product (formed).
- Saying that a catalyst changes the position of equilibrium or the enthalpy change.

## Exam technique

- **Enzyme action**: substrate fits the active site, enzyme–substrate complex, products leave, enzyme unchanged.
- **Stereospecific**: say that the active site is chiral and the other enantiomer's groups cannot all bond.
- **Inhibitors**: shape similar to the substrate, binds in the active site, blocks it.
- **Computers**: model the active site and drug, test many candidates quickly and cheaply.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is the enzyme a protein or does it contain protein?**
For this specification, enzymes are proteins.

**Why are drugs often supplied as one enantiomer?**
Only one enantiomer may bind to the target. Supplying a single enantiomer avoids giving the patient an inactive or harmful mirror image.

**Do enzymes change the equilibrium of a reversible reaction?**
No. They speed up the forward and reverse reactions equally.
