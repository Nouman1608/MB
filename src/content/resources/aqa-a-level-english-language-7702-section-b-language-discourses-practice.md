These questions practise 3.2.3 Language discourses from AQA A-level English Language (7702). All three texts are
**original**, written for this page.

**Text J**: a newspaper opinion column.

> *"Walk into any café in Britain and you will hear it: 'Can I get a latte?' Get? We used to ask politely. American
> English is creeping into our shops, our schools and our living rooms, one 'awesome' at a time. If we do not defend
> our own tongue, our grandchildren will grow up speaking a language that belongs to someone else."*

**Text K**: an extract from a popular book about English by a linguist.

> *"Complaints about American influence are almost as old as American English itself. Some words now labelled
> 'Americanisms' were once common in Britain and simply survived longer across the Atlantic. Borrowing also runs in
> both directions. A language that absorbs new words is not being invaded; it is doing what English has always
> done."*

**Text L**: a satirical social media post.

> *"Absolutely outraged to hear my nephew say 'cookie' instead of 'biscuit'. Next he'll be driving on the right. I've
> written to my MP, the King and the Oxford English Dictionary. Twice."*

## Section A

**1.** Explain what is meant by a discourse about language, and name **two** discourses about language change. **[3]**

**2.** Explain Deborah Cameron's term "verbal hygiene". **[2]**

**3.** Identify the metaphor in "American English is creeping into our shops" (Text J), and explain how it
represents language change. **[3]**

**4.** Explain how first-person plural pronouns position the reader in Text J. **[3]**

**5.** Identify **two** features that construct an expert identity for the producer of Text K, and explain each.
**[4]**

**6.** Explain how irony works in Text L, and what view it actually supports. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** Compare how Texts J and K represent American English and its influence. **[6]**

**8.** Compare the identities constructed by the producers of Texts J and K. **[6]**

**9.** Analyse how Text J positions its reader and seeks to influence them. **[5]**

**10.** Explain which discourse Text J reproduces, and analyse how Text K challenges it. **[5]**

**11.** Explain how genre and audience shape the language of Texts K and L. **[4]**

**12.** Plan a full comparative analysis of Texts J and K, giving four comparison points, each with evidence from both
texts. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** A discourse about language is a recurring, socially shared way of talking about language that carries
assumptions and values [1]. Two discourses (one mark each): decline or corruption, in which change is damage [1];
natural change or evolution, in which change is normal adaptation [1]. (Also creditable: invasion, standards and
correctness, identity and pride.)

**2.** The urge to regulate, correct or "clean up" language [1], which Cameron argues often reflects social values,
such as order and respectability, as much as concern for language itself [1].

**3.** "Creeping" is a metaphor of stealthy, unwanted movement, as of an intruder or a pest [1]. It represents change
as gradual, hidden and threatening [1], and the list "our shops, our schools and our living rooms" moves the threat
from public spaces into the home, intensifying alarm [1].

**4.** "We used to ask politely" and "our own tongue" create an inclusive in-group of British speakers [1], presupposing
that the reader shares the writer's past habits and loyalties [1]. The conditional "If we do not defend" makes the
reader jointly responsible for the outcome, positioning them as a defender of the language [1].

**5.** Two features, each 1 mark for identification and 1 for explanation. Historical perspective ("almost as old as
American English itself") [1] presents the writer as someone with knowledge across time, which undermines the
column's sense of novelty [1]. Hedged, qualified claims ("Some words", "almost") [1] signal academic caution rather
than sweeping generalisation [1]. (Also creditable: specialist concept "borrowing"; measured declarative register.)

**6.** The post exaggerates outrage (hyperbole: "Absolutely outraged", writing to "the King", "Twice") [1] about a
trivial lexical difference, so the surface complaint is absurd [1]. The irony mocks the anti-Americanism discourse,
so the post actually supports the view that such complaints are overblown [1].

**7.** Up to 6 points, 1 mark each: J represents American English as an intruder ("creeping"), K explicitly rejects
the invasion frame ("is not being invaded") [1]; J represents it as foreign property ("belongs to someone else"), K
as partly shared history (words "once common in Britain") [1]; J represents change through a single comic example
("Can I get a latte?"), K through general claims about borrowing [1]; J treats change as recent and accelerating
("one 'awesome' at a time"), K as long-standing ("almost as old as American English") [1]; K represents borrowing
as two-way ("runs in both directions"), J as one-way [1]; overall, J frames change as loss, K as normal behaviour of
English [1].

**8.** Up to 6 points, 1 mark each: J's producer is a guardian of British English, using inclusive "we" and "our" [1];
J relies on everyday observation ("Walk into any café") rather than evidence, constructing an ordinary-person
authority [1]; J's minor sentence "Get?" performs indignation, a comic, opinionated persona [1]; K's producer is an
expert, signalled by historical knowledge and the concept of borrowing [1]; K uses an impersonal, measured register
with no first person, suggesting objectivity [1]; K's final balanced sentence, with a semicolon contrast, gives a
calm, authoritative conclusion that contrasts with J's warning [1].

**9.** Up to 5 points, 1 mark each: second-person address ("you will hear it") makes the reader a witness [1]; the
rhetorical question "Get?" invites shared disapproval [1]; nostalgic "We used to ask politely" presupposes a better
past and that the new form is impolite [1]; the out-group ("someone else") positions Americans as outsiders and the
reader as a loyal insider [1]; the closing conditional warning about "our grandchildren" appeals to responsibility
for the future, prompting action [1].

**10.** Text J reproduces an invasion or decline discourse, in which outside influence corrupts a native language [1].
Text K challenges it by naming the discourse's metaphor and rejecting it ("is not being invaded") [1]; by showing the
complaint is old, undermining its sense of crisis [1]; by noting some "Americanisms" were British first, which
removes the idea of foreign ownership [1]; and by reframing borrowing as what English "has always done", a
descriptive, natural-change discourse [1].

**11.** Text K is a popular book for non-specialists, so it uses accessible general claims and avoids jargon,
explaining the one technical idea ("borrowing") through plain statements [1] [1]. Text L is a short social media post
for followers who will share the joke, so it uses fragments, hyperbole and a punchline ("Twice.") [1], relying on
the audience to recognise irony without explanation [1].

**12.** Four points, 2 marks each (1 for each text's evidence):
- Representation of change: J "creeping" [1]; K "not being invaded" [1].
- Producer identity: J's "we"/"our" guardian [1]; K's historical expert ("almost as old as American English") [1].
- Reader positioning: J's rhetorical question and warning about grandchildren [1]; K's calm declaratives inviting the
  reader to reconsider [1].
- Discourse and context: J's opinion column reproduces an invasion discourse to provoke [1]; K's popular linguistics
  book challenges it to inform [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Agreeing or disagreeing with Text J instead of analysing it.
- Missing the irony of Text L and treating it as a sincere complaint.
- Writing about Text J and then Text K, with no comparison.
- Naming a discourse without quoting the features that carry it.
