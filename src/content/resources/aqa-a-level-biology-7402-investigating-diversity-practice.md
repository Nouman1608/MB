These questions cover AQA A-level Biology (7402) section 3.4.7, *Investigating diversity*. All data and sequences are invented. Teaching is in the [investigating diversity study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-7402-investigating-diversity/) and the [investigating diversity revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-7402-investigating-diversity-revision-notes/).

## Section A: short questions

**1.** Genetic diversity can be investigated by comparing DNA base sequences. Give two other comparisons named in the specification. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why comparing DNA base sequences can show differences between two species that comparing the amino acid sequence of a protein would not. **[2]**

**3.** Explain why comparing the frequency of an observable characteristic may not give a reliable measure of genetic diversity. **[2]**

**4.** Calculate the mean of these leaf lengths (mm): 46, 52, 49, 55, 43, 50, 51, 48. **[1]**

**5.** Explain how gene technology has changed the way genetic diversity is investigated. **[2]**

**6.** Population A of a fish species has 9 different base sequences of one gene; population B has 3. Which population shows greater genetic diversity at this gene? Explain your answer. **[2]**

## Section B: longer questions

**7.** The coding strand of the same gene was sequenced in three species.

A: ATGCCGTTAGCAAGTCTGGACTTCAAGTAA

B: ATGCCGTTGGCAAGTCTGGATTTCAAGTAA

C: ATGCCATTGGCTAGTCTCGATTTTAAGTAA

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of bases that differ between species A and C. **[2]**

**(b)** Use the sequences to suggest which two species are most closely related. Explain your answer. **[2]**

**(c)** Give one reason why this conclusion may not be reliable. **[1]**

**8.** Describe how a student could take a random sample to compare the length of shells of a snail species on two beaches. **[3]**

**9.** The table shows shell lengths for the snail species on the two beaches.

| Beach | Mean shell length / mm | Standard deviation / mm |
|---|---|---|
| Sheltered | 18.6 | 0.8 |
| Exposed | 15.9 | 2.4 |

**(a)** What does the larger standard deviation on the exposed beach show? **[1]**

**(b)** A student concluded that snails on the sheltered beach have longer shells. Use the standard deviations to evaluate this conclusion. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest why a difference in shell length does not prove a genetic difference between the populations. **[1]**

**10.** On a bar chart, the error bars (± 1 standard deviation) for two means overlap. Explain what this suggests. **[2]**

**11.** The amino acid sequences of a section of one protein were identical in species X and Y, but their DNA base sequences for the gene differed at four positions. Explain how this is possible. **[2]**

**12.** A scientist compared a 30-base section of one gene in four bird species and concluded that two of them were the most closely related of the group. Evaluate this conclusion and suggest how the investigation could be improved. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two [1 each]: frequency of measurable or observable characteristics; base sequence of mRNA; amino acid sequence of proteins. **[2]**

**2.** The genetic code is degenerate: more than one triplet codes for most amino acids [1]. So some base differences do not change the amino acid sequence, and only a DNA comparison detects them [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Characteristics can be affected by the environment as well as by genes [1]; many characteristics are controlled by several genes, or different alleles may give the same phenotype, so genetic differences are only inferred [1]. **[2]**

**4.** 394 ÷ 8 = **49.25 mm** (accept 49.3 mm) [1]. **[1]**

**5.** In the past, genetic differences were inferred from measurable or observable characteristics [1]. Now DNA base sequences can be investigated directly by sequencing [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Population A [1]. More different base sequences means more different alleles of the gene [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Six positions differ (bases 6, 9, 12, 18, 21 and 24) [1]; 6 ÷ 30 × 100 = **20%** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** A and B [1]. They differ at only 2 of 30 bases (6.7%), compared with 4 for B and C and 6 for A and C, so they probably share the most recent common ancestor [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** Only a short section of one gene was compared [1]. **[1]**

*Examiner insight:* Quote the number of differences for every pair, not just the closest.

**8.** Divide each beach into a grid and use a random number generator to choose coordinates [1]. Collect the snail nearest each coordinate [1]. Take the same, large number of snails from each beach and measure each shell in the same way [1]. **[3]**

**9. (a)** Shell lengths on the exposed beach are more spread out around the mean: more variation [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** Mean ± 1 s.d. is 17.8 to 19.4 mm on the sheltered beach and 13.5 to 18.3 mm on the exposed beach [1]. The ranges overlap (17.8 to 18.3 mm), so the difference may be due to chance; a statistical test would be needed to support the conclusion [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** Shell length may be affected by environmental factors, such as food supply or wave action [1]. **[1]**

**10.** The ranges of values around the two means overlap [1], so the difference between the means may be due to chance and is probably not significant [1]. **[2]**

**11.** The code is degenerate [1], so the base changes produced triplets coding for the same amino acids [1]. Accept: the differences were in non-coding sequences (introns). **[2]**

**12.** Thirty bases of one gene is a very small sample of the genome [1], so the result could be due to chance or to that gene changing unusually fast or slowly [1]. The conclusion is therefore tentative [1]. Improvements: compare longer sequences, several genes or whole genomes, or check against amino acid or immunological evidence [1]. **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving only DNA sequencing when asked for methods.
- Forgetting to convert differences into a percentage of the total.
- Saying a large standard deviation means a large mean.
- Treating overlapping error bars as proof of no difference.
- Describing sampling as random without a random method.
