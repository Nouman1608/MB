## Core recall

### Why motivation matters

| Benefit | Consequence for the business |
|---|---|
| Higher productivity | More output per worker, so lower cost per unit |
| Staff retention | Lower recruitment and training costs; experience is kept |
| Better quality and service | Fewer errors and complaints, more repeat custom |

Warning signs of poor motivation: absence, lateness, errors, complaints and staff leaving.

The specification says specific motivational theories (it names Maslow) will **not** be examined.

### Financial methods

| Method | How it works | Suits | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Salary | Fixed yearly sum, paid monthly | Managers and office roles where output is hard to measure | No reward for extra effort |
| Wage | Hourly rate × hours worked, often weekly | Jobs with changing hours, such as retail or factory shifts | Rewards time, not effort or quality |
| Commission | Percentage of the value of sales made | Sales roles | Pressure selling; unstable income |
| Profit sharing | Staff receive part of the business's profit | Encouraging everyone to care about costs and sales | Weak link between one person's effort and the reward; nothing in a loss-making year |

### Non-financial methods

| Method | Why it motivates | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Styles of management | Consulting staff and explaining decisions makes them feel valued | Consulting slows decisions; a directing style may suit untrained staff or emergencies |
| Training | Builds skills and confidence; shows the firm invests in staff; opens promotion routes | Costs money and time; trained staff may leave |
| Greater responsibility | Trust and achievement from more authority or more varied tasks | Can cause stress without support |
| Fringe benefits | Extras such as staff discount, company car or health insurance make the job attractive | Cost the business; only work if staff value them |

### Choosing a method: four questions

1. What is the cause of low motivation in the case?
2. Can the worker's output be measured individually?
3. What does this group of workers value?
4. Can the business afford it?

## Calculations to practise

- **Monthly salary** = yearly salary ÷ 12. £31,200 ÷ 12 = £2,600.
- **Weekly wage** = hourly rate × hours. £12.40 × 30 = £372.
- **Commission** = rate × value of sales. 5% of £18,000 = 0.05 × £18,000 = £900.
- **Total pay with commission** = basic pay + commission. £1,500 + £900 = £2,400.
- **Profit share per worker** = profit set aside ÷ number of workers. 8% of £75,000 = £6,000; shared by 24 staff = £250 each.
- **Output per worker** = total output ÷ number of workers. 3,600 meals ÷ 12 staff = 300 meals.

Always state the unit and, where a period is given, the period (a week, a month, a year).

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Motivation | The drive that makes employees want to work hard and well |
| Productivity | Output per worker in a given time |
| Staff retention | Keeping employees in the business |
| Remuneration | Payment received for work |
| Salary | A fixed annual amount, paid in monthly instalments |
| Wage | Pay based on the hours worked |
| Commission | Pay calculated as a percentage of the value of sales made |
| Profit sharing | Paying employees a share of the business's profits |
| Fringe benefits | Rewards given in addition to pay, such as a company car or staff discount |
| Greater responsibility | Giving an employee more authority or more demanding tasks |

## Exam traps

- Naming a theorist. It earns nothing and wastes time.
- Saying money is always the best motivator; argue both sides.
- Calling a salary a wage, or the reverse.
- Working out commission on profit when the question gives sales.
- Choosing a method that ignores the problem in the case, such as a pay rise for staff who say pay is fine but they are never consulted.
- Forgetting the cost of a non-financial method: training and fringe benefits cost money, and consultation costs time.
- Writing "motivated workers work harder" with no consequence for productivity, retention or costs.

## Self-test

1. State two benefits to a business of a motivated workforce.
2. Distinguish between a salary and a wage.
3. Give one reason why commission might not suit staff in a pharmacy.
4. A worker earns £13.20 an hour and works 25 hours a week. Calculate the weekly wage.
5. A sales assistant has a basic pay of £1,400 a month and earns 4% commission. Sales this month are £21,500. Calculate total pay.
6. A business shares 5% of its £96,000 profit equally between 32 staff. How much does each receive?
7. Explain one drawback of profit sharing as a motivator.
8. State two examples of fringe benefits.
9. How can training motivate employees?
10. When might a manager who gives instructions without consulting staff be appropriate?
11. Will a question ask you to explain Maslow's hierarchy of needs?
12. Staff at a café are well paid but say their work is boring and repetitive. Suggest a suitable method and give a reason.

### Answers

1. Any two of: higher productivity, staff retention, better quality or customer service, lower recruitment costs.
2. A salary is a fixed yearly amount paid monthly; a wage is paid for the number of hours worked, usually weekly.
3. For example, staff might pressure customers into buying medicines they do not need, which could harm customers and the pharmacy's reputation.
4. £13.20 × 25 = **£330**.
5. 0.04 × £21,500 = £860; £1,400 + £860 = **£2,260**.
6. 0.05 × £96,000 = £4,800; £4,800 ÷ 32 = **£150** each.
7. One worker's extra effort has little effect on the whole business's profit, so the reward does not feel linked to their own work; in a loss-making year there is no payment.
8. Any two of: staff discount, company car, free meals, gym membership, health insurance.
9. It builds skills and confidence, shows the business values the worker and can lead to promotion.
10. In an emergency, when quick decisions are needed, or when staff are new and untrained.
11. No. The specification says specific motivational theories will not be examined.
12. Greater responsibility, such as planning the weekly menu, because the problem is boredom, not pay.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which financial methods of motivation do I need to know?**
Salary, wages, commission and profit sharing, with when each suits a business and its weaknesses.

**Which is better: financial or non-financial motivation?**
It depends on the workers and the cause of the problem. Well-paid staff who feel ignored respond more to consultation or responsibility; low-paid staff may value pay most.
