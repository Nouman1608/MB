**Key question 6 of Core Content Option A in Cambridge O Level History 2147 asks what caused the First World War.**
The specified content covers 1890 to 1914: the Alliance System and European diplomacy, the 1902 Anglo-Japanese
Alliance, the arms race, colonial rivalries, the Balkans, and the crisis of June–July 1914. The focus points are framed
as judgements, so the skill being tested is weighing causes against each other and showing how they connected. It was
the Paper 2 prescribed topic for June 2024 and remains available on Paper 1 throughout 2024–2026.

Short recall is in the [First World War causes revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-world-history-2147-causes-first-world-war-revision-notes/),
and exam questions with mark schemes are in the
[First World War causes practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-world-history-2147-causes-first-world-war-practice/).
For the wider option, see the [O Level nineteenth century study guide](/resources/o-level-world-history-nineteenth-century-1848-1914/).

## What the specification sets

Focus points:

1. Did the Alliance System and global diplomacy make war more likely or less likely?
2. How far did colonial problems create tensions between the Great Powers?
3. Why were problems in the Balkans so difficult for the Great Powers to solve?
4. How did the assassination of Franz Ferdinand lead to war?

## Did alliances make war more or less likely?

**How the system formed.** Bismarck built the **Dual Alliance** with Austria-Hungary (1879), extended to Italy as the
**Triple Alliance** (1882), and kept Russia friendly through the Reinsurance Treaty (1887). After his dismissal in
1890, Germany let the Reinsurance Treaty lapse. Russia, isolated, signed the **Franco-Russian Alliance** (1894). Britain
then left its "splendid isolation": the **Anglo-Japanese Alliance** (1902), the **Entente Cordiale** with France (1904)
and the **Anglo-Russian Entente** (1907), which created the Triple Entente.

**Why the 1902 Anglo-Japanese Alliance matters.** The syllabus names it specifically, so use it. Britain was worried
about Russian expansion in East Asia and could not keep a large fleet everywhere. The alliance allowed Britain to rely
on Japan in the Pacific and bring warships home to face the growing German navy. It also gave Japan confidence to
fight Russia in 1904–05. Russia's defeat had two consequences for Europe: it turned Russian ambitions back towards the
Balkans, where they clashed with Austria-Hungary, and it made Russia less threatening to Britain, which helped make the
1907 entente possible. In August 1914 Japan entered the war on the Allied side, citing the alliance.

**More likely or less likely?**

| War more likely | War less likely |
|---|---|
| Germany felt encircled by France and Russia, which shaped the Schlieffen Plan for a two-front war | Alliances were defensive; none obliged a member to support aggression |
| A local quarrel could draw in all the Great Powers | The powers worked together at the Algeciras (1906) and London (1912–13) conferences |
| Military plans, especially Schlieffen, depended on rapid mobilisation | Italy and, in practice, Britain kept their freedom of action |
| Germany's "blank cheque" to Austria in July 1914 turned support into encouragement | Several crises, 1905–13, were settled without war |

A strong answer argues that alliances did not cause war by themselves, but turned the July 1914 crisis into a
continental war within a week.

## How far did colonial problems create tension?

- **Fashoda (1898).** British and French forces met on the Upper Nile; France backed down. Instead of causing war, the
  crisis pushed both towards settling their colonial differences in the 1904 Entente.
- **South Africa.** The Kaiser's telegram to President Kruger (1896), congratulating him on defeating the Jameson Raid,
  and German sympathy for the Boers in the war of 1899–1902 angered British opinion.
- **First Moroccan Crisis (1905–06).** William II visited Tangier to challenge French influence. At the Algeciras
  Conference only Austria-Hungary supported Germany, and the Entente became stronger.
- **Second Moroccan Crisis (1911).** Germany sent the gunboat *Panther* to Agadir. Britain backed France strongly;
  Germany accepted territory in the French Congo in exchange. British naval talks with France followed.

**Judgement:** colonial disputes caused tension and suspicion, but every one was settled. Their main effect was to
draw Britain and France together and to persuade Germany that the Entente was aimed at it. They were not the reason
war broke out in 1914, which came from Europe itself.

## The arms race

The naval race began with Germany's Navy Laws of 1898 and 1900 under Admiral Tirpitz. Britain launched HMS
*Dreadnought* in 1906, making older battleships obsolete and allowing Germany to compete on more equal terms. British
public pressure in 1909 ("We want eight, and we won't wait") forced more building. On land, all the continental powers
expanded their armies, and Russia's Great Programme of 1913–14 alarmed German commanders who feared Russia would soon be
too strong. The arms race created fear and gave generals influence over decisions in July 1914, but by 1912 Britain
was clearly winning the naval race and Anglo-German relations had improved slightly.

## Why were the Balkans so hard to solve?

- **Ottoman decline** left a vacuum that several powers wanted to fill.
- **Rival interests.** Austria-Hungary feared that Serbian nationalism would inspire the Slavs inside its own empire;
  Russia saw itself as protector of the Slavs and wanted access through the Straits.
- **The Bosnian Crisis (1908–09).** Austria-Hungary annexed Bosnia-Herzegovina. Russia, weakened by 1905, had to accept
  it when Germany backed Austria. Russia resolved never to be humiliated again, and Serbia was enraged.
- **The Balkan Wars (1912–13).** The Balkan League drove the Ottomans almost out of Europe, then fought over the spoils.
  Serbia nearly doubled in size; Austria-Hungary blocked it from the sea by supporting a new Albania.
- **Nationalist groups** such as the Black Hand worked for a Greater Serbia, beyond the control of any government.

These problems resisted solution because they involved the survival of Austria-Hungary as a multinational empire and
the prestige of Russia as a Great Power. Neither could give way without appearing to lose its position.

## How did the assassination lead to war?

1. **28 June 1914:** Gavrilo Princip, a Bosnian Serb linked to the Black Hand, killed Archduke Franz Ferdinand and his
   wife in Sarajevo.
2. **5–6 July:** Germany promised Austria-Hungary unconditional support, the "blank cheque".
3. **23 July:** Austria-Hungary sent Serbia an ultimatum designed to be rejected. Serbia accepted almost all of it.
4. **28 July:** Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia.
5. **30 July:** Russia ordered general mobilisation.
6. **1 and 3 August:** Germany declared war on Russia and then France. The Schlieffen Plan required an attack on France
   through Belgium.
7. **4 August:** Germany invaded Belgium; Britain declared war, citing the 1839 Treaty of London guaranteeing Belgian
   neutrality and its own interest in stopping German domination of the Continent.

The chain shows how the long-term causes worked: the Balkan quarrel provided the spark, the alliances spread the war,
and military plans left little time for diplomacy.

## Annotated model answer: a (b) question

*Why did Britain declare war on Germany in August 1914? Explain your answer.*

> Britain declared war because Germany invaded Belgium on 4 August 1914. Britain had guaranteed Belgian neutrality in
> the Treaty of London of 1839, and the invasion gave the government a clear legal and moral reason that united
> Parliament and public opinion. **[Reason explained with evidence.]** A deeper reason was the Entente with France.
> Since the Moroccan crises Britain had held naval talks with France, and if France were defeated Germany would
> dominate Europe and the Channel coast, threatening British security. **[Second reason, developed and linked to the
> decision.]**

Two developed reasons reach the top level. Adding a list of long-term causes of the war would not help, because the
question is about Britain's decision.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the alliances as automatic. Britain had no binding alliance with France in 1914, and Italy stayed neutral.
- Leaving out the Anglo-Japanese Alliance, which the syllabus names.
- Confusing the two Moroccan crises (1905–06 at Tangier and Algeciras; 1911 at Agadir).
- Narrating the July crisis without explaining why each step made the next more likely.

## Exam technique for 2147

- **Describe** (a): precise terms or events, such as what happened at Agadir.
- **Explain** (b): connect cause to consequence for a single decision or crisis.
- **How far** (c): set the named factor against at least two others and decide which mattered most, using "spark",
  "spread" and "underlying cause" as tools.
