## Questions

**1.** An atom absorbs ultraviolet radiation and one electron moves to a higher energy level. Describe what happens to the electron, and what happens when the electron later loses that energy. **[3]**

**2.** Ionisation can happen to an atom.

**(a)** State what is meant by ionisation. **[1]**
**(b)** State the overall charge on the particle that remains, and explain why it has that charge. **[2]**

**3.** A teacher shines a black light lamp on a glass of tonic water, and the liquid glows with visible light. Explain this in terms of electrons. **[3]**

**4.** Radiation is produced by changes in atoms and in nuclei.

**(a)** State the part of the electromagnetic spectrum that is produced by changes in a nucleus. **[1]**
**(b)** State one other part of the spectrum that changes in atoms can produce or absorb. **[1]**

**5.** **(a)** Explain what is meant by the half-life of a radioactive isotope. **[2]**
**(b)** A sample is heated from 20 °C to 400 °C. State and explain what happens to its half-life. **[1]**

**6.** (Higher Tier only) A source has an activity of 2880 Bq. Its half-life is 12 minutes.

**(a)** Calculate the activity after 1 hour. **[3]**
**(b)** State the ratio of the original activity to the final activity. **[1]**

**7.** (Higher Tier only) A sample contains 5.6 × 10⁸ undecayed nuclei. Its half-life is 40 hours. Calculate **(a)** the number of undecayed nuclei after 120 hours, and **(b)** the fraction of the original nuclei that have decayed. **[4]**

**8.** (Higher Tier only) A detector measures a source of half-life 10 minutes. The background count rate is 30 counts per minute.

| Time / min | 0 | 10 | 20 | 30 | 40 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reading / counts per min | 510 | 270 | 150 | 90 | 60 |

**(a)** Calculate the count rate due to the source alone at each time. **[2]**
**(b)** Show from your results that the half-life is 10 minutes. **[2]**
**(c)** Predict the reading at 50 minutes. **[2]**

**9.** Three unknown sources P, Q and R are tested with a Geiger-Müller tube. P is detected with no absorber at 1 cm but not with paper in place. Q is detected through paper but not through 5 mm of aluminium. R is still detected, at a lower rate, behind 2 cm of lead.

**(a)** Identify the radiation from each of P, Q and R. **[3]**
**(b)** State which source is the most strongly ionising. **[1]**
**(c)** Suggest why P might not be detected at all if the tube is moved 20 cm away. **[1]**

**10.** A student models decay with 128 coins. Each throw, all remaining coins are thrown and those landing tails are removed. Each throw represents one half-life.

**(a)** Predict the number of coins left after 4 throws. **[2]**
**(b)** The student's results are 128, 61, 33, 14, 9. Explain why they differ from the prediction. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain what this shows about decay in a very small sample. **[2]**

**11.** A source gives out two kinds of radiation. A tube is used with aluminium plates between it and the source. The background is 30 counts per minute.

| Aluminium thickness / mm | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reading / counts per min | 1190 | 710 | 470 | 350 | 290 | 245 |

**(a)** Calculate the corrected count rates. **[2]**
**(b)** Use your results to explain which two radiations are present. **[3]**
**(c)** Estimate the count rate due to the gamma rays. **[1]**
**(d)** Describe a further test to confirm the gamma rays. **[2]**

**12.** (Higher Tier only) A source and detector give a reading of 1030 counts per minute. The background is 30 counts per minute and the half-life of the source is 18 minutes.

**(a)** Calculate the number of half-lives in 54 minutes. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the count rate from the source after 54 minutes. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the reading on the detector at that time. **[1]**
**(d)** A student says the source will have no activity after 36 minutes, because that is two half-lives. Explain the mistake and give the activity then. **[2]**
**(e)** State the ratio of the count rate from the source after 54 minutes to the original. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** The electron absorbs energy and moves to a higher level [1]. It is excited but stays in the atom [1]. When it loses the energy it falls to a lower level and the energy is emitted as electromagnetic radiation [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers that mention both the rise and the emission of radiation tend to cover the full sequence; saying the electron leaves the atom describes ionisation instead.

**2. (a)** Ionisation is the loss of outer electrons from an atom [1].
**(b)** The particle has a positive charge [1] because it has lost negative electrons while the nuclear charge is unchanged [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A reason that mentions lost electrons tends to earn more than a bare charge; "it gained protons" is a common wrong reason.

**3.** The electrons absorb ultraviolet radiation from the lamp [1]. They are excited to a higher energy level [1]. When they fall back, they emit the energy as visible light [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming ultraviolet as absorbed and visible as emitted shows the change in the radiation; vague statements about "light going in" are less secure.

**4. (a)** Gamma rays [1].
**(b)** Any one of visible light, ultraviolet, infrared or X-rays [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming a particular part of the spectrum is clearer than writing "waves"; avoid listing alpha or beta, which are particles.

**5. (a)** Half-life is the time taken for the number of undecayed nuclei to halve [1], or equivalently the time for the activity to halve [1].
**(b)** It stays the same, because decay is random and not changed by temperature [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition that says "half of the sample decays" without a time loses clarity; include the time taken and what halves.

**6. (a)** Number of half-lives = 60 ÷ 12 = 5 [1]. 2⁵ = 32 [1]. Activity = 2880 ÷ 32 = **90 Bq** [1].
**(b)** 2880 : 90 = **32 : 1** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Showing the count of half-lives before dividing makes the working easy to follow; include the unit Bq.

**7. (a)** Number of half-lives = 120 ÷ 40 = 3 [1]. Nuclei left = 5.6 × 10⁸ ÷ 8 = **7.0 × 10⁷** [1].
**(b)** Fraction remaining is 1/8 [1], so the fraction decayed is **7/8** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep to the quantity asked: the decayed fraction is one minus the remaining fraction, not the remaining fraction itself.

**8. (a)** Subtract 30 from each reading: **480, 240, 120, 60, 30** counts per minute [2].
**(b)** 480 falls to 240 in 10 minutes [1]. 240 falls to 120 in the next 10 minutes, so the time to halve is constant at 10 minutes [1].
**(c)** At 50 minutes, n = 5 and 480 ÷ 32 = 15 [1]. Add background: **45 counts per minute** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A "show that" answer tends to be strongest when it uses at least two intervals; finish by adding the background back for a prediction of a detector reading.

**9. (a)** P is alpha [1], Q is beta [1], R is gamma [1].
**(b)** P (alpha) [1].
**(c)** Alpha particles are absorbed by a few centimetres of air [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Link each absorber result to a radiation; saying "P is weakest" does not name the radiation.

**10. (a)** Each throw halves the number: 128, 64, 32, 16, 8 [1]. After 4 throws there are **8 coins** [1].
**(b)** Each coin has a random chance of being removed [1], so the number removed in any throw varies about the expected half [1].
**(c)** With few nuclei or coins the random scatter is large compared with the total [1], so the pattern is a poor predictor and the half-life is less reliable [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Explanations that use the word random together with a reason tend to carry more weight than "human error", which is not the cause of the scatter here.

**11. (a)** Subtract 30: **1160, 680, 440, 320, 260, 215** counts per minute [2].
**(b)** The corrected rate falls steeply at first, with the fall halving every millimetre (480, 240, 120, 60), which is typical of beta particles stopped by aluminium [1]. The rate then levels off near 200 rather than falling to zero [1], so a second radiation is present that aluminium does not stop: gamma rays [1].
**(c)** About **200 counts per minute** (accept values close to the levelled-off figure) [1].
**(d)** Place a thick lead sheet in front of the tube [1]. The remaining count rate should fall, but not to background, showing that gamma rays are reduced by lead [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers quote figures from the table, such as the differences between readings, rather than writing only "it goes down then flattens".

**12. (a)** 54 ÷ 18 = **3** [1].
**(b)** Source rate = 1030 − 30 = 1000 [1]. After 3 half-lives, 1000 ÷ 8 = **125 counts per minute** [1].
**(c)** 125 + 30 = **155 counts per minute** [1].
**(d)** After one half-life the count halves again, so after two half-lives one quarter remains [1]. Source rate = 1000 ÷ 4 = **250 counts per minute** [1].
**(e)** 125 : 1000 = **1 : 8** [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Subtract background before halving and add it back at the end; the ratio asked in part (e) is source count to original source count, in that order.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing the whole detector reading by 2ⁿ, including the background.
- Treating the decayed fraction and the remaining fraction as the same thing.
- Writing a ratio the wrong way round.
- Omitting units such as Bq or counts per minute.
- Describing ionisation as an electron changing level.
- Saying heat or pressure affects the half-life.
- Saying lead stops gamma rays completely.
- Explaining scatter in a coin or dice model by "mistakes" instead of random behaviour.
- Reading half-life from only one interval on a table or graph.
