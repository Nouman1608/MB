## Questions

**1.** State two features used to tell one market structure from another. **[2]**

**2.** In Country B, five firms make 90% of the sales of packaged tea. Each sells its own brand, and building a tea-packing factory costs a very large sum. Explain, using this information, why this market is not highly competitive. **[3]**

**3.** Cobble is a small shoemaker. It sells 300 pairs a month at £60 a pair. Its fixed costs are £6,000 a month and its variable cost is £35 a pair.

**(a)** Calculate Cobble's profit. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate its profit as a percentage of total revenue, to two decimal places. **[2]**

**4.** Use the Cobble data in question 3.

**(a)** Calculate the number of pairs Cobble must sell a month to break even at a price of £60. **[2]**
**(b)** A downturn cuts the price to £32 while Cobble still sells 300 pairs. Fixed costs cannot be avoided in the short run. Show whether Cobble loses less by continuing or by stopping production. **[3]**

**5.** The market for Cobble's type of shoe sells 5,000 pairs a month. Cobble, at 300 pairs, pursues a growth objective: it cuts its price to £48 and sells 450 pairs. Costs are as in question 3.

**(a)** Calculate Cobble's market share before and after. **[2]**
**(b)** State the change in its market share in percentage points. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate its profit after the change. **[2]**

**6.** Two firms in the same market face a recession. One aims to maximise profit; the other aims to survive. Explain how the different objectives may lead to different behaviour. **[3]**

**7.** In the market for a standard cooking oil in Country C, quantities are in thousands of tonnes and prices in £ per tonne. Demand is Qd = 600 − 20P and supply is Qs = 30P − 150.

**(a)** Find the equilibrium price and the quantity traded. **[2]**
**(b)** Demand rises to Qd = 700 − 20P. Calculate the new equilibrium price and quantity. **[2]**

**8.** In the market in question 7 (before the demand rise), one farm supplies 0.3 thousand tonnes. Explain why this farm is a price taker. **[4]**

**9.** After the demand rise in question 7(b), the typical firm's average cost is £15 a tonne.

**(a)** Calculate the total supernormal profit in the market, in £ thousand. **[2]**
**(b)** New firms enter and supply becomes Qs = 30P − 50. Calculate the percentage change in price and in quantity, to two decimal places. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why supernormal profit disappears. **[2]**

**10.** Explain two factors that affect how competitive a market is. **[4]**

**11.** Market C has 150 sellers of an identical product, with free entry; the price is £15 and average cost is £15 a unit. Market D has three large firms, high barriers to entry, average cost of £12 a unit because of economies of scale, and a price of £16. Evaluate whether profits are likely to be lower in Market C than in Market D. **[8]**

**12.** Orchard Juice sells 4,000 units a month at £5.50. Its fixed costs are £8,000 and its variable cost is £3 a unit. The market sells 40,000 units a month. Plan B cuts the price to £4.60 and sells 6,000 units.

**(a)** Calculate the profit under each plan. **[4]**
**(b)** State the change in market share in percentage points. **[1]**
**(c)** Evaluate which plan suits (i) a firm seeking profit and (ii) a firm seeking market share. Use break-even output in your answer. **[5]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two of: how many firms compete; how different the products are; how easily firms can enter. One mark for each of two features [1] [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming the features is what the question asks for; spend any spare time on the later questions rather than adding explanation.

**2.** There are only five firms, so each has influence over the market and faces few rivals [1]. Brands differ, so the product is differentiated and each firm has some control over its price [1]. The high cost of building a factory is a barrier to entry, which stops new firms competing profit away [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Tie each feature to the data given, then to the effect on competition; repeating the data without that link usually leaves a gap.

**3. (a)** TR = 60 × 300 = £18,000 and TC = 6,000 + (35 × 300) = £16,500 [1]. Profit = 18,000 − 16,500 = **£1,500** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 1,500 ÷ 18,000 × 100 [1] = **8.33%** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Build total cost from fixed plus variable and show it.

**4. (a)** Break-even quantity = fixed costs ÷ (price − variable cost) = 6,000 ÷ (60 − 35) [1] = **240 pairs** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Continuing: TR = 32 × 300 = £9,600 and TC = £16,500, so loss = **£6,900** [1]. Stopping: the fixed costs of £6,000 still have to be paid, so loss = **£6,000** [1]. The price of £32 is below the variable cost of £35, so each pair adds to the loss; stopping loses less [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare the two losses explicitly and state the decision; calculating only one of them does not answer the question.

**5. (a)** Before: 300 ÷ 5,000 × 100 = **6%** [1]. After: 450 ÷ 5,000 × 100 = **9%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 9 − 6 = **3 percentage points** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** TR = 48 × 450 = £21,600 and TC = 6,000 + (35 × 450) = £21,750 [1]. Profit = 21,600 − 21,750 = **−£150, a loss of £150** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Label a change in share as percentage points; writing it as a percentage change is a common slip.

**6.** A profit-maximiser would cut costs or close unprofitable lines and keep prices high enough to earn profit [1]. A firm aiming to survive might cut prices to keep sales and accept lower or negative profit for a time, or delay investment [1]. Same conditions lead to different behaviour because the objective differs [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Give a behaviour for each objective and compare them; naming objectives without actions is descriptive only.

**7. (a)** 600 − 20P = 30P − 150, so 750 = 50P [1]. **P = £15** and Q = 600 − 300 = **300 thousand tonnes** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 700 − 20P = 30P − 150, so 850 = 50P [1]. **P = £17** and Q = 700 − 340 = **360 thousand tonnes** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Check the answer by substituting into both equations; that catches arithmetic slips cheaply.

**8.** Its share is 0.3 ÷ 300 × 100 = 0.1% [1]. The product is identical to rivals' [1]. Buyers know the going price of £15 and would buy elsewhere if it asked for more [1]. So it cannot influence the price and must accept the market price [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A calculation of share is useful evidence, but the explanation must say what it implies for pricing power.

**9. (a)** Price £17 less average cost £15 = £2 per tonne [1]. Total = 2 × 360 = **£720 thousand** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** New equilibrium: 700 − 20P = 30P − 50, so 750 = 50P, P = £15 and Q = 400 [1]. Price: (15 − 17) ÷ 17 × 100 = **−11.76%**; quantity: (400 − 360) ÷ 360 × 100 = **+11.11%** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Supernormal profit attracts new firms because there are no barriers to entry [1]. Entry increases supply and lowers the price until it equals average cost of £15, leaving no supernormal profit [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the cause and effect in order (profit, entry, supply, price); a list of the end result alone does not show reasoning.

**10.** Number of firms: more firms means more rivalry and less control over price [1], so the market is more competitive [1]. Barriers to entry: high barriers (such as high start-up costs or legal protection) keep new firms out [1], so existing firms face less competition and can keep prices above costs [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each factor needs its effect on competition explained; two developed factors answer the question better than a bare list.

**11.** Profit per unit: C = 15 − 15 = £0 and D = 16 − 12 = £4 [1]. So on these figures profit is lower in C [1]. In C there are no barriers, so any supernormal profit would attract entry, supply would rise and price would fall back to cost [1]. In D barriers stop entry, so profit can last, with few rivals to force price down [1]. However, D's cost advantage comes from economies of scale, and its price is only 1 ÷ 15 × 100 = 6.67% above C's [1], so consumers may still gain from lower costs. D's profit could be lower if a price war began among the three firms [1], or if the threat of new entry restrained its price [1]. Judgement: on these figures profits are likely to be lower in C because of free entry, but the gap depends on how real the barriers are and how fiercely the three firms compete [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the figures given, then weigh them; answers that only assert "monopolies make more profit" cannot reach a reasoned judgement.

**12. (a)** Plan A: TR = 5.50 × 4,000 = £22,000 and TC = 8,000 + (3 × 4,000) = £20,000 [1]; profit **£2,000** [1]. Plan B: TR = 4.60 × 6,000 = £27,600 and TC = 8,000 + (3 × 6,000) = £26,000 [1]; profit **£1,600** [1]. **[4]**
**(b)** Share rises from 4,000 ÷ 40,000 = 10% to 6,000 ÷ 40,000 = 15%, a rise of **5 percentage points** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Break-even: A = 8,000 ÷ (5.50 − 3) = 3,200 units; B = 8,000 ÷ (4.60 − 3) = 5,000 units [1]. Margin of safety: A (4,000 − 3,200) ÷ 4,000 = 20%; B (6,000 − 5,000) ÷ 6,000 = 16.7% [1]. A profit-seeking firm prefers Plan A, since £2,000 is 20% more than £1,600 [1]. A firm seeking market share prefers Plan B, which wins 5 more percentage points of the market [1]. Judgement: Plan B has a smaller margin of safety, so a fall in demand could push it into loss sooner; a firm aiming to survive might choose A despite the share gain [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Link each plan to an objective and use the numbers as evidence; a conclusion that ignores the risk shown by break-even output is weaker.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Stating that a firm in a competitive market sets its own price.
- Giving a change in market share as a percent instead of percentage points.
- Forgetting fixed costs in total cost.
- Listing features with no link to price, profit or behaviour.
- Saying a competitive market never earns supernormal profit, with no short-run and long-run split.
- Comparing plans on revenue alone.
- Evaluating without using the figures given.
- Omitting a final judgement.
