**(a)** State the genus of the red fox. **[1]**

**(b)** Name the two ranks immediately above genus in the taxonomic hierarchy. **[1]**

**5.** (18.1.5) Name the kingdom for each organism.

**(a)** Unicellular, eukaryotic, with chloroplasts and no tissues. **[1]**

## Section B

**7.** (18.1.1) Three populations of frogs, X, Y and Z, live in neighbouring ponds. X and Y look identical, but have different mating calls and never interbreed. Z has a different skin colour from X and Y, but interbreeds freely with X and produces fertile offspring.

**(a)** Using the morphological species concept, state how the populations would be grouped into species. **[1]**

**(b)** Using the biological species concept, state how the populations would be grouped into species. Explain your answer. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest which concept is more useful here, and give one limitation of the concept you choose. **[2]**

**8.** (18.1.2, 18.1.3, 18.1.5) The table describes five organisms.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | Absent | Contains peptidoglycan | Absent | Ester bonds | Single cell |

| D | Present | None | Absent | Ester bonds | Many cells, tissues |

**(a)** Name the domain of A and of B, and the kingdom of C, D and E. **[5]**

**(b)** Explain why the base sequences of ribosomal RNA are used to compare organisms in different domains. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** A group of organisms that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring [1]. Limitation, any one: cannot be used for asexually reproducing organisms / fossils / populations that never meet [1].

**2.** Archaea, Bacteria and Eukarya (all three for the mark) [1].

**3.** Membrane lipids: ether bonds and branched chains in Archaea, ester bonds and unbranched fatty acids in Bacteria [1]. Ribosomal RNA: different base sequences [1]. Cell walls: peptidoglycan in Bacteria, absent in Archaea [1].
*Examiner insight:* Each difference needs both sides; "Archaea have ether bonds" alone may not score.

**4. (a)** *Vulpes* [1].

**(b)** Family and order [1].

**5. (a)** Protoctista [1].

**(b)** Animalia [1].

**(c)** Fungi [1].

**6.** Viruses are not cells: they have no cytoplasm, ribosomes or metabolism of their own [1]. Classified by whether the nucleic acid is DNA or RNA [1], and whether it is single-stranded or double-stranded [1].

**7. (a)** X and Y as one species; Z as a second species [1].

**(b)** X and Z as one species, because they interbreed to produce fertile offspring [1]; Y as a separate species, because it does not interbreed with X (reproductively isolated) [1].

**(c)** The biological concept, because it tests whether populations share a gene pool, and here appearance is misleading [1]. Limitation: interbreeding has to be observed or tested, which is not possible for fossils or asexual organisms [1].

**8. (a)** A: Bacteria [1]. B: Archaea [1]. C: Fungi [1]. D: Animalia [1]. E: Protoctista, because it is a single-celled eukaryote; Plantae are multicellular [1].
*Examiner insight:* E is a trap. Chloroplasts and cellulose suggest a plant, but a single cell with no tissues belongs in Protoctista.

**(b)** rRNA is present in all cells, so all organisms can be compared [1]. Its base sequence changes slowly, so differences reflect very long periods of divergence [1].

**9. (a)** Family [1].

**(b)** The lion [1]; they share the same genus, *Panthera*, so they have a more recent common ancestor [1].

**(c)** *Felis catus*: capital F only, italicised or underlined [1].

**10. (a)** K: double-stranded DNA [1]. L: single-stranded RNA [1]. M: double-stranded RNA [1]. N: single-stranded DNA [1].

**(b)** Thymine is present, so it is DNA [1]; A = T (30%) and G = C (20%), as expected when bases pair in two strands [1].

**11.** Indicative content, one mark each, to a maximum of 6:
- Biological: organisms that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring.
- Biological limitation: cannot be applied to asexual organisms, fossils or populations that never meet.
- Morphological: organisms with the same observable structural features.
- Morphological strength: can be used for fossils and preserved specimens.
- Morphological limitation: sexual dimorphism, larval forms or look-alike species mislead.
- Ecological: organisms that occupy the same niche.
- Ecological limitation: niches are hard to measure, or vary with place or life stage.
- No single concept works for all organisms, so biologists use more than one.
*Examiner insight:* A list of three definitions, with no strengths or limitations, scores at most three.

**12. (a)** Any four, each comparing both kingdoms, one mark each, to a maximum of 4:
- Both are eukaryotic and have cell walls.
- Walls: chitin in fungi, cellulose in plants.
- Nutrition: fungi heterotrophic (extracellular digestion and absorption); plants photosynthetic with chloroplasts.
- Storage: glycogen in fungi, starch in plants.
- Structure: fungi usually hyphae forming a mycelium; plants multicellular with tissues and organs.
- Reproduction: fungi by spores.

**(b)** It contains all eukaryotes that do not fit the other kingdoms, so it includes photosynthetic and heterotrophic, unicellular and simple multicellular organisms [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving "fertile" out of the biological species definition.
- Giving only one side of an Archaea–Bacteria difference.
- Putting a single-celled photosynthetic eukaryote in Plantae.
- Capitalising the species name in a binomial.
- Classifying viruses by thymine alone, without checking base pairing for strand number.

## Frequently asked questions

**How are these questions organised?**
Section A has short recall questions; Section B has data and extended questions. The learning outcome is in brackets at the start of each question.

**How do you tell single-stranded from double-stranded nucleic acid from base data?**
In a double-stranded molecule the bases pair, so A equals T (or U) and G equals C. If these pairs are unequal, the molecule is single-stranded.
