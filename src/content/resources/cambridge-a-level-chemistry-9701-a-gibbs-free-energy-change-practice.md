## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** State the Gibbs equation and give the unit in which temperature must be expressed.
**[2]**

**2.** A reaction has a positive ΔH⦵ and a positive ΔS⦵. Which statement is correct?

A: It is feasible at all temperatures.

B: It is never feasible.

C: It is feasible only above a certain temperature.

D: It is feasible only below a certain temperature.

**[1]**

**3.** A reaction has ΔH⦵ = −24.8 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS⦵ = +15.2 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Which statement is
correct?

A: ΔG is negative at all temperatures.

B: ΔG is positive at all temperatures.

C: ΔG becomes positive above 1630 K.

D: ΔG is zero at 298 K.

**[1]**

**4.** Explain why a reaction with a negative value of ΔG⦵ may not take place when the
reactants are mixed at room temperature. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why ΔS⦵ values are usually divided by 1000 before being substituted into the
Gibbs equation. **[1]**

### Section B: structured questions

**6.** Carbon reacts with carbon dioxide: C(s) + CO₂(g) → 2CO(g).
ΔH⦵ = +172.5 kJ mol⁻¹; ΔS⦵ = +175.9 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate ΔG⦵ at 298 K and state whether the reaction is feasible. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the minimum temperature at which the reaction becomes feasible. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain, in terms of the Gibbs equation, why increasing the temperature makes this
reaction more feasible. **[2]**

**7.** In a blast furnace: Fe₂O₃(s) + 3CO(g) → 2Fe(s) + 3CO₂(g).
Enthalpy changes of formation (kJ mol⁻¹): Fe₂O₃(s) −824.2; CO(g) −110.5; CO₂(g) −393.5.
ΔS⦵ = +15.2 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate ΔH⦵ for the reaction. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate ΔG at 1500 K, assuming ΔH and ΔS do not change with temperature. **[2]**

**(c)** State, with a reason, whether the reaction is feasible at all temperatures. **[1]**

**8.** For H₂(g) + Cl₂(g) → 2HCl(g), ΔH⦵ = −184.6 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS⦵ = +20.1 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate ΔG⦵ at 298 K. **[2]**

**(b)** A mixture of hydrogen and chlorine can be kept in the dark without reacting. Explain
why this does not contradict your answer to (a). **[2]**

**9.** Ethene is hydrogenated: C₂H₄(g) + H₂(g) → C₂H₆(g).
Enthalpy changes of formation (kJ mol⁻¹): C₂H₄(g) +52.3; C₂H₆(g) −84.7.
Standard entropies (J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹): C₂H₄(g) 219.6; H₂(g) 130.6; C₂H₆(g) 229.6.

**(a)** Calculate ΔH⦵. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate ΔS⦵. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the temperature above which the reaction is no longer feasible. **[2]**

**10.** Hydrogen peroxide decomposes: 2H₂O₂(l) → 2H₂O(l) + O₂(g).
ΔH⦵ = −196.0 kJ mol⁻¹; ΔS⦵ = +125.6 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate ΔG⦵ at 298 K. **[2]**

**(b)** State how ΔG changes as the temperature is increased. **[1]**

**(c)** Suggest why hydrogen peroxide solution can be stored for months. **[1]**

**11.** Benzene boils at 353 K under standard pressure. Its enthalpy change of vaporisation at
this temperature is +30.7 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the entropy change of vaporisation of benzene,
explaining your method. **[2]**

**12.** For a reaction, ΔG = +20.0 kJ mol⁻¹ at 300 K and ΔG = −10.0 kJ mol⁻¹ at 500 K. Assume
ΔH and ΔS are constant over this range.

**(a)** Calculate ΔS for the reaction, in J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate ΔH for the reaction. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the temperature at which ΔG = 0. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** ΔG⦵ = ΔH⦵ − TΔS⦵ [1]; T in kelvin [1].

**2.** **C** [1]. −TΔS is negative and grows with T, so it eventually outweighs the positive ΔH.

**3.** **A** [1]. ΔH is negative and −TΔS is negative at every temperature.

**4.** The activation energy is high [1], so the rate of reaction is extremely slow; ΔG gives
no information about rate [1].

**5.** ΔS is usually given in J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ and ΔH in kJ mol⁻¹, so they must be in the same energy
unit [1].

**6. (a)** ΔG⦵ = 172.5 − (298 × 0.1759) = 172.5 − 52.4 = **+120.1 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]; positive,
so not feasible at 298 K [1].

**(b)** T = ΔH ÷ ΔS = 172 500 ÷ 175.9 [1] = **981 K** [1].

**(c)** ΔS is positive, so the −TΔS term is negative [1]; as T increases, this term becomes
more negative until it outweighs ΔH and ΔG becomes negative [1].

**7. (a)** ΔH⦵ = [3(−393.5)] − [(−824.2) + 3(−110.5)] = −1180.5 − (−1155.7) [1] =
**−24.8 kJ mol⁻¹** [1].

**(b)** ΔG = −24.8 − (1500 × 0.0152) = −24.8 − 22.8 [1] = **−47.6 kJ mol⁻¹** [1].

**(c)** Yes: ΔH is negative and ΔS is positive, so ΔG is negative at every temperature [1].

**8. (a)** ΔG⦵ = −184.6 − (298 × 0.0201) = −184.6 − 6.0 [1] = **−190.6 kJ mol⁻¹** [1].

**(b)** The reaction is feasible, but its activation energy is high [1], so without light (or
another energy source) the rate is negligible; feasibility does not determine rate [1].

**9. (a)** ΔH⦵ = −84.7 − (+52.3 + 0) [1] = **−137.0 kJ mol⁻¹** [1].

**(b)** ΔS⦵ = 229.6 − (219.6 + 130.6) = **−120.6 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹** [1].

**(c)** At ΔG = 0, T = ΔH ÷ ΔS = (−137 000) ÷ (−120.6) [1] = **1136 K** [1].

**10. (a)** ΔG⦵ = −196.0 − (298 × 0.1256) = −196.0 − 37.4 [1] = **−233.4 kJ mol⁻¹** [1].

**(b)** ΔG becomes more negative (ΔS is positive, so −TΔS becomes more negative) [1].

**(c)** The decomposition has a high activation energy, so without a catalyst it is very slow
[1].

**11.** At the boiling point, liquid and vapour are in equilibrium, so ΔG = 0 and ΔS = ΔH ÷ T
[1]. ΔS = 30 700 ÷ 353 = **+87.0 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹** [1].

**12. (a)** ΔG changes by −30.0 kJ mol⁻¹ when T rises by 200 K, so −ΔS = −30.0 ÷ 200 =
−0.150 kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ [1]; ΔS = **+150 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹** [1].

**(b)** ΔH = ΔG + TΔS = 20.0 + (300 × 0.150) = **+65.0 kJ mol⁻¹** [1].

**(c)** T = 65 000 ÷ 150 = **433 K** [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Combining ΔH in kJ mol⁻¹ with ΔS in J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ without converting.
- Substituting a Celsius temperature.
- Losing a sign in −TΔS when ΔS is negative.
- Giving a numerical ΔG without stating whether the reaction is feasible.
- Claiming that a feasible reaction must happen quickly, instead of referring to activation
  energy.
- Forgetting that elements have ΔH<sub>f</sub> = 0 but non-zero standard entropies.
