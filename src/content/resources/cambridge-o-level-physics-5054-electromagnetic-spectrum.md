This guide covers subtopic **3.3 Electromagnetic spectrum**, from Topic 3,
Waves, of Cambridge O Level Physics 5054 (2026–2028 series). The
[Waves study guide](/resources/o-level-physics-waves/) lists the regions and
their uses; this page explains *why* each region suits the uses the
syllabus names, works through calculations with the wave equation, and
shows how to write the damage answers.

Companion pages: [Electromagnetic spectrum revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-physics-5054-electromagnetic-spectrum-revision-notes/)
and [Electromagnetic spectrum practice questions](/resources/o-level-physics-electromagnetic-spectrum-practice/).

## Syllabus coverage

The syllabus asks you to:

- know the main regions in order of frequency and in order of wavelength
- know that all electromagnetic waves travel at 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s in a vacuum,
  and at approximately the same speed in air
- describe the role of each region in the applications it lists
- describe the damage caused by electromagnetic radiation: heating of soft
  tissue and burns from excessive exposure, and the ionising effects of
  ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays

## The order of the regions

In order of **increasing frequency**:

```
radio → microwaves → infrared → visible → ultraviolet → X-rays → gamma rays
```

In order of **increasing wavelength**, the list runs the other way, from
gamma rays to radio waves. Because every region travels at the same speed in
a vacuum, v = f λ means frequency and wavelength are inversely
proportional: the highest-frequency region (gamma) has the shortest
wavelength.

Within visible light, red sits next to infrared and violet next to
ultraviolet. That is the easiest way to place the two neighbours correctly:
*infra*-red is "below red" in frequency, *ultra*-violet is "beyond violet".

## What every region has in common

All electromagnetic waves:

- are **transverse** waves
- transfer **energy** without transferring matter
- travel at **3.0 × 10⁸ m/s in a vacuum**, and at approximately the same
  speed in air
- can be reflected, refracted and diffracted

What differs between regions is frequency and wavelength, and therefore how
the radiation interacts with matter. That difference explains both the
uses and the dangers.

## Calculations with the wave equation

Use v = f λ with v = 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s unless the question says otherwise.
Convert prefixes first: 1 MHz = 10⁶ Hz, 1 GHz = 10⁹ Hz, 1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m.

**Worked example 1.** A microwave oven uses microwaves of frequency
2.45 GHz. Find the wavelength.

```
λ = v / f = 3.0 × 10⁸ / 2.45 × 10⁹ = 0.122 m  (about 12 cm)
```

**Worked example 2.** Red light has a wavelength of 700 nm and violet
light 400 nm. Find each frequency.

```
red:    f = 3.0 × 10⁸ / 700 × 10⁻⁹ = 4.3 × 10¹⁴ Hz
violet: f = 3.0 × 10⁸ / 400 × 10⁻⁹ = 7.5 × 10¹⁴ Hz
```

The shorter wavelength gives the higher frequency, as the order of the
spectrum predicts.

**Worked example 3.** A satellite television signal travels 3.6 × 10⁷ m
from a satellite to a dish. How long does it take?

```
t = distance / speed = 3.6 × 10⁷ / 3.0 × 10⁸ = 0.12 s
```

Using the speed in a vacuum for a path through air is acceptable because
the speed in air is approximately the same.

## Why each region suits its applications

Examiners reward a reason linked to a property, not just a named use.

**Radio waves — radio and television communications, astronomy.** Long
wavelengths travel well through the atmosphere and diffract round hills and
buildings, so broadcast signals reach receivers that are not in a direct
line of sight. Stars and galaxies emit radio waves, so radio telescopes
collect them to study objects that may be faint in visible light.

**Microwaves — satellite television, mobile phones, Bluetooth, microwave
ovens.** Microwaves pass through the atmosphere, so they reach satellites
and return to dishes on the ground. Their short wavelength (centimetres)
lets a dish send or receive a narrow beam. Mobile phones and Bluetooth
devices use microwaves to carry signals; Bluetooth links devices over short
distances. In an oven, microwaves are absorbed by water molecules in food,
which heats the food.

**Infrared — household electrical appliances, remote controllers, intruder
alarms, thermal imaging, optical fibres.** Grills, toasters and electric
heaters transfer energy by emitting infrared, which is absorbed by the
food or by people in the room. A remote controller sends coded pulses of
infrared to a sensor on the device. All objects emit infrared and warmer
objects emit more, so intruder alarms detect the infrared from a person and
thermal-imaging cameras build a picture from temperature differences, even
in the dark. Infrared also carries signals along optical fibres.

**Visible light — photography, vision.** Eyes and camera sensors respond to
this narrow band, which is why it is the region we use to see and to form
photographic images.

**Ultraviolet — security marking, detecting counterfeit bank notes,
sterilising water.** Certain inks are invisible in normal light but
fluoresce (glow) when ultraviolet falls on them. A security mark written in
such ink shows up only under an ultraviolet lamp; genuine bank notes carry
features that glow in a known pattern, so a forgery that does not glow, or
glows wrongly, is detected. Ultraviolet damages the genetic material of
bacteria and other microorganisms, so passing water under ultraviolet lamps
sterilises it.

**X-rays — medical imaging, security scanners, killing cancerous cells,
detecting cracks in metal.** X-rays pass easily through soft tissue but are
absorbed much more by bone and dense materials, so the detector records a
shadow picture. The same idea lets airport scanners show dense objects in
luggage and lets engineers find cracks inside metal parts. Concentrated
beams can kill cancerous cells.

**Gamma rays — detecting and killing cancerous cells, sterilising food and
medical equipment, detecting cracks in metal.** Gamma rays are very
penetrating and kill living cells. They can pass through packaging, so
food and sealed medical equipment are sterilised after packing. Carefully
aimed beams kill cancerous cells, and gamma sources can be used to detect
cancer. Their penetration also lets them show flaws inside thick metal.

## Damage caused by electromagnetic radiation

The syllabus separates two kinds of harm.

**Heating.** Excessive exposure can heat soft tissues and cause burns.
Answers usually link this to microwaves (absorbed by water in body tissue)
and infrared (absorbed by the skin).

**Ionising effects.** Higher-frequency radiation can ionise atoms in cells,
knocking electrons out of them and damaging molecules such as DNA.

| Region | Ionising effect named in the syllabus |
|---|---|
| Ultraviolet | Skin cancer and cataracts (damage to the eye) |
| X-rays | Cell mutation and cancer |
| Gamma rays | Cell mutation and cancer |

Visible light and the regions below it in frequency are not ionising; the
ionising regions are the three at the high-frequency end. A complete answer
names the region, says whether its harm is heating or ionisation, and gives
the effect.

## Common mistakes

- Reversing the order for wavelength. Write the frequency order first, then
  reverse it.
- Saying gamma rays travel faster than radio waves. All regions travel at
  the same speed in a vacuum.
- Calling microwaves or infrared "ionising". Their harm is heating.
- Listing a use without a reason when the question says *explain*: "X-rays
  for broken bones" earns less than "X-rays pass through soft tissue but are
  absorbed by bone".
- Forgetting to convert GHz, MHz or nm to base units before using v = f λ.
- Mixing up the ultraviolet uses: security marking relies on fluorescence,
  sterilising water relies on killing microorganisms.

## Exam technique for Cambridge command words

- **State** the order: write all seven regions with an arrow showing the
  direction of increasing frequency or wavelength, as the question asks.
- **Describe the role** of a region: name the region, the property it
  relies on, and what that achieves in the application.
- **Describe the damage**: name the region and the specific effect the
  syllabus gives (burns; skin cancer and cataracts; cell mutation and
  cancer).
- **Calculate**: quote v = f λ, substitute in standard form with base units,
  and give two or three significant figures with a unit.
