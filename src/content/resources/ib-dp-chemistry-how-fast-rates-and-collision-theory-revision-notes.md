## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Rate of reaction | How fast the concentration of a named reactant or product changes, per unit time (mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹) |
| Instantaneous rate | Gradient of the tangent to a concentration–time (or volume or mass–time) curve at one moment |
| Initial rate | Gradient of the tangent at t = 0 |
| Successful collision | A collision with energy ≥ Ea and proper orientation, which leads to reaction |
| Activation energy, Ea | Smallest energy a collision must bring for the particles to react |
| Catalyst | Increases the rate by providing an alternative pathway with lower Ea; not used up overall |
| Enzyme | A biological catalyst |
| Kinetically stable | Reacts very slowly because Ea is high, even if the reaction is exothermic |

## Rate (2.2.1): method in steps

**Average rate over an interval**
1. Read the property at the start and end of the interval.
2. Divide the change by the time taken.
3. Give units: mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹, cm³ s⁻¹ or g s⁻¹.

**Rate at one moment (tangent)**
1. Draw a long straight tangent touching the curve at that time.
2. Pick two well-separated points on the tangent, not on the curve.
3. Gradient = Δy/Δx. Quote rate as positive, with units.

**Converting between species.** Use the equation's coefficients. For 2SO₂ + O₂ → 2SO₃, SO₃ forms twice as fast as O₂ is used up.

**Converting units.** Mass of gas: divide by molar mass to get moles. Gas volume: divide by the molar volume you are given for the conditions.

**Worked reminder.** Sodium carbonate solution is poured into excess hydrochloric acid in an open flask, and the balance reading drops by 0.88 g in 25 s. Average rate = 0.88/25 = **0.035 g s⁻¹**. In moles of CO₂: 0.88/44.01 = 0.0200 mol, so the rate = 0.0200/25 = **8.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol s⁻¹**.

**Why the curve flattens.** As reactants are used up their concentration falls, collisions become less frequent and the gradient decreases. The curve becomes horizontal when a reactant is used up.

### Ways to follow a reaction

| Measure against time | Suits |
|---|---|
| Gas volume | Any reaction that gives off a gas |
| Mass loss | CO₂ or another heavy gas leaving an open flask |
| Absorbance (colorimeter) | A coloured reactant or product |
| Conductivity or pH | Ions used up or made |
| Time for a fixed change | Colour change or precipitate; rate ∝ 1/t |

Time is the **independent** variable when you take readings at set times. It is the **dependent** variable when you time a fixed change.

## Collision theory (2.2.2)

- Reaction needs a collision with **E ≥ Ea** and the **proper orientation** (collision geometry).
- Average kinetic energy ∝ absolute temperature (kelvin). K = °C + 273.15.
- Doubling the kelvin temperature doubles the average kinetic energy. Raising 20 °C to 40 °C increases it by only about 7% (293.15 K → 313.15 K).

## Factors (2.2.3): what changes and why

| Factor | Collision frequency | Fraction with E ≥ Ea |
|---|---|---|
| Concentration ↑ | ↑ | no change |
| Pressure ↑ (gases) | ↑ | no change |
| Surface area ↑ | ↑ | no change |
| Temperature ↑ | ↑ (small effect) | ↑ (main effect) |
| Catalyst added | no change | ↑ (because Ea is lower) |

**Controlled variables** in a rate investigation: temperature, volumes and concentrations of solutions, mass and particle size of solids, stirring, apparatus.

**Errors from graphs.** Scatter about the line → random error (reduce by repeats). All points displaced the same way, or an unexpected intercept → systematic error (repeats do not help).

## Maxwell–Boltzmann curves (2.2.4 and 2.2.5)

**Sketch checklist**
- Axes labelled: kinetic energy (x); number or fraction of particles (y).
- Curve starts at the origin.
- Single peak, then a long tail that approaches but never meets the x-axis.
- Ea marked as a vertical line to the right of the peak; shade the area beyond it.

**Higher temperature (T₂ > T₁)**
- Peak lower and shifted right; curve broader.
- Same area under both curves (same number of particles).
- Much more area beyond Ea → far more successful collisions per unit time.

**Catalyst**
- Same single curve (temperature unchanged).
- Second Ea line further left, labelled Ea(cat).
- Larger area beyond Ea(cat) → larger fraction of successful collisions.

## Energy profiles (2.2.5)

| Feature | Exothermic | Endothermic |
|---|---|---|
| Products vs reactants | Lower | Higher |
| Sign of ΔH | Negative | Positive |
| Ea(reverse) | Ea(forward) + size of ΔH | Ea(forward) − ΔH |
| With catalyst | Lower hump, same ΔH | Lower hump, same ΔH |

A catalyst lowers the forward and reverse barriers by the same amount. That is why it speeds both directions equally and leaves the equilibrium position unchanged (Reactivity 2.3).

**Worked reminder.** An endothermic reaction has Ea = 140 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔH = +35 kJ mol⁻¹. Ea(reverse) = 140 − 35 = **105 kJ mol⁻¹**.

## Linking questions to have an answer for

- **Kinetic molecular theory (Structure 1.1)**: collision theory adds Ea and orientation to the idea that particles are always moving, with a spread of kinetic energies.
- **Fuels (Reactivity 1.3)**: a fuel–air mixture is safe to store because its combustion has a high Ea; a spark supplies the energy to start it.
- **Alkanes (Reactivity 3.3)**: strong, essentially non-polar C–C and C–H bonds give a high Ea, so alkanes are kinetically stable even though their combustion is exothermic.
- **Equilibrium (Reactivity 2.3)**: a catalyst increases the forward and backward rates by the same factor.
- **HL link (Structure 3.1)**: transition elements are often used as catalysts.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Average rate vs instantaneous rate**: the first is an interval average; the second is a tangent gradient.
- **More collisions vs more frequent collisions**: rate depends on collisions per unit time.
- **Temperature vs catalyst on a Maxwell–Boltzmann curve**: temperature changes the curve; a catalyst moves the Ea line.
- **Kinetic vs thermodynamic stability**: a high Ea makes a reaction slow; a positive or negative ΔH says nothing about speed. A high Ea is a useful property of a fuel, because it will not ignite until energy is supplied.
- **Celsius vs kelvin**: only kelvin temperatures are proportional to average kinetic energy.

## Quick self-test

1. State the definition of rate of reaction.
2. A tangent to a gas volume–time curve passes through (10 s, 16 cm³) and (50 s, 64 cm³). Calculate the rate at that point.
3. In 2SO₂ + O₂ → 2SO₃, O₂ is used at 1.5 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹. Find the rate of formation of SO₃.
4. Convert 127 °C to kelvin.
5. Give the two conditions for a successful collision.
6. Why does crushing a solid reactant speed up the reaction?
7. Why does a temperature rise increase rate much more than the rise in average kinetic energy suggests?
8. On a Maxwell–Boltzmann curve, what stays the same when the temperature is raised?
9. How do you show a catalyst on a Maxwell–Boltzmann curve?
10. A time-to-colour-change run takes 40 s. Give a value proportional to the rate.
11. State what an enzyme is.

### Answers

1. How fast the concentration of a named reactant or product changes, per unit time.
2. Gradient = (64 − 16)/(50 − 10) = **1.2 cm³ s⁻¹**.
3. 2 × 1.5 × 10⁻³ = **3.0 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹**.
4. **400.15 K** (400 K).
5. Energy at least equal to Ea, and proper orientation.
6. More of the solid's particles are exposed, so collisions happen more often.
7. A larger fraction of collisions now have E ≥ Ea; the extra collision frequency is a small effect.
8. The total area under the curve (the number of particles).
9. One curve only; add a second Ea line to the left of the first.
10. 1/40 = **0.025 s⁻¹**.
11. A biological catalyst.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Reading a single point off a curve when the question asks for the rate at a time: you need a tangent.
- Taking the two gradient points from the curve instead of from the tangent line.
- Missing or wrong units: cm³ s⁻¹ for gas volume, mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ for concentration.
- Forgetting the stoichiometric ratio when asked for the rate of a different species.
- Explaining a temperature effect only through collision frequency.
- Using Celsius in a kinetic energy comparison.
- Maxwell–Boltzmann sketches that start above the origin, touch the x-axis, or show a larger area at higher temperature.
- Adding a second curve for a catalyst, or changing ΔH on an energy profile when a catalyst is added.
- Saying a catalyst changes the yield at equilibrium.
