Section 3.1.3 Theory and Methods closes AQA A-level Sociology (7192) Paper 1, Education with Theory
and Methods. The specification lists eleven areas, starting with "quantitative and qualitative
methods of research; research design" and "sources of data". AQA prints the same list again as 3.3.2
in Paper 3, so this guide concentrates on the half of the list that Paper 1 students meet first and
use most: how research is designed, how each method works in practice, and what shapes the choice of
method. The theory half (structural and action theories, post-modernity, science, values and policy)
is developed in the [Theory and Methods 3.3.2 guide for Paper 3](/resources/aqa-a-level-sociology-7192-theory-and-methods-3-3-2/).

In Paper 1, AQA gives Theory and Methods 10 marks of extended writing, separate from the 20-mark
Methods in Context question. The difference matters: Methods in Context asks you to apply a method to
an education topic, while the Theory and Methods question asks about methods or theory in general,
with no education context required. For the overview of all eleven areas, see the
[Theory and Methods study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-sociology-7192-theory-and-methods/).

## What 3.1.3 asks you to know

The six methods-related areas covered here are:

- quantitative and qualitative methods; research design
- sources of data: questionnaires, interviews, participant and non-participant observation,
  experiments, documents and official statistics
- primary versus secondary data, and quantitative versus qualitative data
- positivism, interpretivism and the nature of "social facts"
- theoretical, practical and ethical considerations in choice of topic, method and conduct of
  research
- the relationship between theory and methods.

## Research design, step by step

Research design is the plan that links a research question to the data collected. A typical sequence:

1. **Choose a topic.** Topics come from the researcher's values and interests, from funding bodies,
   from current social concerns and from gaps in existing research.
2. **Review existing work** so the study adds something new.
3. **Form a hypothesis or a research question.** A hypothesis is a testable statement predicting a
   relationship between variables ("unemployment is associated with higher rates of recorded
   offending"). Positivists favour hypotheses; interpretivists usually start with an open research
   question ("how do young people experience being stopped by police?") so that meanings can emerge
   from the data.
4. **Operationalise concepts.** Abstract ideas must be turned into something measurable. "Social
   class" might be operationalised through occupation, using a scale such as the National
   Statistics Socio-economic Classification (NS-SEC). Every operational definition is a choice, and a
   different indicator could change the findings.
5. **Sample.** Decide who to study (below).
6. **Pilot.** A small trial run checks that questions are understood, that the timing is realistic
   and that answers can be coded. Piloting improves both reliability and validity before money is
   spent on the full study.
7. **Collect, analyse and report** the data.

### Sampling

The **research population** is everyone the study is about; the **sampling frame** is the list from
which a sample is drawn (an electoral register, a school roll). A frame that misses groups produces
an unrepresentative sample however carefully the sample is then chosen.

| Technique | How it works | Main issue |
|---|---|---|
| Simple random | Every member of the frame has an equal chance of selection | Small samples can still be unrepresentative by chance |
| Systematic | Every *n*th name from the frame | Hidden patterns in the list can bias it |
| Stratified random | Frame divided into groups (age, sex, class) and each group sampled in proportion | Needs detailed information about the population |
| Quota | Interviewers fill set numbers in each category, choosing whom to ask | Not random; interviewers pick approachable people |
| Snowball | Each participant introduces the next | Useful for hidden groups; members share networks, so it is unrepresentative |
| Opportunity | Whoever is available | Quick but rarely generalisable |

**Representativeness** (does the sample mirror the population?) is what allows
**generalisation** (claiming the findings apply beyond the sample). Positivists treat both as
essential. Interpretivists accept smaller, unrepresentative samples in exchange for depth.

## The methods in more depth

### Questionnaires

Closed questions give pre-coded answers that are quick to quantify; open questions let respondents
answer in their own words but are harder to compare. Postal and online questionnaires reach large,
scattered samples cheaply, but **response rates** can be low, and those who reply may differ from
those who do not, which damages representativeness. Interpretivists add that fixed categories
**impose the researcher's meanings** on respondents.

### Interviews

Interviews range from **structured** (a questionnaire read aloud), through **semi-structured**, to
**unstructured** (a guided conversation). **Group interviews** and focus groups show how views form
through interaction, but confident members can dominate.

The central problem is that an interview is itself a social interaction. **Interviewer bias** and
**social desirability** effects arise because respondents react to the interviewer's status, age,
gender or ethnicity, or give the answer they think is acceptable. William Labov's 1960s research on
Black American children's speech showed this sharply: a boy who said little in a formal interview
with an unfamiliar adult talked fluently when the setting was made relaxed and informal. Ann Oakley
(1981) argued that the "detached" interviewer ideal suits men's research styles, and that feminist
interviews should build rapport and reciprocity with women interviewees.

### Observation

Observation varies on two dimensions: **participant or non-participant**, and **overt or covert**.

| | Overt | Covert |
|---|---|---|
| **Participant** | Group knows and researcher joins in: William Foote Whyte's *Street Corner Society* (1943), helped by a key informant | Researcher joins in secretly: James Patrick's study of a Glasgow gang (1973) |
| **Non-participant** | Researcher watches openly, for example sitting at the back of a lesson | Researcher watches without being noticed, for example from a public space |

Overt observation is ethical but risks the **Hawthorne effect**: people change their behaviour
because they know they are being studied, a term taken from Elton Mayo's workplace studies at the
Western Electric Hawthorne plant. Covert observation avoids this but involves deception and makes
recording data difficult. Laud Humphreys' covert study *Tearoom Trade* (1970) remains the standard
example of the ethical dispute this creates. Researchers must also manage **getting in, staying in
and getting out** of a group, and the risk of over-identifying with it ("going native").

### Experiments

**Laboratory experiments** control variables to establish cause and effect, but the setting is
artificial and few social questions can be studied that way. **Field experiments** take place in
normal settings. Rosenthal and Jacobson (1968) told teachers that randomly chosen pupils were about
to "spurt" academically, then found many of those pupils made greater gains, evidence for a
self-fulfilling prophecy. Their method also shows the ethical cost: teachers were deceived and other
pupils may have received less attention. The **comparative method**, used by Durkheim in *Suicide*,
is sometimes described as a "thought experiment" carried out on existing data.

### Documents and official statistics

Documents may be public (government reports, media output) or personal (letters, diaries, blogs),
and current or historical. **Content analysis** counts how often themes appear, turning documents
into quantitative data; the Glasgow University Media Group used it in *Bad News* (1976) to study
television news. Official statistics are often divided into **hard** statistics, such as births and
deaths, which are fairly complete, and **soft** statistics, such as recorded crime, which depend on
decisions by the public and officials. Positivists see statistics as useful social facts;
interpretivists see them as social constructions; Marxists add that what is counted, and what is not,
reflects the interests of powerful groups.

## Practical, ethical and theoretical (PET) factors

Use the three headings to explain any choice of method:

- **Practical:** time and cost; funding bodies, which may set the topic and expect particular kinds
  of data; access and gatekeepers; the researcher's personal characteristics (a young researcher may
  fit into a youth group; an older one may not); the skills needed to analyse the data.
- **Ethical:** the British Sociological Association's guidance stresses informed consent,
  confidentiality and anonymity, avoiding harm, and particular care with vulnerable groups such as
  children. Covert research and deception need strong justification.
- **Theoretical:** **reliability** (would another researcher get the same results?), **validity**
  (do the data give a true picture?), **representativeness**, and the researcher's methodological
  perspective.

These factors interact. A topic such as domestic abuse is sensitive (ethical), hard to access
(practical) and needs valid, in-depth data (theoretical), which pushes the researcher towards
carefully conducted unstructured interviews despite their cost.

## Social facts, positivism and interpretivism

Durkheim told sociologists to "treat social facts as things": external, measurable and
constraining. That instruction is what makes quantitative methods attractive to positivists.
Interpretivists question the "nature of social facts" itself: a suicide rate or crime rate exists
only because people define and record acts in particular ways, so the sociologist must study those
definitions. **Realists** take a middle position, arguing that underlying structures are real even
when they cannot be observed directly.

## Theory and methods together

Choice of method is rarely purely theoretical. Many researchers use **methodological pluralism** or
**triangulation**, combining, for example, a questionnaire to establish patterns with unstructured
interviews to explore meanings. Paul Willis's *Learning to Labour* (1977) combined observation,
interviews and group discussions to study a small group of working-class boys.

## Worked example: a 10-mark answer plan

*Outline and explain two problems sociologists may face when using laboratory experiments.* (Original
practice question.)

1. **Artificiality and validity.** Laboratory settings strip away the normal context of behaviour,
   and people who know they are being tested may act differently (a demand characteristic or
   Hawthorne-type effect). Develop: so findings may not generalise to real life, which is why
   interpretivists see them as low in validity for studying meaningful action.
2. **Ethical limits and narrow scope.** Many social causes, such as poverty or family breakdown,
   cannot ethically or practically be manipulated, and deception may be needed to avoid participants
   guessing the aim. Develop: so laboratory experiments can only address small, short-term questions,
   while sociologists turn to field experiments or the comparative method for larger ones.

Each point is identified, explained, and developed with a consequence.

## Common mistakes

- Answering the Paper 1 Theory and Methods question as if it were Methods in Context, or vice versa.
- Confusing reliability with validity.
- Calling quota sampling "random".
- Treating "covert" and "participant" as the same thing: they are separate dimensions.
- Listing PET factors without saying which way they push the choice of method.

## Exam technique

"Outline and explain" requires two separate reasons, each stated clearly, explained, and developed
with a consequence or example.
