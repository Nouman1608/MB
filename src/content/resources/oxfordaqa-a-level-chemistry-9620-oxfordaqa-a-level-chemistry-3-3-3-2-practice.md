This set practises **3.3.3.2 Elimination** from section 3.3.3 Halogenoalkanes of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, assessed in Unit 2 and in the synoptic Unit 5. Revise first with the [halogenoalkane elimination study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-3-2/) or the [halogenoalkane elimination revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-3-2-revision-notes/). Take Ar values as H = 1.0, C = 12.0, O = 16.0, K = 39.1 and Br = 79.9.

## Section A: short questions

**1.** In the elimination of HBr from a bromoalkane by hydroxide ions, the hydroxide ion acts as

A: an electrophile

B: a base

C: an acid

D: a free radical **[1]**

**2.** State the solvent and temperature conditions that favour elimination rather than substitution when a halogenoalkane reacts with potassium hydroxide. **[2]**

**3.** Write an equation for the elimination reaction of bromocyclohexane, C₆H₁₁Br, with potassium hydroxide. **[1]**

**4.** Name the only alkene formed when 2-bromo-2-methylpropane, (CH₃)₃CBr, undergoes elimination. **[1]**

**5.** Explain why 1-bromobutane forms only one alkene by elimination, but 2-bromobutane forms three. **[3]**

**6.** State the name of the substitution product formed when 2-bromopropane reacts with warm aqueous potassium hydroxide. **[1]**

## Section B: structured questions

**7.** 2-Bromopropane reacts with potassium hydroxide. Substitution and elimination happen concurrently.

**(a)** Explain, in terms of electron pairs, the role of the hydroxide ion in each reaction. **[2]**

**(b)** Outline the mechanism for the elimination reaction. **[3]**

**(c)** Outline the mechanism for the substitution reaction. **[2]**

**8.** 2-Chloro-3-methylbutane, CH₃CHClCH(CH₃)₂, is heated under reflux with potassium hydroxide in ethanol.

**(a)** Name the two alkenes that form. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why neither alkene shows E/Z isomerism. **[2]**

**(c)** Name the substitution product that forms in smaller amounts. **[1]**

**9.** 2-Bromopentane, CH₃CHBrCH₂CH₂CH₃, is heated with potassium hydroxide.

**(a)** Name every alkene formed and explain why there are this many. **[4]**

**(b)** 7.55 g of 2-bromopentane (Mr 150.9) reacts completely. 2.20 g of pentan-2-ol (Mr 88.0) is formed and all the remaining 2-bromopentane forms alkenes (C₅H₁₀). Calculate the total mass of alkenes formed. **[3]**

**10.** A student wants to make 2-methylpropene from 2-bromo-2-methylpropane.

(CH₃)₃CBr + KOH → (CH₃)₂C=CH₂ + H₂O + KBr

**(a)** Calculate the percentage atom economy of this reaction. **[2]**

**(b)** State **two** changes to the conditions that would increase the proportion of 2-methylpropene compared with the alcohol. **[2]**

**11.** A student draws this mechanism for the elimination of HBr from bromoethane: an arrow from the C–Br bond to Br; an arrow from the oxygen of OH⁻ to the carbon bonded to bromine; and no other arrows. Explain what is wrong and describe the correct arrows. **[4]**

**12.** Compound P, C₄H₉Br, reacts with hot ethanolic potassium hydroxide to give a single alkene that shows no E/Z isomerism. With warm aqueous potassium hydroxide, P gives a tertiary alcohol. Deduce the structure and name of P, and name the alkene. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]

**2.** Ethanol as the solvent (potassium hydroxide dissolved in ethanol) [1]; hot / heat under reflux [1]

**3.** **C₆H₁₁Br + KOH → C₆H₁₀ + H₂O + KBr** [1]

**4.** **2-Methylpropene**, (CH₃)₂C=CH₂ [1]

**5.** In 1-bromobutane the C–Br carbon has only one adjacent carbon, so C=C can only form between C1 and C2 [1]; in 2-bromobutane H can be removed from C1 or from C3, giving but-1-ene or but-2-ene [1]; but-2-ene has two different groups on each double-bond carbon, so it forms as E and Z isomers, making three alkenes [1]

**6.** **Propan-2-ol** [1]

**7. (a)** Substitution: OH⁻ is a nucleophile, donating a lone pair to the δ+ carbon bonded to bromine [1]; elimination: OH⁻ is a base, using a lone pair to remove H⁺ from a carbon adjacent to the C–Br carbon [1]

**(b)** Arrow from a lone pair on O of OH⁻ to an H on C1 (or C3) [1]; arrow from that C–H bond to the C1–C2 bond [1]; arrow from the C–Br bond to Br, with propene, H₂O and Br⁻ as products [1]

**(c)** Arrow from a lone pair on O of OH⁻ to the δ+ C2 [1]; arrow from the C–Br bond to Br, giving propan-2-ol and Br⁻ [1]

**8. (a)** **3-Methylbut-1-ene**, CH₂=CHCH(CH₃)₂ [1]; **2-methylbut-2-ene**, (CH₃)₂C=CHCH₃ [1]

**(b)** In 3-methylbut-1-ene, C1 carries two identical H atoms [1]; in 2-methylbut-2-ene, C2 carries two identical CH₃ groups [1]

**(c)** **3-Methylbutan-2-ol** [1]

**9. (a)** **Pent-1-ene** [1]; **(E)-pent-2-ene** and **(Z)-pent-2-ene** [1]; H can be lost from C1 or C3, which are not equivalent, giving two positions for C=C [1]; pent-2-ene has two different groups on each double-bond carbon, so it has E and Z isomers [1]

**(b)** n(2-bromopentane) = 7.55 ÷ 150.9 = 0.0500 mol and n(pentan-2-ol) = 2.20 ÷ 88.0 = 0.0250 mol [1]; n(alkenes) = 0.0500 − 0.0250 = 0.0250 mol [1]; Mr(C₅H₁₀) = 70.0, so mass = 0.0250 × 70.0 = **1.75 g** [1]
*Examiner insight:* All the alkene isomers have the same Mr, so they can be treated together in the calculation.

**10. (a)** Mr: (CH₃)₃CBr = 136.9; KOH = 56.1; (CH₃)₂C=CH₂ = 56.0 [1]; atom economy = 56.0 ÷ 193.0 × 100 = **29.0 %** [1]

**(b)** Use ethanol instead of water as the solvent [1]; use a higher temperature (heat under reflux) [1]

**11.** The OH⁻ arrow goes to the carbon, which would be substitution, not elimination [1]; the correct first arrow goes from a lone pair on O of OH⁻ to an H on the CH₃ carbon (the adjacent carbon) [1]; an arrow is missing from that C–H bond to the C–C bond, forming C=C [1]; the C–Br bond to Br arrow is correct, and the products are ethene, water and Br⁻ [1] **[4]**

**12.** A tertiary alcohol forms, so the C–Br carbon is bonded to three other carbons [1]; P is **2-bromo-2-methylpropane**, (CH₃)₃CBr [1]; its three adjacent carbons are equivalent CH₃ groups, so only **2-methylpropene** forms, and C1 has two H atoms so there is no E/Z isomerism [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Link each piece of evidence to a deduction; a bare name earns only one mark.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Hydroxide arrow drawn to carbon in an elimination mechanism.
- Second arrow (C–H bond into C–C) missing.
- "Ethanolic" and "aqueous" swapped.
- E/Z isomers of pent-2-ene or but-2-ene left out.
- Water omitted from elimination equations.
