## Section A

**1.** State **two** items that make up prime cost. **[2]**

**2.** State whether each cost is a direct cost, a factory overhead, or not part of the manufacturing account.

**(a)** Wages of a machine operator **[1]**

**(b)** Rent of the factory **[1]**

**(c)** Salaries of office staff **[1]**

**(d)** Royalties payable on each unit made **[1]**

**3.** Work in progress was 3,200 at the start of the year and 2,700 at the end. Explain how this affects the cost of production. **[2]**

**4.** Raw materials were 5,000 at the start of the year and 4,400 at the end. Purchases were 31,000 and carriage inwards was 600. Direct wages were 22,000 and direct expenses 1,800. Calculate the cost of raw materials used and the prime cost. **[4]**

**5.** Rent of 24,000 is shared by floor area between the factory (400 square metres), the office (100) and the showroom (100). Calculate the amount charged to each. **[3]**

**6.** Explain why depreciation of factory machinery is included in factory overheads, but depreciation of the office computer is not. **[2]**

## Section B

Rent of 15,000 and lighting of 4,000 are shared between the factory and the office. The factory uses 80% of each.

Prepare the manufacturing account for the year ended 30 September 2026, showing prime cost, factory overheads and the production cost of goods completed. **[14]**

**8.** Using your answer to Question 7, and assuming goods are transferred to the trading account at cost, calculate the gross profit. Sales were 150,000, finished goods at 1 October 2025 were 11,000 and finished goods at 30 September 2026 were 12,500. **[4]**

**9.** Dunmore Tools now transfers finished goods to the trading account at the production cost plus 20%. Finished goods are valued at transfer price: 12,000 at 1 October 2025 and 15,000 at 30 September 2026.

**(a)** Calculate the manufacturing profit for the year. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the provision for unrealised profit at each date and the amount to be charged to gross profit for the year. **[3]**

**(c)** Explain why the provision is needed. **[2]**

**10.** Evaluate whether a manufacturer should transfer goods to the trading account at a price above cost. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two: direct materials (raw materials used) [1]; direct labour (direct wages) [1]; direct expenses (such as royalties) [1].

**2. (a)** Direct cost (direct labour) [1]

**(b)** Factory overhead [1]

**(c)** Not part of the manufacturing account (an expense in the statement of profit or loss) [1]

**(d)** Direct cost (direct expense) [1]

**3.** Work in progress fell by 500 [1], so the opening 3,200 is added and the closing 2,700 is deducted, which increases the production cost by 500 [1].

**4.** Cost of raw materials used 5,000 + 31,000 + 600 − 4,400 [1] = **32,200** [1]; prime cost 32,200 + 22,000 + 1,800 [1] = **56,000** [1].

**5.** Total area 600 square metres [1]; factory 24,000 × 400 ÷ 600 = **16,000** [1]; office and showroom 24,000 × 100 ÷ 600 = **4,000** each [1].

**6.** The machinery is used to make the goods, so its depreciation is part of the cost of production [1]; the office computer is used for administration, so its depreciation is an expense in the statement of profit or loss [1].

Raw materials used **49,900** [2]; direct wages and royalties [1]; prime cost **83,900** [1]; indirect wages, power, insurance and depreciation [2]; rent and lighting apportioned at 80% [2]; factory overheads **37,600** [1]; total 121,500 [1]; work in progress adjustments [2]; production cost **121,000** [1]; correct heading [1].

**8.** Cost of sales 11,000 + 121,000 − 12,500 [2] = **119,500** [1]; gross profit 150,000 − 119,500 = **30,500** [1].

**9. (a)** Transfer price 121,000 × 120% = 145,200 [1]; manufacturing profit 145,200 − 121,000 = **24,200** [1].

**(b)** Closing provision 15,000 × 20 ÷ 120 = **2,500** [1]; opening provision 12,000 × 20 ÷ 120 = **2,000** [1]; increase **500**, deducted from gross profit [1].

**(c)** The closing finished goods are valued at a price that includes profit on goods not yet sold [1]; prudence says profit should not be recognised until the goods are sold, so profit and inventory would otherwise be overstated [1].

**10.** For a transfer price above cost: it shows the profit made by the factory as separate from the profit made by selling [1], so the owner can compare factory performance with the cost of buying from outside suppliers [1].
Against: the manufacturing profit is not realised until the goods are sold [1]; the provision adds work and the transfer price may be arbitrary [1]; the combined profit is the same as when goods are transferred at cost, so total profit is not changed [1].
Judgement: transfer at a price above cost is useful for management information when there are separate departments or an outside market price, but it is unnecessary for a small business, and the unrealised profit must always be removed [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Adding closing inventory and work in progress instead of deducting them (Questions 4 and 7).
- Putting indirect wages in prime cost.
- Apportioning rent and lighting on the wrong fraction (Question 7).
- Using 20% of the transfer price instead of 20/120 for unrealised profit (Question 9).
- Charging the whole provision rather than the increase.
- Including office expenses in the manufacturing account.

## Approaching these questions

Make three lists: direct costs, factory overheads and costs that stay in the statement of profit or loss. Apportion the shared costs before you start. Work down the account with a subtotal for each section, then add or deduct work in progress. For unrealised profit, find the profit fraction first (mark-up divided by 100 plus mark-up).

**Is work in progress the same as finished goods?**
No. Work in progress is partly made. Finished goods are complete but unsold.
