## The brief in five phrases

| Phrase in the subject brief | What it asks you to do |
|---|---|
| "the creativity of writers and readers" | Treat reading as making meaning, not receiving it |
| "the critical perspectives of others" | Read with, and against, other readers' lenses |
| "shaped by cultural belief systems" | Ask where a reading comes from, including your own |
| "negotiate meanings for texts" | Reach a reasoned position that keeps what holds up |
| "interpretation, analysis and evaluation" | Judge readings, not just produce them |

The brief prints no numbered list of concepts and names no school of criticism. Use only the ideas it
names; your teacher will share the full guide's wording.

## Concepts at a glance

| Concept | One question | Look for |
|---|---|---|
| Creativity | What did the writer make, and what must the reader make? | Gaps, invented forms, images that need decoding |
| Perspective | Whose view, whose silence, and where do I read from? | Narrator, speaker, who is described but never speaks |
| Culture | Which beliefs are assumed, which questioned? | Customs, religion, class, what counts as normal |
| Transformation | What does the text change? | Reworked sources, shifting forms, a reader's changed view |
| Communication | How does the text reach its reader? How will I argue my reading? | Address, tone, clarity of your own thesis |

A concept is a question you bring to any text. A theme is what one text is about.

## Lenses at a glance

| Lens | Key question | First things to look for |
|---|---|---|
| Formalist | How do form and language make meaning? | Repetition, sound, structure, turns |
| Reader-response | What does the reader do, and why may readers differ? | Ambiguity, withheld information |
| Historical and biographical | What could it mean then and there? | Period details, the writer's circumstances |
| Marxist (class) | How are money, work and power shown? | Owners, workers, wages, institutions |
| Feminist (gender) | How are gender and power shown? | Who speaks, who is looked at, who decides |
| Postcolonial | How is empire shown, and whose voices count? | Centre and margin, language, land |
| Ecocritical | How are nature and its use shown? | Land, animals, weather, who owns a place |

The lens belongs to the reader, not the writer. Choose the lens the evidence invites.

## Method in steps

**Building a reading**

```
1  Notice   a detail: word, image, pattern, shift
2  Ask      the question it raises
3  Pick     the lens the detail invites
4  Claim    something arguable, with a concept in it
5  Test     against the whole text and a rival reading
6  Write    clearly and tentatively
```

**Evaluating a reading: five tests**

```
Evidence         precise quotations, not impressions?
Coverage         accounts for details that do not fit?
Coherence        its parts agree with each other?
Context          plausible for its time, or openly read through today's?
Counter-reading  names and answers a serious alternative?
```

An unusual reading is not wrong. A reading that fails these tests is weak.

## Worked reminder

**Passage (original text written for Marlbridge):**

> **Ferry Terminal, 6 a.m.**
>
> The cleaners leave as the travellers arrive.\
> One of them waves to a boat she will never board.\
> The departures board flicks through cities in gold:\
> Lisbon, Tangier, Genoa. She reads them aloud\
> the way her mother read the names of saints,\
> then clocks out, and the sea keeps all of them.

**Class reading.** "The cleaners leave as the travellers arrive": two groups share a building but
never meet. The phrases "a boat she will never board" and "clocks out" show that her work serves journeys she
cannot afford. The cities are "in gold", the colour of money.

**Culture reading.** She reads the names "the way her mother read the names of saints". A religious
habit passed from mother to daughter becomes a way of longing. The city names are transformed into
a prayer.

**Evaluation.** Both readings have precise evidence. The class reading covers lines 1--4 well but
says little about the mother. The culture reading explains line 5 but not why she will "never
board". The last clause, "the sea keeps all of them", fits both: the places stay out of reach, like
the saints.

**Negotiated reading.** The poem shows longing shaped by both money and inheritance: she cannot buy
the journey, so she prays it instead.

## Connecting two texts with one lens

Assessment objective 2 includes "relationships among different texts". A lens or concept gives two
works a shared question, so the comparison has an axis:

```
1  Choose one question   e.g. who decides how a person is seen?
2  Answer it for work 1  with a quotation
3  Answer it for work 2  with a quotation
4  State the difference  and what causes it (form, context, culture)
5  Say what it reveals   that neither work shows alone
```

For a work studied in translation, add the concept of culture: its first readers may have shared
beliefs that you do not.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Concept vs theme.** "Perspective" is a question; "exile" is a subject a text may be about.
- **Interpretation vs summary.** A summary says what happens. An interpretation argues what it
  means and shows how.
- **Lens vs label.** "A class reading suggests..." is analysis. "This is a Marxist poem" is a label.
- **Context of production vs context of reception.** When and where a text was written, as against
  when and where you, or other readers, read it.
- **Perspective in the text vs perspective on the text.** The narrator's point of view is inside the
  work. A critical perspective is the reader's frame.
- **Tentative vs vague.** "This suggests the speaker resents..." commits to a claim. "It could mean
  many things" does not.
- **Evaluating vs disagreeing.** Evaluating applies tests and keeps what survives. Disagreeing just
  swaps one opinion for another.

## Useful phrasing

- "Read through a class lens, the detail ... suggests ..."
- "This reading accounts for ..., but it struggles to explain ..."
- "A reader today may see ...; readers in [the writer's period] may have seen ..."
- "Both readings are supported, but the second covers more of the text because ..."
- "Taken together, these readings suggest ..."

## Quick self-test

1. Quote the brief's phrase about what readers and writers share.
2. Give the question the concept of perspective asks of a text.
3. State the difference between a concept and a theme.
4. Name the five tests for evaluating an interpretation.
5. Which lens asks who is looked at and who decides?
6. A student writes: "Blake was a Marxist." What is wrong with this?
7. Which test does a reading fail if it ignores the last stanza?
8. Explain "context of reception" in one sentence.
9. What does the brief say readers explore about how interpretations are shaped?
10. In "Ferry Terminal, 6 a.m.", which phrase best supports a class reading, and why?

### Answers

1. "the creativity of writers and readers".
2. Whose view does the text give, whose is missing, and from where does the reader read?
3. A concept is a question you can bring to any text; a theme is what a particular text is about.
4. Evidence, coverage, coherence, context, counter-reading.
5. Feminist (gender).
6. The lens belongs to the reader, not the writer; Marx was born in 1818, after "London" (1794).
   Say "a Marxist reading suggests...".
7. Coverage.
8. The time, place and beliefs of the people reading a text, which may differ from those of its
   first readers.
9. "how such positions are shaped by cultural belief systems".
10. "a boat she will never board": her work serves travel she cannot have. ("clocks out" also earns
    credit.)

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a lens ("a feminist reading") and then summarising the plot, with no analysis of language.
- Using a lens the text gives no evidence for, so the argument rests on assertion.
- Presenting one reading as the only meaning, with no rival reading tested.
- Mentioning a critic's view as proof instead of weighing it against the text.
- Writing "it is open to interpretation" in place of a claim.
- Judging a pre-1900 text by present-day values without saying that is what you are doing.
- Treating a concept as a theme: "the theme is transformation", with nothing about what changes.
- In comparison, applying a lens to one work only, so the two works never meet.
