## What is social control?

**Social control** is the set of ways in which a society encourages people to follow its norms and
discourages them from breaking them. Socialisation teaches norms; social control keeps people to them.
The two overlap, because many agencies (families, schools, religions) do both.

## Formal and informal control

| | Informal social control | Formal social control |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Unofficial pressure to conform, through everyday relationships | Official, written rules enforced by organisations with authority |
| Who exercises it | Families, schools (in everyday interaction), peers, media, religion, workplace | Government and laws, police and other enforcement agencies, criminal justice systems |
| Rules | Unwritten norms | Written laws and regulations |
| Typical sanctions | A disapproving look, gossip, praise, exclusion from a group | Fines, arrest, imprisonment, official awards |

Some agencies use both. A school's written rules and its system of detentions and exclusions are
formal within the school; a teacher's praise or a classmate's teasing is informal. A workplace has
written disciplinary procedures (formal) and colleagues' expectations (informal).

## Sanctions

A **sanction** is a reward or punishment that encourages conformity.

| | Positive (reward) | Negative (punishment) |
|---|---|---|
| **Informal** | Praise, a smile, popularity, being included | A frown, teasing, gossip, being ignored |
| **Formal** | A certificate, a promotion, a medal, a merit | A fine, a detention, dismissal, a prison sentence |

Exam answers gain marks for precise examples in the correct box: a detention is a formal negative
sanction within a school, not an informal one.

## Agencies of informal control

- **Families:** parents reward and punish children's behaviour; adult relatives may pressure members
  over marriage, careers or reputation.
- **Schools:** beyond written rules, teachers' approval and the hidden curriculum encourage
  punctuality, obedience and competition.
- **Peers:** the desire to fit in leads to conformity to group norms, which may support or oppose
  wider norms.
- **Media:** news and entertainment present some behaviours as normal and others as shameful; online
  shaming can act as a powerful negative sanction.
- **Religion:** moral codes, the approval of a religious community, and beliefs about reward and
  punishment.
- **Workplace:** promotion, bonuses and the opinion of colleagues; the threat of losing a job.

## Agencies of formal control

- **Government and laws:** the state creates laws that define what is criminal and sets the
  punishments.
- **Police and other enforcement agencies:** police, customs and border agencies, and regulators
  enforce laws and investigate breaches.
- **Criminal justice systems:** courts decide guilt and sentences; prisons and probation services
  carry them out.

## Consensus and conflict views

A key debate is **whose interests** social control serves.

- **Consensus view:** society is based on shared values, and social control protects them for the
  benefit of everyone. Laws reflect what most people agree is right.
- **Conflict views:** society is divided by unequal power, and social control serves the interests of
  powerful groups, whether a ruling class (Marxism) or men (feminism).

## Four perspectives on social control

### Functionalist

Functionalists take the consensus view. **Durkheim** argued that shared values, which he called the
collective conscience, hold society together. Punishing those who break the rules reinforces those
values and reminds everyone where the boundaries of acceptable behaviour lie. Social control is
therefore necessary and beneficial.

*Evaluation:* it assumes laws reflect everyone's values and ignores whose views shape them.

### Marxist

Marxists take a conflict view: social control maintains the power of the ruling class. **Althusser**
distinguished **repressive state apparatuses**, such as the police, courts and army, which control by
force, from **ideological state apparatuses**, such as education, the media, religion and the family,
which control by shaping beliefs so that people accept inequality as normal. Marxists also argue that
laws protecting private property are enforced more strictly than laws regulating the powerful.

*Evaluation:* it underplays laws that protect everyone, such as those against violence, and ignores
inequalities other than class.

### Feminist

Feminists argue that social control reflects patriarchy. **Frances Heidensohn**, in *Women and Crime*
(1985), argued that women are controlled more closely than men at home (through domestic
responsibilities), in public (through fear of violence and concern for reputation) and at work
(through male supervisors and harassment). She used this to explain why women commit less recorded
crime.

*Evaluation:* women's experiences vary by class, ethnicity and society, and control over women has
changed in many places.

### Social action and interactionist

Interactionists focus on how rules are made and applied in everyday interaction. **Howard Becker**,
in *Outsiders* (1963), argued that deviance is not a quality of an act but the result of others
successfully applying a label to it. Rules are created by groups he called **moral entrepreneurs**,
and they are enforced selectively: the same act may be ignored in one person and punished in another.

*Evaluation:* it explains how control is applied but says less about where the power to label comes
from.

## Worked example 1: a short-answer question

*Explain the difference between formal and informal social control, using an example of each.
[4 marks]*

> Formal social control is exercised by official agencies using written rules (1), such as the police
> arresting someone for theft (1). Informal social control is unofficial pressure from everyday
> relationships to follow unwritten norms (1), such as friends teasing someone who breaks the group's
> dress code (1).

## Worked example 2: planning an extended answer

*Discuss how far social control benefits everyone in society. Use examples from your own society or a
society you know about. [12 marks]*

- **Consensus case:** Durkheim, shared values, boundary maintenance; laws against violence protect
  all.
- **Conflict case:** Althusser's state apparatuses; property laws; selective enforcement (Becker);
  Heidensohn on the control of women.
- **Application:** a named society, for example how its laws on public protest or dress are made and
  enforced.
- **Judgement:** some control benefits everyone, but the way rules are made and enforced often
  reflects unequal power.

## Common mistakes

- Putting a formal sanction in the informal box, or the reverse.
- Treating schools as only informal agencies; they also have formal rules within the institution.
- Listing agencies without explaining **how** each controls behaviour.
- Naming Althusser or Heidensohn without their argument.
- Forgetting that consensus and conflict are the overarching views, with perspectives inside them.
- No example from a named society in an extended answer.
