## What an operations strategy is

An **operations strategy** is a long-term plan for how the operations function will use its resources to support the objectives of the whole business. It turns corporate aims, such as growth, low prices or a reputation for quality, into decisions about what to make, how, where and with what resources.

It sits within a hierarchy. Corporate objectives come first; the functional strategies (operations, marketing, finance, human resources) support them. The operations strategy should be consistent with the others: a strategy of fast growth needs finance for capacity; a high-quality brand needs skilled staff and strict quality systems.

## What it contains

An operations strategy is a set of linked decisions. These are the main ones, each of which has its own heading in the specification.

| Decision area | Typical choices |
|---|---|
| Production method and process | Job, batch, flow; lean methods; scale of output |
| Capacity | How much, and how to change it; utilisation targets |
| Location and logistics | Where to produce; in-house or external delivery; offshoring or re-shoring |
| Technology | What to automate; how to use data and digital systems |
| Stock control | Just in time or buffer stock; reorder methods |
| Quality | Control and assurance; TQM; external standards; benchmarking |
| Productivity and efficiency | Targets; training; economies of scale |
| People | Skills, training and flexibility, working with the human resources strategy |
| Suppliers | Choice, contracts and relationships |

A common way to express the aim of the strategy is through five **performance objectives**: cost, quality, speed, dependability and flexibility. The specification does not name them, but they help structure an answer. A business seldom excels at all five, because they conflict. Very low cost may limit flexibility; very high quality usually raises cost. The strategy chooses which to put first.

## Why a strategy matters

- **Direction.** It links day-to-day operations decisions to the long-term goals.
- **Consistency.** Decisions on capacity, technology and location reinforce each other rather than pulling apart.
- **Competitive advantage.** Lower cost, better quality, faster delivery or greater flexibility can be a reason for customers to choose the business.
- **Resource use.** Investment is directed to what matters most.
- **Response to change.** A strategy considers external influences and prepares responses.
- **Measurement.** It sets targets that can be monitored.

Limits: a strategy based on forecasts can be wrong; it can be rigid if the market changes quickly; implementation depends on the people who must carry it out; and strategy takes time and money to develop.

### Worked example 1: comparing two strategies using expected value

Westbrook Furniture (invented) has been losing sales to cheap imports. The directors consider two operations strategies over the next five years.

**Strategy A: cost leadership.** Invest £400,000 in automation and in moving some production overseas. If it works fully, annual savings are £130,000. If it only partly works, savings are £50,000. The directors estimate a 0.6 chance of full success and 0.4 of partial success.

**Strategy B: quality and flexibility.** Invest £150,000 in design software and staff training to offer made-to-order furniture of high quality, with short lead times. If it works fully, extra annual profit is £60,000. If it works only partly, £25,000. The chance of full success is 0.8 and of partial success 0.2.

Expected annual benefit:

- A: (0.6 × £130,000) + (0.4 × £50,000) = £78,000 + £20,000 = **£98,000**.
- B: (0.8 × £60,000) + (0.2 × £25,000) = £48,000 + £5,000 = **£53,000**.

Over five years, net of investment:

- A: 5 × £98,000 − £400,000 = £490,000 − £400,000 = **£90,000**.
- B: 5 × £53,000 − £150,000 = £265,000 − £150,000 = **£115,000**.

Expected payback: A £400,000 ÷ £98,000 = 4.1 years; B £150,000 ÷ £53,000 = 2.8 years.

If both strategies succeed fully, A gives 5 × £130,000 − £400,000 = £250,000 and B gives 5 × £60,000 − £150,000 = £150,000. A has the higher best case, but B has the higher expected result because of its lower cost and higher chance of success.

**Evaluation.**

- On the expected figures B is better, and its payback is shorter.
- A is riskier: a large sum is committed and success is less certain. Its worst case (partial success) gives 5 × £50,000 − £400,000 = −£150,000, a loss.
- The estimates are subjective, and a small change in probability could reverse the result.
- Non-financial effects matter. A offshoring plan would put UK jobs at risk and lengthen lead times. B develops staff skills, which may raise motivation, but depends on customers valuing made-to-order furniture.
- Fit with objectives: if the directors want to protect high margins and the brand, B fits; if they want volume and price competition, A fits.

**Recommendation.** Westbrook should choose Strategy B, because the expected return is higher, the risk is lower and it fits the strengths of a UK maker, provided market research confirms that customers will pay for quality and speed. It could keep A's ideas as later steps, such as automating the simple processes once B is working.

## Implementing and monitoring

An operations strategy needs targets (key performance indicators), regular measurement and corrective action.

| Measure | Target | Actual | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost per unit | £42.00 | £44.10 | £2.10 above, 5.0% |
| Orders delivered on time | 96% | 92% | 4 percentage points short |
| Defect rate | 1.5% | 2.1% | 0.6 percentage points above |

The cost gap is 2.10 ÷ 42.00 × 100 = 5.0%. The figures show that the strategy is not yet delivering on any measure. Managers should find the causes (for example, supplier delays or new machine teething problems) before changing the strategy itself. A strategy should be reviewed when external conditions change, such as a new law, a rise in costs or a rival's new technology.

## Stakeholders

| Stakeholder | Possible benefit | Possible cost |
|---|---|---|
| Owners | Higher profit and a stronger market position | Large investment; risk of failure |
| Employees | Training, skills, secure jobs in a growing business | Redundancy, relocation or pressure to improve |
| Customers | Better quality, speed, price or choice | Higher prices or reduced choice, depending on strategy |
| Suppliers | Larger or long-term contracts | Pressure on price and standards |
| Community | Local jobs; less waste | Closures, traffic or pollution |

## Recommending an operations strategy

1. **Start from the objectives.** What is the business trying to achieve?
2. **Identify the priority performance objective.** Cost, quality, speed, dependability or flexibility.
3. **Choose consistent decisions.** Match production method, capacity, technology, location and quality to that priority.
4. **Count the cost and risk.** Use payback or expected value if figures are given.
5. **Consider the external environment.** Which laws, costs and competitors could change the plan?
6. **Consider people.** Training, consultation and the effect on jobs.
7. **Set measures and review dates.**
8. **Justify** with evidence from the case, and say what the recommendation depends on.

## Common mistakes

- Repeating a list of operations decisions without linking them to the business's objectives.
- Recommending a strategy that conflicts with the objectives, such as cost-cutting for a luxury brand.
- Treating the strategy as only about cost.
- Ignoring risk and the cost of implementation.
- Forgetting that people carry out the strategy.
- Not saying what the recommendation depends on.

## Exam technique

Open with the objectives of the business, name the priority, and choose a small number of linked operations decisions. Use figures from the case, calculate payback or expected value if you can, and show the risks. Finish with a clear recommendation and its conditions.
