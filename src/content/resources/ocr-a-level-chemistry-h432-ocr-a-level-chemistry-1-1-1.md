This guide goes deeper into **1.1.1 Planning** in OCR A Level Chemistry A (H432), Version 3.1 (May 2026), for first assessment in 2017. Section 1.1 skills are assessed in the written papers, so a planning question can be set on any chemistry in the course. H432 is the full A level, and the section has no tiers.

The [practical skills in the written examination guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-chemistry-practical-skills-written-examination/) covers 1.1.1 to 1.1.4 together. This page covers planning alone: what a complete plan contains, how to size quantities so readings fit the apparatus, how to choose a range and number of values for the independent variable, and how to judge whether a proposed method can meet its aim. Condense it with the [1.1.1 revision notes](/resources/ocr-a-level-chemistry-h432-ocr-a-level-chemistry-1-1-1-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [1.1.1 practice questions](/resources/ocr-a-level-chemistry-h432-ocr-a-level-chemistry-1-1-1-practice/).

The additional guidance for (a) adds "selection of suitable apparatus, equipment and techniques for the proposed experiment" and says you should apply scientific knowledge from the specification to the practical context. HSW3 is using appropriate methodology to answer scientific questions; HSW6 is evaluating methodology, evidence and data.

## What a complete plan contains

An examiner reading your plan should be able to carry it out without asking you anything. Check for each of these:

1. **Aim:** the quantity to be found or the relationship to be tested.
2. **Chemistry:** the equation, and any data needed (Mr, molar gas volume).
3. **Variables:** independent, dependent and controlled, each with how it is set or measured.
4. **Apparatus:** each item named with its size, for example "50 cm³ burette", "250.0 cm³ volumetric flask", "balance reading to 0.01 g".
5. **Quantities:** amounts or concentrations, with a calculation showing the readings will fit the apparatus.
6. **Method:** numbered steps in order, including repeats.
7. **Safety:** the main hazards and how the risk is reduced.
8. **Processing:** how the readings give the answer: a calculation, or a graph and what is read from it.

## Choosing apparatus

Choose each item for the size and precision of the measurement it makes.

| Measurement | Suitable choice | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Exact volume of a solution to react | Pipette | One fixed volume, measured precisely |
| Variable volume added to an end point | Burette | Reads any volume up to its capacity |
| Volume of a reagent in excess | Measuring cylinder | Precision is not needed |
| Making a standard solution | Volumetric flask | Gives one exact total volume |
| Gas produced | Gas syringe, or inverted burette or measuring cylinder over water | Over water only for gases that are not very soluble |
| Mass of a solid | Balance, weighing by difference | The amount transferred is what matters |
| Temperature change | Thermometer or probe with resolution suited to the change | A small change needs fine resolution |

**Apply the chemistry.** A gas that dissolves readily in water, such as ammonia, cannot be collected over water. A reaction that needs a weak acid titrated with a strong base needs an indicator that changes colour in the right pH range: phenolphthalein, not methyl orange.

## Sizing quantities

A plan that produces a reading off the end of the apparatus, or too small to measure well, fails 1.1.1(c). Always calculate the expected reading.

**Worked example 1: planning a vinegar titration.** You want the concentration of ethanoic acid in a vinegar, expected to be about 0.85 mol dm⁻³. You have 0.100 mol dm⁻³ sodium hydroxide, a 50 cm³ burette, a 25.0 cm³ pipette and a 250.0 cm³ volumetric flask.

```
CH₃COOH + NaOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O       (1 : 1)

undiluted: titre = 25.0 × 0.85 ÷ 0.100 = 212.5 cm³    (far beyond 50 cm³)

dilute 25.0 cm³ of vinegar to 250.0 cm³ (factor of 10):
diluted concentration ≈ 0.085 mol dm⁻³
titre = 25.0 × 0.085 ÷ 0.100 = 21.25 cm³               (fits well)
```

The plan: pipette 25.0 cm³ of vinegar into the volumetric flask, make up to the mark with distilled water and invert to mix; pipette 25.0 cm³ of the diluted solution into a conical flask; add phenolphthalein; titrate with NaOH until the first permanent pale pink; repeat to concordant titres; multiply the diluted concentration by 10.

**Worked example 2: a method that cannot work as planned.** A student plans to find the concentration of hydrochloric acid, about 1 mol dm⁻³, by adding excess magnesium to 25.0 cm³ of acid and collecting the hydrogen in a 100 cm³ gas syringe. Use a molar gas volume of 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹.

```
Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂
n(HCl) = 1.0 × 25.0/1000 = 0.025 mol
n(H₂)  = 0.0125 mol
volume = 0.0125 × 24 000 = 300 cm³      (three times the syringe capacity)
```

For 1.1.1(c), the method is not appropriate as planned. Volume of acid giving at most 100 cm³ of hydrogen: n(H₂) = 100 ÷ 24 000 = 4.17 × 10⁻³ mol, so n(HCl) = 8.33 × 10⁻³ mol, which is 8.33 cm³ of acid. Using 5.00 cm³ of acid from a pipette gives about 60 cm³, well within range.

## Variables and how to control them

The independent variable is what you change; the dependent variable is what you measure; controlled variables are everything else that could change the result. Statement 1.1.1(b) says "where appropriate": in a titration to find one concentration there is no independent variable, but the temperature and the indicator are still kept the same.

**Worked example 3: the effect of temperature on a reaction rate.** Sodium thiosulfate reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid to form a precipitate of sulfur:

Na₂S₂O₃(aq) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + S(s) + SO₂(g) + H₂O(l)

Time how long the precipitate takes to hide a cross under the flask; 1/time is a measure of rate.

| Variable | Type | How it is set or controlled |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | Independent | Warm the thiosulfate in a water bath; record the temperature of the mixture itself |
| Time for the cross to disappear | Dependent | Stopwatch, started as the acid is added |
| Concentration and volume of thiosulfate | Controlled | Same solution, same volume by measuring cylinder or burette |
| Concentration and volume of acid | Controlled | Same solution, same volume |
| Total volume, so the depth of liquid | Controlled | Same flask, same volumes |
| The cross and the observer | Controlled | Same printed cross, same person judging the end point |

**Range and number of values.** Use at least five temperatures across a wide range, such as about 20 °C to 60 °C in roughly 10 °C steps, so a graph shows the trend clearly. Repeat each temperature.

**Safety.** Sulfur dioxide is toxic and an irritant, so work in a well-ventilated room, do not inhale over the flask, and take care if anyone has asthma. Dispose of the mixture promptly.

## Judging a method: the 1.1.1(c) questions

When asked whether a method is appropriate to meet the expected outcomes, test it against four questions:

- **Right quantity?** Does it measure what the aim needs, rather than something related?
- **Right size?** Will readings fall within the apparatus range and be large enough for a small percentage uncertainty?
- **Losses or gains?** Can product, gas or heat escape, or can something extra be counted, such as acid spray from a flask on a balance?
- **Side reactions?** Could another reaction change the reading?

Give a reason for your verdict and, where the method fails, say what change would fix it.

## Annotated model answer

**Question.** Plan an experiment to determine the percentage by mass of calcium carbonate in a sample of limestone chips, by titration. Include quantities and show how the result is calculated.

**Model answer.**

1. Weigh by difference about 1.0 g of crushed limestone into a conical flask, recording the mass to 0.01 g. *(Quantity stated; crushing ensures complete reaction.)*
2. Pipette 50.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid into the flask; this is an excess, since 1.0 g of CaCO₃ (0.0100 mol) needs only 0.0200 mol HCl. *(Calculation shows excess.)*
3. When fizzing stops, transfer the mixture with washings to a 250.0 cm³ volumetric flask, make up to the mark and invert. *(Standard dilution technique.)*
4. Titrate 25.0 cm³ portions with 0.100 mol dm⁻³ sodium hydroxide, using methyl orange, to concordant titres. *(Expected titre: 0.0300 mol excess HCl ÷ 10 = 3.00 × 10⁻³ mol, which is 30.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH: fits the burette.)*
5. Processing: n(HCl) remaining = titre × 0.100 × 10; n(HCl) reacted = 0.0500 − remaining; n(CaCO₃) = reacted ÷ 2; mass = n × 100.1; percentage = mass ÷ sample mass × 100. *(Every step of the calculation given.)*

The answer names apparatus with sizes, justifies the quantities with calculations and ends with the processing. That is what turns a list of steps into a plan.

## Common mistakes

- Naming apparatus without its size or resolution.
- Giving no calculation to show readings will fit the apparatus.
- Writing "keep everything the same" instead of naming each controlled variable and how it is controlled.
- Measuring the water bath temperature instead of the reaction mixture.
- Choosing too few values of the independent variable, or a narrow range.
- Collecting a soluble gas over water.
- Forgetting the safety point that is specific to the reaction.
