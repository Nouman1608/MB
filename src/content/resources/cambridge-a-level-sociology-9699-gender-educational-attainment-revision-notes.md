These notes condense section 6.4, *Gender and educational attainment*, of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699), assessed on Paper 3 – Education. Four syllabus strands: gender socialisation and attainment; wider social changes, including changing female expectations and the crisis of masculinity; gender and subcultures; and teacher expectations and gendered classroom behaviour.

Full explanations: [gender and educational attainment study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-gender-educational-attainment/).

## Core argument

In many countries girls now outperform boys on average, a reversal within a generation that biology cannot explain. Wider social change explains *why* the gap reversed; socialisation, subcultures and classroom processes explain how it is maintained and why it varies by class and ethnicity. Subject choice remains strongly gendered.

## Three patterns to separate

| Pattern | Point |
|---|---|
| Overall attainment | Girls ahead on average in many countries |
| Subject choice | Still divided; boys more often in physics and computing, girls in languages and social sciences |
| Access | In some societies girls remain less likely to complete schooling |

## Gender socialisation

- Girls often encouraged towards cooperative behaviour and reading, which fits classroom norms.
- Boys' peer cultures may define studiousness as unmasculine.
- **Reay (2001):** "Spice Girls, nice girls, girlies and tomboys" — several femininities in one primary class.
- **Evaluation:** static; cannot explain change unless socialisation itself changed.

## Wider social changes

| Evidence | Finding |
|---|---|
| Sharpe, *Just Like a Girl* (1976; 1994) | Girls' priorities shifted from love, marriage, husbands and children to jobs, careers and independence |
| Wilkinson, *No Turning Back* (1994) | "Genderquake": young women's values shifted towards independence and paid work |
| Labour market | Growth in service and professional jobs open to women |
| Family | Rising divorce, later marriage — independence matters more |
| Policy and ideas | Equal opportunities; feminism reaching schools and media |

**Feminist split:** liberal feminists see real progress; radical feminists stress continuing patriarchy in subject routes and occupations.

## Crisis of masculinity

- Decline of manual work removes a breadwinner route needing few qualifications.
- **Mac an Ghaill, *The Making of Men* (1994):**
  - macho lads — anti-school, toughness;
  - academic achievers;
  - new enterprisers — newer vocational routes;
  - real Englishmen — middle-class, apparently effortless.
- **Evaluation:** risk of moral panic; middle-class boys still succeed; concern about boys may divert resources from girls.

## Gender and subcultures

- **Lees, *Sugar and Spice* (1993):** labels about sexual reputation control girls' behaviour, dress and friendships.
- Laddishness and "ladette" cultures (see overview).
- Subcultures link structure (class prospects) and agency (identity choices).

## Teacher expectations and classroom behaviour

- **Stanworth, *Gender and Schooling* (1983):** in a further education college, teachers found boys easier to remember and describe and underestimated girls; girls underestimated themselves.
- Boys receive more attention, but much of it is **disciplinary**.
- The paradox: classrooms favour boys' visibility yet girls achieve more — quiet, consistent work suits assessment, and expectations may have changed since older studies.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Gender socialisation | Learning norms and expectations attached to being male or female |
| Changing female expectations | Shift in girls' aims towards careers and independence |
| Genderquake | Wilkinson's term for a fundamental shift in young women's values |
| Crisis of masculinity | Uncertainty about male identity as manual work and breadwinning decline |
| Hegemonic masculinity | The dominant, most valued form of masculinity in a setting |
| Macho lads | Mac an Ghaill's anti-school, working-class boys |
| Moral panic | Exaggerated public concern about a group |
| Reputation labelling | Policing girls through labels about sexual behaviour (Lees) |
| Liberal feminism | Gradual progress through reform and equal opportunity |
| Radical feminism | Patriarchy persists despite reforms |

## Worked essay plan

*"Teacher expectations are the main reason for gender differences in attainment." Evaluate this view.*

1. Define teacher expectations; outline Stanworth (1983) and evidence on classroom attention.
2. Apply the paradox: if classrooms favour boys' visibility, expectations cannot easily explain girls' lead.
3. Alternatives: wider social change (Sharpe, Wilkinson); crisis of masculinity (Mac an Ghaill); peer subcultures (Lees, Reay).
4. Evaluate: older studies may not describe classrooms today; class and ethnicity cut across gender.
5. Judgement: expectations help explain subject choice and confidence more than overall attainment.

## Exam traps

- "Boys are failing" — most boys' results have also risen; the gap is relative.
- Treating the crisis of masculinity as fact, not debate.
- Ignoring subject choice.
- Biological explanations; stick to social causes.
- Treating girls (or boys) as one group; class and ethnicity cut across gender.
- Using old studies as if they describe classrooms today; date the evidence.

## Self-test

1. Give three distinct gender patterns in education.
2. Why does the reversal of the gap challenge biological explanations?
3. What did Sharpe find when she repeated her study?
4. Define "genderquake".
5. Name Mac an Ghaill's four masculinities.
6. What did Lees show about girls' subcultures?
7. What did Stanworth find about teachers' views of girls?
8. Explain the classroom attention paradox.
9. How do liberal and radical feminists interpret girls' improved results differently?
10. What did Reay (2001) show about girls in one primary class?
11. Give one criticism of the crisis of masculinity argument.

### Answers

1. Overall attainment (girls ahead in many countries), subject choice (still divided) and access (girls behind in some societies).
2. It happened within a generation, far too quickly for biological change.
3. Girls' priorities had shifted from love, marriage and children to jobs, careers and independence.
4. Wilkinson's term for a fundamental shift in young women's values towards independence and paid work.
5. Macho lads, academic achievers, new enterprisers and real Englishmen.
6. Labels about sexual reputation were used to control girls' behaviour, dress and friendships.
7. Teachers found boys easier to remember and describe and underestimated girls; girls also underestimated themselves.
8. Boys get more attention, yet girls achieve more; much attention is disciplinary, and girls' consistent work suits assessment.
9. Liberal feminists see real progress through reform; radical feminists argue patriarchy persists in subject routes and jobs.
10. Girls took several different femininities, so they were not one group.
11. It may be a moral panic: middle-class boys still succeed, and it may divert attention from girls' disadvantages.
