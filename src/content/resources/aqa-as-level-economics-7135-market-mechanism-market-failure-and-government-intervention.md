## 3.1.5.1 The three functions of prices

| Function | What it does | Example: a sudden rise in demand for electric bicycles |
|---|---|---|
| Signalling | Price changes tell buyers and sellers that conditions have changed | Rising prices show producers that consumers want more bicycles |
| Incentive | Higher prices raise potential profit, encouraging more supply | Firms expand output and new firms enter |
| Rationing | Higher prices limit demand to the quantity available | Buyers who value bicycles less drop out |

Through these functions the **price mechanism** answers what to produce, how to produce and for whom, without central direction.

## 3.1.5.2 Market failure

Market failure occurs whenever a market leads to a **misallocation of resources**: too much or too little of a good is produced compared with what would maximise economic welfare. **Complete market failure** means a market is missing altogether (a pure public good). **Partial market failure** means the market exists but produces the wrong quantity or at the wrong price (an external cost, a demerit good, monopoly).

## 3.1.5.3 Public goods

A pure public good is **non-rival** (one person's use does not reduce what is left for others) and **non-excludable** (people who do not pay cannot be stopped from benefiting). Together these create the **free-rider problem**: people can benefit without paying, so a private firm cannot raise enough revenue and the good is not provided. A flood barrier protecting a town is a standard example.

A public good can become **quasi-public** when one of the characteristics weakens. A road is non-excludable until a toll is introduced, and it becomes rival when congested. **Technological change** matters too: television broadcasting became excludable once encrypted signals and subscriptions were possible, so it could be sold privately.

## 3.1.5.4 Externalities on demand and supply diagrams

An externality exists when private and social costs or benefits diverge. At AS you show this without MSC/MSB labels.

**Negative production externality.** The firm's supply curve reflects only its own costs. Draw a second supply curve above it, "supply including external costs". The market produces where demand meets the first curve, so output is above the level where demand meets the second: over-production.

**Positive consumption externality.** Demand reflects only private benefits. Draw a second demand curve to the right, "demand including external benefits". The market produces where the private demand curve meets supply, below the level the second curve shows: under-production.

### Worked example: correcting an external cost with a tax

The market for single-use plastic cartons (thousands a week, price in pounds) is Qd = 900 - 50P and Qs = -100 + 50P.

```
Free market: 900 - 50P = -100 + 50P -> P = 10, Q = 400
Each carton imposes an external cost of 2, so a tax of 2 per unit is set.
New supply: Qs = -100 + 50(P - 2) = -200 + 50P
With tax:   900 - 50P = -200 + 50P -> P = 11, Q = 350
Consumers pay 11 (+1); producers keep 11 - 2 = 9 (-1)
Tax revenue = 2 x 350 = 700 thousand pounds a week
```

The tax shifts supply up by 2, output falls by 50 thousand towards the level that accounts for the external cost, and the cost is shared equally because demand and supply have equal slopes here. With price-inelastic demand, consumers would bear more of the tax and output would fall less.

## 3.1.5.5 Merit and demerit goods

Classing a good as merit or demerit rests on a **value judgement** about what is good for people. Such goods may have positive or negative **externalities in consumption**, and under- or over-consumption may also result from **imperfect information**: people underestimate the long-term benefit of a merit good (preventive dental check-ups) or the long-term harm of a demerit good. Not every good with a consumption externality is a merit or demerit good; the classification needs the value judgement.

## 3.1.5.6 Market imperfections

- **Imperfect and asymmetric information**: one side of the market knows more, as when a seller of a used car knows its faults and the buyer does not, so buyers make poor choices.
- **Monopoly and monopoly power**: prices above the competitive level and restricted output (3.1.4).
- **Immobility of factors**: workers cannot easily move between jobs (occupational immobility) or places (geographical immobility), so resources stay unemployed or poorly used when demand changes.

## 3.1.5.7 Inequality

Without intervention the market mechanism is likely to produce a very unequal and inequitable distribution of income and wealth. Since ability to buy depends on income, goods go to those who can pay rather than those with the greatest need, which is a form of misallocation. At AS you need only be aware that **progressive taxes and government spending** are used to reduce inequality, not the detailed causes of inequality.

## 3.1.5.8 Tools of intervention

| Tool | Best suited to | Main drawback |
|---|---|---|
| Indirect tax | Negative externalities, demerit goods | External cost is hard to measure; little effect if demand is inelastic; can be regressive |
| Subsidy | Positive externalities, merit goods | Opportunity cost of government spending; firms may become inefficient |
| Maximum price | Making essentials affordable | Shortages, queues and illegal markets |
| Minimum price | Raising producers' incomes or cutting consumption of a demerit good | Surpluses; illegal sales below the floor |
| State provision | Public goods, merit goods | No price signal, so quantity is hard to judge; financed through taxes |
| Regulation | Harmful products or practices | Monitoring and enforcement costs |

**Maximum price example.** In a city's rental market, Qd = 5,000 - 4R and Qs = -1,000 + 2R, where R is monthly rent. Equilibrium: 6,000 = 6R, so R = 1,000 and 1,000 homes are let. A maximum rent of 800 raises quantity demanded to 1,800 and cuts quantity supplied to 600: a shortage of 1,200 homes. Tenants who find a home gain, but 400 fewer homes are let than before.

## 3.1.5.9 Government failure

Government failure occurs when intervention leads to a misallocation of resources. The specification names **inadequate information**, **conflicting objectives** and **administrative costs** as sources, notes that governments may create rather than remove distortions, and that intervention can have **unintended consequences**. In the rent example, the shortage and a fall in the number of homes let are unintended consequences; a tax set without knowing the true external cost is inadequate information.

## Annotated model paragraph

*Question style: assess whether a subsidy is the best way to increase the use of public bus services.*

> Bus travel creates external benefits, such as less congestion and pollution, so the free market under-provides it. A subsidy lowers firms' costs, shifting supply to the right, so fares fall and passenger numbers rise towards the level that includes the external benefits. However, if demand for bus travel is price inelastic, lower fares bring few extra passengers, and the subsidy's cost has an opportunity cost in other public services. Regulation that gives buses priority lanes may raise use more cheaply, so a subsidy is most effective when combined with measures that make buses quicker.

Why it works: cause of failure, mechanism on a demand and supply diagram, an elasticity condition, opportunity cost, an alternative and a justified conclusion.

## Common mistakes

- Defining a public good as "provided by the government". Use non-rival and non-excludable.
- Drawing MSC/MSB diagrams when an AS demand and supply diagram is all that is needed.
- Calling every good with an externality a merit or demerit good.
- Stating that a tax "solves" the externality, ignoring measurement and elasticity.
- Forgetting that a maximum price only matters if it is set below equilibrium.
- Treating government failure as any policy you disagree with, rather than a misallocation caused by intervention.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** questions on a market failure need the source of the failure, the direction of misallocation (over or under) and why.
- When the data name a policy, **analyse** it with a diagram and the numbers given, then **evaluate** it: elasticity, information, cost to the government, and one possible source of government failure.
- Finish with a judgement that answers the exact question, preferably naming the conditions under which the policy works.
