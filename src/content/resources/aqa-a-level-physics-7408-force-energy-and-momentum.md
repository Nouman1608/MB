## Vectors and equilibrium

A **vector** has magnitude and direction (displacement, velocity, force, momentum); a **scalar** has magnitude only (distance, speed, mass, energy). A force F at angle θ to the horizontal has components **F cos θ** horizontally and **F sin θ** vertically.

**Worked example 1.** A sledge is pulled with 250 N at 35° above the horizontal. Horizontal component = 250 cos 35° = **205 N**; vertical component = 250 sin 35° = **143 N**. The vertical component reduces the normal contact force from the ground.

An object is in **equilibrium** when the resultant force is zero: the components in any two perpendicular directions each sum to zero, and three forces form a closed triangle when drawn head to tail.

**Worked example 2.** A 40 N lamp hangs from two cables, each at 30° to the horizontal. Vertically, 2T sin 30° = 40, so **T = 40 N**. The tension equals the weight here because sin 30° = 0.5; shallower cables need larger tensions.

## Moments

The **moment** of a force = force × perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action. A **couple** is two equal, opposite, parallel forces; its moment = one force × perpendicular distance between them. The **principle of moments**: for an object in equilibrium, the sum of clockwise moments about any point equals the sum of anticlockwise moments about that point. The **centre of mass** is the point where the weight can be taken to act; for a uniform object it is at the geometric centre.

**Worked example 3.** A uniform seesaw is pivoted at its centre. A 300 N child sits 0.80 m from the pivot. Where must a 400 N child sit to balance it? 300 × 0.80 = 400 × d, so **d = 0.60 m** on the other side. A steering wheel turned by two 15 N forces at opposite ends of a 0.36 m diameter experiences a couple of 15 × 0.36 = **5.4 N m**.

## Motion along a straight line

For **uniform acceleration**: v = u + at; s = ½(u + v)t; s = ut + ½at²; v² = u² + 2as. List s, u, v, a, t, mark the three you know and the one you want, and choose the equation without the fifth. Choose a positive direction and keep to it.

**Worked example 4.** A ball is thrown vertically upwards at 14 m s⁻¹. Taking upwards as positive, a = −9.81 m s⁻².

```
maximum height: v = 0, so 0 = 14² − 2 × 9.81 × s → s = 9.99 m
time to return: s = 0, so 0 = 14t − 4.905t² → t = 2.85 s
```

On graphs: the gradient of displacement–time is velocity; the gradient of velocity–time is acceleration; the area under velocity–time is displacement; the area under acceleration–time is change in velocity.

**Required practical 3: determination of g by a free-fall method.** Drop a ball from rest through measured heights h and time it electronically; plot h against t². The gradient is g/2.

## Projectile motion

Horizontal and vertical motions are independent; time links them. Ignoring air resistance, horizontal velocity is constant and vertical acceleration is g downwards.

**Worked example 5.** A ball is kicked at 20 m s⁻¹ at 30° above the horizontal on level ground.

```
uₓ = 20 cos 30° = 17.3 m s⁻¹    u_y = 20 sin 30° = 10.0 m s⁻¹
time of flight: 0 = 10.0t − 4.905t² → t = 2.04 s
range = 17.3 × 2.04 = 35.3 m
maximum height = 10.0² / (2 × 9.81) = 5.10 m
```

With air resistance the range and height are smaller and the path is not symmetrical. **Friction** opposes relative motion; **drag** opposes motion through a fluid and increases with speed; **lift** acts perpendicular to the flow. A falling object reaches **terminal speed** when drag equals weight, so the resultant force and the acceleration are zero.

## Newton's laws

1. An object stays at rest or at constant velocity unless a resultant force acts.
2. F = ma, where F is the resultant force (for constant mass).
3. If A exerts a force on B, B exerts an equal and opposite force of the same type on A.

**Worked example 6.** Blocks of 3.0 kg and 2.0 kg are joined by a light string on a smooth surface. A 15 N force pulls the 3.0 kg block.

```
whole system: a = 15 / 5.0 = 3.0 m s⁻²
2.0 kg block alone: T = 2.0 × 3.0 = 6.0 N
check, 3.0 kg block: 15 − 6.0 = 3.0 × 3.0 ✓
```

Newton's third law pairs act on *different* objects, so they never cancel each other. The weight of a book and the table's push on it are not a third-law pair.

## Momentum and impulse

Momentum p = mv. In a closed system, total momentum is conserved. **F = Δ(mv)/Δt**; **impulse** FΔt = Δ(mv), the area under a force–time graph.

**Worked example 7.** A 0.058 kg tennis ball arrives at 20 m s⁻¹ and leaves in the opposite direction at 25 m s⁻¹ after 4.0 ms of contact.

```
Δp = 0.058 × (25 − (−20)) = 0.058 × 45 = 2.61 N s
F = Δp / Δt = 2.61 / 0.004 = 650 N
```

The change in velocity is 45 m s⁻¹, not 5 m s⁻¹, because the direction reverses.

**Explosions**: starting from rest, total momentum stays zero. A 60 kg skater and a 40 kg skater push off each other; if the 60 kg skater moves at 0.50 m s⁻¹, the 40 kg skater moves at 60 × 0.50 / 40 = **0.75 m s⁻¹** the other way. The kinetic energy comes from the work done by their muscles.

A collision is **elastic** if total kinetic energy is conserved and **inelastic** if it is not. Momentum is conserved in both.

## Work, energy and power

Work done W = Fs cos θ, where θ is the angle between force and displacement. Power P = ΔW/Δt = **Fv**. Efficiency = useful output power ÷ input power.

**Worked example 8.** A car moves at a constant 25 m s⁻¹ against a total resistive force of 600 N. The driving force equals 600 N (no resultant force), so the useful power is P = Fv = 600 × 25 = **15 kW**. If the engine's input power is 50 kW, the efficiency is 15/50 = **30%**.

## Conservation of energy

Energy cannot be created or destroyed. For an object falling or swinging without resistance, mgΔh = ½mv², so v = √(2gΔh), independent of mass. A pendulum bob released from 0.20 m above its lowest point reaches √(2 × 9.81 × 0.20) = **1.98 m s⁻¹** there. With resistive forces, Ep lost = Ek gained + work done against resistance.

## Common mistakes

- Using the slope length instead of the perpendicular distance in a moment.
- Mixing signs in suvat (upward u positive, g positive too).
- Using the launch speed, not its vertical component, for time of flight.
- Treating weight and normal contact force as a third-law pair.
- Forgetting that a velocity reversal adds speeds in Δv.
- Using P = Fv with the resultant force when the driving force is wanted.

## Exam technique

"Calculate" answers need the equation, substitution and answer with unit. "Show that" answers need every step and one more significant figure than the given value. "Explain" questions on terminal speed or crumple zones need a chain of cause and effect: for example, a longer contact time for the same momentum change means a smaller force.

## Frequently asked questions

**When is momentum conserved?**
In any closed system, meaning no external resultant force acts. It is conserved in elastic and inelastic collisions and in explosions.

**What is the difference between an elastic and an inelastic collision?**
In an elastic collision total kinetic energy is conserved. In an inelastic collision some kinetic energy is transferred to other stores, such as heating or deforming the objects.

**Why does a projectile's horizontal velocity stay constant?**
Ignoring air resistance, no horizontal force acts, so by Newton's first law the horizontal velocity does not change. Gravity acts only vertically.
