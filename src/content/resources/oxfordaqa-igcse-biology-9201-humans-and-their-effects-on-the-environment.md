This guide goes deeper into section **3.3.4 Humans and their effects on the environment** of the OxfordAQA
International GCSE Biology (9201) specification, Version 5.2, for exams in May/June 2018 onwards. The qualification
is untiered and any part of the specification may be assessed on either paper. The specification marks the whole of
this section as Biology-only content.

The existing [Decay, the carbon cycle and human effects study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-decay-the-carbon-cycle-and-human-effects-on-the/)
covers 3.3.3 and 3.3.4 together. This page concentrates on 3.3.4 and adds different evidence: a eutrophication
time series, carbon dioxide data, a deforestation survey and a comparison of two methods of collecting data.
Afterwards, use the [Decay, the carbon cycle and human effects revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-decay-the-carbon-cycle-and-human-effects-on-the-revision-notes/)
and the [Humans and their effects on the environment practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-9201-humans-and-their-effects-on-the-environment-practice/).

## What 3.3.4 asks you to know

| Statements | Content |
|---|---|
| a | A growing population and rising standard of living produce more waste; unless handled properly, more pollution results |
| b | Waste may pollute water, air and land; sewage and fertilisers may cause eutrophication |
| c | Large-scale deforestation in tropical areas has led to a loss of biodiversity |
| d | Rising carbon dioxide and methane contribute to global warming, with several possible effects |
| Throughout | Analyse and interpret data on environmental issues, and evaluate methods of collecting such data |

## Waste and pollution

Rapid growth in the human population, and an increase in the standard of living, mean that increasingly more waste is
produced. **Unless waste and chemical materials are properly handled, more pollution will be caused.**

| Pollutes | Pollutant (specification examples) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Water | Sewage, fertiliser, toxic chemicals | Sewage and fertilisers may cause eutrophication |
| Air | Smoke, and gases such as sulfur dioxide | Sulfur dioxide contributes to acid rain |
| Land | Toxic chemicals such as pesticides and herbicides | These may be washed from the land into water |

## Eutrophication

Sewage and fertilisers may cause **eutrophication**. The specification gives four stages, and you should learn them
in order.

1. An **increase in the concentration of mineral ions** in the water stimulates the growth of algae and/or plants.
2. The growth of algae/plants eventually results in some being **unable to receive enough light for photosynthesis**,
   and these organisms die.
3. There is a large increase in the population of **microorganisms that feed on the dead organisms**.
4. The **respiration of the microorganisms depletes the oxygen** concentration in the water, leading to the **death of
   aerobic organisms**.

A common error is to say that the algae use up the oxygen. The oxygen falls mainly because of the respiration of the
microorganisms that feed on dead material, not because of the algae.

### Worked example 1: a lake over eight weeks

Invented data. Fertiliser washes into a lake from week 0.

| Week | 0 | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nitrate (mg per dm³) | 4.0 | 9.0 | 14.0 | 14.5 | 14.0 |
| Algae (cells per cm³) | 2 000 | 18 000 | 95 000 | 40 000 | 12 000 |
| Dissolved oxygen (mg per dm³) | 9.0 | 9.4 | 7.6 | 4.1 | 2.8 |

```
oxygen change from week 2 to week 8 = 2.8 − 9.4 = −6.6 mg per dm³
percentage fall = 6.6 ÷ 9.4 × 100 = 70.2 %  (3 s.f.)
```

Interpretation: nitrate rises, then the algae multiply rapidly (weeks 2 to 4). The algae block the light and many die
(weeks 4 to 6). The dead algae are fed on by microorganisms, whose respiration removes oxygen, so oxygen falls to
2.8 mg per dm³ by week 8. At this level aerobic organisms such as fish would struggle to survive. The oxygen peaked at
week 2 because the algae were photosynthesising, which is evidence that the oxygen fall was not caused by the algae
alone.

## Deforestation

Large-scale deforestation in tropical areas has led to a **reduction in biodiversity**. You should be able to
**describe the effects of deforestation**.

- Habitats are destroyed, so many species lose their food and shelter. Biodiversity falls.
- Less photosynthesis occurs, so less carbon dioxide is removed from the air.
- Burning the trees (combustion) releases carbon dioxide, and the microorganisms decaying the felled wood respire and
  release carbon dioxide too.
- Bare soil can be washed away, and the nutrients with it, into rivers.

### Worked example 2: a biodiversity survey

Invented data. A student counted tree and shrub species in five 400 m² plots in tropical forest and in five plots in
cleared land nearby.

| Plot | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Forest (species) | 48 | 52 | 45 | 50 | 55 |
| Cleared (species) | 6 | 9 | 7 | 8 | 5 |

```
mean, forest = (48 + 52 + 45 + 50 + 55) ÷ 5 = 250 ÷ 5 = 50.0
mean, cleared = (6 + 9 + 7 + 8 + 5) ÷ 5 = 35 ÷ 5 = 7.0
reduction = (50.0 − 7.0) ÷ 50.0 × 100 = 86.0 %
```

Interpretation: cleared land has far fewer species, consistent with the loss of biodiversity. To be sure the
difference is caused by clearing, the plots should have been similar in soil type and altitude before clearing.

## Global warming

Levels of **carbon dioxide and methane** in the atmosphere are increasing and contribute to global warming. An
increase in the Earth's temperature of only a few degrees Celsius may:

- cause big changes in the Earth's climate;
- cause a rise in sea level;
- cause changes in migration patterns, for example in birds;
- result in changes in the distribution of species.

Rising carbon dioxide has several human causes that you can link to the carbon cycle: burning fossil fuels (combustion)
and deforestation (less photosynthesis, more combustion and decay). Methane is released, for example, from rice
paddies and from cattle.

### Worked example 3: reading carbon dioxide data

Invented, rounded data for a monitoring station.

| Year | 1990 | 2000 | 2010 | 2020 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Carbon dioxide (ppm) | 354 | 369 | 389 | 414 |

```
rise over 30 years = 414 − 354 = 60 ppm
percentage rise = 60 ÷ 354 × 100 = 16.9 %  (3 s.f.)
mean rate: 1990-2000 = 15 ÷ 10 = 1.5 ppm per year
           2000-2010 = 20 ÷ 10 = 2.0 ppm per year
           2010-2020 = 25 ÷ 10 = 2.5 ppm per year
```

Interpretation: carbon dioxide has risen, and the rate of increase has also risen each decade. That is consistent with the
specification's statement that levels are increasing. The data show a correlation with time but do not by themselves show
that the rise causes warming. The link to temperature needs further evidence.

## Analysing and evaluating environmental data

The specification says that, when given information, you should **analyse and interpret** data, and **evaluate the methods**
used to collect environmental data, considering **validity and reliability** as evidence for environmental change.

- **Reliability**: would repeating the measurement give similar results? Repeats and means improve reliability.
- **Validity**: does the method measure what it is meant to measure, with other variables controlled?

### Worked example 4: evaluating a method

Invented data. Nitrate was measured in a river upstream and downstream of a farm.

| Reading | 1 | 2 | 3 | Mean |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Upstream (mg per dm³) | 3.1 | 2.9 | 3.0 | 3.0 |
| Downstream (mg per dm³) | 8.2 | 7.9 | 8.4 | 8.2 |

```
downstream mean = (8.2 + 7.9 + 8.4) ÷ 3 = 24.5 ÷ 3 = 8.17 → 8.2 mg per dm³ (2 s.f.)
ratio downstream : upstream = 8.17 ÷ 3.0 = 2.7 times as much
```

The close agreement of the three readings at each site suggests reliable data. For validity: the readings come from
one day only, so they may not show the pattern across the year. Samples should also be taken from the same depth and
time of day. Improvements include more sites, repeated sampling across seasons and checking for other sources of nitrate,
such as a sewage outfall. A single sample from one point would be neither reliable nor valid evidence of a change.

## Common mistakes

- Saying fertiliser "kills" the algae. Fertiliser stimulates their growth first.
- Saying that algae use up the oxygen in eutrophication. The key step is the respiration of the microorganisms.
- Omitting the death of the algae due to lack of light in the sequence.
- Mixing up the greenhouse gases with acid rain gases (sulfur dioxide).
- Saying deforestation affects only the trees. Include habitats and biodiversity.
- Writing "global warming is caused by the hole in the ozone layer". That is a different issue.
- Treating a correlation as proof of cause.

## Exam technique

- For **eutrophication**, write the four stages in order, using the words in the specification.
- For **describe** questions on data, quote values and say whether trends are increasing or decreasing.
- For **evaluate** a method, comment on validity (what is measured and controlled) and reliability (repeats), then
  suggest an improvement.
- For **global warming**, give at least two effects and link them (for example, rising sea level and changed
  distribution of species).
