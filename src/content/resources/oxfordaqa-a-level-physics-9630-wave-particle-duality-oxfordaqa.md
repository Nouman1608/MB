This guide goes deeper into section **3.5.11 Wave particle duality** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630) specification. You should know that "electron diffraction suggests that particles possess wave properties and the photoelectric effect suggests that electromagnetic waves have a particulate nature". The section gives the de Broglie equation λ = h/mv, "where mv is the momentum", asks you to "explain how and why the amount of diffraction changes when the momentum of the particle is changed", and expects an "appreciation of how knowledge and understanding of the nature of matter changes over time". Details of particular methods of particle diffraction are not expected. It is shared AS and A-level content.

The broader [study guide to electron collisions, the photoelectric effect and wave-particle duality](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-electron-collisions-photoelectric-effect-and-wave/) introduces the equation with fixed-speed examples. This page goes further: electrons accelerated through a potential difference, comparisons between particles, and well-structured explanations of the momentum-diffraction link and of how scientific ideas developed. Afterwards use the [wave-particle duality revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-wave-particle-duality-oxfordaqa-revision-notes/) and the [wave-particle duality practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-wave-particle-duality-oxfordaqa-practice/).

Data used: h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, electron mass 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg, proton and neutron mass 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg.

## Two pieces of evidence, two directions

| Evidence | What it shows | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Electron diffraction | Particles have wave properties | Diffraction and the resulting pattern of maxima and minima are wave behaviour; a beam of electrons through a thin crystalline sample produces one |
| Photoelectric effect | Electromagnetic waves have a particulate nature | Energy is delivered in discrete photons, each absorbed whole by one electron |

Neither model is complete on its own. Light shows wave behaviour in interference and particle behaviour in the photoelectric effect. Electrons show particle behaviour when they are deflected as charged particles and wave behaviour when they diffract. The behaviour you observe depends on the experiment.

## The de Broglie equation

λ = h/mv = h/p

Every moving particle has a de Broglie wavelength that is inversely proportional to its momentum. A larger mass or a larger speed gives a shorter wavelength.

## Electrons accelerated through a potential difference

In most electron diffraction set-ups, electrons are accelerated from rest through a p.d. V. Linking the energy and momentum gives a direct route to λ:

```
kinetic energy gained:   Ek = eV
since Ek = p² / 2m:      p = √(2mEk) = √(2meV)
de Broglie wavelength:   λ = h / √(2meV)
```

### Worked example 1: electrons through 150 V

```
Ek = eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 150 = 2.40 × 10⁻¹⁷ J
p = √(2 × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × 2.40 × 10⁻¹⁷) = 6.61 × 10⁻²⁴ kg m s⁻¹
λ = h / p = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ / 6.61 × 10⁻²⁴ = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁰ m
```

This wavelength is about the same as the spacing between atoms in a crystal, which is why such a crystal can diffract the beam strongly.

### Worked example 2: raising the voltage

The p.d. is increased to 600 V, four times larger.

```
λ ∝ 1 / √V, so λ falls by a factor of √4 = 2
λ = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁰ / 2 = 5.0 × 10⁻¹¹ m
```

A direct calculation gives the same value. In an electron diffraction tube the rings on the screen become **smaller** as the voltage rises, because the shorter wavelength is diffracted through smaller angles.

## How and why diffraction depends on momentum

This is the explanation the specification names. A full answer has four links:

1. Diffraction is significant when the wavelength is comparable to the gap or the spacing of the atoms.
2. By λ = h/mv, increasing the momentum decreases the de Broglie wavelength.
3. A shorter wavelength, compared with the same spacing, is diffracted less.
4. So the angles to the maxima (or the radii of the rings) become smaller and the pattern is less spread out.

Reducing the momentum does the reverse: a longer wavelength and a more spread-out pattern.

## Comparing different particles

### Worked example 3: electron and proton with the same kinetic energy

An electron and a proton each have kinetic energy 2.40 × 10⁻¹⁷ J. Compare their wavelengths.

From p = √(2mEk), with equal Ek, momentum is proportional to √m, so

```
λ(electron) / λ(proton) = √(m(proton) / m(electron))
                        = √(1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ / 9.11 × 10⁻³¹) = 42.8
λ(proton) = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁰ / 42.8 = 2.3 × 10⁻¹² m
```

The proton's wavelength is far smaller than the atomic spacing, so the same crystal would diffract it very little.

### Worked example 4: a slow neutron

A neutron moves at 2200 m s⁻¹.

```
λ = h / mv = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ / (1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ × 2200) = 1.8 × 10⁻¹⁰ m
```

Slow neutrons, despite their large mass, have wavelengths comparable to atomic spacings because their speed is low. Momentum, not mass alone, is what matters.

Everyday objects have such enormous momentum that their wavelengths are many orders of magnitude smaller than any gap, so their wave properties are never observed.

## How ideas about the nature of matter changed

The specification asks for an appreciation of how understanding changes over time. The story is a good example of models being revised when new evidence appears.

- **Light as particles or waves.** In the 17th century Newton favoured a particle (corpuscular) model of light, while Huygens proposed a wave model.
- **The wave model wins.** In the early 1800s Young's double-slit experiment showed interference, which only a wave model explains. Later in the century Maxwell's theory described light as an electromagnetic wave.
- **Quanta.** In 1900 Planck proposed that energy is emitted in discrete amounts. In 1905 Einstein explained the photoelectric effect using photons, restoring a particle aspect to light.
- **Matter waves.** In 1924 de Broglie suggested that particles such as electrons have a wavelength h/p. In 1927 electron diffraction was observed, by Davisson and Germer and by G. P. Thomson, confirming the prediction.

The lessons examiners look for: a model is accepted while it explains the evidence and makes predictions that are confirmed; new experimental evidence can force a model to be modified or replaced; and the scientific community tests new ideas by repeating experiments before accepting them.

## Common mistakes

- Saying the photoelectric effect shows electrons are waves. It shows light has a particle nature.
- Saying electron diffraction shows light is a wave. It shows particles have wave properties.
- Using kinetic energy in place of momentum in λ = h/mv.
- Putting the accelerating voltage straight into λ = h/mv without finding the momentum.
- Saying a larger momentum gives more diffraction.
- Claiming duality applies only to light or only to electrons. It applies to all particles and all electromagnetic radiation.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** what electron diffraction shows: diffraction is a wave property, so electrons must have a wavelength.
- **Calculate** λ for an accelerated electron: find Ek = eV, then p = √(2mEk), then λ = h/p, showing each step.
- **Explain** a change in the pattern: use the four-link chain from momentum to angles.
- **Discuss** changing ideas: give the evidence that forced each change, not just dates and names.
