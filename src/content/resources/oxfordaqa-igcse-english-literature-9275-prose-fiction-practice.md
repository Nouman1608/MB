## Section A: short skills questions

Read Extract 1, then answer Questions 1 to 3.

> **Extract 1**
>
> Mr Okafor arrived at the shop each morning at six, a full hour before anyone could want a
> newspaper. He swept the step that did not need sweeping. He straightened the rows of sweets that
> nobody had touched. When the bell above the door finally rang, he looked up so quickly that his
> glasses slid down his nose, and then, seeing it was only the wind, he pushed them back with one
> finger and went on straightening.

**1.** Identify the narrative viewpoint used in Extract 1 and give one effect it creates. **[2]**

**2.** Explain how the writer uses repetition to present Mr Okafor's daily life. Refer to one
example. **[4]**

**3.** How does the final sentence change the reader's understanding of Mr Okafor? **[4]**

Read Extract 2, then answer Questions 4 and 5.

> **Extract 2**
>
> I was eleven that summer, and I believed everything my brother told me, including the story about
> the drowned village under the reservoir. I know now that he made it up on the walk home. But I
> still cannot swim in that water without listening for church bells.

**4.** What is the effect of the narrator looking back from an older age? **[4]**

**5.** Explain how the last sentence links the narrator's past and present. **[4]**

**6.** Rewrite this weak sentence so that it analyses the writer's method rather than retelling the
plot: *"In the novel, the main character gets angry and leaves the house."* You may invent a short
quotation for the purpose. **[3]**

**7.** Give two reasons why context should be linked to a specific moment in the text rather than
written as a separate paragraph. **[2]**

## Section B: exam-style questions (use your own studied prose text)

**8.** Explore how the writer presents a character who changes over the course of your studied prose
text. **[30]**

**9.** How does the writer of your studied prose text use one important setting to develop the
novel's central ideas? **[30]**

**10.** Choose a passage of about one page from near the end of your studied prose text. Explore how
the writer presents the resolution, or lack of resolution, of a central conflict in this passage and
elsewhere in the text. **[30]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** Third-person narration [1]. Any one effect: it lets the reader observe Mr Okafor's habits from
outside, so we notice their oddness before he does; or it creates a slightly detached, gently comic
tone [1].

**2.** One mark for identifying the repetition, up to three for explaining it. The sentences repeat a
pattern ("He swept the step that did not need sweeping. He straightened the rows of sweets that
nobody had touched.") [1]. The parallel structure makes his actions feel routine and mechanical [1].
The repeated negatives ("did not need", "nobody had touched") show that his work has no real purpose
[1], suggesting loneliness or a need to keep busy while waiting for customers who do not come [1].

**3.** Up to four marks for developed points. The ringing bell raises his hopes, shown by how
"quickly" he looks up [1]. The discovery that it is "only the wind" deflates that hope, revealing how
much he longs for company [1]. The small physical detail of the sliding glasses makes the moment
humble and touching rather than dramatic [1]. Returning to "straightening" ends where his day began,
so the structure suggests that the disappointment is part of his routine [1].

**4.** Up to four marks. The retrospective narration creates a gap between the child's belief and the
adult's knowledge [1]. "I know now" signals that the adult understands the trick, which adds gentle
irony [1]. The reader sees both the child's innocence and the adult's awareness [1], so the extract
explores how childhood experiences stay with us even after we understand them [1].

**5.** Up to four marks. "Still" connects past to present [1]. The narrator knows the story was false
but cannot stop "listening for church bells" [1], showing that imagination outlasts reason [1]. The
image of bells under the water suggests that the past is submerged but not gone [1].

**6.** Award up to three marks: a clear claim about presentation [1], short embedded evidence [1],
and a method with its effect [1]. Example: *"The writer presents her anger through action rather
than speech: she 'left without a word', and the silence suggests a resentment too deep to put into
language."*

**7.** Any two: context only earns credit when it explains a choice in the text [1]; a separate
paragraph can turn into a history lesson with no analysis [1]; linking it to a moment keeps the
answer focused on the question [1].

### Section B (approach: marks depend on your studied text)

These questions are marked holistically against level descriptors rather than point by point. The
guidance below describes what a high-level response does.

**8. A changing character.** A strong answer:

- states a clear line of argument about how and why the character changes (AO1, AO3);
- chooses evidence from at least three stages of the text, such as an early impression, a turning
  point and a final position (AO1);
- analyses the methods that show the change: narrative viewpoint, dialogue, how other characters
  respond, and structural placing of key scenes (AO2);
- considers whether the change is complete, partial or ironic, and what the writer may want the
  reader to conclude (AO3);
- uses context only where it explains the change, such as social pressures that shape the character
  (AO1).

A mid-level answer explains clearly what happens to the character with some relevant quotations; a
low-level answer narrates events in order.

**9. Setting and ideas.** A strong answer:

- connects the setting to one or two central ideas from the start, rather than describing the place;
- analyses descriptive language (imagery, pathetic fallacy, sensory detail) and structural use of
  setting (returning to it at key moments, contrasting it with another place) (AO2);
- shows how the meaning of the setting develops across the text (AO1);
- links to context where the setting reflects social or historical realities (AO1);
- offers a personal judgement on what the setting contributes to the novel overall (AO3).

**10. Resolution.** A strong answer:

- briefly identifies the conflict and why it matters to the whole text;
- analyses the chosen passage closely: language, dialogue, narrative voice and how the passage
  closes (AO2);
- moves outward to show how earlier parts of the text prepare for the ending, such as foreshadowing
  or parallel scenes (AO2, AO1);
- judges whether the ending is satisfying, open or deliberately unsettling, and why the writer might
  choose that (AO3).

Answers that only describe the ending, or that ignore either the passage or the wider text, cannot
reach the top levels because they do not answer the whole question.

## How to use this page

Do Section A in about 25 minutes to warm up your close reading. Then plan one Section B question in
five minutes and write it in 40 minutes from memory, as Paper 1 is closed book. Compare your answer
with the bullet points above and highlight every place where you analysed a method rather than
describing events.
