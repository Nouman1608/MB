## Questions

**1.** **(a)** Define scarcity. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why a free good has no opportunity cost. **[2]**

**2.** State whether each statement is positive or normative. **[4]**
(i) Unemployment in Pellora fell from 8% to 6% last year.
(ii) Pellora ought to protect its farmers from foreign competition.
(iii) A rise in bus fares in Dunmere reduced the number of journeys.
(iv) Incomes in Orlen should be shared more equally.

**3.** (calculation) Four points on the PPC of Pellora, which makes tractors and cloth:

| Point | Tractors | Cloth (tonnes) |
|---|---|---|
| A | 0 | 90 |
| B | 15 | 84 |
| C | 30 | 48 |
| D | 45 | 0 |

**(a)** Calculate the opportunity cost, in tonnes of cloth, of one extra tractor when moving from A to B and from B to C. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain what your results show about the shape of the PPC. **[2]**

**4.** (diagram) Orlen produces capital goods and consumer goods. Sketch and label a PPC for Orlen. Show a point of unemployment W, an unattainable point X, and growth in production possibilities, and explain one cause of that growth. **[6]**

**5.** (calculation) In Pellora, S = 45, T = 70, M = 35, I = 60, G = 65, X = 30 (billions of crowns).
**(a)** Calculate total leakages and total injections. **[2]**
**(b)** State and explain the likely effect on the flow of income. **[2]**
**(c)** Name the sector that channels S towards firms' investment. **[1]**

**6.** **(a)** State the meaning of ceteris paribus. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why it matters when testing the hypothesis that a fall in the price of sandals raises the quantity demanded. **[3]**

**7.** A hypothesis states that, ceteris paribus, a rise in cinema ticket prices reduces attendance. On the fictional island of Tavish the price rose from 8 to 10 crowns and weekly attendance rose from 5 000 to 5 400. A rival cinema closed in the same month.
**(a)** State what the hypothesis predicts. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in attendance. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain whether the evidence refutes the hypothesis. **[2]**

**8.** Explain the three basic economic questions and compare how a free market economy and a planned economy answer them, referring to the mixed economy. **[8]**

**9.** Outline the difference between Say's law and the Keynesian view of unemployment, and explain how the two views lead to different policy attitudes. **[6]**

**10.** Evaluate the view that economic models are of little use because their assumptions are unrealistic. **[10]**

**11.** The fictional economy of Veldon can produce 120 tonnes of grain or 40 machines, with a straight-line PPC.
**(a)** Calculate the opportunity cost of one machine. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the maximum grain output if 25 machines are made. **[2]**
**(c)** Veldon makes 25 machines and 30 tonnes of grain. Explain what this shows. **[3]**
**(d)** Distinguish actual growth from growth in production possibilities, using Veldon. **[3]**
**(e)** State two assumptions of the PPC model. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Wants and needs are unlimited [1] while resources are limited [1]. **(b)** A free good uses no scarce resources [1], so consuming it takes nothing from anyone else and nothing is given up [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Define" asks for precise meaning, so an example alone does not replace the definition.

**2.** (i) Positive [1]. (ii) Normative [1]. (iii) Positive [1]. (iv) Normative [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Statement (iii) is positive even if false, because it can be tested; answers lose credit by confusing "positive" with "true".

**3. (a)** A to B: 90 − 84 = 6 tonnes lost [1] over 15 tractors: 6 / 15 = **0.4 tonnes** [1]. B to C: (84 − 48) / 15 = 36 / 15 = **2.4 tonnes** [1].
**(b)** Opportunity cost rises as more tractors are made [1], so the PPC is bowed outwards because resources are not equally suited to both goods [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the subtraction and the division; a bare number earns less than a number with working.

**4.** Axes labelled capital goods and consumer goods, with a curve bowed outwards [1]. W inside the curve [1]. X outside the curve [1]. A second curve shifted outwards [1]. X is unattainable because resources are limited [1]. Cause of growth, for example better technology or more resources, which raises what can be produced [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Diagrams earn credit when axes and curves are labelled and the points are referred to in the written explanation.

**5. (a)** Leakages: 45 + 70 + 35 = **150** [1]. Injections: 60 + 65 + 30 = **155** [1].
**(b)** Injections exceed leakages by 5 [1], so the flow of income tends to rise [1].
**(c)** Banks and the financial sector [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the three terms added in each total, and say which side is larger before stating the effect.

**6. (a)** All other relevant factors are held constant [1]. **(b)** Without it, other influences such as income could change at the same time [1]. Quantity demanded might rise because incomes rose, not because the price fell [1]. Holding them constant isolates the effect of price alone [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Apply ceteris paribus to the sandals example; a general definition repeated in part (b) earns little.

**7. (a)** Attendance falls [1]. **(b)** (5 400 − 5 000) / 5 000 [1] = **8% rise** [1]. **(c)** The result contradicts the prediction [1], but the rival closing means ceteris paribus did not hold, so the hypothesis cannot be refuted from this evidence alone [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* A balanced answer uses the data and the closure; one-sided answers usually stop at "attendance rose".

**8.** The questions: what and how much to produce [1], how to produce [1], for whom [1]. Free market: households and firms decide through prices [1] with private ownership [1]. Planned: a central authority decides [1] and sets output and distribution directly [1]. Mixed: markets operate with government intervention in some areas [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* "Compare" needs both systems addressed against the same questions, not two separate descriptions.

**9.** Say's law: supply creates its own demand [1], so general lack of demand is unlikely and the economy tends towards full employment [1]. Keynesian: total spending can fall short [1], so unemployment can persist [1]. Policy: the classical view favours little intervention [1]; the Keynesian view supports government action to raise demand [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* "Outline" is brief; credit comes from clear contrasts, not extra historical detail.

**10.** A model simplifies reality to show key relationships [1]. Ceteris paribus lets one effect be isolated [1]. PPC and circular flow make opportunity cost and interdependence clear [1]. Assumptions such as fixed technology and two goods are unrealistic [1]. Human behaviour is complex and draws on other disciplines [1]. Predictions can be tested against evidence [1], though a social science cannot run controlled experiments [1]. Models can be revised after refutation [1]. Usefulness depends on purpose and the stated assumptions [1]. A supported conclusion, for example that models are useful as long as their limits are acknowledged [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* "Evaluate" asks you to weigh strengths against limitations and reach a supported judgment.

**11. (a)** 120 / 40 [1] = **3 tonnes of grain** [1]. **(b)** 25 × 3 = 75 tonnes given up [1]; 120 − 75 = **45 tonnes** [1]. **(c)** Maximum is 45 tonnes, actual 30 [1]; 15 tonnes below the maximum, so inside the PPC [1], meaning resources are unemployed or used inefficiently [1]. **(d)** Actual growth is moving from inside the curve towards it, for example from 30 to 45 tonnes [1]. Growth in production possibilities is an outward shift of the curve [1], for example if new technology raises the maximum above 120 tonnes [1]. **(e)** Any two of: two goods only; fixed resources; fixed technology; full and efficient use on the curve [1] [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep each part's working separate so earlier errors do not hide correct later steps.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining opportunity cost as everything given up, not the next best alternative.
- Drawing a PPC without labelled axes, or marking a point without a letter.
- Showing growth as movement along the curve.
- Misplacing leakages and injections.
- Calling a statement positive because it contains a number.
- Describing ceteris paribus without applying it to the data.
- Discussing models without naming a limitation and a strength.
- Listing economists without stating their ideas.
