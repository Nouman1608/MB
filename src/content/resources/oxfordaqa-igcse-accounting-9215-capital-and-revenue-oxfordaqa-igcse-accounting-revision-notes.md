These notes condense the specification statement "the distinction between capital expenditure and revenue expenditure and capital income and revenue income" (section 3.3). The full explanation, with worked corrections, is in the [capital and revenue study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-9215-capital-and-revenue-oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting/). Test yourself afterwards with the [capital and revenue practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-9215-capital-and-revenue-oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-practice/). All figures are invented and in dollars.

## The four categories in one line each

- **Capital expenditure**: buys, creates or improves a non-current asset, or gets it ready for use. Recorded as an asset.
- **Revenue expenditure**: day-to-day running costs and keeping assets in their present condition. Recorded as an expense.
- **Capital income**: receipts outside normal trading, namely sale proceeds of non-current assets and capital introduced by the owner.
- **Revenue income**: receipts from normal trading and the regular use of resources, such as sales, rent received and commission received.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Non-current asset | An asset kept for use in the business for more than one accounting period |
| Improvement | Work that increases an asset's capacity, efficiency or useful life: capital |
| Repair | Work that restores an asset to its previous condition: revenue |
| Cost of an asset | Purchase price plus costs of bringing it into use (delivery, installation, testing, legal fees on property) |
| Capital introduced | Extra money or assets the owner puts into the business; credited to capital |
| Disposal account | Where sale proceeds of a non-current asset are credited and compared with net book value |
| Materiality | Small items that last a long time may still be expensed, because the effect on the statements is insignificant |

## Deciding fast

Ask in this order:

1. Does the business now own a non-current asset it did not have before? Capital.
2. Was the cost needed to get that asset working? Capital.
3. Does the work make the asset better than it was? Capital. Does it only restore it? Revenue.
4. Is the benefit used up this year? Revenue.

Judge by **what the spending produced**, never by the name of the account it was posted to. Wages for building an extension are capital. Carriage on a new machine is capital. Training staff to use a new machine is revenue.

## Where each item goes

| Category | Ledger account | Financial statement |
|---|---|---|
| Capital expenditure | Debit the asset account | Statement of financial position (depreciation goes to the income statement) |
| Revenue expenditure | Debit the expense account | Income statement in full |
| Capital income: asset sale | Credit the disposal account | Only profit or loss on disposal reaches the income statement |
| Capital income: owner's capital | Credit the capital account | Statement of financial position |
| Revenue income | Credit the income account | Income statement |

A loan received is a liability, not income of either kind.

## Effects of misclassification

| Error | Profit for the year | Non-current assets |
|---|---|---|
| Capital expenditure treated as revenue | Understated | Understated |
| Revenue expenditure treated as capital | Overstated | Overstated |
| Asset sale proceeds credited to sales | Revenue and gross profit overstated (the disposal result is also missing) | Asset and its provision still in the books |
| Capital introduced credited to sales | Overstated | No effect; closing capital total is unchanged |

**Depreciation knock-on.** When depreciation is a percentage of cost, moving an amount into or out of the asset account changes the charge. Corrected profit = draft profit ± amount reclassified ∓ change in depreciation.

Quick example: 1,000 of installation was charged to repairs; depreciation is 20% of cost. Profit rises by 1,000 − 200 = 800, and net book value rises by 800.

## Correcting journal entries

| Error | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Capital item posted to an expense | Asset account | Expense account |
| Repair posted to an asset | Repairs | Asset account |
| Extra depreciation needed | Depreciation | Provision for depreciation |
| Owner's capital posted to sales | Sales | Capital |

## Exam traps

- "New" does not mean capital: new tyres on an old van replace worn parts.
- Annual licences, insurance and servicing are revenue even when they relate to a new asset.
- Giving the profit effect without the effect on the statement of financial position loses marks in "explain the effect" questions.
- Forgetting the depreciation effect when calculating a corrected profit.
- Saying capital introduced increases profit. It increases capital directly.
- Treating discount received as capital income. It is revenue income.

## Self-test

1. Classify the cost of fitting a larger engine that extends a lorry's useful life.
2. Classify the cost of repainting an office in its existing colours.
3. Where is the sale price of an old computer credited?
4. A business borrows 10,000 from a bank. Is this capital income?
5. State the effect on profit of treating 600 of delivery costs on new equipment as an expense, ignoring depreciation.
6. Repeat question 5 with depreciation at 25% of cost and a full year charged.
7. Which accounting concept allows a cheap item that lasts several years to be expensed?
8. Give the journal entry to correct a repair of 300 debited to the machinery account.
9. Why is training staff to use a new machine revenue expenditure?
10. Capital introduced of 4,000 was credited to sales. What is the effect on closing capital?
11. Name two examples of revenue income other than sales.

**Answers**

1. Capital expenditure, because it improves the asset by extending its life.
2. Revenue expenditure: it maintains the office in its present condition.
3. To the disposal account.
4. No. It is a liability, recorded in a loan account.
5. Profit understated by 600; non-current assets understated by 600.
6. Profit understated by 600 − 150 = 450; net book value understated by 450.
7. Materiality.
8. Debit repairs 300, credit machinery 300.
9. It does not create or improve an asset the business records; its benefit is treated as a running cost.
10. No effect on the total, because profit is overstated by 4,000 while capital introduced is missing by 4,000.
11. Any two: rent received, commission received, interest received, discount received.
