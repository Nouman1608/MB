## Section A

**1.** Give the meaning of the term "land consolidation". **[2]**

**2.** Outline **two** reasons why a government may need to manage agricultural change. **[4]**

**3.** The table shows results of a land consolidation scheme in Varo, a fictional village.

| Indicator | Before (2012) | After (2022) |
|---|---|---|
| Average number of plots per holding | 9 | 2 |
| Average distance from home to plots (km) | 2.4 | 0.9 |
| Holdings using hired tractors (%) | 10 | 45 |
| Average wheat yield (t per ha) | 2.0 | 2.6 |
| Landless labourers employed at harvest | 140 | 60 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage increase in average wheat yield. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest how consolidation could have led to the changes in tractor use and yield. **[4]**

**(c)** Using the table, identify one group that may have lost from the scheme and explain why. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why guaranteed prices for one or two crops can make agricultural change harder to manage. **[4]**

**5.** The table shows how a fictional fertiliser subsidy in Dorvia was shared between farm-size groups.

| Farm size | Share of all farmers (%) | Share of subsidy received (%) |
|---|---|---|
| Under 1 ha | 60 | 20 |
| 1–5 ha | 30 | 35 |
| Over 5 ha | 10 | 45 |

**(a)** Calculate how many times more subsidy per farmer the over-5 ha group received than the under-1 ha group. Show your working. **[2]**

**(b)** Using the data, evaluate how fair the subsidy was. **[4]**

**6.** Explain the difference between managing agricultural change at the local scale and at the national scale. **[4]**

## Section B

**7.** With reference to one country you have studied, explain the need for agricultural change. **[6]**

**8.** With reference to one country you have studied, explain the difficulties of managing agricultural change at the local scale. **[6]**

**9.** Explain why a solution that succeeds on one farm or in one village may fail when applied across a whole country. **[5]**

**10.** Explain how changes in land tenure can be used to manage agricultural change. **[5]**

**11.** "Attempts to manage agricultural change usually create new problems while solving old ones." With reference to one country you have studied, how far do you agree? **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** Joining scattered, fragmented plots [1] into larger, compact holdings, usually by exchanging land between farmers [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Food insecurity: output may not keep up with population growth or demand [1], so the country depends on imports or aid [1]. Land degradation: practices such as over-irrigation or continuous cropping damage soil and water [1], threatening future output [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* also credit rural poverty, inequality between large and small farmers, or loss of young labour, each with a consequence.

**3. (a)** (2.6 − 2.0) ÷ 2.0 × 100 [1] = 30% increase [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Fewer, larger plots (2 instead of 9) are big enough for tractors to work efficiently [1], so hiring a tractor became worthwhile and tractor use rose from 10% to 45% [1]. Plots closer to home (0.9 km instead of 2.4 km) save time, so farmers can weed, water and guard crops more often [1]. Timely ploughing and sowing by tractor, with better field care, raise yields [1]. **[4]**

**(c)** Landless labourers [1], because harvest employment fell from 140 to 60 as machines replaced hand labour [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Guaranteed prices make those crops less risky than any alternative [1], so farmers keep growing them even where they harm soil or water [1]. Diversifying into other crops becomes harder because they lack the same security [1]. Removing the guarantee is politically difficult, because farmers who depend on it resist [1]. **[4]**

**5. (a)** Over 5 ha: 45 ÷ 10 = 4.5; under 1 ha: 20 ÷ 60 = 0.33 [1]; 4.5 ÷ 0.33 = 13.5 times more per farmer [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** The subsidy was unequal: 60% of farmers, those under 1 ha, received only 20% [1], while the 10% on over 5 ha received 45% [1]. This is partly expected, because larger farms use more fertiliser in total, so per hectare the gap may be smaller [1]. Overall it was unfair to smallholders, who are usually poorest and most in need, so the scheme may have widened inequality [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* "evaluate" needs a judgement; the strongest answers recognise that per-farmer and per-hectare views of fairness differ.

**6.** The local scale concerns decisions on an individual farm, holding or producer, such as changing crops or methods [1]. Change there depends on the household's land, labour, money and knowledge [1]. The national scale concerns government action across the whole country, such as laws, prices or land reform [1], which affects all farms but in different ways in different regions [1]. **[4]**

**7.** Credit any country. Example for China: commune farming from 1958 shared output collectively [1], so households had little incentive to raise production [1]. The 1959–61 famine showed the risks of centrally planned farming [1]. By the late 1970s output per person remained low and rural poverty was widespread [1]. Later, very small plots and rural out-migration kept incomes low [1], and heavy fertiliser use was polluting water and soil, creating a new need for change [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Credit any country. Example for China: holdings are very small, well under a hectare on average [1], and often split into scattered strips [1], so machinery is hard to use. Young adults migrate to cities, leaving older people and women to farm [1], who may be less able to adopt new methods [1]. Collectively owned land cannot be sold or offered as collateral [1], so households struggle to raise money for investment [1]. **[6]**

**9.** A local success may depend on local conditions such as soils, water or a market nearby [1] that do not exist in other regions [1]. Local schemes often have strong leadership or outside funding [1] that cannot be repeated everywhere [1]. National roll-out can meet resistance from groups with different interests, such as tenants, landowners or traders [1]. **[5]**

**10.** Redistribution gives land to tenants or the landless [1], so the people working the land keep the reward and invest more [1]. Longer or more secure contracts encourage long-term improvements such as terracing or irrigation [1]. Allowing land to be rented lets small or absent holders lease plots to larger farmers [1], creating holdings big enough for machinery [1]. **[5]**

**11.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point. The points below are indicative creditworthy content.

*Introduction:* define the management of agricultural change [1]; name the country and its starting problem, such as low output under commune farming in China [1].

*Agree, with case-study evidence:* household contracts raised output [1] but left equal, very small plots that now limit mechanisation [1]. Higher yields came partly from heavy fertiliser use [1], which polluted water and soil and needed new controls from 2015 [1]. Rising farm and urban incomes drew young people away [1], leaving an ageing farm workforce [1]. In India, the Green Revolution ended food shortage [1] but created falling water tables in Punjab [1].

*Disagree:* some solutions solved the original problem with few side effects, such as the rapid rise in output after decollectivisation [1]. Abolishing the agricultural tax in 2006 reduced costs to farmers without obvious new problems [1]. Later reforms, such as separating land rights, are designed to correct earlier side effects [1], so management can adapt [1].

*Analysis:* new problems often arise because solutions target one goal, such as output, and ignore sustainability or equity [1]. Problems appear at a different scale from the solution: national gains, local costs [1]. Time matters: side effects may only appear decades later [1].

*Use of scale:* local-scale evidence from a named village or farm type [1] and national-scale evidence from named policies [1].

*Judgement:* a reasoned conclusion on "how far", for example largely agreeing because most solutions changed the problem rather than ending it [1]. **[20]**

*Examiner insight:* the best answers trace one solution through time and show the new problem it created, rather than listing problems and solutions separately.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Case studies that stay at national scale only.
- Describing a policy without its outcome.
- Calculations that mix up share of farmers and share of subsidy.
- "Need for change" answers that describe the solution instead of the problem.
- Essays with a judgement only in the last line.
