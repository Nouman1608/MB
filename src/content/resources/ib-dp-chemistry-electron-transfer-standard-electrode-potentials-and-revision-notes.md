## Reactivity 3.2.12: definitions (HL only)

- **Standard hydrogen electrode:** H⁺(aq) + e⁻ ⇌ ½H₂(g). Platinum electrode, H₂ at 100 kPa, [H⁺] = 1.00 mol dm⁻³, 298 K. Its E⦵ is **0.00 V by convention**.
- **Why a reference?** A voltmeter can only measure a difference between two half-cells, so one half-cell has to be defined as zero.
- **Standard electrode potential, E⦵:** the voltage a half-cell gives when paired with the hydrogen reference, everything at standard conditions.
- **Sign:** a half-cell that is the negative terminal against hydrogen has a negative E⦵.
- **Reading the table:** values are reductions. The more positive the E⦵, the more easily the oxidized form is reduced (stronger oxidizing agent). The more negative the E⦵, the more easily the reduced form is oxidized (stronger reducing agent).

## Key equations

| Quantity | Equation | Units and notes |
|---|---|---|
| Cell potential | E⦵cell = E⦵(cathode) − E⦵(anode) | V; use both values as tabulated |
| Gibbs energy | ΔG⦵ = −nFE⦵cell | J mol⁻¹; divide by 1000 for kJ mol⁻¹ |
| Rearranged | E⦵cell = −ΔG⦵ ÷ nF | ΔG⦵ in J mol⁻¹ |
| Link to K (Reactivity 1.4) | ΔG⦵ = −RT lnK | R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ |

## Reactivity 3.2.13 Method: is the reaction spontaneous? (HL only)

1. Split the equation into two half-equations.
2. The species gaining electrons is at the cathode. Find its E⦵.
3. The species losing electrons is at the anode. Find the E⦵ of its couple.
4. E⦵cell = E⦵(cathode) − E⦵(anode).
5. Positive: spontaneous as written. Negative: the reverse reaction is spontaneous.

**Worked reminder.** Magnesium and tin half-cells: E⦵cell = −0.14 − (−2.37) = **+2.23 V**. Mg is the anode (negative). Mg(s) + Sn²⁺(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + Sn(s).

## Reactivity 3.2.14 Method: ΔG⦵ from E⦵ (HL only)

1. Write the balanced overall equation.
2. Count n: the moles of electrons transferred in that equation.
3. Put E⦵cell in volts and F = 96 500 C mol⁻¹ into ΔG⦵ = −nFE⦵cell.
4. Convert J mol⁻¹ to kJ mol⁻¹ and keep the sign.

**Worked reminder.** For the magnesium–tin reaction, n = 2: ΔG⦵ = −2 × 96 500 × 2.23 = −430 390 J mol⁻¹ = **−430 kJ mol⁻¹**.

The thermodynamic route (ΔG⦵ = ΔH⦵ − TΔS⦵, Reactivity 1.4) and the electrochemical route give the same quantity, so they always agree on spontaneity.

## Reactivity 3.2.15 Method: products of aqueous electrolysis (HL only)

Electrolytic cell: anode **positive** (oxidation), cathode **negative** (reduction).

**Cathode.** Compare the cation with water, 2H₂O + 2e⁻ → H₂ + 2OH⁻ (−0.83 V).
- Cation E⦵ more positive than −0.83 V (for example Cu²⁺, Ag⁺): the **metal** forms.
- Cation E⦵ more negative (for example Na⁺, K⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺): **hydrogen** forms.

**Anode (inert electrode).** Compare the anion with water, 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ (+1.23 V).
- I⁻ and Br⁻ are oxidized more easily: the **halogen** forms.
- SO₄²⁻ and NO₃⁻ are not oxidized: **oxygen** forms.
- Cl⁻: depends on concentration (below).

**Worked reminder.** Aqueous copper(II) bromide, inert electrodes. Cathode: Cu²⁺ (+0.34 V) is more positive than water (−0.83 V), so Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu(s). Anode: Br⁻ (+1.09 V) is easier to oxidize than water (+1.23 V), so 2Br⁻(aq) → Br₂(aq) + 2e⁻. You would see a pink-brown coating on the cathode and an orange-brown colour near the anode.

**Electrolysis of water** (a little acid or sodium sulfate added): H₂ at the cathode, O₂ at the anode, 2 : 1 by volume. E⦵cell = −1.23 V, so more than 1.23 V must be applied.

### The two guide-specified cases

| Electrolyte and electrodes | Cathode | Anode | Change in solution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Concentrated NaCl(aq), inert | H₂ | Cl₂ | NaOH forms |
| Dilute NaCl(aq), inert | H₂ | mainly O₂ | becomes more concentrated |
| CuSO₄(aq), inert (graphite or Pt) | Cu | O₂ | blue fades; becomes acidic |
| CuSO₄(aq), copper electrodes | Cu | Cu dissolves: Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ | colour unchanged |

## Reactivity 3.2.16 Electroplating (HL only)

- Object to be plated = **cathode**: Mⁿ⁺(aq) + ne⁻ → M(s).
- Plating metal = **anode**: M(s) → Mⁿ⁺(aq) + ne⁻.
- Electrolyte = a solution of the plating metal's ions; its concentration stays roughly constant.
- Practical points: clean and degrease the object first; a low current gives an even, firmly attached coat; weigh before and after to find the mass deposited.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Voltaic vs electrolytic cell:** in a voltaic cell the anode is negative; in an electrolytic cell the anode is positive. Oxidation is at the anode in both.
- **E⦵ vs ΔG⦵ when an equation is scaled:** E⦵cell is unchanged; ΔG⦵ scales with n.
- **Spontaneous vs fast:** a positive E⦵cell predicts direction, not rate.
- **Concentrated vs dilute NaCl(aq):** chlorine vs mainly oxygen at the anode.
- **Inert vs copper anode in CuSO₄(aq):** oxygen vs the anode dissolving.
- **Standard vs non-standard:** E⦵ values hold only for standard conditions; other temperatures or concentrations change the potential.

## Quick self-test

Use the data at the top of the page.

1. Why must one half-cell be assigned a potential of zero?
2. Which is the stronger reducing agent, Fe or Sn?
3. Calculate E⦵cell for a cell made from Mg²⁺/Mg and Cu²⁺/Cu half-cells.
4. Is Ag(s) + Fe³⁺(aq) → Ag⁺(aq) + Fe²⁺(aq) spontaneous under standard conditions?
5. A reaction has E⦵cell = +0.50 V and n = 3. Calculate ΔG⦵.
6. A reaction has ΔG⦵ = −193 kJ mol⁻¹ and n = 1. Calculate E⦵cell.
7. Calculate E⦵cell and ΔG⦵ for 2Al(s) + 3Sn²⁺(aq) → 2Al³⁺(aq) + 3Sn(s).
8. Name the products at each electrode when aqueous sodium nitrate is electrolysed with inert electrodes.
9. Name the anode product for aqueous sodium iodide with inert electrodes.
10. Write the anode half-equation for electrolysis of CuSO₄(aq) with copper electrodes.
11. How does the anode product change when concentrated NaCl(aq) is replaced by a dilute solution?
12. A steel bolt is electroplated with zinc. Name the cathode and anode, and write the cathode half-equation.

### Answers

1. Only potential differences can be measured, so a reference (the hydrogen half-cell) is defined as 0.00 V.
2. **Fe** (−0.45 V is more negative than −0.14 V).
3. 0.34 − (−2.37) = **+2.71 V**.
4. E⦵cell = 0.77 − 0.80 = **−0.03 V**, so **no**; the reverse is spontaneous.
5. −3 × 96 500 × 0.50 = −144 750 J mol⁻¹ = **−145 kJ mol⁻¹**.
6. 193 000 ÷ (1 × 96 500) = **+2.00 V**.
7. E⦵cell = −0.14 − (−1.66) = **+1.52 V**; n = 6, ΔG⦵ = −6 × 96 500 × 1.52 = **−880 kJ mol⁻¹**.
8. Cathode **hydrogen** (Na⁺ at −2.71 V is below water); anode **oxygen** (nitrate is not oxidized).
9. **Iodine** (+0.54 V, easier to oxidize than water).
10. **Cu(s) → Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻**.
11. From **chlorine** to mainly **oxygen**.
12. Cathode: the **bolt**; anode: **zinc**; **Zn²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Zn(s)**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Changing the sign of the anode E⦵ and then subtracting it as well.
- Doubling or tripling an E⦵ value to match the electrons in a balanced equation.
- Taking n as the charge on one ion rather than the total electrons in the overall equation.
- Leaving ΔG⦵ in J mol⁻¹, or dropping the negative sign.
- Saying sodium, potassium or aluminium is deposited from aqueous solution.
- Giving chlorine for dilute NaCl(aq), or oxygen for a copper anode in CuSO₄(aq).
- Mixing up polarities: the anode is negative in a voltaic cell but positive in an electrolytic cell.
- Putting the object at the anode in electroplating.
- Assuming a positive E⦵cell means a fast reaction.
