## Definition and formula

**Price elasticity of supply** measures the responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in the price of the good itself.

```
PES = percentage change in quantity supplied / percentage change in price
```

Always use percentages, never raw changes, so that the answer does not depend on the units of measurement. Work out each percentage from the **original** value.

### Worked example 1: from a schedule

An invented factory makes car parts. The price of a part rises from 50 cu to 55 cu and monthly output rises from 2,000 to 2,300.

```
% change in price            = (55 - 50) / 50 x 100       = +10%
% change in quantity supplied = (2,300 - 2,000) / 2,000 x 100 = +15%
PES = 15 / 10 = +1.5
```

Each 1% rise in price is met by a 1.5% rise in quantity supplied. Supply is **price elastic**.

### Worked example 2: rearranging the formula

Data-response questions often give the PES and ask for one of the percentages. Rearrange:

```
% change in quantity supplied = PES x % change in price
% change in price needed      = % change in quantity supplied / PES
```

- A fishing fleet has PES of 0.4. The price of fish rises by 15%. Quantity supplied rises by 0.4 x 15 = **6%**.
- A government wants farmers to supply 8% more maize. If PES is 0.25, the price would need to rise by 8 / 0.25 = **32%**. If PES were 2, a rise of only 8 / 2 = **4%** would be enough.

The second calculation shows why PES matters for policy: when supply is inelastic, a large price incentive is needed to call forth a small increase in output.

## Size and sign of the coefficient

The **size** compares the two percentage changes.

| Value | Term | What the relative percentages show |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Perfectly inelastic | Whatever the price change, output offered stays the same |
| Between 0 and 1 | Inelastic | A 1% price change brings less than a 1% change in output offered |
| 1 | Unitary | A 1% price change brings exactly a 1% change in output offered |
| Greater than 1 | Elastic | A 1% price change brings more than a 1% change in output offered |
| Infinite | Perfectly elastic | Firms will offer any amount at one price, but nothing below it |

The **sign** is normally **positive**, because a higher price makes supplying more worthwhile, so price and quantity supplied move in the same direction. The positive sign is what separates PES from PED, which is normally negative. If you calculate a negative PES for an ordinary product, check your working first: you may have used the wrong base or swapped the numerator and denominator.

## Factors affecting PES

Each factor works through one question: **how quickly and cheaply can firms change output?**

| Factor | More elastic when... | Less elastic when... |
|---|---|---|
| Time period | firms have longer to adjust plant, labour and entry | the period is too short to change fixed factors |
| Spare capacity | machines and workers are idle | the firm is already at full capacity |
| Stocks | finished goods can be stored and released | the good is perishable or cannot be stored |
| Mobility of factors | resources can be switched easily from other uses | inputs are specialised or skilled labour is scarce |
| Length of production | output can be made quickly | production takes months or years |
| Entry to the market | new firms can join easily | barriers such as licences or high set-up costs exist |

### Applying the factors

- **Cocoa.** New trees take several years to bear fruit, so even a large price rise brings little extra output for years: very inelastic in the short run.
- **Seats at a concert on the night.** The hall holds a fixed number: perfectly inelastic for that event.
- **Fresh fish.** Catches can rise only as far as boats and fish stocks allow, and fish cannot be stored for long: inelastic.
- **Downloadable software.** Once the program exists, one more copy costs almost nothing to supply: very elastic, close to perfectly elastic.
- **Plastic bottles at a factory running one shift.** Extra shifts use idle machines: elastic.

In an answer, name the factor, then say why it speeds up or slows down the response for that product. Listing factors without the product context earns few marks.

## Implications for how firms react

Section 2.3.5 asks what PES means for the **speed and ease** of reaction to changed market conditions. Compare three invented supply curves, all passing through a price of 10 cu and a quantity of 100 units. Demand is Qd = 150 - 5P and then rises to Qd = 170 - 5P, so 20 more units are demanded at the old price.

```
Perfectly inelastic supply (Q fixed at 100):
  100 = 170 - 5P -> P = 14     price +40%, quantity unchanged

Supply Qs = 10P (PES = 1):
  170 - 5P = 10P -> P = 11.33, Q = 113.3   price +13.3%, quantity +13.3%

Perfectly elastic supply (P fixed at 10):
  P = 10, Q = 170 - 50 = 120   price unchanged, quantity +20%
```

The same rise in demand produces very different results.

- **Inelastic supply:** firms cannot react quickly, so the adjustment falls mainly on **price**. Shortages and sharp price rises follow, as in markets for farm produce after a sudden rise in demand.
- **Elastic supply:** firms raise output easily, so the adjustment falls mainly on **quantity** and price hardly changes.

Firms with inelastic supply can make their supply more elastic by holding stocks, keeping some spare capacity, training flexible staff or using shorter production methods. Each of these has a cost, so a firm must weigh the cost of flexibility against the revenue it could gain by responding to price changes.

## Annotated model answer

**Question:** Explain why the price elasticity of supply of agricultural products is often lower than that of manufactured goods. **[4]**

> Farm output depends on **growing seasons**, so when price rises a farmer must wait months, or years for tree crops, before more can be harvested *(1: production time)*. Many farm products are **perishable**, so they cannot be held in stock and released when price rises *(1: stocks)*. Manufacturers can often run **spare capacity**, adding shifts or overtime to raise output within days *(1: spare capacity, contrasted)*. Factory goods can usually be **stored**, so stocks can be sold as soon as price rises *(1: stocks, contrasted)*.

Each mark comes from a named factor applied to the product, with a contrast between the two sectors.

## Common mistakes

- Calculating percentage changes from the new value instead of the original one.
- Putting the price change on top of the formula, which gives the reciprocal.
- Describing a PES of 0.7 as "elastic" because it is close to 1.
- Treating "time" as a factor without explaining what can change as time passes.
- Confusing PES with PED: PES measures producers' response, not consumers'.
- Writing that a perfectly elastic supply curve is vertical. It is horizontal.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate** questions: show the two percentage changes, then the division, and give the sign.
- **Define** PES precisely: responsiveness of **quantity supplied** to a change in the good's **own price**.
- **Explain** a factor: name it, link it to how fast output can change, apply it to the product in the question.
- **Assess** or **Evaluate** in Paper 2 essays: consider time. A product with inelastic supply in the short run can become elastic in the long run, so the right policy or business decision may depend on the period being considered.
