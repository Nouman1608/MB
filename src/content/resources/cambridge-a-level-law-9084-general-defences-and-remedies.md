Sub-topic 4.4 of Cambridge International A Level Law (9084) completes Topic 4. It covers the
**general defences**, which can be raised against most torts, and the **remedies** a court can
award. On Paper 4 Law of Tort almost every scenario ends with defences and remedies, and essays often
ask you to evaluate damages or injunctions. This guide goes beyond the
[Topic 4 overview](/resources/cambridge-a-level-law-law-of-tort-study-guide/) by covering every
defence the syllabus names and the full range of damages and injunctions.

## What the syllabus lists

- **4.4.1 Defences:** volenti non fit injuria (meaning of consent, knowledge and understanding,
  application in employment, medical treatment and sport); contributory negligence (a partial
  defence, apportionment and reduction of damages); inevitable accident, Act of God, statutory
  authority, illegality and necessity.
- **4.4.2 Remedies:** the purpose and calculation of damages; special and general damages;
  non-compensatory damages; damages for personal injury, future losses and death; policy issues and
  possible reforms; injunctions (mandatory, prohibitory and interlocutory), damages in lieu, and
  evaluation of injunctions in tort.

## 4.4.1 Defences

### Volenti non fit injuria

Volenti ("to one who is willing, no harm is done") is a **complete defence**. The defendant must show
that the claimant, with full **knowledge and understanding** of the nature and extent of the risk,
freely agreed to accept it. Knowledge alone is not consent: in Smith v Baker (1891) a quarry worker who
knew stones were being swung overhead had not agreed to run the risk.

- **Employment.** Because employees often have no real choice, the defence rarely succeeds (Bowater v
  Rowley Regis Corporation, 1944). An exception is ICI v Shatwell (1965), where two experienced
  shotfirers deliberately broke safety rules together.
- **Medical treatment.** A patient's consent to a procedure prevents a claim in battery, but consenting
  to treatment is not consenting to negligent treatment.
- **Sport.** Participants accept the risks inherent in the game but not reckless or deliberate
  conduct outside it (Condon v Basi, 1985, a reckless tackle). Spectators accept the risk of an error
  of judgement in the heat of competition, not a reckless disregard for their safety (Wooldridge v
  Sumner, 1963).
- **Limits.** Rescuers are not treated as volunteers (Haynes v Harwood, 1935; Baker v TE Hopkins,
  1959). A driving instructor was not volens in Nettleship v Weston (1971). For car passengers, s149
  of the Road Traffic Act 1988 prevents the defence. It did succeed in Morris v Murray (1991), where the
  claimant flew with a pilot he knew was very drunk.

### Contributory negligence

Under s1(1) of the Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act 1945, where the claimant's own fault
contributes to the damage, damages are reduced to the extent the court thinks **just and equitable**,
having regard to the claimant's share in the responsibility. It is a **partial** defence.

- The claimant's fault need not cause the accident, only contribute to the damage (Froom v Butcher,
  1976, seat belts; O'Connell v Jackson, 1972, a moped rider without a crash helmet).
- The test is objective: the claimant failed to take reasonable care for their own safety (Jones v
  Livox Quarries, 1952). Children are judged by their age: in Gough v Thorne (1966) a 13-year-old girl
  was not contributorily negligent.
- Accepting a lift from a driver known to be drunk can be contributory negligence (Owens v Brimmell, 1977).
- A 100 per cent reduction is not possible, because the claimant would then recover nothing, which the
  Act does not contemplate (Pitts v Hunt, 1991).

### Other general defences

- **Inevitable accident.** The harm could not have been avoided by reasonable care. In Stanley v Powell
  (1891) a pellet that glanced off a tree and hit a beater gave no liability. In negligence this is
  simply a finding of no breach.
- **Act of God.** A natural event that no human foresight could guard against (Nichols v Marsland, 1876,
  an extraordinary rainfall). Contrast Greenock Corporation v Caledonian Railway (1917), where heavy
  rain was not exceptional enough.
- **Statutory authority.** Where Parliament authorises an activity, harm that is its inevitable result
  is not actionable (Allen v Gulf Oil Refining, 1981). The defence does not cover harm caused by
  carrying out the activity negligently.
- **Illegality (ex turpi causa).** A claim arising from the claimant's own serious wrongdoing may be
  barred (Pitts v Hunt, joint illegal enterprise; Gray v Thames Trains, 2009, losses flowing from the
  claimant's own criminal act of manslaughter). It is not automatic: in Revill v Newbery (1996) a
  burglar shot by a householder recovered damages, reduced for contributory negligence. In Patel v Mirza
  (2016) the Supreme Court adopted a range-of-factors approach based on the purpose of the rule broken
  and proportionality.
- **Necessity.** The defendant acted to prevent greater harm to others or property. In Cope v Sharpe
  (1912) a gamekeeper who burned a firebreak on another's land was not liable. In Rigby v Chief
  Constable of Northamptonshire (1985) firing CS gas into a shop to flush out a gunman was necessary,
  though the police were liable in negligence for having no fire-fighting equipment ready.

## 4.4.2 Remedies

### Purpose and types of damages

**Compensatory damages** aim to restore the claimant, as far as money can, to the position they would
have been in had the tort not been committed (Livingstone v Rawyards Coal Co, 1880). They are divided
into:

- **Special damages:** quantifiable losses to the date of trial, such as medical expenses, repairs and
  lost earnings;
- **General damages:** losses not precisely calculable, including pain, suffering and loss of amenity,
  and future losses.

**Other kinds of damages** include:

- **Nominal damages** to mark the infringement of a right where there is no loss, common in trespass.
- **Contemptuous damages**, a tiny sum showing the court's view that the claim, though technically
  successful, had no merit (Grobbelaar v News Group Newspapers, 2002, £1).
- **Aggravated damages**, compensating extra injury to feelings caused by the manner of the tort.
- **Exemplary damages**, punishing the defendant, available only in limited categories set out in
  Rookes v Barnard (1964): oppressive, arbitrary or unconstitutional action by government servants;
  conduct calculated to make a profit exceeding the compensation payable; and where statute authorises
  them.

### Personal injury, future losses and death

- **Pain, suffering and loss of amenity** are awarded even where the claimant cannot appreciate the loss
  (H West and Son v Shephard, 1964).
- **Future loss of earnings and care** are calculated by multiplying the annual loss (the multiplicand)
  by a figure reflecting the period of loss (the multiplier), adjusted using actuarial tables and a
  discount rate for the return on investing the lump sum.
- A **lump sum** is final and must guess the future. The courts may instead make **periodical payment
  orders** under the Damages Act 1996, or award **provisional damages** where there is a chance of
  serious deterioration.
- **Death:** the Law Reform (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1934 allows the deceased's estate to continue
  claims the deceased had; the Fatal Accidents Act 1976 allows dependants to claim for loss of
  financial support and certain relatives to claim a fixed bereavement award.

### Policy and reform

Lump sums can over- or under-compensate; litigation is slow and expensive; the fault requirement means
two people with identical injuries may receive very different outcomes; and the costs fall partly on
insurers and public bodies. Reform options include wider use of periodical payments, no-fault
compensation schemes for particular injuries, and fixed tariffs for minor claims.

### Injunctions

An injunction is an **equitable, discretionary** order.

- **Prohibitory** injunctions order the defendant to stop, for example to limit a nuisance. In
  Kennaway v Thompson (1981) powerboat racing was restricted to certain days rather than banned.
- **Mandatory** injunctions order the defendant to do something positive. Because they are onerous,
  the courts apply strict conditions (Redland Bricks v Morris, 1970).
- **Interlocutory (interim)** injunctions preserve the position until trial. American Cyanamid v Ethicon
  (1975) asks whether there is a serious question to be tried and where the balance of convenience lies.
- **Damages in lieu of an injunction** may be awarded under the court's statutory power, first given by
  Lord Cairns' Act 1858. Shelfer v City of London Electric Lighting (1895) suggested damages only where
  the injury is small, can be measured in money and an injunction would be oppressive; Coventry v
  Lawrence (2014) said that test should not be applied mechanically, and public interest can be weighed.
  Examples include Miller v Jackson (1977) and Dennis v Ministry of Defence (2003), noise from RAF jets.

**Evaluation.** Injunctions stop continuing wrongs, which damages cannot, but they can be blunt, harm
third parties and the public interest, and are hard to enforce. Damages in lieu let the court balance
interests but can amount to allowing a defendant to buy the right to commit a tort.

## Worked example

*Scenario: Tariq, a passenger, is injured when his friend Lewis, whom he knew had drunk heavily,
crashes. Tariq was not wearing a seat belt; wearing it would have reduced but not prevented his
injuries. His losses: special damages £18,000 and general damages £42,000.*

1. **Volenti:** excluded for passengers by s149 Road Traffic Act 1988.
2. **Contributory negligence:** accepting a lift from a drunk driver (Owens v Brimmell) and not wearing a
   belt (Froom v Butcher, 15 per cent where injuries would have been reduced) both count.
3. **Total loss:** £18,000 + £42,000 = £60,000. If the court sets a combined reduction of 30 per cent,
   £60,000 × 0.30 = £18,000 is deducted.
4. **Award:** £60,000 − £18,000 = £42,000.

## Common mistakes

- Treating knowledge of a risk as consent (Smith v Baker).
- Applying contributory negligence as a complete defence, or reducing by 100 per cent.
- Confusing aggravated damages (compensation) with exemplary damages (punishment).
- Assuming an injunction follows automatically from a successful nuisance claim.

*evaluate* essays, weigh lump sums against periodical payments, or injunctions against damages in lieu.
