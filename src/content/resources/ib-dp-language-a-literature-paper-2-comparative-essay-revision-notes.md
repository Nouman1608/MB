## A timing plan (a suggestion, not an IB rule)

| Stage | Minutes |
|---|---|
| Read all four questions, sort them, fit-test two pairs, choose | 5 |
| Thesis, point-by-point plan, jot a quotation cluster for each point | 15 |
| Introduction, four body paragraphs, final paragraph | 75 |
| Check key terms, quotations and balance | 10 |
| **Total** | **105** |

## Method in steps: from question to plan

> 1. **Sort** the question: human concern, craft feature, or readers and contexts (or a mix).
> 2. **Underline** the key terms. Every body paragraph must use at least one.
> 3. **Find the double demand**: "how" *and* "to what effect"; similarity *and* difference.
> 4. **Fit-test**: one precise moment from each work per key term. Fewer than three comparable points
>    means change the pair or the question.
> 5. **Thesis**, then **three or four points**, each with evidence from both works.

## Thesis checks

A Paper 2 thesis passes all four:

1. It uses the question's own key terms.
2. It names both works.
3. It contains a "but", "while" or "whereas": a shared claim *and* a difference.
4. It says *to what effect*, so a reasonable reader could disagree.

Pattern: *Both A and B present [key term] as [shared claim], but where A [method] so that [effect],
B [method] so that [effect].*

## Paragraph moves (the hinged paragraph)

> 1. **Comparative topic sentence**: both works, one point, linked to the question.
> 2. **Work A**: precise evidence, method named, effect explained.
> 3. **Work B**: precise evidence joined by a comparative connective.
> 4. **Hinge**: what the difference (or likeness) in method shows.
> 5. **Link** back to the thesis.

Intro in 4–5 sentences: define the key term for this essay, name each work with author, form and date,
thesis, preview. Final paragraph: answer the question in one sentence, return to the biggest
difference, no new evidence.

### Worked reminder: topic sentences

Question (original, written for Marlbridge): *"Discuss how two works you have studied present a
character who cannot escape the consequences of an earlier choice."*

- *Weak:* "Jekyll makes a bad choice." (one work, no comparison, no key term)
- *Better:* "Jekyll and Dorian both suffer for an earlier choice." (both works, no method)
- *Strong:* "Jekyll and Dorian both try to escape an earlier choice, but Stevenson lets Jekyll explain
  it in his own final statement, while Wilde shows the consequences mainly on the portrait, so one
  consequence is confessed and the other is seen."

The strong version gives the paragraph its evidence (the statement; the portrait) and its hinge
(confessed versus seen).

## Connectives by job

| Job | Connectives |
|---|---|
| Likeness | similarly, in the same way, likewise, both ... |
| Difference | whereas, while, by contrast, unlike, where A ..., B ... |
| Qualification | although, yet, even so, at first ... but later |
| Consequence | so, therefore, as a result, this means |

A connective signals a comparison; it does not make one. The hinge sentence (move 4) does.

## Remembered quotation: the rules

> - **Short**: three to ten words where possible.
> - **Located**: know the speaker or narrator and roughly where it falls in the work.
> - **Tagged**: one technique and one concern for each quotation.
> - **Clustered**: learn quotations in groups that serve several possible questions.
> - **Tested**: cover, write from memory, check every word, repeat after a few days.
> - **Honest**: unsure of the words? Paraphrase precisely, without quotation marks.
> - **Embedded**: fit the quotation into your sentence's grammar and comment on it.

## Time and space in one sentence

Pattern: *Because [work] was written for/in [context], its [method] [effect], whereas [other work]
[method] [effect].*

Example: Because Wilde's novel met accusations of immorality in 1890, the 1891 Preface insists "All art
is quite useless", whereas Stevenson gives readers a case to solve through a lawyer's investigation.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Topic vs method.** "Both are about secrets" compares topics. "Stevenson withholds the secret until
  two written statements at the end, while Wilde shows it changing on the canvas as it happens"
  compares methods. Paper 2 rewards the second.
- **Block vs integrated.** Block: half the essay on each work. Integrated: both works in every body
  paragraph.
- **Quotation vs reference vs paraphrase.** A quotation uses the exact words in quotation marks. A
  reference points precisely to a moment. A paraphrase gives the sense in your own words. All three are
  evidence; only the first needs to be word-perfect.
- **Context that explains vs context that decorates.** Explaining context answers "why does this work
  do it this way?"; decorative context could be cut without loss.
- **Likeness vs difference.** A question that asks "in what ways" still expects both; a thesis with no
  difference has little to argue.

### Answers

1. Two literary works: a comparative essay based on them, written in response to one of four questions.
2. 1.75 hours (1 hour 45 minutes) at both levels.
3. 35% at SL; 25% at HL.
4. Human concern; feature of craft; readers and contexts.
5. Methods (how) and their effect (what they do to the reader's understanding). Both must be answered.
6. Uses the question's key terms; names both works; contains a difference ("but", "while", "whereas");
   says to what effect, so it is arguable.
7. It states what the difference or likeness in method shows, turning two pieces of evidence into a
   comparison.
8. Paraphrase precisely without quotation marks, for example: Utterson resolves to hunt Hyde down,
   punning on the name "Hyde".
9. For example: Standing before the finished portrait, Dorian wishes that "it were I who was to be
   always young", a wish that sets the plot in motion and turns the painting into the one thing that
   ages.
10. Not on its own. It becomes useful only if linked to a method or a difference between the works,
    for example how the narrative's lawyer and documents suit a society of professional reputation.
11. Answer the question directly and return to the most significant difference; avoid new evidence
    and a point-by-point repeat of the introduction.

## Where marks are usually lost

- A thesis that restates the question or names a topic without a claim.
- Half the double demand ignored: methods listed with no effect, or likeness with no difference.
- Body paragraphs about one work each, with "similarly" as the only link.
- One work supported by exact quotations, the other by plot summary.
- Misquotation, including merging two separate quotations into one.
- Quotations dropped in without comment, or not fitting the grammar of the sentence.
- A prepared essay forced onto a question it does not answer.
- Context paragraphs of biography that explain nothing about either work's methods.
- A final paragraph that only repeats the introduction.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long is Paper 2 in IB Language A: Literature?**
Paper 2 lasts 1 hour 45 minutes at both SL and HL. It is a comparative essay on two literary works, answering one of four questions, worth 35% at SL and 25% at HL.

**What are the four checks for a Paper 2 thesis?**
The thesis should use the question's key terms, name both works, contain a difference signalled by "but", "while" or "whereas", and say to what effect, so that a reasonable reader could disagree.

**What is the difference between block and integrated comparison?**
Block comparison spends half the essay on each work. Integrated comparison discusses both works in every body paragraph, which is what Paper 2 rewards.

**What is the difference between a quotation, a reference and a paraphrase?**
A quotation uses the exact words in quotation marks; a reference points precisely to a moment; a paraphrase gives the sense in your own words. All three count as evidence, but only a quotation must be word-perfect.
