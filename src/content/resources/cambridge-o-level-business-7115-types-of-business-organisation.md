This guide covers subtopic **1.4 Types of business organisation** of Cambridge O Level Business Studies 7115 (2026 series). The combined [Types of Business Organisation and Business Objectives study guide](/resources/o-level-business-types-of-business-organisation-and-business-objectives-and-stakeholder/) sets out the main features of each form with calculations of liability and profit shares. This page focuses on the reasoning behind the choice: how the forms differ in ownership, control, risk and continuity, how a business moves between forms as it grows, and how to write a recommendation that is fully justified.

## What the syllabus asks for

Section 1.4.1, the main features of different forms of business organisation:

- sole traders, partnerships, private and public limited companies, franchises and joint ventures
- differences between unincorporated businesses and limited companies
- concepts of risk, ownership and limited liability
- recommend and justify a suitable form of business organisation to owners or management in a given situation
- business organisations in the public sector, for example public corporations.

## Four questions that separate the forms

When comparing forms, ask the same four questions each time.

| Question | Sole trader | Partnership | Private limited company | Public limited company |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Who owns it? | One person | Two or more partners | Shareholders, often family or founders | Shareholders, including the general public |
| Who controls it? | The owner | The partners together | Directors, usually also the main shareholders | Directors elected by shareholders |
| What can owners lose? | Everything, including personal assets | Everything, in the usual form | Only the money invested in shares | Only the money invested in shares |
| Does it continue if an owner dies or leaves? | Not as the same business | The partnership usually has to be re-formed | Yes; shares pass to someone else | Yes; shares are sold or passed on |

### Unincorporated or incorporated?

An **unincorporated** business (sole trader, partnership) has no legal identity separate from its owners. Contracts and debts are the owners' own, which is why liability is unlimited.

A **limited company** is **incorporated**: it is a separate legal entity. It owns its assets, signs contracts and can sue or be sued in its own name. Three results follow:

- shareholders have **limited liability**
- the company has **continuity**, because its existence does not depend on any one owner
- it must meet more legal requirements, such as registering and publishing some financial information, which costs time and money and reduces privacy.

### Risk

Risk is the chance of losing money. Two different risks matter: the risk that the business fails, and the risk to the owners' personal wealth if it does. Limited liability does not make a business less likely to fail; it limits how much the owners personally lose. That is why suppliers and banks may ask for personal guarantees from the owners of small companies.

## Ownership and control in a public limited company

In a sole trader business the owner both owns and controls. In a large public limited company, thousands of shareholders own it, but directors and managers run it. This **separation of ownership and control** means:

- shareholders choose the directors but are not involved in daily decisions
- directors may pursue aims, such as rapid growth or high salaries, that do not match shareholders' wish for dividends
- because anyone can buy shares, a rival business could buy enough to gain control (a takeover).

## Franchises from both sides

| | Franchisor (owner of the brand) | Franchisee (runs an outlet) |
|---|---|---|
| Benefits | Grows quickly using franchisees' capital; earns fees and a share of revenue; local owners are motivated to succeed | Uses a known brand, so less risk of failure; receives training, supplies and national promotion |
| Limitations | Less direct control over each outlet; one poor outlet can damage the whole brand | Pays an initial fee and continuing royalties; must follow the franchisor's rules on products, prices and layout |

## Joint ventures

A joint venture is an agreement between two or more businesses to work together on a project, while each keeps its own identity. A typical reason is to enter a new market where one partner has local knowledge and the other has the product. Benefits: shared costs and risks, combined skills. Limitations: disagreements over control, and profits must be shared.

## How a business may change form as it grows

**Worked example (invented business).** Amara starts Tidewater Tutoring as a **sole trader** from her flat. Costs are low, she keeps all profit and decides everything. Two years later she wants to open a centre. She takes in a **partner**, Joel, who brings capital and maths teaching skills. Both now have unlimited liability for a lease of $40,000 a year, which worries them. They form **Tidewater Tutoring Ltd**: they become shareholders, their risk is limited to their shares, and the company continues even if one of them leaves. To open centres across the country quickly without raising all the capital themselves, they later **franchise** the Tidewater name to other tutors. They might eventually consider becoming a **public limited company** to raise large sums, accepting that they would lose some control.

Each change was driven by one of four needs: **more capital, less personal risk, continuity, or faster growth**. Use the same four needs in recommendation questions.

## Recommending and justifying a form

A strong answer does three things: chooses a form, links it to facts in the case, and weighs a drawback or an alternative.

**Annotated model answer.** *Case: Ravi makes handmade leather bags in a rented workshop and sells online. He wants to borrow $60,000 to buy machines and employ four workers. He wants to keep control.*

> "Ravi should form a private limited company. Borrowing $60,000 is a large risk; as a sole trader he would have unlimited liability and could lose his house if the business failed, but as a shareholder in a private limited company his loss would be limited to his investment." *(Application: the loan and personal risk from the case.)*
>
> "He can keep control by owning most or all of the shares, which he could not do in a public limited company, where shares are sold to the public." *(Analysis: links form to his wish for control and rejects an alternative.)*
>
> "The drawback is the cost of setting up the company and publishing some accounts, but this is small compared with the protection limited liability gives him when borrowing so much." *(Evaluation: weighs a drawback and reaches a justified judgement.)*

## Public sector organisations

A **public corporation** is a business owned by the government and run by a board appointed by the government. Examples in many countries include state-owned railways, postal services or electricity suppliers, though this varies.

- **Aims**: providing an essential service, keeping prices affordable, supplying areas that private businesses might not serve, and protecting jobs. They may also be expected to cover their costs.
- **Finance**: government funds, charges to users and sometimes loans.
- **Benefits**: important services are provided for everyone; decisions can consider social as well as financial costs.
- **Limitations**: managers may have less incentive to be efficient if losses are covered by the government; political interference can affect decisions; funding competes with other government spending.

## Common mistakes

- Saying a limited company "cannot fail" or has "no risk".
- Mixing up a public limited company with a public corporation.
- Claiming partners always share profit equally; it depends on the partnership agreement.
- Listing every advantage of a form without linking any of them to the case.
- Recommending a public limited company for a small business that wants to keep control.

## Exam technique for 7115 command words

- **Define**: for limited liability, say the owners can lose only the amount invested.
- **Explain**: give the feature, then why it matters, such as "separate legal entity, so the company continues if a shareholder dies".
- **Recommend and justify / Consider**: choose one form, use at least two facts from the case, and weigh a drawback.
