## What quality means

**Quality** is how well a product or service meets customers' expectations and needs. It is judged by the customer, not just the producer. For a physical good, quality can include reliability, durability, performance, appearance and safety. For a service, it includes the reliability of delivery, the competence and courtesy of staff, speed, and the setting. Quality is not the same as luxury: a basic bus ticket is high quality if the bus is on time, clean and safe.

Quality is also about **fitness for purpose** and **consistency**: customers expect the thousandth item to match the first.

## Methods of improving quality

### Quality control

**Quality control (QC)** means checking products or services, usually after they are made or at set stages, to find and remove those that do not meet the standard. Methods include inspection of every item, or of a **sample** from each batch, and testing against specifications.

- Strengths: catches defects before they reach customers; simple to understand; gives data on defect rates.
- Weaknesses: it finds faults **after** they happen, so waste and rework costs are already incurred; inspection costs money and adds no value; sampling can miss faults; inspectors may be seen as policing, which can reduce workers' sense of responsibility.

### Quality assurance

**Quality assurance (QA)** is a system for building quality into every stage of the process so that defects are **prevented** rather than found later. It covers design, suppliers' materials, training, procedures and continuous improvement. Every worker is responsible for the quality of their own work, and problems are traced to their cause.

Typical features:

- clear standards and procedures at each stage
- checking suppliers and inputs
- **training** and empowering employees
- **quality circles** (small groups who meet to find solutions to quality problems)
- **kaizen**, continuous small improvements
- **benchmarking** against the best in the industry
- **total quality management (TQM)**, a business-wide commitment to quality, with the aim of "right first time".

### Comparing QC and QA

| | Quality control | Quality assurance |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | Finding defects | Preventing defects |
| Timing | During or after production | Throughout, including design and supply |
| Responsibility | Inspectors | Everyone |
| Cost pattern | Ongoing inspection costs; rework and scrap | Higher spending on training and systems; fewer failures |
| Culture | Checking, policing | Ownership, improvement |

In practice most businesses use both: a QA system to prevent faults and some QC (such as sample testing) to check that the system works.

### Other methods

- **Better design and materials** (for example more durable parts).
- **Better equipment and maintenance**, because worn machines produce defects.
- **Customer feedback and mystery shoppers** in services, to measure what customers experience.
- **Technology**, such as sensors and automated inspection with cameras.

## The cost of quality

Businesses can think of quality costs in four groups:

| Type | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Prevention | Spending to stop defects | Training, design, supplier checks |
| Appraisal | Spending to find defects | Inspection, testing |
| Internal failure | Costs of defects found before delivery | Rework, scrap, wasted time |
| External failure | Costs of defects found by customers | Refunds, repairs, recalls, lost reputation |

Spending more on prevention usually reduces failure costs, particularly external failure, which is the most damaging.

### Worked example: from control to assurance

Penhallow Appliances makes 20,000 kettles a month. At present it relies on end-of-line inspection.

**Current position (quality control).**

- Defect rate 5%: 20,000 × 0.05 = **1,000** faulty kettles.
- Inspection catches 90%: **900** caught and reworked at £6 each = £5,400.
- **100** reach customers; each costs £40 in refund, replacement and handling = £4,000.
- Inspection costs £8,000 a month.
- Total quality cost = £8,000 + £5,400 + £4,000 = **£17,400**.

**Proposed position (quality assurance).** Penhallow spends £10,000 a month on training, supplier checks and process controls. The defect rate falls to 1.5% and inspection is reduced to £3,000.

- Faulty kettles: 20,000 × 0.015 = **300**.
- Inspection catches 90%: **270** reworked at £6 = £1,620.
- **30** reach customers at £40 = £1,200.
- Total quality cost = £10,000 + £3,000 + £1,620 + £1,200 = **£15,820**.

**Saving** = £17,400 − £15,820 = **£1,580 a month**. The saving looks small, but it leaves out the lost sales and reputation damage from 70 fewer customers receiving faulty kettles each month, and the improved morale of staff who take responsibility for quality. The case also depends on the figures being right: if the defect rate fell only to 3%, the saving would be smaller. The £10,000 is also a recurring cost, so the business must be sure the improvement lasts.

## Quality in services

Services are produced and consumed at the same moment, so a fault cannot be inspected out before the customer sees it. Quality control after the event is therefore weak in services, and quality assurance matters more. A hotel cannot "recall" a rude welcome. It must instead prevent the problem through recruitment, training, clear service standards, and well-designed processes such as quick check-in. Feedback from guests, reviews and mystery shoppers shows where the standard slips. Technology can help, for example by tracking waiting times, but staff attitude remains central.

## Sampling in quality control

Inspecting every item is costly, and it destroys the product in some tests (a firm cannot test every firework). Many businesses test a **sample** from each batch instead. If a sample of 500 items contains 15 faulty ones, the defect rate is 15 ÷ 500 × 100 = **3%**, and in a batch of 12,000 about 12,000 × 0.03 = **360** faulty items would be expected. The estimate is only as reliable as the sample: it must be random and large enough. Batches that fail the test can be rejected or inspected in full. The business sets an acceptable defect rate in advance, which should be linked to the cost of failure, and kept far lower for safety-critical products.

## Benefits of improving quality

- **Fewer defects**, so less waste, rework and scrap.
- **Fewer returns, refunds and warranty claims.**
- **Customer satisfaction and loyalty**, leading to repeat purchases and recommendations.
- **A better reputation and brand image**, which supports higher prices.
- **Lower costs overall** if prevention is more effective than correction.
- **Competitive advantage** over rivals with lower quality.
- **Employee motivation** when workers are trusted with responsibility and proud of their work.
- Easier to win contracts with demanding customers who insist on quality systems.

## Difficulties of improving quality

- **Cost**: training, new equipment and better materials require investment, with benefits that may take time to appear.
- **Cultural change**: staff and managers must accept responsibility; some resist the change.
- **Time**: reaching consistent quality takes months or years.
- **Potential conflict with other objectives**: cost, speed of response and flexibility may suffer. Extra checks slow production.
- **Customer expectations differ**: higher quality does not always mean the customer will pay more.
- **Measurement problems**, especially for services where quality is subjective.
- **Diminishing returns**: the last few percentage points of improvement may cost far more than the earlier ones.

## Consequences of poor quality

| Consequence | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Lost sales and customers | Dissatisfied customers switch to rivals and tell others |
| Damaged reputation | Reviews and social media spread complaints fast |
| Extra costs | Rework, scrap, returns, refunds, replacements and recalls |
| Legal action | Compensation claims and fines where products are unsafe |
| Lower prices | The brand may have to cut prices to sell poor products |
| Lower staff morale | Staff face complaints and blame; turnover may rise |
| Loss of supplier or retailer contracts | Retailers drop unreliable suppliers |
| In extreme cases, failure of the business | Particularly where safety is involved |

## Technique for exam questions

- Distinguish clearly between QA and QC. Use "prevent" and "detect".
- Link the method to the case, for example sample testing for large batches, or training for a service business.
- Evaluate by comparing the cost of improvement with the cost of failure, and by saying what the business's customers value.

## Common mistakes

- Treating quality as the same as expensive.
- Describing QA as "more inspections".
- Saying quality control prevents defects (it detects them).
- Giving only benefits of improved quality, with no difficulties.
- Forgetting that external failure is usually costlier than internal failure.
- Ignoring the effect of quality on the other operational objectives.
