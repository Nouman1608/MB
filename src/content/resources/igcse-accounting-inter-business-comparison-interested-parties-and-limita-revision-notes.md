## 6.3 Inter-business comparison

### Which factor moves which ratio

| Factor | Ratio it is most likely to move | Typical direction |
|---|---|---|
| Fast-selling, low-price goods | inventory turnover | more times, fewer days |
| Mostly cash sales | trade receivables turnover (days) | very few days |
| Longer credit from suppliers | trade payables turnover (days) | more days |
| Loans or debentures | ROCE | capital employed larger, ROCE lower |
| Premises owned, not rented | expenses and capital employed | rent falls, assets and depreciation rise |
| Bulk buying power | gross profit margin | higher |
| Low price, high volume policy | gross profit margin | lower |
| Different location | expenses and sales | rent, wages and customers differ |
| Different accounting policies | profit, assets, nearly every ratio | not caused by performance |

### Problems of inter-business comparison

- Missing detail (credit figures, opening inventory).
- Policies that differ between the two.
- Year ends that fall on other dates.
- Businesses that are not really similar.
- A single unusual year.
- Non-financial aspects left out.

### Method for a comparison answer

1. Quote the ratio for each business.
2. Say which looks stronger on that measure.
3. Give a factor that could explain the gap that is not management.
4. State one problem that limits the conclusion.

## 6.4 Interested parties

### Cue cards

| Party | Question it asks | Figures it reaches for |
|---|---|---|
| Owners | Is my money earning enough? | profit for the year, ROCE |
| Managers | Where do I act? | margins, expenses, inventory and receivables days |
| Employees | Is my job safe, can I be paid more? | profit trend, liquidity |
| Banks | Will the loan be repaid with interest? | current ratio, acid test, profit, assets |
| Investors | Will it grow, will it pay dividends? | profit trend, ROCE |
| Lenders | Will interest be paid and capital returned? | liquidity, existing loans |
| Suppliers | Will I be paid on time? | liquidity, payables days |
| Customers | Will it still be trading next year? | profit, liquidity |
| Governments / tax authorities | How much tax? Grants? Statistics? | profit for the year |
| Club members | Did subscriptions cover costs? | surplus or deficit |
| Public and environmental bodies | Effect on community and environment, jobs | overall performance |

Answer pattern: party, decision, figure. Investors take a share of risk,
lenders ask to be repaid, so keep them apart.

## 6.5 Limitations of accounting statements

| Cause | What goes wrong | Short example |
|---|---|---|
| Historic cost | Assets shown at purchase price, not current value; money of different years not comparable | Land bought long ago, now worth three times its cost |
| Application of accounting policies | Estimates and choices change profit and asset values | Straight line versus reducing balance depreciation |
| Non-financial aspects | Things not measured in money do not appear | Workforce skill, business location, economic climate |

Effects of a policy choice, other things equal:

| Choice | Effect on profit | Effect on asset |
|---|---|---|
| Higher depreciation charge | lower | non-current asset lower |
| Closing inventory valued lower | lower | inventory lower |
| Larger allowance for irrecoverable debts | lower | net trade receivables lower |

## Worked reminder: one policy, two margins

Fernside Cycles Limited has revenue of 200,000, opening inventory of
15,000 and purchases of 130,000 (figures in $). Its closing inventory is
20,000 at cost.

```
Cost of sales = 15,000 + 130,000 - 20,000 = 125,000
Gross profit  = 200,000 - 125,000          =  75,000
Gross profit margin = 75,000 / 200,000 x 100 = 37.50%
```

A rival values the same closing inventory at 17,000. Its cost of sales is
128,000, gross profit 72,000 and gross profit margin 36.00%. A gap of 1.50
percentage points appears from the valuation alone.

## Must-know distinctions

- A problem of inter-business comparison (6.3) concerns setting two
businesses side by side. A limitation of statements (6.5) concerns what a
single set of statements can show. Several points belong to both, so
label which one the question asks for.
- Historic cost is about what an asset is shown at. Depreciation is the
spreading of that cost. Do not use one word for the other.
- Owners put money in and take the profit. Managers run the business and
may not own it. Both read the statements, for different decisions.
- A non-financial aspect is real and can matter a great deal. It is missing
from the statements because it is not measured in money.

## Method in steps

For a 6.4 question:

1. Name the party.
2. State the decision it faces.
3. Name the figure or ratio that helps.
4. Say what the figure tells it.

For a 6.5 question:

1. Name the cause (historic cost, policy or non-financial aspect).
2. Give a specific example.
3. Say how it makes the statements less reliable or less comparable.

## Ratios used in these answers

Use only the syllabus's formulas.

| Ratio | Formula |
|---|---|
| ROCE | (Profit for the year before interest ÷ capital employed) × 100 |
| Current ratio | Current assets ÷ current liabilities (as a ratio) |
| Acid test | (Current assets - inventory) ÷ current liabilities (as a ratio) |
| Trade receivables turnover | Trade receivables ÷ credit sales × 365 days |
| Trade payables turnover | Trade payables ÷ credit purchases × 365 days |

Capital employed = issued shares + reserves + non-current liabilities.

## Quick self-test

All figures in $.

1. Current assets are 27,000, including inventory of 9,000. Current
liabilities are 12,000. Calculate the acid test ratio.
2. Profit before interest is 24,000. Shares are 70,000, reserves 30,000
and non-current liabilities 20,000. Calculate ROCE.
3. Trade receivables are 9,000 and credit sales 73,000. Calculate trade
receivables turnover in days.
4. Average inventory is 18,000 and cost of sales 219,000. Calculate
inventory turnover in days.
5. Name the three causes of limitation listed in the syllabus.
6. State two problems of inter-business comparison.
7. A machine costs 30,000 with no residual value. Business A depreciates it
straight line over five years. Business B uses 30% reducing balance. State
the first-year difference in profit.
8. A club of 80 members made a deficit of 1,200. Calculate the deficit
per member.
9. State one difference between how investors and lenders use accounting
information.
10. Why might a business with excellent statements still fail?

### Answers

1. (27,000 - 9,000) ÷ 12,000 = 1.50 : 1.
2. Capital employed = 120,000; 24,000 ÷ 120,000 × 100 = 20.00%.
3. 9,000 ÷ 73,000 × 365 = 45.00 days.
4. 18,000 ÷ 219,000 × 365 = 30.00 days.
5. Historic cost; application of accounting policies; non-financial aspects.
6. Any two: missing detail; different policies; different year ends;
businesses not alike; unusual year; non-financial aspects ignored.
7. A: 6,000. B: 9,000. Business B's profit is 3,000 lower.
8. 1,200 ÷ 80 = 15.00 per member.
9. An investor looks for growth and dividends; a lender looks for interest
paid and the loan repaid.
10. Statements leave out items such as workforce skill, location and the
economic climate, any of which can cause failure.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving the reason for a gap without naming the ratio it affects.
- Treating inter-business comparison as a test of management only.
- Writing "different accounting policies" without a policy (depreciation
method, inventory valuation, allowance for irrecoverable debts).
- Listing parties but not saying what each decides.
- Merging investors and lenders into one answer.
- Quoting a non-financial aspect as a number.
- Calling historic cost "wrong" instead of explaining that it shows past
price, not current value.
- Stating a conclusion without one limit on the comparison.
