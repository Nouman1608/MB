## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Condensation polymerisation | Monomers with two functional groups join, losing a small molecule (usually H₂O) at each link |
| Polyester | Condensation polymer with ester links, –COO–, from a dicarboxylic acid and a diol |
| Polyamide | Condensation polymer with amide links, –CONH–, from a dicarboxylic acid and a diamine, or from amino acids |
| Repeating unit | The smallest section that repeats; for two monomers it holds one residue of each |
| Hydrolysis | Breaking a bond by reaction with water; for these polymers, the reverse of condensation |
| Biodegradable | Can be broken down in the environment, eg by hydrolysis |
| Chemically inert | Does not react; true of polyalkenes |

## The polymers you must know (3.3.12.1)

| Polymer | Type | Monomers | Repeating unit | Typical uses |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Terylene | polyester | benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid + ethane-1,2-diol | –[CO–C₆H₄–CO–O–CH₂CH₂–O]– | clothing fibres, drinks bottles |
| Nylon 6,6 | polyamide | hexanedioic acid + hexane-1,6-diamine | –[CO–(CH₂)₄–CO–NH–(CH₂)₆–NH]– | clothing, carpets, ropes |
| Kevlar | polyamide | benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid + benzene-1,4-diamine | –[CO–C₆H₄–CO–NH–C₆H₄–NH]– | body armour, bulletproof vests, ropes |

C₆H₄ is a benzene ring joined at positions 1 and 4. In the lab, nylon 6,6 is often made from
hexanedioyl dichloride instead of the acid; then HCl is lost, not water.

**Amino acids** need only one monomer, because each has –NH₂ and –COOH. 2-aminopropanoic acid,
H₂N–CH(CH₃)–COOH, gives –[NH–CH(CH₃)–CO]–.

### Method: repeating unit from monomers

1. Draw the two monomers side by side, functional groups facing.
2. Remove OH from –COOH and H from –OH or –NH₂; join C to O or C to N.
3. Repeat at the other ends, so one residue of each monomer sits inside the brackets.
4. Draw extension bonds through the brackets and write n outside.
5. In an equation, show 2n H₂O (or 2n HCl from a diacyl dichloride) on the right.

### Method: monomers from a section of chain

1. Find each –CO–O– or –CO–NH– link.
2. Cut between C and O, or C and N.
3. Add OH to the carbonyl carbon (gives –COOH) and H to O or N (gives –OH or –NH₂).
4. Check the monomers by counting carbons in each residue.

**Small reminder.** Section –O–CH₂CH₂–O–CO–(CH₂)₃–CO–O–CH₂CH₂–O–: cut the ester links and you get
ethane-1,2-diol and HOOC–(CH₂)₃–COOH, pentanedioic acid.

### Mr of a repeating unit

Mr(repeating unit) = Mr(monomer 1) + Mr(monomer 2) − 2 × Mr(H₂O). For nylon 6,6: 146.0 + 116.0 − 36.0 =
**226.0**. Number of units in a chain = Mr(chain) / Mr(repeating unit).

## Intermolecular forces (3.3.12.1)

| Polymer type | Forces between chains | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Polyalkene, eg poly(ethene) | van der Waals only | non-polar C–C and C–H bonds |
| Polyester, eg Terylene | van der Waals + permanent dipole–dipole | polar C=O; no H on O or N |
| Polyamide, eg nylon 6,6 | van der Waals + hydrogen bonds | N–H on one chain, lone pair on C=O oxygen of the next |
| Kevlar | as polyamide, very many hydrogen bonds | flat, rigid chains line up in parallel |

Stronger forces between chains → higher melting point and stronger fibres.

## Hydrolysis and biodegradability (3.3.12.2)

- **Polyesters and polyamides** have polar C=O bonds in their links. The δ+ carbon is attacked by
  nucleophiles (H₂O, OH⁻), so the links can be **hydrolysed**. These polymers are **biodegradable**.
- **Polyalkenes** have only strong, non-polar C–C and C–H bonds. There is no δ+ carbon to attack, so
  they are **chemically inert** and **non-biodegradable**.

| Hydrolysis | Polyester gives | Polyamide gives |
|---|---|---|
| Acid (H⁺, heat) | dicarboxylic acid + diol | dicarboxylic acid + ammonium salt of the diamine |
| Alkaline (OH⁻, heat) | carboxylate salt + diol | carboxylate salt + diamine |

## Disposal of polymers (3.3.12.2)

| Method | For | Against |
|---|---|---|
| Landfill | cheap, simple, no sorting | uses land; polyalkenes persist; resource wasted |
| Incineration | less volume; energy for electricity | CO₂ released; PVC gives toxic HCl, which must be removed |
| Mechanical recycling | saves crude oil and energy; less landfill | collecting, sorting and cleaning cost money and energy; quality falls |
| Feedstock recycling | recovers monomers or chemicals | energy-intensive; specialist plant |

### Method: evaluating a disposal method

1. Name the method and the polymer(s) involved.
2. Give one advantage, linked to resources, energy or land.
3. Give one disadvantage, linked to the chemistry (non-biodegradable, CO₂, HCl from PVC, sorting).
4. If the waste is mixed, say what that means for sorting or for the gases produced.
5. End with a judgement only if the question asks you to evaluate or compare.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Addition vs condensation:** addition polymers form from C=C monomers with no other product;
  condensation polymers form from bifunctional monomers and lose H₂O (or HCl).
- **Polyester vs polyamide:** –COO– vs –CONH–; dipole–dipole vs hydrogen bonds between chains.
- **Acid vs alkaline hydrolysis:** in acid, amines end up as ammonium salts; in alkali, acids end up as
  carboxylate salts.
- **Inert vs biodegradable:** polyalkenes inert (no polar bonds); polyesters and polyamides hydrolysable.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the small molecule lost when a diol reacts with a dicarboxylic acid.
2. Give the two monomers of nylon 6,6.
3. Name the linkage in Kevlar.
4. Give one typical use of Terylene.
5. Draw the repeating unit of the polyamide from 2-aminopropanoic acid.
6. A poly(2-aminopropanoic acid) chain has Mr 7.10 × 10³. How many repeating units does it contain?
   (Ar: H = 1.0, C = 12.0, N = 14.0, O = 16.0)
7. Name the strongest type of force between nylon 6,6 chains.
8. Why are there no hydrogen bonds between Terylene chains?
9. Name the organic products when nylon 6,6 is heated with dilute hydrochloric acid.
10. Explain why poly(propene) cannot be hydrolysed.
11. Give one disadvantage of incinerating PVC.
12. Give the monomers from the section –NH–(CH₂)₃–NH–CO–CH₂–CO–.

### Answers

1. Water.
2. Hexanedioic acid and hexane-1,6-diamine.
3. Amide (–CONH–).
4. Clothing fibres, or plastic drinks bottles.
5. –[NH–CH(CH₃)–CO]ₙ–, with extension bonds.
6. Repeating unit C₃H₅NO, Mr = 71.0; 7.10 × 10³ / 71.0 = **100**.
7. Hydrogen bonds.
8. The ester link has no H bonded to O or N, so there is no δ+ H to bond to a lone pair.
9. Hexanedioic acid and the ammonium salt of hexane-1,6-diamine (hexane-1,6-diammonium chloride).
10. It has only non-polar C–C and C–H bonds, so there is no δ+ carbon for water or OH⁻ to attack.
11. Burning PVC releases toxic HCl gas (as well as CO₂).
12. H₂N–(CH₂)₃–NH₂ (propane-1,3-diamine) and HOOC–CH₂–COOH (propanedioic acid).

## Where marks are usually lost

- A two-monomer repeating unit drawn with only one residue, or with two residues of the same monomer.
- Repeating units that keep –OH or –H on the ends instead of extension bonds.
- Equations that miss the 2n H₂O, or show n H₂O for a two-monomer polymer.
- Kevlar or Terylene drawn with the ring joined at the wrong positions.
- Saying polyesters form hydrogen bonds between chains.
- Saying hydrogen bonds form between N–H and N–H, rather than N–H and the C=O oxygen.
- Explaining polyalkene inertness with "weak bonds" instead of non-polar bonds and no δ+ carbon.
- Giving the free amine as a product of acid hydrolysis.
- Disposal answers with an advantage but no disadvantage, or with no chemistry.
