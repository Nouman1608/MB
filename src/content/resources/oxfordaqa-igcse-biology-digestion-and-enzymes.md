## 3.2.4 a: Why food must be digested

Starch (a carbohydrate), proteins and fats are **insoluble**. They are too big to pass through the wall of the gut into the blood. Digestion converts them into **soluble** substances that can be **absorbed into the bloodstream in the wall of the small intestine**.

| Food molecule | Broken down into |
|---|---|
| Starch | Sugars |
| Proteins | Amino acids |
| Fats (lipids) | Fatty acids and glycerol |

In the **large intestine** much of the water mixed with the food is absorbed into the blood. What remains is **indigestible food**, which makes up the bulk of the **faeces**. Faeces leave the body through the **anus**.

### The digestive system

You must recognise these parts on a diagram. Food passes along a continuous tube, with glands alongside.

| Part | Role in this unit |
|---|---|
| Salivary glands | Make saliva containing amylase |
| Oesophagus | Carries food to the stomach |
| Stomach | Makes protease and hydrochloric acid |
| Liver | Makes bile |
| Gall bladder | Stores bile |
| Pancreas | Makes amylase, protease and lipase |
| Duodenum | First part of the small intestine; receives bile and pancreatic juice |
| Small intestine | Makes enzymes; digestion finishes and soluble products are absorbed |
| Large intestine | Absorbs water; forms faeces |
| Anus | Faeces leave the body |

Liver, gall bladder and pancreas are not on the food path. They send secretions into the duodenum.

### Worked example 1: why the large intestine matters

A person has 9.0 litres of fluid enter the gut in a day (food, drink and secretions). The faeces contain 0.2 litres of water.

Water absorbed = 9.0 − 0.2 = **8.8 litres**.
Percentage absorbed = 8.8 ÷ 9.0 × 100 = **98%** (2 significant figures).

This shows why damage to the large intestine causes watery faeces: the water is not reabsorbed.

## 3.2.4 b: Enzymes

**Enzymes are large proteins that act as biological catalysts.** A catalyst increases the rate of a chemical reaction. In the gut, enzymes speed up the breakdown of large molecules into small molecules so they can be absorbed.

A catalyst is not used up, so a small amount of enzyme can break down a lot of food.

### Shape and the active site

Each enzyme has an **active site**. The substrate fits into it, the reaction happens and the products leave. The **shape of an enzyme is vital for its function**. If the active site is altered, the substrate does not fit.

**High temperatures denature the enzyme.** The bonds that hold the protein in shape break, so the shape of the active site changes. A denatured enzyme cannot catalyse the reaction, and cooling it again does not restore it.

### Temperature

- At low temperature, particles move slowly, so enzyme and substrate meet and react less often. The rate is low.
- As temperature rises, the rate rises.
- Above the best temperature, denaturation begins and the rate drops quickly towards zero.

A cold enzyme is only slow. A too-hot enzyme is permanently damaged.

### pH

**Different enzymes work best at different pH values.** Each has a pH at which its rate is greatest. Moving away from that pH reduces the rate, and extreme values can denature the enzyme. This is why stomach enzymes and small intestine enzymes need different conditions (see 3.2.4 c).

### Enzymes that work outside cells

Digestive enzymes are made by **specialised cells in glands and in the lining of the gut**. They pass **out of the cells into the gut**, where they meet the food and **catalyse the breakdown of large molecules into smaller ones**. Most enzymes work inside cells, but digestive enzymes act outside the cells that make them.

### Worked example 2: reading a rate from time

An amylase solution is mixed with starch at four temperatures. The time for the starch to disappear (iodine no longer turns blue-black) is recorded.

| Temperature (°C) | Time (s) | Rate = 1000 ÷ time |
|---|---|---|
| 20 | 280 | 3.57 |
| 30 | 160 | 6.25 |
| 40 | 80 | 12.5 |
| 50 | 125 | 8.00 |

A shorter time means a faster reaction, so rate is proportional to 1 ÷ time. The rate at 40 °C is 12.5, the highest of the four.

Percentage change in rate from 30 °C to 40 °C = (12.5 − 6.25) ÷ 6.25 × 100 = **+100%**, so the rate doubled.
From 40 °C to 50 °C = (8.00 − 12.5) ÷ 12.5 × 100 = **−36%**.

Explanation: from 20 to 40 °C, more collisions per second give a faster rate. At 50 °C some enzyme molecules are denatured, so the rate falls. The best temperature lies near 40 °C, but the data cannot say exactly where, because no temperature between 40 and 50 °C was tested.

### Worked example 3: rate from an amount

A starch solution contains 1.20 g of starch. After 5 minutes with amylase, 0.45 g remains.

Starch broken down = 1.20 − 0.45 = 0.75 g.
Mean rate = 0.75 ÷ 5 = **0.15 g per minute**.
Percentage digested = 0.75 ÷ 1.20 × 100 = **62.5%**.

## Required practical: temperature, pH and the rate of digestion

You investigate how different temperatures and different pH values affect the rate of digestion.

### A typical method for temperature

1. Put equal volumes of amylase solution and starch solution in separate test tubes.
2. Stand the tubes in a water bath at the chosen temperature for several minutes, so both reach it.
3. Mix them and start the stopwatch.
4. Every 30 seconds, remove a drop and add it to iodine solution on a spotting tile.
5. Record the time when the iodine stops turning blue-black.
6. Repeat at other temperatures. Repeat each one at least three times and calculate a mean.

For pH, keep the temperature fixed and add **buffer solutions** of different pH values to each tube before mixing.

### Variables

| Type | What |
|---|---|
| Independent | Temperature, or pH |
| Dependent | Time taken for the starch to disappear (convert to rate) |
| Control | Volume and concentration of enzyme and starch, the buffer or temperature not being tested, drop size, stirring |

### Worked example 4: evaluating the method

A student tests a protease on cloudy milk-protein solution. The time for the cloudiness to clear is recorded at five pH values.

| pH | 1 | 2 | 3 | 5 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Time (s) | 150 | 45 | 110 | 400 | no change in 600 |
| Rate (1000 ÷ time) | 6.67 | 22.2 | 9.09 | 2.50 | 0 |

The rate is highest at pH 2, so the enzyme works best in acid conditions, like a stomach protease. At pH 7 there is no reaction, because the enzyme is not active (or is denatured) at that pH.

Ways to improve: test more pH values between 1 and 3 to find the best value more precisely; repeat and take means; use a water bath to keep the temperature constant; wait for the tubes to reach temperature before mixing. A drop of iodine every 30 seconds limits the timing to 30 seconds, so a clearer end point or shorter intervals would help.

Safety: wear eye protection and take care with hot water, acid and iodine.

## 3.2.4 c: The digestive enzymes

| Enzyme | Made in | Acts in | Reaction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Amylase | Salivary glands, pancreas, small intestine | Mouth and small intestine | Starch into sugars |
| Protease | Stomach, pancreas, small intestine | Stomach and small intestine | Proteins into amino acids |
| Lipase | Pancreas, small intestine | Small intestine | Lipids (fats) into fatty acids and glycerol |

Notice that the place where an enzyme is made is not always where it acts. Pancreatic enzymes travel to the duodenum.

### Hydrochloric acid

The stomach also produces **hydrochloric acid**. Enzymes in the stomach work most effectively in **acid conditions**. A stomach protease would work poorly at the alkaline conditions of the small intestine, and the reverse is also true.

### Bile

The liver produces **bile**, which is **stored in the gall bladder** before being released into the small intestine. Bile has two jobs.

1. **It neutralises the acid** added to food in the stomach. This gives **alkaline conditions** in which the small intestine enzymes work most effectively.
2. **It emulsifies fats**, breaking large drops of fat into smaller droplets. This increases the surface area of the fat for lipase to act upon.

Bile is not an enzyme and does not chemically digest fat. It only breaks up the drops.

### Worked example 5: emulsification and surface area

Treat a fat drop as a cube of side 2 cm. Bile breaks it into eight cubes of side 1 cm.

| | Surface area | Volume | Surface area to volume |
|---|---|---|---|
| One cube, side 2 cm | 6 × 2 × 2 = 24 cm² | 2³ = 8 cm³ | 24 ÷ 8 = 3 |
| Eight cubes, side 1 cm | 8 × 6 × 1 × 1 = 48 cm² | 8 × 1³ = 8 cm³ | 48 ÷ 8 = 6 |

The volume of fat is unchanged. The total surface area **doubles** (48 ÷ 24 = 2). Lipase acts at the surface of fat, so digestion is faster.

## Microbial enzymes at home and in industry

**Some microorganisms produce enzymes that pass out of the cells.** These enzymes have many uses at home and in industry. You should be able to give examples and relate data to enzyme properties.

| Enzyme | Use |
|---|---|
| Protease and lipase | Biological washing powders: remove protein and fat stains |
| Lactase | Makes lactose-free milk by breaking down lactose |
| Pectinase | Helps extract and clear fruit juice |
| Amylase | Breaks starch down into sugars in food manufacture |

Because the microorganisms release the enzyme into their surroundings, it can be collected from the liquid they grow in.

### Worked example 6: relating data to properties

A protease from a bacterium is tested for use in a washing powder. Relative activity (100 is the highest) is measured at four temperatures.

| Temperature (°C) | 20 | 40 | 60 | 80 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Relative activity | 15 | 60 | 100 | 35 |

- The enzyme works best at 60 °C.
- From 20 to 40 °C the activity rises fourfold (60 ÷ 15 = 4).
- From 60 to 80 °C the activity falls by 65% ((35 − 100) ÷ 100 × 100), because denaturation begins.
- A wash at 40 °C would use the enzyme at 60% of its best activity. A hotter wash would suit it better, but not above 60 °C.

## Common errors

- Writing that high temperature "kills" an enzyme. Enzymes are not alive. Say **denatures**.
- Saying the substrate shape changes. It is the shape of the **active site** that changes.
- Saying bile contains enzymes, or that bile digests fat. Bile **emulsifies** fat and **neutralises** acid.
- Writing that cold temperatures denature enzymes. They only slow the reaction.
- Forgetting that protease is made in three places, and that amylase is also made in the pancreas and small intestine.
- Treating a longer time as a faster rate. Rate is proportional to 1 ÷ time.
- Writing "the enzyme is used up". Enzymes are catalysts and are not used up.
