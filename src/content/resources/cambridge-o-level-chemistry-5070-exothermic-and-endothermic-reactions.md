## Reaction and surroundings

The definitions depend on separating two things:

- the **reaction** itself: the particles that break and make bonds
- the **surroundings**: everything else, including the water the reactants are dissolved in, the container, the thermometer and the air

In an **exothermic** reaction, thermal energy is transferred **from the reaction to the surroundings**, so the temperature of the surroundings **increases**. A thermometer dipped in the mixture reads higher.

In an **endothermic** reaction, thermal energy is **taken in from the surroundings**, so the temperature of the surroundings **decreases**. The mixture feels cold.

This is why the syllabus wording always mentions the surroundings: the thermometer measures the surroundings, not the reaction.

### Examples to recognise

| Exothermic (temperature rises) | Endothermic (temperature falls, or heat must be supplied) |
|---|---|
| Combustion of fuels such as methane | Thermal decomposition, such as CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ |
| Neutralisation of an acid by an alkali | Photosynthesis |
| A metal reacting with an acid, such as magnesium with hydrochloric acid | Citric acid reacting with sodium hydrogencarbonate |
| Adding water to anhydrous copper(II) sulfate | Heating hydrated copper(II) sulfate to drive off water |

The last row shows a useful link to subtopic 6.3: when a reversible reaction is exothermic in one direction, it is endothermic by the same amount in the other.

## Enthalpy change, ΔH

The transfer of thermal energy during a reaction is called the **enthalpy change**, ΔH, measured in kJ/mol.

- Exothermic: ΔH is **negative**, because the reaction loses energy to the surroundings.
- Endothermic: ΔH is **positive**, because the reaction gains energy from the surroundings.

The sign describes the reaction, not the thermometer. A temperature **rise** in the surroundings goes with a **negative** ΔH, which students often find backwards until they remember whose energy the sign refers to.

## Activation energy

**Activation energy, Ea**, is "the minimum energy that colliding particles must have to react". Learn that definition exactly: "minimum", "colliding" and "particles" are each worth checking for.

Activation energy explains why a fuel such as methane does not burn until a spark or flame is applied, even though combustion is strongly exothermic. The spark gives some particles enough energy to react; the energy released by those reactions then supplies the activation energy for neighbouring particles, so the reaction continues on its own.

## Reaction pathway diagrams

Outcome 5 asks you to **draw, label and interpret** these diagrams. A correct diagram has:

- a vertical axis labelled **energy** and a horizontal axis labelled **progress of reaction**
- a horizontal line for the **reactants** on the left and one for the **products** on the right
- a curve rising from the reactants to a peak and falling to the products
- an arrow for **Ea** from the reactant line **up to the peak**
- an arrow for **ΔH** from the reactant line **to the product line**

For an exothermic reaction the products are **lower** than the reactants and the ΔH arrow points down. For an endothermic reaction the products are **higher** and the ΔH arrow points up. In both, the Ea arrow starts at the **reactants**, never at the products.

### Worked example 1: reading a diagram

A pathway diagram shows the reactants at an energy of 50 kJ/mol, the top of the curve at 210 kJ/mol and the products at 130 kJ/mol.

1. Products are higher than reactants, so the reaction is **endothermic**.
2. ΔH = products − reactants = 130 − 50 = **+80 kJ/mol** (positive, as expected).
3. Ea = peak − reactants = 210 − 50 = **160 kJ/mol**.

A common wrong answer for Ea is 210 − 130 = 80 kJ/mol, measured from the products.

A catalyst (subtopic 6.2) would lower the peak, reducing Ea, but the reactant and product lines would not move, so **ΔH is unchanged**.

## Why reactions are exothermic or endothermic

Outcome 6: **bond breaking is endothermic** (energy must be supplied to pull atoms apart) and **bond making is exothermic** (energy is released when atoms bond).

Every reaction involves both. The overall enthalpy change depends on which is larger:

- More energy released making new bonds than taken in breaking old ones: **exothermic**, ΔH negative.
- More energy taken in breaking bonds than released making new ones: **endothermic**, ΔH positive.

## Calculating ΔH from bond energies

ΔH = (total energy of bonds broken) − (total energy of bonds made)

### Worked example 2: an exothermic reaction

Calculate ΔH for 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(g), given the bond energies H–H = 436 kJ/mol, O=O = 498 kJ/mol and O–H = 464 kJ/mol.

1. Bonds broken: 2 × H–H + 1 × O=O = (2 × 436) + 498 = **1370 kJ**.
2. Bonds made: each H₂O has two O–H bonds, so 2H₂O has four: 4 × 464 = **1856 kJ**.
3. ΔH = 1370 − 1856 = **−486 kJ/mol**. Exothermic.

### Worked example 3: an endothermic reaction

Calculate ΔH for 2HI(g) → H₂(g) + I₂(g), given H–I = 299 kJ/mol, H–H = 436 kJ/mol and I–I = 151 kJ/mol.

1. Bonds broken: 2 × 299 = **598 kJ**.
2. Bonds made: 436 + 151 = **587 kJ**.
3. ΔH = 598 − 587 = **+11 kJ/mol**. Endothermic: slightly more energy is needed to break the bonds than is released making the new ones.

### Counting bonds correctly

The most frequent error is miscounting. Draw or picture the displayed formula of each substance and multiply by its coefficient. For CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O:

- broken: 4 C–H (one methane) and 2 O=O (two oxygen molecules)
- made: 2 C=O (one carbon dioxide, O=C=O) and 4 O–H (two water molecules)

Double bonds have their own bond energy; use the C=O value, not the C–O value.

## Common mistakes

- **Saying an exothermic reaction "gets hot because it takes in heat".** Energy leaves the reaction and enters the surroundings.
- **Wrong sign of ΔH.** Temperature rise means ΔH is negative.
- **Drawing Ea from the products**, or drawing the ΔH arrow from the peak.
- **Reversing the subtraction** in bond energy calculations. It is always broken minus made.
- **Ignoring coefficients.** 2H₂ means two H–H bonds.
- **Saying "breaking bonds releases energy"**, which is the reverse of the truth.
- **Missing units or sign** in the final answer: "−486 kJ/mol", not "486".

## Command words for 5070 energetics questions

- **State:** a definition or fact, such as "bond making is exothermic".
- **Draw and label:** both axes, reactant and product lines, the curve, and the Ea and ΔH arrows pointing the right way.
- **Interpret:** read values from a diagram or data, and say what they show (exothermic or endothermic).
- **Explain** (enthalpy change in terms of bonds): compare the energy needed to break bonds with the energy released by making bonds, and say which is greater.
- **Calculate:** show bonds broken and bonds made as separate totals, then subtract, with sign and units.
