## Point 1: social construction of health, illness, disability and the body

**Key terms**

- **Biomedical model**: ill health as a physical malfunction, diagnosed and cured by doctors using science.
- **Social model of health**: health shaped by social conditions, meanings and power, not only biology.
- **Illness iceberg**: the idea that most symptoms never reach a doctor, so recorded illness is the visible tip.

**Models of health.** The biomedical model treats the body like a machine, locates illness in the individual and focuses on cure; the social model questions each of these. The World Health Organization's constitution defines health as "a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity". That wider definition sits closer to the social model.

**Lay definitions.** People outside medicine define health in different ways. Zola (1966) found that Irish-American and Italian-American patients described similar symptoms in very different ways, which suggests culture shapes how illness is presented. Blaxter and Paterson (1982), in a three-generation study of Scottish women, found that working-class mothers often saw health as being able to keep going with daily life, and accepted some symptoms as normal. Last (1963) coined the "illness iceberg".

**Functionalism: the sick role.** Parsons (1951) saw illness as deviance, because sick people cannot perform their normal roles, managed through the sick role. The sick person has two rights (exemption from normal duties; not being blamed) and two obligations (wanting to get well; seeking technically competent help and cooperating with it). The doctor acts as gatekeeper.

*Evaluation.* The sick role fits short, acute illnesses. It fits chronic illness badly, because the person may never "get well", and it ignores stigmatised conditions where people are blamed.

**Interactionism and stigma.** Goffman (1963) showed how a "spoiled identity" can follow a visible or known condition, which people manage by controlling information.

**Disability.** In the 1970s the Union of the Physically Impaired Against Segregation (UPIAS) separated **impairment** (a physical difference in the body) from **disability** (the exclusion society creates by failing to adapt). Mike Oliver later named this the "social model of disability" and set it out in *The Politics of Disablement* (1990). Scott (1969), in *The Making of Blind Men*, argued that agencies for blind people socialised clients into dependent, "blind" identities.

*Evaluation.* Tom Shakespeare argues the strong social model underplays the real pain and limits of some impairments.

**The body.** Shilling (1993) described the body in late modern society as a "project" that people work on through diet, exercise and cosmetic surgery. Foucault, in *The Birth of the Clinic* (1963), described the "clinical gaze": a way of seeing that turned the patient into a body to be examined. Oakley (1984, *The Captured Womb*) argues that childbirth has been medicalised under male medical control.

**Worked paragraph.** *Knowledge:* Parsons claims the sick role keeps society stable by limiting time off work. *Application:* a warehouse worker with long-term back pain may not fit this, because he cannot meet the duty to get well. *Analysis:* this shows the sick role assumes acute illness and a cooperative patient. *Evaluation:* Goffman shows that some conditions bring blame rather than exemption, so the model describes an ideal, not real experience.

## Point 2: unequal health chances by class, gender, ethnicity and region

**Key terms**

- **Health chances**: the likelihood of good or poor health across a lifetime.
- **Social gradient**: health gets steadily worse at each step down the social scale, not only at the bottom.

**Social class.** The Black Report (1980), chaired by Sir Douglas Black, found large class differences in death rates. It set out four explanations:

1. **Artefact**: the gap is a product of how class and death are measured.
2. **Social (natural) selection**: ill people drift down the class scale.
3. **Cultural/behavioural**: lower classes smoke, drink and eat in less healthy ways.
4. **Material/structural**: poverty, poor housing and hazardous work cause ill health.

The report favoured the material/structural explanation, as did the Acheson Report (1998) and the Marmot Review, *Fair Society, Healthy Lives* (2010). The Whitehall studies of British civil servants, later led by Michael Marmot, found that the lower a man's employment grade, the higher his death rate. Common risk factors explained only part of this. Low control over one's work may add stress, which supports a **psychosocial** explanation. Wilkinson and Pickett (*The Spirit Level*, 2009) argue that unequal societies have worse health overall.

*Evaluation.* Cultural explanations risk blaming the victim, because behaviour is often a response to stress and low income. Material explanations struggle to explain why gradients exist among people who are not poor, which is where psychosocial ideas help.

**Gender.** Women in the UK live longer than men on average but report more illness and consult GPs more often. Explanations include biology, masculine risk-taking, women's greater willingness to seek help, and (stressed by feminists) women's lower incomes and double shift of paid work and care.

**Ethnicity.** Explanations of differences between ethnic groups include genetics (for example, sickle cell disorder), culture, material deprivation and racism. Nazroo (1997), using a national survey of ethnic minorities, argued that much of the difference reflects socio-economic disadvantage, and that racism and its stress add further harm.

**Region.** Health is generally worse in deprived areas, and on average worse in the north of England than in the south. The Marmot Review 10 Years On (2020) found that improvements in life expectancy had stalled and that the gap between deprived and better-off areas had widened.

**Worked paragraph.** *Knowledge:* materialists argue poor housing and insecure work cause ill health. *Application:* a family in a damp private flat may suffer repeated chest infections however carefully they eat. *Analysis:* this suggests behaviour is shaped by circumstances, not freely chosen. *Evaluation:* the Whitehall gradient shows that even secure, salaried workers in lower grades die earlier, so material factors alone cannot be the full answer.

## Point 3: inequalities in provision of and access to health care

**Key terms**

- **Inverse care law**: Tudor Hart's (1971) observation that "the availability of good medical care tends to vary inversely with the need for it in the population served".

The NHS was founded in 1948 to give care free at the point of use, yet access is still unequal. Deprived areas, with the greatest need, tend to have more stretched services. Middle-class patients may also gain more from the same service, because they have the time, confidence and cultural capital to ask questions, push for referrals and travel to better hospitals. Private health care lets those who can pay avoid waiting lists.

Some minority ethnic patients face language barriers or racism; some disabled people face physical barriers.

**Theoretical views.** Marxists such as Navarro (1976, *Medicine under Capitalism*) and Doyal (1979, *The Political Economy of Health*) argue that health care under capitalism serves profit and keeps workers fit for work, while ignoring the social causes of illness. New Right thinkers argue that markets and choice would make services more efficient.

*Evaluation.* Critics of Marxism note that the NHS has removed financial barriers that market systems keep.

**Worked paragraph.** *Knowledge:* the inverse care law says need and provision run in opposite directions. *Application:* in a coastal town with many older, low-income residents, GP lists may be longest where illness is highest. *Analysis:* so a free service can still produce unequal results. *Evaluation:* yet Tudor Hart said the law works most fully under market forces, so without the NHS the gap could be wider.

## Point 4: the nature and social distribution of mental illness

**Two approaches to the nature of mental illness**

- **Realist approach**: mental illnesses are real conditions that cause genuine distress and have identifiable, often social, causes.
- **Social constructionist approach**: what counts as mental illness reflects the judgements of those with power to define it. Szasz (1961, *The Myth of Mental Illness*) argued that many "illnesses" are really problems in living.

**Labelling.** Scheff (1966) argued that rule-breaking which has no other name can be labelled as mental illness, and that the label may then shape the person's identity. Goffman's *Asylums* (1961) described how total institutions strip away the patient's former self. Rosenhan (1973) reported that sane "pseudopatients" were admitted to psychiatric hospitals and kept there. His study was later questioned by Susannah Cahalan, whose investigation raised doubts about its data, so use it with care.

*Evaluation.* Labelling explains the effect of diagnosis but not the original behaviour, and realists say it understates real suffering.

**Social distribution**

- **Class.** Working-class people are more likely to be diagnosed with mental illness. In Brown and Harris's (1978) study of women in Camberwell, south London, depression was linked to stressful life events plus "vulnerability factors" such as lacking a close confiding relationship and having three or more young children at home. Working-class women faced more of both.
- **Gender.** Women are more often diagnosed with depression and anxiety. Busfield (1996, *Men, Women and Madness*) argued that diagnosis reflects gendered ideas about normal behaviour, while structural explanations point to women's caring burdens. Men may be under-diagnosed because they are less likely to seek help.
- **Ethnicity.** Black people in England are more likely than White people to be diagnosed with psychotic illness and to be detained under mental health law. Explanations include racism and stress, ethnocentric diagnosis, and different routes into care.

**Worked paragraph.** *Knowledge:* Brown and Harris link depression to life events and vulnerability factors. *Application:* a lone mother with no close friend and three small children may be more at risk after losing her job. *Analysis:* this supports a realist, structural view: the illness is real and has social causes. *Evaluation:* the study relied on recall of past events and studied women only.

## Point 5: medicine, the health professions and the globalised health industry

**Functionalist view.** Parsons and Barber (1963) saw professions as altruistic bodies with expert knowledge and a code of ethics, rewarded with high status because they serve society.

**Weberian view.** Freidson (1970, *Profession of Medicine*) argued that doctors use **social closure** to control entry and protect their own interests and income.

**Marxist view.** Navarro argues that doctors serve capitalism by defining illness as individual and treatable, hiding its social causes.

**Feminist views.** Witz (1992, *Professions and Patriarchy*) showed how medicine excluded women and placed nursing and midwifery in a subordinate position.

**Critics of medicine's effectiveness.** Illich (1975, *Medical Nemesis*, later *Limits to Medicine*) argued that medicine causes harm, or **iatrogenesis**: clinical (side-effects and errors), social (dependence on doctors) and cultural (loss of people's ability to cope with pain and death). McKeown (1976) argued that the fall in deaths from infectious disease came mainly from better nutrition and living standards, not medical treatment. Simon Szreter later argued that McKeown underplayed public health measures such as clean water and sanitation.

**Postmodern views.** Haug (1973) suggested **deprofessionalisation**: as patients gain knowledge, doctors lose some of their monopoly.

**The globalised health industry.** Pharmaceutical companies are transnational corporations. Goldacre (*Bad Pharma*, 2012) argues they can shape what research is published, and research may favour profitable conditions in rich countries. Other global features include medical tourism, international patent rules on medicines, and the migration of health workers, which can leave poorer countries short of staff.

**Worked paragraph.** *Knowledge:* Freidson argues the medical profession uses social closure. *Application:* resistance to letting nurses or pharmacists take on prescribing and diagnosis can be read in this way. *Analysis:* this suggests the profession protects status as well as patients. *Evaluation:* functionalists reply that strict entry rules protect patients from unsafe practice, and the growth of nurse-led clinics shows the closure is not complete.

## Common errors

- Listing the Black Report's four explanations with no evidence or evaluation for each.
- Using the word "disability" when the social model would say "impairment", and losing the key distinction.
- Quoting Rosenhan without noting the later doubts about the study.
- Saying "ethnic minorities have worse health" as one group. Patterns differ between groups.
- Ignoring the "globalised health industry" wording in questions on medicine.
- Describing studies with no comment on their research design.
