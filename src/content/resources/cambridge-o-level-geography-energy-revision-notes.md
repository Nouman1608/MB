## The syllabus in brief

- Describe the importance of **fossil fuels, renewables, nuclear power
  and fuelwood** globally and at different levels of development.
- **Evaluate** nuclear power and renewable sources.
- Case study: **energy supply in a country or area**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Non-renewable | A source that will run out because it forms far more slowly than it is used |
| Renewable | A source that is naturally replenished |
| Fossil fuels | Coal, oil and natural gas, formed from ancient plant and animal remains |
| Nuclear power | Electricity from heat released by splitting uranium atoms |
| HEP | Hydroelectric power: water flowing through turbines, often from a dam |
| Geothermal | Energy from heat in rocks below the surface |
| Fuelwood | Wood or charcoal burned for cooking and heating |
| Energy mix | The share of each source in a country's supply |
| Energy security | Having a reliable, affordable supply, ideally not dependent on imports |

## Use by development level

- **Low income**: fuelwood and charcoal dominate household use; low use
  per person; limited grid electricity.
- **Middle income, industrialising**: demand rising fast; coal and oil
  for industry and power; more vehicles.
- **High income**: high use per person; fossil fuels still important;
  growing renewables; nuclear in some countries.

Rule of thumb: **use per person rises** and **the mix shifts** away from
fuelwood as countries develop.

## Nuclear: weigh it

- **For**: large steady output; very low carbon emissions in operation;
  little fuel needed; less reliance on imports.
- **Against**: very high build and decommissioning cost; long-lived
  radioactive waste; rare but severe accidents (Chernobyl 1986,
  Fukushima 2011); needs strict regulation.

## Renewables: weigh each source

| Source | Benefit | Drawback |
|---|---|---|
| HEP | Reliable, controllable output; reservoir stores water | Floods land, displaces people, traps sediment |
| Wind | No emissions in use; cheap to run | Output varies; noise and visual impact |
| Solar | Works off-grid in remote villages | No output at night; less in cloudy seasons |
| Geothermal | Steady output and heating | Only near plate boundaries or hot rocks |
| Tidal and wave | Predictable tides | Few suitable sites; affects estuary habitats |
| Biofuels | Can replace petrol | Uses farmland that could grow food |

## Case study outline: Iceland

- On the Mid-Atlantic Ridge: volcanic heat; also large glacier-fed
  rivers.
- Almost all electricity is renewable: HEP (larger share) and
  geothermal.
- Most homes heated by geothermal hot water; Hellisheiði geothermal
  station near Reykjavík.
- Kárahnjúkar HEP scheme supplies an aluminium smelter at Reyðarfjörður;
  criticised for flooding highland wilderness.
- Still imports oil for transport and fishing.

## Exam traps

- Saying fuelwood is renewable without "if replanted".
- Treating all renewables as having the same impacts.
- One-sided evaluation.
- Saying nuclear power is unreliable (it is reliable; cost, waste and
  risk are the issues).

## Self-test

1. Name the three fossil fuels.
2. Why is nuclear power usually classed as non-renewable?
3. Give one reason fuelwood is widely used in low-income countries.
4. Give one benefit and one disadvantage of HEP.
5. Why can Iceland use so much geothermal energy?
6. A country produces 60 units of electricity, 15 from wind. What
   percentage is this, and what pie chart angle?

**Answers:** 1. Coal, oil, natural gas. 2. It uses uranium, a mined
resource that will eventually run out. 3. It is free or cheap to
collect, and many rural areas have no electricity grid. 4. Benefit:
reliable, controllable output with no emissions in use; disadvantage:
the reservoir floods land and displaces people. 5. It lies on the
Mid-Atlantic Ridge, a plate boundary where hot rocks are close to the
surface. 6. 15 / 60 x 100 = 25%; 25 x 3.6 = 90 degrees.
