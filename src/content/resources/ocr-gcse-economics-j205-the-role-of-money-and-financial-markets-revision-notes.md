Recall notes for section 2.8 of OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics (J205, Version 2.0, June 2026), examined in Component 01. For full explanations and worked examples read the [study guide to money and financial markets](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-the-role-of-money-and-financial-markets/); to apply them, try the [money and financial markets practice questions](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-the-role-of-money-and-financial-markets-practice/). All figures are invented.

## The five things the specification asks

1. Explain money as a **medium of exchange**.
2. Explain the role of the **financial sector**, including banks, building societies and insurance companies.
3. Evaluate its importance for **consumers, producers and government**.
4. Analyse how **different interest rates** affect saving, borrowing and investment.
5. Calculate the effect of **rate changes** on savings and borrowings.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Medium of exchange | Something generally accepted in payment, which can be spent again |
| Barter | Swapping goods directly, without money |
| Double coincidence of wants | Each side of a barter wanting what the other has, at the same time |
| Bank deposit | Money held in an account; the largest part of the money people use |
| Financial sector | Institutions that hold, lend, move and insure money for others |
| Bank | Shareholder-owned firm that takes deposits, lends and runs payments |
| Building society | Mutual institution owned by its members, focused on savings and mortgages |
| Insurance company | Firm that collects premiums and pays claims, pooling risk |
| Premium | The regular payment made for insurance cover |
| Mortgage | A long-term loan to buy property, secured on that property |
| Interest rate | Reward for saving and price of borrowing, as a percentage per year |
| Fixed rate | Stays the same for an agreed period |
| Variable rate | Moves, usually with the central bank's rate |
| Investment | Firms spending on capital goods such as machines and buildings |

## Money in four lines

- Barter fails because of the double coincidence of wants, goods that cannot be divided, and too many exchange rates.
- Money solves this: one price per good, easy to divide, accepted by everyone.
- It works only while people trust it will be accepted.
- Cheap exchange lets people specialise, and specialisation raises output.

## The financial sector's four jobs

| Job | Example |
|---|---|
| Payments | Wages paid into accounts; card payments in shops |
| Linking savers and borrowers | One household's deposit funds another's car loan |
| Funding large purchases | A 25-year mortgage spreads the cost of a home |
| Sharing risk | Premiums from many pay the claims of the few |

**Bank or building society?** Both take deposits and lend. A bank is owned by shareholders and aims at profit; a building society is owned by its members and can return surpluses as better rates.

## Importance: quick evaluation grid

| Group | Benefits | Risks or limits |
|---|---|---|
| Consumers | Safe saving, mortgages, insurance, easy payments | Debt problems; costly credit cards; not everyone can get credit |
| Producers | Loans for investment; insurance lets them take risks; payment services | Repayments still due if sales fall; new firms may be refused |
| Government | Collects taxes and pays benefits through it; borrows from it; its interest rate changes pass through it | A failing bank can damage the whole economy |

Judgement pattern: the sector is essential **if** institutions are stable and lending is affordable; it matters most for those who borrow heavily.

## Interest rates: the chains

**Rate rise:** saving more rewarding → more saving; loans dearer → less borrowing; fewer projects beat the cost of finance → less investment; so spending falls.

**Rate cut:** the reverse.

**Strength depends on:** fixed or variable debt, whether a household is a net saver or net borrower, confidence, and the size of the change.

## Methods

| Task | Method |
|---|---|
| Interest for one year | Amount × rate |
| Change in interest | New interest − old interest |
| Percentage change | Change ÷ original × 100 |
| Monthly change | Yearly change ÷ 12 |
| Interest added each year | Amount × (1 + rate) for each year |
| Investment decision | Invest if the expected return (%) is above the interest rate (%) |

## Exam traps

- **Percentage points, not per cent.** A rate going from 2% to 3% is a 1 percentage point rise but a 50% rise in interest earned.
- Most money is bank deposits, not cash.
- "Mutual" is the word that marks a building society.
- Investment means capital goods, not putting money in a savings account.
- Fixed-rate borrowers are not hit by a rise until their deal ends.
- When the question names three groups, cover all three.
- "Calculate the effect" wants the change, not only the new figure.

## Self-test

1. State what is meant by a medium of exchange.
2. Give two problems of barter.
3. State one difference between a bank and a building society.
4. Explain how an insurance company shares risk.
5. A saver has £4,500 in an account. The rate rises from 1.8% to 2.6%. Calculate the change in interest earned in a year.
6. In question 5, by how many percentage points did the rate rise?
7. £8,000 is saved for two years at 3%, with interest added each year. Calculate the final balance.
8. A variable-rate mortgage of £250,000 moves from 4.5% to 5.25%. Calculate the extra interest per month.
9. A firm expects a machine costing £30,000 to add £900 a year to profit. Will it borrow at 4% to buy it? Explain.
10. A consumer owes £1,200 on a credit card at 24% and could switch to a loan at 8%. How much interest would be saved in a year?
11. Explain why a rate rise might increase spending by a retired couple with savings and no debts.
12. State one reason why a cut in interest rates may not increase investment.

### Answers

1. Something **generally accepted in payment** for goods and services, which the receiver can spend again.
2. Any two: the **double coincidence of wants** is needed; goods are hard to **divide**; there are too many **exchange rates** to compare values.
3. A bank is owned by **shareholders**; a building society is **mutual**, owned by its members.
4. Many customers pay **premiums** into a pool; the few who suffer a loss are paid from it, so no one bears the full loss alone.
5. 4,500 × 0.018 = £81; 4,500 × 0.026 = £117; change = **£36 more**.
6. **0.8 percentage points.**
7. 8,000 × 1.03 × 1.03 = **£8,487.20**.
8. 250,000 × 0.045 = £11,250; 250,000 × 0.0525 = £13,125; difference £1,875 a year, so **£156.25 a month**.
9. Return = 900 ÷ 30,000 × 100 = **3%**, below the 4% cost of borrowing, so **no**: interest of £1,200 would exceed the £900 gain.
10. 1,200 × 0.24 = £288; 1,200 × 0.08 = £96; saving = **£192**.
11. Their interest income rises and they have no repayments to increase, so their **disposable income rises** and they may spend more.
12. Any one: low **business confidence** about future sales; banks may not pass on the cut; firms may already carry large debts.
