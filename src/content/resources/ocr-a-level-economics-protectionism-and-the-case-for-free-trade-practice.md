## Questions

Questions 2 to 7 use one market. Country B (illustrative) imports garden benches. Domestic demand is Qd = 90 − 3P and domestic supply is Qs = 2P − 10, with quantity in thousands and price in currency units. Country B is too small to affect the world price of 12.

**1.** A tariff of 3 is placed on garden benches with a world price of 12. **(a)** Express it as an ad valorem rate. **[1]** **(b)** Distinguish a specific from an ad valorem tariff. **[2]**

**2.** **(a)** Calculate domestic demand, domestic supply and imports under free trade. **[3]** **(b)** A tariff of 3 is imposed. Calculate the domestic price, demand, supply, imports and tariff revenue. **[4]**

**3.** Using question 2, calculate the loss of consumer surplus, the gain in producer surplus, the tariff revenue and the net welfare loss. **[5]**

**4.** A quota of 25 thousand replaces the tariff. **(a)** Explain why the domestic price is again 15. **[2]** **(b)** Work out the quota rent and identify who might collect it. **[2]**

**5.** Demand rises to Qd = 105 − 3P. Calculate the domestic price and imports under the tariff of 3 and under the quota of 25. Explain which gives domestic firms more protection. **[6]**

**6.** Instead, the government pays domestic producers a subsidy of 3 per unit (demand as in question 2). **(a)** Calculate imports and the cost to the government. **[3]** **(b)** Compare the net welfare loss with the tariff's. **[2]**

**7.** Each 1,000 units of bench output needs 8 workers. Using the tariff results, calculate the jobs protected, the net welfare loss per job and the consumer loss per job, then comment. **[4]**

**8.** Over a year, wages rise by 6% and productivity by 2% in Country C. In Country D wages rise by 2% and productivity by 1%. **(a)** Calculate the growth of unit labour costs in each country. **[3]** **(b)** Explain one policy by which Country C could improve its international competitiveness, and one limitation. **[3]**

**9.** Country B has a new solar-panel industry. Evaluate the case for protecting it. **[8]**

**10.** Country E exports mainly one raw material. Evaluate the case for free trade for Country E. **[8]**

**11.** Evaluate the effectiveness of government policies to improve a country's international competitiveness. **[8]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** 3 ÷ 12 = **25%** [1].
**(b)** A specific tariff is a fixed sum per unit imported [1]; an ad valorem tariff is a percentage of the price, so the amount rises with the price [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Giving a number for each type, such as 3 per unit and 25%, makes a distinction clearer than words alone.

**2. (a)** Qd = 90 − 36 = **54** [1]; Qs = 24 − 10 = **14** [1]; imports = **40** [1].
**(b)** Price = 12 + 3 = **15** [1]. Qd = 45 and Qs = 20 [1]. Imports = **25** [1]. Revenue = 3 × 25 = **75** [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the substitution into each equation; a wrong price early on spoils every later line.

**3.** Consumer loss = (54 + 45) ÷ 2 × 3 = **148.5** [1]. Producer gain = (14 + 20) ÷ 2 × 3 = **51** [1]. Revenue = **75** [1]. Net loss = 148.5 − 51 − 75 = **22.5** [1], made of two triangles: 0.5 × 3 × 6 = 9 (production) and 0.5 × 3 × 9 = 13.5 (consumption) [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Treat revenue as a transfer, not a loss, and name what each triangle represents.

**4. (a)** At 15, Qd − Qs = 45 − 20 = 25, equal to the quota [1], so 15 clears the market and consumers face the same price as under the tariff [1].
**(b)** Rent = (15 − 12) × 25 = **75** [1], going to licence holders (importers or foreign exporters), or to the government if licences are auctioned [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A tariff's rectangle is revenue; a quota's rectangle is only revenue if the licences are sold.

**5.** Tariff: the price stays at 15, Qd = 60, Qs = 20, imports = **40** [1]. Quota: 105 − 3P = 2P − 10 + 25, so 5P = 90 and P = **18** [1]. Then Qd = 51, Qs = 26, imports = 25 [1]. Domestic output is 26 with the quota but 20 with the tariff [1], so the quota protects more [1]. A tariff fixes the price and lets quantity adjust, while a quota fixes quantity and lets price adjust, so consumers pay 18 rather than 15 [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Stating what each instrument holds fixed gives the comparison its reason.

**6. (a)** Producers receive 15, so Qs = 20, and consumers pay 12, so Qd = 54 [1]. Imports = 54 − 20 = **34** [1]. Cost = 3 × 20 = **60** [1].
**(b)** Net loss = 0.5 × 3 × 6 = **9**, below the tariff's 22.5 [1], because consumers still pay 12; but taxpayers fund 60 and no revenue is raised [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Say why the loss is smaller, not just that it is.

**7.** Jobs = 6 thousand × 8 = **48** [1]. Net loss per job = 22,500 ÷ 48 = **468.75** [1]. Consumer loss per job = 148,500 ÷ 48 = **3,093.75** [1]. Consumers lose about 3,094 for each job protected, so protection looks costly unless wider benefits exist [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep units in view, because the diagram is in thousands.

**8. (a)** C: 1.06 ÷ 1.02 = 1.0392, so **3.92%** [1]. D: 1.02 ÷ 1.01 = 1.0099, so **0.99%** [1]. Wages minus productivity is a close approximation: 4 points and 1 point [1].
**(b)** Training raises output per worker [1], lowering unit labour costs [1]. Limitation: it is costly and slow, and trained workers can move to other firms [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming the mechanism (productivity up, cost per unit down) works better than saying a policy "improves competitiveness".

**9.** For: the infant industry argument says new firms need time to reach scale and learn [1]; a temporary tariff or subsidy may let costs fall until the industry can compete [1]; it may support jobs and a strategic technology [1]. Against: buyers of panels pay higher prices, as tariff diagrams like question 3 show [1]; protection may reduce pressure to cut costs and the industry may never grow up [1]; trading partners may retaliate against Country B's exports [1]. Judgement: the case is stronger if protection is temporary, falls over time and the industry has a realistic route to scale [1], but weaker if a subsidy could do the job at lower cost [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* A conclusion that states the conditions under which the argument holds usually reads as stronger than a bare list of pros and cons.

**10.** For: specialisation and trade raise output and consumption [1]; export markets allow scale [1]; imports of machinery and technology raise productivity [1]. Against: reliance on one export exposes Country E to price falls and weak terms of trade [1]; low-skill sectors may not grow, leaving structural unemployment [1]; gains may go to a few producers [1]. Judgement: free trade helps most if the country uses export income to diversify and the losers are retrained [1]; without that, gains are uneven and fragile [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Link each point to the one-product detail in the stem, not to trade in general.

**11.** Supply-side policies such as training, infrastructure and research raise productivity and lower unit labour costs [1]. They work in the long run and are hard to copy [1]. Their lags are long, costs are high and success is uncertain [1]. Tax cuts and deregulation raise investment [1] but lose revenue and may cause social or environmental harm [1]. Subsidies or a lower exchange rate act quickly [1], but cost money, may provoke retaliation and are easily matched [1]. Judgement: effectiveness depends on the weakness (price, quality or productivity), the time frame and whether rivals respond, so a mix is usually best [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Separating short-run and long-run effects usually makes evaluation more convincing than listing policies.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing the tariff price but not recalculating quantities demanded and supplied.
- Counting tariff revenue as part of the welfare loss.
- Forgetting that a quota's rectangle goes to licence holders.
- Not saying whether a tariff or a quota holds price or quantity fixed.
- Comparing methods without a reason, such as consumers facing no price rise under a subsidy.
- Ignoring units when the diagram is in thousands.
- Giving arguments for protection without conditions or a judgement.
- Describing free trade in general terms instead of using the country in the stem.
- Calling unit labour costs "wages" and leaving out productivity.
