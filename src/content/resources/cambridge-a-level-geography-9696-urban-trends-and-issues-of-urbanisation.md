## Urban growth and urbanisation are different

**Urban growth** is an increase in the number of people living in urban areas. **Urbanisation** is an increase in the *proportion* of a population that is urban. A country can have urban growth without urbanisation if its rural population grows just as fast.

**Worked example.** *A country's population rises from 40 million to 60 million over 30 years. Its urban population rises from 12 million to 30 million.*

- Urban share: 12 ÷ 40 = **30%** at the start; 30 ÷ 60 = **50%** at the end. Urbanisation has occurred.
- Urban growth: (30 − 12) ÷ 12 × 100 = **150%** increase in urban population.
- Rural population: 28 million to 30 million, a rise of about **7%**. The countryside has not emptied; the city has grown far faster.

At world scale, the United Nations estimated that the urban share passed 50% around 2007, reached 55% in 2018 and is projected to reach about 68% by 2050. Most of the projected increase is in Asia and Africa.

## Causes of urbanisation

**In LICs and MICs today:**

- **Rural-urban migration**, driven by rural poverty and land shortage, and the pull of jobs, services and expected higher incomes (see 5.2).
- **Natural increase within cities.** Urban populations are young, so births greatly exceed deaths. In many fast-growing cities natural increase contributes a large share of urban growth, not migration alone.
- **Reclassification**, when growing villages are redefined as towns, or city boundaries are extended.
- **Industrialisation and globalisation**, as manufacturing and services concentrate in cities with ports, airports and investment.

**In HICs in the past:** the industrial revolution concentrated factory work in cities, while farm mechanisation reduced rural labour demand.

## Consequences of rapid urbanisation in LICs and MICs

| Positive | Negative |
|---|---|
| Economies of scale in providing water, schools and clinics | Housing supply cannot keep up, so informal settlements grow |
| Agglomeration: firms gain from nearby suppliers, workers and customers | Large informal sector with low, insecure pay |
| Higher incomes and access to services than in rural areas | Congestion, air and water pollution |
| Falling fertility as education and urban living change family size | Urban sprawl onto farmland and hazardous land |

Primate cities, far larger than any other in the country, concentrate investment and political power, which can widen the gap between the capital and other regions.

## Stages of urban change in HICs

Van den Berg and colleagues (1982) described a sequence of stages for urban regions in Europe:

1. **Urbanisation:** the core city grows fastest.
2. **Suburbanisation:** the ring around the core grows faster than the core; people move out but still work in the city.
3. **Counterurbanisation (disurbanisation):** the whole urban region loses population to smaller towns and rural areas beyond.
4. **Re-urbanisation:** the core regains population and investment.

The stages are a description, not a law. Not all cities pass through every stage, and several can happen at once in different parts of one region.

**Counterurbanisation.** From the 1970s many HIC cities lost population to smaller towns and villages. Causes include rising car ownership, better roads, the decentralisation of jobs, retirement and a perception of a better quality of life outside cities.

**Re-urbanisation.** From the 1990s many city centres regained residents. Manchester's city centre, for example, grew from a small residential population into one of the fastest-growing districts in England. Causes include:

- regeneration of old warehouses and industrial land into apartments;
- growth of jobs in finance, media, higher education and other services in central areas;
- young, single, childless adults and students who value nightlife and short commutes;
- planning policies that favour building on previously used (brownfield) land.

Re-urbanisation often brings **gentrification**, which raises property values but can displace lower-income residents.

## Competition for land

Urban land is limited, so uses compete:

- **Greenfield versus brownfield.** Greenfield sites on the urban edge are cheaper to build on, but they consume farmland and habitats. Brownfield sites are previously used land within the city: they reuse infrastructure but may need costly decontamination.
- **Green belts.** In Britain, green belts around cities have been national planning policy since 1955 to limit sprawl. They protect open land but raise land prices inside the boundary and push development beyond it, lengthening commutes.
- **Housing versus employment and open space.** Rising land values in central areas push out low-value uses, such as small workshops, allotments and affordable housing.
- **Informal settlements in LICs and MICs** occupy land no one else is using, such as steep slopes, floodplains and land beside railways, because the formal market is unaffordable.

## Urban renewal

Urban renewal is the improvement or redevelopment of run-down urban areas. Approaches have changed:

| Approach | Typical period in HICs | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Clearance and redevelopment | 1950s-1970s | Slums demolished; residents rehoused in estates or high-rise blocks, often far away |
| Property-led regeneration | 1980s-1990s | Development corporations attract private investment, often offices and luxury housing |
| Partnership and mixed-use regeneration | 1990s onwards | Housing, jobs, transport and community projects combined, with local involvement |

**Example: Cheonggyecheon, Seoul (South Korea).** An elevated highway built over a buried stream in central Seoul was demolished and the stream restored as a 5.8 km public park, opened in 2005. Benefits: a green space in the dense centre, lower local air temperatures and a new tourist attraction. Criticisms: high cost, the displacement of many small traders who had worked along the old road, and the fact that the stream depends on water pumped in rather than a natural flow. It shows that renewal can be environmental as well as about housing and jobs.

## World cities

A **world city** (or global city) is one whose influence on the world economy, culture and politics extends far beyond its own country. John Friedmann's world city hypothesis (1986) linked such cities to the global economy; Saskia Sassen's *The Global City* (1991) focused on New York, London and Tokyo as centres of finance and producer services.

**Causes of growth:**

- **Command and control functions:** headquarters of transnational corporations, stock exchanges and major banks.
- **Producer services:** law, accountancy, advertising and consulting firms that serve global clients and cluster near each other.
- **Connectivity:** major airports, ports and digital infrastructure.
- **Political and cultural roles:** international organisations, media, universities and the arts.
- **Cumulative causation:** success attracts skilled migrants and investment, which reinforce success.

**The hierarchy.** The Globalization and World Cities Research Network (GaWC), based at Loughborough University, ranks cities by the office networks of producer-service firms. London and New York are placed at the top level (alpha++). Below them come cities such as Hong Kong, Singapore, Shanghai, Paris, Dubai and Tokyo. The hierarchy changes: cities in Asia and the Gulf have risen sharply as global production and finance have shifted.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Explain why re-urbanisation has occurred in some cities in high income countries. [6]*

> Old industrial and dockside land in city centres became available as manufacturing declined (**supply of land**). Planning policy favoured brownfield sites, so developers converted warehouses into apartments (**policy**). Service jobs in finance, media and universities grew in central areas (**employment pull**). Young, single adults and students value short commutes and nightlife more than gardens (**demographic group**). Investment in public transport and public spaces made centres more attractive (**environment**). Together, these created a cycle in which new residents attracted shops and restaurants, which attracted more residents (**reinforcement**).

## Common mistakes

- Using "urbanisation" when the data show only urban growth.
- Describing counterurbanisation and re-urbanisation as opposites that cannot happen at the same time.
- Treating world cities as simply the largest cities. Lagos is very large but ranks lower than Singapore.
- Describing renewal schemes without saying who gained and who was displaced.
- Ignoring natural increase as a cause of urban growth in LICs.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate** questions on urban share: show the division and give a percentage.
- **Describe** a graph of urban change: give the trend, the rate and any change of trend, with figures.
- **Explain** causes: separate migration, natural increase and reclassification.
- **Evaluate** renewal: aims, evidence of success, winners and losers, and a judgement.
