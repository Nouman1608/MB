These questions cover section **3.3.12.2 Biodegradability and disposal of polymers** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is A-level only content, examined on Paper 2 and Paper 3. Learn the ideas first in the [Biodegradability and disposal study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-biodegradability-and-disposal-of-polymers/) and the [Biodegradability and disposal revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-biodegradability-and-disposal-of-polymers-revision-notes/). Use Aᵣ values H = 1.0, C = 12.0, O = 16.0, Cl = 35.5.

## Questions

**1.** State what is meant by the term biodegradable. **[1]**

**2.** Explain why poly(propene) is not biodegradable. **[3]**

**3.** Explain why polyesters and polyamides can be hydrolysed but polyalkenes cannot. **[3]**

**4.** Write an equation to show the hydrolysis of an amide link, –CO–NH–, by water. **[1]**

**5.** A section of nylon 6,6 is heated with dilute hydrochloric acid.

**(a)** Name the organic products. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why one of the products is a salt rather than a free amine. **[1]**

**6.** A section of Terylene is heated under reflux with aqueous sodium hydroxide.

**(a)** Name the two products. **[2]**

**(b)** State why this is described as hydrolysis. **[1]**

**7.** Describe one advantage and one disadvantage of disposing of waste polymers by landfill. **[2]**

**8.** Waste poly(chloroethene) (PVC) is burned in an incinerator.

**(a)** Name one useful outcome of burning waste polymers. **[1]**

**(b)** Identify one toxic gas formed when PVC is burned and explain where it comes from. **[2]**

**(c)** State what must be fitted to the incinerator to deal with this gas. **[1]**

**9.** Compare mechanical recycling and feedstock recycling of polymers. **[3]**

**10.** 1.92 kg of poly(ethylene terephthalate) (repeating unit Mᵣ = 192.0) is completely hydrolysed. Calculate the mass of ethane-1,2-diol (Mᵣ = 62.0) formed. **[2]**

**11.** 1.00 kg of PVC (repeating unit C₂H₃Cl, Mᵣ = 62.5) is burned and all the chlorine forms hydrogen chloride (Mᵣ = 36.5). Calculate the mass of hydrogen chloride formed. **[3]**

**12.** A town council must decide how to dispose of its waste plastics, which include poly(ethene) bags, PVC pipes and polyester clothing. Evaluate the options of landfill, incineration and recycling. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** It can be broken down by living organisms, such as microorganisms [1]. **[1]**

**2.** Poly(propene) has a chain of strong C–C bonds and C–H bonds [1]; these bonds are non-polar, so there is no δ+ atom for a nucleophile (or an enzyme) to attack [1]; so microorganisms cannot break it down and it is inert and non-biodegradable [1]. **[3]**

**3.** Polyesters and polyamides have polar ester or amide links [1]; the carbon of the C=O group in the link is δ+ and can be attacked by nucleophiles such as water or hydroxide [1]; polyalkenes have only non-polar C–C and C–H bonds, so there is nothing for a nucleophile to attack [1]. **[3]**

**4.** –CO–NH– + H₂O → –COOH + H₂N– [1]. **[1]**

**5. (a)** Hexanedioic acid [1]; 1,6-diaminohexane, as its salt (the diammonium chloride) [1]

**(b)** The amine is a base, so it reacts with the hydrochloric acid to form the ammonium salt [1]. **[3]**

**6. (a)** Disodium benzene-1,4-dicarboxylate (the sodium salt of the acid) [1]; ethane-1,2-diol [1]

**(b)** The ester links are broken by reaction with water (hydroxide ions in aqueous solution) [1]. **[3]**

**7.** Advantage: cheap and simple, with little sorting needed [1]; disadvantage: takes up land, and polyalkenes do not break down (or additives may leach into the soil) [1]. **[2]**

**8. (a)** The energy released can be used to generate electricity or for heating [1]

**(b)** Hydrogen chloride (HCl) [1]; it comes from the chlorine atoms in the PVC [1]

**(c)** A gas scrubber (or other equipment to remove acidic gases) [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Mechanical recycling melts and remoulds the polymer [1]; feedstock recycling breaks the polymer chemically into monomers or small molecules for reuse [1]; mechanical recycling needs sorted, clean plastic and may give lower-quality products, whereas feedstock recycling can handle mixed plastics but needs more energy [1]. **[3]**

**10.** n(units) = 1920 ÷ 192.0 = 10.0 mol [1]; mass of diol = 10.0 × 62.0 = **620 g** (0.620 kg) [1]. **[2]**

**11.** n(units) = 1000 ÷ 62.5 = 16.0 mol [1]; n(HCl) = 16.0 mol (one Cl per unit) [1]; mass = 16.0 × 36.5 = **584 g** [1]. **[3]**

**12.** Landfill: cheap and simple [1], but poly(ethene) and PVC do not biodegrade and land is limited [1]. Incineration: reduces volume and releases energy [1], but produces CO₂ and PVC releases toxic HCl, so scrubbers are needed [1]. Recycling: saves crude oil and reduces waste [1], but needs sorting by type, and polyester and polyalkenes are different, so it is costly [1]. A reasoned conclusion such as using recycling for sorted plastics and incineration with energy recovery for the rest earns the credit for judgement, shown in the quality of the points above. **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* An evaluation scores for both sides of each method. A final decision is credited only if it follows from your points.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "plastic" instead of non-polar, strong bonds and no δ+ site.
- Not stating the nucleophile and the δ+ carbon in a hydrolysis explanation.
- Writing the free diamine as the product of acid hydrolysis.
- One-sided evaluations.
- Claiming incineration releases no pollutants.
- Confusing feedstock and mechanical recycling.
