Larger, more active animals need a mass transport system to carry nutrients, oxygen and waste between their cells and their exchange surfaces. For OCR A Level Biology A (H420) section 3.1.2 you must explain why, compare circulatory systems, relate the structure of each type of blood vessel to its function, explain how tissue fluid forms, describe the heart and the cardiac cycle and how heartbeat is coordinated, interpret ECG traces, and explain how haemoglobin carries oxygen and carbon dioxide.

This guide covers learning outcomes 3.1.2(a) to (j) of the OCR A Level Biology A specification, Version 4.1 (April 2026), first assessment 2025, in Module 3, Exchange and transport. It goes beyond the module-wide [exchange and transport study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-biology-exchange-and-transport/). ## Why transport systems are needed (a)

Diffusion is fast only over very short distances. A large animal has a small SA:V and most cells are far from an exchange surface; an active animal also has a high metabolic rate. A circulatory system moves blood by **mass flow**, so substances are carried quickly between exchange surfaces and every tissue.

## Types of circulatory system (b)

| | Insect | Fish | Mammal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Open or closed | Open: haemolymph bathes organs in the body cavity | Closed: blood stays in vessels | Closed |
| Single or double | — | Single: blood passes through the heart once per circuit | Double: twice per circuit |
| Route | Tubular heart pumps haemolymph forward; it returns through the cavity | heart → gills → body → heart | heart → lungs → heart → body → heart |

Insect haemolymph does not carry oxygen to the tissues; the tracheal system does that. In a fish, blood loses pressure in the gill capillaries, so it flows slowly to the body. A **double circulation** returns blood to the heart after the lungs so it can be pumped to the body at high pressure, while the lungs receive blood at lower pressure that does not damage their delicate capillaries.

## Blood vessels (c)

| Vessel | Structure | Link to function |
|---|---|---|
| Artery | Thick wall; much elastic tissue and smooth muscle; collagen outer layer; narrow lumen; folded endothelium | Elastic tissue stretches and recoils to smooth and maintain high pressure; collagen resists bursting |
| Arteriole | More smooth muscle, less elastic tissue | Smooth muscle contracts or relaxes to control blood flow into capillary beds |
| Capillary | Wall of endothelium one cell thick; lumen about the width of a red blood cell | Short diffusion distance; slow flow; gaps let tissue fluid form |
| Venule | Thin wall, little muscle | Collects blood from capillaries |
| Vein | Thin wall, little elastic tissue or muscle, wide lumen, valves | Low pressure; valves stop backflow; contraction of skeletal muscles squeezes veins and moves blood back to the heart |

## Tissue fluid (d)

At the arteriole end of a capillary, **hydrostatic pressure** (blood pressure) is greater than the **oncotic pressure** created by plasma proteins, which tends to draw water back into the blood. Fluid is forced out through gaps in the capillary wall to form tissue fluid. Along the capillary hydrostatic pressure falls. At the venule end it is lower than the oncotic pressure, so water returns into the capillary by osmosis. Not all of it returns; the excess drains into lymph vessels.

**Worked example 1 (illustrative values).** Hydrostatic pressure is 4.6 kPa at the arteriole end and 2.3 kPa at the venule end; oncotic pressure is 3.3 kPa along the capillary.

```
arteriole end: 4.6 − 3.3 = 1.3 kPa net outward   → tissue fluid forms
venule end   : 2.3 − 3.3 = −1.0 kPa (1.0 kPa net inward) → water re-enters
```

The outward push is larger than the inward pull, which is why some fluid is left to form lymph.

| | Blood plasma | Tissue fluid | Lymph |
|---|---|---|---|
| Red blood cells | Present (in blood) | Absent | Absent |
| Plasma proteins | Many | Very few (too large to leave) | Few |
| White blood cells | Present | Some (can squeeze out) | Many lymphocytes |
| Fatty acids | Some | Some | More, absorbed from the gut |

## The mammalian heart (e)

Externally: coronary arteries on the surface supply the heart muscle; the aorta and pulmonary artery leave from the top, and the vena cava and pulmonary vein bring blood in. Internally: the right atrium and ventricle receive and pump deoxygenated blood to the lungs; the left side pumps oxygenated blood to the body. The **septum** separates the two sides. The **left ventricle wall is thickest** because it pumps blood all round the body against high resistance. **Atrioventricular valves** (tricuspid on the right, bicuspid on the left) are held by tendinous cords so they cannot turn inside out; **semilunar valves** sit at the bases of the aorta and pulmonary artery. In a dissection (PAG2), identify vessels by their walls (arteries thicker) and compare the ventricle walls.

## The cardiac cycle (f)

| Stage | What happens | Valves |
|---|---|---|
| Atrial systole | Atria contract, topping up the ventricles | AV open; semilunar closed |
| Ventricular systole | Ventricles contract from the apex upwards; ventricular pressure exceeds atrial, then arterial pressure | AV close (first heart sound); semilunar open |
| Diastole | Muscle relaxes; blood enters atria and ventricles; arterial pressure exceeds ventricular | Semilunar close (second heart sound); AV open as ventricular pressure falls below atrial |

Rule for any valve: it opens when pressure behind it is higher than pressure in front, and closes when the difference reverses.

**cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume**

**Worked example 2.** Stroke volume 70 cm³, heart rate 72 beats min⁻¹: cardiac output = 70 × 72 = 5040 cm³ min⁻¹ = **5.04 dm³ min⁻¹**.

## Coordinating the heartbeat (g)

Cardiac muscle is **myogenic**: it contracts without nervous stimulation.

1. The **sino-atrial node (SAN)** in the right atrium wall produces waves of excitation at regular intervals.
2. The wave spreads across both atria, which contract.
3. A layer of non-conducting tissue between atria and ventricles stops it passing directly.
4. The **atrio-ventricular node (AVN)** passes the wave on after a short delay, so the atria finish emptying before the ventricles contract.
5. The wave travels down the bundle of His in the septum to the apex, then up the **Purkyne tissue** in the ventricle walls.
6. The ventricles contract from the apex upwards, squeezing blood up into the arteries.

## ECG traces (h)

| Feature | Meaning |
|---|---|
| P wave | Depolarisation of the atria |
| QRS complex | Depolarisation of the ventricles |
| T wave | Repolarisation of the ventricles |

| Trace | Description |
|---|---|
| Tachycardia | Fast heart rate, above 100 beats per minute at rest |
| Bradycardia | Slow heart rate, below 60 beats per minute at rest |
| Fibrillation | Irregular, uncoordinated activity; no clear repeating P waves or QRS pattern |
| Ectopic heartbeat | An extra, early beat followed by a longer gap |

Heart rate = 60 ÷ (time between successive R peaks in seconds). An R-R interval of 0.80 s gives 75 beats per minute.

## Haemoglobin and gas transport (i)-(j)

Each haemoglobin molecule has four polypeptide chains, each with a haem group containing Fe²⁺. One haemoglobin can bind four oxygen molecules **reversibly** to form oxyhaemoglobin. Binding of the first oxygen changes the shape of the molecule and makes the next ones bind more easily, which gives the **S-shaped (sigmoid)** dissociation curve.

**Carbon dioxide** is carried in three ways: dissolved in plasma, bound to haemoglobin as carbaminohaemoglobin, and mostly as hydrogencarbonate ions.

1. CO₂ diffuses into red blood cells and reacts with water, catalysed by **carbonic anhydrase**, to form carbonic acid.
2. Carbonic acid dissociates into H⁺ and HCO₃⁻.
3. H⁺ binds to haemoglobin to form **haemoglobinic acid**, which makes oxyhaemoglobin release its oxygen and buffers the blood pH.
4. HCO₃⁻ diffuses out into the plasma and chloride ions move in to balance the charge: the **chloride shift**.

**Bohr effect.** At higher carbon dioxide concentrations the curve moves to the right: haemoglobin has a lower affinity for oxygen, so it releases more in actively respiring tissues.

**Fetal haemoglobin** has a higher affinity for oxygen than adult haemoglobin, so its curve lies to the left. At the partial pressures in the placenta, fetal haemoglobin takes up oxygen that adult haemoglobin releases.

## Common mistakes

- Saying arteries carry oxygenated blood; the pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood. Arteries carry blood away from the heart.
- Saying capillaries have thin walls "for strength"; one cell thick gives a short diffusion distance.
- Describing tissue fluid with red blood cells or many proteins in it.
- Putting the SAN in the left atrium, or forgetting the AVN delay.
- Confusing the P wave with ventricular contraction.
- Saying the Bohr effect increases oxygen affinity; it lowers it.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** vessel structure: pair each feature with its function.
- **Describe** the cardiac cycle from a pressure graph: name which pressure is higher at each valve event.
- **Calculate** heart rate from an ECG: measure several R-R intervals and use the mean.
- **Explain** a curve shift: say the direction, the change in affinity, and the effect on oxygen release.
