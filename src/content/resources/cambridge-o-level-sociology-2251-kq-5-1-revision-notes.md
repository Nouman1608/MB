These notes condense key question 5.1 of Cambridge O Level Sociology (2251), *What is education and
schooling?* The key question has three parts: formal and informal education (with the official and
hidden curricula), different types of schools, and alternative approaches to education. The syllabus
asks about the *effectiveness* and the *strengths and limitations* of each school type and approach,
so every item below has a judgement attached.

## Syllabus coverage

- **5.1.1** Differences between formal and informal education; the official curriculum (testing,
  syllabus content and subjects); the hidden curriculum (norms and values, punctuality, conformity,
  gender roles).
- **5.1.2** The effectiveness of different types of schools, e.g. state/public, private,
  selective/non-selective, single-sex, co-educational, faith, international; strengths and limitations
  of each.
- **5.1.3** Online learning, homeschooling, unschooling, vocational learning, progressive schooling;
  strengths and limitations of each.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Education | The process of gaining knowledge, skills, norms and values, inside or outside institutions |
| Schooling | The formal part of education that takes place in schools |
| Formal education | Planned learning in institutions, with a set curriculum, trained teachers and assessment |
| Informal education | Unplanned learning from everyday life: family, peers, media, community, work |
| Official curriculum | What schools openly teach: subjects, syllabus content and testing |
| Hidden curriculum | Norms and values learned from the way school is organised rather than from lessons |
| Selective school | Admits pupils on the basis of ability or attainment, often by test |
| Non-selective school | Admits pupils regardless of ability |
| Faith school | A school with a religious character or ethos |
| Unschooling | Child-led learning through interests and daily life, without a set curriculum |
| Progressive schooling | Child-centred schooling that gives pupils freedom and a say in how they learn |
| Vocational learning | Education and training for particular jobs or trades |

## Formal vs informal: four tests

Ask four questions to classify any learning: **Where?** (institution or everyday life) **Planned?**
(curriculum or not) **Who teaches?** (trained teachers or family, peers, media) **Assessed?**
(examinations and certificates or not). Formal education passes all four; informal education
usually passes none. Many situations are mixed: a parent following a homeschool curriculum is formal
learning in an informal setting.

## The official curriculum

- **Subjects**: which subjects are taught and which are compulsory shows what a society values.
- **Syllabus content**: what is included, and what is left out, within each subject.
- **Testing**: examinations measure and rank pupils, and decide who progresses.

Sociologists ask *whose* knowledge the official curriculum contains and who benefits when it is
tested.

## The hidden curriculum

The term is usually credited to Philip Jackson's *Life in Classrooms* (1968).

- **Norms and values**: respect for authority, competition, individual achievement.
- **Punctuality**: bells, registers and penalties for lateness.
- **Conformity**: uniforms, rules, queues and assemblies.
- **Gender roles**: who is chosen for which tasks, sports and prefect roles, and teachers'
  expectations of boys and girls.

**Two readings:** functionalists see it as useful socialisation into shared values; Marxists (Bowles
and Gintis, 1976) see it as training obedient workers for capitalism.

## Judging school types (5.1.2)

Effectiveness depends on the **measure**: examination results, equality of opportunity, social mixing,
values, or preparation for work.

| Type | Effective at | Less effective at |
|---|---|---|
| State/public | Access for all; national standards | Resources and class sizes in many countries |
| Private | Results; facilities; small classes | Equality; social mixing |
| Selective | Stretching high attainers | Fairness to those rejected; social mixing |
| Non-selective | Equal opportunity; mixed intake | Stretching the most able, some argue |
| Single-sex | Some argue: wider subject choice for girls | Experience of mixed settings |
| Co-educational | Reflecting wider society | Boys may dominate attention |
| Faith | Shared values; community | Social and religious mixing |
| International | Global qualifications; languages | Cost; links to local culture |

**Key evaluation point:** a school with high results may have recruited advantaged pupils, so results
alone do not prove the school is more effective.

## Alternatives (5.1.3) in one line each

- **Online learning**: flexible and wide access, but depends on devices and connection; less social
  contact.
- **Homeschooling**: tailored and safe, but depends on parents' time, knowledge and income.
- **Unschooling**: builds curiosity and independence, but risks gaps and makes qualifications harder;
  linked to John Holt and to Ivan Illich's critique of schooling in *Deschooling Society* (1971).
- **Vocational learning**: practical skills and employability, but often lower status.
- **Progressive schooling**: motivation and democratic values, but uneven outcomes and may not suit
  every child; A.S. Neill's Summerhill and Maria Montessori's methods are well-known examples.

## Exam traps

- The hidden curriculum is learned from organisation and routine, not from a hidden subject.
- Homeschooling and unschooling are not the same: one usually follows a curriculum, the other does not.
- "Effective" must be defined: effective at what, and for whom?
- Single-sex schooling is not only about girls; consider effects on boys too.
- Informal education is not the same as learning nothing useful; it teaches norms, skills and values.
- Do not judge a school type only by its examination results.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish education from schooling.
2. Give two features of formal education.
3. Name the three parts of the official curriculum listed in the syllabus.
4. Who is usually credited with the term "hidden curriculum"?
5. Give one example of the hidden curriculum teaching punctuality.
6. How do Marxists interpret the hidden curriculum?
7. Why might a private school's high results not prove it is more effective?
8. Give one strength and one limitation of faith schools.
9. What is the difference between homeschooling and unschooling?
10. Give one limitation of vocational learning.
11. Name one example of progressive schooling.
12. Which writer argued that society should be "deschooled"?

### Answers

1. Education is all learning of knowledge, skills and values; schooling is the formal part that
   happens in schools.
2. Any two: takes place in institutions; planned curriculum; trained teachers; formal assessment.
3. Testing, syllabus content and subjects.
4. Philip Jackson (*Life in Classrooms*, 1968).
5. Bells, registers or detentions for lateness.
6. As training pupils to obey authority and accept hierarchy, preparing them for work under
   capitalism (Bowles and Gintis, 1976).
7. Its pupils may already have advantages such as wealthier, more educated parents, so results may
   reflect intake rather than teaching.
8. Strength: shared values and a strong community; limitation: may separate pupils by religion and
   reduce social mixing.
9. Homeschooling usually follows a curriculum at home; unschooling lets the child decide what and how
   to learn.
10. Any one: lower status than academic routes; may channel working-class pupils into lower-paid work.
11. A.S. Neill's Summerhill School, or Montessori schools.
12. Ivan Illich (*Deschooling Society*, 1971).
