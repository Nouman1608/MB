This guide covers section 3.4.4 Training of the AQA GCSE Business (8132) specification, for first teaching from September 2017, the last section of Topic 4, Human resources. The [Human resources study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-human-resources/) gives a short overview; this page goes into each benefit, each type of training, costed comparisons and how to judge which method suits a particular business. All businesses and figures are invented.

Use it with the [training revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-8132-training-aqa-gcse-business-revision-notes/) and the [training practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-8132-training-aqa-gcse-business-practice/). The course hub is [AQA GCSE Business](/boards/aqa/gcse/business/).

## What the specification asks for

| You must be able to | Covered below |
|---|---|
| Explain why businesses train their workforce: productivity, adapting to technological change, motivation, retention, quality and customer service | Why businesses train |
| Understand induction, on-the-job and off-the-job training, and explain the benefits of induction | The three types |
| Analyse the advantages and disadvantages of on-the-job and off-the-job training | Comparing on and off the job |
| Evaluate which type of training suits different businesses | Choosing a method |

Training is the process of giving employees the skills and knowledge they need to do their job well. It is a cost now in return for benefits later, so most exam questions ask you to weigh one against the other.

## Why businesses train

The specification gives six reasons. For each one, make the chain to a business outcome.

| Reason | Chain to write |
|---|---|
| Productivity | Skilled staff work faster and with fewer mistakes, so output per worker rises and cost per unit falls |
| Adapting to technological change | New machines or software are only useful if staff can operate them; without training the investment is wasted |
| Motivation | Training shows the business values the worker and can lead to promotion, so effort rises |
| Retention | Staff who see a future in the business are less likely to leave, saving recruitment costs |
| Quality | Trained staff follow procedures correctly, so there are fewer faults, returns and complaints |
| Customer service | Staff with good product knowledge and communication skills keep customers happy and loyal |

### Worked example: is training worth it?

Garside Opticians introduces new eye-testing equipment. Training its 8 assistants costs £650 each, a total of 8 × £650 = £5,200. Without training, the manager estimates that slow, error-prone tests would cost about £180 a month in repeat appointments and lost bookings for each assistant.

- Monthly cost of untrained staff: 8 × £180 = £1,440
- Months for the training to pay for itself: £5,200 ÷ £1,440 = about 3.6 months

The training pays for itself in under four months. A full answer adds the risk that a trained assistant might leave, and the benefit to customer service that the figures do not capture.

## The three types of training

### Induction training

Induction is training for new employees when they start. It usually covers:

- the layout of the workplace and where things are kept
- health and safety rules and emergency procedures
- the business's policies, such as dress code and breaks
- introductions to colleagues and managers
- the basic tasks of the job.

**Benefits of induction.** The specification asks you to explain these specifically:

- New staff become productive sooner, because they do not waste time finding out how things work.
- They work safely from day one, reducing the risk of accidents.
- They feel welcome, so they are more likely to stay. A new starter who feels lost may leave within weeks, wasting the cost of recruiting them.
- They learn the business's standards, so quality and customer service are consistent.

### On-the-job training

The employee learns while doing the job at the workplace, often by watching and copying an experienced colleague (shadowing) or being coached by a supervisor.

### Off-the-job training

The employee learns away from their normal work: at a college, a training centre, a supplier's premises, a conference, or through a course run by specialist trainers. Online courses taken away from the work station also count.

## Comparing on and off the job

| | On-the-job | Off-the-job |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | Usually cheaper: no course fees or travel | Usually dearer: fees, travel and sometimes accommodation |
| Output during training | Trainee does some real work; but the experienced colleague's output falls | Trainee produces nothing while away |
| Relevance | Learns the exact tasks and equipment of this business | May be general and need adapting to the business |
| Quality of teaching | Only as good as the colleague, who may pass on bad habits or have no teaching skill | Expert trainers; may lead to a recognised qualification |
| Risk | Mistakes made with real customers or products | Mistakes made safely in a training setting |
| Scope | Limited to the skills already in the business | Can bring in skills nobody in the business has |

Analyse means turning each row into a chain. For example: "On-the-job training is cheaper because there are no course fees, but the supervisor training the new worker spends less time on her own work, so the department's output falls during the training period."

### Worked example: costing the two methods

Mardale Joinery needs two apprentices trained to use a computer-controlled cutting machine.

**On the job:** a senior joiner trains them over 4 weeks. He normally produces £1,500 of work a week but loses half of it while training, so the lost output is 4 × £750 = £3,000. There are no fees.

**Off the job:** the machine's supplier runs a 1-week course at £1,100 per person. The apprentices' wages of £350 a week each are still paid while they produce nothing, and travel costs £120 in total.

- Fees: 2 × £1,100 = £2,200
- Wages paid during the course: 2 × £350 = £700
- Total: £2,200 + £700 + £120 = **£3,020**

The two methods cost almost the same: £3,000 against £3,020. The deciding factors are therefore not money. The supplier's trainers know the machine in detail and the apprentices are trained in 1 week instead of 4. On the other hand, the senior joiner can show how the machine is used on Mardale's own products. A reasonable judgement is off the job for the first two, after which they can train others on the job.

## Choosing a method for different businesses

The specification asks you to evaluate which type suits different businesses. Use the case to decide.

| Feature of the business or task | Points towards |
|---|---|
| Simple, routine tasks, such as stacking shelves or serving coffee | On the job |
| Small business with little spare cash | On the job |
| Skill nobody in the business has, such as a new software system | Off the job |
| High-risk work, such as handling chemicals or caring for patients | Off the job first, for safe practice |
| Recognised qualification needed, such as a licence | Off the job |
| Large business with many new starters each year | Its own in-house training programme, plus on-the-job coaching |

Often the best answer is a combination: off-the-job training for the specialist skill, then on-the-job practice to apply it to the business's own work.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Analyse one benefit to Lowmere Care Homes of induction training for new care assistants.*

"Induction teaches new care assistants the home's health and safety and emergency procedures from their first day. *(benefit applied to a care home)* This matters because residents are vulnerable, so a mistake in moving or feeding them could cause harm. *(why it matters here)* Fewer accidents protect residents and the home's reputation, which affects whether families choose it and whether it passes inspections." *(consequence for the business)*

## Common mistakes

- Saying off-the-job training means training outside working hours. It means away from the normal place of work.
- Treating induction as training for all staff. It is for new starters.
- Writing "training improves productivity" with no chain explaining how.
- Forgetting the cost of lost output during on-the-job training.
- Ignoring the risk that trained staff leave for a competitor.
- Recommending expensive off-the-job training for a simple task in a small business.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Explain:** a benefit or drawback, with the reason it happens.
- **Analyse:** apply the method to the business and follow it through to costs, output, quality or retention.
- **Evaluate:** compare cost, speed, relevance and quality of learning, then choose a method, or a combination, for this business, with a reason.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between on-the-job and off-the-job training?**
On-the-job training takes place at work while doing the job, often by shadowing a colleague. Off-the-job training takes place away from the normal workplace, such as at a college or supplier's training centre.

**What are the benefits of induction training?**
New staff become productive more quickly, work safely, understand the business's standards and are more likely to stay.

**Is on-the-job training always cheaper?**
Usually, because there are no fees, but it has a hidden cost: the experienced worker doing the training produces less while teaching.
