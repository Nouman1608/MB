## What the specification asks for

- "Alkenes can be formed from alcohols by acid-catalysed elimination reactions."
- "Alkenes produced by this method can be used to produce addition polymers without using monomers derived from crude oil."
- You should be able to "outline the mechanism for the elimination of water from alcohols."

Required practical 4 (distil a product from a reaction) sits in the same section and is often set with this reaction, because the alkene can be distilled out as it forms.

## The reaction

The alcohol is heated with a **concentrated acid catalyst**, usually concentrated phosphoric acid (H₃PO₄) or concentrated sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄). A molecule of water is removed from the –OH carbon and an adjacent carbon, and a C=C forms. The reaction is also called **dehydration**.

General equation: alcohol → alkene + H₂O

Example: C₆H₁₁OH → C₆H₁₀ + H₂O  (cyclohexanol → cyclohexene)

## The mechanism, step by step

**Step 1: protonation.** A lone pair on the oxygen of –OH forms a bond to H⁺ from the acid. The product is a protonated alcohol, R–OH₂⁺, with the positive charge on oxygen.

*Why it is needed:* OH⁻ is a poor leaving group, but a neutral water molecule leaves easily. Protonation converts –OH into –OH₂⁺, which can leave as H₂O.

**Step 2: loss of water.** The C–O bond breaks and both its electrons go to oxygen. Water leaves and a **carbocation** forms on the carbon that held the –OH.

**Step 3: loss of H⁺.** A C–H bond on a carbon **adjacent** to C⁺ breaks; its electron pair moves into the C–C bond to form C=C. H⁺ is released, so the acid is **regenerated**: it is a catalyst.

| Step | Arrow starts at | Arrow ends at | Species formed |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | lone pair on O | H⁺ | R–OH₂⁺ |
| 2 | C–O bond | O | carbocation + H₂O |
| 3 | adjacent C–H bond | C–C bond | alkene + H⁺ |

Compare this with [elimination from halogenoalkanes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-3-2/), where a base (OH⁻) removes the proton in a single step and there is no carbocation.

## Counting the alkenes

Because the C=C forms between the carbocation carbon and an adjacent carbon that carries H, the method for counting products is:

1. Mark the C–OH carbon.
2. List the adjacent carbons with at least one H.
3. Non-equivalent neighbours give different positional isomers.
4. Check each alkene for E/Z isomers.

### Worked example 1: pentan-2-ol

*Name all the alkenes formed when pentan-2-ol, CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CH₂CH₃, is heated with concentrated phosphoric acid.*

- C–OH carbon: C2. Neighbours with H: C1 (CH₃) and C3 (CH₂). Not equivalent.
- From C1: **pent-1-ene**, CH₂=CHCH₂CH₂CH₃; C1 has two H, so no E/Z isomers.
- From C3: **pent-2-ene**, CH₃CH=CHCH₂CH₃; C2 has H and CH₃, C3 has H and C₂H₅, so **(E)-pent-2-ene** and **(Z)-pent-2-ene**.
- Total: **three alkenes**.

### Worked example 2: 3-methylpentan-3-ol

*How many alkenes form from 3-methylpentan-3-ol, CH₃CH₂C(OH)(CH₃)CH₂CH₃?*

- C–OH carbon: C3, bonded to two ethyl groups and one methyl group.
- The two CH₂ carbons (in the ethyl groups) are equivalent; the CH₃ carbon is different.
- From a CH₂: **3-methylpent-2-ene**, CH₃CH=C(CH₃)CH₂CH₃. C2 carries H and CH₃; C3 carries CH₃ and C₂H₅. Both carbons have two different groups, so **E and Z isomers** form.
- From the CH₃: **2-ethylbut-1-ene**, CH₂=C(CH₂CH₃)₂. C1 has two H, so no E/Z isomers.
- Total: **three alkenes**.

### Worked example 3: only one product

*Explain why cyclohexanol gives only one alkene.*

The C–OH carbon in the ring has two neighbouring CH₂ carbons, and by the symmetry of the ring they are equivalent. Removing H from either gives the same compound, **cyclohexene**. The C=C is in a ring, so E/Z isomers do not arise for this small ring.

## Polymers without crude oil

The specification makes a sustainability point. Most alkenes for polymers are made by cracking fractions from crude oil, a finite resource. If the **alcohol** comes from a renewable source, such as ethanol made from plant sugars, elimination gives the alkene without crude oil:

```
CH₃CH₂OH → CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O         then     n CH₂=CH₂ → –[CH₂–CH₂]ₙ–
```

The carbon in a plant-derived alcohol came from carbon dioxide absorbed while the plant grew, so this route reduces dependence on fossil sources. The poly(ethene) is chemically identical to poly(ethene) made from crude-oil ethene; only the source differs. The same applies to propene from propan-1-ol or propan-2-ol, giving poly(propene).

### Worked example 4: atom economy and mass

*(a) Calculate the percentage atom economy for making ethene from ethanol. (b) Calculate the minimum mass of ethanol needed to make 1.00 tonne of poly(ethene).*

```
(a) Mr: C₂H₅OH = 46.0; C₂H₄ = 28.0
    atom economy = 28.0 ÷ 46.0 × 100 = 60.9 %
(b) 1 mol ethanol → 1 mol ethene → 1 monomer unit (28.0 g)
    mass of ethanol = 1.00 × 46.0 ÷ 28.0 = 1.64 tonnes
```

The water by-product is the reason the atom economy is below 100 %; polymerisation itself has a 100 % atom economy.

## Linking to Required practical 4

Cyclohexene (boiling point about 83 °C) boils far below cyclohexanol (about 161 °C), because the alcohol forms hydrogen bonds between its molecules and the alkene does not. Heating the mixture with the acid catalyst and collecting what distils near 83 °C removes the alkene as it forms.

*Yield example:* 5.00 g of cyclohexanol (Mr 100.0) gives 3.69 g of cyclohexene (Mr 82.0). n(alcohol) = 0.0500 mol; n(alkene) = 0.0450 mol; yield = **90.0 %**.

## Common mistakes

- Starting the first arrow at H⁺ instead of at the oxygen lone pair.
- Losing water before protonation, as if OH⁻ left.
- Removing H from the C–OH carbon instead of an adjacent carbon.
- Not showing H⁺ released, so the catalyst is not regenerated.
- Writing "dilute acid" or forgetting heat.
- Missing E/Z isomers or a second positional isomer.
- Claiming the bio-route polymer is "biodegradable": it is the same polymer as before.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Outline the mechanism**: three steps, three arrows (lone pair to H⁺; C–O bond to O; C–H bond to C–C), the protonated alcohol and the carbocation.
- **State the conditions**: concentrated H₃PO₄ or H₂SO₄, heat.
- **Name all the alkenes**: use the four-step check and full names with E/Z labels.
- **Explain the advantage** of the route: monomers not derived from crude oil, a finite resource, when the alcohol is from a renewable source.
- **Calculate**: atom economy uses the balanced equation; yields use moles.
