> **Original questions written for Marlbridge.** They are not past-paper questions and do not copy the real exam's structure or mark tariffs. Answers use France as the model country for the war's impact.

**HL only.** These questions practise Regional study 9 in the Europe option (HL Paper 3). Revise first with the [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-europe-second-world-war-revision-notes/) and the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-europe-second-world-war/).

## Questions

**1.** State two weaknesses in the structure of the League of Nations. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why the League failed to stop the Italian invasion of Abyssinia. **[4]**

**3.** Read this short extract, constructed for practice.

> "I return from Munich with a settlement that spares our people from war. Some call it weakness. I call it the duty of a government that knows what war costs, and that is not yet ready for it." (Constructed for practice, in the voice of a British politician in 1938.)

**(a)** Explain what the extract suggests about the reasons for appeasement. **[3]**

**(b)** Analyse one limitation of the extract as evidence of why Britain followed appeasement. **[3]**

**4.** Explain two reasons why Germany and the USSR signed the Nazi-Soviet Pact in 1939. **[4]**

**5.** Analyse the reasons for the Allied victory over Germany. **[6]**

**6.** Examine the political impact of the Second World War on one European country to 1949. **[6]**

**7.** "The invasion of the Soviet Union in 1941 was the key factor in the decision to murder the Jews of Europe." To what extent do you agree? Plan an answer: state your argument, three themes and a counter-argument. **[15]**

**8.** Compare the responses of individuals and of governments to the persecution of Jews in Nazi-occupied Europe. **[4]**

**9.** Discuss how historians disagree about the reasons for appeasement. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two: the USA never joined; decisions needed unanimity; no army of its own; reliance on members to apply sanctions; Germany and the USSR were absent at first. One mark each. **[2]**

**2.** Up to 4. The League condemned Italy and imposed sanctions, but they did not include oil or close the Suez Canal, so they were ineffective (2). Britain and France wanted to keep Mussolini as an ally against Germany and negotiated the Hoare-Laval Pact, so they did not act firmly (2). **[4]**

**3. (a)** Up to 3: the speaker wants to avoid the costs of war, reflecting fear of another conflict (1); the claim that the government is "not yet ready" suggests the need to rearm (1); the extract shows that appeasement was defended against critics as realism, not weakness (1). **[3]**
**(b)** Up to 3: it is one politician's public statement and may be written to win support (1); it is a constructed extract, but a public speech might not state private motives such as anticommunism or empire concerns (1); it does not explain French views or public opinion (1). **[3]**

**4.** Up to 4, two reasons explained. Hitler wanted to avoid a war on two fronts when he invaded Poland (2). Stalin wanted time to rebuild his army, distrusted the western powers after Munich, and gained eastern Poland and the Baltic through the secret protocol (2). **[4]**

**5.** Up to 6 using a markband approach. Allied economic strength, including US production and Lend-Lease (1); Soviet resilience, the move of industry east and victories at Stalingrad and Kursk (1); Hitler's poor strategic decisions and overstretch, including the invasion of the USSR (1); the Grand Alliance and unconditional surrender kept the coalition together (1); Allied air and naval power, and invasions of Italy and Normandy (1); a judgement on the key factor, such as the eastern front (1). **[6]**

**6.** Up to 6. For France: Vichy ended the Third Republic and collaborated (1); de Gaulle and the Resistance gave a basis for postwar legitimacy (1); the provisional government purged collaborators (1); women were given the vote in 1944 (1); the Fourth Republic was founded in 1946 with nationalisations and social security (1); a judgement on how far politics changed (1). **[6]**

**7.** Credit a plan with these features, 3 marks each, 15 in all. Understanding of the claim: the invasion brought millions of Jews under German control and Einsatzgruppen shootings from 1941 (3). The case for war as key: Göring's directive (July 1941), Wannsee (1942) and the extermination camps developed after the invasion (3). Other factors: long-term antisemitism and Nazi ideology, earlier persecution (Nuremberg Laws, Kristallnacht), and the invasion of Poland and ghettos (3). Perspectives: intentionalist and functionalist views of how and when decisions were made (3). Counter-argument and judgement: for example that ideology was the necessary condition but the war created the circumstances and means (3). **[15]**

**8.** Up to 4. Individuals such as Sendler or the villagers of Le Chambon sheltered Jews at risk to themselves (1); governments mostly focused on winning the war and offered limited help, as at Evian and in the delay in acting on information (1); some governments helped, such as Denmark's people and Bulgaria's protection of its citizens, or collaborated, as Vichy did (1); a judgement on why individuals acted more readily than governments (1). **[4]**

**9.** Up to 6: one view, associated with the critics of the 1940s, says appeasement was weak and failed (2); a revisionist view says it was a realistic response to military weakness, public opinion and global commitments (2); explains why they differ, for example by hindsight and by access to government papers (1); a reasoned judgement (1). **[6]**
