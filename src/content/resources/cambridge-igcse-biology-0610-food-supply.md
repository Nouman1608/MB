This guide goes deeper into one sub-topic of the Cambridge IGCSE Biology 0610 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028: **20.1 Food supply**, the first part of topic 20, Human influences on ecosystems. It has three learning outcomes, and all three are **Core**, so every candidate needs them on every paper. There is no Supplement content in 20.1.

The [human influences on ecosystems study guide](/resources/igcse-biology-0610-human-influences-on-ecosystems/) lists each method and each advantage and disadvantage. This page explains the biology behind each one, so that you can write full descriptions, apply them to farms you have not seen before and interpret data about yields. Afterwards, use the [food supply revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-food-supply-revision-notes/) and the [food supply practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-food-supply-practice/).

## What sub-topic 20.1 asks you to do

| Outcome | Syllabus wording (summarised) | Tier |
|---|---|---|
| 20.1.1 | Describe how humans have increased food production, limited to: (a) agricultural machinery to use larger areas of land and improve efficiency; (b) chemical fertilisers to improve yields; (c) insecticides to improve quality and yield; (d) herbicides to reduce competition with weeds; (e) selective breeding to improve production by crop plants and livestock | Core |
| 20.1.2 | Describe the advantages and disadvantages of large-scale monocultures of crop plants | Core |
| 20.1.3 | Describe the advantages and disadvantages of intensive livestock production | Core |

All three outcomes say **describe**. For outcome 1, a description earns more when it says *how* the method raises production, not just that it does.

## 20.1.1 Five ways food production has increased

### (a) Agricultural machinery

Tractors, ploughs, seed drills, sprayers and combine harvesters let a few people farm a very large area. Work that once took many labourers days takes hours. Crops can be sown at the best time and harvested quickly when ripe, before bad weather damages them. The syllabus wording is the key: **larger areas of land** and **improved efficiency**.

### (b) Chemical fertilisers

Plants need mineral ions from the soil. Revise their roles in the [plant nutrition study guide](/resources/igcse-biology-0610-plant-nutrition/):

- **nitrate ions** are needed to make amino acids, and so proteins, for growth;
- **magnesium ions** are needed to make chlorophyll, for photosynthesis.

Every harvest removes these ions from the field in the crop. Fertilisers replace them, so plants grow larger and the **yield** (mass of crop per area) increases.

### (c) Insecticides

Insect pests eat leaves, roots, fruit and grain. Damaged leaves photosynthesise less, so **yield** falls; damaged fruit looks poor and may rot, so **quality** falls. Insecticides kill the pests, protecting both. Remember the pairing in the syllabus: insecticides improve **quality and yield**.

### (d) Herbicides

Weeds are plants growing where they are not wanted. They **compete** with the crop for **light, water, mineral ions and space**. Herbicides kill weeds, so the crop gets more of each resource and grows better. Many herbicides used on cereal fields kill broad-leaved weeds but not the narrow-leaved crop.

### (e) Selective breeding

Humans choose crop plants and livestock with useful features, cross them and repeat over many generations (sub-topic 18.3). Examples of improved production: wheat with larger grains and resistance to disease, cows giving more milk, chickens that grow faster or lay more eggs. See the [selection study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-selection/) for the method.

### Worked example 1: matching the method to the problem

| Problem on a farm | Method that helps | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Wheat leaves are pale and plants are small | chemical fertiliser | supplies nitrate (and magnesium) for growth and chlorophyll |
| Cabbages have holes in their leaves | insecticide | kills caterpillars, improving quality and yield |
| Poppies are growing between barley plants | herbicide | kills the weeds, reducing competition for light, water and ions |
| A farmer cannot harvest 500 hectares in time | machinery | allows a large area to be harvested efficiently |
| Cows give low milk yields | selective breeding | breeding from the highest-yielding cows over many generations |

## 20.1.2 Large-scale monocultures

A **monoculture** is a large area growing a single crop variety, such as thousands of hectares of maize or oil palm.

### Advantages

- **Efficient use of machinery:** one crop is sown, sprayed and harvested at the same time with the same equipment.
- **Specialised conditions:** fertiliser, irrigation and pesticides can be chosen for one crop.
- **Large yield at low cost per tonne**, because of the scale.
- **Uniform crop:** all plants ripen together and are similar in size, which suits processing and sale.

### Disadvantages

- **Reduced biodiversity:** natural habitats and hedgerows are removed to create big fields, and only one plant species is grown, so few other species can live there.
- **Pests and diseases spread easily:** the pest's food is everywhere, and plants of one variety are genetically similar, so a disease that attacks one can attack them all. More pesticide is then needed.
- **Soil mineral ions are depleted:** the same crop removes the same ions every year, so more fertiliser is needed.
- **Pollution risk:** extra fertiliser and pesticide can be washed into rivers, linking to eutrophication in the [pollution study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-pollution/).

## 20.1.3 Intensive livestock production

**Intensive** livestock production means keeping large numbers of animals in a small space, often indoors, with food, water, temperature and lighting controlled. Examples are poultry in sheds and pigs in pens.

### Advantages

- **More food from each animal:** the animals move little and are kept warm, so less of the energy from their food is released in respiration for movement and keeping warm, and more goes into growth. This links to the energy losses in the [food chains and food webs study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-food-chains-and-food-webs/).
- **Fast growth and high output** of meat, eggs or milk.
- **Lower cost per unit** of food produced.
- **Less land** needed per animal.
- Animals are protected from predators and bad weather.

### Disadvantages

- **Disease spreads quickly** between animals kept close together, so antibiotics are often given; this can help select antibiotic-resistant bacteria.
- **Pollution:** large amounts of concentrated faeces and urine can pollute rivers and add nitrate.
- **Animal welfare:** animals have little space and cannot behave naturally.
- **Large amounts of feed** must be grown elsewhere, which uses land, fertiliser and energy.
- **Energy for heating, lighting and ventilation** is needed.

### Worked example 2: interpreting data

A comparison of two egg farms (illustrative figures):

| | Free-range farm | Intensive farm |
|---|---|---|
| Hens | 3 000 | 30 000 |
| Land area / hectares | 12 | 1.5 |
| Eggs per hen per year | 260 | 300 |

**(a) Calculate the eggs produced per hectare per year on each farm.**

- Free-range: 3 000 × 260 = 780 000 eggs; ÷ 12 = **65 000 eggs per hectare per year**.
- Intensive: 30 000 × 300 = 9 000 000 eggs; ÷ 1.5 = **6 000 000 eggs per hectare per year**.

**(b) Suggest why each intensive hen lays more eggs.** It moves less and is kept warm, so less energy from food is released for movement and heat, leaving more for egg production.

**(c) Give one disadvantage the data do not show.** Disease spreads more easily among 30 000 hens kept close together, or animal welfare is poorer.

## Common mistakes

- Mixing up insecticides (kill insects) and herbicides (kill weeds).
- Writing "fertilisers make plants grow" without naming nitrate or another ion, or without "increase yield".
- Saying herbicides improve quality "because weeds are ugly"; the syllabus reason is reduced **competition**.
- Listing "uses machinery" as a disadvantage of monocultures; it is an advantage.
- Describing intensive farming as "cruel" with no biological point. Welfare is one disadvantage; disease spread and pollution are others.
- Forgetting that intensive animals gain more mass because **less energy is lost** in movement and keeping warm.

## Exam technique for 20.1 command words

- **Describe** how food production has increased: name the method and say what it does to yield, quality or efficiency.
- **Describe** advantages and disadvantages: give balanced points, each with its reason.
- **Suggest**: apply a method to the problem in the question, or use energy losses to explain livestock data.
- **Calculate**: show each step and give units such as "per hectare per year".

## Frequently asked questions

**Is food supply Core or Extended in Cambridge IGCSE Biology 0610?**
Sub-topic 20.1 is entirely Core: the five methods of increasing food production and the advantages and disadvantages of monocultures and intensive livestock production.

**What is the difference between insecticides and herbicides?**
Insecticides kill insect pests, improving quality and yield. Herbicides kill weeds, reducing competition with the crop for light, water and mineral ions.

**Why do intensively farmed animals grow faster?**
They move less and are kept warm, so less of the energy in their food is released in respiration for movement and keeping warm, and more is used for growth.
