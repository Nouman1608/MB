This guide covers **1.1 The drainage basin system** in the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026. The syllabus lists five things to know: the **outputs** (evaporation, evapotranspiration and river discharge), the **stores** (interception, soil water, surface water, groundwater and channel storage), the **flows above ground** (throughfall, stemflow, overland flow and channel flow), the **flows below ground** (infiltration, percolation, throughflow, groundwater flow and baseflow), and **underground water** (water tables, groundwater, recharge and springs).

The content is examined in Paper 1 Core Physical Geography, either as a 10-mark data response question in Section A or within the 30-mark structured question on Hydrology and fluvial geomorphology in Section B. Use this guide with the [1.1 drainage basin system revision notes](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-drainage-basin-system-revision-notes/) for quick recall and the [1.1 drainage basin system practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-drainage-basin-system-practice/) to test yourself.

## Thinking in systems

A drainage basin is an **open system**: precipitation enters across its boundary and water leaves it as discharge to the sea or a lake, and as vapour to the atmosphere. Every component in the syllabus list is either a **store** (water held for a time) or a **flow** (water moving between stores). Outputs are the flows that cross the basin boundary.

The most useful idea for exam answers is **residence time**: how long water stays in each store. Interception and surface stores hold water for minutes to hours. Soil water is held for days to weeks. Groundwater can be held for months, years or much longer. A basin dominated by short-residence stores responds quickly to rain; one with large groundwater stores responds slowly and keeps its river flowing in dry spells. Almost every "explain why this river behaves differently" question comes back to which stores the water passes through.

## Outputs: three terms that are not interchangeable

- **Evaporation** is the change of liquid water to vapour from any surface: water bodies, wet soil, wet leaves and puddles.
- **Transpiration** is the loss of water vapour from plants, mainly through stomata on leaves. The syllabus does not list it separately, because it is included in the next term.
- **Evapotranspiration** is evaporation and transpiration combined. Geographers often distinguish **potential evapotranspiration** (what would be lost if water were never in short supply) from **actual evapotranspiration** (what is lost given the water available). In a hot desert basin, potential evapotranspiration is very high but actual evapotranspiration is low, because there is little water to lose.
- **River discharge** is the volume of water leaving the basin through its channel per unit time, measured in cumecs.

## Stores and above-ground flows

**Interception** is water caught on leaves, stems and buildings. It has two fates: it evaporates back to the air (**interception loss**, an output) or it continues to the ground as **throughfall** (dripping from leaves or falling through gaps) or **stemflow** (running down branches and trunks). Interception loss is greatest in light, short showers on dense evergreen canopies, because the canopy can hold a large share of a small rainfall total. In a long storm the canopy soon becomes saturated and most rain passes through.

**Surface water storage** is water held in puddles, hollows, lakes and wetlands. **Overland flow** is water moving across the ground surface. It occurs when rainfall intensity exceeds the infiltration capacity of the soil, or when the soil is already saturated. **Channel flow** is the movement of water within the river channel itself, and **channel storage** is the water held in the channel at any moment.

## Below-ground flows

- **Infiltration** moves water from the surface into the soil. Its maximum rate is the **infiltration capacity**, which is high on sandy, well-structured soils with many root channels and low on clay, compacted or sealed surfaces.
- **Throughflow** moves water downslope within the soil, often fastest along root channels and animal burrows (pipe flow) or above a less permeable soil horizon.
- **Percolation** moves water downwards from the soil into the rock below.
- **Groundwater flow** is the slow movement of water through saturated rock.
- **Baseflow** is groundwater entering the river channel through its bed and banks.

The sequence below ground runs downwards (infiltration, then percolation) and sideways (throughflow in soil, groundwater flow in rock). Mixing up these directions is a common error.

## Underground water: water tables, recharge and springs

Below the surface, rock and soil are divided into two zones. In the **unsaturated zone** the pores contain both air and water. In the **saturated zone** every pore is full of water. The **water table** is the upper surface of the saturated zone. Water in the saturated zone is **groundwater**, and a rock that can store and transmit useful amounts of it is an **aquifer**.

The water table is not flat or fixed:

- It roughly follows the shape of the land, higher under hills and lower under valleys, but with a gentler gradient.
- It rises when **recharge** exceeds losses. Recharge is the addition of water to the saturated zone, mainly by percolation. In a climate with a wet season it is greatest then, because evapotranspiration is low and soils are full.
- It falls in dry periods, when baseflow, evapotranspiration from deep roots and abstraction by wells remove more water than percolation adds.

A **spring** is a point where groundwater emerges at the surface. Springs form where the water table meets the ground surface. A common setting is the junction of a permeable rock lying above an impermeable one: water percolating through the permeable rock cannot pass down into the impermeable layer, so it moves sideways and emerges along the boundary, sometimes as a line of springs along the foot of a hill. Springs can also emerge along faults and joints.

Because the water table moves, the position of springs can move too. In chalk landscapes in southern England, some valleys contain **winterbournes**: streams that flow only when the water table is high in winter and dry up when it falls in summer. This is a clear example of how underground stores control surface flows.

## Worked example 1: interception

*In a woodland plot (fictional data), a storm delivered 40 mm of rain above the canopy. Gauges under the trees recorded 26 mm of throughfall, and collars on the trunks recorded 2 mm of stemflow. Calculate the interception loss as a percentage of the rainfall.*

Water reaching the ground = 26 + 2 = 28 mm.
Interception loss = 40 - 28 = 12 mm.
As a percentage: 12 / 40 x 100 = **30%**.

**Comment:** the same canopy would intercept a higher percentage of a 5 mm shower and a lower percentage of a 100 mm storm, because its storage capacity is fixed. Mention this when asked to explain differences between storms.

## Worked example 2: when does overland flow begin?

*A soil has an infiltration capacity of 15 mm per hour. Rain falls at 25 mm per hour for 2 hours. Assuming the infiltration capacity stays constant, how much infiltration-excess overland flow is generated?*

Excess rate = 25 - 15 = 10 mm per hour.
Over 2 hours: 10 x 2 = **20 mm** of overland flow.

**Comment:** in reality infiltration capacity falls during a storm as the soil wets up, so the true figure would be higher. If the same rain fell at 10 mm per hour for 5 hours, the total would be 50 mm, the same as before, but none would exceed infiltration capacity at the start. This is why rainfall **intensity**, not just total, matters.

## Annotated model answer

*"Explain how the underground stores of a drainage basin affect the flow of a river during a long dry period." [5]*

> During a dry period there is little or no input from precipitation, so overland flow and throughflow soon stop **(identifies which flows end)**. River flow is then maintained by **baseflow**, as groundwater seeps into the channel through its bed and banks **(names the key flow)**. In a basin with a large aquifer, such as permeable sandstone or chalk, a large groundwater store drains slowly, so the river keeps flowing for months **(links store size to outcome)**. As groundwater drains, the water table falls **(process)**; if it falls below the channel bed, baseflow stops and the river may dry up, as winterbournes do in summer **(consequence with example)**.

This scores well because each sentence links a store to a flow and then to the river's behaviour.

## Common mistakes

- Calling infiltration a store. It is a flow; soil water is the store it fills.
- Using evaporation and evapotranspiration as if they were the same output.
- Writing that groundwater "flows quickly". Groundwater flow is the slowest flow in the basin, which is exactly why it sustains rivers.
- Describing the water table as a fixed line. Its rise and fall drive recharge, springs and baseflow.
- Treating throughfall as overland flow. Throughfall is rain reaching the ground through a canopy; it may then infiltrate or run off.
- Forgetting that interception loss is an output, not just a delay.

## Exam technique

- **Define** questions want a precise one-sentence meaning. "Recharge: the addition of water to the saturated zone, mainly by percolation" is enough for the mark.
- **Describe** questions on a diagram or flow chart want named components and figures from the resource, not explanation.
- **Explain** questions want process chains. Use "so" and "because" to link: rain exceeds infiltration capacity, so overland flow begins, so water reaches the channel quickly.
- **Suggest** questions give you an unfamiliar basin. Apply the store-and-flow logic to the information given, such as rock type or vegetation, and avoid answers you could have written without reading the resource.
- In Section B, a labelled systems diagram can support an explanation, but it must be integrated into your answer and referred to in the text.
