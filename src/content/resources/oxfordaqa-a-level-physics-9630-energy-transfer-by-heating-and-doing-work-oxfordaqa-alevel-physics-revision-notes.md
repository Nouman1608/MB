These notes condense section 3.11.1, Energy transfer by heating and doing work, of OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630), the first part of Section 3.11 Thermal physics (International A-level only), including Required practical 8. Full explanations and worked examples are in the [heating and doing work study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-energy-transfer-by-heating-and-doing-work-oxfordaqa-alevel-physics/); test yourself with the [heating and doing work practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-energy-transfer-by-heating-and-doing-work-oxfordaqa-alevel-physics-practice/). Conduction and U-values are summarised in the [first law, heating and conduction revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-first-law-of-thermodynamics-heating-and-conduction-revision-notes/).

## Equations

| Equation | Use | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| ΔU = Q + W | First law | Q to the system by heating; W done on the system |
| Q = mcΔθ | Temperature change | Δθ in K or °C (same size) |
| Q = ml | Change of state | No temperature change |
| P = (Δm/Δt) cΔθ | Continuous flow | Δm/Δt is the mass flow rate |
| c = P / (m × gradient) | From a θ–t heating graph | Constant power, no losses |
| E = VIt | Electrical energy supplied | Required practical 8 |

## Key terms

| Term | What to write |
|---|---|
| Internal energy | Sum of the randomly distributed kinetic and potential energies of the particles in a body |
| Specific heat capacity c | Energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg by 1 K |
| Specific latent heat l | Energy needed to change the state of 1 kg without a change of temperature |
| Latent heat of fusion | Solid to liquid (melting) |
| Latent heat of vaporisation | Liquid to gas (boiling) |
| Continuous flow system | Fluid flowing steadily through a heater or exchanger |

## Condensed recall

- Internal energy rises by heating the system or doing work on it; it falls when the system heats its surroundings or does work on them.
- Sign convention: Q and W are positive when energy goes **in**.
- Temperature reflects mean random kinetic energy. During a change of state the temperature is constant, so kinetic energy is constant; the potential energies change.
- Multi-stage problems: one equation per stage (mcΔθ for warming, ml for melting or boiling), then add.
- Mixing (no losses): energy lost by the hot object = energy gained by the cold one.
- Flow: replace m by the mass per second, and energy by power.
- Heating curve: sloping parts give c; flat parts give l.

## First law sign table

| Process | Q | W | ΔU |
|---|---|---|---|
| Heated, rigid container | + | 0 | + |
| Compressed, insulated | 0 | + | + |
| Expands, insulated | 0 | − | − |
| Cools at fixed volume | − | 0 | − |

## Required practical 8 in brief

- Insulated metal block (or liquid) with an immersion heater and thermometer; ammeter in series, voltmeter across the heater.
- Record θ at regular intervals; energy supplied = VIt.
- Plot θ against t; c = VI / (m × gradient of the straight part).
- Energy losses make Δθ smaller, so the calculated c is **too large**.
- Improvements: insulate; start below room temperature and finish the same amount above; record the highest temperature after switching off; put oil in the thermometer hole.

## Worked reminders

- 250 J of work done on a gas, 90 J lost by heating: ΔU = 250 − 90 = **+160 J**.
- 2.5 kg of water from 15 °C to 85 °C: Q = 2.5 × 4180 × 70 = **7.3 × 10⁵ J**.
- 12.0 V, 3.50 A heater; 1.00 kg block; gradient 0.0465 K s⁻¹: c = 42.0 / 0.0465 = **903 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹**.

## Exam traps

- Using W as work done **by** the gas.
- Saying no energy is supplied during melting.
- Forgetting the melting stage when ice ends up as water.
- Using c for water in the ice stage.
- Using a total mass in a flow problem instead of mass per second.
- Claiming heat losses make the measured c smaller.
- Defining internal energy as "the heat in an object" or omitting potential energy.

## Self-test

1. Define internal energy.
2. State the first law of thermodynamics, explaining each symbol.
3. During melting, which particle energies change?
4. A gas expands, doing 150 J of work, while 400 J is supplied by heating. Find ΔU.
5. How much energy raises 0.80 kg of water by 25 K? (c = 4180 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹)
6. How much energy melts 0.50 kg of ice at 0 °C? (l = 3.34 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹)
7. 0.40 kg of water at 80 °C mixes with 0.60 kg of water at 20 °C. Find the final temperature (no losses).
8. Water flows at 0.020 kg s⁻¹ through a 3.0 kW heater. Find the temperature rise.
9. A θ–t graph for 0.50 kg heated at 60 W has gradient 0.060 K s⁻¹. Find c.
10. The same heater keeps the substance at its melting point for 2500 s while it melts. Find l.
11. Why is it better to find c from the gradient of θ against t than from one pair of readings?
12. Explain why a bicycle pump warms up when used quickly.

### Answers

1. The sum of the randomly distributed kinetic and potential energies of the particles in a body.
2. ΔU = Q + W: ΔU is the change in internal energy, Q the energy transferred to the system by heating, W the work done on the system.
3. The potential energies change; the mean kinetic energy stays constant.
4. ΔU = 400 + (−150) = **+250 J**.
5. 0.80 × 4180 × 25 = **8.4 × 10⁴ J**.
6. 0.50 × 3.34 × 10⁵ = **1.67 × 10⁵ J**.
7. (0.40 × 80 + 0.60 × 20) / 1.00 = **44 °C**.
8. Δθ = 3000 / (0.020 × 4180) = **36 K**.
9. 60 / (0.50 × 0.060) = **2000 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹**.
10. 60 × 2500 / 0.50 = **3.0 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹**.
11. The gradient uses many readings, averages random error and ignores the slow start while the heater warms up.
12. Work is done on the air when it is compressed quickly; there is little time for heating of the surroundings, so Q ≈ 0 and ΔU = W > 0: the air's internal energy and temperature rise, and it warms the pump.
