## Questions

**1.** State what relative purchasing power parity predicts about the exchange rates of two countries with different inflation rates. **[2]**

**2.** A basket of goods costs 330 ceds in Country C and 220 dels in Country D. The market exchange rate is 1.80 ceds per del.

**(a)** Calculate the PPP exchange rate. **[2]**
**(b)** State whether the del is overvalued or undervalued against the ced on this measure, and by what percentage of the PPP rate. **[2]**

**3.** Using the rate of 1.80 ceds per del, Country C has inflation of 8% and Country D has inflation of 3%. Use relative PPP to forecast the new exchange rate, and describe the movement of each currency. **[3]**

**4.** Explain two reasons why the actual exchange rate between two currencies may differ from the PPP rate. **[6]**

**5.** In Country E, a depreciation cuts the foreign price of exports by 12% and export volume rises 15%. The home price of imports rises 12% and import volume falls 6%.

**(a)** Calculate the price elasticity of demand for exports and for imports. **[2]**
**(b)** State the sum and say whether the Marshall-Lerner condition is met. **[2]**

**6.** Country A has balanced trade. It exports 800 units at 50 each and imports 500 units at 80 each, both values in its own currency. A depreciation of 8% raises the home price of imports by 8%. Exporters keep their home price. PEDx = 0.7 and PEDm = 0.4. Calculate the new trade balance. **[5]**

**7.** Explain why the trade balance of an economy might worsen before it improves after a depreciation. **[4]**

**8.** The table shows the invented trade balance of Country G, in billions of its currency, from the quarter of a depreciation (quarter 0).

| Quarter | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Balance | −1.5 | −2.4 | −2.9 | −2.2 | −1.3 | −0.2 |

**(a)** Identify the quarter with the lowest balance and state how far it is below the starting balance. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the total shortfall against the starting balance across quarters 1 to 3. **[2]**
**(c)** State the first quarter in which the balance is better than at the start. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain what the pattern suggests about elasticities of demand for exports and imports. **[2]**

**9.** Country H exports a machine at 120 hals. 1 hal buys 0.80 mels, and the hal falls to 0.72 mels.

**(a)** Calculate the price of the machine in mels before and after, and the percentage change. **[3]**
**(b)** The machine uses a component costing 45 mels. Calculate its price in hals before and after. **[2]**
**(c)** Use your answer to (b) to explain why the exporter's gain may be smaller than the fall in the mel price suggests. **[1]**

**10.** Country A has high unemployment. Evaluate whether a depreciation of its currency will reduce unemployment. **[8]**

**11.** Evaluate whether a government should manage its currency to keep it low in order to improve international competitiveness. **[10]**

**12.** Country J's net exports rise by 4 billion units after a depreciation. Of each extra unit of income, 0.15 is saved, 0.15 is taxed and 0.10 is spent on imports. GDP is 250 billion. Imports are 30% of the consumer price basket and import prices rise 9%.

**(a)** Calculate the multiplier and the rise in national income. **[2]**
**(b)** Express the rise as a percentage of GDP. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the rise in the price level. **[2]**
**(d)** Comment on the result. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** The exchange rate changes by about the difference between the inflation rates [1]. The currency of the higher-inflation country depreciates [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A bare statement that rates "follow prices" gives no direction. Name which currency falls and say that it is the inflation gap, not the level, that drives the change.

**2. (a)** PPP rate = 330 / 220 [1] = **1.50 ceds per del [1]**.
**(b)** The market rate (1.80) is above 1.50, so the del is overvalued [1]. (1.80 − 1.50) / 1.50 = **20% [1]**.
*Examiner insight:* Divide the gap by the PPP rate, not the market rate; 0.30 / 1.80 gives a different percentage. State the currency unit in your answer.

**3.** New rate = 1.80 × 1.08 / 1.03 [1] = **1.887 ceds per del [1]**. The del appreciates by about 4.9% and the ced depreciates [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep the direction straight: more ceds per del means the ced is worth less. Say both currencies, since the question asks for the movement of each.

**4.** Any two reasons, each worth three marks for identification, development and link.
- Non-tradable goods [1]: services such as local rents or haircuts cannot be traded across borders [1], so their price gaps are not arbitraged and goods prices do not equalise, leaving the rate away from PPP [1].
- Capital flows [1]: interest rate differences and speculation move money between countries [1], so the demand for a currency reflects financial flows and not only goods prices [1].
- Other valid reasons: transport costs and trade barriers, differentiated products, and different baskets.
*Examiner insight:* A named reason alone stays at a statement. Developed points finish with the effect on the exchange rate, which is what the question asks.

**5. (a)** PEDx = 15 / 12 = **1.25 [1]**; PEDm = 6 / 12 = **0.5 [1]**.
**(b)** Sum = **1.75 [1]**, which exceeds 1, so the condition is met and the trade balance improves [1].
*Examiner insight:* Treat both elasticities as positive. The comparison with 1 is what answers the question, so say it explicitly.

**6.** Export volume rises 0.7 × 8 = 5.6% to 844.8 [1]. Export revenue = 844.8 × 50 = 42,240 [1]. New import price = 80 × 1.08 = 86.40 [1]. Import volume falls 0.4 × 8 = 3.2% to 484 [1]. Import cost = 484 × 86.40 = 41,817.6, so balance = 42,240 − 41,817.6 = **about +422 [1]**. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Show each volume and price before multiplying. The sum of elasticities is 1.1, so a small surplus is the expected result, which gives you a check on your arithmetic.

**7.** A depreciation raises the home price of imports and lowers the foreign price of exports [1]. In the short run demand is inelastic because of existing contracts, habits and slow supplier changes [1]. Import bills rise more than export revenue, so the balance worsens [1]. Over time elasticities rise, the Marshall-Lerner condition is met and the balance improves [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Link each stage with a cause. Saying "the J-curve happens" repeats the question; the reason is the difference between short-run and long-run elasticities.

**8. (a)** Quarter 2 [1]; 1.4 below the starting balance (2.9 − 1.5) [1].
**(b)** Shortfalls: 0.9 + 1.4 + 0.7 [1] = **3.0 billion [1]**.
**(c)** **Quarter 4** (−1.3 against −1.5) [1].
**(d)** In the short run elasticities are low, so the balance worsens [1]; later they rise and the Marshall-Lerner condition is met [1].
*Examiner insight:* Measure from the starting balance, not from zero. For part (d) use the data to support the point, quoting the trough and the recovery.

**9. (a)** Before: 120 × 0.80 = 96 mels [1]; after: 120 × 0.72 = 86.40 mels [1]; change = **−10% [1]**.
**(b)** Before: 45 / 0.80 = **56.25 hals [1]**; after: 45 / 0.72 = **62.50 hals [1]**.
**(c)** The component costs 6.25 hals more per machine, so costs rise and the exporter keeps less of the gain [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Convert imports by dividing and exports by multiplying with the same quote. Using the same calculation for both is a common slip.

**10.** Chain of reasoning, up to 4 marks: depreciation lowers the foreign price of exports and raises import prices [1]; if the Marshall-Lerner condition holds, net exports rise [1]; aggregate demand rises, with a multiplier effect [1]; firms raise output and hire, so unemployment falls [1].
Evaluation, up to 4 marks: the J-curve delays the gain [1]; unemployment may be structural, so extra demand will not remove it [1]; dearer imported inputs raise costs and may reduce hiring [1]; the final judgement is supported by spare capacity, elasticities and time [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Apply the analysis to unemployment itself, not just to exports. Evaluation earns credit when you state what the answer turns on, such as spare capacity or elasticities. A bare list of pros and cons does not do that.

**11.** Analysis, up to 4 marks: a low rate cuts foreign prices of exports [1]; raises sales and output if elasticities are high [1]; protects firms against imports [1]; central bank intervention or low interest rates are the tools [1].
Evaluation, up to 6 marks: dearer imports and imported inflation [1]; lower living standards [1]; reserves and the cost of holding them [1]; retaliation by partners [1]; non-price factors such as quality matter in the long run [1]; reasoned judgement that depends on elasticities, time and the economy's structure [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* A long answer with a one-sided case rarely shows evaluation. Weigh the short-run gain against the long-run costs, and reach a conclusion that follows from your argument.

**12. (a)** Withdrawals = 0.15 + 0.15 + 0.10 = 0.40, so multiplier = 1 / 0.40 = **2.5 [1]**; income rises by 4 × 2.5 = **10 billion [1]**.
**(b)** 10 / 250 = **4.0% [1]**.
**(c)** 0.30 × 9 = **2.7% [1]**, using the share of imports in the basket [1].
**(d)** Output gains of 4.0% come with a 2.7% rise in prices, showing the trade-off between growth and inflation [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Add all three withdrawals before dividing. A comment should use both of your results, not just restate them.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing the PPP gap by the market rate instead of the PPP rate.
- Reversing the exchange rate quote, so a depreciation seems to raise export prices abroad.
- Adding PEDx and PEDm but not comparing the sum with 1.
- Explaining the J-curve without naming short-run elasticity.
- Describing PPP without any evaluation.
- Calculation answers with no units or no working shown.
- Evaluation as a list rather than a judgement tied to conditions.
- Leaving out the multiplier or imported inflation in the macro chain.
