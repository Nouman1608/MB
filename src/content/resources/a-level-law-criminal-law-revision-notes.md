Condensed, exam-focused notes for Topic 2 of Cambridge AS & A Level Law (9084), 2026-2028 series.

## 2.1 Elements of a crime

- **Actus reus**: conduct, omission (failing to act where a duty exists), causation (defendant's
  conduct actually and legally caused the result).
- **Mens rea**: intention (direct — the result was the actual purpose; indirect/oblique — a virtually
  certain, foreseen consequence) and recklessness (aware of an unjustified risk, took it anyway).

## 2.2 Offences against property (all named statutory sections)

| Offence | Section | Key element |
|---|---|---|
| Theft | s1 Theft Act 1968 | Dishonest appropriation of property belonging to another, intent to permanently deprive |
| Robbery | s8 Theft Act 1968 | Theft + use/threat of force at/before the theft |
| Burglary | s9 Theft Act 1968 | Trespass + intent to commit a listed offence, or committing one having trespassed |
| Aggravated burglary | s10 | Burglary while carrying a weapon |
| Blackmail | s21 Theft Act 1968 | Unwarranted demand with menaces, view to gain/intent to cause loss |
| Handling stolen goods | s22 Theft Act 1968 | Dishonestly receiving/retaining/disposing, knowing/believing stolen |
| Making off without payment | s3 Theft Act 1978 | Dishonestly leaving without paying when payment expected on the spot |
| Criminal damage | Criminal Damage Act 1971 | s1 destroy/damage; s2 threats; s3 possession with intent |
| Fraud | Fraud Act 2006 | s2 false representation; s3 failing to disclose; s4 abuse of position |

- Section A's scenario question typically expects identification of which specific offence(s) apply,
  working through actus reus and mens rea for each — not a loose discussion of "stealing."

## 2.3 Sentencing in England and Wales

- **Adult offenders**: custodial, community, fines, discharges — and the factors courts weigh between
  them.
- **Young offenders**: custodial and community options, plus the role of parental responsibility.
- **Aims of sentencing** (punishment, deterrence, rehabilitation, public protection, reparation) assessed
  separately for adults and young offenders — the two systems don't weight these aims identically.

## Assessment objectives on Paper 2

AO1 (Knowledge and understanding) 35%, AO2 (Analysis and application) 50%, AO3 (Evaluation) 15%. The
heavier AO2 weighting (vs Paper 1's 30%) reflects Section A's scenario-based format, built to test
application of legal rules to facts, not just recall.

## Exam technique for this topic

Learn each property offence as a fixed checklist of actus reus and mens rea elements tied to its
specific statutory section, since Section A's scenario question is marked on whether each element is
identified and applied to the facts — not on a general description of what "counts as theft." Always
apply 2.1's elements-of-a-crime principles (actus reus, causation, mens rea) as the first step before
moving to a specific property offence, since 2.1 underlies every offence in 2.2. For sentencing, keep
adult and young-offender content clearly separated, since the syllabus tests them as parallel but
distinct systems.

## Worked example: applying elements of a crime to a scenario

Consider a scenario where a defendant enters a neighbour's house through an unlocked window intending
to take jewellery, but flees empty-handed when disturbed. A strong answer works through the elements
systematically: the actus reus of burglary under s9(1)(a) requires entering a building as a trespasser
— satisfied here since the defendant had no permission to enter — while the mens rea requires intent to
commit a listed offence (here, theft) at the moment of entry, which the facts support even though the
theft itself was never completed, since s9(1)(a) burglary is complete on entry with the required intent
and does not require the underlying offence to succeed. A weaker answer might wrongly conclude no crime
occurred because nothing was actually stolen, missing that burglary under s9(1)(a) is defined by intent
at entry, not by a completed theft.

## Worked example: distinguishing overlapping property offences

A single fact pattern can raise several property offences at once, and Section A's scenario question
typically expects candidates to identify and work through each one separately rather than picking a
single label. For example, a defendant who enters a shop as a trespasser, threatens a shopkeeper with a
weapon, and takes cash from the till could potentially be liable for burglary (trespassory entry with
intent to steal), robbery (theft with force or threat of force), and aggravated burglary if a weapon was
carried at the point of entry — each with its own actus reus and mens rea requiring separate
identification and application, not a single blended answer that only names "theft" in general terms.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish direct intention from indirect/oblique intention.
2. Which statutory section defines theft, and what are its key elements?
3. What distinguishes robbery from theft?
4. Name the three ways fraud can be committed under the Fraud Act 2006.
5. What does AO2 weight on Paper 2, and why is it heavier than Paper 1's?

**Answers:** 1. Direct = the result was the defendant's actual purpose. Indirect/oblique = the result was
a virtually certain, foreseen consequence. 2. s1 Theft Act 1968 — dishonest appropriation of property
belonging to another with intent to permanently deprive. 3. Robbery is theft accompanied by use or
threat of force, immediately before or at the time of the theft. 4. False representation (s2), failing
to disclose information (s3), abuse of position (s4). 5. 50% — heavier than Paper 1's 30% because
Section A's scenario-based format tests application of legal rules to facts using source material.
