## 2.1 Elements, compounds and mixtures

- An **element** is a substance made of only one type of atom. It cannot be broken down into anything simpler by a chemical reaction.
- A **compound** is a substance made of two or more elements **chemically combined** in a **fixed ratio**.
- A **mixture** is two or more substances that are **not chemically combined**. The proportions can vary.

| Feature | Element | Compound | Mixture |
|---|---|---|---|
| Types of atom | one | two or more, bonded together | can be any; substances not bonded to each other |
| Composition | fixed | fixed ratio (one formula) | variable |
| Properties | its own | different from the elements it is made from | each substance keeps its own properties |
| How to separate | cannot be split | only by a chemical reaction | by physical methods, e.g. filtration, distillation, magnet |

Two points cause confusion.

- **Molecules can be elements.** Oxygen gas is made of O₂ molecules. Each molecule has two atoms, but both are oxygen atoms, so oxygen is an element, not a compound.
- **"Pure" does not mean "element".** Water is a pure substance, but it is a compound, because each molecule contains hydrogen and oxygen atoms bonded together.

### Worked example 1: iron, sulfur and iron(II) sulfide

A student mixes iron filings with powdered sulfur, then heats some of the mixture strongly. A black solid, iron(II) sulfide (FeS), forms. Describe how the unheated mixture differs from the product.

```
Mixture: not bonded; any proportions; a magnet pulls the iron out (physical)
FeS:     Fe and S chemically bonded; fixed ratio 1 : 1; new properties;
         iron recovered only by a chemical reaction
```

### Worked example 2: classifying substances

Classify each substance as an element, a compound or a mixture: argon, carbon dioxide, air, sea water, nitrogen gas, sodium chloride.

```
elements:  argon; nitrogen gas (N2 molecules, but only N atoms)
compounds: carbon dioxide (CO2); sodium chloride (NaCl) - fixed ratios
mixtures:  air; sea water - substances not bonded, composition varies
```

For diagrams of separate atoms and molecules, identify each particle and the elements it contains. One kind of atom or one kind of elemental molecule represents a pure element; one kind of molecule containing different elements represents a pure molecular compound. Different uncombined substances represent a mixture. Ionic compounds need a different check: a pure sodium chloride crystal contains both Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions in one fixed-ratio lattice, so two ion species do not by themselves make a mixture. Check the substance and bonding pattern rather than counting particle kinds alone.

## 2.2 Atomic structure and the Periodic Table

### The structure of the atom

An atom has a tiny central **nucleus** containing **protons** and **neutrons**. **Electrons** are arranged in **shells** around the nucleus. Almost all the mass of the atom is in the nucleus.

| Particle | Where it is | Relative charge | Relative mass |
|---|---|---|---|
| proton | nucleus | +1 | 1 |
| neutron | nucleus | 0 | 1 |
| electron | shells | −1 | 1/1840 (negligible) |

The electron's mass is roughly 1/1840 of a proton's, which is why it is usually called negligible.

### Proton number and mass number

- **Proton number** (atomic number) = the number of protons in the nucleus of an atom.
- **Mass number** (nucleon number) = the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus of an atom.

Use the definitions word for word in an exam. "Number of protons" alone is not enough for mass number, and "number of particles" is too vague.

From these two numbers you can work out everything else:

```
protons   = proton number
neutrons  = mass number − proton number
electrons = proton number   (for a neutral atom)
electrons = proton number − charge   (for an ion)
```

### Worked example 3: counting particles in atoms and ions

Complete the table for an aluminium atom (proton number 13, mass number 27), an aluminium ion Al³⁺, a sulfide ion S²⁻ (proton number 16, mass number 32) and a calcium ion Ca²⁺ (proton number 20, mass number 40).

```
particle   protons   neutrons        electrons
Al         13        27 − 13 = 14    13
Al3+       13        14              13 − 3 = 10
S2−        16        32 − 16 = 16    16 + 2 = 18
Ca2+       20        40 − 20 = 20    20 − 2 = 18
```

Forming an ion changes **only** the number of electrons. The protons and neutrons stay the same, so Al³⁺ is still aluminium.

### Electronic configuration (proton number 1 to 20)

Electrons fill shells from the nucleus outwards. For proton numbers 1 to 20:

```
first shell    up to 2 electrons
second shell   up to 8 electrons
third shell    up to 8 electrons (for this syllabus range)
fourth shell   the remaining electrons (only K and Ca in this range)
```

Write the configuration as numbers separated by commas, one per shell, as the syllabus does: 2,8,3 for aluminium.

### Worked example 4: atoms

Write the electronic configurations of nitrogen (7), silicon (14), argon (18) and calcium (20).

```
N   7 electrons:  2 then 5         -> 2,5
Si  14 electrons: 2, 8, then 4     -> 2,8,4
Ar  18 electrons: 2, 8, then 8     -> 2,8,8
Ca  20 electrons: 2, 8, 8, then 2  -> 2,8,8,2
```

### Worked example 5: ions

Write the electronic configurations of N³⁻, F⁻ and S²⁻.

```
Step 1: find the number of electrons in the ion
  N3−   7 + 3 = 10
  F−    9 + 1 = 10
  S2−   16 + 2 = 18
Step 2: fill the shells with that number
  N3−   2,8
  F−    2,8
  S2−   2,8,8
```

Notice that N³⁻, F⁻ and Al³⁺ all end up as 2,8, the same as neon. S²⁻ and Ca²⁺ are 2,8,8, the same as argon. Why ions form is sub-topic 2.4.

### Group, period and the noble gases

The syllabus asks you to *state* three facts:

1. **Group VIII** noble gases have a **full outer electron shell** (He 2; Ne 2,8; Ar 2,8,8).
2. The number of **outer shell electrons** equals the **group number** in Groups I to VII.
3. The number of **occupied electron shells** equals the **period number**.

Fact 2 does not apply to Group VIII. Helium has only 2 outer electrons, yet it is in Group VIII because its only shell is full.

### Worked example 6: from configuration to position, and back

(a) An element has the configuration 2,8,5. State its group, its period and its proton number.

```
outer shell has 5 electrons   -> Group V
3 shells occupied             -> Period 3
total electrons 2 + 8 + 5 = 15 -> proton number 15 (phosphorus)
```

(b) An element is in Period 2 and Group VI. Write its configuration and identify it.

```
Period 2   -> 2 shells
Group VI   -> 6 outer electrons
configuration 2,6 ; total 8 electrons -> proton number 8 (oxygen)
```

Use the configuration of the **atom** for this, not of an ion. The configuration of O²⁻ (2,8) would wrongly suggest Group VIII.

## 2.3 Isotopes

### Definition

**Isotopes** are different atoms of the same element that have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons. Because the neutron number differs, the mass number differs too.

### Interpreting symbols for atoms and ions

In a symbol such as ¹⁸₈O, the **top** number is the mass number and the **bottom** number is the proton number. For an ion, the charge goes at the top right, as in ³⁵₁₇Cl⁻.

### Worked example 7: oxygen isotopes

Oxygen has three naturally occurring isotopes: ¹⁶₈O, ¹⁷₈O and ¹⁸₈O. Give the numbers of protons, neutrons and electrons in each atom.

```
isotope   protons   neutrons       electrons
16-O      8         16 − 8 = 8     8
17-O      8         17 − 8 = 9     8
18-O      8         18 − 8 = 10    8
```

### Worked example 8: are these isotopes?

Which of these pairs are isotopes of each other? (i) ⁴⁰₁₈Ar and ⁴⁰₂₀Ca (ii) ¹⁹₉F and ¹⁹₉F⁻ (iii) ¹⁶₈O and ¹⁸₈O.

```
(i)   proton numbers 18 and 20 -> different elements -> NOT isotopes
      (same mass number does not matter; neutrons are 22 and 20)
(ii)  same protons (9) and same neutrons (10); only electrons differ
      -> an atom and its ion, NOT isotopes
(iii) same protons (8), neutrons 8 and 10 -> isotopes
```

### Why isotopes have the same chemical properties (Extended only)

Isotopes of the same element have the **same number of electrons** and therefore the **same electronic configuration**. Chemical reactions depend on electrons, so isotopes react in the same way. A full answer needs both links: same number of electrons, so same configuration, so same chemical properties. Saying only "same number of protons" does not explain it.

### Calculating relative atomic mass (Extended only)

Relative atomic mass, Aᵣ, is an average of the masses of an element's isotopes, weighted by how common each one is. (Sub-topic 3.2 describes Aᵣ as the average mass of the isotopes compared with 1/12 of the mass of an atom of carbon-12; see [formulae, equations and the mole](/resources/formulae-equations-and-the-mole/).)

```
Ar = sum of (relative isotopic mass × abundance) ÷ total abundance
```

When abundances are percentages, the total is 100.

### Worked example 9: boron (Extended only)

Boron is about 20% boron-10 and 80% boron-11. Calculate its relative atomic mass.

```
Ar = (10 × 20 + 11 × 80) ÷ 100
   = (200 + 880) ÷ 100
   = 1080 ÷ 100
   = 10.8
```

The answer lies between 10 and 11 and closer to 11, because boron-11 is more common. That is a quick sense check. A plain average, (10 + 11) ÷ 2 = 10.5, ignores abundance and scores no credit.

### Worked example 10: three isotopes (Extended only)

Magnesium is about 79% magnesium-24, 10% magnesium-25 and 11% magnesium-26. Calculate Aᵣ to one decimal place.

```
check abundances: 79 + 10 + 11 = 100
Ar = (24 × 79 + 25 × 10 + 26 × 11) ÷ 100
   = (1896 + 250 + 286) ÷ 100
   = 2432 ÷ 100
   = 24.32
   = 24.3 (1 d.p.)
```

## Common errors

- Calling a substance made of molecules a compound automatically. O₂, N₂ and Cl₂ are elements.
- Giving the electron a relative mass of exactly 0. Its mass is very small, not zero: write 1/1840 or "negligible".
- Defining mass number as "the mass of the atom" or "the number of protons". It is the total number of protons and neutrons.
- Changing the proton number when writing an ion. Only the electrons change.
- Reading the group from an ion's configuration instead of the atom's.
- Treating ⁴⁰Ar and ⁴⁰Ca as isotopes because the mass numbers match.
- (Extended) Taking a simple average of isotope masses, or forgetting to divide by the total abundance.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between an element, a compound and a mixture in IGCSE Chemistry 0620?**
An element is made of only one type of atom. A compound contains two or more elements chemically combined in a fixed ratio, while a mixture contains substances that are not chemically combined and whose proportions can vary.

**Is oxygen gas an element or a compound?**
Oxygen gas is an element. It is made of O₂ molecules, but both atoms in each molecule are oxygen, so it contains only one type of atom.

**What is the relative mass of an electron?**
The electron's relative mass is very small but not zero: write 1/1840 or "negligible". Giving it as exactly 0 is a common error.

**Are argon-40 and calcium-40 isotopes?**
No. Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons. Argon-40 and calcium-40 have the same mass number but different proton numbers.

**Is calculating relative atomic mass Core or Extended in 0620?**
Calculating relative atomic mass from isotope abundances is Extended content, as is explaining why isotopes have the same chemical properties.
