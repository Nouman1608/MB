**This practice set covers topic 1.5, obsessive-compulsive disorder, in Specialist Option 1, Clinical Psychology, of Cambridge International A Level Psychology 9990 (syllabus for 2024 to 2026).** Section A uses Paper 3 style short answers and a structured essay. Section B uses Paper 4 style questions on the key study, measures and planning. All people in the scenarios are fictional.

## Section A: Paper 3 style questions

**1.** Outline what is meant by a *compulsion*, giving an example of a mental act. **[2]**

**2.** Explain the cognitive (thinking error) explanation of OCD, using the idea of inflated responsibility. **[3]**

**3.** Outline the psychodynamic explanation of OCD. **[3]**

**4.** Explain **one** weakness of the biochemical explanation of OCD. **[2]**

**5. (a)** Describe exposure and response prevention (ERP) as a treatment for OCD. **[6]**

**5. (b)** Evaluate treatments for OCD, including a comparison of biological and psychological approaches. **[10]**

## Section B: Paper 4 style questions

**6.** Priya checks that the gas hob is off up to 30 times before leaving home. She says that if a fire started, it would be entirely her fault.

**(a)** Identify **one** obsession and **one** compulsion in this description. **[2]**

**(b)** Identify the thinking error shown in Priya's statement. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why the Y-BOCS might be more useful than the MOCI for measuring change in Priya's symptoms during treatment. **[3]**

**7. (a)** Explain what is meant by a *non-inferiority* trial, using Lovell et al. (2006). **[2]**

**7. (b)** Outline **one** strength of the way patients were allocated to conditions in Lovell et al. **[2]**

**7. (c)** Suggest **one** reason why telephone CBT might be particularly useful for people with OCD. **[2]**

**8.** Explain **one** ethical issue raised by the use of children in research on OCD, such as Lehmkuhl et al. (2008), and how it can be addressed. **[3]**

**9.** A researcher wants to test whether ERP plus an SSRI reduces Y-BOCS scores more than ERP alone.

**(a)** Write a non-directional hypothesis for this study. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why the researcher should use a double-blind procedure with a placebo tablet in the ERP-alone group. **[3]**

**10.** Explain how cultural differences might affect the diagnosis of OCD. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Up to 2 marks. A repetitive behaviour or mental act the person feels driven to perform in response to an obsession or according to rigid rules, to reduce anxiety (1). Example of a mental act: silently counting to a particular number, or repeating a phrase or prayer (1).

**2.** Up to 3 marks. Most people have occasional intrusive thoughts, but people with OCD interpret them as dangerous and meaningful (1). Inflated responsibility is the belief that they are personally responsible for preventing harm, for example "if I don't check, any break-in will be my fault" (1). The compulsion is performed to prevent the harm and relieve this sense of responsibility, so the thinking error drives the behaviour (1).

**3.** Up to 3 marks. OCD arises from conflict between the id's unacceptable impulses and the ego's attempts to control them (1), linked to fixation in the anal stage, for example through very strict toilet training, leading to preoccupation with order and cleanliness (1). Compulsions act as defence mechanisms, such as undoing, which symbolically cancels an unacceptable thought, or reaction formation (1).

**4.** Up to 2 marks. For example: SSRIs help many patients only partly (1), which suggests serotonin is not the whole cause and other neurotransmitters or psychological factors are involved (1). Or: the fact that a drug reduces symptoms does not prove that low serotonin caused them.

**5. (a)** Up to 6 marks. Based on behavioural principles: compulsions are maintained by negative reinforcement (1). The therapist and patient build a hierarchy of triggers from least to most anxiety-provoking (1). The patient is exposed to each trigger, for example touching a door handle (1), and is prevented from performing the compulsion, such as washing (1). Anxiety rises and then falls without the ritual (habituation) (1). The patient moves up the hierarchy as anxiety reduces at each step, learning that the feared outcome does not happen (1).

**5. (b)** Level-based: 9–10 marks for a detailed comparison and a judgement. Indicative content:

- *SSRIs*: easy to take and need little effort; help many; but take several weeks, have side effects, and relapse is common when stopped.
- *ERP*: tackles the learned link directly, and benefits often last after therapy; but it is distressing at first, so some patients drop out.
- *CBT and access*: Lovell et al. (2006) showed telephone CBT is as effective as face-to-face, widening access.
- *Reductionism*: SSRIs treat a chemical; psychological therapies address behaviour and thought, which is more holistic.
- *Individual differences*: Lehmkuhl et al. shows ERP can be adapted for a child with autism; idiographic tailoring.
- *Conclusion*: psychological therapy, possibly combined with medication, gives the most lasting results.

**6.** **(a)** 1 mark each. Obsession: the intrusive fear that a fire will start because the hob is on (1). Compulsion: checking the hob up to 30 times before leaving (1).

**(b)** 1 mark: inflated responsibility.

**(c)** Up to 3 marks. The Y-BOCS rates severity on each item from 0 to 4, giving a total out of 40 (1), so small changes in time spent checking, distress or control can be detected (1). The MOCI uses true/false answers, so it may show the same result even when Priya's checking has reduced, making it less sensitive to change (1).

**7. (a)** Up to 2 marks. A trial that tests whether a new treatment is no worse than an existing one, rather than better (1). Lovell et al. tested whether telephone CBT was not inferior to face-to-face CBT on Y-BOCS scores (1).

**7. (b)** Up to 2 marks. Patients were randomly allocated to telephone or face-to-face treatment (1), so participant variables such as initial severity were likely to be spread evenly, and differences could be attributed to the mode of delivery (1).

**7. (c)** Up to 2 marks. For example: some people with OCD find it hard to leave home or travel because of their symptoms, such as contamination fears on public transport (1), so telephone therapy removes this barrier and makes treatment accessible (1).

**8.** Up to 3 marks. Children cannot give fully informed consent (1), so consent must be obtained from parents or guardians, with the child's own agreement in age-appropriate language (1). Exposure tasks may cause distress, so steps should be graded, the child should be able to stop at any time and a trained clinician should supervise (1).

**9.** **(a)** Up to 2 marks. For example: "There will be a difference in Y-BOCS scores after 12 weeks between patients who receive ERP plus an SSRI and patients who receive ERP plus a placebo." 1 for non-directional, 1 for operationalised variables.

**(b)** Up to 3 marks. The placebo means both groups take a tablet, so any difference cannot be due to expectations of taking medication (1). Double-blind means neither patients nor the clinicians rating the Y-BOCS know who has the SSRI (1), so the clinician-rated scores cannot be biased by the rater's expectations (1).

**10.** Up to 2 marks. The content of obsessions and what counts as normal ritual vary between cultures, for example religious cleansing or prayer rituals (1), so a clinician unfamiliar with the culture might mistake normal religious practice for compulsions, or miss symptoms expressed differently (1).

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need the Y-BOCS severity bands?**
No; knowing that it has ten items scored 0–4, with a maximum of 40, is enough to apply it.

**Is Lehmkuhl et al. (2008) a key study?**
No. It is a suggested example for ERP, so a brief description is enough; Lovell et al. (2006) is the key study.
