## Section A: Poetry and Prose Texts (30 marks, 45 minutes)

**1.** Choose a poem or prose extract you have studied from Part 2 of the Anthology. Explore how the
writer presents a moment of tension or conflict. In your answer you should consider the writer's use of
language and structure. **[30]**

## Section B: Imaginative Writing (30 marks, 45 minutes)

**2.** Choose **one** of the following titles and write an imaginative piece:
(a) "The Waiting Room"
(b) Write about a time someone had to make a difficult decision.
(c) "Too Late" **[30]**

---

## Model approach: Section A

A strong response to Question 1 identifies **two or three** specific moments in the chosen text where
tension or conflict is presented, rather than summarising the whole text's plot. For each moment, the
response should:

- Quote or closely reference the specific language used (imagery, word choice, sentence length, dialogue).
- Explain the **effect** that language choice has on the reader's sense of tension — not just that
  tension is present, but *how* the writer's craft creates it.
- Consider structure alongside language — for example, a shift in pace, a change in narrative viewpoint,
  or where a text places a moment of revelation.

**Example commentary structure (using a hypothetical prose extract):**
```
Point:      The writer builds tension through short, fragmented sentences
            as the character approaches a locked door.
Evidence:   "She reached the door. Stopped. Listened."
Effect:     The clipped syntax slows the pace of the description, mirroring
            the character's own hesitation, and the absence of any other
            detail isolates the reader's attention on this single moment,
            heightening suspense before anything is revealed.
```

This pattern — point, evidence, effect — repeated across two or three well-chosen moments, rather than a
broader but shallower sweep across the whole text, is what the higher mark bands for this question
reward.

## Model approach: Section B

For title (a) "The Waiting Room," a strong response plans before writing: establishing a clear setting,
a distinct narrative voice, and — critically — a planned structure with a deliberate ending, rather than
writing freely and hoping for a natural stopping point. A brief plan might look like:

```
Opening:   establish setting and narrator's state of mind through
           sensory detail (sound, light, smell of the waiting room)
Middle:    build tension through what the narrator notices about
           other people waiting, hinting at why they are all there
           without stating it directly
Turn:      a small event shifts the mood -- a name called, a door
           opening, a phone ringing
Ending:    a deliberate final image or line that resolves or
           deliberately leaves the tension unresolved, rather than
           the writing simply running out
```

Examiners specifically credit adapting form, tone and register for the chosen purpose (AO4) and accurate,
varied sentence structures with a wide vocabulary (AO5) — a response that plans its structure in advance,
as shown above, is far more likely to demonstrate both consistently than one written without a plan.

## Exam technique for this component

Because Section A's question is set on material from the Anthology's Part 2, made available in the exam
itself, systematic familiarity with all of Part 2 — not just a favourite poem or story — is what allows a
candidate to answer confidently regardless of which specific text the question names. Practise the
point-evidence-effect structure demonstrated above on several different Anthology texts so it becomes an
automatic habit rather than something reconstructed from scratch under exam pressure. For Section B,
resist the temptation to start writing immediately: even two or three minutes spent sketching an opening,
a turning point and a planned ending produces noticeably stronger, better-paced writing than a piece
that begins strongly but drifts or is cut off unfinished when time runs out.

## Balancing the two sections' distinct skills

Because Section A and Section B assess genuinely different skills — analytical reading versus
imaginative writing — treating revision for the two as separate but equally weighted priorities matters
more than it might first appear. A candidate strong in analysis but weaker in creative writing (or the
reverse) can be tempted to over-invest revision time in their stronger section, but since both carry
equal marks, balanced preparation across both consistently produces a better overall result than
excelling in one section while under-preparing the other. Building a revision timetable that allocates
genuinely equal time to Anthology analysis practice and imaginative writing planning practice, rather
than defaulting to whichever feels more comfortable, is worth doing deliberately rather than by instinct.

## A quick final check before submitting either answer

Before moving on, reread the opening line of the question itself: for Section A, has the response
actually engaged with tension or conflict specifically, rather than drifting into general description of
character or setting; for Section B, does the finished piece genuinely fulfil the chosen title, rather
than a piece that could just as easily have answered a different title on the list. This last check takes
under a minute but catches drift that can otherwise cost marks even in an otherwise well-written answer.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Summarising a Section A text's plot instead of analysing specific language and structural choices.
- Naming a technique without explaining its effect on the reader, in either section.
- Starting Section B's writing without a plan, producing a piece that drifts or has no deliberate ending.
- Running out of time on Section B because Section A was allowed to overrun its 45-minute allocation.
- Choosing an imaginative writing title based on which sounds most impressive rather than which the candidate can genuinely sustain for a full, well-structured 30-mark response.
