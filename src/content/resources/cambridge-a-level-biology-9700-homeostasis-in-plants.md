This guide covers section **14.2 Homeostasis in plants** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Biology 9700 syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027. It is A Level content, examined in Paper 4, and its data often appear in Paper 5 contexts. The [Homeostasis study guide](/resources/a-level-biology-9700-homeostasis/) sets out the basic opening and closing sequence; this page goes further, showing how to reason about the trade-off, interpret rhythm and hormone data, and write the water potential argument precisely. ## What 14.2 asks you to do

| Outcome | In short |
|---|---|
| 14.2.1 | Explain that stomata open and close in response to environmental conditions, balancing CO₂ uptake by diffusion against water loss by transpiration |
| 14.2.2 | Explain that stomata have daily rhythms of opening and closing |
| 14.2.3 | Describe the structure and function of guard cells and explain how they open and close stomata |
| 14.2.4 | Describe the role of abscisic acid in closing stomata during water stress, including calcium ions as a second messenger |

## The trade-off behind every stomatal response

A stoma is a single pore that serves two opposing needs. CO₂ diffuses **in** through it for photosynthesis; water vapour diffuses **out** through it in transpiration. A plant cannot have one without the other, so stomatal aperture is a compromise. The useful way to reason about any condition is to ask two questions: *is CO₂ needed now?* and *can the plant afford to lose water now?*

| Condition | CO₂ needed? | Water loss affordable? | Typical response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bright light, plenty of water in soil | Yes | Yes | Open |
| Darkness | No (no photosynthesis) | Water would be lost for no gain | Close |
| Low CO₂ concentration inside the leaf | Yes, strongly | Usually | Open wider |
| High CO₂ concentration inside the leaf | Less | Usually | Close partly |
| Soil dry, leaf losing turgor | Yes | No | Close (abscisic acid) |

This is homeostasis because stomatal control helps keep the **water content of the plant's tissues** within limits while still allowing photosynthesis.

## Daily rhythms

Stomata open during the day and close at night, a cycle of about 24 hours. Two observations show that this rhythm is not simply a response to light:

- Stomata often begin to open **before** dawn, so they are ready for photosynthesis as soon as light arrives.
- If a plant is moved into **constant light** (or constant darkness), the opening and closing cycle continues for some days with a period close to 24 hours.

Both point to an **internal timing mechanism** in the plant. Light and dark still matter, because they keep the rhythm in step with the real day.

### Worked example 1: interpreting a rhythm

Illustrative data: stomatal aperture of a plant kept for two days in **constant light** after normal days and nights.

| Time of day | 06:00 | 12:00 | 18:00 | 00:00 | 06:00 | 12:00 | 18:00 | 00:00 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Aperture / µm | 4.0 | 9.5 | 5.5 | 1.5 | 3.5 | 8.5 | 5.0 | 2.0 |

*Describe and explain the results.* Aperture still peaks around midday (9.5 µm, then 8.5 µm) and is smallest around midnight (1.5 µm, then 2.0 µm), even though light is constant. So the opening and closing follows an **internal rhythm** of about 24 hours, not the light itself. The peaks become slightly smaller on day 2, which suggests the rhythm is weakening without the normal light–dark signal.

## Guard cell structure linked to function

- Guard cells occur in **pairs**, one on each side of the pore.
- The cell wall next to the pore is **thicker and less elastic** than the outer wall.
- **Cellulose microfibrils** in the walls run **radially** (like hoops), so the cells can lengthen but cannot easily get wider.
- Guard cells have **chloroplasts** and many **mitochondria**, supplying ATP for active transport.
- Their cell surface membranes contain **proton pumps** and **channel proteins** for K⁺ and anions.

When water enters, the thin outer wall stretches more than the thick inner wall, and the radial microfibrils stop the cells swelling sideways. Both cells therefore **bow outwards**, pulling apart at the middle, and the pore opens.

## Opening: the water potential argument in full

1. **Proton pumps** in the guard cell membrane actively transport H⁺ **out** of the cell, using ATP.
2. The inside of the cell becomes more negative, which opens **K⁺ channels**; K⁺ ions diffuse **into** the guard cells.
3. Negative ions also accumulate inside, keeping the charges balanced.
4. The increased solute concentration **lowers the water potential** of the guard cells below that of the surrounding epidermal cells.
5. Water moves **into** the guard cells **by osmosis**.
6. The guard cells become **turgid** and bow apart: the stoma **opens**.

### Worked example 2: direction of osmosis

Before opening, a guard cell has water potential −800 kPa and the neighbouring epidermal cell −1000 kPa. After K⁺ uptake, the guard cell is −1600 kPa.

- Before: water tends to move **from** the guard cell (−800 kPa, higher) **to** the epidermal cell (−1000 kPa, lower). The guard cell is not gaining water.
- After: the guard cell (−1600 kPa) now has the **lower** water potential, so water moves **into** it by osmosis and it becomes turgid.

Always state which cell has the lower (more negative) water potential before naming the direction.

## Closing in water stress: abscisic acid

When the plant is short of water, **abscisic acid (ABA)** is produced and reaches the guard cells.

1. ABA **binds to receptors** on the guard cell surface membrane.
2. This causes **calcium ions to enter the cytoplasm**, from outside the cell and from internal stores such as the vacuole.
3. Ca²⁺ acts as a **second messenger**: it relays the signal from the receptor to the proteins that carry out the response.
4. Ca²⁺ causes channels to open that let **negative ions and K⁺ leave** the cell, and the **proton pumps are inhibited**, so no more K⁺ is drawn in.
5. Losing ions **raises the water potential** of the guard cells above that of neighbouring cells.
6. Water leaves **by osmosis**; the guard cells become **flaccid** and the stoma **closes**, reducing transpiration.

Compare this with glucagon in section 14.1: both hormones bind to a surface receptor and use a **second messenger** inside the cell (Ca²⁺ here, cAMP for glucagon).

### Worked example 3: testing the role of calcium

Illustrative data: mean stomatal aperture of leaf strips after 30 minutes.

| Treatment | Aperture / µm |
|---|---|
| Buffer only (control) | 8.0 |
| ABA | 2.4 |
| ABA + a drug that blocks calcium channels | 6.8 |

Percentage decrease caused by ABA = (8.0 − 2.4) ÷ 8.0 × 100 = **70 %**. With the calcium blocker, the decrease is (8.0 − 6.8) ÷ 8.0 × 100 = **15 %**. Blocking Ca²⁺ entry largely prevents ABA from closing the stomata, which supports the idea that Ca²⁺ is needed as a second messenger. The small remaining effect could come from calcium released from internal stores, which the drug may not block.

## Common mistakes

- Saying stomata close at night "to stop photosynthesis". They close because **no CO₂ is needed** in the dark, so water would be lost for nothing.
- Writing that water enters guard cells by active transport. **Ions** are moved actively or by facilitated diffusion; **water** moves by osmosis.
- Writing that K⁺ is pumped out to open stomata. H⁺ is pumped out; K⁺ moves **in**.
- Calling ABA a second messenger. ABA is the **hormone**; **Ca²⁺** is the second messenger.
- Explaining the bowing of guard cells without mentioning the **uneven wall thickness** or microfibril arrangement.
- Describing the rhythm only as "open in the day". The syllabus point is that it is a **rhythm**, persisting without light changes.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** (14.2.1–3): give both sides of the trade-off, or each link from ion movement to water potential to osmosis to turgor to shape.
- **Describe** (guard cell structure, ABA): list the features or steps in order, using terms such as radial microfibrils, proton pump and second messenger.
- **Suggest** (unfamiliar data): use the trade-off table; for example, a plant in hot, dry wind is likely to close its stomata even in bright light.
- With numerical aperture data, quote values and calculate percentage changes from the control.
