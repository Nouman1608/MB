Condensed notes for **1.3 Size of business** in Cambridge International AS & A Level Business 9609 (2026–2028). For full explanations and worked examples, use the [Size of business study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-size-of-business/); to test yourself, use the [Size of business practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-size-of-business-practice/). All businesses and figures are invented; sums are in dollars.

## What 1.3 asks you to handle

- **1.3.1**: how appropriate each method of measuring size is.
- **1.3.2**: advantages and disadvantages of being small; family businesses; the importance and role of small businesses.
- **1.3.3**: organic growth; horizontal, vertical and conglomerate growth by merger and takeover; friendly mergers and hostile takeovers; stakeholder impact; why deals succeed or fail; joint ventures and strategic alliances.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Capital employed | Long-term finance invested in the business |
| Market share | Business's sales ÷ total market sales × 100 |
| Market capitalisation | Share price × number of shares (public limited companies only) |
| Family business | A business owned, and often managed, by members of one family |
| Organic (internal) growth | Expansion using the business's own resources |
| Merger | Two businesses agree to combine into one |
| Takeover | One business buys a controlling interest in another |
| Hostile takeover | A takeover opposed by the target's board |
| Horizontal integration | Joining a firm in the same industry at the same stage of production |
| Vertical integration | Joining a firm at a different stage: backward (towards suppliers) or forward (towards customers) |
| Conglomerate diversification | Joining a firm in an unrelated industry |
| Synergy | Gains from combining, such as cost savings, so the whole is worth more than the parts |
| Joint venture | Two or more firms create and jointly own a separate business or project |
| Strategic alliance | Firms agree to cooperate while staying independent |

## Measures of size: when each fits

| Measure | Fits | Misleads when |
|---|---|---|
| Employees | Management and employment questions | Firms are capital-intensive or use part-time staff |
| Revenue | Comparing sales activity | Margins differ; says nothing about profit |
| Capital employed | Asset-heavy firms | Assets valued differently |
| Market share | Competitive position | Market definitions differ |
| Market capitalisation | Comparing plcs | Share prices swing; private firms excluded |

**Worked reminder.** A firm with $4.5m sales in a $30m market has a market share of 4.5 ÷ 30 × 100 = **15%**.

## Small businesses

**Advantages:** customer contact, quick decisions, flexibility, niche focus, low overheads.
**Disadvantages:** limited finance, few economies of scale, owner overload, dependence on few customers.

**Family businesses** — strengths: commitment, trust, long-term outlook, reputation. Weaknesses: limited management talent, disputes, succession, resistance to outside finance.

**Importance:** jobs, innovation, competition, supply to large firms, a route into enterprise.
**Industrial structure:** dominant where demand is local or personal and scale economies are small; survive alongside giants by specialising or supplying them.

## Growth

**Organic:** keeps control; lower risk; slow. Years to fund from profit = cost ÷ annual retained profit.

| External type | Motive |
|---|---|
| Horizontal | Market share, economies of scale, fewer rivals |
| Vertical backward | Secure supplies, control input quality and cost |
| Vertical forward | Control distribution, reach customers |
| Conglomerate | Spread risk |

**Merger market share:** add the shares if sales hold. 14% + 9% = **23%**.

**Premium and payback.** Target valued at $8m, bid $10m: premium $2m, or 2 ÷ 8 × 100 = **25%**. Savings of $0.5m a year recover the premium in 2 ÷ 0.5 = **4 years**.

## Why deals succeed or fail

| Succeed | Fail |
|---|---|
| Real cost savings | Overpaying |
| Complementary products or skills | Culture clash |
| Fast access to new markets | Key staff leave |
| Careful integration | Diseconomies of scale; customers switch |

## Stakeholders in a takeover

Target shareholders gain the premium; bidder shareholders gain only if synergies arrive; employees face job cuts or new opportunities; customers may get more choice or less competition; suppliers face a stronger buyer; communities gain or lose local jobs.

## Joint ventures and alliances

Cheaper and less risky than a takeover, easier to end, useful for entering foreign markets. Risks: disputes over control and profit; a partner may become a competitor.

## Exam traps

- One measure treated as the truth.
- Market share read as absolute size.
- "Small means weak."
- Naming a growth type without its motive.
- Assuming a takeover's planned savings will arrive.
- Forgetting the impact on a specific stakeholder named in the case.

## Self-test

1. Why can number of employees understate a software firm's size?
2. A firm's sales are $2m in a market worth $25m. Calculate its market share.
3. Give one limitation of market share as a measure of size.
4. State two advantages of being a small business.
5. Give one strength and one weakness of family businesses.
6. Explain one role small firms play in an industry dominated by large firms.
7. A shop costing $90 000 is funded from retained profit of $30 000 a year. How long does it take?
8. A car maker buys a tyre manufacturer. Which type of integration is this?
9. Distinguish a friendly merger from a hostile takeover.
10. A target worth $20m is bought for $26m. Calculate the premium as a percentage.
11. Give two reasons a takeover may fail to meet its objectives.
12. Why might a firm entering a foreign market choose a joint venture?

### Answers

1. Software firms rely on a few highly skilled staff and generate high revenue per employee.
2. 2 ÷ 25 × 100 = **8%**.
3. It depends on how the market is defined, so it cannot compare firms in different markets.
4. Any two: personal service; quick decisions; flexibility; niche focus; low overheads.
5. Strength: trust or long-term outlook. Weakness: succession problems or family disputes.
6. They may specialise in supplying components or services to the large firms, or serve niches the large firms ignore.
7. 90 000 ÷ 30 000 = **3 years**.
8. Vertical backward integration.
9. A friendly merger is agreed by both boards; a hostile takeover is opposed by the target's board, and the bidder appeals directly to shareholders.
10. Premium $6m; 6 ÷ 20 × 100 = **30%**.
11. Any two: overpaying; culture clash; loss of key staff; diseconomies of scale; customers switch.
12. To gain a local partner's market knowledge and contacts while sharing the cost and risk.
