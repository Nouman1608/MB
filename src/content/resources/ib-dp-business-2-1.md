## The role of HRM: think in terms of objectives

Examiners reward answers that connect HR work to what the business is trying to achieve. A useful habit is to ask, for any HR activity, "which objective does this serve, and what happens if it is done badly?"

| Business objective | What HRM contributes | Cost of getting it wrong |
|---|---|---|
| Growth into new sites | Forecasting and recruiting enough people with the right skills in time | Sites open understaffed; service suffers |
| Cutting costs | Matching staff numbers to demand; flexible contracts | Over-cutting leaves gaps and overloads remaining staff |
| Better quality or innovation | Training, development and reward systems that encourage skill | Skills fall behind rivals |
| Strong reputation as an employer | Fair treatment, welfare and consultation | Recruitment becomes harder and more expensive |

HRM therefore has two sides. Labour is a cost that must be controlled, and it is also the source of the skills and effort the business relies on. Strong answers recognise both sides when a case involves job cuts or pay.

## Human resource planning as a process

The guide asks about the factors that influence human resource planning, so it helps to know what the planning itself involves. A standard sequence is:

1. **Workforce audit.** Record the current workforce: numbers, skills, ages, contracts and locations.
2. **Forecast demand for labour.** Use the business plan to estimate how many people, with which skills, will be needed.
3. **Forecast supply of labour.** Estimate how many current staff will remain (after retirements and leavers) and what the outside labour market can supply.
4. **Identify the gap.** Compare demand with supply, skill by skill.
5. **Act and review.** Recruit, train, redeploy or reduce staff, then check the plan against what happens.

### Worked example: Tarnwell Veterinary Group

Tarnwell Veterinary Group is an invented chain of animal clinics, and every figure here is invented. It employs 48 vets across 8 clinics and plans to open 2 more clinics within three years. Each clinic needs 6 vets.

* Demand in three years: 10 clinics × 6 vets = **60 vets**.
* The audit shows 9 vets aged 60 or over. Managers expect all 9 to retire within three years, and they expect about 3 other vets to leave each year.
* Supply from the current workforce: 48 − 9 − (3 × 3) = **30 vets**.
* Gap: 60 − 30 = **30 vets** to recruit or train over three years, not just the 12 that the new clinics alone suggest.

Descriptive statistics, which the guide suggests as a tool for this unit, sharpen the audit. If 9 of 48 vets are 60 or over, that is 18.75% of the workforce close to retirement. A plan that looked only at new clinics would underestimate the recruitment need by more than half.

## Factors that influence human resource planning

The internal and external factors are listed in the core guide. Here the skill is analysis: each factor changes either the **demand** for labour or the **supply** of labour, and sometimes both.

| Factor | Type | Effect on the plan | Likely HR response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Expansion objective | Internal | Raises demand for labour | Recruitment campaign; internal promotion |
| Ageing workforce | Internal | Cuts future supply of experienced staff | Succession planning; mentoring of younger staff |
| Training budget | Internal | Limits how fast supply can be built from within | More external hiring of trained staff |
| Flexi-time offered | Internal | Widens supply, because it attracts carers and parents | Rotas must still cover busy periods |
| Demographic change | External | Fewer young people entering the local labour market | Recruit older workers; automate some tasks |
| Higher labour mobility | External | Easier to attract staff, but also easier to lose them | Retention schemes; competitive pay |
| Immigration | External | Can fill skill shortages | Depends on rules the business cannot control |
| Gig economy | External | Flexible supply for short-term or peak work | Use for peaks; keep core skills permanent |

The same factor can push both ways. Higher labour mobility helps Tarnwell attract vets from other regions, but it also lets rivals tempt Tarnwell's vets away. Saying this shows analysis rather than description.

## Diagnosing resistance to change

Many answers list reasons for resistance without asking which one applies. Classify the cause first, because the cause decides the cure.

| Cause of resistance | Signs in a case study |
|---|---|
| Self-interest: fear of losing pay, status or the job | Supervisors whose roles are merged; staff facing new shifts |
| Misunderstanding or low trust | Change announced without reasons; poor history of management promises |
| Lack of skills or confidence | New software or equipment; older staff unsure of retraining |
| Genuine disagreement | Experienced staff believe the change will harm customers or quality |
| Disruption to routine and social groups | Teams split up or moved to another site |

Note that genuine disagreement is not irrational. Staff who know the work may have spotted a real weakness in the plan, and listening to them can improve the change itself.

## Matching HR strategies to the cause

This is the AO3 content point. A strategy works best when it removes the cause of resistance. Training does little for a supervisor who fears losing status, and a pay incentive does little for staff who simply do not understand why the change is happening.

| Cause | Best-fitting strategy | Why it fits |
|---|---|---|
| Fear of job loss | Job security guarantees, redeployment, voluntary redundancy | Removes or softens the threat |
| Misunderstanding | Early communication of reasons and timescale | Replaces rumour with facts |
| Skills gap | Training before the change goes live | Removes the source of anxiety |
| Disagreement | Consultation and participation in the design | Uses staff knowledge and builds ownership |
| Disrupted routine | Phased introduction or a pilot | Gives time to adjust |

### Annotated model answer

*Question: Tarnwell plans to introduce a new booking and records system that changes how every vet works. Recommend an HR strategy to reduce resistance.*

> Tarnwell's vets are likely to resist mainly because of a skills gap, since 18.75% are near retirement and may lack confidence with new software. **[applies the case]** Training before launch fits this cause best, because it removes the anxiety rather than only rewarding compliance. **[links strategy to cause]** A pilot in one clinic would also let problems be fixed before the system spreads to all 8. **[second strategy, with reason]** However, training takes vets away from appointments, which costs revenue, and older vets close to retirement may see little point in learning a new system. **[limitation]** On balance, I recommend training combined with a single-clinic pilot, because the change affects core daily work and errors in patient records would be costly. If the system had to launch within weeks, clear communication and on-site support would be the more realistic choice. **[judgement with condition]**

## Force field analysis (HL tool)

The guide suggests force field analysis, a technique associated with Kurt Lewin, as an HL tool for this unit. Driving forces and restraining forces are scored, often from 1 (weak) to 5 (strong). For Tarnwell's new system, invented scores might be: driving forces of faster bookings (4), fewer record errors (4) and rival clinics already using such systems (3), total 11; restraining forces of training time (3), fear among older vets (4) and cost (2), total 9. Driving forces lead by 2, so the change can proceed, but reducing the strongest restraint (fear, scored 4) through training would make it far more secure than adding more pressure.

## Common mistakes

* Describing HR roles in general without saying which business objective they support.
* Treating HR planning as recruitment only. Supply from the current workforce matters as much as demand.
* Listing factors without saying whether they change demand for labour or supply of labour.
* Recommending one strategy for every type of resistance.
* Ending an AO3 answer without a judgement and the condition it depends on.

## Exam technique for 2.1 command terms

* **Explain** (AO2): give the factor or reason and its consequence for the business in the case.
* **Analyse** (AO2): break the issue into parts, such as demand and supply effects, and show how they connect.
* **Evaluate**, **discuss** or **recommend** (AO3): weigh at least two strategies, give strengths and limits in context, and finish with a justified choice.
