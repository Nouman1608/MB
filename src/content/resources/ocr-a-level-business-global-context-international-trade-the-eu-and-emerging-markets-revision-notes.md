## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Globalisation | growing integration of economies, markets and businesses across borders |
| Global strategy | one firm's deliberate plan to operate in world markets |
| Multinational | a business operating in more than one country |
| Global brand | a brand recognised and sold in many countries |
| International trade | buying and selling goods and services between countries |
| Barrier to trade | anything that makes imports harder or dearer to sell |
| Exchange rate | the cost of a unit of one currency in another currency |
| Free trade | trade without tariffs, quotas or other barriers |
| Trading bloc | countries agreeing to reduce trade barriers between themselves |
| Emerging market | a fast-growing, industrialising economy with rising incomes, not yet fully developed |

## Globalisation in one page

**Facilitators:** multinationals; transport infrastructure; trade liberalisation; e-commerce; Internet and communication technologies.

**Why firms are affected differently:** depends on imported inputs, overseas sales, overseas rivals, product tradability, customer price sensitivity and the firm's resources.

**Multinationals:** move capital and technology, locate production in low-cost places, spread global brands, link supply chains.

- For: investment, jobs, tax, choice.
- Against: crowding out local firms, mobility, bargaining power over suppliers.

**Opportunities and threats**

| Opportunities | Threats |
|---|---|
| larger markets | overseas competitors |
| cheaper inputs | exchange rate exposure |
| economies of scale | supply chain disruption |
| risk spread across countries | pressure on prices |

**Recommend and justify.** Method in steps:

1. State your decision in one sentence.
2. Give two reasons using the case facts.
3. Weigh the best alternative and say why it is weaker.
4. Mention a condition that would change your view.

## Global brands

- **Opportunities from rising global brands:** trust and scale to lean on, licensing or supply deals, a "local alternative" position.
- **Threats:** price cuts through scale, heavy promotion, loss of shelf space and loyalty.
- **Judgement:** depends on whether the firm competes head-on or in a niche.

## International trade

**Reasons:** missing resources; specialisation; economies of scale; choice; growth when the home market is saturated; spreading risk.

**Link to globalisation:** more globalisation raises trade; more trade deepens globalisation.

**Barriers**

| Barrier | Nature | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Tariff | tax on imports | revenue, dearer imports |
| Quota | limit on quantity | protect local producers |
| Embargo | ban | political or security aims |
| Subsidy | payment to local firms | undercut imports |
| Rules, paperwork, standards | administrative | protect consumers or slow imports |

**Factors when trading abroad**

| Factor | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| Language | what must be translated? |
| Culture and customs | what is acceptable or expected? |
| Logistics | how will goods travel, how long, at what cost? |
| Currency | in which currency is the price set? |
| Buying habits | how do customers shop and what do they pay? |

**Support for traders:** financial (export credit insurance or guarantees, bank trade finance, grants where available) and non-financial (government advice and market information, trade fairs and missions, chambers of commerce and trade associations, agents and distributors).

## Exchange rates

Rate form used here: foreign units per £1.

The conversion rule and the barrier list are standard background, not specification wording; so are the EU and euro descriptions and UK Export Finance.

- Home to foreign: **multiply**.
- Foreign to home: **divide**.
- Pound **appreciates**: imports cheaper in pounds, exports dearer abroad.
- Pound **depreciates**: imports dearer, exports cheaper abroad.

**Worked reminder (Sandringle Cycles).** Sandringle, a UK maker, buys tyres invoiced at USD 3,000, with a rate of £1 = USD 1.25.

1. Cost in pounds: 3,000 ÷ 1.25 = £2,400.
2. The pound falls to £1 = USD 1.10: 3,000 ÷ 1.10 = £2,727.27.
3. Extra cost: £2,727.27 − £2,400 = £327.27.
4. A frame priced at £480 sells to a US buyer at 480 × 1.25 = USD 600. At 1.10 the same frame costs 480 × 1.10 = USD 528, so it is cheaper for the buyer.

**Evaluating a change in rate:**

- Who gains and loses: exporters, importers, shareholders, employees, customers, suppliers.
- Size of the change and how long it lasts.
- Existing contracts or fixed prices that delay the effect.
- Whether costs and sales are in the same currency.

## Free trade and trading blocs

| | Advantages to a business | Disadvantages to a business |
|---|---|---|
| Free trade | bigger markets, cheaper inputs, efficiency, scale | stronger rivals, dependence on foreign suppliers, job losses elsewhere in the economy |
| Trading bloc | tariff-free access to members, common rules, bigger market | more competition, rules set by the bloc, barriers to non-members, less national flexibility |

## The EU

- **Single European Market:** goods, services, capital and people move largely freely among members.
- **Enlargement:** more customers, suppliers and workers under common rules, but also more competition.
- **Single currency (euro, Eurozone):** no currency risk or conversion cost between members; one central interest rate for all members.
- **UK outside the EU and the Eurozone:** own trade deals, own interest rate, own regulation, but extra checks and paperwork, possible tariffs, exchange rate exposure and less influence over EU rules.
- **UK outside the Single European Market:** freedom over product and employment rules, but loss of frictionless access and possible extra certification or local operations.
- **Expand into Europe and/or global markets?** Europe is near and large but now involves more paperwork. Global markets may grow faster but bring distance, culture and currency risk.

## Emerging markets and the global environment

- Features: fast growth, rising incomes, growing middle class, still developing institutions and infrastructure.
- Impact on a business: new customers and cheaper production sites, but also new rivals, price pressure and risk.
- Changes in the global environment: exchange rates, trade policy, technology, political events, regulation, tastes, competitors.
- Responses: diversify markets and suppliers, protect against currency risk, adapt products, invest in innovation, or stay local and differentiate. Justify against alternatives and cost.

## Evaluation prompts

Use these to turn a description into an evaluation of opportunities and threats:

- **It depends on size:** a small exporter has fewer resources to absorb a currency swing than a large multinational.
- **It depends on time:** a currency fall may help exporters in the short run, but higher import costs can erode the gain later.
- **It depends on the product:** a distinctive product can carry a higher price abroad; a standard product competes on price.
- **It depends on the stakeholder:** shareholders, employees, customers and suppliers rarely gain or lose equally.
- **Judgement:** finish with the factor that matters most for this business and say why.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Globalisation vs global strategy:** one is an environmental trend; the other is a choice by a firm.
- **Tariff vs quota:** a tax on imports versus a limit on quantity.
- **Free trade vs trading bloc:** no barriers anywhere versus reduced barriers among members.
- **Appreciation vs depreciation:** pound buys more versus pound buys less.
- **Single market vs single currency:** free movement of goods, services, capital and people versus a shared currency used by some members.
- **Financial vs non-financial support:** money or insurance versus advice and contacts.

## Quick self-test

1. State two facilitators of globalisation.
2. What is the difference between globalisation and a global strategy?
3. Convert £250 into euros at £1 = €1.18.
4. Convert €590 into pounds at £1 = €1.18.
5. Convert 11,400 yen into pounds at £1 = 190 yen.
6. Does a falling pound help or hurt an importer, and why?
7. Give two purposes of a tariff.
8. State one financial and one non-financial source of support for exporters.
9. Give one advantage and one disadvantage for a UK business, from the UK being outside the Eurozone.
10. The pound rises from £1 = €1.20 to €1.32. What is the percentage rise in the rate?

### Answers

1. Any two of: the Internet or communication technologies; e-commerce; trade liberalisation; transport infrastructure; multinationals.
2. Globalisation is a trend in the environment; a global strategy is one firm's plan to operate across world markets.
3. 250 × 1.18 = **€295**.
4. 590 ÷ 1.18 = **£500**.
5. 11,400 ÷ 190 = **£60**.
6. It **hurts**, because each unit of foreign currency costs more pounds, so import costs rise.
7. Raise government revenue; make imports dearer than local goods; protect local producers.
8. Financial: export credit insurance. Non-financial: trade fair or market information (other answers are valid).
9. Advantage: it sets its own interest rate. Disadvantage: exchange rate risk when selling in euros.
10. (1.32 − 1.20) ÷ 1.20 × 100 = **10%**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving a definition and stopping, with no application to the business in the case.
- Listing language, culture, logistics, currency and buying habits without linking one to the product.
- Multiplying when the conversion needs dividing, or the reverse.
- Saying "exchange rates affect trade" without a direction, a cost or a price effect.
- Calling every change in the pound good or bad for all firms, instead of separating importers from exporters.
- Mixing up the EU, the Eurozone and the Single European Market.
- Describing emerging markets only as an opportunity and ignoring risk.
- Ending a "recommend and justify" answer without a decision or a reason why the alternative is weaker.
