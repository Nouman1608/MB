## Section A

**1.** Define the term tourism balance. **[2]**

**2.** Outline what is meant by enclave tourism. **[2]**

**3.** Explain why cruise tourism often brings little spending to port cities. **[2]**

**4.** Distinguish between relocation diffusion and hierarchical diffusion of a sport. **[2]**

**5.** Outline what is meant by glocalisation in international sport. **[2]**

**6.** Distinguish between the hard legacy and the soft legacy of an international sporting event. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** The table shows invented tourism data, in billions of currency units.

| | Country X | Country Y |
|---|---|---|
| Tourism receipts | 1.8 | 45 |
| Residents' spending abroad | 0.3 | 52 |
| GDP | 4.5 | 1,500 |
| Total exports | 2.4 | 300 |

**(a)** Calculate the tourism balance for each country. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate tourism receipts as a percentage of total exports for each country. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain which country is more vulnerable to a fall in international arrivals. **[2]**

**8.** Two invented islands each receive 500,000 visitors a year. On Island P most stay in foreign-owned all-inclusive resorts. On Island Q most stay in locally owned guesthouses.

**(a)** Suggest why Island Q is likely to retain more tourist spending. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest one advantage Island P may have over Island Q. **[2]**

**9.** Explain how the concept of the pleasure periphery can be used to describe international tourist flows, and outline one limitation of the concept. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how broadcasting has changed the international geography of a sport you have studied. **[4]**

## Section C

**11.** Evaluate the legacy of one or more international sporting events for host cities. **[10]**

**12.** "International tourism increases inequality more than it reduces it." Discuss this statement. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** The difference between tourism receipts (spending by international visitors in a country) and tourism expenditure (spending by the country's residents abroad) [1]; a positive balance means tourism earns more foreign currency than residents spend abroad [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Tourism in self-contained resorts that provide accommodation, food, entertainment and excursions on one site [1], so visitors spend most of their money inside the enclave and little in the surrounding area [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Ships provide their own accommodation, food and entertainment, which passengers have usually paid for in advance [1], and passengers spend only a few hours ashore, so spending per visitor in the port is low [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Relocation diffusion occurs when people migrate and take a sport with them to a new area [1]; hierarchical diffusion occurs when a sport spreads first to major cities or elite institutions and then down to smaller places [1]. **[2]**

**5.** A global sports product, such as a league or club brand, is adapted to local markets [1], for example by scheduling matches or tours to suit audiences in distant time zones [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Hard legacy is the physical inheritance, such as venues, housing and transport [1]; soft legacy is intangible, such as national image, skills, volunteering or changes in sport participation [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Country X: 1.8 - 0.3 = **+1.5 billion** [1]. Country Y: 45 - 52 = **-7 billion** [1].

**(b)** Country X: 1.8 / 2.4 x 100 = **75%** [1]. Country Y: 45 / 300 x 100 = **15%** [1].

**(c)** Country X, because tourism provides 75% of its exports (and 40% of its GDP, 1.8 / 4.5) [1], so a fall in arrivals would cut most of its foreign-currency income, whereas Country Y earns far more in total but relies on tourism for only 15% of exports [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** Guesthouse owners are local, so profits stay on the island [1], and they are more likely to buy food, services and labour from local suppliers, strengthening the multiplier effect [1].

**(b)** One advantage identified [1] and explained [1], for example: foreign-owned resorts can draw on the global marketing and booking networks of their parent companies, so the island attracts more reliable year-round bookings; or they may bring investment in infrastructure, such as an airport or water supply, that the island could not fund itself. **[4]**

**9.** The pleasure periphery describes destinations in lower-income regions, such as tropical coasts and islands, surrounding the wealthy core regions where most tourists come from [1]. Flows run from core to periphery, often long-haul and seasonal, with decisions and profits controlled from the core [1] [1]. Limitation: most international trips are intra-regional, and outbound travel from emerging economies is growing rapidly, so flows are no longer simply core to periphery [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Credit any sport studied, for example football:

- Leagues sell television rights worldwide, so top clubs have fans in countries where they have never played [1].
- Broadcast income concentrates wealth in a few leagues and clubs, increasing their ability to attract players from around the world [1].
- Clubs tour and market in distant countries, and matches are scheduled for global audiences [1].
- Local leagues in smaller countries may lose spectators to televised foreign leagues [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Indicative content:

- **Hard legacy benefits:** improved transport and housing, regeneration of former industrial land, as in Stratford after London 2012 [1] [1].
- **Hard legacy costs:** venues underused after the event, as with several Rio de Janeiro 2016 venues, and long-term debt, as with Montreal 1976 [1] [1].
- **Soft legacy:** national image and international profile, skills and volunteering [1]; reputational damage from labour or rights concerns [1].
- **Distribution:** construction firms, sponsors and wealthier districts gain more; poorer residents may face displacement or higher rents [1].
- **Time:** short-term tourism boosts may fade, while transport improvements can last decades [1].
- **Planning:** reuse of existing and temporary venues improves outcomes [1].
- **Judgement:** a supported conclusion, such as that legacy is positive only where investment matches long-term local needs [1]. **[10]**

**12.** Indicative content:

- **Reducing inequality between countries:** tourism transfers income from high-income source countries to lower-income destinations and provides foreign exchange [1].
- **Jobs:** tourism creates many jobs for people with few formal qualifications [1].
- **Leakage:** foreign ownership, all-inclusive packages and cruises mean much spending returns to the core [1] [1].
- **Within destinations:** benefits go to owners and investors and to resort areas, while jobs are often seasonal and low-paid [1]; land and housing costs may rise for residents [1].
- **Dependency:** small economies relying on tourism are exposed to shocks, which hit the poorest workers hardest [1].
- **Pleasure periphery and dependency view:** decision-making stays in the core [1].
- **Conditions for reducing inequality:** local ownership, linkages to local suppliers and community-based tourism [1].
- **Judgement:** for example, tourism can narrow gaps between countries while widening gaps within them, depending on ownership and management [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* "discuss" and "evaluate" both need balance and a judgement; distinguishing inequality between countries from inequality within them is an effective way to structure the second essay.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing the size of tourism income with dependency on it.
- Forgetting that the tourism balance can be negative for wealthy countries.
- Treating all forms of tourism as retaining the same share of spending.
- Judging an event's legacy only by its stadiums.
