This guide goes deeper into section 3.5.1 The properties of acids and bases (statements a to g) of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Chemistry (9202) specification, Version 6.2, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. The qualification is untiered. It concentrates on the skills that trip students up: deducing salt formulae, writing balanced and ionic equations, and explaining pH in terms of ions. | Statement | Content |
|---|---|
| 3.5.1 a | Metal oxides and hydroxides are bases; soluble hydroxides are alkalis |
| 3.5.1 b | Acids react with bases to form salts: neutralisation |
| 3.5.1 c | The salt depends on the acid (chlorides, nitrates, sulfates) and on the metal in the base or alkali |
| 3.5.1 d | Ammonia dissolves in water to give an alkaline solution and is used to make ammonium salts |
| 3.5.1 e | Limewater (calcium hydroxide solution) reacts with carbon dioxide to give calcium carbonate |
| 3.5.1 f | H⁺(aq) makes solutions acidic, OH⁻(aq) makes them alkaline; the pH scale 0 to 14; universal indicator |
| 3.5.1 g | H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l) |

## Classifying bases and alkalis

Ask two questions about any compound: *is it a metal oxide or metal hydroxide?* (then it is a base) and *does it dissolve in water?* (then it is also an alkali).

| Compound | Base? | Soluble? | Alkali? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zinc oxide, ZnO | Yes | No | No |
| Iron(III) hydroxide, Fe(OH)₃ | Yes | No | No |
| Potassium hydroxide, KOH | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Lithium hydroxide, LiOH | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)₂ | Yes | Slightly (limewater is its solution) | Yes |

Ammonia is a special case. It is neither a metal oxide nor a metal hydroxide, but its solution is alkaline because it makes hydroxide ions when it dissolves (see below).

## Deducing the formula of a salt

The name of a salt comes from the metal and the acid (3.5.1 c). The **formula** comes from the charges on the two ions, which must cancel.

| Positive ions | Negative ions |
|---|---|
| Na⁺, K⁺, Li⁺, NH₄⁺ | Cl⁻ (chloride), NO₃⁻ (nitrate), OH⁻ (hydroxide) |
| Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺, Zn²⁺, Cu²⁺, Fe²⁺ | SO₄²⁻ (sulfate), O²⁻ (oxide) |
| Al³⁺, Fe³⁺ | |

Rule: use enough of each ion to make the total charge zero, and put brackets round a group ion when you need more than one of it.

### Worked example 1: aluminium oxide and sulfuric acid

*Name the salt and write a balanced equation.*

1. Salt: metal **aluminium**, acid gives **sulfate**, so aluminium sulfate.
2. Formula: Al³⁺ and SO₄²⁻. Two Al³⁺ give +6; three SO₄²⁻ give −6. So **Al₂(SO₄)₃**.
3. Unbalanced: Al₂O₃ + H₂SO₄ → Al₂(SO₄)₃ + H₂O.
4. Three sulfates are needed, so 3H₂SO₄. That supplies 6 H, which makes 3H₂O.

**Al₂O₃ + 3H₂SO₄ → Al₂(SO₄)₃ + 3H₂O**

Check: Al 2 = 2; S 3 = 3; O 3 + 12 = 15 on the left and 12 + 3 = 15 on the right; H 6 = 6.

### Worked example 2: lithium oxide and nitric acid

Li⁺ and NO₃⁻ cancel one-to-one, so the salt is **lithium nitrate, LiNO₃**. Lithium oxide, Li₂O, contains two lithium ions, so two nitrates are needed:

**Li₂O + 2HNO₃ → 2LiNO₃ + H₂O**

Lithium oxide dissolves in water to form lithium hydroxide, so in solution this is an acid–alkali neutralisation.

## From full equation to ionic equation (3.5.1 g)

Every acid–alkali neutralisation reduces to the same ionic equation. You can show this in three steps.

**Step 1: write the full equation with state symbols.**
KOH(aq) + HNO₃(aq) → KNO₃(aq) + H₂O(l)

**Step 2: split every dissolved ionic substance into its ions.** Water is a liquid made of molecules, so it stays together.
K⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) + H⁺(aq) + NO₃⁻(aq) → K⁺(aq) + NO₃⁻(aq) + H₂O(l)

**Step 3: cross out ions that appear unchanged on both sides.** K⁺ and NO₃⁻ are **spectator ions**.
**H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)**

With sulfuric acid and sodium hydroxide, step 3 leaves 2H⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → 2H₂O(l), which simplifies to the same equation. The spectator ions are what remain as the salt when the water is evaporated.

## Ammonia in water (3.5.1 d)

Ammonia is very soluble in water. Some of the dissolved molecules take a hydrogen ion from water molecules:

NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)

The hydroxide ions make the solution alkaline. The reaction is reversible, and only some of the ammonia reacts at any moment.

With an acid, ammonia takes a hydrogen ion from the acid instead, so the ionic change is NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺. The ammonium ion then plays the part a metal ion plays in other salts, which is why the products are named **ammonium** chloride, nitrate or sulfate. Ammonium salts made this way are important fertilisers, which links to the Haber process in 3.8.3.

## Limewater and carbon dioxide (3.5.1 e)

Limewater is an alkali: calcium hydroxide dissolved in water. Carbon dioxide reacts with the hydroxide to make calcium carbonate, which does not dissolve, so the clear solution turns cloudy white. This reaction is the basis of the carbon dioxide test in 3.4.2.

## pH and ions (3.5.1 f)

- **Acidic** solutions contain more H⁺(aq) than OH⁻(aq). The more hydrogen ions, the **lower** the pH.
- **Alkaline** solutions contain more OH⁻(aq) than H⁺(aq). The more hydroxide ions, the **higher** the pH.
- **Neutral** solutions, pH 7, have no excess of either.

Two consequences examiners like to test:

1. **Diluting** an acid with water spreads the same hydrogen ions through a larger volume, so the pH rises towards 7 (but stays below 7). Diluting an alkali lowers its pH towards 7.
2. **Adding alkali to acid** removes H⁺ by neutralisation, so the pH rises; at exact neutralisation it is 7; extra alkali then leaves OH⁻ in excess and the pH goes above 7.

Universal indicator gives only an **approximate** pH, read by matching its colour against a chart.

### Worked example 3: explaining a pH change

*Sodium hydroxide solution is added a little at a time to dilute hydrochloric acid containing universal indicator until the alkali is in excess. Describe and explain the colour changes.* **[4]**

**Model answer.** At first the indicator is red because the acid contains a high concentration of H⁺ ions [1]. Each addition of alkali adds OH⁻ ions, which react with H⁺ ions to form water, so the H⁺ concentration falls and the colour moves through orange and yellow [1]. When all the H⁺ ions have reacted the solution is neutral and green (pH 7) [1]. Further alkali leaves OH⁻ ions in excess, so the colour turns blue and then purple [1].

**Why it scores:** each colour is tied to a change in ions, not just listed.

## Common mistakes

- Writing the formula of aluminium sulfate as AlSO₄ or Al₂SO₄₃. Balance the charges and use brackets.
- Leaving spectator ions in an "ionic equation", or splitting water into ions.
- Saying an alkali "contains no H⁺": in exams, describe alkaline solutions as having OH⁻ in excess.
- Saying dilution makes an acid neutral. It moves the pH towards 7 but cannot pass it.
- Calling ammonia an "ammonium hydroxide base" without explaining the OH⁻ comes from its reaction with water.

## Exam technique: command words

- **Name** the salt: two words, metal then ending (for example, iron(III) chloride).
- **Write a balanced equation:** formula of the salt first, then balance; check every element.
- **Explain** a pH or colour change: link each change to H⁺ or OH⁻ ions.
- **Describe** how to use universal indicator: add it, compare with the chart, read the approximate pH.
