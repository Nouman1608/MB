This guide covers **Paper 2 Texts and Genres**, the second of two exam
papers in AQA A-Level English Literature B (7717). Where Paper 1 Literary
Genres builds outward from two long-established dramatic genres --
tragedy and comedy -- Paper 2 works with genres that are newer, more
fluid, and still visibly evolving: crime writing and political and
social protest writing. The specification is explicit that these labels
describe *elements* rather than fixed categories, since many of the set
texts pre-date the genre being discussed as a recognised literary
tradition.

## Where this fits in 7717

Paper 2 is worth 40% of the A-level, assessed by a 3-hour open-book
written exam worth 75 marks. Being open book is the key practical
difference from Paper 1: candidates may take clean, unannotated copies
of their set texts into the exam room. This changes the revision
emphasis -- there is less value in memorising quotations for their own
sake, and more in knowing the text well enough to locate and use
material quickly under exam pressure.

## Syllabus coverage

**AQA A-LEVEL ENGLISH LITERATURE B (7717) — PAPER 2: TEXTS AND GENRES**

Candidates choose **one** of two options:

- **Option 2A: Elements of Crime Writing** — texts in which a significant
crime drives the narrative, and where the crime's execution and
consequences shape the text's structure. Sub-elements the specification
identifies as worth exploring include: the specific sub-type of crime
text (detective fiction, revenge tragedy, an account of a life lost to
crime); settings created as backdrops for criminal action; the nature of
crimes, criminals and their motives; detection and investigation;
whether moral order is restored; guilt, remorse and confession; the
detective-hero and their nemesis; the treatment of victims and
suffering; and how the text's language draws on criminal, legal or
police registers.
- **Option 2B: Elements of Political and Social Protest Writing** —
texts with issues of power and powerlessness at their core, across both
public and private settings. Sub-elements include: the specific
sub-type of text (post-modern novel, satirical poetry, political drama);
settings for power struggles, real or imagined; the pursuit of power and
rebellion against it; corruption and control; the connection between
individual and collective experience; gender politics and social class;
and how the text's structure heightens or resolves political tension.

Within the chosen option, candidates study **three texts**: one post-2000
prose text, one poetry text, and one further text, one of which must be
written pre-1900. The exam also includes a compulsory unseen-passage
question, which may draw on poetry, prose or drama from either genre,
including literary non-fiction.

Set texts for **Option 2A** include Charles Dickens's *Oliver Twist*,
Shakespeare's *Hamlet*, Agatha Christie's *The Murder of Roger Ackroyd*,
Graham Greene's *Brighton Rock*, Ian McEwan's *Atonement*, Kate
Atkinson's *When Will There Be Good News?*, Samuel Taylor Coleridge's
*The Rime of the Ancient Mariner*, and a poetry selection spanning
Crabbe, Browning and Wilde.

Set texts for **Option 2B** include Charles Dickens's *Hard Times*,
Shakespeare's *Henry IV Part I*, William Blake's *Songs of Innocence and
of Experience*, Margaret Atwood's *The Handmaid's Tale*, Khaled
Hosseini's *The Kite Runner*, Jim Crace's *Harvest*, Henrik Ibsen's *A
Doll's House* (Bloomsbury Methuen Drama edition, translated by Michael
Meyer), and Tony Harrison's selected poems.

## Exam structure

The 3-hour paper is divided into three sections, each worth 25 marks:

- **Section A** — a compulsory question on the unseen passage.
- **Section B** — an essay question on one of the candidate's own set
texts.
- **Section C** — an essay question that connects two of the candidate's
set texts.

## How to approach it

Because the paper is open book, the highest-value revision work is
building a mental map of each text -- which sections deal with which
"elements" from the specification's list -- so that material can be
located quickly rather than recalled from memory alone. For Section C,
practise planning connective essays that argue across texts rather than
writing two mini-essays side by side: a strong response uses one text to
illuminate the other, tracking a specific element (such as the treatment
of guilt, or the structure of a power struggle) across both. For the
unseen-passage question in Section A, practise applying the same
"elements" framework used for the set texts, since the unseen extract is
drawn from the same genre territory and rewards the same analytical
habits. Because texts can satisfy more than one requirement at once (a
pre-1900 poetry text, for instance, can cover both the poetry and the
pre-1900 requirements simultaneously), check with your teacher exactly
which three texts your centre has chosen and how they map onto the
specification's requirements before building a revision plan around
them.

## Common mistakes

Treating "elements of crime writing" or "elements of political and
social protest writing" as if they named a rigid genre with fixed rules,
rather than a set of features to identify and weigh in each text.
Writing Section C as two separate text analyses instead of a genuinely
connective essay. Bringing an annotated or additional-material copy of a
set text into the open-book exam, which is not permitted. Neglecting the
unseen passage because it feels less "revisable" than the set texts --
it carries the same 25 marks as each essay section.

## Quick revision checklist

- Know which option (2A or 2B) and which three texts your course covers.
- For each text, be able to identify at least three of the specification's
named "elements" and locate supporting material quickly in an open book.
- Practise Section C essays that connect two texts around a single
element, rather than treating them separately.
- Apply the same elements-based analysis to unseen extracts as to set
texts.
- Confirm which of your three texts is pre-1900 and which is the
post-2000 prose text, since both are compulsory requirements.
