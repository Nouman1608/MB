**This guide covers one feature of the Paper 2 source pack in OxfordAQA International GCSE English
Language (9270).** The specification says the reading texts "will relate to modern world themes and
issues. Some of the texts may be brief or solely visual. Texts may be fiction or non-fiction." The
[Paper 2 Section A reading study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-english-language-9270-section-a-reading/)
introduces visual and brief texts with a checklist of presentational features; this page explains
how to analyse them in depth, how to give evidence when there are few or no words, and how to link
them to the longer texts in the pack.

## Why these texts need a different approach

Most reading answers rest on quotation. A slogan may have four words; a photograph or chart may have
none. The reading objective that applies most directly is R4, which asks you to "explain and evaluate
how writers use linguistic, grammatical, structural and presentational features". For a visual text,
the "writer" is the designer or photographer, and "presentational features" carry most of the
meaning. For a brief text, every word is chosen under pressure of space, so each one deserves
attention.

## Giving evidence from a visual text

You cannot quote an image, but you can **describe it precisely**. Precise description is the visual
equivalent of a short quotation.

- **Weak:** "The picture shows a sad person."
- **Strong:** "An elderly man sits alone at the far left of the frame, looking down at his hands,
  while the rest of the image is empty pavement."

The strong version tells the examiner exactly which feature you are analysing, so your explanation of
its effect can follow.

## A method for visual texts: five layers

Work through the image in this order. Each layer gives you at least one point.

**1. Salience: what you see first.** The largest, brightest, most central or sharpest element is the
main message. Ask why the designer made it dominant.

**2. Composition: where things are placed.** Foreground and background, left and right, top and
bottom, crowded and empty space. Placing a figure alone in a large empty space suggests isolation;
crowding many figures together suggests pressure or community.

**3. Gaze and angle.** A figure looking directly at the viewer makes a demand on you, creating
personal address. A figure looking away invites you to observe, as an onlooker. A low camera angle
makes the subject seem powerful; a high angle makes it seem small or vulnerable.

**4. Colour, light and typography.** Colour carries associations and contrasts: a single bright
object in a grey scene draws the eye and the message. Typography matters too: capitals, bold type,
handwriting-style fonts and size all signal tone.

**5. Image and words together.** If there is a caption, slogan or logo, ask how it changes the image.
A caption can **anchor** the meaning, fixing one reading of an image that could be read several ways.
A photograph of a crowded beach could mean holiday fun or environmental pressure until a caption
tells you which.

## Charts and infographics

A chart or infographic is a visual text with a perspective, even when it looks neutral.

- **Selection:** what has been measured, and what has been left out?
- **Comparison:** which bars, segments or figures are placed side by side, and what does the
  comparison suggest?
- **Emphasis:** which number is largest or highlighted in colour?
- **Labelling:** do headings use neutral or loaded words ("Rise in costs" or "Families squeezed")?
- **Icons:** do pictures of people, coins or trees make abstract data emotional?

When you write about a chart, describe what it shows accurately, then analyse how it is presented to
influence the reader. Never add figures the chart does not give.

## Brief texts

Brief texts include slogans, headlines, short notices, social media posts, captions and text messages
in a story extract. They work by **compression**.

| Feature | Example (original) | What to analyse |
|---|---|---|
| Imperative | "Switch off. Look up." | Direct command; the reader is made to act |
| Ellipsis | "Fewer cars. Cleaner air." | Missing verbs make the link seem obvious and certain |
| Wordplay | "Make every drop count" | A double meaning (water and money, or effort) |
| Inclusive pronoun | "Our streets, our choice" | Builds a community the reader is invited to join |
| Contrast | "Yesterday's rubbish, tomorrow's playground" | Before-and-after transformation in a few words |
| Hashtag or tag | "#StreetsForPeople" | Signals a campaign and invites sharing |

Because there are so few words, you can analyse grammar closely: the absence of a verb, the choice of
a second-person "you", the rhythm of a short phrase.

## Fiction and non-fiction in brief form

The specification says texts "may be fiction or non-fiction". A brief text can be either: a slogan is
non-fiction, but a tiny story, a short poem or a fictional diary entry can also be brief. For a brief
fiction text, ask what situation is implied and what the reader must infer.

## Worked example

Because this page contains no images, the visual source is **described in words**. It is an
**original poster created for this guide**.

> *A poster for a water-saving campaign. Almost the whole poster is a close-up photograph of a child's
> cupped hands, holding a small pool of water. The background is dry, cracked earth in pale brown.
> The only colour is the clear blue of the water. Across the bottom, in small white capitals: "EVERY
> DROP IS SOMEONE'S." Below that, a small campaign logo.*

**Question:** How does the poster use presentational features to influence the reader?

**Model answer (extract):**
The poster makes the child's cupped hands its most salient feature, filling almost the whole frame,
so the reader's attention goes first to the water being carefully held. The close-up removes the
child's face, which makes the hands representative: they could belong to any child, so the issue
seems universal rather than personal. Colour drives the message. Against the pale, cracked earth,
the clear blue water is the only bright element, so it appears precious, and the cracks suggest
drought without any words being needed. The slogan "EVERY DROP IS SOMEONE'S" anchors the image:
without it the photograph might suggest play, but the caption turns the water into something owned
and needed. The small, quiet typography contrasts with the large image, suggesting that the
photograph should do the persuading and the words simply confirm it.

*Why it works:* it describes features precisely, analyses each for effect, explains how image and
words interact, and stays focused on influence on the reader.

## Linking brief and visual texts to the rest of the pack

Questions on pairs of texts may set a visual source beside a written one. Compare **methods** as
well as ideas: a photograph may create an emotional response in an instant, while an article builds
the same response through argument and anecdote. Note these links during your first survey of the
pack, and remember that ideas from visual sources can be reworked in your Section B writing.

## Common mistakes

- **Describing without analysing**: "There is a picture of a tree" with no effect.
- **Vague description**: name position, size, colour, gaze.
- **Ignoring the words** on a visual text, or ignoring the image when there are words.
- **Treating a chart as neutral**: it has selected and arranged its data.
- **Inventing details** that are not in the source.
- **Writing too little** because the text is short: a brief text can support detailed analysis.

## Exam technique

For a visual source, spend a minute working through the five layers before you write. For a brief
text, look at every word and its grammar. Check which text or texts each question names, and divide
your time by the marks shown.
