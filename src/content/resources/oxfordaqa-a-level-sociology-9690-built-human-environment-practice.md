## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by rural depopulation. **[2]**

**2.** Identify two features of cities that Wirth argued shape urban social life. **[2]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by gentrification. **[3]**

**4.** Outline two differences between peasants and landless labourers. **[4]**

**5.** Outline two reasons why states have pressed nomadic peoples to settle. **[4]**

**6.** Study the table, which gives fictional data for the fictional country of Tessaly.

| Indicator (fictional) | 1990 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|
| Total urban population (millions) | 8.0 | 22.0 |
| Urban residents living in informal settlements (millions) | 3.0 | 6.6 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of Tessaly's urban population living in informal settlements in 1990 and in 2025. **[2]**
**(b)** Using the table, explain how the data could be used both to support and to challenge Mike Davis's view of urbanisation. **[4]**

### Section B: longer and structured questions

**7.** Read the item.

> In the fictional village of Kervan, one family owns most of the land and controls the only irrigation canal. Most other households farm as sharecroppers, giving half of each harvest to the landowning family. Several sharecroppers borrowed money from the same family to pay for a wedding or medical bills and now work extra days to repay it. Over the last ten years, most young men and many young women have left for the city. The village school has merged with one in the next village.

**(a)** Identify two forms of inequality shown in the item. **[2]**
**(b)** Using the item, analyse how power shapes life in rural areas such as Kervan. **[6]**

**8.** Explain two ways in which cities both contribute to and are affected by climate change. **[4]**

**9.** Outline three different sociological views of informal settlements. **[6]**

**10.** Analyse how gated communities and segregation reflect power and inequality in cities. **[8]**

**11.** Evaluate demographic transition theory as an explanation of population change in less developed countries. **[12]**

**12.** To what extent has urbanisation weakened community? Use examples from your own society or a society you know about. **[16]**

## Answers

Extended answers (Questions 10, 11 and 12) are shown as indicative creditworthy points. The real exam marks extended responses with levels of response, judging the answer as a whole.

### Section A

**1.** The decline in the number of people living in rural areas [1], usually because young adults move to towns and cities for work, leaving ageing villages [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any two: size [1]; density [1]; heterogeneity (social diversity). **[2]**

**3.** The movement of middle-class residents into a working-class area [1], where they renovate housing and raise property values [1], so rents rise and original residents are displaced (a term coined by Ruth Glass) [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Peasants own or control a small plot of land [1], while landless labourers own no land and must sell their labour [1]. Peasants produce partly for their own household's use [1], while landless labourers depend on wages or a share of someone else's crop, such as sharecropping [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Nomads are hard for states to tax, count and school [1], so settlement increases state control and administration [1]. States and investors want grazing land for farming, mining or conservation [1], so moving nomads frees land for other uses, as in Soviet collectivisation of Kazakh herders [1]. **[4]**

**6. (a)** 1990: 3.0 / 8.0 x 100 = 37.5% [1]. 2025: 6.6 / 22.0 x 100 = 30% [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Support: the number living in informal settlements more than doubled, from 3.0 to 6.6 million [1], supporting Davis's view that slum growth is a central feature of urbanisation in poorer countries [1]. Challenge: the share in informal settlements fell from 37.5% to 30% [1], suggesting formal housing grew faster than informal housing, so urbanisation is not only producing slums [1]. **[4]**

### Section B

**7. (a)** Any two: unequal landownership [1]; control of irrigation by one family [1]; sharecroppers giving half the harvest; debt owed to the landowning family. **[2]**
**(b)** One family owns most land and the only canal, so it controls the resources others need to farm [1], which gives it power over production and income in the whole village [1]. Sharecroppers giving half of each harvest shows how landlessness transfers wealth upwards [1], keeping households poor regardless of how hard they work [1]. Loans for weddings and medical bills that must be repaid with extra labour resemble debt bondage [1], and the departure of young people and the school merger show that rural depopulation follows when local opportunity is blocked [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Cities concentrate emissions from transport, buildings and industry [1], so urban growth adds to global heating, although dense cities with public transport use less energy per person [1]. Cities suffer from heat islands and flooding, especially coastal megacities such as Jakarta [1], where subsidence and sea-level rise have pushed the government to plan a new capital [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Perlman: residents are not marginal but integrated as workers and voters [1], and are exploited and stigmatised rather than excluded [1]. Turner: residents build and improve their homes effectively [1], so governments should support self-help instead of demolishing settlements [1]. De Soto: legal titles would turn homes into capital [1], allowing residents to borrow, although critics say titles can raise land values and displace the poorest [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Indicative content:
- Gated communities use walls and controlled entry to separate the wealthy from public space [1].
- Caldeira: fear of crime in São Paulo produced fortified enclaves [1].
- Low: residents sought safety, "niceness" and social homogeneity [1].
- Segregation can be legal, as under the Group Areas Act (1950) in South Africa [1].
- Market segregation follows prices, prejudice and housing policy [1].
- Wacquant: advanced marginality and stigma in excluded neighbourhoods [1].
- Analysis: the powerful choose separation; the poor have it imposed [1].
- Counter: some minority groups cluster by choice for support, so not all separation is imposed [1].
**[8]**

**11.** Indicative content:
- Four stages: high birth and death rates; falling death rates; falling birth rates; low rates [1].
- Application: falling death rates through vaccination and clean water fit stage 2 [1].
- Urbanisation and girls' education help explain falling birth rates (stage 3) [1].
- Boserup: population pressure can drive innovation, against Malthus [1].
- Based on European history, so it may not fit other societies [1].
- Death rates fell faster in many poorer countries, with modern medicine, than in Europe [1].
- State policy, such as China's one-child policy, changed fertility directly [1].
- Religion and cultural norms can keep fertility high despite urbanisation [1].
- Migration is left out of the model [1].
- Below-replacement fertility in Japan and South Korea suggests a fifth stage [1].
- The theory describes rather than explains when change will happen [1].
- Judgement: useful as a description, weak as a prediction for less developed countries [1].
**[12]**

**12.** Indicative content:
- Tönnies: Gemeinschaft gives way to Gesellschaft [1].
- Simmel: the blasé attitude as a defence against overstimulation [1].
- Wirth: size, density and heterogeneity make relationships impersonal [1].
- Own-society application: anonymity in apartment blocks or commuting [1].
- Augé: non-places such as malls and airports lack shared history [1].
- Gated communities and segregation divide residents by class [1].
- Rural depopulation also weakens village communities left behind [1].
- Counter: Gans found "urban villagers" with close ties [1].
- Informal settlements often have strong mutual aid, as in the Orangi Pilot Project [1].
- Perlman: favela residents are integrated, not isolated [1].
- Own-society application: religious, ethnic or migrant associations in cities [1].
- Community may be based on networks or interests rather than place [1].
- Non-places can be places of community for workers in them [1].
- Classic theories idealise village life, ignoring rural surveillance and inequality [1].
- Analysis: effects differ by class, gender and migrant status [1].
- Judgement: urbanisation changes the form of community more than it destroys it [1].
**[16]**
*Examiner insight:* Set at least one classic theorist against contemporary evidence; a list of thinkers who all agree cannot show evaluation.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing peasants with landless labourers.
- Treating informal settlements only as places of crime or despair.
- Ignoring the difference between a number and a share in data questions.
- Describing DTT stages without evaluating the theory.
- Using no own-society example in an extended answer that asks for one.
