## 3.2.3.1 The four trends at a glance

| Down the group, F → I | Direction | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|
| Electronegativity | falls | larger atom, more shielding, so the bonding pair is held less tightly |
| Boiling point | rises | more electrons per molecule, so stronger van der Waals forces |
| Oxidising ability of X₂ | falls | an incoming electron is further from the nucleus and more shielded |
| Reducing ability of X⁻ | rises | an outer electron is further out and more shielded, so easier to lose |

Add a fifth for the tests: the solubility of AgX in ammonia **falls** from AgCl to AgI.

**Definition:** electronegativity is an atom's power to attract the electron pair in a covalent bond.
Learn the specification's wording exactly.

**Data worth knowing:** electrons per molecule F₂ 18, Cl₂ 34, Br₂ 70, I₂ 106. Boiling points roughly
85 K, 239 K, 332 K, 457 K. Pauling electronegativities roughly 4.0, 3.2, 3.0, 2.7.

### Method: a boiling point explanation in four steps

1. Name the structure: simple molecular, diatomic.
2. Compare electrons per molecule, with numbers.
3. Link more electrons to stronger van der Waals (induced dipole–dipole) forces between molecules.
4. More energy is needed to overcome them.

The X–X covalent bond is not broken on boiling. Writing that it is costs the explanation.

## 3.2.3.1 Displacement in aqueous solution

Rule: a halogen oxidises the halide ions of any halogen lower in the group.

| Halogen added | Cl⁻(aq) | Br⁻(aq) | I⁻(aq) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cl₂ | -- | Br₂ forms | I₂ forms |
| Br₂ | none | -- | I₂ forms |
| I₂ | none | none | -- |

General ionic equation, where X sits above Y: **X₂ + 2Y⁻ → 2X⁻ + Y₂**.

**Colour tip:** shake with cyclohexane. Iodine turns the upper organic layer violet; in water it looks
brown. Bromine gives an orange organic layer.

## 3.2.3.1 Solid sodium halides with concentrated H₂SO₄

**Step 1, all halides (acid–base):** NaX + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HX. You see steamy fumes.

**Step 2, only if X⁻ is a strong enough reducing agent (redox):**

| Halide | Reduces S to | S oxidation state | Extra signs |
|---|---|---|---|
| F⁻, Cl⁻ | -- | +6 (no change) | none |
| Br⁻ | SO₂ | +4 | orange-brown vapour, choking gas |
| I⁻ | SO₂, S, H₂S | +4, 0, −2 | purple vapour or black solid, yellow solid, bad-egg smell |

**Sulfur half-equations (all reductions):**

```
H₂SO₄ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → SO₂ + 2H₂O         S: +6 → +4
H₂SO₄ + 6H⁺ + 6e⁻ → S + 4H₂O           S: +6 → 0
H₂SO₄ + 8H⁺ + 8e⁻ → H₂S + 4H₂O         S: +6 → −2
```

Pair each with 2X⁻ → X₂ + 2e⁻, scaled so the electrons cancel (×1, ×3 or ×4).

### Method: an overall equation that starts from the solid

1. Count electrons: how far does sulfur fall? That fixes the number of halide ions.
2. Write the redox part (halogen, sulfur product).
3. Every Na⁺ ends up in NaHSO₄, so add one H₂SO₄ per sodium.
4. Balance O and H with water, then check every element.

Example, iodide going only as far as SO₂: 2NaI + 3H₂SO₄ → 2NaHSO₄ + I₂ + SO₂ + 2H₂O.

## 3.2.3.1 Silver nitrate test

**Method in steps:**

1. Dissolve the sample and add dilute nitric acid.
2. Add silver nitrate solution and note any precipitate.
3. Add dilute ammonia; if the precipitate remains, try concentrated ammonia.

| Precipitate | Ion | Dilute NH₃ | Conc. NH₃ |
|---|---|---|---|
| none | F⁻ | -- | -- |
| white | Cl⁻ | soluble | soluble |
| cream | Br⁻ | insoluble | soluble |
| yellow | I⁻ | insoluble | insoluble |

**The three "explain why" answers:**

| Choice | Reason |
|---|---|
| silver nitrate | precipitates of different colours form with Cl⁻, Br⁻ and I⁻ |
| acidified with HNO₃ | removes ions such as CO₃²⁻ and OH⁻, which would also precipitate with Ag⁺; not HCl, which adds Cl⁻ |
| ammonia added | the three colours look alike; solubility in ammonia confirms which halide |

Dissolving: AgCl(s) + 2NH₃(aq) → [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq).

## 3.2.3.2 Chlorine equations

| Reaction | Equation | Type |
|---|---|---|
| with water | Cl₂ + H₂O ⇌ HClO + HCl | disproportionation (0 → +1 and −1), reversible |
| with water in sunlight | 2Cl₂ + 2H₂O → 4H⁺ + 4Cl⁻ + O₂ | redox: Cl reduced, O oxidised |
| with cold, dilute NaOH | Cl₂ + 2NaOH → NaClO + NaCl + H₂O | disproportionation (0 → +1 and −1) |

**Names:** ClO⁻ is the chlorate(I) ion; NaClO is sodium chlorate(I). Its solution is bleach, also used to
disinfect.

**Water treatment, in four points:** bacteria are killed; water-borne disease is prevented; chlorine is
toxic and can produce chlorinated organic compounds; on balance, health gains win over toxic effects.
Any chemical added to a water supply (fluoride is another) is judged by weighing pros against cons.

## Small worked reminders

**Reminder 1, a silver iodide mass.** A solution holds 0.00200 mol of iodide ions. Excess acidified silver
nitrate gives 0.00200 mol AgI. Mr(AgI) = 107.9 + 126.9 = 234.8, so the mass is 0.00200 × 234.8 =
**0.470 g**.

**Reminder 2, electrons in the NaOH reaction.** In Cl₂ + 2OH⁻ → Cl⁻ + ClO⁻ + H₂O, one Cl atom gains one
electron (0 → −1) and the other loses one (0 → +1). The totals match, so no electrons appear in the
overall equation.

**Reminder 3, the mole ratio for bleach.** One Cl₂ gives one NaClO. To make 0.0400 mol NaClO you need
0.0400 mol Cl₂, which is 0.0400 × 71.0 = **2.84 g**.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Halogens** are oxidising agents (weaker down); **halide ions** are reducing agents (stronger down).
- **Steamy fumes** (HX, acid–base) versus **coloured vapour** (X₂, redox).
- **Dilute** ammonia dissolves only AgCl; **concentrated** dissolves AgCl and AgBr.
- **Chlorine water** in the dark (an equilibrium, disproportionation) versus **in sunlight** (oxygen
  released, not disproportionation).
- **Nitric acid** to acidify silver nitrate; **acidified barium chloride** for sulfate.

## Quick self-test

1. Why is chlorine more electronegative than iodine?
2. Which halogen has the weakest van der Waals forces between its molecules, and why?
3. Bromine water is added to sodium chloride solution. What happens, and why?
4. Write the ionic equation for chlorine displacing iodine from iodide ions.
5. Which sodium halides give only steamy fumes with concentrated sulfuric acid?
6. Name the gas with a bad-egg smell from sodium iodide and concentrated sulfuric acid, and give the
   oxidation state of sulfur in it.
7. A precipitate from acidified silver nitrate dissolves in concentrated ammonia but not dilute. Name it.
8. Calculate the mass of AgCl formed from 0.00150 mol of chloride ions. (Mr AgCl = 143.4)
9. In the sunlight reaction of chlorine water, which element is oxidised?
10. State one use of the solution made from chlorine and cold, dilute sodium hydroxide.
11. Which reagent tests for sulfate ions in Required practical 3?

### Answers

1. Chlorine's atom is smaller with less shielding, so the bonding pair is closer to the nucleus and
   attracted more strongly.
2. Fluorine; F₂ has the fewest electrons per molecule (18).
3. No reaction; bromine is a weaker oxidising agent than chlorine, so it cannot oxidise Cl⁻.
4. Cl₂ + 2I⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + I₂.
5. Sodium fluoride and sodium chloride.
6. Hydrogen sulfide, H₂S; −2.
7. Silver bromide, AgBr (cream).
8. 0.00150 × 143.4 = **0.215 g**.
9. Oxygen (−2 in water to 0 in O₂).
10. Bleach (or a disinfectant for cleaning).
11. Acidified barium chloride solution.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing electronegativity with "nuclear charge falls"; it rises, but distance and shielding win.
- Saying covalent bonds break on boiling.
- Comparing boiling points by molecule "size" without electron numbers.
- Mixing the two redox trends: halogen oxidising power falls, halide reducing power rises.
- Writing a redox step for sodium chloride.
- Unbalanced charges in the sulfuric acid half-equations.
- Forgetting the extra H₂SO₄ that forms NaHSO₄ in an equation from the solid salt.
- Quoting "AgBr is insoluble in ammonia" without saying dilute.
- Calling the sunlight reaction disproportionation.
