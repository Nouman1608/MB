These notes condense section 14.3 of Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696), for examination in 2025 and 2026: regional disparities in social and economic development; the concept of core-periphery; and cumulative causation from initial advantage(s), with spread and backwash effects, regional divergence and convergence. The full explanation and the Brazil example are in the [14.3 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-regional-development-within-countries/), and exam-style questions are in the [14.3 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-regional-development-within-countries-practice/). For the policy case study that applies these ideas, see the [14.4 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-management-of-regional-development-revision-notes/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Regional disparity | A difference in economic or social development between regions of one country |
| Core | Region with the greatest concentration of people, investment, decision-making and wealth |
| Periphery | Region with lower incomes, dependent on the core |
| Initial advantage | The asset that first attracts investment to a region |
| Cumulative causation | Self-reinforcing growth (or decline) in which each change causes further change in the same direction |
| Multiplier effect | Extra rounds of spending and jobs created by an initial investment |
| Agglomeration economies | Benefits firms gain from locating close together |
| Diseconomies of agglomeration | Costs of over-concentration: congestion, high land prices, pollution |
| Backwash effects | Flows that harm the periphery: migration, capital and competition drawn to the core |
| Spread effects | Flows that help the periphery: demand, relocation, remittances, diffusion of investment |
| Divergence | Widening regional disparities |
| Convergence | Narrowing regional disparities |

## Measuring disparities

- **Index** = regional value ÷ national value × 100. Above 100 = above average.
- **Ratio** = richest region ÷ poorest region.
- **Absolute gap** = richest region − poorest region.
- Relative and absolute measures can point in opposite directions. Core 20,000 → 40,000 and periphery 5,000 → 12,000: ratio falls from 4.0 to 3.3 (relative convergence), but the gap rises from 15,000 to 28,000 (absolute divergence).
- Use social indicators too: life expectancy, infant mortality, schooling, access to health care and internet.

## Initial advantages

Natural resource; site and access (port, river crossing); political function (capital city); early start in industrialisation; favourable climate or fertile land.

## Cumulative causation chain

Initial advantage → new industry → jobs and in-migration → multiplier effect → agglomeration economies → higher tax revenue → better infrastructure and skills → more investment → repeat.

In decline the chain reverses: job losses → less spending → lower tax revenue → poorer services → out-migration.

## Spread versus backwash

| Backwash | Spread |
|---|---|
| Migration of young and skilled workers to the core | Core demand for food, raw materials and components |
| Capital flows to the core | Firms relocate to escape core congestion and costs |
| Core firms out-compete peripheral firms | Remittances to source regions |
| Services concentrate in the core | Diffusion of innovation and investment along transport routes |

**Spread wins when:** core diseconomies rise; transport links improve; governments invest in or give incentives to the periphery; the periphery has its own assets or a growing sub-core.

## Models to name

- **Myrdal (1957):** cumulative causation; backwash and spread.
- **Hirschman (1958):** polarisation and trickle-down.
- **Friedmann (1966):** four-stage core-periphery model ending in an integrated space economy.
- **Williamson (1965):** regional inequality rises in early development and falls later (inverted U).

**Evaluation:** convergence is not automatic; old industrial regions can decline in rich countries; measures may disagree.

## Applied example: Brazil

- Core: the South-east, around São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro; early advantages of ports, coffee wealth and early industry.
- Periphery: the North-east, with lower incomes and drought in the semi-arid interior.
- Backwash: migration from the North-east to São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro.
- Policy attempts at spread: Brasília (1960) and the Manaus Free Trade Zone (1967).

## Exam traps

- A disparity with no indicator or figures.
- A model named with no mechanism.
- Only backwash effects given.
- Convergence claimed from a ratio without checking the absolute gap.
- Treating "the periphery" as one uniform region.
- Spending a 14.3 answer on policy detail that belongs in 14.4.

## Self-test

1. Define regional disparity and give one economic and one social indicator.
2. Calculate the index for a region with GDP per capita of 6,000 when the national average is 8,000.
3. Give three kinds of initial advantage.
4. Put these in order: multiplier effect, initial advantage, better infrastructure, new industry.
5. What are agglomeration economies?
6. Give two backwash effects.
7. Give two spread effects.
8. Under what conditions might spread effects outweigh backwash?
9. Explain how a ratio could show convergence while the absolute gap widens.
10. What did Williamson suggest about regional inequality over time?
11. Name Brazil's core region and one peripheral region.
12. Why might an old industrial region decline in a high-income country?

### Answers

1. A difference in development between regions of one country; for example GDP per capita and life expectancy.
2. 6,000 ÷ 8,000 × 100 = 75.
3. Any three of: natural resource, port or site, capital city function, early start, fertile land or climate.
4. Initial advantage, new industry, multiplier effect, better infrastructure.
5. Benefits firms gain from locating close together, such as shared suppliers, skilled labour and ideas.
6. Migration of young or skilled workers to the core; capital flowing to the core; core firms out-competing peripheral firms.
7. Core demand for peripheral products; relocation of firms; remittances; diffusion of investment.
8. When core diseconomies rise, transport links improve, governments invest in the periphery, or the periphery has its own assets.
9. If both regions grow but the core starts from a higher base, the core can add more in absolute terms even though the periphery grows faster in percentage terms.
10. It rises in early stages of national development and falls later (an inverted U).
11. Core: the South-east (São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro). Periphery: the North-east (or the North).
12. Its initial advantage (for example coal or heavy industry) is lost, so cumulative causation works in reverse.
