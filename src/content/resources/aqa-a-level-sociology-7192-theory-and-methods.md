Theory and Methods is the part of AQA A-level Sociology (7192) that asks how sociologists know what
they know, and how they explain society. The specification sets out the same list of Theory and
Methods content twice — as 3.1.3 in Paper 1 and 3.3.2 in Paper 3 — so one body of knowledge is
examined on two papers: a 10-mark question in Paper 1, and 30 marks in Paper 3. It covers research
methods and sources of data, positivism and interpretivism, the major sociological theories,
modernity and post-modernity, whether sociology is a science, values, and sociology's relationship
with social policy.

For applying methods to education specifically, see the
[Methods in Context study guide](/resources/a-level-sociology-methods-in-context/).

## Specification coverage

AQA AS and A-level Sociology specification (7191/7192), A-level content 3.1.3 and 3.3.2 Theory and
Methods (identical lists). Students must examine:

1. quantitative and qualitative methods of research; research design
2. sources of data, including questionnaires, interviews, participant and non-participant
   observation, experiments, documents and official statistics
3. the distinction between primary and secondary data, and between quantitative and qualitative data
4. the relationship between positivism, interpretivism and sociological methods; the nature of
   "social facts"
5. the theoretical, practical and ethical considerations influencing choice of topic, choice of
   method(s) and the conduct of research
6. consensus, conflict, structural and social action theories
7. the concepts of modernity and post-modernity in relation to sociological theory
8. the nature of science and the extent to which Sociology can be regarded as scientific
9. the relationship between theory and methods
10. debates about subjectivity, objectivity and value freedom
11. the relationship between Sociology and social policy.

**Assessment:** Paper 1 (Education with Theory and Methods) includes Theory and Methods extended
writing worth 10 marks; Paper 3 (Crime and Deviance with Theory and Methods) includes Theory and
Methods extended writing worth 30 marks.

## How do research methods and sources of data compare? (points 1–3)

| Method / source | Data | Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Questionnaires** | Primary, quantitative | Large samples; reliable; easy to analyse | Low validity; low response rates; imposition of categories |
| **Structured interviews** | Primary, quantitative | Standardised; higher response | Interviewer bias; limited depth |
| **Unstructured interviews** | Primary, qualitative | Rapport; depth; validity | Time, cost, small samples; hard to compare |
| **Participant observation** | Primary, qualitative | Natural behaviour; verstehen | Access; going native; ethics if covert; hard to replicate |
| **Non-participant observation** | Primary | Less influence on group | Observer effect; less insight into meaning |
| **Laboratory experiments** | Primary, quantitative | Control of variables; cause and effect | Artificial; ethical limits; small samples |
| **Field experiments** | Primary | More natural | Less control; ethical issues of consent |
| **Documents** (public and personal) | Secondary, usually qualitative | Access to past; cheap | Authenticity, credibility, representativeness, meaning — the four criteria from John Scott (*A Matter of Record*, 1990) |
| **Official statistics** | Secondary, quantitative | Large scale; trends; free | Socially constructed; definitions change; dark figures |

**Research design** includes choosing a topic, forming hypotheses or research questions,
operationalising concepts, sampling, piloting and analysing data.

## What is the difference between positivism and interpretivism? (point 4)

**Positivism** — associated with Auguste Comte and Émile Durkheim — holds that society is an objective
reality made of **social facts**: "ways of acting, thinking and feeling, external to the individual"
that constrain behaviour (Durkheim, *The Rules of Sociological Method*, 1895). Sociologists should
use scientific, quantitative methods to discover causal laws. Durkheim's *Suicide* (1897) used
official statistics to show that suicide rates varied with social integration and regulation.

**Interpretivism** — influenced by Max Weber's idea of **verstehen** — argues that people act on the
meanings they give to situations, so sociology must understand those meanings using qualitative
methods. J. Maxwell Atkinson (*Discovering Suicide*, 1978) argued that suicide statistics are the
product of coroners' interpretations, and Jack Douglas (*The Social Meanings of Suicide*, 1967)
called for studying the meanings behind suicidal acts.

## What shapes the choice of topic and method? (point 5)

- **Theoretical:** positivists favour reliable, quantitative methods; interpretivists favour valid,
  qualitative ones; a researcher's theoretical perspective shapes which questions seem important.
- **Practical:** time, money, funding bodies' requirements, access, the researcher's personal
  characteristics and skills.
- **Ethical:** the British Sociological Association's Statement of Ethical Practice stresses informed
  consent, confidentiality, avoiding harm and care with vulnerable groups; covert methods raise
  particular problems of deception.

## What are consensus, conflict, structural and social action theories? (point 6)

| Theory | Type | Core idea | Key names |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Functionalism** | Structural, consensus | Society as a system of interrelated parts based on shared values | Durkheim; Talcott Parsons; Robert Merton (manifest and latent functions) |
| **Marxism** | Structural, conflict | Society shaped by class conflict between bourgeoisie and proletariat; ideology legitimates exploitation | Karl Marx; Louis Althusser (ideological and repressive state apparatuses); Antonio Gramsci (hegemony) |
| **Feminism** | Mainly conflict | Society structured by patriarchy; liberal, radical, Marxist and difference feminisms disagree on causes and solutions | Ann Oakley; Sylvia Walby (*Theorizing Patriarchy*, 1990) |
| **Symbolic interactionism** | Social action | People create society through interaction and shared meanings | George Herbert Mead; Herbert Blumer (*Symbolic Interactionism*, 1969) |
| **Phenomenology / ethnomethodology** | Social action | Studies how people make sense of the world and produce social order in everyday life | Alfred Schutz; Harold Garfinkel (*Studies in Ethnomethodology*, 1967) |
| **Structuration** | Bridges structure and action | Structure and agency reproduce each other | Anthony Giddens (*The Constitution of Society*, 1984) |

## What are modernity and post-modernity? (point 7)

**Modernity** — the era from the Enlightenment and industrialisation, marked by the nation state,
capitalism, rationality, science and belief in progress. Classical theories ("grand narratives" or
"meta-narratives") such as functionalism and Marxism are products of modernity.

**Post-modernity** — Jean-François Lyotard (*The Postmodern Condition*, 1979) described an
"incredulity towards meta-narratives"; Jean Baudrillard argued that media signs create
**hyperreality**. Society is said to be fragmented, media-saturated and based on consumer choice
and identity.

**Late modern theorists** reject the claim that modernity has ended: Giddens argues we live in late or
"high" modernity marked by **reflexivity**; Ulrich Beck (*Risk Society*, 1986) describes a society
preoccupied with manufactured risks.

## Is sociology a science? (point 8)

- **Positivists:** yes — sociology can and should follow the logic of the natural sciences.
- **Karl Popper** (*The Logic of Scientific Discovery*, 1934; English 1959): science is defined by
  **falsification**; he criticised Marxism as unfalsifiable.
- **Thomas Kuhn** (*The Structure of Scientific Revolutions*, 1962): science works within
  **paradigms**; sociology is "pre-paradigmatic" because sociologists disagree on fundamentals.
- **Realists** — Russell Keat and John Urry (*Social Theory as Science*, 1975) and Andrew Sayer —
  distinguish **open** and **closed** systems; sociology, like meteorology, studies open systems and
  can still be scientific in seeking underlying causes.
- **Interpretivists:** sociology studies meaningful action, so natural-science methods are
  inappropriate.

## How do theory and methods relate? (point 9)

Theoretical perspective often shapes method choice: structural theories and positivism lean towards
quantitative methods; social action theories and interpretivism lean towards qualitative ones. But
the link is not fixed — many researchers use **methodological pluralism** and **triangulation**, and
practical factors may override theory.

## Can sociology be value-free? (point 10)

- **Classical positivists** (Comte, Durkheim) believed sociology could be objective and should guide
  society.
- **Weber** argued values inevitably shape choice of topic and interpretation, but researchers should
  be value-free in data collection.
- **Committed sociology** — Howard Becker ("Whose side are we on?", 1967) argued sociologists should
  side with the underdog; Alvin Gouldner ("Anti-Minotaur: The Myth of a Value-Free Sociology",
  1962) said claims of value freedom often hid support for the powerful.
- **Feminists** such as Oakley argue research should serve women's interests.

## What is the relationship between sociology and social policy? (point 11)

- **Positivists and functionalists** see sociology providing objective evidence to solve problems
  through piecemeal social engineering.
- **Social democratic** sociologists such as Peter Townsend (*Poverty in the United Kingdom*, 1979)
  argued research should lead to policies redistributing resources; the Black Report (1980) on
  health inequalities is a classic example of research meant to shape policy.
- **Marxists** argue policy in capitalist states mainly serves capitalism.
- **Feminists** influenced policies on equal pay and domestic violence, but critics say liberal
  reforms do not end patriarchy.
- **New Right** writers such as Charles Murray argue welfare policy creates dependency.
- **Postmodernists** doubt that sociology can produce the objective knowledge needed for policy.

Practical constraints — funding, electoral considerations and governments' ideologies — limit how much
research actually influences policy.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is the same content listed twice?** AQA assesses Theory and Methods in both Paper 1 and Paper 3,
so the specification repeats the same list under 3.1.3 and 3.3.2.

**How is Theory and Methods different from Methods in Context?** Theory and Methods is assessed in the
abstract; Methods in Context (Paper 1) requires applying methods to a specific education issue.

**Which debates are most often examined?** Positivism versus interpretivism, sociology as a science,
value freedom, and the usefulness of major theories are common focuses; prepare all eleven points.
