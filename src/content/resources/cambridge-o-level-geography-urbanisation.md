This guide goes deeper into **sub-topic 1.7 Urbanisation**, the final
sub-topic of Theme 1 Population and Settlement in Cambridge O Level Geography
(2217), 2026 series (Version 3). The
[Population and Settlement study guide](/resources/o-level-geography-population-and-settlement/)
gives the theme overview. To consolidate, use the
[Urbanisation revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-geography-urbanisation-revision-notes/)
and then the [Urbanisation practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-geography-urbanisation-practice/).

## What 1.7 asks you to do

The syllabus sets two learning outcomes:

1. **Identify and suggest reasons for rapid urban growth.** Reference should
   be made to the physical, economic and social factors that cause **rural
   depopulation** and the movement of people to major cities.
2. **Describe the impacts of urban growth on both rural and urban areas,
   along with possible solutions to reduce the negative impacts.** This
   includes the effects of urbanisation on people and the natural
   environment, the **characteristics of squatter settlements**, and
   strategies to reduce the negative impacts.

The required case study is **a rapidly growing urban area in a developing
country and migration to it**. Note that the case study must cover the
migration as well as the city.

## Getting the terms right

- **Urbanisation** is an increase in the *proportion* of a country's
  population living in urban areas.
- **Urban growth** is an increase in the *size* or *population* of urban
  areas. A city can grow without urbanisation if the rural population grows
  just as fast.
- **Rural depopulation** is a fall in the number of people living in the
  countryside, usually through out-migration.

The United Nations estimates that since around 2007 more than half of the
world's population has lived in urban areas. Most of today's fastest urban
growth is in low- and middle-income countries in Asia and Africa.

## Why cities grow so fast

Rapid urban growth has **two causes working together**:

1. **Rural–urban migration.** People move from the countryside to the city.
2. **Natural increase within the city.** Migrants are mostly young adults,
   so birth rates in the city are high and death rates relatively low. This
   second cause is often forgotten.

The syllabus asks you to sort the reasons for migration into three groups.

| Type of factor | Push factors (rural areas) | Pull factors (cities) |
|---|---|---|
| **Physical** | Drought, desertification, soil erosion, floods, crop failure, natural hazards | Less exposure to harvest failure; reliable water supply in some districts |
| **Economic** | Low and uncertain farm incomes, mechanisation replacing farm labour, land divided into ever-smaller plots, debt, few non-farm jobs | Jobs in factories, construction and services; higher wages; informal-sector work such as street trading |
| **Social** | Few schools, clinics or hospitals; no electricity; conflict; isolation | Better education and health care; entertainment; relatives already in the city; perception of a better life |

**Chain migration** is where earlier migrants send information and money home
and help relatives find housing and work, so more people follow from the same
village. It explains why particular districts of a city are often settled by
people from the same region.

## Impacts on rural areas

| Positive | Negative |
|---|---|
| **Remittances** sent home pay for food, schooling and farm improvements | Loss of young, economically active adults, especially men |
| Less pressure on land and food supply | Population left behind is older and more female, so farm labour is short |
| Returning migrants bring skills and ideas | Some farmland is abandoned and food output can fall |
| | Falling population means services may lose their threshold and close |

## Impacts on urban areas

**On people:**

- Housing shortage, leading to overcrowding and **squatter settlements**.
- More job seekers than formal jobs, so **unemployment, underemployment**
  and a large **informal sector**.
- Overloaded water supply, sanitation, schools, clinics and transport.
- Disease spread by contaminated water and poor sanitation.
- Positive impacts too: a large labour force, economic growth, and better
  access to services than in many rural areas.

**On the natural environment:**

- Air pollution from traffic, industry and burning waste.
- Water pollution as untreated sewage and industrial waste reach rivers and
  the sea.
- Large volumes of solid waste and open dumping.
- Loss of farmland, forest and wetland as the city sprawls.
- Building on steep slopes increases the risk of landslides; more
  impermeable surfaces increase flood risk.

## Characteristics of squatter settlements

Squatter settlements have different local names: *favelas* in Brazil,
*katchi abadis* in Pakistan and *bustees* in parts of India. Their typical
characteristics are:

- **Illegal or insecure tenure:** residents do not own the land.
- **Self-built homes** made at first from scrap wood, corrugated iron, plastic
  and cardboard, improved over time with brick and concrete.
- **Location on marginal land:** steep hillsides, flood plains, beside
  railways and roads, next to rubbish dumps, or on the city edge.
- **Very high density**, with narrow, unpaved alleys.
- **Few basic services:** limited piped water, sanitation, electricity or
  rubbish collection.
- **Active informal economies**, with workshops, small shops and recycling
  businesses inside the settlement.

Use the full list when asked to "describe": you will gain more marks from a
range of characteristics than from repeating that homes are poor.

## Strategies to reduce the negative impacts

| Strategy | How it works | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| **Self-help schemes** | Residents are given materials, tools or low-interest loans to improve their own homes | Depends on residents' time and skill; quality varies |
| **Site-and-service schemes** | Authorities provide plots with water, sewerage and roads; residents build homes | Plots may be far from jobs; costs may exclude the poorest |
| **In-situ upgrading** | Existing settlements are given paved paths, drains, water and legal tenure, such as Rio de Janeiro's Favela-Bairro programme from the 1990s | Expensive; improvement can raise rents and push out the poorest |
| **Community sanitation** | Residents build and maintain low-cost sewers lane by lane, as in the Orangi Pilot Project in Karachi, started in 1980 | Needs strong community organisation |
| **Mass transit** | Bus rapid transit and rail move many people cheaply (for example the Lagos BRT, opened in 2008) | High construction cost; may not reach every district |
| **Rural development** | Irrigation, rural electrification, clinics and schools reduce push factors | Slow to take effect; needs sustained funding |
| **New towns and satellite cities** | Planned settlements take pressure off the main city | Jobs may not follow; long commutes |

Strategies that reduce the **causes** (rural development) are different in
kind from strategies that manage the **effects** (upgrading, transport). An
evaluation that makes this distinction is stronger. Clearing squatter
settlements by demolition is also a "strategy" in practice, but it usually
moves the problem elsewhere rather than solving it.

## Worked example 1: interpreting data

The table below is **illustrative data** for an unnamed developing country.

| Year | Urban population (% of total) |
|---|---|
| 1980 | 25 |
| 2000 | 33 |
| 2020 | 44 |

*Describe the trend and calculate the change between 1980 and 2020.* [3]

**Answer.** The proportion of people living in urban areas increased
throughout the period [1], and the increase was faster between 2000 and 2020
(11 percentage points) than between 1980 and 2000 (8 percentage points) [1].
The overall change was 44 − 25 = **19 percentage points** [1].

Be careful with units: a change from 25% to 44% is an increase of 19
*percentage points*, not 19%.

## Worked example 2: a case-study plan

*For a named rapidly growing urban area in a developing country, describe the
causes of migration to it and explain the impacts of this migration on the
city.* [7]

A plan for **Karachi, Pakistan**:

- **Context:** Pakistan's largest city and main port on the Arabian Sea;
  grew rapidly after Partition in 1947 as migrants arrived from India, and has
  continued to attract migrants from across Pakistan.
- **Causes:** pull of port, industrial and service jobs; push of low farm
  incomes, small landholdings and lack of services in rural areas; physical
  push factors such as floods and drought.
- **Impacts:** large katchi abadis such as Orangi Town; pressure on water
  supply, sewerage and transport; traffic congestion and air pollution; a
  large informal sector.
- **Responses:** community-led sanitation through the Orangi Pilot Project;
  regularising tenure in some settlements; public transport projects.

Add reliable figures from your textbook or teacher. The syllabus does not name
case studies, so Lagos, Mumbai, Dhaka or São Paulo are equally valid choices if
you know them well.

## Common mistakes

- Using *urbanisation* and *urban growth* as if they mean the same thing.
- Explaining growth only by migration and forgetting natural increase.
- Giving push and pull factors without saying whether they are physical,
  economic or social when the question asks for this.
- Describing only negative impacts, or only impacts on the city when the
  question asks about rural areas as well.
- Naming a strategy without explaining how it works or how far it succeeds.

## Exam technique for 1.7

- **Identify** asks for a name or selection, often from a resource.
- **Describe** asks for characteristics, so for squatter settlements list
  several distinct features.
- **Explain** needs a reason and a link: "Mechanisation reduces the need for
  farm labour, *so* young people move to the city to find work."
- **Suggest** asks you to apply ideas to the place shown in a resource.
- **To what extent** or **How far do you agree** needs a judgement supported
  by evidence on both sides.

Look at the sub-marks: a 3-mark "explain" usually needs one fully developed
point or three linked statements, not three one-word answers.
