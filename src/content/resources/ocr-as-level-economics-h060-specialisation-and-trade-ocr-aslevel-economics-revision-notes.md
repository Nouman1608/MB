## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Specialisation | Concentrating on producing a limited range of goods or services |
| Division of labour | Splitting a production process into tasks, each done by a different worker |
| Labour productivity | Output per worker (or per worker-hour) |
| Exchange | Giving one good, service or sum of money in return for another |
| Barter | Direct exchange of goods for goods, without money |
| Double coincidence of wants | The need, under barter, for each party to want what the other offers |
| Money | Anything generally accepted as payment for goods and services |
| Medium of exchange | Money's function of being accepted in payment |
| Measure of value | Money's function as a common unit for pricing goods (unit of account) |
| Store of value | Money's function of holding purchasing power over time |
| Standard of deferred payment | Money's function as the unit for settling future debts |

## Condensed recall

**Specialisation and the division of labour**

- Specialisation applies to people, firms, regions and countries.
- The division of labour is specialisation by task inside one process.
- Productivity rises through practice, less switching, specialist equipment, matching skills to tasks and shorter training.
- Costs: monotony and lower motivation, dependence on every stage, narrow skills, less variety.

**Scarcity**

- Specialisation gets more output from the same scarce resources.
- It does not create new resources, and over-specialisation brings vulnerability.

**Trade**

- Specialists produce surpluses of one good and none of others, so they must exchange.
- Gains from specialisation exist only if trade is possible.

**Barter versus money**

- Barter needs a double coincidence of wants.
- With n goods, barter needs n(n - 1) / 2 exchange rates; with money, n prices.
- Barter struggles with indivisible goods and with storing value.
- Money's four functions remove these problems, which allows much greater specialisation.
- High inflation weakens money as a store of value.

## Worked reminders

- Sandwich kitchen: four workers making 120 an hour rises to 180 with division of labour. Output per worker rises from 30 to 45 (+50%). At £12 an hour each, labour cost per sandwich falls from £0.40 to £0.27.
- Ana (10 loaves or 4 baskets a day) and Ben (4 loaves or 8 baskets): splitting time gives 7 loaves and 6 baskets in total; specialising gives 10 loaves and 8 baskets.
- Ten goods under barter: 10 x 9 / 2 = 45 exchange rates.

## Linking to other topics

- Specialisation raises output from scarce resources, which links back to the basic economic problem and the PPC.
- Specialisation by region creates risk: a town dependent on one employer suffers high unemployment if it closes.
- Comparative advantage, which explains what countries should specialise in, is developed under international trade.

## Exam traps

- Using "specialisation" and "division of labour" as if they mean the same thing.
- Stating "productivity rises" without the reason.
- Forgetting the disadvantages in an evaluation.
- Ignoring the need for exchange.
- Listing three functions of money and missing standard of deferred payment.
- Confusing measure of value (pricing goods) with store of value (saving).
- Treating any object as money; it must be generally accepted.

## Self-test

1. Define specialisation.
2. Distinguish specialisation from the division of labour.
3. Give two reasons why the division of labour raises productivity.
4. Give two disadvantages of the division of labour for workers or firms.
5. Six workers each make 15 chairs a week. After a division of labour, the team makes 120 a week. Find the percentage change in output per worker.
6. Why does specialisation make exchange necessary?
7. What is meant by a double coincidence of wants?
8. How many exchange rates are needed under barter with 6 goods?
9. State the four functions of money.
10. Which function of money allows a worker to save part of her wages for next year?
11. Which function of money allows a firm to agree to pay a supplier in three months?
12. Why might high inflation weaken money's role in supporting specialisation?

### Answers

1. Concentrating on producing a limited range of goods or services.
2. Specialisation can apply to people, firms, regions or countries; the division of labour is specialisation by task within one production process.
3. Any two: practice builds skill, less time lost switching tasks, use of specialist equipment, matching skills to tasks, shorter training.
4. Any two: monotony and lower motivation, dependence on every stage working, narrow skills, less variety of product.
5. Output per worker rises from 15 to 120 / 6 = 20, a rise of 33.3%.
6. A specialist produces more of one good than they need and none of others, so they must exchange to obtain the goods they want.
7. Each party must want what the other has to offer.
8. 6 x 5 / 2 = 15.
9. Medium of exchange, measure of value, store of value, standard of deferred payment.
10. Store of value.
11. Standard of deferred payment.
12. If money loses purchasing power quickly, people are less willing to hold it or accept it, so exchange becomes harder and some may return to barter.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does specialisation always benefit workers?**
Not always. It raises productivity, which can support higher pay, but repetitive tasks and narrow skills can lower job satisfaction and security.

**Is a bank card money?**
A card is a way of moving money held in a bank account; the bank deposit is the money, because it is generally accepted in payment.
