> **Original questions written for Marlbridge.** They are not past-paper questions and do not copy the real exam's structure or mark tariffs. Answers use Spain as the model country for Napoleonic rule.

**HL only.** These questions practise Regional study 4 in the Europe option (HL Paper 3). Revise first with the [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-french-revolution-revision-notes/) and the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-french-revolution/).

## Questions

**1.** State two reasons why the Estates General of 1789 became deadlocked. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why the financial crisis was important in causing the revolution. **[4]**

**3.** Read this short extract, constructed for practice.

> "We were told the king was our father, yet he ran from Paris in the night like a thief. How can we trust a man who would leave his people and ask foreign armies to come?" (Constructed for practice, in the voice of a Paris shopkeeper in 1791.)

**(a)** Explain what the extract shows about attitudes to the monarchy after Varennes. **[3]**

**(b)** Analyse one limitation of using this extract to show opinion across France. **[3]**

**4.** Explain two reasons why the Directory fell in 1799. **[4]**

**5.** Analyse the reasons why the revolution radicalized between 1791 and 1794. **[6]**

**6.** Examine the impact of Napoleon's rule on women in France. **[6]**

**7.** "The invasion of Russia in 1812 was the main reason for Napoleon's defeat." To what extent do you agree? Plan an answer: state your argument, three themes and a counter-argument. **[15]**

**8.** Compare the political impact of Napoleonic rule in France with its impact on one other country you have studied. **[4]**

**9.** Discuss how historians disagree about the causes of the Terror. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two: dispute over voting by order versus by head; the privileged orders wanted to keep their advantage; the Third Estate wanted equal weight; the crown gave unclear guidance. One mark each. **[2]**

**2.** Up to 4. France was close to bankruptcy because of war debts, including support for the American colonists (1). The tax system exempted the privileged, so the crown could not raise enough money (1). Reform attempts by Turgot, Necker, Calonne and Brienne failed against resistance (1). The crisis forced the king to call the Estates General, which created the political opening for the revolution (1). **[4]**

**3. (a)** Up to 3: the writer feels betrayed because the king fled (1); trust in the king as a father figure is broken (1); the fear that foreign armies would be called shows suspicion of the king's loyalty to France (1). **[3]**
**(b)** Up to 3: it is one voice, a Paris shopkeeper, so it cannot show opinion across France (1); Paris was more radical than the countryside, where support for the king and the Church was often stronger (1); it is constructed and written to express a view, so it reflects the strength of feeling of some people, not all (1). **[3]**

**4.** Up to 4, two reasons explained. Political weakness: it annulled elections to stay in power and lost credibility, facing both royalist and Jacobin opposition (2). Dependence on the army and the need for stable government and successful wars, which made a successful general attractive, as with Napoleon (2). Accept corruption and financial weakness. **[4]**

**5.** Up to 6 using a markband approach. War from April 1792 and defeats created fear of treason (1). The flight to Varennes and the king's execution removed a moderate option (1). Counter-revolution in the Vendée and foreign invasion in 1793 led to emergency government (1). Economic crisis, inflation and food shortages increased the demands of the sans-culottes (1). Political ideas and rivalry between Girondins and Jacobins pushed the revolution toward extremes (1). Analysis of which factors mattered most, or how they interacted (1). **[6]**

**6.** Up to 6: the revolution had given women inheritance rights and divorce (1); Napoleon's Civil Code placed wives under husbands' authority (1); limits on divorce and property control (1); no political rights before or after, and women's clubs had been banned in 1793 (1); women had economic roles in work and support for the army (1); a judgement that Napoleon reversed some of the revolution's gains (1). **[6]**

**7.** Credit a plan with these features, 3 marks each, 15 in all. Understanding of the claim: the invasion of June 1812 destroyed most of the Grande Armée through retreat, winter, hunger and disease (3). The case for Russia: loss of experienced soldiers, shock to French prestige, and encouragement to Prussia and Austria to rejoin the coalition (3). Other factors: Continental System and the Peninsular War as a drain on resources, British money for coalitions, growing nationalism, and the defeat at Leipzig in 1813 (3). Perspectives: a historian's view, for example those who stress structural overreach against those who stress military mistakes (3). Counter-argument and judgement: for instance that Russia was a turning point but not the sole cause, since the Peninsular War and the economic strain made the empire vulnerable before 1812 (3). **[15]**

**8.** Up to 4. In France Napoleon created a centralized state with a Civil Code, prefects and plebiscites (1); in Spain he placed Joseph on the throne, which lacked legitimacy and produced a guerrilla war (1); both saw reforms that challenged old privileges, but in Spain they were linked to foreign occupation (1); a judgement on the difference (1). **[4]**

**9.** Up to 6: one view sees the Terror as a response to circumstances such as war, civil war and food shortage (2); another sees ideology and the logic of revolution as driving violence (2); explains why they differ, such as what evidence they use and when they stress it (1); a reasoned judgement or combination (1). **[6]**
