## Questions

**1.** Define an embargo and give one example of a good a government might embargo. **[2]**

**2.** Country D imports wooden toys at a world price of 40 each. It then adds an ad valorem tariff of 30%. Imports fall from 800 to 620 a month.

**(a)** Calculate the price of a toy in Country D after the tariff. **[1]**
**(b)** Work out the government's monthly income from the tariff. **[2]**
**(c)** Find the percentage fall in imports. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the price elasticity of demand for imports over this range. **[2]**

**3.** Country E imported 120,000 tonnes of cement a year at a world price of 90 a tonne. It sets a quota of 90,000 tonnes. The home price rises to 99 a tonne.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage by which the quota cuts imports. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why the home price rises. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the extra income earned by foreign sellers on the 90,000 tonnes, and state who would receive it if the government sold the import licences. **[2]**

**4.** Country F pays a subsidy of 9 per unit to its jacket makers, who produce 450 units a month.

**(a)** Calculate the monthly cost to the government. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain one advantage of a subsidy over a tariff for consumers. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain one disadvantage of a subsidy compared with a tariff. **[2]**

**5.** A start-up in Country G makes electric fans. The world price is 55 a fan. Its costs are shown below.

| Output per month | Total cost |
|---|---|
| 50 | 6,500 |
| 100 | 9,000 |
| 150 | 10,950 |
| 200 | 12,400 |
| 250 | 13,750 |

**(a)** Work out the average cost at each output and state the first output at which the firm can match the world price. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why this is an example of an infant industry argument. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the protection should be temporary. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why a government might protect a strategic industry. **[4]**

**7.** A foreign firm sells steel pipes in Country H at 66 a unit. The same pipes sell in its home market for 88.

**(a)** Define dumping. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate by what percentage the price in Country H is below the home price of the foreign firm. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the specific tariff that would raise the price to the home price. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain why dumping harms domestic producers even though consumers gain. **[4]**

**8.** Country J imports heaters at a world price of 30. It then adds a tariff of 6 a heater. Before the tariff, quantity demanded was 1,200 and home output was 300. After the tariff, quantity demanded is 1,100 and home output is 360.

**(a)** Work out the number of heaters imported before the tariff and after it. **[2]**
**(b)** Work out the money the government collects from the tariff. **[2]**
**(c)** Find the percentage change in home output. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain two groups in Country J that are worse off and one that is better off. **[4]**

**9.** Country K exported 25,000 tonnes of tea a year to Country L at 2,400 a tonne. Country L introduces a tariff and exports fall to 17,500 tonnes. The price received by Country K stays at 2,400.

**(a)** Calculate the fall in Country K's export revenue. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate this as a percentage of the original revenue. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain how the fall could affect workers and the government in Country K. **[3]**
**(d)** Explain how retaliation by Country K could affect exporters in Country L. **[3]**

**10.** Country M puts a tariff of 6 on a drug whose import price is 30. Imports fall from 7,000 to 6,650 units.

**(a)** Calculate the price elasticity of demand for imports. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the tariff revenue. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain whether the tariff is effective in protecting home producers. **[4]**

**11.** Country N makes 6,000 motorbikes and imports 18,000 a year at 900 each. It adds a 20% ad valorem tariff. Afterwards it imports 13,500 bikes and home firms make 8,400, all sold at 1,080.

**(a)** Calculate the tariff revenue. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in home output. **[2]**
**(c)** Evaluate whether the tariff is a good policy for Country N. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** An embargo is a ban on trade with a country or in a particular good [1]. Example: a ban on arms exports (or imports) [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A good definition includes the idea of a ban. "Restriction" alone also describes a quota, so it is too vague to separate the two.

**2. (a)** Tariff = 0.30 x 40 = 12, so price = **52** [1].
**(b)** Revenue = 12 x 620 [1] = **7,440** [1].
**(c)** (800 - 620) / 800 x 100 = **22.5%** [1].
**(d)** Price change is +30% [1]. Elasticity = -22.5 / 30 = **-0.75**, inelastic [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show that the tariff is charged on the 620 units still imported, not the 800, or the revenue figure is wrong.

**3. (a)** (120,000 - 90,000) / 120,000 x 100 = **25%** [1].
**(b)** The quota cuts supply to the market [1]. Demand is unchanged, so there is a shortage at the old price [1]. Buyers bid the price up until demand matches the lower supply [1].
**(c)** (99 - 90) x 90,000 = **810,000** [1]. The government would receive it [1].
*Examiner insight:* Link the price rise to a shortage or reduced supply. Repeating that "the quota raises the price" is a restatement, not an explanation.

**4. (a)** 9 x 450 [1] = **4,050** [1].
**(b)** A subsidy lets home firms make more without raising the price to buyers [1], so consumers keep paying the world price [1].
**(c)** A subsidy costs the government money and must be funded by taxes [1], whereas a tariff raises revenue [1].
*Examiner insight:* Compare the two methods directly. An answer that says only what a subsidy does does not meet "compared with".

**5. (a)** Average costs: 130, 90, 73, 62, 55 [1] (all five worked out [1]). First output at or below 55: **250** [1].
**(b)** The firm is new and small [1], and its cost per unit is high until output grows [1].
**(c)** Once it reaches 250 units it can match the world price [1]. Staying protected would let buyers keep paying more and remove the pressure to keep costs low [1].
*Examiner insight:* Quote the figures from the table. A statement that costs "fall" gains less than naming the 250 units and the price of 55.

**6.** Strategic industries supply goods the country needs in a crisis, such as food or energy [1]. If the country relied on imports, supply could be cut by war or a dispute [1]. Protection keeps a home industry alive [1], so the country can still supply itself, at the cost of higher prices to consumers [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Give a reason and a consequence. Naming "food" or "defence" without the security reason is a list, not an explanation.

**7. (a)** Dumping means selling abroad [1] for less than it costs to make or for less than it charges at home [1].
**(b)** (88 - 66) / 88 x 100 [1] = **25%** [1].
**(c)** 88 - 66 = **22** [1].
**(d)** Domestic firms cannot match the low price [1], so they lose sales [1] and may close [1]. The foreign firm may then raise prices when it faces less competition [1].
*Examiner insight:* The percentage is calculated on the home price (88), as the question says. Dividing by 66 gives a different figure.

**8. (a)** Before: 1,200 - 300 = 900 [1]. After: 1,100 - 360 = **740** [1].
**(b)** 6 x 740 [1] = **4,440** [1].
**(c)** (360 - 300) / 300 x 100 [1] = **20%** [1].
**(d)** Consumers pay 36 instead of 30 and buy fewer heaters [1], and firms that use heaters as an input face dearer costs [1]. Home heater producers sell more and earn more [1], which can protect jobs [1]. Credit a government gain instead of the producers' gain.
*Examiner insight:* Name the groups and say what happens to each. Writing "the economy" gives no group to credit.

**9. (a)** Before: 25,000 x 2,400 = 60,000,000 [1]. After: 17,500 x 2,400 = 42,000,000. Fall = **18,000,000** [1].
**(b)** 18,000,000 / 60,000,000 x 100 = **30%** [1].
**(c)** Tea firms sell less, so they may cut output and lay off workers [1]. Fewer wages and profits mean lower income and tax receipts [1], and less foreign currency comes in [1].
**(d)** Country K taxes imports from Country L [1], so Country L goods cost more in K and sell less [1], which cuts income and jobs among its exporters [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show both the before and after revenue in (a). The difference is what the question asks for.

**10. (a)** Imports change = -350 / 7,000 = -5% [1]. Price change = +20%. Elasticity = -5 / 20 = **-0.25** [1].
**(b)** 6 x 6,650 [1] = **39,900** [1].
**(c)** Demand is very inelastic (-0.25), so imports fall by only 5% [1]. Home producers gain little extra market [1], so the tariff protects them poorly [1]. It does raise revenue of 39,900 [1].
*Examiner insight:* Use the elasticity figure to reach a judgement on effectiveness. Saying "it depends" with no number is weak.

**11. (a)** Tariff per bike = 0.20 x 900 = 180 [1]. Revenue = 180 x 13,500 = **2,430,000** [1].
**(b)** (8,400 - 6,000) / 6,000 x 100 [1] = **40%** [1].
**(c)** For: home output rises 40%, so firms and workers gain, and the government earns 2,430,000 [1]. Imports fall from 18,000 to 13,500, which is 25% [1]. Against: the price rises from 900 to 1,080, a 20% rise, so buyers pay more [1], and total sales fall from 24,000 to 21,900, down 8.75% [1]. Partners lose sales and may retaliate [1]. Judgement: the policy is worth it only if the home industry can become competitive and the tariff is temporary, since otherwise buyers keep paying more [1].
*Examiner insight:* A mark-earning evaluation uses figures from the question for both sides and finishes with a judgement tied to a stated condition.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining an embargo as any restriction, not a ban.
- Applying a tariff to the imports before the tariff, not after.
- Writing that a quota always gives the government revenue.
- Taking the new figure rather than the old one as the base for a percentage.
- Describing a reason for protection without saying why it matters.
- Writing about consumers only when the question asks about trading partners.
- Ignoring the data and giving a general answer to an evaluation.
- Leaving out a judgement at the end.
