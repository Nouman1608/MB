## Definitions to know

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Birth rate | Births per 1,000 people per year |
| Death rate | Deaths per 1,000 people per year |
| Natural change | Births minus deaths |
| Immigration | People moving into a country to live |
| Emigration | People leaving a country to live elsewhere |
| Net migration | Immigration minus emigration |
| Optimum population | The population that gives the highest output per head |
| Under-population | Fewer people than the optimum; resources not fully used |
| Over-population | More people than the optimum; output per head falls |
| Productivity | Output per worker (or per hour) |
| Primary, secondary and tertiary sectors | Raw materials and farming; making goods and building; services such as retail, banking and transport |

## Formulas

| Quantity | Formula |
|---|---|
| Birth rate | births ÷ population × 1,000 |
| Death rate | deaths ÷ population × 1,000 |
| Natural increase (%) | (births − deaths) ÷ population × 100 |
| Net migration | immigration − emigration |
| Total change | natural change + net migration |
| Output per head | total output ÷ population |
| Output per worker | total output ÷ number of workers |
| Growth in output per head | (1 + GDP growth) ÷ (1 + population growth) − 1 |
| Saving ratio | saving ÷ income × 100 |
| Dependency ratio (a helpful measure; the syllabus does not name it) | (aged 0-14 + aged 65+) ÷ aged 15-64 × 100 |

## 5.3.1 and 5.3.2 Population growth and why rates differ

Method for a population-change question, in steps:

1. Find natural change: births − deaths.
2. Find net migration: immigration − emigration.
3. Add them for total change.
4. Divide by the opening population for a percentage; add to it for the closing population.

### Small worked reminder (Country A, illustrative)

Opening population 5,000,000. Births 100,000. Deaths 40,000. Immigration 5,000. Emigration 15,000.

```
Birth rate = 100,000 / 5,000,000 x 1,000 = 20 per 1,000
Death rate = 40,000 / 5,000,000 x 1,000 = 8 per 1,000
Natural change = +60,000 (12 per 1,000)
Net migration  = 5,000 - 15,000 = -10,000
Total change   = +50,000 = 1.0%
Closing population = 5,050,000
```

### Causes at a glance

| Factor | Pushes it up | Pushes it down |
|---|---|---|
| Birth rate | Children as labour or old-age support; child deaths; limited contraception; low female education | Higher incomes; women in work and education; costly child-rearing; pensions |
| Death rate | Famine; epidemic; war; poor sanitation; many elderly people | Better healthcare, food, clean water, vaccination |
| Net migration | Wages, jobs and safety at home or abroad (pull); conflict and unemployment (push) | Entry controls; weak job market at the destination |

### Must-know distinctions

- Natural change is not the same as total change. Migration can reverse the sign.
- A rate is per 1,000. A number is a count.
- Low-income countries tend to have higher birth rates. A rich ageing country can still have a higher death rate than a poorer, younger one.
- Death rate falls first as healthcare improves. Birth rate falls later. Growth is fastest in between.

## 5.3.3 Effects of changes in population

### Optimum population

Optimum = highest output per head. Check by working out output ÷ population in every row.

### Small worked reminder (Country G, illustrative)

| Population (millions) | Output (units, billions) | Output per head |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | 20 | 5 |
| 8 | 56 | 7 |
| 12 | 96 | 8 |
| 16 | 112 | 7 |

The optimum is 12 million, with 8 units per head. At 16 million the country is over-populated. The optimum can rise with new technology, more capital or better skills.

### Effects of size change

| Change | Possible benefits | Possible costs |
|---|---|---|
| Population rises | Bigger workforce and market; more taxpayers | Pressure on land, housing, water; unemployment; lower output per head if over the optimum |
| Population falls | Less pressure on resources | Labour shortages; fewer taxpayers; falling demand |

### Age and gender: reading a pyramid

Read in this order: base (young), middle (workers), top (elderly). Then compare male and female bars in each band.

| Shape | What it suggests | Effects |
|---|---|---|
| Wide base, narrow top | High birth rate, lower life expectancy | Spending on schools and child health; many future workers; need for jobs |
| Narrow base, wide top | Low birth rate, long life expectancy | Pension and healthcare bills; fewer workers per pensioner; labour shortage |
| Bulge in working ages | Many people of working age | Possible gain if there are jobs |
| More women at the top | Women live longer on average | More elderly women needing care |
| Fewer women than men at working ages | Perhaps inward migration of male workers or higher female death rates | Affects the labour supply and family formation |

### Small worked reminder: dependency ratio

Country H (illustrative): 28% aged 0-14, 65% aged 15-64, 7% aged 65+. Ratio = (28 + 7) ÷ 65 × 100 = 53.8. Roughly 54 dependants rely on each 100 people aged 15-64.

### Evaluation checklist for population questions

- Compare with resources, capital and technology.
- Say whether the effect is short run or long run.
- Say who gains and who loses: firms, workers, taxpayers, the elderly.
- Mention government policy: education and training, pensions, retirement age, family support, migration rules.

## 5.4.1 Differences in development

| Factor | Cause of the gap | Impact |
|---|---|---|
| Income | Low productivity, low investment | Poor housing, diet, health and schooling |
| Productivity | Little capital, weak skills, poor health | Low wages and low output |
| Population growth | Falling death rate with high birth rate | Output per head grows slowly; more demand for schools and food |
| Sector size | Most workers in low-value primary production | Lower income; volatile raw-material prices |
| Saving and investment | Low income leaves little to save | Little capital, so low productivity |
| Education | Not enough schools, teachers or money | Low skills and poor use of technology |
| Healthcare | Few clinics, weak sanitation | Illness, lost workdays, short working life |

### Small worked reminder: growth per head

GDP growth 4.0%, population growth 3.0%: 1.04 ÷ 1.03 − 1 = 0.97% a year. Saving ratio: saving 35 out of income 500 gives 7%. Output per worker: output 90 from 30 workers gives 3 each.

### Evaluation chain: the income cycle

Low income → low saving → low investment → low productivity → low income. Education, healthcare, investment and lower population growth can each break the cycle. No single measure always works best. Averages hide inequality.

## Quick self-test

1. A country has immigration of 25,000 and emigration of 40,000. Find net migration.
2. A town of 300,000 people has 6,000 births. Find the birth rate.
3. A country of 450,000 people has 2,700 deaths. Find the death rate.
4. Give one reason a rich country can have a higher death rate than a poorer one.
5. A country has 20% aged 0-14, 70% aged 15-64 and 10% aged 65+. Find the dependency ratio, to 1 decimal place.
6. Output is 48 billion units and there are 8 million workers. Find output per worker.
7. The same output of 48 billion units is shared among 12 million people. Find output per head.
8. Primary workers are 8% and secondary workers 24%. What is the tertiary share?
9. Define optimum population.
10. State one reason low saving keeps income low.
11. GDP grows 4% and population 3%. Find the growth in output per head to 2 decimal places.

### Answers

1. 25,000 − 40,000 = **−15,000** (net emigration).
2. 6,000 ÷ 300,000 × 1,000 = **20 per 1,000**.
3. 2,700 ÷ 450,000 × 1,000 = **6 per 1,000**.
4. A large share of elderly people raises deaths even where healthcare is good.
5. (20 + 10) ÷ 70 × 100 = **42.9**.
6. 48 billion ÷ 8 million = **6,000 units**.
7. 48 billion ÷ 12 million = **4,000 units**.
8. 100 − 8 − 24 = **68%**.
9. The population size that gives the highest output per head, given the country's resources and technology.
10. Low saving funds little investment, so capital and productivity stay low.
11. 1.04 ÷ 1.03 − 1 = **0.97%**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using a count where a rate is asked, or leaving out "per 1,000".
- Ignoring emigration, or subtracting in the wrong order.
- Saying "more people means more output" without comparing with resources and capital.
- Naming the optimum as the largest population, not the highest output per head.
- Describing a pyramid without reading all three parts: base, middle and top.
- Giving a cause without the impact on income, productivity or living standards.
- Writing an evaluation that only repeats the points made earlier, with no judgement or condition.
- Calling a fast-growing GDP a rise in living standards when population grew just as fast.
