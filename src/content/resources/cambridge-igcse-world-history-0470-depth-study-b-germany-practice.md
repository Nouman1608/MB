These questions cover all the key questions of Depth Study B: Germany, 1918-45.

## Section A

**1.** Give **three** weaknesses of the Weimar Constitution. **[3]**

**2.** State **two** threats to the Republic from the left in 1919. **[2]**

**3.** Identify **three** foreign policy achievements of Stresemann. **[3]**

**4.** Give **three** ideas in the Nazi Party's programme of the 1920s. **[3]**

**5.** State **two** ways in which the Nazis controlled the churches. **[2]**

**6.** Identify **three** ways in which the Nazis used the radio and cinema. **[3]**

**7.** Give **two** examples of opposition to the Nazis from young people. **[2]**

## Section B

**8.** Describe the Munich Putsch of 1923. **[4]**

**9.** Describe how the Nazis tried to control young people through education. **[4]**

**10.** Why did the Nazis have little success before 1930? Explain your answer. **[6]**

**11.** Why did life for German civilians become harder after 1942? Explain your answer. **[6]**

**12.** "Nazi Germany was a totalitarian state." How far do you agree? Explain your answer. **[10]**

**13.** "Nazi policies towards women and the family were successful." How far do you agree? Explain your answer.
**[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Any three, 1 mark each: proportional representation produced many small parties (1); no party won a majority,
so governments were unstable coalitions (1); Article 48 let the president rule by decree (1); the president was
powerful and could appoint and dismiss the chancellor (1); the constitution allowed anti-democratic parties to
compete freely (1). **[3]**

**2.** Any two, 1 mark each: the Spartacist rising in Berlin in January 1919 (1); the Bavarian Soviet Republic in
Munich in April 1919 (1); workers' and soldiers' councils demanding a socialist republic (1); strikes organised by the
communists (1). **[2]**

**3.** Any three, 1 mark each: the Dawes Plan (1924) rescheduled reparations (1); the Locarno Treaties (1925)
accepted Germany's western borders (1); Germany joined the League of Nations in 1926 with a permanent seat on the
Council (1); the Kellogg-Briand Pact (1928) (1); the Young Plan (1929) reduced reparations (1); Allied troops agreed to
leave the Rhineland early (1). **[3]**

**4.** Any three, 1 mark each: abolish the Treaty of Versailles (1); unite all German-speaking people (1); gain land
for living space (1); only those of German blood could be citizens, so Jews were excluded (1); a strong central
government (1); nationalisation of large businesses or profit-sharing (1). **[3]**

**5.** Any two, 1 mark each: the Concordat of 1933 with the Catholic Church (1); setting up a Reich Church under a
Nazi-approved bishop (1); arresting pastors such as Niemöller (1); closing church youth groups and schools (1). **[2]**

**6.** Any three, 1 mark each: cheap People's Receivers so most homes could hear broadcasts (1); loudspeakers in
streets and workplaces (1); Hitler's speeches broadcast (1); newsreels before films in cinemas (1); propaganda films
such as Riefenstahl's *Triumph of the Will* (1); anti-Semitic films (1); censorship of foreign music and news (1).
**[3]**

**7.** Any two, 1 mark each: the Swing Youth listened to banned jazz and dressed in American styles (1); the Edelweiss
Pirates fought Hitler Youth patrols and some helped deserters (1); the White Rose students distributed anti-Nazi
leaflets (1); many young people avoided Hitler Youth activities (1). **[2]**

**8.** Any four, 1 mark each: on 8 November 1923 Hitler and the SA burst into a meeting in a Munich beer hall (1);
Hitler forced the Bavarian leader Kahr to agree to support a march on Berlin (1); Kahr withdrew his support once
released (1); on 9 November the Nazis marched through Munich with Ludendorff (1); the police opened fire and 16 Nazis
were killed (1); Hitler fled but was arrested (1); he was sentenced to five years and served less than one (1). **[4]**

**9.** Any four, 1 mark each: teachers had to join the Nazi Teachers' League and could be dismissed if disloyal (1);
textbooks were rewritten (1); history taught German greatness and the injustice of Versailles (1); race studies
taught Aryan superiority and anti-Semitism (1); more time for physical education (1); girls were taught domestic
skills (1); Jewish teachers were removed and later Jewish children excluded (1); elite schools trained future
leaders (1). **[4]**

**10.** Levels. Level 4 (6): explains two reasons. Level 3 (4-5): explains one reason; 5 for a developed explanation
with specific support. Level 2 (2-3): identifies or describes reasons. Level 1 (1): general comment. Indicative
content: **Weimar's recovery**, because the Rentenmark, Dawes Plan and American loans brought prosperity after 1924,
so fewer voters wanted extreme solutions, and the Nazis won only 12 seats in 1928; **the failure of the Munich
Putsch**, because Hitler was in prison and the party was banned and split during 1924; **Stresemann's success abroad**,
because Locarno and League membership weakened the Nazi complaint about national humiliation; **limited appeal**,
because until the Depression Nazi ideas attracted only a small fringe, mainly in rural and Protestant areas. **[6]**

**11.** Levels as for Question 10. Indicative content: **total war**, because after Stalingrad Goebbels declared total
war in February 1943, so shops and businesses not needed for the war were closed and working hours rose;
**bombing**, because Allied raids destroyed cities such as Hamburg (1943), killing many thousands and making millions
homeless; **shortages**, because rations were cut as the war turned against Germany; **the labour demands of war**,
because Speer's drive for armaments needed women and foreign forced labour; **increased terror**, because defeatism
was punished more harshly by the Gestapo and People's Courts. **[6]**

**12.** Levels. Level 5 (10): explains both sides and reaches a supported judgement on "how far". Level 4 (7-9):
explains both sides (7 for one explanation on each side, 1 more for each additional explanation). Level 3 (4-6):
explains one side. Level 2 (2-3): identifies or describes. Level 1 (1): general comment. Indicative content.
**Agree:** a one-party state after July 1933; the SS, Gestapo and camps removed opponents; propaganda and censorship
controlled the media and culture; youth movements, the DAF and Strength Through Joy reached into private life; the
army swore loyalty to Hitler personally. **Disagree:** opposition survived, from the churches (Galen's protest of
1941), youth groups and the White Rose to the July 1944 bomb plot; the Gestapo was small and relied on informers,
which means many Germans co-operated willingly rather than being forced; Nazi leaders competed with each other, so
government was often chaotic; the Catholic Church kept some independence. **Judgement:** for example, Nazi Germany
aimed at total control and came close in many areas, but private nonconformity and the reliance on consent show it
was not completely totalitarian. **[10]**

**13.** Levels as for Question 12. Indicative content. **Agree:** marriage loans and the Mother's Cross were popular;
the number of marriages rose in the early years; many women supported the regime and joined Nazi women's
organisations; women were pushed out of professions such as law and medicine, as the Nazis intended. **Disagree:** the
birth rate rose, but partly because of economic recovery, and not as far as the Nazis hoped; the policy of keeping
women at home was reversed when workers were needed for rearmament and war; many women resented losing careers; the
Hitler Youth took children's time away from the family the policies claimed to protect; the policies excluded Jewish
and other "undesirable" families entirely. **Judgement:** for
example, the policies achieved some aims in the 1930s, but the needs of rearmament and war undermined them, so they
were only partly successful. **[10]**
