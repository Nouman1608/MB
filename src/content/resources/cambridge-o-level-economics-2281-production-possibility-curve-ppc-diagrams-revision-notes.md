For full explanations and worked examples, use the [study guide on PPC diagrams](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-production-possibility-curve-ppc-diagrams/). ## Key terms

| Term | Definition to learn |
|---|---|
| Production possibility curve | The maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce with all resources fully and efficiently used and current technology |
| Productive capacity | The maximum output an economy can produce |
| Opportunity cost | The next best alternative forgone; on a PPC, the amount of one good given up for more of the other |
| Increasing opportunity cost | Each extra unit of a good costs more of the other good; gives a curve bowed outwards |
| Constant opportunity cost | Each extra unit costs the same; gives a straight-line PPC |
| Capital goods | Man-made goods used to produce other goods, such as machines |
| Consumer goods | Goods bought by households for their own use |

## Drawing checklist

- Both axes labelled with the named good and its units.
- Even scales starting at zero.
- Points plotted accurately and joined with a smooth curve.
- The curve labelled (PPC, or PPC1 and PPC2 for a shift).
- Any points named in the question marked and labelled.

## Shape

- **Bowed outwards (concave):** resources are not equally suited to both goods, so opportunity cost rises as more of one good is made.
- **Straight line:** resources equally suited, so opportunity cost is constant.

**Worked reminder: straight-line PPC.** An island (invented) can make 60 boats or 120 huts, and every boat uses the same resources as 2 huts. Opportunity cost of 1 boat = 120 ÷ 60 = **2 huts** at every point. The curve is a straight line from (0 boats, 120 huts) to (60 boats, 0 huts).

## Location of points

| Point | Meaning | Typical cause |
|---|---|---|
| On the curve | Full and efficient use of resources | — |
| Inside the curve | Unemployed or inefficiently used resources | Recession, unemployment, poor organisation |
| Outside the curve | Unattainable now | Needs more or better resources or technology |

## Movements along the curve

Opportunity cost of extra X = amount of Y given up. Per unit: Y given up ÷ X gained.

**Worked reminder.** An economy (invented) produces food and clothing.

| Point | Food (million units) | Clothing (million units) |
|---|---|---|
| P | 100 | 0 |
| Q | 90 | 20 |
| R | 70 | 40 |
| S | 40 | 60 |
| T | 0 | 80 |

- Q to R: clothing +20, food −20 → OC of 1 unit of clothing = **1 food**.
- R to S: clothing +20, food −30 → OC = **1.5 food**.
- S to T: clothing +20, food −40 → OC = **2 food**.
- Rising opportunity cost confirms a curve bowed outwards.

A point at (70 food, 25 clothing) lies inside the curve, because R shows 40 clothing is possible with 70 food. Moving from there to R gains 15 million units of clothing at no opportunity cost.

## Shifts

**Outward shift: causes**

- More labour (population growth, net immigration, higher retirement age).
- Better labour (education, training, healthcare).
- More capital (investment) or better capital (new technology).
- New natural resources discovered.
- Better enterprise or organisation.

**Inward shift: causes**

- Natural disasters or war destroying capital and land.
- Large-scale emigration.
- Depletion of natural resources.

**One-good shift (pivot):** new technology in one industry only moves the end of the curve on that good's axis; the other end stays put.

**Consequences**

- Outward shift → higher productive capacity → potential for more output, incomes and living standards.
- The potential is realised only if resources are fully used (the economy produces on the new curve).
- Choosing more capital goods now means fewer consumer goods today but a larger outward shift in future.

## Must-know distinctions

- Movement along the curve (reallocation, with opportunity cost) versus shift of the curve (change in productive capacity).
- Moving from inside to on the curve (using spare resources) versus an outward shift (more resources or better technology).
- Inside the curve = inefficient or unemployed resources; outside = unattainable.
- Opportunity cost measured in goods, not money.

## Exam traps

- Labelling axes "Good X" when the question names the goods.
- Joining points with straight lines when the curve should be smooth.
- Inverting the opportunity cost ratio.
- Calling a point outside the curve inefficient.
- Saying a fall in unemployment shifts the PPC outwards. It moves the economy towards the existing curve.
- Forgetting to give the consequence of a shift, not just its cause.

## Quick self-test

1. Define a production possibility curve.
2. What does a point inside the curve show?
3. What does a point outside the curve show?
4. Why is a PPC usually bowed outwards?
5. What shape is a PPC with constant opportunity cost?
6. Using the food and clothing table, state the opportunity cost of moving from R to S.
7. Give **two** causes of an outward shift of a PPC.
8. Give **one** cause of an inward shift.
9. New technology improves only the production of clothing. Describe how the PPC changes.
10. Why might producing more capital goods now lead to faster growth?
11. Unemployment falls in a recession recovery. Does the PPC shift? Explain.
12. What is the opportunity cost of moving from a point inside the curve to a point on it, if neither good falls?

### Answers

1. The maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce when all resources are fully and efficiently used, with current technology.
2. Unemployed or inefficiently used resources.
3. A combination not attainable with current resources and technology.
4. Resources are not equally suited to both goods, so opportunity cost rises.
5. A straight line.
6. 30 million units of food for 20 million units of clothing, so 1.5 units of food per unit of clothing.
7. Any two: more labour; better-trained labour; investment in capital; new technology; discovery of resources.
8. Any one: natural disaster; war; large-scale emigration; depletion of resources.
9. The end on the clothing axis moves outwards; the end on the food axis stays the same.
10. More capital raises productive capacity, so the PPC shifts outwards further in future.
11. No. The economy moves from inside the curve towards it; productive capacity is unchanged.
12. None: unused resources are brought into production, so nothing is given up.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does an outward shift of the PPC always mean living standards rise?**
Not automatically. It shows that more can be produced, but output rises only if resources are fully used, and living standards also depend on how output is shared.

**How do I show economic growth on a PPC?**
Draw a second curve outside the first, label it PPC2, and explain the cause using the quantity or quality of the factors of production.
