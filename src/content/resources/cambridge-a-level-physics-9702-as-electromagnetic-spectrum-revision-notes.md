Condensed notes on subtopic 7.4 Electromagnetic spectrum (Cambridge 9702, AS
Topic 7). For full explanations and worked examples, use the
[electromagnetic spectrum study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-as-electromagnetic-spectrum/);
for the whole topic, see the [Waves revision notes](/resources/as-physics-waves-revision-notes/).

## What 7.4 asks you to do

- State that all electromagnetic waves are **transverse** and travel at the **same speed c in free space**
- Recall approximate free-space wavelength ranges from radio waves to γ-rays
- Recall that **400–700 nm** in free space is visible to the human eye

## The two shared properties

> All electromagnetic waves are **transverse** waves, and all travel at the **same speed c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ in free space**.

Supporting facts:

- Oscillating electric and magnetic fields, perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel.
- No medium needed; they travel through a vacuum.
- Can be polarised, because they are transverse.

## Approximate wavelengths (free space)

| Region | Order-of-magnitude wavelength |
|---|---|
| Radio | > 10⁻¹ m (up to km) |
| Microwave | 10⁻³ m to 10⁻¹ m |
| Infrared | 7 × 10⁻⁷ m to 10⁻³ m |
| Visible | 4 × 10⁻⁷ m to 7 × 10⁻⁷ m |
| Ultraviolet | 10⁻⁸ m to 4 × 10⁻⁷ m |
| X-ray | 10⁻¹³ m to 10⁻⁸ m |
| γ-ray | < 10⁻¹⁰ m |

- Boundaries are approximate; learn powers of ten.
- X-ray and γ-ray ranges **overlap**; the name usually reflects the origin (γ-rays from nuclei).
- Wavelength decreases down the table; frequency increases.

## The visible band

- 400 nm (violet) to 700 nm (red), in free space.
- Red: longest visible wavelength, lowest visible frequency.
- Violet: shortest visible wavelength, highest visible frequency.
- Just beyond red: infrared. Just beyond violet: ultraviolet.

## Calculations: c = fλ

```
f = c / lambda          lambda = c / f          c = 3.00 x 10^8 m s^-1
```

**Visible band in frequency:**

```
700 nm:  f = 3.00 x 10^8 / 7.00 x 10^-7 = 4.3 x 10^14 Hz
400 nm:  f = 3.00 x 10^8 / 4.00 x 10^-7 = 7.5 x 10^14 Hz
```

**Example — identify the region.** f = 6.0 × 10¹⁰ Hz → λ = 3.00 × 10⁸ ÷ 6.0 × 10¹⁰ = 5.0 × 10⁻³ m → **microwave**.

**Example — radio.** A station broadcasts at 200 kHz → λ = 3.00 × 10⁸ ÷ 2.0 × 10⁵ = **1500 m** → radio.

**Example — boundary frequency.** The microwave–infrared boundary at about 1 mm corresponds to f = 3.00 × 10⁸ ÷ 1.0 × 10⁻³ = **3 × 10¹¹ Hz**.

**Example — X-rays.** Radiation of wavelength 0.20 nm: λ = 2.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ m, f = 3.00 × 10⁸ ÷ 2.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ = **1.5 × 10¹⁸ Hz**, in the X-ray range.

## Order-of-magnitude anchors

One memorable wavelength per region makes the ranges easier to recall:

- Radio: metres to kilometres (a 100 MHz broadcast has λ = 3.0 m).
- Microwave: centimetres (a 2.45 GHz oven: about 12 cm).
- Infrared: micrometres (about 10 μm for radiation from objects near room temperature).
- Visible: hundreds of nanometres (green about 550 nm).
- Ultraviolet: tens to hundreds of nanometres.
- X-ray: around 10⁻¹⁰ m, the spacing of atoms in a solid.
- γ-ray: 10⁻¹² m and shorter.

## Entering a material

| Quantity | What happens |
|---|---|
| Speed | Decreases below c |
| Frequency | **Unchanged** (fixed by the source) |
| Wavelength | Decreases |

That is why the syllabus specifies wavelengths "in free space".

## Unit conversions

- 1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m; 1 μm = 10⁻⁶ m; 1 pm = 10⁻¹² m.
- 1 kHz = 10³ Hz; 1 MHz = 10⁶ Hz; 1 GHz = 10⁹ Hz.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning to recall |
|---|---|
| Electromagnetic wave | Transverse wave of oscillating electric and magnetic fields |
| Free space | A vacuum, where all EM waves travel at c |
| Speed of light c | 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ in free space |
| Visible spectrum | Wavelengths 400–700 nm in free space |
| Principal regions | Radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-ray, γ-ray |

## Exam traps

- Only one of the two shared properties stated.
- Visible range given in μm or mm instead of nm.
- Order of regions reversed when the question asks for **increasing frequency**.
- "EM waves travel at c in glass" — only in free space.
- Assuming wavelength stays the same in a new medium; it is frequency that stays the same.
- Region identified from frequency without converting to wavelength, then compared with a wavelength table.

## Self-test

1. State two properties shared by all electromagnetic waves.
2. List the principal regions in order of increasing frequency.
3. State the range of visible wavelengths in free space.
4. Give an approximate wavelength for microwaves.
5. Find the frequency of light of wavelength 600 nm.
6. Find the wavelength of a 90 MHz radio signal.
7. Which region(s) could radiation of wavelength 5 × 10⁻¹¹ m belong to?
8. Name the region with wavelength 10 μm.
9. Light passes from air into glass. State what happens to its speed, frequency and wavelength.
10. Which has the longer wavelength, red or violet light?
11. Identify the region of radiation with frequency 1.0 × 10¹⁰ Hz.
12. Why are the wavelength ranges quoted for free space?

**Answers:**
1. All are transverse; all travel at the same speed c in free space.
2. Radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-ray, γ-ray.
3. **400–700 nm**.
4. About 10⁻² m (anything from about 1 mm to 10 cm).
5. 3.00 × 10⁸ ÷ 6.00 × 10⁻⁷ = **5.0 × 10¹⁴ Hz**.
6. 3.00 × 10⁸ ÷ 9.0 × 10⁷ = **3.3 m**.
7. **X-ray or γ-ray** (the ranges overlap).
8. **Infrared**.
9. Speed decreases; frequency unchanged; wavelength decreases.
10. **Red**.
11. λ = 0.030 m: **microwave**.
12. The speed, and so the wavelength, is smaller in a material, while the frequency is unchanged; free-space values are unambiguous.
