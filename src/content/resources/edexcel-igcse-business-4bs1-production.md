Point 5.2.1 is production processes: the different types (job, batch, flow); labour and capital intensive production; the impact of different types of production; and the calculation of productivity and the impact of productivity improvements. Point 5.2.3 is the impact of technology in production: the use of robotics, and balancing cost, productivity, quality and flexibility.

## Job, batch and flow: what decides the choice

| | Job | Batch | Flow |
|---|---|---|---|
| What is made | One-off items to order | Groups of identical items, then switch | A continuous stream of identical items |
| Volume | Very low | Medium | Very high |
| Variety | Highest | Some | Very little |
| Set-up and equipment cost | Low | Medium, plus time lost changing over | Very high |
| Cost per unit | Highest | Middle | Lowest at high volume |
| Typical labour | Skilled, varied tasks | Semi-skilled | Repetitive tasks or machine minding |

The deciding factor is **demand**: how many identical items customers want, and how much they value something made specially.

### Worked example 1: when does flow production pay?

Brennock Tables could make dining tables in three ways (cost figures exclude premises, which are the same for all three).

- **Job**: each table made individually, costing 380 in materials and skilled labour.
- **Batch**: runs of 50 identical tables. Materials and labour cost 230 per table, and each run needs 2,000 to set up machines. Cost per table = 230 + 2,000 ÷ 50 = **270**.
- **Flow**: an assembly line costing 600,000 a year to run, with materials and labour of 150 per table. Cost per table = 150 + 600,000 ÷ output.

| Tables a year | Flow cost per table |
|---|---|
| 2,000 | 150 + 300 = **450** |
| 5,000 | 150 + 120 = **270** |
| 10,000 | 150 + 60 = **210** |

At 2,000 tables, flow is dearer even than job production. At 5,000 it matches batch. Only at around 10,000 a year does it become clearly cheapest. A business that sells a few thousand tables in many designs should use batch production; a flow line only makes sense for high, steady demand for one design.

### The impact of each type

- **On customers**: job production gives personal products but high prices and longer waits; flow gives low prices but little choice.
- **On workers**: job work can be satisfying and skilled; flow work can be repetitive, which can lower motivation.
- **On the business**: flow brings low unit costs but high risk, because the line is costly and inflexible; job production is flexible but limits growth.

Labour-intensive production (more workers relative to machines) is common in job production; capital-intensive production (more machinery relative to workers) is typical of flow. The difference is developed in the [factors of production study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-factors-of-production/).

## Productivity: calculating it properly

The specification asks for the calculation of productivity and the impact of improvements. It gives no formula; the standard form is:

**Labour productivity = output ÷ number of workers** (or ÷ number of worker-hours)

Always state the unit and the period, such as "boxes per worker-hour".

### Worked example 2: productivity and unit labour cost

Rillington Packaging has 24 workers on an 8-hour shift, packing 5,760 boxes.

- Worker-hours = 24 × 8 = 192.
- Productivity = 5,760 ÷ 192 = **30 boxes per worker-hour**.

After Kaizen suggestions rearrange the packing benches, the same team packs 6,480 boxes per shift.

- Productivity = 6,480 ÷ 192 = **33.75 boxes per worker-hour**.
- Increase = (33.75 − 30) ÷ 30 × 100 = **12.5%**.

Impact: workers are paid 13 an hour. Labour cost per box falls from 13 ÷ 30 = **0.43** to 13 ÷ 33.75 = **0.39** (to 2 decimal places). Lower unit costs allow lower prices or higher profit, and the business can meet more orders without hiring. A full answer also checks whether quality held up, because faster work that causes errors is not a real improvement.

## Lean production in more depth

Lean production means making what customers want while wasting as little time, materials, space and money as possible.

### Just-in-time (JIT)

Materials arrive just when they are needed and finished goods leave quickly, so little inventory is held.

**Worked example 3.** Rillington holds an average of 40,000 of packaging materials. Holding inventory (storage space, insurance, damage) costs about 20% of its value a year: 0.20 × 40,000 = **8,000**. Under JIT, average inventory falls to 10,000, costing 0.20 × 10,000 = **2,000**. Saving: **6,000 a year**, and 30,000 of cash is no longer tied up in stock.

The risk: if one delivery is late, packing stops. If a lost day costs 4,500 in lost contribution, two late deliveries a year would wipe out most of the saving. JIT therefore needs reliable suppliers, good communication and accurate demand forecasts.

### Kaizen

Kaizen means continuous improvement through many small changes, often suggested by the workers who do the job. It works because the people closest to the work see waste that managers miss. It needs time for team meetings, managers who act on suggestions and a culture where staff are not blamed for raising problems. Each change is small, so the benefit builds up over time rather than all at once.

### Using resources effectively

Every wasted resource raises cost per unit: materials scrapped, machines standing idle, energy left running, workers waiting for parts, space filled with unused stock. Using resources effectively keeps unit costs low, frees cash and reduces environmental impact, which can matter to customers.

## Technology and robotics: balancing four factors

The specification names four factors to balance.

| Factor | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| Cost | Does the saving in labour and errors outweigh the purchase, installation and maintenance cost? |
| Productivity | How much more output per worker or per hour? |
| Quality | Will output be more consistent, with fewer defects? |
| Flexibility | Can the robot switch tasks or products when demand changes? |

### Worked example 4: should a robot replace a packing team?

A packing robot costs 180,000. It would replace 3 workers each paid 28,000 a year and costs 12,000 a year to maintain. It would also cut the defect rate on 50,000 parcels a year from 3% to 0.5%, and each defect costs 6 to put right.

- Yearly labour saving after maintenance: 3 × 28,000 − 12,000 = **72,000**.
- Time to recover the purchase cost from this saving: 180,000 ÷ 72,000 = **2.5 years**.
- Defect cost before: 0.03 × 50,000 × 6 = 9,000. After: 0.005 × 50,000 × 6 = 1,500. Extra saving: **7,500 a year**.

Cost, productivity and quality all point towards the robot. Flexibility may not: if the business packs many different parcel shapes, or demand is uncertain, a robot that is costly to reprogramme could stand idle, while people adapt easily. The decision should also weigh the effect on the three workers, who could be retrained for other roles.

## Annotated model answer

*Justify whether Brennock Tables should use batch or flow production if it expects to sell 5,000 tables a year in six designs.*

> At 5,000 tables a year, batch and flow both cost about 270 a table, so cost does not decide it. Flow needs a 600,000 line that makes one standard design efficiently, but Brennock sells six designs, and changing a flow line is slow and costly. Batch production lets it make runs of each design and switch between them. Flow becomes cheaper only above 5,000 tables, and if sales fall, the fixed cost of the line would raise cost per table sharply. Brennock should choose batch production, because it gives the same unit cost with more flexibility and much lower risk.

## Common mistakes

- Calling job production "cheap because it is small". It usually has the highest unit cost.
- Calculating productivity as output ÷ cost.
- Saying JIT means "no stock" with no mention of supplier risk.
- Treating Kaizen as a single big change.
- Weighing robotics on cost alone and ignoring quality and flexibility.
