This guide covers **Readers, Writers and Texts**, one of three
syllabus components for IB Diploma Programme Language A: Language and
Literature, first assessment 2021. The [full syllabus
guide](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-language-and-literature-syllabus-guide/)
confirms all three components carry equal teaching hours (50 SL / 80
HL each), and that this component establishes the analytical
vocabulary the course's other two components -- [Time and
Space](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-language-literature-time-and-space/)
and Intertextuality -- then apply to more specific contexts and
comparisons.

## Where this fits in the syllabus

Note that this subject, Language A: Language and Literature, is a
separate IB Diploma Programme course from [Language A:
Literature](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-literature-readers-writers-texts/),
even though both study a component with the same name. Language A:
Literature studies only literary texts; Language A: Language and
Literature studies both literary and non-literary texts side by side,
and this dual scope shapes how Readers, Writers and Texts is taught
and examined here specifically -- every technique in this guide is
revised in a literary application and a non-literary application, not
one or the other.

## The core question this component asks

How do a writer's choices of language, form and structure shape
meaning, and how does a reader actively construct interpretation
rather than passively receive it? This is a two-part question, not
one: first, identify a specific choice the writer or producer of a
text has made (word choice, sentence structure, the structure of the
whole piece, visual layout); second, explain the effect that choice
creates and why a reader responds to it in a particular way. Stopping
at the first part -- naming a technique without explaining its effect
-- is the single most common way this component's marks are lost.

## A shared analytical toolkit, applied to two kinds of text

Because this course studies both literary and non-literary material,
revise this component's techniques in pairs -- one literary
application, one non-literary application -- rather than only in the
literary or only in the non-literary direction. Narrative and
structural choices might mean chronological order versus flashback in
a literary text, or which fact is placed in the headline versus buried
in the final paragraph of a news report. Tone and register might be
built through word connotation and rhythm in a poem, or through direct
address ("you") and informal phrasing to create intimacy with an
audience in an advertisement. Visual and spatial elements are relevant
to both a graphic novel's panel layout and a website or advert's use
of image, white space and typography to guide the eye and imply
meaning beyond the words alone. Audience and purpose shape every text,
literary or non-literary -- persuade, inform, move, entertain --
and identifying purpose early in an analysis focuses everything that
follows.

## Worked example: from technique-spotting to effect

Take a short opening line from a hypothetical opinion column: "It is,
of course, entirely reasonable to assume that no one in government has
ever read a bus timetable." A technique-spotting answer might simply
note "the writer uses irony." A stronger answer identifies the
specific choice -- the ironic hedging phrase "of course, entirely
reasonable" paired with a deliberately mundane, almost absurd example
-- and explains its effect: it invites the reader to share the
writer's implied scepticism without stating a direct accusation,
building a conspiratorial tone between writer and reader before the
argument proper has even begun. This explanation connects the specific
words used to a concrete effect on the reader, rather than stopping at
the label "irony," and is exactly the difference examiners are trained
to reward.

## Building an analysis, step by step

Identify the text type and its likely purpose and audience before
analysing detail, since this framing shapes which techniques are
actually relevant to look for. Identify two or three specific,
quotable choices of language, form or structure. For each choice,
explain the effect it creates, connecting back to purpose and audience
rather than listing techniques with no explanation of effect. Where
relevant, comment on how confidently or ambiguously a reader can
settle on one meaning, since texts are not always designed to produce
a single, fixed interpretation.

## Common exam pitfalls

Technique-spotting without effect -- naming a device (metaphor,
alliteration, a pull-quote, a bold headline) without explaining what it
actually does to meaning or reader response -- scores far lower than a
shorter analysis that properly connects device to effect. Treating
non-literary texts as "easier" or less worthy of close reading is a
particular risk in this course: because Paper 1's guided textual
analysis is set on unseen non-literary passages specifically,
under-preparing this side of the course leaves a real gap on the paper
that is, structurally, the clearest signal of how central non-literary
analysis is here. Ignoring visual and spatial elements is another
common gap -- for multimodal texts (adverts, webpages, graphic
narratives), analysis that discusses only the words and ignores
layout, image and typography misses half of how such texts actually
communicate.

## How to approach it

Practise applying the same close-reading questions to both text types
every time you revise this component, rather than defaulting to
whichever type feels more familiar. Build a habit of naming the
specific choice and its effect in the same sentence, rather than
listing techniques first and explaining effect afterwards as an
afterthought. Because Readers, Writers and Texts is not tested as a
separate, named section on either paper, its vocabulary should feel
fluent and automatic by the time Paper 1's unseen passage or Paper 2's
comparative essay requires it under timed conditions.
