## Questions

**1.** State which component of the current account each item belongs to, and whether it is a credit or a debit for Country S. (i) Country S sells software licences to a foreign firm. (ii) Dividends are paid by a Country S firm to overseas shareholders. (iii) Workers in Country S send money to families abroad. (iv) Country S imports vehicles. **[4]**

**2.** Country S, one illustrative year. Goods: credits 88, debits 97. Services: credits 45, debits 31. Primary income: credits 12, debits 20. Secondary income: credits 14, debits 6. GDP is 250.

**(a)** Calculate the balance of trade in goods. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the balance of trade in services. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the balance of trade in goods and services. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the current account balance and state whether it is a deficit or a surplus. **[2]**
**(e)** Express the current account balance as a percentage of GDP. **[2]**

**3.** Explain two causes of a current account deficit. **[4]**

**4.** Country T has goods imports of 220 and goods exports of 200. Its income rises by 4%, and the income elasticity of demand for its imports is 1.5. Incomes in its partners rise by 2.5%, and the income elasticity of demand for its exports is 0.8.

**(a)** Calculate the new value of goods imports. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the new value of goods exports. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the new balance of trade in goods and the change from the original. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why growth at home can worsen the current account. **[2]**

**5.** Explain two consequences of a persistent current account deficit for the domestic economy and one for the external economy. **[6]**

**6.** Explain one benefit and one problem of a large persistent current account surplus. **[4]**

**7.** Explain two reasons why a government may seek stability of the current account. **[4]**

**8.** In Country U the marginal propensity to save is 0.15, the marginal propensity to be taxed is 0.20 and the marginal propensity to import is 0.15. The government cuts spending by 8 to improve the current account.

**(a)** Calculate the value of the multiplier. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the fall in national income. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the fall in imports. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain two reasons why the current account may improve by less than expected. **[2]**

**9.** Explain how supply-side policy could improve a country's current account. **[5]**

**10.** An imported good costs 60 cu a unit and 400 units are bought each year. The government imposes a tariff of 25%. The price elasticity of demand for the good is −0.4.

**(a)** Calculate the new price to buyers and the new quantity. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the change in payments to foreign suppliers. **[2]**
**(c)** Evaluate whether the tariff is likely to correct a current account deficit. **[5]**

**11.** Country V has a current account deficit that comes from rapid growth in consumer spending financed by cheap credit. Evaluate the use of higher interest rates to reduce the deficit. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** (i) Trade in services, a credit [1]. (ii) Primary income, a debit [1]. (iii) Secondary income, a debit [1]. (iv) Trade in goods, a debit [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Give both the component and the direction. Income from investment is primary income, but a transfer with nothing in return is secondary income.

**2. (a)** 88 − 97 = **−9** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 45 − 31 = **+14** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** −9 + 14 = **+5** [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** Primary income is 12 − 20 = −8 and secondary income is 14 − 6 = +8 [1]. CAB = 5 − 8 + 8 = **+5 bn cu, a surplus** [1]. **[2]**
**(e)** 5 ÷ 250 × 100 [1] = **2.0%** of GDP [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show each line before the total, and keep the signs. A correct method is easier to reward when the working is visible.

**3.** Cause one: faster growth at home than abroad raises incomes and so import spending [1], which widens the deficit if imports are income elastic [1]. Cause two: higher costs or inflation than rivals make exports dearer abroad and imports cheaper at home [1], so exports fall and imports rise [1]. Other valid causes: weak foreign demand, an overvalued currency, a fall in a main export price, low quality. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A cause needs its effect on exports, imports or income flows. A bare list of causes usually scores less than two developed ones.

**4. (a)** Imports rise 1.5 × 4 = 6% [1], so 220 × 1.06 = **233.2** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Exports rise 0.8 × 2.5 = 2%, so 200 × 1.02 = **204** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** New balance = 204 − 233.2 = **−29.2** [1]. Original balance = −20, so the balance worsens by **9.2** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Higher income raises spending on imports [1]. Because the income elasticity of imports (1.5) exceeds that of exports (0.8), imports grow faster than exports and the gap widens [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* State both elasticities and compare them. A growth-and-imports answer with no comparison stops at description.

**5.** Domestic one: net exports are negative, so aggregate demand is lower than it would be, which can slow growth [1] and cost jobs in export and import-competing firms [1]. Domestic two: pressure on the currency may push it down, raising import prices and costs, which can push up inflation [1]. Support: the effect is larger if the deficit is large or lasts [1]. External: partners with surpluses gain demand for their exports [1], and the deficit must be financed by borrowing, asset sales or reserves, so foreign ownership or debt rises [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Say whether each consequence is for the domestic or external economy. Judge size and duration, because a small, temporary deficit may not matter.

**6.** Benefit: net exports add to aggregate demand, so output and employment may rise [1], and the country can build reserves or lend abroad [1]. Problem: the surplus may reflect weak domestic demand and low consumption [1], or may provoke trading partners with matching deficits to use protection [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Show you know a surplus is not automatically good. Two developed points beat four one-word items.

**7.** Reason one: a large persistent deficit needs finance from abroad [1], and lenders may withdraw or ask for higher returns, which creates instability [1]. Reason two: a falling currency raises import prices and can feed inflation [1]. Stable external trade also supports growth and employment [1]. A large surplus is a further reason, since it can invite retaliation. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The objective is stability, not a balance of exactly zero. Saying so avoids a common slip.

**8. (a)** Withdrawals = 0.15 + 0.20 + 0.15 = 0.50 [1]. Multiplier = 1 ÷ 0.50 = **2** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Income falls 8 × 2 = **16** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Imports fall 0.15 × 16 = **2.4** [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** Exports may fall if trading partners slow at the same time [1]. Households may cut spending on home goods more than on imports, so the true mpm is lower than assumed and imports fall by less [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Part (d) asks for reasons, so state a reason and a link to the account. Naming a drawback with no effect on the account does not answer the question.

**9.** Policies such as education, training, infrastructure and research raise productivity and quality [1]. Higher productivity lowers unit costs, so export prices can fall [1]. Better quality improves non-price competitiveness [1]. Exports rise and home goods replace some imports [1]. The effect is lasting and does not cut output, but takes years [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Build the chain from policy to productivity to costs to trade. A claim that exports "go up" with no mechanism is thin.

**10. (a)** New price = 60 × 1.25 = **75 cu** [1]. Quantity falls 0.4 × 25 = 10% to **360** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Before: 400 × 60 = 24,000. After: 360 × 60 = 21,600 [1]. Payments to foreign suppliers fall by **2,400 cu** [1]. The government collects 360 × 15 = 5,400 cu, which is not part of that payment. **[2]**
**(c)** The tariff reduces the outflow, but only by 10% because demand is inelastic [1]. Buyers' outlay rises from 24,000 to 360 × 75 = 27,000 [1]. Trading partners may retaliate and cut exports [1]. Dearer imports can raise costs for firms using them [1]. It works better if demand is elastic or the deficit is caused by this good, and a judgement depends on the cause [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Separate payments to foreign suppliers from buyers' outlay. A judgement that names the condition for success is worth more than a list.

**11.** Higher rates raise borrowing costs and cut credit-fuelled spending [1], so consumption falls [1] and income falls [1]. Lower income reduces imports by the mpm times the fall in income [1], so the deficit narrows [1]. The cause is rapid consumer growth, so the policy targets the cause directly [1]. Against this, higher rates may attract foreign funds and raise the currency, making exports dearer [1]. Investment, growth and jobs may suffer, and the effect depends on how sensitive spending is to rates [1]. If the mpm is low, imports fall little [1]. Judgement: the policy suits a credit-driven import boom, but works best alongside supply-side policy if the fall in competitiveness is the lasting cause, and the balance depends on the cause, speed and the size of the exchange-rate effect [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Tie the answer to the cause given in the stem. A reasoned judgement that depends on a condition scores better than a summary of points.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving a deficit without a minus sign, or a CAB without a unit.
- Ignoring primary and secondary income, or confusing the two.
- Using the balance of goods and services as the CAB.
- Using the wrong base for a percentage of GDP.
- Quoting an elasticity without applying it to a base value.
- Treating tariff revenue as a payment abroad, or outlay as the same thing.
- Stating a policy without the link to imports, exports or income.
- Ending an evaluation with a summary and no judgement or condition.
