## Questions

**1.** Explain two reasons why the United States supported the overthrow of the Guatemalan government in 1954. **[4]**

**2.** Read the extract (constructed for practice).

> "We were promised a partnership for progress. We received speeches and a few loans, while the generals who own the land stayed where they were. When we ask for reform we are told we are communists." (Constructed for practice: a paraphrase of a Latin American trade unionist's complaint in the 1960s.)

**(a)** Identify two criticisms of US policy in the extract. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how the extract could be used to assess the Alliance for Progress, and one limitation of using it alone. **[4]**

**3.** Distinguish between the Bay of Pigs invasion and the Cuban Missile Crisis in terms of their causes. **[3]**

**4.** Analyse the domestic impact of the Vietnam War on the United States during Johnson's presidency. **[6]**

**5.** Explain the role of the United States in the events in Chile between 1970 and 1973. **[6]**

**6.** Examine the effects of the Cold War on Canada's foreign policy and its relations with the USA. **[8]**

**7.** Discuss the impact of the Cold War on one country in Latin America. **[8]**

**8.** To what extent did Jimmy Carter change US policy towards Latin America? **[8]**

**9.** Paper 3 style essay question: "Anti-communism was the main reason for US intervention in Latin America between 1945 and 1988." To what extent do you agree? **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Two developed points, 2 marks each. Economic interests: Árbenz's land reform (Decree 900) took land from the United Fruit Company [1], a US firm with links to senior officials [1]. Anti-communism: Árbenz's government included communists and was seen as a Soviet base in the hemisphere [1], at a time when the Eisenhower administration favoured covert action under the New Look [1]. Credibility: the success of Iran in 1953 suggested covert action would work cheaply [1] and avoided open war [1]. **[4]**

**2. (a)** US aid was limited and slow, with more promised than delivered [1]; US-supported elites blocked reform, and those who sought reform were labelled communists [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** It shows that, for some Latin Americans, the Alliance's promises did not match results, and that landowners resisted land reform [1], supporting the view that the Alliance achieved less than promised [1]. Limitation: it is one critic's view, perhaps from the left, and does not show what the programme achieved elsewhere, for example in health or housing [1]; the source is a paraphrase of a statement made for a purpose, so its tone may be partial [1]. **[4]**

**3.** The Bay of Pigs (1961) resulted from the US decision, under Eisenhower and carried out under Kennedy, to remove Castro by using Cuban exiles because he had nationalised US property and moved towards the USSR [1]. The Missile Crisis (1962) was caused by the Soviet decision to place missiles in Cuba, to protect Cuba and to counter US missiles in Turkey and Italy [1], after the Bay of Pigs had shown Cuba's vulnerability [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Political: Johnson escalated after the Gulf of Tonkin (1964) and the "credibility gap" grew as official claims clashed with reality [1]; he declined to run again in 1968 after the Tet Offensive [1]. Social: anti-war protests on campuses and the draft divided society [2]. Economic: war spending competed with the Great Society, and inflation grew [1]. Judgement: the war weakened trust in government and ended Johnson's presidency [1]. **[6]**

**5.** Allende, a socialist, won the 1970 election with about 37 per cent of the vote [1]. Nixon and Kissinger feared "another Cuba" [1]. The CIA tried to prevent Allende taking office, including supporting a plot in which General René Schneider was killed [1]. The US cut aid and pressured credit while supporting opponents [1]. In September 1973, a coup led by Pinochet overthrew Allende [1]. Historians disagree about direct US involvement in the coup, but the US supported the opposition and quickly recognised the military government [1]. **[6]**

**6.** NATO and NORAD: Canada was a founder of NATO and joined NORAD, tying defence to the US [2]. Peacekeeping: Pearson's Suez initiative and Canada's middle power role [2]. Tension: the Bomarc missile dispute of 1962 to 1963 and trade with Cuba; opposition to the Vietnam bombing in 1965 [2]. Anti-communism at home: the Gouzenko affair [1]. Judgement: Canada followed the US in security but kept room for independent action [1]. **[8]**

**7.** Choose Cuba. Regional: the Rio Pact and the OAS, then exclusion in 1962 [1]. Domestic political: a one-party state, repression of opponents, large military justified by US threat [2]. USA: embargo, Bay of Pigs, hostility [1]. USSR: oil, aid and sugar purchases, Comecon (1972), Cuban troops in Angola [2]; tensions after the Missile Crisis [1]. Judgement on whether the Cold War helped or limited Cuba [1]. **[8]**

**8.** Change: human rights policy, cuts in aid to some military governments [2]; the Panama Canal Treaties (1977) [2]; limited pressure on dictators such as in Argentina and Chile [1]. Continuity: the US kept ties with many military regimes, the aim of containing communism remained, and Carter changed course in 1979 to 1980 after the Sandinista victory and the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan [2]. Judgement: a shift in tone and some achievements, but limited structural change [1]. **[8]**

**9.** Use the Paper 3 markbands. A top answer (13 to 15 marks) shows in-depth understanding of the argument, evaluates a range of perspectives and reaches a substantiated judgement. Anti-communism (about 4 marks): Guatemala 1954, Dominican Republic 1965, Chile 1973, Nicaragua under Reagan, the language of containment. Other motives (about 4 marks): economic interests (United Fruit, ITT and copper in Chile), strategic interests (the Canal), domestic politics, and desire to maintain regional dominance. Perspectives (about 3 marks): orthodox interpretations stressing security; revisionist ones stressing economic and imperial interests; post-revisionist ones that stress mixed motives and local actors. Judgement (about 4 marks): for example, that anti-communism was the stated and often sincere motive but that it was bound up with economic and strategic interests, and was applied where reform threatened US interests. **[15]**
