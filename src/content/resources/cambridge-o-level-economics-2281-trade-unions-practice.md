This set tests section 3.4 **Trade unions** of the Cambridge O Level Economics 2281 syllabus for examination in 2026. All unions, firms and figures are invented. Teaching is in the [trade unions study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-trade-unions/) and the [trade unions revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-trade-unions-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define a trade union. **[2]**

**2.** What is collective bargaining?

A: A worker negotiating his own pay with a manager

B: Negotiation between a union, for its members, and an employer over pay and conditions

C: The government setting a minimum wage

D: Firms agreeing with each other to fix prices

**[1]**

**3.** Which factor is most likely to weaken a trade union?

A: A shortage of the skills its members have

B: Low unemployment in the economy

C: Members who can easily be replaced by machines

D: A highly profitable employer

**[1]**

**4.** State two forms of industrial action other than a strike. **[2]**

**5.** A union wins a 2% pay rise in a year when prices rise by 5%.

**(a)** State whether members' real wages rise or fall. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the approximate percentage change in the real wage. **[1]**

**6.** A worker earning $700 a week strikes for 4 weeks. The final settlement is 2% higher than the employer's original offer. Calculate how many weeks of the higher pay it takes to recover the pay lost during the strike. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** A factory employs 80 workers at $450 a week, producing 9,000 units a week. The union negotiates a 4% pay rise.

**(a)** Calculate labour cost per unit before the rise. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate labour cost per unit after the rise if output is unchanged. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the weekly output needed to keep labour cost per unit at its original level. **[1]**

**(d)** Explain one way the firm might respond if output does not rise. **[2]**

**8.** Explain the role of trade unions in protecting employment. **[3]**

**9.** Explain two advantages of trade unions from the viewpoint of firms. **[4]**

**10.** Explain why a union of nurses might be stronger than a union of shop assistants. **[4]**

**11.** Explain two disadvantages of trade union activity from the viewpoint of the government. **[4]**

**12.** Discuss whether trade unions benefit workers. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** An organisation of workers [1] formed to protect and promote its members' interests, such as pay, working conditions and job security [1]. **[2]**

**2.** B [1]. A is individual bargaining, C is government policy and D is collusion between firms. **[1]**

**3.** C [1]. Easily replaced members cannot put much pressure on an employer. A, B and D strengthen a union. **[1]**

**4.** Any two: overtime ban; work-to-rule; go-slow [1 each]. **[2]**

**5. (a)** Fall [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** 1.02 ÷ 1.05 ≈ 0.971, a fall of about 3% (accept 2.9% or 3%) [1]. **[1]**

**6.** Pay lost: 4 × 700 = $2,800; extra pay: 2% of 700 = $14 a week [1]. Weeks to recover: 2,800 ÷ 14 = 200 weeks [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** 80 × 450 = $36,000; 36,000 ÷ 9,000 = $4.00 per unit [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** New wage bill: 36,000 × 1.04 = $37,440 [1]. Per unit: 37,440 ÷ 9,000 = $4.16 [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** 37,440 ÷ 4 = 9,360 units a week [1]. **[1]**

**(d)** It could raise its price to cover the higher cost [1], which is possible if customers will keep buying, for example because there are few substitutes [1]. (Credit also: accept lower profit; employ fewer workers; invest in machinery to raise productivity.) **[2]**

**8.** Unions resist redundancies, or negotiate fair redundancy pay and retraining when job cuts happen [1]. They represent members facing discipline or unfair dismissal [1]. They press for safe workplaces, which protects members' health and their ability to keep working [1]. **[3]**

**9.** First: the firm negotiates with one body rather than every worker [1], which saves management time and gives a clear agreement for the whole workforce [1]. Second: the union gives workers a voice and a way of raising problems [1], so staff may be more motivated and less likely to leave, cutting recruitment and training costs [1]. (Credit also: productivity agreements; a channel for explaining change.) **[4]**

**10.** Nurses are highly trained, so they are hard to replace quickly [1], and action by nurses quickly affects patients, putting pressure on the employer and the government to settle [1]. Shop assistants need less training, so employers can find replacements more easily [1], and if many people are looking for shop work, the threat of action carries less weight [1]. (Credit also: membership share, public support, union funds.) **[4]**

**11.** First: strikes reduce output [1], so incomes and tax revenue fall, and essential public services may be disrupted [1]. Second: large pay rises raise firms' costs [1], which may be passed on as higher prices, adding to inflation, or lead to job losses that raise spending on benefits [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Benefits: collective bargaining can win higher pay than workers would get alone, because the employer cannot easily replace the whole workforce [1]; unions press for safer conditions and represent members facing dismissal [1]. Costs: members pay fees and lose pay during strikes, and the gain from a strike can take years to recover [1]; if pay rises faster than productivity, the firm may employ fewer workers, so some members lose their jobs [1]; a money pay rise may still be a real cut if prices rise faster [1]. Judgement: unions are most likely to benefit workers where the employer is profitable and pay rises are matched by productivity; where the firm is struggling, pushing for large rises may cost jobs [1]. **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* The strongest answers name the conditions under which unions help workers, rather than simply saying "they can be good or bad".

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing unions only as strike organisers.
- Treating a money pay rise as a gain without comparing it with inflation.
- Giving one viewpoint when the question names firms or the government.
- Listing union strength factors without explaining why each one matters.
- Forgetting that labour cost per unit depends on output as well as on wages.
- Ending a discussion without a supported judgement.
