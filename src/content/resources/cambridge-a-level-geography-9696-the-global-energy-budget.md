This guide covers **2.2 The global energy budget** in Topic 2, Atmosphere and weather, of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for 2025 and 2026. The syllabus has three parts:

- **The latitudinal pattern of radiation:** excesses and deficits.
- **Atmospheric transfers:** wind belts and ocean currents.
- **Seasonal variations in temperature, pressure and wind belts:** the influence of latitude, land/sea distribution and ocean currents.

It is examined in Paper 1, often through world maps of temperature or pressure, or graphs of radiation against latitude. The [2.2 global energy budget revision notes](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-global-energy-budget-revision-notes/) summarise the key terms and the three-cell model; this guide works through the reasoning, with data examples. ## Why some latitudes have a surplus and others a deficit

Incoming solar radiation is unevenly spread over the Earth because of its curved surface:

- **Sun angle:** near the equator the sun is high in the sky all year, so a beam of radiation is concentrated on a small area. Towards the poles the same beam strikes the surface at a low angle and is spread over a larger area.
- **Path length:** low-angle radiation passes through more atmosphere, so more is absorbed, scattered and reflected before it reaches the ground.
- **Albedo:** high-latitude ice and snow reflect a large share of what arrives.
- **Day length:** polar regions have continuous daylight in summer but none in winter, so the annual total is low.

Outgoing longwave radiation, by contrast, varies much less with latitude, because the atmosphere emits radiation upwards everywhere and the warm tropics only emit somewhat more than the cold poles. The result is a **net radiation surplus** at low latitudes and a **net radiation deficit** at high latitudes, with the change from surplus to deficit at roughly 35 to 40° north and south.

## Worked example 1: reading a net radiation table

*The table shows illustrative, simplified annual mean values for the northern hemisphere.*

| Latitude (°N) | 0 | 20 | 30 | 40 | 50 | 60 | 70 | 80 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Incoming absorbed (W/m²) | 300 | 290 | 260 | 220 | 180 | 140 | 110 | 90 |
| Outgoing longwave (W/m²) | 240 | 245 | 240 | 230 | 215 | 200 | 190 | 180 |
| Net (W/m²) | +60 | +45 | +20 | -10 | -35 | -60 | -80 | -90 |

**Describe:** the net value is positive from 0 to 30°N, falling from +60 to +20 W/m², and negative from 40°N, reaching -90 W/m² at 80°N. The change from surplus to deficit lies between 30° and 40°N.

**Interpret:** incoming radiation falls by 210 W/m² from the equator to 80°N, but outgoing radiation falls by only 60 W/m². If no heat were moved, the tropics would get steadily hotter and the poles steadily colder. Because this does not happen, energy must be transferred polewards.

## How energy is transferred

**Atmospheric transfers** move both sensible heat (warm air) and latent heat (water vapour that releases heat when it condenses elsewhere). The pattern of **wind belts** comes from the three-cell model:

- In the **Hadley cell**, air heated at the equator rises at the **Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ)**, flows polewards at height and sinks at about 30°, forming the **subtropical high-pressure** belt. Surface air returns towards the equator as the **trade winds**, deflected by the Coriolis effect to blow from the north-east in the northern hemisphere and the south-east in the southern hemisphere.
- Between about 30° and 60°, surface winds blow polewards as the **westerlies**. In these latitudes, travelling depressions and anticyclones carry much of the heat, mixing warm tropical air with cold polar air.
- Near the poles, cold dense air sinks and flows outwards as the **polar easterlies**, meeting the westerlies at the **subpolar low** at about 60°, the polar front.
- **Jet streams**, fast winds in the upper troposphere, steer depressions and so influence where heat is delivered.

**Ocean currents** carry heat stored in water, which has a high heat capacity. **Warm currents** flow polewards on the western sides of ocean basins, such as the Gulf Stream and its extension, the North Atlantic Drift, which keeps north-west Europe milder in winter than other places at the same latitude. **Cold currents** flow towards the equator on the eastern sides of ocean basins, such as the Humboldt (Peru) Current off western South America and the Benguela Current off south-west Africa. Cold currents cool coastal air and make it stable, which helps explain the dry coastal deserts in those regions.

Both systems matter. The atmosphere carries the larger share of the poleward transfer overall, and it responds within days; the oceans move heat more slowly but store vast amounts, smoothing seasonal and year-to-year changes.

## Seasonal variations

The overhead sun migrates between the Tropic of Cancer (about 23.5°N, in June) and the Tropic of Capricorn (about 23.5°S, in December). The whole pattern of heating, pressure and winds follows it, with a lag of a month or more.

- **Latitude:** the zone of maximum heating moves north in the northern summer and south in the southern summer. The ITCZ moves with it, bringing a wet season to places it passes over. Wind belts shift by several degrees of latitude between seasons.
- **Land/sea distribution:** land heats and cools much faster than water, because water has a higher specific heat capacity, is transparent so heat penetrates deeper, and mixes heat downwards. Continental interiors therefore have hot summers, cold winters and large annual ranges of temperature. Over Asia, strong summer heating creates low pressure that draws in moist air from the oceans (the South Asian monsoon), while intense winter cooling over Siberia creates a large high-pressure area. The ITCZ moves much further from the equator over land than over the oceans.
- **Ocean currents:** warm currents raise winter temperatures on the coasts they pass, and cold currents lower summer temperatures, so isotherms bend polewards over warm currents and equatorwards over cold ones.

## Worked example 2: continentality

*Two fictional stations at 50°N. Station C is on a west coast; station D is 2000 km inland.*

| | Mean January (°C) | Mean July (°C) | Annual range (°C) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Station C (coast) | 6 | 16 | 10 |
| Station D (interior) | -20 | 20 | 40 |

Station D's range is four times larger. In winter, the land loses heat rapidly by longwave radiation under clear, high-pressure skies; in summer, the land heats quickly. At station C, the ocean releases stored heat in winter and absorbs heat in summer, and prevailing westerly winds carry this maritime influence onshore. A warm current offshore would raise station C's January figure further.

## Annotated model answer

*"Explain why the global energy budget requires a transfer of energy from low to high latitudes." [5]*

> At low latitudes the sun is high, so radiation is concentrated on a small area and passes through little atmosphere **(cause of surplus)**. At high latitudes, low sun angles, long atmospheric paths and high albedo of ice mean little radiation is absorbed **(cause of deficit)**. Outgoing longwave radiation varies far less with latitude **(key contrast)**, so there is a net surplus equatorwards of about 35 to 40° and a net deficit polewards **(pattern with values)**. Without a transfer, the tropics would keep warming and the poles cooling, so winds and ocean currents must carry heat polewards to keep temperatures broadly stable **(conclusion)**.

## Common mistakes

- Saying the poles are cold "because they are further from the sun".
- Describing a surplus without explaining why outgoing radiation does not balance it.
- Getting the direction of Coriolis deflection wrong for the hemisphere.
- Placing warm currents on the eastern sides of oceans.
- Treating the ITCZ as fixed at the equator.
- Explaining continentality without saying why land and sea heat differently.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a world map: quote isotherm values and name places or latitudes; note where lines bend and by how much.
- **Explain** seasonal change: start with the movement of the overhead sun, then apply latitude, land/sea contrasts and currents.
- **Compare** two locations: make direct statements about range, maximum and minimum.
- Diagrams of the three-cell model must show directions of air movement and the pressure belts at the surface.
