## What the specification asks for

This is the longest subtopic in section 3.3.4. Its content statements are:

- "Addition polymers are formed from alkenes and substituted alkenes."
- "The repeating unit of addition polymers."
- "IUPAC rules for naming addition polymers."
- "Addition polymers are unreactive."
- "Appreciate that knowledge and understanding of the production and properties of polymers has developed over time."
- "Typical uses of poly(chloroethene), commonly known as PVC, and how its properties can be modified using a plasticiser."

The skills are to draw the repeating unit from a monomer and from a section of chain, draw the monomer from a section of chain, explain why addition polymers are unreactive, and explain the nature of the intermolecular forces between polyalkene molecules.

## From monomer to repeating unit

In addition polymerisation, the C=C of each monomer opens and the monomers join end to end. No other product forms.

**Method:**

1. Draw the monomer with its C=C carbons in a row and every substituent pointing up or down.
2. Change C=C to C–C.
3. Keep **every** substituent on the same carbon it started on.
4. Add an extension bond to the left of the first carbon and to the right of the second, draw brackets through those bonds, and write n.

### Worked example 1: two less familiar monomers

*Draw the repeating unit of the polymer formed from (a) but-2-ene, CH₃CH=CHCH₃, and (b) phenylethene, C₆H₅CH=CH₂.*

```
(a)     H    H                 (b)     H    H
        |    |                         |    |
   —(— C —— C —)—n                —(— C —— C —)—n
        |    |                         |    |
        CH₃  CH₃                       H    C₆H₅
```

In (a) the methyl groups are **side groups**, one on each backbone carbon. The backbone is only the two carbons that were in C=C, so the repeating unit is –CH(CH₃)–CH(CH₃)–, not a four-carbon chain. This is a common place to lose the mark.

## From a section of chain to the repeating unit and monomer

1. Find the shortest two-carbon pattern that repeats along the backbone.
2. That pattern, with extension bonds, is the repeating unit.
3. Put a double bond between its two carbons to get the monomer.

### Worked example 2: a chain with two substituents

*A section of polymer is –CH(CH₃)–CHCl–CH(CH₃)–CHCl–CH(CH₃)–CHCl–. Give the repeating unit, the monomer and the polymer's name.*

- Repeating unit: **–[CH(CH₃)–CHCl]–**.
- Monomer: **CH₃CH=CHCl**. Numbering from the Cl end gives the double bond at C1: **1-chloroprop-1-ene**.
- Polymer: **poly(1-chloroprop-1-ene)**.

Check: a chain section of six backbone carbons contains three repeating units, and each repeating unit contains exactly one monomer's worth of atoms.

## IUPAC naming

The rule is **poly + (monomer name)**. Brackets are used around the monomer name.

| Monomer | Formula | Polymer name |
|---|---|---|
| ethene | CH₂=CH₂ | poly(ethene) |
| chloroethene | CH₂=CHCl | poly(chloroethene) |
| tetrafluoroethene | CF₂=CF₂ | poly(tetrafluoroethene) |
| phenylethene | C₆H₅CH=CH₂ | poly(phenylethene) |
| 1,1-dichloroethene | CH₂=CCl₂ | poly(1,1-dichloroethene) |

A polymer chain has no C=C left, yet the name keeps "-ene" because it names the monomer.

### Worked example 3: chain length

*A sample of poly(phenylethene) has an average Mr of 2.08 × 10⁵. Calculate the average number of monomer units in each chain. (Ar: C = 12.0, H = 1.0.)*

Mr(C₈H₈) = 8(12.0) + 8(1.0) = 104.0. Number of units = 2.08 × 10⁵ ÷ 104.0 = **2000**. The repeating unit has the same formula as the monomer, because no atoms are lost.

## Why addition polymers are unreactive

After polymerisation the backbone is **saturated**: only C–C and C–H bonds (plus the bonds to substituents). There is no C=C left for electrophiles to attack. The C–C and C–H bonds are strong and almost non-polar, so they offer no δ+ site for nucleophiles either. Chemically, a polyalkene behaves like a very long alkane.

That unreactivity is useful (containers, pipes, insulation) but also means polyalkenes are not easily broken down, a point taken further in International A2.

## Forces between polyalkene chains

Poly(ethene) and poly(propene) chains are non-polar, so the only forces between them are **van der Waals forces** (induced dipole–dipole forces). They are individually weak, but each chain contains a huge number of electrons and touches its neighbours along a great length, so the total attraction is large enough to make these polymers solids.

Substituents can add stronger forces. In PVC the polar C–Cl bonds give **permanent dipole–dipole forces** between chains, in addition to van der Waals forces.

How closely chains pack also matters: chains that can lie close together have stronger total van der Waals forces than chains held apart.

## Understanding that developed over time

The history of poly(ethene) shows how production and properties became linked:

- In **1933**, chemists at ICI (Eric Fawcett and Reginald Gibson) made poly(ethene) by accident during a high-pressure experiment with ethene. The high-pressure process gives **branched** chains that cannot pack closely: **low-density poly(ethene)**, soft and flexible, used for films and bags.
- In the **1950s**, Karl Ziegler developed catalysts that polymerise ethene at much lower pressures, and Giulio Natta extended this work to propene; they shared the 1963 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. These catalysts give chains with **few branches**, which pack closely: **high-density poly(ethene)**, with stronger forces between chains, so it is stiffer and has a higher melting point, used for bottles and pipes.

The monomer is the same in both cases. Understanding how the chain structure controls the forces between chains allowed chemists to produce different materials on purpose.

## PVC and plasticisers

Poly(chloroethene) is commonly known as **PVC**.

- **Unplasticised PVC** is rigid and hard: window frames, drainpipes and water pipes.
- A **plasticiser** is a substance made of small molecules that is mixed into the polymer. The molecules fit between the chains and push them further apart. The forces between chains become weaker, so the chains slide past each other more easily and the PVC becomes **flexible**: electrical cable insulation, flooring and waterproof clothing.

Note what a plasticiser does **not** do: it does not react with the chains or break covalent bonds. It changes physical properties only.

## Common mistakes

- Leaving a C=C in the repeating unit.
- Putting side groups such as CH₃ into the backbone, giving a four-carbon repeating unit for but-2-ene.
- Missing extension bonds or the n.
- Explaining unreactivity by "strong intermolecular forces"; it is the **lack of C=C** and the strong, non-polar bonds.
- Saying a plasticiser "weakens the bonds" without saying the **forces between chains**.
- Naming the polymer without brackets, for example "polychloroethene" in an IUPAC question.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Draw** the repeating unit: two backbone carbons, single bond, all substituents, extension bonds, brackets, n.
- **Deduce** a monomer: find the two-carbon repeat and restore C=C.
- **Explain** unreactivity: saturated backbone, strong non-polar C–C and C–H bonds.
- **Explain** the forces: name the type (van der Waals; also permanent dipole–dipole for polar side groups) and why the total is large.
- **Describe** the effect of a plasticiser: molecules between chains → chains further apart → weaker forces → chains slide → flexible.
