## Alkanes and crude oil (3.3.2.1)

An **alkane** is a **saturated hydrocarbon**. Hydrocarbon means it contains hydrogen and carbon only.
Saturated means it has only single C–C bonds, so no more hydrogen can be added. The general formula of the
non-cyclic alkanes is **CₙH₂ₙ₊₂**: methane CH₄, ethane C₂H₆, propane C₃H₈, butane C₄H₁₀ and so on.

**Petroleum (crude oil)** is a mixture consisting mainly of alkanes, with a wide range of chain lengths.
It is separated into
**fractions** by **fractional distillation**. Each fraction is a mixture of hydrocarbons with similar
boiling points.

How the column works:

1. The crude oil is heated until most of it vaporises.
2. The vapour enters near the bottom of a tall fractionating column.
3. The column has a temperature gradient: hottest at the bottom, coolest at the top.
4. Vapour rises. Each hydrocarbon condenses when it reaches a level that is below its boiling point, and
   is collected there.
5. Short-chain alkanes have low boiling points, so they rise furthest and leave near the top (some as
   gases). Long-chain alkanes have high boiling points and condense low down. The heaviest residue does
   not vaporise and leaves at the bottom.

**Why chain length controls boiling point.** Only **van der Waals forces** act between alkane molecules. A
longer chain has more electrons and more surface contact, so these forces are stronger and more energy
is needed to separate the molecules. Boiling does not break C–C or C–H bonds, so fractional distillation
is a physical process. That is the key contrast with cracking.

## Cracking (3.3.2.2)

**Cracking** breaks **C–C bonds** in long-chain alkanes to make shorter molecules. If the products are all
hydrocarbons, there is not enough hydrogen for every product molecule to be an alkane, so at least one
product is an **alkene**.

| | Thermal cracking | Catalytic cracking |
|---|---|---|
| Pressure | high | slight pressure |
| Temperature | high | high |
| Catalyst | none | zeolite |
| Main products | a high percentage of alkenes | motor fuels and aromatic hydrocarbons |

You do not need a mechanism for either type. The specification describes the conditions in words, as in
the table.

### Worked example 1: cracking equations

Dodecane, C₁₂H₂₆, is thermally cracked. One molecule forms one molecule of octane and ethene only. Write
the equation.

```
Octane  = C8H18 (alkane, CnH2n+2)
Carbon left for ethene: 12 − 8 = 4, so 2 × C2H4
Check H: 18 + 2 × 4 = 26  ✓

C12H26 → C8H18 + 2C2H4
```

Write the alkane product first, then share what is left between the alkenes (CₙH₂ₙ).

### Economic reasons for cracking

You must be able to **explain** these, not just list them.

- Fractional distillation gives more of the long-chain fractions than the market wants, and less of the
  short-chain fractions such as petrol than the market wants. Supply and demand do not match.
- Short-chain fractions are in higher demand, so they are worth more. Cracking turns low-value, surplus
  long-chain fractions into high-value products.
- Cracking makes **alkenes**, which are more reactive than alkanes and are valuable starting materials for
  making polymers and other chemicals.
- Catalytic cracking makes **motor fuels** and **aromatic hydrocarbons**, which are valuable products.

## Combustion of alkanes (3.3.2.3)

Alkanes are used as **fuels** because their combustion releases a lot of energy. Combustion of alkanes and
other organic compounds can be **complete** or **incomplete**.

- **Complete combustion** (plenty of oxygen): the products are **carbon dioxide and water**.
- **Incomplete combustion** (limited oxygen): the products include **carbon monoxide** and/or
  **carbon** (soot), plus water.

### Worked example 2: combustion equations for octane

Write equations for the combustion of octane, C₈H₁₈, to form (a) CO₂, (b) CO and (c) C, with water in
each case.

```
Balance C first, then H, then O last.
(a) 8 CO2 + 9 H2O needs 16 + 9 = 25 O atoms = 12½ O2
    C8H18 + 12½O2 → 8CO2 + 9H2O
(b) 8 CO + 9 H2O needs 8 + 9 = 17 O atoms = 8½ O2
    C8H18 + 8½O2 → 8CO + 9H2O
(c) 8 C + 9 H2O needs 9 O atoms = 4½ O2
    C8H18 + 4½O2 → 8C + 9H2O
```

Halves are fine; double every coefficient if you need whole numbers. The same method works for other organic compounds: ethanol burns completely as
C₂H₅OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O (remember to count the oxygen already in the fuel).

### Worked example 3: amounts from a combustion equation

(a) Calculate the mass of carbon dioxide formed when 1.00 kg of octane burns completely.
(Ar: C = 12.0, H = 1.0, O = 16.0)

```
Mr(C8H18) = 8 × 12.0 + 18 × 1.0 = 114.0
n(C8H18)  = 1000 ÷ 114.0 = 8.772 mol
n(CO2)    = 8 × 8.772 = 70.18 mol        (1 : 8 ratio)
mass      = 70.18 × 44.0 = 3088 g = 3.09 kg (3 s.f.)
```

(b) Calculate the volume of oxygen, in dm³, needed to burn 0.250 mol of propane completely at 100 kPa and
298 K. (R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹)

```
C3H8 + 5O2 → 3CO2 + 4H2O
n(O2) = 5 × 0.250 = 1.25 mol
V = nRT / p = 1.25 × 8.31 × 298 ÷ 100 000 = 0.0310 m³ = 31.0 dm³
```

Use p in Pa to get V in m³; multiply by 1000 for dm³.

### Pollutants from the internal combustion engine

The internal combustion engine produces several gaseous pollutants:

| Pollutant | How it forms | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| NOₓ (NO and NO₂) | Nitrogen and oxygen from the air react at the high temperature in the engine: N₂ + O₂ → 2NO; then 2NO + O₂ → 2NO₂ | contributes to acid rain and photochemical smog |
| CO | incomplete combustion | toxic |
| carbon (soot particles) | incomplete combustion | harmful when breathed in |
| unburned hydrocarbons | fuel that has not burned | contributes to photochemical smog |

### Catalytic converters

These gaseous pollutants can be removed using a **catalytic converter** fitted to the exhaust. The catalyst
speeds up reactions that turn the pollutants into less harmful gases: carbon dioxide, nitrogen and water.

```
2CO + 2NO → 2CO2 + N2
C8H18 + 25NO → 8CO2 + 9H2O + 12½N2
```

CO or the hydrocarbon is oxidised, and NO is reduced to N₂.

### Sulfur dioxide and flue gases

Some fuels, especially coal and some crude oil fractions, contain sulfur compounds. When they burn, the
sulfur forms **sulfur dioxide**, which causes air pollution (it contributes to acid rain):

```
S + O2 → SO2
```

Power stations remove SO₂ from their **flue gases** (waste gases) using **calcium oxide** or **calcium
carbonate**. You must explain **why** this works:

- SO₂ is an **acidic** oxide.
- CaO is a **basic** oxide, and CaCO₃ also reacts with acids.
- So they react in a neutralisation-type reaction, and the SO₂ is trapped as a calcium salt.

```
CaO + SO2 → CaSO3
CaCO3 + SO2 → CaSO3 + CO2
```

The flue-gas mass calculation for CaO and CaCO₃ is worked through on the
[Group 2 study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-group-2-the-alkaline-earth-metals/), so it is not
repeated here.

## Chlorination of methane (3.3.2.4)

Methane reacts with chlorine in **ultraviolet (UV) light**. A hydrogen atom is replaced by a chlorine atom,
so it is a **substitution** reaction. It happens through **radicals**, so it is called **free-radical
substitution**.

```
Overall: CH4 + Cl2 → CH3Cl + HCl
```

A **radical** is a species with an **unpaired electron**. The specification represents the unpaired
electron by a **dot**, and does **not** require curly arrows for radical mechanisms (see
[introduction to organic chemistry](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-introduction-to-organic-chemistry/)). You must write
balanced equations for each step.

### The three stages

**Initiation** — UV light breaks the Cl–Cl bond. Each chlorine atom keeps one electron of the bonding pair,
so two radicals form:

```
Cl2 → 2Cl•
```

**Propagation** — a radical reacts with a molecule to make a new radical. Two steps form a chain that
repeats many times:

```
Cl• + CH4 → •CH3 + HCl
•CH3 + Cl2 → CH3Cl + Cl•
```

The Cl• made in the second step repeats the first step, so the chain continues.

**Termination** — two radicals join to form a molecule. This removes radicals and stops the chain:

```
Cl• + Cl• → Cl2
•CH3 + Cl• → CH3Cl
•CH3 + •CH3 → C2H6
```

### Worked example 4: further substitution

Explain how dichloromethane, CH₂Cl₂, forms when methane reacts with chlorine, and write the propagation
steps.

A chlorine radical can remove a hydrogen atom from chloromethane instead of from methane:

```
Cl• + CH3Cl → •CH2Cl + HCl
•CH2Cl + Cl2 → CH2Cl2 + Cl•
```

Further substitution can continue to CHCl₃ and CCl₄, so the product is a mixture. A large excess of
methane makes it more likely that Cl• collides with CH₄ rather than with CH₃Cl, which increases the
proportion of chloromethane. A large excess of chlorine favours further substitution.

**Check:** each propagation step has one radical in and one out; each termination step has two in and
none out.

## Common errors

- Saying that fractional distillation breaks bonds. It only separates by boiling point; cracking breaks
  C–C bonds.
- Explaining the boiling point trend without mentioning stronger van der Waals forces.
- Swapping the cracking conditions: thermal is high pressure; catalytic is slight pressure with a zeolite.
- Giving "to make petrol" as the whole economic reason.
- Unbalanced combustion equations, usually from forgetting the oxygen in water or in the fuel.
- Saying the nitrogen in NOₓ comes from the fuel.
- Saying CaO "absorbs" SO₂ without the acid–base explanation.
- Putting the dot on the wrong atom (•CH₃ has the unpaired electron on carbon) or writing propagation
  steps that do not regenerate a radical.
- Forgetting UV light as the condition for initiation.
