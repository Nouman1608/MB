**Key question 4 of Core Content Option A in Cambridge O Level History 2147 asks why the United States fought a civil
war and what the war changed.** The specified content runs from 1820 to 1877. This page goes beyond the outline of
events: it explains why the compromises that held the Union together for forty years stopped working, why the South
lost a war it had real chances of winning, and why Reconstruction is judged a partial success at best. For 2147
candidates this is a high-priority question: it was the Paper 2 prescribed topic in **November 2025** and is again in
**June 2026**.

## What the specification sets

Focus points:

1. How far did slavery cause the Civil War?
2. What was the significance of Lincoln's election as president?
3. Why was the North able to win the war?
4. Did the war change anything?

Specified content: differences between North and South; slavery, slave states and free states, abolitionism; the 1860
election and the secession of the Southern states; reasons for the North's victory and the South's defeat; the role of
Lincoln; Reconstruction and the extent of its success.

## Why did compromise stop working?

From 1820 to 1850 Congress kept the peace by balancing slave and free states. Three things destroyed that method in
the 1850s.

**1. Western expansion made the question unavoidable.** Every new territory forced a decision on slavery. During the
Mexican War a Northern congressman, David Wilmot, proposed in 1846 that slavery be banned from any land taken from
Mexico. The Wilmot Proviso passed the House but not the Senate, and it showed that voting was dividing by section
rather than by party.

**2. Each compromise left one side feeling cheated.** The Compromise of 1850 admitted California as a free state but
included a Fugitive Slave Act that made Northerners help return escaped people. Several Northern states passed
"personal liberty laws" to obstruct it. The Kansas–Nebraska Act (1854) then cancelled the Missouri line, and the
fighting over Kansas, including the rival pro-slavery Lecompton constitution, showed that "popular sovereignty"
produced violence rather than agreement.

**3. The national parties broke up.** The Whig Party collapsed in the mid-1850s over slavery. The new Republican Party
had almost no support in the South. In the Lincoln–Douglas debates of 1858 for an Illinois Senate seat, Stephen
Douglas argued that settlers could keep slavery out of a territory by refusing to pass laws to protect it; this
"Freeport doctrine" cost him Southern support. In 1860 the Democrats split into Northern and Southern wings. With no
party left that drew votes in both sections, there was no one to arrange a new bargain. The last attempt, the
**Crittenden Compromise** of December 1860, proposed restoring the 36°30′ line across the West; Lincoln would not
accept any extension of slavery, and it failed.

**Weighing slavery as a cause.** Arguments about states' rights, tariffs and economic differences were real, but
almost every one of them came back to slavery: the right a state claimed was the right to protect slavery, and the
Southern economy rested on enslaved labour. A top answer explains that slavery was the underlying cause, while the
breakdown of the party system explains *why* the conflict became war in 1860–61 rather than earlier.

## What was the significance of Lincoln's election?

The Republican platform of 1860 promised not to interfere with slavery in the states where it existed, but to stop its
spread into the territories. For Southern leaders this was enough. If slavery could not expand, free states would
eventually outnumber slave states and could amend the Constitution against it. Lincoln won the Electoral College
without a single Southern electoral vote, which proved that the North could now govern the nation without the South.

The election was therefore significant in three ways: it was the **trigger** for secession, beginning with South
Carolina in December 1860; it showed the **sectional division of politics** was complete; and it placed in power a
president who would refuse to let the Union break up, so secession led to war rather than separation.

## Why was the North able to win?

The North's resources are well known, but the exam rewards explaining *why the South could not overcome them*. Five
Confederate weaknesses matter:

- **States' rights hampered the Confederate government.** Governors such as Joseph Brown of Georgia resisted
  conscription and kept troops and supplies for their own states, weakening Jefferson Davis's central control.
- **Finance.** With little gold and few taxes, the Confederacy printed paper money. Inflation soared, and by the end
  of the war Confederate currency was nearly worthless.
- **Class resentment.** The Confederacy introduced conscription in 1862, but exempted men who supervised twenty or more
  enslaved people. "A rich man's war and a poor man's fight" became a common complaint, and desertion grew.
- **Slavery itself.** Enslaved people escaped to Union lines whenever armies came near, depriving the South of labour
  and later supplying Union soldiers.
- **Strategy.** Lee's invasions of the North, ending at Antietam (1862) and Gettysburg (1863), cost men the South
  could not replace.

The North also had problems: early defeats such as First Bull Run (1861), cautious generals, and the New York draft
riots of July 1863. It overcame them because Lincoln persisted until he found commanders, above all Grant, who would
fight a war of attrition, and because the blockade and Sherman's campaign of 1864 destroyed the Southern economy.

## Lincoln's role

Lincoln held the border states by presenting the war first as a war to save the Union. He moved to emancipation only
when it served the war effort, issuing the Emancipation Proclamation in 1862–63, which applied to areas in rebellion.
He gave the war a moral purpose in the **Gettysburg Address** (November 1863). He won re-election in 1864, which
ended Southern hopes of a negotiated peace, and pressed Congress to pass the **13th Amendment** in January 1865. A
judgement on his role should balance this political skill against the military factors he did not control.

## Did the war change anything? Reconstruction in three phases

| Phase | What happened | Effect on freed people |
|---|---|---|
| Presidential, 1863–66 | Lincoln's Ten Percent Plan (1863) allowed states back when 10% of voters swore loyalty. Andrew Johnson pardoned many Confederates; Southern states passed Black Codes | Freedom from slavery, but tight control over labour and movement |
| Congressional (Radical), 1866–c.1870 | Civil Rights Act (1866) passed over Johnson's veto; Reconstruction Acts (1867) put the South under military districts; 14th and 15th Amendments | Citizenship, votes for Black men, Black office-holders, schools |
| Retreat, c.1870–77 | Ku Klux Klan violence; Enforcement Acts (1870–71) only partly effective; economic depression from 1873 turned Northern attention away; Compromise of 1877 | Rights remained on paper while white "Redeemer" governments took control |

The war changed a great deal: the Union was preserved, the federal government became stronger than the states, and
about four million people were freed. Yet most freed people became sharecroppers, bound by debt to landowners, and
the political rights of 1868–70 were taken away in practice in the decades after 1877. The best answers separate
**legal change**, which was revolutionary, from **change in daily life**, which was limited.

## Annotated model answer: a (c) question

*"The Civil War changed very little for African Americans." How far do you agree? Explain your answer.*

> In one sense the war changed everything for African Americans, because the 13th Amendment of 1865 ended slavery for
> about four million people. **[Clear point with precise evidence.]** The 14th and 15th Amendments went further,
> making them citizens and banning racial bars to voting, and in the late 1860s Black men voted and were elected, such
> as Hiram Revels to the Senate in 1870. **[Development.]** However, for most freed people daily life changed less than
> the law. Without land, they became sharecroppers in debt to white landowners, and Klan violence and the end of
> federal protection in 1877 allowed Southern states to remove their votes in practice. **[Counter-argument with
> evidence.]** Overall I disagree with the statement: the legal change was permanent and was later used by the civil
> rights movement, even though economic and political equality was not achieved. **[Judgement with a criterion: what
> lasted.]**

## Common mistakes

- Saying the North fought from the start to end slavery. Lincoln's first aim was to save the Union.
- Claiming the Emancipation Proclamation freed all enslaved people. It applied only to rebel areas.
- Ignoring Confederate weaknesses and writing only about Northern strengths.
- Treating Reconstruction as one policy instead of a changing process from 1863 to 1877.

## Exam technique for 2147

- **Describe** (a): precise features, for example the terms of the Compromise of 1850.
- **Explain** (b): reasons with links, for example why Lincoln's election led to secession.
- **How far** (c): weigh factors and conclude with a reason.
- **Paper 2**: sources on this topic are often speeches, newspaper reports and cartoons from 1850–65. Check whether a
  Northern or Southern author is writing, and whether the source was written to persuade an audience.
