Section 1.2 of the Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 syllabus (2026–2028) is short: candidates must "describe and explain diffusion in terms of kinetic particle theory" and "describe and explain the effect of relative molecular mass on the rate of diffusion of gases". Both outcomes are required for every 5070 candidate. Short as it is, the section is examined through experiments, data and comparisons between gases, so you need more than a definition.

For the overview of the whole states of matter topic, see the [States of Matter and Kinetic Particle Theory guide](/resources/states-of-matter-and-kinetic-particle-theory/). This guide concentrates on diffusion alone: how to explain it, how to compare gases, and how to interpret the classic experiments.

## What diffusion is

**Diffusion** is the net movement of particles from a region where they are at a higher concentration to a region where they are at a lower concentration, as a result of the random motion of the particles.

Three phrases in that definition earn marks:

- **net movement**: individual particles move in every direction, but more move one way than the other overall
- **higher to lower concentration**: the direction of the overall spread
- **random motion of the particles**: the cause. Nothing pushes or pulls the particles; diffusion needs no stirring, wind or pump

Once the particles are evenly spread, random motion carries on, but there is no longer a net movement in any direction.

## Explaining diffusion with kinetic particle theory

Kinetic particle theory says that particles in liquids and gases are in constant, random motion. Picture a gas jar of brown bromine vapour placed under a jar of air, with the cover between them removed.

1. Bromine molecules and air molecules are both moving randomly and colliding with each other.
2. Some bromine molecules move upwards into the air jar; some air molecules move down into the bromine jar.
3. Because there are more bromine molecules in the lower jar at the start, more move up than move down: a net movement upwards.
4. After some time the brown colour is the same throughout both jars. The mixture is uniform, although the particles are still moving.

### Why diffusion is slower than the particles themselves

Gas molecules at room temperature move at hundreds of metres per second, yet a smell takes many seconds to cross a room. The reason is collisions. A diffusing molecule constantly bumps into air molecules and changes direction, so it follows a zigzag path. Its overall progress in any one direction is far slower than its actual speed. A good "explain" answer for slow diffusion mentions these collisions.

### Diffusion in liquids

When a crystal of purple potassium manganate(VII) is placed at the bottom of a beaker of water, the purple colour spreads slowly through the water. The dissolved particles move randomly among the water molecules and spread from high to low concentration. Diffusion in liquids is much slower than in gases, because liquid particles are close together and collide far more often, and they move more slowly on average.

### Why not solids?

At this level, diffusion does not happen in solids. Solid particles vibrate about fixed positions and cannot move from place to place.

## Factors affecting the rate of diffusion

### Relative molecular mass (the syllabus outcome)

At the same temperature, gas particles with a **lower relative molecular mass** move **faster** on average, so **lighter gases diffuse faster** than heavier gases.

The reason: at the same temperature, particles of different gases have the same average kinetic energy. A lighter particle with the same kinetic energy must be moving faster.

To compare gases, work out each Mr from relative atomic masses (H = 1, C = 12, N = 14, O = 16, S = 32, Cl = 35.5), then rank them.

**Worked example 1.** Put these gases in order of rate of diffusion, fastest first: carbon dioxide, hydrogen, methane, chlorine.

| Gas | Formula | Mr |
|---|---|---|
| Hydrogen | H₂ | 2 × 1 = 2 |
| Methane | CH₄ | 12 + 4 = 16 |
| Carbon dioxide | CO₂ | 12 + 32 = 44 |
| Chlorine | Cl₂ | 2 × 35.5 = 71 |

*Answer.* Hydrogen > methane > carbon dioxide > chlorine. The lowest Mr diffuses fastest.

Note that you compare **molecular** masses, not atomic masses. Chlorine exists as Cl₂ molecules, so its Mr is 71, not 35.5.

### Temperature

Although the syllabus outcome names relative molecular mass, the kinetic theory explanation also covers temperature: at a higher temperature particles have more kinetic energy and move faster, so diffusion is faster. Potassium manganate(VII) spreads more quickly in warm water than in cold water.

## The ammonia and hydrogen chloride experiment

A long glass tube is set up horizontally. Cotton wool soaked in concentrated ammonia solution is placed in one end and cotton wool soaked in concentrated hydrochloric acid in the other, and both ends are sealed with bungs. Ammonia gas (NH₃) and hydrogen chloride gas (HCl) diffuse along the tube. Where they meet, they react to form a white solid ring of ammonium chloride:

NH₃(g) + HCl(g) → NH₄Cl(s)

**Worked example 2.** Explain why the white ring forms closer to the end containing hydrochloric acid.

*Annotated answer.*
- Mr of NH₃ = 14 + 3 = 17; Mr of HCl = 1 + 35.5 = 36.5. *(Calculating both values shows the reasoning.)*
- Ammonia has the lower Mr, so its molecules move faster and it diffuses faster. *(The key link between mass and speed.)*
- In the same time, ammonia travels further along the tube than hydrogen chloride, so the gases meet nearer the hydrogen chloride end. *(Links the rate to the position of the ring.)*

Two follow-up points examiners like:

- The ring takes several minutes to appear, even though the molecules move very fast, because they collide with air molecules in the tube.
- The ends are sealed so that the gases do not escape (and for safety, since both gases are harmful), and to prevent draughts moving the gases; the result must come from diffusion alone.

### Predicting with a different pair

The same reasoning works for any two gases that meet. Suppose hydrogen sulfide (H₂S, Mr 34) and sulfur dioxide (SO₂, Mr 64) were released from opposite ends of a tube. Hydrogen sulfide is lighter, so it diffuses faster and travels further; the gases would meet nearer the sulfur dioxide end.

## The porous pot experiment

An unglazed porous pot full of air is connected to a U-tube of coloured water. A beaker of hydrogen is held over the pot. Gases can pass through the tiny holes in the pot in both directions.

- Hydrogen (Mr 2) diffuses **into** the pot faster than air (mainly nitrogen, Mr 28, and oxygen, Mr 32) diffuses **out**.
- So, for a time, there are more gas particles inside the pot than before: the pressure inside rises and the water in the U-tube is pushed down on the pot side.

If the pot is filled with hydrogen and surrounded by air instead, hydrogen diffuses out faster than air diffuses in, and the pressure inside falls. Questions on this apparatus test whether you can apply "lighter diffuses faster" to explain a pressure change.

## Common mistakes

- Defining diffusion as particles moving "from high to low" without saying **concentration**, or without saying the movement is caused by **random motion**.
- Saying that diffusion stops when the mixture is uniform because the particles stop. They keep moving; only the net movement stops.
- Using the atomic mass of chlorine or oxygen instead of the molecular mass of Cl₂ or O₂.
- Saying heavier particles are "slower to start" or "harder to push". The comparison is about speed at the same temperature.
- Explaining the ammonium chloride ring position without stating which gas is lighter and that it travels further in the same time.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** an experiment: state the apparatus, what you would see, and where.
- **Explain** a result: give Mr values, link lower Mr to faster particles, and link faster particles to the observation.
- **Suggest** questions often use unfamiliar gases. Work out each Mr; never guess from the size of the formula.
- When asked why the result is slow, mention collisions with other particles.
