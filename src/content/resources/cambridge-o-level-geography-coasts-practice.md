## Section A

**1.** Define the term *fetch*. **[1]**

**2.** State **two** characteristics of a destructive wave. **[2]**

**3.** Name **two** processes by which the sea erodes a cliff. **[2]**

**4.** Name **two** types of coral reef. **[2]**

**5.** State **two** ways in which mangrove swamps benefit people living on
the coast. **[2]**

## Section B

**6. (a)** Explain the formation of a spit. **[4]**
**(b)** Suggest why a salt marsh often forms behind a spit. **[2]**

**7.** Explain how coastal sand dunes form. **[4]**

**8.** A survey of cliff retreat at a village gives the following
**illustrative data**.

| Year | Distance from cliff edge to church (m) |
|---|---|
| 1995 | 90 |
| 2010 | 63 |
| 2025 | 30 |

**(a)** Calculate the mean rate of retreat per year between 1995 and 2025.
**[2]**
**(b)** Describe how the rate of retreat changed over the period. Use data.
**[2]**
**(c)** Suggest **two** reasons why cliffs made of soft clay retreat rapidly.
**[2]**

**9. (a)** Describe the conditions needed for coral reefs to develop. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain **two** threats to coral reefs. **[4]**

**10.** For a named area of coastline, describe the hazards it presents and
evaluate the methods used to manage coastal erosion. **[7]**

## Answers

**1.** The distance of open water over which the wind blows to form waves
[1].

**2.** Any two of: high wave height [1]; frequent, many per minute [1];
backwash stronger than swash [1]; steep, plunging form [1]; removes material
from the beach [1].

**3.** Any two of: hydraulic action [1]; abrasion [1]; attrition (of the
material that then abrades the cliff) [1]; solution [1].

**4.** Any two of: fringing reef [1]; barrier reef [1]; atoll [1].

**5.** Any two of: protect the coast from erosion and storm surges [1];
nursery areas for fish, supporting fishing [1]; timber and fuelwood [1];
honey and other products [1]; tourism [1].

**6. (a)** Up to 4 marks: waves approach the coast at an angle, so longshore
drift moves sediment along the shore [1]; where the coastline changes
direction, for example at an estuary or bend [1], the sediment continues to be
carried and is deposited in the open water [1]; the ridge grows out from the
land [1]; changes in wind or wave direction can curve its end into a hook [1].
**(b)** The spit shelters the water behind it from waves, so energy is low
[1]; fine mud and silt settle out, and salt-tolerant plants colonise it, trapping
more sediment [1].

**7.** Up to 4 marks: a wide beach provides a large supply of sand, which dries
out at low tide [1]; a strong onshore wind blows dry sand inland by saltation
[1]; the sand is trapped by an obstacle such as driftwood or a plant and builds
up [1]; marram grass colonises the dune, and its long roots bind the sand,
allowing the dune to grow taller [1]; dunes further inland are older and more
vegetated [1].

**8. (a)** 90 − 30 = 60 m in 30 years [1]; 60 ÷ 30 = **2 m per year** [1].
**(b)** The rate of retreat increased [1]: between 1995 and 2010 the cliff
retreated 27 m (1.8 m per year), but between 2010 and 2025 it retreated 33 m
(2.2 m per year) [1].
**(c)** Any two of: soft clay is easily eroded by hydraulic action and
abrasion [1]; clay becomes saturated after rain and slumps [1]; a narrow beach
gives little protection from waves [1]; a long fetch produces powerful
destructive waves [1].

**9. (a)** Any three of: warm water, usually above about 18 °C [1]; shallow
water so sunlight can reach the algae in the coral [1]; clear water free of
sediment [1]; salt water, so away from river mouths [1]; well-oxygenated water
[1].
**(b)** Two explained threats, 2 marks each: **rising sea temperatures**
cause coral bleaching, as the coral loses the algae it depends on and may die
[2]; **sediment and pollution** from farming, deforestation or sewage cloud the
water and block sunlight [2]; **overfishing** or destructive fishing damages
the reef ecosystem [2]; **tourism** damages coral through trampling, anchors
and souvenir collecting [2].

**10.** Levels-style marking:
- **Level 3 (6–7 marks):** a named coastline with specific places; hazards
  clearly described; at least two management methods described and evaluated,
  including effects on other parts of the coast, and a judgement.
- **Level 2 (3–5 marks):** hazards and methods described with some detail;
  limited evaluation.
- **Level 1 (1–2 marks):** generic hazards and defences with no
  place-specific detail.

Example of Level 3 content (Holderness, England): soft boulder clay cliffs
attacked by powerful waves and slumping after rain, making this one of the
fastest-eroding coastlines in Europe; homes, farmland and roads are lost.
Sea walls and groynes protect Hornsea and Withernsea; at Mappleton, rock
groynes and rock armour built in 1991 protect the village and road; rock
armour protects the Easington gas terminal. Evaluation: the defences protect
high-value places, but groynes trap longshore drift, so cliffs to the south of
Mappleton have lost beach protection and eroded faster; defending the whole
coast would be too costly, so most of it is left to retreat.

## A note on exam technique for this topic

For every "explain the formation of" question, start from the **process**
(longshore drift, wind, wave attack) and work in order to the finished
landform. When evaluating defences, always mention one effect on another
part of the coast, such as beach starvation down-drift, since this is the
point that most often separates the top level from the middle.
