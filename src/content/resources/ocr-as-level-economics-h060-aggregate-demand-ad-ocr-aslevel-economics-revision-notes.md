These notes condense **Aggregate demand (AD)** from Component 02, Macroeconomics, of the OCR *AS Level Economics (H060)* specification. The [aggregate demand study guide](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-aggregate-demand-ad-ocr-aslevel-economics/) has the full explanations, and the [aggregate demand practice questions](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-aggregate-demand-ad-ocr-aslevel-economics-practice/) give exam-style work. All figures are invented.

## What you must be able to do

| Specification wording | In one line |
|---|---|
| Explain aggregate demand and its components | AD = C + I + G + (X - M), total planned spending at each price level |
| Explain, with a diagram, the relationship between AD and the price level | Downward slope: wealth, interest rate and international trade effects |
| Explain, with a diagram, shifts in the AD curve | Any change in a component not caused by the price level |
| Evaluate the relationship between changes in income and consumption | Income matters most, but the effect depends on permanence, wealth, credit, distribution and confidence |
| Evaluate the role of expectations | Confidence and expected prices or policy can move AD on their own |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Aggregate demand | Total planned spending on domestic output at each price level |
| Consumption (C) | Household spending on goods and services |
| Investment (I) | Firms' spending on capital goods and additions to stocks |
| Government spending (G) | State spending on goods, services and public sector pay, excluding transfers |
| Net exports (X - M) | Exports minus imports |
| Disposable income | Income after direct taxes and including benefits |
| Consumption function | The relationship between consumption and income, such as C = a + bY |
| Marginal propensity to consume (MPC) | The change in consumption divided by the change in income |
| Average propensity to consume (APC) | Consumption divided by income |
| Wealth effect | A change in real wealth that changes spending |
| Consumer confidence | Households' optimism about their future incomes and jobs |
| Animal spirits | Keynes's term for waves of business optimism and pessimism |

## Why AD slopes downwards

| Effect | Chain |
|---|---|
| Wealth | Higher price level, real value of savings falls, households consume less |
| Interest rate | Higher price level, more money needed for transactions, interest rates rise, C and I fall |
| International trade | Higher domestic prices relative to foreign, exports fall and imports rise, X - M falls |

A change in the price level gives a **movement along** AD, not a shift.

## What shifts AD

| Component | Shifts AD right when... |
|---|---|
| C | Income tax falls, interest rates fall, asset prices rise, credit is easier, confidence rises |
| I | Expected demand rises, interest rates fall, business confidence rises, new technology appears, investment tax allowances rise |
| G | Government raises spending |
| X | Trading partners grow, the currency depreciates |
| M | Imports fall, for example because the currency depreciates (this raises X - M) |

### Worked reminder: size matters

C = 900, I = 200, G = 300, X = 400, M = 450, so AD = **1,350**. A 5% rise in G (+15) and a 5% fall in C (-45) together cut AD by 30, about 2.2%.

### Worked reminder: consumption function

C = 50 + 0.6Y. At Y = 500, C = 350 and APC = 0.70. At Y = 1,000, C = 650 and APC = 0.65. MPC = 0.6 at every level: each extra 100 of income adds 60 of consumption.

### Worked reminder: MPC from data

Income rises from 800 to 900; consumption rises from 680 to 755. MPC = 75 / 100 = **0.75**.

## Evaluating income and consumption

- **Permanent or temporary?** Permanent income rises are spent more fully than one-off gains.
- **Whose income?** Lower-income households have a higher MPC than higher-income households.
- **Wealth.** Rising house prices can lift spending with no change in income.
- **Credit.** Easy borrowing breaks the link between income and spending.
- **Interest rates.** Higher rates reward saving and make borrowing dearer.
- **Uncertainty.** Fear of job loss raises precautionary saving.

## Evaluating expectations

- They can **shift AD before** any change in income or policy.
- They can be **self-fulfilling**: expected recession leads to cuts in investment, which cause a recession.
- Expected **inflation** brings spending forward; expected **deflation** delays it.
- They are **hard to measure** and **hard to steer**, which makes AD less predictable.
- Central banks and governments try to anchor expectations through clear targets and communication.

## Exam traps

- Axes are **price level** and **real output**.
- A higher price level moves along AD; it does not shift it.
- Transfer payments are not G.
- A rise in imports **reduces** AD.
- Investment means capital goods, not buying shares or saving in a bank.
- MPC is a change divided by a change; APC is a level divided by a level.
- A shift in AD does not tell you the effect on output or prices until you add AS.

## Self-test

1. Write the formula for aggregate demand.
2. Which component of AD is usually largest, and which is usually most volatile?
3. Give the three reasons why the AD curve slopes downwards.
4. Explain why higher interest rates shift AD to the left.
5. C = 20 + 0.9Y. Calculate C and the APC when Y = 400.
6. Income rises from 1,200 to 1,300 and consumption from 1,000 to 1,070. Calculate the MPC.
7. Why might a one-off tax rebate raise consumption by less than a permanent tax cut of the same size?
8. Give one reason why a rise in house prices may raise consumption.
9. State two factors that affect investment.
10. How does an appreciation of the currency affect AD?
11. Explain how expectations of a recession could cause a recession.
12. Why might expected inflation raise AD in the short run?

### Answers

1. AD = C + I + G + (X - M).
2. Consumption is largest; investment is most volatile.
3. Wealth effect, interest rate effect, international trade effect.
4. Borrowing becomes dearer and saving more rewarding, so C and I fall at every price level.
5. C = 20 + 360 = **380**; APC = 380 / 400 = **0.95**.
6. 70 / 100 = **0.7**.
7. Households smooth spending: a one-off gain is mostly saved, while a permanent rise in income changes long-run spending plans.
8. Higher wealth makes households feel richer and lets them borrow against their homes.
9. Any two: expected demand, interest rates, business confidence, technology, corporation tax.
10. Exports become dearer abroad and imports cheaper, so X - M falls and AD shifts left.
11. Firms cut investment and hiring and households save more, so AD falls and output falls.
12. Households and firms buy before prices rise, bringing spending forward.
