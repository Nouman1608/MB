## Section A

**1.** State the marks allocated to each of Paper 2's three parts. **[3]**

**2.** Name the assessment objective tested across all three parts of
Paper 2. **[1]**

**3.** State how many questions a candidate must answer in Part 2
(Poetry) and in Part 3 (Prose), out of how many offered. **[2]**

## Section B

**4.** Explain why Part 1's Unseen Passage cannot be prepared by
memorising a specific text, and what candidates should practise
instead. **[3]**

**5.** A candidate answers a Part 2 Poetry question by summarising a
prescribed ghazal's general subject matter, without quoting any
specific lines.

**(a)** Explain why this answer is unlikely to reach the top mark
band. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe the structure a stronger answer would follow. **[3]**

**6.** A candidate prepares only enough of the prescribed poetry and
prose list to have exactly one usable option for each of Part 2 and
Part 3.

**(a)** Explain the risk in this approach. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest a more resilient level of preparation. **[2]**

**7.** Explain the difference between Cambridge O Level First Language
Urdu (3247) and Second Language Urdu (3248) in terms of what their
respective Paper 2 components test. **[2]**

**8.** A candidate spends significantly longer than planned on Part
1's Unseen Passage, leaving insufficient time for Part 2 and Part 3.

**(a)** Explain why this timing approach risks losing marks overall. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest how a candidate should divide their time in practice to
avoid this. **[1]**

**9.** Explain what distinguishes a top-band response from a mid-band
response on Paper 2's levels-based mark schemes, even when both
responses show accurate comprehension of the text. **[3]**

**10.** Explain why answering the Unseen Passage purely as a
comprehension exercise — correctly stating what the passage says — is
insufficient to score highly. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Part 1 (Unseen Passage): 20 marks; Part 2 (Poetry): 15 marks;
Part 3 (Prose): 15 marks [3].

**2.** Assessment Objective 3 (Literature) [1].

**3.** One question from a choice of two in Part 2, and one question
from a choice of two in Part 3 [2].

**4.** Because the passage is genuinely unseen, no specific text can
be pre-learned for it [1]; candidates should instead practise reading
unfamiliar Urdu poetry and prose extracts regularly and identifying
how the writer creates effect — through word choice, imagery and
structure — rather than only understanding what the passage says [2].

**5. (a)** This answer is unlikely to reach the top mark band because
it only demonstrates general understanding of the poem's subject
matter, without the close, well-selected textual reference and
specific quotation the highest mark-scheme levels require [2].
**(b)** A stronger answer identifies specific lines (sher) from the
poem, explains the imagery or word choice used within them, and
connects that language choice explicitly to the theme or emotion named
in the question, rather than paraphrasing the poem's content in
general terms [3].

**6. (a)** The risk is having no fallback option if the one prepared
question in Part 2 or Part 3 does not suit the candidate well on the
day, or if it asks about an aspect of the text they have not prepared
in enough depth [2].
**(b)** A more resilient approach is knowing roughly six of the ten
prescribed ghazals, two of the three nazms, and five of the eight
prose articles in close textual depth, with specific quotable lines
ready for each, so a genuine choice remains available on the day [2].

**7.** Cambridge O Level First Language Urdu (3247) is a distinct
specification from Second Language Urdu (3248); 3247's Paper 2 (Texts)
is built entirely around literature — an unseen extract plus
prescribed poetry and prose — while 3248's equivalent paper (Grammar,
Writing and Translation) tests a different skill set altogether, so
the two are not interchangeable despite sharing the same subject name
[2].

**8. (a)** Because Part 2 and Part 3 both require sustained, essay-length
engagement with prescribed texts, leaving insufficient time for them
after overrunning on Part 1 risks underdeveloped essay answers that
cannot access full marks in either of the remaining parts, even if
Part 1 itself was answered well [2].
**(b)** Time each part of Paper 2 in practice sessions specifically, so
that no single part is allowed to crowd out the others under real exam
conditions [1].

**9.** A top-band response shows sustained, perceptive engagement with
the writer's craft — explicitly explaining how specific language,
imagery or structural choices create effect and connecting this to a
personal, informed response — whereas a mid-band response may show
accurate comprehension of the text's meaning without this deeper level
of engagement with how that meaning is constructed [3].

**10.** Because the mark scheme rewards understanding that goes beyond
literal surface meaning, explicit engagement with how the writer
achieves their effects, and close textual reference — a purely
comprehension-based answer addresses only the most basic requirement
and cannot access the higher levels of the mark scheme, which
specifically look for analysis of technique, not just accurate
understanding of content [2].

## A note on exam technique for this topic

Because Part 1 tests a transferable skill rather than memorised
content, the most valuable preparation is regular practice on genuinely
unfamiliar extracts throughout the course, building the habit of
asking not just "what does this say" but "how does the writer make
this effective." For Parts 2 and 3, prioritise depth over breadth:
knowing a manageable subset of the prescribed ghazals, nazms and prose
articles with precise, quotable detail will serve a candidate far
better under exam pressure than shallow familiarity with the entire
list. In every essay answer, follow the identify-explain-connect
structure — a specific quotation, an explanation of the technique it
demonstrates, and a direct link to the theme or emotion the question
asks about — since this is what separates a top-band response from an
accurate but purely descriptive one.
