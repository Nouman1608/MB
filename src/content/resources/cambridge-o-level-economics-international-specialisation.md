## 6.1.1 What national specialisation is

**Specialisation at a national level** means a country puts most of its productive capacity into a limited range of goods and services, sells part of that output abroad and imports the rest of what it needs. The syllabus gives two broad reasons.

**Superior resource allocation.** Productive resources are unevenly spread between countries. A country with mineral deposits, fertile land, a skilled workforce or a large stock of capital does better to put its resources where they produce the most. The same resources then yield more total output.

**Cheaper production methods.** A country may make a good at lower cost per unit because of better technology, more productive workers or large-scale production. Cheaper does not mean lower wages alone, as the second example shows.

The syllabus text is broad, so opportunity cost (what is given up) is used here only as a teaching device to show why one country might make one good and another a different one. Section 6.1 does not ask for the calculation.

### Worked example 1: gains from using resources where they are best used

Country P and Country Q each make cocoa and machines. The figures show the most each can make of one good if it makes none of the other.

| | Cocoa (tonnes) | Machines |
|---|---|---|
| Country P, all resources on one good | 90 | 30 |
| Country Q, all resources on one good | 40 | 80 |

Opportunity costs:

- Country P: 1 machine costs 90 / 30 = 3 tonnes of cocoa. 1 tonne of cocoa costs 30 / 90 = 1/3 of a machine.
- Country Q: 1 machine costs 40 / 80 = 0.5 tonnes of cocoa. 1 tonne of cocoa costs 80 / 40 = 2 machines.

P gives up less to make cocoa. Q gives up less to make machines.

Before specialising, P puts two thirds of its resources into cocoa and one third into machines: 60 tonnes and 10 machines. Q splits resources equally: 20 tonnes and 40 machines. World output is 80 tonnes and 50 machines.

After specialising, P makes 90 tonnes of cocoa and Q makes 80 machines. World output is 90 tonnes and 80 machines, which is 10 more tonnes and 30 more machines.

Trade shares the gain. Suppose the countries swap at 1 machine for 1.5 tonnes, a rate between the two opportunity costs (0.5 and 3). P buys 16 machines and pays 16 x 1.5 = 24 tonnes.

- P ends with 90 - 24 = 66 tonnes and 16 machines, against 60 and 10 before.
- Q ends with 80 - 16 = 64 machines and 24 tonnes, against 40 and 20 before.

Both countries consume more of both goods than before. **Reasoning chain:** resources go to their best use, total output rises, trade lets each country consume more than it could alone.

### Worked example 2: cheaper production methods

Unit labour cost = daily wage / output per worker per day.

- Country R pays 30 per day and a worker makes 10 units: 30 / 10 = **3.00** per unit.
- Country S pays 12 per day and a worker makes 3 units: 12 / 3 = **4.00** per unit.

Low wages do not make S cheaper. R's higher productivity, from better machines and training, gives lower cost. If S buys machines that lift output to 5 units per worker, its cost becomes 12 / 5 = **2.40**, a fall of (4.00 - 2.40) / 4.00 x 100 = **40%**, and S is now cheaper than R.

### Worked example 3: lower unit costs from a bigger market

A furniture industry in Country T has fixed costs of 900,000 and variable costs of 18 per unit. Average cost = (fixed cost + variable cost x output) / output.

- Home market only, 25,000 units: (900,000 + 18 x 25,000) / 25,000 = 1,350,000 / 25,000 = **54**.
- With exports, 100,000 units: (900,000 + 18 x 100,000) / 100,000 = 2,700,000 / 100,000 = **27**.

Average cost falls by (54 - 27) / 54 x 100 = **50%**. Fixed costs are spread over four times as many units. This is an economy of scale made possible by selling abroad.

## 6.1.2 Advantages of specialisation

### Consumers

- **Lower prices** when output is cheaper.
- **Wider choice** because the country can import goods it does not make.
- **Better quality** where firms improve through practice and competition.

*Worked example 4.* The price of an imported shirt in Country T falls from 40 to 34. The fall is (34 - 40) / 40 x 100 = **-15%**. A household spending 340 buys 340 / 40 = 8.5 shirts before and 340 / 34 = 10 after, which is 17.6% more. Real purchasing power has risen.

### Firms

- **Larger markets**, since exporters sell at home and abroad.
- **Economies of scale**, as worked example 3 showed.
- **Expertise and technology** build up as firms concentrate on what they do best.
- **Higher profit** from lower unit costs or from a larger sales volume.

### The economy

- **Higher output and income**, because the same inputs produce more, so living standards can rise.
- **Export earnings** that pay for imports.
- **Jobs** in expanding export industries.
- **Efficient allocation**, since resources move to uses where they produce most.

## 6.1.2 Disadvantages of specialisation

### Consumers

- **Choice from home producers may shrink**, because local firms in non-specialised industries close.
- **Prices of imports can rise** if export earnings fall and the currency weakens, or if a foreign supplier raises prices.
- **Supplies can be disrupted**, for example if a trading partner cuts exports of food or fuel.

### Firms

- **Firms in non-specialised industries** may face cheaper imports and close.
- **Dependence on a few foreign customers**, so a loss of orders can threaten survival.
- **Exposure to price changes**, since a firm's revenue may depend on one world price.

### The economy

- **Over-dependence on one or a few exports.** Falls in world price or demand hit income, jobs and tax revenue together.
- **Structural unemployment.** Workers in declining industries may lack the skills for new ones.
- **Depletion of resources** such as minerals, or damage to land and the environment, if a country exploits one resource heavily.
- **Vulnerability to events abroad**, such as a recession in a main trading partner.

### Worked example 5: a fall in the price of the main export

Country U (illustrative) has GDP of 200 and exports of 60, mostly one mineral. The world price falls 25% and volume sold falls 10%.

- New export earnings = 60 x 0.75 x 0.90 = **40.5**.
- Fall = 60 - 40.5 = 19.5, which is 19.5 / 60 x 100 = **32.5%** of export earnings.
- As a share of GDP = 19.5 / 200 x 100 = **9.75%**, before any knock-on effects on jobs and spending.

### Worked example 6: why diversification matters

In Country V, one mineral is 70% of export earnings. In Country W, the leading export is 25% of earnings. The world price of each country's leading export falls 30%, other exports unchanged.

- Country V: 70% x 30% = **21%** fall in total export earnings.
- Country W: 25% x 30% = **7.5%** fall.

The more concentrated the exports, the bigger the shock.

### Worked example 7: structural unemployment

In Country V (illustrative) a declining industry loses 12,000 jobs. New export firms create 5,000 jobs, but they need skills the displaced workers do not have. If none of the displaced workers can fill the new posts, then 12,000 workers are out of work and the net change in jobs is 5,000 - 12,000 = **-7,000**. With a labour force of 400,000, net job loss is 7,000 / 400,000 x 100 = **1.75%** of the labour force. Retraining reduces this.

## Evaluating specialisation

Judging whether specialisation is a good idea needs a conclusion, backed by reasons. Useful chains:

1. **Gains:** higher output and income, lower prices, wider choice. **But:** shocks hit harder when exports are concentrated. **Judgement:** it depends on how diversified the economy is, how quickly workers can retrain, and whether the specialised goods have stable demand.
2. **Short run versus long run:** adjustment costs such as lost jobs come first, while gains from lower costs build up. A country may gain over time even if some workers lose out now.
3. **Who gains:** consumers and export firms usually gain. Workers and firms in industries that cannot compete may lose.
4. **Type of good:** a country specialising in a good with stable demand faces less risk than one relying on a good with volatile prices.

## Common errors

- Writing "specialisation means doing everything well". It means concentrating on a narrow range.
- Saying low wages always give cheaper production. Output per worker matters too.
- Listing a disadvantage for "the economy" without saying what happens to income, jobs or tax revenue.
- Mixing up the groups: a larger market is a benefit for firms, lower prices a benefit for consumers.
- Describing only advantages. Section 6.1.2 asks for both sides.
- Using the opportunity cost of the wrong good when comparing countries.
