These questions cover section 3.6.4 Making human resource decisions: improving motivation and engagement of the AQA A-level Business (7132) specification. All businesses and figures are invented. Revise first with the [motivation and engagement study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-motivation-and-engagement/) and the [motivation and engagement revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-motivation-and-engagement-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** A worker is paid £0.90 per unit and produces 420 units in a week. What is the weekly pay? **[1]**

A: £378

B: £420

C: £466.67

D: £4.67

**2.** Which **one** of the following is a motivator in Herzberg's two-factor theory? **[1]**

A: Pay

B: Working conditions

C: Recognition for achievement

D: Company policy

**3.** Define the term "job enrichment". **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** disadvantage of performance-related pay. **[3]**

**5.** A sales representative earns a basic salary of £1,500 a month plus 6% commission on sales above £15,000. Sales one month are £32,000.

**(a)** Calculate the commission earned. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the total pay. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain **one** risk to the business of paying commission. **[2]**

**6.** A factory announces that one of its plants may close.

**(a)** Identify the level of Maslow's hierarchy that is most threatened for the plant's employees. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain **one** action the business could take to improve motivation in line with Maslow's theory. **[2]**

**7.** A packer is paid £9 an hour for 40 hours (£360 a week) and packs 450 units. The business offers piece rate pay of £0.80 per unit. The packer then produces 540 units.

**(a)** Calculate the original labour cost per unit. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate pay under piece rate. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the percentage increase in pay. **[1]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**8.** A call centre has high staff turnover. Analyse how its managers could use Herzberg's theory to improve motivation. **[6]**

**9.** A car dealership is deciding how to motivate its sales staff. Assess whether performance-related pay is the best approach. **[9]**

**10.** Analyse **two** benefits to a business of offering flexible working. **[6]**

**11.** Explain **two** limitations of applying Taylor's approach to motivation in a modern business. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate whether financial methods of motivation are more effective than non-financial methods for supermarket checkout staff. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** **A** [1]. 420 × £0.90 = £378. **[1]**

**2.** **C** [1]. Recognition is a motivator; pay, working conditions and policy are hygiene factors. **[1]**

**3.** Giving employees more responsibility, challenge and variety in their job [1], so that they use more of their skills and take on tasks previously done at a higher level [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Targets may be unfair or hard to measure, so employees feel the pay is not linked to their effort [1]. Those who miss targets may be demotivated and resentful [1]. PRP can also encourage a short-term focus and damage cooperation between colleagues, which lowers overall performance [1]. **[3]**

**5. (a)** Sales above £15,000 = £32,000 − £15,000 = £17,000 [1]; 6% × £17,000 = **£1,020** [1].

**(b)** £1,500 + £1,020 = **£2,520** [1].

**(c)** Representatives may use aggressive selling to earn commission [1], which could damage customer relationships and the firm's reputation, or lead them to ignore service tasks that earn no commission [1]. **[5]**

**6. (a)** Safety (security) [1].

**(b)** Be open about the plan and offer guarantees of redeployment, retraining or fair redundancy terms [1], which reassure employees about their security so that their motivation to work is not dominated by fear [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** £360 ÷ 450 = **£0.80** per unit [1].

**(b)** 540 × £0.80 = **£432** [1].

**(c)** (£432 − £360) ÷ £360 × 100 = **20%** [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Hygiene factors first [1]: pay, supervision and conditions must be fair, or staff are dissatisfied [1], so the call centre should check pay is competitive, supervisors are supportive and the environment is pleasant [1]. Motivators next [1]: call-handlers need chances for achievement, responsibility and recognition [1], so the centre could give staff authority to resolve complaints, recognise success and offer promotion routes [1]. Accept: a pay rise alone will not motivate for long. **[6]**

**9.** For: sales staff's results are measurable, so targets are clear [1]. PRP rewards those who sell more, which aligns their effort with the dealership's aim [1]. The cost rises only when sales rise [1]. It attracts ambitious people who respond to money, as Taylor suggested [1]. Against: it may push staff to sell hard and ignore customer care, harming repeat sales [1]. Staff may not control sales because of the economy or cars in stock, which makes the targets seem unfair [1]. Staff may compete rather than cooperate [1]. Income is unstable [1]. Judgement: PRP is useful for sales staff, but is best combined with a basic salary and with measures of customer satisfaction, so that long-term relationships are valued [1]. **[9]**

**10.** Benefit 1: it helps to recruit and keep staff [1]. Employees value control over hours, so a firm that offers it stands out from competitors [1], which reduces turnover and the costs of recruitment and training [1]. Benefit 2: lower absence and higher productivity [1]. Staff can arrange work around family or health needs, so fewer take time off [1], and rested, motivated staff produce better work; the firm may also save office costs with remote working [1]. **[6]**

**11.** Limitation 1: it ignores social and psychological needs [1]. Modern employees, such as those in skilled and service jobs, also want recognition, interesting work and teamwork, which money alone does not provide [1]. Limitation 2: it suits only routine, measurable work [1]. Many modern jobs need creativity and judgement, and close supervision and narrow tasks would demotivate staff and reduce quality [1]. **[4]**

**12.** For financial: checkout staff often work for pay and the cost of living matters, so a fair wage is the basic motivator [1]. Bonuses linked to speed or accuracy are easy to measure and can raise productivity [1]. Money is simple to understand and quick to reward [1]. Financial rewards may be needed to compete in the labour market for staff [1]. For non-financial: the work is repetitive, so enrichment and rotation (for example to customer service or stock) reduce boredom [1]. Flexible hours suit many staff and reduce absence [1]. Recognition and team working improve morale and customer service [1]. Herzberg suggests pay prevents dissatisfaction but does not by itself motivate [1]. Piece-rate type incentives could cause errors and queues [1]. Factors: the workforce (students want flexibility; others want income), the cost and the competition for staff [1]. Judgement: financial reward must be fair to avoid dissatisfaction, but non-financial methods are likely to give longer-lasting motivation in repetitive work, so a combination is most effective [1], and it should be tested through absence, turnover and customer feedback [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* Name the theory, apply it to evidence in the case, and judge whether the method suits the type of work.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating money as the only motivator, or as irrelevant.
- Describing theories without applying them.
- Confusing job rotation with job enrichment.
- Forgetting the quality and teamwork risks of piece rate and commission.
- Calling pay a motivator in Herzberg's terms.
- Recommending one method for every employee.
