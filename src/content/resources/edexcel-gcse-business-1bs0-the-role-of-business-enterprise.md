This guide goes deeper into content item 1.1.3, The role of business enterprise, in Topic 1.1 Enterprise and entrepreneurship of the Pearson Edexcel Level 1/Level 2 GCSE (9-1) in Business (1BS0) specification, Issue 2, July 2022, part of Theme 1 (Paper 1, 1BS0/01). The broader [Enterprise and entrepreneurship study guide](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-enterprise-and-entrepreneurship/) gives the definitions; this page shows how adding value changes a business's price and sales, how to judge which method of adding value suits a business, and how to apply the entrepreneur's three roles to a case. All businesses and figures are invented.

Companion pages: the [1.1.3 revision notes](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-1bs0-the-role-of-business-enterprise-revision-notes/) and the [1.1.3 practice questions](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-1bs0-the-role-of-business-enterprise-practice/).

## What 1.1.3 asks you to know

| Strand | Specification wording |
|---|---|
| Purpose of business activity | to produce goods or services; to meet customer needs; to add value: convenience, branding, quality, design, unique selling points |
| The role of entrepreneurship | an entrepreneur: organises resources, makes business decisions, takes risks |

## Part 1: producing goods or services

- **Goods** are physical products you can touch and keep: a loaf, a bicycle, a phone case.
- **Services** are things done for a customer: a haircut, a repair, a lesson, a delivery.

Many businesses supply both. A bike shop sells bikes (goods) and repairs them (services). The distinction matters in exam answers because it changes what adding value looks like. A good can be improved by better materials or design; a service is often improved by speed, convenience or the way staff treat customers.

## Part 2: meeting customer needs

A business exists because customers have needs it can satisfy at a price they will pay. If it stops meeting those needs, customers go elsewhere and the business cannot survive. Topic 1.2 develops this through customer needs and market research; for 1.1.3, the key point is that producing something is not enough. The product must be something customers want.

## Part 3: adding value

Added value is the difference between the price a customer pays and the cost of the bought-in materials and services used to make the product. The specification does not give a formula for it; this is the standard meaning. Adding value lets a business either charge a higher price for the same inputs or sell more at the same price. Both raise the money available to pay wages and overheads and leave a profit.

| Method | How it adds value | Effect it aims for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Convenience | Saves the customer time or effort | Customers pay extra or choose this business over a cheaper rival | Extra costs such as delivery drivers or longer opening hours |
| Branding | A recognised name and image that customers trust | Customers accept a higher price and stay loyal | A brand takes time and money to build |
| Quality | Better materials, finish or service | Higher price; fewer complaints and returns | Higher input costs reduce the gain |
| Design | Appearance, ease of use, function | Product stands out; customers pay for it | Designs can be copied |
| Unique selling point (USP) | A feature no rival offers | Gives customers a reason to choose this business | A USP lasts only until rivals copy it |

### Worked example: Fernhill Honey

Fernhill Honey buys honey from local beekeepers and sells it in jars.

**Before.** Plain jar with a printed label, sold at £4.50. Bought-in costs: honey £1.80, jar and lid £0.40, so £2.20 per jar.
- Added value per jar = £4.50 − £2.20 = **£2.30**

**After.** The owner switches to a distinctive hexagonal jar (+£0.25 a jar) and a label telling the story of the farms that supply the honey (+£0.10). Price rises to £7.00.
- Bought-in costs = £2.20 + £0.25 + £0.10 = £2.55
- Added value per jar = £7.00 − £2.55 = **£4.45**, an increase of £2.15 a jar

**Total added value.** Before the change Fernhill sold 3,000 jars a year: 3,000 × £2.30 = £6,900. If the higher price cuts sales to 2,400 jars: 2,400 × £4.45 = **£10,680**. Even with 600 fewer jars, the business has £3,780 more to cover its other costs and make a profit.

Which methods did it use? **Design** (the jar) and **branding** (the story on the label). The local farm story could also act as a **USP** if no rival can make the same claim.

Remember that added value is not profit. Fernhill still has to pay wages, rent and delivery from the £10,680.

### Choosing the right method

The best method depends on the business's customers.

- A business serving busy commuters gains most from **convenience**.
- A business selling gifts gains most from **design** and **branding**, because appearance matters to the buyer.
- A business selling safety or long-lasting products gains most from **quality**.
- A small business in a crowded market needs a **USP** to give customers a reason to choose it.

In a case study, find the evidence about the customers, then argue which method fits.

## Part 4: the role of the entrepreneur

The specification lists three roles. They overlap, but each needs to be shown separately in a case.

1. **Organises resources.** The entrepreneur brings together premises, equipment, materials, staff and money so the business can produce. Organising means choosing and combining them efficiently, not just buying them.
2. **Makes business decisions.** What to sell, at what price, to whom, where, and how much to spend. Every decision carries a trade-off, such as quality against cost.
3. **Takes risks.** The entrepreneur commits money and time before knowing whether customers will buy. This links back to 1.1.2 Risk and reward.

### Applying the roles: Quayside Paddleboards

Lena rents a lock-up by a harbour, buys twelve paddleboards with a £6,000 loan, hires two qualified instructors for summer weekends and decides to offer only group lessons for beginners, priced at £30 a session.

| Role | Evidence from the case |
|---|---|
| Organises resources | Premises (lock-up), equipment (twelve boards), staff (two instructors), finance (£6,000 loan) |
| Makes business decisions | Group lessons only; beginners as the target market; price of £30 |
| Takes risks | The loan must be repaid even if a wet summer cuts bookings |

## Annotated model answer

**Question (Outline):** Outline **one** way Fernhill Honey has added value to its product. **[2]**

> Fernhill has changed the design of its jar to a distinctive hexagonal shape (point in context), which makes the honey look like a premium gift, so customers are willing to pay £7.00 rather than £4.50 (linked point).

Two linked points, both tied to Fernhill, match the Outline definition in Appendix 1 of the specification.

## Common mistakes

- Treating added value as profit.
- Naming a method of adding value without saying how it lets the business charge more or sell more.
- Writing that a USP lasts for ever.
- Describing the entrepreneur's roles in general terms with no evidence from the case.
- Mixing up goods and services when describing what the business produces.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Give** or **State:** a short answer from knowledge or from the case.
- **Explain:** a method of adding value, then how it changes customers' behaviour, then the effect on revenue.
- **Analyse:** link the method to the specific customers in the case and to price, sales or reputation.
- **Justify:** compare two methods, such as better quality or more convenience, and recommend one using the case evidence.
