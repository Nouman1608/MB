## Four types, one clean distinction each

| Type | Distinguishing feature |
|---|---|
| **Primary** | Early learning of norms/values, mainly within the family |
| **Secondary** | Later learning, through wider agencies (school, peers, media, workplace) |
| **Re-socialisation** | Learning new norms/values that replace previously learned ones, often after a major life change |
| **Inadequate** | Socialisation that fails to properly instil shared norms/values |

A common error is treating primary and secondary as vague synonyms —
the test is simple: is this the family, early on, or a wider agency,
later on?

## Agencies: name the mechanism, not just the agency

Listing "family, school, peers, media, religion, workplace" earns
little on its own. Pair each with its specific process:

- **Family** — direct instruction and role modelling.
- **School** — the formal curriculum, plus the "hidden curriculum" of
implicit rules and expectations.
- **Media** — modelling behaviours and norms through the content it
presents.
- **Peers, religion, workplace** — each has its own norm-transmission
mechanism; be ready to state one for whichever agencies your revision
covers in depth.

## Four perspectives — one claim, one mechanism each

| Perspective | View of socialisation |
|---|---|
| **Functionalist** | Instils shared norms/values, creating value consensus; agencies function positively for social cohesion |
| **Marxist** | Ruling class uses it to instil obedience and acceptance of inequality, reproducing capitalism |
| **Feminist** | Reproduces gender roles and inequality — e.g. family/media modelling different expectations for boys and girls |
| **Social action/interactionist** | Individuals actively interpret and negotiate norms, rather than passively absorbing them |

**The functionalist/Marxist contrast is the most tested pair**: both
agree socialisation instils values, but functionalism frames this as
broadly beneficial and consensual, while Marxism frames it as serving
one class's interests over another's.

## Structuring a "compare two perspectives" answer

1. State perspective A's claim.
2. State perspective B's claim.
3. Give an **explicit point of contrast** — not just two descriptions
placed side by side.

Skipping step 3 is the single most common reason a comparison answer
under-scores despite covering both perspectives accurately.

## Key term precision

- **Socialisation** — the lifelong process of learning a culture's
norms, values and behaviours.
- **Norms** — specific, unwritten rules of behaviour for a given
situation.
- **Values** — broader beliefs about what is important or desirable.
- **Social construction of identity** — the idea that identity is built
through interaction with socialising agencies, not fixed or natural.

Mark schemes distinguish candidates using these terms precisely from
those using them as loose synonyms — keep norms and values clearly
separate, and be precise about what counts as an "agency" versus a
"process."

## Where this fits within the wider topic

3.2.2 Socialisation and Social Control has three parts: 3.2.2.1
Individuals and Society, 3.2.2.2 Socialisation (covered here), and
3.2.2.3 Social Identities and Social Change. These notes deliberately
do not cover the sociological imagination or concepts of culture
(3.2.2.1) or social control and identity (3.2.2.3) — revise those as
separate blocks so this section's socialisation-specific content stays
sharp rather than blurring into the adjacent sub-topics.

## Exam traps

- Confusing primary and secondary socialisation.
- Omitting re-socialisation and inadequate socialisation as distinct
concepts.
- Naming agencies without explaining their specific socialising
mechanism.
- Describing a perspective vaguely ("Marxists think it's about class")
rather than naming its specific mechanism.
- Comparing two perspectives without an explicit point of contrast.

## Using evidence, not just assertion

A strong answer on any of the four perspectives supports its claim with
a brief, concrete example rather than stating the perspective's view in
the abstract. For the feminist perspective, this might mean naming how
media content models different behavioural expectations for boys and
girls; for the social action perspective, naming a situation where an
individual visibly negotiates or resists an agency's expected norm.
Sociology mark schemes consistently reward this kind of applied,
evidenced point over an accurate but unsupported general statement.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish primary from secondary socialisation.
2. What is re-socialisation, and give an example trigger for it.
3. Name one specific mechanism each for family and school as agencies
of socialisation.
4. What is the key point of contrast between the functionalist and
Marxist views of socialisation?
5. What must a strong "compare two perspectives" answer include, beyond
describing each perspective?

**Answers:** 1. Primary socialisation is early learning of norms and
values mainly within the family; secondary socialisation happens later,
through wider agencies like school, peers, media and the workplace. 2.
Learning new norms and values that replace previously learned ones,
often triggered by a major life change (e.g. joining the military,
migration, imprisonment — any valid example). 3. Family: direct
instruction and role modelling; school: the formal curriculum and the
"hidden curriculum" of implicit rules. 4. Functionalism presents
socialisation as broadly beneficial and consensual (creating value
consensus); Marxism presents it as serving the ruling class's interests
by instilling obedience and acceptance of inequality. 5. An explicit
point of contrast between the two perspectives, not just two separate
descriptions placed side by side.
