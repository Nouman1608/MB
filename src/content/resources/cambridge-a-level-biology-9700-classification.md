This guide teaches section 18.1 Classification from Topic 18, Classification, biodiversity and conservation, in the Cambridge International AS & A Level Biology 9700 syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027. It covers all six learning outcomes. This is A Level content, examined on Paper 4.

The [Classification, biodiversity and conservation study guide](/resources/a-level-biology-9700-classification-biodiversity-and-conservation/) gives a one-table summary of each idea. This page goes further: how to use each species concept on an unfamiliar example, why ribosomal RNA was the key to the three domains, how to place an organism from a description, and how to classify a virus from data. Afterwards use the [Classification revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-biology-9700-classification-revision-notes/) and the [Classification practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-biology-9700-classification-practice/).

## Species concepts in use (18.1.1)

"Discuss" means you must weigh each concept: what it uses as its test, and where it breaks down. The table puts that side by side.

| Concept | Test for "same species" | Works well for | Breaks down when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Biological | Can interbreed to produce **fertile** offspring | Sexually reproducing animals and plants living today | Organisms reproduce asexually (bacteria, many fungi); only fossils exist; populations never meet, so interbreeding cannot be tested; some species hybridise in the wild |
| Morphological | Share the same structural features | Fossils, museum specimens, quick field identification | Males and females look different; larvae look nothing like adults; two species look almost identical but do not interbreed; one species varies greatly in form |
| Ecological | Occupy the same niche | Explaining why similar forms coexist or not | Niches are hard to measure; one species may occupy different niches in different places or at different life stages |

**Applying the concepts.** Suppose two groups of snails look identical, live in the same stream, but mate in different seasons and never produce hybrids. The morphological concept would call them one species; the biological concept would call them two. Answers that state which concept gives which verdict, and why, earn the "discuss" marks.

Remember that a horse and a donkey can mate, but the mule produced is infertile. That is why the biological definition specifies *fertile* offspring.

## The three domains (18.1.2 and 18.1.3)

A **domain** is the highest rank in classification, above kingdom. All cellular organisms belong to one of three:

- **Bacteria**: prokaryotes, the familiar bacteria.
- **Archaea**: prokaryotes, many living in extreme conditions such as hot springs or very salty water, though others live in ordinary soils and oceans.
- **Eukarya**: all organisms whose cells have a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles.

The three-domain system came from comparing the base sequences of **ribosomal RNA** (rRNA). rRNA is found in every cell and changes slowly, so it can compare even very distant organisms. Work led by Carl Woese, starting in the 1970s, showed that archaeal rRNA differs from bacterial rRNA as much as either differs from eukaryotic rRNA, and in 1990 Woese and colleagues proposed the three domains. Two groups that look alike under a microscope (both prokaryotic) turned out to be fundamentally different.

The syllabus limits the Archaea–Bacteria differences to three:

| Feature | Archaea | Bacteria |
|---|---|---|
| Membrane lipids | Branched hydrocarbon chains joined to glycerol by **ether** bonds | Unbranched fatty acids joined to glycerol by **ester** bonds |
| Ribosomal RNA | Base sequences distinct from bacteria (in some ways closer to Eukarya) | Base sequences distinct from archaea |
| Cell wall | **No peptidoglycan** | Cell wall contains **peptidoglycan** (murein) |

Both have 70S ribosomes, circular DNA and no nucleus; these similarities are why both are prokaryotes. Do not list them as differences.

## The taxonomic hierarchy (18.1.4)

Within Eukarya, organisms are grouped into **seven ranks**, each containing fewer, more closely related organisms than the one above:

**kingdom → phylum → class → order → family → genus → species**

A memory aid many students use: **K**ing **P**hilip **C**ame **O**ver **F**or **G**ood **S**oup.

**Worked example: the lion.**

| Rank | Taxon | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Kingdom | Animalia | Multicellular heterotroph, no cell walls |
| Phylum | Chordata | Has a notochord (backbone in vertebrates) |
| Class | Mammalia | Hair, mammary glands |
| Order | Carnivora | Teeth adapted to eating meat |
| Family | Felidae | Cats |
| Genus | *Panthera* | Large cats |
| Species | *Panthera leo* | Lion |

The binomial name is **genus + species**. Write it in italics (underline each word when handwritten), with a capital letter for the genus only: *Panthera leo*. A tiger, *Panthera tigris*, shares every rank with the lion down to genus, which is why they are described as closely related.

Rules worth knowing for data questions: two organisms in the same genus must also share family, order, class, phylum and kingdom; two in the same order need not share a family.

## The four eukaryotic kingdoms (18.1.5)

The syllabus asks for an **outline** of characteristic features: a short list for each kingdom.

**Protoctista**
- Eukaryotic; mostly unicellular, some multicellular with little cell specialisation (for example seaweeds).
- Some photosynthesise (algae, with chloroplasts); some are heterotrophic (protozoa such as *Amoeba*, or *Plasmodium*, which causes malaria).
- A varied group of eukaryotes that do not fit the other three kingdoms.

**Fungi**
- Eukaryotic; no chlorophyll, so heterotrophic.
- Feed by secreting enzymes onto food and absorbing products (saprotrophic), or as parasites.
- Cell walls made of **chitin**.
- Body usually made of thread-like **hyphae** forming a **mycelium**; yeasts are unicellular.
- Store carbohydrate as **glycogen**; reproduce by **spores**.

**Plantae**
- Multicellular eukaryotes with cells differentiated into tissues and organs.
- Chloroplasts with chlorophyll; autotrophic by photosynthesis.
- Cell walls of **cellulose**; often a large permanent vacuole.
- Store carbohydrate as **starch** (and sucrose).

**Animalia**
- Multicellular eukaryotes with differentiated tissues and organs.
- No cell walls; heterotrophic, usually ingesting food.
- Nervous coordination; most can move.
- Store carbohydrate as **glycogen**.

### Placing an organism from a description

**Worked example.** *An organism is multicellular, has cell walls containing chitin, feeds by extracellular digestion of dead leaves and stores glycogen. Name its kingdom and give two reasons why it is not in Plantae.*

- Chitin walls + extracellular digestion + glycogen → **Fungi**.
- Not Plantae: no chloroplasts, so it cannot photosynthesise; its walls are chitin, not cellulose; it stores glycogen, not starch. Any two.

Note that "multicellular" and "has cell walls" do not discriminate between Fungi and Plantae. Pick features that do.

## Classifying viruses (18.1.6)

Viruses are **not cells**: they have no cytoplasm, no ribosomes and no metabolism of their own, so they belong to no domain. The syllabus limits their classification to two questions:

1. Is the nucleic acid **DNA or RNA**?
2. Is it **single-stranded (ss) or double-stranded (ds)**?

| Group | Examples |
|---|---|
| dsDNA | Herpes viruses, smallpox virus |
| ssDNA | Parvoviruses |
| dsRNA | Rotaviruses |
| ssRNA | HIV, influenza virus, measles virus, tobacco mosaic virus |

**Using base composition data.** In a double-stranded molecule, bases pair, so A = T (or A = U in RNA) and C = G. If a nucleic acid contains uracil and the percentages are A 29%, U 20%, G 26%, C 25%, then A ≠ U and G ≠ C: it is **single-stranded RNA**. If thymine were present with A = T and C = G, it would be double-stranded DNA.

## Common mistakes

- Defining a biological species without the word **fertile**.
- Listing "no nucleus" as a difference between Archaea and Bacteria; it is a similarity.
- Saying archaea have peptidoglycan walls, or swapping ether and ester bonds.
- Putting the ranks in the wrong order, especially order and family, or class and order.
- Writing *Panthera Leo* with a capital species name, or not italicising.
- Saying fungi photosynthesise, or that fungal walls are cellulose.
- Placing viruses in Bacteria or Protoctista. They are outside the domains.

## Exam technique

- **Discuss** (18.1.1): for each concept give its test and a limitation, and apply it to the example if one is given.
- **Describe** (18.1.2, 18.1.4): state the names in order; for the hierarchy, give all seven ranks.
- **State** (18.1.3): one difference per feature, with both sides (archaea *and* bacteria).
- **Outline** (18.1.5, 18.1.6): brief listed features, using the precise terms chitin, cellulose, glycogen, hyphae, ss and ds.
