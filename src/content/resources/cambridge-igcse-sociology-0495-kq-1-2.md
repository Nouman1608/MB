Key question 1.2 of Cambridge IGCSE Sociology (0495) asks *What types of data and evidence do
sociologists use?* It matters twice in Paper 1 Question 1: part (a) gives you a printed source to
interpret and criticise, and part (e) asks which secondary evidence you would use in your own plan.
To score well you need three things: clear distinctions between kinds of data, a strength and a
limitation for each type of secondary evidence, and a routine for reading tables, charts and graphs.

For how this fits with research approaches and research design, see the
[research approaches, data and investigation overview](/resources/igcse-cambridge-sociology-research-approaches-data-and-investigation/).

## Syllabus coverage

Cambridge IGCSE Sociology 0495, syllabus for 2025, 2026 and 2027, **1.2 What types of data and
evidence do sociologists use?**

- **1.2.1 Sources of data** — differences between primary and secondary sources of data; differences
  between qualitative and quantitative data; strengths and limitations of each type of data.
- **1.2.2 Secondary evidence** — examples of official statistics, historical and personal documents,
  digital sources and media content; strengths and limitations of each type of evidence.
- **1.2.3 Analysis, interpretation and evaluation of data from qualitative and quantitative
  sources** — summaries of sociological studies, diagrams, tables, charts and graphs.

## Two separate distinctions, four combinations

Students often blur two different questions:

1. **Who produced the data?** If the researcher collected it for this study, it is **primary**. If it
   already existed, produced by someone else for another purpose, it is **secondary**.
2. **What form is the data in?** Numbers are **quantitative**; words, images and descriptions are
   **qualitative**.

The two distinctions cross, giving four combinations:

| | Quantitative | Qualitative |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary** | Results of your own closed-question questionnaire | Transcripts of your own unstructured interviews |
| **Secondary** | Government census or crime figures | Diaries, letters, news articles, social media posts |

So "secondary" does not mean "qualitative", and "primary" does not mean "numbers".

## Strengths and limitations of each type of data (1.2.1)

| Type | Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary** | Collected to fit the research aims; the researcher knows how it was gathered and can control quality | Costly and slow; access to some groups may be refused; limited in scale |
| **Secondary** | Cheap or free; often covers very large populations; allows study of the past and comparison over time | Produced for other purposes, so may not measure what the researcher needs; definitions may be unclear or changed; possible bias of the producer |
| **Quantitative** | Easy to compare and present in tables; shows patterns, trends and correlations; usually reliable | Can lack depth and meaning; categories chosen by the researcher may not fit people's experience |
| **Qualitative** | Rich detail and meaning; high validity; lets people speak in their own words | Hard to compare and summarise; often small samples; interpretation can be subjective |

## Secondary evidence in depth (1.2.2)

### Official statistics

Numerical data produced by governments and their agencies, such as a national **census**,
registrations of births, marriages and deaths, unemployment figures, examination results and
recorded crime.

- **Strengths:** large-scale and often near-complete coverage; collected regularly, so trends can be
  tracked; free to use; allow comparisons between regions or countries.
- **Limitations:** they record only what the state chooses to count; definitions change (for example
  how unemployment is measured), breaking up trends; some statistics are **socially constructed** —
  crime figures, for example, depend on whether victims report offences and police record them, so
  many crimes stay hidden (the "dark figure" of crime).

J. Maxwell Atkinson's *Discovering Suicide* (1978) is a classic interpretivist criticism: he argued
that whether a death is recorded as suicide depends on how coroners interpret clues such as notes
or the manner of death, so suicide statistics reflect officials' judgements as well as behaviour.

### Historical and personal documents

**Historical documents** include parish registers, old official reports and archives; **personal
documents** include diaries, letters, autobiographies and photographs. Peter Laslett used parish
records in *The World We Have Lost* (1965) to argue that most households in pre-industrial England
were small, challenging the belief that large extended families were the norm before
industrialisation. Thomas and Znaniecki's *The Polish Peasant in Europe and America* (1918–1920)
drew on letters between migrants and their families.

- **Strengths:** the only evidence available about the past; give insight into personal meanings;
  produced without a researcher present, so no interviewer effect.
- **Limitations:** questions of **authenticity** (is it genuine?) and **credibility** (is it sincere
  and accurate?); people who wrote diaries and letters were not typical of all people, so documents
  may be unrepresentative; documents may be lost or incomplete.

### Digital sources

Websites, blogs, social media posts, online forums, search data and other material produced online.

- **Strengths:** vast amounts of current data; often produced naturally rather than in response to a
  researcher; can reach groups that are hard to meet in person; cheap to collect.
- **Limitations:** hard to verify who produced material and whether it is true; online users are not
  representative of whole populations (age and income affect internet use); posts are often a
  performed or edited self; ethical problems over **informed consent** and **privacy** when posts are
  collected without users knowing.

### Media content

Newspapers, television, films, advertising, music and video. Sociologists often use **content
analysis**, counting how often certain images or words appear. The Glasgow University Media Group's
*Bad News* (1976) analysed television news coverage of industrial disputes and argued that it tended
to favour management's view over workers'.

- **Strengths:** shows how groups and issues are represented; easy to access; can be compared over
  time.
- **Limitations:** content reflects the agenda of owners, editors and journalists rather than
  reality; counting images can miss how audiences interpret them; deciding categories involves the
  researcher's own judgement.

## Analysing sources (1.2.3)

The syllabus lists five forms: summaries of sociological studies, diagrams, tables, charts and
graphs. Use the same four-step routine for each.

1. **Read the labels first** — title, axis labels, units, dates, who produced it and sample size.
2. **Find the exact figure or trend asked for**, and quote it with its unit ("34%", "rose from 12 to
   19 per 1,000").
3. **Describe the pattern** — highest, lowest, rising, falling, a gap between groups.
4. **Question the evidence** — sample size, how it was collected, missing groups, age of the data,
   unclear definitions, correlation not causation.

### Worked example: interpreting a table

*Source (invented for practice): A survey of 400 adults in one city asked how often they read news
online. Results by age group:*

| Age group | Daily | Weekly | Rarely or never |
|---|---|---|---|
| 16–29 | 71% | 20% | 9% |
| 30–49 | 58% | 27% | 15% |
| 50 and over | 33% | 30% | 37% |

- **Identify:** 37% of people aged 50 and over rarely or never read news online.
- **Describe:** daily online news reading falls with age, from 71% of 16–29s to 33% of those aged 50
  and over.
- **Problems with the evidence:** the table does not say how many people were in each age group, so
  a percentage may be based on few respondents; the study covers one city, so findings may not be
  generalisable to rural areas; it measures self-reported behaviour, which may lack validity; and
  if the survey was conducted online, older people who do not use the internet would be under-
  represented, making the gap look smaller than it really is.

The last point earns credit because it links a design feature to a specific distortion — the
skill examiners look for in Question 1(a)(iii).

### Summaries of sociological studies

When a source summarises a study in words, ask: what method was used, how many people, chosen how,
and when? A short summary often hides weaknesses — such as a volunteer sample — that you can point
out.

## Common mistakes

- Calling any statistic "unreliable" without a reason. Say *why*: changing definitions, under-
  reporting, a different recording practice.
- Confusing **primary/secondary** with **quantitative/qualitative**.
- Copying the whole table instead of selecting the one figure asked for.
- Forgetting units or misreading "per 1,000" as a percentage.
- Treating digital sources as automatically representative because they are large.

## Exam technique

- **Identify** and **Give** (from a source): one precise answer, quoted accurately. No explanation is
  needed.
- **Explain** a problem with evidence: name the problem, link it to the source, state its effect
  (for example, "only volunteers replied, so the sample may over-represent people with strong
  views").
- **Evaluate** the usefulness of official statistics or documents: strengths and limitations, then a
  judgement such as "useful for showing trends, less useful for understanding meanings".
- In Question 1(e), name a **specific** secondary source and give a reason, for example "school
  attendance records, because they cover every student and are free".
