These questions cover section 3.5.2 Analysing financial performance of the AQA A-level Business (7132) specification. All businesses and figures are invented. Revise first with the [financial performance study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-analysing-financial-performance/) and the [financial performance revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-analysing-financial-performance-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** A product sells for £25 and has a variable cost of £16 per unit. What is the contribution per unit? **[1]**

A: £9

B: £16

C: £25

D: £41

**2.** Which **one** of the following is a favourable variance? **[1]**

A: Actual sales revenue is below budget

B: Actual materials cost is above budget

C: Actual wages cost is below budget

D: Actual advertising spend is above budget

**3.** Define the term "margin of safety". **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** value of budgeting to a business. **[3]**

**5.** Quillon Candles sells candles at £8 each. Variable cost is £3 a candle and fixed costs are £30,000 a year.

**(a)** Calculate the break-even output. **[2]**

**(b)** Sales are 8,000 candles. Calculate the margin of safety in units. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the profit at 8,000 candles. **[2]**

**6.** A small business forecasts: January opening balance £2,000, receipts £18,000, payments £21,000; February receipts £24,000, payments £20,000.

**(a)** Calculate the closing balance for January. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the closing balance for February. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain **one** action the business could take to deal with the January position. **[2]**

**7.** A department's budget was revenue £60,000 and costs £42,000. Actual revenue was £57,000 and actual costs were £44,000.

**(a)** Calculate the variance for revenue and for costs, stating whether each is adverse or favourable. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the variance for profit. **[1]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**8.** Analyse **two** ways in which break-even analysis could help the owner of a new café. **[6]**

**9.** A growing hotel chain is introducing budgets for each hotel. Assess whether budgeting is likely to improve the chain's performance. **[9]**

**10.** Quillon Candles' variable cost per candle rises from £3 to £4, with the price and fixed costs unchanged. Analyse the effects on its break-even output, margin of safety (at sales of 8,000) and profit. **[6]**

**11.** Explain **two** reasons why a cash flow forecast may turn out to be inaccurate. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate the usefulness of profit margins in judging the performance of a retailer. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** **A** [1]. £25 − £16 = £9. **[1]**

**2.** **C** [1]. Lower costs than budget raise profit, so the variance is favourable; the other options reduce profit. **[1]**

**3.** The difference between actual (or budgeted) output and the break-even output [1], showing how far sales can fall before the business makes a loss [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Budgets set targets that give managers a clear aim [1], and by comparing actual results with the budget, the business can see variances early [1] and take action, such as cutting costs where they are overspent, before losses grow [1]. Accept: control of spending; coordination of departments; motivation. **[3]**

**5. (a)** Contribution = £8 − £3 = £5 [1]; break-even = 30,000 ÷ 5 = **6,000 candles** [1].

**(b)** 8,000 − 6,000 = **2,000 candles** [1].

**(c)** Total contribution = 8,000 × £5 = £40,000 [1]; profit = £40,000 − £30,000 = **£10,000** [1]. **[5]**

**6. (a)** £2,000 + £18,000 − £21,000 = **−£1,000** [1].

**(b)** −£1,000 + £24,000 − £20,000 = **£3,000** [1].

**(c)** Arrange an overdraft for January [1], so that wages and suppliers can be paid, with the forecast showing the position recovering in February so it is only short-term borrowing [1]. Accept: asking customers to pay sooner; delaying payments to suppliers. **[4]**

**7. (a)** Revenue: £60,000 − £57,000 = £3,000 adverse [1]. Costs: £44,000 − £42,000 = £2,000 adverse [1].

**(b)** Budgeted profit £18,000; actual profit £13,000; variance **£5,000 adverse** [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Benefit 1: it shows how many cups and meals must be sold to cover costs [1]. If fixed costs are £60,000 and contribution per customer £4, break-even is 15,000 customers a year, so the owner can judge whether that is realistic for the location [1], which helps decide whether to open or apply for a loan [1]. Benefit 2: it supports pricing decisions [1]. The owner can see how a price change alters break-even, for example a higher price lowering the number of sales needed [1], so the café can weigh that against the risk of losing customers [1]. Credit also: margin of safety shows the risk. **[6]**

**9.** For: budgets give each hotel manager clear targets and spending limits [1]. Comparing actual results with budgets shows which hotels are over- or under-performing [1], so central managers can act early [1]. Budgets can also motivate managers if targets are agreed with them [1]. Against: budgets for hotels depend on forecasts of occupancy, which may be wrong [1]. Managers may pad their budgets or overspend at the end of the year to avoid a cut [1]. A focus on cost targets might harm service quality [1]. Judgement: budgeting is likely to improve performance if targets are realistic, agreed with managers and reviewed often [1], with flexibility to change them when conditions alter [1]. **[9]**

**10.** New contribution per candle = £8 − £4 = £4 [1]. Break-even output rises to 30,000 ÷ 4 = **7,500 candles** (from 6,000) [1]. Margin of safety at 8,000 candles falls to 8,000 − 7,500 = **500 candles** (from 2,000) [1], so the business is far more exposed to a fall in sales [1]. Profit falls to 8,000 × £4 − £30,000 = **£2,000** (from £10,000) [1]. Quillon could respond by raising its price, finding cheaper wax or cutting waste [1]. **[6]**

**11.** Reason 1: sales are uncertain [1]. If demand is lower than forecast, receipts will be smaller than predicted [1]. Reason 2: customers may pay late [1], so receipts arrive in a later month than the forecast assumes; unexpected costs, such as a repair or price rise, may also increase payments [1]. **[4]**

**12.** For: margins show how much profit is made from each pound of sales, which allows comparison between years and with competitors of different size [1]. A falling gross margin suggests rising purchase costs or discounting [1]. A falling operating margin suggests overheads are not under control [1]. Margins are simple to calculate from the published accounts [1]. Against: margins ignore the amount of profit; a supermarket's low margin on huge sales can earn more than a boutique's high margin [1]. Retail types differ, so comparisons must be with similar retailers [1]. Margins are based on past data and tell nothing about cash flow [1]. They ignore the capital used to earn the profit [1]. Seasonal effects and one-off items can distort a single year [1]. Factors: the type of retailer, the stage of the business and the purpose of the analysis [1]. Judgement: margins are a useful starting point but need to be combined with profit amounts, return on capital, cash flow and non-financial measures such as customer satisfaction [1], and with trends over several years [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* Work out the figure, then state what it means for the business and what should be investigated next.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Marking a cost overspend as favourable.
- Leaving fixed costs out of the total cost line.
- Using total contribution instead of contribution per unit in the break-even formula.
- Mixing up gross, operating and net margins.
- Calculating a closing balance without carrying forward the opening balance.
- Describing what a figure is without saying what it means.
