These questions cover subtopic 6.7 of Cambridge IGCSE ICT 0417: the characteristics and uses of patient records and pharmacy records, and 3D printing of prosthetics, tissue, artificial blood vessels and customised medicines. All hospitals, patients and scenarios are invented.

## Section A: short answers

**1.** Which **one** of the following would be the key field in a hospital's patient table? **[1]**

A: Surname

B: Date of birth

C: Patient ID number

D: Blood group

**2.** Give **three** items of data stored in a patient record, other than name and address. **[3]**

**3.** State what is meant by a *prosthetic*. **[1]**

**4.** State **one** reason why a pharmacy system stores a patient's allergies. **[1]**

**5.** State what is meant by *tissue engineering*. **[1]**

## Section B: structured questions

**6.** A patient is brought unconscious into a hospital emergency department. The hospital has computerised patient records.

**(a)** Describe how staff can use the records to treat the patient safely. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain **one** reason why the records must be protected. **[2]**

**7.** A pharmacy uses a computerised system.

**(a)** Describe what the system does when a prescription is entered. **[3]**

**(b)** Describe how the system manages the pharmacy's stock of medicines. **[2]**

**8.** A seven-year-old child needs a prosthetic hand.

**(a)** Describe how the prosthetic would be produced using a 3D printer. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain **one** advantage of a 3D-printed prosthetic for this child. **[2]**

**9.** A patient takes four different tablets each morning. Her doctor suggests a customised 3D-printed tablet. Explain **two** benefits for the patient. **[4]**

**10.** A hospital moves from paper notes to computerised patient records. Give **two** problems the hospital might face during the change. **[2]**

## Section C: extended questions

**11.** Discuss the advantages and disadvantages of storing patient records on a computer system shared by all the hospitals in a region. **[6]**

**12.** Evaluate the use of 3D printers in medicine. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** **C** [1]. It is unique to each patient; names and dates of birth can be shared by more than one person. **[1]**

**2.** Allergies [1]; medical history [1]; current medicines [1] (also credit: date of birth, blood group, test results, next of kin, appointments). **[3]**

**3.** An artificial body part, such as a hand or leg [1]. **[1]**

**4.** So that a prescription for a medicine the patient is allergic to is flagged before it is dispensed [1]. **[1]**

**5.** Using a 3D printer to build up layers of living cells to form tissue [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** Staff identify the patient, for example from an ID card, and open the record [1]; they check allergies so that no harmful medicine is given [1]; they check blood group, medical history and current medicines before treatment [1].

**(b)** Medical records contain highly sensitive personal data [1]; if they were seen by unauthorised people the patient's privacy would be breached and the hospital could break data protection law [1]. **[5]**

**7. (a)** The system finds the patient's record using the patient ID [1]; it checks the new medicine against the patient's allergies and current medicines [1]; it warns the pharmacist about any interaction or unusual dose, and otherwise prints a label with the dosage instructions [1].

**(b)** Each time a medicine is dispensed the stock level is reduced [1]; when it falls to the reorder level, a new order is placed automatically [1]. **[5]**

**8. (a)** The child's arm and hand are scanned [1]; the scan is used to design a prosthetic that fits exactly using CAD software [1]; the design is sent to the 3D printer, which builds it layer by layer [1].

**(b)** As the child grows, a new, larger hand can be printed quickly and cheaply from an updated design [1], so the child always has a prosthetic that fits comfortably [1]. **[5]**

**9.** All four medicines can be combined into one tablet [1], so she is less likely to forget a tablet or take the wrong one [1]. The dose can be set exactly for her [1], so she gets the right amount even if it is not a standard dose made by manufacturers [1] (also credit: layers can release medicines at different times). **[4]**

**10.** Paper notes must be typed or scanned into the new system, which takes time and may introduce errors [1]; staff need training before they can use the system confidently [1] (also credit: cost of hardware and software; records unavailable during system failures). **[2]**

**11.** Advantages: a patient's full record is available at any hospital in the region, even in an emergency [1]; test results are shared instantly, so tests are not repeated [1]; there are no paper notes to be lost or misread [1]. Disadvantages: one shared system is an attractive target for hackers, so a breach could expose many patients' data [1]; more staff can access the system, so strict access levels and passwords are needed [1]; if the network fails, every hospital may lose access to records at once [1]. **[6]**

**12.** For: items such as prosthetics are customised to fit the individual [1]; they can be made faster and often more cheaply than by traditional methods [1]; tissue engineering may reduce the need for donors [1]. Against: printers and suitable materials are expensive [1]; printing living tissue is still being developed and must be tested carefully before use in patients [1]. Judgement: 3D printing is already valuable for prosthetics and customised medicines, while its use for tissue and blood vessels is promising but still developing [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Listing the contents of a record when the question asks how it is used.
- Forgetting the allergy and interaction check in pharmacy questions.
- Describing 3D printing without the scan and design stages.
- Stating that printed organs are routine.
