These questions cover the section 3.5 statement on the limitations of financial statements and ratio analysis, including financial and non-financial factors. Revise first with the [limitations study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-9215-limitations-of-financial-statements-oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting/) and the [limitations revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-9215-limitations-of-financial-statements-oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-revision-notes/). All businesses and figures are invented, and amounts are in dollars.

## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between a financial limitation and a non-financial limitation of ratio analysis, giving an example of each. **[2]**

**2.** State **two** non-financial factors a supplier would consider before offering credit to a new customer. **[2]**

**3.** A business calculates trade receivable days using total revenue, although a third of its sales are for cash. What is the effect?

A: Receivable days are overstated

B: Receivable days are understated

C: Receivable days are unaffected

D: Payable days are understated

**[1]**

**4.** Explain why the current ratio calculated from a statement of financial position may not represent the business's normal liquidity. **[2]**

**5.** Explain how the money measurement concept limits the usefulness of financial statements. **[2]**

**6.** Explain how two businesses with identical trading could report different returns on capital employed because of their depreciation methods. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Corran Supplies made credit sales of 182,500 and cash sales of 73,000 in the year. Trade receivables at the year end were 25,000. It allows customers 30 days' credit.

**(a)** Calculate trade receivable days using credit sales. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate trade receivable days using total revenue. **[2]**
**(c)** Comment on the difference between your answers. **[2]**

**8.** Two printing businesses each made profit from operations of 45,000. Inkwell's capital employed is 150,000; most of its machines are ten years old. Pressfirst's capital employed is 300,000; its machines were bought last year.

**(a)** Calculate the return on capital employed of each business. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why this comparison may mislead an investor. **[3]**

**9.** Halvergate Hardware's revenue was 400,000 in Year 1 and 432,000 in Year 3. Over the same two years, prices of the goods it sells rose by 12%.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage increase in revenue. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate Year 3 revenue at Year 1 prices. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain what this shows about the business's performance. **[2]**

**10.** A shop sells two product ranges. Range A always earns a gross profit margin of 40% and Range B always earns 20%. In Year 1 it sold 60,000 of A and 40,000 of B. In Year 2 it sold 40,000 of A and 60,000 of B.

**(a)** Calculate the overall gross profit margin for each year. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain why the change in margin should not be blamed on price cuts or higher purchase costs. **[2]**

**11.** Reyna is a sole trader with revenue of 120,000 and profit for the year of 27,000. Brightway Ltd, a similar business, also has revenue of 120,000; it pays its only director a salary of 21,000 and its profit for the year is 6,000.

**(a)** Calculate profit in relation to revenue for each business. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the comparison is misleading, and show an adjusted figure for Reyna. **[3]**

**12.** Malik is thinking of buying a share in a café. He has been given the café's ratios for the last two years. Evaluate the usefulness of these ratios to Malik. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** A financial limitation is a weakness in the figures themselves, for example statements use historic cost and ignore inflation [1]. A non-financial limitation is something important that the statements do not record at all, for example the morale of staff [1].

**2.** Any two: the customer's reputation for paying on time; the experience of its managers; whether its market is growing; the level of competition it faces; references from other suppliers [1] [1].

**3.** B [1]. Including cash sales makes the denominator too large.

**4.** It is based on one date, which may be untypical, for example before or after a busy season [1]; the business may also have arranged transactions just before the year end, such as paying suppliers, to change the ratio [1].

**5.** Only items that can be given a money value are recorded [1], so factors such as staff skill, customer loyalty and reputation, which affect future performance, are left out [1].

**6.** Reducing balance charges more depreciation in early years than straight line, so the business using it reports lower profit [1] and a lower net book value; both profit and capital employed change, so ROCE differs although trading is the same [1].

**7. (a)** 25,000 ÷ 182,500 × 365 [1] = **50.0 days** [1].

**(b)** 25,000 ÷ 255,500 × 365 [1] = **35.7 days** [1].

**(c)** Using total revenue suggests customers pay in about 36 days, close to the 30-day terms [1]; the correct figure of 50 days shows credit customers take 20 days longer than allowed, so credit control needs attention [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** Inkwell: 45,000 ÷ 150,000 × 100 = **30%** [1]. Pressfirst: 45,000 ÷ 300,000 × 100 = **15%** [1].

**(b)** Capital employed is based on historic cost less depreciation [1]. Inkwell's old machines are heavily depreciated, so its capital employed is low and its ROCE looks high [1]. Inkwell will soon need to replace its machines at current prices, which will raise its capital employed and lower its ROCE, so Pressfirst may be no less efficient [1]. **[5]**

**9. (a)** (432,000 − 400,000) ÷ 400,000 × 100 = **8%** [1].

**(b)** 432,000 ÷ 1.12 [1] = **385,714** (about 385,700) [1].

**(c)** In real terms revenue has fallen by about 3.6% [1], so the volume of goods sold has gone down; the 8% increase only reflects higher prices, and the statements, which ignore inflation, overstate growth [1]. **[5]**

**10. (a)** Year 1: gross profit 24,000 + 8,000 = 32,000 [1]; margin 32,000 ÷ 100,000 × 100 = **32%** [1]. Year 2: 16,000 + 12,000 = 28,000 [1]; margin **28%** [1].

**(b)** The margin on each range is unchanged [1]; the fall is caused by the shift in product mix towards the lower-margin Range B, which the overall ratio hides [1]. **[6]**

**11. (a)** Reyna: 27,000 ÷ 120,000 × 100 = **22.5%** [1]. Brightway: 6,000 ÷ 120,000 × 100 = **5%** [1].

**(b)** Reyna's reward for her work is taken as drawings, which are not an expense, while Brightway charges the director's salary as an expense [1]. Deducting a similar 21,000 for Reyna's work gives 27,000 − 21,000 = 6,000 [1], which is 5% of revenue, the same as Brightway, so the businesses perform similarly [1]. **[5]**

**12.** Usefulness: the ratios let Malik compare the café's profitability and liquidity over two years [1], for example whether the gross profit margin is stable and whether the café can pay its suppliers [1]. Limitations: the figures are historic, and two years is a short trend [1]; a café is seasonal, so liquidity ratios at one year-end date may be untypical [1]; the ratios ignore non-financial factors such as the location, the chef's skill, reviews and new competitors nearby [1]. Judgement: the ratios are a useful first check, but Malik should also look at forecasts, compare with similar cafés and visit the business before investing [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Generic limitations with no link to the business or ratio in the question.
- Using total revenue for receivable days when credit sales are given.
- No judgement at the end of an evaluate answer.
- Treating a rise in revenue as growth without considering price rises.
