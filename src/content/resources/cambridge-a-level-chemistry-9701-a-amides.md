This guide covers subtopic **34.3, Amides**, from Topic 34, Nitrogen compounds, of Cambridge
International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is A Level content. The combined
[Amides and Amino Acids study guide](/resources/a-amides-and-amino-acids/) introduces each reaction
with ethanamide; this page extends them to N-substituted amides, which is where most exam marks are
lost, and adds structured ways to identify an unknown amide.

## What the syllabus asks for

Subtopic 34.3 has three learning outcomes:

1. recall the production of amides by "the reaction between ammonia and an acyl chloride at room
   temperature" and "the reaction between a primary amine and an acyl chloride at room temperature"
2. describe the reactions of amides: "hydrolysis with aqueous alkali or aqueous acid" and "the
   reduction of the CO group in amides with LiAlH₄ to form an amine"
3. state and explain why amides are much weaker bases than amines

## Naming amides

The amide group is –CONH–. The carbon chain including the C=O carbon gives the stem name, ending
in **-amide**. Groups on nitrogen are shown with **N-**.

| Structure | Name | Type |
|---|---|---|
| CH₃CONH₂ | ethanamide | primary amide (two H on N) |
| CH₃CH₂CONH₂ | propanamide | primary amide |
| C₆H₅CONH₂ | benzamide | primary amide |
| CH₃CONHCH₃ | N-methylethanamide | N-substituted (one H on N) |
| CH₃CONHC₆H₅ | N-phenylethanamide | N-substituted |
| CH₃CON(CH₃)₂ | N,N-dimethylethanamide | N,N-disubstituted (no H on N) |

A useful check: N-methylethanamide and propanamide are isomers, C₃H₇NO. The position of the extra
carbon (on N or in the chain) changes the name and every reaction product.

## Producing amides

Ammonia or a primary amine acts as a nucleophile towards the acyl chloride, by addition–elimination,
at **room temperature**:

    CH₃CH₂COCl + NH₃ → CH₃CH₂CONH₂ + HCl              (propanamide)
    CH₃CH₂COCl + CH₃CH₂NH₂ → CH₃CH₂CONHCH₂CH₃ + HCl   (N-ethylpropanamide)

To plan an amide synthesis, split the amide at the **C–N bond**. The carbonyl side becomes the acyl
chloride; the nitrogen side becomes ammonia or the amine. With excess ammonia or amine, the HCl is
neutralised to NH₄Cl or the alkylammonium chloride.

## Hydrolysis

Amides are much less reactive than acyl chlorides, so hydrolysis needs **heating under reflux** with
aqueous acid or aqueous alkali. The **C–N bond breaks**. What you get depends on the conditions,
because an acid or base is always present to react with one of the products.

**In aqueous acid** (for example HCl(aq)): the carboxylic acid is released, and the nitrogen part is
protonated.

    CH₃CONH₂ + H₂O + HCl → CH₃COOH + NH₄Cl
    CH₃CONHCH₃ + H₂O + HCl → CH₃COOH + CH₃NH₃⁺Cl⁻

**In aqueous alkali** (for example NaOH(aq)): the carboxylic acid is deprotonated to its salt, and
ammonia or the amine is released.

    CH₃CONH₂ + NaOH → CH₃COONa + NH₃
    CH₃CONHCH₃ + NaOH → CH₃COONa + CH₃NH₂

| Conditions | Carbon-side product | Nitrogen-side product |
|---|---|---|
| aqueous acid, heat | carboxylic acid, RCOOH | ammonium or alkylammonium salt |
| aqueous alkali, heat | carboxylate salt, RCOO⁻Na⁺ | ammonia or amine (gas or volatile) |

**Using alkaline hydrolysis as evidence.** Warming a primary amide with NaOH(aq) releases ammonia,
which turns damp red litmus paper blue. A volatile amine from an N-substituted amide does the same,
so the litmus test shows that nitrogen was present, and the identity of the amine shows which kind
of amide it was.

## Reduction with LiAlH₄

LiAlH₄ reduces the **C=O group to CH₂**. The nitrogen keeps whatever groups it had:

    CH₃CONH₂ + 4[H] → CH₃CH₂NH₂ + H₂O          (primary amide → primary amine)
    CH₃CONHCH₃ + 4[H] → CH₃CH₂NHCH₃ + H₂O      (N-substituted amide → secondary amine)

Compare this with hydrolysis. Hydrolysis **breaks** the molecule at C–N; reduction **keeps** it whole
and changes only the carbonyl. A question that asks for a product "containing the same number of
carbon atoms" after LiAlH₄ is pointing you to the reduction.

## Why amides are much weaker bases than amines

**State:** amides are essentially neutral in water; they do not turn red litmus blue, whereas amines
give alkaline solutions.

**Explain:** in an amide the nitrogen is bonded to the carbonyl carbon. The lone pair on nitrogen
overlaps with the π bond of C=O and is **delocalised** over N, C and O. The electronegative oxygen
draws this electron density towards itself. As a result:

- the lone pair is **much less available** to accept a proton;
- the C–N bond gains partial double-bond character, and the N, C and O atoms with the groups
  attached to them lie in one plane.

In an amine, by contrast, the lone pair is localised on nitrogen, and alkyl groups push electron
density towards it.

| | Ethylamine, CH₃CH₂NH₂ | Ethanamide, CH₃CONH₂ |
|---|---|---|
| N lone pair | localised on N | delocalised over N–C=O |
| Effect of neighbour | ethyl group donates electron density | C=O withdraws electron density |
| Aqueous solution | alkaline | about neutral |
| With HCl(aq) at room temperature | forms CH₃CH₂NH₃⁺Cl⁻ | no salt formation; hydrolysis needs heat |

## Worked example 1: identifying an amide from its hydrolysis products

**Question.** Amide **A**, C₄H₉NO, is heated with NaOH(aq). The gas given off turns damp red litmus
blue and has a fishy smell. The remaining solution, after acidification, contains ethanoic acid.
Identify **A**.

**Answer.**

- Ethanoic acid means the acyl part is **CH₃CO–** (2 carbons).
- **A** has 4 carbons, so the nitrogen carries **2 carbons**.
- A fishy smell suggests an amine rather than ammonia, so the nitrogen part is **ethylamine**,
  CH₃CH₂NH₂ (dimethylamine is also possible from the formula alone, but it would make **A** an
  N,N-disubstituted amide, which is not a 34.3 product).
- **A** is **N-ethylethanamide, CH₃CONHCH₂CH₃**.

*Examiner's note:* count carbons on each side of the C–N bond. This one check sorts out most
amide-identification questions.

## Worked example 2: an annotated basicity answer

**Question.** Explain why ethanamide is a much weaker base than ethylamine. **[3]**

**Model answer.** In ethanamide the lone pair on nitrogen is delocalised into the adjacent C=O group
**[1]**, and the electronegative oxygen withdraws electron density from nitrogen **[1]**, so the lone
pair is much less available to accept a proton than in ethylamine, where the ethyl group donates
electron density to nitrogen **[1]**.

*Why answers lose marks:* describing only ethanamide and never mentioning ethylamine, or saying the
amide "has no lone pair".

## Common mistakes

- **Hydrolysing at room temperature.** Amides need heating under reflux.
- **Giving the free amine as the product of acid hydrolysis.** In acid it is protonated: write the
  ammonium or alkylammonium salt.
- **Giving the free acid as the product of alkaline hydrolysis.** In alkali it is the carboxylate
  salt.
- **Using hydrolysis when reduction is meant.** LiAlH₄ keeps the carbon skeleton and gives an amine.
- **Reducing an N-substituted amide to a primary amine.** The N substituent stays, so you get a
  secondary amine.
- **Calling amides basic** because they contain nitrogen.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Recall** production: acyl chloride + NH₃ or primary amine, room temperature.
- **Describe** hydrolysis: give the conditions (aqueous acid or alkali, heat) and both products in
  the correct form for those conditions.
- **State and explain** basicity: one sentence stating amides are much weaker bases, then the
  delocalisation argument with a comparison to an amine.
- **Deduce** an amide from data: split at C–N and count carbons.
