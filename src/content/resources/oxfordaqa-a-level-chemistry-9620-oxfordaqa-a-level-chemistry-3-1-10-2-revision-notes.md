## Core recall

- [H⁺] in aqueous solution covers a **very wide range**, so a **logarithmic** scale is used.
- **pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]**; **[H⁺] = 10^(−pH)**.
- Skills: convert [H⁺] ↔ pH; calculate the pH of a **strong acid** from its concentration.
- Monoprotic strong acid: **[H⁺] = [acid]**.
- pH has no units; give it to **2 decimal places**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| pH | −log₁₀ of the hydrogen ion concentration in mol dm⁻³ |
| [H⁺] | Concentration of hydrogen ions, mol dm⁻³ |
| Strong acid | Completely dissociated in aqueous solution |
| Monoprotic acid | Releases one H⁺ per molecule (HCl, HNO₃, HBr, HI) |
| Logarithmic scale | Each unit is a factor of 10 |
| Calibration | Setting a pH meter using buffer solutions of known pH |

## The scale in numbers

| Change in pH | Change in [H⁺] |
|---|---|
| Falls by 1 | × 10 |
| Falls by 2 | × 100 |
| Rises by 1 | ÷ 10 |
| Rises by 0.30 | ÷ 2 (approximately) |

Lower pH = more acidic = higher [H⁺].

## Method boxes

**[H⁺] → pH**

1. pH = −log₁₀[H⁺] (use log, not ln).
2. Check: [H⁺] between 10⁻ⁿ and 10⁻⁽ⁿ⁻¹⁾ means pH between n − 1 and n.

**pH → [H⁺]**

1. [H⁺] = 10^(−pH) (SHIFT + log on most calculators).
2. Give the answer in standard form with units.

**Strong acid from a mass**

1. n = mass ÷ Mr.
2. c = n ÷ volume in dm³.
3. [H⁺] = c (monoprotic); pH = −log₁₀[H⁺].

**Dilution of a strong acid**

1. New [H⁺] = old [H⁺] × (old volume ÷ new volume).
2. Tenfold dilution: pH rises by exactly 1.

**Mixing two strong acids**

1. Add the **moles** of H⁺ from each.
2. Divide by the **total** volume.
3. Never average pH values.

## Worked reminders

- **[H⁺] → pH**: 0.0060 mol dm⁻³ HCl → pH = −log₁₀(0.0060) = **2.22**. Check: between 10⁻³ and 10⁻², so between 2 and 3.
- **pH → [H⁺]**: pH 3.60 → [H⁺] = 10^(−3.60) = **2.51 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³**.
- **Comparing two solutions**: pH 2.50 against pH 3.20 is a difference of 0.70, so [H⁺] is 10^0.70 = **5.0 times** greater in the pH 2.50 solution.
- **Halving [H⁺]**: raises the pH by about **0.30**; doubling lowers it by about 0.30.
- **Target pH**: for pH 0.50 you need [H⁺] = 10^(−0.50) = **0.316 mol dm⁻³** of a monoprotic strong acid.

## Special cases

- **pH below 0**: [H⁺] above 1 mol dm⁻³; for example 2.0 mol dm⁻³ HCl has pH −0.30.
- **Very dilute acids**: pH approaches 7 but never exceeds it, because water supplies about 10⁻⁷ mol dm⁻³ H⁺ at 25 °C.
- **Sulfuric acid**: use [H⁺] = 2 × [H₂SO₄] **only** if told to assume complete dissociation.

## Determining pH

- **pH meter**: numerical reading to 2 decimal places; calibrate with buffers; rinse the probe with deionised water; wait for the reading to settle.
- **Universal indicator**: approximate pH from a colour only.
- A meter reading of pH 2.47 for a solution calculated to have pH 2.40 means the measured [H⁺] is 10^(−0.07), about 0.85 times the calculated value, so the solution is less concentrated than intended or the meter needs recalibrating.

## Exam traps

- Minus sign dropped.
- ln used instead of log₁₀.
- pH values averaged after mixing.
- Volume of one solution used instead of the total.
- [H⁺] rounded before taking the log.
- Sulfuric acid doubled without being told to.
- A negative pH "corrected" to a positive one.
- Concluding a diluted acid can have pH above 7.

## Quick self-test

1. Define pH.
2. Calculate the pH when [H⁺] = 4.7 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³.
3. Calculate [H⁺] at pH 11.62.
4. Calculate the pH of 0.0075 mol dm⁻³ HBr.
5. 1.46 g of HCl (Mr = 36.5) is made up to 250 cm³ of solution. Calculate the pH.
6. 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ HNO₃ is diluted by a factor of 100. Give the pH before and after.
7. 20.0 cm³ of 0.500 mol dm⁻³ HCl is made up to 100 cm³ with water. Calculate the pH.
8. 30.0 cm³ of 0.200 mol dm⁻³ HCl is mixed with 70.0 cm³ of 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ HNO₃. Calculate the pH.
9. Calculate the pH of 5.0 mol dm⁻³ HCl.
10. How many times greater is [H⁺] at pH 2.30 than at pH 4.30?
11. Why can a dilute solution of HCl never have a pH above 7?
12. Why must a pH meter be calibrated before use?

### Answers

1. pH = −log₁₀[H⁺], where [H⁺] is the hydrogen ion concentration in mol dm⁻³.
2. **3.33**.
3. **2.40 × 10⁻¹² mol dm⁻³**.
4. **2.12**.
5. n = 0.0400 mol; [H⁺] = 0.0400 ÷ 0.250 = 0.160 mol dm⁻³; pH = **0.80**.
6. Before **1.30**; after **3.30** (100-fold dilution adds 2).
7. [H⁺] = 0.500 × 20.0 ÷ 100 = 0.100 mol dm⁻³; pH = **1.00**.
8. n(H⁺) = 6.00 × 10⁻³ + 3.50 × 10⁻³ = 9.50 × 10⁻³ mol in 0.100 dm³; [H⁺] = 0.0950 mol dm⁻³; pH = **1.02**.
9. **−0.70**.
10. **100 times** (two pH units).
11. Water itself supplies about 10⁻⁷ mol dm⁻³ H⁺ at 25 °C, so [H⁺] can never fall below that value, and adding acid only increases it.
12. So that its readings match known pH values; the probe's response drifts, and calibration with buffers corrects it.
