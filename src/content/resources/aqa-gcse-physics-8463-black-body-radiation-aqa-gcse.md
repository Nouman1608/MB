This guide goes deeper into section **4.6.3 Black body radiation** of the AQA GCSE Physics (8463) specification,
for first teaching 2016. It is the last part of Topic 6, Waves, and is examined on Paper 2. The emission,
absorption and black body statements are for both tiers; the statements about a body's temperature and the
balance of radiation, including the Earth, are **Higher tier only** and are labelled below.

interpretation, but data questions can ask you to compare rates in watts (joules per second).

## Every object emits and absorbs

A common belief is that only hot objects give out radiation. The specification says the opposite: **all
bodies, no matter what their temperature, emit and absorb infrared radiation**. An ice cube, your hand and a
kettle are all emitting infrared right now. What changes with temperature is **how much** is emitted in a
given time, and **which wavelengths** carry most of it.

Two statements to keep separate:

- **Emission depends on the body's own temperature.** The hotter the body, the more radiation it gives out
  each second.
- **Absorption depends on the radiation arriving and on the surface.** A body absorbs some of the radiation
  that falls on it; the rest is reflected or transmitted.

## The perfect black body

A **perfect black body** is an object that **absorbs all of the radiation incident on it**. It does not
reflect any radiation and it does not transmit any. It is an idealised model: no real surface absorbs
absolutely everything, but a matt black surface comes close for many wavelengths.

The specification then links absorbing and emitting: **a good absorber is also a good emitter**. So a
perfect black body, which is the best possible absorber, would also be the **best possible emitter**. At a
given temperature, nothing can emit more radiation per second than a perfect black body of the same size.

Why "black"? The name describes absorption, not the colour it glows. A perfect black body at a high enough
temperature emits visible light and does not look black at all. A hot stove ring glowing red is still
behaving close to a black body.

| Surface | Absorbs | Reflects | Transmits | Emits (at the same temperature) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Perfect black body | All incident radiation | None | None | The most possible |
| Matt black paint | Most | Little | None | Close to the most |
| Shiny silver | Little | Most | None | Little |

The surface comparison links back to Required practical 10 (the Leslie cube), which is covered in the Waves
study guide; here the point is the theory behind it.

## Temperature, intensity and wavelength distribution

A body does not emit one single wavelength. It emits a **spread** of wavelengths, called its **wavelength
distribution**. On a graph of intensity against wavelength, the curve rises to a peak and then falls away.

When the temperature of the body rises, two things happen to the curve:

1. **Intensity increases at every wavelength.** The whole curve is higher, so the total radiation emitted
   each second is greater.
2. **The peak moves to a shorter wavelength.** A larger share of the emission is at shorter wavelengths.

This explains the colour sequence of a heated metal bar. At room temperature it emits only infrared, which
you cannot see. Heat it strongly and it glows dull red: the short-wavelength tail of the distribution has
reached visible light, starting at the red end (longest visible wavelength). Heat it further and it looks
orange, then yellow-white, as more of the emission moves to shorter visible wavelengths.

### Reading an intensity–wavelength graph

When a question shows two or more curves:

- The curve that is **higher everywhere** belongs to the **hotter** body.
- The curve whose **peak is further left** (shorter wavelength) also belongs to the hotter body.
- The **area under** a curve represents the total radiation emitted per second, so a higher curve means more
  energy emitted each second.
- The curves for different temperatures **do not cross**: a hotter black body emits more at every
  wavelength, not just at the peak.

## Higher tier only: the radiation balance

A body is absorbing and emitting radiation all the time. Its temperature depends on which rate is bigger.

| Situation | Effect on temperature |
|---|---|
| Absorbs radiation **faster** than it emits | Temperature **increases** |
| Emits radiation **faster** than it absorbs | Temperature **decreases** |
| Absorbs and emits at the **same rate** | Temperature stays **constant** |

A constant temperature does **not** mean nothing is happening. It means the two rates are equal.

The balance also corrects itself. If a body starts to warm because it absorbs faster than it emits, its rising
temperature makes it emit faster. Eventually the emission rate catches up with the absorption rate, and the
temperature settles at a new, higher constant value. The same happens in reverse when a body cools.

### Everyday examples

- **A car parked in sunshine.** At first the car body absorbs radiation from the Sun faster than it emits, so
  it warms. As it gets hotter it emits more, until the rates are equal and its temperature stops rising.
- **A drink left outside on a clear night.** It emits infrared faster than it absorbs radiation from its
  cooler surroundings, so it cools. (Other energy transfers also act, but the question will usually ask you
  to focus on radiation.)

### The temperature of the Earth

The Earth is the specification's main example. Its temperature depends on:

- the **rate at which it absorbs radiation**, mostly from the Sun
- the **rate at which it emits radiation** into space
- how much incoming radiation is **reflected** back into space, for example by clouds or ice, and so is never
  absorbed.

A useful diagram has three arrows: sunlight arriving, some reflected straight back, and infrared emitted by
the warmed surface. The Sun is very hot, so most of its radiation arrives at short wavelengths (visible light
and near infrared), which pass through the atmosphere. The surface is much cooler, so the radiation it emits
is at **longer infrared wavelengths**. Some of this outgoing infrared is absorbed by gases in the atmosphere,
which then emit radiation in all directions, including back towards the surface. This slows the rate at
which radiation escapes into space.

If less radiation escapes while the absorption rate is unchanged, the Earth absorbs faster than it emits and
its temperature rises until a new balance is reached. If more sunlight is reflected (for example more cloud or
ice cover), less is absorbed and the balance temperature is lower.

## Worked examples

**Worked example 1 (both tiers).** A student says: "A perfect black body is black, so it cannot be the best
emitter of light." Explain why the student is wrong.

> A perfect black body is defined by what it absorbs: it absorbs all radiation incident on it and reflects
> none. A good absorber is also a good emitter, so it is the best possible emitter at any temperature. It
> appears black only because it reflects nothing; when hot enough it emits visible light.

The marks come from three linked ideas: definition (absorbs all), the absorber–emitter link, and the
conclusion about emission.

**Worked example 2 (Higher tier only).** These figures are invented for practice. A panel on a satellite
absorbs radiation at 450 W and emits radiation at 380 W. Describe and explain what happens to its temperature.

```
absorbed per second = 450 J;  emitted per second = 380 J
net gain = 450 − 380 = 70 J every second
```

> The panel absorbs radiation faster than it emits it, so its temperature increases. As it gets hotter it
> emits radiation at a greater rate. When the emission rate rises to 450 W, the rates are equal and the
> temperature stays constant at a new, higher value.

## Common mistakes

- Saying cold objects do not emit radiation. All bodies emit, at any temperature.
- Defining a black body as "an object that is black" or "an object that emits all radiation". It absorbs all
  incident radiation and reflects and transmits none.
- Forgetting the link "a good absorber is also a good emitter", which is the reason a black body is the best
  emitter.
- Saying a hotter body emits only shorter wavelengths. It emits more at every wavelength; the peak shifts.
- (Higher tier only) Writing that a body at constant temperature "is not absorbing or emitting". The rates are
  equal.
- (Higher tier only) Saying the Earth's temperature rises "because heat is trapped" with no mention of rates.
  Name the rate of absorption and the rate of emission.

## Exam technique

**State** or **give** needs one fact, such as "all bodies emit infrared". **Describe** a graph by naming what
happens to the height and to the peak position. **Explain** needs a reason linked to the physics: "the
temperature rises **because** it absorbs radiation faster than it emits it". **Compare** two curves by making
each point about both bodies in the same sentence. For a Higher tier balance question, use the word **rate**
in every sentence, and finish with what happens once the rates become equal again.
