Sub-topic 3.4 of the Cambridge O Level Environmental Management (5014) syllabus for 2027 to 2029 is called Marine aquaculture, but it covers two linked ideas: the **exploitation** of wild marine species by fishing and harvesting, and **farming** marine species as an alternative. Its five statements ask you to explain the impacts of exploitation (3.4.1), define marine aquaculture (3.4.2), explain its impacts (3.4.3), explain strategies for managing harvesting (3.4.4) and discuss those strategies (3.4.5). Notice that the management strategies in 3.4.4 are about controlling **wild harvesting**, not about running a fish farm.

## Exploitation of marine species

### Overfishing and overharvesting

**Overfishing** happens when fish are caught faster than the population can replace them by breeding. **Overharvesting** is the same idea applied to any marine species, such as shellfish or seaweed. Causes include rising demand for food, larger and more powerful boats, bigger nets, sonar that finds shoals, and the difficulty of policing the open sea. The result is a falling stock: catches first rise, then fall even though fishing effort increases, and the population may collapse.

A useful explanation chain: large adults are caught → fewer fish reach breeding age → fewer young are produced → the stock shrinks each year → fishers catch smaller and younger fish → the decline speeds up.

### Target and bycatch species

The **target species** is the one fishers intend to catch. Its population falls, its average size drops, and fewer breeding adults remain. **Bycatch** is the unintended catch of other species, such as young fish, turtles, dolphins, sharks and seabirds, which are often thrown back dead or dying. Bycatch matters because it harms species that are not even being sold, some of which breed slowly and are already rare.

### Effects on food chains

Removing a species affects those above and below it.

- If a predatory fish is overfished, its prey may increase and overgraze their own food, changing the whole community.
- If small fish that many animals eat are overfished, seabirds, seals and larger fish lose their food and decline.
- Bottom trawling can also destroy the seabed habitat on which other organisms depend.

## What marine aquaculture is

The syllabus defines **marine aquaculture** as the farming of marine species in captivity, limited to **fish**, **crustaceans** (such as prawns and shrimps) and **seaweeds**. You do not need to know specific species. Farms may use floating net cages in sheltered bays, ponds on the coast, or ropes and frames for seaweed and shellfish.

## The eight impacts of marine aquaculture

| Impact | Positive or negative | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Reduced exploitation of natural fisheries | Positive | Farmed supply can meet demand that would otherwise come from wild stocks |
| Increased food supply for humans | Positive | Reliable protein all year, plus jobs and export income |
| Risk of escape | Negative | Escaped farmed animals compete with wild ones and may breed with them, weakening wild populations |
| Risk of disease | Negative | Crowded animals spread disease and parasites quickly, and these can pass to wild animals nearby |
| Local food webs | Negative | Predators are attracted to cages; wild species may be displaced; habitats such as mangroves may be cleared for ponds |
| Nutrient enrichment from waste | Negative | Uneaten feed and faeces add nutrients, which can lead to algal growth and oxygen loss below cages |
| Energy usage | Negative | Energy is used to pump and aerate water, make feed, run boats and refrigerate and transport the product |
| Source of food for farmed species | Negative for carnivorous species | Many farmed fish are fed on meal made from wild-caught fish, so pressure on wild stocks moves rather than disappears |

Seaweed farming is a useful contrast in a discussion: seaweeds need no added feed and take up nutrients from the water, so several of the negative impacts are much smaller.

## Managing the harvesting of marine species

The nine strategies fall into three groups.

**Controlling the gear**
- **Limits on the size of boats and overall net size** reduce how much one boat can catch on each trip.
- **Increased mesh size of nets** lets young, small fish swim through, so they survive to breed.
- **Sustainable methods: pole and line** catch one fish at a time, so bycatch is very low and the catch is selective.

**Controlling how much and when**
- **Quotas** set the maximum amount of a species that may be caught in a period, often shared out between countries or boats.
- **Closed seasons** ban fishing at certain times, usually the breeding season.
- **Limited number of fishing days** restricts the time each boat may spend at sea.

**Controlling where and by law**
- **Protected areas** close parts of the sea to fishing, so populations can recover and spread into nearby waters.
- **Conservation laws** protect named species or set rules such as minimum sizes that may be landed.
- **International agreements** are needed because fish move across national boundaries. They only work with **implementation and monitoring**, for example inspections at ports, observers on boats and tracking of vessels.

## Discussing the strategies

Strengths and weaknesses recur across the list, so learn them as patterns.

- Gear controls are simple to check at port, but fishers may break rules at sea, and larger mesh still catches some bycatch.
- Pole and line is highly selective but catches far less per trip, so fish cost more and fewer people are employed per tonne.
- Quotas limit the total catch, but fish caught over the quota may be thrown back dead, and quotas set too high do not protect the stock.
- Closed seasons and limited days protect breeding, but reduce fishers' income at those times.
- Protected areas let whole ecosystems recover, but need patrolling and push fishing effort into other areas.
- International agreements tackle shared stocks, but some countries may not sign, and monitoring a whole ocean is expensive.

A good judgement usually says that several strategies combined, with effective monitoring, work better than any single rule.

## Worked example: where does the feed come from?

*Figures invented for practice.* A fish farm produces 1,000 tonnes of fish a year. It uses 1,500 tonnes of feed, and 30% of the feed is made from wild-caught fish.

1. Wild fish in the feed = 1,500 × 0.30 = 450 tonnes.
2. Farmed fish produced per tonne of wild fish = 1,000 ÷ 450 = 2.2 tonnes (to one decimal place).

Interpretation: in this example the farm produces more fish than it removes from the sea, so it does reduce pressure on wild stocks, but not by the full 1,000 tonnes. If the feed were 80% wild fish, the wild fish used would be 1,200 tonnes, more than the farm produces. This is why "source of food for farmed species" is listed as an impact.

## Worked example: a shared quota

*Figures invented for practice.* Scientists estimate a stock at 400,000 tonnes and advise that no more than 15% is caught each year. The quota is 400,000 × 0.15 = 60,000 tonnes. If it is shared between three countries in the ratio 3 : 2 : 1, they receive 30,000, 20,000 and 10,000 tonnes. The calculation is simple; the hard part, which a "discuss" answer should mention, is making sure each country's boats stay within their share.

## Common mistakes

- Defining aquaculture as "fishing in the sea". It is farming in captivity.
- Assuming aquaculture always reduces pressure on wild fish; feed may come from wild stocks.
- Confusing bycatch (unwanted species) with the target species.
- Writing "quotas" with no explanation of what they limit.
- Giving fish-farm siting rules when the question asks about managing harvesting.

## Exam technique

**Define** questions need the syllabus wording: farming of marine species in captivity. **Describe and explain** questions need the effect and the reason; for a 4-mark question on two strategies, give each strategy, how it works and why that protects the stock. **Discuss** questions need benefits for the stock, limitations for fishers or enforcement, and a judgement.
