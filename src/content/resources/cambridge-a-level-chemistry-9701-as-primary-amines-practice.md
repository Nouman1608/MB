## Section A

**1.** Name the compound CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂NH₂. **[1]**

**2.** Give the reagent and conditions needed to convert bromoethane into ethylamine. **[2]**

**3.** Name the type of mechanism in the reaction between a halogenoalkane and ammonia. **[1]**

**4.** Explain why ammonia can act as a nucleophile. **[1]**

**5.** Write the overall equation for the formation of methylamine from chloromethane. **[1]**

## Section B

**6.** 1-bromopropane reacts with ammonia to form propylamine.

**(a)** Describe, in terms of curly arrows, the first step of the mechanism. **[2]**

**(b)** Give the formula of the ion formed in the first step. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain the role of a second molecule of ammonia, and write an equation for this step. **[2]**

**7.** The reaction in question 6 is carried out by heating the reactants in ethanol in a sealed tube.

**(a)** Explain why a sealed tube is used. **[1]**

**(b)** Suggest why ethanol, rather than water, is used as the solvent. **[1]**

**(c)** Name the organic product that would form instead if 1-bromopropane were heated with NaOH(aq).
**[1]**

**8.** 1-chlorobutane, 1-bromobutane and 1-iodobutane are each heated with ammonia in ethanol under the same
conditions. Put them in order of increasing rate of reaction and explain the order. **[3]**

**9.** 9.25 g of 1-chlorobutane is heated with excess ammonia in ethanol under pressure. 5.11 g of
butylamine, C₄H₁₁N, is obtained.

**(a)** Calculate the amount, in moles, of 1-chlorobutane used. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage yield of butylamine. **[2]**

**10.** Outline a two-step synthesis of ethylamine from ethanol. Give the reagent for each step and name the
intermediate. **[3]**

**11.** A student plans to make propylamine, CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₂, from propene by adding HBr(g) and then heating
the product with ammonia in ethanol under pressure. Explain why the main amine formed is not propylamine,
and give the formula of the main amine. **[2]**

**12.** 1-bromopropane is heated separately with (i) KCN in ethanol and (ii) NH₃ in ethanol under pressure.

**(a)** Give the structural formula of the organic product of each reaction. **[2]**

**(b)** State how the number of carbon atoms in each product compares with that in 1-bromopropane. **[1]**

**13.** Two primary amines have the molecular formula C₃H₉N.

**(a)** Give the structural formulae of both amines. **[2]**

**(b)** Name the halogenoalkane, containing bromine, that would be used to make each amine. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Butylamine [1].

**2.** Ammonia (in excess) [1]; in ethanol, heated under pressure (in a sealed tube) [1].

**3.** Nucleophilic substitution [1].

**4.** The nitrogen atom has a lone pair of electrons that it can donate to an electron-deficient carbon
[1].

**5.** CH₃Cl + 2NH₃ → CH₃NH₂ + NH₄Cl [1].

**6. (a)** A curly arrow from the lone pair on the nitrogen of NH₃ to the δ+ carbon bonded to Br [1]; a
curly arrow from the C–Br bond to the Br, which leaves as Br⁻ [1].

**(b)** CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₃⁺ [1].

**(c)** It acts as a base and removes H⁺ from the –NH₃⁺ group to release the free amine [1]:
CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₃⁺ + NH₃ → CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₂ + NH₄⁺ [1].

**7. (a)** Ammonia is a gas, so in an open vessel it would escape on heating; the sealed tube keeps it in the
reaction mixture under pressure [1].

**(b)** Ethanol dissolves both the halogenoalkane and ammonia, and avoiding water prevents the
halogenoalkane being hydrolysed to an alcohol [1].

**(c)** Propan-1-ol [1].

**8.** 1-chlorobutane < 1-bromobutane < 1-iodobutane [1]. The bond energies are C–Cl 340, C–Br 280 and
C–I 240 kJ mol⁻¹ [1]; the weaker the C–X bond, the more easily it breaks, so the faster the substitution
[1].

**9. (a)** Mr(C₄H₉Cl) = 4(12.0) + 9(1.0) + 35.5 = 92.5; n = 9.25 / 92.5 = 0.100 mol [1].

**(b)** Mr(C₄H₁₁N) = 4(12.0) + 11(1.0) + 14.0 = 73.0; n = 5.11 / 73.0 = 0.0700 mol [1]; yield =
0.0700 / 0.100 × 100 = 70.0% [1].

**10.** Step 1: react ethanol with PCl₅ or SOCl₂ (or HBr(g)) [1] to give a halogenoethane, for example
chloroethane [1]. Step 2: heat the halogenoethane with NH₃ in ethanol under pressure to give ethylamine
[1].

**11.** HBr adds to propene mainly via the more stable secondary carbocation, so the main halogenoalkane is
2-bromopropane, not 1-bromopropane [1]. Substitution by ammonia then puts NH₂ on C2: CH₃CH(NH₂)CH₃ [1].

**12. (a)** (i) CH₃CH₂CH₂CN (butanenitrile) [1]; (ii) CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₂ (propylamine) [1].

**(b)** The nitrile has one more carbon atom (four) than 1-bromopropane, while the amine has the same
number (three) [1].

**13. (a)** CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₂ [1]; CH₃CH(NH₂)CH₃ [1].

**(b)** 1-bromopropane for CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₂ [1]; 2-bromopropane for CH₃CH(NH₂)CH₃ [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing one NH₃ and HX instead of 2NH₃ and NH₄X in the overall equation.
- Leaving out "in ethanol" or "under pressure" from the conditions.
- Drawing the first curly arrow from the N atom or an H atom rather than from the lone pair.
- Forgetting the positive charge on the alkylammonium intermediate.
- Explaining rate differences with electronegativity instead of C–X bond strength.
- Ignoring Markovnikov addition when a route starts from an unsymmetrical alkene.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is ammonia used in excess?**
The equation needs two moles of ammonia per mole of halogenoalkane, and an excess also makes it more likely
that each halogenoalkane molecule reacts with ammonia. The wider consequences of using less ammonia are
studied at A Level.

**Does the reaction change the length of the carbon chain?**
No. The –NH₂ group simply replaces the halogen, so the amine has the same carbon skeleton as the
halogenoalkane. Substitution by cyanide, by contrast, adds a carbon atom.
