## Section A

**1.** Which change shows that a reaction is endothermic?

A: The temperature of the surroundings rises.

B: The temperature of the surroundings falls.

C: A gas is given off.

D: A precipitate forms.

**[1]**

**2.** State what is meant by an exothermic reaction. **[2]**

**3.** Ethanol burns in air. State whether this is exothermic or endothermic and give the evidence you would observe. **[2]**

**4.** *(Supplement)* State the sign of ΔH for an exothermic reaction and for an endothermic reaction. **[1]**

**5.** *(Supplement)* Define activation energy. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** A student mixes solutions and records the temperatures.

Experiment X: 22 °C before mixing, 31 °C after.

Experiment Y: 22 °C before mixing, 17 °C after.

**(a)** Classify each reaction as exothermic or endothermic. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain your answer for Experiment Y in terms of energy transfer. **[2]**

**7.** A reaction pathway diagram shows the products at a higher energy than the reactants.

**(a)** State whether the reaction is exothermic or endothermic. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain how the diagram shows this. **[1]**

**8.** *(Supplement)* Sulfur dioxide reacts with oxygen to form sulfur trioxide; ΔH is negative and the reaction has an activation energy. Describe how you would draw and label the reaction pathway diagram for this reaction. **[4]**

**9.** *(Supplement)* Explain, in terms of bond breaking and bond making, why the decomposition of calcium carbonate is endothermic. **[3]**

**10.** *(Supplement)* Hydrogen reacts with bromine: H₂ + Br₂ → 2HBr. Bond energies (kJ/mol): H–H 436; Br–Br 193; H–Br 366.

**(a)** Calculate the energy needed to break the bonds in the reactants. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the energy released making the bonds in the products. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate ΔH and state whether the reaction is exothermic or endothermic. **[2]**

**11.** *(Supplement)* Ethene reacts with hydrogen: C₂H₄ + H₂ → C₂H₆. Bond energies (kJ/mol): C=C 612; C–C 348; C–H 413; H–H 436. Calculate ΔH for this reaction. **[3]**

**12.** *(Supplement)* Hydrogen burns: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. Bond energies (kJ/mol): H–H 436; O=O 498; O–H 464.

**(a)** Calculate ΔH. **[3]**

**(b)** A student gets +486 kJ/mol. Identify the error. **[1]**

**13.** *(Supplement)* A student states: "Reactions with a large activation energy are always endothermic." Explain why this is wrong. **[2]**

**14.** *(Supplement)* On a reaction pathway diagram for a reaction, the reactants are at an energy of 50 kJ/mol, the top of the hump is at 180 kJ/mol and the products are at 120 kJ/mol.

**(a)** Calculate the activation energy. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate ΔH, giving its sign. **[1]**

**(c)** State whether the reaction is exothermic or endothermic. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1].

**2.** A reaction that **transfers thermal energy to the surroundings** [1], so the **temperature of the surroundings increases** [1].

**3.** **Exothermic** [1]; the flame gives out heat / the surroundings get **hotter** [1].

**4.** Exothermic **negative**, endothermic **positive** (both needed) [1].

**5.** The **minimum energy** [1] that **colliding particles must have to react** [1].

**6.**

**(a)** X: **exothermic** [1]. Y: **endothermic** [1].

**(b)** The temperature of the surroundings **fell** [1], so thermal energy was **taken in from the surroundings** by the reaction [1].

**7.**

**(a)** **Endothermic** [1].

**(b)** The products have **more energy** than the reactants, so energy has been **taken in** [1].

**8.** Axes labelled **energy** and **progress of reaction**, with reactants (2SO₂ + O₂) on the left [1]; products (2SO₃) drawn **lower** than the reactants [1]; a **single hump** with **Ea** arrow from the **reactants to the peak** [1]; **ΔH** arrow from **reactants down to products**, labelled negative [1].

**9.** Breaking bonds **needs energy (endothermic)**; making bonds **releases energy (exothermic)** [1]. In this decomposition, **more energy is needed to break the bonds** in the reactant [1] than is **released when the bonds in the products form** [1], so there is a net intake of energy.

**10.**

**(a)** 436 + 193 = **629 kJ/mol** [1].

**(b)** 2 × 366 = **732 kJ/mol** [1].

**(c)** ΔH = 629 − 732 = **−103 kJ/mol** [1]; **exothermic** [1].

**11.** Broken: C=C + 4 C–H + H–H = 612 + 1652 + 436 = 2700 kJ/mol [1]. Made: C–C + 6 C–H = 348 + 2478 = 2826 kJ/mol [1]. ΔH = 2700 − 2826 = **−126 kJ/mol** [1]. (Shortcut: four C–H bonds appear on both sides and cancel, so 612 + 436 − 348 − 2 × 413 gives the same answer.)

**12.**

**(a)** Broken: 2 × 436 + 498 = 1370 kJ/mol [1]. Made: 4 × 464 = 1856 kJ/mol [1]. ΔH = 1370 − 1856 = **−486 kJ/mol** [1].

**(b)** The student calculated **bonds made − bonds broken**, reversing the subtraction [1].

**13.** Activation energy is the energy **needed to start** the reaction, and every reaction has one [1]. Whether a reaction is exothermic or endothermic depends on **ΔH** — the energy of the products compared with the reactants — not on Ea [1].

**14.**

**(a)** Ea = 180 − 50 = **130 kJ/mol** (measured from the reactants to the peak) [1].

**(b)** ΔH = 120 − 50 = **+70 kJ/mol** [1].

**(c)** **Endothermic** — the products are higher in energy than the reactants [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Linking a temperature rise to "taking in heat" instead of giving it out.
- Drawing the Ea arrow from the products, or the ΔH arrow to the top of the hump.
- Leaving off arrowheads or labels on a pathway diagram.
- Missing the coefficients or the double bonds when counting bonds.
- Subtracting the wrong way round, so the sign of ΔH is reversed.
- Giving ΔH with no sign or no units.
- Defining activation energy without "minimum" or without "colliding particles".
