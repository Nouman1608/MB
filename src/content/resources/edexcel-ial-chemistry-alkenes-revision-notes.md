## Key definitions (5.1)

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Alkene | Hydrocarbon with at least one C=C; one C=C, open chain: CₙH₂ₙ |
| Cycloalkene | Ring hydrocarbon with a C=C; one ring and one C=C: CₙH₂ₙ₋₂ |
| Unsaturated | Contains a C=C, so atoms can be added across it |
| σ bond | Head-on overlap of orbitals on the axis between the two nuclei |
| π bond | Sideways overlap of p orbitals; electron density above and below the plane |
| Electrophile | Species that accepts an electron pair |
| Carbocation | Ion with a positively charged carbon atom |

Key idea in one line: the π bond is weaker and its electrons are exposed, so alkenes undergo **addition**, and electrophiles attack them.

Shape: each C of the C=C is trigonal planar, bond angle about 120°.

## Geometric isomerism (5.2) and E–Z naming (5.3)

**Two conditions, both needed**

1. A C=C, which has restricted rotation because rotating would break the π overlap.
2. Two different atoms or groups on **each** carbon of the C=C.

**E–Z method in steps**

1. For each C=C carbon, compare the atomic numbers of the two atoms bonded directly to it; higher wins.
2. Tie? Step out one atom and compare again until something differs.
3. Winners together = **Z**; winners across = **E**.

**Why E–Z is needed:** cis–trans relies on a matching group on each carbon. When three or four of the groups are different, cis–trans is ambiguous. E–Z always gives one answer.

Quick reminder: in CH₃CH=C(Br)CH₂CH₃, carbon 2 ranks CH₃ over H, and carbon 3 ranks Br (35) over the ethyl carbon (6). So the isomer with CH₃ and Br on the same side is Z.

## Reactions table (5.4)

| Reagent | Conditions | Type | Product type |
|---|---|---|---|
| H₂ | Ni catalyst, about 150 °C | addition (hydrogenation) | alkane |
| Br₂ / Cl₂ | room temperature | electrophilic addition | di-substituted halogenoalkane |
| HBr / HCl / HI | room temperature | electrophilic addition | mono-substituted halogenoalkane |
| H₂O (steam) | acid catalyst (phosphoric acid), high T and P | addition (hydration) | alcohol |
| KMnO₄ / H⁺ | room temperature | oxidation | diol (purple → colourless) |

Small worked reminders:

- But-2-ene + H₂ → butane.
- Propene + Br₂ → 1,2-dibromopropane, CH₃CHBrCH₂Br.
- Propene + steam → mainly propan-2-ol.
- But-2-ene + [O] + H₂O → butane-2,3-diol, CH₃CH(OH)CH(OH)CH₃.

## Test for C=C (5.5)

Add bromine water and shake. **Orange-brown → colourless** with an alkene. No change with an alkane in the dark.

## Mechanisms (5.6)

**Checklist for any electrophilic addition diagram**

1. Draw the C=C and the electrophile with its dipole (δ+ on the atom that bonds first).
2. Arrow 1 starts on the C=C and ends on the δ+ atom.
3. Arrow 2 starts on the bond in the electrophile and ends on the δ− atom.
4. Draw the carbocation with a full + on the correct carbon, and the anion with a lone pair and − charge.
5. Arrow 3 starts on a lone pair of the anion and ends on C⁺.
6. Draw and name the product.

**Specifics**

- Br₂ + ethene: Br₂ is polarised by the π electrons (induced dipole). Intermediate: CH₂BrCH₂⁺.
- HBr + ethene: permanent dipole Hδ+–Brδ−. Intermediate: CH₃CH₂⁺.
- HBr + propene: secondary CH₃CH⁺CH₃ (major route) → 2-bromopropane; primary CH₃CH₂CH₂⁺ (minor) → 1-bromopropane.

**Carbocation stability:** tertiary > secondary > primary. More alkyl groups push more electron density onto C⁺ (positive inductive effect), stabilising it.

**Evidence for a two-step mechanism:** ethene with bromine in sodium chloride solution gives BrCH₂CH₂Cl as well as BrCH₂CH₂Br. A carbocation must form first and then be attacked by any available nucleophile. No ClCH₂CH₂Cl appears, so Cl⁻ does not start the attack.

## Addition polymerisation (5.7)

| Monomer | Repeat unit | Polymer |
|---|---|---|
| CH₂=CH₂ | –[CH₂–CH₂]ₙ– | poly(ethene) |
| CH₂=CHCH₃ | –[CH₂–CH(CH₃)]ₙ– | poly(propene) |
| CH₂=CHCl | –[CH₂–CHCl]ₙ– | poly(chloroethene), PVC |
| CH₂=C(CH₃)COOCH₃ | –[CH₂–C(CH₃)(COOCH₃)]ₙ– | Perspex |

**Rules:** two carbons in the main chain of the repeat unit; single bond between them; side groups kept; bonds through both brackets; n outside. Reverse the process to find the monomer. Atom economy is 100% because only one product forms.

## Further suggested practicals (Topic 5)

These are suggested in the specification, not Core Practicals: comparing alkanes and alkenes (combustion, bromine water, acidified potassium manganate(VII)); making cyclohexene from cyclohexanol; extracting limonene from orange peel by steam distillation; making Perspex from methyl 2-methylpropenoate. Know the observations: alkenes decolourise both bromine water and the purple manganate(VII) solution, and burn with a smokier flame than alkanes of similar size.

## Polymer disposal (5.8)

- Poly(alkenes) are saturated and their C–C and C–H bonds are strong, so microorganisms cannot break them down.
- **Biodegradable polymers** are designed to be broken down by microorganisms, for example polymers based on starch or lactic acid. Many need composting conditions to degrade quickly.
- **Incineration** recovers energy but can release toxic gases. PVC gives HCl. Scrubbers containing a base such as calcium hydroxide remove it: Ca(OH)₂ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + 2H₂O.

## Must-know distinctions

- **σ vs π:** head-on overlap on the axis vs sideways p overlap above and below; π is weaker.
- **Di- vs mono-substituted:** halogen (X₂) gives two halogen atoms; hydrogen halide (HX) gives one.
- **Hydrogenation vs hydration:** H₂ with Ni gives an alkane; H₂O with acid catalyst gives an alcohol.
- **Major vs minor product:** decided by the more stable carbocation, not by which carbon "looks" more crowded.
- **Repeat unit vs monomer:** repeat unit has no C=C; monomer does.
- **Cis–trans vs E–Z:** cis–trans needs a matching group; E–Z uses priority by atomic number.

## Quick self-test

1. Give the molecular formula of the open-chain alkene with seven carbon atoms.
2. Give the molecular formula of cyclopentene.
3. State the two conditions needed for geometric isomerism.
4. Name the E or Z isomer of ClCH=CHBr in which Cl and Br are on opposite sides.
5. State the reagent and conditions to convert propene into propan-2-ol.
6. What volume of hydrogen, in cm³, reacts with 2.52 g of hex-1-ene, C₆H₁₂ (Mr 84.0)? Take the molar volume as 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹.
7. What mass of bromine, Br₂ (Mr 159.8), reacts with 0.0150 mol of propene?
8. Which carbocation forms faster from 2-methylpropene and HBr, and why?
9. Draw the repeat unit of the polymer from CH₂=CHCN.
10. A poly(propene) chain has Mr 1.26 × 10⁵. How many monomer units does it contain? (Mr of propene 42.0)
11. Name the gas formed when PVC is burned and one substance used to remove it.

### Answers

1. C₇H₁₄.
2. C₅H₈.
3. Restricted rotation from a C=C, plus two unlike groups on both of its carbons.
4. Carbon 1 ranks Cl over H; carbon 2 ranks Br over H. Opposite sides: **(E)-1-bromo-2-chloroethene**.
5. Steam with a phosphoric acid catalyst at high temperature and pressure.
6. n = 2.52 ÷ 84.0 = 0.0300 mol; one H₂ per C=C, so V = 0.0300 × 24 000 = **720 cm³**.
7. 1 : 1 ratio, so 0.0150 × 159.8 = **2.40 g**.
8. The tertiary carbocation, (CH₃)₃C⁺, because three alkyl groups release electron density towards C⁺ and stabilise it more than the primary alternative.
9. –[CH₂–CH(CN)]ₙ–.
10. 1.26 × 10⁵ ÷ 42.0 = **3000**.
11. Hydrogen chloride; calcium hydroxide (slaked lime) or another base in a scrubber.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing CₙH₂ₙ for a cycloalkene instead of CₙH₂ₙ₋₂.
- Describing a π bond as "a second σ bond" or omitting that it is above and below the plane.
- Naming only one condition for geometric isomerism.
- Ranking E–Z groups by size rather than atomic number of the attached atom.
- Drawing curly arrows that begin on an atom instead of on the C=C, a bond or a lone pair.
- Missing the dipole on Br₂, or the lone pair and charge on Br⁻.
- Drawing the primary carbocation for propene and HBr, or explaining stability without mentioning electron release by alkyl groups.
- Forgetting the acid catalyst for hydration or the nickel catalyst for hydrogenation.
- Writing "goes clear" for bromine water instead of "orange-brown to colourless".
- Leaving a C=C inside a polymer repeat unit.
