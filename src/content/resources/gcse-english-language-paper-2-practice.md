## Section A — Reading (practise on two thematically linked, unseen non-fiction extracts of your choice — ideally one from the 19th century and one from the 20th or 21st)

**1.** Read Source A only (your 19th-century extract). List **four**
things the text states explicitly about the writer's situation, the
place described, or the people involved. **[4]**

**2.** From Source A, select **one** short phrase you find effective
and explain the writer's use of language, including the effect
created. **[8]**

**3.** Using **both** sources, compare how each writer conveys their
attitude or feelings toward the subject they are writing about. Refer
to specific methods each writer uses. **[12]**

**4.** Compare how the two writers present their different
perspectives on [the shared theme of your chosen extracts], referring
to specific methods each writer uses to convey their viewpoint. **[16]**

## Section B — Writing

**5.** "Growing up in a small town limits young people's opportunities
rather than protecting them." Write a **letter to a local newspaper**
giving your own point of view on this statement. **[40]**

---

## Answers (worked approach, since Section A depends on your chosen extracts)

**1.** As with Paper 1's Question 1, look strictly for information the
text **states directly**, not what you infer — four separate, clearly
stated details from Source A alone score full marks. Do not use Source
B here; Question 1 tests careful reading of a single text.

**2.** Follow the same three-step method as any language-analysis
question: quote a short, precise phrase; name the technique (word
class, imagery, connotation); explain the specific effect created,
tied to the writer's evident attitude in this extract specifically —
not a generic comment that could apply to any text.

**3.** A strong answer identifies at least two or three genuinely
comparable points — not simply "Writer A feels X, Writer B feels Y" —
and explains *how* each writer's specific choices (word choice,
imagery, structural emphasis) build that attitude. Cross-reference
between the two texts throughout the answer, rather than discussing
each source in a separate, self-contained paragraph.

**4.** The highest-value skill this question rewards is genuine,
sustained comparison of **method**, not just content: for each point,
state what each writer does, quote briefly from each, and explain how
their choices differ in achieving a broadly similar (or contrasting)
purpose. Structuring the answer point-by-point across both texts
("both writers open with…, but where Writer A uses…, Writer B instead
uses…") consistently outperforms a two-halves structure that discusses
Source A fully, then Source B fully, with comparison only added at
the very end.

**5.** Mark against content and organisation (a clear, sustained
viewpoint; a structure suited to a letter — opening address, developed
argument, closing call to action; register appropriate to a newspaper
readership) and technical accuracy (accurate spelling, punctuation and
a range of sentence structures). A strong response commits to a single
position early, rather than hedging between both sides, since Section
B rewards a clear, persuasively sustained viewpoint over balanced
even-handedness.


## Building comparison points quickly

The comparison questions (3 and 4) are where most of Paper 2's marks
sit, so it is worth practising a fast method for generating comparison
points before writing a full answer. A reliable approach: read both
sources once each for overall sense, then draw a simple two-column
table headed with each source's title, and fill in short notes under
shared prompts — attitude to the topic, choice of tone (serious,
ironic, urgent, nostalgic), one striking method each writer uses, and
who each writer seems to be addressing. Five minutes spent building
this table before writing consistently produces a more genuinely
comparative answer than starting to write immediately and hoping
connections emerge along the way.

## Timing across the whole paper

Paper 2 lasts 1 hour 45 minutes for 80 marks split evenly between
Section A and Section B — a rough guide is just over a minute per mark,
so roughly 50 minutes on Section A (allowing extra time for planning
Question 4's comparison) and 50 minutes on Section B, with 5 minutes
spared at the start for reading both sources carefully before answering
anything. Because Question 4 is worth four times as much as Question 1,
time should scale with marks: spending equal time on all four reading
questions is a common, costly mistake, since a beautifully thorough
Question 1 answer cannot earn more than 4 marks no matter how much time
is spent on it.

## A second Section B brief, for extra practice

Try planning (without necessarily writing in full) a second viewpoint
task: your local council is proposing to close the town's only youth
centre to save money. Write a **speech for your school assembly**
giving your point of view. Before drafting, decide: what tone suits a
spoken address to peers (direct, energetic, rhetorical questions) as
opposed to a written letter to adults? What is the single strongest
point to open with, given a speech's opening moments matter more than
a letter's? Practising this planning step across different specified
forms (letter, speech, article, review) — not just the one used in
Question 5 above — builds genuine flexibility for whichever form
actually appears in the exam.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Answering Question 1 using both sources instead of Source A alone.
- Summarising each source's content separately in the comparison
  questions instead of comparing specific writing methods directly.
- Ignoring the historical gap between a 19th-century source and a
  modern one, reading period-appropriate vocabulary or sentence
  structure as simply "old fashioned" rather than a deliberate,
  analysable feature.
- Writing a narrative or descriptive piece in Section B when the task
  calls for a clear, sustained viewpoint in a specified form.
- Hedging between both sides of an issue in Section B instead of
  committing to and developing a single, persuasive position.
