Readers, writers and texts is the foundational component that the [full syllabus
guide](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-language-and-literature-syllabus-guide/) says establishes the
analytical vocabulary the course's other two components then apply. These notes go deeper into that
vocabulary, alongside the
[subject overview](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-language-and-literature-subject-guide/) and
[assessment revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-language-and-literature-assessment-revision-notes/)
already on the site.

## The core question this component asks

How do a writer's choices of language, form and structure shape meaning, and how does a reader
actively construct interpretation rather than passively receive it? Revise this as a two-part
question, not one: first, identify a specific choice the writer or producer of a text has made
(word choice, sentence structure, structure of the whole piece, visual layout); second, explain the
effect that choice creates and why a reader responds to it in a particular way.

## A shared analytical toolkit, applied to two kinds of text

Because this course studies both literary and non-literary material, revise this component's
techniques in pairs -- one literary application, one non-literary application -- rather than only
in the literary or only in the non-literary direction:

- **Narrative/structural choices** -- in a literary text, this might be chronological order versus
  flashback; in a non-literary text, such as a news report, it might be which fact is placed in the
  headline versus buried in the final paragraph.
- **Tone and register** -- in a poem, tone might be built through word connotation and rhythm; in an
  advertisement, register is often built through direct address ("you") and informal phrasing to
  create intimacy with the audience.
- **Visual and spatial elements** -- relevant to both a graphic novel's panel layout and a website or
  advert's use of image, white space and typography to guide the eye and imply meaning beyond the
  words alone.
- **Audience and purpose** -- every text, literary or non-literary, is shaped by who it is for and
  what it is trying to do (persuade, inform, move, entertain); identifying purpose early in an
  analysis focuses everything that follows.

## Building an analysis, step by step

1. Identify the text type and its likely purpose and audience before analysing detail -- this framing
   shapes which techniques are actually relevant to look for.
2. Identify two or three specific, quotable choices of language, form or structure.
3. For each choice, explain the effect it creates, connecting back to purpose and audience rather than
   listing techniques with no explanation of effect.
4. Where relevant, comment on how confidently or ambiguously a reader can settle on one meaning --
   texts are not always designed to produce a single, fixed interpretation.

## Common exam pitfalls

- **Technique-spotting without effect** -- naming a device (metaphor, alliteration, a pull-quote,
  a bold headline) without explaining what it actually does to meaning or reader response scores far
  lower than a shorter analysis that properly connects device to effect.
- **Treating non-literary texts as "easier" or less worthy of close reading** -- because Paper 1's
  guided textual analysis is set on unseen non-literary passages specifically, under-preparing this
  side of the course leaves a real gap on the paper that is, structurally, the clearest signal of how
  central non-literary analysis is to this course.
- **Ignoring visual/spatial elements** -- for multimodal texts (adverts, webpages, graphic
  narratives), analysis that discusses only the words and ignores layout, image and typography misses
  half of how such texts actually communicate.

## A worked mini-example

Take a short opening line from a hypothetical opinion column: "It is, of course, entirely
reasonable to assume that no one in government has ever read a bus timetable." A technique-spotting
answer might simply note "the writer uses irony." A stronger answer identifies the specific choice
(the ironic hedging phrase "of course, entirely reasonable" paired with a deliberately mundane,
almost absurd example) and explains its effect: it invites the reader to share the writer's
implied scepticism without stating a direct accusation, building a conspiratorial tone between
writer and reader before the argument proper has even begun. Notice that this explanation connects
the specific words used to a concrete effect on the reader, rather than stopping at the label
"irony."

## Self-test

1. What two things must an analysis do with a specific textual choice, beyond simply naming it?
2. Give one literary and one non-literary example of how "structural choices" might shape meaning
   differently.
3. Why is it a mistake to treat non-literary texts as less deserving of close, careful analysis in
   this course?
4. What four questions should you ask about a text before analysing its specific techniques?

**Answers:** 1. Identify the specific choice, and explain the effect that choice creates for the
reader, connected to purpose and audience. 2. Literary: choosing flashback over chronological order to
control when a reader learns key information. Non-literary: a news report placing a key fact in the
headline versus burying it in a later paragraph, shaping what a reader takes away even if they only
skim. 3. Because Paper 1's guided textual analysis is set on unseen non-literary passages specifically,
making non-literary close-reading skill directly, externally examined -- not a secondary skill to
literary analysis. 4. What type of text is it, who is its likely audience, what is its purpose, and
(for multimodal texts) what visual/spatial elements does it use alongside words.
