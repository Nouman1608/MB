**These notes condense one element of Option 2A:** "guilt and remorse, confession and the desire for
forgiveness". The [guilt and confession study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-english-literature-7717-guilt-remorse-and-confession/)
has full analysis and a model paragraph; the
[Elements of Crime Writing revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-english-literature-7712-7717-option-2a-elements-of-crime-writing-7717-revision-notes/)
cover the option as a whole.

## Keep the four ideas apart

- **Guilt**: responsibility for the act, in law, morality or the character's own mind.
- **Remorse**: sorrow for the act itself, not merely fear of punishment.
- **Confession**: telling the crime; always ask *to whom*.
- **Desire for forgiveness**: seeking pardon from God, the victim, society or oneself.

The mismatches are the arguments: guilt without remorse, confession without repentance, guilt
without a legal crime.

## Text grid

| Text | Guilt and confession | Forgiveness |
|---|---|---|
| *Hamlet* | Claudius confesses in prayer but will not give up crown, ambition or queen | Impossible without repentance; his words rise, his thoughts do not |
| "My Last Duchess" | The Duke's involuntary confession; "all smiles stopped together" | Not sought; no remorse |
| "Porphyria's Lover" | Confession to the reader; God "has not said a word" | Ambiguous: defiance or waiting for judgement |
| "The Laboratory" | Gleeful preparation of poison | Not sought; the reader's repugnance judges |
| "Peter Grimes" | Haunted by visions of his father and the dead boys | A dying account wrung out by guilt |
| *The Rime of the Ancient Mariner* | Penance at sea; blessing the water-snakes; shriving by the Hermit | Partial: the tale must be told again and again |
| *Oliver Twist* | Sikes haunted by Nancy's eyes; Fagin's terror in the condemned cell | None; fear rather than sorrow |
| *Brighton Rock* | Pinkie chooses damnation; Rose's final confession to a priest | "The appalling strangeness of the mercy of God" |
| *The Murder of Roger Ackroyd* | The narrative is the murderer's account; final chapter "Apologia" | Not sought; confession precedes self-punishment |
| *Atonement* | Briony's false accusation; the 1999 coda reveals her authorship | Attempted through fiction, and questioned by Briony herself |

## Who hears the confession?

| Confessor | Example | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| God | Claudius at prayer | Exposes the gap between words and will |
| A priest or holy man | The Hermit; Rose's priest | Ritual of absolution, offered or withheld |
| A silent listener | The envoy in "My Last Duchess" | Power: the Duke controls the conversation |
| The reader | Dr Sheppard; Briony | The reader becomes judge |
| No one | Sikes | Guilt turns inward and becomes haunting |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Remorse | Deep sorrow for a wrong done |
| Contrition | Sorrow for sin with the intention to change, in religious terms |
| Penance | Suffering or action undertaken to atone |
| Absolution | Formal forgiveness of sin, as by a priest |
| Shrive | To hear confession and absolve (archaic) |
| Atonement | Making amends; the reconciling of wrongdoer and wronged |
| Dramatic monologue | A poem spoken by one character to a silent listener, revealing more than intended |
| Unreliable narrator | A narrator whose account cannot be fully trusted |
| Apologia | A formal written defence of one's conduct |
| Externalised guilt | Guilt shown as something outside the mind, such as eyes or ghosts |

## Signals of guilt in an unseen passage

- First-person justification or explanation.
- Omissions, hesitations, a story told twice.
- A listener in the text, or the reader addressed.
- Religious, legal or medical vocabulary.
- Imagery of stains, marks, weights, eyes.
- A return to one scene, suggesting memory that cannot be escaped.

Then judge: sincere, strategic or involuntary?

## Exam traps

- Treating guilt and remorse as the same thing.
- Assuming confession brings forgiveness.
- Ignoring who hears the confession.
- Missing that a whole narrative can be a confession (Christie, McEwan).
- Moral condemnation in place of analysis of method.
- Long quotations copied from the open book instead of short, exact ones.

## Self-test

1. Quote the AQA element covered here.
2. Why can Claudius not be forgiven, by his own reasoning?
3. What dramatic irony surrounds Hamlet's decision in the prayer scene?
4. Why is the dramatic monologue suited to involuntary confession?
5. How does the Duke reveal his crime in "My Last Duchess"?
6. What moment marks the turning point of the Mariner's penance?
7. How does Dickens show Sikes's guilt?
8. What does the old priest say to Rose at the end of *Brighton Rock*?
9. How does the final chapter of *The Murder of Roger Ackroyd* change the novel?
10. What does the 1999 section of *Atonement* reveal?
11. Give two signals of guilt to look for in an unseen passage.
12. Name three possible confessors in crime writing.

## Answers

1. "Guilt and remorse, confession and the desire for forgiveness".
2. He still possesses what the murder gained him, his crown, his ambition and his queen, and will not
   give them up.
3. Hamlet spares Claudius because he seems to be praying and might go to heaven, but the audience has
   heard that his prayer has failed.
4. One speaker addresses a silent listener, so the reader can see what the speaker reveals without
   meaning to.
5. Through a casual account of giving commands, after which all her smiles stopped, so murder is
   presented as a matter of taste.
6. He blesses the water-snakes "unaware", and the albatross falls from his neck.
7. Through Nancy's eyes, which he imagines following him, so guilt is externalised as haunting.
8. He speaks of "the appalling strangeness of the mercy of God".
9. It reveals that the narrative is the murderer's own account, an "Apologia", so the detective story
   becomes a confession.
10. That Briony is the author of the narrative and that the lovers' reunion was invented, so the novel
    is her attempt at atonement.
11. Any two: first-person justification; omissions or hesitation; an internal listener; religious or
    legal vocabulary; imagery of stains or eyes; a repeated return to one scene.
12. Any three: God, a priest or holy man, a silent listener, the reader, a court, no one.
