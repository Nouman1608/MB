Condensed recall for **3.1.2 Section B: Hot desert systems and landscapes** (OxfordAQA International
AS and A-level Geography 9635, Unit 1, option 1a). For the full explanation, read the

## Systems ideas

Use **inputs, outputs, energy, stores, flows, feedback and dynamic equilibrium**. Add **sediment
sources, cells and budgets**: a desert landscape balances sediment gained against sediment lost.

| Systems term | Desert example |
|---|---|
| **Inputs** | Insolation, rare rainfall, wind energy, sediment from weathering and rivers |
| **Stores** | Dune fields, alluvial fans, groundwater, playa deposits |
| **Flows / transfers** | Saltation of sand, sheet floods, flash floods in wadis |
| **Outputs** | Evaporation, dust carried beyond the desert, water draining away |
| **Negative feedback** | A dune grows until its windward slope is steep enough for sand to avalanche down the slip face, keeping its shape stable |
| **Positive feedback** | Loss of vegetation exposes soil to deflation, which removes fertile material, so even less vegetation grows |

**Sources of energy** in hot deserts: **insolation** (drives weathering and convection), **winds**
(move sediment) and **runoff** (episodic but powerful).

Distinguish a **landform** (a single feature such as a barchan) from a **landscape** (an assemblage of
related landforms, such as a sand sea or a mountain front with pediment, bahada and playa).

## Where hot deserts are

Hot deserts and their **arid and semi-arid margins** lie mainly between about 15 and 30 degrees north
and south of the equator: the Sahara, Arabian, Thar, Kalahari, Sonoran and Australian deserts.
Coastal deserts such as the **Atacama** (South America) and **Namib** (south-west Africa) lie on
western coasts beside cold currents.

## Characteristics: climate, soils, vegetation

- **Climate:** very low and unreliable rainfall; high daytime temperatures and a large daily range
  under clear skies; high potential evaporation.
- **Soils:** thin, coarse, low in organic matter (aridisols). Evaporation draws water up through the
  soil, depositing salts at the surface (**salinisation**) and sometimes hard crusts such as calcrete.
- **Vegetation:** sparse, widely spaced xerophytes with deep or wide roots, small or waxy leaves and
  water storage; ephemeral plants complete their life cycle quickly after rain.
- **Interaction:** sparse vegetation adds little organic matter, so soils stay poor; poor soils and
  low water limit plant growth; bare surfaces are exposed to wind and sheet wash.

## Causes of aridity

| Cause | Why it dries the climate |
|---|---|
| Pressure | Sinking air under high pressure warms, so cloud and rain are suppressed |
| Winds | Prevailing winds from land bring little moisture |
| Continentality | Far from the sea, little moisture arrives |
| Relief | Rain shadow: air dried as it crosses mountains |
| Cold ocean currents | Cool the air above, reducing evaporation and rain on nearby coasts |

The **water balance** and the **aridity index** (rainfall compared with potential evaporation)
define dryness.

**Linking pressure to the global circulation:** air rising at the equator diverges at high level and
descends around 30 degrees in the **Hadley cell**, forming the **subtropical high-pressure belt**.
Descending air warms adiabatically, so its relative humidity falls and condensation is prevented.

**Aridity index in practice:** the ratio of precipitation to potential evapotranspiration (P/PET).
A commonly used UNEP classification treats values below 0.05 as hyper-arid, 0.05 to 0.20 as arid and
0.20 to 0.50 as semi-arid. A negative water balance means potential evaporation far exceeds
rainfall, so there is little soil moisture or surface water for most of the year.

## Arid weathering

- **Thermal fracture:** heating and cooling cause expansion and contraction until rock cracks.
- **Exfoliation:** outer layers peel off in sheets.
- **Chemical weathering:** limited by lack of water but still occurs.
- **Block disintegration:** rock breaks along joints into blocks.
- **Granular disintegration:** individual grains loosen from the rock.

Rock is a poor conductor of heat, so the outer layer heats and cools more than the interior,
setting up stresses parallel to the surface (exfoliation). **Granular disintegration** affects
coarse-grained rocks such as granite, where dark and light minerals expand by different amounts.
Dew and occasional rain supply enough moisture for **salt crystallisation** and limited chemical
attack, which often works together with heating and cooling.

**Mass movement** is also a geomorphological process here: weathered debris falls or slides from
steep rock faces to build scree slopes at their base.

## Wind

- **Erosion:** **deflation** lifts loose particles; **abrasion** wears surfaces with wind-blown grains.
- **Transport:** suspension (fine dust), saltation (bouncing sand), surface creep (rolling).
- **Deposition** occurs when wind energy falls.

Saltating sand rarely rises more than a metre or so above the ground, so **abrasion is concentrated
near the surface**: this undercuts rock pillars and shapes yardangs and zeugen.

## Water

Water sources are **exogenous** (from outside, such as a river from wetter regions), **endorheic**
(drains inland, never reaching the sea) and **ephemeral** (short-lived). Water acts **episodically**:
**sheet flooding** and **channel flash flooding** after rare intense rain do much of the erosion
and transport.

Flash floods are so effective because rain is intense, soils are crusted and there is little
vegetation to intercept or slow it, so infiltration is low and runoff is rapid. The **Nile** in
Egypt is an exogenous river; the Okavango and Lake Eyre basins are examples of inland (endorheic)
drainage.

## Landforms

| Wind (aeolian) | Water |
|---|---|
| Deflation hollows | Wadis (dry valleys) |
| Desert pavements | Bahadas (merged fans) |
| Ventifacts (wind-shaped stones) | Pediments (gentle rock slopes) |
| Yardangs (streamlined ridges) | Playas (dry lake beds) |
| Zeugen (table-like rock masses) | Inselbergs (isolated hills) |
| Barchan and seif dunes | |

**Formation in brief:**

- **Desert pavement:** deflation removes fine particles, leaving a closely packed surface of stones
  that protects the ground beneath.
- **Zeugen:** horizontal layers of hard rock over softer rock; abrasion along joints cuts the soft
  rock beneath, leaving tabular blocks with a resistant cap.
- **Yardangs:** ridges of rock aligned **parallel** to the prevailing wind, streamlined by abrasion.
- **Barchans:** crescent-shaped dunes with horns pointing **downwind**, forming where sand supply is
  limited and the wind blows mainly from one direction.
- **Seif dunes:** long, sharp-crested ridges formed where winds blow from two main directions.
- **Wadis:** steep-sided valleys cut by flash floods, dry for most of the year.
- **Alluvial fans and bahadas:** a flash flood emerging from a mountain wadi spreads out and loses
  energy, depositing a fan; neighbouring fans merge into a bahada.
- **Pediments:** gently sloping rock surfaces at the foot of a mountain front, shaped by sheet floods
  and the retreat of the slope above.
- **Playas:** flat, salt-crusted beds of temporary lakes in inland basins, left as water evaporates.
- **Inselbergs:** steep-sided residual hills of resistant rock rising from a plain, such as
  **Uluru** in central Australia.

Link **process, time, landforms and landscapes**: many desert landforms, especially water-cut ones,
may partly reflect wetter conditions in the past.

## Desertification

Know the **changing extent of hot deserts over the last 10 000 years**, the causes (**climate
change and human impact**), areas at risk, impacts and **alternative futures**.

- **Changing extent:** in the early to middle Holocene much of today's Sahara was wetter, with
  grassland, lakes and wildlife -- the "Green Sahara" -- before drying to its current state. Deserts
  have grown and shrunk naturally.
- **Human causes:** overgrazing, over-cultivation, removal of trees for fuelwood, and poor
  irrigation that causes salinisation. Population growth increases pressure on marginal land.
- **Areas at risk:** semi-arid margins such as the **Sahel** south of the Sahara.
- **Impacts:** soil erosion and loss of fertility, falling crop yields, food insecurity, migration
  to towns, dust storms.
- **Climate change:** predicted higher temperatures and more variable rainfall may increase drought
  risk at desert margins.

## Case studies

A hot desert environment engaging with field data, and a local-scale landscape where desertification
has occurred. Use named places and figures you are sure of.

**Desertification example: the Sahel.** Severe droughts in the 1970s and 1980s, combined with
pressure from growing populations and herds, degraded land and caused famine. Responses include the
African Union's **Great Green Wall** initiative (launched in 2007) to restore land across the Sahel,
and local methods in **Burkina Faso** such as **zai pits** (small planting pits filled with manure)
and **stone lines** along contours that slow runoff and trap soil and water. Evaluate: local methods
are cheap and community-led; large-scale schemes have been slower and patchier than planned.

**Field data:** coastal dunes can be used to collect primary data on aeolian processes, such as
sediment size and sorting, dune profiles and vegetation cover.

## Exam traps

- Saying deserts are shaped only by wind: episodic water matters.
- Naming landforms with no formation process.
- Mixing up exogenous, endorheic and ephemeral.
- Treating desertification as natural only, or human only.
- Explaining aridity with one cause only.
- Placing barchan horns upwind -- they point downwind.

## Self-test

1. Why does high pressure cause aridity?
2. Distinguish deflation from abrasion.
3. Name the three modes of wind transport.
4. What is a wadi?
5. Give two causes of desertification.
6. Give an example of positive feedback in a desert system.
7. How is the aridity index calculated?
8. Why is abrasion concentrated close to the ground?
9. How does a bahada form?
10. Distinguish a barchan from a seif dune.
11. Why are flash floods so effective in deserts?
12. Explain how zai pits help combat desertification.

**Answers:** 1. Sinking air warms, which suppresses condensation and rain. 2. Deflation removes loose
particles; abrasion wears surfaces with wind-blown grains. 3. Suspension, saltation, surface creep.
4. A dry valley carved by water in rare floods. 5. Climate change and human impact. 6. Vegetation loss
exposes soil to deflation, which reduces fertility and leads to further vegetation loss. 7.
Precipitation divided by potential evapotranspiration. 8. Saltating sand grains rarely rise far above
the surface. 9. Alluvial fans deposited where flash floods leave mountain wadis merge together. 10.
A barchan is crescent-shaped with horns downwind, formed by a single dominant wind and limited sand;
a seif is a long linear ridge formed by winds from two directions. 11. Intense rain falls on crusted,
bare ground with low infiltration, so runoff is rapid and powerful. 12. They concentrate water and
manure around plants, raising yields and soil fertility on degraded land.
