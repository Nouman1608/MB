These notes condense section **3.4.1 Kinetic theory** (statements a to e) of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Physics (9203) specification, for exams May/June 2018 onwards, including the stearic acid cooling curve practical. The qualification is untiered. Read the [kinetic theory study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-kinetic-theory/) for full worked examples, then use the [kinetic theory practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-kinetic-theory-practice/).

## Condensed recall

**States of matter (a).**

- **Solid**: particles close, regular pattern, vibrate about fixed positions. Least energy. Fixed shape and volume.
- **Liquid**: particles close, irregular, move around each other. More energy. Flows; fixed volume.
- **Gas**: particles far apart, random, fast. Most energy. Fills its container; easily compressed.

**Specific heat capacity (b).** Energy needed to change the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C.

> E = m c Δθ (E in J, m in kg, c in J/kg °C, Δθ in °C)

**Specific latent heat of vaporisation (c).** Energy needed to change 1 kg from liquid to vapour with no change in temperature.

**Specific latent heat of fusion (d).** Energy needed to change 1 kg from solid to liquid with no change in temperature.

> E = m L_V and E = m L_F (L in J/kg)

**During a change of state** the temperature is constant. The energy overcomes forces between particles instead of increasing their kinetic energy. The same energy is released on freezing or condensing.

**Impurities (e).** Lower the melting point and raise the boiling point; the change of state happens over a range of temperatures.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Kinetic theory | Model in which matter is made of particles in constant motion |
| Specific heat capacity, c | Energy to raise the temperature of 1 kg by 1 °C |
| Specific latent heat of fusion, L_F | Energy to melt 1 kg with no temperature change |
| Specific latent heat of vaporisation, L_V | Energy to turn 1 kg of liquid to vapour with no temperature change |
| Melting point | Temperature at which a solid becomes liquid (same as the freezing point) |
| Boiling point | Temperature at which a liquid boils to form a gas |
| Plateau | Flat section of a temperature–time graph during a change of state |
| Impurity | A different substance mixed into a pure one |

## Temperature–time graph checklist

| Section of a heating graph | What the particles are doing |
|---|---|
| Rising (solid) | Vibrating faster |
| Flat at melting point | Breaking free of fixed positions |
| Rising (liquid) | Moving faster around each other |
| Flat at boiling point (longest) | Separating completely |
| Rising (gas) | Moving faster still |

- Constant power: gentler slope means larger specific heat capacity.
- Boiling plateau is longer than melting plateau because L_V is larger than L_F.
- Energy during a plateau = power × time on the flat section.

## Stearic acid cooling curve

- Melt the acid in hot water, then record its temperature every 30 s as it cools.
- Curve: falls, goes flat at the freezing point, then falls again.
- Flat section: energy released as particles bond into a solid balances energy lost to the room.
- Cooling slows as the temperature difference with the room shrinks.
- An impure sample gives a lower, sloping freezing section.

## Calculation reminders

1. Convert grams to kilograms (divide by 1000) and kJ to J (multiply by 1000).
2. For several stages, find the energy of each stage separately, then add.
3. Use the right c for each state (ice and water have different values).
4. Rearrange before substituting: Δθ = E ÷ (m c), m = E ÷ L.
5. Practical values of c or L are usually too high, because some energy heats the surroundings.

## Exam traps

- Saying temperature rises while a substance melts.
- Mixing up L_F and L_V.
- Forgetting the warming stage before boiling or melting.
- Describing gas particles as "vibrating". They move freely and quickly.
- Claiming impurities raise melting points. They lower them.

## Quick self-test

1. Describe the arrangement and movement of particles in a liquid.
2. Why is a gas easy to compress but a solid is not?
3. Define specific heat capacity.
4. How much energy raises 3.0 kg of water by 12 °C? (c = 4200 J/kg °C)
5. A 0.75 kg block gains 48 000 J and warms by 64 °C. Find c.
6. Define specific latent heat of fusion.
7. How much energy melts 0.80 kg of ice at 0 °C? (L_F = 3.34 × 10⁵ J/kg)
8. 0.050 kg of steam condenses at 100 °C. How much energy is released? (L_V = 2.26 × 10⁶ J/kg)
9. Why does the temperature stay constant while water boils?
10. A 100 W heater keeps a 0.25 kg solid at its melting point for 300 s. Find L_F.
11. How does salt affect the melting point of ice?
12. On a heating graph for water, why is the boiling plateau longer than the melting plateau?

### Answers

1. Close together in an irregular arrangement; they move around each other.
2. Gas particles have large spaces between them; solid particles are already touching.
3. The energy needed to change the temperature of 1 kg of the substance by 1 °C.
4. E = 3.0 × 4200 × 12 = **151 200 J** (about 1.5 × 10⁵ J).
5. c = 48 000 ÷ (0.75 × 64) = **1000 J/kg °C**.
6. The energy needed to change 1 kg of a substance from solid to liquid with no change in temperature.
7. E = 0.80 × 3.34 × 10⁵ = **2.67 × 10⁵ J**.
8. E = 0.050 × 2.26 × 10⁶ = **1.13 × 10⁵ J**.
9. The energy separates the particles (overcomes the forces between them) instead of making them move faster.
10. E = 100 × 300 = 30 000 J; L_F = 30 000 ÷ 0.25 = **1.2 × 10⁵ J/kg**.
11. It lowers the melting point.
12. The specific latent heat of vaporisation is larger than that of fusion, so more energy, and more time at constant power, is needed.
