Key question 1.2 of Cambridge O Level Sociology (2251) asks *What types of data and evidence do
sociologists use?* In Paper 1 it is tested in two places. Question 1(a) prints a source, which may be
qualitative, quantitative or both, and asks you to identify information, explain an aspect of the
research and explain problems with the evidence. Question 1(e) asks you to choose secondary evidence
for a research plan of your own. This guide gives you a way to judge any piece of evidence and a
routine for reading tables, charts and graphs accurately under time pressure.

## What the syllabus covers

- **1.2.1 Sources of data:** differences between primary and secondary sources; differences between
  qualitative and quantitative data; strengths and limitations of each type of data.
- **1.2.2 Secondary evidence:** examples of official statistics, historical and personal documents,
  digital sources and media content, with the strengths and limitations of each.
- **1.2.3 Analysis, interpretation and evaluation of data from qualitative and quantitative sources:**
  summaries of sociological studies, diagrams, tables, charts and graphs.

## Two separate questions about any piece of data

Every item of evidence can be classified twice, and students often merge the two.

1. **Who collected it, and for what purpose?** **Primary** data is gathered by the researcher for the
   current study. **Secondary** data already exists, produced by someone else for another reason.
2. **What form does it take?** **Quantitative** data is numerical. **Qualitative** data is words,
   images or sound that describe meanings and experiences.

So a government household survey is secondary and quantitative for a student who uses it, while a
diary is secondary and qualitative. Your own unstructured interview transcript is primary and
qualitative. Classify both ways before you evaluate, because the strengths follow from the
classification: secondary data saves time and money but was not designed for your aims, and
quantitative data is easy to compare but rarely explains *why*.

## Judging secondary evidence

A useful test comes from John Scott's *A Matter of Record* (1990), which proposed four questions for
any document:

| Criterion | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| **Authenticity** | Is it genuine, and is it what it claims to be? |
| **Credibility** | Is it sincere and accurate, free from distortion? |
| **Representativeness** | Is it typical of documents of its kind, and do enough survive? |
| **Meaning** | Can the researcher understand what it meant to the people who produced it? |

Apply these four questions to each type of secondary evidence the syllabus names.

### Official statistics

Official statistics are numerical data collected by governments and their agencies: censuses, birth,
marriage and death registrations, school results, recorded crime, unemployment counts.

- **Strengths:** large or complete coverage of the population; collected regularly, so trends over
  time can be compared; free and quick to obtain; favoured by positivists because they are
  quantitative and can show correlations between groups.
- **Limitations:** categories and definitions are chosen by government and can change, which breaks
  comparisons over time; some activities are hidden, as with the **dark figure of crime** (offences
  never reported to or recorded by the police); interpretivists argue that statistics are **socially
  constructed** by the decisions of the officials who record them, so they show how institutions
  work as much as how people behave.

### Historical documents

Historical documents include old government records, court reports, newspapers, letters and
photographs. W. I. Thomas and Florian Znaniecki's *The Polish Peasant in Europe and America*
(1918–1920) made extensive use of letters between Polish migrants and their families to study how
migration changed family life.

- **Strengths:** often the only evidence about the past; allow comparison between past and present;
  produced without a researcher present, so there is no observer effect.
- **Limitations:** authenticity can be hard to establish; survival is uneven, so records of the
  powerful and literate are over-represented; meanings may be hard to interpret across time.

### Personal documents

Diaries, letters, autobiographies, blogs and photograph albums written by individuals for themselves
or people they know.

- **Strengths:** rich qualitative detail about feelings and motives, which supports verstehen; written
  in the person's own words.
- **Limitations:** credibility may be low if the writer exaggerates or presents a flattering image;
  rarely representative, since only some people write diaries or keep letters; access may raise
  questions of privacy and consent.

### Digital sources

Websites, social media posts, online forums, search trends, digital archives and government data
published online.

- **Strengths:** vast quantities of current material; can reach hidden or geographically scattered
  groups; often free and searchable.
- **Limitations:** posts may be anonymous, fake or produced by automated accounts, so authenticity is
  hard to check; users are not representative of the whole population, because access to the
  internet varies by age, income and region; people present a selected version of themselves online;
  using public posts without consent raises ethical issues.

### Media content

Newspapers, television, films, advertisements, music and online video. Sociologists often use
**content analysis**, counting and categorising how often something appears. The Glasgow University
Media Group's *Bad News* (1976) analysed television news coverage of industrial disputes and argued
that it tended to present events from the point of view of management. Gaye Tuchman (1978) used the
phrase "symbolic annihilation" for the way women were under-represented and trivialised in the mass
media.

- **Strengths:** cheap, plentiful and available for analysis at any time; useful for studying
  representations of gender, ethnicity, age or crime; content analysis can be repeated, so it can be
  reliable.
- **Limitations:** media content reflects the priorities of owners, editors and advertisers, so it is
  not a direct picture of society; the researcher's choice of categories can introduce bias; counting
  images says little about how audiences interpret them.

## Reading quantitative sources: a five-step routine

Use this order every time a source contains a table, chart, diagram or graph.

1. **Title, source, date:** what is being measured, by whom, and when?
2. **Units:** percentages, numbers, rates per 1,000, or index values? Percentages hide how many people
   are behind each figure.
3. **The overall pattern first:** rising, falling, stable, or different between groups?
4. **The precise detail second:** quote exact figures, with units, to support the pattern.
5. **The problem:** sample size, who is missing, how terms were defined, how the data was collected,
   and whether a correlation is being presented as a cause.

### Worked example: a table (invented data)

*A survey of 400 pupils in one town asked how many hours a day they spend on social media. The table
shows the percentage in each group who reported more than three hours a day. The figures are invented
for practice.*

| Age | Girls | Boys |
|---|---|---|
| 13 | 34% | 28% |
| 15 | 46% | 37% |
| 17 | 52% | 41% |

**Identify:** the percentage of 15-year-old boys reporting more than three hours is 37%.

**Describe the pattern:** reported heavy use rises with age for both girls and boys. Girls report
higher use than boys at every age, and the gap widens from 6 percentage points at 13 to 11 points at
17.

**Explain a problem:** the data is **self-reported**, so pupils may underestimate or exaggerate their
time online, which lowers validity. A survey in one town may not be **representative** of pupils
elsewhere. With 400 pupils split into six groups, each percentage may rest on only about 65 to 70
pupils, so small differences could be due to chance.

Notice that the answer says "percentage points" when comparing two percentages: the gap between 52%
and 41% is 11 percentage points, not 11%.

## Reading qualitative sources and study summaries

A summary of a sociological study is a short paragraph describing who was studied, how and what was
found. Before answering, underline four things: the **method**, the **sample** (size and how it was
chosen), the **findings**, and any **detail that suggests a problem**, such as a researcher who was
known to participants or a sample drawn from one place. Most "explain a problem" marks come from that
fourth item.

## Common mistakes

- Calling every number "official statistics": a researcher's own survey produces primary quantitative
  data.
- Describing a table figure by figure without stating the overall pattern.
- Writing "%" when you mean percentage points.
- Giving a limitation that would apply to any evidence ("it might be wrong") rather than one tied to
  the source.
- Treating media content as evidence of what audiences think.

## Exam technique

- **Identify** (part a): one precise item from the source, with units; no explanation needed.
- **Explain** (part a): link the feature of the source to a concept, such as reliability or
  representativeness, and say why.
- **Evaluate** (part d): give strengths and limitations of the type of evidence, then judge its
  usefulness for a specific purpose.
- **Part (e) plans:** name one secondary source that fits the topic and give a reason for using it.
