## 1. What government failure is

**Government failure** occurs when government intervention leads to a less efficient allocation of resources than before, or fails to improve it as much as it could have, so that there is a net welfare loss from the intervention.

There are two degrees to recognise:

- **Complete government failure:** intervention makes the outcome worse than the free market.
- **Partial government failure:** intervention improves on the market but leaves a welfare loss that a better-designed policy would have avoided, or costs more than necessary.

Government failure is the mirror image of market failure. Market failure is a reason to consider intervention; government failure is the risk that the cure is worse than the disease.

## 2. Worked example: the wrong tax

Use an invented market for disposable plastic cutlery: demand Qd = 300 − 10P and supply Qs = 20P − 60, with Q in thousand boxes and P in pounds. Each box imposes a marginal external cost of £3.

- **Free market:** 300 − 10P = 20P − 60 gives P = £12 and Q = **180**.
- **Social optimum:** a £3 tax gives supply 20P − 120, so P = £14 and Q = **160**.
- **Welfare loss with no tax:** ½ × (180 − 160) × 3 = **£30 thousand**.

Now suppose officials overestimate the external cost.

| Tax per box | New supply | Price | Quantity | Welfare loss (£ thousand) | Compared with no tax |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| £0 | 20P − 60 | £12 | 180 | 30 | Benchmark |
| £3 (correct) | 20P − 120 | £14 | 160 | 0 | Market failure corrected |
| £6 | 20P − 180 | £16 | 140 | 30 | No better than no tax |
| £9 | 20P − 240 | £18 | 120 | 120 | Four times worse |

How the £9 row is found: at Q = 120, buyers value the last box at £18 (from demand) and the social cost is £9 + £3 = £12 (from supply plus the external cost), a gap of £6. The loss from cutting output 40 below the optimum is ½ × 40 × 6 = **£120 thousand**.

A £6 tax is government failure: it cuts output too far and leaves welfare exactly where the market left it, while imposing extra costs on buyers. A £9 tax is complete government failure: the intervention causes four times the original loss. The lesson is that intervention based on poor information can be worse than none.

## 3. Worked example: a maximum price and a black market

In the same market, suppose the government imposes a maximum price of **£9** to keep the product cheap for small food stalls.

- Qd = 300 − 90 = 210; Qs = 180 − 60 = 120. Shortage = **90 thousand boxes**.
- Only 120 thousand boxes are supplied. Buyers who cannot get them at £9 would pay up to the price on the demand curve at Q = 120: 300 − 10P = 120 gives **P = £18**.

So a black market can emerge in which boxes resell at up to £18, which is double the legal price and £6 above the free-market price. The policy meant to make the good cheaper leaves many buyers paying more, while legal sellers earn less and output falls. This is an **unintended consequence**, one of the most common causes of government failure.

## 4. Causes of government failure

| Cause | How it leads to failure |
|---|---|
| Inadequate information | Officials cannot measure external costs or benefits accurately, so taxes, subsidies or limits are set at the wrong level |
| Unintended consequences | Black markets, smuggling, shortages and avoidance behaviour that the policy did not anticipate |
| Distortion of price signals | Controls stop prices from rationing goods and signalling where resources are needed |
| Administrative costs | Running, monitoring and enforcing a policy can cost more than the welfare gain |
| Conflicting objectives | A policy that serves one aim harms another, such as a fuel tax that cuts emissions but hits rural households hardest |
| Political self-interest and short-termism | Policies may be chosen to win votes before an election rather than to maximise welfare |
| Regulatory capture | A regulator comes to serve the interests of the firms it regulates rather than the public |
| Time lags | By the time a policy takes effect, the problem may have changed |

**Administrative cost example.** Suppose a scheme removes a welfare loss of £30 thousand a year but costs £45 thousand a year to administer and enforce. Society is **£15 thousand** a year worse off than with no scheme, even though the policy "works" in the market itself.

## 5. Consequences of government failure

- **Misallocation of resources:** output ends up too high or too low, creating new welfare losses.
- **Wasted public money:** spending on schemes that do not achieve their aims has an opportunity cost in schools, health care or lower taxes.
- **Higher prices or shortages:** as with the black-market example.
- **Weakened incentives:** subsidies can reduce pressure on firms to cut costs; price support can encourage overproduction.
- **Inequity:** costs may fall on groups the policy did not intend, such as low-income households.
- **Loss of trust:** visible failures can make future, well-designed interventions harder to introduce.

## 6. Evaluating government failure

- **Compare like with like.** The real choice is between an imperfect market and imperfect intervention. A £4 tax in the example still leaves a small loss, but it is far better than no tax.
- **Size and likelihood.** Government failure is more likely where information is poor, where the policy involves price controls, and where enforcement is weak.
- **Design matters.** Pilot schemes, gradual introduction, regular review and independent bodies to set or monitor policy can reduce the risk.
- **Choice of tool.** Market-based tools such as taxes and permits keep price signals working, while price controls switch them off and so carry a higher risk of black markets.
- **Time.** A policy that fails at first can be adjusted; the long-run cost of leaving a serious market failure alone may be greater.

A balanced judgement says government failure is a reason to design intervention carefully and to choose tools well, not a reason never to intervene.

## Annotated model answer

*Question:* "Explain one cause of government failure."

> One cause is inadequate information *[cause named]*. To correct an external cost with a tax, the government must know the marginal external cost, but costs such as damage to health have no market price *[why information is hard to get]*. If it overestimates the cost and sets a £6 tax when the true cost is £3, output falls from 180 to 140 instead of to the optimum of 160 *[applied with figures]*. The welfare loss is then £30 thousand, no better than with no tax, so intervention has failed to improve resource allocation *[consequence linked to the definition]*.

## Common mistakes

- Defining government failure as "a policy that does not work". It is about resource allocation and net welfare.
- Listing causes without explaining how each worsens allocation.
- Assuming any government failure means the market should be left alone.
- Forgetting administrative costs when judging a policy.

## Exam technique

- **Explain:** name the cause, explain the mechanism and finish with its effect on allocation or welfare.
- **Evaluate:** weigh the risk of government failure against the size of the market failure, consider better-designed alternatives and reach a judgement that depends on information and policy design.
