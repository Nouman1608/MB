## The multiplier: definition and process

The multiplier is the ratio of the final change in national income to the initial change in injections (or in autonomous spending) that caused it. An injection is spending that enters the circular flow from outside the domestic household spending loop: investment, government spending and exports.

The reason it exceeds 1 is that one person's spending is another person's income. Take a closed economy with no government, where households spend 0.75 of each extra unit of income and save the rest. Firms receive an injection of 100 cu in new investment orders.

| Round | New income | Of which spent (75%) | Cumulative income |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 100.00 | 75.00 | 100.00 |
| 2 | 75.00 | 56.25 | 175.00 |
| 3 | 56.25 | 42.19 | 231.25 |
| 4 | 42.19 | 31.64 | 273.44 |

The rounds go on, each smaller than the last, and the total converges on 100 x 1/(1 - 0.75) = 400 cu. The multiplier is 4. The process runs in reverse for a fall in injections, and it takes time: income does not reach the new level at once.

## Average and marginal propensities

Average propensities relate a total to income at one level. Marginal propensities relate a change to the change in income that caused it.

| Measure | Formula |
|---|---|
| apc, aps | C / Yd and S / Yd, where Yd is disposable income; apc + aps = 1 |
| mpc, mps | change in C / change in Yd and change in S / change in Yd; mpc + mps = 1 |
| apm, mpm | M / Y and change in M / change in Y |
| art, mrt | T / Y and change in T / change in Y |

Here C is consumption, S is saving, M is imports, T is tax revenue and Y is national income.

**Worked example 1.** Households in Country A have this data (cu).

| Yd | C | S |
|---|---|---|
| 200 | 190 | 10 |
| 300 | 260 | 40 |
| 400 | 330 | 70 |

At Yd = 300: apc = 260/300 = 0.867 and aps = 40/300 = 0.133. From 200 to 300: mpc = (260 - 190)/(300 - 200) = 70/100 = 0.7 and mps = 30/100 = 0.3. The data fit C = 50 + 0.7Yd, with autonomous consumption of 50. Because of that fixed 50, the apc falls as income rises (0.95, 0.867, 0.825) while the mpc stays at 0.7.

## Formulae for the multiplier

Every unit of extra income either is spent on domestic output or leaks out. The multiplier is the reciprocal of the share that leaks out:

| Economy | Formula |
|---|---|
| Closed, no government | k = 1/mps, which equals 1/(1 - mpc) |
| Closed, with government | k = 1/(mps + mrt) |
| Open, no government | k = 1/(mps + mpm) |
| Open, with government | k = 1/(mps + mrt + mpm) |

The denominator is the marginal propensity to withdraw (mpw). A larger mpw means a smaller multiplier.

**Which base are the propensities measured on?** In the table above, mps, mrt and mpm are each a share of an extra unit of national income. Questions sometimes give mpc and mps on disposable income instead. Then convert: each extra unit of income is taxed at rate t, so only (1 - t) becomes disposable income. Saving per unit of national income is mps x (1 - t), and the multiplier is:

```
k = 1 / (1 - mpc(1 - t) + mpm)  =  1 / (mps(1 - t) + t + mpm)
```

**Worked example 2.** Country B has mps = 0.15, mrt = 0.20 and mpm = 0.15, each per unit of national income.
mpw = 0.15 + 0.20 + 0.15 = 0.50, so k = 1/0.50 = 2.
Government spending rises by 60 cu: change in Y = 2 x 60 = 120 cu.

**Worked example 3 (disposable-income form).** Country C has mpc = 0.8 on disposable income, a tax rate of 25% and mpm = 0.1.
k = 1/(1 - 0.8 x 0.75 + 0.1) = 1/(1 - 0.6 + 0.1) = 1/0.5 = 2.
Check through withdrawals: mps(1 - t) + t + mpm = 0.2 x 0.75 + 0.25 + 0.1 = 0.15 + 0.25 + 0.10 = 0.5, so k = 2. Both routes agree.

## National income determination

There are two equivalent approaches.

**AD approach.** Equilibrium national income is where planned aggregate expenditure equals income: Y = AD = C + I + G + (X - M). On a diagram with income on the horizontal axis and planned expenditure on the vertical axis, the equilibrium is where the AD line cuts the 45-degree line. The AD line starts at the autonomous level of spending and slopes upward with a gradient equal to the marginal propensity to spend on domestic output (1 - mpw).

**Income approach (injections equal withdrawals).** Equilibrium is also where planned injections (I + G + X) equal planned withdrawals (S + T + M). If injections exceed withdrawals, income rises; if withdrawals exceed injections, income falls.

**Worked example 4.** Country D has these planned behaviours (cu):

```
C = 100 + 0.8Yd      I = 150      G = 200      X = 120
T = 0.25Y            M = 0.1Y     Yd = Y - T
```

AD approach:
```
Y = 100 + 0.8(Y - 0.25Y) + 150 + 200 + 120 - 0.1Y
Y = 570 + 0.6Y - 0.1Y = 570 + 0.5Y
0.5Y = 570, so Y = 1,140
```
Autonomous spending is 570 and mpw = 0.5, so k = 2 and Y = 2 x 570 = 1,140.

Income approach at Y = 1,140: Yd = 855; S = Yd - C = 855 - (100 + 684) = 71; T = 285; M = 114. Withdrawals = 71 + 285 + 114 = 470. Injections = 150 + 200 + 120 = 470. They match, so Y = 1,140 is equilibrium.

Check why other levels are not equilibrium:

| Y | AD = 570 + 0.5Y | AD - Y | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| 900 | 1,020 | +120 | Stocks fall, output rises |
| 1,140 | 1,140 | 0 | Equilibrium |
| 1,400 | 1,270 | -130 | Stocks build up, output falls |

Equilibrium is not necessarily full employment. The income approach shows only where planned injections and withdrawals balance.

## Effect of a change in AD on national income

Change in Y = k x change in autonomous spending. Only autonomous changes shift the AD line; a rise in Y itself moves along it.

**Worked example 5 (Country D continued).**
- Government spending rises by 40: change in Y = 2 x 40 = +80, so Y = 1,220.
- Exports fall by 30: change in Y = 2 x (-30) = -60, so Y = 1,080.
- Autonomous consumption rises by 25: change in Y = +50, so Y = 1,190.
- To raise income by 90, the required net injection is 90/2 = 45.

**A change in a propensity changes the multiplier itself.** Suppose the tax rate rises from 25% to 35%, with nothing else altered.
k = 1/(1 - 0.8 x 0.65 + 0.1) = 1/0.58 = 1.724. Y = 570 x 1.724 = 982.8, a fall of 157.2 cu (13.8%). The AD line pivots to a flatter slope. Compare this with a change in autonomous spending, which shifts the line in parallel.

**Limits of the multiplier.** The figure from the formula is a prediction under assumptions. Evaluate it with these points:
- Spare capacity: if the economy is near full capacity, extra spending bids up prices more than output, so the real multiplier is smaller.
- Time lags: each round takes time, so the full effect may be spread over many months, and other changes may occur meanwhile.
- Crowding out: if government borrowing raises interest rates, private investment may fall.
- Stable propensities: the mpc, tax rate and mpm are assumed constant but may change with confidence, expectations or exchange rates.
- High leakages: a country that imports heavily or taxes heavily has a small multiplier, so a given injection achieves less.

## Common errors

- Using 1/mpc instead of 1/(1 - mpc) or 1/mps.
- Leaving out a leakage that the question mentions, or adding one the economy lacks.
- Mixing disposable income and national income bases without converting.
- Finding apc from a change in C rather than from the level of C.
- Multiplying by the multiplier a total change in AD that includes induced spending. Apply it to the autonomous change only.
- Giving a change in Y without its sign or units.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is the multiplier AS or A Level content in Cambridge 9708?**
The multiplier is outcome 9.1.1 in the Cambridge International AS & A Level Economics 9708 syllabus. Topic 9 is A Level content, so it is examined on the A Level papers rather than at AS.

**What is the multiplier in economics?**
The multiplier is the ratio of the final change in national income to the initial change in injections or autonomous spending that caused it. It is greater than 1 because one person's spending becomes another person's income.

**What are injections and withdrawals in the circular flow?**
Injections are investment, government spending and exports. Withdrawals (leakages) are saving, taxation and imports, and national income is in equilibrium where planned injections equal planned withdrawals.

**Why might the real multiplier be smaller than the formula predicts?**
Near full capacity, extra spending raises prices more than output. Time lags, crowding out of private investment and changing propensities can also reduce the effect, and economies with high taxes or imports have small multipliers.
