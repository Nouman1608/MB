## Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Investment appraisal | Evaluating whether an investment opportunity is worth its cost, using forecast returns |
| Capital cost | The money spent at the start of the project (also called the original cost) |
| Returns | The forecast net money earned from the project each year |
| Payback period | The time taken for returns to repay the capital cost |
| Average rate of return (ARR) | The average yearly gain after recovering the capital cost, as a percentage of the capital cost |
| Net present value (NPV), HL only | The total of the present values of the returns, minus the original cost |
| Discount rate, HL only | The rate used to convert future returns into present values |
| Discount factor, HL only | The number by which a future return is multiplied to give its present value |

## Formulae

| Method | Formula | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Payback (even returns) | capital cost ÷ annual net return | Standard form (the guide does not print one) |
| Payback (uneven returns) | years until cumulative returns reach the cost, plus the fraction of the final year needed | Standard form |
| ARR | [(total returns − capital cost) ÷ years of use] ÷ capital cost × 100 | Printed in the guide |
| NPV (HL only) | Σ present values of return − original cost | Printed in the guide |
| Present value (HL only) | return × discount factor | Standard form |

The guide says a copy of its formulae will be provided for the examination, and that HL candidates are given a discount table in the paper when required. The table covers 4%, 6%, 8%, 10% and 20% for years 1 to 10.

## Method in steps

**Payback with uneven returns**

1. Write the cumulative returns year by year.
2. Find the year in which the running total passes the capital cost.
3. Fraction = amount still needed at the start of that year ÷ that year's return.
4. Payback = completed years + fraction. Convert to months if asked (fraction × 12).

**ARR**

1. Total returns − capital cost = total net gain.
2. Total net gain ÷ years of use = average annual gain.
3. Average annual gain ÷ capital cost × 100.

**NPV (HL only)**

1. Pick the factor for each year at the stated rate from the table.
2. Return × factor = present value, year by year.
3. Add the present values.
4. Subtract the original cost.

## Worked reminder

Polbrook Cycles is considering a workshop extension costing 90,000 (dollars). Forecast net returns: year 1, 25,000; year 2, 35,000; year 3, 45,000; year 4, 30,000.

Payback: cumulative returns are 25,000, 60,000 and 105,000. After two years 30,000 is still needed, and year 3 brings 45,000. Fraction = 30,000 ÷ 45,000 = 0.667. Payback = 2.7 years (2 years 8 months).

ARR: total returns = 135,000. Net gain = 135,000 − 90,000 = 45,000. Per year = 45,000 ÷ 4 = 11,250. ARR = 11,250 ÷ 90,000 × 100 = 12.5%.

NPV at 6% (HL only): factors 0.9434, 0.8900, 0.8396, 0.7921. Present values: 23,585; 31,150; 37,782; 23,763. Total = 116,280. NPV = 116,280 − 90,000 = 26,280.

NPV at 10% (HL only): factors 0.9091, 0.8264, 0.7513, 0.6830. Present values: 22,727.5; 28,924; 33,808.5; 20,490. Total = 105,950. NPV = 15,950. The NPV falls as the discount rate rises, but stays positive here.

## Must-know distinctions

| | Payback | ARR | NPV (HL only) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Measured in | Time | Percentage | Money |
| Uses whole project life | No | Yes | Yes |
| Considers timing of returns | Only crudely (when cost is recovered) | No | Yes |
| Time value of money | Ignored | Ignored | Included |
| Main use | Speed of recovery, liquidity, risk | Comparing average profitability with a target | Judging overall value in today's terms |
| Key weakness | Ignores returns after payback | Ignores when returns arrive | Depends on the chosen discount rate and on forecasts |

Other contrasts to remember:

- A short payback does not mean a high ARR or a high NPV, and the reverse.
- A project can have a positive ARR but a negative NPV if the discount rate is high enough.
- Higher discount rates reduce present values, so NPV falls.

## Evaluating an appraisal

Quantitative results are one input. Strong answers also weigh:

- Reliability of forecasts and the risk that returns do not arrive.
- Liquidity and how the project would be financed.
- Fit with objectives and strategy.
- Effects on stakeholders, including ethical and environmental effects.
- Whether the business has a benchmark such as a target payback or a cost of borrowing.

Structure for a recommendation: state the result, interpret it for this business, give the limitation that matters most, give a counter-point, then reach a judgement.

## Quick self-test

1. A business buys equipment for 84,000 and expects net returns of 12,000 each year. Calculate the payback period.
2. A project costs 50,000 and returns 10,000 in year 1, 20,000 in year 2 and 30,000 in year 3. Calculate the payback period in years to two decimal places.
3. A machine costs 80,000 and brings total returns of 104,000 over four years. Calculate the ARR.
4. A shop refit costs 200,000 and brings 50,000 each year for six years. Calculate the ARR to two decimal places.
5. State one limitation of ARR.
6. (HL only) Using the 10% factor of 0.7513 for year 3, find the present value of a return of 40,000 received in year 3.
7. (HL only) A project costs 30,000 and returns 12,000 in year 1 and 18,000 in year 2. At 4%, the factors are 0.9615 and 0.9246. Calculate the NPV.
8. (HL only) Explain why the NPV of a project falls if the discount rate rises.
9. Project X has a payback of 2 years and an ARR of 6%. Project Y has a payback of 3 years and an ARR of 14%. State which project each method favors.
10. Identify two non-financial factors a business might consider before investing.
11. (HL only) A project has an NPV of +5,000 at 8% and −2,000 at 10%. Explain what this suggests about the project.

### Answers

1. 84,000 ÷ 12,000 = **7 years**.
2. Cumulative: 10,000; 30,000; 60,000. After two years 20,000 is still needed. 20,000 ÷ 30,000 = 0.67. Payback = **2.67 years**.
3. (104,000 − 80,000) ÷ 4 = 6,000. 6,000 ÷ 80,000 × 100 = **7.5%**.
4. Total returns = 300,000. (300,000 − 200,000) ÷ 6 = 16,666.67. 16,666.67 ÷ 200,000 × 100 = **8.33%**.
5. It ignores the timing of returns (or the time value of money), so a project paying late can look as good as one paying early. Forecasts across the whole life may also be unreliable.
6. 40,000 × 0.7513 = **30,052**.
7. Present values: 12,000 × 0.9615 = 11,538; 18,000 × 0.9246 = 16,642.8. Total = 28,180.8. NPV = 28,180.8 − 30,000 = **−1,819.2** (a negative NPV).
8. A higher rate gives smaller discount factors, so each future return has a smaller present value. The total falls while the original cost stays the same, so NPV falls.
9. Payback favors X (shorter). ARR favors Y (higher).
10. Any two, for example: effect on employees, environmental impact, fit with the business's objectives, effect on reputation, ethical concerns.
11. The project earns more than the required return at 8% but fails to at 10%, so the decision is sensitive to the rate. The business should check which rate best reflects its cost of finance and how reliable its forecasts are.

## Command words in this topic

- Calculate: work out a number, showing each step, with units.
- Define or state: give a precise meaning or a short fact.
- Explain: give a reason or consequence, not only a name.
- Analyse: break the issue into parts and link each to the business in the case.
- Evaluate: weigh strengths against limitations, using the data, and reach a supported conclusion.
- Justify or recommend: choose an option and support the choice with reasons from the case, while acknowledging what could change it.

For a decision question, a useful habit is to write the numbers first, then one line on what each number tells you, then the judgement. This keeps the calculation and the evaluation connected.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Stopping at the cumulative table without turning the final fraction into years or months.
- Using the wrong discount factor, for example the year 2 factor for a year 1 return.
- Reporting total present value as the NPV and forgetting to deduct the original cost.
- Dividing ARR by total returns instead of by the capital cost.
- Quoting a limitation (such as ignoring the time value of money) that does not apply to the method in question.
- Writing a recommendation based on one number with no counter-argument.
- Giving general advantages and disadvantages without using the figures or facts in the case.
- Leaving out units: years, months, percentage or dollars.
