## The four specification points

1. Distinguish between customers and consumers.
2. Distinguish marketing methods and approaches appropriate for business to business (B2B) and business to consumer (B2C) marketing.
3. Evaluate marketing methods, approaches and strategies appropriate for B2C marketing.
4. Evaluate marketing methods, approaches and strategies appropriate for B2B marketing.

## Core recall

### Customer or consumer

- Customer: buys the product. Consumer: uses it.
- Same person: a student buying a laptop. Different: parent buys cereal, child eats it.
- A business customer buys for use in its operations or to resell.
- Marketing must reach the buyer and often the user, who may influence the choice.

### B2B and B2C

| Feature | B2C | B2B |
|---|---|---|
| Buyers | Many | Often few |
| Order size | Small | Large, repeated |
| Decision | Quick, emotional, image | Slower, rational, several people |
| Demand | Direct | Often derived |
| Relationship | Brand loyalty | Contracts, account management |
| Price | Fixed or promotional | Negotiated, volume discounts, credit |
| Methods | Advertising, social media, retail, promotions, loyalty | Personal selling, trade shows, tendering, technical information |

Derived demand: a business buys because its own customers want something, so B2B suppliers are sensitive to changes in the final market.

### B2B methods

| Method | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Personal selling, account management | Trust; tailored; technical answers | Expensive per contact |
| Trade shows | Many buyers; demonstrations | Stand and staff costs |
| Tendering | Large contracts | Slow; price-driven |
| Technical website | Cheap; self-service research | No relationship alone |
| Volume discounts, credit | Larger repeat orders | Lower margin; credit risk |

### B2C methods

| Method | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| TV and online advertising | Wide reach; brand image | Costly; hard to measure |
| Social media, influencers | Targeted; viral potential | Unpredictable |
| Promotions | Quick sales; trial | Lower margin |
| Loyalty schemes | Repeat purchase; data | Cost; copied |
| Branding | Lasting differentiation | Slow, expensive |

### Choosing

- Few buyers, high value: personal selling and relationships.
- Many buyers, low value: mass and social media, retail presence.
- Complex product: demonstration and information in either market.
- Judge by cost, reach, measurability and return for the business in the case.

## Quick worked reminder

Calder Fixings (invented): sales cost 3 × £48,000 + £12,000 = £156,000; 24 new accounts gives £6,500 each. An account buys £30,000 a year at 22% margin, a contribution of £6,600, so payback is about 0.98 years. Four years gives £26,400. A client worth £540,000 of £1,800,000 sales is 30% of revenue; losing it removes £118,800 of contribution.

Nimbus (invented): £12,000 campaign, 9,600 visits, 3% buy = 288 orders at £24, 40% margin = £9.60 each. Contribution £2,764.80; cost per order £41.67; break-even 1,250 orders. First sales alone lose money, so judge by lifetime value.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Customer | The person or organisation that buys a product |
| Consumer | The person who uses a product |
| B2B | Business to business marketing |
| B2C | Business to consumer marketing |
| Derived demand | Demand that depends on demand for another product |
| Key account management | Dedicated service for a firm's most valuable customers |
| Tendering | Bidding for a contract against competitors |
| Lifetime value | Total contribution a customer is expected to bring |

## Exam traps

- A customer is not always a consumer.
- Both markets use several methods; say which dominates and why.
- Use cost per sale or customer value to judge a method.
- Remember concentration risk for a B2B supplier.
- Finish with a judgement for the business in the case.

## Evaluation prompts

- How many buyers are there, and how much is each worth? Few and valuable points to personal selling; many and small points to mass media.
- What is the cost per sale or per account, and how long does it take to pay back?
- Does the method build a relationship or only a single sale?
- How exposed is the business to one large customer, or to a fall in the final market (derived demand)?
- Could a different mix for each market keep the brand consistent while fitting each buyer?
- Finish with a judgement for the business named in the case.

## Command words

- **Distinguish:** give the difference between customer and consumer, or B2B and B2C, with an example.
- **Evaluate:** weigh a method's strength against its weakness for the buyers in the case, using cost per sale or account value.
- **Calculate:** show cost per account, payback and contribution step by step.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish a customer from a consumer.
2. Give an example in which they are different.
3. Give two features of B2B buying.
4. What is derived demand?
5. Why is personal selling common in B2B markets?
6. Give one weakness of tendering.
7. Give one strength and one weakness of social media advertising.
8. A sales team costs £90,000 and wins 15 accounts. Find the cost per account.
9. An account gives £20,000 sales at a 25% margin. Find the contribution per year.
10. A campaign costs £5,000 and wins 250 orders. Find the cost per order.
11. A product earns £8 contribution per sale. How many sales are needed to cover £5,000 of promotion?
12. Why should a business judge a B2C campaign by lifetime value?

### Answers

1. The customer buys the product; the consumer uses it.
2. A parent buys a toy and a child plays with it.
3. Any two: few buyers; large orders; slower, rational decisions; several people involved; long-term relationships; negotiated prices.
4. Demand for a product that results from demand for another product.
5. Each sale is large and complex, so relationships, answers to technical questions and negotiation are worth the cost.
6. Any one: time-consuming; mainly price-driven; no guarantee of winning.
7. Strength: targeted and can spread widely at low cost. Weakness: results are unpredictable.
8. 90,000 ÷ 15 = **£6,000**.
9. 20,000 × 0.25 = **£5,000**.
10. 5,000 ÷ 250 = **£20**.
11. 5,000 ÷ 8 = **625 sales**.
12. First purchases are small and may not cover the cost; repeat purchases and recommendations create the return.
