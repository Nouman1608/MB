> **Course note:** these notes follow IB DP Psychology for **first assessment 2019**, whose last
> examination session is November 2026. The replacement course is first assessed in May 2027.

Abnormal psychology is one of the four IB DP Psychology options, examined on Paper 2 through 22-mark essays. It asks how abnormality is defined and diagnosed, what explains disorders and how well treatments work; most schools study one disorder in depth -- usually major depressive disorder, used as the example in these notes -- and use the biological, cognitive and sociocultural approaches to explain and treat it.

|---|---|

## Definitions of abnormality

| Approach | Idea | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Statistical infrequency | Behaviour that is rare | Some rare behaviours are desirable |
| Deviation from social norms | Behaviour that breaks norms | Norms vary across cultures and time |
| Failure to function adequately | Distress and inability to cope | Judgement is subjective |
| Deviation from ideal mental health | Absence of features such as self-actualisation (Jahoda, 1958) | Ideals are culture-bound |

Classification systems: **DSM-5** (American Psychiatric Association, 2013) and **ICD-11** (World Health Organization). Major depressive disorder in DSM-5 requires five or more symptoms over at least two weeks, including depressed mood or loss of interest.

## Diagnosis: key studies

| Study | Finding | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Rosenhan (1973) | Eight pseudopatients reporting a single hallucinated voice were admitted to 12 hospitals; most were diagnosed with schizophrenia and discharged "in remission" | Validity of diagnosis; labelling |
| Cooper et al. (1972) | US psychiatrists diagnosed schizophrenia more often, and UK psychiatrists depression, from the same videotaped interviews | Reliability; cultural bias |
| Regier et al. (2013) | DSM-5 field trials found low inter-rater reliability for major depressive disorder (kappa about 0.28) | Reliability of modern diagnosis |

Clinical biases: confirmation bias, cultural bias, gender bias, and **somatisation** in some cultures -- Kleinman (1982) found many Chinese patients diagnosed with neurasthenia met Western criteria for depression but reported physical symptoms.

## Explaining depression

| Approach | Explanation | Research |
|---|---|---|
| Biological | Genetic vulnerability; gene-environment interaction | Kendler et al. (2006), Swedish twins: heritability about 38%, higher in women; Caspi et al. (2003), Dunedin cohort: carriers of the short 5-HTT allele who experienced stressful life events were more likely to become depressed |
| Cognitive | Negative schemas and the cognitive triad (self, world, future) | Beck (1967); Alloy et al. (1999) longitudinal study linking negative cognitive style to later depression |
| Sociocultural | Social vulnerability and stressors | Brown and Harris (1978): women in Camberwell with vulnerability factors (such as lack of an intimate relationship, three or more young children at home) were more likely to become depressed after a severe life event |

**Prevalence:** women are diagnosed with depression roughly twice as often as men in many countries; explanations include biological differences, rumination, social roles and differences in help-seeking or diagnosis.

## Treatment and effectiveness

| Treatment | Evidence | Evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| Antidepressants (SSRIs) | Kirsch et al. (2008): meta-analysis of trial data submitted to the US regulator found small differences from placebo except in severe depression | Publication bias; side effects; relapse after stopping |
| Cognitive behavioural therapy | Elkin et al. (1989), NIMH collaborative study: CBT, interpersonal therapy and imipramine all reduced symptoms; DeRubeis et al. (2005): CBT as effective as medication for moderate to severe depression | Requires motivation; cost; therapist skill |
| Culture in treatment | Culturally adapted therapies and matching of therapist and client language or ethnicity can improve engagement | Studies often small; "culture" hard to define |

Assessing effectiveness: randomised controlled trials, placebo control, double-blind procedures, relapse rates, meta-analysis; the placebo effect and spontaneous remission complicate interpretation.

## Common mistakes / exam traps

- **Discussing "abnormality" in general without a disorder.** Questions on etiology and treatment require a named disorder.
- **Describing Rosenhan without linking to validity.** Explain what the study shows about diagnosis.
- **Treating heritability as "genes cause X%".** Heritability describes variation within a population, not a percentage of one person's disorder.
- **One-sided treatment essays.** Compare biological and psychological treatments and assess effectiveness.
- **Over-generalising cultural findings.** Kleinman's sample was Chinese patients in the 1980s; avoid sweeping claims.

## Frequently asked questions

**How is this option examined?**
On Paper 2: three 22-mark ERQs are set on each option, and you answer one from your option (SL) or one from each of two options (HL).

**Do I need more than one disorder?**
The syllabus requires knowledge of disorders for etiology and treatment; most schools teach one in depth and refer to others briefly. Check with your teacher.

**What earns criterion D (critical thinking)?**
Evaluation of studies and theories -- methods, ethics, cultural limits, alternative explanations -- linked to the question.

## Self-test

1. Name two classification systems. *DSM-5 and ICD-11.*
2. What did Caspi et al. (2003) find? *The short 5-HTT allele increased the risk of depression after stressful life events.*
3. What is the cognitive triad? *Negative views of the self, the world and the future.*
4. What did Kirsch et al. (2008) suggest? *Antidepressants differ little from placebo except in severe depression.*
5. What does inter-rater reliability measure? *Agreement between clinicians diagnosing the same case.*
