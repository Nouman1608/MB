This guide maps **Topic 2 Thermal physics** of Cambridge IGCSE Physics
0625 for the 2026–2028 examination series. Each sub-section below
separates what every candidate must know (Core; a Core-only entry is
eligible for grades C to G) from what is required only at the Extended
tier (Supplement). Extended candidates are eligible for grades A* to G,
so Supplement content is needed for grades A* to B, and Cambridge
advises candidates aiming for grades A* to C to be taught the Extended
content.

## Where this fits in 0625

Thermal physics is the second of the six 0625 topics, sitting between
Motion, forces and energy and Waves. It leans directly on two ideas from
Topic 1: pressure as force per unit area reappears when gas pressure is
explained through particle collisions, and the concept of internal
energy connects back to the energy-stores language introduced with work
and power. It is also the topic where the particle picture of matter —
which returns later in the course when radiation and states of matter
are discussed — is set up properly, so gaps here tend to surface again
in later topics.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE IGCSE PHYSICS 0625 — TOPIC 2 THERMAL PHYSICS**

This list summarises what the syllabus requires; it is not a list of what
this page explains. The sections below, the
[revision notes](/resources/igcse-physics-thermal-physics-revision-notes/)
and the practice sets teach it between them.

- 2.1 Kinetic particle model of matter, in three parts. *States of
matter* (2.1.1, Core) — the distinguishing properties of solids, liquids
and gases, and the terms for changes of state (gas-to-solid and
solid-to-gas transfers are not required). *Particle model* (2.1.2,
Core) — particle arrangement, separation and motion in each state with
simple particle diagrams; the link between particle motion and
temperature, including absolute zero (−273 °C) as the lowest possible
temperature where particles have least kinetic energy; gas pressure
explained through particle collisions with surfaces; and the random
motion of microscopic particles in suspension (Brownian motion) as
evidence for the model. The Supplement adds explaining properties
through the forces and distances between particles, treating pressure as
the force per unit area created by colliding particles, and knowing that
microscopic particles are moved by collisions with light, fast-moving
molecules. *Gases and the absolute scale of temperature* (2.1.3, Core) —
the qualitative effect on the pressure of a fixed mass of gas of
changing temperature at constant volume or changing volume at constant
temperature, and converting between kelvin and degrees Celsius with
T (in K) = θ (in °C) + 273; the Supplement adds pV = constant for a
fixed mass of gas at constant temperature, including its graph.
- 2.2 Thermal properties and temperature, in three parts. *Thermal
expansion* (2.2.1, Core) — qualitative expansion of solids, liquids and
gases at constant pressure, with everyday applications and consequences;
the Supplement explains the relative order of magnitude of expansion of
the three states through particle motion and arrangement. *Specific heat
capacity* (2.2.2) — the Core asks only that a temperature rise increases
an object's internal energy; the Supplement defines specific heat
capacity as energy per unit mass per unit temperature increase, uses
c = ΔE/(mΔθ), links temperature rise to the average kinetic energy of
the particles, and requires experiments to measure the specific heat
capacity of a solid and of a liquid. *Melting, boiling and evaporation*
(2.2.3, Core) — melting and boiling as energy input without temperature
change, the melting and boiling temperatures of water at standard
atmospheric pressure, condensation and solidification in particle
terms, evaporation as the escape of more-energetic particles from a
liquid surface, and the cooling effect of evaporation; the Supplement
adds the differences between boiling and evaporation, how temperature,
surface area and air movement affect evaporation, and explaining the
cooling of an object in contact with an evaporating liquid.
- 2.3 Transfer of thermal energy, in four parts. *Conduction* (2.3.1,
Core) — experiments demonstrating good and bad thermal conductors; the
Supplement explains conduction in all solids through lattice vibrations
plus free-electron movement in metals, explains why gases and most
liquids conduct badly, and notes that many solids conduct better than
insulators but less well than good conductors. *Convection* (2.3.2,
Core) — convection as an important transfer method in liquids and
gases, explained through density changes and shown by experiment.
*Radiation* (2.3.3, Core) — thermal radiation as infrared emitted by
all objects, requiring no medium, and the effect of surface colour
(black or white) and texture (dull or shiny) on emission, absorption
and reflection; the Supplement covers the balance of energy transfer at
constant temperature, what happens when incoming and outgoing rates
differ, how that balance controls the temperature of the Earth,
experiments distinguishing good and bad emitters and absorbers, and how
emission rate depends on surface temperature and surface area.
*Consequences of thermal energy transfer* (2.3.4, Core) — everyday
applications such as heating a kitchen pan and heating a room by
convection; the Supplement examines applications where more than one
transfer type matters at once, such as a wood or coal fire and a car
radiator.

## How to approach it

Almost every mark in this topic is earned by arguing from particles, so
practise writing two-to-three-sentence explanations that name the
arrangement, separation and motion of particles before drawing any
conclusion — examiners reward the mechanism, not the bare fact. Keep the
three transfer processes strictly separated: conduction needs particle
contact, convection needs a fluid whose density can change, radiation
needs nothing at all, and questions frequently describe a situation
(a vacuum flask, a black car roof) and ask which process dominates.
Extended candidates should give the equation work — c = ΔE/(mΔθ) and
pV = constant — the same rehearsal as the Topic 1 equations, and be
ready to describe the specific heat capacity experiments with the
measurements taken and how the result is calculated. Finally, learn the
evaporation–boiling distinctions as a table: where it happens, at what
temperature, and how fast.

## Changes of state in terms of particles

At standard atmospheric pressure, pure water **melts at 0 °C** and
**boils at 100 °C**.

- **Condensation** (gas to liquid): as a gas cools, its particles lose
  kinetic energy and move more slowly; the attractive forces between
  them pull them close together, so they form a liquid. Energy is
  transferred to the surroundings.
- **Solidification** (freezing, liquid to solid): as a liquid cools,
  its particles lose energy until the forces between them hold them in
  fixed positions in a regular arrangement; they can then only vibrate.
  Energy is transferred to the surroundings.
- While either change is happening, the temperature stays constant (for
  pure water at standard atmospheric pressure, steam condenses at 100 °C
  and water freezes at 0 °C) even though energy is being transferred
  away, just as it stays constant during melting and boiling while energy
  is supplied.

## Experiments to describe

**Good and bad thermal conductors (Core).** Fix rods of equal size made
of different materials (for example copper, iron, glass and wood) so one
end of each is heated equally, with a drawing pin stuck to the far end of
each by a blob of wax. The pin on the best conductor falls first.

**Convection (Core).** Drop a crystal of potassium manganate(VII) to the
bottom of a beaker of water at one side and heat gently beneath it. The
purple colour rises above the heated spot, moves across the top and sinks
on the far side, showing the convection current: heated water expands,
becomes less dense and rises, and cooler, denser water sinks to replace
it.

**Specific heat capacity** *(Supplement)*. Put an electric heater and a
thermometer into holes in a metal block of known mass (or into a known
mass of water in an insulated beaker), and wrap the block in insulation
before heating to reduce energy losses. Measure the energy supplied with a
joulemeter, or from E = IVt using an ammeter, a voltmeter and a clock.
Record the temperature rise and calculate c = ΔE/(mΔθ). For example, a 12 V heater taking 4.0 A for
5.0 minutes supplies E = 4.0 × 12 × 300 = 14 400 J; if a 1.0 kg
aluminium block warms by 16 °C, c = 14 400 ÷ (1.0 × 16) =
**900 J/(kg °C)**. Energy lost to the surroundings makes the measured
value higher than the true value.

**Emitters and absorbers of infrared** *(Supplement)*. *Emitters:* fill
a metal cube whose faces are dull black, shiny silver, white and so on
with hot water, and point an infrared detector (or a thermometer with a
blackened bulb) at each face from the same distance. The dull black face
gives the highest reading. *Absorbers:* place a dull black and a shiny
metal plate the same distance from a radiant heater, each with a drawing
pin fixed to its back with wax. The wax on the black plate melts first,
so its pin falls first.

## Radiation balance *(Supplement)*

An object stays at a **constant temperature** when it transfers energy
away at the same rate as it receives energy. If it receives energy
faster than it loses it, its temperature rises; if it loses energy
faster, its temperature falls. A hotter surface, and a larger surface
area, emit radiation at a greater rate. The Earth's temperature depends
on the balance between the radiation it receives from the Sun and the
infrared radiation its surface emits; anything that reduces how much of
the emitted radiation escapes into space (for example more greenhouse
gases in the atmosphere) makes the Earth warm until the rates balance
again.

## Conductors in between and complex applications *(Supplement)*

Thermal conduction is not all-or-nothing: many solids, such as glass,
brick and concrete, conduct thermal energy better than thermal
insulators (such as plastic foam, wool and trapped air) but much less
well than good conductors (metals).

Where more than one transfer process matters:

- **A fire burning wood or coal:** the burning fuel radiates infrared
  directly to people and objects in the room; the hot gases rise by
  convection up the chimney, drawing in fresh air to keep the fire
  burning (and carrying much of the energy away); conduction heats the
  grate and the hearth in contact with the fire.
- **A car radiator:** the coolant is heated by conduction through the
  hot engine walls and carries the energy to the radiator as it flows
  (the flow is pumped); the energy passes by conduction through the thin
  metal tubes and fins, and air moving over the large surface area of the
  fins carries it away by convection. Despite its name, a car radiator
  transfers energy mainly by conduction and convection, not radiation.
