## Section A

**1.** Identify **two** contents of a company's annual report. **[2]**

**2.** Explain **one** reason why a potential lender might find a company's annual report useful, and **one** limitation of it for that purpose. **[4]**

**3.** Explain why an increase in dividends reduces dividend cover. **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** way in which a sale and leaseback of property could affect a company's ratio results. **[3]**

**5.** Explain **two** limitations of using ratio analysis to compare a business with a competitor. **[4]**

## Section B

**6.** Harrowgate Retail plc has profit for the year of 2,400 and pays dividends of 800. It has 16,000 thousand shares and a share price of $1.80.

**(a)** Calculate the dividend per share, dividend cover and dividend yield. **[3]**

**(b)** The directors propose raising dividends to 1,200. Calculate the new dividend cover and the new dividend yield, assuming the share price is unchanged. **[2]**

**(c)** Evaluate the proposal to raise the dividend. **[8]**

**7.** Larkspur Foods plc has profit from operations of 1,800, issued shares of 5,000, reserves of 4,000 and non-current liabilities of 3,000 on which it pays 5% interest. It has 8,000 thousand shares. Tax is 20% of profit after interest.

Larkspur needs 3,000 to build a new factory, which is expected to add 450 a year to profit from operations. It can either borrow at 7% or issue 1,500 thousand new shares at $2.00 each.

**(a)** Calculate Larkspur's current ROCE, gearing and earnings per share. **[4]**

**(b)** Calculate gearing and earnings per share under each option after the expansion. **[6]**

**(c)** Recommend how Larkspur should finance the factory. **[10]**

**8.** Over three years, Brannock Engineering's profit margin fell from 12% to 8%, its current ratio fell from 1.7 to 1.1, and its gearing rose from 20% to 45%. A rival's figures were steady. Assess the impact of these ratio results on Brannock's future strategy. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two: audited financial statements with notes [1]; chair's or directors' statement on performance and strategy [1]; auditor's report; governance information; environmental or wider information.

**2.** Useful: the statement of financial position shows existing borrowing and gearing [1], so the lender can judge whether the company can carry more debt and repay it [1]. Limitation: the figures are historical and may be several months old [1], so the company's position may have worsened since the year end [1].

**3.** Dividend cover is profit for the year divided by the annual dividend [1]; if the dividend rises while profit is unchanged, the profit covers the dividend fewer times [1].

**4.** Selling property raises cash [1], so the current and acid test ratios improve [1]; however, rent becomes a new expense, reducing the profit margin [1]. (Alternatively: if the cash repays loans, gearing falls.)

**5.** Businesses may use different accounting policies, for example on depreciation or inventory valuation [1], so their profit figures are not calculated on the same basis [1]. Year ends may differ, and a seasonal business can look very different at different dates [1], so the comparison may reflect timing rather than performance [1].

**6. (a)** Dividend per share = 800 ÷ 16,000 = **5 cents** [1]. Dividend cover = 2,400 ÷ 800 = **3 times** [1]. Dividend yield = 0.05 ÷ 1.80 × 100 = **2.8%** [1].

**(b)** Cover = 2,400 ÷ 1,200 = **2 times** [1]. Dividend per share 7.5 cents; yield = 0.075 ÷ 1.80 × 100 = **4.2%** [1].

**(c)** For: a higher yield may attract income-seeking investors [1] and could support the share price [1]; cover of 2 times still means profit is twice the dividend [1]. Against: retained profit falls from 1,600 to 1,200 [1], leaving less internal finance for investment, so Harrowgate may need to borrow, raising gearing [1]; cash paid out weakens liquidity [1]. If profit falls, the higher dividend may have to be cut later, which can damage confidence more than not raising it [1]. Judgement: the rise is reasonable only if Harrowgate has no major investment planned and profit is stable; otherwise a smaller rise would balance shareholder income and growth [1]. **[13]**

**7. (a)** Capital employed = 5,000 + 4,000 + 3,000 = 12,000; ROCE = 1,800 ÷ 12,000 × 100 = **15%** [1]. Gearing = 3,000 ÷ 12,000 × 100 = **25%** [1]. Profit for the year = (1,800 − 150) × 0.8 = 1,320 [1]; EPS = 1,320 ÷ 8,000 = **16.5 cents** [1].

**(b)** New profit from operations = 2,250; capital employed = 15,000 under both options. Loan: interest = 150 + 210 = 360 [1]; profit for the year = (2,250 − 360) × 0.8 = 1,512; EPS = 1,512 ÷ 8,000 = **18.9 cents** [1]; gearing = 6,000 ÷ 15,000 = **40%** [1]. Shares: profit for the year = (2,250 − 150) × 0.8 = 1,680 [1]; EPS = 1,680 ÷ 9,500 = **17.7 cents** [1]; gearing = 3,000 ÷ 15,000 = **20%** [1].

**(c)** For the loan: EPS rises to 18.9 cents against 17.7 cents [1], because the factory earns 15% (450 ÷ 3,000) while the loan costs 7% [1], and existing shareholders keep control [1]. Against the loan: gearing rises from 25% to 40% [1], and interest rises to 360 a year, a fixed cost even if food demand or margins fall [1]. For shares: gearing falls to 20%, leaving room to borrow for future projects [1], and there is no extra fixed interest [1]. Against shares: EPS is diluted and control is shared with new investors [1]. Judgement: borrowing is better if Larkspur's profits are stable, as food demand often is, and interest rates are not expected to rise [1]; if the factory's returns are uncertain, the lower risk of a share issue is worth the smaller EPS [1]. **[20]**

**8.** The falling profit margin suggests costs are rising faster than prices or that Brannock has cut prices [1], so strategy should focus on controlling costs or improving value before expanding [1]. The current ratio of 1.1 shows weakening liquidity [1], so Brannock may need to improve working capital, for example through credit control or reducing inventory [1]. Gearing of 45% means heavy interest commitments [1], so further debt-financed growth would be risky; a share issue or retained profit may be needed [1]. As the rival is steady, the problems are specific to Brannock, not the industry [1]. However, ratios do not explain causes [1]; the decline could reflect a deliberate investment that will pay off later. Judgement: the ratios point to a strategy of consolidation, rebuilding margins and liquidity before further growth [1], provided investigation confirms the causes are not temporary [1]. **[10]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Forgetting existing interest when calculating profit for the year after a loan.
- Using the old number of shares for EPS after a share issue.
- Saying ROCE differs between debt and equity finance.
- Treating a higher dividend as good for every stakeholder.
- Drawing strategic conclusions from ratios without noting that they do not explain causes.
