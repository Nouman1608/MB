This guide goes deeper on one section of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Biology (9610) specification, Version 5.1: **3.1.4 Transport into and out of cells**, in Unit 1, The diversity of living organisms. Unit 1 is part of the International AS and counts towards the full International A-level.

The section answers one question in three ways: how do substances cross the plasma membrane? Some cross without energy from the cell (diffusion, facilitated diffusion and osmosis). Others are pushed against their gradient using ATP (active transport). The skill the specification asks for is to relate the structure and properties of exchange surfaces to their function. Test yourself afterwards with the [transport practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-biology-9610-transport-into-and-out-of-cells-practice/).

## Plasma membranes

The **fluid-mosaic model** describes a membrane as a bilayer of phospholipids in which proteins and other molecules are scattered like tiles in a mosaic.

- **Phospholipids.** The hydrophilic phosphate heads face the water on both sides and the hydrophobic fatty acid tails face inwards. The bilayer is a barrier to water-soluble substances.
- **Proteins.** Some span the membrane (channels and carriers); others sit on one surface. They transport substances, act as receptors or are enzymes.
- **Carbohydrates.** Short chains attached to proteins (glycoproteins) or lipids (glycolipids) lie on the outside surface and are involved in cell recognition.
- **Cholesterol.** Present in the membrane, it restricts the movement of the other molecules in the membrane. It makes the membrane less fluid and so more stable.

The model is "fluid" because the phospholipids and many proteins can move sideways within their own layer.

**Microvilli** are tiny finger-like extensions of the cell-surface membrane. They increase the surface area of the membrane, so more transport proteins can be held there and more of the cell's surface is in contact with the contents of the gut.

**Worked example 1: microvilli and surface area.** An epithelial cell has a flat surface of 8 µm x 8 µm. Assume 200 microvilli, each a cylinder 0.1 µm in diameter and 1.0 µm long.

1. Area of flat surface = 8 x 8 = 64 µm2.
2. Side area of one microvillus = pi x diameter x length = 3.142 x 0.1 x 1.0 = 0.314 µm2. The tip replaces the base area it covers, so it adds nothing.
3. Added area for 200 microvilli = 200 x 0.314 = 62.8 µm2.
4. Total area = 64 + 62.8 = 126.8 µm2, almost double the flat area.

## Diffusion

**Diffusion** is the passive movement of molecules or ions from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, down a concentration gradient. It needs no energy from the cell, because the molecules move by their own kinetic energy.

The phospholipid bilayer limits diffusion. Small, non-polar molecules such as oxygen and carbon dioxide, and lipid-soluble molecules, dissolve in the hydrophobic centre and cross easily. Ions and polar molecules such as glucose cannot cross the hydrophobic core unaided. Large molecules cross slowly or not at all.

### Factors affecting the rate

The rate of diffusion across an exchange surface increases with:

- a larger **surface area**;
- a greater **concentration difference** across the surface;
- a thinner **exchange surface**, because the diffusion distance is shorter.

These three are combined in the idea that rate is proportional to (surface area x concentration difference) / thickness.

**Worked example 2.** Exchange surface A has an area of 20 units, a concentration difference of 6 units and a thickness of 2 units. Surface B has an area of 30 units, the same concentration difference and a thickness of 1 unit. Compare their rates.

1. Rate of A is proportional to 20 x 6 / 2 = 60.
2. Rate of B is proportional to 30 x 6 / 1 = 180.
3. B diffuses 180 / 60 = 3 times faster.

### Facilitated diffusion

**Facilitated diffusion** also moves substances down a concentration gradient without using ATP, but it uses proteins in the membrane. These can act as **channels**, which have a pore of a particular size and charge that suits a specific ion, or as **carriers**, which bind a specific molecule and change shape to release it on the other side. Facilitated diffusion allows ions and polar molecules, which cannot cross the bilayer, to cross. The rate is limited by the number of proteins, so it levels off when all are in use.

## Osmosis

**Osmosis** is a special case of diffusion in which water moves from a solution of higher water potential to one of lower water potential through a partially permeable membrane.

**Water potential** is a measure of the tendency of water molecules to move. Pure water has the highest water potential, which is 0 kPa. Adding solute lowers the water potential, so solutions have negative values. A solution at -600 kPa has a lower water potential than one at -300 kPa.

For an animal cell in a solution of higher water potential than the cytoplasm, water enters and the cell may burst. In a solution of lower water potential, water leaves and the cell shrinks. A plant cell has a cell wall. When water enters, the vacuole swells and pushes the membrane against the wall, making the cell **turgid**. The wall prevents bursting. In a solution of lower water potential, water leaves and the membrane pulls away from the wall. The cell is **plasmolysed**.

## Active transport

**Active transport** moves substances **against** a concentration gradient, from lower to higher concentration. It uses **carrier proteins** and energy from **ATP**.

1. The substance binds to a specific carrier protein.
2. ATP binds to the carrier protein and is hydrolysed to ADP and phosphate.
3. The energy released changes the shape of the carrier.
4. The substance is released on the other side of the membrane.

ATP can be made from ADP and phosphate, and it provides the immediate source of energy for biological processes. Because active transport needs ATP, cells that carry out a lot of it contain many mitochondria, and the process slows if respiration stops or is inhibited.

| Feature | Diffusion | Facilitated diffusion | Active transport |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direction | Down the gradient | Down the gradient | Against the gradient |
| ATP needed | No | No | Yes |
| Membrane proteins | No | Channels or carriers | Carriers |
| Saturates | No | Yes | Yes |

## Exchange surfaces

The specification asks you to relate the structure of exchange surfaces to their function. Look for these features in any question:

- large surface area (folds, microvilli, flattened cells);
- thin barrier (a single layer of cells);
- a steep gradient maintained (a blood supply that carries substances away);
- transport proteins and many mitochondria if active transport is involved.

## Required practical 2: solute concentration and plant tissue

**Aim:** find how the concentration of a solution affects the uptake or loss of water from plant tissue.

1. Cut identical cylinders of potato tissue with a cork borer, and blot and weigh each one.
2. Place each in a different concentration of sucrose solution, from 0.0 to 0.8 mol dm-3.
3. Leave for the same time at the same temperature, then blot and reweigh.
4. Calculate the percentage change in mass: (final mass - initial mass) / initial mass x 100.
5. Plot percentage change in mass against concentration. The concentration at which the line crosses zero is where the tissue has the same water potential as the solution.

**Worked example 3.** Cylinders of 2.40 g began in 0.0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6 and 0.8 mol dm-3 sucrose solutions and ended at 2.76, 2.58, 2.42, 2.28 and 2.13 g.

1. Percentage changes: 15.0, 7.5, 0.8, -5.0 and -11.3 per cent.
2. The change is positive at low concentrations because the tissue has the lower water potential, and water enters by osmosis.
3. The line crosses zero between 0.4 and 0.6. Interpolating between +0.8 and -5.0 gives 0.4 + 0.2 x (0.8 / 5.8) = 0.43 mol dm-3.
4. At this concentration, the water potential of the solution equals that of the tissue.

Percentage change is used because the starting masses differ slightly, so raw masses cannot be compared fairly.

## Common mistakes

- Saying water moves "to where there is less water" instead of from higher to lower water potential.
- Writing that active transport "uses energy" without naming ATP and the carrier protein.
- Saying that facilitated diffusion is active.
- Forgetting that cholesterol restricts movement of other molecules in the membrane.
- Using mass change (grams) rather than percentage change to compare tubes.
- Describing the phospholipid bilayer as "a barrier to everything".

## Exam technique

- **Name the membrane component.** If asked how a substance crosses, say whether it passes through the bilayer, a channel or a carrier.
- **Use water potential vocabulary.** Always say higher or lower water potential.
- **For a surface area question, show the maths.** State the formula and the units.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is osmosis always a diffusion of water through the bilayer?**
Water is a small polar molecule, and it crosses the bilayer slowly. The specification treats osmosis as diffusion of water through a partially permeable membrane, so you do not need to describe the route in more detail.

**Why does active transport need many mitochondria?**
Mitochondria make most of the ATP. A cell that does a lot of active transport needs a large, continuous supply.
