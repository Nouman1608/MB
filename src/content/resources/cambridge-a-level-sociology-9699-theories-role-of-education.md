Section 5.1 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699) asks one big question: what is education *for*? The syllabus names five strands: functionalist views of how education builds value consensus and social solidarity; education and role allocation; Marxist views of how education maintains the capitalist economic system; education as an instrument of ideological control and cultural reproduction; and New Right and social democratic views of the relationship between education and the economy. The section links to the key concept of Power, control and resistance, and it is examined on Paper 3 – Education.

This page goes deeper than the [education, society and the curriculum overview](/resources/a-level-sociology-9699-education-society-and-the-curriculum/), which introduces Durkheim, Parsons, Althusser, Bowles and Gintis and Chubb and Moe. Here the focus is on how the theories argue, where they collide, and how to use them in each Paper 3 question type.

## The question each theory is answering

Every theory in 5.1 takes a position on two issues. First, **whose interests does education serve?** Society as a whole (functionalism), the capitalist class (Marxism), consumers and the economy (New Right), or the whole population through fairer chances (social democrats). Second, **how far do pupils shape their own schooling?** Functionalism and classical Marxism are structural theories that treat pupils as largely shaped by the system; neo-Marxists and interactionists give more weight to agency. Keeping these two axes in mind lets you turn a list of theorists into an argument.

| Theory | Whose interests? | Main mechanism | Typical critics |
|---|---|---|---|
| Functionalist | Society as a whole | Shared values, specialist skills, sifting by merit | Marxists, Hargreaves, Tumin |
| Marxist | Capitalist class | Ideology, correspondence, cultural reproduction | Functionalists, neo-Marxists, postmodernists |
| New Right | Economy and parents as consumers | Competition, choice, standards | Social democrats, Marxists |
| Social democratic | Everyone, through equal opportunity | State investment, comprehensive provision | New Right, Marxists |

## Functionalism: consensus and solidarity

Durkheim's lectures, published after his death as *Moral Education* (1925), argued that schools must teach a morality that no longer comes from religion alone: discipline, attachment to social groups and self-control. Punishment at school, for Durkheim, should make pupils understand why a rule matters to the group, not simply make them afraid.

Talcott Parsons, in "The School Class as a Social System" (1959), described the school class as the main agency that moves a child from family values to the values of wider society, especially **achievement** and **equality of opportunity**. Pupils learn that they are judged on what they do, and that the race is fair. This is how education produces value consensus in a modern society: by teaching everyone to accept the same rules for winning and losing.

**Evaluating the consensus claim**

- **David Hargreaves**, in *The Challenge for the Comprehensive School* (1982), argued that secondary schooling teaches individual competition rather than solidarity, and that its academic focus strips many working-class pupils of dignity. If schools rank pupils against each other constantly, it is hard to see how they build a shared sense of belonging.
- **Dennis Wrong**'s critique of the "over-socialised conception of man" (1961) applies here: functionalism assumes pupils absorb values passively, which ethnographies of anti-school subcultures contradict.
- In culturally diverse societies, the question becomes *whose* values form the consensus. This links to the ethnocentric curriculum in 5.3.

## Role allocation

Role allocation means education sifting and sorting people into the occupations that fit their abilities. The argument has three steps: some jobs matter more to society; they need scarce talent and long training; so qualifications and higher rewards channel the most able into them. Davis and Moore (1945) set out the case; Melvin Tumin's reply (1953) argued that "importance" cannot be measured objectively and that unequal rewards let privileged families pass on advantage, which blocks the free flow of talent.

A useful application: if role allocation worked as functionalists say, attainment would be explained by ability and effort alone. Evidence in [social class and educational attainment](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-social-class-educational-attainment/) shows class background predicting results even among pupils of similar measured ability, which weakens the claim.

## Marxism: maintaining the capitalist economic system

Marxists argue that education does three jobs for capitalism:

1. **Reproduces labour power** — produces workers with the skills and attitudes employers need, at different levels for different classes.
2. **Legitimates inequality** — the belief that rewards follow merit makes class inequality look fair, so those who fail blame themselves.
3. **Transmits ideology** — passes on ideas that support the system, such as individualism and acceptance of hierarchy.

Bowles and Gintis captured the first two with the idea that schooling lies in "the long shadow of work": its organisation, rewards and hierarchy are modelled on the workplace. Their claim about IQ is often overlooked: they argued that the link between family background and later income cannot be explained by measured intelligence, which supports the view that schools reward class-related attitudes rather than ability.

## Ideological control and cultural reproduction

Althusser's ideological state apparatus explains *control*; Bourdieu explains *reproduction*. In *Reproduction in Education, Society and Culture* (with Passeron; French 1970, English 1977), Bourdieu described **symbolic violence**: the imposition of the dominant class's culture as if it were simply "good taste" or "intelligence". Working-class pupils are not forced out of education; they are made to feel that academic culture is not for them, and many withdraw by choice. The violence is symbolic because it works through meaning, not force.

**Neo-Marxist challenge.** Henry Giroux (*Theory and Resistance in Education*, 1983) argued that schools are sites of struggle, where pupils and teachers contest dominant ideas. Resistance can reproduce inequality, as Willis showed, but it can also open room for change. This lets you evaluate Marxism from inside the conflict tradition: Marxists may be right about the system's aims but wrong about how smoothly it achieves them.

## New Right and social democratic views on education and the economy

Both views tie education to the economy; they disagree about who should run it.

**Social democrats** see education as a public investment. Theodore Schultz's address "Investment in Human Capital" (1961) argued that spending on skills raises productivity in the same way as spending on machines. Fairness and growth support each other: if talent from every class is developed, the economy gains. Hence support for comprehensive schools, free state education and expansion of higher education.

**The New Right** accepts that education should serve the economy but argues that the state does this badly. Milton Friedman's essay "The Role of Government in Education" (1955) proposed vouchers so that parents, not officials, would choose schools. New Right thinkers also back **vocationalism**: training that matches employers' needs.

**Critiques of each**

- Of social democrats: expansion has not closed class gaps, and Wolf questions the automatic link between spending and growth.
- Of the New Right: Phillip Brown (1990) described a shift to **parentocracy**, where a child's education depends on the wealth and wishes of parents rather than ability and effort. Marxists add that vocational schemes can train young people in attitudes for low-paid work; Dan Finn (1987) argued that British youth training schemes supplied cheap labour and lowered unemployment figures rather than providing real skills.

## Worked example: Question 3 style

*Quotation:* "Education exists mainly to meet the needs of the economy." Give two arguments against this view.

> **Argument 1.** Functionalists argue that education's first role is moral, not economic. Durkheim saw schools as teaching discipline and attachment to the group, so that individuals feel part of society. A school assembly that marks national or community events builds belonging but adds nothing directly to output. This suggests education meets social needs that the quotation ignores, although Marxists would reply that "shared values" are themselves ruling-class ideas.
>
> **Argument 2.** Pupils do not simply turn into the workers the economy wants. Willis's lads rejected school, and Giroux argues schools are sites of struggle. If education were efficiently geared to economic need, resistance and failure on this scale would be hard to explain. The view therefore overstates how far schooling is controlled by economic demand.

Each argument states a counter-position, supports it with sociological material, applies it, and evaluates. That pattern matches the AO2 and AO3 skills the syllabus attaches to Question 3.

## Worked example: essay plan

*"Evaluate the functionalist view of the role of education."*

1. Define value consensus, solidarity and role allocation; outline Durkheim and Parsons.
2. Support: shared curricula and citizenship teaching; exams as open competition.
3. Critique from Marxism: consensus as ideology; Bowles and Gintis on legitimation.
4. Critique from within: Hargreaves on competition; Tumin on role allocation.
5. Interactionist evidence: labelling and subcultures show schools can create failure.
6. Judgement: functionalism describes what schools *claim* to do; conflict theories better explain persistent inequality, but functionalism still explains why societies invest in mass schooling.

## Common mistakes

- Writing "Marxists think school is bad". Marxists argue it serves capitalism very effectively.
- Treating social democrats and the New Right as opposites on everything: both link education closely to the economy.
- Forgetting that Bourdieu explains reproduction through culture, not money.
- Ending with "both theories have strengths" instead of a reasoned judgement.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Describe**: state the main features of a theory; no evaluation is needed.
- **Explain**: give the reason and link it to evidence. In Question 2, develop each explanation separately.
- **Give** (arguments against): every point must oppose the quotation; agreeing earns nothing.
- **Evaluate**: weigh strengths and limitations and reach a judgement that follows from your evidence.
