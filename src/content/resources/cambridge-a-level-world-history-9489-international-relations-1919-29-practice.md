## Section B

**8.** Explain why Italy was dissatisfied with the peace settlement of 1919-20. **[10]**

**9.** "The absence of the USA was the main weakness of the League of Nations in the 1920s." How far do you agree? **[20]**

**10.** Read the two invented sources below. Compare Sources A and B as evidence about the occupation of the Ruhr.

*Source A (invented)* -- From a statement by the French government, January 1923. "Germany has wilfully failed to deliver the coal and timber she owes. France has waited four years for justice. Our engineers enter the Ruhr not as conquerors but as creditors, to collect what is due. When Germany pays, they will leave."

*Source B (invented)* -- From a German trade-union leaflet distributed in Essen, January 1923. "French bayonets now stand at our pit-heads. They say they have come for coal; they have come to break Germany. Not a wheel shall turn for the invader. Workers, do not dig one ton for France." **[15]**

**11.** Read the two further invented sources below, together with Sources A and B in Question 10. How far do Sources A to D support the view that French policy was the main cause of international tension in 1919-23?

*Source C (invented)* -- From a British newspaper editorial, February 1923. "France has chosen force where patience was needed. By occupying the Ruhr she has destroyed the German currency and with it any chance that Germany will pay. Britain did not fight a war to make France master of the Continent."

*Source D (invented)* -- From a speech by an Italian nationalist politician, 1923. "Fiume is Italian, Corfu has learned respect, and the Adriatic will be ours. The peace of 1919 robbed Italy of what she had won with her blood. We will not wait for the League in Geneva to give it back." **[25]**

**12.** Explain why Germany joined the League of Nations in 1926. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Any three, 1 mark each: Alsace-Lorraine returned to France (1); West Prussia and Posen to Poland, creating the Polish Corridor (1); Danzig made a Free City under the League (1); the Saar placed under League administration for fifteen years (1); Eupen-Malmédy to Belgium (1); northern Schleswig to Denmark after a plebiscite (1); Memel placed under Allied control (1); German colonies made mandates (1). **[3]**

**2.** Any two, 1 mark each: the defeated powers were excluded from negotiations and presented with terms (1); Soviet Russia was not invited (1); decisions were taken by a small group of great powers, leaving smaller allies such as Belgium or the new states little influence (1); colonial peoples' delegations were largely ignored (1). **[2]**

**3.** Any three, 1 mark each: Japan gained Germany's rights in Shandong, which it wanted (1); it received German Pacific islands north of the equator as mandates (1); it was recognised as one of the great powers with a permanent Council seat (1); but its proposed racial equality clause was rejected, which was seen as a humiliation (1); its gains in Shandong were challenged by China and the USA (1). **[3]**

**4.** Any three, 1 mark each: the Assembly of all members, meeting annually (1); the Council of permanent and elected members, meeting more often to handle disputes (1); the Secretariat in Geneva, the League's civil service (1); the Permanent Court of International Justice at The Hague (1); specialist agencies and commissions such as the ILO and the Mandates Commission (1). **[3]**

**5.** Any two, 1 mark each: the Refugees Commission under Nansen returned hundreds of thousands of prisoners of war and issued Nansen passports to stateless people (1); the ILO set standards for working hours and conditions and collected information (1); the Slavery Commission's work led to the Slavery Convention of 1926 (1); health work by the League's health organisation against epidemics such as typhus (1). **[2]**

**6.** 1 mark each: it replaced the Treaty of Sèvres, which Turkish nationalists under Mustafa Kemal had refused to accept (1); Turkey regained eastern Thrace and Smyrna and paid no reparations (1); it showed that a treaty could be revised by force, a precedent for other dissatisfied powers (1). **[3]**

**7.** Two limitations, 2 marks each: it contained no method of enforcement or sanctions against a state that broke it (2); signatories kept the right to fight in self-defence, which they could define for themselves (2); the USA, a sponsor, remained outside the League and would not commit itself to collective action (2). **[4]**

**8.** Indicative content. **Broken promises:** the secret Treaty of London (1915) had promised Italy territory including Dalmatia; at Paris Wilson opposed giving Italy lands inhabited by South Slavs. **Fiume:** the port was not awarded to Italy; Orlando left the conference in protest and D'Annunzio seized the city in 1919. **Colonies:** Italy received no German colonies or Ottoman mandates. **Cost of the war:** heavy casualties and debts made the gains -- South Tyrol, Trentino, Trieste and Istria -- seem inadequate; nationalists called it a "mutilated victory". **Domestic politics:** economic crisis, unrest and weak governments allowed nationalists, including Mussolini, to exploit the grievance. **Linking:** the gap between the promises of 1915 and the outcome of 1919 created the grievance; domestic weakness turned it into a political force. **[10]**

**9.** Indicative content. **For:** without the USA the League lacked the world's largest economy, so economic sanctions could be evaded; Wilson's absence left leadership to Britain and France; the League looked like a club of the victors. **Against:** other membership gaps mattered -- Germany until 1926 and the USSR throughout; Britain and France disagreed over enforcement and often bypassed the League (the Conference of Ambassadors over Corfu and Teschen); unanimity made action slow; there was no army, so enforcement depended on members willing to act. Evidence from disputes: the League succeeded with small powers (Aaland, Greco-Bulgarian) and failed with great powers or their allies (Corfu, Vilna) -- a weakness of will more than of membership. **Judgement:** strong answers argue, for example, that US absence weakened sanctions, but the reluctance of Britain and France to act against an ally or great power was the more fundamental weakness in the 1920s. **[20]**

**10.** Indicative content. **Similarities:** both agree the occupation is about coal and reparations deliveries; both present it as a struggle between France and Germany. **Differences:** A says France acts as a lawful creditor and will leave when paid; B says the aim is "to break Germany" and calls for workers to refuse to dig. A presents Germany as wilfully defaulting; B presents France as an invader. **Explaining:** A is official French justification for international and domestic audiences, stressing legality; B is a German trade-union appeal in the occupied city, organising the passive resistance that the government supported. Knowledge: the Reparations Commission had declared Germany in default; passive resistance followed, and the government's printing of money to support strikers fed hyperinflation. **[15]**

**11.** Indicative content. **Support:** B and C portray France as aggressive; C blames the Ruhr occupation for destroying the German currency; A shows France acting alone. Knowledge: France insisted on strict enforcement of Versailles, sought alliances in eastern Europe and occupied Frankfurt briefly in 1920 as well as the Ruhr in 1923. **Challenge:** D shows Italian nationalism (Fiume, Corfu) as a separate source of tension; A argues German default caused the crisis. Knowledge: the treaties themselves created grievances; the successor states quarrelled over borders (Teschen, Vilna); Lithuania seized Memel; Soviet Russia and Germany drew together at Rapallo; Turkey overturned Sèvres by war. **Provenance:** C is British opinion, critical of France as Anglo-French views diverged; D is a nationalist speech boasting of success. **Judgement:** the sources show French policy as the main cause of tension in western Europe, but D and wider knowledge show that tension elsewhere had other causes -- above all the settlements themselves. **[25]**

**12.** Indicative content. **Stresemann's strategy:** fulfilment meant working within the Versailles system to win revision; membership gave Germany a voice in decisions on reparations, the Saar and minorities. **Locarno:** the 1925 treaties made entry a condition of the settlement, and Germany's voluntary acceptance of its western borders made the Allies willing to admit it. **Equal status:** Germany insisted on, and received, a permanent Council seat, recognising it again as a great power. **Economic recovery:** after the Dawes Plan Germany needed international confidence to keep attracting loans. **Allied motives:** Britain in particular wanted Germany inside the system to stabilise Europe. **Linking:** Stresemann's aim of peaceful revision explains why Germany wanted to join; Locarno explains why it happened in 1926. **[10]**
