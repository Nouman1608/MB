## Questions

**1.** Define sustainable development. **[2]**

**2.** (calculator-free) Harrow Sound is an invented fishing ground. Its fish stock is 150,000 tonnes and regenerates by 8% of the stock each year. Fishers catch 15,000 tonnes a year.

**(a)** Calculate the yearly regeneration of the stock. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the stock at the end of the first year. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why continuing to catch 15,000 tonnes a year is unsustainable. **[2]**

**3.** (calculator-free) A fictional country holds 900 million tonnes of an ore.

**(a)** It extracts 18 million tonnes a year. Calculate how many years the reserves last. **[1]**
**(b)** Instead, extraction starts at 18 million tonnes and rises by 2 million tonnes every year (18, 20, 22 and so on). Show that the ore is used up during the 23rd year. **[3]**

**4.** A fictional country opens free secondary schools for girls in rural districts. Identify two Sustainable Development Goals this supports and explain the link in each case. **[4]**

**5.** Explain why a fishing ground that nobody owns is likely to be fished beyond a sustainable level. **[4]**

**6.** Explain two constraints that a low-income country may face in pursuing sustainable development. **[6]**

**7.** A fictional country dredges a coral reef to build a deep-water port that will increase exports. Explain how this may involve a conflict between Sustainable Development Goals. **[4]**

**8.** Analyse why economic growth that depends on exhausting a natural resource may not amount to sustainable development. **[8]**

**9.** (**HL only**) Explain two ways in which environmental degradation can deepen poverty. **[6]**

**10.** (**HL only**) (calculator-free) Dunvale is an invented village of 500 households. Each household burns 1.8 tonnes of wood a year. The nearby woodland holds 5,400 tonnes and regrows 720 tonnes a year.

**(a)** Calculate the yearly demand for wood and the yearly shortfall against regrowth. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate how long the woodland lasts if nothing changes, ignoring any fall in regrowth. **[1]**
**(c)** Find how many households must switch to a non-wood fuel to remove the shortfall. **[2]**
**(d)** A scheme pays 120 sols per switching household. Calculate the total cost. **[1]**

**11.** (**HL only**) Analyse the two-way relationship between poverty and the sustainable use of resources in a low-income farming region. **[8]**

**12.** Veltmark is a fictional country with 600,000 hectares of forest. The forest regrows by 3% of its area each year. Logging for timber export clears 30,000 hectares a year and employs many low-paid rural workers.

**(a)** Calculate the yearly regrowth. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate how many years the forest lasts at the current rate, ignoring any fall in regrowth. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage cut in logging needed to make it sustainable. **[2]**
**(d)** Apply the Sustainable Development Goals to the logging, giving at least one that it supports and one that it works against. **[3]**
**(e)** Suggest one policy that could move Veltmark towards sustainability and explain one constraint on it. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Sustainable development means raising living standards and meeting present needs [1] while leaving later generations able to meet theirs, which means limiting resource depletion and degradation [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Advice: include both the present and the future in a definition.

**2. (a)** 8% × 150,000 = **12,000 tonnes** [1].
**(b)** Stock = 150,000 + 12,000 − 15,000 [1] = **147,000 tonnes** [1].
**(c)** The catch exceeds regeneration by 3,000 tonnes a year, so the stock falls [1]. A smaller stock regenerates less (8% of 147,000 is 11,760 tonnes), so the gap widens each year [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Advice: set the catch against regeneration, then say what happens to the stock over time.

**3. (a)** 900 ÷ 18 = **50 years** [1].
**(b)** Extraction after n years totals 18n + n(n − 1), since the increases of 2 add up to 2 × (0 + 1 + ... + (n − 1)) [1]. After 22 years: 396 + 462 = 858, which is below 900 [1]. After 23 years: 414 + 506 = 920, which is above 900, so the ore runs out in the **23rd year**, 27 years earlier than the constant-rate figure [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Advice: for a "show that" answer, display both checks (year 22 below the total, year 23 above it) so the conclusion follows from your working.

**4.** Goal 4, quality education: free schooling raises girls' attendance and skills [1], which supports learning and later earning [1]. Goal 5, gender equality: girls gain access to education on the same terms as boys [1], which narrows the gap in opportunity between the sexes [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Advice: name the goal by number and title, then explain the link to this case; a list of goals without links tends to earn little.

**5.** Nobody owns the fishing ground, so no one can stop others from fishing [1]. Each fisher gains the full value of an extra fish but shares the cost of a smaller stock with everyone [1]. The private cost is therefore below the social cost, so the total catch rises above the regeneration rate [1]. The stock falls, which lowers future catches and the incomes of all fishers [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Advice: show the gap between private and social cost, not only sharing.

**6.** Constraint one, limited income: clean technology, conservation and monitoring need funds [1]; basic needs such as food and health compete for the same funds [1], so environmental spending may be delayed [1]. Constraint two, short time horizons: the costs of protecting a resource arrive now while the benefits come later [1]; urgent needs favour present income [1], so harmful use continues [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Advice: for each constraint give the cause, the way it blocks action, and the result in that order.

**7.** The port supports goal 9 (infrastructure) and goal 8 (growth and jobs through exports) [1]. Dredging the reef damages goal 14 (life below water) [1]. The goals conflict because the same project advances one aim while harming another [1]. The reef may support fishing and tourism, so the long-run loss may outweigh the export gain [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Advice: name both sides of the conflict and say who gains and loses, now and later.

**8.** Growth measured by output can rise while the resource stock falls [1]. Income earned today then comes at the cost of lower income later, because the stock cannot be used again [1]. The market price may not include the cost of depletion, so output and profit look larger than the true gain [1]. If the resource is non-renewable or used faster than it regenerates, the income stream ends or falls [1]. The jobs created are not secure, so living standards fall when the resource runs out [1]. The gains may go to a few owners, so poverty falls little [1]. Growth can be sustainable if the income is reinvested in other assets such as education, infrastructure or alternative industries, which replace what is lost [1]. So the result depends on whether use is within regeneration and what the income is used for [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Advice: tie your points to the generational definition and end with a short conditional judgement.

**9. (HL only)** Way one, lower incomes: degradation such as soil exhaustion or a falling fish stock reduces output from the resource [1], which cuts the income of households that depend on it [1], so more fall below the poverty line [1]. Way two, health and vulnerability: polluted water or smoky fuel raises illness [1], which reduces the ability to work and learn and raises health costs [1], so the household is poorer and less able to recover [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Advice: finish each mechanism on its effect on poverty.

**10. (HL only) (a)** Demand = 500 × 1.8 = **900 tonnes** [1]. Shortfall = 900 − 720 = **180 tonnes** [1].
**(b)** 5,400 ÷ 180 = **30 years** [1].
**(c)** Demand must fall to 720 tonnes, which is 720 ÷ 1.8 = 400 households using wood [1], so 500 − 400 = **100 households** must switch [1].
**(d)** 100 × 120 = **12,000 sols** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Advice: show the shortfall before using it in later parts, so an early slip does not undermine everything that follows.

**11. (HL only)** Poverty to resource pressure: households with low and uncertain income value present needs far above the future, so they cut trees, graze or fish heavily [1]. They depend directly on local resources and lack affordable alternatives such as credit or clean energy [1]. Fuelwood use above regrowth shrinks the woodland [1]. Resource pressure to poverty: lower yields, a smaller woodland or soil loss reduce incomes [1], and the poor are most exposed to drought, flood and pollution and least able to recover [1]. The two links form a vicious circle in which poverty and degradation reinforce each other [1]. Policy can address both at once, for example subsidised clean energy or secure credit to invest in soil care [1]. A limit is cost and whether households can sustain the change after support ends [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Advice: show both directions and say how they connect; an essay about only one direction covers half of the relationship.

**12. (a)** 3% × 600,000 = **18,000 hectares** [1].
**(b)** Net loss = 30,000 − 18,000 = 12,000 hectares a year [1]. Forest lasts 600,000 ÷ 12,000 = **50 years** [1].
**(c)** Logging must fall to 18,000 hectares, a cut of 12,000 [1], which is 12,000 ÷ 30,000 = **40%** [1].
**(d)** Supports goal 8 (employment and export growth) [1] and possibly goal 1 (higher incomes for rural workers) [1]. Works against goal 15 (life on land), and goal 13 if the forest stored carbon [1].
**(e)** Policy: a logging quota set at regrowth, or a tax on timber that includes the cost of lost forest [1], backed by replanting or support for other rural jobs [1]. Constraint: the workers are low-paid and depend on logging, so a quota cuts their income unless alternatives exist [1], and the government may lack the funds or enforcement to monitor the forest [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* Advice: in an "apply" part use the case facts (jobs, hectares); in "suggest" give a policy and a reasoned limit, not just a policy name.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining sustainable development without the idea of future generations.
- Reciting goal names with no link to the case in front of you.
- Calling a resource use unsustainable without comparing it with regeneration.
- Missing the fall in regeneration as a stock shrinks.
- Leaving out working in short calculations, or leaving out units.
- Treating the poverty link (HL) as one-directional.
- Writing constraints as bare labels, with no explanation of how they limit action.
