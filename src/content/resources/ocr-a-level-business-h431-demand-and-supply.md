This guide goes deeper into one content area of **External influences** in the OCR A Level Business (H431) specification, Version 2.1 (January 2026), final assessment summer 2027: **demand and supply** (2.2 on this site; OCR does not number its content areas). The broader [market, market size and demand and supply guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-the-market-market-size-and-growth-and-demand-and-supply/) introduces the curves; this page concentrates on reading markets the way a business decision-maker must. Companions: [demand and supply revision notes](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-h431-demand-and-supply-revision-notes/) and [demand and supply practice questions](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-h431-demand-and-supply-practice/).

## What the specification asks

The specification lists eight things you should be able to do:

- explain what is meant by demand, supply and equilibrium
- explain how demand and supply interact to establish equilibrium
- explain the determinants of demand and supply
- explain how changes in the determinants affect price and output decisions
- construct and interpret diagrams showing how those changes affect equilibrium
- evaluate factors which affect demand and supply in a market
- construct and interpret diagrams showing excesses and shortages
- evaluate the effect of excesses and shortages in a market

Notice the business angle: how a market change alters the **price and output decisions** a firm takes.

## Demand: wanting is not enough

Demand counts only buyers who are **willing and able** to pay. A student who would love a new laptop but cannot afford one adds nothing to demand. This is why income matters so much: it turns wants into effective demand.

Two phrases must be kept apart:

- **Quantity demanded** is the amount bought at one particular price. It changes only when the product's own price changes, which is a **movement along** the demand curve (an extension when price falls, a contraction when price rises).
- **Demand** is the whole relationship between price and quantity. It changes when any other determinant changes, which **shifts** the curve.

The same split applies to supply: a change in the product's own price causes an extension or contraction of supply; anything else shifts the supply curve.

## Determinants and the decisions they trigger

A useful habit is to pair every determinant with a decision a business might take.

| Determinant | Example change | Curve and direction | Likely business decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| Income | Wage rises across the region | Demand right for most goods | Raise output, review price upward |
| Price of a substitute | Rival coffee chain raises prices | Demand for ours right | Hold price to win switchers |
| Price of a complement | Games consoles become cheaper | Demand for games right | Order more stock of games |
| Tastes and trends | Health campaign against sugary drinks | Demand for fizzy drinks left | Reformulate, cut output |
| Costs of production | Energy prices rise | Supply left | Pass on cost or absorb it |
| Technology | Faster packing machine | Supply right | Cut price or keep the margin |
| Indirect tax | Duty added per unit | Supply left | Decide how much to pass to buyers |
| Number of sellers | New entrants open nearby | Supply right | Differentiate to protect price |

Complements are bought together, so their demand moves in the **same** direction when one becomes cheaper. Substitutes compete, so a price rise for one **raises** demand for the other. Mixing these up is one of the most common lost marks.

## How equilibrium is reached

Equilibrium is not a price someone chooses. It is where buyers' plans and sellers' plans match, so there is no pressure to change. The adjustment works through three signals:

1. **Rationing.** When goods run short, a higher price limits purchases to those who value them most.
2. **Signalling.** A rising price tells producers that buyers want more.
3. **Incentive.** A higher price makes extra output more profitable, so firms supply more.

When there is a shortage, sellers find they can raise price without losing sales, and quantity supplied extends as quantity demanded contracts until the gap closes. With an excess, unsold stock pushes sellers to discount until the market clears.

## Worked example 1: a market with a shortage and an excess

Cases of cold-pressed juice sold weekly to cafés in a city (all figures invented). Price P is in pounds per case.

Demand: QD = 1,200 − 40P. Supply: QS = 60P − 300.

| Price (£) | Quantity demanded | Quantity supplied | Position |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | 800 | 300 | shortage of 500 |
| 12 | 720 | 420 | shortage of 300 |
| 15 | 600 | 600 | equilibrium |
| 18 | 480 | 780 | excess supply of 300 |
| 20 | 400 | 900 | excess supply of 500 |

Setting 1,200 − 40P = 60P − 300 gives 1,500 = 100P, so **P = £15** and **Q = 600 cases**. Revenue for all sellers is 15 × 600 = £9,000 a week.

**Constructing the diagram.** Put price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis. Plot two points for each line from the table and join them. Label D and S, mark the crossing at (600, £15), and draw dashed lines to both axes. To show the shortage at £12, draw a horizontal line at £12: it meets supply at 420 and demand at 720, and the gap of 300 between those points is the shortage. An excess is shown the same way above equilibrium.

## Worked example 2: when both curves shift

Examiners like cases where two things happen at once. Suppose a health campaign adds 200 cases to demand at every price (QD = 1,400 − 40P) while a poor fruit harvest removes 100 cases from supply at every price (QS = 60P − 400).

- Demand shift alone: 1,400 − 40P = 60P − 300 gives P = £17, Q = 720.
- Supply shift alone: 1,200 − 40P = 60P − 400 gives P = £16, Q = 560.
- Both together: 1,400 − 40P = 60P − 400 gives 1,800 = 100P, so **P = £18, Q = 680**.

Both changes push price up, so price must rise. Quantity is pulled in opposite directions: the demand rise adds to it and the supply fall reduces it. Here the demand effect is larger, so quantity rises from 600 to 680. If the harvest had removed 400 cases instead (QS = 60P − 700), the new equilibrium would be P = £21 and Q = 560, so quantity would fall. The lesson: **with a double shift, one variable is certain and the other depends on the relative size of the shifts.** Say so in your answer rather than guessing.

## Price and output decisions

A firm's response depends on how much control it has over price.

- **In a competitive market** with many similar sellers, a firm largely accepts the market price. Its decision is mainly about output: produce more when the market price rises, cut back or leave when it falls.
- **A firm with a strong brand or few rivals** can choose its own price. When demand rises it may raise price, expand capacity, or hold price to build share and loyalty.
- **When costs push supply left**, a firm must judge whether buyers will accept a higher price. If customers have many alternatives, passing on the full cost may lose too many sales, so the firm absorbs part of it and accepts a lower margin.

Timing matters too. Output can rarely change overnight: a juice producer can add a shift within a week, but a new pressing line takes months. In the short run a demand rise often shows up as a higher price and longer delivery times; in the long run as more output.

## Evaluating factors that affect demand and supply

Evaluation means judging which factor matters most **for this business**. Useful questions:

- **How big is the change?** A 1% rise in incomes barely matters to a bakery; a 20% rise in flour costs does.
- **Is it temporary or lasting?** A heatwave lifts ice-cream demand for a fortnight; an ageing population changes demand for decades.
- **Can the business predict or influence it?** It can shape tastes through promotion, but not the weather.
- **Do rivals face the same change?** A cost rise that hits every firm can be passed on more easily than one that hits only you.
- **How responsive are buyers to price?** If customers would not cut purchases much, a firm can pass on cost rises more safely.

## Excesses and shortages: why they matter to a firm

**Excess supply** means unsold stock, storage costs, discounting, and wasted output if the product is perishable. For the juice producer, an excess is serious because the product has a short shelf life. For a maker of tinned food the same excess is a minor stocking cost.

**A shortage** means lost sales and disappointed customers, but also a chance to raise price, to bring in extra capacity, or to prioritise the most profitable customers. A short shortage can even build a product's reputation. A long one invites rivals and new entrants to fill the gap.

The effect depends on how quickly price can adjust. Where prices are fixed by contract or printed in menus and catalogues, imbalances last longer and the costs are larger.

## Common mistakes

- Saying "demand rose" when price fell. That is an extension of demand, a movement along the curve.
- Shifting the demand curve for a cost change. Costs affect supply.
- Drawing a shortage above the equilibrium price. Shortages occur below it.
- Claiming quantity must rise in a double shift without comparing the sizes of the shifts.
- Finishing an evaluation with "it depends" and no statement of what it depends on.

## Exam technique for OCR command words

- **Explain**: give the cause, the mechanism and the result. "Lower energy costs shift supply right, so at the old price there is an excess, which pushes price down and quantity up."
- **Construct and interpret**: draw neatly, label axes and curves, mark old and new equilibria, then state the change in price and quantity in words.
- **Analyse**: build a chain of reasoning linked to the case business, such as how a shortage affects its revenue, its customers and its rivals.
- **Evaluate**: weigh at least two factors or outcomes, then reach a judgement that names the deciding condition, such as whether the change is lasting or how much pricing power the firm has.
