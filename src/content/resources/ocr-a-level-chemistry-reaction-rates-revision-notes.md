## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Rate of reaction | Speed at which reactants are consumed or products made: quantity change ÷ time |
| Activation energy, Ea | Minimum energy required for a reaction to take place (3.2.1(c)) |
| Catalyst | Increases rate without being used up by the overall reaction; gives an alternative route with lower Ea |
| Homogeneous catalyst | Same physical state as the reactants |
| Heterogeneous catalyst | Different physical state from the reactants |
| Boltzmann distribution | Spread of molecular energies in a sample of gas at one temperature |

## 3.2.2(a): Collision frequency

- Reaction needs a collision with energy of at least Ea.
- Higher concentration means more particles per dm³, so **more frequent collisions** and a higher rate.
- Higher gas pressure at constant temperature means the same molecules in a smaller volume, so a **higher gas concentration** and more frequent collisions.
- Concentration and pressure change the **frequency** of collisions, not the energy of each collision.
- As a reaction runs, reactant concentration falls, collisions get less frequent and the rate drops. That is why quantity-time curves level off.

## 3.2.2(b): Rate from a graph

### Method in steps: instantaneous rate

1. Put a ruler against the curve at the time you need, touching it at one point.
2. Extend the line over most of the graph paper.
3. Choose two well-separated points on the line, not on the curve.
4. Gradient = change in y ÷ change in x.
5. Give the rate as a positive number with units from the axes.

### Units at a glance

| Quantity on the y-axis | Rate units |
|---|---|
| Concentration / mol dm⁻³ | mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ |
| Gas volume / cm³ | cm³ s⁻¹ |
| Mass / g | g s⁻¹ |
| Amount / mol | mol s⁻¹ |

### Converting to moles

- Gas volume: amount (mol) = volume (cm³) ÷ 24 000, using 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹ at RTP.
- Mass loss: amount (mol) = mass lost (g) ÷ Mr of the escaping gas.
- Do the conversion on the rate itself: 1.2 cm³ s⁻¹ ÷ 24 000 = 5.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol s⁻¹.

### Small worked reminder

A reactant falls from 0.240 to 0.195 mol dm⁻³ between 30 s and 60 s. Mean rate = 0.045 ÷ 30 = **1.5 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹**.

Mean rate over an interval and rate at an instant are different. The tangent at t = 0 gives the **initial rate**, which is usually the largest rate on the curve.

## 3.2.2(c): How a catalyst works

- It provides a **different route** with a **lower activation energy**.
- Overall it is not consumed. It may react in one step and be regenerated in a later step.
- On an enthalpy profile, reactant and product levels are fixed, so **ΔH is unchanged**. Only the hump is lower.
- Reverse Ea = forward Ea − ΔH. For an exothermic reaction (ΔH negative), the reverse Ea is the bigger one.
- The catalyst lowers the forward and reverse Ea by the same amount.
- Details of how any specific catalyst works are not required.

### Small worked reminder

An endothermic reaction has ΔH = +25 kJ mol⁻¹. Its uncatalysed Ea is 130 kJ mol⁻¹ and its catalysed Ea is 85 kJ mol⁻¹.

- The catalyst lowers Ea by 130 − 85 = **45 kJ mol⁻¹**.
- Reverse Ea, uncatalysed: 130 − 25 = **105 kJ mol⁻¹**.
- Reverse Ea, catalysed: 85 − 25 = **60 kJ mol⁻¹**, also 45 kJ mol⁻¹ lower.
- Here the products sit above the reactants, so the reverse hump is the smaller one.

### Enthalpy profile checklist

- Axes: enthalpy (y) and progress of reaction (x).
- Reactant and product lines at the correct relative heights.
- Two humps, catalysed lower than uncatalysed.
- Arrows from reactants up to each peak, labelled Ea and Ea(cat).
- Arrow from reactants to products labelled ΔH, pointing down for exothermic.

## 3.2.2(d): Catalyst types and sustainability

| Type | Example | Phases |
|---|---|---|
| Homogeneous | H⁺ in esterification in solution | Liquid catalyst, liquid reactants |
| Homogeneous | Cl radicals in ozone breakdown | All gases |
| Heterogeneous | Fe in ammonia production | Solid catalyst, gaseous reactants |
| Heterogeneous | Ni in hydrogenation of alkenes | Solid catalyst, fluid reactants passing over it |

### Why catalysts matter

- Industrial processes reach a useful rate at a **lower temperature**.
- Less energy is needed, so **less fossil fuel is burned**.
- That means **lower CO₂ emissions** and lower costs, so improved sustainability.
- Against this: some catalysts are **toxic**, so they need careful handling, recovery and disposal (HSW9,10).

A good evaluation answer gives both sides. State the benefit chain in full, then the drawback, then a judgement, for example that the saving is worth having only if the toxic catalyst is kept out of the environment.

## 3.2.2(e): Measuring rates (PAG9)

| Measure | Apparatus | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| Mass lost | Conical flask with cotton wool plug on a balance | A heavy gas, such as CO₂, escapes |
| Gas volume | Gas syringe; or an upturned burette or measuring cylinder filled with water | A gas forms; avoid water if the gas dissolves readily |
| Time | Stopwatch or timer | Timing a fixed change; 1/t is proportional to mean rate |

- PAG9 is continuous monitoring, with measurement of time and software for processing data.
- Control temperature with a water bath. Keep volumes, concentrations and solid mass and surface area fixed apart from the one you change.
- Start timing as reactants mix.

## 3.2.2(f): The Boltzmann distribution

Rules for the curve:

- y-axis: number of molecules. x-axis: energy.
- Starts at the **origin**.
- One peak, at the most probable energy.
- Long tail at high energy that **never meets the x-axis**.
- Total area represents every molecule in the sample.
- Region beyond Ea: molecules able to react on collision.

## 3.2.2(g): Temperature and catalysts

### Must-know distinction: what moves?

| Change | Curve | Ea line | Area beyond Ea |
|---|---|---|---|
| Higher temperature | Peak lower and further right; same area | Stays put | Larger |
| Add a catalyst | Unchanged | Moves left (lower) | Larger |
| Higher concentration or pressure | Same shape (same temperature) | Stays put | Same proportion; collisions just more frequent |

### Model answer pattern

1. Draw or describe the curve(s), with Ea marked.
2. Say what moves (curve or Ea line).
3. State that a **greater proportion of molecules** has energy of at least Ea.
4. Link to **more successful collisions per second**, so a higher rate.

## Quick self-test

1. A gas syringe collects 45 cm³ in the first 25 s. Calculate the mean rate.
2. A reactant falls from 0.150 to 0.105 mol dm⁻³ in 90 s. Calculate the mean rate.
3. A flask loses 0.176 g of CO₂ (Mr 44.0) in 40 s. Calculate the mean rate in mol s⁻¹.
4. A tangent passes through (0 s, 0.80 mol dm⁻³) and (200 s, 0.20 mol dm⁻³). Calculate the rate.
5. A reaction has forward Ea = 95 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔH = −40 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the reverse Ea.
6. Solid V₂O₅ speeds up the reaction of SO₂(g) with O₂(g). Classify the catalyst.
7. What happens to the total area under a Boltzmann curve when the gas is heated?
8. Why does the Boltzmann curve start at the origin?
9. The pressure of a gas mixture is tripled at constant temperature. What happens to its concentration?
10. State the units of rate taken from a graph of mass against time.
11. Why is mass loss a poor way to follow a reaction that gives hydrogen?

### Answers

1. 45 ÷ 25 = **1.8 cm³ s⁻¹**
2. 0.045 ÷ 90 = **5.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹**
3. 0.176 ÷ 44.0 = 0.004 00 mol; 0.004 00 ÷ 40 = **1.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol s⁻¹**
4. 0.60 ÷ 200 = **3.0 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹**
5. 95 − (−40) = **135 kJ mol⁻¹**
6. **Heterogeneous**: solid catalyst, gaseous reactants
7. It **stays the same**; the number of molecules is unchanged
8. **No molecules have zero energy**
9. It **triples**
10. **g s⁻¹**
11. Hydrogen has a very small Mr, so the **mass change is too small** to measure precisely

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing that higher concentration makes collisions "more energetic" or "harder".
- Saying "more collisions" without "per second" or "more frequent".
- Taking a gradient from a chord between two table points when a tangent at one time was asked for.
- Reading tangent points off the curve instead of off the drawn line.
- Leaving units off, or mixing cm³ and dm³ when converting gas volumes to moles.
- Drawing the hotter Boltzmann curve with a higher peak, or with a larger area.
- Shifting the Boltzmann curve to show a catalyst instead of moving Ea.
- Stating that a catalyst changes ΔH or is used up.
- Classifying a catalyst without stating the physical states involved.
- Listing sustainability benefits without the chain: lower temperature, less fuel burned, less CO₂.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does increasing concentration increase the rate of reaction?**
A higher concentration means more reactant particles per dm³, so collisions are more frequent and more collisions per second have energy of at least the activation energy. Concentration changes the frequency of collisions, not the energy of each one.

**Does a catalyst change the enthalpy change of a reaction?**
No. On an enthalpy profile the reactant and product levels are fixed, so ΔH is unchanged; only the activation energy hump is lower. The catalyst lowers the forward and reverse activation energies by the same amount.

**How do you convert a gas volume rate into moles per second?**
Divide the rate in cm³ s⁻¹ by 24 000, using the molar gas volume of 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹ at RTP. For example, 1.2 cm³ s⁻¹ ÷ 24 000 = 5.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol s⁻¹.

**What are the sustainability benefits of using catalysts in industry?**
A catalyst lets a process reach a useful rate at a lower temperature, so less energy is needed, less fossil fuel is burned, and carbon dioxide emissions and costs fall. Against this, some catalysts are toxic and need careful handling, recovery and disposal.
