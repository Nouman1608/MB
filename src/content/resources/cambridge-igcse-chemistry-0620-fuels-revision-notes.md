Condensed recall for **11.3 Fuels**, Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620 (2026–2028). All of 11.3 is **Core**. For full explanations and worked examples use the [fuels study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-fuels/); for exam-style questions use the [fuels practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-fuels-practice/).

## The seven outcomes in one list

1. Name the fossil fuels: **coal, natural gas, petroleum**.
2. Name **methane** as the main constituent of natural gas.
3. Hydrocarbons contain **hydrogen and carbon only**.
4. Petroleum is a **mixture** of hydrocarbons.
5. Describe **fractional distillation** of petroleum.
6. Describe how fraction properties change **from bottom to top**: chain length, volatility, boiling point, viscosity.
7. Name the **uses** of the eight fractions.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning to give in the exam |
|---|---|
| Fossil fuel | coal, natural gas or petroleum |
| Hydrocarbon | a compound containing hydrogen and carbon only |
| Petroleum (crude oil) | a mixture of hydrocarbons |
| Fraction | a mixture of hydrocarbons with similar boiling points, collected together |
| Fractionating column | tall column with a temperature gradient, hottest at the bottom, coolest at the top |
| Volatility | how readily a liquid evaporates |
| Viscosity | how thick a liquid is; high viscosity means it flows slowly |
| Chemical feedstock | a raw material used to make other chemicals |

## Fractional distillation: the sequence

- Petroleum is **heated** and **vaporised**.
- Vapour enters the **bottom** of the column.
- Vapours **rise and cool**.
- Each fraction **condenses** where the temperature drops **below its boiling point** and is drawn off at that level.
- Short-chain, low-boiling fractions leave at the **top**; long-chain, high-boiling fractions at the **bottom**.

What makes it work: the hydrocarbons in petroleum have **different boiling points**, and boiling point increases with chain length.

## The four trends (bottom → top)

| Going up the column | Change |
|---|---|
| Chain length | decreases |
| Volatility | higher |
| Boiling point | lower |
| Viscosity | lower |

Reason in one line: shorter molecules have **weaker intermolecular forces**, so less energy separates them.

Going **down** the column, reverse every trend.

## Fractions and uses (top → bottom)

| Fraction | Use |
|---|---|
| Refinery gas | heating and cooking |
| Gasoline / petrol | fuel for cars |
| Naphtha | chemical feedstock |
| Kerosene / paraffin | jet fuel |
| Diesel oil / gas oil | fuel for diesel engines |
| Fuel oil | fuel for ships and home heating systems |
| Lubricating oil | lubricants, waxes and polishes |
| Bitumen | making roads |

Memory hook: the uses move from **gas** (cooking) → **vehicles** (cars, jets, diesel engines) → **heavy fuels** (ships, home heating) → **thick materials** (lubricants, roads). Naphtha is the odd one out, used to make chemicals rather than burned.

Fuel oil and lubricating oil have overlapping boiling ranges and are drawn in different orders in different textbooks; be secure on the trend rather than on that one pair.

## Linking properties to uses

- **Refinery gas** is the most volatile fraction: already a gas at room temperature, so it is piped or bottled for cooking.
- **Petrol** must vaporise easily inside a car engine, so it is a volatile, low-viscosity liquid.
- **Lubricating oil** must cling to moving parts, so its high viscosity is an advantage.
- **Bitumen** is so viscous that it is almost solid at road temperatures, which is why it binds road surfaces.

## Answering questions on unfamiliar fractions

Questions often describe fractions only by their boiling range or by where they leave the column. Use a fixed chain of reasoning:

1. **Position** → lower in the column means a higher boiling range.
2. **Boiling range** → higher boiling range means longer chains.
3. **Chain length** → longer chains mean stronger intermolecular forces.
4. **Property** → stronger forces mean higher boiling point, higher viscosity, lower volatility.

Write at least two links of the chain whenever the command word is **explain**; for **state** or **name**, the final answer alone is enough.

## Exam traps

- "Hydrocarbons contain carbon and hydrogen" scores nothing without **only**.
- Petroleum is a **mixture**, not a compound; a fraction is also a mixture, not a pure substance.
- The column is **hot at the bottom**. Writing "hot at the top" loses the gradient mark.
- Fractions **condense** at their level; do not say they "boil off" there.
- Short chains are **more** volatile and **less** viscous.
- The main constituent of natural gas is **methane**; "natural gas" itself is not an acceptable answer to "name the hydrocarbon".
- Naphtha, not petrol, is the **chemical feedstock**.
- Fractional distillation is a **physical** separation: no bonds within the molecules break. Cracking, which does break bonds, belongs to 11.5.

## Self-test

1. Name the three fossil fuels.
2. What is the main constituent of natural gas?
3. Define a hydrocarbon.
4. Why is petroleum described as a mixture?
5. Where in the fractionating column is the temperature highest?
6. State what happens to a vapour when it reaches a level of the column below its boiling point.
7. How does the chain length of fractions change from the bottom to the top of the column?
8. Which fraction is used as jet fuel?
9. Which fraction is used as a chemical feedstock?
10. State the use of bitumen.
11. Fraction A has a higher boiling range than fraction B. State and explain which is more viscous.
12. Is ethanol, C₂H₅OH, a hydrocarbon? Give a reason.

**Answers:**

1. Coal, natural gas and petroleum.
2. Methane.
3. A compound containing hydrogen and carbon only.
4. It contains many different hydrocarbons that are not chemically combined with each other, each keeping its own boiling point.
5. At the bottom.
6. It condenses to a liquid and is collected at that level.
7. It decreases.
8. Kerosene / paraffin.
9. Naphtha.
10. Making roads.
11. Fraction A: a higher boiling range means longer chains, which have stronger intermolecular forces and flow less easily.
12. No: it contains oxygen as well as carbon and hydrogen.
