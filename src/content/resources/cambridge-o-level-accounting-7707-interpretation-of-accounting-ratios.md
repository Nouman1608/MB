This guide goes deeper into section **6.2 Interpretation of accounting ratios** of the Cambridge O Level Accounting 7707 syllabus for examination in 2027, 2028 and 2029. The syllabus asks for an understanding of "how to prepare and comment on statements showing comparison of results for different years", "how to interpret the ratios calculated in 6.1", and to "make suggestions and recommendations for improving profitability, liquidity and working capital". It names "the gross profit margin and the profit margin as indicators of a business's profitability", how gross profit "can be affected by the valuation of inventory, sales quantity and changes in selling and purchasing prices", how profit for the year "can be affected by changes in gross profit, other income and expenses", and "the reasons for the difference between cash and profit".

The ratios themselves are calculated in the [ratio calculation study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-calculation-and-understanding-of-accounting-ratios/). This page works through one business over two years and models the comments that earn interpretation marks. Recall the key points with the [interpretation revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-interpretation-of-accounting-ratios-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [interpretation practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-interpretation-of-accounting-ratios-practice/). All figures are invented and amounts are in dollars.

## A comparison statement

A comparison statement sets two years side by side with the change in dollars and as a percentage of the earlier year. Tamsin runs a bookshop. She cut some prices in 2028 to win more school orders.

| | 2027 | 2028 | Change | Change % |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 150,000 | 180,000 | +30,000 | +20.00 |
| Cost of sales | 90,000 | 117,000 | +27,000 | +30.00 |
| Gross profit | 60,000 | 63,000 | +3,000 | +5.00 |
| Rent received | 4,000 | 1,000 | -3,000 | -75.00 |
| Expenses | 38,000 | 41,800 | +3,800 | +10.00 |
| Profit for the year | 26,000 | 22,200 | -3,800 | -14.62 |

A comment on this statement does more than repeat it. Compare the speed of change between lines: cost of sales grew faster than revenue (30% against 20%), so gross profit grew by only 5%. Expenses grew more slowly than revenue (10% against 20%), which is good control. Profit still fell, because the small rise in gross profit was wiped out by the extra expenses and the loss of most of the rent received.

## Profitability: reading the two margins together

| Ratio | 2027 | 2028 |
|---|---|---|
| Gross profit margin | 40.00% | 35.00% |
| Profit margin | 17.33% | 12.33% |
| Expenses as % of revenue | 25.33% | 23.22% |
| Rent received as % of revenue | 2.67% | 0.56% |

The syllabus names these two margins as the indicators of profitability. Read them as a pair.

- The **gross profit margin** fell by 5 percentage points. Tamsin earns $35 of gross profit on each $100 of sales instead of $40. The price cuts are the likely cause; a rise in supplier prices would have the same effect.
- The **profit margin** also fell by 5 percentage points. Expenses took a smaller share of revenue, so the fall is explained by the lower gross profit margin and the lost rent income, not by poor cost control.

A useful rule: if the gross profit margin is steady and the profit margin falls, look at expenses and other income. If both fall by similar amounts, look at the trading section.

## What changes gross profit

| Cause | Effect on gross profit | Effect on gross profit margin |
|---|---|---|
| Closing inventory overvalued | Higher this year (and lower next year) | Higher this year |
| More units sold at the same prices and costs | Higher | Unchanged |
| Selling prices raised, costs unchanged | Higher, if customers keep buying | Higher |
| Purchase prices rise, selling prices unchanged | Lower | Lower |

Inventory valuation works through closing inventory. If Tamsin's closing inventory of $24,000 had been overvalued by $1,500, her 2028 gross profit would be $64,500 and her margin 35.83% instead of 35.00%. Her 2029 gross profit would then be $1,500 too low, because the same figure becomes the opening inventory.

Tamsin's revenue rose because she sold more books at lower prices. Sales quantity raised gross profit, while the lower selling prices cut the margin.

## What changes profit for the year

Profit for the year = gross profit + other income - expenses. A change in any one of the three moves it. In Tamsin's case gross profit rose $3,000, other income fell $3,000 and expenses rose $3,800, so profit fell by $3,800.

## Liquidity and working capital

| Ratio | 2027 | 2028 |
|---|---|---|
| Current ratio | 2.50 : 1 | 1.79 : 1 |
| Acid test ratio | 1.17 : 1 | 0.64 : 1 |
| Trade receivables turnover | 36.50 days | 54.75 days |

Tamsin allows schools 30 days' credit. A model comment: "The acid test ratio fell from 1.17 : 1 to 0.64 : 1, so Tamsin now has only $0.64 of liquid assets for each $1 of current liabilities and may struggle to pay suppliers on time. The main cause is that trade receivables now take 54.75 days to pay against 30 days allowed, and inventory rose from $16,000 to $24,000, tying up cash." Each sentence states the change, quantifies it, explains it and gives a consequence.

## Why cash and profit differ

Tamsin made a profit of $22,200, yet her bank moved from $8,000 in hand to a $6,000 overdraft, a fall of $14,000.

| Item | Effect on bank |
|---|---|
| Profit for the year | +22,200 |
| Depreciation (an expense with no payment) | +2,300 |
| Increase in inventory | -8,000 |
| Increase in trade receivables | -7,500 |
| Increase in trade payables | +3,000 |
| Drawings (not an expense) | -20,000 |
| Purchase of new shelving (capital expenditure) | -6,000 |
| Change in bank | -14,000 |

Other common reasons are loans received or repaid, capital introduced, and accrued or prepaid expenses.

## Recommendations

Suggestions must fit the problem the ratios reveal.

- **Profitability**: review which price cuts actually won orders; seek trade discounts from publishers; find a new tenant for the spare room to restore rent income.
- **Liquidity**: chase overdue school accounts and offer a small cash discount for payment within 30 days; replace the overdraft with a long-term loan.
- **Working capital**: reduce inventory by ordering in smaller quantities; reduce drawings until the bank is positive.

## Common mistakes

- Describing a ratio's change without a reason or consequence.
- Saying "profit fell because the margin fell" without naming what caused the margin to fall.
- Treating a high current ratio as always good: excess inventory or idle cash also raises it.
- Giving a recommendation that does not match the problem, such as raising prices to fix slow-paying customers.
- Listing reasons for cash differing from profit without using the question's figures.

## Exam technique

Paper 2 assesses evaluation as well as analysis. Interpretation tasks draw on syllabus command words such as **explain**, **analyse**, **suggest**, **advise** and **evaluate**. For each ratio: state the direction and size of the change, give a reason from the case, and state the consequence. For **advise**, finish with a recommendation tied to the weakest ratio. For **evaluate**, weigh one improvement against one weakness before reaching a judgement.
