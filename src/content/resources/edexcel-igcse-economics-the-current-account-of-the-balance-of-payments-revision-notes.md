## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Balance of payments | A record of all money flowing into and out of a country in a period |
| Current account | The part of the balance of payments recording trade in goods and services (fuller accounts also record income earned abroad and transfers; the 4EC1 specification lists only goods and services) |
| Current account surplus | More money coming in than going out on trade in goods and services |
| Current account deficit | More money going out than coming in on trade in goods and services |
| Visibles | Traded goods |
| Invisibles | Traded services, such as tourism, banking and insurance |
| Exchange rate | The price of a unit of one currency, stated in another currency |
| Depreciation | The currency buys less foreign currency than before |
| Appreciation | The currency buys more foreign currency than before |
| Foreign reserves | Stocks of foreign currency held to pay for imports and other overseas payments |

## Formulae

| Item | Formula |
|---|---|
| Visible balance | Visible exports - visible imports |
| Invisible balance | Invisible exports - invisible imports |
| Current account balance | Visible balance + invisible balance |
| Balance as % of GDP | Balance / GDP x 100 |
| Percentage change in a rate | (new - old) / old x 100 |
| Years reserves last | Reserves / annual deficit (if reserves alone fund it) |

### Method in steps: find the balance

1. Subtract visible imports from visible exports.
2. Subtract invisible imports from invisible exports.
3. Add the two answers, keeping the signs.
4. State the result as a surplus (positive), a deficit (negative) or balance (zero), with units.

Worked reminder: Country S has visible exports 54 and imports 61, invisible exports 22 and imports 15 (illustrative). Visible = -7, invisible = +7, total = **0**, so the account is in balance.

## Which direction does the money go?

| Transaction | Counts as |
|---|---|
| A foreign firm buys a country's machinery | Visible export |
| A resident buys a foreign phone | Visible import |
| A foreign tourist stays in a hotel at home | Invisible export |
| A resident buys foreign insurance | Invisible import |
| A foreign company uses a home bank | Invisible export |

Test: does the money come in (export) or go out (import)? Is the thing bought a good (visible) or a service (invisible)?

## Exchange rates and the current account

### Method in steps: convert and compare

1. Write the rate as 1 unit of home currency = x units of foreign currency.
2. Foreign price of an export = home price x x.
3. Home price of an import = foreign price / x.
4. Compare before and after using (new - old) / old x 100.

Worked reminder: 1 pel = 2.00 gem. An export costs 50 pels, so it costs 100 gem. The pel falls to 1.60 gem. The export now costs 50 x 1.60 = 80 gem, a fall of 20%. An import costing 90 gem went from 90 / 2.00 = 45 pels to 90 / 1.60 = 56.25 pels, a rise of 25%.

| Change in the home currency | Exports | Imports | Current account tends to |
|---|---|---|---|
| Depreciation | Cheaper abroad | Dearer at home | Improve |
| Appreciation | Dearer abroad | Cheaper at home | Worsen |

### The link in the other direction

Under a market-determined rate, a deficit means the supply of the home currency (to buy imports) exceeds demand (to buy exports), so the currency tends to fall. A surplus means demand exceeds supply, so it tends to rise. A falling currency then helps to reduce the deficit. These are tendencies, and the outcome depends on how strongly buyers respond to price changes.

### Real-world rates

- A quote written as A/B means the number of units of B that 1 unit of A buys.
- Multiply to go from A to B. Divide to go from B to A.
- Practise with a current quotation from a bank, newspaper or website. Rates change daily, so use a fresh one each time and check whether the currency has gone up or down.

## Reasons for deficits and surpluses

| Cause | Deficit if | Surplus if |
|---|---|---|
| Quality of domestic goods | Poor: buyers switch to imports | Good: exports sell well |
| Quality of foreign goods | High: more imports | Low: fewer imports |
| Price of domestic goods | High relative to rivals | Low relative to rivals |
| Price of foreign goods | Low: imports attractive | High: imports unattractive |
| Exchange rate | Rises: exports dearer, imports cheaper | Falls: exports cheaper, imports dearer |

Chain of reasoning to write out: cause, then buyer response, then change in exports or imports, then effect on the balance.

## Impact of a deficit

1. **Leakage.** Imports take money out of the circular flow of income. More leaving than entering means less spending in the home economy.
2. **Inflationary if prices rise abroad.** Imported materials cost more. Firms may raise their selling prices.
3. **Low demand for exports.** Exporters sell less, so they may cut output and jobs.
4. **Problems finding foreign reserves.** Deficits are paid from reserves or by borrowing. Reserves can run out.

Worked reminder: reserves of 45 and a deficit of 9 a year last 45 / 9 = 5 years. A deficit of 8 on GDP of 400 is 8 / 400 x 100 = 2.0% of GDP.

### Evaluation points

- A small deficit relative to GDP may be easy to fund.
- The reason matters: imports of machinery may raise future output, while imports of consumer goods may not.
- A surplus is not free of cost: the country gives up goods it could have enjoyed, and a stronger currency can hurt exporters.
- A high or rising deficit as a share of GDP is more worrying than one that is small and stable.

## Model chain of reasoning

Question type: explain why a country might run a current account deficit.

1. State the cause: the country's clothing is of lower quality than imported clothing.
2. Show the buyer response: consumers at home switch to the foreign product and foreign buyers do not choose the home product.
3. Show the effect: imports of clothing rise and exports fall.
4. Link to the account: money leaving for visibles exceeds money entering, so the visible balance, and perhaps the whole current account, moves into deficit.
5. Add context if given: for example, a strong currency makes the imported clothing cheaper still, which strengthens the effect.

Extension to a consequence: lower export sales mean exporting firms earn less, may cut output and jobs, and the deficit must be funded from foreign reserves, which are limited.

## Must-know distinctions

| Pair | Difference |
|---|---|
| Visible vs invisible | Goods vs services |
| Depreciation vs deflation | A fall in a currency's value vs a fall in the general price level |
| Surplus vs balance | Inflows exceed outflows vs inflows equal outflows |
| Foreign tourist visiting vs resident travelling abroad | Invisible export vs invisible import |

## Self-test questions

1. Define the current account.
2. Classify: a foreign student paying fees at a home university.
3. Visible exports 80, visible imports 95, invisible exports 31, invisible imports 20. Find the current account balance.
4. Express a deficit of 12 as a percentage of GDP of 600.
5. 1 pel = 2.50 gem. Find the gem price of an export costing 40 pels.
6. The pel falls to 1 pel = 2.00 gem. Find the percentage change and the new gem price of the export.
7. State one way poor domestic quality leads to a deficit.
8. Explain how a deficit can be inflationary.
9. A country holds reserves of 36 and runs a deficit of 9 a year. How long do they last?
10. Give one reason a deficit might not be a serious problem.

### Answers

1. The part of the balance of payments that records trade in goods and services with the rest of the world.
2. Invisible export, because money comes in for a service.
3. Visible = 80 - 95 = -15. Invisible = 31 - 20 = +11. Total = **-4**, a deficit.
4. 12 / 600 x 100 = **2.0%**.
5. 40 x 2.50 = **100 gem**.
6. (2.00 - 2.50) / 2.50 x 100 = **-20%**. New price = 40 x 2.00 = **80 gem**.
7. Buyers switch to imported goods, so imports rise and exports fall.
8. Imported materials cost more when foreign prices rise or the currency falls, so firms raise their prices.
9. 36 / 9 = **4 years**.
10. It is small relative to GDP, or it is funding imports of machinery that raise future output.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying "imports are bad" without linking to the money leaving the economy.
- Reversing the direction of a depreciation: the price of exports abroad falls.
- Leaving out the sign or the word deficit or surplus after a calculation.
- Writing "services" but giving no example, when asked to explain invisibles.
- Using only price as a reason and ignoring quality.
- Listing the four impacts without explaining how each one works.
- Forgetting units, such as billions of the local currency.
- Giving an evaluation point that is not tied to the context given.
