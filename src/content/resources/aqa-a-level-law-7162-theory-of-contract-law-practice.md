These questions test the section 3.4 row on the theory of contract law: freedom of contract and consumer protection, offers and the postal rule, consideration with privity and duress, exemption clauses and remedies.

---

## Section A

**1.** Explain what is meant by freedom of contract. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why goods displayed on a shop shelf are treated as an invitation to treat rather than an offer. **[2]**

**3.** State **two** reasons given for the postal rule. **[2]**

**4.** Explain how an offeror can prevent the postal rule from applying, with an authority. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why the law requires consideration for a promise to be enforceable. **[2]**

**6.** Identify the effect of the Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Act 1999 on the doctrine of privity. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain the relationship between consideration and economic duress, with reference to **two** cases. **[5]**

**8.** Explain whether the postal rule should apply to acceptance by email. **[5]**

**9.** Explain how the law controls exemption clauses, and assess how effective those controls are for consumers. **[6]**

**10.** Explain why the court awarded damages for loss of amenity rather than the cost of cure in *Ruxley Electronics v Forsyth* (1996), and what the case shows about the purpose of damages. **[4]**

**11.** "Freedom of contract is now a myth for consumers." Discuss. **[8]**

**12.** Evaluate the effectiveness of the consumer remedies for faulty goods under the Consumer Rights Act 2015. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Freedom of contract is the principle that parties are free to decide whether to contract, with whom and on what terms [1], and that courts should enforce the agreement as made rather than rewrite it (*Printing and Numerical Registering Co v Sampson*, 1875) [1].

**2.** If a display were an offer, the shop would be bound to sell to anyone who picked up the goods, even if stock ran out or it did not wish to sell [1]; treating it as an invitation to treat means the customer offers and the shop decides whether to accept (*Pharmaceutical Society v Boots*, 1953) [1].

**3.** Any two from: the offeror chose or accepted the post as a method, so should bear the risk of loss or delay [1]; it gives the offeree certainty about when the contract is made [1]; the offeree can act on the contract immediately; the offeror can exclude it if they wish.

**4.** By stating that acceptance must actually be received, for example by requiring "notice in writing" [1]: *Holwell Securities v Hughes* (1974) [1].

**5.** Consideration distinguishes bargains, which the law enforces, from gratuitous promises, which it does not [1]. It is the price paid for the other's promise and shows the parties meant to be bound (*Dunlop v Selfridge*, 1915) [1].

**6.** It allows a third party to enforce a term of a contract [1] if the contract expressly says so or the term purports to confer a benefit on them, unless the parties did not intend it to be enforceable by them [1].

**7.** Traditionally, performing an existing contractual duty is not consideration for a promise of extra pay (*Stilk v Myrick*, 1809) [1], partly to prevent parties extorting more by threatening not to perform [1]. In *Williams v Roffey Bros* (1991) a practical benefit, such as avoiding a penalty clause, was held to be good consideration [1], provided the promise was not obtained by economic duress or fraud [1]. Duress therefore now does the protective work: in *Atlas Express v Kafco* (1989) a carrier's threat not to deliver made the promise to pay more unenforceable [1].

**8.** The postal rule makes acceptance effective on posting because the offeror chose the post and the offeree cannot know whether a letter arrives [1]. For instantaneous methods, acceptance takes effect on receipt (*Entores*, 1955) [1], and *Brinkibon* (1983) held that there is no universal rule: it depends on the parties' intentions and where risk should lie [1]. With email, the sender can usually check delivery and resend, so the reasons for placing the risk on the offeror do not apply [1]. A receipt rule is therefore more appropriate, although no binding appellate decision has settled the point [1].

**9.** The law controls exemption clauses at three stages [1]: incorporation, requiring notice before or at the time of contracting (*Olley*, 1949; *Thornton*, 1971) [1]; construction, reading ambiguity against the party relying on the clause [1]; and statute, under which the CRA 2015 makes clauses excluding implied terms or liability for death or personal injury ineffective against consumers (ss31, 65) and subjects other terms to a fairness test [1]. These controls are strong in law [1], but consumers may not know their rights, and an unenforceable clause can still deter them from complaining, so practical effectiveness is weaker [1].

**10.** A swimming pool was built shallower than agreed, but its value was not reduced [1]. Rebuilding it would have been very expensive and out of all proportion to the benefit [1], so the House of Lords refused the cost of cure and awarded a modest sum for loss of amenity [1]. The case shows that damages aim to compensate actual loss reasonably, not to punish the defendant or provide a windfall [1].

**11.** Freedom of contract assumes parties of equal bargaining power who negotiate terms [1]. For consumers this is often unrealistic: they contract on standard terms they cannot negotiate and rarely read [1]. Parliament has responded with the Consumer Rights Act 2015, implying terms that cannot be excluded (ss9–11, 31) [1] and making unfair terms not binding (s62) [1], which supports the view that freedom has declined, as Atiyah argued [1]. However, consumers still choose whether to contract, with whom, and at what price, and competition gives them choice [1]. Between businesses, freedom remains strong (*Photo Production v Securicor*, 1980) [1]. Conclusion: for consumers freedom of contract is limited rather than a myth; the law has replaced it with protected minimum standards [1].

**12.** The CRA gives a tiered set of remedies for goods that are not of satisfactory quality, fit for purpose or as described [1]. The short-term right to reject (s20) within 30 days gives a quick full refund [1]. After that, the consumer can require repair or replacement (s23) [1], and if that fails, a price reduction or final right to reject (s24) [1]. These remedies are effective because they are simple and can be used without going to court [1]. Their weaknesses are that consumers may not know them, the 30-day limit is short for some goods, and enforcement still depends on the trader cooperating or the consumer being willing to sue [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Explaining a rule without its rationale.
- Treating the email question as settled law.
- Missing the link between *Williams v Roffey* and economic duress.
- Writing a list without a conclusion in "discuss" questions.
