This set covers section 11.2 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699) Paper 4:
functionalist and Marxist accounts of religion and social order, and the strengths and limitations
of each. Section A tests concepts; Section B asks for developed explanations and essays.

## Section A

**1.** Define the term *collective conscience*. **[2]**

**2.** Explain what Durkheim meant by *collective effervescence*. **[2]**

**3.** Explain **one** criticism Worsley made of Durkheim's study of totemism. **[3]**

**4.** Explain how Parsons saw religion contributing to social order. **[3]**

**5.** Explain what Marx meant by describing religion as "the opium of the people". **[3]**

**6.** Identify **two** ways in which religion may legitimate inequality, according to Marxists.
**[2]**

**7.** Explain what Gramsci meant by *organic intellectuals* in relation to religion. **[3]**

**8.** Explain **one** similarity between functionalist and Marxist views of religion. **[2]**

## Section B

**9.** Explain **two** ways in which religion may contribute to social solidarity. **[8]**

**10.** Explain **two** ways in which neo-Marxists have developed Marx's view of religion. **[8]**

**11.** "Religion mainly serves the interests of the ruling class." Evaluate this view. **[35]**

**12.** "Functionalist theory is of little use for understanding religion in modern societies."
Evaluate this view. **[35]**

## Answers

**1.** The shared norms, values, beliefs and knowledge of a society [1] that make social life and
cooperation possible (Durkheim) [1]. **[2]**

**2.** The intense shared emotion produced at collective rituals [1], which makes participants feel
part of something greater than themselves and strengthens their attachment to the group [1].
**[2]**

**3.** Identify [1], explain [1], consequence [1]: Worsley (1956) argued that different clans share
the same totems [1], so a totem cannot simply symbolise one clan's society [1]; this weakens
Durkheim's claim that worship of the totem is worship of society [1]. Also creditable: there is no
sharp division between sacred and profane among Aboriginal Australians. **[3]**

**4.** Religion provides core values, such as Christian ideas reflected in law, that become the basis
of norms [1]; it also answers "ultimate questions" about suffering and injustice [1], helping
people adjust to events that might otherwise weaken their commitment to society's values [1].
**[3]**

**5.** Religion eases the pain of exploitation, like a drug [1], by offering comfort and the promise
of reward [1], but it dulls awareness of the real, economic causes of suffering, so it discourages
change [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Any two: presenting inequality as God-given, such as the divine right of kings [1]; promising
compensation in an afterlife [1]; presenting social hierarchy as sacred order, as in caste [1];
religious institutions allying with rulers [1]. **[2]**

**7.** Thinkers who emerge from or side with the working class [1] and help it develop ideas that
challenge ruling-class hegemony [1]; Gramsci argued clergy could play this role, so religion can
support resistance [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Both see religion as conservative [1], maintaining the existing social order rather than
changing it, though they disagree about whom that order benefits [1]. **[2]**

**9.** Up to 4 marks per way: identify [1], explain [1], develop with a study [1], example or
evaluation [1].
- **Collective ritual** [1]: shared worship generates collective effervescence [1], reinforcing the
  collective conscience (Durkheim, 1912) [1]; national ceremonies that combine religious and civic
  symbols show this in modern societies [1].
- **Coping with crisis** [1]: rites at times of death or danger help individuals manage stress [1],
  as Malinowski argued, so disruptive events do not break social bonds [1]; funeral rites
  reintegrate the bereaved into the group [1].
- Also creditable: civil religion integrating diverse societies (Bellah); core values as the basis
  of norms (Parsons). **[8]**

**10.** Up to 4 marks per way: identify [1], explain [1], develop with a study [1], evaluate [1].
- **Religion and hegemony** [1]: Gramsci saw religion as part of a struggle over ideas, not simply
  ruling-class ideology [1]; Billings (1990) found Kentucky miners' lay preachers supporting unions
  [1]; this shows religion can support resistance as well as accommodation [1].
- **Relative autonomy** [1]: Maduro (1982) argued religion is partly independent of the economy [1],
  so clergy can side with the oppressed [1]; this anticipates liberation theology (11.3) [1].
- Also creditable: Engels on early Christianity as a movement of the oppressed. **[8]**

**11.** Marked in levels. The top level explains Marxist arguments with evidence, then weighs them
against functionalist, neo-Marxist and interactionist views. Indicative content:
- **For the view:** Marx (1844) on opium and compensation; ideology and legitimation (divine
  right, caste); Lenin's "spiritual gin"; the Halévy thesis and Thompson (1963) on Methodism.
- **Against (functionalist):** religion benefits society as a whole through solidarity and coping
  (Durkheim, Malinowski, Parsons, Bellah).
- **Against (neo-Marxist):** Engels on early Christianity; Gramsci's organic intellectuals;
  Billings (1990); Maduro's relative autonomy; liberation theology as religion opposing the ruling
  class.
- **Other critiques:** Berger's sacred canopy (meaning for individuals); feminist view that religion
  serves men's interests (12.2).
- **Evaluation:** whether religion serves the ruling class depends on who controls religious
  institutions and the historical context.
- **Judgement:** for example, religion has often served ruling-class interests but can be captured
  by the oppressed. **[35]**

**12.** Marked in levels. The top level explains functionalism accurately, tests it against features
of modern societies and considers what it still explains. Indicative content:
- **For the view:** religious diversity and pluralism undermine one shared collective conscience;
  secularisation (heading 12); religious conflict and Huntington's (1996) disputed thesis; Worsley's
  criticism of Durkheim's evidence; neglect of change and inequality.
- **Against the view:** civil religion (Bellah) in diverse nations; continued rites of passage;
  national ceremonies and collective mourning; religious communities' support in crises;
  Durkheim's prediction of a cult of the individual fits the modern emphasis on human rights.
- **Alternatives:** Marxist, feminist and interactionist views as competing explanations.
- **Evaluation:** functionalism may explain specific functions (coping, ritual) better than overall
  social integration.
- **Judgement:** for example, functionalism remains useful for explaining ritual and meaning but
  cannot explain conflict and diversity. **[35]**
