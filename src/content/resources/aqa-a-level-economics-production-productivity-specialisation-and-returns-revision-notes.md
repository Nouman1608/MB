## Definitions to learn word for word

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Production | Converting inputs, or the services of factors of production such as capital and labour, into final output |
| Productivity | Output per unit of input |
| Labour productivity | Output per worker, or output per worker-hour |
| Specialisation | Concentrating on the tasks or goods you do best |
| Division of labour | Breaking production into separate tasks, with one worker per task |
| Medium of exchange | Something generally accepted in payment for goods and services |
| Short run | The period in which some factor cannot be changed |
| Long run | The period in which every factor can be changed |
| Law of diminishing returns | With another factor held fixed, each extra unit of a variable factor eventually adds less to output than the last |
| Returns to scale | The change in output when all inputs change by the same proportion |

## Productivity

Labour productivity = output ÷ workers, or output ÷ total hours worked.

**Method in steps**

1. Find total hours: workers × hours each.
2. Divide output by workers or hours, as asked.
3. Percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100.
4. Compare with the change in output and inputs before you comment.

**Worked reminder: Country A (illustrative).**

| | Output (units, billions) | Hours (billions) | Output per hour |
|---|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | 600 | 20.0 | 30.00 |
| Year 2 | 636 | 20.4 | 31.18 |

Output rose by 6%. Hours rose by 2%. Output per hour rose by (31.18 − 30) ÷ 30 × 100 = **3.9%**. Part of the growth in output came from more hours, and part from more output per hour.

**Why productivity rises:** more or better capital, training, better organisation, new technology, better health. **Why it matters:** it allows higher output without more inputs, and can reduce costs per unit.

## Specialisation, division of labour and money

| Benefits | Costs and limits |
|---|---|
| Skill grows from repetition | Boredom, absenteeism, turnover |
| No time lost switching tasks | One stage stopping halts the line |
| Capital is kept in use | Workers become less adaptable |
| Workers are matched to tasks | Gains end when tasks cannot be split further |
| Output per worker rises, so unit cost can fall | Checking and handling between stages take time |

**Why exchange is needed.** A specialist makes little of what they consume. Barter needs a double coincidence of wants (each party holds what the other wants) and has no common unit of account. Barter exchange rates: n(n − 1) ÷ 2. Money as a medium of exchange removes the double coincidence of wants, saves search time and gives one price per good.

| Goods | Barter exchange rates | Money prices |
|---|---|---|
| 8 | 8 × 7 ÷ 2 = 28 | 8 |
| 20 | 20 × 19 ÷ 2 = 190 | 20 |

**Chain of reasoning.** Division of labour → more skill and less switching → higher output per worker → lower cost per unit and more output to sell → needs a reliable means of exchange → money makes trade fast and cheap → more specialisation is possible.

## Returns to a variable factor (short run)

| Term | Meaning | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| Total returns | Total output (TP) | |
| Average returns | Output per worker (AP) | TP ÷ workers |
| Marginal returns | Extra output from the last worker (MP) | TP(n) − TP(n − 1) |

**Worked reminder: a car valet bay (illustrative).** One bay is fixed. The wage is £80 a day. The output is cars cleaned a day.

| Workers | TP | MP | AP | MC (£80 ÷ MP) | AVC (£80 × workers ÷ TP) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 5 | 5 | 5.00 | 16.00 | 16.00 |
| 2 | 14 | 9 | 7.00 | 8.89 | 11.43 |
| 3 | 26 | 12 | 8.67 | 6.67 | 9.23 |
| 4 | 34 | 8 | 8.50 | 10.00 | 9.41 |
| 5 | 38 | 4 | 7.60 | 20.00 | 10.53 |
| 6 | 38 | 0 | 6.33 | not defined | 12.63 |

- Diminishing marginal returns start at worker 4 (MP falls from 12 to 8).
- AP falls from worker 4 as well, because MP (8) is below AP (8.67).
- TP is still rising from worker 4 to worker 5. It stops rising at worker 6.
- MC and AVC are lowest at 3 workers, where MP and AP are highest.

**Rules to remember**

- MP above AP: AP rises. MP below AP: AP falls. MP crosses AP at AP's maximum.
- Diminishing returns means MP falls. It does not mean TP falls.
- Wage constant: MC = wage ÷ MP and AVC = wage ÷ AP.

## Returns to scale (long run)

| Output rises by… | Returns to scale | Cost per unit if input prices are constant |
|---|---|---|
| more than inputs | Increasing | Falls |
| same as inputs | Constant | Unchanged |
| less than inputs | Decreasing | Rises |

**Worked reminder: a delivery fleet (illustrative).** The fleet is 4 vans and 4 drivers, and delivers 200 parcels a day. Both are doubled to 8 vans and 8 drivers, and the fleet delivers 380 parcels. Inputs rose 100%. Output rose (380 − 200) ÷ 200 × 100 = 90%. Output rose by less than inputs, so returns to scale are **decreasing**.

## Must-know distinctions

| | Diminishing returns | Returns to scale |
|---|---|---|
| Period | Short run | Long run |
| Inputs | One variable, others fixed | All change together |
| Measures | MP, AP, TP | Total output against inputs |
| Cost link | MC rises (AVC follows once MP is below AP) | Long-run cost per unit falls, stays flat or rises |

Other pairs: productivity and output; marginal and average; specialisation and division of labour (division of labour is specialisation inside a firm).

## Evaluation points

- **Is division of labour always good?** It helps most in repetitive tasks with high volume. Where work needs creativity or flexibility, narrow roles can cut quality and motivation.
- **Is the short run a fixed time?** No. It depends on how long it takes to change the fixed factor, so a bakery stall adapts faster than a steelworks.
- **Does a rise in output show a rise in productivity?** No. Compare output with the inputs used.

## Quick self-test

1. Define labour productivity.
2. A workshop makes 2,400 units with 30 workers, then 2,700 units with the same 30 workers. Find the percentage change in output per worker.
3. A firm makes 5,400 pieces with 45 workers who each work 8 hours. Find output per worker-hour.
4. How many barter exchange rates exist among 20 goods?
5. Why does specialisation need a medium of exchange?
6. Say what is true of factors of production in the short run.
7. TP is 70 with 5 workers and 72 with 6 workers. Find MP of the sixth worker, and AP at 5 and at 6 workers.
8. State the law of diminishing returns in your own words.
9. All inputs double and output goes from 120 to 264. State the percentage change in output and the type of returns to scale.
10. The wage is £60 a day and a worker's MP is 15. Find the extra cost per unit of output.
11. True or false: diminishing returns means total output must fall.

### Answers

1. Output per worker, or per worker-hour.
2. 2,400 ÷ 30 = 80; 2,700 ÷ 30 = 90; (90 − 80) ÷ 80 × 100 = **12.5%**.
3. Hours = 45 × 8 = 360; 5,400 ÷ 360 = **15 pieces per hour**.
4. 20 × 19 ÷ 2 = **190**.
5. A specialist makes little of what they want to consume, so they must trade. Barter needs a double coincidence of wants. Money removes it.
6. At least one factor is fixed.
7. MP = 72 − 70 = **2**. AP at 5 = 70 ÷ 5 = **14**. AP at 6 = 72 ÷ 6 = **12**.
8. With another factor fixed, each extra unit of the variable factor eventually adds less than the previous one.
9. (264 − 120) ÷ 120 × 100 = **120%**, which exceeds the 100% rise in inputs: **increasing returns to scale**.
10. 60 ÷ 15 = **£4**.
11. **False.** MP falls. TP still rises while MP is positive.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "productivity" when the answer needs output per worker-hour.
- Dividing by workers when the question gives hours and the hours changed.
- Reading higher output as higher productivity.
- Placing diminishing returns in the long run.
- Saying total product falls when marginal product falls.
- Confusing diminishing returns with decreasing returns to scale.
- Listing benefits of division of labour without linking them to the firm in the question.
- Explaining money without linking it to specialisation.
- Naming a change in cost per unit without saying that input prices are held constant.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are diminishing returns and decreasing returns to scale the same?**
No. Diminishing returns happen in the short run when one factor is varied and others are fixed. Decreasing returns to scale happen in the long run when all inputs increase together and output rises by a smaller proportion.

**Does total product fall when marginal product falls?**
No. When marginal product falls but is still positive, total product keeps rising, just more slowly.

**How are diminishing returns linked to costs?**
With a constant wage, marginal cost is the wage divided by marginal product, so falling marginal product makes marginal cost rise.

**What assumption links returns to scale to cost per unit?**
Input prices are held constant. Then increasing returns to scale lower long-run cost per unit and decreasing returns raise it.
