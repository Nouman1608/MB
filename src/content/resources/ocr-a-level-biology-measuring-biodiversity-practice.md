These questions cover section 4.2.1(a) to (e) of the OCR A Level Biology A (H420) specification, Version 4.1 (April 2026), for first assessment in 2025: levels of biodiversity, sampling, richness and evenness, Simpson's Index of Diversity and genetic diversity. Revise first with the [study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-biology-measuring-biodiversity/) and the [revision notes](/resources/ocr-a-level-biology-measuring-biodiversity-revision-notes/). See also the [OCR A Level Biology hub](/boards/ocr/a-level/biology/), the [checklist](/checklists/ocr/a-level/biology/) and the [free diagnostics](/diagnostics/). Simpson's Index is D = 1 − Σ(n/N)².

## Questions

**1.** State what is meant by habitat biodiversity, species biodiversity and genetic biodiversity, and give an example of each. **[3]**

**2.** Name the most suitable sampling technique for each of the following. **[3]**

**(a)** Ground-living beetles in a woodland.
**(b)** Small insects on the leaves of a bush, collected alive without damage.
**(c)** Flying insects in long meadow grass.

**3.** Describe how you would use random sampling with quadrats to estimate the abundance of a plant species in a field measuring 20 m by 10 m. **[4]**

**4.** A student wants to find how the plants change from the strandline to the back of a sand dune. Explain why systematic sampling is more suitable than random sampling, and state one limitation of systematic sampling. **[4]**

**5.** Ten random quadrats, each 0.5 m by 0.5 m, were placed in a lawn of area 600 m². The numbers of plantain plants were 4, 7, 2, 6, 5, 3, 8, 5, 4 and 6. Estimate the number of plantain plants in the lawn. **[3]**

**6.** A sample from a stream contained five invertebrate species, with 34, 21, 12, 8 and 5 individuals. Calculate Simpson's Index of Diversity (D). Give your answer to 3 significant figures. **[5]**

**7.** Two grassland sites were sampled in the same way. Each sample had 80 invertebrates in six species.

| Species | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Site A | 22 | 18 | 15 | 12 | 8 | 5 |
| Site B | 60 | 6 | 5 | 4 | 3 | 2 |

**(a)** The value of D for site A is 0.80. Calculate D for site B to 2 decimal places. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain the difference between the two values of D, although both sites have the same species richness. **[3]**

**8.** In a captive population of an endangered bird, 55 gene loci were studied. Twenty-two loci were polymorphic. Ten years later, after a fall in the number of breeding birds, 14 of the same loci were polymorphic.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of polymorphic loci in the first study. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage decrease in the number of polymorphic loci. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. **[2]**

**9.** A rare breed of pig is kept in three herds. The table shows the loci examined and the number that were polymorphic.

| Herd | Loci examined | Polymorphic loci |
|---|---|---|
| A | 40 | 14 |
| B | 36 | 9 |
| C | 40 | 23 |

**(a)** Calculate the proportion of polymorphic loci in each herd, as a percentage. **[3]**
**(b)** Which herd is most at risk from a new disease? Explain your answer. **[3]**
**(c)** Suggest two ways in which the herds could be managed to maintain genetic diversity. **[2]**

**10.** A student sampled a woodland and a meadow once each. The woodland sample had six species, with 12, 9, 7, 6, 4 and 2 individuals. The meadow sample had four species, with 40, 25, 10 and 5 individuals.

**(a)** Calculate D for each habitat to 3 significant figures. **[4]**
**(b)** The student concludes that woodland is always more diverse than meadow. Evaluate this conclusion. **[3]**

**11.** Explain why Simpson's Index of Diversity gives more information about a habitat than a count of the species present. **[3]**

**12.** Plan an investigation to compare the biodiversity of ground-living invertebrates in a woodland edge and an open meadow. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Habitat biodiversity is the range of different habitats in an area, for example sand dunes, woodland, meadows or streams [1]. Species biodiversity is the variety of species in a habitat, made up of species richness and evenness [1]. Genetic biodiversity is the variety of alleles within a species, for example the different breeds of sheep [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming a type of each level, not just defining it, is the safest way to show you can tell them apart; "genetic" must refer to alleles or genes within one species.

**2. (a)** Pitfall traps [1].
**(b)** A pooter [1].
**(c)** A sweep net [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Match the technique to where the animal lives and how it moves. Answers such as "net" without saying sweep, or "quadrat" for moving insects, are too vague or unsuitable.

**3.** Lay two tape measures at right angles along two sides of the field [1]. Take pairs of random digits or generator output as coordinates, within 20 m along one side and 10 m along the other [1]. Place the quadrat at each coordinate and count the plants of the species inside it [1]. Take many quadrats, calculate the mean per quadrat, and scale up to the field area [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Say how the positions are chosen at random (random numbers as coordinates). Throwing a quadrat is not a reliable random method.

**4.** The conditions change along the dune, so a belt or line transect with samples at regular intervals shows the gradient [1]. Random samples might cluster in one zone and miss others [1]. The same effort at each distance allows comparison between zones [1]. A limitation is that samples are not random, so a pattern in the habitat at the interval chosen could bias the results (or only one line is sampled, which may not represent the whole dune) [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A good answer links systematic sampling to a gradient. "It is easier" alone gains no credit.

**5.** Total = 50, so mean = 50 ÷ 10 = 5.0 plants per quadrat [1]. Quadrat area = 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25 m², so density = 5.0 ÷ 0.25 = 20 plants per m² [1]. Population = 20 × 600 = **12 000 plants** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Work out the quadrat area from its sides before dividing; using 0.5 m² instead of 0.25 m² halves the answer. State that this is an estimate.

**6.** N = 34 + 21 + 12 + 8 + 5 = 80 [1]. The fractions are 0.425, 0.2625, 0.15, 0.1 and 0.0625 [1]. The squares are 0.1806, 0.0689, 0.0225, 0.01 and 0.0039 [1]. Σ(n/N)² = 1830 ÷ 6400 = 0.2859 [1]. D = 1 − 0.2859 = **0.714** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Show each stage, since rounding the squares early can shift the final digit. Subtracting from 1 is a separate step that is easy to omit.

**7. (a)** N = 80 and Σ(n/N)² = (3600 + 36 + 25 + 16 + 9 + 4) ÷ 6400 = 3690 ÷ 6400 = 0.5766 [2]. D = 1 − 0.5766 = **0.42** [1].
**(b)** The two sites have the same richness, so the difference is due to evenness [1]. In site B, species 1 makes up 75% of individuals, so the numbers are very uneven [1]. In site A the individuals are spread more evenly, so D is higher (0.80) and site A is more diverse [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* For an explanation, state which of richness and evenness is the same, which differs, and use figures from the table.

**8. (a)** 22 ÷ 55 = 0.40 [1], so **40%** [1].
**(b)** Decrease = 22 − 14 = 8 loci, and 8 ÷ 22 × 100 [1] = **36.4%** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A percentage decrease is calculated relative to the original number of polymorphic loci. Dividing by 55 gives percentage points, not the percentage decrease.

**9. (a)** A: 14 ÷ 40 = **35.0%** [1]. B: 9 ÷ 36 = **25.0%** [1]. C: 23 ÷ 40 = **57.5%** [1].
**(b)** Herd B [1] has the lowest proportion of polymorphic loci, so the least genetic variation [1]. A new disease is less likely to find some individuals with resistance alleles, so the herd is more likely to be badly affected [1].
**(c)** Any two: exchange breeding animals between herds [1]; use pedigree records to avoid mating between close relatives [1]; increase the number of breeding animals (no further credit). (Maximum 2.) **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b) name the herd and use its figure; link low variation to the chance that some individuals can survive the change.

**10. (a)** Woodland: N = 40, Σ(n/N)² = 330 ÷ 1600 = 0.20625 [1], D = **0.794** [1]. Meadow: N = 80, Σ(n/N)² = 2350 ÷ 6400 = 0.3672 [1], D = **0.633** [1].
**(b)** The woodland has the higher D and more species in this sample, which supports the conclusion for these two samples [1]. "Always" is not justified: one sample of each, on one occasion, with different sample sizes (40 and 80 individuals), may not represent the habitats [1]. More random samples at different times, using the same effort for each, would give means and allow a statistical test [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Give a verdict on the claim, then say what the data support and what limits the conclusion.

**11.** Species richness only counts the species, so a habitat with one dominant species and a habitat with evenly spread species can have the same count [1]. D uses the number of individuals of each species, so it takes both richness and evenness into account [1]. A high or low D can then be interpreted, for example to compare habitats or detect a disturbance [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Mention evenness explicitly. "D is more accurate" is too vague.

**12.** Choose several sites in each habitat using random coordinates (or stratified sampling if the habitat has distinct zones) [1]. Use pitfall traps sunk level with the soil, with covers against rain [1]. Use the same number of traps and the same trapping time in both habitats [1]. Control other variables, such as trap type, season, weather and time of day [1]. Identify the animals with a key and count the individuals of each species [1]. Release the animals unharmed, and handle them with care [1]. Calculate richness and D for each habitat [1]. Compare the means, with a Student's t-test (or the standard deviation to show spread) [1]. Repeat on several occasions, because a single trapping period may not be representative [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Plans earn credit for specific, testable detail, such as number of traps, trap duration and the calculation made. A list of general phrases such as "keep it fair" does not.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a method as random without saying how the positions are chosen.
- Using one technique and claiming it samples the whole range of organisms.
- Failing to square n/N, or forgetting to subtract from 1.
- Using the number of species as N instead of the total number of individuals.
- Rounding intermediate values and then giving the answer to too many significant figures.
- Saying equal richness means equal diversity.
- Discussing a high D without mentioning evenness.
- Dividing by the wrong total when calculating a proportion of polymorphic loci.
- Confusing percentage points with percentage change.
- Treating one sample on one day as proof about a whole habitat.
