These questions cover section 4.4.3 (Superposition) of the OCR Advanced GCE in Physics A specification (H556), Version 3.0 (March 2026), for first assessment in 2017. Use c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ and a speed of sound of 340 m s⁻¹. Questions 8, 9 and 11 use d sin θ = nλ and gratings, which the specification says are assessed at A level only. Revise first with the [study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-physics-superposition-interference-and-the-double-slit/) or the [revision notes](/resources/ocr-a-level-physics-superposition-interference-and-the-double-slit-revision-notes/).

## Questions

**1.** Two pulses travel towards each other along a rope. One is a triangular pulse 6.0 cm above the rest line of the rope. The other is a triangular pulse 4.0 cm below it.

**(a)** State the principle of superposition. **[2]**
**(b)** State the displacement of the rope when the centres of the two pulses coincide. **[1]**
**(c)** Describe what happens to the pulses after they have passed through each other. **[1]**

**2.** Two waves of the same frequency have displacements y₁ = 4.0 sin θ and y₂ = 4.0 sin(θ + 120°), in cm.

**(a)** Find the resultant displacement at θ = 30° and at θ = 90°. **[3]**
**(b)** The largest resultant displacement is 4.0 cm. Explain why the interference is neither fully constructive nor fully destructive. **[1]**

**3.**
**(a)** Define coherent sources. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why two filament lamps side by side do not produce interference fringes. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why two slits lit by one laser beam can act as coherent sources. **[1]**

**4.** Two loudspeakers are connected in phase to one signal generator, which produces a note of 1360 Hz. A microphone is moved to point P, which is 3.15 m from one speaker and 3.90 m from the other. It is then moved to point R, which is 3.00 m and 3.625 m from them.

**(a)** Calculate the wavelength of the sound. **[1]**
**(b)** State and justify whether the sound at P is loud or quiet. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the phase difference between the two waves at R. **[2]**
**(d)** One connection to one speaker is now reversed. State what is heard at P. **[1]**

**5.** A microwave transmitter shines on a metal plate with two narrow gaps 15.0 cm apart. A probe receiver is moved along a line parallel to the plate, 1.20 m beyond it.

**(a)** Describe how you would find the distance between adjacent maxima as accurately as possible. **[3]**
**(b)** Five spacings between adjacent maxima add up to 1.00 m. Calculate the wavelength. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the frequency of the microwaves. **[1]**

**6.** A double slit has slit separation 0.45 mm. A screen is 1.85 m away.

**(a)** Light of wavelength 470 nm is used. Calculate the fringe spacing. **[2]**
**(b)** The light is changed to wavelength 650 nm. Calculate the new fringe spacing and state how the pattern changes. **[2]**
**(c)** Describe the pattern seen with white light. **[2]**

**7.** A student uses slits 0.60 mm apart and measures the fringe spacing x for different slit-to-screen distances D.

| D / m | 1.00 | 1.50 | 2.00 | 2.50 | 3.00 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| x / mm | 1.03 | 1.56 | 2.06 | 2.59 | 3.09 |

**(a)** State what graph to plot and what its gradient represents. **[2]**
**(b)** Use the data to find the wavelength of the light. **[3]**

**8.** (A level only) A grating has 240 lines per mm. Light of wavelength 560 nm is incident normally.

**(a)** Calculate the grating spacing. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the angle of the third-order maximum. **[2]**
**(c)** Find the largest order of maximum that can be seen. **[2]**

**9.** (A level only) A laser beam passes at normal incidence through a grating with 1200 lines per mm. A screen is 0.800 m from the grating. The first-order maxima are 0.664 m either side of the central maximum.

**(a)** Calculate the angle of the first-order maximum. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the wavelength. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the small-angle approximation cannot be used here. **[1]**
**(d)** Suggest one way to reduce the uncertainty in y. **[1]**

**10.** Derive λ = ax/D for a double slit, where a is the slit separation, D the distance to the screen and x the fringe spacing. State the condition for the result to be valid. **[6]**

**11.** (A level only) A grating has 360 lines per mm. White light has wavelengths from 410 nm to 690 nm.

**(a)** Calculate the grating spacing. **[1]**
**(b)** Show that the second-order spectrum overlaps the third-order spectrum. **[4]**
**(c)** Explain why Young's double-slit experiment gave a classical confirmation of the wave nature of light. **[3]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** When two waves meet, the resultant displacement is the sum of the individual displacements [1], taking account of their directions (signs) [1]. **(b)** 6.0 − 4.0 = **+2.0 cm** (above the rest line) [1]. **(c)** Each pulse continues with its original shape and speed [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Waves add" with no mention of direction or sign leaves out the idea that makes the 2.0 cm in part (b) possible.

**2. (a)** At 30°: y₁ = 4.0 sin 30° = 2.0 cm and y₂ = 4.0 sin 150° = 2.0 cm [1], so the resultant is **4.0 cm** [1]. At 90°: y₁ = 4.0 cm and y₂ = 4.0 sin 210° = −2.0 cm, so the resultant is **2.0 cm** [1]. **(b)** The phase difference is 120°, which is neither 0° (constructive) nor 180° (destructive), so the amplitude is between 0 and 8.0 cm [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Sine values at 150° and 210° are where signs go wrong, so write each term out before adding.

**3. (a)** Sources with a constant phase difference [1]. **(b)** The lamps emit light in random short bursts, so the phase difference between them changes randomly and rapidly [1]. No stable pattern of maxima and minima forms, so the screen looks uniformly lit [1]. **(c)** Both slits are lit by the same wavefront, so any change in the laser affects both equally and their phase difference stays constant [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Same frequency" alone is incomplete. Say the phase difference is constant.

**4. (a)** λ = v/f = 340 / 1360 = **0.25 m** [1]. **(b)** Path difference = 3.90 − 3.15 = 0.75 m [1] = 3.0λ, a whole number of wavelengths, so the waves are in phase and the sound is **loud** [1]. **(c)** Path difference = 3.625 − 3.00 = 0.625 m = 2.5λ [1]. Phase difference = 2.5 × 2π = **5π rad** (15.7 rad, 900°) [1]. **(d)** The speakers are now in antiphase, so P, with a path difference of 3λ, gives destructive interference: **quiet** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Give the path difference in wavelengths, then the conclusion.

**5. (a)** Use one transmitter so the two gaps are coherent [1]. Move the probe slowly along a line parallel to the plate and note where the meter reading is greatest [1]. Measure the distance across several maxima with a ruler and divide by the number of spacings, repeating to check [1]. **(b)** x = 1.00 / 5 = 0.200 m [1]. λ = ax/D = (0.150 × 0.200) / 1.20 = **0.0250 m** [1]. **(c)** f = c/λ = 3.00 × 10⁸ / 0.0250 = **1.2 × 10¹⁰ Hz** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Count the gaps between maxima, not the maxima.

**6. (a)** x = λD/a [1] = (470 × 10⁻⁹ × 1.85) / (0.45 × 10⁻³) = **1.9 × 10⁻³ m** (1.93 mm) [1]. **(b)** x is proportional to λ, so the fringes are further apart [1]: x = (650 × 10⁻⁹ × 1.85) / 0.45 × 10⁻³ = **2.7 × 10⁻³ m** (2.67 mm) [1]. **(c)** The central fringe is white [1]; the other fringes are coloured spectra, with blue nearer the centre than red [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Convert mm and nm to metres before substituting. Check the answer's size against typical fringe widths of about a millimetre.

**7. (a)** Plot x against D, which gives a straight line through the origin [1]. The gradient is λ/a [1]. **(b)** Gradient = (3.09 − 1.03) × 10⁻³ / (3.00 − 1.00) = 1.03 × 10⁻³ [1]. λ = gradient × a = 1.03 × 10⁻³ × 0.60 × 10⁻³ [1] = **6.2 × 10⁻⁷ m** (618 nm) [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Read the gradient using two well-separated points on the best-fit line, not neighbouring data points.

**8. (a)** d = 1 / (240 × 10³) = **4.17 × 10⁻⁶ m** [1]. **(b)** sin θ = nλ/d = (3 × 560 × 10⁻⁹) / 4.167 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.403 [1], θ = **23.8°** [1]. **(c)** d/λ = 4.167 × 10⁻⁶ / 560 × 10⁻⁹ = 7.44 [1]; the highest order is **7** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Rounding 7.44 up to 8 gives an order needing sin θ above 1.

**9. (a)** tan θ = 0.664 / 0.800 = 0.830 [1], so θ = **39.7°** [1]. **(b)** d = 1 / (1.2 × 10⁶) = 8.33 × 10⁻⁷ m, [1], then λ = d sin θ / n = 8.33 × 10⁻⁷ × sin 39.7° = **5.3 × 10⁻⁷ m** [1]. **(c)** At 39.7°, sin θ = 0.639 but tan θ = 0.830, so sin θ is not close to tan θ [1]. **(d)** Measure the separation of the two first-order spots and halve it, which avoids locating the central spot [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Using y/L in place of sin θ gives about 6.9 × 10⁻⁷ m, a clearly different value; show the angle step.

**10.** For a point P at angle θ to the central line, the path difference between waves from the slits is a sin θ [1]. A bright fringe needs path difference nλ, so a sin θ = nλ [1]. For small θ, sin θ ≈ tan θ = y/D, where y is the distance from the centre [1]. So y = nλD/a [1]. Neighbouring fringes (n and n + 1) differ by x = λD/a [1]. Rearranging gives **λ = ax/D**, valid when a ≪ D (so angles are small) [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* State the approximation at the step where it is used, and give the validity condition.

**11. (a)** d = 1 / (360 × 10³) = **2.78 × 10⁻⁶ m** [1]. **(b)** Second-order red: sin θ = 2 × 690 × 10⁻⁹ / 2.78 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.497 [1], θ = 29.8° [1]. Third-order violet: sin θ = 3 × 410 × 10⁻⁹ / 2.78 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.443 [1], θ = 26.3°, which is smaller than 29.8°, so part of the third order lies within the second [1]. **(c)** Interference with dark fringes needs light from two sources to cancel [1], which is what waves can do by superposition [1]. Young's fringes therefore supported Huygens's wave model and not a purely particle model such as Newton's [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* "Overlap" needs a comparison of two angles, not just a statement.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining coherence as "same phase" rather than a constant phase difference.
- Giving a conclusion without the path difference in wavelengths.
- Forgetting to swap the conditions when the sources are in antiphase.
- Counting maxima instead of the spacings between them.
- Leaving a in mm or λ in nm in a substitution.
- Using sin θ ≈ tan θ for grating angles.
- Rounding the highest grating order up.
- Deriving λ = ax/D without stating that a ≪ D.
