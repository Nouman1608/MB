## Section A

**1.** Which case recognised a "negative" freedom not to be compelled to join a trade union? A: ASLEF v United Kingdom. B: Young, James and Webster v United Kingdom. C: Redfearn v United Kingdom. D: Appleby v United Kingdom. **[1]**

**2.** Which word in Article 11.1 limits the kind of assembly protected? A: lawful. B: public. C: peaceful. D: authorised. **[1]**

**3.** State the two freedoms protected by Article 11.1. **[2]**

**4.** State what was decided in DPP v Jones (1999). **[2]**

**5.** Identify the positive obligation recognised in Plattform "Ärzte für das Leben" v Austria (1988). **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Explain how the European Court decides whether an assembly is "peaceful", referring to two cases. **[4]**

**7.** Explain why the House of Lords held the police action in R (Laporte) v Chief Constable of Gloucestershire (2006) to be unlawful. **[4]**

**8.** Explain the difference between the decisions in ASLEF v United Kingdom (2007) and Redfearn v United Kingdom (2012). **[4]**

**9.** Explain the significance of DPP v Ziegler (2021) for protesters charged with obstructing the highway. **[3]**

**10.** *Campaigners want to hand out leaflets inside a privately owned retail park about plans to close a local library. The owners refuse permission. The campaigners can use the pavement outside, the town square and social media.* Suggest why the owners' refusal is unlikely to breach Article 11. **[3]**

**11.** *Workers at a factory form a new union. The employer tells them anyone who joins will lose their annual bonus. Separately, Josh, an existing worker, is told he must join the union his employer recognises or be dismissed.* Advise the workers and Josh whether their Article 11 rights are engaged. **[6]**

**12.** *A peaceful march of 2,000 people against a new road is joined by about 30 people who start smashing shop windows. Police arrest everyone in the front section of the march, including Asha, who had been walking peacefully with a banner. Asha is later fined by her professional body for "associating with criminal behaviour".* Consider whether Asha's rights under Article 11.1 have been interfered with. **[8]**

---

## Answers

**1.** B: Young, James and Webster v United Kingdom. **[1]**

**2.** C: peaceful. **[1]**

**3.** Freedom of **peaceful assembly** [1] and freedom of **association with others**, including forming and joining trade unions [1].

**4.** A **peaceful, non-obstructive assembly on the highway** [1] can be a **reasonable use of the highway**, so it is not automatically a trespass [1].

**5.** The state must take **reasonable and appropriate measures** to enable lawful demonstrations to proceed peacefully [1], including protection from **counter-demonstrators**; it is an obligation of means, not a guarantee of results [1].

**6.** An assembly falls outside Article 11 only where its organisers or participants have **violent intentions** [1]. A peaceful individual does **not lose protection because of violence by others**: in **Ezelin v France (1991)** a lawyer could not be disciplined for attending a demonstration where others committed offences [1]. **Disruption alone does not make an assembly non-peaceful** [1]: in **Kudrevičius v Lithuania (2015)** road blockades were still a peaceful assembly, although deliberate serious disruption was not at the core of the right and the convictions were justified [1].

**7.** The police stopped coaches carrying protesters to an RAF base and escorted them back to London [1]. A breach of the peace was **not imminent** at the point of interception, so the common law power to prevent a breach of the peace did not arise [1]. The action was also **premature and indiscriminate**, affecting everyone on the coaches whether or not they posed any risk [1], and so was a **disproportionate** interference with Articles 10 and 11 [1].

**8.** In **ASLEF** the Court protected the **union's** freedom of association [1]: a union must be free to choose its members, so UK law preventing it expelling a BNP member was a breach [1]. In **Redfearn** the Court protected an **individual's** freedom of association [1]: a bus driver dismissed for BNP membership had no unfair dismissal remedy, so the UK failed in its **positive obligation** to protect him [1].

**9.** Protesters charged with obstructing the highway can rely on a **lawful excuse** [1]. The Supreme Court held that whether there is a lawful excuse requires a **proportionality assessment** under Articles 10 and 11 on the facts [1], so peaceful, targeted protest causing limited obstruction may be lawful; the protesters' **acquittals were restored** [1].

**10.** In **Appleby v UK (2003)** exclusion from a privately owned shopping centre did not breach Articles 10 or 11 [1]. Article 11 does **not give a right to protest on private land** [1] where, as here, the campaigners have **other effective ways** to reach the public, such as the pavement, town square and social media [1].

**11.** The workers are exercising freedom of **association**, which expressly includes the right to **form and join trade unions** [1]. Withdrawing a bonus to deter membership is a **penalty for joining**, which may engage the state's positive obligation to protect union membership in private employment [1], and the employer's conduct would need justification [1]. For Josh, **Young, James and Webster v UK (1981)** recognises a **negative freedom** not to be compelled to join a union [1]; a threat of dismissal for refusing to join strikes at the substance of the freedom [1]. The state is responsible if domestic law permits this, so both have arguable Article 11 claims [1].

**12.** One mark per developed point, to a maximum of 8:
- The march is a **peaceful assembly**; the organisers and the great majority have no violent intention [1].
- The 30 window-smashers are **not peaceful** and are outside Article 11 protection [1].
- Under **Ezelin**, Asha does **not lose protection** because of violence by others while she stays peaceful [1].
- Her **arrest** is an interference with her assembly right; it must have a legal basis and be justified under 11.2 [1].
- Arresting a whole section **indiscriminately** is hard to justify; compare **Laporte**, where action against people posing no threat was disproportionate [1].
- The police also had a **positive obligation** to protect the peaceful march from the violent minority (Plattform "Ärzte für das Leben") [1].
- The professional body's fine closely mirrors **Ezelin**, where a professional penalty for peaceful attendance was a breach [1]; if the body is a public authority or the state permits the penalty, Article 11 is engaged.
- **Conclusion**: Asha's rights under 11.1 have been interfered with by both the arrest and the fine, and it is unlikely either could be justified under 11.2 [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a protest non-peaceful simply because it was disruptive or because others were violent.
- Forgetting the positive obligation to protect lawful assemblies.
- Leaving out the association half of Article 11, including the negative freedom.
- Confusing whose freedom was protected in ASLEF (the union) and Redfearn (the individual).
- Citing Laporte without explaining the imminence requirement.
