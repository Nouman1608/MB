Condensed recall for Unit S1, section 1: "The basic ideas of mathematical modelling as applied in probability and statistics." For worked examples and fuller explanation, use the [mathematical models study guide](/resources/edexcel-a-level-mathematics-yma01-s1-mathematical-models-edexcel-alevel-maths/).

## The core definition

A **mathematical model** is a simplification of a real-world situation, expressed mathematically, that can be used to make predictions. In S1 the model is usually a probability distribution or a set of probabilities. Because it is a probability model, single observations are expected to vary; it is the overall pattern that tests the model.

## The modelling cycle

```
1  real-world problem recognised
2  assumptions made -> model devised
3  model used to make predictions
4  experimental data collected
5  predictions compared with data
6  model refined (change an assumption or parameter) -> back to 3
```

Memory hook: **problem, assume, predict, collect, compare, refine**. In a six-step answer the comparison and the refinement are the steps most often missing.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Mathematical model | Simplified mathematical description of a real situation, used to predict |
| Assumption | A simplifying statement the model relies on, such as independence or equal likelihood |
| Parameter | A number that fixes a model, such as a probability, a mean or a standard deviation |
| Prediction | What the model says should happen: a probability, expected frequency, mean or spread |
| Observed data | Results actually recorded from the real situation |
| Refinement | A change to the model's assumptions or parameters to improve its fit |
| Deterministic model | Gives a single exact outcome; no randomness |
| Probability model | Gives the probability of each possible outcome; outcomes vary |
| Expected frequency | Number of trials × probability under the model |
| Relative frequency | Observed frequency ÷ total trials; an estimate of a probability |

## Common assumptions and how to question them

| Assumption | Ask yourself |
|---|---|
| Outcomes are equally likely | Is there anything physical or human that could favour some outcomes? |
| Trials are independent | Do items share a cause (same batch of soil, same bus, same machine)? |
| Probability is constant | Could it change over time, with fatigue, wear or the season? |
| The variable is continuous | Is it really measured to the nearest unit? Is that close enough? |
| The distribution is symmetric | Do the data show skew? Are mean, median and mode close? |
| Values outside the data still follow the model | Is the prediction far beyond the range tested? |

## Advantages and disadvantages

**Advantages**

- Quick and cheap compared with gathering complete data.
- Simplifies a complex situation so it can be understood.
- Makes predictions about situations not yet observed.
- Parameters can be varied to explore "what if" questions.

**Disadvantages**

- Simplification can leave out an important factor.
- Valid only where its assumptions hold.
- Predictions outside the range of the testing data are unreliable.

## Judging a model against data (no test needed in S1)

Compare three things, with numbers:

- **Location:** observed mean or median against the predicted value.
- **Spread:** observed range, interquartile range or standard deviation against the predicted spread, or simply how far observations stray.
- **Shape:** observed frequencies outcome by outcome against expected frequencies; symmetry against skew.

Then state a conclusion in context: "the model fits well", "the model fits the mean but not the spread", or "outcome B occurs more often than the model predicts".

**Mini example.** A model says a fair coin gives heads with probability 0.5. In 120 tosses there were 66 heads. Expected 60; observed is 6 more. That is a modest difference for 120 tosses, so the data give no strong reason to doubt the model.

## Writing a refinement

A good refinement names exactly what changes and how it is then tested:

- "Replace the equal probabilities with relative frequencies from this experiment, then test the new model on a further 200 spins."
- "Allow the germination probability to depend on the tray's position, then compare predictions with new trays."

Weak refinements, such as "collect more data" or "make it more accurate", earn little on their own.

## Exam traps

- Listing the cycle steps out of order.
- Saying a model is "wrong" because one value differs from the prediction.
- Comparing only means when the data show a difference in spread.
- Giving a refinement that is not specific.
- Testing a refined model on the same data used to build it.
- Writing out of context: name the real objects in every sentence.

## Self-test

1. Define a mathematical model.
2. What is the difference between a deterministic model and a probability model?
3. List the six stages of the modelling cycle in order.
4. Give two advantages of using a mathematical model.
5. Give two disadvantages of using a mathematical model.
6. A die is modelled as fair and rolled 300 times. What is the expected frequency of a six?
7. Name a parameter of a Normal model.
8. Customers at one checkout are modelled as arriving independently. Suggest why this assumption may fail.
9. A refined model's probabilities were estimated from an experiment. Why should it be tested on new data?
10. A model predicts a mean of 32 and the observed mean is 30, but three values are far below the rest. What should your comment mention besides the mean?
11. Why is "collect more data" a weak refinement?
12. Why are predictions far outside the range of tested data unreliable?
