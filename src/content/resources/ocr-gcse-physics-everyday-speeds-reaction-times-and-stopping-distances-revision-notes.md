## Typical speeds (P8.1a)

Learn these as approximate values. A value in the right region is what counts.

| Situation | Speed (m/s) |
|---|---|
| Walking | 1.5 |
| Running | 3 |
| Cycling | 6 |
| Car in town (30 mph) | 13 |
| Car on motorway | 30 |
| Aircraft cruising | 250 |
| Sound in air | 340 |
| Breeze | 5 |
| Storm-force wind | 25 or more |

Sense-check: walking is about 5 km/h, town traffic is a few tens of km/h, and sound in air is faster than any road vehicle. A wind speed in m/s multiplied by 3.6 gives km/h.

## Estimating accelerations (P8.1b)

| Equation | Meaning |
|---|---|
| a = Δv ÷ t | a in m/s², Δv in m/s, t in s |

Method in steps:

1. Choose a sensible start speed and end speed.
2. Choose a sensible time.
3. Divide the change in speed by the time.
4. Round to one significant figure and compare with a known value.

| Situation | Magnitude (m/s²) |
|---|---|
| Lift, cyclist pulling away | about 1 |
| Car pulling away | 2 to 3 |
| Sprinter at start | 3 to 5 |
| Emergency braking, car | 6 to 8 |
| Free fall | about 10 |

Worked reminder: a train goes from rest to 20 m/s in 40 s. a = 20 ÷ 40 = 0.5 m/s².

## Unit conversion, ratios and rates (P8.1c)

| Convert | Rule |
|---|---|
| mph to m/s | × 1609, then ÷ 3600 |
| m/s to km/h | × 3.6 |
| km/h to m/s | ÷ 3.6 |
| min to s | × 60 |
| h to s | × 3600 |
| km to m | × 1000 |
| cm to m | ÷ 100 |
| g to kg | ÷ 1000 |

Method in steps:

1. Write the quantity with its unit.
2. Multiply or divide by the ratio, keeping units visible.
3. Check the size: does the answer sit in a sensible range for the situation?
4. Round sensibly to 2 or 3 significant figures.

Proportion: at constant speed, distance is directly proportional to time. Twice the time gives twice the distance. A rate is a quantity per unit time, such as metres per second or litres per minute.

Worked reminder: a wind of 25 m/s is 25 × 3.6 = 90 km/h. A cyclist rides 18 km in 45 minutes. Distance = 18,000 m, time = 2700 s, speed = 18,000 ÷ 2700 = 6.7 m/s (2 s.f.).

## Measuring reaction time (P8.1d)

Reaction time: the delay between a stimulus and the start of the response.

| Method | How it works | Main strengths and weaknesses |
|---|---|---|
| Ruler drop | Ruler dropped without warning; distance caught converts to time | Cheap and quick; limited by how precisely the ruler is read, and the dropper may give clues |
| Computer test | Computer records time between stimulus and click | Electronic timing; no timer error; needs equipment |
| Two people, stopwatch | One person starts and stops the clock | Poor: the timer's own reaction time is as large as the value being measured |

Ruler-drop steps:

1. Hold the ruler with its zero level with the top of the thumb, with no contact.
2. Drop without warning.
3. Read the mark at the top of the thumb.
4. Convert the distance to a time using the table or relationship provided.
5. Repeat and take the mean.

Reliability checklist: repeat; calculate the arithmetic mean; check for anomalies; same start position; same hand; change only one variable.

Typical results: about 0.2 s for an alert person on a ruler drop; longer, up to around one second, when tired, distracted or facing a complex decision.

Worked reminder: distances 12, 15, 14 and 15 cm give a mean of 56 ÷ 4 = 14 cm, which is a reaction time of roughly 0.17 s from a free-fall table (14 cm falls in about 0.17 s).

## Stopping distance and safety (P8.1e)

Stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance.

Thinking distance = speed × reaction time.

| Factor | Thinking distance | Braking distance |
|---|---|---|
| Higher speed | increases | increases (much more) |
| Tiredness | increases | no effect |
| Alcohol or drugs | increases | no effect |
| Distraction | increases | no effect |
| Wet or icy road | no effect | increases |
| Worn tyres | no effect | increases |
| Worn brakes | no effect | increases |
| Greater mass | no effect | increases |

Why braking distance rises with speed: kinetic energy depends on speed squared. With the same braking force, work done = force × distance, so double the speed gives four times the braking distance.

Why the road surface matters: less friction between the tyre and the road means a smaller maximum braking force, so the vehicle takes more distance to lose the same kinetic energy.

Safety implications to link to each factor:

- Lower speed limits where pedestrians are present.
- Bigger gaps to the vehicle ahead in wet or icy conditions.
- Rules against drink-driving, drug-driving and phone use.
- Checks of tyres and brakes.

Worked reminder for thinking distance: a driver at 90 km/h has a reaction time of 0.60 s. Speed = 90 ÷ 3.6 = 25 m/s. Thinking distance = 25 × 0.60 = 15 m. If the driver is distracted and the reaction time rises to 0.90 s, the thinking distance becomes 22.5 m, which is 7.5 m further.

Method for an "explain" question on stopping distance:

1. Say which distance is affected, thinking or braking.
2. Name the cause: reaction time for thinking distance; braking force or kinetic energy for braking distance.
3. Link the cause to the factor given.
4. State the consequence for stopping distance and safety.

## Must-know distinctions

- Thinking distance depends on the driver; braking distance depends on the vehicle and the road.
- Speed affects both distances, but not in the same way: thinking distance doubles, braking distance quadruples.
- Time to convert: seconds in SI calculations, not minutes or hours.
- Mean, not a single value, for reaction time.

## Quick self-test

1. State a typical cycling speed in m/s.
2. Convert 144 km/h to m/s.
3. Convert 25 m/s to km/h.
4. Convert 20 mph to m/s, given 1 mile = 1609 m. Give 2 significant figures.
5. A scooter goes from rest to 15 m/s in 6.0 s. Calculate its acceleration.
6. Four reaction times are 0.24, 0.20, 0.28 and 0.24 s. Calculate the mean.
7. A car travels at 20 m/s and the driver's reaction time is 0.50 s. Calculate the thinking distance.
8. A jogger runs 3.0 km in 15 minutes. Calculate the speed in m/s, to 2 significant figures.
9. State two factors that increase thinking distance but not braking distance.
10. A car's speed doubles. State what happens to its braking distance for the same braking force, and why.
11. Give one reason why a computer reaction test is better than two people using a stopwatch.

### Answers

1. About 6 m/s (accept values from about 4 to 8 m/s).
2. 144 ÷ 3.6 = 40 m/s.
3. 25 × 3.6 = 90 km/h.
4. 20 × 1609 ÷ 3600 = 8.9 m/s.
5. 15 ÷ 6.0 = 2.5 m/s².
6. 0.96 ÷ 4 = 0.24 s.
7. 20 × 0.50 = 10 m.
8. 3000 m ÷ 900 s = 3.3 m/s.
9. Any two from tiredness, alcohol, drugs, distraction.
10. It becomes four times as large, because kinetic energy depends on speed squared, and the same force has to do four times the work.
11. The timing is done electronically, so there is no extra human reaction time in the measurement.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing km/h by 60 or 10 instead of 3.6.
- Leaving minutes or hours in a speed calculation.
- Placing alcohol, tiredness or distraction under braking distance.
- Placing wet roads, worn tyres or worn brakes under thinking distance.
- Saying braking distance "doubles" when speed doubles.
- Giving one ruler drop as the reaction time, with no mean.
- Writing "faster reaction" when the stem asks about the effect of tiredness, which makes the reaction slower.
- Estimating an acceleration with an unreasonable time, such as 0 to 27 m/s in 1 s for a car.
- Not linking a factor to its effect: a wet road lowers friction and so the braking force, and a heavier vehicle has more kinetic energy to remove.
- Forgetting the unit, or giving m when the question asks for a time in s.
