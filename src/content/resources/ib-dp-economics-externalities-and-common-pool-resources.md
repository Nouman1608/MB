## Socially optimum output

A market left alone reflects only the **private** costs and benefits of the people trading. Economists add the effect on everyone else to get **social** values:

- MSC (marginal social cost) is the producer's own marginal cost, MPC, plus the extra cost borne by others.
- MSB (marginal social benefit) is the buyer's own marginal benefit, MPB, plus the extra benefit enjoyed by others.

The socially optimum output is where **MSB = MSC**, the point on a diagram where the two curves cross. This is **allocative efficiency**: every unit that is worth more to society than it costs is made, and no other unit is. At that output community surplus (consumer surplus plus producer surplus, adjusted for external effects) is as large as it can be.

An **externality** is a side effect of buying or selling that lands on an outsider to the deal, who is neither paid for a benefit nor compensated for a cost. With no externality, MPB = MSB and MPC = MSC, so the free market reaches the social optimum. With an externality the market output differs from the optimum. That gap is a **market failure**.

## Negative externalities and welfare loss

A negative externality of **production** arises when making a good harms others. Take the fictional country Veltria, where a dye works in a river valley (output in thousand litres a week, prices in Veltrian crowns, VC, per litre).

- Demand (MPB = MSB): P = 90 − 3Q
- Supply (MPC): P = 10 + 2Q
- Marginal external cost of river pollution: 15 VC per litre, so MSC = 25 + 2Q

**Market equilibrium:** 90 − 3Q = 10 + 2Q, so 5Q = 80, Q = 16 and P = 42 VC.

**Social optimum:** 90 − 3Q = 25 + 2Q, so 5Q = 65, Q = 13 and P = 51 VC.

The market overproduces by 3 thousand litres.

Key points to plot (quantity, price in VC):

| Quantity | Demand (MSB) | MPC | MSC |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 90 | 10 | 25 |
| 13 | 51 | 36 | 51 |
| 16 | 42 | 42 | 57 |

Demand slopes down; MPC and MSC slope up, with MSC a constant 15 above MPC. MSC cuts demand at Q = 13 (price 51), and MPC cuts demand at Q = 16 (price 42).

Label the axes (price or cost, quantity), the MPC and MSC curves (MSC above MPC), the demand curve, the market output where MPC meets demand, the social optimum where MSC meets demand, and the **welfare loss triangle** between the two outputs. The triangle exists because units between Q = 13 and Q = 16 cost society more than they are worth.

**HL only: welfare loss.** The triangle has base 16 − 13 = 3 (thousand litres). Its height is the gap between MSC and MSB at the market output: MSC = 25 + 2 × 16 = 57 and MSB = 90 − 3 × 16 = 42, a gap of 15, which is the marginal external cost. Area = ½ × 3 × 15 = **22.5 thousand VC**.

A negative externality of **consumption** arises when using a good harms others. In the fictional city of Calder, car journeys cause congestion and fumes (thousand journeys a day):

- MPB: P = 48 − 2Q
- MPC: P = 6 + Q
- Marginal external cost: 9 VC, so MSB = MPB − 9 = 39 − 2Q (the social benefit is lower than the private benefit)

Market: 48 − 2Q = 6 + Q gives Q = 14, P = 20 VC. Optimum: 39 − 2Q = 6 + Q gives Q = 11, P = 17 VC. On the diagram, MSB lies **below** MPB, and the welfare loss triangle sits between 11 and 14. **HL only:** at Q = 14, MPC = 20 and MSB = 39 − 28 = 11, a gap of 9, so the loss is ½ × 3 × 9 = **13.5 thousand VC**.

**Demerit goods** are goods that people consume too much of, because they underestimate the harm to themselves or because others are harmed too. Tobacco is the usual example. A demerit good often involves a negative externality of consumption, so its diagram looks like the Calder one.

## Positive externalities and welfare loss

A positive externality of **consumption** gives benefits to others. In the fictional country Norland, adult evening literacy courses (thousand places a term, prices in Norland marks, NM):

- MPB (demand): P = 80 − 2Q
- MPC (supply): P = 20 + Q
- Marginal external benefit: 12 NM, so MSB = 92 − 2Q

Market: 80 − 2Q = 20 + Q gives Q = 20, P = 40 NM. Optimum: 92 − 2Q = 20 + Q gives Q = 24, P = 44 NM. The market **underproduces** by 4 thousand places. On the diagram MSB lies **above** MPB. **HL only:** at Q = 20, MSB = 92 − 40 = 52 and MPC = 40, a gap of 12. Welfare loss = ½ × 4 × 12 = **24 thousand NM**.

A positive externality of **production** benefits others as a by-product of supply. A hillside orchard in Norland feeds a neighbour's bees. Suppose demand is P = 70 − 2Q, MPC = 10 + 2Q and the marginal external benefit is 8 NM, so MSC = 2 + 2Q (the social cost is lower than the private cost). Market: Q = 15, P = 40 NM. Optimum: 70 − 2Q = 2 + 2Q, so Q = 17, P = 36 NM. On the diagram MSC lies **below** MPC. **HL only:** at Q = 15, MSB = 40 and MSC = 32, a gap of 8, so the loss is ½ × 2 × 8 = **8 thousand NM**.

**Merit goods** are goods that people consume too little of, because they undervalue the benefit to themselves or because others benefit too. Education and vaccination are standard examples. A merit good often has a positive externality of consumption, so it is drawn like the literacy case.

| Type | Curve that moves | Market output | Welfare loss |
|---|---|---|---|
| Negative, production | MSC above MPC | Too high | Triangle between outputs |
| Negative, consumption | MSB below MPB | Too high | Triangle between outputs |
| Positive, production | MSC below MPC | Too low | Triangle between outputs |
| Positive, consumption | MSB above MPB | Too low | Triangle between outputs |

## Common pool resources

A **common pool** (common access) resource is **rivalrous but non-excludable**. One person's use leaves less for others, yet nobody can easily be kept out. Open fisheries, shared grazing land and groundwater are examples.

Each user gains the full benefit of an extra unit taken but shares the cost of depletion with everyone. So users take too much. This is the **tragedy of the commons**: individually rational use destroys the resource that all depend on. The overuse is a negative externality, and **unsustainable production** follows when the stock is harvested faster than it renews.

Fictional example: Lake Orrin in Veltria. Each boat costs 11 thousand VC a season, and each tonne of fish sells for 1 thousand VC.

| Boats | Total catch (tonnes) | Average catch | Extra catch from last boat | Net gain (thousand VC) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 20 | 20 | 20 | 9 |
| 3 | 54 | 18 | 16 | 21 |
| 5 | 80 | 16 | 12 | 25 |
| 6 | 90 | 15 | 10 | 24 |
| 8 | 104 | 13 | 6 | 16 |
| 10 | 110 | 11 | 2 | 0 |

Boats keep entering while the **average** catch exceeds the cost of 11, so open access settles at **10 boats** with net gain of zero. Society gains most where the **extra** catch still covers the cost: the fifth boat adds 12 (above 11), the sixth only 10 (below 11). The best fleet is **5 boats**, with a net gain of 25 thousand VC. The lake is overfished by 5 boats.

## Government responses

- **Indirect (Pigouvian) taxes** equal to the marginal external cost shift MPC up to MSC. In Veltria a tax of 15 VC a litre moves output to 13. Consumers pay 51 VC (9 more than before). Producers keep 51 − 15 = 36 VC (6 less). Revenue is 15 × 13 = 195 thousand VC.
- **Carbon taxes** are a Pigouvian tax on the carbon content of a fuel or a product. Draw the market of the particular polluting industry (such as steel or cement) with MPC shifting up by the tax.
- **Subsidies** work for positive externalities. In Norland, a subsidy of 12 NM per place shifts supply down to 8 + Q. Then 80 − 2Q = 8 + Q gives Q = 24. Students pay 80 − 48 = 32 NM and providers receive 44 NM. The cost to government is 12 × 24 = 288 thousand NM.
- **Legislation and regulation** set limits, bans or standards. On the diagram a legal cap is a vertical line at the permitted quantity, such as 13 in Veltria, which output cannot pass.
- **Education and awareness** campaigns change preferences, shifting demand for demerit goods left or demand for merit goods right.
- **Tradable permits** cap total pollution and let firms trade the right to emit. Two firms each emit 60 units and the cap is 80. Firm A cuts a unit for 3 VC, firm B for 8 VC. Without trading each cuts 20, costing 20 × 3 + 20 × 8 = 220 VC. With trading A cuts 40 and sells 20 permits to B, costing 40 × 3 = 120 VC. The same cap is met for 100 VC less, because the cheapest cuts happen first.
- **International agreements** bind several countries to common targets.
- **Collective self-governance** lets a community agree and police its own rules, such as catch quotas on Lake Orrin.
- **Government provision** supplies the good directly, such as free vaccination.

## Strengths and limitations

Judge each policy using three lenses from the guide.

1. **Measurement.** Nobody observes the external cost directly. Estimating the right tax or subsidy needs a money value for harm such as illness or lost habitat, so a tax may be too high or too low.
2. **Effectiveness.** A tax cuts output by less when demand is price inelastic, so it may need to be high to change behaviour. Permits fix the quantity but not the price. Regulation is simple but gives firms no reason to cut below the limit.
3. **Stakeholders.** Consumers pay higher prices, workers may lose jobs, producers' profits fall and governments gain revenue or bear costs. Poorer households may be hit hardest by taxes on necessities.

## International cooperation

Sustainability issues such as climate change cross borders, so one country acting alone cannot solve them. Cooperation is hard because countries differ in income, in their share of the harm and in what they gain or lose from acting. Each is tempted to let others bear the cost. Agreements need **monitoring** (measuring emissions or catches honestly) and **enforcement** (penalties for those who break the terms), and sovereign states can be hard to penalise.

## Common errors

- Drawing the MSC curve **through** the market equilibrium. It must sit above or below MPC by the external amount.
- Shading the whole area under the curves instead of only the triangle between the two outputs.
- Treating a subsidy as shifting demand when it is paid to suppliers. Say which curve moves and label the new curve.
- Calling every shared resource a public good. A common pool resource is rivalrous; a public good is not.
- (HL) Using the price, or the whole quantity gap, as the triangle height. The height is the MSC to MSB gap at the market output.
