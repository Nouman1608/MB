## Urban metabolism: putting numbers on the system

**Urban metabolism** is the idea that a city, like an organism, takes in resources, transforms them and releases wastes. Treating each flow quantitatively shows where losses happen and where planning can intervene.

### Worked example 1: a city water budget

A fictional city of 3.0 million people takes in 900,000 m³ of treated water a day. Leaks in old pipes lose 20% before it reaches users. Of the water delivered, 85% returns as wastewater to the sewers.

```
Leakage            = 0.20 × 900,000 = 180,000 m³ per day
Delivered          = 900,000 − 180,000 = 720,000 m³ per day
Delivered per person = 720,000,000 L ÷ 3,000,000 = 240 L per person per day
Wastewater collected = 0.85 × 720,000 = 612,000 m³ per day
```

Two management points follow. Repairing pipes cuts the input needed by up to 180,000 m³ a day without asking residents to change anything. And 612,000 m³ of wastewater a day is a potential resource: treated water can be reused for industry or irrigation, which turns a linear flow into a partly circular one. The same method works for energy, food or waste flows.

## Urbanisation versus urban growth

These terms are often confused.

- **Urbanisation** is the rise in the *proportion* of people living in urban areas.
- **Urban growth** is the rise in the *number* of urban residents or in the area a city covers.

A city can grow without urbanisation if the rural population grows just as fast. Globally, the urban share passed 50% around 2007, and cities of 10 million or more people are called **megacities**.

### Worked example 2

A fictional country has 50 million people, 21 million of them urban. Ten years later it has 58 million, with 29 million urban.

```
Urban share then = 21 ÷ 50 × 100 = 42.0%
Urban share now  = 29 ÷ 58 × 100 = 50.0%
Urban growth     = (29 − 21) ÷ 21 × 100 = 38.1%
Total growth     = (58 − 50) ÷ 50 × 100 = 16.0%
```

The urban population grew more than twice as fast as the total population, so both urban growth and urbanisation occurred. Planners must supply housing, water and transport for 8 million extra people in a decade.

## Density, sprawl and car dependence

Population density links planning to energy use.

```
City A: 4.2 million people on   600 km² → 7,000 people per km²
City B: 4.2 million people on 2,100 km² → 2,000 people per km²
```

In a low-density city, homes, shops and workplaces are far apart, public transport has too few passengers per stop to run frequently, and most trips are made by car. Fuel use per person and land taken from farmland and habitats are therefore higher. Higher density supports frequent transit, walking and cycling, and shorter utility networks. It can also bring crowding, less private space and, if poorly planned, more exposure to traffic pollution, so density is a means, not an end.

**Urban sprawl** is the low-density spread of a city into surrounding countryside. Drivers include cheap land at the edge, car ownership, road building and demand for larger homes.

## Planning tools and how to evaluate them

### Zoning and mixed use

**Zoning** assigns land to uses such as housing, industry or commerce. Strict single-use zoning separates homes from jobs and lengthens journeys. **Mixed-use zoning** allows homes, shops and offices together, shortening trips and supporting street life. Planners keep some separation for heavy industry because of noise and pollution.

### Green belts

A **green belt** is a protected ring of open land around a city where most building is banned. London's green belt has restricted outward growth since the mid-twentieth century. Benefits: it protects farmland and habitats and keeps recreation space close to the city. Limitations: it can raise land and house prices within the belt, and development may "leapfrog" to towns beyond it, lengthening commuting.

### Transit-oriented development and bus rapid transit

**Transit-oriented development** concentrates housing and jobs around rail or bus stations. **Bus rapid transit** uses dedicated lanes, prepaid boarding and frequent services to give metro-like speed at a lower cost. The Brazilian city of Curitiba developed a pioneering bus rapid transit system from the 1970s, combined with land-use rules that allowed high-density building along its main bus corridors. It is widely used as an example of planning land use and transport together.

### Upgrading informal settlements

Rapid urban growth often outpaces formal housing. **In-situ upgrading** improves an informal settlement where it stands, adding water, sanitation, drainage, paved paths and secure tenure, rather than demolishing it and moving residents to the city edge. It keeps people near jobs and social networks, though it can be slow and depends on residents' involvement and land rights.

### Reducing the urban heat island

Cool roofs and green roofs change the surface energy balance from 6.1. Suppose 800 W m⁻² of sunlight falls on a roof:

```
Dark roof, albedo 0.10:     800 × (1 − 0.10) = 720 W m⁻² absorbed
Cool roof, albedo 0.65:     800 × (1 − 0.65) = 280 W m⁻² absorbed
Reduction = 440 W m⁻² (about 61%)
```

Green roofs and street trees add cooling through evapotranspiration and shade, and slow stormwater runoff.

## Measuring a sustainable city

Indicators turn "sustainable" into something measurable and comparable:

| Indicator | Example of calculation |
|---|---|
| Green space per person | 18 km² of parks for 1.5 million people = 18,000,000 m² ÷ 1,500,000 = 12 m² per person |
| Modal share | Percentage of trips made by public transport, walking or cycling |
| Water use per person | Litres delivered per person per day |
| Waste per person | kg of solid domestic waste per person per year |
| Air quality | Annual mean concentration of PM2.5 or NO₂ |

Indicators must be read with care: a city may score well on one and badly on another, and a good average can hide poor conditions in some districts.

## Common mistakes

- Using urbanisation to mean urban growth.
- Treating higher density as automatically better, with no mention of crowding or planning quality.
- Describing a green belt only as a benefit; mention price rises and leapfrogging.
- Listing planning strategies without stakeholders: who pays, who benefits, who is displaced?
- Building a flow budget without units or without checking that outputs do not exceed inputs.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate:** shares, growth rates, densities and per-person indicators; always give units.
- **Explain** a pattern such as car dependence: density, distance between uses, viability of transit, resulting fuel use.
- **Evaluate** a planning tool: environmental effect, social effect, cost and enforcement, then a judgement for the city in the case.
- **Discuss** with perspectives: residents, developers, commuters, farmers and conservation groups value land differently.
