This guide covers topic 1.2 Migration in Theme 1 (Population and settlement) of the Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026. The [Migration revision notes](/resources/igcse-geography-0460-migration-revision-notes/) already summarise push and pull factors and work through Poland to the UK and Syrian refugees; this guide adds a framework for explaining *why* migrants choose a destination, a three-way method for impacts, and contrasting case studies of labour migration and forced migration. ## Classifying a migration

Every migration can be described on three scales at once. Use all three in a case study introduction.

| Scale | Options | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Distance | Internal (within a country) or international (across a border) | Rural Bihar to Mumbai is internal; Bangladesh to Qatar is international |
| Choice | Voluntary (the migrant chooses) or involuntary/forced (conflict, persecution, disaster, eviction) | A nurse taking a job abroad is voluntary; fleeing a war is involuntary |
| Time | Temporary (including seasonal and contract work) or permanent | A two-year building contract is temporary |

Some movements fall between categories. A farmer who leaves after three failed harvests chose to go, but had little real choice; say so in an answer, because recognising this earns credit in "explain" and "evaluate" questions.

## Explaining why people migrate

Push and pull factors are the start, not the whole explanation. A strong answer uses a four-stage chain:

1. **Push factors** make the origin less attractive: low incomes, unemployment, lack of services, drought, conflict, persecution.
2. **Pull factors** make a destination attractive: jobs, higher wages, education, healthcare, safety, family already there.
3. **Intervening obstacles** must be overcome: cost of travel, immigration laws, distance, language, recruitment fees.
4. **Information and networks** steer people to particular places: migrants usually go where relatives or neighbours have already gone, because they can find housing and work through them.

This explains why migrants from one village often end up in the same city or country, and why the poorest people may not migrate at all: they cannot afford the obstacles.

**Internal example: rural-urban migration in China.** Since the economic reforms of the late 1970s and the growth of factories in coastal provinces, hundreds of millions of people have moved from inland rural areas to cities such as Shenzhen and Shanghai. The pull of factory and construction wages far above farm incomes was the main driver. China's household registration system (hukou) has long tied access to schools, healthcare and housing benefits to a person's registered home area, so many migrants have lived in cities without full access to services, and children were often left with grandparents in their villages.

## Impacts: a three-way method

The syllabus names three groups. Plan every impacts answer as a grid and include at least one positive and one negative for each group asked about.

| | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| **Destination** | Fills labour shortages; migrants pay taxes and spend money; cultural diversity; young workers offset an ageing population | Pressure on housing, schools and health services; wages may be held down in some jobs; possible tension if integration is poor |
| **Origin** | Remittances fund housing, schooling and small businesses; less pressure on land and jobs; returning migrants bring skills and savings | Loss of young, skilled workers (brain drain); an older, female-dominated population left behind; farms short of labour |
| **Migrants themselves** | Higher income; safety for refugees; education and skills | Separation from family; poor working or living conditions; debt to recruiters; discrimination; insecure legal status |

## Case study 1: South Asian labour migration to the Gulf (voluntary, international)

**What happened.** After the oil price rises of the 1970s, the Gulf states (including the United Arab Emirates, Qatar, Kuwait and Saudi Arabia) used oil revenue to build cities, airports and infrastructure very quickly. Their own populations were small, so they recruited foreign workers on a very large scale. Many come from India, Pakistan, Bangladesh and Nepal, as well as the Philippines. In the UAE, foreign nationals make up the large majority of residents.

**Why they move.** Push factors include low wages, underemployment and small farm plots at home. Pull factors are wages several times higher than at home, plentiful construction, domestic and service jobs, and established networks of recruitment agents. Most workers move on temporary contracts linked to a sponsoring employer, a system known as *kafala*, and they cannot usually settle permanently or bring their families.

**Impacts on the destination.** Migrant labour built much of modern Dubai and Doha and keeps services running. The resident population is heavily male and of working age, which produces a distinctive population pyramid (see topic 1.3). Governments have faced international criticism over workers' conditions and have made reforms, including summer bans on outdoor work in the hottest hours.

**Impacts on the origin.** Remittances are a major source of income: India receives more remittances than any other country, and the Gulf is one of its main sources. Money sent home pays for brick houses, school fees and small businesses, and supports family members. On the other hand, villages lose young men for years, wives and grandparents take on farm work, and children grow up with an absent father.

**Impacts on the migrants.** Earnings can transform a family's prospects. However, many workers pay high fees to recruiters and start in debt, work long hours in extreme heat and live in crowded labour camps, and the sponsorship system makes changing employer difficult.

## Case study 2: Rohingya refugees, Myanmar to Bangladesh (involuntary, international)

From August 2017, more than 700,000 Rohingya, a mostly Muslim minority, fled violence in Rakhine State in Myanmar and crossed into the Cox's Bazar district of Bangladesh, joining earlier refugees. The camps there, including Kutupalong, are among the largest in the world.

- **On the migrants**: safety from violence, but very crowded shelters on steep hillsides at risk of landslides in the monsoon, dependence on aid, and limited access to formal education and work.
- **On the destination area**: hills were cleared of trees for shelters and firewood; local people faced competition for low-paid work and rising prices; aid agencies and the Bangladesh government carry heavy costs.
- **On the origin**: villages emptied and communities were broken up; return depends on conditions in Myanmar that refugees regard as unsafe.

This contrast is useful: in forced migration the push factors dominate and the destination is chosen for safety and proximity, not for jobs.

## Model answer: explain the impacts on the origin

*Question: Explain the impacts of international labour migration on the country the migrants leave.*

> The main positive impact is remittances (knowledge). For example, workers from Nepal and Bangladesh in the Gulf send part of their wages home (application), which families spend on school fees and better housing, raising living standards even in remote villages (analysis). However, the migrants are mostly young men, so the origin loses workers in their most productive years and farms rely on women and older people (analysis). Whether the overall effect is positive depends on whether the money is invested in businesses and skills, or only spent on consumption (evaluation).

## Common errors

- Writing push and pull factors only, with no explanation of how they lead to migration.
- Giving impacts on the destination when the question asks about the origin or the migrants.
- Calling all refugees "economic migrants", or ignoring the overlap between forced and voluntary movement.
- Case studies with no named origin, destination or dates.
- Saying "migrants take jobs" without considering labour shortages or the jobs migrants create through spending.

## Exam technique

- **Identify / state**: name the type of migration in full, for example "voluntary international labour migration".
- **Explain**: connect a push or pull factor to the decision to move: "because wages in construction are higher, ...".
- **Describe impacts**: cover both positive and negative, and the group the question names.
- **Evaluate / to what extent**: weigh benefits against costs and reach a judgement, noting that impacts differ for different groups.
