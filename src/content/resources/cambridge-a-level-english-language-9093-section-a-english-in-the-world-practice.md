## Section A

**1.** Explain Kachru's concentric circles model, placing one example country in each circle. **[6]**

**2.** Explain the difference between a pidgin and a creole, and describe the process of creolisation. **[4]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by the sociolect continuum, using the terms basilect, mesolect and acrolect. **[4]**

**4.** Outline the five phases of Schneider's Dynamic Model of postcolonial Englishes. **[5]**

**5.** Explain what is meant by language shift, and how it can lead to language death. **[3]**

## Section B

Read the passage below (original, written for this page) and answer Questions 6 to 9.

> *When I started work at the call centre, my manager gave me a list of "approved" phrases and a recording of a voice I
> was meant to sound like. I spent two weeks practising vowels that had never been mine. My English was fluent; I had
> studied in it since I was six. But it was the wrong English, apparently. Customers abroad, I was told, would trust
> me more if I sounded like them. Now I code-switch all day: one voice on the headset, another at lunch with
> colleagues, a third at home with my parents, who speak to me in a language my manager has never heard of. Sometimes
> I wonder which of these voices is really me. Sometimes I think the answer is all of them.*

**6.** Identify **two** issues the passage raises about attitudes to varieties of English, and explain each with reference to the text. **[6]**

**7.** Explain how the passage illustrates multilingualism and code-switching, and evaluate whether code-switching should be seen as a skill or as a loss. **[6]**

**8.** How far does the passage support or challenge Kachru's model? **[6]**

**9.** Write a plan for an essay discussing the most important issues the passage raises about **English and employment**. Give an introduction sentence, three paragraph points with a concept for each, and a concluding judgement. **[10]**

**10.** Explain Phillipson's argument about linguistic imperialism and give **one** criticism of it. **[4]**

**11.** Explain **one** government policy concerning English and the debate it reflects. **[4]**

**12.** "English will remain the world's main international language for the foreseeable future." Discuss, referring to **two** views of the future of English. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** **Inner circle**: countries where English is the main first language [1], e.g. the UK, the USA, Australia [1]. **Outer circle**: countries where English has an institutional role (law, government, education), usually after colonial history [1], e.g. India, Nigeria, Singapore [1]. **Expanding circle**: countries where English is learned as a foreign language for international communication [1], e.g. China, Japan, Brazil [1].

**2.** A **pidgin** is a reduced contact language that develops between groups without a shared language and has **no native speakers** [1] [1]. **Creolisation** occurs when children grow up speaking the pidgin as a **first language** [1], and its grammar and vocabulary **expand** into a full language, the creole [1].

**3.** The continuum describes **variation between a creole and the standard** in one community [1]. The **basilect** is furthest from the standard [1]; **mesolects** are intermediate [1]; the **acrolect** is closest to the standard, and speakers move along the continuum according to setting and audience [1].

**4.** **Foundation**: English is brought to a new territory [1]. **Exonormative stabilisation**: norms follow the external, colonial standard [1]. **Nativisation**: local features of vocabulary, pronunciation and grammar develop [1]. **Endonormative stabilisation**: local norms are accepted as standard [1]. **Differentiation**: internal varieties emerge within the new national variety [1].

**5.** **Language shift** is a community's gradual move from one language to another, usually a dominant one, across domains and generations [1] [1]. When children no longer learn the heritage language, it can reach **language death**, when its last speakers die [1].

**6.** Any two, each worth three marks. **Accent prejudice and native-speaker norms**: the "approved" phrases and the recording show an employer ranking one accent above another [1], even though the writer is "fluent" [1]; this treats inner-circle norms as the only trustworthy English, an unofficial attitude with real economic effects [1]. **Identity**: "vowels that had never been mine" and "which of these voices is really me" show that imposed norms affect self-identity [1], since a variety is part of who a speaker is [1]; this connects to debates about endonormative versus exonormative standards [1].

**7.** The writer moves between **three varieties or languages in different domains**: headset, colleagues, home [1] [1]. This is **multilingualism with domain separation**, typical of many English users worldwide [1]. Evaluation: as a **skill**, code-switching shows control of audience and context, and the ending "all of them" frames it as a rich identity [1]; as a **loss**, the pressure to suppress a variety at work suggests inequality, and the home language is unknown to the manager, implying it has no status [1]. Judgement: the passage suggests both, with the skill forced by unequal attitudes [1].

**8.** Support: the writer appears to be from an **outer-circle setting**, educated in English since childhood, while the customers are associated with an **inner-circle standard**, so the model describes the power relationship [1] [1]. Challenge: the writer is a **fluent, lifelong user**, which the circles' focus on nationality cannot capture [1]; the model implies the inner circle is the norm-giver, the very attitude the passage questions [1]; the writer's **multiple varieties** do not fit one circle [1]. Judgement: the model describes the hierarchy the passage shows but is too simple for the writer's identity [1].

**9.** **Introduction**: identifies the aspect and a line of argument, e.g. "The passage suggests English opens employment but on terms set by others" [1] [1]. **Point 1**: accent and native-speaker norms at work, with **exonormative standards / Kachru** [1] [1]. **Point 2**: identity and code-switching as both skill and cost, with **multilingualism and domains** [1] [1]. **Point 3**: equality of opportunity, English as gatekeeper versus access to global jobs, with **Phillipson or Crystal** [1] [1]. **Conclusion**: a judgement rather than a summary, e.g. English widens employment access but unequal attitudes to varieties limit it [1] [1].

**10.** Phillipson argues that the **dominance of English is actively maintained** [1] through **unequal allocation of resources and promotion of English by powerful nations and institutions** [1], to their advantage [1]. Criticism: it underplays **speakers' own choices**: people adopt and adapt English for their own purposes [1].

**11.** Example: **Singapore's Speak Good English Movement** (2000) promoted standard English [1] [1]. It reflects a debate between **international intelligibility and economic competitiveness** on one side [1] and **Singlish as a marker of local identity** on the other [1]. Alternatively, France's Toubon Law (1994), requiring French in certain public and commercial contexts, reflects concern about the influence of English on national language and culture.

**12.** View 1: English **will remain dominant** because of its established role in science, business, the internet and safety-critical fields such as aviation, and because so many people already learn it [1] [1]. View 2: **Graddol's argument** that as English becomes a basic skill, monolingual English speakers lose their advantage and other languages may grow in international importance [1] [1]. Alternatively, the fragmentation view, that English may split into separate varieties, with the counter that global media keep varieties in contact. Evaluation: **dominance depends on the power of speakers (Crystal)**, which can change [1]; English as a **lingua franca may be increasingly shaped by non-native users** rather than inner-circle norms [1]. Conclusion: likely to remain a main international language, but **not necessarily in inner-circle form or alone** [1] [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a model without applying it to the text or evaluating it.
- Summarising the passage instead of selecting issues.
- Treating accents and creoles as incorrect English.
- Plans that list topics without concepts or a judgement.
- One-sided answers to "discuss" questions.
