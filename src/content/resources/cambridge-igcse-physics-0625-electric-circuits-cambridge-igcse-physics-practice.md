## Section A

**1.** State what happens to the resistance of a light-dependent resistor (LDR) as the light falling on it gets brighter. **[1]**

**2.** Two 2.0 V cells and one 1.5 V cell are connected in series, all facing the same way. Calculate the combined e.m.f. **[1]**

**3.** Calculate the combined resistance of a 22 Ω resistor and a 47 Ω resistor connected in series. **[1]**

**4.** In a series circuit, an ammeter placed before a lamp reads 0.25 A. State the reading on an ammeter placed after the lamp. **[1]**

**5.** State **two** advantages of connecting the lamps in a house lighting circuit in parallel. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** A 6.0 V battery is connected in series with a 5.0 Ω resistor and a 15 Ω resistor.

**(a)** Calculate the combined resistance. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the current in the circuit. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the p.d. across the 15 Ω resistor. **[2]**
**(d)** State the p.d. across the 5.0 Ω resistor. **[1]**

**7.** Two identical lamps are connected in parallel to a cell. An ammeter in each branch reads 0.20 A.

**(a)** State the reading on an ammeter placed next to the cell. **[1]**
**(b)** One lamp is unscrewed. State and explain what happens to the other lamp. **[2]**
**(c)** State how the combined resistance of the two lamps in parallel compares with the resistance of one lamp. **[1]**

**8.** A lamp is connected in series with a cell and a variable resistor.

**(a)** Describe and explain what happens to the lamp when the resistance of the variable resistor is increased. **[2]**
**(b)** The variable resistor is replaced by an NTC thermistor. Describe and explain what happens to the lamp as the thermistor is warmed. **[2]**

**9.** A car's starter motor needs a very large current. It is switched on using a relay, operated by a small current from the ignition switch. Explain how the relay allows the small current to switch on the large one. **[3]**

**10.** *(Extended)* A 10 Ω resistor and a 15 Ω resistor are connected in parallel to a 6.0 V supply.

**(a)** Calculate the combined resistance. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the current in each resistor. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the current from the supply, and state the rule you use. **[2]**

**11.** *(Extended)* An 8.0 Ω resistor is connected in series with a parallel pair of 5.0 Ω and 20 Ω resistors. The supply p.d. is 6.0 V.

**(a)** Calculate the combined resistance of the parallel pair. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the current from the supply. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the p.d. across the parallel pair. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the current in the 20 Ω resistor. **[1]**

**12.** *(Extended)* A potential divider is made from a 4.0 kΩ fixed resistor and an NTC thermistor in series with a 12 V supply. The output is taken across the thermistor.

**(a)** At 15 °C the thermistor's resistance is 8.0 kΩ. Calculate the output p.d. **[2]**
**(b)** At 60 °C its resistance is 2.0 kΩ. Calculate the output p.d. **[2]**
**(c)** Describe and explain how the output p.d. changes as the temperature rises. **[2]**

**13.** *(Extended)* An LED is connected in series with a resistor and a battery, and it lights.

**(a)** The battery is turned round. State what happens to the LED. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain your answer to (a). **[1]**
**(c)** State how an LED differs in its behaviour from an ordinary diode. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** Its resistance **decreases** [1]. **[1]**

**2.** 2.0 + 2.0 + 1.5 = **5.5 V** [1]. **[1]**

**3.** 22 + 47 = **69 Ω** [1]. **[1]**

**4.** **0.25 A**: the current is the same at every point in a series circuit [1]. **[1]**

*Common mistake:* giving a smaller reading after the lamp. Current is not used up by components.

**5.** Any two: each lamp receives the full supply p.d.; each lamp can be switched on and off separately; if one lamp fails the others stay lit [1 each]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** 5.0 + 15 = **20 Ω** [1].
**(b)** I = V / R = 6.0 ÷ 20 [1] = **0.30 A** [1].
**(c)** V = IR = 0.30 × 15 [1] = **4.5 V** [1].
**(d)** 6.0 − 4.5 = **1.5 V** [1]. **[6]**

**7. (a)** 0.20 + 0.20 = **0.40 A** [1].
**(b)** It stays lit at the same brightness [1], because it is on its own branch, still connected across the full p.d. of the cell [1].
**(c)** It is **less** than the resistance of one lamp (half, for identical lamps) [1]. **[4]**

**8. (a)** The lamp gets dimmer [1], because the total resistance increases so the current decreases [1].
**(b)** The lamp gets brighter [1], because the thermistor's resistance falls as its temperature rises, so the current increases [1]. **[4]**

**9.** The small current flows through the relay's coil, which becomes an electromagnet [1]. The electromagnet attracts a soft-iron armature [1], which closes the switch contacts in the separate starter-motor circuit, so the large current flows [1]. **[3]**

**10. (a)** 1/R = 1/10 + 1/15 = 5/30 [1]; R = **6.0 Ω** [1].
**(b)** Each resistor has the full 6.0 V: 6.0 ÷ 10 = **0.60 A**; 6.0 ÷ 15 = **0.40 A** [1 each].
**(c)** 0.60 + 0.40 = **1.0 A** [1]; the current into a junction equals the current out (or I = 6.0 ÷ 6.0 = 1.0 A) [1]. **[6]**

*Common mistake:* giving R = 25 Ω. That is the series value; in parallel the combination is less than 10 Ω.

**11. (a)** 1/R = 1/5.0 + 1/20 = 5/20 [1]; R = **4.0 Ω** [1].
**(b)** Total R = 8.0 + 4.0 = 12 Ω [1]; I = 6.0 ÷ 12 = **0.50 A** [1].
**(c)** p.d. = 0.50 × 4.0 = **2.0 V** [1].
**(d)** I = 2.0 ÷ 20 = **0.10 A** [1]. **[6]**

**12. (a)** V_thermistor / V_fixed = 8.0 / 4.0, so the 12 V is shared 2 : 1 [1]; output = **8.0 V** [1].
**(b)** Ratio 2.0 : 4.0 = 1 : 2 [1]; output = 12 × 2.0 / 6.0 = **4.0 V** [1].
**(c)** The output p.d. **falls** [1], because the thermistor's resistance falls, so it takes a smaller share of the supply p.d. [1]. **[6]**

**13. (a)** It goes out (does not light) [1].
**(b)** An LED conducts in one direction only; reversed, its resistance is very high and there is no current [1].
**(c)** An LED emits light when it conducts [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying current falls after passing through a lamp.
- Adding resistors in parallel as if they were in series.
- Getting the direction of thermistor and LDR changes backwards.
- Describing a relay without saying the coil becomes an electromagnet.
- In a potential divider, pairing each p.d. with the wrong resistor.
