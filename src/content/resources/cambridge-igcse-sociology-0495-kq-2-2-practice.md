us?* Parts (a) to (f) follow the six-part shape the syllabus describes for Paper 1 Question 2; parts

## Questions

**(a)** Define the terms:
- (i) *positive sanction* **[2]**
- (ii) *digital surveillance* **[2]**

**(b)** Give **two** examples of youth sub-cultures. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **three** reasons why the police may not be effective in producing social
conformity. **[6]**

**(d)** Explain **three** ways the workplace controls people's behaviour. **[6]**

**(e)** Discuss the feminist view of social control. **[8]**

**(f)** Evaluate the view that digital surveillance has made formal social control more effective.
**[14]**

### Extra practice (2.2.1 to 2.2.7)

**(g)** Explain the difference between deviant and non-conformist behaviour. **[4]**

**(h)** Explain **two** ways religion acts as an agency of informal social control. **[4]**

**(i)** Explain **two** reasons why some people join online sub-cultures. **[4]**

**(j)** Explain the functionalist view of punishment. **[4]**

**(k)** Explain **two** ways the media acts as an agency of social control. **[4]**

## Answers and mark guidance

**(a)(i) [2]** A reward given for conforming to norms or laws (1), for example praise from parents,
a merit at school or promotion at work (1).

**(a)(ii) [2]** Monitoring people's behaviour using technology (1), such as CCTV, facial recognition or
tracking of phone and internet data (1).

**(b) [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: mods; rockers; punks; skinheads; goths; hip-hop or other music-based
youth styles. Credit accurate local examples.

**(c) [6]** Three reasons, 2 marks each (identify + explain):

- **Unreported crime** — many victims do not report offences, so the police never know about them and
  offenders face no sanction.
- **Low detection** — many recorded crimes are never solved, so potential offenders may expect not to
  be caught.
- **Lack of legitimacy** — where communities distrust the police, perhaps because of unequal
  treatment, people are less willing to cooperate or obey.
- **Limited resources** — officers cannot be everywhere; large anonymous cities are hard to police.
- **Selective focus** — Marxists argue the police concentrate on working-class crime, so white-collar
  and corporate offences go largely unpoliced.

**(d) [6]** Three ways, 2 marks each:

- **Dismissal from a job** — the threat of losing income is a strong negative sanction for breaking
  rules.
- **Promotion and pay rises** — positive sanctions reward punctuality, effort and loyalty.
- **Rules and supervision** — managers and codes of conduct set and monitor dress, timekeeping and
  behaviour.
- **Workplace training** — induction teaches what is expected, so new staff internalise norms.
- **Colleagues' reactions** — informal approval or exclusion by co-workers.
- **Digital monitoring** — tracking of computer use or delivery routes.

**(e) [8]** Levels: 7–8 a clear, developed account with evidence and some evaluation; 4–6 some
explanation; 1–3 basic points.

- Feminists see social control as maintaining **patriarchy** — male dominance.
- Heidensohn (1985): women are controlled **at home** through domestic and childcare
  responsibilities, **in public** through fear of violence and concern about reputation, and **at work**
  through male supervisors and sexual harassment.
- Girls are supervised more closely by parents than boys, limiting opportunities for deviance.
- Women who break gender norms may face harsher informal sanctions, such as damage to reputation.
- **Evaluation:** helps explain why women commit less recorded crime; but women's freedom and
  participation in work and public life have grown in many societies, and the view may underplay
  differences between women by class and ethnicity.

**(f) [14]** Levels: 11–14 balanced, developed and evaluated with a conclusion; 7–10 developed but
less balanced; 4–6 limited; 1–3 basic.

- *For:* CCTV, facial recognition and phone data help detect and prosecute offenders; visible cameras
  deter some crime in shops, transport and streets; Foucault's panopticon — people who believe they may
  be watched discipline themselves; data monitoring reaches online crime that patrols cannot;
  electronic tags allow control without prison.
- *Against:* offenders may move to areas without cameras (displacement) or hide their faces;
  surveillance records crime more often than it prevents it; privacy and civil liberty concerns can
  reduce the legitimacy of formal agencies; some groups may be watched more than others, which conflict
  theorists see as unequal control; much conformity still depends on informal agencies.
- *Conclusion:* surveillance has increased the reach and detection power of formal control, but its
  effect on conformity is limited without legitimacy and support from informal control. Credit a
  reasoned judgement.

**(g) [4]** **Deviant** behaviour breaks a society's norms (1) and attracts negative sanctions, for
example vandalism (1). **Non-conformist** behaviour rejects mainstream expectations without
necessarily breaking rules (1), for example choosing an alternative lifestyle or style of dress (1).

**(h) [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each:

- **Religious rewards** — respect from the community or the promise of spiritual reward encourages
  people to follow moral rules (1 + 1).
- **Religious sanctions** — disapproval, exclusion from rituals or fear of sin discourage deviance
  (1 + 1).
- **Community supervision** — members of a congregation watch and comment on one another's behaviour
  (1 + 1).

**(i) [4]** Two reasons, 2 marks each:

- **Belonging** — people with interests not shared by those around them, such as a niche game, find a
  group that accepts them (1 + 1).
- **Status** — skill or knowledge earns respect within the group that may not be available at school
  or work (1 + 1).
- **Anonymity and freedom** — people can express identities they hide offline (1 + 1).

**(j) [4]** Functionalists see punishment as protecting shared values (1). Durkheim argued that
punishing offenders publicly reminds everyone of where the boundaries of acceptable behaviour lie (1),
which strengthens collective values (1) and unites the law-abiding majority against the offender,
maintaining social order (1).

**(k) [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each:

- **Positive sanctions** — social media "likes" and shares reward behaviour and images that fit
  popular norms (1 + 1).
- **Public shaming** — negative comments, exposure or "cancelling" punish people who break norms
  (1 + 1).
- **Representations** — news reports and dramas show deviance being punished, reinforcing norms; media
  exaggeration can create moral panics that lead to tougher formal control (Cohen, 1972) (1 + 1).

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I use the same example for formal and informal control?** Yes, if you explain the difference —
for instance, police cameras in a town centre (formal surveillance) versus the disapproving
looks of passers-by (informal).

**How many perspectives should I use in (f)?** Not required, but a conflict view on unequal
surveillance adds strong evaluation.
