The human development context of IB Psychology (first assessment 2027) asks how and why behaviour and cognition change over time. Under **models of development**, the guide asks you to understand one or more of cognitive, social, moral or language development. It then lists an area called **brain development** with three learning objectives: the role of **brain maturation** in human development, the extent to which **critical periods** explain human development, and the role of **neuroplasticity** in human development. This guide teaches those three ideas, shows how to apply them to cognitive, social, moral or language development, and gives evaluation points. The guide names no required studies, so use ones you can describe and evaluate.

Related: [developmental psychology study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-psychology-developmental-psychology-study-guide/), [empathy and theory of mind](/resources/ib-ib-dp-psychology-empathy-and-theory-of-mind/), [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-brain-development-revision-notes/) and [practice set](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-brain-development-practice/).

## Why brain development matters for the context

Developmental psychologists compare accounts of what drives change. Some emphasise biological factors, others culture and social influence. Brain development belongs to the biological side, but it also shows that biology and experience interact: the brain's structure changes with age, and experience changes the brain. Keep three ideas separate in your head and use each precisely.

| Idea | What it says | The key question |
|---|---|---|
| Maturation | The brain changes in an ordered way with age, mostly driven by genes | Which abilities depend on the brain being ready? |
| Critical period | A limited time window when experience is needed for normal development | Is development impossible or reduced if experience is missed? |
| Neuroplasticity | The brain's structure and function change in response to experience | How far can the brain change, and at what ages? |

## 1. Brain maturation

**Brain maturation** is the biological process by which the brain grows and refines with age. Key processes are:

- **Synaptogenesis.** Neurons form many connections (synapses), especially in early childhood.
- **Synaptic pruning.** Connections that are used are strengthened and those that are not used are removed, making the brain more efficient.
- **Myelination.** Fatty myelin coats axons and speeds up signals. It continues for many years.
- **Regional order.** MRI studies that follow children over time show that regions mature at different rates. Sensory and motor areas mature earlier. The prefrontal cortex, linked to planning, impulse control and judgement, continues to mature into the early twenties.

**Application to development.**
- *Cognitive development:* improvements in planning, working memory and abstract thinking in adolescence coincide with prefrontal maturation. A neo-Piagetian could argue that readiness for formal operational thought depends on brain readiness.
- *Social and moral development:* adolescents may be more sensitive to peer reward because reward-related regions mature earlier than control regions. This is one explanation of greater risk-taking in groups, though it is only one factor among others, including culture and opportunity.
- *Language development:* the ability to produce speech depends on maturation of motor and language areas.

**Worked example.** A 7-year-old can follow a simple rule but forgets it when distracted. A 16-year-old can plan a revision timetable but still sometimes acts on impulse when with friends. Brain maturation helps explain both, since planning circuits develop gradually and are not fully mature in adolescence. A strong answer adds that experience and teaching also contribute, so maturation is necessary but not the whole explanation.

**Evaluation.**
- *Strength:* brain imaging gives objective, non-invasive evidence and lets researchers track the same children over time (a longitudinal design).
- *Limitation:* imaging data are correlational. A structural change at the same age as a skill does not prove that the change caused the skill. Experience may also drive the change.
- *Limitation:* many samples come from Western, educated populations, so the timing may not be universal.
- *Reductionism:* explaining behaviour only by brain structure ignores learning, culture and motivation.
- **Concepts:** causality, measurement (imaging), perspective (biological versus sociocultural).

## 2. Critical periods

A **critical period** is a limited time window when a particular experience is needed for a function to develop normally. A related, weaker idea is a **sensitive period**, when the brain is especially responsive but development is still possible later with more effort. Many researchers now prefer "sensitive period" for most human abilities.

**Animal evidence.** Hubel and Wiesel studied the visual system in kittens. When one eye was covered during a limited early period, the visual cortex stopped responding normally to that eye, and vision did not fully recover when the eye was uncovered later. This suggests a critical period for visual development. The research is a strong demonstration of cause and effect, but it used animals and deprivation, which raises ethical questions and limits generalisation to humans.

**Human evidence: language.** Lenneberg proposed that language is acquired most easily before puberty. Support comes from the case of **Genie** (Curtiss and colleagues), a girl who was isolated until about 13 and who acquired vocabulary but remained limited in grammar despite later teaching. Unfortunately the case is confounded by severe abuse and malnutrition, so the cause of her limits cannot be separated from trauma. A larger study by Johnson and Newport (1989) found that immigrants who arrived in the United States at younger ages performed better in English grammar in adulthood than those who arrived later, with a decline around puberty. This suggests an age effect, but later arrivals also differ in education, motivation and amount of exposure.

**Human evidence: social development.** The English and Romanian Adoptees study followed children adopted into British families after severe early deprivation in institutions. Many caught up, especially those adopted earlier, but a larger proportion of those who had stayed longer showed lasting difficulties in attachment and attention. This supports a sensitive period for early social experience, but the findings show a spread of outcomes, not a simple cut-off.

**Extent to which critical periods explain development.** The guide asks "the extent to which", so conclude with a judgement:
- They explain well-defined sensory functions (vision) and some aspects of language.
- For most abilities the evidence supports sensitive periods rather than fixed deadlines. Recovery is often partial and varies by person, which fits with neuroplasticity.
- Ethical and practical limits mean there are no true experiments on humans. Evidence comes from natural experiments and case studies, which have confounds.

**Concepts:** causality (case studies cannot isolate causes), responsibility (ethics of deprivation research and of using vulnerable cases), change, bias (small samples, researcher interpretation).

## 3. Neuroplasticity

**Neuroplasticity** is the brain's ability to change its structure and function in response to experience. It includes forming new synapses, strengthening used connections, pruning unused ones and, in some regions, forming new neurons.

**Evidence.**
- *Maguire et al. (2000):* London taxi drivers had a larger posterior hippocampus than controls, and volume was related to years of driving. The study is correlational, but it suggests that heavy use of spatial memory can shape the brain.
- *Draganski et al. (2004):* adults who learned to juggle showed an increase in grey matter in regions related to visual motion processing after about three months, and the increase partly reversed when practice stopped. This is a longitudinal design with a control group, which is stronger evidence for experience causing change.
- *Environmental enrichment in animals:* rats raised in stimulating environments developed a thicker cortex than those in bare cages, a finding that needs cautious generalisation to humans.
- *Recovery after injury:* children often recover language after early brain damage better than adults do, which suggests that other regions can take over some functions.

**Application to development.** Neuroplasticity explains why experiences such as teaching, practice and relationships shape cognitive and social development. It also gives hope for intervention: early help (reading support, enriched environments, therapy) may change the developmental course. It qualifies critical periods by showing that change remains possible.

**Worked example.** Two six-year-olds begin to learn a musical instrument. One practises daily with a teacher, and the other rarely practises. Neuroplasticity predicts that structural and functional changes in auditory and motor regions will be greater in the first child. A good answer states what could be measured (for example MRI of grey matter or tests of skill), and notes that children who practise are probably more motivated, so motivation is a confounding variable.

**Evaluation.**
- *Strength:* supported by controlled, longitudinal imaging and by animal experiments, and linked to practical applications.
- *Limitation:* imaging changes are small and the functional meaning is unclear. Plasticity can also be maladaptive (for example pain that persists after injury).
- *Limitation:* the evidence from animals involves ethical and generalisation issues.
- **Concepts:** change, causality, measurement.

## Bringing the three ideas together

Maturation sets a timetable. Critical or sensitive periods say when experience matters most. Neuroplasticity says how experience changes the brain, and how much change is still possible later. A good Section C response uses all three, then weighs them against sociocultural accounts. For example: "Brain maturation explains why abilities such as planning improve with age, but the same maturation depends on experience, so a purely biological account is reductionist. Critical period research shows the importance of early experience for language and attachment, but the evidence is limited by ethics and confounds, and the concept has softened into sensitive periods. Neuroplasticity shows that development continues across life, which supports intervention."

## Common mistakes

- Using "critical period" and "sensitive period" as if they were identical.
- Describing Genie's case without noting the confounds of abuse.
- Claiming that brain imaging proves that a brain change causes a behaviour.
- Giving a list of brain structures without linking them to a type of development.
- Ignoring the "extent to which" in a critical-period question.
