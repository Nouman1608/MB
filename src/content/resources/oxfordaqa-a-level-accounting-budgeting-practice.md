These questions cover section 3.1.9, Budgeting, of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Accounting
(9615) specification, Version 1.2, for International AS exams May/June 2025 onwards and International
A-level exams May/June 2026 onwards. Section 3.1.9 is International AS content on Paper 2: Financial &
Management Accounting. The specification states that cash, sales, purchases, production and labour
budgets "will be assessed at A-level", so questions on them are labelled (Assessed at A-level).

## Questions

**1.** Which **one** of these reduces budgeted profit but is never entered in a cash budget? **[1]**

A Payment for a new delivery van
B Drawings
C Depreciation of equipment
D Wages paid in the month

**2.** State **two** benefits to a manufacturer of preparing a purchases budget. **[2]**

**3.** (Assessed at A-level) Sorrel Lane Pottery plans to sell 2,000 bowls in April, 2,600 in May,
2,300 in June and 2,500 in July. Closing inventory of finished bowls each month is 10% of the next month's
sales. Opening inventory on 1 April is 180 bowls. Prepare the production budget for April, May and June. **[4]**

**4.** (Assessed at A-level) Each bowl in question 3 needs 2 kg of clay at 0.50 per kg.
Each month ends with clay equal to 20% of the following month's usage; April starts with 600 kg.
Prepare the purchases budget, in kg and in value, for April and May. **[5]**

**5.** (Assessed at A-level) Yarrowby Cycles plans to assemble 420 bicycles in June and 480 in July.
Each bicycle takes 3.5 labour hours. The wage rate is 18 an hour, rising by 5% from 1 July. Prepare the
labour budget, in hours and in value, for June and July. **[4]**

**6.** (Assessed at A-level) Tallowmere Candles plans to sell 800 boxes in July, 900 in August and
1,100 in September. The price is 12 a box, rising to 13 from 1 September. 20% of sales are for cash; credit
customers pay in the month after sale.

**(a)** Prepare the sales budget in value for July, August and September. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the budgeted receipts from sales for August and September. **[3]**

**7.** (Assessed at A-level) Imogen Saldanha owns Saldanha Florists. The bank balance on 1 October 2027
is 4,600. Sales: August 18,000, September 21,000, October 19,000, November 24,000, December 35,000.
Customers pay cash for 40% of sales; the rest pay two months later. Purchases each month are 50%
of that month's sales and are paid for in the following month. Wages are 4,800 a month (December 6,200). Insurance of 2,400 for the year is paid in October. A van costing 16,000 is bought in
November: a 4,000 deposit is paid in November and the balance in January 2028. Shop fittings are
depreciated at 300 a month. Drawings are 1,800 a month.

**(a)** Prepare the cash budget for October, November and December 2027. **[9]**
**(b)** Explain why two of the items listed above do not appear in your cash budget. **[2]**

**8.** Hamish Oyelaran owns Tamberline Print Studio. His statement of financial position at 1 April 2027
showed: machinery at cost 60,000, accumulated depreciation 18,000; inventory 3,400; trade receivables
7,200; bank 1,900; trade payables 2,600; capital 51,900. The budget for the year ending 31 March 2028 is:

- revenue 140,000, all on credit; closing trade receivables 8,400
- purchases 46,000, all on credit; closing trade payables 3,100; closing inventory 4,200
- wages paid 38,500; other expenses paid 16,300, of which 900 relates to the following year
- machinery depreciated at 20% a year on the reducing balance; drawings 24,000; no new non-current assets.

**(a)** Prepare the budgeted income statement for the year ending 31 March 2028. **[5]**
**(b)** Calculate the budgeted bank balance at 31 March 2028. **[3]**
**(c)** Prepare Hamish's budgeted statement of financial position as at 31 March 2028. **[4]**

**9.** Explain **two** limitations of a cash budget as a planning tool. **[4]**

**10.** Haddonvale Fitness prepared this budget for March. Actual results are also shown.

| | Budget | Actual |
|---|---|---|
| Membership income | 42,000 | 39,600 |
| Staff costs | 18,500 | 19,240 |
| Utilities | 4,200 | 3,850 |
| Marketing | 3,000 | 3,000 |

**(a)** Calculate the difference for each line and for profit, labelling each favourable or adverse. **[4]**
**(b)** State **one** action the owner could take as a result of this comparison. **[1]**

**11.** The owner of Haddonvale Fitness says: "Preparing budgets is a waste of time for a business of our
size. We just increase last year's figures a little." Evaluate this view. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** **C** -- depreciation is an expense but no cash moves [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Check each option: does it reduce profit, and does cash move? A and B fail the first test; D fails the second.

**2.** Materials are ordered in time for planned production, so output is not stopped by shortages [1]. Excess inventory is avoided, so cash is not tied up and storage costs stay low [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A benefit of budgeting in general, such as motivation, does not answer a question about a specific budget.

**3.** Closing inventory: April 260, May 230, June 250 [1].
April 2,000 + 260 - 180 = **2,080** [1]; May 2,600 + 230 - 260 = **2,570** [1]; June 2,300 + 250 - 230 = **2,320** [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Lay each month out as sales, add closing, less opening, so your method shows.

**4.** Usage: April 4,160 kg, May 5,140 kg, June 4,640 kg [1]. Closing: April 1,028 kg, May 928 kg [1].
April purchases 4,160 + 1,028 - 600 = **4,588 kg** [1]; May 5,140 + 928 - 1,028 = **5,040 kg** [1].
Value: April **2,294**; May **2,520** [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Usage comes from production in question 3, not sales; write the usage line out so your method is visible.

**5.** June: 420 x 3.5 = **1,470 hours** [1]; 1,470 x 18 = **26,460** [1].
July rate 18 x 1.05 = 18.90 [1]; 480 x 3.5 = 1,680 hours; 1,680 x 18.90 = **31,752** [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the new rate as a separate step, so an error in it can be traced rather than leaving only a wrong final figure.

**6. (a)** July 9,600; August 10,800 [1]; September 1,100 x 13 = **14,300** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** August: cash 2,160 + credit from July 7,680 [1] = **9,840** [1]. September: 2,860 + 8,640 = **11,500** [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The price rise affects September's sales only; August's credit receipts use the old price.

**7. (a)**

| | October | November | December |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash sales | 7,600 | 9,600 | 14,000 |
| Credit customers | 10,800 | 12,600 | 11,400 |
| Total receipts | 18,400 | 22,200 | 25,400 |
| Suppliers | 10,500 | 9,500 | 12,000 |
| Wages | 4,800 | 4,800 | 6,200 |
| Insurance | 2,400 | | |
| Van deposit | | 4,000 | |
| Drawings | 1,800 | 1,800 | 1,800 |
| Total payments | 19,500 | 20,100 | 20,000 |
| Net cash flow | (1,100) | 2,100 | 5,400 |
| Opening balance | 4,600 | 3,500 | 5,600 |
| Closing balance | 3,500 | 5,600 | **11,000** |

Cash sales [1]; credit receipts [1]; total receipts [1]; suppliers [1]; wages [1]; insurance, deposit and drawings [1]; total payments [1]; net cash flow [1]; balances [1] **[9]**
**(b)** Depreciation is a non-cash expense, so no cash leaves the business [1]. The van balance of 12,000 is paid in January 2028, outside the budget period [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show every total and carry each closing balance forward visibly, so your method shows even if an earlier line is wrong.

**8. (a)**

```
Tamberline Print Studio
Budgeted income statement for the year ending 31 March 2028
Revenue                                       140,000
Cost of sales (3,400 + 46,000 - 4,200)        (45,200)
Gross profit                                   94,800
Wages                                38,500
Other expenses (16,300 - 900)        15,400
Depreciation (20% x 42,000)           8,400   (62,300)
Profit for the year                            32,500
```

Cost of sales [1]; gross profit [1]; other expenses 15,400 [1]; depreciation 8,400 [1]; profit **32,500** [1] **[5]**
**(b)** Receipts 7,200 + 140,000 - 8,400 = 138,800 [1]; supplier payments 2,600 + 46,000 - 3,100 = 45,500 [1];
bank 1,900 + 138,800 - 45,500 - 38,500 - 16,300 - 24,000 = **16,400** [1] **[3]**
**(c)**

```
Budgeted statement of financial position at 31 March 2028
Machinery (60,000 - 26,400)                    33,600
Inventory 4,200; trade receivables 8,400;
other receivables 900; bank 16,400             29,900
Trade payables                                 (3,100)
Net current assets                             26,800
Net assets                                     60,400
Capital 51,900 + 32,500 - 24,000               60,400
```

Machinery at carrying amount [1]; current assets including the prepayment [1]; net current assets [1]; capital **60,400** [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The bank figure uses the 16,300 paid; the income statement uses only 15,400. Mixing them unbalances the statement.

**9.** It depends on forecasts of sales and payment patterns [1]; if customers pay later than planned, the predicted balance is wrong and finance may be arranged too late [1]. It ignores profitability [1], so a business can show a healthy cash balance while making losses, for example by delaying supplier payments [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Explain" needs each limitation developed into its effect on the business; a bare statement of a limitation is only half an answer.

**10. (a)** Income 2,400 adverse [1]; staff costs 740 adverse [1]; utilities 350 favourable, marketing nil [1]; profit 16,300 budget and 13,510 actual, **2,790 adverse** [1] **[4]**
**(b)** Investigate why membership income fell, for example by checking cancellations [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* A difference without the word favourable or adverse is incomplete.

**11.** For the view: a small gym has few income and cost lines, which the owner may know well [1]. Detailed budgets take time from customers and staff [1]. Adding a little to last year's figures, incremental budgeting, is quick and cheap [1] and suits stable costs such as rent [1].
Against: question 10 shows profit 2,790 below plan; without a budget the owner would not have spotted this so clearly [1]. A budget names who is responsible for staff costs, which ran 740 over [1]. A cash budget would warn early if falling memberships threatened cash [1]. Incremental budgeting carries past waste forward [1]; zero-based budgeting of discretionary spending such as marketing would test whether its 3,000 brings in members [1]. However, membership forecasts are uncertain [1], and over-ambitious targets could demotivate staff [1].
Judgement: the view is mostly wrong; a simple budget with monthly comparison is cheap and gave useful warnings here, and ZBB could be used occasionally for marketing while routine costs stay incremental [1] **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* An "evaluate" answer needs points on both sides, applied to the gym's own figures, and a justified conclusion; a generic list of benefits is much weaker.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Working materials or labour from sales instead of production.
- Receiving credit sales in the wrong month.
- Including depreciation, or a payment outside the period, in the cash budget.
- Charging cash paid, not the expense incurred, in the income statement.
- Writing an evaluation with no judgement.
