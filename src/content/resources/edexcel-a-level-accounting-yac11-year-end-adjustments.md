This guide covers outcomes **1.3.3 Prepaid or accrued income or expenditure**, **1.3.4 The nature and use of provisions**, **1.3.5 Provisions for depreciation** and **1.3.6 Irrecoverable debts and allowances for irrecoverable debts**. They sit in Topic 1.3, Financial statements of organisations, in Unit 1 (The Accounting System and Costing) of the Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Subsidiary/Advanced Level in Accounting (YAC11), Specification Issue 2, September 2018. This is International AS content. All amounts are in dollars and every business named is invented.

Use it with the [financial statements of organisations revision notes](/resources/edexcel-ial-accounting-financial-statements-of-organisations-revision-notes/) and the [year-end adjustments practice questions](/resources/edexcel-a-level-accounting-yac11-year-end-adjustments-practice/). The statements the adjustments feed are taught in the [sole trader guide](/resources/edexcel-a-level-accounting-yac11-financial-statements-of-sole-traders/), and the methods of depreciation are in the [non-current asset depreciation guide](/resources/edexcel-a-level-accounting-yac11-non-current-asset-depreciation/).

## What the specification asks for

| Outcome | Wording | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|---|
| 1.3.3 | Prepaid or accrued income or expenditure | Adjust expenses and income to the amount that belongs to the year; show the balances in the statement of financial position |
| 1.3.4 | The nature and use of provisions | Define a provision and explain why businesses create them |
| 1.3.5 | Provisions for depreciation | Calculate the charge, record it and show cost, accumulated depreciation and net book value |
| 1.3.6 | Irrecoverable debts and allowances for irrecoverable debts | Write off debts, create and adjust an allowance, and show receivables net of the allowance |

The idea behind all four is the **accruals concept**: income and expenses belong to the period in which they are earned or used, not the period in which cash moves. The **prudence** concept adds that losses are recognised as soon as they are likely, but gains only when they are realised.

## 1.3.3 Prepayments and accruals

A trial balance shows cash paid or received during the year. Four situations need an adjustment.

| Situation | Effect on the statement of profit or loss | Where the balance goes |
|---|---|---|
| Expense **accrued** (used but not yet paid) | Add to the expense | Current liability: accruals |
| Expense **prepaid** (paid but not yet used) | Deduct from the expense | Current asset: prepayments |
| Income **accrued** (earned but not yet received) | Add to the income | Current asset: other receivables |
| Income **received in advance** | Deduct from the income | Current liability: deferred income |

### The ledger method

An expense account for the year always contains: the cash paid, plus any opening accrual or minus any opening prepayment, plus any closing accrual or minus any closing prepayment.

**Example: insurance.** Opening prepayment 450, paid during the year 1,860, closing prepayment 520.

Expense for the year = 1,860 + 450 − 520 = **1,790**.

The opening prepayment was paid last year but used up this year, so it is added. The closing prepayment is paid now but belongs to next year, so it is deducted.

**Example: electricity.** Opening accrual 380, paid during the year 3,150, closing accrual 520.

Expense for the year = 3,150 − 380 + 520 = **3,290**.

The opening accrual was paid this year but belonged to last year, so it is removed. The closing accrual belongs to this year but is unpaid, so it is added.

A quick test is to ask what the expense *should be* for the year. If the business rents premises for 12 months at 800 a month, the expense is 9,600, whatever was paid.

## Worked example: Marlow Garden Supplies

Marlow Garden Supplies has gross profit of $68,000 for the year ended 30 September 2026. The trial balance shows wages 31,200, rent 9,600, insurance 2,640, electricity 3,300, general expenses 4,100 and commission received 2,100.

**Notes at the year end**

1. Wages of 900 are owing.
2. Rent of 1,200 has been paid in advance.
3. Insurance of 660 has been paid in advance.
4. Electricity of 450 is owing.
5. Commission of 300 has been received in advance.
6. Depreciation: vehicles cost 24,000 with provision b/d of 9,000, at 20% reducing balance; fittings cost 10,000, 10% straight line on cost.
7. Trade receivables are 18,500. A debt of 500 is to be written off. The allowance for irrecoverable debts is 600 and is to be 4% of the remaining receivables.

**Step 1: adjust each expense**

| Item | Working | Amount for the year |
|---|---|---:|
| Wages | 31,200 + 900 | 32,100 |
| Rent | 9,600 − 1,200 | 8,400 |
| Insurance | 2,640 − 660 | 1,980 |
| Electricity | 3,300 + 450 | 3,750 |
| Commission received | 2,100 − 300 | 1,800 |

**Step 2: depreciation**

Vehicles: (24,000 − 9,000) × 20% = 3,000. Fittings: 10,000 × 10% = 1,000. Total charge 4,000.

**Step 3: irrecoverable debts**

Receivables after the write-off: 18,500 − 500 = 18,000. New allowance 18,000 × 4% = 720. The allowance rises from 600 to 720, so the **increase** of 120 is an expense. Total expense: 500 written off + 120 = 620.

**Step 4: profit for the year**

| | $ | $ |
|---|---:|---:|
| Gross profit | | 68,000 |
| Commission received | | 1,800 |
| | | 69,800 |
| Wages | 32,100 | |
| Rent | 8,400 | |
| Insurance | 1,980 | |
| Electricity | 3,750 | |
| General expenses | 4,100 | |
| Depreciation | 4,000 | |
| Irrecoverable debts | 620 | (54,950) |
| **Profit for the year** | | **14,850** |

**Check:** before adjustments, profit would have been 68,000 + 2,100 − 50,840 = 19,260. The adjustments reduce it by 4,410. The expense changes net to −510 (900 − 1,200 − 660 + 450), commission falls by 300, depreciation adds 4,000 and irrecoverable debts add 620, giving 510 − 300 − 4,000 − 620 = −4,410. Both routes agree.

**Step 5: statement of financial position extracts**

| | $ | $ |
|---|---:|---:|
| Trade receivables | 18,000 | |
| Less allowance for irrecoverable debts | (720) | 17,280 |
| Prepayments (1,200 + 660) | | 1,860 |
| Accruals (900 + 450) | | 1,350 |
| Deferred income | | 300 |
| Vehicles: cost 24,000, accumulated depreciation 12,000, net book value | | 12,000 |

Accumulated depreciation on vehicles is 9,000 + 3,000 = 12,000, so the net book value is 24,000 − 12,000 = 12,000.

## 1.3.4 The nature and use of provisions

A **provision** is an amount set aside out of profit for a cost or loss that has arisen from past events and is likely to happen, but where the amount or timing is uncertain. It differs from an accrual, where the amount is known or can be measured with reasonable certainty, and from a reserve, which is a part of the owner's interest and is not set aside for a particular expense.

Businesses create provisions because of two concepts. **Prudence** says that a likely loss is recognised now rather than waiting for it to happen. **Accruals** (matching) says that the cost belongs to the period in which the related revenue was earned, so a sale made this year should carry its share of future bad debts or depreciation.

Common provisions for a sole trader are the provision for depreciation and the allowance for irrecoverable debts. A provision may also be made for a legal claim or a warranty. The charge goes in the statement of profit or loss, and the balance is shown in the statement of financial position, usually deducted from the related asset.

## 1.3.5 Provisions for depreciation

Depreciation spreads the cost of a non-current asset over its useful life. Each year's charge is an expense; the total charged so far is the provision for depreciation (accumulated depreciation), which is deducted from cost.

- **Straight line:** (cost − residual value) ÷ useful life. A machine costing 40,000 depreciated at 10% on cost has a charge of 4,000 every year.
- **Reducing balance:** a fixed percentage of the net book value. A machine of cost 40,000 with provision b/d of 12,000 at 25% has a charge of (40,000 − 12,000) × 25% = 7,000. The provision becomes 12,000 + 7,000 = 19,000 and the net book value is 40,000 − 19,000 = 21,000.

The double entry is debit depreciation expense, credit provision for depreciation. The asset account stays at cost. The expense account is closed to the statement of profit or loss at the end of the year.

## 1.3.6 Irrecoverable debts and allowances

**Irrecoverable debts** are amounts owed by customers that the business has decided it will not receive. They are written off: debit irrecoverable debts expense, credit trade receivables (the customer's account).

An **allowance for irrecoverable debts** is a provision against receivables that may prove irrecoverable in future. It can be a *general* allowance, a percentage of receivables, or a *specific* allowance against named customers.

**The rule.** Write off first, then calculate the allowance on the remaining receivables.

**Example: increase.** Receivables 15,400, write off 400, allowance of 5% required, existing allowance 620.

- Remaining receivables 15,400 − 400 = 15,000
- New allowance 15,000 × 5% = 750
- Increase 750 − 620 = 130, charged as an expense
- Statement of financial position: receivables 15,000 less allowance 750 = 14,250

**Example: decrease.** If the allowance is to be 3% of 16,000, it is 480. If the old allowance was 620, it falls by 140. A decrease is *credited* to the statement of profit or loss, as a reduction of expenses.

**Irrecoverable debts recovered.** If a customer written off later pays, debit bank and credit irrecoverable debts recovered. The recovery is income, or a reduction of the irrecoverable debts expense.

Only the **change** in the allowance goes through the statement of profit or loss. Charging the whole allowance every year is a very common error.

## Common mistakes

- Adding a prepayment to an expense or deducting an accrual from it. Test: should the expense be bigger or smaller?
- Forgetting the opening prepayment or accrual in a ledger account.
- Calculating the allowance on the original receivables instead of the figure after the write-off.
- Charging the whole new allowance, not just the increase.
- Treating a decrease in the allowance as an expense.
- Applying reducing balance to cost instead of net book value.
- Recording the provision for depreciation as a debit.
- Putting prepayments in current liabilities or deferred income in current assets.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate** questions need a working line for each figure. Write the adjustment next to the original (for example 31,200 + 900).
- **Prepare** questions are marked on the adjusted figures, so adjust before you enter the statement.
- **Explain** (the use of a provision) needs a reason, such as prudence, linked to a consequence such as profit not being overstated.
- Keep a list of the notes and tick each one off as you use it. Missed notes are the main source of lost marks in long questions.
