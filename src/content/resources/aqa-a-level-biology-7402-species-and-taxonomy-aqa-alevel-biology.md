This guide covers **3.4.5 Species and taxonomy** from AQA AS and A-level Biology (7401/7402), in Topic 4, *Genetic information, variation and relationships between organisms*. It is AS content, examined on both AS papers and on A-level Papers 1 and 3. The broader guide to [species, taxonomy and investigating diversity](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-species-taxonomy-and-investigating-diversity/) sets out the definitions alongside 3.4.7; this page goes further on applying them to data: reading hierarchies, judging relatedness from antibody and sequence results, and drawing conclusions from courtship observations. ## Applying the species definition

The test is whether two organisms **can produce fertile offspring**. Breeding data in exams usually fall into three cases:

| Result of a cross | Conclusion |
|---|---|
| Offspring produced, and they breed successfully | Same species |
| Offspring produced, but they are infertile | Different species |
| No offspring | Different species (or, if they are the same species, something else prevented mating) |

The mule, produced when a horse and a donkey mate, is the standard example of an infertile hybrid: horses and donkeys are therefore separate species. Note that "can interbreed" alone is not the definition; the offspring must be fertile.

## Courtship behaviour

Courtship is a sequence of behaviours performed before mating. The specification gives two roles:

1. **A necessary precursor to successful mating.** Mating happens only if the sequence is completed. Each behaviour by one partner triggers the next response from the other.
2. **Species recognition.** Courtship signals are species specific, so an animal responds only to a member of its own species. This prevents mating attempts with other species, which would produce no offspring or infertile ones and so waste time, energy and gametes.

Many textbooks also link courtship to recognising a mate of the opposite sex, recognising a partner that is ready to mate, and forming a pair bond. The male three-spined stickleback, for example, performs a zigzag display that leads a receptive female to his nest; a female responds only when the full sequence is performed.

**Interpreting courtship data.** A typical question gives observations such as "females responded to recorded calls of their own species but not to calls of a related species". The conclusion is that the call is a species-recognition signal. If a question shows that a hybrid's call is intermediate and attracts neither parent species, you can suggest that courtship differences reduce interbreeding.

## Phylogenetic classification and the hierarchy

**Phylogenetic** means based on evolutionary history. Species are grouped by how recently they shared a common ancestor, not just by how similar they look.

The hierarchy, from largest group to smallest:

**domain → kingdom → phylum → class → order → family → genus → species**

Two rules define it: smaller groups are placed within larger groups, and there is **no overlap** — a species belongs to only one genus, a genus to only one family, and so on.

### Worked example 1: reading a classification

| Taxon | Grey wolf | Red fox | Brown bear |
|---|---|---|---|
| Domain | Eukarya | Eukarya | Eukarya |
| Kingdom | Animalia | Animalia | Animalia |
| Phylum | Chordata | Chordata | Chordata |
| Class | Mammalia | Mammalia | Mammalia |
| Order | Carnivora | Carnivora | Carnivora |
| Family | Canidae | Canidae | Ursidae |
| Genus | *Canis* | *Vulpes* | *Ursus* |
| Species | *lupus* | *vulpes* | *arctos* |

> The wolf and fox share every taxon down to family but differ at genus. The bear shares taxa with them only down to order. So the wolf and fox share a more recent common ancestor with each other than either does with the bear.

The lowest taxon two species share tells you how closely related they are.

## Binomials

Each species has a two-part scientific name: the genus, then the species. *Canis lupus*: genus *Canis*, species *lupus*.

- Genus starts with a capital letter; species name all lower case.
- Printed in italics; underlined separately when handwritten.
- The binomial is universal, so it avoids confusion between common names in different languages and regions.
- Two species in the same genus share the first word, which shows close relationship.

## Immunology: comparing proteins with antibodies

The logic: closely related species have similar proteins, because their genes have had less time to diverge. Antibodies bind to antigens with a complementary shape.

1. A protein (for example from blood serum) is taken from species A and injected into another animal, which makes antibodies against it.
2. These antibodies are mixed with the same protein from species B, C and D.
3. The more similar a protein is to the original, the more antibodies bind and the more precipitate forms.

### Worked example 2 (invented data)

Antibodies against a serum protein from species A were mixed with serum from other species. Precipitate is shown relative to species A itself.

| Species | Precipitate (% of species A) |
|---|---|
| A | 100 |
| B | 85 |
| C | 52 |
| D | 9 |

> B is most closely related to A: its protein binds the most antibodies, so it has the most similar shape and therefore the most similar amino acid sequence. D is most distantly related. The method compares only one protein, so a conclusion is more reliable when several proteins are compared.

## Genome sequencing

Sequencing gives the base sequence of DNA directly. Comparing the sequences of the same genes, or whole genomes, shows how many bases differ. The fewer the differences, the more recent the common ancestor. Sequencing gives far more data than comparing a few visible features or a single protein, and can reveal relationships that appearance hides, because unrelated organisms can look similar when they live in similar environments.

### Worked example 3 (invented data)

Percentage of bases that differ in one gene:

| | P | Q | R |
|---|---|---|---|
| P | — | 2 | 11 |
| Q | 2 | — | 10 |
| R | 11 | 10 | — |

> P and Q differ by only 2%, so they share the most recent common ancestor; R branched off earlier. In a phylogenetic tree, P and Q would be on neighbouring branches joined at the most recent branch point, with R joining further back.

## Common mistakes

- Defining a species as organisms that "can interbreed", leaving out "fertile offspring".
- Saying courtship "attracts a mate" without species recognition.
- Writing the hierarchy out of order; a mnemonic helps, but check that domain comes first.
- Writing *Canis Lupus* or *canis lupus*.
- Saying more precipitate means a *less* closely related species.
- Concluding relatedness from one protein or one gene without noting the limitation.
- Recalling three domain or five kingdom details, which are not required.

## Exam technique for AQA command words

| Command word | What earns the marks |
|---|---|
| Explain | Give the mechanism: similar DNA → similar amino acid sequence → similar shape → more antibody binding |
| Use the data | Quote the values that show which species are most and least related |
| Suggest | Apply the species definition or courtship role to an unfamiliar organism |
| Evaluate | Weigh the evidence: how many genes or proteins, and whether data agree |
