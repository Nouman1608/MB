This guide goes deeper into **3.2.1 Scalars and vectors**, the opening subtopic of Section 3.2 Mechanics and materials in the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630) specification. It is International AS content, assessed in Unit 1 (Mechanics, materials and atoms). Every later mechanics subtopic, from moments to momentum, assumes you can add, subtract and resolve vectors without hesitation.

When you have read the guide, condense it with the [scalars and vectors revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-scalars-and-vectors-oxfordaqa-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [scalars and vectors practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-scalars-and-vectors-oxfordaqa-practice/). For a combined treatment of vectors and moments, see the [vectors, equilibrium of forces and moments study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-vectors-equilibrium-of-forces-and-moments/).

## What the specification asks for

- the nature of scalars and vectors, with examples including velocity and speed, mass, force and weight, acceleration, and displacement and distance
- addition of vectors by calculation or by scale drawing
- conditions for equilibrium for two or three coplanar forces acting at a point
- appreciation that objects at rest or moving with constant velocity are in equilibrium.

## Scalars and vectors

A **scalar** has magnitude only. A **vector** has magnitude and direction. The specification pairs quantities so that you can contrast them:

| Scalar | Vector | The difference |
|---|---|---|
| Distance | Displacement | Displacement is the straight-line distance in a stated direction from the start |
| Speed | Velocity | Velocity is the rate of change of displacement |
| Mass | Weight | Weight is a force, mg, acting towards the centre of the Earth |
| — | Force | Every force has a direction |
| — | Acceleration | Rate of change of velocity, so it has a direction |

Other scalars you will meet include energy, power, time, temperature and density; momentum, from 3.2.6, is another vector.

### Worked example 1: a lap of a track

**Question.** A runner completes one lap of a 400 m track in 80 s, finishing where she started. Find her average speed and average velocity.

**Method.** Average speed = distance ÷ time = 400 ÷ 80 = **5.0 m s⁻¹**. Her displacement is zero, so average velocity = 0 ÷ 80 = **0 m s⁻¹**.

**Comment.** Only a vector can be zero while motion is clearly happening. If a question gives a closed path, that is the cue to check whether it wants a scalar or a vector.

### Signs in one dimension

On a straight line, direction is shown by sign. Choose a positive direction, write it down, and give every vector a sign. A ball moving up at 4.0 m s⁻¹ and later down at 4.0 m s⁻¹ has velocities of +4.0 and −4.0 m s⁻¹ (taking up as positive): same speed, different velocity.

## Adding vectors

Vectors are added **tip to tail**: draw the second vector starting at the tip of the first. The resultant runs from the tail of the first to the tip of the last. The order does not matter.

### Perpendicular vectors

Use Pythagoras for the magnitude and trigonometry for the direction.

**Worked example 2: an aircraft in a crosswind.** An aircraft heads due north at 220 m s⁻¹ relative to the air. A wind blows towards the east at 35 m s⁻¹. Find its resultant velocity.

1. Magnitude = √(220² + 35²) = √49 625 = **223 m s⁻¹**.
2. Angle east of north: tan θ = 35/220, so θ = **9.0°**.
3. Resultant: 223 m s⁻¹ on a bearing 9.0° east of north.

### Vectors at any angle: resolve, add, recombine

The most reliable method for any pair of vectors is to resolve each into perpendicular components, add the components, then recombine.

**Resolving.** A vector F at angle θ to the horizontal has components:

- horizontal: F cos θ
- vertical: F sin θ

The cosine goes with the component *adjacent* to the angle you were given. If θ is measured from the vertical, the components swap.

**Worked example 3: two forces at 60°.** Forces of 30 N (along the x-axis) and 20 N (at 60° to the x-axis) act at a point. Find the resultant.

1. x-components: 30 + 20 cos 60° = 30 + 10 = 40 N.
2. y-components: 0 + 20 sin 60° = 17.3 N.
3. Magnitude = √(40² + 17.3²) = **43.6 N**.
4. Direction: tan θ = 17.3/40, so θ = **23.4°** above the x-axis.

**Check.** The resultant lies between the two forces and closer to the larger one: 23.4° is between 0° and 60°, nearer 0°. A resultant outside that range means a sign error.

### Subtracting vectors

A change in a vector is final minus initial: Δv = v − u. Subtracting a vector is the same as adding its reverse.

**Worked example 4: turning a corner.** A cyclist travelling east at 6.0 m s⁻¹ turns and travels north at 6.0 m s⁻¹. Find the change in velocity.

1. Δv = v − u = (6.0 north) + (6.0 west).
2. Magnitude = √(6.0² + 6.0²) = **8.5 m s⁻¹**, directed towards the **north-west**.

**Comment.** Her speed has not changed, yet her velocity has changed by 8.5 m s⁻¹. A change of direction alone is a change of velocity, so a resultant force must have acted.

### Scale drawing

1. Choose a scale that makes the diagram large, for example 1 cm : 5 N, and state it.
2. Draw the first vector with a ruler and protractor.
3. Draw the second from its tip, at the correct angle.
4. Draw and measure the resultant; convert with the scale; measure its angle.

A drawing can only be as good as your ruler and protractor: a 1 mm or 1° slip matters less on a large diagram, so fill the space available and use a sharp pencil.

## Equilibrium of coplanar forces at a point

A point object is in **equilibrium** when the resultant force on it is zero.

- **Two forces:** they must be equal in size, opposite in direction and act along the same line.
- **Three forces:** drawn tip to tail, they form a **closed triangle**. Equivalently, the components in any two perpendicular directions each sum to zero.

### Worked example 5: a hanging sign

**Question.** A sign of weight 120 N hangs from the midpoint of a rope; each half of the rope makes 25° with the horizontal. Find the tension in the rope.

1. Horizontal: T cos 25° (left) = T cos 25° (right), so the tensions are equal by symmetry.
2. Vertical: 2T sin 25° = 120.
3. T = 120 ÷ (2 × 0.423) = **142 N**.

**Comment.** The tension is larger than the weight. The flatter the rope, the smaller sin θ, and the larger the tension: a perfectly horizontal rope could not hold any weight.

### Worked example 6: finding a third force

**Question.** Two horizontal forces act on a point: 30 N east and 40 N north. Find the third force that keeps the point in equilibrium.

1. Resultant of the two = √(30² + 40²) = 50 N, at tan⁻¹(40/30) = 53.1° north of east.
2. The third force must cancel it: **50 N at 53.1° south of west**.

## Equilibrium does not mean "at rest"

An object moving at **constant velocity** is in equilibrium too: zero resultant force, zero acceleration. A box pushed across a floor at steady speed has a push equal to the friction on it; a skydiver at terminal speed has drag equal to weight. If the velocity is changing in size or direction, the object is not in equilibrium.

## Common mistakes

- Calling weight a scalar, or giving mass a direction.
- Adding magnitudes of vectors that are not parallel: 30 N + 40 N at right angles is 50 N, not 70 N.
- Using sin where cos is needed after measuring the angle from the vertical.
- Writing Δv = u − v.
- Giving a resultant with no direction, or a direction with no reference ("at 37°" but from what?).
- Saying an object moving at constant speed in a circle is in equilibrium.

## Exam technique for OxfordAQA command words

- **State** the difference between a scalar and a vector: magnitude only, versus magnitude and direction.
- **Calculate**: show the components or the Pythagoras step, and give both magnitude and direction.
- **Draw** a scale diagram: state the scale, label each vector, and measure the resultant.
- **Explain** why an object is in equilibrium: zero resultant force, so zero acceleration; constant velocity or at rest.
- **Show that**: give the calculated value to one more significant figure than the value in the question.
