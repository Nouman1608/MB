This guide goes deeper into **3.3.9.2 Acylation**, the second half of section 3.3.9 Carboxylic acids and derivatives in the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International A2 content, assessed in Unit 4 (Organic 2 and Physical 2) and in the synoptic Unit 5, and Required practical 10 is attached to it. Questions on acylation reward precise mechanisms, correct by-products and the ability to work backwards from a target molecule, so those are the focus here.

## What the specification asks for

- The structures of **acid anhydrides**, **acyl chlorides** and **amides**.
- The **nucleophilic addition–elimination** reactions of water, alcohols, ammonia and primary amines with acyl chlorides and acid anhydrides.
- The **industrial advantages of ethanoic anhydride over ethanoyl chloride** in making aspirin.
- Outline the mechanism of addition–elimination for **acyl chlorides** with all four nucleophiles (no mechanism is needed for anhydrides).
- Required practical 10: prepare a pure organic solid and test its purity.

## Naming acyl compounds

All three families contain the **acyl group**, R–CO–, and take their name from the carboxylic acid with the same carbon count.

| Family | Ending | Example | Name |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acyl chloride | -oyl chloride | (CH₃)₂CHCOCl | 2-methylpropanoyl chloride |
| Acid anhydride | -oic anhydride | (CH₃CH₂CO)₂O | propanoic anhydride |
| Primary amide | -amide | CH₃CH₂CH₂CONH₂ | butanamide |
| N-substituted amide | N-alkyl...amide | CH₃CONHCH₂CH₂CH₃ | N-propylethanamide |

In an N-substituted amide, the group on nitrogen comes from the primary amine and the "-amide" part from the acyl compound. The letter N shows that the alkyl group is on nitrogen, not on the carbon chain.

## Why acyl chlorides react so readily

The carbonyl carbon of an acyl chloride is bonded to two electronegative atoms, oxygen and chlorine. Both pull electron density away, so the carbon carries a large δ+ charge and is attacked readily by nucleophiles with a lone pair. Chloride is then lost easily as Cl⁻. As a result, acyl chlorides react rapidly with water at room temperature, giving steamy fumes of hydrogen chloride in moist air. Acid anhydrides react in the same way but more slowly, so they are often warmed.

Compare making an ester three ways:

| Reagent with an alcohol | Conditions | Extent | By-product |
|---|---|---|---|
| Carboxylic acid | Warm, conc. H₂SO₄ catalyst | Reversible, incomplete | Water |
| Acid anhydride | Warm | Goes to completion | Carboxylic acid |
| Acyl chloride | Room temperature | Goes to completion, vigorous | Hydrogen chloride |

## The mechanism with water

The same four steps apply whichever nucleophile is used. Learn them once and change only the attacking atom.

### Worked example 1: 2-methylpropanoyl chloride and water

Outline the mechanism and give the products.

1. **Addition, arrow 1**: a lone pair on the oxygen of H₂O attacks the δ+ carbonyl carbon.
2. **Addition, arrow 2**: the C=O π bond pair moves onto oxygen, which becomes O⁻.
3. **Intermediate**: a tetrahedral carbon carrying O⁻, Cl, the (CH₃)₂CH– group and an –O⁺H₂ group. Show both charges.
4. **Elimination, arrows 3 and 4**: a lone pair on O⁻ moves back to re-form C=O, and the C–Cl bond pair moves onto chlorine, which leaves as Cl⁻.
5. **Loss of H⁺, arrow 5**: an O–H bond pair on the –O⁺H₂ group moves onto oxygen, releasing H⁺ (which joins Cl⁻ as HCl).
6. Products: **2-methylpropanoic acid**, (CH₃)₂CHCOOH, and **HCl**.

`(CH₃)₂CHCOCl + H₂O → (CH₃)₂CHCOOH + HCl`

With an alcohol, the attacking atom is again O and the product is an ester. With ammonia or a primary amine, the attacking atom is N, the intermediate carries N⁺, and a second molecule of the nitrogen nucleophile removes the H⁺. Mark schemes look for: the arrow from the lone pair (not from the atom), the arrow to oxygen from the C=O bond, the charged intermediate, re-formation of C=O with loss of Cl⁻, and removal of H⁺.

## Working backwards from a target

Split the target at the bond that formed: C(=O)–O for an ester or acid, C(=O)–N for an amide. The side with the C=O came from the acyl compound; the other side came from the nucleophile.

### Worked example 2: choosing reagents

Give an acyl chloride and a nucleophile that make each compound, and write the equation.

- **(a) Methyl benzoate, C₆H₅COOCH₃.** Split at C(=O)–O: benzoyl chloride and methanol.
  `C₆H₅COCl + CH₃OH → C₆H₅COOCH₃ + HCl`
- **(b) 2-Methylpropanamide, (CH₃)₂CHCONH₂.** Split at C(=O)–N: 2-methylpropanoyl chloride and ammonia. Two NH₃ are needed.
  `(CH₃)₂CHCOCl + 2NH₃ → (CH₃)₂CHCONH₂ + NH₄Cl`
- **(c) N-Propylethanamide, CH₃CONHCH₂CH₂CH₃.** Split at C(=O)–N: ethanoyl chloride and propylamine, two molecules of amine.
  `CH₃COCl + 2CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₂ → CH₃CONHCH₂CH₂CH₃ + CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₃⁺Cl⁻`

With an anhydride in place of the acyl chloride, the by-product changes: ethanoic anhydride and propylamine give N-propylethanamide and ethanoic acid (or its propylammonium salt if a second amine molecule is present).

### Worked example 3: a reacting-mass problem

3.14 g of ethanoyl chloride (Mr 78.5) is added to excess propylamine. After purification, 3.03 g of N-propylethanamide (Mr 101.0) is obtained. Calculate the minimum mass of propylamine (Mr 59.0) needed and the percentage yield.

1. n(ethanoyl chloride) = 3.14 ÷ 78.5 = 0.0400 mol.
2. Two amine molecules per acyl chloride: n(propylamine) = 0.0800 mol, mass = 0.0800 × 59.0 = **4.72 g**.
3. Maximum amide = 0.0400 mol × 101.0 = 4.04 g.
4. Yield = 3.03 ÷ 4.04 × 100 = **75.0%**.

Using a 1 : 1 ratio in step 2 gives half the true mass of amine, and the reaction would stop half-way because the HCl formed protonates the remaining amine.

## Aspirin: the industrial choice

Aspirin is made by acylating the –OH group of 2-hydroxybenzoic acid. The specification asks why ethanoic anhydride is chosen over ethanoyl chloride. A good answer gives reasons linked to cost, safety and control: the anhydride is cheaper, less corrosive, reacts less violently, is less readily hydrolysed by moisture, and its by-product is ethanoic acid rather than corrosive hydrogen chloride gas.

### Worked example 4: atom economy is not the whole story

Calculate the percentage atom economy for aspirin (C₉H₈O₄, Mr 180.0) by each route. Mr: 2-hydroxybenzoic acid 138.0, ethanoic anhydride 102.0, ethanoyl chloride 78.5.

- Anhydride route: 180.0 ÷ (138.0 + 102.0) × 100 = **75.0%**.
- Acyl chloride route: 180.0 ÷ (138.0 + 78.5) × 100 = **83.1%**.

The acyl chloride route wastes fewer atoms, yet industry uses the anhydride. The safety and handling advantages outweigh the difference, and the ethanoic acid by-product is less hazardous to deal with than hydrogen chloride. If a question asks you to evaluate, give both sides like this. Atom economy is also discussed in the [organic synthesis guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-organic-synthesis/).

## Required practical 10 in this subtopic

Aspirin, or an amide made from an acyl compound, is a typical solid for this practical. Points that link to acylation:

- Acyl chlorides and anhydrides react with water, so glassware must be dry until the acylation is complete.
- A recrystallisation solvent should dissolve the product well when hot and poorly when cold; soluble impurities stay in the cold solution.
- Purity is tested by melting point. A sample melting sharply at the data-book value is pure; one melting lower and over a wider range still contains impurities.

## Common mistakes

- Calling the mechanism "nucleophilic substitution". The specification term is nucleophilic addition–elimination.
- Starting arrow 1 from the oxygen or nitrogen atom rather than from its lone pair.
- Leaving the charges off the intermediate, or forgetting the final loss of H⁺.
- Writing HCl as the by-product of an anhydride reaction.
- Using one molecule of ammonia or amine per acyl chloride.
- Naming CH₃CONHCH₃ "methylethanamide" without the N.
- Saying ethanoyl chloride is avoided because its atom economy is lower; it is higher.

## Exam technique

- **Outline the mechanism**: draw every curly arrow, the intermediate with charges and the products; a description in words must say where each arrow starts and ends.
- **Explain** the reactivity of acyl chlorides: name both electronegative atoms and the δ+ carbon.
- **Suggest** reagents: give names or formulae and the equation, including the second molecule of amine.
- **Evaluate** a route: combine atom economy with safety, cost and by-products.
