This guide covers the first strand of geographical enquiry in section 3.3.2 of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Geography (9230) specification, Version 4.3: "1 Suitable question for geographical enquiry". The specification lists "The factors that need to be considered when selecting suitable questions/hypotheses for geographical enquiry", "The geographical theory/concept underpinning the enquiry", "Appropriate sources of primary and secondary evidence, including locations for fieldwork" and "The potential risks of both human and physical fieldwork and how these risks might be reduced". Exam questions can also be set on "planning the enquiry, defining the aims, deciding on a location for study, setting up a hypothesis or enquiry question", on "researching the background to the enquiry from secondary sources, including the internet", and on "considering health and safety aspects of the enquiry". For the whole unit, start with the [Geographical and fieldwork skills study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-geography-9230-geographical-and-fieldwork-skills/).

## Where planning is assessed

Paper 3 has three compulsory sections of 20 marks. **Section B** tests the enquiry process "based on fieldwork in unfamiliar contexts", so you may be asked to plan or judge an enquiry you have never done. **Section C** tests your own individual enquiry, and you "will have to identify the title of their individual enquiry". Planning questions appear in both, so you need general principles and the details of your own plan.

## Aims, questions and hypotheses

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Aim | What the enquiry sets out to find out, in general terms | To investigate how a river changes downstream |
| Enquiry question | A question the data will answer | How do river width and depth change downstream along the (named) river? |
| Hypothesis | A testable statement that predicts a result | River width increases with distance downstream |
| Sub-questions | Smaller questions that build towards the main one | Does velocity also change? Does pebble size decrease? |

A hypothesis is useful because the data can show it to be supported or not supported. A question is useful when you do not want to predict the answer in advance.

## What makes a question or hypothesis suitable

The specification asks about "the factors that need to be considered when selecting suitable questions/hypotheses". Check each idea against these factors:

- **Geographical**: it is about places, processes or patterns, and links to a theory or concept.
- **Testable**: it names variables you can actually measure, such as width, pedestrian numbers or vegetation cover.
- **Specific**: it names the place and, if needed, the time.
- **Manageable**: the data can be collected in the time available, with the equipment and people you have.
- **Safe and accessible**: sites can be reached legally and safely.
- **Likely to show variation**: if every site will give the same result, there is nothing to analyse.
- **Supported by secondary data**: background information exists to set the context.

**Weak:** "What is the town like?" (not testable, no variable, no theory).
**Better:** "Do pedestrian numbers decrease with distance from the centre of (named town)?"

## The theory or concept behind the enquiry

The specification requires "the geographical theory/concept underpinning the enquiry". The theory gives you a reason to expect a pattern, which is what your hypothesis predicts. Examples that schools often use:

| Enquiry focus | Theory or concept | Expected pattern |
|---|---|---|
| River channel change | The idea that a river's channel and flow change downstream as more water joins it | Width, depth and velocity increase; sediment becomes smaller and rounder |
| Land use in a town | Urban land-use models and the idea that land is most valuable near the centre | Shops and offices at the centre; housing density falls outwards |
| Sand dune ecosystems | Succession: plant communities change as dunes become older and more stable | Vegetation cover and variety increase inland |
| Environmental quality | Differences between areas of a city in housing, open space and deprivation | Quality varies between older inner areas and newer outer areas |
| Microclimate | Surfaces and buildings change local temperature and wind | Warmer over tarmac and between buildings than over grass |

Choose a theory that fits your question and say what it predicts. Your conclusion will later compare your results with that prediction.

## Choosing a location

A suitable location:

- clearly shows the feature or process you are studying;
- has enough sites to show change, such as a river with several accessible points, or a road leading out of a town centre;
- is safe and legal to access, with permission where land is private;
- can be reached in the time available;
- has secondary data available, such as maps or census information.

Name the location precisely in your plan and mark sites on a sketch map.

## Background research from secondary sources

The specification expects background research "before embarking on any data collection". It helps you choose sites, refine your hypothesis and understand the context. Useful secondary sources include:

- large-scale maps to locate sites and estimate distances and heights;
- census and local authority data on population, housing or land use;
- geo-spatial data in a geographical information system (GIS) and satellite imagery, both named in the specification;
- previous studies, local news reports and websites of organisations such as river or park authorities.

Record every source you use: the specification expects a bibliography "listing sources consulted at the beginning of the enquiry".

## Primary and secondary evidence

Plan which evidence will be primary (collected by you or your group, such as measurements, counts and questionnaires) and which secondary (collected by others, such as census data). Many good enquiries combine the two: primary data for the local detail and secondary data for comparison and context.

## Risks and how to reduce them

The specification asks about "the potential risks of both human and physical fieldwork and how these risks might be reduced". A **risk assessment** names each hazard, how likely and serious it is, and the precaution.

| Setting | Risk | How to reduce it |
|---|---|---|
| River | Deep or fast water; slippery rocks | Check river levels beforehand; work only in shallow sections; wear suitable footwear; never work alone |
| River | Water-borne infection | Cover cuts; wash hands; do not drink the water |
| Coast | Incoming tide cutting off the beach | Check tide times; set a turn-back time |
| Coast | Falling rocks at cliff bases | Keep away from cliff foot; wear a helmet if required |
| Town | Traffic | Stay on pavements; use crossings; wear high-visibility clothing |
| Town | Approaching strangers for questionnaires | Work in pairs or groups; stay in public places; carry identification; be polite and leave if uncomfortable |
| Any | Bad weather, heat or cold | Check the forecast; carry water, sun protection or waterproofs; change the date if needed |

A precaution must match the risk. "Be careful" is not a precaution.

## Worked example: a plan in outline

**Title:** "Does environmental quality improve with distance from the centre of (named town)?"

- **Aim:** to investigate how environmental quality changes from the centre outwards.
- **Hypothesis:** environmental quality scores increase with distance from the town centre.
- **Theory:** urban land-use models suggest older, denser housing and more traffic near the centre and newer, lower-density housing with more open space further out.
- **Location:** one main road running 2 km from the centre to the edge of town, chosen because it crosses several types of housing and has pavements throughout.
- **Background research:** a town map to plan sites at regular intervals; census data on housing and open space for each area.
- **Risks:** traffic at junctions, reduced by working on pavements and using crossings; approaching residents for a short questionnaire, reduced by working in pairs during daylight.

The plan names the place, the variable to measure, the theory and the expected pattern, which is exactly what a later conclusion can test.

## Common mistakes

- **Questions that cannot be measured**, such as "Is the town nice?"
- **No theory**, so the hypothesis has no reason behind it.
- **No named location** in a Section C answer.
- **Risks without precautions**, or vague precautions.
- **Background research left until after data collection.**
- **A plan too large** to collect in the time available.
