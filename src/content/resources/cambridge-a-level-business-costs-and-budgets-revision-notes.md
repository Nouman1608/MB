## Definitions to know

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Fixed cost | Stays the same whatever the output, over the short run (rent, insurance) |
| Variable cost | Changes with output (ingredients, packaging) |
| Direct cost | Can be traced to a product or cost centre |
| Indirect cost | Cannot be traced to one product; shared (overheads) |
| Total cost | Fixed costs + variable costs |
| Average cost | Total cost ÷ output |
| Marginal cost | Extra cost of one more unit |
| Contribution | Selling price − variable cost (per unit), or total revenue − total variable costs |
| Margin of safety | How far actual (or forecast) output is above break-even output |
| Budget | Financial plan with targets for a future period |
| Variance | Actual figure compared with budgeted figure |

The syllabus gives no formulas for 5.4 and 5.5. The ones below are the standard forms.

## Formulas

| Item | Standard form |
|---|---|
| Total cost | Total fixed costs + (variable cost per unit × output) |
| Average cost | Total cost ÷ output |
| Contribution per unit | Selling price less variable cost for one unit |
| Total contribution | Units sold × contribution per unit |
| Profit | Total contribution − fixed costs (or revenue − total costs) |
| Break-even output | Fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit |
| Output for target profit | Fixed costs plus target profit, divided by contribution per unit |
| Margin of safety (units) | Actual output less break-even output |
| Margin of safety, per cent | Margin of safety as a share of actual output, × 100 |
| Cost-plus price | Unit cost × (1 + mark-up as a decimal) |
| Variance | Budgeted figure compared with actual figure, labelled adverse or favourable |

## Costing: full versus contribution

| | Full costing | Contribution costing |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed costs | Shared across products (apportioned) | Not shared; deducted in total |
| Key figure | Profit per unit | Contribution per unit |
| Best for | Cost-plus pricing, full picture of profitability | Short-term, one-off decisions such as special orders |
| Main limitation | The overhead basis is a judgement | Ignores fixed costs, so it can hide long-run losses |

Contribution is not profit. Contribution pays towards fixed costs; profit is what is left after them.

### Method: full costing in steps

1. Add up all overheads.
2. Choose a basis (for example, labour hours) and total it across products.
3. Overhead rate = overheads ÷ total basis.
4. Overhead per unit = rate × units of the basis used per unit.
5. Full cost per unit = variable cost + overhead per unit.

## Uses of cost information

- **Decisions:** compare average and marginal cost; if marginal cost is below price, an extra unit adds to profit.
- **Pricing:** cost-plus adds a mark-up. A floor price in the short run is marginal (variable) cost.
- **Monitoring:** compare actual unit cost with target to spot waste. Profit = revenue − total costs.
- **Special orders:** accept if the price is above variable cost, spare capacity exists and fixed costs do not rise, then check non-financial factors.

Worked reminder. Talgarth Tea Rooms gets a one-off order for 200 afternoon teas at £6.50. Variable cost is £4.00 a tea. Contribution = £2.50 × 200 = £500. Accept financially, but ask whether regular customers will expect the same price.

## Break-even in steps

Talgarth Tea Rooms sells afternoon teas at £9, with variable cost of £4 and fixed costs of £12,000 a month.

1. Contribution per tea = £9 − £4 = £5.
2. Break-even = £12,000 ÷ £5 = 2,400 teas (revenue £21,600).
3. Actual sales 2,900 teas, so margin of safety = 500 teas (17.2% of actual).
4. Profit = 2,900 × £5 − £12,000 = £2,500.
5. Target profit £3,000 needs (£12,000 + £3,000) ÷ £5 = 3,000 teas.

### The chart in brief

Horizontal axis: output. Vertical axis: money. Lines: fixed costs (flat), total costs (start at the fixed cost level, upward sloping), revenue (starts at origin, steeper). Break-even is where revenue and total costs cross. Margin of safety is the gap along the output axis between break-even and actual output. A higher price steepens the revenue line, so break-even moves left. Higher fixed costs raise the cost lines, so break-even moves right.

### Uses and limitations

| Uses | Limitations |
|---|---|
| Shows minimum sales needed | Assumes all output is sold |
| Easy to draw and explain | Assumes constant price and unit variable cost |
| Helps with loan applications and planning | Fixed costs may step up |
| Tests what-if changes | Based on forecasts; one product only |

## Budgets

- **Purposes:** measure performance, allocate resources, control and monitor.
- **Benefits:** clear targets, coordination, motivation, early warning.
- **Drawbacks:** time-consuming, forecast errors, short-term focus, can be rigid.

| Type | How it works | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Incremental | Last period plus or minus a percentage | Quick and simple | Repeats old inefficiency |
| Flexible | Variable items adjusted to actual output | Fairer comparison | More work; needs cost behaviour known |
| Zero | Start from £0 and justify every item | Challenges all spending | Slow and costly |

### Variances in steps

1. For revenue: actual above budget is favourable; below is adverse.
2. For costs: actual below budget is favourable; above is adverse.
3. State the amount and the label (£1,400 favourable).
4. If output changed, flex the variable costs first.
5. Suggest causes and what the manager should do.

Worked reminder: Talgarth budgets for 2,000 covers and £8,000 of food. Food is £4 a cover. Actual covers are 2,400, so the flexed food budget is £9,600. Actual food spend of £9,900 is a £300 adverse variance, not a favourable one against £8,000.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Fixed versus indirect:** fixed concerns behaviour with output; indirect concerns traceability.
- **Variable versus direct:** a cost can be direct and fixed, or indirect and variable.
- **Contribution versus profit:** fixed costs separate them.
- **Margin of safety versus break-even:** one is the buffer above the other.
- **Adverse versus favourable:** judged by effect on profit, not whether the number is bigger.

## Applying it in a case study

Numbers alone earn little in a case study. After a calculation, say what it means for this business: a thin margin of safety means a small fall in demand creates a loss. For an evaluate or justify question, weigh both sides, then reach a judgement that depends on the facts given, such as whether spare capacity exists or whether the firm's cost data are reliable.

## Quick self-test

1. Talgarth Tea Rooms has fixed costs of £4,000 a month and a variable cost of £2.50 a cover. Find total cost and average cost for 1,200 covers.
2. Define marginal cost.
3. Price £9, variable cost £4, fixed costs £12,000. Find break-even output.
4. Actual output is 2,900 and break-even is 2,400. Find the margin of safety as a share of actual output, in per cent to 1 d.p.
5. How many units give a profit of £3,000 under the figures in question 3?
6. Unit cost is £30 and the mark-up is 25%. Find the price.
7. Budgeted sales revenue is £15,000 and actual is £16,400. State the variance.
8. Budget: 2,000 covers and £8,000 of food. Flex the food budget for 2,400 covers.
9. Name one limitation of contribution costing.
10. Fixed costs rise to £13,500 (other figures as in question 3). Find new break-even output.

### Answers

1. Total cost = £4,000 + 1,200 × £2.50 = £7,000. Average cost = £7,000 ÷ 1,200 = £5.83.
2. The extra cost of producing one more unit.
3. £12,000 ÷ £5 = 2,400 units.
4. 500 ÷ 2,900 = 17.2%.
5. (£12,000 + £3,000) ÷ £5 = 3,000 units.
6. £30 × 1.25 = £37.50.
7. £1,400 favourable.
8. £8,000 ÷ 2,000 = £4 a cover; £4 × 2,400 = £9,600.
9. It ignores fixed costs, so prices set on contribution alone may not cover overheads over time (or it relies on a clear fixed/variable split).
10. £13,500 ÷ £5 = 2,700 units.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing break-even as fixed costs ÷ price instead of ÷ contribution.
- Not rounding break-even output sensibly when it is not a whole number.
- Calling contribution "profit".
- Giving a variance with no adverse or favourable label.
- Comparing actual costs with an unflexed budget after output has changed.
- Describing a break-even assumption with no link to the case (apply it to the business).
- Listing benefits of budgets without any drawbacks when asked to evaluate.
- Accepting a special order on the numbers alone with no mention of non-financial factors.
