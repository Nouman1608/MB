This guide goes deeper into section **7.1 Accounting concepts** of the Cambridge O Level Accounting 7707 syllabus for examination in 2027, 2028 and 2029. The syllabus lists ten concepts: "business entity, consistency, duality, going concern, historic cost, matching / accruals, materiality, money measurement, prudence, realisation". The topic introduction says the concepts are introduced "together with how they are applied in accounting records and statements", so knowing a definition is only the start.

The shared [accounting concepts, ethics, technology and sustainability guide](/resources/igcse-accounting-accounting-concepts-ethics-technology-and-sustainabilit/) defines each concept with examples. This page shows where each concept sits behind the procedures in Topics 2 to 6, then works through a draft profit that needs correcting concept by concept. Recall the points with the [accounting concepts revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-accounting-concepts-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [accounting concepts practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-accounting-concepts-practice/). All businesses and figures are invented, and amounts are in dollars.

## The ten concepts in one line each

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Business entity | The business is separate from its owner; only business transactions are recorded |
| Consistency | The same methods are used from one period to the next |
| Duality | Every transaction has two equal aspects, a debit and a credit |
| Going concern | The business is assumed to continue trading for the foreseeable future |
| Historic cost | Assets are recorded at their original cost |
| Matching / accruals | Revenue and the expenses of earning it are recorded in the period they belong to, not when cash moves |
| Materiality | Items too small to affect users' decisions may be treated in the simplest way |
| Money measurement | Only items that can be measured reliably in money are recorded |
| Prudence | Profits and assets are not overstated; losses are recognised as soon as they are expected |
| Realisation | Revenue is recognised when goods or services pass to the customer, not when an order is taken or cash is received |

## Where the concepts hide in other topics

Concepts often sit inside questions on another topic. The table links each procedure to the concept behind it.

| Procedure you already know | Concept behind it |
|---|---|
| Double entry; the accounting equation | Duality |
| Owner's private spending recorded as drawings | Business entity |
| Depreciating a non-current asset over its useful life | Matching / accruals, relying on going concern |
| Accrued and prepaid expenses and income | Matching / accruals |
| Allowance for irrecoverable debts; writing off a bad debt | Prudence |
| Inventory at the lower of cost and net realisable value | Prudence |
| Revenue recorded on delivery, deposits held as a liability | Realisation |
| Non-current assets at cost less depreciation, not market value | Historic cost |
| Not changing depreciation method each year | Consistency |
| Small tools charged as an expense | Materiality |
| Staff skill and reputation not in the statements | Money measurement |

## Worked example: Sana's Tailoring

Sana's draft profit for the year ended 31 December 2028 is $36,500. Her accountant finds six items.

1. Sana's personal phone bill of $480 was paid by the business and charged to telephone expenses.
2. A customer paid a $1,600 deposit in December for a suit to be made and delivered in February. It was recorded as revenue.
3. A sewing machine bought for $6,000 was charged to repairs. Sana's policy is to depreciate machinery at 20% of cost a year, with a full year's charge in the year of purchase.
4. Electricity of $340 for December is unpaid and unrecorded.
5. Fabric in closing inventory cost $2,200 but can now be sold for only $1,500.
6. A pair of scissors costing $25, expected to last several years, was charged as an expense.

| Item | Concept | Adjustment to profit |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Business entity: private spending is drawings | +480 |
| 2 | Realisation: no suit has been delivered, so the deposit is a liability | -1,600 |
| 3 | Matching: remove the $6,000 expense, charge depreciation of $1,200 | +4,800 |
| 4 | Matching / accruals: December's electricity belongs to 2028 | -340 |
| 5 | Prudence: inventory at net realisable value | -700 |
| 6 | Materiality: too small to matter; no adjustment | 0 |

Corrected profit = 36,500 + 480 - 1,600 + 4,800 - 340 - 700 = **$39,140**.

Each line names the concept, says what it requires in this case and shows the figure. That is the structure Paper 2 rewards. Item 3 also uses going concern: depreciating over several years only makes sense if the business will continue to use the machine.

## When concepts work together or pull apart

- **Prudence and realisation.** If Sana's fabric could be sold for more than cost, the gain is not recorded until a sale is made (realisation). If it can only be sold for less, the loss is recorded now (prudence). Expected gains wait; expected losses do not.
- **Going concern and historic cost.** Recording assets at cost less depreciation assumes the business will keep using them. If a business is about to close, its assets would be worth only what they can be sold for, and the going concern basis no longer fits.
- **Materiality and consistency.** A business sets a limit below which items are expensed. Applying the same limit each year keeps its results comparable.
- **Matching and prudence.** Matching spreads an asset's cost over its life; prudence chooses a realistic, not optimistic, useful life.

## Effect on the statement of financial position

Concepts affect assets and liabilities as well as profit. In Sana's case: the sewing machine appears as a non-current asset at $4,800; the deposit of $1,600 is a current liability; the electricity owing is an other payable of $340; inventory falls by $700; and drawings rise by $480, reducing capital.

## Model answer: an explain question

*Question: Sana's trade receivables are $8,000 and she expects about 5% not to be paid. Explain how the prudence concept applies.*

"Prudence means that profit and assets should not be overstated, and expected losses should be recognised as soon as they are foreseen. Sana expects 5% of her receivables, $400, not to be paid, so she should create an allowance for irrecoverable debts of $400. With no allowance in place before, this reduces profit for the year by $400 and shows trade receivables in the statement of financial position at $7,600, the amount she realistically expects to collect."

The answer defines the concept briefly, applies it to the business, calculates the figure and states the effect on both statements.

## Common mistakes

- Defining a concept when the question asks you to apply it.
- Confusing matching with realisation: matching is about the period of expenses and income; realisation is about when revenue is earned.
- Describing prudence as "understating profit" instead of "not overstating it".
- Naming "accruals" for a drawings error, which is business entity.
- Forgetting that a materiality decision depends on the size of the business.

## Exam technique

A Paper 1 question may give a treatment and ask which concept it follows; check each option against what the treatment actually does. In Paper 2, **identify** or **state** questions need only the name; **explain** questions need the name, what it requires and the effect on the figures in the case. For a question on correcting profit, set out a table like the one above with a column for the concept.
