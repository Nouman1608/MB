## Set 1

**1(a)** Explain **two** ways in which public health in 1848 was different from public health in 1875.
**[6]**

**1(b)** Explain **two** causes of the development of penicillin as a usable drug in the years 1928–44.
**[8]**

**1(c)** "The work of individuals was the main reason for improvements in surgery in the years
c1848–1918." How far do you agree? **[16]**

You may use the following in your answer:
- James Simpson and chloroform
- the First World War

You **must** also use information of your own.

## Set 2

**2(a)** Explain **two** ways in which the role of women in medicine in 1854 was different from their
role in 1918. **[6]**

**2(b)** Explain **two** causes of the Liberal government's health reforms of 1906–11. **[8]**

**2(c)** "Government action was more important than scientific discovery in improving the nation's health
in the years 1848–1948." How far do you agree? **[16]**

You may use the following in your answer:
- the Public Health Act (1875)
- the work of Koch

You **must** also use information of your own.

## Answers and mark guidance

### 1(a) [6]

- **Compulsion:** the 1848 Act set up a General Board of Health but local boards were voluntary, so many
  towns did nothing; the 1875 Act forced councils to provide clean water, sewers and drainage.
- **Understanding:** in 1848 action was based on miasma theory and Chadwick's link between dirt and
  disease; by 1875 germ theory (1861) and Snow's work gave a stronger case for clean water.

### 1(b) [8]

- **Scientific research:** Florey and Chain at Oxford purified penicillin and tested it on mice and then
  patients (1940–41), proving its value where Fleming had been unable to produce it.
- **The Second World War:** the need to treat infected wounds led the US government to fund mass production
  by drug companies, so enough was available by D-Day (1944).

### 1(c) [16]

**Individuals:** Simpson (chloroform, 1847) solved pain; Lister applied Pasteur's germ theory with
carbolic acid (1865); Landsteiner's blood groups (1901); Gillies's plastic surgery in the First World War.
**Other factors:** science and technology (microscopes, steam sterilisation, X-rays); the First World War,
which created urgent need and huge numbers of cases (mobile X-ray units, stored blood); communication of
ideas; government and hospital investment; opposition and attitudes slowing change (resistance to
chloroform and to Lister's spray).
**Judgement:** individuals made breakthroughs, but science, technology and war determined how quickly they
spread. Answers should cover the whole period, including aseptic surgery after 1875.

### 2(a) [6]

- **Status and training:** in 1854 nursing was a low-status job with little training until Nightingale;
  by 1918 nursing was a trained profession and women could qualify as doctors (since 1876).
- **Range of roles:** in 1854 women served only as nurses; by 1918 they worked as doctors, surgeons and
  ambulance drivers in the war, as in the Scottish Women's Hospitals.

### 2(b) [8]

- **Evidence of poor health:** many volunteers for the Boer War (1899–1902) were unfit, and studies by
  Booth and Rowntree showed widespread poverty and illness.
- **Politics:** the Liberals' landslide in 1906, the rise of the Labour Party and the example of German
  social insurance encouraged action to win working-class support.

### 2(c) [16]

**Government action:** 1848 and 1875 Public Health Acts; Bazalgette's London sewers; Liberal reforms
(1906–11), including National Insurance; wartime funding of penicillin; the NHS (1948).
**Scientific discovery:** germ theory (Pasteur), Koch's identification of specific germs, vaccines,
Ehrlich's Salvarsan, X-rays, blood groups, penicillin.
**Links:** government action often followed scientific understanding (1875 after germ theory) and
science often needed government funding to have impact (penicillin).
**Judgement:** science explained disease, but government action delivered health improvements to the
whole population, especially through public health and the NHS. Reward sustained comparison across
at least two key topics.

## Set 3 — extra practice

**3(a)** Explain **two** ways in which hospital treatment of wounded soldiers in the Crimean War
(1854–56) was different from treatment in the First World War (1914–18). **[6]**

**3(b)** Explain **two** causes of the founding of the NHS in 1948. **[8]**

### 3(a) [6]

- **Infection:** at Scutari, before germ theory, hygiene relied on Nightingale's cleanliness; by
  1914–18 antiseptic and aseptic methods, and the cutting away of infected tissue, were standard.
- **Technology and techniques:** Crimean surgeons had chloroform but no X-rays or transfusion; First
  World War doctors used mobile X-ray units to find shrapnel and stored blood for transfusions.

### 3(b) [8]

- **The Second World War:** the Emergency Medical Service showed that a national hospital system could
  work, and the shared sacrifice of war created support for fairer services.
- **The Beveridge Report and Labour's victory:** Beveridge (1942) proposed a free national health service
  to defeat disease, one of the "five giants"; Labour won the 1945 election and Bevan carried the NHS
  Act (1946), overcoming opposition from many doctors.

## Where marks are usually lost

- In (a), describing only one of the two dates.
- In (b), giving one cause twice in different words.
- In (c), writing a list of discoveries without comparing factors.
