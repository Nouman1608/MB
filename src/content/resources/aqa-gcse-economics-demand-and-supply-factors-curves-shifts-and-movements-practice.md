## Questions

**1.** (a) State what economists mean by demand. **[2]**
**(b)** Distinguish between a change in quantity demanded and a change in demand. **[2]**

**2.** Dev buys plant pots each month. His demand is shown below (invented data).

| Price per pot (£) | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 | 10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pots demanded | 14 | 11 | 8 | 5 | 2 |

**(a)** State the quantity demanded at £6. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in quantity demanded when the price rises from £6 to £8. **[2]**
**(c)** Describe how you would draw Dev's demand curve from the table. **[3]**
**(d)** Say whether the change in (b) is a move along the same curve or a move of the curve. **[1]**

**3.** Decide whether each event below moves the market along its demand curve or shifts the curve, and give the direction. Assume the good is normal. **[4]**
- (i) The price of the good falls.
- (ii) Incomes in the town fall.
- (iii) A rival good becomes cheaper.
- (iv) A successful advertising campaign for the good.

**4.** Pasta and pasta sauce are used together. Explain how a rise in the price of pasta sauce affects the demand for pasta. **[3]**

**5.** A bakery's cost of making each extra batch of 10 loaves is shown (invented data).

| Batch | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cost (£) | 12 | 14 | 17 | 21 | 26 | 33 |

**(a)** Calculate the number of batches the bakery will supply at a price of £21 per batch. **[2]**
**(b)** Complete the supply schedule for prices of £12, £17, £26 and £33. **[2]**
**(c)** Describe the shape of the bakery's supply curve and explain it. **[2]**

**6.** Energy prices rise and add £3 to the cost of every batch in question 5. **(a)** Calculate the number of batches supplied at £21 after the rise. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in the quantity supplied at £21. **[2]**
**(c)** State the direction of the shift of the supply curve. **[1]**

**7.** Decide whether each event below moves the market along its supply curve or shifts the curve, and give the direction. **[4]**
- (i) A cheaper machine is introduced.
- (ii) The government imposes a tax on producing the good.
- (iii) The price of the good rises.
- (iv) Frost destroys much of a farm crop.

**8.** Explain why a supply curve slopes upwards. **[4]**

**9.** Three households buy jars of honey (invented data).

| Price (£) | Household A | Household B | Household C |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | 9 | 7 | 4 |
| 5 | 6 | 5 | 0 |

**(a)** Calculate market demand at £3 and at £5. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in market demand when the price rises from £3 to £5. **[2]**
**(c)** A fourth household moves in and buys 5 at £3 and 3 at £5. Calculate the new market demand at each price and explain whether this is a movement or a shift. **[3]**

**10.** A firm makes garden gates. The cost of each extra gate is £40, £48, £58, £70 and £85. The government pays the firm a grant of £8 on each gate (invented data). **(a)** Calculate the number of gates supplied at £62 before and after the grant. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in quantity supplied at £62. **[2]**
**(c)** State the direction of the shift of the supply curve. **[1]**

**11.** A café chain says, "Cutting our price is the best way to increase the demand for our coffee." Evaluate this view. **[9]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** How much of a good or service consumers are both willing and able to purchase [1] at a particular price over a stated period [1].
**(b)** A change in quantity demanded happens when the good's own price changes and nothing else does [1]; a change in demand is caused by any other factor and shifts the whole curve [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Include both "willing" and "able"; a definition that says only "wants to buy" is incomplete.

**2. (a)** **8 pots** [1].
**(b)** Change = 5 − 8 = −3 [1]. Percentage = −3 / 8 × 100 = **−37.5%** [1].
**(c)** Put price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis, using even scales [1]. Plot each price and quantity pair as a point, for example (14, £2) and (2, £10) [1]. Join the points and label the axes with units and the curve D [1].
**(d)** A **movement along** the curve (a contraction) [1].
*Examiner insight:* Work the percentage change from the starting figure, here 8; dividing by 5 gives a different answer and is the usual slip.

**3.** (i) Movement, an extension down the curve [1]. (ii) Shift left [1]. (iii) Shift left [1]. (iv) Shift right [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Both the type (movement or shift) and the direction are needed in each case; naming only one does not complete the statement.

**4.** Pasta sauce is a complement to pasta [1]. A higher sauce price makes the pair costlier to use together, so buyers buy less pasta [1]. Demand for pasta falls, so its curve shifts left [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Take the chain one link at a time: say what buyers do, then which curve moves and in which direction.

**5. (a)** Batches with a cost of £21 or less are batches 1, 2, 3 and 4 [1], so the bakery supplies **4 batches** [1].
**(b)** At £12: 1 batch [1]. At £17: 3. At £26: 5. At £33: 6 (all three correct) [1].
**(c)** The curve slopes upward from left to right [1]. A higher price covers the greater cost of the extra batches and gives more profit, so the bakery supplies more [1].
*Examiner insight:* Counting every batch whose cost is at or below the price is the method; stopping one batch early is the usual slip.

**6. (a)** New costs are £15, £17, £20, £24, £29 and £36 [1]. At £21, batches 1, 2 and 3 qualify, so **3 batches** [1].
**(b)** Change = 3 − 4 = −1 [1]. −1 / 4 × 100 = **−25%** [1].
**(c)** The curve shifts **left** [1].
*Examiner insight:* A rise in costs lowers supply at each price. Show the new costs first, so a slip in counting does not hide your method.

**7.** (i) Shift right [1]. (ii) Shift left [1]. (iii) Movement, an extension up the curve [1]. (iv) Shift left [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A tax and a frost both reduce supply; check that you have not treated the good's own price rise as a shift.

**8.** A higher price brings more profit on each unit [1], so firms have a reason to produce more [1]. Extra units usually cost more per unit to make as resources are stretched [1], so a higher price is needed before firms will supply them [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Give two linked reasons, each developed, not a list of single words such as "profit" and "costs".

**9. (a)** At £3: 9 + 7 + 4 = **20** [1]. At £5: 6 + 5 + 0 = **11** [1].
**(b)** Change = 11 − 20 = −9 [1]. −9 / 20 × 100 = **−45%** [1].
**(c)** At £3: 20 + 5 = **25**. At £5: 11 + 3 = **14** [1]. Demand has risen at every price [1], and the cause is a new buyer, not the price, so the curve **shifts right** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Add the quantities at each price; adding the prices is wrong. Say why the cause is not the price.

**10. (a)** Before the grant, gates costing £62 or less are the first three, so **3** [1]. After the grant the effective costs are £32, £40, £50, £62 and £77, so **4** gates [1].
**(b)** Change = 4 − 3 = 1 [1]. 1 / 3 × 100 = **33.3%** [1].
**(c)** The supply curve shifts **right** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Subtract the grant from each cost before counting; a final answer without the new costs shown is harder to credit if it is wrong.

**11.** Cutting the price raises the quantity demanded: an extension along the curve, not an increase in demand [1]. More people buy because the coffee is cheaper than substitutes such as tea or rival cafés [1]. This works only if enough buyers respond to the lower price [1]. A shift of the curve comes from other factors: an advertising campaign makes the coffee fashionable, so demand shifts right at every price [1]. Rising incomes mean customers can afford more coffee [1]. A rise in the price of a rival's coffee moves buyers to the chain [1]. A price cut lowers the profit on each cup sold, and rivals may match it [1]. Which is best depends on the café's costs, how strong its rivals are and its aims [1]. Judgement: a price cut is a quick way to raise sales, but it is a movement and not a rise in demand, so lasting growth in demand is more likely to come from other factors such as advertising [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* An evaluation gains credit by weighing points against each other and reaching a reasoned conclusion; a list of factors with no judgement stays descriptive.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying "demand falls" after a price rise, where "quantity demanded falls" is correct.
- Shifting a curve for a change in the good's own price.
- Giving the type of change (movement or shift) but no direction.
- Calculating a percentage change from the new figure rather than the starting figure.
- Adding prices instead of quantities when building a market demand curve.
- Counting units whose cost is above the price when building a supply schedule.
- Forgetting to subtract a subsidy (or add a cost rise) before counting units.
- Drawing an axis with no label, units or curve label.
- Confusing substitutes and complements and so reversing the direction of the shift.
