These notes condense subtopic 3.2, **Factors that affect enzyme action**, from the Cambridge International AS & A Level Biology 9700 syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027. It is AS Level content (Topic 3, Enzymes), set on Papers 1 and 2 and assumed knowledge for Paper 4. For full explanations and worked examples, use the [Factors that affect enzyme action study guide](/resources/a-level-biology-9700-factors-that-affect-enzyme-action/). For the mechanism of catalysis (subtopic 3.1), see the [Enzymes revision notes](/resources/a-level-biology-enzymes-revision-notes/).

## The four learning outcomes

1. Investigate and explain the effects of **temperature, pH (using buffers), enzyme concentration, substrate concentration and inhibitor concentration** on rate.
2. Explain that **Vmax** is used to derive **Km**, and that Km compares enzymes' **affinity** for their substrates.
3. Explain the effects of **reversible** inhibitors: **competitive** and **non-competitive**.
4. Investigate **immobilised (alginate) vs free** enzyme activity; state the **advantages** of immobilisation.

## Measuring rate

| Method | When to use it | How |
|---|---|---|
| Initial rate | You have a progress curve (product or substrate against time) | Gradient of the tangent at t = 0 |
| 1/t | You timed the reaction to a fixed end point | Rate ∝ 1/t (often written 1000/t) |
| Colorimeter | The reaction involves a colour change | Change in absorbance or transmission per unit time |

Use **initial** rate because substrate concentration is the same in every tube at t = 0. Later, the rate falls as substrate is used up, not because the enzyme is used up.

## The five factors at a glance

| Factor | Graph of rate against the factor | Key explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | Rises to an optimum, then falls steeply | Below optimum: more kinetic energy, more collisions, more E–S complexes. Above: hydrogen and ionic bonds break, active site changes shape, enzyme denatured |
| pH | Peak at the optimum, falling either side | H⁺ concentration changes the charge on R groups; ionic and hydrogen bonds disrupted; active site shape and charges change |
| Enzyme concentration | Straight line through origin (substrate in excess); levels off if substrate is limited | More active sites, more E–S complexes per second |
| Substrate concentration | Rises steeply, then levels off at Vmax | Low: substrate is limiting. High: all active sites occupied (saturated); enzyme concentration is limiting |
| Inhibitor concentration | Rate falls as inhibitor concentration rises | A greater proportion of enzyme molecules is inhibited at any moment |

## Investigation checklist

- **Independent variable:** the one factor you change, with at least five values.
- **Dependent variable:** what you measure (volume of oxygen, time for iodine colour to go, absorbance).
- **Standardised variables:** everything else. For enzyme work that means temperature (water bath), pH (buffer), enzyme concentration, substrate concentration and volumes.
- **Equilibrate** enzyme and substrate separately at the test temperature before mixing.
- **Replicates:** at least three at each value so you can calculate a mean and spot anomalies.
- **Control:** boiled (denatured) enzyme in place of active enzyme shows the change is caused by enzyme activity.

**Buffers.** In a pH investigation, each tube gets a different buffer. In every other investigation, every tube gets the same buffer.

## Dilutions

| Type | Formula or pattern | Gives |
|---|---|---|
| Proportional | volume of stock = (wanted ÷ stock) × total volume; top up with distilled water | Evenly spaced concentrations |
| Serial | Each solution is diluted by the same factor (often ×½ or ×⅒) from the previous one | A wide range of concentrations |

## Method in steps: Km from Vmax

1. Plot initial rate (y) against substrate concentration (x); draw a smooth curve.
2. Estimate **Vmax** from where the curve levels off (not just the highest point plotted).
3. Calculate **½Vmax**.
4. Read across from ½Vmax to the curve, then down: that x-value is **Km**.

**Km = substrate concentration at which rate = ½Vmax.**
**Low Km → high affinity** (E–S complexes form readily, so half the maximum rate is reached at low substrate concentration).
**High Km → low affinity.**

Small reminder: Vmax = 64 units, so ½Vmax = 32 units. If the curve passes 32 units at 1.8 mmol dm⁻³, then Km = 1.8 mmol dm⁻³.

## Reversible inhibitors

| | Competitive | Non-competitive |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | Similar to substrate | Not similar to substrate |
| Binds to | Active site | A site other than the active site |
| Effect on active site | Occupies it; substrate cannot enter | Changes its shape |
| More substrate? | Reduces the inhibition | No effect on the inhibition |
| Vmax | Unchanged | Lowered |
| Km | Increased (lower apparent affinity) | Unchanged |
| Inhibition at high vs low substrate | Much smaller at high substrate | Same proportion at both |

**Reversible** means the inhibitor binds by weak interactions and can leave, so activity returns when it is removed.

**Data test.** Calculate the inhibited rate as a percentage of the uninhibited rate at a low and a high substrate concentration. Percentage much higher at high substrate → competitive. About the same → non-competitive.

## Immobilised enzymes

**Making beads:** enzyme + **sodium alginate** solution, dripped from a syringe into **calcium chloride** solution → calcium alginate beads with enzyme trapped inside; rinse with distilled water.

**Fair comparison with free enzyme:** same quantity of enzyme, same substrate volume and concentration, same temperature and pH; measure rate the same way (for example, time for starch to stop giving a blue-black colour with iodine).

**Typical findings and reasons**

- Initial rate is often **lower** for beads: substrate must diffuse into the gel and product out; some active sites are less accessible.
- Beads **keep more activity** after exposure to high temperature or extreme pH: the gel holds each molecule and limits unfolding.
- Beads can be **reused** many times with little loss of activity.

**Advantages to state:** product not contaminated with enzyme; enzyme recovered and reused (cheaper); greater stability over a wider range of temperature and pH; allows a continuous process in a column.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Denatured vs inhibited:** denaturation is a permanent change in tertiary structure; reversible inhibition ends when the inhibitor leaves.
- **Vmax vs Km:** Vmax is a rate; Km is a substrate concentration.
- **Substrate-limited vs enzyme-limited:** the rising part of a substrate curve is substrate-limited; the plateau is enzyme-limited.
- **Initial rate vs mean rate:** the tangent at t = 0 is not the same as total product ÷ total time.
- **Serial vs proportional dilution:** serial multiplies by a fixed factor each step; proportional mixes set volumes of stock and water.

## Quick self-test

1. A reaction takes 80 s to reach its end point. Calculate the rate as 1/t, with units.
2. An enzyme has Vmax = 64 arbitrary units. What rate do you read across from to find Km?
3. Adding an inhibitor reduces a rate from 18 to 12 units. Calculate the percentage decrease.
4. How would you make 5.0 cm³ of 0.6% enzyme solution from a 1.5% stock?
5. List the concentrations from a serial dilution of 10% substrate, halving four times.
6. Enzyme X has Km 0.8 mmol dm⁻³; enzyme Y has Km 3.0 mmol dm⁻³ for the same substrate. Which has the higher affinity?
7. A graph of rate against enzyme concentration levels off. Suggest why.
8. State the effect of a competitive inhibitor on Vmax and on Km.
9. Explain why the rate falls above the optimum temperature even though collisions are more frequent.
10. Why is a buffer used when temperature is the independent variable?
11. Give one reason why alginate beads may give a lower initial rate than the free enzyme.
12. Name the two solutions used to make alginate beads.

### Answers

1. 1/80 = **0.0125 s⁻¹** (or 1000/t = 12.5).
2. **32 units** (½Vmax).
3. (18 − 12) ÷ 18 × 100 = **33%** decrease.
4. Volume of stock = (0.6 ÷ 1.5) × 5.0 = **2.0 cm³ stock + 3.0 cm³ distilled water**.
5. **10, 5, 2.5, 1.25, 0.625%**.
6. **Enzyme X** (lower Km).
7. Substrate has become the **limiting factor**: there are free active sites but not enough substrate.
8. **Vmax unchanged; Km increased.**
9. Hydrogen and ionic bonds break, the **active site changes shape** (denaturation), so fewer E–S complexes form.
10. To keep **pH constant**, so any change in rate is caused only by temperature.
11. Substrate must **diffuse** into the bead (or some active sites are less accessible).
12. **Sodium alginate** and **calcium chloride**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "the enzyme is killed" instead of "the active site changes shape so it is no longer complementary to the substrate".
- Stating "rate increases with temperature" without the mechanism: kinetic energy, collision frequency, more E–S complexes.
- Explaining pH effects without mentioning the charges on R groups and the bonds holding tertiary structure.
- Saying the substrate curve levels off "because the enzyme is used up". Active sites are saturated; enzymes are not used up.
- Reading Km at half the highest plotted rate when the curve has not levelled off.
- Defining Km as "half the maximum rate". It is a **substrate concentration**, not a rate.
- Saying competitive inhibitors lower Vmax, or that non-competitive inhibitors change Km.
- Describing non-competitive inhibitors as blocking the active site.
- Comparing free and immobilised enzyme using different quantities of enzyme.
- Listing "cheaper" as an advantage of immobilisation without explaining that the enzyme can be reused.
