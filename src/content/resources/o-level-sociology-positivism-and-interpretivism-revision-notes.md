Key question 1.1 of Cambridge O Level Sociology (2251) asks *How do sociologists approach the study
of society?* There are two main approaches: positivism, which treats sociology as a science and
measures society with numbers, and interpretivism, which tries to understand people's meanings
using in-depth qualitative evidence. Researchers can also combine methods, and every research choice
can be judged using concepts such as validity, reliability and representativeness.

Fuller explanations: [O Level research approaches and types of data guide](/resources/o-level-sociology-research-approaches-and-data/).

## Syllabus coverage

2251 syllabus 2025–2027, **1.1**: 1.1.1 the positivist approach; 1.1.2 the interpretivist approach;
1.1.3 approaches that combine different research methods and evidence (triangulation, longitudinal
studies); 1.1.4 the analysis and evaluation of research choices (bias, interviewer effect,
Hawthorne/observer effect, validity, reliability, representativeness, generalisability).

## Definitions to learn

| Term | Meaning in one line |
|---|---|
| **Positivism** | Studying society scientifically to discover causes |
| **Interpretivism** | Studying the meanings people give to their actions |
| **Social facts** | Social structures outside the individual that shape behaviour |
| **Macro / micro** | Whole society / small-scale interaction |
| **Quantitative / qualitative** | Numerical / descriptive data |
| **Patterns and trends** | Regularities and changes over time in data |
| **Verstehen** | Seeing the world through participants' eyes |
| **Objectivity / subjectivity** | Free from / shaped by personal values |
| **Triangulation** | Checking findings with more than one method |
| **Longitudinal study** | Following the same group over time |
| **Bias** | Distortion of research by values or design |
| **Interviewer effect** | Answers shaped by the interviewer's characteristics |
| **Hawthorne effect** | Behaviour changes because people know they are studied |
| **Generalisability** | Applying findings to the wider population |

## The two approaches compared

| Question | Positivist answer | Interpretivist answer |
|---|---|---|
| What is society? | A structure of social facts | A product of interaction and meanings |
| What is the aim? | Discover patterns, trends and causes | Understand motivations and experiences |
| What data? | Quantitative | Qualitative |
| What matters most? | Reliability and objectivity | Validity and depth |
| Which methods? | Questionnaires, structured interviews, official statistics | Unstructured interviews, participant observation, diaries |
| Example | Townsend's household survey of poverty (1979) | Goffman's fieldwork in a psychiatric hospital (*Asylums*, 1961) |

**Criticisms in brief:** positivism can impose the researcher's categories and miss meaning;
interpretivism uses small samples that are hard to repeat or generalise.

## Combining approaches

- **Triangulation:** Barker (1984) used observation, interviews and questionnaires to study members
  of a new religious movement — each method checked the others.
- **Longitudinal:** the *Young Lives* study (from 2002) follows children in four countries to see how
  poverty shapes their lives. Problem: attrition (people dropping out) and high cost.

## Evaluating research — exam checklist

When a question asks you to explain problems with research, check:

1. **Sample** — how big, how chosen, who is missing? (representativeness, generalisability)
2. **Method** — would it be repeated the same way? (reliability)
3. **Truthfulness** — could people lie, forget or exaggerate? (validity)
4. **Researcher** — could their presence or values affect results? (bias, interviewer effect,
   Hawthorne effect)

## Worked example: a 4-mark "explain" answer

*Explain why the interviewer effect may reduce the validity of research.* (practice)

- Respondents may change their answers because of the interviewer's age, gender, ethnicity or
  status — for example students giving "acceptable" answers about cheating to a teacher (2).
- This means the data does not reflect what respondents really think or do, so it is less valid —
  a true picture has not been obtained (2).

## Worked example: a 14-mark evaluation plan

*Evaluate the view that quantitative data is more useful than qualitative data.* (practice)

1. **For (positivist):** numbers allow patterns, trends and comparisons; standardised methods are
   reliable and can be repeated; large samples make findings representative and generalisable;
   useful for government policy, for example national surveys of household poverty (Townsend, 1979).
2. **Against (interpretivist):** numbers cannot capture meanings and motivations; closed questions
   impose the researcher's categories, lowering validity; qualitative work such as Goffman's
   *Asylums* (1961) reveals experiences a survey would miss.
3. **Middle ground:** triangulation combines both — Barker (1984) used questionnaires alongside
   observation and interviews.
4. **Conclusion:** usefulness depends on the research aim; the strongest research often combines
   both types.

## Common mistakes and exam traps

- Writing "positivists use numbers" without explaining *why* (to be scientific, objective, find
  causes).
- Thinking interpretivists ignore reliability completely — they simply value validity more.
- Confusing **representativeness** (is the sample typical?) with **reliability** (is the method
  repeatable?).
- Assuming the Hawthorne effect only happens in observations; it can affect any research where
  people know they are being studied.
- Forgetting the syllabus asks about **causation and correlation** as part of positivism.

## Quick self-test

1. Give three features of positivism.
2. Define verstehen.
3. Give one strength of triangulation.
4. What is attrition in a longitudinal study?
5. Explain one reason why a study of one village may not be generalisable.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is one approach correct?** No. The approach should fit the research aim; many sociologists combine
both.

**What does "macro" mean in an exam answer?** Looking at society as a whole — structures such as the
economy, education system or religion — rather than individual interactions.

**How do I show evaluation in research questions?** Use the 1.1.4 concepts by name and apply them to
the research in the question or source.
