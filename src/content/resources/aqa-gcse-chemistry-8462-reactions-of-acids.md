This guide covers **4.4.2 Reactions of acids** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) specification, for teaching
from September 2016. It sits in topic 4.4, Chemical changes, and is assessed on Paper 1. Most of it is for
both tiers. Three parts are Higher tier only: the redox view of acids with metals (4.4.2.1), titration
calculations (4.4.2.5) and strong and weak acids (4.4.2.6). Titrations are also marked chemistry only, so
they are not assessed in the combined science course. Required practical 1 (making a soluble salt) and
required practical 2 (titration) belong here.

Use the [reactions of acids revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-reactions-of-acids-revision-notes/)
for quick recall and the [reactions of acids practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-reactions-of-acids-practice/)
to test yourself. The wider [chemical changes study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-chemical-changes/)
covers the reactivity series and electrolysis as well; this page goes deeper on acids only.

## 4.4.2.1 Acids with metals

**Acids react with some metals to produce a salt and hydrogen.**

metal + acid → salt + hydrogen

The specification limits the reactions you must know to **magnesium, zinc and iron** with **hydrochloric
acid and sulfuric acid**. The acid decides the family of salt:

| Acid | Salt family |
|---|---|
| Hydrochloric acid, HCl | Chlorides |
| Sulfuric acid, H₂SO₄ | Sulfates |
| Nitric acid, HNO₃ | Nitrates |

So magnesium with hydrochloric acid gives magnesium chloride, and zinc with sulfuric acid gives zinc sulfate.

- Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂
- Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂
- Fe + 2HCl → FeCl₂ + H₂ (iron forms iron(II) chloride here)

Observations to quote: bubbles of gas, the metal getting smaller and disappearing, and the test tube warming.
Magnesium fizzes quickly, zinc more slowly and iron slowest, which matches their order in the reactivity series.

### Higher tier only: why it is a redox reaction

Take magnesium with acid in ionic form: Mg + 2H⁺ → Mg²⁺ + H₂. Magnesium atoms **lose** two electrons each, so
magnesium is **oxidised**. Hydrogen ions **gain** one electron each, so hydrogen ions are **reduced**.
Oxidation and reduction happen together, so the reaction is **redox**. The chloride or sulfate ions do not
change, so they are **spectator ions** and are left out of the ionic equation.

## 4.4.2.2 Neutralisation and salt production

An acid is **neutralised** by three kinds of substance:

- an **alkali** (a soluble metal hydroxide): acid + alkali → salt + water
- a **base** (an insoluble metal hydroxide or a metal oxide): acid + base → salt + water
- a **metal carbonate**: acid + carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide

The salt has two parts. The **first name** comes from the **positive ion** in the alkali, base or carbonate.
The **second name** comes from the **acid used**. Work through this order and you can predict any product.

### Worked example 1: predicting products

*Name the salt and write the word equation for copper(II) oxide with sulfuric acid.*

1. Positive ion comes from copper(II) oxide: copper(II).
2. Acid is sulfuric: sulfate.
3. Copper oxide is a base, so the other product is water.

copper(II) oxide + sulfuric acid → copper(II) sulfate + water
CuO + H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + H₂O

### Worked example 2: deducing a formula from ions

*Write the balanced equation for sodium carbonate with hydrochloric acid.*

1. Sodium ion is Na⁺, chloride is Cl⁻, so the salt is NaCl.
2. Carbonate gives water and carbon dioxide.
3. Unbalanced: Na₂CO₃ + HCl → NaCl + H₂O + CO₂.
4. Two sodium atoms on the left need 2NaCl, which needs 2HCl.

Na₂CO₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H₂O + CO₂

To get formulae of salts, use the charges of common ions: Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺, Zn²⁺ and Al³⁺ with Cl⁻, NO₃⁻ and
SO₄²⁻. Magnesium nitrate is Mg(NO₃)₂ because one Mg²⁺ needs two NO₃⁻.

## 4.4.2.3 Making soluble salts (required practical 1)

You must be able to describe how to make a **pure, dry sample** of a named soluble salt. The specification's
practical uses an insoluble oxide or carbonate. The solid is added to **warm dilute acid** until no more
reacts. Then:

1. **Warm** the dilute acid in a beaker, using a water bath or a Bunsen burner.
2. Add the **insoluble solid in small portions**, stirring, until it stops reacting and some solid is left.
   Excess solid shows that **all the acid has been used up**.
3. **Filter** to remove the excess solid. The filtrate is the salt solution.
4. **Evaporate** some of the water (a water bath or electric heater) until crystals start to form at the edge
   or when a drop on a glass rod crystallises.
5. Leave to cool so crystals grow, **filter** them, then **dry** them between filter papers or in a warm
   oven.

Reasons worth knowing: the solid is added in **excess** so that no acid is left in the product; the leftover
solid is insoluble, so **filtering** separates it; slow crystallisation gives larger crystals; and you do not
boil to dryness because that can spoil the crystals.

This method works with metals, oxides, hydroxides and carbonates. For metals that react violently (such as
sodium) or very slowly, it is not used. For a soluble alkali, no solid is left to filter, so titration is used
instead.

## 4.4.2.4 pH and neutralisation

Acids produce **hydrogen ions, H⁺**, in aqueous solution. Aqueous solutions of alkalis contain **hydroxide
ions, OH⁻**. The **pH scale** runs from 0 to 14 and measures acidity or alkalinity:

- pH below 7: acidic
- pH 7: neutral
- pH above 7: alkaline

pH can be measured with **universal indicator** (or a wide range indicator) for an approximate value, or a
**pH probe** for a more accurate one. In neutralisation, hydrogen ions react with hydroxide ions to make
water:

**H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)**

When a strong acid is added to a strong alkali, the pH rises from a low value, rises steeply near the point
of neutralisation and ends at a high value. That rapid jump is why an indicator changes colour sharply at the
end point of a titration.

## 4.4.2.5 Titrations (chemistry only)

A titration measures the **volumes of acid and alkali that react exactly**. The specification restricts you to
strong acids and strong alkalis: sulfuric, hydrochloric and nitric acids only.

### Method (required practical 2)

1. Use a **pipette** and filler to put a fixed volume (usually 25.0 cm³) of alkali into a conical flask.
2. Add a few drops of a suitable **indicator** (for example phenolphthalein, which is pink in alkali and
   colourless in acid, or methyl orange).
3. Fill a **burette** with the acid and record the starting reading.
4. Add acid slowly, **swirling** the flask, until the indicator just changes colour. This is the **end
   point**. Add drop by drop near the end.
5. Record the final reading. The **titre** is final minus start.
6. Repeat until you have **concordant** results (within 0.10 cm³ of each other), and use their mean. Ignore a
   rough first run.

### Higher tier only: titration calculations

Steps: moles of the known solution = volume (dm³) × concentration (mol/dm³); use the **balanced equation
ratio**; then concentration = moles ÷ volume. Convert cm³ to dm³ by dividing by 1000.

**Worked example 3 (1 : 1 ratio).** 25.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol/dm³ sodium hydroxide neutralises a mean of 22.5 cm³
of hydrochloric acid. Find the acid concentration in mol/dm³ and g/dm³ (Mr of HCl = 36.5).

```
NaOH + HCl -> NaCl + H2O        (1 : 1)
moles NaOH = 0.0250 dm3 x 0.100 = 0.00250 mol
moles HCl  = 0.00250 mol
concentration = 0.00250 / 0.0225 = 0.111 mol/dm3
g/dm3 = 0.1111 x 36.5 = 4.06 g/dm3
```

**Worked example 4 (1 : 2 ratio).** 20.0 cm³ of 0.150 mol/dm³ sodium hydroxide needs 15.0 cm³ of sulfuric acid.
Find the acid concentration in mol/dm³ and g/dm³ (Mr of H₂SO₄ = 98).

```
2NaOH + H2SO4 -> Na2SO4 + 2H2O   (2 : 1)
moles NaOH   = 0.0200 x 0.150 = 0.00300 mol
moles H2SO4  = 0.00300 / 2    = 0.00150 mol
concentration = 0.00150 / 0.0150 = 0.100 mol/dm3
g/dm3 = 0.100 x 98 = 9.8 g/dm3
```

Skipping the "divide by 2" step is the most common error.

## 4.4.2.6 Strong and weak acids (Higher tier only)

- A **strong acid** is **completely ionised** in aqueous solution. Examples: hydrochloric, nitric and
  sulfuric acids.
- A **weak acid** is **only partially ionised**. Examples: ethanoic, citric and carbonic acids.
- **Concentrated** and **dilute** describe the amount of acid in a given volume of solution (amount of
  substance). **Strong** and **weak** describe the degree of ionisation. The two pairs are independent: you
  can have a dilute strong acid or a concentrated weak acid.

At the same concentration, the stronger acid has the **lower pH**, because more of it ionises to make H⁺.
As pH **falls by one unit**, the hydrogen ion concentration **rises by a factor of ten**. A solution of pH 2 has
ten times the H⁺ concentration of pH 3, and a hundred times that of pH 4. The specification asks for order of
magnitude calculations with whole numbers only.

**Worked example 5.** Solution X has pH 5 and solution Y has pH 2. How many times more concentrated is the
hydrogen ion concentration in Y? The pH difference is 3, so the factor is 10 × 10 × 10 = **1000**.

## Exam technique

- For "predict the products", write the two products by the pattern above, then name the salt from the
  positive ion plus the acid.
- For a salt-making method, give the **order** (warm acid, add excess, filter, evaporate, crystallise, dry)
  and the reason for the excess solid.
- In titration calculations, show the moles of the known solution first, then the ratio, then the final
  division. Marks are given for each step.
- Never say a weak acid is "dilute" or a strong acid is "concentrated" as if they meant the same thing.

## Common mistakes

- Writing "hydrogen" as a product of acid + carbonate or acid + oxide. Those give water (and carbon dioxide
  for carbonates).
- Naming the salt from the wrong acid, for example a sulfate from hydrochloric acid.
- Using cm³ directly in the concentration formula instead of converting to dm³.
- Forgetting that excess solid, not excess acid, is used in the soluble salt method.
- Saying that pH 1 has twice the H⁺ of pH 2. It has ten times.
