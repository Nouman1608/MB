## Questions

**1.** Define the terms *gene pool* and *allele frequency*. **[2]**

**2.** State three conditions that must apply for the Hardy-Weinberg principle to hold. **[3]**

**3.** In a population of 10 000 fish, 144 lack scales. Scalelessness is caused by a recessive allele.

**(a)** Calculate the frequency of the recessive allele. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the number of fish expected to be heterozygous. **[2]**

**4.** A population of 300 snails has 180 BB, 90 Bb and 30 bb individuals.

**(a)** Calculate the frequency of the B allele. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the number of Bb snails expected if the population were in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, and suggest what the difference from the observed number shows. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why siblings from the same parents may differ in phenotype. **[4]**

**6.** A population of 2000 insects has a recessive allele r at frequency 0.4. A pesticide kills every RR insect before breeding. Rr and rr insects survive and breed.

**(a)** Calculate the number of RR, Rr and rr insects before spraying. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the frequency of r among the survivors. **[3]**
**(c)** Name this type of selection. **[1]**

**7.** A population of 20 lizards on a small island has allele T at a frequency of 0.30. In the next generation T is present as 7 of 40 gene copies.

**(a)** Calculate the change in the frequency of T. **[1]**
**(b)** The same loss of 5 gene copies happens in a mainland population of 20 000 lizards. Calculate the change in frequency there. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why genetic drift is important only in small populations. **[2]**

**8.** Name the type of selection described in each case.

**(a)** In a plant, both very early and very late flowering individuals are eaten by seed predators, and intermediate flowering time is favoured. **[1]**
**(b)** In a fish, small and large individuals both breed successfully but medium-sized fish are caught by a predator. **[1]**
**(c)** In a bird, a colder climate favours larger body size, and the mean size increases over generations. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain how selection in (c) leads to a change in allele frequency. **[3]**

**9.** Explain how a population of mice split by a newly formed mountain range could give rise to two species. **[6]**

**10.** Compare allopatric and sympatric speciation. **[4]**

**11.** The table shows the frequency of allele a in two populations of the same species over five generations.

| Generation | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Population X (about 5000 individuals) | 0.50 | 0.50 | 0.49 | 0.50 | 0.50 |
| Population Y (10 individuals) | 0.50 | 0.35 | 0.45 | 0.20 | 0.00 |

**(a)** Describe the changes shown by Population Y. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain the difference between the two populations. **[3]**
**(c)** Suggest what the data show about the allele in Population Y at generation 5 and what that means for future generations. **[2]**

**12.** Explain how natural selection and isolation over a long period have produced the great diversity of species. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Gene pool: the complete set of alleles, for every gene, in a population at a given time [1]. Allele frequency: how often an allele occurs in the gene pool (as a proportion or percentage) [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Both definitions need the population and the time reference. Writing "all the genes" instead of "all the alleles" is a common slip.

**2.** Three from: large population [1]; random mating [1]; no mutation [1]; no migration or gene flow; no natural selection (all genotypes equally likely to survive and reproduce). Each correct condition earns a mark, to a maximum of three. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Give three separate conditions. "Closed population" and "no migration" are one point, not two.

**3. (a)** q² = 144 ÷ 10 000 = 0.0144 [1]; q = **0.12** [1].
**(b)** p = 1 − 0.12 = 0.88; 2pq = 2 × 0.88 × 0.12 = 0.2112 [1]; 0.2112 × 10 000 = **2112 fish** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Start from the recessive phenotype, because only it equals q². Show the square root, since a bare answer leaves nothing to credit if it is wrong.

**4. (a)** B alleles = (2 × 180) + 90 = 450 [1]; p = 450 ÷ 600 = **0.75** [1].
**(b)** q = 0.25; expected Bb = 2 × 0.75 × 0.25 × 300 = **112.5** (about 113) [1]. Observed 90 is fewer, so there is a deficit of heterozygotes; the population is not in equilibrium, which suggests a condition such as random mating is not met [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the numerical difference to a named condition. "It is not in equilibrium" with no reason gains less than a reason.

**5.** Genetic: different combinations of alleles from meiosis through crossing over and independent segregation [1]; random fertilisation means any sperm fuses with any egg [1]; mutation may have produced new alleles in a gamete [1]. Environmental: differences in diet, disease or other conditions affect how genes are expressed, so the phenotype reflects both genotype and environment [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Name a genetic cause and an environmental cause. Repeating "genes and environment" without naming processes is vague.

**6. (a)** p = 0.6; RR = 0.36 × 2000 = **720** [1]; Rr = 2 × 0.6 × 0.4 × 2000 = **960** and rr = 0.16 × 2000 = **320** [1].
**(b)** Survivors = 960 + 320 = 1280 [1]; r alleles = 960 + (2 × 320) = 1600 of 2560 [1]; frequency = **0.625** [1].
**(c)** **Directional** selection [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Count alleles, not individuals. Dividing 1600 by 1280 gives a value above 1, which should alert you to the error.

**7. (a)** 7 ÷ 40 = 0.175; change = 0.30 − 0.175 = **0.125** (a decrease) [1].
**(b)** The mainland population has 40 000 gene copies [1]; 5 ÷ 40 000 = **0.000 125** [1].
**(c)** Chance events affect a few copies, which is a large share of a small gene pool, so frequencies change a lot or an allele is lost [1]. In a large population the same chance events are a tiny share and average out [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Support the explanation with the figures. Saying "small populations have fewer alleles" does not explain why chance matters.

**8. (a)** Stabilising [1]. **(b)** Disruptive [1]. **(c)** Directional [1].
**(d)** Large birds are more likely to survive and reproduce in the cold [1]; they pass on the alleles for large size to offspring [1]; the frequency of these alleles in the gene pool rises across generations [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* An explanation of selection should end with the allele frequency rising, not just "large birds survive".

**9.** The mountain range is a geographical barrier, so there is no gene flow between the two populations [1]. Mutations arise independently in each [1]. The environments differ, so natural selection favours different alleles in each [1]. Genetic drift may also change allele frequencies in either population [1]. Over many generations the gene pools accumulate differences [1]. The populations can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring, so they are now two species [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Allopatric speciation is a sequence. Name the barrier, the lack of gene flow, the different selection, and finish with the failure of fertile interbreeding.

**10.** Allopatric: populations are separated by a geographical barrier [1]. Sympatric: the populations share the same area [1]. Allopatric isolation comes from physical separation, while sympatric speciation needs another barrier, such as different breeding times, behaviour or a change in chromosome number [1]. In both, gene pools diverge until members cannot interbreed to produce fertile offspring [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A comparison needs a similarity or difference stated in the same sentence for each feature. Two lists with no link tend to gain less.

**11. (a)** Allele a falls overall from 0.50 to 0.00 [1]; the change is irregular, with a rise in generation 3 (0.35 to 0.45) [1].
**(b)** Population Y is small, so chance events have a large effect on allele frequency (genetic drift) [1]. In Population X (large) chance effects average out, so the frequency stays near 0.50 [1]. Drift is random, so it shows no steady direction [1].
**(c)** Allele a has been lost from Population Y [1]; it cannot reappear unless by mutation or migration, so the population is now fixed for the other allele [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote figures in a describe answer. A reference to a size difference alone does not explain the pattern; link the small size to chance.

**12.** Variation arises by mutation, meiosis and random fertilisation [1]. Selection pressures such as predation, disease or competition favour some phenotypes, so favourable alleles become more frequent [1]. Where populations are isolated, their gene pools diverge by different selection and drift [1]. Over a long time, repeated cycles of divergence lead to groups that cannot interbreed to produce fertile offspring, so new species arise from existing species, increasing diversity [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the words "allele frequency", "isolated" and "fertile offspring". An answer that says species "adapted to their environment" does not describe a mechanism.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing evolution as something individuals do rather than a change in a population's allele frequencies.
- Using the dominant phenotype as q² instead of the recessive.
- Counting individuals instead of alleles when finding p and q.
- Giving a calculated frequency without a final answer, or mixing frequencies with numbers.
- Not naming a broken condition when observed genotypes differ from expected.
- Leaving out the passing on of the allele in a natural selection explanation.
- Describing drift as if it improves survival.
- Omitting "fertile" from the end point of speciation.
