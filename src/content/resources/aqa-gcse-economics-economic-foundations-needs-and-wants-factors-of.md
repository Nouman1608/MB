## 3.1.1.1 Economic activity

### Needs and wants

A **need** is anything a person must have in order to stay alive and healthy, for example food, drinking water, a roof and warm clothes. A **want** is anything that would be enjoyable to own or do but that a person can manage without, for example a games console or a holiday abroad.

The line between them moves. Three things shift it:

- **Time and technology.** Once a new product becomes normal, going without it can make everyday life hard. A household that now needs an internet connection to apply for jobs or to do homework may treat it as a need, while a century ago nobody could.
- **Income and place.** A coat is a need in a cold climate and closer to a want in a warm one. A richer household can treat more things as ordinary.
- **Society and age.** Expectations differ between communities, and the needs of a child differ from those of a pensioner.

Answers are stronger when they give a definition and then a reason for change, not just an example.

### The central purpose of economic activity

Economic activity exists for one central purpose: **producing goods and services that satisfy needs and wants**. Goods are physical (bread, laptops); services are not (haircuts, bus journeys). Every business, farm and public service exists to supply one or both.

### The key economic decisions

Every society must answer three questions.

| Decision | Meaning | Example (Country A, illustrative) |
|---|---|---|
| What to produce | The mix of outputs, and the quantity of each | More homes or more shopping centres |
| How to produce | Which resources and methods | Hand-picking fruit or using a harvesting machine |
| Who is to benefit | Who receives what is produced | Sold to anyone who can pay, or shared by need |

**Worked example: how to produce.** A farm in Country A can produce 1,000 units of vegetables each week in two ways. Labour-intensive: 20 workers at £400 a week each. Capital-intensive: 5 workers at £400 each plus a machine costing £3,500 a week to run.

- Labour-intensive cost: 20 × 400 = £8,000, so £8.00 a unit.
- Capital-intensive cost: (5 × 400) + 3,500 = £5,500, so £5.50 a unit.
- The capital-intensive method saves £2,500 a week.

Cost is not the only factor. The machine method needs 15 fewer workers, so those people lose their jobs, and the farm is committed to the machine whether or not demand holds up. A good answer weighs both sides.

### The main economic groups and their interactions

The three main groups are **consumers** (households who buy goods and services), **producers** (firms and other organisations that make and sell them) and **government** (which taxes, spends, regulates and provides services).

```
          wages, rent, interest, profit
   +--------------------------------------+
   v                                      |
CONSUMERS  --- spending on goods --->  PRODUCERS
   |       <-- goods, services -----       |
   | labour and other resources -------->  |
   |                                       |
   +-- taxes ---> GOVERNMENT <-- taxes ----+
       <-- benefits, public services, subsidies, regulation --
```

In words: households sell their labour (and sometimes land or savings) to producers and receive income. They spend that income on what producers make. Producers pay wages and other rewards and receive revenue. Government collects taxes from both groups and returns the money through public services such as schools, welfare payments, and subsidies to firms. It also sets rules that producers and consumers must follow, such as safety standards.

A change in one group passes to the others. If government raises a sales tax, consumers face higher prices, and producers may sell less.

## 3.1.1.2 The factors of production

### The nature of an economic resource

An **economic resource** is an input used in producing goods and services. Resources have three features: they are **limited in supply**, they can be **put to different uses**, and using them to make one thing means they are not available for another. Resources are grouped into four **factors of production**.

| Factor | What it is | Examples | Reward |
|---|---|---|---|
| Land | All natural resources | Farmland, minerals, forests, fish stocks, water | Rent |
| Labour | Human effort, physical or mental | Bakers, nurses, accountants | Wages or salary |
| Capital | Equipment and buildings that were made to help produce further output | Ovens, lorries, computers, factory buildings | Interest |
| Enterprise | Bringing the other factors together and bearing the risk of failure | Someone who starts and runs a business | Profit |

Points that cause confusion:

- **Capital in economics means physical equipment**, not cash. A firm borrows money to buy capital goods, and the lender receives interest. Interest is therefore the reward for providing the money that pays for capital.
- **Land includes natural resources**, not just ground. A coal seam and a fishing ground count.
- **Enterprise earns profit, which can be negative.** The entrepreneur is paid last, after rent, wages and interest, so the reward is not guaranteed.
- A bakery's flour is a **raw material**, bought from other producers, not a separate factor.

### Worked example: rewards to the factors

A small bakery in Town B (illustrative) takes in £8,000 revenue in one month. Its owner pays:

- £1,200 rent for the premises (land)
- £2,800 wages to three staff (labour)
- £150 interest on a £36,000 loan used to buy ovens (capital)
- £1,950 on flour and other ingredients

Profit is revenue minus all payments:

```
Payments = 1,200 + 2,800 + 150 + 1,950 = £6,100
Profit   = 8,000 - 6,100 = £1,900
Profit as % of revenue = 1,900 / 8,000 x 100 = 23.75%, about 23.8%
```

The interest check: 5% a year on £36,000 is £1,800 a year, or £1,800 / 12 = £150 a month. Wages take 2,800 / 8,000 = 35% of revenue; rent takes 15%. The owner (enterprise) keeps the £1,900 and bears the risk that revenue falls next month.

## 3.1.1.3 Making choices

### The basic economic problem

The **basic economic problem** is **scarcity**: resources are limited, but human needs and wants are unlimited. Because not everything can be produced, every person, firm and government has to choose. If resources were unlimited, no choice would be needed and nothing would have a cost.

Scarcity is not the same as poverty, and it is not just about a shortage of one product. A very rich country faces scarcity because its land, labour and capital cannot satisfy every want.

### Costs and benefits of a choice

Choices are made by weighing **costs** against **benefits**. A cost is what is given up; a benefit is what is gained. Costs and benefits can be financial or non-financial (time, environmental damage, wellbeing).

### Opportunity cost

The **opportunity cost** of a choice is whatever the **best alternative given up** was worth. It is not the total of every option you passed over, only the single best one forgone.

**Worked example 1: a firm.** A bakery has one oven for 8 hours a day. It can bake 60 loaves an hour or 24 cakes an hour.

- Spending 3 hours on cakes produces 3 × 24 = 72 cakes.
- Those 3 hours could have made 3 × 60 = 180 loaves, so the opportunity cost of 72 cakes is **180 loaves**.
- For one cake: 60 / 24 = **2.5 loaves**.
- The reverse: one loaf costs 24 / 60 = 0.4 cakes.

**Worked example 2: government.** Country A has £120 million. It can build one hospital wing (£120m) or three schools (£40m each).

- 120 / 40 = 3 schools. If it builds the wing, the opportunity cost is **three schools**.
- Reversed: if it spends the £120m on three schools, the opportunity cost is the hospital wing.
- The £120m price tag measures the money spent. The opportunity cost names what that money could otherwise have produced.

**Worked example 3: weighing a choice.** A town council in Region C (illustrative) has funds for only one of three projects. Figures are in £ thousands:

| Option | Benefits | Costs | Net benefit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Park | 90 | 60 | 30 |
| Car park | 70 | 35 | 35 |
| Library | 80 | 55 | 25 |

Net benefit is benefits minus costs. The car park has the highest net benefit (35), so on these figures it is chosen. The opportunity cost of the choice is the next best option, the park, worth 30 net.

The evaluation matters more than the sum. The figures are estimates. Some benefits, such as the wellbeing of residents, are hard to measure. Different groups gain differently, and the car park may harm people who prefer green space. A decision that looks best on money alone might not be best overall.

## Common errors

- Defining opportunity cost as "the money you spend". It is the value of the next best use of the resource.
- Listing all alternatives as the opportunity cost. Only the single best one forgone counts.
- Calling interest the reward to "capital" as if capital were cash.
- Saying profit is guaranteed. It can be zero or a loss.
- Treating a want as always a want. State that classification can change over time.
