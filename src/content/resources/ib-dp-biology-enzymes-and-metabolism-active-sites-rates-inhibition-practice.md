## Questions

**1.** Distinguish between anabolism and catabolism, giving one example of each. **[4]**

**2.** An enzyme's active site is made of only a few amino acids. Explain how the rest of the enzyme helps the active site to catalyse a reaction. **[3]**

**3.** Describe induced-fit binding between an enzyme and its substrate. **[3]**

**4.** Explain why a substrate molecule and an active site must collide for catalysis, and give one situation in which the enzyme or the substrate is immobilised. **[3]**

**5.** Explain, using the structure of the active site, why a protease does not digest starch, and why heating the protease strongly stops it working. **[4]**

**6.** A bacterium from a hot spring makes an enzyme. The mass of product formed per minute (mg min⁻¹) was recorded at different temperatures.

| Temperature / °C | 40 | 50 | 60 | 70 | 80 | 90 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rate / mg min⁻¹ | 2.0 | 4.1 | 8.0 | 12.4 | 3.1 | 0.2 |

**(a)** State the range of temperatures that contains the optimum. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in rate between 80 and 90 °C. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain the rise in rate from 40 to 70 °C. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain the fall in rate above 70 °C. **[2]**

**7.** **(a)** Explain why an enzyme's rate falls when pH moves far from its optimum. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why the rate stops rising when substrate concentration is very high. **[2]**

**8.** Oxygen released by an enzyme from hydrogen peroxide was measured as the total volume of gas collected.

| Time / s | 0 | 15 | 30 | 45 | 60 | 75 | 90 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Volume / cm³ | 0 | 12 | 21 | 27 | 31 | 33 | 34 |

**(a)** Calculate the initial rate over the first 30 s, with units. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the mean rate between 60 and 90 s. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain the difference between the two rates. **[2]**

**9.** Energy values for a reaction are: reactants 55, products 70, peak of the uncatalysed pathway 120, peak of the catalysed pathway 78 (all kJ mol⁻¹).
**(a)** Calculate the activation energy with and without the enzyme. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the overall energy change and state whether the enzyme alters it. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain how the enzyme lowers the activation energy. **[2]**

**10.** (HL only) **(a)** Distinguish between intracellular and extracellular enzyme-catalysed reactions, with one example of each. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why metabolism always generates heat, and state one use of this heat in mammals. **[2]**
**(c)** State one cyclical metabolic pathway. **[1]**

**11.** (HL only) Rates of an enzyme reaction (arbitrary units) with two reversible inhibitors, X and Y, are shown.

| Substrate / mM | 2 | 4 | 8 | 16 | 32 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| No inhibitor | 25 | 40 | 57 | 73 | 84 |
| With X | 10 | 18 | 31 | 47 | 64 |
| With Y | 15 | 24 | 34 | 44 | 51 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage reduction in rate caused by X and by Y at 2 mM and at 32 mM. **[4]**
**(b)** Identify the type of inhibition shown by X and by Y. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain the difference using the way each inhibitor binds. **[3]**

**12.** (HL only) **(a)** Explain how isoleucine regulates its own synthesis from threonine. **[4]**
**(b)** Penicillin is a mechanism-based inhibitor of transpeptidase. Explain how penicillin affects a bacterium, and how a change to transpeptidase gives resistance. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Anabolism builds larger molecules from smaller ones, using condensation [1]; example: protein synthesis, glycogen formation or photosynthesis [1]. Catabolism breaks larger molecules into smaller ones [1]; example: hydrolysis in digestion or oxidation in respiration [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A "distinguish" answer should state both sides; a definition of only one is usually not enough, and each example must match its type.

**2.** Interactions (such as hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds and hydrophobic interactions) between amino acids throughout the protein hold the three-dimensional structure [1]. This keeps the few active-site amino acids in the correct positions [1], giving the active site the shape and chemical properties needed for catalysis [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Say that the whole structure positions the active-site amino acids; "the enzyme is a protein" alone does not answer the question.

**3.** Substrate binds to the active site [1]. The enzyme changes shape slightly as it binds [1], and the substrate changes shape too, which strains its bonds and eases the reaction [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Mentioning that the substrate also changes shape separates induced fit from a rigid lock-and-key description.

**4.** Molecules move randomly in solution, and a reaction needs the substrate to collide with the active site in the right orientation [1]. Without collision there is no binding [1]. Example: an enzyme embedded in a membrane, or a large immobilised substrate [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The example must be one where something is fixed in place; a free enzyme in solution does not earn the mark.

**5.** The active site of the protease has a shape and charge that match protein substrates, not starch [1], so starch cannot bind [1]. Heat breaks bonds holding the tertiary structure [1], so the active site is distorted and the enzyme is denatured [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the loss of activity to the active site changing shape; "the enzyme dies" gains nothing.

**6. (a)** Between 60 and 80 °C (accept 70 °C as the highest reading) [1]
**(b)** (0.2 − 3.1) / 3.1 × 100 [1] = **−94%** (a 94% fall) [1]
**(c)** Higher temperature gives more kinetic energy, so more frequent collisions between substrate and active sites [1], and more collisions have enough energy to react [1]
**(d)** Bonds holding the enzyme's shape break [1], the active site is distorted, so substrate cannot bind [1] **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* A percentage-change answer needs the sign or the word fall; the optimum is a range because no reading lies between 70 and 80 °C.

**7. (a)** Away from the optimum, R group charges change [1], which disrupts ionic and hydrogen bonds [1], so the active site changes shape and the enzyme may be denatured [1]
**(b)** Every active site is already occupied nearly all the time [1], so extra substrate cannot increase the rate [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* A plateau is explained by saturated active sites; "enzyme runs out" is a common wrong explanation.

**8. (a)** 21 / 30 [1] = **0.70 cm³ s⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** (34 − 31) / 30 [1] = **0.10 cm³ s⁻¹** [1]
**(c)** The rate is lower later because substrate has been consumed [1], so collisions with active sites are rarer [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A rate is a change divided by time, with units; give the units even when the question does not repeat them.

**9. (a)** Without enzyme: 120 − 55 = **65 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]; with enzyme: 78 − 55 = **23 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** 70 − 55 = **+15 kJ mol⁻¹** (energy is taken in overall) [1]; the enzyme does not alter it [1]
**(c)** It binds the substrate and strains its bonds [1], so less energy is needed to reach the transition state [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Activation energy is measured from the reactants, not from zero, and the sign of the overall change should be stated.

**10. (a)** Intracellular reactions occur inside the cell producing the enzyme [1], for example glycolysis in the cytoplasm [1]. Extracellular reactions occur outside it, after secretion, for example digestion in the gut [1]
**(b)** Metabolic reactions are not 100% efficient at transferring energy [1]; heat helps to maintain a constant body temperature [1]
**(c)** The Krebs cycle (or the Calvin cycle) [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Pair each type with a correct example; glycolysis is intracellular and linear, so it does not answer part (c).

**11. (a)** X at 2 mM: (25 − 10) / 25 = **60%** [1]; at 32 mM: (84 − 64) / 84 = **24%** [1]. Y at 2 mM: (25 − 15) / 25 = **40%** [1]; at 32 mM: (84 − 51) / 84 = **39%** [1]
**(b)** X is competitive [1]; Y is non-competitive [1]
**(c)** X resembles the substrate and binds reversibly to the active site [1], so more substrate outcompetes it [1]. Y binds reversibly to an allosteric site and changes the active site, so extra substrate does not remove the effect [1] **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Cite the figures that show the reduction shrinking for one inhibitor and staying near 40% for the other; naming the type without data is weaker.

**12. (a)** Isoleucine is the end product of the pathway [1]. It binds to and inhibits an early enzyme, threonine deaminase [1]. When isoleucine is plentiful the pathway slows [1]; when it falls, inhibition lifts and synthesis speeds up [1]
**(b)** Penicillin binds irreversibly to the active site of transpeptidase [1], so cross-links in the cell wall are not formed and the wall is weakened [1]. A changed transpeptidase cannot bind penicillin [1], so those bacteria still make cell walls, survive and reproduce [1] **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the enzyme inhibited by isoleucine and describe the loop in both directions; for penicillin, "irreversible" is the key word.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying enzymes are killed, rather than denatured.
- Stating that a substrate "does not fit" without naming the change in active site shape.
- Explaining a plateau as the enzyme being used up.
- Omitting units on a rate, or reading a rate from the wrong part of a curve.
- Giving percentage change without the sign or without dividing by the original value.
- Measuring activation energy from zero instead of from the reactants.
- Naming an inhibitor type without using the data or the binding site to justify it.
- Saying competitive inhibition cannot be overcome, or non-competitive inhibition can.
