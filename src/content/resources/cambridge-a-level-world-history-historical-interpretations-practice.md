Cambridge 9489 Paper 3 gives you one extract on your chosen topic and one question: "What can you learn from this extract about the interpretation and approach of the historian who wrote it?" The three practice extracts below -- one for each topic in the 2027-2029 syllabus -- are constructed to argue a clear main interpretation, so that you can practise identifying it as a whole and testing it with your own knowledge. Allow 1 hour 15 minutes per extract.

## How Paper 3 answers are marked

Published 9489 Paper 3 mark schemes use two grids of six levels each, with 20 marks for AO4 (interpretation) and 20 for AO1 (knowledge). Markers judge AO4 first; the AO1 mark is usually awarded at the same level.

| Level | AO4 marks | What the answer does with the interpretation (summarised) |
|---|---|---|
| 6 | 18-20 | Complete understanding of the interpretation and approach; explains all elements of it |
| 5 | 15-17 | Sound understanding; engages with elements accurately but not with the interpretation as a whole |
| 4 | 12-14 | Partial understanding; identifies elements but treats lesser messages as equally important |
| 3 | 9-11 | Explains only sub-messages, or a different interpretation in each paragraph |
| 2 | 5-8 | Summarises what the extract says |
| 1 | 1-4 | Refers to some aspects of the extract |

| Level | AO1 marks | Knowledge |
|---|---|---|
| 6 | 18-20 | Detailed, accurate and entirely relevant |
| 5 | 15-17 | Detailed, mostly accurate, mainly relevant |
| 4 | 12-14 | Mostly relevant and accurate |
| 3 | 9-11 | Generally accurate and relevant |
| 2 | 5-8 | Some accurate and relevant knowledge |
| 1 | 1-4 | Limited knowledge |

Knowledge used without reference to the interpretation cannot be rewarded.

## Question 1: The origins of the First World War

*Constructed extract (Marlbridge, for practice).*

> It has become fashionable to say that Europe stumbled into war, that no one wanted it and that the machinery of alliances simply carried the statesmen along. The documents do not bear this out. In Berlin, the men who mattered had concluded long before July 1914 that time was against them. Russia's railway building and the rapid growth of its army meant that within a few years the Schlieffen Plan would no longer work. The army chiefs said so repeatedly, and the Chancellor listened. When the archduke was murdered, Germany did not merely stand by an ally; it urged Vienna to act fast and act hard, promising support whatever happened. Berlin knew that a strike on Serbia was likely to bring in Russia and, through Russia, France. It accepted that risk because a war fought now seemed better than a war fought later on worse terms. The appeals for restraint that came from London were treated as a nuisance. Other governments made mistakes -- Russian mobilisation was hasty, and Austria-Hungary pursued its own interests in the Balkans with reckless determination -- but these were reactions within a crisis that Germany had chosen to escalate. The war aims drawn up in the autumn, with their plans for a German-dominated central Europe, only confirm what the July decisions already reveal.

**What can you learn from this extract about the interpretation and approach of the historian who wrote it? Use the extract and your knowledge of the origins of the First World War to explain your answer. [40]**

### Indicative content

**Main interpretation:** Germany bore the main responsibility because its leaders deliberately chose to escalate the July crisis, accepting a general war as preferable to a later war on worse terms. A complete answer explains both parts: the *deliberate choice* in July and the *preventive motive* rooted in fear of Russian growth.

**Approach:** The historian rejects "shared responsibility" and "sleepwalking" explicitly, stresses documents from Berlin and connects pre-war decisions to wartime aims. This is close to the Fischer thesis and to later historians who restated German responsibility, for example during the centenary debate. The dismissive line about "fashionable" views suggests the author is writing against Clark-style multi-national accounts.

**Sub-messages to keep in proportion:** Russian mobilisation was hasty; Austria-Hungary pursued its own aims; British appeals were ignored.

**Knowledge to test the claim (AO1):**
- Support: the "blank cheque" of 5-6 July 1914; Moltke's anxiety about Russian rearmament and the Great Programme of 1913; the War Council of 8 December 1912 used by Fischer; the September Programme (1914).
- Challenge: Austria-Hungary's ultimatum to Serbia (23 July) and its own decision for war; Russia's general mobilisation (30 July) before Germany's; Bethmann Hollweg's late attempt to restrain Vienna (29-30 July); critics such as Ritter argued that wartime aims cannot be read back into July.

## Question 2: The Holocaust

*Constructed extract (Marlbridge, for practice).*

> There was no blueprint for genocide in 1933. What existed was a fanatical hatred of Jews at the top of the regime and a state without clear lines of authority, in which ministries, party offices and the SS competed to show that they were carrying out the leader's will. Each new measure -- the boycott, the Nuremberg Laws, the expropriations after November 1938 -- created new problems that rival agencies tried to solve in ever harsher ways. Emigration was the declared aim in the 1930s; plans to deport Jews to Lublin or Madagascar followed in 1939 and 1940. It was the war in the East that broke the remaining limits. Once millions of Jews were under German control, once the war against the Soviet Union was framed as a war of annihilation, and once local commanders found that mass shootings met no objection from above, killing became the solution. Hitler did not need to sign an order; he needed only to signal approval. The decisions of 1941 were taken in steps, encouraged by victory and then by frustration, and they were carried out by officials who understood what was expected of them.

**What can you learn from this extract about the interpretation and approach of the historian who wrote it? Use the extract and your knowledge of the Holocaust to explain your answer. [40]**

### Indicative content

**Main interpretation:** The Holocaust was not planned in advance; it emerged from the radicalising competition of a polycratic Nazi state, driven by Hitler's ideology but brought to the point of mass murder by the contingency of war in the East in 1941. This is a synthesis leaning towards structuralist and functionalist positions.

**Approach:** The historian accepts ideology as the motive ("fanatical hatred... at the top") but places the weight on structures (competing agencies) and circumstances (war, territorial control). Phrases such as "Hitler did not need to sign an order" and officials "who understood what was expected" echo Kershaw's "working towards the Führer"; "decisions... taken in steps" echoes Browning's account of decisions in 1941.

**Knowledge (AO1):**
- Support: April 1933 boycott; Nuremberg Laws (1935); Kristallnacht and the "Aryanisation" decrees (1938); Nisko/Lublin plan (1939) and Madagascar plan (1940); Einsatzgruppen shootings after June 1941; Wannsee Conference (January 1942) co-ordinating an existing programme.
- Challenge (intentionalist): Hitler's Reichstag "prophecy" of 30 January 1939; *Mein Kampf*; historians such as Dawidowicz who argue the goal was fixed early. A strong answer notes the extract *concedes* the importance of ideology, so it is not purely structuralist.

## Question 3: The origins and development of the Cold War

*Constructed extract (Marlbridge, for practice).*

> The Cold War was nobody's plan. In 1945 both Washington and Moscow wanted security above all, but each defined it in a way the other could only see as a threat. For Stalin, security meant friendly governments on the Soviet Union's western border, a buffer against a third invasion in a generation. For the Americans, it meant an open, prosperous Europe in which democracy could survive and American trade could flourish. Each measure taken for defence looked like aggression from the other side. The imposition of communist governments in Poland and Romania alarmed Western leaders; the Truman Doctrine and Marshall Aid persuaded the Kremlin that the United States meant to buy Europe and encircle the Soviet Union. Neither side had full information about the other, and both read the worst into ambiguous moves. Personalities mattered less than this structure of mutual suspicion: a world with only two great powers left standing, and an ideological gulf between them, produced rivalry as surely as any decision in Berlin in 1948.

**What can you learn from this extract about the interpretation and approach of the historian who wrote it? Use the extract and your knowledge of the origins and development of the Cold War to explain your answer. [40]**

### Indicative content

**Main interpretation:** Neither side was to blame: the Cold War arose from mutual misperception within a bipolar structure, as each power's defensive search for security appeared threatening to the other. This is a post-revisionist interpretation.

**Approach:** The historian treats both powers symmetrically, emphasises security, perception and the international system over ideology or individual leaders, and explicitly downplays personality. A Level 6 answer explains how this approach (systemic, "security dilemma") produces the no-fault conclusion.

**Knowledge (AO1):**
- Support: Soviet losses in the war; Polish question at Yalta (February 1945); Novikov telegram (September 1946) reading US policy as expansionist; Molotov's walk-out from the Paris talks on Marshall Aid (July 1947) and the creation of Cominform.
- Challenge: traditionalist emphasis on Stalin's ideology and the Long Telegram (February 1946); revisionist emphasis on US economic aims and atomic diplomacy; "New" Cold War history after the opening of Soviet archives, notably Gaddis's *We Now Know* (1997), which returned the weight to Stalin -- the post-post-revisionism the syllabus names.

## Model plan for any extract (about 10 minutes)

1. Read the whole extract twice; write the main interpretation in one sentence.
2. Underline evidence in the extract for each part of that sentence.
3. List sub-messages separately so you do not mistake them for the main claim.
4. Note the approach: whose actions are stressed, what kind of cause, what tone towards other views.
5. Choose four or five precise pieces of knowledge that test the claim, for and against.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should I evaluate whether the historian is right?**
Testing the interpretation with knowledge is how AO1 is rewarded, but the AO4 focus is on understanding the interpretation and approach. Do both, keeping the extract at the centre.

**What if the extract is not about causation?**
The November 2024 Paper 3 mark scheme for the Holocaust used a bystander extract and warned that forcing causation labels onto it showed misunderstanding. Explain what the extract blames, in its own terms.

**How much should I quote?**
Short quotations that prove the main interpretation are enough; long copying is summary, which is Level 2.
