## Key terms

- **Policy conflict (trade-off):** pursuing one objective moves another away from its target.
- **Spare capacity:** actual output below potential output. Conflicts are weaker here.
- **Stagflation:** rising inflation together with rising unemployment.
- **Sticky wages and prices:** slow to fall when demand falls.
- **Adaptive expectations:** forecasts based on past outcomes. **Rational expectations:** forecasts use all available information.
- **Credibility:** belief that policy-makers will do what they announce.
- **Policy mix:** several instruments used together.

## The five trade-offs at a glance

| Conflict | Chain in short | Eases when |
|---|---|---|
| Unemployment and inflation | AD up, jobs up, wage pressure, prices up | Spare capacity; supply-side gains; low expectations |
| Growth and inflation | AD-led growth uses capacity, bottlenecks, prices up | Long-run AS shifts right |
| Inflation and balance of payments | Higher prices than rivals, dearer exports, cheaper imports, deficit | Currency floats down; productivity rises |
| Growth and balance of payments | Income up, imports up (marginal propensity to import) | Exports grow alongside; import substitution |
| Growth and income and wealth equality | Gains concentrated at the top; asset prices rise | Broad-based job growth; redistribution; skills |

## Method: writing a conflict answer

1. State the two objectives and the instrument.
2. Give the chain, step by step.
3. Say if spare capacity exists.
4. Give the consequence for each group.
5. Name a way to reduce the conflict and its limits.
6. Judge: depends on size, cause (demand or supply shock), time and who bears the cost.

## Formulas and reminders

| Item | Rule | Example (invented) |
|---|---|---|
| Output gap % | (actual − potential) ÷ potential × 100 | (679 − 700) ÷ 700 = −3.0% |
| Real exchange rate change | (1 + domestic inflation) ÷ (1 + foreign inflation) − 1, nominal rate fixed | 1.03 ÷ 1.01 − 1 = 2.0% |
| Import change | marginal propensity to import × change in income | 0.2 × 50 = 10 |
| Real interest rate | (1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + expected inflation) − 1 | 1.05 ÷ 1.03 − 1 = 1.9% |
| Output-price split of AD growth | price ≈ AD growth ÷ (1 + e); output ≈ AD growth × e ÷ (1 + e) | 6% with e = 2: price 2%, output 4% |
| Quantity theory | MV = PY; inflation ≈ (1 + money growth)(1 + velocity change) ÷ (1 + output growth) − 1 | 1.04 ÷ 1.015 − 1 = 2.5% |
| Recovery needed after a fall of x | 1 ÷ (1 − x) − 1 | after −25%: 33.3% |

## Resolving or reducing conflicts

| Method | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Supply-side policy | Raises capacity and lowers the natural rate | Slow, costly, uneven |
| Policy mix | Matches instruments to targets | Interaction, coordination |
| Prioritise one objective | Clear, credible | Sacrifices another |
| Exchange rate change | Helps the external balance | Import prices rise |
| Redistribution | Offsets inequality | Incentive effects, cost |
| Timing | Act when capacity allows | Lags, uncertain output gap |

**Evaluation hooks:** short run against long run; size of spare capacity; demand-pull against cost-push; credibility; who pays.

## Worked reminders

**Jobs against inflation (Country A, invented).** The labour force is 40 million. Unemployment falls from 7.0% to 5.5%: 1.5% × 40 million = 600,000 more people employed. If inflation rises from 2.0% to 3.5% on the way, each extra 100,000 jobs cost 0.25 points of inflation. Always state both sides of the exchange.

**Growth against the external balance (Country G).** GDP is 500, the marginal propensity to import is 0.4, and GDP grows 4% (a rise of 20). Imports rise by 8. Exports rise by 5. The balance moves by 5 − 8 = −3, so the deficit widens by 3.

**Growth against equality (Country H).** Two equal-sized groups: mean incomes 5,000 and 40,000. They grow 1% and 4%, giving 5,050 and 41,600. The mean rises from 22,500 to 23,325, up 3.7%. The ratio of richer to poorer rises from 8.00 to 8.24, so growth came with wider inequality.

**Fixed exchange rate (Country I).** Prices rise 5% at home and 1% abroad: 1.05 ÷ 1.01 − 1 = 4.0% loss of price competitiveness, with no currency movement to offset it.

## Must-know distinctions

- Demand-pull inflation (spare capacity used up) against cost-push (supply shock): the first fits a demand-side trade-off, the second shifts the whole trade-off.
- Short-run against long-run trade-off: expectations can remove the long-run one.
- Nominal against real: wages, interest rates, exchange rates and debts.
- Adaptive against rational expectations: the second gives credible announcements more power.
- Cyclical against structural unemployment: only the first responds well to demand policy.

## Debates

- **Wage and price flexibility.** Flexible: markets clear and unemployment is temporary (neo-classical). Sticky: demand shocks leave lasting unemployment (Keynesian). A big wage cut also cuts spending.
- **AS flexibility.** The more elastic AS is, the more of an AD rise shows in output. Steep AS near capacity turns it into prices. Supply-side measures raise elasticity.
- **Expectations.** Higher expected inflation lowers the real interest rate and raises wage claims. Credible announced policy changes behaviour sooner under rational expectations.

## Why approaches changed

| Episode | Problem | Response and lesson |
|---|---|---|
| Great Depression | Output and prices fell, mass unemployment, wages did not restore full employment | Keynes: deficient AD; government should act; debt-deflation raises real debts |
| Keynesian demand management, 1970s | Supply shocks and wage claims: inflation and unemployment together | AD expansion fuelled inflation; trade-off unstable; cost-push matters |
| Monetarism, 1980s | Inflation and weak confidence in demand management | Money supply control, expectations, supply-side reform; velocity unstable; cost in jobs |
| 2007-08 crisis and aftermath | Bank losses, credit freeze, deep recession | Low rates, quantitative easing, stimulus then consolidation, tighter bank rules |

**Pattern to remember:** each approach was shaped by the problem of its day. Mass unemployment favoured demand management. Inflation with unemployment favoured control of money, expectations and supply. A banking collapse brought unusual central bank tools and a lasting argument about the right balance between stimulus and consolidation. In an answer, name the circumstance, the policy response, and one limit of that response.

## Quick self-test

1. Define a policy trade-off.
2. Output is 679 and potential output is 700. Calculate the output gap.
3. Domestic prices rise 3% and foreign prices 1%, with a fixed nominal rate. Calculate the change in the real exchange rate, to 2 d.p.
4. The marginal propensity to import is 0.2 and income rises by 50. What happens to imports?
5. Nominal interest is 5% and expected inflation 3%. Calculate the real rate, to 2 d.p.
6. Nominal AD rises 6% and AS elasticity is 2. Estimate the price and output changes.
7. With MV = PY, money grows 4%, velocity is constant and output grows 1.5%. Estimate inflation, to 2 d.p.
8. A debt of 150 is fixed in money terms. Prices fall 10%. What is its real value, to 1 d.p.?
9. After a 25% fall in output, what rise restores the old level, to 1 d.p.?
10. State two supply-side policies that reduce the growth and inflation conflict.
11. Why did demand management lose support in the 1970s?
12. Give two features of the policy response after 2007-08.

### Answers

1. A situation in which pursuing one objective moves another away from its target.
2. (679 − 700) ÷ 700 = **−3.0%**.
3. 1.03 ÷ 1.01 − 1 = **1.98%** (competitiveness worsens).
4. Imports rise by 0.2 × 50 = **10**.
5. 1.05 ÷ 1.03 − 1 = **1.94%**.
6. Price 6 ÷ 3 = **2%**, output 6 × 2 ÷ 3 = **4%**.
7. 1.04 ÷ 1.015 − 1 = **2.46%**.
8. 150 ÷ 0.9 = **166.7**.
9. 1 ÷ 0.75 − 1 = **33.3%**.
10. For example, education and training, and investment in infrastructure or capital, shifting long-run AS right.
11. Supply shocks and wage pressure produced inflation and unemployment together, and AD expansion fed inflation.
12. For example, policy rates cut very low with quantitative easing, and tighter regulation of bank capital.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a trade-off with no step-by-step chain.
- Ignoring spare capacity, so the conflict seems the same at all times.
- Saying the Phillips Curve is always stable or always broken, without saying when.
- Treating supply-side policy as quick and free.
- Listing historical events without linking them to why the approach changed.
- Mixing up wage growth and price inflation, or nominal and real values.
- Concluding with a summary instead of a judgement tied to the question.
- Giving percentages with no working or unit.
