## Questions

**1.** Which statement explains why water has a high latent heat of vaporisation? **[1]**

- **A** Covalent bonds inside water molecules must be broken before water can evaporate.
- **B** Hydrogen bonds between water molecules must be broken before water can evaporate.
- **C** Water molecules are non-polar, so they leave the liquid surface slowly.
- **D** Water has a high specific heat capacity, so it stores heat before evaporating.

**2.**

**(a)** Explain why a water molecule is polar. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe how a hydrogen bond forms between two water molecules. **[2]**

**3.** Draw a labelled diagram of two water molecules joined by one hydrogen bond. Show the charges on the atoms. **[3]**

**4.** Root hair cells absorb potassium ions (K⁺) and chloride ions (Cl⁻) from the soil solution.

**(a)** Describe how water molecules are arranged around a K⁺ ion and around a Cl⁻ ion in solution. **[2]**
**(b)** State why this property of water is important for the movement of mineral ions to the leaves. **[1]**

**5.** A student stirs a spoonful of sucrose into one beaker of water and a few drops of olive oil (mostly triglycerides) into another. The sucrose dissolves. The oil forms a separate layer on the surface. Explain these observations. **[3]**

**6.** An aquarium holds 40 kg of water. Over one afternoon the water absorbs 504 kJ of energy from a heater and sunlight, with no heat lost. The body temperature of the fish in the tank follows the water temperature.

**(a)** Calculate the rise in temperature of the water. Take the specific heat capacity of water as 4.2 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹. Show your working. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest why the size of this temperature rise is important for the fish. **[2]**

**7.** During a long cycle ride, a cyclist must lose 1.8 MJ of heat by evaporation of sweat. Take the latent heat of vaporisation of water as 2.4 kJ g⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate the mass of sweat that must evaporate. **[2]**
**(b)** Only 75% of the sweat produced evaporates; the rest drips off. Calculate the total mass of sweat the cyclist must produce. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why sweat that drips off the skin does little to cool the cyclist. **[1]**

**8.** In an investigation, two plants of the same species were kept in bright light at the same air temperature. Plant P was well watered and its stomata were open. Plant Q was in dry soil and its stomata were closed. The leaf temperature of P was 29 °C and that of Q was 34 °C. Explain the difference in leaf temperature. **[3]**

**9.** Distinguish between the specific heat capacity of water and the latent heat of vaporisation of water. **[2]**

**10.** Water makes up most of the mass of a mammal's cells and blood.

**(a)** Explain how the properties of water allow substances to be transported in blood plasma. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain how hydrogen bonding between water molecules helps a mammal to keep a stable body temperature on a hot day. Refer to two different properties of water. **[5]**

**11.** This question compares the two thermal properties of water. Take the latent heat of vaporisation as 2.4 kJ g⁻¹ and the specific heat capacity as 4.2 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate the energy removed when 10 g of water evaporates. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the fall in temperature this amount of energy would cause if it were removed from 2.0 kg of liquid water. Give your answer to 2 significant figures. **[2]**
**(c)** A small desert mammal has very little water to spare. Use your answer to (a) to explain why evaporative cooling can still be useful to it. **[2]**
**(d)** Suggest why panting cools a dog less well in very humid air. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* A is the classic distractor: the covalent O–H bonds are not broken when water evaporates, and choosing it shows the two bond types have been confused.

**2. (a)** Oxygen attracts the shared electrons in the O–H bonds more strongly than hydrogen, so oxygen is δ− and each hydrogen is δ+ [1]. The molecule is bent, so the charge is unevenly spread, with a δ− side and a δ+ side [1].
**(b)** The δ+ hydrogen of one water molecule [1] is attracted to the δ− oxygen of a neighbouring water molecule; this weak attraction is the hydrogen bond [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b), "the molecules attract each other" is not enough; the mark needs the specific atoms, δ+ H on one molecule and δ− O on the other.

**3.** Two molecules each drawn as one O joined to two H atoms by solid lines, in a bent shape [1]. δ− labelled on each O and δ+ on each H [1]. A dashed or dotted line from an H of one molecule to the O of the other, labelled hydrogen bond [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A dotted line drawn from O to O, or between two H atoms, loses the third mark even if it is labelled correctly.

**4. (a)** Around K⁺, water molecules turn their δ− oxygen towards the ion [1]. Around Cl⁻, water molecules turn their δ+ hydrogens towards the ion [1].
**(b)** The ions are dissolved, so they can be carried in the water moving up the xylem [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Both ions must be described for (a); a reversed answer (H towards K⁺) scores zero for that ion.

**5.** Sucrose has many –OH groups, so it is polar [1]. Its –OH groups form hydrogen bonds with water molecules, so it dissolves [1]. Triglycerides are non-polar and cannot form hydrogen bonds with water, so the oil does not dissolve and stays as a separate layer [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Oil is hydrophobic" restates the observation; the third mark needs the reason, that it cannot form hydrogen bonds with water.

**6. (a)** temperature change = 504 000 ÷ (40 000 × 4.2) [1] = **3.0 °C** [1].
**(b)** The fish's body temperature changes only a little, so its enzymes stay near their optimum temperature [1] and are not denatured, so its rate of metabolism stays steady [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* In (a), converting only one quantity (kg to g, or kJ to J) gives an answer out by a factor of 1 000 and loses the accuracy mark; the method mark can still be awarded.

**7. (a)** mass = 1 800 ÷ 2.4 [1] = **750 g** [1].
**(b)** total = 750 ÷ 0.75 [1] = **1 000 g** (1.0 kg) [1].
**(c)** Heat is only removed from the skin when water evaporates, by breaking hydrogen bonds; liquid sweat that drips off has not taken in the latent heat of vaporisation [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b), multiplying 750 by 0.75 instead of dividing is a common slip; the answer must be larger than (a), and a value smaller than 750 g scores no marks.

**8.** In P, water evaporates from the mesophyll cell walls and leaves through open stomata in transpiration [1]. Evaporation needs a lot of energy because hydrogen bonds between water molecules must break, as water has a high latent heat of vaporisation [1]. This energy is taken from the leaf, cooling it; in Q the stomata are closed, so there is little evaporation and little cooling [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* An answer that only says "P loses more water" misses the point; marks come from linking evaporation, latent heat and the transfer of energy away from the leaf.

**9.** Specific heat capacity is the energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 g (or 1 kg) of liquid water by 1 °C [1]. Latent heat of vaporisation is the energy needed to change liquid water into water vapour, with no change in temperature [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Each definition must say what changes, a rise in temperature for one and a change of state for the other, or the two marks merge into one.

**10. (a)** Water molecules are polar [1]. They surround ions and form hydrogen bonds with polar molecules such as glucose and amino acids, so these substances dissolve [1]. Dissolved substances are carried in the plasma as the blood flows round the body [1].
**(b)** Water molecules are held together by many hydrogen bonds [1]. Much energy is used to break hydrogen bonds before the temperature rises, so water has a high specific heat capacity [1]. So the body's temperature rises only slowly as heat is gained [1]. When sweat evaporates, hydrogen bonds must break, so water has a high latent heat of vaporisation [1]. The evaporating water takes a large amount of energy from the skin, cooling the body with little water lost [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b), credit for each property needs its own hydrogen-bond reason and its own effect on the mammal; naming the two properties without explanation earns little.

**11. (a)** 10 × 2.4 = **24 kJ** [1].
**(b)** 24 000 ÷ (2 000 × 4.2) [1] = **2.9 °C** [1].
**(c)** Evaporating a small mass of water removes a large amount of energy, as (a) shows [1], so the mammal can lose enough heat while losing little water [1].
**(d)** Humid air already holds a lot of water vapour, so less water evaporates from the mouth and airways [1]. Less evaporation means less energy is removed, so less cooling [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b), 2.857 must be rounded to 2.9 as asked; giving 2.86 or 3 loses the accuracy mark, though the method mark stands.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling the O–H bond inside a water molecule a hydrogen bond, or picking that idea in a multiple-choice item.
- Describing a hydrogen bond without naming the δ+ hydrogen and δ− oxygen on different molecules.
- Reversing the orientation of water molecules around positive and negative ions.
- Writing "hydrophobic" as the explanation instead of "cannot form hydrogen bonds with water".
- Mixing kg with g, or kJ with J, in thermal calculations.
- Dividing where a multiplication is needed (or the reverse) when a percentage of sweat evaporates.
- Linking transpiration only to water loss, not to energy removed from the leaf.
- Giving a property with no role, or a role with no hydrogen-bond reason.
- Saying high specific heat capacity keeps organisms "warm" rather than keeping temperature stable.
