This guide teaches section **3.1.3.7 Forces between molecules** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Papers 1 and 2 (and Paper 3). The specification asks you to explain the existence of three types of force between **familiar and unfamiliar** molecules, and to explain how melting and boiling points are influenced by them. Most marks are won by naming the correct force, saying why it exists and comparing its strength with another. The [Bonding study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-bonding/) gives a shorter treatment. ## The three types

### Induced dipole–dipole (London, dispersion) forces

These act between **all** molecules, polar or not, including the noble gases.

1. Electrons in a molecule are constantly moving, so at any instant the distribution may be uneven. This forms a **temporary (instantaneous) dipole**.
2. The temporary dipole induces a dipole in a neighbouring molecule, because the δ+ end attracts electrons (or the δ− end repels them).
3. The two dipoles attract each other. The dipoles fluctuate, but they stay in step, so the attraction is present all the time.

**Strength increases with the number of electrons** in the molecule. A bigger molecule (greater relative molecular mass, more electrons) has a larger electron cloud that is easier to distort, so the temporary dipoles are bigger and the attractions are stronger. Shape also matters. Long, straight-chain molecules can lie side by side with a large area of contact, which gives stronger forces than compact branched molecules of the same formula.

### Permanent dipole–dipole forces

Between **polar molecules**. The δ+ end of one molecule attracts the δ− end of a neighbour. These forces are in addition to the London forces already present. For molecules of similar size, polar molecules have higher boiling points than non-polar ones.

### Hydrogen bonding

A hydrogen bond is a strong type of permanent dipole–dipole attraction. It forms between:

- a hydrogen atom covalently bonded to **N, O or F**, which carries a large δ+ charge, and
- a **lone pair** on an N, O or F atom of a neighbouring molecule.

The three atoms lie in a straight line (angle 180°) around the hydrogen. In a diagram, show the lone pair, the δ+ and δ− labels and a dotted line for the bond, with the line between the hydrogen and the lone pair.

Why these three elements? N, O and F are small and highly electronegative, so the X–H bond is strongly polar. The small N, O or F atom also has a lone pair that can approach the hydrogen closely. Hydrogen has no inner electrons to shield its nucleus, so the δ+ hydrogen is strongly attracted.

## Order of strength

For molecules of similar size: **hydrogen bonds > permanent dipole–dipole > London forces**. All of these intermolecular forces are far weaker than covalent bonds. This is why a molecular substance has a low melting point compared with a giant structure. However, for large molecules the London forces can add up to more than a hydrogen bond in a smaller one, so the order is only a guide when molecules differ greatly in size.

## Boiling points and melting points

When a molecular substance boils, the intermolecular forces are overcome. **The covalent bonds within the molecules do not break.** The stronger the forces, the more energy is needed and the higher the boiling point.

### Worked example 1: the halogens

| Halogen | Cl₂ | Br₂ | I₂ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Boiling point / °C | −34 | 59 | 184 |

Explain the trend. All three are non-polar, so the only force is London forces. The number of electrons increases from Cl₂ to I₂, so the temporary dipoles are larger and the attractions between molecules are stronger. More energy is needed to separate the molecules, so the boiling point rises.

### Worked example 2: isomers of C₅H₁₂

| Isomer | Pentane | 2-methylbutane | 2,2-dimethylpropane |
|---|---|---|---|
| Boiling point / °C | 36 | 28 | 9 |

All three have the same number of electrons, so the difference comes from shape. Pentane is a straight chain with the largest area of contact between molecules and the strongest London forces. 2,2-dimethylpropane is almost spherical, so molecules touch at fewer points and the attractions are weakest.

### Worked example 3: butane and propanone

Butane, C₄H₁₀ (Mᵣ 58), boils at about −1 °C. Propanone, CH₃COCH₃ (Mᵣ 58), boils at 56 °C.

The molecules have the same relative molecular mass and so similar London forces. Propanone has a polar C=O bond, so it has a permanent dipole, and the extra permanent dipole–dipole forces raise its boiling point. Butane has only London forces.

### Worked example 4: ethanol and methoxymethane

Ethanol, C₂H₅OH, and methoxymethane, CH₃OCH₃, both have the formula C₂H₆O (Mᵣ 46). Ethanol boils at 78 °C and methoxymethane at −24 °C.

Ethanol has an O–H bond, so its molecules form hydrogen bonds with each other. Methoxymethane has no hydrogen bonded to oxygen, so it has only permanent dipole–dipole and London forces, which are weaker.

## Anomalous boiling points: the hydrides

| Group | 14 | 15 | 16 | 17 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Period 2 | CH₄ −162 | NH₃ −33 | H₂O 100 | HF 20 |
| Period 3 | SiH₄ −112 | PH₃ −88 | H₂S −60 | HCl −85 |
| Period 4 | GeH₄ −88 | AsH₃ −62 | H₂Se −41 | HBr −67 |
| Period 5 | SnH₄ −52 | SbH₃ −17 | H₂Te −2 | HI −35 |

(boiling points in °C)

- In Group 14 the boiling point rises steadily with the number of electrons: the molecules are non-polar and only London forces act.
- In Groups 15, 16 and 17 the boiling points of NH₃, H₂O and HF are much **higher** than the trend from the other hydrides would predict. These molecules form **hydrogen bonds**, which are stronger than the forces in the other hydrides.
- After the first member, the boiling points rise with the number of electrons, because London forces grow.

### Worked example 5: explaining HF against HCl

Explain why hydrogen fluoride (20 °C) boils at a much higher temperature than hydrogen chloride (−85 °C).

Both are polar. In HF the hydrogen is bonded to fluorine, so molecules form hydrogen bonds between the δ+ hydrogen of one molecule and a lone pair on fluorine in another. In HCl, chlorine is not small enough and not electronegative enough for this, so only the weaker permanent dipole–dipole and London forces act. More energy is needed to overcome the hydrogen bonds in HF.

## Hydrogen bonding in water and ice

Water boils at 100 °C, far higher than H₂S (−60 °C), because each water molecule can form up to **four** hydrogen bonds: two using its two hydrogen atoms and two using the two lone pairs on its oxygen atom.

In ice the molecules are held in an open lattice, with each molecule hydrogen bonded to four others at tetrahedral angles. This arrangement holds the molecules **further apart** than in liquid water, where some hydrogen bonds are broken and molecules pack more closely. Ice is therefore **less dense** than liquid water, so it floats. This keeps lakes from freezing solid from the bottom, which helps aquatic life.

## Common mistakes

- Writing that covalent bonds break when a molecular substance boils.
- Saying "hydrogen bonds are between hydrogen and oxygen" without naming a lone pair or an electronegative atom in another molecule.
- Forgetting that London forces exist between all molecules, including polar ones.
- Saying that hydrogen bonding explains the trend in the halogens or the alkanes.
- Referring to "atoms" attracting each other instead of molecules.
- Describing London forces as "weak" without saying how they are caused.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Explain** a boiling point difference: name the force in each substance, compare their strength and say that more energy is needed to overcome the stronger one.
- **Draw** hydrogen bonding: show the lone pair, the δ+ and δ− charges, and a straight dotted line.
- **State** the conditions for hydrogen bonding: H bonded to N, O or F, and a lone pair on N, O or F in another molecule.
- **Suggest** a reason for a trend: mention the number of electrons, shape or polarity, whichever the data show.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do polar molecules also have London forces?**
Yes. London forces act between all molecules; polar molecules have permanent dipole–dipole forces in addition.

**Is a hydrogen bond a covalent bond?**
No. It is a strong intermolecular attraction. It is much weaker than a covalent bond.

**Which has the higher boiling point, a small hydrogen-bonded molecule or a large non-polar one?**
It depends on the sizes. Compare the data. For large molecules the total London forces can outweigh hydrogen bonding in a small molecule.
