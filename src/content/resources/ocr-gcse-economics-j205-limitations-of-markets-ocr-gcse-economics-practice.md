These questions cover section 3.8 of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205, Version 2.0, June 2026), Limitations of markets. All markets, firms and figures are invented. Revise first with the [limitations of markets study guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-limitations-of-markets-ocr-gcse-economics/) and the [limitations of markets revision notes](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-limitations-of-markets-ocr-gcse-economics-revision-notes/).

## Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** State what is meant by a negative externality. **[1]**

**2.** Give an example of a positive externality and identify the third party who benefits. **[2]**

**3.** Producing one unit of a chemical has a private cost of £120 and an external cost of £35. Calculate the social cost per unit. **[1]**

**4.** Which one of the following policies is most likely to be used to correct a positive externality? **[1]**

- A. A tax on each unit sold
- B. A ban on the product
- C. A subsidy to producers
- D. A rise in interest rates

**5.** Explain why a free market overproduces a good that creates a negative externality. **[2]**

**6.** Explain one way legislation can reduce a negative externality. **[2]**

**7.** A government spends £4m on a campaign warning about the health risks of vaping. State one opportunity cost of this spending. **[1]**

## Section B: structured and extended questions

**8.** Plastic packaging creates an external cost of £50 per tonne through litter and waste. The market produces 400,000 tonnes a year at a price of £300 per tonne. The government introduces a tax of £50 per tonne. The price rises to £335 and output falls to 380,000 tonnes.

**(a)** Calculate the total external cost per year before the tax. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the yearly tax revenue. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate how much of the tax per tonne is paid by buyers and how much by producers. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the reduction in external cost per year. **[1]**
**(e)** Explain why output fell by only a small amount. **[1]**

**9.** At present 40,000 adults a year pay £200 each for numeracy courses. Each course gives an external benefit of £250, through higher productivity and lower benefit spending. The government decides to provide the courses free, at a cost of £180 per place, and 70,000 adults now take them.

**(a)** Calculate the cost to the government. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the external benefit from the additional people who take a course. **[1]**
**(c)** Evaluate whether free state provision is a good use of government money in this case. **[3]**

**10.** Analyse how an information campaign about the dangers of drink-driving could reduce a negative externality. **[3]**

**11.** Evaluate whether regulation is better than taxation for reducing air pollution from factories. **[8]**

**12.** Evaluate the costs and benefits of subsidising bus travel to reduce traffic congestion in a city. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** A cost imposed on a third party, outside the transaction, that is not reflected in the market price [1]. **[1]**

**2.** For example, a person being vaccinated [1] benefits others nearby, who are less likely to catch the disease [1]. (Credit also: education benefiting employers and colleagues; a well-kept garden benefiting neighbours.) **[2]**

**3.** 120 + 35 = **£155** [1]. **[1]**

**4.** **C** [1]. A subsidy lowers the cost of producing the good, so more is supplied. **[1]**

**5.** Producers and consumers consider only their private costs and benefits, not the cost to third parties [1]. The market price is therefore lower than the full social cost, so more is produced and consumed than is best for society [1]. **[2]**

**6.** A law can set a limit on emissions or ban a harmful activity, such as dumping waste in rivers [1]. Firms that break the law face fines, so they reduce the harmful activity and third parties suffer less [1]. **[2]**

**7.** For example, the £4m could have been spent on treatment services, school equipment or a tax cut [1]. **[1]**

**8. (a)** 400,000 × £50 = **£20m** [1].
**(b)** 380,000 × £50 = **£19m** [1].
**(c)** Buyers: 335 − 300 = **£35** [1]. Producers: 50 − 35 = **£15** [1].
**(d)** (400,000 − 380,000) × £50 = **£1m** [1].
**(e)** Demand is price inelastic: a price rise of about 12% led to a fall in quantity of only 5%, perhaps because there are few substitutes for plastic packaging [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Comment on the result. The tax raised a lot of revenue but reduced the harm only a little, which is a key point for evaluation.

**9. (a)** 70,000 × £180 = **£12.6m** [1].
**(b)** 30,000 extra × £250 = **£7.5m** [1].
**(c)** The extra external benefit of £7.5m is less than the £12.6m cost, and £7.2m of the cost goes on 40,000 people who would have paid anyway [1]. However, the new learners also gain private benefits, such as higher wages, and access no longer depends on ability to pay, which may help low-income adults most [1]. Judgement: free provision may not be the best use of money compared with a smaller subsidy targeted at those who cannot afford the course, which would cut the opportunity cost [1]. **[5]**

**10.** The campaign informs drivers about the risk of killing or injuring other people, third parties to their decision [1]. Better-informed drivers are less likely to drink and drive, so the number of accidents falls [1]. Fewer accidents mean less harm to others and lower costs for the health service and police, so the external cost is reduced [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Regulation: a legal limit on emissions gives certainty about the maximum pollution [1], which suits harmful pollutants where any excess is dangerous [1]. But it needs inspectors and enforcement, which cost money, and firms have no reason to cut pollution below the limit [1]. Taxation: a tax per unit of pollution makes firms pay for the harm, so they cut pollution where it is cheapest to do so [1], and it raises revenue that could fund health care or clean technology [1]. But it is hard to measure the external cost to set the right tax, and if firms can pass the tax on, pollution may fall little [1]. Evaluation: the better policy depends on how harmful the pollutant is and how easily emissions can be measured [1]. Judgement: regulation is better for dangerous pollutants, while a tax is better for widespread, less dangerous pollution, so many governments use both [1]. **[8]**

**12.** Benefits: lower fares make buses cheaper relative to driving, so some people switch from cars [1], reducing congestion, journey times and pollution for third parties [1]. It also helps low-income households, who are more likely to use buses [1]. Costs: the subsidy costs taxpayers, with an opportunity cost in other services [1]. Many passengers would have used the bus anyway, so part of the subsidy does not reduce congestion, and car users may not switch if buses are slow or infrequent [1]. Judgement: the subsidy is most effective if bus services are reliable and frequent, and may work better combined with a charge on driving in the city centre [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Not naming the third party.
- Claiming a tax removes the externality completely.
- Forgetting the opportunity cost of subsidies and state provision.
- Calculating the burden of a tax without saying who pays what.
- Evaluating with no judgement or no link to the case.
