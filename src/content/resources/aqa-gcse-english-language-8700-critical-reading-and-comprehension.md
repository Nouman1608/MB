Critical reading is the skill underneath every reading question in AQA GCSE English Language (8700). Before you can
analyse a metaphor or compare two writers, you have to understand what a text says, what it implies, and how far you
trust it. This guide goes deeper into that skill than the paper-level guides do. For quick recall use the
[critical reading revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-english-language-8700-critical-reading-and-comprehension-revision-notes/),
and to test yourself use the
[critical reading practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-english-language-8700-critical-reading-and-comprehension-practice/).

## What the specification includes

The first content bullet of 3.1.1 Critical reading and comprehension is long, and every part of it is examinable. In
plain terms it asks you to:

- identify and interpret themes, ideas and information in literature and other high-quality writing;
- read in different ways for different purposes, and judge how useful, relevant and well presented content is;
- draw inferences and justify them with evidence;
- support a point of view by referring to the text;
- identify bias and misuse of evidence, telling apart statements that are supported by evidence from those that are
  not;
- reflect critically on a text, using its context and what you know from wider reading;
- recognise that a text can be read in more than one way.

Two assessment objectives carry most of this: **AO1** (identify and interpret explicit and implicit information and
ideas) and **AO4** (evaluate texts critically and support this with appropriate textual references).

| Where it is tested | What critical reading looks like there |
|---|---|
| Paper 1, Question 1 (4 marks) | Finding information in a named section of a fiction extract, precisely |
| Paper 1, Question 4 (20 marks) | Testing a statement about the extract and reaching your own supported judgement (AO4) |
| Paper 2, Question 1 (4 marks) | Deciding which statements about a non-fiction source are true |
| Paper 2, Question 2 (8 marks) | Inferring from both sources to summarise a difference or similarity |

Paper 1 Question 4 is the heaviest single test of this subtopic: it is worth as much as the other three Paper 1
reading questions together.

## Key idea 1: explicit, implicit and the inference chain

**Explicit** information is stated: "The kitchen was empty." **Implicit** information is suggested: a kettle "still
ticking as it cooled" implies that someone left in a hurry. An inference is only as strong as the evidence beneath it,
so build every one as a chain:

```
Detail (short quotation) -> what it suggests -> why that matters to the focus of the question
```

Use tentative verbs that match the strength of the evidence: *suggests*, *implies*, *hints*, *perhaps*. Save *shows*
and *proves* for what is stated outright. A single detail can carry more than one inference; offering two possible
readings of the same detail is one of the clearest signs of critical rather than literal reading.

## Key idea 2: reading in different ways for different purposes

Each question needs a different kind of reading:

- **Scanning** for a short question confined to particular lines: stay inside those lines and pick out exactly what is
  asked for.
- **Skimming** for overall sense on a first read, so you know the shape of the whole text before any question.
- **Close reading** for evaluation: slow, line-by-line attention to one section, asking what each choice does to the
  reader.

Judging "usefulness, relevance and presentation" means asking whether a text does its job for its purpose. A
19th-century travel letter may be vivid but tell a modern reader little that is reliable about the place; a report may
be accurate but presented so drily that it fails to persuade.

## Key idea 3: evaluating a statement

Paper 1 Question 4 gives you a view of part of the extract and asks how far you agree. Evaluation is not the same as
analysis. Analysis explains *how* a method creates an effect; evaluation judges *how well* the writer achieves what the
statement claims, and whether the statement is fair.

A reliable way to test a statement is to split it into its parts. If it says a character is presented as "lonely and
frightened", those are two claims. You might find strong evidence for loneliness, weaker evidence for fear, and a hint
that the character is in fact defiant. That gives you a judgement with a shape: mostly agree, with a qualification.

## Key idea 4: bias, evidence and unsupported claims

This part of the specification is mostly met in non-fiction, especially Paper 2's sources. Ask of every claim: what
supports it? Watch for:

- **generalisation** from one example ("every teenager I meet is glued to a screen");
- **anecdote presented as proof** (one story standing in for a pattern);
- **loaded language** that judges before it informs ("so-called experts");
- **selective evidence**, where only facts that help the writer's case are given;
- **opinion stated as fact**, with no source or reason.

Spotting these is not the same as dismissing the writer. A biased text can still be powerful, and saying *why* a
writer selected or slanted evidence is a strong critical point.

## Key idea 5: context, wider reading and different responses

Use context only when it sharpens a reading. Knowing that a 19th-century writer assumed servants were part of every
respectable household helps you see why a detail passes without comment. Wider reading helps you recognise
conventions: a writer who withholds a character's name may be building mystery, as many short-story openings do.

Recognising different responses means acknowledging, briefly and with evidence, another way to read a detail ("A
reader could instead see her silence as calm control"), then saying which reading you find more convincing and why.

## Worked example: testing a statement

The passage below is original, written for this guide.

> *Mara waited at the bus stop long after the last bus had gone. She checked the timetable twice, though she knew it by
> heart. When a car slowed beside her, she stepped back into the shelter and pretended to read her phone, which had
> been dead since lunch.*

**Statement:** "In this part of the story, the writer presents Mara as helpless."

**Model paragraph (annotated):**

> I partly agree. Mara is clearly stranded: she waits "long after the last bus had gone", which suggests she has no
> other way home **[inference from evidence]**. Yet "helpless" is too simple. Checking a timetable she "knew by heart"
> implies anxiety, but it is also a small act of control, as if routine could keep her calm **[second reading of one
> detail]**. Most tellingly, she "pretended to read her phone, which had been dead since lunch". The writer withholds
> the dead battery until the end of the sentence, so the reader first sees a confident gesture and then realises it is
> a bluff **[evaluates method and effect]**. Mara is vulnerable, but she is resourceful in protecting herself, so the
> writer presents her as frightened rather than helpless **[sustained, qualified judgement]**.

Why this reaches higher levels: it gives a clear judgement in the first words, tests the key word of the statement,
offers more than one reading of a detail, and evaluates the effect of the writer's choice rather than only naming it.

## Worked example: supported or unsupported?

> *Original extract:* "Our town centre is dying. Three shops on the high street closed last year, and the council's own
> survey found that footfall fell between the two counts it carried out. Nobody under thirty shops locally any more."

- "Three shops... closed last year": a factual claim that could be checked; supported.
- "The council's own survey found that footfall fell": supported by a named source.
- "Nobody under thirty shops locally any more": a sweeping generalisation with no evidence; unsupported.
- "Our town centre is dying": a conclusion from limited evidence; the metaphor makes it sound more certain than the
  facts allow.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating implicit as invented.** An inference must be traceable to a word or phrase in the text.
- **Agreeing totally without testing.** Simple agreement with the statement leads to description, not evaluation.
- **Going outside the lines.** Paper 1 Question 1 credits only information from the section named.
- **Retelling the plot** as if it were interpretation.
- **Calling a text biased and stopping there**, rather than explaining what is slanted and to what effect.
- **Bolting on context** that does not change how the text is read.

## Exam technique by command word

- **List:** short, separate points from the specified lines; full sentences are not needed, but each point must be
  accurate and distinct.
- **Choose... true statements:** check each statement against the exact lines; watch for statements that are almost
  right but overstate or change a detail.
- **To what extent do you agree?:** open with your judgement, test each part of the statement, evaluate the writer's
  methods, and end by restating a judgement that has been earned.
- **Evaluate:** keep linking effect to the statement's focus. Ask "how successfully?", not just "how?".
