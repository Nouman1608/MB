Condensed notes for section 3.2 (Market research) of the Cambridge IGCSE Business 0264 syllabus for examination in 2027, 2028 and 2029, content statement 3.2.1 Methods of market research. Worked examples on survey design, demand estimates and comparing designs are in the [market research study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-business-0264-market-research/). Test yourself with the [market research practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-business-0264-market-research-practice/). The combined [marketing and market research notes](/resources/igcse-business-0264-marketing-and-the-market-and-market-research-revision-notes/) also cover section 3.1.

All examples are invented.

## Why businesses use market research

- Find out what customers want and how they rate existing products.
- Reduce the risk of a new product, location or price failing.
- Estimate how many people might buy.
- Find out what competitors offer and charge.
- Spot changes in the market early.

Exam point: always link the reason to the *decision* in the case, such as "to estimate whether enough students would buy smoothies to cover the rent".

## Primary research (new data, collected for this purpose)

| Method | Best for | Main drawback |
|---|---|---|
| Questionnaire or survey | Numbers from many people: how many, how often, how much | Poor questions or low response give misleading results |
| Interview | Detailed answers that can be followed up | Slow and costly per person; interviewer can influence answers |
| Focus group | Reasons behind opinions; reactions to a design | Small group may not represent the market; loud members dominate |
| Observation | What people actually do | Shows what, not why |

**Overall:** up to date, specific to the question, not seen by rivals; but costly and slow.

### Question types

| Type | Example | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Closed | "Do you own a bicycle? Yes / No" | Easy to count | No reasons given |
| Open | "Why did you choose this shop?" | Detail and new ideas | Slow to analyse |
| Rating scale | "Rate the taste from 1 to 5" | Easy to compare products | Does not say why |
| Leading (avoid) | "Don't you love our new flavour?" | None | Pushes people to agree |

## Sampling

- **Sample:** a smaller group chosen to represent the whole target group.
- **Why useful:** asking everyone costs too much and takes too long; a sample gives a guide more cheaply and quickly.
- **A good sample is** representative, large enough and chosen fairly.
- **Trade-off:** bigger and better-chosen means more accurate but more expensive.

## Secondary research (existing data, collected for another purpose)

| Source | Gives | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Competitor websites | Prices, products, locations | Only what rivals choose to show |
| Government sources | Population, ages, incomes by area | May be old; not about the product |
| Market reports | Market size, trends | Can be expensive; often national, not local |
| Trade magazines | Industry news and trends | General; not about the business's own customers |

**Overall:** cheaper and quicker, wide view of the market; but may be out of date, not specific, and available to rivals too.

## Factors affecting accuracy

| Factor | How it distorts results |
|---|---|
| Sample too small | A few unusual answers swing the result |
| Biased sample | Leaves out or over-represents groups |
| Leading or vague questions | Answers reflect the wording, not real views |
| Dishonest or careless answers | People say what sounds good or answer quickly |
| Out-of-date data | Market has changed since it was collected |
| Unreliable source | Source may have a reason to exaggerate |
| Intentions not actions | People who say they will buy may not |

## Analysing data in four steps

1. Convert counts into percentages so different-sized groups can be compared.
2. Pick out the highest, lowest or biggest difference.
3. Say what it means for the decision.
4. Add one limit of the data.

Worked reminder: 72 of 240 shoppers say they would use a click-and-collect service.

```
72 / 240 x 100 = 30%
```

If the town has 9,000 households and the sample is representative, an estimate is 30 / 100 x 9,000 = 2,700 households.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Market research | Collecting and analysing information about customers, competitors and the market |
| Primary research | Collecting new information first-hand for a specific purpose |
| Secondary research | Using information that already exists, collected for another purpose |
| Sample | A smaller group chosen to represent the whole target group |
| Bias | A distortion that makes results unrepresentative |
| Focus group | A small group discussing a product, guided by a leader |
| Leading question | A question worded to push people towards an answer |

## Exam traps

- Compare percentages, not raw numbers, when groups differ in size.
- "Primary research is accurate" is wrong without conditions: it depends on the sample and the questions.
- "Secondary research is free" is wrong: market reports can be costly.
- A method recommendation must fit the business's budget, time and what it needs to know.
- Survey intentions are estimates, not sales.

## Quick self-test

1. State two reasons why businesses use market research.
2. Define primary research.
3. Identify two secondary research sources named in the syllabus.
4. Give one advantage of observation as a research method.
5. Explain why a business uses a sample rather than asking everyone.
6. State two features of a good sample.
7. Why is "Don't you agree our prices are fair?" a poor survey question?
8. Give one disadvantage of a focus group.
9. 45 out of 150 people surveyed would buy a product. What percentage is this?
10. Using Question 9, estimate how many of 6,000 potential customers might buy.
11. State one limitation of secondary data.
12. Give one reason why the estimate in Question 10 may be inaccurate.

### Answers

1. Any two: find out customer wants; reduce risk; estimate demand; find out about competitors; spot changes in the market.
2. Collecting new information first-hand for a specific purpose.
3. Any two: competitor websites, government sources, market reports, trade magazines.
4. It records what people actually do, not what they say they do.
5. Asking everyone would cost too much and take too long; a sample gives a guide more cheaply and quickly.
6. Any two: representative of the target market; large enough; chosen fairly without bias.
7. It is a leading question, pushing people to agree, so answers do not show real views.
8. Any one: a small group may not represent the market; strong members can dominate; it is costly to run.
9. 45 / 150 x 100 = 30%.
10. 30 / 100 x 6,000 = 1,800.
11. Any one: may be out of date; collected for another purpose so may not fit; may be costly; available to competitors.
12. Any one: the sample may not be representative; people may not do what they say; the sample may be too small.
