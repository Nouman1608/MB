These questions practise Question 2 of Component 1 in Cambridge International AS & A Level Global Perspectives & Research (9239), 2026-2028, which "requires a longer response based on one of the source documents" in which candidates "focus on the evidence in the document". For the overview of the paper, see the [Component 1 Written Exam study guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-global-perspectives-written-exam/).

> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge**, with an original source. The organisation, people, places and figures are fictional. The real Question 2 is a single 15-mark response; this set breaks the skill into smaller questions first, then ends with an extended evaluation. Use it alongside the specimen and past papers your teacher provides.

Revise first with the [Question 2 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-global-perspectives-9239-written-exam-question-2/) and the [Question 2 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-global-perspectives-9239-written-exam-question-2-revision-notes/).

## Source

**Summary of a report by Keep Velaro, a fictional heritage charity**

> Velaro's old town is being loved to death, and the council should cap daily visitors at 12,000. City entry data show that average daily visitors rose from 8,000 in 2012 to 21,000 in 2024, almost tripling. In our online survey of 400 residents, 81% supported a cap. The architect who led the restoration of the cathedral says foot traffic is wearing the medieval paving faster than at any time in its history. One baker told us she lost half her regular customers after residents moved out to make way for holiday rentals. When the port city of Amel limited cruise ship arrivals in 2019, residents began moving back into its centre within three years. Our volunteers counted litter on the main square every Sunday in August and found that it doubled between 2022 and 2024. A cap would protect both the buildings and the people who live here.

## Section A

**1.** Identify **two** pieces of numerical evidence in the source. **[2]**

**2.** Identify the expert evidence in the source and give one reason why it might be credible. **[2]**

**3.** Explain one weakness of the online survey as evidence that residents support a cap. **[2]**

**4.** Identify one vested interest relevant to the evidence and explain how it could affect that evidence. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why the baker's account gives only weak support to the claim that a cap would protect "the people who live here". **[2]**

**6.** Identify the claim that the litter count is used to support, and explain one limitation of the litter count. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** The source says visitor numbers "almost tripled". Calculate the actual increase as a multiple and as a percentage, and comment on the source's wording. **[3]**

**8.** Explain one cause gap in the source's use of evidence. **[3]**

**9.** Evaluate the example of Amel as evidence for a visitor cap in Velaro. **[4]**

**10.** Suggest **two** pieces of other evidence that would help to substantiate or refute the claim that a cap would protect the buildings. For each, say what result would strengthen the claim. **[4]**

**11.** Evaluate the strength of the evidence that the old town's buildings are being damaged by visitors. **[6]**

**12.** Evaluate the evidence in the source. How far does it support the conclusion that the council should cap daily visitors at 12,000? **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two: average daily visitors rose from 8,000 in 2012 to 21,000 in 2024 [1]; 81% of 400 survey respondents supported a cap [1]; litter doubled between 2022 and 2024 [1]; Amel's residents returned within three years of its 2019 limit (accept as numerical only if the dates are quoted) [1]. **[2]**

**2.** The architect who led the restoration of the cathedral [1]. Credible because the architect has relevant expertise in the condition of old stonework and first-hand knowledge of these buildings [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Any one, developed: an online survey is self-selecting, so residents who feel strongly about tourism are more likely to respond [1], which may inflate the 81% figure compared with residents as a whole [1]. Or: the charity wrote the questions, and wording is not given, so leading questions cannot be ruled out [1]; a question such as "Should Velaro be protected from overtourism?" would push answers towards support [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Keep Velaro is a heritage campaign that wants a cap [1], so it may have chosen the survey method, the litter dates or the examples that best support its case, and left out evidence that does not [1]. Also accept: the architect's reputation is tied to protecting the restoration, so they may stress damage [1] [1]. **[2]**

**5.** It is a single anecdote, so it cannot show how common the problem is [1]. It also links the baker's loss to holiday rentals, not directly to daily visitor numbers, so it supports a claim about housing more than one about a visitor cap [1]. **[2]**

**6.** The claim: visitors are harming the old town, including the people who live there [1]. Limitation, any one: Sundays in August are likely to be the busiest days, so the count shows a peak rather than a typical day; or volunteers' counting methods may vary; or litter may reflect cleaning schedules rather than visitor numbers [1]. **[2]**

**7.** 21,000 ÷ 8,000 = 2.625, so numbers rose about 2.6 times [1]. The increase is 13,000 ÷ 8,000 × 100 = 162.5% [1]. "Almost tripled" overstates a rise that is closer to two and a half times, and the average hides seasonal peaks, so the wording makes the evidence sound stronger than it is, although the rise is still large [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Any one, developed: the source links the fall in the baker's trade to holiday rentals and treats this as a result of visitor numbers [1], but residents could be leaving because of rents, jobs or other changes in the city [1], so the evidence shows two trends together without showing that daily visitors caused the departure [1]. Or: the rise in litter is linked to visitors [1], but litter could also rise because cleaning was reduced or more events were held [1], so the increase does not show that visitor numbers are the cause [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Strength: it is a real-world case where a limit was followed by residents returning, so it suggests the policy can work [1]. Weaknesses: Amel limited cruise ships, not daily visitors to an old town, so the policy differs [1]; a port city may differ from Velaro in housing, economy and tourism type [1]; residents moving back three years later may have other causes, such as falling rents, and no comparison is given [1]. Allow a judgement for one of the marks: a relevant but weak analogy. **[4]**

**10.** Any two, 2 marks each (1 for the evidence, 1 for the result):
- Measured surveys of paving wear over several years [1]; if wear rates rise in step with visitor numbers, the claim is strengthened [1].
- Wear data from a similar old town that already limits visitors [1]; if its paving wears more slowly, the claim that a cap protects buildings is strengthened [1].
- A conservation body's independent condition report [1]; if it identifies foot traffic, not weather or pollution, as the main cause of damage, the claim is substantiated [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Indicative content; credit a developed, balanced answer.
- The only evidence of damage is the architect's statement [1].
- Strength: the architect has directly relevant expertise and first-hand knowledge of the paving [1].
- Weakness: the architect is unnamed and no measurement of wear is given, so the claim cannot be checked [1].
- "Faster than at any time in its history" is a sweeping comparison that would need historical records [1].
- The visitor figures show more people, but do not measure damage, so there is a measure gap [1].
- Judgement: some support from relevant expertise, but weak overall because there is no measured evidence of damage or its cause [1]. **[6]**

**12.** Real responses are marked holistically by level; indicative content:
- States the main conclusion and gives an opening verdict [1].
- Visitor data: specific and from the city, a credible producer; but an average that hides peaks, and the "almost tripled" wording overstates it [1] [1].
- Survey: a large-sounding sample but self-selected and run by the campaign; wording unknown [1] [1].
- Expert: relevant but unnamed and unmeasured [1].
- Anecdote and litter count: vivid but untypical and possibly peak-day evidence; cause gaps [1] [1].
- Amel: a weak analogy because the policy and setting differ [1].
- No evidence is offered for the figure of 12,000 specifically, so the size of the cap is unsupported [1].
- Suggests one specific piece of further evidence, such as visitor counts by hour matched to measured paving wear [1].
- Weighed conclusion: good support that visitor numbers have risen sharply; weak support that the rise is causing the harms described; no support for the 12,000 figure [1]. **[12]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Evaluating the charity's conclusion instead of its evidence.
- Calling the survey "biased" without explaining the self-selection or the campaign's role.
- Accepting the source's description of its own figures without checking the arithmetic.
- Treating an analogy as strong because it had a good outcome, without checking how similar the cases are.
- Suggesting "more evidence" without saying what evidence or what result would matter.
- Missing that the specific recommendation (12,000) is not supported by any evidence at all.
