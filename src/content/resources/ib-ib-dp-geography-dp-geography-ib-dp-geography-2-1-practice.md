## Section A

**1.** Define a sea surface temperature anomaly. **[1]**

**2.** Outline why water warms and cools more slowly than land. **[2]**

**3.** State which two places are compared in the Southern Oscillation Index. **[1]**

**4.** Distinguish between the solubility pump and the biological pump. **[2]**

**5.** Surface seawater pH falls from 8.2 to 8.0. By what factor does the hydrogen ion concentration increase? **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** The table shows invented data for two stations at similar latitudes.

| | Station A | Station B |
|---|---|---|
| Warmest month mean (degrees Celsius) | 16 | 27 |
| Coldest month mean (degrees Celsius) | 4 | -19 |

**(a)** Calculate the annual temperature range at each station. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest which station is coastal, and explain your choice. **[3]**

**7.** The table shows invented three-month sea surface temperature anomalies (degrees Celsius) for the central equatorial Pacific.

| Period | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Anomaly | -0.2 | -0.6 | -0.9 | -1.2 | -1.0 | -0.6 | -0.4 | -0.5 |

**(a)** Using a threshold of 0.5 degrees for at least five consecutive periods, identify whether this is an El Nino episode, a La Nina episode or neither. Justify your answer. **[2]**

**(b)** Describe the likely state of the trade winds during periods 3 to 5. **[2]**

**8.** Explain how a slow-moving tropical cyclone can weaken itself over the ocean. **[3]**

**9.** Explain why ocean warming may reduce the ocean's uptake of carbon dioxide. **[4]**

**10.** In one month, air pressure at Tahiti is 1.5 hPa below its long-term average and air pressure at Darwin is 1.0 hPa above its average.

**(a)** State whether the Southern Oscillation Index is likely to be positive or negative. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain what this suggests about the trade winds and sea surface temperatures in the eastern Pacific. **[3]**

**11.** Explain what is meant by the thermal inertia of the oceans and why it matters for future climate. **[3]**

## Section C

**12.** Examine the role of the oceans in regulating global climate. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** The difference between the observed sea surface temperature and the long-term average for that place and time of year [1]. **[1]**

**2.** Water has a higher specific heat capacity, so more energy is needed to change its temperature [1]; heat is also spread through a deep layer by mixing and by sunlight penetrating the water, and some energy goes into evaporation [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Tahiti and Darwin [1]. **[1]**

**4.** The solubility pump moves carbon to the deep ocean because carbon dioxide dissolves in cold surface water that then sinks [1]; the biological pump moves carbon down through phytoplankton photosynthesis and the sinking of dead organisms and waste [1]. **[2]**

**5.** pH change = 0.2, so the factor = 10 to the power 0.2 [1] = **1.58**, an increase of about 58 per cent [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** Station A: 16 - 4 = **12 degrees** [1]. Station B: 27 - (-19) = **46 degrees** [1].

**(b)** Station A is coastal [1]. Its smaller range reflects the ocean's high heat capacity, which keeps summers cooler and winters milder [1], whereas Station B's very large range shows a continental interior far from the moderating effect of the sea [1]. **[5]**

**7. (a)** **La Nina** [1]: periods 2 to 6 are all at -0.5 degrees or lower, five consecutive periods [1]. (Period 7 breaks the run; period 8 alone does not extend it.)

**(b)** Stronger than usual [1], pushing warm surface water further west and enhancing upwelling of cold water in the eastern Pacific [1]. **[4]**

**8.** The cyclone's strong winds mix the upper ocean, bringing cooler water from below to the surface [1]. If the storm moves slowly, it stays over this cooled water [1], so evaporation and the release of latent heat that power it are reduced and it weakens [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Carbon dioxide is less soluble in warmer water [1], so a warmer surface absorbs less of it [1]. Warming also strengthens stratification of the upper ocean [1], reducing the mixing that carries dissolved carbon downward, so the sink weakens and more carbon dioxide stays in the atmosphere [1]. **[4]**

**10. (a)** Negative [1].

**(b)** A smaller than usual pressure difference between Tahiti and Darwin means weaker trade winds [1]. Weaker trade winds let warm surface water spread east across the Pacific [1], so sea surface temperatures in the eastern Pacific are likely to be above average, with reduced upwelling, as in El Nino conditions [1]. **[4]**

**11.** The oceans warm slowly because of their high heat capacity and the large volume of water mixed by currents [1], so they delay the full surface warming caused by greenhouse gases already emitted [1]. Some further warming is therefore expected even if emissions stopped rising, which affects how quickly climate targets can be met [1]. **[3]**

**12.** Indicative content:

- **Heat storage:** high specific heat capacity lets oceans store vast amounts of energy and smooth seasonal and daily temperature changes [1]; they have absorbed most of the extra heat from global warming, giving thermal inertia [1].
- **Heat transfer:** surface currents such as the Gulf Stream and North Atlantic Drift move heat poleward [1]; thermohaline circulation redistributes heat and carbon through the deep ocean [1].
- **Coupled variability:** ENSO shows the ocean altering pressure, winds and rainfall across the Pacific and beyond [1].
- **Carbon:** the solubility and biological pumps remove carbon dioxide from the atmosphere [1].
- **Limits and change:** warming may weaken the carbon sink [1]; the AMOC is projected to weaken, which could alter regional climates [1]; acidification damages marine ecosystems that contribute to the biological pump [1].
- **Conclusion:** the oceans are the main regulator of climate on human timescales, but their capacity to regulate is itself changing under warming [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* "Examine" needs more than a list of roles: show how they interact and where they are weakening, and finish with a judgement.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Subtracting a negative temperature as if it were positive.
- Declaring an ENSO episode from the peak value without checking duration.
- Describing pH change as linear.
- Writing about currents without saying in which direction they move heat.
