## Section A

**1.** Distinguish formal justice from substantive justice. **[2]**

**2.** State the **two** rules of natural justice. **[2]**

**3.** Identify the theorist associated with the "veil of ignorance" and the theorist associated with "entitlement" theory. **[2]**

**4.** Name the body that can refer a possible wrongful conviction back to the Court of Appeal. **[1]**

## Section B

**5.** Explain what is meant by procedural justice, using **one** case on natural justice. **[4]**

**6.** Explain the utilitarian theory of justice and **one** criticism of it. **[4]**

**7.** Explain Perelman's theory of formal justice and how the doctrine of judicial precedent reflects it. **[4]**

**8.** Explain how compensatory damages in tort reflect Aristotle's idea of corrective justice. **[3]**

**9.** Explain how the Criminal Justice Act 2003 changed the rule against double jeopardy, and whether this promotes justice. **[5]**

**10.** Eli is convicted of a strict liability offence after selling food that was contaminated before it reached his shop. He had taken every reasonable precaution. Explain whether this outcome achieves justice, using **two** types of justice. **[5]**

**11.** Explain **two** ways in which civil procedure reforms have tried to make civil justice fairer, and **one** continuing obstacle. **[6]**

**12.** "Miscarriages of justice prove that the criminal law does not achieve justice." Discuss. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Formal justice is the consistent application of the same rules to like cases [1]; substantive justice concerns whether the content of the rules and the outcomes they produce are themselves fair [1].

**2.** No one should be a judge in their own cause (the rule against bias) [1]; both sides must be heard (the right to a fair hearing) [1].

**3.** John Rawls [1]; Robert Nozick [1].

**4.** The Criminal Cases Review Commission [1].

**5.** Procedural justice is concerned with whether the process used to reach a decision is fair, regardless of the outcome [1], for example an impartial decision-maker and a chance to answer the case [1]. In *Ridge v Baldwin* (1964) a chief constable was dismissed without being told the case against him or given an opportunity to respond [1], and the House of Lords held the dismissal void for breach of natural justice [1]. (Also accept *Dimes* or *ex p Pinochet (No 2)* on bias.)

**6.** Utilitarianism, associated with Bentham and Mill, holds that the just rule or decision is the one that produces the greatest happiness of the greatest number [1]; justice is judged by consequences [1]. Criticism: it can justify sacrificing an individual's rights if that benefits the majority [1], for example imposing an exemplary sentence on one offender to deter others, or even punishing an innocent person to calm public fear [1].

**7.** Perelman argued that formal justice requires beings of the same essential category to be treated in the same way [1]; it is about consistency rather than the content of the rule [1]. Precedent reflects this because courts must follow earlier decisions where the material facts are sufficiently similar [1], so like cases are decided alike and parties can predict the outcome [1].

**8.** Corrective justice requires a wrong between two parties to be put right by restoring the balance between them [1]. Compensatory damages aim to put the claimant, as far as money can, in the position they would have been in had the tort not occurred [1], so the defendant who caused the loss bears it rather than the innocent claimant [1].

**9.** Before the Act, a person acquitted could not be tried again for the same offence [1]. The Criminal Justice Act 2003 allows the Court of Appeal to quash an acquittal for a serious offence and order a retrial where there is new and compelling evidence and a retrial is in the interests of justice [1] [1]. It promotes justice by allowing the guilty to be convicted, as when Gary Dobson was convicted in 2012 of the murder of Stephen Lawrence after an earlier acquittal [1]; but it reduces finality and certainty for acquitted defendants, which is itself an aspect of fairness [1].

**10.** Formal justice may be achieved because the offence is applied consistently to every seller of contaminated food [1], and strict liability protects public health by encouraging high standards [1]. Substantive justice is not achieved because Eli is convicted although he took every reasonable precaution and is not blameworthy [1], so he bears a criminal label without fault [1]. A judgement: the outcome is formally just but substantively unjust, which is the usual criticism of strict liability [1].

**11.** The Woolf reforms led to the Civil Procedure Rules 1998, with an overriding objective of dealing with cases justly and at proportionate cost [1], and judges actively managing cases to reduce delay [1]. The track system allocates claims by value and complexity, with the small claims track offering an informal, cheaper process for lower-value disputes [1] [1]. Obstacle: cost remains high and legal aid for most civil claims was removed by LASPO 2012 [1], so many people cannot obtain advice or representation and appear as litigants in person [1].

**12.** For the statement: miscarriages such as the Guildford Four (quashed 1989) show that innocent people can be convicted [1]; in Sally Clark's case flawed statistical evidence and undisclosed medical evidence led to wrongful murder convictions [1]; Andrew Malkinson spent seventeen years in prison before his conviction was quashed in 2023, showing that correction can be extremely slow [1]; such cases damage public confidence in the system [1]. Against: the convictions were eventually quashed, showing the appeal system and the Criminal Cases Review Commission can correct errors [1]; miscarriages are exceptional compared with the number of cases tried [1]; safeguards such as the burden and standard of proof and jury trial deliver procedural justice in most cases [1]; reforms such as the Criminal Appeal Act 1995 creating the Commission show the system learning from failure [1]. Conclusion: miscarriages prove the system is imperfect rather than that it fails to achieve justice altogether; the strongest criticism is the delay in correcting errors [1] [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a theory without explaining how it applies to the rule in the question.
- In scenario questions, saying an outcome is "unfair" without naming which type of justice is missing.
- In essays on miscarriages, ignoring the mechanisms that corrected them.
- Using emotive language instead of evidence.
