## 4.1 Magnetism

- Like poles repel; unlike poles attract. Only repulsion proves that an object is a magnet.
- Magnetic: iron, steel, cobalt, nickel. Not magnetic: copper, aluminium, plastic.
- Induced magnetism: the end of a magnetic material nearest an N pole becomes an S pole.
- Temporary magnet: soft iron, easy to magnetise and demagnetise. Permanent magnet: steel, keeps its magnetism.
- Field: a region where a magnetic pole feels a force. Direction = force on an N pole. Outside the magnet, lines run N to S, never cross, and bunch up where the field is stronger.
- Plotting: place a plotting compass, mark both ends of the needle, move it along, repeat, join the dots. The needle's N end shows the field direction; iron filings show the pattern.
- Uses: permanent magnets in compasses; electromagnets in cranes and relays, where the field must switch on and off.

## 4.2 Charge, current, e.m.f., p.d., resistance

**Charge.** Measured in coulombs. Friction moves electrons only: the gainer is negative and the loser is positive. Like charges repel; unlike attract.

**Electric field lines** point the way a positive charge would be pushed.

| Source of field | Pattern |
|---|---|
| Point charge | Radial: out for positive, in for negative |
| Charged conducting sphere | Radial, as for a point charge |
| Oppositely charged plates | Equally spaced parallel lines, positive to negative |

**Conductors and insulators.** Conductors (metals, graphite) have free electrons. Insulators (plastic, glass, rubber) hold their electrons in atoms. Test with a lamp in a gap in a simple circuit.

**Current.** Conventional current runs positive to negative; free electrons drift negative to positive. D.c. flows one way; a.c. reverses. Ammeter in series, voltmeter in parallel. Choose a range above the expected reading.

**E.m.f. versus p.d.**

| E.m.f. | P.d. |
|---|---|
| Work done by a source per unit charge | Work done by unit charge passing through a component |
| Energy given to charge, round the whole circuit | Energy taken from charge, across one component |
| Cells in series add; identical cells in parallel give one cell's e.m.f. | Series p.d.s add to the supply p.d. |

**Resistance and wires.** R grows in direct proportion to length and falls in inverse proportion to cross-sectional area. Twice the length means twice R; twice the area means half R.

**Ohm's law.** Current is proportional to p.d. while the temperature stays fixed.

| Component | I-V graph | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed resistor | Straight line through origin | Constant R |
| Filament lamp | Curve, flattening | Hot filament has higher R |
| Diode | Near zero one way, steep rise the other | One-way conduction |

A hotter metal conductor has higher resistance: atoms vibrate more and collide with free electrons more often.

**Finding resistance.** Circuit with a variable resistor, ammeter in series, voltmeter across the test component. Take several pairs of readings, work out V ÷ I each time, average.

## 4.3 Circuits

### Method in steps: mixed series and parallel

1. Combine each parallel pair into one resistance.
2. Sum the series resistances for the total.
3. Find the supply current: I = V ÷ R.
4. Use that current in the series part to find its p.d.
5. Take that from the supply p.d. to get the p.d. across the parallel part.
6. Divide by each branch resistance to get branch currents, and check they add up.

### Components

- NTC thermistor: hotter means lower resistance.
- LDR: brighter light means lower resistance.
- Diode: one direction only. LED: a diode that lights up.
- Relay: a small current in a coil closes a separate switch.
- Variable potential divider: slider takes any fraction of the supply p.d.
- Fuse: wire melts when the current is too large.

### Sensor circuit reasoning

Put the sensor in series with a fixed resistor. Whichever has more resistance gets more of the supply p.d. If a thermistor warms, its resistance falls, its p.d. falls, and the fixed resistor's p.d. rises. For an LDR, replace "warms" with "light increases".

## 4.4 Practical electricity

- Parallel lamps: each has the full supply p.d., works independently, and one failure leaves the rest on.
- 1 kW h = energy transferred when a 1 kW appliance runs for 1 hour.
- Hazards: damaged insulation, overheated cables, damp conditions, overloaded sockets and leads.
- Mains wires: live, neutral, earth.
- Fuse or trip switch: in the live wire, rating just above normal current. A trip switch can be reset.
- Switch: in the live wire, so the appliance is dead when off.
- Earthed metal case: if live touches it, a large current flows to earth, the fuse melts or trip operates and the case is safe.
- Double insulation: non-conducting case, so it cannot become live. A case needs earthing or double insulation.

## 4.5 Electromagnetic effects

**Induction.** Moving a magnet and a coil relative to each other induces an e.m.f. No movement, no e.m.f. Bigger with faster cutting of field lines and more turns. Reversing the direction of movement reverses the e.m.f.

**Lenz's law.** An induced current acts to oppose the change that causes it.

**A.c. generator.** Rotating coil in a field, with slip rings and brushes (or a rotating magnet inside a fixed coil). Coil parallel to the field gives the peak or trough; coil perpendicular to the field gives zero. Faster rotation raises the peaks and shortens the period.

**Fields from currents.** Straight wire: circles, wider spaced further out. Solenoid: bar-magnet pattern. Bigger current: stronger field. Reversed current: reversed field. Right-hand grip rule for direction.

**Relay and loudspeaker.** Relay: low current operates an electromagnet that switches a high-current circuit. Loudspeaker: varying current in a coil in a permanent magnet's field moves a cone.

**Force on a conductor.** If the current or the field is reversed, the force reverses; if both are, nothing changes. Fleming's left-hand rule: first finger gives field, second finger current, thumb force. Same-direction currents in parallel wires attract; opposite-direction currents repel.

**D.c. motor.** Turning effect rises with more turns, larger current, stronger field. Split-ring commutator and brushes reverse the coil current every half turn.

**Transformer.** Two coils on an iron core; a.c. only. Step-up: Ns greater than Np. Step-down: Ns less than Np. High-voltage transmission means lower current for the same power, so less p.d. and less power lost in the cables.

## 4.6 Oscilloscope

- Shows a waveform as a trace.
- Voltage = vertical divisions × Y-gain.
- Time = horizontal divisions × timebase.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Charge versus current:** charge is in coulombs; current is charge per second.
- **Conventional current versus electron flow:** opposite directions.
- **E.m.f. versus p.d.:** the source supplies energy; the component transfers it.
- **Series versus parallel:** the same current in series; the same p.d. across parallel branches.
- **Fuse versus trip switch:** both protect; only the trip switch resets.
- **Earthing versus double insulation:** one gives the fault current a path to ground; the other prevents the case becoming live.
- **Generator versus motor:** a generator turns motion into an induced e.m.f.; a motor turns a current in a field into motion.

## Quick self-test

1. A current of 0.60 A passes for 40 s. How much charge is that?
2. 540 C passes a point in 30 minutes. What is the current?
3. Four identical 1.5 V cells are connected in series, then in parallel. What is the e.m.f. of each arrangement?
4. A component has 18 V across it and carries 0.45 A. What is its resistance?
5. A 6.0 Ω and a 12 Ω resistor are joined (a) in parallel, (b) in series. Find each combined resistance.
6. A transformer has 2300 primary turns on a 230 V supply and 150 secondary turns. What is the secondary p.d., and is it step-up or step-down?
7. A 2.4 kW heater runs for 3.0 hours. Electricity costs 15 cents per kW h. How much does this cost?
8. A 9.0 V supply is across a 2.0 kΩ and a 4.0 kΩ resistor in series. What is the p.d. across the 2.0 kΩ resistor?
9. Wire A has resistance 4.0 Ω. Wire B is the same material but has double the length and double the cross-sectional area. Find the resistance of B.
10. An oscilloscope has Y-gain 5.0 V/div and timebase 2.0 ms/div. A trace peaks 1.5 divisions high, and one cycle is 8.0 divisions wide. Find the peak p.d. and the period.
11. A lamp takes 0.50 A from a 12 V supply for 60 s. How much energy does it transfer?
12. Explain why a fuse is fitted in the live wire and not the neutral wire.

### Answers

1. Q = It = 0.60 × 40 = **24 C**.
2. t = 30 × 60 = 1800 s, so I = 540 ÷ 1800 = **0.30 A**.
3. Series: 4 × 1.5 = **6.0 V**. Parallel: **1.5 V**.
4. R = 18 ÷ 0.45 = **40 Ω**.
5. (a) (6.0 × 12) ÷ 18 = **4.0 Ω**; (b) 6.0 + 12 = **18 Ω**.
6. Vs = 230 × 150 ÷ 2300 = **15 V**, step-down.
7. Energy = 2.4 × 3.0 = 7.2 kW h; cost = 7.2 × 15 = **108 cents**.
8. V = 9.0 × 2.0 ÷ (2.0 + 4.0) = **3.0 V** (the other resistor has 6.0 V).
9. Double the length doubles R and double the area halves it, so R = 4.0 × 2 ÷ 2 = **4.0 Ω**.
10. Peak p.d. = 1.5 × 5.0 = **7.5 V**; period = 8.0 × 2.0 = **16 ms**.
11. E = IVt = 0.50 × 12 × 60 = **360 J**.
12. If the fuse were in the neutral wire, the appliance would still be connected to the live supply after the fuse melted, so touching it could still cause a shock. In the live wire, a blown fuse disconnects the appliance from the live supply.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "electricity flows through the fuse" instead of saying the thin wire heats and melts.
- Describing the voltmeter or ammeter connections the wrong way round.
- Leaving out "at constant temperature" when stating Ohm's law.
- Forgetting to convert minutes to seconds or kilowatts to watts.
- Adding resistances in parallel instead of using the combination equation.
- Saying a thermistor's resistance "decreases when it is cold".
- Describing Lenz's law as "the current opposes" without saying it opposes the change causing it.
- Putting the switch or fuse in the neutral wire.
- Giving an oscilloscope reading in divisions without multiplying by the Y-gain or timebase.
