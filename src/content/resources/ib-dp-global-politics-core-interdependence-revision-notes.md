## The six prescribed content rows

1. Global governance and international law
2. The UN -- structure, roles and functions (Charter, General Assembly, Security Council); achievements and limitations
3. Participation of IGOs and non-state actors in global governance
4. Cooperation and competition between political actors
5. Treaties, collective security, strategic alliances, economic cooperation
6. Global interactions and networks

Paper 1 is set on the core topics, so any of these can anchor a set of sources. Paper 2 section B integrating questions can also draw on them.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning in one line |
|---|---|
| Interdependence | Mutual reliance on access to resources that sustain ways of life -- economic, security and sustainability dimensions |
| Asymmetric interdependence | One side relies on the other more than the reverse; the less dependent side gains leverage |
| Sensitivity | How badly an actor is hit by a change elsewhere |
| Vulnerability | How costly it is for an actor to adjust (find another supplier, market or ally) |
| Government | Formal institutions with authority to make and enforce binding rules over a territory |
| Governance | Steering behaviour through rules, norms and networks, with or without a central authority |
| Global governance | Overlapping arrangements among states, IGOs and non-state actors -- no world government |
| Supranational | Decisions bind members even if an individual member disagrees (for example, much EU law) |
| Intergovernmental | States cooperate but keep a veto or right to opt out (for example, ASEAN's consensus) |
| Soft law | Non-binding standards that still shape behaviour (for example, the Basel Accords) |
| Collective security | All members of a system act against any member that commits aggression (the UN model) |
| Collective defence | Allies defend each other against an outside attack (NATO's Article 5) |

## Sources of international law

| Source | Creates law or identifies it? | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Treaties | Creates (binds parties) | Montreal Protocol; NPT |
| International custom | Creates (practice plus sense of obligation) | Diplomatic immunity, long observed before it was written into a treaty |
| General principles of law | Creates | Good faith |
| Decisions of international, regional and national courts | Identifies | ICJ judgments |
| Scholarly writings | Identifies | Leading legal commentaries |

## National and international law -- the three comparison points

- **Scope:** national law covers everyone in the territory; international law mainly covers states and IGOs, with some rules reaching individuals.
- **Nature:** national law has a legislature, compulsory courts and police; international law rests on consent, with no central enforcer.
- **Dynamic:** national law changes by legislation; international law changes through negotiation, state practice and court decisions.

## The UN in one box

```
Charter (1945)     Art. 2(4) no threat or use of force; Art. 51 self-defence
General Assembly   all members, one vote each; mostly recommendations;
                   approves the budget
Security Council   15 members: 5 permanent (China, France, Russia, UK, USA)
                   + 10 elected for two-year terms
                   substantive decision = 9 votes AND no permanent-member veto
                   Chapter VII decisions bind all members
Other organs       ECOSOC, ICJ, Secretariat, Trusteeship Council (inactive)
```

### Worked reminder: does the resolution pass?

- **11 in favour, 1 against (a permanent member), 3 abstentions.** Fails. Nine votes are reached, but the "no" is a veto.
- **9 in favour, 0 against, 6 abstentions including two permanent members.** Passes. Abstention is not a veto, and nine votes are reached.
- **8 in favour, 0 against, 7 abstentions.** Fails. Fewer than nine votes in favour.

## Evaluating the UN -- method in steps

1. Name the body (UNDP, UNHRC, ICJ, Security Council, General Assembly).
2. State its function in one line.
3. Give one achievement with a specific case.
4. Give one limitation with a specific case.
5. Explain the limitation using a key concept -- usually **sovereignty** (consent, veto, non-binding findings) or **power** (permanent members).
6. Judge: is the limitation built into the design, or fixable?

| Body | Achievement to cite | Limitation to cite |
|---|---|---|
| Security Council | Resolution 1973 (2011) on Libya passed with China and Russia abstaining | Russian veto on Ukraine (2022) |
| General Assembly | Emergency special session on Ukraine (2022) showed wide agreement | Resolution not binding |
| UNDP | Human Development Index widened how development is measured | Voluntary funding; needs host consent |
| UNHRC | Universal Periodic Review covers every member state | Members with poor records; findings not binding |
| ICJ | The Gambia v. Myanmar (2019) under the Genocide Convention | Jurisdiction needs consent; advisory opinions not binding |

## IGOs and non-state actors -- quick map

- **Regional:** EU (supranational), AU (can intervene over genocide, war crimes and crimes against humanity; suspends members after coups), ASEAN (consensus, non-interference), Mercosur (trade bloc; Venezuela suspended 2016).
- **Specific scope:** IEA (energy security, emergency stocks), OPEC (producers' oil policy; OPEC+ with Russia since 2016), Commonwealth of Learning (distance learning), Digital Nations (digital government).
- **Non-state:** NGOs (campaigning, monitoring -- the landmines campaign and the 1997 Ottawa Treaty), companies (standards, investment), city networks (C40), expert bodies (Basel Committee).

## Forms of cooperation -- which one is it?

| Ask yourself | If yes, it is... | Example from the guide |
|---|---|---|
| Is there a binding written agreement? | Treaty | NPT, Montreal Protocol |
| Do members promise to respond together to a threat? | Collective security (or defence) | NATO, OAS |
| Is it a partnership for shared strategic aims, often less formal? | Strategic alliance | USA and Israel; China and partners in Africa and Latin America |
| Is it about trade, integration or regulating production? | Economic cooperation | WTO agreements, trade blocs |
| Is there coordination without a formal agreement? | Informal cooperation | Extraordinary rendition; technology harmonisation; cultural exchange |

## Must-know distinctions

- **ICJ vs International Criminal Court.** The ICJ hears disputes between states; the ICC tries individuals.
- **GA resolution vs SC resolution.** A GA resolution recommends; an SC resolution under Chapter VII binds.
- **Cooperation vs harmony.** Cooperation means adjusting behaviour despite different interests. If interests already match, no cooperation is needed.
- **Interdependence vs dependence.** Interdependence is mutual; dependence is one-way. Most real cases sit between the two.
- **Sensitivity vs vulnerability.** Being hit by a shock is sensitivity; being unable to adjust cheaply is vulnerability.

## Linking to the other key concepts

- **Power:** asymmetric interdependence gives leverage (sanctions, energy supplies, market access).
- **Sovereignty:** joining treaties and IGOs pools or limits freedom of action, but states consent to it.
- **Legitimacy:** global governance bodies are judged on who is represented and whether they deliver.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the three dimensions of interdependence the guide mentions.
2. Distinguish government from governance in one sentence each.
3. List the five sources of international law the guide gives.
4. How many votes does a substantive Security Council resolution need?
5. A resolution gets 10 votes in favour, 1 against from France and 4 abstentions. Does it pass?
6. Which UN body carries out the Universal Periodic Review?
7. Why are the Basel Accords described as governance without government?
8. Give one limitation of the ICJ.
9. State the difference between collective security and collective defence.
10. Which treaty on ozone-depleting substances has been ratified by every UN member state?
11. Name one form of informal cooperation from the guide.
12. What makes the EU supranational rather than intergovernmental?

### Answers

1. Economic, security and sustainability.
2. Government is formal institutions with authority to make and enforce binding rules; governance is steering behaviour through rules, norms and networks, with or without a central authority.
3. Treaties; international custom; general principles of law; decisions of international, national and regional courts; scholarly writings.
4. Nine of fifteen, with no veto from a permanent member.
5. No. France is a permanent member, so its vote against is a veto.
6. The UN Human Rights Council.
7. They are standards agreed by regulators, not a treaty; each state applies them through its own rules.
8. It needs the states' consent to hear a dispute (or: its advisory opinions are not binding).
9. Collective security means all members act against any member that commits aggression; collective defence means allies defend each other against an outside attack.
10. The Montreal Protocol (1987).
11. Extraordinary rendition, technology harmonisation or cultural exchange.
12. EU law takes precedence over national law in its areas of competence, and many decisions bind members outvoted by qualified majority.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing an organisation's structure when the question asks how it manages interdependence.
- Writing "the UN decided" without saying which organ, or whether the decision binds.
- Treating a veto and an abstention as the same thing.
- Calling interdependence equal; the strongest answers show who depends on whom more.
- Mixing up the ICJ and the ICC.
- Quoting a source's view without comparing it point by point with the other source in a comparison question.
- Giving one-sided evaluations; Paper 1's longest question rewards exploring and evaluating different perspectives.
- Naming a treaty without saying what it requires and whether states comply.
