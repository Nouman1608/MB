## Key equations

| Quantity | Equation | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Kelvin and Celsius | T = θ + 273 | T in K, θ in °C. A change of 1 K equals a change of 1 °C |
| Specific heat capacity | c = ΔE / (mΔθ) | m in kg, ΔE in J, c in J/(kg °C) |
| Energy for a temperature change | ΔE = mcΔθ | Rearranged from the line above |
| Gas at constant temperature | p1V1 = p2V2 | Fixed mass of gas; same units on both sides |
| Electrical energy (for experiments) | E = P × t | t in seconds |

Fixed values to recall: absolute zero is −273 °C, which is 0 K. Under standard atmospheric pressure, water melts at 0 °C and its boiling temperature is 100 °C.

## 2.1 Particle model

### States at a glance

| | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
|---|---|---|---|
| Particle spacing | Close together | Close together | Far apart |
| Arrangement | Regular pattern | Irregular | Random |
| Movement | Vibrate in place | Slide past each other | Fast and random, with straight paths between collisions |
| Forces between particles | Strong | Weaker | Negligible |
| Shape and volume | Both fixed | Volume fixed, shape not | Neither fixed |
| Compressible? | No | No | Yes |

Names for changes: melting (solid to liquid), solidification (liquid to solid), boiling and evaporation (liquid to gas), condensation (gas to liquid).

### Temperature and gas pressure

- Higher temperature means higher average kinetic energy of the particles.
- Absolute zero is the lowest possible temperature. The particles have their least kinetic energy.
- Gas pressure is the force per unit area from particles colliding with a surface.

### Link the three variables

| Fixed | Change | Particle explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Volume | Temperature up, pressure up | Faster particles hit the walls more often and harder |
| Pressure | Temperature up, volume up | Volume must grow so that, although the particles are faster, each wall is hit less often and the pressure stays the same |
| Temperature | Volume down, pressure up | Shorter distances between collisions, so more hits per second on each unit area |

### Method: using p1V1 = p2V2

1. Check the temperature and the mass of gas are constant.
2. Put both volumes in the same unit, and both pressures in the same unit.
3. Write p1V1 = p2V2 and rearrange for the unknown.
4. Check direction: smaller volume means higher pressure.

The graph of p against V at constant temperature is a falling curve that never meets the axes. Plotting p against 1/V gives a straight line through the origin.

## 2.2.1 Expansion

- Particles vibrate or move more strongly when heated, so the average spacing increases. The particles themselves stay the same size.
- Order of expansion for the same temperature rise: gases greatest, then liquids, then solids.
- Applications and consequences: liquid-in-glass thermometer (liquid expands up a narrow tube), expansion gaps in rails and bridges, slack overhead cables.

## 2.2.2 Specific heat capacity

- Higher temperature means higher internal energy and higher average kinetic energy of all the particles.
- **Definition:** specific heat capacity is the energy required per unit mass per unit temperature increase.
- A high value of c means much energy must be supplied to warm the substance by 1 °C, and much is released as it cools.

### Method: measuring c in the laboratory

1. Measure the mass of the solid block or the liquid in an insulated container.
2. Fit a heater and thermometer. For a block, use a drop of oil in the thermometer hole. Stir a liquid.
3. Record start temperature, power and time.
4. Record the highest temperature reached.
5. Calculate E = P × t, then c = E / (mΔθ).
6. Expect the result to be a little high, since some energy escapes to the surroundings.

## 2.2.3 Changes of state

- Melting, solidification, boiling and condensation: energy is transferred but the temperature stays constant.
- Latent heat is the energy transferred for a substance to change state. It overcomes the forces between particles, increasing their potential energy rather than their kinetic energy.
- Boiling needs more latent heat than melting because the particles have to be separated completely.

### Boiling versus evaporation

| | Boiling | Evaporation |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | One fixed temperature | Any temperature |
| Location | Throughout the liquid | At the surface only |
| Bubbles | Yes | No |
| Rate | Fast | Slow |

### Evaporation facts

- Only the fastest particles at the surface leave, so the remaining liquid has a lower average kinetic energy. This is why evaporation causes cooling.
- Faster evaporation: higher temperature, larger surface area, moving air over the surface.

## 2.3 Transfer of thermal energy

| | Conduction | Convection | Radiation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Where | Solids (best), also liquids and gases poorly | Liquids and gases | Any, including a vacuum |
| Mechanism | Lattice vibrations; free electrons in metals | Density differences make warm fluid rise | Infrared electromagnetic waves |
| Needs a medium? | Yes | Yes | No |

### Radiation by surface

| Surface | Emission | Absorption | Reflection |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dull black | Best | Best | Worst |
| Shiny silver or white | Worst | Worst | Best |

A hotter object and a larger surface area both increase the rate of emission.

### Experiments to remember

| Aim | Set-up | What you see |
|---|---|---|
| Good and bad conductors | Rods of equal size heated at one end, pin stuck on with wax at the other | The pin falls first from the best conductor |
| Convection | Crystal of potassium permanganate in water, heated at one side | Coloured streaks rise on the heated side and sink on the other |
| Good and bad emitters | Identical containers of hot water with black and shiny surfaces, detector at equal distance | Higher reading from the black surface |
| Good and bad absorbers | Black and shiny plates the same distance from a heater, thermometer or wax behind each | Black plate warms faster |

### Applications

- **Pans:** metal base conducts, plastic handle insulates.
- **Room heater:** warm air rises, cool air sinks, a convection current warms the room.
- **Infrared thermometer:** detects infrared emitted by the object, no contact needed.
- **Insulation:** air trapped in small pockets cuts conduction and convection; shiny surfaces reduce radiation; a lid reduces evaporation and convection from a hot drink.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Heat and temperature:** energy transferred versus average kinetic energy of the particles.
- **Boiling and evaporation:** see the table above.
- **Conduction and convection:** conduction passes energy between neighbouring particles that stay in place; convection moves the heated fluid itself.
- **Absorbing and emitting:** black is good at both, shiny is poor at both.

## Quick self-test

1. Convert 25 °C to kelvin.
2. Convert 250 K to degrees Celsius.
3. A fixed mass of gas at 90 kPa has a volume of 50 cm³. It is compressed to 30 cm³ at constant temperature. Find the new pressure.
4. A fixed mass of gas at 120 kPa has a volume of 25 cm³. At constant temperature its pressure is raised to 200 kPa. Find the new volume.
5. A heater raises the temperature of 2.0 kg of water by 10 °C in 5.0 minutes. Take c for water as 4200 J/(kg °C). Find the power, assuming no losses.
6. A 0.40 kg copper block (c = 390 J/(kg °C)) absorbs 7800 J. Find its temperature rise.
7. Give the particle explanation of why a gas in a rigid sealed tin has higher pressure when warm.
8. State one reason why liquids that evaporate cool.
9. Name the process by which thermal energy crosses a vacuum.
10. Why is the water at the top of a hot-water tank warmer than the water at the bottom?

### Answers

1. 25 + 273 = **298 K**
2. 250 − 273 = **−23 °C**
3. p2 = 90 × 50 / 30 = **150 kPa**
4. V2 = 120 × 25 / 200 = **15 cm³** (smaller, as expected for a higher pressure)
5. E = 2.0 × 4200 × 10 = 84 000 J; P = 84 000 / 300 = **280 W**
6. Δθ = 7800 / (0.40 × 390) = **50 °C**
7. The particles move faster, hit the walls more often and with greater force, so the force per unit area increases.
8. The fastest surface particles leave, so those remaining have a lower average kinetic energy.
9. **Radiation** (infrared).
10. Heated water expands and is less dense, so it rises by convection and collects at the top.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying the particles expand or get bigger when a substance is heated.
- Leaving time in minutes or mass in grams in calculations.
- Giving only one effect of faster particles on pressure, not both frequency and force of collisions.
- Mixing up boiling and evaporation: bubbles throughout the liquid mean boiling, a slow change at the surface means evaporation.
- Writing that heat or cold rises, rather than that warm fluid is less dense and rises.
- Stating that conduction needs a vacuum, or that radiation needs a medium.
- Describing a comparison experiment without keeping it fair: equal rod size, equal distance and equal starting temperature.
- Saying the temperature rises during melting or boiling. Energy is transferred, but the temperature stays constant until the change is complete.
