## The assessment in one table

| Component | What the syllabus says | Marks and weighting |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Written Exam | Four compulsory questions on sources about a global issue; 1 hour 25 minutes | 70 marks, 35% |
| 2 Individual Report | Your own global research question; 1500-2000 words; local and/or national and global perspectives | 60 marks, 30% |
| 3 Team Project | Teams of two to five; a local issue; Explanation of Research and Planning (300-400 words), Evidence of Action, and a Reflective Paper (750-1000 words) each | 70 marks, 35% |

Component 1 question types (from section 4): Question 1 analyses information, arguments and perspectives in several sources (18 marks); Question 2 evaluates research or evidence and suggests how to test a claim (16 marks); Question 3 evaluates arguments and makes a reasoned judgement (16 marks); Question 4 assesses actions using all sources (20 marks).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Issue (syllabus) | An important subject or problem for discussion |
| Perspective (syllabus) | A viewpoint on an issue based on evidence and reasoning |
| Evidence (syllabus) | Information that helps develop understanding or prove something true or false |
| Argument (syllabus) | Statements with reasons and evidence that support a claim |
| Invention | A new idea, device or process |
| Innovation | Putting a new idea into use so it changes how things are done |
| Patent | A legal right to stop others using an invention for a set period |
| Intellectual property | Legal rights over creations of the mind, such as patents |
| Industrialisation | A shift from farming towards manufacturing |
| R&D | Research and development: spending on new products or processes |
| Technology transfer | Sharing technology or know-how between firms or countries |
| Subsidy | Government money to lower costs or encourage an activity |
| Planned obsolescence | A claim that products are designed to fail or date early |

**Source-analysis terms listed in the syllabus:** bias, claim, fact, generalisation, opinion, prediction, value, vested interest.

## Real examples at a glance

You will not be marked on knowing these. Use them to practise evaluation, and check any you cite in the Individual Report.

| Example | What happened | Perspectives | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| WTO COVID-19 vaccine decision | India and South Africa proposed a waiver in October 2020; in June 2022 the WTO agreed a narrower decision for vaccines, for five years | Waiver supporters vs many drug firms and some high-income governments | Each side's claim about what limited supply (patents or factories) is hard to test |
| US CHIPS and Science Act | Signed 9 August 2022; $52.7 billion for chip research, manufacturing and workforce | Supporters: jobs and security. Critics: subsidies to profitable firms | Benefits are predictions, not yet facts |
| Rwanda drone deliveries | Launched 14 October 2016 with Zipline, delivering blood to clinics | Government and partners vs those asking about cost and transfer to other places | Early reports came from partners with a vested interest |
| EU right-to-repair directive | Adopted 13 June 2024; member states apply it by 31 July 2026 | Consumer and environment groups vs some manufacturers | Too new for strong evidence of effects |
| Repair Café | First held in Amsterdam, 18 October 2009, organised by Martine Postma | Local volunteers and residents | Good model for a measurable Team Project action |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Invention vs innovation:** an invention is the new idea; innovation is putting it into use.
- **Fact vs prediction:** "The plant employs 900 people" can be checked; "the plant will create 1,200 jobs" cannot yet.
- **Claim vs evidence:** a claim states something is true; evidence is what backs it.
- **Vested interest vs bias:** a vested interest is a reason someone might gain; bias is a one-sided presentation. A source can have one without the other.
- **Correlation vs cause:** wages rising after a factory opened does not prove the factory caused the rise.
- **Global vs local issue:** the Individual Report needs a global issue; the Team Project needs a local one.
- **Course of action vs consequence:** an action is what someone does; a consequence is what results.
- **Perspective vs opinion:** in this syllabus a perspective rests on evidence and reasoning; an opinion may not.

## Language to watch in sources

Words such as "revolutionary", "game-changing", "always" and "never" are persuasive or sweeping. Ask what evidence sits behind them, who wrote them, and who gains if you believe them. A company's press release and a campaign group's blog can both use loaded language.

## Mini essay plan: assessing actions (Component 1 Question 4 style)

*Question type: "Which action would be most effective in responding to a planned high-technology factory near a town?"*

1. **Name the criteria.** Effectiveness (does it tackle a cause?), feasibility, who gains and who loses, sustainability.
2. **Action 1: approve with conditions.** Use source details (jobs, water use). Strength: keeps jobs and limits harm. Weakness: needs enforcement.
3. **Action 2: reject the factory.** Strength: protects river and nature. Weakness: loses jobs; demand for the product does not disappear.
4. **Action 3: delay for a full independent assessment.** Strength: better evidence. Weakness: the firm may go elsewhere.
5. **Judge the evidence behind each.** Who wrote the source? Any vested interest? Is the data a prediction?
6. **Reach a judgement.** Choose one action, say why it beats the others, and state a condition that would change your mind.

## Mini plan: Individual Report on this topic

- **Question:** for example, "Should manufacturers be required to make products repairable?"
- **Global perspective:** international bodies, global firms, environmental groups.
- **National perspective:** a government or national consumer group.
- **Local perspective:** a repair shop owner, residents, your school.
- **Causes and consequences:** product design, costs, waste, jobs.
- **Courses of action:** voluntary schemes, laws, community repair. Choose one and explain how it would be implemented.
- **Source evaluation:** who funded each source; fact or prediction.
- **Reflection:** how your view changed, and why.

### Answers

1. It is not assessed; topics are contexts for practising skills.
2. A viewpoint on an issue based on evidence and reasoning.
3. Invention is the new idea; innovation is putting it into use.
4. A prediction: it cannot be checked yet.
5. Any two of: bias, claim, fact, generalisation, opinion, prediction, value, vested interest.

7. Vested interest.

10. 300-400 words.
11. Any one: tackles a cause, feasibility, who gains and loses, sustainability, evidence it has worked.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Explaining how a technology works instead of analysing the issue in the sources.
- Copying a company's forecast as if it were fact.
- Calling a source "biased" without naming who gains or which words are loaded.
- Treating new technology as simply good or simply bad, without saying who gains and who loses.
- Saying a factory "caused" a change when the source only shows two things happened together.
- Listing perspectives without explaining why each group holds its view.
- In Question 4 style answers, describing actions but not reaching a judgement.
- In the Individual Report, choosing an issue that does not divide opinion.
- In the Team Project, an action whose success cannot be measured.
