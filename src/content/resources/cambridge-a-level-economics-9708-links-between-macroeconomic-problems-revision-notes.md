## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Internal value of money | Purchasing power at home; falls as the price level rises |
| External value of money | Purchasing power abroad, shown by the exchange rate |
| Price competitiveness | How cheap a country's goods are compared with rivals', allowing for exchange rates |
| Marginal propensity to import | Share of extra income spent on imports |
| Stagflation | High inflation and high unemployment (or low growth) together |
| Traditional Phillips curve | Inverse relationship between inflation (originally wage growth) and unemployment |
| Short-run Phillips curve | The trade-off for a given expected inflation rate |
| Long-run Phillips curve | Vertical at the natural rate of unemployment |
| Natural rate of unemployment | Unemployment when actual and expected inflation are equal |
| Credibility | How far people believe a policymaker's commitment |

## Two-way chains

```
Inflation > partners  -> exports dearer, imports cheaper -> currency falls
Currency falls        -> import prices up                -> inflation up
Inflation > partners  -> competitiveness down            -> current account worse
Deficit               -> currency weaker -> import prices -> cost-push inflation
Large surplus         -> AD up near capacity              -> demand-pull inflation
AD-led growth near capacity -> demand-pull inflation
LRAS-led growth             -> lower inflationary pressure
High, volatile inflation    -> less investment            -> lower growth
Growth -> more imports (mpm x rise in income) -> current account worse
Export-led growth           -> current account better
```

## Quick calculations

- Internal value: price index 100 → 120 means money buys 100 ÷ 120 = 0.833, a fall of 16.7%.
- Competitiveness: home inflation 6%, partners 2%, depreciation 3%: 1.06 × 0.97 ÷ 1.02 − 1 = +0.8%, so home goods are still 0.8% dearer.
- Imports: income +30, mpm 0.2 → imports +6.

## Phillips curves

| Version | Claim | Policy meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Traditional (A. W. Phillips, 1958, UK data) | Stable inverse relation between wage inflation and unemployment | Choose a point on the curve |
| Expectations-augmented (Friedman and Phelps, late 1960s) | Trade-off only in the short run; SRPC shifts with expected inflation | No long-run trade-off; LRPC vertical at the natural rate |

**Moves on the diagram:**

- Expansionary policy: move up the SRPC (lower unemployment, higher inflation); expectations rise; SRPC shifts up; unemployment returns to the natural rate at higher inflation.
- Disinflation: move down the SRPC (higher unemployment, lower inflation); expectations fall; SRPC shifts down; unemployment returns to the natural rate at lower inflation.
- Supply shock: SRPC shifts up, giving stagflation.
- Supply-side policy that lowers the natural rate: LRPC shifts left.

**Disinflation example:** inflation = expected − 0.8 × (unemployment − 6). Unemployment held at 8.5% cuts inflation by 2 points a year: 8 → 6 → 4 → 2. Credible policy that lowers expectations directly reduces the unemployment cost.

## What the links depend on

- Spare capacity (growth and inflation).
- Price elasticities of demand for exports and imports (inflation and the current account).
- The exchange-rate system (whether the currency can fall).
- The marginal propensity to import (growth and the current account).
- How quickly expectations adjust, and credibility (Phillips curve).

## Exam traps

- Write chains in both directions where the syllabus asks for a relationship.
- Compare a depreciation with the inflation gap before claiming it restores competitiveness.
- Growth does not always worsen the current account.
- The LRPC sits at the natural rate, not at zero unemployment.
- The Phillips curve is an association, not proof that one causes the other.
- Apply the curve to the scenario; do not just name it.

## Self-test

1. Define the internal value of money.
2. The price index rises from 100 to 125. By how much does the internal value of money fall?
3. Explain how home inflation above partners' can cause a depreciation.
4. Explain how a depreciation can raise inflation.
5. Home inflation is 5%, partners' is 1% and the currency depreciates by 4%. Has competitiveness improved?
6. Explain how a large current account surplus can cause inflation.
7. Income rises by 50 and the marginal propensity to import is 0.3. By how much do imports rise?
8. Give one case in which growth improves the current account.
9. What did the traditional Phillips curve show?
10. Why is the long-run Phillips curve vertical?
11. What shifts the short-run Phillips curve upward?
12. Why does credibility reduce the cost of disinflation?

**Answers:** 1. What money buys at home; its purchasing power over domestic goods. 2. To 100 ÷ 125 = 0.8, a fall of 20%. 3. Exports become dearer and imports cheaper, so demand for the currency falls and supply rises, pushing its value down. 4. Import prices rise in home currency, raising firms' costs and the price index. 5. 1.05 × 0.96 ÷ 1.01 − 1 = −0.2%, so home goods are about 0.2% cheaper than before: competitiveness has improved slightly. 6. Net exports raise AD; near full capacity, extra demand pulls up prices. 7. 0.3 × 50 = 15. 8. Export-led growth (or productivity-led growth that improves competitiveness). 9. An inverse relationship between the rate of change of money wages and unemployment. 10. In the long run actual and expected inflation are equal and unemployment returns to the natural rate, whatever the inflation rate. 11. A rise in expected inflation or an adverse supply shock. 12. If people believe inflation will fall, expected inflation falls directly, so less unemployment is needed to bring actual inflation down.
