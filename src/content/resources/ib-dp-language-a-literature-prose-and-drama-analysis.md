This study guide teaches you how to read and write about **prose fiction and drama** for IB
Diploma Programme Language A: Literature, at SL and HL. It follows the current IB subject brief
(first assessments for SL and HL 2021). It maps to the areas of exploration **Time and space** and
**Intertextuality: connecting texts**, and it covers narrative perspective, characterisation, time,
setting and structure in prose, then dramatic conventions, staging and dialogue in drama.

### Where this is assessed

The brief lists four assessed components. **Paper 1** is a guided analysis of unseen literary
passages "from different text types" (1.25 hours at SL, 2.25 hours at HL; 35% at both levels), so
you need a method that works on a prose extract or a scene from a play. **Paper 2** is a comparative
essay on two literary works, answering one of four questions (1.75 hours; 35% SL,
25% HL). The **HL essay** is a 1,200–1,500-word essay on one work studied (20%, HL only). The
**individual oral** pairs a work originally written in the language studied with a work studied in
translation, around a common global issue (30% SL, 20% HL). Your teacher will share the assessment
criteria for each. For timing and exam technique, use the
[exam preparation guide](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-literature-exam-preparation/).

## Prose fiction

### Narrative perspective

Start every prose analysis with two questions: **who is telling** (the narrator) and **whose eyes
are we seeing through** (the focaliser). They are not always the same person.

- **First-person narration** gives one character's voice and limits you to what they know or admit.
- **Third-person limited** narration stays close to one character's thoughts.
- **Third-person omniscient** narration can move between minds and comment on events.
- **Free indirect style** blends the narrator's voice with a character's thoughts without "she
  thought". Jane Austen uses it throughout *Emma* (1815).
- An **unreliable narrator** gives an account the reader learns to question.

Retrospective first-person narration adds a gap between the older narrator and the younger self.
Charles Dickens opens *Great Expectations* (1861) like this:

> My father's family name being Pirrip, and my Christian name Philip, my infant tongue could make of
> both names nothing longer or more explicit than Pip.

In "my infant tongue" the adult speaks about the child. The formal sentence belongs to a grown man;
the name it arrives at, "Pip", belongs to a small boy. From the first line you read two versions of
one person, which lets the adult judge his younger self.

### Characterisation

Writers build characters in two ways. **Direct characterisation** tells you ("She was proud").
**Indirect characterisation** shows you, through speech, action, appearance and how others react.
Indirect methods make you infer, so they give you more to analyse.

E. M. Forster's *Aspects of the Novel* (1927) introduced the terms **flat** (built round a single
idea, does not change) and **round** (capable of surprising the reader). "This character is round"
is description. "Her late change makes us re-read her earlier coldness as fear" is analysis.

### Time

Every narrative has two timelines: the **story** (events in the order they happened) and the
**telling** (the order and speed in which the text presents them).

- **Analepsis** (flashback) moves back in time.
- **Prolepsis** (flash-forward) moves ahead, often creating suspense or a sense of fate.
- **Pace**: a **scene** gives events at roughly the speed they happen; a **summary** covers a long
  period in a sentence; an **ellipsis** skips time completely.

Always ask why the writer tells events in this order.

### Setting

Setting covers place, time of day, season, weather and social world. It can mirror a character's
state, contrast with it, or act almost as a character itself. Thomas Hardy gives the whole first
chapter of *The Return of the Native* (1878) to Egdon Heath before any person appears, so the
landscape is established as a lasting force against which the characters' plans look small.
**Pathetic fallacy** (weather reflecting human feeling) is one tool; explain what the link adds.

Keep two ideas apart. **Time and setting inside the text** are craft choices. The **Time and
space** area of exploration is about the contexts in which a work was written and is read. Both
matter, but they answer different questions.

### Structure

Look at openings, endings, chapter breaks and framing. A **frame narrative** places one story inside
another. Mary Shelley's *Frankenstein* (1818) nests Victor's story, and the creature's story inside
it, within Walton's letters, so each account reaches you through a listener who may not be neutral.
Emily Brontë's *Wuthering Heights* (1847) works in a similar way through Lockwood and Nelly Dean.
Endings can be **closed** (resolved) or **open** (left uncertain).

### Worked example: prose

> *Original text written for Marlbridge*
>
> The bus was late again, and Leo counted the puddles. Eleven. His mother would say he should have
> walked. His mother would say a great many things, later, when she saw the letter in his bag. For
> now there were only the puddles, and the lit windows of the bakery opposite, and the long, cold
> minute before the bus turned the corner.

**Step 1 -- perspective.** Third-person limited, focalised through Leo. "His mother would say he
should have walked" is free indirect style: Leo's own anticipation of her voice, not the narrator's
judgement.

**Step 2 -- time.** "Later, when she saw the letter in his bag" is a prolepsis. It tells you
trouble is coming but not what the letter says, so a dull wait becomes tense.

**Step 3 -- setting.** Cold puddles against "the lit windows of the bakery opposite" set Leo outside
warmth he cannot reach, a quiet image of his isolation.

**Step 4 -- pace and structure.** "Eleven." is a one-word sentence: time moves slowly enough to count
puddles. "The long, cold minute" stretches sixty seconds, and the list joined by "and ... and"
slows the reading to match his dread.

**Step 5 -- thesis.** The writer turns an ordinary wait into suspense by letting a flash-forward
hang over a slowed-down present, so the reader shares Leo's dread before learning its cause.

## Drama

### Reading a play as a script

A play is written to be performed, so imagine it on stage: who is visible, and what the
audience can see that a character cannot. Stage directions are part of the
text, and so are silences. Susan Glaspell's *Trifles* (1916) builds its meaning through props: two
women notice domestic details, such as a broken birdcage, that the men searching the house dismiss,
and the audience watches the truth assembled from "trifles".

### Dramatic conventions

- **Soliloquy**: a character alone on stage speaks their thoughts. The audience gets private access
  no other character has, as in Hamlet's "To be, or not to be" speech.
- **Aside**: a brief remark to the audience or to one character, unheard by the others.
- **Dramatic irony**: the audience knows what a character does not.
- **Act and scene structure**: breaks control time jumps, location changes and where tension peaks.
- **Off-stage action**: events reported rather than shown. What a playwright keeps off stage can
  matter as much as what is shown.

### Staging

Set, props, costume, lighting, sound and exits all carry meaning. At the end of Henrik
Ibsen's *A Doll's House* (1879), Nora leaves and the audience hears the door slam behind her: an
off-stage sound closes the play. Anton Chekhov's *The Cherry Orchard* (1904) ends with the distant sound of
an axe striking the trees of the orchard. In both, the audience is left with a sound rather than a speech.

### Dialogue

Dialogue moves the plot and reveals character. Look at **who
speaks most**, **who interrupts**, **who changes the subject**, and **what is not said** (subtext).
Short alternating lines raise tension; a long speech can show power or loss of control. Oscar
Wilde's *The Importance of Being Earnest* (1895) gives Lady Bracknell this line:

> To lose one parent, Mr. Worthing, may be regarded as a misfortune; to lose both looks like
> carelessness.

The balanced structure (one parent / both; misfortune / carelessness) sounds like reasoned
judgement, but its logic is absurd. The gap between confident form and ridiculous content shows a
character who treats propriety as more real than grief.

### Worked example: drama

> *Original text written for Marlbridge*
>
> *A kitchen. Night. A single lamp on the table. MARGARET, sixties, sits with an unopened envelope.
> Her son RAY enters, car keys still in his hand.*
>
> RAY: You rang.
> MARGARET: I did.
> RAY: At midnight, Mum.
> MARGARET: *(sliding the envelope across the table)* It came this morning.
> *(RAY does not pick it up.)*

**Staging.** One lamp isolates the two figures. The unopened envelope draws every eye. The keys in
Ray's hand suggest he means to leave soon.

**Dialogue.** The lines are short and clipped. "You rang." / "I did." gives nothing away; the
tension lies in what neither says.

**Convention.** The audience does not know what is in the envelope either, so suspense is shared
with Ray.

**Action as meaning.** The final stage direction is a refusal. Ray does not pick it up, so a
non-action ends the scene, and the actor's stillness carries the moment.

**Thesis.** The playwright creates tension through what is withheld: an unopened letter, an
unexplained phone call and a son who will not touch either.

## Comparing prose and drama

Intertextuality is partly about how the same concern changes across forms. A novel can enter a
mind directly through narration; a play must find another route, such as a soliloquy, an aside, a
prop or a silence. In a Paper 2 comparison of a novel and a play, this difference in method is
often your strongest point: show what each reader or audience gains or loses. The [intertextuality study guide](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-literature-intertextuality/)
covers echo, response and subversion; the
[readers, writers and texts guide](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-literature-readers-writers-texts/)
covers how readers construct meaning.

## Writing about prose and drama

Use a four-part pattern in each analytical point:

1. **Claim** linked to the question.
2. **Evidence**: a short quotation embedded in your sentence, or a precise stage direction.
3. **Technique** named accurately.
4. **Effect**: what the choice does to the reader or audience, and why it matters for the whole text.

## Common errors

- Confusing the narrator with the author, or a soliloquy's speaker with the playwright.
- Calling any third-person narrator "omniscient" when it enters only one mind.
- Retelling the plot instead of analysing the order in which it is told.
- Treating stage directions as background instead of evidence.
- Writing "the audience feels tension" without saying what on stage creates it.
- Mixing up in-text setting with the context of production.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is free indirect style?**
Free indirect style blends the narrator's voice with a character's thoughts without "she thought". Jane Austen uses it throughout Emma.

**What is the difference between flat and round characters?**
E. M. Forster's terms describe a flat character as built round a single idea and not changing, and a round character as capable of surprising the reader. Saying what a change makes the reader re-read is analysis; simply labelling a character round is description.

**What is the difference between analepsis and prolepsis?**
Analepsis is a flashback that moves back in time; prolepsis is a flash-forward that moves ahead, often creating suspense or a sense of fate.

**What is a frame narrative?**
A frame narrative places one story inside another, as in Frankenstein, where Victor's story reaches the reader through Walton's letters. Each account comes through a listener who may not be neutral.
