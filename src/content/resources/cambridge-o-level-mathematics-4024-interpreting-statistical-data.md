This guide covers section 9.2, Interpreting statistical data, in the Statistics topic of the Cambridge O Level Mathematics (Syllabus D) 4024 syllabus for 2025, 2026 and 2027. Every candidate studies it, and it is tested inside almost every statistics question as the "explain", "compare" or "comment" part. The broader page [Statistics: Classifying Data, Averages, Spread and Statistical Charts](/resources/cambridge-o-level-mathematics-statistics-classifying-data-averages-spread-and/) gives one comparison and a list of restrictions; this guide shows how to build full answers. ## What section 9.2 asks for

| Outcome | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| 9.2 (1) | Read, interpret and draw inferences from tables and statistical diagrams |
| 9.2 (2) | Compare sets of data using tables, graphs and statistical measures, for example comparing averages and measures of spread between two data sets |
| 9.2 (3) | Appreciate restrictions on drawing conclusions from given data |

These outcomes are about words as much as numbers. A correct number with no conclusion, or a conclusion with no number, usually earns only part of the credit.

## Reading, interpreting and inferring

Three levels of answer are possible from any table or diagram.

- **Reading** gives a value that is shown: "12 students chose tennis."
- **Interpreting** says what the values mean together: "Tennis was the least popular sport."
- **Inferring** goes beyond the data to a reasoned suggestion: "The school might have more demand for a football club than a tennis club."

An inference should be worded cautiously ("suggests", "on average", "in this sample") because it is not certain.

### Worked example 1: counts or proportions?

Two schools record how students travel.

| | Walk | Other | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| School A | 60 | 90 | 150 |
| School B | 40 | 40 | 80 |

A newspaper says "Walking is more popular at School A." Is this justified?

More students walk at School A (60 compared with 40), but the schools are different sizes. Compare proportions:

```
School A: 60 ÷ 150 = 0.4 = 40%
School B: 40 ÷ 80  = 0.5 = 50%
```

A **greater proportion** of students walk at School B. The claim is true for the number of walkers but not for how popular walking is. When groups have different totals, compare fractions or percentages, not raw counts.

## Comparing two data sets

A full comparison has **two statements**, each with numbers and a conclusion in context:

1. **An average** (mean, median or mode): which group is generally higher or lower.
2. **A measure of spread** (range, or interquartile range from a cumulative frequency diagram): which group is more consistent.

Name the measure, give both values, and say what they show about the people or things in the question.

### Worked example 2

Eight students in each of two groups timed how long, in seconds, they took to solve a puzzle.

```
Group A: 14 16 17 17 18 19 20 23
Group B: 11 13 15 18 20 22 24 25
```

| | Mean | Median | Range |
|---|---|---|---|
| Group A | 18 | 17.5 | 9 |
| Group B | 18.5 | 19 | 14 |

**Model answer.**

"The median time for Group A (17.5 s) is lower than for Group B (19 s), so on average Group A solved the puzzle faster. The range for Group A (9 s) is smaller than for Group B (14 s), so Group A's times were more consistent."

Notice what earns the credit: each sentence names the measure, quotes both values and turns them into a statement about the students. "Group B has a bigger range" alone is a reading; "so Group A's times were more consistent" is the interpretation.

**Which average?** The means here are close (18 and 18.5), so a comparison of means would say the groups were similar. Questions often tell you which measure to use. If they do not, choose one and use it consistently, and avoid comparing the mean of one group with the median of another.

**Smaller is not always worse.** For times, a lower average is better performance. For test scores, a higher average is better. Read the context before saying which group "did better".

## Interpreting statistical diagrams

Before using any diagram, read its title, axis labels, scale and key.

- **Scales.** Check the value of one small square. A vertical axis going up in 5s, not 1s, is easy to misread.
- **Truncated axes.** If a bar chart's vertical axis starts at a value above zero, bar heights are not in proportion to frequencies.
- **Keys and symbols.** In a pictogram, check what one whole symbol represents.
- **Pie charts.** A pie chart shows proportions only. Two pie charts of the same size can represent very different totals.

### Worked example 3: a misleading bar chart

A bar chart shows sales of 42 thousand in Year 1 and 48 thousand in Year 2. Its vertical axis starts at 40 thousand. The second bar looks four times as tall as the first. Explain why the chart is misleading.

The visible heights are 42 − 40 = 2 and 48 − 40 = 8, giving a ratio of 4 : 1. The real increase is from 42 to 48, which is 6 ÷ 42 ≈ 14%. Because the axis does not start at zero, the chart exaggerates the growth. A fair answer quotes the real values and the reason.

## Restrictions on drawing conclusions

The syllabus asks you to appreciate why some conclusions cannot be drawn. The common reasons are:

| Restriction | Example |
|---|---|
| Small sample | 10 people asked cannot reliably represent a town |
| Unrepresentative sample | asking only people at a gym about exercise |
| Grouped data | exact values, so exact mean and range, are not known |
| One measure only | equal means can hide very different spreads |
| Correlation is not cause | two quantities rising together do not prove one causes the other |
| Outside the data | predicting far beyond the values collected |
| Different-sized groups | counts compared instead of proportions |

### Worked example 4

"A survey of 20 people at a sports centre found that 18 exercise every week. So 90% of people in the town exercise every week." Give two reasons why this conclusion may not be valid.

1. The sample of 20 is **small**, so the percentage may not reflect the whole town.
2. People at a sports centre are **more likely to exercise** than others, so the sample is not representative of the town.

Each reason is specific to this survey. Generic answers such as "the data might be wrong" do not show the restriction.

## Common mistakes

- Comparing counts when the groups have different totals.
- Writing comparisons with no numbers, or numbers with no conclusion.
- Comparing an average only, with no comment on spread.
- Saying "Group B is better" without saying better in what way.
- Reading a truncated axis as if it started at zero.
- Claiming certainty: "this proves" instead of "this suggests".

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Compare:** two sentences, one on an average and one on spread, each with values and context.
- **Explain** or **give a reason:** link the evidence to the conclusion with "because" or "so".
- **Comment on** a claim: say whether it is supported, and why, quoting figures.
- **What can you infer:** a cautious statement going beyond the figures, tied to them.
