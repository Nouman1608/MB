## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by an income statement. **[2]**

**2.** A business has revenue of £200,000 and cost of sales of £120,000. Calculate its gross profit and gross profit margin. **[3]**

**3.** Distinguish between operating profit and net profit. **[3]**

**4.** Which **one** of the following is deducted to arrive at profit before tax?

A: Dividends.

B: Corporation tax.

C: Interest paid.

D: Retained profit. **[1]**

**5.** Explain **one** reason why a business with a large profit may still have a cash-flow problem. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Torridge Foods plc has these figures for the year ended 30 June.

- Revenue £800,000.
- Opening inventory £40,000; purchases £420,000; closing inventory £50,000.
- Distribution costs £60,000; administrative expenses £140,000.
- Interest paid £15,000.
- Tax at 25% of profit before tax.
- Dividends £50,000.

**(a)** Calculate the cost of sales and the gross profit. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the operating profit and the profit before tax. **[3]**

**(c)** Calculate the tax charge, the profit for the year and the retained profit. **[3]**

**(d)** Calculate the gross, operating and net profit margins. **[3]**

**7.** Torridge's results for the previous year were: revenue £720,000; gross profit £335,000; operating profit £160,000; profit for the year £108,000.

**(a)** Calculate the three profit margins for the previous year. **[3]**

**(b)** Analyse the change in Torridge's profitability between the two years. **[6]**

**(c)** Evaluate how reliable these figures are as a guide to Torridge's financial health. **[6]**

**8.** At the year end, Torridge's closing inventory was valued at £50,000. A check shows that £10,000 of it is damaged and could not be sold. Explain how the correct valuation would affect the income statement. **[4]**

**9.** Evaluate the usefulness of the income statement to a bank considering a loan to a company, and to the company's shareholders. **[8]**

**10.** A company's gross profit margin rises from 40% to 44% but its net profit margin falls from 10% to 8%. Analyse possible reasons for this. **[5]**

## Answers

**1.** An income statement shows a business's revenue, costs and profit [1] over a period of time, usually a year [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Gross profit = £200,000 − £120,000 = **£80,000** [1] [1]. Gross margin = 80,000 ÷ 200,000 × 100 = **40%** [1]. **[3]**

**3.** Operating profit is the profit from normal trading, found after deducting operating expenses from gross profit, before interest and tax [1] [1]. Net profit (profit for the year) is the profit left after interest and tax have also been deducted [1]. **[3]**

**4.** **C** [1]. **[1]**

**5.** Profit includes sales made on credit that customers have not yet paid for [1], and does not show spending on assets or loan repayments, so cash may be short even when profit is high [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** Cost of sales = £40,000 + £420,000 − £50,000 [1] = **£410,000** [1]. Gross profit = £800,000 − £410,000 = **£390,000** [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Operating profit = £390,000 − £60,000 − £140,000 = **£190,000** [1] [1]. Profit before tax = £190,000 − £15,000 = **£175,000** [1]. **[3]**

**(c)** Tax = 25% × £175,000 = **£43,750** [1]. Profit for the year = £175,000 − £43,750 = **£131,250** [1]. Retained profit = £131,250 − £50,000 = **£81,250** [1]. **[3]**

**(d)** Gross margin = 390,000 ÷ 800,000 × 100 = **48.75%** [1]. Operating margin = 190,000 ÷ 800,000 × 100 = **23.75%** [1]. Net margin = 131,250 ÷ 800,000 × 100 = **16.4%** [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** Gross margin = 335,000 ÷ 720,000 × 100 = **46.5%** [1]. Operating margin = 160,000 ÷ 720,000 × 100 = **22.2%** [1]. Net margin = 108,000 ÷ 720,000 × 100 = **15.0%** [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Revenue rose by 11.1% (80,000 ÷ 720,000) [1]. Profit for the year rose by 21.5% (23,250 ÷ 108,000) [1], faster than revenue, so profitability improved [1]. The gross margin rose from 46.5% to 48.75% [1], which suggests cost of sales grew more slowly than revenue, perhaps because of a price rise or cheaper supplies [1]. The operating margin rose from 22.2% to 23.75% and the net margin from 15.0% to 16.4%, so running costs did not grow faster than sales [1]. **[6]**

**(c)** Reliable: margins have improved at all three levels, so the improvement is not due to one item [1]; the statement follows standard rules and is likely to be audited [1].
Not fully reliable: it covers only two years, so may not show a trend [1]; profit depends on judgements such as inventory valuation and depreciation [1]; it does not show cash, so the business could still have a cash problem [1]. Ratios should be compared with competitors [1].
Judgement: the figures suggest improved health, but they should be read with the statement of financial position and cash-flow information [1]. (Maximum 6 marks.) **[6]**

**8.** If £10,000 of inventory is damaged, closing inventory should be £40,000, not £50,000 [1]. A lower closing inventory increases cost of sales (opening inventory + purchases − closing inventory) by £10,000 [1], so gross profit falls by £10,000 [1] and profit before tax and profit for the year also fall (by £7,500 after 25% tax) [1]. This follows the prudence convention. **[4]**

**9.** Bank: the income statement shows whether profit is enough to cover interest, for example operating profit of £190,000 against interest of £15,000 [1]; it shows the trend, which suggests whether repayments are likely to be met [1]; it shows how much profit is retained to build the business [1].
Shareholders: it shows the profit available for dividends and growth [1]; margins help compare the company with others [1].
Limits: it is historic and does not show cash, assets or debts [1]; it depends on judgement and may not show one-off items [1]. A bank would also want the statement of financial position and cash-flow forecast [1].
Judgement: very useful for both, but not enough on its own [1]. (Maximum 8 marks.) **[8]**

**10.** The gross margin rose, so cost of sales fell as a share of revenue, perhaps due to price rises or cheaper supplies [1]. The net margin fell, so costs below gross profit rose faster than revenue [1]: possible causes are higher administration costs, more marketing, higher distribution costs, or higher interest costs from borrowing [1] [1]. A one-off item such as a restructuring cost may also explain it [1]. **[5]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving out opening and closing inventory in cost of sales.
- Deducting tax from operating profit.
- Treating dividends as a cost before profit for the year.
- Mixing up gross, operating and net margins.
- Analysing without a comparison or a reason.
