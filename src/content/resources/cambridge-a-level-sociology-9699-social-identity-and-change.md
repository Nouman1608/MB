Section 1.3, *Social identity and change*, closes the socialisation half of Paper 1 in Cambridge International AS & A Level Sociology (9699). It moves from how people learn their culture (1.1) and why they conform (1.2) to who they think they are, and whether that is changing. The syllabus links it to the key concepts *Socialisation, culture and identity*, *Structure and human agency* and *Social change and development*.

For quick recall use the [social identity and change revision notes](/resources/a-level-cambridge-sociology-social-identity-and-change-revision-notes/), and test yourself with the [social identity and change practice questions](/resources/a-level-cambridge-sociology-social-identity-and-change-practice/). This guide adds studies, explanations and an essay plan that those pages do not include, so read it first.

## What the syllabus lists

- Social class, gender, ethnicity and age as elements in the construction of social identity.
- How social class, gender, ethnicity and age identities may be changing due to globalisation, increased choice and the creation of new/hybrid identities.

The first bullet is about *how identity is built*; the second is about *change*. Essays on 1.3 almost always need both: you cannot judge whether an identity is changing until you have explained what it was built from.

## Thinking about identity

- **Personal identity** is a sense of being a unique individual; **social identity** comes from belonging to groups and from how others place you.
- Identities are **ascribed** (given) or **achieved** (gained). Class of origin, sex category, ethnicity and age are largely ascribed, but sociologists argue that what they *mean* is learned and can be changed.
- Identity has two sides: how you see yourself, and how others see and treat you. A person may reject a label, but if others act on it, it still shapes their life.
- One identity can become a **master status** that overrides the others in how a person is treated, as when a criminal record dominates every other fact about someone.

## How class constructs identity

**Traditional class identities.** David Lockwood (1966) described "proletarian traditionalists", workers in close-knit industrial communities such as mining towns, with a strong sense of "us and them". John Goldthorpe and David Lockwood's *Affluent Worker* study of car workers in Luton (published 1968–69) found a newer "privatised instrumental" outlook: home-centred workers who saw their job mainly as a source of income rather than of class solidarity. This is early evidence that class identity was changing.

**Class as culture.** Pierre Bourdieu's *Distinction* (1979) argued that taste in food, music, art and sport marks class position. Middle-class children inherit cultural capital that schools reward, so class identity is reproduced through everyday preferences, often without people describing themselves in class terms.

**Disidentification.** Beverley Skeggs' *Formations of Class and Gender* (1997), a study of working-class women in north-west England, found that they actively distanced themselves from being seen as working class, because of the negative judgements attached to it, while class still shaped their opportunities. This is a powerful point for evaluation: weaker class *self-description* does not mean class has stopped mattering.

## How gender constructs identity

- Gender identity is learned through socialisation and reinforced by schools, media and peers.
- Sue Sharpe's *Just Like a Girl* (1976) found working-class girls in London prioritising love, marriage and children. When she repeated the research in the early 1990s (published 1994), girls ranked jobs and careers much higher. This shows gender identity changing within one generation.
- R.W. Connell argued that there are many masculinities, ranked beneath a dominant "hegemonic" form, and femininities shaped in relation to it.
- Judith Butler (*Gender Trouble*, 1990) went further: gender is *performed* through repeated acts, so it can be performed differently. This underpins postmodern claims that gender identity is becoming more fluid.

Evaluation: changes in attitudes and opportunities are clear, but pay gaps and the unequal division of domestic labour suggest that structures change more slowly than identities.

## How ethnicity constructs identity

- Ethnicity is a shared culture, language, religion, history or sense of origin, not a biological category.
- Tariq Modood and colleagues' *Ethnic Minorities in Britain* (1997) found that minority groups attached different importance to religion, language, dress and family in defining who they were, so "ethnic identity" is not one thing.
- Les Back's *New Ethnicities and Urban Culture* (1996), based on fieldwork with young people in south London, described young people of different ethnic backgrounds sharing music and slang and creating local, mixed identities.
- Claire Alexander's *The Asian Gang* (2000) challenged media stereotypes of Asian young men, showing friendship groups as fluid and identity as negotiated.
- Paul Gilroy's *The Black Atlantic* (1993) described Black cultures formed through movement across Africa, the Americas and Europe, a foundation for ideas of diaspora and hybridity.

Evaluation: ethnic identity can be chosen and mixed, but racism and discrimination also impose identities on people from outside.

## How age constructs identity

- Age stages are socially constructed: when childhood ends, what youth means and when someone counts as old all vary between societies and over time.
- Age is also a **generation** identity: people born in the same period share formative events and technologies, which shapes tastes and values.
- Mike Featherstone and Mike Hepworth argued that consumer culture promotes "positive ageing" and that many older people experience the ageing body as a "mask" hiding a younger sense of self.
- Longer life expectancy is creating new stages, such as an active "third age" after retirement, while extended education delays the markers of adulthood for many young people.

Evaluation: chosen later-life identities depend on health and income, so they are more open to the affluent.

## How identities may be changing

**Globalisation.** Global media, migration and trade bring people into contact with many cultures. Three possible outcomes are useful in an essay:

- **Homogenisation**: George Ritzer's *The McDonaldization of Society* (1993) suggested that the principles of the fast-food restaurant spread across sectors and societies, which can make identities more similar.
- **Glocalisation**: Roland Robertson (1992) argued that global products and ideas are adapted to local cultures, producing mixed results rather than uniformity.
- **Cultural defence**: some groups strengthen traditional or religious identity in reaction to global influences.

**Increased choice.** Anthony Giddens (*Modernity and Self-Identity*, 1991) argued that in late modern societies identity becomes a "reflexive project": people continually make and remake their own biography. Zygmunt Bauman (*Liquid Modernity*, 2000) described identities as fluid and short-lived, like consumer choices. Critics reply that choice needs money, and that class, gender and ethnicity still shape who can choose.

**New and hybrid identities.** Hybrid identities combine elements of more than one culture, often among the children of migrants, and new identities arise around lifestyle, sexuality or online communities. Hybridity is a cultural mixing, not a biological one.

## Worked example: planning a 26-mark essay

*Question (practice): "Traditional social identities have been replaced by chosen identities." Evaluate this view.*

1. **Introduction.** Define social identity; distinguish traditional (ascribed, stable) from chosen (achieved, fluid) identity; state the debate between postmodern and structural views.
2. **For the view: class.** Goldthorpe and Lockwood's privatised workers; decline of industrial communities; Skeggs' disidentification.
3. **For the view: gender and ethnicity.** Sharpe's changed priorities; Butler's performativity; Back's new ethnicities.
4. **For the view: theory.** Giddens' reflexive self and Bauman's liquid identities; globalisation widening choice.
5. **Against: structure persists.** Bourdieu's cultural capital still reproduces class; Skeggs shows class shaping life chances even when rejected as a label; racism imposes ethnic identity; age limits.
6. **Against: cultural defence.** Globalisation can strengthen traditional and religious identities.
7. **Conclusion.** Judge by group: choice is wider for the young, urban and affluent; for others, identity is still largely ascribed. "Replaced" overstates a partial change.

The plan scores well because each paragraph applies named evidence to the claim and the conclusion answers "how far".

## Common mistakes

- Explaining how identity is formed but never addressing change.
- Treating hybrid identity as "mixed race".
- Assuming that if people stop calling themselves working class, class no longer affects them.
- Leaving out age, which the syllabus lists alongside class, gender and ethnicity.
- Presenting globalisation as only making cultures the same.
