Key question 4.2 of Cambridge O Level Sociology (2251) asks *What is the role of the family for the
individual and society?* It is the theory core of the Family question. Three perspectives are named,
each with its own list of concepts, and the syllabus then asks you to weigh them and to judge whether
family life is positive or negative for its members. Longer answers on Paper 2 usually turn on this
material, so this guide explains each view through its *mechanism*, then shows how to set the views
against each other.

## Functionalism: the family meets society's needs

Functionalists see society as a set of institutions that work together, like organs in a body. The
question they ask is: *what does the family do that society needs?*

**George Murdock (1949)** studied a large number of societies and identified four functions of the
nuclear family:

1. **Sexual**: a stable outlet for sexual relationships, which limits conflict.
2. **Reproductive**: producing the next generation.
3. **Economic**: providing food and shelter through shared resources.
4. **Educational**: socialising children into the norms and values of society.

**Talcott Parsons (1955)** argued that in modern industrial society the family keeps two "basic and
irreducible" functions: the **primary socialisation of children** and the **stabilisation of adult
personalities**. The second is often called the **"warm bath" theory**: the family is a place where an
adult, usually pictured as a working husband, relaxes and recovers from the stress of work, which
keeps him productive and keeps society stable.

**The loss of functions debate.** Parsons argued that as society industrialised, specialised
institutions took over jobs the family once did. Factories replaced the family as the unit of
production; schools took over formal education; hospitals and the welfare state took on health care.
The family lost functions but became *more specialised* in the two it kept. **Ronald Fletcher (1966)**
disagreed: the family's functions have not been lost but have grown in importance. Parents now
supervise health and education more closely than before, and the state supports the family rather
than replacing it.

**Importance of the nuclear family.** Parsons argued that the isolated nuclear family "fits" the needs
of industrial society: it is small enough to move where jobs are, and status in it is not tied to an
extended kin network.

## Marxism: the family serves capitalism

Marxists see society as divided between the ruling class, who own the means of production, and the
working class. Their question is: *how does the family help capitalism continue?*

- **How the family benefits capitalism and reproduces class inequality.** Friedrich Engels (1884)
  argued that the monogamous family developed so that men could be sure who their heirs were and pass
  private property to them. Inheritance keeps wealth in the same families from one generation to the
  next, so class inequality is reproduced.
- **Exploitation of family members.** Unpaid domestic work by women feeds, clothes and cares for the
  current workforce and raises the next one at no cost to employers. Margaret Benston (1969), a
  Marxist feminist, argued this makes the family a free support system for capitalism.
- **Family as a unit of consumption.** Families buy goods and services, generating profit. Advertising
  targets parents and children, and families feel pressure to keep up with others through what they
  own. Eli Zaretsky (1976) argued that the family gives people an illusion of a private life separate
  from work, while in reality it props up the economy.
- **Family and ideological control.** Children learn in the family to accept hierarchy and authority,
  which prepares them to accept inequality at work. Louis Althusser listed the family among the
  institutions that spread ruling-class ideas.

## Feminism: the family serves patriarchy

Feminists argue that the family benefits **men** more than women. Their question is: *who does the
work, who holds power, and who is harmed?*

- **Domestic division of labour.** Ann Oakley's *The Sociology of Housework* (1974) found that women
  took responsibility for most housework and childcare, even when husbands "helped".
- **Joint and segregated conjugal roles.** Elizabeth Bott (1957) distinguished **segregated** roles
  (separate tasks and leisure for husband and wife) from **joint** roles (tasks and leisure shared).
  Feminists argue that apparently joint roles often hide unequal responsibility.
- **Dual burden and triple shift.** Women in paid work often carry a **dual burden** of a job plus
  domestic labour. Jean Duncombe and Dennis Marsden added a third shift, **emotion work**: managing
  the feelings and relationships of the family.
- **Decision-making.** Stephen Edgell (1980) found that in middle-class couples men took the
  decisions seen as important, such as moving house or major finances, while women decided routine
  matters.
- **Domestic violence and abuse.** R. Emerson Dobash and Russell Dobash (1979) found that violence
  against wives was often triggered when husbands felt their authority was challenged. Feminists see
  this as a product of patriarchy, not of a few "sick" individuals.
- **Gendered socialisation.** Oakley described **manipulation** (encouraging behaviour seen as
  gender-appropriate) and **canalisation** (directing children towards gendered toys and activities),
  which prepares girls for domestic roles.

Feminists differ among themselves: liberal feminists see gradual progress towards equality, while
radical and Marxist feminists see the family as a core source of women's oppression.

## Weighing the three views (4.2.4)

| View | Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| Functionalist | Explains why the family exists in some form in most societies; links family to other institutions | Idealised and based on middle-class nuclear families; ignores conflict, abuse and inequality; assumes family members share interests |
| Marxist | Explains inheritance of wealth and the commercial pressure on families | Ignores benefits families give members; ignores gender inequality within the working class; does not explain families in non-capitalist societies |
| Feminist | Exposes unequal work, power and violence that other views ignore | May understate change towards joint roles and dual-earner equality; may treat all women as having the same experience regardless of class or ethnicity |

## Positive or negative? (4.2.5)

This is a debate in its own right, so it needs evidence on both sides.

- **Positive:** emotional support; care in illness and old age; primary socialisation; economic
  security; the functionalist "haven" of family life.
- **Negative:** domestic violence and child abuse; unequal workloads; control of young people and
  women; conflict between generations. Edmund Leach (1967) argued that the isolated nuclear family is
  under such pressure that it becomes a source of discontent rather than a haven.
- **Judgement:** experience varies by gender, age, class and family type. The same family may be
  positive for one member and harmful for another, which is the strongest line for a conclusion.

## Worked example: an annotated essay plan

**Question:** Evaluate the view that the family mainly benefits its members.

1. **Introduction:** functionalists support the view; Marxists and feminists say the family mainly
   benefits capitalism or men. *Sets out the debate in two sentences.*
2. **For:** Parsons's primary socialisation and warm bath theory; Murdock's economic and educational
   functions; Fletcher on the family's growing role in health and education. *Each point explains
   how a member benefits.*
3. **Against (Marxist):** unpaid domestic labour and consumption benefit employers and owners;
   inheritance reproduces class. *Shifts the beneficiary from members to capitalism.*
4. **Against (feminist):** Oakley on housework, Edgell on decision-making, Dobash and Dobash on
   violence. *Shows some members benefit more than others.*
5. **Evaluation:** functionalists generalise from one family type; Marxists and feminists may ignore
   genuine support and change. *Judges each view, not only the claim.*
6. **Conclusion:** families benefit many members, but not equally; the claim needs to ask "which
   members?" *Answers the question directly.*

## Common mistakes

- Writing "functionalists think the family is good" without naming a function or a mechanism.
- Attributing the warm bath theory to the wrong perspective, or saying it stabilises children.
- Treating the loss of functions debate as one view rather than Parsons against Fletcher.
- Confusing **dual burden** (paid work plus domestic work) with the **triple shift** (adding emotion
  work).
- Writing a Marxist paragraph that only says "the family supports capitalism" without explaining
  consumption, inheritance or ideology.
- Ending an evaluation without a judgement.

## Exam technique for 2251 command words

- **Define** concepts such as *unit of consumption* or *segregated conjugal roles* in one precise
  sentence.
- **Explain** each point with a mechanism: *what* happens, *how* it benefits a group, and *who* gains.
- **Discuss** a view by setting out its argument, supporting it with a named study, and giving at
  least one criticism.
- **Evaluate** by setting perspectives against each other and reaching a reasoned conclusion.
