## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Explain why plant virus diseases are economically important. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why the transmission of plant viruses usually involves a vector. **[2]**

**3.** Aphid stylets are inserted into which part of a plant?

A: Xylem vessels

B: Phloem vessels

C: Root hair cells

D: Stomata **[1]**

**4.** Explain how leaf distortion caused by a virus could reduce the yield of a crop. **[2]**

**5.** A virus is introduced into the phloem of one leaf. Suggest why it can later be found in the roots and growing shoots. **[2]**

**6.** Describe the different roles of wingless and winged aphids in the spread of a virus. **[2]**

### Section B: longer questions

**7.** Describe how aphids can be used to collect phloem sap from a plant, and explain what the results show about the contents of phloem vessels. **[4]**

**8.** Sap was collected from aphid stylets left in the stem of a plant. The rate of sap flow was measured at different times of day.

| Time of day | 06:00 | 12:00 | 18:00 | 00:00 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rate of sap flow (mm³ per hour) | 0.6 | 1.4 | 1.0 | 0.5 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage increase in the rate of flow between 06:00 and 12:00. **[1]**

**(b)** Use the mass flow hypothesis to explain why the rate is highest at 12:00. **[3]**

**9.** A field was divided into two plots of 250 plants each. One plot was covered with fine mesh that aphids could not pass through. After eight weeks, 4 plants were infected with a virus in the covered plot and 61 in the uncovered plot.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of plants infected in each plot. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain what these results suggest about how the virus spreads, and why this design gives stronger evidence than trap catches alone. **[2]**

**10.** A farmer sprays insecticide to kill aphids only after virus symptoms appear in many plants. Suggest why this is much less effective at reducing virus spread than spraying earlier. **[3]**

**11.** In some aphid species, winged females are produced when a colony becomes crowded. Suggest why this increases the spread of a virus through a crop. **[3]**

**12.** Use the mass flow hypothesis to explain why a phloem vessel punctured close to a photosynthesising leaf releases sap faster than one punctured close to a root. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Viruses cause losses in crop production (yield) [1] and in the quality of produce, so it is worth less or cannot be sold; these losses occur in all parts of the world [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The specification names both production and quality. Give both.

**2.** Plants are immobile, so infected and healthy plants do not come into contact [1]; a vector such as an aphid carries virus particles between plants and delivers them through the cell walls into the phloem [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Viruses cannot move themselves, so "the virus travels to another plant" does not score.

**3.** **B** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Aphids feed on phloem sap, which contains sucrose under pressure.

**4.** Distorted leaves have a smaller area facing the light, so less light is absorbed [1]. The rate of photosynthesis falls, so less sugar is available for growth and storage, reducing yield [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the symptom to photosynthesis, then to yield.

**5.** The virus is carried in the phloem sap [1], which moves by mass flow (translocation) from sources to sinks such as roots and growing shoots [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Name translocation in the phloem. "It spreads through the plant" restates the question.

**6.** Wingless aphids spread the virus within a plant or to neighbouring plants that they can walk to [1]. Winged females migrate to new host plants further away and start colonies there, spreading the virus over longer distances [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The contrast is distance. Each type needs its own point.

**7.** An aphid is allowed to insert its stylets into a phloem vessel [1]. The aphid is anaesthetised and its body cut away, leaving the stylets in place [1]. Sap continues to flow out of the cut stylets, showing that phloem contents are under pressure [1]. The collected sap can be analysed and is found to contain sucrose and other organic substances [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The pressure point is the key finding. It supports the mass flow hypothesis.

**8. (a)** (1.4 − 0.6) ÷ 0.6 × 100 = **133%** [1].

**(b)** At midday the rate of photosynthesis is highest, so more sucrose is made and loaded into the phloem at the source [1]. This lowers the water potential in the phloem more, so more water enters by osmosis [1]. The hydrostatic pressure is higher, so sap is forced through the phloem, and out of the stylet, faster [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Follow the sequence: sucrose, water potential, osmosis, pressure, flow.

**9. (a)** Covered: 4 ÷ 250 × 100 = **1.6%** [1]. Uncovered: 61 ÷ 250 × 100 = **24.4%** [1].

**(b)** Infection was much lower when aphids were excluded, suggesting the virus is mainly spread by aphids [1]. The mesh compares plants with and without aphids while other conditions are similar, so it tests the link directly rather than relying on a correlation [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Mention that the comparison is controlled. Trap data alone only show that aphid numbers and infection rise together.

**10.** Symptoms take time to appear after infection, so many more plants are already infected but do not show it yet [1]. By the time spraying begins, aphids have already carried the virus to many plants, including other fields if winged females have migrated [1]. Killing aphids does not cure infected plants, which remain sources of virus for any surviving or newly arriving aphids [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the time lag between infection and symptoms. It is the same idea as the delay seen in field data.

**11.** Crowding means many aphids are feeding on one plant, which may be infected, so many carry the virus [1]. Winged females fly to new host plants further away [1] and start new colonies there, each of which can spread the virus to nearby plants [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Two ideas: many carriers, and long-distance spread followed by new colonies.

**12.** Near a photosynthesising leaf (a source), sucrose is loaded into the phloem, lowering the water potential, so water enters by osmosis [1]. This creates a high hydrostatic pressure near the source [1]. Near the root (a sink), sucrose is removed, so water leaves and the pressure is lower; sap is released faster where the pressure is higher [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Mass flow depends on a pressure gradient from source to sink. State both ends.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying aphids bite or chew leaves.
- Saying aphids suck sap by their own effort.
- Linking symptoms to yield without mentioning photosynthesis.
- Describing the stylet technique without stating what it shows.
- Leaving out the osmosis step in mass flow explanations.
- Treating trap catches as proof that aphids carry the virus.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are these OxfordAQA past paper questions?**
No. They are original questions for section 3.2.9 of OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Biology (9610). Use them with the official past papers.

**Will I need to know about mass flow for questions on aphids?**
Often, yes. Aphids feed because phloem sap is under pressure, and their stylets are used as evidence for the mass flow hypothesis in section 3.2.8.2.
