Condensed notes for the section 3.4 row **Contract terms: general**: "Express and implied terms" and "Conditions, warranties and innominate terms". For explanation and a worked problem, use the [contract terms: general study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-7162-contract-terms-general/).

## The sequence

```
1 Statement made  ->  TERM or REPRESENTATION?
2 If term         ->  EXPRESS or IMPLIED (fact, law, custom, statute)
3 Classify        ->  CONDITION / WARRANTY / INNOMINATE
4 Breach          ->  terminate and/or damages, or damages only
```

## Term or representation?

| Factor | Case | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Importance stressed | *Bannerman v White* (1861) | Term (no sulphur) |
| Specific assurance on a direct question | *Couchman v Hill* (1947) | Term (heifer unserved) |
| Discouraging checks | *Schawel v Reade* (1913) | Term (horse sound) |
| Maker has special knowledge | *Dick Bentley v Harold Smith* (1965) | Term (dealer, mileage) |
| Maker lacks special knowledge | *Oscar Chess v Williams* (1957) | Representation (private seller, car age) |
| Time gap; omitted from writing | *Routledge v McKay* (1954) | Representation (motorcycle date) |

**Parol evidence rule:** oral terms not generally added to a written contract.
**Collateral contract:** separate enforceable promise: *City and Westminster Properties v Mudd* (1959).

## Implied terms

| Route | Test | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| In fact: business efficacy | Needed to make the contract work | *The Moorcock* (1889) |
| In fact: officious bystander | So obvious: "oh, of course" | *Shirlaw v Southern Foundries* (1939) |
| In fact: modern restatement | Necessity, not reasonableness | *Marks and Spencer v BNP Paribas* (2015) |
| In law | Implied into all contracts of that type | *Liverpool CC v Irwin* (1977) |
| Custom | Established trade or local custom | *Hutton v Warren* (1836) |
| Statute | Implied by Parliament | Consumer Rights Act 2015 (separate row) |

## Classification of terms

| Type | Effect of breach | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Condition | Terminate or affirm; damages | *Poussard v Spiers* (1876) |
| Warranty | Damages only | *Bettini v Gye* (1876) |
| Innominate | Terminate only if breach deprives of substantially the whole benefit | *Hong Kong Fir* (1962); *The Hansa Nord* (1976) |

**How classified:**
1. Statute.
2. Parties: label not conclusive (*Schuler v Wickman*, 1974); "of the essence" effective (*Lombard v Butterworth*, 1987).
3. Precedent: "expected ready to load" a condition (*The Mihalis Angelos*, 1971); time clauses in mercantile contracts usually conditions (*Bunge v Tradax*, 1981).
4. Otherwise innominate.

**Strictness:** *Arcos v Ronaasen* (1933): rejection for slightly thicker staves. Courts now reluctant to allow rejection for trivial breaches (*Reardon Smith Line*, 1976; *Hansa Nord*).

## Applying it in a problem

- Quote the statement from the facts and weigh at least two factors before deciding term or representation.
- If a term, say whether it is express or implied, and by which route.
- Classify using statute, the parties' wording, then precedent; only then fall back on the innominate approach.
- State the remedy precisely: "may terminate and claim damages" or "damages only".

## Evaluation points

- **Term or representation:** factor tests are flexible but unpredictable; outcomes depend on how a court weighs expertise, timing and importance.
- **Implied terms:** *Marks and Spencer* restores the primacy of the written bargain, supporting certainty and freedom of contract.
- **Conditions:** give certainty and allow quick termination in commerce, but can be used to escape a bad bargain (*Arcos*).
- **Innominate terms:** match remedy to the seriousness of breach, but leave parties unsure whether they may terminate, risking wrongful termination themselves.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Term | A promise forming part of the contract |
| Representation | A pre-contract statement inducing the contract but not part of it |
| Express term | A term the parties stated |
| Implied term | A term added by fact, law, custom or statute |
| Parol evidence rule | Oral evidence cannot generally add to a written contract |
| Collateral contract | A separate contract containing an oral promise |
| Condition | An essential term; breach allows termination |
| Warranty | A minor term; breach gives damages only |
| Innominate term | A term whose breach is judged by its consequences |
| Affirm | Choose to continue the contract after breach |

## Exam traps

- A false statement is not automatically a term.
- "Condition" in the contract does not decide classification (*Schuler*).
- Implied terms need necessity or obviousness, not fairness.
- Breach of a condition gives a choice: the innocent party may affirm.
- Do not use *Hong Kong Fir* where precedent has already classified the term as a condition.

## Self-test

1. Why was the statement in *Bannerman v White* a term? *The buyer made clear its importance; he would not buy treated hops.*
2. Why was the statement in *Oscar Chess v Williams* a representation? *The private seller had no special knowledge and relied on the logbook.*
3. What made the statement a representation in *Routledge v McKay*? *The time gap and its omission from the written contract.*
4. What is a collateral contract? *A separate contract containing an oral promise given in return for entering the main contract.*
5. What test did *Marks and Spencer v BNP Paribas* confirm for implied terms in fact? *Necessity for business efficacy or obviousness, not reasonableness.*
6. What term was implied in *Liverpool CC v Irwin*? *That the landlord would take reasonable care to maintain the common parts.*
7. What is the effect of breach of a condition? *The innocent party may terminate or affirm, and claim damages.*
8. Distinguish *Poussard* and *Bettini*. *Missing opening nights broke a condition; missing rehearsals broke a warranty.*
9. What question did *Hong Kong Fir* ask? *Did the breach deprive the innocent party of substantially the whole benefit?*
10. Why was the "condition" in *Schuler* not treated as one? *The parties could not have intended one missed visit to allow termination.*
11. What did *Bunge v Tradax* decide? *Time clauses in mercantile contracts are generally conditions.*
12. Why is *Arcos v Ronaasen* criticised? *It allowed rejection for a trivial breach to escape a falling market.*
