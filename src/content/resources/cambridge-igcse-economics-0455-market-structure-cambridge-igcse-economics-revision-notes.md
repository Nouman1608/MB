These notes condense section 3.8, Market structure, of the Cambridge IGCSE Economics 0455 syllabus for examination in 2026: competitive markets and monopoly markets. The theory of perfect and imperfect competition and all diagrams are not required. All figures are invented. ## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Market structure | The features of a market, especially the number of firms and ease of entry, that shape how firms behave |
| Competitive market | A market with a high number of firms selling similar products |
| Monopoly | A market with a single seller (pure monopoly) or one dominant seller |
| Market share | A firm's sales as a percentage of total market sales |
| Barriers to entry | Obstacles that stop new firms entering a market |
| Price maker | A firm able to set its own price |
| Natural monopoly | An industry where one firm can supply the market at lower cost than several |

```
Market share (%) = firm's sales / total market sales x 100
```

## 3.8.1 A high number of firms: four effects

| Effect | Direction | Reason in one line |
|---|---|---|
| Price | Lower | Buyers switch to cheaper rivals |
| Quality | Higher or maintained | Firms compete for customers |
| Choice | Wider | Many varieties and suppliers |
| Profit | Lower | Price competition; high profits attract new firms |

**The entry process:** high profits → new firms enter → supply rises and firms compete → price falls → profit per firm falls.

Worked reminder: 5 firms share sales of 50,000 at a profit of 2 per unit, so each earns 2 x 10,000 = 20,000. After 5 more firms enter, profit per unit falls to 0.80 and total sales rise to 60,000: each earns 0.80 x 6,000 = 4,800.

**Limits:** small firms may miss economies of scale; low profits limit research; cost-cutting may harm quality.

## 3.8.2 Monopoly characteristics

- Single seller or dominant firm.
- No close substitutes.
- Barriers to entry.
- Price maker (but higher price means fewer sales).
- High profits that can last.

## Barriers to entry

| Barrier | Example |
|---|---|
| Patents and licences | A new medicine; a broadcasting licence |
| High start-up costs | Building an airline or a power station |
| Economies of scale | A large incumbent with low cost per unit |
| Control of raw materials or outlets | Owning the only quarry |
| Brand loyalty | Long-established brand trusted by customers |
| Government grant of sole supply | A state postal service |

## Natural monopoly in one line

When the main cost is a single network (water pipes, rail track, power lines), duplicating it raises cost per customer, so one supplier is cheaper; it is usually owned or regulated by the government to stop high prices.

Worked reminder: a network costs 4 million a year whether used by one firm or duplicated; running costs are 40 per customer for 100,000 customers.

```
One network: (4,000,000 + 4,000,000) / 100,000 = 80 per customer
Two networks: (8,000,000 + 4,000,000) / 100,000 = 120 per customer
```

## Advantages and disadvantages of monopoly

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Economies of scale can cut cost per unit | Higher prices than with competition |
| Profits can fund research and development | Less choice for consumers |
| Avoids wasteful duplication (natural monopoly) | Little pressure to improve quality or service |
| Stable supply; long-term planning | Inefficiency: no rival forces cost cutting |
| Large firm may compete abroad | Barriers keep out better or cheaper rivals |
| | May pay suppliers and workers less |

## Evaluation questions to ask

- Does the monopoly **pass on** cost savings as lower prices?
- Is it **regulated** or owned by the government?
- Are there **substitutes** in other markets that limit its power?
- Are **barriers** permanent (natural monopoly) or temporary (a patent that expires)?
- Does it use profits for **research** that benefits consumers?

## Exam traps

- **No diagrams.** Answer in words; the syllabus does not require market structure diagrams.
- **Price maker is not unlimited power.** Raising price reduces quantity sold.
- **Monopoly is not always one firm.** A firm with a dominant share can act like a monopoly.
- **Competition is not always better.** Many small firms may have higher costs.
- **Use all four effects** when asked about a high number of firms: price, quality, choice and profit.
- **Barriers explain lasting profit.** Without barriers, high profits attract entrants.

## Self-test

1. State the four effects of a high number of firms named in the syllabus.
2. Define monopoly.
3. A firm sells 18,000 units in a market of 24,000. Calculate its market share.
4. Give two barriers to entry.
5. Why do high profits in a competitive market tend to fall over time?
6. What is a natural monopoly?
7. Give one advantage of monopoly for consumers.
8. Give two disadvantages of monopoly for consumers.
9. Why might a monopoly be less efficient than a competitive firm?
10. Give one disadvantage of a market with very many small firms.
11. Why can a monopoly not charge any price it likes and keep all its sales?
12. Name one way a government can reduce the disadvantages of a natural monopoly.

### Answers

1. Lower price, higher quality, wider choice, lower profit.
2. A market with a single seller of a product with no close substitutes (or one dominant seller).
3. 18,000 / 24,000 x 100 = 75%.
4. Any two: patents or licences; high start-up costs; economies of scale; control of raw materials; brand loyalty; legal sole right to supply.
5. High profits attract new firms; extra competition lowers price and each firm's share of sales.
6. An industry where one firm can supply the whole market more cheaply than several, usually because of a costly network.
7. Lower prices if economies of scale are passed on; or new products funded by profits.
8. Any two: higher prices; less choice; poorer quality or service.
9. No rivals force it to cut costs, so waste can continue.
10. Small firms may not gain economies of scale, so costs and prices may be higher; or low profits limit research.
11. Consumers buy less at higher prices; some switch to other goods.
12. Regulate its prices, or run it as a state-owned firm.
