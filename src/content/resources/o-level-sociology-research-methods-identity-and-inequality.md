This guide covers **Paper 1 Research Methods, Identity and
Inequality**, for Cambridge O Level Sociology 2251, Version 3,
2025–2027 series. Confirm with your teacher the full paper structure
for your own examination series before finalising a revision plan. Independently confirmed against 2251's own
specification PDF: it shares identical paper titles and structure with
Cambridge IGCSE Sociology 0495. Paper 1 is compulsory across all three
topics — three questions, candidates answer two, with Question 1
compulsory.

## Where this fits in 2251

Research Methods is the foundational topic of the syllabus — the
methods introduced here are assumed knowledge across the rest of the
qualification, since exam questions routinely ask candidates to
suggest or evaluate a research method for studying whatever
substantive topic — identity, inequality, family, education, crime —
the question is actually about. This means Research Methods is never
really "finished" as a revision topic — it resurfaces embedded within
questions on every other substantive area throughout the course.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE O LEVEL SOCIOLOGY 2251 — PAPER 1 RESEARCH METHODS,
IDENTITY AND INEQUALITY**

- 1.1 Research Methods — the main methods sociologists use to collect
data (including surveys, interviews, observation and secondary
sources), and their strengths and limitations, together with the
concepts of reliability and validity used to evaluate them
- 1.2 Identity: Self and Society — how identity is shaped by social
factors including socialisation, culture, and agents such as family,
education, media and religion, distinguishing primary from secondary
socialisation
- 1.3 Social Stratification and Inequality — how societies are
divided into unequal groups by factors such as class, gender and
ethnicity, and the causes and consequences of that inequality, including
its effect on life chances

## How to approach it

As with the equivalent IGCSE syllabus, Research Methods rewards
evaluation over description — for any given research question,
practise stating one strength and one limitation of a questionnaire
versus an interview versus observation for that specific topic, since
"identify a method and justify your choice" is a recurring question
type. A well-chosen method for one topic can be entirely the wrong
choice for another, so avoid learning a single "best" method — instead
practise justifying different method choices for different research
scenarios. For 1.2 and 1.3, use named sociological concepts precisely (e.g.
primary versus secondary socialisation, ascribed versus achieved
status) rather than everyday language that only loosely gestures at the
same idea, since precise terminology is specifically what the mark
scheme rewards over a looser, everyday-language paraphrase of the same
underlying idea. Because Paper 1 has a compulsory Question 1 plus a choice of
one more from the remaining two, do not skip revising any of the three
topics entirely, even if one feels weaker — being forced into an
unprepared topic by the compulsory question is a common and avoidable
source of lost marks. If Question 1 happens to be on a candidate's
weakest topic, there is no way to avoid it, which makes even a "just
enough to answer confidently" level of preparation on every topic
essential, rather than optional. Linking 1.2 and 1.3 in revision also pays off, since
identity and inequality are closely connected in the syllabus —
for instance, how social class or gender shapes the identity someone
develops — and questions sometimes reward candidates who can draw that
connection rather than treating the two topics as entirely separate.

## Evaluating a research method — the four-part structure

A strong evaluation of a research method against a specific topic touches four elements: practical
issues (time, cost, access to participants), ethical issues (informed consent, harm, confidentiality
— especially significant when researching sensitive topics), theoretical issues (whether the method
produces valid, in-depth data or reliable, generalisable data — the same reliability/validity
trade-off that runs through the wider sociology syllabus), and representativeness (whether the
sample or data genuinely reflects the wider population being studied). A method-evaluation answer
that only covers practical issues, however detailed, will not reach the top mark band, since the
theoretical and ethical dimensions are what distinguish sociological evaluation from a purely
commonsense assessment of feasibility.

## Identity and inequality — a genuinely connected pair

1.2 (Identity: Self and Society) and 1.3 (Social Stratification and Inequality) are not simply
adjacent topics but causally linked: the class, gender or ethnic identity a person develops through
socialisation is itself shaped by, and in turn reinforces, their position within the wider system of
social stratification examined in 1.3. A strong answer linking the two might explain how a working-
class identity is partly constructed through the socialisation processes and agencies covered in
1.2, while also being shaped by, and reinforcing, class-based inequality covered in 1.3 — this kind
of explicit two-way link is what separates a synthesised answer from two disconnected topic
summaries.

## Why sampling matters as much as method choice

Beyond simply choosing between a questionnaire, an interview or observation, the *sample* a
sociologist studies — who is included, and how they were selected — is often examined as a
distinct issue in its own right. A representative sample allows findings to be generalised to a
wider population with more confidence; a small, unrepresentative, or self-selecting sample
undermines the value of even a well-designed method. Practise identifying sampling issues
specifically (not just method issues) in a described research scenario, since Cambridge mark
schemes credit this as a separate evaluative point from the choice of method itself.
