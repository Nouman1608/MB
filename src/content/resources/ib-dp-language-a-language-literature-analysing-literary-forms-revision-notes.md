## Key terms: prose

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Narrator | The voice telling the story; not the same as the author |
| First person | Narrator is a character ("I"); limited to what they know and admit |
| Third person limited | "She/he", filtered through one character's view |
| Omniscient | Knows all minds and events; may comment and judge |
| Unreliable narrator | One whose account the reader has reason to doubt |
| Free indirect discourse | Third-person narration carrying a character's own words and thoughts |
| Focalisation | Whose eyes we see through (can differ from who narrates) |
| Analepsis / prolepsis | Flashback / flash-forward |
| Frame narrative | A story told inside another story |
| Scene / summary | Slow, moment-by-moment time / fast, compressed time |

## Key terms: poetry

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Speaker | The voice of the poem; not automatically the poet |
| Stanza | Couplet (2 lines), tercet (3), quatrain (4), sestet (6), octave (8) |
| Petrarchan sonnet | Octave + sestet; turn (volta) at line 9 |
| Shakespearean sonnet | Three quatrains + couplet; ABAB CDCD EFEF GG |
| Metre | Regular pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables |
| Iambic pentameter | Five iambs (x /), ten syllables |
| Blank verse | Unrhymed iambic pentameter |
| Free verse | No regular metre or rhyme |
| Enjambment | Sense runs on past the line end |
| Caesura | Strong pause inside a line |
| Half-rhyme | Near rhyme, e.g. *heart/hurt* |
| Dramatic monologue | One speaker, a silent listener, unintended self-revelation |

## Key terms: drama

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Stage directions | Instructions for setting, movement, tone, props, lighting |
| Soliloquy | Character alone on stage speaks thoughts to the audience |
| Aside | Short remark heard by the audience, not by other characters |
| Monologue | Long speech by one character to others on stage |
| Dramatic irony | The audience knows what a character does not |
| Stichomythia | Rapid alternation of single short lines |
| Exposition | Early information the audience needs |
| Climax | Point of highest tension |

## Method in steps

### Close-reading any literary extract

1. **Form first.** Prose, poetry or drama? Which sub-form (sonnet, frame narrative, soliloquy)?
2. **Voice.** Who speaks or narrates, to whom, and how much do they know?
3. **Structure.** Where does it turn, jump in time, speed up or slow down?
4. **Language.** Imagery, sound, sentence length, register.
5. **Effect and meaning.** For every feature: what does it make the reader or audience feel, notice or
   believe?

### Scanning a line of verse

1. Count the syllables.
2. Mark the natural stresses as you would say the words (/ stressed, x unstressed).
3. Group into feet and name the most common foot.
4. Count the feet: tetrameter (4), pentameter (5).
5. Check two or three more lines before naming the metre.
6. Look for the line that **breaks** the pattern; that is usually the one to write about.

Worked reminder (original line written for Marlbridge):

```
x    /     x    /     x    /     x     /    x    /
The  rain  had  kept  its  prom- ise  all  the  day
```

Ten syllables, five iambs: **iambic pentameter**. Now try "The rain kept every promise it had
made". If you say *every* as two syllables (EV-ry), the line has ten; as three, eleven. Count as you
would naturally say the line, and state your reading if it is ambiguous.

### Reading a stage direction

1. What does it show that the dialogue does not say?
2. Does it agree with or contradict the words?
3. What does the audience see or hear at this moment, and who on stage does not?

## Must-know distinctions

- **Narrator vs author**: "the narrator claims", not "Poe believes".
- **Speaker vs poet**: the "I" of a poem is a constructed voice.
- **Enjambment vs caesura**: enjambment runs *over* the line end; caesura pauses *inside* the line.
- **Metre vs rhythm**: metre is the pattern; rhythm is how the line actually sounds, including
  variations.
- **Soliloquy vs aside vs monologue**: alone and long / brief and unheard by others / long and heard
  by others.
- **Dramatic irony vs verbal irony**: the audience knows more than a character / a speaker says the
  opposite of what they mean.
- **First person vs third person limited**: "I" is a character in the story; limited third stays
  close to one mind but is not that character.
- **Petrarchan vs Shakespearean sonnet**: 8 + 6 with a turn at line 9 / 4 + 4 + 4 + 2 with the
  couplet often turning or summing up.
- **Summary vs analysis**: *what happens* vs *how the form makes it matter*.

## Worked reminders

**Rhyme scheme.** Give each new end sound the next letter. Original quatrain written for Marlbridge:

> The lamps went out along the pier,
> the boats lay silent in the bay;
> I told myself I felt no fear,
> and then I turned and walked away.

pier/fear = A, bay/away = B, so **ABAB**. Each line has eight syllables in four iambs: **iambic
tetrameter**. The semicolon marks the shift from the scene to the speaker; "I told myself" hints
that the fear was real.

**Unreliable voice.** Original text written for Marlbridge: *"I have never been jealous. I simply
noticed, every time, who she spoke to first."* The denial is undercut by "every time": the narrator's
own detail proves the jealousy.

**Form and context.** A 19th-century omniscient narrator who judges characters directly
("she was foolish") may have read as trustworthy guidance then; a reader today may hear it as
intrusive. Name the convention, then say how its effect has shifted.

## Quick self-test

1. How many lines does a sonnet have, and what is the rhyme scheme of a Shakespearean sonnet?
2. Name the foot in the word *below*.
3. How many syllables and stresses does a regular line of iambic pentameter have?
4. Original text: *"I swear I never touched the money. Ask anyone."* What kind of narrator might this
   suggest, and why?
5. What is the difference between a soliloquy and an aside?
6. Original lines: *The wind / came back without her.* Name the technique at the line break and give
   its effect.
7. What is analepsis?
8. In which type of sonnet does the turn usually fall at line 9?
9. What is the metre of Blake's "Tyger Tyger, burning bright" (1794)?
10. Define dramatic irony.
11. What is the eight-line opening section of a Petrarchan sonnet called?
12. A narrator says "she thought" in one sentence and, in the next, "How dull the party was!" with no
    tag. What technique is the second sentence?

### Answers

1. Fourteen lines; ABAB CDCD EFEF GG.
2. Iamb: unstressed then stressed (be-**LOW**).
3. Ten syllables, five stresses.
4. First person, possibly unreliable: the swearing and the appeal to others protest too much, which
   invites doubt.
5. A soliloquy is spoken by a character alone on stage; an aside is a brief remark to the audience
   that other characters present do not hear.
6. Enjambment. The break after "wind" makes us expect the wind itself; "came back without her" then
   arrives as a delayed shock, so the absence is felt at the moment it is read.
7. A flashback: the narrative moves to an earlier time.
8. The Petrarchan (Italian) sonnet.
9. Trochaic tetrameter with the last unstressed syllable dropped: seven syllables, stressed first
   (**TY**-ger **TY**-ger **BURN**-ing **BRIGHT**).
10. The audience knows something that a character on stage does not.
11. The octave.
12. Free indirect discourse: the character's own thought in the narrator's third-person voice.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a feature ("there is caesura") without saying what it does in *this* line.
- Writing "the poet says" when the poem has a clearly constructed speaker.
- Calling a metre from one line, or miscounting syllables in words such as *every*, *heaven* or
  *afternoon*.
- Labelling any 14-line poem a Shakespearean sonnet without checking the rhyme scheme.
- Treating a first-person narrator's claims as facts.
- Discussing only dialogue in drama and ignoring stage directions and what the audience sees.
- Quoting whole sentences instead of embedding the two or three words that carry the effect.
- Retelling plot instead of analysing structure (flashback, pace, where the text turns).
- Discussing context as background, not as something that changes how a convention works.
