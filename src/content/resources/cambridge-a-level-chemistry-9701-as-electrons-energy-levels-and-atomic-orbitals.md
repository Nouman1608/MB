This guide covers subtopic **1.3, Electrons, energy levels and atomic orbitals**, from Topic 1 (Atomic structure) of Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is AS Level content. Every atom or ion is in its ground state, and only hydrogen to krypton is assessed. The broader [Atomic Structure: Orbitals and Ionisation Energy guide](/resources/as-atomic-structure-orbitals-and-ionisation-energy/) introduces sub-shells, the filling order and Hund's rule. This page goes further: a reliable method for any atom or ion up to krypton, the chromium and copper exceptions explained, counting unpaired electrons, and recognising free radicals.

## Syllabus coverage

Candidates should be able to:

1. understand the terms shells, sub-shells and orbitals; principal quantum number (n); and ground state, limited to electronic configuration;
2. describe the number of orbitals in s, p and d sub-shells and the number of electrons that can fill them;
3. describe the order of increasing energy of the sub-shells within the first three shells and the 4s and 4p sub-shells;
4. describe electronic configurations to include the number of electrons in each shell, sub-shell and orbital;
5. explain electronic configurations in terms of energy of the electrons and inter-electron repulsion;
6. determine the configuration of atoms and ions from proton number and charge, in full form (Fe: 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶3d⁶4s²) or shorthand form ([Ar] 3d⁶4s²);
7. understand and use electrons-in-boxes notation;
8. describe and sketch the shapes of s and p orbitals;
9. describe a free radical as a species with one or more unpaired electrons.

## Shells, sub-shells and orbitals

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Shell | Main energy level, numbered by the **principal quantum number** n = 1, 2, 3, 4 |
| Sub-shell | Group of orbitals of equal energy within a shell: s, p or d |
| Orbital | Region of space holding up to two electrons of opposite spin |
| Ground state | The lowest-energy arrangement of the electrons |

| Shell (n) | Sub-shells | Orbitals | Maximum electrons |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1s | 1 | 2 |
| 2 | 2s, 2p | 1 + 3 = 4 | 8 |
| 3 | 3s, 3p, 3d | 1 + 3 + 5 = 9 | 18 |
| 4 (to 4p) | 4s, 4p (4d and 4f not assessed) | 1 + 3 | 8 |

Each shell holds a maximum of 2n² electrons: 2, 8, 18 for n = 1, 2, 3.

## Writing a configuration: a method that always works

1. Find the number of electrons: proton number, minus the charge for a positive ion, plus the charge for a negative ion.
2. Fill sub-shells in order of increasing energy: 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p.
3. Write the result in shell order (3d before 4s), which is how the syllabus writes iron.
4. For a positive ion of an element beyond calcium, remove electrons from 4s before 3d.
5. Check: total electrons equals your step 1 number.

### Worked example: atoms

| Element | Electrons | Full configuration | Shorthand |
|---|---|---|---|
| S | 16 | 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁴ | [Ne] 3s²3p⁴ |
| V | 23 | 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶3d³4s² | [Ar] 3d³4s² |
| Se | 34 | 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶3d¹⁰4s²4p⁴ | [Ar] 3d¹⁰4s²4p⁴ |

Per shell, selenium has 2, 8, 18, 6 electrons. Counting by shell is a quick check of a long configuration.

### Worked example: ions

| Ion | Electrons | Configuration | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|---|
| S²⁻ | 18 | 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶ | Two electrons added to 3p |
| Ca²⁺ | 18 | 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶ | Two 4s electrons removed |
| V³⁺ | 20 | [Ar] 3d² | Both 4s electrons removed first, then one 3d |
| Zn²⁺ | 28 | [Ar] 3d¹⁰ | Both 4s electrons removed; 3d full |
| Cu⁺ | 28 | [Ar] 3d¹⁰ | The single 4s electron removed |

A common error is to write V³⁺ as [Ar] 3d⁰4s² by removing 3d electrons. Once 3d is occupied, 4s electrons are the ones lost first.

## Explaining configurations: energy and repulsion

The syllabus asks you to explain configurations, not just recall them. Two ideas do all the work.

**Electrons occupy the lowest available energy levels.** This gives the filling order. In potassium and calcium the 4s sub-shell is lower in energy than 3d, so it fills first.

**Electrons repel each other.** Within a sub-shell, all orbitals have the same energy. Two electrons in the same orbital are close together and repel strongly, so electrons occupy separate orbitals singly, with parallel spins, before any pairing occurs. This is why nitrogen's 2p electrons are in three separate orbitals.

### The chromium and copper exceptions

| Element | Expected from filling order | Actual ground state |
|---|---|---|
| Cr (24) | [Ar] 3d⁴4s² | [Ar] 3d⁵4s¹ |
| Cu (29) | [Ar] 3d⁹4s² | [Ar] 3d¹⁰4s¹ |

By the time 3d is filling, the 3d and 4s sub-shells are very close in energy. In chromium, moving one electron from 4s into the empty 3d orbital gives six electrons, each alone in its own orbital. This removes the repulsion of the pair in 4s, and the half-filled 3d sub-shell is the lower-energy arrangement. In copper, moving one electron from 4s completes the 3d sub-shell, a full 3d¹⁰ arrangement that is lower in energy.

The ions follow the usual rule: Cr³⁺ is [Ar] 3d³ and Cu²⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁹.

## Electrons-in-boxes notation

Each box is an orbital; each arrow an electron, with the direction showing spin. Two arrows in a box point in opposite directions.

**Worked example: oxygen, 1s²2s²2p⁴.**

    1s [↑↓]   2s [↑↓]   2p [↑↓][↑ ][↑ ]

The first three 2p electrons go into separate boxes with parallel spins; the fourth pairs up in the first box. Oxygen has **two unpaired electrons**.

**Worked example: iron, [Ar] 3d⁶4s².**

    3d [↑↓][↑ ][↑ ][↑ ][↑ ]   4s [↑↓]

Five 3d electrons fill each box singly; the sixth pairs. Iron has **four unpaired electrons**. Fe³⁺, [Ar] 3d⁵, has **five**, one in each 3d box.

| Species | Configuration (outer part) | Unpaired electrons |
|---|---|---|
| N | 2p³ | 3 |
| O | 2p⁴ | 2 |
| Cr | 3d⁵4s¹ | 6 |
| Fe | 3d⁶4s² | 4 |
| Cu | 3d¹⁰4s¹ | 1 |
| Zn²⁺ | 3d¹⁰ | 0 |

## Shapes of s and p orbitals

- An **s orbital** is spherical, centred on the nucleus. A 2s orbital is larger than a 1s orbital, and 3s larger again, because the electrons are further from the nucleus on average.
- A **p orbital** has two lobes on opposite sides of the nucleus, like a dumbbell. The three p orbitals of a sub-shell point along three axes at right angles, labelled px, py and pz.

When sketching, draw the nucleus at the centre, a circle for s, and two equal lobes along an axis for p. Label the three p orbitals on separate axes.

## Free radicals

A **free radical** is a species with one or more unpaired electrons. Examples include the chlorine atom, Cl• (3p⁵: one unpaired electron), and the methyl radical, •CH₃, where carbon has only three bonds and one unpaired electron. The dot in the formula represents the unpaired electron. Radicals form when a covalent bond breaks evenly, one electron going to each atom, as when ultraviolet light splits Cl₂ into two Cl• atoms. Their unpaired electron makes them very reactive.

## Common mistakes

- Writing 3d before 4s in the *filling* order. Fill 4s first; write 3d first.
- Removing 3d electrons before 4s when forming transition-element ions.
- Writing chromium as 3d⁴4s² or copper as 3d⁹4s².
- Pairing electrons in a p or d sub-shell before every orbital has one.
- Drawing two arrows in one box pointing the same way.
- Describing a p orbital as a figure of eight in a flat plane without showing it lies along an axis.
- Defining a free radical as "a charged species". Radicals are defined by unpaired electrons, not charge.

## Exam technique

- **Determine** a configuration: show the electron count first, then the configuration.
- **Explain** a configuration: use the words *lower energy* and *repulsion* explicitly.
- **Describe** orbitals: give number of orbitals per sub-shell and two electrons per orbital.
- **Sketch** orbitals: label axes and the nucleus.
