**Transport and telecommunications is Section 7 of Paper 2 in Cambridge O Level Pakistan Studies
2059.** It asks how and why the density of roads, railways and airports varies across Pakistan,
which routes and ports link it to other countries, what a dry port is, and how phones, broadcasting
and the internet support development. Map interpretation is the core skill: many questions begin
with a network map you must describe before you explain it.

## Where does Section 7 fit in 2059 Paper 2?

Paper 2 asks you to answer three 25-mark questions from five, each built around resources, with
part (d) testing analysis. Section 7 depends on Section 1 (relief, rivers and climate decide where
routes can run) and supports Sections 4, 5 and 6, because farms, factories and traders all need to
move goods. It also links to Section 8: better transport and telecommunications change where
people live and work.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE O LEVEL PAKISTAN STUDIES 2059, 2026 — PAPER 2, SECTION 7: TRANSPORT AND
TELECOMMUNICATIONS**

- **7(a) Internal transport:** interpret maps to describe regional variations in the density and
  pattern of road, rail and air networks; explain factors helping and hindering the location,
  maintenance and development of roads, railways and airports; describe recent improvements and
  consider the feasibility of new developments; compare road, rail and air for goods and people;
  evaluate new schemes, including motorways and airports
- **7(b) International transport:** identify on a map the roads (with the passes they use) and
  railways that cross the border and are used for at least part of the year; identify the ports of
  Keamari, Qasim and Gwadar and the cities with international airports; explain the factors
  affecting the location and development of cross-border routes, seaports, dry ports and airports;
  explain what a dry port is, name one, and explain why dry ports have been developed in many cities
- **7(c) Telecommunications:** explain the importance of radio, television, phones, fax, email and
  the internet to education, industry, services and trade; the problems of providing
  telecommunications in some parts of Pakistan; evaluate their role in development

## How do you describe the pattern of Pakistan's transport networks?

Use the glossary's language: density, and patterns such as linear or radial, located in relation
to provinces and rivers.

- **Densest:** the Indus Plain of central and northern Punjab, where roads form a close grid linking
  Lahore, Faisalabad, Gujranwala, Sialkot and Multan, and the main railway runs linearly along the
  Indus from Karachi to Peshawar.
- **Linear corridors:** in Sindh, routes run north–south along both banks of the Indus (the N-5 and
  N-55 roads, and the main and secondary rail lines), with few links across the Thar desert.
- **Sparse:** most of Balochistan, where a few long roads (Quetta to Karachi, Quetta to Taftan,
  the Makran Coastal Highway) cross empty plateau; and the northern mountains, where routes follow
  narrow valleys such as the Indus and Hunza.
- **Air:** airports are spread widely because air is the only quick link to remote towns such as
  Gilgit, Skardu, Chitral and Turbat.

## What helps and hinders building and maintaining routes?

| Helps | Hinders |
|---|---|
| Flat, stable alluvial plain (Punjab, Sindh) | Steep slopes, landslides and earthquakes in the north and Balochistan |
| Dense population and industry giving traffic and revenue | Low, scattered population giving little traffic |
| Government and foreign funding, e.g. CPEC projects | Monsoon floods washing out roads and bridges (as in 2010 and 2022) |
| Rivers and canals already bridged on main routes | Many rivers and canals to bridge elsewhere; soft soils and waterlogging |
| Strategic need (border areas, ports) | Snow closing passes; extreme heat buckling tracks; shifting sand in deserts |
| | Security problems in some border districts; maintenance budgets too small |

## What improvements have taken place recently?

- **Motorways:** the M-2 Lahore–Islamabad (opened 1997, South Asia's first motorway), the M-1
  Islamabad–Peshawar, the M-4 Pindi Bhattian–Multan, the M-5 Multan–Sukkur (opened 2019), the M-9
  Karachi–Hyderabad, the Lahore–Sialkot motorway and the Hazara Motorway towards Mansehra.
- **Karakoram Highway:** realigned around Attabad Lake (formed by a landslide in 2010) through
  tunnels opened in 2015.
- **Urban mass transit:** bus rapid transit in Lahore, Rawalpindi–Islamabad, Multan, Peshawar and
  Karachi, and Lahore's Orange Line metro train (2020).
- **Rail:** new locomotives and freight trains; the planned upgrade of the main Karachi–Peshawar
  line (ML-1) under CPEC, whose feasibility depends on large loans.
- **Air:** the new Islamabad International Airport (2018) and the New Gwadar International Airport
  (opened for flights in 2025).

## How do road, rail and air compare?

| | Road | Rail | Air |
|---|---|---|---|
| Goods | Door-to-door; carries most freight; suits perishables such as mangoes | Cheapest for bulky goods over long distance (coal, oil, containers, wheat) | Small, high-value or urgent goods (surgical instruments, fresh fish to the Gulf) |
| People | Flexible buses and cars; reaches villages | Cheap long-distance travel; slower; fewer services | Fastest; links remote northern towns; expensive; weather cancellations |
| Drawbacks | Accidents, congestion, road damage by overloaded trucks, fuel imports | Old track and rolling stock, losses, fixed routes | Costly fuel, small loads, limited airports |

## Which routes cross Pakistan's borders?

| Neighbour | Road crossing and pass | Rail link |
|---|---|---|
| China | Karakoram Highway over the Khunjerab Pass (about 4,700 m), usually closed in the winter months | None |
| Afghanistan | Torkham via the Khyber Pass; Chaman (Quetta–Kandahar road, crossing the Khojak area) | Line to Chaman via the Khojak Tunnel, ending at the border |
| Iran | Taftan (Quetta–Zahedan road) | Quetta–Taftan–Zahedan line |
| India | Wagah near Lahore | Wagah–Attari line, with services suspended since 2019 |

**Seaports:** Keamari (Karachi Port) on the natural harbour of Karachi; **Port Qasim**, east of
Karachi, built to relieve congestion and handle bulk cargo such as coal, oil and LNG; **Gwadar**,
a deep-water port in Balochistan developed with Chinese support as the end point of CPEC.
**International airports:** Karachi, Lahore, Islamabad, Peshawar, Quetta, Multan, Faisalabad,
Sialkot (the first privately built, 2007), Gwadar and others; check the current list in your
atlas.

## What is a dry port, and why have so many been built?

A **dry port** is an inland terminal, linked to a seaport by rail or road, where goods are cleared
through customs and packed into or out of sealed containers. Examples: Lahore, Faisalabad,
Sialkot, Multan, Peshawar, Quetta and **Sost** in Gilgit-Baltistan, which handles trade with China
over the Khunjerab Pass. They exist because industry is concentrated inland, far from Karachi:
customs clearance near the factory saves time and theft, reduces congestion at the seaports, and
lets exporters such as Sialkot's sports-goods firms ship directly.

## Why do telecommunications matter for development?

By 2024 Pakistan had roughly 190 million mobile subscriptions and well over 100 million broadband
users, according to the Pakistan Telecommunication Authority. Their uses include:

- **Education:** Allama Iqbal Open University (since 1974) uses radio and TV; the Virtual University
  (2002) teaches online; TV lessons reached children when schools closed in 2020.
- **Industry and trade:** email and the internet let Faisalabad and Sialkot exporters deal with
  buyers abroad, share designs and track shipments; fax is now rarely used.
- **Services:** mobile banking such as Easypaisa and JazzCash, e-commerce, freelancing and call
  centres; farmers check crop prices and weather by phone.
- **Broadcasting:** radio and TV spread news, health campaigns and flood warnings.

**Problems:** high cost of masts and fibre in mountains and deserts; scattered, low-income
populations giving little revenue; power cuts; low literacy limiting internet use; damage from
floods and earthquakes; and occasional security-related shutdowns. The Universal Service Fund pays
operators to extend coverage to unserved areas. **Evaluate:** telecommunications have widened access
to education, banking and markets, but the urban–rural and male–female digital gaps mean benefits
are uneven.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which border crossing links Pakistan with China?**
The Karakoram Highway crosses the Khunjerab Pass, at about 4,700 metres, and is usually closed by
snow for part of the winter, so trade is seasonal.

**What is the difference between Keamari and Port Qasim?**
Keamari is the original Karachi Port on the natural harbour; Port Qasim lies east of Karachi and
was built later to take bulk cargo and relieve congestion.

**Why is rail freight declining when it is cheaper for bulk?**
Old track, too few locomotives, slow services and poor door-to-door links mean many firms choose
trucks despite higher costs.

**Why does Gwadar matter?**
It is a deep-water port close to the Strait of Hormuz and the planned southern end of the
China–Pakistan Economic Corridor, but it still lacks the road, rail, water and power links needed
for heavy traffic.
