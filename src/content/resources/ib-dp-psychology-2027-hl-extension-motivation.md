This page is **higher level (HL) only**. Motivation is one of the three HL extensions and is assessed in **Paper 3**, which SL students do not sit. The guide describes motivation as "the impetus that gives purpose or direction to behaviour", operating at a conscious or unconscious level. Like the other extensions, it is studied inside the four contexts. The guide names self-determination theory but prints no named studies, so the studies below are examples.

Related: [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-hl-extension-motivation-revision-notes/) and [practice set](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-hl-extension-motivation-practice/).

## What the guide says

- **Primary motives** are biological, such as hunger, thirst and the need for sleep. **Secondary motives** are learned or social, such as affiliation, competition, and personal interests and goals.
- **Intrinsic motivation** comes from within (interest, enjoyment). **Extrinsic motivation** comes from outside (rewards or punishments) and can encourage or discourage a behaviour.
- **Measuring primary motives** can use physiological tests. The guide's example is glucose tests to find out about motivation to eat in people with eating disorders.
- **Measuring secondary motives** often uses direct methods, such as asking people why they acted as they did. These rely on self-reported data and are open to **demand characteristics**. Experiments can also test the effect of different external motivators.
- **Self-determination theory (SDT)** explains internal motivation: people's innate growth tendencies and psychological needs shape their choices when there are no external influences.

The guide links motivation to **change** and **causation**, and says motivation for learning, especially intrinsic motivation, has been shown to result in long-term improvements in cognition. Motivation is also linked to the learning and cognition context and to the questioning that drives student inquiry.

## Self-determination theory in detail

Deci and Ryan's SDT proposes three basic psychological needs.

1. **Autonomy:** feeling that you choose and control your own actions.
2. **Competence:** feeling effective and able to master tasks.
3. **Relatedness:** feeling connected to others.

When these needs are supported, motivation becomes more internal and well-being improves. When they are blocked or when rewards and controls are experienced as pressure, motivation shifts towards the external and may fall once pressure is removed. SDT sees motivation as a continuum from external control to intrinsic motivation, rather than two separate boxes.

**Application.** In a school, a teacher who gives choice of project (autonomy), clear and achievable goals with feedback (competence) and group work (relatedness) is supporting intrinsic motivation. In health, a counsellor who helps a client choose their own goals is applying SDT.

**Evaluation.** SDT has support from many studies in education, health and sport. It is often tested by questionnaire, so there are demand characteristics. Critics question whether the three needs are truly universal, or whether the importance of autonomy differs across cultures. It also overlaps with other theories.

## Example studies and theories

**Lepper, Greene and Nisbett (1973).** Preschool children who liked drawing were put into three conditions. One group expected a "good player" award for drawing, one received the award unexpectedly and one received no award. Later, in free play, the children who had expected the reward spent noticeably less time drawing than the other groups. This is called the **overjustification effect**: an external reward for something already enjoyed can reduce intrinsic motivation. Evaluation: a field experiment with a clear control group and a behavioural measure, which reduces demand characteristics; the participants were young children from one nursery, and the effect may depend on how the reward is framed.

**Maslow's hierarchy of needs (1943).** Needs form a pyramid from physiological and safety needs to belonging, esteem and self-actualisation. It links primary and secondary motives. Evaluation: influential and intuitive but hard to test, and people may prioritise differently in different cultures.

**Keys' Minnesota semi-starvation study.** Conscientious objectors in the 1940s were put on a restricted diet. Participants became preoccupied with food and showed changes in mood and behaviour. It is used as evidence for the strength of a primary motive; it also raises ethical issues about harm.

**Goal-setting theory (Locke and Latham).** Specific, challenging goals with feedback generally lead to better performance than easy or vague goals. It is often applied to employee performance and to learning.

**Models of behaviour change in health.** The transtheoretical model (Prochaska and DiClemente) describes stages of change from pre-contemplation to maintenance. It is often used in health, but critics say the stages are not distinct.

**Growth mindset (Dweck).** Believing that ability can be developed is linked with more persistence after setbacks. Evidence for large effects has been mixed, which makes it a good example for discussing replication.

## Applying motivation in each context

- **Health and well-being.** Motivation to change a health behaviour depends on whether the person values the goal and feels able to reach it. A prevention programme for smoking may use information, incentives or support for the person's own reasons. Evaluate by asking how long the change lasted and whether people who volunteered were already motivated (sampling bias).
- **Human development.** Children's motivation changes with age and with the way adults respond. Praise for effort rather than for fixed ability is linked with persistence, but extrinsic rewards for activities children already enjoy may lower interest, as the overjustification research suggests.
- **Human relationships.** In groups and workplaces, fair recognition, clear goals and a sense of belonging are linked with satisfaction and performance. Competition and cooperation both motivate, but in different ways and with different effects on relationships.
- **Learning and cognition.** Interest, a sense of competence and choice support independent learning. Motivation can influence attention and memory because motivated learners engage more with material. How far motivation can be assessed depends on the measure: a questionnaire reports beliefs, while time on task records behaviour.

## Research methods and problems

The guide notes problems that Paper 3 sources may illustrate.

- **Self-report and demand characteristics.** Asking "why did you do that?" may produce answers that please the researcher or fit social expectations. Participants may not know their own motives, especially if they are unconscious.
- **Measurement.** Motivation is a construct. It is operationalised as time spent on a task, effort, persistence, questionnaire scores (for example on intrinsic motivation) or, for primary motives, physiological measures. The extent to which motivation **can be assessed** is itself an area of inquiry.
- **Causality.** Many studies are correlational: motivated students may achieve more, or achievement may increase motivation. This is bidirectional ambiguity.
- **Experiments on external motivators.** They allow causal conclusions about rewards, but lab tasks may lack mundane realism.
- **Bias.** Researcher bias in school-based action research, and sampling bias when only motivated people volunteer.
- **Responsibility.** Manipulating rewards with children or people with health problems requires care.

## Worked mini-analysis (constructed for practice)

A teacher gives points for reading to one class (Class A) but not to another (Class B). After the points scheme ends, the teacher records minutes of free reading per week: Class A 41, Class B 55. She concludes that "rewards reduce motivation".

- **Analysis:** minutes fell below the comparison class, which is consistent with overjustification. The difference is 14 minutes.
- **Limitations:** the classes were not randomly assigned; one teacher who is also the researcher may influence results (researcher bias); only one measure of motivation is used; no baseline before the scheme.
- **Conclusion:** the finding is consistent with the idea that extrinsic rewards can reduce later intrinsic motivation, but the design does not justify "rewards reduce motivation" as a general claim.

## Common mistakes

- Treating intrinsic motivation as always good and extrinsic always bad. Rewards can help when they give useful feedback or the task is not interesting.
- Describing a theory without applying it to the context in the question.
- Confusing primary and secondary motives.
- Stating that motivation "causes" achievement from correlational data.
- Not mentioning demand characteristics when the data are self-reported.
