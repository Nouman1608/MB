## 1.6.1 Users of accounting information

Different readers use the same statements to answer different questions. Your task is to match the information to the question.

| Stakeholder | Typical question | Information that answers it |
|---|---|---|
| Owners | Is my business earning a fair return? | Profit for the year, profit margin, return on capital employed |
| Managers | Where are costs and cash tied up? | Detailed ratios, trends, expense and working capital figures |
| Employees | Is my job secure and is pay affordable? | Profit trend, liquidity, revenue growth |
| Investors | Is this worth buying into or holding? | Return on capital employed, profit trend, comparison with other companies |
| Lenders | Will I be repaid, with interest? | Liquidity, profit from operations, assets available |
| Suppliers | Will I be paid on time? | Current ratio, acid test ratio, trade payables days |
| Customers | Will this business still be trading next year? | Overall stability, liquidity |
| Government | Is the right tax paid and are rules followed? | Profit, revenue, employment cost |
| Public and environmental bodies | What is the effect on the community and surroundings? | Spending on pollution control, jobs provided, local contribution |

Communication matters as much as calculation. Give each reader only what they need, name the ratio and its formula, show the comparison (last year, a similar business or a benchmark), and finish with a one-sentence judgement. A supplier wants one line on payment risk; an owner wants profit and return. Sending a supplier a long profit analysis hides the answer they want.

## 1.6.2 The ratios

Use the appendix formulae exactly. Ratios are not given in the question paper, so learn them.

| Area | Measure | Calculation |
|---|---|---|
| Profitability | Gross profit margin | Gross profit ÷ Revenue × 100 |
| | Profit margin | Profit for the year ÷ Revenue × 100 |
| | Mark-up | Gross profit ÷ Cost of sales × 100 |
| | Expenses to revenue | Expenses ÷ Revenue × 100 |
| | Operating expenses to revenue | Operating expenses ÷ Revenue × 100 |
| | Return on capital employed | Profit from operations ÷ Capital employed × 100 |
| Liquidity | Current ratio | Current assets : Current liabilities, shown as x : 1 |
| | Acid test ratio | (Current assets - inventory) : Current liabilities, shown as x : 1 |
| Efficiency | Non-current asset turnover (times) | Net revenue ÷ Total net book value of non-current assets |
| | Trade receivables turnover (days) | Trade receivables ÷ Credit sales × 365 |
| | Trade payables turnover (days) | Trade payables ÷ Credit purchases × 365 |
| | Inventory turnover (days) | Average inventory ÷ Cost of sales × 365 |
| | Rate of inventory turnover (times) | Cost of sales ÷ Average inventory (the reverse fraction) |

Capital employed adds together issued shares, reserves and non-current liabilities. The appendix gives profit margin with profit for the year, and also as profit for the year (after interest). Use the profit figure the question names. Liquidity ratios are presented as ratios, such as 1.7 : 1.

### Worked example: Hollin Fold Joinery Ltd

Hollin Fold Joinery Ltd makes fitted kitchens. All amounts are in dollars and cover the twelve months to 31 March 2026.

**Statement of profit or loss (extract)**

| | $ |
|---|---|
| Revenue (credit sales 720,000) | 960,000 |
| Opening inventory | 70,000 |
| Purchases (credit purchases 468,000) | 638,000 |
| Closing inventory | (84,000) |
| Cost of sales | (624,000) |
| Gross profit | 336,000 |
| Operating expenses | (252,000) |
| Profit from operations | 84,000 |
| Finance costs | (12,000) |
| Income tax | (14,000) |
| Profit for the year | 58,000 |

**Statement of financial position (extract)**

| | $ |
|---|---|
| Non-current assets (net book value) | 340,000 |
| Inventory | 84,000 |
| Trade receivables | 118,000 |
| Bank | 9,000 |
| Current assets | 211,000 |
| Trade payables | 92,000 |
| Tax payable and accruals | 34,000 |
| Current liabilities | 126,000 |
| Bank loan (non-current liability) | 150,000 |
| Issued shares | 180,000 |
| Reserves | 95,000 |

Net assets are 340,000 + 211,000 - 126,000 - 150,000 = 275,000, which equals shares plus reserves. Capital employed = 180,000 + 95,000 + 150,000 = 425,000.

| Ratio | Working | 2026 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gross profit margin | 336,000 ÷ 960,000 × 100 | 35.0% | 36.0% |
| Mark-up | 336,000 ÷ 624,000 × 100 | 53.8% | 56.3% |
| Profit margin | 58,000 ÷ 960,000 × 100 | 6.0% | 7.8% |
| Return on capital employed | 84,000 ÷ 425,000 × 100 | 19.8% | 24.8% |
| Operating expenses to revenue | 252,000 ÷ 960,000 × 100 | 26.3% | 25.0% |
| Current ratio | 211,000 : 126,000 | 1.7 : 1 | 1.9 : 1 |
| Acid test ratio | (211,000 - 84,000) : 126,000 | 1.0 : 1 | 1.2 : 1 |
| Non-current asset turnover | 960,000 ÷ 340,000 | 2.8 times | 2.7 times |
| Trade receivables turnover | 118,000 ÷ 720,000 × 365 | 59.8 days | 51.3 days |
| Trade payables turnover | 92,000 ÷ 468,000 × 365 | 71.8 days | 64.9 days |
| Inventory turnover | 77,000 ÷ 624,000 × 365 | 45.0 days | 43.1 days |
| Rate of inventory turnover | 624,000 ÷ 77,000 | 8.1 times | 8.5 times |

Average inventory for 2026 is (70,000 + 84,000) ÷ 2 = 77,000. The 2025 column is given to you as prior-year information.

## Evaluating performance

One ratio on its own tells you little. Set it beside last year, a comparable firm or an industry benchmark, then say what it means and why it may have changed.

**Profitability.** Revenue grew by 6.7% (from 900,000 in 2025) yet gross profit margin fell from 36.0% to 35.0%, so cost of sales rose faster than revenue: higher material prices or discounting are possible causes. Operating expenses took a larger share of revenue (26.3% against 25.0%). The two effects explain why profit margin and return on capital employed both fell.

**Liquidity.** The current ratio and acid test ratio fell, but the acid test ratio is still about 1 : 1, so liquid assets roughly cover current liabilities. Bank is only 9,000, so the company relies on collecting receivables.

**Efficiency.** Customers take longer to pay (59.8 days against 51.3), while the company pays suppliers more slowly too (71.8 against 64.9). Slower payment to suppliers is funding slower collection, which is a warning if suppliers tighten terms. Inventory moves at a similar speed.

### Comparing with a benchmark

Suppose a trade body reports these averages for kitchen makers.

| Ratio | Hollin Fold | Benchmark | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gross profit margin | 35.0% | 33.0% | Better |
| Operating expenses to revenue | 26.3% | 24.0% | Worse |
| Return on capital employed | 19.8% | 18.0% | Better |
| Acid test ratio | 1.0 : 1 | 0.8 : 1 | Better |
| Trade receivables turnover | 59.8 days | 45 days | Worse |
| Inventory turnover | 45.0 days | 55 days | Better |

Hollin Fold earns a good gross margin but controls overheads and debt collection less well than the benchmark. Comparison has problems: businesses may use different accounting policies, such as depreciation methods, may have different year ends, sizes and products, and may finance themselves differently. A benchmark is an average, not a target to copy.

## Measures to improve

| Area | Possible measure | Possible drawback |
|---|---|---|
| Profitability | Raise selling prices | Customers may buy elsewhere |
| | Negotiate cheaper materials | Quality or reliability may fall |
| | Cut operating expenses | Staff morale or service may suffer |
| | Sell more higher-margin products | Needs marketing spend |
| Liquidity | Collect receivables faster | Customers may be lost |
| | Reduce inventory | Risk of stockouts |
| | Raise long-term finance to replace short-term | Interest costs rise |
| | Reduce drawings or dividends | Owners and investors may object |
| Efficiency | Tighter credit control, settlement discounts | Discount cost |
| | Just in time ordering | Depends on reliable suppliers |
| | Sell idle non-current assets | Capacity lost for later growth |

### Worked example: testing a measure

Hollin Fold proposes a 2% discount for early payment, hoping to cut receivables to 45 days of credit sales.

Receivables at 45 days = 720,000 × 45 ÷ 365 = 88,767 (to the nearest dollar). Cash released = 118,000 - 88,767 = 29,233.

If customers owing half of credit sales take the discount, it costs 720,000 × 50% × 2% = 7,200 a year. The company must judge whether 29,233 of released cash is worth 7,200 of lost revenue, perhaps because it would let the company repay overdraft-type borrowing or hold more bank. A balanced answer gives both sides and a justified decision.

## Limitations of accounting information

- It records the past at historic cost, so it may not show current values or predict the future.
- Figures rest on estimates and judgements, for example depreciation and inventory valuation, so they can be managed.
- It leaves out non-financial matters: staff skill, reputation, customer satisfaction and environmental effects.
- A year-end snapshot may not be typical, for instance in a seasonal business.
- Ratios depend on how profit, capital employed and other terms are defined, so use the appendix formulae.
- Comparison with other businesses is weakened by different policies, size, products and finance.

## Common errors

- Using profit before tax or profit for the year in return on capital employed instead of profit from operations.
- Leaving out non-current liabilities when finding capital employed.
- Using total sales rather than credit sales or credit purchases for days ratios.
- Using closing inventory for inventory days when the formula uses average inventory.
- Subtracting inventory from current liabilities rather than current assets in the acid test.
- Describing a ratio without a reason, a comparison or a consequence.
