**HL only.** This is the eighth study in the Regional study for the Americas, examined on Paper 3 at Higher Level. Paper 3 asks you to evaluate an argument and reach a judgement. The study has two halves. The first is the United States: why and how it expanded and how its global role changed. The second is **one other country** of the Americas, for the changing global role and for the domestic impact of the two world wars. This guide uses **Canada** as the main example, with Brazil and Argentina for comparison.

## The six lines of inquiry

The guide names six inquiry topics: reasons for US expansionist policy; its impact; the changing regional and global role of the USA; the changing role of one other country; the domestic impact of the First World War on one country; and the domestic impact of the Second World War on one country. The concepts of **cause and consequence**, **continuity and change** and **significance** apply throughout. See the [four concepts guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-four-concepts/).

## Key chronology

- 1890: Mahan publishes *The Influence of Sea Power upon History*; the census declares the frontier closed.
- 1898: Spanish-American War; annexation of Hawaii.
- 1901: Platt Amendment. 1903: Panama breaks from Colombia. 1904: Roosevelt Corollary. 1914: Panama Canal opens.
- 1909 to 1913: Taft's dollar diplomacy. 1913 to 1921: Wilson's moral diplomacy.
- 1914: war in Europe; Canada at war. April 1917: US enters the war.
- 1919 to 1920: Treaty of Versailles; US Senate rejects it.
- 1933: Good Neighbor Policy; Montevideo Conference. 1934: Platt Amendment repealed.
- September 1939: war in Europe; Canada declares war. December 1941: Pearl Harbor.
- August 1945: atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki.

## Reasons for US expansionist foreign policy

**Political factors.** The end of continental expansion in 1890 led some leaders to look overseas. Naval officer Alfred Thayer Mahan argued that a great power needed a strong navy, overseas bases and a canal across Central America. Politicians such as Theodore Roosevelt and Henry Cabot Lodge supported this. The Monroe Doctrine gave a long-standing claim to a US sphere of influence in the Americas.

**Economic factors.** US factories and farms produced more than the home market could take. After the depression of 1893, business leaders and politicians sought overseas markets and investment, especially in Latin America and China. Control of strategic sites, such as a canal and Pacific coaling stations, supported trade.

**Social factors.** Religious leaders such as Josiah Strong spoke of a duty to spread Christianity and "civilisation". Newspapers, notably those of William Randolph Hearst and Joseph Pulitzer, whipped up public support for action in Cuba. A sense of national strength after the Civil War and industrial growth also played a part.

**The role of ideology.** Manifest destiny was extended beyond the continent. Social Darwinism and ideas of Anglo-Saxon superiority were used to justify empire. Some opposed expansion on principle. The Anti-Imperialist League, which included Mark Twain, argued that taking colonies contradicted American ideals.

## Impact of US expansionist foreign policy

**The Spanish-American War (1898).** The US supported Cuban rebels against Spain. After the battleship *Maine* exploded in Havana harbour in February 1898, public opinion pushed Congress to declare war. Within months, the US Navy destroyed Spanish fleets at Manila Bay and Santiago. In the Treaty of Paris (1898), Spain gave up Cuba and ceded Puerto Rico, Guam and the Philippines to the US for $20 million. The Philippines became a source of conflict, as Filipino forces fought against US rule in the Philippine-American War (from 1899). Cuba became nominally independent but under the Platt Amendment (1901), which allowed the US to intervene and lease a naval base at Guantánamo.

**Roosevelt Corollary and "big stick" diplomacy.** In 1904 Roosevelt added to the Monroe Doctrine that the US could act as an "international police power" in the hemisphere if a state showed "chronic wrongdoing". He used it in the Dominican Republic in 1905, where US officials took over customs collection to pay foreign debts. The US supported Panama's separation from Colombia in 1903 and built the Panama Canal, opened in 1914. Critics saw this as bullying.

**Dollar diplomacy.** Under President Taft (1909 to 1913), the US promoted loans and investment to stabilise countries and open markets, backed by the threat of force. US Marines were sent to Nicaragua in 1912 and stayed, with breaks, until 1933.

**Moral diplomacy.** Woodrow Wilson claimed to promote democracy and law, but he also intervened often. He sent troops to Veracruz in 1914, occupied Haiti (from 1915) and the Dominican Republic (from 1916). Historians debate whether his approach really differed from his predecessors'.

## Changing regional and global role of the USA

**The First World War.** The US stayed neutral from 1914, though it traded mainly with the Allies. German submarine warfare, including the sinking of the *Lusitania* (1915) and the resumption of unrestricted attacks in 1917, and the Zimmermann Telegram, pushed it to declare war in April 1917. It sent about two million troops to Europe, and its supplies and finance were decisive in 1918.

**Wilson's peace ideals and the League.** Wilson's Fourteen Points (January 1918) set out principles for peace, including self-determination and a League of Nations. At Versailles, he compromised on many points, and the Senate, led by Henry Cabot Lodge, refused to ratify the treaty in 1919 to 1920, partly from concern about Article 10 and the loss of congressional power to declare war. The US did not join the League. This is often called isolationism, though the US remained active in trade, finance and disarmament (the Washington Naval Conference of 1921 to 1922).

**Good Neighbor Policy.** After Hoover's initial moves, Franklin Roosevelt declared in 1933 that the US would be a good neighbour. At the Montevideo Conference (1933) the US accepted a principle of non-intervention. Marines left Haiti in 1934, the Platt Amendment was repealed, and the US accepted Mexico's oil nationalisation in 1938 without force. The policy reflected the Depression, which made costly interventions hard to defend, concern over rising fascism and a wish for regional unity. Inter-American conferences at Buenos Aires (1936), Lima (1938) and Havana (1940) built cooperation.

**The Second World War.** The Neutrality Acts of the 1930s restricted US involvement. After 1939, the US moved to aid Britain with "cash and carry" and Lend-Lease (1941). Japan's attack on Pearl Harbor in December 1941 brought the US into the war. Its industry became the "arsenal of democracy". The **Manhattan Project** produced the atomic bomb, tested in July 1945, and bombs were dropped on Hiroshima (6 August) and Nagasaki (9 August). The US also took a leading role in building the postwar order: Bretton Woods (1944) and the United Nations (1945).

## Changing role of one other country: Canada

**Participation in the First World War.** Canada entered in 1914 automatically as part of the British Empire. About 620,000 Canadians served and some 60,000 died. The capture of Vimy Ridge in April 1917 became a symbol of national pride. Canada's contribution brought it a separate seat at the Paris Peace Conference and separate membership in the League of Nations (1920).

**League of Nations and autonomy.** In the 1920s Canada asserted independence from Britain: in the Chanak crisis (1922) it refused to commit automatically, and the Statute of Westminster (1931) gave it full legislative independence. Canada was a cautious League member and was wary of binding commitments.

**Second World War.** Canada declared war separately on 10 September 1939, a week after Britain. About one million served, and Canada became a major supplier of food, munitions and ships. Canada worked more closely with the US, as in the Ogdensburg Agreement (1940).

**Economic and trade relations.** The US replaced Britain as Canada's chief trading partner and investor during this period. Comparisons: Brazil declared war in 1917 and in 1942 and sent an expeditionary force to Italy in 1944, while Argentina stayed neutral until 1945.

## Domestic impact of the First World War on Canada

**Economic and social.** War orders boosted industry and agriculture, and the government raised funds through Victory Bonds and introduced a "temporary" income tax in 1917. Inflation hurt workers. The Halifax Explosion (December 1917) killed about 2,000 people. The influenza pandemic of 1918 added to the toll.

**Political.** Volunteering fell, and the Conservative government introduced conscription in 1917. English Canada largely supported it, while French Canada largely opposed it, causing a deep split and riots in Quebec in 1918. A Union government was formed. The Wartime Elections Act gave the vote to female relatives of soldiers and removed it from some recent immigrants.

**Women.** Women worked in munitions factories, as nurses and in offices. Many women won the vote federally by 1918.

**Marginalised groups.** Indigenous men volunteered in large numbers despite not having full citizenship. Ukrainian Canadians and others from enemy states were registered, and some were interned. Black Canadians were at first refused as recruits, and a segregated construction battalion was later formed.

## Domestic impact of the Second World War on Canada

**Economic and social.** Industry expanded under C. D. Howe's Department of Munitions and Supply. The government controlled wages and prices, rationed goods and borrowed heavily. Unemployment disappeared. The government introduced family allowances in 1944.

**Political.** The 1942 plebiscite on conscription showed the divide again: most English Canadians voted yes, and most Quebec voters no. The government avoided overseas conscription until late 1944.

**Women.** Hundreds of thousands of women worked in factories, including the "Bren Gun Girl". Women served in the armed forces' women's divisions. Many were encouraged to leave jobs after 1945.

**Marginalised groups.** About 22,000 Japanese Canadians were removed from the British Columbia coast, their property sold and many sent to camps. Indigenous people served in the forces, but still lacked equal rights. These experiences contributed to postwar rights movements.

## Arguments for Paper 3

- Was US expansion driven mainly by economics, security or ideology?
- Was Wilson different from Roosevelt and Taft, or did all three share the same aims?
- Was the Good Neighbor Policy a real change or a change of method?
- Did the wars strengthen national unity in Canada, or divide it?

## Common mistakes

- Treating the Spanish-American War as the whole of US expansion.
- Forgetting the second country.
- Writing narrative of wartime events without domestic impact.
