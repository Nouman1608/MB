This guide covers sub-topic **8.3 Transpiration** of the Cambridge IGCSE Biology 0610 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. Statements 1 to 3 are Core and can be tested on every paper. Statements 4 to 7 are Supplement content, so they are **Extended only** (Papers 2 and 4). Transpiration is a named experimental context for Paper 5 and Paper 6, so method and data questions are common.

The [transport in plants study guide](/resources/igcse-biology-0610-transport-in-plants/) gives the definitions, a factors table, and worked examples on mass loss in a fan and a potometer bubble reading. This page adds what those leave out: why water vapour moves out of a leaf at all, a full potometer method with its sources of error, a temperature data set, a petroleum-jelly experiment on stomata, the transpiration pull as a chain of steps, and combined-factor explanations. Condense it with the [transpiration revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-transpiration-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [transpiration practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-transpiration-practice/).

## Why water leaves the leaf (Core)

Mesophyll cells are covered in a thin film of water. Water **evaporates** from these wet surfaces into the **air spaces**, so the air inside the leaf holds a lot of water vapour. The air outside usually holds less. Water vapour therefore **diffuses** from the air spaces, **through the stomata**, into the outside air, down a concentration gradient.

Keep the two processes in their places: **evaporation** happens inside the leaf at the cell surfaces; **diffusion** carries the vapour out through the stomata. The cuticle is waxy, so very little water escapes through the rest of the leaf surface.

## Investigating temperature and wind speed (Core)

### A potometer method

1. Cut a leafy shoot **under water**, so no air enters the xylem.
2. Fit the shoot into the potometer **under water**, so the apparatus is full of water with no air bubbles except the one used for measuring.
3. Seal the joint between shoot and tube with petroleum jelly so it is **airtight**.
4. Dry the leaves gently, then leave the shoot for a few minutes to settle in the conditions being tested.
5. Introduce an air bubble into the capillary tube and record its starting position on the scale.
6. Measure how far the bubble moves in a set time, such as 5 minutes.
7. Reset the bubble using the reservoir and repeat at least twice; calculate a mean.

**Varying wind speed:** place a fan at different distances from the shoot, or use its different speed settings, and keep the room temperature the same.
**Varying temperature:** use rooms or a controlled chamber at different temperatures, with no fan, keeping the same shoot.

### What a potometer actually measures

A potometer measures **water uptake** by the shoot, not water loss. Almost all the water taken up is lost by transpiration, but a little is used by the cells, for example in photosynthesis. Uptake is therefore a close estimate of the transpiration rate.

### Sources of error to mention

- Air leaking in at the joint makes the bubble move without water being taken up.
- Air in the cut xylem blocks the flow.
- Different shoots have different numbers of leaves, so use the same shoot for each condition.

### Worked example: temperature data

The bubble readings below are for practice only. Each value is the distance moved in 5 minutes.

| Temperature / °C | Trial 1 / mm | Trial 2 / mm | Trial 3 / mm | Mean rate / mm per min |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 15 | 19 | 21 | 20 | 4.0 |
| 20 | 29 | 31 | 30 | 6.0 |
| 25 | 41 | 39 | 43 | 8.2 |
| 30 | 52 | 56 | 54 | 10.8 |

Working for 25 °C: (41 + 39 + 43) ÷ 3 = 123 ÷ 3 = 41.0 mm in 5 min; 41.0 ÷ 5 = **8.2 mm per min**.

A Core description: as temperature increases from 15 °C to 30 °C, the rate of transpiration increases, from 4.0 to 10.8 mm per min. Between 15 °C and 30 °C the rate is 10.8 ÷ 4.0 = 2.7 times faster.

## Stomata and internal surface area (Extended only)

- The **interconnecting air spaces** between mesophyll cells give a **large internal surface area** of wet cell surface. The larger this surface, the more water can evaporate into the air spaces, so more water vapour is available to diffuse out.
- **More stomata**, or **larger stomata** (wider open), give more area through which water vapour can diffuse out, so the rate of water vapour loss is greater.

### Worked example: petroleum jelly on leaves

Four similar leaves were hung up for 24 hours. Petroleum jelly, which water vapour cannot pass through, was spread on some surfaces. The masses are for practice only.

| Leaf | Jelly on | Start mass / g | End mass / g | Percentage loss |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | no surface | 2.40 | 1.92 | 20.0% |
| B | upper surface | 2.50 | 2.05 | 18.0% |
| C | lower surface | 2.45 | 2.38 | 2.9% |
| D | both surfaces | 2.50 | 2.48 | 0.8% |

Working for leaf C: 2.45 − 2.38 = 0.07 g; 0.07 ÷ 2.45 × 100 = **2.9%**.

Explanation: covering the lower surface (C) cuts water loss far more than covering the upper surface (B), so most water vapour leaves through the **lower** surface. This is because most **stomata** are in the lower epidermis. Percentage loss is used because the leaves started at different masses.

## The transpiration pull (Extended only)

Write it as a chain:

1. Water evaporates from mesophyll cells and diffuses out through the stomata.
2. Water moves into those mesophyll cells from the xylem in the leaf veins.
3. This creates a **transpiration pull** on the water in the xylem.
4. Water molecules are held together by **forces of attraction between water molecules**, so the water in the xylem moves as a **continuous column**.
5. The whole column is drawn up the stem from the roots.

The structure of xylem fits this: vessels with no cross walls and no cell contents form a continuous tube, and lignified walls stop them collapsing under the pull (see [xylem and phloem](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-xylem-and-phloem/)).

## Explaining the three factors (Extended only)

| Factor increased | Effect | Chain |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | rate increases | water molecules gain kinetic energy → faster evaporation from mesophyll surfaces → faster diffusion out |
| Wind speed | rate increases | moving air removes water vapour from around the leaf → concentration gradient stays steep → faster diffusion |
| Humidity | rate decreases | more water vapour in the outside air → shallower concentration gradient → slower diffusion |

### Combining factors

**A plant loses water fastest on a hot, dry, windy afternoon and slowest on a cool, humid, still night. Explain.** On the afternoon, high temperature speeds evaporation, low humidity gives a steep gradient, and wind keeps it steep by removing vapour. At night all three work the other way. Each factor needs its own linked reason.

## Wilting (Extended only)

**Why:** wilting happens when the plant loses water by transpiration faster than it takes water up, for example in hot, dry, windy weather or dry soil.

**How:** cells lose water, become **flaccid** instead of turgid, and no longer press out against their cell walls, so the leaves and soft stems lose support and droop.

Plants often wilt in the hottest part of the day and recover in the evening, when transpiration slows and uptake catches up, so the cells become turgid again.

## Common mistakes

- "Water evaporates through the stomata." It evaporates inside the leaf and diffuses out through the stomata.
- Treating potometer readings as water loss without noting that they measure uptake.
- Comparing leaves by mass lost instead of percentage lost when start masses differ.
- (Extended) Saying the roots push water up. The pull comes from the leaves.
- (Extended) Saying wind "blows water off the leaf" with no concentration gradient.
- (Extended) Explaining humidity backwards: high humidity slows transpiration.
- (Extended) Explaining wilting with "the plant has no water" and no mention of turgor or flaccid cells.

## Exam technique for 8.3

- **Describe** results: trend + values with units.
- **Explain** a factor (Extended): factor → evaporation or concentration gradient → diffusion rate.
- **Investigate** questions: name the independent variable, how it is varied, what is measured, two controlled variables, repeats.
- **Calculate**: mean, then rate = distance ÷ time; percentage change = change ÷ start × 100.
