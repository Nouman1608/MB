## 3.1 Kinetic energy, potential energy, work and power

**Kinetic energy** of a particle of mass m and speed v is ½mv². **Gravitational potential energy** is mgh, where h is the height above a chosen zero level. Only changes in potential energy matter, so choose the zero level that makes the working easiest.

**Work done by a force** is the force multiplied by the distance moved in the direction of the force. For a constant force F moved through a distance d along its own line of action, work = Fd. If the force makes an angle θ with the direction of motion, work = Fd cos θ.

**Power** is the rate of doing work. For a force F acting on an object moving at speed v in the direction of F, power = Fv. Engine power P gives a driving force of P/v.

### The work-energy principle

Work done by driving forces, minus work done against resistances, equals the change in total mechanical energy (KE plus PE):

```
Work done by the driving force − work done against resistance
   = (change in KE) + (change in PE)
```

If there are no driving forces other than gravity and no resistance, mechanical energy is conserved: KE + PE stays constant. This is the **principle of conservation of mechanical energy**.

### Method

1. Sketch the motion, mark the start and end, and choose a zero level for PE.
2. Write KE and PE at the start and end.
3. Find the work done by each driving force and against each resistance.
4. Substitute into: initial energy + driving work − resistance work = final energy.

### Worked example 1: constant force and resistance on a slope

A crate of mass 12 kg is pulled from rest up a rough plane inclined at 20° to the horizontal, along the steepest direction. A constant force of 90 N acts parallel to the slope. The constant resistance to motion is 25 N. Find the speed of the crate after it has moved 15 m.

```
Work done by the 90 N force = 90 × 15 = 1350 J
Work done against resistance = 25 × 15 = 375 J
Gain in PE = 12 × 9.8 × 15 sin 20° = 603.3 J
Work-energy: 1350 − 375 = ½ × 12 × v² + 603.3
½ × 12 × v² = 371.7
v² = 61.95
v = 7.87 m s⁻¹ (3 s.f.)
```

### Worked example 2: power on a hill

A car of mass 900 kg has engine power 24 kW. The resistance to motion is a constant 600 N. The road is inclined at α to the horizontal where sin α = 1/20. Find (a) the steady speed up the hill, (b) the acceleration up the hill when the speed is 15 m s⁻¹.

(a) At a steady speed the driving force equals the sum of resistance and the weight component down the slope.

```
Driving force F = 600 + 900 × 9.8 × (1/20) = 600 + 441 = 1041 N
P = Fv, so v = 24 000 / 1041 = 23.05
Steady speed = 23.1 m s⁻¹ (3 s.f.)
```

(b) At 15 m s⁻¹ the driving force is 24 000 / 15 = 1600 N.

```
F − 600 − 441 = 900a
1600 − 1041 = 900a
a = 559/900 = 0.621 m s⁻² (3 s.f.)
```

### Worked example 3: conservation of energy, then resistance

A particle of mass 0.8 kg slides from rest down a smooth curved slide, dropping through a vertical height of 2.5 m. It then moves on rough horizontal ground against a constant resistance of 4 N. Find its speed at the bottom of the slide and the distance it travels on the ground before it stops.

```
Smooth slide: mechanical energy conserved
½ × 0.8 × v² = 0.8 × 9.8 × 2.5 = 19.6
v² = 49, so v = 7 m s⁻¹
On the ground: work against resistance = loss of KE
4 × d = 19.6
d = 4.9 m
```

## 4.1 Momentum, impulse and conservation of momentum

Momentum is a vector: **p = mv**. For velocities written as i and j components, multiply each component by the mass.

The **impulse-momentum principle** states that impulse equals final momentum minus initial momentum:

```
I = mv − mu
```

with u the starting velocity and v the final velocity. Impulse is a vector in the direction of the net force. For a constant force F acting for time t, I = Ft.

### Worked example 4: impulse as a vector

A ball of mass 0.4 kg moving with velocity (5i − 2j) m s⁻¹ is struck so that its velocity becomes (−i + 4j) m s⁻¹. Find the impulse and its magnitude. If the contact lasted 0.05 s, find the average force.

```
I = 0.4[(−i + 4j) − (5i − 2j)] = 0.4(−6i + 6j) = (−2.4i + 2.4j) N s
|I| = 2.4√2 = 3.39 N s (3 s.f.)
F = I / t = (−2.4i + 2.4j) / 0.05 = (−48i + 48j) N
```

### Conservation of linear momentum

When two particles collide and no external horizontal impulse acts, total momentum is unchanged:

```
m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂
```

This holds as a vector equation. In straight-line problems pick one direction as positive and give every velocity a sign.

### Worked example 5: coalescing particles

Particle A (mass 2 kg, velocity (3i + 2j) m s⁻¹) collides with particle B (mass 3 kg, velocity (−i + 4j) m s⁻¹). They join and move together. Find the common velocity, the speed, and the loss of kinetic energy.

```
Total momentum = 2(3i + 2j) + 3(−i + 4j) = (3i + 16j)
5v = 3i + 16j, so v = (0.6i + 3.2j) m s⁻¹
Speed = √(0.36 + 10.24) = √10.6 = 3.26 m s⁻¹ (3 s.f.)
KE before = ½(2)(13) + ½(3)(17) = 13 + 25.5 = 38.5 J
KE after = ½(5)(10.6) = 26.5 J
Loss = 12 J
```

## 4.2 Direct impact and Newton's law of restitution

In a direct impact the particles move along the line joining their centres before and after. **Newton's law of restitution** relates speeds of separation and approach:

```
(speed of separation) = e × (speed of approach)
```

The **coefficient of restitution** e satisfies 0 ≤ e ≤ 1. If e = 1 the impact is perfectly elastic and no mechanical energy is lost. If e = 0 the particles do not separate, so they move on together. Always use **two equations**: conservation of momentum and the law of restitution.

For velocities v₁ and v₂ along the same positive direction, with particle 1 behind particle 2, the law reads v₂ − v₁ = e(u₁ − u₂).

### Worked example 6: direct impact

Particle A of mass 2 kg moves at 5 m s⁻¹ and collides directly with particle B of mass 3 kg moving at 1 m s⁻¹ in the same direction. The coefficient of restitution is 0.6. Find the speeds after impact and the loss of kinetic energy.

```
Momentum: 2(5) + 3(1) = 2vA + 3vB, so 2vA + 3vB = 13
Restitution: vB − vA = 0.6(5 − 1) = 2.4
Substitute vB = vA + 2.4: 5vA + 7.2 = 13, so vA = 1.16
vB = 3.56
KE before = 25 + 1.5 = 26.5 J
KE after = ½(2)(1.16)² + ½(3)(3.56)² = 1.3456 + 19.0104 = 20.356 J
Loss = 6.144 J
```

Both velocities are positive, so both particles continue forwards. A negative velocity would mean that particle reversed.

## 4.3 Successive impacts

Take each collision in turn. After every impact write the new velocities of **all** particles, then decide whether any two will meet. Two particles moving the same way collide if the one behind is faster.

For a collision with a **smooth plane or wall**, treat the wall as an immovable object: the speed after impact is e times the speed before, and the direction reverses. No momentum equation is used for the wall because the wall absorbs momentum.

### Worked example 7: two particles and a wall

A smooth horizontal surface has a fixed vertical wall on the right. Particle B (mass 3 kg) is at rest between particle A (mass 1 kg) and the wall. A is projected towards B at 4 m s⁻¹. The coefficient of restitution between A and B is 0.5 and between B and the wall is 0.6. Show that A and B collide a second time, and find their speeds afterwards.

Take towards the wall as positive.

```
First collision
Momentum: vA + 3vB = 4
Restitution: vB − vA = 0.5 × 4 = 2
Solve: vB = 1.5, vA = −0.5
A moves away from the wall at 0.5 m s⁻¹; B moves towards it at 1.5 m s⁻¹
B hits the wall: rebound speed = 0.6 × 1.5 = 0.9 m s⁻¹ away from the wall
```

Both now move away from the wall. B (speed 0.9) is behind A (speed 0.5) and faster, so they collide again. Take away from the wall as positive.

```
Second collision
Momentum: 1(0.5) + 3(0.9) = a + 3b, so a + 3b = 3.2
Restitution: a − b = 0.5(0.9 − 0.5) = 0.2
Solve: a = 0.95, b = 0.75
```

A moves away at 0.95 m s⁻¹ and B at 0.75 m s⁻¹. A is ahead and faster, so there are no more collisions.

### A ball and a floor

A ball dropped onto a smooth horizontal floor is the simplest plane problem. If it falls through height h, it hits at speed √(2gh) and rebounds at e√(2gh). It then rises to e²h. Dropped from 2 m with e = 0.5, it arrives at 6.26 m s⁻¹, leaves at 3.13 m s⁻¹ and rises to 0.5 m.

## Common errors

- Using the law of restitution with a sign slip. Draw a diagram of before and after with arrows, and label the positive direction.
- Using only one equation in a collision. You need both momentum and restitution.
- Forgetting the PE term on a slope. The weight component along the slope is mg sin α, and the height gained is distance × sin α.
- Mixing up driving force and power. If a speed is given, find F from P = Fv before using F = ma.
- Assuming a collision with a wall conserves momentum. The wall changes the particle's momentum, so only use the restitution law.
- Treating momentum as a scalar when the question gives i and j components. Give vectors as vectors, or magnitude and direction if asked.
