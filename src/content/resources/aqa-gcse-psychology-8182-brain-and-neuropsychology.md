This guide goes deeper on **3.2.3 Brain and neuropsychology**, the third topic in **Paper 2: Social
context and behaviour** of AQA GCSE Psychology (8182), first teaching 2017. Paper 2 lasts 1 hour 45
minutes, is worth 100 marks and counts for 50% of the GCSE. The
[Social Context and Behaviour study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-psychology-social-context-and-behaviour/)
summarises all four Paper 2 topics; this page explains the biology step by step, evaluates the named
studies and shows how to answer application questions.

## What 3.2.3 covers

1. **Structure and function of the nervous system** -- the central nervous system (CNS) and
   peripheral nervous system (PNS); the somatic and autonomic nervous systems; the autonomic nervous
   system and the fight-or-flight response; the James-Lange theory of emotion.
2. **Neuron structure and function** -- sensory, relay and motor neurons; synaptic transmission,
   including excitation and inhibition; Hebb's theory of learning and neuronal growth.
3. **Structure and function of the brain** -- the frontal, temporal, parietal and occipital lobes
   and the cerebellum; localisation of function (motor, somatosensory, visual, auditory and language
   areas); Penfield's study of the interpretive cortex.
4. **An introduction to neuropsychology** -- cognitive neuroscience; CT, PET and fMRI scans;
   Tulving's 'gold' memory study; how neurological damage, such as a stroke or injury, can affect
   motor abilities and behaviour.

## The nervous system

| Division | Made up of | Job |
|---|---|---|
| Central nervous system (CNS) | Brain and spinal cord | Processes information and makes decisions |
| Peripheral nervous system (PNS) | All other nerves | Carries messages between the CNS and the body |
| Somatic nervous system (part of PNS) | Sensory and motor nerves to skeletal muscles | Senses and voluntary movement |
| Autonomic nervous system (part of PNS) | Sympathetic and parasympathetic branches | Involuntary functions such as heart rate, breathing and digestion |

**Fight-or-flight.** When we perceive a threat, the **sympathetic** branch of the autonomic nervous
system is activated and the adrenal glands release **adrenaline**. Heart rate and breathing rate
increase, pupils dilate, blood is diverted to the muscles and digestion slows. This prepares the
body to fight or run. When the threat passes, the **parasympathetic** branch returns the body to its
resting state (sometimes called "rest and digest"). The response was useful for our ancestors facing
physical dangers, but it can be triggered by modern stressors such as exams, where fighting or
running does not help.

**The James-Lange theory of emotion.** Common sense says we see a bear, feel afraid and then our
heart races. James and Lange argued the order is the other way round: we see the bear, our body
reacts (the heart races), and we then **interpret** that bodily change as fear. In their view, the
emotion **is** the perception of the physical change, so without the physical change there would be
no emotion.

*Evaluation:* the theory fits the experience of feeling a jolt of panic a moment after a near miss
while driving. However, many emotions share similar bodily changes (a racing heart can come with
fear, excitement or anger), so physical arousal alone cannot tell us which emotion we feel. People
with spinal cord injuries, who receive reduced feedback from their bodies, still report
experiencing emotions, which challenges the theory.

## Neurons and synaptic transmission

| Neuron | Carries messages | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Sensory | From receptors (for example, in the skin) to the CNS | Mainly in the PNS |
| Relay | Between other neurons | Within the CNS |
| Motor | From the CNS to muscles and glands | From the CNS out to the body |

A neuron has **dendrites** (which receive signals), a **cell body**, an **axon** (which carries the
electrical impulse) and **axon terminals**. Many axons are insulated by a **myelin sheath**, which
speeds the impulse up.

**Synaptic transmission, step by step:**

1. An electrical impulse travels down the axon to the axon terminal of the presynaptic neuron.
2. This causes **neurotransmitters** to be released from vesicles into the **synapse** (the small
   gap between neurons).
3. The neurotransmitter crosses the gap and binds to **receptors** on the postsynaptic neuron.
4. **Excitation**: some neurotransmitters make the next neuron more likely to fire.
   **Inhibition**: others make it less likely to fire. Whether the next neuron fires depends on the
   overall balance of excitatory and inhibitory signals it receives.

**Hebb's theory of learning and neuronal growth.** Hebb (1949) proposed that when neurons are
repeatedly active at the same time, the connections between them become stronger, forming **cell
assemblies**. Learning a skill or a fact therefore physically changes the brain, and frequently used
pathways become more efficient. This is often summarised as "neurons that fire together, wire
together". The theory helps explain why practice and revision work, and it laid the foundation for
the idea of brain **plasticity**.

## Brain structure and localisation of function

| Area | Location | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Frontal lobe | Front of brain | Thinking, planning, decision-making; contains the **motor area** (voluntary movement) and, usually in the left hemisphere, **Broca's area** (speech production) |
| Parietal lobe | Top, behind the frontal lobe | **Somatosensory area**: touch, temperature, pain |
| Occipital lobe | Back of brain | **Visual area** |
| Temporal lobe | Side of brain | **Auditory area** and memory; usually in the left hemisphere, **Wernicke's area** (understanding language) |
| Cerebellum | Below the occipital lobe at the back | Balance and co-ordination of movement |

**Localisation of function** means that particular areas of the brain carry out particular jobs.
The motor and somatosensory areas are arranged so that each side of the brain controls and receives
sensation from the **opposite** side of the body.

**Penfield's study of the interpretive cortex.** Penfield was a neurosurgeon who operated on patients
with severe epilepsy. Because the brain itself has no pain receptors, patients could stay awake
under local anaesthetic. To find areas he must avoid damaging, Penfield stimulated parts of the
cortex with a weak electrical current and recorded what patients reported. When some areas of the
**temporal lobes** were stimulated, a few patients reported vivid experiences such as hearing music
or reliving a past event. Penfield called this region the **interpretive cortex**.

*Evaluation:* the study gave direct evidence of localisation in living, conscious people. However,
only a small minority of patients reported such experiences; all had epilepsy, so their brains may
not be typical; and it is unclear whether the experiences were real memories or something more like
dreams.

## Neuropsychology and scanning

**Cognitive neuroscience** studies how the structure and function of the brain relate to thinking
and behaviour.

| Scan | How it works | Shows | Points to note |
|---|---|---|---|
| CT | Many X-ray images combined | Structure | Quick; good for detecting damage such as tumours or bleeding; uses radiation |
| PET | A radioactive tracer is injected; active areas use more of it | Activity | Shows function; involves radioactivity |
| fMRI | Detects changes in blood oxygen as active areas need more oxygen | Activity | No radiation; detailed images; the person must lie still in a noisy scanner |

**Tulving's 'gold' memory study (1989).** Tulving and colleagues used a PET-type method in which a
small amount of a radioactive form of gold was injected into the bloodstream of a very small number
of participants, including Tulving himself. Participants thought about either **episodic** memories
(personal experiences, such as a holiday) or **semantic** memories (facts, such as history they had
learned). Episodic memories were associated with more blood flow in the **frontal** areas of the
cortex, and semantic memories with more in the **back** of the cortex. This suggests different types
of long-term memory are localised in different areas.

*Evaluation:* the study gave biological evidence that episodic and semantic memory are different.
However, the sample was tiny and included the researcher, who knew what was being tested; the
results were not consistent for every participant; and the difference between episodic and semantic
memories is not always clear-cut.

**Neurological damage.** Damage from a **stroke** (a blocked or burst blood vessel) or a head
**injury** can affect behaviour according to where it happens. Damage to the **motor area** in one
hemisphere can cause weakness or paralysis on the **opposite** side of the body. Damage to Broca's
area can make speech slow and effortful, even though the person understands what is said to them.
Damage to the frontal lobe can affect planning, self-control and personality, as in the
nineteenth-century case of Phineas Gage, whose behaviour was described as changing after an iron rod
passed through his frontal lobe.

## Worked example

*Question: Tom had a stroke in the left side of his brain. Afterwards he could understand his
family's questions but struggled to say words, and his right arm was weak. Explain these effects.
[4]*

A strong answer: "The stroke probably damaged **Broca's area** in the left **frontal lobe** [1],
which is responsible for producing speech, so Tom can understand language but not produce it
easily [1]. It also probably damaged the **motor area** in the left frontal lobe [1]; because each
hemisphere controls the opposite side of the body, damage on the left causes weakness in his right
arm [1]."

## Common mistakes

- Reversing the James-Lange order. Bodily change comes **first**; the emotion is our interpretation.
- Saying relay neurons are in the PNS. They are within the **CNS**.
- Writing "the neuron fires across the synapse". The **neurotransmitter** crosses the gap; the
  impulse is electrical only along the neuron.
- Confusing CT (structure) with PET and fMRI (activity).
- Placing the visual area in the frontal lobe. Vision is the **occipital** lobe.

## Exam technique

For **describe** questions on processes (synaptic transmission, fight-or-flight), use a numbered
sequence with key terms in order. For **explain** questions, link the brain area to the behaviour
("because this area controls..."). For **evaluate** questions on Penfield or Tulving, comment on the
sample, the method and what the results can and cannot show. Labelled diagrams you can sketch in
your head help with the lobes and neuron structure.
