## Facts from the brief

| Item | What the brief states |
|---|---|
| Paper name | Paper 2 (receptive skills) |
| Structure | Separate sections for listening and reading |
| Listening | 25 marks, 25% of the final grade |
| Reading | 40 marks, 25% of the final grade |
| Paper 2 total | 50% of the final grade |
| SL vs HL | Outlines identical; "the nature of the assessment" differs |
| HL expectation | Understand a wider range of language, and more complex language |
| Texts in the course | Written, audio, visual and audio-visual, across five themes |

**Not in the brief:** section timings, number of texts, question formats. Get these from your
teacher and your school's specimen and past papers.

Themes for the model texts in this unit: identities, experiences, human ingenuity, social
organization. Reading lenses from the brief's conceptual understandings: **context, audience,
purpose, meaning, variation**.

## Method in steps: any reading question

1. Circle the question word (*who, why, how many*). It fixes the answer type.
2. Underline the key words. Note the marks.
3. List two or three synonyms for the key words.
4. Scan for the synonyms or the idea.
5. Read the full sentence and the next one.
6. Answer only what is asked, as briefly as the marks allow.

## Method in steps: each format

| Format | Steps |
|---|---|
| Short answer | Find the detail; give the fact, not the paragraph |
| True/false + justification | Decide; quote the **shortest** words that prove it |
| Multiple choice | Find the line; reject each wrong option with a reason |
| Word that means... | Same word class and form; test it in the sentence |
| Reference word | Look back; check agreement; substitute and reread |
| Sentence completion / matching | Fit the meaning **and** the grammar |
| Own-words explanation | Find the line; restate the idea with new vocabulary |
| Inference | Quote → literal meaning → implied meaning |

## Method in steps: listening

- **Before:** read all questions; predict answer types (number, place, reason, feeling).
- **During:** notes and symbols, not sentences. Keep going if you miss one.
- **Traps:** self-corrections, two numbers, options mentioned then rejected.
- **After:** fill gaps from notes; check spelling and agreement in the target language.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Gist vs detail.** Gist is what a text is broadly about. Most questions ask for detail: a time, a
  reason, a person's view.
- **Synonym vs paraphrase.** A synonym swaps one word (*begin* / *start*). A paraphrase restates a
  whole idea (*she could not afford it* / *it was beyond her budget*).
- **Literal vs inferred.** Literal: what the words say. Inferred: what they imply. An inference must
  still rest on quoted words.
- **Inference vs invention.** If you cannot point to the supporting words, it is invention.
- **Justify vs explain.** *Justify* wants evidence (usually a quotation). *Explain* wants a reason in
  your own words.
- **Writer vs quoted speaker.** A view inside quotation marks belongs to the speaker, not the writer.
- **Plan vs result (listening).** *We hoped*, *we thought*, *at first* often introduce what did not
  happen.

## Signal words to know in your target language

| Function | English examples | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Contrast | *but, however, although* | The answer usually follows the contrast |
| Correction | *actually, in fact, sorry, rather* | The first detail is often the distractor |
| Time and change | *still, no longer, by then, finally* | Shows what changed and when |
| Attitude | *only, even, supposedly, sadly* | Carries tone for inference questions |
| Cause | *because, so, as a result* | Answers *why* questions |

Build the same table in your target language, theme by theme.

## Timing formula

```
minutes per mark = (time allowed - checking time) / marks
```

Example: a practice set of 40 marks in 50 minutes, keeping 5 to check: 45 / 40 = 1.125 minutes per
mark, so a 4-mark question gets 4.5 minutes. Use your teacher's real timings for your level.

## Worked reminder: one paragraph, four skills

*Original text written for Marlbridge (human ingenuity / social organization):*

> When our school replaced paper timetables with an app, most teachers welcomed it. Mr Varga did
> not. He kept printing his own copy and pinning it to the classroom door. "An app is fine until the
> battery dies," he said. By November, after two power cuts, three other teachers had quietly asked
> him for copies of their own.

- **Locate:** *How many power cuts were there?* → "after two power cuts" → **two**.
- **Reference:** *it* in "welcomed it" → **the app** (the change to an app).
- **Paraphrase:** Mr Varga's reason → **he did not trust a device that needs power**.
- **Infer:** "quietly" → the teachers **did not want to admit openly that he had been right**.

## The five lenses as questions

The brief names five conceptual understandings. Turn each into a question you ask before answering
anything about a text.

| Lens | Question to ask | What it helps you answer |
|---|---|---|
| Context | Where and when does this appear? | Why a detail matters; what a reference points to |
| Audience | Who is it written for? | Register, formality, why things are explained or left out |
| Purpose | What does the writer want to achieve? | Attitude and inference questions |
| Meaning | What is said, and what is implied? | Literal vs inferred questions |
| Variation | How would this change in another text type or culture? | Comparing two texts on one theme |

## Worked reminder: listening for the real answer

*Original transcript written for Marlbridge (experiences):*

> GUIDE: The museum opens at nine -- well, at half past nine on Sundays -- and the boat leaves from
> the north pier. People often go to the south pier by mistake, so do check the sign.

- **When does the museum open on a Sunday?** Nine is said first, then corrected. **Half past nine.**
- **Where does the boat leave from?** South is mentioned only as a mistake. **The north pier.**
- **Why does the guide mention the sign?** Paraphrase: **people often go to the wrong pier**.

The pattern: a first detail, a signal word (*well*, *by mistake*), then the detail you need. Collect
the target-language equivalents of these signals and listen for them.

## Quick self-test

Questions 3–8 use the Mr Varga paragraph above.

1. How many marks does the Paper 2 listening section carry, and what weighting?
2. What does the brief say is the same, and what differs, between SL and HL assessment?
3. Find the word in the paragraph that means "attaching".
4. What does "his" in "his own copy" refer to?
5. True or false: Mr Varga refused to use paper timetables. Justify with a quotation.
6. Explain in your own words why Mr Varga kept a paper copy.
7. What does "By November" tell you about how opinions changed?
8. Which word class and form must a synonym for "welcomed" have?
9. A speaker says: "The coach leaves at ten -- sorry, quarter past ten." What time does it leave?
10. You have 45 minutes for 30 marks and keep 5 minutes to check. How long should a 3-mark question
    take?
11. Which lens from the brief helps you most with a question about a writer's aim?
12. What is the difference between *justify* and *explain*?

### Answers

1. 25 marks; 25% of the final grade.
2. The assessment outlines are identical; the nature of the assessment differs.
3. **pinning**.
4. **Mr Varga**.
5. **False.** "He kept printing his own copy".
6. He thought the app would stop working if the device lost power.
7. Opinions changed slowly: only after months, and after the power cuts, did other teachers come
   round to his view.
8. A verb in the past tense (for example *greeted*, *accepted*).
9. **Quarter past ten** (10:15). "Sorry" signals the correction.
10. (45 − 5) / 30 = 1.33 minutes per mark, so **4 minutes** for 3 marks.
11. **Purpose.**
12. *Justify* asks for evidence, usually a quotation; *explain* asks for a reason in your own words.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Answering with a nearby but different detail, because it shares a keyword with the question.
- Quoting a whole paragraph for a justification, so the proof is buried or includes a wrong detail.
- Giving a synonym in the wrong form: *refusal* for *refused*, a plural for a singular.
- Leaving out agreement or spelling in target-language answers, so the word no longer matches.
- Treating a quoted speaker's view as the writer's view.
- Stating a literal fact when the question asks what a phrase *suggests*.
- Writing the first number heard before the speaker corrects it.
- Copying the text for an "in your own words" question.
- Losing time on one hard question and rushing the last text.
