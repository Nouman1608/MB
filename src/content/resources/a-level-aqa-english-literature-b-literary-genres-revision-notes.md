[Paper 1 Literary Genres study guide](/resources/a-level-aqa-english-literature-b-literary-genres/).
Paper 1 tests one genre option in depth — Aspects of Tragedy or Aspects of Comedy — across a
Shakespeare play plus further studied texts drawn from drama, prose and poetry.

## Paper 1 at a glance

2 hours 30 minutes, closed book, **75 marks = 40% of the A-level**.

| Section | Focus | Marks |
|---|---|---|
| A | Passage-based, Shakespeare (one question per prescribed play — answer on the one you studied) | 25 |
| B | Essay, Shakespeare (choice of two questions on the play studied) | 25 |
| C | Essay linking two further texts (choice of two questions; one text must be drama, one pre-1900) | 25 |

All three sections are compulsory once the genre option is chosen — but each section itself offers
a choice of question, unlike a fixed single-question paper.

Choose **one** option: Aspects of Tragedy or Aspects of Comedy. Students choose the option
themselves; in practice a centre typically teaches only one, so confirm with your teacher which
genre option your course is actually studying before beginning detailed revision.

## The genre-led mindset

This is not "three separate texts" — it's **one genre, explored through several texts**. Every
answer should engage with the genre's conventions (tragic hero, hamartia, catharsis for tragedy;
comic resolution, misunderstanding, social correction for comedy) and how each text **uses or
departs from** them. This genre-led approach is what distinguishes English Literature B from more
text-led A-Level literature specifications, and examiners actively look for it — a well-written
essay that simply summarises a text's plot and features, without connecting back to the genre
question, underperforms even where the individual textual analysis is strong.

## The three questions

- **Q1 (passage-based, Shakespeare):** close analysis of a set extract, connected to the genre.
- **Q2 (essay, Shakespeare):** a genre-focused essay on the whole play.
- **Q3 (linking essay):** connects two further texts (from drama, prose, poetry) within the genre option.

All three questions draw on the same genre option throughout the paper — there is no switching
between Tragedy and Comedy mid-paper, so the genre-specific conventions and vocabulary you build up
revising for Q1 carry directly into Q2 and Q3.

## Building genre debate

Don't just identify genre features ("this is a tragic hero because...") — **debate** them: does
the text conform to, subvert, or complicate the convention? AQA's AOs explicitly reward this kind
of critical debate over simple feature-spotting. A useful revision habit is to keep a small bank of
critical viewpoints on your genre and texts that you can draw on and, where appropriate, challenge —
since contextual and critical interpretation is credited alongside close textual analysis.

## Where Paper 1 sits in the full qualification

Paper 1 (75 marks, 40%) sits alongside Paper 2 (Texts and Genres, also 75 marks / 40%, but
**open book**) and a teacher-assessed, AQA-moderated non-exam component worth the remaining 20% (two
1,250-1,500 word essays, one of which can be a re-creative response with an accompanying
commentary). Because Paper 1 is closed book while Paper 2 is open book, revision technique needs to
differ between the two: Paper 1 demands a memorised quotation bank the way Paper 2 does not, so
building that bank is a Paper-1-specific priority rather than something that transfers automatically
from Paper 2 preparation.

## Genre conventions worth knowing cold

For **Aspects of Tragedy**: the tragic hero, hamartia (the fatal flaw), hubris, catharsis, and the
inevitability of downfall despite — or because of — the hero's own choices. For **Aspects of
Comedy**: comic resolution, misunderstanding or mistaken identity, social correction, and the
restoration of order by the play or text's end. Treat each of these as a genuine critical question
about your specific texts — does the ending really achieve full catharsis, or does it complicate it?
— rather than a checklist to confirm mechanically is present.

## Exam traps

- Writing about texts in isolation instead of exploring how they relate to the genre and each other.
- Feature-spotting ("this is dramatic irony") without debating what it contributes to the genre reading.
- Ignoring the passage in Q1 and writing a general essay instead of close, extract-based analysis.
- Forgetting AQA has a revised specification for exams from 2027 with a refreshed text list — always confirm which version applies.
- Preparing for Paper 1 as if it were open book like Paper 2, under-investing in a memorised quotation bank.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is AQA English Literature B Paper 1 open book?**
No. Paper 1 Literary Genres is closed book, lasting 2 hours 30 minutes for 75 marks and 40% of the A-level, so you need a memorised quotation bank. Paper 2, Texts and Genres, is open book.

**Can I write about both tragedy and comedy in Paper 1?**
No. You study one genre option, Aspects of Tragedy or Aspects of Comedy, and all three questions draw on that same option, with no switching mid-paper.

**What is the non-exam assessment in AQA English Literature B?**
The non-exam component is worth 20% of the A-level: two teacher-assessed, AQA-moderated essays of 1,250 to 1,500 words each, one of which can be a re-creative response with a commentary.

**Is there a new AQA English Literature B specification?**
These notes say AQA has a revised specification for exams from 2027 with a refreshed text list, so always confirm which version applies to you.
