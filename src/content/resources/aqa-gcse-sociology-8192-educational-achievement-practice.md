These questions cover 3.4.3 Educational achievement, from the AQA GCSE Sociology (8192) specification, assessed in Paper 1: class, gender and ethnicity as factors, functionalist, feminist and Marxist views, Halsey on class-based inequalities and Ball on parental choice and competition between schools. All towns, schools and data in the items are fictional.

## Section A: short questions

**1.** In Halsey, Heath and Ridge's study, how were the class groups defined?

A. By the pupil's examination results
B. By the father's occupation
C. By household income
D. By the type of school attended

**[1]**

**2.** Which term describes middle-class knowledge, tastes and confidence that schools reward?

A. Cultural deprivation
B. Material deprivation
C. Cultural capital
D. Social mobility

**[1]**

**3.** Define the term "marketisation of education". **[2]**

**4.** Identify two pressures on schools that Ball, Bowe and Gewirtz linked to parental choice and league tables. **[2]**

**5.** Describe one limitation of Halsey, Heath and Ridge's study. **[2]**

**6.** Identify and explain one material factor that may affect the achievement of working-class pupils. **[3]**

**7.** Explain the difference between cultural deprivation and cultural capital as explanations of class differences in achievement. **[4]**

## Section B: longer and structured questions

**8.** Study the table. The data are fictional.

> **Rivermouth (a fictional town): pupils achieving grade 4 or above in GCSE English and maths, by free school meal (FSM) eligibility and gender**

| Group | Pupils | Achieved grade 4 or above |
|---|---|---|
| Girls eligible for FSM | 120 | 66 |
| Boys eligible for FSM | 130 | 52 |
| Girls not eligible for FSM | 400 | 300 |
| Boys not eligible for FSM | 380 | 247 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of boys eligible for FSM who achieved grade 4 or above. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the difference in percentage points between girls not eligible for FSM and boys eligible for FSM. Show your working. **[2]**
**(c)** Using the data, explain whether FSM eligibility or gender is linked to the larger gap in achievement in Rivermouth. **[3]**

**9.** Read the item.

> In Haddon Vale (a fictional town), parents can list three secondary schools in order of preference. Priya's parents, both solicitors, visited six schools, read inspection reports and rented a flat near the top school. Jamie's mother works two shifts and put the nearest school first because it is on the bus route.

Using the item and the work of Gewirtz, Ball and Bowe, explain how parental choice could widen class differences in achievement. **[4]**

**10.** Explain two reasons why girls' achievement has risen relative to boys'. **[4]**

**11.** Identify and explain one factor that may affect the educational achievement of some ethnic minority pupils. **[3]**

**12.** Discuss how far sociologists would agree that social class is the most important factor affecting educational achievement. **[12]**

**13.** Discuss how far sociologists would agree that the education system is meritocratic. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** B. By the father's occupation [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* The specification describes the three groups by father's occupation; "household income" is a common wrong guess.

**2.** C. Cultural capital [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Cultural deprivation is the opposite argument: it blames home culture rather than what schools reward.

**3.** Running education like a market, in which schools compete for pupils [1] through parental choice and published league tables [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The second mark needs a mechanism: choice, league tables or competition.

**4.** Pressure to reintroduce streaming and setting [1]; a tendency to focus on the more able pupils [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Both points come from the specification's summary of the study; learn them word for word.

**5.** The sample excluded females [1], which might have made a significant difference to the findings, so the study cannot tell us about girls' class chances [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Alternatively, credit the point that the men were born between 1913 and 1952, so the findings may not describe education today, if it is explained.

**6.** Lack of money for learning resources [1], such as a computer, books or a quiet place to do homework [1], means working-class pupils may complete less homework and revision, lowering their results [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Finish the chain to achievement; naming poverty alone earns one mark.

**7.** Cultural deprivation argues that some working-class families lack the values, attitudes or language that help at school [1], so it locates the cause of underachievement in the home [1]. Cultural capital argues that middle-class knowledge and confidence match what schools reward [1], so it locates the cause in schools favouring middle-class culture rather than working-class culture being deficient [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The difference is where the problem lies; say it directly.

**8. (a)** 52 ÷ 130 × 100 = 40% [1]
**(b)** Girls not eligible for FSM: 300 ÷ 400 × 100 = 75% [1]. 75 − 40 = 35 percentage points [1].
**(c)** The FSM gap is 20 percentage points for girls (75% against 55%) and 25 for boys (65% against 40%) [1]. The gender gap is 10 percentage points among pupils not eligible for FSM (75% against 65%) and 15 among those eligible (55% against 40%) [1]. So in Rivermouth FSM eligibility, a measure of low income, is linked to the larger gap, though gender also matters and the two combine, with FSM boys lowest [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote figures for both comparisons before you judge; a conclusion without numbers cannot reach full marks.

**9.** Gewirtz, Ball and Bowe found that middle-class parents are better placed to use choice [1]. Priya's parents are skilled choosers: they have the knowledge to read inspection reports and the time to visit six schools [1]. They also have the money to rent a flat near the top school, securing a place there [1]. Jamie's mother is a disconnected chooser who lacks time and picks the nearest school, so successful schools fill with middle-class pupils and the class gap in achievement may widen [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each mark pairs an item detail with a feature of the study.

**10.** First, feminism changed girls' ambitions [1]; Sharpe found girls' priorities shifted from love and marriage in the 1970s towards jobs and careers by the 1990s, so they had more reason to succeed at school [1]. Second, equal opportunities laws and policies, such as the Sex Discrimination Act 1975 [1], and a national curriculum making core subjects compulsory for all, reduced barriers girls had faced [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Two different reasons are needed; two versions of "feminism" earn only half the marks.

**11.** An ethnocentric curriculum [1] centres one culture's history, language and values [1], so some ethnic minority pupils may feel their culture is ignored, lose interest in school and achieve less [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Material factors, language and institutional racism are also credited if explained through to achievement.

**12.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Halsey, Heath and Ridge found clear class inequalities in education [1].
- A service-class boy was eleven times as likely as a working-class boy to attend university [1].
- Material deprivation limits resources for learning, such as a computer or quiet space [1].
- Cultural capital gives middle-class pupils an advantage because schools reward their culture [1].
- Ball and colleagues showed middle-class parents are better placed to use parental choice [1].
- Marxists argue class is the main factor and schools hide this with the myth of meritocracy [1].
- Against: girls now outperform boys at most levels, so gender is also a major factor [1].
- Feminism and equal opportunities policies help explain girls' rising achievement [1].
- Achievement varies between ethnic groups, linked to the ethnocentric curriculum and institutional racism [1].
- The factors overlap: some ethnic gaps may partly be class gaps [1].
- Halsey's sample was male only and historical, so it cannot settle the question for today [1].
- Judgement: class shows the widest and most persistent gaps, but it works together with gender and ethnicity, so it is the most important single factor rather than the only one [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* "Most important" asks you to rank factors; an answer that lists them without comparing them stays in the middle band.

**13.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Meritocracy means rewards based on ability and effort rather than background [1].
- Functionalists such as Parsons argue schools operate on meritocratic principles, with status achieved [1].
- Public examinations treat every candidate by the same rules, supporting the functionalist view [1].
- Girls' rising achievement shows that formal barriers can be removed [1].
- Against: Halsey, Heath and Ridge found large class gaps, which differences in ability are unlikely to explain [1].
- Material deprivation means pupils do not compete on equal terms [1].
- Cultural capital means schools reward the culture of the middle class [1].
- Ball and colleagues found middle-class parents better placed to use choice [1].
- Marxists call meritocracy a myth that legitimises class inequality [1].
- Feminists argue gendered subject choice and the glass ceiling mean equal results do not bring equal rewards [1].
- Institutional racism may disadvantage some ethnic minority pupils [1].
- Judgement: schools have meritocratic features, but class, gender and ethnicity still shape outcomes, so meritocracy is only partly achieved [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* Use the three perspectives the specification names for 3.4.3: functionalist, Marxist and feminist.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Applying Halsey's figures to girls, or to "all pupils".
- Mixing up Ball's two studies.
- In data questions, giving a conclusion without quoting the figures.
- Confusing a percentage with a difference in percentage points.
- Naming a factor without explaining how it changes achievement.
- In "most important" questions, never comparing factors.
