## Questions

**1.** Define free trade. **[1]**

**2.** Distinguish between a tariff and a quota. **[2]**

**3.** Country J imports washing machines priced at 300 units. It then imposes a tariff of 15%.
**(a)** Calculate the new price of an imported washing machine. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain **one** effect of the tariff on domestic producers of washing machines. **[2]**

**4.** Explain **two** benefits to a country of joining a free-trade agreement. **[4]**

**5.** Explain **one** argument against free trade. **[3]**

**6.** Explain how new technology has contributed to globalisation. **[3]**

**7.** Define a multinational company. **[1]**

**8.** Explain **one** benefit and **one** drawback of globalisation for workers in a less developed country. **[4]**

**9.** Explain **one** drawback of globalisation for UK workers. **[3]**

**10.** A UK clothing retailer buys from factories abroad that pay very low wages. Explain **one** ethical issue this raises. **[3]**

**11.** "Globalisation has been good for UK consumers but bad for UK workers." Evaluate this statement. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Trade between countries without barriers such as tariffs or quotas [1]. **[1]**

**2.** A tariff is a tax on imports [1]; a quota is a limit on the quantity of a good that can be imported [1]. **[2]**

**3. (a)** 300 × 1.15 = **345 units** [1].
**(b)** Imports become dearer, so domestic machines are more price-competitive [1]; domestic producers may sell more and protect jobs [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Any two developed: exporters gain tariff-free access to members' markets [1], so they can sell more and grow [1]; consumers get cheaper imports [1] and more choice [1]; the country may attract investment [1] from firms wanting access to the whole market [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Any one developed: domestic industries may not compete with cheaper imports [1], so firms close [1] and workers become structurally unemployed [1]; or infant industries need protection [1] until they can compete [1], otherwise they fail before they grow [1]. **[3]**

**6.** The internet and better communications make it cheap to contact suppliers and customers worldwide [1]; container shipping cuts transport costs [1], so firms can produce and sell across many countries [1]. **[3]**

**7.** A company that operates in more than one country [1]. **[1]**

**8.** Benefit: new jobs from MNC investment [1], often paying more than local alternatives [1]. Drawback: some jobs have low pay or poor conditions [1], and local firms may be pushed out [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Firms may move production to countries with lower costs [1], so factories in the UK close [1], leaving workers whose skills may not match new jobs unemployed [1]. **[3]**

**10.** The low price to UK shoppers depends on workers abroad being paid very little [1]; the retailer may be seen as exploiting them [1], and consumers may question whether it should check its suppliers' wages and safety [1]. **[3]**

**11.** *Consumers:*
- Lower prices because goods are made where costs are lowest [1].
- Wider choice of goods from around the world [1].
- But supply chains can be disrupted, and some people value locally made goods [1].

*Workers:*
- Some UK workers lost jobs when production moved abroad [1], especially in manufacturing [1].
- But others gained jobs in export industries and in MNCs based in the UK [1], and in services the UK sells abroad [1].

*Judgement:* The statement is partly true: consumers have gained widely, while the effect on workers depends on the industry, so it is too simple to say globalisation is bad for workers as a whole [1] [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing tariffs with quotas.
- Listing benefits and drawbacks without saying which group is affected.
- Writing about globalisation in general when the question names a particular country or group.
- Missing the moral or ethical angle when the question asks for it.
