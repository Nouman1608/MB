These questions cover quantitative skill QS4, "construct and interpret a range of standard graphical forms", from section 3.5 of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Economics (9640) specification. Where a question asks you to construct a graph, sketch it on paper and check it against the description in the answer. Every country and figure is invented; "cu" means currency units. Read the [QS4 study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-economics-9640-qs4-quantitative-skills/) first, or refresh the methods with the [QS4 revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-economics-9640-qs4-quantitative-skills-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** State what is measured on each axis of a Lorenz curve diagram. **[2]**

**2.** On a production possibility diagram, an economy moves from 15 thousand cars and 60 thousand tractors to 25 thousand cars and 52 thousand tractors. Calculate the opportunity cost of one car in tractors. **[1]**

**3.** Explain what a point outside a production possibility boundary represents. **[1]**

**4.** A straight-line consumption function passes through the points (50, 70) and (250, 190), where income and consumption are in cu billion. Calculate the marginal propensity to consume. **[2]**

**5.** State one reason why the vertical axis of a bar chart comparing sales should normally start at zero. **[1]**

## Section B

**6.** Country E can produce these combinations of consumer goods and capital goods (thousand units) when all resources are fully used.

| Combination | P | Q | R | S | T |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Consumer goods | 0 | 20 | 40 | 60 | 80 |
| Capital goods | 80 | 76 | 66 | 48 | 0 |

**(a)** Describe how you would construct the production possibility diagram from these data. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the opportunity cost of one unit of consumer goods for each move from P to Q, Q to R, R to S and S to T. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain what these results show about the shape of the boundary, and why it has this shape. **[2]**

**(d)** State what the point (40, 50) represents. **[1]**

**7.** The poorest to richest fifths of households in Country F receive 3%, 7%, 13%, 22% and 55% of income.

**(a)** Calculate the cumulative shares of income needed to plot the Lorenz curve. **[2]**

**(b)** Compare the share of income received by the poorest 60% of households with the share they would receive under perfect equality. **[1]**

**(c)** After reforms, the shares become 5%, 9%, 15%, 23% and 48%. Explain how the Lorenz curve and the Gini coefficient change. **[2]**

**8.** Using an AD/AS diagram, explain the likely effect of a fall in business confidence on the price level and real national output. Describe the diagram you would draw. **[4]**

**9.** Country G's budget balance, as a percentage of GDP, over five years was −4.5, −3.0, −1.5, +0.5 and −0.5.

**(a)** State the year with the largest deficit. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the change in the balance from year 2 to year 4, in percentage points. **[1]**

**(c)** Describe the trend shown by a bar chart of these data. **[2]**

**10.** A firm's total cost is 2,000 cu at an output of 100 units and 2,600 cu at an output of 140 units.

**(a)** Calculate the gradient of the total cost curve between these outputs. **[2]**

**(b)** State what this gradient represents. **[1]**

**11.** State the most suitable graphical form for each of the following. **[4]**

(i) The inflation rate in each of the last ten years.

(ii) The shares of government spending going to different departments in one year.

(iii) The relationship between spending on education and GDP per capita across 30 countries.

(iv) How unequally income is distributed across all households.

**12.** Using the data in Question 6, the government moves resources so that the economy goes from combination S to combination R. Explain the opportunity cost of this move and its likely effect on the production possibility boundary in the future. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Horizontal: the **cumulative percentage of households (or population)**, from poorest to richest [1]. Vertical: the **cumulative percentage of income** [1]. Total: 2

**2.** 8 thousand tractors are given up for 10 thousand cars, so the opportunity cost is 8/10 = **0.8 tractors per car** [1]. Total: 1

**3.** A combination that cannot be produced with the economy's current resources and technology [1]. Total: 1

**4.** MPC = gradient = (190 − 70)/(250 − 50) [1] = 120/200 = **0.6** [1]. Total: 2

**5.** A truncated axis exaggerates differences, making small changes look large [1]. Total: 1

**6. (a)** Put consumer goods on the horizontal axis and capital goods on the vertical axis, each labelled in thousand units, with even scales [1]. Plot the five combinations and join them with a smooth curve, labelled as the production possibility boundary [1].

**(b)** P to Q: 4/20 = **0.2**; Q to R: 10/20 = **0.5** [1]. R to S: 18/20 = **0.9**; S to T: 48/20 = **2.4** capital goods per unit of consumer goods [1].

**(c)** The opportunity cost rises as more consumer goods are produced, so the boundary gets steeper and is concave (bowed outwards) [1]. This is because resources are not equally suited to producing both goods, so the resources moved last are the least suited to consumer goods [1].

**(d)** At 40 consumer goods the economy could produce 66 capital goods, so (40, 50) lies inside the boundary and shows unemployed or inefficiently used resources [1]. Total: 7

**7. (a)** **3%, 10%, 23%, 45%, 100%** [2] (one mark for three correct values).

**(b)** The poorest 60% receive 23% of income, compared with 60% under perfect equality [1].

**(c)** The new cumulative shares are 5%, 14%, 29%, 52%, 100%, so the curve moves closer to the line of equality at every point [1]. Inequality has fallen and the Gini coefficient is lower [1]. Total: 5

**8.** The diagram has the price level on the vertical axis and real national output on the horizontal axis, with AD₁ and an upward-sloping SRAS [1]. Lower confidence reduces investment, so AD shifts left to AD₂ [1]. The new equilibrium is where AD₂ meets SRAS [1]. The price level falls from P₁ to P₂ and real output falls from Y₁ to Y₂ (or the rate of inflation slows) [1]. Total: 4

**9. (a)** **Year 1**, with a deficit of 4.5% of GDP [1].

**(b)** +0.5 − (−3.0) = **+3.5 percentage points** [1].

**(c)** The deficit narrows steadily from year 1 to year 3 and turns into a small surplus in year 4 [1], before returning to a small deficit in year 5, so the improvement was not sustained [1]. Total: 4

**10. (a)** Gradient = (2,600 − 2,000)/(140 − 100) [1] = 600/40 = **15 cu per unit** [1].

**(b)** The extra cost of producing one more unit over this range: the (average) marginal cost [1]. Total: 3

**11.** (i) **Line graph** [1]. (ii) **Pie chart** (or bar chart) [1]. (iii) **Scatter graph** [1]. (iv) **Lorenz curve** [1]. Total: 4

**12.** The economy gains 18 thousand capital goods and gives up 20 thousand consumer goods, which is the opportunity cost [1]. Lower consumption now means lower current living standards [1]. More capital goods raise future productive capacity, so the boundary is likely to shift outwards in later years [1]. Total: 3

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving the opportunity cost the wrong way up, such as consumer goods per capital good.
- Leaving out the line of equality, or starting a Lorenz curve away from the origin.
- Drawing an AD/AS diagram with "price" and "quantity" on the axes.
- Describing a shift without saying which curve shifts and in which direction.
- Calling a smaller deficit a surplus.
- Giving a graphical form without matching it to the question it answers.
