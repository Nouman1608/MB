This set practises **3.3.3.1 Nucleophilic substitution** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, assessed in Unit 2 and in the synoptic Unit 5. Revise first with the [nucleophilic substitution study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-3-1/) or the [nucleophilic substitution revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-3-1-revision-notes/). Take Ar values as H = 1.0, C = 12.0, N = 14.0, O = 16.0, Cl = 35.5, K = 39.1 and Br = 79.9.

## Section A: short questions

**1.** Which species is **not** a nucleophile?

A: OH⁻

B: NH₃

C: CN⁻

D: H⁺ **[1]**

**2.** Explain why the carbon atom bonded to chlorine in chloroethane is attacked by nucleophiles. **[2]**

**3.** Give the reagent and conditions needed to convert 1-bromopentane into pentan-1-ol. **[2]**

**4.** Name the organic product formed when each halogenoalkane is heated under reflux with potassium cyanide in ethanol and water.

**(a)** iodomethane **[1]**

**(b)** 2-bromopropane **[1]**

**(c)** 1-chloro-2,2-dimethylpropane, (CH₃)₃CCH₂Cl **[1]**

**5.** Write the overall equation for the reaction of 1-iodobutane with excess ammonia. **[1]**

**6.** Put 1-bromohexane, 1-chlorohexane and 1-iodohexane in order of increasing rate of hydrolysis by aqueous hydroxide ions. **[1]**

## Section B: structured questions

**7.** 2-Chlorobutane reacts with warm aqueous sodium hydroxide.

**(a)** Outline the mechanism for this reaction. Show the relevant dipole and lone pair. **[3]**

**(b)** Name the organic product. **[1]**

**8.** 2-Iodopropane is heated in a sealed tube with an excess of ammonia dissolved in ethanol.

**(a)** Outline the two-stage mechanism for this reaction. **[4]**

**(b)** Explain why the ammonia is used in excess. **[2]**

**9.** A student writes: "Fluoroalkanes are the most reactive halogenoalkanes because the C–F bond is the most polar, so the carbon is the most δ+."

Use the bond enthalpy data below to evaluate this statement.

| Bond | Mean bond enthalpy / kJ mol⁻¹ |
|---|---|
| C–F | 467 |
| C–Cl | 346 |
| C–I | 228 |

**[4]**

**10.** Three test tubes contain 1-chlorobutane, 1-bromobutane and 1-iodobutane. A few drops of each are added to separate warm portions of aqueous ethanolic silver nitrate.

**(a)** State the colour of the precipitate formed in each tube. **[1]**

**(b)** State and explain the order in which the precipitates appear. **[3]**

**11.** 1-Bromopropane is converted into butanenitrile:

CH₃CH₂CH₂Br + KCN → CH₃CH₂CH₂CN + KBr

**(a)** Outline the mechanism for the reaction of 1-bromopropane with cyanide ions. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage atom economy for making butanenitrile by this route. **[2]**

**(c)** State why this reaction is useful in planning a synthesis. **[1]**

**12.** 8.21 g of 2-bromobutane, CH₃CHBrCH₂CH₃, is heated under reflux with excess potassium cyanide. The yield of 2-methylbutanenitrile is 65.0 %.

**(a)** Explain why the product is named 2-methylbutanenitrile rather than pentanenitrile. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the mass of 2-methylbutanenitrile obtained. **[3]**

**13.** A student's mechanism for the reaction of bromoethane with hydroxide ions shows: an arrow from the bromine atom to the C–Br bond; an arrow from the oxygen of OH⁻ to a hydrogen atom on the CH₃ group. Identify and correct **two** errors. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** **D** (H⁺ has no lone pair; it is an electron pair acceptor) [1]

**2.** Chlorine is more electronegative than carbon [1]; so the C–Cl bond is polar and the carbon is δ+ / electron deficient, attracting lone pairs [1]

**3.** Aqueous sodium (or potassium) hydroxide [1]; warm / heat under reflux [1]
*Examiner insight:* "NaOH" without "aqueous" is usually not credited, because ethanolic hydroxide favours elimination.

**4. (a)** **Ethanenitrile**, CH₃CN [1]

**(b)** **2-Methylpropanenitrile**, (CH₃)₂CHCN [1]

**(c)** **3,3-Dimethylbutanenitrile**, (CH₃)₃CCH₂CN [1]
*Examiner insight:* Count from the nitrile carbon; the chain is always one carbon longer than in the reactant.

**5.** **CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂I + 2NH₃ → CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂NH₂ + NH₄I** [1]

**6.** **1-chlorohexane < 1-bromohexane < 1-iodohexane** [1]

**7. (a)** Dipole Cδ+–Clδ− shown on the C–Cl bond [1]; arrow from a lone pair on O of OH⁻ to the δ+ carbon [1]; arrow from the C–Cl bond to Cl, with Cl⁻ shown as a product [1]

**(b)** **Butan-2-ol** [1]

**8. (a)** Arrow from the lone pair on N of NH₃ to the δ+ carbon (C2) [1]; arrow from the C–I bond to I [1]; intermediate (CH₃)₂CH–NH₃⁺ with the positive charge on N [1]; a second NH₃ removes H⁺: arrow from its lone pair to H on N⁺ and arrow from the N–H bond to N, giving (CH₃)₂CHNH₂ and NH₄⁺ [1]

**(b)** The amine product has a lone pair on nitrogen, so it is itself a nucleophile and can react with more 2-iodopropane [1]; excess ammonia makes it much more likely that the halogenoalkane collides with NH₃ rather than with the amine, maximising the primary amine [1]

**9.** The statement is incorrect: fluoroalkanes are the least reactive [1]; the C–F bond enthalpy (467 kJ mol⁻¹) is the highest, so most energy is needed to break it [1]; the activation energy is highest, so the rate is slowest [1]; although C–F is the most polar, polarity does not control the rate; bond enthalpy does, which is why C–I (228 kJ mol⁻¹), the least polar, reacts fastest [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote the data. An answer that only says "C–F is strong" without the comparison to C–I limits the marks.

**10. (a)** Chloride: white; bromide: cream; iodide: yellow (all three needed) [1]

**(b)** Order: iodide first, then bromide, then chloride [1]; bond enthalpy decreases C–Cl > C–Br > C–I [1]; so the C–I bond is broken most easily, giving the lowest activation energy and the fastest release of halide ions [1]

**11. (a)** Arrow from the lone pair on the carbon of CN⁻ to the δ+ carbon of C–Br [1]; arrow from the C–Br bond to Br, giving Br⁻ [1]

**(b)** Mr: C₃H₇Br = 122.9; KCN = 65.1; CH₃CH₂CH₂CN = 69.0 [1]; atom economy = 69.0 ÷ (122.9 + 65.1) × 100 = **36.7 %** [1]

**(c)** It increases the length of the carbon chain by one carbon atom [1]

**12. (a)** The new carbon bonds to carbon 2 of the old chain, so the longest chain including the nitrile carbon has four carbons with a methyl branch on carbon 2 [1]

**(b)** n(2-bromobutane) = 8.21 ÷ 136.9 = 0.0600 mol [1]; theoretical mass of nitrile = 0.0600 × 83.0 = 4.98 g [1]; actual mass = 4.98 × 0.650 = **3.24 g** [1]

**13.** Error 1: the arrow is drawn from Br to the bond [1]; it should start at the C–Br bond and end on Br [1]. Error 2: the OH⁻ arrow goes to a hydrogen, which describes elimination/base behaviour [1]; it should go from a lone pair on O to the δ+ carbon bonded to bromine [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Identify and correct" needs both parts for each error; naming the error without the correction scores half.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Cyanide arrows drawn from nitrogen.
- No lone pair on the nucleophile, or no dipole on C–X.
- Only one ammonia molecule in the equation, or no stage 2.
- Reactivity explained by polarity instead of bond enthalpy.
- Nitrile names that ignore the extra carbon.
