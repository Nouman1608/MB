## What photosynthesis is

**Photosynthesis is the process by which plants make carbohydrates from raw materials (carbon dioxide and water) using energy from light.** Oxygen is released as a by-product.

The raw materials and where they come from:

- **Carbon dioxide** enters the leaf from the air, through the stomata.
- **Water** is taken up by the roots and carried to the leaf in the xylem.
- **Light energy** is absorbed by chlorophyll.

### Chlorophyll

**Chlorophyll** is a green pigment found in the **chloroplasts**. It absorbs light energy, mainly red and blue light, and **transfers light energy into chemical energy** that is stored in glucose and other carbohydrates. Green light is mostly reflected, which is why leaves look green.

### The equations

Word equation:

carbon dioxide + water, in light and with chlorophyll, gives glucose + oxygen

Balanced chemical equation:

6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2

Count the atoms to check. Left side: 6 carbon, 12 oxygen (from 6 CO2) plus 6 oxygen (from 6 H2O), so 18 oxygen, and 12 hydrogen. Right side: C6H12O6 has 6 carbon, 12 hydrogen and 6 oxygen, plus 6 O2 gives 12 oxygen, so 18 oxygen in total. The equation balances. Write "light" and "chlorophyll" above or below the arrow if the question asks for conditions.

## What happens to the carbohydrates made

The first carbohydrate made is glucose. It is then used or changed as follows.

| Use | Detail |
|---|---|
| Respiration | Glucose is broken down to release energy for the cell |
| Starch | Glucose is made into starch, an insoluble **energy store**, in the leaf, root and seeds |
| Cellulose | Glucose is used to build **cell walls** |
| Sucrose | Glucose is changed into sucrose, which is soluble and is **transported** through the plant in the phloem |

Starch is stored because it is insoluble and so does not affect the water potential of the cell.

## Investigating photosynthesis

### Testing a leaf for starch

Starch in a leaf shows that photosynthesis has happened, because a leaf makes starch from the glucose that photosynthesis produces. The method is:

1. **Boil the leaf** in water for about a minute. *This kills the cells and stops chemical reactions, and it softens the leaf so that iodine can enter.*
2. **Heat the leaf in hot ethanol** (in a water bath, never over a flame). *This removes chlorophyll so that the colour change is easy to see.*
3. **Rinse in water.** *The leaf is brittle after the ethanol; water softens it.*
4. **Add iodine solution.** *Starch turns the iodine from orange-brown to blue-black.*

Before any experiment, a plant is usually **destarched** by keeping it in the dark for 24 to 48 hours, so that any starch found later was made during the experiment.

### The need for chlorophyll

Use a variegated leaf, such as one with green and white patches. After light, test for starch. The **green** areas turn blue-black, but the **white** areas stay orange-brown, because they have no chlorophyll and so did not photosynthesise. The control is the green part of the same leaf.

### The need for light

Cover part of a destarched leaf, still attached to the plant, with black paper or foil, and leave it in the light. After testing, the covered part is orange-brown and the uncovered part is blue-black. The covered part is the test and the uncovered part is the **control**.

### The need for carbon dioxide

Place two destarched potted plants in sealed bell jars. In one, put a dish of potassium hydroxide solution (or soda lime), which **absorbs carbon dioxide**. In the other (the control), put a dish of water, or sodium hydrogencarbonate solution which releases carbon dioxide. Leave both in the light. Then test a leaf from each for starch. The leaf from the jar without carbon dioxide stays orange-brown, and the control leaf turns blue-black.

In every one of these experiments, only **one** factor is changed, and the others, such as temperature and time, are kept the same.

## The rate of photosynthesis

### Using a submerged aquatic plant

A water plant such as *Elodea* releases bubbles of oxygen when it photosynthesises. **The rate** can be measured by counting the bubbles per minute, or by collecting the gas in a capillary tube.

Method: place a piece of *Elodea* in a beaker of water with sodium hydrogencarbonate (the carbon dioxide source). Put a lamp at a measured distance. Wait for the plant to adjust, then count the bubbles for a minute. Repeat and find a mean.

### Hydrogencarbonate indicator

Hydrogencarbonate indicator solution shows the carbon dioxide concentration of the water by its colour.

| Colour | Carbon dioxide level |
|---|---|
| Yellow | High |
| Orange or red | Atmospheric level, unchanged |
| Purple | Low |

When a plant photosynthesises faster than it respires, it takes in carbon dioxide and the indicator goes **purple**. In the dark, respiration adds carbon dioxide and the indicator goes **yellow**.

### Worked example: light intensity

*A lamp is placed at different distances from Elodea. The mean bubble counts are in the table.*

| Distance of lamp (cm) | Mean bubbles per minute |
|---|---|
| 10 | 52 |
| 20 | 31 |
| 30 | 17 |
| 40 | 10 |

As the lamp moves further away, light intensity falls, and the rate falls. The change from 10 cm to 20 cm is a fall of 21 bubbles per minute, which is 21 divided by 52 x 100 = 40.4 per cent. **Explanation**: less light energy is absorbed by chlorophyll, so less energy is available to make glucose.

A fair test keeps the temperature the same (a heat shield or water bath stops the lamp warming the water) and the same plant, and keeps the carbon dioxide supply constant.

## Factors that affect the rate

**Light intensity.** As light intensity increases, the rate increases at first, because there is more energy to be absorbed. At high intensity the rate levels off, because another factor has become limiting.

**Carbon dioxide concentration.** More carbon dioxide gives a faster rate until another factor limits it. Carbon dioxide is a raw material, so a shortage slows the process.

**Temperature.** The reactions are controlled by enzymes. Rate increases as temperature rises, because the particles have more kinetic energy and collide more often. Above the **optimum** (roughly 35 to 40 degrees Celsius) the enzymes are denatured and the rate falls sharply.

### Limiting factors

A **limiting factor** is the factor in shortest supply, which stops the rate from increasing further. On a graph of rate against light intensity:

- On the rising part of the line, **light intensity is limiting**. Increasing light increases the rate.
- On the flat part, light is no longer limiting. Something else is, for example **carbon dioxide** or **temperature**. Increasing light has no effect, but increasing the limiting factor raises the plateau.

### Worked example: identifying the limiting factor

*At 20 degrees Celsius and 0.04 per cent carbon dioxide, the rate levels off at high light intensity. At 30 degrees Celsius it levels off at a higher rate. What was limiting at 20 degrees Celsius?*

The rate rose when temperature was raised, with light and carbon dioxide unchanged. So **temperature** was the limiting factor on the plateau at 20 degrees Celsius.

In a greenhouse, growers can raise light with lamps, raise carbon dioxide by burning fuel or adding gas, and raise temperature by heating.

## Common mistakes

- Writing "plants make food" without naming carbohydrates or glucose.
- Writing the equation with the wrong numbers or reversing it.
- Saying chlorophyll "makes" energy; it transfers light energy into chemical energy.
- Heating ethanol with a flame.
- Saying that the temperature graph levels off when it actually falls at high temperature.
- Naming a factor as limiting when the graph shows the rate rising with it.

## Command words

- **State**: give the equation or fact without explanation.
- **Describe**: give the stages of a method or the shape of a graph.
- **Explain**: give the reason, for example why the rate levels off.
- **Investigate**: plan or carry out a practical with controls.
- **Identify**: name the limiting factor from the data.
