## Questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following is a semi-variable cost? **[1]**

A. Rent of £1 500 a month
B. Sales commission of 5% of sales
C. A salesperson paid £1 200 a month plus 2% commission on sales
D. Machine hire of £800 per machine, with one machine needed for every 500 units

**2.** A product sells for £25 and has a variable cost of £15 per unit. Fixed costs are £42 000. What is
the break-even point? **[1]**

A. 1 680 units
B. 2 800 units
C. 4 200 units
D. 105 000 units

**3.** Tarnside Ceramics makes mugs. Classify each cost as direct or indirect, and state its behaviour
(variable, fixed, semi-variable or stepped).

**(a)** Clay used in the mugs **[1]**
**(b)** Kiln electricity: a standing charge plus a rate per firing **[1]**
**(c)** Business rates on the workshop **[1]**
**(d)** Packers' wages, with one packer employed for every 3 000 mugs a month **[1]**

**4.** Explain the difference between marginal cost and full cost, and why marginal cost is used for
short-term decisions. **[3]**

**5.** Harbour Paddleboards sells boards at £340 each. Variable cost is £204 per board and fixed costs
are £95 200 a year. Budgeted sales are 1 000 boards.

**(a)** Calculate the contribution per board. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the break-even point in boards. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the break-even revenue. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the margin of safety in boards and as a percentage of budgeted sales. **[2]**
**(e)** Calculate the number of boards needed to make a profit of £27 200. **[2]**

**6.** A break-even chart for a single product shows: a fixed cost line at £30 000; revenue and total
cost lines crossing at 2 500 units and £75 000; a maximum output of 4 000 units.

**(a)** Calculate the selling price per unit. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the variable cost per unit. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the profit at maximum output. **[2]**

**7.** Explain **three** limitations of break-even analysis for a business that sells several different
products. **[6]**

**8.** Tamar Valves makes a component it uses in its products, needing 20 000 a year. Cost per
component: direct materials £6.40, direct labour £5.20, variable overheads £1.80, fixed overheads
absorbed £3.60. A supplier offers the component at £15.50.

**(a)** The production manager says buying will save £1.50 per component. Calculate the true effect on
annual profit of buying in. **[3]**
**(b)** If the component is bought, the space freed could be rented out for £30 000 a year and £8 000 of
supervision costs would be avoided. Calculate the revised effect on annual profit and state the
decision on financial grounds. **[3]**
**(c)** State **two** non-financial factors Tamar Valves should consider. **[2]**

**9.** Corvid Print can print 50 000 books a year and currently prints 40 000. Normal selling price is
£12 per book; variable cost is £7.50 per book; fixed costs are £120 000. An overseas customer offers a
one-off order for 8 000 books at £8.40 each. The order needs extra packaging at £0.25 per book and a
one-off set-up cost of £2 000.

**(a)** Calculate the effect on profit of accepting the order. **[4]**
**(b)** Advise Corvid Print whether to accept, considering financial and non-financial factors. **[3]**

**10.** Lune Valley Foods makes three pies. Oven time is limited to 5 300 hours next year. Fixed costs
are £64 000.

| Pie | Selling price £ | Variable cost £ | Oven hours per pie | Maximum demand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| P | 18.00 | 10.80 | 0.40 | 5 000 |
| Q | 24.00 | 13.50 | 0.75 | 4 000 |
| R | 15.00 | 9.00 | 0.30 | 6 000 |

**(a)** Calculate the contribution per oven hour for each pie and rank the pies. **[4]**
**(b)** Calculate the production plan that maximises profit. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the maximum profit. **[2]**
**(d)** State **one** non-financial factor to consider before cutting output of pie Q. **[1]**

**11.** Wendle Sports has three product lines. Its statement for the year:

| | Bikes £ | Boards £ | Skates £ | Total £ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sales | 240 000 | 150 000 | 90 000 | 480 000 |
| Variable costs | 144 000 | 84 000 | 63 000 | 291 000 |
| Fixed costs (apportioned by floor area) | 66 000 | 42 000 | 36 000 | 144 000 |
| Profit/(loss) | 30 000 | 24 000 | (9 000) | 45 000 |

If Skates closed, £11 000 of its fixed costs would be avoided; the rest would continue.

**(a)** Calculate the contribution of each product line. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the total profit if Skates closed. **[3]**
**(c)** The manager suggests using the Skates space to expand Boards, which would raise Boards'
contribution by 20%. Evaluate whether Wendle Sports should close Skates. **[6]**

**12.** Juniper Candles expects to sell 6 000 candles next year. Variable cost is £4.60 per candle and
fixed costs are £21 000. The owner wants a profit of £15 000.

**(a)** Calculate the selling price needed. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why a lower price might be accepted for a one-off order but not for all sales. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** **C** -- a fixed salary plus a variable commission [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* D is a stepped cost, the usual wrong answer here.

**2.** Contribution £10; 42 000 ÷ 10 = **C, 4 200 units** [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* D is break-even revenue in £, not units; read the unit in each option.

**3. (a)** Direct, variable [1]
**(b)** Indirect, semi-variable [1]
**(c)** Indirect, fixed [1]
**(d)** Indirect (or direct if packing is treated as direct labour), stepped [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each mark needs both classifications; one correct label alone does not earn it.

**4.** Marginal cost is the variable cost of one extra unit [1]; full cost adds absorbed fixed
overheads [1]. Short-term decisions do not change fixed costs, so marginal cost shows the true change in
profit [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The third mark needs the reason (fixed costs unchanged), not just a definition.

**5. (a)** 340 − 204 = **£136** [1]
**(b)** 95 200 ÷ 136 [1] = **700 boards** [1]
**(c)** 700 × 340 = **£238 000** [1]
**(d)** 1 000 − 700 = **300 boards** [1]; 300 ÷ 1 000 = **30%** [1]
**(e)** (95 200 + 27 200) ÷ 136 [1] = **900 boards** [1] **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Parts (b) to (e) all build on (a), so show working; a slip there spreads.

**6. (a)** 75 000 ÷ 2 500 = **£30** [1]
**(b)** Variable costs at break-even = 75 000 − 30 000 = £45 000 [1]; 45 000 ÷ 2 500 = **£18** [1]
**(c)** Contribution = 30 − 18 = £12; (4 000 × 12) − 30 000 [1] = **£18 000** [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* At break-even, revenue equals total cost, not variable cost; using £75 000 as the variable cost loses (b).

**7.** Any three, each with a point and a multi-product explanation:
- Break-even depends on the sales mix [1]; a shift to low-contribution products raises break-even
  revenue [1].
- Shared fixed costs must be apportioned [1], so one product's break-even rests on an arbitrary
  split [1].
- It assumes constant prices and variable costs [1], but discounts to sell more of one line, or bulk
  buying, change contribution per unit [1].
(Also creditworthy: all output assumed sold; stepped costs.) **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A limitation stated without linking it to several products earns only the first mark of each pair.

**8. (a)** Marginal cost of making = 6.40 + 5.20 + 1.80 = £13.40 [1]. Buying costs 15.50 − 13.40 =
£2.10 more per component [1]; 20 000 × 2.10 = profit **falls by £42 000** [1].
**(b)** Benefits = 30 000 + 8 000 = £38 000 [1]; net effect 38 000 − 42 000 [1] = profit **falls by
£4 000**, so continue to make [1].
**(c)** Any two [1] [1]: supplier quality; reliability of delivery; dependence on one supplier; loss of
in-house skills. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Comparing £15.50 with the £17.00 full cost is the method error this question tests; no credit for that comparison.

**9. (a)** Marginal cost = 7.50 + 0.25 = £7.75 [1]; contribution = 8.40 − 7.75 = £0.65 per book [1];
8 000 × 0.65 = £5 200 [1]; less set-up 2 000 = profit **rises by £3 200** [1].
**(b)** Accept: spare capacity (10 000 books) covers the order and profit rises by £3 200 [1]. Risk:
regular customers may demand the lower price [1]. Benefit: possible repeat overseas orders [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* "Advise" needs a clear decision as well as factors; points with no decision lose the last mark.

**10. (a)** Contribution per pie: P £7.20, Q £10.50, R £6.00 [1]. Per oven hour: P 7.20 ÷ 0.40 =
£18, Q 10.50 ÷ 0.75 = £14 [1]; R 6.00 ÷ 0.30 = £20 [1]. Rank **R, P, Q** [1].
**(b)** Demand needs 2 000 + 3 000 + 1 800 = 6 800 hours, more than 5 300, so oven time is limiting [1].
Make R 6 000 (1 800 hours) and P 5 000 (2 000 hours) [1]; the remaining 1 500 hours give 1 500 ÷ 0.75 =
**2 000 of Q** [1].
**(c)** Contribution = 36 000 + 36 000 + 21 000 = £93 000 [1]; profit = 93 000 − 64 000 = **£29 000** [1].
**(d)** Customers who cannot get pie Q may stop buying the other pies too [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Q has the highest contribution per pie; ranking on that instead of per oven hour loses the ranking and plan marks.

**11. (a)** Bikes 240 000 − 144 000 = **£96 000** [1]; Boards **£66 000** [1]; Skates **£27 000** [1].
**(b)** Contribution lost £27 000 [1]; fixed costs saved £11 000 [1]; profit = 45 000 − 27 000 + 11 000
= **£29 000**, a fall of £16 000 [1].
**(c)** Extra Boards contribution = 66 000 × 20% = £13 200 [1]; profit = 29 000 + 13 200 = £42 200,
still £2 800 below the current £45 000 [1]. Skates' loss comes from apportioned costs; it earns positive
contribution [1]. Against closure: redundancies and morale [1]; customers who buy skates may also buy
bikes or boards [1]. Judgement: keep Skates unless Boards' contribution could rise by over 24.2% (16 000 ÷ 66 000) [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* The judgement mark needs a decision supported by the figures, not "it depends" alone.

**12. (a)** Contribution needed = 21 000 + 15 000 = £36 000 [1]; per candle 36 000 ÷ 6 000 = £6 [1];
price = 4.60 + 6 = **£10.60** [1].
**(b)** With spare capacity, a one-off order adds profit at any price above the £4.60 marginal cost,
as fixed costs are already covered [1]. All sales must cover fixed costs and the target profit [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers must name marginal cost as the floor; "it is still profitable" with no reason does not earn the mark.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using full cost instead of marginal cost in make-or-buy and special-order decisions.
- Ignoring avoidable fixed costs or income from freed space.
- Ranking by contribution per unit when a limiting factor applies.
- Treating all apportioned fixed costs as saved on closure.
- Reading break-even where revenue meets the fixed cost line.
- Giving factors with no final recommendation on "advise" or "evaluate".
- Rounding target-profit units down.
