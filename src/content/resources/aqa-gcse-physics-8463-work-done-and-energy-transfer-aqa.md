marked (HT only). Paper 2 can also draw on the energy ideas from Topic 1, and work done is the link between

## What the specification asks

| The specification says you should be able to... | What that means in an answer |
|---|---|
| understand that a force does work when it causes a displacement | Check the object actually moves, in the direction of the force |
| recall and apply W = F s | Substitute, rearrange and give the answer in joules |
| state that 1 joule of work is done when 1 N causes a displacement of 1 m | 1 J = 1 N m |
| describe the energy transfer involved when work is done | Name the store energy leaves and the store it goes to |
| convert between newton-metres and joules | The numbers are the same: 250 N m = 250 J |
| explain that work done against friction raises temperature | Link friction to the thermal energy store |

## What "work done" means

When a force causes an object to move through a distance, **work is done on the object**. Put another way,
a force does work when it causes a **displacement** of the object.

```
work done = force × distance (moved along the line of action of the force)
W = F s      W in joules (J), F in newtons (N), s in metres (m)
```

You must recall this equation. The distance must be measured **along the line of action of the force**:
the direction in which the force acts.

### When no work is done

| Situation | Why no work is done by the force named |
|---|---|
| Holding a heavy bag still | The bag does not move, so there is no displacement |
| Pushing hard on a wall that does not move | No displacement |
| Carrying a bag across a level floor (the upward force from your arm) | The bag moves horizontally, at right angles to the upward force, so it does not move along that force's line of action |
| A satellite in a circular orbit (the gravitational force) | The force is towards the centre; the motion is at right angles to it |

You may feel tired holding a bag still, but your muscles are using energy internally, not doing work on the
bag.

## 1 joule = 1 newton-metre

One joule of work is done when a force of one newton causes a displacement of one metre. So:

```
1 J = 1 N m
```

Converting between newton-metres and joules changes only the unit name: 480 N m = 480 J. The conversion
that changes the number is of **prefixes** (kN, kJ, cm), which you must do before using W = F s.

**Worked example 1.** A winch pulls a boat 250 cm up a ramp with a force of 3.6 kN along the ramp. Find
the work done.

```
F = 3.6 kN = 3600 N         s = 250 cm = 2.50 m
W = F s = 3600 × 2.50 = 9000 J = 9.0 kJ
```

**Worked example 2.** A crane does 2.4 kJ of work lifting a load with a force of 300 N. How far does it
lift the load?

```
s = W / F = 2400 / 300 = 8.0 m
```

## Describing the energy transfer

Doing work transfers energy from one store to another. **Work done = energy transferred.** To describe
the transfer, name the store the energy comes from and the store it goes to.

| Work done | Energy transferred from | Energy transferred to |
|---|---|---|
| Lifting a box onto a shelf | Chemical store of the person | Gravitational potential energy store of the box |
| An engine accelerating a car | Chemical store of the fuel | Kinetic energy store of the car (and thermal stores of the surroundings) |
| Brakes stopping a bicycle | Kinetic energy store of the bicycle | Thermal energy store of the brakes and wheel rims |
| Pushing a box at a steady speed across a rough floor | Chemical store of the person | Thermal energy store of the box and floor (the box's kinetic store does not change) |
| Stretching a spring | Chemical store of the person | Elastic potential energy store of the spring |

**Worked example 3.** A student lifts a 15 kg box through a height of 1.2 m at a steady speed. Take
g = 9.8 N/kg. Find the work done and describe the energy transfer.

```
force needed = weight = m g = 15 × 9.8 = 147 N
W = F s = 147 × 1.2 = 176.4 J ≈ 180 J
```

The work done equals the gain in the box's gravitational potential energy store. You get the same answer
from Eₚ = m g h = 15 × 9.8 × 1.2 = 176.4 J, which is why the two equations agree.

## Work done against friction

Friction opposes motion. When an object moves against friction, work is done against the frictional force,
and energy is transferred to the **thermal energy store** of the object and its surroundings. The
specification states that this **causes a rise in the temperature** of the object.

- Rubbing your hands together warms them.
- A car's brakes get hot when it stops: friction between the brake pads and the disc does work, reducing the
  car's kinetic energy.
- A drill bit gets hot as it cuts into wood.

**Worked example 4.** A cyclist brakes. The friction force from the brakes is 900 N and the bicycle travels
12 m while braking. How much energy is transferred to the brakes?

```
W = F s = 900 × 12 = 10 800 J = 10.8 kJ
```

The kinetic energy store of the cyclist and bicycle falls by 10.8 kJ, and the thermal energy store of the
brakes rises by the same amount (ignoring other resistive forces).

### Link to power

Power is the rate at which work is done (Topic 1). If a student does 9000 J of work in 15 s, the power is
9000 / 15 = 600 W. Questions on Paper 2 often combine W = F s with P = W / t.

## Annotated model answer

**Question (3 marks).** A worker pushes a heavy crate at a steady speed across a rough floor. Describe the
energy transfers.

> The worker does work on the crate by pushing it through a distance **[work linked to force and
> displacement]**. Energy is transferred from the chemical store of the worker **[source store]**. Because
> the speed is steady, the crate's kinetic energy does not change; the work done against friction is
> transferred to the thermal energy store of the crate and floor, which get warmer **[destination store,
> with the friction link]**.

The word "steady" in the question is a clue: the kinetic store does not change, so all the work is done
against friction.

## Common mistakes

- Using a distance that is not along the line of action of the force, such as a horizontal distance for a
  vertical lifting force.
- Leaving kN, kJ or cm unconverted before substituting.
- Saying "energy is lost" instead of naming the store it is transferred to.
- Writing that energy is "used up". It is transferred to other stores.
- Giving work done in newtons or newton-metres when the question asks for joules. Convert the unit name.
- Saying friction "creates heat". Say work done against friction transfers energy to the thermal store and
  raises the temperature.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate**: write W = F s, convert units, substitute, then give the unit. If the question asks for kJ,
  divide by 1000 at the end.
- **Describe** an energy transfer: one store before, one store after, and what does the work.
- **Explain** why something warms up: name friction, say work is done against it, and link it to the thermal
  energy store and temperature rise.
- Show every step on a two-stage calculation, such as weight then work done; the first step often earns a
  mark on its own.
