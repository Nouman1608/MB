## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define the term quality control. **[2]**

**2.** Which **one** of the following is an example of quality assurance? **[1]**

A: Inspecting finished tables before they are sold

B: Training staff and agreeing quality standards with suppliers

C: Testing a sample of products at the end of production

D: Removing faulty items from a production line

**3.** Which **two** of the following are costs to a business of poor quality? **[2]**

A: Refunds to customers

B: Higher customer loyalty

C: Reworking faulty products

D: The ability to charge a premium price

E: More positive reviews

**4.** Hollybank Bricks checks 12,500 bricks and finds 375 faulty. Calculate the defect rate. **[2]**

**5.** Kirkdale Shirts makes 6,000 shirts a month and 2.5% are faulty. Each faulty shirt costs £9 in wasted materials and labour.

**(a)** Calculate the monthly cost of faulty shirts. **[2]**

**(b)** A quality assurance programme costing £600 a month would cut the fault rate to 1%. Calculate the net monthly saving. **[2]**

**6.** Seaview Hotel pays a mystery guest to stay twice a year and report on the service. It also requires every receptionist to complete a customer service course. State **one** method of quality control used by Seaview Hotel. **[1]**

**7.** Explain **one** way good quality can help a business control its costs. **[3]**

### Section B: longer questions

**8.** Thornfield Window Cleaners promises streak-free windows and returns free of charge if a customer is not satisfied. Outline **one** way this approach to quality could give Thornfield a competitive advantage. **[2]**

**9.** Explain **one** disadvantage of relying on quality control rather than quality assurance. **[3]**

**10.** Bayside Takeaway received 42 complaints from 6,000 orders in March and 30 complaints from 7,500 orders in April.

**(a)** Calculate the number of complaints per 1,000 orders in each month. **[2]**

**(b)** Analyse how improving quality could affect Bayside Takeaway. **[6]**

**11.** Greystone Furniture makes 5,000 chairs a year and 6% are faulty. Its inspectors catch 85% of faulty chairs. A fault caught in the factory costs £40 to put right; a fault that reaches a customer costs £200 in collection, repair and goodwill.

Option A: employ an extra inspector for £4,000 a year. Inspectors would catch 98% of faulty chairs; the fault rate stays at 6%.

Option B: introduce a quality assurance programme costing £8,000 a year. The fault rate would fall to 2%; inspectors would still catch 85% of faulty chairs.

Justify which option Greystone Furniture should choose. **[9]**

**12.** Larkfield Leisure Centre has 1,500 members paying £30 a month. Each year about 25% of members cancel, many complaining about dirty changing rooms and unhelpful staff. The manager proposes a £12,000-a-year programme of staff training and cleaning standards, which she believes would cut cancellations to 18% a year.

Evaluate whether Larkfield Leisure Centre should introduce the programme. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Checking products or services, usually at the end of production or at set stages [1], to find faults and remove them before they reach customers [1]. **[2]**

**2.** B [1]. **[1]**

**3.** A [1]. C [1]. **[2]**

**4.** 375 ÷ 12,500 × 100 [1] = **3%** [1]. **[2]**

**5. (a)** 6,000 × 2.5% = 150 faulty shirts [1]. 150 × £9 = **£1,350** [1]

**(b)** New faults = 6,000 × 1% = 60, costing 60 × £9 = £540 [1]. Saving = £1,350 − £540 − £600 = **£210** a month [1] **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* Remember to subtract the cost of the programme from the saving on faults.

**6.** Employing a mystery guest to report on the service [1]. **[1]**

*Examiner insight:* The customer service course prevents poor service, so it is quality assurance.

**7.** Good quality means fewer products are faulty [1], so less material and labour are wasted on items that must be scrapped or reworked [1]. The business also pays fewer refunds and spends less staff time handling complaints, so its total costs fall [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Customers can trust that Thornfield will get the job right, or put it right free [1], so they are more likely to choose it over cheaper window cleaners, recommend it to neighbours and stay loyal [1]. **[2]**

**9.** Quality control finds faults after products have been made [1], so materials and labour have already been spent on faulty items [1]. This means waste and rework costs remain, whereas quality assurance prevents many faults from being made at all [1]. **[3]**

**10. (a)** March: 42 ÷ 6,000 × 1,000 = **7 per 1,000** [1]. April: 30 ÷ 7,500 × 1,000 = **4 per 1,000** [1]

**(b)** The complaint rate has already fallen from 7 to 4 per 1,000, showing quality is improving as orders rise [1]. Fewer complaints mean fewer refunds and remade meals, lowering Bayside's costs [1]. Customers who receive hot, correct orders are more likely to order again and leave good reviews on delivery apps [1], which helps Bayside win customers from other takeaways nearby [1]. Better quality may also allow a small price rise without losing customers [1]. However, improving quality further, for example with extra staff at busy times, adds costs, so Bayside must check the benefit is greater than the cost [1]. **[8]**

*Examiner insight:* Raw complaints fell by 12, but the rate fell by more than 40% because orders rose. Using rates shows the full improvement.

**11.** Current cost: faults = 300; caught 255 × £40 = £10,200; reaching customers 45 × £200 = £9,000; total **£19,200** [1]. Option A: caught 294 × £40 = £11,760; reaching customers 6 × £200 = £1,200; plus £4,000 = **£16,960** [1][1]. Option B: faults = 100; caught 85 × £40 = £3,400; reaching customers 15 × £200 = £3,000; plus £8,000 = **£14,400** [1][1]. Option B is £2,560 a year cheaper than Option A [1]. Option B prevents 200 faults rather than just catching more of them, so less material and labour are wasted [1]. Option A sends fewer faulty chairs to customers (6 against 15), which better protects Greystone's reputation in the short term [1]. **Judgement:** choose Option B, because it gives the lowest total cost and tackles the cause of faults; but it depends on the training actually cutting faults to 2%, so Greystone could also review whether 15 faulty chairs reaching customers is acceptable [1]. **[9]**

*Examiner insight:* A Justify answer should compare total costs including the cost of the option itself, then weigh a non-financial point such as reputation.

**12.** Members kept = 1,500 × (25% − 18%) = 105 more a year [1]. Extra revenue = 105 × £30 × 12 = **£37,800** a year [1], more than three times the £12,000 cost [1]. For: complaints point directly at cleanliness and staff, so the programme targets the real causes of cancellations [1]. Satisfied members are more likely to recommend the centre, attracting new members at little cost [1]. Against: the 18% is the manager's belief, not a tested figure; even a fall to 23% would keep 30 members, worth £10,800, which would not cover the cost [1]. Training takes time to change behaviour, so benefits may come slowly [1]. Some members may leave for reasons unrelated to quality, such as moving away or cost [1]. **Conclusion:** introduce the programme, because the gain is large if cancellations fall to anywhere near 18% and it addresses the specific complaints; the centre should measure cancellations and complaint rates each quarter to check it is working [1]. **[9]**

*Examiner insight:* Testing a smaller improvement shows how much the decision depends on the forecast, which is what makes an Evaluate conclusion convincing.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling staff training "quality control".
- Forgetting the cost of a quality programme when calculating the saving.
- Comparing raw complaint numbers when volumes have changed.
- Claiming quality leads to competitive advantage without saying how customers behave differently.
- Ignoring the cost of faults that reach customers, which are usually far higher than those caught early.
