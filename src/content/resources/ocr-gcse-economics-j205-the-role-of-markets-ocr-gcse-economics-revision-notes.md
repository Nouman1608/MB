These notes condense section 2.1, The role of markets, of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205), Version 2.0, for first assessment in 2019 (component J205/01). The specification is not tiered. The worked chain of production and the specialised-town example are in the [2.1 study guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-the-role-of-markets-ocr-gcse-economics/); then try the [2.1 practice questions](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-the-role-of-markets-ocr-gcse-economics-practice/). All figures are invented.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Market | Any arrangement that brings buyers and sellers together to exchange |
| Primary sector | Extracting or growing natural resources |
| Secondary sector | Manufacturing and construction: turning materials into goods |
| Tertiary sector | Providing services |
| Good | A tangible product that can be stored |
| Service | An intangible product, usually produced and consumed at the same time |
| Product market | Where goods and services are bought and sold |
| Factor market | Where factors of production, such as labour, are bought and sold |
| Specialisation | Concentrating on a limited range of products or tasks |
| Division of labour | Splitting production into separate tasks, each done by different workers |
| Exchange | Trading what you produce for what you need, usually using money |

## Markets

- Buyers and sellers need not meet: markets can be physical, online, local, national or global.
- In every market, demand and supply interact to determine price and the quantity traded.
- Markets help answer what, how and for whom to produce.

## The three sectors

| Sector | Examples |
|---|---|
| Primary | Farming, fishing, forestry, mining, oil extraction |
| Secondary | Car making, clothing factories, house building, food processing |
| Tertiary | Shops, banks, hairdressers, transport, teaching, health care |

- Classify the **activity**, not the whole firm: a farm shop is tertiary even though the farm is primary.
- Sectors depend on each other along a chain of production.

**Worked reminder.** A loaf sells for £1.50. The farmer receives £0.15 for the wheat, the bakery sells the loaf to the shop for £0.90. Value added: primary £0.15 (10%), secondary £0.75 (50%), tertiary £0.60 (40%).

## Goods versus services

| Goods | Services |
|---|---|
| Tangible | Intangible |
| Can be made in advance and stored | Usually produced as they are consumed |
| Quality can be checked before buying | Quality often judged during or after |

## Factor and product markets

- Households **sell** labour in factor markets and **buy** goods in product markets.
- Firms **buy** factors and **sell** products.
- **Interdependence chain:** fall in product demand → firms need fewer workers (factor market) → workers' incomes fall → spending in product markets falls.

## Specialisation and exchange

| Group | Benefit | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Producers | More output, lower cost per unit, expertise | Dependence on one product |
| Workers | Skill and speed in one task; possibly higher pay | Boredom; narrow skills; harder to switch jobs |
| Regions | Clusters of suppliers and skilled labour | Mass job losses if the industry declines |
| Countries | Greater total output; trade widens choice | Dependence on imports or one main export |

- Specialisation needs exchange; money makes exchange easier than swapping goods.
- Judgement depends on stability of demand, ease of retraining, how gains are shared and the time scale.

## Evaluation chains to learn

**Specialisation for a country.** Specialising raises total output → the country exports what it produces well and imports the rest → consumers gain wider choice and often lower prices. But if world demand for its main export falls, export earnings and jobs fall together. Judgement: beneficial where the country has several export industries, risky where it relies on one.

**Specialisation for workers.** Division of labour raises output per worker → firms can pay more and stay competitive. But repetitive tasks lower motivation, and narrow skills make redundancy harder to recover from. Judgement: depends on training and whether skills transfer to other jobs.

## Exam traps

- "A market is a place" is incomplete: it is any arrangement for exchange.
- Calling a supermarket secondary because it sells goods: retail is a service.
- Describing factor and product markets with no link between them.
- Evaluating specialisation only for firms when workers, regions or countries are named.
- Forgetting that specialisation and exchange go together.

## Quick self-test

1. Define a market.
2. Classify: a fish farm, a furniture factory, an insurance company.
3. Give two differences between goods and services.
4. A fishing company also runs a seafood restaurant. Classify each activity.
5. State who sells and who buys in a labour market.
6. Explain how a rise in demand for new houses could affect a factor market.
7. Give one benefit of specialisation for workers.
8. Give one cost of specialisation for a region.
9. Why does specialisation depend on exchange?
10. A phone sells for £400. The factory sold it to the retailer for £260 and paid £60 for materials from mines and farms. Calculate the value added by the retailer as a percentage of the final price.
11. Give one reason a country that specialises might be vulnerable.

### Answers

1. Any arrangement that brings buyers and sellers together to exchange goods, services or factors.
2. Fish farm: primary; furniture factory: secondary; insurance company: tertiary.
3. Goods are tangible and can be stored; services are intangible and usually consumed as they are produced.
4. Fishing: primary; restaurant: tertiary.
5. Workers (households) sell labour; employers (firms) buy it.
6. Builders need more workers, so demand for construction labour rises, raising wages or employment.
7. Workers become skilled and fast at one task and may earn more.
8. Many jobs are lost at once if the main industry declines.
9. A specialist produces only a limited range of products, so it must trade to obtain everything else it needs.
10. Value added = 400 − 260 = £140; 140 ÷ 400 × 100 = **35%**.
11. It depends on imports for goods it no longer makes, or on world prices for its main export.
