## Definitions to know

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Fiscal policy | Changing government spending and taxes to steer demand and the economy |
| Direct tax | Paid by the person or firm it is levied on (income tax, corporate profits tax) |
| Indirect tax | Collected from sellers and passed on in prices (sales tax) |
| Current expenditure | Day-to-day running costs of government |
| Capital expenditure | Spending on lasting assets such as roads and schools |
| Transfer payment | Money passed to recipients with no output produced in return |
| Budget balance | Revenue minus expenditure; negative is a deficit |
| Expansionary policy | Higher spending, lower taxes, or both; AD moves right |
| Contractionary policy | Less spending and/or higher taxes; shifts AD left |
| Deflationary (recessionary) gap | Actual output below potential output |
| Inflationary gap | Actual output above potential output |

Four sources of revenue: direct taxes, indirect taxes, sales of goods and services by state-owned enterprises, and sale of government assets (usually one-off).

## Six goals of fiscal policy

1. Low and stable inflation.
2. Low unemployment.
3. A stable environment for long-term growth.
4. Reduced business cycle fluctuations.
5. A fairer distribution of income.
6. External balance.

Goals can conflict. A boost to demand that cuts unemployment may push up inflation or imports.

| Goal | Example action |
|---|---|
| Low unemployment | Raise government spending in a slump |
| Low inflation | Cut spending or raise taxes in a boom |
| Fairer income distribution | Progressive taxes and transfer payments |
| Long-term growth | Capital spending on infrastructure, stable tax rules |
| External balance | Restrain spending on imports, or support exports |

## Diagram method

### Keynesian

1. Axes: price level and real GDP.
2. Draw AS flat, then rising, then vertical at potential output.
3. Put AD1 on the flat part, left of potential output.
4. Shift to AD2 for expansion. Output rises with little price rise.
5. For contraction, start with AD past potential output on the steep part and shift left.

### Monetarist/new classical

1. Draw vertical LRAS at potential output and rising SRAS.
2. Start at long-run equilibrium.
3. Expansion: AD shifts right, and output rises above potential in the short run.
4. Costs rise, SRAS shifts left, and output returns to potential at a higher price level.
5. Contraction works the same way in reverse.

Difference to state: the Keynesian view allows an equilibrium below full employment to persist and so supports policy action. The monetarist/new classical view expects automatic return to potential output and so sees fiscal policy changing mainly prices in the long run.

## Budget arithmetic reminder

Orsland's revenue is 320 and expenditure is 345, so the balance is 320 − 345 = −25. If 20 of the revenue came from selling a state asset, the underlying position is 300 − 345 = −45. One-off receipts flatter the balance, so strip them out when judging whether a deficit is sustainable.

## Multiplier (HL only)

| Form | Formula | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| Simple | 1 / (1 − MPC) | Saving is the only leakage |
| Full | 1 / (MPS + MPT + MPM) | Tax and imports also leak |

Change in GDP = multiplier × change in injection. Injections are investment, government spending and exports.

Checking the form: with MPC = 0.9 and no tax or imports, the simple form gives 1 / 0.1 = 10. In an economy where the same extra income is also taxed and spent on imports, leakages are larger and the real multiplier is much smaller. Always use every leakage the question gives.

Method in steps:

1. Add the leakages.
2. Divide 1 by the sum.
3. Multiply by the injection.
4. Give the unit and the direction of the change.

Worked reminder. Orsland has MPS = 0.10, MPT = 0.10 and MPM = 0.30.

```
Leakages   = 0.10 + 0.10 + 0.30 = 0.50
Multiplier = 1 / 0.50 = 2
Government spending +30  ->  GDP +60
Exports +18              ->  GDP +36
Gap of 50 needs an injection of 50 / 2 = 25
```

## Effectiveness

| | Point | One-line explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Constraint | Political pressure | Tax rises and cuts are unpopular, so needed contraction may be avoided |
| Constraint | Time lags | Recognition, decision and spending delays can bring the policy too late |
| Constraint | Sustainable debt | Deficits raise debt and interest costs, shrinking future room to act |
| Constraint (HL only) | Crowding out | Borrowing raises interest rates and cuts private investment |
| Strength | Targeting | Spending can be aimed at specific sectors or regions |
| Strength | Deep recession | Government spending adds demand when private spending will not respond |
| Stabiliser (HL only) | Progressive taxes, unemployment benefits | Soften swings with no new decision |

Crowding out, worked reminder (HL only): government spending rises by 16 and is fully borrowed. Private investment falls by 6. Net injection = 16 − 6 = 10. With a multiplier of 2, GDP rises by 20, not the 32 that ignoring crowding out gives. On the diagram, AD shifts right, then part of the way back.

Automatic stabilisers (HL only): they act with no new decision, so they avoid the lags of discretionary policy. They are weaker than a deliberate stimulus in a deep slump, because they only soften the fall.

Judging effectiveness, by objective:

- **Growth:** capital spending raises capacity, and demand support helps in a slump. Debt can limit this.
- **Unemployment:** works on cyclical unemployment, less on skills mismatch.
- **Inflation:** contractionary policy can restrain it, at the cost of output and jobs.

## Must-know distinctions

- Direct tax versus indirect tax.
- Current versus capital expenditure.
- Transfer payments versus spending on goods and services.
- Keynesian AS (flat to steep) versus LRAS (vertical).
- Simple multiplier versus full multiplier.
- Discretionary policy (a new decision) versus automatic stabilisers (HL only).
- Short-run effect versus long-run effect in the monetarist/new classical view.

## Command terms for this topic

The guide defines the terms as follows, in brief. Calculate: get a number and show the working. Explain: give reasons or causes. Evaluate: weigh strengths and limitations. Discuss: a balanced review with a supported conclusion. Show that: reach the stated result by clear steps. Draw: a labelled, accurate diagram.

## Quick self-test

1. State two direct taxes and one indirect tax.
2. Orsland has revenue of 320 and expenditure of 345. What is the budget balance?
3. Is unemployment benefit current expenditure, capital expenditure or a transfer payment?
4. Which gap does contractionary policy target, and which way does AD shift?
5. (HL only) MPC = 0.6. Calculate the multiplier.
6. (HL only) MPS = 0.10, MPT = 0.10, MPM = 0.30. Calculate the multiplier.
7. (HL only) With a multiplier of 2, what is the rise in GDP from an export increase of 18?
8. (HL only) With a multiplier of 2, what injection closes a gap of 50?
9. (HL only) Government spending rises by 16, funded by borrowing, and investment falls by 6. The multiplier is 2. Find the change in GDP.
10. Name two constraints on fiscal policy that are available at SL.
11. In the monetarist/new classical view, what is the long-run effect of expansionary fiscal policy on output?
12. (HL only) Why do progressive taxes act as automatic stabilisers?

### Answers

1. Income tax and corporate profits tax; sales tax.
2. 320 − 345 = −25, a deficit of 25.
3. A transfer payment.
4. An inflationary gap; AD shifts left.
5. 1 / (1 − 0.6) = 1 / 0.4 = 2.5.
6. 1 / (0.10 + 0.10 + 0.30) = 1 / 0.50 = 2.
7. 2 × 18 = 36.
8. 50 / 2 = 25.
9. Net injection 16 − 6 = 10; 10 × 2 = 20.
10. Any two of political pressure, time lags, sustainable debt.
11. None: output returns to potential and only the price level is higher.
12. Tax payments fall by more than proportionately when incomes fall, so disposable income falls less, and the reverse in a boom.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating the multiplier but not multiplying by the injection.
- Using 1 / (1 − MPC) when the question gives MPT and MPM.
- Treating a rise in the budget deficit as a rise in the national debt figure without saying the debt is the accumulated total.
- Shifting AD with no labelled equilibria or axes.
- Drawing a vertical LRAS for the Keynesian answer.
- Listing constraints without saying why each limits policy.
- Describing crowding out as always happening, with no link to borrowing and interest rates.
- Evaluation without a final judgment tied to the case in the question.
