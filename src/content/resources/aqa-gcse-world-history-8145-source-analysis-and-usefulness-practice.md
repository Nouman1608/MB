The skills tested are those the specification names for questions 1 and 2: "the analysis and evaluation of sources", using "visual and written sources". Each set is labelled with its depth study; attempt your own option first.

## Section A: the method

**1.** Identify three aspects of a source's provenance that you could use in a usefulness answer. **[3]**

**2.** Explain the difference between the usefulness and the reliability of a source. **[2]**

**3.** A student writes: "Source B is a propaganda poster, so it is useless to an historian." Explain why this is a weak judgement. **[2]**

## Section B: source sets

### BA The First World War, 1894-1918

**Source A** *(invented)* -- A description of a cartoon imagined as published in a German comic magazine in 1909. A fat, smug figure of John Bull sits on top of a mountain of battleships labelled "Dreadnought", holding a trident. A small German sailor in a rowing boat looks up at him. John Bull says: "The sea belongs to me. Go and play in your bath."

**4.** Source A opposes British naval policy. How do you know? Explain your answer using Source A and your contextual knowledge. **[4]**

### BB The inter-war years, 1918-1939

**Source B** *(invented)* -- From a speech imagined as made by an opposition MP in the House of Commons, October 1938.
"The Prime Minister tells us he has brought back peace. What he has brought back is a piece of paper. Czechoslovakia was not even allowed into the room while her frontiers were given away. Every dictator in Europe has learned this week that Britain will not stand by a small democracy."

**5.** Source B opposes the Munich Agreement. How do you know? Explain your answer using Source B and your contextual knowledge. **[4]**

**Source H** *(invented)* -- A description of a poster imagined as printed in Germany in 1919. A German worker in chains kneels on the ground. Three well-dressed men labelled "Wilson", "Lloyd George" and "Clemenceau" pull the chains tighter. The caption reads: "The Peace of Shame: Germany must pay for ever."

**Source I** *(invented)* -- From an editorial imagined as published in a Paris newspaper, June 1919.
"France has bled for four years, and what is she given? The Rhineland is left in German hands, the German army may yet rebuild, and the Americans speak of fairness to the enemy. Our children will pay for this soft peace with another war."

**6.** How useful are Sources H and I to an historian studying reactions to the Treaty of Versailles? Explain your answer using Sources H and I and your contextual knowledge. **[12]**

### BC East and West, 1945-1972

**Source C** *(invented)* -- A description of a poster imagined as issued by the East German government in August 1961. A strong East German soldier stands in front of a new wall, his rifle held across his chest. Behind the wall, small figures labelled "spies", "speculators" and "warmongers" try to climb over from the West. The caption reads: "Our wall protects peace."

**Source D** *(invented)* -- From a diary imagined as kept by a teacher in West Berlin, 15 August 1961.
"Since Sunday the city has been cut in two. In Bernauer Strasse people threw bedding from their windows and jumped to the western pavement below. Frau K. cannot reach her daughter, who lives three streets away in the East. The Americans say nothing. Are we to be left alone?"

**7.** Source C supports the building of the Berlin Wall. How do you know? Explain your answer using Source C and your contextual knowledge. **[4]**

**8.** How useful are Sources C and D to an historian studying the building of the Berlin Wall? Explain your answer using Sources C and D and your contextual knowledge. **[12]**

### BD Asia, 1950-1975

**Source E** *(invented)* -- A description of a cartoon imagined as published in an American newspaper in February 1968. President Johnson stands up to his waist in quicksand on a map of Vietnam; the quicksand is labelled "Tet". Beside him, a general in a clean uniform holds a chart with an arrow pointing upward and shouts: "Stay calm, Mr President, we are winning!"

**9.** Source E is critical of American policy in Vietnam. How do you know? Explain your answer using Source E and your contextual knowledge. **[4]**

### BE The Gulf and Afghanistan, 1990-2009

**Source F** *(invented)* -- From a speech imagined as made by a British government minister in Parliament, March 2003.
"For twelve years Saddam Hussein has defied the United Nations. He has hidden his weapons, deceived the inspectors and murdered his own people. We cannot wait until those weapons are used against us. The time for patience has passed."

**Source G** *(invented)* -- A description of a photograph imagined as taken at an anti-war march in London, February 2003. A huge crowd fills a street. In the front row an elderly woman holds a hand-painted placard reading "Not in my name. Let the inspectors finish." Behind her, a banner reads "No blood for oil."

**10.** How useful are Sources F and G to an historian studying the reasons for, and opposition to, the invasion of Iraq in 2003? Explain your answer using Sources F and G and your contextual knowledge. **[12]**

## Answers

**How these guides work.** Question 1-style (4 marks): content detail explained [2], contextual knowledge linked [1], provenance used [1]. Question 2-style (12 marks): for each source, content linked to the enquiry and supported by knowledge [3] and provenance evaluated for what it makes the source useful or less useful for [3].

**1.** Any three, [1] each: who produced it; when it was produced; where or in which country; the type of source (cartoon, diary, speech); its purpose; its intended audience.

**2.** Usefulness is what a source can tell an historian about a particular enquiry [1]; reliability is how far it can be trusted as an accurate account [1].

**3.** Propaganda is useful evidence of what its makers wanted people to believe and how they tried to persuade them [1]; the student has judged by type alone without considering the enquiry [1].

**4.** Content [2]: John Bull is drawn as fat and smug, sitting on a "mountain" of Dreadnoughts, and mocks the German sailor ("Go and play in your bath"), showing Britain as arrogantly claiming the seas for itself. Context [1]: after HMS *Dreadnought* was launched in 1906, Germany and Britain competed to build the new battleships; Germany's Navy Laws aimed for a fleet to match its world power ambitions, which Britain saw as a threat. Provenance [1]: a German magazine of 1909, at the height of the naval race, would reflect German resentment at Britain's insistence on naval supremacy.

**5.** Content [2]: it mocks Chamberlain's claim to have brought back peace ("a piece of paper"), complains that Czechoslovakia "was not even allowed into the room", and warns that dictators will see Britain as weak. Context [1]: at Munich in September 1938 Britain, France, Germany and Italy agreed that Germany could take the Sudetenland; Czechoslovakia was not represented. Provenance [1]: an opposition MP speaking in the Commons debate after Munich would challenge the government's policy of appeasement.

**6.** Source H [6]: content and context [3]: it shows German anger that the treaty was a humiliating *Diktat* imposed by the Big Three, and that Germany "must pay for ever"; this fits German objections to the war guilt clause and reparations, later set at £6,600 million in 1921, and to losses of land and colonies. Provenance [3]: a German poster of 1919 is propaganda aimed at German readers, so it is very useful for the depth of German resentment that later fuelled nationalist parties; it is less useful as evidence of what the treaty actually said, as it exaggerates the Allies' cruelty. Source I [6]: content and context [3]: it shows that some French opinion thought the treaty too soft, mentioning that the Rhineland stayed German and that the army might rebuild; Clemenceau had wanted to weaken Germany more, but under pressure from Wilson and Lloyd George the Rhineland was demilitarised rather than separated from Germany, and the army was limited to 100,000. Provenance [3]: a Paris editorial of June 1919, the month the treaty was signed, is useful for French public opinion and fear of future war; it is less useful for German or British views. Together they are highly useful because they show the treaty was condemned from opposite directions.

**7.** Content [2]: the wall is shown protecting "peace", with a strong soldier guarding it against "spies", "speculators" and "warmongers" from the West. Context [1]: East Germany called the wall a defence against the West, but in reality it was built from 13 August 1961 to stop East Germans escaping; around three million had left through Berlin since 1949. Provenance [1]: a government poster issued in August 1961 was designed to justify the wall to East Germans.

**8.** Source C [6]: content and context [3]: it shows the official East German justification that the wall protected East Germany from Western spies and profiteers; this fits the communist claim that West Berlin was a base for Western subversion, although the real reason was the flow of skilled workers to the West. Provenance [3]: as government propaganda it is very useful for how the East German and Soviet leadership wanted the wall to be seen; it is less useful for the reasons they hid. Source D [6]: content and context [3]: it shows the human impact, families separated and people jumping from windows in Bernauer Strasse, and Western Berliners' fear that the Americans would not act; Kennedy did protest but accepted the wall as preferable to war, although US and Soviet tanks faced each other at Checkpoint Charlie in October 1961 and he visited West Berlin in 1963. Provenance [3]: a private diary written two days after the border was closed records immediate, unguarded reactions, useful for the experience of ordinary Berliners; it is less useful for the decisions of the superpowers. Together they show the official reasons and the human reality.

**9.** Content [2]: Johnson is shown sinking in quicksand labelled "Tet", while a general with a rising chart insists "we are winning", suggesting the government was stuck in an unwinnable war and misleading itself and the public. Context [1]: the Tet Offensive of January 1968 saw attacks on over a hundred towns, including the US embassy in Saigon; though a military defeat for the communists, it contradicted claims of progress and created a "credibility gap". Provenance [1]: an American newspaper in February 1968, when television coverage of Tet was turning opinion against the war, reflects growing press scepticism.

**10.** Source F [6]: content and context [3]: it shows the British government's case for war: Saddam's defiance of UN resolutions since 1991, the claim that he was hiding weapons of mass destruction, and his treatment of his own people, such as the Kurds and Shia; this fits Blair's support for Bush, Resolution 1441 (November 2002) and the work of UN and IAEA inspectors. Provenance [3]: a minister's speech to Parliament in March 2003, days before the invasion, is very useful for how the government justified war; it is less useful for whether the claims were true, since no stockpiles of weapons of mass destruction were found after the invasion. Source G [6]: content and context [3]: it shows mass public opposition, the demand to let inspectors finish and the belief that the war was about oil; the February 2003 march in London was one of the largest demonstrations in British history, and France, Germany and Russia also opposed war without a further UN resolution. Provenance [3]: a photograph records the scale of the crowd and the slogans chosen, useful for public opinion; it is less useful for the reasons behind government decisions, and placards simplify complex arguments. Together they show the official case and the opposition it faced.
