This set tests section 2.5 **Price determination** of the Cambridge O Level Economics 2281 syllabus for examination in 2026: market equilibrium and market disequilibrium. All markets and figures are invented. Teaching is in the [price determination study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-price-determination/) and the [price determination revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-price-determination-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define market equilibrium. **[2]**

**2.** The price of a product is set below its equilibrium level. Which describes the market?

A: Quantity supplied is greater than quantity demanded

B: There is a surplus and unsold stock builds up

C: Quantity demanded is greater than quantity supplied

D: The demand curve shifts to the left

**[1]**

**3.** In a market, Qd = 500 − 25P and Qs = 25P − 100. What are the equilibrium price and quantity?

A: $10 and 250 units

B: $12 and 200 units

C: $12 and 250 units

D: $14 and 150 units

**[1]**

**4.** The table shows the daily market for mangoes in a town.

| Price ($ per kg) | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Quantity demanded (kg) | 500 | 420 | 340 | 260 | 180 |
| Quantity supplied (kg) | 100 | 220 | 340 | 460 | 580 |

**(a)** State the equilibrium price and equilibrium sales. **[2]**

**(b)** Identify the type of disequilibrium at $5 and calculate its size. **[2]**

**(c)** State the quantity of mangoes actually sold at $5. **[1]**

**(d)** Calculate sellers' total revenue at equilibrium and at $5. **[2]**

**5.** State two signs that a market is experiencing a shortage. **[2]**

**6.** In a market for printer ink, Qd = 90 − 3P and Qs = 2P − 10.

**(a)** Calculate the equilibrium price and quantity. **[2]**

**(b)** The price is fixed at $16. Calculate the size of the disequilibrium and state its type. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** Explain, with the help of a demand and supply diagram described in words, how a market reaches equilibrium when the price starts above the equilibrium level. **[5]**

**8.** A football club sells match tickets at a price below the equilibrium price. Explain two likely consequences. **[4]**

**9.** Explain why fewer units are bought and sold at a disequilibrium price than at the equilibrium price. **[3]**

**10.** A diagram shows D and S crossing at a price of $8 and a quantity of 400. A horizontal line at $6 meets S at 300 units and D at 520 units.

**(a)** Identify the type of disequilibrium at $6 and calculate its size. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain what will happen to the price. **[2]**

**11.** Analyse why a surplus of fresh strawberries is likely to be cleared more quickly than a surplus of washing machines. **[4]**

**12.** Discuss whether a market will always reach its equilibrium price. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** The position where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied [1], so there is no tendency for the price to change (the market clears) [1]. **[2]**

**2.** C [1]. A price below equilibrium gives excess demand. A and B describe a price above equilibrium; D is a shift, not a result of a price setting. **[1]**

**3.** B [1]. 500 − 25P = 25P − 100, so 600 = 50P and P = 12; Q = 500 − 300 = 200. **[1]**

**4. (a)** Equilibrium price $3 per kg [1]; equilibrium sales 340 kg [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** A surplus [1] of 580 − 180 = 400 kg [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** 180 kg, because buyers only want 180 kg at that price [1]. **[1]**

**(d)** At equilibrium: 3 × 340 = $1,020 [1]. At $5: 5 × 180 = $900 [1]. **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* Revenue at a disequilibrium price uses the quantity actually sold. Using 580 kg would count mangoes that nobody bought.

**5.** Any two from: queues or waiting lists; goods selling out quickly; sellers limiting how many each customer may buy; buyers offering more than the asking price [1 each]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** 90 − 3P = 2P − 10, so 100 = 5P and P = $20 [1]. Q = 90 − 60 = 30 (check: 40 − 10 = 30) [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** At $16, Qd = 90 − 48 = 42 and Qs = 32 − 10 = 22 [1]. There is a shortage of 20 [1]. **[2]**

**7.** On a diagram with price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis, D slopes down and S slopes up, crossing at Pe and Qe [1]. At a price above Pe, a horizontal line meets D at a smaller quantity than S, so there is a surplus [1]. Sellers find stock building up and cut the price to clear it [1]. As the price falls, quantity demanded extends along D and quantity supplied contracts along S [1]. The surplus shrinks until the two quantities are equal at Pe, where the price stops falling [1]. **[5]**

*Examiner insight:* Neither curve moves. Answers that shift D to the right to "remove" the surplus confuse adjustment with a change in market conditions.

**8.** First consequence: a shortage, because more fans want tickets than there are seats [1], so some fans who are willing to pay the ticket price cannot get one, and queues or online waiting lists form [1]. Second consequence: tickets may be resold at higher prices by people who bought them [1], because some fans are willing to pay more than the official price, so the club earns less than the tickets are worth to buyers [1]. (Credit also: the club may ration tickets, for example to members.) **[4]**

**9.** At a disequilibrium price only the smaller of quantity demanded and quantity supplied can be traded [1]. With a shortage, buyers can only buy what sellers offer, which is less than equilibrium sales [1]; with a surplus, sellers can only sell what buyers want, which is also less than equilibrium sales [1]. **[3]**

**10. (a)** A shortage [1] of 520 − 300 = 220 units [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Buyers compete for the limited supply, so the price rises [1]. Quantity supplied extends and quantity demanded contracts until they are equal at $8 and 400 units [1]. **[2]**

**11.** Strawberries are perishable, so unsold stock loses its value within days [1]; sellers must cut the price quickly or throw the fruit away, so the price falls fast and quantity demanded extends [1]. Washing machines can be stored for months [1], so sellers can wait, cut output instead, or reduce the price gradually, which means the surplus may last longer [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Yes: in a free market a surplus pushes sellers to cut prices and a shortage lets them raise prices [1], so the price moves towards the level where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied [1]. No: where prices are fixed by contract or printed in advance, they may stay wrong for some time, so a shortage or surplus lasts [1]; where a government sets a maximum or minimum price by law, the price cannot move to equilibrium [1]; and if conditions of demand or supply keep changing, the equilibrium itself keeps moving, so the market may always be chasing it [1]. Judgement: most free markets move towards equilibrium, quickly where prices can change easily, but legal price controls and fixed prices can stop it being reached [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a price above equilibrium a shortage, or below it a surplus.
- Giving one quantity rather than the gap between quantity demanded and quantity supplied.
- Using the larger quantity when calculating sales or revenue at a disequilibrium price.
- Shifting a curve to explain how the market returns to equilibrium.
- Not checking an equation answer by substituting back into both equations.
- Ending a discussion without saying what the outcome depends on.
