## Definitions to learn

| Term | Definition | Ref |
|---|---|---|
| Mixed economy | Resources are allocated by both the market (private sector) and the government (public sector) | 1.1.5 a |
| Private sector | Part of the economy owned and run by individuals and firms | 1.1.5 b |
| Public sector | Part of the economy owned and controlled by the government | 1.1.5 b |
| Market failure | Resources are used inefficiently in a free market | 1.1.5 e |
| Public good | A good with both characteristics: non-excludable and non-rival | 1.1.5 g |
| Non-excludable | Nobody can be stopped from using it once it exists | 1.1.5 g |
| Non-rival | Use by one person leaves the same amount for everyone else | 1.1.5 g |
| Free rider problem | People use a good without paying, so private firms cannot cover costs and do not provide it | 1.1.5 g |
| Privatisation | A business or asset moves from government ownership to private ownership | 1.1.5 j |
| External cost | A cost of production that falls on a third party | 1.1.6 a |
| External benefit | A benefit of consumption that goes to a third party | 1.1.6 c |

## Public versus private sector

| | Private | Public |
|---|---|---|
| Ownership | Individuals, shareholders | Government |
| Control | Owners and managers | Ministers and officials |
| Usual aim | Profit, growth | Welfare, access, meeting needs |

Take care: this is the usual emphasis. A state-owned firm may be told to cover its costs, and a private firm may care about reputation.

## What, how and for whom in a mixed economy

| Question | Market answer | Government answer |
|---|---|---|
| What | What consumers will pay for, because demand signals profit | Public goods, and services such as education and healthcare; taxes and subsidies change the mix |
| How | Cheapest method that works | Rules on safety and pollution; public budgets for its own services |
| For whom | Those with income to pay | Taxes, benefits and free or cheap services shift goods towards lower incomes |

Link in one line: market gives efficiency and choice; government adds fairness and fills gaps.

## Market failure and intervention

- Market failure means an inefficient allocation of resources.
- Two causes in this unit: no provision (public goods) and wrong amount (externalities).
- Reasons to intervene: provide what the market will not, reduce harm, increase beneficial activity, help those who cannot pay.
- Tools: tax, subsidy, regulation, direct provision, ownership.
- Limit: intervention costs money and officials may get it wrong.

## Public goods in steps

Method for "Explain why a good is a public good":

1. Say it is non-excludable and explain: nobody can be stopped from using it.
2. Say it is non-rival and explain: use by one does not reduce use by others.
3. Link to free riding: people use it without paying.
4. Conclude: private firms cannot earn revenue, so the market does not provide it, and the government provides it using tax.

Examples: street lighting, flood defences, national defence. Not public goods: a toll bridge (excludable), an ordinary school place (limited and rival), a restaurant meal (rival and excludable).

Small worked reminder: a village of 50 households wants a drain costing 400. Voluntary scheme: only 3 give 10 each = 30, which is 370 short. A local tax of 400 ÷ 50 = 8 each pays for it.

## Roles and importance of the sectors (1.1.5 h, i)

- Private sector: most goods and services bought by consumers.
- Public sector: public goods, and often education, healthcare, policing, some transport and utilities.
- Importance varies by economy. Measures: government spending as a share of output, share of workers employed by government, share of output from state-owned firms.
- Formula: government spending ÷ national output × 100. Example: 90 ÷ 360 × 100 = 25%.

## Privatisation (1.1.5 j, k)

| Group | Possible gain | Possible loss |
|---|---|---|
| Consumers | Quality, choice, lower prices if competition grows | Higher prices, reduced service in unprofitable areas |
| Workers | Higher pay in a growing firm | Redundancies, tougher conditions |
| Businesses | Profit motive, efficiency, freedom to invest | Shareholder pressure, competition |
| Government | Sale proceeds, lower subsidy | Loses future profits and direct control |

Small worked reminders:

- Workforce 500 to 425: (425 − 500) ÷ 500 × 100 = −15%.
- Price 4.00 to 3.60: (3.60 − 4.00) ÷ 4.00 × 100 = −10%.
- Proceeds 60, subsidy saved 8 a year: 60 ÷ 8 = 7.5 years of subsidy.

Evaluation prompts: Is the firm a monopoly? Are there regulators? Which group is considered? Short run or long run?

## Externalities (1.1.6)

| | External cost | External benefit |
|---|---|---|
| Arises from | Production (or any activity that harms others) | Consumption |
| Examples | Pollution, congestion, environmental damage | Education, healthcare, vaccinations |
| Market result | Too much produced, because firm ignores the cost | Too little consumed, because buyers ignore the gain |
| Formula | Social cost = private cost + external cost | For benefits, add the external part to the private part to get the social benefit |

In full, the benefit rule reads: social benefit = private benefit + external benefit. Rearranged: external cost = social cost − private cost; external benefit = social benefit − private benefit.

Method for externality calculations:

1. Write the formula.
2. Substitute values per unit.
3. Multiply by the quantity if a total is asked.
4. State units and say who bears the cost or receives the benefit.

Small worked reminder: a quarry has private cost 40 per tonne and external cost 12 per tonne for dust. Social cost = 40 + 12 = 52 per tonne. For 500 tonnes, external cost = 12 × 500 = 6,000.

Chain for an external cost: firm ignores harm, so its cost looks low, so it supplies too much, so society suffers net loss. A tax of 12 per tonne would make the firm pay the external cost.

Chain for an external benefit: buyer counts only own gain, so demand is low, so too little is bought, so society misses out. A subsidy or free provision can raise the amount.

## Building a longer answer

For an "explain" or "evaluate" question, use a short chain: point, development, link to the question, then a judgement if asked.

- Point: privatisation may raise prices for consumers.
- Development: a private owner seeks profit, so it may raise fares or cut unprofitable services.
- Link: this matters most if the firm has no competitors.
- Judgement: the effect on consumers depends on competition, so the gain to the government must be weighed against the loss to consumers.

Use the same pattern for externalities: name the third party, say what they gain or lose, say what the decision-maker ignores, and state the result for the amount produced or consumed.

## Must-know distinctions

- Non-excludable (cannot stop use) versus non-rival (use does not reduce supply). Both are needed.
- Public good (a type of good) versus public sector (who owns and runs).
- Public sector provision versus privatisation: the first is government supply, the second moves ownership away from government.
- Private cost (to the producer) versus social cost (private plus external).
- External cost of production versus external benefit of consumption: learn the pairing the specification uses, and keep the wording of each definition separate.

## Quick self-test

1. Define a mixed economy.
2. State the two characteristics of a public good.
3. A good has private cost 75 and external cost 30 per unit. Find the social cost.
4. Social cost is 140 and private cost is 90. Find the external cost.
5. A vaccination has private benefit 20 and external benefit 35. Find the social benefit.
6. Government spending is 90 and national output is 360. Calculate the public sector share.
7. A privatised firm cuts its workforce from 500 to 425. Find the percentage change.
8. Give two examples of external benefits.
9. A firm makes 500 units, each with an external cost of 18. Find the total external cost.
10. Explain in one sentence why a free rider problem stops the market providing a good.

### Answers

1. An economy where resources are allocated by both the private sector (the market) and the public sector (the government).
2. Non-excludable and non-rival.
3. 75 + 30 = 105.
4. 140 − 90 = 50.
5. 20 + 35 = 55.
6. 90 ÷ 360 × 100 = 25%.
7. (425 − 500) ÷ 500 × 100 = −15%, a fall of 15%.
8. Any two from education, healthcare, vaccinations.
9. 18 × 500 = 9,000.
10. People can use the good without paying, so firms cannot earn enough revenue to cover costs and do not supply it.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Quoting just one characteristic of a public good, or confusing non-rival with non-excludable.
- Saying that any state-provided service is a public good.
- Defining an externality without mentioning a third party.
- Mixing the formulae, for example adding external cost to private benefit.
- Giving an example with no reasoning, such as "pollution" with no explanation of who bears the cost.
- Writing that privatisation is always good or always bad, with no condition or conclusion.
- Listing effects on consumers, workers, businesses and government without saying which are gains and which are losses.
- Leaving out units, or forgetting to multiply by the quantity when a total is asked for.
