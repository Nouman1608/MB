## Section A

**1.** Which of the following is a qualified right? A: Article 2. B: Article 3. C: Article 5. D: Article 10. **[1]**

**2.** In which case did the European Court describe the Convention as a living instrument? A: Tyrer v UK. B: Airey v Ireland. C: Engel v Netherlands. D: Campbell v MGN. **[1]**

**3.** Distinguish between a limited right and a qualified right. **[2]**

**4.** State what is meant by a positive obligation under the European Convention on Human Rights. **[2]**

**5.** Identify two sources of human rights rules in the law of England and Wales other than the Human Rights Act 1998. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Explain the principle that Convention rights must be "practical and effective". **[3]**

**7.** Explain how Convention rights can affect a dispute between two private parties. **[4]**

**8.** Explain the principle of legality and its role in protecting rights at common law. **[4]**

**9.** Explain the positive obligations that the state may have under Articles 2 and 8, referring to one case for each. **[5]**

**10.** *A council bans a residents' group from distributing leaflets criticising its planning decisions, saying the leaflets are defamatory of the council. It also refuses to investigate threats of violence made against the group's leader by a property developer, which the police have reported to it as serious.* Suggest which rights are engaged and how the rules apply. **[6]**

**11.** *Parliament passes an Act giving a minister power "to make such regulations as she thinks fit for the management of prisons". The minister makes a regulation banning prisoners from all contact with journalists, including about possible miscarriages of justice.* Advise a prisoner who wishes to challenge the regulation, using both common law and Convention rules. **[8]**

**12.** Discuss whether the interpretive principles used by the European Court of Human Rights give it too much power. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** D: Article 10. **[1]**

**2.** A: Tyrer v UK. **[1]**

**3.** A limited right may be restricted **only in the situations listed in the Article itself**, such as Articles 2 and 5 [1]. A qualified right may be restricted where the interference is **prescribed by law, for a legitimate aim and necessary in a democratic society**, such as Articles 8, 10 and 11 [1].

**4.** A duty on the state to **take active steps to protect** a Convention right [1], including against interference by **private individuals**, rather than simply refraining from interfering itself [1].

**5.** Any two of: the **Convention text** and **Strasbourg case law** [1]; **statutes** such as PACE 1984 or the Public Order Act 1986; **common law** cases such as Entick v Carrington (1765) or Derbyshire CC v Times Newspapers (1993) [1].

**6.** The Convention guarantees rights that are **practical and effective, not theoretical or illusory** [1]. In **Airey v Ireland (1979)** a woman who could not afford a lawyer to seek a judicial separation had no real access to court [1], so the state breached the Convention even though there was no formal bar [1].

**7.** Convention rights are primarily **vertical**, binding public authorities under **s6 HRA 1998** [1]. But **courts are public authorities**, so they must develop the common law compatibly with Convention rights [1]. This gives **indirect horizontal effect** [1], as in **Campbell v MGN (2004)**, where the tort of misuse of private information was developed between a model and a newspaper by balancing Articles 8 and 10 [1].

**8.** The principle of legality means that **Parliament can override fundamental rights, but only by clear words** [1]; general words in a statute are presumed not to authorise interference with them [1]. In **R v SSHD, ex parte Simms (1999)** Lord Hoffmann said Parliament must "squarely confront" what it is doing [1]. It lets courts protect rights at common law, without the Convention, by reading statutory powers narrowly [1].

**9.** Under **Article 2**, the state must take reasonable steps to protect a person from a **real and immediate risk to life** that the authorities knew or ought to have known about [1], as in **Osman v UK (1998)** [1], and must **investigate** deaths [1]. Under **Article 8**, the state must provide effective protection of private life, including through the criminal law [1]; in **X and Y v Netherlands (1985)** a gap preventing prosecution of a sexual assault breached Article 8 [1].

**10.** The leaflet ban engages **Article 10**, a qualified right [1]; the council is a **public authority** under s6 [1]. Protecting its reputation is unlikely to justify the ban; at common law **Derbyshire CC v Times Newspapers (1993)** shows a council cannot sue in defamation [1], and a total ban on criticism is unlikely to be **proportionate** [1]. The refusal to act on serious threats may breach the **positive obligation under Article 2** [1] if there was a **real and immediate risk** the authorities knew of (Osman) [1].

**11.** At common law, the **principle of legality** applies: general words such as "as she thinks fit" do not authorise interference with fundamental rights [1]. In **ex parte Simms (1999)** a blanket ban on prisoners' interviews with journalists about their convictions was held unlawful on this basis [1], so the regulation is likely **ultra vires** [1]. Under the Convention, the ban engages **Article 10**, including the right to impart information [1]. It may also engage **Article 8** (correspondence) [1]. A **blanket** ban is unlikely to be **necessary and proportionate** to the aim of prison order [1]. The minister is a **public authority** under s6, and the prisoner a **victim** under s7 [1]. Conclusion: judicial review on both grounds, with the regulation likely to be quashed [1].

**12.** Award one mark per developed point, to a maximum of 8:
- The **living instrument** approach (Tyrer) keeps the Convention relevant as social values change [1].
- But critics argue it lets **unelected judges** extend rights beyond what states agreed in 1950 [1].
- **Practical and effective** (Airey) prevents states avoiding duties through formal compliance [1].
- **Positive obligations** extend the reach of rights into policy and resources, which some see as overreach [1].
- **Autonomous meanings** stop states escaping the Convention by relabelling measures [1].
- The **margin of appreciation** limits the Court's power and respects national choices on moral issues [1].
- The Court's judgments bind states only in international law, and national parliaments keep the final word in the UK (HRA s4) [1].
- A **reasoned conclusion**, for example that the principles are necessary for effective protection, with the margin of appreciation acting as a check [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Applying the wrong test because the right was not classified first.
- Forgetting positive obligations when the threat comes from a private person.
- Ignoring common law rules, such as the principle of legality, when a statute uses general words.
- Naming principles without a supporting case.
- Writing a one-sided essay without a conclusion.
