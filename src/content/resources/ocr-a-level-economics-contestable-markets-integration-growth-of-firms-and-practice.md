## Questions

**1.** State three characteristics of a perfectly contestable market. **[3]**

**2.** In Town B (illustrative) a single incumbent charges £20 with average cost £15 and sells 400 units a week. An entrant charges £18, wins the whole market and keeps its average cost at £15. The incumbent takes three weeks to react. Entry costs £4,000.

**(a)** Calculate the entrant's total profit before the incumbent reacts, ignoring the entry cost. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the net outcome if the entry cost is wholly sunk and if it is wholly recoverable. State which case is contestable. **[3]**

**3.** Explain, with reference to a diagram, why a firm in a perfectly contestable market is both productively and allocatively efficient. **[5]**

**4.** Evaluate the contribution of contestable market theory to the analysis of the behaviour of firms. **[6]**

**5.** Firm A makes 50,000 units at average cost £6.20 and firm B makes 20,000 at £6.80. After a horizontal merger the combined firm makes 70,000 units at average cost £5.60. Calculate the percentage fall in total cost. **[4]**

**6.** Identify the type of integration in each case. **[3]**
(i) A cinema chain buys a rival chain.
(ii) A brewer buys a hop farm that supplies it.
(iii) A furniture maker buys a software company.

**7.** A manufacturer takes over the shops that sell its goods. Evaluate whether consumers are likely to benefit. **[8]**

**8.** A firm has 10% of a £60m market. A year later it has 12% of a £63m market.

**(a)** Calculate its revenue in each year. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate by what percentage revenue grew. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate how much of the extra revenue came from winning share rather than from market growth. **[1]**

**9.** A regulator allows a firm to raise its price by inflation minus X. Inflation is 4%, X is 2.5% and the current price is £80.

**(a)** Calculate the maximum permitted price. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why this rule gives the firm an incentive to cut costs. **[2]**
**(c)** State one risk of the rule. **[1]**

**10.** Members of a cartel expect to gain £9m a year for three years. The chance of detection is 0.25 and the fine is 8% of annual turnover of £150m.

**(a)** Calculate the expected fine. **[2]**
**(b)** State whether the cartel is deterred. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the fine needed to deter the cartel. **[1]**

**11.** Evaluate the extent to which firms are interdependent. **[8]**

**12.** Evaluate the impact of regulation and ownership on the behaviour of firms. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Three from: no barriers to entry or exit [1]; no sunk costs [1]; entrants can match incumbents' technology and costs [1]. Also creditworthy: entrants have good information, and entry and exit are faster than incumbents' price changes. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the feature rather than describing the market as simply competitive; sunk costs is an easy characteristic to leave out.

**2. (a)** Weekly profit = (18 − 15) × 400 = £1,200 [1]. Over three weeks, 3 × 1,200 = **£3,600** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Sunk: 3,600 − 4,000 = **−£400** [1]. Recoverable: the cost is regained, so the gain is **£3,600** [1]. The recoverable case is contestable, because sunk costs deter entry [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* State clearly which case is contestable and why; a correct calculation without that link leaves the economics unsaid.

**3.** Supernormal profit attracts hit-and-run entry [1]. So price is driven down to average cost, leaving normal profit [1]. Cost above the minimum would let an entrant undercut, so the firm must produce at the lowest point of the AC curve: productive efficiency [1]. At that point MC equals AC [1]. Since price equals AC, price equals MC: allocative efficiency [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* A labelled diagram with AR meeting AC at its minimum, and MC passing through that point, supports each step. Say where the curves meet, not just that they do.

**4.** Contribution: it shifts attention from number of firms to barriers and sunk costs [1]; it provides a benchmark and supports policy that lowers barriers [1]. Limits: real markets have sunk costs such as advertising [1]; incumbents can respond quickly or price strategically [1]. Judgement: it is a matter of degree [1] and the theory is most helpful in markets with low sunk costs [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation credit comes from weighing the points and reaching a judgement; a list of strengths followed by a list of weaknesses leaves the weighing undone.

**5.** Old total cost = 50,000 × 6.20 + 20,000 × 6.80 = 310,000 + 136,000 = £446,000 [1]. New total cost = 70,000 × 5.60 = £392,000 [1]. Saving = £54,000 [1]. Percentage fall = 54,000 ÷ 446,000 × 100 = **12.1%** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Show both totals before dividing. Use the old total as the base for a percentage fall.

**6.** (i) Horizontal [1]. (ii) Vertical backward [1]. (iii) Conglomerate [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* For vertical integration, state the direction as well as the type.

**7.** Forward vertical integration [1]. Benefits: removes a retail margin, so lower price is possible [1]; closer control of quality and service [1]; economies from coordinated stock and distribution [1]. Risks: the manufacturer may refuse to supply rival shops, reducing choice [1]; market power may raise prices [1]. Judgement: it depends on the strength of competing manufacturers and whether the market is contestable [1], so consumers benefit if rivalry forces the savings to be passed on [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep the answer on consumers. Points about shareholders or workers may be true but do not answer the question as set.

**8. (a)** Year one: 0.10 × 60 = **£6.0m** [1]. Year two: 0.12 × 63 = **£7.56m** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** (7.56 − 6.0) ÷ 6.0 × 100 = **26%** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** At constant share, revenue would be 0.10 × 63 = £6.3m, so share gain explains 7.56 − 6.30 = **£1.26m** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Give units (£m) with each figure; mixing percentages and millions in the same line is easy to misread.

**9. (a)** Permitted rise = 4 − 2.5 = 1.5% [1]. Price = 80 × 1.015 = **£81.20** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The price can rise by less than inflation, so costs must grow more slowly to keep the margin [1]; any cost saving beyond X is kept as extra profit [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Any one: firm cuts quality or investment; regulator lacks information; regulatory capture [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Part (b) asks about incentive, so tie the answer to what the firm keeps rather than only restating the rule.

**10. (a)** Fine = 0.08 × 150 = £12m [1]. Expected fine = 0.25 × 12 = **£3m** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Total gain is 3 × 9 = £27m, far above £3m, so the cartel is not deterred [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Fine needed = 27 ÷ 0.25 = **£108m**, which is 72% of turnover [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Include the probability of detection; a comparison of gain with the fine alone overstates deterrence.

**11.** Interdependence means one firm's decisions affect rivals [1]. It is high in oligopoly, where few firms must anticipate reactions [1]. It is low in perfect and monopolistic competition, where many firms act independently [1]. Even a monopoly may be constrained by potential entrants if the market is contestable [1]. All firms are linked through suppliers, customers and labour markets [1]. The degree depends on number of firms, product differentiation and barriers [1], and on how the market is defined and the time period [1]. Judgement: interdependence is a matter of degree, strongest in concentrated markets with similar products [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Reach a reasoned conclusion on extent. Describing each market structure without a comparison leaves the question of how far unanswered.

**12.** Ownership: private owners usually pursue profit [1]; public bodies may pursue welfare or political targets [1]; principal-agent problems can weaken cost discipline in either [1]. Regulation: a price cap changes incentives to cut cost [1]; quality standards protect consumers [1]; information gaps and capture reduce effectiveness [1]. Context: effects depend on how competitive the industry is and how costly entry is [1]; over time a strict cap may reduce investment [1]. Judgement: regulation matters more where competition is weak, while ownership shapes objectives everywhere [1], and the best outcome combines clear incentives with monitoring [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Cover both regulation and ownership, and use an industry context of your choosing to ground the argument; a one-sided answer leaves half the command unaddressed.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying a contestable market needs many firms.
- Citing low barriers without mentioning sunk costs.
- Claiming allocative efficiency without showing price equal to MC.
- Giving profit figures without the entry cost and the reaction time.
- Using the later value as the base when finding growth.
- Describing integration without stating the direction of vertical integration.
- Treating every merger as harmful, or every cost saving as passed on.
- Ignoring the probability of detection in a deterrence calculation.
- Evaluating regulation or ownership without a judgement.
