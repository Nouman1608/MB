This guide covers outcomes **2.1.4 to 2.1.11**: capital and revenue reserves, how six named reserves are created and used, authorised, issued and called-up share capital, rights and bonus issues, the ledger accounts and journal entries for share issues, the features of shares and debentures, capital gearing and the creation and features of provisions. They form the second group in Topic 2.1, Limited companies, in Unit 2 (Corporate and Management Accounting) of the Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Subsidiary/Advanced Level in Accounting (YAC11), Specification Issue 2, September 2018. This is Unit 2 (A2) content. The forfeiture of shares is not examined. All amounts are in dollars and every company named is invented.

## 2.1.4 and 2.1.5 Reserves

Equity is share capital plus **reserves**. A reserve is a part of equity that is not share capital.

- **Capital reserves** arise from capital events, not from trading profit. They cannot be paid out as dividends. Examples: share premium, revaluation reserve, capital redemption reserve.
- **Revenue reserves** arise from trading profit. They can be distributed as dividends. Examples: retained earnings and general reserve.

| Reserve | How it is created | How it is used |
|---|---|---|
| **Retained earnings** | Profit for the year not paid out as dividends | Dividends; transfers to other reserves; bonus issues |
| **General reserve** | Transfer from retained earnings | Kept for future needs; may be transferred back; bonus issues |
| **Share premium** | Shares issued above their nominal value | Bonus issues; writing off share issue costs; not for dividends |
| **Revaluation reserve** | Gain on revaluing non-current assets | Bonus issues; transferred to retained earnings when the asset is sold |
| **Capital redemption reserve** | Created when shares are redeemed or bought back out of profits | Bonus issues only |
| **Foreign exchange reserve** | Exchange differences on translating a foreign operation | Held in equity; transferred to profit when the operation is disposed of |

**Examples.** (1) A company transfers 30,000 from retained earnings to general reserve: Dr retained earnings 30,000, Cr general reserve 30,000. Total equity does not change. (2) Land is revalued from 200,000 to 260,000: Dr land 60,000, Cr revaluation reserve 60,000. (3) A company redeems 50,000 redeemable preference shares of $1 at par out of retained earnings: Dr redeemable preference shares 50,000, Cr bank 50,000; and Dr retained earnings 50,000, Cr capital redemption reserve 50,000. The second entry keeps the capital protected for creditors, because the capital has been replaced by an undistributable reserve. (4) A foreign subsidiary's net assets, retranslated at the closing rate, give an exchange gain of 12,000: Dr investment 12,000, Cr foreign exchange reserve 12,000 (shown in other comprehensive income).

## 2.1.6 Authorised, issued and called-up share capital

- **Authorised share capital:** the maximum nominal value the company is allowed to issue under its constitution.
- **Issued share capital:** the nominal value of shares actually issued to shareholders.
- **Called-up share capital:** the amount of issued capital the company has asked shareholders to pay so far.

Example: a company may issue up to 1,000,000 $1 shares (authorised). It has issued 600,000 (issued). It has called 75 cents per share, so called-up capital is 600,000 × 0.75 = 450,000. If a shareholder has not paid a call, the unpaid amount is a receivable called *calls in arrears*.

## 2.1.7 and 2.1.8 New issues, rights issues and bonus issues

### New issue with a premium

A company issues 80,000 $1 ordinary shares at $1.50. The shares are payable: application 0.40, allotment 0.70 (including the premium of 0.50) and first and final call 0.40. All shares are applied for and paid.

| Stage | Calculation | Bank | Share capital | Share premium |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Application | 80,000 × 0.40 | 32,000 | 32,000 | |
| Allotment | 80,000 × 0.70 | 56,000 | 16,000 | 40,000 |
| Call | 80,000 × 0.40 | 32,000 | 32,000 | |
| **Total** | 80,000 × 1.50 | **120,000** | **80,000** | **40,000** |

Journal for application: Dr bank 32,000, Cr application and allotment account 32,000; then Dr application and allotment account 32,000, Cr share capital 32,000. Journal for allotment: Dr application and allotment account 56,000, Cr share capital 16,000, Cr share premium 40,000; and Dr bank 56,000, Cr application and allotment account 56,000. Call: Dr call account 32,000, Cr share capital 32,000; Dr bank 32,000, Cr call account 32,000.

### Bonus issue

A **bonus issue** gives existing shareholders free shares in proportion to their holdings. A company with 400,000 $1 shares makes a 1 for 4 bonus issue, so it issues 100,000 new shares. No cash is received. Journal: Dr share premium (or retained earnings or another reserve) 100,000, Cr share capital 100,000. Total equity is unchanged. The reserves are converted into share capital, which makes them undistributable. The number of shares rises by 25%, so the price per share falls, which can make the shares easier to trade. Existing shareholders' total wealth is unchanged.

### Rights issue

A **rights issue** offers existing shareholders the right to buy new shares, usually below the market price, in proportion to their holdings. A 1 for 5 rights issue at $1.20 when the market price is $1.80 on 400,000 $1 shares means 80,000 new shares and cash of 80,000 × 1.20 = 96,000. Journal: Dr bank 96,000, Cr share capital 80,000, Cr share premium 16,000.

Theoretical ex-rights price = (5 × 1.80 + 1 × 1.20) ÷ 6 = 10.20 ÷ 6 = **1.70**. A shareholder who takes up the rights is not worse off. One who sells the rights receives the value of the discount.

| | Bonus issue | Rights issue |
|---|---|---|
| Cash raised | None | Yes |
| Change in total equity | None | Increases |
| Price paid by shareholders | Nil | Below market price |
| Purpose | Capitalise reserves, increase the number of shares | Raise new finance from existing owners |

## 2.1.9 Features of shares and debentures

| | Ordinary shares | Preference shares | Debentures |
|---|---|---|---|
| Status | Ownership | Ownership, with preference | Loan, not ownership |
| Return | Dividend that varies with profit | Fixed dividend rate | Fixed interest |
| Priority | Paid last | Paid before ordinary dividends | Paid before all dividends; interest is an expense |
| Voting | Normally yes | Normally no | No |
| On liquidation | Last | After debentures | First, especially if secured |
| Repayment | Not repayable | May be redeemable | Repayable on a fixed date |

Preference shares may be **cumulative** (unpaid dividends carry forward) or **non-cumulative**, and may be **redeemable**. Debentures may be secured on assets. Interest on debentures is deducted before profit; dividends are an appropriation of profit.

## 2.1.10 Capital gearing

**Capital gearing** compares the money borrowed or fixed-return capital with the total long-term capital:

Gearing = (non-current liabilities, including preference shares) ÷ (equity + non-current liabilities) × 100

For Rowan Components Ltd (equity 868,000, debentures 200,000): 200,000 ÷ 1,068,000 × 100 = **18.7%**, which is low gearing.

**Implications.** Two companies each have 800,000 of capital. A has equity of 800,000 and no debt. B has equity of 400,000 and 8% debentures of 400,000, so gearing is 400,000 ÷ 800,000 = 50%. B pays 32,000 of interest each year.

| Profit before interest | Return on equity: A | Return on equity: B |
|---|---:|---:|
| 40,000 | 40,000 ÷ 800,000 = 5.0% | (40,000 − 32,000) ÷ 400,000 = 2.0% |
| 100,000 | 100,000 ÷ 800,000 = 12.5% | (100,000 − 32,000) ÷ 400,000 = 17.0% |

When profit is high, high gearing magnifies returns to ordinary shareholders. When profit is low, it magnifies the fall. Higher gearing also means a greater risk that the company cannot pay interest, so lenders may refuse more loans or ask for higher interest. A highly geared company needs steady profits. Low gearing is safer but may miss the benefit of cheaper debt finance.

## 2.1.11 Provisions

A **provision** is a liability of uncertain timing or amount. It is recognised when the company has a present obligation from a past event, an outflow of resources is probable, and a reliable estimate can be made. Examples: a claim for damages, a warranty provision or a restructuring plan that has been announced.

Creation: Dr statement of comprehensive income (expense), Cr provision (liability in the statement of financial position). If the amount changes, only the increase or decrease goes to profit. When the cost occurs, it is charged against the provision. A provision differs from a reserve because it is a charge against profit for a known obligation, whereas a reserve is an appropriation of profit that forms part of equity. If an outflow is only possible, not probable, the item is a contingent liability, disclosed in a note and not provided for.

## Common mistakes

- Treating share premium as distributable or as a revenue reserve.
- Recording a bonus issue as a receipt of cash.
- Forgetting the share premium on a rights issue.
- Using the wrong gearing formula or not stating which formula is used.
- Mixing up authorised, issued and called-up capital.
- Confusing a provision with a reserve, or with an accrual.
- Recording the capital redemption reserve entry but not the redemption entry (or the reverse).
- Treating preference dividends as an expense in the statement of comprehensive income.

## Exam technique

- Show each journal with a narrative: debit, credit, amount and reason.
- For gearing, state the formula, calculate, and explain what the percentage means for risk and return.
- **Explain** a reserve: where it comes from, whether it is distributable, how it can be used.
- **Compare** rights and bonus issues using cash, equity and purpose.
- Check that total equity changes only when something real enters or leaves the company.
