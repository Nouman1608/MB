This guide covers **Topic 1 The Nature and Variety of Living
Organisms**, the first of five topics in Pearson Edexcel International
GCSE Biology (4BI1), first teaching September 2017. The qualification
is untiered and linear, assessed across two written papers.

## Where this fits in 4BI1

Topic 1 establishes the characteristics shared by living organisms
and the variety of living organisms (the common features of plants,
animals, fungi, protoctists and bacteria, and pathogens including
viruses). Cell structure is not part of Topic 1; it belongs to Topic 2.
Topic 1 provides the foundation for the four topics that follow:
Structure and functions in living organisms, Reproduction and inheritance, Ecology and the
environment, and Use of biological resources.

## Syllabus coverage

**PEARSON EDEXCEL INTERNATIONAL GCSE BIOLOGY (4BI1) — TOPIC 1 THE
NATURE AND VARIETY OF LIVING ORGANISMS**

Topic 1 covers two named sub-topics: the characteristics common to
all living organisms, and the variety of living organisms, including
their classification into major groups. In total the specification
sets out four numbered outcomes across these two sub-topics. The
structure and function of plant and animal cells, and the movement of
substances into and out of cells, are Topic 2 sub-topics and are
covered in the Cell structure and biological molecules guide.

## How to approach it

Because this topic sets out a small number of named outcomes, work
through the specification's own points systematically when revising,
checking off each one, rather than relying on general topic summaries
that can miss specific testable detail. Classification questions often
ask candidates to place an unfamiliar organism into a group based on
described features, so practise applying classification criteria to
new examples rather than only memorising named groups. (Cell
structure and the movement of substances by diffusion and osmosis are
tested in Topic 2, not here.)

## Characteristics of living organisms

All living organisms share eight characteristics, remembered as **MRS GREN**: movement, respiration, sensitivity, growth, reproduction, excretion, nutrition — with the eighth, control of internal conditions, often added as homeostasis.

The distinction that catches candidates out is **excretion** versus **egestion**. Excretion removes waste made *by* the body's own reactions, such as carbon dioxide and urea. Egestion removes undigested food that never entered the cells at all.

**Growth** is defined precisely as a **permanent increase in dry mass**, not simply an increase in mass or size. An organism that takes up water increases in mass but not in dry mass, so water uptake alone does not count as growth — the "dry" qualifier is what the mark scheme is checking for.

## The five kingdoms

| Kingdom | Cell type | Cell wall | Nutrition | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Animals | Eukaryotic | None | Heterotrophic | mammals, insects |
| Plants | Eukaryotic | Cellulose | Autotrophic, photosynthesis | flowering plants, ferns |
| Fungi | Eukaryotic | Chitin | Saprotrophic | mushrooms, yeast, Mucor |
| Protoctists | Eukaryotic | Sometimes | Varies | Amoeba, Chlorella |
| Prokaryotes | Prokaryotic | Not cellulose | Varies | bacteria, Lactobacillus |

Fungi feed **saprotrophically**: they secrete enzymes onto food outside the body, digest it externally, and absorb the products. Many fungi are multicellular with hyphae forming a mycelium, though yeast is single-celled.

Prokaryotes have no nucleus, mitochondria or chloroplasts. Their DNA is a circular loop, often with plasmids.

**Storage compounds** also distinguish the kingdoms and are examined more often than expected: **plants** store carbohydrate as **starch and sucrose**, while **animals and fungi** both store carbohydrate as **glycogen**. This is easy to mix up, since fungi are sometimes wrongly assumed to store starch alongside plants simply because both are non-animal kingdoms — the correct pairing is by nutrition type, not by "plant-like" appearance.

## Viruses

Viruses sit outside the kingdoms because they are not cells. They have no cytoplasm, no organelles and no metabolism of their own. Each is a protein coat around either DNA or RNA, and can reproduce only inside a host cell, which makes every virus a parasite. Examples include the tobacco mosaic virus, influenza and HIV.

Whether viruses are alive is a legitimate question — they fail most of MRS GREN outside a host.

## Levels of organisation (Topic 2 sub-topic (a)) and pathogens

The levels-of-organisation hierarchy below is strictly sub-topic (a) of Topic 2, Structures and Functions in Living Organisms, not Topic 1 content -- it is included here for continuity with the classification material above. Organisms are organised as organelles, cells, **tissues**, **organs**, **organ systems**. A **tissue** is a group of cells with similar structure and function, e.g. glandular tissue in the stomach lining, which secretes digestive enzymes. An **organ** is a group of *different* tissues working together to perform a specific function, e.g. the stomach, made up of glandular, muscular and epithelial tissue. An **organ system** is a group of organs with related functions, e.g. the digestive system, made up of the stomach, small and large intestines, and associated glands. A pathogen is a microorganism that causes disease, and can be from any of several groups: bacteria (cholera), fungi (athlete's foot), protoctists (malaria, via *Plasmodium*) or viruses (influenza).

## Worked example

Explain why a mushroom is not classified as a plant.

```
No chlorophyll        -> cannot photosynthesise
Nutrition             -> saprotrophic, not autotrophic
Cell wall             -> made of chitin, not cellulose
Body structure        -> hyphae forming a mycelium, not tissues and organs
Storage carbohydrate  -> glycogen, not starch
```

Any three of those, clearly contrasted with plants, will score.

## Common mistakes

Confusing excretion with egestion. Saying fungi "eat" their food — they digest externally then absorb. Classifying viruses in a kingdom, or describing them as cells. Stating all fungi are multicellular, forgetting yeast. Writing that prokaryotes have "no DNA" rather than no nucleus. Calling *Plasmodium* a bacterium when it is a protoctist.

## Quick revision checklist

- List the characteristics of living organisms and distinguish excretion from egestion.
- Describe the five kingdoms with cell type, cell wall, nutrition and named examples.
- Explain saprotrophic nutrition step by step.
- Describe the structure of a virus and explain why it is not placed in a kingdom.
- Name a pathogen from each group and the disease it causes.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is saprotrophic nutrition?**
Saprotrophic nutrition is the way fungi feed: they secrete enzymes onto food outside the body, digest it externally and absorb the products.

**Why are viruses not placed in any kingdom?**
Viruses are not cells. They have no cytoplasm, organelles or metabolism of their own; each is a protein coat around DNA or RNA and can reproduce only inside a host cell.

**How is growth defined in Edexcel IGCSE Biology?**
Growth is a permanent increase in dry mass. Taking up water increases mass but not dry mass, so it does not count as growth.

**Is cell structure part of Topic 1 in Edexcel 4BI1?**
No. Topic 1 covers the characteristics and variety of living organisms. The structure of plant and animal cells, and the movement of substances into and out of cells, belong to Topic 2.
