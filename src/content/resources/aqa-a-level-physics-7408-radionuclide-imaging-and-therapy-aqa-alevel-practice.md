These questions cover section **3.10.6 Radionuclide imaging and therapy** of the AQA AS and A-level Physics specification (7407/7408), part of the A-level only Medical physics option. Learn the content first in the [radionuclide imaging and therapy study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-physics-7408-radionuclide-imaging-and-therapy-aqa-alevel/) and the [radionuclide imaging and therapy revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-physics-7408-radionuclide-imaging-and-therapy-aqa-alevel-revision-notes/).

Data: mass of electron = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg; c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. Biological half-lives are illustrative values for practice.

## Section A

**1.** Define biological half-life. **[1]**

**2.** State two properties of a radioisotope that make it suitable as a tracer for a gamma camera. **[2]**

**3.** Explain why technetium-99m gives a patient a lower dose than iodine-131 for an imaging scan. **[2]**

**4.** A photomultiplier tube has 9 dynodes, and each dynode releases 5 electrons for every electron that strikes it. Calculate the gain of the tube. **[2]**

**5.** State why a course of external X-ray therapy is divided into several sessions. **[1]**

**6.** Explain why implants for internal radiotherapy contain beta emitters. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** A tracer has a physical half-life of 6.0 h and a biological half-life of 10 h. A patient is given 800 MBq.

**(a)** Calculate the effective half-life. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the activity in the patient's body after 9.0 h. **[2]**
**(c)** State the activity that would be predicted after 9.0 h using the physical half-life only, and explain why it is too high. **[1]**

**8.** Indium-111 has a physical half-life of 2.8 days. A sample has an activity of 150 MBq.

**(a)** Calculate the decay constant of indium-111 in s⁻¹. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the number of indium-111 nuclei in the sample. **[1]**
**(c)** Suggest why indium-111, rather than technetium-99m, is used to label white blood cells to follow a process that takes two days. **[2]**

**9.** Many hospitals obtain technetium-99m from a molybdenum-technetium generator. Molybdenum-99 has a half-life of 66 h.

**(a)** Describe how the generator provides technetium-99m. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the fraction of the original molybdenum-99 activity that remains after 3.0 days. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the generator is important to hospitals. **[1]**

**10.** In a PET scan, the tracer emits positrons.

**(a)** Describe what happens to a positron emitted in the body and the radiation that results. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the total energy of the photons produced in one annihilation. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain how the detector ring locates where the annihilation took place. **[2]**

**11.** This question is about the gamma camera.

**(a)** Explain the function of the lead collimator. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe how a photomultiplier tube turns a flash of light from the crystal into an electrical pulse. **[3]**
**(c)** State the effect on image resolution of using a collimator with wider holes. **[1]**

**12.** Describe three methods used to limit the exposure of healthy cells when a tumour is treated with external high-energy X-rays. **[3]**

**13.** A patient needs an image of a kidney. Compare a gamma camera scan using a Tc-99m tracer with a CT scan in terms of resolution, convenience and safety. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** The time taken for half of a substance to be removed from the body by biological processes [1]

**2.** Any two: emits gamma radiation only (so it escapes the body without extra dose); half-life of a few hours (long enough to scan, short enough to limit dose); gamma energy that escapes the body but is detected in the crystal; can be labelled to a compound that collects in the target organ [2]

**3.** Tc-99m emits only gamma rays, most of which leave the body [1]; I-131 also emits beta particles, which are absorbed in the body and add dose (and it has a much longer half-life) [1]

**4.** Gain = 5⁹ [1]; = **2.0 × 10⁶** [1]

**5.** To give healthy cells time to recover between doses [1]

**6.** Beta particles have a short range in tissue [1]; so the dose is delivered to the tumour and very little reaches healthy tissue further away [1]

**7. (a)** 1/T_E = 1/6.0 + 1/10 = 0.267 h⁻¹ [1]; T_E = **3.75 h** [1]
**(b)** A = 800 × (1/2)^(9.0/3.75) [1]; = **150 MBq** [1]
**(c)** 800 × (1/2)^1.5 = 280 MBq; too high because biological removal also reduces the activity in the body [1]

**8. (a)** λ = ln 2/(2.8 × 86 400) [1]; = **2.9 × 10⁻⁶ s⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** N = A/λ = 1.5 × 10⁸/2.87 × 10⁻⁶ = **5.2 × 10¹³** [1]
**(c)** Tc-99m's half-life of about 6 h means its activity would be too low after two days [1]; indium-111's half-life of 2.8 days keeps enough activity to image throughout the process [1]

**9. (a)** Molybdenum-99 is held on a column and decays (by beta-minus emission) to technetium-99m [1]; saline is passed through the column [1]; it washes out (elutes) the technetium-99m, leaving the molybdenum behind [1]
**(b)** 3.0 days = 72 h; n = 72/66 = 1.09 [1]; fraction = (1/2)^1.09 = **0.47** [1]
**(c)** Tc-99m has a short half-life and could not be stored or transported far, so the longer-lived parent provides a supply on site for about a week [1]

**10. (a)** The positron travels a very short distance and annihilates with an electron [1]; two gamma photons are produced [1]; travelling in opposite directions, each of energy about 0.51 MeV [1]
**(b)** 2m_ec² = 2 × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × (3.00 × 10⁸)² = **1.64 × 10⁻¹³ J** [1]
**(c)** Two detectors on opposite sides of the ring register photons at the same time (in coincidence) [1]; the annihilation lies on the line joining them, and a computer combines many lines to locate the source [1]

**11. (a)** Only photons travelling parallel to the holes reach the crystal; others are absorbed by the lead [1]; so each point on the crystal corresponds to a point in the patient and the image is not blurred [1]
**(b)** Light from the crystal releases electrons from the photocathode [1]; the electrons are accelerated to a series of dynodes at increasing potentials [1]; each dynode releases several electrons per incident electron, so a large pulse of charge reaches the anode [1]
**(c)** Resolution becomes poorer (photons from a wider range of directions are accepted) [1]

**12.** Any three: the beam is directed from several directions (or the source rotates) so that only the tumour receives the full dose [1]; the beam is shaped to match the tumour [1]; healthy organs nearby are shielded, or the treatment is split into several sessions to let healthy tissue recover, or the patient is positioned precisely [1]

**13.** Resolution: CT gives a much higher-resolution image of the kidney's structure; the gamma camera has lower resolution but shows how the kidney is functioning [1]; convenience: the gamma camera needs a tracer prepared and given, and the scan takes longer; CT is quick but expensive [1]; safety: both use ionising radiation; the tracer dose is spread through the body for some hours, while CT gives a high dose to the scanned region [1]

## Where marks are usually lost

- Adding half-lives instead of reciprocals.
- Mixing hours and days, or forgetting to convert days to seconds for λ.
- Saying a tracer needs a long half-life.
- Saying the collimator focuses gamma rays.
- Naming PET photons as nuclear gamma emission rather than annihilation radiation.
