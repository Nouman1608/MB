This guide covers **4.2.2.2 State symbols** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) specification, for teaching from
September 2016 onwards (version 1.2). It is part of topic 4.2, Bonding, structure, and the properties of
matter, examined on Paper 1 at Foundation and Higher Tier. No statement in it is marked (HT only), and there
is no required practical. The skill is short, but it is tested again and again, because state symbols appear
in equations throughout the specification.

For quick recall use the [state symbols revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-state-symbols-revision-notes/),
and test yourself with the [state symbols practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-state-symbols-practice/).

## What the specification asks for

| Statement or skill | What to be ready for |
|---|---|
| In chemical equations, the three states of matter are shown as (s), (l) and (g) | Reading and writing the symbols |
| (aq) is used for aqueous solutions | Knowing that (aq) means dissolved in water |
| Include appropriate state symbols in equations for the reactions in the specification | Adding symbols to a given balanced equation |

## The four symbols

| Symbol | Stands for | Typical examples |
|---|---|---|
| (s) | solid | Mg(s), CaCO₃(s), Cu(s), a precipitate |
| (l) | liquid | H₂O(l), Br₂(l), molten NaCl(l), Hg(l) |
| (g) | gas | H₂(g), O₂(g), CO₂(g), Cl₂(g) |
| (aq) | aqueous: dissolved in water | HCl(aq), NaOH(aq), CuSO₄(aq) |

The symbol is written in brackets straight after the formula, with no space, and it belongs to that one
substance. In a balanced equation the big number in front of a formula is a multiplier. It goes before the
formula and the state symbol goes after it, for example 2H₂O(l).

### What "aqueous" means

An **aqueous solution** is a substance dissolved in water. The water is the **solvent** and the dissolved
substance is the **solute**. Writing NaCl(aq) tells you that sodium chloride is dissolved in water. Writing
NaCl(s) tells you it is solid salt, and NaCl(l) tells you it is molten. These are three different situations.

Water itself is **never** written (aq). Pure water is H₂O(l), and steam is H₂O(g). The (aq) symbol is
reserved for a substance dissolved in water.

## Deciding which symbol to write

Work through the substances one at a time and ask three questions.

1. **Is it an element or a compound?** Most metals are solid at room temperature. Hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen
   and chlorine are gases. Bromine and mercury are liquids.
2. **Is it dissolved in water?** Acids such as hydrochloric acid and sulfuric acid are solutions, so they are
   (aq). Alkalis such as sodium hydroxide solution are (aq). A salt that stays in solution after an
   acid-base reaction is (aq).
3. **Is it a solid that formed in a solution?** A precipitate is an insoluble solid, so it is (s).

The question will usually give clues: "solid", "solution", "gas given off", "molten", "dilute" and "excess
solid" all point to a symbol. When the question says a salt solution is formed, use (aq). The specification
describes soluble salts being made by adding a solid to an acid until no more reacts and filtering off the
excess solid, which leaves the salt in solution.

## Worked examples

All the balanced equations here are correct, so your job is only to add the symbols.

### Worked example 1: a metal and an acid

*Magnesium ribbon is added to dilute hydrochloric acid. Bubbles of hydrogen are seen. Add state symbols to:*

Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂

```
Mg:    a metal ribbon                         -> (s)
HCl:   dilute hydrochloric acid, a solution   -> (aq)
MgCl2: the salt stays dissolved in solution   -> (aq)
H2:    bubbles of gas                         -> (g)

Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> MgCl2(aq) + H2(g)
```

### Worked example 2: a carbonate and an acid

*Calcium carbonate (chalk) reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid. Carbon dioxide is given off.*

CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂

```
CaCO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)
```

Notice that water is a product here and is a liquid, **(l)**, not (aq). It is the product of the reaction. It
is not a substance dissolved in a solvent.

### Worked example 3: a Group 1 metal and water

*Sodium reacts with water. A colourless solution of sodium hydroxide forms and hydrogen is given off.*

2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂

```
2Na(s) + 2H2O(l) -> 2NaOH(aq) + H2(g)
```

Here water is a **reactant**, so it is (l). The sodium hydroxide is dissolved in the leftover water, so it is
(aq).

### Worked example 4: a precipitation reaction

*Aqueous lead nitrate is mixed with aqueous potassium iodide. A yellow solid forms.*

Pb(NO₃)₂ + 2KI → PbI₂ + 2KNO₃

```
Pb(NO3)2(aq) + 2KI(aq) -> PbI2(s) + 2KNO3(aq)
```

The yellow solid is the precipitate, so it is (s). The other product stays dissolved, so it is (aq).

### Worked example 5: electrolysis of a molten compound

*Molten sodium chloride is electrolysed. Liquid sodium forms at one electrode and chlorine gas at the other.*

2NaCl → 2Na + Cl₂

```
2NaCl(l) -> 2Na(l) + Cl2(g)
```

The word "molten" tells you the ionic compound is a liquid, not a solution. If it were dissolved in water, it
would be NaCl(aq) and the products would be different.

### Worked example 6: a change of state

State symbols can also show a change of state. Water boiling is written H₂O(l) → H₂O(g). Water freezing is
written H₂O(l) → H₂O(s). The formula is the same each time because the substance has not changed. Only its
state has. This is a physical change, not a chemical reaction.

## Reading an equation from its state symbols

State symbols also tell you what you would **observe** and how you could **separate** the products.

### Worked example 7: thermal decomposition

*Calcium carbonate is heated strongly. Add state symbols and say what happens to the mass of the solid.*

CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂

```
CaCO3(s) -> CaO(s) + CO2(g)
One solid reactant, one solid product and one gas product.
The gas escapes from an open container, so the mass of solid falls.
```

The (g) in the equation is the clue that a product can leave the reaction vessel, which links to the
conservation of mass work in topic 4.3.

### Worked example 8: a displacement reaction

*An iron nail is placed in blue copper(II) sulfate solution. Brown copper coats the nail and the solution turns
pale green.*

Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu

```
Fe(s) + CuSO4(aq) -> FeSO4(aq) + Cu(s)
Both salts are in solution, so they are (aq).
The brown coating is solid copper, so it is (s).
```

### Worked example 9: reduction of a metal oxide with carbon

*Copper(II) oxide powder is heated with carbon powder. Copper metal and carbon dioxide gas form.*

2CuO + C → 2Cu + CO₂

```
2CuO(s) + C(s) -> 2Cu(s) + CO2(g)
```

### Reversible reactions

In a reversible reaction the state symbols go on exactly as before. In the Haber process, nitrogen and hydrogen
gases form ammonia gas: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g). The symbol (g) on every substance tells you all three are gases,
which matters when you think about pressure.

## Extra practice equations

Try adding symbols to these, then check the answers below.

| Description | Balanced equation | With state symbols |
|---|---|---|
| Sulfuric acid solution neutralised by sodium hydroxide solution | H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O | H₂SO₄(aq) + 2NaOH(aq) → Na₂SO₄(aq) + 2H₂O(l) |
| Magnesium ribbon heated with copper oxide powder | Mg + CuO → MgO + Cu | Mg(s) + CuO(s) → MgO(s) + Cu(s) |
| Burning methane in a flame (products at flame temperature) | CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O | CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(g) |

## The same substance in different states

The symbol tells you the situation, not the substance.

| Written | Meaning |
|---|---|
| NaCl(s) | Solid sodium chloride, the crystals |
| NaCl(aq) | Sodium chloride dissolved in water |
| NaCl(l) | Molten sodium chloride, above 801 °C |
| H₂O(l) | Liquid water |
| H₂O(g) | Steam, water vapour |

When methane burns, the products are CO₂ and H₂O. At the temperature of the flame the water is steam, so you
may see CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(g). At room temperature the water would condense to H₂O(l). Use the
information in the question.

## Do state symbols change the balancing?

No. State symbols have no effect on the numbers of atoms, so they play no part in balancing. Balance first
using the numbers in front of the formulae. Add the symbols last. Do not change a balancing number to "fit" a
state symbol.

## Common mistakes

- Writing H₂O(aq) for pure water. Pure water is (l).
- Using (aq) for any liquid. A liquid that is not a solution, such as molten sodium chloride or liquid
  bromine, is (l).
- Putting the symbol before the formula or putting a number after it, for example (g)H₂ or H₂2(g).
- Forgetting the symbol on the hydrogen: bubbles or "gas given off" means (g).
- Writing (s) for a salt that has dissolved. If the product is a solution, it is (aq).
- Omitting symbols from some substances. If the question asks you to include state symbols, every formula
  needs one.

## Exam technique

- **Include state symbols:** every reactant and every product needs one.
- **Read the description:** underline words such as "solid", "dilute", "solution", "gas", "molten" and
  "precipitate" before you write.
- **Check at the end:** count the formulae and count the symbols. The two numbers should match.
