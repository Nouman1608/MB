This guide covers subtopic **2.3, Formulas**, from Topic 2 (Atoms, molecules and stoichiometry) of Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is AS Level content. The broader [Atoms, Molecules and Stoichiometry at AS guide](/resources/as-atoms-molecules-and-stoichiometry/) shows the iron(III) oxide formula and a lead iodide ionic equation. This page works through each learning outcome in more depth: formulas with brackets and Roman numerals, ionic equations for four reaction types, empirical formulas from percentages and from combustion data, and finding the water of crystallisation in a hydrate.

## Syllabus coverage

Candidates should be able to:

1. write formulas of ionic compounds from ionic charges and oxidation numbers (shown by a Roman numeral), including (a) predicting ionic charge from position in the Periodic Table and (b) recalling the names and formulas of NO₃⁻, CO₃²⁻, SO₄²⁻, OH⁻, NH₄⁺, Zn²⁺, Ag⁺, HCO₃⁻ and PO₄³⁻;
2. (a) write and construct balanced equations, including ionic equations without spectator ions; (b) use appropriate state symbols;
3. define and use the terms empirical and molecular formula;
4. understand and use the terms anhydrous, hydrated and water of crystallisation;
5. calculate empirical and molecular formulas using given data.

## Ionic charges

| Position in the Periodic Table | Usual ion | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Group 1 | 1+ | Na⁺, K⁺ |
| Group 2 | 2+ | Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺ |
| Group 13 (Al) | 3+ | Al³⁺ |
| Group 15 (N, P) | 3− | N³⁻, P³⁻ |
| Group 16 | 2− | O²⁻, S²⁻ |
| Group 17 | 1− | Cl⁻, Br⁻ |

Ions to recall without being given them:

| Name | Formula | Name | Formula |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nitrate | NO₃⁻ | Ammonium | NH₄⁺ |
| Carbonate | CO₃²⁻ | Hydrogencarbonate | HCO₃⁻ |
| Sulfate | SO₄²⁻ | Phosphate | PO₄³⁻ |
| Hydroxide | OH⁻ | Zinc | Zn²⁺ |
| Silver | Ag⁺ | | |

For elements that form more than one ion, the **Roman numeral** gives the oxidation number, which for a simple metal ion equals its charge: copper(I) is Cu⁺, copper(II) is Cu²⁺, iron(III) is Fe³⁺, manganese(IV) is Mn⁴⁺.

## Writing ionic formulas

The total positive charge must equal the total negative charge. Use the lowest common multiple of the charges, and put a polyatomic ion in brackets whenever more than one is needed.

| Compound | Ions | Balance | Formula |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calcium phosphate | Ca²⁺, PO₄³⁻ | 3 × (+2) = 2 × (−3) | Ca₃(PO₄)₂ |
| Ammonium carbonate | NH₄⁺, CO₃²⁻ | 2 × (+1) = −2 | (NH₄)₂CO₃ |
| Aluminium hydroxide | Al³⁺, OH⁻ | +3 = 3 × (−1) | Al(OH)₃ |
| Magnesium hydrogencarbonate | Mg²⁺, HCO₃⁻ | +2 = 2 × (−1) | Mg(HCO₃)₂ |
| Copper(I) oxide | Cu⁺, O²⁻ | 2 × (+1) = −2 | Cu₂O |
| Manganese(IV) oxide | Mn⁴⁺, O²⁻ | +4 = 2 × (−2) | MnO₂ |
| Silver nitrate | Ag⁺, NO₃⁻ | +1 = −1 | AgNO₃ |

Brackets matter: Ca₃PO₄₂ is meaningless, and NH₄₂CO₃ suggests 42 hydrogen atoms. Never change the formula of a polyatomic ion to make charges balance.

## Balanced equations and state symbols

A balanced equation has the same number of each type of atom, and the same total charge, on both sides. Balance by changing the numbers in front of formulas, never the subscripts.

| State symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| (s) | solid, including precipitates |
| (l) | liquid, including water formed in a reaction |
| (g) | gas |
| (aq) | dissolved in water |

### Ionic equations in four reaction types

To write an ionic equation: write the full equation with state symbols, split only the **aqueous ionic** substances into ions, cancel ions that are unchanged on both sides (spectator ions), and check atoms and charge.

**1. Neutralisation.** Nitric acid and potassium hydroxide:
HNO₃(aq) + KOH(aq) → KNO₃(aq) + H₂O(l)
Ionic: **H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)**. K⁺ and NO₃⁻ are spectators.

**2. Precipitation.** Silver nitrate and sodium chloride:
AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq)
Ionic: **Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s)**.

**3. Acid and a solid carbonate.** Calcium carbonate and hydrochloric acid:
CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)
Ionic: **CaCO₃(s) + 2H⁺(aq) → Ca²⁺(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)**. The solid stays whole, because its ions are not free in solution. Only Cl⁻ is a spectator.

**4. Displacement.** Zinc in copper(II) sulfate solution:
Zn(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)
Ionic: **Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s)**. Charge check: +2 on each side.

## Empirical and molecular formulas

- **Empirical formula:** the simplest whole-number ratio of the atoms of each element in a compound.
- **Molecular formula:** the actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule.

The molecular formula is a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula: ethane is C₂H₆, empirical formula CH₃. Ionic and giant covalent substances have only an empirical formula, such as NaCl or SiO₂.

### Worked example: from percentages

A compound contains 40.0 % C, 6.7 % H and 53.3 % O by mass, and Mr = 60.0.

| | C | H | O |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mass in 100 g | 40.0 | 6.7 | 53.3 |
| Amount (÷ Ar) | 3.33 | 6.7 | 3.33 |
| Ratio (÷ smallest) | 1 | 2.0 | 1 |

Empirical formula CH₂O; its mass is 30.0. Mr ÷ 30.0 = 2, so the molecular formula is **C₂H₄O₂**.

### Worked example: a ratio that is not whole

An oxide of iron contains 72.4 % iron (Fe 55.8, O 16.0).

1. Fe: 72.4 ÷ 55.8 = 1.297 mol; O: 27.6 ÷ 16.0 = 1.725 mol.
2. Divide by the smaller: Fe 1, O 1.33.
3. 1.33 is close to 4/3, so multiply both by 3: Fe 3, O 4.
4. Empirical formula **Fe₃O₄**.

Recognise the common fractions: 0.5 (multiply by 2), 0.33 or 0.67 (by 3), 0.25 or 0.75 (by 4). Do not round 1.33 down to 1.

### Worked example: from combustion data

0.600 g of a hydrocarbon burns completely to give 1.76 g of CO₂ and 1.08 g of H₂O. Its Mr is 30.0.

1. Mass of C = 1.76 × 12.0 ÷ 44.0 = 0.480 g; amount = 0.0400 mol.
2. Mass of H = 1.08 × 2.0 ÷ 18.0 = 0.120 g; amount = 0.120 mol.
3. Check: 0.480 + 0.120 = 0.600 g, so there is no oxygen in the compound.
4. Ratio C : H = 0.0400 : 0.120 = 1 : 3, empirical formula CH₃ (mass 15.0).
5. 30.0 ÷ 15.0 = 2, molecular formula **C₂H₆**.

Step 3 matters: if the masses of C and H do not add up to the sample mass, the difference is oxygen.

## Hydrated and anhydrous compounds

- **Hydrated:** a crystalline compound containing water molecules within its structure.
- **Water of crystallisation:** the water molecules held in the crystal, shown after a dot: CuSO₄·5H₂O.
- **Anhydrous:** without water of crystallisation: CuSO₄.

### Worked example: finding x

2.50 g of hydrated copper(II) sulfate, CuSO₄·xH₂O, is heated until its mass is constant at 1.60 g.

1. Mass of water lost = 2.50 − 1.60 = 0.90 g; amount = 0.90 ÷ 18.0 = 0.0500 mol.
2. Amount of CuSO₄ = 1.60 ÷ 159.6 = 0.0100 mol.
3. x = 0.0500 ÷ 0.0100 = 5.0, so the formula is **CuSO₄·5H₂O**.

Heating to *constant mass* makes sure all the water has gone; otherwise x comes out too small.

## Common mistakes

- Missing brackets around polyatomic ions, or altering the ion's formula to balance charge.
- Using a Roman numeral as a subscript: iron(III) oxide is Fe₂O₃, not FeO₃.
- Splitting solids, water or gases into ions in an ionic equation.
- Leaving spectator ions in, or not checking that charges balance.
- Rounding a ratio such as 1 : 1.5 or 1 : 1.33 to the nearest whole number.
- Dividing by the mass instead of the Ar when finding moles of each element.
- Forgetting oxygen when combustion masses of C and H do not add up to the sample mass.

## Exam technique

- **Write** a formula: show the ions and their charges in your working.
- **Construct** an equation: include state symbols every time unless told not to.
- **Calculate** an empirical formula: use a table with mass, moles and ratio rows.
- **Deduce** a molecular formula: divide Mr by the empirical formula mass and show the multiplier.
