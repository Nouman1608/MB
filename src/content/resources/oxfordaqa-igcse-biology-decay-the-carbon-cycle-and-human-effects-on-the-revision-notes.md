## 3.3.3 a-d: Decay

**Key points**

- Organisms remove materials from the environment to grow and for other processes.
- Materials return in waste or when organisms die and decay.
- Decay happens because microorganisms digest (break down) the material.
- Decay releases substances plants need to grow.
- In a stable community, removal of materials is balanced by return, so the materials are part of a constant cycle.

**Decay conditions**

| Condition | Effect on decay | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Warm | Faster | Enzymes work faster; microorganisms grow and reproduce faster |
| Too hot | Stops | Enzymes denature; microorganisms die |
| Cold | Slow | Enzyme action and growth slow down |
| Moist | Faster | Water needed for enzyme action and cell growth |
| Dry | Slow | Microorganisms cannot grow or digest |
| Aerobic | Faster | Oxygen for aerobic respiration, which supplies energy |
| Waterlogged | Slow | Little oxygen; spaces filled with water |

**Detritus feeders** (earthworms, woodlice, maggots) eat dead material and waste. They break it into small pieces, which gives microorganisms a larger surface area.

**Method in steps: explain a decay result**

1. Say what the microorganisms do: digest the material using enzymes.
2. Name the condition that changed (temperature, water or oxygen).
3. Say how it changes enzyme action or respiration.
4. Link back to the result: faster or slower mass loss.

**Worked reminder.** Dry mass falls from 20.0 g to 9.8 g. Mass lost = 10.2 g. Percentage lost = 10.2 ÷ 20.0 × 100 = 51.0%. Over 21 days the mean rate is 10.2 ÷ 21 = 0.49 g per day.

## 3.3.3 e: The carbon cycle

| Step | Process | Carbon form |
|---|---|---|
| Carbon dioxide taken from air | Photosynthesis (green plants, algae) | CO₂ becomes carbohydrates, fats, proteins |
| Passes along food chain | Feeding | Fats and proteins in consumers |
| Returned to air | Respiration (plants, algae, animals, microorganisms) | CO₂ |
| Dead bodies and waste | Feeding by animals and microorganisms; decay | Eventually CO₂ |
| Returned to air | Combustion of wood and fossil fuels | CO₂ |

Learn the cycle as a loop in words:

Air carbon dioxide, then photosynthesis, then plant and algal compounds, then feeding, then animal compounds, then death and waste, then feeding by detritus feeders and microorganisms, then respiration, then air carbon dioxide.

Respiration arrows also run from plants, algae and animals straight to the air, and combustion adds more.

**Energy note.** By the time microorganisms and detritus feeders have broken down waste and dead bodies and cycled the materials as plant nutrients, all the energy originally absorbed by green plants and algae has been transferred. Carbon is recycled; energy is not.

**Method in steps: apply the carbon cycle**

1. List every process that removes CO₂ from the air (photosynthesis).
2. List every process that adds CO₂ (respiration by all groups, combustion).
3. Compare totals: more removed means carbon is being stored; more added means the air gains CO₂.
4. A balanced total fits a stable community.

**Worked reminder.** Removed 120 units, returned 55 + 48 + 7 + 4 = 114. Net removal = 6 units, stored as extra biomass.

**Applying the principles to unfamiliar situations**

- A sealed, lit jar with a plant and a snail can run for a long time. Photosynthesis takes in CO₂, respiration by the plant, snail and microorganisms returns it, and decay returns mineral ions. Nothing is added, so the materials are cycling.
- Fallen leaves in a dry, cold place stay for years because decay is slow. The same leaves in a warm, damp, well-aerated compost heap are digested quickly.
- A field crop is harvested and removed. The mineral ions taken up by the plants are not returned by decay, so fertiliser may be needed. This breaks the balance of a stable community.
- Burning a forest turns carbon stored in wood into CO₂ at once, far faster than decay would.

## 3.3.4 a-b: Waste and pollution

- Rapid population growth and a rising standard of living produce more waste.
- Waste and chemicals that are not handled properly cause pollution.

| Medium | Pollutant | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Water | Sewage | Eutrophication |
| Water | Fertiliser | Eutrophication |
| Water | Toxic chemicals | Harm to aquatic organisms |
| Air | Smoke | Air pollution |
| Air | Sulfur dioxide | Contributes to acid rain |
| Land | Pesticides, herbicides | Toxic to organisms; can be washed into water |

**Eutrophication in four stages**

1. Mineral ions rise, so algae and/or plants grow more.
2. Dense growth shades others; some cannot get enough light for photosynthesis and die.
3. Microorganisms feeding on the dead organisms increase greatly.
4. Their respiration depletes the oxygen; aerobic organisms die.

Key point: the oxygen is used by the microorganisms' respiration. Dead algae do not use oxygen themselves.

**Worked reminder.** A lake's dissolved oxygen falls from 10.4 to 1.2 mg per dm³. Fall = 9.2 mg per dm³. Percentage fall = 9.2 ÷ 10.4 × 100 = 88.5%. Fish and other aerobic organisms die because they cannot get enough oxygen for aerobic respiration.

**Other pollution to link up**

- Sulfur dioxide comes from burning fuels that contain sulfur. It dissolves in rainwater and contributes to acid rain, which harms aquatic life and plants.
- Pesticides and herbicides used on fields can be washed from land into streams and lakes, so land pollution becomes water pollution.
- Untreated sewage and fertiliser both supply mineral ions, so both can cause eutrophication.

## 3.3.4 c: Deforestation

- Large-scale tropical deforestation has reduced biodiversity (fewer species and fewer habitats).
- Less photosynthesis, so less CO₂ removed from the air.
- Burning releases CO₂ by combustion; decay of remains releases CO₂ by microorganisms' respiration.

**Worked reminder.** Forest mean 30.5 species per plot, cleared mean 9.5. Reduction = (30.5 − 9.5) ÷ 30.5 × 100 = 68.9%.

## 3.3.4 d: Global warming

- Carbon dioxide and methane in the atmosphere are increasing and contribute to global warming.
- A rise of only a few degrees Celsius may cause: big changes in climate; a rise in sea level; changes in migration patterns (for example birds); changes in the distribution of species.

**Worked reminder.** CO₂ rises from 317 to 414 ppm. Rise = 97 ppm. Percentage = 97 ÷ 317 × 100 = 30.6%. Over 60 years the mean rise is 97 ÷ 60 = 1.6 ppm per year.

**Linking effects to biology**

- Rising sea level reduces the habitat of coastal organisms.
- Warmer conditions can move the range in which a species can live, so its distribution changes.
- Birds that migrate may arrive earlier or later than before, so migration patterns change.

## Analysing data and evaluating methods

| Term | Meaning | Improved by |
|---|---|---|
| Valid | Measures what it claims to measure | Controlling other variables; representative sites |
| Reliable | Gives similar results if repeated | Repeats, larger samples, a mean |
| Precise | Small differences can be detected | Suitable instruments |

**Method in steps: describe a trend from a table**

1. Quote the first and last values with units.
2. Give the size of the change, and the percentage if useful.
3. Mention any peak, fall or change in rate.
4. Link to the biology.

## Must-know distinctions

- Decay (microorganisms, digestion) versus respiration (energy release in cells): microorganisms do both.
- Carbon cycles; energy is transferred and lost to the surroundings.
- Eutrophication (too many nutrients) versus acid rain (sulfur dioxide).
- Correlation versus cause.

1. Name the microorganisms mainly responsible for decay.
2. State three conditions that make decay faster.

4. Which process removes carbon dioxide from the air?

6. Calculate the percentage lost when 40.0 g falls to 31.0 g.
7. Name a gas that contributes to acid rain.
8. In eutrophication, which organisms use up the oxygen?
9. Give two effects of tropical deforestation.
10. Name two gases that are increasing and contribute to global warming.
11. Give two possible effects of a few degrees of warming.
12. What does "reliable" mean?

### Answers

1. Bacteria and fungi.
2. Warm, moist, aerobic.
3. Enzymes denature and the microorganisms die.
4. Photosynthesis.
5. Respiration; combustion (also decay by microorganisms, which is their respiration).
6. 9.0 ÷ 40.0 × 100 = 22.5%.
7. Sulfur dioxide.
8. The microorganisms feeding on the dead algae and plants.
9. Reduced biodiversity; less CO₂ removed from the air (or more CO₂ released by burning).
10. Carbon dioxide and methane.
11. Rise in sea level; changed species distribution (or changed migration patterns, big changes in climate).
12. Repeating the measurements gives similar results.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "bacteria eat the leaves" instead of describing digestion by enzymes.
- Saying decay happens fastest in hot conditions without noting that enzymes denature at very high temperature.
- Leaving out oxygen as a condition, or ignoring that waterlogging removes it.
- Forgetting respiration by plants and microorganisms in the carbon cycle.
- Drawing a carbon cycle where CO₂ goes straight from animals to plants.
- Saying the algae use up the oxygen in eutrophication.
- Calculating percentage change using the wrong starting value.
- Missing units in data answers (ppm, mg per dm³, g per day).
- Calling a poorly designed sample "reliable" instead of naming what makes it invalid.

## Frequently asked questions

**What do detritus feeders do in decay?**
Detritus feeders such as earthworms, woodlice and maggots eat dead material and waste and break it into small pieces. This gives microorganisms a larger surface area to digest.

**What are the four stages of eutrophication?**
Mineral ions from fertiliser or sewage make algae and plants grow more; dense growth shades others, which die; microorganisms feeding on the dead organisms increase greatly; and their respiration depletes the oxygen, so aerobic organisms die.

**What causes acid rain?**
Sulfur dioxide from burning fuels that contain sulfur dissolves in rainwater and contributes to acid rain. This is different from eutrophication, which is caused by too many nutrients in water.

**What are the effects of tropical deforestation?**
Large-scale tropical deforestation reduces biodiversity and means less photosynthesis, so less carbon dioxide is removed from the air. Burning releases carbon dioxide by combustion, and decay of the remains releases more through microorganisms' respiration.
