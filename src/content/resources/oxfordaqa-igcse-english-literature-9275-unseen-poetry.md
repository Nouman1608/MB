This guide covers **unseen poetry** in OxfordAQA International GCSE English Literature (9275). It
serves **both routes**:

- **Route A: 3.2.2 Unseen poetry**, assessed in Section B of Paper 2a (Poetry and Unseen Texts);
- **Route B: 3.3.2 Unseen poetry**, assessed in Section B of Paper 2b (Poetry).

On both routes you answer one 30-mark question on a poem you have not seen before, printed on the
paper. The skill and the marks are the same; the difference is what sits around it. Route A
candidates go on to an unseen prose question afterwards, so they need to finish the poem on time;
Route B candidates have only the anthology and the unseen poem on their paper.

## What the specification asks

The specification asks you to experience a wide range of poetry so that you can closely analyse
unfamiliar poems, and analyse key features such as content, theme, structure and use of language.
In other words, you cannot revise the poem itself; you revise a **method** and build reading
experience.

The three assessment objectives apply:

- **AO1**: understanding of the poem's ideas and themes, shown through a clear interpretation;
- **AO2**: analysis of how the poet creates meanings and effects through language, form and structure;
- **AO3**: an informed personal response, with appropriate terminology and accurate, organised writing.

No background knowledge about the poet is expected or needed. Everything you write should come from
the poem on the page.

## A method that works under pressure

### Step 1: read for sense (3 minutes)

Read the title, then the whole poem, without annotating. Ask: who is speaking, to whom, and about
what? What happens, or what is described? Then summarise the poem in one sentence. This stops you
hunting for hidden meanings before you have grasped the surface meaning.

### Step 2: read for shape (3 minutes)

Look at the poem as an object. How many stanzas? Are they regular? Are lines long or short? Is there
rhyme, and does it break anywhere? Where does the tone or direction change? Mark the **turn**, if
there is one: many poems shift in their final lines.

### Step 3: read for detail (4 minutes)

Now annotate selectively. Underline striking images, circle repeated words, and note sound effects
only where they clearly create meaning. Choose the five or six details you will actually use.

### Step 4: plan (3 to 5 minutes)

Write a one-sentence interpretation that answers the question. Then choose three or four points,
ideally moving through the poem from beginning to end, so that your answer follows its development.
Each point should combine a method and its effect.

### Step 5: write (about 30 minutes)

Open with your interpretation. Write three or four developed paragraphs. End with a short conclusion
that returns to the poem's overall effect, often by commenting on the ending.

## Worked example

The poem below is an original poem written for this guide.

> **The Lighthouse Keeper's Daughter**
>
> Each night my father climbed the stairs
> to tell the dark where it should end.
> I counted steps. One hundred and four.
> I never heard him come back down.
>
> At breakfast he was always there,
> salt in his beard, his hands too careful
> with the cups, as if the light
> were still in them and might go out.
>
> They closed the tower in the spring.
> A machine does now what he did.
> He sits by the window every evening
> watching it turn, and says nothing,
> and counts.

**Question:** How does the poet present the speaker's feelings about her father?

**One-sentence interpretation:** The speaker presents her father with admiration and tenderness, and
the poem gradually reveals how much of his identity depended on his work.

**Point 1: the father as a heroic figure.** The metaphor "to tell the dark where it should end"
makes his job sound almost god-like: he commands the night. The child's perspective, "I counted
steps", shows her awe, and the precise number, "One hundred and four", suggests how closely she
followed his routine.

**Point 2: tenderness and fragility.** In the second stanza the heroic image becomes domestic. His
hands are "too careful / with the cups", and the enjambment holds "careful" at the line end, inviting
the reader to pause on his gentleness. The simile "as if the light / were still in them and might go
out" links the job to his body, as though he carries responsibility home with him.

**Point 3: loss through structure.** The final stanza begins with a blunt, end-stopped sentence:
"They closed the tower in the spring." The short, plain line contrasts with the earlier imagery, so
the change in his life arrives as suddenly for the reader as it did for him. "A machine does now
what he did" reduces his role to a mechanism.

**Point 4: the ending.** The last line, "and counts.", is the shortest in the poem and is isolated by
the line break. Counting connects the father to the child who once counted his steps, so the poem
comes full circle. The repeated "and" in the final sentence slows the pace, mirroring the empty
evenings. The speaker's feelings are never stated directly; the reader infers sympathy and sadness
from what she notices.

This plan earns credit across all three assessment objectives: a clear interpretation (AO1), methods
and effects (AO2), and an organised, personal reading (AO3).

## Common mistakes

- **Searching for a hidden meaning first.** Grasp the literal situation before interpreting.
- **Feature spotting.** "There is alliteration in line 3" earns nothing unless you explain what it
  does.
- **Getting stuck in stanza one.** Plan points across the whole poem, and always discuss the ending.
- **Inventing biography.** You are not expected to know the poet; do not guess at their life.
- **Retelling.** Paraphrasing the poem line by line is description, not analysis.
- **Forcing terminology.** Use technical terms when they help, but a precise plain description of an
  effect is better than a wrongly named device.

## Exam technique and command words

- **How does the poet present...?** Focus on methods and their effects in relation to the named
  focus (a feeling, a relationship, a place).
- **Explore** invites you to suggest more than one reading of an image or of the ending; tentative
  language such as "perhaps" or "this could suggest" shows a thoughtful response.

Keep quotations short. Refer to the poem's structure at least once, because many candidates write
only about language. Watch the clock: on Route A, time saved here goes to unseen prose.

## Building experience before the exam

- Read one short, unfamiliar poem a week and write a one-sentence interpretation.
- Once a fortnight, write a full timed answer of 45 minutes.
- Vary the poems: older and modern, rhymed and free verse, poems from different countries.
