This guide covers subtopic **34.1, Primary and secondary amines**, from Topic 34, Nitrogen
compounds, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is A Level
content. The broader [Amines: Aliphatic and Aromatic study guide](/resources/a-amines-aliphatic-and-aromatic/)
lists the routes and the basicity order; this page explains how to choose a route, how to control
which amine you get, and how to write the basicity answer in full.

## What the syllabus asks for

Subtopic 34.1 has three learning outcomes:

1. recall the reagents and conditions for producing primary and secondary amines by:
   - (a) "reaction of halogenoalkanes with NH₃ in ethanol heated under pressure"
   - (b) "reaction of halogenoalkanes with primary amines in ethanol, heated in a sealed tube / under
     pressure"
   - (c) "the reduction of amides with LiAlH₄"
   - (d) "the reduction of nitriles with LiAlH₄ or H₂ / Ni"
2. describe the condensation reaction of ammonia or an amine with an acyl chloride at room
   temperature to give an amide
3. describe and explain the basicity of aqueous solutions of amines

At AS Level (subtopic 19.1) classification of amines was not tested; at A Level you must use it.

## Classifying amines

Count the carbon groups bonded to the nitrogen:

| Class | General formula | Example | Name |
|---|---|---|---|
| primary | RNH₂ | CH₃CH₂NH₂ | ethylamine |
| secondary | R₂NH | (CH₃CH₂)₂NH | diethylamine |
| secondary (mixed) | RR′NH | CH₃NHCH₂CH₃ | N-methylethylamine |
| tertiary | R₃N | (CH₃)₃N | trimethylamine |

The classification is about the **nitrogen**, not the carbon it is attached to. (CH₃)₂CHNH₂ has its
nitrogen on a secondary carbon, but it is a **primary** amine because nitrogen carries only one
carbon group.

## Route (a): halogenoalkane and ammonia

    CH₃CH₂Br + NH₃ → CH₃CH₂NH₂ + HBr
    (with excess NH₃: CH₃CH₂Br + 2NH₃ → CH₃CH₂NH₂ + NH₄Br)

- **Conditions:** ammonia dissolved in **ethanol**, **heated under pressure** in a sealed vessel.
  Ammonia is a gas at the reaction temperature, so a sealed vessel keeps it in contact with the
  halogenoalkane.
- **Mechanism:** nucleophilic substitution; the nitrogen lone pair attacks the δ+ carbon and Br⁻
  leaves.
- **The control problem.** The product amine still has a lone pair on nitrogen, and alkyl groups
  make that lone pair *more* available, so ethylamine competes with ammonia for the remaining
  bromoethane. Further substitution gives diethylamine, then triethylamine, then a quaternary
  ammonium salt. Using a **large excess of ammonia** makes it far more likely that each bromoethane
  molecule meets an ammonia molecule, so the primary amine is the main product.

## Route (b): halogenoalkane and a primary amine

This is the same reaction one stage on, used deliberately to make a **secondary** amine:

    CH₃CH₂Br + CH₃CH₂NH₂ → (CH₃CH₂)₂NH + HBr

- **Conditions:** the primary amine in **ethanol**, **heated in a sealed tube / under pressure**.
- Two different groups can be put on nitrogen by choosing the halogenoalkane and the amine:
  CH₃Br + CH₃CH₂NH₂ → CH₃NHCH₂CH₃ + HBr.
- Further substitution to a tertiary amine is again possible, so the same excess argument applies
  (excess primary amine favours the secondary amine).
- If the amine is in excess, the HBr is removed as a salt such as CH₃CH₂NH₃⁺Br⁻.

## Route (c): reduction of amides with LiAlH₄

LiAlH₄ reduces the C=O of an amide to CH₂. The nitrogen and its substituents are untouched, so
the class of amine depends on the amide:

    CH₃CONH₂ + 4[H] → CH₃CH₂NH₂ + H₂O              (primary amide → primary amine)
    CH₃CONHCH₃ + 4[H] → CH₃CH₂NHCH₃ + H₂O          (N-substituted amide → secondary amine)

Because the amide can be made cleanly from an acyl chloride and an amine (outcome 2), this route
avoids the mixture problem of routes (a) and (b): only one product forms.

## Route (d): reduction of nitriles

    CH₃CH₂CN + 4[H] → CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₂

- **Reagents:** LiAlH₄, or hydrogen with a nickel catalyst (H₂/Ni).
- The product is always a **primary** amine with the –NH₂ on a CH₂ group.
- The nitrile is made from a halogenoalkane with KCN in ethanol (AS 19.2), so halogenoalkane →
  nitrile → amine **adds one carbon** to the chain. Bromoethane gives propylamine this way, but
  ethylamine by route (a).

## Choosing a route

| You want | Best route | Why |
|---|---|---|
| primary amine, same carbon count as the halogenoalkane | (a), excess NH₃ | one step |
| primary amine with one extra carbon | KCN, then (d) | nitrile adds a carbon |
| a pure secondary amine | acyl chloride + primary amine, then (c) | no over-substitution |
| a secondary amine in one step | (b) | fewer steps, but a mixture |

## Outcome 2: amide formation from ammonia or an amine

Ammonia or a primary or secondary amine reacts with an acyl chloride at **room temperature** to give
an amide and HCl. It is a **condensation** because two molecules join and HCl is lost:

    CH₃CH₂COCl + CH₃NH₂ → CH₃CH₂CONHCH₃ + HCl

The amine acts as a **nucleophile** here, using the same lone pair that makes it a base. With excess
amine, the second molecule acts as a base and removes HCl as an alkylammonium chloride.

## Outcome 3: why aqueous amines are basic

An amine is a Brønsted–Lowry base: the nitrogen lone pair accepts a proton. In water:

    CH₃CH₂NH₂ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃CH₂NH₃⁺ + OH⁻

The equilibrium lies to the left (amines are weak bases), but enough OH⁻ forms to make the solution
alkaline: it turns red litmus blue and has a pH above 7. With acids, amines form salts:
CH₃CH₂NH₂ + HCl → CH₃CH₂NH₃⁺Cl⁻.

**Comparing strength.** The more available the lone pair, the stronger the base.

- Alkyl groups are **electron-donating** (positive inductive effect). They push electron density
  onto nitrogen, making the lone pair more available, and they help stabilise the positive charge
  of the alkylammonium ion.
- So ethylamine is a **stronger base than ammonia**.
- Diethylamine, with two electron-donating groups, is a **stronger base than ethylamine** in water.

Restrict this argument to the primary and secondary amines in the syllabus; do not extend it to
claim that tertiary amines are always the strongest in water.

## Worked example 1: planning a secondary amine synthesis

**Question.** Devise a route to N-methylpropylamine, CH₃CH₂CH₂NHCH₃, that gives a single product.

**Answer.**

1. Make propanoyl chloride from propanoic acid with SOCl₂.
2. React propanoyl chloride with **methylamine** at room temperature:
   CH₃CH₂COCl + CH₃NH₂ → CH₃CH₂CONHCH₃ + HCl.
3. Reduce the amide with **LiAlH₄**: CH₃CH₂CONHCH₃ + 4[H] → CH₃CH₂CH₂NHCH₃ + H₂O.

*Examiner's note:* route (b) using 1-bromopropane and methylamine would also be credited for the
reagents, but the amide route is the answer to "gives a single product", because it cannot
over-substitute.

## Worked example 2: an annotated basicity answer

**Question.** Explain why an aqueous solution of ethylamine has a higher pH than an aqueous solution
of ammonia of the same concentration. **[3]**

**Model answer.** Both accept a proton using the lone pair on nitrogen **[1]**. The ethyl group is
electron-donating and increases the electron density on nitrogen, so the lone pair is more available
to accept H⁺ **[1]**. Ethylamine therefore reacts with water to a greater extent, giving a higher
concentration of OH⁻ **[1]**.

*Why answers lose marks:* writing "ethylamine has more lone pairs" (both have one), or stopping
before linking basicity to the OH⁻ concentration and pH.

## Common mistakes

- Omitting **pressure** or **sealed tube** from routes (a) and (b).
- Forgetting that a nitrile route **adds a carbon**.
- Writing H₂/Ni for the reduction of amides; the syllabus gives **LiAlH₄** only for amides.
- Calling (CH₃)₂CHNH₂ a secondary amine.
- Explaining basicity with electronegativity of nitrogen alone, instead of the availability of the
  lone pair.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Recall:** reagent, solvent and condition: "NH₃ in ethanol, heat under pressure".
- **Describe** the basicity: write the equilibrium with water and say the solution is alkaline.
- **Explain** a basicity difference: lone pair availability, the inductive effect, then the outcome.
- **Suggest** why excess ammonia is used: refer to the competing nucleophile and further
  substitution.
