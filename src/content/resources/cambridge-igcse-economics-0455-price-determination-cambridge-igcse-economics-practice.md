Revise first with the [price determination study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-price-determination-cambridge-igcse-economics/) and the [price determination revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-price-determination-cambridge-igcse-economics-revision-notes/).

Several questions use this weekly schedule for loaves of bread in the town of Arno (invented).

| Price per loaf | Quantity demanded | Quantity supplied |
|---|---|---|
| 1.00 | 1,200 | 400 |
| 1.50 | 1,000 | 600 |
| 2.00 | 800 | 800 |
| 2.50 | 600 | 1,000 |
| 3.00 | 400 | 1,200 |

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** In a market, the price is above the equilibrium price. Which of the following describes the situation? **[1]**

A: Quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied

B: There is a shortage, so the price will rise

C: There is a surplus, so the price will fall

D: The market clears

**2.** Using Arno's schedule, what quantity of bread is actually bought and sold at a price of 1.50? **[1]**

A: 400

B: 600

C: 1,000

D: 1,600

**3.** Define market disequilibrium. **[2]**

**4.** Using Arno's schedule:

**(a)** Identify the equilibrium price and quantity. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate total spending on bread at equilibrium. **[1]**

**5.** Using Arno's schedule, calculate the size of the surplus at a price of 3.00. **[1]**

**6.** Explain why the quantity traded at a disequilibrium price is the smaller of quantity demanded and quantity supplied. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**7.** Using Arno's schedule:

**(a)** Calculate the shortage at a price of 1.00. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate total spending on bread at a price of 1.00 and at a price of 2.50. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why total spending is higher at the equilibrium price than at either of these prices. **[2]**

**8.** Explain, using a demand and supply diagram, how a market moves to equilibrium when the price starts above the equilibrium level. **[6]**

**9.** In a weekly market for school shoes, the gap (quantity demanded minus quantity supplied) is +90 pairs at a price of 30 and −60 pairs at a price of 35. The gap changes by equal amounts for equal price changes.

**(a)** State between which two prices the equilibrium lies. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the equilibrium price. **[2]**

**10.** A concert promoter sets a ticket price of 25. All 2,000 tickets sell within ten minutes, and 3,500 people were trying to buy them. Explain what this shows about the ticket price and the likely consequences. **[4]**

**11.** Explain why markets for perishable products, such as fresh flowers, tend to move quickly towards equilibrium when there is a surplus. **[3]**

**12.** Discuss whether a market will always reach its equilibrium price. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** **C** [1]. Above equilibrium, quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded, so sellers cut the price. **[1]**

**2.** **B** [1]. There is a shortage; only the 600 loaves offered by sellers can be traded. **[1]**

**3.** A situation in which quantity demanded and quantity supplied are not equal at the current price [1], causing a shortage or a surplus [1]. **[2]**

**4. (a)** Price **2.00** [1]; quantity **800 loaves a week** [1].

**(b)** 2.00 × 800 = **1,600** [1]. **[3]**

**5.** 1,200 − 400 = **800 loaves** [1]. **[1]**

**6.** No buyer can be forced to buy more than they want and no seller can be forced to sell more than they offer [1], so trade stops when the shorter side of the market is satisfied [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** 1,200 − 400 = **800 loaves** [1].

**(b)** At 1.00: 1.00 × 400 = **400** [1]; at 2.50: 2.50 × 600 = **1,500** [1].

**(c)** At 1.00 the quantity traded is limited by sellers, and at 2.50 by buyers [1]. At equilibrium both are satisfied, so 800 loaves are traded at 2.00, giving 1,600, more than at either disequilibrium price [1]. **[5]**

**8.** Diagram with labelled axes, a downward-sloping demand curve and upward-sloping supply curve [1], showing a price above equilibrium with the surplus marked between the curves [1]. At that price quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded, so unsold stock builds up [1]. Sellers cut the price to clear stock [1]. As the price falls, quantity demanded extends and quantity supplied contracts [1], until they are equal at the equilibrium price and quantity, where the curves cross [1]. **[6]**

**9. (a)** Between **30 and 35** [1].

**(b)** The gap closes by 90 + 60 = 150 for a rise of 5, so 30 per 1 rise in price [1]; 90 ÷ 30 = 3, so the equilibrium price is 30 + 3 = **33** [1]. **[3]**

**10.** The price of 25 is below equilibrium [1], because quantity demanded (3,500) exceeds quantity supplied (2,000): a shortage of 1,500 tickets [1]. Many fans miss out, and tickets may be resold at much higher prices [1]. The promoter could have charged more without unsold seats, so it gave up revenue [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Unsold perishable stock loses value quickly and may have to be thrown away [1], so sellers have a strong incentive to cut prices at once [1]. Lower prices extend quantity demanded, so the surplus clears and the market moves rapidly to equilibrium [1]. **[3]**

**12.** Markets tend towards equilibrium: shortages lead sellers to raise prices and surpluses lead them to cut prices [1], and competition between buyers and between sellers pushes the price to where quantities match [1]. But adjustment may be slow if prices are fixed in advance or costly to change, such as printed price lists [1]; a government may set a maximum or minimum price that stops the price moving [1]; and conditions of demand and supply change often, so the equilibrium itself keeps moving [1]. Conclusion: markets move towards equilibrium where prices are free to change, but in many real markets the price may stay above or below it for some time [1]. **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* Separate the tendency to move towards equilibrium from the claim that the market is always there. Naming a reason the price cannot move earns the evaluation marks.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a price above equilibrium a shortage.
- Giving the larger quantity as the amount traded at a disequilibrium price.
- Shifting curves to show adjustment, instead of movements along them.
- Missing units ("loaves a week") when identifying equilibrium.
- In Question 9, choosing the price with the smallest gap rather than calculating where the gap is zero.
