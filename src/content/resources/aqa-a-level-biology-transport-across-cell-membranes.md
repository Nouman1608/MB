Section 3.2.3 of the AQA A-level Biology (7402) specification, *Transport across cell membranes*, asks you to
describe the fluid-mosaic model of membrane structure and to explain five ways substances cross membranes: simple
diffusion, facilitated diffusion, osmosis, active transport and co-transport. You must also explain how surface
area, the number of carrier and channel proteins, and gradients of concentration or water potential change the rate
of transport, and you must know **required practicals 3 and 4**.

## What is the fluid-mosaic model?

The membrane is a **phospholipid bilayer**: hydrophilic phosphate heads face the watery cytoplasm and tissue fluid
on each side, and the hydrophobic fatty acid tails face inwards. It is **fluid** because individual phospholipids
and many proteins can move sideways within the layer, and a **mosaic** because proteins are scattered through it.

| Component | Arrangement | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Phospholipids | Bilayer, tails inwards | Barrier to water-soluble (polar) substances and ions; allow lipid-soluble substances through |
| Intrinsic proteins (channel and carrier proteins) | Span the bilayer | Transport polar molecules and ions across |
| Extrinsic proteins | On the surface | For example receptors or enzymes |
| Glycoproteins | Protein with a carbohydrate chain on the outer surface | Cell recognition (antigens), receptors, cell attachment |
| Glycolipids | Lipid with a carbohydrate chain on the outer surface | Cell recognition and stability |
| Cholesterol | Between phospholipids | **Restricts the movement** of other molecules in the membrane, making it less fluid and more stable |

## How do substances diffuse across membranes?

**Simple diffusion** is the net movement of molecules from a higher to a lower concentration, down a concentration
gradient, without energy from ATP. Small, non-polar molecules such as oxygen and carbon dioxide pass straight
through the bilayer. The bilayer **limits** simple diffusion: ions and polar or large molecules, such as glucose,
cannot pass through the hydrophobic core.

**Facilitated diffusion** is also passive and down a gradient, but uses proteins:

- **Channel proteins** form water-filled pores that let specific ions through.
- **Carrier proteins** bind a specific molecule (such as glucose), change shape and release it on the other side.

Because the number of proteins is limited, the rate of facilitated diffusion levels off at high concentration
gradients, when all the proteins are in use.

## How do you explain osmosis using water potential?

**Osmosis** is the net movement of water across a partially permeable membrane from a solution of **higher (less
negative) water potential** to a solution of **lower (more negative) water potential**.

- Water potential is shown by the Greek letter psi and measured in kilopascals (kPa).
- Pure water has the highest possible water potential: **0 kPa**.
- Adding solute lowers the water potential, so every solution has a **negative** value.

**Worked example 1.** Cell A has a water potential of -300 kPa and cell B, next to it, -500 kPa. -300 is higher than
-500, so water moves by osmosis **from A to B**.

Animal cells placed in a solution of higher water potential gain water and may burst; in lower water potential
they shrink. Plant cells gain water until the cell wall resists (they become turgid), and in a solution of lower
water potential they lose water and the membrane pulls away from the wall (plasmolysis).

## What is active transport?

**Active transport** moves molecules or ions **against** a concentration gradient, from lower to higher
concentration, using **carrier proteins** and energy from the **hydrolysis of ATP**. The molecule binds to the
carrier; ATP is hydrolysed to ADP and phosphate, releasing energy that changes the shape of the carrier protein so
the molecule is released on the other side. Cells that carry out a lot of active transport have many mitochondria
to supply ATP by respiration.

## How does co-transport absorb glucose in the ileum?

The specification illustrates co-transport with the absorption of **sodium ions and glucose** by the epithelial
cells lining the mammalian ileum.

1. Sodium ions are **actively transported** out of the epithelial cell into the blood, by a carrier protein using
   ATP. This keeps the sodium ion concentration inside the cell low.
2. Sodium ions in the lumen of the ileum now **diffuse into** the epithelial cell down their concentration
   gradient, through a **co-transporter protein**.
3. The co-transporter carries **glucose** in with the sodium ions, so glucose enters the cell **against** its own
   concentration gradient.
4. Glucose then moves from the cell into the blood by **facilitated diffusion**.

The energy comes indirectly from ATP: it is used to set up the sodium ion gradient, and that gradient drives glucose
uptake.

## What affects the rate of transport across membranes?

| Factor | Effect on rate | Example of an adaptation |
|---|---|---|
| Surface area of membrane | Greater area, faster rate | Microvilli on ileum epithelial cells; folded inner mitochondrial membrane |
| Number of channel or carrier proteins | More proteins, faster facilitated diffusion and active transport | Many glucose carriers in ileum cells |
| Concentration or water potential gradient | Steeper gradient, faster diffusion or osmosis | Blood flow removing absorbed substances |

Remember that the gradient affects passive processes; active transport depends on ATP supply and carriers.

## How do you carry out required practical 3?

The aim is to **produce a dilution series** of a solute and use a **calibration curve** to identify the water
potential of plant tissue.

**Making a dilution series.** To make 10 cm cubed of 0.4 mol dm-3 sucrose from a 1.0 mol dm-3 stock, mix
**4 cm cubed of stock with 6 cm cubed of distilled water** (0.4 / 1.0 x 10 = 4).

**Method.** Cut equal cylinders of potato (or another plant tissue), blot and weigh each. Place one in each solution
(for example 0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8 and 1.0 mol dm-3) at constant temperature for a set time. Blot and reweigh, then
calculate the **percentage change in mass**.

**Worked example 2.** A cylinder's mass changes from 2.50 g to 2.35 g. Percentage change = (2.35 - 2.50) / 2.50 x 100
= **-6.0%**.

Plot percentage change in mass (y-axis) against concentration (x-axis). Where the line crosses zero change, the
solution has the **same water potential as the tissue**. A calibration curve (or table) relating concentration to
water potential then converts that concentration into the tissue's water potential (MS 3.4: using the intercept).
Percentage change is used, not change in mass, because the cylinders start with different masses.

## How do you carry out required practical 4?

The aim is to investigate the effect of **a named variable** on the **permeability of cell-surface membranes**.
Beetroot cells contain a red pigment in their vacuoles; damaged membranes let it leak out.

1. Cut equal-sized beetroot pieces and rinse them to remove pigment from cut cells.
2. Place each in the same volume of water at a different temperature (or different concentration of ethanol) for a
   set time.
3. Remove the pieces and measure the colour of the liquid with a **colorimeter** (absorbance), using a blue-green
   filter, calibrated with distilled water.

Higher absorbance means more pigment leaked, so the membranes are more permeable. As temperature rises,
phospholipids move more and gaps appear; at high temperatures membrane proteins denature, so permeability rises
sharply. Ethanol, a solvent, dissolves lipids in the membrane and also increases permeability.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does pure water have a water potential of 0 kPa?**
It is defined that way. Water potential measures the tendency of water to move out of a solution, and pure water
has the greatest tendency, so any solute lowers it below zero.

**What is the difference between active transport and co-transport?**
Active transport uses ATP directly to move a substance against its gradient. In co-transport, ATP is used to make a
sodium ion gradient; sodium ions diffusing back in carry glucose with them against glucose's gradient.

**Why does facilitated diffusion level off?**
At high concentration gradients all the carrier or channel proteins are occupied, so the number of proteins limits
the rate.

**What does cholesterol do in a membrane?**
It restricts the movement of the other molecules in the membrane, making it less fluid and more stable.

## Common mistakes

- Writing that water moves from a "high to low concentration" in osmosis instead of using water potential.
- Saying -500 kPa is a higher water potential than -300 kPa.
- Saying glucose is actively transported directly into ileum cells; it enters by co-transport.
- Forgetting that facilitated diffusion is passive and needs no ATP.
- In practical 3, using change in mass instead of percentage change.
- In practical 4, not naming absorbance or a colorimeter as the measure of pigment.
