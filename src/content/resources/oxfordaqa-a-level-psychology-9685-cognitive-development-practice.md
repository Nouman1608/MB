## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Distinguish between assimilation and accommodation, using an example. **[2]**

**2.** Identify the Piagetian stage shown by each child.

> A: Ravi, aged 9, knows a squashed ball of clay has the same amount as before, but struggles with "What if people had no need to sleep?"
> B: Mei, aged 4, thinks her aunt on the phone can see the picture she is holding up.
> C: Ben, aged 6 months, stops looking for a toy once it is covered by a blanket.

**[3]**

**3.** Outline what is meant by class inclusion, and describe how a pre-operational child typically responds to a class inclusion question. **[2]**

**4.** In a fictional violation of expectation study, eight infants aged 4 months watched a possible and an impossible event. Looking times (seconds) were:

| Infant | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Possible event | 12 | 9 | 14 | 11 | 10 | 13 | 8 | 11 |
| Impossible event | 19 | 15 | 22 | 17 | 18 | 20 | 14 | 19 |

**(a)** Calculate the mean looking time for each event. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain what these results suggest about the infants' understanding. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain **one** limitation of using looking time as a measure. **[2]**

**5.** Explain the role of the more knowledgeable other in Vygotsky's theory. **[2]**

**6.** Explain what is meant by theory of mind. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**7.** A fictional nursery repeated a number conservation study. Forty children aged 4-6 saw a row of counters rearranged by the experimenter (standard condition); a different forty saw a "naughty teddy" rearrange them "by accident". In the standard condition, 7 children conserved; in the naughty teddy condition, 26 conserved.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of children who conserved in each condition. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the results of the two conditions might differ. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain what these results suggest about Piaget's theory. **[2]**

**8.** Zara, aged 8, is learning to bake bread with her grandfather. At first he weighs the flour with her and shows her how to knead; later he only reminds her of the next step. By the end of the month she bakes alone. Explain Zara's learning using Vygotsky's theory. **[4]**

**9.** Explain how the mirror neuron system may contribute to social cognition, and give **one** limitation of this explanation. **[4]**

**10.** Evaluate Piaget's theory of cognitive development. **[8]**

**11.** Discuss research into theory of mind, including the Sally-Anne studies. **[8]**

**12.** Discuss the view that Piaget underestimated the abilities of young children. **[12]**

## Answers

Questions 10 to 12 are extended responses. The real exam marks these with levels of response, not point by point; the [1] points show the kind of content that earns credit.

**1.** Assimilation is fitting new information into an existing schema, such as a child calling a zebra a "horse" [1]. Accommodation is changing a schema or creating a new one when information does not fit, such as forming a separate "zebra" schema once told it is not a horse [1]. **[2]**

**2.** A: concrete operational [1]. B: pre-operational [1]. C: sensorimotor [1]. **[3]**

**3.** Class inclusion is understanding that a class can contain subclasses, for example that brown beads are also wooden beads [1]. Asked whether there are more brown beads or more wooden beads, a pre-operational child usually answers "brown", comparing the two subclasses instead of the whole class [1]. **[2]**

**4. (a)** Possible: 88 / 8 = 11.0 s [1]. Impossible: 144 / 8 = 18.0 s [1].
**(b)** Every infant looked longer at the impossible event, suggesting it violated their expectations [1]. This implies they understood that hidden objects continue to exist, earlier than Piaget claimed [1].
**(c)** Longer looking may reflect interest in something novel or perceptually different rather than understanding [1], so the measure needs interpretation and may overestimate infants' knowledge [1]. **[6]**

**5.** The MKO is anyone with greater skill or knowledge, such as a parent, teacher or peer [1], who guides the child's learning within the zone of proximal development, enabling them to do what they could not do alone [1]. **[2]**

**6.** The understanding that other people have their own beliefs, desires and intentions [1], which may differ from one's own and from reality, allowing a person to predict and explain others' behaviour [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Standard: 7 / 40 = 17.5% [1]. Naughty teddy: 26 / 40 = 65% [1].
**(b)** In the standard condition the experimenter deliberately changed the row and then asked the question again [1], which may have suggested to children that a different answer was expected [1]. The "accidental" change made the question more natural, so children could show their real understanding [1].
**(c)** Many children aged 4-6 could conserve, so Piaget may have underestimated pre-operational children [1], because his method, rather than their ability, produced failure [1]. **[7]**

**8.** Zara's grandfather is a more knowledgeable other [1]. Baking is within her zone of proximal development: she cannot do it alone at first but can with help [1]. He scaffolds the task, first demonstrating and working with her [1], then withdrawing support to reminders until she is independent [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Mirror neurons fire both when a person acts and when they observe someone else perform the same action [1]. This may let us simulate others' actions internally and so understand their intentions, supporting theory of mind and empathy [1]. Limitation: most direct recordings come from macaque monkeys, so evidence in humans is indirect [1], and critics such as Hickok (2009) argue the role in understanding intentions has been overstated [1]. **[4]**

**10.** *Indicative content (levels marking applies).* Brief outline: schemas, equilibration and four stages [1]. Strength: hugely influential, shaping discovery learning in education [1]. Strength: the sequence of stages is broadly supported across cultures [1]. Limitation: Hughes (1975) found about 90% of young children passed a policeman doll task, so egocentrism was overestimated [1]. Limitation: McGarrigle and Donaldson (1974) found conservation in about 62% of 4- to 6-year-olds with the naughty teddy [1]. Limitation: Baillargeon's research suggests object permanence appears months earlier [1]. Limitation: Piaget underplayed social and cultural influences emphasised by Vygotsky [1]. Judgement: a valuable framework whose ages and methods need revising [1]. **[8]**

**11.** *Indicative content (levels marking applies).* Theory of mind: understanding others' beliefs and intentions [1]. Wimmer and Perner (1983) found most children pass false-belief tasks at around four [1]. Sally-Anne: Sally's marble moved while she was away; children asked where she would look [1]. About 85% of typically developing children and 86% of children with Down syndrome passed, against 20% of autistic children [1]. Strength: simple, replicable and useful for understanding autism [1]. Limitation: dolls make the task artificial [1]. Limitation: failure may reflect language or memory demands rather than theory of mind [1]. Not all autistic children failed, so theory of mind cannot fully explain autism [1]. **[8]**

**12.** *Indicative content (levels marking applies).* Piaget claimed pre-operational children are egocentric and cannot conserve, and that object permanence appears at about 8 months [1]. Hughes (1975): about 90% of 3.5- to 5-year-olds took another's perspective in the policeman doll task [1]. The three mountains task may have been too abstract [1]. McGarrigle and Donaldson (1974): about 62% conserved with the naughty teddy, against 16% in the standard version [1]. Repeated questioning may have misled children [1]. Baillargeon and DeVos (1991): infants of about 3.5 months looked longer at the impossible carrot event [1]. However, looking time may reflect novelty, not understanding [1]. Counter-argument: later studies confirm Piaget's sequence of development [1]. Some critics' tasks may give extra cues that overestimate ability [1]. Piaget's own methods were innovative and revealed real differences in reasoning [1]. Vygotsky would add that, with scaffolding, children can do more than Piaget's solo tasks show [1]. Judgement: Piaget underestimated the ages at which abilities appear, but not the general direction of development [1]. **[12]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Stage identification without using the clue in the scenario.
- Means calculated without dividing by the correct number.
- Describing Baillargeon's findings without explaining the logic of looking time.
- Applying Vygotsky without naming the MKO, ZPD and scaffolding.
- Extended answers listing studies without a judgement.
