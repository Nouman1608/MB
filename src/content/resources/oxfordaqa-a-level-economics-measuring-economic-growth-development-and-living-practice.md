## Questions

**1.** In Country K, GDP is 1,250 (billion units), income received from abroad is 70, income paid abroad is 130 and capital depreciation is 110.

**(a)** Calculate Gross National Income. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate Net National Income. **[1]**

**2.** Country L's nominal GDP rises from 2,800 to 3,150 while its price index rises from 100 to 108.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage rise in nominal GDP. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the rate of real economic growth, to one decimal place. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why real growth is the main measure of economic growth. **[2]**

**3.** Real GDP in Country M rises by 5% and its population by 3%.

**(a)** Calculate the growth in real GDP per head, to one decimal place. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why living standards of a typical household may still not have risen. **[2]**

**4.** The same basket of goods costs 450 j-units in Country J and 90 k-units in Country K. The market exchange rate is 8 j-units per k-unit. GDP per head is 60,000 j-units in Country J and 11,000 k-units in Country K.

**(a)** Calculate the PPP exchange rate. **[1]**
**(b)** Convert Country J's GDP per head into k-units at the market rate and at the PPP rate. **[2]**
**(c)** State which country has the higher GDP per head when PPP is used. **[1]**

**5.** A country has HDI dimension indices of 0.48 (health), 0.72 (education) and 0.90 (income).

**(a)** Calculate its HDI as a geometric mean, to three decimal places. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why HDI uses a geometric mean rather than an arithmetic mean. **[2]**

**6.** In Country P, 24% of people are MPI-poor and they are deprived in 50% of weighted indicators on average. In Country Q, 40% are MPI-poor and deprivation averages 35%.

**(a)** Calculate the MPI for each country. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain what the MPI shows that a simple headcount does not. **[2]**

**7.** Country R's income shares for its five quintiles, poorest to richest, are 5%, 9%, 14%, 23% and 49%.

**(a)** Calculate the cumulative income shares. **[1]**
**(b)** Estimate the Gini coefficient, showing your method. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the quintile ratio. **[1]**
**(d)** State one limitation of this Gini estimate. **[1]**

**8.** Over ten years, Country Z records the following.

| | Start | End |
|---|---|---|
| Real GDP per head (index) | 100 | 148 |
| Infant deaths per 1,000 live births | 52 | 47 |
| Adult literacy rate | 60% | 61% |
| Gini coefficient | 0.41 | 0.52 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage fall in the infant mortality rate, to one decimal place. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain whether the data show economic growth with economic development. **[5]**

**9.** Describe three general characteristics that distinguish an emerging economy from a typical LEDC. **[3]**

**10.** Explain what is meant by sustainable development and one way in which fast growth could threaten it. **[4]**

**11.** Assess whether real GDP per head is a reliable indicator of living standards. **[9]**

**12.** Country D has an HDI of 0.81 and an IHDI of 0.64. Country E has an HDI of 0.74 and an IHDI of 0.69.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage loss in HDI due to inequality for each country. **[2]**
**(b)** Evaluate which country is likely to offer the better living standards to a typical person. **[6]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** GNI = GDP + income from abroad − income paid abroad [1] = 1,250 + 70 − 130 = **1,190** [1]
**(b)** NNI = 1,190 − 110 = **1,080** [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Writing the formula before substituting shows the method, and the units (billions of currency units) belong in the final line.

**2. (a)** (3,150 − 2,800) ÷ 2,800 × 100 = **12.5%** [1]
**(b)** Real GDP at base prices = 3,150 ÷ 1.08 = 2,916.7 [1]. Real growth = (2,916.7 − 2,800) ÷ 2,800 = **4.2%** [1]
**(c)** Nominal GDP can rise through higher prices alone [1]. Real GDP removes inflation, so a rise shows more goods and services produced [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* An answer that gives 12.5% for part (b) has not used the price index; state that you are deflating before you calculate.

**3. (a)** 1.05 ÷ 1.03 [1] = 1.0194, so **1.9%** [1]
**(b)** GDP per head is an average, so if the gains went mainly to high earners the typical household gains little [1]. A median or the share of the poorest fifth would show this better [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Simple subtraction (5 − 3 = 2%) is close but not exact; the ratio method is safer when a decimal place is asked for.

**4. (a)** 450 ÷ 90 = **5 j-units per k-unit** [1]
**(b)** Market rate: 60,000 ÷ 8 = 7,500 k-units [1]. PPP: 60,000 ÷ 5 = 12,000 k-units [1]
**(c)** **Country J** (12,000 against 11,000) [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The ranking reverses between the two conversions, so state both comparisons rather than only the final one.

**5. (a)** (0.48 × 0.72 × 0.90)^(1/3) [1] = 0.31104^(1/3) = **0.678** [1]
**(b)** A geometric mean penalises an unbalanced result [1], so a high income index cannot fully offset a low health or education index (the arithmetic mean here is 0.700, higher than 0.678) [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Explain the effect on the result; saying only that it is "more accurate" gives the marker nothing to credit.

**6. (a)** Country P: 0.24 × 0.50 = **0.12** [1]. Country Q: 0.40 × 0.35 = **0.14** [1]
**(b)** The headcount counts only how many are poor [1]. MPI also reflects the intensity of overlapping deprivations, so it shows how badly the poor are deprived [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Linking each part of H × A to its meaning, extent and depth, is more convincing than repeating the formula.

**7. (a)** Cumulative shares: 5, 14, 28, 51, 100 [1]
**(b)** Pair each cumulative share with the one before it and sum: 5 + 19 + 42 + 79 + 151 = 296 [1]. Gini = 1 − (0.2 × 296 ÷ 100) = 1 − 0.592 = **0.408** [1]
**(c)** 49 ÷ 5 = **9.8** [1]
**(d)** It ignores inequality inside each quintile, so it understates the true Gini [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the cumulative shares first; a diagram or table of strips makes the method visible if one number is wrong.

**8. (a)** (52 − 47) ÷ 52 × 100 = **9.6%** [1]
**(b)** Growth: real GDP per head rose 48% [1]. Some development: infant deaths fell [1]. But progress was small: the fall was under 10% and literacy rose by only one percentage point [1]. The Gini rose from 0.41 to 0.52, so the gains were unevenly shared and the poor probably gained less than the average [1]. Judgement: this looks like growth with limited development, because the rise in output was not matched by health, education or fairness [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote figures from the table in each point; a judgement that cites at least two indicators is better supported than one resting on GDP alone.

**9.** Any three, each framed as a contrast with an LEDC: industrial output and manufactured exports growing faster [1]; incomes per head rising quickly [1]; urbanisation and a growing middle class, or larger FDI inflows, or a more diversified economy less reliant on primary products [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A bare list of features earns less than features framed as differences from an LEDC.

**10.** Sustainable development improves well-being of current and future generations [1] and does not borrow from future living standards by running down natural resources [1]. For example, fast growth based on burning more fossil fuel [1] increases pollution and climate damage that future generations inherit [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Say how the growth causes the harm; naming "pollution" alone does not explain the threat.

**11.** Strengths: it measures average output per person, widely available for comparison across countries and years [1]; usually correlates with life expectancy and education, as higher output funds health and schools [1]. Weaknesses: it is an average, so unequal income distribution can leave most people below the figure [2]. Informal and unpaid work is missed, understating poorer countries [1]. Pollution and resource depletion are not subtracted, so growth that harms future living standards still raises GDP [2]. Comparisons across countries depend on exchange rates, so PPP is needed [1]. Judgement: it is a useful starting indicator, but reliability depends on inequality, the share of informal activity and whether growth is sustainable, so it should be used with HDI, IHDI and distribution measures [1] **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Each weakness should end with a consequence; a judgement that names the conditions it depends on is stronger than "it depends".

**12. (a)** Country D: (0.81 − 0.64) ÷ 0.81 = **21.0%** [1]. Country E: (0.74 − 0.69) ÷ 0.74 = **6.8%** [1]
**(b)** Country D has the higher average achievement [1] but loses much more to inequality, so its typical person is closer to Country E's position (IHDI 0.64 against 0.69) [2]. Country E has the higher IHDI, so its gains are shared more evenly [1]. Limits: IHDI still omits environment and freedom, and survey data may be weak [1]. Judgement: Country E likely offers the better living standard to a typical person, though the gap is small and depends on the weight put on inequality [1] **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* The two sets of figures point in opposite directions, so state which one you rely on and why.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Reporting nominal rather than real growth.
- Dropping units, such as "per 1,000 live births".
- Describing indicators without saying what the number means for living standards.
- Treating a high average as proof that most people are better off.
- Using growth in total GDP when per head or distribution is asked.
- Listing limitations with no consequence or no judgement at the end.
- Confusing PPP with the market exchange rate.
- Mixing up IHDI (adjusted average) and MPI (counted deprivation).
