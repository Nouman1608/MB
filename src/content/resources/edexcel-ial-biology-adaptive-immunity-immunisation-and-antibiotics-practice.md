These questions cover statements 6.10 to 6.15 (B and T cells, types of immunity, evasion by pathogens, antibiotics, Core Practical 14 and hospital-acquired infections) of the Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Subsidiary/Advanced Level Biology specification (YBI11), Issue 2. Topic 6 is International A2 (IA2) and there are no tiers. ## Questions

**1.** State one function of each of the following cells in the immune response: T helper cells, T killer cells and T memory cells. **[3]**

**2.** Compare B effector cells and B memory cells. **[4]**

**3.** Name the type of immunity in each case.
**(a)** A newborn receives antibodies in its mother's milk. **[1]**
**(b)** A hiker bitten by a snake is injected with antivenom. **[1]**
**(c)** A child recovers from chickenpox and cannot catch it again. **[1]**
**(d)** An adult is given a toxoid vaccine against tetanus. **[1]**
**(e)** Explain why the protection in (b) is short-lived. **[2]**

**4.** A volunteer is given a vaccine dose on day 0 and a second dose on day 35. The table shows antibody concentration in arbitrary units (AU).

| Day | 7 | 14 | 21 | 28 | 35 | 42 | 49 | 56 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Antibody (AU) | 0 | 5 | 12 | 8 | 4 | 40 | 95 | 70 |

**(a)** Calculate the mean rate of increase in antibody concentration between day 35 and day 49. **[2]**
**(b)** Express the second peak as a multiple of the first peak, to 2 significant figures. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain the difference between the two responses. **[3]**

**5.** Explain how antigenic variation in influenza viruses, and the effect of HIV on T helper cells, each support the theory of an evolutionary race between pathogens and hosts. **[5]**

**6.** Penicillin prevents bacteria from building their cell walls. Tetracycline binds to bacterial ribosomes and stops protein synthesis.
**(a)** Distinguish between a bactericidal and a bacteriostatic antibiotic. **[2]**
**(b)** Decide which type each of the two antibiotics is most likely to be, and explain how its action leads to that effect. **[2]**

**7.** A culture of bacteria contained 2.0 × 10⁶ viable cells per cm³. After 8 hours the counts were: no antibiotic, 6.4 × 10⁸; antibiotic X, 2.1 × 10⁶; antibiotic Y, 4.0 × 10³.
**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in viable cells for X and for Y. **[2]**
**(b)** Classify each antibiotic as bacteriostatic or bactericidal, giving a reason. **[2]**
**(c)** The culture with Y still looked cloudy. Explain why, and suggest how to measure the effect properly. **[2]**

**8.** Four discs, each 6 mm in diameter, were placed on a lawn of bacteria. Disc U contained sterile water. The table shows the diameters of the clear zones (including the disc) in mm.

| Disc | Repeat 1 | Repeat 2 | Repeat 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| S | 19 | 21 | 20 |
| T | 10 | 9 | 11 |
| U | 6 | 6 | 6 |
| V | 15 | 16 | 14 |

**(a)** Calculate the mean zone diameter and the zone area for T. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate how many times greater the clear area beyond the disc is for S than for V. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain the purpose of disc U and what its result shows. **[2]**

**9.** Describe how you would investigate the effect of three different antibiotics on a species of bacteria, including how you would reduce the risks. **[7]**

**10.** Antibiotic-resistant bacteria have become common on a hospital ward.
**(a)** Explain how this could have happened. **[3]**
**(b)** Describe two prescribing practices that reduce the risk, and explain how each works. **[4]**

**11.** A hospital ward recorded 48 infections in 16 000 patient-days. After new procedures, it recorded 27 infections in 15 000 patient-days.
**(a)** Calculate the infection rate per 1000 patient-days before and after, and the percentage reduction in the rate. **[3]**
**(b)** Give two causes of hospital-acquired infections and, for each, a hospital practice that reduces the risk. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** T helper cells release cytokines that activate B cells, T killer cells or phagocytes [1]. T killer cells destroy body cells showing the antigen, such as infected cells [1]. T memory cells stay in the body and form T helper and T killer cells quickly on a second exposure [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A function means what the cell does. "Helps" or "kills" alone is too vague; say what it acts on.

**2.** Both form from a selected B cell that divided by mitosis, and carry the same receptor for the antigen [1]. B effector cells (plasma cells) secrete antibody [1]; B memory cells do not secrete antibody now [1]. Effector cells are short-lived, whereas B memory cells persist and respond if the antigen returns [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A comparison needs both cells in each sentence. Writing about each cell in a separate paragraph leaves the link to the reader.

**3. (a)** Natural passive [1]. **(b)** Artificial passive [1]. **(c)** Natural active [1]. **(d)** Artificial active [1]. **(e)** The injected antibodies are not made by the hiker's own B cells, so no memory cells form [1], and the antibodies are broken down and not replaced [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the two-word names. Define active as "makes own antibodies", and artificial as "medical treatment", if a question asks for a reason.

**4. (a)** 95 − 4 = 91 AU [1]; 91 / 14 = **6.5 AU per day** [1]. **(b)** 95 / 12 = **7.9 times** [1]. **(c)** The first dose produced B memory cells [1]. They were already selected and more numerous than the original matching B cells, so they divided quickly into plasma cells [1]. More plasma cells made more antibody, sooner, so the peak was higher [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Take values from the table and show the subtraction. Say "memory cells" rather than "the body remembers".

**5.** Influenza viruses mutate, changing surface antigens [1]. Memory cells from an earlier infection no longer recognise the new antigen, so the variant survives and reproduces [1]. HIV infects T helper cells [1] and destroys them, so B cells and T killer cells are not activated [1]. In both cases the pathogen variants that evade the immune response are selected, and hosts then need new defences, which is an evolutionary race [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Connect each example to selection. Listing the examples without saying why they spread earns less than a linked chain.

**6. (a)** Bactericidal antibiotics kill bacteria [1]; bacteriostatic antibiotics stop them growing or reproducing without killing them [1]. **(b)** Penicillin is bactericidal because, with no cell wall, water enters by osmosis and the cell bursts [1]. Tetracycline is bacteriostatic because, without protein synthesis, the bacteria cannot grow or divide, but they are not necessarily killed [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Most likely" signals that you should give the mechanism. A named type with no reason gains little.

**7. (a)** X: (2.1 − 2.0) / 2.0 × 100 = **+5 %** [1]. Y: (4.0 × 10³ − 2.0 × 10⁶) / 2.0 × 10⁶ × 100 = **−99.8 %** [1]. **(b)** X is bacteriostatic, as viable cells are the same while the control grew [1]. Y is bactericidal, as viable cells fell [1]. **(c)** Dead cells still scatter light, so the turbidity stays high [1]. Use a viable count, such as dilution plating [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Give the sign of a percentage change and the figures used. A fall quoted without a minus sign is ambiguous.

**8. (a)** Mean = (10 + 9 + 11) / 3 = 10.0 mm [1]; area = π × 5² = **78.5 mm²** [1]. **(b)** S: π × 10² = 314 mm²; minus the disc, π × 3² = 28.3 mm², gives 286 mm² [1]. V: π × 7.5² = 177 mm², minus 28.3 gives 148 mm² [1]. 286 / 148 = **1.9 times** [1]. **(c)** U is a control disc [1]; the zone is the size of the disc, so water and the disc do not inhibit growth, and the zones are due to the antibiotics [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Halve the diameter before squaring. Say whether your area includes the disc, so a reader can follow the method.

**9.** Wash hands, disinfect the bench and use sterile equipment and aseptic technique [1]. Spread a standard volume of a harmless bacterial culture over sterile nutrient agar to make a lawn [1]. Place discs of each antibiotic with sterile forceps, with a control disc of sterile water [1]. Tape the lid at two points, invert the plate and incubate at a set temperature, for example 25 °C [1]. After the same time, measure the diameter of each clear zone [1]. Repeat and calculate means, keeping the density of bacteria, agar depth, disc size and concentration constant [1]. Do not open the plates; use a non-pathogenic strain and autoclave or disinfect plates afterwards [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Cover the independent variable, the measurement, the controls and the safety in turn. Write "the same volume of culture" rather than "a fair test".

**10. (a)** A random mutation gave one bacterium a resistance allele [1]. Sensitive bacteria were killed by the antibiotic, leaving resistant individuals to reproduce with less competition [1]. Offspring inherited the allele, and plasmids may have carried it into other bacteria, so resistant bacteria became common [1]. **(b)** Prescribe antibiotics only when they are needed [1], so fewer bacteria are exposed and there is less selection for resistance [1]. Test which antibiotic the bacteria are sensitive to before choosing one [1], so a narrow, effective antibiotic can be used and the others are saved for resistant infections [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Use "selection" and "allele" rather than "the bacteria become resistant". For each practice, add the reason it lowers risk.

**11. (a)** Before: 48 / 16 000 × 1000 = **3.0** [1]. After: 27 / 15 000 × 1000 = **1.8** [1]. (3.0 − 1.8) / 3.0 × 100 = **40 %** [1]. **(b)** Hands carry bacteria between patients [1], so wash hands or use alcohol gel [1]. Surgical wounds and devices let bacteria in [1], so use aseptic technique or remove devices early [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Use rates, not raw counts, when the patient-days differ. Pair each cause with a practice that targets that cause.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing that T cells make antibodies, or that plasma cells are memory cells.
- Describing the second response as "better" without saying faster, higher and why.
- Mixing up natural and artificial, or active and passive, and forgetting that passive gives no memory cells.
- Saying bacteria "become resistant" or "get used to" an antibiotic.
- Treating a clear zone as proof that an antibiotic kills the bacteria.
- Using diameter in place of radius in area calculations, or leaving out the squared term.
- Missing the control disc, the inverted plate or the safety measures in a method.
- Quoting infection counts when the number of patient-days is different.
- Naming a hospital practice with no link to the cause it addresses.
