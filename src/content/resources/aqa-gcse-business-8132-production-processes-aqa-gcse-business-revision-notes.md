## Core recall

### What the specification lists

- Methods of production: job and flow. Know when each is appropriate.
- Efficiency in production: lean production and just in time (JIT). Consider how lean techniques can make production more efficient.

### Job and flow side by side

| | Job production | Flow production |
|---|---|---|
| What is made | One-off items to individual order | Large volumes of identical items |
| When production starts | When an order arrives | Continuously, ahead of demand |
| Labour | Highly skilled, varied tasks | Specialised, often repetitive tasks |
| Equipment | General purpose tools | Specialised machinery and production lines |
| Cost per unit | High | Low |
| Price | Premium | Competitive |
| Flexibility | High | Low |
| Stock of finished goods | Little | Large |
| Main risk | Slow output, irregular revenue | High set-up cost, breakdowns stop the line |

### Choosing a method: five questions

1. Does each customer want something different? Job.
2. Is demand large and steady? Flow.
3. Will customers wait for their product? Job.
4. Must the price compete with rivals? Flow.
5. Can the business afford production-line machinery? Flow needs it.

### Lean production in one sentence

An approach to production that aims to cut every kind of waste: overproduction, waiting, unnecessary movement, defects and excess stock.

### Lean techniques

- Just in time (JIT): materials arrive just before use; make to order.
- Continuous improvement (kaizen): many small improvements suggested by workers.
- Right first time: check quality at each stage.
- Workplace layout: reduce movement and waiting.

### Lean benefits and drawbacks

| Benefits | Drawbacks |
|---|---|
| Lower costs, less waste | Relies on reliable suppliers |
| Less cash tied up in stock | No buffer if demand jumps |
| Faster production | Training and time to introduce |
| Better quality | Staff may resist change |
| Staff involvement | More deliveries can raise transport costs |

### Efficiency calculations

- Productivity = total output ÷ number of workers
- Reject (defect) rate = rejected items ÷ total output × 100
- Percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100

Figures invented: 12 workers make 9,600 items a week. Productivity = 9,600 ÷ 12 = 800 per worker. If output rises to 10,800 with the same staff, productivity = 900, a rise of 12.5%.

### Links to other functions

- Finance: flow needs large investment; lean frees cash from stock.
- Human resources: job needs skilled staff; flow needs fewer skills but risks low motivation; lean needs training and staff involvement.
- Marketing: job supports premium pricing and personalisation; flow supports low prices and wide distribution.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Production | Turning inputs into goods or services |
| Job production | Making one-off products to meet individual customer orders |
| Flow production | Continuous production of large volumes of identical products |
| Standardised | Every unit is the same |
| Efficiency | Producing output using as few resources as possible |
| Productivity | Output per worker in a period |
| Lean production | Production that aims to minimise waste |
| Just in time (JIT) | Materials arrive just as they are needed; little stock held |
| Kaizen | Continuous improvement through small, regular changes |
| Unit cost | The average cost of producing one item |

## Exam traps

- **Job vs flow confused with JIT vs JIC.** Production methods and stock methods are separate decisions.
- **"Flow is always cheaper."** Only per unit, and only with high steady demand.
- **Lean equals JIT only.** Name other techniques too.
- **Ignoring people.** Flow can demotivate; lean needs staff support.
- **No context.** Choose the method that suits the product described.
- **Percentage change from the wrong base.** Divide by the old figure.

## Self-test

1. Name the two methods of production in the specification.
2. Give one example of a product made by job production and one by flow production.
3. Why is the cost per unit higher in job production?
4. Give two disadvantages of flow production.
5. Why does flow production need high, steady demand?
6. Define lean production.
7. Name two lean production techniques.
8. Give one risk of using JIT.
9. A factory has 25 workers producing 15,000 units a week. Calculate productivity.
10. Output rises from 15,000 to 16,500 with the same workforce. Calculate the percentage rise in productivity.
11. Why might workers on a flow production line be less motivated than those in job production?
12. Which function of a business is most affected by the high set-up cost of flow production?

### Answers

1. Job and flow.
2. For example: job, a made-to-measure suit; flow, tinned soup.
3. Skilled labour spends a long time on each item, and materials are bought in small quantities.
4. Any two: high set-up cost, inflexible, repetitive work, a breakdown stops the line.
5. The expensive machinery must be used fully to spread its cost over many units; otherwise unit costs rise.
6. An approach to production that aims to cut waste of all kinds.
7. Any two: JIT, continuous improvement (kaizen), right first time, reorganising the workplace.
8. A late delivery can stop production, or there is no spare stock if demand rises.
9. 15,000 ÷ 25 = 600 units per worker per week.
10. New productivity 660; (660 − 600) ÷ 600 × 100 = 10%.
11. They repeat one task many times, which can be boring and gives little sense of achievement.
12. Finance, because it must raise and budget for the large investment.
