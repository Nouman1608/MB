## Definitions

- **Work**: the single literary text you write about, studied in class.
- **Non-literary body of work**: a group of related non-literary texts studied together. Your
  teacher confirms what counts for your class.
- **Line of inquiry**: the one focused, arguable question your essay answers.
- **Thesis**: your answer to that question, stated in the introduction and proved in the body.
- **Evidence**: exact words, images or features from the work, with a location reference.
- **Analysis**: explaining *how* specific choices create meaning, and *why* that matters to the
  line of inquiry.
- **Primary source**: the work itself. **Secondary source**: anything written about it or its context.
- **Citation**: the in-text reference that shows where a quotation or idea came from.
- **Works cited / bibliography**: the full list of sources at the end.

## Three ways into a line of inquiry

| Area of exploration | Ask | Example angle |
|---|---|---|
| Readers, writers and texts | How do the writer's choices make meaning? | How does a first-person diary form limit what the reader is told? |
| Time and space | How does context of writing or reading change meaning? | How does a 1890s story about medical "rest" read now? |
| Intertextuality: connecting texts | How does the work use or bend conventions of other texts? | How does a story borrow haunted-house conventions for an ordinary home? |

## Method in steps

### Choosing the work

1. List the works you studied that you have a real question about.
2. For each, find five precise moments linked to that question.
3. Drop any work where the moments are only plot events.
4. Keep the one where your question is narrowest and your evidence is richest.

### Building the line of inquiry

1. Start with a topic (e.g. control in a marriage).
2. Log evidence before you write the question.
3. Name the pattern the evidence shows.
4. Ask *how* the writer creates it: feature + effect + tension.
5. Write a one-sentence working thesis that answers it.

### Building a body paragraph

1. **Claim** linked to the line of inquiry.
2. **Evidence**: a short, embedded quotation.
3. **Analysis**: specific words, syntax or form, and their effect.
4. **Development**: second example, contrast or wider effect.
5. **Link** back to the thesis.

### Final checks

1. Every paragraph answers the line of inquiry.
2. Every quotation is word-for-word correct.
3. Every quotation and borrowed idea has a citation.
4. The works cited list includes the work itself.
5. The word count is 1,200–1,500 under your school's counting rule.

## Worked reminder: word budget

Draft is 1,640 words. Limit is 1,500. Cut at least **140 words**. If the draft has four body
sections, cutting 35 from each removes exactly 140. Cut plot summary and repeated points first.

## Worked reminder: description vs analysis (original text written for Marlbridge)

> *Description:* The campaign poster says "Every drop counts" and tells people to turn off the tap.
>
> *Analysis:* The slogan "Every drop counts" turns a reservoir-sized problem into a unit small enough
> to feel personal; the verb "counts" also borrows the language of money, so wasting water is framed
> as careless spending.

The second version names the words, the effect, and why it matters.

## Worked reminder: topic to line of inquiry for a body of work

Topic: *a water-saving campaign*. Evidence across the texts: the same slogan on a poster, a social
media post and a radio advert; small units ("Two minutes", "one song"); ordinary voices before the
organisation's name. Pattern: a public shortage is made to feel like a private habit. Line of
inquiry: *How does the campaign turn a town-wide shortage into a matter of household habit?* The
question names a method and an effect, and every text in the body of work can answer it.

## Essay structure at a glance

| Part | Job | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | Name the work, state the line of inquiry, give the thesis | Plot summary longer than two or three sentences |
| Body sections | One claim each, proved with short embedded quotations and analysis | Paragraphs that follow the plot, not the argument |
| Conclusion | Answer the line of inquiry directly | New evidence; repeating the introduction |
| Works cited | List the work and every secondary source | Missing online sources |

## Referencing checklist

- Quotations copied word for word, with (…) for cuts and square brackets for added words.
- Every quotation embedded in your own sentence.
- A page, line or text reference after each quotation, in your school's style.
- Paraphrased ideas from critics cited as well as quotations.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Topic vs line of inquiry**: a topic names an area; a line of inquiry asks an arguable *how* or
  *why* question about it.
- **Description vs analysis**: description says what is there; analysis says what the choice does
  and why it matters.
- **Quotation vs paraphrase**: a quotation uses the exact words and needs quotation marks and a
  citation; a paraphrase uses your words and still needs a citation if the idea is someone else's.
- **Primary vs secondary source**: the work is primary; criticism and background are secondary and
  only support your argument.
- **HL essay vs Paper 2**: the HL essay is coursework on one work or body of work; Paper 2 is a
  timed comparative essay on two literary works, written in response to one of four questions.
- **Context that argues vs context that decorates**: use context only when it changes how a line is
  read.
- **Embedded vs dropped quotation**: embedded quotations sit inside your sentence; dropped ones stand
  alone with no link to your point.

1. How long must the HL essay be?
2. What percentage of the HL final grade is it worth?
3. What may the essay be written on?
4. Do SL students write it?
5. Turn this topic into a line of inquiry: "Nature in a poetry collection".
6. Which is analysis? (a) "The writer uses imagery." (b) "The verb 'clutched' makes the gift sound
   like something stolen."
7. A draft is 1,575 words. How many words must you cut to reach the maximum?
8. You paraphrase a critic's idea in your own words. Do you need a citation?
9. Name the five parts of a body paragraph in this guide's build.
10. Which area of exploration does this question use: "How does a 1920s advert read differently
    today?"
11. Why should secondary reading come after your own close reading?
12. What should a conclusion avoid?

### Answers

1. 1,200–1,500 words.
2. 20%.
3. One literary work or a non-literary body of work studied in the course.
4. No. It is HL only.
5. Any focused *how/why* question, e.g. "How does the poet use seasonal imagery to track the
   speaker's changing sense of loss?"
6. (b): it names a specific word and its effect.

8. Yes. Borrowed ideas need a citation as well as quotations.
9. Claim, evidence, analysis, development, link.

11. So that the argument stays yours, and critics support it rather than replace it.

## Where marks are usually lost

- A broad topic ("identity in the novel") instead of a question you can prove in 1,500 words.
- Paragraphs that retell events in order rather than advance a claim.
- Techniques named ("imagery", "tone") with no specific words quoted and no effect explained.
- Long quotations that use up the word count and hide which words matter.
- Quotations copied inaccurately, so the analysis rests on words the writer never used.
- Context pasted in as background, not used to change a reading.
- For a body of work, discussing each text separately instead of the pattern across them.
- A conclusion that repeats the introduction instead of answering the question.
- Missing citations for paraphrased ideas from critics or websites.
- Going over 1,500 words because no budget was set before drafting.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a line of inquiry in the IB HL essay?**
A line of inquiry is the one focused, arguable question your essay answers. A topic names an area, such as control in a marriage; a line of inquiry asks how or why the writer creates an effect within it.

**How do you build a line of inquiry?**
Start with a topic, log evidence from the work before writing the question, name the pattern the evidence shows, then ask how the writer creates it. Finish with a one-sentence working thesis that answers it.

**What is the difference between the HL essay and Paper 2?**
In IB DP Language A: Language and Literature, the HL essay is coursework on one literary work or one non-literary body of work. Paper 2 is a timed comparative essay on two literary works, answering one of four questions.

**What should you cut first if your HL essay is over the word limit?**
Cut plot summary and repeated points first, not analysis. Setting a word budget for each section before drafting makes large cuts less likely.
