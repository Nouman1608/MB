## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** What is meant by the activation energy of a reaction? **[1]**

**2.** Give one difference between the lock and key model and the induced fit model of enzyme action. **[1]**

**3.** In an investigation, 18 cm3 of oxygen was released in 90 seconds. Calculate the mean rate of reaction in cm3 per second. **[1]**

**4.** Which statement about a competitive inhibitor is correct?

A: It binds to a site other than the active site.

B: It has a shape similar to the substrate.

C: Its effect cannot be reduced by adding more substrate.

D: It permanently changes the shape of the enzyme. **[1]**

**5.** Explain why an enzyme will catalyse only one reaction, or a very small number of reactions. **[2]**

### Section B: longer questions

**6.** A student measured the time taken for a catalase to release 10 cm3 of oxygen from hydrogen peroxide at different temperatures.

| Temperature / degrees Celsius | Time to collect 10 cm3 / s |
|---|---|
| 10 | 250 |
| 20 | 125 |
| 30 | 62 |
| 40 | 40 |
| 50 | 100 |
| 60 | 500 |

**(a)** Calculate the rate of reaction, in cm3 per second, at 30 degrees Celsius and at 50 degrees Celsius. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain the increase in rate between 10 and 40 degrees Celsius. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain the decrease in rate between 40 and 60 degrees Celsius. **[3]**

**(d)** The student's enzyme and substrate were mixed at room temperature and then placed in each water bath. Explain why this method is not valid. **[2]**

**7.** A progress curve for an enzyme reaction showed the following volume of product: 0 cm3 at 0 s, 8 cm3 at 10 s, 14 cm3 at 20 s, 18 cm3 at 30 s, 20 cm3 at 40 s and 20 cm3 at 50 s.

**(a)** Calculate the rate of reaction between 0 and 10 s, and between 20 and 30 s. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why the rate changes during the reaction. **[3]**

**8.** The table shows the rate of an enzyme-controlled reaction at different substrate concentrations, without an inhibitor and with inhibitor Z. Rates are in arbitrary units.

| Substrate concentration | Rate without inhibitor | Rate with inhibitor Z |
|---|---|---|
| 0.1 | 2.0 | 1.0 |
| 0.2 | 3.6 | 1.8 |
| 0.4 | 5.6 | 2.8 |
| 0.8 | 7.4 | 3.7 |
| 1.6 | 8.4 | 4.2 |

**(a)** Calculate the rate with inhibitor Z as a percentage of the rate without inhibitor at a substrate concentration of 0.4. **[2]**

**(b)** Use all of the data to decide whether inhibitor Z is competitive or non-competitive. Explain your answer. **[3]**

**9.** The enzyme maltase is specific to maltose. Explain how a change in pH could lower the rate of the reaction it catalyses. **[4]**

**10.** Describe how you would investigate the effect of pH on the rate of an enzyme-controlled reaction. Include the control of two variables. **[5]**

## Answers

**1.** The energy needed to start a reaction (to break or form bonds to reach the transition state) [1]. **[1]**

**2.** In lock and key the active site has a fixed shape that fits the substrate exactly, whereas in induced fit the active site changes shape when the substrate binds [1]. **[1]**

**3.** 18 / 90 = 0.20 cm3 per second [1]. **[1]**

**4.** **B** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Options A, C and D describe a non-competitive inhibitor or denaturation.

**5.** The active site has a specific tertiary structure and shape [1]. Only substrates with a complementary shape fit and form an enzyme-substrate complex [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** 30 degrees: 10 / 62 = 0.16 cm3 per second [1]. 50 degrees: 10 / 100 = 0.10 cm3 per second [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Molecules have more kinetic energy and move faster [1], so there are more frequent collisions between enzyme and substrate, and more of them have enough energy to react [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** The hydrogen bonds and other bonds holding the tertiary structure break [1]. The active site changes shape and the substrate no longer fits [1]. The enzyme is denatured and fewer enzyme-substrate complexes form, so the rate falls despite more frequent collisions [1]. **[3]**

**(d)** The enzyme and substrate would start reacting at room temperature before reaching the water bath temperature [1], so the reaction would not take place at the temperature being investigated [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Each should be brought to temperature separately and then mixed.

**7. (a)** 0 to 10 s: 8 / 10 = 0.8 cm3 per second [1]. 20 to 30 s: (18 - 14) / 10 = 0.4 cm3 per second [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** The rate falls as the reaction proceeds [1]. The substrate is used up, so the concentration of substrate falls and collisions between enzyme and substrate become less frequent [1]. The rate reaches zero when all the substrate has been converted (20 cm3 total) [1]. **[3]**

**8. (a)** 2.8 / 5.6 [1] x 100 = 50 % [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the division. A wrong final value can still earn the method mark.

**(b)** Non-competitive [1]. The rate with the inhibitor is 50 % of the rate without it at every substrate concentration (1.0/2.0, 1.8/3.6, 2.8/5.6, 3.7/7.4, 4.2/8.4) [1]. A competitive inhibitor would have less effect at high substrate concentrations, because the substrate would outcompete it, so the maximum rate would be similar [1]. **[3]**

**9.** pH changes alter the charges on the R-groups in and around the active site [1]. Ionic and hydrogen bonds that hold the tertiary structure break [1]. The shape of the active site changes [1], so maltose does not fit, fewer enzyme-substrate complexes form and the rate falls [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Use buffer solutions at a range of pH values, for example pH 3 to 9 [1]. Keep the temperature constant, for example in a water bath, and bring enzyme and substrate to this temperature before mixing [1]. Keep the volume and concentration of enzyme and substrate the same in each tube [1]. Measure the volume of product in a fixed time, or the time for a fixed change [1]. Repeat each pH and calculate a mean, and plot rate against pH to find the optimum [1]. **[5]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Mixing up the explanation of collisions (low or moderate temperature) and denaturation (high temperature).
- Leaving out the units in rate calculations.
- Saying that an inhibitor "kills" or "uses up" the enzyme.
- Not stating which part of the data supports a conclusion about an inhibitor.
- Giving a method without stating what is kept constant.
