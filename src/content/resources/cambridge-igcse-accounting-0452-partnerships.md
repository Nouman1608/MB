## What 5.2 covers

Candidates should understand: how a partnership can be a trading, service or
manufacturing business, or a combination; the advantages and disadvantages of
forming a partnership; the importance and contents of a partnership agreement;
the purpose of an appropriation account; how to prepare statements of profit or
loss, appropriation accounts and statements of financial position; how to record
interest on partners' loans, interest on capital, interest on drawings, partners'
salaries and the division of the balance of profit or loss; adjustments as in
5.1; the uses of and differences between capital and current accounts; and how
to prepare capital and current accounts in ledger form and in the statement of
financial position.

The syllabus **excludes** dissolution, changes in the profit-sharing ratio,
limited liability partnerships, and statements on the admission or departure of
a partner.

## Forming a partnership

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| More capital from several owners | Unlimited liability: partners' personal possessions can be used to pay the firm's debts |
| Skills and workload are shared | Decisions need agreement and can be slower |
| Losses are shared | Profit is shared |
| Cover during illness or holidays | Disagreements can damage the business |

## The partnership agreement

The agreement sets the rules before money is at stake, so disputes are less
likely. It normally states:

- the capital each partner contributes
- the profit- and loss-sharing ratio
- the rate of interest on capital, if any
- whether interest is charged on drawings, and how
- any salaries paid to partners
- the rate of interest on loans made by partners to the business
- any limit on drawings

Its **importance**: each partner knows what they will receive, rewards can be
matched to effort and investment (for example a salary for the partner who
manages the business), and the accounts can be prepared without argument.

## Expense or appropriation?

The **statement of profit or loss** measures the profit made by the business.
The **appropriation account** shows how that profit is shared among the partners.
This decides where each item goes.

| Item | Where | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Interest on a partner's loan | Statement of profit or loss, as an expense | The partner is acting as a lender; the business would pay interest to any lender |
| Partner's salary | Appropriation account | A share of profit, not wages to an employee |
| Interest on capital | Appropriation account | A reward for investing, paid out of profit |
| Interest on drawings | Appropriation account, **added** | A charge on partners who withdraw early, increasing the profit to share |
| Residual profit or loss | Appropriation account | Shared in the agreed ratio |

## Worked example: Coastline Kayaks

Ahmed and Bianca hire out and sell kayaks. Their agreement provides:

- interest on capital at 5% a year
- a salary of 9,000 a year to Bianca, who runs the hire business
- interest on drawings: 320 for Ahmed and 210 for Bianca for 2028
- the balance of profit or loss shared Ahmed 3/5, Bianca 2/5

Fixed capitals are Ahmed 60,000 and Bianca 40,000. On 1 July 2028 Ahmed lent the
partnership 10,000 at 6% a year; the interest had not been paid at
31 December 2028. Profit **before** loan interest was 52,600. Current account
balances at 1 January 2028 were Ahmed 2,400 credit and Bianca 600 debit. Drawings
were Ahmed 18,000 and Bianca 21,500.

**Step 1: loan interest in the statement of profit or loss.**
10,000 × 6% × 6/12 = 300. Profit for the year = 52,600 − 300 = **52,300**.
The 300 is credited to Ahmed's current account because it is unpaid.

**Step 2: the appropriation account.**

```
Coastline Kayaks: Appropriation account for the year ended 31 December 2028
Profit for the year                                          52,300
Add interest on drawings:  Ahmed                  320
                           Bianca                 210           530
                                                             52,830
Less salary:               Bianca                             9,000
Less interest on capital:  Ahmed                3,000
                           Bianca               2,000         5,000
                                                             38,830
Balance of profit shared:  Ahmed (3/5)         23,298
                           Bianca (2/5)        15,532        38,830
```

**Step 3: current accounts in columnar form.**

```
Current accounts ($)
                      Ahmed   Bianca                         Ahmed   Bianca
1 Jan 28 Balance b/d      –      600  1 Jan 28 Balance b/d   2,400        –
Drawings             18,000   21,500  Loan interest            300        –
Interest on drawings    320      210  Salary                     –    9,000
31 Dec 28 Balance c/d 10,678   4,222  Interest on capital    3,000    2,000
                                      Share of profit       23,298   15,532
                     ------   ------                        ------   ------
                     28,998   26,532                        28,998   26,532
                     ======   ======                        ======   ======
                                      1 Jan 29 Balance b/d  10,678    4,222
```

Bianca began the year with a **debit** balance: in an earlier year she withdrew
more than she was credited with, so she owed the business. This year her salary
and share of profit exceed her drawings, and she ends in credit.

**Step 4: the statement of financial position.**

```
Capital accounts:   Ahmed         60,000
                    Bianca        40,000      100,000
Current accounts:   Ahmed         10,678
                    Bianca         4,222       14,900
                                              114,900
Non-current liabilities
   Loan from Ahmed (6%)                        10,000
```

The loan is **not** part of Ahmed's capital. It is a liability and will be repaid
on the agreed terms; his capital stays in the business until the partnership ends.

## Capital and current accounts

| Capital account | Current account |
|---|---|
| Records the long-term investment each partner agreed to keep in the business | Records the partner's share of profit and other appropriations, less drawings |
| Usually fixed: changes only if a partner adds or withdraws capital permanently | Changes every year |
| Normally a credit balance | Can be a credit or a debit balance |
| The base for interest on capital | Shows whether the partner has withdrawn more than they earned |

Keeping the two separate means the agreed capital is not hidden by year-to-year
movements, and interest on capital is calculated on a stable figure.

## Common mistakes

- Putting interest on a partner's loan in the appropriation account.
- Deducting interest on drawings instead of adding it to profit to be shared.
- Calculating interest on capital using the current account balance.
- Showing a partner's salary among wages in the statement of profit or loss.
- Entering a debit opening current account balance on the credit side.
- Adding a partner's loan to the capital section.

## Exam technique

- **Prepare** an appropriation account under its own heading, starting from
  profit for the year, and show each partner's figure separately.
- **Explain** the purpose of the appropriation account: it shows how profit is
  divided according to the agreement.
- **Advise** questions on whether to form a partnership: weigh more capital and
  shared skills against unlimited liability and shared profit, for the people in
  the scenario.
