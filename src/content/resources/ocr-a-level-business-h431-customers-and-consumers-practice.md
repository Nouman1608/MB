## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Distinguish between a customer and a consumer. **[3]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by derived demand, using an example. **[3]**

**3.** Explain **one** reason why personal selling is often used in B2B marketing. **[3]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**4.** Hollin Engineering makes valves for water companies. It spends £14,000 on a trade show stand. The stand produces 35 sales leads and 20% of the leads become new accounts. An average account buys £12,000 of valves a year at a 25% margin.

**(a)** Calculate the number of new accounts won. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the cost of winning each new account. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the yearly contribution from one account. **[2]**

**(d)** Calculate the payback period for the cost of winning an account, in years to two decimal places. **[2]**

**(e)** Evaluate the usefulness to Hollin Engineering of trade shows compared with tendering for contracts. **[9]**

**5.** Tern Tea sells loose-leaf tea direct to consumers. It spends £6,000 on a social media campaign. The campaign attracts 15,000 website visits and 2% of visitors buy once. The average order is £18 and the margin is 35%.

**(a)** Calculate the number of orders. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the cost of the campaign per order. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the contribution from each order. **[2]**

**(d)** Calculate the average number of purchases each customer must make for the campaign to break even. Give your answer to one decimal place. **[3]**

**(e)** Evaluate whether social media is an appropriate method for Tern Tea to use. **[9]**

**6.** Brightwell Paints sells paint to professional decorators through trade counters and to homeowners through DIY stores. Decorators buy large amounts at agreed prices and expect advice. Homeowners buy a few tins at a time, mostly with colour in mind. Brightwell's marketing manager proposes using the same television advertising for both groups. Evaluate whether this is a good strategy. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** A customer is the person or organisation that buys the product [1]. A consumer is the person who uses it [1]. They can be different people, for example a parent who buys a toy and a child who plays with it [1]. **[3]**

**2.** Demand for a product that arises from demand for another product [1]. For example, demand for steel by a car maker depends on consumers buying cars [1], so if car sales fall, the steel supplier's sales fall too [1]. **[3]**

**3.** B2B sales are often large, complex and agreed with a small number of buyers [1], so a salesperson can explain technical details, tailor the offer and negotiate [1], and build a long-term relationship that makes the buyer less likely to switch supplier [1]. **[3]**

**4. (a)** 35 × 0.20 = **7 accounts** [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** £14,000 ÷ 7 = **£2,000** [1]. **[1]**

**(c)** £12,000 × 25% [1] = **£3,000** [1]. **[2]**

**(d)** £2,000 ÷ £3,000 [1] = **0.67 years** [1]. **[2]**

**(e)** Trade shows: they put Hollin in front of many water-company buyers in one place, and products can be demonstrated [1]; at £2,000 per account and a payback of about eight months, the cost is low compared with the value of an account [1]; face-to-face contact builds trust, which matters when a valve failure would be expensive for the buyer [1].
Limits: the cost of £14,000 is spent before any sales are certain [1]; the number of leads (35) is small and results could vary between shows [1].
Tendering: water companies often buy by tender, so Hollin may be unable to win large contracts without bidding [1]; tenders are time-consuming and often decided largely on price, which may reduce margin [1].
Judgement: both are useful and they complement each other [1]. Trade shows build relationships that can lead to being invited to tender, so Hollin should use both, but should track the lifetime value of the accounts won to check that the shows pay [1]. **[9]**

**5. (a)** 15,000 × 0.02 = **300 orders** [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** £6,000 ÷ 300 = **£20** [1]. **[1]**

**(c)** £18 × 35% [1] = **£6.30** [1]. **[2]**

**(d)** Cost per customer is £20 [1]. £20 ÷ £6.30 = 3.17 [1], so about **3.2 purchases** each on average [1]. **[3]**

**(e)** For: social media can target tea drinkers by interest, so reach is relevant [1]; it can create word of mouth cheaply [1]; links in posts take buyers straight to the website, and the results can be tracked [1]; consumers can be encouraged to reorder, which is how the campaign pays back [1].
Against: each order earns only £6.30 against £20 to win it, so the campaign loses money unless customers buy about 3.2 times [1]; results are uncertain and depend on the content and influencers [1]; competing tea brands also use social media, which raises costs [1].
Judgement: social media suits a B2C product with a modest price and repeat purchase, if combined with email or a subscription to encourage reorders and the campaign is judged by lifetime value rather than the first order [1]; without a way to secure repeat sales, it is a risky use of £6,000 [1]. **[9]**

**6.** Against a single television campaign: decorators are a small group of professional buyers, so television reaches many people who are not customers, which wastes money [1]; their decisions depend on price, product performance, advice and credit terms, not on image [1]; personal selling, trade counters and trade press suit them better [1].
For homeowners: they are many buyers with small orders, so mass media can build brand awareness and recognition at the DIY store shelf [1]; colour and image influence them [1].
For a common campaign: a brand that decorators and homeowners both recognise can reinforce trust, and a single campaign costs less than two [1]; some decorators may be influenced by homeowners' demand for a brand [1].
Judgement: a single campaign is not a good strategy for both groups. Television may suit homeowners, but decorators need different methods such as trade counters, advice and account terms [1]; the brand's identity should be consistent, with the mix adapted to each group [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing B2B and B2C without applying the features to the case.
- Using the first order only to judge a consumer campaign.
- Forgetting that the consumer can differ from the customer.
- Evaluating a method without a judgement for the business named.
