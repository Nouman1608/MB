Condensed recall for **2.2 Interactions between oceans and coastal places** (IB DP Geography, Option B: Oceans and coastal margins). See the [Oceans and coastal margins study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-oceans-coastal-margins-study-guide/) for the full account.

## What shapes coasts

Waves, tides, currents, geology, sea level and human activity.

## Wave processes

- **Erosion:** hydraulic action, abrasion, attrition, corrosion.
- **Constructive waves** tend to build beaches; **destructive waves** remove sediment.
- **Longshore drift** moves sediment along the coast in the direction of the prevailing wave approach.

### How waves get their energy

Wave size depends on **wind speed**, **wind duration** and **fetch** (the distance of open water over which the wind blows). Coasts facing a long fetch, such as Atlantic-facing shores, receive larger, more powerful waves. As a wave reaches shallow water, friction with the bed slows its base, the wave steepens and breaks, sending **swash** up the beach; water returns as **backwash**.

| | Constructive wave | Destructive wave |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | Low, long wavelength | High, steep, short wavelength |
| Swash and backwash | Swash stronger than backwash | Backwash stronger than swash |
| Effect | Builds up beaches and berms | Removes sediment, steepening then lowering beaches |

**Wave refraction:** waves slow and bend as they enter shallower water off a headland, concentrating energy on the headland and spreading it out in bays. This is why headlands erode and bays become sites of deposition.

## Landforms

| Erosion | Deposition |
|---|---|
| Cliffs, wave-cut platforms | Beaches |
| Caves, arches, stacks | Spits, bars, tombolos |

Explain each as a sequence of steps, not just a name.

### Sequences to learn

- **Wave-cut platform:** waves attack the cliff base between high and low tide, forming a **wave-cut notch**; the overhang collapses; the cliff retreats, leaving a gently sloping rock platform exposed at low tide.
- **Cave, arch, stack, stump:** waves exploit a joint or fault in a headland; hydraulic action and abrasion widen it into a cave; caves on either side meet to form an arch; the arch roof collapses, leaving a stack, which is later eroded to a stump. Old Harry Rocks in Dorset, England, are a well-known stack group.
- **Spit:** longshore drift carries sediment along the coast; where the coastline changes direction, as at an estuary mouth, deposition continues out into open water; changes in wind and wave direction curve the end into a **recurved tip**; sheltered water behind allows salt marsh to form. Spurn Head at the mouth of the Humber is a classic example.
- **Tombolo:** a ridge of deposited sediment that joins an island to the mainland, such as the link formed by Chesil Beach to the Isle of Portland.
- **Bar:** a spit that grows across a bay, closing it off to form a lagoon.

### Geology matters

Resistant rocks such as granite and chalk form steep cliffs and headlands; weaker clays and sands erode quickly. On a **discordant** coast, rock bands run at right angles to the shore, producing alternating headlands and bays; on a **concordant** coast they run parallel, so a resistant outer band protects weaker rock behind until it is breached, as at Lulworth Cove in Dorset. The Holderness coast in north-east England, made of soft glacial till, is among the fastest-eroding coasts in Europe, losing on average roughly 2 m a year.

## Sediment cells

A **sediment cell** treats a stretch of coast as a system with inputs, transfers, stores and outputs. Interrupting the flow, for example with a groyne or harbour wall, can starve beaches downdrift.

| Part | Examples |
|---|---|
| Inputs | Cliff erosion, rivers, offshore sediment |
| Transfers | Longshore drift, onshore and offshore movement |
| Stores | Beaches, spits, dunes, offshore bars |
| Outputs | Sediment lost to deep water or carried out of the cell |

## Sea level change

- **Eustatic:** change in the global volume of water.
- **Isostatic:** land rising or sinking.
- Present-day **relative** sea level rise reflects both.
- Low-lying deltas, atolls and reef islands are especially exposed.

After the last glaciation, land that had been pressed down by ice sheets began to rise (**post-glacial rebound**), so parts of Scotland and Scandinavia are still rising while southern England is slowly sinking. Falling relative sea level leaves **emergent** landforms such as raised beaches; rising sea level drowns valleys to form **submergent** landforms: **rias** (drowned river valleys) and **fjords** (drowned glacial troughs, as in Norway).

## Coastal ecosystems

Coral reefs, mangroves and salt marshes:

- dissipate wave energy (natural protection);
- support fisheries;
- store carbon.

Threats: warming, acidification, pollution, coastal development, destructive fishing. Losing the ecosystem raises coastal vulnerability.

The Sundarbans, the world's largest mangrove forest, spans the delta of the Ganges and Brahmaputra in Bangladesh and India and buffers storm surges from Bay of Bengal cyclones. Mangroves are lost to shrimp farms, rice paddies and urban growth, which removes this buffer.

## Human modification

People change coastal systems directly and indirectly: ports, land reclamation, sand mining and coastal tourism alter sediment budgets and habitats. Dams far inland can starve deltas: after the Aswan High Dam trapped Nile sediment, parts of the Nile delta coast began to erode.

## Worked check

A cliff top retreats 3.6 m in 8 years: 3.6 / 8 = 0.45 m per year. Retreat is episodic (most happens in storms), so extrapolating a mean rate is risky.

## Model paragraph

*Explain how a spit forms.* "A spit forms where longshore drift carries sediment along the coast and the coastline changes direction, for example at a river mouth. Sediment continues to be deposited in the direction of drift, extending a ridge of sand and shingle out into the sea. Occasional winds and waves from another direction curve the end, forming a recurved tip. Sheltered, low-energy water behind the spit allows fine sediment to settle and salt marsh to develop, as at Spurn Head."

## Exam traps

- Describing a landform without the process that formed it.
- Forgetting that coasts are systems: action in one place has knock-on effects elsewhere.
- Mixing eustatic and isostatic change.
- Treating mean erosion rates as steady.
- Mixing up swash and backwash dominance in constructive and destructive waves.
- Forgetting that inland dams can affect coasts by cutting sediment supply.
