The [Design Cycle revision notes](/resources/ib-myp-design-design-cycle-revision-notes/) and
[assessment revision notes](/resources/ib-myp-design-assessment-revision-notes/) set out the design
cycle's four stages and the four criteria that map onto them. These notes go further, working through
what genuinely top-band evidence looks like for each criterion with a worked example, since a project
can move through all four design-cycle stages without producing top-band evidence for the
corresponding criterion at each one.

## Criterion A: Inquiring and analysing

Top-band evidence identifies a genuine problem needing a solution, analyses the need for that
solution with reference to a real target audience or client, and researches existing similar
products or solutions before writing a design brief.

**Worked example:** A lower-band response to "design a solution for reducing food waste in a school
canteen" might jump straight to a brief without establishing why food waste happens at that specific
canteen. Top-band Criterion A evidence first investigates the actual problem (e.g. surveying students
and staff, observing canteen practices, researching existing food-waste-reduction solutions used
elsewhere), and only then writes a design brief grounded in that specific, evidenced analysis of the
target audience's actual need.

## Criterion B: Developing ideas

Top-band evidence writes a detailed, testable design specification that genuinely drives development
of a solution, generates and evaluates a range of feasible design ideas against that specification,
and presents the chosen solution with justified reasoning for why it was selected over the
alternatives.

**Worked example:** Top-band Criterion B evidence does not present only one idea -- it generates
several genuinely different possible solutions, evaluates each against the specification's testable
criteria (e.g. cost, materials, target-audience appeal), and explains specifically why the chosen
design best meets the specification compared with the rejected alternatives, rather than simply
asserting that the final choice is best.

## Criterion C: Creating the solution

Top-band evidence follows a detailed, logically sequenced plan to create a prototype sufficient for
testing and evaluation, including how time, resources and any risks were managed during creation, with
changes to the original plan justified and recorded as they occur.

**Worked example:** A top-band Criterion C log records not just the final prototype but the sequence
followed to build it, and explicitly notes any point where the original plan had to change (e.g. a
material substitution due to availability), explaining why the change was made and how it affected the
following steps -- a project that produces only a finished prototype with no record of the process
followed to reach it cannot demonstrate top-band Creating the solution evidence, regardless of how good
the final prototype looks.

## Criterion D: Evaluating

Top-band evidence designs specific, measurable tests against the original specification, carries them
out, and objectively evaluates the solution's success against each specification point individually --
including identifying specific areas for improvement and explaining the solution's real impact on its
intended client or target audience, rather than a general statement that the project "worked well."

**Worked example:** Rather than concluding "the prototype canteen food-waste solution was successful,"
top-band Criterion D evidence tests the solution against each specific point in the original
specification (e.g. did it reduce measured food waste by a target amount over a trial period?),
reports the actual test results honestly even where they fall short of the specification, and
identifies specific, actionable improvements based on that evidence.

## Why the four criteria mirror the design cycle's own stages

Because each criterion maps directly onto one design-cycle stage -- inquiring and analysing (the
problem stage), developing ideas (the specification stage), creating the solution (the build stage),
evaluating (the test stage) -- a project that moves through all four stages in sequence has a natural
structure for generating evidence for all four criteria, provided each stage is documented with the
depth described above rather than treated as a box to tick before moving to the next.

## A note on the ePortfolio

Because MYP Design's eAssessment is submitted as an ePortfolio rather than a timed written paper, the
depth described above for each criterion is not optional supporting detail -- it is the actual
evidence a moderator sees and marks. A finished prototype photograph alone, without the accompanying
inquiry, specification, planning and testing documentation described in each section above, gives a
moderator no basis to award marks against Criteria A, B or D at all, regardless of how well the
prototype itself performs.

## Self-test

1. What must top-band Criterion A evidence include beyond identifying a problem?
2. Why is generating only one design idea insufficient for top-band Criterion B evidence?
3. What must a top-band Criterion C log record beyond the finished prototype?
4. Why does concluding "the project worked well" fail to demonstrate top-band Criterion D evidence?
5. Which design-cycle stage does each of the four criteria correspond to?

**Answers:** 1. A genuine analysis of the need for a solution, grounded in evidence about a real
target audience or client, plus research into existing similar solutions, before writing the design
brief. 2. Because top-band evidence requires generating and evaluating a range of feasible ideas
against the specification, and justifying why the chosen idea was selected over specific alternatives,
which is impossible to demonstrate with only one idea presented. 3. A record of the sequence followed
to create the prototype, including any changes made to the original plan and the reasoning behind
them. 4. Because top-band evaluation requires testing the solution against each specific point in the
original specification individually and reporting honest results, not a general, unsupported
statement of success. 5. Criterion A -- inquiring and analysing (the problem stage); Criterion B --
developing ideas (the specification stage); Criterion C -- creating the solution (the build stage);
Criterion D -- evaluating (the test stage).
