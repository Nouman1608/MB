## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define the term *secondary data*. **[2]**

**2.** Identify **two** sampling techniques that do not require a sampling frame. **[2]**

**3.** Distinguish between a *research question* and a *hypothesis*. **[4]**

**4.** Explain what is meant by *operationalisation*, using an example. **[3]**

**5.** Explain **one** reason why official crime statistics may not give an accurate picture of crime. **[3]**

**6.** Explain **one** advantage of carrying out a pilot study. **[3]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** Explain **two** limitations of using questionnaires to study people's religious beliefs. **[6]**

**8.** Explain **two** ways a researcher could assess the usefulness of personal documents such as diaries. **[6]**

**9.** Evaluate the use of content analysis to study how women are represented in advertising. **[8]**

**10.** Evaluate the use of group interviews to study young people's attitudes to school. **[8]**

**11.** *"Qualitative methods always provide a truer picture of social life than quantitative methods."*

- **(a)** Explain the view expressed in the quotation. **[6]**
- **(b)** Using sociological material, give **two** arguments against the view expressed in the quotation. **[10]**

**12.** "Choosing the right sample is the most important stage of research design." Evaluate this view. **[26]**

## Answers

### 1. Secondary data [2]

Data that already exists, collected or produced by someone other than the researcher [1], for a different purpose, such as official statistics, diaries or newspaper articles [1]. **[2]**

### 2. Techniques without a sampling frame [2]

Any two, one mark each: quota sampling, snowball sampling, opportunity sampling, purposive sampling [1] [1]. **[2]**

### 3. Research question and hypothesis [4]

- A research question is an open question the study sets out to answer [1], for example "How do migrants experience their first year in a new country?" [1].
- A hypothesis is a testable prediction about the relationship between two or more variables [1], for example "Pupils in smaller classes achieve higher test scores than those in larger classes" [1]. **[4]**

### 4. Operationalisation [3]

Turning an abstract concept into something that can be observed or measured [1] by choosing indicators [1]. Example: operationalising "social class" by occupation, income and highest qualification, or "religiosity" by attendance, frequency of prayer and stated belief [1]. **[3]**

### 5. Official crime statistics [3]

One reason identified [1], explained [1] and developed [1]. For example: many crimes are never reported to the police [1], because victims think they are too trivial, fear retaliation or do not trust the police [1]; this "dark figure" means recorded crime underestimates the true amount, especially for crimes such as domestic violence [1]. Also credit police recording decisions, changes in counting rules, or targeting of certain groups and areas. **[3]**

### 6. Pilot study [3]

Advantage identified [1], explained [1] and linked to a research concept [1]. For example: it tests whether questions are understood as intended [1], so confusing or leading wording can be changed before the main study [1]; this improves the validity of the final data and saves time and money [1]. **[3]**

### 7. Questionnaires on religious belief [6]

Up to 3 marks per limitation, applied to religious belief.

- **Imposed categories:** closed questions force respondents into the researcher's definitions of "belief", which may not fit personal or non-institutional faith, reducing validity.
- **Social desirability:** respondents may overstate or understate belief depending on what is seen as acceptable in their community.
- **Lack of depth:** questionnaires record whether people believe but not what belief means to them or how it shapes daily life.
- **Response rate:** those with strong views may be more likely to reply, making the sample unrepresentative. **[6]**

### 8. Assessing personal documents [6]

Up to 3 marks per way, based on John Scott's (1990) criteria.

- **Authenticity:** checking that the diary is genuine and complete, for example by examining handwriting, dates and its history.
- **Credibility:** asking whether the author was sincere and accurate; a diary written for publication may be less frank.
- **Representativeness:** asking whether this document is typical; diaries survive more often from literate, wealthier people.
- **Meaning:** asking whether the researcher can understand the language and references as the author intended. **[6]**

### 9. Content analysis of advertising [8]

Up to 4 marks for strengths and up to 4 for limitations, applied.

**Strengths:**
- A coding frame (for example, counting women shown in domestic, work or decorative roles) allows systematic study of large numbers of adverts [1].
- The method can be repeated by other researchers with the same coding frame, giving high reliability [1].
- Adverts are publicly available, so it is cheap and raises few ethical problems [1].
- Results can be compared over time to measure change in representation [1].

**Limitations:**
- Coding decisions are subjective: deciding whether an image is "sexualised" depends on the researcher's judgement, reducing objectivity [1].
- It counts how often images appear but not what they mean to viewers, so it cannot show effects [1].
- The selection of adverts (which channels, magazines or websites) may be unrepresentative [1].
- It misses context such as tone, irony or the intended audience, limiting validity [1]. **[8]**

### 10. Group interviews on attitudes to school [8]

**Strengths:**
- Young people may feel more relaxed with friends than alone with an adult, so they speak more freely [1].
- Discussion reveals how attitudes are shaped in interaction with peers, which matters for school subcultures [1].
- Several people are interviewed at once, saving time [1].
- Participants can prompt and challenge each other, producing rich qualitative data [1].

**Limitations:**
- Dominant members may lead the discussion while quieter ones agree, so individual views are hidden [1].
- Peer pressure may cause exaggeration, for example of anti-school attitudes, reducing validity [1].
- Confidentiality cannot be guaranteed because other participants hear what is said [1].
- Groups are small and self-selecting, so findings are hard to generalise and hard to repeat exactly [1]. **[8]**

### 11(a). Explaining the view [6]

- The view reflects interpretivism, which holds that sociology must understand the meanings people give to their actions [1].
- Qualitative methods such as unstructured interviews and participant observation let people speak in their own words [1], so researchers avoid imposing their categories [1].
- Rapport and depth give a more valid picture of experience [1].
- Weber's idea of *verstehen*, empathetic understanding, requires this closeness [1].
- Quantitative methods turn experience into numbers, losing meaning and context [1]. **[6]**

### 11(b). Arguing against the view [10]

Up to 5 marks per argument.

- **Researcher bias and interpretation:** in qualitative work the researcher selects what to record and quote, and may go native, so the picture can be shaped by their values; this is less "true" than it appears.
- **Representativeness and generalisation:** small samples in qualitative studies cannot show how common a pattern is; large quantitative surveys can, which matters for questions about whole populations.
- **Reliability:** quantitative methods can be repeated and checked; qualitative findings are hard to verify, so claims to truth are harder to test.
- **Methodological pluralism:** many sociologists argue that the "truest" picture comes from triangulating both types of data.

Credit any two developed arguments. **[10]**

### 12. Essay: sampling in research design [26]

Marked by levels across AO1, AO2 and AO3.

**Indicative content for the view:**
- Representativeness determines whether findings can be generalised; a biased sample undermines everything that follows.
- Positivist research depends on good sampling frames and random or stratified samples.
- Examples of sampling problems: incomplete frames, low response rates, self-selection in online surveys.

**Indicative content against the view:**
- Operationalisation: poor indicators produce invalid data however good the sample.
- Choice of method and strategy: the method must fit the research question.
- Pilot studies prevent design faults.
- Ethics and access can decide whether research is possible at all.
- Interpretivist research often seeks depth over representativeness, so sampling matters less; snowball samples are acceptable for hidden groups.
- Interpreting results: correlation and causation, researcher bias.

**Evaluation:** importance depends on the aim (generalisation or understanding) and theoretical approach; research design is a chain in which weakness at any stage harms the whole.

**Level guidance:** top-level answers evaluate several stages with applied examples and reach a supported judgement; middle-level answers describe stages with limited evaluation; low-level answers list stages.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a sampling technique without saying why it suits the study.
- Generic method evaluation not applied to the topic in the question.
- Confusing reliability and validity.
- Treating qualitative and quantitative as a simple good-versus-bad contrast.
- Essays that describe all eight stages equally without arguing which matters most.
