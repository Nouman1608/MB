These questions cover section 1.1 (What is a business?) of the IB *Business management guide*, first assessment 2024, for SL and HL: the nature of business, the four sectors, entrepreneurship, and the challenges and opportunities of starting up. All businesses and figures are invented, and money is in dollars. Learn the content from the [1.1 study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-business-dp-business-management-ib-dp-business-1-1/) and the [1.1 revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-business-dp-business-management-ib-dp-business-1-1-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** Define the term *value added*. **[2]**

**2.** Outline the role of the operations function in a business. **[2]**

**3.** Identify the sector of each business activity.

**(a)** Drilling for natural gas **[1]**

**(b)** Designing microchips, with manufacturing done by another firm **[1]**

**(c)** Assembling bicycles from bought-in parts **[1]**

**(d)** Moving households' furniture to a new home **[1]**

**4.** Pressley Prints buys paper and ink costing 1.80 per poster and sells each poster for 12.00.

**(a)** Calculate the value added per poster. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the value added on an order of 500 posters. **[1]**

**5.** State two elements of entrepreneurship. **[2]**

**6.** Explain one way in which the marketing and operations functions of a business depend on each other. **[3]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**7.** A chocolate bar passes through three businesses. A cocoa farm sells the beans needed for one bar for 0.40, having bought inputs (fertiliser, sacks) costing 0.10. A chocolate factory buys those beans and other inputs costing 0.60, and sells the bar to a shop for 1.50. The shop has other bought-in costs of 0.20 per bar and sells it for 2.80.

**(a)** Calculate the value added at each of the three stages. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why the shop adds the most value, even though it does not change the product. **[2]**

**(c)** Comment on the share of the final price received by the cocoa farm. **[2]**

**8.** Explain two reasons why the share of employment in the tertiary sector may rise in an economy. **[4]**

**9.** Distinguish between an entrepreneur and a social entrepreneur. **[3]**

**10.** Halvard plans to open a bicycle repair workshop. Start-up costs are 28,000. He has savings of 12,000, a grant of 4,000 and a loan offer of 8,000. Monthly fixed costs will be 2,600. He expects monthly contribution of 1,800 for the first four months and 3,400 a month after that.

**(a)** Calculate the funding gap before opening. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the total cash shortfall over the first four months. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the month by the end of which the early shortfall is recovered. **[2]**

**(d)** State the total extra finance Halvard needs to arrange. **[1]**

**11.** Using the data in Question 10, evaluate whether Halvard should open the workshop now. **[8]**

**12.** Explain one opportunity and one challenge that a shift toward the quaternary sector could create for a traditional printing business. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** The difference between the price a business charges for its output [1] and the cost of the inputs it buys from other firms [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Operations turns inputs into outputs [1], making sure goods or services are produced efficiently, on time and to the required quality [1]. **[2]**

**3. (a)** Primary [1]

**(b)** Quaternary, because the output is knowledge (design) [1]

**(c)** Secondary [1]

**(d)** Tertiary [1]

*Examiner insight:* (b) is the trap. The firm is linked to manufacturing, but its own activity is design and research, so classify the activity, not the industry.

**4. (a)** 12.00 − 1.80 = **10.20** [1]

**(b)** 500 × 10.20 = **5,100** [1]

**5.** Any two, one mark each, from: spotting an opportunity; bearing risk; organizing resources; innovating [1][1]. **[2]**

**6.** Marketing forecasts and creates demand, for example through a promotion [1]. Operations must have the capacity, materials and staff to meet that demand [1]. If it cannot, customers are let down and the promotion damages the brand, so the two functions must plan together [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** Farm: 0.40 − 0.10 = **0.30** [1]. Factory: 1.50 − (0.40 + 0.60) = **0.50** [1]. Shop: 2.80 − (1.50 + 0.20) = **1.10** [1]

**(b)** Customers pay for the convenience of buying the bar where and when they want it [1], and for the shop's location, display and service, so the shop can charge well above its input costs [1]

**(c)** The farm's sale is 0.40/2.80 × 100 = 14.3% of the final price, and its value added only 10.7% [1]. This suggests most of the value is captured later in the chain, which may leave the farm with low incomes and little bargaining power [1]

*Examiner insight:* "comment" asks for a brief judgement based on the figures. A percentage alone is a calculation, not a comment.

**8.** Rising incomes [1]: as people become better off, they spend a larger share on services such as tourism, dining and health care, so service firms hire more staff [1]. Automation in manufacturing [1]: factories need fewer workers per unit, so jobs move toward services such as maintenance, logistics and retail [1]. (Other valid reasons, such as manufacturing moving to lower-cost countries, also earn credit.) **[4]**

**9.** Both identify opportunities, organize resources and bear risk [1]. An entrepreneur's main aim is usually profit for the owner [1], while a social entrepreneur's main aim is a social or environmental purpose, with any surplus mainly reinvested in that purpose [1]. **[3]**

**10. (a)** 28,000 − (12,000 + 4,000 + 8,000) = **4,000** [1]

**(b)** Monthly loss: 2,600 − 1,800 = 800 [1]. Over four months: 4 × 800 = **3,200** [1]

**(c)** From month 5, monthly gain: 3,400 − 2,600 = 800 [1]. 3,200 / 800 = 4 months, so recovered by the end of **month 8** [1]

**(d)** 4,000 + 3,200 = **7,200** [1]

**11.** Award up to 8 marks for a balanced, supported judgement. Credit points such as:

- The workshop recovers its early losses by the end of month 8, so the plan looks viable if forecasts are met [1].
- But Halvard needs 7,200 more, almost twice the 4,000 funding gap he can see, so opening without it risks running out of cash [1].
- The figures depend on contribution rising to 3,400 from month 5; a slower build-up would delay recovery and widen the gap [1].
- Opportunity: bicycle repair may have steady local demand and low competition, which supports the forecast [1].
- Opportunity: as a sole owner he keeps control and profit [1].
- Challenge: further borrowing increases fixed repayments, raising the monthly costs that must be covered [1].
- Waiting a year to save would cut borrowing but could let a competitor take the gap in the market [1].
- Judgement: open now only if the extra 7,200 is secured (for example a larger loan or a partner); otherwise delay, because the cash risk in the first months is the deciding factor [1]. **[8]**

*Examiner insight:* the strongest answers use the calculations from Question 10 as evidence on both sides and then decide. A list of general start-up challenges with no figures stays in the lower mark range.

**12.** Opportunity: firms in the growing quaternary sector need reports, data visualisations and specialist documents [1], so the printer could offer design and digital services to them and add more value [1]. Challenge: much information is now shared digitally instead of on paper [1], so demand for traditional printing may fall and the business may need to retrain staff or invest in new equipment [1]. **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Classifying a firm by its industry instead of its own activity.
- Treating value added as profit.
- Giving percentages or totals with no comment on what they mean.
- Defining an entrepreneur without the idea of risk.
- Forgetting that early cash shortfalls add to the funding gap.
- Ending an "evaluate" answer without a judgement and a condition.
