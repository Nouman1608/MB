## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Pathogen | Organism that causes disease: virus, bacterium, fungus or protist (not archaea) |
| Innate immunity | Fixed, broad response; phagocytes |
| Adaptive immunity | Specific response that builds memory; lymphocytes |
| Antigen | Recognition molecule, usually protein or glycoprotein on a pathogen surface |
| Antibody | Protein made by plasma cells that binds one antigen |
| Plasma cell | Activated B-cell clone member that secretes antibody |
| Memory cell | Long-lived lymphocyte that gives immunity |
| Immunity | Being able to clear an infectious disease from the body, thanks to memory cells |
| Zoonosis | Infectious disease that transfers from other species to humans |
| Herd immunity | Transmission impeded because enough of the population is immune |

## First lines of defence (C3.2.1 to C3.2.3)

- Pathogens: viruses, bacteria, fungi, protists. Examples: cholera (bacterium), COVID-19 (virus), athlete's foot (fungus), malaria (protist).
- Nature of science: Semmelweis (childbed fever, Vienna) and Snow (cholera, London) used careful observation to control disease.
- Skin: physical barrier (tough, keratinised dead cells) and chemical barrier (acidic, antimicrobial secretions).
- Mucous membranes: sticky mucus traps microorganisms; cilia move it along; enzymes in secretions attack bacteria.
- Clotting steps in order:

1. Platelets release clotting factors.
2. A cascade of reactions follows.
3. Thrombin forms.
4. Thrombin converts soluble fibrinogen to insoluble fibrin.
5. A fibrin mesh traps erythrocytes, making the clot.

## Innate versus adaptive (C3.2.4, C3.2.5)

| | Innate | Adaptive |
|---|---|---|
| Specificity | Broad categories | One pathogen |
| Changes over life | No | Yes |
| Memory | No | Yes |
| Cells | Phagocytes | B-cells and T-cells |

Method in steps: phagocytosis

1. Amoeboid movement from blood to infected tissue.
2. Recognise pathogen as foreign.
3. Engulf by endocytosis into a vesicle.
4. Lysosomes fuse and enzymes digest the pathogen.

## Adaptive response (C3.2.6 to C3.2.10)

- Lymphocytes circulate in blood and are held in lymph and lymph nodes.
- Huge number of different B-cells, each making one antibody type.
- Antigens on erythrocytes can cause antibody production after transfusion of a different blood group.

Method in steps: clonal selection

1. B-cell binds its specific antigen.
2. A helper T-cell activated by the same antigen contacts it.
3. Activated B-cell divides by mitosis (few B-cells match, so numbers must rise).
4. Clone becomes plasma cells (antibody) and memory cells.
5. Memory cells give faster, larger response on re-exposure.

Worked reminder: 1 cell, division every 6 hours, 96 hours = 16 divisions = 2¹⁶ = 65 536 cells.

## HIV and AIDS (C3.2.11, C3.2.12)

- Transmitted in body fluids: blood, semen, vaginal fluids, breast milk.
- Routes: unprotected sex, shared needles, mother to child, unscreened transfusion.
- Infects and kills helper T-cells only (the lymphocytes affected).
- Fewer helper T-cells means fewer activated B-cells, less antibody and opportunistic infections.
- AIDS is the later condition caused by the loss of immune function.

## Antibiotics and resistance (C3.2.13, C3.2.14)

| Antibiotic target | Why it spares human cells |
|---|---|
| Cell wall synthesis | Animal cells have no cell wall |
| Bacterial ribosomes | Differ from eukaryotic ribosomes |

- Viruses: no metabolism or ribosomes of their own, live inside host cells, so no target.
- Resistance: random mutation, antibiotic as selection pressure, survivors reproduce, allele frequency rises.
- Slowing it: use only when needed, finish the course, limit routine use in animals.
- Worked reminder: 10 resistant cells, 30-minute generations, 6 hours = 12 doublings = 10 × 2¹² = 40 960.

## Zoonoses (C3.2.15)

| Disease | Pathogen | Route |
|---|---|---|
| Tuberculosis | *Mycobacterium bovis* (some cases) | Milk or contact with infected cattle |
| Rabies | Virus | Bite, saliva |
| Japanese encephalitis | Virus | Mosquito bite |
| COVID-19 | SARS-CoV-2 | Animal origin, then spread between humans |

## Vaccines and herd immunity (C3.2.16, C3.2.17)

- Vaccine = antigens, or DNA or RNA coding for antigens.
- Stimulates memory cells without causing disease.
- Herd immunity: with enough immune people, transmission is greatly impeded and the unvaccinated gain protection.
- Nature of science: media report before evaluation ends; risk is minimal, not zero.

## Percentage calculations (C3.2.18)

| Calculation | Formula |
|---|---|
| Percentage change | (new − original) ÷ original × 100 |
| Percentage difference | difference ÷ mean of the two × 100 |

Worked reminder: admissions 84 and 12 per 100 000. Difference 72, mean 48, so percentage difference 150%. Percentage change from 84 to 12 is −85.7%.

## Primary and secondary responses

Graphs of antibody concentration against time are common. Read them in this order.

| Feature | First exposure | Second exposure |
|---|---|---|
| Lag before antibody appears | Longer | Shorter |
| Peak concentration | Lower | Higher |
| Time antibody stays high | Shorter | Longer |
| Reason | Few matching B-cells, clone must be built | Memory cells already present |

Worked reminder: peaks of 40 and 640 arbitrary units. Percentage change = (640 − 40) ÷ 40 × 100 = 1500%, or 16 times higher.

Link to the vaccine idea: a vaccine gives the first exposure safely, and a later booster or real infection gives the second.

## Phagocytes and lymphocytes side by side

| Cell | System | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Phagocyte | Innate | Moves to infection, engulfs and digests pathogens |
| B-cell | Adaptive | Binds specific antigen, divides, makes plasma and memory cells |
| Plasma cell | Adaptive | Secretes antibody |
| Helper T-cell | Adaptive | Activated by antigen, then activates B-cells |
| Memory cell | Adaptive | Long-lived, gives faster response next time |

Blood group link: erythrocyte surface antigens differ between groups. Transfused blood with the wrong antigens can stimulate antibody production, which is why donor and recipient are matched.

## Evaluating data (C3.2.18 and the nature of science points)

Use this checklist when a question asks you to evaluate COVID-19 data or a claim about a vaccine.

1. Is the measure a raw count or a rate per head of population? Rates allow fair comparison between places of different size.
2. How large is the sample? Small samples give less reliable conclusions.
3. Were the compared groups similar apart from the factor tested?
4. Was data collected in the same way over the whole period, for example the same testing policy?
5. Does a correlation show a cause? Several factors often change together.
6. Has the research been published and evaluated, or is it a media report of early findings?
7. Is the risk stated honestly? Side effects of vaccines are minimal but not nil.

Worked reminder: if a vaccinated group shows 15 admissions per 100 000 and an unvaccinated group 60, the percentage difference is 45 ÷ 37.5 × 100 = 120%, while the percentage change from unvaccinated to vaccinated is −75%. State which one you calculated.

## Writing good answers

- "Explain" needs a reason as well as a statement. For antibiotics: block a bacterial process, and that process is missing from human cells.
- "Distinguish" needs both sides stated, preferably in the same sentence.
- "Outline" is brief, so give the main steps in order. Clotting is a good example.
- "Evaluate" needs strengths and limitations, then a judgement.
- In calculations, show the formula and the substitution, give the unit or percent sign, and keep sensible significant figures.

Common pairs that get swapped: lysosome and ribosome (digestion versus protein synthesis), endocytosis and exocytosis (phagocytes take pathogens in; plasma cells secrete antibody out), mitosis and meiosis (clonal expansion uses mitosis).

## Must-know distinctions

- HIV (virus) is not AIDS (condition).
- Antigen (on pathogen) is not antibody (made by plasma cell).
- Plasma cell secretes antibody; memory cell waits.
- Antibiotics act on bacteria; vaccines prepare the immune system.
- Percentage change needs a starting value; percentage difference does not.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the four groups usually called pathogens.
2. Which protein is formed from fibrinogen in a clot?
3. State two differences between innate and adaptive immunity.
4. Which process do phagocytes use to take in a pathogen?
5. Why must an activated B-cell divide many times?
6. Which cells does HIV infect?
7. Why does penicillin not harm human cells?
8. Why are antibiotics useless against influenza?
9. Name one zoonosis spread by mosquitoes.
10. Cases fall from 800 to 200. Calculate the percentage change.
11. Values of 30 and 50 are compared. Calculate the percentage difference.

### Answers

1. Viruses, bacteria, fungi and protists.
2. Fibrin (formed by thrombin).
3. Innate: broad, unchanging, no memory. Adaptive: specific, improves, memory.
4. Endocytosis.
5. Few B-cells match the antigen, so cloning is needed for enough plasma cells.
6. Helper T-cells.
7. Human cells have no cell wall.
8. Viruses lack bacterial targets and use host machinery.
9. Japanese encephalitis.
10. (200 − 800) ÷ 800 × 100 = −75%.
11. 20 ÷ 40 × 100 = 50%.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "antibodies" in a description of a vaccine.
- Leaving out helper T-cells in B-cell activation.
- Describing phagocytes as specific.
- Saying that bacteria "build immunity" to antibiotics.
- Giving casual contact as a route of HIV transmission.
- Calculating percentage change with the wrong starting value.
- Quoting raw counts when a rate per 100 000 is needed for fair comparison.
- Describing herd immunity without saying who is protected.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between innate and adaptive immunity?**
Innate immunity gives a broad response to categories of pathogen, does not change during life and has no memory; phagocytes are its cells. Adaptive immunity is specific to one pathogen, improves after exposure and builds memory using B-cells and T-cells.

**How does a B-cell become activated?**
A B-cell must bind its specific antigen and be contacted by a helper T-cell activated by the same antigen. It then divides by mitosis to form a clone of plasma cells, which secrete antibody, and memory cells.

**Why do antibiotics not work against viruses?**
Antibiotics block processes specific to bacteria, such as cell wall synthesis or bacterial ribosomes. Viruses have no metabolism or ribosomes of their own and use the host cell's machinery, so there is nothing for the antibiotic to target.

**What is herd immunity?**
Herd immunity is when enough of a population is immune that transmission of a pathogen is greatly impeded, so unvaccinated people also gain protection.

**What is a zoonosis?**
A zoonosis is an infectious disease that transfers from other species to humans; examples in IB DP Biology C3.2 include tuberculosis, rabies, Japanese encephalitis and COVID-19.
