## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by an external quality standard. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between a British Standard (BS) and an International Standard (ISO). **[2]**

**3.** Explain **one** benefit to a toy manufacturer of meeting an external safety standard. **[2]**

**4.** Explain what is meant by benchmarking. **[2]**

**5.** Which **one** of the following is an example of benchmarking?

A: A business setting its own quality targets without outside data.

B: A bakery comparing its waste rate and delivery times with the best bakery in the industry.

C: Inspecting a sample of goods at the end of production.

D: Paying a fine for selling a faulty product.

**[1]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Rowan Foods supplies sauces to supermarkets. One supermarket will buy £90,000 of sauces a year, with a 10% profit margin to Rowan, but only if Rowan gains an international food-safety certificate. Certification costs £9,000 to set up and £2,500 a year in audit fees.

**(a)** Calculate the annual profit from the contract after audit fees. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the payback period on the set-up costs, to one decimal place. **[2]**

**(c)** Evaluate whether Rowan Foods should seek certification. **[8]**

**7.** Rowan Foods' cost per bottle of sauce is £5.60. The best firm in the industry has a cost per bottle of £4.90. Rowan makes 80,000 bottles a year.

**(a)** Calculate the gap as a percentage of the benchmark cost, to one decimal place. **[2]**

**(b)** Rowan sets a target of closing half of the gap. Calculate the annual saving if the target is met. **[3]**

**(c)** Evaluate the usefulness of benchmarking to Rowan Foods. **[8]**

**8.** Hendrick Toys sells children's furniture in the UK and wants to begin selling in several European countries. Evaluate the importance of meeting external quality standards to Hendrick Toys and its stakeholders. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** An external quality standard is a set of requirements for a product, service or process [1] that is set by an independent organisation outside the business, and which the business can choose or be required to meet [1]. **[2]**

**2.** A British Standard is issued by the UK national standards body for use in the UK [1], whereas an International Standard is agreed by an international body for use across many countries [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Parents and shops can trust that the toys have been independently tested for safety [1], which builds reputation, increases sales and reduces the risk of accidents, claims and recalls [1]. Accept: some retailers will not stock toys without the mark. **[2]**

**4.** Benchmarking is comparing a business's performance with that of the best performer in the industry, or another organisation [1], in order to identify gaps and find ways to improve [1]. **[2]**

**5.** **B** [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** Profit = 10% × £90,000 = £9,000 [1]. Audit fees = £2,500 [1]. Net = **£6,500** [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Payback = £9,000 ÷ £6,500 [1] = **1.4 years** [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** Financial: the payback of 1.4 years is short, so certification pays for itself quickly if the contract lasts [1]. The contract is worth £6,500 a year after audit fees [1].
Other benefits: other supermarkets may also demand certification, so the certificate could open more markets [1]; the written procedures can reduce errors and the risk of a recall [1].
Costs and risks: staff time for training and audits is not in the figures [1]; if the supermarket cancels the contract, the business is left with annual fees [1]. A certificate is also a minimum standard, which does not guarantee good products [1].
Judgement: Rowan should seek certification because it is a condition of a profitable contract and may lead to others; it should check that the supermarket will give a contract of at least two years before committing [1]. **[8]**

**7. (a)** Gap = £5.60 − £4.90 = £0.70 [1]. As a percentage of the benchmark = 0.70 ÷ 4.90 × 100 = **14.3%** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Half the gap = £0.35 [1]. Annual saving = £0.35 × 80,000 [1] = **£28,000** [1]. **[3]**

**(c)** Benefits: the gap of 14.3% shows Rowan that its costs are high compared with the best, and the target is based on evidence rather than guesswork [1]. By studying the benchmark firm, Rowan can learn which methods give lower costs, such as purchasing, waste control or machinery [1]. The saving of £28,000 for closing half of the gap is a large one [1].
Limitations: the best firm may be larger and have economies of scale which Rowan cannot match [1], and its data may be difficult to obtain or measured in a different way [1]. Meeting the benchmark only brings Rowan level with its rival and does not create an advantage [1]. It uses management time [1].
Judgement: benchmarking is useful as a starting point, because it shows which costs are out of line, but Rowan should use it alongside its own cost analysis and customer research [1]. **[8]**

**8.** Importance to the business: parents and retailers in Europe will expect furniture to meet European safety standards, and in many cases the law requires it [1], so without compliance Hendrick may not be able to sell there [1]. Meeting the standards also gives a marketing message that builds trust in a product used by children [1].
Costs: testing, certification fees and possible redesign of products add costs and delay launch [1]. Small firms find these costs a larger share of income.
Stakeholders: customers (children and parents) benefit from safer products [1]; employees may need training and extra checks [1]; owners gain access to larger markets and reduce the risk of recalls and claims, but bear the cost [1]; European retailers can stock the product with confidence.
Evaluation: the importance is high because of the safety risk involved and because access to the market depends on it, whatever the cost [1]. Judgement: Hendrick should treat compliance as a condition of entering each market and include the cost in its pricing, as the risk to children and to its reputation outweighs the cost [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Trying to name individual standards by number, instead of explaining what they do.
- Saying that a certificate guarantees quality.
- Forgetting audit fees and staff time.
- Describing benchmarking without saying what is compared and with whom.
- Not giving a judgement.
