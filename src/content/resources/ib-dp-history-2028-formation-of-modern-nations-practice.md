**HL only.** These questions practise the Paper 3 regional study on the formation of modern nations in the Americas, 1860 to 1929. Choose one country and answer from its evidence. The answers mainly use the USA. Revise first with the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-formation-of-modern-nations/) and the [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-formation-of-modern-nations-revision-notes/).

## Questions

**1.** Explain two ways in which railway construction changed one country in the Americas. **[4]**

**2.** Read the extract (constructed for practice).

> "The man who dies rich dies disgraced. Wealth must be used for the good of the community, and the rich man is only a trustee for the poor." (Constructed for practice: a paraphrase of the argument of an industrialist's essay of about 1889.)

**(a)** Identify one belief about wealth in the extract. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain how this view could be used both to defend and to criticise inequality. **[3]**

**3.** Distinguish between Social Darwinism and Progressivism. **[3]**

**4.** Analyse the effects of European and Asian immigration on one country. **[6]**

**5.** Explain how the Dawes Act (1887) affected Native Americans, or another policy affected Indigenous people in one country. **[6]**

**6.** Examine the role of women in the expansion of democracy in one country, up to 1929. **[8]**

**7.** Discuss the extent to which labour movements improved conditions for workers in one country. **[8]**

**8.** To what extent did economic dependency limit the development of one country in Latin America? **[8]**

**9.** Paper 3 style essay question: "Progressive reforms transformed the political and social life of the nation." To what extent do you agree, with reference to one country? **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Two developed points, 2 marks each. Market integration: the transcontinental railroad (1869) and later lines linked farms and cities, creating national markets and allowing Midwestern grain and cattle to reach the East [1], which supported urban growth and specialisation [1]. Economic stimulus: railroads created demand for steel, coal and capital, driving industrial growth [1], and led to new forms of business organisation [1]. Settlement and displacement: lines opened the West to settlers [1] and displaced Native peoples and the buffalo [1]. In Canada, the Canadian Pacific Railway bound the new country together [2]. **[4]**

**2. (a)** Wealth carries a duty to the community; the rich should give [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Defence: the rich could argue that inequality was acceptable as long as wealthy people gave generously through philanthropy [1]. Criticism: the same statement suggests that those who keep large fortunes are failing a moral duty, and it could be used by reformers, as well as exposing that wealth might be gained at workers' expense [1]. Either use depends on who is speaking and whether the giving matched the wealth [1]. **[3]**

**3.** Social Darwinism applied ideas of competition and "survival of the fittest" to society [1], and was used to defend inequality, laissez-faire and sometimes racial hierarchy [1]. Progressivism argued that industrial society needed active reform and regulation to correct abuses and improve conditions [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Choose a country, for example the USA. Economic: immigrants supplied labour for mills, mines and railroads and helped industrial growth [1]. Urban: they swelled cities such as New York and Chicago, creating ethnic neighbourhoods and overcrowding [1]. Cultural and religious: more Catholics, Jews and Orthodox Christians changed religious life [1]. Political: city machines sought immigrant votes [1]. Reaction: nativism, the Chinese Exclusion Act (1882) and the 1924 quota law [1]. Judgement on the overall impact: positive for growth, but with conflict and exclusion [1]. **[6]**

**5.** The Dawes Act divided reservation land into individual allotments [1], with surplus land sold to white settlers [1]. It aimed to assimilate Native Americans as farmers and weaken tribal ownership [1]. Effect: Native-held land fell sharply, from about 138 million acres in 1887 to about 48 million by 1934 [1]. Many allottees lost land through sale, debt or fraud [1]. It damaged tribal structures and left poverty [1]. (Alternative: Argentina's Conquest of the Desert, or Canadian residential schools, with equivalent marks.) **[6]**

**6.** Women's suffrage movements: campaigning by organisations such as the National American Woman Suffrage Association and more militant groups [2]; the 19th Amendment in 1920 [1]; state-level gains in the West earlier [1]. Contribution of wartime work and Progressive reform to arguments [1]. Limits: many Black women were still excluded by southern restrictions, and Indigenous women were not all citizens until 1924 [1]. Comparison with other countries, for example Argentina in 1947 [1]. Judgement on the role of women [1]. **[8]**

**7.** Gains: the American Federation of Labor won recognition and better pay for skilled workers [2]; Progressive-era laws on child labour and workplace safety [1]; some shorter hours [1]. Limits: employers and the state often broke strikes, as at Homestead (1892) and Pullman (1894) [2]; workers outside craft unions had less protection [1]. Judgement: improvement was partial and uneven [1]. **[8]**

**8.** Choose a country, for example Argentina. Dependency: reliance on beef and wheat exports to Britain [1], British ownership of railways and finance [1], vulnerability to price falls [1]. Evidence of growth: the export boom raised incomes, attracted immigrants and built cities [2]. Perspectives: dependency theorists argue that structure limited autonomy; others stress local choices such as limited industrial policy and land concentration [2]. Judgement [1]. **[8]**

**9.** Use the Paper 3 markbands. A top answer (13 to 15 marks) shows in-depth understanding of the argument, evaluates a range of perspectives and reaches a substantiated judgement. Evidence for transformation (about 4 marks): 16th to 19th Amendments, trust-busting under Roosevelt, the Federal Reserve, food and drug laws, women's suffrage. Limits (about 4 marks): segregation and Black disenfranchisement continued, immigration restriction, labour repression and the Red Scare. Perspectives (about 3 marks): Progressivism as democratic reform versus a movement of middle-class interests; its relationship to nativism and Prohibition. Judgement (about 4 marks): for example, that reforms changed the machinery of government and regulation substantially but left deep inequalities. **[15]**
