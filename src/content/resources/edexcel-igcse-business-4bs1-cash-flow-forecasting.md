## Why cash matters: three chains

1. **Paying suppliers.** If a business cannot pay for materials on time, suppliers stop delivering or demand cash in advance, so production stops and orders are lost.
2. **Paying overheads and employees.** Rent, electricity and wages fall due on fixed dates. Unpaid staff leave; an unpaid landlord can end the lease.
3. **Preventing insolvency.** A business is insolvent when it cannot pay its debts as they fall due. It may then be forced to close, even if its products sell well.

The point examiners reward is that **a business fails from running out of cash, not from lack of profit alone**. A cash-flow forecast is how it sees a shortage coming.

## The structure of a forecast

| Line | How it is found |
|---|---|
| Cash inflows | All money expected in: cash sales, payments from credit customers, loans received, owner's capital, sale of assets |
| Total inflows | Sum of the inflow lines |
| Cash outflows | All money expected out: materials, wages, rent, utilities, equipment, loan repayments, interest |
| Total outflows | Sum of the outflow lines |
| Net cash flow | Total inflows − total outflows |
| Opening balance | Last month's closing balance |
| Closing balance | Opening balance + net cash flow |

Two facts make most questions straightforward: the opening balance of each month **is** the previous closing balance, and a negative number in brackets, such as (3,500), means the business is overdrawn.

## Worked example 1: sales on credit

Juniper Events hires out marquees. Customers pay **half in the month of the event and half the following month**. March sales were 6,000. Forecast sales are April 8,000, May 12,000, June 16,000 and July 10,000. Supplies cost 40% of each month's sales and are paid in the same month. Wages are 3,000 and rent 1,500 every month. New equipment costing 6,000 is bought in May. The opening balance in April is 2,500.

**Receipts each month** = half this month's sales + half last month's:

- April: 4,000 + 3,000 = 7,000
- May: 6,000 + 4,000 = 10,000
- June: 8,000 + 6,000 = 14,000
- July: 5,000 + 8,000 = 13,000

| | April | May | June | July |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Receipts from customers | 7,000 | 10,000 | 14,000 | 13,000 |
| **Total inflows** | 7,000 | 10,000 | 14,000 | 13,000 |
| Supplies | 3,200 | 4,800 | 6,400 | 4,000 |
| Wages | 3,000 | 3,000 | 3,000 | 3,000 |
| Rent | 1,500 | 1,500 | 1,500 | 1,500 |
| Equipment | 0 | 6,000 | 0 | 0 |
| **Total outflows** | 7,700 | 15,300 | 10,900 | 8,500 |
| **Net cash flow** | (700) | (5,300) | 3,100 | 4,500 |
| Opening balance | 2,500 | 1,800 | (3,500) | (400) |
| **Closing balance** | 1,800 | (3,500) | (400) | 4,100 |

Reading it:

- Sales rise strongly from April to June, yet cash falls. Growth is **using** cash, because supplies are paid now while half of each month's sales arrive a month later.
- The lowest point is (3,500) at the end of May. That figure tells the owner how large an overdraft is needed: at least 3,500, plus a margin for safety.
- The 6,000 equipment purchase falls in the same month as rising supply costs. Delaying it until July would keep every closing balance positive: May's closing balance would be 2,500 and June's 5,600 (before July's 6,000 outflow).

## Worked example 2: completing a table

The command word "complete the table" asks you to work out missing values. Rearrange the two rules:

- Net cash flow = inflows − outflows, so outflows = inflows − net cash flow, and inflows = outflows + net cash flow.
- Closing = opening + net, so opening = closing − net.

| | Month 1 | Month 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Total inflows | 9,400 | **?** |
| Total outflows | **?** | 8,600 |
| Net cash flow | (1,150) | 950 |
| Opening balance | 2,300 | **?** |
| Closing balance | **?** | **?** |

- Month 1 outflows: 9,400 − (−1,150) = **10,550**.
- Month 1 closing: 2,300 − 1,150 = **1,150**.
- Month 2 opening: equals Month 1 closing = **1,150**.
- Month 2 inflows: 8,600 + 950 = **9,550**.
- Month 2 closing: 1,150 + 950 = **2,100**.

Check each answer by putting it back into the rule. Most errors come from subtracting a negative number wrongly.

## Worked example 3: cash and profit for the same business

Over April to July, Juniper Events earns sales of 46,000. Its costs for the period are supplies 18,400, wages 12,000 and rent 6,000, a total of 36,400. Profit is 46,000 − 36,400 = **9,600**. Its total net cash flow over the same four months is −700 − 5,300 + 3,100 + 4,500 = **1,600**.

Why are they 8,000 apart?

- The equipment cost 6,000 in cash, but it will be used for years, so its whole cost is not counted against these four months' profit.
- Half of July's sales, 5,000, will be received in August, so it is in profit but not yet in cash.
- 3,000 from March sales arrived in April. It is cash in this period but belongs to March's profit.

6,000 + 5,000 − 3,000 = 8,000, which is the gap. This is the specification's "difference between cash and profit" made concrete: timing of receipts and payments, and spending on long-lasting assets.

## Interpreting a forecast

A good interpretation answer does four things.

1. **Identify** the problem months: negative closing balances, or a large fall in net cash flow.
2. **Explain the cause** from the table: a large one-off payment, slow-paying customers, seasonal sales.
3. **Suggest a response that matches the cause**: an overdraft for a short gap; delaying a purchase; asking customers to pay sooner; asking suppliers for longer credit; a loan if the shortage lasts.
4. **Comment on reliability**: the forecast rests on assumptions about sales, costs and when customers pay. If any are wrong, the real figures will differ.

### Annotated model answer

*Analyse one way Juniper Events could avoid a negative closing balance in May.*

> Juniper could delay buying the 6,000 of equipment until July. May's outflows would fall from 15,300 to 9,300, so net cash flow would be +700 and the closing balance 2,500 instead of (3,500). By July the business has a positive net cash flow of 4,500, so it can afford the purchase then. The drawback is that without the new equipment Juniper may have to turn away some June events, which are its busiest month, so it would lose revenue.

Why it works: one specific action, recalculated with figures, a chain to the effect and a drawback in context.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate** and **complete the table** questions: show the formula you are using and keep brackets for negatives consistent.
- **Explain** why cash matters: statement plus two expansion points, for example "without cash a business cannot pay wages, so staff may leave, so output falls".
- **Analyse** and **evaluate** questions on forecasts: use the months and figures; a general list of ways to improve cash flow scores poorly.
- Never add the opening balance into total inflows.

## Common mistakes

- Treating sales as receipts when customers pay later.
- Using the wrong month's closing balance as the next opening balance.
- Saying a profitable business cannot have cash problems.
- Recommending a long-term loan for a one-month shortage.
- Forgetting that a forecast is a prediction, not a record.
