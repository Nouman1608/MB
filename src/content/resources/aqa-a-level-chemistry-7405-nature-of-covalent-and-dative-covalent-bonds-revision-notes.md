These notes condense section **3.1.3.2 Nature of covalent and dative covalent bonds** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Papers 1 and 2 (and Paper 3). The [Covalent and dative covalent bonds study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-nature-of-covalent-and-dative-covalent-bonds/) explains each idea with worked examples, and the [practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-nature-of-covalent-and-dative-covalent-bonds-practice/) give exam-style questions.

## What the specification asks

- A single covalent bond contains a **shared pair of electrons**.
- **Multiple bonds** contain multiple pairs of electrons.
- A **co-ordinate (dative covalent) bond** contains a shared pair with **both electrons supplied by one atom**.
- **Represent** a covalent bond using a **line** and a co-ordinate bond using an **arrow**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Covalent bond | A shared pair of electrons between two atoms |
| Single / double / triple bond | One / two / three shared pairs |
| Lone pair | A pair of outer electrons on one atom not used in bonding |
| Dative covalent (co-ordinate) bond | A shared pair where both electrons came from one atom |
| Donor | The atom that supplies the pair (needs a lone pair) |
| Acceptor | The atom or ion that receives the pair (needs a vacancy or empty orbital) |
| Displayed formula | Shows every atom and every bond, with lines for bonds |

## Drawing conventions

| Feature | Representation |
|---|---|
| Single covalent bond | One line, H–H |
| Double bond | Two lines, O=O |
| Triple bond | Three lines, N≡N |
| Dative bond | Arrow from donor to acceptor, N → H |
| Lone pair | Two dots (or a short line) on the atom |
| Ion | Square brackets with the charge at the top right |

## Dot-and-cross method

1. Add the outer electrons of all atoms.
2. Add one for each negative charge; subtract one for each positive charge.
3. Share pairs to complete each outer shell (eight, or two for hydrogen).
4. Put remaining electrons on atoms as lone pairs.
5. Check that the total matches step 2.

## Common examples

| Species | Electrons counted | Bonds |
|---|---|---|
| N₂ | 10 | N≡N, one lone pair on each N |
| CO₂ | 16 | O=C=O, two lone pairs on each O |
| HCN | 10 | H–C≡N, one lone pair on N |
| C₂H₄ | 12 | C=C with four C–H bonds |
| NH₃ | 8 | Three N–H, one lone pair on N |

## Dative bonds you must recognise

| Species | How the dative bond forms |
|---|---|
| NH₄⁺ | The lone pair on N in NH₃ is donated to H⁺ |
| H₃O⁺ | A lone pair on O in H₂O is donated to H⁺ |
| NH₃BF₃ | The lone pair on N is donated to the empty orbital on B in BF₃ |
| Al₂Cl₆ | A lone pair on Cl in one AlCl₃ is donated to Al in the other |
| CO | One of the three bonds is dative, from O to C |

Key points:

- The **donor** must have a lone pair.
- The **acceptor** must have a vacancy: H⁺ has an empty 1s orbital; boron and aluminium in BF₃ and AlCl₃ have only six electrons.
- After formation, a dative bond is **identical** to other covalent bonds with the same atoms. All four N–H bonds in NH₄⁺ are equal.
- The arrow goes **from the donor to the acceptor**.

## Bond order, length and strength

| Bond | Length / pm (approx.) | Mean bond enthalpy / kJ mol⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|
| C–C | 154 | 347 |
| C=C | 134 | 612 |
| C≡C | 120 | 838 |

More shared pairs gives a **shorter, stronger** bond. The double bond is stronger than a single bond but less than twice as strong.

## Worked reminders

**Draw NH₄⁺.** Nitrogen has 5 outer electrons and four hydrogens bring 4, minus 1 for the positive charge: 8 electrons, so four bonding pairs. Draw N with three lines to H and one arrow N → H, in square brackets with a charge of 1+.

**Define a dative bond.** "A shared pair of electrons in which both electrons come from the same atom." Use both phrases.

**Why can BF₃ accept a lone pair?** Boron has only six outer electrons in BF₃ (three bonding pairs), so it has an empty orbital that can take a lone pair.

**Explain why NH₃ can form a dative bond but CH₄ cannot.** NH₃ has a lone pair on nitrogen. CH₄ has no lone pair and carbon already has a full outer shell.

## Common mistakes

- An arrow for a covalent bond or a line for a dative bond.
- An arrow pointing from acceptor to donor.
- Leaving out lone pairs or the charge on an ion.
- Saying a dative bond is weaker than a normal covalent bond.
- Forgetting that the donor needs a lone pair.
- Defining a dative bond without saying both electrons come from one atom.

## Self-test

1. Define a covalent bond.
2. Define a dative covalent bond.
3. How many electrons are shared in a triple bond?
4. Draw the displayed formula of ethene, C₂H₄, and state how many shared pairs are between the carbon atoms.
5. How many outer electrons are there to place in a dot-and-cross diagram of the ion NH₄⁺?
6. Explain how the bond between NH₃ and H⁺ forms.
7. Explain why BF₃ can accept a lone pair while H₂O can donate one.
8. Which bond is longer, C=C or C–C? Which has the larger mean bond enthalpy?
9. How many lone pairs does the oxygen atom have in H₃O⁺?
10. State what is meant by an arrow in a displayed formula.
11. Draw the dot-and-cross structure of Cl₂ and say how many lone pairs there are in total.
12. Carbon monoxide has a triple bond between C and O. Explain how this is made up of two normal bonds and one dative bond.

### Answers

1. A shared pair of electrons between two atoms.
2. A shared pair of electrons in which both electrons are supplied by one atom.
3. Six (three pairs).
4. H₂C=CH₂ with each carbon bonded to two hydrogens; two shared pairs between the carbons.
5. Eight (5 + 4 − 1).
6. The lone pair on nitrogen is donated to the empty orbital of H⁺, giving a fourth N–H bond.
7. Oxygen in H₂O has lone pairs that it can donate. Boron in BF₃ has only six outer electrons, so it has an empty orbital that can accept a lone pair.
8. C–C is longer; C=C has the larger mean bond enthalpy.
9. One.
10. A dative bond, with the arrow pointing from the atom donating the pair to the atom accepting it.
11. One shared pair between the two chlorine atoms and three lone pairs on each atom, **six lone pairs** in total.
12. Carbon and oxygen each contribute one electron to two of the pairs, and oxygen supplies both electrons of the third pair, which is the dative bond.
