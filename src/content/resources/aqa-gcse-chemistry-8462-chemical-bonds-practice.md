These questions cover section **4.2.1.1 Chemical bonds** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) specification,
for teaching from September 2016 onwards (version 1.1). The content is examined on Paper 1 at Foundation and
Higher Tier. Use a periodic table to decide which elements are metals.

Learn the content first in the [chemical bonds study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-chemical-bonds/)

## Section A

**1.** Name the type of bonding in each substance.

**(a)** Potassium iodide, KI **[1]**
**(b)** Hydrogen fluoride, HF **[1]**
**(c)** Magnesium, Mg **[1]**
**(d)** An alloy of aluminium and magnesium **[1]**

**2.** Here are four elements: helium, hydrogen, sulfur, sodium. Identify the element that has no strong
bonds between its particles and give a reason. **[2]**

**3.** A student says sodium carbonate, Na₂CO₃, has covalent bonding "because carbon and oxygen are
non-metals". Explain why the student's reasoning is wrong. **[2]**

**4.** Match each description to the type of strong bond it describes.

**(a)** Electrons pass from one kind of atom to another, leaving particles with opposite charges. **[1]**
**(b)** Two atoms hold a pair of electrons between them. **[1]**
**(c)** Outer electrons are free to move through a regular arrangement of positive particles. **[1]**

**5.** **(a)** State what is meant by an electrostatic force of attraction. **[1]**
**(b)** Name the two kinds of particle that attract each other in metallic bonding. **[1]**

**6.** Choose from this list: Br₂, CaF₂, Cu, CO, Li₂S, Ar.

**(a)** Give the **two** substances with ionic bonding. **[1]**
**(b)** Give the **two** substances with covalent bonding. **[1]**
**(c)** Give the substance with metallic bonding. **[1]**
**(d)** Give the substance with no strong bonds between its atoms. **[1]**

## Section B

**7.** Potassium reacts with oxygen to form potassium oxide, K₂O. Explain how the particles in potassium
oxide are held together. **[4]**

**8.** Hydrogen exists as H₂ molecules.

**(a)** Explain how the two atoms in a hydrogen molecule are held together. **[3]**
**(b)** Hydrogen chloride, HCl, is described as a compound but hydrogen, H₂, is not. Explain why. **[1]**

**9.** Compare the bonding in solid sodium chloride with the bonding in sodium metal. **[6]**

**10.** Two students write about bonding.

**(a)** Student A writes: "Ionic bonding happens between two non-metals that share electrons." Identify
**two** errors in this statement. **[2]**
**(b)** Student B writes: "Metallic bonds must be weak, because the electrons are free to move away."
Explain why Student B is wrong. **[2]**

**11.** Element X is in Group 1, element Z is in Group 7 and element W is in Group 6. Z and W are
non-metals.

**(a)** Predict the type of bonding in the compound formed between X and Z. **[1]**
**(b)** Predict the type of bonding in the compound formed between Z and W. **[1]**
**(c)** Predict the type of bonding in element Z on its own. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain, in terms of electrons, how the compound in **(a)** forms its bonding. **[2]**

**12.** Brass is an alloy of copper and zinc.

**(a)** Name the type of bonding in brass and explain how its particles are held together. **[3]**
**(b)** Brass contains two different elements. Explain why its bonding is not ionic. **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** **Ionic** [1]
**(b)** **Covalent** [1]
**(c)** **Metallic** [1]
**(d)** **Metallic** [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (d) the two elements are both metals, so an alloy cannot be ionic; ionic bonding
needs a non-metal as well.

**2.** **Helium** [1]; it is a Group 0 (noble gas) element, so its atoms do not bond to each other and
exist as separate atoms [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Hydrogen and sulfur are non-metals with covalent bonds; sodium is metallic.

**3.** Sodium carbonate contains **sodium, a metal** [1]; a compound of a metal combined with non-metals is
classed as **ionic**, not covalent [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The mark depends on spotting the metal, so name sodium explicitly.

**4. (a)** **Ionic** [1]
**(b)** **Covalent** [1]
**(c)** **Metallic** [1]

**5. (a)** An attraction between **oppositely charged** particles [1].
**(b)** **Positive metal ions** and **delocalised electrons** [1] (both needed).

**6. (a)** **CaF₂ and Li₂S** [1]
**(b)** **Br₂ and CO** [1]
**(c)** **Cu** [1]
**(d)** **Ar** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Each mark in (a) and (b) needs both substances; Br₂ is an element but is still covalent.

**7.** Electrons are **transferred** from potassium atoms to oxygen atoms [1]. Potassium atoms become
**positive ions** (K⁺) [1] and oxygen atoms become **negative ions** (O²⁻) [1]. There is a strong
**electrostatic attraction** between the oppositely charged ions [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Potassium shares its electron with oxygen" scores nothing; ionic bonding is transfer.

**8. (a)** The two atoms **share a pair of electrons** [1], one electron from each atom [1]; the shared
pair is attracted to the **positive nuclei of both atoms**, which holds the atoms together [1].
**(b)** HCl contains **two different elements** chemically combined; H₂ contains only one element [1].

**9.** Indicative content, one mark per point, up to 6:
- Both are strong bonds based on electrostatic attraction [1].
- Both contain positive sodium ions (sodium metal as positive metal centres) [1].
- In sodium chloride, electrons are **transferred** from sodium to chlorine [1].
- Sodium chloride contains **negative chloride ions**; sodium metal has no negative ions [1].
- In sodium metal, the outer electrons are **delocalised** and free to move through the structure [1].
- In sodium chloride, the attraction is between **oppositely charged ions**; in sodium metal it is between
  positive ions and **delocalised electrons** [1].
- Both form giant structures with no separate molecules [1].
**[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Comparisons must cover both substances; describing only sodium chloride well caps the
answer at about half marks.

**10. (a)** Any two [1] [1]: ionic bonding needs a **metal** combined with a non-metal, not two non-metals;
ionic bonding involves the **transfer** of electrons, not sharing; two non-metals sharing electrons
describes **covalent** bonding. **[2]**
**(b)** Metallic bonds are **strong** [1]; the delocalised electrons stay within the structure and are
**strongly attracted to the positive metal ions**, which is what holds the structure together [1]. **[2]**

**11. (a)** **Ionic** [1]
**(b)** **Covalent** [1]
**(c)** **Covalent** [1]
**(d)** Atoms of X (a metal) **lose** an electron to atoms of Z [1], forming oppositely charged ions that
are held by electrostatic attraction [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Group 7 elements exist as molecules such as Cl₂, which is why (c) is covalent.

**12. (a)** **Metallic** [1]; the outer electrons of the copper and zinc atoms are **delocalised** [1];
there is strong electrostatic attraction between the **positive metal ions** and the **delocalised
electrons** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Both copper and zinc are **metals**; ionic bonding needs a metal combined with a **non-metal** [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Missing the metal in a long formula such as Na₂CO₃.
- Saying "share electrons" for covalent bonds without the word **pair** or without naming the nuclei.
- Describing metallic bonding with negative ions or "molecules of metal".
- Writing a comparison that only discusses one of the two substances.
- Classifying noble gases as covalent.
