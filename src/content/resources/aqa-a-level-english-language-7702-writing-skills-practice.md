These questions practise 3.2.4 Writing skills from AQA A-level English Language (7702): academic, analytical and
non-specialist writing. All quoted sentences and drafts are **original**, written for this page. Answers give model
responses and the marking points they meet.

---

## Section A

**1.** Rewrite this sentence in an academic register: *"Loads of people reckon slang is ruining English but that's
rubbish because it's always been around."* **[3]**

**2.** Add hedging to this overclaim, and explain why the hedged version is more academic: *"Women use more tag
questions than men."* **[2]**

**3.** Rewrite this summary sentence as analysis: *"Text A says that texting is bad for children's spelling."* **[3]**

**4.** Explain "prescriptivism" to an audience of 14-year-olds in no more than two sentences. **[3]**

**5.** A draft speech to a parents' evening begins: *"Accent prejudice is a form of linguistic discrimination
documented in sociolinguistic research."* Identify **three** conventions of a speech it lacks. **[3]**

**6.** Explain **two** problems with this paragraph for a parents' magazine, then rewrite it: *"Children's virtuous
errors evidence over-generalisation of morphological rules, supporting nativist accounts."* **[4]**

## Section B

**7.** Write an introduction for an academic essay responding to: *"Evaluate the view that Standard English should be
taught as the only correct form of English."* **[5]**

**8.** Write one paragraph for an essay evaluating the view that social media is the main cause of language change,
using the claim, evidence, explanation, evaluation pattern. **[4]**

**9.** Write a comparative analytical paragraph on these two original quotations. Text X: *"Our once-proud language
is drowning in a sea of 'likes' and 'innits'."* Text Y: *"'Like' is doing real work: it marks quotation, hesitation
and approximation."* **[5]**

**10.** Write the opening (about 100 words) of a blog post for adults on whether young people's use of "like" is a
problem. **[5]**

**11.** Write the ending (about 80 words) of a talk to new secondary school pupils about why no accent is better than
another. **[4]**

**12.** Plan a script for a local radio talk, aimed at older listeners, arguing that changes in English are nothing
new. Give the opening, four content points with examples, and the ending. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Model: *"It is often claimed that slang is damaging English; however, this view overlooks the fact that slang
has been a feature of the language throughout its history."* Formal lexis replaces colloquialisms ("loads",
"reckon", "rubbish") [1]; the claim is attributed impersonally ("It is often claimed") [1]; the counter-argument is
reasoned and signposted ("however... overlooks") rather than dismissive [1].

**2.** Model: *"Some studies have suggested that, in certain contexts, women may use more tag questions than men."* [1]
The hedges recognise that findings vary with sample and context and that later research questioned universal gender
differences, so the claim matches the evidence [1].

**3.** Model: *"Text A represents texting as a threat to literacy, framing non-standard spellings through the
evaluative noun 'damage' and positioning the reader as a worried parent."* Analytical verb ("represents") [1];
named feature with embedded quotation [1]; effect on the reader or link to discourse [1].

**4.** Model: *"Prescriptivism is the belief that there is one correct way to speak and write, and that everyone
should follow its rules. It's the voice that tells you never to start a sentence with 'and'."* Accurate definition
[1]; plain vocabulary [1]; a familiar example for the audience [1].

**5.** Any three, 1 mark each [1] [1] [1]: no greeting or direct address to the audience ("Good evening,
everyone"); no hook such as an anecdote or question; no inclusive pronouns or second-person address; jargon not
explained for listeners; no spoken signposting of what is coming.

**6.** Problems, 1 mark each: undefined technical terms ("virtuous errors", "morphological", "nativist") exclude a
non-specialist reader [1]; the dense nominalised academic syntax has no example to make the idea concrete [1].
Rewrite (2 marks), for example: *"When your toddler says 'I runned' or 'two mouses', they aren't copying you: you've never
said those words. They've worked out a rule, add '-ed' or '-s', and applied it everywhere. Some linguists see this
as evidence that children are born ready to find grammar."* Credit accuracy of the idea [1] and accessibility through
example [1].

**7.** Credit, 1 mark each: defines Standard English (the prestige dialect of education and public life) [1];
distinguishes "correct" (a value judgement) from "standard" (a social convention) [1]; acknowledges the case for
teaching it (access, employment, shared written form) [1]; signals the counter-argument (other dialects are
rule-governed; "only correct" stigmatises speakers) [1]; states a clear line, for example teaching Standard English
alongside respect for other varieties [1].

**8.** Model: *"Social media spreads new forms rapidly, but it rarely creates the processes behind them [claim]. The
verb 'to unfriend' combines an old prefix with a converted noun [evidence], processes English has used for
centuries [explanation]. Social media therefore seems to accelerate diffusion rather than cause change itself
[evaluation]."* One mark per element present and linked [1] [1] [1] [1].

**9.** Credit, 1 mark each: comparative structure handling both texts in one paragraph [1]; Text X's metaphor
"drowning in a sea" represents change as overwhelming disaster [1]; "once-proud" uses nostalgic modification to
imply decline [1]; Text Y uses the functional metaphor "doing real work" and a list of three functions to represent
"like" as purposeful [1]; the paragraph links each to a discourse (decline versus descriptive, functional
explanation) [1].

**10.** Credit, 1 mark each: an engaging hook (anecdote, question or overheard example) [1]; a personal blog voice
with direct address [1]; the issue made clear early [1]; accurate linguistic content introduced accessibly (for
example "like" as a quotative or a hedge) [1]; controlled, accurate writing [1]. Model opening: *"'And she was like,
no way, and I was like, way.' If that sentence makes you wince, you're not alone. But before we ban 'like' from the
dinner table, it's worth asking what it's actually doing. Listen closely and you'll hear it working hard: introducing
what someone said, softening a guess, buying a second to think. Your teenager may not be lazy. They may be
fluent."*

**11.** Credit, 1 mark each: direct address to the pupils [1]; a clear restatement of the key idea in accessible
terms [1]; a memorable technique (repetition, a triple or a return to an opening image) [1]; a call to action or
uplifting close [1]. Model: *"So the next time someone laughs at the way you say 'grass', remember this: every accent
in this hall follows its own rules. None is broken. None is better. Your accent carries your family, your street and
your story. Use it proudly, listen kindly to others, and judge people by what they say, not how they sound."*

**12.** Credit, 1 mark each: opening suited to radio and older listeners (for example a remembered phrase from their
youth) [1]; point on words changing meaning, such as "nice" or "awful" [1]; point on old complaints, such as
eighteenth-century calls to fix the language [1]; point on borrowing over centuries, such as "bungalow" or "café"
[1]; point on technology and words from earlier eras, such as "broadcast" or "telephone" [1]; an ending that
reassures and connects listeners' own experience to change [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Rewrites that change words but keep the original register.
- Hedging so heavily that no claim remains.
- Directed writing openings that announce the topic instead of engaging the reader.
- Accurate linguistic ideas left in jargon, or accessible writing with no linguistic ideas.
