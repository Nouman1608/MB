AQA GCSE English Literature (8702) is assessed through two written papers of **unequal weighting**,
not tiered: Paper 1 (Shakespeare and the 19th-century novel, 1h45, 64 marks, 40%) and Paper 2 (Modern
texts and poetry, 2h15, 96 marks, 60%). Both papers are closed-book -- any stimulus material is
provided in the assessment itself. These notes turn that structure into an exam-day plan, alongside
the [Paper 1](/resources/aqa-gcse-english-literature-paper-1-revision-notes/) and
[Paper 2](/resources/aqa-gcse-english-literature-paper-2-revision-notes/) revision notes already on
the site.

## Paper 2's larger weighting should shape your revision time, not just your exam-day time

Paper 2 is worth 60% of the qualification against Paper 1's 40%, and covers three separate
components -- a modern prose/drama essay, a comparative poetry-anthology question, and an
unseen-poetry comparison -- against Paper 1's two. **Exam-preparation priority**: allocate revision
time roughly in proportion to marks and component count, not evenly split between the two papers,
since an even split under-prepares Paper 2's larger and more varied demand.

## Both papers are closed-book: quotation recall is a distinct, practisable skill

Because no extract booklet or copy of the text is provided beyond what the exam paper itself includes,
students must recall relevant quotations from memory for whole-text discussion, not just analyse a
given extract. **Exam-preparation priority**: build and actively test a bank of short, precise
quotations for each studied text -- ideally organised by theme or character -- rather than relying on
vague paraphrase, since specific textual reference is required to access the highest mark bands.

## Command words and how much detail they expect

**Identify** and **explain** require a reasoned account grounded in the text. **Analyse** requires
close attention to how a writer's specific choices of language, structure or form create meaning and
effect. **Compare** (tested directly on Paper 2's poetry questions) requires identifying both
similarities and differences between texts, not treating them one after the other with no explicit
connection made between them.

## Worked practice scenario: linking an extract to the whole text

Question (Shakespeare, Paper 1 style): "Starting with this extract, explain how Shakespeare presents
ambition as dangerous across the whole play."

```
Step 1 - close analysis of the extract itself:
Identify a specific quotation or technique in the extract that
presents ambition as dangerous (e.g. violent or unsettling imagery
associated with the ambitious character), and explain its effect.

Step 2 - explicit bridge to the whole text:
State clearly that you are now moving beyond the extract: "Beyond this
extract, Shakespeare continues to present ambition as dangerous
through..."

Step 3 - a second, whole-text example with its own quotation:
Give a specific moment elsewhere in the play, with a short supporting
quotation, showing the same idea developing or escalating.

Step 4 - link to authorial intention or context:
Briefly connect the pattern to what Shakespeare may be suggesting
about ambition more broadly (e.g. a period-appropriate view on
disrupting a natural or social order).
```

The explicit bridge in Step 2 is the step most commonly missed -- without it, an answer risks staying
entirely inside the extract and never demonstrating whole-text knowledge, which this question type
specifically requires.

## Timing that matches each paper's larger and smaller components

Paper 1's 64 marks over 1h45 and Paper 2's 96 marks over 2h15 both work out to roughly 1.6-1.7 minutes
per mark, but Paper 2's three separate components (modern text, poetry comparison, unseen poetry) mean
timing has to be actively managed within the paper, not just across it. **Exam-preparation priority**:
practise a fixed time budget for each of Paper 2's three components in advance -- for example,
roughly 45 minutes for the modern text essay, 45 minutes for the poetry-anthology comparison, and 45
minutes for the unseen-poetry comparison -- so that a strong start on the first component does not
leave the unseen-poetry comparison rushed at the end, where marks are just as available.

## Before/during exam checklist

- **Before the exam**: allocate revision time roughly in proportion to each paper's marks and
  component count, weighting Paper 2 more heavily; build and actively test a quotation bank per
  studied text, organised by theme or character; practise the extract-to-whole-text bridging
  structure specifically for Paper 1's Shakespeare and novel questions.
- **During Paper 1**: use the given extract as a starting point, then explicitly signal the move to
  whole-text discussion rather than staying inside the extract for the whole answer.
- **During Paper 2**: for the poetry comparison, identify both similarities and differences
  explicitly, rather than analysing each poem separately with no direct comparison made.
- **On every paper**: support every analytical point with a specific, accurately recalled quotation,
  not a vague paraphrase of what a text "is about."

## Self-test

1. Why should revision time be allocated unevenly between Paper 1 and Paper 2?
2. Why is quotation recall a distinct, practisable skill on this specification?
3. What step do students most commonly miss when answering an extract-based whole-text question?
4. What does "compare," as a command word, specifically require on Paper 2's poetry questions?

**Answers:** 1. Because Paper 2 is worth 60% of the qualification (against Paper 1's 40%) and covers
three separate components against Paper 1's two, so an even revision split under-prepares Paper 2's
larger and more varied demand. 2. Because both papers are closed-book -- no extract booklet or copy of
the text is provided beyond what the paper itself includes -- so quotations must be recalled from
memory, not read off a provided text. 3. The explicit bridge signalling the move from analysing the
given extract to discussing the whole text -- without it, an answer risks staying entirely inside the
extract. 4. Identifying both similarities and differences between the two poems being compared, with
an explicit connection made between them, rather than analysing each poem separately with no direct
comparison.
