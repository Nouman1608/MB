This guide covers key question **6.2 What are the patterns of and explanations for crime and deviance?** in Cambridge IGCSE Sociology (0495) Paper 2, 2025–2027 syllabus. The [crime patterns, explanations and punishment revision notes](/resources/igcse-cambridge-sociology-crime-patterns-and-punishment-revision-notes/) list each explanation in a table; this guide shows *how* each explanation works, how the theories connect to the patterns, and how to build evaluation into parts (e) and (f).

## What key question 6.2 covers

- **6.2.1 Patterns of crime and victimisation** by age, social class, gender and ethnicity.
- **6.2.2–6.2.5 Explanations** based on age, ethnicity, gender and social class, using the factors the syllabus names for each.
- **6.2.6 Theories**: postmodernism; interactionism (including labelling and moral panic theory); functionalism (including the New Right, subculturalism and strain theory); Marxism; feminism.
- **6.2.7** Strengths and limitations of the explanations for each group and of the theories.

The central skill is to separate two questions. **Do some groups commit more crime?** And **are some groups more likely to be caught, recorded and punished?** Almost every evaluation in 6.2 comes back to that distinction.

## Patterns of crime and victimisation (6.2.1)

Official statistics in many societies show four broad patterns. Describe them in words; do not invent figures.

- **Age**: recorded offending peaks in the late teens and early twenties and then falls. Young people are also more likely to be victims of violent and street crime.
- **Social class**: working-class people are over-represented among those convicted and imprisoned. Victimisation is also concentrated in poorer areas.
- **Gender**: men commit most recorded crime, especially violent crime. Men are more often victims of violence in public; women are more often victims of domestic abuse and sexual violence.
- **Ethnicity**: in some societies, members of certain minority ethnic groups are over-represented in stop and search, arrest and prison figures, and minority groups may also face higher risks of victimisation, including hate crime.

## Explanations by social characteristic (6.2.2–6.2.5)

### Age

- **Police targeting and stop and search**: young people spend more time in public spaces, where police patrol and stop people.
- **Stereotyping and self-fulfilling prophecy**: if police and the public expect youths to cause trouble, more youthful behaviour is treated as crime, and labelled young people may live up to the label.
- **Gang culture and status frustration**: young people with little status at school or work may seek respect from peers through deviance.
- **Socialisation and social control**: teenagers are moving away from parental control but are not yet tied down by jobs, mortgages or children, so informal control is weaker.
- **Lack of opportunity**: youth unemployment and low-paid work block legitimate routes to goals.

### Social class

- **Material and relative deprivation**: poverty creates motives for property crime. John Lea and Jock Young (1984) stressed **relative deprivation**, feeling deprived compared with others, which can motivate crime even where absolute poverty is low.
- **Social exclusion and lack of opportunity**: people shut out of education and work have less stake in conformity.
- **Inadequate socialisation**: the New Right blames weak parenting in an "underclass".
- **Status frustration**: Albert Cohen (1955) argued working-class boys who fail at school form delinquent subcultures to gain status.
- **Power inequalities and police targeting**: working-class areas are policed more heavily, while middle-class white-collar crime is harder to detect and often handled outside the courts.

### Gender

- **Chivalry thesis**: Otto Pollak (1950) argued male police officers and judges treat women more leniently, so women's crime is under-recorded.
- **Culture of masculinity**: toughness, risk-taking and dominance are valued among some groups of men; James Messerschmidt (1993) argued some men use crime to "accomplish" masculinity when other routes are blocked.
- **Socialisation and social control**: Frances Heidensohn (1985) argued women are controlled more tightly at home, in public and at work, leaving fewer opportunities to offend.
- **Misogyny**: hostility towards women helps explain men's violence against women and is relevant to patterns of victimisation.
- **Stereotyping, gang culture and lack of opportunity**: women may be seen as less likely to offend; gang membership is mainly male; women have had fewer opportunities for some crimes, such as corporate crime.

### Ethnicity

- **Institutional racism**: the Macpherson Report (1999) found the Metropolitan Police institutionally racist, which can affect who is stopped, arrested and charged.
- **Prejudice, discrimination, stereotyping and police targeting**: Paul Gilroy argued in the 1980s that a "myth of black criminality" shaped policing and public attitudes in Britain.
- **Material and relative deprivation**: some minority groups are more likely to live in poverty and in heavily policed inner-city areas.
- **Gang culture and status frustration**: blocked opportunities, including through discrimination in education and employment, may lead some young people to seek status in gangs.

## Theories of crime (6.2.6)

**Functionalism.** Émile Durkheim saw some crime as normal and functional, but too much crime as a sign of **anomie** (normlessness). Robert Merton's (1938) **strain theory** argued that when society values goals such as wealth but blocks legitimate means, people adapt in five ways: **conformity**, **innovation** (crime to reach goals), **ritualism**, **retreatism** and **rebellion**. **Subculturalism** includes Albert Cohen's status frustration and Richard Cloward and Lloyd Ohlin's (1960) three subcultures (criminal, conflict and retreatist), which depend on what illegitimate opportunities exist locally. The **New Right**, represented by Charles Murray, blames inadequate socialisation in an underclass.

**Interactionism.** Howard Becker (1963) argued deviance is created by **labelling**. A label can become a **master status**, pushing a person into a **deviant career**. Edwin Lemert distinguished **primary deviance** (acts not yet labelled) from **secondary deviance** (deviance that follows from the label). **Moral panic theory**: Stanley Cohen (1972) showed how media exaggeration of clashes between mods and rockers turned them into **folk devils**, leading to tougher control and more deviance (a deviancy amplification spiral). Stuart Hall and colleagues (1978) argued a moral panic about "mugging" in the 1970s diverted attention from a crisis of capitalism.

**Marxism.** Laws protect the property and interests of the ruling class; enforcement is **selective**, falling heavily on the working class; **crimes of the powerful**, such as corporate fraud and safety violations, are under-policed. Capitalism itself encourages greed, which motivates crime across classes.

**Feminism.** Feminists explain women's low recorded crime through **patriarchal control** (Heidensohn). Pat Carlen (1988) argued women conform when they gain from the **class deal** (rewards of paid work) and the **gender deal** (rewards of family life); those who gain from neither are more likely to offend. Feminists also highlight women as **victims** of domestic and sexual violence.

**Postmodernism.** Postmodernists argue no single theory explains crime in a diverse, fragmented society. Consumer culture creates desires and identities some pursue through crime; new forms of crime, such as cyber crime and hate crime, reflect changing identities and technologies; and some crime is about excitement and emotion rather than material need.

## Evaluating explanations (6.2.7)

| Explanation | Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Strain theory | Explains utilitarian working-class crime | Ignores non-utilitarian crime and crimes of the powerful |
| Subcultural theory | Explains group delinquency | Assumes working-class boys share middle-class goals |
| New Right | Highlights parenting and community | Blames individuals; ignores structural poverty |
| Labelling | Shows agencies help create crime patterns | Does not explain the first deviant act; neglects victims |
| Marxism | Explains white-collar crime going unpunished | Ignores intra-class victims and gender |
| Feminism | Explains the gender gap | May treat all women as the same |
| Postmodernism | Fits new and diverse crimes | Offers little explanation of persistent patterns by class |

## Worked example: planning a part (f) answer

*Evaluate the view that gender differences in crime are mainly the result of socialisation and social control.*

- **For**: Heidensohn's argument that women are more controlled at home, in public and at work; girls socialised into caring, less aggressive roles; boys encouraged towards risk and toughness.
- **Against**: the chivalry thesis suggests part of the gap is lenient treatment, not lower offending; Messerschmidt's masculinity argument stresses structured inequality as well as socialisation; Carlen's class and gender deals stress economic rewards; women's recorded crime has risen in some societies as opportunities change.
- **Judgement**: socialisation and control explain much of the gap, but recording practices and opportunity also matter.

**Annotated sample sentence:** *"However, the chivalry thesis [names an alternative] suggests the statistics exaggerate the gap, because police may caution women where they would charge men [mechanism], so lower recorded female crime may not mean lower offending [link to the view]."*

## Common mistakes

- Treating official statistics as proof of who commits crime.
- Confusing Albert Cohen (status frustration) with Stanley Cohen (moral panics).
- Listing Merton's adaptations without explaining which one is crime (innovation) and why.
- Using one theory for every group: apply each explanation to the pattern asked about.
- Ignoring victimisation, which 6.2.1 includes explicitly.

## Exam technique

- **Explain three**: name the explanation, show the mechanism, link it to the group in the question.
- **Discuss**: for a theory question, give three developed points and a strength or limitation.
- **Evaluate**: always include the statistics debate, plus at least one alternative explanation, and conclude.
