This set tests **3.2.1.1 Operations management** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Business (9725) specification, Version 1.1 (International AS, Unit 2). Section A has short-answer and calculation questions; Section B has longer, structured questions. Every business and figure is invented. The [operations management study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-operations-management-9725/) teaches the methods and the [operations management revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-operations-management-9725-revision-notes/) summarise the formulas.

## Section A

**1.** Define the term capacity utilisation. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between job production and flow production. **[3]**

**3.** Tern Ceramics has monthly fixed costs of $24,000 and a variable cost of $6 per mug. Last month it produced 5,000 mugs. Calculate:

**(a)** its total costs for the month **[2]**

**(b)** its unit cost. **[2]**

**4.** Tern Ceramics can produce a maximum of 6,400 mugs a month. Using the output in Question 3, calculate its capacity utilisation to one decimal place. **[2]**

**5.** Tern Ceramics had 18 employees at the start of the month and 22 at the end. Calculate its labour productivity for the month, using the average number of employees. **[3]**

**6.** Of the 5,000 mugs Tern Ceramics produced, 150 were defective. Of the 4,800 mugs it sold, 72 were returned. Calculate:

**(a)** the proportion of defects **[2]**

**(b)** the proportion of returned items. **[2]**

**7.** A dental surgery recorded a total waiting time of 2,592 minutes for 144 patients in one week. Calculate the average waiting time. **[2]**

**8.** State **two** environmental objectives an operations department might set. **[2]**

## Section B

**9.** Explain **one** trade-off between flexibility and unit cost for a print shop that produces leaflets for local businesses. **[4]**

**10.** Breckon Furniture is choosing between two production methods for a new range of chairs (monthly figures):

| | Labour-intensive | Capital-intensive |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed costs | $18,000 | $48,000 |
| Variable cost per chair | $9 | $4 |

**(a)** Calculate the unit cost of each method at an output of 5,000 chairs a month. **[4]**

**(b)** Calculate the monthly output at which the two methods have the same total costs. **[2]**

**(c)** Breckon forecasts sales of 7,000 chairs a month, but the range is new. Explain which method Breckon should choose. **[4]**

**11.** Delphine's Chocolates makes hand-decorated chocolates to individual customer orders using job production. Orders have tripled in two years. Analyse whether Delphine's should move to batch production. **[6]**

**12.** Swiftbay Couriers delivers parcels across a large city. Its average delivery time is 52 minutes, against 40 minutes for its main rival. Its cost per delivery is $6.10, against the rival's $6.40. Swiftbay's vans run at 92% capacity utilisation, and 2% of its parcels are reported damaged. An online retailer that provides 30% of Swiftbay's work has said it will move to the rival unless delivery times improve. Evaluate whether Swiftbay should make reducing delivery times its main operations objective, even if unit costs rise. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** Actual output [1] as a percentage of the maximum potential output (capacity) [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Job production makes one-off products to each customer's specification [1], while flow production makes a standard product continuously along a production line [1]; job has high unit costs but high variety, flow has the lowest unit costs but little variety [1]. **[3]**

**3. (a)** 24,000 + (6 × 5,000) [1] = **$54,000** [1].

**(b)** 54,000 ÷ 5,000 [1] = **$10.80** per mug [1]. **[4]**

**4.** 5,000 ÷ 6,400 × 100 [1] = **78.1%** [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Average employees = (18 + 22) ÷ 2 = 20 [1]. Labour productivity = 5,000 ÷ 20 [1] = **250 mugs per employee** [1]. **[3]**

**6. (a)** 150 ÷ 5,000 × 100 = **3%** [1] [1].

**(b)** 72 ÷ 4,800 × 100 = **1.5%** [1] [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* Defects use items produced; returns use items sold. Swapping the bases is the most common slip.

**7.** 2,592 ÷ 144 [1] = **18 minutes** [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Any two, such as: reduce energy use per unit [1]; reduce waste sent to landfill [1]; cut the carbon footprint of deliveries; use more recycled materials. **[2]**

**9.** To be flexible, the print shop must accept small, varied orders at short notice [1]. Each change of job means setting up the press again, during which nothing is printed [1]. Short runs therefore spread the set-up time and fixed costs over fewer leaflets, raising unit cost [1]. If it insisted on long runs to cut unit cost, it would lose customers who need small orders quickly [1]. **[4]**

**10. (a)** Labour: 18,000 + (9 × 5,000) = $63,000 [1]; 63,000 ÷ 5,000 = **$12.60** [1]. Capital: 48,000 + (4 × 5,000) = $68,000 [1]; 68,000 ÷ 5,000 = **$13.60** [1].

**(b)** Difference in fixed costs ÷ difference in variable cost per unit = 30,000 ÷ 5 [1] = **6,000 chairs** [1].

**(c)** At 7,000 chairs capital-intensive is cheaper: $76,000 against $81,000 a month [1], and it gives more consistent quality [1]. However, 7,000 is only 1,000 above the 6,000 at which costs are equal, and the range is new, so sales may fall short [1]. If sales are below 6,000, the higher fixed costs make capital-intensive dearer, so Breckon could start labour-intensive and switch once demand is proven [1]. **[10]**

**11.** Tripled orders mean Delphine's may lack the time and skilled staff to make every order individually [1]. Batch production of popular designs would let staff make trays of one design at a time, spreading set-up time and lowering unit costs [1], so more orders could be met with the same team [1]. However, the brand rests on individual decoration, which may justify a premium price [1]; standard batches could weaken that appeal and reduce the price customers will pay [1]. Delphine's could use batches for its best-selling designs while keeping job production for personalised orders [1]. **[6]**

**12.** Credit chains of reasoning and a judgement, for example:

- Delivery time is 12 minutes slower than the rival's, about 30% longer [1].
- The online retailer provides 30% of Swiftbay's work, so losing it would cut revenue sharply [1] and leave vans idle, raising unit costs anyway [1].
- Swiftbay's cost advantage ($6.10 against $6.40) may matter less to retailers whose own customers expect fast delivery [1].
- Faster delivery may need more vans or drivers, because at 92% utilisation there is little spare capacity [1], which would raise cost per delivery [1].
- If costs rose above $6.40, Swiftbay would lose its price advantage over the rival [1].
- Better route planning could cut delivery times without many extra vans [1].
- The 2% damage rate is another quality measure the retailer may compare, so speed alone may not keep the contract [1].
- Other customers may value low prices more than speed, so the objective should fit the main customer groups [1].
- Judgement: Swiftbay should make delivery time its main objective for now, because the contract at risk is large [1], but set a limit on cost per delivery so that it keeps some price advantage [1]. **[12]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing total costs by capacity instead of output.
- Using the end-of-month headcount instead of the average for productivity.
- Rounding utilisation too early or leaving off the % sign.
- Choosing a production method on cost alone when the case stresses quality or variety.
- Ending an evaluation without saying what the decision depends on.
