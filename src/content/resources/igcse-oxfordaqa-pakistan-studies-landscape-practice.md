## Section A

**1.** Name **two** major landscape types found in Pakistan. **[2]**

**2.** State **two** renewable energy resources used in Pakistan. **[2]**

## Section B

**3.** Explain the human and physical causes of **one** named physical hazard in Pakistan, and describe its impacts. **[7]**

**4.** Explain how the monsoon and western disturbances differ as causes of regional climate variation in Pakistan. **[8]**

**5.** "Non-renewable energy resources will always be more important than renewable resources for Pakistan's economy." How far do you agree? **[10]**


---

## Answers

**1.** Any two: **mountains** [1]; **plateaus** [1]; **deserts** [1]; **flood plains** [1]; **coastal areas** [1] (also accept named biomes: forests, mangroves).

**2.** Any two: **hydel (hydroelectric) power** [1]; **solar** [1]; **wind** [1] (also accept: tidal, bioenergy, geothermal).

**3.** Named hazard: **flooding** (also accept earthquake or tsunami with equivalent structure). **Physical causes**: intense monsoon rainfall combined with glacial meltwater raises river levels rapidly, particularly where flood plains channel the excess water [2]. **Human causes**: settlement and agriculture on flood plains, and deforestation upstream reducing the land's capacity to absorb rainfall, increase the population and area exposed to flooding [2]. **Impacts**: displacement of communities and loss of housing [1], damage to agricultural land and crops, threatening food supply and livelihoods [2].

**4.** The **monsoon** is a seasonal wind system that brings the bulk of Pakistan's rainfall in summer, originating from moisture-laden winds from the southwest [2] [2]. **Western disturbances** are winter weather systems originating over the Mediterranean and Caspian regions, bringing rain and snow to northern and western Pakistan at a different time of year and from a different direction [2] [2]. A strong answer explicitly states that these are two distinct systems affecting different regions and seasons, rather than treating all of Pakistan's precipitation as monsoon-driven throughout the year.

**5.** A balanced answer should **not simply agree**, since the specification requires weighing genuine advantages and disadvantages of both resource types [2]. **For non-renewables**: established infrastructure and reliable, high-output supply currently support most industrial and household demand [2], but reserves are finite and Pakistan faces growing import dependency as domestic reserves decline [2]. **For renewables**: Pakistan's river systems and glacial meltwater give hydel power genuine long-term potential with lower running costs [2], but high upfront construction costs and dependency on seasonal river flow currently limit how quickly renewables can replace non-renewable supply [2]. A well-supported conclusion should note that the balance is **likely to shift over time** as renewable infrastructure develops, rather than treating either resource type's dominance as permanent [2].

## Approaching "how far do you agree" questions

As with the equivalent question style in the Paper 1 practice
questions, the strongest answers to Question 5 do not simply pick a
side and describe it. State a clear initial position, but qualify it
where the evidence genuinely supports qualification — here, a
one-sided answer favouring either non-renewables or renewables loses
marks even if the individual points made are accurate, because the
specification's own wording ("advantages and disadvantages") signals
that a balanced, weighed judgement is what is being tested. Support
each point with a named resource (natural gas, hydel power) rather
than a generic reference to "non-renewables" or "renewables" as
undifferentiated categories.

## Linking landscape to natural resources

Questions that combine landscape and resource content, like Question 3
above, reward candidates who can connect a physical process to its
human consequence in a single, developed answer rather than
describing the physical process and the human impact as two
unconnected halves. Before answering any question on a named hazard,
plan the causal chain explicitly: physical trigger, why the landscape
or climate makes Pakistan vulnerable to it, and the specific human
consequence that follows — this structure transfers directly to
questions on flooding, earthquakes or tsunamis alike.

## Building a hazard case study in advance

Because Question 3 rewards named, specific detail rather than general
statements, prepare one hazard as a fully worked case study before the
exam, with four fixed elements ready to write from memory: what
happened (a real or representative example), its physical cause, the
human impact it produced, and — where relevant — a named response or
mitigation measure taken afterwards. Having this ready in full detail
for a single hazard, rather than a shallow list of several, transfers
directly to whichever specific hazard a question names, since the same
four-part structure (event, cause, impact, response) applies whether
the question asks about a flood, an earthquake or a tsunami.

## Naming resources specifically, not generically

Section B questions on natural resources and energy consistently
reward candidates who name a specific resource — natural gas rather
than "fossil fuels," hydel power rather than "renewables" — because a
generic answer cannot demonstrate the located, specific knowledge the
mark scheme is built around. Before the exam, prepare a short list of
one or two named examples for each resource category covered in
3.2.1.3 and 3.2.1.4 (one named mineral, one named forest resource, one
named non-renewable and one named renewable energy source), so that
whichever category a question focuses on, a specific example is
already to hand rather than needing to be improvised under time
pressure.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a landform or resource without linking it to a hazard, use,
  or importance for the economy, when the question asks for
  explanation rather than description.
- Treating the monsoon and western disturbances as a single weather
  system, or attributing all rainfall to the monsoon regardless of
  season.
- Giving a one-sided renewable/non-renewable comparison, favouring one
  resource type without weighing genuine disadvantages on both sides.
- Describing a hazard's physical cause without also addressing its
  human causes or impacts, when the question specifically asks for
  both.
