## Questions

**1.** State **two** properties of CFCs that allowed them to reach the stratosphere. **[2]**

**2.** Outline how ozone in the stratosphere absorbs ultraviolet radiation while its concentration stays steady. **[3]**

**3.** Table 1 shows one year's consumption of four substances in a fictional country, Country V.

| Substance | Consumption (t) | ODP |
|---|---|---|
| CFC-12 | 60 | 1 |
| Halon-1211 | 15 | 3 |
| Methyl bromide | 40 | 0.6 |
| HCFC-22 | 1,200 | 0.055 |

**(a)** Calculate the total consumption in ODP tonnes. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage of the total ODP tonnes that comes from halon-1211. **[1]**
**(c)** HCFC-22 makes up over 90% of the mass. Explain why it should not necessarily be the first substance phased out. **[2]**

**4.** Distinguish between ozone depletion potential and global warming potential. **[2]**

**5.** Explain the sequence of events that leads to severe ozone loss over Antarctica in September and October. **[4]**

**6.** Table 2 shows the mean October total-ozone reading at a fictional Antarctic station, Station M.

| Year | 1979 | 1989 | 1999 | 2009 | 2019 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ozone (DU) | 300 | 180 | 135 | 140 | 155 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change from 1979 to 1999. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the mean rate of change from 1999 to 2019. **[1]**
**(c)** Assuming this rate continues, estimate the year in which the reading first reaches 220 DU. **[2]**
**(d)** Suggest **two** reasons why this estimate is uncertain. **[2]**

**7.** Explain why ozone losses over the Arctic are usually smaller than those over the Antarctic. **[2]**

**8.** Outline **one** effect of increased UV-B on human health and **one** effect on marine ecosystems. **[4]**

**9.** The atmospheric concentration of a fictional ozone-depleting gas was 240 parts per trillion (ppt) in 2000, when its production ended. Its half-life in the atmosphere is 30 years.

**(a)** Calculate its concentration in 2060, assuming no further release. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain **two** reasons why ozone recovery lags far behind the end of production. **[2]**

**10.** This question is about the pollution management model.

**(a)** Outline **one** strategy for ozone-depleting substances at each of the three levels of the model. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why strategies at the first level were essential in this case. **[2]**

**11.** Explain how the replacement of CFCs linked the problems of ozone depletion and climate change. **[3]**

**12.** Evaluate the Montreal Protocol as a model for other international environmental agreements. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two: chemically unreactive, so not broken down in the troposphere [1]; insoluble, so not washed out by rain [1]; long atmospheric lifetime, decades to a century [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Stable" alone is acceptable for one mark, but two marks need two different properties, not "stable" and "unreactive".

**2.** UV splits oxygen molecules, and the free oxygen atoms combine with O₂ to form ozone [1]. UV also splits ozone into O₂ and O, and the energy absorbed is converted to heat [1]. Formation and breakdown occur at matching rates, so the concentration is a dynamic equilibrium [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The third mark needs the idea of balance between formation and breakdown; describing the two reactions without saying they balance stops at 2.

**3. (a)** CFC-12: 60 × 1 = 60; halon-1211: 15 × 3 = 45; methyl bromide: 40 × 0.6 = 24; HCFC-22: 1,200 × 0.055 = 66 [1 for at least two correct conversions] [1 for all four]. Total = **195 ODP t** [1]
**(b)** 45 ÷ 195 × 100 = **23.1%** [1]
**(c)** Its ODP is very low, so its share of damage is only 66 ÷ 195 = 33.8%, far below its share of the mass [1]. Phasing out higher-ODP substances such as halons first gives a larger cut in damage per tonne removed, and HCFC-22 may be needed as a transition substitute until alternatives are ready [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Adding the masses (1,315 t) instead of ODP tonnes loses all three marks in (a); follow-through is allowed in (b).

**4.** ODP compares the ozone destroyed by 1 tonne of a substance with 1 tonne of CFC-11 [1]; GWP compares the warming caused by 1 tonne of a gas with 1 tonne of CO₂, usually over 100 years [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Distinguish" needs both reference substances; naming only the effect (ozone versus warming) earns 1.

**5.** In winter the polar vortex isolates the air over Antarctica [1]. Very low temperatures allow polar stratospheric clouds to form [1]. Reactions on cloud particles convert reservoir compounds into chlorine molecules, which accumulate in the dark [1]. When sunlight returns in spring, UV splits them into chlorine atoms, which catalytically destroy ozone rapidly [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each step must lead to the next. "It is very cold, so ozone is destroyed" misses the clouds and the role of returning sunlight.

**6. (a)** (135 − 300) ÷ 300 × 100 [1] = **−55%**, a decrease of 55% [1]
**(b)** (155 − 135) ÷ 20 = **1.0 DU per year** [1]
**(c)** (220 − 155) ÷ 1.0 = 65 years [1]; 2019 + 65 = **2084** [1]
**(d)** Any two: the rate may not stay constant, since ODS concentrations fall slowly and unevenly [1]; year-to-year variation in stratospheric temperature and the vortex changes readings [1]; new illegal emissions or uncontrolled gases such as nitrous oxide could slow recovery [1]; climate change may alter stratospheric temperatures [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* In (a) the base is the 1979 value. In (d), "the data might be wrong" is too vague; name a process.

**7.** The Arctic stratosphere is usually warmer [1], and its vortex is weaker and less stable, so fewer polar stratospheric clouds form and less chlorine is released from reservoirs [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The second mark needs the link to fewer clouds; "it is warmer" alone is only half the explanation.

**8.** **Human health:** more UV-B damages DNA in skin cells [1], increasing the risk of skin cancer (or cataracts, or weakened immune response) [1]. **Marine ecosystems:** UV-B reduces the productivity of phytoplankton near the surface [1], which reduces energy available to the rest of the marine food web [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each effect needs a consequence. "Harms phytoplankton" without the food-web link earns only 1 of 2.

**9. (a)** 60 years = 2 half-lives [1]; 240 × 0.5² = **60 ppt** [1]
**(b)** Any two: long-lived gases break down only slowly, so they keep reaching the stratosphere for decades [1]; banks in old fridges, air-conditioners and foams continue to leak after production ends [1]; illegal production adds new emissions [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Halving 240 four times (to 15 ppt) treats 60 years as four half-lives; count half-lives before multiplying.

**10. (a)** Altering human activity: replace CFCs with alternatives such as hydrocarbon refrigerants [1]. Controlling release: recover and destroy refrigerant from old equipment [1]. Clean-up and restoration: there is no practical way to remove ODS from the stratosphere, so only natural recovery over decades applies [1].
**(b)** ODS cannot be removed once released and they last for decades [1], so the only effective way to limit damage was to stop the release at source by changing products and production [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Recognising that clean-up is not possible earns the third mark in (a); inventing a clean-up technology does not.

**11.** CFCs were first replaced by HCFCs, which still deplete ozone, then by HFCs, which do not [1]. HFCs are strong greenhouse gases, so growing HFC use added to warming [1]. The Kigali Amendment (2016) added an HFC phase-down to the Montreal Protocol, so an ozone treaty now also cuts greenhouse gases [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Saying CFCs are greenhouse gases is true but is not the link the question asks about, which is the substitutes.

**12.** Indicative points, one mark each up to 9. Real essays are marked holistically, so treat these as a guide.
- Evidence of success: every UN member state ratified it, ODS concentrations are falling and the Antarctic layer is projected to recover around the 2060s [1].
- It was flexible: amendments tightened schedules as the science became clearer [1].
- The Multilateral Fund paid developing countries' costs of switching, so poorer countries could join [1].
- Trade controls with non-parties gave an incentive to sign [1].
- It acted before the science was complete, an example of the precautionary principle that other agreements could copy [1].
- Limitation: conditions were unusually favourable, with few producers, substitutes available and a clear health harm; climate change involves the whole energy system [1].
- Limitation: substitutes created a new climate problem, only addressed by Kigali decades later [1].
- Limitation: enforcement depends on monitoring, as the illegal CFC-11 emissions reported in 2018 showed, and nitrous oxide remains uncontrolled [1].
- Judgement: its design features (funding, review, trade measures) are a useful model, but its success depended on the nature of the problem, so it cannot simply be copied [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* An answer that only describes the Protocol cannot reach the top band; the question asks whether its features transfer to other problems.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Adding raw masses of different ODS instead of converting to ODP tonnes.
- Confusing ODP with GWP, or ozone depletion with global warming.
- Explaining the Antarctic hole without the clouds, the chlorine release and the return of sunlight.
- Using the final value as the base for a percentage change.
- Counting half-lives incorrectly.
- Inventing a clean-up method for stratospheric ODS.
- Essays that describe one treaty and never judge whether it works as a model.
