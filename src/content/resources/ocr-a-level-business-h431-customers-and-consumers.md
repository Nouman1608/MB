## Customers and consumers

A **customer** is the person or organisation that buys the product. A **consumer** is the person who uses it. They are sometimes the same and sometimes different.

- A parent buys cereal (customer) and a child eats it (consumer).
- A company buys a fleet of vans (customer) and its drivers use them (consumers).
- A student buys and uses their own laptop, so is both customer and consumer.

The distinction matters because marketing must reach the right people. A cereal firm must appeal to the parent's concern for health and price and to the child's taste. A hospital buying beds is the customer, but nurses and patients are the consumers and may influence the choice.

Customers can also be **businesses**. A customer that buys goods to use in its own operations or to resell is a business customer. Marketing aimed at them is **business to business (B2B)**. Marketing aimed at individuals buying for their own use is **business to consumer (B2C)**.

## B2B and B2C compared

| Feature | B2C | B2B |
|---|---|---|
| Number of buyers | Many | Often few |
| Order size | Small | Often large and repeated |
| Decision | Quick, often emotional or influenced by image | Slower, rational, based on specification, price, reliability and service |
| Who decides | Individual or household | Several people: users, technical staff, finance, purchasing manager |
| Demand | Direct | Often derived from consumer demand for what the buyer makes or sells |
| Relationship | Often a single transaction; brand loyalty | Long-term, built through contracts and account management |
| Price | Fixed or promotional prices | Often negotiated, volume discounts, credit terms |
| Typical methods | Advertising, social media, retail display, promotions, loyalty schemes | Personal selling, key account management, trade shows, tendering, technical information, trade publications |

**Derived demand** means a business buys only because its own customers want something. A flour mill's sales depend on how much bread consumers buy. This makes B2B suppliers sensitive to changes in the final market.

A business can be in both markets. A paper company may sell to printers (B2B) and to shoppers in stationery shops (B2C). It would use different packaging, price levels and promotion for each.

## Worked example 1: the economics of winning a B2B account

**Case.** Calder Fixings sells screws and brackets to builders' merchants and manufacturers. It employs three sales staff at £48,000 each, with £12,000 of travel and trade-show costs. Last year the team won 24 new accounts. An average account buys **£30,000** a year and Calder's margin before sales costs is **22%**.

**Step 1: yearly cost of selling.** 3 × £48,000 + £12,000 = **£156,000**.

**Step 2: cost per new account.** £156,000 ÷ 24 = **£6,500**.

**Step 3: yearly contribution from one account.** £30,000 × 22% = **£6,600**.

**Step 4: payback.** £6,500 ÷ £6,600 = **0.98 years**, or about twelve months.

**Step 5: value over the life of the account.** If an account lasts on average four years, the contribution is 4 × £6,600 = **£26,400**. Compared with the £6,500 cost of winning it, that is just over four times (26,400 ÷ 6,500 = 4.06).

**Interpretation.** A high-cost approach such as personal selling is justified because each account is worth so much. The business can afford to spend about a quarter of an account's lifetime contribution to win it. The calculation also shows the danger of **concentration**. If one client buys £540,000 of Calder's £1,800,000 sales, it is 30% of revenue, and losing it would cut contribution by £540,000 × 22% = **£118,800**. Calder should therefore invest in service and account management to retain such customers, and keep winning new ones to reduce dependence.

### Evaluating B2B methods

| Method | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Personal selling and account management | Builds trust; can tailor offers and answer technical questions | Expensive per contact; depends on individual staff |
| Trade shows | Many buyers in one place; products can be demonstrated | Cost of stand and staff; events are periodic |
| Tendering | Gives access to large contracts, especially in the public sector | Time-consuming; often decided mainly on price; no guarantee |
| Technical website and catalogues | Cheap; supports buyers who research alone | Does not build a relationship by itself |
| Volume discounts and credit terms | Encourage larger, repeat orders | Reduce margin; credit risks late payment |
| Trade press | Reaches a defined audience | Limited reach beyond the industry |

The more valuable and complex the sale, the more likely personal selling and relationships are to be the best approach.

## Worked example 2: the economics of a B2C sale

**Case.** Nimbus sells reusable water bottles direct to consumers. It spends **£12,000** on an influencer campaign, which sends **9,600** visits to its website. **3%** of visitors buy once, at **£24**, with a margin of **40%**.

1. Orders = 9,600 × 3% = **288**.
2. Revenue = 288 × £24 = **£6,912**.
3. Contribution = £6,912 × 40% = **£2,764.80**, or £9.60 per bottle (£24 × 40%).
4. Cost per order = £12,000 ÷ 288 = **£41.67**.
5. Orders needed to break even on the campaign = £12,000 ÷ £9.60 = **1,250**.

**Interpretation.** On the first sale alone, the campaign loses money: the contribution is £2,764.80 against £12,000 of spending, and each order costs £41.67 to win but earns only £9.60. B2C sales are small, so promotion pays only if it reaches very large numbers, builds a brand that keeps working after the campaign ends, or leads to repeat purchases and recommendations. Nimbus must therefore judge the campaign by lifetime value, not the first order.

### Evaluating B2C methods

| Method | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Advertising on television or online | Wide reach; builds brand image | Costly; hard to link directly to sales |
| Social media and influencers | Targeted; can be viral and low cost per reach | Results are unpredictable; trust can be damaged by poor choices |
| Promotions and discounts | Quick sales; trial | Reduce margin; may train customers to wait for offers |
| Loyalty schemes | Encourage repeat purchase and give customer data | Cost; competitors copy them |
| Branding and packaging | Long-term differentiation | Slow to build and expensive |
| Retail availability | Allows impulse and planned purchases | Retailers take a share of the margin |

## Choosing between them

An approach should fit the number of buyers, the value of each sale and the way decisions are made.

- **Few buyers, high value:** personal selling and relationships.
- **Many buyers, low value:** mass media, social media and retail presence.
- **Complex product:** technical information and demonstration, whether B2B or B2C (for example, a mortgage).
- **Mixed markets:** separate the mix for each, but keep brand values consistent.

A good evaluation links the choice to cost, reach, measurability and the likely return, and states a judgement for the business in the case.

## Common mistakes

- Saying customers and consumers are the same thing.
- Treating B2B as "no advertising" and B2C as "no personal selling". Both use several methods; the emphasis differs.
- Evaluating a method without referring to the cost per sale or the value of a customer.
- Forgetting derived demand when analysing a B2B supplier's risks.
- Ignoring the influence of consumers on a customer's choice.

## Exam technique

When asked to distinguish, give a clear difference and an example of each. When asked to evaluate, name the method, give a strength and a weakness in the context of the case, and judge which fits best, using figures where given.
