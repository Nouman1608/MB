## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define the term *reliability*. **[2]**

**2.** Identify **two** ethical principles a sociologist should follow when carrying out research. **[2]**

**3.** Distinguish between *validity* and *representativeness*. **[4]**

**4.** Explain what is meant by a *gatekeeper* in sociological research. **[3]**

**5.** Explain **one** way in which the personal characteristics of a researcher may affect the conduct of research. **[3]**

**6.** Explain **one** reason why quantitative research may not be fully objective. **[3]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** Explain **two** practical factors that may influence a sociologist's choice of research topic. **[6]**

**8.** Explain **two** ways in which the funding of research may bias its findings. **[6]**

**9.** Evaluate the use of unstructured interviews to study victims of domestic violence. **[8]**

**10.** Evaluate the use of official statistics to study educational achievement. **[8]**

**11.** *"Ethical concerns should always take priority over the validity of research."*

- **(a)** Explain the view expressed in the quotation. **[6]**
- **(b)** Using sociological material, give **two** arguments against the view expressed in the quotation. **[10]**

**12.** "The choice of research method is determined mainly by practical factors." Evaluate this view. **[26]**

## Answers

### 1. Reliability [2]

The extent to which a research method produces the same results when repeated [1] by the same or another researcher using the same procedures, as with a standardised questionnaire [1]. **[2]**

### 2. Ethical principles [2]

Any two, one mark each: informed consent; confidentiality; anonymity; avoiding harm; avoiding deception; protecting vulnerable groups; acting within the law [1] [1]. **[2]**

### 3. Validity and representativeness [4]

- Validity is the extent to which data gives a true picture of what it claims to measure [1], for example whether interview answers reflect what people really think and do [1].
- Representativeness is whether the sample is typical of the wider population being studied [1], so that findings can be generalised, for example a stratified sample matching the population by age and sex [1]. **[4]**

### 4. Gatekeeper [3]

A person who controls access to a group, setting or institution [1], such as a head teacher, prison governor or the leader of a gang or religious group [1]. Gatekeepers can refuse access or set conditions, for example limiting whom the researcher may speak to, which may bias the findings [1]. **[3]**

### 5. Researcher characteristics [3]

Characteristic identified [1], effect explained [1], developed with an example or concept [1]. For example: the researcher's sex [1] may affect rapport, so women may speak more openly to a female interviewer about sensitive experiences [1]; this interviewer effect can raise or lower validity depending on the match [1]. Also credit age, ethnicity, class or religion affecting access to groups. **[3]**

### 6. Objectivity in quantitative research [3]

Reason identified [1], explained [1], developed [1]. For example: the researcher chooses the questions and answer categories [1], so their assumptions shape what can be recorded [1]; operational definitions, such as how "poverty" is measured, reflect values, so numbers are not neutral [1]. Also credit choice of topic and funding, or interpretation of statistics. **[3]**

### 7. Practical factors in topic choice [6]

Up to 3 marks per factor.

- **Funding:** governments, charities and businesses fund topics that interest them, so researchers may choose topics that can attract money.
- **Access:** some groups, such as elites or criminal networks, are very hard to reach, which may rule out a topic.
- **Time:** topics needing long-term study, such as social mobility across generations, may be impractical for a short project.
- **Researcher's characteristics and skills:** a researcher may choose topics where their language, age or background gives them access. **[6]**

### 8. Funding and bias [6]

Up to 3 marks per way.

- **Shaping questions:** a sponsor may favour questions likely to produce helpful answers, or exclude sensitive areas, narrowing the research.
- **Publication:** unwelcome findings may not be published, so the overall body of research is skewed.
- **Design and timescale:** funders may require quick, cheap methods, such as online surveys, which reduce validity or representativeness.
- **Researcher self-censorship:** researchers wanting future funding may interpret findings in ways that suit the sponsor. **[6]**

### 9. Unstructured interviews with victims of domestic violence [8]

Up to 4 marks for strengths and up to 4 for limitations, applied.

**Strengths:**
- Rapport and a sensitive, flexible style help victims talk about painful experiences, increasing validity [1].
- Victims can describe experiences in their own words, revealing meanings and patterns of control that closed questions would miss [1].
- The researcher can respond to distress, pause or stop, which is more ethical with a vulnerable group [1].
- Feminist researchers such as Oakley (1981) argue that a non-hierarchical approach suits research with women [1].

**Limitations:**
- Small samples, often reached through refuges or support groups, are not representative of all victims [1].
- Recalling events may cause psychological harm, raising ethical risks [1].
- Answers are hard to compare and repeat, lowering reliability [1].
- The interviewer's reactions or sex may influence what is disclosed; confidentiality and safety must be protected if an abuser could find out [1]. **[8]**

### 10. Official statistics on educational achievement [8]

**Strengths:**
- Exam results cover whole populations of pupils, so they are highly representative [1].
- They are collected in a standardised way each year, allowing reliable comparison over time [1].
- They can be broken down by gender, ethnicity, region or eligibility for support, revealing patterns of inequality [1].
- They are freely available and raise few ethical issues, as individuals are not identified [1].

**Limitations:**
- Categories are set by the state (for example ethnic group categories), which may not match how people see themselves [1].
- They show patterns but not causes, such as why some groups achieve less [1].
- Changes in exam systems or grading rules make comparisons over time misleading [1].
- Interpretivists argue achievement is shaped by school processes, such as labelling, that statistics cannot reveal [1]. **[8]**

### 11(a). Explaining the view [6]

- Sociologists have a duty to protect participants from harm [1].
- Informed consent respects participants' rights to decide whether to take part [1].
- Deception, as in covert research, breaks trust between researchers and the public [1].
- Unethical research damages the reputation of sociology and may make future access harder [1].
- Vulnerable groups such as children need particular protection [1].
- Professional guidelines, such as those of the British Sociological Association, put participants' welfare first [1]. **[6]**

### 11(b). Arguing against the view [10]

Up to 5 marks per argument.

- **Some knowledge can only be gained covertly:** closed or deviant groups would refuse access or change behaviour, so covert research such as Patrick's study of a Glasgow gang (1973) produces valid knowledge otherwise unavailable.
- **Benefits can outweigh costs:** research exposing harm, abuse or corruption may benefit far more people than the minor deception involved.
- **Overt research distorts:** the Hawthorne effect means fully informed participants may act differently, making findings invalid, which itself wastes participants' time.
- **Ethics can be balanced, not ranked:** anonymity and careful publication can reduce harm while keeping validity, so the choice is not all-or-nothing.

Credit any two developed arguments. **[10]**

### 12. Essay: practical factors and choice of method [26]

Marked by levels across AO1, AO2 and AO3.

**Indicative content for the view:**
- Time, money and funding requirements limit what is possible.
- Access and gatekeepers decide whether observation or interviews can happen.
- The researcher's characteristics and skills shape methods.
- The nature of the topic: hidden groups require snowball samples or covert methods.

**Indicative content against the view:**
- Theoretical factors: positivists choose reliable, quantitative methods; interpretivists choose valid, qualitative ones. Theory may decide method before practical issues arise.
- Ethical factors: vulnerable groups and sensitive topics rule out some methods regardless of cost.
- The research question and aim (generalising or understanding) guide method choice.
- Mixed methods show researchers trading off practical, theoretical and ethical factors.

**Evaluation:** the factors interact; practical factors often set limits while theory decides the preferred method within them; examples from named studies strengthen the argument.

**Level guidance:** top-level answers evaluate the interaction of practical, theoretical and ethical factors with applied examples and a clear judgement; middle-level answers explain factors with limited evaluation; low-level answers list factors.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing validity, reliability and representativeness.
- Ethics reduced to "consent" alone.
- Method evaluation not applied to the group in the question, such as victims or pupils.
- Arguing against the quotation without reference to validity.
- Essays that list practical factors without weighing them against theory and ethics.
