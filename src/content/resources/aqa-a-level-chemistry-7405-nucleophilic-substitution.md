## Why the carbon atom is attacked

Halogens are more electronegative than carbon, so in a C–X bond the shared pair sits closer to X. The carbon carries a partial positive charge (δ+) and the halogen a partial negative charge (δ−).

```
   δ+   δ−
   C ── X
```

A **nucleophile** is a species that donates a lone pair of electrons to form a new covalent bond. Hydroxide, cyanide and ammonia all have a lone pair, and each is attracted to the δ+ carbon. When the new bond forms, the old C–X bond breaks and the halogen leaves as a halide ion. One group has replaced another, so this is **substitution**. The attacking species is a nucleophile, so the reaction is **nucleophilic substitution**.

Two points that students often blur:

- Nucleophiles are attracted to **positive** or **partly positive** centres. Do not call them "electron-loving".
- The curly arrow always starts on the electron pair (a lone pair or a bond) and points to where the pair ends up.

## The three nucleophiles

| Nucleophile | Reagent and conditions | Organic product | Inorganic product |
|---|---|---|---|
| OH⁻ | Aqueous sodium or potassium hydroxide, warm or heat under reflux | Alcohol | Halide ion |
| CN⁻ | Potassium cyanide dissolved in ethanol and water, heat under reflux | Nitrile | Halide ion |
| NH₃ | Excess ammonia in ethanol, heated in a sealed tube | Primary amine | Ammonium halide |

### Hydroxide: halogenoalkane to alcohol

```
CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂Cl + OH⁻ → CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂OH + Cl⁻
```

The solvent matters. The hydroxide must be **aqueous**. In ethanol, hydroxide behaves differently, as section 3.3.3.2 (Elimination) shows. This reaction is also called **hydrolysis**, because the halogenoalkane is split using a hydroxide or water.

### Cyanide: halogenoalkane to nitrile

```
CH₃CH₂Br + CN⁻ → CH₃CH₂CN + Br⁻
```

This step makes a new C–C bond, so the carbon chain grows by one. That is why it is valuable in synthesis. Name the nitrile by counting all carbons including the one in C≡N: bromoethane gives **propanenitrile**. Nitriles can later be reduced to amines (see the amines sub-topics).

### Ammonia: halogenoalkane to primary amine

```
CH₃CH₂Br + 2NH₃ → CH₃CH₂NH₂ + NH₄Br
```

Two molecules of ammonia are used. The first acts as the nucleophile. The second removes a proton from the intermediate to give the free amine. The ammonia must be in **large excess** and in a **sealed tube**: ammonia is a gas that would escape from an open flask, and excess ammonia stops the amine product from competing for the halogenoalkane.

## Writing a mechanism that earns every mark

For hydroxide with 1-bromopropane:

```
         δ+  δ−
CH₃CH₂CH₂ ─ Br        HO⁻  (lone pair on O)

Arrow 1: from the lone pair on O of OH⁻ to the δ+ carbon
Arrow 2: from the middle of the C–Br bond to Br

Products: CH₃CH₂CH₂OH   +   :Br⁻
```

Marking points to include every time:

1. Show the dipole on the C–X bond (δ+ on C, δ− on X).
2. Start arrow 1 on the **lone pair** of the nucleophile. The arrow must land on the carbon.
3. Start arrow 2 on the **bond** and finish on the halogen atom.
4. Show the halide ion with its charge.

For cyanide, the lone pair that attacks is on **carbon**, not nitrogen. For ammonia, the lone pair is on nitrogen. The mechanism gives a positively charged intermediate, R–NH₃⁺, and a second ammonia molecule removes H⁺, forming NH₄⁺.

```
CH₃CH₂Br + :NH₃ → CH₃CH₂–NH₃⁺ + Br⁻
CH₃CH₂–NH₃⁺ + :NH₃ → CH₃CH₂NH₂ + NH₄⁺
```

## Why the carbon–halogen bond enthalpy controls the rate

Compare chloro-, bromo- and iodoalkanes with the same carbon chain, reacting with the same nucleophile.

| Bond | Approximate mean bond enthalpy / kJ mol⁻¹ | Rate of substitution |
|---|---|---|
| C–Cl | 346 | Slowest |
| C–Br | 290 | Intermediate |
| C–I | 228 | Fastest |

The rate order is **iodoalkane > bromoalkane > chloroalkane**. The reason is that the C–X bond has to break in the reaction, and the weaker the bond, the less energy is needed to break it, so the lower the activation energy and the faster the reaction.

The common trap is to argue from polarity. The C–Cl bond is **more polar** than C–I, so it might be expected to react fastest. It does not. Bond enthalpy decides the rate. If an exam question asks you to "explain the trend in rate", the answer is about the **strength of the C–X bond**, not about electronegativity. Fluoroalkanes have a very strong C–F bond (about 467 kJ mol⁻¹) and react extremely slowly.

## The silver nitrate rate experiment

The nucleophile here is water, which hydrolyses the halogenoalkane slowly. The halide ion released is detected with silver nitrate.

```
R–X + H₂O → R–OH + H⁺ + X⁻
Ag⁺(aq) + X⁻(aq) → AgX(s)
```

Method outline:

1. Put the same volume of ethanol and the same volume of each halogenoalkane (1-chlorobutane, 1-bromobutane, 1-iodobutane) in separate test tubes. Ethanol is the common solvent, so the halogenoalkane and water mix.
2. Stand the tubes in a water bath at the same temperature, for example 50 °C.
3. Add the same volume of aqueous silver nitrate to each tube and start a timer.
4. Record the time for a precipitate to appear.

| Halogenoalkane | Precipitate | Time to appear |
|---|---|---|
| 1-chlorobutane | White AgCl | Longest |
| 1-bromobutane | Cream AgBr | Intermediate |
| 1-iodobutane | Yellow AgI | Shortest |

Control variables: the same carbon skeleton and position of the halogen, equal volumes and concentrations, and the same temperature. This is a fair test because only the halogen changes. The shortest time means the fastest hydrolysis and the weakest C–X bond.

## Worked example 1: amount of halide released

A student hydrolyses 1-bromobutane and collects the bromide ions as silver bromide. The dry precipitate has mass 1.878 g. Calculate the mass of 1-bromobutane that was hydrolysed. Use Aᵣ values Ag = 107.9, Br = 79.9, C = 12.0, H = 1.0.

```
Mr(AgBr)      = 107.9 + 79.9 = 187.8
n(AgBr)       = 1.878 ÷ 187.8 = 0.01000 mol
n(C₄H₉Br)     = n(Br⁻) = n(AgBr) = 0.01000 mol    (1 : 1 : 1)
Mr(C₄H₉Br)    = 4(12.0) + 9(1.0) + 79.9 = 136.9
mass          = 0.01000 × 136.9 = 1.369 g
```

Each halogenoalkane gives one halide ion, which gives one formula unit of silver halide, so all the ratios are 1 : 1.

## Worked example 2: percentage yield of an alcohol

12.29 g of 2-bromopropane (Mᵣ = 122.9) is heated under reflux with excess aqueous potassium hydroxide. 4.80 g of propan-2-ol (Mᵣ = 60.0) is isolated.

```
C₃H₇Br + OH⁻ → C₃H₇OH + Br⁻         (1 : 1)
n(C₃H₇Br)           = 12.29 ÷ 122.9 = 0.1000 mol
theoretical mass    = 0.1000 × 60.0 = 6.00 g
percentage yield    = 4.80 ÷ 6.00 × 100 = 80.0 %
```

Yields are below 100 % partly because some of the halogenoalkane also reacts by elimination (see the Elimination sub-topic) and partly because of losses during separation.

## Worked example 3: choosing reagents for a two-step route

Starting from 1-bromopropane, design a route to butylamine, CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂NH₂, a primary amine with one more carbon than the starting material.

- Step 1: heat under reflux with potassium cyanide in ethanol and water. CH₃CH₂CH₂Br + CN⁻ → CH₃CH₂CH₂CN + Br⁻. This is the nucleophilic substitution in this sub-topic and it gives butanenitrile, adding one carbon.
- Step 2: reduce the nitrile, for example with hydrogen and a nickel catalyst or with lithium tetrahydridoaluminate in dry ether. CH₃CH₂CH₂CN + 4[H] → CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂NH₂.

Using ammonia directly on 1-bromopropane would give propylamine with no extra carbon, and would also give a mixture of amines. The cyanide route is chosen because it extends the chain. The reduction step is explained in the Amines sub-topic on preparation.

## Common mistakes

- Writing the curly arrow from the hydrogen of OH⁻ instead of from the lone pair on oxygen.
- Drawing the second arrow from the halogen to the carbon. It starts on the bond and ends on the halogen.
- Leaving the charge off the halide ion, or the dipole off the C–X bond.
- Using ethanolic hydroxide when aqueous hydroxide is required.
- Explaining the rate trend by polarity or by the size of the halogen alone, rather than the bond enthalpy.
- Forgetting that excess ammonia is needed, or giving a mixture of amines.
- Describing the cyanide nucleophile as attacking through nitrogen.
- Calling the precipitate colour wrongly: chloride white, bromide cream, iodide yellow.

## Exam technique

- **Outline the mechanism**: dipole, two curly arrows, lone pair shown, products with charges.
- **Explain the trend in rate**: link rate to bond enthalpy, then to activation energy, in that order.
- **Give reagent and conditions**: name the solvent (aqueous or ethanolic), the heating method and any pressure.
- **Describe the rate experiment**: state the controlled variables and what you measure.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is hydroxide a nucleophile or a base?**
Both. In aqueous solution at moderate temperature it acts mainly as a nucleophile. In ethanol, it also acts as a base and removes H⁺, giving elimination. That second role is covered in the Elimination sub-topic.

**Why not use water instead of hydroxide?**
Water is a much weaker nucleophile, so the reaction is slow. Hydroxide carries a full negative charge, so it attacks the δ+ carbon more strongly.

**Does the mechanism differ for primary and tertiary halogenoalkanes?**
At this level you outline one mechanism for each nucleophile, with attack and loss of the halide shown together. The specification does not ask you to distinguish between different mechanism types.
