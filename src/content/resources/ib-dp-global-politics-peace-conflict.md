This guide covers **Peace and Conflict**, one of three compulsory
thematic studies for IB Diploma Programme Global Politics, first
assessment 2026. All three thematic studies are compulsory at both SL
and HL, as confirmed by the [full syllabus
guide](/resources/ib-dp-global-politics-syllabus-guide/), unlike the
teacher-selected options found in some other DP individuals and
societies subjects.

## Where this fits in the syllabus

Peace and Conflict is examined through the four core concepts
introduced in "Understanding power and global politics" — power,
sovereignty, legitimacy and interdependence — applied to genuine
political content rather than recalled as a separate list. Because
all three thematic studies are compulsory, Peace and Conflict should
not be treated as optional relative to Rights and Justice or
Development and Sustainability: Paper 2's extended-response questions
can draw on any of the three, and at HL, the Global political
challenges extension (Paper 3) asks for synthesis across all three
rather than treating any one in isolation.

## Syllabus coverage

**IB DP GLOBAL POLITICS — THEMATIC STUDY: PEACE AND CONFLICT**

Peace and Conflict examines the causes, dynamics and resolution of
political conflict, distinguishing different scales and types:
**interstate** (between states), **intrastate** (within a state, for
example civil war), and **non-state or asymmetric conflict**
(involving actors other than states, such as insurgent or terrorist
groups). Applying the four core concepts to this content:

- **Power** — who holds power in a conflict (military, economic,
informational), and how the balance of power between parties shapes
the conflict's dynamics and outcome
- **Sovereignty** — how conflict can challenge or reassert a state's
sovereignty, particularly in intrastate conflicts involving
separatist or secessionist movements
- **Legitimacy** — how combatants and outside actors claim (or deny)
legitimacy for their use of force, and how legitimacy shapes
international responses to a conflict
- **Interdependence** — how conflicts in one state or region create
effects (refugee flows, economic disruption, security concerns) that
spread beyond that conflict's own borders

The syllabus also distinguishes categories of cause (political,
economic, ethnic or religious, ideological) and approaches to
resolving conflict: **conflict management** (limiting intensity or
spread without necessarily resolving the root cause), **conflict
resolution** (addressing underlying causes to produce a durable
settlement), and **peacebuilding** (post-conflict work to prevent
recurrence, such as institution rebuilding and reconciliation).

## How to approach it

Before analysing any described conflict scenario, name which type it
actually is — interstate, intrastate, or non-state/asymmetric — since
a strong answer establishes this classification before building an
argument around it. Practise identifying which of the four core
concepts a given scenario foregrounds before writing an extended
answer, since this concept-application skill, not just conflict-
specific factual recall, is what the syllabus is built to test. When
discussing causes, name more than one contributing category and
explain how they interact, since real conflicts are rarely explained
by a single cause alone. Because the course is explicitly built around
contemporary real-world examples, build a running case log with at
least one interstate conflict, one intrastate conflict, and one
resolution or peacebuilding process, each with genuine dates, actors
and outcomes — a current example discussed with genuine up-to-date
detail tends to carry more weight than a well-known but dated case the
whole cohort is also using.

## Worked example: applying legitimacy to explain differing international responses

A question asks why the international community responds differently
to two conflicts that appear militarily similar.

```
Conflict A:    use of force widely perceived as defensive or
               authorised by an international body -- legitimacy
               is broadly recognised by other states and
               institutions

Conflict B:    use of force widely perceived as unprovoked aggression
               or lacking international authorisation -- legitimacy
               is broadly denied

Consequence:   Conflict A tends to attract more measured or
               supportive international responses; Conflict B tends
               to attract condemnation, sanctions, or intervention

Conclusion:    legitimacy is partly a matter of perception and
               recognition by other actors, not simply a description
               of the conflict's scale or intensity -- two
               militarily similar conflicts can provoke very
               different international responses once legitimacy is
               applied as the analytical lens
```

Naming legitimacy explicitly and explaining how perception shapes
response, rather than only describing what other states did, is the
skill this thematic study rewards.

## Common mistakes

Describing a conflict's events without naming which of the four core
concepts a given point is illustrating. Reducing a conflict's causes
to a single label (e.g. "it was an ethnic conflict") rather than
identifying and connecting multiple contributing causes. Confusing
conflict management with conflict resolution — the former limits a
conflict's intensity or spread, the latter addresses its underlying
causes. Using a well-known but dated case study that the entire cohort
also relies on, rather than building at least one genuinely current
example.

## Quick revision checklist

- Be able to classify a described conflict as interstate, intrastate,
  or non-state/asymmetric before analysing it.
- Practise applying all four core concepts (power, sovereignty,
  legitimacy, interdependence) to conflict scenarios, not just
  conflict-specific vocabulary.
- Build a running case log with at least one interstate conflict, one
  intrastate conflict, and one resolution or peacebuilding process.
- Distinguish conflict management, conflict resolution and
  peacebuilding, with a named mechanism or actor for each.
- HL: build case studies that can be discussed from more than one
  thematic angle, since Paper 3 requires synthesis across all three
  thematic studies.
