## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by redundancy. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between fair and unfair dismissal. **[3]**

**3.** Explain **one** reason why a dismissal for gross misconduct could be fair. **[2]**

**4.** Explain the purpose of an employment tribunal. **[2]**

**5.** Which **one** of the following is an unfair reason for dismissal?

A: Repeated theft from the business.

B: The employee reported a serious safety risk to the authorities.

C: The post is no longer needed.

D: The employee cannot do the job after training.

**[1]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Keswick Glass plans to make 8 production workers redundant. Each earns £480 a week and has 5 years' service. The business offers 1.5 weeks' pay for each year of service, and pays 4 weeks' notice pay.

**(a)** Calculate the redundancy pay per person and the total redundancy and notice cost for the 8 workers. **[4]**

**(b)** Calculate the annual wage saving and the payback period in months, to one decimal place. **[3]**

**(c)** Keswick estimates that the output of the remaining staff will fall by 3% of a £3 million contribution for a year. Calculate this loss and the net benefit in the first year. **[2]**

**(d)** Evaluate whether Keswick Glass should go ahead with the redundancies. **[8]**

**7.** A shop assistant at Keswick Glass raises a complaint that her supervisor regularly makes unkind comments about her. Explain the steps that a good grievance procedure would follow and evaluate its effectiveness for the business and the employee. **[9]**

**8.** Analyse **two** reasons why a business should follow a fair procedure when dismissing an employee. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Redundancy occurs when an employee's job is no longer needed [1], for example because the business is closing, moving or reducing the work, and not because of the employee's conduct or ability [1]. **[2]**

**2.** A fair dismissal has a fair reason, such as capacity, conduct, redundancy or some other substantial reason, and follows a fair procedure [1]. An unfair dismissal has no fair reason, or does not follow a fair procedure [1], or is for a reason the law treats as unfair, such as pregnancy or trade union membership [1]. **[3]**

**3.** Gross misconduct is serious conduct, such as theft or violence, which breaks the trust between employer and employee [1], so the employer may be justified in dismissing at once after a fair investigation and hearing [1]. **[2]**

**4.** An employment tribunal hears disputes between employers and employees, such as claims for unfair dismissal [1] and decides whether the law has been broken and what remedy to give, in a way that is less formal and cheaper than a court [1]. **[2]**

**5.** **B** [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** Redundancy pay per person = 1.5 × 5 × £480 = **£3,600** [1]. For 8 workers = £28,800 [1]. Notice pay = 4 × £480 × 8 = £15,360 [1]. Total = £28,800 + £15,360 = **£44,160** [1]. **[4]**

**(b)** Annual saving = 8 × £480 × 52 = **£199,680** [1]. Payback = £44,160 ÷ £199,680 × 12 [1] = **2.7 months** [1]. **[3]**

**(c)** Loss = 3% × £3,000,000 = **£90,000** [1]. Net benefit = £199,680 − £44,160 − £90,000 = **£65,520** [1]. **[2]**

**(d)** The first-year net benefit of £65,520 is positive and the payback is quick, so the redundancies save money if demand has fallen permanently [1]. If the demand fall is temporary, the business loses skilled workers it must rehire later at extra cost [1].
The estimate of lost output (£90,000) is uncertain and could be larger if good staff leave voluntarily, or if morale falls further [1]. Remaining staff may fear further cuts and be less productive [1].
Employees affected face loss of income and stress, though redundancy pay helps [1]; the local community loses jobs and spending.
Alternatives: voluntary redundancy, a recruitment freeze or reduced hours might reduce the harm [1]. The business must also consult and use fair selection to avoid claims for unfair dismissal [1].
Judgement: Keswick should proceed only if the fall in demand is expected to last, using voluntary redundancy first, honest communication and support for those leaving [1]. **[8]**

**7.** Steps: the employee first raises it informally with her line manager, or a more senior person since the supervisor is the cause [1]. If this fails, she makes a formal written complaint [1]. A meeting is held, at which she may be accompanied by a colleague or union representative [1]. The business investigates and gives a written decision with reasons [1]. She can appeal to a more senior manager [1].
Effectiveness for the employee: she has a fair hearing, a right to be accompanied and a route to challenge the decision [1]. Effectiveness for the business: early resolution prevents a problem escalating, improves morale and reduces the risk of a claim, such as harassment or constructive dismissal [1].
Limits: the process may be slow, a small business may lack HR skills, and the employee may fear it will harm her relationship with the supervisor [1].
Judgement: the procedure is effective if it is clear, impartial and acted on, and if the business protects the employee from victimisation, but it depends on managers following it in practice [1]. **[9]**

**8.** Any two reasons, 3 marks each. Reason 1, legal risk: if the procedure is unfair, the employee may win a claim at a tribunal [1], with costs of compensation, legal fees and management time that are likely to be far greater than the cost of a fair process [1], and damage to the business's reputation [1]. Reason 2, effect on other employees: staff watch how colleagues are treated [1], and a fair, consistent process builds trust and morale [1], while an unfair one makes staff fear for their own jobs and may increase turnover [1]. Accept consistency, evidence for the decision and protection of the business's reputation, explained in the same way. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing about poor performance when the question is about redundancy.
- Giving reasons for dismissal without explaining them.
- Ignoring indirect costs of redundancy such as lost output.
- Mixing up a grievance (employee's complaint) with disciplinary action.
- Saying that a procedure guarantees a fair outcome.
