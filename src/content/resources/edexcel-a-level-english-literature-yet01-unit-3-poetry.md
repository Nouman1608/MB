Section A of Unit 3 (WET03) is the only part of the International A Level where you meet a poem for the
first time in the exam. The specification frames it simply: you study "how to apply the poetry learning
from Unit 1 to unseen post-1900 poetry". This guide explains what that means in practice, offers a
reading sequence for the planning time, and models an answer on an original poem. The
[Unit 3 study guide](/resources/edexcel-a-level-english-literature-unit-3-poetry-and-prose-study-guide/)
covers the paper as a whole.

## What Section A covers

| Detail | Section A: Poetry |
|---|---|
| Task | One compulsory essay on one unseen post-1900 poem |
| Text | Printed in a Source Booklet |
| Marks | 20 of the paper's 50 |
| AOs | AO1 and AO2 only |
| Paper | 2 hours, open book; Section B is a 30-mark comparative prose essay |

There is no choice of question and no comparison. Context (AO3) and connections (AO4) are not assessed
here, so every sentence should be about this poem, read closely.

## What the learning outcomes ask

The specification's 3.3 outcomes ask you to:

- show knowledge and understanding of the function of key features in poetry
- read for detail of how poets use and adapt language, form and structure in poems, responding
  critically and creatively
- communicate fluently, accurately and effectively your critical evaluation
- identify and explore how attitudes and values are expressed
- use literary critical concepts and terminology with understanding and discrimination
- refer accurately and appropriately to texts.

Two phrases deserve attention. **"Function of key features"** means explaining what a feature *does*
in this poem, not that it exists. **"Attitudes and values"** means the poem's stance: what it seems to
admire, fear, mourn or question.

## AO1 and AO2 in an unseen essay

| AO | In this section it means |
|---|---|
| AO1 | A personal, informed reading with a clear thesis, sustained argument, accurate terminology and controlled expression |
| AO2 | Analysis of how meanings are shaped: form, structure, language, imagery, sound, voice, tone |

"Personal and creative" (AO1) does not mean anything goes. It means a reading of your own, argued from
evidence, that can acknowledge ambiguity.

## A reading sequence for the planning time

Spend roughly ten minutes reading and planning before you write.

1. **Situation and voice.** Who speaks, to whom, where, when? Is there a persona, or an addressee?
2. **First impression.** Write one sentence: what the poem seems to be about and how it feels.
3. **Shape.** Stanza pattern, line length, rhyme or its absence, regularity or disruption. Is it a
   recognisable form (sonnet, villanelle, dramatic monologue, free verse, prose poem)?
4. **Movement.** Where does the poem turn? Look for changes of tense, tone, addressee or sentence
   length, and for a volta.
5. **Language clusters.** Group images and words into two or three semantic fields; notice where they
   collide.
6. **Sound and rhythm.** Read silently "aloud": where does it speed up, slow down, break?
7. **The ending.** What has changed between first and last lines?
8. **Thesis.** One or two sentences answering the question, ideally including an ambiguity.

Then plan four or five paragraphs that follow the poem's movement or its main methods.

## Handling unfamiliar forms and difficulty

- **Recognise forms but do not force them.** If a poem has fourteen lines and a turn, discuss its
  relationship to the sonnet; if it breaks the expected pattern, the break is your point.
- **Free verse is still shaped.** Line breaks, white space and stanza lengths are choices.
- **Difficult lines** are not to be avoided. Offer a tentative reading ("this may suggest...",
  "one reading is...") and show how the rest of the poem supports it.
- **Ambiguity is credit-worthy** when you explain how the poem holds two meanings at once.

## Worked example on an original poem

> **The Lighthouse Keeper's Daughter** *(an original poem written for this page)*
>
> Every night my father climbed  
> the hundred steps to keep a stranger's ship  
> from breaking. I would count them from my bed,  
> his boots, then silence, then the lamp.
>
> He never spoke of storms. At breakfast  
> he would butter bread with careful hands  
> as if the knife might slip and sink us all.  
> We ate. The sea kept on rehearsing.
>
> They have automated it now. A box  
> turns on the light. No one climbs.  
> And I, who used to count, lie wide awake  
> to hear the hundred steps that no one takes.

*Question (original):* Explore the poet's presentation of memory and loss in this poem. In your answer
you should consider the poet's use of language, form and structure.

**Introduction:**

> The poem presents memory as something carried in the body and the ear: the speaker remembers her
> father less through what he said than through sounds and gestures she counted as a child. Its
> three-stanza structure moves from the past, through a portrait of his silent care, to a present in
> which the lighthouse works without him, so that the loss of the father and the loss of a way of life
> become the same absence.

**Body paragraph:**

> The final stanza turns on the plain statement "They have automated it now", whose flat, impersonal
> pronoun and present tense break the remembered rhythm of the first two stanzas. The short sentences
> "A box / turns on the light. No one climbs." reduce the father's nightly labour to a mechanism, and the
> enjambment after "box" isolates the word, as if the machine were a coffin for the old life. The last
> two lines then reverse the poem's opening: the child who once counted real steps now hears "the
> hundred steps that no one takes". The paradox of hearing absence, sharpened by the closing half-rhyme of
> "wide awake" with "takes", suggests that memory has become a kind of sleeplessness, faithful to
> something that no longer exists.

What earns credit: a clear, personal thesis (AO1); analysis of structure (tense shift, stanza
movement), syntax, enjambment, paradox and rhyme, each tied to meaning (AO2); an interpretation that
is tentative where the poem is ambiguous ("as if... a coffin").

## Building the essay

- **Introduction:** situation, overall reading, and how the poem's structure carries it.
- **Body:** four or five paragraphs following the poem's movement or its key methods. Do not analyse
  line by line; group lines by function.
- **Each paragraph:** point on the question focus, short quotation, method, effect, link to the overall
  reading.
- **Conclusion:** what the poem's ending leaves the reader with; acknowledge an alternative reading.

## Common mistakes

- **Context or biography:** guessing the poet's life or period earns nothing in Section A.
- **Comparison** with poems from Unit 1: not assessed here.
- **Line-by-line commentary** that never builds an argument.
- **Feature-spotting:** "there is enjambment in line 6".
- **Ignoring form** in a free-verse poem.
- **Overreaching:** a symbolic reading with no evidence.
- **Overrunning:** Section B carries 30 marks and needs the larger share of the two hours.

## Exam technique

- Allocate time roughly in proportion to marks: Section A is 20 of 50.
- Annotate the Source Booklet: circle the turn, bracket image clusters, mark the ending.
- Use the question's key words in your thesis and topic sentences.
- Quote a few words at a time and analyse them.
- Practise regularly with unfamiliar post-1900 poems of different forms and periods.
