## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between a capture fishery and aquaculture. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why upwelling zones support large fisheries. **[2]**

**3.** Define *maximum sustainable yield*. **[2]**

**4.** Outline what is meant by "fishing down the food web". **[2]**

**5.** Outline how shifting baseline syndrome can delay action to protect a fish stock. **[2]**

**6.** State two ways in which farming mussels differs in environmental impact from farming salmon. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** (Data response) A fictional stock has a carrying capacity of 240 000 t and a maximum growth rate of 0.5 per year. Surplus yield = r × N × (1 − N ÷ K).

**(a)** Calculate the surplus yield when the stock is 60 000 t. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the MSY and the stock size at which it occurs. **[2]**
**(c)** The stock is 60 000 t and the fleet lands 30 000 t a year. Predict what will happen to the stock, giving a reason. **[2]**
**(d)** Suggest why managers might set a quota below the MSY you calculated. **[1]**

**8.** (Data response) The table shows fictional data for a fishery over four decades.

| Decade | Mean annual catch / thousand t | Mean trophic level of catch | Mean fishing effort / thousand boat-days |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1980s | 420 | 3.8 | 60 |
| 1990s | 450 | 3.6 | 90 |
| 2000s | 440 | 3.4 | 125 |
| 2010s | 380 | 3.2 | 140 |

**(a)** Calculate catch per unit effort for the 1980s and the 2010s. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in catch per unit effort between these decades. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain what the trophic level data suggest about the fishery. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why the catch data alone gave a misleading picture in the 1990s and 2000s. **[2]**

**9.** Outline three factors that contributed to the collapse of the Grand Banks cod fishery. **[3]**

**10.** Evaluate individual transferable quotas as a way of managing a fishery. **[4]**

**11.** Explain how integrated multi-trophic aquaculture applies ecological principles to reduce the environmental impact of fish farming. **[4]**

**12.** To what extent can aquaculture meet the growing demand for aquatic food sustainably? **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** A capture fishery harvests wild organisms from natural populations [1]; aquaculture rears organisms under controlled conditions, such as cages, ponds or tanks, where people manage feeding and breeding [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Rising deep water brings nutrients to the sunlit surface [1], so phytoplankton productivity is high, supporting large populations of fish at higher trophic levels [1]. **[2]**

**3.** The largest catch that can be taken from a stock year after year [1] without reducing the size of the stock, obtained when the stock is about half its carrying capacity [1]. **[2]**

**4.** As large, high-trophic-level predatory fish are depleted [1], fisheries shift to species lower in the food web, so the average trophic level of the catch falls [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Each generation judges the stock against the state it first knew, which may already be depleted [1], so gradual long-term decline is not recognised and management responses come too late [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Any two: mussels are filter feeders and need no added feed [1]; mussels can remove nutrients from the water instead of adding waste [1]; salmon need feed containing fishmeal from wild forage fish; salmon farms can spread disease and parasites to wild fish. **[2]**

**7. (a)** 0.5 × 60 000 × (1 − 60 000 ÷ 240 000) = 0.5 × 60 000 × 0.75 [1] = **22 500 t per year** [1].
**(b)** MSY occurs at N = K ÷ 2 = **120 000 t** [1]; MSY = 0.5 × 120 000 × 0.5 = **30 000 t per year** [1].
**(c)** The stock will decline [1], because the catch of 30 000 t exceeds the surplus yield of 22 500 t at this stock size, and the surplus falls further as the stock shrinks [1].
**(d)** Any one: K and r are uncertain estimates; the stock fluctuates naturally; to leave food for predators; to allow the stock to rebuild [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Part (c) is the key idea of the subtopic: MSY is safe only at the stock size that produces it.

**8. (a)** 1980s: 420 ÷ 60 = **7.0 t per boat-day** [1]; 2010s: 380 ÷ 140 = **2.71 t per boat-day** [1].
**(b)** (2.71 − 7.0) ÷ 7.0 × 100 = **−61%** (a fall of about 61%) [1].
**(c)** The mean trophic level fell from 3.8 to 3.2 [1], suggesting large predatory species were depleted and the fishery shifted to smaller species lower in the food web (fishing down the food web) [1].
**(d)** Catches stayed at 440–450 thousand t [1] only because effort rose from 60 to 125 thousand boat-days, so each unit of effort was catching less and the stock was declining [1]. **[7]**

**9.** Any three: industrial factory trawlers and sonar greatly increased fishing power [1]; quotas were based on optimistic stock estimates, so catches exceeded the surplus [1]; technology kept catch rates high and hid the decline [1]; expansion of the domestic fleet after the 1977 200-nautical-mile limit; removal of large spawning fish reduced reproduction. **[3]**

**10.** The total allowable catch is shared out, so fishers no longer race to catch fish before others do [1]. Because shares are worth more when the stock is healthy, owners have an incentive to support sustainable catches and comply with rules [1]. However, shares can be bought up by large companies, so small-scale and community fishers may be pushed out [1]. ITQs work only if the total allowable catch is set at a sustainable level and enforced, so they are effective as part of a wider management system [1]. **[4]**

**11.** In an ecosystem, the waste of one organism is a resource for another, so matter is recycled [1]. In IMTA, fish are farmed alongside seaweed and filter-feeding shellfish [1]. Seaweed takes up dissolved nutrients such as nitrogen and phosphorus released by the fish, reducing the risk of eutrophication [1]. Shellfish filter out particles of waste feed and faeces, and both can be harvested as extra products [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Indicative content. Extended answers in the real exam are marked holistically against the IB's criteria, so treat these points as a guide:
- Capture production has levelled off, so nearly all growth in supply has come from aquaculture [1].
- Aquaculture can reduce pressure on wild stocks and provide protein and income [1].
- Farming at low trophic levels (seaweed, shellfish) needs no feed and can improve water quality [1].
- Carnivorous species such as salmon and shrimp need feed that may contain fishmeal from wild forage fish [1].
- Waste from cages causes nutrient pollution; disease, parasites and antibiotics affect wild populations [1].
- Shrimp ponds have replaced mangroves, removing nurseries for wild fish and coastal protection [1].
- IMTA, recirculating systems and alternative feeds can reduce these impacts [1].
- Certification and regulation can raise standards, but need enforcement [1].
- Judgement: aquaculture can meet much of the extra demand sustainably if it shifts towards low-trophic species and better systems, but not if expansion continues to depend on wild-caught feed and habitat loss [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using N ÷ K as a percentage in the surplus-yield expression.
- Forgetting units on catch per unit effort.
- Saying a stock is healthy because catches are steady.
- Evaluating ITQs without social effects.
- Treating all aquaculture as the same, regardless of trophic level and system.
