This guide teaches section **3.1.5.5 Catalysts** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Paper 2 (and Paper 3). Catalysts are used in most industrial processes, and the examination questions follow a set pattern: define a catalyst, say how it works, draw the reaction profile and use the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution to explain the increase in rate. This page deals with each of these and with the things a catalyst does **not** do. The [Kinetics study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-kinetics/) covers the whole topic. ## The definition

**A catalyst is a substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction without being changed in chemical composition or amount.**

Both parts matter. A catalyst is not used up: it can be recovered unchanged at the end and used again. It may take part in the mechanism, but it is regenerated. A catalyst is needed in only a small amount and appears in the mechanism but not in the overall equation.

## How a catalyst works

A catalyst provides an **alternative reaction route (pathway) with a lower activation energy**. It does not give the particles more energy and it does not make them collide more often. Because the activation energy is lower, a greater proportion of collisions has enough energy to react at the same temperature, so the rate increases.

For many catalysts, in particular heterogeneous ones, the reactants are held on the surface of the catalyst in a position that helps bonds to break and form. For example, in the Haber process, nitrogen and hydrogen molecules are adsorbed onto the iron surface, where the strong N≡N bond is weakened.

### Reaction profile with and without a catalyst

```
 enthalpy
   ▲         ___
   │        /   \       without catalyst: high Eₐ
   │       /     \
   │      /  ___  \
   │     /  /   \  \    with catalyst: lower Eₐ
   │ ___/  /     \  \___
   │ reactants        products
   └──────────────────────► progress of reaction
```

The two curves start at the same level and end at the same level. The catalysed route has a lower peak.

Key points for the diagram:

- The reactants and products are at the **same levels** with and without the catalyst.
- Therefore **ΔH is unchanged**.
- The catalysed curve has a **lower peak**, so a lower Eₐ.
- A catalysed reaction in several steps may show two small peaks, each lower than the uncatalysed peak.

## Maxwell–Boltzmann explanation

The Maxwell–Boltzmann curve for the gas at a given temperature is **unchanged** by a catalyst, because the temperature is the same. What changes is the activation energy.

1. Draw one distribution curve.
2. Mark the activation energy without a catalyst, Eₐ(uncatalysed), on the energy axis.
3. Mark the **lower** activation energy with a catalyst, Eₐ(catalysed), to the **left** of it.
4. Shade the area to the right of each line.

```
 number of
 molecules
   ▲   ,-.
   │  /   \
   │ /     \__
   │/         `-.___
   └──────┬────┬──────────────► energy
        Eₐcat  Eₐ
```

The area to the right of Eₐ(catalysed) is greater than the area to the right of Eₐ(uncatalysed). So a greater proportion of molecules has energy at least equal to the (lower) activation energy, a greater proportion of collisions is successful, and the rate increases.

### Worked example 1: a full-mark explanation

Explain, with the aid of a Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution, how a catalyst increases the rate of a reaction between gases.

The catalyst provides an alternative route with a lower activation energy. The distribution of molecular energies does not change, but the activation energy moves to the left, to a region where the area under the curve is greater. A greater proportion of the molecules therefore has energy equal to or greater than the activation energy, so more collisions are successful and the rate increases.

### Worked example 2: how large can the effect be?

This is background beyond the specification, but it shows why lowering Eₐ matters. The proportion of molecules with energy of at least Eₐ is of the order of e^(−Eₐ/RT). Lowering Eₐ from 100 to 70 kJ mol⁻¹ at 298 K multiplies this proportion by

```
e^(30 000 ÷ (8.31 × 298)) = e^12.1 ≈ 1.8 × 10⁵
```

A lowering of 30 kJ mol⁻¹ therefore increases the proportion able to react by a factor of about 180 000, without any change in temperature.

## What a catalyst does not do

| A catalyst does not | Reason |
|---|---|
| Change the enthalpy change, ΔH | Reactants and products are the same |
| Change the position of equilibrium | It speeds up the forward and reverse reactions equally (see Le Chatelier's principle) |
| Change the amount of product formed | The yield at equilibrium or the final amount of product from a given amount of reactant stays the same |
| Give the particles more energy | The distribution is unchanged |
| Get used up | It is regenerated |
| Make a reaction happen that is not feasible | It only changes the rate of a possible reaction |

### Worked example 3: the effect on equilibrium

A catalyst is added to a reversible reaction at equilibrium. State the effect on the position of equilibrium and explain.

There is no effect on the position. The catalyst lowers the activation energy of both the forward and reverse reactions by the same amount, so both rates increase equally. The equilibrium is reached more quickly but the equilibrium composition is the same.

## Why catalysts are useful in industry

| Benefit | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Lower temperature can be used | Less energy is needed, which lowers cost and reduces carbon dioxide emissions from burning fuels |
| Faster production | More product is made per hour, and a smaller plant may do the job |
| Less waste | A more selective catalyst can reduce by-products |
| Reusable | The catalyst is not used up, although it may become poisoned or lose activity |

Examples: iron in the Haber process (ammonia); vanadium(V) oxide in the Contact process; platinum and rhodium in catalytic converters; nickel in the hydrogenation of alkenes; an acid catalyst in making esters.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that a catalyst "lowers the activation energy" without "by providing an alternative route".
- Saying that a catalyst "gives the particles more energy".
- Drawing the catalysed profile with different reactant or product levels.
- Moving the distribution curve in the Maxwell–Boltzmann diagram instead of moving Eₐ.
- Saying that a catalyst increases the yield.
- Saying that a catalyst is "used up" or "changed".
- Drawing the Eₐ for the catalyst to the right of the original Eₐ.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Define**: "increases the rate without being changed in chemical composition or amount".
- **Explain** how it works: "provides an alternative route with a lower activation energy".
- **Draw**: one distribution curve and two activation energy lines, with areas shaded.
- **Sketch** a profile: same start and finish, a lower peak.
- **State** the effect on equilibrium: no effect on the position.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does a catalyst change ΔH?**
No. The enthalpy of the reactants and products is the same, so ΔH is unchanged.

**Is the catalyst really "unchanged"?**
It may take part in the reaction but is reformed at the end. Its chemical composition and amount are the same.

**Why does a catalyst not move the equilibrium?**
It speeds up the forward and backward reactions equally.
