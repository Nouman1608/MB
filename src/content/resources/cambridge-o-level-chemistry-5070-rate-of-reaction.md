## Collision theory in four ideas

For particles to react they must **collide**, and the collision must have at least the **activation energy, Ea**, the minimum energy colliding particles must have to react. The rate of reaction depends on how many such successful collisions happen per second. The syllabus names four ideas you should use:

1. **Number of particles per unit volume:** more particles crowded into the same space.
2. **Frequency of collisions:** the number of collisions per second, not simply "more collisions".
3. **Kinetic energy of particles:** faster-moving particles collide more often and with more energy.
4. **Activation energy:** only collisions with energy equal to or greater than Ea lead to reaction.

## Each factor explained

| Change | What happens to the particles | Effect on collisions | Rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Higher concentration of a solution | More reacting particles per unit volume | Higher collision frequency | Increases |
| Higher pressure of a gas | Same particles squeezed into smaller volume: more per unit volume | Higher collision frequency | Increases |
| Larger surface area of a solid (smaller pieces, powder) | More particles exposed at the surface | Higher collision frequency | Increases |
| Higher temperature | Particles gain kinetic energy and move faster | Higher collision frequency **and** a greater proportion of collisions have energy ≥ Ea | Increases |
| Adding a catalyst | Activation energy is lowered | A greater proportion of collisions have energy ≥ Ea | Increases |

Temperature is the factor with a **two-part** explanation. Both parts gain credit, but the second (more collisions with enough energy) is the more important one, because a small rise in temperature greatly increases the number of particles with energy at or above Ea.

## Catalysts and enzymes

The syllabus states that a catalyst **increases the rate** of a reaction, **decreases the activation energy**, and is **unchanged at the end** of the reaction. Because it is not used up, a small amount works for a long time; the same mass of catalyst can be recovered afterwards.

**Enzymes** are biological catalysts: they speed up reactions in living things, and are used in industry, for example in fermentation. Removing a catalyst returns the rate to its uncatalysed value.

A catalyst does not change the amount of product. On a pathway diagram it lowers the peak but leaves the reactant and product energies, and so ΔH, unchanged.

## Measuring rate in the laboratory

Rate is the change in the amount of a reactant or product per unit time. The two methods named in the syllabus follow a product that leaves as a gas.

**Method 1: change in mass.** The reaction flask stands on a balance, with a loose cotton wool plug in the neck. As gas escapes, the mass falls; readings are taken at regular intervals.

- The cotton wool lets gas out but stops acid spray escaping, which would make the mass loss too large.
- Works well for a dense gas such as carbon dioxide. It is poor for hydrogen: 0.00500 mol of H₂ has a mass of only 0.0100 g, too small to follow accurately on a typical school balance.

**Method 2: formation of a gas.** The gas is collected in a gas syringe (or an upturned measuring cylinder filled with water) and its volume read at regular intervals.

- A gas syringe measures any gas directly and accurately.
- Collecting over water is less suitable for gases that dissolve in water, such as carbon dioxide, which is slightly soluble, so the volume recorded is too low.

**Fair testing.** Change one factor only. Keep the same volumes and concentrations of solutions, the same mass of solid and the same temperature, unless that is the factor being investigated. Start the timer at the moment the reactants are mixed, and seal the apparatus quickly.

## Worked example 1: interpreting gas data

0.12 g of magnesium ribbon (0.00500 mol) reacts with excess dilute hydrochloric acid. The hydrogen is collected in a gas syringe.

| Time / s | 0 | 10 | 20 | 30 | 40 | 50 | 60 | 70 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Volume / cm³ | 0 | 45 | 78 | 100 | 112 | 118 | 120 | 120 |

1. **Mean rate in the first 10 s** = 45 ÷ 10 = **4.5 cm³/s**.
2. **Mean rate between 30 s and 40 s** = (112 − 100) ÷ 10 = **1.2 cm³/s**.
3. **Why it slows down:** the acid's concentration and the magnesium's surface area both fall as they are used up, so collision frequency falls.
4. **When it stops:** at 60 s the volume stops changing because the magnesium, the limiting reactant, is used up.
5. **Check the final volume:** Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂, so 0.00500 mol Mg gives 0.00500 mol H₂ = 0.00500 × 24 000 = **120 cm³**, matching the data.

On a graph of this data, the curve is **steepest at the start** (fastest rate), becomes **less steep**, and finally **levels off** (reaction finished). The steepness, or gradient, at any point is the rate at that moment.

## Worked example 2: predicting a new curve

Excess marble chips react with 50 cm³ of 1.0 mol/dm³ hydrochloric acid. The acid is the limiting reactant: 0.050 mol HCl gives 0.025 mol CO₂, which is 600 cm³ at r.t.p.

CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂

How does the curve change in each case?

- **Powdered marble, same mass:** larger surface area, so the curve is **steeper** at the start, but the final volume is still **600 cm³** because the amount of acid is unchanged.
- **50 cm³ of 0.50 mol/dm³ acid:** lower concentration, so the curve is **less steep**, and only 0.025 mol HCl is present, so the final volume **halves to 300 cm³**.
- **100 cm³ of 0.50 mol/dm³ acid:** less steep (lower concentration), but the amount of HCl is again 0.050 mol, so the final volume is **600 cm³**.

The rule: **rate** decides the steepness; the **amount of limiting reactant** decides where the curve levels off.

## Common mistakes

- **"More collisions"** instead of "more frequent collisions" or "more collisions per second".
- **"Particles have more energy so they collide more"** for temperature, without mentioning the activation energy.
- **Saying a catalyst gives more product.** It gives the same product, faster.
- **Saying higher concentration means particles move faster.** Concentration changes the number of particles per unit volume, not their speed.
- **Changing the final volume** on a sketch when only the rate has changed.
- **Mass loss with hydrogen**: the change is too small to measure well; choose a gas syringe.
- **Using "surface area" for a powder without explaining** that more particles are exposed to collisions.

## Command words in 5070 rate questions

- **Describe** (an effect): say what happens to the rate, such as "the rate increases".
- **Explain** (an effect): use collision theory: particles per unit volume, collision frequency, energy compared with Ea.
- **Evaluate** (a method): give strengths and weaknesses linked to the gas and the apparatus, and reach a judgement.
- **Interpret** (data or a graph): calculate mean rates, identify where rate is greatest, and state when and why the reaction stops.
- **Sketch:** show the correct starting gradient and final level relative to the original curve.
