Recall notes for section 3.1 of OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics (J205, Version 2.0, June 2026), examined in Component 02. The [economic growth study guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-economic-growth/) has the full explanations; the [economic growth practice questions](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-economic-growth-practice/) test them. All figures are invented.

## What the specification asks

- Explain what is meant by **economic growth**.
- Calculate and explain how growth is measured with **GDP** and **GDP per capita**.
- Analyse **recent and historical GDP data**.
- Analyse the **determinants**: investment, changes in technology, size of workforce, education and training, natural resources, government policies.
- Evaluate **costs and benefits**, including economic, social and environmental **sustainability**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Economic growth | An increase in the output of goods and services over time |
| GDP | Value of all final goods and services produced in a country in a period |
| Nominal GDP | GDP at current prices, not adjusted for inflation |
| Real GDP | GDP adjusted for inflation, so it measures output |
| GDP per capita | GDP divided by population: output per person |
| Growth rate | Percentage change in real GDP |
| Negative growth | A fall in real output |
| Recession | A period of falling output; often two quarters of negative growth in a row |
| Productivity | Output per worker or per hour worked |
| Sustainability | Meeting today's needs without harming the ability of future generations to meet theirs |

## Formulae

| Task | Formula |
|---|---|
| Growth rate | (new GDP − old GDP) ÷ old GDP × 100 |
| GDP per capita | GDP ÷ population |
| Real GDP | Nominal GDP ÷ price index × 100 |
| Growth over several years | Start × (1 + rate) for each year |

Remember: rising population means GDP per capita grows **more slowly** than GDP.

## Worked reminders

**Real growth.** Nominal GDP rises from £720bn to £756bn (5.0%) while the price index rises from 100 to 103. Real GDP = 756 ÷ 103 × 100 = £734.0bn, so real growth = 14.0 ÷ 720 × 100 = **1.9%**. Most of the nominal rise was inflation.

**Per capita.** Real GDP grows from £734bn to £749bn (2.0%) while population grows from 36.7 million to 37.8 million (3.0%). GDP per capita goes from £20,000 to about £19,815, a **fall of about 0.9%**. A bigger economy, but less output per person.

**Compounding.** Growth of 2% a year for five years: 1.02⁵ = 1.104, so output is **10.4%** higher, not 10%.

## Reading GDP data: five moves

1. Quote the highest and lowest growth figures with dates.
2. Describe the trend: rising, slowing, negative, recovering.
3. Spot any recession (consecutive negative quarters).
4. Calculate something: a change, an average or per capita figure.
5. Suggest a cause and say whether the data is real or nominal, total or per head.

**Trap:** slower growth still means output rose. Only negative growth means output fell.

## The six determinants, sorted

| More resources | Better use of resources |
|---|---|
| Investment in capital | Changes in technology |
| Larger workforce | Education and training |
| Natural resources found | Government policies (infrastructure, stable rules, research support) |

Determinants that raise **output per worker** (investment, technology, education) lift GDP per capita. A larger workforce mainly raises total GDP.

## Sustainability in three questions

| Type | Question to ask | Example of unsustainable growth |
|---|---|---|
| Economic | Can it continue? | Growth built on borrowing or a resource that will run out |
| Social | Is it shared fairly? | Gains go to one region or the richest households |
| Environmental | Does it harm the future? | Output rises through more fossil-fuel burning and waste |

## Benefits and costs

- **Benefits:** higher incomes, more jobs, more tax revenue, better public services, more profit for investment.
- **Costs:** pollution and resource use, possible inflation, unequal gains, congestion and stress.
- **Evaluate with:** how the growth is produced, who gets the gains, how fast it is, and the country's starting point. A poor country may gain more from growth than a rich one.

## Exam traps

- Nominal GDP rising is not proof of growth.
- "Recession" needs falling output, not slower growth.
- GDP per capita is an average; it hides inequality.
- A list of determinants earns little without the link to output.
- Evaluation must end with a judgement and its reason.

## Self-test

1. Define economic growth.
2. Nominal GDP rises from £800bn to £860bn while the price index rises from 100 to 104. Calculate real growth to one decimal place.
3. A country's GDP is £1,200bn and its population is 60 million. Calculate GDP per capita.
4. GDP rises by 2% and population by 3%. What happens to GDP per capita?
5. Quarterly growth is 0.3%, −0.1%, −0.2%. Is there a recession on the common definition?
6. Growth falls from 3% to 1%. Did output fall?
7. Name two determinants that raise output per worker.
8. Explain why a larger workforce may not raise GDP per capita.
9. An economy of £400bn grows 3% a year for two years. What is its size after two years?
10. State one environmental cost of growth.
11. What is meant by economically sustainable growth?
12. Give one government policy that could raise growth and explain how.

### Answers

1. An **increase in real output** of goods and services over time.
2. Real GDP = 860 ÷ 104 × 100 = £826.9bn; growth = 26.9 ÷ 800 × 100 = **3.4%**.
3. 1,200bn ÷ 60m = **£20,000**.
4. It **falls**, by about 1% (1.02 ÷ 1.03 = 0.990).
5. **Yes**: two consecutive quarters of negative growth.
6. **No**: output still rose, but more slowly.
7. Any two: **investment**, **technology**, **education and training**.
8. If the extra workers do not find jobs, or output rises less than population, output per person may stay the same or fall.
9. 400 × 1.03 × 1.03 = **£424.36bn**.
10. Any one: air pollution, carbon emissions, waste, using up finite resources, habitat loss.
11. Growth that **can continue** over time, for example based on rising productivity rather than borrowing or running down resources.
12. For example, **spending on transport infrastructure** cuts firms' costs and journey times, so they can produce and deliver more.
