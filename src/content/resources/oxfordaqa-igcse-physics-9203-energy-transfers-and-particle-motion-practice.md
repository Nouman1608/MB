Most marks are for explanations in terms of particles. For help, see the [energy transfers study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-energy-transfers-and-particle-motion/) and the [energy transfers revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-energy-transfers-and-particle-motion-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Name the particles that make metals good conductors of energy. **[1]**

**2.** Which of these increases the rate of evaporation of water from a puddle?

A: colder air

B: still air

C: a wind blowing across it

D: more humid air

**[1]**

**3.** Explain why convection cannot take place in a solid. **[1]**

**4.** State why most substances expand when heated, in terms of particles. **[2]**

**5.** A block has dimensions 8 cm × 8 cm × 1 cm. Calculate its surface area to volume ratio. **[3]**

**6.** State what happens to the rate of energy transfer from a hot object as its temperature gets closer to that of its surroundings. **[1]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** One end of a copper rod is placed in a flame. Soon the other end is hot.

**(a)** Describe how energy is transferred along the rod by particle vibrations. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why energy travels along the copper rod much faster than along a glass rod. **[2]**

**8.** A room is heated by an electric heater placed near the floor.

**(a)** Explain how a convection current is set up in the room. **[4]**

**(b)** Suggest why the heater would warm the room less effectively if it were fixed near the ceiling. **[1]**

**9.** After swimming, a girl stands in a breeze and feels cold.

**(a)** Explain, using kinetic theory, why the evaporation of water from her skin cools her. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why the breeze increases the cooling effect. **[1]**

**10.** A student investigates cooling. A beaker of hot water is left in a room at 20 °C.

| Time (min) | 0 | 4 | 8 | 12 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Temperature (°C) | 70 | 58 | 49 | 42 |

**(a)** Calculate the fall in temperature in each 4-minute interval. **[1]**

**(b)** Use the data to describe how the rate of cooling changes, and explain why. **[3]**

**(c)** Suggest one change to the beaker that would make the water cool more slowly, and explain how it works. **[2]**

**11.** A computer processor is fitted with a metal block that has many thin fins.

**(a)** Explain how the fins help to keep the processor cool. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest why the fins are made of metal rather than plastic. **[1]**

**(c)** The fennec fox lives in hot deserts and has very large ears. The Arctic fox lives in cold regions and has small ears. Explain how each adaptation helps the animal. **[4]**

**12.** A thermostat contains a bi-metallic strip of brass and iron. Brass expands more than iron for the same temperature rise.

**(a)** Describe and explain what happens to the strip when it is heated. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain how this can be used to switch off a heater when a room becomes too warm. **[2]**

**(c)** Give one example where expansion on heating is a hazard and state how the hazard is reduced. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Free electrons [1]. **[1]**

**2.** **C** [1]. **[1]**

**3.** The particles in a solid cannot move from place to place (they only vibrate about fixed positions), so a current of moving material cannot form [1]. **[1]**

**4.** The particles move or vibrate more [1], so they take up more space (the spaces between them increase) [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Surface area = 2 × (8 × 8 + 8 × 1 + 8 × 1) = 2 × 80 = 160 cm² [1]. Volume = 64 cm³ [1]. Ratio = 160 ÷ 64 = **2.5 per cm** [1]. **[3]**

**6.** It decreases (energy is transferred more slowly) [1]. **[1]**

**7. (a)** Particles at the hot end gain energy and vibrate more [1]. They pass the vibrations on to neighbouring particles, so energy spreads along the rod [1].

**(b)** Copper has free electrons [1] that gain kinetic energy at the hot end and move quickly through the metal, transferring energy by collisions further along; glass has no free electrons [1]. **[4]**

**8. (a)** Air near the heater is warmed and its particles move faster and further apart [1]. The warm air expands and becomes less dense [1], so it rises [1]. Cooler, denser air sinks to take its place near the heater, setting up a circulating current [1].

**(b)** Warm air near the ceiling would stay at the top, so little circulation would occur and the lower part of the room would stay cool [1]. **[5]**

**9. (a)** The most energetic water particles escape from the surface [1]. The particles left have a lower average kinetic energy [1], so the temperature of the remaining water and her skin falls [1].

**(b)** The breeze carries escaped water particles away, so fewer return and evaporation is faster [1]. **[4]**

**10. (a)** 12 °C, 9 °C, 7 °C [1].

**(b)** The rate of cooling decreases (the temperature falls by less in each interval) [1]. As the water cools, the temperature difference between the water and the room gets smaller [1], and a smaller temperature difference gives a slower rate of energy transfer [1].

**(c)** Any one with explanation: add a lid, which reduces evaporation [1] (and so the energy lost with escaping particles) [1]; or wrap the beaker in insulation such as cotton wool, which traps air [1], a poor conductor [1]. **[6]**

**11. (a)** The fins greatly increase the surface area for a small increase in volume [1], so energy is transferred to the surrounding air faster [1].

**(b)** Metal is a good conductor, so energy travels quickly from the processor into the fins [1].

**(c)** The fennec fox's large ears give a large surface area [1], so it loses energy to the surroundings faster and stays cool [1]. The Arctic fox's small ears reduce its surface area [1], so it loses energy more slowly and stays warm [1]. **[7]**

**12. (a)** The strip bends [1] because brass expands more than iron [1]; the brass is on the outside of the curve [1].

**(b)** The bending strip moves away from a contact [1], breaking the circuit so the heater switches off; when it cools it straightens and the circuit is completed again [1].

**(c)** Any one example with its solution, for example: bridges or railway tracks could buckle [1], so expansion gaps are left [1]; or metal roofs could warp [1], so they are fixed in a way that allows movement [1]. **[7]**

*Examiner insight:* For part (a) the mark for direction is lost if you say the strip bends "towards the brass". The metal that expands more ends up on the longer, outer side.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "heat rises" instead of explaining density.
- Omitting free electrons in a metal conduction answer.
- Saying evaporation cools because "particles escape" without saying they are the most energetic.
- Comparing surface areas instead of surface area to volume ratios.
- Describing a bi-metallic strip without saying the two metals expand by different amounts.
- Stating that particles get bigger when a substance expands.
