## Questions

**1.** A mixture of zinc powder and copper(II) oxide is heated. A glow spreads through the mixture and pink-brown copper forms: Zn + CuO → ZnO + Cu

**(a)** State which substance is oxidised. Give a reason. **[2]**
**(b)** Identify the reducing agent. **[1]**

**2.** Silicon can be made in the laboratory by heating dry sand (silicon dioxide) with magnesium powder: SiO₂ + 2Mg → Si + 2MgO

Explain, in terms of oxygen, which substance is oxidised and which is reduced, and identify the oxidising agent. **[3]**

**3.** *(Higher Tier only)* Aluminium powder reacts with copper(II) sulfate solution. The ionic equation is: 2Al + 3Cu²⁺ → 2Al³⁺ + 3Cu

**(a)** Write the two half equations for this reaction. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain, in terms of electrons, which species is oxidised. **[1]**
**(c)** State which species is the oxidising agent. **[1]**

**4.** Acid and alkali solutions both contain ions.

**(a)** Give the name of the ion every acid produces in water. **[1]**
**(b)** Write an equation to show the ions formed when nitric acid, HNO₃, dissolves in water. **[1]**
**(c)** A student writes: "In hydrochloric acid, the hydrogen ions stay joined to the chlorine." Explain why the student is wrong. **[1]**
**(d)** Name the ion present in every alkali solution. **[1]**

**5.** Write a balanced equation for each reaction.

**(a)** Potassium carbonate with hydrochloric acid. **[2]**
**(b)** Zinc with hydrochloric acid, forming zinc chloride. **[2]**
**(c)** Copper(II) carbonate with nitric acid, forming copper(II) nitrate, Cu(NO₃)₂. **[2]**

**6.** Barium hydroxide solution is neutralised by dilute nitric acid.

**(a)** Describe what is meant by neutralisation. **[1]**
**(b)** Name the salt formed. **[1]**
**(c)** Write the ionic equation for this neutralisation, with state symbols. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why the barium ions and nitrate ions are not shown in the ionic equation. **[1]**

**7.** Describe how to make a pure, dry sample of copper(II) nitrate crystals from copper(II) carbonate powder and dilute nitric acid. Explain how you would know that all the acid had reacted. **[6]**

**8.** *(Higher Tier only)* A technician has four bottles of acid.

| Bottle | Acid | Concentration (mol/dm³) |
|---|---|---|
| P | hydrochloric acid | 2.0 |
| Q | hydrochloric acid | 0.05 |
| R | ethanoic acid | 2.0 |
| S | ethanoic acid | 0.05 |

**(a)** Identify the bottle that contains a dilute, strong acid. **[1]**
**(b)** Identify the bottle that contains a concentrated, weak acid. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain what makes an acid strong rather than weak. **[2]**
**(d)** In a simple model of a weak acid solution, 6 molecules out of every 500 are ionised. Calculate the percentage ionised. **[1]**
**(e)** Bottle T holds 0.25 mol of nitric acid in 125 cm³ of solution. Calculate its concentration in mol/dm³, and state which bottle in the table has the same concentration. **[2]**

**9.** *(Higher Tier only)*

**(a)** A solution changes from pH 13 to pH 10. Describe how the hydrogen ion concentration changes, and by what factor. **[2]**
**(b)** An acid solution changes from pH 3 to pH 5 when water is added. By what factor has the hydrogen ion concentration decreased? **[1]**
**(c)** 10 cm³ of a strong acid with pH 1 is diluted with water to a total volume of 100 cm³. Predict the new pH. Show your reasoning. **[2]**

**10.** *(Higher Tier only)* A student adds hydrochloric acid from a burette to 25.0 cm³ of sodium hydroxide solution, recording the pH with a probe.

| Acid added (cm³) | 0 | 10 | 20 | 24 | 24.9 | 25.0 | 25.1 | 26 | 30 | 50 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| pH | 13 | 13 | 12 | 11 | 10 | 7 | 4 | 3 | 2 | 1 |

**(a)** Describe the shape of the pH data as the acid is run in. **[3]**
**(b)** State the volume of acid that exactly neutralises the alkali. **[1]**
**(c)** State the pH at that point and what it shows. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain, in terms of ions, why the pH changes only slowly between 0 and 20 cm³. **[1]**

**11.** A student tests four unknown solutions, W to Z, with universal indicator paper.

| Solution | W | X | Y | Z |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Colour | yellow | red | purple | green |

**(a)** Put the solutions in order from most acidic to most alkaline, and identify the neutral solution. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe how to use a pH probe to measure the pH of solution W. **[3]**
**(c)** Give two advantages of a pH probe over universal indicator paper. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** **Zinc** is oxidised [1] because it gains oxygen, forming zinc oxide [1].
**(b)** **Zinc** (Zn) [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (a), the reason needs the word "gains" and the link to oxygen; just saying "it forms zinc oxide" leaves out why that counts as oxidation.

**2.** Magnesium gains oxygen (forms MgO), so **magnesium is oxidised** [1]. Silicon dioxide loses oxygen (forms Si), so **silicon dioxide is reduced** [1]. The oxidising agent is **silicon dioxide** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Naming "silicon" as the substance reduced is a common slip: silicon is the product, while silicon dioxide is the substance that lost oxygen.

**3. (a)** **Al → Al³⁺ + 3e⁻** [1]; **Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu** [1]
**(b)** **Aluminium (atoms)** is oxidised because each atom loses electrons (three) [1].
**(c)** **Copper(II) ions, Cu²⁺** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Half equations are usually written for one atom or ion, as here; check each balances for charge, because a half equation with electrons on the wrong side shows the opposite process.

**4. (a)** **Hydrogen ion, H⁺** [1]
**(b)** **HNO₃ → H⁺ + NO₃⁻** [1]
**(c)** In water the acid splits into ions, so the H⁺ ions are separate and free to move in the solution, not bonded to chlorine [1].
**(d)** **Hydroxide ion, OH⁻** [1]
*Examiner insight:* "Hydrogen" alone in (a) is ambiguous; give the ion and its charge so it cannot be read as hydrogen gas.

**5. (a)** Products potassium chloride, water and carbon dioxide [1]; **K₂CO₃ + 2HCl → 2KCl + H₂O + CO₂** [1]
**(b)** Products zinc chloride and hydrogen [1]; **Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂** [1]
**(c)** Products copper(II) nitrate, water and carbon dioxide [1]; **CuCO₃ + 2HNO₃ → Cu(NO₃)₂ + H₂O + CO₂** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Count atoms on both sides before moving on; the balancing mark depends on every element matching, including oxygen in the carbonate.

**6. (a)** An acid reacts with an alkali (or base) to form a **salt and water** [1].
**(b)** **Barium nitrate** [1]
**(c)** **H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)** [1] correct state symbols [1]
**(d)** They are **spectator ions**: they are in the solution before and after the reaction and do not change [1].
*Examiner insight:* When a question asks for state symbols, leaving them out loses credit even if every formula is right.

**7.**
- Warm the dilute nitric acid in a beaker [1].
- Add copper(II) carbonate a little at a time, stirring [1].
- Keep adding until fizzing stops and some solid stays undissolved: the acid has all reacted [1].
- Filter to remove the excess copper(II) carbonate [1].
- Warm the filtrate in an evaporating basin until crystals start to appear at the edge, then set it aside to cool and crystallise [1].
- Filter off or pick out the crystals and dry them between filter paper or in a warm oven [1].

*Examiner insight:* A six-mark method needs steps in a workable order; describing filtration before the excess solid has been added, or heating the solution to dryness, weakens the answer.

**8. (a)** **Q** [1]
**(b)** **R** [1]
**(c)** A strong acid is **completely ionised** in water [1]; a weak acid is only **partially ionised** [1].
**(d)** 6 ÷ 500 × 100 = **1.2%** [1]
**(e)** 125 cm³ = 0.125 dm³; 0.25 ÷ 0.125 = **2.0 mol/dm³** [1]; same as **P** (and R) [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (c), "strong means more concentrated" confuses the two pairs of words and scores nothing; the answer must refer to ionisation.

**9. (a)** The H⁺ concentration **increases** [1] by a factor of **1000** (10 × 10 × 10) [1].
**(b)** **100** [1]
**(c)** Volume increases 10 times, so H⁺ concentration falls by a factor of 10 [1]; pH rises by 1, to **pH 2** [1].
*Examiner insight:* In (a), stating the factor without the direction (increase) answers only half the question; check whether the pH went up or down first.

**10. (a)** The pH starts high, at 13 [1], and falls slowly at first [1]; near 25 cm³ it drops steeply through 7, then levels off at a low pH (about 1) [1].
**(b)** **25.0 cm³** [1]
**(c)** **pH 7**: the solution is **neutral** [1].
**(d)** There is still a large excess of OH⁻ ions, so neutralising some of them hardly changes the pH [1].
*Examiner insight:* Describing a curve gains credit for using the data: quote the starting pH, the volume at the steep drop and the final level rather than "it goes down".

**11. (a)** **X, W, Z, Y** (most acidic to most alkaline) [1]; **Z** is neutral [1].
**(b)** Calibrate the probe using buffer solutions of known pH [1]. Rinse the probe with distilled water [1]. Put the probe into solution W and read the pH on the display when it is steady [1].
**(c)** Any two: more precise reading [1]; not affected by the colour of the solution [1]; no subjective colour matching; can record pH continuously.
*Examiner insight:* "More accurate" on its own is vague; say what makes it better, such as a reading to a decimal place or no colour judgement.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming the product (copper, silicon) instead of the oxide as the substance reduced.
- Calling the oxidising agent "oxidised" when it is reduced.
- (Higher Tier only) Showing electrons as gained in a half equation for an oxidation.
- Missing water or carbon dioxide from an acid + carbonate equation.
- Forgetting brackets in formulae such as Cu(NO₃)₂.
- Missing (aq) and (l) in an ionic equation that asks for state symbols.
- Describing salt preparation without saying the solid is added in excess.
- (Higher Tier only) Using "strong" to mean "concentrated".
- (Higher Tier only) Treating a pH change of 3 as a factor of 3 rather than 1000.
- Not converting cm³ to dm³ in a concentration calculation.
