These questions cover sections 3.7 and 3.8 of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics (J205) specification, Version 2.0 (June 2026): supply side policies, externalities and government correction. The specification is untiered. All countries, firms and figures are invented and illustrative.

## Questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by the term negative externality. **[2]**

**2.** State two supply side policies and, for each, state one economic objective it could help achieve. **[4]**

**3.** Country B has 10 million workers and produces £250bn of output a year. After a training policy, output is £258.75bn and the workforce is unchanged.

**(a)** Calculate output per worker before and after the policy. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage rise in output per worker. **[2]**

**4.** In a small town, the weekly market for disposable cups has demand Qd = 1,600 − 40P and supply Qs = 40P, where P is the price in pounds. Each cup causes £4 of external cost through litter.

**(a)** Calculate the equilibrium price and quantity. **[2]**
**(b)** The government introduces a tax of £4 per cup. Supply becomes Qs = 40(P − 4). Calculate the new equilibrium price and quantity. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the tax revenue. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the external cost avoided by the tax. **[2]**

**5.** A region's market for adult literacy classes has demand Qd = 900 − 10P and supply Qs = 20P − 300. Each class place gives £6 of external benefit.

**(a)** Calculate the equilibrium price and quantity. **[2]**
**(b)** The government pays a subsidy of £6 per place. Supply becomes Qs = 20(P + 6) − 300. Calculate the new equilibrium price and quantity. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the cost of the subsidy to the government. **[1]**

**6.** Explain two reasons why a government information campaign may fail to reduce a negative externality. **[4]**

**7.** A government sets a legal limit on the waste a factory may release into a river.

**(a)** Explain how the limit corrects a negative externality. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain one cost of this policy. **[2]**

**8.** A local government is considering free vaccination clinics, provided by the state. Explain one benefit and one cost of this policy, including an opportunity cost. **[4]**

**9.** Country E plans a training programme that costs £5bn a year. It is expected to raise annual output by 1.6% of its current output of £300bn. The government receives 30% of any extra output as tax revenue.

**(a)** Calculate the extra output. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the net annual cost to the government. **[2]**
**(c)** A new health centre costs £50m. Calculate how many health centres £5bn could build. **[1]**
**(d)** Evaluate whether the government should go ahead with the programme. **[4]**

**10.** A chemical factory in a town releases fumes that harm local residents. Evaluate whether a tax on the factory's output is the best way to reduce this problem. **[8]**

**11.** After a tax on a good, its price rises from £8.00 to £8.80 and the quantity sold falls from 5,000 to 4,700 units.

**(a)** Calculate the price elasticity of demand. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain what the result means for the tax as a way of cutting consumption. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** A cost [1] imposed on a third party and not reflected in the market price [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* name the third party and say the cost is not paid by the buyer or seller; "pollution is bad" alone does not show it is an externality.

**2.** Any two policies, each with an objective: for example, education and training [1] to raise growth or cut unemployment [1]; investment in transport [1] to raise growth or lower costs and ease price rises [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* the objective must be one of the economic objectives, such as growth, low unemployment or price stability, and it should match the policy chosen.

**3. (a)** Before: £250bn ÷ 10 million = **£25,000** per worker [1]. After: £258.75bn ÷ 10 million = **£25,875** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Rise = £25,875 − £25,000 = £875 [1]; £875 ÷ £25,000 × 100 = **3.5%** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* show the change divided by the original value; dividing by the new value gives a different answer.

**4. (a)** 1,600 − 40P = 40P, so 80P = 1,600 [1]; **P = £20 and Q = 800** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Set 1,600 − 40P = 40P − 160 [1]; 80P = 1,760 [1]; **P = £22 and Q = 720** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** 720 × £4 = **£2,880** [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** Fall in quantity = 800 − 720 = 80 cups [1]; 80 × £4 = **£320** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* write each step of the algebra; working that is written out is easier to check and shows your method clearly.

**5. (a)** 900 − 10P = 20P − 300, so 30P = 1,200 [1]; **P = £40 and Q = 500** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Set 900 − 10P = 20P − 180 [1]; 30P = 1,080 [1]; **P = £36 and Q = 540** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** 540 × £6 = **£3,240** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* a subsidy is paid on every unit sold after the subsidy, so use the new quantity, not the old one.

**6.** Any two developed reasons. People may ignore the message [1] because the harmful product is enjoyable or habit-forming, so behaviour barely changes [1]. Firms may advertise heavily [1], which can outweigh the campaign's message [1]. Other valid reasons include slow results and uncertainty. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* a development such as "because ..." or "which means ..." is needed after each reason; a list of two phrases shows less reasoning.

**7. (a)** The law caps the waste, so the factory cannot ignore the harm [1]. It must adopt cleaner methods or cut output [1]. The external cost falls, and output moves closer to the level best for society [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Firms face higher costs, for example for cleaner equipment [1]; these may be passed on as higher prices or fewer jobs [1]. (Enforcement cost to the government is also valid.) **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* a cost should be developed into a consequence for a named group, such as firms, consumers or taxpayers.

**8.** Benefit: free access means more people are vaccinated [1], which cuts the spread of disease to others, a positive externality [1]. Cost: it is paid for from taxation [1]; the opportunity cost is that the money could have been spent on another service such as schools [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "opportunity cost" means naming the next best use of the money; saying "it costs a lot" is not an opportunity cost.

**9. (a)** 1.6% × £300bn [1] = **£4.8bn** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Extra tax = 30% × £4.8bn = £1.44bn [1]; net cost = £5bn − £1.44bn = **£3.56bn** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** £5bn ÷ £50m = **100** health centres [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** Benefit: output rises by £4.8bn a year, and skills may last [1]. Cost: £5bn is spent and £3.56bn net, so on a yearly basis the extra output is less than the outlay [1]; the opportunity cost is 100 health centres [1]. Judgement: it depends on how long the benefit lasts and on whether skills match jobs, so go ahead only if the gains are long-lasting [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* use the figures you have calculated in the evaluation, then finish with a judgement that gives a reason.

**10.** Explain: a tax raises the factory's costs, so supply falls, price rises and output falls, reducing fumes [1]; ideally the tax equals the external cost [1]. Benefit: it raises revenue that could fund other services or clean-up [1], and firms can choose how to cut output [1]. Limit: the external cost is hard to measure [1], and if demand is inelastic, output falls little [1]. Alternative: a legal limit gives certainty but costs more to enforce [1]. Judgement: a tax with a limit on the worst emissions may work best, because the tax encourages cleaner methods while the limit sets a cap [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* "evaluate" in the specification means weighing up both sides or comparing alternatives, then reaching a supported judgement; a one-sided answer, or one without a final choice, has not done this.

**11. (a)** Price change = 0.80 ÷ 8.00 × 100 = +10% [1]; quantity change = −300 ÷ 5,000 × 100 = −6% [1]; PED = −6 ÷ 10 = **−0.6** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Demand is price inelastic [1], so the price rise causes a smaller percentage fall in quantity; the tax raises revenue but cuts consumption only slightly [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* show the percentage changes before dividing, and say what the sign and size mean for the policy.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing an externality without mentioning a third party.
- Using the old quantity instead of the new one when calculating tax revenue or subsidy cost.
- Calculating percentage change against the new value instead of the original.
- Describing what a policy is without explaining its effect on output.
- Giving "it costs money" as an opportunity cost, with no alternative named.
- Treating a tax as certain to work, with no thought of elasticity or measurement.
- Giving one-sided evaluation, or no judgement at the end.
- Forgetting to link a supply side policy to an objective.
