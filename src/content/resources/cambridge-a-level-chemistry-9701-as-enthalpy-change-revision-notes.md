## Signs

| Reaction type | ΔH | Energy flow | Surroundings | Products on pathway diagram |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Exothermic | negative | system → surroundings | warm up | below reactants |
| Endothermic | positive | surroundings → system | cool down | above reactants |

## Reaction pathway diagrams

- Axes: **enthalpy** (y) against **progress of reaction** (x).
- **E_A**: arrow from reactants **up to the peak**.
- **ΔH**: arrow from reactants to products (down if exothermic, up if endothermic).
- Reverse reaction: E_A(reverse) = E_A(forward) − ΔH(forward).
- For an endothermic reaction, E_A is always **greater than** ΔH.

## Standard conditions and definitions

Standard conditions (this syllabus): **298 K and 101 kPa**, shown by ⦵.

| Symbol | Enthalpy change when… |
|---|---|
| ΔH_r⦵ | the amounts in the equation react |
| ΔH_f⦵ | **1 mol** of a **compound** forms from its **elements in their standard states** |
| ΔH_c⦵ | **1 mol** of a substance burns **completely in oxygen** |
| ΔH_neut⦵ | an acid and an alkali react to form **1 mol of water** |

- ΔH_f⦵ of an **element** in its standard state = **0**.
- ΔH_c⦵ and ΔH_neut⦵ are always **negative**.
- Use fractions in equations to keep 1 mol of the named substance.

## Bonds and energy

- Breaking bonds: **endothermic** (bond energies are positive).
- Making bonds: **exothermic**.

        ΔH_r = Σ(bonds broken) − Σ(bonds formed)

- **Exact** bond energies: bonds found in only one substance, e.g. H–H, O=O, Cl–Cl (diatomic molecules).
- **Average** bond energies: bonds found in many compounds, e.g. C–H, C–C, O–H.
- Bond energies apply to **gases**; if a product is a liquid, the calculated ΔH differs from the measured one.

**Example** (data booklet values): H₂(g) + Cl₂(g) → 2HCl(g)

    broken: 436 + 242 = 678;   formed: 2 × 431 = 862;   ΔH_r = −184 kJ mol⁻¹

## Experimental ΔH

    q = mcΔT          ΔH = −mcΔT / n
    c = 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ (data booklet); 1.00 cm³ of solution ≈ 1.00 g

| Check | Rule |
|---|---|
| m | mass of what is **heated**: the water or the total solution, not the fuel |
| ΔT | a change of 1 °C = a change of 1 K |
| n | moles of the **limiting** reactant (or of water formed, for ΔH_neut) |
| units | q in J → divide by 1000 for kJ |
| sign | temperature rise → ΔH negative |

**Common sources of error**

| Experiment | Errors | Effect on ΔH |
|---|---|---|
| Calorimetry in solution | heat loss to surroundings and cup; heat capacity of cup ignored | less exothermic than true value |
| Combustion of fuels | heat loss; incomplete combustion; fuel evaporates from wick | much less exothermic |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Enthalpy change, ΔH | Heat energy change at constant pressure |
| Activation energy, E_A | Minimum energy required for a collision to be effective |
| Standard state | Physical state of a substance under standard conditions |
| Bond energy | Energy needed to break one mole of a particular covalent bond in the gaseous state |
| Specific heat capacity | Energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 g of a substance by 1 K |

## Exam traps

- Definitions need **one mole**, **standard conditions** and, for ΔH_f, **elements in standard states**.
- ΔH_neut is per mole of **water**, not per mole of acid (matters for H₂SO₄).
- m is the **total** solution mass in mixing experiments.
- Always include the **sign** and **kJ mol⁻¹**.
- Bonds broken **minus** bonds formed.
- Average bond energies give **approximate** answers.

## Self-test

1. State the sign of ΔH for an endothermic reaction and what happens to the temperature of the surroundings.
2. Define standard enthalpy change of formation.
3. Write the equation for the standard enthalpy change of formation of methane.
4. State the value of ΔH_f⦵ for O₂(g) and explain.
5. Define standard enthalpy change of neutralisation.
6. A reaction has E_A = +120 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔH = +40 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate E_A for the reverse reaction.
7. Explain why the C–H bond energy is an average value but H–H is exact.
8. Using C=C 610, H–Br 366, C–C 350, C–H 410 and C–Br 280 kJ mol⁻¹, calculate ΔH for CH₂=CH₂(g) + HBr(g) → CH₃CH₂Br(g).
9. 50.0 cm³ of solution warms by 4.0 K. Calculate q.
10. 0.0200 mol of a reactant releases 1.88 kJ. Calculate ΔH.
11. Give two reasons why a spirit-burner value of ΔH_c is less exothermic than the data-book value.
12. Why does a bond-energy calculation for methane combustion differ from ΔH_c⦵?

**Answers:**

1. Positive; the surroundings cool down.
2. The enthalpy change when one mole of a compound is formed from its elements in their standard states under standard conditions.
3. C(s) + 2H₂(g) → CH₄(g).
4. Zero: it is an element in its standard state, so no change is involved in forming it.
5. The enthalpy change when an acid and an alkali react to form one mole of water under standard conditions.
6. 120 − 40 = +80 kJ mol⁻¹.
7. C–H occurs in many compounds, and its strength varies slightly between them; H–H occurs only in H₂.
8. Broken: C=C + H–Br = 610 + 366 = 976; formed: C–C + C–H + C–Br = 350 + 410 + 280 = 1040; ΔH = −64 kJ mol⁻¹ (the four C–H bonds that do not change can be left out).
9. q = 50.0 × 4.18 × 4.0 = 836 J.
10. ΔH = −1.88 / 0.0200 = −94.0 kJ mol⁻¹.
11. Any two: heat lost to surroundings; incomplete combustion; evaporation of fuel; heat absorbed by the container.
12. Bond energies are averages and refer to gases, whereas ΔH_c⦵ forms liquid water.
