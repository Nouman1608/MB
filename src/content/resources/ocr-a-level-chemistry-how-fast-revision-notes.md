## Definitions (5.1.1(a))

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Rate of reaction | Change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time (mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹) |
| Order (with respect to a reactant) | Power of that reactant's concentration in the rate equation |
| Overall order | Sum of the individual orders |
| Rate constant, k | Proportionality constant in the rate equation; fixed at one temperature |
| Half-life, t½ | Time for a reactant's concentration to halve |
| Rate-determining step | Slowest step of a mechanism |

## Equations you need

| Equation | Use |
|---|---|
| rate = k[A]^m[B]^n | m, n = 0, 1 or 2 in H432 |
| k = rate ÷ ([A]^m[B]^n) | k from one experiment |
| k = ln 2/t½ | First order only (ln 2 = 0.693) |
| k = Ae^(–Ea/RT) | Arrhenius equation |
| ln k = –Ea/RT + ln A | Straight-line form: gradient –Ea/R, intercept ln A |

R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. OCR's additional guidance says R and the Arrhenius equations are provided on the Data Sheet. Integrated rate equations are not required, and you will not be asked to derive k = ln 2/t½.

## Orders from initial rates (b)

**Method in steps**

1. Pick two experiments where only one concentration changes.
2. Work out the factor for that concentration and the factor for the rate.
3. Rate factor = 1: order 0. Rate factor = concentration factor: order 1. Rate factor = (concentration factor)²: order 2.
4. Repeat for each reactant, then write the rate equation.

**Worked reminder.** [P] trebles and the rate trebles: first order in P. [Q] doubles and the rate goes up 4 times: second order in Q. So rate = k[P][Q]².

## Units of k (c)

**Method in steps**

1. Write k = rate ÷ (concentration terms).
2. Put in units: mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ on top, (mol dm⁻³) to the power of the overall order underneath.
3. Cancel and write the result with positive powers first.

| Overall order | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Units of k | mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ | s⁻¹ | dm³ mol⁻¹ s⁻¹ | dm⁶ mol⁻² s⁻¹ | dm⁹ mol⁻³ s⁻¹ |

**Worked reminder.** rate = k[P][Q]², rate = 4.8 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ at [P] = 0.20 and [Q] = 0.10 mol dm⁻³. k = 4.8 × 10⁻⁴ ÷ (0.20 × 0.10²) = **0.24 dm⁶ mol⁻² s⁻¹**.

## Graph shapes (d) and (g)

| Order | Concentration–time graph | Rate–concentration graph |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Straight line sloping down; constant gradient | Horizontal line |
| 1 | Curve with constant half-life | Straight line through origin; gradient = k |
| 2 | (not required from this graph) | Upward curve through origin |

- Rate at a time t = gradient of the tangent at t on a concentration–time graph. Quote it as a positive rate.
- For zero order, the gradient of the concentration–time line is the rate, and rate = k.
- For first order, divide a tangent rate by the concentration at that point to get k.

## Half-life (e) and (f)

- Read two or more **successive** half-lives (for example 0.80 → 0.40, then 0.40 → 0.20).
- Equal half-lives mean first order. A zero-order reactant has half-lives that get shorter as it is used up.
- **Worked reminder.** t½ = 46 s, so k = 0.693 ÷ 46 = **1.51 × 10⁻² s⁻¹**.

## Practical techniques (h)

**Continuous monitoring (PAG9):** one mixture followed over time by gas volume, mass loss or colorimetry. Gives a concentration–time graph.

**Colorimetry:** use a filter of the colour the species absorbs. Absorbance ∝ concentration; a calibration curve from standard solutions converts readings to concentrations. Zero the colorimeter with a blank first.

**Initial rates (PAG10):** separate runs, one concentration changed each time, temperature and the other concentrations kept constant. Initial rate from the tangent at t = 0, or from a clock reaction.

**Clock reaction:** time t for a fixed small amount of product to form. Initial rate ∝ 1/t. Valid only while the reaction has not proceeded too far, so the rate stays nearly constant over t.

## Mechanisms and the rate-determining step (i)

- Species in the rate equation take part in the rate-determining step, or in a step before it.
- The orders give the number of each particle involved up to and including that step.
- A zero-order reactant joins after the slow step.
- All steps must add up to the overall equation; intermediates cancel.

**Worked reminder.** 2NO₂(g) + F₂(g) → 2NO₂F(g), rate = k[NO₂][F₂].

- Slow: NO₂ + F₂ → NO₂F + F
- Fast: NO₂ + F → NO₂F

One NO₂ and one F₂ in the slow step match the rate equation. F is the intermediate.

## Temperature and k (j)

Higher temperature: Boltzmann curve shifts to higher energy, a greater proportion of molecules have energy ≥ Ea, more successful collisions per second. Concentrations are unchanged, so **k increases**. Lower temperature does the reverse.

## Arrhenius (k)

**Method in steps (graph)**

1. Convert T to kelvin; calculate 1/T and ln k for each point.
2. Plot ln k (y) against 1/T (x); draw the best-fit line.
3. Gradient from two points far apart on the line.
4. Ea = –gradient × R (J mol⁻¹); divide by 1000 for kJ mol⁻¹.
5. ln A = ln k + (Ea/R)(1/T) using a point on the line; A = e^(ln A). A has the units of k.

**Worked reminder.** Gradient = –8.42 × 10³ K. Ea = 8.42 × 10³ × 8.314 = 7.00 × 10⁴ J mol⁻¹ = **70.0 kJ mol⁻¹**.

**Worked reminder (direct use).** Ea = 45 kJ mol⁻¹, A = 3.0 × 10⁷ dm³ mol⁻¹ s⁻¹, T = 350 K. Ea/RT = 45 000 ÷ (8.314 × 350) = 15.46. k = 3.0 × 10⁷ × e^(–15.46) = **5.8 dm³ mol⁻¹ s⁻¹**.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Rate vs rate constant:** rate changes as concentrations fall; k does not, unless the temperature changes.
- **Order vs stoichiometry:** orders come from experiment, not from the balanced equation.
- **Concentration–time vs rate–concentration:** the first shows one run over time; the second compares initial rates from several runs.
- **Continuous monitoring vs initial rates:** one mixture followed over time vs many mixtures each measured at the start.
- **Rate-determining step vs overall equation:** a species can be in the overall equation and still be zero order.

## Quick self-test

1. State the overall order for rate = k[A][B]².
2. Give the units of k for rate = k[C]².
3. Give the units of k for a zero-order reaction.
4. [D] is tripled and the rate rises 9 times. State the order in D.
5. [G] is halved and the rate does not change. State the order in G.
6. A first-order reaction has t½ = 210 s. Calculate k.
7. A first-order reaction has k = 0.0150 s⁻¹. Calculate t½.
8. A rate–concentration graph is a straight line through the origin with gradient 3.6 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹. State the order and k.
9. For X + 2Y → XY₂, rate = k[X]². Which particles are in the rate-determining step?
10. A plot of ln k against 1/T has gradient –1.05 × 10⁴ K. Calculate Ea in kJ mol⁻¹.
11. Explain why 1/t from a clock reaction can be used as a measure of initial rate.

### Answers

1. **3**
2. **dm³ mol⁻¹ s⁻¹**
3. **mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹**
4. 9 = 3², so **second order**
5. **Zero order**
6. k = 0.693 ÷ 210 = **3.30 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹**
7. t½ = 0.693 ÷ 0.0150 = **46.2 s**
8. **First order; k = 3.6 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹**
9. **Two X particles**; Y reacts in a later, fast step.
10. Ea = 1.05 × 10⁴ × 8.314 = 8.73 × 10⁴ J mol⁻¹ = **87.3 kJ mol⁻¹**
11. Only a small fixed amount of product forms in time t, so the rate is nearly constant and close to the initial rate; rate = amount ÷ t, so **rate ∝ 1/t**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing orders that match the coefficients in the balanced equation.
- Treating a change by a factor of 2.5 or 3 as a doubling when comparing experiments.
- Units of k with the powers the wrong way round, such as mol² dm⁻⁶ s⁻¹.
- Quoting a negative rate from the tangent to a falling reactant curve.
- Measuring each half-life from t = 0 instead of between successive halvings.
- Using k = ln 2/t½ for a zero-order or second-order reaction.
- Putting a zero-order reactant into the proposed slow step.
- Plotting ln k against T instead of 1/T, or using T in °C.
- Giving Ea in J mol⁻¹ when kJ mol⁻¹ was asked for, or dropping the minus sign in Ea = –gradient × R.
