These questions cover section 1.1.1, The economic problem, of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Economics (4EC1) specification, Issue 3, February 2026. There is a single tier of entry. All countries, people and figures are invented. Read the [economic problem study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-economics-4ec1-the-economic-problem/) and the [economic problem revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-economics-4ec1-the-economic-problem-revision-notes/) first.

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** What is meant by scarcity? **[2]**

**2.** Which of the following is the best definition of opportunity cost?

A: the price paid for a good

B: the total value of all alternatives given up

C: the next best alternative forgone when a choice is made

D: the cost of producing one more unit

**[1]**

**3.** A point lies inside a country's production possibility frontier. Which statement is correct?

A: the combination is unobtainable

B: the country has unemployed resources

C: the country has experienced economic growth

D: the country is producing at its maximum potential

**[1]**

**4.** State **two** possible causes of positive economic growth. **[2]**

**5.** Amira has 2 free hours. She ranks her options: revising economics first, playing football second and watching a film third. She revises. State the opportunity cost of her choice. **[1]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Country K produces computers and rice. The table shows points on its PPF (thousands of units a year).

| Point | Computers | Rice |
|---|---|---|
| A | 0 | 80 |
| B | 10 | 72 |
| C | 20 | 58 |
| D | 30 | 35 |
| E | 40 | 0 |

**(a)** Calculate the opportunity cost of one thousand computers when moving from B to C. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the opportunity cost of one thousand computers when moving from D to E. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the opportunity cost changes as more computers are produced. **[2]**

**(d)** State whether a combination of 30 thousand computers and 50 thousand tonnes of rice is possible, and give a reason. **[2]**

**7.** Draw a production possibility frontier for Country K showing the effect of a major flood that destroys farmland but leaves computer factories unharmed. **[3]**

**8.** A furniture firm can use its factory to make 400 desks a month, earning a profit of 25 each, or 250 wardrobes a month, earning a profit of 45 each.

**(a)** Calculate the profit from each option. **[2]**

**(b)** The firm chooses desks. State the opportunity cost, in profit, of this choice. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain how opportunity cost may affect the firm's future decisions. **[3]**

**9.** Explain **one** way in which opportunity cost affects a government. **[3]**

**10.** Explain why investment in education can lead to positive economic growth. **[3]**

**11.** Country T's government is deciding whether to spend more on capital goods, such as roads and machines, or on consumer goods, such as subsidised food. Evaluate whether it should spend more on capital goods. **[6]**

**12.** Explain **one** reason why a rich country still faces the problem of scarcity. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Wants are unlimited [1]; but resources are finite, so choices have to be made [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "What is meant by" needs two separate parts; "not enough of something" alone earns at most one.

**2.** C [1]. **[1]**

**3.** B [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* A describes a point outside the frontier; D a point on it.

**4.** Any two: investment in capital goods [1]; education and training [1]; (also accept new technology, discovery of natural resources, immigration of workers). **[2]**

**5.** Playing football [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Only the next best option counts, so "football and the film" is wrong.

**6. (a)** 72 − 58 = 14 thousand tonnes of rice for 10 thousand computers [1]; 14 ÷ 10 = **1.4 thousand tonnes of rice** per thousand computers [1].

**(b)** 35 − 0 = 35 for 10 [1]; 35 ÷ 10 = **3.5 thousand tonnes of rice** [1].

**(c)** Resources are not equally suited to producing both goods [1]; as more computers are made, resources better suited to rice are moved, so more rice must be given up for each extra computer [1].

**(d)** It is not possible (unobtainable) [1]; because the PPF shows a maximum of 35 thousand tonnes of rice at 30 thousand computers, so the point lies outside the frontier [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* In (a) and (b) the division by 10 is what turns the change into a cost per thousand computers.

**7.** Axes labelled computers and rice, with the original PPF drawn [1]; a new curve that starts lower on the rice axis but meets the computer axis at the same point (a pivot inwards) [1]; curves labelled PPF1 and PPF2 (or arrow showing the inward movement) [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A parallel inward shift of the whole curve loses the second mark, because computer production capacity is unchanged.

**8. (a)** Desks: 400 × 25 = **10 000** [1]; wardrobes: 250 × 45 = **11 250** [1].

**(b)** 11 250 (the profit from wardrobes) [1].

**(c)** The firm gives up more profit than it earns by making desks [1]; so it is likely to switch the factory to wardrobes [1]; because resources earn the highest return there, unless desks have other benefits such as steadier demand [1]. **[6]**

**9.** A government has a limited budget, so spending on one service means another is not funded [1]; for example, building a new hospital may mean a new school is not built [1]; so the government must weigh the benefits of each, or raise taxes or borrow to pay for both [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Education raises the skills and knowledge of workers (human capital) [1]; so the quality of labour rises and each worker produces more [1]; so the productive potential of the economy increases and the PPF shifts outwards [1]. **[3]**

**11.** For capital goods: more roads and machines raise the quantity of capital [1]; so productive potential rises and the PPF shifts outwards in future [1]; allowing more of both goods to be produced later [1]. Against: the opportunity cost is fewer consumer goods now, so living standards may fall in the short run [1]; poorer households may suffer if food subsidies are cut [1]. Judgement: it depends on how poor the population is now and how quickly the investment raises output; a supported conclusion [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluate needs a conclusion that names the deciding factor; listing benefits of capital goods alone cannot reach full marks.

**12.** Wants are unlimited, so even rich people want more or better goods [1]; resources such as land and labour are still finite [1]; so choices must still be made, for example between spending on healthcare and defence [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- One-part definitions for "what is meant by".
- Listing all forgone options as the opportunity cost.
- Forgetting to divide by the number of units gained.
- Drawing a parallel shift when only one good's capacity changes.
- Describing a point outside the curve as "inefficient" rather than unobtainable.
- Evaluations with no judgement.
