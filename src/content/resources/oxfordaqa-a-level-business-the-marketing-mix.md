## The marketing mix and why it must fit together

The marketing mix is the set of choices a firm makes on its **product**, **price**, **promotion** and **place** so that it meets the needs of a chosen target market.

Applying the mix to different contexts changes the emphasis:

- **Goods** are tangible: design, packaging, quality, storage.
- **Services** are intangible and produced as consumed. Hartwick Fitness cannot stock a gym session, so its product is the experience and its place is the location and booking app.
- **B2B** buyers are few, buy in volume and judge on specification, reliability and support. Lathkill Components sells bearings to machine makers, so a sales force, long contracts and negotiated prices matter more than television advertising.
- **B2C** buyers are many; brand, emotion and convenience carry more weight.

**Importance of an integrated mix.** The elements send one message; a premium chocolate at a discount price gives mixed signals. Integration reinforces positioning, avoids wasted spend and is harder to copy. **Influences on the mix:** target market, budget, life cycle stage, competitors, costs, technology and objectives.

## Product

### New product development (NPD)

**Influences on NPD:** changing tastes, competitors' launches, technology, budget and skills, and declining products. **Value of NPD:** competitive advantage, spread risk, new segments, protected revenue. It is expensive and can fail.

The six **stages of new product development**, in order:

1. **Idea generation:** collect ideas from staff, customers, research and competitors.
2. **Screening:** reject ideas that do not fit the objectives, resources or market.
3. **Concept testing:** show the idea (a description or sketch) to potential customers to gauge interest.
4. **Business analysis:** estimate demand, costs, price and profit to see whether the project is worth pursuing.
5. **Product testing:** build a prototype or sample and test it for performance and appeal.
6. **Launch:** introduce the product to the market.

*Worked example.* Daventrie Biscuits wants a seeded oat biscuit. Staff suggest 14 flavours; management drops 9 because the machines cannot make them (screening). Shoppers read a description of the five survivors and rate two highly (concept testing). Finance estimates 400,000 packs a year to break even against realistic demand of 520,000 (business analysis). A test batch is tasted (product testing), then it is put on shelves (launch).

**Factors affecting success or failure** include: a real need found by research, product quality, price relative to value, promotion and distribution ready at launch, timing, competitors' responses, forecast accuracy and capacity to meet demand.

### Extension strategies

When a product reaches maturity, sales growth slows. An **extension strategy** delays decline. The four listed are:

- **Advertising:** reaches old and new buyers; costly, and fades if stopped.
- **Rebranding:** refreshes perceptions; may confuse loyal customers.
- **Lowering the price:** lifts volume; shrinks unit margin, may start a price war.
- **Adapting the product:** new flavours or features; development cost, may cannibalise the original.

### Branding

A **brand** (writer's wording) is a name, symbol or design that identifies and differentiates a product. **Value of branding:** recognition, a premium price, trust, easier launches and less switching. **Brand loyalty** means customers repeatedly choose the brand even when alternatives exist, stabilising sales. An **own label brand** (writer's wording) is a product sold under a retailer's name, made by another producer or the retailer. The retailer gains margin and control; quality problems damage its reputation, and the maker may be squeezed on price.

## Pricing

### Influences on price

- **Marketing objectives:** market share points to lower prices, profit to higher margins.
- **Target market** and **level of demand:** price must suit what the segment will pay.
- **Price elasticity of demand:** customers' sensitivity to price changes.
- **Costs:** price should normally cover costs over time.
- **Competitors' prices and actions:** a rival's cut may force a response.
- **The rest of the marketing mix:** a luxury image needs a matching price.

### Methods of pricing

| Type | Method | How it works | Fits when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost based | Cost-plus (standard form) | Unit cost plus a percentage mark-up | Costs are clear, many products to price |
| Demand based | Penetration | Low price at launch to win share | Price-sensitive buyers, wants volume |
| Demand based | Price skimming | High price at launch, lowered later | New, distinctive product, early adopters |
| Demand based | Dynamic pricing | Price changes frequently with demand | Online sales, perishable capacity |
| Competition based | Premium | Priced above rivals | Strong brand, quality image |
| Competition based | Going rate | Priced in line with rivals | Similar products, many competitors |
| Competition based | Discount | Priced below rivals | Cost advantage, price-led market |

*Worked example (cost-plus, standard form).* Daventrie's box of biscuits costs £1.20 to make. A 35% mark-up gives £1.20 × 1.35 = **£1.62**. Profit per box is £0.42, a profit **margin** of 0.42 ÷ 1.62 = 25.9% of the selling price. A mark-up is on cost; a margin is on price, so they differ.

### Price elasticity of demand (PED)

**PED = percentage change in quantity demanded ÷ percentage change in price.** (This is the standard form.) The sign is normally negative for a normal good; the size shows sensitivity. If the size is above 1 demand is **elastic**; below 1 it is **inelastic**.

*Worked example (elastic).* Burnthwaite Cycles sells a helmet at £40 and 2,000 a month. It raises the price to £44 and sales fall to 1,700.

- % change in price = 4 ÷ 40 = +10%.
- % change in quantity = −300 ÷ 2,000 = −15%.
- PED = −15 ÷ 10 = **−1.5**, elastic.
- Revenue before = £80,000; after = £44 × 1,700 = £74,800; revenue **falls by £5,200**.

*Worked example (inelastic).* Ingleton Tea charges £5.00 for a tin and sells 10,000. PED is estimated at −0.6. A 10% rise to £5.50 should cut quantity by 6% to 9,400. Revenue moves from £50,000 to £51,700, a **rise of £1,700**.

The rule: with elastic demand a price rise cuts revenue and a cut raises it; with inelastic demand a rise raises revenue. PED is an estimate, and revenue is not profit.

### Cross-price elasticity of demand (XED)

**XED = % change in quantity demanded of product A ÷ % change in price of product B.** (Standard form.) A **positive** result means **substitutes**; a **negative** result means **complements**. A larger size means a stronger link.

*Worked example.* Torrisdale Mobility sells scooters. A rival's scooter rises from £400 to £424 (+6%) and Torrisdale's sales go from 3,000 to 3,270 (+9%). XED = 9 ÷ 6 = **+1.5**: substitutes, and fairly strongly linked. Torrisdale also sells replacement batteries. When it raises the scooter price by 5%, battery sales fall from 4,000 to 3,700 (−7.5%). XED = −7.5 ÷ 5 = **−1.5**: complements. A scooter price cut can also lift battery sales.

## The promotional mix

**Purpose and value of promotion:** to inform, persuade and remind; build awareness and image; raise sales.

| Element | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|---|---|
| Advertising | Wide reach, controls the message | Costly, hard to measure, message can be ignored |
| Sales promotions (discounts, free gifts, buy one get one free) | Quick boost, encourages trial | Margins fall, may attract one-off buyers |
| Sales force | Personal, answers questions, suits B2B | High cost per contact, depends on staff quality |
| Public relations | Credible, low cost | Little control over coverage |
| Sponsorship | Builds image through association | Costly, results hard to prove |
| Social media | Targeted, cheap, two-way, can be measured | Bad comments spread fast, needs constant management |

**Influences on the promotional mix:** budget, target market's media habits, product (complex goods need a sales force), life cycle stage, competitors' promotion and B2B or B2C.

## Distribution (place)

A **channel of distribution** is the route from producer to customer. **Intermediaries** (writer's wording) sit between them:

- **Wholesaler:** buys in bulk, sells smaller lots to retailers. Reaches many outlets; takes margin.
- **Retailer:** sells to final customers. Controls shelf space and may demand lower prices.
- **Agent:** sells on commission without owning stock. Local knowledge, low fixed cost; less control.
- **Online (direct):** sells direct by website or app. Wide reach, keeps the whole margin; needs delivery and service.

**Multi-channel distribution** uses several routes at once, such as a shop, a website and a wholesaler. It reaches more customers and spreads risk, but can cause channel conflict and raises management cost.

**Influences on distribution decisions:** product nature (perishable, bulky, high value), target market habits, number of customers, channel cost, control wanted, competitors' routes and technology.

## The importance of marketing

- **Technology:** digital marketing, social media, dynamic pricing and e-commerce allow precise targeting and fast price changes, but need skills, data security and constant updating.
- **Ethical issues:** misleading promotions and targeting vulnerable groups, such as children. These risk complaints and lost trust even when they lift short-term sales.
- **Competitiveness:** good decisions on product, price, promotion and place create advantage, which wins customers from rivals.
- **Interrelationships:** marketing needs operations to deliver (capacity, quality), finance for the budget and human resources for skilled staff. A promotion that outruns capacity creates late deliveries.

## If your course is 9625

The 9625 text for 3.1.2.4 (Version 3.1) differs from 9725 as follows. The six NPD stages and cross-price elasticity are not printed in this section of the 9625 text; ask your teacher whether they are needed.

- **Seven Ps:** the section is headed "7Ps". Besides product, price, promotion and place it lists **people, process and physical environment** (influences on them). The mix is considered for industrial and consumer goods and services, B2B and B2C, national and international markets and e-commerce.
- **Product:** influences on and value of NPD; the implications of different stages of the **product life cycle**, including extension strategies; the **Boston Matrix** with cash cows, stars, dogs and problem children; the value of branding, including own label brands.
- **Pricing:** influences on price; price skimming, price penetration and **price discrimination**. Students interpret PED data, analyse the impact of price changes on revenue and assess the value of PED to a business.
- **Promotion:** the elements named are advertising, sales promotions, sales force, public relations and **exhibitions**. Distribution: channels including online, and multi-channel.

Definitions below are standard forms or the writer's own wording, not the specification's:

- **People:** the staff who deal with customers; their skills, attitude and training shape the service. **Process:** how the product reaches and is served to the customer, such as ordering, payment and delivery steps. **Physical environment:** the setting a customer experiences, such as shop layout or website design. Influences include the target market, budget and type of product.
- **Product life cycle:** stages of introduction, growth, maturity and decline (standard form). Extension strategies delay decline.
- **Boston Matrix:** sorts a firm's products on two axes, share and growth. **Stars** have high share in a growing market. **Cash cows** hold a large share where growth is slow, and fund other products. **Problem children** have low share in a growing market. **Dogs** have low share in a slow market. It uses simple categories and ignores other factors.
- **Price discrimination:** selling one product at different prices to separate customer groups, such as peak and off-peak users. It needs separable markets and can raise revenue; customers may see it as unfair.
- **Value of PED:** it helps predict revenue effects, but it is an estimate that changes over time.
- **Exhibitions:** events where firms show products to buyers. Strength: direct contact and demonstration; cost: stand hire and staff time.

## Common errors

- Writing "price rises" without saying what happens to revenue.
- Listing the stages of NPD without applying them to the product given.
