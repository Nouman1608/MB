## Section A

**1.** Define the term *adverse variance*. **[2]**

**2.** Identify **two** purposes of a budget. **[2]**

**3.** Explain **one** difference between incremental budgeting and zero budgeting. **[2]**

**4.** Pemberton Café's budgeted food costs were $8 400 for March. Actual food costs were $9 100. Calculate the variance and state whether it is adverse or favourable. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why involving managers in setting their budgets may improve performance. **[3]**

**6.** Explain what is meant by a *flexible budget*. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Halsey Signs' budget and actual results for June:

| | Budget | Actual |
|---|---|---|
| Sales revenue | $72 000 | $75 600 |
| Materials | $21 000 | $23 100 |
| Labour | $18 000 | $17 400 |
| Overheads | $15 000 | $15 900 |

**(a)** Calculate the variance for each item and label it adverse or favourable.

**(b)** Calculate the budgeted profit, the actual profit and the profit variance. **[7]**

**8.** Lowther Bakery budgeted to make 20 000 loaves in a month with a flour budget of $7 000. It actually made 24 000 loaves, and flour cost $8 160.

**(a)** Calculate the flexed flour budget.

**(b)** Calculate the flour variance against the flexed budget.

**(c)** Explain why comparing actual flour cost with the original budget would be misleading. **[6]**

**9.** Explain **two** reasons why a favourable labour variance might not be good news for a hospital. **[4]**

**10.** Analyse **two** drawbacks of using budgets in a fast-changing technology business. **[4]**

**11.** A council leisure department has used incremental budgeting for many years. Explain why it might switch to zero budgeting. **[4]**

**12.** Tarrant Hotels' managers are judged on whether they keep within their budgets. The finance director proposes moving from imposed incremental budgets to flexible budgets set with each manager. Evaluate whether this change would improve the hotels' performance. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** A difference between budgeted and actual figures that reduces profit [1]: revenue below budget or costs above budget [1].

**2.** Any two: measuring performance [1]; allocating resources [1]; controlling spending; monitoring progress.

**3.** Incremental budgeting adjusts last period's budget by a percentage [1], whereas zero budgeting starts each item from nothing and requires it to be justified [1].

**4.** 9 100 − 8 400 = $700 [1], adverse because costs are above budget [1].

**5.** Managers know their areas, so the budget is more realistic [1]. They feel ownership of figures they helped set [1], so they are more committed to meeting them, which may improve control of costs [1].

**6.** A budget that adjusts variable cost and revenue figures to the actual level of output [1], so that actual results can be compared fairly with what should have been spent at that output [1].

**7.** **(a)** Revenue: $3 600 F [1]. Materials: $2 100 A [1]. Labour: $600 F [1]. Overheads: $900 A [1].

**(b)** Budgeted profit = 72 000 − 21 000 − 18 000 − 15 000 = $18 000 [1]. Actual profit = 75 600 − 23 100 − 17 400 − 15 900 = $19 200 [1]. Profit variance = $1 200 F [1].

**8.** **(a)** Flour per loaf = 7 000 ÷ 20 000 = $0.35 [1]; flexed budget = 0.35 × 24 000 = $8 400 [1].

**(b)** 8 400 − 8 160 = $240 [1], favourable [1].

**(c)** Against the original budget, flour appears $1 160 over budget, suggesting poor control [1]. But the bakery made 4 000 more loaves, so more flour was needed; the flexed comparison shows flour was actually used efficiently [1].

**9.** Any two, explained. It may be due to unfilled vacancies [1], so wards are short-staffed and patient care or waiting times suffer [1]. It may be due to using less experienced, cheaper staff [1], which could raise the risk of errors [1]. Allow: cancelled training or reduced overtime that leaves work undone.

**10.** Any two, analysed. Forecasts quickly become out of date when technology and demand change fast [1], so budgets based on them give poor targets and misleading variances [1]. Budgets can make managers rigid [1], so they may reject a new opportunity, such as a promising product, because it was not budgeted for [1]. Allow: time spent preparing budgets that soon need revising.

**11.** Incremental budgets carry forward past spending without asking whether it is still needed [1], so money may still go to activities with few users [1]. Zero budgeting makes managers justify every item [1], which could free funds for services residents now use more, or help the council meet spending cuts [1].

**12.** For the change: flexible budgets adjust for the number of guests, so managers are not blamed for higher costs caused by more bookings [1]; this gives a fairer picture of cost control [1]; managers who help set budgets are more likely to be committed to them [1]; their local knowledge makes budgets more realistic [1].
Against: managers involved in setting budgets may build in slack to make targets easy [1]; flexible budgets need costs split into fixed and variable, which takes time and skill [1]; moving away from incremental budgets does not on its own remove past waste, which zero budgeting would address [1].
Judgement: the change is likely to improve performance by making budgets fairer and more realistic [1], but the finance director should check managers' proposals to limit slack [1]. The improvement depends on how much guest numbers vary and on managers' willingness to set challenging targets [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Labelling a cost above budget as favourable.
- Comparing actual costs with the original budget when output has changed.
- Calculating variances but not explaining their likely causes.
- Assuming a favourable variance is always good.
- Evaluating a budgeting method without referring to the organisation in the question.
