## Questions

**1.** Define short-run aggregate supply and explain why the SRAS curve slopes upwards. **[3]**

**2.** In Country X, AD and SRAS (real output, in units) are as follows. LRAS is vertical at 700.

| Price level | 100 | 110 | 120 | 130 | 140 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| AD | 800 | 750 | 700 | 650 | 600 |
| SRAS | 600 | 650 | 700 | 750 | 800 |

**(a)** State the equilibrium price level and real output. **[2]**
**(b)** AD rises by 100 at every price level. Find the new short-run equilibrium and the percentage changes in the price level and real output. **[4]**
**(c)** Explain the long-run adjustment and give the long-run price level. **[3]**

**3.** Distinguish between the Keynesian and neo-classical approaches to long-run aggregate supply. **[4]**

**4.** For each event, state whether SRAS, LRAS or both shift, and in which direction.
**(i)** A subsidy on firms' energy use. **(ii)** A training programme that raises productivity. **(iii)** A storm destroys one season's crops. **(iv)** Emigration reduces the labour force permanently. **[4]**

**5.** In Country Y the price level is 100 and real output 2,500 in year 1. After a rise in AD, the price level is 104 and real output 2,650 in year 2. Calculate real growth, inflation, and the growth of nominal GDP (price level × real output / 100). **[5]**

**6.** Explain two assumptions of the AD/AS model and explain how one of them limits the conclusions you can draw. **[6]**

**7.** Country J has output 5% below potential, unemployment of 10% and inflation of 0.8%. Country K has output 4% above potential, unemployment of 3.2% and inflation of 6.5%. Using AD/AS, explain which policy approach suits each country, and one limit of this data. **[6]**

**8.** In Country Z the AS curve is flat at a price level of 100 up to output 800, rises with output = 8 × price level up to a price level of 125 (output 1,000), then is vertical. AD starts at output = 940 − 2P.
**(a)** AD shifts to output = 1,000 − 2P. Find the new equilibrium and the percentage changes. **[3]**
**(b)** AD then shifts to output = 1,100 − 2P. Find the new equilibrium and the percentage changes. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why the results differ. **[2]**

**9.** Evaluate whether supply-side policies are more appropriate than demand-side policies for managing an economy. **[10]**

**10.** In Country W, AD is output = 1,000 − 4P and SRAS is output = 6P. LRAS is vertical at 600.
**(a)** A cost shock shifts SRAS to output = 6P − 90. Find the new equilibrium and the percentage changes. **[4]**
**(b)** The government raises AD to restore output of 600. Find the price level and the new AD equation. **[2]**
**(c)** Comment on the outcome. **[2]**

**11.** Evaluate how an increase in aggregate demand may affect economic growth, unemployment and inflation. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** SRAS is the total real output firms will supply at each price level when some costs, such as wages, are fixed [1]. If prices rise while wage costs are fixed, profit per unit rises [1], so firms increase output, giving an upward slope [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* the link from fixed costs to profit to output is the explanation; a statement that "supply rises with price" repeats the question.

**2. (a)** AD equals SRAS where both are 700 [1]: price level **120**, output **700** [1].
**(b)** New AD is 1,400 − 5P and SRAS is 100 + 5P, so they are equal where 10P = 1,300 [1]. New equilibrium price level **130**, output **750** [1]. Price level +10/120 = **8.3%** [1]. Output +50/700 = **7.1%** [1].
**(c)** Output 750 is above potential 700, so labour and inputs are scarce [1] and wages and costs rise, shifting SRAS left [1]. Equilibrium returns to output 700 on the new AD (1,400 − 5P): P = 140 [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* percentage changes should use the starting value as the base, and a long-run answer should end with the stated price level, not only a direction.

**3.** Neo-classical: LRAS is vertical at potential output [1] because wages and prices are flexible, so markets clear in the long run [1]. Keynesian: LRAS can slope upwards or be flat at low output [1] because wages are sticky downwards, so the economy can stay below full employment [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* the contrast needs both the shape and the reason (flexibility of wages and prices); a shape alone describes rather than distinguishes.

**4.** (i) SRAS shifts right [1]. (ii) Both shift right [1]. (iii) SRAS shifts left [1]. (iv) Both shift left [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* the long-run test is whether productive capacity changes. A one-season shock or a subsidy does not change it.

**5.** Real growth = 2,650/2,500 − 1 = **6.0%** [1]. Inflation = 104/100 − 1 = **4.0%** [1]. Nominal GDP year 1 = 100 × 2,500/100 = **2,500** [1]. Year 2 = 104 × 2,650/100 = **2,756** [1]. Nominal growth = 2,756/2,500 − 1 = **10.24%** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* show that nominal growth is about real growth plus inflation (1.06 × 1.04 = 1.1024) as a check on your own working.

**6.** Assumption one: a single price level and output represent the whole economy [1], so the model hides which sectors or groups gain or lose [1]. Assumption two: wages and costs are sticky in the short run but flexible in the long run [1], which gives SRAS its slope and LRAS its vertical shape [1]. Limitation: if wages are in fact sticky for years, the long-run return to potential output may take far longer than the diagram suggests, [1] so a policy conclusion could be wrong [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* naming an assumption earns less than saying what it does to the curves and so to the conclusion.

**7.** J: weak AD with spare capacity, high unemployment and low inflation [1], so a demand-side boost shifting AD right raises output with little inflation risk [1]. K: output above capacity, low unemployment and rising prices [1], so restraining AD, or raising capacity with supply-side measures, reduces pressure on prices [1]. Both countries might also need supply-side action to raise potential output over time [1]. Limit: the data do not show the cause of the problem, for example whether SRAS or AD moved, so the best policy is uncertain [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* tie each recommendation to a figure in the data, such as the output gap or inflation rate, rather than naming a policy in general terms.

**8. (a)** At P = 100, AD = 940 − 200 = 740, which is on the flat section, so equilibrium is (100, 740). After the shift AD = 800 at P = 100, which is the end of the flat section [1]: equilibrium **(100, 800)**. Output +60/740 = **8.1%** [1]. Price level **0%** [1].
**(b)** On the rising section: 1,100 − 2P = 8P, so P = 110 [1], output = 880. Output +80/800 = **10.0%** [1]. Price level +10/100 = **10.0%** [1].
**(c)** With spare capacity, extra demand is met by extra output at stable costs [1]. As capacity is approached, bottlenecks raise costs, so more of the demand boost shows up as higher prices [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* identify which section of the curve each equilibrium is on before calculating, so the working matches the zone.

**9.** Supply-side policies aim to shift LRAS right [1], raising potential output and growth and often lowering the price level [1]. They tackle causes where weak capacity, skills or high costs hold the economy back [1]. Demand-side policies shift AD [1]. They are effective when there is spare capacity and weak demand [1], but at full capacity they raise the price level more than output [1]. Evaluation: supply-side policies are slow and their results uncertain [1], and may cost the government large sums [1]. Demand-side policies work faster but often only temporarily [1]. The better choice depends on diagnosis: demand shortfall favours demand-side policy, sustained growth favours supply-side, and many economies need both [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* a one-sided case is limited. Answers that reach a conditional judgement ("it depends on whether the problem is demand or capacity") show evaluation.

**10. (a)** Set 1,000 − 4P = 6P − 90: P = **109** [1], output = **564** [1]. Price level +9/100 = **9.0%** [1]. Output −36/600 = **−6.0%** [1].
**(b)** Restoring output 600 on the new SRAS: 600 = 6P − 90, so P = **115** [1]. New AD passes through (115, 600): output = **1,060 − 4P** [1].
**(c)** Output is restored [1] but the price level is 15.0% above its start, so demand policy trades higher inflation for higher output; a supply-side response that cuts costs would avoid this [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* using the numbers you have calculated, such as the 15.0% rise, makes the comment specific rather than general.

**11.** An AD rise shifts the AD curve right, raising equilibrium output (growth) [1], which needs more labour so unemployment falls [1], and raises the price level (inflation) [1]. A diagram with the old and new equilibrium labelled on both axes [1]. The size of each effect depends on spare capacity: with a flat or shallow AS, output and jobs rise with little inflation [1]; near capacity, a steep AS means mostly inflation [1]. In the short run output rises above potential, but on the neo-classical view wages rise and SRAS shifts left, so the long-run effect is only a higher price level [1] and no lasting fall in unemployment [1]. A bigger or repeated rise, and any multiplier effect, increases the impact [1]. If workers expect higher inflation, SRAS shifts sooner and output gains fade faster [1]. Judgement: AD helps most in a deep recession with idle resources, but it cannot raise potential output [1], so sustained growth also needs supply-side action [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* addressing all three variables, growth, unemployment and inflation, and then separating short run from long run, is what turns a list of effects into an evaluated answer.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "supply" or "demand" as a vague cause without naming the curve that shifts.
- Treating a price-level change as a shift.
- Giving a percentage change without stating the base.
- Forgetting the long-run adjustment when output is above potential.
- Using a Keynesian curve for a neo-classical question, or the reverse.
- Describing a diagram without actually using it to answer the question.
- Writing a one-sided case on policy questions.
- Quoting data in the answer but not comparing it with potential output or the inflation rate.
