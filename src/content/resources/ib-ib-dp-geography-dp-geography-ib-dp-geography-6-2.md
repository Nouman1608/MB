It is examined in Paper 1 if your school teaches this option. The [6.2 revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-geography-food-health-food-systems-and-spread-of-diseases-revision-notes/) already describe types of food system, the three diffusion types and barriers, malaria, the cholera and Ebola cases, and the epidemiological transition. ## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Explain how food systems differ and how they connect places.
- Explain how the structure of a food system affects food security.
- Explain how diseases spread through space, and the factors that speed or slow them.
- Explain the links between food production and disease.

## How food systems transmit shocks

Global food trade concentrates supply. A small number of countries export most of the world's traded wheat, maize, rice and soybeans, and a small number of firms handle much of that trade. When a major exporter has a poor harvest, restricts exports or is affected by conflict, world prices rise quickly and the effect is felt most by countries that import a large share of their staples.

During the 2007 to 2008 price spike, several exporters restricted grain exports to protect their own consumers, which pushed world prices higher still. In 2022, war in Ukraine disrupted Black Sea exports of wheat, maize and sunflower oil. Both episodes show that globalised food systems spread risk between places as well as spreading food.

### Worked example 1: import dependency

The **import dependency ratio** measures how much of a country's food supply comes from abroad:

**import dependency ratio = imports / (production + imports - exports) x 100**

Invented cereal data for one year, in millions of tonnes:

| | Country M | Country N |
|---|---|---|
| Production | 4.0 | 30.0 |
| Imports | 6.0 | 2.0 |
| Exports | 0.0 | 7.0 |

- Country M: 6.0 / (4.0 + 6.0 - 0.0) x 100 = 6.0 / 10.0 x 100 = **60%**.
- Country N: 2.0 / (30.0 + 2.0 - 7.0) x 100 = 2.0 / 25.0 x 100 = **8%**.

Now suppose world cereal prices rise from 250 to 400 currency units per tonne. Country M's import bill rises from 6.0 million x 250 = 1,500 million to 6.0 million x 400 = 2,400 million, an extra 900 million. Country N, a net exporter, may even gain from higher export prices. Exposure to the same global shock therefore depends on the structure of each country's food system.

## How diffusion unfolds over time

Torsten Hagerstrand's work on spatial diffusion, first applied to the spread of innovations, is often used to describe the spread of an infection. Over time an outbreak passes through four broad stages:

1. **Primary stage:** a few cases cluster around the source.
2. **Diffusion stage:** rapid spread outwards and the appearance of new centres some distance away.
3. **Condensing stage:** growth continues everywhere, and the difference between the source and other areas narrows.
4. **Saturation stage:** spread slows because few susceptible people remain, or because barriers such as vaccination take effect.

Plotted as cumulative cases against time, this gives an **S-shaped curve**: slow start, steep middle, levelling off. Plotted as new cases per week, it gives the familiar **epidemic curve**, which rises to a peak and falls. Be clear which graph a source shows: a falling epidemic curve means fewer new cases, not fewer total cases.

### Worked example 2: hierarchy or contagion?

An invented outbreak is recorded in five places.

| Place | Population | Distance from source (km) | Week of first case |
|---|---|---|---|
| Capital (source) | 2,000,000 | 0 | 1 |
| Second city | 600,000 | 400 | 2 |
| Rural district beside capital | 30,000 | 25 | 4 |
| Regional town | 80,000 | 380 | 4 |
| Remote village | 2,000 | 60 | 7 |

Reading the table: the second city, 400 km away, was reached in week 2, before the rural district only 25 km from the source. Distance alone does not explain the order; **settlement size** does. That is the signature of **hierarchical diffusion**, carried by air, rail and long-distance road links between large cities. Once in each city, spread to nearby places such as the rural district is **contagious**. The remote village, though close to the capital, was reached last, perhaps because it has few transport links: an example of a partial barrier.

## Network distance, not straight-line distance

Transport networks shrink **effective distance** between well-connected places. Two capitals linked by frequent flights are, for disease, closer together than a capital and a village 50 km away without a paved road. This explains why new infections often appear in distant cities before nearby rural areas, and why air hubs are central to surveillance. In an answer, link the speed of spread to the type and frequency of connection, not only to kilometres.

## Environment and vector-borne disease

A vector-borne disease can only spread where its vector can survive and breed, and where people and vectors come into contact.

- **Temperature** controls whether the vector survives and how fast the parasite or virus develops inside it.
- **Rainfall and standing water** provide breeding sites, so transmission often peaks in and after the rainy season.
- **Land use** matters. Irrigation channels and dams create standing water. Urban areas with water stored in open containers and discarded tyres provide breeding sites for *Aedes* mosquitoes, which carry dengue. Dengue is therefore an increasingly urban disease, unlike malaria, which is more often rural.
- **Human movement** reintroduces disease to areas that had controlled it.

These links mean disease maps often resemble maps of climate and land use, but the overlap is never complete, because housing quality, bed nets, drainage and health care decide whether exposure turns into infection.

## The food chain as a pathway for disease

The food system can spread disease in three ways:

- **Food-borne outbreaks.** A contaminated product distributed through a supply chain can cause cases in many places at once, all traced back to one source. In Germany in 2011, a severe outbreak of a toxin-producing *E. coli* was traced to sprouted seeds. The spatial pattern is a scatter that follows distribution routes, not a ring around a source.
- **Livestock disease.** Highly pathogenic avian influenza (H5N1) has spread through poultry flocks via trade in birds and through wild bird migration, leading to large-scale culling and, occasionally, infection of people in close contact with birds.
- **Contact with wildlife.** Clearing forest and hunting wild animals increase contact between people and animal reservoirs, raising the risk of new zoonotic infections.

## Annotated model answer

*Explain how the structure of a country's food system affects its vulnerability to global food price shocks.* **(sample paragraph)**

"A country that imports a large share of its staple cereals has a high import dependency ratio, so a rise in world prices directly raises the cost of its food supply **[mechanism linked to a measure]**. If poor households spend a large share of their income on food, higher prices reduce access, the dimension of food security most sensitive to price **[link to food security]**. In contrast, a net exporter may gain from higher prices, while a country with a large subsistence sector is partly shielded because many households grow their own food **[comparison of structures]**. Vulnerability therefore depends on both national dependency and how households obtain their food **[conclusion across scales]**."

## Common mistakes

- Reading a falling epidemic curve as a fall in total cases.
- Explaining spread only by straight-line distance, ignoring transport links.
- Writing that the climate "causes" vector-borne disease, rather than enabling the vector.
- Treating globalised food systems only as a benefit, or only as a risk.
- Forgetting that food-borne outbreaks follow distribution routes.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** spread by naming the diffusion type and the mechanism that drives it.
- **Analyse** a table or map by ordering places by time and testing distance against settlement size.
- **Distinguish** an S-curve from an epidemic curve, and expansion from relocation diffusion, precisely.
- **Examine** the role of a food system by tracing both the benefits it brings and the risks it carries.
