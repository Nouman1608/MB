This guide goes deeper into section 3.4.2 Identification of common gases of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Chemistry (9202) specification, Version 6.2, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. It covers statements a to e: the tests for hydrogen, oxygen, carbon dioxide, ammonia and chlorine. The qualification is untiered, so every candidate needs all of it. The wider unit, with purity and chromatography, is taught in the [purity, chromatography and gas tests guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-chemistry-purity-chromatography-and-gas-tests/).

## What the specification asks

| Statement | Gas | What you must know |
|---|---|---|
| 3.4.2 a | Hydrogen | A lighted splint placed near the gas gives a pop |
| 3.4.2 b | Oxygen | A glowing splint relights in a test tube of the gas |
| 3.4.2 c | Carbon dioxide | It turns limewater (calcium hydroxide solution) cloudy white |
| 3.4.2 d | Ammonia | Sharp, choking smell; turns damp red litmus blue; forms white smoke of ammonium chloride with hydrogen chloride gas from concentrated hydrochloric acid |
| 3.4.2 e | Chlorine | Sharp, choking smell; turns damp blue litmus red, then bleaches it white |

Every test has two halves: **what you do** and **what you observe**. An answer with only one half earns at most half the marks.

## Why each test works

Knowing the chemistry behind a test lets you explain it, not just recall it, and stops you mixing up similar tests.

### Hydrogen: the squeaky pop

Hydrogen burns. When a lighted splint meets hydrogen mixed with the air at the mouth of a test tube, the mixture burns so quickly that it makes a small explosion, heard as a pop:

2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l)

The product is water, which is why a little condensation can sometimes be seen in the tube afterwards. The splint is held **at the mouth** of the tube: the oxygen for the pop comes from the air there.

### Oxygen: the splint relights

A glowing splint is wood that is barely burning. Air is only about one-fifth oxygen; in a tube of much purer oxygen, burning speeds up so much that the splint bursts back into flame. Oxygen does not burn itself: it supports burning. That is the key difference from hydrogen, which is the fuel in its own test.

### Carbon dioxide: limewater turns cloudy

Limewater is a solution of calcium hydroxide. Carbon dioxide reacts with it to form calcium carbonate, which is insoluble, so tiny particles of white solid make the liquid cloudy:

Ca(OH)₂(aq) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s) + H₂O(l)

The gas must be **bubbled through** the limewater, or shaken with it, so that it actually reaches the solution. This is the same reaction you meet in 3.5.1 e.

### Ammonia: alkaline in water

Ammonia gas cannot change dry litmus. The water on **damp** red litmus dissolves some ammonia, and dissolved ammonia produces hydroxide ions:

NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)

The hydroxide ions make the paper alkaline, so it turns blue. The second test brings two gases together: hydrogen chloride escaping from concentrated hydrochloric acid meets ammonia in the air and the two combine to form solid ammonium chloride. The solid forms as very fine particles, which is why it looks like white smoke:

NH₃(g) + HCl(g) → NH₄Cl(s)

### Chlorine: acid first, then bleach

Chlorine dissolves in the water on damp blue litmus to form an acidic solution, so the paper first turns red. Chlorine is also a bleach, so the dye is then destroyed and the paper turns white. The order matters: **red, then white**.

### Smell is not a test

Ammonia and chlorine both have a sharp, choking smell, so smell alone cannot tell them apart. Both are toxic. In practice, work with them in a fume cupboard and identify them with litmus.

## Where these gases come from in the course

Gas-test questions are often set inside other topics. You are told what reaction is happening and asked to name and test the gas made.

| Reaction elsewhere in the specification | Gas produced | Test |
|---|---|---|
| Metal + dilute acid (preparation of salts) | Hydrogen | Lighted splint: pop |
| Group 1 metal + water | Hydrogen | Lighted splint: pop |
| Electrolysis of sodium chloride solution | Hydrogen at the cathode, chlorine at the anode | Pop; damp blue litmus red then white |
| Electrolysis of copper sulfate solution (required practical) | Oxygen at the anode | Glowing splint relights |
| Carbonate + dilute acid, or heating a carbonate such as copper carbonate | Carbon dioxide | Limewater cloudy white |
| Haber process | Ammonia | Damp red litmus blue; white smoke with HCl |

## Worked example 1: two gases from one electrolysis

*Sodium chloride solution is electrolysed. Gas collects at both electrodes. Identify each gas and describe a test to confirm each one.* **[6]**

**Model answer**

- At the negative electrode (cathode) the gas is **hydrogen**. Hold a lighted splint at the mouth of the tube; **a pop is heard**.
- At the positive electrode (anode) the gas is **chlorine**. Hold **damp** blue litmus paper in the gas; it **turns red and then white** (bleached).

**Why it scores:** each gas is named, each test names the apparatus and its condition (lighted, damp), and each result is an observation. "Chlorine bleaches litmus" alone would lose the colour change; "test with a splint" would not say which splint.

## Worked example 2: identifying an unknown gas

*A colourless gas, X, has no smell. Damp red and damp blue litmus papers do not change colour. A lighted splint held at the mouth of a tube of X goes out without a pop. Identify X and describe a test that confirms it.*

**Reasoning**

1. No smell and no change to either litmus paper: X is not ammonia or chlorine.
2. No pop: X is not hydrogen.
3. The lighted splint went out, so X does not support burning strongly: X is not oxygen.
4. Of the five gases, only carbon dioxide is left. But a splint going out is **not** a test for carbon dioxide, because many gases put out a flame.

**Answer:** X is probably carbon dioxide. Bubble X through limewater: the limewater turns **cloudy white**.

## Worked example 3: one strip of litmus, two gases

*A student has only damp red litmus paper. Explain how it can still tell ammonia from chlorine.* **[3]**

- In ammonia the damp red litmus turns **blue**, because ammonia dissolves to give an alkaline solution.
- In chlorine the red litmus does not turn blue; it is **bleached white**, because chlorine is a bleach.
- So the two gases give clearly different results on the same paper.

This works because chlorine bleaches any colour of litmus. The red-then-white sequence in 3.4.2 e only appears with blue litmus, since blue litmus has to turn red first.

## Common mistakes

- Swapping the splints: **lighted** for hydrogen, **glowing** for oxygen.
- Writing "the splint goes out" as a result for carbon dioxide.
- Writing "limewater turns milky" without saying the gas was bubbled through it, or giving the colour as anything other than cloudy white.
- Leaving out **damp** before litmus. Dry litmus does not change.
- Giving only "red" for chlorine. The full result is red, then bleached white.
- Saying ammonia gives white smoke "with hydrochloric acid" without making clear that hydrogen chloride gas from **concentrated** acid is held near the ammonia.
- Using smell to distinguish ammonia from chlorine.

## Exam technique: command words

- **Describe a test for...** Give the method and the result. Lay it out as two short clauses: *"Bubble the gas through limewater; the limewater turns cloudy white."*
- **Identify** or **Name the gas...** One word is enough, but spell it correctly (chlorine, not chloride).
- **Explain why the litmus must be damp...** Give the reason, not a restatement: the gas must dissolve in water to act as an acid or alkali.
- **Suggest...** Apply the tests to an unfamiliar situation, such as a gas from a reaction you have not met. Use the clues given (smell, colour, the reactants) to rule gases in or out.
- **Write an equation...** The limewater and ammonium chloride equations are the two most often asked. Add state symbols if the question asks for them.
