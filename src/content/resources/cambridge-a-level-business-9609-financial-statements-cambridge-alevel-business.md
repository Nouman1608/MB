This guide goes deeper into **section 10.1 Financial statements** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Business 9609 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. It is A Level content. The syllabus says candidates are not expected to draw up a set of accounts, but they must understand them, amend them and explain the impact of a given change.

The [financial statements and analysis of published accounts guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-financial-statements-and-analysis-of-published-accounts/) sets out each statement with one example. This page concentrates on the skill most often tested: tracing a change through both statements. Afterwards, use the [financial statements revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-financial-statements-cambridge-alevel-business-revision-notes/) and the [financial statements practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-financial-statements-cambridge-alevel-business-practice/).

All businesses are invented. Figures are in $000 unless stated.

## What the syllabus asks for

| Part | Content |
|---|---|
| 10.1.1 Statement of profit or loss | Meaning and purpose; contents (revenue, cost of sales, gross profit, expenses, profit from operations, taxation, profit for the year, dividends, retained earnings); amendment; impact of a given change |
| 10.1.2 Statement of financial position | Meaning and purpose; contents (non-current assets, current assets, current liabilities, net current assets, net assets, non-current liabilities, reserves, equity); amendment; relationships with the statement of profit or loss |
| 10.1.3 Inventory valuation | Difficulties of valuing inventory; net realisable value method |
| 10.1.4 Depreciation | Role; impact of straight-line depreciation on both statements |

## Two statements, two questions

| | Statement of profit or loss | Statement of financial position |
|---|---|---|
| Question answered | How much profit was made **over a period**? | What does the business own and owe **on one date**? |
| Like | A video of the year | A photograph at the year end |
| Main users' interest | Performance and dividends | Liquidity, solvency, value of the owners' stake |

Profit is not cash. A business can be profitable yet short of cash if customers pay late or if it has spent heavily on inventory or equipment. This is why both statements are needed.

## The reference company: Linwood Kitchens plc

**Statement of profit or loss, year ended 31 March**

| Line | $000 |
|---|---|
| Revenue | 6,000 |
| Cost of sales | (3,900) |
| **Gross profit** | 2,100 |
| Expenses | (1,500) |
| **Profit from operations** | 600 |
| Taxation (25%) | (150) |
| **Profit for the year** | 450 |
| Dividends | (200) |
| **Retained earnings for the year** | 250 |

**Statement of financial position at 31 March**

| Line | $000 |
|---|---|
| Non-current assets | 3,400 |
| Inventory 600, trade receivables 500, cash 100 | |
| **Current assets** | 1,200 |
| **Current liabilities** | (800) |
| **Net current assets** | 400 |
| Non-current liabilities | (1,000) |
| **Net assets** | 2,800 |
| Issued shares | 1,500 |
| Reserves | 1,300 |
| **Equity** | 2,800 |

Check: 3,400 + 400 − 1,000 = 2,800 = 1,500 + 1,300.

## Amending the statement of profit or loss

**Method:** find the line that changes, change it, then recalculate **every line below it**. Lines above are unaffected.

| Change | First line affected | Effect on profit for the year |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue rises 10%, volume up, cost of sales rises in proportion | Revenue and cost of sales | Revenue 6,600; cost of sales 4,290; gross profit 2,310; profit from operations 810; tax 202.5; profit for the year 607.5 |
| Rent rises by 120 | Expenses | Profit from operations 480; tax 120; profit for the year 360 |
| Tax rate rises to 30% | Taxation | Tax 180; profit for the year 420 |
| Dividends double to 400 | Dividends | Profit for the year unchanged at 450; retained earnings 50 |

Note the last row: dividends come **after** profit for the year, so they change retained earnings but not profit.

Also note the first row: a 10% rise in revenue raised profit from operations by 35% (from 600 to 810), because expenses did not change. This effect is worth explaining in "impact" questions.

## Amending the statement of financial position

**Every transaction has two effects.** Find both, then check that net assets still equal equity.

| Transaction at Linwood | Effect 1 | Effect 2 | Net assets |
|---|---|---|---|
| Buys equipment for 80 in cash | Non-current assets +80 | Cash −80 (net current assets 320) | Unchanged at 2,800 |
| Buys equipment for 300 with a 5-year loan | Non-current assets +300 | Non-current liabilities +300 | Unchanged |
| Buys inventory of 80 on credit | Inventory +80 | Current liabilities +80 | Unchanged |
| Customers pay 150 they owe | Cash +150 | Trade receivables −150 | Unchanged |
| Issues new shares for 500 cash | Cash +500 | Issued shares +500 | Rises to 3,300 |
| Repays 60 of loan from cash | Cash −60 | Non-current liabilities −60 | Unchanged |

Only transactions involving the owners (new shares) or profit change net assets. Swapping one asset for another, or borrowing to buy an asset, does not.

## Relationships between the two statements

Retained earnings are the main bridge:

```
Opening reserves               1,050
+ Retained earnings for year     250
= Closing reserves             1,300   (shown in equity)
```

Other links:

- **Inventory**: closing inventory is a current asset, and its value sets cost of sales, so it affects gross profit.
- **Trade receivables and payables**: arise from credit sales and credit purchases recorded in the statement of profit or loss.
- **Taxation**: charged as an expense; unpaid tax sits in current liabilities.
- **Depreciation**: an expense that reduces profit; it also reduces the net book value of non-current assets.
- **Interest**: an expense; it arises from non-current liabilities.

## Inventory valuation

**Why it is difficult:** goods may be damaged, obsolete or out of fashion; prices change between buying and selling; large or varied stock is hard to count; and partly finished goods need judgement about how much of the cost to include.

**Net realisable value (NRV)** = estimated selling price − costs needed to complete and sell. Inventory is valued at the lower of cost and NRV.

**Worked example: Linwood's end-of-line worktops.** Linwood holds three types (figures in dollars):

| Item | Units | Cost each | Selling price each | Cost to sell each | NRV each | Value used |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Granite | 40 | 450 | 700 | 50 | 650 | Cost: 40 × 450 = 18,000 |
| Laminate | 120 | 90 | 85 | 15 | 70 | NRV: 120 × 70 = 8,400 |
| Oak | 30 | 300 | 320 | 40 | 280 | NRV: 30 × 280 = 8,400 |

Value at cost would be 18,000 + 10,800 + 9,000 = 37,800. Value used is 18,000 + 8,400 + 8,400 = **34,800**. The write-down of 3,000 increases cost of sales, so gross profit falls by 3,000, and current assets fall by the same amount. Compare **item by item**: Granite's surplus NRV cannot offset losses on other items.

## Depreciation

**Role:** spreads the cost of a non-current asset over the years it helps to earn revenue, so profit each year is not distorted by one large purchase. Depreciation is **not** a cash payment and does not create a fund to replace the asset.

**Straight-line:** annual charge = (cost − residual value) ÷ useful life.

**Worked example: Linwood's cutting machine** (dollars): cost 90,000, residual value 10,000, life 4 years. Annual charge = 80,000 ÷ 4 = 20,000.

| Year | Charge in statement of profit or loss | Net book value at year end |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 20,000 | 70,000 |
| 2 | 20,000 | 50,000 |
| 3 | 20,000 | 30,000 |
| 4 | 20,000 | 10,000 |

**Judgement in estimates.** If Linwood assumed a 5-year life, the charge would be 16,000, so profit would be 4,000 higher each year and the machine's book value higher. Depreciation depends on estimates of life and residual value, which is one reason published accounts must be read with care.

## Annotated model answer

*Explain the impact on Linwood's financial statements of buying equipment for $300,000 using a new five-year bank loan.*

> "On the statement of financial position, non-current assets rise by $300,000 and non-current liabilities rise by $300,000, so net assets and equity are unchanged. In the statement of profit or loss, the loan adds interest to expenses, reducing profit from operations, and the equipment will be depreciated each year, which also lowers profit. If the equipment raises output or cuts costs, revenue or cost of sales may improve enough to offset these charges."

It gives both effects on each statement and states the condition for the longer-term impact.

## Common mistakes

- Changing only the line that is altered and not recalculating the lines below it.
- Deducting dividends before profit for the year.
- Recording only one side of a balance sheet transaction.
- Comparing cost and NRV for the stock as a whole rather than item by item.
- Calling depreciation money saved for a replacement.

## Exam technique

**Calculate** questions need a layout with labelled lines. **Explain the impact** needs the effect on each statement, line by line. **Analyse** links the change to a result for stakeholders, such as lower dividends or weaker liquidity. **Evaluate** questions on financial statements usually ask whether the statements give a reliable picture: mention estimates in inventory and depreciation.
