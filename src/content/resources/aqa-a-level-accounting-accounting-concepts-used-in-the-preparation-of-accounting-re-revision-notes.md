## 3.5 The ten general accounting concepts

| Concept | What it says | Effect on the figures | Typical exam trigger |
|---|---|---|---|
| Money measurement | Record only items with a reliable money value | Staff skill, reputation, location not recorded | "Why is the workforce not an asset?" |
| Duality | Every transaction has two equal effects | Debits = credits; assets = capital + liabilities | Any ledger entry |
| Cost | Record assets at what they cost | Premises shown at cost, not market value | Asset now worth more than cost |
| Going concern | Business will continue for the foreseeable future | Cost less depreciation, not break-up value | Business closing down |
| Accruals | Match income and expenses to the period they relate to | Accruals, prepayments, depreciation, closing inventory | Expense unpaid or paid in advance |
| Consistency | Use the same methods each period | Profits comparable year to year | Owner wants to change method |
| Prudence | Don't overstate assets or profit; recognise foreseeable losses | Inventory at NRV if lower; provision for doubtful debts | Damaged goods, doubtful customer |
| Materiality | Only items that would affect users' decisions need strict treatment | Small items expensed, not depreciated | Cheap tool or stationery |
| Realisation | Revenue recognised when earned (goods accepted by the customer) | No revenue on orders or on unaccepted goods | Sale or return, deposits |
| Business entity | Business and owner are separate | Private spending = drawings; owner's money in = capital | Owner pays bills personally |

**Memory hook:** three concepts tell you *how much* to record (cost, money measurement, prudence), two tell you
*when* (accruals, realisation), two tell you *whose* and *how* (business entity, duality), and three apply to
the accounts as a whole (going concern, consistency, materiality).

## 3.5 Concepts applied to the named situations

| Situation | Main concepts | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| Preparing financial statements | All, especially accruals, prudence, business entity | Adjust draft profit item by item and name the concept |
| Asset valuation | Cost, going concern, prudence | Cost (less depreciation); lower figure if impaired |
| Depreciation of non-current assets | Accruals, consistency, prudence | Spread cost over useful life; keep the method |
| Inventories | Prudence, cost, accruals | Lower of cost and NRV, item by item |
| Recording purchase of non-current assets | Cost, accruals, materiality | Capitalise all costs of getting the asset ready for use |
| Recording transactions in ledger accounts | Duality, business entity, money measurement | Equal debit and credit; owner's items via capital or drawings |
| Goods on sale or return | Realisation, prudence | Unaccepted goods out of revenue, into inventory at cost |

## Method boxes

### Inventory at the lower of cost and NRV

```
1. For each item: NRV = selling price - costs to complete and sell
2. Compare NRV with cost for THAT item
3. Use the lower figure x quantity
4. Add the item values
5. Write-down = total cost - total value used
```

### Goods on sale or return at the year end

```
1. Find goods still held by customers, not yet accepted
2. Revenue:            reduce by selling price
3. Trade receivables:  reduce by selling price
4. Closing inventory:  increase by cost (or NRV if lower)
5. Profit falls by:    selling price - cost  (the unrealised profit)
```

Mark-up on cost: cost = selling price ÷ (1 + mark-up). Margin on selling price: cost = selling price ×
(1 − margin).

### Capital or revenue expenditure?

| Capital (debit the asset account) | Revenue (income statement) |
|---|---|
| Purchase price | Insurance, road tax |
| Delivery and installation | Fuel, power, consumables |
| Legal fees on buying premises | Repairs and maintenance |
| Modifications that make it fit for use | Annual service contracts |

Immaterial items with a long life may still be expensed (materiality).

### Correcting a draft profit

```
Draft profit
+ items wrongly treated as expenses (private spending, capital items)
- unrecorded expenses (accruals), depreciation on newly capitalised assets
- inventory write-downs, unrealised profit on sale or return
= Corrected profit
```

Name the concept on every line. Give two things for each adjustment: the figure, then the reason. Show additions and
deductions clearly, starting from the draft profit, so that a slip in one figure still lets the marker give
own-figure credit for the corrected total. Remember the knock-on effects in the statement of financial position:
private spending raises drawings, accrued expenses appear in other payables, and inventory write-downs and sale or
return goods change the closing inventory figure.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Prudence vs materiality.** Prudence is about not overstating; materiality is about whether an item matters
  enough to need strict treatment.
- **Realisation vs accruals.** Realisation decides *when revenue is earned*. Accruals matches expenses and
  income to the period.
- **Cost vs going concern.** Cost gives the starting figure; going concern justifies not switching to break-up
  values.
- **Going concern vs consistency.** Going concern is an assumption about the business's future; consistency is
  about using the same methods.
- **Drawings vs expenses.** Anything for the owner's private benefit is drawings, even if paid from the business
  bank.
- **NRV vs selling price.** NRV deducts the costs still needed to sell the goods.

## Quick self-test

1. A business charges a £20 calculator to office expenses rather than to equipment. Which concept allows this?
2. An item cost £60. It can be sold for £75 but needs £20 of repairs first. At what value per unit is it
   included in inventory?
3. Goods with a selling price of £1 500 were sent on sale or return and recorded as sales. Mark-up is 25% on
   cost. The customer has not accepted them. By how much is profit overstated?
4. Is a £400 delivery charge on a new machine capital or revenue expenditure?
5. The owner pays the business rent from a private account. Give the double entry and the concept.
6. A customer places an order in May; the goods are delivered and accepted in June. In which month is revenue
   recognised, and under which concept?
7. A trader changes depreciation method every year to keep profit steady. Which concept is breached?
8. A business will close in two months. Which concept no longer applies, and how are assets valued?
9. A machine costs £15 000, has a residual value of £3 000 and a life of 4 years. Calculate the straight-line
   depreciation per year and name the concept it applies.
10. 40 units cost £12 each. Their NRV is now £9.50 each. Calculate the write-down.
11. Why is a loyal customer base not shown as an asset?
12. A customer who owes £900 has gone into liquidation. Which concept requires action now?

### Answers

1. Materiality.
2. NRV = 75 − 20 = £55, which is lower than cost of £60, so **£55**.
3. Cost = 1 500 ÷ 1.25 = £1 200; profit overstated by 1 500 − 1 200 = **£300**.
4. **Capital** (cost concept: part of getting the asset ready for use).
5. **Dr Rent, Cr Capital**; business entity (the owner has put money into the business).
6. **June**, when the goods are accepted; realisation.
7. **Consistency**.
8. **Going concern**; assets are valued at what they are expected to realise.
9. (15 000 − 3 000) ÷ 4 = **£3 000 per year**; accruals (matching cost to the periods that benefit).
10. 40 × (12.00 − 9.50) = **£100**.
11. Money measurement: it has no reliable money value.
12. Prudence: write the debt off as irrecoverable (recognise the loss now).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a concept with no explanation of its effect on profit or on a statement of financial position figure.
- Comparing total cost with total NRV rather than item by item, so losses on some lines are hidden.
- Using selling price as NRV and forgetting the costs to sell.
- Adding sale or return goods back to inventory at selling price instead of cost.
- Removing sale or return goods from revenue but leaving them in trade receivables.
- Mixing up mark-up on cost and margin on selling price when finding cost.
- Charging delivery or installation to the income statement, or capitalising insurance and fuel.
- Treating the owner's private bills as business expenses rather than drawings.
- Describing prudence as "deliberately understating profit"; it means not overstating.
- Saying "realisation means when the cash is received".
