## The economic problem

**Unlimited wants, limited resources.** Therefore choices, and therefore **opportunity cost** — the **next best** alternative forgone.

Factors of production: land (rent), labour (wages), **capital** (interest), enterprise (profit). **Capital means machinery and equipment, not money.**

**Production possibility curves** (a teaching aid -- the specification does not name this diagram):
on the curve = efficient; inside = unemployment or inefficiency; outside = unattainable. An outward
shift is economic growth. These notes assume a straight-line curve (constant opportunity cost) for
simplicity; real PPCs are typically bowed outward.

**Main economic groups** — consumers, producers and the government — are **interdependent**: consumers buy from producers and supply labour to them; producers pay wages and supply goods; government taxes both and provides public services and regulation in return.

**The three fundamental questions** every economy must answer: what to produce, how to produce it, and for whom. A **market economy** leaves these to the price mechanism; a **planned economy** leaves them to the state; a **mixed economy** — what almost every real economy actually is — combines both.

**Specialisation and exchange.** Specialisation raises output through practice, mechanisation and economies of scale, but creates interdependence, which requires exchange. Without money, trade needs a **double coincidence of wants** — each party wanting exactly what the other offers — which barter rarely delivers. Money solves this by acting as a medium of exchange, a measure of value, a store of value, and a means of deferred payment.

## Where this topic's content stops

Demand, supply, elasticity, competition and the role of money in financial markets are **Topic 2, The Role of Markets and Money** content (the second half of Component 01), not Introduction to Economics — see that topic's own notes. This topic's content is limited to the main economic groups, factors of production, and the basic economic problem (1.1 and 1.2) above.

## Worked example: classifying factors of production

A bakery uses a rented shop unit, hires two part-time staff, buys a
new industrial oven, and is run day-to-day by its owner, who
organises staff, buys ingredients and takes on the financial risk of
the business.

```
Rented shop unit:       Land      -> reward: Rent
Two part-time staff:    Labour    -> reward: Wages
New industrial oven:    Capital   -> reward: Interest
                         (capital is a man-made resource used to
                         produce further goods, not the money itself)
Owner organising staff,
buying ingredients and
taking on risk:          Enterprise -> reward: Profit
```

Practising this exact classification -- naming which factor a
described resource belongs to, then stating its reward -- is a more
reliable way to avoid the common capital/money confusion than
memorising the four definitions in isolation.

## Worked example: interdependence between economic groups

A question asks you to explain how consumers, producers and the
government are interdependent, using a specific example.

```
Consumers:   Buy goods from producers, and supply their labour to
             producers in exchange for wages.
Producers:   Pay wages to consumers (as workers) and supply goods
             and services back to them (as customers).
Government:  Taxes both consumers' income and producers' profits,
             and in return provides public services (e.g. roads,
             healthcare, education) and regulation that both groups
             rely on.
```

The mark is earned by describing the **two-way flow** between each
pair of groups, not simply listing what each group does in
isolation -- interdependence specifically means each group both gives
something to, and receives something from, the others.

## Command words for this topic

| Command word | What's expected |
|---|---|
| State / Identify | A single named fact — a factor of production, an economic system |
| Describe | The pattern or feature, without explaining why it exists |
| Explain | The reasoning chain — why interdependence exists, why money solves the double coincidence of wants |
| Discuss / Evaluate | A comparison of strengths and weaknesses, e.g. market vs planned vs mixed economies |

A question on economic systems that asks you to "discuss" the
advantages of a mixed economy over a purely market or purely planned
one expects both strengths **and** weaknesses named for the systems
being compared, not a one-sided description of the mixed economy's
benefits alone.

## Worked example: identifying an economic system from a scenario

A country's government sets production targets for factories, decides
what goods are made, and allocates resources directly rather than
through prices.

```
System identified:  Planned economy
Justification:       The government, rather than the price
                     mechanism, is making the "what, how, and for
                     whom" decisions directly -- setting production
                     targets and allocating resources itself, which
                     is the defining feature of a planned system
                     rather than a market one.
```

Recognising which of the three fundamental questions a described
scenario is answering, and by which mechanism (price vs state), is
the specific skill this sub-topic tests in scenario-based questions.

## Exam traps

- Calling money a factor of production.
- Confusing opportunity cost with "everything given up" rather than the *next best* alternative forgone.
- Describing a point inside the PPC as impossible rather than inefficient.

## Self-test

1. Define opportunity cost.
2. Name the three fundamental economic questions, and the three systems that answer them.
3. A country can produce 80 units of food or 40 units of machinery, or combinations between. It currently produces 50 food and 10 machinery. Is it on, inside, or outside its production possibility curve?
4. Explain why money solves the double coincidence of wants problem created by specialisation and exchange.

**Answers:** 1. The next best alternative forgone when a choice is made. 2. What, how, and for whom to produce; a market economy uses the price mechanism, a planned economy uses the state, and a mixed economy combines both. 3. The opportunity cost ratio is 2 food = 1 machinery. At 50 food, the curve allows (80 − 50) ÷ 2 = 15 machinery, but the economy produces only 10, so it lies inside the curve — resources are unemployed or used inefficiently. 4. Barter requires each party to want exactly what the other offers; money removes this requirement by acting as a medium of exchange that both parties accept, alongside serving as a measure of value, a store of value, and a means of deferred payment.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the three fundamental economic questions?**
Every economy must decide what to produce, how to produce it and for whom. A market economy answers through the price mechanism, a planned economy through the state, and a mixed economy combines both.

**What is the double coincidence of wants?**
Without money, trade needs each party to want exactly what the other has to offer at the same time. Money solves this problem by acting as a medium of exchange.

**How do you show interdependence between economic groups?**
Describe the two-way flow between each pair of groups: consumers buy from producers and supply labour to them, producers pay wages and supply goods, and the government taxes both and provides public services and regulation in return.

**How does specialisation raise output?**
Specialisation raises output through practice, mechanisation and economies of scale. It also creates interdependence, which requires exchange.
