This guide goes deeper on one section of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Biology (9610) specification, Version 5.1: **3.1.1 Biological molecules**, in Unit 1, The diversity of living organisms. Unit 1 is part of the International AS and counts towards the full International A-level.

The section is built on one idea. Large biological molecules are polymers made by joining small monomers with condensation reactions, and they are broken apart again by hydrolysis. Once you can draw that idea for sugars, lipids and proteins, most of the rest of the section is about relating structure to function. Test yourself afterwards with the [biological molecules practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-biology-9610-biological-molecules-practice/).

## What the specification asks

| Section | Content |
|---|---|
| 3.1.1.1 | Monomers and polymers; condensation and hydrolysis |
| 3.1.1.2 | Carbohydrates: glucose isomers, glycosidic bonds, maltose, sucrose, starch, cellulose; Benedict's and iodine tests |
| 3.1.1.3 | Lipids: triglycerides and ester bonds, saturated and unsaturated R-groups, phospholipids; the emulsion test |
| 3.1.1.4 | Proteins: amino acids, peptide bonds, hydrogen bonds and disulfide bridges, four levels of structure; the biuret test |

Two statements say what you must be able to do. When you are given the molecular structures, you should be able to show how carbohydrates not named in the specification are joined by condensation or broken down by hydrolysis. You should also be able to recognise types of lipid from diagrams and formulae, and relate the named properties of proteins to their structure.

## Monomers, polymers, condensation and hydrolysis

A **monomer** is a small unit that can be joined to many others. A **polymer** is the long molecule that results. The three monomer families here are monosaccharides, amino acids and (for nucleic acids, covered in another section) nucleotides.

**Condensation** joins two molecules with a chemical bond and eliminates a molecule of water. **Hydrolysis** breaks that bond in a reaction that uses a molecule of water. They are exact opposites.

A counting rule saves marks in calculations. When n monomers join in a single unbranched chain, n minus 1 bonds form and n minus 1 water molecules are released. Joining 250 glucose molecules into one chain releases 249 water molecules. Hydrolysing the chain completely uses 249 water molecules.

The same rule explains a mass calculation. Glucose has a relative molecular mass of 180. Maltose is two glucose molecules less one water (180 + 180 - 18 = 342).

## Carbohydrates

### Monosaccharides and the two glucose isomers

Common monosaccharides are glucose and fructose. Glucose exists as two isomers, **alpha-glucose** and **beta-glucose**. They have the same atoms but differ in the position of the hydroxyl group on carbon 1. In the diagrams the specification uses, the OH on carbon 1 of alpha-glucose is below the ring and in beta-glucose it is above. Draw this carefully, because the difference decides everything that follows.

### Disaccharides

Two monosaccharides join by condensation to form a **glycosidic bond**.

- **Maltose** is two alpha-glucose molecules.
- **Sucrose** is one alpha-glucose and one fructose.

### Polysaccharides

Many monosaccharides join by condensation to form a polysaccharide.

- **Starch** is made by condensation of alpha-glucose and is a mixture of amylose and amylopectin. Amylose is an unbranched chain that coils into a helix. Amylopectin is branched.
- **Cellulose** is made by condensation of beta-glucose.

### Structure and function in plant cells

Starch is the storage polysaccharide of plant cells. Its job is to hold glucose without affecting water movement, and its structure suits that job.

- It is a large molecule, so it is insoluble. It does not change the water potential of the cell and it does not diffuse out.
- The helix of amylose is compact, so a lot of glucose is stored in a small space.
- Amylopectin has many branch ends, so enzymes can hydrolyse the glycosidic bonds at many points at once and release glucose quickly when the cell needs it.

Cellulose is the structural polysaccharide of plant cell walls. Because the beta-glucose units alternate in orientation, the chain is straight rather than coiled. Hydrogen bonds link parallel chains into strong fibres, so the wall resists the pressure of water entering the cell. This is why cellulose is not simply "another starch": the single difference in the isomer produces a different shape and a different function.

### Tests for carbohydrates

- **Benedict's reagent** tests for reducing sugars. Heat the sample with the reagent in a water bath. A positive result moves from blue through green, yellow and orange to brick red. Glucose, fructose and maltose are reducing sugars.
- **Non-reducing sugars** such as sucrose give no change with Benedict's reagent. Boil a fresh sample with dilute acid, which hydrolyses the glycosidic bond, then neutralise the acid with sodium hydrogencarbonate and repeat the Benedict's test. A positive result now shows that a non-reducing sugar was present.
- **Iodine in potassium iodide solution** tests for starch. The colour changes from orange-brown to blue-black.

## Lipids

### Triglycerides

A **triglyceride** forms by condensation of one glycerol molecule and three fatty acid molecules. Three **ester bonds** form and three water molecules are released.

A fatty acid can be written RCOOH. The R-group is a hydrocarbon chain. It may be **saturated**, with no carbon-carbon double bonds, or **unsaturated**, with one or more double bonds. A double bond puts a kink in the chain.

Consequences you can use in explanations:

- Saturated chains are straight, pack closely and tend to be solid at room temperature.
- Unsaturated chains are kinked, pack less closely and tend to be liquid.
- All triglycerides are hydrophobic, store a lot of energy per gram and can be stored in a compact form.

### Phospholipids

In a **phospholipid**, one of the three fatty acids is replaced by a phosphate-containing group. The phosphate head is hydrophilic and the two fatty acid tails are hydrophobic. This split explains why phospholipids form a bilayer in water, with heads facing the water and tails facing inwards. The structure is the foundation of the plasma membrane.

### Emulsion test

Shake the sample with ethanol, then pour the ethanol into water. A cloudy white emulsion shows that lipid is present. Note that the test needs ethanol first and water second, and that it detects lipids, not sugars.

## Proteins

### Amino acids and peptide bonds

The monomers of proteins are **amino acids**. Each has an amino group (H2N), a carboxyl group (COOH), a hydrogen atom and an R-group attached to a central carbon. There are twenty amino acids common to all organisms and they differ only in their R-groups.

Two amino acids join by condensation to form a **peptide bond**, giving a **dipeptide** and a molecule of water. Many amino acids joined in this way form a **polypeptide**.

### Four levels of structure

| Level | What it is |
|---|---|
| Primary | The sequence of amino acids in the polypeptide chain |
| Secondary | Regular folding of the chain, such as the alpha helix, held by hydrogen bonds |
| Tertiary | Further folding of the whole chain into a three-dimensional shape, held by hydrogen bonds, disulfide bridges and other interactions between R-groups |
| Quaternary | Two or more polypeptide chains held together in one protein |

**Hydrogen bonds** are weak individually but there are many of them. **Disulfide bridges** are stronger covalent links between sulfur-containing R-groups. The primary structure decides where these bonds can form, so a change in one amino acid can change the shape, and a changed shape can change the function.

Link structure to function in the way the specification expects. An enzyme's active site depends on its tertiary structure. A protein such as haemoglobin works because its quaternary structure holds several chains together. Heating or extreme pH breaks hydrogen bonds, and the shape changes irreversibly. This is denaturation.

### Biuret test

Add sodium hydroxide, then dilute copper(II) sulfate solution. A colour change from blue to purple (lilac) shows that peptide bonds, and so protein, are present. No heating is needed.

## Worked example 1: building a molecule

**Question:** A tripeptide is made from three glycine molecules. Glycine has a relative molecular mass of 75. State the number of peptide bonds and calculate the relative molecular mass of the tripeptide.

1. Three amino acids joined in a chain make 3 - 1 = 2 peptide bonds.
2. Each bond releases one water molecule, so 2 water molecules are lost.
3. Mass of three glycine molecules: 3 x 75 = 225.
4. Mass of two water molecules: 2 x 18 = 36.
5. Relative molecular mass of tripeptide: 225 - 36 = **189**.

## Worked example 2: the sucrose test

**Question:** A student heats a sample with Benedict's reagent and the solution stays blue. The student then boils a second sample with dilute acid, neutralises it and heats it with Benedict's reagent, and an orange-red precipitate forms. State and explain the conclusion.

1. The first test is negative, so no reducing sugar was present in the sample.
2. The acid hydrolysed a glycosidic bond, releasing monosaccharides that are reducing sugars.
3. The second test is positive, so the sample contained a non-reducing sugar such as sucrose.
4. The neutralising step matters because Benedict's reagent will not work in acid.

## Common mistakes

- Writing "glucose" without saying alpha or beta when asked about starch or cellulose.
- Saying a condensation reaction "adds water". It releases water.
- Calling the bond in a disaccharide a "peptide" bond, or the bond in a triglyceride a "glycosidic" bond.
- Forgetting to neutralise before the Benedict's test on a hydrolysed sample.
- Saying a saturated fatty acid "has no hydrogen". It has the maximum number of hydrogen atoms and no double bonds in the chain.
- Saying hydrogen bonds are the only force holding the tertiary structure. Disulfide bridges are named in the specification too.
- Saying cellulose is insoluble because it is "big" and stopping there. Link the straight chains and hydrogen bonding to the strength of the cell wall.

## Exam technique

- **Name the bond and the reaction.** "Condensation forming a glycosidic bond" earns more than "the molecules join".
- **Link structure to property to function.** For example, branched amylopectin gives many ends, so glucose is released quickly.
- **For tests, give the reagent, the method and the positive result.** Include heating for Benedict's and the colour change.
- **Use the unfamiliar-molecule skill.** If a question gives you a new disaccharide, find the two monosaccharides and the bond that links them, then say what hydrolysis would produce.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to draw the full structure of the amino acid and glucose?**
You should be able to draw the general structure of an amino acid and recognise the structures of alpha-glucose and beta-glucose. In an examination, structures are usually provided, and the skill tested is to show condensation or hydrolysis using them.

**Why does the specification give the tests for reducing and non-reducing sugars together?**
The non-reducing test builds on the reducing test. You must first show a negative Benedict's result, then hydrolyse and test again. The comparison is what proves a non-reducing sugar.

**Is cholesterol part of this section?**
Cholesterol appears in the section on plasma membranes, 3.1.4.1, not here.
