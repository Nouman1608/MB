This guide covers **section 5.3 Monetary policy** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Economics 9708 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. It is AS Level content, and it is assumed knowledge for the A Level papers. The syllabus lists four statements: the "definition of monetary policy", the "tools of monetary policy: interest rates, money supply and credit regulations", the "distinction between expansionary and contractionary monetary policy", and "AD/AS analysis" of the impact of each on equilibrium national income, real output, the price level and employment.

The broader [monetary policy and supply-side policy study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-monetary-policy-and-supply-side-policy/) introduces each tool with calculations. This page goes further into the routes by which each tool reaches aggregate demand (AD), how the result depends on the shape of aggregate supply (AS), and how to write the chains of reasoning that Paper 2 rewards. All countries and figures below are invented.

## 5.3.1 A definition that earns both marks

A complete definition has three parts: **who** acts (the central bank or the government), **what** it changes (interest rates, the money supply or the rules on credit), and **why** (to influence aggregate demand and so meet objectives such as price stability, low unemployment and economic growth). A definition that mentions only interest rates, or that leaves out the purpose, is incomplete.

Keep monetary policy separate from fiscal policy (section 5.2). Fiscal policy uses government spending and taxation. A change in a sales tax or in a welfare benefit is fiscal, even if it affects borrowing indirectly.

## 5.3.2 How each tool reaches aggregate demand

### Interest rates: four routes

When the central bank changes its policy rate, commercial banks usually move their lending and deposit rates in the same direction. A rise in rates then works through four routes. Learn them as chains, because examiners reward the links rather than the list.

| Route | Chain after a rise in interest rates | Component of AD |
|---|---|---|
| Cost of borrowing | Loans and variable-rate mortgages cost more, so households have less disposable income and buy fewer goods on credit | Consumption (C) |
| Reward for saving | Saving earns more, so some income is saved rather than spent | Consumption (C) |
| Investment | Fewer projects earn a return above the cost of borrowing, so firms invest less | Investment (I) |
| Exchange rate | Higher rates attract money from abroad, demand for the currency rises and it appreciates; exports become dearer abroad and imports cheaper at home | Net exports (X − M) |

A fall in rates reverses every chain. The exchange-rate route connects this section to 6.4 Exchange rates, and it is the route students most often forget.

**Worked example 1: the exchange-rate route.** The central bank of Country K raises its policy rate. The home currency rises from 1 home unit = 0.80 foreign units to 1 home unit = 0.84 foreign units, an appreciation of 0.04 ÷ 0.80 = 5%.

- A Country K export priced at 50 home units cost foreign buyers 50 × 0.80 = 40 foreign units. It now costs 50 × 0.84 = 42 foreign units, 5% more.
- An import priced at 21 foreign units cost Country K buyers 21 ÷ 0.80 = 26.25 home units. It now costs 21 ÷ 0.84 = 25.00 home units, a fall of 4.8%.

Export volumes are likely to fall and import volumes to rise, so net exports fall and AD shifts left, adding to the effect on consumption and investment. Cheaper imports also lower the price of imported goods and inputs directly, which helps to reduce inflation.

### Money supply: how a central bank changes it

The central bank can change the quantity of money in the economy. The usual method is to buy or sell government bonds. When it **buys** bonds from banks and other investors, it pays with new money, so banks hold more reserves and can lend more. When it **sells** bonds, money is drained out of the banking system. Large-scale purchases of this kind, used when interest rates are already very low, are often called quantitative easing.

The money supply and the interest rate are two sides of one decision. Given the demand for money, a larger money supply lowers the interest rate and a smaller one raises it. So a central bank cannot freely set both at once: it chooses a rate and supplies the money needed to hold it, or it chooses a quantity and accepts the rate that results.

### Credit regulations: limiting lending directly

Credit regulations act on the quantity of lending rather than on its price. Common examples are:

- a maximum **loan-to-value** ratio for mortgages, which sets a minimum deposit
- a maximum **loan-to-income** multiple, which caps how much a household may borrow relative to its income
- a **reserve requirement**, the share of deposits a bank must hold rather than lend
- minimum deposits or maximum repayment periods for buying goods on credit

**Worked example 2: two credit rules.**

- A household earns 40,000 home units a year. With a loan-to-income cap of 4.5, it may borrow up to 180,000. If the cap is cut to 4.0, the limit falls to 160,000, which is 20,000 or 11.1% less.
- A bank holds deposits of 500 million. With a reserve requirement of 10% it keeps 50 million and can lend up to 450 million. If the requirement rises to 12%, it keeps 60 million and can lend up to 440 million.

Both rules reduce the credit available without any change in interest rates, so borrowing-financed consumption and investment fall. Credit rules are useful when policymakers want to cool one market, such as housing, without raising the cost of borrowing for every firm.

## 5.3.3 Expansionary or contractionary: matching the stance to the problem

**Expansionary** monetary policy cuts interest rates, increases the money supply or relaxes credit rules, so AD shifts right. It suits an economy with spare capacity: weak growth, rising unemployment and inflation at or below target.

**Contractionary** monetary policy raises interest rates, reduces the money supply or tightens credit rules, so AD shifts left. It suits an economy where AD is growing faster than the capacity to produce, with demand-pull inflation above target.

The stance is judged by the direction of change. A central bank that cuts its rate from 8% to 7% is loosening policy, even though 7% may still be high compared with other countries.

## 5.3.4 AD/AS analysis: why the shape of AS matters

Draw the price level on the vertical axis and real output on the horizontal axis. Monetary policy shifts AD. What happens next depends on where the economy sits on the AS curve. The syllabus allows LRAS to be drawn as a vertical line or in three sections: highly elastic, upward sloping and vertical.

| Starting position | Effect of expansionary policy (AD shifts right) |
|---|---|
| Highly elastic section: deep recession, much spare capacity | Real output and employment rise; the price level barely changes |
| Upward-sloping section: some spare capacity | Real output, employment and the price level all rise |
| Vertical section: full capacity | The price level rises; real output and employment do not rise in the long run |

Contractionary policy works in reverse. Near full capacity it mainly reduces inflationary pressure. In the upward-sloping section it reduces the price level (or its rate of increase) but also costs output and jobs. Equilibrium national income moves in the same direction as real output.

**Worked example 3: an annotated model answer.**

*Question:* Explain how a reduction in interest rates might affect employment.

> A cut in interest rates lowers the cost of borrowing, so households pay less on variable-rate loans and are more willing to borrow to buy cars and household goods. **[Mechanism, linked to consumption.]** Firms find that more investment projects now earn a return above the cost of borrowing, so investment rises. **[Second route.]** As C and I are components of AD, AD shifts to the right. **[Link to AD.]** Firms respond to higher demand by raising output, and as labour is a derived demand, they hire more workers, so employment rises. **[Link to the variable in the question.]** The rise in employment is largest when the economy has spare capacity; near full capacity firms cannot find extra workers and the main effect is a higher price level. **[Condition that shows judgement.]**

Notice that the answer ends on employment, the variable in the question, and adds a condition rather than a bare assertion.

## Common mistakes

- Writing that lower interest rates make saving more attractive. They make it less attractive.
- Shifting AS for a change in interest rates. Monetary policy works on AD.
- Treating the money supply and the interest rate as independent tools that can be set separately.
- Forgetting the exchange-rate route, or getting its direction wrong: higher rates usually mean appreciation, not depreciation.
- Claiming that expansionary policy always raises output. At full capacity it raises the price level instead.
- Ignoring time lags. Many loans are on fixed rates, so a change in the policy rate reaches borrowers only gradually.

## Exam technique for 9708 command words

- **Define:** give the precise meaning, including the tools and the purpose.
- **Explain:** set out the chain step by step, from the tool to a component of AD and on to the variable named in the question.
- **Analyse:** use an AD/AS diagram with labelled axes, both AD curves and both equilibria, and refer to it in your writing.
- **Assess** and **Evaluate:** weigh how far the policy works and reach a judgement that depends on conditions, such as the cause of inflation, the amount of spare capacity, the level of household debt or business confidence.

Paper 1 is multiple choice, so practise identifying the direction of each tool quickly. The Section C essays in Paper 2 have two parts and need evidence of evaluation.
