**This guide covers two linked aspects that AQA lists for Option 1A Aspects of Tragedy:** "the
structural pattern of the text as it moves through complication to catastrophe, from order to
disorder, through climax to resolution, from the prosperity and happiness of the hero to the tragic
end" and "the use of plots and sub-plots". The [Aspects of Tragedy study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-english-literature-7712-7717-option-1a-aspects-of-tragedy-7717/)
introduces every aspect and the set texts; this page goes deeper into structure, which students often
mention in a sentence but rarely analyse.

## Why structure matters in a tragedy answer

AO2 asks you to analyse "ways in which meanings are shaped", and the specification adds that AO2
has "particular focus on the structures of texts as a form of shaping". In tragedy, structure is
where meaning is clearest: the order in which a writer releases information decides when the
audience feels dread, when they pity the hero, and whether the ending feels inevitable or
accidental. An answer that only analyses imagery misses the most distinctive feature of the genre.

AQA's wording gives you four pairs of terms to argue with:

| AQA's pair | What to ask |
|---|---|
| Complication to catastrophe | Where does the first complication appear, and how quickly does it grow? |
| Order to disorder | What order exists at the start, and what replaces it? |
| Climax to resolution | Where is the point of no return, and what does the ending settle? |
| Prosperity and happiness to the tragic end | How high does the hero begin, and how far does the fall reach? |

None of these is a fixed rule. A strong answer tests each pair against the text: some tragedies
start in disorder, some resolutions settle very little.

## Shapes of tragic structure

**The rising and falling arc.** Many critics describe five-act tragedy as a rise to a turning point
and a fall to catastrophe. The reversal (peripeteia) and the recognition (anagnorisis) usually sit
on this arc. In *Othello*, the temptation scene in Act 3 Scene 3 sits at the centre of the play and
works as the hinge: before it Othello is secure in his marriage, after it he is committed to
revenge.

**The see-saw.** In *Richard II*, one man's fall is another's rise. Bolingbroke returns from exile
as Richard's power drains away, and Richard himself imagines the crown as a well with two buckets,
one rising as the other sinks. The structure becomes an argument about kingship: power is not lost
into nothing but passes to someone else.

**The episodic series.** The anthology's extracts from "The Monk's Tale" tell a sequence of falls
rather than one. Repetition is the structural point: the de casibus pattern shows fortune turning
again and again, so the tragedy lies in the pattern, not in a single hero.

**The retrospective structure.** *The Great Gatsby* is narrated by Nick after the events, and
Gatsby's past is released in fragments. The reader learns the truth about James Gatz only after
the dream has been displayed, so the structure itself enacts the gap between the self Gatsby
invented and the self he came from. *Death of a Salesman* interweaves Willy's present with scenes
from memory, and withholds the Boston hotel discovery until late in Act 2, so the audience
understands Biff's change only near the catastrophe.

**The phased fall.** Hardy divides *Tess of the D'Urbervilles* into seven "Phases", from "The
Maiden" to "Fulfilment". The titles impose a pattern on Tess's life, and the final title is bitterly
ironic, since her "fulfilment" is execution. Analysing the phase titles is a precise way to discuss
Hardy's structure.

## Order and disorder

AQA's phrase "from order to disorder" invites you to define the order first. In *King Lear* the
opening scene shows a hierarchy apparently intact: a king, a court, a map, and daughters required to
speak their love. Lear's decision to divide the kingdom breaks that order, and the storm in Act 3
makes the disorder physical. In *Othello*, the opening is already disturbed (a night alarm, an angry
father), but Othello calms it with authority before the Senate; the move to Cyprus removes the
structures of Venice and leaves the characters in a military outpost where Iago can work.

The test for a top-band answer is whether order returns. At the end of *King Lear* survivors speak
of the weight of the sad time, but the dead outnumber the living; at the end of *Othello*, Lodovico
restores the state's authority, yet the marriage and the hero are destroyed. You can argue that the
resolution restores public order while leaving private loss unresolved.

## Plots and sub-plots

A sub-plot is a second line of action that runs beside the main plot. In tragedy it usually does one
of three jobs.

1. **Mirror.** The sub-plot repeats the main plot's pattern so the theme becomes general. In *King
   Lear*, Gloucester, like Lear, misjudges his children: he believes the lying son (Edmund) and
   banishes the loyal one (Edgar), as Lear believes Goneril and Regan and banishes Cordelia.
2. **Intensify.** The sub-plot makes literal what the main plot shows figuratively. Lear is
   metaphorically blind; Gloucester is physically blinded. The sub-plot turns an image into an
   event, which is why the blinding scene is so hard to watch.
3. **Contrast.** A second strand can show another way of living. In *Death of a Salesman*, Charley
   and Bernard are a contrast to Willy and Biff: success without boasting, care without illusion.
   Charley's quiet offers of work and money measure Willy's pride.

The sub-plot can also connect to the main plot causally. Edmund links the two plots of *King Lear*:
he rises through Gloucester's family and then becomes entangled with Goneril and Regan, so the two
plots converge in the final act.

## Worked example: a structural paragraph

**Question shape (original):** "In *King Lear*, the sub-plot adds nothing that the main plot does not
already show." Discuss.

> The Gloucester plot does more than repeat Lear's story; it changes how the audience reads it. By
> the time Gloucester is blinded in Act 3 Scene 7, Lear's mind is already breaking in the storm, so
> Shakespeare places the two catastrophes side by side: one man loses reason, the other loses sight.
> The juxtaposition makes the main plot's metaphor of blindness concrete, so that the audience can
> no longer treat Lear's failure to see Cordelia's love as an abstract error. Yet the sub-plot also
> diverges. Gloucester is saved from despair by a son he does not recognise, and his death is
> reported as a heart that burst between joy and grief, whereas Lear dies after Cordelia's death in
> a final scene that offers no comparable reconciliation. The sub-plot therefore sets up a pattern
> of suffering followed by consolation which the main plot then refuses, and that refusal is part
> of what makes the ending so bleak.

**Why it works.** It names a precise structural choice (placing scenes side by side), explains its
effect on the audience, uses the AO2 language of juxtaposition and divergence, and argues against
the view rather than retelling the sub-plot.

## Structure in poetry texts

If your two further texts include Keats or the anthology, structure still matters.

- "La Belle Dame Sans Merci" ends by returning almost exactly to its opening images, so the circular
  form traps the knight in his loss.
- "The Eve of St. Agnes" ends with the lovers' escape into the storm, but the final stanza turns to
  the deaths of Angela and the Beadsman. The structure undercuts the happy ending and raises AQA's
  point about the absence of an aspect.
- "The Convergence of the Twain" is built in two movements: the ship lying on the seabed, then the
  iceberg growing towards it. The poem's structure enacts the "convergence" it describes, which is
  why fate seems built into the form.
- "'Out, out…'" moves from an ordinary afternoon to death within a single short poem, then ends on
  the survivors turning back to their affairs. The abrupt structure is the tragedy.

## Common mistakes

- **Narrating the plot in order** and calling it structure. Structure means choices about order,
  pace, placement and parallels.
- **Using "Freytag's pyramid" as a template** and forcing every text into five stages.
- **Treating the sub-plot as a separate story** instead of analysing what it does to the main plot.
- **Ignoring endings.** The resolution is a structural choice and often the best evidence for a
  judgement on the question.
- **Forgetting the passage in Section A.** Structure includes where the printed passage sits in the
  play: before or after the turning point changes how it reads.

## Exam technique

Questions on this aspect may ask about "the significance" of the sub-plot, or offer a view about
endings to debate. Plan two or three structural points that cover beginning, turning point and
ending, then use AO5 by weighing whether the resolution restores order. In Section A, open by
placing the passage in the play's structure in one sentence, then analyse the passage itself.
