These questions test the section 3.3 row on vicarious liability: its nature and purpose, testing employment status, the course of employment and other areas.

---

## Section A

**1.** Explain what is meant by vicarious liability. **[2]**

**2.** Identify the **two** stages of the test for vicarious liability. **[2]**

**3.** Explain the integration test of employment status. **[2]**

**4.** Explain the decision in *Ferguson v John Dawson & Partners* (1976). **[2]**

**5.** Explain why the employer was liable in *Rose v Plenty* (1976). **[3]**

**6.** State the rule in *Lister v Romford Ice and Cold Storage* (1957). **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain the test in *Ready Mixed Concrete v Minister of Pensions* (1968). **[4]**

**8.** Explain how the courts have extended vicarious liability to relationships akin to employment, using **two** cases. **[5]**

**9.** Explain the close connection test, with reference to **one** case where the employer was liable and **one** where it was not. **[5]**

**10.** Explain who is vicariously liable for an employee lent by one employer to another. **[3]**

**11.** Bright Gyms Ltd employs Kit as a personal trainer. Kit is told never to use free weights with beginners. During a session he hands a heavy dumbbell to Rae, a beginner, who drops it and breaks her foot. After the session, Rae complains about Kit to the manager. Kit follows her to the car park and pushes her, and she cuts her head. Advise Rae whether Bright Gyms Ltd is vicariously liable for each injury. **[8]**

**12.** Discuss whether the expansion of vicarious liability since 2012 has been justified. **[6]**

---

## Answers

**1.** Vicarious liability is where one person, usually an employer, is held liable for a tort committed by another, usually an employee [1], even though the employer was not personally at fault [1].

**2.** First, a relationship of employment or one akin to employment between the defendant and the wrongdoer [1]; second, a close connection between that relationship and the tort, traditionally the course of employment [1].

**3.** The integration test asks whether the person's work is an integral part of the business or only accessory to it [1]. It was stated by Denning LJ in *Stevenson Jordan & Harrison v Macdonald & Evans* (1952) [1].

**4.** A labourer working for a builder was described as self-employed [1]. The court held that the label was not decisive; looking at the reality of the relationship, including control, he was an employee [1].

**5.** A milkman, against his employer's orders, let a 13-year-old boy help him deliver milk [1]. The boy was injured by the milkman's negligent driving [1]. The employer was liable because the boy was helping with the employer's business, so the milkman was doing his job in an unauthorised way [1].

**6.** An employer who has been held vicariously liable [1] may in principle recover its loss from the employee whose negligence caused it [1].

**7.** MacKenna J set out three conditions for a contract of employment [1]. The worker agrees to provide work and skill in return for a wage [1]; the worker agrees, expressly or impliedly, to be subject to the other's control to a sufficient degree [1]; and the other terms of the contract are consistent with its being a contract of employment [1].

**8.** The Supreme Court has held that a relationship need not be formal employment if it is sufficiently akin to it [1]. In *Various Claimants v Catholic Child Welfare Society* (2012) brothers teaching at a school were in a relationship akin to employment with their religious institute [1], so the institute was liable for their abuse of pupils [1]. In *Cox v Ministry of Justice* (2016) a prisoner working in the prison kitchen injured a catering manager [1]; his work was integrated into the prison's activities and for its benefit, so the Ministry of Justice was liable [1]. (Credit *Armes* or *E v English Province*.)

**9.** For deliberate wrongdoing, the question is whether there is a close connection between the employee's job and the tort, so that it is fair and just to hold the employer liable [1]. In *Mohamud v WM Morrison* (2016) a kiosk attendant attacked a customer after an exchange at the counter [1]; there was an unbroken sequence of events from his job of serving customers, so the employer was liable [1]. In *WM Morrison v Various Claimants* (2020) an employee leaked payroll data [1] to harm his employer in a personal vendetta, so there was no close connection and no liability [1].

**10.** The general employer usually remains vicariously liable [1], because it keeps the right to control how the work is done: *Mersey Docks and Harbour Board v Coggins & Griffith* (1947), the crane driver [1]. It is possible for both employers to be liable: *Viasystems v Thermal Transfer* (2005) [1].

**11.** **Stage 1:** Kit is employed by Bright Gyms, so the relationship stage is met [1]. **Broken foot:** Kit committed negligence by giving a heavy weight to a beginner [1]. Although prohibited, it was an unauthorised way of doing his job of training clients, so it is within the course of employment [1] (*Limpus*; *Rose v Plenty*) [1]. Bright Gyms is likely liable [1]. **Head injury:** Kit committed battery, a deliberate tort [1]. Applying *Mohamud*, the push followed a complaint about his work, which may be an unbroken sequence from his job [1]; however, following her to the car park after the session may be a personal matter, as in *Morrison* (2020), so liability is arguable either way, with *Mohamud* the closer analogy [1].

**12.** Since 2012 the courts have extended stage 1 to relationships akin to employment (*Catholic Child Welfare Society*; *Cox*; *Armes*) [1]. This is justified because victims of abuse by people placed in positions of trust by organisations can be compensated [1], and those organisations created the risk and can insure [1]. However, it imposes liability without fault [1] and makes the boundaries uncertain [1]. The Supreme Court's limits in *Barclays Bank* (2020) and *Morrison* (2020) restored some certainty, so the expansion is justified as long as these limits hold [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Not identifying the wrongdoer's tort.
- Deciding employment status from a label.
- Treating the two injuries in a problem as one.
- Forgetting the limits set in 2020.
