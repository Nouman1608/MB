Sub-topic 5.1 Ecosystems in the Cambridge O Level Environmental Management (5014) syllabus for 2027 to 2029 is the science base for the whole of Topic 5. It has nine statements, from the vocabulary of ecosystems (5.1.1 and 5.1.2) through interactions between species (5.1.3 and 5.1.4) to the processes that move energy and carbon (5.1.5 to 5.1.9). Many marks come from precise definitions and from interpreting food webs, pyramids and cycles, so this guide uses one example ecosystem, a freshwater pond, throughout.

## Components of an ecosystem (5.1.1)

| Term | Meaning | Pond example |
|---|---|---|
| Habitat | The place where an organism lives | The muddy bottom, where snails live |
| Species | A group of organisms that can breed together to produce fertile offspring | One kind of frog |
| Population | All the organisms of one species living in an area | All the frogs in the pond |
| Community | All the populations of different species living in an area | Every plant, animal and microorganism in the pond |
| Niche | The role of a species in its ecosystem: what it eats, where and when it feeds, and how it interacts with others | A beetle that hunts tadpoles at night among water plants |
| Prey | An animal that is hunted and eaten by another | Tadpoles |
| Predator | An animal that hunts and eats other animals | Dragonfly larvae |
| Apex predator | A predator at the top of a food chain with no natural predators | A heron feeding on the pond |

The difference between habitat and niche is the one most often tested. A habitat is an address; a niche is a job. Two species can share a habitat but, if they have exactly the same niche, they compete strongly and one usually replaces the other.

## Biotic and abiotic components (5.1.2)

**Biotic** (living) components are grouped by feeding role:

- **Producers**: plants and algae that make their own food by photosynthesis.
- **Primary consumers**: herbivores that eat producers, such as tadpoles grazing algae.
- **Secondary consumers**: animals that eat primary consumers, such as small fish.
- **Tertiary consumers**: animals that eat secondary consumers, such as a heron.
- **Decomposers**: bacteria and fungi that break down dead organisms and waste, releasing nutrients.

**Abiotic** (non-living) components named in the syllabus are **temperature, water, oxygen, carbon dioxide, salinity, light and pH**. Each one limits where organisms can live. In the pond, light decides how deep plants can grow; dissolved oxygen falls in warm water, affecting fish; pH changes if acid rain falls; salinity matters in coastal ponds that sea water can reach.

## Biotic interactions (5.1.3)

- **Native and invasive species**: a native species occurs naturally in an area. An invasive species has been introduced, often by people, and spreads rapidly, frequently because it has no natural predators or diseases there. Invasive species can out-compete or eat native species and reduce biodiversity.
- **Competition**: organisms competing for the same limited resource, such as light, space, food or mates. Competition can be between members of the same species or between different species.
- **Predation**: one animal hunting and eating another. Predator and prey numbers are linked: if prey increase, predators have more food and increase; more predators then reduce the prey, and predator numbers fall again.
- **Pollination**: the interaction between flowering plants and their pollinators, covered in the next statement.

## Pollination (5.1.4)

Pollination is the **transfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma**. You do not need to name the other flower parts.

- **Insect pollination**: an insect pollinator, such as a bee, visits a flower, picks up pollen and carries it to the stigma of another flower.
- **Wind pollination**: pollen is light and is blown by the wind from one plant to another.
- Pollination leads to **fertilisation**, followed by the formation of **seeds and fruit**.

The environmental importance is that many crops depend on insect pollinators, so a fall in pollinator numbers can reduce harvests and wild plant reproduction.

## Photosynthesis (5.1.5)

Producers make glucose by photosynthesis.

- Glucose is synthesised from **carbon dioxide** and **water**.
- **Sunlight** is the source of energy.
- **Chlorophyll** captures the light energy.
- Word equation: **carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen**.

Photosynthesis is the way energy enters almost every food chain, and it removes carbon dioxide from the air, linking to the carbon cycle and to climate change.

## Energy flow, food chains, food webs and trophic levels (5.1.6)

A **trophic level** is a feeding level in a food chain: producer, primary consumer, secondary consumer, tertiary consumer. The syllabus states that about **10% of energy is passed** from one trophic level to the next. The rest is used or lost in **life processes: movement, respiration, digestion and excretion**. Respiration releases energy that is eventually lost as heat, and some material is not eaten or not digested.

Because 90% is lost at each step, food chains are short and there is far less energy available at the top. In a food web, an animal can occupy more than one trophic level. A small fish that eats tadpoles is a secondary consumer; if it also eats algae it is a primary consumer too.

## Ecological pyramids (5.1.7)

- A **pyramid of numbers** shows the number of organisms at each trophic level.
- A **pyramid of energy** shows the energy at each trophic level, usually per unit area per year.

A pyramid of energy always narrows upwards, because energy is lost at every level. A pyramid of numbers is often pyramid-shaped, but it can be **inverted** or irregular when one large producer supports many small consumers, or when many parasites feed on one host. When you interpret a pyramid, say what it shows and explain any unusual shape using the size of the organisms.

## Aerobic respiration (5.1.8)

Aerobic respiration is the **breakdown of glucose in the presence of oxygen to release energy**, with **carbon dioxide and water** as waste products.

Word equation: **glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water**.

It happens in all living organisms, including plants, all the time. Notice that it is the reverse of photosynthesis: learning the two equations together halves the effort.

## The carbon cycle (5.1.9)

| Process | Carbon moves from | Carbon moves to |
|---|---|---|
| Photosynthesis | Carbon dioxide in air or water | Glucose in producers |
| Respiration | Organisms | Carbon dioxide in the air |
| Feeding | Producers or prey | Consumers |
| Decomposition | Dead organisms and waste | Carbon dioxide released by decomposers' respiration |
| Formation of fossil fuels | Dead organisms buried and compressed over millions of years | Coal, oil and natural gas |
| Combustion | Fossil fuels and wood | Carbon dioxide in the air |

In a balanced cycle, photosynthesis removes about as much carbon dioxide as respiration and decomposition release. Combustion of fossil fuels releases carbon stored over millions of years, and deforestation reduces photosynthesis, so the balance tips towards more carbon dioxide in the air.

## Worked example 1: energy through a pond food chain

*Figures invented for practice.* Algae in a pond capture 45,000 kJ per m² per year.

1. Using the syllabus 10% figure: water fleas receive 4,500 kJ; small fish 450 kJ; heron 45 kJ.
2. A survey later finds water fleas actually receive 5,400 kJ per m² per year. Efficiency = 5,400 ÷ 45,000 × 100 = 12%.
3. Energy not passed to water fleas = 45,000 − 5,400 = 39,600 kJ, used or lost in the algae's life processes or in algae that are not eaten.

The 10% value is an approximation; real transfers vary, so in a calculation question use whichever figure the question gives.

## Worked example 2: an inverted pyramid of numbers

*Figures invented for practice.* A single large tree supports 4,000 caterpillars, which are eaten by 30 small birds, which are eaten by 1 hawk.

The pyramid of numbers has a narrow base (1 tree), a wide second bar (4,000 caterpillars) and then narrows (30, then 1). It looks inverted at the bottom because one tree is very large. A pyramid of energy for the same chain would still narrow upwards, because the tree captures far more energy than the caterpillars receive.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing habitat (where it lives) and niche (its role).
- Drawing food chain arrows from consumer to food. Arrows show the direction of energy flow, towards the eater.
- Saying energy is "used up" at each level without naming the life processes.
- Writing that only animals respire.
- Mixing up the photosynthesis and respiration equations.
- Claiming all pyramids narrow upwards. Only pyramids of energy always do.

## Exam technique

**Describe** questions on components need a definition plus, where possible, an example. **Describe and interpret** questions on food webs need you to use the organisms given: name them, trace the arrows, and predict effects of removing one. **Describe, interpret and explain** questions on pyramids need the shape, what it shows and the reason for it. For the carbon cycle, name each process and the direction carbon moves.
