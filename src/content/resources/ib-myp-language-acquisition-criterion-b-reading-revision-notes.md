These revision notes cover Criterion B -- Reading for IB MYP Language Acquisition, aligned to the International Baccalaureate Organization, Middle Years Programme Subject Brief -- *Language acquisition*, from 2020 (first eAssessment May 2023/November 2023). They suit MYP years 4 and 5 (no SL/HL split) and apply to school reading tasks and the on-screen examination from the May 2023 and November 2023 sessions onwards. MYP has no prescribed content list -- schools design their own units -- and this page covers a skill named by one of the brief's four criteria. Model texts are in English; apply every method to texts in your target language.

## Key facts from the brief

| Fact | What the brief says |
|---|---|
| Criterion name | Criterion B: Reading |
| What it assesses | Constructing meaning from written, spatial and visual aspects of texts, and how they work with the words to convey ideas, values and attitudes |
| Achievement levels | Eight (1-8), in four bands; four criteria weighted equally |
| On-screen exam | Three tasks; "comprehending written multimodal texts" = Criterion B, 24 marks |
| Proficiency levels | Emergent, capable or proficient |
| Topics | Any topic from the language acquisition list |

Anything else -- strand wording, descriptors, task lengths -- comes from your teacher, who will share the task-specific clarifications.

## Definitions

- **Multimodal text:** a text that makes meaning through more than one mode -- words plus images, layout, colour or typography.
- **Main idea:** the central point of a whole text or section, usually in your own words.
- **Supporting detail:** a fact, example, figure or reason that backs up a main idea.
- **Text-type conventions:** the typical features of a kind of text (a blog's title, date and comments; a leaflet's headings and contact details).
- **Visual aspect:** photos, drawings, icons, charts, logos, colour.
- **Spatial aspect:** where things sit on the page, their size, boxes, columns, grouping, white space.
- **Purpose:** why the text was written (inform, persuade, entertain, advise, instruct).
- **Audience:** who the text is for, shown by register, vocabulary and where it appears.
- **Perspective:** whose point of view the text presents.
- **Values and attitudes:** what the writer shows they believe is important, and how they feel about the subject.
- **Inference:** a conclusion the text supports but does not state directly.

## Feature, question, effect

| Feature | Ask | Typical effect |
|---|---|---|
| Large headline at the top | What is read first? | Sets the main message or hook |
| Photo of people | Who, doing what, with what expression? | Makes the message personal; suggests who the audience is |
| Icons in a row | What do they stand for? | Lets readers scan quickly |
| Coloured box or strip | What is separated from the rest? | Highlights rules, warnings or practical details |
| Bold or capitals | Which words stand out? | Stresses a key instruction or claim |
| Chart or infographic | What trend or comparison? | Gives evidence in a quick visual form |
| Caption | Does it repeat, add or change the image's meaning? | Adds humour, context or a viewpoint |

## The three-pass routine

| Pass | What you do | What it gives you |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Skim | Title, headings, images, captions, first and last lines | Text type, audience, rough main idea |
| 2. Scan | Question key words, then the same words or synonyms in the text | The right place to look |
| 3. Close read | The sentences around the match; negatives, contrasts, time words | The precise answer and your evidence |

## In your target language

- Learn the question words (who, when, why, according to, what does ... mean) so you know exactly what each question asks.
- Learn the connectives that flip meaning (but, however, although, not, never, only) -- they decide many true/false answers.
- Keep a vocabulary list for talking about visuals: photo, caption, heading, icon, colour, layout, in the foreground, stands out.
- Collect short authentic texts on your unit topics and practise the steps below on them every week.

## Method in steps

**Finding the main idea**

1. Read the title, headings, first and last sentences.
2. Ask what every paragraph has in common.
3. Write one sentence in your own words.
4. Check it covers the whole text, not one detail.

**Explaining a visual or spatial element**

1. **Name** it (photo, icon, green strip, bold heading).
2. **Describe** it precisely (who, what, where on the page).
3. **Explain** its effect: does it repeat, add to or contrast with the words?
4. **Link** it to purpose or audience.

**Identifying purpose and attitude**

1. Label the purpose (one or more).
2. Quote the words that show it (commands persuade or instruct; figures inform; jokes entertain).
3. For attitude, find loaded word choice or tone ("finally", "sadly", "at last!").
4. Add a visual that supports the same reading.

**True or false, with justification**

1. Find the part of the text the statement refers to.
2. Check negatives, contrast words and time words.
3. Decide, then copy the shortest phrase that proves it.

## Small worked reminders

- Statement: "Volunteers at the community garden must bring their own tools." Text: "Tools and gloves provided." **False** -- "Tools and gloves provided".
- "Why is the time and place in a separate green strip?" -- It groups the practical details so readers can find them quickly; green links to nature and the garden.
- Attitude from word choice: "finally trusted with the big knife" shows pride in progress; "allowed to use a knife" would be neutral.

## Must-know distinctions

| Pair | Difference |
|---|---|
| Main idea vs detail | The main idea covers the whole text; a detail supports one point |
| Purpose vs perspective | Purpose = why it was written; perspective = whose view it gives |
| Stated vs implied | Stated = in the words; implied = you infer it and must give evidence |
| Describe vs explain a visual | Describe = what it shows; explain = what it adds to the meaning |
| Fact vs opinion | A fact can be checked; an opinion shows a judgement or feeling |
| Visual vs spatial | Visual = what is shown; spatial = where and how it is placed |

## Quick self-test

Use this original leaflet for questions 1-7.

> **Source -- original text written for Marlbridge**
>
> **Brookside Leisure Centre (fictional) -- New swimming timetable**
> [header photo: a lifeguard kneeling at the poolside, helping a small child with a float]
> Pool open daily 07:00-21:00. Lane swimming only before 09:00. Family splash sessions: Saturday and Sunday, 14:00-16:00.
> Prices: adults £4.50, under-16s £2.25. Under-8s must be accompanied in the water by an adult.
> [red box with a warning-triangle icon] Please shower before entering the pool. No running on the poolside.
> [bottom corner: QR code] Scan to book. Sessions fill up fast!

1. Give two purposes of the leaflet.
2. For how many hours is the pool open each day?
3. You want to swim casually, not in lanes, at 08:00. Can you? Justify with the text.
4. What do two adults and two 12-year-olds pay in total?
5. Explain why the safety rules are placed in a red box with a warning triangle.
6. What does the header photo add to the written text?
7. Find the phrase that creates a sense of urgency.
8. Explain the difference between a main idea and a supporting detail.
9. Explain the difference between purpose and perspective.
10. Which three aspects of texts does the brief's description of Criterion B name?
11. How many marks does Criterion B carry in the on-screen examination, and how many achievement levels does each criterion have?

### Answers

1. To inform (times, prices, rules) and to persuade readers to book ("Scan to book").
2. **14 hours** (07:00 to 21:00).
3. **No** -- "Lane swimming only before 09:00."
4. **£13.50** (2 × £4.50 + 2 × £2.25).
5. Red and the triangle signal a warning; the box separates the rules from the rest, so readers notice them.
6. It shows staff helping a young swimmer, suggesting the pool is safe and family-friendly; it supports the family sessions and the under-8s rule.
7. "Sessions fill up fast!"
8. The main idea is the central point of the whole text; a supporting detail is a fact or example that backs it up.
9. Purpose is why the text was written; perspective is whose point of view it presents.
10. Written, spatial and visual.
11. **24 marks**; **eight levels (1-8)** in four bands.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving one detail as the main idea of the whole text.
- Describing an image ("there is a photo of a lifeguard") without explaining what it adds.
- Ignoring layout, colour and typography because "it's a reading task".
- Missing "only", "not" or "before" and reversing a true/false answer.
- True or false with no quotation, or a whole paragraph copied as evidence.
- Lifting a full sentence when the question asks for your own words.
- Naming a purpose or attitude with no evidence from the text.
- Answering from general knowledge instead of the text in front of you.
- Mixing up the writer's perspective with your own opinion.
