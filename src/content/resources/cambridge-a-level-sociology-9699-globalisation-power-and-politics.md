Section 7.3 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699) turns globalisation into a question about power. The syllabus lists three strands: the spread of liberal democracy and human rights; global social movements and attempts to oppose globalisation; and debates about the role of the nation state in tackling global social and environmental problems. It links to the key concepts of Power, control and resistance and Social change and development, and is examined in Paper 4 Section A.

The [globalisation debates guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-sociology-globalisation-debates-and-perspectives/) introduces Fukuyama, the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, the Seattle protests, the World Social Forum, Occupy, Fridays for Future, Castells, Ohmae, Kyoto, Paris and Beck. This page adds the evidence and theory needed to evaluate each strand in a 35-mark essay.

## The spread of liberal democracy

**Liberal democracy** combines free, competitive elections with protected civil liberties, the rule of law and limits on state power.

**Evidence of spread.** Samuel Huntington, in *The Third Wave* (1991), described a wave of democratisation beginning with Portugal's revolution in 1974 and continuing through southern Europe, Latin America, parts of Asia and, after 1989, central and eastern Europe. The protests of the Arab Spring from late 2010 showed how global media and social networks can spread democratic demands across borders.

**Why globalisation might spread democracy:**

- global media expose citizens to democratic ideals and make repression visible;
- trade and aid can be made conditional on political reform;
- international organisations and regional blocs set democratic membership standards;
- educated middle classes produced by economic growth may demand representation.

**Evidence against.** Most Arab Spring uprisings did not produce lasting democracy. Larry Diamond (2015) described a "democratic recession": in the years after the mid-2000s, democratic gains stalled and in places reversed. Economic globalisation can coexist with authoritarian rule, and some critics argue that "democracy promotion" has served Western strategic interests.

**Globalisation and democracy in tension.** Even in established democracies, global markets and transnational corporations may constrain what elected governments can do, for example over taxes or regulation. David Held, in *Democracy and the Global Order* (1995), argued for **cosmopolitan democracy**: democratic accountability extended to international institutions, because decisions that affect people now cross borders.

## Human rights

Human rights are rights held by every person because they are human. Globalisation is linked to their spread through international declarations and courts, global civil society organisations that monitor abuses, and media that publicise them.

**Evaluating the spread of human rights:**

- **Universalism versus relativism.** In the 1990s some Asian governments argued that "Asian values" prioritised community and order over individual rights. Critics replied that this argument was often used to justify authoritarian rule. Sociologists can treat the debate as evidence that rights are contested, not simply diffused.
- **Selective enforcement.** Powerful states may escape scrutiny, while weaker ones face pressure, which Marxists and post-colonial critics see as evidence that rights discourse reflects global power.
- **Agency.** Local movements often use international rights language to make their own claims, so rights are not only imposed from outside.

## Global social movements and opposition to globalisation

Global social movements are networks of organisations and individuals acting across borders on shared issues. Mary Kaldor, in *Global Civil Society: An Answer to War* (2003), described a **global civil society** of movements, NGOs and networks operating beyond the state.

**Types of opposition:**

| Type | Aim | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Anti-corporate | Challenge the power and practices of transnational corporations | Campaigns against sweatshop labour; Naomi Klein's *No Logo* (1999) |
| Alter-globalisation | Seek a fairer form of globalisation rather than none | World Social Forum |
| Local resistance with global reach | Defend a community while building international support | The Zapatista uprising in Chiapas, Mexico, launched on 1 January 1994, the day the North American Free Trade Agreement came into force |
| Single-issue global campaigns | Pressure states and institutions on one problem | Climate and debt campaigns |

**Evaluation.**

- **Strengths:** they use global communications to coordinate quickly and to publicise local struggles worldwide; they have shifted public debate on issues such as debt and labour standards.
- **Weaknesses:** they often struggle to turn protest into lasting policy; they can be dominated by activists from richer countries; and opposition to globalisation is not only progressive, since nationalist movements also oppose open borders and trade.
- **Paradox:** movements against corporate globalisation depend on the global networks and technologies that globalisation created.

## The nation state and global problems

The syllabus asks whether nation states can still tackle global social and environmental problems such as climate change, pandemics, migration and financial crises.

**The case that states are weakened.**

- Susan Strange, in *The Retreat of the State* (1996), argued that power had shifted from states to markets and to non-state actors such as transnational corporations.
- Problems such as climate change cross borders, so no single state can solve them alone.
- Capital mobility lets firms relocate to avoid regulation or tax.

**The case that states remain central.**

- States still control borders, armies, law, taxation and citizenship.
- During the coronavirus pandemic from 2020, states closed borders and imposed national public health measures, showing their continuing capacity.
- The Paris Agreement (2015) relies on each state setting its own emissions commitments, so even global environmental action is delivered nationally.

**The transformationalist middle position.** States are not disappearing but **pooling sovereignty** in international bodies such as the World Trade Organization (established 1995) and through groupings such as the G20, whose leaders have met at summits since 2008. Their power is exercised through cooperation rather than alone.

**Perspective map:**

- **Globalist:** states losing power (Ohmae, Strange).
- **Sceptic:** states still the main actors.
- **Transformationalist:** states changing role (Held).
- **Marxist:** states serve global capital, so their "weakness" in regulating corporations reflects class interests rather than incapacity.

## Worked paragraph

> One argument is that globalisation has weakened the nation state's ability to tackle global problems. Strange argues that power has shifted from states to markets, and climate change shows why: emissions cross borders, so one state's action can be undone by others. However, the coronavirus pandemic showed states closing borders and imposing national measures, so their capacity to act remains considerable. The Paris Agreement suggests a transformationalist conclusion: global problems are tackled through states cooperating, not by states being bypassed.

## Building a 35-mark essay

*"Global social movements are an effective way of opposing globalisation." Evaluate this view.*

1. Define global social movements and the forms of opposition.
2. For: Kaldor's global civil society; Zapatistas; anti-corporate campaigns; alter-globalisation forums; Castells on networked movements.
3. Against: limited policy change; dominance of richer-country activists; reliance on global networks; nationalist opposition shows other forms.
4. Alternative routes: nation states and international agreements.
5. Judgement: for example, effective at shaping debate, less effective at changing structures of power.

## Common mistakes

- Treating all opposition to globalisation as left-wing.
- Assuming the spread of democracy is continuous; evidence shows waves and reversals.
- Equating human rights with Westernisation without evaluating local agency.
- Ignoring environmental problems, which the syllabus names explicitly.
