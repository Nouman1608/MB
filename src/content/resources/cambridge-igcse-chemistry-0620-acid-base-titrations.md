This guide covers subtopic **12.2 Acid–base titrations** of Topic 12, Experimental techniques and chemical analysis, in the Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620 syllabus for 2026–2028. Both outcomes are **Core**. Titrations are also one of the experimental contexts listed for the practical papers, and the titration calculations in Topic 3 are **Supplement**; the worked calculation below is labelled *(Extended)*. Use the [acid–base titrations revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-acid-base-titrations-revision-notes/) for quick recall and the [acid–base titrations practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-acid-base-titrations-practice/) to test yourself. Choosing apparatus in general is covered in the [experimental design study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-experimental-design/).

## What 12.2 asks you to do

1. **Describe an acid–base titration** to include the use of a **burette**, a **volumetric pipette** and a **suitable indicator**.
2. **Describe how to identify the end-point** of a titration using an indicator.

A titration finds the exact volume of one solution that reacts completely with a measured volume of another. In an acid–base titration, the reaction is neutralisation: acid + alkali → salt + water.

## The apparatus and why each piece is used

| Apparatus | Role | Why this and not something else |
|---|---|---|
| **Volumetric pipette** (with pipette filler) | measures an exact, fixed volume, often 25.0 cm³, of one solution into the flask | far more accurate than a measuring cylinder for a fixed volume |
| **Burette** | adds the other solution and measures the volume added | accurate for any volume, and lets solution be added drop by drop |
| **Conical flask** | holds the solution being titrated | can be swirled without spilling |
| **Indicator** | shows the end-point by a sudden colour change | — |
| **White tile** | under the flask | makes the colour change easier to see |

## Choosing a suitable indicator

The syllabus reagent list includes **methyl orange** and **thymolphthalein**, and Topic 7 gives their colours:

| Indicator | Colour in acid | Colour in alkali |
|---|---|---|
| methyl orange | red | yellow |
| thymolphthalein | colourless | blue |

A **suitable** indicator changes colour **sharply** at the end-point. **Universal indicator is not suitable**: it passes gradually through a range of colours, so there is no single, sudden change to mark the end-point.

## The method, step by step

Here the alkali is in the flask and the acid in the burette; the method works equally well the other way round.

1. **Rinse the burette** with a little of the acid, then fill it using a small funnel. Remove the funnel, and run a little acid out so that the jet below the tap is full and has no air bubble.
2. **Record the initial burette reading** to the nearest 0.05 cm³, reading the bottom of the meniscus at eye level. It does not have to be 0.00 cm³.
3. **Rinse the volumetric pipette** with the alkali. Using a pipette filler, draw alkali up to the line and transfer exactly 25.0 cm³ into a conical flask.
4. **Add a few drops of indicator** to the flask, and stand it on a white tile.
5. **Run acid in from the burette** while swirling the flask.
6. **Near the end-point**, add the acid **drop by drop**, swirling after each drop.
7. **Stop at the end-point**, when one drop causes a permanent colour change. Record the final burette reading.
8. **Titre** = final reading − initial reading.
9. **Repeat** until you have **concordant** titres: results that agree closely, typically within 0.10 cm³.

### Why rinse with the solution, not water?

If the burette or pipette is wet with water, the solution inside is diluted, and the volume used no longer contains the expected amount of acid or alkali. Rinsing with the solution itself avoids this. The **conical flask**, however, can be rinsed with distilled water, because adding water does not change the **amount** of alkali delivered by the pipette.

## Identifying the end-point

The **end-point** is when the indicator **just changes colour permanently**, showing that the acid and alkali have exactly reacted. Describe it by naming the indicator and both colours.

- Acid added to alkali with **thymolphthalein**: **blue → colourless**.
- Acid added to alkali with **methyl orange**: **yellow → orange** (the colour between yellow and red), which turns red if too much acid is added.
- Alkali added to acid: reverse the colours (thymolphthalein colourless → blue; methyl orange red → orange).

A **rough titration** is done first, adding solution quickly to find the approximate end-point. It usually overshoots, so it is not used in the mean.

## Worked example 1: processing titration results

| | Rough | Run 1 | Run 2 | Run 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Final reading / cm³ | 24.80 | 24.15 | 24.90 | 25.30 |
| Initial reading / cm³ | 0.00 | 0.50 | 1.00 | 1.65 |
| Titre / cm³ | 24.80 | 23.65 | 23.90 | 23.65 |

1. Calculate each titre: final − initial. Run 1: 24.15 − 0.50 = 23.65 cm³.
2. Ignore the rough titre.
3. Choose **concordant** results: Runs 1 and 3 agree exactly; Run 2 differs by 0.25 cm³, so it is left out.
4. Mean titre = (23.65 + 23.65) ÷ 2 = **23.65 cm³**.

Every reading is recorded to **two decimal places**, ending in 0 or 5, because a burette is read to the nearest half-division (0.05 cm³).

## Worked example 2: a titration calculation *(Extended)*

*25.0 cm³ of sodium hydroxide solution is neutralised by a mean titre of 23.65 cm³ of 0.100 mol/dm³ hydrochloric acid. Calculate the concentration of the sodium hydroxide.*

```
HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O
```

1. Moles of HCl = 0.100 × (23.65 ÷ 1000) = 0.002365 mol.
2. Ratio 1 : 1, so moles of NaOH = 0.002365 mol.
3. Concentration = 0.002365 ÷ (25.0 ÷ 1000) = **0.0946 mol/dm³**.

With sulfuric acid the ratio changes: H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O, so moles of acid = **half** the moles of alkali. Always use the equation, not an assumed 1 : 1.

## Titration and making salts

Titration is also how a **soluble salt** is made from an acid and an alkali (Topic 7). Find the volumes needed with an indicator, then **repeat without the indicator**, using the same volumes, so the salt solution is not contaminated. Then crystallise the salt (see [separation and purification](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-separation-and-purification/)).

## Common mistakes

- **Writing "pipette" or "measuring cylinder"** for the fixed volume: use **volumetric pipette**.
- **Choosing universal indicator.**
- **Giving one colour** for the end-point instead of the change from one to the other.
- **Recording burette readings to one decimal place**, or values like 23.63.
- **Including the rough titre** in the mean.
- **Rinsing the burette with water** as the final rinse.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** (a titration): name each piece of apparatus as you use it, the indicator, swirling, drop-by-drop addition, the colour change and repeating for concordant results.
- **Describe** (end-point): indicator, starting colour, final colour.
- **Explain** (a step): give the reason, such as "white tile, to see the colour change clearly".
- **Calculate** *(Extended)*: moles from the known solution, ratio from the equation, concentration of the unknown.
