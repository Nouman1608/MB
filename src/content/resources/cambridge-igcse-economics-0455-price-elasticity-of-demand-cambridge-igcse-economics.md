## 2.7.1 Definition

**Price elasticity of demand (PED)** measures the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in the price of the product.

```
PED = percentage change in quantity demanded / percentage change in price
```

## 2.7.2 Calculation and interpretation

Because price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions, PED is negative. Judge elasticity by its size, ignoring the sign.

| Size of PED | Description | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Perfectly inelastic | Quantity does not respond at all |
| Between 0 and 1 | Inelastic | Quantity changes by a smaller percentage than price |
| 1 | Unitary | Quantity and price change by the same percentage |
| Greater than 1 | Elastic | Quantity changes by a larger percentage than price |
| Infinite | Perfectly elastic | Any price rise causes quantity demanded to fall to zero |

### Rearranging the formula

Many questions give PED and ask for a quantity or a price.

```
% change in quantity demanded = PED x % change in price
% change in price needed = % change in quantity wanted / PED
```

**Worked example 1: predicting sales.** A gym charges 40 a month and has 1,500 members. PED for membership is −1.6. It raises the fee by 5%.

1. % change in quantity = −1.6 × 5 = **−8%**.
2. New membership = 1,500 × 0.92 = **1,380**.
3. New fee = 40 × 1.05 = 42.
4. Revenue changes from 40 × 1,500 = 60,000 to 42 × 1,380 = **57,960**, a fall of 2,040. Demand is elastic, so the price rise lowered revenue.

**Worked example 2: finding the price change needed.** A bus company wants 12% more passengers. PED for its tickets is −0.4.

1. % change in price needed = 12 ÷ (−0.4) = **−30%**.
2. A ticket costing 2.50 would have to fall to 2.50 × 0.70 = **1.75**.

Because demand is inelastic, a large price cut is needed for a modest rise in passengers.

### Diagrams showing different PED

Draw on the same scales so that slopes can be compared.

- **Perfectly inelastic:** a vertical demand curve.
- **Perfectly elastic:** a horizontal demand curve.
- **Inelastic over a price range:** a steep curve; a large price change gives a small quantity change.
- **Elastic over a price range:** a shallow curve; a small price change gives a large quantity change.
- **Unitary at every point:** a curve that bends towards the origin, so price × quantity stays the same along it.

Slope and elasticity are not the same thing. On a straight-line demand curve, the slope is constant but PED is higher at high prices and lower at low prices, because the same change in price is a smaller percentage of a high price.

## 2.7.3 Determinants of PED

| Determinant | Makes demand more elastic when | Makes demand more inelastic when |
|---|---|---|
| Substitutes | Many close substitutes exist | Few or no close substitutes |
| Necessity | The product is a luxury or easy to do without | The product is a necessity |
| Share of income | Spending on it is a large part of income | Spending on it is a tiny part of income |
| Time | Buyers have time to find alternatives | The period is short |
| Definition of the product | Narrow: one brand | Broad: the whole product category |
| Habit | Bought on impulse | Habit-forming or addictive |

**Worked example 3: ranking products.** Rank these by PED, most elastic first, with reasons.

1. **One brand of bottled water:** many identical rival brands, so a small price rise sends buyers elsewhere. Most elastic.
2. **Foreign holidays:** a luxury and a large share of income, so buyers respond strongly, but there is no exact substitute for a holiday abroad.
3. **Salt:** a tiny share of income and few substitutes. Very inelastic.

The ranking depends on reasoning from the determinants, not on memorising products.

## 2.7.4 PED and total spending (revenue)

Total spending by consumers on a product equals the revenue of its sellers: price × quantity.

| Demand | Price rise | Price cut |
|---|---|---|
| Elastic | Revenue falls | Revenue rises |
| Inelastic | Revenue rises | Revenue falls |
| Unitary | Revenue unchanged | Revenue unchanged |

### Measuring the change on a diagram

Revenue is the rectangle formed by the price and quantity. After a price cut from P1 to P2, with quantity rising from Q1 to Q2:

- **Revenue lost** = (P1 − P2) × Q1: the lower price on the units already being sold.
- **Revenue gained** = P2 × (Q2 − Q1): the new price on the extra units.

**Worked example 4.** A café cuts the price of a sandwich from 5 to 4, and daily sales rise from 100 to 150.

1. Revenue lost = (5 − 4) × 100 = **100**.
2. Revenue gained = 4 × (150 − 100) = **200**.
3. Net change = 200 − 100 = **+100**. Revenue rises from 500 to 600.
4. PED = (+50%) ÷ (−20%) = **−2.5**: elastic, which is why the gain area is larger than the loss area.

If sales had risen only to 115, the gain would be 4 × 15 = 60, less than the loss of 100, so revenue would fall to 460. PED would be +15% ÷ −20% = −0.75, inelastic.

## 2.7.5 Significance of PED

### Consumers

- When demand is inelastic, consumers keep buying after a price rise, so they spend more in total and have less for other things. Necessities such as basic food and fuel take a larger share of a poor household's budget.
- When demand is elastic, consumers protect themselves by switching to substitutes.

### Producers

- **Pricing:** a firm with inelastic demand can raise price and increase revenue; a firm with elastic demand may raise revenue by cutting price.
- **Passing on cost increases:** if costs rise, a firm facing inelastic demand can pass more of the rise to buyers without losing many sales.
- **Discounts:** firms may offer lower prices to groups whose demand is elastic, such as students, while charging more to others.

### Government

- **Taxation:** taxing products with inelastic demand raises more revenue, because sales fall little. A tax of 2 on a product selling 10 million units, with sales falling only 3%, raises 2 × 9.7 million = **19.4 million**.
- **Discouraging consumption:** for the same reason, a tax on a harmful product with inelastic demand reduces consumption only slightly; other measures may be needed alongside it.
- **Subsidies:** a subsidy that lowers the price of a product with elastic demand, such as public transport in a city with good alternatives, raises its use a lot more than if demand were inelastic.

## Common mistakes

- Putting price on top of the formula.
- Calling a PED of −2 "inelastic" because it is negative. Use its size.
- Using the new value as the base for a percentage change.
- Stating the revenue rule without saying whether demand is elastic or inelastic.
- Confusing a steep curve with a perfectly inelastic one. Only a vertical curve has PED of zero.
- Naming a determinant without linking it to the product in the question.

## Exam technique for the 0455 command words

- **Define:** "the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in price".
- **Calculate:** show both percentage changes, then the division; then state elastic or inelastic and why.
- **Explain:** link a determinant to buyer behaviour: "few substitutes, so buyers cannot switch, so quantity falls little".
- **Analyse:** use the revenue rectangles to show why revenue rises or falls.
- **Discuss:** weigh PED against other factors, such as competitors' reactions or costs, before recommending a price change or tax.
