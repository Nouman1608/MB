Edexcel International A Level English Literature (YET01) is modular: four externally assessed units,
each 2 hours, **50 marks** and 25% of the full IAL. Units 1 (Post-2000 Poetry and Prose) and 2 (Drama)
form the International Advanced Subsidiary; Units 3 (Poetry and Prose) and 4 (Shakespeare and
Pre-1900 Poetry) complete the full IAL. Their internal splits differ: Units 1, 2 and 4 each carry two
sections of 25 marks, while **Unit 3 carries one 20-mark section and one 30-mark section**. These
notes complement the site's guides to
[Post-2000 Poetry and Prose](/resources/a-level-edexcel-english-literature-post-2000-poetry-and-prose/)
and [Unit 2 Drama](/resources/a-level-edexcel-english-literature-drama/).

## Fifty marks in two hours is unusually generous -- and that is a trap

At 2.4 minutes per mark, this is one of the more time-rich A-level papers, and the temptation is to
write at length. Length is not the criterion. **Exam-preparation priority**: use the time on planning
and on precision rather than on volume. Ten minutes planning a 25-mark essay is a proportionate
investment here in a way it would not be on a denser paper, and it consistently produces a better
structured argument than starting to write at minute two.

## Unit 3's sections are not equal -- plan the split deliberately

A 20-mark section and a 30-mark section in a single two-hour paper means roughly 48 minutes and 72
minutes respectively. **Exam-preparation priority**: rehearse Unit 3 to that uneven split
specifically. Treating it like Units 1, 2 and 4 -- an hour a section -- gives twelve minutes to the
20-mark section that it does not need and takes twelve from the 30-mark section that does.

## Comparison must be integrated, not sequential

Where units require comparison, marks come from the comparative argument itself. **Exam-preparation
priority**: practise paragraphs organised by *point of comparison* rather than by text. A paragraph
that discusses how both writers handle a particular technique or theme, moving between them,
demonstrates comparison; two paragraphs discussing each text in turn leave the comparison for the
examiner to construct.

## Context earns marks when it explains the writing, not when it precedes it

Contextual knowledge -- literary, historical, generic -- is credited when it illuminates a specific
choice the writer made. **Exam-preparation priority**: attach context to particular moments in your
notes rather than keeping it as a separate paragraph of background. A sentence of context inside an
analytical paragraph is worth considerably more than an introductory paragraph of biography.

## Pre-1900 poetry and Shakespeare reward close attention to form

Unit 4's material makes formal features -- metre, rhyme, structure, dramatic verse -- available as
analytical evidence in a way that prose does not. **Exam-preparation priority**: practise writing
about form specifically: what a caesura does at a particular line, why a rhyme falls where it does,
how a soliloquy's placement affects the audience. Formal analysis is the most reliable way to
demonstrate the close reading these units credit.

## Worked routine: an integrated comparative paragraph

The routine below is an original model written for this resource, not a reproduction of any official
past paper, set text or mark scheme.

```
Step 1 - open with the point of comparison, not a text:
"Both writers use enclosed domestic settings to signal
constraint, but to different ends."

Step 2 - evidence from the first text, analysed:
A short embedded quotation, one word analysed, the method named.

Step 3 - pivot with a comparative connective:
"Where X confines her protagonist physically, Y..." -- the pivot
sentence is what makes the paragraph comparative.

Step 4 - evidence from the second text, analysed:
Same depth. An imbalanced paragraph reads as two half-arguments.

Step 5 - close on the difference and why it matters:
What does the contrast reveal about each writer's concerns? This
sentence is the comparative judgement.
```

Step 3 is the mechanical heart of comparison. Without an explicit pivot, a paragraph containing two
texts is still two separate discussions.

## Before/during exam checklist

- **Before the exams**: use the generous time budget on planning, not volume; rehearse Unit 3 to its
  uneven 20/30 split (about 48 and 72 minutes); practise integrated comparative paragraphs; attach
  contextual notes to specific moments in the texts; practise writing about form for Unit 4.
- **During any unit**: spend up to ten minutes planning a 25-mark essay before writing.
- **In every comparative paragraph**: open on the point of comparison, evidence both texts to equal
  depth, pivot explicitly, and close on what the difference reveals.
- **In every use of context**: place it inside an analytical paragraph explaining a specific choice.

## Self-test

1. How much time per mark do these units allow, and what should it be spent on?
2. How does Unit 3 differ structurally from Units 1, 2 and 4?
3. What makes a paragraph genuinely comparative?
4. When does contextual knowledge earn marks?

**Answers:** 1. About 2.4 minutes per mark (120 minutes for 50 marks) -- which should be spent on
planning and precision rather than on length. 2. Unit 3 has a 20-mark section and a 30-mark section,
whereas Units 1, 2 and 4 each have two 25-mark sections -- so its time should be split roughly 48/72
rather than evenly. 3. An explicit pivot between the texts within a single paragraph organised around
a point of comparison, with both texts evidenced to equal depth. 4. When it explains a specific choice
the writer made, inside an analytical paragraph -- not as a separate paragraph of background.
