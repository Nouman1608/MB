## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by a market. **[2]**

**2.** State the sector of the economy for each activity.

**(a)** a dairy farm **[1]**
**(b)** a cheese factory **[1]**
**(c)** a supermarket **[1]**
**(d)** an online bank **[1]**

**3.** Explain **one** difference between a good and a service, using an example of each. **[2]**

**4.** State the difference between a factor market and a product market. **[2]**

**5.** Explain **one** benefit of specialisation for a producer. **[2]**

**6.** A jar of jam sells for £3.00. The fruit grower is paid £0.45 and the factory sells the jar to the shop for £1.80. Calculate the value added by the shop as a percentage of the final price. **[2]**

### Section B: longer and structured questions

**7.** The table shows employment by sector in Country Z.

| Sector | Year 1 (millions) | Year 20 (millions) |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | 3.0 | 1.5 |
| Secondary | 4.5 | 3.5 |
| Tertiary | 7.5 | 12.0 |
| Total | 15.0 | 17.0 |

**(a)** Calculate the tertiary sector's share of total employment in Year 1 and in Year 20. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in primary sector employment. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain **one** likely reason for the growth of tertiary employment. **[2]**

**8.** A large new airport opens near a town. Analyse how this could affect both factor markets and product markets in the town. **[6]**

**9.** Explain **two** costs of specialisation for workers. **[4]**

**10.** Read the extract.

> The Easton region (invented) has 48,000 jobs. Of these, 14,400 are at the region's steelworks, and local suppliers to the steelworks employ a further 3,600 people. The steelworks has a skilled workforce and its own rail link, but world steel prices have been falling.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of the region's jobs at the steelworks. **[1]**
**(b)** If the steelworks and its suppliers closed, calculate the percentage of the region's jobs that would be lost. **[1]**
**(c)** Evaluate whether specialising in steel has been good for the Easton region. **[8]**

**11.** Explain why specialisation depends on exchange, and why money makes exchange easier. **[3]**

**12.** Evaluate the costs and benefits of specialisation for a country. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** A market is any arrangement that brings buyers and sellers together [1] to exchange goods, services or factors of production; it need not be a physical place, as online markets show [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "A place where things are sold" alone earns one mark at most.

**2. (a)** Primary [1]. **(b)** Secondary [1]. **(c)** Tertiary [1]. **(d)** Tertiary [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A supermarket sells goods, but retailing is a service, so it is tertiary.

**3.** A good is tangible and can be stored, such as a bicycle [1]; a service is intangible and usually consumed as it is produced, such as a haircut [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Each mark needs the feature and a matching example.

**4.** In a factor market, factors of production such as labour are bought and sold [1]; in a product market, goods and services are bought and sold [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Adding who buys and who sells in each market strengthens the answer.

**5.** By specialising in one product, a firm's workers become skilled and use specialist machinery [1], so output per worker rises and cost per unit falls, making the firm more competitive [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The second mark is for the effect on cost or output, not just "it is better".

**6.** Value added by shop = 3.00 − 1.80 = £1.20 [1]. 1.20 ÷ 3.00 × 100 = **40%** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The grower's £0.45 is already included in the factory's £1.80, so do not subtract it again.

**7. (a)** Year 1: 7.5 ÷ 15.0 × 100 = **50%** [1]. Year 20: 12.0 ÷ 17.0 × 100 = **70.6%** [1].
**(b)** (1.5 − 3.0) ÷ 3.0 × 100 = **−50%** [1].
**(c)** As incomes rise, people spend a larger share on services such as leisure, health and finance [1], so firms in the tertiary sector hire more workers [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Machinery replacing farm workers explains the fall in primary jobs, not the rise in tertiary jobs, unless you link the two.

**8.** The airport needs workers, so demand for labour in the town rises [1], which may raise wages or reduce unemployment [1]. Firms may also demand more land, raising rents [1]. Workers' higher incomes are spent locally, so demand rises in product markets such as shops and restaurants [1]. Visitors also buy hotel rooms, taxis and meals [1], so these firms expand and demand still more labour, showing the two markets are linked [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Name both types of market and connect them. A list of jobs created is not analysis.

**9.** Work can become repetitive and boring [1], which lowers motivation and job satisfaction [1]. Workers' skills become narrow [1], so if the job disappears they may struggle to find other work without retraining [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Two separate costs, each developed, earn all four marks.

**10. (a)** 14,400 ÷ 48,000 × 100 = **30%** [1].
**(b)** (14,400 + 3,600) ÷ 48,000 × 100 = **37.5%** [1].
**(c)** Benefits: the region has built a skilled workforce [1] and infrastructure such as the rail link, which attracts suppliers and lowers costs [1]; the steelworks provides many well-paid jobs that support local spending [1]. Costs: 37.5% of jobs depend on one industry [1], so a closure would cause mass unemployment and falling spending in local shops [1]; falling world steel prices make this risk real [1]. Judgement: specialisation has brought income and skills, but the region is now highly exposed [1]; whether it has been good overall depends on whether workers' skills can transfer to other industries and whether new employers can be attracted [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the extract: the rail link, the falling prices and the 37.5% figure all support evaluation.

**11.** A specialist produces only a narrow range of products, so it must trade them for the other goods it needs [1]. Without money, it would need to find someone who wants its product and has what it wants, at the same time [1]. Money is accepted by everyone, so it lets the specialist sell to one person and buy from another [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The idea that swapping goods needs both sides to want what the other has is the key to the third mark.

**12.** Benefits: a country concentrating on what it produces well can raise total output [1] and lower costs per unit [1], then export and import the rest, so consumers enjoy wider choice and lower prices [1]. Export earnings create jobs and incomes [1]. Costs: the country depends on imports for goods it no longer makes, which may be disrupted [1]; if world demand or prices for its main export fall, incomes and jobs fall sharply [1]. Judgement: specialisation is likely to benefit a country overall [1], especially if it specialises in several products rather than one, so that a fall in one market does not damage the whole economy [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* A judgement that says what it depends on (here, how many products the country relies on) shows evaluation.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling retail or banking secondary.
- Describing factor and product markets without linking them.
- Forgetting to subtract earlier stages when calculating value added.
- Evaluations that list benefits and costs with no judgement.
- Ignoring figures and details given in an extract.
