Like the exam, this set uses a **single literary non-fiction text divided into sections**, with
questions on individual sections and on the whole text.

## The text: "The Last Ferry" (original travel writing)

**Section 1**

> The guidebook promised that the ferry left "every hour, weather permitting". It did not say who
> decided what the weather permitted. On the quay at Port Ellan, a man in an orange coat sat on an
> upturned crate, smoking with the slow concentration of someone who had all day, which, it turned
> out, he did. "Wind," he said, before I had asked anything. He pointed at a sky so blue and still
> that a cloud would have looked like a mistake.

**Section 2**

> I waited. I bought tea from a café whose owner apologised for the cups, then for the tea, then for
> the island in general. I counted the boats in the harbour: eleven, all tied up, all rocking gently
> as if asleep. Children fished from the harbour wall with string and bacon rind and caught nothing,
> loudly. By three o'clock I had read the guidebook twice and begun to resent its confidence. By four
> I had stopped looking at my watch. Somewhere in that hour the island stopped being an obstacle and
> became a place.

**Section 3**

> At half past five the man in the orange coat stood up, stretched, and walked to the smallest boat.
> "Last ferry," he said, as if it had been his plan all along. Out on the water the wind arrived
> exactly as he had promised, sudden and cold, slapping spray across the deck. I held the rail and
> watched Port Ellan shrink to a line of white houses, then a line, then nothing. I had come to see
> the famous cliffs on the far side. I never did see them properly; it was dark when we landed. What
> I remember is the quay, the apologetic tea, and a man who knew the sky better than any book.

## Section A — Short questions

**1.** From Section 1, list **four** things you learn about the man in the orange coat. **[4]**

**2.** In Section 1, what does the writer suggest about the guidebook? Use **one** quotation. **[2]**

**3.** Explain the effect of the comparison "a sky so blue and still that a cloud would have looked like
a mistake". **[3]**

**4.** In Section 2, the café owner "apologised for the cups, then for the tea, then for the island in
general". Explain how the structure of this phrase creates humour. **[3]**

**5.** Explain what the writer means by "the island stopped being an obstacle and became a place".
**[2]**

**6.** Identify **two** words or phrases in Section 3 that show the force of the wind, and explain the
effect of **one** of them. **[3]**

## Section B — Longer questions

**7.** How does the writer use language in Section 2 to show the passing of time? **[8]**

**8.** How does the writer present the man in the orange coat across the **whole text**? **[8]**

**9.** How has the writer structured the text to interest the reader? You could write about the
opening, shifts in focus, and the ending. **[8]**

**10.** A student said: "This text is less about travel than about learning to be patient." To what
extent do you agree? Refer to the **whole text**. **[10]**

**11.** Write **one** paragraph analysing the final sentence of the text, explaining how it sums up the
writer's perspective. **[4]**

## Answers

**How these are marked.** Short questions are point-marked as shown. For the longer questions, the
bracketed credits show one way to reach the tariff; an answer that analyses methods precisely and
links them to effects and the writer's perspective would sit in the top band. Other valid points of
the same quality earn the same credit.

**1.** Any four, [1] each: he wears an orange coat; he sits on an upturned crate; he is smoking; he is
in no hurry / has all day; he speaks before he is asked; he says the problem is wind; he points at the
sky. Maximum 4.

**2.** The guidebook is unreliable or over-confident [1], shown by "It did not say who decided what the
weather permitted", which mocks the vague promise of "every hour" [1].

**3.** The exaggeration (hyperbole) stresses how perfect the weather appears [1]; calling a cloud "a
mistake" makes the sky seem flawless, almost artificial [1], which makes the man's claim of "Wind"
seem absurd to the writer and builds humour and curiosity about whether he is right [1].

**4.** The list grows from small things to something huge [1]: from "cups" to "tea" to "the island in
general" [1]. The bathos of apologising for an entire island exaggerates the owner's nervous
politeness and makes the reader smile [1].

**5.** At first the island was only something in the way of the journey [1], but as the writer slowed down, they
began to notice and value it as somewhere worth experiencing for itself [1].

**6.** Any two words or phrases [1]: "sudden and cold"; "slapping spray"; "held the rail". Effect of
one [2], for example: "slapping" is an aggressive verb, almost like a blow, so the wind seems to attack
the deck, proving the man right and contrasting with the still sky earlier [2].

**7.** Credit up to 8: analysis of time phrases "By three o'clock… By four", which mark the hours
and show slow progress [2]; the change from resenting the guidebook to "stopped looking at my watch",
showing a shift from impatience to acceptance [2]; the list of small observations (counting eleven
boats, children fishing) suggesting the writer fills empty time by noticing details [2]; the boats "rocking
gently as if asleep" and the children catching "nothing, loudly", creating a lazy, unhurried mood in
which nothing happens [2].

**8.** Credit up to 8: in Section 1 he seems lazy and unhelpful, sitting on a crate "with all day",
which the writer gently mocks [2]; his one-word answer "Wind" makes him seem mysterious and
knowing [2]; in Section 3 he acts at the right moment, "as if it had been his plan all along", so the
reader realises he understood the sea all the time [2]; the final description, "a man who knew the sky
better than any book", presents him as wiser than the guidebook, reversing the opening impression [2].

**9.** Credit up to 8: the opening contrasts the guidebook's promise with reality, setting up
the tension between plans and nature [2]; Section 2 slows the pace with small details and time
markers, so the reader experiences the waiting [2]; Section 3 brings sudden movement and the wind,
a turning point that proves the man right [2]; the ending is reflective and cyclical, returning to the
quay and the man rather than the cliffs the writer came to see, showing what truly mattered [2].

**10.** Credit up to 10. A judgement [2]; agreeing points such as the writer's shift from resenting the
delay to accepting it, shown by "stopped looking at my watch" [2], and the ending, where the memories
are of waiting, not the cliffs [2]; qualifying points such as the travel details (harbour, ferry,
spray) that still give a strong sense of place [2]; and an overall conclusion linking both, for
example that the travel experience is the means by which the lesson about patience is learned [2].

**11.** Credit up to 4: the list "the quay, the apologetic tea, and a man…" gathers the small moments of
waiting [1], replacing the famous cliffs with everyday memories [1]; the comparison "better than any
book" sets local knowledge above the guidebook [1], so the writer's final perspective is that real
understanding comes from people and patience rather than plans [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Retelling events in Questions 7–9 instead of analysing the writer's methods.
- Using evidence from Section 3 for a question that names Section 2.
- In Question 10, agreeing or disagreeing without weighing the other view.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is the real exam text about this length?**
The specification does not fix a length. Practise with texts of different lengths and focus on reading
each section closely.

**Why is there no comparison question?**
Paper 1 Section A sets a single text. Comparing two texts belongs to Paper 2.
