Revise first with the [2.1 diurnal energy budgets study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-diurnal-energy-budgets/) and the [2.1 revision notes](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-diurnal-energy-budgets-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Define the term "albedo". **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between sensible heat transfer and latent heat transfer. **[2]**

**3.** A dry sand surface with an albedo of 0.35 receives 650 W/m² of incoming solar radiation. Calculate the solar radiation it absorbs. Show your working. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why the lowest surface temperature usually occurs around dawn. **[2]**

**5.** Explain how the formation of dew affects the night-time energy budget of a surface. **[3]**

**6.** Explain why cloud cover reduces the diurnal range of temperature. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** The table shows fictional midday energy fluxes for a forest canopy.

| Component | Flux (W/m²) |
|---|---|
| Incoming solar radiation | 700 |
| Reflected solar radiation (albedo 0.15) | 105 |
| Longwave radiation (net loss) | 90 |
| Subsurface absorption | 25 |
| Sensible heat transfer | 150 |
| Latent heat transfer | X |

**(a)** Assuming the budget balances, calculate the value of X. Show your working. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate X as a percentage of the absorbed solar radiation. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why latent heat transfer is large over a forest. **[3]**

**8.** The table shows air temperatures (°C) over 24 hours on a clear day at two fictional sites at the same latitude: site X in a hot desert and site Y on a humid tropical coast.

| Time | 00:00 | 03:00 | 06:00 | 09:00 | 12:00 | 15:00 | 18:00 | 21:00 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Site X | 14 | 10 | 8 | 22 | 36 | 40 | 30 | 20 |
| Site Y | 24 | 23 | 23 | 26 | 29 | 30 | 28 | 26 |

**(a)** Calculate the diurnal temperature range at each site. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why both sites reach their maximum temperature at 15:00 rather than at 12:00. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain the difference in diurnal range between the two sites, referring to components of the energy budget. **[5]**

**9.** Explain how the night-time energy budget of a concrete surface differs from that of a grass field. **[5]**

**10.** Explain the factors that affect the amount of incoming shortwave radiation reaching a surface. **[4]**

**11.** "Water availability is the most important factor affecting the diurnal energy budget of a surface." Discuss this view. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** The proportion of incoming solar radiation [1] that is reflected by a surface, often given as a decimal or percentage [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Sensible heat transfer is the movement of heat between the surface and the air by conduction and convection, which can be felt as a temperature change [1]. Latent heat transfer is energy used in, or released by, a change of state of water, such as evaporation or condensation, without a change in temperature [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Reflected = 650 x 0.35 = 227.5 W/m² [1]; absorbed = 650 - 227.5 = 422.5 W/m² [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Through the night there is no solar input and the surface loses energy by longwave radiation [1], so it cools continuously until the sun rises and the budget returns to surplus [1]. **[2]**

**5.** As the surface cools to the dew point, water vapour condenses on it [1]. Condensation releases latent heat to the surface [1], which offsets part of the longwave loss and slows further cooling [1]. **[3]**

**6.** By day, cloud reflects and absorbs incoming solar radiation, so less reaches the surface and the maximum temperature is lower [1]. By night, cloud absorbs longwave radiation from the surface and re-radiates it back downwards [1], reducing the net loss so the minimum temperature is higher [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** Absorbed = 700 - 105 = 595 W/m² [1]; X = 595 - 90 - 25 - 150 = 330 W/m² [1].

**(b)** 330 / 595 x 100 = 55.5% [1].

**(c)** Trees transpire large amounts of water drawn from the soil through their roots [1]. Rain intercepted on the large leaf area evaporates directly [1]. Moist air within the canopy is mixed with drier air above by wind, so evaporation continues [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** Site X: 40 - 8 = 32°C [1]; site Y: 30 - 23 = 7°C [1].

**(b)** Incoming radiation peaks around midday, but the surface continues to gain more energy than it loses into the early afternoon [1], so temperature keeps rising until the budget comes into balance at about 15:00 [1].

**(c)** Site X has little water, so very little absorbed energy is used as latent heat [1] and most goes into sensible heat, so the surface and air heat strongly by day [1]. Desert sand may also have a fairly high albedo, but clear skies give intense incoming radiation [1]. At night, clear skies and dry air allow large longwave losses, so site X cools rapidly [1]. At site Y, evaporation from moist surfaces uses energy by day, and humid air and cloud absorb and return longwave radiation at night, so both heating and cooling are reduced [1]. **[9]**

**9.** Concrete absorbs and stores more heat during the day because it conducts heat well and has a low albedo [1], so it returns more absorbed energy to the surface at night [1]. Its surface therefore stays warmer and emits more longwave radiation, but cools more slowly overall [1]. Grass is a poor conductor with little stored heat, so its surface cools quickly [1]. Grass is more likely to cool to the dew point, so dew forms and releases latent heat, while concrete rarely reaches it [1]. **[5]**

**10.** Sun angle: a high sun concentrates radiation on a smaller area and passes through less atmosphere [1], so latitude and season matter [1]. Day length and time of day determine how long and how strongly radiation is received [1]. Cloud, dust and pollution reflect and absorb radiation before it reaches the surface [1]. **[4]**

**11.** For the view: water availability decides how much energy is used in evaporation as latent heat [1], so wet surfaces warm less by day than dry ones [1]. Humid air absorbs longwave radiation, reducing night-time cooling [1]. Moisture affects dew formation, which releases latent heat at night [1].

Against the view: albedo controls how much radiation is absorbed in the first place, as shown by the contrast between snow and asphalt [1]. Cloud cover has a large effect on both incoming radiation by day and longwave loss by night [1]. The conductivity of the material decides how much energy is stored and returned at night [1].

Judgement: water availability is the main control on how absorbed energy is partitioned, but albedo and cloud control how much energy there is to partition, so its importance depends on the surface and conditions [1]. **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating reflected radiation and stopping there when the question asks for absorbed radiation.
- Explaining night-time cooling without mentioning longwave radiation.
- Describing temperature data without quoting the maximum, minimum and range.
- Saying cloud keeps nights warm "because it acts like a blanket" without explaining the longwave mechanism.
- Answering "discuss" questions with only one side.
