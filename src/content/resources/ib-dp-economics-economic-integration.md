## Preferential trade agreements

A preferential trade agreement gives the countries in it better terms of trade with each other than with outsiders. The guide names three kinds.

- **Bilateral:** between two countries only. Fictional Veldoria and Tamarek agree to cut tariffs on each other's goods.
- **Regional:** between several countries, normally neighbours. A group of countries in one area agree common trading terms.
- **Multilateral:** between many countries across the world. The guide gives the WTO as the multilateral body.

The difference is the number of parties and the geographic spread. A multilateral deal applies terms to a very wide group, while bilateral and regional deals favour a smaller group and so discriminate against everyone else.

## Types of trading bloc

The three types differ in how deep the integration goes. Each type includes the features of the one before it.

| Feature | Free trade area | Customs union | Common market |
|---|---|---|---|
| No tariffs or quotas between members | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Each member sets its own tariffs on outsiders | Yes | No | No |
| Common external tariff on outsiders | No | Yes | Yes |
| Free movement of labour and capital between members | No | No | Yes |

**Free trade area.** Trade between members is free, yet each one keeps its own policy towards non-members. This creates a problem. Suppose Tamarek charges 5% on goods from non-member Orsin, and Veldoria charges 30%. An Orsin good costs 80 at the factory. Sent directly to Veldoria, it costs 80 × 1.30 = 104. Sent through Tamarek, it costs 80 × 1.05 = 84 and then enters Veldoria duty-free. The saving is 104 − 84 = 20 a unit. Free trade areas therefore apply rules of origin, which test whether a good truly comes from a member.

**Customs union.** All members charge the same external tariff. Orsin goods now cost 104 in both Tamarek and Veldoria, so the detour pays nothing. The price is that members lose control over their own tariff rates.

**Common market.** A common market adds free movement of labour and capital. A worker in Tamarek can take a job in Veldoria without a work permit. Firms can build factories in any member state.

## Advantages of trading blocs

### Greater access to markets and economies of scale

A firm in a small country has a small home market. Inside a bloc it can sell to all members without tariffs. Higher output can lower average cost.

Worked example. Fictional firm Kessan Bearings has fixed costs of 600 000 and a variable cost of 4 a unit. At home it sells 50 000 units.

Average cost = (600 000 + 4 × 50 000) ÷ 50 000 = 800 000 ÷ 50 000 = **16**.

After the bloc forms it sells 300 000 units.

Average cost = (600 000 + 4 × 300 000) ÷ 300 000 = 1 800 000 ÷ 300 000 = **6**.

Average cost falls by 10, a fall of 62.5%. The firm can cut prices and still profit. Evaluation: this only helps if demand in partner states is large enough and the firm can actually raise output.

### Greater employment opportunities

Where there is freedom of labour, workers can move to where jobs are. Suppose Tamarek has a labour force of 2 000 000 and 11% are unemployed, which is 220 000 people. If 40 000 unemployed workers move to Veldoria and find jobs, Tamarek's unemployed fall to 180 000 and its labour force to 1 960 000. Unemployment is 180 000 ÷ 1 960 000 = **9.2%**. The workers gain, and Veldoria's firms fill vacancies. Evaluation: Tamarek may lose skilled workers it needs, and wages in Veldoria may come under pressure.

### Stronger bargaining power

A bloc negotiates as one party in multilateral talks. A group of small economies has more weight together than any member has alone.

### Greater political stability and cooperation

Countries with strong trade links have more to lose from conflict. Regular meetings to manage the bloc build habits of cooperation.

### Trade creation (HL only)

Trade creation happens when a member switches from a high-cost domestic producer to a lower-cost partner. Resources move to their better use, so efficiency rises and prices fall.

Worked example. Fictional Veldoria makes gadgets at a cost of 100. Partner Tamarek makes them at 90, and outsider Orsin at 80. Before the bloc, Veldoria charges 30% on all imports.

| Source | Cost | Price in Veldoria before the bloc | Price after the bloc |
|---|---|---|---|
| Domestic | 100 | 100 | 100 |
| Tamarek | 90 | 90 × 1.30 = 117 | 90 |
| Orsin | 80 | 80 × 1.30 = 104 | 104 |

Before the bloc, domestic gadgets are cheapest at 100. After it, Tamarek's price of 90 beats both. Veldoria switches to Tamarek. This is **trade creation**. If 1 000 units switch, the saving in production cost is (100 − 90) × 1 000 = 10 000.

## Disadvantages of trading blocs

### Trade diversion (HL only)

Trade diversion happens when a member drops a cheaper non-member supplier for a dearer partner, only because partner goods now enter tariff-free.

Worked example. Veldoria also buys textiles. Its own cost is 135, Tamarek's is 100 and Orsin's is 80. The 30% tariff applies before the bloc.

| Source | Cost | Price before | Price after |
|---|---|---|---|
| Domestic | 135 | 135 | 135 |
| Tamarek | 100 | 100 × 1.30 = 130 | 100 |
| Orsin | 80 | 80 × 1.30 = 104 | 104 |

Before the bloc, Veldoria buys from Orsin at 104. After it, Tamarek's price of 100 is lowest, so the trade is **diverted** to Tamarek.

Consumers pay 4 less (104 − 100). But the real resource cost rises by 100 − 80 = 20 a unit, because the world's cheapest producer lost the sale. The government also loses the tariff it used to collect on Orsin's goods, 80 × 0.30 = 24 a unit. Net effect per unit = 4 − 24 = −20, which equals the rise in resource cost. On 5 000 units the net loss is 100 000. A bloc with more trade creation than diversion is better for its members. The result depends on how far partner costs are from world costs.

### Loss of sovereignty

Members accept common rules. In a customs union they cannot set their own external tariff. In a common market they also accept free movement of workers. Decisions are made jointly, so a government cannot always do what its voters want.

### Challenge to multilateral trading negotiations

Blocs favour insiders and discriminate against outsiders. Members may feel less need for a global deal, and negotiating blocs are harder to bring to agreement than individual countries. Many separate deals can also overlap and conflict. This can slow the WTO's work.

## Monetary union

In a monetary union, member countries adopt a single currency run by one central bank. It goes beyond a common market because the exchange rate between members disappears.

### Advantages and disadvantages (HL only)

The guide lists the heading only, so the points below are standard analysis. Check them against what your teacher expects.

**Advantages**

- No conversion costs between members. A firm that converts 2 400 000 units of currency a year at a 1.5% cost saves 2 400 000 × 0.015 = **36 000** a year.
- No exchange-rate risk between members, so planning and investment are easier.
- Prices are easy to compare across borders, which raises competition.
- A credible central bank may bring lower inflation.

**Disadvantages**

- Members lose their own monetary policy. One interest rate must suit all. If Veldoria has inflation of 7% and Tamarek 0.5%, and their weights in the union are 60% and 40%, the union-wide figure is 0.6 × 7 + 0.4 × 0.5 = **4.4%**. A policy suited to 4.4% is wrong for both.
- Members cannot use a falling exchange rate to recover competitiveness after a shock.
- A weak member may need support from stronger ones, which creates political tension.

## The World Trade Organization

The WTO is the multilateral body in the guide. Its objectives are to lower barriers to trade, to promote fair competition and to help trade flow predictably.

Its main functions are:

- **A forum** where members negotiate trade rules.
- **Administering** the trade agreements members have signed.
- **Settling disputes** between members through a formal process.
- **Monitoring** members' trade policies.
- **Helping** developing members with technical assistance.

The guide asks for factors that affect its influence.

- **Difficulty of agreement on services and primary products.** Services barriers are rules and regulations, not simple tariffs, which makes them hard to compare and negotiate. Primary products, especially farm goods, matter for jobs and food security, so governments protect them fiercely.
- **Unequal bargaining power.** Large economies can threaten large retaliation and employ teams of trade lawyers. Small economies often cannot, so they accept deals that favour bigger members.

## Evaluating integration

Use this chain when you answer an AO3 question.

1. **Claim:** state the advantage or disadvantage.
2. **Mechanism:** explain how it works.
3. **Stakeholders:** who gains and loses (consumers, firms, workers, government, outsiders)?
4. **Weight:** how large is it, and over what time?
5. **Judgement:** which side outweighs the other, and why?

Judgement depends on the size of the members, how different their costs are, how fast labour can move, and how much they lose in sovereignty.

## Common errors

- Giving a free trade area a shared outside tariff. Each member keeps its own.
- Treating trade diversion as the same as a trade war. Diversion is a switch of supplier inside a bloc's policy.
- Calling a rise in consumer savings a net gain when tariff revenue is lost.
- Listing monetary union points without applying them to a case.
- Giving a one-sided answer to an AO3 question.
