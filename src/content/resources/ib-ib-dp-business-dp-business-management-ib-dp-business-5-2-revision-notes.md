These notes condense section 5.2, Operations methods, from Unit 5 of the IB *Business management guide*, first assessment 2024. The section is for SL and HL, and every content point is set at AO3, so you must be ready to judge which method suits a business. Read the [5.2 study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-business-dp-business-management-ib-dp-business-5-2/) for full explanations and the worked cost comparison, then try the [5.2 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-business-dp-business-management-ib-dp-business-5-2-practice/). ## The four methods in one line each

| Method | One-line recall | Typical example |
|---|---|---|
| Job production | One unique item made to a customer's specification | Architect-designed house, bespoke website |
| Batch production | A group of identical items made together, then equipment is reset | Bread varieties, school uniforms in sizes |
| Mass/flow production | Continuous output of a standardized product, each unit passing stage to stage | Soft drinks, light bulbs |
| Mass customization | Standard modules combined to give each customer a personalized version at scale | Made-to-order trainers, configured laptops |

## Volume-variety grid

- High volume, low variety: **mass/flow**.
- High volume, high variety: **mass customization**.
- Medium volume, some variety: **batch**.
- Low volume, high variety: **job**.

Moving up the volume scale lowers cost per unit because fixed costs are spread. Moving up the variety scale needs flexibility: skilled labour, adaptable machines or modular design.

## Key terms

| Term | Working definition |
|---|---|
| Changeover (set-up) time | Time when equipment is stopped and reset between batches |
| Work-in-progress | Part-finished goods waiting between stages |
| Standardization | Making every unit to the same design so parts and tasks can be repeated |
| Module | A standard component that can be combined with others in different ways |
| Made to order | Production starts only after the customer orders, so no finished stock is held |
| Capacity utilization | Output as a percentage of the most a site can produce (standard form) |
| Economies of scale | Falling cost per unit as output grows, mainly because fixed costs are spread |

## Factors in choosing a method

Use the case to test each factor.

| Factor | Points towards |
|---|---|
| High, stable demand | Mass/flow |
| Seasonal or uncertain demand | Batch |
| Unique customer requirements | Job |
| Customers want personal choice and a mass-market price | Mass customization |
| Little capital available | Job or batch |
| Highly skilled, multi-skilled workforce | Job |
| Digital ordering and flexible automation available | Mass customization |
| Craft identity or ethical concern about waste | Job, batch or made to order |

## Switching method: what goes wrong

| Move | Main risks |
|---|---|
| Job to batch | Loss of exclusivity; craft workers resist repetition |
| Batch to flow | Capital cost; inflexibility; redundancies; idle line if demand falls |
| To mass customization | Software and supply-chain complexity; longer delivery; component shortages |
| Flow to batch | Under-used equipment; changeover costs return |

Always link a switch to at least one other function: finance (funding), human resources (skills and jobs) or marketing (price, variety, delivery time).

## Services count too

| Service | Method |
|---|---|
| Wedding planner designing one event | Job |
| Coach tour with fixed departures | Batch |
| Fast-food assembly kitchen | Flow |
| Meal-kit website with chosen components | Mass customization |

## Cost crossover reminder

To compare two methods, write total cost for each as fixed costs plus variable cost per unit times output, set the two equal and solve for output. Above the crossover, the method with the lower variable cost per unit is cheaper. The guide does not require this formula; it is a tool to support an AO3 judgement.

Mini reminder with new figures: method A has fixed costs of 4,000 and variable cost of 30 a unit; method B has fixed costs of 16,000 and 18 a unit. 4,000 + 30Q = 16,000 + 18Q, so 12Q = 12,000 and Q = 1,000. Below 1,000 units A is cheaper; above it B is cheaper.

## Exam traps

- Saying mass customization is job production. It uses mass-production methods with modules.
- Recommending flow production without evidence of high, stable demand.
- Listing advantages for one method without comparing it with an alternative.
- Forgetting that a business can mix methods across product lines.
- Treating batch production as low quality. Quality depends on control, not method.
- Ending an AO3 answer without a judgement and the conditions it depends on.
- Using "cheaper" without saying "per unit" and at what output.

## Self-test

1. Define batch production.
2. State the method that suits high volume and high variety.
3. Give two reasons why a small business might use job production even if another method is cheaper per unit.
4. Explain why flow production depends on stable demand.
5. Name one problem a business faces when it moves from job to batch production.
6. Give a service example of batch production.
7. Method A: fixed costs 9,000, variable cost 25 a unit. Method B: fixed costs 21,000, variable cost 19 a unit. Find the output at which total costs are equal.
8. State one way mass customization reduces unsold stock.
9. Which business function must fund a switch to flow production?
10. Outline one ethical or sustainability point in the choice of method.
11. Why might a business keep two methods at the same time?

### Answers

1. Making a group of identical items together before the equipment is reset for the next group.
2. Mass customization.
3. Any two: the market wants unique products; demand is too small for larger methods; the owners lack capital; the workforce is skilled; craftsmanship is part of the brand.
4. The line has a high fixed cost. If demand falls, output falls, fixed cost is spread over fewer units and cost per unit rises sharply; unsold stock may build up.
5. Craft workers may be demotivated by repetitive work, or the product may lose its exclusive image.
6. A coach tour or a cinema screening at set times, where the same service is provided to a group together.
7. 9,000 + 25Q = 21,000 + 19Q, so 6Q = 12,000 and Q = 2,000 units.
8. Products are made to order, so no finished goods are made before a customer has paid for them.
9. Finance.
10. Flow production can create waste if output runs ahead of demand; made-to-order methods reduce waste. Repetitive flow work may also affect employee welfare.
11. Different product lines have different demand: a regular line suits batch or flow, special orders suit job production.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the four operations methods in IB Business Management?**
Job production, batch production, mass/flow production and mass customization, all in section 5.2 and all at AO3.

**How do you decide between batch and flow production?**
Check the size and stability of demand and the variety customers want. Flow suits high, stable demand for one standard product; batch suits several lines or demand that varies.

**What should an AO3 answer on operations methods include?**
Arguments for and against at least two methods, evidence from the case, links to other business functions and a judgement that states what it depends on.
