## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by a fixed cost. **[2]**

**2.** Penhale Pies has fixed costs of £18,000 and variable costs of £2.20 per pie. It makes and sells 8,000 pies at £5.00 each. Calculate its total cost, average cost and profit. **[4]**

**3.** Distinguish between a direct cost and an overhead. **[3]**

**4.** Which **one** of the following is a profit centre?

A: A payroll department.

B: A shop in a retail chain, with its own sales and costs recorded.

C: A security team.

D: A cleaning contract. **[1]**

**5.** Explain **one** disadvantage of using cost centres. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Ferris Cabinets makes two products. Overheads of £27,000 are absorbed on the basis of machine hours.

| | Product X | Product Y |
|---|---|---|
| Output | 500 | 1,000 |
| Price | £60 | £45 |
| Variable cost per unit | £30 | £29 |
| Machine hours per unit | 3 | 3 |

**(a)** Calculate the absorption rate per machine hour. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the full cost per unit and profit or loss per unit of each product. **[4]**

**(c)** Calculate the total profit of the business. **[2]**

**(d)** The owner proposes stopping Product Y because it makes a loss. Calculate the contribution per unit of each product, and the profit the business would make if Product Y were stopped (assuming overheads stay at £27,000). **[4]**

**(e)** Evaluate whether Ferris Cabinets should stop making Product Y. **[6]**

**7.** Penhale Pies' variable costs rise by 10%. Calculate the new profit if output and price stay the same. **[3]**

**8.** Explain **two** reasons why a business with several products might separate direct costs from overheads. **[4]**

**9.** Evaluate the usefulness of profit centres to a hotel group and its stakeholders. **[8]**

**10.** A manufacturer has made a profit of £250,000, up from £180,000 last year. Revenue is £2,500,000, up from £1,600,000. Evaluate the profit level of the manufacturer. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** A fixed cost does not change as output changes in the short run [1], for example rent or managers' salaries, which must be paid whether the business makes 100 or 10,000 units [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Total variable cost = 8,000 × £2.20 = £17,600; total cost = £17,600 + £18,000 = **£35,600** [1] [1]. Average cost = £35,600 ÷ 8,000 = **£4.45** [1]. Profit = (8,000 × £5) − £35,600 = £40,000 − £35,600 = **£4,400** [1]. **[4]**

**3.** A direct cost can be traced to a particular product, for example the materials used in it [1]. An overhead is a general cost of running the whole business, such as administration or head office rent [1]. An overhead cannot be traced to one product and has to be shared or absorbed [1]. **[3]**

**4.** **B** [1]. **[1]**

**5.** Managers of a cost centre may cut spending to keep within budget [1], which can harm quality of service or other departments that depend on them [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** Total hours = (500 × 3) + (1,000 × 3) = 4,500 [1]. Rate = £27,000 ÷ 4,500 = **£6 per hour** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Overhead per unit = 3 × £6 = £18 [1]. Product X: full cost = £30 + £18 = **£48**; profit = £60 − £48 = **£12** [1] [1]. Product Y: full cost = £29 + £18 = **£47**; profit = £45 − £47 = **−£2**, a loss of £2 per unit [1]. **[4]**

**(c)** Profit = (500 × £12) + (1,000 × −£2) [1] = £6,000 − £2,000 = **£4,000** [1]. **[2]**

**(d)** Contribution: X = £60 − £30 = **£30**; Y = £45 − £29 = **£16** [1] [1]. If Y stopped, contribution from X = 500 × £30 = £15,000 [1]. Less overheads of £27,000 = **−£12,000**, a loss [1]. **[4]**

**(e)** Against stopping: Product Y earns a contribution of £16,000, which helps to cover overheads [1]; if overheads stay at £27,000, stopping Y turns a £4,000 profit into a £12,000 loss [1]. The "loss" arises from allocating overheads by machine hours [1].
For stopping: if Y's machine hours could be used to make a more profitable product [1]; or if the overheads could be cut with Y, for example by closing a work area [1].
Judgement: Ferris should not stop Product Y unless overheads can be cut or the capacity used to earn more than £16 per unit [1]. **[6]**

**7.** Variable cost per pie = £2.20 × 1.10 = £2.42 [1]. Extra cost = 8,000 × £0.22 = £1,760 [1]. New profit = £4,400 − £1,760 = **£2,640** [1]. (Check: 8,000 × £2.42 = £19,360; £40,000 − £18,000 − £19,360 = £2,640.) **[3]**

**8.** Pricing: the business can make sure the price of each product covers its direct costs and a fair share of overheads [1] [1]. Control: managers can control direct costs per unit and overheads as a total budget, so they know where to act when costs rise [1] [1]. (Also accept decisions about dropping products.) **[4]**

**9.** For: each hotel has its own revenue and costs, so managers are accountable for profit and can be rewarded for results [1]; the group can compare hotels and decide where to invest or close [1]; managers have an incentive to find ways to raise revenue and cut waste [1].
Stakeholders: owners get a clear picture of where profit comes from [1]; managers may be motivated by bonuses but may feel unfairly judged on shared head office costs [1]; staff in a loss-making centre may fear closure [1].
Against: managers may focus on their own hotel's profit, for example by not sharing customers with other hotels [1].
Judgement: profit centres are useful if shared costs are allocated fairly and the targets include customer service as well as profit [1]. **[8]**

**10.** Size: profit rose by £70,000, or 38.9% (70,000 ÷ 180,000) [1]. Margin: this year 250,000 ÷ 2,500,000 = 10%; last year 180,000 ÷ 1,600,000 = 11.25% [1] [1]. So the margin fell, even though profit rose [1], which suggests costs grew faster than revenue [1].
Other factors: whether the rise came from one large order, price rises or extra sales [1]; comparison with competitors and targets [1].
Judgement: the higher profit is positive, but the falling margin suggests that growth was bought at the cost of efficiency, so the profit level is satisfactory rather than strong [1]. **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using fixed cost instead of total cost to find average cost.
- Forgetting that overheads remain if a product is stopped.
- Calculating a margin without dividing by revenue.
- Mixing up price and total revenue.
- Giving a calculation but no interpretation.
