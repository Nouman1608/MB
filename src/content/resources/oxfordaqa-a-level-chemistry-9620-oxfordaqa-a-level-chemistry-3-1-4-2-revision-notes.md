These notes condense **3.1.4.2 Calorimetry** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3, including **required practical 2: measure an enthalpy change**. It is International AS content, examined in Unit 1; Unit 5 can test the practical. For worked examples, read the [calorimetry study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-4-2/); for exam-style questions, use the [calorimetry practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-4-2-practice/).

## Core ideas

- **q = mcΔT** gives the heat transferred to or from the water or solution.
- m is the mass of the substance whose **temperature changes**.
- c is given in questions; you do not need to recall it.
- Divide q (in kJ) by the moles of the limiting reactant to get a molar enthalpy change.
- Sign: temperature **rises** → ΔH **negative**; temperature **falls** → ΔH **positive**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| q | Heat energy transferred, in J |
| Specific heat capacity, c | Energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 g of a substance by 1 K (J g⁻¹ K⁻¹) |
| ΔT | Temperature change, in K (same number as the change in °C) |
| Calorimeter | Apparatus in which the heat from a reaction is transferred to a known mass of water or solution |
| Limiting reactant | The reactant not in excess; it fixes the amount that reacts |
| Extrapolation | Extending the cooling line back to the time of mixing to estimate the maximum temperature with no heat loss |

## Five-step method

1. q = m × c × ΔT (J).
2. ÷ 1000 → kJ.
3. n of the limiting reactant (mass ÷ Mr, or concentration × volume in dm³).
4. q ÷ n → kJ mol⁻¹.
5. Add the sign.

## Units check

| Quantity | Unit to use |
|---|---|
| m | g (1.00 cm³ of dilute solution = 1.00 g) |
| c | J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ |
| ΔT | K |
| q | J, then kJ |
| ΔH | kJ mol⁻¹ |

## Required practical 2 summary

- Polystyrene cup with lid, in a beaker; thermometer to 0.1 °C or a probe.
- Take temperatures every 30 s for a few minutes **before** mixing.
- Add the second reagent (solid in excess) at a recorded time; stir.
- Keep recording well after the maximum.
- Plot temperature against time; extrapolate the cooling line back to the mixing time; ΔT = extrapolated value − starting value.

## Combustion experiments

- Spirit burner under a copper can of water; weigh the burner before and after.
- Values are less exothermic than data-book values because of: heat loss to the air and the can, incomplete combustion, fuel evaporating from the wick, and non-standard conditions.
- Improvements: draught shield, lid on the can, flame close to the can, short burning time with frequent stirring.

## Quick reminders (illustrative numbers)

- 50.0 cm³ solution warms by 6.0 K: q = 50.0 × 4.18 × 6.0 = 1254 J = 1.25 kJ.
- 1.25 kJ from 0.0250 mol of limiting reactant: ΔH = −1.25 ÷ 0.0250 = −50.2 kJ mol⁻¹ (using the unrounded 1.254 kJ).
- A temperature fall of the same size gives +50.2 kJ mol⁻¹.

## Exam traps

- m taken as the mass of the solid or fuel.
- Joules not converted to kilojoules.
- No sign, or the wrong sign.
- Moles of the reactant in excess used instead of the limiting one.
- Highest recorded temperature used when an extrapolated value is asked for.
- "Heat loss" given as an error without saying it makes the measured ΔT too small.
- When two solutions are mixed, the mass of only one solution used; use the **total** volume.

## Self-test

1. Write the equation for heat transferred in calorimetry, with units for each quantity.
2. What mass is m in a combustion experiment with a spirit burner?
3. Why is the temperature change in K the same as in °C?
4. A reaction in solution makes the temperature fall. What is the sign of ΔH?
5. Why is a polystyrene cup used rather than a glass beaker?
6. Why are readings taken before the reagents are mixed?
7. Why is the cooling line extrapolated back to the time of mixing?
8. Calculate q when 100 g of water warms by 12.5 K (c = 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹).
9. 0.0200 mol of a fuel gives q = 15.4 kJ. Calculate ΔcH.
10. Give two reasons why a spirit-burner value of ΔcH is less exothermic than the data-book value.
11. 40.0 cm³ of acid is mixed with 40.0 cm³ of alkali. What mass do you use in q = mcΔT?
12. Calculate the mass of water heated by 15.0 K when q = 9.41 kJ.

### Answers

1. q = mcΔT: q in J, m in g, c in J g⁻¹ K⁻¹, ΔT in K.
2. The mass of the water being heated.
3. One kelvin is the same size as one degree Celsius; only the zero points differ.
4. Positive (endothermic).
5. Polystyrene is a good insulator and has a low heat capacity, so less heat is lost or absorbed.
6. To find a reliable starting temperature for the extrapolation.
7. To estimate the maximum temperature that would have been reached with no heat loss to the surroundings.
8. q = 100 × 4.18 × 12.5 = **5225 J** (5.23 kJ).
9. ΔcH = −15.4 ÷ 0.0200 = **−770 kJ mol⁻¹**.
10. Any two from: heat loss to surroundings or the can; incomplete combustion; evaporation of fuel; non-standard conditions.
11. 80.0 g (total volume 80.0 cm³, density 1.00 g cm⁻³).
12. m = q ÷ (cΔT) = 9410 ÷ (4.18 × 15.0) = **150 g**.
