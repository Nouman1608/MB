This guide teaches **section 13.1 New and emerging technologies** of Topic 13 in the Cambridge International AS & A Level Information Technology 9626 syllabus for 2025, 2026 and 2027. It is A Level content, examined in Paper 3 Advanced Theory. The [new and emerging technologies revision notes](/resources/a-level-cambridge-ict-new-and-emerging-technologies-revision-notes/) summarise the topic; this page explains each technology and its impact in more depth. Use it with the [practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-ict-9626-new-and-emerging-technologies-practice/).

## What section 13.1 covers

| Syllabus strand | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| Types | Describe fourteen technologies (listed below) |
| Impact | Discuss effects on individuals, organisations, medicine and healthcare, scientific research and the environment |
| Evaluation | Give advantages and disadvantages of each technology |

A technology is **emerging** when it is new or developing and its full effect is not yet known. Because the field changes, learn the **principle** of each technology and a typical use, rather than the names of products.

## The fourteen technologies

**Artificial intelligence (AI).** Computer systems that perform tasks that normally need human intelligence, such as recognising speech and images, making decisions and learning from data (machine learning). *Uses:* virtual assistants, medical image analysis, fraud detection, recommendations. *For:* works at speed on huge data and does not tire. *Against:* decisions may be biased by poor training data and are hard to explain; jobs may be replaced; accountability is unclear when it errs.

**Augmented reality (AR).** Digital information is overlaid on a live view of the real world through a phone, tablet or glasses. *Uses:* navigation arrows on a street view, trying furniture in a room, repair instructions over a machine. *For:* adds helpful information without leaving the real setting. *Against:* needs sensors, cameras and good lighting; can distract users in unsafe places; raises privacy concerns about cameras.

**Virtual reality (VR).** The user wears a headset and sees a fully computer-generated 3D world that responds to head and hand movement. *Uses:* training pilots and surgeons, games, virtual tours. *For:* safe practice of dangerous tasks; lower training cost. *Against:* costly equipment; motion sickness; isolation from the real world.

**Robotics.** Machines that sense, decide and act, often to perform tasks automatically. *Uses:* factory production, surgery, bomb disposal, warehouse picking, exploring dangerous places. *For:* accuracy, strength and constant work. *Against:* high cost; job losses; limited flexibility.

**Computer-assisted translation (CAT).** Software that translates text or speech between languages, often using a stored memory of previous translations. *Uses:* translating websites, documents and live conversations. *For:* fast and cheap; helps communication across languages. *Against:* idioms and context can be wrongly translated, so human checking is still needed.

**Holographic imaging.** Creating three-dimensional images using light, which can be seen from different angles without special glasses. *Uses:* medical scans viewed in 3D, security features on cards, displays. *For:* a realistic view of depth. *Against:* costly equipment; the technology is still developing.

**Holographic and 4th generation optical data storage.** Data is stored throughout the volume of a crystal or disc using light, instead of only on the surface. *For:* very large capacity and fast access of many bits at once; long life. *Against:* expensive drives and media; not widely available.

**3D printing.** Building a solid object layer by layer from a digital design, using plastic, metal, resin or other materials. *Uses:* prototypes, custom prosthetics, spare parts, models. *For:* low cost for one-off items; complex shapes; little waste. *Against:* slow for large quantities; limited materials and strength; copyright and safety concerns, such as printing weapons.

**Vision enhancement.** Technology that improves sight, including night vision, thermal imaging and electronic implants or glasses that help people with poor sight. *For:* helps people see better or see in the dark. *Against:* cost; ethical questions about enhancing normal ability; possible surgery.

**Wearable computing.** Computing devices worn on the body, such as smart watches and fitness bands. *Uses:* tracking heart rate, steps and sleep; notifications; payments. *For:* convenient; supports health monitoring. *Against:* battery life; privacy of personal data; inaccurate readings.

**Blockchain technology.** A shared ledger copied across many computers, in which linked blocks of records are very hard to alter (see section 12.1). *Uses:* cryptocurrencies, tracking goods in supply chains, recording ownership. *For:* secure, transparent records without a central authority. *Against:* slow and uses much power in some systems; mistakes cannot easily be corrected.

**Internet of Things (IoT).** Everyday objects with sensors and network connections that collect and exchange data. *Uses:* smart homes, smart meters, connected cars, farm monitoring. *For:* automation and detailed data. *Against:* weak security gives attackers entry points; privacy concerns; large amounts of data.

**Molecular data storage.** Data stored in individual molecules, such as synthetic DNA. *For:* extremely dense storage that could last for a very long time. *Against:* slow and costly to write and read; still experimental.

**Autonomous transport systems.** Vehicles, trains, drones and ships that navigate and operate with little or no human input, using sensors and AI. *For:* may reduce accidents caused by human error; more efficient routes; helps people who cannot drive. *Against:* sensors can fail in poor weather; legal responsibility is unclear after an accident; hacking risk; job losses for drivers.

## Impact of new and emerging technologies

### On individuals

Benefits include convenience, health tracking, new ways to learn, shop and play, and support for people with disabilities (vision enhancement, voice control). Concerns include loss of privacy through sensors and cameras, dependence on devices, job loss or the need for retraining, cost, and unequal access, which widens the digital divide.

### On organisations

Benefits include lower costs through automation, faster decisions from AI, new products, and better supply chain tracking. Concerns include high set-up costs, the need to retrain staff, cybersecurity risks, and responsibility for decisions made by AI.

### On medicine and healthcare

The syllabus names these: patient care and monitoring; development of prosthetics and medical products; tissue engineering; artificial blood vessels; the design of medical tools and equipment.

- **Patient care and monitoring:** wearable devices and IoT sensors send heart rate or glucose readings to staff who respond to changes. AI can help interpret scans.
- **Prosthetics:** 3D printing can produce a limb to fit one person, quickly and at lower cost; robotics can control a limb through nerve or muscle signals.
- **Tissue engineering:** 3D printing with living cells builds tissue for research and, in future, for repair. Artificial blood vessels may also be produced this way.
- **Medical tools and equipment:** 3D printing makes custom instruments and surgical guides, and robotics assists with precise operations. VR and AR help train surgeons.

Concerns: cost, safety testing, privacy of health data, and fairness of access.

### On scientific research

AI finds patterns in huge datasets. Simulations in VR allow experiments that would be too dangerous or costly. Robotics explores deep seas or other planets. New storage keeps the data. A concern is accuracy: results from AI must be checked.

### On the environment

The syllabus names e-waste, recycling, power consumption, manufacturing processes and energy from wireless signals.

- **E-waste:** short device lifetimes and many connected objects create large amounts of electronic waste with toxic materials.
- **Recycling:** 3D printing can use recycled plastic, and devices can be designed for recovery of metals.
- **Power consumption:** data centres for AI, blockchain mining and always-on IoT devices use much electricity. Efficient design can reduce it.
- **Manufacturing processes:** 3D printing adds material only where needed, so less is wasted than when cutting material away. Robots can improve precision and reduce errors and waste.
- **Energy from wireless signals:** small devices may harvest power from radio waves, reducing batteries and waste.

## Worked examples

**Worked example 1: a 3D printed part.** A part is 60 mm tall and is printed in layers 0.2 mm thick. It uses 120 g of plastic costing £25 per kilogram, and each layer takes 40 seconds.

- Layers = 60 ÷ 0.2 = **300**.
- Time = 300 × 40 = 12,000 seconds = 12,000 ÷ 3,600 = **3.3 hours**.
- Material cost = 0.12 kg × £25 = **£3.00**.

A single custom part for £3 of material is cheap for one item, but if 1,000 were needed, printing one at a time would take 3,333 hours, so a mould would be a faster choice.

**Worked example 2: IoT data volume.** A farm has 2,000 soil sensors. Each sends a 100-byte reading every 10 seconds.

- Readings per sensor per day = 86,400 ÷ 10 = 8,640.
- Bytes per sensor per day = 8,640 × 100 = 864,000.
- For all sensors = 864,000 × 2,000 = 1,728,000,000 bytes, about **1.7 GB per day**, or 51.8 GB in 30 days.

The farm needs enough storage and bandwidth, and must protect the data and the sensors from attack.

## Common mistakes

- Defining a technology without a use: "VR is virtual" tells the examiner nothing.
- Confusing augmented reality (digital items added to the real view) with virtual reality (a completely artificial world).
- Mixing up AI with robotics. A robot may use AI, but AI can run without a robot.
- Giving environmental points that are only positive or only negative.
- Writing "it will take jobs" for every technology, without naming which jobs and why.
- Treating wearable computing and IoT as identical. Wearables are worn by a person.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a technology: what it does, how it works at a basic level and one use.
- **Impact answers**: name the group (individual, organisation, medicine, research, environment), the effect and the reason.
- **Evaluate** or **assess**: weigh benefits against costs and risks, then give a judgement for the scenario.
- Use the named medicine and environment examples (prosthetics, tissue engineering, e-waste, power use) to give specific marks.
