Condensed notes for **7.1 Organisational structure** (Cambridge International AS & A Level Business 9609, 2026–2028; A Level content). For a worked organisation chart, a delayering calculation and a model answer, see the [Organisational structure study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-organisational-structure-cambridge-alevel-business/); for exam-style questions, see the [Organisational structure practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-organisational-structure-cambridge-alevel-business-practice/).

## What 7.1 asks you to handle

7.1.1 structure and objectives · 7.1.2 types of structure and formal features · 7.1.3 delegation and accountability · 7.1.4 control, authority and trust · 7.1.5 centralisation and decentralisation · 7.1.6 line and staff.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Organisational structure | How roles, reporting lines and decision-making are arranged |
| Level of hierarchy | A layer of management or staff with similar authority |
| Chain of command | Route by which instructions pass down and reports pass up |
| Span of control | Number of subordinates reporting directly to one manager |
| Responsibility | Duty to carry out a task |
| Authority | Power to make decisions and give instructions |
| Delegation | Passing authority for a task to a subordinate |
| Accountability | Having to answer for the outcome |
| Centralised | Key decisions taken at the top |
| Decentralised | Decisions taken lower down or locally |
| Delayering | Removing one or more levels of hierarchy |
| Line function | Directly involved in producing or selling |
| Staff function | Supports and advises line functions |
| Matrix structure | Staff report to a functional manager and a project or product manager |

## Attributes of a good structure (7.1.1)

Flexibility · meets the needs of the business · allows growth and development · encourages intrapreneurship.

## Structure follows objectives

- Cut costs → fewer layers, wider spans, centralised buying.
- Innovate → matrix teams, decentralised decisions.
- Expand abroad → divisions by geographical area.
- Consistent brand → centralised standards.
- Fast customer response → short chains, local authority.

## Types of structure

| Type | For | Against |
|---|---|---|
| Functional | Specialist expertise; no duplication | Departments pursue own aims; slow cross-department decisions |
| Tall (narrow spans) | Close supervision; promotion steps | Slow communication; costly; distant from customers |
| Flat (wide spans) | Fast communication; lower cost; more delegation | Overloaded managers; weaker supervision; fewer promotions |
| Matrix | Shares skills; flexible; innovative | Two bosses; conflicting instructions |

**Grouping**: function (one main range), product (distinct ranges), geographical area (regional differences). Often combined.

## Span and levels

For a given workforce, **wider spans → fewer levels**. Spans are often wider lower down where work is routine.

## Delayering: evaluate

- Net saving = salaries removed − extra pay for redistributed duties.
- For: lower cost, faster decisions, more empowerment.
- Against: overload, fewer promotion steps, weaker supervision, lower morale among remaining staff.

## Delegation

- Manager stays **accountable**; subordinate becomes responsible for the task.
- Conditions: authority matches responsibility; clear objectives; skills or training; proportionate monitoring.
- Impact: frees managers, develops and motivates staff, speeds decisions; risks errors, inconsistency, training costs.
- Accountability processes: targets, reports, appraisal, audits, review meetings.

## Control against trust

- More control → less trust, demotivation; less control → risk of errors.
- More trust when: experienced staff, low-risk decisions, good reporting.
- More control when: new staff, high-risk decisions, legal requirements.

## Centralisation and decentralisation

- Decide **which decisions** go where.
- Centralise: bulk buying, legal compliance, major investment, brand standards.
- Decentralise: rotas, local promotions, customer service decisions.
- Technology (real-time data) makes decentralised units easier to monitor.

## Line and staff

- Line: production, sales; direct authority.
- Staff: HR, finance, IT, legal; advise and support.
- Conflict: rules against freedom to hit targets; resentment of advice; competition for budgets.
- Reduce: involve line managers in procedures, agree service standards, clarify final authority.

## Exam traps

- You can delegate authority, **not** accountability.
- Wide spans are not always better; they depend on staff skills and task complexity.
- Include the extra costs when calculating delayering savings.
- Responsibility without authority leads to frustration and missed targets.
- Centralisation is rarely all-or-nothing.
- Use the case's objectives to judge whether a structure fits.

## Self-test

1. Define span of control.
2. What is the relationship between span of control and levels of hierarchy?
3. Give one advantage and one disadvantage of a matrix structure.
4. Why might a firm organise by geographical area?
5. A firm removes 8 managers paid $50 000 each and gives 40 staff an extra $2 500 each. Calculate the net saving.
6. Can a manager delegate accountability? Explain.
7. Give one condition for successful delegation.
8. Give one decision a retail chain might centralise and one it might decentralise.
9. Distinguish a line function from a staff function, with an example of each.
10. Give one reason for conflict between line and staff.
11. Name two accountability processes.
12. Which attribute of a structure helps staff develop new ideas within the business?

**Answers**

1. The number of subordinates who report directly to one manager.
2. For a given workforce, wider spans mean fewer levels; narrower spans mean more levels.
3. Advantage: shares specialist skills across projects. Disadvantage: two managers can give conflicting instructions.
4. To respond to regional differences in language, laws or customer tastes.
5. 8 × 50 000 = $400 000 saved; 40 × 2 500 = $100 000 extra; net saving $300 000.
6. No; the manager can pass authority for the task, but remains answerable for the result.
7. For example, giving authority that matches the responsibility, or ensuring the subordinate has the skills.
8. Centralise: buying from suppliers. Decentralise: staff rotas or local promotions.
9. Line functions produce or sell, such as production; staff functions support and advise, such as HR.
10. For example, staff specialists set rules that line managers see as slowing them down.
11. Any two: targets, reports, appraisal, audits, review meetings.
12. Encouraging intrapreneurship.
