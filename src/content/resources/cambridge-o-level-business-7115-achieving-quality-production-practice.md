## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define quality assurance. **[2]**

**2.** Identify **two** ways a hotel could check the quality of its service. **[2]**

**3.** Explain **one** reason why quality is important for a bus company. **[2]**

**4.** State **two** differences between quality control and quality assurance. **[2]**

**5.** A drinks factory fills 8,000 bottles a day. 2.5% are faulty and must be scrapped. Each bottle costs $0.60 to produce. Calculate the number of faulty bottles and the daily cost of the scrapped bottles. **[3]**

**6.** Explain **one** weakness of quality control. **[2]**

### Section B: longer questions

**7.** Westgate Electronics makes 4,000 kettles a month. Inspectors check a sample of finished kettles at the end of the production line. Customer returns of faulty kettles have risen for three months in a row.

**(a)** Explain **two** problems for Westgate of relying on inspection at the end of production.

**(b)** Explain **two** ways Westgate could introduce quality assurance. **[8]**

**8.** Ashby Cases makes 10,000 phone cases a month. Each case costs $2 to make and sells for $6. Currently 4% of cases are faulty: half are found and scrapped in the factory and half are sold and later refunded at the full selling price. A quality assurance programme would cost $500 a month and reduce the fault rate to 1.5%, with the same split.

**(a)** Calculate the current monthly cost of scrapped cases and refunds.

**(b)** Calculate the monthly cost of scrapped cases and refunds after the programme, and the net monthly saving. **[6]**

**9.** Crestwood Bakery produces 40,000 loaves a day for supermarkets. Consider whether it should rely mainly on quality control or on quality assurance. Justify your answer. **[6]**

**10.** A dental clinic wants to improve the quality of its service.

**(a)** Explain what quality means for the clinic's patients.

**(b)** Explain **two** ways the clinic could achieve quality. **[6]**

**11.** Explain **two** reasons why quality is important even for a business that sells low-priced products. **[4]**

**12.** **(a)** Explain how agreeing standards with suppliers helps quality assurance.

**(b)** Explain how training employees helps quality assurance. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Making sure that quality standards are set and met at every stage of production [1], so that faults are prevented rather than found at the end [1].

**2.** Any two [1] [1]: a supervisor inspects rooms after cleaning; guest feedback forms or online reviews; checking how long guests wait at check-in; reviewing complaints; staff checking room-service orders before delivery.

**3.** One reason identified [1] and explained [1]: passengers who find buses late, dirty or unsafe will switch to other transport, reducing revenue; or a safety failure could lead to accidents, legal action and a damaged reputation.

**4.** Any two [1] [1]: quality control finds faults, quality assurance prevents them; control is done mainly by inspectors, assurance involves all employees; control checks at stages or at the end, assurance applies throughout the process.

**5.** Faulty bottles = 8,000 × 2.5% = **200** [1]. Cost = 200 × 0.60 [1] = **$120** a day [1].

**6.** One weakness identified [1] and explained [1]: the fault has already been made, so materials and labour are wasted; or checking only a sample means some faulty items still reach customers; or employing inspectors adds to costs.

**7.** **(a)** Two problems, each identified [1] and explained [1]: faults are found only after the kettle is finished, so the materials and labour used are wasted; a sample cannot check every kettle, so faulty ones still reach customers, as the rising returns show; production workers may feel quality is the inspectors' job and take less care; the cause of faults is not found, so the same faults keep happening.

**(b)** Two ways, each identified [1] and explained [1]: train workers to check their own work at each stage, so faults are corrected immediately; agree standards with component suppliers so faulty parts do not enter production; service machines on a schedule so they stay accurate; record the causes of returned kettles and change the process to remove them.

**8.** **(a)** Faulty cases = 10,000 × 4% = 400; 200 scrapped and 200 refunded [1]. Scrapped: 200 × $2 = $400; refunds: 200 × $6 = $1,200 [1]. Total = **$1,600** a month [1].

**(b)** Faulty cases = 10,000 × 1.5% = 150; 75 scrapped and 75 refunded. Cost = (75 × 2) + (75 × 6) = 150 + 450 = **$600** [1] [1]. Net saving = 1,600 − 600 − 500 = **$500** a month [1].

**9.** Quality control: inspectors could check samples of loaves before dispatch, stopping some faulty loaves reaching supermarkets [1], but at 40,000 loaves a day only a small proportion can be checked [1]. Quality assurance: standards for flour, mixing times and oven temperatures at every stage would prevent faults across the whole output [1], reducing waste and protecting its contracts with supermarkets, which may stop buying from an unreliable supplier [1]. However, assurance needs training and new systems, which cost money and take time [1]. Judgement: rely mainly on quality assurance, because the volume is too large to inspect fully and losing a supermarket contract would be very costly, while keeping some final sample checks [1].

**10.** **(a)** Patients receive the correct treatment safely and hygienically [1], with short waiting times and clear explanations, every time they visit [1].

**(b)** Two ways, each identified [1] and explained [1]: set clear standards for hygiene and appointment times and train all staff to follow them; check equipment and sterilisation regularly; ask patients for feedback after visits and act on complaints; a senior dentist reviews treatment records to check standards.

**11.** Two reasons, each identified [1] and explained [1]: customers still expect a cheap product to work and to last for a reasonable time, so poor quality leads to returns and lost sales; faulty products must be refunded or replaced, and with low prices the profit on each item is small, so faults quickly wipe out profit; bad reviews spread quickly and damage the brand; unsafe products can lead to legal action whatever their price.

**12.** **(a)** Materials and components arrive already meeting the required standard [1], so fewer faults enter production and less checking of deliveries is needed [1].

**(b)** Trained employees know the standard expected at their stage and can check their own work [1], so faults are spotted and corrected immediately instead of being found at the end [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing quality assurance as "more inspection".
- Forgetting that refunds lose the selling price, not the production cost, in question 8.
- Leaving out the $500 programme cost when calculating the net saving.
- Recommending one approach in question 9 without using the scale of production.
- Defining quality as "expensive" or "luxury" in question 10 or 11.
