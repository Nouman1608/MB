Section A of Paper 1 is the Shakespeare question. You study one play from the specification's list of
six, and you study the whole text. In the exam you answer one question on your play: it prints an
extract, asks you to write in detail about that extract, and then asks you to write about the play as
a whole. This guide goes beyond the [Paper 1 overview](/resources/aqa-gcse-english-literature-paper-1/)
to look closely at the methods a playwright uses, how to read Shakespeare's verse and prose, and how
to build a single argument that runs from the extract out across the play.

## What the question rewards

The question is worth 30 marks, plus 4 further marks for AO4: vocabulary, sentence structures,
spelling and punctuation. AQA's published mark schemes split the 30 as AO1 12, AO2 12 and AO3 6, but
the examiner marks the answer as a whole against level descriptors rather than ticking off each
objective separately.

- **AO1** – an informed personal response with a critical style, supported by apt references,
  including quotation. The response must answer *this* question, not a remembered essay.
- **AO2** – analysis of how Shakespeare uses language, form and structure to create meanings and
  effects, with subject terminology where it helps.
- **AO3** – understanding of how the play relates to the context in which it was written.
- **AO4** – accurate, varied and clear expression.

The question usually begins "Starting with this extract, explore how Shakespeare presents..." and
gives two bullet points: one about the extract, one about the play as a whole. "Explore" invites you
to weigh more than one reading; "presents" tells you to focus on the dramatist's choices, not on the
character as if they were a real person.

## Reading a play as drama

Shakespeare wrote for performance. A script is a set of instructions for actors and an audience
experience, so the strongest answers keep asking what an audience sees and hears.

### Dramatic methods worth knowing

| Method | What to say about it |
|---|---|
| Soliloquy | A character alone on stage speaks thoughts aloud. The audience hears private motives no other character knows, which can create sympathy or unease. |
| Aside | A brief remark to the audience or another character that others on stage do not hear. It builds a secret alliance with the audience. |
| Dramatic irony | The audience knows something a character does not, so ordinary lines carry a second, darker or comic meaning. |
| Entrances and exits | Who arrives or leaves at a key moment, and who is left alone, can shift power within a scene. |
| Embedded stage directions | Shakespeare's texts contain few stage directions; actions are often implied in the dialogue ("give me your hand"), so the words tell actors what to do. |
| Juxtaposition of scenes | A violent scene followed by a comic or domestic one sharpens both through contrast. |
| Shared lines | When one character finishes another's line of verse, it can suggest closeness, urgency or one character overpowering another. |

### Verse, prose and rhyme

Shakespeare moves between three modes, and the movement itself is meaningful.

- **Blank verse** – unrhymed lines usually of ten syllables in a da-DUM pattern (iambic
  pentameter). It is often used by high-status characters or for serious, controlled speech.
- **Prose** – ordinary sentences without metre. It is often used by lower-status characters, for
  comedy, for letters, or for a character whose mind is breaking down.
- **Rhyming couplets** – two rhymed lines, often closing a scene or speech. They can sound decisive,
  proverbial or falsely neat.

These are tendencies, not rules. The interesting moments are the exceptions: a noble character
slipping into prose, a servant speaking in verse, a regular rhythm breaking with an extra syllable or a
mid-line pause (caesura). When the metre disturbs, ask what it shows about the speaker's state of mind.

### Structure across the play

Think about where your extract sits. Is it near the opening, where situations and relationships are
established? Is it a turning point, where a decision changes everything? Is it close to the ending,
where earlier ideas are resolved, punished or left uneasy? Plays often echo themselves: an image,
phrase or action at the start returns later with its meaning changed. Tracking those echoes is one of
the clearest ways to write about structure.

## Context that earns credit

AO3 is about the relationship between the play and the context in which it was written. Useful
contexts include the theatre itself (the audience, the absence of women on the public stage in
Shakespeare's lifetime, the bare stage that made language do the work of scenery), beliefs about
hierarchy, gender, religion and authority, and the expectations of the genre (tragedy, comedy or
romance). Use only what is relevant to the question and to your play, and link it to a specific
moment. A paragraph of history with no link to the text earns little.

A strong move is to show how a modern audience and an original audience might respond differently,
as long as you anchor both in what the text does.

## Worked example

The passage below is **original**, written in the style of a Shakespeare soliloquy to model the
method. It is not from a set play. A nobleman, Aldric, has just been offered his brother's lands if he
betrays him.

> *Original passage:*
> What is a brother but a name we wear,
> A borrowed coat that warms us while it fits?
> And yet this coat was cut from my own cloth.
> The night is kind; it does not ask my name.
> Go, conscience, sleep. I'll wake thee when I'm crowned.

**Annotated model paragraph:**

> Aldric's soliloquy presents ambition as a force that persuades itself. His opening rhetorical
> question reduces "brother" to a "name we wear", a clothing metaphor that makes family loyalty sound
> temporary and removable **[AO2: language]**. Yet the third line turns on "And yet", and the image
> rebounds on him: the coat was "cut from my own cloth", so the metaphor he chose to dismiss loyalty
> ends up proving it **[AO2: structure within the speech]**. Because this is a soliloquy, the audience
> alone hears the argument collapse, which makes us complicit in his choice and builds dramatic irony
> for every later scene in which he greets his brother warmly **[AO2: form; AO1: whole-play link]**.
> His final command, "Go, conscience, sleep", treats conscience as a servant he can dismiss, and an
> audience who believed betrayal of kin to be a sin against natural order might hear this as the
> moment he places himself outside that order **[AO3, tied to the line]**.

Notice that every sentence makes a point about meaning, the method is named only when it helps, the
context is attached to a specific phrase, and there is already a signal of where the argument goes
next in the play.

## Building the extract-to-whole-play argument

The extract is a starting point, not the whole answer. A reliable plan:

1. **Read the question and the extract twice.** Underline the focus word (for example, "loyalty" or
   "fear") and mark four or five details in the extract that relate to it.
2. **Write a one-sentence argument** that answers the question across the play: "Shakespeare presents
   loyalty as something characters claim most loudly when they are about to break it."
3. **Analyse the extract first**, in two or three paragraphs, choosing details that support your
   argument.
4. **Move outward** to two or three other moments, ideally one earlier and one later, using memorised
   quotations or precise references to events.
5. **Conclude** with a judgement about what Shakespeare is suggesting by the end of the play.

Some candidates prefer to weave the extract and the wider play together in every paragraph. Either
approach can reach the top levels as long as both bullet points are covered and the argument is
consistent.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating characters as real people.** Write "Shakespeare presents Aldric as..." rather than
  "Aldric is...". The marks are for analysing choices.
- **Spending the whole answer on the extract**, or ignoring it after one paragraph.
- **Feature-spotting**: "There is iambic pentameter here." Say what the rhythm, or the break in it,
  does.
- **Plot summary of the wider play** instead of analysis of selected moments.
- **Bolt-on context**, especially generic history unrelated to the question.
- **Misquoting** under pressure. A precise reference to an event is better than an invented
  quotation.
- **Neglecting AO4.** Four marks reward clear, accurate writing; spell the characters' names and
  Shakespeare's name correctly.

## Exam technique

- Allow time for the question roughly in proportion to its marks: Section A carries 34 of the
  paper's 64 marks, so it deserves a little over half of the 1 hour 45 minutes.
- Spend about five minutes planning; a clear line of argument raises AO1.
- Quote briefly. Single words and short phrases are easier to analyse and remember.
- Use tentative language for interpretation ("perhaps", "may suggest") and confident language for
  your overall argument.
- Refer to the audience at least once in a way that shows you are thinking about performance.
- Finish with a sentence that answers the question directly.

## Revising your play

Because the exam is closed book, build revision around retrieval rather than rereading. Make a
timeline of the play's key scenes with one short quotation for each. For every major character and
theme, note how they appear at the start, middle and end. Practise with random extracts: open the play
at any scene, give yourself ten minutes, and plan how that extract links to the rest of the play.
