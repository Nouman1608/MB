## 2.9 The composition of dry air

"Dry" means the water vapour has been left out. Water vapour varies from place to place and day to day, so the fixed percentages are always quoted for dry air.

| Gas | Approximate % by volume |
|---|---|
| Nitrogen, N₂ | 78 |
| Oxygen, O₂ | 21 |
| Argon, Ar | 0.9 |
| Carbon dioxide, CO₂ | 0.04 |

The rest is tiny amounts of other noble gases and trace gases. Learn the order as well as the numbers: argon is more abundant than carbon dioxide. Oxygen is about one-fifth of air, so removing the oxygen from a sample should shrink its volume by about one-fifth.

### Worked example 1: volumes in a sample

**Question.** Estimate the volume of each of the four main gases in 250 cm³ of dry air.

**Working.** Multiply the total volume by each percentage.

```
nitrogen:       250 × 78/100   = 195 cm³
oxygen:         250 × 21/100   = 52.5 cm³
argon:          250 × 0.9/100  = 2.25 cm³
carbon dioxide: 250 × 0.04/100 = 0.1 cm³
```

**Answer.** About 195 cm³ nitrogen, 52.5 cm³ oxygen, 2.25 cm³ argon and 0.1 cm³ carbon dioxide.

## 2.10 and 2.14 Measuring the percentage of oxygen in air

Every method traps a known volume of air and lets a substance react with its oxygen, forming a solid (or a product that dissolves). After returning to room temperature, the decrease in gas volume is the volume of oxygen.

```
volume of oxygen = starting volume of air − final volume of gas
% oxygen = (volume of oxygen ÷ starting volume of air) × 100
```

Always divide by the **starting** volume, never by the final volume.

### Method A: iron (a metal)

1. Wet the inside of a measuring cylinder (or a graduated tube) and push a small plug of iron wool to the closed end.
2. Turn the cylinder upside down in a trough of water and read the volume of trapped air.
3. Leave it for about a week. The iron rusts: iron + oxygen + water → hydrated iron(III) oxide. Rusting needs both water and oxygen, which is why the iron wool is damp.
4. As oxygen is used up, water rises into the cylinder. When the level stops changing, read the final volume.

Iron wool has a large surface area, so it reacts faster than a nail. No heating is needed, but the method is slow.

### Method B: phosphorus (a non-metal)

A small piece of phosphorus is placed in a dish floating on water inside a bell jar or tube of trapped air, and lit (usually with a hot wire). It burns with a bright flame and gives dense white smoke of phosphorus(V) oxide:

```
4P(s) + 5O₂(g) → P₄O₁₀(s)
```

The oxide dissolves in the water, so water rises to take the place of the oxygen that was used. Phosphorus is hazardous: this is normally a teacher demonstration. You must wait for the gas to cool to room temperature before reading the volume, because hot gas has expanded.

### Method C: copper and gas syringes (another metal)

A known volume of air is pushed back and forth between two gas syringes over strongly heated copper. The pink-brown copper turns black: 2Cu(s) + O₂(g) → 2CuO(s). Keep passing the air over until the volume stops falling, so that all the oxygen reacts, then cool and read the final volume.

### Worked example 2: iron wool result

**Question.** A student traps 94.0 cm³ of air over damp iron wool. After eight days the gas volume is 74.6 cm³ and no longer changing. Calculate the percentage by volume of oxygen in the air.

**Working.**

```
volume of oxygen = 94.0 − 74.6 = 19.4 cm³
% oxygen = 19.4 ÷ 94.0 × 100 = 20.6%
```

**Answer.** **20.6%**, close to the accepted 21%.

### Worked example 3: why reading too early gives a low answer

**Question.** In a phosphorus experiment, a tube holds 60.0 cm³ of air. Straight after burning, a student reads 50.5 cm³. After cooling to room temperature, the volume is 47.8 cm³. Calculate the percentage of oxygen from each reading and explain which is correct.

**Working.**

```
warm reading: 60.0 − 50.5 = 9.5 cm³  →  9.5 ÷ 60.0 × 100 = 15.8%
cool reading: 60.0 − 47.8 = 12.2 cm³ →  12.2 ÷ 60.0 × 100 = 20.3%
```

**Answer.** The cooled reading, **20.3%**, is correct. The warm gas had expanded, so its volume was too large and the apparent decrease was too small.

### Why results can come out low or high

- **Low:** not all the oxygen reacted (too little iron or copper, not enough time, too few passes over the copper); reading the volume while the gas is still warm; air leaking in at a joint.
- **High:** reading the final volume when the apparatus is colder than at the start.

## 2.11 Combustion of elements in oxygen

Combustion is a reaction with oxygen that releases heat and light. Elements burn far more brightly in pure oxygen than in air, because air is only about one-fifth oxygen. Each element forms its oxide.

| Element | What you see | Product | Equation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Magnesium | Very bright white flame (don't look straight at it); white powder left | Magnesium oxide | 2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s) |
| Hydrogen | Pale blue flame, hard to see; a mixture with air ignites with a squeaky pop | Water | 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l) |
| Sulfur | Melts, then burns with a blue flame | Sulfur dioxide, a colourless gas with a choking smell | S(s) + O₂(g) → SO₂(g) |

Water forms as steam in the flame and condenses on a cold surface, so H₂O(l) is the usual state symbol at room temperature.

### Worked example 4: describing a combustion

**Question.** Describe what you would see when a piece of magnesium ribbon is heated and lowered into a gas jar of oxygen. Write the balanced equation with state symbols.

**Answer.** A very bright white flame; a white powder is left.

```
2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s)
```

Two marks need two separate observations. "It burns" alone is not an observation.

## 2.12 Carbon dioxide from metal carbonates

Thermal decomposition is breaking down a compound by heat alone. Metal carbonates decompose to the metal oxide and carbon dioxide:

```
metal carbonate → metal oxide + carbon dioxide
CuCO₃(s) → CuO(s) + CO₂(g)
```

Copper(II) carbonate is a green powder. On heating it turns **black** (copper(II) oxide) and gives off carbon dioxide. Other examples:

- ZnCO₃(s) → ZnO(s) + CO₂(g): white solid, yellow while hot, white again on cooling.
- CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g): needs strong heating.

How easily a carbonate decomposes varies. Copper(II) carbonate breaks down easily in a Bunsen flame; calcium carbonate needs much stronger heating; sodium and potassium carbonates do not decompose at Bunsen temperatures.

**Apparatus.** Heat the carbonate in a boiling tube fitted with a delivery tube that dips into limewater. Carbon dioxide turns the limewater milky (cloudy). When you stop, lift the delivery tube out of the limewater **before** you remove the flame, otherwise cold limewater can be sucked back into the hot tube and crack it.

### Worked example 5: reacting masses

**Question.** 9.88 g of copper(II) carbonate is heated until there is no further change. Calculate (a) the mass of copper(II) oxide left and (b) the mass of carbon dioxide given off. (Ar: Cu = 63.5, C = 12, O = 16)

**Working.**

```
Mr CuCO₃ = 63.5 + 12 + (3 × 16) = 123.5
Mr CuO   = 63.5 + 16 = 79.5
Mr CO₂   = 12 + (2 × 16) = 44

moles CuCO₃ = 9.88 ÷ 123.5 = 0.0800 mol
1 mol CuCO₃ → 1 mol CuO + 1 mol CO₂

(a) mass CuO = 0.0800 × 79.5 = 6.36 g
(b) mass CO₂ = 0.0800 × 44 = 3.52 g
```

**Answer.** (a) **6.36 g** (b) **3.52 g**. Check: 6.36 + 3.52 = 9.88 g, so mass is conserved. In practice the mass loss on the balance is the mass of CO₂ that escaped.

**(c) Paper 2 only (uses point 1.35C).** Volume of CO₂ at rtp = 0.0800 × 24 = **1.92 dm³**.

## 2.13 Carbon dioxide as a greenhouse gas

You need to know two things: carbon dioxide is a greenhouse gas, and increasing amounts of it in the atmosphere **may contribute to climate change**. A clear explanation runs in steps:

1. The Sun's radiation passes through the atmosphere and warms the Earth's surface.
2. The warm surface gives out infrared (heat) radiation.
3. Greenhouse gases such as carbon dioxide absorb some of this infrared and send some of it back towards the surface.
4. This keeps the lower atmosphere warmer than it would otherwise be.
5. Burning fossil fuels and cutting down forests increase the amount of carbon dioxide, so more heat is trapped. This may contribute to climate change, for example rising average temperatures, melting ice, rising sea levels and changes in weather patterns.

Use the specification's wording: "may contribute to climate change".

## Common errors

- Swapping argon and carbon dioxide in the abundance order, or giving CO₂ as 4% instead of 0.04%.
- Dividing the volume decrease by the final volume instead of the starting volume.
- Reading the gas volume before the apparatus has cooled after burning phosphorus or heating copper.
- Forgetting that iron needs water as well as oxygen to rust in the iron-wool method.
- Calling the sulfur product "sulfur trioxide" or writing S + O₂ → SO₃.
- Describing copper(II) carbonate as "blue" (it is green) or its oxide as "brown" (it is black).
- Removing the flame before the delivery tube, causing suck-back.
