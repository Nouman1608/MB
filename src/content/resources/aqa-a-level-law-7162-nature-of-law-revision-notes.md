Condensed notes on the **Nature of law** row of AQA A-level Law (7162) section 3.1: legal rules and principles versus other rules and norms; criminal versus civil law; and custom, statute and the common law as sources. For the full explanation and a worked answer, use the [Nature of Law study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-7162-nature-of-law/).

## The one-line test

A rule is a **legal** rule when a **recognised state authority** made it, it **binds everyone in the jurisdiction**, and the **state enforces it through the courts** with formal sanctions. Everything else (school rules, club rules, etiquette, religious codes) is a rule or norm of behaviour, enforced by the group itself or by conscience.

## Five contrasts to learn as pairs

1. **Origin:** legal rules are made at an identifiable time by Parliament or the courts; norms evolve gradually.
2. **Scope:** legal rules bind all within the jurisdiction; norms bind members or those who accept them.
3. **Enforcement:** courts, police, fines, imprisonment, damages; versus disapproval, exclusion, guilt.
4. **Change:** an Act can change the law overnight; norms shift slowly and unevenly.
5. **Challenge:** only through legal routes such as appeal or judicial review; norms can simply be ignored at social cost.

**A norm can become law.** Smoking in enclosed workplaces went from bad manners to an offence under the Health Act 2006.

## Rules and principles

- **Rule:** specific and mechanical (a 30 mph limit).
- **Principle:** general and guiding (presumption of innocence; no one profits from their own wrong).
- Judges reach for principles when rules give no answer; this is why the specification names both.

## Two theorists for precise explanation

- **Austin:** law is the sovereign's command backed by a sanction. Weakness: ignores power-conferring rules such as making a will.
- **Hart, *The Concept of Law* (1961):** law is primary rules (conduct) plus secondary rules (recognition, change, adjudication). A school rule is not law because the rule of recognition does not recognise the school as a law-maker.

## Criminal versus civil: the extra points

| Point | Criminal | Civil |
|---|---|---|
| Who starts it | Usually the Crown Prosecution Service for the state | The claimant |
| Citation | *R v Smith* | *Smith v Jones* |
| Verdict wording | Guilty / not guilty | Liable / not liable |
| Who controls it | The state; victim cannot drop it | The claimant; can settle or discontinue |
| Aim | Protect society, punish | Protect private rights, remedy |

**Same facts, two cases:** careless driving that injures a cyclist can lead to a prosecution and a negligence claim. Different standards of proof mean an acquittal does not prevent civil liability.

## Sources of law

### Custom

Tests for a valid local custom:

- existed since **time immemorial** (1189);
- exercised **peaceably, openly and as of right**;
- **reasonable**;
- **certain** as to place and beneficiaries;
- **consistent with statute**.

| Case | Outcome | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *Mercer v Denne* (1905) | Custom upheld | Fishermen had long dried nets on the land |
| *Simpson v Wells* (1872) | Custom failed | Fair traced to fourteenth-century statute sessions, so not time immemorial |
| *Wolstanton Ltd v Newcastle-under-Lyme Corporation* (1940) | Custom failed | Taking minerals without compensation was unreasonable |

### Statute

Acts of Parliament (and delegated legislation made under them). **Highest domestic source**; can abolish custom and reverse common law, e.g. the Law Reform (Year and a Day Rule) Act 1996.

### Common law

Judge-made law through precedent. Murder is a common law offence; negligence grew from *Donoghue v Stevenson* (1932). Yields to statute.

**Ranking:** statute > common law > custom.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Legal rule | A rule made by a state authority, binding generally, enforced by the courts |
| Norm | An expected standard of behaviour within a group, not state-enforced |
| Sanction | The consequence imposed for breaking a rule: for law, a court-imposed penalty or remedy |
| Claimant | The person who brings a civil case |
| Prosecution | The party, usually the state, bringing a criminal case |
| Liable | Legally responsible in a civil case |
| Custom | A long-standing local practice recognised as law if it meets the tests |
| Time immemorial | Legal memory, fixed at 1189 |
| Statute | An Act of Parliament |
| Common law | Law developed by judges through decided cases |

## Exam traps

- Using importance or popularity as the test for a legal rule.
- "Guilty" in a civil case; "prosecute" for a claimant.
- Giving custom without the tests, or treating it as a significant modern source.
- Saying common law can override statute.
- Listing differences without an example; every "explain" answer needs one.

## Self-test

1. What single feature most clearly separates legal rules from other norms?
2. Give one example of a norm that became a legal rule.
3. Distinguish a legal rule from a legal principle.
4. What are Hart's secondary rules?
5. Why are criminal cases cited as *R v* a defendant?
6. Can a victim stop a prosecution by refusing to continue?
7. Why might a defendant acquitted of careless driving still be liable in negligence?
8. What date marks time immemorial?
9. Why did the custom in *Simpson v Wells* fail?
10. Why did the custom in *Wolstanton* fail?
11. Which is the highest domestic source of law, and why?
12. Give an example of a common law offence.

**Answers:** 1. Enforcement by the state through the courts with formal sanctions. 2. Smoking in enclosed workplaces, made an offence under the Health Act 2006. 3. A rule is specific and applied directly; a principle is general and guides how rules are applied or developed. 4. Rules about rules: the rule of recognition, rules of change and rules of adjudication. 5. The prosecution is brought in the name of the Crown, representing the state. 6. No; the prosecution belongs to the state, although lack of evidence may lead the prosecutor to discontinue. 7. The civil standard of proof (balance of probabilities) is lower than the criminal standard (beyond reasonable doubt). 8. 1189. 9. The fair dated back only to fourteenth-century statute sessions, so it did not exist from time immemorial. 10. It allowed minerals to be taken without compensation, which was unreasonable. 11. Statute, because of parliamentary supremacy: an Act can override custom and common law. 12. Murder.
