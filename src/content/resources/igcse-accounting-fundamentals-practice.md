## Section A

**1.** State the accounting equation and explain why capital appears alongside liabilities. **[3]**

**2.** Name the book of prime entry for each: credit sales, cash paid to a supplier, goods returned by a customer, purchase of a machine on credit. **[4]**

## Section B

**4.** State the two accounts to be debited and credited for each transaction.

**(a)** The owner pays $5000 of their own money into the business bank account. **[2]**

**(b)** Goods are bought on credit from Khan Ltd for $800. **[2]**

**(c)** A customer, Ali, pays $300 by cheque. **[2]**

**(d)** Rent of $450 is paid by cheque. **[2]**

**5.** A trial balance balances, but the bookkeeper is told errors still exist.

**(a)** Name and explain **four** types of error that do not affect trial balance agreement. **[8]**

**(b)** A payment for a new delivery van was recorded in the motor expenses account. Name this type of error and state its effect on profit and on non-current assets. **[3]**

**6.** A business has: premises $80 000, inventory $12 000, trade receivables $6000, bank $4000, loan $30 000, trade payables $8000.

**(a)** Calculate the capital. **[3]**

**(b)** State whether the loan is a current or non-current liability, with a reason. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** **Assets = Capital + Liabilities** [1]. Under the **business entity concept** the business is treated as separate from its owner [1], so capital is an amount the business **owes back to the owner** [1].

**2.** Credit sales — **sales journal** [1]. Cash paid to a supplier — **cash book** [1]. Goods returned by a customer — **sales returns journal** [1]. Machine bought on credit — **general journal** [1].

**3.** Drawings are the owner **taking value out of the business for personal use** [1], not a cost incurred in earning revenue, so they **reduce capital rather than profit** [1].

**4. (a)** Debit **bank** $5000 [1]; credit **capital** $5000 [1].
**(b)** Debit **purchases** $800 [1]; credit **Khan Ltd (payables)** $800 [1].
**(c)** Debit **bank** $300 [1]; credit **Ali (receivables)** $300 [1].
**(d)** Debit **rent** $450 [1]; credit **bank** $450 [1].

**5. (a)** Any four, each named [1] and explained [1]:
**Omission** — the transaction is left out of the books entirely.
**Commission** — the correct *type* of account but the **wrong account**, e.g. posted to the wrong customer.
**Principle** — the **wrong type of account** entirely, e.g. an asset recorded as an expense.
**Original entry** — the **same incorrect figure** used for both the debit and the credit.
**Complete reversal** — the debit and credit entries are the **wrong way round**.
**Compensating** — **two separate errors of equal value** cancel each other out.

**(b)** An error of **principle** [1] — capital expenditure has been treated as revenue expenditure.
Profit is **understated** [1] and non-current assets are **understated** [1].

**6. (a)** Assets = 80 000 + 12 000 + 6000 + 4000 = **$102 000** [1].
Liabilities = 30 000 + 8000 = **$38 000** [1].
Capital = 102 000 − 38 000 = **$64 000** [1].

**(b)** **Non-current** [1], because it is due for repayment **after more than one year** [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Recording cash sales in the sales journal.
- Treating drawings as an expense.
- Confusing errors of commission and principle.
- Giving only one effect of misclassifying capital expenditure.
- Assuming a balanced trial balance means the books are correct.
