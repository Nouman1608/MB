## 3.5 Government spending and revenue

**Purposes of government spending**
- Provide public goods and merit goods, such as roads, defence, schools and healthcare.
- Redistribute income through pensions and benefits (transfer payments).
- Influence the economy: more spending raises demand, less spending reduces it.
- Support firms or consumers, for example through subsidies.
- Pay interest on past borrowing.

**Sources of revenue**
- **Direct taxes** are paid straight to the government from income or profit: income tax, national insurance, corporation tax.
- **Indirect taxes** are added to the price of goods and services, so the seller collects them: VAT and excise duties on fuel, alcohol or tobacco.
- Other sources: borrowing, selling state assets and charges for services.

| | Direct tax | Indirect tax |
|---|---|---|
| Paid on | Income or profit | Spending |
| Paid by | The person or firm taxed | Seller collects, burden shared with the buyer |
| Fits ability to pay? | Easily made progressive | Often regressive |
| Avoidance | Possible through legal planning | Harder, but people can buy less |

## Budgets: balanced, surplus, deficit

A **balanced budget** has revenue equal to spending. A **budget surplus** has revenue above spending. A **budget deficit** has spending above revenue, and the gap is borrowed.

**Worked example: Country A (illustrative year, billions of cu)**

| Spending | cu | Revenue | cu |
|---|---|---|---|
| Health | 90 | Income tax | 110 |
| Education | 60 | National insurance | 55 |
| Welfare | 100 | Corporation tax | 35 |
| Defence | 30 | VAT | 80 |
| Debt interest | 20 | Excise duties | 25 |
| Other | 40 | Other | 20 |
| **Total** | **340** | **Total** | **325** |

Budget balance = 325 − 340 = −15, a **deficit of 15 billion cu**. Direct taxes = 110 + 55 + 35 = 200. Indirect taxes = 80 + 25 = 105. Share of revenue from indirect taxes = 105 ÷ 325 = 32.3%. Health is 90 ÷ 340 = 26.5% of spending. If GDP is 1,700 billion cu, spending is 340 ÷ 1,700 = 20.0% of GDP and the deficit is 15 ÷ 1,700 = 0.88% of GDP.

In a recession, tax receipts tend to fall and benefit payments rise, so a deficit can widen without any decision to spend more. Deficits add to the national debt.

## Fiscal policy and economic objectives

**Fiscal policy** means a government changing how much it spends and how much tax it raises in order to steer the economy towards its objectives.

- **Expansionary** (reflationary): higher spending or lower taxes. It raises aggregate demand and is used when growth is weak or unemployment is high.
- **Contractionary** (deflationary): lower spending or higher taxes. It reduces aggregate demand and is used when inflation is too high.

**Chain: tax cut and growth.** Lower income tax raises disposable income, so consumer spending rises. Aggregate demand rises, firms produce more, output grows and they hire more workers, so unemployment falls. If the economy is near full capacity, firms raise prices instead, so inflation can rise.

**Worked example: a tax cut.** Country A cuts the 20% band to 18% on incomes between 12,000 and 40,000 cu. A worker earning 30,000 cu has 18,000 cu in that band, so gains 18,000 × 0.02 = 360 cu a year. Anyone earning over 40,000 cu gains 28,000 × 0.02 = 560 cu.

**Worked example: knock-on effect.** The specification does not use the word multiplier, but you can use the idea to explain why spending has a larger effect than its own size. Suppose the government spends 200 cu on a new bridge. If recipients spend 60% of extra income, 120 cu is re-spent, then 72 cu, and so on. The total rise in income = 200 × 1 ÷ (1 − 0.6) = 200 × 2.5 = 500 cu. If they re-spend 80%, it is 200 × 5 = 1,000 cu. Saving, taxes and imports slow each round.

## Taxes and spending in markets

**Worked example: an indirect tax.** In a fictional market for a snack, demand is Qd = 900 − 50P and supply is Qs = 100P − 300, with P in cu.

| | Before tax | After a 3 cu per-unit tax |
|---|---|---|
| Price paid by buyers | 8 | 10 |
| Quantity | 500 | 400 |
| Price kept by sellers | 8 | 7 |

The tax shifts supply upward by 3 cu. Buyers pay 2 cu more and sellers keep 1 cu less, so the burden is shared: consumers pay 2 × 400 = 800 cu of it and producers 1 × 400 = 400 cu. Tax revenue = 3 × 400 = 1,200 cu (800 + 400). Quantity falls from 500 to 400, a fall of 20%, while the price paid rises by 25%.

**Worked example: a subsidy.** A 1.5 cu per-unit subsidy in the same market shifts supply down. The new equilibrium is price 7 and quantity 550. Buyers save 1 cu, sellers receive 7 + 1.5 = 8.5 cu, up 0.5 cu. Cost to government = 1.5 × 550 = 825 cu, split 550 to consumers and 275 to producers.

Compare the two: tax raises revenue and cuts output, a subsidy costs money and raises output. Governments use taxes on harmful goods and subsidies on goods with benefits to others.

## Costs and benefits, including opportunity cost

| Benefits | Costs |
|---|---|
| Can raise demand quickly in a recession | Opportunity cost: money spent on one project cannot be used on another |
| Funds public and merit goods | Borrowing raises debt and future interest payments |
| Can reduce inequality | Higher taxes may reduce work effort, saving or investment |
| Can target regions or groups | Time lags: policy takes time to decide and take effect |

**Opportunity cost** is the next best alternative given up. If Country A spends 40 billion cu on a rail line, it cannot also spend those 40 billion cu on school repairs. Evaluate by asking who gains, who loses, how large the effect is and how long it lasts.

## Redistribution and progressive taxes

Governments redistribute income through taxes, benefits and free services. Tax systems can be:
- **Progressive**: the average rate rises as income rises.
- **Proportional**: the same average rate at all incomes.
- **Regressive**: richer households pay a smaller share of their income.

**Worked example.** Country A charges 0% on the first 12,000 cu, 20% on 12,000 to 40,000 cu and 40% above that.

| Income | Tax | Average rate | After tax |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20,000 | 8,000 × 0.2 = 1,600 | 8.0% | 18,400 |
| 30,000 | 18,000 × 0.2 = 3,600 | 12.0% | 26,400 |
| 55,000 | 5,600 + 6,000 = 11,600 | 21.1% | 43,400 |

Higher incomes face a bigger average rate, which makes the tax progressive. A 20% sales tax is regressive in effect. A household on 20,000 cu spending 18,000 cu pays 18,000 ÷ 1.2 × 0.2 = 3,000 cu, which is 15% of income. A household on 55,000 cu spending 33,000 cu pays 5,500 cu, which is 10%.

**Evaluation.** Redistribution reduces inequality, helps lower-income households (who tend to spend more of extra income) and funds services. Against that, very high rates may reduce incentives, encourage avoidance or discourage skilled workers, and generous benefits cost taxpayers. The strength of each point depends on the size of the rates and the state of the economy.

## 3.6 Monetary policy

**Monetary policy** is the use of interest rates (and sometimes the money supply) by the central bank to influence the economy. In the UK the Bank of England sets Bank Rate, which feeds into the rates banks charge and pay. Lower rates are expansionary and higher rates are contractionary.

**Rate cut chain.** Borrowing is cheaper and saving is less attractive, so consumer spending and investment rise. Aggregate demand rises, so growth rises and unemployment falls, but inflation pressure increases. A rate rise works in reverse and is used to cut inflation.

| Group | Effect of higher interest rates |
|---|---|
| Consumers | Dearer loans, more reward for saving, so less spending |
| Borrowers | Higher repayments |
| Savers | Higher return |
| Firms | Higher borrowing cost, so less investment |

**Worked example: borrowing.** An interest-only loan of 150,000 cu costs 6,000 cu a year at 4% and 9,000 cu at 6%. The extra is 3,000 cu a year, or 250 cu a month, reducing the household's spending.

**Worked example: saving and real returns.** 10,000 cu earns 200 cu at 2% and 100 cu at 1%. If the rate is 3% and inflation is 4%, the real return is about 3 − 4 = −1%, so savers lose purchasing power despite earning interest.

**Worked example: investment.** A bakery buys an oven for 20,000 cu and expects 1,300 cu a year extra profit before interest, a 6.5% return. Borrowing at 5% costs 1,000 cu, leaving 300 cu, so it invests. At 8% the cost is 1,600 cu, a loss of 300 cu, so it does not.

**Evaluation.** Monetary policy is flexible and quick to change, but the full effect takes time. It may fail if confidence is low, if banks do not pass on changes, or if households hold large debts. Savers gain from rises and lose from cuts, while borrowers gain from cuts.

## Common errors
- Confusing a deficit (annual gap) with debt (total owed).
- Calling national insurance an indirect tax.
- Describing fiscal policy as the central bank's job.
- Writing "progressive" when only the total tax paid rises; it is the average rate that must rise.
- Giving only benefits in an evaluation, with no cost, judgement or condition.
