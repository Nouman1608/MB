## Questions

**1.** State two characteristics of a monopoly. **[2]**

**2.** A firm faces the demand curve P = 70 − 2Q. Calculate its total revenue at 8 units and at 9 units, and the marginal revenue of the 9th unit. **[3]**

**3.** A sole supplier of a patented irrigation valve in Country C has demand P = 104 − 4Q and total cost TC = 180 + 14Q + Q².

**(a)** Calculate the profit-maximising output and price. **[4]**
**(b)** Calculate the maximum profit. **[2]**

**4.** Explain, with the aid of a diagram, why a monopolist is a price maker and can earn supernormal profit in the long run. **[4]**

**5.** Define the term monopsony and give an example. **[3]**

**6.** A sole district heating network in Country D has TC = 750 + 4Q (so MC = 4) and demand P = 44 − 0.5Q.

**(a)** Calculate the profit-maximising output, price and profit. **[4]**
**(b)** Calculate the output and price where P = ATC. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why this is a natural monopoly. **[2]**

**7.** Use the firm in question 3.

**(a)** Calculate the output and price at which P = MC. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the deadweight loss of the monopoly. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the monopolist is not productively efficient. **[2]**

**8.** A coach operator has constant MC = 8. Peak demand is P = 44 − 2Q and off-peak demand is P = 30 − 2Q.

**(a)** Find the output and price that maximise profit in each of the two markets. **[4]**
**(b)** Calculate total profit. **[1]**
**(c)** Show how much extra profit discrimination earns compared with one price for both markets. **[3]**

**9.** Explain the conditions needed for a firm to price discriminate. **[4]**

**10.** Evaluate the impact of the existence of a natural monopoly. **[8]**

**11.** "Monopoly power always harms consumers." Evaluate this claim. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two from: a single or dominant seller [1]; high barriers to entry [1]; no close substitutes or being a price maker. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Two distinct characteristics earn credit; repeating one idea in different words does not.

**2.** TR at 8 = (70 − 16) × 8 = 54 × 8 = **432** [1]. TR at 9 = (70 − 18) × 9 = 52 × 9 = **468** [1]. MR of the 9th unit = 468 − 432 = **36** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Marginal revenue is the change in total revenue; showing both totals makes your method visible even if one slips.

**3.** (a) MR = 104 − 8Q [1]. MC = 14 + 2Q [1]. MC = MR: 104 − 8Q = 14 + 2Q, so 10Q = 90 and Q = **9** [1]. P = 104 − 36 = **68** [1].
**(b)** TR = 68 × 9 = 612; TC = 180 + 126 + 81 = 387 [1]. Profit = 612 − 387 = **225** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* The price comes from the demand curve above the chosen output; quoting the MC value of 32 as the price is a common slip.

**4.** The firm is the only seller, so it faces the downward-sloping market demand curve [1]. It sets output where MC = MR and reads the price from demand above that output [1]. Supernormal profit is the rectangle between price and ATC at that output [1]. High barriers to entry stop new firms competing the profit away, so it can last in the long run [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A diagram with MR below AR, the MC = MR output marked and the profit rectangle shaded supports your words; "barriers" is the link that explains long-run profit.

**5.** A monopsony exists where there is only one buyer, or one dominant buyer [1]. It has the power to push the price it pays below the competitive level [1]. Example: a sole buyer of milk from many small farms [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* State that the power lies on the buying side; many answers describe a monopoly instead.

**6.** (a) MR = 44 − Q [1]. MR = MC: 44 − Q = 4, so Q = **40** [1]. P = 44 − 20 = **24** [1]. Profit = 24 × 40 − (750 + 160) = 960 − 910 = **50** [1].
**(b)** 44 − 0.5Q = 750/Q + 4 [1]. Multiply by 2Q: Q² − 80Q + 1,500 = 0, so Q = 50 or 30 [1]. Take Q = **50**, where ATC is still falling and the price is 44 − 25 = **19** [1].
**(c)** ATC = 750/Q + 4 falls at every output [1]. One firm making 50 units has ATC 19, but two firms making 25 each would have ATC = 30 + 4 = 34 each [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* When a quadratic has two roots, say which you reject and why; a natural monopoly answer should use the falling ATC rather than just "high fixed costs".

**7.** (a) P = MC: 104 − 4Q = 14 + 2Q, so Q = **15** [1] and P = 104 − 60 = **44** [1].
**(b)** At Q = 9, MC = 32 and P = 68. DWL = 0.5 × (15 − 9) × (68 − 32) [1] = **108** [1].
**(c)** Minimum ATC occurs where Q = √180 = 13.4, with ATC about 40.8 [1]. The monopolist produces 9 units at ATC 43, above that minimum, so it is not productively efficient [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* The deadweight loss triangle sits between demand and MC, between the monopoly and efficient outputs; shading the profit rectangle instead loses the point.

**8.** (a) Peak: MR = 44 − 4Q = 8, so Q = **9**, P = 44 − 18 = **26** [2]. Off-peak: 30 − 4Q = 8, so Q = **5.5**, P = 30 − 11 = **19** [2].
**(b)** Peak profit = (26 − 8) × 9 = 162. Off-peak profit = (19 − 8) × 5.5 = 60.5. Total = **222.5** [1].
**(c)** Combined demand is Q = (44 − P)/2 + (30 − P)/2 = 37 − P, so P = 37 − Q and MR = 37 − 2Q [1]. MR = 8 gives Q = 14.5 and P = 22.5, so profit = 14.5 × 14.5 = 210.25 [1]. Extra profit = 222.5 − 210.25 = **12.25** [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Treat each market separately, because the price follows each market's own demand; the more inelastic peak market gets the higher price.

**9.** The firm needs market power, so it can set price [1]. It must keep the groups apart, for example by age, place or time [1]. Resale between the groups must be prevented, or low-price buyers would sell on to the high-price group [1]. The markets need different price elasticities of demand, so that different prices raise revenue [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each condition needs a short reason, not just a name; "no resale" is often left without saying why it matters.

**10.** A natural monopoly has ATC falling over the whole market, so one firm gives the lowest cost per unit [1]. Duplication would raise cost: two firms of 25 units would each have ATC 34, against 19 for one firm of 50 [1]. Unregulated, the firm sets MR = MC, so output is 40 and price 24, rather than 50 and 19 at average-cost pricing [1]. That means higher prices and lower output for consumers [1]. A price cap near ATC raises output and keeps the firm viable [1], but the regulator may not know the true cost [1]. Pricing at MC (output 80, price 4) would need a subsidy to cover a loss of 750 [1]. Judgement: the impact is favourable if scale economies are large and regulation is effective, and unfavourable if the firm is unregulated, so it depends on the strength of regulation [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation gains credit when it weighs the benefit of one firm against the risk of exploitation and ends with a reasoned view, not just a list of points.

**11.** Harm: the monopolist sets MC = MR, so with the firm in question 3 output is 9 and price 68, against 15 and 44 at P = MC [1]. This is allocative inefficiency with a deadweight loss of 108 [1]. Consumers lose surplus and choice [1]. Lack of rivals may cause X-inefficiency [1]. Against: if the firm has large economies of scale, its costs may be low enough that price is below what competitive firms would charge [1]. Supernormal profit can fund research and development, leading to new products [1]. A patent-protected firm may only invest because of that protection [1]. Regulation or the threat of entry can limit the harm [1]. Judgement: "always" is too strong, because the result depends on cost advantage, reinvestment and regulation [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Challenge the word "always" directly; a closing judgement that says what the outcome turns on usually reads better than another list of points.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Quoting an MC or MR value as the monopoly price when it should come from AR.
- Setting MR equal to price, or giving MR the same slope as demand.
- Shading supernormal profit between price and MC.
- Giving a deadweight loss without naming the two outputs it lies between.
- Saying "natural monopoly" without referring to falling average cost.
- Naming price discrimination conditions with no reason.
- Evaluating with a list of pros and cons and no final judgement.
- Dropping the "may be" in efficiency answers when scale economies are large.
