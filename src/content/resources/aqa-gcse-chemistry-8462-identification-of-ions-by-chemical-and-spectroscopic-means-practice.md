These questions cover section **4.8.3 Identification of ions by chemical and spectroscopic means (chemistry
only)** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) specification, for teaching from September 2016. The content is
assessed on Paper 2 in the separate chemistry qualification. The specification marks nothing in this section
(HT only), so no question is labelled Higher tier only. Question 8 is based on required practical 7.

## Questions

**1.** State the flame colour produced by each metal ion.

**(a)** Lithium **[1]**
**(b)** Potassium **[1]**
**(c)** Copper(II) **[1]**
**(d)** Calcium **[1]**

**2.** A student does a flame test on a salt and sees a yellow flame.

**(a)** Name the metal ion. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why a second ion in the sample might not be noticed. **[1]**
**(c)** Describe how the student should avoid contamination between tests. **[2]**

**3.** Sodium hydroxide solution is added to solutions of four metal compounds. The observations are shown.

| Solution | Observation with a few drops | Observation with excess |
|---|---|---|
| A | White precipitate | Precipitate dissolves |
| B | Blue precipitate | No change |
| C | Brown precipitate | No change |
| D | Green precipitate | No change |

**(a)** Identify the metal ion in each solution. **[4]**
**(b)** Write the ionic equation, including state symbols, for the formation of the precipitate in B. **[2]**

**4.** Solutions of magnesium chloride and calcium chloride are both colourless. Both give a white precipitate with sodium hydroxide that does not dissolve in excess.

**(a)** Describe a test that would distinguish the two solutions. **[2]**
**(b)** Give the result for each. **[2]**

**5.** A student adds dilute acid to a solid. Bubbles form.

**(a)** Describe how to show the gas is carbon dioxide. **[2]**
**(b)** Name the type of ion in the solid. **[1]**

**6.** Silver nitrate solution and dilute nitric acid are added to three halide solutions.

**(a)** Give the colour of the precipitate with chloride, bromide and iodide. **[3]**
**(b)** Write the ionic equation for the formation of silver chloride, including state symbols. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why nitric acid is added. **[1]**

**7.** A solution X gives a white precipitate with barium chloride solution and dilute hydrochloric acid.

**(a)** Name the ion present. **[1]**
**(b)** Write the ionic equation, including state symbols, for the formation of the precipitate. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why dilute hydrochloric acid is added before the barium chloride. **[2]**

**8.** A technician is given two unlabelled white solids. One is sodium chloride and the other is potassium sulfate. Plan a series of tests to tell them apart, giving the expected results. **[5]**

**9.** An unknown solid Y is tested.
- Flame test: lilac flame.
- Dissolved in water, then sodium hydroxide: no precipitate.
- Dissolved in water, then silver nitrate and nitric acid: yellow precipitate.

**(a)** Identify Y. **[2]**
**(b)** State which ions are ruled out by the sodium hydroxide result. **[1]**

**10.** State **three** advantages of using instrumental methods rather than chemical tests to identify ions. **[3]**

**11.** Flame emission spectroscopy is used to analyse a solution. The table shows which reference lines are present in the spectra of four metal ions and in the sample.

| Line | Li | Na | K | Ca | Sample |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ✓ | | | | |
| 2 | | ✓ | | | ✓ |
| 3 | | | ✓ | | |
| 4 | | | | ✓ | ✓ |
| 5 | | ✓ | | | ✓ |
| 6 | | | | ✓ | ✓ |

**(a)** Identify the metal ions in the sample. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why flame emission spectroscopy is better than a flame test for this sample. **[2]**
**(c)** State what else, apart from the identity of the ions, the technique can provide. **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Crimson [1] **(b)** Lilac [1] **(c)** Green [1] **(d)** Orange-red [1]

**2. (a)** **Sodium** [1]
**(b)** The strong yellow of sodium can **mask** the colours of other ions [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** **Clean the wire loop** with dilute hydrochloric acid [1] and heat it until it gives no colour before the next test (or use a new splint) [1]. **[2]**

**3. (a)** A: **aluminium** [1]; B: **copper(II)** [1]; C: **iron(III)** [1]; D: **iron(II)** [1] **[4]**
**(b)** Cu²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Cu(OH)₂(s) [species and balance 1; state symbols 1] **[2]**

**4. (a)** Do a **flame test** on each solution [1] (clean loop, blue flame). **[2 with b]**
**(b)** Calcium gives an **orange-red** flame [1]; magnesium gives **no colour** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Sodium hydroxide cannot separate these two, because both precipitates are white and insoluble in excess.

**5. (a)** Bubble the gas through **limewater** [1]; it turns **milky** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** **Carbonate** (CO₃²⁻) [1]

**6. (a)** Chloride: **white**; bromide: **cream**; iodide: **yellow** [1] [1] [1] **[3]**
**(b)** Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s) [1] [state symbols 1] **[2]**
**(c)** To remove other ions, such as **carbonate**, that would also form a precipitate with silver ions [1]. **[1]**

**7. (a)** **Sulfate** [1]
**(b)** Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s) [1] [state symbols 1] **[2]**
**(c)** To remove **carbonate ions** [1], which would form a white precipitate of barium carbonate and give a false positive [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Any five [1] each: **dissolve** a sample of each solid in water; add **dilute hydrochloric acid and barium chloride** solution to each; the potassium sulfate gives a **white precipitate** and the sodium chloride gives no precipitate; add **dilute nitric acid and silver nitrate** to each; the sodium chloride gives a **white precipitate** and potassium sulfate gives none; a **flame test**: sodium chloride gives a **yellow** flame and potassium sulfate a **lilac** flame. **[5]**

**9. (a)** Lilac flame: **potassium** [1]; yellow precipitate with silver nitrate: **iodide**; Y is **potassium iodide** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Aluminium, calcium, magnesium, copper(II), iron(II) and iron(III) (any of these, as hydroxide precipitates would have formed) [1]. **[1]**

**10.** Any three [1] each: **rapid**; **accurate**; **sensitive** (detect very small amounts); can use **small samples**; can **measure concentration**; less reliant on judging colours by eye. **[3]**

**11. (a)** **Sodium** (lines 2 and 5) [1] and **calcium** (lines 4 and 6) [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Sodium's yellow flame can **mask** calcium's orange-red, so a flame test might show sodium only [1]; the spectrum shows the lines of **both** ions separately [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** The **concentration** of the ions [1]. **[1]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "white solid" instead of "white precipitate".
- Forgetting the excess sodium hydroxide test for aluminium.
- Not adding the nitric acid or hydrochloric acid, or not saying why.
- Leaving out the state symbols in ionic equations.
- Mixing up the colours of iron(II) and iron(III) hydroxides.
