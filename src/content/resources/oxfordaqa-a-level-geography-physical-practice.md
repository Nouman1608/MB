## Answers

**1.** The Park model plots **quality of life (or level of normality) against time** before, during and after a hazard event [1]. Quality of life **falls sharply at the event and through the relief phase** (hours to days of search and rescue and emergency aid) [1]. It then recovers through **rehabilitation** (weeks to months) and **reconstruction** (months to years), and may end **above, level with or below** its pre-disaster level depending on how well the community recovers and mitigates future risk [1].

**2.** Any two, 2 marks each (1 for the factor, 1 for how it shapes perception). **Wealth** — wealthier people can afford insurance, protection or relocation, so they tend to see the hazard as manageable and adjust to it [1], whereas poorer people may accept the risk because they cannot afford to leave the land or livelihood that exposes them [1]. **Religious or cultural beliefs** — where a hazard is seen as an act of God or of fate [1], people are more likely to respond with fatalism than with preparation [1]. **Education and access to information** — better-informed people understand the likelihood and consequences of the hazard more accurately [1], so they are more likely to support prediction, preparedness and mitigation [1].

**3.** Any three conditions, 2 marks each (1 for the condition, 1 for its explanation). **Vegetation type** — some vegetation, such as eucalyptus or conifers rich in oils and resins, ignites and burns very readily [1] [1]. **Fuel characteristics** — dry, fine fuels such as grass, twigs and leaf litter ignite easily, and an abundant, continuous fuel load, including ladder fuels, lets fire spread and climb into the canopy [1] [1]. **Climate and recent weather** — a long dry season or drought lowers the moisture content of fuel, while high temperatures, low humidity and strong winds preheat fuels and drive the fire forward [1] [1]. **Fire behaviour** — fire spreads faster uphill, and an intense fire can create its own winds and throw embers ahead of the front, starting spot fires [1] [1].

**4.** Marked by levels of response, assessing knowledge and understanding (AO1) and application, analysis and evaluation (AO2).

Indicative content (not exhaustive; credit other valid points):
- **Physical factors:** magnitude; focal depth; distance of the epicentre from population centres; ground conditions, such as liquefaction of unconsolidated sediments; secondary hazards such as landslides and tsunamis; the timing of the event.
- **Development and vulnerability:** building design and enforcement of building codes; preparedness, education and drills; monitoring and warning systems (for example tsunami warnings); the capacity of emergency and health services; governance; insurance and the resources for rehabilitation and reconstruction; population density and informal housing.
- **Evaluation:** development usually has a stronger influence on death tolls than magnitude, which is why events of similar magnitude have very different outcomes in areas at contrasting levels of development; but a very high magnitude event, or one that triggers a tsunami, can overwhelm even a well-prepared country; and wealthier areas often suffer higher absolute economic losses because the value of property at risk is greater. A top-level answer reaches a supported judgement using evidence from recent seismic events it has studied.

**5.** A **system** is a set of **interrelated components working together** [1]. **Inputs** are what enters the system and **outputs** what leaves it [1]. **Stores** are where matter or energy is held, and **flows (transfers)** are the movements between stores [1].

**6. Dynamic equilibrium** is a state in which **inputs and outputs are balanced over time**, so the system is stable although its components are constantly changing [1]. **Negative feedback** is a **self-regulating response that counteracts a change and returns the system towards equilibrium** [1] — for example, when storm waves erode sand from a beach and deposit it offshore as a bar, the bar makes later waves break further out, dissipating their energy and reducing further erosion of the beach [1].

**7.** Any two causes, 2 marks each (1 for the cause, 1 for its explanation). **Subtropical high pressure** — air descending in the subtropics warms as it sinks, so its relative humidity falls [1] and cloud and rain rarely form [1]. **Cold ocean currents** — cold offshore currents cool the air above them, so it holds little moisture [1] and tends to produce coastal fog rather than rain, as along the Atacama and Namib coasts [1]. **Continentality** — places deep in continental interiors are far from the sea [1], so the air reaching them has lost most of its moisture [1]. **Relief (rain shadow)** — air that has risen over mountains and lost its moisture on the windward side [1] descends and warms on the leeward side, giving dry conditions [1]. **Winds** — prevailing winds that blow from over land, such as offshore trade winds [1], carry little moisture [1].

**8.** Erosion, up to 4 marks: **deflation** — wind lifts and removes loose, fine particles [1], lowering the surface to form deflation hollows or leaving a desert pavement of coarser stones [1]; **abrasion** — sand grains carried by the wind blast exposed rock surfaces [1], most effectively close to the ground where moving grains are concentrated, shaping ventifacts, yardangs and zeugen [1]. Transport, up to 2 marks: fine dust is carried in **suspension** [1]; sand grains move by **saltation**, bouncing along the surface [1]; coarser grains are pushed along by **surface creep**, driven by the impact of saltating grains [1].

**9.** A **sediment cell** is a **largely self-contained stretch of coastline within which the movement of sediment is effectively closed** [1], bounded by major headlands or estuaries that prevent significant transfer to adjacent cells [1]. It contains **sources, transfers and sinks** of sediment [1].
It matters for management because **intervention at one point affects the whole cell** [1] — building groynes at one resort starves beaches downdrift, so management must be **planned at the scale of the whole cell**, which is why shoreline management plans are organised on this basis [1].

**10.** Waves approach the coast at an angle set by the prevailing wind, so the **swash carries sediment obliquely up the beach and the backwash returns it straight down** under gravity [1], giving a **net movement of sediment along the coast by longshore drift** [1]. Where the **coastline changes direction**, for example at the mouth of a bay or estuary, sediment continues to be carried and deposited into more open, sheltered water, so a **ridge of sand or shingle extends out from the land** [1]. Waves from a secondary direction and wave refraction can **curve the end of the spit into a recurved or hooked tip** [1]. The **sheltered, low-energy water behind the spit** allows fine sediment to settle, so mudflats and saltmarsh can develop there [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing the Park model without naming its relief, rehabilitation and reconstruction phases.
- Listing wildfire conditions without explaining how each makes a fire more intense.
- Answering an evaluation question on one side only, or without evidence from recent events you have studied.
- Confusing stores with flows.
- Describing the sediment cell without linking it to management.
