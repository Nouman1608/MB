The **historical investigation** is the internal assessment of IB DP
History in the course first assessed in 2017. Every student, SL and HL,
writes an investigation of up to **2,200 words** on a historical topic
of their own choice, in **three sections**: identification and
evaluation of sources, the investigation itself, and a reflection. It
is marked out of **25** by the teacher, moderated by the IB, and worth
**25% at SL and 20% at HL**, with 20 hours of recommended teaching time.

## Where does the investigation fit in the course?

It is the only component where you choose the content. The topic does
not have to come from your prescribed subject, world history topics or
HL region, so it is a chance to work on something that interests you,
provided you can find enough good sources. It assesses the skills the
exams test through the key concepts, especially **perspectives** and
the evaluation of evidence, and adds one skill the exams do not:
reflecting on how historians work. See the
[syllabus guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-syllabus-guide/) for the rest
of the course.

## What are the three sections and their marks?

| Section | What it must do | Marks |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Identification and evaluation of sources | State the question clearly; explain the nature and relevance of two sources chosen for detailed analysis; analyse their value and limitations with reference to their origins, purpose and content | 6 |
| 2. Investigation | Present a well-organised critical analysis focused on the question, using a range of evidence, with different perspectives where relevant, reaching a reasoned conclusion | 15 |
| 3. Reflection | Reflect on what the investigation highlighted about the methods used by historians, and the challenges and limitations of those methods | 4 |

A **bibliography** is required and references must be consistent. The
2,200-word limit covers the three sections; the bibliography is not
counted. A commonly used guideline is roughly 500 words for Section 1,
1,300 for Section 2 and 400 for Section 3, in proportion to the marks.

## How do you choose a good question?

A good question is narrow enough to answer in about 1,300 words of
argument and open enough to allow debate.

**Worked example: narrowing a topic**

```
Broad topic:   the Cuban Missile Crisis
Too broad:     "What caused the Cuban Missile Crisis?"
Better:        "To what extent was Khrushchev's decision to place
                missiles in Cuba in 1962 motivated by the defence of
                Castro's regime?"
Why better:    one decision, one actor, one year, a "to what extent"
               judgement, and sources (memoirs, Soviet documents,
               historians) that disagree.
```

Avoid questions that only produce narrative ("What happened at…?") and
questions so recent or obscure that sources are scarce.

## How do you evaluate the two Section 1 sources?

Choose two sources that are genuinely useful for the question and,
ideally, different in type or perspective, for example a primary
memoir and a secondary work by a historian. For each, explain
**origin** (who, when, where), **purpose** (why it was produced, for
whom) and **content**, then analyse **value** and **limitations** *for
your question*. A source is never valuable in general; it is valuable
for something.

## How do you write Section 2?

Section 2 carries 15 of the 25 marks. It should be an argument, not a
report. Open with your line of argument, organise paragraphs by
factor or theme, use a range of evidence (not only your two Section 1
sources), bring in different perspectives or historians'
interpretations and weigh them, and finish with a conclusion that
answers the question directly. The key concepts help: a "to what
extent" question about motives is a causation question, so rank the
motives.

## What goes in the reflection?

Section 3 asks what the investigation taught you about **historians'
methods**: for example, the problem of selecting evidence, of relying
on translated or incomplete sources, of reconciling contradictory
accounts, of bias and hindsight, or the differences between the
historian's work and that of a journalist or scientist. It should
refer to your own investigation, not discuss methods in the abstract.

## What does a strong Section 2 paragraph look like?

A strong paragraph opens with a claim that answers part of the
question, supports it with specific evidence from more than one
source, explains how strongly the evidence supports the claim, brings
in a different perspective where it matters, and closes by linking the
point to the overall judgement. For the Cuban example: "The defence of
Cuba was a genuine motive, but a secondary one. Khrushchev's memoirs
stress protecting Castro after the Bay of Pigs (April 1961), yet the
scale of the deployment, including intermediate-range missiles able to
reach most of the United States, suggests a wider aim of correcting the
strategic imbalance revealed when the US announced its missile
superiority in 1961." Each sentence does one job: claim, evidence,
analysis, link.

## Common mistakes

- A question that is too broad or purely descriptive.
- Section 1 that summarises the sources instead of evaluating them.
- Value and limitations not linked to the question.
- Section 2 that tells the story rather than arguing a case.
- A generic reflection that could have been written about any topic.
- Exceeding 2,200 words: material beyond the limit is not assessed.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many sources must be analysed in Section 1?**
Two, in detail, with reference to their origins, purpose and content.
Section 2 should use a wider range of evidence.

**Is there a reflection section in the current IA?**
Yes. In the course first assessed in 2017, Section 3, the reflection,
is worth 4 marks.

**Does the topic have to be on the syllabus?**
No. Any historical topic is allowed if you can find and evaluate
suitable sources.

**Who marks the investigation?**
Your teacher marks it against the IB criteria, and the IB moderates a
sample from each school.
