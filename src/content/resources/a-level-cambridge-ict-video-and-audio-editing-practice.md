## Questions

**1.** Describe the difference between trimming a video clip and splicing video clips. **[2]**

**2.** A college posts recorded lectures on its website. Explain why captions should be added to the videos. **[3]**

**3.** A film club's video ends with scrolling credits. The video uses two music tracks and several photographs found online. Describe how the credits should be set out. **[4]**

**4.** A square photograph, 2000 × 2000 pixels, is to be inserted into a video that is 1280 × 720 pixels (16:9).

**(a)** Explain why the photograph should not simply be resized to 1280 × 720. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the size the photograph should be cropped to, keeping its full width. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the scale factor needed to resize the cropped photograph, and show that it gives 1280 × 720. **[2]**

**5.** A 2-minute stereo recording is sampled at 20,000 Hz with a sampling resolution of 16 bits. Take 1 MB = 1,000,000 bytes.

**(a)** Calculate the uncompressed file size in MB. Show your working. **[3]**
**(b)** State the file size if the recording is changed to mono and 8 bits, all else unchanged. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why file size depends on the sampling rate and the sampling resolution. **[2]**

**6.** A podcast is made from two interview clips recorded on different days. One clip is much quieter than the other, one shows a DC offset, and both have a steady low hum. Describe how audio editing software would be used to prepare the clips. **[6]**

**7.**

**(a)** Explain the difference between delay/echo and reverberation. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain the difference between a high pass filter and a low pass filter. **[2]**

**8.** A school concert video is to be released on DVD, on the school website and in a version for mobile phones.

**(a)** Explain how the compression settings should differ for the three versions. **[3]**
**(b)** Describe how lossy compression affects the quality of the video. **[3]**

**9.**

**(a)** Explain the difference between saving audio as a file and saving it in a container. **[2]**
**(b)** An author sells an audiobook as a download. Compare WAV and MP4a as the format for the download. **[4]**

**10.** A 150-second clip of a science experiment must fit a 120-second slot in a video.

**(a)** Calculate the playback speed needed, as a percentage. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe what could happen to the clip's original sound and how the editor might deal with it. **[2]**

**11.** A local radio advert is made from a music track and a recorded voice.

**(a)** Explain why the finished advert might be changed from stereo to mono. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe how the voiceover is overdubbed onto the music so the words are clear. **[3]**

**12.** A charity is making a 60-second appeal video for social media from phone footage shot in different lighting, a set of still photographs and a narrator's recording. Evaluate the editing features and export choices the editor should use. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Trimming removes unwanted footage from the start and/or end of a single clip [1]. Splicing joins two or more clips together end to end to make one sequence [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Two separate points are needed: describe each term; "cutting" on its own does not separate them.

**2.** Captions show the speech and describe other sounds [1], so deaf or hard-of-hearing students can follow the lecture [1]. They also allow watching where sound cannot be played, such as a library [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Explain" needs reasons tied to the context; naming "accessibility" alone earns little.

**3.** Group the credits under headings such as cast, crew, music, images [1]. Put blank lines between the groups so each reads separately [1]. Start and end the list with blank lines so the text scrolls on and off the screen cleanly [1]. For each music track and photograph give its creator and source, such as the website it came from [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The question mentions borrowed music and photographs, so a description without source information misses the point.

**4. (a)** The photograph is 1:1 and the video is 16:9, so resizing to 1280 × 720 would stretch it and distort the picture [1].
**(b)** Height = 2000 × 9 ÷ 16 [1] = 1125, so crop to **2000 × 1125** [1].
**(c)** Scale factor = 1280 ÷ 2000 = **0.64** [1]; 2000 × 0.64 = 1280 and 1125 × 0.64 = 720, giving **1280 × 720** [1].
*Examiner insight:* In (c), show both multiplications; stating 1280 × 720 without working does not show that one factor was used both ways.

**5. (a)** 2 minutes = 120 s [1]. 20,000 × 16 × 2 × 120 = 76,800,000 bits [1]. ÷ 8 = 9,600,000 bytes = **9.6 MB** [1].
**(b)** Mono halves it and 8 bits halves it again: **2.4 MB** [1].
**(c)** The sampling rate is the number of samples taken each second and the sampling resolution is the number of bits per sample [1]. More samples, or more bits for each one, means more data must be stored for every second of sound, so the file is larger [1].
*Examiner insight:* Without a calculator, write each stage of the working so any slip is easy to spot and the method stays visible.

**6.** Import both clips as tracks [1]. Remove the DC offset to centre the waveform on zero [1] before normalising, so the level is set correctly [1]. Normalise both clips to the same peak level so the quiet clip matches the louder one [1]. Use noise reduction, learning the hum from a section with no speech, or a high pass filter, to remove the low hum [1]. Trim silences and splice the clips into one, adding a fade in and fade out [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Order matters here: saying DC offset is removed before normalising, with a reason, shows understanding rather than a list of features.

**7. (a)** Delay/echo produces distinct repeats of the sound after a set time, each quieter [1]. Reverberation blends many close reflections so the sound seems to be in a room or hall, without separate repeats [1].
**(b)** A high pass filter lets frequencies above its cut-off through and removes lower ones such as rumble [1]. A low pass filter lets frequencies below its cut-off through and removes higher ones such as hiss [1].
*Examiner insight:* Describe what each filter lets through; the names are easily reversed in a hurry.

**8. (a)** DVD: standard-definition resolution in a format DVD players accept [1]. Website: a resolution and bit rate that stream smoothly [1]. Mobile: lower resolution and a smaller file for the small screen, saving storage and data [1].
**(b)** Lossy compression permanently removes detail to make the file smaller [1]. Stronger compression or a lower bit rate makes artefacts visible, such as blockiness, blurring or colour banding [1], especially in fast movement such as dancers or a moving camera [1].
*Examiner insight:* In (b), describe what the viewer would actually see; "worse quality" alone is too vague to credit fully.

**9. (a)** A plain file holds one audio stream encoded one way [1]. A container is a wrapper holding one or more streams (such as audio and video) plus information about them; the codec decides the compression [1].
**(b)** WAV is usually uncompressed, so it keeps full quality [1], but the files are very large, slow to download and use a lot of storage [1]. MP4a usually holds AAC, so it is much smaller for good speech quality [1], although a few older players may not support it [1]. *Conclusion:* MP4a suits a download.
*Examiner insight:* "Compare" needs points about both formats; a list of WAV features alone does not compare.

**10. (a)** Speed = 150 ÷ 120 × 100 [1] = **125%** [1].
**(b)** Played faster, the original sound may become faster and higher in pitch, which sounds unnatural [1]. The editor could remove that audio and add music or a narration instead [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show the division in (a); writing 125% with no method gives no evidence if the value is wrong.

**11. (a)** Many radio listeners use a single speaker, and speech gains little from stereo [1]. Mono stores one channel instead of two, so the file is about half the size [1].
**(b)** Record or import the voice as a separate track above the music track [1]. Line it up with the music on the timeline [1]. Lower the music volume while the voice speaks, and raise it in gaps, so the words are clear [1].
*Examiner insight:* For (a), name the effect on size and give a reason linked to radio; "it sounds better" is not credited.

**12.** Indicative points:
- Trim each clip to the strongest moments so the appeal fits 60 seconds and holds attention on social media [1].
- Apply colour correction so phone shots taken in different light match [1].
- Crop and resize each photograph to the video's aspect ratio so none is stretched [1], and add slow pan and zoom so the stills do not look static [1].
- Add the narration as an overdubbed voiceover, lowering any music under it [1].
- Add captions, because many people watch social media with the sound off [1].
- Use simple transitions and a fade at the end; too many effects distract from the message [1].
- End with a text-based slide giving how to donate, in large, high-contrast text [1].
- Export as MP4, which most social media platforms and phones play, with lossy compression keeping the file small for quick loading [1].
- Conclusion: keeping the clips short, captioned and in MP4 balances visual quality against small file size for viewers on mobile data [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* "Evaluate" needs reasons and a judgement; a list of features with no link to social media or the charity's aim reads as description only.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing "captions" when the question wants a reason for a specific audience, or confusing captions with subtitles.
- Leaving out sources in credits when the scenario mentions borrowed music or images.
- Resizing a still image straight to the video size and ignoring the change of shape.
- Dropping the ÷ 8 or the factor of 2 for stereo in a file-size calculation.
- Writing "normalising removes noise" or "noise reduction makes it louder".
- Listing editing features in a long answer without linking any of them to the audience or medium.
