## Questions

**1.** Define the term *hybrid identity* and give **one** example. **[4]**

**2.** Explain **two** ways in which gender identity is constructed through socialisation. **[6]**

**3.** Explain **two** reasons why age identities may be changing in modern societies. **[6]**

**4.** *"Globalisation is making the world's cultural identities the same."*

- **(a)** Explain the view expressed in the quotation. **[6]**
- **(b)** Using sociological material, give **two** arguments against the view expressed in the
  quotation. **[10]**

**5.** "Social class is no longer the most important influence on a person's identity." Evaluate
this view. **[26]**

## Answers and mark guidance

### 1. Hybrid identity [4]

- **Definition (2 marks):** an identity that combines cultural elements from two or more sources,
  for example the culture of a family's country of origin and the culture of the society they now
  live in. Award 1 mark for a partial definition such as "a mixed identity".
- **Example (2 marks):** a second-generation young person of South Asian heritage in Britain who
  mixes Bhangra and hip hop, speaks two languages and celebrates both Eid and national holidays;
  or a young person in Singapore combining global consumer culture with local Malay or Chinese
  traditions. Award 1 mark for a bare example, 2 for one explained.

### 2. Gender socialisation [6]

Up to 3 marks for each explanation: 1 for identifying the process, up to 2 for developing it.

- **Manipulation and canalisation in the family** (Oakley, 1972): parents encourage
  gender-appropriate behaviour and channel children towards gendered toys and activities, so
  children learn to see themselves as "boys" or "girls" in a particular way.
- **Media representations:** advertising, film and social media present idealised masculinities
  and femininities that young people copy and measure themselves against.
- **Education:** the hidden curriculum, subject choices and teacher expectations may reinforce
  gender roles.
- **Peer group:** teasing and policing of "unmanly" or "unladylike" behaviour, linked to Connell's
  hegemonic masculinity (1987).

### 3. Changing age identities [6]

Up to 3 marks per reason.

- **Rising life expectancy:** a longer, healthier old age creates new identities for older people
  (active retirement, grandparenting, later-life consumers) rather than simple dependency.
- **Extended youth:** longer education and difficulty buying homes delay traditional markers of
  adulthood, so a longer stage between childhood and adulthood emerges.
- **Consumer culture and media:** markets target specific age groups (children, teens, older
  consumers), giving each a distinct lifestyle identity.
- **Digital technology:** generational differences in internet use create identities such as
  "digital natives".

### 4(a). Explaining the view [6]

Credit explanation of the **cultural convergence / homogenisation** argument:

- Global media, brands and the internet spread the same products and values worldwide; George
  Ritzer's *The McDonaldization of Society* (1993) describes the spread of standardised Western
  practices.
- Western ideology, especially consumerism and individualism, is exported through film, music and
  advertising (Westernisation).
- Young people in different countries increasingly share fashions, music and online platforms, so
  local identities weaken.

Level guidance: 1–2 marks for a simple point; 3–4 for a developed explanation; 5–6 for two or more
developed points clearly linked to identity.

### 4(b). Arguing against the view [10]

Up to 5 marks per argument; the strongest answers use evidence and evaluation.

- **Cultural defence and ethnic revitalisation:** globalisation can provoke people to strengthen
  local, national or religious identities in response to perceived threat. Steve Bruce describes
  religion acting as cultural defence (*Religion in the Modern World*, 1996).
- **Glocalisation:** Roland Robertson (1992) argued that global products are adapted to local
  culture, so outcomes are mixed rather than uniform; global firms change menus and advertising
  for each market.
- **Hybridity:** Bhabha (1994) and Hall describe new identities that are neither wholly global nor
  wholly local, which is diversity, not sameness.
- **Unequal access:** many people, especially the rural poor, have limited access to global media,
  so convergence is uneven.

### 5. Essay: is class still the most important influence? [26]

**Indicative content (AO1):**
- *For the view:* postmodernists such as Pakulski and Waters (1996) argue that consumption and
  lifestyle have replaced class; decline of manual industry has broken up working-class
  communities; gender, ethnicity, sexuality and religion are increasingly emphasised; globalisation
  supplies new identities.
- *Against the view:* class still shapes life chances in education, health and income; Bourdieu's
  capitals show class reproduced through taste and education; the Great British Class Survey
  (Savage et al., 2013) found distinct classes; Marxists argue that class remains objective even
  where class consciousness is weak.

**Application (AO2):** use examples from your own society, for example how school type, housing or
access to the internet varies by class; apply concepts such as false consciousness, consumer
identity and intersectionality.

**Analysis and evaluation (AO3):**
- Distinguish **objective** class (position) from **subjective** class identity (awareness).
- Consider **intersectionality**: class interacts with gender and ethnicity rather than competing
  with them.
- Reach a judgement, for example that class may matter less to how people *describe* themselves
  but still matters greatly to their *opportunities*.

**Level guidance:** top-band answers (around 20–26) show wide, accurate knowledge, apply it to the
question throughout, and sustain evaluation leading to a reasoned conclusion. Middle-band answers
describe theories with limited evaluation. Low-band answers make a few general points about
identity without sociological support.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long should a 26-mark essay be?** Aim for a structured response with an introduction, several
developed points on each side and a conclusion; quality of evaluation matters more than length.

**Can I use examples from my own country?** Yes. Cambridge encourages relevant local examples, and
applying concepts to them earns AO2 credit.

**What does "argue against" mean in 4(b)?** Only arguments against the quotation are credited; do
not spend time supporting it.
