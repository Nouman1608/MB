This guide covers subtopic **26.1, Simple rate equations, orders of reaction and rate
constants**, from Topic 26, Reaction kinetics, of Cambridge International AS & A Level
Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is A Level content. The broader
[Reaction Kinetics: Rate Equations and Catalysis guide](/resources/a-reaction-kinetics-rate-equations-and-catalysis/)
introduces rate equations and a pre-equilibrium mechanism; this page works through a full
iodination data set, the half-life method from a concentration–time table, a slow-first-step
mechanism, and the reasoning examiners expect at each step.

## What the syllabus asks for

The learning outcomes for 26.1 require you to:

- explain and use the terms **rate equation, order of reaction, overall order, rate constant,
  half-life, rate-determining step** and **intermediate**;
- use rate equations rate = k[A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ with m and n equal to **0, 1 or 2**;
- deduce orders from **concentration–time graphs**, **initial rates** and **half-lives**, and
  interpret concentration–time and rate–concentration graphs;
- **calculate an initial rate** from concentration data and **construct a rate equation**;
- show that the half-life of a **first-order** reaction is independent of concentration, and
  use it in calculations;
- calculate k from initial rates or from **k = 0.693 ÷ t½**;
- for multi-step reactions, link mechanisms, rate-determining steps, intermediates, catalysts
  and rate equations in both directions;
- describe **qualitatively** how temperature affects the rate constant and so the rate.

## The vocabulary, precisely

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Rate equation | rate = k[A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ, found by experiment |
| Order with respect to a reactant | the power of that reactant's concentration in the rate equation |
| Overall order | sum of the individual orders, m + n |
| Rate constant, k | the constant of proportionality in the rate equation; fixed at a given temperature |
| Half-life, t½ | time for the concentration of a reactant to fall to half its value |
| Rate-determining step | the slowest step in a mechanism, which controls the overall rate |
| Intermediate | a species formed in one step and used up in a later step; not in the overall equation |

Orders come **only** from experiment. The coefficients in the balanced equation do not give
them.

## Reading graphs

**Concentration–time graphs** (reactant concentration against time):

- **zero order**: a straight line sloping down; the rate (gradient) is constant;
- **first order**: a curve with **constant successive half-lives**;
- **second order**: a curve that is steep at first and levels off more gradually, with
  successive half-lives that **get longer**.

**Rate–concentration graphs** (rate against reactant concentration):

- zero order: horizontal line;
- first order: straight line through the origin;
- second order: upward curve through the origin.

To get a rate from a concentration–time graph, draw a **tangent** at the chosen time and find
its gradient. The **initial rate** is the gradient of the tangent at t = 0.

## Worked example 1: the initial-rates method

The acid-catalysed reaction of propanone with iodine:
CH₃COCH₃ + I₂ → CH₃COCH₂I + HI (catalysed by H⁺). *Invented data:*

| Exp. | [CH₃COCH₃] / mol dm⁻³ | [I₂] / mol dm⁻³ | [H⁺] / mol dm⁻³ | Initial rate / mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.400 | 0.00200 | 0.100 | 2.20 × 10⁻⁶ |
| 2 | 0.800 | 0.00200 | 0.100 | 4.40 × 10⁻⁶ |
| 3 | 0.400 | 0.00400 | 0.100 | 2.20 × 10⁻⁶ |
| 4 | 0.400 | 0.00200 | 0.200 | 4.40 × 10⁻⁶ |

**Orders.** Exp. 1 → 2: [propanone] doubles, rate doubles: **first order** in propanone.
Exp. 1 → 3: [I₂] doubles, rate unchanged: **zero order** in iodine. Exp. 1 → 4: [H⁺] doubles,
rate doubles: **first order** in H⁺.

**Rate equation:** rate = k[CH₃COCH₃][H⁺]; overall order 2.

**Rate constant** from Exp. 1: k = 2.20 × 10⁻⁶ ÷ (0.400 × 0.100) =
**5.50 × 10⁻⁵ dm³ mol⁻¹ s⁻¹**

**Calculating an initial rate** for new concentrations, [CH₃COCH₃] = 0.200 and
[H⁺] = 0.300 mol dm⁻³: rate = 5.50 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.200 × 0.300 = **3.30 × 10⁻⁶ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹**.
[I₂] is not needed because it does not appear in the rate equation.

**What the rate equation says about the mechanism.** Iodine is zero order, so it is **not
involved** in the rate-determining step or any step before it; it reacts in a fast step
afterwards. H⁺ appears in the rate equation but not in the overall equation, so it is a
**catalyst** that takes part in or before the slow step.

## Units of k

Rearrange the rate equation and substitute units (rate in mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹, concentration in
mol dm⁻³):

| Overall order | Units of k |
|---|---|
| 0 | mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ |
| 1 | s⁻¹ |
| 2 | dm³ mol⁻¹ s⁻¹ |
| 3 | dm⁶ mol⁻² s⁻¹ |

## Worked example 2: the half-life method

*Invented data* for the decomposition of a compound X:

| t / s | 0 | 340 | 680 | 1020 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [X] / mol dm⁻³ | 0.0800 | 0.0400 | 0.0200 | 0.0100 |

Each halving takes 340 s regardless of the starting concentration, so the reaction is **first
order** in X.

k = 0.693 ÷ t½ = 0.693 ÷ 340 = **2.04 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹**

Rate when [X] = 0.0500 mol dm⁻³: rate = k[X] = 2.04 × 10⁻³ × 0.0500 =
**1.02 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹**

Time to fall to one-eighth of the starting concentration: three half-lives = **1020 s**.

The half-life is independent of concentration only for first-order reactions. That is why a
constant half-life is the test for first order.

## Worked example 3: from mechanism to rate equation

For NO₂(g) + CO(g) → NO(g) + CO₂(g) at low temperature, a two-step mechanism is:

Step 1 (slow): NO₂ + NO₂ → NO₃ + NO

Step 2 (fast): NO₃ + CO → NO₂ + CO₂

- **Rate equation:** the slow step involves two NO₂ molecules, so rate = k[NO₂]². CO is
  zero order because it reacts only after the slow step.
- **Intermediate:** NO₃, formed in step 1 and used in step 2.
- **Check:** adding the steps and cancelling NO₃ and one NO₂ gives the overall equation.

When working the other way, from a rate equation to a mechanism, the slow step must contain
the species in the rate equation in the same numbers as their orders (or be preceded by fast
steps that supply them), and the steps must add up to the overall equation.

## Temperature and the rate constant

The rate constant **increases** as temperature increases. At a higher temperature a greater
proportion of molecules have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy, so a
greater proportion of collisions succeed. Because rate = k[A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ, the rate increases at the
same concentrations. Temperature changes **k**, not the orders. A rate equation should
therefore always be quoted with its temperature.

## Common mistakes

- Taking orders from the balanced equation.
- Comparing two experiments in which more than one concentration changes, without allowing
  for both.
- Giving k without units, or with units for the wrong overall order.
- Using k = 0.693 ÷ t½ for a reaction that is not first order.
- Saying a catalyst cannot appear in a rate equation; H⁺ in Worked example 1 does.
- Saying temperature changes the order of reaction.

## Exam technique

- **Deduce** orders: quote the pair of experiments, say what changes and by what factor, and
  what happens to the rate.
- **Construct** the rate equation using only species with non-zero order.
- **Calculate** k: rearrange first, substitute one experiment, and give units.
- **Explain** a half-life result: "the half-life is constant, so the reaction is first order".
- **Suggest** a mechanism: slow step consistent with the rate equation, steps that add up to
  the overall equation, intermediates labelled.
