## Renewable is a property of use, not only of the resource

A resource is renewable only if it is used no faster than it regenerates. A forest felled faster than it grows is being treated as non-renewable, even though trees regrow. Groundwater shows the same idea: an aquifer recharged by rain each year can supply water indefinitely if abstraction stays below recharge, but some deep aquifers filled thousands of years ago and receive almost no recharge today. Pumping them is like mining.

So in an answer, do not stop at "fish are renewable". Say: "fish stocks are renewable natural capital as long as the catch does not exceed the stock's natural increase."

## Ecosystem services applied: a mangrove coast

Learn the four categories through one example and you can transfer them to any ecosystem in a Paper 1 case study.

| Category | Mangrove example | Who benefits |
|---|---|---|
| Provisioning | Timber, fuelwood, fish and shellfish that breed among the roots | Local households, fishers |
| Regulating | Roots slow waves and storm surges; sediments store carbon; water is filtered | Coastal towns, the global climate |
| Supporting | Nursery habitat for young fish; nutrient cycling; soil building | Offshore fisheries, biodiversity |
| Cultural | Tourism, recreation, education, places of local significance | Visitors, communities |

Notice the conflict: clearing mangroves for shrimp ponds turns some natural capital into a short-term provisioning income while losing the regulating and supporting services that protected the coast and fed the fishery. Good evaluation names the services lost, not just the trees.

## Where maximum sustainable yield comes from

A renewable population usually grows logistically: slowly when small, fastest at intermediate size, and slowly again as it nears the carrying capacity (K). One simple model for the yearly increase is:

```
Increase = r × N × (1 − N ÷ K)
```

Here N is the stock and r is the population's intrinsic growth rate. The sustainable yield at any stock size is this increase. It is largest when N is half of K, and that largest value is the maximum sustainable yield (MSY = r × K ÷ 4).

### Worked example 1: a fishery

A fictional fish stock has K = 200,000 t and r = 0.3 per year.

```
MSY = 0.3 × 200,000 ÷ 4 = 15,000 t per year, at N = 100,000 t

Increase at N = 60,000 t:  0.3 × 60,000 × (1 − 0.3) = 12,600 t
Increase at N = 160,000 t: 0.3 × 160,000 × (1 − 0.8) =  9,600 t
```

Now suppose managers keep the quota at 15,000 t, but the stock has fallen to 60,000 t:

```
Change in stock = 12,600 − 15,000 = −2,400 t in the first year
```

Each year the stock is smaller, so its increase is smaller, so the gap widens. A quota that was sustainable at 100,000 t drives collapse at 60,000 t. This is why setting catches at MSY is risky: stock estimates are uncertain, and a mistake on the low side produces positive feedback towards collapse. Many managers now set catches below MSY as a safety margin.

### Case study: the northern cod

The cod fishery off Newfoundland supported fishing communities for centuries. Industrial trawlers greatly increased catches in the twentieth century, and stock assessments overestimated what remained. The stock collapsed, and in 1992 the Canadian government closed the fishery with a moratorium. Recovery has been slow and incomplete. The case shows three points examiners look for: uncertain data, pressure to keep quotas high to protect jobs, and the time lag before a depleted stock can rebuild.

## Non-renewable resources: reserves are not fixed

For non-renewable natural capital, distinguish the **resource** (all of the material that exists) from the **reserve** (the part that is known and can be extracted economically with current technology). The reserves-to-production ratio estimates how long reserves would last at today's rate of use.

### Worked example 2: reserves-to-production ratio

A fictional country has copper reserves of 4,800 Mt and extracts 160 Mt a year.

```
R/P ratio = 4,800 ÷ 160 = 30 years
```

A new extraction method makes lower-grade ore worth mining and adds 1,600 Mt to reserves:

```
New R/P ratio = 6,400 ÷ 160 = 40 years
```

The ratio is a snapshot, not a forecast. Reserves rise with discoveries, better technology and higher prices; they shrink if prices fall. Use also changes: if extraction grows each year, the reserves run out sooner than the ratio suggests. Recycling extends the life of metal reserves because the metal stays in use.

## Valuing natural capital

Natural capital has value in several ways.

- **Use values:** direct (timber sold, fish eaten), indirect (flood protection, pollination) and option values (keeping a resource for possible future use, such as an undiscovered medicine).
- **Non-use values:** existence value (knowing a species survives) and bequest value (leaving it for future generations).

Markets price direct use values well and the others poorly or not at all, which is one reason regulating services are lost. Value systems also differ. A technocentric view tends to value natural capital for what it provides to people and expects substitutes to be found; an ecocentric view holds that ecosystems also have **intrinsic value** whether or not people use them. Bring this contrast into any "discuss" question on resource management.

## Managing common-pool resources

In 1968 Garrett Hardin's essay "The Tragedy of the Commons" argued that shared, open-access resources will be overused unless they are privatised or regulated by the state. Elinor Ostrom, who received the 2009 Nobel prize in economics, studied fisheries, forests and irrigation systems around the world and found many communities that had managed commons sustainably for generations. Her design principles include:

- clearly defined boundaries for the resource and its users
- rules suited to local conditions
- users taking part in making and changing the rules
- monitoring by people accountable to the users
- graduated sanctions, so penalties grow with repeated breaking of rules
- cheap, local ways to resolve conflicts
- recognition of the community's right to organise by higher authorities

Use her work to qualify Hardin: tragedy is likely under **open access**, not under every kind of shared ownership.

### Tools for a fishery, compared

| Tool | How it works | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Total allowable catch | Annual cap on the whole catch | Fails if the stock estimate is wrong, or if discarding hides the true catch |
| Individual transferable quotas | Each holder owns a share of the cap and can trade it | Shares can concentrate in large firms, hurting small fishers |
| No-take marine reserves | Areas closed to fishing let stocks rebuild and spill over | Needs enforcement; fishers lose grounds in the short term |
| Gear and size limits | Mesh sizes and minimum landing sizes protect young fish | Hard to police at sea |
| Community management | Local users set and enforce rules | Works best where users and boundaries are clear |

## Common mistakes

- Defining sustainable yield as "the amount harvested". It is the natural increase, which the harvest should not exceed.
- Assuming MSY is safe at any stock size. It applies only at about K ÷ 2.
- Treating the reserves-to-production ratio as the year a resource "runs out".
- Saying the tragedy of the commons is inevitable for all shared resources.
- Listing ecosystem services without classifying them or naming who benefits.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate** sustainable yield or a stock change: write the formula first and keep units (t per year).
- **Explain** why a harvest is unsustainable: link harvest, increase and stock size in a loop.
- **Evaluate** a management tool: effectiveness, cost, enforcement, who gains and who loses, then a judgement tied to the case.
- **Discuss** valuation: contrast use and non-use values and at least two value systems.
