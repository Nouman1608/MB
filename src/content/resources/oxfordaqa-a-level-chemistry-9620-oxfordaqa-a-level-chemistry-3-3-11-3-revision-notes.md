These notes condense **3.3.11.3 Nucleophilic properties** of amines from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International A2 content, examined in Unit 4 and able to reappear in Unit 5. For explanations and worked examples read the [amine nucleophilic properties study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-11-3/); then try the [amine nucleophilic properties practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-11-3-practice/).

## The subtopic in five lines

1. The lone pair on N makes ammonia and amines nucleophiles.
2. With halogenoalkanes: nucleophilic substitution, repeated to give primary, secondary, tertiary amines and a quaternary ammonium salt.
3. Quaternary ammonium salts with a long chain are cationic surfactants.
4. With acyl chlorides and acid anhydrides: nucleophilic addition–elimination, giving amides.
5. Mechanisms needed: the substitution reactions, and addition–elimination with acyl chlorides.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning to learn |
|---|---|
| Nucleophile | Electron pair donor that attacks an electron-deficient carbon |
| Nucleophilic substitution | Nucleophile replaces a halogen on a carbon atom |
| Nucleophilic addition–elimination | Nucleophile adds to C=O; C=O re-forms and Cl⁻ or RCOO⁻ leaves |
| Secondary amine | R₂NH: N bonded to two carbons |
| Tertiary amine | R₃N: N bonded to three carbons |
| Quaternary ammonium salt | R₄N⁺X⁻: N bonded to four carbons, no lone pair |
| Cationic surfactant | Surfactant whose active part is a positive ion, such as a long-chain R₄N⁺ |
| N-substituted amide | RCONHR′, made from a primary amine |

## Substitution sequence (ammonia + RX)

| Product | General formula | Example with bromoethane |
|---|---|---|
| Primary amine | RNH₂ | CH₃CH₂NH₂ |
| Secondary amine | R₂NH | (CH₃CH₂)₂NH |
| Tertiary amine | R₃N | (CH₃CH₂)₃N |
| Quaternary salt | R₄N⁺X⁻ | (CH₃CH₂)₄N⁺Br⁻ |

- Excess NH₃ → mainly primary amine.
- Excess RX → mainly quaternary salt.
- The sequence stops at R₄N⁺: no lone pair left.

## Mechanism checklist: substitution

1. Lone pair on N → δ+ carbon of C–X.
2. C–X bond pair → X; X⁻ leaves.
3. Intermediate with N⁺ (for example R₂N⁺H₂).
4. Second amine molecule removes H⁺ from N⁺ (not needed when a quaternary salt forms).

## Mechanism checklist: addition–elimination (acyl chloride)

1. Lone pair on N → δ+ carbonyl carbon.
2. C=O π bond pair → O (O⁻ forms).
3. Tetrahedral intermediate with O⁻ and N⁺.
4. O⁻ lone pair re-forms C=O; C–Cl bond pair → Cl; Cl⁻ leaves.
5. Second NH₃ or amine removes H⁺ from N⁺.

## Amide-forming equations

| Reactants | Products |
|---|---|
| CH₃CH₂COCl + 2NH₃ | CH₃CH₂CONH₂ (propanamide) + NH₄Cl |
| CH₃COCl + 2CH₃CH₂NH₂ | CH₃CONHCH₂CH₃ (N-ethylethanamide) + CH₃CH₂NH₃⁺Cl⁻ |
| (CH₃CO)₂O + 2NH₃ | CH₃CONH₂ (ethanamide) + CH₃COO⁻NH₄⁺ |
| (CH₃CO)₂O + CH₃NH₂ | CH₃CONHCH₃ (N-methylethanamide) + CH₃COOH |

## Cationic surfactants

- Structure: one long hydrocarbon chain plus a positively charged N⁺ head, for example CH₃(CH₂)₁₅N⁺(CH₃)₃ Br⁻.
- Made from: a tertiary amine and a long-chain halogenoalkane.
- Head: ionic, attracted to water and to negatively charged surfaces.
- Tail: non-polar, mixes with oil and grease.
- Uses: fabric softeners, hair conditioners.
- Short-chain salts such as (CH₃)₄N⁺I⁻ are not surfactants.

## Working backwards to reagents

1. Find the bond to N that formed last.
2. For an amine or quaternary salt, the group on that bond came from a halogenoalkane; the rest of the molecule is the nucleophile.
3. For an amide, split at C(=O)–N: the C=O side came from an acyl chloride or anhydride, the N side from ammonia or a primary amine.
4. Example: (CH₃)₂NCH₂CH₂CH₃ from dimethylamine + 1-bromopropane, or from N-methylpropylamine + bromomethane.
5. Example: CH₃CH₂CONHCH₃ from propanoyl chloride + two methylamine molecules.
6. A mixture warning: starting with a primary amine and excess halogenoalkane cannot stop cleanly at a tertiary amine.

## Which mechanism?

| Clue | Mechanism | Product type |
|---|---|---|
| Halogenoalkane (C–Br, C–Cl, C–I on a chain) | Substitution | Amine or quaternary salt |
| –COCl | Addition–elimination | Amide + ammonium chloride |
| –CO–O–CO– | Addition–elimination (no mechanism needed) | Amide + carboxylic acid |

## Exam traps

- One molecule of amine in substitution or acylation equations: two are needed (except for the final quaternary step).
- An H or a lone pair drawn on the N of a quaternary salt.
- "Cationic because of the halide ion": the cation is the R₄N⁺ ion.
- Addition–elimination called substitution.
- Intermediate without O⁻ and N⁺.
- HCl as the anhydride by-product.
- Missing N- in names such as N-ethylethanamide.

## Self-test

Answer without looking, then check below.

1. What feature of ammonia makes it a nucleophile?
2. Give the formula of the secondary amine formed from ethylamine and bromoethane.
3. Why does ethylamine react further with bromoethane?
4. Give the formula of the quaternary salt formed from triethylamine and bromoethane.
5. Why does the quaternary salt not react with more bromoethane?
6. What conditions favour formation of the primary amine?
7. Name the two reagents that form hexadecyltrimethylammonium bromide.
8. Why is this salt described as a cationic surfactant?
9. Give one use of cationic surfactants.
10. Write an equation for ethanoyl chloride reacting with excess ammonia.
11. Name the product when propylamine reacts with ethanoyl chloride.
12. What by-product forms when an acid anhydride reacts with a primary amine in a 1 : 1 ratio?

### Answers

1. The lone pair of electrons on the nitrogen atom.
2. (CH₃CH₂)₂NH.
3. Ethylamine still has a lone pair on N, so it is also a nucleophile.
4. (CH₃CH₂)₄N⁺Br⁻.
5. Its nitrogen has no lone pair left to attack another molecule.
6. A large excess of ammonia.
7. Trimethylamine and 1-bromohexadecane.
8. It has a long non-polar hydrocarbon tail and a positively charged ionic N⁺ head.
9. Fabric softeners (or hair conditioners).
10. CH₃COCl + 2NH₃ → CH₃CONH₂ + NH₄Cl.
11. N-Propylethanamide, CH₃CONHCH₂CH₂CH₃.
12. A carboxylic acid.
