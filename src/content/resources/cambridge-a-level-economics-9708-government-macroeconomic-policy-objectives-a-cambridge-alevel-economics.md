This guide covers **section 10.1 Government macroeconomic policy objectives** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Economics 9708 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. It is A Level content. The syllabus statement is short: "objectives in terms of inflation, balance of payments, unemployment, growth, development, sustainability and redistribution of income and wealth". At AS (section 5.1) only three objectives appear, price stability, low unemployment and economic growth, and policy conflicts are not required. At A Level the list grows to seven, and the conflicts between them follow in sections 10.2 and 10.3.

The broader [macroeconomic objectives and Phillips curve study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-macroeconomic-objectives-and-their-interrelationships/) gives a summary table of the objectives. This page goes further into what a sensible target looks like for each, why the target is rarely zero or "as much as possible", and how to read a data set to decide which objective is most pressing. All countries and figures are invented.

## The seven objectives in depth

### 1. Inflation: low and stable, not zero

The aim is a **low, stable and predictable** rate of increase in the general price level. Many central banks express this as a numerical target for a consumer price index, often a low positive rate.

Why not zero? Three reasons to explain:

- **Deflation risk.** If prices fall, consumers may delay purchases expecting lower prices, and the real value of debts rises, which can deepen a downturn.
- **Measurement.** Price indices may overstate inflation slightly, for example because quality improvements are not fully captured, so measured zero may mean true prices are falling.
- **Flexibility.** A little inflation lets real wages adjust in declining industries without cuts in money wages.

Stability matters as much as the level: unpredictable inflation makes long-term contracts and investment decisions harder.

### 2. Balance of payments: stability of the current account

The aim is to avoid current account deficits or surpluses that are large relative to GDP and persistent. A deficit that is small, temporary or used to import capital goods is not a problem; a large deficit financed by short-term borrowing is. At A Level, remember that the full balance of payments (section 11.1) also includes the financial and capital accounts, which record how a current account imbalance is matched.

### 3. Unemployment: low, not zero

Some unemployment always exists as people move between jobs (frictional unemployment) or as the structure of the economy changes. The realistic goal is to keep unemployment close to its natural rate, with little cyclical unemployment. Long-term unemployment deserves particular attention, because skills and motivation decline the longer people are out of work.

### 4. Economic growth: sustained, close to potential

The aim is a steady rise in **real** GDP, ideally in line with the growth of productive capacity. Growth that is too fast for capacity brings inflation and import surges; growth that is too slow wastes resources. Growth per head of population matters for living standards.

### 5. Development: beyond income

Development means improvements in living standards in a wider sense: health, education, life expectancy, freedom of choice and reduced poverty. Rising GDP can support development, but it does not guarantee it if the gains are unevenly shared or are not spent on health and education. Composite measures that combine income, health and education are covered in section 11.3.

### 6. Sustainability: growth that can continue

Sustainability means meeting present needs without reducing the ability of future generations to meet theirs. In macroeconomic terms it means growth that does not deplete natural resources faster than they can be replaced or create environmental damage that lowers future output and welfare.

### 7. Redistribution of income and wealth

The aim is a fairer distribution of income (a flow) and wealth (a stock). What counts as "fair" is a normative judgement, so governments differ in how far they pursue it. Progress can be judged with the Gini coefficient and measures of poverty.

## Worked example 1: diagnosing priorities from data

Country P, latest year:

| Indicator | Value | Government target or benchmark |
|---|---|---|
| Inflation (consumer prices) | 7.5% | 3% |
| Unemployment | 4.0% | Natural rate estimated at 5% |
| Real GDP growth | 5.2% | Estimated growth of capacity: 3% |
| Population growth | 2.5% | — |
| Current account balance | −6% of GDP | Stability |
| Gini coefficient | 0.48 | Rising over five years |

**Step 1: growth per head.** Real GDP per head grew by about 1.052 ÷ 1.025 − 1 = **2.6%**, much less than the headline 5.2%.

**Step 2: read the pattern.** Growth is above the growth of capacity, unemployment is below its natural rate, inflation is more than double the target and the current account deficit is large. Together these point to an **overheating** economy in which AD is growing faster than capacity.

**Step 3: rank the objectives.** Price stability and current account stability are the most pressing, because both are far from target and both are symptoms of excess demand. Low unemployment is already met. Inequality is rising, which suggests the gains from growth are unevenly shared, a development and redistribution concern that a demand-reducing policy alone will not fix.

**Step 4: judge.** The ranking depends on the time horizon: in the short run, reducing excess demand is the priority; in the long run, raising capacity and addressing inequality matter more.

## Worked example 2: is growth sustainable?

Country Q's real GDP grows by 4% in a year. Carbon emissions per unit of GDP fall by 3%.

- Total emissions change by 1.04 × 0.97 − 1 = **+0.88%**.

Emissions still rise, though far more slowly than output. To reduce total emissions while growing at 4%, emissions per unit of GDP would need to fall by more than 1 − 1 ÷ 1.04 = **3.85%** a year. This shows how growth and sustainability can be judged together with simple data.

## Why objectives differ between countries

- **Level of development.** A low-income country may give growth and development priority over a small current account deficit.
- **Starting point.** A country with very high inflation will rank price stability first; one in recession will rank unemployment and growth first.
- **Values.** Governments weigh redistribution and sustainability differently, because these involve normative choices.
- **Time horizon.** Short-run stabilisation and long-run growth and sustainability can call for different priorities.

## Annotated model answer

*Question:* Explain why a government may aim for low inflation rather than zero inflation.

> Zero measured inflation risks tipping the economy into deflation if a shock reduces demand. **[Risk.]** In deflation, households may postpone spending because they expect lower prices, and the real burden of existing debts rises, both of which reduce aggregate demand and output. **[Mechanism.]** Price indices may also overstate inflation because quality improvements are not fully measured, so a measured rate of zero could hide falling prices. **[Measurement.]** A low positive target therefore gives a safety margin while keeping inflation predictable, which supports investment and planning. **[Conclusion.]**

## Common mistakes

- Writing that the aim is zero inflation or zero unemployment.
- Treating growth in nominal GDP as growth.
- Ignoring population growth when discussing living standards.
- Treating development as the same as growth.
- Presenting redistribution as a positive matter; it rests on value judgements.
- Listing all seven objectives without saying which matters most in the context given.

## Exam technique for 9708 command words

- **Explain:** for any objective, give the target, the reason and the measure.
- **Consider** and **Assess:** use data to identify which objectives are furthest from target, then rank them with a reason.
- **Evaluate:** in unstructured Paper 4 essays, show that priorities depend on context, time horizon and values, and reach a clear judgement.
