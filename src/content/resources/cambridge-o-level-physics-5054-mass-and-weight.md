## Mass: quantity of matter

Mass is measured in kilograms (kg). The phrase "at rest relative to the observer" in the
definition matters: mass is defined for an object that is not moving relative to the person
measuring it. In everyday O Level physics this simply means that mass is a fixed property of the
object. Taking an object to the Moon, melting it or changing its shape does not change its mass.

## Inertia

Mass also measures how strongly an object **resists a change** in its state of rest or motion.
This resistance is called **inertia**.

- A loaded supermarket trolley is harder to start moving, and harder to stop, than an empty one.
- When a bus brakes suddenly, standing passengers lurch forwards: their bodies tend to keep
  moving while the bus slows.
- Pulling a smooth tablecloth sharply from under a heavy plate can leave the plate almost in place:
  the plate's inertia resists the brief force.

Inertia depends on mass, not on weight. An astronaut on the Moon has the same mass as on Earth, so
a heavy box is just as hard to push into motion sideways there, even though it is easier to lift.

## Comparing masses with a beam balance

A beam balance (equal-arm balance) has a beam pivoted at its centre with a pan at each end.

1. Place the unknown object on one pan.
2. Add known standard masses to the other pan until the beam is level.
3. The beam is level when the **weights** on the two pans are equal.

Because both pans are in the same gravitational field, equal weights mean **equal masses**. A
beam balance therefore compares masses directly, and it would give the same result on the Moon,
where both weights would be smaller by the same factor.

## Determining mass with an electronic balance

1. Place the balance on a firm, level surface away from draughts.
2. Switch on and press **zero (tare)** with nothing on the pan, so it reads 0.00 g.
3. To find the mass of a liquid or powder, place the empty container on the pan and tare again,
   so the container's mass is subtracted automatically.
4. Add the object or substance and read the display when it is steady.

An electronic balance actually detects the **force** the object exerts on its pan and converts
this to a mass reading using the gravitational field strength where it was calibrated. It gives
correct masses on Earth, but on the Moon, where the force would be much smaller, it would read
too low unless it were recalibrated. This is a good illustration of the difference between mass
and weight.

## Measuring weight with a force meter

A force meter (newton meter) contains a spring that stretches in proportion to the force.

1. Check the pointer reads zero when nothing is attached, and adjust it if not.
2. Hold the meter vertically and hang the object from the hook.
3. Wait for the object to stop swinging.
4. Read the scale at eye level, in newtons (N).

## Gravitational field strength

A **gravitational field** is a region in which a mass experiences a force due to gravitational
attraction. The Earth, the Moon and every other mass have one around them.

**Gravitational field strength** is the force per unit mass:

```
g = W / m          W = m g          m = W / g
```

On the Earth's surface g ≈ 9.8 N/kg. On the Moon it is about 1.6 N/kg. Gravitational field
strength is a vector: it points towards the centre of the body.

**Why g is also the acceleration of free fall.** If weight is the only force on a falling object,
then from F = ma (subtopic 1.5), W = m × a. But W = m × g, so a = g. The units agree too:

```
1 N/kg = 1 (kg m/s²) / kg = 1 m/s²
```

## Worked examples

**Example 1: weight from mass.** A student has a mass of 68 kg. Find their weight on Earth
(g = 9.8 N/kg) and on the Moon (g = 1.6 N/kg).

```
Earth: W = 68 × 9.8 = 666 N  (670 N to 2 s.f.)
Moon:  W = 68 × 1.6 = 109 N  (110 N to 2 s.f.)
mass is 68 kg in both places
```

**Example 2: mass from a force meter reading.** A force meter reads 3.4 N when a stone hangs
from it. Find the stone's mass (g = 9.8 N/kg).

```
m = W / g = 3.4 / 9.8 = 0.35 kg
```

**Example 3: finding g elsewhere.** A 180 kg rover has a weight of 666 N on Mars.

```
g = W / m = 666 / 180 = 3.7 N/kg
```

**Example 4: g from a graph.** A student hangs masses from 0.050 kg to 0.250 kg on a force meter
and plots weight (y-axis) against mass (x-axis). The best-fit line passes through the origin and
through the point (0.250 kg, 2.45 N).

```
gradient = 2.45 N / 0.250 kg = 9.8 N/kg = g
```

A straight line through the origin shows that weight is proportional to mass.

## Mass and weight compared

| | Mass | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Quantity of matter; resistance to change in motion | Gravitational force on the mass |
| Scalar or vector | Scalar | Vector (acts towards the centre of the Earth) |
| Unit | kg | N |
| Changes from place to place? | No | Yes, with g |
| Measured with | Beam balance (comparison) or electronic balance | Force meter |

## Common mistakes

- Giving weight in kilograms. Weight is a force, in newtons.
- Saying an object has "no mass" in space. It has the same mass; its weight may be small.
- Writing g = m ÷ W instead of g = W ÷ m.
- Confusing inertia with weight: inertia depends on mass alone.
- Forgetting to tare the balance or subtract the container's mass.

## Exam technique for command words

- **State** (the definition): use the syllabus wording, such as "force per unit mass".
- **Describe** (how to measure): give the instrument, the steps in order and what is read.
- **Calculate**: equation, substitution, answer with unit (N or kg).
- **Explain**: give the reason, such as why a beam balance works anywhere.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is "weightless" the same as "massless"?** No. An astronaut far from any planet has very little
weight but the same mass, so still has inertia.

**Why is g given in N/kg in some questions and m/s² in others?** They are the same quantity. Use
N/kg when talking about weight and m/s² when talking about acceleration.

**Do I need to know g on other planets?** No. Questions give any value other than 9.8 N/kg.
