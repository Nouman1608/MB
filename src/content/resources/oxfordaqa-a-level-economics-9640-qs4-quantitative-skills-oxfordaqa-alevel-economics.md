This guide goes deeper into quantitative skill QS4, "construct and interpret a range of standard graphical forms", from section 3.5 of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Economics (9640) specification. The specification says the quantitative skills could be assessed in any of the assessments. It does not list which graphical forms count, so this guide uses forms that the subject content itself names or requires.

The broader [graphs and interpreting information guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-economics-graphs-and-interpreting-written-graphical-and/) covers choosing a chart, time-series line graphs, pie charts, compound bar charts, scatter graphs, a supply and demand diagram and misleading axes. This page adds four forms the specification's content relies on: production possibility diagrams drawn from data (3.1.1.4), Lorenz curves (3.3.5.1), gradients of economic relationships such as the consumption function (3.2.2), and aggregate demand and supply diagrams (3.2.2.2). It ends with bar charts that include negative values. All figures are invented; "cu" means currency units. Condense the page with the [QS4 revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-economics-9640-qs4-quantitative-skills-oxfordaqa-alevel-economics-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [QS4 practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-economics-9640-qs4-quantitative-skills-oxfordaqa-alevel-economics-practice/).

## What "construct" and "interpret" require

- **Construct**: choose the form, choose scales, label both axes with the variable and unit, plot accurately, and label curves and key points.
- **Interpret**: read values, compare them, describe trends, and, above all, read the **gradient** and the **position** of a curve as economic statements.

## 1. A production possibility diagram from data

Section 3.1.1.4 says students should be able to use production possibility diagrams to illustrate resource allocation, opportunity cost and trade-offs, unemployment of resources and economic growth.

### Worked example 1

Country A can produce the following combinations of food and machines (thousand units) when all resources are fully used.

| Combination | V | W | X | Y | Z |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Food | 0 | 10 | 20 | 30 | 40 |
| Machines | 50 | 48 | 42 | 30 | 0 |

**Constructing it.** Put food on the horizontal axis and machines on the vertical axis, each labelled "(thousand units)". A scale of 10 thousand per 2 cm on both axes fits the data. Plot the five points and join them with a smooth curve, since the boundary represents every efficient combination, not only the five listed.

**Reading the gradient as opportunity cost.**

| Move | Extra food | Machines given up | Opportunity cost of 1 food |
|---|---|---|---|
| V to W | 10 | 2 | 0.2 machines |
| W to X | 10 | 6 | 0.6 machines |
| X to Y | 10 | 12 | 1.2 machines |
| Y to Z | 10 | 30 | 3.0 machines |

The boundary gets steeper as more food is produced, so the curve is concave (bowed outwards) and the opportunity cost of food rises. The economic reason is that resources are not equally suited to both goods: the last workers and land moved into farming are the ones best suited to making machines.

**Reading positions.**

- A point such as (20, 30), inside the boundary, shows unemployed or inefficiently used resources: Country A could have more of both goods.
- A point such as (30, 45), outside the boundary, is unattainable with current resources and technology.
- Economic growth is shown by the whole boundary shifting outwards.

## 2. A Lorenz curve from income shares

Section 3.3.5.1 lists the Lorenz curve and Gini coefficients and says students should interpret measures of inequality including the Gini coefficient, but will not be expected to calculate it.

### Worked example 2

The poorest to richest fifths of households in Country B receive 5%, 10%, 15%, 25% and 45% of income. Five years later, after tax and benefit changes, they receive 7%, 12%, 17%, 24% and 40%.

| Cumulative % of households | 0 | 20 | 40 | 60 | 80 | 100 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cumulative % of income, first year | 0 | 5 | 15 | 30 | 55 | 100 |
| Cumulative % of income, five years later | 0 | 7 | 19 | 36 | 60 | 100 |

**Constructing it.** Draw a square graph with cumulative percentage of households on the horizontal axis and cumulative percentage of income on the vertical axis, both from 0 to 100. Draw the line of equality from (0, 0) to (100, 100). Plot each set of points, starting from (0, 0), and join them with a smooth curve that lies below the diagonal. Label each curve with its year.

**Interpreting it.** In the first year the poorest 60% receive 30% of income; five years later they receive 36%. The later curve lies everywhere closer to the line of equality, so inequality has fallen and the Gini coefficient is lower. The Gini coefficient is the area between the line of equality and the Lorenz curve as a fraction of the whole triangle below the line of equality; you interpret it, not calculate it.

If two Lorenz curves cross, you cannot say which distribution is more equal from the diagram alone: each is more equal over a different part of the distribution.

## 3. Gradients as economic relationships

Many economic ideas are gradients: opportunity cost on a production possibility boundary, the marginal propensity to consume on a consumption function, marginal cost on a total cost curve.

### Worked example 3

Country C's national income (Y) and consumption (C), in cu billion:

| Y | 0 | 100 | 200 | 300 | 400 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| C | 40 | 120 | 200 | 280 | 360 |

Plotted with Y on the horizontal axis, the points lie on a straight line. Its gradient is ΔC/ΔY = 80/100 = 0.8, which is the marginal propensity to consume. The line cuts the vertical axis at 40: consumption still takes place when income is zero, financed from savings or borrowing. The line crosses the 45° line, where C = Y, at Y = 200: below that income, consumption exceeds income.

To find a gradient from a drawn straight line, choose two points far apart, read their coordinates, and divide the vertical change by the horizontal change, keeping units.

## 4. Aggregate demand and supply diagrams

Section 3.2.2.2 uses AD/AS analysis throughout. Construct the diagram in this order:

1. Label the vertical axis "Price level" and the horizontal axis "Real national output" (or real GDP). Do not use "price" and "quantity", which belong to a single market.
2. Draw AD sloping downwards and SRAS sloping upwards; if the question involves long-run capacity, add a vertical LRAS.
3. Mark the initial equilibrium where AD meets SRAS, and label P₁ and Y₁ on the axes with dashed lines.
4. Draw the shifted curve, labelled AD₂ (or SRAS₂), with an arrow showing the direction of the shift.
5. Mark the new equilibrium, P₂ and Y₂.
6. In the written answer, refer to the labels: "the price level rises from P₁ to P₂".

**Interpreting.** A rightward shift of AD along an upward-sloping SRAS raises both the price level and real output. The closer the economy is to the vertical LRAS, the more of the shift shows up as a higher price level rather than higher output.

## 5. Bar charts with negative values

Balances such as the current account (3.2.3.4) or the budget balance can be negative.

### Worked example 4

Country D's current account balance, as a percentage of GDP, over five years: −2.1, −3.4, −1.0, +0.5, +1.2.

- Draw the horizontal axis at 0, with the vertical axis running from about −4 to +2 (labelled "Current account balance (% of GDP)").
- Deficits are bars below the axis and surpluses bars above.
- **Interpreting.** The deficit was largest in year 2. From year 2 to year 3 the balance improved by 2.4 percentage points, but the country was still in deficit. The balance turned to surplus only in year 4.

A smaller deficit is not a surplus: say "the deficit narrowed" rather than "the current account rose".

## Common mistakes

- Joining production possibility points with straight lines when the data show a curve, or vice versa.
- Plotting a Lorenz curve without starting at (0, 0) or without drawing the line of equality.
- Labelling an AD/AS diagram "Price" and "Quantity".
- Calculating a gradient from two points that are close together, which magnifies reading errors.
- Describing a narrowing deficit as a surplus.
- Leaving curves unlabelled, so the examiner cannot tell AD₁ from AD₂.

## Exam technique

- **Draw** or **construct**: use a ruler for axes and straight lines, label everything, and give a title if space allows.
- **Using a diagram, explain**: refer to labelled points in your writing; a diagram not mentioned in the text earns little.
- **Interpret** or **what does the graph show**: give a trend, a figure that supports it, and the economic meaning.
- **Calculate from the graph**: show the two points you read and the arithmetic.
