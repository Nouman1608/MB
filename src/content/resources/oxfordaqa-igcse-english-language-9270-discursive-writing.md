**This guide covers one of the task types named for Paper 1 Section B of OxfordAQA International
GCSE English Language (9270).** The specification says the writing section "will consist of three
tasks from which students choose one: descriptive/imaginative/argumentative or discursive". The
[Section B composition study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-english-language-9270-section-b-composition/)
compares argumentative and discursive writing in a table and shows two openings; this page goes
further into discursive writing alone: how to structure a balanced piece, the language of weighing,
how to reach a conclusion, and how each writing objective applies.

## What a discursive task asks for

A discursive composition **explores an issue from more than one angle** and reaches a considered
view. It is not a list of opinions, and it is not an argument in disguise. The reader should finish
feeling that the question is more interesting and more complicated than it first seemed, and that
the writer has thought it through.

Typical task wordings include "Discuss…", "Explore the arguments for and against…", "Consider
different views on…" or a statement followed by "Discuss this view." Read the instruction carefully:
the specification's own wording, "argumentative and/or discursive", shows that a task may call for
one, the other, or a blend of the two.

## Three structures

**1. Block structure.** All the points on one side, then all on the other, then a conclusion.
Simple to plan, but it can read like two separate essays, and the conclusion has to do all the
weighing.

**2. Alternating structure.** Each paragraph takes one aspect of the issue and weighs both sides
within it. This shows thinking throughout and usually reads as more mature.

**3. Thematic structure.** Paragraphs are organised by theme (for example, effects on individuals,
on families, on society), and each theme is weighed in turn. This suits wide questions.

| Structure | Best for | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Block | Short, clear-cut issues | Feels like two lists; weighing left to the end |
| Alternating | Most tasks | Needs careful planning so paragraphs do not repeat |
| Thematic | Broad social questions | Can lose the balance if one side dominates each theme |

## The language of weighing

Discursive writing depends on vocabulary that holds ideas in balance. Use it precisely, not as
decoration.

- **Concession:** *although, while, admittedly, it is true that, granted*.
- **Contrast:** *however, on the other hand, conversely, yet, nevertheless*.
- **Qualification:** *to some extent, in certain cases, for some people, not always*.
- **Modality:** *may, might, could, tends to, is likely to*. These show that you are judging
  evidence, not announcing facts.
- **Evaluation:** *more convincing, a stronger case, a weaker point, the most serious objection*.

A sentence that begins with a concessive clause carries both sides in one structure: "Although
online learning allows students to work at their own pace, it can leave those without a quiet space
at home further behind." That is W2 (sentences for purpose) and W3 (cohesion) working at once.

## Evidence without invented numbers

You will not have sources on Paper 1, so evidence comes from **reasoning, examples and experience**.

- **Realistic examples:** a student who works a weekend job; a family that shares one computer.
- **Reasoned consequences:** "If homework were abolished, teachers would need to cover practice in
  lessons, which would leave less time for new material."
- **General knowledge you are sure of:** widely known facts stated carefully.

Avoid made-up statistics and "experts say" claims. A precise, believable example is more persuasive
than a number the examiner knows you invented.

## Voice and register

A discursive piece is usually **formal or semi-formal**. You may write in the first person,
especially in the conclusion, but the voice should be measured and reflective. Rhetorical questions
can open a paragraph by posing the issue, and direct address can engage the reader, but heavy
emotive language and repeated exclamations belong to argument, not discussion.

## Concluding: a judgement, not a summary

The conclusion should do more than repeat both sides. Options:

- **A clear but qualified view:** "On balance, the benefits outweigh the costs, provided that…"
- **A conditional view:** "The answer depends on…"
- **A reframing:** "Perhaps the real question is not whether…, but how…"

Each shows that the discussion has led somewhere. Whichever you choose, the judgement should grow
from the aspects you have weighed: a conclusion that introduces a brand-new point suggests the plan
was incomplete, while one that names the deciding factor shows the reader why the balance tipped.

## Worked example: plan and paragraph

**Task (original):** *"Young people today have more freedom than any generation before them."
Discuss this view.*

```
Structure: alternating, three aspects
Opening:   image of a teenager with a phone at a bus stop, connected to the world
           and watched by it -> pose the question of what "freedom" means
1. Information: more access to knowledge and opinions | but more pressure and
   less privacy
2. Movement and choices: travel, study, careers | but cost, competition, and
   adult supervision of time
3. Expression: identity and voice online | but permanence of mistakes,
   judgement by strangers
Conclusion: reframe -> more choices, but not necessarily more freedom
```

**Model paragraph (aspect 1):**

> It is tempting to measure freedom by access, and by that measure today's teenagers are richer than
> any before them: a question that once needed a library and an adult can now be answered in
> seconds. Yet access works in both directions. The same device that opens the world also lets the
> world look back, so that a careless comment or an unflattering photograph may follow its owner for
> years. Freedom to find information, then, has arrived together with a new kind of exposure, and
> whether young people are freer depends on whether we count the doors that have opened or the walls
> that have disappeared.

*Why it works:* the concessive structure ("It is tempting… Yet…") holds both sides; the examples are
realistic and need no statistics; the final sentence weighs rather than summarises, and its image of
doors and walls gives the paragraph a memorable close (W1).

## Applying the writing objectives

- **W1:** a measured, engaging voice and vocabulary chosen to show precise distinctions.
- **W2:** concessive and complex sentences for balance; an occasional short sentence for a key
  judgement.
- **W3:** a planned structure; topic sentences that name the aspect; links that show the logic of
  the discussion.
- **W4:** accurate punctuation of complex sentences, especially commas after introductory clauses.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing an argument** when the task says "discuss".
- **A block structure that never weighs**, followed by "In conclusion, there are good points on both
  sides".
- **Invented statistics** and vague authorities.
- **Repeating the same point** in different paragraphs.
- **A conclusion that only summarises.**

## Exam technique

Decide in the first minutes whether the task wants argument, discussion or a blend, then choose a
structure and plan three aspects. Write your conclusion's judgement into the plan before you begin.
Leave time to check the punctuation of long sentences.
