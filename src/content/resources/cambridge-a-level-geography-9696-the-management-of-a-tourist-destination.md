Section 13.4 of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026 is a single case study. The syllabus wording is: "candidates must study one tourist area or resort, its growth and development, showing the issues of sustainability it faces and evaluating the impacts of tourism on the destination's environment(s), society and economy." It sits in Topic 13, Global interdependence, one of the four Advanced Human Geography options in Paper 4.

The [Topic 13 study guide](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-global-interdependence/) already uses Venice as its 13.4 example. This page goes further: it shows how to structure any destination case study so it answers the four demands of the syllabus, works through a second, smaller destination (Maya Bay in Thailand) in full, and gives an essay plan. Test yourself with the [13.4 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-management-of-a-tourist-destination-revision-notes/) and the [13.4 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-management-of-a-tourist-destination-practice/).

## What the four demands mean

| Syllabus phrase | What the examiner wants to see |
|---|---|
| "one tourist area or resort" | A place at the scale of a resort, island, bay, national park or historic city, not a whole country |
| "its growth and development" | A sequence: why tourists first came, what changed, what types of tourism now dominate |
| "issues of sustainability it faces" | Named pressures on the environment, residents and the local economy, now and in future |
| "evaluating the impacts" | A judgement on whether impacts are positive or negative overall, for whom, and how far management has changed the balance |

The last demand carries most weight. Paper 4 gives evaluation the largest share of marks, so a case study that only narrates events will be capped however detailed it is.

## The building blocks of a destination case study

Before learning a place, learn the tools you will use to analyse it. These are the ideas from 13.3 that 13.4 applies to one real destination.

**Carrying capacity in practice.** A destination can exceed its ecological carrying capacity (coral, dunes or wildlife degrade) long before it exceeds its physical capacity (the space available). Perceptual carrying capacity is reached when visitors find a place too crowded to enjoy, or when residents turn against tourism. Good case studies say which type was exceeded first.

**Stakeholders.** Every management decision creates winners and losers. Typical groups: residents, local business owners, workers (often seasonal or migrant), tour operators and transport firms from outside the area, the local or national government, a park or heritage authority, conservation groups and scientists, and the tourists themselves. Evaluation is stronger when it names which group gains and which loses.

**Management tools.** Most destinations combine several of these:

- **Demand limits**: caps on daily numbers, timed entry slots, booking systems.
- **Pricing**: entry fees, tourist taxes, higher prices at peak times.
- **Spatial controls**: zoning, closing fragile areas, moving access points away from sensitive sites.
- **Temporal controls**: seasonal closures to allow recovery, spreading visits across the year.
- **Hardening**: boardwalks, moorings and paths that concentrate trampling and anchoring where damage is least.
- **Regulation of activities**: bans on swimming, feeding wildlife, anchoring or large vessels.
- **Education**: guides, signs and briefings that change behaviour.
- **Monitoring**: repeated surveys of coral, water quality or resident attitudes to show whether the tools work.

**Butler's model as an evaluative frame.** Placing the destination on the tourism area life cycle lets you argue whether management produced rejuvenation, delayed stagnation or simply moved pressure elsewhere.

## Worked case study: Maya Bay, Ko Phi Phi Leh, Thailand

Maya Bay is a small bay enclosed by limestone cliffs on Ko Phi Phi Leh, an uninhabited island in Krabi Province in the Andaman Sea. It lies inside a national park. It suits 13.4 because it is a clearly bounded tourist area with a sharp growth phase, an obvious sustainability crisis and a well-documented management response.

### Growth and development

- **Exploration and involvement.** Before 2000 the Phi Phi islands were visited mainly by divers and backpackers, reached by boat from Phuket and Krabi.
- **Development.** The film *The Beach* (2000) was shot at Maya Bay. It turned the bay into a global image of an untouched paradise and an essential day trip. Speedboats and long-tail boats from Phuket, Krabi and Ko Phi Phi Don brought visitors on short, packaged tours.
- **Consolidation and stagnation.** By the 2010s the bay was reported to receive several thousand visitors on busy days. Boats anchored inside the bay, and the beach was crowded for most of the day. The experience no longer matched the image that drew people: a sign that perceptual carrying capacity had been passed.

### Sustainability issues

- **Environmental.** Boat anchors and propellers broke coral; sunscreen, fuel and waste polluted shallow water; trampling compacted the beach and damaged vegetation behind it. Ecological carrying capacity was clearly exceeded.
- **Economic.** The tour industry around Phuket and Krabi depended on one site. Day-trip business brings large total spending, but much of it goes to tour firms based outside the islands rather than to the island economy.
- **Social.** Ko Phi Phi Leh has no residents, so the social pressures fall on nearby Ko Phi Phi Don and the wider coast: crowding, waste and water supply pressure where tourists stay overnight.

### Management

- In **June 2018** Thailand's national park authority closed Maya Bay to tourists. The closure, first planned for a few months, was extended to allow coral and beach recovery.
- During the closure, coral fragments were replanted and the beach was left undisturbed. Blacktip reef sharks returned to the shallows in large numbers, which became a key piece of evidence that recovery was under way.
- The bay **reopened in January 2022** under new rules: boats may no longer enter the bay, and visitors land at a pier on the other side of the island and walk in along a boardwalk; swimming in the bay is banned; visits are limited to timed slots with a cap on how many people can be on the beach at once.
- The bay has since been closed each year in the low season, usually August to September, for recovery.

### Evaluating the impacts

| Dimension | Before 2018 | After reopening | Judgement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Environment | Coral and beach degraded by uncontrolled boats and crowds | Recovery of coral and sharks; boats kept out | Clearly improved, but depends on rules being enforced |
| Economy | High volume, much of the income going to firms based elsewhere | Lower volume; tour operators lost business during closure | Short-term loss for operators; long-term protection of the asset they sell |
| Society | Crowding transferred to Ko Phi Phi Don and coastal resorts | Pressure redistributed to other islands and bays | Least improved: management protected one site, not the wider area |

**Overall judgement.** Maya Bay shows that strict limits can reverse ecological damage, moving a destination from stagnation towards managed rejuvenation. But it is a small, uninhabited site that a single authority could close completely. That option does not exist in a lived-in city such as Venice, where residents, businesses and heritage overlap. The case therefore supports the view that management works best where one body controls access and the cost of closure falls on outsiders. Watch also for displacement: if day-trippers simply go to the next beach, the regional impact has moved rather than shrunk.

## Annotated essay plan

*"Evaluate the extent to which the management of one tourist destination you have studied has made it more sustainable." [20]*

1. **Introduction.** Define sustainability in three dimensions (environment, society, economy). Locate Maya Bay and state your line: management made it more environmentally sustainable, less clearly socially or economically.
2. **Context paragraph.** Growth after 2000 and the passing of ecological and perceptual carrying capacity. Use Butler's stages briefly; do not narrate the whole model.
3. **Environmental paragraph.** Closure, replanting, boardwalk and boat ban; evidence of recovery. Judgement: strong success, conditional on enforcement and annual closures.
4. **Economic paragraph.** Lost income during closure versus protection of the asset; who captures spending. Judgement: mixed, and depends on time scale.
5. **Social and spatial paragraph.** Displacement of crowds to nearby islands. Judgement: weakest dimension, because the management unit is too small.
6. **Comparison.** Contrast with a lived-in destination to show why the same tools would be harder to apply.
7. **Conclusion.** Answer "to what extent" directly: more sustainable environmentally, partly economically, little change socially; success depends on scale and control of access.

## Common mistakes

- Choosing a whole country ("tourism in Thailand") instead of an area or resort.
- Writing two pages on growth and one line on evaluation.
- Listing management tools without evidence that they changed anything.
- Treating "the economy" as one group: tour operators, workers, residents and government gain or lose differently.
- Forgetting displacement: protecting one site can push pressure onto another.
- Using Butler's model as a description rather than a way to judge whether management worked.

## Command words in this case study

- **Describe** the growth of a destination: give the sequence with dates and named causes.
- **Explain** sustainability issues: say why each pressure arises and what it damages.
- **Assess** or **evaluate** impacts or management: weigh positives against negatives, say for whom, and reach a clear conclusion.
- **To what extent**: give a position on a scale (largely, partly, little) and justify it with evidence from your case study.
