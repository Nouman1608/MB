This set covers the global environment of business in the OCR A Level Business (H431) specification, Version 2.1 (January 2026): globalisation, trade, exchange rates, the EU, emerging markets and the international points under other headings. Read the [study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-the-global-environment-of-business/) and [revision notes](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-the-global-environment-of-business-revision-notes/) first; the [earlier global context practice set](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-global-context-international-trade-the-eu-and-emerging-markets-practice/) has more. All businesses and figures are invented, and every exchange rate is given as foreign units per £1.

## Questions

**1.** **(a)** Distinguish between a national business and a multinational business. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how a multinational that makes parts in one country and assembles them in another helps to deepen globalisation. **[2]**

**2.** Greaves Bakery is a village shop. Orbital Gadgets sells phone accessories online to several countries. Explain why Orbital Gadgets is likely to be more affected by globalisation than Greaves Bakery. **[4]**

**3.** Hollinfirth Audio, a UK firm, sells speakers abroad for USD 390 each and buys parts invoiced at ¥1,710,000. The rates are £1 = USD 1.30 and £1 = ¥190.
**(a)** Calculate the pound price of one speaker. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the pound cost of the parts invoice. **[2]**
**(c)** The rates change to £1 = USD 1.20 and £1 = ¥180. Hollinfirth sells 500 speakers and pays one invoice a month. Calculate the net change in its monthly pound position. **[4]**

**4.** A country puts a 12% tariff on imported tiles. A UK firm exports tiles that cost the importer £45 before the tariff.
**(a)** Calculate the cost to the importer including the tariff. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain the purpose of the tariff and one effect on the UK firm. **[3]**

**5.** **(a)** Distinguish between free trade and membership of a trading bloc. **[2]**
**(b)** Tyrrelby Tools, a UK hand-tool maker, buys steel abroad and sells mostly at home. A free trade agreement removes all tariffs between the UK and the steel-producing country. Analyse **one** advantage and **one** disadvantage of this agreement for Tyrrelby Tools and its employees. **[4]**

**6.** Penrhallow Homeware makes 30,000 kettles a year in the UK at a unit cost of £26.00. It plans to offshore production. The overseas unit cost is £15.50, with a tariff of 10% of that cost on entry to the UK and shipping of £3.10 a unit. The one-off set-up cost is £120,000.
**(a)** Calculate the landed cost per unit. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the annual saving. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the payback period in months, to one decimal place. **[2]**
**(d)** State **two** non-financial reasons Penrhallow might keep production in the UK. **[2]**

**7.** Explain how political instability in an overseas country may affect a UK firm that exports to it. **[4]**

**8.** UK interest rates rise. Explain what this may do to the pound against other currencies, and the effect on a UK importer. **[3]**

**9.** Fernbury Skincare sells moisturiser in the UK and plans to enter a new overseas market. Option 1 is a standardised mix: UK packaging and price, expected sales of 60,000 units a year with a contribution of £6.00 a unit. Option 2 is an adapted mix: local-language packs, smaller sizes and a lower price, expected sales of 95,000 units a year with a contribution of £4.20 a unit, and a one-off adaptation cost of £110,000. Evaluate which mix Fernbury should use. **[9]**

**10.** Strathorne Foods is considering an emerging market with 3.2 million middle-income households, expected to grow by 11% a year.
**(a)** State **two** features of an emerging market. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the number of middle-income households after four years (to two decimal places, in millions) and the percentage rise (to one decimal place). **[2]**
**(c)** Evaluate the impact of this emerging market on Strathorne Foods. **[6]**

**11.** Kestlemoor Robotics makes warehouse sensors in the UK and sells 20,000 a year at £85 each. Variable cost is £52 a unit and fixed costs are £420,000 a year. An overseas rival now sells a similar sensor in the UK for £70. Kestlemoor's options are as follows. Response A: keep the price at £85, with sales expected to fall to 14,000. Response B: cut the price to £72, keeping sales at 20,000 and costs unchanged. Response C: buy the housings and circuit boards abroad, which cuts variable cost (including tariff and shipping) to £41 a unit, and cut the price to £72 with sales at 20,000. Response C adds £60,000 a year to fixed costs for quality checking, plus a one-off £90,000 retooling cost in year one. Recommend and justify how Kestlemoor should respond to increasing globalisation. **[12]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** A national business operates in one country [1]; a multinational operates in more than one [1].
**(b)** Linking factories in different countries into one chain [1] ties together the economies, workers and suppliers involved, so trade and dependence between countries grow [1].
*Examiner insight:* A distinction needs both sides in one answer. Two separate definitions without a contrast are weaker.

**2.** Orbital buys and sells across borders, so it faces overseas rivals and currency changes [1] [1]. E-commerce makes it easy for customers to compare prices worldwide [1]. Greaves serves local customers with local inputs and few overseas rivals [1].
*Examiner insight:* Both businesses need to appear in the answer. A general statement about globalisation without applying it to Orbital would not answer "why more affected".

**3. (a)** 390 ÷ 1.30 [1] = **£300** [1].
**(b)** 1,710,000 ÷ 190 [1] = **£9,000** [1].
**(c)** Speaker revenue: 500 × 390 = USD 195,000, which is £150,000 at 1.30 and £162,500 at 1.20, a gain of £12,500 [1] [1]. Invoice: 1,710,000 ÷ 180 = £9,500, a rise of £500 [1]. Net gain **£12,000** a month [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show each conversion step, and state whether the net is a gain or a loss.

**4. (a)** 45 × 0.12 = £5.40 [1]; 45 + 5.40 = **£50.40** [1].
**(b)** A tariff raises the price of imports [1], to protect home producers or raise revenue [1]. The UK firm's tiles become dearer than local ones, so it may sell fewer [1].
*Examiner insight:* Purpose and effect are separate asks. Tie the effect to the exporting firm, not to the country.

**5. (a)** Free trade has no tariffs, quotas or similar barriers between trading partners [1]; a bloc cuts barriers among its members only, so outsiders still face them [1].
**(b)** Advantage: steel arrives without a tariff [1], so costs fall and Tyrrelby can cut prices or raise margins [1]. Disadvantage: overseas toolmakers also reach the UK tariff-free [1], so Tyrrelby may lose sales and employees may face reduced hours or redundancy [1].
*Examiner insight:* Analysis needs a chain: the feature, then the effect on this business. A bare list of points scores less.

**6. (a)** Tariff = 0.10 × 15.50 = £1.55 [1]; landed cost = 15.50 + 1.55 + 3.10 = **£20.15** [1].
**(b)** Saving per unit = 26.00 − 20.15 = £5.85 [1]; annual saving = 5.85 × 30,000 = **£175,500** [1].
**(c)** 120,000 ÷ 175,500 = 0.684 years [1] = **8.2 months** [1].
**(d)** Any two: quality control, shorter supply lines, protecting the "made in the UK" reputation, currency or political risk abroad [1] [1].
*Examiner insight:* Convert years to months at the end, keeping the unrounded figure until then.

**7.** Instability can disrupt supply routes and deliveries [1] and may lead to sudden changes in rules, tariffs or payment [1]. Customers may cut spending or be unable to pay [1], so the exporter faces lower and less certain sales and may need to find other markets [1].
*Examiner insight:* Link each effect to exporting: orders, payment and risk.

**8.** Higher UK rates attract overseas savers [1], who need pounds, so demand for pounds rises and the pound tends to appreciate [1]. An importer then pays fewer pounds for goods priced in foreign currency [1].
*Examiner insight:* State the direction of the pound and then the effect on the importer. Reversing exporter and importer is a common slip.

**9.** Option 1 earns 60,000 × 6.00 = £360,000 a year [1]. Option 2 earns 95,000 × 4.20 = £399,000 a year [1], but £289,000 in year one after the £110,000 cost [1]. Cumulatively Option 2 trails after two years (£688,000 against £720,000) and leads after three (£1,087,000 against £1,080,000) [1]. Adapting suits local language and buying habits and may win more customers [1]; standardising keeps one brand image and avoids upfront cost [1]. The demand forecasts are uncertain, and the lead is small [1]. *Judgement:* adapt if Fernbury plans to stay in the market for at least three years and trusts the forecast [1]; otherwise a standardised launch is safer [1].
*Examiner insight:* Compare the two options with the figures and give a condition under which the answer would change. Quoting numbers without a comparison does not make a judgement.

**10. (a)** Any two: rapid economic growth, industrialisation, rising incomes, less developed or less stable than advanced economies [1] [1].
**(b)** 3.2 × 1.11⁴ = **4.86 million** [1]; the rise is 1.11⁴ − 1 = **51.8%** [1].
**(c)** *Opportunity:* a 51.8% rise in middle-income households suggests strong growth in demand for packaged food [1] [1]. *Threats:* local rivals know tastes and distribution [1]; currency swings could cut pound profit [1]; instability may disrupt trade [1]. *Judgement:* the growth justifies entry, probably through a local distributor to limit risk until the firm knows the market [1].
*Examiner insight:* Use the calculated growth in the evaluation. Judgement that rests on a stated condition reads as more convincing than a bare "it depends".

**11.** *Response A:* contribution is 14,000 × (85 − 52) = £462,000, so profit is £462,000 − £420,000 = £42,000 [1] [1]. *Response B:* 20,000 × (72 − 52) = £400,000, so a loss of £20,000 [1] [1]. *Response C:* unit contribution is 72 − 41 = £31, giving £620,000; fixed costs are £480,000, so profit is £140,000 a year [1] [1], or £50,000 in year one after retooling [1]. In year one C already beats A by £8,000, and by £98,000 a year after that [1]. *Weighing up:* A adds no new risk but profit falls by 82.5% from £240,000 and stays exposed to further price cuts [1]. B makes a loss, so it is rejected. C carries risks: quality control, longer supply lines and currency movements [1]. *Recommendation:* choose C [1], provided sales hold near 20,000 and quality stays reliable; if buyers valued UK provenance enough for A's volume to hold, A would be safer [1].
*Examiner insight:* A "recommend and justify" answer needs a chosen response, supporting figures and a reason the alternatives are weaker. Calculations alone leave the question unanswered.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the wrong operation in a currency conversion.
- Stating an exchange rate effect without saying whether it helps the exporter or importer.
- Describing a tariff or quota without both its nature and its purpose.
- Writing textbook definitions that never mention the business in the case.
- Quoting case numbers without comparing them.
- Writing only advantages, or only disadvantages, on an evaluate question.
- Rounding part-way through a long calculation.
