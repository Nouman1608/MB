The 9675 specification lists "the victims of crime and the inclusion of suffering" among the
elements that might be explored in Unit 3, Elements of Crime and Mystery. This guide develops that
single element. For the unit as a whole, including the closed-book exam and the full set-text list,
start with the [Unit 3 study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-english-literature-unit-3-elements-of-crime-and-mystery-study-guide/).
Here the question is narrower: how do these texts treat the people who are harmed, and why does it
matter for their meaning?

## Why victims are easy to overlook

Crime narratives tend to pull attention towards the criminal and the investigator. The criminal has
motive and secrets; the detective has intelligence and a quest. Victims are often dead before the
story starts, silent, or reduced to a body that serves as evidence. That imbalance is itself
significant. When you write about victims, you are often writing about what a text chooses not to
show, which the genre makes into a method.

## Six questions about victims

1. **Voice.** Does the victim speak, and if so, before or after the crime? Who tells their story?
2. **Innocence.** Is the victim blameless, compromised or guilty of something else?
3. **Visibility of suffering.** Is suffering shown, reported, implied or skipped?
4. **Range of victims.** Who else suffers: families, communities, animals, the criminal, a nation?
5. **Function.** Is the victim a person, a clue, a symbol or a plot device?
6. **Readers' response.** Does the text invite pity, repugnance or the excitement of suspense,
   and does it make readers uneasy about enjoying another's suffering?

The last question links to the specification's closing element: how crime stories affect readers,
"creating suspense, repugnance, excitement and relief".

## The set texts

**Macbeth.** Duncan dies offstage, so his suffering is imagined through Macbeth's horror; Banquo is
killed on stage; and the attack on Macduff's family, in which his son is killed in front of the audience, is the
point at which Macbeth's crimes turn from political to merely cruel. Scotland itself is presented as
a suffering victim, a country that bleeds under a tyrant. The play also asks whether the criminals
become victims of their own crime, through sleeplessness and madness.

**The Rime of the Ancient Mariner.** The first victim is the albatross, shot without reason; the
crew who die are victims of a punishment for a crime they approved; and the Mariner's long
suffering becomes his penance. The Wedding-Guest, held by the tale, leaves changed, so the poem
extends suffering to its listener.

**Browning, Selected Poems.** In the dramatic monologues the criminal speaks and the victim cannot.
The Duchess in "My Last Duchess" survives only as a painting the Duke controls; the woman in
"Porphyria's Lover" is killed and then arranged by her lover's words; the rival in "The Laboratory"
never appears. The victim's silence is the form's central effect. "Count Gismond" offers a
contrast: a woman slandered in public is defended and her accuser killed, so the poem gives the
victim a rescuer. "The Pied Piper of Hamelin" makes children the victims of the town's broken
promise.

**Oliver Twist.** Oliver is a child victim of institutions as much as of criminals: the workhouse,
the parish officials and the magistrates are part of the system that harms him. Nancy is both a
member of the criminal world and its most important victim; her murder by Sikes is told in detail,
and its aftermath, as Sikes is haunted, shows suffering spreading to the murderer.

**Poe, Collected Stories.** Poe's confessing narrators reduce victims to objects: the old man in
"The Tell-Tale Heart" is defined by his eye, and in "The Black Cat" the wife's death is told almost
in passing beside the cat's. In "The Murders in the Rue Morgue" the victims' injuries are evidence
for Dupin's reasoning. "The Pit and the Pendulum" makes the narrator himself the victim, so the
reader experiences suffering from inside.

**Stevenson, Collected Stories.** In *Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde* the trampled child and the beaten Sir
Danvers Carew are the first signs of Hyde's nature, seen through witnesses. "The Body Snatcher"
turns victims into supplies for anatomy, and Fettes recognises a young woman's body on the
dissecting table. "Markheim" begins with the killing of a dealer and spends the story on the
murderer's conscience.

**The Murder of Roger Ackroyd.** Mrs Ferrars, driven to suicide by blackmail, and Roger Ackroyd are
the victims, but the novel's structure keeps attention on the puzzle. Because the narrator turns out
to be the murderer, his calm account of the victims is retrospectively chilling.

**Death of a Red Heroine.** Guan Hongying, a celebrated model worker found dead in a canal, is a
victim whose public image hides a private life, and Chief Inspector Chen's investigation asks whether
the political system will allow justice for her when the suspect is well connected.

**When Will There Be Good News?** The novel opens with the murder of a child's family in the
countryside, and follows the survivor as an adult. It is unusually concerned with the long
aftermath of crime: memory, trauma and the vulnerability of the young, including the teenage Reggie.

## Building an essay

1. **Choose your claim about victims.** For example, that the text silences victims to focus on the
   criminal mind, or that it insists on suffering to make crime matter morally.
2. **Range across the text.** Use the first crime, a turning point and the ending.
3. **Analyse method.** Point of view, offstage or onstage presentation, description of the body,
   how other characters speak about the victim.
4. **Weigh a counter-reading.** A text that seems to ignore victims may invite readers to notice the
   gap.
5. **Link to context where it helps.** Victorian attitudes to women and children, or the state's
   power in 1990s China, explain specific choices.

## Worked paragraph

This model uses *Oliver Twist*, a set text, without long quotation, as in a closed-book answer.

> Dickens refuses to let Nancy become merely a plot device, and the detail of her death is what
> turns the novel's crime story into a moral one. Up to this point Nancy has moved between the
> criminal world and Oliver's rescuers, and her choice to help him gives her a conscience the
> novel values. When Sikes kills her, the narrative stays with the violence and with her final plea,
> so the reader cannot treat it as an offstage event to be solved. The aftermath extends her
> suffering into his: Sikes is pursued by the memory of her eyes, and his flight ends in a death
> that seems almost willed by the crowd and by his own guilt. Dickens therefore includes suffering
> not to create suspense, which is low because the reader knows who the killer is, but to make
> repugnance and pity the reader's main response.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing only about criminals and detectives.** Victims are an element the specification names.
- **Assuming victims are always innocent.** Mrs Ferrars and Nancy are both compromised.
- **Ignoring non-human victims.** The albatross and the cat carry meaning.
- **Moral outrage in place of analysis.** Explain how the text shapes your response.

## Exam technique

Unit 3 is a closed-book exam: two essays, each on a different set text, 25 marks each, in 2 hours.
Questions often ask you to **explore the significance** of an element or debate a view. For this
element, prepare three moments per text: a victim's voice or silence, a scene of suffering, and the
aftermath. Learn short quotations for each.
