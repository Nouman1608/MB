## 4.4.1 Uses of electricity

Electricity is used for **heating** (kettles, irons, heaters), **lighting**,
**charging batteries** (phones, cars), and **powering motors** (fans,
washing machines, pumps) and **electronic systems** (computers,
televisions). In each case the electrical work done by the supply is
transferred to another energy store.

### Lamps in parallel

House lighting circuits connect lamps in parallel. The advantages to state:

- each lamp receives the **full supply p.d.**, so it works at its normal
  brightness
- each lamp can be **switched on and off independently**
- if one lamp fails, the **others stay on**, because their branches are
  still complete

In series, the supply p.d. would be shared, every lamp would be dim, and
one failure would put them all out.

### Power and energy

```
P = I V            power (W) = current (A) × p.d. (V)
E = I V t          energy (J) = current (A) × p.d. (V) × time (s)
```

**Worked example 1.** A 2.3 kW kettle runs on a 230 V supply. Find the
current, and the energy transferred in 3.0 minutes.

```
I = P / V = 2300 / 230 = 10 A
E = I V t = 10 × 230 × 180 = 414 000 J  (4.1 × 10⁵ J)
```

Power must be in watts and time in seconds when the answer is in joules.

### The kilowatt-hour

The joule is too small a unit for household bills. The **kilowatt-hour
(kW h)** is the energy transferred by a **1 kW** appliance in **1 hour**:

```
1 kW h = 1000 W × 3600 s = 3 600 000 J  (3.6 × 10⁶ J)
energy (kW h) = power (kW) × time (h)
cost = energy (kW h) × price per kW h
```

**Worked example 2.** Prices here are invented for practice. Three 100 W
lamps are used for 5.0 hours a day for 30 days. Electricity costs $0.20
per kW h.

```
power = 3 × 100 W = 300 W = 0.30 kW
time  = 5.0 × 30 = 150 h
energy = 0.30 × 150 = 45 kW h
cost  = 45 × 0.20 = $9.00
```

**Worked example 3.** A 2.0 kW heater is used for 90 minutes. Energy =
2.0 × 1.5 = **3.0 kW h**. Converting minutes to hours (90 min = 1.5 h)
before multiplying is the step most often missed.

## 4.4.2 Electrical safety

### Hazards

| Hazard | What can happen |
|---|---|
| Damaged insulation | Live conductor exposed: electric shock if touched; wires touching can cause a short circuit and fire |
| Overheating cables | Too much current for the cable's thickness heats it; insulation can melt and start a fire |
| Damp conditions | Water conducts, so current can pass through a person to earth: shock risk |
| Excess current from overloading plugs, extension leads, single and multiple sockets | Many appliances drawing current through one socket or lead overheat the cables and connections: fire risk |

### Fuses

A fuse is a short, thin piece of wire in the live wire that **heats up
and melts** when the current exceeds its rating, **breaking the circuit**.
It protects the cable and appliance from overheating and fire.

**Choosing a rating:** calculate the normal working current with I = P / V,
then choose the **smallest available rating that is above** it. Too low and
the fuse melts in normal use; too high and a dangerous fault current would
not melt it.

**Worked example 4.** Fuses of 3 A, 5 A and 13 A are available; the supply
is 230 V.

```
920 W appliance:  I = 920 / 230 = 4.0 A   → choose 5 A
2.8 kW appliance: I = 2800 / 230 = 12.2 A → choose 13 A
```

### Trip switches (circuit breakers)

A trip switch contains an electromagnet (or a similar sensing device) that
**opens a switch** when the current exceeds its setting. Compared with a
fuse it acts faster and can be **reset** once the fault is cleared, rather
than replaced. Its setting is chosen in the same way as a fuse rating: just
above the normal working current of the circuit it protects.

### Earthing and double insulation

The three wires in a mains circuit are:

- **live (line)**: at a high alternating p.d., the dangerous wire
- **neutral**: completes the circuit, at a p.d. close to zero
- **earth**: a safety wire, connected to the metal case of an appliance
  and to the ground; it normally carries no current

**When a live wire touches an earthed metal case.** The case is connected
to earth by a wire of very low resistance, so a **very large current flows
from live through the case to earth**. This large current **melts the fuse
or trips the circuit breaker** at once, disconnecting the appliance from
the live supply. The case never stays at a dangerous p.d., so anyone
touching it is protected.

**Why the casing must be non-conducting or earthed.** If a metal case
were not earthed and a live wire touched it, the case would become live
and a person touching it would provide the path to earth: an electric
shock. An appliance with a **non-conducting (double-insulated)** casing
cannot become live in this way, so it does not need an earth wire.

### Switches and fuses in the live wire

A switch must be in the **live** wire. When it is opened, the appliance is
**disconnected from the high p.d.** If the switch were in the neutral wire,
the appliance would stop working but its internal parts would still be
connected to the live wire, and someone repairing it could get a shock.

For the same reason, **fuses and circuit breakers** go in the live wire:
when they break the circuit, the appliance is isolated from the live
supply, not left connected to it.

## Common mistakes

- Using kW with seconds, or W with hours, in energy calculations. Pair kW
  with hours for kW h, and W with seconds for joules.
- Choosing a fuse below the working current, or picking the largest
  available "to be safe".
- Saying the fuse protects the *person*. Its job is to stop overheating
  and fire; earthing with the fuse protects against shock.
- Saying the earth wire carries current all the time. It carries current
  only when a fault connects live to the case.
- Writing that the switch can go in either wire.
- Saying lamps in parallel each get "a share" of the p.d. They each get
  the full p.d.

## Exam technique for Cambridge command words

- **State the hazard**: name it and the danger (shock or fire).
- **Explain** earthing as a chain: fault → large current to earth → fuse
  melts or breaker trips → live supply disconnected → case safe.
- **Choose a fuse**: show I = P / V, then justify the choice as the
  smallest rating above the working current.
- **Calculate a cost**: convert to kW and hours, find kW h, then multiply by
  the price; keep units on every line.
