## Section A: short questions

**1.** What is meant by **deviance**? **[2]**

**2.** Identify **two** examples of green or environmental crime. **[2]**

**3.** What is meant by **corporate crime**? **[2]**

**4.** Identify **two** examples of cybercrime. **[2]**

**5.** Study **Item A** and answer the questions that follow.

> **Item A** — A news report
>
> *Original extract, invented for this practice question.*
>
> "TERROR ON THE PROMENADE. Gangs of hooded teenagers on electric scooters brought fear to the seafront
> on Saturday, witnesses say. One shopkeeper said a window was cracked. Police confirmed two arrests
> for minor public order offences. Residents fear the summer will bring worse. 'They all wear the same
> black hoods,' said one. 'You know trouble when you see it.'"

**(a)** Using Item A, identify **one** example of exaggeration. **[1]**

**(b)** Using Item A and Cohen's work, explain how this report could contribute to a moral panic.
**[3]**

## Section B: longer questions

**6.** Explain the difference between **crime** and **deviance**, using an example of each. **[4]**

**7.** Explain what is meant by the social construction of crime, using an example of change over
time. **[4]**

**8.** Explain the difference between white-collar crime and corporate crime. **[4]**

**9.** Explain **two** reasons why state crimes are difficult to punish. **[6]**

**10.** Explain why sociologists disagree about whether some crimes are "victimless". **[6]**

**11.** Discuss how far crime and deviance are socially constructed. Use examples from your own
society or a society you know about. **[12]**

## Answers: Section A

**A1** [2 marks]

Behaviour that **breaks the norms** of a group or society [1], which may attract disapproval or other
informal sanctions even if it is not illegal [1].

**A2** [2 marks — 1 each]

Any two: illegal logging; wildlife trafficking; illegal dumping of waste; pollution beyond legal
limits; illegal fishing.

**A3** [2 marks]

Crime committed **by or on behalf of a company** [1] to benefit the organisation, such as price fixing
or breaking safety laws [1].

**A4** [2 marks — 1 each]

Any two: hacking; online fraud; phishing; ransomware; online harassment; identity theft online.

**A5 (a)** [1 mark]

Any one: the headline "TERROR ON THE PROMENADE"; "Gangs"; "brought fear", when only two arrests for
minor offences were made [1].

**A5 (b)** [3 marks]

Cohen found that the media exaggerate, predict further trouble and use symbols [1]. The report
exaggerates minor events and predicts that "the summer will bring worse" [1], and turns "black hoods"
and scooters into symbols of a threat, so teenagers who look similar may be treated as folk devils,
increasing public alarm and police attention [1].

## Answers: Section B

**A6** [4 marks]

**Crime** is behaviour that breaks the criminal law [1], such as burglary [1]. **Deviance** is
behaviour that breaks norms [1], such as queue-jumping, which is deviant but not criminal [1].

**A7** [4 marks]

Social construction means crime is not a fixed quality of an act [1] but is defined by a society's
laws and reactions, which change [1]. For example, smoking in enclosed public places was legal in
England until it was banned in 2007 [1], reflecting changing values about health and the rights of
non-smokers [1].

**A8** [4 marks]

White-collar crime is committed by **high-status individuals in the course of their work** [1] for
their own benefit, such as embezzlement (Sutherland) [1]. Corporate crime is committed **by or for a
company** [1] to benefit the organisation, such as price fixing or illegal pollution [1].

**A9** [6 marks — up to 3 per reason]

Two from: **the state controls the justice system** [1] — the police and courts that would prosecute
are part of the state [1], so officials may be protected [1]; **secrecy** [1] — governments can hide
evidence or restrict reporting [1], so crimes may not be known for years [1]; **power and
sovereignty** [1] — international courts depend on states' co-operation [1], so powerful states or
leaders may avoid trial [1]; **definition** [1] — states write the laws [1] and may make their own
actions legal [1].

**A10** [6 marks]

Some crimes, such as illegal drug use or illegal gambling, appear to have **no direct victim** [1],
because those involved take part willingly [1]. Some argue the law should not punish behaviour that
harms only the person doing it [1]. Critics argue there are **hidden victims** [1], such as families
affected by addiction or debt [1], and communities affected by related crime [1]. Maximum 6.

**A11** [12 marks — levels]

| Level | Marks | Descriptor |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | 10–12 | Detailed knowledge; well applied to a named society; balanced discussion with a clear, justified conclusion |
| 3 | 7–9 | Good knowledge; some application; discussion with a conclusion |
| 2 | 4–6 | Some relevant knowledge; limited application or one-sided |
| 1 | 1–3 | Basic, mainly descriptive points |

Indicative content:

- **Socially constructed:** differences between societies (alcohol, cannabis); changes over time
  (smoking, drink-driving, rape within marriage); Becker's labelling and selective enforcement;
  Cohen's moral panics; moral entrepreneurs.
- **Limits:** some acts, such as murder, are condemned in almost all societies; harm is real whatever
  the label; victims experience crime regardless of definitions.
- **Power:** who has the power to make laws and labels (Marxist and feminist points).
- **Application:** a named society, for example a recent change in its law and the values behind it.
- **Judgement:** for example, the boundaries of crime are socially constructed, though some serious
  harms are condemned almost everywhere.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating crime and deviance as the same.
- Confusing white-collar and corporate crime.
- Giving an example of change with no explanation of changing values.
- Describing Cohen's study without the stages of a moral panic.
- An extended answer with no named society.
