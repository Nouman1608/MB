## Section A

**1.** Define the time constant of a circuit in which a capacitor discharges through a resistor. **[2]**

**2.** A charged capacitor is connected across a resistor at time t = 0. Explain why the current in the resistor decreases exponentially with time. **[3]**

**3.** A 220 μF capacitor discharges through a 33 kΩ resistor. Calculate the time constant. **[1]**

**4.** The p.d. across a discharging capacitor falls from 12 V to 3.0 V in 8.0 s. Determine the time constant of the circuit. **[2]**

**5.** A capacitor discharges through resistor R. The experiment is repeated with the same starting p.d. but with a resistor of resistance 2R. Describe how the graph of p.d. against time for the second experiment differs from the first. **[2]**

**6.** Show that after three time constants the charge on a discharging capacitor is about 5% of its initial value. **[1]**

## Section B

**7.** A 1000 μF capacitor is charged to 6.0 V and then discharged through a 2.2 kΩ resistor.

**(a)** Calculate the time constant. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the initial current. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the p.d. across the capacitor 5.0 s after discharge begins. **[2]**

**(d)** Calculate the current at this time. **[1]**

**8.** A data-logger records the p.d. V across a capacitor as it discharges through a resistor.

| t / s | 0 | 5.0 | 10.0 | 15.0 | 20.0 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| V / V | 10.0 | 6.7 | 4.5 | 3.0 | 2.0 |

**(a)** Use the data to show that the decay is exponential. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the natural logarithm of each p.d. (with V in volts) and use the values to determine the time constant. **[3]**

**(c)** The capacitance is 100 μF. Calculate the resistance. **[1]**

**9.** A 0.47 F capacitor is used as a back-up supply for a clock circuit. It is charged to 5.0 V. The clock circuit behaves like a fixed resistor and draws 20 μA when the p.d. across it is 5.0 V. The clock stops working when the p.d. falls below 3.0 V.

**(a)** Calculate the resistance of the clock circuit. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the time constant. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate how long the clock runs on the back-up supply, in hours. **[2]**

**10.** A 470 μF capacitor charged to 12 V discharges through a 1.0 kΩ resistor.

**(a)** Calculate the initial energy stored. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the time constant. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the energy stored after one time constant. **[2]**

**(d)** Explain why the stored energy falls to 37% of its initial value in less than one time constant. **[1]**

**11.** A graph of current I against time t for a capacitor discharging through a resistor starts at 3.0 mA. The total area under the curve is found to be 6.0 mC. The initial p.d. across the capacitor was 12 V.

**(a)** State what the area under the curve represents. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the capacitance. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the resistance. **[1]**

**(d)** Calculate the time constant, and check that it is consistent with the area and the initial current. **[2]**

**(a)** Calculate the time constant when the capacitors are connected in parallel. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the time constant when the capacitors are connected in series. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why the series arrangement discharges faster. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** The time taken for the charge (or p.d. or current) to fall to 1/e, about 0.37, of its initial value [1]; it is equal to RC [1].

**2.** The p.d. across the resistor equals the p.d. across the capacitor, V = Q/C [1]. The current I = V/R = Q/(RC), so the rate at which charge leaves is proportional to the charge remaining [1]. A quantity that decreases at a rate proportional to its size decays exponentially, and I is proportional to Q, so I decays exponentially [1].

**3.** τ = RC = 33 × 10³ × 220 × 10⁻⁶ = 7.3 s [1].

**4.** 12 → 6.0 → 3.0 V is two half-lives, so t½ = 4.0 s [1]; τ = 4.0 ÷ 0.693 = 5.8 s [1].

**5.** Both curves start at the same p.d. [1], but the second curve falls more slowly: its time constant is doubled, so it takes twice as long to reach any given fraction of the starting value [1].

**6.** e⁻³ = 0.050, which is 5% [1].

**7. (a)** τ = 2.2 × 10³ × 1000 × 10⁻⁶ = 2.2 s [1].

**(b)** I₀ = V₀/R = 6.0 ÷ 2200 = 2.7 × 10⁻³ A [1].

**(c)** V = 6.0e^(−5.0/2.2) [1] = 6.0 × 0.103 = 0.62 V [1].

**(d)** I = V/R = 0.618 ÷ 2200 = 2.8 × 10⁻⁴ A [1].

**(e)** t = RC ln(V₀/V) = 2.2 × ln(6.0/0.50) [1] = 2.2 × 2.48 = 5.5 s [1].

**8. (a)** Ratios for equal 5.0 s intervals: 6.7/10.0 = 0.67, 4.5/6.7 = 0.67, 3.0/4.5 = 0.67, 2.0/3.0 = 0.67 [1]. The ratio is constant for equal time intervals, so the decay is exponential [1].

**(b)** ln values: 2.30, 1.90, 1.50, 1.10, 0.69 [1]. Gradient = (0.69 − 2.30) ÷ 20.0 = −0.080 s⁻¹ [1]. Gradient = −1/RC, so RC = 1 ÷ 0.080 = 12.5 s (accept 12 s to 13 s) [1].

**(c)** R = τ/C = 12.5 ÷ 100 × 10⁻⁶ = 1.25 × 10⁵ Ω [1].

**9. (a)** R = V/I = 5.0 ÷ 20 × 10⁻⁶ = 2.5 × 10⁵ Ω [1].

**(b)** τ = RC = 2.5 × 10⁵ × 0.47 = 1.2 × 10⁵ s [1].

**(c)** t = RC ln(5.0/3.0) = 1.175 × 10⁵ × 0.511 = 6.0 × 10⁴ s [1]; 6.0 × 10⁴ ÷ 3600 = 17 hours [1].

**10. (a)** W = ½CV² = ½ × 470 × 10⁻⁶ × 12² = 0.034 J [1].

**(b)** τ = 1.0 × 10³ × 470 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.47 s [1].

**(c)** After one time constant V = 12e⁻¹ = 4.4 V [1]; W = ½ × 470 × 10⁻⁶ × 4.41² = 4.6 × 10⁻³ J (or 0.034 × e⁻² = 4.6 × 10⁻³ J) [1].

**(d)** Energy is proportional to V², so it decays as e^(−2t/RC), with an effective time constant of RC/2 [1].

**11. (a)** The charge that flowed off the capacitor (its initial charge Q₀) [1].

**(b)** C = Q₀/V₀ = 6.0 × 10⁻³ ÷ 12 = 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ F [1].

**(c)** R = V₀/I₀ = 12 ÷ 3.0 × 10⁻³ = 4.0 × 10³ Ω [1].

**(d)** τ = RC = 4.0 × 10³ × 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ = 2.0 s [1]. For exponential decay, total area = I₀τ = 3.0 × 10⁻³ × 2.0 = 6.0 × 10⁻³ C, which matches [1].

**12. (a)** Parallel: C = 100 μF; τ = 100 × 10³ × 100 × 10⁻⁶ = 10 s [1].

**(b)** Series: C = 25 μF; τ = 100 × 10³ × 25 × 10⁻⁶ = 2.5 s [1].

**(c)** The series combination has a smaller capacitance, so it stores less charge at the same p.d. while the initial current V/R is the same, so the charge is removed in a shorter time [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing the time constant as the time for complete discharge.
- Using RC instead of 0.693RC for the half-life, or the reverse.
- Forgetting prefix conversions: kΩ × μF gives milliseconds, not seconds.
- Showing an exponential decay with only one ratio; at least three equal-interval ratios are needed.
- Reading the gradient of a ln V–t graph as RC rather than −1/RC.
- Assuming energy falls with the same time constant as p.d.
