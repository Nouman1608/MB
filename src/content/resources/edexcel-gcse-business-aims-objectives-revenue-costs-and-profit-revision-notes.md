## 1.3.1 Aims and objectives at a glance

| Term | Meaning (standard definitions) |
|---|---|
| Business aim | A long-term, general goal for the business |
| Business objective | A specific, usually measurable target that moves the business towards its aim |

### Financial and non-financial

| Type | Listed in the specification | One-line reminder |
|---|---|---|
| Financial | Survival | Stay open and cover costs |
| Financial | Profit | Revenue above total costs |
| Financial | Sales | Sell more units or earn more sales revenue |
| Financial | Market share | A bigger proportion of the whole market |
| Financial | Financial security | A dependable income and a cash cushion |
| Non-financial | Social objectives | Help people or the community |
| Non-financial | Personal satisfaction | Enjoy the work |
| Non-financial | Challenge | Test yourself with something hard |
| Non-financial | Independence and control | Be your own boss and decide |

### Why aims and objectives differ between businesses

Learn five reasons and apply one to the case in front of you.

1. Stage: new businesses often start with survival.
2. Owner's motives: independence or personal satisfaction can outweigh profit.
3. Size and ownership: investors tend to press for profit.
4. Type of business: community projects may add social objectives.
5. Market conditions: tough competition can move the focus to survival.

### Must-know distinctions

- **Sales vs profit.** Sales can rise while profit falls, for example after a price cut.
- **Market share vs sales.** Market share compares the business with the whole market. Sales only count the business.
- **Aim vs objective.** An aim gives direction. An objective is the measurable step.
- **Survival vs financial security.** Survival is staying open. Financial security is a stable income and a cushion.

## 1.3.2 Formulae (Appendix 3 forms)

| Item | Formula |
|---|---|
| Revenue | price × quantity |
| Total cost | TC = TFC + TVC |
| Profit or loss | revenue − total costs (standard form); a negative answer is a loss |
| Interest on loans (%) | (total repayment − borrowed amount) ÷ borrowed amount × 100 |
| Break even point in units | fixed cost ÷ (sales price − variable cost) |
| Break even point in costs or revenue | break even point in units × sales price |
| Margin of safety | actual or budgeted sales − break even sales |

### Fixed or variable?

- Fixed: does not change with output in the period. Examples: rent, insurance, a loan interest bill, a software subscription.
- Variable: changes as output changes. Examples: raw materials, packaging, parts.

## Method in steps

**Break even and margin of safety**

1. Work out the contribution per unit: price − variable cost per unit.
2. Divide fixed costs by the contribution. That is the break even output in units.
3. Multiply by price if the question asks for break even revenue.
4. Subtract break even output from actual or budgeted sales for the margin of safety.
5. Write the units, and round up if the answer is not a whole number.

**Reading a break even diagram**

1. Find where total revenue crosses total costs. Read down to the output axis.
2. At any output, the vertical gap between the two lines is the profit (revenue above) or loss (revenue below).
3. Margin of safety runs along the output axis, from break even output across to planned output.
4. A new price moves the revenue line. New fixed costs lift the start of the total costs line. New variable costs change its slope.

## Worked reminder: Kestrel Phone Repairs

Kestrel charges £40 a repair. Variable cost is £15 a repair. Fixed costs are £5,000 a month. It plans to carry out 260 repairs.

| Step | Working | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | £40 × 260 | £10,400 |
| TVC | £15 × 260 | £3,900 |
| TC | £5,000 + £3,900 | £8,900 |
| Profit | £10,400 − £8,900 | £1,500 |
| Break even | £5,000 ÷ (£40 − £15) | 200 repairs |
| Break even revenue | 200 × £40 | £8,000 |
| Margin of safety | 260 − 200 | 60 repairs |

### Effects of changes

| Change | What moves | Break even output |
|---|---|---|
| Price rises to £45 | Revenue line steeper | £5,000 ÷ £30 = 166.67, so 167 |
| Fixed costs rise to £6,000 | Total costs line starts higher | £6,000 ÷ £25 = 240 |
| Variable cost falls to £12 | Total costs line flatter | £5,000 ÷ £28 = 178.57, so 179 |

Costs rising means break even output rises and the margin of safety falls. Prices rising does the opposite, as long as customers keep buying.

## Using the command words

Appendix 1 of the specification defines the command words. These are the ones this topic tends to use:

- **Define:** give the meaning of a term from the content, such as variable costs.
- **State** and **Identify:** a short answer taken from the information or a graph or table.
- **Calculate:** use the given data. Workings should be shown.
- **Complete the table:** work out the missing values in a table of data.
- **Outline:** two connected points set in the business context given.
- **Explain:** one statement of fact plus two further points that expand on it.
- **Analyse:** an extended answer placed in context.
- **Justify:** draw on the data supplied to back one of two options for the owner.
- **Evaluate:** a longer answer using subject knowledge to arrive at a conclusion that is backed up.

## Sentence starters for diagram and change questions

These are the writer's suggestions, not specification wording.

- "Break even output is where the revenue line meets the total costs line, which is at ... units."
- "At ... units the business makes a loss, because total costs are above revenue."
- "The margin of safety is ... units, so sales could fall by this much before a loss."
- "A rise in fixed costs lifts the total costs line, so break even output rises and the cushion shrinks."
- "This only helps if customers keep buying at the new price."

## Quick self-test

Use the Kestrel data (price £40, variable cost £15, fixed costs £5,000) unless a question says otherwise.

1. State the difference between a business aim and a business objective.
2. Give one financial and one non-financial objective from the specification.
3. Calculate the revenue from 260 repairs.
4. Calculate total costs for 260 repairs.
5. Calculate the profit for 260 repairs.
6. Calculate the break even level of output.
7. Calculate break even revenue.
8. Calculate the margin of safety when 260 repairs are planned.
9. Calculate the profit or loss if only 150 repairs are carried out.
10. Calculate the new break even output if fixed costs rise to £6,000.
11. A different owner borrows £4,000 and repays £4,360. Calculate the interest as a percentage.
12. Explain one reason why a new business might set survival as its first objective.

### Answers

1. An aim is a long-term, general goal. An objective is a specific, measurable target that helps reach it.
2. Any financial one from survival, profit, sales, market share, financial security. Any non-financial one from social objectives, personal satisfaction, challenge, independence and control.
3. £40 × 260 = £10,400.
4. £5,000 + (£15 × 260) = £5,000 + £3,900 = £8,900.
5. £10,400 − £8,900 = £1,500.
6. £5,000 ÷ (£40 − £15) = £5,000 ÷ £25 = 200 repairs.
7. 200 × £40 = £8,000.
8. 260 − 200 = 60 repairs.
9. Revenue £6,000 (150 × £40). Total costs £5,000 + £2,250 = £7,250. Loss of £1,250.
10. £6,000 ÷ £25 = 240 repairs.
11. Interest = £4,360 − £4,000 = £360. £360 ÷ £4,000 × 100 = 9%.
12. A new firm has little cash and few regular customers, so one bad month could force it to close. Setting survival first means it prioritises covering its costs before chasing profit or growth.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Mixing up aim and objective, or giving an objective with no target in it.
- Naming an objective from the list without connecting it to the firm in the stem.
- Treating more sales as automatically more profit.
- Saying aims are the same for every business, when the question asks why they differ.
- Using the selling price, and not the contribution per unit, as the divisor in the break even formula.
- Swapping unit variable cost and total variable cost.
- Drawing the total costs line from the origin.
- Reading the margin of safety from the wrong axis, or giving a revenue figure when units were asked for.
- Leaving out units or the £ sign after a calculation.
- Saying how a change shifts the lines without stating the consequence for the owner.
