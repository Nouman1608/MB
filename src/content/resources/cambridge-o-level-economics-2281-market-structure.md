This guide covers section 3.8 **Market structure** of the Cambridge O Level Economics 2281 syllabus for examination in 2026. The syllabus has two parts. **Competitive markets** asks for "the effect of having a high number of firms on price, quality, choice, profit", and notes that "the theory of perfect and imperfect competition and diagrams are not required". **Monopoly markets** asks for the "characteristics, advantages and disadvantages of monopoly", again with no diagrams.

The combined [trade unions, factor demand and market structure guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-trade-unions-factor-demand-labour-and-capital/) lists the effects of competition and the features of monopoly. This page goes further: it measures how concentrated a market is, follows what happens when new firms enter a profitable market, explains each kind of barrier to entry, and gives you a framework for judging whether a particular monopoly is good or bad for consumers. Everything is in words and numbers, as the syllabus requires. Companion pages: [market structure revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-market-structure-revision-notes/) and [market structure practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-market-structure-practice/). All firms and figures are invented.

## What the syllabus asks for

| Section | You must be able to |
|---|---|
| 3.8.1 Competitive markets | Explain how a high number of firms affects price, quality, choice and profit |
| 3.8.2 Monopoly markets | Describe the characteristics of monopoly and explain its advantages and disadvantages |

**Market structure** means the features of a market that affect how firms behave, above all the number and size of the firms and how easily new firms can enter.

## Measuring how concentrated a market is

**Market share** is a firm's sales as a percentage of total market sales.

### Worked example 1

Annual sales of mobile data in an invented country:

| Firm | Sales ($ million) | Market share |
|---|---|---|
| Firm 1 | 540 | 540 ÷ 900 = 60.0% |
| Firm 2 | 180 | 20.0% |
| Firm 3 | 120 | 13.3% |
| Ten small firms together | 60 | 6.7% |
| Total | 900 | 100% |

There are 13 firms, so the market is not a pure monopoly, but the largest three hold 93.3% of sales and Firm 1 alone holds 60%. Counting firms can mislead: what matters is whether buyers have real alternatives. A market with a dominant firm can behave much like a monopoly even if many small rivals exist.

## 3.8.1 Competitive markets

A **competitive market** has a high number of firms, each with a small share, selling similar products, with buyers able to compare them and new firms able to enter.

### The four effects

| Effect | What happens | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Price | Kept low, close to costs | A firm that charges more than rivals loses customers to them |
| Quality | Pushed up | Firms try to attract buyers with better products and service |
| Choice | Wide | Many firms offer different versions, sizes, styles and locations |
| Profit | Low in the long run | High profit attracts new firms, which competes it away |

### Worked example 2: entry competes profit away

A small town has one bakery. It charges $3 a loaf, its cost is $2 a loaf and it sells 1,000 loaves a day, so its profit is (3 − 2) × 1,000 = $1,000 a day.

Seeing this profit, two new bakeries open. To win customers, all three cut their price to $2.40. Each now sells 400 loaves, a total of 1,200.

- Each bakery's profit: (2.40 − 2.00) × 400 = $160 a day; together $480.
- Consumers pay 20% less per loaf and buy 20% more bread.
- Total profit in the market has fallen from $1,000 to $480 a day.

This shows the link between all four effects. Lower prices and wider choice for consumers come with lower profit for each firm. If price falls below cost, the weakest firms leave.

### Limits of competition

Competition is not always best. Each small firm may be too small to gain **economies of scale**, so its costs per unit stay high. Low profits leave little money for research and new products. Some "choice" may be only small differences in packaging. And small firms may cut corners on safety or quality if buyers cannot check.

## 3.8.2 Monopoly markets

A **monopoly** is a market with a single supplier, or one firm so dominant that it controls the market. In practice economists also describe firms with a very large market share as having monopoly power.

### Characteristics

- **One seller (or one dominant seller)** of the product.
- **No close substitutes**, so buyers cannot easily switch.
- **Barriers to entry** that stop new firms competing.
- **The firm is a price maker**: it can choose its price, although buyers then decide how much to buy at that price.
- **High profits can last** in the long run, because barriers stop rivals entering to compete them away.

### Barriers to entry

| Barrier | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Legal barriers | The law gives one firm the sole right to supply | Patents on a new medicine; a licence to run the only national postal service |
| High start-up costs | New firms cannot raise the huge sums needed | Building a steel works or a railway network |
| Economies of scale | The established firm's costs per unit are so low that a small entrant cannot match its price | A large electricity generator |
| Control of resources or outlets | The firm owns the key raw material or the shops through which the product is sold | A firm owning the only local quarry |
| Brand loyalty and advertising | Buyers stay with a familiar brand, so entrants must spend heavily to be noticed | A long-established soft drink brand |

A **natural monopoly** arises where one firm can supply the whole market at a lower cost than two or more firms could. Water supply is the usual example: one network of pipes serves every house, and building a second network alongside it would double the cost without adding any water.

### Advantages of monopoly

- **Economies of scale**: a single large firm can have lower costs per unit, which can mean lower prices.
- **Funds for research**: lasting profits can pay for research and new products; patents reward the risk of inventing.
- **No wasteful duplication**: in natural monopolies one network is cheaper than several.
- **Stability**: a large firm may be better able to survive bad years and keep supply going.

### Disadvantages of monopoly

- **Higher prices and lower output**: with no rivals, the firm can raise prices; some buyers then go without.
- **Less choice**: buyers must accept the monopoly's product or go without.
- **Less pressure on quality and costs**: without competition, a monopoly may become complacent, letting service or efficiency slip.
- **Exploiting suppliers and workers**: as the only buyer of some inputs, it may pay low prices or wages.
- **Profits may not be passed on**: economies of scale only help consumers if lower costs lead to lower prices.

## Judging a monopoly

A strong answer weighs the advantages against the disadvantages in the case given:

- **Are economies of scale passed on?** Lower costs help consumers only if prices fall.
- **Is it regulated?** Governments may control prices or service standards in monopoly industries.
- **Could new firms enter?** Even a single firm keeps prices down if it fears new rivals.
- **Are there substitutes?** A monopoly rail company still competes with buses and cars.
- **Is it a natural monopoly?** If one network is cheapest, competition may waste resources.

## Common mistakes

- Drawing diagrams. They are not required for this section and earn nothing extra.
- Saying a competitive market means "lots of firms" without explaining the effect on price, quality, choice or profit.
- Calling every large firm a monopoly. Check the market share and whether buyers have alternatives.
- Saying a monopoly can charge any price and still sell the same amount. Buyers buy less at higher prices.
- Writing only disadvantages of monopoly when asked to discuss.

## Exam technique for 3.8

- **Describe** monopoly with its characteristics: one seller, no close substitutes, barriers to entry, price maker.
- **Explain** each effect of competition with its mechanism: "firms that charge more lose customers to rivals, so prices stay close to costs".
- **Calculate** market shares and changes in profit, showing the division.
- **Analyse** a barrier to entry by saying why it stops a new firm competing.
- **Discuss** whether a monopoly is in consumers' interests: give advantages, disadvantages and a judgement based on regulation, substitutes and whether lower costs are passed on.
