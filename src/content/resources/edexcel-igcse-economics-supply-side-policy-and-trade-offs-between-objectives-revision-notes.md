## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Supply-side policy | Government action to raise productive capacity by making resources more productive or more plentiful |
| Productivity | Output per worker (or per hour) |
| Total output | All the goods and services an economy produces |
| Productive potential | The most an economy can produce when resources are fully used |
| Trade-off | Improving one objective makes another worse |
| Percentage points | The difference between two rates (unemployment 8% to 5% is down 3 points) |

## Productivity and output

- Productivity = total output / number of workers.
- % change = (new - old) / old x 100.
- Higher productivity means more output from the same resources, or the same output at lower cost.
- On a production possibility frontier, higher productive potential moves the curve outwards.

Reminder: 4,600 loaves from 8 workers is 575 each. From 500 each, that is a rise of 15%.

## The seven policies at a glance

| Policy | Chain | Main gain | Main limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Privatisation | Private owners seek profit, cut waste, face competition | Lower cost, higher productivity | Private monopoly, job losses, neglect of unprofitable services |
| Deregulation | Fewer rules, easier entry | More firms, more competition, more jobs | Less protection for safety and environment |
| Education and training | Better skills | Higher productivity, less structural unemployment | Slow, costly, skills may not match jobs |
| Regional policy | Grants and incentives for areas of high unemployment | Jobs where they are needed | Firms may leave, skills mismatch, opportunity cost |
| Infrastructure | Lower transport and communication costs | More investment, faster deliveries | High cost, long build time |
| Lower business taxes | Firms keep more profit | More investment, capital grows | May go to dividends, weak demand, less tax revenue |
| Lower income tax | Higher take-home pay | More people work or work longer | Behaviour may not change, less tax revenue |

## Method: explaining a supply-side policy

1. Name the policy and say what the government does.
2. Say what firms or workers do in response.
3. Link this to productivity or output.
4. Link to a named objective (growth, unemployment, inflation, current account).
5. Add a limit or condition, then a short judgement.

## Small worked reminders

**Lower income tax.** Gross 2,000, tax 20%: net = 2,000 x 0.80 = 1,600. At 15%: net = 1,700. Rise = 100 / 1,600 x 100 = 6.25%.

**Lower business tax.** Profit 80,000: at 25% tax it keeps 60,000, at 20% it keeps 64,000, so it keeps 4,000 more.

**Regional unemployment.** 24,000 unemployed out of 200,000 gives 12%. After 6,000 find work, 18,000 / 200,000 = 9%.

**Infrastructure.** A trip falls from 5 hours to 3.5 hours: (5 - 3.5) / 5 x 100 = 30% quicker.

**Privatisation.** Average cost = total cost / output. 4,000,000 / 100,000 = 40. A 10% cost fall gives 3,600,000 / 100,000 = 36.

## Trade-offs at a glance

| Pair | Why it arises | How it can be eased |
|---|---|---|
| Unemployment and inflation | Cutting unemployment through higher demand uses up spare labour, so wages and prices rise | Supply-side policies that raise output without pressure on prices |
| Growth and inflation | Demand grows faster than supply, so shortages push prices up | Growth that comes from productive potential |
| Growth and environmental protection | More output uses more resources and causes more pollution; rules raise costs | Cleaner technology, so emissions per unit fall |
| Inflation and current account | High domestic inflation makes exports dear and imports cheap, moving the balance towards deficit | Lower inflation, or a fall in the exchange rate |

### Calculation reminders

```
Unemployment rate = unemployed / labour force x 100
   800,000 / 10,000,000 = 8%
Real GDP = nominal GDP / price index x 100
   856.8 / 102 x 100 = 840
Trade balance = exports - imports
   108 - 112 = -4 (a deficit)
Emissions per unit of GDP = emissions / GDP
   40.8 / 520 = 0.078
```

Percentage change in exports: (108 - 120) / 120 x 100 = -10%.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Supply-side versus demand-side**: supply-side policy increases the ability to produce. Fiscal and monetary policy mainly change spending and are covered separately.
- **Percentage change versus percentage points**: unemployment moving from 8% to 5% is a fall of 3 points, but a fall of 37.5% in the rate.
- **Nominal versus real**: nominal GDP includes price changes. Real GDP removes them.
- **Structural versus cyclical unemployment**: training and regional policy help the first. Higher demand helps the second.
- **Short run versus long run**: trade-offs are sharp in the short run. Supply-side policy works over years.

## Model chains to learn

**Lower unemployment, higher inflation.** The government raises spending, so demand rises. Firms hire more workers, and unemployment falls. As spare workers run out, firms offer higher wages to attract staff. Costs rise and firms pass them on in prices, so inflation rises. The trade-off is sharper when the economy is close to full employment.

**Training eases the trade-off.** Workers gain skills, so output per worker rises. Firms can fill vacancies without bidding wages up. Output grows without extra pressure on prices, so unemployment and inflation can both stay low. The gain takes years to appear.

**Growth and the environment.** Higher output needs more energy and materials, so emissions and waste rise. A government that limits pollution raises costs for firms, which can slow growth. The judgement depends on whether cleaner technology is available and on how much the country values present income against future damage.

**Inflation and the current account.** If prices at home rise faster than abroad, foreign buyers switch away from our exports, and domestic buyers switch towards imports. The trade balance moves towards deficit. Higher imports are a leakage from the circular flow, and a large deficit can put pressure on the exchange rate.

## Evaluation toolkit

- It depends on the size of the spare capacity.
- It depends on how quickly firms and workers respond.
- Cost, and what else the money could do.
- Short-run effects can differ from long-run effects.
- Which objective matters most to the country?
- End with a supported judgement: say which factor decides the outcome.

## Quick self-test

1. Define supply-side policy.
2. A workshop's 20 staff make 3,000 chairs; later they make 3,300. Find the percentage change in productivity.
3. Give one reason privatisation might not raise efficiency.
4. A region has 150,000 in its labour force and 12,000 unemployed. Find its unemployment rate.
5. Explain how infrastructure spending could attract investment.
6. A firm makes 50,000 profit. Tax falls from 30% to 25%. How much more profit does it keep?
7. Name one reason a cut in income tax might not raise labour supply.
8. Unemployment falls from 6% to 4% and inflation rises from 2% to 3.5%. State both changes in percentage points.
9. Nominal GDP rises from 200 to 231 while prices rise 5%. Find real growth.
10. Exports 90 to 81 and imports 80 to 88. Find the new balance.
11. State a way economic growth can harm the environment.

### Answers

1. Government action to raise the economy's productive capacity (productivity and output).
2. Before 3,000 / 20 = 150; after 3,300 / 20 = 165. Change = 15 / 150 = 10%.
3. A private monopoly may raise prices and not cut costs, because there is no competition.
4. 12,000 / 150,000 x 100 = 8%.
5. Better roads or ports cut transport costs and delays, so locating in the area becomes more profitable.
6. At 30% it keeps 35,000. At 25% it keeps 37,500. It keeps 2,500 more.
7. Workers who reach their income target sooner may work fewer hours. Or no extra jobs may be available.
8. Unemployment down 2 percentage points; inflation up 1.5 percentage points.
9. Real GDP = 231 / 1.05 = 220. Growth = 20 / 200 = 10%.
10. 81 - 88 = -7, a deficit of 7.
11. More output means more resources used, more pollution and faster depletion of natural resources.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a policy without explaining the chain to output or productivity.
- Defining supply-side policy as cutting interest rates or changing government spending on demand.
- Reversing the trade-off, or stating it as "both rise" without a reason.
- Quoting a percentage change when the question asks for percentage points.
- Quoting nominal growth as if it were real growth.
- Writing a calculation answer with no working or no units.
- Listing policies without applying them to the country or context in the question.
- Ending an evaluation answer with no judgement, or a judgement not supported by what came before.
