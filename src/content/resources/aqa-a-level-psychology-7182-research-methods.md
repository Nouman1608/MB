**Research methods is the part of AQA A-level Psychology (7182) that teaches how psychologists design studies, collect and analyse data, and judge whether findings are trustworthy.** It is compulsory content examined on Paper 2: Psychology in Context, and research methods skills are also assessed across all three papers. You need to know each method, the scientific processes behind good research, descriptive statistics, and how to choose and interpret inferential tests.

This guide follows the AQA AS and A-level Psychology (7181, 7182) specification, Version 1.4 (1 September 2025), for teaching from September 2025. Research methods is section 4.2.3 of the specification (with 4.2.3.1 Scientific processes, 4.2.3.2 Data handling and analysis and 4.2.3.3 Inferential testing) and topic 7 in the A-level subject content. Use it with the [Research methods revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-psychology-7182-research-methods-revision-notes/) and [Research methods practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-psychology-7182-research-methods-practice/). The other Paper 2 topics are in the [Approaches in Psychology guide](/resources/a-level-aqa-psychology-approaches-in-psychology/) and the [Biopsychology guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-psychology-7182-biopsychology/).

## How is research methods examined?

Paper 2 is a 2-hour exam worth 96 marks and 33.3% of the A-level, assessing compulsory content 5 to 7. Its specification lists Section A and Section B at 24 marks each and Section C at 48 marks, all "multiple choice, short answer and extended writing"; Section C, the largest, follows Approaches and Biopsychology in the content order. The specification also states that knowledge and understanding of research methods, practical research skills and mathematical skills are assessed in every paper, and the specification's mathematical requirements annex sets the maths you must be able to do.

## Which research methods do you need to know?

**Experiments.** An experiment manipulates an **independent variable (IV)** and measures its effect on a **dependent variable (DV)**, while controlling other variables.

| Type | Definition | Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Laboratory | IV manipulated in a controlled, artificial setting | High control; cause and effect; easy to replicate | Low ecological validity; demand characteristics |
| Field | IV manipulated in a natural setting | Higher ecological validity; participants may be unaware | Less control; ethical issues of consent |
| Natural | IV varies naturally (e.g. a policy change); researcher records the effect | Studies variables that could not ethically be manipulated | No random allocation; event may be rare |
| Quasi | IV is a characteristic of participants (e.g. age, gender) | Allows comparison of existing groups | Participant variables confound; no random allocation |

**Observation.** **Naturalistic** observation happens where the behaviour normally occurs; **controlled** observation sets up some conditions. **Covert** observation is hidden; **overt** observation is known to participants. In **participant** observation the researcher joins the group; in **non-participant** observation they watch from outside. Covert observation reduces participant reactivity but raises consent issues; participant observation gives insight but risks losing objectivity.

**Self-report.** **Questionnaires** use written questions; they reach many people quickly but can suffer social desirability bias. **Structured interviews** use fixed questions in a fixed order, which makes them easy to compare. **Unstructured interviews** follow the conversation, which gives rich detail but makes analysis harder and depends on the interviewer's skill.

**Correlations.** A correlation measures the relationship between two **co-variables**. Unlike an experiment, nothing is manipulated, so correlation cannot show cause and effect; a third variable may explain the link.

**Content analysis** turns qualitative material (texts, media, interview transcripts) into data by coding it into categories. **Case studies** investigate one person, group or event in depth, often over time; they give rich, idiographic data but cannot easily be generalised.

## What are the scientific processes?

**Aims and hypotheses.** An **aim** is a general statement of what the study investigates. A **hypothesis** is a precise, testable prediction. A **directional** hypothesis states the direction of the difference or relationship ("participants who sleep 8 hours will recall more words than those who sleep 4 hours"); a **non-directional** hypothesis predicts a difference without stating which way. Directional hypotheses are usually used when previous research suggests the outcome.

**Sampling.** The **population** is the group the researcher wants to study; the **sample** is the people who actually take part.

| Technique | How it works | Main issue |
|---|---|---|
| Random | Every member of the population has an equal chance (e.g. random number generator) | Needs a full list; may still be unrepresentative by chance |
| Systematic | Every nth person from a list | Unbiased if the list has no pattern |
| Stratified | Sub-groups sampled in proportion to the population | Representative but time-consuming |
| Opportunity | Whoever is available | Quick but biased |
| Volunteer | People who respond to an advert | Easy, but volunteer bias |

Bias limits **generalisation** from sample to population.

**Pilot studies** are small-scale trial runs that check procedures, materials and timings, so problems can be fixed before the main study.

**Experimental designs.** **Repeated measures**: each participant does every condition; controls participant variables but risks order effects, handled by **counterbalancing**. **Independent groups**: different participants in each condition; no order effects but participant variables can confound, reduced by **random allocation**. **Matched pairs**: participants are matched on a key variable and one of each pair goes into each condition; controls key participant variables but matching is time-consuming.

**Observational design.** **Behavioural categories** break behaviour into observable, measurable units. **Event sampling** records every time a behaviour occurs; **time sampling** records behaviour at set intervals.

**Questionnaire and interview design.** **Open questions** give qualitative answers in the participant's own words; **closed questions** give fixed choices and quantitative data. Good design avoids leading, double-barrelled and jargon-filled questions. Interviews need a schedule, a suitable setting and a plan for recording.

**Variables.** The IV is manipulated; the DV is measured. **Extraneous variables** are any other variables that might affect the DV; when they do affect it systematically, they become **confounding variables**. **Operationalisation** means defining variables in measurable terms, such as "number of words recalled out of 20".

**Control.** **Random allocation** to conditions, **counterbalancing** (e.g. ABBA), **randomisation** of materials or order and **standardisation** (same instructions and procedures for all participants) reduce bias.

**Demand characteristics** are cues that let participants guess the aim and change their behaviour. **Investigator effects** are any influence of the researcher on the result, such as tone of voice. Single-blind and double-blind procedures reduce them.

**Ethics.** The **British Psychological Society (BPS) code of ethics** guides research. Key issues are informed consent, deception, protection from harm, privacy and confidentiality, and the right to withdraw. Ways of dealing with them include presumptive or prior general consent, full debriefing, cost–benefit judgements by an ethics committee, and anonymising data.

**Peer review** is the assessment of a study by other experts before publication. It checks validity and quality and prevents fraudulent or poor research being published, but reviewers may be biased towards findings that fit current theories, and positive results are more likely to be published (publication bias).

**Implications for the economy.** Research can have economic benefits, such as treatments that help people with mental health problems return to work, or findings that reduce absence or improve productivity.

**Reliability** is consistency. **Test–retest** reliability gives the same test to the same people on two occasions and correlates the scores. **Inter-observer reliability** compares two observers' records; a correlation of +0.80 or above is usually taken as good. Reliability can be improved by clearer behavioural categories, observer training and standardised procedures.

**Validity** is whether a study measures what it claims to measure. **Face validity**: the measure looks as if it measures the right thing. **Concurrent validity**: results match those of an established measure. **Ecological validity**: findings generalise to real-life settings. **Temporal validity**: findings remain true over time. Validity is improved by control groups, standardisation, blind procedures, anonymous questionnaires and triangulation.

**Features of science.** Science relies on **objectivity** and the **empirical method** (knowledge from direct observation and experiment). It requires **replicability** (repeating studies to check findings) and **falsifiability** (Popper: a theory must be capable of being proved wrong). **Theory construction and hypothesis testing** move between theory and evidence. Kuhn argued that a science has a shared **paradigm**, and that a **paradigm shift** happens when contradictory evidence builds up enough to replace it.

**Reporting.** A scientific report has an **abstract** (short summary), **introduction** (literature review and hypotheses), **method** (design, sample, materials, procedure, ethics), **results** (descriptive and inferential statistics), **discussion** (interpretation, limitations, implications) and **referencing** (sources in a standard format).

## How do you handle and analyse data?

**Types of data.** **Quantitative** data are numerical; **qualitative** data are descriptive (words, meanings). **Primary** data are collected first-hand for the study; **secondary** data were collected by someone else. A **meta-analysis** combines the results of many studies to find an overall effect size.

**Descriptive statistics.** The **mean** adds all scores and divides by the number of scores; it uses all data but is distorted by extreme values. The **median** is the middle value in order; it is not affected by outliers. The **mode** is the most frequent value. The **range** is the highest minus the lowest score (often plus 1 in some conventions; follow your teacher's guidance); it is easy to calculate but affected by extremes. The **standard deviation** measures average spread around the mean; a larger value means more spread. You must be able to calculate mean, median, mode, range and percentages.

**Displaying data.** **Tables** summarise; **bar charts** show discrete categories with gaps between bars; **histograms** show continuous data with no gaps; **scattergrams** show correlations; line graphs show change over time.

**Distributions.** In a **normal distribution**, the curve is symmetrical and the mean, median and mode are at the same point. In a **positive skew**, most scores are low with a long tail to the right, and the mean is higher than the median, which is higher than the mode. In a **negative skew**, most scores are high with a tail to the left, and the mean is lowest.

**Correlation coefficients** range from -1 (perfect negative) through 0 (no correlation) to +1 (perfect positive). The size shows strength; the sign shows direction.

**Levels of measurement.** **Nominal** data are categories (counts in groups). **Ordinal** data can be ranked but intervals are not equal (ratings). **Interval** data use units of equal size (time, temperature).

**Content analysis, coding and thematic analysis.** In **coding**, the researcher develops categories and counts how often each occurs, producing quantitative data. In **thematic analysis**, the researcher identifies recurring themes and keeps the data qualitative.

## How does inferential testing work?

**The sign test** is used for a difference, with related data (repeated measures or matched pairs) at nominal level. To calculate it: record a + or - for each participant's change, ignore any zero differences, count the less frequent sign (this is S), and compare S with the critical value for the number of non-zero differences (N). If S is **equal to or less than** the critical value, the result is significant.

**Probability and significance.** Psychologists usually use a significance level of **p ≤ 0.05**: a 5% or lower probability that the result occurred by chance if the null hypothesis is true. A stricter level such as 0.01 is used when a mistake would be costly. Statistical tables give **critical values**; you need to know whether the hypothesis is one-tailed (directional) or two-tailed (non-directional), N (or degrees of freedom) and the significance level. For some tests the calculated value must be equal to or greater than the critical value; for others (including the sign test, Wilcoxon and Mann-Whitney) it must be equal to or less.

**Type I and Type II errors.** A **Type I error** is a false positive: rejecting the null hypothesis when it is true, more likely with a lenient level such as 0.10. A **Type II error** is a false negative: accepting the null hypothesis when it is false, more likely with a strict level such as 0.01.

**Choosing a test.** The specification states that the choice depends on factors including level of measurement and experimental design.

| | Unrelated design (independent groups) | Related design (repeated measures, matched pairs) | Correlation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nominal** | Chi-squared | Sign test | Chi-squared |
| **Ordinal** | Mann-Whitney | Wilcoxon | Spearman's rho |
| **Interval** | Unrelated t-test | Related t-test | Pearson's r |

The three t-tests and Pearson's r are **parametric** tests: they need interval data, a roughly normal distribution and similar variance between groups.

## Common errors

- Writing hypotheses that are not operationalised ("memory will be better").
- Confusing opportunity and volunteer sampling.
- Saying counterbalancing removes order effects; it balances them across conditions.
- Confusing ecological validity with generalisability to other people.
- Using a bar chart for continuous data, or a histogram for categories.
- Forgetting that for the sign test, Wilcoxon and Mann-Whitney the calculated value must be equal to or less than the critical value.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between reliability and validity?**
Reliability is consistency: getting the same result again under the same conditions. Validity is accuracy: whether the study measures what it claims to and whether results apply beyond it. A measure can be reliable without being valid.

**When do you use a directional hypothesis?**
When previous research suggests the direction of the result. Otherwise use a non-directional hypothesis, which affects whether you read one-tailed or two-tailed critical values.

**How do I remember which statistical test to use?**
Ask three questions: is it a difference or a correlation; is the design related or unrelated; and is the data nominal, ordinal or interval? The answers point to one cell of the table above.

**Is research methods only on Paper 2?**
Its content is listed under Paper 2, but the specification states research methods, practical skills and maths are assessed in all papers, so expect methods questions on Papers 1 and 3 too.
