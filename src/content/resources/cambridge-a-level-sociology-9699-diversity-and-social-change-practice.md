## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define the term *cohabitation*. **[2]**

**2.** Identify **two** types of household other than the nuclear family. **[2]**

**3.** Distinguish between a *family* and a *household*. **[4]**

**4.** Explain what is meant by *cultural diversity* in families. **[3]**

**5.** Explain **one** reason why divorce has become more common in some societies. **[3]**

**6.** Explain what Chester meant by the *neo-conventional family*. **[3]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** Explain **two** consequences of rising rates of divorce for family life. **[6]**

**8.** Explain **two** ways in which social policy may influence family life. **[6]**

**9.** Evaluate the New Right view of family diversity. **[8]**

**10.** Evaluate the postmodernist view of family diversity. **[8]**

**11.** *"The nuclear family is no longer the dominant family form."*

- **(a)** Explain the view expressed in the quotation. **[6]**
- **(b)** Using sociological material, give **two** arguments against the view expressed in the quotation. **[10]**

**12.** "Changes in the position of women are the main reason for changing patterns of marriage and divorce." Evaluate this view. **[26]**

## Answers

### 1. Cohabitation [2]

A couple living together in a sexual or romantic relationship [1] without being legally married [1]. **[2]**

### 2. Types of household [2]

Any two, one mark each: extended family, lone-parent family, reconstituted family, single-person household, shared household of unrelated people [1] [1]. **[2]**

### 3. Family and household [4]

- A family is a group of people related by kinship, marriage or adoption [1], who may or may not live together, such as grandparents living elsewhere [1].
- A household is one person or a group living at the same address and sharing facilities [1]; it need not be a family, for example students sharing a flat or a person living alone [1]. **[4]**

### 4. Cultural diversity [3]

Differences in family life linked to ethnicity, religion or culture [1], for example the importance of extended kin, marriage customs or household size [1]; the Rapoports (1982) identified cultural diversity as one of five dimensions of family diversity [1]. **[3]**

### 5. Reasons for rising divorce [3]

Reason identified [1], explained [1] and developed [1]. For example: women's education and paid work [1] mean they are less financially dependent on husbands [1], so they can leave unhappy or unequal marriages; feminists see this as increased freedom [1]. Also credit legal change, declining stigma, secularisation, rising expectations (Fletcher) or individualisation (Beck and Beck-Gernsheim). **[3]**

### 6. Neo-conventional family [3]

Chester (1985) argued that the nuclear family has changed rather than disappeared [1]; the typical family is now a nuclear family in which both partners are in paid work [1], so diversity is exaggerated and most people still live in or pass through nuclear families [1]. **[3]**

### 7. Consequences of rising divorce [6]

Up to 3 marks per consequence.

- **More lone-parent families:** usually headed by mothers, often with lower incomes; the New Right link this to disadvantage for children.
- **More reconstituted families:** children gain step-parents and step-siblings, which can bring tension over roles and loyalties as well as new support.
- **Serial monogamy:** people have several partners over a lifetime, changing kinship networks.
- **Single-person households:** divorced people may live alone.
- **Children across two households:** organisational diversity as children divide time between parents. **[6]**

### 8. Social policy and family life [6]

Up to 3 marks per way.

- **Population policy:** China's one-child policy limited family size, producing many single-child families.
- **Welfare and tax:** child benefits, tax allowances and parental leave make some family forms easier to sustain.
- **Family law:** divorce, marriage and adoption laws decide which relationships are recognised.
- **Surveillance:** Donzelot (1977) argued that social workers and health visitors monitor and regulate families, especially poorer ones. **[6]**

### 9. Evaluating the New Right view [8]

Up to 4 marks for strengths and up to 4 for limitations.

**Strengths:**
- Draws attention to the disadvantages some children of lone parents face, such as lower income [1].
- Dennis and Erdos (1992) raise concerns about the absence of fathers as role models [1].
- Has influenced policy debates in several countries, showing its relevance [1].
- Links family change to wider issues of social order and welfare [1].

**Limitations:**
- Poverty rather than family structure may explain most disadvantage [1].
- Feminists argue the ideal of a male breadwinner preserves women's dependence [1].
- It blames individuals for structural problems such as unemployment [1].
- It treats one family type as natural and ignores cultural diversity and the possible harm of conflict in two-parent families [1]. **[8]**

### 10. Evaluating the postmodernist view [8]

**Strengths:**
- Reflects the visible growth of lone-parent, reconstituted and single-person households in many societies [1].
- Stacey (1990) shows women actively creating new family forms [1].
- Recognises agency and choice rather than treating people as shaped by structure [1].
- Avoids judging any family form as superior [1].

**Limitations:**
- Overstates choice: income, class, law and religion still limit family options [1].
- Chester argues diversity is exaggerated and the neo-conventional family remains common [1].
- Patterns differ between societies; in many, traditional family forms remain dominant [1].
- Says little about inequality within families, such as gender [1]. **[8]**

### 11(a). Explaining the view [6]

- Lone-parent families have grown with divorce and births outside marriage [1].
- Reconstituted families are more common after remarriage [1].
- Single-person households have increased [1].
- Cohabitation has replaced marriage for many couples [1].
- Postmodernists such as Stacey (1990) argue that there is no longer one dominant family type [1].
- The Rapoports (1982) showed diversity across organisational, cultural and class dimensions [1]. **[6]**

### 11(b). Arguing against the view [10]

Up to 5 marks per argument.

- **Neo-conventional family:** Chester (1985) argued that the nuclear family has adapted, with both partners working, but remains the main form.
- **Life-course:** household snapshots exaggerate diversity; most people live in a nuclear family at some stage.
- **Cross-cultural variation:** in many societies nuclear or extended families remain the norm, so the claim reflects only some Western societies.
- **Ideology and policy:** the nuclear family remains the ideal in media, advertising and social policy, so it remains dominant culturally.

Credit any two developed arguments. **[10]**

### 12. Essay: women's position and changing marriage and divorce [26]

Marked by levels across AO1, AO2 and AO3.

**Indicative content for the view:**
- Women's education and paid work reduce economic dependence on marriage.
- Feminism and changing gender expectations; women less willing to accept unequal roles.
- Women may initiate divorce when the dual burden or emotion work is unequal.
- Delayed marriage as women pursue careers.

**Indicative content against the view:**
- Legal change: easier divorce, for example irretrievable breakdown in England and Wales from 1971.
- Secularisation and declining stigma.
- Rising expectations of marriage (Fletcher).
- Individualisation (Beck and Beck-Gernsheim, 1995) and Giddens' pure relationship (1992).
- Economic change and cost of weddings or housing delaying marriage.

**Evaluation:** factors interact (law changes follow changing attitudes; women's independence makes legal rights usable); importance varies between societies; judge which factor is necessary for others to operate.

**Level guidance:** top-level answers weigh women's position against other causes with accurate evidence and a supported judgement; middle-level answers list causes with limited evaluation; low-level answers describe trends.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating a household and a family as the same thing.
- Explaining divorce trends with no reference to a society.
- Evaluating New Right or postmodern views one-sidedly.
- Arguing against the quotation without mentioning Chester or the life-course.
- Essays listing causes without judging which matters most.
