## 3.1.5.1 Identifying market structures

Key idea: there is a **range** of structures, from many small producers to a single producer.

| Factor | Competitive | Non-competitive |
|---|---|---|
| Number of producers | Many | One or few |
| Product differentiation | Little | Often high (branding, design) |
| Ease of entry | Easy | Hard |

Definitions to learn word for word:

- **Product differentiation:** making a product different from rivals' so that buyers prefer it.
- **Ease of entry:** how easily new firms can set up in the market.
- **Market share:** a firm's sales as a percentage of total market sales.

Method for a market share question:

1. Add up all shares and check they total 100%.
2. Add the shares of the largest firms asked about.
3. Comment: a high total means a few producers dominate.

Quick reminder: shares of 40, 30, 20, 10 give the top two 40 + 30 = 70%.

## 3.1.5.2 Competitive markets

**Definition:** a market where many producers compete for the same customers.

Characteristics:

- many producers, each with a small share
- similar products
- easy entry and exit
- buyers can compare and switch easily
- firms are price takers in practice, with little power over price.

How producers operate: keep costs low, price close to rivals, compete on quality, service and speed, and respond fast to customers.

### Impact summary

| On | Effect of competition |
|---|---|
| Price | Lower, and held close to costs |
| Choice | Wider: many firms and many versions |
| Consumers | Lower prices, better quality and service, easy switching |
| Producers | Squeezed profit margins, pressure to cut costs and innovate, weak firms may fail |
| Workers | More firms hiring means more opportunities; but cost pressure may limit pay or threaten jobs |

### Why profits are lower than where a few producers dominate

Memorise this four-link chain:

1. Many rivals → firms cannot raise price without losing customers.
2. Prices stay close to costs → small profit per unit.
3. High profit attracts new entrants (entry is easy) → supply rises → price falls.
4. Each firm has a small share → small total profit.

Where a few dominate, entry is blocked, so high profits last.

### Worked reminder: margin comparison

A small bakery in a competitive market (invented): price £3.00 per loaf, cost £2.60 per loaf, 500 loaves a week.

- Profit per loaf = 3.00 − 2.60 = £0.40
- Weekly profit = 0.40 × 500 = **£200**
- Margin = 0.40 ÷ 3.00 × 100 = **13.3%**

Two new bakeries open nearby and the price falls to £2.80. Costs and sales stay the same.

- New profit per loaf = 2.80 − 2.60 = £0.20
- New weekly profit = 0.20 × 500 = **£100**
- Fall in profit = (200 − 100) ÷ 200 × 100 = **50%**
- Fall in price = 0.20 ÷ 3.00 × 100 = **6.7%**

A 6.7% price fall halves the profit. This is why competition squeezes profits so sharply.

## 3.1.5.3 Non-competitive markets

**Definition:** a market where competition is limited because one firm or a few firms dominate.

Characteristics:

- one or a few large producers
- high barriers to entry
- often differentiated or branded products
- firms have power to set prices.

How producers operate: set prices and keep profit high, spend on branding and advertising, may avoid price competition.

Impact on **price and choice:** higher prices and narrower choice than in a competitive market, because buyers have fewer alternatives.

### Monopoly and oligopoly

| | Monopoly | Oligopoly |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | A single producer supplies the whole market | A few large sellers hold most of the sales |
| Key feature | No direct rival, so strong price-setting power | Interdependence: each firm must consider rivals' reactions |
| Typical behaviour | Can raise price, limited pressure to improve | Avoids price wars, competes on branding, advertising and features; may collude (usually unlawful) |

A firm with a very large share but not 100% has monopolistic power.

### Worked reminder: monopoly price change

A sole supplier of ferry crossings to an island (invented) raises a ticket from £8.00 to £10.40.

- Rise = 10.40 − 8.00 = £2.40
- Percentage rise = 2.40 ÷ 8.00 × 100 = **30%**

If 5,000 trips a month fall to 4,600:

- Revenue before = 8.00 × 5,000 = £40,000
- Revenue after = 10.40 × 4,600 = **£47,840**
- Trips fall = 400 ÷ 5,000 × 100 = **8%**

Customers with no alternative cut back little, so revenue rises. This shows monopoly power.

### Causes of monopolistic and oligopolistic power

| Cause | How it creates power |
|---|---|
| High start-up costs | Few can afford to enter |
| Economies of scale | Big firms have lower unit costs than newcomers |
| Patents, copyrights, licences | Legal sole rights |
| Brand loyalty | Customers will not switch |
| Control of resources or outlets | Rivals cannot get supplies or reach customers |
| Mergers and takeovers | Fewer producers |
| Price cutting to deter entrants | Newcomers cannot survive |
| Natural growth | Successful firms win share |

### Consequences

| For | Disadvantages | Advantages |
|---|---|---|
| Consumers | Higher prices, less choice, poorer service | Lower prices if scale savings are passed on; innovation |
| Producers | Less pressure to be efficient; small rivals squeezed out | High profit; funds for research |
| Workers | Fewer employers; possible job losses after mergers | Job security and training in large firms |

### Worked reminder: economies of scale

A producer of garden furniture (invented) has a unit cost of £9.00 at 2,000 units and £7.20 at 8,000 units. The market price is £8.00.

- Small firm: 8.00 − 9.00 = −£1.00 per unit, a loss of 1.00 × 2,000 = **£2,000**
- Large firm: 8.00 − 7.20 = £0.80 per unit, 0.80 × 8,000 = **£6,400** profit

Only the large firm survives at £8.00, so scale creates dominance and a barrier to small entrants.

### Worked reminder: a price war in an oligopoly

Two firms (invented) each charge £50 and each earn £30,000 profit a month. Firm P cuts to £44 to win customers. Firm Q matches the cut.

- Price cut = 6 ÷ 50 × 100 = **12%**
- If both now earn £18,000, each has lost 12,000 ÷ 30,000 × 100 = **40%** of its profit
- Neither gained customers, because the other copied. Total profit fell from £60,000 to £36,000.

This is why oligopolists tend to avoid cutting prices and compete through branding and advertising instead.

### Matching exercise: which structure?

| Description | Best fit |
|---|---|
| One company supplies all the tap water in a region | Monopoly |
| Fifty market stalls sell similar fruit | Competitive |
| Four large banks hold most accounts and advertise heavily | Oligopoly |
| Entry is easy, there are many sellers and products are alike | Competitive |

## Evaluation toolkit

Say "it depends on" and name the factor:

- how high the barriers to entry are
- whether lower costs are passed on to consumers
- whether the firm fears new rivals
- whether the market is regulated.

Chain template: cause → effect on competition → effect on price or choice → who gains or loses → judgement.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Monopoly vs oligopoly:** one producer vs a few large producers.
- **Large firm vs monopoly:** size alone does not make a monopoly.
- **Price competition vs non-price competition:** oligopolists prefer the second.
- **Cause vs consequence:** barriers to entry are causes; higher prices are consequences.

## Quick self-test

1. List the three factors that separate one market structure from another.
2. In a market, four firms have shares of 35%, 30%, 20% and 15%. What share do the top two hold?
3. Define a competitive market.
4. A firm sells 250 units at £40 with a cost of £36 per unit. Calculate its profit and profit margin.
5. Give two reasons why profits are lower in a competitive market.
6. What does oligopoly mean?
7. State two causes of monopoly power.
8. Why do oligopolists often avoid price cuts?
9. Give one possible advantage of a monopoly to consumers.
10. A price rises from £50 to £65. Calculate the percentage rise.

### Answers

1. Number of producers, degree of product differentiation, ease of entry.
2. 35 + 30 = **65%**.
3. A market in which many producers compete for the same customers, with similar products and easy entry.
4. Revenue = 250 × 40 = £10,000. Cost = 250 × 36 = £9,000. Profit = **£1,000**. Margin = 1,000 ÷ 10,000 × 100 = **10%**.
5. Any two: firms cannot raise prices without losing customers; high profit attracts new entrants and drives price down; each firm has a small share.
6. A market where a few big sellers hold most sales and each reacts to the others.
7. Any two, for example: costly set-up, lower unit costs for big firms, legal protection for one seller, strong brands, owning key supplies, takeovers.
8. Rivals may match the cut, starting a price war that lowers profit for all.
9. Lower prices if economies of scale are passed on, or innovation funded by profit.
10. 15 ÷ 50 × 100 = **30%**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a market without using the three factors when asked to distinguish structures.
- Stating "competition is good" with no link to price, choice or profit.
- Treating every large firm as a monopoly.
- Saying monopolies always charge high prices with no reason given.
- Writing advantages or disadvantages for consumers only, forgetting producers and workers.
- Giving a list of causes of market power with no explanation of how each creates power.
- Calculating a percentage change using the new figure as the base rather than the original.
- Leaving out units, such as pounds, or the percentage sign.
