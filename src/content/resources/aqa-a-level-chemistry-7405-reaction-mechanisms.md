This guide teaches section **3.3.1.2 Reaction mechanisms** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Paper 2 at AS and A-level. The [Introduction to organic chemistry study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-introduction-to-organic-chemistry/) covers the whole of 3.3.1. This page sets out the rules for writing mechanisms, which every later organic topic uses: what a radical is, how to write the steps of a free-radical mechanism as balanced equations, and exactly where a curly arrow starts and finishes. Recall is in the [Reaction mechanisms revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-reaction-mechanisms-revision-notes/), and questions are in the [Reaction mechanisms practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-reaction-mechanisms-practice/).

## What the specification asks

| Content | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| Reactions of organic compounds can be explained using mechanisms | Describe a mechanism as the sequence of steps by which a reaction happens |
| Free-radical mechanisms: the unpaired electron in a radical is represented by a dot; curly arrows are not required | Write balanced equations for the steps in a free-radical mechanism |
| A covalent bond forms when a curly arrow starts from a lone pair or from another covalent bond | Draw arrows that show bond formation |
| A covalent bond breaks when a curly arrow starts from the bond | Draw arrows that show bond breaking |
| | Outline mechanisms by drawing the structures of the species and curly arrows for the movement of electron pairs |

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Mechanism | The step-by-step pathway showing how bonds break and form |
| Radical | A species with an unpaired electron, shown with a dot (Cl•, •CH₃) |
| Homolytic fission | A covalent bond breaks and each atom takes one electron from the shared pair, forming two radicals |
| Heterolytic fission | A covalent bond breaks and one atom takes both electrons, forming a positive ion and a negative ion |
| Nucleophile | An electron-pair donor, attracted to a δ+ carbon (OH⁻, CN⁻, NH₃) |
| Electrophile | An electron-pair acceptor, attracted to a region of high electron density (H⁺, δ+ H in HBr) |
| Intermediate | A species formed in one step and used in the next, such as a carbocation |

## Bond fission

### Homolytic fission

In homolytic fission, a shared pair of electrons is split evenly between the two atoms.

```
Cl–Cl → Cl• + Cl•
```

It needs energy, usually **ultraviolet light** or high temperature, and it happens when the bond is non-polar or when the energy is large. The products are radicals. Radicals are very reactive because the unpaired electron seeks a partner.

### Heterolytic fission

In heterolytic fission, one atom takes both electrons of the shared pair.

```
CH₃CH₂–Br → CH₃CH₂⁺ + Br⁻
```

It is common for polar bonds. The more electronegative atom (bromine here) takes the pair and forms a negative ion. The other fragment is a positive ion (a carbocation).

## Free-radical mechanisms

The unpaired electron in a radical is shown by a **dot** next to the symbol. Curly arrows are not required in the AQA specification for radical mechanisms. What you must do is write **balanced equations** for each step. A radical mechanism has three types of step.

| Stage | What happens | Number of radicals |
|---|---|---|
| Initiation | A bond breaks homolytically, producing radicals | Two radicals formed |
| Propagation | A radical reacts with a molecule, forming a new radical | One radical in, one radical out |
| Termination | Two radicals combine, forming a molecule | Two radicals removed |

### Worked example 1: ethane with chlorine

The overall reaction under UV light is C₂H₆ + Cl₂ → C₂H₅Cl + HCl.

```
Initiation:     Cl₂ → 2Cl•
Propagation 1:  Cl• + C₂H₆ → •C₂H₅ + HCl
Propagation 2:  •C₂H₅ + Cl₂ → C₂H₅Cl + Cl•
Termination:    Cl• + Cl• → Cl₂
                •C₂H₅ + Cl• → C₂H₅Cl
                •C₂H₅ + •C₂H₅ → C₄H₁₀
```

Checks for each step:

- The atoms balance on both sides.
- In both propagation steps, one radical is used and one is made. The chlorine radical used in step 1 is regenerated in step 2, which is why a few chlorine radicals can convert many molecules.
- In termination steps, no radicals remain on the product side.
- The termination step •C₂H₅ + •C₂H₅ → C₄H₁₀ explains why traces of butane form as a by-product.

## Curly arrows

A **curly arrow** shows the movement of **a pair of electrons**. Because it represents an electron pair, it has a full head. You do not need half-headed arrows for single electrons, because curly arrows are not required for radical mechanisms. The tail of the arrow shows where the electrons come from and the head shows where they go.

### Rules for arrows

1. **Forming a bond:** the arrow starts at a **lone pair** on an atom (or at another covalent bond, such as a C=C double bond) and ends at the atom that will be bonded.
2. **Breaking a bond:** the arrow starts at the **middle of the bond** and ends on the atom that takes both electrons (usually the more electronegative atom).
3. Draw the arrow from where the electrons are, **never from a positive charge** and never starting from an atom with no electrons to give.
4. Show lone pairs on the attacking species when the arrow begins from them.
5. Charges must balance across the whole step, and show partial charges (δ+ and δ–) on polar bonds.

### Worked example 2: a nucleophile attacking a polar bond (describe in words)

Bromoethane reacts with hydroxide ions: CH₃CH₂Br + OH⁻ → CH₃CH₂OH + Br⁻.

- The C–Br bond is polar: carbon is δ+ and bromine is δ–.
- Arrow 1 starts at a lone pair on the oxygen of OH⁻ and points to the carbon atom (δ+). This forms the new C–O bond.
- Arrow 2 starts at the middle of the C–Br bond and points to the bromine atom. This breaks the C–Br bond and the bromine leaves as Br⁻.
- The product is ethanol and a bromide ion. The hydroxide ion is a **nucleophile**.

### Worked example 3: an electrophile attacking a C=C bond (describe in words)

Ethene reacts with hydrogen bromide: CH₂=CH₂ + HBr → CH₃CH₂Br.

- The C=C double bond is a region of high electron density. The H in HBr is δ+ (an electrophile).
- Arrow 1 starts at the middle of the C=C double bond and points to the hydrogen atom of HBr. This forms a new C–H bond.
- Arrow 2 starts at the middle of the H–Br bond and points to the bromine atom. This breaks the H–Br bond; bromine leaves as Br⁻.
- The intermediate is a carbocation, CH₃CH₂⁺.
- Arrow 3 starts at a lone pair on Br⁻ and points to the carbon with the positive charge. This forms the C–Br bond, giving bromoethane.

### Worked example 4: elimination (describe in words)

Hydroxide ions acting as a base remove a hydrogen from a halogenoalkane, forming an alkene and Br⁻.

- Arrow 1 starts at a lone pair on OH⁻ and points to a hydrogen on the carbon next to the C–Br carbon.
- Arrow 2 starts at the middle of that C–H bond and points to the space between the two carbons (forming the C=C).
- Arrow 3 starts at the middle of the C–Br bond and points to bromine.

## Selecting the right type of mechanism

| Reagent attacks | Type | Example reagent |
|---|---|---|
| A δ+ carbon | Nucleophilic substitution | OH⁻, CN⁻, NH₃ |
| A C=C double bond | Electrophilic addition | HBr, Br₂ |
| An alkane C–H bond | Free-radical substitution | Cl₂ with UV light |
| A hydrogen next to a C–Hal carbon | Elimination | OH⁻ in ethanol |

## Common mistakes

- Starting an arrow at the atom instead of the lone pair.
- Pointing an arrow from the nucleophile to a hydrogen instead of to the δ+ carbon.
- Not drawing the lone pair on OH⁻, CN⁻ or Br⁻ when it is the start of an arrow.
- Writing radicals without the dot, or putting the dot on the wrong atom.
- Missing the radical in a propagation step, so that no radical is regenerated.
- Writing a termination step that still has a radical on the right-hand side.
- Using half-headed arrows in a heterolytic mechanism.
- Forgetting the charges on the intermediate.
- Describing homolytic fission as forming ions.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Write the equations for the steps**: label initiation, propagation and termination; show dots.
- **Outline the mechanism**: draw the structures, curly arrows, partial charges, lone pairs and the intermediate.
- **State the type of bond fission**: homolytic gives radicals, heterolytic gives ions.
- **Explain why a step is propagation**: a radical is used and a radical is formed.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does UV light start a radical reaction?**
It supplies enough energy to break a covalent bond homolytically. Chlorine has a relatively weak Cl–Cl bond, so it breaks easily.

**Can a curly arrow start at a positive charge?**
No. The arrow shows where the electrons come from. A positive charge means the atom lacks electrons.

**Do I need half-headed arrows?**
No. Curly arrows are not required for radical mechanisms, and the arrows used elsewhere in the specification show electron pairs, so they have full heads.
