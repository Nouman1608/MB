## Key ideas at a glance

| Statement | One-line summary |
|---|---|
| B4.1a | Materials (for example carbon, nitrogen, water) are recycled between abiotic and biotic parts of an ecosystem |
| B4.1b | Microorganisms decompose dead material and waste, returning carbon dioxide and mineral ions |
| B4.1c | Carbon and water cycles supply carbon, fresh water and nutrients and help maintain habitats |
| B4.1d | Decay is fastest when warm (up to an optimum), moist but not waterlogged, and with plenty of oxygen |

## Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Abiotic | Non-living parts of an ecosystem (air, water, soil, rock) |
| Biotic | Living organisms and their remains |
| Cycle | Materials pass again and again between abiotic and biotic parts |
| Decomposer | A microorganism (bacterium or fungus) that breaks down dead material and waste |
| Decomposition (decay) | Breakdown of dead organisms and waste into simpler substances |
| Aerobic | With oxygen |
| Anaerobic | Without oxygen |
| Composting | Controlled decay of plant waste to make a nutrient-rich material |
| Rate of decay | Mass lost per unit time |

## Microorganisms and decomposition

Method in steps:

1. Decomposers secrete enzymes onto the dead material.
2. Enzymes break large insoluble molecules into small soluble ones.
3. Decomposers absorb the products for growth and respiration.
4. Respiration releases carbon dioxide.
5. Unused mineral ions enter the soil for plants to absorb.

Remember what comes back and where: carbon dioxide to the air, mineral ions to the soil. Producers need both.

Earthworms and other small animals only break material into pieces; bacteria and fungi do the chemical breakdown.

## The carbon cycle in six words

Photosynthesis in, respiration out, feeding passes it on, decay and combustion return it.

| Process | Direction of carbon |
|---|---|
| Photosynthesis | Carbon dioxide (air or water) into producers |
| Feeding | From one organism to another |
| Respiration | From all organisms into carbon dioxide |
| Decomposition | From dead material into carbon dioxide |
| Combustion | From wood or fossil fuels into carbon dioxide |

Why it matters: every organic molecule contains carbon; the cycle keeps carbon dioxide available for photosynthesis and passes carbon along food chains (flow of nutrients).

## The water cycle in order

Evaporation (and transpiration), then condensation, then precipitation, then run-off and drainage, then uptake by roots or drinking. Then it repeats.

Why it matters: fresh water for land organisms; solvent that carries mineral ions into plants; reactant in photosynthesis; keeps rivers, ponds and wetlands as habitats.

## Factors affecting the rate of decay

| Factor | Rate | Say this |
|---|---|---|
| Low temperature | Slow | Enzymes work slowly and microorganisms are barely active |
| Warm, near the optimum | Fast | Enzymes work faster and microorganisms grow and respire quickly |
| Too hot | Falls to almost nothing | Enzymes denature and microorganisms die |
| Dry | Slow | Not enough water for enzyme action and growth |
| Moist | Fast | Water available and air spaces remain |
| Waterlogged | Slow | Water fills air spaces, so oxygen is short |
| Oxygen plentiful (aerobic) | Fast | Aerobic respiration releases more energy |
| Little oxygen (anaerobic) | Slow | Decay is by anaerobic microorganisms, which can produce methane |

Examples: peat bogs (waterlogged, anaerobic, very slow decay); compost heaps turned to add air.

## Calculations

| Skill | Formula |
|---|---|
| Mass lost | Start mass - final mass |
| Percentage loss | Mass lost / start mass x 100 |
| Mean rate of decay | Mass lost / time |
| Fraction remaining | Final mass / start mass |

Worked reminder. A 90 g sample falls to 54 g in 9 days.

```
Mass lost = 90 - 54 = 36 g
Percentage loss = 36 / 90 x 100 = 40 %
Rate = 36 / 9 = 4.0 g per day
Fraction remaining = 54 / 90 = 3/5
```

Graphs: the factor you changed goes along the bottom, the measured result goes up the side, both with units, and the scales should spread the points over most of the grid.

## Materials that cycle (B4.1a)

| Material | Leaves the abiotic store by | Returns by |
|---|---|---|
| Carbon | Photosynthesis | Respiration, decomposition, combustion |
| Water | Uptake by roots, drinking | Transpiration, respiration, evaporation, waste |
| Nitrogen | Uptake of nitrogen-containing ions by plants | Decomposition of dead material and waste |

Only the idea is needed for nitrogen: it is a cycled material, and microorganisms return it to the soil. The specification gives no stages for the nitrogen cycle.

## Reading data on decay

1. Read the axis titles and units first.
2. Find the highest and lowest values and say where they occur.
3. Describe the trend in order: rises, peaks, falls, with figures from the table or graph.
4. Compare two values with a calculation (a ratio, a difference or a percentage change).
5. Explain with biology: enzymes, respiration, water, oxygen.
6. Write a conclusion that only claims what the data show.

Worked reminder. Mass lost by 100 g samples in 10 days: 2 g at 5 °C, 12 g at 15 °C, 30 g at 25 °C, 20 g at 45 °C.

```
Rate at 15 C = 12 / 10 = 1.2 g per day
Rate at 25 C = 30 / 10 = 3.0 g per day
Ratio = 3.0 / 1.2 = 2.5 times faster
Drop from 25 C to 45 C = 30 - 20 = 10 g, which is 10 / 30 x 100 = 33.3 %
```

The best temperature must lie somewhere between 15 °C and 45 °C, and the data cannot say exactly where.

## Answer frame for a longer explanation

For a question such as "Explain why a gardener turns a compost heap and keeps it damp":

- Turning mixes air through the heap, so oxygen is available for aerobic respiration.
- Aerobic respiration releases more energy, so the decomposers grow and break material down faster.
- Moisture gives the enzymes and microorganisms the water they need.
- Not too wet, because a soggy heap loses its air spaces and becomes anaerobic and slow.
- Decomposers release mineral ions and carbon dioxide; the finished compost returns mineral ions to soil when it is spread.

## Must-know distinctions

- Decomposition releases carbon dioxide; photosynthesis removes it.
- Aerobic means with oxygen; anaerobic means without. The terms are about oxygen, not about air in general.
- Rate of decay (g per day) is not the same as percentage loss (%).
- Evaporation is the change to vapour; transpiration is evaporation from plant leaves; precipitation is the water falling.
- Too little water and too much water both slow decay, for different reasons.

## Investigating the best conditions for composting

- Same mass and type of material in each container; same size of pieces.
- Change one factor only; keep the others constant.
- Use mesh bags or measure a property that changes during decay; reweigh at set intervals.
- Repeat and find a mean to deal with variation.
- Name a hazard: microorganisms grow in warm, moist conditions, so wash your hands and keep the containers closed.

## Quick self-test

1. Name two materials that cycle through ecosystems.
2. What do decomposers secrete, and why?
3. Which gas do decomposers release into the air?
4. Name the process that removes carbon dioxide from the air.
5. List the stages of the water cycle in order, starting with evaporation.
6. Leaf litter falls from 60 g to 45 g in 5 days. Calculate the mean rate of decay.
7. In question 6, what is the percentage loss of mass?
8. A 360 g heap loses one quarter of its mass. What mass remains?
9. A 150 g sample decays at a mean 12 g per week. What mass remains after 6 weeks?
10. Explain why decay is slow in a waterlogged soil.
11. Give two reasons why decay stops almost completely at 60 °C.

### Answers

1. Any two of carbon, nitrogen, water.
2. Enzymes, which break large insoluble molecules into small soluble ones that can be absorbed.
3. Carbon dioxide (from respiration).
4. Photosynthesis.
5. Evaporation (and transpiration), condensation, precipitation, run-off and drainage, uptake by plants and animals.
6. (60 - 45) / 5 = 3.0 g per day.
7. 15 / 60 x 100 = 25 %.
8. 360 x 3/4 = 270 g.
9. Lost 12 x 6 = 72 g, so 150 - 72 = 78 g remains.
10. Water fills the air spaces, so oxygen is scarce; aerobic respiration is limited and decay is slower.
11. The enzymes of the decomposers are denatured, and many of the microorganisms are killed.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing that decomposers "eat" or "digest inside" the dead material without mentioning enzymes.
- Forgetting to say that mineral ions are returned to the soil as well as carbon dioxide to the air.
- Leaving out respiration when describing how carbon returns to the air.
- Leaving out a stage of the water cycle, or writing "rain" without naming precipitation.
- Saying that rate increases with temperature without mentioning an optimum and denaturing.
- Explaining slow decay in a wet heap as "no water" instead of "no oxygen".
- Giving a rate without units, or giving a percentage when asked for a rate.
- Calculating from the wrong starting value, or mixing weeks and days.
- Plotting a graph with the axes the wrong way round, or with a scale that squashes the data into a corner.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does turning a compost heap make it decay faster?**
Turning mixes air through the heap, so oxygen is available for the decomposers' aerobic respiration. Aerobic respiration releases more energy, so the microorganisms grow and break material down faster.

**Why does a waterlogged compost heap decay slowly?**
Too much water fills the air spaces in the heap and removes oxygen, so decay becomes anaerobic and slow. The problem is a lack of oxygen, not a lack of water.

**What is the difference between rate of decay and percentage loss of mass?**
Rate of decay is mass lost divided by time, in units such as g per day. Percentage loss is mass lost divided by the starting mass, multiplied by 100, and has no time unit.

**What is the difference between evaporation and transpiration in the water cycle?**
Evaporation is liquid water from seas, lakes and soil changing to water vapour. Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from plant leaves, which also adds water to the air.
