## Rates and calculations

```
natural change = birth rate - death rate
population change = natural change + net migration
```

- **Birth rate** and **death rate** are live births and deaths per 1000 people per year.
- A positive result is natural **increase**; a negative one is natural **decrease**.
- Forgetting net migration is the commonest slip in a population-change calculation.

**Worked example (illustrative):** a country has a birth rate of 32 per 1000 and a death rate of 9
per 1000. Natural change = 32 - 9 = **+23 per 1000**, which is **2.3 per cent** a year (divide by
10 to turn "per 1000" into a percentage). If net migration is -3 per 1000, population change =
23 + (-3) = **+20 per 1000**.

### More key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Infant mortality rate** | Deaths of babies under one year old per 1000 live births per year |
| **Fertility rate** | Average number of children born to each woman |
| **Replacement level** | A fertility rate of about 2.1, at which a population replaces itself in the long term |
| **Life expectancy** | Average number of years a person born now can expect to live |
| **Population explosion** | The very rapid rise in world population since the twentieth century |

## World population growth

World population grew slowly for most of history, then rose very rapidly from the twentieth century
-- the **population explosion** -- and passed **8 billion** in 2022. Growth is now slowing as birth
rates fall in many countries, but the total is still rising, mainly in Africa and parts of Asia.
Use this pattern to explain why most growth today happens in low- and middle-income countries.

## Why rates are high or low

| | Birth rate | Death rate |
|---|---|---|
| **High** | No contraception or family planning, high infant mortality, children needed as farm labour, religious or cultural beliefs | Poor healthcare, unclean water, poor diet, disease |
| **Low** | Contraception, education and careers for women, high cost of raising children, later marriage | Good healthcare, clean water and sanitation, good diet |

**The infant mortality link:** where many babies die, parents have more children to make sure some
survive to support them in old age. When healthcare improves and infant mortality falls, families
eventually choose to have fewer children -- but only after a delay.

## The Demographic Transition Model

| Stage | Birth rate | Death rate | Natural increase | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 High fluctuating | High | High | Low | Remote groups |
| 2 Early expanding | High | Falling fast | Very high | Afghanistan, Niger |
| 3 Late expanding | Falling | Low | Slowing | Kenya, Ghana |
| 4 Low fluctuating | Low | Low | Low or zero | UK, USA |
| 5 Decline | Below death rate | Low | Negative | Japan, Germany |

**Rapid growth in stage 2 is a timing gap:** the death rate falls first (medicine, sanitation, clean
water, food), but attitudes to family size change much more slowly, so births stay high.

**Why the birth rate falls in stage 3:** contraception becomes available, more girls stay in
education and women take up paid work, children cost more as schooling lasts longer, fewer children
are needed for farm work as people move to cities, and falling infant mortality means fewer births
are needed for children to survive.

**Why death rates can rise slightly in stage 5:** an ageing population has more elderly people, so
the number of deaths per 1000 can creep up even with excellent healthcare.

**Limits of the model:** built from European experience, ignores migration, assumes every country
follows the same path, gives no timescale.

## Over-population and under-population

- **Over-population:** too many people for the resources, technology and organisation available,
  at the current standard of living.
- **Under-population:** more resources than the population can use fully, so output per person could
  rise with more people.
- Both describe people **relative to resources**, not a raw total -- always judge a named country.

**Signs of over-population:** unemployment and underemployment, food and water shortages, overcrowded
housing and services, pressure on farmland and the environment, and out-migration.

**Signs of under-population:** labour shortages, resources such as minerals or farmland not fully
used, governments encouraging immigration.

**Case study cards (well-documented examples):**

- **Over-populated -- Bangladesh.** One of the most densely populated countries, much of it on the
  low-lying Ganges-Brahmaputra delta. Large areas flood regularly, farmland per person is small and
  many people seek work in Dhaka or abroad. Note that Bangladesh has also cut its fertility rate
  sharply through family planning, so its growth has slowed.
- **Under-populated -- Australia.** A large country rich in minerals, energy and farmland, but with
  a relatively small population of about 26 million, most in coastal cities. It has used a
  points-based immigration system to attract skilled workers to fill labour shortages.

## Population policies

| Type | Aim | Example | Evaluation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anti-natalist | Lower the birth rate | China's former one-child policy | Cut fertility, but ageing population, shrinking workforce, skewed sex ratio |
| Pro-natalist | Raise the birth rate | France: child benefits, parental leave, childcare | Only partly successful; costly |
| Immigration | Fill labour shortages | Germany recruiting foreign workers | Pressure on housing and services; tension if integration is poor |

**China's one-child policy in more detail:** introduced around **1979-1980** to slow rapid growth.
Couples who had one child received benefits such as better housing and education; those who had more
could face fines and loss of benefits. Rules were often relaxed in rural areas and for ethnic
minorities. It was replaced by a **two-child policy in 2016** and a **three-child policy in 2021**,
because the government became worried about an ageing population and a shrinking workforce. A
traditional preference for sons led to more boys than girls in some age groups.

**Evaluating a policy:** did it change the birth rate? What were the side effects? Was it acceptable
to the people affected? Did it work in all parts of the country? What did it cost?

## Exam traps

- Saying the birth rate falls in stage 2 -- it falls in stage 3.
- Leaving out migration from population change.
- Treating over-population as "a lot of people" rather than too many for the resources.
- Describing a policy without evaluating its costs.
- Confusing birth rate (per 1000 people) with fertility rate (children per woman).
- Forgetting that a falling growth rate still means the population is growing.

## Self-test

1. Write the formula for population change in full.
2. Why is natural increase highest in stage 2 of the DTM?
3. Give two reasons for a low birth rate.
4. Define under-population.
5. Give one benefit and one problem of an anti-natalist policy.
6. Birth rate 18 per 1000, death rate 11 per 1000, net migration +4 per 1000. Calculate population
   change.
7. Define infant mortality rate.
8. Why does a falling infant mortality rate eventually reduce the birth rate?
9. Give two signs that a country may be over-populated.
10. Why did China replace the one-child policy?
11. Why might the death rate rise slightly in stage 5?
12. Give one limitation of the Demographic Transition Model.

**Answers:** 1. Birth rate minus death rate, plus net migration. 2. The death rate has fallen but the
birth rate is still high, so the gap is widest. 3. For example contraception and women's education or
careers. 4. More resources than the population can fully use. 5. Benefit: slower growth; problem: an
ageing population and shrinking workforce later. 6. (18 - 11) + 4 = +11 per 1000. 7. Deaths of babies
under one year old per 1000 live births per year. 8. Parents no longer need extra births to make sure
some children survive, so family size falls after a delay. 9. For example unemployment and food or
water shortages. 10. Concern about an ageing population and shrinking workforce. 11. The population
contains more elderly people, so deaths per 1000 rise. 12. For example it ignores migration or
assumes every country follows Europe's path.
