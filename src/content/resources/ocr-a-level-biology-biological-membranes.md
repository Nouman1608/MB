Biological membranes are partially permeable phospholipid bilayers with proteins, cholesterol, glycoproteins and
glycolipids, described by the fluid mosaic model. For OCR A Level Biology A (H420) section 2.1.5 you must explain the
roles of membranes, the function of each component (including membrane-bound receptors for hormones and drugs), how
temperature and solvents affect permeability, how molecules cross membranes passively and actively, and how
osmosis changes plant and animal cells, along with the practical work in PAG5 and PAG8.

2026), first assessment 2025, in Module 2, Foundations in biology. Use the

## What do membranes do in cells? (a)

- **Partially permeable barriers.** The cell-surface membrane separates the cell from its environment; membranes
  around organelles (such as the nucleus and lysosomes) separate their contents from the cytoplasm; and membranes
  **within** organelles (such as the thylakoids in chloroplasts and the cristae in mitochondria) divide them into
  compartments.
- **Sites of chemical reactions.** Enzymes and electron carriers are fixed in membranes, for example the inner
  mitochondrial membrane in respiration and the thylakoid membranes in photosynthesis.
- **Sites of cell communication (cell signalling).** Receptors on the cell-surface membrane bind signalling
  molecules such as hormones.

## What is the fluid mosaic model? (b)

The membrane is a **phospholipid bilayer** about 7 nm thick. Hydrophilic phosphate heads face the aqueous
cytoplasm and tissue fluid; hydrophobic fatty acid tails face inwards. Phospholipids and many proteins move
sideways, so the membrane is **fluid**; proteins are scattered through it like tiles, so it is a **mosaic**.

| Component | Role |
|---|---|
| Phospholipids | Form the bilayer; the hydrophobic core is a barrier to ions and polar molecules |
| Cholesterol | Fits between phospholipids; regulates fluidity and makes the membrane more stable (less fluid at higher temperatures, keeps it fluid at low temperatures) |
| Channel proteins | Intrinsic proteins with a hydrophilic pore that lets specific ions or polar molecules through |
| Carrier proteins | Intrinsic proteins that bind a specific molecule and change shape to move it across, in facilitated diffusion or active transport |
| Glycoproteins | Proteins with carbohydrate chains on the outside; act as receptors and antigens and help cells attach to one another |
| Glycolipids | Lipids with carbohydrate chains; act as antigens for cell recognition |

### Why are membrane-bound receptors important for medicine?

Receptors are glycoproteins (or proteins) with a binding site complementary to a specific signalling molecule.
Hormones such as insulin bind to receptors on target cells. Many medicinal drugs are designed to have shapes that
bind these receptors, either to trigger the same response as the natural molecule or to block it.

## What affects membrane structure and permeability? (c)

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| Increasing temperature | Phospholipids gain kinetic energy and move more, so the membrane becomes more fluid and more permeable; at high temperatures membrane proteins denature, leaving gaps, so permeability rises sharply |
| Very low temperature | Phospholipids pack closely and the membrane is rigid; proteins may be damaged by ice crystals, which can pierce membranes |
| Organic solvents (such as ethanol) | Dissolve phospholipids and disrupt the bilayer; higher concentration, greater permeability |

**Practical (PAG5, PAG8).** Equal pieces of beetroot are washed to remove pigment from cut cells, then placed in
water at different temperatures or in different ethanol concentrations for a set time. The red pigment that leaks
from damaged membranes is measured with a **colorimeter**. Higher absorbance (lower transmission) means more
pigment, so greater permeability.

## How do molecules move across membranes? (d)

### Passive processes

- **Diffusion** is the net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to lower concentration,
  down a concentration gradient, until equilibrium. Small, non-polar molecules (oxygen, carbon dioxide) diffuse
  through the bilayer.
- **Facilitated diffusion** is diffusion through channel or carrier proteins, for ions and polar molecules such as
  glucose. Its rate is limited by the number of proteins.

Rate of diffusion increases with a steeper concentration gradient, larger surface area, higher temperature and a
shorter diffusion distance.

### Processes needing ATP

- **Active transport** moves substances against a concentration gradient using carrier proteins. ATP binds to the
  carrier and is hydrolysed, causing the protein to change shape and move the substance.
- **Endocytosis** takes in large molecules or particles: the membrane folds around them and pinches off to form a
  vesicle (phagocytosis of bacteria by neutrophils is an example).
- **Exocytosis** releases substances: vesicles from the Golgi apparatus fuse with the cell-surface membrane and
  release their contents (for example digestive enzymes or hormones).

Both endocytosis and exocytosis need ATP, as the **immediate source of energy**, to move vesicles and change the
shape of the membrane.

### How do you investigate diffusion in model cells? (PAG8)

Cubes of agar containing an indicator (for example agar made pink with phenolphthalein and sodium hydroxide) are
placed in dilute acid. As acid diffuses in, the pink colour disappears. Smaller cubes go colourless sooner because
they have a larger surface area to volume ratio.

**Worked example 1.** A 1 cm cube has surface area 6 x 1 x 1 = 6 cm squared and volume 1 cm cubed: ratio **6 : 1**.
A 2 cm cube has surface area 6 x 2 x 2 = 24 cm squared and volume 8 cm cubed: ratio 24 : 8 = **3 : 1**. Doubling the
side halves the ratio.

**Worked example 2.** Acid diffused 4.5 mm into a cube in 6 minutes. Rate = 4.5 / 6 = **0.75 mm per minute**.

Visking (dialysis) tubing filled with a solution can also act as a model cell: change in mass or the appearance of a
solute outside the tubing shows diffusion or osmosis.

## How does osmosis affect cells? (e)

**Osmosis** is the net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water potential to lower water
potential, across a partially permeable membrane. Water potential is measured in kPa; pure water is **0 kPa**, and
solutes make it **negative**.

| Solution outside the cell | Animal cell (for example red blood cell) | Plant cell |
|---|---|---|
| Higher water potential than the cell | Water enters; cell swells and may burst (haemolysis), as there is no cell wall | Water enters; vacuole swells and the cell becomes **turgid**; the cell wall prevents bursting |
| Equal water potential | No net movement; no change | No net movement; cell may be flaccid |
| Lower water potential than the cell | Water leaves; cell shrinks and the membrane wrinkles (crenation) | Water leaves; the protoplast shrinks and the membrane pulls away from the wall (**plasmolysis**) |

**Practical (PAG8).** Plant tissue (for example potato) is placed in a range of sucrose solutions and the percentage
change in mass is calculated. **Worked example 3.** Mass falls from 3.20 g to 2.88 g: (2.88 - 3.20) / 3.20 x 100 =
**-10%**. The concentration giving zero change has the same water potential as the tissue. Red onion epidermis
under the microscope shows plasmolysis directly; red blood cells can be viewed in different salt solutions.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is the membrane described as partially permeable?**
It lets some substances, such as water and small non-polar molecules, cross freely, but stops ions and large or
polar molecules unless there are specific proteins to transport them.

**What is the difference between facilitated diffusion and active transport?**
Both use proteins, but facilitated diffusion is passive and goes down a concentration gradient, whereas active
transport goes against the gradient and needs ATP.

**Why do plant cells not burst in pure water?**
The cell wall resists the expanding protoplast, so the cell becomes turgid instead.

**Why do drugs often target membrane receptors?**
Receptors have specific binding sites. A drug with a complementary shape can bind them to trigger or block a
response in only the cells that carry the receptor.

## Common mistakes

- Saying water moves from high to low concentration of water instead of using water potential.
- Saying endocytosis and exocytosis are passive; both need ATP.
- Describing cholesterol only as "making the membrane rigid"; it regulates fluidity.
- In the beetroot practical, forgetting to wash the pieces first, or not naming the colorimeter.
- Saying plant cells burst in a solution of higher water potential.
