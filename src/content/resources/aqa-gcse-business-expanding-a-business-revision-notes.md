## The five content points

| Point | One-line recall |
|---|---|
| Methods of expansion | Franchising, new stores, e-commerce, outsourcing, mergers and takeovers |
| Benefits and drawbacks | Discuss both sides of each method, applied to the business |
| Economies of scale | Average unit cost falls as output rises |
| Diseconomies of scale | Average unit cost rises when the business is too big |
| Average unit cost | Total cost divided by output; calculate and interpret |

## Methods of expansion: benefits and drawbacks

| Method | Benefit | Drawback |
|---|---|---|
| Opening new stores | Owners keep control; same brand and methods; new customers | Costly premises and staff; a bad location may lose money |
| Franchising | Fast growth using the franchisee's capital; fees and a share of revenue come in | Less control; shares profit; one weak outlet harms the brand |
| E-commerce | Wider market; open at all times; no extra shop needed | Website, delivery and returns costs; many online rivals |
| Outsourcing | Specialist may be cheaper or better; business focuses on its core | Less control of quality and timing; reliance on the supplier |
| Merger | Joint resources, customers and skills; agreed by both sides | Cultures may clash; jobs may go; benefits may not appear |
| Takeover | Quick growth and immediate control of the target's assets | Often expensive; staff and managers of the target may resist |

Organic growth (from the business's own activity) is usually slower but gives more control. External growth (merger or takeover) is faster but costs more and carries integration risk.

## Economies of scale

**Definition:** a unit cost advantage from growth, so average unit cost falls as output rises.

| Type | What happens | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Purchasing | Bulk orders earn a lower price per unit from suppliers | A pet-food maker pays 15% less per tonne when it orders larger loads |
| Technical | Larger or specialised machinery; its cost is spread over more units | A big printing press cuts the cost per leaflet |

Remember: total cost still rises. Only the cost per unit falls.

## Diseconomies of scale

**Definition:** average unit cost rises because the business has grown too large.

The three causes named in the specification:

1. Poor communication.
2. Coordination issues.
3. Reduced staff motivation.

With growth, the risk of these increases. They are not guaranteed.

Cause and effect chain: more layers and sites, then slower messages and mistakes, then rework, delays and staff leaving, then higher cost per unit.

## Average unit cost

```
Average unit cost = total cost / output
```

This is the standard form of the formula.

Method in steps:

1. Write the formula.
2. Substitute total cost and output (check the units: pounds and items).
3. Calculate and give the unit, for example £2.40 per unit.
4. Compare with another output level.
5. Interpret: fall means economies of scale; rise means diseconomies.

Worked reminder. A tile maker's total cost is £90,000 for 12,000 tiles.

```
Average unit cost = 90,000 / 12,000 = £7.50 per tile
```

Rearranged: total cost = average unit cost x output. If the average unit cost is £3.40 and output is 15,000, total cost is £51,000.

Percentage change in average unit cost: (new - old) / old x 100. A fall from £5.00 to £4.20 is a fall of 16%.

## Small worked reminders

Purchasing economy. A shop pays £8.00 per unit for an order of 200 units and £6.80 per unit for an order of 1,000 units. The price per unit is 15% lower on the larger order: (8.00 - 6.80) / 8.00 x 100.

Technical economy. A machine costs £1,200 a week to run. Spread over 400 units it adds £3.00 a unit (1,200 / 400). Spread over 3,000 units it adds £0.40 a unit (1,200 / 3,000).

Diseconomy. Total cost is £4,200 at 1,400 units, so average unit cost is £3.00. At 1,600 units total cost is £5,600, so average unit cost is £3.50. The £0.50 rise is a 16.7% increase (0.50 / 3.00 x 100). Output grew, but each unit now costs more, which points to diseconomies of scale.

## Command words in this topic

| Command word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| State, identify | A short answer with no reasoning needed |
| Define | Say what a term means, for example economies of scale |
| Calculate | Show the formula, substitution and answer with units |
| Explain | Give a point and a reason, linked to the business |
| Analyse | Develop a chain of reasoning: point, cause, effect on the business |
| Discuss | Give advantages and disadvantages, applied to the case |
| Evaluate, justify, recommend | Weigh both sides and reach a reasoned judgement |

## Must-know distinctions

| Compare | Key difference |
|---|---|
| Merger and takeover | Merger is agreed by both; takeover means one gains control |
| Purchasing and technical economies | Cheaper inputs per unit versus more efficient production methods |
| Economies and diseconomies | Unit cost falling versus unit cost rising as output grows |
| Total cost and average unit cost | Whole bill versus cost per unit |
| Organic and external growth | Own resources versus joining or buying another business |
| Franchisor and franchisee | Brand owner versus the person who runs the outlet |

## Choosing between methods

| If the business... | It may favour... | Because |
|---|---|---|
| Has little capital but a strong brand | Franchising | The franchisee provides money and runs the outlet |
| Has healthy cash reserves and wants control | New stores | It keeps the brand and decisions in its own hands |
| Sells a product that posts easily | E-commerce | It reaches a wider market without new premises |
| Is stretched on a non-core task | Outsourcing | A specialist can take over the task |
| Wants a rival's customers quickly | Takeover | It gains them at once, if it can afford the price |

Always test the suggestion against the facts in the stem. The same method can suit one business and not another.

## Applying and evaluating

- Name the method, then explain a benefit and a drawback in the context of the case study.
- Use figures if the stem supplies them.
- For a judgement, weigh up cost, speed, control and risk, and say which matters most for this business and why.
- A reasoned conclusion is stronger when it names a condition, such as "provided the business has the cash reserves".

## Quick self-test

1. State the formula for average unit cost.
2. A business has a total cost of £90,000 and an output of 12,000 units. Calculate the average unit cost.
3. Average unit cost is £3.40 and output is 15,000. Calculate total cost.
4. Average unit cost falls from £5.00 to £4.20. Calculate the percentage fall.
5. Name two causes of diseconomies of scale.
6. Explain what a purchasing economy of scale is.
7. Give one benefit and one drawback of franchising for the franchisor.
8. Distinguish a merger from a takeover.
9. A café's total cost is £48,000 at 8,000 meals and £63,000 at 9,000 meals. Which output has the lower average unit cost, and what does it suggest?
10. Give one drawback of outsourcing.

### Answers

1. Average unit cost = total cost divided by output.
2. £90,000 / 12,000 = **£7.50**.
3. £3.40 x 15,000 = **£51,000**.
4. (5.00 - 4.20) / 5.00 x 100 = **16%**.
5. Any two of: poor communication, coordination issues, reduced staff motivation.
6. A lower price per unit on materials or supplies because the business buys in bulk, which lowers average unit cost.
7. Benefit: fast growth using the franchisee's money (or fees and a share of revenue). Drawback: less control, so a badly run outlet can damage the brand (or it shares the profit).
8. A merger is two businesses agreeing to join as one; in a takeover one business gains control of another, which the target may not want.
9. £48,000 / 8,000 = £6.00; £63,000 / 9,000 = £7.00. The lower cost is at 8,000 meals. The rise to £7.00 suggests diseconomies of scale.
10. Less control over quality or deadlines, or reliance on a supplier that may raise its price.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying total cost falls when output rises. Write "average unit cost".
- Dividing the wrong way round in the formula.
- Leaving out the unit, such as £ per unit, in a calculation.
- Describing purchasing economies as "cheaper" without saying the price per unit falls because of bulk orders.
- Naming a method but giving no benefit or drawback.
- Writing generic points that could apply to any business instead of using the case study.
- Treating diseconomies of scale as inevitable.
- Confusing franchising with outsourcing.
- Finishing a discuss question with no judgement.
