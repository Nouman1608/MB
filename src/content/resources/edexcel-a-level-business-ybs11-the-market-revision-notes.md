## What shifts the curves

| Demand shifts because of | Supply shifts because of |
|---|---|
| Price of substitutes (up → demand for this product up) | Costs of production (up → supply left) |
| Price of complements (up → demand for this product down) | New technology (→ supply right) |
| Consumer incomes (direction depends on YED) | Indirect taxes (→ supply left) |
| Fashions, tastes and preferences | Government subsidies (→ supply right) |
| Marketing, advertising and branding | External shocks (often → supply left) |
| Demographics | |
| External shocks | |
| Seasonality | |

**Own price changes never shift the curve:** they cause a movement along it (extension or contraction).

## Equilibrium and diagrams

- Equilibrium: quantity demanded = quantity supplied.
- Above equilibrium price: surplus, so price is pushed down. Below: shortage, so price is pushed up.
- Demand right → price up, quantity up. Demand left → price down, quantity down.
- Supply right → price down, quantity up. Supply left → price up, quantity down.
- Both shift: one variable is certain, the other depends on the size of each shift.
- An indirect tax shifts supply up by the tax per unit, but price usually rises by **less** than the tax.

**Diagram checklist:** labelled axes (Price, Quantity); D and S labelled; original equilibrium P1, Q1 with dotted lines; new curve labelled with an arrow; new equilibrium P2, Q2.

## PED

```
PED = % change in quantity demanded / % change in price
% change = (new - original) / original x 100
```

| Value (ignoring sign) | Type | Price rise → revenue | Price cut → revenue |
|---|---|---|---|
| > 1 | Elastic | Falls | Rises |
| = 1 | Unitary | No change | No change |
| < 1 | Inelastic | Rises | Falls |

**Influences:** substitutes, necessity versus luxury, share of income, brand loyalty and habit, time period.

**Predicting quantity from PED:** % change in quantity = PED × % change in price. A 5% price rise with PED −0.6 cuts quantity by 3%.

## YED

```
YED = % change in quantity demanded / % change in income
```

| YED | Type of good | Example behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| Above +1 | Normal, income elastic (luxury) | Sales grow fast in a boom, fall fast in a recession |
| 0 to +1 | Normal, income inelastic (necessity) | Sales fairly stable |
| Below 0 | Inferior | Sales rise when incomes fall |

**Influences:** necessity or luxury; the income level of customers; better alternatives to trade up to. **Uses:** sales forecasting, capacity and staff planning, balancing the product portfolio.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Demand | Quantity customers are willing and able to buy at each price |
| Supply | Quantity firms are willing and able to sell at each price |
| Substitute | A product that can be used instead of another |
| Complement | A product used together with another |
| External shock | An unexpected event that suddenly changes demand or supply |
| Indirect tax | A tax on spending, added to the price of a good |
| Subsidy | A payment from government that lowers a producer's costs |
| Equilibrium price | The price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied |
| Price elastic | Quantity changes proportionally more than price |
| Inferior good | A good with negative YED |

## Exam traps

- "Demand fell because price rose" describes a contraction, not a shift.
- Taxes and subsidies move **supply**, not demand.
- Keep the sign on YED; drop it only when classifying PED.
- Percentage change always uses the **original** figure as the base.
- Revenue is not profit: an elastic price cut can raise revenue but cut profit.
- A PED estimate from research is not a fact; question its reliability in evaluation.

## Self-test

1. The price of tea rises. What happens to demand for coffee, and why?
2. State two factors that shift supply to the right.
3. What happens to equilibrium price and quantity if demand shifts left?
4. A $2 tax per unit is imposed. Will price normally rise by $2?
5. Price rises from $20 to $22 and quantity falls from 5,000 to 4,600. Calculate PED.
6. Is the demand in question 5 elastic or inelastic?
7. Calculate total revenue before and after the change in question 5.
8. PED is −1.5. Price is cut by 8%. By how much will quantity change?
9. Incomes rise 4%, demand rises 2%. Calculate YED and classify the good.
10. Give two factors that make demand more price inelastic.
11. Why might a firm selling an income-elastic product worry about a recession?
12. Demand rises and supply rises at the same time. Which variable definitely rises?

### Answers

1. Demand for coffee shifts right, because coffee is a substitute and is now relatively cheaper.
2. Any two: lower costs of production, new technology, a subsidy.
3. Both fall.
4. Usually not: some customers buy less, so firms absorb part of the tax and price rises by less than $2.
5. % change in Q = (4,600 − 5,000) ÷ 5,000 × 100 = −8%; % change in P = +10%; PED = −8 ÷ 10 = **−0.8**.
6. Inelastic, since 0.8 is less than 1.
7. Before: 20 × 5,000 = **$100,000**. After: 22 × 4,600 = **$101,200**. Revenue rises, as expected with inelastic demand.
8. −1.5 × −8% = **+12%**.
9. YED = 2 ÷ 4 = **+0.5**: a normal, income-inelastic good (a necessity).
10. Any two: few close substitutes, strong brand loyalty, the product is a necessity, it takes a small share of income, a short time period.
11. Falling incomes cut demand by proportionally more than the fall in income, so sales and revenue drop sharply.
12. Quantity.
