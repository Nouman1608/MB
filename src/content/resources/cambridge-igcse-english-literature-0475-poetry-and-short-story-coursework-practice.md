**How to use this set.** The syllabus requires that an assignment using poetry or short stories
covers "a minimum of two poems or stories", without requiring comparison. Section A tests the rules
and planning; Section B uses the two original poems below, written for this page, to practise
writing.

**Poem 1: The Lighthouse Keeper's Daughter** *(original poem)*

> All night the beam goes round the room
> and finds me, loses me, and finds me.
> My father climbs to keep it turning.
> I learn to sleep in pieces.

**Poem 2: Exam Hall** *(original poem)*

> Two hundred desks. The clock is loud.
> Somebody's pen is clicking, clicking.
> I know the answer. It was here.
> I had it in my hand this morning.

## Section A

**1.** A student submits an assignment that analyses one poem in great detail. Explain the problem
and how to fix it. **[2]**

**2.** A student asks whether they must compare the two stories in their assignment. Answer them,
using the syllabus's reasoning. **[2]**

**3.** A student wants to write about two poems they have read in an English translation. Explain
whether this meets the requirements. **[2]**

**4.** Rewrite this title so that it allows scope for all four assessment objectives: "Write about
two short stories by the same writer." **[3]**

**5.** Explain **one** advantage of a sequential structure and **one** advantage of an integrated
structure. **[2]**

**6.** A student's assignment is 1,150 words, including 180 words of quotation and a 60-word
bibliography. Is it within the guidance? Explain. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Read Poem 1. Explore how the poet presents the speaker's childhood in this poem. **[6]**

**8.** Read Poem 2. Explore how the poet creates a sense of panic. **[6]**

**9.** Write a linking sentence that could open the second half of a sequential assignment on the

**10.** Plan an integrated assignment of about 900 words on the title in Question 9, with **three**

**11.** Here is a weak conclusion: "In conclusion both poems are about feelings and use lots of

**12.** A student's draft on two short stories has three problems: no titles of the stories, a

## Answers

**1.** An assignment on poetry must cover a minimum of two poems, so one poem alone does not meet the
requirement [1]; the student should add at least one further poem with a shared focus and revise the
title to cover both [1].

**2.** No, comparison is not required [1], because the syllabus assumes the assignment is based on
the study of a wider selection broadly equivalent to a short stories set text [1].

**3.** It does not [1]: coursework texts must have been originally written in English [1].

**4.** A focus [1], an invitation to analyse methods [1] and room for judgement [1], for example:
"How does the writer create sympathy for outsiders in two stories you have studied, and in which is
this more successful?"

**5.** Sequential: easier to control, giving each piece clear space [1]. Integrated: produces a more
developed argument, because each paragraph explores an aspect across both pieces [1].

**6.** Yes [1]: the 1,150 words include quotations, which count, but the bibliography is excluded,
so the assignment is 1,090 words of counted text, within 600–1200 [1].

**7.** Any three developed points, [2] each:
- "All night the beam goes round the room" presents a childhood lived by the lighthouse's rhythm
  rather than a normal day and night [2].
- "finds me, loses me, and finds me": the repetition imitates the turning beam and suggests a child
  sometimes noticed and sometimes not [2].
- "My father climbs to keep it turning" shows the father devoted to duty, perhaps at a distance from
  the child [2].
- "I learn to sleep in pieces": the metaphor makes broken sleep a lesson of childhood, suggesting
  adaptation and quiet sadness [2].
- The short, four-line form and plain statements give a calm voice to an unsettled experience [2].
Maximum 6.

**8.** Any three developed points, [2] each:
- "Two hundred desks." The minor sentence and the large number create an impersonal, overwhelming
  setting [2].
- "The clock is loud" uses sound to show heightened awareness of time running out [2].
- The repeated "clicking, clicking" reproduces an irritating noise that the speaker cannot escape [2].
- "I know the answer. It was here." Short sentences, and the shift from present to past tense, show
  certainty slipping away [2].
- "I had it in my hand this morning" makes knowledge seem like a physical object that has been lost,
  ending on helplessness [2].
Maximum 6.

**9.** A sentence that connects both poems [1] and introduces the different treatment in Poem 2 [1],
for example: "Where the first poem presents unsettlement as a rhythm the speaker slowly learns to
live with, the second compresses it into a single moment of panic in an exam hall."

**10.** One mark for a clear introduction with an argument [1], one for a conclusion with a judgement
[1], and [1] for each of three aspects that refer to both poems [3], plus [1] for a sensible word
allocation. For example:
1. *Introduction (100 words):* both speakers are unsettled; one over years, one in minutes.
2. *Aspect 1, sound (250 words):* the turning beam's rhythm and the clicking pen and loud clock.
3. *Aspect 2, repetition (250 words):* "finds me, loses me, and finds me" and "clicking, clicking".
4. *Aspect 3, endings (200 words):* "I learn to sleep in pieces" (adaptation) against "I had it in
   my hand this morning" (loss).
5. *Conclusion (100 words):* judgement on which presents unsettlement more powerfully.

**11.** Award up to [4]: returns to the focus of the title [1]; distinguishes the two poems [1];
refers to a key method or quotation [1]; gives a justified personal judgement [1]. For example: "Both
poems present unsettlement through sound and repetition, but in different time-scales. The first
speaker has learned to 'sleep in pieces', so unease has become a way of life; the second loses
certainty in a single morning. I find the lighthouse poem more powerful, because its calm voice
suggests a child who has stopped expecting anything else."

**12.** One mark for each piece of advice [3]: give each story's title in quotation marks so the
scope is clear [1]; reference the website with its address and the date accessed, as the syllabus
requires, to avoid plagiarism [1]; add a linking sentence and a conclusion that draws both stories
together into one argument [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Covering only one poem or story.
- Separate halves with no shared argument.
- Titles without a focus or room for judgement.
- Analysing content without methods such as repetition, line breaks or endings.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I write about three poems?**
Yes, but within 600–1200 words each will get less space; two often allows deeper analysis.

**Do the two pieces have to be by the same writer?**
The syllabus wording requires a minimum of two poems or stories and does not state that they must be
by one writer; follow your teacher's guidance and the course your centre has planned.
