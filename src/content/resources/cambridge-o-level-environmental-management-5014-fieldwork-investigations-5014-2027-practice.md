Environmental Management (5014) syllabus, together with the skills in the syllabus's Fieldwork requirements

## Section A

**1.** State what is meant by a *pilot study*. **[1]**

**2.** Name the apparatus used to measure light intensity. **[1]**

**3.** State **one** difference between random sampling and systematic sampling. **[1]**

**4.** Name the sampling technique that uses a container sunk into the ground. **[1]**

## Section B

**5.** Students investigate whether trampling on a footpath affects the number of plant species.
(a) Suggest a testable hypothesis. **[1]**
(b) Identify the independent and the dependent variable. **[2]**
(c) State **two** control variables. **[2]**

**6.** Describe how the students in Question 5 could use random sampling with quadrats to compare the
footpath with the grass beside it. **[4]**

**7.** A grid quadrat with 100 squares is placed at five points. The number of squares at least half covered
by clover is: 34, 29, 36, 31, 30.
(a) Calculate the mean percentage cover. **[2]**
(b) Calculate the range. **[1]**

**8.** A student counts a plant species in eight 0.5 m² quadrats placed at random in a 250 m² area. The
counts are 3, 5, 2, 4, 6, 3, 4, 5.
(a) Calculate the mean number per quadrat. **[1]**
(b) Estimate the total population in the area. **[2]**
(c) Suggest how the estimate could be made more reliable. **[1]**

**9.** The table shows soil moisture along a transect from a stream bank. *The data are invented for
practice.*

| Distance from stream / m | Reading 1 / % | Reading 2 / % | Reading 3 / % |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 62 | 60 | 64 |
| 4 | 48 | 50 | 46 |
| 8 | 35 | 12 | 37 |
| 12 | 24 | 22 | 26 |

(a) Identify the anomalous result and suggest **one** reason for it. **[2]**
(b) Calculate the mean soil moisture at 8 m, excluding the anomaly. **[1]**
(c) Describe the relationship between distance and soil moisture. **[2]**

**10.** Explain why systematic sampling is more suitable than random sampling for the investigation in
Question 9. **[2]**

**11.** Describe how to use a pooter to collect small insects, and give **one** limitation of the method.
**[3]**

**12.** Identify **three** hazards in a stream-bank investigation and suggest a safety precaution for each.
**[3]**

## Section C

**13.** Discuss the benefits and limitations of pitfall traps, sweep nets and automated sampling for
measuring the population of invertebrates in a woodland. **[6]**

**14.** A conservation group wants to monitor the area of vegetation on a large sand-dune system every
year. Discuss whether aerial photography and drones or quadrats along transects would be the better
choice. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** A small trial run of the method to find and fix problems before the main data collection. **[1]**

**2.** A light meter. **[1]**

**3.** Random sampling chooses positions by chance; systematic sampling takes samples at regular intervals
(for example along a transect). **[1]**

**4.** Pitfall trap. **[1]**

**5.** (a) For example: "There are fewer plant species on the trampled footpath than on the untrampled grass
beside it." **[1]**
(b) Independent: amount of trampling (footpath or not) **[1]**; dependent: number of plant species **[1]**.
(c) Any two: quadrat size; number of quadrats; time of year or day; the same person identifying species;
similar slope or aspect. **[2]**

**6.** Lay two tapes at right angles to make a grid in each area **[1]**; use random numbers to choose
co-ordinates for each quadrat **[1]**; place the same size quadrat and count the number of species in each
**[1]**; use the same number of quadrats in each area (for example ten) and calculate a mean for each **[1]**.

**7.** (a) Total = 34 + 29 + 36 + 31 + 30 = 160 **[1]**; mean = 160 / 5 = **32%** **[1]**.
(b) 36 - 29 = **7 percentage points** **[1]**.

**8.** (a) Total = 32; mean = 32 / 8 = **4 per quadrat** **[1]**.
(b) Density = 4 / 0.5 = 8 per m² **[1]**; population = 8 x 250 = **2 000 plants** **[1]**.
(c) Use more quadrats (or repeat the sampling on another day). **[1]**

**9.** (a) 12% at 8 m **[1]**; any sensible reason, for example the reading was taken on a dry patch or a stone,
or the meter was not pushed fully into the soil **[1]**.
(b) (35 + 37) / 2 = **36%** **[1]**.
(c) Soil moisture decreases as distance from the stream increases **[1]**, from a mean of 62% at 0 m to 24%
at 12 m **[1]**.

**10.** The aim is to see how moisture changes with distance from the stream (a gradient) **[1]**; samples at
regular intervals along a transect cover the whole gradient, whereas random samples might miss parts of it
**[1]**.

**11.** Place the end of the collecting tube over the insect **[1]** and suck through the mouthpiece tube, which
has gauze to stop the insect entering the mouth, so the insect is drawn into the jar **[1]**. Limitation: only
very small animals can be collected, or it is slow **[1]**.

**12.** One mark for each hazard with a matching precaution, up to three: deep or fast water, so stay on the
bank and work in groups **[1]**; slippery or steep banks, so wear suitable footwear **[1]**; contaminated
water or soil, so wear gloves and wash hands **[1]**; biting or stinging animals, so check the site and carry
first aid **[1]**. **[max 3]**

**13.** **[6]** One mark per point, up to six, covering all three. Pitfall traps: cheap and catch
ground-dwelling animals over a long period **[1]**; but predators may eat other animals in the trap, and
traps must be checked regularly **[1]**. Sweep nets: quick for invertebrates in vegetation **[1]**; but hard to
standardise and they miss ground animals, and woodland has little tall vegetation **[1]**. Automated
sampling: records continuously, including night-active animals, without disturbance **[1]**; but cameras are
costly and miss very small invertebrates **[1]**. Conclusion: pitfall traps suit ground animals in a woodland,
with other methods added for animals in vegetation **[1]**. **[max 6]**

**14.** **[6]** One mark per point, up to six. Aerial photography and drones: cover the whole large dune
system quickly **[1]**; show vegetation area directly and can be repeated from the same height each year
**[1]**; but equipment is costly and may need permission or calm weather **[1]**. Quadrats along transects:
cheap and give detailed cover and species data **[1]**; but cover only a small sample of a large site **[1]**,
and trampling dunes may damage the vegetation being measured **[1]**. Conclusion: drones are better for
measuring total area each year, with occasional transects to check what the images show **[1]**. **[max 6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving a hypothesis that cannot be tested ("Trampling is bad for plants").
- Describing a method without saying how many samples or how positions were chosen.
- Including an anomaly in a mean after identifying it.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is capture-mark-recapture in 5.4?**
No. The 2027-2029 syllabus lists seven techniques for 5.4.4: pitfall traps, pooters, sweep nets, quadrats,
transects, aerial photography and drones, and automated sampling.

**Do I need to draw apparatus?**
The Fieldwork requirements section says candidates should be able to identify apparatus from diagrams or
descriptions and draw, complete or label diagrams of apparatus.
