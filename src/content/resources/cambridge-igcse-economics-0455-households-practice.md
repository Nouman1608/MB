These questions cover section 3.2, Households, of the Cambridge IGCSE Economics 0455 syllabus for examination in 2026. Economics 0455 is not tiered. All households and figures are invented. Learn the content first from the [households study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-households/) and the [households revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-households-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define saving. **[2]**

**2.** Which change is most likely to cause households to borrow more?

A: a rise in income tax

B: a fall in the rate of interest

C: a fall in consumer confidence

D: a rise in unemployment

**[1]**

**3.** A household has disposable income of 4,500 a month and spends 3,960. Calculate its saving as a percentage of disposable income. **[2]**

**4.** State **two** reasons why a low-income household saves a small proportion of its income. **[2]**

**5.** Which household is most likely to be dissaving?

A: a middle-aged couple at the peak of their careers

B: a retired person with no income from work

C: a high-income worker saving for a house

D: a young worker living with parents

**[1]**

**6.** State what is meant by consumer confidence. **[1]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**7.** Household J has disposable income of 2,800 a month and spends 2,520. After a pay rise its disposable income is 3,200 and it spends 2,800.

**(a)** Calculate the household's saving before and after the pay rise. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the proportion of the extra income that is saved. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why a household may save a larger share of extra income than of its original income. **[2]**

**8.** The central bank of Country P raises the rate of interest from 2% to 5%.

**(a)** Mira has a variable-rate, interest-only loan of 40,000. Calculate the increase in her yearly interest payments. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain **two** ways the higher interest rate may reduce household spending. **[4]**

**9.** Explain why the same rise in interest rates might lead a retired couple to spend more but a young family to spend less. **[4]**

**10.** The table shows invented average monthly figures for households in Country Q.

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Disposable income | 3,000 | 3,150 |
| Spending | 2,700 | 2,646 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in disposable income and in spending. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest and explain **one** reason why spending fell while income rose. **[3]**

**11.** Analyse how a fall in consumer confidence affects household spending, saving and borrowing. **[5]**

**12.** Discuss whether income is the most important influence on how much a household spends. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** The part of disposable income [1]; that is not spent on goods and services in a period [1]. **[2]**

**2.** B [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* A, C and D all reduce the ability or willingness to borrow.

**3.** Saving = 4,500 - 3,960 = 540 [1]; 540 / 4,500 x 100 = **12%** [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Most of the income is needed for necessities such as food and rent [1]; little is left after essential spending, and the household may need to borrow for emergencies [1]. **[2]**

**5.** B [1]. **[1]**

**6.** How optimistic households feel about their future jobs, income and the economy [1]. **[1]**

**7. (a)** Before: 2,800 - 2,520 = **280** [1]; after: 3,200 - 2,800 = **400** [1].

**(b)** Extra income 400, extra saving 120, so **30%** of the extra income is saved [1].

**(c)** Necessities are already paid for from the original income [1]; so more of each extra unit of income is left over and can be saved [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Before the rise, saving was 280 / 2,800 = 10% of income, so 30% of the extra income is a larger share.

**8. (a)** 40,000 x 0.05 = 2,000; 40,000 x 0.02 = 800 [1]; increase = **1,200** a year [1].

**(b)** Borrowing becomes more expensive, so households take out fewer loans for cars or furniture [1], which reduces spending on these goods [1]. Households with existing variable-rate loans pay more interest [1], leaving less disposable income for other spending [1]. (Accept: saving becomes more rewarding, so households save rather than spend, developed.) **[6]**

**9.** A retired couple may hold savings and live partly on the interest [1]; higher rates raise their interest income, so they may spend more [1]. A young family is more likely to have a home loan or other debts [1]; higher rates raise their repayments, leaving less to spend on other goods [1]. **[4]**

**10. (a)** Income: (3,150 - 3,000) / 3,000 x 100 = **+5%** [1]; spending: (2,646 - 2,700) / 2,700 x 100 = **-2%** [1].

**(b)** A fall in consumer confidence [1]; for example because of fears of job losses, so households saved more as a cushion [1]; even though their income had risen [1]. (Accept a rise in interest rates making saving more attractive and borrowing dearer, developed.) **[5]**

**11.** Households expect their future income to be less secure, for example because unemployment is rising [1]. They save more as a precaution against losing their job [1], so spending falls even if current income has not changed [1]. They delay buying expensive durable goods such as cars [1]. They are also unwilling to take on new debt they may not be able to repay, so borrowing falls [1]. **[5]**

**12.** For: income sets the limit on what most households can afford [1]; a rise in income lets households spend more on both necessities and luxuries, so spending usually rises with income [1].
Against: interest rates affect the cost of borrowing, so spending on large items can change without any change in income [1]; confidence can cause spending to fall even when incomes rise, as households save more [1]; wealth and access to credit also matter [1].
Judgement: income is the most important influence over the long run, because spending cannot exceed income plus borrowing for long; but in the short run interest rates and confidence can be more important, especially for households with large debts [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving saving as a total stock (savings) rather than the part of income not spent.
- Calculating a percentage change with the new value as the base.
- Assuming a rate rise reduces spending for every household.
- Explaining confidence without a reason such as fear of job losses.
- Listing influences in a "discuss" question without weighing them.
