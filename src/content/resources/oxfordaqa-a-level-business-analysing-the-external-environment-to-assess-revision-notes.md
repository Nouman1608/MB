## The big idea

An external change matters only through its effect on **this** business. Every answer should follow one chain:

**change → effect on costs or demand → functional area (marketing, operations, finance, HR) → strategic response → judgement (how big, how likely, how soon)**

External environments differ between countries, and in a global economy a change in one country (a recession, a tariff, a currency move) reaches businesses in another.

## PESTLE

| Letter | Covers | Example opportunity | Example threat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Political | Government policy, trade, support | A new grant for energy-saving kit | A tariff in an export market |
| Economic | GDP, tax, exchange rates, inflation, unemployment, interest rates | Rising incomes | Higher borrowing costs |
| Social | Population, migration, workforce, culture, CSR | Growing demand from older buyers | Skills shortage |
| Technological | Digital, disruptive technology, AI | Online channels, lower unit costs | A cheaper rival business model |
| Legal | Competition, consumer, employment, environmental, health and safety law | Rules that keep out low-quality rivals | Higher compliance costs |
| Environmental | Climate change | Demand for low-carbon products | Extreme weather disrupting supply |

**Value:** structured outward look; early warning; feeds the O and T of SWOT; useful before entering a new country.
**Limits:** quickly dated; unranked list; categories overlap; future change is uncertain; gathering data costs time and money.

## Political and legal: quick list

**Law within a country affects** (specific laws are not required):

- **competition**: bans on cartels and abuse of dominance; merger controls
- **consumer protection**: safety, labelling, advertising, refunds
- **the labour market**: pay floors, hours, dismissal, discrimination, employee rights
- **environmental issues**: emissions, waste, permits
- **health and safety**: risk assessment, training, equipment

**Government policy to know:**

- **support for business**: grants and subsidies; cheaper inputs, but conditions apply
- **ease of doing business**: speed and cost of registering, permits, tax filing, contract enforcement
- **international trade agreements**: lower barriers and common standards both ways
- **protectionism**: tariffs, quotas, trade regulations
- **infrastructure**: investment in and quality of transport, energy and digital networks

## Economic formulas and methods

| Task | Method |
|---|---|
| Foreign price to home currency | If £1 = X foreign units: home cost = foreign price ÷ X |
| Home price to foreign currency | If £1 = X foreign units: foreign price = home price × X |
| Percentage change | (new − old) ÷ old × 100 |
| Change in a price index | (new index − old index) ÷ old index × 100 |
| Real change in a value | (1 + % change in value) ÷ (1 + inflation rate) − 1 |
| Annual interest | outstanding balance × interest rate |
| Annual repayment | capital repaid + interest on the balance |
| Price including sales tax | price before tax × (1 + tax rate) |
| Profit after corporation tax | profit before tax × (1 − tax rate) |

### Method in steps: exchange rate questions

1. Write the rate as "£1 = X foreign units".
2. Converting a foreign cost into pounds? **Divide** by X. Converting a pound price into foreign currency? **Multiply** by X.
3. Do it at the old rate and the new rate.
4. Find the difference and the percentage change.
5. Say what it means: who gains, who loses, and what the business could do.

### Method in steps: price index questions

1. Find the change in points.
2. Divide by the **earlier** index value, not by 100.
3. Compare with the business's own price, cost or pay change to decide whether it has gained or lost in real terms.

### Method in steps: interest questions

1. Use the **outstanding** balance, not the original loan.
2. Interest = balance × rate, at the old and the new rate.
3. Add any capital repayment to get the total payment.
4. Comment on profit, cash flow and whether planned investment is still affordable.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Appreciation vs depreciation.** An appreciation makes exports dearer abroad and imports cheaper at home; a depreciation does the reverse.
- **Volatility** is unpredictability, not direction. It makes costing, pricing and planning harder even if the average rate is unchanged.
- **Inflation vs deflation.** A **lower** inflation rate still means prices are rising, only more slowly. Deflation means the index itself falls.
- **Recession vs slower growth.** Slower growth means GDP still rises; recession means it falls.
- **Tariff vs quota vs trade regulation.** A tariff is a tax on imports; a quota limits the quantity; a regulation sets standards, licences or paperwork.
- **Income tax vs sales tax vs corporation tax.** Income tax affects disposable income and take-home pay; sales tax affects the shelf price; corporation tax affects profit after tax and so funds for dividends and investment.
- **Digital vs disruptive technology.** Digital technology improves how firms work; disruptive technology replaces a product or business model.
- **Shareholder vs stakeholder concept.** Shareholder: the firm exists mainly to maximise owners' returns. Stakeholder: the firm should balance the interests of everyone it affects.

## Social, technological and environmental: quick points

- **Age structure**: older populations shift demand to health, care and leisure; fewer young workers.
- **Migration**: bigger, more varied labour pool and new markets; emigration can drain skills.
- **Workforce size and composition**: affects recruitment, shift patterns and pay.
- **Education and training**: supports high-skill strategies; gaps raise training costs.
- **Cultural issues**: adapt products, promotion and management style between countries.
- **CSR**: for (reputation, loyalty, recruitment, investor support, lower long-term risk); against (cost, hard to measure, short-term profit, risk of claims not matching conduct). Pressures come from customers, pressure groups, media, staff, investors and government.
- **AI**: lower costs and faster decisions; job changes, retraining, errors, bias and data concerns.
- **Climate change**: physical disruption, pressure to cut emissions, demand for low-carbon goods.

## Response toolkit by function

| Function | Typical responses to an external change |
|---|---|
| Marketing | Reprice, launch a cheaper or greener range, target new segments, switch markets |
| Operations | Change suppliers or inputs, relocate, automate, adjust capacity |
| Finance | Fix interest or exchange rates in advance, protect cash, delay or bring forward investment |
| Human resources | Recruit from new groups, retrain, change pay or working patterns |
| Strategy | Enter or leave a market, invest inside a tariff wall, use a trade agreement, reposition |

A strong answer picks the response that fits the firm's resources and the likely size and duration of the change.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the six PESTLE factors.
2. The rate moves from £1 = ₹108 to £1 = ₹100. Has the pound appreciated or depreciated?
3. A component costs ₹27,000. Find its cost in pounds at both rates in question 2, and the percentage change.
4. A firm sells a product for £60. The rate moves from £1 = CHF1.10 to £1 = CHF1.04. Find the Swiss franc price before and after, and the percentage change.
5. A price index rises from 140 to 147. What is the inflation rate?
6. A price index falls from 102.0 to 100.98. What has happened to prices?
7. A firm has £500,000 outstanding on a loan. The interest rate rises from 4% to 5.5%. Find the change in annual interest.
8. A product costs £24 before a 20% sales tax. What is the price with tax?
9. Give **two** ways the law can affect the labour market.
10. State **one** reason a business might decide against CSR.
11. What makes a technology "disruptive"?
12. Unemployment falls sharply. Give **one** effect on the HR function.

### Answers

1. Political, economic, social, technological, legal, environmental.
2. **Depreciated**: £1 now buys fewer rupees.
3. 27,000 ÷ 108 = **£250**; 27,000 ÷ 100 = **£270**; (270 − 250) ÷ 250 × 100 = **8% rise**.
4. 60 × 1.10 = **CHF66.00**; 60 × 1.04 = **CHF62.40**; a fall of 3.60 ÷ 66 × 100 = **5.45%**, cheaper for Swiss buyers.
5. (147 − 140) ÷ 140 × 100 = **5%**.
6. (100.98 − 102.0) ÷ 102.0 × 100 = −1%: **1% deflation**.
7. 500,000 × 0.04 = £20,000; 500,000 × 0.055 = £27,500; **£7,500 more**.
8. 24 × 1.20 = **£28.80**.
9. Any two: minimum pay, maximum working hours, fair dismissal rules, protection from discrimination, paid leave.
10. Any one: it costs money and cuts short-term profit; benefits are hard to measure; shareholders may want higher returns instead.
11. It creates a new product or business model that displaces established ones, often starting as a cheaper, simpler offer.
12. Any one: harder to recruit; pressure to raise pay; more training of existing staff; more reason to automate.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Converting the wrong way: dividing when the rate has to be multiplied, or the reverse.
- Treating a rise in index points as the percentage inflation rate.
- Calculating interest on the original loan when an outstanding balance is given.
- Forgetting to add capital repayments when the question asks for the total annual payment.
- Saying "lower inflation means prices are falling".
- Naming a PESTLE factor without explaining whether it is an opportunity or a threat for the business in the case.
- Writing about specific laws or government budgets, which the specification says are not required.
- Treating CSR as automatically good without weighing its cost against its benefits.
- Analysing an external change without saying how the business could respond.
- Ending an evaluation without judging which factor matters most and why.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the six PESTLE factors?**
The six factors are political, economic, social, technological, legal and environmental. In OxfordAQA A-level Business (9725) each factor should be judged as an opportunity or a threat for the specific business.

**How do you convert a foreign cost into pounds?**
Write the rate as £1 equals a number of foreign units, then divide the foreign cost by that number to get pounds. To find a foreign price from a pound price, multiply by the rate instead.

**How do you calculate inflation from a price index?**
Find the change in index points, then divide by the earlier index value, not by 100, and multiply by 100 to get a percentage. Compare the result with the business's own price rise to judge the change in real terms.

**What does a currency appreciation do to exports and imports?**
An appreciation makes a country's exports dearer abroad and its imports cheaper at home. A depreciation does the reverse.
