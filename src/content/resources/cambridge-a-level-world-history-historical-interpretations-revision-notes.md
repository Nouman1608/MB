Historical interpretations is Cambridge 9489 Paper 3: one unseen, unattributed extract of up to 600 words from a historian, and one 40-mark question asking what you can learn about the historian's **interpretation and approach**. You study one of three topics -- the origins of the First World War, the Holocaust, or the origins and development of the Cold War -- and the examiner rewards a response that identifies the extract's *main* interpretation as a whole and tests it with accurate contextual knowledge.

Related: [Historical interpretations study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-world-history-historical-interpretations-study-guide/) and
[practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-world-history-historical-interpretations-practice/).

## Syllabus coverage (9489, 2027-2029)

| Paper 3 topic | Over-arching key question | Context period |
|---|---|---|
| Topic 1: The origins of the First World War | "Who or what was to blame for the First World War?" | c.1890-1914 |
| Topic 2: The Holocaust | "Who or what was to blame for the Holocaust?" | Antisemitism before 1933 to the end of the war |
| Topic 3: The origins and development of the Cold War | "Who or what was to blame for the Cold War?" | Europe, 1941-49 |

For every topic the syllabus also asks you to explain *why* historians differ: the fragmentary nature of evidence, selection of evidence, new evidence and new interpretations over time, and the influence of the time and place in which a historian works, their questions and their ideology.

## What the exam means by "interpretation" and "approach"

| Term | Cambridge's meaning (9489 syllabus, Paper 3) |
|---|---|
| Interpretation | What can be inferred from the extract about the historian's claims and conclusions |
| Approach | What the historian brings to the study: interests, questions asked, ideology and beliefs |
| Sub-message | A secondary point inside the extract; explaining only these caps the answer |
| AO4 (20 marks) | Analysing and evaluating how the past has been interpreted |
| AO1 (20 marks) | Accurate, detailed, relevant knowledge used to test the extract |

You do **not** need to separate interpretation from approach, and you will never be asked to name the author.

## Topic 1: The origins of the First World War -- key positions

| Position | Associated historians / moments | Core claim |
|---|---|---|
| War guilt | Article 231, Treaty of Versailles (1919) | Germany and its allies responsible |
| 1920s-30s revisionism | Harry Elmer Barnes (1926); Sidney Fay (1928) | Blame lies elsewhere (France, Russia) or is shared |
| Shared responsibility | Lloyd George's "slithered over the brink" (*War Memoirs*); Franco-German historians' agreement (1951) | No single government planned a general war |
| Fischer thesis | Fritz Fischer, *Griff nach der Weltmacht* (1961), *War of Illusions* (1969) | German leaders pursued world-power aims and accepted war in 1914; continuity with later war aims (September Programme) |
| German reaction | Gerhard Ritter and the "Fischer controversy" of the 1960s | Fischer read wartime aims back into 1914 |
| Modern multi-national | Christopher Clark, *The Sleepwalkers* (2012); Sean McMeekin (2011) on Russia | Decision-makers in several capitals share blame; contingency matters |
| Structural | James Joll (1984), A.J.P. Taylor's "war by timetable" (1969) | Alliances, militarism, mobilisation plans, "unspoken assumptions" |

**Centenary (2014):** Clark's book sold strongly in Germany and was read as easing German guilt; historians such as Annika Mombauer and John Röhl restated the weight of German and Austro-Hungarian decisions.

## Topic 2: The Holocaust -- key positions

| Position | Associated historians | Core claim |
|---|---|---|
| Long-term racism | Daniel Goldhagen (1996) on "eliminationist antisemitism" | Pre-Nazi German culture made genocide possible |
| Intentionalism | Lucy Dawidowicz (1975), Gerald Fleming (1982) | Hitler's ideology and intention drove policy towards murder |
| Structuralism | Martin Broszat, Hans Mommsen ("cumulative radicalisation") | A chaotic, competitive Nazi state radicalised itself |
| Functionalism | Karl Schleunes, *The Twisted Road to Auschwitz* (1970) | Contingent factors -- above all war -- shaped each step |
| Synthesis | Ian Kershaw ("working towards the Führer"); Christopher Browning, *The Origins of the Final Solution* (2004); Saul Friedländer | Ideology set the goal; war and initiative from below set the pace |
| Perpetrators | Raul Hilberg; Browning, *Ordinary Men* (1992); Jan Gross, *Neighbors* (2001) | Many "ordinary" people took part; non-German participation was significant |
| Victims and resistance | Hilberg's early critique; Yehuda Bauer's broad definition of resistance (*amidah*) | How resistance is defined changes the verdict |
| Bystanders | David Wyman, *The Abandonment of the Jews* (1984); William Rubinstein (1997) | Did the USA and Britain do too little, or was little possible? |

Key context dates: Nuremberg Laws (1935); Kristallnacht (November 1938); Evian Conference (July 1938); invasion of the USSR (June 1941); Wannsee Conference (January 1942); Warsaw Ghetto Uprising (April 1943); Bermuda Conference (April 1943); War Refugee Board (January 1944).

## Topic 3: The Cold War -- key positions

| School | Associated historians | Core claim | Shaped by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Traditionalist (orthodox) | Herbert Feis; Arthur Schlesinger Jr (1967) | Soviet expansion and ideology to blame; the USA reacted defensively | Early Cold War, Western official sources |
| Revisionist | William Appleman Williams (1959); Gabriel Kolko (1968); Gar Alperovitz (1965) | US economic expansion ("open door") and atomic diplomacy to blame | Vietnam War era, New Left |
| Post-revisionist | John Lewis Gaddis (1972); Melvyn Leffler (1992) | Both or neither: mutual misperception and security dilemmas | Access to more US archives, détente |
| "New" Cold War history / post-post-revisionism | Gaddis, *We Now Know* (1997); Zubok and Pleshakov (1996) | Soviet archives show Stalin's ideology and personality mattered most | Opening of Soviet-bloc archives after 1991 |

Russian perspectives: Soviet-era writing blamed US imperialism; post-Soviet historians with archive access have produced more varied and often more critical accounts of Stalin.

Key context documents and events: Yalta (February 1945); Potsdam (July-August 1945); Kennan's Long Telegram (February 1946); Churchill's "iron curtain" speech (March 1946); Novikov telegram (September 1946); Truman Doctrine (March 1947); Marshall Plan (announced June 1947); Berlin Blockade (June 1948-May 1949); NATO (April 1949).

## Worked example: finding the main interpretation

An extract argues that Austria-Hungary chose war on Serbia to save itself, that Germany's "blank cheque" encouraged this, and that Russia's early mobilisation turned a local war into a general one.

1. **Main interpretation (whole extract):** responsibility was shared, with the decisive choices made in Vienna and St Petersburg, not only in Berlin.
2. **Sub-messages:** the blank cheque; Russian mobilisation. Explaining only one of these is Level 3 territory.
3. **Approach:** emphasis on decision-makers across several capitals and on contingency -- a modern multi-national approach (compare Clark).
4. **Test with knowledge:** Austro-Hungarian ultimatum to Serbia (23 July 1914); Russian general mobilisation (30 July); German declaration of war on Russia (1 August). Where does the evidence support or strain the claim?

## Common mistakes / exam traps

- **Labelling instead of explaining.** "This is revisionist" earns little unless the extract's claims are explained. Cambridge mark schemes warn that forcing a historiographical label onto an extract that does not fit (for example, a bystander extract on the Holocaust) shows misunderstanding.
- **Chasing sub-messages.** Explaining a different point in each paragraph is described in the mark scheme as Level 3 behaviour.
- **Context without the extract.** Knowledge with no reference to the interpretation cannot be rewarded.
- **Guessing the author.** It is not required and wastes time.
- **Dating the historian by vocabulary alone.** Infer the approach from the argument and emphasis, not from a single phrase.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long is the Paper 3 extract?**
No more than 600 words, from a single historian, unattributed. You have 1 hour 15 minutes for one 40-mark answer.

**Do I need to know named historians?**
Not for the question itself, but knowing the main schools helps you explain the approach. Use names only to illuminate the extract's argument.

**How are the marks split?**
The syllabus weights Paper 3 at 50% AO1 knowledge and 50% AO4 interpretations; published mark schemes award up to 20 marks for each, usually at the same level.

**Can I study more than one topic?**
You answer on one topic only, so most centres teach one in depth.

## Self-test

1. What does Article 231 state? *Responsibility of Germany and its allies for the war.*
2. What did Fischer's *War of Illusions* add to his argument? *That German leaders were moving towards war from around the War Council of December 1912.*
3. Define "cumulative radicalisation". *Mommsen's term for Nazi policy escalating through competition inside the regime.*
4. What changed Cold War historiography after 1991? *Access to Soviet-bloc archives.*
5. What caps a Paper 3 answer at Level 3? *Explaining only sub-messages, or different interpretations in different paragraphs.*
