These notes condense section **3.5.4 Longitudinal and transverse waves** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630) specification: the "nature of longitudinal and transverse waves", the direction of displacement of "particles/fields relative to the direction of energy propagation", the equal vacuum speed of all electromagnetic waves, the "use of ultrasound in medicine" and "polarisation as evidence for the nature of transverse waves". Malus's law is not expected.

The explanations and worked examples are in the [longitudinal and transverse waves study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-longitudinal-and-transverse-waves-oxfordaqa/). Test yourself afterwards with the [longitudinal and transverse waves practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-longitudinal-and-transverse-waves-oxfordaqa-practice/).

## Core recall

- **Transverse:** displacement of particles or fields at 90° to the direction of energy propagation. Examples: waves on a string, all electromagnetic waves.
- **Longitudinal:** displacement along the direction of energy propagation, giving compressions and rarefactions. Example: sound.
- Electromagnetic waves are oscillating electric and magnetic fields, perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel. They need no medium.
- All electromagnetic waves travel at 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ in a vacuum, whatever their frequency.
- Sound needs a medium: it cannot travel through a vacuum.
- Only transverse waves can be polarised. Polarisation is the evidence that a wave is transverse.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Transverse wave | Oscillation perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer |
| Longitudinal wave | Oscillation parallel to the direction of energy transfer |
| Compression | Region of a longitudinal wave where particles are closer together than normal; higher pressure |
| Rarefaction | Region where particles are further apart than normal; lower pressure |
| Ultrasound | Sound with frequency above the range of human hearing (above about 20 kHz) |
| Transducer | Device in an ultrasound probe that emits pulses and detects echoes |
| Plane-polarised | Oscillations confined to one plane containing the direction of travel |
| Unpolarised | Oscillations in many directions perpendicular to the direction of travel |
| Polaroid | Material that transmits only the component of the electric field along its transmission axis |
| Transmission axis | Direction of oscillation a polariser lets through |

## Longitudinal graphs

On a displacement-distance graph for sound, "displacement" means along the direction of travel (positive = forwards).

| Point on the graph | Particles nearby | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| Zero crossing, curve going down | Those behind displaced forwards, those ahead backwards | Centre of a compression |
| Zero crossing, curve going up | Those behind displaced backwards, those ahead forwards | Centre of a rarefaction |
| Maximum or minimum | All displaced by nearly the same amount | Normal pressure |

Compression to next compression = λ. Compression to neighbouring rarefaction = λ/2.

## Ultrasound in medicine: the points that score

- The probe sends short pulses and times the echoes from boundaries between tissues.
- Depth of a boundary = (speed × echo time) / 2, because the pulse travels there and back.
- A coupling gel removes air between probe and skin; without it most of the ultrasound is reflected at the air boundary.
- Higher frequency: shorter wavelength, finer detail, but stronger absorption and less depth.
- Lower frequency: deeper imaging, coarser detail.
- Ultrasound is non-ionising, which is why it is used for imaging a fetus.
- It images poorly through air-filled organs such as the lungs and is strongly reflected and absorbed by bone.

## Polarisation: the argument in three lines

1. A transverse wave has an oscillation direction perpendicular to its travel that can be selected.
2. A polariser passes one direction of oscillation and absorbs the perpendicular component.
3. A longitudinal wave has no perpendicular oscillation to select, so a polariser has no effect.

## Applications

| Application | What happens |
|---|---|
| Two Polaroids, axes parallel | Light polarised by the first passes through the second |
| Two Polaroids, axes crossed (90°) | Almost no light passes |
| Rotating one Polaroid through 360° | Two maxima and two minima of transmitted intensity |
| Polaroid sunglasses | Vertical axis absorbs much of the horizontally polarised glare reflected from water or roads |
| Rod aerials | Receiver must be parallel to the transmitter's electric field for the strongest signal |
| Microwave transmitter and receiver | Rotating the receiver about the beam gives maxima and near-zero minima, showing the waves are polarised |

## Quick reminders with numbers

- A 500 Hz sound wave at 340 m s⁻¹ has λ = 0.68 m, so compressions are 0.68 m apart.
- At 1540 m s⁻¹ in tissue, 5.0 MHz ultrasound has λ = 0.31 mm.
- An echo returning after 52 μs at 1540 m s⁻¹ comes from a boundary 4.0 cm deep.
- Light and radio from a source 3.0 × 10⁹ m away both arrive after 10 s.

## Exam traps

- "Transverse means up and down" earns nothing; compare with the direction of energy travel.
- Compressions are at points of zero displacement, not maximum displacement.
- Polarisation is evidence that a wave is transverse, not evidence that it is a wave.
- Do not use Malus's law: the specification says it will not be expected.
- Higher-frequency ultrasound does not go deeper.
- Rotating a vertical rod aerial about its own axis changes nothing; tilting it away from vertical does.
- Sound cannot be polarised because it is longitudinal, not because it is "too slow" or "not electromagnetic".

## Self-test

1. Define a transverse wave.
2. Give one example of a longitudinal wave and two of transverse waves.
3. State the speed of X-rays in a vacuum.
4. Describe how the electric and magnetic fields in an electromagnetic wave are arranged.
5. On a sound wave's displacement-distance graph, the curve crosses zero going downwards at x = 1.2 m. What is at that point?
6. Compressions in a sound wave are 0.85 m apart and the speed is 340 m s⁻¹. Find the frequency.
7. Why is gel used between an ultrasound probe and the skin?
8. Give one advantage and one disadvantage of using a higher ultrasound frequency.
9. An echo arrives 39 μs after the pulse. The speed in tissue is 1540 m s⁻¹. How deep is the boundary?
10. Explain why sound cannot be polarised.
11. Two Polaroid sheets are crossed. Describe what is seen through them.
12. A transmitter's aerial is horizontal. How should a receiving rod aerial be placed?

### Answers

1. A wave in which the oscillation of the particles or fields is perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer.
2. Longitudinal: **sound**. Transverse: any two of **waves on a string, light, radio waves, microwaves** (any electromagnetic wave).
3. **3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹**, the same as all electromagnetic waves.
4. They oscillate **perpendicular to each other and both perpendicular** to the direction of travel.
5. The centre of a **compression**.
6. λ = 0.85 m, so f = 340/0.85 = **400 Hz**.
7. To remove the air gap, because nearly all the ultrasound would be **reflected** at an air boundary.
8. Advantage: **finer detail** (shorter wavelength). Disadvantage: **more absorption**, so less depth.
9. d = 1540 × 39 × 10⁻⁶ / 2 = **0.030 m** (3.0 cm).
10. Sound is **longitudinal**: its oscillation is along the direction of travel, so there is no perpendicular direction for a polariser to select.
11. **Almost no light** passes; the view is dark.
12. **Horizontal and parallel** to the transmitting aerial.
