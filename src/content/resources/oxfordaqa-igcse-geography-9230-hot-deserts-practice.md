## Section A

**1.** State the approximate latitudes at which most hot deserts are found. **[1]**

**2.** Give **one** adaptation of a desert animal. **[1]**

**3.** Define the term desertification. **[1]**

**4.** Name **two** causes of desertification. **[2]**

**5.** State **one** way that trees can reduce the risk of desertification. **[1]**

## Section B

**6.** The table shows monthly climate data for an invented desert location.

| Month | Mean temperature (°C) | Rainfall (mm) |
|---|---|---|
| January | 14 | 4 |
| April | 27 | 2 |
| July | 38 | 0 |
| October | 28 | 6 |

Total annual rainfall for the year is 14 mm.

**(a)** Calculate the range of mean monthly temperatures shown in the table. **[1]**

**(b)** Describe **two** features of the climate that show this is a hot desert. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the temperature at night in a hot desert is often much lower than the daytime temperature. **[2]**

**7.** Explain why hot deserts are found at about 30° north and south of the equator. **[4]**

**8.** Explain how **two** features of a named desert plant or animal help it to survive in the desert environment. **[4]**

**9.** Explain why the soils of a hot desert are low in nutrients and organic matter. **[3]**

**10.** Explain why biodiversity in hot deserts is vulnerable to human activity. **[4]**

**11.** For an area you have studied, explain the causes of desertification. **[6]**

**12.** For an area you have studied, assess the effectiveness of strategies used to reduce the risk of desertification. **[6]**

**13.** "Overgrazing is the main cause of desertification." To what extent do you agree? **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** About 15° to 30° north and south of the equator (accept 20° to 30°) [1]. **[1]**

**2.** Any one from: large ears to lose heat (fennec fox); nocturnal behaviour; burrowing; ability to get water from food (kangaroo rat); a hump of fat (camel); wide feet [1]. **[1]**

**3.** The degradation of land in dry areas, so that it becomes desert-like and cannot support plants, animals and people [1]. **[1]**

**4.** Any two from: climate change; population growth; removal of fuel wood; overgrazing; over-cultivation; soil erosion [1 each]. **[2]**

**5.** Roots bind the soil; or trees act as a windbreak; or provide shade, fuel or fodder [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** 38 - 14 = 24°C [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** Very low rainfall of only 14 mm a year [1]. High temperatures, with a summer mean of 38°C [1]. (Accept: a large seasonal range; dry in all months.) **[2]**

**(c)** The skies are clear, with few clouds [1], so heat escapes quickly after sunset, and there is little moisture in the air to hold the heat [1]. **[2]**

**7.** Air rises at the equator, cools and loses its moisture as rain [1]. It spreads out at height and sinks at about 30° [1]. Sinking air warms and is compressed, creating high pressure [1]. As it warms, clouds do not form, so there is very little rain [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Named plant or animal [1]. First feature explained, for example a cactus stores water in its stem, so it can survive long dry periods [2]. Second feature explained, for example wide shallow roots collect water quickly after rare rain [2]. (Marks: 1 for the name or both features named, 3 for explanations.) Maximum 4. **[4]**

**9.** There is little vegetation, so few dead leaves and roots are added to the soil [1]. Dry conditions mean slow decay, and few organisms live in the soil [1]. Sparse plant roots and rapid runoff or evaporation mean that nutrients are not retained, and salts can build up [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Conditions are extreme, so species are specialised and have a narrow range of tolerance [1]. Vegetation grows and recovers very slowly, so damage lasts [1]. Water is scarce and often concentrated at oases, which are used by both wildlife and people [1]. Activities such as farming, mining, off-road vehicles and hunting remove or disturb habitats [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Named area, such as the Sahel [1]. Causes with developed detail, up to 5 further marks. For example: climate change has brought erratic rain and droughts that kill vegetation [2]; overgrazing by growing herds removes plant cover and exposes soil [2]; cutting trees for fuel wood removes roots that bind the soil [2]; population growth increases pressure on land [2]; over-cultivation exhausts the soil [2]. Maximum 6. **[6]**

**12.** Named area [1]. Strategies described, for example stone lines and planting pits to trap water and soil, tree planting or protecting regrowing trees, appropriate technology such as fuel-efficient stoves [2]. Evidence of success, for example improved yields or increased tree cover [1]. Limitations, for example high cost, insecurity, trees dying, schemes not maintained [1]. Judgement on effectiveness, for example that low-cost local schemes are more successful than large projects, with a reason [1]. Maximum 6. **[6]**

**13.** Indicative content:
- Agree: overgrazing removes the plant cover that protects soil, which leads directly to erosion [1].
- Agree: large herds on the desert edge can cause damage over wide areas [1].
- Disagree: climate change and drought can kill vegetation even without grazing [1].
- Disagree: fuel wood removal and over-cultivation also strip cover and exhaust soils [1].
- Disagree: population growth is an underlying cause that increases all the other pressures [1].
- Judgement: overgrazing is a major immediate cause, but it works together with climate, population and cultivation, so no single cause is the main one everywhere; a justified conclusion is needed [1]. Maximum 6. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing that deserts have "no rain" rather than low, unreliable rain.
- Not using figures from the table, or leaving out the unit.
- Explaining desert location without mentioning sinking air and high pressure.
- Giving a cause of desertification as a single word with no mechanism.
- Giving strategies with no explanation of how they work.
- Case-study answers with no named place.
