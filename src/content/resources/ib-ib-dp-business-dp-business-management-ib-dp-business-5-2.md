This guide goes deeper into section 5.2, Operations methods, of the IB *Business management guide* (first assessment 2024). The section applies to SL and HL. It names four methods: job production, batch production, mass/flow production and mass customization, and sets them at AO3, so the exam expects you to weigh one method against another for a named business, not just describe each.

The [Unit 5 guide to operations management and operations methods](/resources/ib-dp-business-management-introduction-to-operations-management-and-operations-methods/) already defines the four methods, lists their strengths and limitations and costs one example of each. This page builds on it with three things that guide does not cover in detail: the factors that decide which method suits a business, the problems of switching from one method to another, and how to write an AO3 answer on method choice. Condense it with the [5.2 revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-business-dp-business-management-ib-dp-business-5-2-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [5.2 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-business-dp-business-management-ib-dp-business-5-2-practice/). The course hub is [IB DP Business Management](/boards/ib/ib-dp/business/).

## What 5.2 asks you to do

| Content point | Depth | What a strong answer shows |
|---|---|---|
| Job production | AO3 | When a one-off, made-to-order product is worth its higher cost |
| Batch production | AO3 | When groups of identical items, with changeovers between them, suit demand |
| Mass/flow production | AO3 | When continuous output of a standard product is justified by demand |
| Mass customization | AO3 | When standard parts can be combined to personalize at scale |

The guide gives no formulas for 5.2. Any calculation below is a standard costing tool used to support a judgement, not content you must reproduce.

## Two questions that place every method

Ask two questions about any product or service.

1. **How many identical units are needed?** One, a group, or a continuous stream.
2. **How much does each customer want something different?** Not at all, a choice of a few lines, or a personal version.

| | Low variety | High variety |
|---|---|---|
| **High volume** | Mass/flow production | Mass customization |
| **Medium volume** | Batch production | Batch with many short runs |
| **Low volume** | Small batches of a standard item | Job production |

The grid explains why the methods exist. As volume rises, the cost of equipment and set-up can be spread over more units. As variety rises, the business needs flexible equipment and skilled labour. Mass customization is the attempt to have both: high volume from standard modules, and variety from the way modules are combined.

## Factors that decide the method

When a question asks which method suits a business, test each option against these factors and use evidence from the case.

- **Size and stability of demand.** Flow production needs high, predictable demand to keep the line busy. Seasonal or uncertain demand favours batch.
- **Nature of the product.** A bridge, a wedding dress or a software system for one client can only be made as a job. A product built from interchangeable parts suits flow or mass customization.
- **Customer expectations.** Customers who pay for uniqueness accept a long wait and a high price. Customers who want speed and a low price prefer a standard product from stock.
- **Finance available.** Flow lines and flexible automated cells need large capital spending. A small business may only be able to afford job or batch methods.
- **Labour skills.** Job production depends on multi-skilled workers. Flow production uses more specialized, often repetitive tasks, or machines.
- **Technology.** Digital ordering, computer-controlled machines and 3D printing make mass customization possible where it once was not.
- **Objectives and values.** A business that sees craftsmanship as its identity may keep job production even if another method is cheaper. The guide's concepts of ethics and sustainability matter too: flow production can create waste if output runs ahead of demand, while made-to-order methods produce less unsold stock.

## Services use the same methods

The four methods are not only for factories. A wedding planner designing one event works by job production. A tour company running the same coach trip at fixed departure times works in batches. A fast-food kitchen with an assembly line works by flow. A meal-kit website letting customers choose dishes from a fixed menu of components uses mass customization. Applying a method to a service business is a good way to show understanding.

## Many businesses mix methods

A single business can use more than one method. An invented bakery, Corrowen Bakes, makes bread in batches each morning, makes celebration cakes to order by job production, and supplies one standard roll to a café chain on a continuous line. Each product line gets the method that fits its demand. In an exam answer, saying that a business could keep one method for one line and change it for another is often more realistic than recommending a single switch for everything.

## Worked example: where each method becomes cheapest

Saltmarsh Kites is an invented maker of kites. All figures are invented and in dollars. Its accountant estimates yearly costs under three methods.

| Method | Fixed costs per year | Variable cost per kite |
|---|---|---|
| Job | 0 (simplified) | 85 |
| Batch | 6,000 | 40 |
| Mass/flow | 30,000 | 22 |

**Job against batch.** Set total costs equal: 85Q = 6,000 + 40Q, so 45Q = 6,000 and Q = 133.3. Batch is cheaper from 134 kites a year.

**Batch against flow.** 6,000 + 40Q = 30,000 + 22Q, so 18Q = 24,000 and Q = 1,333.3. Flow is cheaper from 1,334 kites a year.

At 900 kites a year:

- Batch: 6,000 + (900 × 40) = 42,000, which is 46.67 a kite.
- Flow: 30,000 + (900 × 22) = 49,800, which is 55.33 a kite.

At 2,000 kites a year:

- Batch: 6,000 + (2,000 × 40) = 86,000, which is 43.00 a kite.
- Flow: 30,000 + (2,000 × 22) = 74,000, which is 37.00 a kite.

So the cheapest method depends on volume. The numbers do not settle the decision on their own. If forecast demand is 1,500 kites but could fall to 900, flow production carries the risk of a high cost per unit with an idle line. Batch production also lets the business offer several designs, which the cost table ignores.

## Switching from one method to another

Questions often describe a business that is growing or changing its market. The move between methods affects the whole organization.

| Change | Likely reasons | Problems to manage |
|---|---|---|
| Job to batch | Repeat orders for the same design; growth | Craft workers may resist repetitive work; product may lose its exclusive image |
| Batch to mass/flow | Demand has become high and stable | Large capital cost; retraining or redundancies; less variety; risk if demand falls |
| Batch or flow to mass customization | Customers want personal products; online ordering possible | New software, flexible machines and a reliable supply of components; longer delivery times |
| Flow back to batch | Demand has fallen or fragmented | Expensive line under-used; some workers no longer needed |

Link each problem to another business function. Finance must fund the investment. Human resources must recruit, retrain or reorganize. Marketing must decide whether the new price, variety and delivery time still suit the target market. The guide's conceptual understanding that change in operations affects other business activities is exactly this point.

## Annotated model answer

*Question (AO3 style):* Corrowen Bakes is considering replacing batch production of its bread with a flow line. Discuss this proposal.

> A flow line would cut cost per loaf if demand stays high, because the large fixed cost is spread over continuous output, so Corrowen could lower prices to win supermarket contracts.

*Annotation:* a benefit, with the reason and a link to the business.

> However, Corrowen sells eight varieties of bread, and a flow line suits one standard product. It would either lose variety, which its café customers value, or need several lines it cannot afford.

*Annotation:* a limitation drawn from the case, not a general list.

> The change would also need capital spending and fewer, less skilled workers, which could harm morale in a small business that sees itself as a craft bakery.

*Annotation:* links to finance and human resources.

> On balance, Corrowen should keep batch production for most varieties and consider a flow line only for its best-selling loaf, if contract demand for that loaf is secure.

*Annotation:* a justified judgement that says what it depends on.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Define or outline (AO1):** one precise sentence on what the method is, with a short example.
- **Explain or analyse (AO2):** give the cause and the effect for the business in the case, such as "flow production lowers cost per unit because fixed costs are spread over more units".
- **Discuss, evaluate or recommend (AO3):** compare at least two methods using case evidence, give arguments on both sides, then reach a judgement that names the conditions it depends on.
- **Calculate (AO4):** show each step and give units. Then use the number in your argument.

## Common mistakes

- Treating mass customization as a type of job production. It uses mass-production methods and standard modules.
- Calling a method "best" without saying for which product, volume or customer.
- Recommending a switch to flow production without checking that demand is high and stable.
- Ignoring the people involved when a method changes.
- Forgetting that a business can use different methods for different product lines.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which IB Business Management section covers operations methods?**
Section 5.2 in Unit 5 of the guide (first assessment 2024). It covers job, batch, mass/flow production and mass customization at AO3, for SL and HL.

**How do you choose the right operations method?**
Weigh the size and stability of demand, the nature of the product, customer expectations, finance, labour skills, technology and the business's objectives, using evidence from the case.

**Can a business use more than one operations method?**
Yes. Many businesses use batch production for regular lines, job production for special orders and flow for a high-volume standard product.

**Is mass customization the same as job production?**
No. Job production makes a unique product from scratch. Mass customization combines standard components in different ways, using mass-production methods.
