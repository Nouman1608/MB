## Section A: knowledge

**1.** Give two reasons why Germany adopted the Schlieffen Plan. **[2]**

**2.** Name two weapons or features of trench warfare that made defence stronger than attack. **[2]**

**3.** Give two reasons why the British army attacked on the Somme in 1916. **[2]**

## Section B: source, account and essay questions

**Source A** *(invented)* -- From a letter imagined as written by a British sergeant to his brother, 4 July 1916.
"They told us the guns had smashed everything and we could stroll across. We went over at half past seven in broad daylight, in lines, at a walk. The wire was not cut. Fritz came up out of his holes as soon as the shelling stopped and his machine guns did the rest. The generals had it all on paper. Paper does not stop bullets."

**4.** Source A is critical of the way the Battle of the Somme was planned. How do you know? Explain your answer using Source A and your contextual knowledge. **[4]**

**5.** Write an account of how the Schlieffen Plan failed in 1914. **[8]**

**6.** Write an account of how the Battle of Verdun developed in 1916. **[8]**

**7.** Write an account of how the Third Battle of Ypres (Passchendaele) developed in 1917. **[8]**

**8.** Write an account of how new technology changed fighting on the Western Front in the years 1915-16. **[8]**

**9.** "The main reason for stalemate on the Western Front was the strength of defensive weapons." How far do you agree with this statement? Explain your answer. **[16 + 4 SPaG]**

**10.** "The Battle of the Somme was a failure for the Allies." How far do you agree with this statement? Explain your answer. **[16 + 4 SPaG]**

**11.** "Verdun was the most significant battle on the Western Front in 1916-17." How far do you agree with this statement? Explain your answer. **[16 + 4 SPaG]**

## Answers

**How these guides work.** Source question: content explained [2], context [1], provenance [1]. Accounts: an accurate sequence of events [3], causal links between them ("this led to") [3], a clear outcome explained [2]. Essays: the case for the statement, developed with evidence [6]; the case against or other factors [6]; a sustained, substantiated judgement [4]; spelling, punctuation and grammar [4].

**1.** Any two, [1] each: to avoid fighting a war on two fronts at the same time; to defeat France quickly, within about six weeks, before Russia had mobilised; to avoid the strong French border forts by going through Belgium.

**2.** Any two, [1] each: machine guns; barbed wire; artillery; deep dugouts; railways bringing reserves; the trench system of support and reserve lines.

**3.** Any two, [1] each: to relieve pressure on the French at Verdun; to break through the German lines; to wear down the German army through attrition.

**4.** Content [2]: the sergeant says they were told the guns "had smashed everything" but "the wire was not cut", and that soldiers advanced "in lines, at a walk" into machine guns, mocking the generals' planning ("Paper does not stop bullets"). Context [1]: the week-long bombardment failed because many shells were duds and Germans sheltered in deep dugouts; there were about 57,000 British casualties on 1 July. Provenance [1]: a private letter written by a front-line soldier three days after the attack records his anger frankly, not intended for publication.

**5.** Sequence [3]: Germany invaded Belgium in August 1914; Belgian forts held up the advance; the BEF fought at Mons; Russia mobilised faster than expected; the Germans turned east of Paris; the Battle of the Marne followed in September. Causal links [3]: because the plan depended on speed, Belgian resistance and the BEF's arrival cost vital time; Russia's early attack led Moltke to send troops east, weakening the western sweep; turning east of Paris opened a gap that the French and British counter-attacked into at the Marne. Outcome [2]: the Germans retreated to the Aisne and dug trenches, and after the race to the sea a continuous line meant the war of movement had become stalemate.

**6.** Sequence [3]: Falkenhayn attacked on 21 February 1916 after a huge bombardment; forts fell early; Pétain organised the defence; supplies came along the *Voie Sacrée*; fighting continued through the year; the French counter-attacked late in 1916. Causal links [3]: Falkenhayn chose Verdun because its symbolic importance meant France would defend it at any cost, so the battle became one of attrition; because the French held on, pressure grew on their allies, leading the British to bring forward the Somme offensive; as German troops were pulled away to the Somme, the French could recover ground. Outcome [2]: by December France had recovered most of the lost ground; both sides suffered hundreds of thousands of casualties, so Verdun became a symbol of French endurance but weakened the French army.

**7.** Sequence [3]: Messines Ridge was captured in June 1917 after mines were exploded; the main attack began on 31 July; heavy rain fell; fighting continued into the autumn; Canadians took Passchendaele village in November. Causal links [3]: the attack was launched because Haig wanted to reach the Belgian coast and U-boat bases, and because the French army was weakened by mutinies; shelling and rain destroyed the drainage of the low ground, so men, horses and guns sank in the mud and the advance slowed. Outcome [2]: the British gained only a few miles at a cost of hundreds of thousands of casualties, so Passchendaele became a symbol of the futility of trench warfare, though the German army was also worn down.

**8.** Sequence [3]: chlorine gas at the Second Battle of Ypres (April 1915); phosgene and gas masks; aircraft used for reconnaissance and artillery spotting; creeping barrages; tanks first used on the Somme (15 September 1916). Causal links [3]: gas was introduced to break the deadlock, which led to gas masks that reduced its effect; aircraft made artillery more accurate, which increased the power of bombardments; tanks were developed to cross wire and trenches, but early breakdowns meant they could not yet achieve a breakthrough. Outcome [2]: each innovation was quickly countered or was unreliable, so new technology changed fighting without ending the stalemate in 1915-16, though it laid foundations for the combined tactics of 1918.

**9.** Agree [6]: machine guns, barbed wire and artillery gave defenders a huge advantage; on 1 July 1916 machine guns cut down attackers; railways let defenders bring reserves faster than attackers could advance; deep dugouts protected defenders during bombardments. Disagree [6]: the failure of the Schlieffen Plan at the Marne created the trench line in the first place; the race to the sea meant there were no flanks left to go round; commanders' tactics, such as long bombardments that warned the enemy and slow infantry advances, contributed; the balance of forces meant neither side could achieve decisive superiority. Judgement [4]: for example, the failure of the Schlieffen Plan created the stalemate, but defensive weapons explain why it lasted so long; a top answer explains how the factors were linked. SPaG [4]: accurate spelling of terms such as Schlieffen, attrition, artillery and reconnaissance.

**10.** Agree [6]: about 57,000 British casualties on 1 July; the bombardment failed to cut the wire; the Allies advanced only a few miles by November; no breakthrough was achieved; criticism of Haig as a "butcher". Disagree [6]: it relieved pressure on Verdun, helping France survive; the German army suffered heavy losses it could not easily replace; tanks were introduced; the British army learned tactics such as the creeping barrage that helped in 1918. Judgement [4]: for example, it was a failure if judged by the aim of breaking through, but partly a success as attrition and in relieving Verdun. SPaG [4].

**11.** Agree [6]: Verdun drew in huge French forces and caused enormous losses on both sides; it became a national symbol for France; it shaped the timing of the Somme; Falkenhayn's attrition strategy failed and he was replaced. Disagree [6]: the Somme involved the British army on a far larger scale, introduced tanks and was a turning point in British tactics; Passchendaele (1917) weakened both armies and showed the limits of attrition; neither battle ended the stalemate. Judgement [4]: for example, Verdun was most significant for France and for causing the Somme, but the Somme was more significant for the Allied war effort as a whole. SPaG [4].
