The health and well-being context of IB Psychology (first assessment 2027) begins with mental health disorders. The guide asks you to understand **one or more of** major depressive disorder, eating disorders, post-traumatic stress disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorders or anxiety disorders. It then lists four areas of study: biological explanations, cognitive models, cultural differences and environmental factors. This guide uses **major depressive disorder** (depression) as the main worked example, because its evidence is broad, and shows where to swap in another disorder. The guide does not name required studies, so choose examples you can explain and evaluate.

Related: [health psychology study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-psychology-health-psychology-study-guide/), [assessing treatment effectiveness](/resources/ib-ib-dp-psychology-assessing-treatment-effectiveness/), [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-mental-health-disorders-revision-notes/) and [practice set](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-mental-health-disorders-practice/).

## Where this fits

The guide describes mental and physical health as linked through the mind-body interaction, and says any separation is artificial. It also says mental and physical health can be measured objectively to some extent, while well-being is reported subjectively. For a mental health disorder, the questions to keep asking are: what is the explanation, what is the evidence, how was the disorder measured, and who is missing from the sample? The guide's sub-headings are not used to form exam questions, so the ideas below can appear in any health and well-being question and may combine with the content approaches and with concepts such as bias, causality, change and measurement.

Note that treatment of the same disorder is a separate area ("Prevention and treatment"), covered in the page on assessing treatment effectiveness. This page focuses on explanation and prevalence.

## Defining the disorder

Depression (major depressive disorder) is diagnosed from a cluster of symptoms lasting at least two weeks, including persistent low mood or loss of interest, with changes in sleep, appetite, energy and concentration, and feelings of worthlessness. Classification systems such as the DSM and ICD list symptoms, and diagnosis depends on clinical judgement. Remember that a diagnosis is a **measurement** decision: symptom checklists operationalise a complex experience, and thresholds can differ by clinician and culture.

## 1. Biological explanations

The guide requires **one or more biological explanations of one disorder**. Three families of explanation are common.

**Genetics.** Twin and family studies compare identical twins (who share all their genes) with non-identical twins (who share about half). If depression is more similar in identical twins, heredity plays a role. A meta-analysis by Sullivan, Neale and Kendler (2000) estimated heritability of major depression at roughly a third to two-fifths. Heritability does not mean fixed destiny: it describes variation in a population, and the remainder is environmental. The diathesis-stress model says a genetic vulnerability (diathesis) makes a disorder more likely when stress occurs.

**Neurotransmitters.** The monoamine hypothesis proposed that low serotonin (and noradrenaline) levels contribute to depression. It was supported by the effect of drugs that raise serotonin availability (SSRIs). However, a recent large review (Moncrieff et al., 2022) concluded there is no consistent evidence that low serotonin causes depression. SSRIs also take weeks to work, though they raise serotonin quickly, so the story must be more complex. A fair evaluation: neurotransmitters are involved, but the simple deficit idea is not supported, and a drug working does not prove its target was the cause (the reverse inference problem).

**Brain structure and the stress system.** Many people with depression have an overactive stress response (raised cortisol from the HPA axis), and imaging studies report smaller hippocampal volume in some groups. These are correlational, so it is unclear whether the brain differences cause depression or result from long exposure to stress.

**Worked example (a two-part claim).** "Genes influence vulnerability to depression because identical twins show higher concordance than non-identical twins (mechanism and evidence). However, concordance is far from 100 per cent, so environmental factors such as stressful events must also matter (limitation)." Always pair a claim with its evidence and a limitation.

**Evaluation.**
- Strengths: objective measures, biological explanations lead to testable treatments, and gene-environment ideas fit the data well.
- Limitations: reductionism (ignores thoughts and social context), causal direction is unclear in imaging, and twin studies assume that identical and non-identical twins share the same environments.
- **Concepts:** causality, perspective (reductionism), measurement.

## 2. Cognitive models

The guide asks for **the value of cognitive models** in understanding a disorder. A cognitive model explains the disorder through thinking patterns.

**Beck's cognitive model.** Beck proposed that people with depression hold negative **schemas** formed in earlier experience, which are activated by stress and produce the **cognitive triad**: negative views of the self, the world and the future. Cognitive biases, such as focusing on failures and overgeneralising from one setback, keep the cycle going. For example, a student fails one test and thinks "I'm useless; nothing will change", then avoids studying, which leads to another poor result.

**Learned helplessness and attributional style.** Seligman's theory, later reformulated with attributions, says depression is more likely when a person explains bad events as internal, stable and global ("it's all my fault, it will always be like this, and it affects everything").

**Value of the models.** They are testable, they link thinking to behaviour and feeling, and they underpin cognitive behavioural therapy. Evaluation points: much of the evidence is correlational, so negative thinking may be a symptom rather than a cause. Depressed people may also sometimes be more realistic ("depressive realism"), which complicates the picture. Models can underplay biology and social conditions such as poverty.

**Other disorders.** For anxiety disorders, the cognitive model stresses threat bias and catastrophic thinking. For eating disorders, it stresses distorted beliefs about weight and shape. For PTSD, it stresses how traumatic memories are processed, with intrusive memories and avoidance. For OCD, it stresses misinterpretation of normal intrusive thoughts as dangerous.

**Concepts:** causality, bias (cognitive bias as both a feature of the disorder and a research problem), change (models inform treatment).

## 3. Cultural differences in prevalence and approaches

The guide asks for **factors that may explain differences in prevalence rates between cultures or populations**, and **examples of cultural differences in approaches to mental health**. The word "may" matters: prevalence figures are hard to compare, so you must discuss explanations and cautions.

Possible reasons for differences in reported prevalence:

- **Real differences in risk.** Exposure to conflict, poverty, urban stress or social isolation varies between populations. Social support structures also vary.
- **Cultural expression of symptoms.** In some cultures distress is expressed through bodily complaints (somatisation) rather than words for sadness. Kleinman's research on patients in China, for example, found that many described physical symptoms and received different diagnoses than a Western clinician would give.
- **Stigma and help-seeking.** Where mental illness is highly stigmatised, people may not seek diagnosis, so recorded prevalence is lower.
- **Diagnostic and measurement bias.** Classification systems and screening tools were developed mainly in Western settings, so applying them elsewhere may miss or mislabel conditions (an imposed etic).
- **Gender and cultural bias in diagnosis.** Clinicians may diagnose differently depending on the patient's gender or ethnic background.

**A cultural example for eating disorders.** Becker and colleagues (2002) studied adolescent girls in Fiji before and after television arrived. Disordered eating attitudes and behaviours became more common after exposure to Western programmes. This suggests a media and cultural influence, but it is a natural experiment with no random allocation, so other changes in society may have contributed.

**Approaches to mental health.** Cultures differ in the explanations they give (spiritual, family, biomedical) and in who is expected to help (healers, family elders, doctors). Combining local and biomedical approaches can improve acceptance.

**Evaluation.** Cross-cultural comparison supports etic claims, but emic research is needed to understand meaning. Prevalence data are affected by sampling and measurement, so they cannot be treated as exact.

**Concepts:** bias, measurement, perspective (etic and emic), responsibility (avoiding stigma).

## 4. Environmental factors

The guide asks for **the role of environmental factors in explaining mental health disorders**. Environmental factors include early life experience, stressful events, relationships, poverty, discrimination, trauma and the physical environment.

- **Life events and vulnerability.** Brown and Harris (1978) interviewed women in an inner-London district. Severe life events and ongoing difficulties were strongly linked to depression, and vulnerability factors such as lacking a close confiding relationship made the effect more likely. This supports a stress-vulnerability model.
- **Childhood adversity.** Adverse childhood experiences are associated with a higher risk of later mental and physical health problems, as in the large survey by Felitti and colleagues (1998). The evidence is retrospective self-report, so memory bias is a limitation.
- **Trauma and PTSD.** PTSD requires exposure to a traumatic event, so its environmental cause is part of the definition, though not everyone exposed develops it, which shows the importance of individual vulnerability.
- **Social media, isolation and discrimination** are further factors discussed in current research, often with correlational designs where the direction of cause is unclear.

**Evaluation.** Environmental accounts are supported by large, real-world studies, but retrospective reporting and the inability to randomise exposure limit causal claims. They are best combined with biological and cognitive explanations in an interactionist account.

**Concepts:** causality (complexity and interaction), change (prevention targets the environment), responsibility.

## Bringing the areas together

A strong Section C response uses at least three of the four areas and shows them interacting. For instance: a genetic vulnerability (biological) is triggered by a stressful life event (environmental), which activates negative schemas (cognitive), and the way symptoms are expressed and recognised depends on culture. Use the diathesis-stress model as a bridge. Then say which factor has the best evidence for which group of people, and what remains uncertain.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a drug effect as proof of cause ("SSRIs work, so low serotonin causes depression").
- Quoting prevalence rates as if they were directly comparable across countries.
- Describing a cognitive model without saying what its "value" is. Value means usefulness for explanation, prediction and treatment, with its limits.
- Writing about treatment only, when the area asks for explanation.
- Using stigmatising language. Refer to "people with depression", not "depressives".
