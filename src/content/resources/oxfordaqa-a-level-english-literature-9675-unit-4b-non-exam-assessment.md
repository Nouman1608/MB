This guide covers one route through Unit 4 of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level English Literature (9675) specification, Version 5.3: **4b Non-exam assessment**. The other route, an examination on unseen texts, is covered in the [Unit 4a guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-english-literature-9675-unit-4a-examination/). Unit 4 is assessed at International A-level only, and a student takes one route or the other.

In 4b you choose what to read. You read widely, choose your own texts (with your teacher), and write two essays that argue for your own readings. Condense this guide with the [Unit 4b revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-english-literature-9675-unit-4b-non-exam-assessment-revision-notes/) and try the [practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-english-literature-9675-unit-4b-non-exam-assessment-practice/).

## What the specification says about 4b

| Feature | What the specification states |
|---|---|
| Format | Non-exam assessment; study of two texts, one poetry text and one prose text |
| Writing | Two essays of 1,250 to 1,500 words, each responding to a different text, written as separate responses |
| Marks | 50 marks; 30% of the International A-level |
| Marking | Assessed by teachers and externally moderated. Each essay is marked out of 25, giving a total out of 50 |
| Purpose | To allow students to read widely, to choose their own texts (if appropriate) and to understand that the study of literature needs to be informed by an understanding that literature is representational |
| Range | A range of differentiated texts and tasks will ideally be seen across a centre's coursework submission |
| Responsibility | The head of the school or college is responsible for making sure the NEA is conducted in line with OxfordAQA's instructions |

The specification also states that NEA marks can be carried forward when re-sitting a qualification.

The word counts give a total of 2,500 to 3,000 words of writing across the two essays (2 x 1,250 to 2 x 1,500).

## Rules for choosing texts

The specification sets out the following rules.

- **Poetry text:** it must be by a **single author**. The number of poems that make up the text should be **equivalent to those seen in Unit 2 and Unit 3**.
- **Short stories:** if short stories are used, they must be by a **single author**, and their number should be **equivalent to those seen in Unit 3**.
- **Prose text:** the student writes about one prose text. The specification allows short stories as a prose text, within the rule above.
- **Different areas:** each text must be linked to a **different topic** chosen from the four areas: childhood, war, women and men, and race, class and culture.
- **Not on set lists:** students **cannot choose texts from any of the AS or A-level exam set text lists**.
- **Translation:** texts in translation may be chosen.
- **Critical range:** each text must allow access to a **range of critical views and interpretations**, and must **maximise opportunities for writing with reference to ways in which meanings can be found in literature**.

The practical consequence is that the choice is a decision about the quality of the text as well as your interest in it. Before you commit, test the text with these questions (Marlbridge suggestions):

1. Is it by a single author, and is the amount of text comparable to the set-text units?
2. Does it fit one of the four areas clearly, and is the other text linked to a different area?
3. Is it absent from every set text list on the specification?
4. Can you find more than one way of reading it, so that you can write an argument?
5. Does it have enough rich detail, such as voice, form, structure and imagery, to support close analysis?

Your teacher will confirm that the choice is appropriate.

## Understanding representation

The four areas are not prescribed topics and you are not expected to bring extensive contextual knowledge of the topic or wider reading. They are areas you explore **through the analysis of your own selected texts**. The word to focus on is **representation**: the specification uses it to emphasise the process of showing a view of the world, rather than the actual world itself, and so opening texts to varieties of interpretation.

For each essay the central question is therefore: **how does this writer represent this aspect of this area, and what are the possible interpretations?**

## The task title

An essay needs a task that is specific enough to produce an argument. The specification gives an example linked to race, class and culture:

> 'Forster has written A Passage to India in such a way that it is impossible to sympathise with any of the English characters as there is so little to redeem them.' Exploring Forster's representation of the English in India, say how far you agree with this view.

Notice the features of this task, which are the features of a good title:

- it states a **view** that can be argued with or against;
- it names the **writer** and the **aspect** of representation;
- it ends with an instruction that invites a judgement ("say how far you agree").

A task template (original, for practice):

> '[Writer] presents [a group or idea] as [a claim].' Exploring [Writer]'s representation of [aspect], say how far you agree with this view.

A task for a poetry text on childhood might read: "'In this collection, childhood is a time of loss rather than growth.' Exploring the poet's representation of childhood, say how far you agree." This is an example of the form only. The poet and collection would be your own choice.

## The essay in practice

### Structure

For an essay of about 1,400 words, a possible plan is (a suggestion):

| Part | Words |
|---|---|
| Introduction: the view, your line of argument | 120 |
| Four body sections, each about 290 words | 1,160 |
| Conclusion: judgement | 120 |
| Total | 1,400 |

Each body section can follow this pattern: **claim**, **evidence** from the text, **method** and its effect, **context** where relevant to the text, and an **alternative reading**.

### Worked example: building a paragraph

Imagine the poem "Hand-me-downs" (the original poem from the [Unit 4a guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-english-literature-9675-unit-4a-examination/)) is one of the poems in a single-author collection. The task is the childhood example above.

**Claim.** The poem presents childhood as inheriting what others have used, which is loss as well as growth.

**Evidence and method.** "The coat was Ben's before it was mine" puts the speaker second. The simile "as you do / into a promise others made for you" treats growing up as living up to someone else's plans.

**Context.** A reader might link the second-hand coat to a family with limited means, but the poem itself does not name money. It only hints at it through "good wool" and the mother's practical words.

**Alternative reading.** The mother's line "It will do" may be care. The final discovery of the initials may be comforting or unsettling.

**Link to the task.** The poem supports "loss" because the child receives a past that is not hers. It also resists the view that childhood is only loss, because "I grew to fit it" shows slow growth.

**Written up:**

> The poem partly supports the view that childhood is a time of loss, since the speaker begins second in line: "The coat was Ben's before it was mine". The garment carries another child's smell and history, and the simile "into a promise others made for you" shows that even growth is defined by adults. However, the poem does not present only loss. The verb "grew" suggests a slow and real change, and the mother's practical care in pressing the collar flat could be read as love. The discovery of the initials in the last line breaks the pattern of the rhyme, leaving the reader to decide whether the speaker feels comforted by the link to Ben or displaced by it.

The paragraph argues, uses short quotations, names methods, offers two readings and returns to the task.

## How the work is marked

Each essay is marked out of 25 using the same bands, based on the three assessment objectives, which are equally weighted. AO1 concerns how readers find meanings (genre, contexts, interpretations). AO2 concerns authorial methods. AO3 concerns the argued response, concepts and terminology, and accurate writing.

| Band | Marks | Descriptor words | In brief |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 21 to 25 | Perceptive and assured | Sophisticated, confident argument; perceptive understanding of methods and contexts |
| 4 | 16 to 20 | Coherent and thorough | Logical, thorough argument; precise expression; careful explanations |
| 3 | 11 to 15 | Straightforward and relevant | Clear, relevant ideas in a sensible order; some use of concepts and terminology |
| 2 | 6 to 10 | Simple and generalised | Unelaborated ideas; generalised comments; simple expression |
| 1 | 1 to 5 | Largely irrelevant, largely misunderstood, largely inaccurate | Vague points; little sense of methods, contexts or interpretation |

The specification defines the terms. "Perception" is shown when students are showing depth of understanding and responding sensitively to the texts and task. "Assuredness" is shown when students write with confidence and conviction. "Coherence" is logical, consistent argument, and "thoroughness" is writing carefully and precisely with detail. "Straightforward" work makes its ideas clearly known, and "relevant" work is focused on the task and uses detail in an appropriate and supportive way. "Simple" work is unelaborated and basic, and "generalised" work is written without regard to particular details.

To move up a band, a useful target is to replace a general comment by one that uses a specific detail, then add a reason, then add an alternative reading.

## Common mistakes

- Choosing a text from a set text list for any AS or A-level paper.
- Choosing two texts linked to the same area.
- A poetry text by several poets, or short stories by different authors.
- A title that is a topic ("childhood in the poems") and not an argument.
- Retelling the text instead of analysing methods.
- A long account of the historical background that does not connect to the writing.
- Writing a single reading with no sense that other readings exist.
- Going beyond 1,500 words or well under 1,250.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I use critical books or articles?**
The specification requires only that the texts allow access to a range of critical views and interpretations. It does not set a reading list. Use critical views when they help you to argue, and say clearly which ideas are yours and which come from others.

**Can both texts come from the same period?**
The specification does not say that they cannot, but each must be linked to a different area from the four named.

**What if my text is in translation?**
Texts in translation may be chosen. Comment on the language as it appears in the translation you studied.
