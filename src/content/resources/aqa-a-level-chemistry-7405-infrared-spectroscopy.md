## How infrared absorption works

Bonds in a molecule are not rigid. They stretch and bend, each with its own natural frequency. If infrared radiation shines on a molecule and the radiation's frequency matches the natural frequency of a bond, the bond absorbs the energy and vibrates more strongly. Radiation of other frequencies passes through.

- Different bonds vibrate at different frequencies, so each absorbs at its own **characteristic wavenumber**.
- A bond that vibrates with a **change in dipole** absorbs. Bonds between different atoms (C=O, O–H) absorb strongly.
- **Wavenumber** is the number of waves per centimetre, in cm⁻¹. It is proportional to frequency: a larger wavenumber means higher energy.

The spectrum plots **percentage transmittance** against wavenumber. Wavenumber decreases from left to right, typically from 4000 to 500 cm⁻¹. Where a bond absorbs, the transmittance falls, so absorptions appear as **troughs pointing downwards**. The deeper the trough, the stronger the absorption.

## Converting wavelength to wavenumber

```
wavenumber (cm⁻¹) = 10 000 ÷ wavelength (µm)
```

For infrared radiation of wavelength 5.88 µm: 10 000 ÷ 5.88 ≈ 1700 cm⁻¹, which falls in the C=O region.

## Typical absorption ranges

Use the table in your Data Booklet in the exam. The values below are approximate and are for revision.

| Bond | Where found | Approximate wavenumber / cm⁻¹ | Appearance |
|---|---|---|---|
| O–H (alcohols) | Alcohols | 3230 to 3550 | Broad |
| N–H | Amines, amides | 3300 to 3500 | Medium |
| C–H | Most organic compounds | 2850 to 3100 | Sharp, strong |
| O–H (carboxylic acids) | Carboxylic acids | 2500 to 3000 | Very broad, overlaps C–H |
| C≡N | Nitriles | 2220 to 2260 | Sharp |
| C=O | Aldehydes, ketones, acids, esters, amides | 1680 to 1750 | Sharp, very strong |
| C=C | Alkenes | 1620 to 1680 | Weak to medium |
| C–O | Alcohols, esters, acids | 1000 to 1300 | Strong |

### Reading the spectrum: a strategy

1. Start at the left of the spectrum and check for a **broad O–H** absorption.
2. Look for a strong sharp absorption near **1700 cm⁻¹** (C=O).
3. Check the **C–H** region just below 3000 cm⁻¹. Almost every organic compound has it, so it rarely helps.
4. Check the **C–O** region at 1000 to 1300 cm⁻¹ if you suspect an alcohol, an acid or an ester.
5. Draw a conclusion about the group, then test it against the formula.

## Telling groups apart

| Features in the spectrum | Likely group |
|---|---|
| Broad trough at about 3350, no strong C=O | Alcohol |
| Strong C=O at about 1710, no O–H | Aldehyde or ketone |
| Very broad 2500 to 3000 plus C=O at about 1710 | Carboxylic acid |
| C=O at about 1740, C–O at about 1240, no O–H | Ester |
| Sharp peak at about 2240 | Nitrile |
| Medium trough at 3300 to 3500 and no C=O | Amine |
| Weak peak at about 1650 with C–H, no O–H or C=O | Alkene |

Two reminders for the commonest confusions:

- The **O–H of an alcohol** is a broad trough centred near 3300 to 3400. The **O–H of a carboxylic acid** is wider still and lies lower, 2500 to 3000, overlapping the C–H absorptions.
- A C=O absorption does not tell you whether the compound is an aldehyde, ketone, acid or ester by itself. Use the other peaks too.

## Fingerprinting

The region below about 1500 cm⁻¹ contains many overlapping absorptions from the bending and stretching of the whole molecule. It is different for every compound, even for closely related ones. This is the **fingerprint region**. To confirm the identity of a sample, its spectrum is compared with spectra in a database. An exact match across the whole spectrum, especially in the fingerprint region, identifies the compound.

## Identifying impurities

An impurity in a sample shows up as an **absorption that should not be there**.

- A purified ketone with a broad O–H absorption near 3300 cm⁻¹ still contains alcohol (the starting material) or water.
- A purified alcohol with a C=O peak at about 1710 cm⁻¹ has been partly oxidised.
- A dried ester with a very broad O–H absorption in the 2500 to 3000 region contains unreacted carboxylic acid.

Compare the spectrum with the one expected for the pure compound, and name the extra bond that absorbs.

## Infrared and global warming

The Earth absorbs radiation from the Sun and re-emits energy as infrared radiation. Some atmospheric gases have bonds that absorb infrared radiation:

- the C=O bonds in **carbon dioxide**,
- the C–H bonds in **methane**,
- the O–H bonds in **water vapour**.

These gases absorb some of the infrared radiation leaving the surface. The absorbed energy makes the bonds vibrate more, and it is passed on to the atmosphere as heat, or re-emitted in all directions, including back towards the surface. This slows the loss of energy to space. The result is a rise in the temperature of the lower atmosphere, called the **greenhouse effect**. As the concentrations of these gases increase, the effect strengthens, and this contributes to **global warming**.

Gases such as nitrogen and oxygen, made of molecules with two identical atoms, do not absorb infrared radiation in this way, because their vibration does not change the dipole, so they do not contribute.

## Worked example 1: carbonyl or alcohol?

**Question.** The infrared spectrum of a compound of formula C₃H₆O has a strong, sharp absorption at 1715 cm⁻¹ and no broad absorption above 3000 cm⁻¹. Which functional group is present?

The 1715 cm⁻¹ peak is in the C=O range. There is no O–H, so the compound is not an alcohol or acid. The compound is a carbonyl compound: propanal or propanone. Infrared alone does not tell you which, so use Tollens' reagent (a silver mirror for propanal only).

## Worked example 2: acid or ester?

**Question.** A compound of formula C₃H₆O₂ gives an infrared spectrum with a very broad absorption between 2500 and 3000 cm⁻¹ and a strong peak at 1710 cm⁻¹. Identify the functional group.

The broad 2500 to 3000 cm⁻¹ absorption is the O–H of a carboxylic acid and the 1710 peak is C=O. Together they show a carboxylic acid: **propanoic acid**, CH₃CH₂COOH. An ester of the same formula, such as methyl ethanoate, would show C=O near 1740 and C–O, but no O–H.

## Worked example 3: product of an oxidation

Ethanol is oxidised by acidified dichromate. A spectrum of the distilled product shows a strong peak at 1730 cm⁻¹ and no broad peak above 3000 cm⁻¹. What was formed?

C=O is present and O–H is absent, so the product is an aldehyde, **ethanal**, not ethanoic acid (which would show the very broad O–H absorption).

## Worked example 4: a pair of data sets

A compound has a molecular ion at m/z 74.0366 in the mass spectrum (formula C₃H₆O₂) and a strong peak at 1745 cm⁻¹ and one at 1240 cm⁻¹ but no peaks above 3000 cm⁻¹ in the infrared spectrum. It is an ester: the C=O at 1745 and C–O at 1240 are in range, and there is no O–H. Possible esters of C₃H₆O₂ are methyl ethanoate and ethyl methanoate.

## Common mistakes

- Giving the wavenumber range from memory instead of from the table, with errors.
- Calling an absorption a "peak" that points down and confusing it with a high value. Absorption is a trough in transmittance.
- Saying that the carboxylic acid O–H appears at the same place as an alcohol O–H.
- Assuming a C=O absorption proves a ketone.
- Using the C–H absorption to identify a group.
- Listing the fingerprint region as the place to find functional groups. It is used for comparison, not group identification.
- Stating that infrared radiation breaks the bonds. It makes them vibrate more.
- Saying nitrogen and oxygen are greenhouse gases because they are in the atmosphere.

## Exam technique

- **Identify a bond**: quote the wavenumber of the peak, name the bond and name the group.
- **Identify an impurity**: state what absorption is extra, which bond it shows, and what compound it suggests.
- **Explain fingerprinting**: match the whole spectrum to a database, especially the fingerprint region.
- **Greenhouse effect**: name the bond, the radiation and what the bond does.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is the O–H absorption broad?**
Hydrogen bonding means that the O–H bonds in a sample experience many different environments, so they absorb over a range of wavenumbers.

**Can infrared tell me the molecular formula?**
No. It gives functional groups. Combine it with mass spectrometry for the formula.

**Which gases in the atmosphere are greenhouse gases?**
Those whose molecules have bonds that absorb infrared radiation, such as carbon dioxide, methane and water vapour.
