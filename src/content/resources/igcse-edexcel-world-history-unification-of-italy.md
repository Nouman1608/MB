"Development of a nation: unification of Italy, 1848–70" is Depth Study 2 on Paper 1 of Pearson
Edexcel International GCSE History (4HI1). It explains how a peninsula of separate states, many
dominated by Austria, became the Kingdom of Italy (proclaimed in 1861) and then gained Venetia
(1866) and Rome (1870). Exam questions ask you to explain effects, interpret an extract and judge
the relative importance of people such as Cavour, Garibaldi, Victor Emmanuel II and Napoleon III.

## Syllabus coverage

The 4HI1 specification (Issue 5, February 2026) sets five key topics:

1. **The 1848 Revolutions in the Italian states** — causes, constitutions in Tuscany and Piedmont,
   Mazzini's democratic revolt against the Pope in Rome, reasons for failure, and the legacy:
   Victor Emmanuel and the Statuto, Austrian dominance, the papacy and the French occupation of
   Rome.
2. **Developments in Piedmont, 1849–54** — Victor Emmanuel II, the appointment of Cavour (1852),
   his financial and domestic reforms, economic expansion and his policies to reduce the
   Church's influence.
3. **The defeat of Austria, 1854–59** — the Crimean War, the Orsini Affair, the Pact of
   Plombières, Magenta and Solferino, Villafranca, Cavour's resignation, the annexation of the
   central states and the loss of Nice and Savoy.
4. **Garibaldi and the Papal States** — Garibaldi's motives, his relationship with Cavour and
   Victor Emmanuel, his conquest of Sicily and Naples, the great powers' reaction and the
   proclamation of the Kingdom of Italy.
5. **Venice and Rome and the extent of unification by 1870** — "Piedmontisation", the
   Austro-Prussian War and Venetia, the problem of Rome, Garibaldi's failures (1862–67), the
   Franco-Prussian War and the acquisition of Rome, and factors for and against unity.

## Why did the 1848 revolutions in Italy happen and fail?

In 1848 Italy was divided into states including the Kingdom of Piedmont-Sardinia, Lombardy and
Venetia (ruled directly by Austria), the duchies of Tuscany, Parma and Modena, the Papal States
and the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies (Naples and Sicily). Causes of revolution included economic
hardship, liberal demands for constitutions, nationalist hostility to Austria and the example of
revolution in France in February 1848.

Rulers in Naples, Tuscany and Piedmont granted constitutions; Charles Albert of Piedmont issued
the **Statuto** in March 1848. Milan rose against the Austrians in the Five Days (March 1848) and
Venice declared a republic under Daniele Manin. Charles Albert declared war on Austria but was
defeated at **Custoza** (July 1848) and, after renewing the war, at **Novara** (March 1849). He
abdicated in favour of his son, **Victor Emmanuel II**.

In Rome, Pope Pius IX refused to support a war against Catholic Austria (April 1848) and fled the
city after his minister Rossi was assassinated. A **Roman Republic** was proclaimed in February
1849, with Mazzini as one of its leaders and Garibaldi commanding its defence. French troops sent
by Louis Napoleon captured Rome in mid-1849 and restored the Pope; a French garrison stayed.

**Why did 1848 fail?** Austria's army under Radetzky was far stronger; the revolutionaries were
divided between monarchists, moderates and republicans; the Pope withdrew; most peasants took no
part; and no foreign power helped. **Legacy:** Piedmont alone kept its constitution, Victor
Emmanuel gained a reputation as the "honest king", Austria's dominance was re-established and the
papacy became firmly hostile to nationalism.

## What did Cavour change in Piedmont?

Count Camillo di **Cavour** became Prime Minister in **November 1852**, after an alliance with the
centre-left under Rattazzi (the *connubio*). He modernised Piedmont's finances, signed free-trade
treaties, encouraged banks and built railways, so that Piedmont became the most economically
advanced Italian state. He continued the anti-clerical policy begun with the Siccardi Laws (1850),
which removed Church courts, and in 1855 a law closed many religious houses. These measures made
Piedmont a model liberal state that attracted exiles and nationalists from the rest of Italy.

## How was Austria defeated in 1859?

Piedmont joined Britain and France in the **Crimean War** (1855), which gave Cavour a seat at the
Congress of Paris (1856) to raise the Italian question. The **Orsini Affair** (January 1858), an
Italian nationalist's bomb attack on Napoleon III, persuaded the emperor to act on Italy. At
**Plombières** (July 1858) Napoleon III and Cavour secretly agreed that France would help Piedmont
in a war against Austria provided Austria could be made to appear the aggressor; Piedmont would
gain northern Italy and France would receive Nice and Savoy.

Austria declared war in April 1859. French and Piedmontese forces won at **Magenta** and
**Solferino** (June 1859), but Napoleon III, alarmed by casualties and the threat of Prussian
intervention, made the **armistice of Villafranca** (July 1859) without consulting Cavour.
Piedmont gained Lombardy but not Venetia, and Cavour **resigned** in protest. He returned in
January 1860; the central states (Tuscany, Parma, Modena and the Romagna) voted in plebiscites to
join Piedmont, and in return Piedmont ceded **Nice and Savoy** to France.

## What was Garibaldi's role in 1860?

Giuseppe **Garibaldi**, a republican and a native of Nice, was angered by its loss. In May 1860 he
led the **Expedition of the Thousand** to Sicily, where a revolt had begun. He won at Calatafimi,
took Palermo, crossed to the mainland and entered **Naples** in September 1860. Cavour feared he
would attack Rome and provoke France, so the Piedmontese army marched through the Papal States
(defeating papal forces at Castelfidardo) to reach the south. At **Teano** (October 1860)
Garibaldi handed his conquests to Victor Emmanuel. Plebiscites confirmed union, and the
**Kingdom of Italy** was proclaimed in **March 1861**. Cavour died in June 1861.

## How were Venice and Rome added by 1870?

The new state faced deep problems. "Piedmontisation" imposed Piedmont's laws, taxes and
conscription on the rest of Italy, and the south saw years of armed resistance known as
brigandage. Garibaldi's attempts to seize Rome failed at **Aspromonte** (1862), where he was
wounded by Italian troops, and at **Mentana** (1867), where French and papal forces defeated him.

**Venetia** came through alliance with Prussia: although Italy was defeated at the second battle
of **Custoza** in 1866, Prussia's victory over Austria meant Venetia was handed (via France) to
Italy and confirmed by plebiscite. **Rome** came when the Franco-Prussian War of 1870 forced
France to withdraw its garrison; Italian troops entered Rome in September 1870, and it became the
capital. The Pope refused to accept the loss.

## How united was Italy in 1870?

Factors promoting unity included a single monarchy and constitution, a national army and
parliament, and growing railways. Factors working against it included the North–South divide,
regional loyalties, low literacy, the fact that most Italians spoke regional dialects, and the
Pope's hostility, which discouraged Catholics from taking part in national politics. The phrase
"We have made Italy; now we must make Italians", often attributed to Massimo d'Azeglio, sums up
the problem.

## Frequently asked questions

**Who was most important to unification?** That is the classic 16-mark debate. Cavour's diplomacy
won French help; Garibaldi delivered the south; Napoleon III's armies defeated Austria; Prussia's
wars delivered Venetia and Rome. A strong answer weighs them rather than listing them.

**Did Cavour want a united Italy?** Historians debate this. His actions up to 1859 aimed mainly
at expanding Piedmont in the north; he moved to take the south only once Garibaldi had conquered it.

**Is Mazzini on the syllabus?** Yes, through the 1848 revolutions and the Roman Republic.
