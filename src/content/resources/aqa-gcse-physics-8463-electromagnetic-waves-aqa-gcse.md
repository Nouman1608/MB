## 4.6.2.1 The spectrum

Electromagnetic waves are **transverse** and **transfer energy from the source of the waves to an
absorber**. They form a **continuous spectrum**, and all of them travel at the **same velocity through a
vacuum or air**. Questions give this speed, usually 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s.

From **long wavelength / low frequency** to **short wavelength / high frequency**:

```
radio → microwave → infrared → visible (red … violet) → ultraviolet → X-rays → gamma rays
```

Because the speed is the same for all of them, v = f λ means a shorter wavelength always goes with a higher
frequency. Our eyes detect only visible light, a very small part of the spectrum.

Examples of energy transfer: infrared and visible light from the Sun warming the ground; microwaves heating
food in an oven; radio waves carrying energy from a transmitter to an aerial.

**Worked example 1.** A radio station broadcasts at 100 MHz. Find the wavelength.

```
f = 100 MHz = 100 × 10⁶ Hz = 1.00 × 10⁸ Hz
λ = v / f = 3.0 × 10⁸ / 1.00 × 10⁸ = 3.0 m
```

## 4.6.2.2 Refraction and how materials respond

**Refraction ray diagrams (both tiers).** Draw the normal where the ray meets the boundary. Entering a
denser medium such as glass from air, the ray bends **towards** the normal; leaving it, the ray bends **away
from** the normal. A ray along the normal passes straight through.

**Higher tier only.** Different substances may absorb, transmit, refract or reflect EM waves in ways that
vary with wavelength. For example, soft tissue transmits most X-rays while bone absorbs them; water in food
absorbs microwaves strongly.

**Higher tier only: wave fronts.** Refraction is caused by the change in the wave's speed. Draw wave fronts
(lines joining the crests) approaching the boundary at an angle. The end of each wave front that enters the
glass first slows down first, while the rest is still moving faster in air. The wave front swings round and
the direction of travel changes. In the glass the wave fronts are closer together, because the wave is
slower and its frequency has not changed, so its wavelength is shorter.

**Required practical 10.** Investigate how the amount of infrared radiation absorbed or radiated by a
surface depends on the nature of the surface. With a Leslie cube of hot water, keep the detector at the same
distance from each face, take readings at the same time after filling, and repeat. Matt black surfaces are
the best emitters (and absorbers); shiny silver surfaces are the poorest.

## 4.6.2.3 Origins, circuits and hazards

**Higher tier only.** Radio waves can be produced by **oscillations in electrical circuits**. When radio
waves are absorbed, for example by an aerial, they may create an **alternating current with the same
frequency** as the radio wave, so radio waves can themselves induce oscillations in a circuit.

**Origins.** Changes in atoms and in the nuclei of atoms can generate or absorb EM waves over a wide
frequency range. **Gamma rays originate from changes in the nucleus.**

**Hazards.** Ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays can harm body tissue. The effect depends on the **type of
radiation** and the **size of the dose**.

- **Ultraviolet** can make skin age prematurely and increases the risk of skin cancer.
- **X-rays and gamma rays** are **ionising**: they can cause the mutation of genes and cancer.

**Radiation dose** (in sieverts) is a measure of the risk of harm from an exposure of the body to the
radiation; 1000 mSv = 1 Sv. You will not need to recall the unit, but you may need to draw conclusions from
given dose data: compare doses in the same unit, say how many times larger one is, and weigh the risk against
the benefit.

**Worked example 2 (invented data).** A CT scan gives 4.2 mSv; a dental X-ray gives 0.0050 mSv; yearly
background is 2.1 mSv.

```
CT ÷ dental = 4.2 / 0.0050 = 840 times
CT ÷ yearly background = 4.2 / 2.1 = 2 years' worth
```

Conclusion: the dental X-ray adds a tiny risk; the CT scan adds about two years of background dose, so it
should be used only when the medical benefit justifies it.

## 4.6.2.4 Uses, and why each wave suits its use

| Group | Applications | Why suitable (Higher tier only) |
|---|---|---|
| Radio | Television and radio | Can be produced and detected by electrical circuits; travel long distances through air |
| Microwaves | Satellite communications; cooking food | Pass through the atmosphere to and from satellites; absorbed by water molecules in food, heating it |
| Infrared | Electrical heaters; cooking food; infrared cameras | Absorbed by surfaces, transferring energy to their thermal stores; warm objects emit it, so cameras can form images from it |
| Visible light | Fibre optic communications | Travels along thin glass fibres, carrying signals over long distances |
| Ultraviolet | Energy efficient lamps; sun tanning | Absorbed by coatings that then emit visible light; causes the skin to tan |
| X-rays and gamma rays | Medical imaging and treatments | Penetrate soft tissue (X-rays absorbed by bone for images); high-energy gamma kills cancer cells |

For a "why suitable" answer, name a **property** of the wave and link it to what the application needs.

## 4.6.2.5 Lenses (physics only)

A lens forms an image by **refracting** light.

- A **convex** lens brings parallel rays to a focus at the **principal focus**. The distance from the lens to
  the principal focus is the **focal length**.
- A **concave** lens spreads parallel rays out so they appear to come from a principal focus on the same
  side as the light came from.
- A convex lens can form a **real** image (light actually meets there; it can be shown on a screen) or a
  **virtual** image (light only appears to come from it). A concave lens always forms a **virtual** image.

**Constructing a ray diagram (convex lens).** From the top of the object, draw:

1. a ray parallel to the principal axis, which refracts through the principal focus on the far side;
2. a ray through the centre of the lens, which passes straight through.

Where the two rays cross is the top of the image. If they do not cross on the far side, extend them back
with dashed lines: the image is virtual. A concave lens diagram uses the same centre ray, with the
parallel ray diverging as if from the principal focus on the object's side.

```
magnification = image height / object height     (equation sheet; no units)
```

Measure both heights in the same unit, mm or cm. An object 1.5 cm tall with an image 42 mm tall: convert to
4.2 cm, then magnification = 4.2 / 1.5 = **2.8**.

## 4.6.2.6 Visible light (physics only)

- Each colour has its own narrow band of wavelength and frequency; red has the longest wavelength, violet the
  shortest.
- **Specular reflection**: from a smooth surface, in a single direction (a clear image in a mirror).
  **Diffuse reflection**: from a rough surface, scattered in many directions (no image from paper).
- **Filters** absorb some wavelengths and transmit others: a blue filter transmits blue and absorbs the rest.
- An **opaque** object's colour is the wavelengths it reflects most strongly; the others are absorbed. It
  looks white if it reflects all wavelengths equally, black if it absorbs them all.
- Objects that transmit light are **transparent** (clear image through them) or **translucent** (light
  passes, but scattered).

**Worked example 3.** A white flower with a red centre is lit only by light that has passed through a red
filter. The white petals reflect the red light, so they look red; the red centre also looks red. Through a
green filter instead, the petals look green and the red centre looks black, because it reflects no green
light.

## Exam technique

- **Give** or **name**: one group or one use; no explanation needed.
- **Explain why suitable** (Higher tier only): property of the wave, then the need it meets.
- **Construct** a ray diagram: ruler, arrows on rays, normal or principal axis labelled.
- **Draw conclusions** from dose data: compare in one unit, quote the ratio, then judge risk against benefit.

## Common mistakes

- Reversing the order of the spectrum, or saying gamma rays travel faster than radio waves.
- Saying refraction happens because light "bends"; the cause is the **change in speed**.
- Using a frequency in MHz or GHz without converting it to Hz before λ = v / f.
- Giving uses without a matching property in a "why suitable" answer.
- Mixing up real and virtual images, or saying a concave lens can form a real image.
- Giving magnification a unit, or using mm for one height and cm for the other.
