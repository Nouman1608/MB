These notes condense section **3.1.8.1 Born–Haber cycles** of the AQA A-level Chemistry specification (7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is A-level only content, examined on Paper 1 (and Paper 3). The [Born–Haber cycles study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-born-haber-cycles/) explains each cycle with worked examples, and the [practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-born-haber-cycles-practice/) give exam-style practice.

## What the specification asks

- **Lattice enthalpy** defined as enthalpy of lattice **dissociation** or **formation**.
- **Born–Haber cycles** use enthalpy of formation, ionisation energy, enthalpy of atomisation, bond enthalpy and electron affinity.
- **Define** each term, **construct** cycles, and calculate lattice enthalpies or another missing value.
- **Compare** Born–Haber lattice enthalpies with those from the perfect ionic model, as evidence of covalent character.
- Use cycles with lattice enthalpy and **enthalpy of hydration** to calculate **enthalpy of solution**.

## Definitions

| Term | Meaning (per mole) | Sign |
|---|---|---|
| ΔfH⦵ | Compound formed from its elements in standard states | Usually negative |
| First IE | Gaseous atoms to gaseous 1+ ions | Positive |
| Second IE | Gaseous 1+ ions to gaseous 2+ ions | Positive |
| ΔatH⦵ | Gaseous atoms formed from the element in its standard state | Positive |
| Bond enthalpy | Covalent bond broken in the gaseous state | Positive |
| First EA | Gaseous atoms gain electrons to form 1− ions | Usually negative |
| Second EA | Gaseous 1− ions gain electrons to form 2− ions | Positive |
| Lattice formation | Gaseous ions to one mole of solid | Negative |
| Lattice dissociation | One mole of solid to gaseous ions | Positive |
| ΔhydH | Gaseous ions to aqueous ions | Negative |

## Building the cycle

1. Elements in standard states.
2. Atomise the metal and the non-metal (half the bond enthalpy per atom for X₂).
3. Ionise the metal (all ionisation energies needed).
4. Add electrons to non-metal atoms (all electron affinities needed).
5. Lattice formation to the solid.
6. Direct arrow for ΔfH.

**ΔfH = ΔatH(metal) + ΣIE + ΔatH(non-metal) + ΣEA + ΔH(lattice formation)**

Multiply for stoichiometry: MgCl₂ needs 2 Cl atoms and 2 EA₁.

## Worked values (kJ mol⁻¹)

**NaCl.** ΔfH −411; ΔatH(Na) +107; IE₁ +496; ½ Cl–Cl +122; EA₁(Cl) −349.
Sum of steps = 376. Lattice formation = −411 − 376 = **−787**.

**MgCl₂.** ΔfH −641; ΔatH(Mg) +148; IE₁ + IE₂ = +2189; 2 × ½ Cl–Cl = +244; 2 × EA₁ = −698.
Sum of steps = 1883. Lattice formation = −641 − 1883 = **−2524**.

**MgO.** ΔfH −602; ΔatH(Mg) +148; IE₁ + IE₂ = +2189; ΔatH(O) +249; EA₁(O) −141; EA₂(O) +798.
Sum of steps = 3243. Lattice formation = −602 − 3243 = **−3845**.

**Finding EA₁(Cl).** −411 = 107 + 496 + 122 + EA + (−787), so EA = **−349**.

## Ionic model comparison

| Compound | Born–Haber | Perfect ionic model | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|
| NaCl | −787 | −770 | Close; mostly ionic |
| AgCl | −905 | −864 | More exothermic; some covalent character |
| AgI | −889 | −778 | Much more exothermic; more covalent character |

(Illustrative values.)

- The ionic model assumes perfectly spherical ions and purely electrostatic bonding.
- If the Born–Haber value is **more exothermic**, there is an extra covalent contribution.
- Covalent character is larger when the cation is small and highly charged (high charge density) and the anion is large and easily polarised.
- The cation **polarises** the anion's electron cloud, pulling electron density towards the cation.

## Enthalpy of solution

**ΔsolH = ΔH(lattice dissociation) + ΣΔhydH(ions)**

NaCl: +787 + (−406) + (−364) = **+17 kJ mol⁻¹**. A small difference between large numbers.

## Why trends in lattice enthalpy occur

| Factor | Effect on lattice enthalpy |
|---|---|
| Higher ionic charge | More exothermic (MgO ≫ NaCl) |
| Smaller ionic radius | More exothermic (LiF > KF) |
| Greater covalent character | Experimental value more exothermic than ionic model |

## Common mistakes

- Using formation when dissociation is needed (sign error).
- Full bond enthalpy for one atom of chlorine.
- Forgetting IE₂ for Group 2.
- Forgetting EA₂ for oxygen or sulfur.
- Not multiplying for two chlorine atoms in MgCl₂.
- Arrows in the wrong direction.
- Writing that EA₂ is exothermic.
- Not mentioning polarisation when explaining covalent character.

## Self-test

1. Define the enthalpy of lattice formation.
2. Define the first electron affinity.
3. Define the enthalpy of atomisation.
4. Why is the second electron affinity of oxygen positive?
5. For KCl: ΔfH −437; ΔatH(K) +89; IE₁ +419; Cl–Cl +244; EA₁(Cl) −349. Calculate the enthalpy of lattice formation.
6. For CaCl₂: ΔfH −796; ΔatH(Ca) +178; IE₁ +590; IE₂ +1145; Cl–Cl +244; EA₁(Cl) −349. Calculate the enthalpy of lattice formation.
7. For LiF: ΔfH −617; ΔatH(Li) +159; F–F +158; EA₁(F) −328; lattice formation −1049. Calculate the first ionisation energy of lithium.
8. Why is the lattice enthalpy of MgO more exothermic than that of NaCl?
9. A Born–Haber lattice enthalpy is more exothermic than the ionic model value. What does this indicate?
10. Which would show more covalent character, silver chloride or silver iodide? Explain.
11. Enthalpy of lattice dissociation of MgCl₂ = +2524; hydration of Mg²⁺ = −1920; hydration of Cl⁻ = −364. Calculate ΔsolH.
12. State the relationship between lattice formation and lattice dissociation.

### Answers

1. The enthalpy change when one mole of an ionic solid is formed from its gaseous ions.
2. The enthalpy change when one mole of gaseous atoms gains one mole of electrons to form one mole of gaseous 1− ions.
3. The enthalpy change when one mole of gaseous atoms is formed from the element in its standard state.
4. The electron is added to a negative ion (O⁻), so it is repelled and energy must be supplied.
5. Steps = 89 + 419 + 122 − 349 = 281; −437 − 281 = **−718 kJ mol⁻¹**.
6. Steps = 178 + 590 + 1145 + 244 − 698 = 1459; −796 − 1459 = **−2255 kJ mol⁻¹**.
7. Known steps = 159 + 79 − 328 − 1049 = −1139; IE = −617 − (−1139) = **+522 kJ mol⁻¹**.
8. Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ have larger charges and smaller radii than Na⁺ and Cl⁻, so the electrostatic attraction is stronger.
9. The bonding has some covalent character, as well as ionic attraction.
10. Silver iodide: the iodide ion is larger and more easily polarised by the silver ion.
11. 2524 − 1920 − 2(364) = **−124 kJ mol⁻¹**.
12. They have the same magnitude and opposite signs.
