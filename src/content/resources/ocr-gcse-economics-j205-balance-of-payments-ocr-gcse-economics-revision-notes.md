Recall notes for section 4.2 of OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics (J205, Version 2.0, June 2026), examined in Component 02. The [balance of payments study guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-balance-of-payments-ocr-gcse-economics/) explains every method with worked examples; the [balance of payments practice questions](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-balance-of-payments-ocr-gcse-economics-practice/) test them. Figures are invented.

## What the specification asks

- Explain the **balance of payments on current account**.
- Explain a **balanced current account**, a **surplus** and a **deficit**.
- **Calculate** deficits and surpluses.
- **Analyse** recent and historical data on exports and imports.
- **Evaluate** the importance of the current account to the **UK economy**.
- **Evaluate** the **causes** of surpluses and deficits.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Balance of payments | Record of all transactions between a country and the rest of the world |
| Current account | The part recording trade in goods and services and income flows |
| Credit | Money flowing into the country |
| Debit | Money flowing out of the country |
| Trade balance | Exports minus imports of goods and services |
| Primary income | Wages, interest, profits and dividends earned across borders |
| Secondary income | Transfers with nothing in return, such as aid and money sent to family |
| Current account surplus | Credits exceed debits |
| Current account deficit | Debits exceed credits |
| Competitiveness | Ability to sell goods abroad on price and quality |

## Four parts: sort the transaction

| Part | Credit | Debit |
|---|---|---|
| Goods | Exports of goods | Imports of goods |
| Services | Foreign tourists in the UK; foreign firms using UK insurers | UK tourists abroad; UK firms using foreign shipping |
| Primary income | Profits and interest earned abroad by UK residents | Profits and interest paid to foreign owners |
| Secondary income | Transfers received | Overseas aid; money sent abroad to family |

**Two questions:** money in or out? Payment for a good, a service, income from assets or work, or a transfer?

## Methods

| Task | Method |
|---|---|
| Balance of one part | Credits − debits |
| Current account | Sum of the four balances (or total credits − total debits) |
| As a share of GDP | Balance ÷ GDP × 100 |
| Growth in exports | (new − old) ÷ old × 100 |
| Exports as % of imports | Exports ÷ imports × 100 |
| Missing value | Rearrange: balance = credits − debits |

## Worked reminders

**Full account.** Goods −£45bn, services +£38bn, primary income −£6bn, secondary income −£12bn: current account = **−£25bn**.

**Share of GDP.** A £36bn deficit with GDP of £1,800bn = **2.0%** of GDP.

**Data.** Exports rise from £400bn to £460bn (**+15%**) while imports rise from £420bn to £450bn (**+7.1%**): the trade balance moves from −£20bn to **+£10bn**.

## Causes

| Leads to deficit | Leads to surplus |
|---|---|
| Strong exchange rate | Weak exchange rate |
| Inflation above trading partners' | Inflation below trading partners' |
| Fast growth in domestic incomes | Slow domestic growth |
| Slow growth abroad | Fast growth abroad |
| Low productivity or quality | High productivity and quality |
| Few natural resources | Plentiful resources to export |

Judge whether a cause is **temporary** (boom, short-lived strong currency) or **structural** (weak competitiveness).

## The UK

- Usually a **deficit in goods** and a **surplus in services** (finance, insurance, education, business services).
- A current account deficit in most years for several decades.

## Does a deficit matter?

| Matters more when | Matters less when |
|---|---|
| Large (several % of GDP) | Small (1-2% of GDP) |
| Long-lasting | Short-lived |
| Caused by weak competitiveness | Caused by importing machinery for investment |
| Financed by short-term borrowing | Financed by long-term foreign investment |

## Exam traps

- A **current account** deficit is not a **budget** deficit.
- Tourism is a **service**.
- Primary income is earnings; secondary income is **transfers**.
- Keep minus signs throughout.
- A deficit is not automatically bad: say why it matters in the context.

## Self-test

1. What does the current account record?
2. Define a current account deficit.
3. An American tourist spends money in London. Which part, and credit or debit?
4. A UK company pays dividends to shareholders abroad. Which part, and credit or debit?
5. Exports of goods are £250bn and imports of goods £310bn. What is the balance on goods?
6. Goods −£45bn, services +£38bn, primary income −£6bn, secondary income −£12bn. Calculate the current account balance.
7. A deficit of £36bn with GDP of £1,800bn is what percentage of GDP?
8. Exports rise from £400bn to £460bn. Calculate the percentage increase.
9. State one cause of a current account deficit.
10. Why may a deficit caused by importing machinery be less worrying?
11. Describe the UK's usual pattern in goods and services trade.
12. State the difference between a current account deficit and a budget deficit.

### Answers

1. **Trade in goods and services** and **income flows** (primary and secondary) between a country and the rest of the world.
2. When **debits exceed credits**: more money flows out for imports and income than flows in.
3. **Services, credit.**
4. **Primary income, debit.**
5. 250 − 310 = **−£60bn**.
6. −45 + 38 − 6 − 12 = **−£25bn**.
7. 36 ÷ 1,800 × 100 = **2.0%**.
8. 60 ÷ 400 × 100 = **15%**.
9. Any one: **strong exchange rate**; **high inflation** relative to others; **fast domestic growth**; slow growth abroad; low productivity.
10. The machines **raise future output and exports**, so the deficit funds investment rather than consumption.
11. A **deficit in goods** and a **surplus in services**.
12. A current account deficit is about **trade and income flows with other countries**; a budget deficit is **government spending above tax revenue**.
