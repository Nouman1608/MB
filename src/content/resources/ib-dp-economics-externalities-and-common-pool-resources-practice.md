## Questions

**1.** Define a negative externality of production. **[2]**

**2.** State two characteristics of a common pool resource and name the problem that results from them. **[3]**

**3.** Make clear how a merit good differs from a demerit good, with a real-world illustration of each. **[4]**

**4.** (calculator-free) In the fictional country Veltria the market for tanned leather has demand (MPB) P = 60 − 2Q and supply (MPC) P = 12 + 2Q, where Q is in thousand sheets and P in crowns. Tanning pollutes a river, a marginal external cost of 8 crowns per sheet.

**(a)** Find the market equilibrium output and price. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the socially optimum output. **[2]**
**(c)** Find the indirect tax per sheet that achieves the optimum, and the revenue it raises. **[2]**
**(d)** (HL only) Calculate the welfare loss at the market output. **[2]**

**5.** On the shared pasture of the fictional village of Dunmere, the total milk (in litres a day) from different herd sizes is: 1 cow 30, 2 cows 56, 3 cows 78, 4 cows 96, 5 cows 110, 6 cows 120, 7 cows 126, 8 cows 128. Each cow costs 16 litres a day to keep, and herders can add cows freely.

**(a)** Find the herd size under open access. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the herd size that maximises the village's net gain, and that gain. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why individual herders push the herd beyond the best size. **[3]**

**6.** (calculator-free) Two fictional firms, P and R, each emit 50 units of a pollutant. The cap is 70 units in total. Cutting one unit costs P 4 crowns and R 10 crowns.

**(a)** Find the total cost of meeting the cap if each firm cuts the same amount. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the total cost if the firms trade permits so that the cap is met at least cost. **[2]**
**(c)** State the range in which the permit price per unit would settle. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain one limitation of a tradable permit scheme. **[2]**

**7.** Explain, using a diagram, why a market produces too little of a good with a positive externality of production, and how a subsidy could correct this. **[6]**

**8.** (calculator allowed) In the fictional steel industry of Orland, demand (MPB) is P = 80 − Q and supply (MPC) is P = 20 + Q, with Q in million tonnes and P in crowns a tonne. Carbon emissions impose a marginal external cost of 10 crowns a tonne.

**(a)** Find the market output and price. **[2]**
**(b)** The government introduces a carbon tax of 10 crowns a tonne. Find the new output and the price paid by buyers. **[3]**
**(c)** Find the price producers keep after tax and the tax revenue. **[2]**
**(d)** (HL only) Calculate the welfare loss before the tax. **[2]**
**(e)** Explain one effect of the carbon tax on a stakeholder other than buyers and the government. **[3]**

**9.** Evaluate the use of legislation and regulation compared with tradable permits to reduce industrial pollution. **[10]**

**10.** Explain why international cooperation is needed to deal with a global pollutant, and why it is hard to achieve. **[6]**

**11.** Explain two challenges in measuring externalities and how they affect policy. **[4]**

**12.** (calculator-free) In the fictional country Norland the market for booster vaccination appointments has demand (MPB) P = 50 − Q and supply (MPC) P = 14 + Q, with Q in thousand appointments and P in marks. Each appointment gives other people a marginal external benefit of 6 marks.

**(a)** Find the market equilibrium output and price. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the socially optimum output. **[2]**
**(c)** Find the subsidy per appointment that achieves it, and its total cost. **[2]**
**(d)** (HL only) Calculate the welfare loss at the market output. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** A cost [1] imposed on a third party by the production of a good, which is not paid for by the producer or reflected in the market price [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition needs both a third party and no compensation; "pollution" alone is an example.

**2.** The resource is rivalrous (one person's use reduces what is left for others) [1] and non-excludable (people cannot easily be kept out) [1]. The result is overuse, called the tragedy of the commons [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "State" asks for names, not explanation; "shared" alone does not name a characteristic.

**3.** A merit good is one people consume too little of, because they undervalue its benefits or because it creates external benefits [1]; education [1]. A demerit good is one people consume too much of, because they underestimate the harm or because it harms others [1]; tobacco [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Distinguish" needs the difference made clear; an example helps only if it fits your definition.

**4. (a)** 60 − 2Q = 12 + 2Q, so 4Q = 48, Q = 12 [1]; P = 60 − 24 = 36 crowns [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** MSC = 12 + 2Q + 8 = 20 + 2Q [1]; 60 − 2Q = 20 + 2Q gives Q = 10 [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Tax = marginal external cost = 8 crowns [1]; revenue = 8 × 10 = 80 thousand crowns [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** At Q = 12, MSC = 20 + 24 = 44 and MSB = 36, gap 8 [1]. Area = ½ × (12 − 10) × 8 = 8 thousand crowns [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Set demand equal to the social curve for the optimum; keep units.

**5. (a)** Average output per cow is 128 ÷ 8 = 16 at 8 cows, equal to the cost of 16 [1]; so herders keep adding cows until 8 and net gain is zero **8 cows** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Extra milk from each cow: 30, 26, 22, 18, 14, ... [1]. The fifth cow adds 14, less than the cost of 16, so stop at **4 cows** [1]. Net gain = 96 − 4 × 16 = **32 litres** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** A herder compares the average share of milk with the cost of a cow [1]. Each extra cow reduces the milk from all the others, a cost the herder does not bear [1]. So cows are added until the average equals the cost, beyond the point where the extra milk equals it [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the extra (marginal) return for the best level and the average return for open access; mixing them gives a wrong herd size.

**6. (a)** Cuts needed = 100 − 70 = 30, so each cuts 15 [1]. Cost = 15 × 4 + 15 × 10 = 60 + 150 = **210 crowns** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The cheaper firm P makes all 30 cuts [1]: cost = 30 × 4 = **120 crowns** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Between 4 and 10 crowns a unit [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** For example, the cap may be set too high or too low because the regulator lacks information [1]; so pollution may stay above the level that is best for society, or cost firms more than necessary [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A limitation gains more when you say what it does to the outcome.

**7.** A positive externality of production means the activity benefits third parties [1]. The firm ignores this, so MSC lies below MPC [1]. The market output is where MPC meets demand (MPB = MSB), which is lower than the social optimum where MSC meets demand [1]. The triangle between the two outputs is welfare loss [1]. A subsidy to producers equal to the marginal external benefit shifts MPC down to MSC [1]. Output rises to the optimum and the welfare loss is removed [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A labelled diagram with MPC, MSC, both outputs and the shaded triangle supports the written points; label which curve moves.

**8. (a)** 80 − Q = 20 + Q gives Q = 30 [1], P = 50 crowns [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** MSC = 30 + Q [1]; 80 − Q = 30 + Q gives Q = **25** [1]. Buyers pay P = 80 − 25 = **55 crowns** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Producers keep 55 − 10 = **45 crowns** [1]. Revenue = 10 × 25 = **250 million crowns** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** At Q = 30, MSC = 60 and MSB = 50, gap 10 [1]. Area = ½ × 5 × 10 = **25 million crowns** [1]. **[2]**
**(e)** For example, steelworkers [1]: if output falls from 30 to 25, firms may employ fewer people [1], so some face job losses [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the stakeholder effect to the figures; a group named without a reason and a direction of change is thin.

**9.** Indicative points: legislation sets a clear, certain limit [1] that is simple to apply [1], but gives firms no reason to cut below it [1] and treats firms alike even when their costs differ [1]. It needs monitoring and penalties [1]. Permits cap the total and let the cheapest cuts happen first [1], with a continuing reason to cut further [1]; but the cap may be set wrongly, since the external cost is hard to measure [1], and the first allocation may be unfair [1]. A judgement that depends on the pollutant, the number of firms and the ability to monitor [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Weigh the two policies against each other and end with a supported judgement.

**10.** The pollutant crosses borders, so one country cannot control the harm [1]. A country that cuts bears the cost while others share the benefit, which tempts them to free ride [1]. Countries differ in income and harm suffered, so they disagree on who pays [1]. Agreements need accurate monitoring of emissions [1]; they need enforcement against countries that break them [1], which is hard because states are sovereign [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Cover both parts, why cooperation is needed and why it is hard; an answer on one alone is incomplete.

**11.** The external cost has no market price [1], so it must be estimated, for example by valuing health damage or lost habitat [1]. Estimates are uncertain [1], so a tax or subsidy set from them may be too high or too low, leaving some welfare loss or burdening firms and consumers needlessly [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Tie each challenge to a consequence for policy; naming "hard to measure" twice adds nothing.

**12. (a)** 50 − Q = 14 + Q gives Q = 18 [1]; P = 32 marks [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** MSB = 56 − Q [1]; 56 − Q = 14 + Q gives Q = 21 [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Subsidy = marginal external benefit = 6 marks [1]; cost = 6 × 21 = 126 thousand marks [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** At Q = 18, MSB = 38 and MPC = 32, gap 6 [1]. Area = ½ × 3 × 6 = 9 thousand marks [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* For a positive externality of consumption add the benefit to demand; the wrong curve gives the wrong optimum in every later part.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Setting the optimum where the market curves cross instead of where MSB meets MSC.
- Adding an external benefit to supply or an external cost to demand.
- Using the price, not the gap between MSB and MSC at the market output, as the triangle height.
- Mixing average and marginal return in common pool questions.
- Naming a policy without saying which curve it moves.
- Calling a common pool resource a public good.
