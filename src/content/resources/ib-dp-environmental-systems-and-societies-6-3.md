## Net zero and the carbon budget

Because warming from CO₂ depends on cumulative emissions (6.2), temperature stops rising only when net CO₂ emissions reach about zero. **Net zero** means that any remaining emissions are balanced by an equal amount of removal from the atmosphere. A **carbon budget** is the total CO₂ that can still be emitted while keeping warming below a chosen limit, such as 1.5 °C or 2 °C. The faster the budget is used, the steeper the cuts needed later.

Net zero targets are only as strong as their detail. Questions to ask of any pledge: by when, covering which gases and sectors, how much depends on removals rather than cuts, and what happens in the next decade rather than in thirty years.

## Mitigation options by sector

| Sector | Main options |
|---|---|
| Electricity | Wind, solar, hydro, nuclear; storage and grids that balance supply |
| Transport | Electric vehicles, public transport, cycling and walking, cleaner shipping and aviation fuels |
| Buildings | Insulation, heat pumps, efficient appliances |
| Industry | Efficiency, electrified heat, low-carbon steel and cement |
| Agriculture and land | Less deforestation, reduced methane from livestock and rice, better fertiliser use |
| Waste | Capturing landfill methane, reducing food waste |

### Worked example: ranking options by abatement cost

A fictional city must cut 500 000 t CO₂e a year. It has four options. Costs are invented and given per tonne abated (negative means the option saves money over its life).

| Option | Abatement potential / t CO₂e per year | Cost / $ per t CO₂e |
|---|---|---|
| Building insulation | 150 000 | −20 |
| Landfill methane capture | 100 000 | 10 |
| Solar farms | 200 000 | 30 |
| Carbon capture at a cement plant | 250 000 | 90 |

```
Take the cheapest options first until the target is met:
insulation      150 000 t  →  cumulative 150 000 t
landfill gas    100 000 t  →  cumulative 250 000 t
solar           200 000 t  →  cumulative 450 000 t
cement capture   50 000 t  →  cumulative 500 000 t (target met)

annual cost = (150 000 × −20) + (100 000 × 10) + (200 000 × 30) + (50 000 × 90)
            = −3 000 000 + 1 000 000 + 6 000 000 + 4 500 000
            = $8 500 000 per year
average cost = 8 500 000 ÷ 500 000 = $17 per t CO₂e
```

**Interpretation.** Ranking by cost shows that some mitigation pays for itself, and that the most expensive options are needed only for the last part of the target. Real decisions also weigh how quickly each option can be built, co-benefits (insulation cuts fuel poverty; solar reduces air pollution), who pays and public acceptance.

## Carbon pricing

Putting a price on emissions makes polluters pay for the external cost of climate change.

- **Carbon tax**: a fixed price per tonne emitted. Businesses know the cost in advance, but the amount of emissions cut is uncertain.
- **Cap-and-trade (emissions trading)**: a cap fixes the total allowed emissions, and permits are traded, so the price is set by the market. The amount cut is certain, but the price can be unstable. The European Union's Emissions Trading System, launched in 2005, is the largest long-running example.

Both encourage the cheapest cuts first, as in the worked example. Criticisms: carbon prices can fall hardest on low-income households unless revenue is returned to them, and firms may move production to countries without a price (carbon leakage).

## Removing carbon

**Natural removal**:

- **Afforestation and reforestation** store carbon in trees and soils, with biodiversity benefits if native species are used. Limits: land competition with food, risk of release by fire or logging, and slow uptake.
- **Blue carbon**: mangroves, seagrass meadows and salt marshes store carbon in waterlogged sediments, where it decomposes very slowly. Protecting and restoring them also shields coasts from storms (an adaptation co-benefit).
- **Soil carbon**: cover crops, reduced tillage and restoring peatlands (see 5.1).

**Technological removal**:

- **Carbon capture and storage (CCS)** captures CO₂ from power stations or industry and stores it underground. On its own it reduces emissions rather than removing past CO₂.
- **Bioenergy with CCS (BECCS)**: crops or wood are burned for energy and the CO₂ is captured, giving net removal, but large areas of land would be needed.
- **Direct air capture (DAC)**: machines extract CO₂ from ambient air; it needs a lot of energy and is currently expensive.

### Worked example: how much can planting offset?

A fictional city emits 1.2 million t CO₂ a year and plans 2 000 ha of new forest. Assume each hectare absorbs 8 t CO₂ a year once established.

```
uptake = 2 000 ha × 8 t CO₂ ha⁻¹ per year = 16 000 t CO₂ per year
share of emissions = 16 000 ÷ 1 200 000 × 100 = 1.3 %
area to offset all emissions = 1 200 000 ÷ 8 = 150 000 ha
```

**Interpretation.** Planting offsets only a small share of the city's emissions; offsetting them all would need 150 000 ha. Removal complements deep cuts but cannot replace them.

## Solar geoengineering

**Solar radiation modification** would reflect more sunlight to cool the planet, for example by injecting aerosols into the stratosphere (mimicking a volcanic eruption) or brightening marine clouds. Risks to weigh:

- It does nothing about ocean acidification.
- It could change rainfall patterns, with winners and losers between regions.
- If stopped suddenly, the masked warming could return rapidly (**termination shock**).
- It raises governance questions: who decides the global thermostat?
- It may reduce pressure to cut emissions (moral hazard).

## Adaptation in more depth

- **Hard (engineered) adaptation**: sea walls, flood barriers, reservoirs, raised buildings.
- **Soft adaptation**: early-warning systems, insurance, land-use planning, changing crop calendars, education.
- **Ecosystem-based adaptation**: restoring mangroves, wetlands and floodplains to absorb storm surges and floodwater, often cheaper and with biodiversity benefits.
- **Managed retreat**: moving people and infrastructure away from the most exposed land.

**Maladaptation** is an action that increases vulnerability or emissions in the long run. Examples: a sea wall that protects one stretch of coast but increases erosion further along; air conditioning powered by fossil fuels; building in flood zones because defences give a false sense of security. Good adaptation increases **resilience**, the ability of a system to absorb shocks and recover.

## Sharing responsibility

- The UNFCCC (1992) set out the principle of **common but differentiated responsibilities**: all countries share the duty to act, but richer countries with larger historical emissions should lead.
- Under the Paris Agreement, countries' nationally determined contributions are reviewed through a **global stocktake**; the first was completed in 2023.
- **Climate finance** moves money from richer to poorer countries for mitigation and adaptation, for example through the Green Climate Fund.
- At COP27 in 2022, countries agreed to set up a fund for **loss and damage**, to help vulnerable countries with harm that cannot be avoided by adaptation.

Value systems shape preferred responses: technocentrists tend to favour technological removal and geoengineering research; ecocentrists favour reduced consumption, protection of ecosystems and local resilience.

## Model answer: distinguish

**Question.** Distinguish between a carbon tax and a cap-and-trade system. **[3]**

**Answer.** A carbon tax sets a fixed price per tonne of emissions, so the cost is certain but the amount of emissions reduced is not [1]. Cap-and-trade sets a fixed limit on total emissions and lets firms trade permits, so the amount reduced is certain but the price varies with the market [1]. Both put a price on emissions, but one fixes the price and the other fixes the quantity [1].

## Common mistakes

- Calling CCS on a power station "carbon removal"; it prevents new emissions but does not remove past CO₂.
- Treating tree planting as a full offset for current emissions.
- Writing that solar geoengineering "solves climate change"; it masks warming and ignores acidification.
- Calling every coastal defence adaptation without considering maladaptation.
- Confusing a global stocktake with a new treaty.
- Evaluating a strategy without cost, time scale or who pays.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate**: rank options by cost per tonne and total them step by step.
- **Evaluate**: effectiveness, cost, speed, co-benefits, side effects, equity, judgement.
- **Compare and contrast**: use paired sentences (for example, carbon tax versus cap-and-trade).
- **To what extent**: in essays on whether adaptation is now more important than mitigation, argue both sides with examples and conclude.
