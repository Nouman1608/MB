## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Aggregate supply (AS) | Total planned real output that producers in an economy will supply at each general price level, over a period |
| Short run | A period in which some costs, such as wage rates and capital, are fixed |
| Long run | A period in which all factors of production and their prices can change |
| SRAS | Short-run aggregate supply: upward sloping, drawn for given costs |
| LRAS | Long-run aggregate supply: the economy's productive capacity |
| Movement along AS | Caused only by a change in the general price level |
| Shift of SRAS | Caused by a change in firms' costs at every output level |
| Shift of LRAS | Caused by a change in the quantity or quality of resources |
| Classical LRAS | Vertical at full-employment output; long-run output independent of the price level |
| Keynesian LRAS | Horizontal, then upward sloping, then vertical at full capacity |
| Spare capacity | Unused resources, such as idle workers and machines |
| Bottleneck | A shortage of a particular input that raises costs as output expands |
| Productivity | Output per worker or per hour worked |

## Item 1: the AS curve

- Axes: **general price level** (vertical) against **real national output** (horizontal).
- SRAS slopes upward because, with some costs fixed in the short run, a higher price level makes extra output profitable; and squeezing more output from fixed capital raises the cost of each extra unit.
- Price level changes: **movement along**. Everything else that affects supply: **shift**.

## Item 2: SRAS factors (the three named in the specification)

| Change | Direction of SRAS |
|---|---|
| Costs of raw materials and energy rise | Left |
| Costs of raw materials and energy fall | Right |
| Exchange rate depreciates (imports dearer) | Left |
| Exchange rate appreciates (imports cheaper) | Right |
| Tax rates that add to firms' costs rise | Left |
| Those tax rates fall | Right |

**Chain to learn.** Cost change → unit cost at every output level changes → firms supply a different output at each price level → SRAS shifts → new equilibrium price level and real output.

**Exchange rates have two effects.** A depreciation shifts SRAS left (dearer imported inputs) but can shift AD right (cheaper exports, dearer imports). Mention both in evaluation.

## Item 3: LRAS shapes

**Classical (vertical).**

- Wages and prices are flexible in the long run.
- Output returns to the full-employment level after any shock.
- AD changes affect only the price level in the long run.
- Policy message: raise output through supply-side improvements.

**Keynesian (three sections).**

```
section 1  horizontal      spare capacity: output rises, prices steady
section 2  upward sloping  bottlenecks: output and prices both rise
section 3  vertical        full capacity: only prices rise
```

- Wages and prices are slow to fall, so the economy can stay below full employment.
- Policy message: in a recession, higher AD can raise output with little inflation.

## Item 3: LRAS factors (the six named in the specification)

| Factor | Example of a rightward shift | Example of a leftward shift |
|---|---|---|
| State of technology | Automation spreads across industry | Ageing equipment not replaced |
| Productivity | Better management raises output per hour | Poor infrastructure slows work |
| Education and skills | Wider access to vocational training | Falling school attendance |
| Government regulations and tax | Simpler business regulation; lower tax on enterprise | Costly new compliance rules |
| Demography and net migration | Net inflow of working-age migrants | Ageing population, shrinking workforce |
| Competition policy | Breaking up monopolies, opening markets | Weak enforcement lets firms become complacent |

**Calculation pattern.** Capacity = workers (or hours) × output per worker (or hour). Percentage changes combine by multiplying growth factors: a 2% rise in workers and a 3% rise in productivity give 1.02 × 1.03 = 1.0506, a 5.06% rise.

## Evaluation points that fit most AS questions

- **Time lags:** education, migration and technology change LRAS slowly.
- **Size:** a small cost change across a few industries may barely move SRAS.
- **Shape of LRAS:** the same AD shift has different effects in each Keynesian section and under the classical view.
- **Both curves:** technology and productivity shift SRAS and LRAS.
- **Interaction with AD:** exchange rates and tax changes affect AD as well as AS.
- **Permanence:** a temporary energy shock moves SRAS; a lasting one may reduce capacity.

## Exam traps

- **Price level shifting AS.** It is a movement along the curve.
- **Demand-side causes of AS shifts.** Consumer confidence or interest rates shift AD, not AS.
- **Wrong curve.** Cost shocks shift SRAS; changes in resources shift LRAS.
- **Unnamed factors.** Use the specification's lists; they are what the mark scheme expects.
- **Keynesian curve without reasons.** Each section needs a reason: spare capacity, bottlenecks, full capacity.
- **Percentage changes added instead of multiplied** in a capacity calculation.

## Quick self-test

1. Define aggregate supply.
2. What causes a movement along the SRAS curve?
3. Name the three factors the specification lists for SRAS.
4. Explain the effect of a depreciation of the exchange rate on SRAS.
5. A firm's unit cost is 80, of which raw materials are 24. Raw material prices rise by 25%. Find the new unit cost and the percentage rise.
6. Describe the classical LRAS curve and explain its shape.
7. Name and explain the three sections of the Keynesian LRAS curve.
8. On which section of the Keynesian curve does a rise in AD raise real output with no rise in the price level?
9. Explain how competition policy can shift LRAS.
10. A labour force of 20 million produces 30 000 each. The labour force rises by 2% and productivity by 3%. Find the new capacity and its percentage rise.
11. Why might an ageing population shift LRAS to the left?
12. Which curve or curves shift when a new production technology spreads?

### Answers

1. The total planned real output that producers in an economy will supply at each general price level, over a given period.
2. A change in the **general price level**.
3. Changes in **costs of raw materials and energy**, **exchange rates** and **tax rates**.
4. A depreciation raises the home-currency price of imported raw materials and components, so unit costs rise at every output level and **SRAS shifts left**.
5. 24 × 1.25 = 30, so the unit cost is 80 − 24 + 30 = **86**, a rise of 6 ÷ 80 × 100 = **7.5%**.
6. **Vertical** at full-employment output: in the long run flexible wages and prices return output to capacity, so long-run output does not depend on the price level.
7. **Horizontal** (spare capacity, no pressure on costs), **upward sloping** (bottlenecks raise costs), **vertical** (full capacity, no more output possible).
8. The **horizontal** section.
9. Stronger competition pushes firms to cut costs, use resources efficiently and innovate, raising productivity and capacity, so **LRAS shifts right**.
10. Before: 20 m × 30 000 = 600 bn. After: 20.4 m × 30 900 = **630.36 bn**, a rise of **5.06%** (1.02 × 1.03 = 1.0506).
11. A larger share of people are past working age, so the **labour force shrinks** (or grows more slowly), reducing productive capacity.
12. **Both**: unit costs fall straight away (SRAS right) and productive capacity rises (LRAS right).
