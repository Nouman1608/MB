## Key definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Phillips Curve | Inverse relationship between unemployment and inflation (money wage growth) |
| Short-run Phillips Curve (SRPC) | Trade-off for a given level of expected inflation |
| Natural rate of unemployment | Unemployment left when the labour market clears: frictional plus structural, with no cyclical element |
| NAIRU | Non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment: the rate at which inflation is stable |
| Adaptive expectations | Expected inflation is based on past inflation, e.g. this year's expectation equals last year's actual rate |
| Long-run Phillips Curve (LRPC) | Vertical line at the natural rate: no lasting trade-off |
| Cyclical unemployment | Actual unemployment minus the natural rate when it is positive |
| Malinvestment | Austrian term for investment misdirected by artificially cheap credit |

## The Phillips Curve in six lines

1. Axes: unemployment (%) horizontal; wage or price inflation (%) vertical.
2. Low unemployment means labour shortages, higher wage growth, higher costs and prices.
3. The curve slopes down and is convex; it is flatter at high unemployment.
4. Each SRPC is drawn for one level of expected inflation.
5. It cuts the vertical LRPC at the point where actual equals expected inflation.
6. A rise in expected inflation shifts the SRPC up.

## Formulas and method

| Result | Formula |
|---|---|
| Unemployment rate | unemployed ÷ labour force × 100 |
| Expectations-augmented curve | inflation = expected inflation + b × (U* − U) |
| Adaptive expectations | expected inflation this year = actual inflation last year |
| Unit labour cost growth | (1 + w) ÷ (1 + p) − 1, with w = pay growth and p = output per worker growth |
| Cumulative growth over n years | (1 + g)ⁿ − 1 |
| Unemployment needed for a target | U = U* − (target − expected) ÷ b |

**Method: adaptive-expectations table** (the example below uses Country B, illustrative).
1. Write the equation and fix expected inflation for year 1.
2. Compute actual inflation for year 1.
3. Set next year's expected inflation equal to this year's actual.
4. Repeat. If U is below U*, inflation rises each year by b × (U* − U); if above, it falls by b × (U − U*).

**Worked reminder.** Country B: inflation = expected + 0.6 × (5 − U), starting at 3%.
- Hold U = 3.5%: gain each year 0.6 × 1.5 = 0.9. Inflation is 3.9%, 4.8%, 5.7% in years 1 to 3.
- Return U to 5%: inflation stays at 5.7%. No gain in jobs, higher inflation.
- Then hold U = 6.5% from 5.7%: inflation falls 0.6 × 1.5 = 0.9 a year, to 4.8%, 3.9%, 3.0%. Disinflation costs jobs.

**Why unemployment is not tied to inflation in the long run.** Workers and firms adjust expectations until actual equals expected inflation. That happens only at the natural rate.

## Natural rate and NAIRU in one table

| Actual U compared with natural rate | Labour market | Inflation |
|---|---|---|
| Below | Excess demand for labour | Higher than expected, so expectations rise and inflation accelerates |
| Equal | Equilibrium | Equals expected inflation: stable |
| Above | Excess supply of labour | Lower than expected, so inflation decelerates |

Shifts of the natural rate:
- **Down (LRPC moves left):** better training, mobility, job information, less generous or shorter benefits, more flexible hiring.
- **Up (LRPC moves right):** hysteresis after long slumps, mismatch of skills and vacancies, rigid pay.

## Evaluating the Phillips Curve

**Explains well when:** demand shocks dominate; expectations are stable; the period is short.

**Explains poorly when:**
- supply shocks push up costs while unemployment is also high
- expectations change, shifting the SRPC
- globalisation, weak bargaining power or flexible contracts flatten the curve
- unemployment is mismeasured (hidden unemployment, underemployment)
- wage growth and price inflation diverge because of productivity

**Usefulness to policymakers.**
- For: shows the short-run cost of disinflation; warns against pushing unemployment below NAIRU; supports credible inflation targets; points to supply-side reform.
- Against: NAIRU cannot be observed and changes; the SRPC shifts; policy lags; fine-tuning risks error.

Worked reminder: Country B, expected inflation 3%, slope 0.6, U* = 5%. For wage growth of 4.2%: U = 5 − (4.2 − 3) ÷ 0.6 = 3.0%. If U* is really 6%, the same U gives 3 + 0.6 × 3 = 4.8%, an overshoot. A small error in U* gives a large policy error.

## Diagram checklist

Label the axes (unemployment % across, inflation % up) and name each curve:

| Curve | Shape and position |
|---|---|
| LRPC | Vertical line at U* on the unemployment axis |
| SRPC, lower expected inflation | Downward-sloping, convex, crossing the LRPC at a low inflation rate |
| SRPC, higher expected inflation | Same shape, shifted up, crossing the LRPC at a higher inflation rate |


- Two SRPCs: the higher one has higher expected inflation. Both cross the LRPC at U*.
- Government pushes unemployment left of U*: a move up the first SRPC, then a shift up to the second as expectations rise.
- Supply-side reform: LRPC shifts left to a lower U*.

## A plan for an evaluation answer

1. Define the term and give the mechanism in two or three linked steps.
2. Give one or two points for and against, each with a reason.
3. Say what the answer depends on: time period, source of shock, size of expectations effect, or quality of data.
4. Reach a judgement that follows from your points and applies to the context given.

## The three schools

| | Keynesian | Neo-classical | Austrian |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core claim | AD drives output; slumps can persist | Markets clear; economy self-corrects | Credit expansion causes boom and bust |
| Wages | Sticky | Flexible | Flexible and informative |
| Long-run AS | Not necessarily vertical | Vertical | Set by real saving and capital |
| Unemployment | Can be well above natural | Mostly natural | Adjusts as malinvestment is cleared |
| Policy | Fiscal and monetary stimulus | Supply-side policy; stable money | Sound money; do not bail out or stimulate |
| Strength | Explains long slumps | Explains long-run limits of stimulus | Explains credit cycles |
| Weakness | Inflation, debt, lags, stagflation | Slow adjustment is costly | Remedy of letting failures run is costly |

**Chain of reasoning for a fall in spending**
- Keynesian: spending falls, firms cut output, wages do not fall, unemployment stays high, so government raises AD.
- Neo-classical: wages and prices fall, output returns to potential, so intervention is unnecessary and may harm.
- Austrian: the fall follows an earlier credit-fuelled boom, so the bust corrects past errors, and stimulus would repeat them.

**Austrian arithmetic reminder.** Credit grows 8% a year for 4 years: 1.08⁴ − 1 = 36.0%. Real income grows 2%: 1.02⁴ − 1 = 8.2%. Credit has grown more than four times as fast as income.

**Evaluating a school.** Check the time period, the cause of the problem (demand, supply or credit) and the costs of acting or waiting. Do not declare one school simply correct.

## Quick self-test

1. A country has 50 million in the labour force and 2.5 million unemployed. Calculate the unemployment rate.
2. In Country B, wage growth = 3 + 0.6 × (5 − U). Find wage growth when U = 4%.
3. State why the natural rate is called NAIRU.
4. Wage growth is 6% and productivity growth is 3%. Calculate unit labour cost growth to one decimal place.
5. Under adaptive expectations, last year's inflation was 4% and U is 1 point below U* with slope 0.6. State this year's inflation.
6. Why is the LRPC vertical?
7. Name two factors that would shift the LRPC left.
8. State one reason the Phillips Curve may fail to predict inflation.
9. State the Austrian explanation for a recession in one sentence.
10. State one reason Keynesians favour demand management.
11. Credit grows 8% and income 2% over four years. Calculate the cumulative growth of credit.

### Answers

1. 2.5 ÷ 50 × 100 = **5.0%**.
2. 3 + 0.6 × (5 − 4) = **3.6%**.
3. Inflation is stable (not accelerating) at that rate because actual equals expected inflation.
4. 1.06 ÷ 1.03 − 1 = **2.9%**.
5. 4 + 0.6 × 1 = **4.6%**.
6. Expectations adjust until actual equals expected inflation, which holds only at the natural rate, whatever the inflation level.
7. Better training and greater labour mobility (also better job-matching or more flexible hiring).
8. Supply shocks or changed expectations shift the curve (also globalisation or mismeasured unemployment).
9. Artificially cheap credit causes malinvestment in a boom, and the recession liquidates it.
10. Wages and prices are sticky, so the economy can stay below full employment without help.
11. 1.08⁴ − 1 = **36.0%**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Drawing a Phillips Curve with unemployment on the wrong axis or with no labelled axes.
- Describing the long-run curve as vertical without explaining expectations catching up.
- Saying NAIRU is the rate at which inflation is zero. It is the rate at which inflation does not accelerate.
- Treating the natural rate as zero unemployment or as including cyclical unemployment.
- Confusing wage growth with price inflation, and ignoring productivity.
- Listing weaknesses of the curve without judging how far they matter and over what period.
- Describing Keynesian, neo-classical and Austrian views in separate paragraphs and never comparing them.
- Calling a school "wrong" with no evidence or context from the question.
