This set covers subtopic 7.3, Solid waste, from Topic 7 of IB DP Environmental Systems and Societies, first assessment 2026. Every question suits SL and HL. All data and cities are fictional. Learn the content first in the [7.3 study guide](/resources/ib-dp-environmental-systems-and-societies-7-3/) and the [7.3 revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-environmental-systems-and-societies-7-3-revision-notes/).

## Questions

**1.** Distinguish between biodegradable and non-biodegradable waste, with **one** example of each. **[2]**

**2.** Outline why hazardous household waste, such as batteries, should be collected separately from other solid domestic waste. **[2]**

**3.** Table 1 shows solid domestic waste data for two fictional cities.

| | City A | City B |
|---|---|---|
| Population (millions) | 2.0 | 1.5 |
| SDW per year (t) | 600,000 | 750,000 |
| Organics (%) | 58 | 30 |
| Paper and card (%) | 10 | 25 |
| Plastics (%) | 9 | 16 |
| Glass and metals (%) | 5 | 12 |
| Other (%) | 18 | 17 |

**(a)** Calculate the waste per person in each city, in kg per year. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the mass of plastics produced in each city. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest, with reasons, a priority strategy for each city. **[4]**

**4.** Explain how a sanitary landfill reduces pollution compared with an open dump. **[4]**

**5.** Compare composting and anaerobic digestion as ways of treating organic waste. **[3]**

**6.** A fictional incinerator burns 200,000 t of waste a year. Bottom ash is 22% and fly ash 3% of the input mass.

**(a)** Calculate the mass of bottom ash and of fly ash produced each year. **[2]**
**(b)** State why fly ash needs special disposal. **[1]**
**(c)** Evaluate incineration with energy recovery for a city whose landfill sites are nearly full. **[4]**

**7.** A kerbside scheme collects 12,000 t of recyclables a year. The share rejected because of contamination rises from 8% to 20%.

**(a)** Calculate the fall in the mass actually recycled. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest **two** ways to reduce contamination. **[2]**

**8.** Distinguish between a linear economy and a circular economy, using **one** product as an example. **[3]**

**9.** Explain the environmental and health impacts of informal recycling of electronic waste. **[3]**

**10.** Evaluate deposit-return schemes for drinks containers. **[4]**

**11.** Discuss how ecocentric and technocentric perspectives might lead to different waste management policies. **[6]**

**12.** To what extent is the waste hierarchy a useful guide for a city's solid waste policy? **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Biodegradable waste can be broken down by decomposers, such as food scraps [1]; non-biodegradable waste cannot be broken down this way and persists, such as glass or most plastics [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Each mark needs the meaning and a correct example.

**2.** Batteries contain toxic metals that can leak from landfill in leachate or be released by incineration [1]; separate collection allows them to be treated safely and their metals recovered for reuse [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Fire risk from lithium batteries in collection vehicles is also credited.

**3. (a)** City A: 600,000,000 kg ÷ 2,000,000 = **300 kg per person** [1]; City B: 750,000,000 ÷ 1,500,000 = **500 kg per person** [1]
**(b)** City A: 0.09 × 600,000 = **54,000 t** [1]; City B: 0.16 × 750,000 = **120,000 t** [1]
**(c)** City A: composting or anaerobic digestion [1], because organics are 58% of its waste (348,000 t), the largest fraction, and would otherwise release methane in landfill [1]. City B: reduction and recycling of packaging, such as extended producer responsibility or deposit-return [1], because paper, plastics, glass and metals together make up 53% and waste per person is higher [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* The reason mark needs a figure from the table; "City A has lots of food waste" without data earns only the strategy mark.

**4.** A liner stops leachate seeping into soil and groundwater [1], and collection pipes drain the leachate for treatment [1]. Gas wells capture methane so it is flared or used for energy rather than escaping [1]. Daily cover reduces windblown litter, smells and pests that spread disease [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each mark needs the feature and the pollution it controls.

**5.** Both divert organic waste from landfill and return nutrients to soil [1]. Composting is aerobic and produces a soil improver, while anaerobic digestion takes place without oxygen in sealed tanks [1] and also produces biogas that can be used as fuel, so it recovers energy [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Compare" needs at least one similarity and one difference.

**6. (a)** Bottom ash: 0.22 × 200,000 = **44,000 t** [1]; fly ash: 0.03 × 200,000 = **6,000 t** [1]
**(b)** It contains concentrated toxic substances, such as heavy metals and dioxins, caught by the filters [1].
**(c)** For: it greatly reduces the volume needing landfill [1] and generates electricity or heat from waste [1]. Against: it needs flue-gas cleaning and still produces hazardous ash and CO₂ [1], and its need for a steady supply of waste can discourage reduction and recycling, which sit higher in the hierarchy [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* A judgement is expected in (c); for example, incineration may be justified for residual waste only, after reduction and recycling.

**7. (a)** At 8%: 12,000 × 0.92 = 11,040 t; at 20%: 12,000 × 0.80 = 9,600 t [1]; fall = **1,440 t** [1]
**(b)** Any two: clearer labelling of what can be recycled [1]; simpler sorting rules or separate collections for each material [1]; education campaigns [1]; feedback tags on bins that are wrongly filled [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Subtracting the percentages (12 percentage points) without converting to mass gives no final answer; the question asks for tonnes.

**8.** In a linear economy materials are taken, made into products, used and thrown away [1]; in a circular economy products and materials are kept in use through durable design, repair, reuse and recycling, and waste is designed out [1]. Example: a linear phone is discarded after a few years; a circular phone is designed with replaceable parts and returned for refurbishment and metal recovery [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The example must show the difference, not just name a product.

**9.** Burning cables and plastics to recover copper releases toxic fumes, harming workers' lungs [1]. Acids used to extract metals and leftover residues contaminate soil and water with heavy metals such as lead [1]. These pollutants can enter crops and food chains, affecting nearby communities, often including children [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the process and the pollutant; "it is dangerous" earns nothing.

**10.** For: a refundable deposit gives a direct incentive to return containers, so return rates are high [1], and the collected material is clean, so it is easier to recycle or reuse [1]. Against: it covers only drinks containers, a small part of the waste stream [1], and needs collection points and machines, adding cost for retailers [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A one-line judgement weighing these, such as "effective for its target materials but only one part of a wider strategy", strengthens the answer.

**11.** Indicative points, up to 6.
- Ecocentric: emphasises reducing consumption and changing lifestyles [1].
- Favours reuse, repair, composting and local, small-scale schemes [1].
- Supports regulation such as bans on single-use items [1].
- Technocentric: trusts technology to manage waste outputs [1].
- Favours advanced recycling, incineration with energy recovery and engineered landfill [1].
- Comment: technocentric solutions can manage existing waste quickly but may lock in high waste volumes, while ecocentric solutions tackle causes but depend on public behaviour change [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Link each perspective to specific policies; describing the perspectives in general earns little.

**12.** Indicative points, one mark each up to 9. Real essays are marked holistically, so treat these as a guide.
- The hierarchy ranks options: reduce, reuse, recycle and compost, recover energy, dispose [1].
- Useful: it directs effort at causes rather than outcomes, matching the first level of the pollution management model [1].
- Useful: it is simple to communicate to the public and to set policy targets [1].
- Limitation: the best option depends on the material; composting suits organics, while some plastics cannot be recycled well [1].
- Limitation: local conditions matter, such as the absence of markets for recycled materials or space for landfill [1].
- Limitation: life-cycle impacts can reverse the order, for example when transporting recyclables long distances [1].
- Limitation: the top levels depend on producers and consumers, which a city controls only partly [1].
- Evidence from data, such as a city with mostly organic waste, should shape the priorities [1].
- Judgement: a useful starting framework, but policy should be fitted to the waste composition and local conditions [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong essays apply the hierarchy to a real or given city and end with a conditional judgement.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Strategies chosen without reference to the waste composition in the data.
- Landfill descriptions that omit the controls, or confuse sanitary landfill with open dumps.
- Forgetting hazardous fly ash in incineration answers.
- Working in percentage points when the question asks for tonnes.
- "Compare" answers with no similarity.
- Perspective answers that never name a policy.
