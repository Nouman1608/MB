Unit 4 of the 9675 specification names four broad areas for literary representations. This guide
covers one of them: "Representations of race/class/culture". It applies to both routes: 4a, the
examination on unseen prose and poetry, and 4b, the non-exam assessment. The unit as a whole,
including what "representation" means and how the two routes differ, is explained in the
[Unit 4 study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-english-literature-unit-4-literary-representations-study-guide/).
This page is about the particular methods writers use to represent race, class and culture, and how
to write about them precisely.

## What the specification asks

The specification stresses that the four areas "are not topics which are prescribed for detailed
study", and that students "are not expected to draw on extensive contextual knowledge of the topic,
nor are they expected to refer to wider reading". The skill is reading how a text constructs a view
of race, class or culture. Its own example 4a question asks for "the significance of Auden's
representation of social class and culture" in an unseen poem, and its example 4b task asks how far
a reader can sympathise with Forster's English characters in *A Passage to India*.

Two consequences follow. First, an answer is judged on analysis of the text in front of you, not on
what you know about a society. Second, because the area combines three terms, a question will
usually direct you to one aspect, such as class and culture, or race and belonging. Read the wording
carefully and answer the aspect named.

## Six things to look for

1. **Viewpoint: inside or outside.** Is the group represented by one of its members or by an
   observer? An outsider's view may reveal prejudice, curiosity or fascination; an insider's may
   show pride, shame or divided loyalty.
2. **Markers.** Writers signal class and culture through clothing, food, furniture, work, money,
   education, religion and rituals. Ask what each marker is made to mean, and who judges it.
3. **Voice and language.** Dialect, accent, code-switching between languages, formal and informal
   registers. Is a character's speech given dignity, or used for comedy or contempt?
4. **The gaze.** Who looks at whom? Being looked at, described or measured is often a sign of less
   power. Notice when the looked-at character looks back.
5. **Stereotype and its subversion.** A text may use a stereotype in order to break it, or may
   repeat it uncritically. Decide which, and justify it from the text.
6. **Space and movement.** Who may enter which room, street or country; who serves and who is
   served; who migrates and who stays.

## Reading the date and other information

In 4a the unseen text is usually given with an author and date of first publication. Use it
lightly. A date can explain why a text treats class as fixed or as changing, or why a writer from a
colonised country writes in a particular way about the coloniser's language. It does not license a
paragraph of history. A useful test: every contextual sentence should change how you read a word or
image in the text.

Be careful of two errors. One is **anachronistic judgement**, dismissing an older text because its
attitudes differ from modern ones, without analysing how it presents them. The other is **assuming
the writer shares a character's view**: a narrator's snobbery or prejudice may be the target of the
text's irony.

## Methods particular to this area

- **Contrastive description**: two households, two meals, two ways of speaking placed side by side.
- **Free indirect style**: a narrator slips into a character's thoughts, so the reader hears their
  judgement of others without the narrator endorsing it.
- **Lists of possessions**: objects reveal economic position and aspiration, and their order can
  carry irony.
- **Naming**: who is named, who is called by a group label, who is given a nickname by others.
- **Silence**: a character from the less powerful group who does not speak, or whose words are
  reported rather than heard.
- **Imagery of distance or barriers**: windows, walls, counters, uniforms.

## Writing about race respectfully and precisely

Use the text's own terms when quoting, and your own careful terms when analysing. Write about
"representation of" a group rather than making claims about the group itself. If a text uses
offensive language, discuss its effect and function without repeating it more than necessary. These
habits are also more accurate: your subject is how the text builds a picture of people.

## Worked example on an original passage

The passage is **original**, written for this guide in the style of mid-twentieth-century literary
non-fiction. It is not from any real text.

> My mother cleaned the doctor's house on Tuesdays and Fridays, and in the school holidays I went
> with her. I was allowed to sit in the kitchen, which was larger than our whole downstairs, and I
> was not allowed to touch the piano, which nobody played. Mrs Hale spoke to my mother in a slow,
> clear voice, as if English were a ladder she was helping her climb. My mother answered in the same
> careful English, then said something in our own language to me, very fast, that made me laugh,
> and Mrs Hale smiled at us both without understanding.

**Model paragraph:**

> The passage represents class and culture through the spaces a child may occupy and the languages
> each woman controls. The kitchen, "larger than our whole downstairs", measures the gap between the
> two households in domestic terms, while the untouched piano "which nobody played" turns a cultural
> object into a sign of wealth rather than use, so culture belongs to those who can afford to waste it.
> Mrs Hale's "slow, clear voice" is presented through the simile "as if English were a ladder she was
> helping her climb", which exposes her assumption that language marks rank and that she stands at the
> top. The final sentence reverses that hierarchy: the mother's rapid speech in "our own language"
> creates a private space Mrs Hale cannot enter, and her smile "without understanding" makes the
> employer, for once, the outsider. Written as memoir, the passage gives the child's retrospective
> voice the authority to judge the employer's kindness as a form of condescension.

## Applying this to 4b

In the non-exam route each text must be linked to a different one of the four areas, poetry must
be by a single author, and no text may come from the exam set-text lists. For a race, class or
culture task, choose a text that supports more than one reading, as the Forster example does by
inviting debate about sympathy. Frame the task around representation, not around the society
itself: "Explore the significance of the representation of..." keeps the focus where the mark
scheme rewards it.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing about society instead of the text.** Extensive context is not expected.
- **Equating narrator and author.** Prejudice voiced by a character may be under attack.
- **Answering all three terms.** Focus on the aspect the question names.
- **Listing markers.** Explain what each marker is made to mean.

## Exam technique

Unit 4a lasts 2 hours 30 minutes, with one compulsory prose question and one compulsory poetry
question, 25 marks each, on different areas. Spend the first minutes annotating viewpoint, markers
and voice, then build an argument about what the text suggests about the group or relationship it
represents.
