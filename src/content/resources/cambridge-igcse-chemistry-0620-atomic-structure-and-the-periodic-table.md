## What the syllabus asks for

| Outcome | You must be able to |
|---|---|
| 1 | Describe the atom as a central nucleus containing neutrons and protons, surrounded by electrons in shells |
| 2 | State the relative charges and relative masses of a proton, a neutron and an electron |
| 3 | Define proton number (atomic number) as the number of protons in the nucleus |
| 4 | Define mass number (nucleon number) as the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus |
| 5 | Determine the electronic configuration of elements and their ions with proton number 1 to 20, for example 2,8,3 |
| 6 | State that noble gases in Group VIII have a full outer shell; that outer-shell electrons equal the group number in Groups I to VII; and that the number of occupied shells equals the period number |

## The structure of the atom

| Particle | Where it is | Relative charge | Relative mass |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proton | Nucleus | +1 | 1 |
| Neutron | Nucleus | 0 | 1 |
| Electron | Shells around the nucleus | −1 | 1/1840 (negligible) |

Three consequences follow from this table, and each is examined.

- **Almost all the mass is in the nucleus**, because protons and neutrons each have a relative mass of 1 and electrons almost none.
- **An atom has no overall charge**, because it has equal numbers of protons (+1 each) and electrons (−1 each). Neutrons add no charge.
- **The nucleus is tiny compared with the atom**: most of an atom's volume is the space occupied by the electron shells.

Protons and neutrons together are called **nucleons**, which is why mass number is also called nucleon number.

## Proton number and mass number

- **Proton number (atomic number), Z**: the number of protons. It identifies the element: every atom with 13 protons is aluminium.
- **Mass number (nucleon number), A**: protons + neutrons.

So: **neutrons = mass number − proton number**. In a neutral atom, **electrons = proton number**.

### Worked example 1: particles in atoms

| Atom | Proton number | Mass number | Protons | Neutrons | Electrons |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lithium | 3 | 7 | 3 | 7 − 3 = 4 | 3 |
| Fluorine | 9 | 19 | 9 | 19 − 9 = 10 | 9 |
| Aluminium | 13 | 27 | 13 | 27 − 13 = 14 | 13 |
| Phosphorus | 15 | 31 | 15 | 31 − 15 = 16 | 15 |
| Calcium | 20 | 40 | 20 | 40 − 20 = 20 | 20 |

## Electronic configuration, 1 to 20

Electrons fill shells from the nucleus outwards. For proton numbers 1 to 20:

- the first shell holds up to **2** electrons
- the second shell holds up to **8**
- the third shell takes **8** before the fourth shell starts (this is what happens for potassium and calcium).

Method: write the number of electrons, fill 2, then 8, then 8, then the rest.

### Worked example 2: atoms

| Element | Electrons | Configuration |
|---|---|---|
| Beryllium | 4 | 2,2 |
| Nitrogen | 7 | 2,5 |
| Silicon | 14 | 2,8,4 |
| Sulfur | 16 | 2,8,6 |
| Calcium | 20 | 2,8,8,2 |

Check: the numbers in a configuration must add up to the number of electrons. For calcium, 2 + 8 + 8 + 2 = 20.

## Electronic configuration of ions

An ion forms when an atom gains or loses electrons. The number of protons does not change, so the element does not change.

- A **positive ion** has **lost** electrons: electrons = protons − charge.
- A **negative ion** has **gained** electrons: electrons = protons + size of charge.

Method for an ion: find the number of electrons first, then fill the shells exactly as for an atom.

### Worked example 3: ions

| Ion | Protons | Electrons | Configuration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Li⁺ | 3 | 3 − 1 = 2 | 2 |
| Al³⁺ | 13 | 13 − 3 = 10 | 2,8 |
| N³⁻ | 7 | 7 + 3 = 10 | 2,8 |
| S²⁻ | 16 | 16 + 2 = 18 | 2,8,8 |
| Ca²⁺ | 20 | 20 − 2 = 18 | 2,8,8 |

Notice that Al³⁺ and N³⁻ both have the configuration 2,8, the same as neon, and S²⁻ and Ca²⁺ both have 2,8,8, the same as argon. Ions of elements in this range usually end up with a full outer shell like a noble gas. Different ions can share a configuration, but they are still different ions, because their numbers of protons differ.

### Worked example 4: particles in an ion from its symbol

For ³²₁₆S²⁻: protons 16 (the bottom number); neutrons 32 − 16 = 16; electrons 16 + 2 = 18.

For ⁴⁰₂₀Ca²⁺: protons 20; neutrons 40 − 20 = 20; electrons 20 − 2 = 18.

## Configuration and the Periodic Table

The Periodic Table is arranged in order of increasing proton number. The electronic configuration tells you where an element sits:

- **Group number** (Groups I to VII) = number of electrons in the **outer shell**.
- **Period number** = number of **occupied shells**.
- **Group VIII** (the noble gases) have a **full outer shell**.

Helium is the special case. Its configuration is 2, so it has only two outer-shell electrons, but its only shell is full. That is why it belongs in Group VIII, and why the rule "outer electrons = group number" applies to Groups I to VII only.

### Worked example 5: from configuration to position

| Configuration | Outer electrons | Shells | Group | Period |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2,5 | 5 | 2 | V | 2 |
| 2,8,4 | 4 | 3 | IV | 3 |
| 2,8,8,2 | 2 | 4 | II | 4 |
| 2,8,8 | 8 (full) | 3 | VIII | 3 |

### Worked example 6: from position to identity

An element is in Group III and Period 3. Its configuration has 3 occupied shells and 3 outer electrons: 2,8,3. Total electrons 13, so proton number 13: **aluminium**.

## Common mistakes

- Giving the electron a relative mass of 0 or −1. Its mass is about 1/1840; its charge is −1.
- Placing electrons in the nucleus.
- Calculating neutrons as mass number + proton number.
- Putting 8 electrons in the first shell, or writing potassium as 2,8,9.
- For ions, adding electrons for positive ions and removing them for negative ions; it is the other way round.
- Saying helium is in Group II because it has two outer electrons.
- Saying an ion has become a different element.

## Exam technique

- **Define**: give the exact wording, such as "the number of protons in the nucleus of an atom".
- **State**: values from the particle table, with no working needed.
- **Determine** a configuration: show the number of electrons first, especially for ions.
- **Deduce** group and period: quote the outer electrons and the number of shells as your evidence.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does the third shell take only 8 electrons for elements 1 to 20?**
For potassium (19) and calcium (20), the 19th and 20th electrons go into the fourth shell after the third shell has 8. The syllabus only asks for configurations up to proton number 20.

**Do ions with the same configuration have the same properties?**
No. S²⁻ and Ca²⁺ both have 2,8,8, but they have different numbers of protons and opposite charges, so they are different ions.
