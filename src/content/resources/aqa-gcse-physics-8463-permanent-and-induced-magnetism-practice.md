## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Complete the sentence: like poles ________ and unlike poles ________. **[1]**

**2.** Give two differences between a permanent magnet and an induced magnet. **[2]**

**3.** Which of these materials is magnetic: brass, copper, nickel, aluminium? **[1]**

**4.** State what is meant by a magnetic field. **[1]**

**5.** State the direction of the field shown by a field line outside a bar magnet. **[1]**

**6.** Explain why the force between a magnet and an unmagnetised iron bar is always attractive. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** A student hangs a steel paper clip from the N pole of a bar magnet, then hangs a second clip from the bottom
of the first.

**(a)** Explain why the second clip is held up even though it does not touch the magnet. **[2]**
**(b)** The student pulls the top clip away from the magnet. Describe and explain what happens to the second clip. **[2]**

**8.** A technician has three metal bars, labelled R, S and T. She brings each end of each bar, in turn, near the
N pole of a known bar magnet. The results are shown.

| Bar | End 1 | End 2 |
|---|---|---|
| R | Attracted | Repelled |
| S | Attracted | Attracted |
| T | No force | No force |

**(a)** Identify which bar is a permanent magnet. Explain how you know. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest what bar S could be made of, and explain why the test does not prove that S is not a magnet. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest a material for bar T. **[1]**

**9.** Describe how to use a plotting compass to plot the magnetic field pattern around a bar magnet. **[4]**

**10.** A student draws the magnetic field of a bar magnet. The teacher marks it wrong.

**(a)** The student's lines are evenly spaced all around the magnet. Explain why this is wrong. **[2]**
**(b)** Two of the lines cross each other. Explain why field lines cannot cross. **[1]**
**(c)** Give one other feature a correct diagram must show. **[1]**

**11.** A compass is carried to several different countries. In each, away from other magnets, its needle settles
pointing roughly north–south.

**(a)** Explain what this shows about the Earth. **[2]**
**(b)** The N pole of the needle points towards the Earth's geographic North Pole. Explain what type of magnetic
pole is near the geographic North Pole. **[2]**

**12.** A recycling plant uses a large magnet above a conveyor belt to separate steel cans from aluminium cans.
Explain how the magnet separates the cans. Your answer should refer to induced magnetism and magnetic
materials. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Repel; attract (both needed) [1]

**2.** Any two from: a permanent magnet produces its own field, an induced magnet only has a field while in another field [1]; a permanent magnet can attract or repel, an induced magnet only attracts [1]; an induced magnet loses most or all of its magnetism quickly when removed from the field, a permanent magnet keeps it [1]
*Examiner insight:* Each difference must give both sides; "an induced magnet is temporary" alone is half a comparison.

**3.** **Nickel** [1]

**4.** The region around a magnet where a force acts on another magnet or on a magnetic material [1]

**5.** From the north (seeking) pole to the south (seeking) pole [1]

**6.** The iron becomes an induced magnet in the field [1]; the end nearest the magnet becomes the opposite pole, and unlike poles attract [1]

**7. (a)** The first clip is in the magnet's field and becomes an induced magnet [1]; its field attracts the second clip, which becomes an induced magnet too [1]
**(b)** The second clip falls [1]; the first clip loses most or all of its induced magnetism quickly once it leaves the magnet's field, so it no longer attracts the second clip [1] **[4]**

**8. (a)** **R** [1]; end 2 is repelled, and only a permanent magnet can be repelled (induced magnetism always attracts) [1]
**(b)** Iron or steel (or cobalt or nickel) [1]; a magnet's S pole would also be attracted to the N pole, so attraction at both ends shows only that S is magnetic material; to prove it is not a magnet, both ends must fail to be repelled by both poles of the known magnet [1]
**(c)** A non-magnetic material such as copper, aluminium or brass [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b), "it was attracted, so it is not a magnet" scores zero; attraction cannot rule out a magnet.

**9.** Draw round the magnet on paper and place the compass near the N pole [1]; mark the point the needle's N end points to [1]; move the compass so the tail of the needle is on that mark and mark again, repeating until the S pole is reached [1]; join the marks with a smooth curve with an arrow from N to S, and repeat from other starting points [1]

**10. (a)** The field is strongest at the poles [1]; so the lines should be closest together at the poles and further apart away from the magnet [1]
**(b)** At any point the field has only one direction [1]
**(c)** Arrows pointing from N to S (or lines starting and finishing on the poles) [1] **[4]**

**11. (a)** The compass is a small magnet that lines up in a magnetic field [1]; since it lines up everywhere, the Earth has a magnetic field, which is evidence that its core is magnetic [1]
**(b)** Unlike poles attract [1]; so the magnetic pole near the geographic North Pole is a south (seeking) magnetic pole [1] **[4]**

**12.** Steel is a magnetic material and aluminium is not [1]; steel cans in the magnet's field become induced magnets [1]; induced magnetism always causes attraction, so the steel cans are lifted from the belt while aluminium cans pass underneath [1]; aluminium cans do not become induced magnets, so they are not attracted and stay on the belt to be collected separately [1]
*Examiner insight:* The induced-magnet step is the one most often skipped; naming steel as magnetic without it gives at most two marks.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using attraction as proof that something is a magnet.
- Writing comparisons with only one side.
- Field diagrams with S-to-N arrows, crossing lines or even spacing.
- Leaving out the step "the material becomes an induced magnet" before explaining attraction.
- Describing compass plotting without moving the compass tail to the previous mark.
- Calling the Earth's magnetic pole in the Arctic a north magnetic pole.
