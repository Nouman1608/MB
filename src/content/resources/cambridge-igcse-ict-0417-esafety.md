Section 8.2 of Cambridge IGCSE ICT 0417 has three parts. **Data protection**: "the principles of a typical data protection act and why data protection legislation is required". **Personal data**: its characteristics, with the syllabus's own examples ("personal name, address, date of birth, a photograph in school uniform, medical history"), and "the need for personal data to be kept confidential and protected to avoid inappropriate disclosure". **eSafety**: the need for it "when using the internet, email, social media, online gaming", and how to minimise the dangers of each.

The [safety and security study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-ict-safety-and-security-study-guide/) lists the principles and the safe practices in tables. This page explains the reasons behind them and shows how to apply them to scenarios. For recall see the [eSafety revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-ict-0417-esafety-revision-notes/), and practise with the [eSafety practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-ict-0417-esafety-practice/).

## Why data protection legislation is required

Schools, shops, banks, hospitals and websites store huge amounts of data about people. That data can be copied in seconds, combined with other data and sent anywhere in the world. Without legal rules, an organisation could:

- collect more data than it needs, or collect it without people knowing;
- use it for a purpose people did not agree to, such as selling a customer list to advertisers;
- keep inaccurate data that leads to wrong decisions, such as refusing someone a loan;
- keep data for ever;
- store it carelessly, so it is lost, stolen or seen by the wrong people.

Data protection legislation gives people (**data subjects**) rights over their data and places legal duties on the organisations that collect and hold it. Organisations that break the law can be fined, and people can complain or claim compensation.

## Principles of a typical data protection act

The syllabus says "a typical" act, because each country has its own law. The principles are similar across most of them. Learn them with an example, because questions often ask you to apply a principle to a scenario.

| Principle: data must be... | Example of following it |
|---|---|
| Processed fairly and lawfully | A gym tells new members what data it collects and why |
| Used only for the purpose it was collected for | A school uses pupils' contact details to contact parents, not to sell to a uniform company |
| Adequate, relevant and not excessive | An online shop does not ask for a customer's religion |
| Accurate and kept up to date | A doctor's surgery checks patients' addresses each year |
| Not kept longer than necessary | A recruitment firm deletes unsuccessful applicants' CVs after a set period |
| Processed in line with the data subject's rights | A customer can ask to see the data held about them |
| Kept secure | Records are password-protected and encrypted |
| Not transferred to another country without adequate protection | A company checks that an overseas cloud provider protects data to the same standard |

The principles are **duties on the organisation**, not rules for the individual. "Do not share your password" is good eSafety advice but is not a data protection principle.

## Personal data and sensitive data

**Personal data** is any data that relates to a living person who can be identified from it, either alone or combined with other data. The syllabus examples, and why each is personal:

- **Personal name**: identifies the person directly.
- **Address**: shows where the person lives and can be used to find them.
- **Date of birth**: combined with a name, it confirms identity and is often used in security questions.
- **A photograph in school uniform**: shows what the person looks like *and*, through the uniform, which school they attend and where they can be found during the day.
- **Medical history**: reveals private health information.

Some personal data is **sensitive**: data that could cause particular harm or discrimination if disclosed. Medical history is the syllabus example. Others usually treated as sensitive include ethnic origin, religious beliefs, political views and criminal records.

### Why personal data must be kept confidential

Inappropriate disclosure of personal data can lead to:

- **identity theft and fraud**: a criminal with someone's name, address and date of birth can apply for credit in their name;
- **physical danger**: a stranger who knows where a child lives or goes to school can find them;
- **discrimination**: an employer who learns of a medical condition may treat the person unfairly;
- **embarrassment and distress**, such as private information being shared online;
- **targeted scams**, using known details to make a fraudulent message look genuine.

## eSafety: why it is needed

eSafety means using technology in a way that protects yourself and others from harm. The internet, email, social media and online games all let people contact you, often without you knowing who they really are, and anything you share can be copied and spread beyond your control.

### The internet

- **Use only trusted websites recommended by teachers**: these are known to be safe and reliable, reducing the chance of meeting inappropriate content, malware or scams.
- **Use a search engine that only allows access to age-appropriate websites**: a child-safe search filter blocks results with unsuitable content.

### Email

- **Be aware of the danger of opening or replying to an email from an unknown person**: it may contain links to fake websites or attachments containing malware, and replying confirms your address is active.
- **Be aware of the risks of sending personal identifiable data or images by email**: once sent, an email can be forwarded to anyone, sent to the wrong address by mistake, or intercepted.

### Social media

- **Know how to block and report unwanted users**: this stops further contact from someone who is abusive or makes you uncomfortable, and alerts the platform.
- **Be aware of the dangers of meeting an online contact face to face**: a person may not be who they claim to be. If a meeting does happen, it should be with a trusted adult, in a public place.
- **Avoid distributing inappropriate images**: images can be copied and shared permanently, and some images are illegal to share.
- **Avoid using inappropriate language**: it can hurt others, be seen by employers or schools later, and may break the platform's rules or the law.
- **Respect the confidentiality of other people's personal data**: do not post other people's photos, addresses or private information without permission.

### Online gaming

- **Do not use your real name**: use a username that does not reveal your identity, age or location.
- **Do not give out personal or financial data**: other players may be strangers, and some try to obtain card details or account logins.

## Worked example: applying eSafety to a scenario

*Leila, aged 14, has a public social media profile. Her profile shows her full name, a photo of her in her school uniform, and a post saying "Home alone tonight!". She has also accepted a friend request from someone she has never met who says he is 15. Identify three risks and explain how she should reduce each.*

1. **The photo in school uniform** shows which school she attends, so a stranger could find her. She should remove it and set her profile to private, so only approved friends see her posts.
2. **The post saying she is home alone** tells everyone that she is alone and, with other details, where. She should not post her location or when she is alone.
3. **The unknown friend** may not be who he says he is. She should remove him, block and report him if he makes her uncomfortable, and never agree to meet him face to face.

Each answer names the risk, explains why it is a risk and gives a matching action.

## Common mistakes

- Giving eSafety rules as data protection principles, or the reverse.
- Saying medical history is "personal but not sensitive". It is both.
- Explaining why a photo in school uniform is personal without mentioning the school it reveals.
- Writing general advice ("be careful online") instead of the specific action for the context in the question.
- Forgetting the reason: "do not use your real name" needs "so other players cannot identify or find you".

## Exam technique

**State** a principle: use the wording "data must be...". **Explain** why legislation is needed: give a risk to the individual and how the law reduces it. **Describe** how to stay safe: match the context (internet, email, social media, gaming) and give the action plus its purpose.
