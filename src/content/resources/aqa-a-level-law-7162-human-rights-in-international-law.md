This guide covers **Human Rights in international law** in section 3.5 Human Rights of AQA A-level Law
(7162). The specification lists three headings: "The Second World War and its aftermath", "The United
Nations and the Universal Declaration of Human Rights 1948" and "The Council of Europe and the European
Convention on Human Rights 1953". It is a context topic: it explains why the Convention exists and how
it differs from other international instruments. For the whole option in outline, start with the
[Human Rights study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-human-rights-study-guide/).

## The Second World War and its aftermath

Before 1939, how a state treated its own citizens was largely regarded as a matter for that state. The
Second World War changed this view.

- **The scale of atrocity.** The Holocaust and other mass killings were carried out by states, often
  under their own laws. This showed that domestic legality was no guarantee of justice, and that rights
  needed protection above the level of the state.
- **War aims.** In 1941 President Franklin D. Roosevelt spoke of "four freedoms": freedom of speech and
  expression, freedom of worship, freedom from want and freedom from fear. These ideas later shaped the
  Universal Declaration.
- **The Nuremberg trials (1945 to 1946).** The International Military Tribunal tried leading Nazis for
  crimes including **crimes against humanity**. It established that individuals can be criminally
  responsible under international law, and that obeying orders or domestic law is not a complete
  defence.
- **A new international order.** States wanted institutions to keep the peace and to prevent a repeat
  of the abuses. Two followed: the United Nations at global level and the Council of Europe at regional
  level.

## The United Nations and the Universal Declaration of Human Rights 1948

### The United Nations

The **United Nations Charter** was signed in San Francisco in June 1945, and the UN came into existence
in October 1945. Among its purposes the Charter lists promoting and encouraging "respect for human
rights and for fundamental freedoms for all without distinction as to race, sex, language, or
religion". The Charter did not list the rights, so the UN set up a **Commission on Human Rights** to
draft them.

### The Universal Declaration

- **Drafting.** The Commission was chaired by **Eleanor Roosevelt**. Contributors included René Cassin
  of France, John Humphrey of Canada, who prepared an early draft, Peng Chun Chang of China and Charles
  Malik of Lebanon.
- **Adoption.** The UN General Assembly adopted the Declaration in Paris on **10 December 1948**, with 48
  votes in favour, none against and eight abstentions.
- **Content.** Its 30 Articles include **civil and political rights**, such as life, liberty, a fair
  trial, privacy, expression and assembly, and **economic and social rights**, such as social security,
  work, education and an adequate standard of living. The preamble recognises the "inherent dignity" of
  all members of the human family.
- **Legal status.** It is a **resolution of the General Assembly**, not a treaty, so it does not bind
  states as a treaty would. Its influence has nonetheless been great: many of its provisions are argued
  to reflect customary international law, and it is the model for later treaties and national
  constitutions.

### From declaration to binding treaties

The UN turned the Declaration into two binding treaties in **1966**: the **International Covenant on
Civil and Political Rights** and the **International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights**.
Together with the Declaration they are often called the **International Bill of Human Rights**. The
Covenants are monitored mainly by expert committees that review state reports. Their enforcement
mechanisms are much weaker than a court with binding judgments, which is the key contrast with the
European system.

## The Council of Europe and the European Convention on Human Rights 1953

### The Council of Europe

The **Council of Europe** was created by the **Treaty of London on 5 May 1949**. Its ten founding states
were Belgium, Denmark, France, Ireland, Italy, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Norway, Sweden and the United
Kingdom. Its seat is in **Strasbourg**. It aimed to promote democracy, the rule of law and human rights
among European states, partly as a safeguard against a return to dictatorship and partly in the context
of the emerging Cold War.

The Council of Europe is **not the European Union**. The European Union is a separate organisation with
its own Court of Justice in Luxembourg and its own Charter of Fundamental Rights. The United Kingdom
left the European Union in 2020 but remains a member of the Council of Europe and a party to the
Convention.

### The Convention

- **Drafting.** British lawyers played a leading part; **Sir David Maxwell Fyfe**, a Conservative MP who
  had been a prosecutor at Nuremberg, was closely involved.
- **Signature.** The Convention was signed in **Rome on 4 November 1950**.
- **Ratification.** The United Kingdom was the first state to ratify, in **March 1951**.
- **Entry into force.** It came into force on **3 September 1953**, once ten states had ratified, which
  is why the specification dates it 1953.
- **Content.** It protects civil and political rights only, closely modelled on the Declaration, and adds
  rights through later **protocols**.

### The original machinery and its development

| Stage | Development |
|---|---|
| 1954 | European Commission of Human Rights set up to filter applications |
| 1959 | European Court of Human Rights set up in Strasbourg |
| Original scheme | Individual petition and acceptance of the Court's jurisdiction were optional for each state |
| 1966 | The UK accepted the right of individual petition and the Court's jurisdiction |
| 1998 | Protocol 11 abolished the Commission and created a single, full-time Court; individual petition became compulsory |

The **Committee of Ministers**, made up of the foreign ministers of member states or their deputies,
supervises the execution of the Court's judgments. Membership has grown to include most European states;
**Russia was expelled in 2022** after its invasion of Ukraine and ceased to be a party to the Convention.

## Comparing the instruments

| Feature | UDHR 1948 | ICCPR and ICESCR 1966 | ECHR (in force 1953) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Body | UN General Assembly | UN | Council of Europe |
| Legal form | Resolution, not a treaty | Treaties | Treaty |
| Rights | Civil, political, economic and social | Civil and political; economic, social and cultural | Civil and political, plus protocols |
| Enforcement | None directly | Reporting to expert committees | Court with binding judgments, supervised by the Committee of Ministers |

## Annotated plan: "Explain how the Second World War led to the creation of the European Convention on Human Rights."

```
Point 1   Atrocities committed by states under their own laws showed that
          domestic law alone could not protect rights.
Point 2   Nuremberg (1945–46): individual responsibility for crimes
          against humanity under international law.
Point 3   UN Charter 1945 and UDHR 1948: universal standards, but the UDHR
          was not binding and had no enforcement machinery.
Point 4   Council of Europe 1949: regional organisation of like-minded
          democracies, able to agree a binding treaty.
Point 5   ECHR signed 1950, in force 1953: enforceable rights, with a
          Commission and Court; later strengthened by Protocol 11.
```

## Common mistakes

- Confusing the Council of Europe with the European Union, or the European Court of Human Rights with the
  Court of Justice of the European Union.
- Saying the Universal Declaration is a binding treaty.
- Dating the Convention 1950 without explaining 1953, or the reverse.
- Claiming individual petition was available against the UK from 1953; the UK accepted it in 1966.
- Forgetting that the UK remains a party to the Convention after leaving the European Union.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** questions: give a clear chronology with dates and say what each development added.
- **Discuss** questions: evaluate the effectiveness of international protection, contrasting the
  Declaration and Covenants with the enforceable Convention system.
- In problem answers, this context supports points about enforcement and the binding effect of
  Strasbourg judgments.
