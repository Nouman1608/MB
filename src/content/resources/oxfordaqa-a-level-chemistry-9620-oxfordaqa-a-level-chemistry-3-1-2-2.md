## What the specification asks for

- The Avogadro constant as the number of particles in a mole.
- The mole as applied to electrons, atoms, molecules, ions, formulas and equations.
- The concentration of a substance in solution, measured in mol dm⁻³.
- Calculations using the Avogadro constant; using mass of substance, Mr and amount in moles; and using concentration, volume and amount of substance in a solution.

You will not be expected to recall the value of the Avogadro constant; it is given when needed. These pages use L = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.

## The mole and the Avogadro constant

A **mole** is the amount of substance that contains 6.02 × 10²³ particles. That number is the **Avogadro constant**, L, the number of particles in a mole, with units of mol⁻¹. It is chosen so that one mole of carbon-12 atoms has a mass of 12 g.

The link to 3.1.2.1 is the key idea: because Ar and Mr compare masses with one-twelfth of a carbon-12 atom, **one mole of any substance has a mass in grams equal to its Mr**. One mole of water (Mr 18.0) has a mass of 18.0 g and contains 6.02 × 10²³ molecules.

### The mole applies to any named particle

Always say which particle you are counting. One mole of CaCl₂ contains:

- 1 mol of formula units
- 1 mol of Ca²⁺ ions and 2 mol of Cl⁻ ions, so 3 mol of ions in total
- 6.02 × 10²³ formula units, but 1.806 × 10²⁴ ions.

For electrons, add up the electrons in each particle. One NH₃ molecule has 7 + 3 = 10 electrons, so 0.500 mol of NH₃ contains 5.00 mol of electrons, or 3.01 × 10²⁴ electrons.

The mole also applies to equations: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O can be read as "2 mol of hydrogen molecules react with 1 mol of oxygen molecules to give 2 mol of water molecules". This is the basis of reacting-mass calculations in 3.1.2.5.

## The three core relationships

| Relationship | Equation | Units |
|---|---|---|
| Particles | N = n × L | n in mol, L in mol⁻¹ |
| Mass | n = m ÷ Mr | m in g, n in mol |
| Solutions | n = c × V | c in mol dm⁻³, V in dm³ |

To convert volumes: 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³, so V in dm³ = V in cm³ ÷ 1000.

### Worked example 1: from mass to particle count

**Question.** How many oxygen atoms are present in 1.64 g of calcium nitrate, Ca(NO₃)₂ (Mr 164.1)? (L = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹)

**Method.**

1. n(Ca(NO₃)₂) = 1.64 ÷ 164.1 = 9.99 × 10⁻³ mol
2. Each formula unit has 6 O atoms, so n(O) = 6 × 9.99 × 10⁻³ = 0.0600 mol
3. N(O) = 0.0600 × 6.02 × 10²³ = **3.61 × 10²² atoms**

The step most often missed is step 2: multiplying by the number of atoms of the element in one formula unit.

### Worked example 2: mass of a single particle

**Question.** Calculate the mass, in grams, of one atom of iron (Ar 55.8).

**Method.** One mole of iron atoms has a mass of 55.8 g and contains 6.02 × 10²³ atoms. Mass of one atom = 55.8 ÷ 6.02 × 10²³ = **9.27 × 10⁻²³ g**.

Turning this round lets you find Mr from the mass of one molecule: if one molecule has a mass of 7.31 × 10⁻²³ g, Mr = 7.31 × 10⁻²³ × 6.02 × 10²³ = 44.0.

## Concentration

Concentration in **mol dm⁻³** is the amount of solute in each cubic decimetre of solution. Concentration can also be given in **g dm⁻³**; convert between them using Mr:

- mass concentration (g dm⁻³) = c (mol dm⁻³) × Mr
- c (mol dm⁻³) = mass concentration (g dm⁻³) ÷ Mr

### Concentration of ions

A solution's concentration refers to the formula of the solute. In 0.100 mol dm⁻³ Na₂CO₃, [Na⁺] = 0.200 mol dm⁻³ and [CO₃²⁻] = 0.100 mol dm⁻³, because each formula unit releases two Na⁺ ions.

### Worked example 3: making a solution

**Question.** What mass of hydrated copper(II) sulfate, CuSO₄·5H₂O (Mr 249.6), is needed to make 250 cm³ of a 0.100 mol dm⁻³ solution of copper(II) sulfate?

**Method.**

1. n = c × V = 0.100 × (250 ÷ 1000) = 0.0250 mol
2. One mole of the hydrate supplies one mole of CuSO₄, so 0.0250 mol of hydrate is needed.
3. m = n × Mr = 0.0250 × 249.6 = **6.24 g**

Using the anhydrous Mr (159.6) would give 3.99 g and a solution that is far too dilute if the solid weighed is the hydrate.

### Dilution

Diluting a solution does not change the amount of solute, only the volume. So c₁V₁ = c₂V₂. Diluting 25.0 cm³ of 2.00 mol dm⁻³ acid to 400 cm³ gives c₂ = 2.00 × 25.0 ÷ 400 = 0.125 mol dm⁻³. The volumes can stay in cm³ here, because the same unit appears on both sides.

## Significant figures and standard form

- Give answers to the same number of significant figures as the least precise data, usually three.
- Carry unrounded values through a multi-step calculation and round only at the end.
- Write large and small numbers in standard form with a ×10 power, such as 6.02 × 10²³, not as a calculator display.

## Common mistakes

- Using cm³ instead of dm³ in n = c × V, giving answers 1000 times too large.
- Forgetting to multiply by the number of atoms or ions in one formula unit.
- Dividing the Avogadro constant by moles instead of multiplying.
- Using the anhydrous Mr when the solid is a hydrate, or the reverse.
- Treating the concentration of a salt as the concentration of every ion in it.
- Writing "moles" as a unit; the unit is mol and the quantity is "amount of substance".
- Rounding intermediate answers to one or two significant figures.

## Exam technique for OxfordAQA command words

- **Calculate**: write the equation you are using, substitute with units, and give the answer with units and sensible significant figures.
- **Show that**: give every step, including the final value to more figures than the target, so the examiner can see you did not work backwards.
- **Deduce**: use a ratio from the formula (such as 2 Cl⁻ per MgCl₂) and state it.
- **Explain**: for concentration of ions, refer to how many ions each formula unit releases.
