## The one question to ask

Is the factor about the **situation** (social) or the **person** (dispositional)? Every behaviour
in this topic has both.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Conformity | Changing behaviour or belief to fit in with a majority |
| Obedience | Following a direct order from an authority figure |
| Confederate | An actor who follows the researcher's script |
| Autonomous state | Acting on your own conscience; feeling responsible |
| Agentic state | Acting as an agent of authority; responsibility passed upward |
| Authoritarian Personality | Rigid, status-conscious personality from harsh upbringing (Adorno); measured by the F-scale |
| Diffusion of responsibility | Each bystander feels less responsible when others are present |
| Cost-reward | Bystanders weigh the cost of helping against the cost of not helping |
| Social loafing | Putting in less individual effort when working in a group |
| Deindividuation | Loss of personal identity and self-restraint in an anonymous crowd |

## Studies at a glance

**Asch (1951) -- conformity.** Male US students judged line lengths after confederates gave the
same wrong answer on critical trials. About a third of critical-trial answers conformed; about
three-quarters conformed at least once; about a quarter never did. Variations: conformity rose with
group size up to about three confederates, fell when answers were private, rose when the lines were
more similar.
*Evaluation:* controlled and standardised; but artificial task, narrow 1950s sample, deception.

**Dispositional factors in conformity:** **personality**, for example people with a strong sense that they control their own behaviour, or with high self-confidence, are more likely to resist the majority; **expertise**, because someone who knows a lot about the task trusts their own judgement more than the group's.

**Milgram (1963) -- obedience.** 40 men, "teacher" role, fake shocks in 15 V steps to 450 V, verbal
prods from an experimenter in a lab coat. All went to 300 V; 65% went to 450 V. Obedience fell in a
run-down office (authority), with teacher and learner in the same room or touching (proximity),
and with orders given by telephone (proximity of authority).
*Evaluation:* controlled variations show which factors matter; but deception and distress raise
ethical issues, and the lab setting may lack ecological validity.

**Agency theory, the social explanation:** people move between an **autonomous state** (acting on their own conscience) and an **agentic state** (acting on behalf of an authority, so responsibility feels passed upward). Milgram argued that in a hierarchy people shift into the agentic state when given orders by someone they see as a legitimate authority. The social factors he identified:

- **Agency**: participants who felt the experimenter was responsible were more willing to continue.
- **Authority**: obedience was higher when the setting and the experimenter's uniform signalled legitimate authority.
- **Proximity**: obedience fell when the victim was closer, or when the authority figure was further away.
- **Culture**: replications in different countries have produced different levels of obedience, which suggests that societies differ in how much they value deference to authority.

**Adorno (1950) -- dispositional obedience.** Harsh, strict parenting leads to an authoritarian
personality: black-and-white thinking, deference to higher status, hostility to lower status.
F-scale questionnaire.
*Evaluation:* obedient Milgram-type participants tended to score higher on the F-scale; but it
cannot explain obedience across a whole society.
A further problem is that the F-scale was worded so that agreeing always scored as authoritarian, which may have inflated scores for people who tend to agree with statements in general. Social and dispositional explanations work best together: situations create pressure, and personality affects who gives in to it.

**Piliavin (1969) -- bystanders.** Field experiment on New York subway trains: a confederate
collapsed, appearing ill (cane) or drunk. Cane victim helped on the great majority of trials, drunk
victim on about half; most first helpers were men; no diffusion of responsibility found.
*Evaluation:* high ecological validity; but no consent or debrief, and little control of
passengers.

**Factors in bystander behaviour:**

- **Presence of others** (social): in many settings more bystanders means each feels less responsible, but on a crowded subway carriage with no escape Piliavin found help was still common.
- **Cost of helping** (social): helping a drunk victim seemed more costly (risk of harm, disgust, embarrassment), so help was slower and less frequent.
- **Similarity to victim** (dispositional): people are more willing to help those they see as like themselves.
- **Expertise** (dispositional): people with relevant skills, such as first-aid training, are more confident they can help and less worried about making things worse.

## Crowd behaviour in one line each

- **Social loafing:** less effort per person in groups (Latané, Williams and Harkins: clapping and
  shouting).
- **Deindividuation:** anonymity reduces restraint (Diener's Halloween study: anonymous children in
  groups took more sweets).
- **Culture:** loafing appears less common in collectivist cultures.
- **Personality and morality:** strong personal standards help people resist the crowd or act
  prosocially.

**Prosocial and antisocial crowds:** crowds are not always harmful. Crowds can be **prosocial**, as when people at a festival or a sports event help someone who has collapsed, or a crowd raises money for charity. They become **antisocial** when anonymity and arousal weaken self-restraint, as in some riots or vandalism. In Diener's study, children who were anonymous **and** in a group took the most extra sweets, which shows deindividuation is strongest when both conditions are present.

## Applying it to a scenario

*Scenario: in a dark, crowded stadium, fans wearing identical masks start throwing litter onto the pitch. Kemal, who has strong views about respecting others, keeps his mask off and does not join in.*

- **Social factor**: **deindividuation**: masks and darkness make fans anonymous, so normal self-restraint weakens.
- **Dispositional factor**: Kemal's **morality**: strong personal standards help him resist the crowd.
- **Link**: name the factor, explain how it works, and point to "identical masks" or "strong views" in the stem.

## Model evaluation paragraph

*Topic: evaluate Milgram's study of obedience.*

> One strength of Milgram's study is that it was highly controlled: every participant heard the same recorded protests and the same verbal prods, so the variations he ran, such as moving to a run-down office, could show which situational factors changed obedience. However, participants were deceived about the shocks and many showed signs of serious distress, which breaks the ethical guideline of protection from harm, although they were debriefed afterwards. The task was also unusual, since people are rarely asked to harm a stranger in a laboratory, so it may lack ecological validity. Overall, the study gives strong evidence that situations shape obedience, but its ethics and artificiality limit how far it can be used.

## Exam traps

- Asch is conformity; Milgram is obedience. Never swap them.
- Adorno is dispositional, not social.
- Piliavin did **not** find diffusion of responsibility.
- In application questions, link every factor to a detail in the scenario.
- Agency theory is a **social** explanation of obedience; the Authoritarian Personality is **dispositional**.
- Social loafing is reduced **effort** in a group, not anti-social behaviour.
- Deindividuation is about **anonymity**, not simply being in a large group.
- Asch's task was unambiguous; that is why conformity in it is striking. Making the lines more similar increased conformity.

## Self-test

1. Name the three social factors affecting conformity.
2. What is the agentic state?
3. Give one way Milgram showed proximity affects obedience.
4. Which questionnaire did Adorno use?
5. Why did the drunk victim in Piliavin's study receive less help?
6. Define deindividuation.
7. Give one ethical criticism of Piliavin's study.
8. Name the two dispositional factors affecting conformity.
9. What happened to obedience when Milgram moved the study to a run-down office?
10. Give one criticism of the F-scale.
11. What is social loafing, and which study showed it?
12. Give one dispositional factor affecting bystander behaviour and explain it.

**Answers:** 1. Group size, anonymity, task difficulty. 2. A state in which a person sees
themselves as acting for an authority figure and no longer feels responsible for their actions.
3. Any one: obedience fell when the teacher was in the same room as the learner, when the teacher
had to touch the learner, or when orders came by telephone. 4. The F-scale. 5. Helping seemed more
costly (risk, disgust, embarrassment), so by the cost-reward idea people helped less. 6. The loss
of personal identity and self-restraint that can happen in an anonymous crowd. 7. Passengers did
not give consent and were not debriefed. 8. Personality and expertise. 9. It fell, because the setting carried less authority. 10. Agreeing with every item scored as authoritarian, so people who tend to agree may have scored high regardless of personality. 11. Putting in less individual effort in a group; Latané, Williams and Harkins found people clapped and shouted less loudly per person in groups. 12. Expertise: people with skills such as first aid feel more able to help; or similarity: people help those they see as like themselves.
