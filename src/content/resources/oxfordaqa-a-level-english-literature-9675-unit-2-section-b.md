**Section B of Unit 2 Place in Literary Texts** in OxfordAQA International AS and A-level English
Literature (9675) is one essay question, worth 25 marks, on your poetry set text: a selection of
poems by one poet. The exam is open book. The
[Place in Literary Texts unit overview](/resources/a-level-oxfordaqa-english-literature-place-in-literary-texts/)
introduces the unit; this guide is about how poems create place and how to write about a
selection rather than a single poem.

## The four selections

Version 5.3 lists four poets, each with a named selection:

- **William Wordsworth** (8 poems): including "Lines composed a few miles above Tintern Abbey",
  "Nutting", "Michael", "Composed Upon Westminster Bridge", "Yarrow Unvisited" and the stanzas on
  Peele Castle.
- **Robert Frost** (12 poems): including "Mending Wall", "After Apple-Picking", "The Wood-Pile",
  "Birches", "'Out, Out –'", "Stopping by Woods on a Snowy Evening", "A Brook in the City" and
  "Desert Places".
- **Thomas Hardy** (15 poems): including "Neutral Tones", "The Darkling Thrush", "Channel Firing",
  "The Convergence of the Twain", "The Going", "After a Journey", "Beeny Cliff", "At Castle
  Boterel", "Places" and "The Oxen".
- **Seamus Heaney** (15 poems): including "Mid-Term Break", "Personal Helicon", "Requiem for the
  Croppies", "Bogland", "Anahorish", "The Tollund Man", "North", "Punishment", "Act of Union" and
  "The Toome Road".

Learn the full list for your poet from the specification; set texts can change between versions,
and a revised 9675 for teaching from 2027 has a refreshed list.

## How poems make place

A novel can describe a place over pages; a poem does it in a few lines, so every choice is
concentrated. Five methods do most of the work.

**Naming.** Place-names anchor a poem in a real location and can carry history. Heaney's
"Anahorish" builds a whole poem from an Irish place-name, so the sound of the word becomes a way of
claiming a landscape and a language. Hardy's titles ("Beeny Cliff", "At Castle Boterel") fix grief
to particular Cornish places.

**The speaker's position.** Where does the speaker stand, and when? Wordsworth's speaker in "Tintern
Abbey" stands above the Wye after five years away, so the place is seen through memory; "Composed
Upon Westminster Bridge" fixes the speaker at one point, at dawn, looking at a city made still.

**Time layered on place.** Hardy's poems of 1912-13, written after the death of his first wife,
Emma, return to the places where they courted, so the landscape holds two times at once. A strong
essay notices verb tense and shifts between past and present.

**Nature and its despoliation.** The specification lists the natural world, "its beauty and possibly
its despoliation". Frost's "A Brook in the City" imagines a brook buried under city building;
Hardy's "Channel Firing" sets naval gunnery against a quiet churchyard.

**Form.** Sonnets, blank verse, ballad-like quatrains and free verse each shape how a place is
experienced. A tightly rhymed form can contain a wild landscape; a loose form can mirror a mind
moving through it. Line breaks and enjambment can trace a path or a view.

## Place as more than landscape

The specification's angles show that place in poetry is often social and political:

- **Home and homeland**: the farm in "Michael" and what is lost when Luke leaves for the city;
  Heaney's rural Derry childhood.
- **Place as political space**: Heaney's "The Toome Road", where armoured vehicles pass through his
  home district, and "Act of Union", which uses a relationship as an image of Britain and Ireland.
- **Relationships**: the neighbours and the wall in "Mending Wall"; Hardy's places of courtship.
- **History in the ground**: Heaney's bog poems, in which preserved bodies link Iron Age Jutland to
  violence in Northern Ireland; "Requiem for the Croppies", on the 1798 rebellion.

## Writing about a selection

Because you have studied a body of poems, the strongest essays usually range across more than one.
Read the question carefully for any instruction about how many poems to discuss. A sensible
default is to analyse two poems in depth and refer more briefly to a third, choosing poems that
show different aspects of the question rather than repeating one point.

Make your comparisons purposeful: link poems by method (both use place-names) or by idea (both
present home as lost), then show the difference within the similarity.

## Worked example on an original poem

The poem below is **original**, written for this guide. It is not from any set selection.

> *The Ford*
>
> We crossed here, the summer you were ten,
> your boots tied round your neck, the water loud.
> Now there's a bridge. I drive across it, then
> pull in and walk back down. The river's proud
> and narrow, quieter than then. A plaque
> explains the bridge. Nobody explains the ford.
> I stand where the stones were. I don't go back.

**Model reading:** The poem sets two crossings of one river against each other: the shared, noisy
ford of childhood and the solitary drive across a bridge. Place carries time, since the river is
"quieter than then" and the speaker must "walk back down" from the new road to reach the old one.
The rhyme scheme holds firm until the end, where "ford" fails to find a rhyme, so the form itself
marks the gap left by what has gone. The contrast between the official "plaque" and the unexplained
ford sets public history against private memory: the place has been rewritten, and only the
speaker can read the older version. The final monosyllables, "I don't go back", turn a literal
statement into a refusal: the past remains in the place but cannot be re-entered.

Use the same moves on your poet: position and time, method and form, and what the place finally
means.

## A plan using a set selection

**Question shape (original):** "In the poems, places matter because of the people who are absent
from them." Explore this view of Hardy's poems.

- **Argument:** largely true of the poems of 1912-13, less true where Hardy's places are shaped by
  history, war or chance.
- **Poem 1, "At Castle Boterel":** the speaker revisits a Cornish road where he and Emma once
  walked; the landscape is valued because it once held them both, and the closing stanzas see the
  phantom figure shrinking as he drives away. Method: past and present tense held together; the
  enduring rocks set against fleeting human time.
- **Poem 2, "Beeny Cliff":** the sea and cliff are vividly present, yet the final stanza turns to
  her absence; the landscape survives the person.
- **Counter-poem, "Channel Firing":** here the place, a churchyard by the sea, matters because of
  the dead who are present and the guns of 1914 that wake them; absence is not the point.
- **Context:** Emma's death in 1912 and Hardy's journey back to Cornwall; the approaching war in
  "Channel Firing".
- **Conclusion:** Hardy's places matter most when they outlast people, whether the absent beloved
  or the dead in the ground.

The plan names poems precisely, gives each a distinct role, and includes a poem that tests the
view.

## Common mistakes

- **One poem only** when the selection invites range.
- **Paraphrase** in place of analysis of language, sound and form.
- **Background biography** with no link to the words of the poem.
- **Place as scenery**: missing the political, social and personal meanings.
- **Comparison as a list**: two poem summaries placed side by side.

## Exam technique

As with Section A, questions may ask you to explore the significance of an aspect of place or to
discuss a view. Allow about an hour. Use the open book to select short, exact quotations; do not
copy whole stanzas.
