This guide goes deep on sub-topic **1(c) Forces, movement, shape and
momentum**, statements 1.11 to 1.33 of the Pearson Edexcel International
GCSE in Physics (4PH1), Issue 4. Statements 1.11 to 1.24 are core content
for both papers. Statements 1.25P to 1.33P (momentum, Newton's third law
and moments) are Physics-only and are assessed on Paper 2P. For Topic 1 as
a whole, see the
[Forces and Motion study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-physics-forces-and-motion/).

## What the specification asks

- **Forces and their effects (1.11–1.16):** effects of forces (changes in
  speed, shape or direction); types of force such as gravitational or
  electrostatic; vectors and scalars; force as a vector; resultant of
  forces along a line; friction opposes motion.
- **Force and acceleration (1.17–1.18):** F = m × a and W = m × g, both
  "know and use".
- **Stopping and falling (1.19–1.21):** stopping distance = thinking
  distance + braking distance and the factors affecting it; forces on
  falling objects and terminal velocity.
- **Shape (1.22–1.24):** the practical on extension of helical springs,
  metal wires and rubber bands; Hooke's law in the initial linear region;
  elastic behaviour.
- **Momentum (1.25P–1.28P):** p = m × v; safety features; conservation of
  momentum; F = (mv − mu) ÷ t.
- **Newton's third law and moments (1.29P–1.33P):** third-law pairs;
  moment = force × perpendicular distance; centre of gravity; principle of
  moments; upward forces on a beam supported at its ends.

## Vectors, scalars and resultant force

A **scalar** has size (magnitude) only: mass, distance, speed, time,
energy. A **vector** has size and direction: force, velocity,
displacement, acceleration, momentum. Because force is a vector, two
forces only add simply when they act along the same line.

For forces along a line, choose one direction as positive. Forces in that
direction are added; forces in the opposite direction are subtracted.

**Worked example 1.** A boat of mass 400 kg is pushed forward by an engine
force of 1500 N. Water resistance is 900 N and wind exerts 200 N against
the motion.

```
resultant force = 1500 − 900 − 200 = 400 N forwards
a = F / m = 400 / 400 = 1.0 m/s²
```

If the resultant force is zero, the forces are **balanced**: the object
stays at rest or keeps moving at constant velocity. Only an **unbalanced**
(resultant) force produces acceleration, which is why 1.17 says
"unbalanced force" in F = m × a.

**Friction** always acts in the direction opposing motion (or attempted
motion) between surfaces. Air resistance and water resistance (drag) are
types of friction that increase with speed.

## Weight

Weight is the gravitational force on a mass: W = m × g, with g = 10 N/kg
on Earth. Weight acts vertically downwards through the object's centre of
gravity. On the Moon, g is smaller, so the same mass has a smaller weight,
but its mass is unchanged.

## Stopping distance (1.19–1.20)

```
stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance
```

**Thinking distance** is the distance travelled during the driver's
reaction time, at constant speed. It increases with speed and with
reaction time, which is lengthened by tiredness, alcohol, some drugs and
distractions such as a mobile phone.

**Braking distance** is the distance travelled while the brakes act. It
increases with speed, with the mass of the vehicle (a larger mass needs a
larger force for the same deceleration), and with poor road conditions
(wet, icy or loose surfaces reduce friction between tyres and road). Worn
tyres and worn brakes also increase it.

For a given speed, thinking distance = speed × reaction time. A driver
with a reaction time of 0.70 s at 20 m/s has a thinking distance of
20 × 0.70 = 14 m. At 40 m/s it doubles to 28 m.

## Falling objects and terminal velocity (1.21)

An object falling through air has two forces on it: weight downwards and
air resistance upwards.

1. At first, speed is low, so air resistance is small; the resultant force
   is downwards and the object accelerates.
2. As speed increases, air resistance increases, so the resultant force
   and the acceleration decrease.
3. Eventually air resistance equals weight; the resultant force is zero,
   so the acceleration is zero and the object falls at a constant
   **terminal velocity**.

Opening a parachute greatly increases the surface area and so the air
resistance. Air resistance becomes larger than weight, the resultant force
is upwards, and the skydiver decelerates until the forces balance again at
a lower terminal velocity.

## Forces and shape (1.22–1.24)

**The practical.** Clamp a helical spring (or rubber band) vertically next
to a metre rule. Record the length with no load, then add masses in equal
steps (for example 100 g, giving 1.0 N of weight each), recording the new
length each time. Extension = new length − original length. Plot force on
one axis against extension on the other.

- Read the rule at eye level, using a pointer fixed to the bottom of the
  spring, to avoid parallax error.
- Remove the masses at the end to check whether the spring returns to its
  original length.
- A metal wire extends very little, so it must be long (a few metres) and
  the extension measured with a scale fixed close to a marker on the wire.
- Wear eye protection: a stretched spring or wire can snap.

**Results.** For a spring and a metal wire, the graph starts as a straight
line through the origin: extension is directly proportional to force.
This initial linear region is associated with **Hooke's law**. Beyond the
limit of proportionality the graph curves. A rubber band gives a curved
graph for most of its range, so it does not obey Hooke's law, even though
it is elastic.

**Elastic behaviour** is the ability of a material to recover its original
shape after the forces causing deformation are removed. A spring loaded
too far does not return to its original length: it has been permanently
deformed.

## Momentum (1.25P–1.28P)

momentum = mass × velocity (p = m × v), unit kg m/s. Momentum is a vector:
choose a positive direction and give objects moving the other way a
negative velocity.

**Conservation of momentum.** When objects interact (collide or push
apart) with no external force, total momentum before = total momentum
after.

**Worked example 2: an explosion.** A 2.0 kg trolley and a 3.0 kg trolley
are at rest, touching. A spring between them is released and the 3.0 kg
trolley moves off to the right at 0.40 m/s.

```
momentum before = 0
momentum after  = (3.0 × 0.40) + (2.0 × v) = 0
1.2 + 2.0v = 0
v = −0.60 m/s, so 0.60 m/s to the left
```

The lighter trolley moves faster, and the two momenta are equal and
opposite.

**Force and change in momentum.** F = (mv − mu) ÷ t. A given change in
momentum produced over a longer time needs a smaller force. **Safety
features** use this: crumple zones, airbags, seat belts, cycle helmets
and crash mats all increase the time over which a person's momentum
changes to zero, which reduces the force on the person.

**Worked example 3.** A 70 kg passenger moving at 15 m/s is brought to
rest by a seat belt and airbag in 0.50 s.

```
change in momentum = 70 × 0 − 70 × 15 = −1050 kg m/s
F = −1050 / 0.50 = −2100 N, so 2100 N against the motion
```

Without them, stopping in about 0.05 s against the dashboard would need a
force ten times larger.

## Newton's third law (1.29P)

If body A exerts a force on body B, body B exerts a force on body A that
is equal in size, opposite in direction and of the same type. The two
forces act on different bodies, so they never cancel. In worked example 2,
the spring pushes each trolley with equal and opposite forces for the same
time, which is why the momenta are equal and opposite.

## Moments (1.30P–1.33P)

moment = force × perpendicular distance from the pivot, unit Nm. The
**weight** of a body acts through its **centre of gravity**. For a body in
equilibrium, the **principle of moments** applies: sum of clockwise
moments = sum of anticlockwise moments about any pivot.

**Worked example 4: a beam on two supports.** A light beam 2.0 m long rests
on supports at each end, A and B. A 600 N load is placed 0.50 m from A.

```
moments about A:  R_B × 2.0 = 600 × 0.50
                  R_B = 150 N
upward forces balance the load: R_A + R_B = 600
                  R_A = 450 N
```

The support nearer the load carries the larger force. As the load moves
towards B, R_B increases and R_A decreases; with the load at the centre
each support carries 300 N. Taking moments about one support removes that
support's unknown force from the equation, which is why you should always
choose a support as the pivot.

## Common mistakes

- Using total force instead of resultant force in F = m × a.
- Saying a parachute makes the skydiver "go up". The velocity is still
  downwards; it decreases.
- Saying mass affects thinking distance. Mass affects braking distance
  only.
- Forgetting the negative sign for a velocity in the opposite direction in
  momentum calculations.
- Naming weight and the normal contact force as a Newton's third law pair.
  They act on the same body and are different types of force.
- Using the length along a slanted lever instead of the perpendicular
  distance.

## Exam technique with command words

- **Explain** terminal velocity or safety features: give a chain of linked
  steps, each with a reason ("so", "because").
- **Describe** the practical: give apparatus, what is measured, how, and
  how accuracy is improved.
- **Calculate**: equation, substitution, answer with unit; for momentum
  state the direction.
- **Suggest** factors in a new context: apply the stopping-distance
  factors to the situation described.
