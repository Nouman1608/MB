## Section A

**1.** Name the four aspects of context that Section A credits, and give **one** linguistic feature that could signal each. **[8]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by a "constructed, idealised reader", using this sentence as an example: *"You've worked hard all year, so why not treat yourself to the holiday you deserve?"* **[4]**

**3.** Explain the difference between intertextuality and interdiscursivity, with an example of each. **[4]**

**4.** Give **three** features that make a text-message conversation a hybrid of spoken and written modes. **[3]**

**5.** Explain why a short spoken turn such as *"Okay everyone, phones away, and well done on Friday's match"* can be described as multi-purpose. **[3]**

## Section B

Read the two texts below (original, written for this page). Both concern **cycling to school**.

> **Text A** (leaflet from a city council, sent to parents)
>
> **Safer Routes to School**
> Cycling to school is good for your child's health and helps reduce traffic around school gates. Our new protected
> cycle lanes now connect all secondary schools in the district. Before your child sets off:
> - check brakes and tyres weekly
> - make sure a helmet is worn and fitted correctly
> - plan the route together
> Find a route map at our website or call our helpline.

**Text B** (transcript of a local radio phone-in; CALLER is a 15-year-old student)

```
PRESENTER: Jade on line two (.) you cycle in every day
CALLER:    yeah every day (.) it's actually (.) it's actually brilliant now with the
           new lanes (.) like before my mum wouldn't let me
PRESENTER: what changed her mind
CALLER:    honestly (.) she rode it with me one Saturday (.) and she was like (.) okay
           fine (.) you win
PRESENTER: [laughs] a convert
```

**6.** Compare how Text A and Text B address their audiences. **[6]**

**7.** Analyse how the purposes of the two texts differ, and how each text's language reflects its purpose or purposes. **[6]**

**8.** Analyse **two** genre features of Text A and **two** of Text B, explaining what each does. **[8]**

**9.** Compare the modes of the two texts, using the language levels to support your answer. **[8]**

**10.** Text B includes the caller's report of her mother's words: *"she was like (.) okay fine (.) you win"*. Analyse the pragmatic and discourse function of this reported speech. **[4]**

**11.** Write the opening paragraph of a full Section A response comparing the two texts. It should give an overview of audience, purpose, genre and mode for both texts. **[6]**

**12.** Explain how a candidate could use their analysis of these texts to prepare for a Section B directed writing task. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** One mark for each aspect named and one for a valid signal. **Audience** [1]: e.g. direct address "you", glossed technical terms, forms of address [1]. **Purpose** [1]: e.g. imperatives for persuasion or instruction, statistics in declaratives for information [1]. **Genre** [1]: e.g. headline, bullet points, hyperlinks, greeting and sign-off [1]. **Mode** [1]: e.g. fillers and repairs for speech, layout and punctuation for writing, emoji in hybrids [1].

**2.** A constructed reader is **the reader a text builds and assumes** through its language, rather than simply an actual reader [1]. The sentence **presupposes** that the reader "worked hard all year" [1] and **deserves** a reward, constructing a hard-working, slightly weary adult [1]; the **rhetorical question "why not"** assumes the reader is already inclined to agree, so refusing feels unreasonable [1].

**3.** **Intertextuality** is a text's reference to or echo of another text [1], e.g. an advert headline that parodies a well-known film line [1]. **Interdiscursivity** is the mixing of different discourses or genre conventions within one text [1], e.g. a university brochure using the language of luxury marketing [1].

**4.** Any three: **written** but with **rapid, interactive turn-taking** like conversation [1]; **informal, speech-like lexis and ellipsis** ("on my way", "u ok?") [1]; **emoji or punctuation** standing in for facial expression and intonation [1]. Alternatively, little editing and frequent self-repair in follow-up messages.

**5.** The turn **takes control of the floor** with "Okay everyone" [1], **instructs** through the imperative "phones away" [1], and **builds relationships** by praising the group, "well done on Friday's match" [1].

**6.** Text A addresses **parents as responsible adults**: "your child" positions the reader as decision-maker [1], and **imperatives** ("check", "make sure", "plan") assign them duties [1]; the impersonal institutional voice "Our new protected cycle lanes" keeps an authoritative tenor [1]. Text B addresses a **general radio audience indirectly**, through the presenter's interaction with a caller [1]; the presenter's **informal, friendly address** ("Jade on line two") and the caller's peer-like voice create an approachable tenor [1]. Comparison: A speaks *to* parents with authority; B lets listeners overhear a teenager's experience, which may persuade parents more personally [1].

**7.** Text A is **informative** ("now connect all secondary schools") in order to **persuade** parents to let children cycle [1] [1], with **instructive** imperatives for safety [1]. Text B's primary purpose is **entertainment and engagement of listeners** through a personal story [1], but it also **informs** about the lanes and implicitly **persuades** through the mother's conversion [1]; the presenter's "a convert" frames the story as a satisfying narrative [1].

**8.** Text A, any two, each two marks: **heading "Safer Routes to School"** identifies the leaflet's topic and reassures [1] [1]; **bullet-pointed imperatives** let busy readers scan and act [1] [1]. Alternatively, contact details close the leaflet with a route to action. Text B, any two, each two marks: **presenter's introduction of the caller** ("Jade on line two") manages the broadcast and hands over the floor [1] [1]; **presenter's evaluative closing comment** "a convert" sums up for listeners and moves the programme on [1] [1]. Alternatively, the question-and-answer adjacency pairs structure the phone-in.

**9.** Text A is **planned written mode**: complete sentences, a heading and **graphological** bullet points [1] [1]. Text B is **spontaneous speech** within a semi-planned broadcast: **repetition and repair** ("it's actually (.) it's actually") [1], **micro-pauses** [1], the **discourse marker "like"** and **"honestly"** [1], and **adjacency pairs** of question and answer controlled by the presenter [1]. Lexically, A's "protected cycle lanes" is precise and formal; B's "brilliant" is evaluative and informal [1]. Comparison: A's mode supports clarity and reference; B's creates immediacy and authenticity [1].

**10.** The quotative **"was like"** introduces reported speech in an informal, performed way [1], letting the caller **dramatise** her mother's surrender for comic effect [1]. Pragmatically, "okay fine (.) you win" **implies a contest** the caller has won, presenting the mother's change of mind as reluctant [1]; in discourse terms it forms the **climax of a short narrative**, which the presenter's laughter and "a convert" ratify [1].

**11.** Credit: **the common theme stated** [1]; **audience of each text identified precisely** (parents; radio listeners via a teenage caller) [1]; **purposes of each, including mixed purposes** [1]; **genre of each named** [1]; **mode of each, including the semi-planned nature of a phone-in** [1]; **a comparative line of argument** that the paragraph sets up, e.g. authority versus personal testimony [1].

**12.** The candidate can **note content** that could be reused, such as the lanes, the safety advice and the mother's story [1], and **note the language that suits each context**, which helps them adapt register for a new audience [1]. They can **identify which material suits each Section B option** [1], and avoid copying by planning how to **transform** rather than lift phrases [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Analysing one text and then the other with no comparison.
- Missing the mixed purposes in apparently simple texts.
- Treating Text B's repairs and fillers as errors.
- Calling the radio phone-in "spoken" without noting its semi-planned broadcast structure.
- Naming genre features without explaining their function.
