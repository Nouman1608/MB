## The pattern: electrophilic addition

The pi bond of an alkene is rich in electrons. An **electrophile** (an electron-pair acceptor) is attracted to it. The pi bond breaks, a carbocation forms, and a nucleophile then bonds to the positive carbon. The result is a saturated molecule.

Three arrows, in order:

1. Arrow from the C=C bond to the electrophile (the δ+ end of the reagent).
2. Arrow from the bond in the reagent to the atom that leaves, as an ion.
3. Arrow from a lone pair on the negative ion to the carbocation.

## Hydrogen bromide

Hydrogen bromide is a polar molecule, Hδ⁺–Brδ⁻. The hydrogen is the electrophile.

For ethene:

```
CH₂=CH₂ + HBr → CH₃CH₂Br

Step 1: arrow from C=C to the H of H–Br;
        arrow from the H–Br bond to Br
        → CH₃–CH₂⁺ (carbocation) + Br⁻
Step 2: arrow from a lone pair on Br⁻ to the C⁺
        → CH₃CH₂Br
```

Conditions: room temperature, with gaseous HBr or concentrated aqueous solution.

## Concentrated sulfuric acid

Sulfuric acid is also polar, with the H of an O–H bond as the electrophile: H–O–SO₂–OH.

```
CH₂=CH₂ + H₂SO₄ → CH₃CH₂OSO₂OH      (ethyl hydrogensulfate)
```

The mechanism is the same as for HBr: arrow from C=C to H, the O–H bond breaks to give HSO₄⁻, and a lone pair on an oxygen of HSO₄⁻ attacks the carbocation. Conditions: concentrated sulfuric acid, cold.

The product reacts with water on warming to give an alcohol and regenerate the acid:

```
CH₃CH₂OSO₂OH + H₂O → CH₃CH₂OH + H₂SO₄
```

This two-stage route turns an alkene into an alcohol.

## Bromine

Bromine is a non-polar molecule, but when it approaches the electron-rich double bond, the pi electrons repel the electrons in the Br–Br bond. This **induces a dipole**: the near bromine atom becomes δ+ and the far atom δ−.

```
CH₂=CH₂ + Br₂ → CH₂BrCH₂Br      (1,2-dibromoethane)

Step 1: arrow from C=C to the nearer Br (δ+);
        arrow from the Br–Br bond to the farther Br
        → CH₂Br–CH₂⁺ + Br⁻
Step 2: arrow from lone pair on Br⁻ to C⁺
        → CH₂BrCH₂Br
```

Conditions: room temperature. The reaction needs no catalyst and no light.

### The bromine water test

Bromine water is orange-brown. Shaken with an alkene at room temperature, it becomes **colourless**. An alkane does not decolourise it in the dark. The colour change is the positive test for unsaturation. Write "orange to colourless", not "clear".

## Unsymmetrical alkenes: major and minor products

An alkene is **symmetrical** if the groups on each carbon of the double bond are identical (ethene, but-2-ene). An unsymmetrical alkene (propene, but-1-ene) can give two different products with HBr or H₂SO₄.

Take propene, CH₃CH=CH₂, with HBr. The hydrogen can add to either carbon of the double bond:

| H adds to | Carbocation formed | Type | Product after Br⁻ attack |
|---|---|---|---|
| C1 (the CH₂) | CH₃–CH⁺–CH₃ | Secondary | 2-bromopropane |
| C2 (the CH) | CH₃CH₂–CH₂⁺ | Primary | 1-bromopropane |

### Why the secondary carbocation wins

Alkyl groups push electron density towards the positive carbon (the positive inductive effect). The more alkyl groups attached to the carbon with the positive charge, the more its charge is spread out, and the more stable the carbocation.

```
stability:  tertiary  >  secondary  >  primary
```

A more stable carbocation forms faster, because the transition state leading to it has lower energy. So the secondary carbocation forms preferentially, and **2-bromopropane is the major product** while 1-bromopropane is the minor product.

The same reasoning applies to H₂SO₄: propene gives mainly propan-2-yl hydrogensulfate, which hydrolyses to propan-2-ol as the major alcohol.

## Worked example 1: HBr and 2-methylbut-2-ene

The alkene is (CH₃)₂C=CH–CH₃.

- H adds to the CH carbon: the positive charge ends up on the carbon bonded to two methyl groups and an ethyl group, (CH₃)₂C⁺–CH₂CH₃. This is a **tertiary** carbocation. It gives **2-bromo-2-methylbutane** (major).
- H adds to the C(CH₃)₂ carbon: the positive charge is on CH⁺ with two carbon neighbours, (CH₃)₂CH–CH⁺–CH₃. This is **secondary** and gives **2-bromo-3-methylbutane** (minor).

Tertiary is more stable than secondary, so the first product is major.

## Worked example 2: concentrated sulfuric acid and but-1-ene

**Question.** Outline the formation of the major alcohol from but-1-ene using concentrated sulfuric acid.

- Step 1: H⁺ from H₂SO₄ adds to C1, giving the secondary carbocation CH₃CH₂–CH⁺–CH₃.
- Step 2: HSO₄⁻ attacks the carbocation, giving CH₃CH₂CH(OSO₂OH)CH₃.
- Step 3: warm with water, giving **butan-2-ol** and regenerating H₂SO₄.

The minor route (primary carbocation) would give butan-1-ol, which forms in smaller amounts.

## Worked example 3: percentage yield with bromine

5.60 g of but-1-ene (Mᵣ = 56.0) reacts with excess bromine. 17.2 g of 1,2-dibromobutane (Mᵣ = 215.8) is obtained. Calculate the percentage yield. Use Aᵣ values C = 12.0, H = 1.0, Br = 79.9.

```
C₄H₈ + Br₂ → C₄H₈Br₂
n(C₄H₈)               = 5.60 ÷ 56.0 = 0.100 mol
theoretical mass       = 0.100 × 215.8 = 21.58 g
percentage yield       = 17.2 ÷ 21.58 × 100 = 79.7 %
```

Bromine adds in a 1 : 1 ratio, so the moles of product equal the moles of alkene.

## Worked example 4: identifying an unknown from the products

Alkene Y reacts with HBr to give a single product, 2-bromobutane. Y is symmetrical, so it must be but-2-ene (either isomer), because but-1-ene would give a major and a minor product, and the minor product would be 1-bromobutane. A mixture is the expected outcome for an unsymmetrical alkene.

## A quick rule for spotting unsymmetrical alkenes

Look at the two carbons of the C=C bond. Ask whether the set of groups on one carbon matches the set on the other.

| Alkene | Groups on each carbon | Symmetrical? | Number of products with HBr |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ethene | H, H and H, H | Yes | One |
| But-2-ene | H, CH₃ and H, CH₃ | Yes | One |
| Propene | H, CH₃ and H, H | No | Two (one major) |
| But-1-ene | H, C₂H₅ and H, H | No | Two (one major) |
| 2-methylpropene | CH₃, CH₃ and H, H | No | Two (one major) |

For 2-methylpropene the major product is 2-bromo-2-methylpropane, through a tertiary carbocation, and the minor product is 1-bromo-2-methylpropane, through a primary one. The difference in stability between tertiary and primary is large, so the minor product forms in very small amounts.

## Choosing the right mechanism language

| Reagent | Electrophile atom | Intermediate | Product |
|---|---|---|---|
| HBr | H (δ+) | Carbocation | Bromoalkane |
| H₂SO₄ | H of O–H (δ+) | Carbocation | Alkyl hydrogensulfate |
| Br₂ | Br (induced δ+) | Carbocation | Dibromoalkane |

Each is electrophilic addition. The mechanisms differ only in what the electrophile and nucleophile are.

## Common mistakes

- Drawing the first arrow from the reagent to the double bond. It starts at the double bond.
- Writing the carbocation with the positive charge on the wrong carbon, so the "major" product is the minor one.
- Forgetting the induced dipole on bromine, or showing Br₂ as already polar.
- Writing the product of HBr addition to propene as 1-bromopropane without saying it is the minor product.
- Saying the secondary carbocation forms because it "has more hydrogens". It forms because alkyl groups donate electron density and stabilise the charge.
- Missing "orange to colourless" and writing "turns clear".
- Giving the product of H₂SO₄ addition as an alcohol with no hydrolysis step.
- Forgetting that the same alkene can give two products only if it is unsymmetrical.

## Exam technique

- **Outline the mechanism**: show the dipole or the induced dipole, three correctly started arrows, the carbocation with its charge and the final product.
- **Explain major and minor products**: name each carbocation as primary, secondary or tertiary, state the stability order and give the reason (electron-donating alkyl groups).
- **Describe the test**: name the reagent, the colour before and after.
- **State conditions**: room temperature for HBr and Br₂; cold, concentrated for H₂SO₄.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is bromine a suitable electrophile when it is non-polar?**
The electron-rich double bond repels the electrons in the Br–Br bond, which induces a dipole. The nearer atom then acts as the electrophile.

**What is the major product when HBr adds to but-2-ene?**
There is only one possible product, 2-bromobutane, because the alkene is symmetrical.

**Do all alkenes decolourise bromine water?**
Alkenes do. Other compounds with C=C bonds do too, as do some other reducing agents, so a positive test shows unsaturation, not a particular compound.
