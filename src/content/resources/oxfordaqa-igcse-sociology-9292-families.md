This guide covers **section 3.1.1 Families**, Section A of Paper 1 (Introducing Sociology) in OxfordAQA International GCSE Sociology (9292). Section A is worth **22 of the paper's 60 marks**, through short and extended response questions. The [Introducing Sociology guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-sociology-introducing-sociology/) outlines all of Paper 1; this page goes deeper into families alone, with the studies, examples and exam technique you need.

## What section 3.1.1 covers

- **3.1.1.1 Families, marriage and kinship**: types of family (extended, vertically and horizontally; nuclear; single parent); alternatives to families (communes, living alone, foster care, children's homes); **Therborn's** types of family system; matriarchal and patriarchal families; arranged and love marriages, and monogamy.
- **3.1.1.2 Perspectives**: functionalism (functions of the family, how the nuclear family "fits" industrial society); Marxism (families as agents of social control); feminisms (families shaping the position of men and women).
- **3.1.1.3 Social change**: demographic changes and their explanations; families and industrialisation and urbanisation; newer family types and explanations of family diversity, including laws and social policies.
- **3.1.1.4 Gender, age and family life**: traditional roles, the triple shift, domestic labour, power and decision making, violence against women and abuse; children, child-centredness, boomerang families; older people and grandparents.

The specification names **Engels, Oakley, Parsons, Smart, Stacey and Therborn**, and credits the appropriate use of other sociologists. It also expects you to use **examples from your own society, or one you know about**, in some extended answers.

## Family types and alternatives

| Type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Nuclear | Two generations: parents and their dependent children |
| Vertically extended | Three or more generations, such as grandparents, parents and children |
| Horizontally extended | Relatives of the same generation, such as brothers and their wives, living together |
| Single parent | One parent with dependent children |
| Reconstituted (blended) | A couple with children from a previous relationship |

**Alternatives** to living in a family include **communes**, where unrelated adults share property and childcare; **living alone**, increasingly common in many cities; and **foster care** and **children's homes**, where the state or others take over the care of children.

**Therborn** compared family systems across the world's regions, such as European, East Asian, South Asian, West Asian and North African, and sub-Saharan African systems, and traced how patriarchy declined unevenly in each during the twentieth century. His key point is that no single family form is "normal" worldwide. **Patriarchal** families give authority to the father or eldest male; **matriarchal** families give authority to the mother.

## Perspectives on families

**Functionalism (Parsons).** The family performs functions society needs. Parsons argued that the modern family has two essential functions: **primary socialisation** of children and the **stabilisation of adult personalities**. The isolated **nuclear family** "fits" industrial society because it is small and geographically mobile, able to move where work is, and because status at work is achieved rather than inherited from kin.

*Criticisms*: the historian **Laslett** found that most households in pre-industrial England were already nuclear, and functionalism ignores conflict and abuse within families.

**Marxism (Engels).** **Engels** argued that the monogamous family developed with **private property**: men needed to know who their legitimate heirs were so that wealth could be passed on. Marxists see families as **agents of social control**: they teach children to accept authority and inequality, and families act as units of consumption that keep capitalism profitable.

*Criticisms*: ignores the benefits family members gain; families vary greatly across classes and societies.

**Feminisms (Oakley).** Feminists argue that families shape the unequal position of men and women. **Oakley's** research on housework, based on interviews with London housewives, found that women carried most of the burden and that men's "help" was limited. Liberal feminists see gradual progress; radical feminists see families as a key site of patriarchy.

*Criticisms*: may understate change and the variety of women's experiences.

## Social change and changing families

**Demographic changes** to know:

| Change | Typical explanations |
|---|---|
| Falling **birth rates** and smaller families | Contraception; more education and paid work for women; children becoming an economic cost rather than a contribution |
| Falling **death rates**, including **infant and child mortality** | Better nutrition, clean water, sanitation, vaccination and healthcare |
| **Ageing populations** | Longer life expectancy plus fewer births; Japan is a well-known example |
| **Migration**, internal and international | Moving from rural to urban areas for work; moving abroad, sometimes leaving children with grandparents |

**Industrialisation and urbanisation.** Parsons linked industrialisation to the nuclear family. But **Young and Willmott** found close extended-family networks in a working-class area of East London in the 1950s, and argued that families later became more **symmetrical**, with husbands and wives sharing more. In many rapidly urbanising societies today, migration to cities can weaken extended families while money sent home keeps kinship ties strong.

**Family diversity** includes **pivot or sandwich generations** (caring for both children and ageing parents), **reconstituted or blended** families, **living apart together** couples, **child-free couples** and **beanpole families** (many generations, few members in each). **Stacey** described diverse, negotiated "postmodern" families, including "divorce-extended" families linked by former marriages. **Smart** emphasised **personal life**: people define for themselves who counts as family, including friends and former partners.

**Laws and social policies** shape families. China's one-child policy (introduced in 1980) reduced family size and contributed to an ageing population; it was replaced by a two-child policy in 2016 and a three-child policy in 2021. Divorce laws, welfare benefits and childcare provision all affect family forms in other societies.

## Gender, age and family life

- **Traditional roles**: Parsons' **instrumental** (male breadwinner) and **expressive** (female, emotional and caring) roles.
- **The triple shift**: many women do paid work, domestic work and **emotional work**, a term associated with Duncombe and Marsden.
- **Power and decision making**: even where tasks are shared, studies have found men often make the biggest financial decisions.
- **Violence and abuse**: Dobash and Dobash showed how domestic violence is linked to male power. The WHO estimates that about 1 in 3 women worldwide have experienced physical or sexual violence.
- **Children**: the historian Ariès argued childhood is socially constructed; many societies have become **child-centred**, while in others children still work or care for siblings.
- **Adult children**: **boomerang** families, where adult children return home, often because of housing costs.
- **Older people and grandparents**: status varies; in some societies elders hold authority, while in others they may be marginalised. Grandparents often provide childcare.

## Worked example 1: a short-answer question

*Explain one way in which families act as agents of social control. [3 marks]*

> Marxists argue that families teach children to obey parental authority (1). This prepares them to accept the authority of employers and the state without questioning it (1), which helps keep an unequal capitalist society stable (1).

One point, **developed** in three linked steps, earns all three marks.

## Worked example 2: planning an extended answer

*Discuss how far the nuclear family is the main family type in modern societies.*

```
FOR       Parsons: nuclear family fits industrial society
          still a common ideal and household type in many societies
AGAINST   diversity: reconstituted, single parent, LAT, beanpole
          Stacey and Smart: negotiated, chosen families
          extended families remain strong in many societies
          (use an example from your own society)
JUDGEMENT the nuclear family remains important, but one type among
          many; how far depends on the society studied
```

## Common mistakes

- Confusing vertically and horizontally extended families.
- Naming a perspective without its sociologist, or a sociologist without their argument.
- Describing demographic change without explaining its causes.
- Treating the family in one society as universal, which Therborn's work challenges.
- Forgetting to give an example from a society you know when the question asks for one.

## Exam technique

- **Identify** or **name**: a brief point, no development needed.
- **Describe**: give the features of something, with detail.
- **Explain**: give a reason and develop it with a linked consequence or example.
- **Discuss** or **evaluate**: present arguments on more than one side and reach a judgement.
