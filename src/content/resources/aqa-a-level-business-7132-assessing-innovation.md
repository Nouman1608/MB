This guide goes deeper into section **3.9.2 Assessing innovation** of the AQA AS and A-level Business specification (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). Section 3.9 is for the A-level only. The specification's content is:

- **the pressures for innovation**, with types of innovation to include product and process innovation
- **the value of innovation**
- **the ways of becoming an innovative organisation**, including Kaizen, research and development, intrapreneurship and benchmarking
- **how to protect innovation and intellectual property**, including patents and copyrights
- **the impact of an innovation strategy on the functional areas** of the business.

All businesses and figures are invented.

## What is innovation?

**Innovation** is the successful introduction of something new: a new product, a new process or a new way of doing business. It is different from **invention**, which is the creation of a new idea. An invention only becomes an innovation when it is turned into something customers pay for or the business uses to reduce cost. Many inventions never make it.

Two types appear in the specification:

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Product innovation | A new or significantly improved product or service | A bank launches an app that lets customers freeze lost cards |
| Process innovation | A new or improved way of producing or delivering | A bakery installs a robot to shape dough, cutting waste |

Innovation can be **radical** (a major breakthrough, such as a new technology) or **incremental** (a series of small improvements). Both matter, but radical change carries bigger risk and a possible bigger reward.

## Pressures for innovation

- **Competition:** rivals' new products make older ones look dated; innovating can create an advantage.
- **Customer expectations:** customers expect better, cheaper, faster, and increasingly personalised products.
- **Technological change:** new technology makes new products possible and makes old ones obsolete.
- **Product life cycles:** products decline, so new ones are needed to replace the lost sales.
- **Cost pressure:** process innovation reduces costs when prices are under pressure.
- **Regulation and social pressure:** rules on emissions or packaging force new designs.
- **Shareholders and managers:** growth targets need new sources of revenue.
- **Globalisation:** low-cost overseas rivals force high-cost firms to innovate rather than compete on price.

## The value of innovation

- **Competitive advantage:** a unique product can command a premium price; a new process can lower cost (see Porter's strategies in section 3.8.2).
- **Higher revenue and profit:** new products add sales; extended product life.
- **Brand image:** a reputation for innovation attracts customers, staff and investors.
- **Entry to new markets** through new products.
- **Lower costs** through process improvements.
- **Staff motivation:** people like to work for innovative firms.

**Limits and costs:** innovation is expensive and uncertain, many new products fail, and rivals can copy. Innovation may take years to pay back, and focusing on new products can distract from running the existing business.

### Worked example: the cost and return of R&D

Fenner Components has revenue of £20m and spends 8% on R&D.

- R&D spend: £20m × 8% = **£1.6m**
- New products launched in the last three years bring in 25% of revenue: £20m × 25% = **£5m**
- Revenue from new products per £1 of R&D: 5 ÷ 1.6 = **£3.13**

**Analysis.** A quarter of revenue comes from products that did not exist three years ago, so the spending is generating sales. But revenue is not profit; the new products have their own production and marketing costs. **Judgement.** The R&D budget appears to be effective, but Fenner should look at the profit earned and whether the 8% could be spent more productively.

## Ways of becoming an innovative organisation

### Research and development (R&D)

Systematic, planned work to discover new knowledge (research) and turn it into products or processes (development).

- *Value:* leads to patentable, unique products; builds long-term capability.
- *Limits:* costly; results uncertain; takes years; benefits may go to rivals if not protected; small firms may lack funds. Some firms buy innovative firms instead.

### Kaizen

A Japanese approach meaning continuous improvement: all employees, at every level, make small regular improvements to processes and products.

- *Value:* low cost; uses employees' knowledge; improves quality and cuts waste; raises motivation and ownership; builds a habit of change.
- *Limits:* gains are incremental and may not lead to breakthrough products; needs management commitment and time for staff; improvements can plateau; may be less suited to fast-moving markets.
- *Example:* a factory receives 200 employee suggestions in a year, each saving an average £800: **£160,000** a year in savings.

### Intrapreneurship

**Intrapreneurs** are employees who act like entrepreneurs inside a larger organisation, given freedom, time and resources to develop new ideas. Methods include innovation labs, "20% time" for staff projects, or internal competitions.

- *Value:* uses staff creativity; keeps entrepreneurial staff who might otherwise leave; faster than formal R&D; can lead to new businesses within the firm.
- *Limits:* needs a culture that tolerates failure; hard to manage alongside normal duties; conflict with existing departments; ideas may be taken up by competitors if staff leave.

### Benchmarking

Comparing the firm's performance, processes or products with the best in the industry or other industries, then adopting the practices that explain the difference.

- *Value:* sets realistic targets; identifies gaps; learns from proven methods.
- *Limits:* it copies rather than leads, so the firm may only catch up; good comparison data may not be available; the practice may not suit the firm's culture; rivals may not share information.
- *Example:* a firm's defect rate is 3.2% against 1.1% for the best-performing rival. On 500,000 units, the gap is 2.1% × 500,000 = 10,500 extra defective units; at £12 per defect, benchmarking identifies a potential saving of **£126,000**.

## Protecting innovation and intellectual property

**Intellectual property (IP)** is the creations of the mind that can be owned. Unprotected innovations can be copied, which wipes out the return on the investment.

| Protection | What it covers | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Patent | Inventions: a new product or process | Gives the owner exclusive rights, typically for up to 20 years; it must be applied for and costs money; the invention is published; protection is limited to the countries where it is granted |
| Copyright | Original works such as software code, writing, music, designs, images | Arises automatically on creation; does not protect ideas, only their expression |

Other methods include trademarks for brand names and logos, keeping know-how secret (trade secrets), and being first to market.

**Value of protection:** allows the business to earn a return, deters imitation, and creates value that can be licensed or sold.
**Limits:** costs of applying for and enforcing rights, which small firms may find hard; patent applications reveal how the invention works; rivals may find a way around the patent; enforcement overseas can be difficult.

## Impact on the functional areas

| Function | Impact of an innovation strategy |
|---|---|
| Finance | Funding R&D and launch costs; uncertain returns; cash-flow pressure before sales arrive |
| Marketing | Launch campaigns; educating customers; pricing a new product (skimming or penetration); managing the product portfolio |
| Operations | New processes, equipment and training; flexible production; quality control; supply chains |
| Human resources | Recruiting creative and technical staff; training; a culture that accepts risk; reward systems for ideas |

## Assessing an innovation strategy

Ask:

1. Does it fit the objectives and strengths of the business?
2. Can it be afforded and sustained for long enough to pay off?
3. What is the risk of failure, and can the downside be limited by testing or staging?
4. Can the innovation be protected?
5. Is there a market for it, and how quickly could rivals respond?
6. Is the organisation's culture suited to innovation?

## Innovation and the product life cycle

Innovation links to the product life cycle. A product moves through introduction, growth, maturity and decline. In maturity, sales flatten and rivals copy the product, so profit is squeezed. A business that waits until decline to innovate is late. Firms therefore keep a **portfolio**: some products in growth, some mature products providing cash, and new products in development. R&D spending is sensible when the cash from mature products funds it.

**Product innovation** often extends the life of a brand (a new version), while **process innovation** protects margins when the product matures and prices fall. A strong answer decides which type of innovation matters most at the stage the product has reached.

## Costs and risks to weigh

| Factor | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| Cost | How much must be spent before any revenue, and for how long? |
| Probability | How likely is success, and has the market been tested? |
| Speed | Can rivals copy before the investment has paid back? |
| Protection | Can a patent, copyright or secrecy protect the return? |
| Culture | Does the firm tolerate failure and listen to staff ideas? |

## Exam technique

- **Analyse:** pick a pressure or a method and explain, step by step, how it leads to a benefit or a cost for this business.
- **Evaluate:** weigh value against cost and risk, consider the size of the business and its market, and reach a judgement.
- Distinguish **product** from **process** innovation clearly and use named methods (Kaizen, R&D, intrapreneurship, benchmarking) accurately.

## Common mistakes

- Treating innovation as only a new product.
- Confusing invention with innovation.
- Saying R&D guarantees success.
- Describing Kaizen as a one-off project rather than continuous improvement.
- Writing about patents as if they protect ideas that are not inventions, or as permanent.
