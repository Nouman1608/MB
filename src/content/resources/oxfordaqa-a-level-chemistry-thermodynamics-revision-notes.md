## 3.1.8.1 The terms you must define

The specification lists five enthalpy terms used as data in a Born–Haber cycle, plus lattice
enthalpy and enthalpy of hydration. Every definition is **per mole** and comes with an equation.

| Term | Key words in the definition | Equation pattern | Sign |
|---|---|---|---|
| Enthalpy of formation | 1 mol of compound from its elements in standard states | Sr(s) + Br₂(l) → SrBr₂(s) | Usually – |
| Enthalpy of atomisation | 1 mol of **gaseous atoms** from the element in its standard state | ½Br₂(l) → Br(g) | + |
| Bond enthalpy | 1 mol of a covalent bond broken; all species gaseous | Br₂(g) → 2Br(g) | + |
| First ionisation energy | 1 mol of electrons removed from 1 mol of gaseous atoms | Sr(g) → Sr⁺(g) + e⁻ | + |
| Second ionisation energy | 1 mol of electrons removed from 1 mol of gaseous 1+ ions | Sr⁺(g) → Sr²⁺(g) + e⁻ | + |
| First electron affinity | Each atom in 1 mol of gaseous atoms gains one electron | Br(g) + e⁻ → Br⁻(g) | – |
| Lattice enthalpy of dissociation | 1 mol of ionic solid → its gaseous ions | SrBr₂(s) → Sr²⁺(g) + 2Br⁻(g) | + |
| Lattice enthalpy of formation | Gaseous ions → 1 mol of ionic solid | Sr²⁺(g) + 2Br⁻(g) → SrBr₂(s) | – |
| Enthalpy of hydration | 1 mol of gaseous ions → 1 mol of aqueous ions | Sr²⁺(g) → Sr²⁺(aq) | – |

**Remember:** lattice enthalpy can be defined either way. The two have the same size and opposite
signs, so always say which one you mean.

## Formula table

| Use | Relationship | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Born–Haber cycle | ΔfH = ΣΔatH + ΣIE + ΣEA + lattice enthalpy of formation | Multiply each step by how often it occurs |
| Atomisation of a gaseous diatomic element | ΔatH = ½ × bond enthalpy | Not for Br₂(l) or I₂(s) |
| Enthalpy of solution | ΔsolH = lattice enthalpy of dissociation + ΣΔhydH | Count every ion in the formula |
| Entropy change | ΔS = ΣS(products) – ΣS(reactants) | Units J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ |
| Gibbs free-energy change | ΔG = ΔH – TΔS | T in K; ΔS ÷ 1000 to match kJ |
| Feasibility | ΔG ≤ 0 | "Zero or negative" |
| Temperature at which a reaction becomes feasible | T = ΔH / ΔS | From ΔG = 0 |

The specification states that the derivation of ΔG = ΔH – TΔS is not required.

## Born–Haber cycle: method in steps

1. Write the formation equation for the solid, with state symbols.
2. Atomise the metal (× number of metal atoms).
3. Atomise the non-metal. For a gaseous diatomic element, two atoms need one whole bond enthalpy.
4. Ionise the metal: one ionisation energy per electron lost.
5. Add an electron affinity for each electron gained (× number of anions).
6. Close the cycle with the lattice enthalpy of formation.
7. Set the two routes equal (Hess's law) and solve for the single unknown.
8. Sense-check: atomisations and ionisations positive; lattice formation negative.

**Small reminder (Al₂O₃ counting):** 2 × ΔatH(Al); 2 × (first + second + third IE of Al);
3 × ΔatH(O), which is 1½ O=O bonds; 3 × (first + second EA of O).

## Perfect ionic model: what to write

- **The model:** perfectly spherical ions, charge evenly spread, purely electrostatic attraction.
- **Born–Haber value close to the model value:** almost purely ionic bonding.
- **Born–Haber value more exothermic than the model value:** extra attraction, so **covalent
  character**.
- **Cause:** the cation **polarises** the anion, distorting its electron cloud towards the cation.
- **Most polarisation:** small, highly charged cation with a large anion (for example, iodides more
  than fluorides of the same cation).

## Enthalpy of solution: method in steps

1. ΔsolH = lattice enthalpy of **dissociation** + hydration enthalpies of all the ions.
2. Given a lattice enthalpy of **formation**? Reverse its sign first.
3. Multiply each ΔhydH by the number of those ions in the formula.
4. If ΔsolH is given, rearrange for the unknown.

**Why hydration is exothermic:** water's δ– oxygen is attracted to cations and its δ+ hydrogens to
anions. Smaller and more highly charged ions attract water more strongly, so their ΔhydH is more
exothermic.

## 3.1.8.2 Entropy and Gibbs free energy

**Why ΔH is not sufficient:** some endothermic changes happen on their own (melting of ice above
273 K, many salts dissolving). Feasibility also depends on **entropy**, the degree of disorder.

**ΔS is positive for:**
- physical changes towards more disorder: solid → liquid → gas;
- chemical changes that increase the moles of gas, or turn a solid or liquid into a gas.

**ΔS is negative for** the reverse of these.

### ΔG: method in steps

1. Calculate ΔS from absolute entropies, using coefficients.
2. Divide ΔS by 1000 to get kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
3. Use T in kelvin.
4. ΔG = ΔH – TΔS. Feasible if ΔG ≤ 0.
5. For the temperature at which a reaction becomes feasible, set ΔG = 0: T = ΔH/ΔS.
6. Use the signs to say "above" or "below" that temperature.

### How ΔG varies with temperature

| ΔH | ΔS | Effect of raising T | Feasible |
|---|---|---|---|
| – | + | ΔG more negative | Always |
| + | – | ΔG more positive | Never |
| + | + | ΔG falls through zero | Above T = ΔH/ΔS |
| – | – | ΔG rises through zero | Below T = ΔH/ΔS |

**Small reminder:** a reaction with ΔH = +30 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS = +80 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ has
ΔG = 30 – 300 × 0.080 = +6 kJ mol⁻¹ at 300 K (not feasible) but ΔG = 30 – 400 × 0.080 = –2 kJ mol⁻¹
at 400 K (feasible).

## Must-know distinctions

- **Atomisation vs bond enthalpy:** per mole of atoms formed vs per mole of bonds broken.
- **Lattice formation vs dissociation:** same size, opposite sign.
- **Hydration vs solution:** gaseous ions → aqueous ions vs solid → aqueous ions.
- **First vs second electron affinity of oxygen:** exothermic vs endothermic.
- **Feasible vs fast:** ΔG ≤ 0 says a reaction can happen, not how quickly. A high activation energy
  can make a feasible reaction too slow to observe.
- **Absolute entropy vs ΔfH⦵ of an element:** S of an element is never zero; ΔfH⦵ of an element in
  its standard state is zero.

## Quick self-test

1. Write an equation for the lattice enthalpy of dissociation of BaF₂.
2. The H–H bond enthalpy is +436 kJ mol⁻¹. State ΔatH⦵ of hydrogen.
3. Write the equation for the enthalpy of atomisation of iodine.
4. How many first electron affinities of chlorine appear in the Born–Haber cycle for AlCl₃?
5. Lattice enthalpy of dissociation of CsCl = +676 kJ mol⁻¹; ΔhydH(Cs⁺) = –276; ΔhydH(Cl⁻) = –381.
   Calculate ΔsolH(CsCl).
6. ΔsolH(MgBr₂) = –186 kJ mol⁻¹; lattice enthalpy of dissociation = +2405; ΔhydH(Br⁻) = –335.
   Calculate ΔhydH(Mg²⁺).
7. A compound's Born–Haber lattice enthalpy of formation is –735 kJ mol⁻¹ and its perfect ionic
   model value is –733 kJ mol⁻¹. What does this show?
8. State the sign of ΔS for 2KClO₃(s) → 2KCl(s) + 3O₂(g), with a reason.
9. For 2NO(g) + O₂(g) → 2NO₂(g), ΔH = –114 kJ mol⁻¹. Absolute entropies (J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹): NO 211,
   O₂ 205, NO₂ 240. Calculate ΔS and the temperature range over which the reaction is feasible.
10. State what happens to ΔG as temperature rises when ΔS is negative.

### Answers

1. BaF₂(s) → Ba²⁺(g) + 2F⁻(g)
2. **+218 kJ mol⁻¹** (half of 436, per mole of H atoms).
3. ½I₂(s) → I(g)
4. **Three**: three Cl⁻ ions form.
5. ΔsolH = 676 – 276 – 381 = **+19 kJ mol⁻¹**
6. –186 = 2405 + ΔhydH(Mg²⁺) + 2(–335), so ΔhydH(Mg²⁺) = **–1921 kJ mol⁻¹**
7. The values almost match, so the compound is very close to purely ionic (little covalent
   character).
8. Positive: a solid forms 3 moles of gas, so disorder increases.
9. ΔS = 2(240) – [2(211) + 205] = **–147 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹**; T = 114/0.147 = **776 K**; feasible
   **below 776 K**.
10. –TΔS is positive and grows, so ΔG becomes **more positive** (less negative).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining lattice enthalpy without "1 mole" of solid or without "gaseous ions".
- Writing Br₂(g) → 2Br(g) or I₂(s) → 2I(g) for an atomisation instead of forming 1 mole of atoms.
- Missing the second (or third) ionisation energy for a 2+ (or 3+) ion.
- Treating a second electron affinity as exothermic.
- Using lattice enthalpy of formation in a solution cycle without reversing the sign.
- Counting a hydration enthalpy once when the formula holds two of that ion.
- Saying a Born–Haber value that is *less* exothermic than the model shows covalent character.
- Substituting ΔS in J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ with ΔH in kJ mol⁻¹, or using °C.
- Giving T = ΔH/ΔS with no statement of "above" or "below".
