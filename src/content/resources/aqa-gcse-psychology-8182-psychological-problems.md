This guide goes deeper on **3.2.4 Psychological problems**, the fourth topic in **Paper 2: Social
context and behaviour** of AQA GCSE Psychology (8182), first teaching 2017. Paper 2 lasts 1 hour 45
minutes, is worth 100 marks and counts for 50% of the GCSE. The
[Social Context and Behaviour study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-psychology-social-context-and-behaviour/)
summarises all four Paper 2 topics; this page adds definitions, evaluation of each explanation and
treatment, and a worked comparison for addiction.

## What 3.2.4 covers

1. **An introduction to mental health** -- what good mental health looks like, cultural variation in
   beliefs about mental health problems, and why the recorded incidence of significant mental health
   problems changes over time.
2. **Effects of significant mental health problems on individuals and society.**
3. **Characteristics of clinical depression** -- unipolar depression, bipolar depression and
   sadness; diagnosis using the **International Classification of Diseases (ICD)**.
4. **Theories of depression and interventions** -- the biological explanation (neurotransmitter
   imbalance, such as serotonin) and the psychological explanation (negative schemas and
   attributional style); antidepressant drugs and cognitive behaviour therapy (CBT); Wiles' study of
   CBT (2013).
5. **Characteristics of addiction** -- addiction or dependence, substance misuse and substance abuse;
   the ICD features of dependence.
6. **Theories of addiction and interventions** -- the biological explanation (hereditary factors and
   genetic vulnerability, including Kaij's twin study of alcohol abuse, 1960) and the psychological
   explanation (peer influence); aversion therapy and self-management programmes such as self-help
   groups and twelve-step programmes.

## Mental health and why rates change

Good mental health is more than the absence of illness. It includes coping with the normal stresses
of life, working productively, forming positive relationships and contributing to the community.
Beliefs about mental health problems **vary between cultures**: behaviour seen as a sign of illness
in one culture may be seen as normal, or given a spiritual meaning, in another.

The **recorded incidence** of significant mental health problems changes over time for several
reasons, and good answers separate them:

- **Increased recognition** -- better understanding means problems are more likely to be identified
  and diagnosed.
- **Less stigma** -- as attitudes change, more people seek help, so more cases are recorded.
- **Challenges of modern living** -- for example, social isolation, pressures at work or school, and
  financial insecurity may increase the number of people affected.

The first two increase **recorded** cases without necessarily meaning more people are unwell; the
third suggests a real increase.

## Effects on individuals and society

| Individuals | Society |
|---|---|
| Damage to relationships with family and friends | Greater need for health and care services |
| Difficulty coping with day-to-day life, such as work or study | Costs to the economy through lost working days and benefits |
| Negative effects on physical health, such as poor sleep and diet | Possible links with crime, where untreated problems contribute to offending |

## Clinical depression

- **Sadness** is a normal, short-lived response to events such as a loss.
- **Unipolar depression** is persistent low mood, without episodes of mania.
- **Bipolar depression** (bipolar disorder) involves periods of depression alternating with periods
  of **mania**: elevated mood, high energy and reckless behaviour.

The **ICD** classifies depressive episodes as mild, moderate or severe according to the **number and
severity** of symptoms. Symptoms include **low mood**, **loss of interest and enjoyment**, **reduced
energy**, **changes in sleep** and **appetite**, and **decreased self-confidence**.

**Biological explanation.** Depression is linked to an **imbalance of neurotransmitters**,
particularly low levels or reduced activity of **serotonin**, which is involved in regulating mood.
*Evaluation:* antidepressants that increase serotonin activity help many patients, which supports
the explanation; but drugs take weeks to work although they change serotonin levels quickly, and they
do not help everyone, suggesting serotonin is not the whole story. It is also unclear whether low
serotonin causes depression or results from it.

**Psychological explanation.** **Negative schemas** are mental frameworks, often developed through
early experiences, that lead people to see themselves, the world and the future negatively.
**Attributional style** is how people explain events: a depressive style blames negative events on
**internal** ("it's my fault"), **stable** ("it will always be like this") and **global** ("it ruins
everything") causes. *Evaluation:* this leads directly to an effective therapy (CBT); but it may be
that depression causes negative thinking rather than the reverse, and it can seem to blame the
individual while overlooking life circumstances.

**Antidepressant drugs** (for example, SSRIs) increase the availability of serotonin at the synapse.
*Strengths:* easy to take and require little effort from the patient. *Weaknesses:* side effects,
weeks before they take effect, and symptoms can return when medication stops because the cause is
not addressed.

**CBT** helps people identify negative, irrational thoughts and challenge them, and replace unhelpful
behaviour with more positive activity. *Strengths:* tackles the cause as the psychological
explanation sees it, and skills can last after therapy ends. *Weaknesses:* requires motivation and
effort, can be costly and has waiting lists.

**Wiles' study (2013).** Wiles and colleagues ran a randomised controlled trial with patients in UK
GP practices whose depression had not responded to antidepressants. Patients were randomly allocated
to continue their usual care (including medication) or to receive usual care plus CBT. Symptoms were
measured with the Beck Depression Inventory. The CBT group showed a greater reduction in symptoms at
six months, and the benefit was still present at twelve months. *Evaluation:* random allocation and
a large sample make the results trustworthy, and they support combining treatments; but patients
knew which treatment they received, and those who agreed to take part may have been more motivated
than typical patients.

## Addiction

- **Addiction / dependence** -- a compulsion to use a substance despite harm, with physical and
  psychological features.
- **Substance misuse** -- using a substance in a way that is harmful or not intended, such as
  drinking too much on one occasion.
- **Substance abuse** -- a repeated pattern of harmful use, without necessarily being dependent.

The **ICD dependence syndrome** includes a **strong desire** to take the substance, **difficulty
controlling** use, a physiological **withdrawal** state when use stops, **tolerance** (needing more
for the same effect), **neglect of other pleasures and interests**, and **persistent use despite
clear evidence of harm**.

**Biological explanation: genetic vulnerability.** Some people inherit genes that make them more
vulnerable to addiction. **Kaij's twin study (1960)** examined male twin pairs in Sweden where at
least one twin was registered for alcohol abuse, and found higher **concordance** for alcohol abuse
in identical (monozygotic) twins than in non-identical (dizygotic) twins. *Evaluation:* supports a
genetic contribution; but concordance was not 100%, so environment must matter too; identical twins
may also share a more similar environment; and older methods of deciding whether twins were
identical were less reliable.

**Psychological explanation: peer influence.** People take up and continue substance use because of
the group they belong to: they observe and imitate friends (social learning), want to fit in, and
come to see heavy use as normal. *Evaluation:* explains why use often begins in adolescence in
groups; but the link is correlational -- people may choose friends who already share their habits
-- and it does not explain why only some members of a group become dependent.

**Aversion therapy** uses classical conditioning: the substance is paired with something unpleasant,
for example a drug that causes nausea when alcohol is drunk, so the person learns to associate the
substance with feeling ill. *Weaknesses:* unpleasant, raising ethical concerns; the effect may fade
outside the clinic.

**Self-management programmes** include self-help groups and **twelve-step programmes** such as
Alcoholics Anonymous, where members support each other, follow structured steps and take
responsibility for recovery. *Strengths:* free, widely available, provide a new supportive peer
group. *Weaknesses:* high drop-out rates; success depends on motivation; difficult to evaluate
scientifically because members are anonymous and self-selected.

## Worked example: comparing explanations of addiction

*Question: Compare the biological and psychological explanations of addiction. [4]*

A strong answer: "The biological explanation sees addiction as caused by **inherited genetic
vulnerability**, supported by Kaij's finding of higher concordance in identical twins, whereas the
psychological explanation sees it as **learned through peer influence** [1] [1]. The biological view
is on the **nature** side, the psychological on the **nurture** side [1]. They also lead to different
interventions: the psychological view supports changing the person's social environment, for example
through the new peer group of a self-help programme, while a genetic account suggests identifying
vulnerable people early [1]." Note how each sentence contrasts the two, rather than describing them
separately.

## Common mistakes

- Defining depression as "being very sad". Use clinical features and the ICD.
- Mixing up unipolar and bipolar: bipolar includes **mania**.
- Saying Kaij proved addiction is genetic. Concordance was not 100%.
- Confusing substance misuse with dependence.
- Evaluating treatments only with "it works". Give a reason or evidence, and a weakness.

## Exam technique

**Compare** means say how two things are similar or different in the same sentence. **Evaluate**
treatments with effectiveness, side effects, cost, ethics and whether they tackle the cause. In
application questions, match the person's symptoms to the ICD features and name them.
