This guide covers subtopic **3.3, Metallic bonding**, from Topic 3, Chemical bonding, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is AS Level content. The outcome itself is a single definition, but that definition is the tool you use later to explain the melting points and conductivity of metals in Topic 4 (giant metallic lattices, including copper) and Topic 9 (the Period 3 elements sodium, magnesium and aluminium). The broader [Chemical Bonding: Electronegativity, Ionic and Metallic Bonds](/resources/as-chemical-bonding-electronegativity-ionic-and-metallic-bonds/) guide introduces the model; this page shows how to use it in explanations. Condensed notes are in the [metallic bonding revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-as-metallic-bonding-revision-notes/), and you can test yourself with the [metallic bonding practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-as-metallic-bonding-practice/).

## Syllabus coverage

**3.3 Metallic bonding.** Candidates should be able to:

1. define metallic bonding as the electrostatic attraction between positive metal ions and delocalised electrons

## The definition, word by word

Each phrase in the definition carries a mark-worthy idea:

- **Electrostatic attraction**: like ionic and covalent bonding, metallic bonding is an attraction between opposite charges. It is not a shared pair of electrons between two particular atoms.
- **Positive metal ions**: in the solid, each metal atom has released its outer-shell electrons. What sits at each lattice position is therefore a **cation**, not a neutral atom. Writing "metal atoms" in the definition loses the mark.
- **Delocalised electrons**: the released electrons are not attached to any one ion. They are spread through the whole structure and are free to move. "Delocalised" is the word examiners look for; "free electrons" or "a sea of electrons" is a description, best used alongside the correct term rather than instead of it.

Because every ion attracts the delocalised electrons around it in all directions, metallic bonding is **non-directional** and extends through the whole lattice. A piece of metal is a giant structure, so you never talk about "metallic molecules".

## How many electrons are delocalised?

Each atom contributes its **outer-shell (valence) electrons**. For the metals you meet most often:

| Metal | Configuration | Electrons delocalised per atom | Ion in lattice |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sodium | [Ne] 3s¹ | 1 | Na⁺ |
| Magnesium | [Ne] 3s² | 2 | Mg²⁺ |
| Aluminium | [Ne] 3s² 3p¹ | 3 | Al³⁺ |

Across this series, the ion charge rises from +1 to +3, and the number of delocalised electrons per ion rises from one to three. The ions also get smaller, because the nuclear charge increases while the electrons removed all came from the same (third) shell.

## What makes metallic bonding stronger

The attraction between the ion lattice and the delocalised electrons is stronger when:

1. the **charge on the metal ion is higher**, and
2. there are **more delocalised electrons** per ion, and
3. the **metal ions are smaller**, so the delocalised electrons are closer to the nuclei.

These factors explain the trend in melting points across Period 3. Sodium melts at 98 °C; magnesium at 650 °C; aluminium at 660 °C. The jump from sodium to magnesium is large; from magnesium to aluminium the rise is small. Questions at AS ask you to explain the general direction, so state the three factors and link them to the energy needed to overcome the attraction.

Going **down Group 1**, the opposite happens. Each ion still carries a +1 charge and contributes one delocalised electron, but the ions get larger, so the delocalised electrons are further from the nuclei. The attraction weakens and melting points fall: lithium 181 °C, sodium 98 °C, potassium 63 °C.

### Worked example 1: comparing two metals

**Question.** Explain why magnesium has a higher melting point than sodium. [3]

**Model answer, annotated.**

- Magnesium forms Mg²⁺ ions and contributes two delocalised electrons per atom; sodium forms Na⁺ and contributes one. *(one mark: charge and electron count)*
- The Mg²⁺ ion is also smaller than Na⁺. *(one mark: size)*
- So the electrostatic attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons is stronger in magnesium, and more energy is needed to overcome it when the metal melts. *(one mark: link to the bond and to energy)*

A common weak answer says "magnesium has stronger metallic bonds" with no reason. That is a restatement of the question.

## Using the model to explain conductivity

Metals conduct electricity because the **delocalised electrons are mobile**. When a potential difference is applied, the electrons move through the lattice towards the positive terminal, carrying charge. This works in both the solid and the liquid, because melting does not remove the delocalised electrons. The metal ions themselves do not move in the solid; it is the electrons that carry the current. This is the key contrast with an ionic compound, where the ions carry the charge and so conduction only happens once the ions are free to move.

Copper, the example the syllabus names for a giant metallic lattice in Topic 4, is a regular lattice of positive copper ions surrounded by delocalised electrons. It is an excellent conductor in the solid state for exactly this reason.

### Worked example 2: predicting from data

**Question.** Element X has a melting point of 1085 °C. It conducts electricity as a solid and when molten, and is insoluble in water and in hexane. Deduce the type of bonding in X. [2]

Conduction in **both** solid and liquid states requires mobile charge carriers that are present in the solid. An ionic solid does not conduct, and simple molecular or giant covalent solids (apart from graphite) do not conduct. A high melting point with conduction in both states points to **metallic bonding**: a lattice of positive ions held by electrostatic attraction to delocalised electrons. (These are the properties of copper.)

## Placing metallic bonding alongside the other bond types

All three strong bond types in Topic 3 are electrostatic. The difference is **what attracts what**:

| Bond type | Electrostatic attraction between |
|---|---|
| Ionic | oppositely charged ions (cations and anions) |
| Metallic | positive metal ions and delocalised electrons |
| Covalent | the nuclei of two atoms and a shared pair of electrons |

Learning them as a set prevents the most common mix-up, which is describing a metal as "ions held by ionic bonds". There are no anions in a pure metal; the negative charge comes from the delocalised electrons.

## Common mistakes

- **"Attraction between metal atoms and electrons."** The particles in the lattice are positive ions.
- **"Attraction between positive and negative ions."** That is ionic bonding.
- **Leaving out "delocalised".** "Electrons" alone does not distinguish metallic from covalent bonding.
- **Saying the ions carry the current in a solid metal.** Only the delocalised electrons move.
- **Explaining melting point trends without naming a factor.** Give charge, number of delocalised electrons and ion size.
- **Saying the number of delocalised electrons increases down a group.** Down Group 1 it stays at one per atom; it is the ion size that changes.

## Exam technique for 3.3

- **Define**: reproduce the syllabus definition exactly. All three ideas (electrostatic attraction, positive metal ions, delocalised electrons) are needed.
- **Explain** a property: start from the definition, name the relevant factor, then link to energy or to mobile charge.
- **Deduce** from data: a high melting point together with conduction in the solid **and** the liquid is the signature of metallic bonding.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is "sea of electrons" acceptable in a definition?**
It describes the picture, but the syllabus term is delocalised electrons. Use "delocalised electrons" in a definition and add the description only if it helps.

**Why do metals conduct when solid but ionic compounds do not?**
In a metal, the delocalised electrons are already mobile in the solid. In an ionic solid the ions are fixed in place, so there are no mobile charge carriers until it melts or dissolves.

**Does every metal atom give up the same number of electrons?**
No. Each atom contributes its outer-shell electrons: one for sodium, two for magnesium and three for aluminium.
