This guide covers **3.1.1 Section A: Living with hazards** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Geography (9635) specification, Version 3.2. Every student studies this section, whichever Unit 1 option follows it, and it is assessed in Section A of the Unit 1 exam (40 marks, all questions compulsory). The specification divides it into seven parts: 3.1.1.1 the concept of hazard, 3.1.1.2 plate tectonics, 3.1.1.3 volcanic hazards, 3.1.1.4 seismic hazards, 3.1.1.5 storm hazards, 3.1.1.6 fires in nature and 3.1.1.7 case studies.

The [living with hazards revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-geography-9635-living-with-hazards-revision-notes/) already list the plate margins, hazard types and the main case-study options. This guide goes further on the parts students find hardest to turn into marks: linking responses to hazard characteristics, using the Park model and the management cycle analytically, describing hazard patterns in the specification's own terms, and building the two synoptic case studies. Test yourself afterwards with the [living with hazards practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-geography-9635-section-a-living-with-hazards-practice/).

## Why responses differ: the logic of 3.1.1.1

The specification asks you to relate each **characteristic human response** -- fatalism, prediction, adjustment/adaptation, mitigation, management and risk-sharing -- to five properties: hazard **incidence**, **intensity**, **magnitude**, **distribution** and **level of development**. Most answers list the responses; few explain why one is chosen over another. Use this reasoning:

| Property | How it steers the response |
|---|---|
| **Incidence** (how often events occur) | Frequent events justify permanent investment such as building codes and drills; rare events encourage complacency or fatalism because the risk feels remote |
| **Intensity** (effects at a place) | High-intensity zones near a fault or vent need land-use zoning or exclusion; low-intensity areas can rely on adaptation of buildings |
| **Magnitude** (size of the event) | Very large events can overwhelm any defence, so evacuation and risk-sharing matter more than engineering |
| **Distribution** (where events occur) | Hazards confined to known belts (plate margins, storm tracks) can be mapped and zoned; scattered hazards such as wildfire on a dry continent are harder to plan for |
| **Level of development** | Wealth funds monitoring, insurance and engineering; poorer communities may rely on informal adaptation and international aid, or have no choice but to accept the risk |

**Perception** sits behind all of these. Its **economic** determinants include income, insurance and the cost of moving; its **cultural** determinants include religious belief, community memory of past events and trust in authorities. A farmer on a fertile volcanic slope may perceive the volcano as a benefit as much as a threat, which explains why people return to hazardous land after an eruption.

## Using the Park model and the management cycle

The **Park model** plots quality of life (or level of normality) against time. A strong answer does more than name the phases of relief, rehabilitation and reconstruction; it reads the *shape* of the curve:

- **Depth of the fall** reflects vulnerability and event magnitude.
- **Length of the relief phase** reflects emergency capacity and access to the affected area.
- **Slope of recovery** reflects funding, governance and insurance.
- **End level** compares the community after recovery with before. Ending above the original line implies improvements in building or planning; ending below implies long-term damage.
- **Pre-event dip** (shown on some versions) represents preparedness actions and warnings before the event.

The **hazard management cycle** links four stages -- **preparedness, response, recovery, mitigation** -- so that lessons from one event shape preparation for the next. The two models work together: the Park model describes what happens to one community after one event, while the cycle describes how management should improve between events. In an evaluation, argue that a place "moved round the cycle" when, for example, rebuilding included new building codes, which is mitigation for the next event.

## Describing hazard patterns in the specification's terms

For volcanic, seismic and storm hazards the specification names the same set of descriptive terms: **spatial distribution, magnitude, frequency, regularity and predictability** (and for seismic hazards, **randomness**). Use them as a checklist:

- **Spatial distribution:** volcanoes and earthquakes cluster along plate margins and over magma plumes; tropical storms form over warm oceans between about 5 and 30 degrees latitude and track westwards and then polewards.
- **Magnitude:** the moment magnitude scale is logarithmic, so each whole number represents about 32 times more energy released. Volcanic eruptions are compared on the **Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI)**. Tropical storm strength is described on the Saffir-Simpson scale.
- **Frequency and regularity:** small earthquakes are far more frequent than large ones. Some volcanoes erupt almost continuously, others after centuries of dormancy. Tropical storms follow a season, so they are regular in timing but not in exact track.
- **Randomness and predictability:** earthquakes are effectively random in timing; the location can be forecast as a probability but not the date. Volcanoes give measurable warnings. Storm tracks are forecast days ahead with improving accuracy.

## Building the required case studies

The specification requires impacts and responses for a recent volcanic event, a recent seismic event, two recent tropical storms in contrasting areas and a recent wildfire, plus two synoptic studies. Organise each one under the same headings so they can be compared: *physical cause; primary and secondary impacts (environmental, social, economic, political); short-term and long-term responses; risk management through preparedness, mitigation, prevention and adaptation.*

**Examples that are well documented:**

- **Volcanic -- Soufrière Hills, Montserrat (from 1995):** eruptions at a destructive margin in the Caribbean; pyroclastic flows in June 1997 killed 19 people; the capital, Plymouth, was abandoned and buried; the south of the island became an exclusion zone and many residents left the island. The Montserrat Volcano Observatory now monitors the volcano and sets hazard zones -- a clear example of prediction and land-use management.
- **Seismic -- Christchurch, New Zealand (22 February 2011):** a shallow earthquake killed 185 people; widespread **liquefaction** damaged eastern suburbs, and much of the central city was demolished. Long-term responses included rebuilding the centre to stricter standards and abandoning the worst-affected residential land.
- **Contrasting storms -- Cyclone Nargis, Myanmar (May 2008) and Hurricane Katrina, USA (2005):** Nargis's storm surge swept across the low Irrawaddy delta, and around 138,000 people died or went missing; the government's initial restriction of foreign aid slowed relief. Katrina killed more than 1,800 people, most in and around New Orleans after levees failed. The comparison shows that a high-income country can still suffer heavily where defences fail and poorer residents cannot evacuate.
- **Wildfire -- the Camp Fire, California (November 2018):** the fire largely destroyed the town of Paradise and killed 85 people; it was started by electrical transmission equipment, an example of human agency, and spread rapidly in dry fuel driven by strong winds.

**The multi-hazardous environment (3.1.1.7)** must show the hazards, the social, economic and environmental risks, and how **resilience, adaptation, mitigation and management** allow people to keep living there. Japan works well: it faces earthquakes, tsunami, volcanoes and typhoons. The magnitude 9.0 Tōhoku earthquake of 11 March 2011 generated a tsunami that left around 18,000 people dead or missing and caused the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear accident -- evidence that even advanced preparation has limits.

**The local-scale place in a hazardous setting** asks how a community's **economic, social and political character** reflects the hazard. Choose somewhere small enough to describe in detail: one town, district or valley. Strong studies explain, for example, how insurance costs shape the local property market, how community groups organise drills, or how hazard zoning has shaped where people may build.

## Worked example: an annotated paragraph

*Question: Assess the importance of the level of development in determining the impacts of tropical storms.* **[9]**

> Level of development strongly shapes impacts because it determines vulnerability and capacity to cope **(links to the risk idea)**. Cyclone Nargis's surge reached a densely populated, low-lying delta where many homes were lightly built and warnings did not reach many villages, and delays in admitting foreign aid lengthened the relief phase **(specific evidence)**. However, Hurricane Katrina shows development is not decisive on its own: New Orleans lies largely below sea level, and the failure of its levees, together with the poverty of residents without cars, produced over 1,800 deaths in a high-income country **(counter-evidence)**. Physical factors -- surge height, coastal shape and the storm's track -- set the scale of the threat, while development decides how much of that threat becomes disaster **(judgement that weighs both)**.

Notice the structure: a claim, named evidence, a counter-case, then a weighted judgement. "Assess" questions reward that final weighing more than extra description.

## Exam technique for 3.1.1

- **Outline / describe:** state features accurately, with one detail each; no explanation needed.
- **Explain:** give the mechanism, linked by "because" or "so". For a storm surge, explain low pressure raising the sea surface and onshore winds piling water against the coast.
- **Compare:** make direct comparative statements ("Nargis's death toll was far higher than Katrina's, whereas..."), not two separate descriptions.
- **Assess / evaluate / to what extent:** weigh factors, use named evidence on both sides and finish with a judgement that answers the question.
- **Resource-based items:** quote figures from the map, graph or table before interpreting them.

## Common mistakes

- Listing all six responses without linking any of them to incidence, magnitude or development.
- Drawing the Park model without explaining what the curve's shape shows.
- Saying a magnitude 8 earthquake is "twice" as strong as a magnitude 4.
- Calling earthquakes "unpredictable" without distinguishing location (forecastable) from timing (not).
- Using a case study that is not recent, or with no dates.
- Writing a local-scale case study about a whole country.
