## Section A: short questions

**1.** What is meant by a **norm**? **[2]**

**2.** Identify **two** examples of ascribed status. **[2]**

**3.** What is meant by **role conflict**? **[2]**

**4.** Identify **two** ways in which members of an ethnic group in a multicultural society may
maintain a distinct culture. **[2]**

**5.** Study **Item A** and answer the questions that follow.

> **Item A** — Extract from an unstructured interview
>
> *Original extract, invented for this practice question.*
>
> "At home we speak our parents' language and we always eat together on Fridays. At school I speak
> English all day and I'm in the football team. My friends from the same background do the same. We
> make jokes in both languages. My grandmother says we are losing our culture, but I think we have
> two."

**(a)** Using Item A, identify **one** norm the speaker follows at home. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain how Item A shows a hybrid identity. **[3]**

## Section B: longer questions

**6.** Explain the difference between **values** and **norms**, using an example. **[4]**

**7.** Explain what Mills meant by connecting **personal troubles** and **public issues**. **[4]**

**8.** Explain **two** ways in which stereotypes may affect how people are treated in society. **[6]**

**9.** Describe what sociologists mean by a **subculture**, using one example. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how the sociological imagination could be used to understand high rates of young
people leaving education early in a society. **[6]**

**11.** Discuss how far individuals' life chances are shaped by their ascribed status. Use examples
from your own society or a society you know about. **[12]**

## Answers: Section A

**A1** [2 marks]

A specific, expected **rule of behaviour** [1] in a particular situation, which puts a group's values
into practice, such as queueing or removing shoes indoors [1].

**A2** [2 marks — 1 each]

Any two: sex; age; caste; ethnicity; a royal or noble title; family of birth.

**A3** [2 marks]

When the demands of **two or more roles** a person holds [1] **clash**, so meeting one makes it hard to
meet the other, for example a working parent with an ill child on the day of an important meeting [1].

**A4** [2 marks — 1 each]

Any two: speaking a heritage language at home; religious practice; festivals; food; family norms
such as shared meals; community organisations.

**A5 (a)** [1 mark]

Speaking the parents' language at home, or eating together on Fridays [1].

**A5 (b)** [3 marks]

A hybrid identity combines elements of more than one culture [1]. The speaker follows heritage norms
at home but also takes part in mainstream school life, speaking English and playing football [1], and
makes jokes "in both languages", saying "we have two" cultures, which shows a new identity drawing on
both [1].

## Answers: Section B

**A6** [4 marks]

**Values** are broad shared beliefs about what is good or important [1], such as respect for elders
[1]. **Norms** are specific rules of behaviour that put values into practice [1], such as standing
when an older person enters the room [1].

**A7** [4 marks]

Mills argued that people often see their problems as private **personal troubles**, explained by
their own situation [1]. Many problems are really **public issues** that affect large numbers of
people and arise from the structure of society [1]. For example, one person being unemployed may be a
personal trouble [1], but mass unemployment is a public issue caused by the economy, so it needs a
social explanation [1].

**A8** [6 marks — up to 3 per way]

Two from: **employment** [1] — employers may judge applicants by stereotypes about their gender or
ethnicity [1], so some groups are less likely to be hired or promoted [1]; **education** [1] — teachers
may label pupils according to stereotypes [1], which can lead to a self-fulfilling prophecy [1];
**policing** [1] — stereotypes about young men from some groups [1] can lead to them being stopped and
searched more often [1]; **othering** [1] — stereotyped groups may be seen as different and inferior
[1], leading to exclusion or hostility [1].

**A9** [4 marks]

A subculture is a group within a wider society [1] whose members share some norms and values that
differ from the mainstream [1], while still sharing much of the wider culture [1]. For example, a
youth music subculture with its own styles of dress and language [1].

**A10** [6 marks]

The sociological imagination asks us to link individual lives to wider social structures [1]. One
student leaving early may be a personal trouble [1], but high rates across a society suggest a public
issue [1]. Social causes might include poverty and the need for young people to earn [1], the cost of
fees or uniforms, or long distances to secondary schools [1], or gender norms that expect girls to
marry early or do domestic work [1]. Accept other relevant structural causes. Maximum 6.

**A11** [12 marks — levels]

| Level | Marks | Descriptor |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | 10–12 | Detailed knowledge; well applied to a named society; balanced discussion with a clear, justified conclusion |
| 3 | 7–9 | Good knowledge; some application; discussion with a conclusion |
| 2 | 4–6 | Some relevant knowledge; limited application or one-sided |
| 1 | 1–3 | Basic, mainly descriptive points |

Indicative content:

- **Ascribed status shapes life chances:** caste or class of birth affecting education and
  occupation; sex and gender norms limiting women's access to some jobs; ethnicity and racism in
  employment; inherited wealth.
- **Limits to this view:** achieved status through education and work; social mobility; laws against
  discrimination; individuals negotiating norms (interactionist view).
- **Sociological imagination:** patterns affecting many people with the same ascribed status suggest
  public issues rather than personal troubles.
- **Application:** a named society the candidate knows, for example changing opportunities for women
  in its labour market.
- **Judgement:** for example, ascribed status still shapes life chances strongly, but its influence
  varies between societies and can be reduced by policy.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Swapping the definitions of norms and values.
- Confusing status and role.
- Naming Mills without explaining personal troubles and public issues.
- Describing a subculture as completely separate from society.
- An extended answer with no named society.
