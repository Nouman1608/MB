OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics (J205) is untiered and linear, with two compulsory components of equal size:
Component 01 (Introduction to economics) and Component 02 (National and international economics), each
1 hour 30 minutes, 80 marks and 50%. Unusually, OCR's own specification-at-a-glance page records that
**both components include synoptic assessment** -- not just the second one. These notes complement the
site's guides to
[Introduction to Economics](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-introduction-to-economics/) and
[The Role of Markets and Money](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-the-role-of-markets-and-money/).

## Synoptic assessment on both papers means the whole course is live on both days

This is the distinctive feature of J205's assessment design. Neither paper can be treated as testing
only its own content list. **Exam-preparation priority**: revise the specification as one connected
body rather than as two halves to be prepared in sequence. In practice this means being able to move
between micro and macro reasoning -- explaining, for instance, how a change in a single market
connects to employment or prices more broadly -- because that connection is what synoptic questions
ask for.

## Eighty marks in ninety minutes rewards short answers being short

At about 1.1 minutes per mark, an over-long answer to a two-mark definition is directly funded by the
extended question at the end. **Exam-preparation priority**: rehearse matching answer length to
tariff. A definition question wants a definition, not a definition plus an example plus a diagram.

## Diagrams are a fast way to earn marks -- if they are labelled

Demand and supply diagrams, and their macroeconomic equivalents, can carry several marks and take less
time than the equivalent prose. **Exam-preparation priority**: practise drawing them quickly with axes
labelled, curves labelled, the shift or movement clearly shown with an arrow, and the new equilibrium
marked. An unlabelled diagram earns little; a labelled one referred to in the text ("as shown by the
shift from D1 to D2") earns well.

## The shift-versus-movement distinction recurs on both papers

Confusing a shift of a curve with a movement along it is the single most common analytical error at
this level, and it appears in both micro and macro contexts. **Exam-preparation priority**: drill the
rule until it is automatic -- only a change in the good's own price moves you along the curve;
anything else shifts it. This one distinction protects marks across a large share of the
specification.

## Extended questions want a chain, then a judgement

The higher-tariff questions reward reasoning that moves in linked steps and finishes with a supported
view. **Exam-preparation priority**: practise the chain explicitly, and practise finishing.
Presenting two effects and stopping is the most common way to lose the final marks on an eight-mark
question.

## Worked routine: a chain-of-reasoning answer

The routine below is an original model written for this resource, not a reproduction of any official
past paper or mark scheme.

```
Question type: "Explain the likely effect of [a change] on
[a market or the economy]."

Step 1 - identify precisely what has changed:
Is it a price, an income, a cost, a tax, an interest rate? Naming
it correctly determines everything downstream.

Step 2 - state the first effect, with its mechanism:
"Higher interest rates raise the cost of borrowing, so households
with variable-rate debt have less disposable income."

Step 3 - state the second effect, following from the first:
"With less disposable income, consumer spending falls..."

Step 4 - state the outcome in the market or economy:
"...which reduces aggregate demand, putting downward pressure on
prices and on output."

Step 5 - add the qualification that earns evaluation marks:
"The size of this effect depends on how many households hold
variable-rate debt and on how quickly spending responds."
```

Each step names the mechanism rather than assuming it. An answer that jumps from step 1 to step 4 has
the right conclusion and few of the marks.

## Before/during exam checklist

- **Before the exams**: revise the specification as one connected body, since both components are
  synoptic; drill the shift-versus-movement distinction; practise drawing labelled diagrams quickly;
  rehearse matching answer length to mark tariff.
- **During either component**: keep low-tariff answers short to protect time for the extended
  questions.
- **In every diagram**: label axes and curves, show the shift or movement with an arrow, mark the new
  equilibrium, and refer to the diagram in the prose.
- **In every extended answer**: build the chain link by link, then finish with a judgement and the
  factor it depends on.

## Self-test

1. What is unusual about synoptic assessment on this specification?
2. How much time is available per mark, and what should follow?
3. What is the shift-versus-movement rule?
4. What is typically missing from an incomplete eight-mark answer?

**Answers:** 1. Both components include synoptic assessment, not just the second -- so the whole
specification is examinable on both papers and should be revised as one connected body. 2. About 1.1
minutes per mark (90 minutes for 80 marks), so answers to low-tariff questions must be kept short to
fund the extended ones. 3. Only a change in the good's own price produces a movement along the curve;
any other change -- income, costs, related goods, expectations -- shifts the curve. 4. The final
judgement and the qualification: what the answer depends on, stated explicitly rather than left
implied.
