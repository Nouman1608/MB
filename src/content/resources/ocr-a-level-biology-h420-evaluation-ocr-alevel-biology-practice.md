These questions cover learning outcomes 1.1.4(a) to (e) of the OCR A Level Biology A specification (H420), Version 4.1 (April 2026), first assessment 2025. Questions 2, 4 and 5 practise calculations linked to this section. All contexts and data are invented for practice. Where percentage error is asked for, use percentage error = 2 × absolute uncertainty ÷ quantity measured × 100 %, taking the absolute uncertainty as half the smallest division.

Related: [evaluation study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-biology-h420-evaluation-ocr-alevel-biology/) and [evaluation revision notes](/resources/ocr-a-level-biology-h420-evaluation-ocr-alevel-biology-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** A student measures the volume of oxygen released by a catalase reaction in 2 minutes. Three repeats at 30 °C give 8.0, 8.5 and 12.5 cm³. Identify the anomalous result and suggest **one** possible cause. **[2]**

**2.** Calculate the mean volume in question 1, first with and then without the anomalous result. State which mean should be used and why. **[3]**

**3.** Explain the difference between the precision and the accuracy of a set of measurements. **[2]**

**4.** A measuring cylinder with 1 cm³ divisions is used to measure 8 cm³ of solution.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage error. **[2]**

**(b)** A syringe reading to 0.1 cm³ is used to measure the same volume. Calculate the percentage error and state what the comparison shows. **[2]**

**5.** A potato cylinder is weighed on a balance reading to 0.01 g before and after being left in a solution. Its mass is 6.40 g before and 5.92 g after.

**(a)** Calculate the change in mass and its uncertainty. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage error in the change in mass. **[1]**

**6.** A thermometer in a water bath always reads 2 °C higher than the true temperature. State the type of error and explain why taking repeat readings does not remove it. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** The table shows the mean rate of an enzyme reaction, with the standard deviation of three repeats, at four temperatures.

| Temperature / °C | Mean rate / cm³ min⁻¹ | Standard deviation |
|---|---|---|
| 20 | 4.2 | 0.1 |
| 30 | 8.2 | 0.2 |
| 40 | 16.2 | 0.3 |
| 50 | 10.7 | 0.3 |

Evaluate how far the data support the conclusion that the optimum temperature of the enzyme is 40 °C. **[4]**

**8.** A student investigates the effect of temperature on the time taken for the colour of a solution to disappear. Tubes are placed in a beaker of water heated on a hotplate. The student decides when the colour has gone by looking at the tube and times it with a stopwatch. Only one tube is used at each of three temperatures.

Identify **three** limitations of this method and explain the effect of each on the results. **[6]**

**9.** For each of the following weaknesses, suggest a specific improvement.

**(a)** Gas is collected over water in a measuring cylinder that has 1 cm³ divisions, and the volumes are small. **[1]**

**(b)** The end point is judged by eye. **[1]**

**(c)** The tissue pieces are cut with a knife and vary in size. **[1]**

**(d)** The optimum lies somewhere between 30 and 50 °C but only 30, 40 and 50 °C were tested. **[1]**

**10.** A student concludes from an investigation at 20, 30 and 40 °C that "enzyme activity keeps increasing as temperature rises". Evaluate this conclusion. **[2]**

**11.** Explain why measuring a larger change in mass reduces the percentage error in the change. **[2]**

**12.** A student states that the results of an experiment are "reliable because the experiment was repeated three times". Evaluate this statement. **[2]**

## Answers

**How these answers work.** Marking points are shown in brackets. The mark total for each question is the sum of the points. Alternative wording that carries the same meaning is credited.

**1.** 12.5 cm³ is the anomalous result, because it is far from the other two [1]. Any one cause [1]: an air bubble in the syringe; a timing error; a different piece of tissue; extra enzyme or substrate added; the temperature of the water bath was higher. **[2]**

**2.** With the anomaly: (8.0 + 8.5 + 12.5) ÷ 3 = **9.7 cm³** [1]. Without it: (8.0 + 8.5) ÷ 2 = **8.25 cm³** [1]. The mean without the anomaly should be used, because the anomalous value does not fit the pattern and probably has a cause, or the measurement should be repeated [1]. **[3]**

**3.** Precision is how close repeated measurements are to one another [1]. Accuracy is how close a measurement is to the true value [1]. **[2]**

**4. (a)** Absolute uncertainty = ±0.5 cm³ [1]. Percentage error = 2 × 0.5 ÷ 8 × 100 = **12.5 %** [1].

**(b)** Absolute uncertainty = ±0.05 cm³; percentage error = 2 × 0.05 ÷ 8 × 100 = **1.25 %** [1]. The syringe has a smaller percentage error, so it gives a more precise volume [1]. **[4]**

**5. (a)** Change = 6.40 − 5.92 = **0.48 g** [1]. Uncertainty = 0.005 + 0.005 = **±0.01 g** [1].

**(b)** 0.01 ÷ 0.48 × 100 = **2.1 %** [1]. **[3]**

**6.** A systematic error [1]. Every reading is shifted by the same amount in the same direction, so repeating and taking a mean gives the same shift; the thermometer must be calibrated or replaced [1]. **[2]**

**7.** Any four [1 each]: the mean rate increases from 4.2 to 16.2 cm³ min⁻¹ between 20 and 40 °C and then falls to 10.7 at 50 °C, so the highest rate is at 40 °C [1]; the standard deviations are small (0.1 to 0.3) compared with the differences between the means, so the repeats are precise and the differences are probably real [1]; the temperatures are 10 °C apart, so the true optimum could be anywhere between 30 and 50 °C [1]; so the data support a peak near 40 °C but do not prove 40 °C exactly [1]; more temperatures, such as 35 and 45 °C, are needed [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Any three limitations, each with an effect [1 + 1]. Water on a hotplate: the temperature drifts, so the reaction is not carried out at the stated temperature [2]. The end point is judged by eye: it is subjective, so times vary between observers and repeats [2]. One tube at each temperature: there are no repeats, so no mean can be calculated and anomalies cannot be identified [2]. Only three temperatures: a trend and an optimum cannot be located [2]. The tubes were not pre-warmed or the temperature was not monitored: the reaction starts at the wrong temperature [2]. **[6]**

**9. (a)** Use a gas syringe, which reads to 0.1 cm³ [1]. **(b)** Use a colorimeter to measure absorbance against time, or compare the colour with a standard colour chart [1]. **(c)** Use a cork borer and a ruler, or cut pieces to the same mass or length [1]. **(d)** Add temperatures at 35 and 45 °C, in a thermostatically controlled water bath [1]. **[4]**

**10.** The data cover only 20 to 40 °C, so the conclusion cannot be extended to higher temperatures [1]. Above the optimum, enzymes denature and the rate falls, so the statement is not true for all temperatures [1]. **[2]**

**11.** The uncertainty of each balance reading stays the same, so the uncertainty in the change is the same [1]. A larger change makes the percentage error (uncertainty ÷ change) smaller [1]. **[2]**

**12.** Repeating three times allows a mean to be calculated and anomalies to be seen [1], but "reliable" needs the repeats to agree closely (low standard deviation) and the method to be valid; three repeats do not remove a systematic error [1]. **[2]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Question 1: stating a cause but not identifying the result.
- Question 2: removing the anomaly without saying why.
- Question 3: mixing up precision and accuracy.
- Question 4: forgetting the factor of 2 in the formula, or using the full division as the uncertainty.
- Question 5: forgetting that the uncertainties of two readings add.
- Question 7: stating the optimum is exactly 40 °C.
- Question 8: giving a limitation with no effect.
- Question 9: suggesting "use a better measuring device" without naming it.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I have to use the formula with the factor of 2 for percentage error?**
The specification gives that form. Use it in questions that ask for a percentage error, and show the substitution.

**How many limitations should I give?**
As many as the marks allow. A six-mark question usually wants three limitations, each with an effect.
