## Questions

**1.** Distinguish between production and productivity. **[4]**

**2.** Cranmoor Printing made 14,400 leaflets in a week with 18 workers. After it bought a faster press it made 16,800 leaflets in a week with 16 workers.

**(a)** Calculate labour productivity before the new press. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate labour productivity after the new press. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage change in labour productivity, to one decimal place. **[2]**

**3.** Starlingdale Tyres makes car tyres and keeps a large store of finished tyres.

**(a)** State **two** reasons why a business holds inventories. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain **one** cost to Starlingdale Tyres of holding too many inventories. **[3]**

**4.** Greystoke Fabrics wants to raise efficiency. Explain **one** way that improving the skills of its weaving staff could increase efficiency, and **one** benefit to the business of becoming more efficient. **[4]**

**5.** Bramblewick Bakery plans to use just-in-time (JIT) inventory control for flour and butter.

Analyse **one** benefit and **one** risk to Bramblewick Bakery of using JIT. **[6]**

**6.** Lanternfield Lighting assembles table lamps in teams of eight.

**(a)** State what is meant by Kaizen. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain **one** benefit to Lanternfield Lighting of using Kaizen. **[3]**

**7.** Ivywood Furniture makes wardrobes to fit the odd-shaped bedrooms of individual customers.

**(a)** State **two** features of job production. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain **one** limitation of job production for Ivywood Furniture. **[3]**

**8.** Pebblebrook Dairy fills yoghurt pots on a flow production line. The line runs for 24 hours a day and fills 1,500 pots every hour. Total costs of running the line are 5,040 dollars a day.

**(a)** Calculate the daily output of the line. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the average cost per pot, in dollars. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain **one** limitation of flow production for Pebblebrook Dairy. **[3]**

**9.** Quarryhill Stoneworks makes concrete paving slabs in three designs. Each week customers order about 200 to 300 slabs of each design. Changing a mould between designs takes one hour. The owners want to keep prices low but cannot afford a fully automated line.

Recommend and justify the most suitable method of production for Quarryhill Stoneworks. **[8]**

**10.** Beaconsfield Pens is a small business that designs and makes fountain pens. It is considering using computers in design and manufacturing.

Explain **two** ways that this technology could change how Beaconsfield Pens produces its pens. **[6]**

**11.** Redwater Bikes has 40 workers who make 2,000 bicycles a month. It is considering two changes.

- Robot welders would raise output to 2,300 bicycles a month with 38 workers.
- Just-in-time (JIT) delivery of components would cut the value of components held in stock from 60,000 dollars to 15,000 dollars. The firm estimates that holding stock costs 2% of its value each month. One delivery in ten from its main supplier is currently late.

**(a)** Calculate the labour productivity before and after the robot welders, and the percentage change, to one decimal place. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the monthly saving in holding costs if the firm adopts JIT. **[2]**
**(c)** Evaluate whether Redwater Bikes should adopt JIT. **[5]**

## Answers

**1.** Production is the total output of goods or services that a business makes in a period, by managing its resources [1]. Productivity measures output in relation to the resources used, for example output per worker [1]. For example, a factory that makes 500 units with 10 workers has production of 500 units and productivity of 50 units per worker [1]. The two can move differently: production can rise because more workers were hired while productivity stays the same [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Two separate definitions often earn less than answers that also contrast the terms, so add a short example.

**2. (a)** Labour productivity = output / workers = 14,400 / 18 [1] = **800 leaflets per worker per week** [1].
**(b)** 16,800 / 16 [1] = **1,050 leaflets per worker per week** [1].
**(c)** Change = (1,050 - 800) / 800 x 100 [1] = **31.3%** increase [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the division and give units. Use the old figure as the base for the percentage.

**3. (a)** Any two reasons, one mark each, such as: to keep production going if a supplier is late; to meet a sudden rise in demand; to supply customers straight away; to buy in bulk at a lower price; to prepare for seasonal demand [1][1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Costs include storage space and insurance [1]. Tyres are bulky, so a large store needs a big warehouse, which raises rent and heating bills [1]. Money spent on tyres that are not yet sold is tied up and cannot be used elsewhere, which can strain cash flow [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A cost named without development, such as "it costs money", tends to gain little. Link it to the product, here bulky tyres.

**4.** Trained weavers can run the looms faster and make fewer mistakes [1], so output per worker rises and less cloth is wasted [1]. A benefit of greater efficiency is a lower average cost per metre of fabric [1], which lets Greystoke cut its price to win customers or keep a higher profit margin [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Connect the way to the benefit with words such as "so" or "which means" to show the chain.

**5.** Benefit: with JIT, flour and butter arrive just as they are needed, so little needs storing [1]. This lowers holding costs and means fewer ingredients go stale and are thrown away [1]. Lower waste and costs raise profit, or allow lower prices [1]. Risk: Bramblewick relies completely on its suppliers [1]. If a delivery of flour is late, the ovens cannot run and bread cannot be baked [1], so sales are lost and customers may go elsewhere [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Analyse means break a point into cause and effect. A list such as "less waste, lower cost" without saying why JIT leads to it usually stays at description.

**6. (a)** Kaizen is continuous improvement [1] made up of many small changes, usually suggested by the workers themselves [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The assembly teams can suggest small changes, such as moving tools closer to the bench [1]. This reduces wasted movement and time, so each lamp is assembled faster [1]. Faster assembly lowers cost per lamp, and workers who help shape the process may feel more involved and motivated [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Applying the idea to the lamps or the teams shows application. A generic claim that Kaizen "improves quality" could fit any firm.

**7. (a)** Any two features, one mark each, such as: each item is made individually; made to a customer's order; skilled workers are used; each product is different; the price is usually high [1][1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Job production is slow and labour intensive [1]. Each wardrobe has to be measured, cut and fitted separately, so skilled workers spend a long time on one item [1]. This raises the cost per wardrobe, so Ivywood must charge a high price, which may limit the number of customers it can attract [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Choose a limitation that fits the business, and still say why it arises.

**8. (a)** Daily output = 1,500 x 24 [1] = **36,000 pots** [1].
**(b)** Average cost = total cost / output = 5,040 / 36,000 [1] = **0.14 dollars per pot** [1].
**(c)** Flow production is inflexible [1]. The line is built for one standard pot, so changing size or recipe means stopping and altering the machinery [1]. If one machine breaks down the whole line stops, so output of all 1,500 pots an hour is lost until it is fixed [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Write the formula before substituting. In (c), say what happens at Pebblebrook because of the limitation.

**9.** Recommendation: batch production [1].

Justification: orders are for lots of 200 to 300 identical slabs of each of three designs [1], which suits making one design as a batch and then switching moulds [1].

Job production would make each slab separately, which is too slow and gives a high cost per slab [1], and that conflicts with the aim of low prices [1].

Flow production gives the lowest unit cost, but it needs a large, automated line that the owners cannot afford [1]. Demand of a few hundred slabs per design is also modest, so such a line would be idle much of the time [1].

The drawback of batch is the hour lost at each mould change, but with three designs this is a small share of the working week, and I judge the cost of lost time less than the cost of the alternatives [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* A strong recommendation uses figures from the case and compares with at least one alternative.

**10.** First way: computer-aided design (CAD) lets designers draw and test new pens on screen [1]. Changes can be made quickly without making a physical model each time, so development takes less time [1] and costs less, which helps a small business launch new designs [1].
Second way: computer-aided manufacturing (CAM) means computers control the machines that shape the pens [1]. Each pen is made to the same accurate size, so there are fewer defects [1], and the machines can run overnight, so output rises and unit cost falls [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* "Two ways" means two separate points, each developed.

**11. (a)** Before: 2,000 / 40 = 50 bicycles per worker [1]. After: 2,300 / 38 = 60.5 bicycles per worker [1]. Change = (60.5 - 50) / 50 x 100 = 21.0% increase [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Current cost = 2% of 60,000 = 1,200 dollars per month; JIT cost = 2% of 15,000 = 300 dollars per month [1]. Saving = **900 dollars per month** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** In favour: JIT saves 900 dollars a month in holding costs and frees up cash that was tied up in components [1]. It also reduces the storage space needed [1]. Against: one delivery in ten is already late, and with little stock a late delivery would stop the assembly line [1]. The loss of output from a stopped line, with 2,000 bicycles a month at stake, could be far larger than the 900-dollar saving [1]. Judgement: Redwater should not adopt JIT yet, or should do so only after it has a more reliable supplier or agrees a small safety stock, because the saving is small compared with the risk [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* An evaluation needs a decision and a reason that weighs both sides, using the figures given.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving productivity as a total instead of per worker.
- Dividing a percentage change by the later figure instead of the starting one.
- Describing JIT as "no stock at all" and ignoring the dependence on suppliers.
- Writing "Kaizen improves quality" with no mention of small, continuous changes.
- Recommending a method of production without comparing it with an alternative.
- Writing one-sided evaluations with no final judgement.
- Giving a single developed point when two ways or two reasons are requested.
