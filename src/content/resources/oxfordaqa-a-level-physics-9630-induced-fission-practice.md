These questions cover section 3.12.3, Induced fission, of OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630), which is International A-level only content. Learn the content first with the [induced fission study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-induced-fission/) and the [induced fission revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-induced-fission-revision-notes/). Use 1 MeV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ J and 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg. All collisions are head-on and elastic unless stated otherwise.

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by induced fission. **[2]**

**2.** State what is meant by a thermal neutron and why a thermal reactor needs such neutrons. **[2]**

**3.** Uranium-235 undergoes fission: ²³⁵₉₂U + ¹₀n → ⁹⁰₃₈Sr + ¹⁴³₅₄Xe + x ¹₀n. Find x and show that charge is conserved. **[2]**

**4.** Define critical mass. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why a moderator should be made of a material with light nuclei. **[2]**

**6.** Distinguish between the functions of the moderator and the control rods. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** A neutron of mass m and speed u collides with a stationary deuterium nucleus of mass 2m.

**(a)** Using conservation of momentum and kinetic energy, show that the neutron's velocity after the collision is −u/3. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the fraction of its kinetic energy that the neutron keeps. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the minimum number of such collisions needed to reduce a 1.0 MeV neutron to below 1.0 eV. **[2]**

**(d)** Explain why the actual number of collisions needed is larger. **[1]**

**8.** In a reactor each fission releases on average 2.4 neutrons. Of these, 0.30 escape from the core, 0.55 are captured by uranium-238 without causing fission and 0.20 are absorbed by the moderator and coolant. The rest are absorbed by the control rods or cause further fission.

**(a)** Calculate the number of neutrons per fission that the control rods must absorb for the reactor to operate at a steady power. **[2]**

**(b)** The control rods are raised so that they absorb 0.30 neutrons per fission. Calculate the number of neutrons per fission that now cause further fission. **[1]**

**(c)** Describe and explain what happens to the power of the reactor over successive generations of fission after this change. **[2]**

**9.** **(a)** Explain why a small piece of uranium-235 cannot sustain a chain reaction. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why a sphere of uranium-235 has a smaller critical mass than a thin sheet of the same material. **[2]**

**(c)** State one other factor that affects the critical mass. **[1]**

**10.** A thermal reactor produces 2.5 GW of thermal power. Each fission of uranium-235 releases 200 MeV.

**(a)** Calculate the number of fissions each second. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the mass of uranium-235 that undergoes fission in one year (3.16 × 10⁷ s). **[2]**

**(c)** The power station is 33% efficient. Calculate its electrical power output. **[1]**

**11.** **(a)** State two properties required of a control rod material and name a suitable material. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why a reactor moderated by ordinary water needs fuel enriched in uranium-235, while one moderated by heavy water does not. **[2]**

**(c)** State two properties required of a coolant. **[2]**

**12.** A student models moderation on an air track. Glider A of mass 0.20 kg moves at 0.60 m s⁻¹ and collides elastically with a stationary glider B.

**(a)** Glider B also has mass 0.20 kg. State the velocities of both gliders after the collision. **[2]**

**(b)** Glider B is replaced by one of mass 2.4 kg. Calculate the velocities of both gliders after the collision. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain how the model shows what makes a good moderator. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** A nucleus (such as uranium-235) absorbs a neutron and becomes unstable [1]; it splits into two smaller nuclei, releasing two or three neutrons and energy [1] **[2]**

**2.** A neutron whose kinetic energy has been reduced to the same order as the thermal energy of its surroundings (about 0.03 eV) [1]; uranium-235 is much more likely to undergo fission when it absorbs a slow neutron than a fast one [1] **[2]**

**3.** 236 = 90 + 143 + x, so x = 3 [1]; protons: 38 + 54 = 92, equal to the proton number of uranium [1] **[2]**

**4.** The minimum mass of fissile material [1] in which a chain reaction can sustain itself [1] **[2]**

**5.** In an elastic collision the share of kinetic energy passed to the target is largest when its mass is close to that of the neutron [1], so light nuclei slow neutrons in fewer collisions; a heavy nucleus makes the neutron bounce back with nearly the same speed [1] **[2]**

**6.** The moderator slows neutrons down so they are more likely to cause fission [1]; control rods absorb neutrons to reduce or control the rate of fission [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* A moderator does not absorb neutrons; good moderators are chosen precisely because they absorb very few.

**7. (a)** Momentum: mu = mv + 2mV; kinetic energy (or relative speed): u = V − v [1]; solving gives v = u(m − 2m)/(m + 2m) = −u/3 [1]

**(b)** (1/3)² = 1/9 [1]

**(c)** (1/9)ⁿ ≤ 1.0 eV/1.0 MeV = 10⁻⁶, so n ≥ ln(10⁶)/ln 9 = 6.3 [1]; minimum 7 collisions [1]

**(d)** Most collisions are glancing, not head-on, so less energy is lost in each [1] **[6]**

**8. (a)** For steady power exactly 1.00 neutron per fission must cause another fission [1]; rods absorb 2.4 − 1.00 − 0.30 − 0.55 − 0.20 = 0.35 [1]

**(b)** 2.4 − 0.30 − 0.55 − 0.20 − 0.30 = 1.05 [1]

**(c)** Each generation has 5% more fissions than the one before [1], so the power rises steadily (the reactor is supercritical) until the control rods are lowered again [1] **[5]**

**9. (a)** Neutrons are produced throughout the volume but escape through the surface [1]; a small piece has a large surface area compared with its volume, so most neutrons escape before causing another fission [1]

**(b)** A sphere has the smallest surface area for a given volume [1], so a smaller fraction of its neutrons escapes and a chain reaction can be sustained with less material [1]

**(c)** Any one: enrichment (proportion of uranium-235); density; presence of a moderator [1] **[5]**

**10. (a)** Energy per fission = 200 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ = 3.2 × 10⁻¹¹ J [1]; fissions per second = 2.5 × 10⁹/3.2 × 10⁻¹¹ = 7.8 × 10¹⁹ [1]

**(b)** Fissions per year = 7.81 × 10¹⁹ × 3.16 × 10⁷ = 2.47 × 10²⁷ [1]; mass = 2.47 × 10²⁷ × 235 × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ = 9.6 × 10² kg [1]

**(c)** 0.33 × 2.5 = 0.83 GW [1] **[5]**

**11. (a)** It absorbs neutrons strongly; it keeps its strength and shape at high temperature (or resists radiation damage) [1]; boron or cadmium [1]

**(b)** Hydrogen-1 nuclei in ordinary water absorb some neutrons, so fewer are left to cause fission [1]; enriching the fuel in uranium-235 makes fission more likely and keeps the reaction critical, whereas heavy water absorbs so few neutrons that natural uranium is enough [1]

**(c)** Any two: high specific heat capacity; absorbs few neutrons; easy to pump (low viscosity); does not corrode the circuit [1] [1] **[6]**

**12. (a)** A stops (0 m s⁻¹) [1]; B moves off at 0.60 m s⁻¹ [1]

**(b)** v_A = 0.60 × (0.20 − 2.4)/(0.20 + 2.4) = −0.51 m s⁻¹, so A rebounds [1]; v_B = 2 × 0.20 × 0.60/2.6 = 0.092 m s⁻¹ [1]

**(c)** When the target has the same mass, all the kinetic energy is transferred in one collision; when it is much heavier, almost none is [1]; so a moderator should have nuclei with mass close to that of a neutron, that is, light nuclei [1] **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* Link the model back to neutrons explicitly; describing the gliders alone does not answer "explain how the model shows".

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying the moderator absorbs neutrons or slows the reaction.
- Forgetting the incoming neutron when balancing a fission equation.
- Giving a rebounding neutron a positive velocity.
- Treating critical mass as the mass needed for an explosion.
- Listing a coolant's properties without relating them to removing energy.
