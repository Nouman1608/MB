This guide covers topic 3.2 **Monetary policy** in Implementing policy, Component 02, Macroeconomics, of the OCR *AS Level Economics (H060)* specification, Version 3.0 (June 2026). The specification asks you to explain, with the aid of a diagram, changes in interest rates, changes in the money supply, inflation rate targets, quantitative easing and the influence of exchange rates, and to evaluate the effectiveness of using monetary policy to achieve the government's macroeconomic objectives. Examples with households, firms and currencies use invented figures.

Use it with the [monetary policy revision notes](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-monetary-policy-revision-notes/) and the [monetary policy practice questions](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-monetary-policy-practice/). The A Level treatment is in [monetary policy and inflation targeting](/resources/ocr-a-level-economics-monetary-policy-and-inflation-targeting/), and the alternative demand-side tool is in [fiscal policy](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-fiscal-policy/).

## 1. What monetary policy is and who runs it

**Monetary policy** is the use of interest rates, the money supply and related tools to influence aggregate demand, mainly to control inflation. In the UK it is run by the **Bank of England**, which has had operational independence to set interest rates since 1997. Decisions are taken by the nine-member **Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)**.

The main instrument is **Bank Rate**, the interest rate the Bank of England pays on reserves that commercial banks hold with it. Changes in Bank Rate feed through to the rates that banks charge and pay their customers.

## 2. Inflation rate targets

The government sets the target; the Bank decides how to meet it. The UK target is **2% for CPI inflation**. If inflation is more than one percentage point away from the target in either direction, the Governor must write an open letter to the Chancellor explaining why and what the Bank will do.

Why have a target?

- **Anchoring expectations.** If households and firms believe inflation will be about 2%, they set wages and prices accordingly, which helps keep inflation at 2%.
- **Transparency and accountability.** A public, numerical target makes it clear whether the Bank is succeeding.
- **Credibility.** An independent central bank is less tempted than a government to cut rates before an election.

Because changes in interest rates take time to affect inflation, the Bank aims at forecast inflation over the next couple of years, not today's figure.

## 3. Changes in interest rates: the transmission mechanism

A change in Bank Rate works through several channels. Take a **rise** in Bank Rate.

1. **Borrowing costs.** Mortgage, loan and overdraft rates rise. Households with variable-rate mortgages have less disposable income, and borrowing for cars and holidays becomes dearer, so consumption falls.
2. **Saving.** Higher rates reward saving, so households save more and spend less.
3. **Investment.** The cost of borrowing for capital projects rises, and some projects are no longer profitable, so investment falls.
4. **Asset prices.** Higher rates tend to lower house and share prices, reducing household wealth and confidence.
5. **Exchange rate.** Higher returns attract financial inflows, raising demand for the currency. An appreciation makes exports dearer and imports cheaper, reducing net exports.

Each channel reduces a component of AD, so **AD shifts left**. On an AD/AS diagram, the price level and real output both fall relative to what they would otherwise have been. A cut in Bank Rate works the other way, shifting AD right.

**Worked example 1: a household.** A family has a 200,000 variable-rate, interest-only mortgage. When its rate rises from 4% to 5%, annual interest rises from 8,000 to 10,000, about 167 a month. Their disposable income falls by that amount, so they cut other spending.

**Worked example 2: a firm.** A firm expects a new machine to earn a return of 6% a year. If it can borrow at 5%, the project is profitable. If the borrowing rate rises to 7%, the cost exceeds the return and the project is shelved. Across the economy, many such decisions reduce investment.

## 4. Changes in the money supply

The **money supply** is the total stock of money in the economy, mostly bank deposits. Draw the money market with the **interest rate** on the vertical axis and the **quantity of money** on the horizontal axis.

- The **demand for money** slopes downwards: at lower interest rates, holding money instead of interest-bearing assets costs less.
- The **supply of money** is drawn as vertical, set by the central bank and the banking system.

If the money supply increases, the supply curve shifts right and the equilibrium interest rate falls. Lower interest rates raise borrowing and spending, so AD shifts right. If the money supply grows much faster than output for a long time, the result is mainly inflation.

## 5. Quantitative easing

**Quantitative easing (QE)** is the creation of new central bank money to buy financial assets, mainly government bonds, from financial institutions. The Bank of England first used QE in March 2009, when Bank Rate was already close to zero and could not usefully be cut further.

How QE works:

1. The central bank buys bonds, which pushes up their prices and lowers their yields, so **long-term interest rates fall**.
2. The sellers receive new money, which they may use to buy other assets such as shares or corporate bonds, raising **asset prices** and wealth.
3. Lower long-term borrowing costs and higher wealth encourage **consumption and investment**, shifting AD right.
4. It can also signal that interest rates will stay low, supporting **confidence**.

The reverse, **quantitative tightening**, means selling the bonds or not replacing them when they mature, which tends to raise long-term rates.

## 6. The influence of exchange rates

Exchange rates link monetary policy to trade.

**Worked example 3.** The pound appreciates from 1 pound = 1.25 dollars to 1 pound = 1.30 dollars, a rise of 4%.

- A UK export priced at 40 pounds rises in dollar terms from 50 to **52** dollars, so US buyers buy fewer.
- A US import priced at 65 dollars falls in pound terms from 52 to **50** pounds, so UK buyers buy more.
- Net exports fall, reducing AD. Cheaper imports also lower costs for UK firms and shoppers, reducing inflation directly.

So a rise in interest rates that causes an appreciation lowers inflation in two ways: weaker AD and cheaper imports. Exporters and import-competing industries bear much of the cost, which can widen the current account deficit.

## 7. Evaluating the effectiveness of monetary policy

**Strengths**

- Decisions can be made and changed quickly, at regular meetings, without legislation.
- Independence and a clear target support credibility and anchor expectations.
- It works well against **demand-pull** inflation.

**Limitations**

- **Time lags.** Changes in interest rates take a year or more to have their full effect, so policy relies on forecasts that may be wrong.
- **Uncertain strength.** If many mortgages are on fixed rates, the effect of a rate rise is delayed. If confidence is very low, rate cuts may not persuade people to borrow and spend.
- **The lower limit.** Once rates are near zero, they cannot be cut much further, which is why QE was introduced.
- **Cost-push inflation.** If inflation comes from higher energy prices, raising rates reduces demand and output without addressing the cause, and risks higher unemployment.
- **A blunt instrument.** One interest rate applies to the whole country, whatever the conditions in each region or sector.
- **Side effects.** Low rates and QE raise asset prices, benefiting those who own property and shares; high rates hurt borrowers and exporters.
- **Conflicts.** Higher rates may reduce inflation but slow growth and raise unemployment.

## Common mistakes

- Saying the government sets interest rates in the UK: it sets the target, the MPC sets Bank Rate.
- Describing QE as "printing banknotes": it creates central bank reserves to buy assets.
- Forgetting the exchange rate channel.
- Claiming a rate rise lowers inflation immediately.
- Using the money market diagram without labelling the axes (interest rate, quantity of money).
- Treating monetary policy as equally effective against cost-push and demand-pull inflation.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** a rate change as a chain through one or two named channels to a component of AD, then to the price level and output.
- **Explain, with the aid of a diagram:** shift AD on an AD/AS diagram, or shift money supply on a money market diagram, labelling axes and equilibria.
- **Evaluate** using lags, the type of inflation, the strength of each channel, side effects and conflicts, and conclude on effectiveness for the objective named in the question.
