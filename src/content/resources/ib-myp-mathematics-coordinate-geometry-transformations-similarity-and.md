## Coordinates, y = mx + c and parallel lines

A point is written (x, y): across first, then up. Gradient means vertical change over horizontal change. For a line y = mx + c, the gradient is m and the line meets the y-axis at (0, c), the y-intercept. To find the x-intercept, set y = 0 and solve.

Lines are parallel exactly when their gradients are equal.

**Worked example 1.** A line passes through A(−2, 5) and B(4, −7).

1. Gradient m = (−7 − 5)/(4 − (−2)) = −12/6 = −2.
2. Substitute A into y = −2x + c: 5 = −2(−2) + c, so 5 = 4 + c and c = 1.
3. The line is y = −2x + 1. Check with B: −2(4) + 1 = −7.
4. For the x-intercept, 0 = −2x + 1, so x = 1/2.
5. A line parallel to this one through (3, 4) also has m = −2. Then 4 = −6 + c, so c = 10 and the line is y = −2x + 10.

If a line is given as 6x + 2y = 10, rearrange it to y = −3x + 5 before reading the gradient.

## Distance, midpoint and gradient formulae

For P(x₁, y₁) and Q(x₂, y₂):

- distance PQ = √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²]
- midpoint = ((x₁ + x₂)/2, (y₁ + y₂)/2)
- gradient = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁)

**Worked example 2.** P(−3, 2) and Q(5, 8).

1. Horizontal change 8, vertical change 6.
2. PQ = √(8² + 6²) = √100 = 10.
3. Midpoint = ((−3 + 5)/2, (2 + 8)/2) = (1, 5).
4. Gradient = 6/8 = 3/4.

**Worked example 3 (classifying a quadrilateral).** A(1, 1), B(5, 2), C(6, 6), D(2, 5).

1. AB² = 4² + 1² = 17, BC² = 1² + 4² = 17, CD² = 4² + 1² = 17, DA² = 1² + 4² = 17. All four sides are √17 long.
2. Gradient of AB is 1/4 and of DC is (6 − 5)/(6 − 2) = 1/4. Gradient of BC is 4 and of AD is 4. Opposite sides are parallel.
3. ABCD is a rhombus. It is not a square: the gradients of AB and BC multiply to 1, not −1, so the angle at B is not a right angle.

### Perpendicular gradients (extended)

Two lines at right angles have gradients whose product is −1. So the partner of m is −1/m. The diagonals of the rhombus above show it: AC has gradient 5/5 = 1 and BD has gradient (5 − 2)/(2 − 5) = −1, and 1 × (−1) = −1.

**Worked example 4.** Find the line through (4, 1) perpendicular to y = 2x − 3.

1. The new gradient is −1/2.
2. 1 = −(1/2)(4) + c, so c = 3.
3. The line is y = −x/2 + 3.

**Worked example 5 (perpendicular bisector).** In example 2 the midpoint of PQ is (1, 5) and PQ has gradient 3/4, so the bisector has gradient −4/3. Then y − 5 = −(4/3)(x − 1), which gives 4x + 3y = 19.

## Symmetry and reflection

A shape has line symmetry if a mirror line splits it into two parts that reflect onto each other. It has rotational symmetry of order n if it fits onto itself n times in a full turn. A regular hexagon has 6 lines of symmetry and order 6.

To reflect a point, go the same perpendicular distance beyond the mirror line. On a grid:

- in the x-axis: (x, y) → (x, −y)
- in the y-axis: (x, y) → (−x, y)
- in y = x: (x, y) → (y, x)
- in x = k: (x, y) → (2k − x, y)
- in y = k: (x, y) → (x, 2k − y)

**Worked example 6.** Reflect A(2, 5) in x = −1. A is 3 units right of the line, so the image is 3 units left: x = −4. Image (−4, 5). Reflect (3, 4) in y = −1: y = 2(−1) − 4 = −6, so (3, −6). In y = x, the triangle (1, 2), (4, 2), (4, 6) becomes (2, 1), (2, 4), (6, 4).

## Rotation and the isometric transformations

To define a rotation, give its centre, angle and direction. A reflection, rotation or translation is isometric: lengths and angles do not change, so the image is congruent to the object. Orientation changes under reflection only.

Rotating about the origin:

- 90° anticlockwise: (x, y) → (−y, x)
- 180°: (x, y) → (−x, −y)
- 90° clockwise: (x, y) → (y, −x)

For any other centre C, subtract C from the point, rotate, then add C back.

**Worked example 7.** Rotate A(5, 2) by 90° clockwise about C(1, −1).

1. Relative to C: (4, 3).
2. Rotate 90° clockwise: (3, −4).
3. Add C back: (4, −5).

A translation slides every point by the same column vector; (3, −2) as a column means 3 right and 2 down.

To describe a transformation, give its type and details: mirror equation; centre, angle and direction; vector; or centre and scale factor. 

## Congruence

Two shapes are congruent when one maps exactly onto the other by isometric transformations. For triangles, any one of these proves congruence:

- SSS: three pairs of equal sides
- SAS: two sides and the angle they enclose
- ASA or AAS: two angles and a side in matching position
- RHS: a right angle with equal hypotenuses and one more equal side

Equal angles alone prove similarity, not congruence.

**Worked example 8.** In kite WXYZ, WX = WZ and YX = YZ. Prove that the diagonal WY bisects angle XWZ.

1. WX = WZ (given).
2. YX = YZ (given).
3. WY is common to both triangles.
4. So triangle WXY is congruent to triangle WZY (SSS).
5. Corresponding angles in congruent triangles are equal, so angle XWY = angle ZWY. WY bisects angle XWZ.

Write each statement with its reason, name the condition and state the conclusion. Criterion C asks for complete, coherent lines of reasoning, and criterion B at Year 5 asks you to prove or justify general rules.

## Similarity

Similar shapes have equal matching angles and matching sides in the same ratio. For triangles, two equal angles (AA) are enough. The scale factor k = new length ÷ old length.

**Worked example 9.** In triangle ABC, a line MN parallel to BC meets AB at M and AC at N. AM = 6, MB = 4, MN = 9 and AN = 7.2. Find BC and AC.

1. Angle A is common and angle AMN = angle ABC (corresponding angles, MN ∥ BC), so triangles AMN and ABC are similar (AA).
2. AB = 6 + 4 = 10, so k = 10/6 = 5/3.
3. BC = 9 × 5/3 = 15.
4. AC = 7.2 × 5/3 = 12.

**Worked example 10 (shadows).** A 1.8 m pole casts a 2.4 m shadow while a tree casts 14 m. The triangles are similar, so height = 1.8/2.4 × 14 = 10.5 m. State your assumption (flat ground, upright objects); criterion D asks whether a solution makes sense.

## Enlargement

The guide lists enlargements as standard and enlargement around a given point as extended, so the centre-based method below is extended.

An enlargement is fixed by its centre C and scale factor k. Multiply the vector from C to P by k to reach the image: P′ = C + k(P − C).

**Worked example 11.** Centre (1, 2), k = 3, point A(2, 4). A − C = (1, 2). Times 3 gives (3, 6). Add C: (4, 8).

Lengths multiply by k and angles stay the same, so the image is similar to the object. It is not isometric unless k is 1 or −1.

### Rational and negative factors (extended)

A factor between 0 and 1 gives a smaller image; a negative factor puts it across the centre from the object.

**Worked example 12.** Centre (−3, 1), k = 2/3, P(6, 7). P − C = (9, 6). Times 2/3 gives (6, 4). Add C: (3, 5). With centre (0, 0) and k = −1/2, the point (8, −6) maps to (−4, 3).

**Worked example 13 (finding the centre).** A(3, 2) maps to A′(5, 6) with k = 2. The centre lies on the line AA′. Solving A′ = C + k(A − C) gives C = (A′ − kA)/(1 − k) = (−1, 2)/(−1) = (1, −2). Check: A − C = (2, 4), doubled (4, 8), plus C gives (5, 6).

## Identical representation of transformations (extended)

Different descriptions can give the same mapping. This page reads the framework row this way.

- Reflection in the x-axis, then in the y-axis, equals a rotation of 180° about the origin. Test A(3, −2): (3, 2), then (−3, 2). A 180° turn gives (−3, 2).
- Reflection in y = x, then in the y-axis, equals a 90° anticlockwise rotation about the origin: (a, b) → (b, a) → (−b, a).
- Reflection in x = 1, then in x = 4 (parallel mirrors) equals a translation of 6 to the right, which is twice the gap between the mirrors.
- A 90° clockwise rotation equals a 270° anticlockwise one. An enlargement with k = −1 equals a 180° rotation about the same centre.

## Tessellations

A tessellation covers the plane with copies of shapes, with no gaps and no overlaps. At each meeting point the angles must add to 360°.

- Equilateral triangles: 6 × 60° = 360°.
- Squares: 4 × 90° = 360°.
- Regular hexagons: 3 × 120° = 360°.

A regular polygon with n sides has interior angle 180° − 360°/n. For a regular pentagon that is 108°, and 360 ÷ 108 is not a whole number, so it cannot tessellate alone. Two regular octagons and one square do fit: 135 + 135 + 90 = 360. Any triangle tessellates: a copy rotated 180° about the midpoint of a side joins it to make a parallelogram, and the angles at each point are the three angles of the triangle twice, 360°. Describe a pattern by the transformations that generate it.

## Enrichment ideas (not required)

- **Dilation**: the same idea as enlargement, seen as the plane stretching about a fixed centre.
- **Polar coordinates** give (r, θ): distance from the origin and angle from the positive x-axis. (6, 60°) has x = 6 cos 60° = 3 and y = 6 sin 60° ≈ 5.196. The point (4, 3) has r = 5 and θ ≈ 36.9°.
- **Vector notation** uses AB to mean the vector from A to B. For A(1, 2) and B(5, 5), AB = (4, 3) and its magnitude is 5.
- **Vector spaces**: sets of vectors that stay inside themselves under addition and scalar multiplication. All vectors (x, 0) do. Those with x + y = 1 do not, since two of them add to a vector with x + y = 2.

## Common errors

- Reversing the subtraction on only the numerator or denominator of a gradient.
- Calling gradients 2 and −2 perpendicular; 2 and −1/2 are.
- Reflecting in x = k by only changing the sign of x.
- Rotating about the origin when another centre is named.
- Leaving out a centre or a direction when describing a transformation.
- Claiming congruence from AAA or SSA.
