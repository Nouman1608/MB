**1.** Define resistance. **[1]**

**2.** State Ohm's law. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** A filament lamp carries a current of 0.30 A when the p.d. across it is 3.0 V, and 0.45 A when the p.d. is 9.0 V.

**(a)** Calculate the resistance of the lamp at each p.d. **[2]**

**(b)** A student finds the resistance at 9.0 V by drawing a tangent to the I–V curve and calculating 1 ÷ gradient. Explain why this method is wrong. **[2]**

**(c)** Describe the shape of the lamp's I–V graph for p.d.s from −9.0 V to +9.0 V. **[2]**

**8.** A nichrome wire of length 0.800 m and diameter 0.32 mm has a resistance of 10.9 Ω.

**(a)** Calculate the cross-sectional area of the wire. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the resistivity of nichrome. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the length of the same wire needed to make a 25 Ω resistor. **[2]**

**9.** Describe an experiment to determine the resistivity of the metal of a wire. Include the measurements made, the instruments used and how the result is obtained from a graph. **[6]**

**10.** A uniform wire has a resistance of 6.0 Ω. It is drawn out uniformly until its length is doubled. The volume of the metal does not change.

**(a)** State the effect on the cross-sectional area. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the new resistance. **[2]**

**(c)** State whether the resistivity has changed. **[1]**

**11.** Wires X and Y are made of the same metal. Y is twice as long as X and has twice the diameter.

**(a)** Determine the ratio of the resistance of Y to the resistance of X. **[2]**

**(b)** The same p.d. is applied across each wire in turn. State the ratio of the current in Y to the current in X. **[1]**

**12.** An LDR has a resistance of 8.0 kΩ in dim light and 400 Ω in bright light. It is connected alone to a 6.0 V supply of negligible internal resistance.

**(a)** Calculate the current in the LDR in dim light and in bright light. **[2]**

**(b)** A metal wire and an NTC thermistor are both heated from 20 °C to 80 °C. Compare how their resistances change. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Resistance is the ratio of the potential difference across a component to the current in it (R = V/I) [1].

**2.** For a metallic conductor at constant temperature [1], the current is directly proportional to the potential difference across it [1].

**3.** ρ = RA/L, so units = Ω × m² ÷ m = Ω m [1].

**4.** Its resistance decreases as its temperature increases [1]; so on cooling its resistance increases [1].

**5.** As the current increases, the temperature of the filament increases [1]. The metal ions vibrate with larger amplitude [1], so the free electrons collide with them more frequently, and the resistance increases [1].

**6.** Reverse bias: almost zero current, so very high resistance [1]. Forward bias: very small current until a threshold p.d. (roughly 0.5–0.7 V) [1], after which the current increases steeply, so the resistance becomes low [1].

**7. (a)** At 3.0 V: R = 3.0 ÷ 0.30 = 10 Ω [1]. At 9.0 V: R = 9.0 ÷ 0.45 = 20 Ω [1].

**(b)** Resistance is defined as V/I at that point [1]; the tangent's gradient gives the rate of change of current with p.d., which is not the same as I/V for a curved graph [1].

**(c)** A curve through the origin whose gradient decreases as the p.d. increases [1]; symmetrical, with the negative half the same shape as the positive half but inverted through the origin [1].

**8. (a)** A = π(0.16 × 10⁻³)² = 8.0 × 10⁻⁸ m² [1].

**(b)** ρ = RA/L = 10.9 × 8.04 × 10⁻⁸ ÷ 0.800 [1] = 1.1 × 10⁻⁶ Ω m [1].

**(c)** R ∝ L, so L = 0.800 × 25 ÷ 10.9 [1] = 1.8 m [1].

**9.** Any six [1 each]: measure the diameter with a micrometer screw gauge; at several points along the wire and in perpendicular directions, taking the mean; calculate A = πd²/4; measure lengths L of wire with a metre rule; connect an ammeter in series and a voltmeter across the length of wire; calculate R = V/I for each length; keep the current small (or switch off between readings) to avoid heating; plot a graph of R against L; ρ = gradient × A.

**10. (a)** It halves (volume = AL is constant) [1].

**(b)** R = ρL/A: length × 2 and area ÷ 2 gives R × 4 [1]; new R = 24 Ω [1].

**(c)** No: resistivity depends only on the material (and temperature) [1].

**11. (a)** R ∝ L/d², so R_Y/R_X = 2 ÷ 2² [1] = 0.5 [1].

**(b)** I ∝ 1/R for the same p.d., so I_Y : I_X = 2 : 1 [1].

**12. (a)** Dim: I = 6.0 ÷ 8000 = 7.5 × 10⁻⁴ A (0.75 mA) [1]. Bright: I = 6.0 ÷ 400 = 0.015 A [1].

**(b)** The resistance of the metal wire increases [1]; the resistance of the thermistor decreases [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving out "constant temperature" in Ohm's law.
- Using a tangent's gradient to find resistance.
- Not squaring the radius, or using the diameter in πr².
- Forgetting the negative quadrant when describing a filament lamp graph.
- Treating resistivity as if it depended on length or area.
- Describing a resistivity experiment with no graph or with no way of finding A.
