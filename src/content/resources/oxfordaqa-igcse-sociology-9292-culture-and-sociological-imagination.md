This guide covers **3.2.2.1 Individuals and society**, the first part of Socialisation and Social
Control in Paper 2 of OxfordAQA International GCSE Sociology (9292). The specification lists:
"Culture: norms and values; roles; status; stereotypes; subcultures, including ethnic groups in
multicultural societies" and "The sociological imagination (Mills); connecting personal troubles and
public issues." The existing [Socialisation study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-sociology-socialisation/)
covers 3.2.2.2 and notes that this part is not covered there; this page fills that gap. The
qualification is new (first examined May/June 2028), so check OxfordAQA's specimen materials for
question styles.

## Why this part matters

These concepts are the vocabulary of the whole Paper 2 topic. Socialisation (3.2.2.2) is the process
by which culture is learned; social control (3.2.2.3) is how its norms are enforced; crime and
deviance (3.2.2.4) are defined by breaking them. Precise definitions here earn AO1 marks across the
section, and the sociological imagination gives you a way of thinking that strengthens AO2 and AO3
answers anywhere on the course.

## Culture

**Culture** is the whole way of life of a group or society: its shared language, beliefs, customs,
knowledge, values and norms, passed on from one generation to the next. Sociologists stress that
culture is **learned**, not inherited biologically. That is why it varies so widely between
societies and changes over time.

### Values and norms

- **Values** are broad, shared beliefs about what is good, important or desirable: for example
  respect for elders, individual freedom, religious devotion, education.
- **Norms** are specific, expected rules of behaviour that put values into practice in particular
  situations: removing shoes before entering a home, queueing, greeting elders first.

A single value can produce different norms in different societies. Respect for elders may be shown
by standing when they enter, by using a special form of address, or by caring for them at home.

### Status and roles

- **Status** is a position in a social group or society, such as daughter, teacher, prefect or chief.
  An **ascribed status** is given at birth (sex, caste, a royal title); an **achieved status** is
  earned through effort (a qualification, a job, a sporting title).
- A **role** is the set of behaviours expected of someone with a particular status. A teacher is
  expected to be fair, prepared and in control.
- **Role conflict** occurs when the demands of two roles clash: a working parent whose child is ill
  on the day of an important meeting.

### Stereotypes

A **stereotype** is an oversimplified, generalised image of a group, applied to every member
regardless of individual differences: for example assumptions that girls are naturally less
interested in science, or that teenagers are irresponsible. Stereotypes are learned through
socialisation and are often spread by the media. They matter sociologically because they shape how
people are treated, linking to labelling in education and to othering and stigmatised identities in
3.2.2.3.

### Subcultures

A **subculture** is a group within a wider society whose members share some norms and values that
differ from the mainstream, while still sharing much of the wider culture. Examples include youth
subcultures built around music and style, occupational groups, and religious communities.

The specification highlights **ethnic groups in multicultural societies**. In a multicultural
society, several ethnic groups live side by side. Members of a minority ethnic group may keep a
distinct language, religion, food, festivals and family norms, while also taking part in the
mainstream culture of school and work. Young people in such groups may combine elements of both,
creating new, **hybrid** identities (a term the specification uses in 3.2.1.5).

## The sociological imagination

The American sociologist **C. Wright Mills** introduced the idea of the **sociological imagination**
in his book *The Sociological Imagination* (1959). He argued that people often experience their lives
as a series of private problems, without seeing the wider social forces that shape them. The
sociological imagination is the ability to connect individual lives (biography) to the society and
historical period in which they are lived (history).

Mills distinguished:

- **Personal troubles:** problems experienced by an individual within their immediate surroundings,
  which can be explained by their own character or circumstances.
- **Public issues:** problems that affect large numbers of people and arise from the structure of
  society: its economy, institutions and policies.

His best-known example is **unemployment**. If one person in a large city is out of work, that may be
a personal trouble, explained by their skills or luck. If millions are out of work across a country,
the explanation cannot lie in millions of individual failings; it is a public issue, rooted in the
economy and needing a collective response.

### Applying the idea

The same move can be made with many topics on the course:

| Experience | As a personal trouble | As a public issue |
|---|---|---|
| A student leaves school early | Lack of motivation | Poverty, child labour, distance to schools, gender norms |
| A couple divorces | Incompatible personalities | Changing laws, women's employment, secularisation |
| A young man is arrested | Poor choices | Unemployment, area policing patterns, stereotyping |
| A graduate cannot find work | Weak interview skills | Credentialism, economic downturn, discrimination |

This links Paper 2 to Paper 1: the same reasoning explains attainment gaps (3.1.2.4) and family
change (3.1.1.3).

## Worked example 1: a short-answer question

*Explain what Mills meant by the difference between personal troubles and public issues. [4 marks]*

> Mills argued that a personal trouble is a problem that affects an individual and can be explained by
> their own situation (1). A public issue affects many people and is caused by the way society is
> organised, such as its economy (1). For example, one person losing their job may be a personal
> trouble, but mass unemployment is a public issue (1), because it cannot be explained by the
> failings of so many individuals and needs a social explanation (1).

## Worked example 2: planning an extended answer

*Discuss how far individuals' problems are shaped by society. Use examples from your own society or
a society you know about. [12 marks]*

- **For (society shapes problems):** Mills' sociological imagination; unemployment as a public issue;
  stereotypes affecting how groups are treated; ascribed statuses such as caste or sex limiting
  opportunities.
- **Against, or qualifying:** individuals make choices and can achieve status; interactionists stress
  that people interpret and negotiate norms; some troubles are genuinely individual.
- **Application:** a named society, for example how youth unemployment in your country affects young
  people's family lives and migration.
- **Judgement:** problems are usually shaped by both, but the sociological imagination shows that
  patterns affecting many people need social explanations.

## Common mistakes

- Treating **norms and values** as the same thing.
- Defining **status** as wealth or popularity rather than a social position.
- Confusing **role** (expected behaviour) with **status** (position).
- Describing a **subculture** as completely separate from the wider culture.
- Using the **sociological imagination** as a vague phrase without the troubles/issues distinction.
- No example from a **society you know** when the question asks for one.

## Command words

- **Identify**: name a point briefly.
- **Describe**: give the features in detail.
- **Explain**: give a reason, developed with a consequence or example.
- **Discuss how far**: arguments on both sides, with sociologists and examples, and a reasoned
  conclusion.
