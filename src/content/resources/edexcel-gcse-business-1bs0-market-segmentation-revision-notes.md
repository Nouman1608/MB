## The specification in brief

How businesses use market segmentation to target customers:
- identifying market segments: location, demographics, lifestyle, income, age;
- market mapping to identify a gap in the market and the competition.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Market | All the customers who might buy a type of product |
| Market segment | A group of customers within a market who share characteristics |
| Market segmentation | Dividing a market into segments |
| Target market | The segment or segments a business chooses to aim at |
| Location | Segmenting by where customers live, work or travel |
| Demographics | Segmenting by population characteristics such as gender, family size or occupation |
| Lifestyle | Segmenting by how people live, their interests and values |
| Income | Segmenting by how much customers earn or can spend |
| Age | Segmenting by age group |
| Market map | A diagram placing existing businesses on two axes of features customers care about |
| Gap in the market | An area of the map, or a customer need, that no business serves |

## Condensed recall

### Benefits of segmenting
- Products designed for a group's needs, so more of that group buys.
- Promotion aimed at the right people, so less is wasted.
- Prices set to suit the group's income.
- Underserved segments become visible.

### Drawbacks of segmenting
- A narrow segment may be too small to support the business.
- Research is needed to find and size segments, which costs time and money.
- Customers outside the target may be ignored.

### Valuing a segment (standard method)
1. Interested customers = people in segment × share interested.
2. Segment value = interested customers × spend per customer per period.
3. Compare value, not just size.

Mini example: 3,000 households, 8% interested, £25 a month each. 3,000 × 8% = 240 households; 240 × £25 = £6,000 a month.

### Market mapping steps
1. Choose two features customers care about (price and quality, basic and luxury, and so on).
2. Place each existing business on the map.
3. Crowded area = strong competition.
4. Empty area = possible gap.
5. Test the gap with research before acting.

### Limits of a market map
- Positions are opinions.
- A gap may have no demand.
- Only two features are shown.
- It goes out of date as rivals change.

## Mini case for recall: Juniper Yoga

Juniper Yoga runs classes in a city. It segments its market in three ways.

- **Age:** gentle chair-based classes for people over 65 on weekday mornings.
- **Lifestyle:** early-morning classes before work for busy professionals who value fitness.
- **Income:** a lower-priced community class on Sunday for people on tight budgets.

Its market map of local studios uses price and class intensity (gentle to intense). Most rivals cluster at high price and high intensity, so the competition is strongest there; the gap is gentle classes at a mid price, which suits its over-65 segment. It must still check, through research, that enough older people want to attend.

## Exam traps

- Picking the largest segment without checking spending or interest.
- Merging age into demographics when the question asks for the five specification bases.
- Treating a gap as proof that customers will buy.
- Ignoring the case's own figures when recommending a target.
- Saying a map shows sales or profits. It shows positions only.
- Writing about segmentation without saying how the business's decisions change.

## Self-test

1. List the five ways of identifying market segments in the specification.
2. Define market segment.
3. Give two benefits of market segmentation for a small business.
4. A travel firm sells adventure holidays to people who enjoy rock climbing. Which basis is this?
5. A gym offers a lower price to people on low incomes. Which basis is this?
6. A town has 6,000 people in a segment and 5% are interested. How many interested people are there?
7. If each interested person in question 6 spends £12 a month, what is the segment's monthly value?
8. State the two things the specification says a market map is used to identify.
9. What does a crowded area on a market map suggest?
10. Give two limitations of a market map.
11. Why might a business choose a smaller segment rather than a larger one?
12. Explain why a business should research a gap before filling it.

### Answers

1. Location, demographics, lifestyle, income, age.
2. A group of customers within a market who share similar characteristics.
3. Any two: products suit the group's needs; promotion is less wasted; prices can match incomes; gaps can be spotted.
4. Lifestyle.
5. Income.
6. 6,000 × 5% = 300.
7. 300 × £12 = £3,600.
8. A gap in the market and the competition.
9. Many businesses offer similar products, so competition is strong.
10. Any two: positions are judgements; a gap may have no demand; only two features are shown; it can go out of date.
11. The smaller segment may spend more per customer, be more interested, or face less competition, so it can be worth more.
12. A gap shows that no business supplies that position, not that customers want it; research checks there is enough demand to cover costs.
