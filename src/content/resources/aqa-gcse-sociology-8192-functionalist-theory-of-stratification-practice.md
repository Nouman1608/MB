## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Which of the following did Davis and Moore describe as a "universal necessity"?

A. Social mobility
B. Social stratification
C. Value consensus
D. Meritocracy

**[1]**

**2.** Which perspective argues that the functionalist theory of stratification ignores unpaid domestic and caring work?

A. Functionalist
B. New Right
C. Feminist
D. Interactionist

**[1]**

**3.** Define the term "functionally important role". **[2]**

**4.** Identify **two** of the four things Davis and Moore said must happen for a society to run efficiently. **[2]**

**5.** Explain what is meant by "performance linked to the promise of rewards". **[3]**

**6.** Identify and explain **one** Marxist criticism of Davis and Moore's theory. **[3]**

### Section B: longer and structured questions

**7.** Read the item.

> In the fictional country of Varland, the government publishes average annual pay for selected jobs: hospital porter £22,000; paramedic £34,000; airline pilot £90,000; football club chief executive £450,000. A newspaper columnist writes: "These figures prove that each job is paid what it is worth to society."

**(a)** Using the item, explain how Davis and Moore would support the columnist's view. **[2]**
**(b)** Using the item, explain **one** reason why a sociologist might reject the columnist's view. **[2]**

**8.** Explain **two** ways in which feminists would criticise the functionalist theory of stratification. **[4]**

**9.** Read the item.

> At Elmfield Engineering (a fictional firm), the managing director inherited the company from her father. A trainee engineer says: "My manager tells us that if we pass our exams we will rise up the firm. But the director never sat an exam here."

Using the item, explain how the trainee's experience could be used both to support and to criticise the idea of effective role allocation. **[4]**

**10.** Explain **two** criticisms Tumin made of Davis and Moore's theory. **[4]**

**11.** Discuss how far sociologists would agree that unequal rewards ensure the most talented people fill the most important roles in society. **[12]**

**12.** Read the item.

> A fictional survey in Harwell found that among adults whose parents were in professional jobs, a much higher proportion had trained for a professional career themselves than among adults whose parents were in routine manual jobs.

Discuss how far sociologists would agree that the functionalist theory of stratification explains inequality in the UK today. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** B. Social stratification [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* the phrase "universal necessity" is in the specification's summary of Davis and Moore; learn it word for word.

**2.** C. Feminist [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* the New Right option catches students who remember a criticism but not which perspective makes it.

**3.** A position in society that needs the highest levels of skill or carries great responsibility for directing and organising others [1], so that society would suffer if it were filled badly [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* the second mark comes from explaining why the role matters to society, not from naming an example alone.

**4.** One need correctly identified [1] and a second need correctly identified [1], from: all roles must be filled; roles must be filled by those best able to perform them; the necessary training must take place; roles must be performed conscientiously. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "people must be paid" is not one of the four; it is the solution Davis and Moore propose.

**5.** Once people are in a role, the rewards attached to it keep them working hard [1]. The promise of continued pay, prestige or promotion motivates them to perform conscientiously [1]. Without these rewards, Davis and Moore argue, people in demanding roles would have little reason to give their best effort [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* students often explain only how rewards attract people into roles; this question is about what happens after they are appointed.

**6.** Marxists argue rewards come from ownership of the means of production, not from functional importance [1]. For example, people who inherit wealth or businesses gain high rewards without competing for a position at all [1]. This shows the system is not meritocratic but protects ruling class interests, and the theory itself can be seen as ruling class ideology that makes inequality look fair [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* name ownership or inheritance; "Marxists say it is unfair" on its own earns one mark at most.

**7. (a)** Davis and Moore would say the pilot is paid more than the porter because the pilot's role needs rare skills, long training and responsibility for many lives [1], so high pay attracts the most able candidates into a functionally important role [1].
**(b)** The paramedic, who saves lives, earns far less than the football club chief executive [1], which suggests pay reflects bargaining power or market demand rather than importance to society [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* both parts say "using the item", so quote job titles and figures; a general answer about doctors and nurses misses the application marks.

**8.** First, feminists argue the theory ignores unpaid work in the home [1], such as childcare and caring for elderly relatives, which is essential to society yet unrewarded and done mainly by women [1]. Second, women are concentrated in lower-paid work whatever their talent [1], and the glass ceiling blocks them from top posts, so gender rather than functional importance shapes rewards [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* two distinct mechanisms are needed: unpaid work and labour market inequality are separate points.

**9.** Support: the trainee is told that passing exams leads to promotion [1], which matches the idea that people compete through qualifications and the most able rise to important roles [1]. Criticism: the director inherited the firm without competing [1], so her position is ascribed rather than achieved, which challenges the idea that the most important roles go to the most talented [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* the word "both" means a one-sided answer cannot score more than half marks.

**10.** First, Tumin argued that unequal access to education and training means many able people never get the chance to compete [1], so the pool of talent is wasted rather than fully used [1]. Second, he argued that stratification can be dysfunctional [1] because it can create hostility and distrust between social groups rather than binding society together [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Tumin also questioned whether training is really a "sacrifice" needing large rewards; that is an acceptable alternative point.

**11.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Davis and Moore argue stratification is a universal necessity [1].
- They identify four needs: roles filled, by the best able, with training, performed conscientiously [1].
- Functionally important roles need rare skills or high responsibility [1].
- Unequal rewards encourage competition so the most talented succeed: effective role allocation [1].
- Applied example: long, selective training for surgeons or pilots is made worthwhile by high rewards [1].
- Against: low-paid roles such as nursing are also vital, so rewards do not follow importance [1].
- Pay may reflect power rather than importance, for example groups able to bargain for high pay [1].
- There is no agreed measure of talent; there is no formal educational requirement for government ministers [1].
- Marxist: inheritance and ownership mean some gain rewards without competing [1].
- Feminist: unpaid caring work is ignored and the glass ceiling limits women regardless of talent [1].
- Tumin: unequal access to education wastes talent, so the most talented may never compete [1].
- Judgement: rewards may motivate some people to train, but class, inheritance and gender stop the system being a fair contest, so the claim is only partly supported [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* a judgement that weighs the evidence ("partly, because...") reaches higher levels than one that simply repeats a side.

**12.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Functionalists explain inequality as the result of unequal rewards for functionally important roles [1].
- Davis and Moore see this as necessary and as motivating talented people [1].
- The theory assumes a meritocracy in which positions are achieved, not ascribed [1].
- The Harwell item shows children of professionals more likely to train for professional careers [1].
- This could suggest ability or motivation passes between generations, a functionalist reading [1].
- But it more likely shows advantage passing on through money, schooling and contacts, which challenges meritocracy [1].
- Tumin: unequal access to training means talent from poorer backgrounds is wasted [1].
- Marxist: inherited wealth and ownership of the means of production explain the highest rewards [1].
- Feminist: women's lower pay and unpaid caring work are not explained by functional importance [1].
- Low-paid essential workers, such as care workers, challenge the link between importance and reward [1].
- The Prime Minister is paid far less than the chief executive of a typical major corporation [1].
- Judgement: the theory explains why some rewards exist, but not why background and gender predict who gains them, so it is a limited explanation of inequality today [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* "today" invites contemporary examples; the item gives you one, so use its comparison directly.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing about "rich people" instead of rewards attached to positions.
- Giving the four needs in vague form, such as "society needs workers".
- Ignoring the item in questions that say "using the item".
- Attributing feminist criticisms to Marxists, or the reverse.
- Presenting one side only in a "both support and criticise" question.
- Finishing an extended answer without a judgement.
