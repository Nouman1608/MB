This guide covers section 3.1.4.1, Hot desert landscapes, in Section D (Physical landscapes: Hot deserts or River landscapes) of Unit 1 of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Geography (9230) specification, Version 4.3. In Section D, students study **either** hot desert landscapes **or** river landscapes, so you only need this theme if your class chose the desert option. The key ideas are: "Hot deserts are shaped by a number of physical processes"; "Distinctive landforms result from different physical processes in hot deserts"; and "Development of hot desert environments creates opportunities and challenges." For the whole unit, start with the [Living with the Physical Environment study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-geography-living-with-the-physical-environment/). For climate, plants and animals in hot deserts, see the [hot deserts study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-geography-9230-hot-deserts/).

## What the specification lists

- **Wind (aeolian) processes:** erosion by abrasion and deflation; transport by surface creep, saltation and suspension; why wind deposits sediment.
- **Water processes:** erosion by abrasion and hydraulic power; transport by traction, saltation and suspension; why water deposits sediment.
- **Landforms from wind:** yardangs, zeugen and dunes.
- **Landforms from water:** badlands, wadis, alluvial fans and salt lakes.
- **Examples** from the region, integrated into the teaching.
- **Development:** opportunities (oil and mineral extraction, energy, farming, tourism) and challenges (extreme temperatures, water supply, inaccessibility), using examples.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Aeolian | Relating to the wind |
| Abrasion | Wearing away of rock by particles carried by wind or water |
| Deflation | Removal of loose, fine material from a surface by the wind |
| Surface creep | Rolling of large grains along the ground |
| Saltation | Bouncing of sand-sized grains along the surface |
| Suspension | Carrying of fine particles in the air or water |
| Hydraulic power | The force of moving water on rock and loose material |
| Traction | Rolling of large stones along a river bed |
| Flash flood | A sudden, short flood after intense rain |
| Wadi | A dry river valley in a desert that flows only after heavy rain |

## Wind processes

**Erosion.**

- **Abrasion** is sandblasting. Wind-carried sand strikes rock surfaces and wears them away. Most sand is bounced close to the ground, so the base of a rock is worn faster than the top.
- **Deflation** is the lifting and removal of fine, loose material such as dust and sand. It lowers the surface and leaves behind a layer of stones (desert pavement). In some places it creates shallow hollows, such as the Qattara Depression in Egypt.

**Transportation.** The size of the particle decides how it moves.

| Method | Particle size | How it moves |
|---|---|---|
| Surface creep | Large grains | Pushed and rolled along the surface by the wind and by impacts from bouncing grains |
| Saltation | Medium grains (sand) | Bounce along in a series of short hops, close to the ground |
| Suspension | Very fine particles (dust) | Carried high in the air for long distances, even between continents |

**Deposition.** Wind drops its load when it loses energy. This happens when wind speed falls, when the wind meets an obstacle such as a rock or a plant, or when there is a change of surface. Sand then piles up to form dunes.

## Water processes

Rain is rare, but when it comes it can be intense. The ground is hard and dry, so little soaks in and a sudden surge of water runs off as a **flash flood**.

**Erosion.** Abrasion happens when the stones and sand carried by the flood wear the bed and banks. Hydraulic power is the force of the water itself, which forces air into cracks and removes loose rock.

**Transportation.** Flash floods can move huge loads. The biggest boulders are rolled along the bed (traction), medium particles bounce (saltation) and fine silt and clay are carried in the flow (suspension).

**Deposition.** The water soon loses energy as it spreads out, sinks into the dry ground and evaporates. The load is dropped, with the largest material first.

## Landforms made by the wind

### Yardangs

A yardang is a long, streamlined ridge of rock, aligned with the prevailing wind, like an upturned boat hull. It forms where bands of hard and soft rock are exposed. Abrasion cuts into the softer rock and deflation removes the debris, leaving long grooves. The harder rock is left between them as ridges. They can be tens of metres high and kilometres long. The Dasht-e Lut desert in Iran is well known for its large yardangs.

### Zeugen

A zeugen is a table-shaped rock mass with a hard cap rock sitting on softer rock. It forms where horizontal layers of hard rock overlie softer rock. Joints in the cap rock are widened by weathering, and the wind-blown sand cuts into the soft rock below, particularly at its base where most sand strikes. Over time the cap rock is left on a pedestal, up to tens of metres high, and may eventually fall.

### Dunes

Dunes form where sand is deposited. A small obstacle, such as a rock or plant, slows the wind and sand builds up. As the pile grows, it becomes its own obstacle. A typical dune has:

- a **gentle windward slope**, where sand is pushed up by saltation;
- a **crest**;
- a steeper **slip face** on the sheltered side, where sand slides down at about 30 to 35 degrees.

Because sand is constantly moved from windward to leeward side, dunes slowly migrate downwind.

Common types are **barchans** (crescent-shaped, with horns pointing downwind, formed where sand is limited and the wind is steady), **seif or linear dunes** (long ridges parallel to the wind) and **star dunes** (pyramid-shaped with arms, formed where winds change direction). The Rub' al Khali in southern Arabia has all of these.

## Landforms made by water

### Wadis

A wadi is a steep-sided, flat-floored valley, usually dry, cut by occasional floods. During a flash flood it fills quickly with fast-flowing, sediment-laden water, and then drains or dries within hours. Wadis are common in Arabia and North Africa, and some, such as Wadi Rum in Jordan, are famous.

### Alluvial fans

An alluvial fan is a fan-shaped deposit of sediment at the foot of a mountain or at the mouth of a wadi. When a flood leaves a narrow valley and enters an open plain, the water spreads out, slows and sinks into the ground. It drops its load: large boulders near the apex, finer material further out. Over many floods the deposit builds up a fan, and fans from neighbouring valleys may merge. Death Valley in the USA has some of the best-known examples.

### Badlands

Badlands are dry areas of soft rock or clay with almost no vegetation, cut into a maze of steep gullies and ridges by running water. Without plants to absorb rain, water runs off quickly and erodes the surface. Badlands are useless for farming and difficult to cross.

### Salt lakes

Salt lakes (sometimes called playas) form in desert basins where there is no outlet to the sea. After a flood, water collects in the lowest area and forms a temporary lake. The water evaporates quickly and leaves behind a crust of salt and minerals. Examples include the Chott el Djerid in Tunisia and Lake Eyre in Australia. Some salt lakes are mined for minerals.

## Development: opportunities and challenges

### Opportunities

| Opportunity | Example (Arabian Peninsula) |
|---|---|
| Oil and mineral extraction | Saudi Arabia and neighbouring states have some of the world's largest oil reserves, with oil bringing large export earnings; deserts also contain phosphates, salt and other minerals |
| Energy | Cloudless skies and open land are ideal for solar power; Dubai has built a large solar park |
| Farming | Irrigation with groundwater, using circular centre-pivot systems, allowed crops to be grown in the desert; drip irrigation in greenhouses saves water |
| Tourism | Warm winter sunshine, dunes, desert safaris, luxury resorts and cities such as Dubai attract visitors |

### Challenges

| Challenge | Detail |
|---|---|
| Extreme temperatures | Summer temperatures can exceed 45°C, making outdoor work dangerous and raising the cost of cooling buildings |
| Water supply | Rainfall is tiny; people depend on desalination, which uses a lot of energy, or on ancient groundwater that is being used faster than it recharges |
| Inaccessibility | Sand, distance and a lack of roads make it expensive to build and maintain transport, pipelines and power lines; settlements are far apart |

Check these examples against your own notes, as your class may have studied a different region, such as the Sahara or the Thar Desert.

## Worked example: a 4-mark answer

**Question:** Explain how an alluvial fan is formed. [4 marks]

**Model answer:** "Heavy rain in the mountains causes a flash flood that carries a large load of rock, sand and silt down a wadi. When the water reaches the open plain at the foot of the mountains, it spreads out and slows down. The water also sinks into the dry ground and evaporates, so it loses energy. The river can no longer carry its load, so it deposits the heaviest boulders first near the mouth of the valley and finer material further out. Over many floods the deposits build up into a fan-shaped landform."

The answer follows the sequence of events and uses the idea of loss of energy to explain deposition.

## Common mistakes

- **Saying that deserts are shaped only by wind.** Water, in the form of flash floods, is also important.
- **Mixing up erosion and transport terms.** Abrasion is erosion; saltation is transport.
- **Describing a landform without explaining its formation.** The question usually asks how it formed.
- **Forgetting the idea of energy for deposition.** Wind and water deposit when they lose energy.
- **Saying that dunes move upwind.** They migrate downwind.
- **Treating "opportunities" as all positive.** Each also has costs; for example, irrigation draws on limited groundwater.
