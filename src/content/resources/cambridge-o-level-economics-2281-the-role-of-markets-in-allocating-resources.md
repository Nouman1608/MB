## 2.2.1 How a market system works

A **market** is any arrangement in which buyers and sellers exchange goods, services or factors of production. It can be a street market, a shop, an online platform or a phone call between two firms.

- **Buyers** (consumers in goods markets, firms in factor markets) want to get as much as possible for their limited income.
- **Sellers** (firms in goods markets, households selling their labour in factor markets) want to earn as much as possible.

In a **market system**, decisions about the use of scarce resources are made through millions of such exchanges, guided by prices, rather than by a central plan.

### Equilibrium and disequilibrium

**Market equilibrium** is the situation where the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied at the current price, so there is no pressure for the price to change. The price at which this happens is the equilibrium price.

**Market disequilibrium** is any situation where quantity demanded and quantity supplied are not equal.

- **Excess demand (shortage):** quantity demanded is greater than quantity supplied, at a price below equilibrium.
- **Excess supply (surplus):** quantity supplied is greater than quantity demanded, at a price above equilibrium.

**Worked example 1: mangoes in Port Selan (invented).**

| Price per kg (cu) | Quantity demanded (kg a day) | Quantity supplied (kg a day) | Excess demand (+) or supply (−) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.00 | 900 | 300 | +600 |
| 1.50 | 750 | 450 | +300 |
| 2.00 | 600 | 600 | 0 |
| 2.50 | 450 | 750 | −300 |
| 3.00 | 300 | 900 | −600 |

The equilibrium price is 2.00 cu and 600 kg a day are traded.

At 3.00 cu, sellers bring 900 kg but buyers want only 300 kg, so 600 kg are unsold. Sellers with unsold stock lower their prices. As the price falls, buyers want more and sellers bring less, and the surplus shrinks until the price reaches 2.00 cu.

At 1.50 cu, buyers want 750 kg but only 450 kg are offered, a shortage of 300 kg. Buyers compete for the limited mangoes and sellers find they can charge more. As the price rises, some buyers drop out and sellers bring more, until the shortage disappears at 2.00 cu.

Disequilibrium is therefore temporary in a free market: price movements remove it.

## 2.2.2 The three key questions

Because resources are finite and wants unlimited (section 1.1), every economy must decide:

1. **What to produce?** Which goods and services, and how much of each. More hospitals means fewer of something else.
2. **How to produce?** Which combination of factors and methods, for example many workers with simple tools, or few workers with machines.
3. **For whom to produce?** Who receives the goods and services that are made.

These questions face every economy, whatever its system. Section 2.2 is about how a market system answers them; other systems are covered in sections 2.9 and 2.11.

## 2.2.3 The price mechanism

The **price mechanism** is the way that changes in prices, caused by changes in demand and supply, allocate resources in a market system. Prices do three jobs.

| Job | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Signalling | A price change tells buyers and sellers that conditions have changed | A rising price of solar panels tells firms that buyers want more |
| Incentive | Higher prices and profits encourage firms to supply more; lower prices encourage buyers to buy more | Firms expand panel production because it is now more profitable |
| Rationing | When a good is scarce, a higher price limits it to those willing and able to pay | Buyers who value panels less, or cannot afford them, drop out |

### How prices answer the three questions

**Worked example 2: a change in tastes.** Demand for electric bicycles rises and demand for petrol scooters falls (invented).

1. **What:** the price of electric bicycles rises and the price of scooters falls. Making bicycles becomes more profitable, so firms produce more of them and fewer scooters.
2. **Resources move:** to expand, bicycle makers hire more workers and buy more battery components, raising wages and prices in those factor markets. Scooter makers cut output, releasing workers and machines. The signal in one goods market has moved factors of production between industries.
3. **For whom:** the bicycles go to those willing and able to pay the price. Buyers with higher incomes are more able to pay.

**Worked example 3: how to produce.** A farm harvests 1 hectare a day. By hand it needs 10 workers plus 20 cu of tools. By machine it needs 2 workers plus 240 cu to hire the machine.

| Daily wage (cu) | Hand method cost (cu) | Machine method cost (cu) | Cheaper method |
|---|---|---|---|
| 15 | 10 × 15 + 20 = 170 | 2 × 15 + 240 = 270 | Hand |
| 25 | 270 | 290 | Hand |
| 30 | 320 | 300 | Machine |
| 40 | 420 | 320 | Machine |

The costs are equal when 10w + 20 = 2w + 240, so 8w = 220 and w = 27.50 cu. Above that wage the farm switches to machines. The relative prices of factors answer the question "how".

### Evaluation

The price mechanism answers all three questions without central planning and adjusts quickly to change. But:

- "For whom" depends on income, so people with low incomes may go without essentials.
- Prices reflect only what buyers are willing and able to pay, so some goods may be under-provided or over-provided (market failure, section 2.10).
- Adjustment can take time: a farmer cannot grow more mangoes overnight.

## Common errors

- Saying equilibrium means "everyone gets what they want". It means quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.
- Describing a shortage as "too much demand" without comparing quantity demanded with quantity supplied at a price.
- Listing what, how and for whom without saying they arise from scarcity.
- Explaining the price mechanism with no chain: signal, incentive, response, result.
- Forgetting that factor markets (labour, capital) are also part of the market system.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** how the market system works: buyers and sellers, prices as signals, equilibrium, and how disequilibrium is removed.
- **Calculate** excess demand or supply as quantity demanded minus quantity supplied at a given price.
- **Analyse** a change in one market by following each of the three jobs of price.
- **Discuss** the price mechanism: give its strengths and its limits, then conclude.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the three functions of the price mechanism?**
Signalling (telling buyers and sellers that conditions have changed), incentive (encouraging more supply or demand) and rationing (limiting scarce goods to those willing and able to pay).

**Is a market always a physical place?**
No. A market is any arrangement that brings buyers and sellers together, including online platforms and telephone sales.
