## What you must be able to do

- 2.4 (a) Explain the terms capital expenditure and revenue expenditure.
- 2.4 (b) Explain the importance of their correct treatment.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Capital expenditure | Spending to acquire, add to or improve non-current assets, including costs of getting them ready for use |
| Revenue expenditure | Spending on day-to-day running, including maintaining assets in their existing condition |
| Non-current asset | Item kept for use in the business for more than one period, not for resale |
| Cost of a non-current asset | Purchase price plus delivery, installation, legal fees and other costs of making it ready for use |
| Improvement | Work that makes an asset better than its original condition, such as more capacity or a longer life |
| Maintenance | Work that keeps an asset in its existing condition |

## Where each goes

| | Capital expenditure | Revenue expenditure |
|---|---|---|
| Ledger | Debit a non-current asset account | Debit an expense account |
| Financial statement | Statement of financial position (at cost less depreciation) | Income statement, in full this period |
| Periods that benefit | Several | This one |
| Charged to profit through | Depreciation over the asset's life | The expense itself |

## Three-question test

1. Does it create, add to or improve a non-current asset? Capital.
2. Is it needed to make the asset ready for first use? Capital.
3. Does it only keep things running as before, or is it used up this period? Revenue.

## Borderline items to learn

| Capital | Revenue |
|---|---|
| Delivery and installation of a new asset | First year's insurance on the asset |
| Own staff's wages for installing the asset | Training staff to use the asset |
| Legal fees on buying premises | Rent of premises |
| Extension to a building | Repainting a building |
| Engine upgrade that extends a vehicle's life | New tyres, fuel, servicing, road tax |
| Fitting new shelving in a new shop | Repairing broken shelving |

## Importance of correct treatment: four points

1. **Profit**: misclassification under- or overstates profit for the year.
2. **Assets**: non-current assets, and so capital, are misstated.
3. **Later years**: depreciation follows the asset, so the error spreads across its life.
4. **Users**: owner (drawings), lender (security), tax authority (taxable profit), comparisons between years.

## Effects of each error

| Error | Profit this year | Non-current assets | Later years |
|---|---|---|---|
| Capital treated as revenue | Understated by cost less depreciation that should have been charged | Understated | Profit overstated by the missing depreciation |
| Revenue treated as capital | Overstated by the expense (less any depreciation wrongly charged on it) | Overstated | Profit understated by any depreciation charged on it |

Mini-example: equipment of 5,000, 5-year straight line, nil residual (1,000 a year), expensed in Year 1. Year 1 profit understated by 5,000 − 1,000 = 4,000; Years 2 to 5 overstated by 1,000 each; carrying value missing at end of Year 1: 4,000.

## Exam traps

- "Expensive means capital": a big repair is still revenue.
- Including first-year insurance or training in the asset's cost.
- Leaving out delivery or installation from the asset's cost.
- Saying the error does not matter because it evens out over time.
- Ignoring depreciation when calculating the effect on profit.
- Forgetting the effect on capital (equity) in the statement of financial position.
- Giving the effect without a direction: say "overstated" or "understated".

## Quick self-test

1. Define capital expenditure.
2. Define revenue expenditure.
3. Is legal fees on buying a warehouse capital or revenue?
4. A van costs 21,000 plus 450 delivery and 300 for signwriting the business name on it before first use, plus 900 insurance for the first year. What is the van's cost?
5. Is replacing a cracked shop window with an identical one capital or revenue?
6. A business debits 3,600 of redecoration to the premises account. State the effect on profit and on non-current assets.
7. Machinery costing 12,000 with a 6-year life, nil residual, straight line, was debited to repairs in Year 1. By how much is Year 1 profit understated?
8. In question 7, what is the effect on Year 2 profit?
9. Give two users who would be misled by a misclassification, and why.
10. Why is a training course for staff on a new machine revenue expenditure?
11. Which accounting concept requires capital expenditure to be spread over the periods that benefit?
12. Does a misclassification affect the owner's capital? Explain.

### Answers

1. Spending to acquire, add to or improve non-current assets, including costs of making them ready for use.
2. Spending on the day-to-day running of the business, including maintaining assets in their existing condition.
3. Capital: part of the cost of acquiring the property.
4. 21,000 + 450 + 300 = 21,750. The insurance (900) is revenue.
5. Revenue: it restores the asset to its existing condition.
6. Profit overstated by 3,600; non-current assets overstated by 3,600.
7. Depreciation should be 12,000 ÷ 6 = 2,000, so profit is understated by 12,000 − 2,000 = 10,000.
8. Overstated by 2,000, because no depreciation is charged.
9. Any two: owner, who may draw too much or too little; lender, who judges security from assets; tax authority, which taxes profit; anyone comparing years.
10. It develops the staff, not the asset, and is used up in the period.
11. Accruals (matching).
12. Yes. Profit is added to capital, so a misstated profit misstates capital, matching the misstatement of assets.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are delivery and installation part of an asset's cost?**
Yes. Any cost needed to bring the asset into working condition at its place of use is capital expenditure.

**How do I explain the importance of correct treatment?**
State the effect on profit and on assets with a direction, mention later years through depreciation, and name a user who would be misled.
