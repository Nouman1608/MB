**Ethics** means the moral principles that guide what is right and wrong. In commerce, an **ethical** business does more than keep to the law. It treats customers, workers, suppliers and the environment fairly and honestly, even when a cheaper or easier choice is available. Section 6.2 of the Cambridge IGCSE Commerce (0715) syllabus has one sub-section, 6.2.1, with three statements: the **meaning of ethics**, the **role of fair trade in commerce**, and **ethical issues in commerce**, namely **misleading advertising** and **harmful disposal of products**. This guide covers each, with two worked examples and an annotated answer.

## What is meant by ethics?

An ethical choice asks "Is this fair and honest?" and not only "Is this legal and profitable?"

| Question | Legal view | Ethical view |
|---|---|---|
| Can we claim our product is the best in the world, with no proof? | May be allowed if vague | Not honest if untrue |
| Can we pay workers the lowest wage allowed? | Yes, if it is above the legal minimum | Is it enough to live on? |
| Can we dump waste if no one checks? | Possibly not enforced | Harms people and nature |

Ethics is therefore about **behaviour**: honesty, fairness, respect and responsibility. Different people may disagree on some cases, so ethical decisions often involve weighing profit against the interests of others.

### Why do businesses act ethically?

- **Reputation.** Customers trust businesses that are honest.
- **Customer loyalty.** People return to firms they believe treat others fairly.
- **Staff motivation.** Employees prefer to work for a business they respect.
- **Avoiding penalties.** Unethical acts may break the law and bring fines.
- **Doing the right thing.** Some owners hold personal values that guide the business.

### What can it cost?

Ethical behaviour can raise costs, for example by paying higher prices to suppliers or by spending on safe disposal. In the short run profit may be lower. Over the long run a good reputation can protect sales.

## What is the role of fair trade in commerce?

**Fair trade** is an arrangement in which producers in poorer countries, often small farmers and craft workers, receive a fair price for their goods and decent working conditions. Products that meet agreed standards carry a fair trade label.

### Typical features of fair trade

- A **guaranteed minimum price** that covers the producer's costs, even if the world price falls.
- An extra **premium** paid to the producers' community to spend on schools, clean water or health care.
- **Safe working conditions** and no child or forced labour.
- **Long-term trading relationships** so producers can plan.
- Support for **environmentally friendly** farming methods.

### The role of fair trade in commerce

| Role | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Fairer pay for producers | Farmers and workers receive a stable income, which helps them escape poverty |
| Better conditions | Safe workplaces; no child labour |
| Consumer choice | Shoppers can choose goods that follow ethical standards |
| Brand image for businesses | Retailers and manufacturers can show that their supply chains are fair |
| Community development | Premium money funds local projects |

### Advantages and disadvantages for businesses

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Attracts customers who care about ethics | Higher purchase prices, which can raise selling prices or lower profit |
| Improves reputation | Customers may not pay more |
| Reliable supply of quality goods from committed producers | Certification and checking cost money |
| Staff feel proud | Only some products carry a label, so choice is limited |

## What are the ethical issues in commerce?

The syllabus names two: **misleading advertising** and **harmful disposal of products**.

### Misleading advertising

**Misleading advertising** is advertising that gives a false or unclear impression of a product, so that customers are deceived.

Examples:

- Claiming a snack is "low fat" when it is high in fat.
- Showing a photograph of a model that the real product cannot match.
- Using words such as "up to 70% off" when only a few items are reduced that much.
- Saying a product is "100% natural" or "eco-friendly" without evidence (sometimes called greenwashing).
- Hiding charges in small print.

**Effects:**

| On customers | On the business |
|---|---|
| Wasted money; possible harm, for example from a false health claim | Loss of trust and reputation |
| Disappointment and loss of confidence | Complaints, refunds and returns |
| Unfair advantage over honest sellers | Fines and legal action |

Honest advertising states facts that can be proved, shows prices clearly and does not exaggerate.

### Harmful disposal of products

**Harmful disposal** means getting rid of products, packaging or waste in a way that damages people or the environment.

Examples:

- Dumping chemicals in a river.
- Burning plastic, which releases poisonous fumes.
- Throwing out electronic waste, which contains harmful metals, in ordinary rubbish.
- Leaving unsold goods or packaging to rot in open land.

**Effects:**

- Poisoned water and soil; harm to wildlife and fish.
- Health problems for people living nearby.
- Fines and clean-up costs for the business.
- Damage to the firm's reputation.

**Ethical alternatives** include safe treatment of chemicals, recycling schemes, take-back programmes for old products and careful sorting of waste.

## Worked example 1: the cost and benefit of fair trade (invented figures)

*A cocoa farmer sells 4,000 kg a year. The market price is $1.50 per kg. A fair trade buyer pays a guaranteed minimum price of $1.90 per kg, plus a community premium of $0.25 per kg paid into a village fund.*

1. Income at market price: 4,000 x 1.50 = **$6,000**.
2. Income at fair trade price: 4,000 x 1.90 = **$7,600**.
3. Extra income for the farmer: 7,600 - 6,000 = **$1,600**.
4. Community premium: 4,000 x 0.25 = **$1,000** for the village fund.

**Judgement:** the farmer earns $1,600 more, and the village receives $1,000 for projects. The chocolate maker pays more for its cocoa, so it must decide whether customers will accept a higher price, or whether it will accept a lower profit to show it is ethical.

## Worked example 2: the cost of a misleading claim (invented figures)

*A juice company printed "100% natural" on cartons although the juice contained added flavouring. A regulator found the claim misleading. The company must print new cartons and pays a fine.*

| Item | Cost |
|---|---|
| Cartons already printed and unusable | $9,000 |
| New cartons | $11,000 |
| Fine | $15,000 |

1. Total direct cost: 9,000 + 11,000 + 15,000 = **$35,000**.
2. Sales also fall after the story appears. If sales fall by $4,000 each month for 5 months, lost sales are 4,000 x 5 = **$20,000**.
3. Total cost: 35,000 + 20,000 = **$55,000**.

**Judgement:** the saving the company hoped for from an attractive label was small compared with the $55,000 cost. Honest labels would have been cheaper.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Explain two reasons why a supermarket might choose to sell fair trade bananas.* **[4]**

> Fair trade products can **attract customers who care about ethics** [1], because shoppers may be willing to pay a little more for goods they know supported farmers, so sales may rise [1]. It also **improves the supermarket's reputation** [1], because showing that its supply chain is fair builds trust and loyalty [1].

Why it scores: each reason is named and then linked to a consequence for the supermarket.

## What are the common mistakes in 6.2?

- Treating **ethical** and **legal** as the same thing.
- Describing fair trade as "free" or as charity. Producers are paid a fair **price** for their goods.
- Forgetting that fair trade can raise costs for the buyer.
- Calling any exaggeration "misleading advertising" without saying what makes it false or unclear.
- Mixing up **harmful disposal** (damaging waste practices) with simple recycling.
- Giving only the producer's side of fair trade in questions about businesses.

## Exam technique

- **Define** ethics in a single sentence that includes fairness and honesty.
- **Describe** fair trade: give at least two features, such as a minimum price and a premium.
- **Explain** an ethical issue: say what the firm does, who is harmed and what the result is for the business.
- **Assess** whether a business should act ethically: weigh the cost against reputation, loyalty and risk of penalties, then decide.
