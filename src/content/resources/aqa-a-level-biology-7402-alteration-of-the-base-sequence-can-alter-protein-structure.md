This guide covers section 3.8.1 of the AQA AS and A-level Biology specification (7401/7402), for first teaching 2015: *Alteration of the sequence of bases in DNA can alter the structure of proteins*. It is A-level only content. The specification ends the section with the skill that most questions test: students should be able to "relate the nature of a gene mutation to its effect on the encoded polypeptide". Everything below builds towards that skill.

The broader unit page, [gene mutations and stem cells](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-gene-mutations-and-stem-cells/), lists the six mutation types. This page goes further: reading template and coding strands correctly, following a mutation all the way to protein shape, and judging how serious a change is likely to be. Test yourself afterwards with the [revision notes on base sequence and protein structure](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-7402-alteration-of-the-base-sequence-can-alter-protein-structure-revision-notes/) and the [practice questions on base sequence and protein structure](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-7402-alteration-of-the-base-sequence-can-alter-protein-structure-practice/).

## What the specification says

The content statements are short, so learn them almost word for word:

- Gene mutations might arise during DNA replication.
- They include addition, deletion, substitution, inversion, duplication and translocation of bases.
- Gene mutations occur spontaneously, and the mutation rate is increased by mutagenic agents.
- Mutations can result in a different amino acid sequence in the encoded polypeptide.
- Some change only one triplet; because the genetic code is degenerate, not all of these change the amino acid.
- Some change all the base triplets downstream from the mutation: a frame shift.

## Why replication is the danger point

During semi-conservative replication, DNA helicase separates the strands and free nucleotides pair with each exposed base before DNA polymerase joins them. Copying is very accurate but not perfect. If a nucleotide with the wrong base is joined into the new strand, or a base is skipped or copied twice, the new molecule carries a changed sequence. When that molecule is itself copied, the change is passed on to both daughter molecules. A mismatch has become a permanent mutation.

This is why "spontaneous" does not mean "without cause": it means the mutation arose from the normal chemistry of the cell, with no outside agent. **Mutagenic agents** raise the rate. Ionising radiation, such as X-rays and gamma rays, can break DNA strands. Ultraviolet light and some chemicals, such as several of those in tobacco smoke, can alter bases so that they pair wrongly at the next replication. A mutagen does not target a particular gene; it increases the chance that some base, somewhere, changes.

## From a changed base to a changed protein

Work through the same chain every time:

1. **DNA base sequence** changes.
2. The **mRNA codon** transcribed from that region changes.
3. A **different tRNA** may bring a different amino acid, or none at all if the codon is now a stop codon.
4. The **primary structure** (amino acid sequence) changes.
5. The new R group may form **different hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds or disulfide bridges**, or fail to form ones that were there before.
6. The **tertiary structure** changes, so the protein's 3D shape is different.
7. The **function** may change: an enzyme's active site may no longer be complementary to its substrate, or a receptor may no longer bind its signal.

Steps 5 to 7 are where most marks are lost. Candidates write "the protein changes shape" without naming the bonds. Protein structure is covered in [lipids and protein structure](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-lipids-and-protein-structure/).

## Reading strands correctly

Questions often give the **template strand**, which is the strand RNA polymerase reads. mRNA is complementary to the template strand, so it has the same sequence as the other, **coding** strand, with U in place of T.

| Strand | Sequence |
|---|---|
| Template DNA (given) | TAC CGA ACG CTT ACC TTT GTA ACT |
| mRNA | AUG GCU UGC GAA UGG AAA CAU UGA |
| Coding DNA | ATG GCT TGC GAA TGG AAA CAT TGA |

Using the genetic code, this mRNA gives Met-Ala-Cys-Glu-Trp-Lys-His, then stop. Always convert to mRNA before using a codon table, because codon tables are written for mRNA.

## Worked example 1: five mutations, one gene

Use the mRNA above. Codons supplied: GCU and GCC Ala; UGC Cys; AGC Ser; UGG Trp; UGA stop.

| Mutation in the mRNA | New codon | Effect on polypeptide |
|---|---|---|
| Third base of codon 2, GCU to GCC | Ala to Ala | None, because the code is degenerate |
| First base of codon 3, UGC to AGC | Cys to Ser | One amino acid changed |
| Third base of codon 5, UGG to UGA | Trp to stop | Polypeptide ends after Glu: only 4 amino acids |
| Third base of codon 8, UGA to UGG | stop to Trp | Stop codon lost: translation continues past the normal end |
| Deletion of the first base of codon 3 | Frame shift | Met-Ala-Ala-Asn-Gly-Asn-Ile: every amino acid after Ala changes |

Now relate each change to the protein.

- **Silent substitution.** Primary structure unchanged, so the protein is unchanged.
- **Cys to Ser.** Only one amino acid differs, but cysteine's R group contains sulfur and can form a disulfide bridge with another cysteine. Serine cannot. If that bridge held part of the tertiary structure in place, the protein may fold differently and lose function. If cysteine at this position formed no bridge, the effect may be small.
- **New stop codon.** A short polypeptide lacks most of its sequence, so it almost certainly cannot fold into the functional shape.
- **Lost stop codon.** Extra amino acids are added until the ribosome meets another stop codon in the same frame. The longer chain may fold wrongly.
- **Frame shift.** Many amino acids change, so the primary structure, and therefore the tertiary structure, is very different. The original stop codon is often no longer in frame, and a new stop codon may appear early or late.

## Worked example 2: a well-known substitution

In sickle cell anaemia, one base substitution in the gene for the beta chain of haemoglobin changes the codon GAG to GUG. GAG codes for glutamic acid and GUG for valine. Glutamic acid has a charged R group; valine has a non-polar, hydrophobic R group. When oxygen concentration is low, the hydrophobic valine on the surface of one haemoglobin molecule allows it to stick to others, and the molecules form long fibres that distort red blood cells. One base out of the whole gene is enough to change the behaviour of the protein, because the change is to an R group with very different properties.

Exam point: you are not expected to recall this example, but you are expected to use the same reasoning if a question supplies similar information.

## How serious will a mutation be?

There is no single rule, but these factors decide the likely effect.

| Factor | Smaller effect | Larger effect |
|---|---|---|
| Type | Substitution, or addition or deletion of a multiple of three bases | Addition, deletion or duplication that is not a multiple of three |
| Position in the codon | Third base (often silent) | First or second base |
| Position in the gene | Near the end of the coding sequence | Near the start, so a frame shift alters almost every amino acid |
| Position in the protein | Away from the active site or binding site | In the active site, or at an amino acid forming key bonds |
| New R group | Similar properties to the old one | Very different charge, polarity or size |
| Region of DNA | Intron or other non-coding DNA (removed or never translated) | Exon that codes for the polypeptide |

A mutation in an intron usually does not alter the polypeptide, because introns are removed from pre-mRNA by splicing before translation. Splicing and introns are covered in [DNA, genes, chromosomes and protein synthesis](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-dna-genes-chromosomes-and-protein-synthesis/).

### Why the third base is special

For many amino acids, changing the third base of the codon gives a codon for the same amino acid. Take GGU (glycine). The three possible third-base changes give GGC, GGA and GGG, which all code for glycine. Changes at the first or second base give AGU (serine), CGU (arginine), UGU (cysteine), GAU (aspartic acid), GCU (alanine) and GUU (valine). So of the nine possible single-base substitutions in this codon, three are silent: a probability of 1/3.

## Inversion, duplication and translocation in the frame

An **inversion** keeps the number of bases, so the frame is kept but triplets inside the inverted section may change. A **duplication** or **translocation** adds or moves bases, so it causes a frame shift unless the section is a multiple of three bases.

## Common mistakes

- Reading the template strand straight into a codon table without converting to mRNA.
- Saying a substitution "causes a frame shift". It replaces one base and keeps the frame.
- Stating that every mutation is harmful. Many are silent or have little effect, and a few are beneficial.
- Writing "the shape of the protein changes" without the steps in between: amino acid, R group, bonds, tertiary structure.
- Claiming a mutagen "causes a particular mutation". It raises the rate of mutation in general.
- Forgetting that a mutation that removes a stop codon also changes the polypeptide.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** the mutation: name the type and where it is ("substitution of the second base in the fourth triplet").
- **Explain** the effect on the protein: give the full chain from triplet to function. A six-mark answer needs every link.
- **Suggest** why a mutation had no effect: degenerate code, a mutation in an intron, or an amino acid change away from the active site with an R group of similar properties.
- When the answer must use data, quote the codons and amino acids from the table supplied rather than recalled ones.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is the genetic code called degenerate?**
Most amino acids are coded for by more than one triplet. A substitution may therefore change a triplet without changing the amino acid it codes for.

**Can a single amino acid change stop an enzyme working?**
Yes, if the new R group forms different bonds so that the tertiary structure, and therefore the active site, changes shape. A change far from the active site with a similar R group may have little effect.
