## Section A — Short questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following is an example of semantic memory?
A Remembering your tenth birthday party
B Knowing that water boils at 100 °C
C Being able to swim
D Remembering what you ate this morning **[1]**

**2.** Identify the main type of coding used in short-term memory according to the multi-store model. **[1]**

**3.** Explain the difference between encoding and retrieval. **[2]**

**4.** State the capacity **and** the duration of short-term memory according to the multi-store model. **[2]**

**5.** Name the component of the working memory model that would be used to hold the route around a building you are picturing, and state its type of coding. **[2]**

**6.** Rhea moved from a school where she learned French to one where she is learning Spanish. When she tries to say a Spanish word, the French word comes to mind instead. Identify the type of interference Rhea is experiencing and explain your answer. **[3]**

## Section B — Longer questions

**7.** Describe Murdock's study of the serial position effect. In your answer, refer to the method and the results. **[4]**

**8.** Explain how the primacy effect and the recency effect support the multi-store model of memory. **[4]**

**9.** A researcher asks one group of participants to remember a list of words while tapping a rhythm, and another group to remember the same list while repeating "the, the, the" aloud. Using the working memory model, explain which group is likely to recall fewer words. **[4]**

**10.** Outline the theory of reconstructive memory, including the concept of effort after meaning. **[4]**

**11.** Describe **and** evaluate Bartlett's War of the Ghosts study. **[6]**

**12.** Daniel revised for his psychology exam in his bedroom while listening to the same playlist every evening. The exam takes place in a silent sports hall. Use your knowledge of factors affecting the accuracy of memory to explain why Daniel might recall less than expected, and suggest **one** thing he could do about it. **[4]**

**13.** Evaluate the multi-store model of memory. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1].

**2.** **Acoustic** (by sound) [1].

**3.** Encoding is **changing information into a form that can be stored**, such as by sound or meaning [1]. Retrieval is **finding stored information and bringing it back** into conscious awareness when needed [1].

**4.** Capacity: around **5-9 items** (accept 7 plus or minus 2) [1]. Duration: around **18-30 seconds** without rehearsal [1].

**5.** The **visuo-spatial sketchpad** [1]; its coding is **visual and spatial** [1].

**6.** **Proactive interference** [1]. Older learning (French) is disrupting recall of newer learning (Spanish) [1]; the two languages are similar types of information, which increases interference [1].

**7.** Murdock presented participants with **lists of words** one at a time [1] and asked them to recall the words in **any order** (free recall) [1]. Recall was **high for the first few words** (primacy effect) [1] and **high for the last few words** (recency effect), with **poorer recall of the middle words**, producing a U-shaped serial position curve [1].

**8.** The **primacy effect** happens because the first words have more time to be **rehearsed**, so they transfer into **long-term memory** [1] [1]. The **recency effect** happens because the last words are **still in short-term memory** when recall begins [1]. Two different effects that are explained by two different stores support the model's claim that short-term and long-term memory are **separate stores** [1].

**9.** The group repeating "the, the, the" is likely to recall fewer words [1]. Remembering a word list uses the **phonological loop** [1]. Repeating a word aloud also uses the **phonological loop**, so both tasks compete for the same limited component [1]. Tapping a rhythm uses a **different** component, so it interferes less with remembering the words [1].

**10.** Memory is **not an exact recording** but is actively rebuilt each time it is recalled [1]. Fragments of the original event are combined with **schemas** — existing knowledge and expectations [1]. **Effort after meaning** is the tendency to make a memory **fit what we already understand** [1], so unfamiliar or confusing details are changed or left out [1].

**11.** *Description (up to 3):* English participants read **War of the Ghosts**, an unfamiliar Native American folk story [1], and **reproduced it several times** over a period of time [1]. Their versions became **shorter**, lost unfamiliar details such as the supernatural elements, and changed details to fit their own culture, such as canoes becoming boats [1].
*Evaluation (up to 3):* a strength is that it gives clear, real-life-style **qualitative evidence** for reconstructive memory and effort after meaning [1]. A limitation is that the procedure was **not standardised** — participants did not have strict instructions or fixed intervals — so some alterations could reflect participants inventing details rather than genuinely misremembering [1]. The story was deliberately unusual, so the findings may **exaggerate** distortion in everyday memory [1].

**12.** Information is easier to retrieve when the **surroundings during recall are the same as those during learning** [1]. The bedroom and the playlist acted as **retrieval cues** [1], and these cues are **absent in the silent sports hall**, so retrieval may fail even though the information is stored [1]. Suggestion, any one: **revise in a range of settings, including a quiet one**; or **mentally picture the bedroom** during the exam to reinstate the cues [1].

**13.** Three strengths and three limitations, 1 mark each (accept other valid, developed points):
- **Strength:** the serial position curve (Murdock) supports separate short-term and long-term stores [1], because primacy and recency are explained by different stores [1].
- **Strength:** differences in coding (acoustic versus semantic) suggest the stores really are separate [1].
- **Limitation:** dual processing research (Baddeley and Hitch) shows short-term memory has **several components**, not one store, because tasks using different components can be done together [1].
- **Limitation:** the model overemphasises **rehearsal** — many things enter long-term memory without being rehearsed [1].
- **Limitation:** long-term memory is treated as one store, but there are **episodic, semantic and procedural** types [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving short-term memory a duration in minutes instead of seconds.
- Saying the recency effect is due to long-term memory.
- In the working memory scenario, naming the right group without naming the component both tasks use.
- Describing Bartlett's study when the question also asks for evaluation, or evaluating with no description.
- Writing a general account of context without linking it to Daniel's bedroom and the sports hall.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which named studies do I need for memory in 9218?**
Murdock's serial position study, Baddeley and Hitch's dual processing tasks and Bartlett's War of the Ghosts study. Know the method, results and conclusion of each, and what each is evidence for.

**Do I need to know the duration of working memory components?**
The specification names coding and capacity for each component, not duration, so focus on those two features.

**How do I evaluate a memory model well?**
Name a piece of evidence, explain what it showed, then link it back to the model to say whether it supports or challenges it.
