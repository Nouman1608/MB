## 2.4 Capital and revenue expenditure

| Term | Meaning | Where it goes |
|---|---|---|
| Capital expenditure | Buying, adding to or improving a non-current asset | Asset account, then the statement of financial position |
| Revenue expenditure | Running costs and keeping an asset in working order | Expense account, then the income statement |

**Cost of an asset** = purchase price + delivery + installation + legal fees on property (anything needed to get it ready for use).

**Quick sorting guide**

| Item | Type |
|---|---|
| New delivery van | Capital |
| Fuel for the van | Revenue |
| Fitting shelves in a new shop | Capital |
| Repair of a broken window | Revenue |
| Adding a second floor to a warehouse | Capital |
| Annual service of a machine | Revenue |

**Why correct treatment matters (2.4 b)**

| Mistake | Profit | Assets | Later years |
|---|---|---|---|
| Capital item charged as an expense | Understated | Understated | Depreciation is missed |
| Expense put into an asset account | Overstated | Overstated | Depreciation is charged on a cost that is not an asset |

**Worked reminder.** Greywell Printing debits a 1,000 repair to its equipment account. Depreciation is 20% of cost. Profit is overstated by 1,000 − 200 = **800**. In a second case it debits a 5,000 new guillotine to repairs. Profit is understated by 5,000 − 1,000 = **4,000** because 1,000 of depreciation should have been charged.

Give the effect on both profit and assets in the same answer, and add the reason (a cost belongs to the years it benefits). An answer that names only one of the two usually reads as incomplete.

## 2.5 a Causes of depreciation

Depreciation spreads what a non-current asset cost across the years it is used. No cash is paid out when it is charged.

| Cause | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Wear and tear | Physical use and ageing |
| Obsolescence | Newer technology or design makes the asset out of date |
| Passage of time | The asset has a fixed term, for example a lease |
| Inadequacy | The asset is too small or no longer suits demand |

## 2.5 b and c Methods and calculations

| Feature | Straight line | Reducing balance |
|---|---|---|
| Charge | Same each year | Falls each year |
| Formula | (cost − residual value) ÷ life, or a % of cost | % × carrying value at the start of the year |
| Residual value used? | Yes | No, the percentage is applied to carrying value |
| Suits | Assets used evenly | Assets that lose usefulness faster early on |

Carrying value = cost − accumulated depreciation.

**Method in steps (calculation)**

1. Read the method, rate, life and residual value from the question.
2. Straight line: work out one yearly charge. Reducing balance: start with cost, then multiply by the rate each year using the new carrying value.
3. Keep a table: year, opening carrying value, charge, closing carrying value.
4. Check that cost minus the total of the charges equals the closing carrying value.

**Worked reminder.** Greywell Printing buys a press for 18,400, residual value 2,400, life 5 years.

- Straight line: (18,400 − 2,400) ÷ 5 = **3,200** a year.
- Reducing balance at 40%: year 1 = 18,400 × 40% = **7,360**; carrying value 11,040. Year 2 = 11,040 × 40% = **4,416**; carrying value 6,624.

## 2.5 c Recording depreciation

| Step | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Charge for the year | Depreciation expense | Provision for depreciation |
| Close off at year end | Income statement | Depreciation expense |

- The asset account stays at cost.
- The provision account builds up year by year. Its closing balance is carried down and brought down.
- In the statement of financial position, each class of asset appears with its cost, the depreciation to date and the resulting carrying value.

**Worked reminder.** The Greywell Printing press (cost 18,400, charge 3,200 a year) is bought on 1 January of Year 1. At the end of Year 1 the entries are debit depreciation expense 3,200 and credit provision for depreciation 3,200. The statement of financial position then shows cost 18,400, accumulated depreciation 3,200 and carrying value 15,200. At the end of Year 2 the provision has risen to 3,200 + 3,200 = 6,400, so carrying value is 18,400 − 6,400 = 12,000.

**Method in steps (ledger)**

1. Work out the depreciation charge for the year on the assets held at the year end (follow any instruction on purchases during the year).
2. Post the charge to the depreciation expense and the provision accounts.
3. Balance the provision account. Carry the balance down.
4. Transfer the expense to the income statement.
5. Show cost less accumulated depreciation in the statement of financial position.

## 2.5 d Disposal of non-current assets

| Step | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Remove cost | Disposal | Asset at cost |
| Remove accumulated depreciation | Provision for depreciation | Disposal |
| Record proceeds | Bank (or cash) | Disposal |
| Balancing figure | Disposal if profit, Income statement if loss | Income statement if profit, Disposal if loss |

**Profit or loss = proceeds − carrying value.**

- Proceeds above carrying value: profit, shown as income in the income statement.
- Proceeds below carrying value: loss, shown as an expense in the income statement.

Before you build the disposal account, make sure the depreciation charged up to the date of sale is in the provision. The full cost of the asset, and the full accumulated depreciation on it, move to the disposal account.

**Worked reminder.** Oakvale Laundry sells a machine that cost 12,000, with accumulated depreciation of 7,500. Carrying value = 12,000 − 7,500 = 4,500. It sells for 5,200, so profit = 5,200 − 4,500 = **700**.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Capital vs revenue:** asset kept for more than a year vs day-to-day or maintenance cost.
- **Straight line vs reducing balance:** equal charges vs falling charges; residual value used vs not used.
- **Cost vs carrying value:** original price plus costs vs cost less accumulated depreciation.
- **Depreciation expense vs provision for depreciation:** the yearly cost in the income statement vs the running total in the statement of financial position.
- **Depreciation vs cash:** depreciation is an allocation of past spending, not money paid out in the year.

## Quick self-test

1. Define capital expenditure.
2. Is repainting an office wall routine capital or revenue expenditure? Is fitting a new lift?
3. Give two effects of treating a repair as capital expenditure.
4. A machine costs 27,000, residual value 3,000, life 4 years. What is the straight line charge?
5. An asset costs 15,000 and is depreciated at 20% reducing balance. Give the year 2 charge and the carrying value at the end of year 2.
6. Is residual value used when applying a reducing balance percentage?
7. State the double entry for the yearly depreciation charge.
8. Define carrying value.
9. An asset cost 10,000, accumulated depreciation is 6,400 and it is sold for 3,100. State the carrying value and the profit or loss.
10. Where is a loss on disposal shown?
11. State two causes of depreciation.

### Answers

1. Spending on buying, adding to or improving a non-current asset.
2. Repainting is revenue; the new lift is capital.
3. Profit is overstated; non-current assets are overstated (and depreciation is wrongly charged on the repair).
4. (27,000 − 3,000) ÷ 4 = **6,000**.
5. Year 1 charge 3,000, carrying value 12,000. Year 2 charge = 12,000 × 20% = **2,400**; carrying value **9,600**.
6. No. The percentage is applied to the carrying value.
7. Debit depreciation expense, credit provision for depreciation.
8. Cost less accumulated depreciation.
9. Carrying value = 10,000 − 6,400 = 3,600. Loss = 3,100 − 3,600 = **500 loss**.
10. In the income statement, as an expense.
11. Two from: wear and tear, obsolescence, time running out (a lease), inadequacy.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Classifying delivery or installation of a new asset as revenue expenditure.
- Saying "profit is wrong" without stating whether it is overstated or understated, and why.
- Using the straight line formula for a reducing balance question, or subtracting residual value before applying the percentage.
- Using cost, not carrying value, as the base for the reducing balance percentage in the second year.
- Crediting the asset account with the depreciation charge when the question uses a provision account.
- Forgetting to carry down and bring down the provision balance.
- Leaving accumulated depreciation out of the disposal account, so the balancing figure is wrong.
- Writing profit on disposal as a loss (or the reverse) because the sides of the disposal account were not checked.
- Describing depreciation as "putting money aside" or "the asset's market value".
- Giving one cause of depreciation when the question asks for two.
