## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Central bank | Public institution at the centre of the financial system |
| Operational independence | The bank chooses how to hit a target that the government sets |
| Lender of last resort | Central bank lends to solvent but illiquid banks against collateral |
| Solvent | Assets exceed liabilities |
| Illiquid | Cannot meet immediate demands for cash |
| Moral hazard | Protection from loss encourages more risk |
| Systemic risk | One failure spreads and threatens the whole system |
| Capital ratio | Capital ÷ risk-weighted assets |
| Leverage ratio | Capital ÷ total assets |
| Macroprudential policy | Rules aimed at stability of the whole system |
| Conditionality | Policy conditions attached to IMF loans |

## Functions of a central bank

- Runs monetary policy (policy rate, asset purchases).
- Issues the currency.
- Acts as the government's bank.
- Holds settlement accounts for commercial banks and clears payments between them.
- Lender of last resort.
- Supervises or helps regulate financial institutions.
- Holds and manages foreign reserves where required.

## Independent versus government-controlled

| | Independent | Controlled |
|---|---|---|
| Decisions | Experts, committee | Ministers |
| Target | Set in advance | Can shift with politics |
| Strength | Credibility, no election cycle effect | Co-ordination, accountability |
| Weakness | Democratic deficit, may clash with fiscal policy | Short-termism, deficit financing, higher expected inflation |

Evaluation hook: independence helps most where institutions are strong, the target is clear and the bank must explain misses. The government can still set the target.

## Policy measures

| Objective | Measure | Chain of reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Primary: price stability | Higher policy rate | Borrowing costs up, consumption and investment down, AD down, inflation down |
| Primary | Ending or reversing asset purchases, or forward guidance | Shapes long-term rates and expectations |
| Secondary: growth and employment | Lower rate, asset purchases | AD up, output up, unemployment down, if inflation is on target |
| Financial stability | Capital buffers, loan limits, stress tests | Fewer risky loans, stronger banks |

**Method for pass-through questions.**

1. Multiply the policy change by the pass-through share.
2. Multiply by the loan size for the yearly cost.
3. Divide by 12 for the monthly figure.

Reminder: a 1.0 point cut with 60% pass-through gives 0.6 points. On a CU 90,000 interest-only loan, 90,000 × 0.006 ÷ 12 = CU 45 a month.

**Effectiveness checklist.** Pass-through, time lags, type of shock, low-rate limits, expectations and confidence, and conflicts between objectives.

## Inflation targeting

**Method for inflation questions.**

1. Inflation = (new index ÷ old index − 1) × 100.
2. Compare with the target to find the gap.

Reminder: an index moving from 250.0 to 257.5 gives 7.5 ÷ 250 = 3.0%.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Transparent | Ignores other objectives after a supply shock |
| Anchors expectations | Backward-looking data, lags |
| Rules-based, limits political pressure | May miss asset bubbles |
| Accountable | Missed targets damage credibility |

## Lender of last resort

**Chain.** Rumour, withdrawals, liquid assets run low, forced asset sales, losses, contagion. The central bank lends against collateral at a penalty rate and with a haircut.

**Reminder.** Liquidity coverage ratio = high-quality liquid assets ÷ expected 30-day net outflows. With CU 90m and CU 75m the ratio is 120%.

| For | Against |
|---|---|
| Stops contagion and runs | Moral hazard |
| Protects payments and credit | Taxpayer risk if collateral falls |
| Penalty rate limits abuse | Hard to separate solvent from insolvent |

## Why regulate, and how

- **Why.** Information asymmetry, externalities and systemic risk, moral hazard, essential payment system, consumer protection.
- **Under-regulation.** High leverage, risky lending, weak liquidity, opaque products, failures, bailouts, lost output.
- **How.** Capital requirements, leverage ratio, liquidity rules, stress tests, deposit insurance, conduct rules, ring-fencing, resolution plans.

**Reminder: stress test.** A bank with capital CU 32m and risk-weighted assets of CU 400m (8.0%) suffers a stress loss of CU 20m. Capital falls to CU 12m, a ratio of 12 ÷ 400 = 3.0%. It falls below a 6% minimum used for this example.

**Reminder: leverage ratio.** CU 32m of capital against CU 1,000m of assets is 3.2%. The reciprocal, 1,000 ÷ 32 = 31.25, is the leverage multiple.

**Reminder: deposit insurance.** If cover is CU 85,000 per depositor, someone with CU 120,000 can lose up to 35,000. That keeps some incentive to monitor the bank.

## Regulatory change and 2007-08

Link each change to its effect.

| Change | Effect |
|---|---|
| Looser rules on activities | Banks combine safe deposits with risky trading |
| Securitisation | Risk spread and hidden, weaker lending standards |
| Shadow banking | Activity outside capital and liquidity rules |
| Reliance on ratings and models | Underestimated correlation of defaults |
| Firm-by-firm supervision | System-wide build-up missed |

Outcome: falling asset prices, losses, loss of trust between banks, frozen lending, recession, bailouts and then tighter rules.

## Benefits and importance of regulation

- **Benefits.** Fewer failures, more confidence, protected savers, fewer bailouts.
- **Costs.** Less lending when capital rules tighten, compliance costs, activity moving to lightly regulated firms.
- **Cost reminder.** With CU 32m capital, supportable assets are CU 400m at 8% and CU 320m at 10%, so CU 80m less.
- **Judgement.** Importance rises with the size of the financial sector and the harm of past failures. Timing matters: tightening in a downturn deepens it.

## The IMF and World Bank

| | IMF | World Bank |
|---|---|---|
| Main work | Surveillance, lending for balance of payments problems, capacity development | Long-term development finance and advice |
| Role in the system | Monitors risks, crisis support, advice | Supports institutions and reform |
| Criticisms | Conditionality, weighted voting | Conditions, project focus |

Neither regulates individual banks. National authorities do that. Evaluation points: size of resources, whether governments follow advice, speed of response, and side effects of conditions such as spending cuts.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Primary versus secondary objective.** Price stability comes first. Growth and employment are supported only while the target is safe.
- **Operational independence versus target independence.** The bank usually chooses the means. The government usually sets the target.
- **Capital ratio versus leverage ratio.** The first uses risk-weighted assets. The second uses total assets, so it cannot be gamed by low risk weights.
- **Capital versus liquidity.** Capital absorbs losses. Liquidity meets withdrawals.
- **Microprudential versus macroprudential.** The first looks at the health of each firm. The second looks at risks across the whole system.
- **IMF versus World Bank.** Short-term balance of payments support and surveillance, against longer-term development finance.

## Worked evaluation chain

Question type: "Evaluate the case for an inflation target when a supply shock raises prices."

1. Define the target and the shock: costs rise, so prices rise without excess demand.
2. Argue for: a firm target keeps wage and price expectations anchored, so a one-off price rise does not become a spiral.
3. Argue against: raising rates to bring inflation back quickly cuts output and employment, so the secondary objectives suffer.
4. Qualify: much depends on how fast the bank must return to target. A flexible time frame softens the cost.
5. Judge: the target is worth having for credibility, provided the bank can look through a temporary shock and explain why.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing about the instruments of monetary policy without linking the chain to the stated objective.
- Treating independence as meaning the government has no say over the target.
- Mixing up illiquid and insolvent banks.
- Describing the lender of last resort without one cost, or both sides.
- Describing what happened in 2007-08 without linking a specific regulatory change to its effect.
- Calling the IMF a regulator of banks.
- Giving the benefits of regulation with no cost, so no judgement.
- Forgetting to convert pass-through, ratios and index changes to the same unit before comparing.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between an illiquid and an insolvent bank?**
An insolvent bank has liabilities greater than its assets. An illiquid bank may be solvent but cannot meet immediate demands for cash, which is the case a lender of last resort is designed to help.

**What is the difference between the capital ratio and the leverage ratio?**
The capital ratio is capital divided by risk-weighted assets, while the leverage ratio is capital divided by total assets. Because it uses total assets, the leverage ratio cannot be gamed by low risk weights.

**What is operational independence for a central bank?**
Operational independence means the central bank chooses how to hit a target that the government sets. The government usually keeps the power to set the target, so independence does not mean the government has no say.

**What is the difference between microprudential and macroprudential regulation?**
Microprudential regulation looks at the health of each individual financial firm. Macroprudential policy looks at risks across the whole system, using tools such as capital buffers, loan limits and stress tests.

**How did changes in financial regulation contribute to the 2007-08 crisis?**
Looser rules on bank activities, securitisation that spread and hid risk, shadow banking outside capital and liquidity rules, reliance on ratings and models, and firm-by-firm supervision that missed system-wide risks all played a part. Each change should be linked to its effect.
