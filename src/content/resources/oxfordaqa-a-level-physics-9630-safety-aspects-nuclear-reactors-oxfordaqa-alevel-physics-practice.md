These questions cover section 3.12.4, Safety aspects of nuclear reactors, of OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630), which is International A-level only content. Learn the content first with the [reactor safety study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-safety-aspects-nuclear-reactors-oxfordaqa-alevel-physics/) and the [reactor safety revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-safety-aspects-nuclear-reactors-oxfordaqa-alevel-physics-revision-notes/). Use 1 MeV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ J and take the specific heat capacity of water as 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Explain why spent fuel is moved by remote handling. **[2]**

**2.** State two reasons why spent fuel is kept under water in a cooling pond. **[2]**

**3.** The control rods of a reactor are held above the core by electromagnets. Explain why this arrangement makes the reactor safer. **[2]**

**4.** The γ intensity from a small source is 5000 units at 1.0 m. Calculate the distance at which the intensity falls to 50 units. **[2]**

**5.** α particles are stopped by a sheet of paper. Explain why α-emitting waste is still treated as hazardous. **[2]**

**6.** Explain how the steel structure of a reactor becomes radioactive. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** A container of high-level waste has an activity of 1.2 × 10¹⁶ Bq. Each decay deposits on average 0.60 MeV in the container.

**(a)** Calculate the rate at which thermal energy is produced in the container. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest why the container must be kept in a cooled store. **[1]**

**(c)** The activity is mainly due to a nuclide of half-life 30.1 years. Calculate the activity after 10 years. **[2]**

**(d)** Calculate the heating power after 10 years. **[1]**

**8.** A reactor operating at 2.0 GW of thermal power is shut down in an emergency. Just after shut-down, decay heating is 5.0% of the operating power.

**(a)** Calculate the decay heating power. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the mass of cooling water needed each second if the water may warm by 25 K. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the control rods are designed to fall into the core under gravity. **[2]**

**(d)** State a second, independent way of stopping the chain reaction. **[1]**

**9.** **(a)** Name the three categories of radioactive waste and give one example of each. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why high-level waste is stored for several years before any long-term disposal. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest why deep underground storage in stable rock is planned for long-lived waste. **[1]**

**10.** Spent fuel contains strontium-90 (half-life 28.8 years) and plutonium-239 (half-life 24 100 years).

**(a)** Calculate the fraction of the strontium-90 that remains after 100 years. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the time for the activity of the plutonium-239 to fall to 1.0% of its initial value. **[2]**

**(c)** State what your answers suggest about how the two nuclides must be stored. **[1]**

**11.** **(a)** Explain why the thick concrete shield around a reactor core is not needed to stop α and β particles. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why reactor shielding uses both dense materials and materials containing light nuclei. **[2]**

**(c)** A worker 2.0 m from a small γ source is exposed to an intensity I. Calculate the distance at which the intensity would be I/25. **[1]**

**12.** A country must choose between building a new nuclear power station and a new coal-fired power station to supply continuous electricity. Discuss the risks and benefits of the nuclear option compared with the coal option, and reach a conclusion. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Spent fuel emits intense β and γ radiation (and is hot) [1]; workers close to it would receive a dangerous dose, so machines controlled from behind shielding move it [1] **[2]**

**2.** The water absorbs the radiation, shielding workers [1]; the water removes the heat produced by radioactive decay [1] **[2]**

**3.** If electrical power fails, the electromagnets release the rods [1], which fall into the core under gravity, absorbing neutrons and stopping the chain reaction without needing any power supply [1] **[2]**

**4.** I ∝ 1/r², so r² = 1.0² × 5000/50 = 100 [1]; r = 10 m [1] **[2]**

**5.** If α-emitting material escapes and is breathed in or swallowed [1], the α particles deposit their energy in a small volume of tissue inside the body, causing severe damage [1] **[2]**

**6.** Neutrons from the core are absorbed by nuclei in the steel [1], producing unstable (radioactive) isotopes [1] **[2]**

**7. (a)** Power = activity × energy per decay = 1.2 × 10¹⁶ × 0.60 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ [1] = 1.2 × 10³ W [1]

**(b)** The decay heat is produced continuously and would raise the temperature of the container and its contents until they were damaged [1]

**(c)** A = A₀e^(−λt) with λ = ln 2/30.1 y⁻¹ [1]; A = 1.2 × 10¹⁶ × e^(−0.693 × 10/30.1) = 9.5 × 10¹⁵ Bq [1]

**(d)** 9.5 × 10¹⁵ × 0.60 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ = 9.2 × 10² W [1] **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* Ten years is not a whole number of half-lives, so the exponential equation is needed.

**8. (a)** 0.050 × 2.0 × 10⁹ = 1.0 × 10⁸ W [1]

**(b)** Δm/Δt = P/(cΔθ) [1] = 1.0 × 10⁸/(4200 × 25) = 9.5 × 10² kg s⁻¹ [1]

**(c)** The shut-down then works even if electrical power or control systems fail [1]; gravity always acts, so the rods enter the core and absorb neutrons automatically [1]

**(d)** Inject a neutron-absorbing liquid (for example a boron solution) into the coolant [1] **[6]**

**9. (a)** Low-level, for example protective clothing or tools [1]; intermediate-level, for example fuel cladding or reactor components [1]; high-level, for example spent fuel or separated fission products [1]

**(b)** Its activity and heat output are very high at first [1]; many fission products have short half-lives, so storage allows the activity and heating to fall before the waste is sealed for disposal [1]

**(c)** Stable rock deep underground isolates the waste from people and from groundwater for the very long times over which it remains hazardous [1] **[6]**

**10. (a)** A/A₀ = e^(−λt) = e^(−0.693 × 100/28.8) [1] = 0.090 [1]

**(b)** t = ln(A₀/A)/λ = ln 100 × 24 100/0.693 [1] = 1.6 × 10⁵ years [1]

**(c)** Strontium-90 needs secure storage for centuries; plutonium-239 needs isolation for hundreds of thousands of years [1] **[5]**

**11. (a)** α and β particles have short ranges and are stopped by the fuel cladding and the steel pressure vessel [1]; the concrete is needed for the penetrating radiations, neutrons and γ rays [1]

**(b)** Dense materials (steel, concrete) absorb γ rays [1]; light nuclei (in water and concrete) slow neutrons by collisions so they can be absorbed [1]

**(c)** I ∝ 1/r², so the distance must be multiplied by 5: 10 m [1] **[5]**

**12.** Marking points, one mark each, up to 6 **[6]**:

- Benefit: nuclear fuel releases far more energy per kilogram, so fuel quantities and transport are small [1]
- Benefit: no carbon dioxide is released by the reactor during operation, unlike burning coal [1]
- Benefit: coal combustion also releases air pollutants; nuclear generation does not [1]
- Risk: a rare accident could release radioactive material over a wide area [1]
- Risk: long-lived high-level waste must be isolated for thousands of years; decommissioning is costly [1]
- Conclusion weighing these, for example: both give continuous output, but nuclear power avoids the certain, continuing harm from carbon dioxide and pollution in exchange for a low-probability but severe risk that can be reduced by design, shielding and waste management, so nuclear is preferred (another reasoned conclusion is accepted) [1]

*Examiner insight:* The final mark requires a conclusion that follows from the points made; a list without a judgement cannot reach full marks.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying control rods cool the reactor.
- Forgetting that decay heat continues after shut-down.
- Using the inverse-square law with r instead of r².
- Treating 10 years as a whole number of half-lives.
- Listing risks and benefits without reaching a conclusion.
