## Questions

**1.** Define the term "differentiation". **[2]**

**2.** Belmarsh Skincare sells a face serum. Its sales in units were: Year 1: 300; Year 2: 1,100; Year 3: 2,900; Year 4: 3,000; Year 5: 2,950; Year 6: 2,100.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in sales from Year 1 to Year 2. Give your answer to one decimal place. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in sales from Year 5 to Year 6. Give your answer to one decimal place. **[2]**
**(c)** Identify the phase of the product life cycle the serum had entered by Year 6. **[1]**

**3.** Give the three parts of the design mix named in the specification. **[3]**

**4.** Explain one benefit to a business of differentiating its product. **[3]**

**5.** Pickwith Foods makes a pasta sauce with a unit cost of £14.50 for a case of twelve jars. It uses cost-plus pricing with a 30% mark-up. Calculate the selling price of one case. **[3]**

**6.** Hobrook Biscuits sells a ginger biscuit whose sales have fallen for three years. Outline one extension strategy the business could use. **[2]**

**7.** Sutcombe Gadgets has designed a pocket projector. The variable cost is £48 a unit and fixed costs are £9,000 a month. The directors are choosing between two plans.

Plan A: price skimming. Launch price £130, expected sales 300 units a month.
Plan B: penetration pricing. Launch price £95, expected sales 520 units a month.

**(a)** Calculate the monthly profit under each plan. **[4]**
**(b)** Justify which plan Sutcombe Gadgets should choose. **[6]**

**8.** Explain one way in which technology can influence a business's pricing strategy. **[3]**

**9.** Caldmore Café sells a mid-morning drink to two market segments. On a typical weekday, 60 commuters pay £3.80 each and 90 retired customers pay £2.60 each.

**(a)** Calculate the total revenue from the two segments. **[2]**
**(b)** Analyse how the existence of these two segments could influence Caldmore Café's pricing. **[4]**

**10.** Moorvale Drones is launching a new model into a market with several well-known rivals. The model has features no rival offers. Analyse how the phases of the product life cycle could affect the pricing strategy Moorvale Drones uses. **[6]**

**11.** Marrow & Finch sells a chocolate bar at £1.50 with a variable cost of £0.90 a bar. Annual sales are falling and are expected to reach 40,000 bars next year. The owners are considering an extension strategy: a new flavour and wrapper, costing £9,000, expected to hold sales at 62,000 bars.

**(a)** Calculate the expected net gain from the extension strategy. **[3]**
**(b)** Evaluate whether Marrow & Finch should go ahead with the extension strategy. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Making a product or service different from those of rivals [1] in a way that customers notice and value [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition is likely to read more convincingly if it says customers must value the difference, since "being different" alone does not explain why it helps.

**2. (a)** Percentage change = change ÷ original × 100 [1]. (1,100 − 300) ÷ 300 × 100 = **+266.7%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** (2,100 − 2,950) ÷ 2,950 × 100 [1] = **−28.8%** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** **Decline** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Dividing by the original figure, not the new one, is the usual route to the correct percentage; showing the subtraction first makes the method clear even if the final rounding slips.

**3.** Function [1]; aesthetics [1]; cost [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Three words are enough here, so there is nothing to gain from explaining them; check each is one of the three named parts and not a general design idea such as quality.

**4.** One benefit is that differentiation can support a higher price [1]. This is because customers who value the difference are willing to pay more than for a similar rival product [1]. As a result, the business earns more revenue per unit sold and may raise its profit [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A chain of a benefit followed by two linked expansion points is the usual shape for an "explain" question; naming a second, separate benefit is less likely to help than developing the first.

**5.** Mark-up = unit cost × mark-up percentage = 14.50 × 0.30 [1] = £4.35 [1]. Selling price = 14.50 + 4.35 = **£18.85** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Adding only the mark-up to give £4.35 is a common slip; the final step of adding it to the unit cost is worth showing clearly, with the pound sign.

**6.** One extension strategy is to launch a new flavour, such as a spiced ginger and orange biscuit [1]. This could attract new customers or renew interest among existing ones, so sales stop falling [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* An "outline" answer is likely to do better when the strategy is tied to the biscuit and its falling sales, not given as a general list of methods.

**7. (a)** Plan A: revenue 130 × 300 = £39,000; variable costs 48 × 300 = £14,400; total cost 9,000 + 14,400 = £23,400 [1]. Profit = 39,000 − 23,400 = **£15,600** [1].
Plan B: revenue 95 × 520 = £49,400; variable costs 48 × 520 = £24,960; total cost 9,000 + 24,960 = £33,960 [1]. Profit = 49,400 − 33,960 = **£15,440** [1]. **[4]**
**(b)** The profits are almost the same: Plan A is £160 a month higher [1]. Plan B earns higher revenue (£49,400 against £39,000) and wins more customers, which could build brand awareness and market share [1]. A penetration price may also deter rivals from entering [1]. However, Plan B needs 520 sales a month, which is more uncertain for a new product, whereas Plan A needs only 300 [1]. Plan A leaves room to cut the price later, when rivals arrive, while a low launch price is hard to raise [1]. Judgement: choose Plan A, because it earns the same profit from fewer sales, which carries less risk and keeps the option to lower the price later [1]. A reasoned choice of Plan B, based on market share, could also be supported. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A "justify" answer is likely to gain more from recommending one plan and giving a reason than from describing both plans equally; the figures from part (a) are better used as evidence than repeated.

**8.** Technology lets customers compare prices across many sellers in seconds [1]. This puts pressure on a business to keep prices close to rivals', or lose sales to a cheaper seller [1]. As a result, it may adopt competitive pricing and have less room to charge a premium [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming the technology (such as price-comparison websites or apps) and then following it through to a pricing consequence is the likely route to the full range of credit.

**9. (a)** Commuters: 60 × 3.80 = £228 [1]. Retired customers: 90 × 2.60 = £234, so total = 228 + 234 = **£462** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The segments appear willing to pay different prices, since commuters pay £3.80 and retired customers £2.60 [1]. Charging each group a different price (for example a discount for retired customers) lets the café keep both [1]. A single price of £3.80 might lose retired customers, who are the larger group at 90 customers [1]. A single price of £2.60 would give up income from commuters who would pay more [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Analysis answers are likely to score better when they use the two prices and group sizes from the case, not a general statement that segments are different.

**10.** In introduction, the new model has features no rival offers, so Moorvale Drones could use price skimming [1]. Early buyers who want the newest features may accept a high price, giving high profit per unit [1]. In growth, rivals are likely to copy the features, so the business may have to lower its price or move to competitive pricing [1]. In maturity, many rivals sell similar drones, so competitive pricing keeps sales without a large premium [1]. In decline, promotional pricing could clear unsold stock [1]. The strategy has to change as competition grows, because a high price that suited introduction would lose customers later [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Moving through the phases in order, with a different pricing consequence for each, is likely to read as analysis; repeating the same "price will change" comment for every phase is less likely to earn credit.

**11. (a)** Contribution per bar = 1.50 − 0.90 = £0.60 [1]. Extra bars = 62,000 − 40,000 = 22,000, so extra contribution = 22,000 × 0.60 = £13,200 [1]. Net gain = 13,200 − 9,000 = **£4,200** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** For going ahead: the extension is expected to add £4,200, so it more than covers its cost [1]. The sales it holds, 62,000 bars at £1.50, mean revenue of £93,000, against £60,000 if nothing is done [1]. A new flavour may attract new customers and refresh the brand [1]. It needs only 15,000 extra bars to cover its cost (9,000 ÷ 0.60), far fewer than the 22,000 forecast [1]. Against this, the 62,000 is a forecast, and if the new flavour attracts fewer customers the gain could disappear [1]. The gain of £4,200 is small compared with the £9,000 outlay, so the margin for error is thin [1]. The extension may also only delay decline, not stop it [1]. Rivals could copy a flavour quickly, reducing the benefit [1]. Judgement: go ahead, because the break-even sales are well below the forecast, but monitor sales closely and have a plan for the next renewal of the product [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* A balanced evaluation is likely to use the calculation from part (a) and then weigh risks against it; a final judgement that says why is likely to read as stronger than one that restates the figures.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Not writing the formula or substitution, so a slip in the final figure leaves no working to credit.
- Dividing by the new value instead of the original when calculating percentage change.
- Adding the mark-up but forgetting to add it to the unit cost.
- Giving a list of life cycle phases when the question asks for analysis linked to pricing.
- Describing a pricing strategy without linking it to the product, the rivals or the segment in the case.
- Confusing penetration and skimming.
- Writing about extension strategies in general without using the figures or the product named.
- Evaluating without reaching a judgement, or reaching one that the earlier points do not support.
- Leaving out the pound sign or units in calculations.
