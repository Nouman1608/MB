Condensed recall for **2.5 Simple molecules and covalent bonds**, Cambridge IGCSE
Chemistry 0620. For the full method and worked diagrams, use the
[Simple Molecules and Covalent Bonds study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-simple-molecules-and-covalent-bonds/).

> **Tier note:** items marked *(Supplement)* are for Extended candidates only.

## Must-know statements

- **Covalent bond:** a pair of electrons **shared between two atoms**, leading to noble gas
  electronic configurations.
- Covalent bonds form between **non-metal atoms**.
- Simple molecular substances have **low melting and boiling points** and **poor
  electrical conductivity**.
- *(Supplement)* Low melting and boiling points are due to **weak intermolecular forces**,
  which need little energy to overcome. Specific types of force are not required.
- *(Supplement)* Poor conductivity: molecules are neutral; **no ions or free electrons** to
  carry charge.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Covalent bond | Shared pair of electrons between two atoms |
| Single bond | One shared pair (H–H) |
| Double bond | Two shared pairs between the same two atoms (O=O, C=C, C=O) |
| Triple bond | Three shared pairs (N≡N) |
| Lone pair | Pair of outer electrons not used in bonding |
| Simple molecule | Small, separate group of covalently bonded atoms |
| Intermolecular forces | Weak attractions *between* molecules |
| Displayed formula | Shows every atom and every bond as a line |

## Bonds each atom forms

```
H  → 1 bond, no lone pairs
Cl → 1 bond, 3 lone pairs
O  → 2 bonds, 2 lone pairs
N  → 3 bonds, 1 lone pair
C  → 4 bonds, no lone pairs
```

Rule: bonds = electrons needed to reach eight (two for hydrogen).

## The eleven named molecules

**Core:** H₂, Cl₂, H₂O, CH₄, NH₃, HCl — all single bonds.

**(Supplement):**

| Molecule | Key feature | Lone pairs |
|---|---|---|
| O₂ | O=O double bond | 2 on each O |
| N₂ | N≡N triple bond | 1 on each N |
| CO₂ | two C=O double bonds | 2 on each O, 0 on C |
| C₂H₄ | C=C double bond + four C–H | none |
| CH₃OH | C–O and O–H single bonds + three C–H | 2 on O |

**Electron totals to check your diagrams** (outer electrons drawn in total):
H₂ 2 · Cl₂ 14 · HCl 8 · H₂O 8 · NH₃ 8 · CH₄ 8 · O₂ 12 · N₂ 10 · CO₂ 16 · C₂H₄ 12 · CH₃OH 14.

## The two-forces picture

```
inside each molecule:   covalent bonds           STRONG  — not broken on melting/boiling
between molecules:      intermolecular forces    WEAK    — overcome on melting/boiling
```

Bigger molecules → stronger intermolecular forces → higher boiling point (e.g. Cl₂ gas,
Br₂ liquid, I₂ solid at room temperature).

## Drawing in the exam: four checks

1. **Symbols:** all of one atom's electrons are dots and its partner's are crosses, so the
   origin of every electron in a shared pair is clear.
2. **Pairs in overlaps:** one dot plus one cross per shared pair.
3. **Shell count:** eight around each atom except hydrogen (two).
4. **Total count:** matches the electron totals above.

## Describe versus explain

| Command | Core answer (describe) | Supplement answer (explain) |
|---|---|---|
| Boiling point of chlorine | Low; chlorine is a gas at room temperature | Weak intermolecular forces between Cl₂ molecules need little energy to overcome |
| Conductivity of methane | Does not conduct | Molecules are neutral; no ions or free electrons to carry charge |

## Exam traps

- "Covalent bonds are weak" — wrong. They are strong; the **intermolecular** forces are weak.
- Writing "intermolecular bonds" can cost the mark; write "intermolecular **forces**".
- Lone pairs left off O in H₂O, N in NH₃, or Cl in HCl.
- Using a single bond in N₂ or O₂: count the electrons and each atom ends short of eight.
- Drawing all electrons as dots: the two atoms' electrons must be distinguishable.
- Giving "no free electrons" alone for conductivity: say **no ions or electrons free to
  move**, because molecules are neutral.
- Saying covalent substances "never" conduct: graphite does, and so do solutions of some
  molecular substances that react with water to form ions.

## Self-test

1. Define a covalent bond.
2. How many lone pairs are on the oxygen atom in water?
3. Why does a hydrogen atom form only one covalent bond?
4. How many electrons are shared between the two atoms in a chlorine molecule?
5. *(Supplement)* How many shared pairs are there in a molecule of oxygen, O₂?
6. *(Supplement)* State the total number of electrons in the bonds of a CO₂ molecule.
7. *(Supplement)* Describe the bonding in ethene, C₂H₄, in terms of shared pairs.
8. *(Supplement)* How many lone pairs are on each nitrogen atom in N₂?
9. Phosphorus is in the same group as nitrogen. Predict the formula of the simplest
   compound of phosphorus and hydrogen, and the number of lone pairs on phosphorus.
10. Describe two physical properties of simple molecular substances.
11. *(Supplement)* Explain why ammonia is a gas at room temperature.
12. Why does pure liquid water conduct electricity poorly?

**Answers**

1. A pair of electrons shared between two atoms, giving each a noble gas configuration.
2. Two.
3. It has one electron and needs only one more to fill its first shell (two electrons).
4. Two electrons (one shared pair).
5. Two shared pairs (a double bond).
6. Eight (two double bonds, each of two pairs).
7. One C=C double bond (two shared pairs) and four C–H single bonds; no lone pairs.
8. One.
9. PH₃, with one lone pair on phosphorus (same pattern as NH₃).
10. Low melting and boiling points; poor electrical conductivity.
11. Its molecules are held by weak intermolecular forces, which need little energy to
    overcome, so it boils below room temperature.
12. It consists of neutral molecules, so there are almost no ions or free electrons to carry
    charge.
