Condensed recall for **2.4 The human impact** on the atmosphere: the enhanced greenhouse effect, global warming and urban climate. For full explanations, read the [Core Physical Geography study guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-geography-core-physical-geography/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Natural greenhouse effect | Gases such as water vapour and carbon dioxide keep the Earth warmer than it would otherwise be |
| Enhanced greenhouse effect | Extra warming from human emissions of greenhouse gases |
| Greenhouse gases | Include carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide |
| Global warming | Rise in average global temperature |
| Urban heat island | City warmer than its rural surroundings |
| Positive feedback | A change that triggers processes amplifying the original change |
| Anthropogenic heat | Heat released directly by human activity: buildings, vehicles, industry |
| Urban canyon | A street lined by tall buildings, which traps radiation and channels wind |
| Sky view factor | The share of the sky visible from a point; low in narrow streets, limiting night-time heat loss |

## How the greenhouse effect works

Shortwave radiation from the sun passes through the atmosphere largely unabsorbed and heats the surface. The surface emits longwave radiation, which greenhouse gases absorb and re-emit in all directions, including back down. Without this natural effect the Earth's average surface temperature would be well below freezing. Adding more greenhouse gases increases the longwave energy returned to the surface, so the lower atmosphere warms further: the **enhanced** effect.

## Enhanced greenhouse effect

**Causes (human):** burning fossil fuels, deforestation (less carbon dioxide removed), livestock and rice farming (methane), and fertiliser use. **Natural factors** also change climate: orbital changes, solar output, volcanic eruptions.

| Gas | Main human sources |
|---|---|
| Carbon dioxide | Burning coal, oil and gas; cement making; deforestation |
| Methane | Livestock digestion, rice paddies, landfill, leaks from gas and coal extraction |
| Nitrous oxide | Nitrogen fertilisers, some industrial processes |
| Halocarbons (such as CFCs) | Refrigerants and aerosols; also damage the ozone layer |

**Evidence:** instrumental temperature records, ice-core records of past carbon dioxide, retreating glaciers and ice sheets, and rising sea level. Interpret evidence critically: records vary in length and reliability.

- **Carbon dioxide records:** direct measurements at Mauna Loa in Hawaii since 1958 (the Keeling curve) show a steady rise with a seasonal cycle. Concentrations were about 280 parts per million before industrialisation and have risen above 400 parts per million this century.
- **Temperature:** the IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report (2021) found that global surface temperature in 2011 to 2020 was about 1.1 degrees C above the 1850 to 1900 average, and that human influence has warmed the climate.
- **Ice cores** from Antarctica and Greenland preserve air bubbles showing past gas levels, and show that carbon dioxide and temperature have varied together over hundreds of thousands of years.
- **Critical points:** early thermometer records cover fewer places; some urban weather stations are affected by the heat island; proxy records such as tree rings and ice cores need careful interpretation. Natural factors cannot explain the recent rise on their own.

**Atmospheric impacts:** higher temperatures, changes in rainfall patterns, more frequent extreme weather in some regions.

More specific impacts to explain:

- more frequent and intense heatwaves;
- a warmer atmosphere holds more water vapour, so heavy rainfall events can be more intense;
- shifts in rainfall belts, with some regions wetter and others drier;
- warming greatest in the Arctic, partly because melting snow and ice lower the albedo, a **positive feedback**;
- water vapour itself is a greenhouse gas, so warming that raises vapour content amplifies the warming.

## Required urban climate case study

Choose one urban area and know its effects on:

- **Temperature:** heat island, strongest on calm clear nights. Causes: low-albedo materials store heat, little vegetation and evaporation, heat from buildings and traffic.
- **Humidity:** usually lower than in rural areas because surfaces are sealed and drained quickly.
- **Precipitation:** convection over warm cities and pollution particles acting as condensation nuclei can increase rain and thunderstorms.
- **Winds:** buildings reduce mean speeds, but tall blocks can channel gusts.

## Worked case study outline: London

- **History:** London is one of the earliest studied heat islands; Luke Howard recorded that the city was warmer than the surrounding countryside in *The Climate of London*, published in the early nineteenth century.
- **Temperature:** the centre is often several degrees Celsius warmer than rural areas at night, with the greatest difference on calm, clear nights with high pressure. Isotherm maps show warmth peaking over the dense core and falling away towards green belt areas, with cooler patches over large parks and the river.
- **Why:** brick, concrete and asphalt store heat by day and release it at night; little vegetation means less energy used in evapotranspiration; narrow streets lower the sky view factor, trapping longwave radiation; heating, air conditioning and traffic add anthropogenic heat; pollution can absorb and re-emit longwave radiation.
- **Humidity:** rain drains rapidly into sewers, so less water evaporates and relative humidity is usually lower than in the countryside, although warmer air can still hold more vapour.
- **Precipitation:** summer thunderstorms may be triggered or intensified by rising warm air and by condensation nuclei from pollution; effects tend to be greatest downwind.
- **Winds:** greater surface roughness lowers average wind speeds; tall buildings around districts such as the City and Canary Wharf can produce strong gusts and wind tunnel effects at street level.
- **Responses:** green roofs, street trees and parks, light-coloured surfaces, and planning guidance on tall buildings and wind. These lower the heat island and improve comfort, but their effect city-wide depends on how widely they are adopted.

Any city can be used; choose one where you can describe all four elements with real evidence.

## Annotated model paragraph

*"Explain why the urban heat island is strongest on calm, clear nights."*

> By day, urban materials with low albedo absorb and store heat (**storage**). On clear nights, rural areas lose longwave radiation quickly (**rural cooling**), but urban surfaces release stored heat and narrow streets trap longwave radiation (**urban warmth**). Calm air means no wind mixes the warm urban air with cooler rural air (**condition**), so the contrast is greatest (**conclusion**).

## Exam traps

- Distinguish the natural greenhouse effect (essential) from the enhanced one (human).
- Use data from the case study only if you are sure of it; otherwise write in general terms.
- Do not say cities make rain everywhere: effects are strongest downwind.
- Name causes and consequences, then link them.
- The greenhouse effect is not the same as ozone depletion; CFCs are linked to both, but the processes differ.
- Cover all four elements (temperature, humidity, precipitation, winds) in a full urban climate answer.
