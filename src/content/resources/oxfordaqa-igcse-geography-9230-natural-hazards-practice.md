## Section A

**1.** Define the term natural hazard. **[1]**

**2.** State the difference between a hazard and a disaster. **[2]**

**3.** Name **two** types of hazard that are caused by processes in the atmosphere. **[2]**

**4.** What is meant by a secondary hazard? **[1]**

**5.** Give **two** characteristics of a hazard that affect the level of risk. **[2]**

## Section B

The table shows information about two invented coastal towns that are both exposed to the same type of tropical storm.

| | Town A | Town B |
|---|---|---|
| Population | 400 000 | 20 000 |
| Homes built from weak materials | 60% | 10% |
| Storm shelters | None | Several |
| Warning system | Radio only, patchy coverage | Sirens and text messages |
| Average income | Low | High |

**6.** Using the table, describe **two** differences between the towns. **[2]**

**7.** Explain why the risk from a storm is higher in Town A than in Town B. **[4]**

**8.** Suggest **two** ways in which the risk to Town A could be reduced. **[4]**

**9.** Town B suffers fewer deaths than Town A in a storm, but a larger bill for damage. Suggest why. **[3]**

**10.** Explain how an earthquake can lead to a secondary hazard. **[3]**

**11.** Explain why deforestation on hillsides can increase the risk from landslides and floods. **[4]**

**12.** Describe how the speed of onset of a hazard affects the risk to people. **[3]**

**13.** "Wealth is the most important factor in reducing hazard risk." To what extent do you agree? **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** A natural event or process that threatens people or property [1]. **[1]**

**2.** A hazard is a threat or potential danger [1]. A disaster is when a hazard actually happens and causes serious harm to a community [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Any two from: tropical storm; tornado; drought; heatwave; severe cold spell [1 each]. Accept hurricane, cyclone or typhoon. Do not accept flood alone (hydrological). **[2]**

**4.** A hazard that is triggered by a primary hazard, for example a tsunami or landslide caused by an earthquake [1]. **[1]**

**5.** Any two from: magnitude; frequency; duration; speed of onset; predictability; spatial extent [1 each]. **[2]**

**6.** Any two differences, 1 mark each, with a comparison: Town A has a much larger population (400 000 compared with 20 000) [1]; Town A has far more weak homes (60% compared with 10%) [1]; Town A has no shelters whereas Town B has several [1]; Town A has a patchy radio warning, Town B has sirens and texts [1]. **[2]**

**7.** The storm is the same, so the difference is in vulnerability and capacity to cope [1]. Town A has a larger population, so more people are exposed [1]. 60% of its homes are weak, so many are likely to be destroyed or damaged [1]. There are no shelters and the warning is patchy, so people cannot protect themselves or may not know to leave [1]. Low incomes mean less money to rebuild or evacuate [1]. Maximum 4. **[4]**

**8.** Any two ways, each with a developed reason (1 mark for the way, 1 for the reason): build storm shelters so that people have somewhere safe to go [2]; improve the warning system with sirens or mobile phone alerts so that people have time to evacuate [2]; strengthen homes or enforce building codes so that fewer collapse [2]; run education and drills so that people know what to do [2]. **[4]**

**9.** Town B has strong buildings, shelters and a good warning, so fewer people are killed [1]. Its homes and businesses are worth more, and it has more valuable property and goods such as cars and equipment [1], so the cost of damage is higher even when damage is limited [1]. **[3]**

**10.** An earthquake shakes the ground and can destabilise slopes [1], which can set off landslides that block roads or bury homes [1]. If the epicentre is under the sea, the movement of the seabed can displace water and cause a tsunami [1]. (Accept fires from broken gas pipes, or liquefaction.) Maximum 3. **[3]**

**11.** Trees intercept rainfall and their roots bind the soil [1]. When trees are removed, more rainwater reaches the ground and runs off quickly [1]. Loose soil is easier to wash away, so landslides become more likely on steep slopes [1]. Rapid runoff reaches rivers quickly and increases the chance of flooding downstream [1]. **[4]**

**12.** A sudden onset, such as an earthquake, gives no warning, so people cannot evacuate [1]. A slow onset, such as drought or a river flood, gives time to prepare or move [1]. The shorter the warning, the higher the risk of death and injury [1]. **[3]**

**13.** Indicative content:
- Agree: wealth pays for monitoring, warning systems and emergency services [1].
- Agree: wealth pays for strong buildings and defences that reduce damage [1].
- Agree: wealthier households can insure, evacuate and rebuild more easily [1].
- Disagree: governance and enforcement matter; a rich country with poor planning can still suffer [1].
- Disagree: education, drills and community preparedness can reduce harm at low cost [1].
- Disagree: a very large event can overwhelm any country, so the hazard's magnitude also matters [1].
- Judgement: wealth is important because it enables most protection measures, but it is not enough alone; a justified conclusion is needed [1]. Maximum 6. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "disaster" when the question asks about a hazard.
- Stating a factor with no effect (for example, "poverty").
- Comparing two places by describing them one after the other, without linking.
- Forgetting to use the data in the table for Questions 6 and 7.
- Saying that wealthy countries do not suffer from hazards.
- Giving a judgement in a "to what extent" question with no reasons.
