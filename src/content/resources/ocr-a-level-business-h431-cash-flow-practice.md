## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by a cash-flow forecast. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between cash-flow and profit. **[3]**

**3.** A business has an opening balance of £6,000, receipts of £24,000 and payments of £21,000 in a month. Calculate the closing balance. **[2]**

**4.** Which **one** of the following is a cash outflow?

A: Receiving a bank loan.

B: Customers paying an invoice.

C: Paying a loan repayment.

D: Selling an old van. **[1]**

**5.** Explain **one** reason why depreciation does not appear in a cash-flow forecast. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Nethercott Pottery has an opening bank balance of £6,000 on 1 July. It has produced this forecast of receipts and payments.

| | July | August | September | October |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Receipts | £24,000 | £19,000 | £22,000 | £28,000 |
| Payments | £21,000 | £25,000 | £27,000 | £24,000 |

**(a)** Calculate the net cash flow and the closing balance for each month. **[4]**

**(b)** State the month in which the business first goes overdrawn and the largest overdraft. **[2]**

**(c)** Receipts in August and September turn out to be 10% lower than forecast. Calculate the new closing balance for each month and the largest overdraft. **[5]**

**(d)** Evaluate **two** strategies that Nethercott Pottery could use to deal with the shortage in part (c). **[8]**

**7.** In one month a builder invoices £50,000 for work on credit. The customer pays nothing that month. The builder pays £30,000 of costs in the month. Calculate the profit for the month and the net cash flow, and explain what this shows. **[5]**

**8.** Explain **two** reasons why a bank might ask for a cash-flow forecast before lending to a new business. **[4]**

**9.** Evaluate the usefulness of cash-flow forecasting to a new restaurant and its stakeholders. **[8]**

**10.** A manufacturer reports a profit of £90,000 but its cash balance has fallen by £35,000. Analyse **two** possible reasons for the difference. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** A cash-flow forecast is an estimate of the cash a business expects to receive and pay out [1] in future periods, usually month by month, showing the expected cash balance at the end of each [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Profit is revenue earned less costs incurred in a period [1]. Cash-flow is the money actually received and paid in a period [1]. They differ because of credit sales, the timing of payments for equipment and non-cash items such as depreciation [1]. **[3]**

**3.** £6,000 + £24,000 − £21,000 [1] = **£9,000** [1]. **[2]**

**4.** **C** [1]. **[1]**

**5.** Depreciation is an accounting cost that spreads the price of an asset over its life [1], but no cash leaves the business in that period; the cash left when the asset was bought [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** July: +£3,000, closing £9,000. August: −£6,000, closing £3,000. September: −£5,000, closing −£2,000. October: +£4,000, closing £2,000 [1] [1] [1] [1]. **[4]**

**(b)** September [1]; the largest overdraft is £2,000 [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** August receipts = £19,000 × 0.9 = £17,100; September receipts = £22,000 × 0.9 = £19,800 [1]. August: net −£7,900, closing £1,100 [1]. September: net −£7,200, closing −£6,100 [1]. October: net +£4,000, closing −£2,100 [1]. The largest overdraft is **£6,100** in September [1]. **[5]**

**(d)** Overdraft: it covers the shortage quickly and is flexible; the interest on £6,100 at 12% for two months is about £122 [1]. But the bank may refuse it or charge a high rate for a business already in difficulty [1].
Chasing customers for earlier payment or offering a small discount: cash comes in sooner without borrowing [1], but discounts reduce revenue and customers may refuse [1].
Delaying payments to suppliers: it eases the shortage at no direct cost [1] but may damage relationships or lose supply or discounts [1].
Judgement: the overdraft is the most suitable for a temporary, small shortage of about £6,100 that is expected to reverse in October [1]; it should be combined with faster collection if the problem is likely to recur [1]. (Other sensible strategies may be credited. Maximum 8 marks.) **[8]**

**7.** Profit = £50,000 − £30,000 = **£20,000** [1] [1]. Net cash flow = £0 − £30,000 = **−£30,000** [1] [1]. This shows that a business can be profitable and still run out of cash, because the sale is counted when made but the cash arrives only when the customer pays [1]. **[5]**

**8.** A forecast shows whether the business will have enough cash to make loan repayments [1], so the bank can judge the risk of default [1]. It shows how much and for how long the business will need to borrow [1], which helps the bank set the size and length of the loan [1]. It also shows how well the owner has planned [1]. (Maximum 4 marks.) **[4]**

**9.** For: a restaurant has high fixed costs and perishable stock, so a forecast shows when cash will be short [1]; it helps plan the opening period when income is low [1]; it supports a loan application [1]; it allows "what if" tests, for example if customers are fewer than hoped [1].
Stakeholders: owners can see if they need to put in more money [1]; lenders see risk [1]; suppliers may offer credit if they are shown good planning [1].
Limits: a new restaurant has no history, so sales estimates are guesses [1]; the forecast must be updated often [1].
Judgement: very useful for a new restaurant if it is realistic, updated and includes a downside scenario [1]. (Maximum 8 marks.) **[8]**

**10.** Reason 1: customers have taken longer to pay, so receivables have risen [1]. Sales are counted as profit when made but the cash has not arrived [1]. Reason 2: the business bought new equipment or built up inventory [1]. Equipment cost is spread through depreciation in profit but paid in full in cash; inventory is paid for before it is sold [1]. Other reasons: repaying a loan or paying dividends do not reduce profit but do reduce cash [1]. A comment on the effect: the manufacturer may need an overdraft unless the cash is recovered [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Counting credit sales as cash in the month of sale.
- Forgetting to carry the closing balance forward.
- Treating depreciation as a cash payment.
- Suggesting strategies without cost or risk.
- Not linking the strategy to the cause of the shortage.
