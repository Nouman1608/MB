## 1. Absorption costing

Absorption costing gives each product a **full cost**: its direct costs plus a share of the
production overheads.

```
Total (full) cost per unit = direct materials + direct labour + direct expenses
                             + production overheads absorbed
```

### The terms

- **Allocation**: charging a whole cost to the one product or cost centre that caused it. Direct
  materials and direct labour are allocated to products. An overhead that belongs entirely to one
  department, such as that department's supervisor, is allocated to that department.
- **Apportionment**: sharing an indirect cost between cost centres on a fair basis, because no single
  centre caused it. Rent is shared on floor area, for example.
- **Absorption**: charging each department's overheads to the products that pass through it, using an
  **overhead absorption rate (OAR)**.
- **Under-absorption**: overheads absorbed into products are **less** than the overheads actually
  incurred.
- **Over-absorption**: overheads absorbed are **more** than the overheads actually incurred.

### Choosing the apportionment basis

| Indirect cost | Usual basis |
|---|---|
| Rent, rates, heating, building insurance | Floor area |
| Depreciation and insurance of machinery | Cost or value of machinery |
| Power for machines | Machine hours (or a power meter reading) |
| Canteen, staff welfare, personnel costs | Number of employees |

### Overhead absorption rates

The OAR is set **in advance**, from budgeted figures:

```
OAR = budgeted overheads of the cost centre ÷ budgeted level of activity
```

Pick the activity that drives the department's costs:

- **per machine hour** in a machine-intensive department
- **per direct labour hour** in a labour-intensive department
- **per unit** only when every unit is identical.

### Worked example 1: Brackenfield Furniture

Brackenfield makes dining tables in two production departments, Machining and Assembly. Budgeted
overheads for the year:

| Overhead | £ | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Indirect labour: Machining £38 000, Assembly £51 000 | 89 000 | Allocated |
| Rent and rates | 60 000 | Floor area: M 1 200 m², A 1 800 m² |
| Depreciation of machinery | 45 000 | Machinery cost: M £180 000, A £45 000 |
| Power | 30 000 | Machine hours: M 40 000, A 10 000 |
| Canteen | 18 000 | Employees: M 10, A 20 |
| **Total** | **242 000** | |

**Step 1: allocate and apportion.**

| | Machining £ | Assembly £ |
|---|---|---|
| Indirect labour (allocated) | 38 000 | 51 000 |
| Rent (1 200 : 1 800) | 24 000 | 36 000 |
| Depreciation (180 000 : 45 000) | 36 000 | 9 000 |
| Power (40 000 : 10 000) | 24 000 | 6 000 |
| Canteen (10 : 20) | 6 000 | 12 000 |
| **Total** | **128 000** | **114 000** |

Check: 128 000 + 114 000 = £242 000.

**Step 2: OARs.** Machining is machine-intensive; Assembly is labour-intensive, with 30 000 budgeted
direct labour hours.

- Machining: 128 000 ÷ 40 000 = **£3.20 per machine hour**
- Assembly: 114 000 ÷ 30 000 = **£3.80 per direct labour hour**

**Step 3: full cost of one table.** Each table uses £84.40 of materials, 5 direct labour hours at £12
(2 in Machining, 3 in Assembly), and 6 machine hours in Machining.

| | £ |
|---|---|
| Direct materials | 84.40 |
| Direct labour (5 × £12) | 60.00 |
| Machining overheads (6 × £3.20) | 19.20 |
| Assembly overheads (3 × £3.80) | 11.40 |
| **Total cost** | **175.00** |

### Under- and over-absorption

Because the OAR uses budgets, overheads absorbed rarely equal actual overheads.

```
Overheads absorbed = OAR × actual level of activity
Difference         = overheads absorbed − actual overheads
  positive → over-absorbed  (too much charged to products: add back to profit)
  negative → under-absorbed (too little charged: deduct from profit)
```

**Brackenfield, actual results:**

| | Machining | Assembly |
|---|---|---|
| Actual activity | 41 500 machine hours | 28 800 labour hours |
| Absorbed | 41 500 × 3.20 = £132 800 | 28 800 × 3.80 = £109 440 |
| Actual overheads | £131 000 | £113 200 |
| Result | **£1 800 over-absorbed** | **£3 760 under-absorbed** |

Overall, overheads are under-absorbed by 3 760 − 1 800 = **£1 960**. The costs charged to products
were £1 960 too low, so £1 960 is deducted from profit in the statement of profit or loss.

Causes are a different actual level of activity from the budget, a different actual spend, or both.
Assembly worked 1 200 fewer hours than budgeted, so it absorbed 1 200 × 3.80 = £4 560 too little; spending
£800 below the £114 000 budget offsets part of this, leaving £3 760 under-absorbed.

## 2. Activity based costing (ABC)

A labour-hour rate assumes overheads are caused by labour time. Many are not: a product made in small
batches needs more set-ups, inspections and orders, whatever its labour hours. ABC charges overheads
according to the activities that cause them.

### The terms

- **Cost pool**: a group of overhead costs caused by the same activity, such as all machine set-up
  costs.
- **Cost driver**: the factor that causes the cost of a pool to change, such as the number of
  set-ups.
- **Attribution**: charging the cost of each pool to products according to how much of the driver
  each product uses.

### Method in steps

1. Allocate direct costs to each product.
2. Group overheads into cost pools and identify each pool's cost driver.
3. Cost driver rate = cost of the pool ÷ total quantity of the driver.
4. Attribute: driver rate × driver quantity used by each product.
5. Divide each product's attributed overheads by its units; add to direct costs.

### Worked example 2: Tilby Audio

Tilby makes two speakers. Production overheads total £220 000.

| | Standard | Premium |
|---|---|---|
| Units made | 10 000 | 2 500 |
| Direct materials per unit | £34 | £80 |
| Direct labour per unit (£12 an hour) | 2 hours, £24 | 3 hours, £36 |
| Machine hours per unit | 1 | 4 |

**Absorption costing, one rate per direct labour hour:** labour hours = (10 000 × 2) + (2 500 × 3) =
27 500. OAR = 220 000 ÷ 27 500 = **£8 per hour**. Standard absorbs £16; Premium absorbs £24.

**ABC.** Tilby's overheads fall into four cost pools:

| Cost pool | £ | Cost driver | Standard | Premium | Driver rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Machine set-ups | 60 000 | Number of set-ups | 10 | 30 | £1 500 |
| Quality inspections | 50 000 | Number of inspections | 50 | 150 | £250 |
| Materials ordering | 40 000 | Number of orders | 80 | 80 | £250 |
| Machine running | 70 000 | Machine hours | 10 000 | 10 000 | £3.50 |

Attribution:

| | Standard £ | Premium £ |
|---|---|---|
| Set-ups | 15 000 | 45 000 |
| Inspections | 12 500 | 37 500 |
| Ordering | 20 000 | 20 000 |
| Machine running | 35 000 | 35 000 |
| **Total** | **82 500** | **137 500** |
| Per unit | 82 500 ÷ 10 000 = **£8.25** | 137 500 ÷ 2 500 = **£55.00** |

Check: 82 500 + 137 500 = £220 000.

| Cost per unit | Standard £ | Premium £ |
|---|---|---|
| Direct costs | 58.00 | 116.00 |
| Overheads: absorption | 16.00 | 24.00 |
| **Total: absorption** | **74.00** | **140.00** |
| Overheads: ABC | 8.25 | 55.00 |
| **Total: ABC** | **66.25** | **171.00** |

Low-volume Premium uses most set-ups and inspections; the labour-hour rate loads those costs onto
Standard.

## 3. Setting a selling price

Both methods give a total cost, so a price can be set by adding a profit:

```
Mark-up (% of cost):          price = total cost × (100 + mark-up) ÷ 100
Margin (% of selling price):  price = total cost ÷ (100 − margin) × 100
```

**Brackenfield's table** (total cost £175): a 40% mark-up gives 175 × 1.4 = **£245**; a 30% margin
gives 175 ÷ 0.7 = **£250**. Check the margin: (250 − 175) ÷ 250 = 30%.

**Tilby, 20% mark-up:**

| | Standard | Premium |
|---|---|---|
| Absorption cost × 1.2 | 74.00 × 1.2 = **£88.80** | 140.00 × 1.2 = **£168.00** |
| ABC cost × 1.2 | 66.25 × 1.2 = **£79.50** | 171.00 × 1.2 = **£205.20** |

Priced from absorption cost, Premium sells at £168, below its £171 ABC cost, losing money on each
sale; Standard is over-priced by £9.30.

## 4. Comparing absorption, ABC and marginal costing

### Inventory valuation and profit

- **Marginal costing** values inventory at **variable production cost**. All fixed production
  overheads are charged in full against the period's profit.
- **Absorption costing** values inventory at **full production cost**, carrying some fixed production
  overheads forward.
- **ABC** also uses full production cost, but with different overhead per unit, so valuations differ.

When production equals sales, profit is the same under all three. When inventory rises, absorption and
ABC profits are higher than marginal costing profit by the fixed overheads carried forward in the
increase in inventory.

### Worked example 3: Tilby's profit three ways

In the year Tilby sold 9 000 Standard at £90 and 2 000 Premium at £200. There was no opening
inventory. Closing inventory is 1 000 Standard and 500 Premium. Treat labour as variable and all
production overheads (£220 000) as fixed, with actual overheads equal to the budget. Selling and
administration costs are £60 000.

| £ | Marginal | Absorption | ABC |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 1 210 000 | 1 210 000 | 1 210 000 |
| Cost of production | 870 000 | 1 090 000 | 1 090 000 |
| Less closing inventory | (116 000) | (144 000) | (151 750) |
| Cost of sales | 754 000 | 946 000 | 938 250 |
| Contribution / gross profit | 456 000 | 264 000 | 271 750 |
| Fixed production overheads | (220 000) | – | – |
| Selling and administration | (60 000) | (60 000) | (60 000) |
| **Profit for the year** | **176 000** | **204 000** | **211 750** |

Reconcile with marginal profit:

- Absorption: 1 000 × £16 + 500 × £24 = £28 000 of overheads carried forward; 176 000 + 28 000 =
  £204 000.
- ABC: 1 000 × £8.25 + 500 × £55 = £35 750; 176 000 + 35 750 = £211 750.

ABC profit is highest because unsold Premium units carry a large ABC overhead.

### Benefits and limitations

**Absorption costing**

- Benefits: prices set from full cost aim to cover all production costs; fixed production costs are
  matched with the inventory they helped to make (accruals); simple to apply.
- Limitations: apportionment bases are arbitrary; one rate ignores what causes costs; profit can be
  raised by producing for inventory; budgeted rates cause under- or over-absorption; full cost misleads
  short-term decisions.

**Activity based costing**

- Benefits: more accurate product costs and prices; shows which products are really profitable;
  managers can cut costs by cutting the driver (fewer set-ups or orders).
- Limitations: costly to set up and maintain; choosing pools and drivers needs judgement; some
  overheads (such as rent) have no clear driver; little gain if products are similar.

**Marginal costing**

- Benefits: no apportionment; profit moves with sales, not production; contribution suits short-term
  decisions.
- Limitations: inventory excludes fixed production overheads; prices based on marginal cost may not
  cover fixed costs in the long run; semi-variable costs must be split by estimate.

## Common errors

- Absorbing on **budgeted** activity when the question asks for overheads absorbed: absorbed always
  uses **actual** activity × the predetermined OAR.
- Mixing up the signs: absorbed more than actual is **over**-absorbed and adds to profit.
- Using a margin as a mark-up: a 30% margin on £175 is £250, not £227.50.
- Dividing an ABC pool by units made instead of by total driver quantity.
- Forgetting to divide attributed overheads by units to get a per-unit figure.
- Claiming absorption profit is always higher: only when inventory rises.
