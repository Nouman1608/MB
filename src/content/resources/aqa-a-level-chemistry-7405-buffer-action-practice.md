These questions cover section **3.1.12.6 Buffer action** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is A-level only content, examined on Paper 1 and Paper 3. Learn the ideas first in the [Buffer action study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-buffer-action/) and the [Buffer action revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-buffer-action-revision-notes/). Use Ka(ethanoic acid) = 1.75 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ unless another value is given, and Mᵣ(CH₃COONa) = 82.0.

## Questions

**1.** State what is meant by a buffer solution and name the two components of an acidic buffer. **[3]**

**2.** A buffer is made from ethanoic acid and sodium ethanoate.

**(a)** Explain how the buffer removes a small amount of added acid. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain how the buffer removes a small amount of added alkali. **[2]**

**3.** Calculate the pH of a buffer containing 0.150 mol dm⁻³ of a weak acid HA and 0.250 mol dm⁻³ of its sodium salt. Ka of HA = 6.3 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³. **[3]**

**4.** 25.0 cm³ of 0.400 mol dm⁻³ ethanoic acid is mixed with 20.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ sodium hydroxide solution. Calculate the pH of the mixture and explain why it is a buffer. **[5]**

**5.** A student wants a buffer of pH 4.50 using 0.100 mol of ethanoic acid. Calculate the mass of sodium ethanoate that must be added. **[4]**

**6.** Calculate the pH of a solution containing 0.100 mol dm⁻³ methanoic acid and 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ sodium methanoate. Ka of methanoic acid = 1.78 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³. **[3]**

**7.** A 250 cm³ buffer contains 0.0400 mol of ethanoic acid and 0.0400 mol of sodium ethanoate.

**(a)** State the pH of the buffer. **[1]**

**(b)** 0.0050 mol of sodium hydroxide is added. Calculate the new pH, assuming the volume stays at 250 cm³. **[3]**

**(c)** Calculate the pH if the same amount of sodium hydroxide is added to 250 cm³ of pure water. **[2]**

**8.** A buffer contains aqueous ammonia and ammonium chloride. Explain how it resists change in pH when a small amount of acid is added and when a small amount of alkali is added. **[4]**

**9.** Explain why a solution of ethanoic acid alone is not an effective buffer against added acid. **[2]**

**10.** The pH of blood is kept between about 7.35 and 7.45 by a buffer system containing carbonic acid and hydrogencarbonate ions.

**(a)** Write an equation to show how the system reacts with added acid. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why the pH of blood must be controlled. **[1]**

**11.** Explain why diluting a buffer with water causes almost no change in pH. **[2]**

**12.** A buffer contains 0.200 mol dm⁻³ of a weak acid HA and 0.100 mol dm⁻³ of its sodium salt. Its pH is 3.95. Calculate Ka of HA. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** A buffer is a solution that maintains an approximately constant pH when small amounts of acid or base are added or the solution is diluted [1]; an acidic buffer contains a weak acid [1] and a salt of that weak acid [1]. **[3]**

**2. (a)** Added H⁺ reacts with ethanoate ions: CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺ → CH₃COOH [1]; most of the added H⁺ is removed, so the ratio [CH₃COOH] ÷ [CH₃COO⁻] changes very little and so does the pH [1]

**(b)** Added OH⁻ reacts with ethanoic acid (or with H⁺, causing more acid to dissociate): CH₃COOH + OH⁻ → CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O [1]; the ratio of acid to ethanoate changes only slightly, so the pH changes very little [1]. **[4]**

**3.** [H⁺] = Ka × [HA] ÷ [A⁻] [1]; [H⁺] = 6.3 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.150 ÷ 0.250 = 3.78 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ [1]; pH = **4.42** [1]. **[3]**

**4.** n(CH₃COOH) = 0.400 × 0.0250 = 1.00 × 10⁻² mol and n(NaOH) = 0.100 × 0.0200 = 2.00 × 10⁻³ mol [1]; n(CH₃COO⁻) formed = 2.00 × 10⁻³ mol and n(CH₃COOH) left = 8.00 × 10⁻³ mol [1]; [H⁺] = 1.75 × 10⁻⁵ × 8.00 × 10⁻³ ÷ 2.00 × 10⁻³ = 7.00 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ [1]; pH = **4.15** [1]; the mixture contains a weak acid and its salt in similar amounts, so it is a buffer [1]. **[5]**

**5.** [H⁺] = 10^(–4.50) = 3.16 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ [1]; [HA] ÷ [A⁻] = [H⁺] ÷ Ka = 3.16 × 10⁻⁵ ÷ 1.75 × 10⁻⁵ = 1.81 [1]; n(CH₃COONa) = 0.100 ÷ 1.81 = 0.0553 mol [1]; mass = 0.0553 × 82.0 = **4.54 g** [1]. **[4]**

**6.** [H⁺] = Ka × [HA] ÷ [A⁻] = 1.78 × 10⁻⁴ × 0.100 ÷ 0.0500 [1]; [H⁺] = 3.56 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ [1]; pH = **3.45** [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** [HA] = [A⁻], so [H⁺] = Ka and pH = **4.76** [1]

**(b)** n(CH₃COOH) = 0.0400 – 0.0050 = 0.0350 mol and n(CH₃COO⁻) = 0.0450 mol [1]; [H⁺] = 1.75 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.0350 ÷ 0.0450 = 1.36 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ [1]; pH = **4.87** [1]

**(c)** [OH⁻] = 0.0050 ÷ 0.250 = 0.0200 mol dm⁻³ [1]; [H⁺] = 5.0 × 10⁻¹³ and pH = **12.30** [1]. **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* The buffer's pH rose by about 0.11 units, but the pH of water rose by about 5. This comparison is the clearest way to show what a buffer does.

**8.** Added acid: H⁺ reacts with NH₃ to form NH₄⁺ (NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺) [1], so the ammonia removes most of the added acid and the pH changes little [1]. Added alkali: OH⁻ reacts with NH₄⁺ to form NH₃ and water (NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ → NH₃ + H₂O) [1], so the ammonium ions remove most of the added alkali and the pH changes little [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Ethanoic acid is only slightly dissociated, so very little ethanoate ion is present [1]; there is no reserve of base to react with added H⁺, so the added H⁺ stays in solution, [H⁺] rises and the pH falls significantly [1]. **[2]**

**10. (a)** HCO₃⁻ + H⁺ → H₂CO₃ [1]

**(b)** Enzymes and other biological processes work only in a narrow pH range, so a change can stop them working [1]. **[2]**

**11.** Dilution reduces the concentrations of the acid and the salt by the same factor [1]; the ratio [HA] ÷ [A⁻] is unchanged, so [H⁺] and the pH are almost unchanged [1]. **[2]**

**12.** [H⁺] = 10^(–3.95) = 1.12 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ [1]; Ka = [H⁺][A⁻] ÷ [HA] = 1.12 × 10⁻⁴ × 0.100 ÷ 0.200 [1]; Ka = **5.6 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³** [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying a buffer "stops" pH changing instead of "minimises" it.
- Naming the components without stating that one is a weak acid and the other its salt.
- Writing the ratio upside down in [H⁺] = Ka × [HA] ÷ [A⁻].
- Not subtracting the added alkali from the acid and adding it to the salt.
- Omitting the equation when explaining the buffer action.
