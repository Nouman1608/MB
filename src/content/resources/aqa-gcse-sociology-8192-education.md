This guide covers section 3.4 Education (3.4.1 to 3.4.4) of the AQA GCSE Sociology (8192) specification, Version 1.2 (June 2026), for GCSE exams 2019 onwards. Education is assessed in Paper 1: The sociology of families and education, a written exam of 1 hour 45 minutes worth 100 marks and 50% of the GCSE. Each of its two sections opens with two multiple-choice questions, then short and extended responses.

## 3.4.1 Roles and functions of education

### The three functions the specification lists

- **Serving the needs of the economy:** schools teach skills and qualifications that employers need, and sift people into jobs.
- **Facilitating social mobility:** qualifications let people move up (or down) the class structure, regardless of where they started.
- **Fostering social cohesion:** schools pass on shared norms and values that bind people into one society.

### Durkheim

Durkheim (*Moral Education*, 1925) saw the main function of education as the **transmission of society's norms and values**. Every society must weld a mass of separate individuals into a united whole. Teaching, especially of history, links the individual to society, so children see they belong to something bigger than themselves. School is also where children learn to cooperate with people who are neither kin nor friends. Durkheim believed rules should be strictly enforced so that children learn self-discipline and see that misbehaviour harms society as a whole.

### Parsons

Parsons ("The school class as a social system") saw the school as a **bridge between the family and society**. It takes over as the main agency of socialisation and prepares children for adult life.

- In the family, children are judged by **particularistic standards**: rules that apply only to them as a particular son or daughter.
- In school and wider society, people are judged by **universalistic standards**: the same rules for everyone, regardless of kinship.
- Schools run on **meritocratic principles**. Status is **achieved** through merit, not **ascribed** at birth.
- Schools socialise pupils into a **value consensus** stressing achievement and equality of opportunity.
- Schools **select** individuals for their future roles in society.

**Evaluation.** Marxists argue the values schools pass on may simply be those of the ruling elite. Critics also say equality of opportunity is an illusion in an unequal society, where wealth and privilege matter more than merit. Feminists add that a "shared" culture has often reflected men's interests.

### Other perspectives on the functions of education

- **Marxists** see education as serving capitalism, not society as a whole.
- **Feminists** argue education has reproduced gender inequality through a **gendered curriculum**, subject choices and the **hidden curriculum** (the lessons taught by how school is organised, not by the timetable).

### Types of school

| Type | Key features |
|---|---|
| Primary | Usually ages 4 or 5 to 11 |
| Secondary | Usually ages 11 to 16 or 18 |
| State | Funded by taxation, free to attend; includes comprehensive schools, academies and free schools |
| Selective (grammar) | State schools that select by an entrance test such as the eleven plus |
| Private (independent, fee-paying) | Funded mainly by fees |

The **tripartite system** set up after the Education Act 1944 used the eleven plus to send pupils to grammar, secondary technical or secondary modern schools. Most areas later moved to comprehensive schools, which take pupils of all abilities.

### Alternative forms of provision

- **Home schooling:** parents educate their child at home instead of sending them to school. In England this is legal, but parents must make sure the child receives a suitable full-time education.
- **De-schooling:** the idea, linked to Ivan Illich (*Deschooling Society*, 1971), that formal schooling should be replaced. Illich argued schools teach pupils to depend on institutions and confuse being taught with learning.

### Worked paragraph (knowledge, application, analysis, evaluation)

> Durkheim argued that education fosters social cohesion by passing on shared norms and values. For example, when a school teaches British history to pupils from many backgrounds, they come to see themselves as part of one larger society. This matters because, for Durkheim, without shared values a society of separate individuals would fall apart. However, Marxists would reply that the values taught are those of the ruling class, so what looks like cohesion is really acceptance of inequality.

## 3.4.2 The relationship between education and capitalism

### Bowles and Gintis

Bowles and Gintis (*Schooling in Capitalist America*, 1976) wrote from a Marxist perspective. Their key ideas:

- The major role of education in capitalist societies is the **reproduction of labour power**: producing the kind of workers capitalism needs.
- There is a close **correspondence** between social relationships at school and social relationships at work. This is the **correspondence principle**.
- Through this, school creates a **hardworking, docile, obedient and highly motivated workforce** that is **too divided to challenge the authority of management**.
- They **reject the view that capitalist societies are meritocratic**. Class background is the most important factor in attainment.

| At school | At work |
|---|---|
| Pupils obey teachers and the head | Workers obey supervisors and managers |
| Pupils work for grades, not interest in learning | Workers work for wages, not satisfaction |
| Subjects split into separate lessons | Work split into narrow tasks |
| Pupils compete and are ranked | Workers compete for promotion |

### Alternative views of the correspondence principle

- **Willis** (Marxist, 1977) rejects a direct relationship between the economy and schooling. Education is not a very successful agency of socialisation: the working-class "lads" he studied resisted school rather than becoming obedient. Yet their resistance still led them into manual work.
- **Functionalists** such as Parsons accept that schools prepare pupils for work, but see this as fair role allocation in a meritocracy, not exploitation.
- **Feminists** argue the hidden curriculum also prepares girls and boys for different, unequal roles, so the correspondence is with patriarchy as well as capitalism.
- **Interactionists** say the theory treats pupils as passive, when meanings are negotiated in classrooms.

**Evaluation.** The theory was based on 1970s American schools, and many jobs now value creativity over obedience. But the continuing link between class and attainment supports their rejection of meritocracy.

### Worked paragraph

> Bowles and Gintis argue that school corresponds to work: pupils work for grades just as workers work for wages. A pupil who revises only because a test score is published is learning to value rewards outside the task itself, which is what a factory or office needs. This suggests schools reproduce labour power rather than develop individual talent. However, Willis found that some working-class boys rejected the school's values, so school does not always produce docile workers, even if the outcome for those boys was still manual work.

## 3.4.3 Educational achievement

### Class

**Halsey, Heath and Ridge** (*Origins and Destinations*, 1980) studied a sample of over 8,000 males born between 1913 and 1952. They divided the sample into three groups by father's occupation:

1. **Service class:** professionals, administrators and managers.
2. **Intermediate class:** clerical or sales workers, the self-employed, lower grade technicians and foremen.
3. **Working class:** manual workers in industry and agriculture.

Compared with a working-class boy, a service-class boy had four times the chance of being at school at 16, eight times at 17 and ten times at 18. His chance of attending university was eleven times greater. **Limitation:** the research excluded females, which might have made a significant difference to the findings.

Other explanations of class differences:

- **Material deprivation:** lack of money for books, computers, a quiet place to study or a good diet.
- **Cultural deprivation:** the claim that some working-class families lack the attitudes and language that help at school. Critics say this blames the victim.
- **Cultural capital** (Bourdieu): middle-class knowledge, tastes and confidence that schools reward, giving those pupils a head start.

### Gender

Girls now outperform boys at most levels, including GCSE. Explanations include the impact of feminism on girls' ambitions, equal opportunities policies and fewer traditional manual jobs for boys. Yet subject choice remains gendered. Sue Sharpe (*Just Like a Girl*, 1976) found girls' priorities were love, marriage and children; when she repeated the study in the 1990s, jobs and careers came first.

### Ethnicity

Achievement varies widely between ethnic groups. In England, pupils of Chinese and Indian heritage achieve above the national average on average, while some other groups are below it. Explanations include:

- **Material factors**, since some ethnic groups are more likely to live in poverty.
- **Language** differences for pupils new to English.
- An **ethnocentric curriculum** that centres one culture.
- **Institutional racism** and teacher labelling. Official figures for England have shown higher exclusion rates for some groups, such as Black Caribbean pupils.

Class, gender and ethnicity overlap. A good answer asks how much of an "ethnic" gap is really a class gap.

### Ball on parental choice and competition between schools

**Ball, Bowe and Gewirtz** ("Market forces and parental choice", 1994; the specification prints the name as "Gerwitz") studied fifteen schools in neighbouring local education authorities (LEAs) with different class and ethnic profiles. They looked at the effects of **parental choice** and **league tables** (the **marketisation of education**). Schools felt pressure to **reintroduce streaming and setting** and some tended to **focus on the more able**, who improve a school's results. In a related book (*Markets, Choice and Equity in Education*, 1995), Gewirtz, Ball and Bowe argued that middle-class parents were better placed to use choice, so the market could widen class inequality.

### Perspectives

- **Functionalist:** differences in achievement reflect differences in ability and effort in a meritocratic system.
- **Marxist:** differences reflect class inequality that schools legitimise through the "myth of meritocracy".
- **Feminist:** girls' rising results are real progress, but gendered subject choice and the **glass ceiling** show inequality continues.

### Worked paragraph

> Halsey, Heath and Ridge found that a service-class boy was eleven times as likely as a working-class boy to attend university. If a school today sees most of its sixth form coming from professional families, this pattern would suggest class still shapes who stays in education. It challenges the functionalist claim that schools are meritocratic, because ability is unlikely to differ that much between classes. However, the sample excluded females and covered men born before 1953, so it may not describe education today.

## 3.4.4 Processes within schools

### Key terms

- **Streaming:** pupils placed in one ability group for all subjects.
- **Setting:** pupils placed in ability groups subject by subject.
- **Mixed ability teaching:** pupils of all abilities taught together.
- **Labelling:** attaching a definition to a pupil, such as "bright" or "troublemaker".
- **Self-fulfilling prophecy:** a label shapes how the pupil is treated and how they see themselves, until the prediction comes true.
- **Counter school subculture:** a group whose values oppose the school's.

### Ball on teacher expectations

Ball (*Beachside Comprehensive*, 1981) spent three years as a **participant observer** in a south coast comprehensive. He followed two cohorts: one **banded by ability** and one taught in **mixed ability** classes. Middle-class pupils were more likely than working-class pupils of similar ability to be placed in band 1, which met higher teacher expectations. Band 2 pupils were steered towards easier exams and practical subjects, and were the most likely to develop anti-school attitudes. The mixed ability cohort lets you compare the two ways of grouping pupils.

**Evaluation.** Participant observation gives detailed, valid data. But this is one school, so it may not be representative, and the researcher's presence can change behaviour.

### Willis on counter school cultures

Willis (*Learning to Labour*, 1977) studied twelve working-class boys, "the lads", at a school on a Midlands working-class housing estate. He used observation and participant observation, recorded group discussions, informal interviews and diaries, aiming to see school from the pupils' view.

- The lads formed a **counter culture** opposed to the school's values.
- They felt superior to teachers and to conformist pupils, whom they called "ear'oles".
- Their main aim was to avoid lessons and "have a laff". They resented the school controlling their time and were neither docile nor obedient.
- Willis concluded that rejecting school made them suitable for male-dominated, unskilled or semi-skilled manual work, which was relatively easy to get in the 1970s.

**Evaluation.** The sample of twelve boys is small and all male. Jobs of that kind are now much harder to find, so the link between counter culture and work has weakened.

### Perspectives on processes within schools

- **Interactionist:** labelling and the self-fulfilling prophecy explain achievement from inside the classroom (Ball).
- **Functionalist:** setting allocates pupils efficiently by ability, which is meritocratic.
- **Marxist:** setting and labelling sort pupils by class and prepare them for unequal jobs (Willis, Bowles and Gintis).
- **Feminist:** teacher expectations and the hidden curriculum can steer girls and boys into different subjects.

### Worked paragraph

> Ball found that at Beachside middle-class pupils were more likely to be placed in band 1, which received higher expectations. In a school that sets Year 9 maths by a test, a working-class pupil placed in a low set may be given easier work and come to see themselves as weak at maths, which is a self-fulfilling prophecy. This shows how an internal process can turn a label into lower achievement. However, Beachside was a single school, and factors outside school, such as material deprivation, also shape achievement.

## Common errors

- Writing that Durkheim talked about the correspondence principle. That is Bowles and Gintis.
- Confusing particularistic (family, child-specific) with universalistic (same rules for all) standards.
- Using Halsey's findings as if they covered girls. The sample was male only.
- Mixing up streaming (all subjects) and setting (each subject).
- Saying Willis's lads were docile workers in training. Willis argued the opposite: they resisted, yet still ended up in manual work.
- Treating Ball's two studies as one. Beachside (1981) is about teacher expectations; the 1994 study is about parental choice.
- Describing home schooling and de-schooling as the same thing.
