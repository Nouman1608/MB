This guide covers **4.2.1.5 Metallic bonding** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) specification, for teaching
from September 2016 onwards (version 1.1). It belongs to topic 4.2, Bonding, structure, and the properties of
matter, and is examined on Paper 1 at Foundation and Higher Tier. There are no (HT only) statements and no
required practical. The specification also names a skill for this subsection: recognising substances as
**metallic giant structures** from diagrams showing their bonding.

For a quick recap use the [metallic bonding revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-metallic-bonding-revision-notes/),
and test yourself with the [metallic bonding practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-metallic-bonding-practice/).
The broader [covalent and metallic bonding study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-covalent-and-metallic-bonding/)
introduces the model; this page adds electron counting, diagram checking and more varied examples.

## The specification in four statements

1. Metals consist of **giant structures of atoms** arranged in a **regular pattern**.
2. The **outer-shell electrons** of the metal atoms are **delocalised**.
3. Because they are delocalised, these electrons are **free to move through the whole structure**.
4. The **sharing of delocalised electrons** gives rise to **strong metallic bonds**.

The model drawn in the specification shows positive metal ions in rows, with electrons between them. Being
able to draw this and explain it with electrostatic attraction is the core of the subsection.

## Where the delocalised electrons come from

Each metal atom contributes its **outer-shell electrons** to the structure. What is left behind is a
positive ion, with a charge equal to the number of electrons released.

| Metal | Electronic structure | Delocalised electrons per atom | Ion left in the structure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sodium | 2,8,1 | 1 | Na⁺ |
| Magnesium | 2,8,2 | 2 | Mg²⁺ |
| Aluminium | 2,8,3 | 3 | Al³⁺ |
| Potassium | 2,8,8,1 | 1 | K⁺ |
| Calcium | 2,8,8,2 | 2 | Ca²⁺ |

Two consequences matter in questions:

- The structure is **electrically neutral overall**: the total negative charge of the delocalised electrons
  equals the total positive charge of the ions.
- The electrons are **not lost** from the metal. They have left their own atoms, but they stay within the
  structure.

## Atoms or ions?

The specification describes metals as giant structures of **atoms** and the particles in metallic bonding as
**atoms which share delocalised electrons**. The diagram of metallic bonding, however, labels the particles
as **positive ions**. Both descriptions are used because they describe the same thing from two angles: an
atom that has released its outer electrons into the shared "sea" is a positive ion.

In practice:

- When describing the **structure**, "a giant structure of metal atoms in a regular pattern" is accurate.
- When **explaining the bond**, say what attracts what: **positive metal ions** and **delocalised
  electrons**. That gives you the opposite charges an electrostatic explanation needs.

Never describe metals as containing **negative ions**, and never describe the electrons as being
transferred to another atom: that is ionic bonding.

## Explaining why metallic bonds are strong

The bond is the **strong electrostatic attraction** between the positive metal ions and the delocalised
electrons. Two features make this a strong bond for the structure as a whole:

- **Every ion is attracted to many electrons**, and every delocalised electron is attracted to many ions.
  There is no single partner, just as there is none in an ionic lattice.
- The attraction acts **throughout the whole giant structure**, holding the regular pattern together.

The specification links this model to the properties of metals and alloys later in topic 4.2 (for example,
high melting points and electrical conductivity). In 4.2.1.5 itself, the focus is the bond.

### Worked example 1: describing the bonding in aluminium

*Describe the structure and bonding in aluminium. Refer to the electrons involved.*

```
Structure:  giant structure of aluminium atoms in a regular pattern (layers)
Electrons:  each atom has 3 outer-shell electrons (2,8,3)
            these 3 electrons become delocalised and move through
            the whole structure, leaving Al3+ ions
Bonding:    strong electrostatic attraction between the positive
            aluminium ions and the delocalised electrons
```

The answer earns credit for "giant", "regular", "delocalised", "free to move", "positive ions" and
"electrostatic attraction". The detail of three electrons per atom shows understanding but is extra.

## Drawing the metallic bonding model

A good diagram has:

- **Positive ions** drawn as circles of equal size, each labelled with its charge (for example 2+ for
  magnesium).
- A **regular arrangement**, in rows, with ions close together.
- **Electrons** (shown as e⁻ or as small dots) placed **between** the ions, not in shells around one ion.
- A **label or key**, such as "positive metal ion" and "delocalised electron".

A careful diagram balances the charges. For 9 Mg²⁺ ions, a fully balanced drawing shows 18 electrons.
Many textbook diagrams simply scatter electrons between the ions, but if a question asks you to
**complete** a diagram so that it is neutral, the count matters.

### Worked example 2: checking a diagram

*A student draws 12 circles labelled "Na" in rows, each with one electron drawn on its edge. What is wrong?*

```
1. The circles should be labelled as ions with a charge: Na+
2. The electrons should be shown between the ions, free to move,
   not attached to particular particles
3. A key or label for "delocalised electrons" is needed
Charge check: 12 Na+ and 12 electrons -> neutral, so the NUMBER is right
```

## Recognising a metallic giant structure

| Diagram shows | Structure |
|---|---|
| One kind of positive particle in a regular pattern, with free electrons between them | Metallic giant structure |
| Positive and negative ions alternating | Giant ionic lattice |
| Atoms joined by lines in a network, no charges | Giant covalent structure |
| Separate small groups of joined atoms | Small molecules |

The key difference from an ionic diagram is the **absence of negative ions**: in a metal, the negative charge
is carried by electrons, not by ions. An alloy diagram may show positive particles of **two different
sizes**; it is still a metallic structure, because metallic bonding occurs in alloys as well as in pure
metals.

### Worked example 3: which diagram is metallic?

*Diagram P shows large circles labelled 2+ and small circles labelled 2− in alternating rows. Diagram Q shows
circles labelled 2+ in rows with e⁻ symbols between them. Which shows a metal?*

**Answer: Q.** Q has positive ions and delocalised electrons and no negative ions, so it is a metallic giant
structure. P has positive and negative ions alternating, which is an ionic lattice (for example, magnesium
oxide).

## Limitations of the metallic bonding diagram

- It is two-dimensional, so it shows one layer of a 3D structure.
- It shows electrons in fixed positions, although they are moving.
- It shows only a small part of a giant structure.
- The sizes of the ions and spacing may not be to scale.

## Exam technique

- **Describe:** name the structure (giant, regular pattern) and the particles (positive ions, delocalised
  electrons).
- **Explain:** link "delocalised electrons" to "electrostatic attraction with positive ions" and to "strong
  bonds".
- **Identify from a diagram:** give the structure and the evidence (positive ions with free electrons, no
  negative ions).

## Common mistakes

- Saying the electrons are "lost" or "transferred".
- Describing a "sea of ions" instead of a sea of electrons.
- Writing "the electrons are attracted to the atoms" without saying the ions are positive.
- Drawing electrons orbiting individual ions in shells.
- Describing metallic bonds as weak because the electrons move.
