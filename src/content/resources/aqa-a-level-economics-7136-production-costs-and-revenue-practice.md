## Questions

### Section A

**1.** Distinguish between the short run and the long run in production. **[2]**

**2.** Explain **two** ways in which the division of labour can raise labour productivity. **[4]**

**3.** Distinguish between increasing returns to scale and economies of scale. **[2]**

**4.** A firm producing 200 units has total costs of 3,000, of which 1,000 are fixed. Calculate its average fixed
cost and its average variable cost. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why a firm's average revenue curve is also its demand curve. **[2]**

**6.** Distinguish between invention and innovation, using an example. **[2]**

### Section B

**7.** A bakery has fixed costs of 300 a day. Each worker costs 60 a day.

| Workers | Loaves |
|---|---|
| 0 | 0 |
| 1 | 15 |
| 2 | 40 |
| 3 | 60 |
| 4 | 75 |
| 5 | 85 |

**(a)** Calculate the marginal product and average product of the third and fourth workers. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate total cost and average total cost when 75 loaves are produced. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the marginal cost per loaf of increasing output from 60 to 75 loaves. **[1]**

**(d)** Identify when diminishing returns set in and explain why they occur. **[2]**

**(e)** Loaves sell for 8.00 each. Calculate profit at 75 and at 85 loaves, and use marginal cost to explain why
employing the fifth worker raises profit. **[3]**

**8.** A firm faces the demand schedule below.

| Quantity | Price |
|---|---|
| 1 | 20 |
| 2 | 18 |
| 3 | 16 |
| 4 | 14 |
| 5 | 12 |

**(a)** Calculate total revenue at 4 units and the marginal revenue of the fourth unit. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why marginal revenue is less than average revenue for this firm. **[2]**

**9.** Explain **two** causes of diseconomies of scale. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how the minimum efficient scale of production can influence the number of firms in an industry.
**[4]**

**11.** Explain how a 10% rise in wages could affect a firm's costs of production, and why the effect depends on
what happens to labour productivity. **[6]**

**12.** Assess the view that economies of scale always benefit consumers. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** In the short run **at least one factor of production is fixed**, such as premises or machinery [1]. In the
long run **all factors can be varied**, so the firm can change its scale [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any two of the following, each worth 2 marks (1 for the way, 1 for the reason it raises output per
worker) [2] [2]: workers **repeat one task and build skill**, so each completes it faster; **less time is lost
moving between tasks** and tools; tasks can be **matched to workers' abilities**; simple repeated tasks allow
**specialised machinery** to be used. **[4]**

**3.** Increasing returns to scale relate **inputs to output**: a proportionate rise in all inputs gives a larger
proportionate rise in output [1]. Economies of scale relate **output to cost**: long-run average cost falls as
output rises, which may result from increasing returns or from other causes such as bulk-buying discounts [1].
**[2]**

**4.** AFC = 1,000 ÷ 200 = **5** [1]. AVC = (3,000 − 1,000) ÷ 200 = **10** [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Average revenue is TR ÷ Q, which equals the **price** of each unit [1]. The demand curve shows the price at
which each quantity can be sold, so AR traces out the demand curve [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Invention is creating a **new idea or product** [1]; innovation is **bringing it into commercial use**, for
example a battery chemistry discovered in a laboratory (invention) being used by a firm to make cheaper
electric bikes (innovation) [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Third worker: MP = 60 − 40 = **20**, AP = 60 ÷ 3 = **20** [1]. Fourth worker: MP = 75 − 60 = **15**,
AP = 75 ÷ 4 = **18.75** [1].

**(b)** TC = 300 + (4 × 60) = **540** [1]; ATC = 540 ÷ 75 = **7.20** [1].

**(c)** MC = 60 ÷ 15 = **4.00** per loaf [1].

**(d)** Diminishing returns set in with the **third worker**, when marginal product falls from 25 to 20 [1]. The
ovens and premises are fixed, so each extra worker has less capital to work with [1].

**(e)** At 75: profit = (75 × 8) − 540 = 600 − 540 = **60**. At 85: profit = (85 × 8) − 600 = 680 − 600 = **80** [1].
The fifth worker adds 10 loaves at a marginal cost of 60 ÷ 10 = **6.00** per loaf [1], which is below the price of
8.00, so each extra loaf adds 2.00 to profit [1].

**8. (a)** TR = 4 × 14 = **56** [1]. TR at 3 units = 48, so MR = 56 − 48 = **8** [1].

**(b)** To sell the fourth unit the firm must **lower the price on all units**, not just the extra one [1]. It gains
14 from the extra sale but loses 2 on each of the three units previously sold at 16, so MR (8) is below AR (14)
[1].

**9.** Any two of the following, each worth 2 marks (1 for the cause, 1 for explaining how it raises long-run
average cost) [2] [2]: **coordination** — with many plants and departments, plans are harder to align, so
resources are wasted; **communication** — messages pass through more layers of management, slowing decisions
and causing errors; **motivation** — workers in very large organisations may feel remote from decisions, so
effort and productivity fall. **[4]**

**10.** The MES is the **lowest output at which long-run average cost is minimised** [1]. If MES is **large relative
to market demand**, only a few firms can each produce at that output and still sell it [1]. Smaller entrants would
face higher average costs, so MES acts as a **barrier to entry** and the industry is likely to be concentrated
[1]. If MES is small relative to the market, many firms can produce at lowest cost and the industry can be
competitive [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Wages are a variable cost, so a 10% rise increases **total variable cost and total cost** at each output [1],
shifting the AVC, ATC and MC curves **upwards** [1]. With one variable factor, MC equals the wage divided by
marginal product [1], so if **productivity rises by 10%** at the same time, labour cost per unit is unchanged [1].
If productivity does not rise, unit labour costs rise by 10% [1]. The firm may also **substitute capital for
labour**, reducing the impact over time [1]. **[6]**

**12.** *Benefits:* lower long-run average costs may be passed on as **lower prices** [1]; larger firms may afford
more **research and development**, improving products [1]; in markets with large MES, economies of scale allow
supply at lower cost than many small firms could manage [1]. *Limits:* if economies of scale lead to **few
firms**, they may gain monopoly power and keep prices high, so cost savings become abnormal profit [1];
consumers may face **less choice** [1]; very large firms may suffer **diseconomies of scale**, raising costs [1];
lower costs depend on the gains actually being achieved, which takes time [1]. *Judgement:* consumers benefit when
competition or regulation forces firms to pass on savings; where scale creates market power, the benefit is
uncertain, so the view that they always benefit is too strong [2]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating MC as total cost divided by output.
- Confusing diminishing returns with diseconomies of scale.
- Including fixed costs when calculating AVC.
- Giving MR as the price of the extra unit for a firm with a downward-sloping demand curve.
- Stating that higher wages always raise unit costs without considering productivity.
- Ending an "assess" answer without a judgement.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I find marginal cost from a table with a fixed cost?**
Take the change in total cost and divide by the change in output. Fixed cost does not change, so the same
answer comes from the change in total variable cost.

**Why does MR fall faster than price?**
To sell one more unit the firm lowers the price on all units, so MR is the price of the extra unit minus the
revenue lost on the earlier units.
