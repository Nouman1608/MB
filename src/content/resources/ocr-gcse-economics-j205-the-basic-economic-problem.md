This guide covers section 1.2, The basic economic problem, of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205), Version 2.0, for first assessment in 2019. It sits in component J205/01, Introduction to Economics, and the specification is not tiered. All names and figures are invented.

Here the focus is on applying the ideas: scarcity for different decision-makers, who answers the three questions, opportunity cost in money and time, and a full "evaluate" answer on sustainability. Condense it with the [1.2 revision notes](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-the-basic-economic-problem-revision-notes/), then use the [1.2 practice questions](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-the-basic-economic-problem-practice/).

## What the specification asks

Learners should be able to:

- explain what is meant by scarce resources and unlimited wants;
- explain the economic problem, including the questions of how resources should be allocated, what, for whom and how goods and services should be produced;
- explain what is meant by opportunity cost;
- evaluate the costs and benefits of economic choices, including the impact on economic, social and environmental sustainability.

The last point uses **evaluate**: weigh up both sides or compare alternatives, then reach a supported judgement.

## Scarcity at every level

Scarcity means resources are limited compared with the wants they could satisfy. It applies to every decision-maker, not only to whole countries.

| Decision-maker | Scarce resource | Example of competing wants |
|---|---|---|
| A student | Time and pocket money | Revision, a part-time job, sport, seeing friends |
| A household | Income | Rent, food, a holiday, saving |
| A firm | Budget, staff, factory space | A new product line or a bigger advertising campaign |
| A government | Tax revenue, land, skilled workers | Hospitals, schools, defence, lower taxes |

Two ideas make an explanation stronger:

- **Wants grow with income.** When people can afford what they wanted last year, new wants appear: a better phone, a second car, more travel. So scarcity does not disappear as an economy grows richer.
- **Some resources run out.** Non-renewable resources, such as oil and metal ores, cannot be replaced once used. Renewable resources, such as forests and fish stocks, can recover, but only if they are not used faster than they regrow. This links scarcity directly to sustainability.

A **shortage** of one product, such as a toy that sells out before a festival, is a temporary market problem. **Scarcity** is permanent and affects all resources.

## The economic problem: who answers the questions?

Because resources are scarce, every society must decide **what** to produce, **how** to produce it and **for whom**, and so how resources should be **allocated** between uses. The same questions can be answered in different ways.

| Question | Answered mainly by markets | Answered mainly by government |
|---|---|---|
| What? | Firms make what consumers are willing and able to buy, because that earns profit | The government decides, for example, how many school places to provide |
| How? | Firms choose the cheapest combination of factors | Rules shape methods, such as safety or pollution laws |
| For whom? | Those able and willing to pay the price | Allocated by need, such as health care free at the point of use |

Most economies use a mix. A strong answer explains the trade-off: allocation by price responds quickly to what people want, but people on low incomes may go without; allocation by need can be fairer, but there may be queues or waiting lists because the service is not rationed by price.

**Applying the questions to a context (invented).** The town of Westmere has a disused railway yard. *What*: housing, a business park or a park? *How*: build with low-carbon materials at higher cost, or the cheapest methods? *For whom*: homes sold at market prices, or some kept at lower rents for key workers? Each answer uses the same scarce land and gives up the alternatives.

## Opportunity cost in practice

Opportunity cost is the value of the **next best alternative** given up when a choice is made. Two rules keep answers accurate.

1. **Only one alternative counts:** the best one forgone, not everything that could have been done.
2. **The alternative must be real for that decision-maker:** it must be something they could actually have chosen with the same resources.

**Worked example: a farmer (invented).** A farmer has 10 hectares. Wheat would earn £600 per hectare and barley £500 per hectare; sheep grazing would earn £350 per hectare.

```
Wheat:  10 × 600 = £6,000
Barley: 10 × 500 = £5,000   ← next best
Sheep:  10 × 350 = £3,500
```

She plants wheat. The opportunity cost is the **barley**, or the £5,000 it would have earned, not the barley plus the sheep.

**Opportunity cost of time.** A worker who could earn £12 an hour takes a three-hour afternoon off. If extra paid hours were genuinely available, the opportunity cost is the £36 of earnings forgone. If no extra work was available, the next best use of the time is something else, such as revision or rest.

**"Free" goods still have an opportunity cost.** A public park is free to enter, but the land could have been used for housing, and the tax revenue that maintains it could have funded another service. Free to the user does not mean free to society.

## Evaluating an economic choice

The specification links evaluation to three kinds of sustainability:

- **Economic:** can the choice keep providing income, jobs and profit over time?
- **Social:** does it support people's well-being, fairness and communities?
- **Environmental:** does it protect natural resources, wildlife and the climate?

A clear structure for any "evaluate the costs and benefits" question:

1. State the choice and its opportunity cost.
2. Give the benefits, saying who gains.
3. Give the costs, saying who loses.
4. Consider time: short run against long run.
5. Reach a judgement and say what it depends on.

**Worked example: Packwell Snacks (invented).** The firm sells 50,000 packets a month and is considering switching from plastic to paper packaging. The paper costs 6p more per packet and each switch saves 8 g of plastic. Annual profit is £240,000.

```
Extra cost per month = 50,000 × £0.06 = £3,000
Extra cost per year  = 3,000 × 12 = £36,000
As % of profit       = 36,000 ÷ 240,000 × 100 = 15%
Plastic saved a year = 50,000 × 12 × 8 g = 4,800,000 g = 4.8 tonnes
```

**Model answer outline.**

- *Economic:* the switch cuts profit by 15% unless the firm raises its price. That money could have been spent on a new product line (the opportunity cost). If customers prefer greener packaging, sales may rise and recover some of the cost.
- *Social:* customers and the local community may view the firm more favourably; however, a price rise falls most heavily on lower-income buyers.
- *Environmental:* 4.8 tonnes less plastic waste a year. Paper still uses trees, energy and water, so the gain depends on whether the paper is recycled and responsibly sourced.
- *Judgement:* the switch is likely to be worthwhile if the firm can pass on part of the 6p, because the environmental gain is lasting while the cost is modest relative to profit. If competitors stay with cheaper plastic and customers are price-sensitive, the economic cost may be too high to sustain.

This answer earns its evaluation marks because each point says who is affected, uses the data and ends with a judgement that depends on stated conditions.

## Common mistakes

- Defining scarcity as "running out" of a product in a shop.
- Giving several alternatives as the opportunity cost.
- Writing that public services have no opportunity cost because they are free to use.
- Listing the three questions without applying them to the context.
- Mentioning "sustainability" without saying which kind, or who gains and loses.
- Evaluating without a conclusion, or with a conclusion that ignores the points made.

## Exam technique

- For "explain what is meant by", give the definition and an example in context.
- For opportunity cost, name the single next best alternative; where figures are given, quote the value.
- For "evaluate", aim for at least one benefit and one cost, and a judgement that says *it depends on...*.
- Calculations in this section are usually simple totals or percentages. Show each step.
