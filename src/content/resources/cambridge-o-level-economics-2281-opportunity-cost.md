## 1.3.1 What opportunity cost is

**Opportunity cost** is the next best alternative forgone when a choice is made.

It follows directly from the economic problem (section 1.1). Resources are finite, so using them one way means they cannot be used another way. The cost of any choice is what you could have had instead.

Three words in the definition matter.

- **Next:** only the single best option you gave up counts, not every option.
- **Best:** it is the option you would have chosen if your actual choice had not been available.
- **Forgone:** given up, not merely postponed or reduced in price.

### Worked example 1: picking out the next best alternative

Kofi has a free Saturday. He ranks his options:

1. Work a paid shift at a café (his choice)
2. Play in a football match
3. Visit his cousin
4. Stay home and read

The opportunity cost of working the shift is **the football match**. It is not "the match, the visit and the reading", because he could only have done one of them in the time.

### Opportunity cost is not the same as money cost

The money price of something tells you what you pay. The opportunity cost tells you what you give up. They can differ a lot.

- A concert ticket costs 40 cu. If Kofi would otherwise have spent that 40 cu on a pair of jeans, the opportunity cost is the jeans.
- If the concert also takes up a shift he could have worked for 30 cu, the time used has an opportunity cost too: the earnings he gave up.
- A free good (section 1.1) has a zero opportunity cost. A service provided at no charge, such as a state school place, does not: the teachers and buildings could have been used elsewhere.

### Measuring opportunity cost in goods

When a resource can produce two things, opportunity cost can be measured as the amount of one good given up to get more of the other.

**Worked example 2.** A farmer's field can produce either 40 tonnes of wheat or 25 tonnes of maize in a season.

- Opportunity cost of 25 tonnes of maize = 40 tonnes of wheat.
- Opportunity cost of 1 tonne of maize = 40 ÷ 25 = **1.6 tonnes of wheat**.
- Opportunity cost of 1 tonne of wheat = 25 ÷ 40 = **0.625 tonnes of maize**.

The two answers are reciprocals of each other: 1.6 × 0.625 = 1. Measuring in goods is how opportunity cost is read from a production possibility curve (section 1.4).

## Examples in different contexts

| Context | Choice made | Opportunity cost |
|---|---|---|
| Consumer | Spends 60 cu on a video game | The next best use of 60 cu, such as a pair of trainers |
| Worker | Takes a job paying 1,200 cu a month | The next best job, say one paying 1,100 cu with shorter hours |
| Student | Stays in full-time education for two more years | The wages and work experience from the job they would have taken |
| Producer | Uses a factory line to make bicycles | The scooters that line could have made |
| Government | Spends 2 billion cu on a new airport | The next best project, such as 2 billion cu of new hospitals |

## 1.3.2 How opportunity cost influences decisions

Decision makers compare the benefit of an option with what they give up. When the opportunity cost of a choice rises, that choice becomes less attractive, and some decision makers switch.

### Consumers

A consumer with a limited income chooses the option that gives the most satisfaction for what is given up. If the price of eating out rises, each meal now means giving up more of other goods, so some consumers eat out less.

### Workers

**Worked example 3.** Selin is offered three jobs (invented):

| Job | Monthly pay (cu) | Hours a week |
|---|---|---|
| A: bank clerk | 1,500 | 40 |
| B: hotel receptionist | 1,350 | 35 |
| C: shop assistant | 1,100 | 38 |

If she prefers B to C, the opportunity cost of taking job A is job B: 1,350 cu a month and 5 extra free hours a week. Selin should take A only if the extra 150 cu a month is worth more to her than the 5 hours of leisure she gives up. A worker's decision depends on pay and on what else the time could be used for.

When wages for unskilled jobs rise, the opportunity cost of staying in education rises. Some students then leave education earlier. When wages fall or jobs are hard to find, the opportunity cost of studying falls, and more students stay on.

### Producers

A firm's resources could make several products. Using them for one product means giving up the profit from the next best. **Worked example 4.** A workshop can make 200 chairs a month (profit 15 cu each) or 80 tables (profit 45 cu each). Chairs earn 3,000 cu profit and tables earn 3,600 cu. If the workshop makes chairs, the opportunity cost is the tables and their 3,600 cu profit, which is higher than what it earns. A profit-seeking firm switches to tables. If the profit on chairs rose to 20 cu, chairs would earn 4,000 cu, and the decision would reverse.

### Governments

A government with a fixed budget weighs the benefit of each project against the next best use of the money. Spending on defence has an opportunity cost in health, education or roads. Because the opportunity cost of public spending is often a service people value highly, governments rank projects by priority and consider the effect on different groups. Borrowing to avoid choosing only moves the opportunity cost to the future, when the loan must be repaid.

## Evaluation: limits to the idea

- Opportunity cost depends on the decision maker's own ranking, so two people facing the same choice may have different opportunity costs.
- The value of the next best alternative is not always easy to measure, especially for governments comparing, say, health and the environment.
- Decision makers may not know all their options, so the alternative they give up may not be the true next best.

## Common errors

- Listing every alternative instead of the single next best one.
- Giving a money amount as the opportunity cost when the question asks what is given up. Name the alternative.
- Saying something provided at no charge has no opportunity cost.
- Confusing a fall in price with opportunity cost.
- In calculations, dividing the wrong way round. Ask: "how much of the other good do I give up for one unit of this one?"

## Exam technique

- **Define** in one sentence: "the next best alternative forgone".
- **Identify** the opportunity cost in a case: find the option that would have been chosen next.
- **Calculate**: write the ratio, the division, and units of the good given up.
- **Explain** a decision: benefit of the choice, what is given up, and why the benefit is larger.
- **Discuss** a government choice: give the opportunity cost, the benefits, and who gains or loses, then conclude.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is opportunity cost always measured in money?**
No. It is the next best alternative given up, which can be a good, a service, time or leisure. Money can help measure it, but the answer should name what is given up.

**Why does opportunity cost matter to governments?**
Governments have limited budgets, so every project means another is not funded. Comparing opportunity costs helps them decide which spending gives the greatest benefit.
