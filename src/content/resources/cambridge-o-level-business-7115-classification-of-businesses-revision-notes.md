Condensed recall for subtopic **1.2 Classification of businesses**. For full explanations and worked examples, use the [Classification of businesses study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-business-7115-classification-of-businesses/).

## Two ways to classify

1. **By economic sector**: what the business activity does (primary, secondary, tertiary).
2. **By ownership**: who owns and controls it (private sector or public sector).

A business has one ownership classification but may carry out activities in more than one economic sector.

## The three economic sectors

| Sector | Activity | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Extracting or growing natural resources | Farming, fishing, forestry, mining, quarrying, oil drilling |
| Secondary | Making finished or semi-finished goods from raw materials | Food processing, car assembly, oil refining, construction |
| Tertiary | Providing services | Shops, banks, insurance, transport, hotels, schools, hospitals, software services |

## Linking the sectors

- Products move along a **chain of production** from primary to secondary to tertiary.
- Tertiary services such as transport, banking and insurance support every stage.
- The sectors are **interdependent**: a problem at one stage affects the others.

## Measuring importance

- Share of employment: workers in the sector ÷ total workers × 100.
- Share of output: value of the sector's output ÷ total output × 100.
- A sector's share can fall while its number of workers or its output still rises, if other sectors grow faster.

## Why sector importance changes

**Developing economies (industrialisation):**

- farming is mechanised, so fewer farm workers are needed
- people move to towns for factory jobs
- foreign businesses may build factories to use lower-cost labour
- more raw materials are processed at home rather than exported raw.

**Developed economies (deindustrialisation):**

- high incomes mean more spending on services
- manufacturers face competition from lower-cost countries
- automation reduces the number of factory workers needed
- natural resources run out or cost more to extract than to import.

## Applying a sector trend to a business

- **Growing sector**: more customers, more suppliers nearby and a larger pool of trained workers, but also more new competitors.
- **Shrinking sector**: falling demand and possible closures; a business may need new markets, lower costs or a different product.
- **Supplier effects**: a shrinking secondary sector cuts orders for primary producers and for the transport and finance firms that serve factories.
- Always name the business in the question and say whether the trend is an opportunity or a threat for it.

## Private and public sectors

| | Private sector | Public sector |
|---|---|---|
| Owned by | Individuals or private shareholders | The government |
| Controlled by | Owners or their managers | Government or managers it appoints |
| Typical aim | Profit (social enterprises and charities may put social aims first) | Providing a service to the public |
| Examples | Sole traders, partnerships, private and public limited companies, franchises | State schools and hospitals, public corporations |

A **mixed economy** contains both. The balance between them varies between countries.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Primary sector | Businesses that extract or grow natural resources |
| Secondary sector | Businesses that manufacture or process goods, including construction |
| Tertiary sector | Businesses that provide services |
| Chain of production | The stages a product passes through from raw material to customer |
| Industrialisation | Growth in the importance of the secondary sector |
| Deindustrialisation | Decline in the importance of the secondary sector |
| Private sector | Businesses owned by private individuals or shareholders |
| Public sector | Organisations owned and controlled by the government |
| Mixed economy | An economy with both a private sector and a public sector |

## Exam traps

- **Technology does not change the sector.** A fish farm or a mechanised mine is still primary.
- **Construction is secondary**, not tertiary.
- **A plc is in the private sector.** "Public" in its name means its shares are sold to the public.
- **Share versus number.** A smaller percentage does not always mean fewer workers.
- **Charities are not public sector** unless owned by the government.
- **Give the reason for a change**, then its effect on businesses, not just the trend.

## Self-test

1. Classify each activity: a gold mine; a jewellery workshop; a jewellery shop.
2. Which sector is a building company in?
3. Give two examples of tertiary businesses that support a garment factory.
4. In a country, 4 million of 16 million workers are in the secondary sector. Calculate the secondary sector's share.
5. Ten years later the secondary sector has 5 million of 25 million workers. Has the number of secondary workers risen or fallen? Has its share risen or fallen?
6. Define deindustrialisation.
7. Give two reasons why the tertiary sector grows in developed economies.
8. Give one reason why the primary sector's share falls in a developing economy.
9. Is a public limited company in the public sector? Explain.
10. Give one reason why a government might keep education in the public sector.
11. What is a mixed economy?
12. A tea grower also packs and sells tea in its own shop. Which sectors does it operate in?

## Answers

1. Gold mine: **primary**; workshop: **secondary**; shop: **tertiary**.
2. **Secondary** (construction).
3. Any two: transport firms, banks, insurance companies, shops, advertising agencies.
4. 4 ÷ 16 × 100 = **25%**.
5. The number has **risen** (4 to 5 million), but the share has **fallen** (25% to 20%).
6. A fall in the importance of the secondary (manufacturing) sector in an economy.
7. Any two: higher incomes increase spending on services; manufacturing faces foreign competition so resources shift to services; automation reduces factory jobs.
8. Mechanisation of farming means fewer farm workers are needed, so people move to factory jobs.
9. **No.** It is owned by private shareholders; "public" means its shares can be sold to the general public.
10. Education should be available to everyone whatever their ability to pay.
11. An economy with both private sector businesses and public sector organisations.
12. **Primary** (growing), **secondary** (packing) and **tertiary** (selling in its shop).
