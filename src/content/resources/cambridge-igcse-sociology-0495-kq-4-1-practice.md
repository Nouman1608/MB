## Section A

**1.** Define the term *empty shell marriage*. **[2]**

**2.** Define the term *serial monogamy*. **[2]**

**3.** Give **two** alternatives to living in a family. **[2]**

**4.** Give **two** types of extended family. **[2]**

**5.** State the difference between a family and a household. **[2]**

**6.** Give **two** limitations of the reconstituted family for its members. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain **three** strengths of the extended family. **[6]**

**8.** Explain **three** ways in which family life may differ between social classes. **[6]**

**9.** Explain **three** reasons for the growth of single person households. **[6]**

**10.** Discuss the ways in which family structures vary across different cultures.

Your answer should include:
- at least three developed points with evidence. **[8]**

**11.** Discuss how far friends can be seen as an alternative to the family.

Your answer should include:
- at least three developed points with evidence. **[8]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that lone-parent families are harmful for children.

Your answer should include:
- at least three arguments for the view and three arguments against the view
- a conclusion. **[14]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1. [2]** A marriage in which the couple remain legally married and may share a home, but the relationship has no love, affection or companionship (2). They stay together for reasons such as children, money or religious belief. "A failed marriage" alone: 1.

**2. [2]** Having a series of marriages (or long-term partnerships), one at a time, with divorce or separation between them (2). "Marrying more than once": 1.

**3. [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: single person households (living alone); shared households of unrelated people; friends as family. Credit communal living if described as a household rather than a family.

**4. [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: horizontal extended family; vertical extended family; beanpole family (accept as a form of vertical extension); modified extended family.

**5. [2]** A family is a group linked by blood, marriage, civil partnership or adoption (1); a household is a person or group sharing a home, who may not be related at all (1).

**6. [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: conflict between step-parents and step-children; divided loyalties for children moving between two homes; tension between step-siblings; financial strain of supporting children in more than one household; unclear rules about discipline.

### Section B

**7. [6]** Three strengths, 1 mark for identifying each and 1 for developing it:
- **Shared childcare**: grandparents or aunts can look after children, allowing parents to do paid work and reducing childcare costs.
- **Pooled income and resources**: several earners share housing and bills, giving security during unemployment or illness.
- **Care for older members**: elderly relatives are looked after at home rather than left isolated, fitting values of family obligation.
- **Transmission of culture and advice**: older generations pass on language, religion, traditions and experience to children.
- **Emotional support**: a wider network of people to turn to in a crisis, such as bereavement.

**8. [6]** Three ways, 1 + 1 each:
- **Kinship networks**: Young and Willmott found close-knit extended families in working-class Bethnal Green in the 1950s, while middle-class families are more likely to be geographically mobile and form modified extended families.
- **Resources**: wealthier families can afford larger homes, paid childcare and private activities for children; poorer families rely more on relatives and face more financial stress.
- **Roles**: some research has linked more traditional, segregated roles with manual working-class communities in the past and more joint roles with middle-class dual-career couples.
- **Timing of family life**: middle-class people with long education may marry and have children later.
Credit any accurate, developed difference.

**9. [6]** Three reasons, 1 + 1 each:
- **Divorce and separation** leave many adults living alone, at least for a time.
- **Ageing population**: more older people outlive their partners and live alone as widows or widowers.
- **Later marriage and cohabitation**: young adults spend longer single before settling down.
- **Female empowerment**: women's education and careers give financial independence, so living alone is a realistic choice.
- **Changing attitudes**: living alone carries less stigma and may be seen as freedom.

**10. [8]** Levels: 7–8 good knowledge, three developed points with evidence, terms used well; 4–6 some knowledge, most points supported, some undeveloped; 1–3 limited, descriptive.
Indicative content:
- Extended households sharing a home and income are common in many South Asian and African societies, often linked to values of family duty and to agricultural or family-business economies.
- The isolated nuclear family is more typical of industrial, urban societies where workers move for jobs.
- Israeli kibbutzim historically raised children partly in communal children's houses, showing child-rearing can be shared beyond parents.
- Variation in marriage: arranged marriage is the norm in some cultures and love marriage in others.
- Migration produces hybrid patterns, for example migrant families keeping extended ties across countries through visits and online contact.
- Evaluation: globalisation may be spreading the nuclear ideal, but culture still shapes family form strongly.

**11. [8]** Levels as Question 10. Indicative content:
- **For**: friends can give emotional support, practical help and companionship usually expected from kin; postmodernists see "families of choice" as legitimate, chosen relationships; geographical mobility and divorce weaken ties to relatives, so friends fill the gap; growth of single person households increases reliance on friends.
- **Against**: friends rarely take on legal and financial obligations such as inheritance or caring for a dependent long-term; kinship remains the main source of care for children and older people; in many cultures family obligation is strongly expected; friendship networks can be less stable than kinship.
- A developed answer recognises that friends usually supplement rather than replace family.

**12. [14]** Levels: 12–14 excellent knowledge, three developed and evidenced points on each side, balanced, with a conclusion; 8–11 good knowledge, developed points on both sides but less balance; 4–7 some knowledge, simple two-sided or one-sided answer; 1–3 limited and descriptive. A one-sided answer cannot score more than 6.
*For the view:*
- New Right: children need a male role model and two adults to provide discipline; Murray linked lone parenthood to an underclass whose children are inadequately socialised.
- Lone-parent families are more likely to experience poverty, which affects health, housing and educational achievement.
- One parent doing paid and unpaid work has less time to supervise and support children.
- Children may experience the stress of separation from one parent.
*Against the view:*
- Poverty, not family structure, explains most of the disadvantage; well-resourced lone parents can raise children successfully.
- Ending a conflict-ridden or abusive relationship may benefit children more than keeping two parents together.
- Feminists argue the New Right view blames women and ignores the quality of parenting.
- Postmodernists see lone parenthood as one valid choice among many.
- Children often keep contact with the non-resident parent and wider kin, such as grandparents.
*Conclusion:* a supported judgement, for example that the risks come mainly from poverty and conflict rather than from having one parent as such.
