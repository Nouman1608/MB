These questions cover the Theory and Methods content of AQA A-level Sociology (7192), listed
identically as 3.1.3 (Paper 1) and 3.3.2 (Paper 3). The specification gives Theory and Methods 10
marks of extended writing in Paper 1 and 30 marks in Paper 3; the questions below use 10-mark and
20-mark extended-writing styles, with one item-based 20-mark question.

## 10-mark questions

**1.** Outline and explain **two** reasons why positivists prefer quantitative methods. **[10]**

**2.** Outline and explain **two** problems sociologists may face when using covert participant
observation. **[10]**

**3.** Outline and explain **two** ways in which sociology may influence social policy. **[10]**

## 20-mark questions

**4.** *Item A (invented for practice)*

*Some sociologists argue that society is a structure that shapes individuals' behaviour, much as the
body's organs work together. Others reject this view, arguing that society is created by individuals
through their interactions and the meanings they give to situations. A third group claims that both
views are incomplete.*

Applying material from **Item A** and your knowledge, evaluate the usefulness of structural
theories for an understanding of society. **[20]**

**5.** Evaluate the view that sociology can and should be a science. **[20]**

**6.** Evaluate the view that sociological research can be value-free. **[20]**

**7.** Evaluate the claim that we now live in a postmodern society. **[20]**

## Mark guidance

**Level descriptors (10 marks):** 8–10 two well-developed reasons with sound knowledge and clear
analysis; 4–7 reasons with some development or one well-developed; 1–3 limited, list-like points.

**Level descriptors (20 marks):** 17–20 sophisticated knowledge, accurate application to the item
where given, sustained evaluation and a justified conclusion; 13–16 broad knowledge, some evaluation;
9–12 reasonable knowledge, limited analysis; 5–8 basic, descriptive; 1–4 fragments.

### 1. [10]

- **Reliability and objectivity** — standardised quantitative methods can be repeated by others, so
  results can be checked; positivists see this as the hallmark of science. Develop: objectivity keeps
  the researcher's values out, as Comte and Durkheim sought.
- **Discovering patterns and causes** — positivists see society as made of social facts that can be
  measured; numbers allow correlations and causal laws to be identified, as in Durkheim's *Suicide*
  (1897). Develop: large samples allow generalisation and comparison.
- Also creditable: hypothesis testing; representativeness; usefulness for policy-makers.

### 2. [10]

- **Ethical problems** — deception; no informed consent; risk of harm if identities are revealed;
  the BSA Statement of Ethical Practice discourages covert research unless strongly justified.
- **Practical problems** — getting in, staying in and getting out; recording data without being
  discovered; danger in deviant groups; risk of going native.
- **Validity versus reliability** — rich data but impossible to replicate; findings rely on
  memory if notes cannot be taken.

### 3. [10]

- **Providing evidence of problems** — research such as Townsend's *Poverty in the United Kingdom*
  (1979) or the Black Report (1980) on health inequalities can put issues on the political agenda.
- **Evaluating policies** — sociological research can measure the effects of policies (for example
  compensatory education) and recommend changes.
- **Changing public opinion and concepts** — feminist research on domestic labour and violence helped
  shape policy debates.
- Develop with constraints: funding, government ideology and electoral considerations limit
  influence.

### 4. [20] Structural theories

*Application of Item A:* "structure that shapes behaviour" and "organs work together" = functionalist
organic analogy; "created by individuals through interactions and meanings" = social action theory;
"both views are incomplete" = structuration (Giddens).

*Usefulness of structural theories:*
- **Functionalism** — Durkheim and Parsons explain social order through shared values and
  interrelated institutions; Merton's manifest and latent functions add nuance.
- **Marxism** — explains inequality and conflict through class; Althusser's ideological and
  repressive state apparatuses; Gramsci's hegemony allows for consciousness and resistance.
- **Feminism** — explains gender inequality as structural patriarchy (Walby, 1990).

*Criticisms:* deterministic — people treated as puppets; functionalism struggles with conflict and
change; Marxism reduces everything to class; social action theorists (Mead, Blumer, Garfinkel) show
order being actively produced; postmodernists reject structural meta-narratives; Giddens argues
structure and agency reproduce each other.

*Conclusion:* structural theories explain large-scale patterns and inequalities, but need social
action insights to explain meaning and change.

### 5. [20] Sociology as a science

- **Can be:** positivists (Comte, Durkheim) — social facts can be studied scientifically; Durkheim's
  *Suicide*; realists (Keat and Urry, 1975; Sayer) — open-system sciences like meteorology still seek
  underlying causes.
- **Cannot/should not be:** Popper — many sociological theories are unfalsifiable; Kuhn — sociology is
  pre-paradigmatic; interpretivists — people act on meanings, requiring verstehen; Atkinson — social
  data such as statistics are constructed.
- **Should it be?** Postmodernists question science itself as a meta-narrative; feminists criticise
  "objective" science as male-centred.
- **Evaluate:** depends on the definition of science; Kuhn and realism suggest natural science itself is
  less "positivist" than assumed.

### 6. [20] Value freedom

- **Yes:** Comte and Durkheim — objective science; standardised methods reduce bias.
- **Partly:** Weber — values shape choice of topic and interpretation, but data collection should be
  value-free.
- **No:** Becker (1967) — all research takes sides; Gouldner (1962) — value freedom is a myth that
  serves the powerful; feminists — research should serve women; funding and careers shape research
  agendas.
- **Evaluate:** postmodernists — all knowledge is perspectival; distinguish being value-free from
  being objective in method; conclude that complete value freedom is unlikely but reflexivity can
  reduce bias.

### 7. [20] Postmodern society

- **For:** Lyotard (1979) — incredulity towards meta-narratives; Baudrillard — hyperreality and
  media saturation; fragmentation of identities; globalisation and consumer choice.
- **Against:** Marxists such as Fredric Jameson (*Postmodernism, or, The Cultural Logic of Late
  Capitalism*, 1991) argue postmodern culture is a product of late capitalism, not a new society;
  Giddens — late modernity and reflexivity; Beck — risk society; continuing structural inequalities
  of class, gender and ethnicity.
- **Evaluate:** postmodernism is itself a grand claim; change is real but may be within modernity.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long should I spend on a 10-mark question?** Roughly 15 minutes: two clearly separated reasons,
each explained and developed.

**Do I need an item for every 20-mark question?** No — Paper 3 Theory and Methods questions may or may
not use an item; practise both styles.

**Which theories should I learn for the structure–action debate?** Functionalism, Marxism, feminism,
interactionism, ethnomethodology and Giddens's structuration.
