## Section A

**1.** State **two** civil functions of the Magistrates' Court. **[2]**

**2.** Identify the three divisions of the High Court. **[3]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by a Scott v Avery clause. **[2]**

**4.** State the **two** stages of the test in the Code for Crown Prosecutors. **[2]**

**5.** Describe the time limits for detaining a suspect without charge under PACE. **[3]**

**6.** Explain the purpose of an appropriate adult at a police station. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain the problems identified by Lord Woolf and the main changes introduced by the Civil Procedure Rules 1998. **[8]**

**8.** Describe the pre-trial process for an offence triable either way where the defendant intends to plead not guilty. **[6]**

**9.** Explain the routes of appeal available to a defendant convicted in the Magistrates' Court. **[4]**

**10.** Kaveh is charged with burglary of a dwelling. He has two previous convictions for failing to surrender to bail and no fixed address.

**(a)** Explain the general right to bail and the grounds on which it may be refused. **[4]**
**(b)** Apply the law to advise whether Kaveh is likely to be granted bail, and identify **two** conditions a court might impose. **[4]**

**11.** Lena is stopped by a plain-clothes officer who says, "I'm searching you," without explanation, and finds nothing. She is then arrested for an unrelated theft and held for 26 hours on the authority of a sergeant. In interview, after being shouted at repeatedly, she confesses. Advise Lena on whether the police acted lawfully and whether her confession is admissible. **[10]**

**12.** Assess the advantages and disadvantages of arbitration as a method of resolving commercial disputes. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two, 1 mark each: licensing matters [1]; enforcing debts such as unpaid council tax [1]; family cases when magistrates sit in the Family Court.

**2.** King's Bench Division [1]; Chancery Division [1]; Family Division [1].

**3.** A clause in a contract providing that the parties must refer a dispute to arbitration [1] and that an arbitration award is a condition precedent to any court action, so neither can sue until arbitration is complete [1].

**4.** The evidential stage: is there sufficient evidence for a realistic prospect of conviction [1]? The public interest stage: is a prosecution required in the public interest [1]?

**5.** The basic limit is 24 hours from arrival at the station (s41) [1]. A superintendent may extend this to 36 hours for an indictable offence (s42) [1]. Magistrates may issue warrants of further detention up to a maximum of 96 hours (ss43–44) [1].

**6.** Under PACE Code C an appropriate adult must be present for a juvenile or a vulnerable adult [1] to advise and support them, check they understand what is happening and see that the interview is conducted fairly [1].

**7.** **Problems**: civil litigation was too expensive, with costs often exceeding the sum claimed [1]; too slow, with long delays before trial [1]; too complex for ordinary people [1]; and too adversarial, with lawyers rather than courts controlling the pace [1]. **Changes**: the overriding objective of dealing with cases justly and at proportionate cost [1]; judicial case management with timetables and sanctions [1]; pre-action protocols requiring early exchange of information [1]; Part 36 offers and encouragement of ADR to promote settlement [1].

**8.** The defendant first appears in the Magistrates' Court and indicates a plea at the **plea before venue** hearing [1]. On a not guilty indication, the magistrates hold an **allocation** hearing [1], considering the prosecution's summary of the facts, the defendant's previous convictions and whether their sentencing powers would be adequate, using allocation guidance [1]. If they decline jurisdiction, the case is **sent to the Crown Court** [1]. If they accept jurisdiction, the defendant is told they may still **elect trial by jury** in the Crown Court [1]; if they consent to summary trial, a trial date is fixed and disclosure and bail decisions follow [1].

**9.** The defendant may appeal to the **Crown Court** against conviction, if they pleaded not guilty, or against sentence [1]; an appeal against conviction is a full rehearing before a judge and two magistrates [1]. Either side may appeal **by way of case stated** to the Divisional Court of the King's Bench Division on a point of law or excess of jurisdiction [1], and from there a further appeal lies to the Supreme Court on a certified point of law of general public importance [1].

**10. (a)** Section 4 Bail Act 1976 creates a general right to bail, reflecting the presumption of innocence [1]. For an imprisonable offence, bail may be refused if there are substantial grounds for believing the defendant would **fail to surrender** [1], **commit an offence while on bail** [1] or **interfere with witnesses** or obstruct justice [1].
**(b)** Kaveh's two previous failures to surrender give substantial grounds for believing he may abscond [1], and having no fixed address weakens his community ties [1]. Burglary of a dwelling is serious and carries a significant likely sentence, so refusal is a real possibility [1]. If bail is granted, conditions might include residence at a specified address, reporting to a police station, a curfew, or a surety [1].

**11.** **Stop and search**: s1 PACE requires reasonable grounds for suspicion [1], and under s2 the officer, not being in uniform, must show a warrant card and give their name, station, the object and grounds of the search [1]. None of this happened and no suspicion is shown, so the search was unlawful, also breaching Code A [1]. **Arrest**: under s24 the officer needs reasonable suspicion of the theft and a necessity reason [1], and under s28 Lena must be told she is under arrest and the grounds [1]. **Detention**: the basic limit is 24 hours (s41) [1]; extension beyond that must be authorised by a superintendent for an indictable offence (s42), not a sergeant, so detention after 24 hours was unlawful [1]. **Confession**: under s76 a confession obtained by oppression must be excluded unless the prosecution proves beyond reasonable doubt it was not so obtained [1]; repeated shouting resembles the hectoring questioning in *R v Paris, Abdullahi and Miller* (1993) [1]. Even if not oppressive, the court could exclude it under s78 because unlawful detention makes admission unfair; the confession is likely to be inadmissible [1].

**12.** **Advantages**: the parties choose an arbitrator with technical expertise in the trade [1]; hearings are private, protecting commercial reputation and confidential information [1]; the process is usually quicker and the parties can fix the time and place [1]; the award is binding and enforceable in court under the Arbitration Act 1996 [1]. **Disadvantages**: appeal rights are limited to narrow grounds such as serious irregularity or, unless excluded, a point of law [1]; professional arbitrators' fees and venue costs can make it expensive [1]; a weaker party may be bound by an arbitration clause it had no real chance to negotiate [1]. **Conclusion**: arbitration suits commercial parties of equal strength who value expertise and privacy, but less so where one side is much weaker [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Forgetting to apply the s2 PACE duties when the officer is in plain clothes.
- Writing "the defendant chooses the court" without the allocation stage.
- Listing bail factors without linking them to the defendant's facts.
- Treating arbitration as identical to mediation.
