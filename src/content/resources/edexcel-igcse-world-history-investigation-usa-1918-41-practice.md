## Enquiry 1 — the 1920s

**1(a)** Describe **two** features of the Ku Klux Klan in the 1920s. **[6]**

**Source A** (invented). From an advertisement in a popular magazine, 1926.

> "Why wait? With our easy-payment plan, the newest electric washing machine can be in your home this
> week — for just a few dollars a month. Every modern family deserves the best."

**Source B** (invented). From a letter by a farmer in Iowa to his congressman, 1927.

> "Corn fetches less than it cost to grow, and the bank wants its money for the tractor. My neighbours
> in town talk of radios and motor cars. Out here we talk of who will lose the farm next."

**1(b)** How far does Source A support the evidence of Source B about prosperity in the 1920s? **[8]**

**Extract C** (invented interpretation).

> "The boom of the 1920s was built on easy credit and the hopes of the middle class, and it left
> much of America behind."

**1(c)** How far do you agree with the interpretation in Extract C? Use Extract C, Sources A and B
and your own knowledge. **[16]**

## Enquiry 2 — Depression and New Deal, 1929–41

**2(a)** Describe **two** features of the Bonus Marchers' protest (1932). **[6]**

**Source D** (genuine). From Franklin D. Roosevelt's first inaugural address, 4 March 1933.

> "So, first of all, let me assert my firm belief that the only thing we have to fear is fear
> itself."

**Source E** (invented). From a speech by a businessman to a meeting of opponents of the New Deal,
1935.

> "Washington now tells the factory owner what to pay, the farmer what to plant and the banker how to
> lend. This is not recovery; it is the road to a planned economy."

**2(b)** How far does Source D support the evidence of Source E about attitudes to government action in
the Depression? **[8]**

**Extract F** (invented interpretation).

> "The New Deal failed to bring recovery: it was the Second World War, not Roosevelt, that ended the
> Depression."

**2(c)** How far do you agree with the interpretation in Extract F? Use Extract F, Sources D and E and
your own knowledge. **[16]**

## Answers and mark guidance

### 1(a) [6]

Two developed features: rapid growth to millions of members by the mid-1920s, strongest in the South
and Midwest; its targets — black Americans, Catholics, Jews and immigrants; its methods — rallies,
cross burnings, lynchings and political influence in states such as Indiana; its decline after the
scandal surrounding its Indiana leader in 1925.

### 1(b) [8]

**Support:** limited. A implies widespread prosperity and access to modern goods; B acknowledges that
townspeople enjoy "radios and motor cars", so it agrees that consumer goods were spreading.
**Challenge:** B shows farmers in debt and losing their land; A's "easy-payment plan" hints that buying
depended on credit rather than wealth.
**Provenance:** A is an advertisement designed to sell; B is a private appeal for help, likely to stress
hardship. **Judgement:** they describe different groups, so A supports B only on the spread of consumer
goods in towns.

### 1(c) [16]

**Supporting:** hire purchase and margin buying; middle-class consumers of cars and electrical goods;
those left behind — farmers, coal and textile workers, black Americans, many immigrants; unequal
wealth (Source B).
**Challenging:** real causes of growth — mass production (Ford), new industries, war gains, Republican
tax and tariff policies; rising real wages for many urban workers; the boom's cultural reach through
cinema, radio and sport.
**Judgement:** the boom was real but narrow and dependent on credit, which helps explain the Crash.

### 2(a) [6]

Two features: about 20,000 unemployed First World War veterans marched to Washington in 1932 to demand
early payment of a bonus due in 1945; they camped in a shanty town; the army under General MacArthur
drove them out using tear gas and burned the camp, damaging Hoover's reputation.

### 2(b) [8]

**Agreement:** both accept that government action in the Depression was a major issue.
**Disagreement:** Roosevelt seeks to rebuild confidence and implies strong action is coming; E attacks
government interference in business, farming and banking.
**Provenance:** D is a new president's inaugural address meant to reassure the nation; E is a business
opponent in 1935, after the Hundred Days and as the Second New Deal began — like the Liberty League.
**Judgement:** little support; they reflect opposite views.

### 2(c) [16]

**Supporting:** unemployment stayed high (above 14% through the 1930s); the 1937–38 recession after
spending cuts; full employment came only with rearmament and war production from 1940–41; business
opposition (Source E) and Supreme Court rulings limited reform.
**Challenging:** the banking crisis was ended by the Emergency Banking Act; millions were employed by the
CCC, PWA and WPA; the TVA and REA modernised poor regions; Social Security and the Wagner Act brought
lasting reform; confidence returned (Source D).
**Judgement:** the New Deal brought relief and reform but not full recovery; the extract is partly
right about recovery but ignores relief and reform.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating an advertisement or speech as neutral evidence.
- Using Source D as a policy statement — it is about confidence, not specific measures.
- Discussing only the 1930s in (2c) without considering the war years to 1941.

## Frequently asked questions

**How is the Paper 2 historical investigation question structured?**
Part (a) asks you to describe two features (6 marks), part (b) asks how far one source supports another (8 marks) and part (c) is a 16-mark judgement that uses the sources and your own knowledge.

**How should an advertisement or a speech be treated as evidence?**
As a source with a purpose, to sell or to reassure, not as neutral evidence; its provenance affects how far it can be trusted.

**Which periods do the two enquiries cover?**
The 1920s, including the Ku Klux Klan, and the Depression and New Deal, 1929–41.
