## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Explain the patterns and growth of international tourism and the factors behind them.
- Explain how international tourism affects development and inequality.
- Explain how sport operates internationally: events, players, broadcasting and products.
- Evaluate the costs and benefits of international tourism and sporting events for different stakeholders.

## Measuring what international tourism is worth

International tourism counts as an **export**: foreign visitors spend money in the destination, just as foreign buyers pay for goods. Three measures are commonly used.

- **Tourism receipts:** spending by international visitors in the country.
- **Tourism expenditure:** spending by the country's residents when they travel abroad.
- **Tourism balance:** receipts minus expenditure. A positive balance means tourism brings in more foreign currency than residents spend abroad.

Two further ratios show **dependency**:

- Receipts as a share of **GDP** show how much of the economy tourism supports.
- Receipts as a share of **total exports** show how exposed foreign-currency earnings are to a tourism shock.

### Worked example 1: comparing two countries

Invented figures, in billions of currency units:

| | Country A (large, diversified) | Country B (small island) |
|---|---|---|
| Tourism receipts | 60 | 2.4 |
| Residents' spending abroad | 75 | 0.4 |
| GDP | 2,000 | 6 |
| Total exports | 500 | 3.2 |

1. **Tourism balance.** A: 60 - 75 = -15 (deficit). B: 2.4 - 0.4 = +2.0 (surplus).
2. **Receipts as a share of GDP.** A: 60 / 2,000 x 100 = 3%. B: 2.4 / 6 x 100 = 40%.
3. **Receipts as a share of exports.** A: 60 / 500 x 100 = 12%. B: 2.4 / 3.2 x 100 = 75%.

Country A earns far more from tourism in absolute terms but is not dependent on it, and its residents spend more abroad than visitors spend at home. Country B earns little in absolute terms, but tourism provides three-quarters of its exports, so a pandemic, a hurricane or a recession in its source markets would hit its whole economy. Absolute size and dependency are different questions; answer the one asked.

## Why some tourism retains little

Leakage, covered in the revision notes, varies with the **form** of tourism.

- **Enclave tourism.** Resorts that provide accommodation, food, entertainment and excursions inside one site keep visitors and their spending within the enclave. If the resort is foreign-owned, much of the money leaves the country.
- **All-inclusive packages.** Visitors pay in advance, often to a tour operator in their own country, so little is spent locally.
- **Cruise tourism.** Ships carry their own accommodation, food and entertainment. Passengers spend only a few hours ashore at each port, so port cities get crowds and wear on infrastructure but relatively little spending per visitor.
- **Independent and locally owned tourism** usually retains more, because visitors buy from local guesthouses, restaurants and guides.

So two destinations with the same number of arrivals can gain very different amounts. Development benefit depends on ownership and linkages, not only on visitor numbers.

## Core-periphery and the pleasure periphery

Most outbound tourists come from wealthy, urbanised **core** regions. Many long-haul destinations are in the economic **periphery**: tropical coasts, islands and mountain regions with lower incomes. Turner and Ash (1975) called this ring of destinations the **pleasure periphery**.

The idea links tourism to wider debates on development:

- **Optimistic view:** tourism transfers income from core to periphery, creates jobs and builds infrastructure.
- **Dependency view:** decisions are made by firms and tourists in the core, profits flow back, and the destination's economy is shaped around foreign demand.

Use the model as a lens, not a fact. Rapid growth of outbound travel from emerging economies, and large flows between neighbouring middle-income countries, complicate a simple core-to-periphery pattern.

## Sport across borders

### How sports spread

Diffusion helps explain where sports are played today:

- **Relocation diffusion:** a sport spreads as people migrate and take it with them, such as cricket and rugby spreading with British settlers, soldiers and administrators.
- **Hierarchical diffusion:** a sport spreads first to major cities and elite institutions, then down to smaller places.
- **Contagious diffusion:** a sport spreads outwards from neighbour to neighbour.

Today, broadcasting and social media allow fast global diffusion of fandom even where participation is low. Leagues and clubs adapt their marketing to each country, an example of **glocalisation**: a global product adjusted to local tastes, such as scheduling matches or pre-season tours to suit audiences in distant time zones.

### Sport as a global industry

The production of sporting goods is also international. Brands are often headquartered in high-income countries, while manufacturing takes place in lower-wage countries. Sialkot in Pakistan, for example, is a long-established centre for making footballs. Questions about global sport can therefore cover supply chains and labour conditions, not only events and players.

## Judging an international event's legacy

Sort legacy along three dimensions to avoid one-sided answers.

| Dimension | Possible benefit | Possible cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Hard legacy** (physical) | Transport, housing, venues | Underused venues, maintenance costs |
| **Soft legacy** (intangible) | National image, skills, volunteering, sport participation | Reputational damage from labour or rights concerns |
| **Distribution** | New jobs and amenities for some residents | Displacement and higher rents for others |

Then add **time**: benefits that last for one summer differ from benefits that last for decades. A strong judgement states which dimension matters most for the place in the question.

## Annotated model answer

*Discuss the extent to which international tourism benefits developing countries.* **(sample paragraph)**

"International tourism can bring foreign currency and jobs to countries with few other exports, which is why many small island states earn a large share of their export income from it **[benefit linked to a measure]**. However, the gain depends on the form of tourism: enclave resorts, all-inclusive packages and cruise visits retain little locally when owned by foreign firms **[mechanism of limited benefit]**. High dependency also makes these economies vulnerable to shocks in source markets **[risk]**. The benefit is therefore greatest where ownership and supply chains are local and tourism is one part of a diversified economy **[conditional judgement]**."

## Common mistakes

- Equating high tourism income with high dependency, or the reverse.
- Treating all forms of tourism as having the same leakage.
- Using the pleasure periphery as a description of all flows.
- Discussing sport only through mega-events and ignoring players, broadcasting and products.
- Evaluating legacy only by its physical venues.

## Exam technique

- **Distinguish** between measures: receipts, balance and dependency answer different questions.
- **Explain** flows with push, pull, intervening obstacles and the core-periphery pattern.
- **Discuss** and **to what extent** need both sides and a judgement about which stakeholders benefit.
- When given economic data, calculate ratios first, then interpret them.
