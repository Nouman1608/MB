## Questions

**1.** Define the term metallic bond. **[2]**

**2.** Mercury is a metal that is a liquid at 298 K.

**(a)** Explain why liquid mercury conducts electricity. **[2]**
**(b)** Molten sodium chloride also conducts electricity. Identify the particles that carry the charge in molten sodium chloride and in liquid mercury. **[2]**

**3.** A jeweller hammers a bar of silver into a thin sheet. When a crystal of calcium fluoride is struck with a hammer, it shatters.

**(a)** Explain why silver can be hammered into a new shape. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the calcium fluoride crystal shatters instead. **[2]**

**4.** A copper spoon is placed in a cup of hot tea. Within a few seconds the end of the handle feels warm. Explain this observation in terms of the bonding in copper. **[2]**

**5.** For each use below, state one property of the metal that makes it suitable, and explain that property using the metallic model.

**(a)** Copper pipes in a heating system, which a plumber bends to fit around corners **[2]**
**(b)** Aluminium sheet that is pressed into the shape of a drinks can **[2]**

**6.** Use these melting points of three group 2 metals.

| Metal | Ca | Sr | Ba |
|---|---|---|---|
| Melting point / K | 1115 | 1050 | 1000 |

Describe and explain the trend in melting point down this part of group 2. **[3]**

**7.** (calculator allowed) Rubidium is in group 1 and strontium is in group 2. Both are in period 5. Use these values, and take the Avogadro constant as 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.

| Metal | Density / g cm⁻³ | Molar mass / g mol⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|
| Rb | 1.53 | 85.47 |
| Sr | 2.64 | 87.62 |

**(a)** Calculate the amount, in mol, of atoms in 1.00 cm³ of each metal. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the number of delocalized electrons in 1.00 cm³ of each metal. **[2]**
**(c)** Use your answers to state which metal has the higher melting point. **[1]**

**8.** Arrange caesium, rubidium and strontium in order of increasing melting point. Explain your order. **[3]**

**9.** Aluminium (group 13, period 3) melts at 933 K. Gallium, directly below it in group 13, melts at 303 K. Both form 3+ ions.

**(a)** State the trend the simple metallic model predicts for melting point down a group. **[1]**
**(b)** Suggest what these data show about the simple model when it is applied to p-block metals. **[1]**

**10.** (calculator allowed) An engineer is choosing a metal for a heat sink, a finned block that draws heat away from a laptop processor. The heat sink has a volume of 50.0 cm³. Use these values.

| Metal | Density / g cm⁻³ | Thermal conductivity / W m⁻¹ K⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|
| Copper | 8.96 | 401 |
| Aluminium | 2.70 | 237 |

**(a)** Calculate the mass of the heat sink if it is made from each metal. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain, in terms of bonding, why both metals are good thermal conductors. **[2]**
**(c)** The fins are made by pressing the metal into thin sheets. Explain why this is possible. **[2]**
**(d)** Suggest, with a reason based on the data, which metal is the better choice for a portable laptop. **[2]**

**11.** HL only. This question is about period 4 elements.

| Element | Ca | V | Zn |
|---|---|---|---|
| Melting point / K | 1115 | 2183 | 693 |

**(a)** Write the valence electron configuration of calcium and of vanadium. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why vanadium has a much higher melting point than calcium. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why vanadium is a good conductor of electricity. **[1]**
**(d)** Zinc has the valence configuration 3d¹⁰ 4s². Suggest why its melting point is much lower than that of vanadium. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** An electrostatic attraction [1] that holds a lattice of positive metal ions (cations) to the delocalized electrons around them [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Name both parts of the structure and the type of force; a definition that mentions only "a sea of electrons" leaves out what the electrons are attracted to.

**2. (a)** Mercury still contains delocalized electrons after melting [1]; these are mobile and move when a potential difference is applied, so charge flows [1].
**(b)** Molten sodium chloride: **ions** (Na⁺ and Cl⁻) [1]. Liquid mercury: **delocalized electrons** [1].
*Examiner insight:* State the charge carrier by name in each case; "charged particles" for both does not show that you know the difference between ionic and metallic conduction.

**3. (a)** When the force is applied, layers of silver cations slide over one another [1]; the delocalized electrons move with them, so the non-directional attraction between cations and electrons is kept and the metal does not break [1].
**(b)** In the ionic lattice, sliding brings ions of the same charge next to each other [1]; they repel strongly, so the crystal splits apart [1].
*Examiner insight:* "Layers slide" on its own explains only half of malleability; you also need the reason the metal stays bonded after the layers move.

**4.** The delocalized electrons in the copper near the tea gain kinetic energy [1]; because they are mobile they move quickly along the handle and pass the energy on through collisions, so the far end warms up [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Thermal conductivity answers should mention energy being carried, not just "electrons can move", which is the explanation for electrical conductivity.

**5. (a)** Copper is malleable (or ductile), so the pipe bends without cracking [1]; layers of cations slide while the delocalized electrons keep attracting them [1]. (Good thermal conductivity, explained by mobile electrons carrying kinetic energy, is an acceptable alternative.)
**(b)** Aluminium is malleable [1]; the bonding is non-directional, so layers of Al³⁺ cations can move over each other and stay held by the electron cloud as the sheet is shaped [1].
*Examiner insight:* Match the property to the use described; for a can that is pressed into shape, a reason based on electrical conductivity does not answer the question.

**6.** Melting point decreases from calcium to barium [1]. All three form 2+ cations, but the ion radius increases down the group [1], so the attraction between the cations and the delocalized electrons is weaker and less energy is needed to melt the metal [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Describe" asks for the trend itself, so say that the melting point falls before you explain it; the charge staying the same is part of the explanation.

**7. (a)** Mass of 1.00 cm³ = density × volume; n = m / M.
Rb: 1.53 / 85.47 = **0.0179 mol** [1]
Sr: 2.64 / 87.62 = **0.0301 mol** [1]

**(b)** Rb: 0.0179 × 6.02 × 10²³ × 1 = **1.08 × 10²²** delocalized electrons [1]
Sr: 0.0301 × 6.02 × 10²³ × 2 = **3.63 × 10²²** delocalized electrons [1]
(Rounding the Sr amount early gives 3.62 × 10²², which is also reasonable.)

**(c)** **Strontium**, because it has the higher electron density (more than three times as many delocalized electrons per cm³) as well as the higher cation charge [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show the multiplication by the number of electrons each atom releases; quoting the number of atoms as the number of electrons for strontium is a common slip that changes the answer.

**8.** Order: **caesium < rubidium < strontium** [1]. Cs⁺ and Rb⁺ both have a 1+ charge, but Cs⁺ is larger, so its metallic bonding is weaker than that of rubidium [1]. Sr forms Sr²⁺, which has a higher charge, gives two delocalized electrons per atom and is smaller than Rb⁺, so strontium has the strongest metallic bonding [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Check the direction the question asks for; "increasing" means lowest first, and a correct order written in reverse gives the wrong answer.

**9. (a)** Melting point should decrease down a group, because the ions get larger while the charge stays the same [1].
**(b)** Gallium melts far lower than expected, so the model in terms of charge and radius does not fully explain the melting points of p-block metals [1].
*Examiner insight:* A "suggest" answer needs a reasoned idea, not detailed knowledge of gallium; say plainly that the data limit the model.

**10. (a)** mass = density × volume
Copper: 8.96 × 50.0 = **448 g** [1]
Aluminium: 2.70 × 50.0 = **135 g** [1]

**(b)** Both metals contain delocalized electrons that are free to move [1]; they gain kinetic energy near the processor and carry it rapidly through the metal to the fins [1].

**(c)** The metallic bonding is non-directional [1]; when the metal is pressed, layers of cations slide and the delocalized electrons move with them, so the metal flattens into sheets without breaking [1].

**(d)** **Aluminium** [1]. Copper conducts heat only about 1.7 times better (401 / 237), but the copper heat sink is about 3.3 times heavier (448 g against 135 g), and low mass matters in a portable device [1].
*Examiner insight:* When asked to use the data, quote numbers from the table or your calculation; a choice supported only by "aluminium is lighter" uses less of the evidence than one that weighs both properties.

**11. (a)** Ca: **4s²** [1]; V: **3d³ 4s²** [1].
**(b)** In vanadium, the 3d electrons as well as the 4s electrons can be delocalized [1], so there are more delocalized electrons per atom and a higher electron density than in calcium [1]. The attraction between the small, highly charged cations and the delocalized electrons is stronger, so more energy is needed to disrupt the lattice [1].
**(c)** The delocalized 4s and 3d electrons are mobile and can carry charge through the lattice [1].
**(d)** Zinc's full 3d sublevel contributes little to the delocalized electrons, so its metallic bonding is weaker [1].
*Examiner insight:* The key HL idea is that d-electrons join the delocalized cloud; an answer that explains vanadium's melting point using only its 4s electrons gives no reason why it differs from calcium.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a metal as "positive and negative ions" or as "atoms in a sea of electrons" instead of cations and delocalized electrons.
- Leaving out the word attraction: the bond is the electrostatic attraction, not the electrons themselves.
- Saying cations move when a solid metal conducts electricity.
- Giving the electrical conductivity explanation for a thermal conductivity question, with no mention of energy transfer.
- Stopping at "layers slide" for malleability and not saying why the metal stays bonded.
- Comparing melting points using only one factor, especially across a period where charge and radius both change.
- In calculations, forgetting to multiply by the number of electrons released per atom, or rounding too early.
- Not using the data supplied when a question says to.
- (HL) Ignoring the 3d electrons when explaining transition element properties.
