## Questions

**1.** This question is on nucleophiles in haloalkane hydrolysis.

**(a)** Define the term nucleophile. **[1]**
**(b)** From this list, choose the two species that can act as nucleophiles and explain your choice: H₂O, CH₄, OH⁻, H⁺. **[2]**

**2.** 2-Chloro-2-methylbutane, (CH₃)₂CClCH₂CH₃, is converted into an alcohol.

**(a)** State the reagent and conditions. **[2]**
**(b)** Give a balanced equation, then name the alcohol formed. **[2]**

**3.** Sketch and label the mechanism for the reaction of 1-chloro-2-methylpropane, (CH₃)₂CHCH₂Cl, with hydroxide ions. Show curly arrows, relevant dipoles and lone pairs. **[4]**

**4.** Use these bond enthalpies: C–Cl 342 kJ mol⁻¹, C–Br 283 kJ mol⁻¹, C–I 232 kJ mol⁻¹.

Explain the order of the rates of hydrolysis of 1-chloro-3-methylbutane, 1-bromo-3-methylbutane and 1-iodo-3-methylbutane. **[3]**

**5.** A student compares the rates of hydrolysis of three primary haloalkanes using aqueous silver nitrate and ethanol in a water bath.

**(a)** Explain why ethanol is added. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why all tubes are kept in the same water bath. **[1]**
**(c)** State one other variable the student must control. **[1]**
**(d)** One tube gives a cream precipitate. Write an ionic equation, with state symbols, for its formation. **[1]**
**(e)** Name the halogen present in the haloalkane in that tube. **[1]**

**6.** The student's results at 55 °C are shown.

| Haloalkane | Time for precipitate / s |
|---|---|
| 1-chloro-2-methylpropane | 2400 |
| 1-bromo-2-methylpropane | 300 |
| 1-iodo-2-methylpropane | 20 |

**(a)** Calculate the relative rate, 1/time, for each haloalkane. **[2]**
**(b)** By what factor is the iodo compound's rate greater than the bromo compound's? **[1]**
**(c)** State what the results show about the strength of the carbon–halogen bonds. **[1]**

**7.** In the experiment in question 6, water is the nucleophile.

**(a)** Write an equation for the hydrolysis of 1-bromo-2-methylpropane by water. **[1]**
**(b)** State which atom in water donates the electron pair. **[1]**

**8.** 1,2-Dichloro-1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethane, CF₂ClCF₂Cl, is a CFC.

**(a)** Explain why UV radiation breaks a C–Cl bond and not a C–F bond in this molecule. **[1]**
**(b)** Write an equation for the formation of chlorine radicals from CF₂ClCF₂Cl. **[1]**
**(c)** Write two equations to show how chlorine radicals catalyse the breakdown of ozone. **[2]**
**(d)** Write the overall equation. **[1]**

**9.** Nitrogen monoxide radicals, •NO, also break down ozone. Write two equations for this process and explain why •NO is described as a catalyst. **[3]**

**10.** 5.38 g of 1-chloroheptane, C₇H₁₅Cl, is heated under reflux with excess aqueous sodium hydroxide. 3.94 g of heptan-1-ol, C₇H₁₅OH, is isolated.

**(a)** Write an equation for the reaction and state why heating under reflux is used. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage yield of heptan-1-ol. **[3]**
**(c)** The aqueous layer is acidified with dilute nitric acid before aqueous silver nitrate is added. Explain why the acid is needed. **[1]**
**(d)** The aqueous layer gives 5.17 g of silver chloride. Calculate the percentage of the 1-chloroheptane that was hydrolysed. **[2]**

**11.** A bromoalkane, CₙH₂ₙ₊₁Br, is fully hydrolysed. 3.018 g of the bromoalkane gives 3.756 g of AgBr after adding acidified silver nitrate.

**(a)** What colour is the silver halide formed? **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the relative molecular mass of the bromoalkane and find n. **[4]**
**(c)** The bromoalkane is primary and unbranched. Give its name and the name of the alcohol formed with NaOH(aq). **[2]**
**(d)** Predict, with a reason, whether the matching chloroalkane would be hydrolysed faster or slower. **[1]**

**12.** Bromotrifluoromethane, CF₃Br, was used in fire extinguishers.

**(a)** Write an equation for the action of UV on CF₃Br, and explain which bond breaks. **[2]**
**(b)** Write two equations showing how the radical formed catalyses ozone breakdown. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why CFCs were widely used, why their low reactivity caused a problem, and how scientific evidence led to action. **[3]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** An electron pair donor [1]
**(b)** H₂O and OH⁻ [1]; each has a lone pair on oxygen to donate to an electron-deficient atom, while CH₄ and H⁺ have no lone pair [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "A negative ion" is not a definition; water is neutral, so the answer must mention an electron pair being donated.

**2. (a)** Aqueous sodium hydroxide (or NaOH(aq)) [1]; heat under reflux [1]
**(b)** (CH₃)₂CClCH₂CH₃ + NaOH → (CH₃)₂C(OH)CH₂CH₃ + NaCl [1]; **2-methylbutan-2-ol** [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The OH goes on carbon 2, where the Cl was; check the position number in your name matches.

**3.** Dipole shown: Cδ+–Clδ− [1]; :OH⁻ with lone pair, curly arrow from the lone pair to the δ+ carbon [1]; curly arrow from the C–Cl bond to Cl [1]; products (CH₃)₂CHCH₂OH and :Cl⁻ [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* An arrow that starts on the O atom itself, rather than on its lone pair or negative charge, does not show where the electron pair comes from.

**4.** The C–X bond must be broken in hydrolysis [1]. Bond enthalpy decreases C–Cl > C–Br > C–I, so C–I needs least energy to break [1]. Rate order: **iodo > bromo > chloro compound** [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the data given; an answer built on polarity or electronegativity explains the wrong thing and gains little credit.

**5. (a)** The haloalkane is immiscible with the aqueous silver nitrate; ethanol dissolves both, so they can mix and react [1]
**(b)** Temperature affects rate, so it must be the same in every tube [1]
**(c)** Any one: same volume or amount of each haloalkane; same volume and concentration of AgNO₃(aq); same chain length; same volume of ethanol [1]
**(d)** Ag⁺(aq) + Br⁻(aq) → AgBr(s) [1]
**(e)** Bromine [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* For (c), "same amount" alone is vague; say which substance and whether you mean volume or concentration.

**6. (a)** Chloro: 1/2400 = 4.17 × 10⁻⁴ s⁻¹; bromo: 1/300 = 3.33 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹ [1]; iodo: 1/20 = **0.0500 s⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** 300 ÷ 20 = **15** times faster [1]
**(c)** Bond strength order is C–Cl > C–Br > C–I; the weakest bond breaks fastest [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Give units of s⁻¹ for 1/time, and divide times for a ratio rather than subtracting them.

**7. (a)** (CH₃)₂CHCH₂Br + H₂O → (CH₃)₂CHCH₂OH + H⁺ + Br⁻ (or HBr) [1]
**(b)** The oxygen atom (from one of its lone pairs) [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Water is a reactant here, so write it in the equation; NaOH does not appear in this experiment.

**8. (a)** C–Cl has a lower bond enthalpy than C–F, so less energy is needed to break it [1]
**(b)** CF₂ClCF₂Cl → CF₂ClCF₂• + •Cl [1]
**(c)** •Cl + O₃ → •ClO + O₂ [1]; •ClO + O → •Cl + O₂ [1]
**(d)** O₃ + O → 2O₂ [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Every radical needs its dot, including the organic fragment in (b).

**9.** •NO + O₃ → •NO₂ + O₂ [1]; •NO₂ + O → •NO + O₂ [1]; •NO is regenerated in the second step, so it is not used up overall [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Built by analogy with the chlorine cycle, each equation must still balance atoms on both sides.

**10. (a)** C₇H₁₅Cl + NaOH → C₇H₁₅OH + NaCl [1]; reflux allows long heating without losing the volatile organic compounds [1]
**(b)** Mr C₇H₁₅Cl = 134.5, n = 5.38 ÷ 134.5 = 0.0400 mol [1]; theoretical mass = 0.0400 × 116.0 = 4.64 g [1]; yield = 3.94 ÷ 4.64 × 100 = **84.9%** [1]
**(c)** To remove excess OH⁻, which would otherwise form a brown precipitate with Ag⁺ and hide the result [1]
**(d)** Mr AgCl = 143.4, n(AgCl) = 5.17 ÷ 143.4 = 0.03605 mol [1]; 0.03605 ÷ 0.0400 × 100 = **90.1%** [1] **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Work out Mr of heptan-1-ol (116.0) from the formula; using the haloalkane's Mr for the product is a frequent slip.

**11. (a)** Cream [1]
**(b)** Mr AgBr = 187.8; n(AgBr) = 3.756 ÷ 187.8 = 0.0200 mol [1]; n(bromoalkane) = 0.0200 mol (one Br each) [1]; Mr = 3.018 ÷ 0.0200 = 150.9 [1]; 14.0n + 1.0 + 79.9 = 150.9, so **n = 5** [1]
**(c)** **1-bromopentane** [1]; **pentan-1-ol** [1]
**(d)** Slower, because the C–Cl bond enthalpy is greater than C–Br [1] **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the link from n(AgBr) to n(bromoalkane); stating Mr = 150.9 without the 1 : 1 step leaves the logic unclear.

**12. (a)** CF₃Br → •CF₃ + •Br [1]; C–Br is the weakest bond in the molecule, much weaker than C–F [1]
**(b)** •Br + O₃ → •BrO + O₂ [1]; •BrO + O → •Br + O₂ [1]
**(c)** CFCs are non-toxic, non-flammable and unreactive, so they suited refrigerants, aerosols and solvents [1]; their low reactivity means they survive long enough to reach the stratosphere, where UV releases chlorine radicals [1]; evidence of ozone loss was accepted and governments agreed to phase out CFCs (Montreal Protocol, 1987), leading to chlorine-free replacements [1] **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* In (c), each of the three ideas needs its own clear sentence; a list of uses alone answers only the first part.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining a nucleophile without the words "electron pair donor".
- Drawing the first curly arrow from carbon to oxygen instead of from the lone pair to carbon.
- Omitting the Cδ+–Xδ− dipole or the charge on the halide ion.
- Explaining rate trends with polarity rather than bond enthalpy.
- Writing NaOH in the equation for hydrolysis by water.
- Confusing cream (AgBr) with yellow (AgI).
- Breaking C–F in a CFC or halon radical equation.
- Missing dots on radicals, or leaving the catalyst in the overall equation.
- Using the wrong Mr for the product in yield calculations.
- Subtracting times instead of dividing them when comparing rates.
