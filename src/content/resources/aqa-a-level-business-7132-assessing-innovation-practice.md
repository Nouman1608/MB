These questions cover section 3.9.2 assessing innovation of the AQA A-level Business (7132) specification. All businesses and figures are invented. Revise first with the [study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-assessing-innovation/) and the [revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-assessing-innovation-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** A food factory installs a new robotic line that cuts waste by 15%. What type of innovation is this? **[1]**

A: Product innovation

B: Process innovation

C: Invention

D: Benchmarking

**2.** Which **one** of the following is protected by copyright? **[1]**

A: A new chemical formula for a paint

B: The source code of a computer game

C: A new type of engine

D: An idea for a new business

**3.** Define "Kaizen". **[2]**

**4.** Bryn Toys has revenue of £15m. It spends 6% on R&D, and new products make up 30% of revenue. Calculate its R&D spend and the revenue from new products. **[2]**

**5.** Explain **one** reason why a patent may not give a business full protection. **[3]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**6.** Bryn Toys runs a Kaizen scheme. In a year, 150 suggestions are adopted, saving an average of £600 each. Calculate the total annual saving and explain why this might understate the benefit. **[4]**

**7.** Bryn's return rate on online orders is 5.0%, while the best rival's is 2.0%. Bryn sells 80,000 orders a year, and each return costs £15. Calculate the potential annual saving from matching the rival, and name the technique being used. **[4]**

**8.** Explain **two** pressures that might push Bryn Toys to become more innovative. **[4]**

**9.** Analyse **two** benefits to Bryn Toys of creating an intrapreneurship scheme in which staff can spend part of their time developing new toy ideas. **[6]**

**10.** Bryn invents a new toy mechanism. Explain **two** ways in which it could protect its intellectual property. **[4]**

**11.** Analyse the likely impact of an innovation strategy on the finance and operations functions of a small manufacturer. **[6]**

**12.** Evaluate whether a small toy maker should spend heavily on R&D to compete with larger rivals. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. A new way of producing is process innovation. **[1]**

**2.** **B** [1]. Software code is an original work; copyright arises automatically. The others are inventions or ideas, which copyright does not protect. **[1]**

**3.** A Japanese approach to continuous improvement [1] in which all employees make small, regular improvements to products and processes [1]. **[2]**

**4.** R&D = 6% × £15m = **£900,000** [1]. New product revenue = 30% × £15m = **£4.5m** [1]. **[2]**

**5.** A patent lasts only for a limited time (up to 20 years), after which others can copy [1]. It also protects only in the countries where it is granted [1], and a rival may design a similar product that avoids the patent claims; enforcing it through the courts is costly for a small firm [1]. **[3]**

**6.** 150 × £600 = **£90,000** [1] a year [1]. The figure may understate the benefit because Kaizen also improves quality, morale and staff involvement [1], which raise sales and productivity in ways that are not measured in the savings, and the improvements continue year after year [1]. **[4]**

**7.** Excess returns = (5.0% − 2.0%) × 80,000 = 3% × 80,000 = 2,400 [1]; saving = 2,400 × £15 = **£36,000** [1]. The technique is benchmarking [1] because it compares performance with the best rival [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Pressure 1: competition [1]. Rival toy makers launch new products each year, so Bryn's range may look dated and lose sales unless it innovates [1]. Pressure 2: changing customer expectations and technology [1]. Children and parents expect connected or educational toys, and the product life cycle of a toy is short, so new products are needed to replace declining sales [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Benefit 1: ideas from staff [1]. Employees who work with products and customers have practical knowledge, so they can generate ideas the company would not find through formal R&D [1], at a lower cost than hiring an external design team [1]. Benefit 2: motivation and retention [1]. Giving staff time and freedom shows trust and gives them a chance to see their ideas turned into products [1], which reduces the risk that creative staff leave to start their own company [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Way 1: apply for a patent [1], which gives exclusive rights to make and sell the mechanism for up to 20 years and so stops rivals copying it [1]. Way 2: register trademarks for the toy's name and logo or protect designs and instructions by copyright [1], which prevents rivals using the brand or copying the packaging and manuals [1]. (Credit also: keeping the production method secret.) **[4]**

**11.** Finance: an innovation strategy needs funds for research, equipment and launch before sales arrive [1], so a small firm may need loans or investors [1], and the uncertain returns put pressure on cash flow [1]. Operations: new products need new processes, machinery or training [1], and production must be flexible to handle short runs of new items [1], while quality control is needed to avoid launch problems [1]. **[6]**

**12.** For: innovation can give a small firm a unique product that it can sell at a premium without competing on price against rivals with scale economies [1]. New products allow it to stay ahead of changing tastes [1]. A patented design can be licensed to others for income [1]. Heavy R&D spending may attract a bigger rival to buy the company at a high price [1]. Against: R&D is costly and uncertain, and a small firm has less cash to survive failures [1]. Large rivals can copy successful ideas quickly and use their marketing and distribution power [1]. Many new products fail, and development takes time [1]. Patents are costly to defend. Alternatives: Kaizen and benchmarking cost less [1], and buying in designs, or focusing on one niche, may be cheaper [1]. Factors: the firm's finances and how many ideas it has [1]. Judgement: a small firm should rarely commit heavily to R&D unless it has a strong, protectable idea and finance [1]; a measured approach, such as small trials and staff ideas, is more sensible [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* High-scoring answers link the method to the firm's size and finances, and weigh protection and risk before they decide.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating innovation as only a new product.
- Describing Kaizen as a one-off project.
- Assuming a patent protects an idea for ever.
- Saying R&D guarantees success.
- Stating benefits without linking them to the firm in the case.
