## 4.1 Introduction to marketing

Marketing is the management function responsible for **identifying, anticipating and satisfying customer requirements profitably**. Distinguish **market orientation** (starting from customer needs and designing products to meet them) from **product orientation** (starting from what the business can make, then finding customers for it) — a firm's orientation shapes every later decision in this unit, so identify it first when analysing a case study business.

This sub-topic also carries the unit's only two calculations, both assessed:

```
market share (%)  = (business's sales / total market sales) x 100
market growth (%)  = ((this period's sales - last period's sales) / last period's sales) x 100
```

A rising market share alongside falling market growth can still mean falling sales in absolute terms, so both figures should be interpreted together, not read as automatically good or bad news in isolation.

## 4.2 Marketing planning

A marketing plan sets **objectives** (e.g. market share, brand awareness) and the **marketing mix strategy** to reach them, informed by market research and forecasting. Marketing objectives should link back to the business's overall (corporate) objectives from Unit 1 — a strong answer connects a marketing decision to the broader business goal it serves, not just to "increasing sales" generically.

Before setting the mix, a marketing plan requires **segmentation, targeting and positioning (STP)**: segmentation divides a market into groups with similar needs (e.g. by demographics, geography, or lifestyle); targeting selects which segment(s) to serve, often choosing between a **niche market** (a small, specific segment with less competition but a limited customer base) and a **mass market** (a large segment with more competition but greater sales potential); positioning is how the business wants the product perceived relative to competitors, often mapped on a **position map** (also called a perceptual map) with two attributes as axes, such as price against quality. A clear **unique selling point (USP)** -- the specific feature that differentiates a product from competitors -- underpins effective positioning and should be identifiable from the position map.

## 4.3 Sales forecasting (HL only)

This sub-topic itself requires no calculation: it asks only for the **benefits and limitations of sales forecasting**, evaluated for a given business. Forecasts inform production planning, cash flow planning and stock decisions, but they rest on past data and assumptions about the future, so a strong evaluation weighs the value of planning ahead against the risk of an inaccurate forecast in a fast-changing market.

The trend-forecasting calculation itself sits in the quantitative toolkit rather than this sub-topic: **simple linear regression** (HL only) plots data on a **scatter diagram**, fits a **line of best fit**, uses **correlation** to judge how reliable that line is, and **extrapolates** it forward to forecast a future value. Revise the toolkit technique separately from this sub-topic's own focus on evaluating forecasting's usefulness.

## 4.4 Market research

| Type | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary research** | New data collected directly for this purpose | Surveys, interviews, focus groups, observation |
| **Secondary research** | Existing data collected for another purpose, reused | Government statistics, industry reports, competitor data |
| **Quantitative research** | Numerical data | Sales figures, survey ratings |
| **Qualitative research** | Non-numerical, opinion-based data | Focus group discussion, open-ended interview responses |

**Sampling methods** (random, quota, convenience) each trade off representativeness against cost and speed — be ready to justify which sampling method suits a given business scenario, not just define each one.

## 4.5 The seven Ps of the marketing mix

The traditional 4 Ps (Product, Price, Place, Promotion) are extended to seven for services and the IB course:

- **Product** — what is sold, including features, quality, branding and the product life cycle.
- **Price** — the pricing strategy used (penetration, skimming, cost-plus, competitive).
- **Place** — the distribution channels used to get the product to the customer.
- **Promotion** — how the business communicates with and persuades customers.
- **People** — staff who deliver the product or service and shape customer experience.
- **Process** — the systems and procedures through which a service is delivered.
- **Physical evidence** — the tangible cues (environment, packaging, branding materials) that signal quality, especially for services.

**People, Process and Physical evidence** are the three extended Ps most relevant to **services** rather than physical products — a strong answer explains *why* these three matter more for a service business (where the "product" is intangible and experienced, not simply purchased) rather than just listing all seven Ps without differentiation.

## 4.6 International marketing (HL only)

Marketing across borders must adapt to differences in **culture, language, legal requirements and consumer behaviour** between markets. Businesses choose between **standardisation** (one marketing approach used globally, lower cost) and **adaptation** (tailoring the mix to each local market, higher relevance) — evaluate this trade-off for the specific product and markets in a case study, rather than asserting one approach is always superior.

## How Unit 4 is examined

Paper 1's pre-released statement can draw on marketing content alongside Unit 1 stakeholder analysis or Unit 3 numerical tools in the same unseen case study, so revising the seven Ps in isolation from the rest of the course leaves a real gap -- practise identifying which Unit 4 sub-topic a given Paper 1 scenario is really testing, in combination with content from other units. HL students should also expect Paper 2's quantitative stimulus material to occasionally draw on sales forecasting (4.3) alongside Unit 3's financial calculations, since both units share a numerical, data-interpretation style of question.

## Common mistakes

- Listing all seven Ps without linking any of them to the specific business or product in the question.
- Confusing primary research (new data, collected directly) with secondary research (existing data, reused).
- Treating a sales forecast as a guaranteed figure rather than a prediction with inherent uncertainty.
- Assuming international marketing always requires full standardisation or full adaptation, instead of evaluating the trade-off for the specific context given.

## Quick self-test

1. Distinguish market orientation from product orientation in one sentence each.
2. Name two types of primary research and two types of secondary research.
3. Explain why People, Process and Physical evidence matter especially for service businesses.
4. Give one advantage of standardisation and one advantage of adaptation in international marketing.
5. Explain how a marketing objective should connect to a business's overall corporate objectives.

**Answers:** 1. Market orientation starts from customer needs and designs products to meet them;
product orientation starts from what the business can make and then finds customers for it. 2.
Primary: surveys and interviews (or focus groups, observation). Secondary: government statistics
and industry reports (or competitor data). 3. Because a service's "product" is intangible and
experienced rather than simply purchased, the people delivering it, the process customers go
through, and the physical evidence surrounding the service all shape the customer's perception of
quality in a way that does not apply in the same way to a physical product. 4. Standardisation:
lower cost, since one marketing approach is used globally. Adaptation: higher relevance, since the
mix is tailored to local culture, language, legal requirements and consumer behaviour. 5. A
marketing objective (e.g. a sales or market share target) should be set so that achieving it
directly supports the business's wider corporate objectives (e.g. growth or profit maximisation),
rather than being pursued in isolation from what the business as a whole is trying to achieve.
