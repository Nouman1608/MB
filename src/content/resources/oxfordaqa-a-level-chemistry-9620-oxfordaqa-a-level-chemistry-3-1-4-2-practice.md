These questions cover **3.1.4.2 Calorimetry** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3, including required practical 2. It is International AS content, examined in Unit 1. Section A has short recall and application questions; Section B has longer, structured questions. Unless told otherwise, take c = 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ for water and dilute solutions, and the density of solutions as 1.00 g cm⁻³. All experimental readings are illustrative. If a point is unfamiliar, read the [calorimetry study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-4-2/) or the [calorimetry revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-4-2-revision-notes/) first.

## Questions

### Section A

**1.** In the equation q = mcΔT, state what m and c represent, with a unit for each. **[2]**

**2.** 75.0 g of water warms from 18.3 °C to 29.7 °C. Calculate the heat energy transferred, in kJ. **[2]**

**3.** Explain why a reaction in solution is carried out in a polystyrene cup with a lid. **[2]**

**4.** A reaction of 0.0150 mol of a reactant releases 4.62 kJ of heat to the water around it. Give the molar enthalpy change, with its sign, and explain the sign. **[2]**

**5.** Calculate the temperature change when 2.09 kJ of heat is taken in by 50.0 g of water. **[2]**

**6.** Give two reasons why a value of ΔcH measured with a spirit burner and a copper can is less exothermic than the data-book value. **[2]**

### Section B

**7.** A student adds excess iron powder to 50.0 cm³ of 0.400 mol dm⁻³ copper(II) sulfate solution: Fe(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → FeSO₄(aq) + Cu(s). The iron is added at 3.0 minutes. Results:

| Time / min | 0.0 | 1.0 | 2.0 | 3.0 | 3.5 | 4.0 | 4.5 | 5.0 | 6.0 | 7.0 | 8.0 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Temperature / °C | 21.0 | 21.0 | 21.0 | — | 28.0 | 33.0 | 34.6 | 34.2 | 33.4 | 32.6 | 31.8 |

**(a)** Explain why the student extrapolates the cooling part of the graph. **[2]**

**(b)** The cooling points from 5.0 to 8.0 minutes lie on a straight line. Use them to find ΔT. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the enthalpy change for the reaction, in kJ mol⁻¹. **[3]**

**(d)** Explain why the iron must be in excess. **[1]**

**8.** Burning 0.480 g of methanol, CH₃OH (Mr 32.0), raises the temperature of 150 g of water from 20.0 °C to 36.5 °C. The data-book value of ΔcH° for methanol is −726 kJ mol⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate the experimental enthalpy of combustion of methanol. **[3]**

**(b)** Express the experimental value as a percentage of the data-book value. **[1]**

**(c)** Give one source of error that makes the experimental value less exothermic, and an improvement that would reduce it. **[2]**

**9.** 3.73 g of potassium chloride (Mr 74.6) is dissolved in 50.0 g of water. The temperature falls by 4.0 K.

**(a)** Calculate the enthalpy change of solution of potassium chloride. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain the sign of your answer. **[1]**

**(c)** Heat flows into the cold solution from the surroundings during the experiment. State and explain the effect this has on the calculated value. **[1]**

**10.** A student wants to heat 200 g of water by 25.0 K by burning hexane, C₆H₁₄ (Mr 86.0, ΔcH° = −4163 kJ mol⁻¹).

**(a)** Calculate the minimum mass of hexane needed, assuming no heat loss. **[3]**

**(b)** Suggest why the student chooses a temperature rise of 25.0 K rather than 2.5 K. **[1]**

**11.** 25.0 cm³ of 0.200 mol dm⁻³ silver nitrate is mixed with 25.0 cm³ of 0.300 mol dm⁻³ sodium chloride: AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq). The temperature rises by 1.6 K.

**(a)** Identify the limiting reactant. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the enthalpy change for the reaction, in kJ mol⁻¹. **[3]**

**(c)** The thermometer reads to ±0.05 °C at each reading. Calculate the percentage uncertainty in ΔT and suggest how the experiment could be changed to reduce it. **[2]**

**12.** Outline how you would carry out required practical 2 to measure the enthalpy change for the reaction between zinc powder and copper(II) sulfate solution, and how you would process the results to obtain ΔH. **[6]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** m: the mass of the substance whose temperature changes (the water or solution), in g [1]; c: its specific heat capacity, in J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ [1] **[2]**

**2.** ΔT = 11.4 K; q = 75.0 × 4.18 × 11.4 = 3574 J [1]; = **3.57 kJ** [1] **[2]**

**3.** Polystyrene is a good thermal insulator and the lid reduces heat loss by evaporation and convection [1]; so more of the heat from the reaction stays in the solution and the measured ΔT is closer to the true value [1] **[2]**

**4.** ΔH = −4.62 ÷ 0.0150 = **−308 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]; negative because heat is released to the surroundings (exothermic) [1] **[2]**

**5.** ΔT = q ÷ (mc) = 2090 ÷ (50.0 × 4.18) [1]; = **10.0 K** (a fall, as heat is taken in) [1] **[2]**

**6.** Heat is lost to the air or the can instead of heating the water [1]; incomplete combustion, or fuel evaporating from the wick (accept either) [1] **[2]**

### Section B

**7. (a)** Heat is lost to the surroundings while the reaction is happening, so the highest reading is lower than the true maximum [1]; extrapolating to the time of mixing estimates the temperature with no heat loss [1]

**(b)** The cooling line falls by 0.8 K per minute, so at 3.0 minutes it reads 34.2 + 2 × 0.8 = 35.8 °C [1]; ΔT = 35.8 − 21.0 = **14.8 K** [1]

**(c)** q = 50.0 × 4.18 × 14.8 = 3093 J = 3.093 kJ [1]; n(CuSO₄) = 0.400 × 0.0500 = 0.0200 mol [1]; ΔH = −3.093 ÷ 0.0200 = **−155 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]

**(d)** So that all the copper(II) sulfate reacts and its amount fixes the heat released [1] **[8]**

*Examiner insight:* Using the highest recorded temperature (34.6 °C) gives ΔT = 13.6 K and a value that is too small; the question asks for the extrapolated value.

**8. (a)** q = 150 × 4.18 × 16.5 = 10 346 J = 10.35 kJ [1]; n(methanol) = 0.480 ÷ 32.0 = 0.0150 mol [1]; ΔcH = −10.35 ÷ 0.0150 = **−690 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]

**(b)** 690 ÷ 726 × 100 = **95.0 %** [1]

**(c)** Heat lost to the surroundings [1]; use a draught shield and a lid on the can (or similar matched improvement) [1] **[6]**

**9. (a)** q = 50.0 × 4.18 × 4.0 = 836 J = 0.836 kJ [1]; n(KCl) = 3.73 ÷ 74.6 = 0.0500 mol [1]; ΔH = +0.836 ÷ 0.0500 = **+16.7 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]

**(b)** The temperature fell, so heat was taken in from the water: the process is endothermic and ΔH is positive [1]

**(c)** The measured fall in temperature is too small, so the calculated ΔH is less positive (smaller) than the true value [1] **[5]**

**10. (a)** q = 200 × 4.18 × 25.0 = 20 900 J = 20.9 kJ [1]; n(hexane) = 20.9 ÷ 4163 = 5.02 × 10⁻³ mol [1]; mass = 5.02 × 10⁻³ × 86.0 = **0.432 g** [1]

**(b)** A larger temperature change gives a smaller percentage uncertainty in ΔT [1] **[4]**

**11. (a)** n(AgNO₃) = 5.00 × 10⁻³ mol and n(NaCl) = 7.50 × 10⁻³ mol, so **silver nitrate** is limiting [1]

**(b)** Total mass = 50.0 g; q = 50.0 × 4.18 × 1.6 = 334 J = 0.334 kJ [1]; n = 5.00 × 10⁻³ mol [1]; ΔH = −0.334 ÷ 5.00 × 10⁻³ = **−66.9 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]

**(c)** Uncertainty in ΔT = 2 × 0.05 = 0.1 K, so 0.1 ÷ 1.6 × 100 = **6.3 %** [1]; use more concentrated solutions (or a larger amount of the limiting reactant in the same volume) to give a larger ΔT [1] **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* In (b), the mass is that of both solutions together; 25.0 g halves q and the answer.

**12.** Measure a known volume (for example 25.0 cm³) of copper(II) sulfate solution of known concentration into a polystyrene cup with a lid, standing in a beaker [1]

Record the temperature every 30 s for about 3 minutes before adding the zinc [1]

Add an excess of zinc powder at a recorded time, stir, and continue recording every 30 s for several minutes after the maximum [1]

Plot temperature against time and extrapolate the cooling line back to the time of mixing to find ΔT [1]

Calculate q = mcΔT using the mass of the solution (volume × 1.00 g cm⁻³) [1]

Calculate moles of copper(II) sulfate from concentration × volume, then ΔH = −q ÷ n, in kJ mol⁻¹ [1] **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the mass of the solid or fuel, or only one solution's volume, as m.
- Forgetting to change J to kJ.
- Leaving off the sign or the units of ΔH.
- Using the highest recorded temperature instead of the extrapolated value.
- Naming an error without saying how it affects the result, or giving an improvement that does not address that error.
