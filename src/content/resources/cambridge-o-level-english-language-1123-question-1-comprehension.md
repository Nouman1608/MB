This guide covers **Question 1, the Comprehension task**, in Section A of Cambridge O Level English
Language 1123 Paper 1 Reading. The syllabus says: "Candidates respond to a series of sub-questions
based on Text A, a narrative text. These are short answers testing understanding of both explicit and
implicit meanings." It carries **16 marks** and tests **R1** (explicit meanings) and **R2** (implicit
meanings and attitudes). For the whole paper, including Question 2 and Section B, start with the
[Paper 1 Reading study guide](/resources/o-level-english-language-paper-1-reading/). This page goes
inside Question 1 only.

## What you are working with

Text A is "approximately 900 words long" and comes from "either the twentieth or the twenty-first
century". It is a **narrative**: a story or an extract from one, with characters, a setting and
events. That shapes the questions. You will be asked what happens and why, but also what characters
feel, what they want, how they see each other, and what a detail tells you that the narrator never
says outright. All of Question 1's sub-questions are short answers; none asks for an essay.

## Explicit and implicit: two different jobs

**Explicit meaning (R1)** is on the surface. The answer is stated in the text and your task is to
find it and give it accurately. A question such as *"What two things did Amira notice when she entered
the kitchen?"* is explicit.

**Implicit meaning (R2)** sits underneath. The text gives clues; you draw the conclusion. *"What does
Amira's reaction suggest about her relationship with her uncle?"* is implicit. No sentence will say
"Amira was afraid of her uncle"; you infer it from what she does, what she avoids and how the narrator
describes her.

Recognising which kind of question you face saves time and marks. Explicit questions reward precise
location and economy. Implicit questions reward a clear inference **anchored in a detail**: the mark is
for the conclusion, and the detail is what makes the conclusion credible.

## How sub-questions are typically worded

Different wordings point to different kinds of answer. The command words defined in the syllabus
help:

- **Identify** ("name/select/recognise"): pick out a word, phrase or detail.
- **Give** ("produce an answer from a given source"): state it from the text.
- **Explain** ("set out purposes or reasons / make the relationships between things clear / say why
  and/or how and support with relevant evidence"): go beyond stating to reasons.

You will also meet phrasings such as *"in your own words"*, *"what does the writer mean by..."*,
*"what does ... suggest about..."* and *"give two reasons why..."*. Each tells you something:

| Wording | What it wants |
|---|---|
| "in your own words" | the meaning, re-expressed; copied key words earn nothing |
| "what does the writer mean by..." | an explanation of a phrase in context |
| "what does ... suggest about..." | an inference (R2) |
| "give two..." | exactly two separate points |
| "quote a word or phrase" | the exact words only, short and precise |

## Matching the answer to the marks

Each sub-question shows its marks. Use them as a guide to the number of distinct points:

- **1 mark**: one point, a phrase or short sentence.
- **2 marks**: two separate points, or a point and its explanation. Make the two visible: "firstly...;
  secondly..." or two short sentences.
- **3 marks**: three points, or an inference with developed support.

Writing more does not add marks; a long answer to a one-mark question uses time that a three-mark
inference needs.

## Answering "in your own words"

This instruction is checking that you understand the words, not that you can find them. Three
methods:

1. **Replace key words** with accurate equivalents: *"reluctant"* becomes *"unwilling"*.
2. **Unpack the phrase**: *"her voice was brittle"* becomes *"she sounded tense, as if she might
   break down"*.
3. **Change the grammar**: a noun phrase becomes a clause, *"his hesitation"* becomes *"he paused
   because he was unsure"*.

Small, everyday words (the, was, house) do not need replacing. The key words of the phrase do.

## Worked example

The extract below is original, written for this guide in the style of a narrative Text A.

> The bus was late again, and by the time Daniel reached the gates, the school was already silent.
> He stood for a moment with his hand on the cold railing, counting the windows of the first floor
> until he found the one that was his classroom. Mr Okafor would be writing the date on the board by
> now, in his slow, careful capitals. Daniel thought of the empty chair by the radiator and of the way
> thirty heads would turn when the door opened. He let go of the railing, then took hold of it again.
> In his bag, the permission slip his mother had not signed seemed to grow heavier with every minute.

**(a)** Why did Daniel arrive after school had started? **[1]**

> *Answer:* His bus was late.

*Commentary:* an explicit point (R1), stated in the first sentence. One mark, one phrase.

**(b)** Explain in your own words what Daniel fears will happen when he enters the classroom. **[2]**

> *Answer:* He is afraid that all his classmates will look at him at once, so he will feel
> embarrassed and singled out in front of everyone.

*Commentary:* "thirty heads would turn" is explained, not copied (*all his classmates will look at
him*), and the second mark is for the feeling it produces (*embarrassed, singled out*).

**(c)** What does the sentence "He let go of the railing, then took hold of it again" suggest about
Daniel's state of mind? **[2]**

> *Answer:* It suggests he is hesitant and torn: he starts to move towards the building but loses his
> courage and holds back.

*Commentary:* an inference (R2). The answer names the state of mind (*hesitant, torn*) and links it to
the action (*starts to move, holds back*). "He is nervous" alone would earn one mark at most.

**(d)** Suggest two reasons, apart from being late, why Daniel might not want to go in. **[2]**

> *Answer:* Firstly, he has a permission slip that his mother has not signed, so he expects to be in
> trouble. Secondly, he dreads the attention of the whole class turning towards him as he walks in.

*Commentary:* two separate points from different parts of the extract. The slip "seemed to grow
heavier" is not about weight: the image shows his worry growing, which supports the first point.

## Common mistakes

- **Answering from imagination** rather than from the text: "he hates school" is not supported here.
- **Quoting a whole sentence** when asked to explain, or explaining when asked to quote.
- **Copying key words** in an "own words" question.
- **One point for two marks**, or two points merged so the examiner cannot separate them.
- **Inference with no anchor**: a feeling named with no link to what the character does or says.
- **Answering a different question**: describing what happens when the question asks why.

## Exam technique

- Read Text A through once before starting; Question 1 follows the story, so knowing its outline
  helps you place each answer.
- Sub-questions usually move through the text in order; use line or paragraph references in the
  question to find your place quickly.
- Underline the key instruction words (*own words*, *suggest*, *two*).
- Keep answers short and precise. Question 1 is 16 of Section A's 25 marks; leave enough time for the
  9-mark Question 2 on the same text.
