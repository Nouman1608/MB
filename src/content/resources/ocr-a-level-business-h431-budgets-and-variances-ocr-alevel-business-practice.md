## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by a budget. **[2]**

**2.** Explain the difference between a favourable and an adverse variance. **[3]**

**3.** A department budgeted £30,000 for wages and spent £32,400. State the amount of the variance, whether it is favourable or adverse, and the variance as a percentage of the budget. **[3]**

**4.** Which **one** of the following would be a favourable variance?

A: Sales revenue £5,000 below budget.

B: Materials cost £2,000 above budget.

C: Advertising cost £1,500 below budget.

D: Wages £800 above budget. **[1]**

**5.** Explain **one** reason why a business should involve managers in setting budgets. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Larkin Tours produced this budget for March and recorded these actual results.

| Item | Budget | Actual |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | £80,000 | £84,000 |
| Wages | £30,000 | £32,400 |
| Marketing | £8,000 | £6,500 |
| Other costs | £20,000 | £20,400 |

**(a)** Calculate the budgeted profit and the actual profit. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate each variance (revenue, wages, marketing, other costs) and state whether it is favourable or adverse. **[4]**

**(c)** Calculate the profit variance and show that it equals the sum of the item variances. **[3]**

**(d)** Analyse the variances for wages and marketing. **[6]**

**7.** Larkin Tours' wages variance continues at the same level each month. Calculate the extra wage cost over a year, and evaluate the action the owner should take. **[7]**

**8.** Evaluate the usefulness of variance analysis to the manager of a hotel and to the hotel's owners. **[8]**

**9.** The finance director of a supermarket chain says that all budgets should be cut by 5% each year. Evaluate the effect of this policy on the managers and the chain. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** A budget is a financial plan for a future period [1], expressed in money terms, setting out expected revenue and allowed spending [1]. **[2]**

**2.** A favourable variance is a difference between budget and actual that increases profit [1], such as revenue above budget or costs below budget [1]. An adverse variance reduces profit, for example revenue below budget or costs above budget [1]. **[3]**

**3.** Variance = £32,400 − £30,000 = **£2,400** [1]. It is **adverse**, because the cost is higher than budget [1]. Percentage = 2,400 ÷ 30,000 × 100 = **8%** [1]. **[3]**

**4.** **C** [1]. **[1]**

**5.** Managers know how their area works, so the budget is more realistic [1], and they are more committed to meeting a budget they helped to set [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** Budgeted profit = 80,000 − 30,000 − 8,000 − 20,000 = **£22,000** [1] [1]. Actual profit = 84,000 − 32,400 − 6,500 − 20,400 = **£24,700** [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Revenue: £4,000 favourable [1]. Wages: £2,400 adverse [1]. Marketing: £1,500 favourable [1]. Other costs: £400 adverse [1]. **[4]**

**(c)** Profit variance = £24,700 − £22,000 = **£2,700 favourable** [1]. Sum of item variances = +4,000 − 2,400 + 1,500 − 400 [1] = +£2,700 [1]. **[3]**

**(d)** Wages: £2,400 over budget, 8% [1]. Higher revenue may have needed more staff or overtime, so the cost may be linked to the extra sales [1]; or wage rates may have risen, or staffing was higher than planned [1]. The owner should check the cause, because a link to sales would be acceptable but poor control would not [1].
Marketing: £1,500 under budget, 18.75% [1]. This may be good if the same revenue was achieved at lower cost, but it may show that planned campaigns were cut, which could reduce future bookings [1]. **[6]**

**7.** Extra cost over a year = 12 × £2,400 = **£28,800** [1]. Evaluation: if the extra wages are caused by extra sales (revenue was £4,000 above budget), the cost may be justified because profit is still £2,700 above budget [1] [1]. If sales return to budget but wages stay high, profit would fall by £2,400 a month [1]. The owner should find out whether the rise comes from overtime, extra staff or higher pay rates [1], and set a flexible budget that rises with sales [1]. Judgement: no drastic action is needed now, because profit is above budget, but the owner should monitor the wages-to-revenue ratio and act if it keeps rising [1]. **[7]**

**8.** For the manager: variance analysis shows where results differ from the plan, such as room revenue or staff costs, so action can be taken at once [1]; it focuses attention on the largest variances [1]; it identifies good performance as well as problems [1].
For the owners: it shows whether managers are meeting targets and spending within limits [1]; it supports decisions about bonuses and investment [1].
Limits: it looks backwards, so the cause may already have gone [1]; an adverse variance may result from events the manager cannot control, such as energy prices [1]; the analysis is useful only if the budget was realistic [1].
Judgement: variance analysis is very useful for control if managers are expected to explain and act on the largest variances, not to be blamed for everything [1]. (Maximum 8 marks.) **[8]**

**9.** Effects on managers: they may be demotivated if cuts are unrealistic [1]; they may cut spending on training, maintenance or staff to meet the budget [1]; or they may build slack into budgets to protect themselves [1].
Effects on the chain: costs fall in the short term, which may raise profit [1]; but service and store appearance may suffer, which could reduce sales [1]; different stores face different circumstances, so an equal cut is unfair and ignores efficiency [1].
Judgement: an across-the-board cut is a poor policy, because it does not target waste, and the director should consider cutting where there is evidence of inefficiency [1], while keeping spending that supports sales [1]. **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a cost overspend favourable.
- Giving a variance without saying favourable or adverse.
- Stopping at the calculation without analysis.
- Forgetting to check that item variances add to the profit variance.
- Treating all favourable variances as good.
