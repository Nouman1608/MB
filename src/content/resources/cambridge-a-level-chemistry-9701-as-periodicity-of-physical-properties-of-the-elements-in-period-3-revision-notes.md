## The four properties in 9.1

You must describe, and indicate the periodicity in, **atomic radius**, **ionic radius**, **melting point** and **electrical conductivity**, and explain melting point and conductivity using **structure and bonding**.

## Atomic radius: one reason, three parts

Decreases from Na to Cl because:

1. nuclear charge increases;
2. electrons go into the **same** (third) shell;
3. shielding stays roughly **constant**.

The stronger attraction pulls the outer shell closer. Argon is not usually compared, because it forms no bonds.

## Ionic radius: two isoelectronic series

```
Na+  >  Mg2+  >  Al3+        10 electrons, 2 shells, nuclear charge 11 -> 13
      (big jump: anions have an extra occupied shell)
P3-  >  S2-   >  Cl-         18 electrons, 3 shells, nuclear charge 15 -> 17
```

- **Within** each series: same electrons, more protons → smaller ion.
- **Between** the series: anions have **one more shell**, so every anion is larger than every cation.
- Cations are smaller than their atoms (lost the third shell). Anions are larger than their atoms (extra electrons repel and the same nuclear charge holds more electrons).

## Melting point: name the structure every time

| Element(s) | Structure | Overcome on melting | Trend |
|---|---|---|---|
| Na → Al | Giant metallic | Metallic bonding | Increases (ion charge +1 → +3, smaller ions, 1 → 3 delocalised electrons per atom) |
| Si | Giant covalent | Many strong covalent bonds | **Maximum** |
| P₄, S₈, Cl₂ | Simple molecular | Weak id-id forces **between** molecules | Low |
| Ar | Monatomic | Weak id-id forces between atoms | Lowest |

Order among the low melting elements: **S₈ (128 electrons) > P₄ (60) > Cl₂ (34) > Ar (18)**. More electrons → stronger id-id forces.

## Electrical conductivity

- **Na < Mg < Al**: all good conductors; delocalised electrons per atom rise from 1 to 3.
- **Si**: semiconductor; conductivity **rises** with temperature.
- **P, S, Cl, Ar**: no mobile charge carriers; insulators.

## Periodicity check: Period 2 repeats the pattern

Li, Be (metallic) → B, C (giant covalent; C highest) → N₂, O₂, F₂ (simple molecular) → Ne (monatomic). Same shape of melting point curve as Period 3. Atomic radius falls across each period and jumps up at the start of the next.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Periodicity | A regular, repeating pattern of properties across successive periods |
| Atomic radius | Half the distance between the nuclei of two bonded atoms of the element |
| Isoelectronic | Having the same number of electrons (and the same electron configuration) |
| Shielding | Repulsion of outer electrons by inner-shell electrons, reducing the nuclear attraction they feel |
| Giant metallic lattice | Positive ions in a lattice held by attraction to delocalised electrons |
| Giant covalent lattice | Atoms joined by covalent bonds in a continuous three-dimensional network |
| Simple molecular | Small discrete molecules held to each other by weak intermolecular forces |
| Semiconductor | A solid whose electrical conductivity is low but increases with temperature |

## Exam traps

- "Bonds get weaker after silicon" instead of "the structure changes to simple molecular".
- "Weak covalent bonds" in phosphorus or sulfur. The weak forces are **between** molecules.
- One smooth ionic radius trend from Na⁺ to Cl⁻.
- Explaining the cation-to-anion jump with nuclear charge rather than the extra shell.
- Leaving silicon out of the conductivity description.
- Writing "van der Waals" vaguely when the question asks you to name the force: give **instantaneous dipole–induced dipole (id-id)**.
- Explaining metallic melting point with only one factor (charge) when two are available (charge and size, or charge and number of delocalised electrons).

## Self-test

1. Give three reasons together explaining why atomic radius decreases across Period 3.
2. Which two Period 3 ions are isoelectronic with neon?
3. Why is Al³⁺ smaller than Na⁺?
4. Why is Cl⁻ larger than Na⁺, although chlorine has more protons?
5. Which Period 3 element has the highest melting point, and why?
6. Explain why the melting point of aluminium is higher than that of sodium.
7. Explain why sulfur has a higher melting point than phosphorus.
8. Why does phosphorus not conduct electricity?
9. How does the conductivity of silicon change on heating, and how does this compare with aluminium?
10. Which element in Period 2 corresponds to silicon's position at the peak of the melting point trend?
11. What is broken, and what is not broken, when solid chlorine melts?

**Answers:**

1. Nuclear charge increases; electrons enter the same shell; shielding is roughly constant. So the outer electrons are attracted more strongly.
2. Any two of Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺ (each has 10 electrons).
3. Both have 10 electrons in two shells, but Al³⁺ has 13 protons and Na⁺ has 11, so the electrons in Al³⁺ are pulled closer.
4. Cl⁻ has three occupied shells, Na⁺ only two.
5. Silicon: giant covalent lattice, so many strong covalent bonds must be broken.
6. Al³⁺ has a higher charge and is smaller than Na⁺, and aluminium has three delocalised electrons per atom compared with one, so the metallic bonding is stronger.
7. S₈ molecules have more electrons (128) than P₄ molecules (60), so the id-id forces between S₈ molecules are stronger.
8. Its electrons are all held in P–P covalent bonds or as lone pairs within P₄ molecules; there are no delocalised electrons or ions free to move.
9. It increases, because more electrons gain enough energy to move. Aluminium, a metal, conducts slightly less well when heated.
10. Carbon.
11. The weak id-id forces between Cl₂ molecules are overcome. The Cl–Cl covalent bonds are not broken.
