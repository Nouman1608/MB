> **Original questions written for Marlbridge.** They are not past-paper questions and do not copy the structure or mark tariffs of the real examination. Use them alongside official past papers.

These questions cover sub-topic **20.3 Pollution** of the Cambridge IGCSE Biology 0610 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. Questions and parts marked **(Extended)** test Supplement content (the explanation of eutrophication); Core candidates can skip them. All data in the questions are invented for practice.

Revise first with the [pollution study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-pollution-cambridge-igcse-biology/) and the [pollution revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-pollution-cambridge-igcse-biology-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Which of these is a source of methane pollution?

A: burning coal in a power station

B: decomposition of waste in a landfill site

C: burning forests to clear land

D: exhaust gases from petrol cars

**[1]**

**2.** Untreated sewage enters a river. Why does the concentration of dissolved oxygen fall?

A: sewage contains a poison that destroys oxygen

B: algae in the sewage use up the oxygen in photosynthesis

C: bacteria decompose the sewage and use oxygen in aerobic respiration

D: fish breathe faster in polluted water

**[1]**

**3.** Describe what is meant by a non-biodegradable plastic. **[1]**

**4.** State **two** sources of carbon dioxide pollution of the air. **[2]**

**5.** Suggest why a sea turtle may die after swallowing a plastic bag. **[2]**

**6.** *(Extended)* During eutrophication, the respiration of one group of organisms increases and dissolved oxygen falls. Name this group of organisms. **[1]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** Students sampled a stream above and below a pipe that releases untreated sewage. They counted mayfly larvae, which need water with a high concentration of dissolved oxygen, and a species of worm that can survive in water containing little oxygen.

| Site | Dissolved oxygen / mg per dm³ | Mayfly larvae | Worms |
|---|---|---|---|
| 50 m above pipe | 9.2 | 64 | 3 |
| 50 m below pipe | 2.8 | 0 | 210 |
| 2 km below pipe | 7.5 | 31 | 22 |

**(a)** Describe how the numbers of mayfly larvae and worms change from above the pipe to 50 m below it. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why there are no mayfly larvae 50 m below the pipe. **[3]**

**(c)** Suggest why the number of worms is so high 50 m below the pipe. **[2]**

**8.** *(Extended)* A student set up two bottles of pond water in a sunny place. She added nitrate ions to bottle Y but not to bottle X. She measured dissolved oxygen each week.

| Week | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bottle X / mg per dm³ | 8.5 | 8.4 | 8.3 | 8.4 |
| Bottle Y / mg per dm³ | 8.5 | 9.6 | 4.1 | 1.8 |

**(a)** Suggest why dissolved oxygen in bottle Y rose in the first week. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why dissolved oxygen in bottle Y fell between week 1 and week 3. **[4]**

**(c)** State why bottle X was included. **[1]**

**9.** A park has many plastic bottles, bags and food wrappers left as litter.

**(a)** Describe the effects of this non-biodegradable plastic litter on the park ecosystem. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why the litter remains in the park for many years if it is not collected. **[1]**

**10.** Methane is released from a large landfill site.

**(a)** Explain how methane released from the landfill site contributes to climate change. **[3]**

**(b)** Describe **two** effects of climate change on ecosystems. **[2]**

**11.** *(Extended)* Untreated sewage leaks into a lake. Several weeks later, many dead fish are found. Explain how the sewage led to the death of the fish, using the process of eutrophication. **[6]**

**12.** Compare the effects of untreated sewage and excess fertiliser on a river ecosystem. Give **one** similarity and **one** difference, and explain each. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1].

*Examiner insight:* A, C and D are sources of carbon dioxide. Methane comes from livestock, rice fields and landfill.

**2.** **C** [1].

**3.** A plastic that cannot be broken down by decomposers [1].

**4.** Any two from: combustion of fossil fuels (in power stations, vehicles, factories); burning of forests [2].

**5.** The bag blocks / fills its gut [1], so it cannot eat or digest food and starves [1].

**6.** Decomposers / bacteria [1].

**7. (a)** Mayfly larvae fall from 64 to 0 [1]; worms rise from 3 to 210 [1].

**(b)** Bacteria decompose organic matter in the sewage [1]. They respire aerobically and use up dissolved oxygen, which falls from 9.2 to 2.8 mg per dm³ [1]. Mayfly larvae need a high concentration of dissolved oxygen, so they die or move away [1].

**(c)** The worms can survive with little oxygen [1]; they have plenty of food from the organic matter / have less competition or fewer predators because other animals have gone [1].

*Examiner insight:* In (b), quoting the oxygen figures strengthens the answer. In (c), the first mark is the worms' tolerance of low oxygen; the second needs a reason why their numbers rise rather than just stay the same.

**8. (a)** The nitrate caused rapid growth of algae / producers [1], which photosynthesised in the light and released oxygen [1].

**(b)** The algae / producers died [1]. Decomposers / bacteria increased and broke down the dead algae [1]. Their aerobic respiration [1] used dissolved oxygen faster than it was replaced, so it fell to 1.8 mg per dm³ [1].

**(c)** As a control, to show that the change in bottle Y was caused by the nitrate [1].

*Examiner insight:* Part (a) is a "suggest" question: living algae produce oxygen. This is exactly why the oxygen loss in (b) must be explained by decomposers, not by the algae.

**9. (a)** Any three from:

- animals eat the plastic, mistaking it for food, which blocks the gut [1]
- animals become trapped or entangled in bags or bottles [1]
- litter covers the ground / soil and makes the habitat less suitable for plants and animals [1]
- plastic persists for a long time, so the effects continue [1]

**(b)** Decomposers cannot break it down [1].

**10. (a)** Methane absorbs thermal energy given out by the Earth's surface [1], so less is lost to space; more methane increases this / causes an enhanced greenhouse effect [1], so the mean global temperature rises, causing climate change [1].

**(b)** Any two from: melting ice and rising sea levels flood coastal habitats; changing rainfall patterns cause droughts or floods; more extreme weather damages habitats; species are forced to move or their populations decline [2].

**11.** Six steps, one mark each:

- the sewage increases the availability of nitrate and other ions in the lake [1]
- this causes increased growth of producers / algae [1]
- when the producers die, there is increased decomposition [1]
- decomposers / bacteria carry out more aerobic respiration [1]
- this reduces the dissolved oxygen in the water [1]
- fish, which require dissolved oxygen, die [1]

*Examiner insight:* Accept the decomposition of the sewage itself as contributing to step 4, but the six-step chain from ions to fish death must be present for full marks.

**12.** Similarity: both add nitrate / mineral ions to the water [1], causing rapid growth of algae, whose death and decomposition reduces dissolved oxygen [1].

Difference: sewage also contains organic matter that bacteria decompose directly, whereas fertiliser does not [1], so dissolved oxygen falls immediately below a sewage outflow without waiting for algae to grow and die [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying algae use up oxygen.
- Missing the death of producers before decomposition.
- Giving a source of carbon dioxide when methane is asked for.
- Describing plastic effects without a consequence for the organism.
- Comparing pollutants by describing only one of them.
