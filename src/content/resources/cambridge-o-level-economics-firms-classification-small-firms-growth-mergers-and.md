## 3.5.1 Classification of firms

**By sector.** Sort a firm by its main activity.

- **Primary:** extracts or grows natural resources, such as a coffee farm, a fishing boat or a quarry.
- **Secondary:** makes or builds, such as a shirt factory or a construction company.
- **Tertiary:** provides services, such as a clinic, a bus company or a clothing shop.

One supply chain can cross all three: a cotton grower (primary), a shirt factory (secondary), a clothing shop (tertiary).

Illustrative table for Country A (shares of national output):

| Sector | Earlier year | Later year | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary | 18% | 6% | -12 points |
| Secondary | 32% | 24% | -8 points |
| Tertiary | 50% | 70% | +20 points |

Each column totals 100%, and the changes total zero. The tertiary share has risen because the other two have fallen. Do not say tertiary output has risen unless the table gives real output; shares can change while output grows.

**By ownership.** **Private sector** firms are owned by individuals or groups and normally aim at profit. **Public sector** firms are owned by the government. Profit may rank below other aims, such as widening access to a service, holding down prices or safeguarding jobs.

**By relative size.** Size depends on the measure: number of employees, turnover (sales revenue), output, capital employed or market share. Measures can disagree.

| | Employees | Turnover | Turnover per employee |
|---|---|---|---|
| Firm X (automated plant) | 40 | 50,000,000 | 1,250,000 |
| Firm Y (call centre) | 400 | 20,000,000 | 50,000 |

Y is larger by employees, X is larger by turnover. Say which measure you use before you call a firm "large".

## 3.5.2 Small firms

**Advantages**

- Close contact with customers, so personal and flexible service.
- Quick decisions, because few people are involved.
- The owner keeps the profit, which motivates effort.
- Low start-up costs and little to manage.
- Can serve a niche that large firms ignore.

**Disadvantages**

- Higher average costs, as the firm may not gain economies of scale.
- Weak bargaining power with suppliers.
- A lack of specialist staff: the owner does many jobs.
- Reliance on one product or one area leaves the firm exposed.

**Challenges**: raising finance, because lenders see small firms as risky and may charge more; competing on price with large rivals; recruiting and keeping skilled staff; surviving a drop in demand with few reserves.

**Reasons for existence**: the market is small or local; customers want personal or made-to-order goods; the industry offers few economies of scale; the owner prefers to stay small; the firm supplies parts or services to large firms.

**Worked example (illustrative).** A phone repair stall charges 25 per repair. Materials cost 9 per repair. Fixed costs are 1,800 a month.

```
Contribution per repair = 25 - 9 = 16
Break-even repairs      = 1,800 / 16 = 112.5, so 113 repairs
Profit at 150 repairs   = 150 x 16 - 1,800 = 600
Profit at 113 repairs   = 113 x 16 - 1,800 = 8
```

At 113 repairs the stall has almost no profit, so a quiet month could produce a loss. That is a challenge of small size: fixed costs must be covered from a small sales base.

## 3.5.3 Causes and forms of growth

**Causes**: higher profit, more market power, lower costs through economies of scale, spreading risk across more products or markets, survival against bigger rivals, and the ambitions of owners and managers.

**Internal (organic) growth** is expansion paid for and run by the firm itself: higher sales, extra branches, new products or new markets. **Increased market share** is the syllabus example. **External growth** joins firms together, for example a merger.

**Worked example (illustrative).** A firm sells 2.40 million units in a market of 20 million units. A year later it sells 2.64 million in a market of 24 million.

```
Share, year 1 = 2.40 / 20 x 100 = 12%
Share, year 2 = 2.64 / 24 x 100 = 11%
Sales growth  = (2.64 / 2.40 - 1) x 100 = 10%
Market growth = (24 / 20 - 1) x 100     = 20%
```

Sales grew, yet share fell by one percentage point, because the market grew twice as fast. Grow faster than the market to gain share.

| | Internal growth | External growth |
|---|---|---|
| Speed | Slow | Fast |
| Cost | Spread over time | A large sum up front |
| Risk | Firm knows its own business | Clashing cultures, job losses, overpaying |
| Control | Owners keep control | Control may be shared |

## 3.5.4 Mergers

A **merger** is when two firms agree to become one.

| Type | Who joins | Illustrative example |
|---|---|---|
| Horizontal | Same industry, same stage | Two bus operators |
| Vertical | Same industry, different stages | A furniture maker buys a timber yard (backward) or a chain of showrooms (forward) |
| Conglomerate | Different industries | A shipping group buys a hotel group |

**Horizontal.** Advantages: economies of scale, bigger market share, fewer rivals, shared costs. Disadvantages: less competition so possible higher prices for consumers, job losses from duplicated roles, and difficulty combining two management teams.

*Worked example (illustrative).* Five bus operators share a city market: A 22%, B 14%, C 30%, D 20%, E 14%. A and B merge.

```
Merged share = 22 + 14 = 36%
Two biggest firms before = 30 + 22 = 52%
Two biggest firms after  = 36 + 30 = 66%
```

The merged firm becomes the largest in the market and the market becomes more concentrated.

**Vertical.** Backward means towards suppliers; forward means towards customers. Advantages: secure supplies or outlets, control of quality, and capturing the supplier's or retailer's profit. Disadvantages: managers may lack experience of the other stage, rivals may stop dealing with the firm, and an inefficient supplier may be kept going.

*Worked example (illustrative).* A furniture maker buys 800 cubic metres of timber a year at 120 each. A timber yard costs 95 per cubic metre to run.

```
Cost when buying = 800 x 120 = 96,000
Cost when owning = 800 x 95  = 76,000
Saving           = 20,000 (800 x the 25 margin)
```

This ignores the price of buying the yard, so the saving must be set against it.

**Conglomerate.** Advantage: risk is spread over unrelated activities. Disadvantages: managers lack knowledge of the new industry and there are few cost savings from scale.

*Worked example (illustrative).* A shipping group (profit 6.0m in a normal year, a loss of 2.0m in a trade slump) buys a hotel group earning 4.0m in both years.

```
Group profit, normal year = 6.0 + 4.0  = 10.0m
Group profit, slump       = -2.0 + 4.0 =  2.0m
```

Before the merger the shipping firm made a loss in the slump. Afterwards the group stays profitable.

## 3.5.5 Scale: economies and diseconomies

Average cost is total cost divided by output. **Economies of scale** exist where that figure falls as the firm produces more. **Diseconomies of scale** exist where it climbs as the firm produces more.

| Output | Total cost | Average cost |
|---|---|---|
| 10,000 | 60,000 | 6.00 |
| 20,000 | 100,000 | 5.00 |
| 30,000 | 135,000 | 4.50 |
| 40,000 | 176,000 | 4.40 |
| 50,000 | 235,000 | 4.70 |
| 60,000 | 318,000 | 5.30 |

From 10,000 to 40,000 units, average cost falls from 6.00 to 4.40, a fall of 26.7%. These are economies of scale. After 40,000 it rises, and by 60,000 it is 20.5% above the minimum. These are diseconomies.

**Internal economies** come from the firm's own growth:

- **Technical:** large machines and mass production cut unit cost.
- **Purchasing:** bulk orders win discounts. Buying 50,000 units at 7.20 instead of 8.00 is a 10% cut, saving 40,000.
- **Financial:** big firms borrow more cheaply.
- **Managerial:** specialist managers raise efficiency.
- **Marketing:** advertising is spread over more units.
- **Risk-bearing:** more products and markets reduce dependence on one.

**Internal diseconomies:** slow communication, weak coordination, low morale in large workforces, and managers who lose touch with what is happening.

**External economies** come from the growth of the whole industry, usually in one area: a pool of skilled workers, specialist suppliers nearby, shared transport and training. Every firm there gains, large or small. **External diseconomies** appear when too many firms crowd together: congestion, scarce workers and higher rents. If workshop rent rises from 800 to 1,100 a month, that is a 37.5% increase for every firm there.

**Worked example (illustrative).** Printer K makes 18,000 jobs at a total cost of 99,000. Printer L makes 30,000 jobs at 135,000. They merge and save 11,000 on administration and 7,000 on bulk paper.

```
K: 99,000 / 18,000 = 5.50 per job
L: 135,000 / 30,000 = 4.50 per job
Combined before: 234,000 / 48,000 = 4.875
Combined after:  (234,000 - 11,000 - 7,000) / 48,000 = 216,000 / 48,000 = 4.50
Fall = 0.375 per job, or 7.7%
```

Link back to 3.5.4: horizontal mergers aim at economies of scale, but the saving is not automatic. A bigger firm that is badly managed can meet diseconomies.

## Evaluating a merger

A strong answer weighs both sides.

1. State the type and the likely benefit, for example lower unit costs.
2. Say who gains: owners through profit, consumers only if lower costs pass into lower prices.
3. Say who may lose: workers if jobs are cut, consumers if competition falls.
4. Judge it: the outcome depends on how many rivals remain, how easily new firms can enter, and whether the cost savings are large.

## Common errors

- Calling a firm "small" without a measure of size.
- Mixing up forward and backward vertical mergers.
- Calling any merger of two firms in the same industry vertical; horizontal firms are at the same stage.
- Writing that economies of scale mean "more profit". They mean lower average cost.
- Treating a rise in sales as a rise in market share.
- Placing external economies inside the firm.
