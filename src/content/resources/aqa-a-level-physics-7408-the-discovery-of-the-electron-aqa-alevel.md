## 3.12.1.1 Cathode rays

A **discharge tube** is a glass tube containing a gas at **low pressure**, with a metal electrode at each end connected to a high p.d. (several kilovolts).

How the rays are produced, step by step:

1. A few gas atoms are always ionised (for example by background radiation). The high p.d. accelerates the positive ions towards the **cathode** (negative electrode).
2. The ions strike the cathode and knock electrons out of its surface.
3. These electrons are accelerated away from the cathode towards the **anode**. At low pressure they travel far enough between collisions to gain enough kinetic energy to ionise more atoms, producing more ions and electrons, so the discharge sustains itself.
4. Electrons also **excite** gas atoms by collision. When the atoms de-excite, they emit photons of particular wavelengths, so the gas **glows** with a colour characteristic of the gas.

The streams leaving the cathode were called **cathode rays**. Experiments in the late nineteenth century showed they travel in straight lines (casting sharp shadows of objects in their path), carry negative charge, and are deflected by electric and magnetic fields as negative particles would be. They are beams of electrons.

## 3.12.1.2 Thermionic emission

Heating a metal filament gives some of its conduction electrons enough kinetic energy to escape from the surface: this is **thermionic emission**. In an **electron gun**, the heated filament (cathode) sits in an evacuated tube near an anode with a hole in it. The anode p.d. V accelerates the electrons; those passing through the hole form a narrow beam. The tube must be evacuated so that electrons are not scattered or slowed by gas molecules.

The work done on each electron by the field equals its gain in kinetic energy. Electrons leave the filament with negligible speed, so

**½mv² = eV**

**Worked example 1.** Find the speed of electrons from a 1200 V electron gun (e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, mₑ = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg).

```
v = √(2eV/m) = √(2 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 1200/9.11 × 10⁻³¹) = 2.05 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹
```

This is about 7% of the speed of light, so the non-relativistic equation is a good approximation.

## 3.12.1.3 Specific charge of the electron

The **specific charge** is charge/mass, e/mₑ, in C kg⁻¹. The specification requires **any one** method. Here is a method based on crossed fields, close in principle to the one Thomson used.

**Step 1: find the speed with balanced fields.** The beam passes between parallel plates (separation d, p.d. V_p), giving an electric field E = V_p/d. A magnetic field B is applied at right angles to both E and the beam, and adjusted until the beam is **undeflected**. The electric force eE then balances the magnetic force Bev:

```
eE = Bev   →   v = E/B
```

**Step 2: find e/m.** Either:

- switch off the electric field: the magnetic force provides the centripetal force, Bev = mv²/r, so **e/m = v/(Br)**; or
- use the anode p.d. V_a: ½mv² = eV_a, so **e/m = v²/(2V_a)**.

**Worked example 2.** Plates 40 mm apart have a p.d. of 800 V. The beam is undeflected when B = 1.0 mT. With the electric field off, the beam follows an arc of radius 0.114 m.

```
E = 800/0.040 = 2.0 × 10⁴ V m⁻¹
v = E/B = 2.0 × 10⁴/1.0 × 10⁻³ = 2.0 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹
e/m = v/(Br) = 2.0 × 10⁷/(1.0 × 10⁻³ × 0.114) = 1.75 × 10¹¹ C kg⁻¹
```

If the anode p.d. was 1140 V, the second route gives e/m = (2.0 × 10⁷)²/(2 × 1140) = 1.75 × 10¹¹ C kg⁻¹, agreeing with the first.

Other acceptable methods include the **fine-beam tube**, where a beam from an electron gun is bent into a circle by a uniform magnetic field (e/m = 2V/(B²r²)), and measuring the deflection of a beam by an electric field between plates.

### Significance of Thomson's determination

- The value of e/m was the **same whatever gas** was in the tube and whatever metal the cathode was made of, so the particles are a **constituent of all matter**.
- e/m for the electron was about **1800 times** the largest specific charge then known, that of the hydrogen ion (the proton).

**Worked example 3.** Compare with the hydrogen ion (charge 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, mass 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg).

```
e/m(H⁺) = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹/1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ = 9.58 × 10⁷ C kg⁻¹
ratio = 1.76 × 10¹¹/9.58 × 10⁷ = 1840
```

If the electron's charge is the same size as the hydrogen ion's, its mass must be about 1/1840 of the mass of the lightest atom. The electron is a **sub-atomic particle**, so atoms are not indivisible: a turning point in understanding the structure of matter.

## 3.12.1.4 Millikan's oil drop experiment

Fine oil drops are sprayed between two horizontal metal plates, a distance d apart. Some drops pick up charge as they are formed (or from ionising radiation). The drops are watched through a microscope and timed between scale marks.

**Field off: find the radius and mass.** A drop quickly reaches terminal speed v₁ when its weight equals the viscous drag given by **Stokes' law**, F = 6πηrv (ignoring upthrust):

```
(4/3)πr³ρg = 6πηrv₁   →   r = √(9ηv₁/(2ρg))
```

From r and the oil density ρ, the mass m = (4/3)πr³ρ follows.

**Field on, drop held stationary:** the electric force balances the weight: **QV/d = mg**, so Q = mgd/V.

**Field on, drop rising at terminal speed v₂:** QV/d = mg + 6πηrv₂, because the drag now acts downwards.

**Worked example 4.** d = 8.0 mm, ρ = 920 kg m⁻³, η = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ N s m⁻². Field off, the drop falls at 8.0 × 10⁻⁵ m s⁻¹. With 500 V across the plates it rises at 2.4 × 10⁻⁵ m s⁻¹.

```
r = √(9 × 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ × 8.0 × 10⁻⁵/(2 × 920 × 9.81)) = 8.47 × 10⁻⁷ m
mg = (4/3)π(8.47 × 10⁻⁷)³ × 920 × 9.81 = 2.30 × 10⁻¹⁴ N
drag while rising = 6πηrv₂ = 6.90 × 10⁻¹⁵ N
Q = (2.30 × 10⁻¹⁴ + 6.90 × 10⁻¹⁵) × 8.0 × 10⁻³/500 = 4.78 × 10⁻¹⁹ C ≈ 3e
```

The p.d. needed to hold this drop still would be mgd/Q = 383 V.

### Significance of Millikan's results

- Every charge measured was a **whole-number multiple** of about 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. Charge is **quantised**: it comes in discrete units of e, the charge of the electron.
- Combined with Thomson's e/m, the value of e gave the **mass of the electron**, about 9.1 × 10⁻³¹ kg.

## Common mistakes

- Saying the cathode rays are produced by heating in a discharge tube: that is thermionic emission, a different process.
- Finding the radius from the field-on equation: r always comes from the field-off terminal speed.
- Forgetting that drag reverses direction when the drop rises.
- Using v = E/B for any beam: it applies only when the fields are balanced.
- Giving the significance of e/m without the comparison with the hydrogen ion.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** an e/m method: name the apparatus, the measurements and how e/m is calculated from them.
- **Explain** the glow in a discharge tube with excitation and de-excitation, not ionisation alone.
- **Derive** r = √(9ηv/(2ρg)) by equating weight and Stokes' drag.
- **State the significance** as a conclusion about matter, not a repeat of the method.
