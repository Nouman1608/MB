Breadth Study B3, "Japan in transformation, 1853–1945", is one of eight breadth studies in change in
Section B of Pearson Edexcel International GCSE History (4HI1) Paper 2. It follows Japan from the
arrival of the American Commodore Perry in 1853, through the fall of the Tokugawa shogunate and the
modernising Meiji era, to empire, the democratic experiment of the Taisho years, the rise of militarism
in the 1930s and defeat in 1945. Questions test change, continuity, causes and significance across long
periods.

## Syllabus coverage

The specification (Issue 5, February 2026) names five **themes**: external influences on Japan; military
expansionism from seclusion to world power and defeat; the modernisation of the economy and the problems
of industrialisation; social transformation; and political change. The (c) essay normally draws on two or
more key topics. The five **key topics** are:

1. **Japan opening up to China and the West, 1853–67** — the Perry Mission, the opening of the ports and
   its effects; causes of the fall of the Tokugawa, including corruption and incompetence and the roles of
   Choshu and Satsuma.
2. **Transformation, 1867–1895** — the economic and social impact of the Tokugawa's fall, the Meiji
   Constitution, the "cultural revolution" and the break-up of the Restoration coalition (1873), and Meiji
   culture: civilisation, enlightenment and Christianity.
3. **An emerging power, 1895–1919** — the Meiji industrial and commercial revolution, Emperor Taisho and
   Taisho Democracy (1912), changes in Taisho culture for youth, women, village and city, and relations with
   China and the West: the Sino-Japanese War (1895), the Boxer Rebellion (1900), the Anglo-Japanese Alliance
   (1902), the Russo-Japanese War (1904–05) and the Treaty of Versailles (1919).
4. **Political and economic challenges, 1919–31** — the struggle over universal suffrage, the labour
   movement, the Public Security Preservation Law (1925), the Great Kanto Earthquake (1923) and the world
   Depression from 1929.
5. **Depression, empire and collapse, 1931–45** — Tenko, the return to military rule, strained relations with
   the USA, the Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere, Manchukuo and the League of Nations, Konoe's New
   Order and the war of 1937–45, and Hiroshima, Nagasaki and the surrender.

## How was Japan opened to the West, 1853–67?

For over two centuries the Tokugawa shoguns had kept Japan largely closed to foreigners. In 1853 US
Commodore **Matthew Perry** arrived with warships demanding trade; the Treaty of Kanagawa (1854) opened two
ports, and the Harris Treaty (1858) and similar agreements with European powers opened more, on **unequal
terms** — low tariffs and foreigners tried in their own courts. Western goods and ideas flowed in, prices
rose and many samurai felt humiliated. The shogunate looked weak and **incompetent**, and critics rallied to
the slogan "revere the emperor, expel the barbarians". The southwestern domains of **Choshu** and **Satsuma**,
after clashes with Western navies showed them the power of modern weapons, allied in 1866. In late 1867 the
last shogun gave up power, and in January 1868 imperial rule was "restored" under the young **Emperor Meiji**.

## How did Meiji Japan transform itself, 1867–95?

The new leaders, mainly samurai from Satsuma and Choshu, aimed to build a "rich country, strong army".
Feudal domains were replaced by prefectures (1871); the samurai lost their privileges and stipends;
**conscription** (1873) created a national army; a new land tax funded the state. The Iwakura Mission
(1871–73) studied Western countries.

In 1873 the **Restoration coalition broke up** over whether to invade Korea; Saigo Takamori left the
government and later led the failed **Satsuma Rebellion** (1877). The **Meiji Constitution** (1889), modelled
partly on Prussia, created an elected Diet but kept sovereignty with the emperor, and the army answered to
him rather than to parliament.

Culturally, the slogan "**civilisation and enlightenment**" encouraged Western clothes, science, railways,
newspapers and education; the writer Fukuzawa Yukichi promoted Western learning. The ban on **Christianity**
was lifted in 1873, though converts remained few. This rapid change is sometimes called a "cultural
revolution".

## How did Japan become a world power, 1895–1919?

Industrial growth was led by the state and by huge family businesses (**zaibatsu**) such as Mitsui and
Mitsubishi; textiles, shipbuilding, railways and, from 1901, the Yawata steelworks grew quickly, but
workers faced long hours and low pay. Victory over China in the **Sino-Japanese War** (1894–95) brought
Taiwan. Japan sent troops to help crush the **Boxer Rebellion** (1900), signed the **Anglo-Japanese Alliance**
(1902) — the first alliance between a Western power and an Asian state on equal terms — and defeated Russia
in the **Russo-Japanese War** (1904–05), gaining influence in Korea, which it annexed in 1910, and in southern
Manchuria. In the First World War Japan seized German possessions in China and the Pacific; at
**Versailles** (1919) it kept them, but its proposal to recognise racial equality was rejected.

Emperor Meiji died in 1912. Under **Emperor Taisho** (1912–26), power shifted from the elder statesmen to
political parties — "**Taisho Democracy**". In 1918 Hara Takashi became the first prime minister who was a
commoner leading a party government. **Taisho culture** brought cafés, cinema, department stores and the
"modern girl" in the cities, new women's magazines and movements, and student activism among Taisho
youth, while villages remained poorer and more traditional.

## What challenges did Japan face, 1919–31?

The campaign for **universal male suffrage** succeeded in 1925, but the same year the **Public Security
Preservation Law** (Peace Preservation Law) allowed the arrest of anyone seeking to change the political
system or abolish private property — a weapon against communists, socialists and the growing **labour
movement**. The **Great Kanto Earthquake** (1923) destroyed much of Tokyo and Yokohama, killing over 100,000
people, and in its aftermath mobs murdered many Koreans. The 1920s brought recession and bank failures, and
the **world Depression** from 1929 devastated silk exports and farm incomes, discrediting party politicians
linked to big business.

## How did militarism lead to collapse, 1931–45?

In 1931 the army in Manchuria staged the **Mukden Incident** and conquered the region, creating the puppet
state of **Manchukuo** (1932). When the League of Nations condemned Japan, it left the League (1933). Prime
Minister Inukai was assassinated by naval officers in 1932, ending party government, and a failed army coup
in February 1936 increased military influence. **Tenko** — the public renunciation of communism by
imprisoned leftists, beginning in 1933 — showed the pressure to conform. Under Prince **Konoe**, full-scale
war with China began in 1937, and Konoe proclaimed a "**New Order**" in East Asia; the National Mobilisation
Law (1938) put the economy on a war footing. Japan's plans for a **Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere**
brought it into conflict with the USA, which imposed an oil embargo in 1941. Japan attacked **Pearl Harbor**
in December 1941 and conquered much of Southeast Asia, where occupied peoples suffered forced labour and
brutality. After the atomic bombs on **Hiroshima** (6 August 1945) and **Nagasaki** (9 August) and the Soviet
declaration of war, the **Showa Emperor** (Hirohito) broadcast Japan's surrender on 15 August 1945.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why did the Tokugawa fall?**
Humiliation by the West, weak and divided government, and the rise of
Choshu and Satsuma.

**Was Taisho Democracy real?**
Partly: party cabinets and male suffrage arrived, but the Peace
Preservation Law and the independence of the army limited it.

**Which theme suits a (c) essay?**
Military expansion, from the 1895 war to 1945, is a common long-span
theme.
