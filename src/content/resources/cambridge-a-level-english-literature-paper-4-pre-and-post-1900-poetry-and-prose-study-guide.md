Paper 4 asks you to write about poetry and prose from two eras: texts written before 1900 in one section and texts written after 1900 in the other. Because the set texts change between examination series and the syllabus text list is the authority on which authors you study, this guide teaches the skills and structure that apply to any text rather than naming a fixed list. The paper rewards close reading, sound argument and an awareness of how a text belongs to its time.

## Where this fits in the specification

Cambridge International A Level English Literature (9695) is built from four papers. Papers 1 and 2 form the AS level, and Papers 3 and 4 complete the full A Level. Paper 4 is the one in which you show that you can handle two literary periods and two genres. Section A covers pre-1900 poetry and prose; Section B covers post-1900 poetry and prose. Check your own syllabus for the question format, the number of questions you must answer and how marks are distributed, as these details are best taken straight from the current document and past papers.

Across the paper, the examiners look for the same four broad qualities: secure knowledge of what happens in the text and what it means; analysis of the writer's choices of language, form and structure; awareness of context, meaning the ways a text connects with the period and culture in which it was written and read; and an organised personal argument written in controlled English.

## Pre-1900 and post-1900: what changes

Do not treat the two sections as two different subjects. The skills are shared. What changes is the kind of context and the conventions you meet.

- **Pre-1900 writing** often uses fixed forms, formal diction and a more public voice. In poetry you may meet sonnets, odes and ballads, regular metre and rhyme. In prose you may meet an intrusive narrator, long sentences, explicit moral commentary and plots shaped by social class, marriage, property or religion.
- **Post-1900 writing** often experiments: free verse, fragmented structure, shifting narrative viewpoint, interior thought and ambiguity. Contexts may include war, empire and its end, migration, changing gender roles or modern psychology.

A caution: do not turn context into a history lecture. A fact about the period is worth including only if it helps explain a choice the writer made.

## Reading poetry

Use a repeatable sequence on any poem.

1. **Gist.** Who is speaking, to whom, about what, and what changes by the end?
2. **Form and structure.** Stanza pattern, rhyme, metre, punctuation, enjambment, the turn or shift in thought.
3. **Language.** Imagery, diction, sound, tone, and any pattern of words that repeats.
4. **Effect.** What does the reader feel or understand as a result of these choices?

Always link technique to meaning. Saying a poem uses alliteration earns little; saying that a cluster of hard consonants makes a line sound clipped and angry, in a way that matches the speaker's frustration, earns credit.

### Worked example: a pre-1900 poem

Take the opening of Shakespeare's Sonnet 18: "Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?" The question form creates a speaker who appears to weigh up a comparison in the very act of speaking. A strong paragraph might run: the opening question invites the reader into the speaker's thinking, but the poem soon argues that the beloved is "more lovely and more temperate", turning the traditional compliment into a claim that the person surpasses nature. The sonnet's tightly controlled fourteen-line form is itself part of the argument, since the closing couplet claims that the poem will make the subject last. Notice the structure of this analysis: a short quotation, a technique named, an effect on meaning, and a link to the poem's overall design.

## Reading prose

For prose, ask different questions.

1. **Narrative voice.** Who tells the story, how reliable are they, and how close are we to the characters' thoughts?
2. **Structure.** How is time handled, and how do the opening, key turning points and ending shape what we think?
3. **Character and relationships.** How are people revealed through speech, action and the narrator's comment?
4. **Language and tone.** Irony, description, dialogue, sentence length and register.
5. **Themes and context.** What does the text suggest about the society it shows?

### Worked example: a pre-1900 novel opening

Jane Austen opens Pride and Prejudice with the sentence "It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife." The grand, general phrasing sounds like a proverb, but the ironic gap between "truth universally acknowledged" and a rather narrow social assumption signals that the narrator is gently mocking that assumption. A paragraph on this would comment on the irony, the voice that sets a social context of marriage and money, and how that voice shapes our expectations of the plot.

### Worked example: a post-1900 poem

Wilfred Owen's "Anthem for Doomed Youth" begins with the question "What passing-bells for these who die as cattle?" Here the abrupt, bitter question and the comparison of soldiers to "cattle" contrast with the ceremonial image of church bells, so the poem opens by exposing the lack of dignity in mass death in war. Analysis would then move to how the poem's sonnet form, associated with love and tradition, is turned to a very different purpose, which says something about the writer's attitude to the war and to the language of honour.

## Responding to question types

Questions in this paper may ask you either to write an essay on the text as a whole or to respond closely to a printed passage or poem. Check your syllabus for the exact choice, but prepare for both.

**Passage-based questions** require close reading of the given extract. Begin with a short sentence stating the extract's place in the text and your main claim. Then move through the passage in a purposeful order, keep your quotation short and embed it in your sentence, and end by connecting the extract to the wider text.

**Essay questions** require an argument about the whole text. Before you write, read the question for its key words, decide your thesis in one sentence and choose three or four moments from across the text that support it. Do not retell the plot. Consider counter-arguments, since a response that recognises more than one interpretation shows maturity.

## Planning and timing

Allocate time in proportion to marks. Spend the first five or so minutes of each answer planning: underline the key words, jot a thesis, list the evidence in the order you will use it, and note the context you will weave in. Leave time at the end for a quick check of expression and for a short conclusion that answers the question directly. A conclusion should add a final judgement rather than repeat your introduction.

## Common mistakes

- Retelling the story instead of analysing the writer's methods.
- Feature spotting: naming techniques without explaining effect.
- Attaching context as a separate paragraph, rather than weaving it in where it clarifies meaning.
- Quoting long sections rather than selecting short, precise evidence.
- Using the same approach to a pre-1900 and a post-1900 text, ignoring differences in form and convention.
- Writing about the poem as if the speaker were the poet, without considering the persona.

## Exam technique and command words

Pay attention to the wording of the question. "How does the writer present..." asks about methods and effects; "To what extent..." invites a balanced argument and a judgement; "Explore" invites a range of ideas supported by evidence. Include the question's key terms in your opening sentence and again in your conclusion so that the examiner can see you are answering what was asked.

## Check your understanding

1. Name two features of structure to comment on in a poem. (For example stanza pattern and the turn in thought, or enjambment and rhyme.)
2. Why is it weak to write "the writer uses alliteration" and stop there? (It names a technique without explaining its effect on meaning.)
3. What is a persona and why does it matter when writing about poetry? (The voice created by the writer; you should not assume it is the writer's own view.)
4. Give two questions you should ask about narrative voice in prose. (For example who tells the story and how reliable they are, and how close we are to characters' thoughts.)
5. How should context be used in an essay? (Woven in to explain a choice or effect, not added as a separate history section.)
6. What is the difference in approach between a passage question and an essay question? (A passage question needs close reading of the extract; an essay needs an argument drawn from the whole text.)
7. What should a conclusion do? (Answer the question directly with a final judgement without repeating the introduction.)
8. Give one way post-1900 texts may differ in form from pre-1900 texts. (For example free verse, fragmentation or shifting viewpoint rather than fixed forms and an intrusive narrator.)
