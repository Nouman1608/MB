This guide covers section **3.2.2 Ethical and environmental considerations** of OxfordAQA International GCSE Business (9225), part of Topic 2, Influences on business. The specification asks you to identify and analyse possible **trade-offs between ethics and profit**; to understand that ethical behaviour means acting in ways stakeholders consider **fair and honest**, including **religious observance and charitable giving**; to know examples of ethical behaviour and its benefits and drawbacks; to understand society's expectation of greater **environmental responsibility** (traffic congestion, recycling, disposing of waste, noise and air pollution) and its costs and benefits; and to analyse trade-offs between **sustainability** (global warming, using scarce resources) and profit. All businesses and figures are invented.

For the whole topic, see the [Influences on Business study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-business-influences-on-business/). Condensed notes are in the [Ethical and Environmental Considerations revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-ethical-and-environmental-considerations-oxfordaqa-igcse-business-revision-notes/) and questions in the [Ethical and Environmental Considerations practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-ethical-and-environmental-considerations-oxfordaqa-igcse-business-practice/).

## Ethics: fair and honest to whom?

Ethics is about doing what is morally right, beyond what the law requires. The specification defines it through stakeholders: a business behaves ethically when stakeholders see its actions as fair and honest. So the best way to analyse an ethical issue is to ask which stakeholder is affected.

| Stakeholder | Fair and honest behaviour looks like... | Unethical behaviour looks like... |
|---|---|---|
| Employees | Fair pay, safe conditions, respect for religious and family needs | Pressure to work unpaid hours, unfair treatment |
| Customers | Truthful advertising and labelling, fair prices, honest refunds | Hidden charges, misleading claims |
| Suppliers | Paying a fair price, paying on time | Delaying payment to keep cash, forcing prices below the supplier's costs |
| Local community | Supporting local causes, limiting disruption | Ignoring complaints, harming the local area |
| Owners | Accurate reporting of results | Hiding problems from investors |

**Religious observance** means making it possible for employees and customers to follow their faith: allowing prayer breaks or providing a prayer room, adjusting shifts during religious festivals or fasting periods, closing on holy days, and sourcing food that meets religious requirements. These actions show respect and help a business keep staff and win loyal customers, but they can cost money, for example in rearranged rotas or lost trading hours.

**Charitable giving** includes donating money or products, sponsoring local events and giving staff paid time to volunteer. It builds goodwill in the community, but every dollar donated is a dollar less profit for the owners.

## The trade-off between ethics and profit

A **trade-off** means gaining one thing by giving up some of another. Ethical behaviour often costs money now, while its benefits come later and are less certain.

| Benefits of ethical behaviour | Drawbacks of ethical behaviour |
|---|---|
| Better reputation and customer loyalty | Higher costs, such as fairer prices to suppliers |
| Can justify a higher price | Lower profit margins if prices cannot rise |
| Easier to recruit and keep staff | Rivals who do not act ethically may undercut on price |
| Less risk of boycotts or bad publicity | Benefits are hard to measure and may take years |

**Worked example 1.** Tamarind Coffee Roasters buys 50,000 kg of coffee beans a year at $4.00 a kg. It is considering paying growers $4.60 a kg so they earn a fair living. It sells 200,000 bags a year and makes a profit of $90,000.

```
Extra cost per kg   = $4.60 - $4.00 = $0.60
Extra cost per year = 50,000 x $0.60 = $30,000
New profit (if nothing else changes) = $90,000 - $30,000 = $60,000
Price rise per bag to cover the cost = $30,000 / 200,000 = $0.15
```

Profit falls by a third unless Tamarind can raise its price. Whether customers will pay 15 cents more per bag depends on how much they value ethical sourcing and how close rival prices are. A strong answer weighs the certain cost against the uncertain gain in loyalty and reputation.

## Environmental considerations

Customers, local communities and governments increasingly expect businesses to take responsibility for their effect on the environment. The specification names four areas.

- **Traffic congestion:** delivery lorries and customer car journeys crowd local roads. A business can schedule deliveries outside rush hours, combine deliveries into fewer journeys, or choose a location near main roads or public transport.
- **Recycling:** reusing materials such as cardboard, plastic and metal reduces waste and can lower costs, but sorting and collecting it takes staff time and space.
- **Disposing of waste:** waste sent to landfill costs money and can harm land and water; hazardous waste needs safe handling. Reducing packaging cuts both cost and waste.
- **Noise and air pollution:** machinery, vehicles and late-night working disturb neighbours; fumes affect health. Quieter equipment, cleaner vehicles and limited working hours reduce complaints but cost money.

**Costs and benefits of environmental responsibility.** Costs include new equipment, staff time, higher-priced materials and changed working practices. Benefits include lower waste and energy bills, an improved reputation, customers who choose green businesses, and fewer complaints from the community.

## Sustainability

Sustainability means meeting today's needs without damaging the ability of future generations to meet theirs. The specification focuses on two issues.

- **Global warming:** burning fossil fuels for energy and transport releases greenhouse gases. Businesses can reduce this by using renewable energy, more efficient machinery and lower-emission vehicles.
- **Using scarce resources:** materials such as water, timber and some metals are limited. Businesses can use less, switch to renewable or recycled materials, and design products that last longer or can be repaired.

**Worked example 2: a sustainability trade-off.** Larkspur Laundry spends $11,000 a year on gas to heat water. Solar water heating would cost $24,000 to install and cut the gas bill by $4,000 a year.

```
Payback period = cost of investment / annual saving
               = $24,000 / $4,000 = 6 years
```

In the short term, profit falls, because $24,000 is spent before any saving arrives. Over the long term, the system pays for itself after 6 years and then saves $4,000 a year, as well as cutting emissions. For a business that is short of cash, or unsure it will still occupy the building in six years, the trade-off may not be worth it; for a stable business, it probably is.

## Common mistakes

- Treating ethical behaviour as free: the specification asks you to identify a trade-off with profit.
- Confusing ethics with the law: ethics is about choices beyond what the law requires.
- Listing environmental issues without saying how they affect the business's costs, revenue or reputation.
- Assuming customers will always pay more for ethical or green products.
- Forgetting short term versus long term when judging a sustainability decision.

## Exam technique for this section

**Explain** questions want a point and its effect on the named business: "Recycling cardboard reduces waste collection charges, which lowers costs." **Analyse** questions want a chain that includes the trade-off: the action, its cost, its benefit, and what happens to profit. **Evaluate** questions want both sides and a judgement that depends on something in the case, such as how price-sensitive customers are, how large the cost is compared with profit, or how long the benefit takes to arrive.
