This guide covers **3.1.2.1 Relative atomic mass and relative
molecular mass** and **3.1.2.2 The mole and the Avogadro constant**,
from AQA A-level Chemistry (7405), for teaching from September 2015.
These two sub-topics open 3.1.2 Amount of substance. They are part of
the AS content, so the same material is examined in AQA AS Chemistry
(7404) and again in the A-level (7405) papers.

## Scope of this guide

3.1.2 Amount of substance has five sub-topics. This guide covers the
first two:

- **3.1.2.1** Relative atomic mass and relative molecular mass (covered here)
- **3.1.2.2** The mole and the Avogadro constant (covered here)
- 3.1.2.3 The ideal gas equation (not covered here)
- 3.1.2.4 Empirical and molecular formula (not covered here)
- 3.1.2.5 Balanced equations and associated calculations, including
  percentage yield, atom economy and titrations (not covered here)

For 3.1.2.3 and 3.1.2.4, use the
[ideal gas equation and empirical formula study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-ideal-gas-equation-and-empirical-formula/).
AQA does not use a molar gas volume in this topic: gas calculations
use pV = nRT with SI units, which is sub-topic 3.1.2.3.

## What the specification requires

The wording below is Marlbridge's summary of the specification's
**content** column. Check the exact wording in the official
specification linked at the end of this page.

**3.1.2.1 Relative atomic mass and relative molecular mass**

- Relative atomic mass and relative molecular mass are defined in
  terms of carbon-12 (¹²C).
- The term relative formula mass is used for ionic compounds.
- You should be able to define relative atomic mass (Aᵣ) and relative
  molecular mass (Mᵣ).

**3.1.2.2 The mole and the Avogadro constant**

- The Avogadro constant is the number of particles in a mole.
- The mole applies to electrons, atoms, molecules, ions, formulas and
  equations.
- The concentration of a substance in solution is measured in
  mol dm⁻³.
- You should be able to carry out calculations using the Avogadro
  constant; using mass of substance, Mᵣ and amount in moles; and using
  concentration, volume and amount of substance in a solution.
- You are not expected to recall the value of the Avogadro constant.

**Mathematical skills linked to 3.1.2.2.** The specification lists
these separately, in its "Opportunities for skills development"
column, not as subject content: using numbers in standard form
(MS 0.1), and reporting a calculated result to an appropriate number
of significant figures, which means no more than the least accurate
measurement allows (MS 1.1).

## Key terms to define precisely

**Relative atomic mass (Aᵣ)** is the weighted mean mass of an atom of
an element, compared with one twelfth of the mass of an atom of
carbon-12. The mean is weighted by the abundance of the element's
isotopes.

**Relative molecular mass (Mᵣ)** is the mean mass of a molecule,
compared with one twelfth of the mass of an atom of carbon-12. You
find it by adding the relative atomic masses of every atom in the
molecular formula.

**Relative formula mass** is the same quantity for an ionic compound.
It is worked out in the same way from the formula of the compound.
AQA uses this term because an ionic compound such as sodium chloride
is a lattice of ions, not separate molecules.

**The mole** is the SI unit of amount of substance (symbol mol). One
mole of a substance contains 6.022 × 10²³ (to four significant
figures) of the particles named: atoms, molecules, ions, electrons or
formula units.

**The Avogadro constant (L)** is the number of particles per mole,
6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹. Its unit, mol⁻¹, matters: the constant links two
different quantities.

```
number of particles = amount in moles × Avogadro constant
```

"Amount in moles" and "number of particles" are different quantities.
Treating them as the same is a common source of error in this
sub-topic.

## How to approach it

Treat relative atomic mass and relative molecular mass as ratios
against carbon-12, not as masses in grams. That is why they have no
units. For an ionic compound, write "relative formula mass", the term
the specification uses.

For 3.1.2.2, the specification names three types of calculation.
Learn each relationship and be ready to rearrange it, because a
question usually gives two quantities and asks for the third.

```
amount in moles     = mass in g ÷ Mᵣ
amount in moles     = concentration in mol dm⁻³ × volume in dm³
number of particles = amount in moles × Avogadro constant
```

You do not need to memorise the Avogadro constant. A question that
needs it prints the value.

## Worked example 1: mass to amount in moles to number of particles

A sample of sodium carbonate, Na₂CO₃, has a mass of 5.30 g. Calculate
the amount, in moles, of sodium carbonate and the number of sodium
ions in the sample. The Avogadro constant is 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.
(Aᵣ: Na = 23.0, C = 12.0, O = 16.0)

```
Step 1: relative formula mass
        Mᵣ = (2 × 23.0) + 12.0 + (3 × 16.0) = 106.0

Step 2: amount in moles
        n = mass ÷ Mᵣ = 5.30 ÷ 106.0 = 0.0500 mol

Step 3: number of formula units
        0.0500 × 6.022 × 10²³ = 3.011 × 10²²

Step 4: number of sodium ions (2 Na⁺ in each Na₂CO₃)
        2 × 3.011 × 10²² = 6.022 × 10²² = 6.02 × 10²² (3 s.f.)
```

The mass, 5.30 g, has three significant figures, so the final answer
is given to three. Keep the extra figure in step 3 and round once, at
the end.

## Worked example 2: concentration to number of particles

A question gives 250 cm³ of a sodium chloride solution with a
concentration of 0.20 mol dm⁻³ and asks for the number of ions
present, given the Avogadro constant as 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.

```
Step 1: convert the volume to dm³
        250 cm³ ÷ 1000 = 0.250 dm³

Step 2: amount of NaCl in moles
        n = concentration × volume = 0.20 × 0.250 = 0.050 mol

Step 3: number of formula units
        0.050 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 3.01 × 10²²

Step 4: number of ions (Na⁺ and Cl⁻, so 2 in each NaCl)
        2 × 3.01 × 10²² = 6.02 × 10²² = 6.0 × 10²² (2 s.f.)
```

The concentration, 0.20 mol dm⁻³, has two significant figures, so the
answer is given to two. Always use the value of the Avogadro constant
printed in the question. Recent AQA papers print 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹;
with that value the answer here is still 6.0 × 10²² to two
significant figures.

gives a different answer.

## Common mistakes

- Forgetting to convert cm³ to dm³ before using a concentration in
  mol dm⁻³.
- Writing "relative molecular mass" for an ionic compound. The
  specification's term is relative formula mass.
- Giving an answer to more significant figures than the least
  accurate value in the question allows.
- Stopping at the amount in moles when the question asks for a number
  of particles.
- Forgetting to multiply by the number of ions in each formula unit
  when the question asks for ions.

## Quick revision checklist

- Define relative atomic mass and relative molecular mass in terms of
  carbon-12.
- Know when to write "relative formula mass".
- Practise all three calculations: mass and Mᵣ, the Avogadro
  constant, and concentration and volume.
- Convert cm³ to dm³ before using a concentration in mol dm⁻³.
- Round once, at the end, to the number of significant figures the
  data allow.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why do relative atomic mass and relative molecular mass have no units?**
Relative atomic mass and relative molecular mass are ratios: each compares a mass with one twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom. A ratio of two masses has no units.

**When should you say relative formula mass instead of relative molecular mass?**
The AQA specification uses relative formula mass for ionic compounds such as sodium chloride, because they are lattices of ions and not separate molecules. Relative molecular mass applies to substances made of molecules.

**Do I need to memorise the Avogadro constant for AQA A-level Chemistry?**
No. The AQA specification says students will not be expected to recall its value, so a question that needs it prints it. Recent AQA papers print it as 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹. Use the value given in the question.

**How do you calculate the number of ions in a solution?**
Convert the volume to dm³, find the amount in moles from concentration × volume, multiply by the Avogadro constant, then multiply by the number of ions in each formula unit. For example, NaCl has two ions in each formula unit and MgCl₂ has three.

**What are common mistakes in mole calculations?**
Common mistakes are forgetting to convert cm³ to dm³, giving an answer to more significant figures than the least accurate value in the question allows, and confusing the amount in moles with the number of particles.
