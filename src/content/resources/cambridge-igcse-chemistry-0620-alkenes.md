This guide covers subtopic **11.5 Alkenes** of Topic 11, Organic chemistry, in the Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620 syllabus for 2026–2028. Outcomes 1–4 are **Core**; outcomes 5 and 6 are **Supplement** and are labelled *(Extended)*. The [alkenes revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-alkenes-revision-notes/) condense it, and the [alkenes practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-alkenes-practice/) test it. Alkenes are made from the alkanes described in the [alkanes study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-alkanes/).

## What 11.5 asks you to do

- **Core 1:** state that alkenes contain a **double carbon–carbon covalent bond** and are **unsaturated hydrocarbons**.
- **Core 2:** describe the manufacture of **alkenes and hydrogen** by **cracking** larger alkane molecules using a **high temperature and a catalyst**.
- **Core 3:** describe the **reasons** for cracking.
- **Core 4:** describe the test that distinguishes saturated from unsaturated hydrocarbons using **aqueous bromine**.
- **Supplement 5:** state that in an **addition reaction only one product** is formed.
- **Supplement 6:** describe addition reactions of alkenes with **bromine or aqueous bromine**, **hydrogen with a nickel catalyst** and **steam with an acid catalyst**, and draw the structural or displayed formulae of the products.

## Structure: the C=C double bond

An alkene has at least one **carbon–carbon double bond**, C=C, which makes it **unsaturated**: atoms can be added across the double bond. Alkenes have the general formula **CₙH₂ₙ**, so the simplest alkene has two carbons.

| Alkene | Molecular formula | Structural formula |
|---|---|---|
| ethene | C₂H₄ | CH₂=CH₂ |
| propene | C₃H₆ | CH₂=CHCH₃ |
| but-1-ene | C₄H₈ | CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃ |
| but-2-ene | C₄H₈ | CH₃CH=CHCH₃ |

The number in but-1-ene and but-2-ene tells you which carbon the double bond starts on; the two are structural isomers. Core candidates need to name and draw ethene; Extended candidates need unbranched alkenes up to four carbons, including both butenes.

Displayed formula of ethene:

```
H       H
 \     /
  C = C
 /     \
H       H
```

## Cracking

**Cracking** breaks **larger alkane molecules** into **smaller** molecules, using a **high temperature** and a **catalyst**. The products are **shorter-chain alkanes, alkenes and sometimes hydrogen**. Unlike fractional distillation, cracking is a **chemical** change: covalent bonds inside the molecules break.

Two typical equations:

```
C₁₀H₂₂ → C₈H₁₈ + C₂H₄        (decane → octane + ethene)
C₂H₆   → C₂H₄  + H₂          (ethane → ethene + hydrogen)
```

### Worked example 1: completing a cracking equation

*Question: Dodecane, C₁₂H₂₆, is cracked to form octane, C₈H₁₈, and one other hydrocarbon only. Identify the other product and classify it.*

1. Carbon: 12 − 8 = 4 carbons left over.
2. Hydrogen: 26 − 18 = 8 hydrogens left over.
3. The leftover atoms total C₄H₈. If it is one molecule, it fits CₙH₂ₙ, so it is an **alkene** (a butene).
4. But if the question says **two molecules of the same product**, divide by two: **2C₂H₄**, ethene.

```
C₁₂H₂₆ → C₈H₁₈ + C₄H₈      or      C₁₂H₂₆ → C₈H₁₈ + 2C₂H₄
```

The method is pure atom-counting, and the general formulae tell you what type of compound each product is. One rule holds in every cracking equation: an alkane cracked into smaller molecules **must** give at least one product with fewer hydrogen atoms than an alkane of that size, so an alkene (or hydrogen) always forms.

### Why cracking is needed

Two reasons are expected:

1. **Supply and demand.** Fractional distillation of petroleum gives more of the long-chain fractions than are needed, and less of the short-chain fractions such as gasoline than are in demand. Cracking converts the surplus long-chain molecules into shorter, more useful ones.
2. **Alkenes are useful.** Alkenes are not present in useful amounts in petroleum, yet they are the starting materials for making polymers (11.8) and other chemicals, including ethanol (11.6). Cracking is how they are made. The hydrogen produced is also a useful product.

A full answer gives **both** reasons. "To make smaller molecules" alone describes what cracking does, not why it is done.

## The bromine test for unsaturation

Shake the hydrocarbon with **aqueous bromine** (bromine water), which is **orange**.

| Hydrocarbon | Observation |
|---|---|
| Unsaturated (alkene) | aqueous bromine turns from **orange to colourless** |
| Saturated (alkane) | **no change**; stays orange |

Two wording points: "colourless" not "clear" (a clear solution can still be coloured), and give **both** colours, the start and the end.

## Addition reactions *(Extended)*

In an **addition reaction**, two molecules join to form **only one product**. The double bond opens, and one atom or group adds to each carbon of the former C=C.

| Reagent | Condition | Product from ethene | Equation |
|---|---|---|---|
| bromine or aqueous bromine | room temperature | 1,2-dibromoethane, CH₂BrCH₂Br | C₂H₄ + Br₂ → C₂H₄Br₂ |
| hydrogen | **nickel catalyst** | ethane, CH₃CH₃ | C₂H₄ + H₂ → C₂H₆ |
| steam | **acid catalyst** | ethanol, CH₃CH₂OH | C₂H₄ + H₂O → C₂H₅OH |

The syllabus gives the steam conditions in 11.6: 300 °C and 6000 kPa with an acid catalyst.

### Worked example 2: drawing an addition product

*Question: Draw the displayed formula of the product formed when propene reacts with bromine.* [1]

Start from propene, CH₂=CH–CH₃. Only the two carbons of the double bond change: each gains one bromine atom, and the double bond becomes single. The CH₃ group is untouched.

```
    H   H   H
    |   |   |
H — C — C — C — H
    |   |   |
    Br  Br  H
```

Product: CH₂BrCHBrCH₃. Common errors are putting both bromine atoms on one carbon, or putting one on the CH₃ carbon, which was never part of the double bond.

## Annotated model answer

**Question:** Describe how you could distinguish between hexane and hex-1-ene. [3]

**Answer:** Add aqueous bromine to each and shake [1]. Hex-1-ene turns the bromine water from orange to colourless [1]. Hexane causes no change; the solution stays orange [1].

*Why it scores:* it names the reagent, gives a result for **each** compound, and states both colours for the alkene.

## Common mistakes

- **Describing cracking as a separation.** It is a chemical reaction that breaks bonds.
- **Leaving out the catalyst** or writing only "heat" instead of a **high temperature**.
- **Giving one reason for cracking** when the question asks for reasons, or describing what cracking does rather than why.
- **Writing "goes clear"** for the bromine result.
- **Unbalanced cracking equations.** Always total the carbons and hydrogens on each side.
- **Placing atoms on the wrong carbon** when drawing addition products.
- **Mixing up catalysts**: nickel for hydrogen, acid for steam.

## Exam technique

- **State / Name:** "unsaturated", "nickel", "colourless".
- **Describe** (cracking): reactant type, conditions, product types.
- **Describe** (test): reagent, observation for the alkene, observation for the alkane.
- **Explain** (reasons): link demand for short-chain fractions and the usefulness of alkenes to the surplus of long chains.
- **Deduce / Complete:** balance atoms, then classify each product by its general formula.
- **Draw:** in displayed formulae every atom and bond appears; the product of an addition reaction has **no** C=C left.
