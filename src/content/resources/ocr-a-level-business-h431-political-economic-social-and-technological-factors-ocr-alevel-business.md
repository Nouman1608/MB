This guide goes deeper into four content areas of **External influences** in OCR A Level Business (H431), Version 2.1 (January 2026), final assessment summer 2027: **political, economic, social and technological factors** (2.5 on this site). The wider [PESTLE study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-political-economic-social-and-technological-factors-and-related-topics/) introduces every factor; this page builds the chains of analysis and the judgements that higher marks need. Companions: [political, economic, social and technological factors revision notes](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-h431-political-economic-social-and-technological-factors-ocr-alevel-business-revision-notes/) and [political, economic, social and technological factors practice questions](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-h431-political-economic-social-and-technological-factors-ocr-alevel-business-practice/).

## What the specification asks

- **Political:** identify political factors; explain how political uncertainty may affect a business; evaluate the benefits of political stability; explain how and why political instability may affect international trade; evaluate how local, national and international government policies affect a business.
- **Economic:** explain key economic indicators; identify the main UK taxes and distinguish direct from indirect; identify the main types and purposes of UK government spending; explain and analyse subsidies; distinguish monetary, fiscal and supply-side policies; explain the link between interest rates and exchange rates; explain GDP and the business cycle and its phases; explain how a business can use the cycle and why some are more affected than others.
- **Social:** describe social factors and evaluate the impact of changes.
- **Technological:** identify technological factors; explain uses of technology and the impact of change; evaluate computer hardware and software; evaluate opportunities and threats.

Every factor ends with the same task: evaluate the **impact and importance** of changes for a business **and its stakeholders**. "Impact" is what happens; "importance" is how much it matters compared with other influences.

## Political factors in depth

Political factors come from the decisions of governments at three levels.

| Level | Examples of decisions that reach a business |
|---|---|
| Local | Planning permission, business rates collected locally, parking and licensing rules |
| National | Taxes, the national minimum wage, regulation, public spending, trade policy |
| International | Trade agreements, tariffs, sanctions, shared product standards |

**Uncertainty versus instability.** Political uncertainty means the rules may change but the system is working, for example in the months before an election. Instability means the system itself is under strain, such as unrest or sudden changes of government. Uncertainty mainly delays decisions: firms postpone investment until they know the tax or trade rules. Instability can stop trade altogether, because contracts may not be honoured, borders may close and payments may be blocked.

**Why stability benefits a business.** Managers can plan long-term investment with confidence, lenders see less risk and may charge lower interest, consumers keep spending, and overseas investors are more willing to build plants. Stability is therefore a reason why multinationals choose some countries over cheaper but riskier ones.

**Analysis chain.** A national government raises the minimum wage → labour costs rise for a care home with many low-paid staff → it must raise fees, cut hours or accept lower profit → but staff morale and retention may improve, cutting recruitment costs. A judgement weighs these against how labour-intensive the business is.

## Economic factors in depth

### Key indicators and what they tell a manager

- **GDP growth**: whether total spending and output are rising, so whether sales are likely to grow.
- **Inflation**: how fast prices are rising, affecting costs, wage demands and pricing.
- **Unemployment**: the pool of available labour and the confidence of consumers.
- **Interest rates**: the cost of borrowing for the firm and its customers.
- **Exchange rates**: the price of imports and the competitiveness of exports.

### Taxation and government spending

Direct taxes are paid straight to government by the person or firm on whose income or profit they fall: income tax, National Insurance contributions and corporation tax. Indirect taxes are added to spending and passed on through prices: VAT and excise duties on fuel, alcohol and tobacco. Business rates are a tax on non-domestic property.

Government spending can be split into **current spending** (day-to-day costs such as NHS staff wages and welfare payments) and **capital spending** (long-term investment such as roads, rail and school buildings). Its purposes are to provide public services, redistribute income and support growth. Capital spending creates direct sales for construction firms and, once built, lowers transport costs for everyone else.

### Monetary, fiscal and supply-side policy

| Policy | Who controls it in the UK | Main tools | Typical speed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monetary | Bank of England | Interest rates | Months |
| Fiscal | Government | Taxation and spending | Months to years |
| Supply-side | Government | Training, infrastructure, deregulation, incentives to invest | Years |

### Worked example 1: an interest rate rise

Hartwell Kitchens (invented) has a variable-rate loan of £500,000 and operating profit of £60,000.

- At 5%, annual interest = £25,000; interest cover = 60,000 ÷ 25,000 = **2.4 times**.
- At 6.5%, interest = £32,500, a rise of £7,500; interest cover falls to **1.8 times** (1 decimal place).

That is the direct effect. The indirect effect is often larger: fitted kitchens are usually bought with credit or from savings, so households facing higher mortgage payments may delay them. Hartwell is hit twice, on cost and on demand.

### Worked example 2: interest rates and the exchange rate

Higher UK interest rates tend to attract overseas savings, so demand for pounds rises and the pound appreciates. Hartwell also exports a kitchen range priced at £20,000.

- At €1.15 to the pound, the Eurozone price is €23,000.
- If the pound rises to €1.25, keeping the sterling price means charging €25,000, about **8.7% dearer** for the customer.
- Keeping the euro price at €23,000 means Hartwell receives only 23,000 ÷ 1.25 = **£18,400**, £1,600 less.

Either the exporter loses competitiveness or it loses margin. A firm that imports components gains, because each pound buys more abroad.

### The business cycle

The phases are boom, downturn (recession), slump and recovery. A recession is commonly defined as two successive quarters of falling real GDP.

**Using the cycle.** A firm that sees recovery coming can build stock and recruit before rivals; one that sees a downturn coming can cut debt, delay expansion and protect cash.

**Why some firms are hit harder.** Sales of luxuries and durable goods (cars, furniture, kitchens) can be postponed, so they fall sharply in a downturn. Firms with high fixed costs or heavy borrowing have less room to absorb falling revenue. Discount retailers and repair services may gain as customers trade down or keep old goods longer.

## Social factors in depth

Social factors include population size and age structure, migration, household make-up, lifestyle, attitudes and fashion. They usually change slowly but persistently, which makes them easier to plan for than economic shocks.

**Worked example 3.** A city-centre sandwich chain finds that more office staff now work from home two or three days a week. Footfall on those days falls, but suburban branches near homes gain trade. Stakeholders are affected differently: staff in city branches may lose hours, landlords of city sites face lower rents, and shareholders may see the chain move to smaller city sites and more suburban ones. The importance is high because the change appears lasting, not seasonal.

## Technological factors in depth

Technology affects **what** a business sells (new products), **how** it makes them (automation, robotics, CAD/CAM), **how it sells** (online channels, apps) and **how it is managed** (stock control, payroll and customer databases).

**Worked example 4: hardware and software.** A distribution firm can buy warehouse software for £45,000 that saves 2,500 hours of staff time a year at £14 an hour.

- Annual saving = 2,500 × £14 = £35,000.
- Payback = 45,000 ÷ 35,000 = **1.3 years** (about 15 months).

The figures look strong, but evaluation adds what they leave out: training time, the risk of system failure during peak season, cyber security, the effect on staff whose hours are cut, and whether customers gain faster delivery.

**Opportunities and threats.** The same technology is usually both. Online ordering lets a small firm reach national customers (opportunity), but it also lets national rivals reach that firm's local customers (threat).

## Common mistakes

- Listing all four factors when the question asks about one, or failing to link the factor to the named business.
- Treating political uncertainty and instability as the same.
- Saying higher interest rates only raise costs, forgetting the effect on customer demand and the exchange rate.
- Claiming a stronger pound helps all firms.
- Calling VAT a direct tax.
- Evaluating technology on cost alone, with no mention of staff, customers or risk.

## Exam technique for OCR command words

- **Identify**: name the factor precisely, such as "a rise in the national minimum wage", not "politics".
- **Explain**: give a cause, a mechanism and an effect on the business.
- **Analyse**: build a chain of at least three linked steps, ideally with a figure from the case.
- **Evaluate**: judge impact and importance, consider different stakeholders, and say what the judgement depends on, such as the size and permanence of the change or how much of the firm's sales are exposed.
