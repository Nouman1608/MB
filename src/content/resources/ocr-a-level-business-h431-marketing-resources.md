It is numbered 3.2 on this site; the specification does not number its content. ## The resources a business needs

Marketing a product needs more than a good idea. The main resources are below.

| Resource | What it provides | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Financial | The budget for research, advertising, sales staff, packaging and discounts | A £60,000 yearly marketing budget |
| Human | Skills and time: market researchers, designers, sales staff, social media managers, agencies | A two-person team with no data analyst |
| Physical | Premises, vehicles, stock, displays and distribution facilities | A shop, a warehouse, a van |
| Technological | Website, e-commerce platform, customer database, analytics software | A booking system that records customer history |
| Intangible | Brand name, reputation, customer data, patents, relationships with retailers | 900 positive online reviews |
| Time | Lead time to design, test and launch | A six-month launch window before the peak season |

The specification asks you to **identify and explain** these. Identification means naming the resource. Explanation means saying what it allows the business to do, for example "a customer database allows the business to email past customers with offers for little cost, which supports a retention objective".

Resources are not equally important for every business. A new online retailer depends on technology and on cash for advertising. A craft maker may depend more on reputation and the skill of one person.

## Resources limit the strategy

The **impact of resources** on the marketing strategy can be positive or negative. A business can only choose a strategy it can afford and carry out. Resource constraints affect:

- **the promotional media:** national television is out of reach for a small budget;
- **the market chosen:** a firm with one van cannot serve a whole country;
- **the speed of growth:** a small team can launch one product at a time;
- **the quality of research:** a firm without analysts may rely on a few opinions;
- **the choice of price:** a firm with little cash may need a high margin quickly, which limits discounting.

Resources can also be an advantage. A strong brand allows a premium price, and a large customer database lowers the cost of every campaign.

### Worked example 1: how the budget narrows the options

**Case.** Tidewell Kayaks is a small coastal business that sells and hires kayaks. It has a marketing budget of **£60,000** for the year. Three options are being considered. The reach and response figures are the owner's estimates.

| Option | Cost | Reach | Response rate | Customers |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A: local radio | £36,000 | 240,000 people | 0.5% | 1,200 |
| B: social media advertising | £24,000 | 150,000 people | 1.2% | 1,800 |
| C: trade show stand | £15,000 | 600 visitors | 10% | 60 |

**Step 1: cost per customer.** Divide the cost by the customers.

- A: £36,000 ÷ 1,200 = **£30.00**
- B: £24,000 ÷ 1,800 = **£13.33**
- C: £15,000 ÷ 60 = **£250.00**

**Step 2: test the combinations that fit the budget.**

- A + B costs £36,000 + £24,000 = £60,000, which uses the whole budget. Customers: 1,200 + 1,800 = 3,000, so the cost per customer is £60,000 ÷ 3,000 = **£20.00**.
- B + C costs £24,000 + £15,000 = £39,000 and leaves £21,000. Customers: 1,800 + 60 = 1,860, so the cost per customer is £39,000 ÷ 1,860 = **£20.97**.

**Step 3: interpret.** Social media advertising is the cheapest way to win a customer, but the owner may still prefer a mix. A + B wins the most customers, but it leaves nothing for staff training or a contingency. B + C wins fewer customers but keeps £21,000 and gains contacts with trade buyers that the response rate does not measure.

**Evaluation.** The budget makes television impossible and forces a choice between media. The estimates are also uncertain: if the radio response rate were 0.25% instead of 0.5%, option A would win only 600 customers and cost £60 each. Resources therefore limit not only what can be done but how confident the business can be in its plan. The sensible conclusion is that Tidewell should put most of the money into social media and test other media on a small scale before committing.

## The SWOT framework

A **SWOT analysis** sorts the factors affecting a business into four groups.

- **Strengths:** internal factors that help (a strong brand, skilled staff, a good location).
- **Weaknesses:** internal factors that hold the business back (limited cash, a narrow range).
- **Opportunities:** external factors the business could use (a growing market, a new technology).
- **Threats:** external factors that could harm it (a new competitor, a rise in costs, a change in the law).

The test is simple: **internal or external?** Strengths and weaknesses are inside the business and can be changed by it. Opportunities and threats are outside, so the business can only respond. A common error is listing "low prices" as a strength when the real factor is the cost advantage that allows them. Another is listing a competitor's action as a weakness.

The **purpose** of a SWOT analysis in marketing is to show the business's position, so that it can match its strengths to opportunities, reduce its weaknesses and prepare for threats. It supports decisions on objectives and strategy.

### Worked example 2: Tidewell's SWOT and its marketing position

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|
| 4.8 out of 5 average rating from 900 online reviews | One shop only |
| Qualified instructors | Two-person marketing team |
| Own repair workshop | 70% of sales fall in June to August |

| Opportunities | Threats |
|---|---|
| Water sport participation growing by 6% a year | Supermarket-brand kayaks at low prices |
| Tourism board offers joint promotion | Poor summer weather |
| Rising interest in guided tours | Rising import costs |

**Analysis (the skill the specification names).** Do not stop at the list. Link the boxes:

- **Strength with opportunity:** the strong reviews and instructors fit the growth in participation and guided tours. Tidewell can promote guided tours to beginners who want safe instruction. The supermarket brands cannot offer this.
- **Weakness with threat:** dependence on summer sales and bad weather is a risk. A wet summer would cut most of the year's income, so Tidewell has a vulnerable position.
- **Using a strength against a threat:** the repair workshop and instructors allow a service-based offer that rivals selling cheap kayaks cannot copy.
- **Reducing a weakness:** offering winter indoor skills sessions and gift vouchers would spread sales across the year.

**A weighted version.** A simple count of items does not show which matter most. Give each factor a weight within its box (weights in a box total 100) and a score for its impact from 1 (small) to 5 (large). The box score is the sum of weight × score ÷ 100.

| Box | Calculation | Score |
|---|---|---|
| Strengths | (40 × 5 + 35 × 4 + 25 × 3) ÷ 100 | 4.15 |
| Weaknesses | (45 × 4 + 35 × 4 + 20 × 2) ÷ 100 | 3.60 |
| Opportunities | (50 × 4 + 30 × 3 + 20 × 3) ÷ 100 | 3.50 |
| Threats | (40 × 4 + 35 × 3 + 25 × 4) ÷ 100 | 3.65 |

Internal position = 4.15 − 3.60 = **+0.55**. External position = 3.50 − 3.65 = **−0.15**. Tidewell is slightly stronger inside than its weaknesses suggest, but the outside environment is marginally unfavourable. The strategy should use its internal strengths to offset the threats, rather than relying on market growth alone. The weights and scores are judgements, so the numbers give the discussion a structure rather than a proof.

## Evaluating SWOT

| Advantage | Limitation |
|---|---|
| Cheap, quick and easy to understand | Subjective: different managers produce different lists |
| Combines internal and external views | A snapshot that dates quickly |
| Helps match strengths to opportunities | Lists factors but does not rank them or say what to do |
| Useful for stakeholders such as lenders and investors reviewing a plan | May leave out important factors if information is poor |
| Can be used at any level, from product to whole business | Can be used to justify a choice already made |

SWOT is most useful when it is based on evidence such as market research and sales data, when factors are weighted, and when it leads to named actions. For **stakeholders**, a shared SWOT can help managers, staff and investors to understand priorities, but it is only the first step and must be followed by research and costed plans.

## Common mistakes

- Putting external factors (a competitor, a recession) in the strengths or weaknesses boxes.
- Listing factors without linking them to each other or to a decision.
- Saying "resources" and meaning only money. Include people, technology, brand and time.
- Forgetting that a resource limit may be relative: a £60,000 budget is large for a small firm and tiny for a national brand.
- Describing SWOT as a strategy. It is an analysis that informs a strategy.

## Exam technique

"Identify" needs the resource named. "Explain" adds the effect on marketing. "Analyse the marketing position" needs links between boxes with the case evidence. "Evaluate usefulness" needs both advantages and limits, and a judgement based on how the analysis is used.
