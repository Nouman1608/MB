This guide covers section 3.5.4 The elements of the marketing mix: price, product, promotion and place (4Ps) of the AQA GCSE Business (8132) specification, for first teaching from September 2017. It is the largest section of Topic 5, Marketing. The [Marketing study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-marketing/) defines every term in the section; this page concentrates on applying them: choosing between pricing methods with figures, deciding on extension strategies and Boston Matrix actions, building a promotional mix from the five named factors, and making the four elements fit together. All businesses and figures are invented.

Use it with the [marketing mix revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-8132-the-marketing-mix-aqa-gcse-business-revision-notes/) and the [marketing mix practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-8132-the-marketing-mix-aqa-gcse-business-practice/). ## Price in action

### Skimming or penetration? A worked comparison

Hensbury Tech launches a new smart thermostat. Each unit costs £40 to make. It forecasts two launch strategies for the first year.

| | Skimming | Penetration |
|---|---|---|
| Price | £120 | £70 |
| Forecast units sold | 6,000 | 15,000 |
| Revenue | 6,000 × £120 = £720,000 | 15,000 × £70 = £1,050,000 |
| Production costs | 6,000 × £40 = £240,000 | 15,000 × £40 = £600,000 |
| Profit before other costs | £480,000 | £450,000 |
| Profit per unit | £80 | £30 |

Skimming gives slightly more profit in year one from far fewer customers. Penetration gives more revenue and builds a customer base 2.5 times larger, which may matter if rivals are about to launch similar products. The deciding factor is competition: skimming works while the product is unique; penetration suits a market where the business needs to win share before rivals arrive. The forecasts are estimates, and the penetration forecast depends on customers actually responding to the lower price.

### Factors influencing price: apply each one

| Factor | Question to ask of the case | Effect on pricing |
|---|---|---|
| Costs | What does each unit cost? | The price must cover costs in the long run, or the business makes a loss |
| Nature of the market | Is it a luxury or everyday market? Are customers price-sensitive? | Luxury markets support high prices; mass markets need value |
| Degree of competition | How many close rivals are there? | Many rivals push towards competitive pricing; no rivals allow skimming |
| Product life cycle | Which stage is the product at? | High at launch for skimming or low for penetration; lower in decline |

### Price and demand

As price rises, demand is likely to fall; as price falls, demand is likely to rise. A price cut only raises revenue if sales rise by enough. Example: a café sells 500 smoothies a week at £4 (revenue £2,000). At £3.50 it sells 600 (revenue £2,100). Revenue rises by £100, but each smoothie earns less, so profit depends on costs as well.

## Product decisions

### New products

Benefits: new sources of revenue, an up-to-date image and an edge over rivals. Risks: development costs are spent before any sales, and the product may fail. Design and image should be driven by what the target market wants, which is why research (3.5.3) comes first.

### Differentiation

A business differentiates its product so customers see it as different from rivals' products, through a **USP** (unique selling point) or a strong **brand image**. Differentiation reduces direct comparison on price, so the business can charge more and keep customers loyal.

### Choosing an extension strategy

The specification names five extension strategies: updating packaging, adding more or different features, changing the target market, advertising, and price reduction. The skill is choosing the right one for the reason sales are falling.

| Why sales are falling | Suitable strategy |
|---|---|
| The product looks dated next to rivals | Updating packaging |
| Rivals offer features it lacks | Adding more or different features |
| The original market is saturated | Changing the target market |
| Customers have forgotten it | Advertising |
| Rivals are cheaper and the product is now standard | Price reduction |

Example: Treve's Crisps has sold the same snack to teenagers for years and sales are falling because teenagers now buy rival brands. Market research shows adults like the flavour but see the packaging as childish. Updating the packaging and changing the target market to adults address the real cause. A price cut would reduce profit per pack without solving the image problem.

### Boston Matrix decisions

The Boston Matrix places each product by **market share** and **market growth**. Use the figures to classify, then recommend.

| Product | Market share | Market growth | Category | Action |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 35% | 2% | Cash cow | Keep, spend little, use its cash to support others |
| B | 30% | 18% | Star | Invest in promotion to keep the lead |
| C | 6% | 20% | Question mark | Invest selectively or drop |
| D | 4% | −3% | Dog | Consider withdrawing |

A balanced portfolio has cash cows funding stars and question marks, so the business is not dependent on one product.

## Building a promotional mix

The specification names five factors. Work through them for the business in the case.

**Case: Kellow Climbing Wall**, a single indoor climbing centre with a small budget, aimed at 16 to 35 year olds in one town. A rival centre has just opened offering a free first session.

| Factor | Application |
|---|---|
| Finance available | Small budget: television is out; social media and local PR are affordable |
| Competitor actions | Rival offers a free first visit, so Kellow needs a sales promotion to compete, such as half-price first visits or a bring-a-friend offer |
| Nature of the product or service | An experience people need to try, so free taster sessions and videos of climbers work well |
| Nature of the market | Local market, so national advertising wastes money |
| Target market | 16 to 35 year olds, who are heavy social media users |

Recommended mix: social media videos plus a bring-a-friend sales promotion, supported by a story in the local newspaper (PR). Each choice is justified by a factor.

## Place: channels and margins

Each step in a channel adds a business that needs a profit, which affects price and control.

Example: Ruthven Soap makes a bar for £1.20.

- **Through a wholesaler and retailer:** sells to the wholesaler at £1.80, who sells to shops at £2.40; shops charge £3.50. Ruthven earns £0.60 a bar but reaches many shops easily.
- **Direct online:** sells at £3.50 but pays about £1.00 a bar for packing and postage, so earns £3.50 − £1.20 − £1.00 = £1.30 a bar, with full control, but must find customers itself.

E-commerce lets Ruthven sell to customers in other countries without opening shops there; m-commerce lets customers buy on their phones. Drawbacks include delivery costs, returns and strong online competition.

Telesales suit products that need explanation or repeat orders, such as office supplies sold to businesses.

## The integrated mix and change over time

The four elements must send the same message. A premium product needs a high price, quality packaging, careful promotion and upmarket stockists. A clash, such as a luxury product sold through discount shops, confuses customers and damages the brand.

| Stage of life cycle | Typical price | Typical promotion | Typical place |
|---|---|---|---|
| Introduction | Skimming or penetration | Heavy, to inform | Selected outlets |
| Growth | Held or slightly reduced | Persuasive | Widened |
| Maturity | Competitive | Reminder | As wide as possible |
| Decline | Reduced | Little | Weak channels dropped |

## Common mistakes

- Choosing a pricing method without using a factor from the case.
- Recommending an extension strategy that does not fix the reason sales are falling.
- Classifying Boston Matrix products by sales revenue instead of share and growth.
- Listing promotion methods with no reference to the five factors.
- Forgetting that intermediaries in a channel each take a margin.
- Treating the 4Ps separately, with choices that do not fit together.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Calculate:** revenue, profit per unit or margin through a channel; show the steps.
- **Explain:** one element applied to the case with a consequence.
- **Analyse:** how one element affects sales, costs or image for this business.
- **Evaluate or justify:** compare options, use the figures and factors in the case, and decide.

## Frequently asked questions

**What factors influence pricing decisions in AQA GCSE Business (8132)?**
Costs, the nature of the market, the degree of competition and the stage of the product life cycle.

**What are the five extension strategies?**
Updating packaging, adding more or different features, changing the target market, advertising and price reduction.

**What factors influence the promotional mix?**
Finance available, competitor actions, the nature of the product or service, the nature of the market and the target market.
