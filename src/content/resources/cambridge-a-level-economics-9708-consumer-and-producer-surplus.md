## Building surplus from individuals

**Consumer surplus** is the difference between the maximum a consumer is willing to pay for a unit and the price actually paid. **Producer surplus** is the difference between the price a producer receives for a unit and the minimum price it would have accepted for that unit.

### Worked example 1: a market of five buyers and five sellers

In an invented market for handmade lamps, each buyer wants one lamp and each seller has one lamp to sell. The market price is 10 cu.

| Buyer | Most they would pay | Buys at 10 cu? | Consumer surplus |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 20 | Yes | 10 |
| B | 16 | Yes | 6 |
| C | 12 | Yes | 2 |
| D | 9 | No | 0 |
| E | 6 | No | 0 |

| Seller | Lowest price they would accept | Sells at 10 cu? | Producer surplus |
|---|---|---|---|
| V | 4 | Yes | 6 |
| W | 7 | Yes | 3 |
| X | 9 | Yes | 1 |
| Y | 12 | No | 0 |
| Z | 15 | No | 0 |

```
Quantity traded     = 3 lamps (3 buyers, 3 sellers: the market clears)
Consumer surplus    = 10 + 6 + 2 = 18 cu
Producer surplus    =  6 + 3 + 1 = 10 cu
Total surplus       = 28 cu
```

The list of maximum prices, ranked from highest to lowest, is the demand curve in steps. The list of minimum prices, ranked from lowest to highest, is the supply curve. With many buyers and sellers the steps smooth into lines, and surplus becomes an **area**: consumer surplus is the area **below the demand curve and above the price**; producer surplus is the area **above the supply curve and below the price**.

## Significance

- **Consumer surplus** measures the benefit consumers gain over and above what they pay. Changes in it show whether consumers are better or worse off after a market change, which is why it is used to judge policies and price changes.
- **Producer surplus** measures the gain to producers over and above the minimum they need to supply. It is closely related to the reward that keeps firms in the market and so to the incentive to produce.
- **Total surplus** (consumer plus producer) is largest at the competitive equilibrium. Every unit traded up to that quantity is worth more to a buyer than it costs a seller to supply. A change that cuts trade below that level loses surplus: this is how the idea links to **inefficiency**.

Two cautions belong in evaluation. Willingness to pay depends on income, so a rich buyer's surplus counts more than a poor buyer's for the same need. And surplus figures depend on estimated demand and supply curves, which are uncertain in practice.

## Causes of changes

### Worked example 2: a straight-line market

An invented market for bottled tea has Qd = 200 - 4P and Qs = 6P - 60, with P in cu and Q in thousands a month. Demand meets the price axis at 50 cu and supply at 10 cu.

```
Equilibrium: 200 - 4P = 6P - 60 -> P = 26, Q = 96
Consumer surplus = 1/2 x (50 - 26) x 96 = 1,152
Producer surplus = 1/2 x (26 - 10) x 96 =   768
Total surplus                          = 1,920   (thousand cu)
```

**Change 1: demand increases** after a popular advertising campaign. Demand becomes Qd = 240 - 4P (price-axis intercept 60 cu).

```
240 - 4P = 6P - 60 -> P = 30, Q = 120
Consumer surplus = 1/2 x (60 - 30) x 120 = 1,800   (up 648)
Producer surplus = 1/2 x (30 - 10) x 120 = 1,200   (up 432)
```

Consumers pay more, yet consumer surplus rises, because they now value the good more highly. Producers gain from both a higher price and more units sold.

**Change 2: costs rise.** Starting again from the original market, a rise in the price of tea leaves shifts supply to Qs = 6P - 90 (price-axis intercept 15 cu).

```
200 - 4P = 6P - 90 -> P = 29, Q = 84
Consumer surplus = 1/2 x (50 - 29) x 84 = 882   (down 270)
Producer surplus = 1/2 x (29 - 15) x 84 = 588   (down 180)
```

Both groups lose. Consumers pay more for fewer units, and producers sell less with higher costs.

Other causes follow the same logic: anything that shifts demand or supply, or changes the price paid or received, changes the two areas. Examples include changes in income, tastes, the prices of related goods, technology, input prices and the number of firms.

## The role of PED and PES

Section 2.5.4 asks how elasticity decides **how far** surplus changes. The limiting cases make the idea clear.

### Worked example 3: two extreme supply curves

Demand for an invented concert series is Qd = 300 - 10P, and it then rises to Qd = 400 - 10P.

**Perfectly inelastic supply: 100 seats, whatever the price.**

```
Before: 100 = 300 - 10P -> P = 20; CS = 1/2 x (30 - 20) x 100 = 500
After:  100 = 400 - 10P -> P = 30; CS = 1/2 x (40 - 30) x 100 = 500
Producer surplus rises by 10 x 100 = 1,000
```

The whole increase in demand appears as a higher price. Consumer surplus is unchanged and all the gain goes to producers.

**Perfectly elastic supply: unlimited seats at 20 cu.**

```
Before: Q = 300 - 200 = 100; CS = 1/2 x (30 - 20) x 100 = 500
After:  Q = 400 - 200 = 200; CS = 1/2 x (40 - 20) x 200 = 2,000
Producer surplus = 0 throughout (price equals the minimum acceptable price)
```

The whole increase in demand appears as extra quantity, and all the gain goes to consumers.

### The general rule

- When **demand shifts**, the elasticity of **supply** decides the split. The less elastic supply is, the more the price rises and the larger the share of the gain going to producers.
- When **supply shifts**, the elasticity of **demand** decides the split. With inelastic demand, a fall in costs mainly lowers price, so consumers gain a lot of surplus per unit; with elastic demand, the change appears mainly as extra quantity.

## Annotated model answer

**Question:** Explain how a fall in the cost of producing solar panels would affect consumer surplus and producer surplus. **[4]**

> Lower costs **shift the supply curve to the right**, so the equilibrium price falls and quantity rises *(1: shift and new equilibrium)*. Consumer surplus **rises**: existing buyers pay less for each panel, and new buyers enter the market and gain surplus on extra units *(1: consumer surplus, with reasons)*. Producer surplus is the area above the new, lower supply curve and below the new price *(1: correct area)*. It is likely to rise because producers sell more units at a lower cost, even though the price is lower; the size of the change depends on the price elasticity of demand *(1: producer surplus, with elasticity)*.

## Common mistakes

- Shading consumer surplus below the price line, or producer surplus above it.
- Forgetting the half in the triangle formula.
- Assuming consumer surplus must fall whenever price rises. When demand increases, consumers value the good more and surplus can rise.
- Using the original intercept after a shift has moved the curve.
- Treating producer surplus as identical to profit. It is related, but fixed costs are not deducted.
- Stating that elasticity matters without saying which curve's elasticity decides the outcome.

## Exam technique

- **Define** using "maximum willing to pay" and "price paid" for consumer surplus, and "price received" and "minimum willing to accept" for producer surplus.
- **Analyse** with a diagram: shade and label each area before and after the change.
- **Calculate** questions: find the equilibrium first, identify both intercepts, then use one half x base x height.
- In a Section B essay, use surplus to judge **who gains and who loses**, then use elasticity to judge **how much**.
