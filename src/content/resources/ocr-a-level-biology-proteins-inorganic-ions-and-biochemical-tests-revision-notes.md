## Amino acids and peptide bonds: 2.1.2(k), (l)

* Elements in proteins: C, H, O, N and usually S.
* General structure: central carbon bonded to NH₂ (amine), COOH (carboxyl), H and a variable R group.
* About twenty amino acids occur in proteins; only the R group differs.
* Condensation: COOH + NH₂ gives a peptide bond (C–N) plus H₂O.
* Hydrolysis: adds H₂O and breaks the peptide bond.
* n amino acids in one chain gives n − 1 peptide bonds and n − 1 water molecules released.

**Quick reminder.** A polypeptide of 85 amino acids: 84 peptide bonds, 84 water molecules. Two chains of 85 joined only by disulfide bonds: 168 peptide bonds.

## Levels of structure: 2.1.2(m)

| Level | Description | Bonding |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Sequence of amino acids | Peptide bonds |
| Secondary | α-helix or β-pleated sheet | Hydrogen bonds along the backbone |
| Tertiary | 3D shape of one polypeptide | Hydrogen, ionic and disulfide bonds; hydrophobic interactions |
| Quaternary | Several polypeptides, with any prosthetic group | The same bonds and interactions between chains |

* Hydrogen bonds: weak, many, broken by heat.
* Ionic bonds: between oppositely charged R groups, affected by pH.
* Disulfide bonds: covalent, between two cysteine residues.
* Hydrophobic interactions: non-polar R groups in the centre. Hydrophilic R groups sit on the outside.

## Globular and fibrous proteins: 2.1.2(n), (o)

| Feature | Globular | Fibrous |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | Compact, rounded | Long strands |
| Solubility | Usually soluble | Insoluble |
| Main role | Metabolic: transport, catalysis, hormones | Structural |
| Examples | Haemoglobin, amylase, insulin | Collagen, keratin, elastin |

* **Haemoglobin:** conjugated protein, four polypeptides plus four haem prosthetic groups, each with Fe²⁺ that binds oxygen.
* **Enzyme (amylase):** the active site is complementary to the substrate.
* **Insulin:** two polypeptides joined by disulfide bonds; soluble hormone that binds receptors.
* **Collagen:** high tensile strength (tendons, bone, artery walls).
* **Keratin:** tough and sulfur-rich (hair, nails, feathers).
* **Elastin:** stretches and recoils (artery walls, lungs, skin).

## Inorganic ions: 2.1.2(p)

| Ion | One role to quote |
|---|---|
| Ca²⁺ | Muscle contraction and bone strength |
| Na⁺ | Nerve impulses; co-transport of glucose |
| K⁺ | Nerve impulse recovery; stomatal opening |
| H⁺ | pH; proton gradients for ATP synthesis |
| NH₄⁺ | Nitrogen cycle, oxidised by bacteria to nitrate |
| NO₃⁻ | Plants use it to make amino acids and nucleotides |
| HCO₃⁻ | Carbon dioxide transport and buffering of blood |
| Cl⁻ | Charge balance when HCO₃⁻ leaves red blood cells |
| PO₄³⁻ | ATP, nucleotides, phospholipids |
| OH⁻ | Raises pH |

## Reagent tests: 2.1.2(q)

| Test | Method | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|---|
| Biuret | NaOH, then dilute copper(II) sulfate | Purple | Blue |
| Benedict's | Reagent, hot water bath | Green, yellow, orange, brick-red | Blue |
| Iodine | Iodine solution | Blue-black | Yellow-brown |
| Emulsion | Ethanol, shake, add to water | Cloudy white | Clear |

**Non-reducing sugar method, in steps:**

1. Run Benedict's on a first portion: stays blue.
2. Boil a second portion with dilute HCl.
3. Cool and neutralise with sodium hydrogencarbonate until fizzing stops.
4. Add Benedict's and heat: a colour change means non-reducing sugar.

* Biuret detects peptide bonds, not free amino acids.
* Only Benedict's and the acid hydrolysis step need heat.
* Use a negative control (water) to compare colours.

## Colorimetry: 2.1.2(r)

1. Pick a filter complementary to the solution colour.
2. Zero with a blank.
3. Make standards by serial dilution.
4. Plot a calibration curve of absorbance against concentration.
5. Read the unknown from the curve, and multiply by any dilution factor.

* Dilution volume: stock volume = (target concentration ÷ stock concentration) × final volume.
* Benedict's filtrate: more reducing sugar gives less blue, so lower absorbance.
* Suitable check: repeat readings and calculate a mean.

## Chromatography: 2.1.2(s)

* Stationary phase: paper (cellulose) or a thin layer of silica on a plate. Mobile phase: solvent.
* Pencil origin line; spots above the solvent level; mark the solvent front immediately.
* Locating agent for colourless spots (ninhydrin gives purple with amino acids) or UV light.
* Proteins are hydrolysed to amino acids first.
* Rf = distance moved by solute ÷ distance moved by solvent. Range 0 to 1, no units.
* Identify by matching Rf, or by spots travelling level with standards on the same plate.
* Distance moved by a spot = Rf × distance moved by solvent.

## Worked reminders

**Peptide count.** Insulin has chains of 21 and 30 amino acids: (21 − 1) + (30 − 1) = 49 peptide bonds, so 49 water molecules were released making it.

**Calibration reading.** Standards: 0.4 g per 100 cm³ gives absorbance 0.61 and 0.6 g per 100 cm³ gives 0.47. A sample reads 0.54, halfway between, so its concentration is 0.50 g per 100 cm³. If it was diluted 1 in 10 first, the original is 5.0 g per 100 cm³.

**Serial dilution.** To make 10 cm³ of a 0.6 standard from a 1.0 stock: (0.6 ÷ 1.0) × 10 = 6 cm³ stock plus 4 cm³ water.

**Rf rearranged.** Spots at 2.4 and 6.3 cm with the solvent front at 9.0 cm give Rf 0.27 and 0.70. For another run with a front at 11.0 cm, a spot with Rf 0.27 sits 0.27 × 11.0 = 3.0 cm from the origin.

## Writing good answers

* **Explain how structure gives function.** Link the feature to the job in one sentence: "Haemoglobin is soluble because hydrophilic R groups are on the outside, so it can be carried inside red blood cells."
* **Name the bond or interaction.** Saying "bonds hold it together" gains little. Say which R groups interact and which bond forms.
* **Describe tests in order.** Reagent, quantity, heat or no heat, positive colour and negative colour.
* **Show the structure of a calculation.** Write the equation, substitute, then give the answer with the right unit (none for Rf).
* **Use data.** In a table or graph question, quote values and say what they show about concentration or identity.

## Protein structure at a glance

| Bond or interaction | Strength | Affected by |
|---|---|---|
| Peptide | Strong, covalent | Hydrolysis by protease |
| Disulfide | Strong, covalent | Reducing agents |
| Ionic | Moderate | pH change |
| Hydrogen | Weak | Heat and pH |
| Hydrophobic | Depends on environment | Change of solvent |

## Must-know distinctions

* Peptide bond (between amino acids) versus disulfide bond (between cysteine R groups).
* Primary structure (sequence) versus tertiary (3D fold of one chain) versus quaternary (more than one chain).
* Conjugated protein (has a prosthetic group) versus simple protein.
* Globular (soluble, metabolic) versus fibrous (insoluble, structural).
* Qualitative test (colour) versus quantitative method (colorimeter reading).

## Quick self-test

1. What four groups are attached to the central carbon of an amino acid?
2. How many peptide bonds are there in a polypeptide of 120 amino acids?
3. Name the bond that links two cysteine R groups.
4. Which type of protein structure involves hydrogen bonds between the backbone C=O and N–H groups?
5. Why is haemoglobin called a conjugated protein?
6. State one property and one location of elastin.
7. Give the symbol for the hydrogencarbonate ion and one role.
8. What colour does a positive biuret test give, and what does it detect?
9. A sample stays blue with Benedict's, but gives orange after boiling with acid and neutralising. What does it contain?
10. A pigment travelled 3.6 cm while the solvent reached 8.0 cm. Calculate its Rf.
11. Why is the origin line drawn in pencil?
12. A solution of 4.0 g per 100 cm³ must be diluted to 1.0 g per 100 cm³ in 20 cm³. What volumes of stock and water?

### Answers

1. Amine group, carboxyl group, hydrogen atom and R group.
2. 119 (n − 1).
3. A disulfide bond.
4. Secondary structure.
5. It is a globular protein with a non-protein prosthetic group (haem).
6. It stretches and recoils; found in artery walls, lungs or skin.
7. HCO₃⁻; carries carbon dioxide in blood or buffers pH.
8. Purple or lilac; peptide bonds, so proteins.
9. A non-reducing sugar, such as sucrose.
10. 3.6 ÷ 8.0 = 0.45.
11. Pencil does not dissolve in the solvent or run.
12. (1.0 ÷ 4.0) × 20 = 5 cm³ stock and 15 cm³ water.

## Where marks are usually lost

* Writing "protein" for a single amino acid, or the reverse.
* Saying the biuret test turns blue-black or that the iodine test turns purple.
* Describing hydrolysis of sucrose without stating the acid, the neutralisation and the second Benedict's test.
* Giving Rf with units, or a value greater than 1.
* Stating that hydrogen bonds hold the primary structure.
* Naming "an ion" without its role, or confusing NH₄⁺ with NO₃⁻.
* Omitting the blank when describing colorimeter use.
* Calling collagen soluble, or describing a globular protein as long and thin.
* Forgetting to multiply by the dilution factor.
