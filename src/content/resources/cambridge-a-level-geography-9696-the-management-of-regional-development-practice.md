These questions cover section 14.4 of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026: one country's regional development policy, its regional disparities, the difficulties of overcoming them, and the evaluation of attempted solutions. Countries and data in Questions 1 to 8 are fictional. Questions 9 to 12 ask you to use your own case study. Revise first with the [14.4 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-management-of-regional-development/) and the [14.4 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-management-of-regional-development-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Define the term "growth pole". **[2]**

**2.** Explain why large, capital-intensive plants in peripheral regions may do little to reduce regional disparities. **[4]**

**3.** The fictional country of Halvia used two schemes to create jobs in its peripheral South.

| Scheme | Total cost (US dollars) | Jobs created |
|---|---|---|
| Steelworks growth pole | 2,400 million | 16,000 |
| Small business grants | 900 million | 12,000 |

**(a)** Calculate the cost per job for each scheme. **[2]**

**(b)** Using the data and your own knowledge, suggest why cost per job is not the only way to judge these schemes. **[3]**

**4.** The table shows GDP per capita, in US dollars, in Halvia's core and South.

| Year | Core | South |
|---|---|---|
| 1990 | 14,000 | 8,000 |
| 2020 | 16,500 | 8,600 |

**(a)** Express the South's GDP per capita as a percentage of the core's in 1990 and in 2020. **[2]**

**(b)** Using your answers, assess whether Halvia's regional policy was successful. **[3]**

**5.** Outline two ways in which a government can try to limit further growth of the core region. **[4]**

**6.** Explain why improving road and rail links between a core and a periphery can increase disparities. **[3]**

**7.** Suggest two political difficulties that may reduce the success of a regional development policy. **[4]**

**8.** Explain the difference between the effectiveness and the efficiency of a regional development policy. **[2]**

## Section B

**9.** For one country you have studied, describe its regional disparities. **[6]**

**10.** For one country you have studied, explain the difficulties faced in trying to overcome regional disparities. **[8]**

**11.** "Regional development policies improve living standards in the periphery but rarely close the gap with the core." With reference to one country you have studied, how far do you agree? **[20]**

**12.** Evaluate the attempted solutions to regional disparities in one country you have studied. **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** A planned concentration of investment around a leading industry in one place [1], intended to create linkages and multiplier effects that spread growth into the surrounding region [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Capital-intensive plants employ relatively few workers for the money invested [1]. They often buy inputs from firms in the core and sell to national markets [1], so local linkages and multiplier effects are weak [1]. Skilled jobs may go to incomers, and profits may leave the region, so local incomes rise little [1]. **[4]**

**3. (a)** Steelworks: 2,400 million / 16,000 = 150,000 US dollars per job [1]. Grants: 900 million / 12,000 = 75,000 US dollars per job [1].

**(b)** The steelworks may create linkages, indirect jobs and long-term skills not counted in direct jobs [1]. Small business jobs may be less secure or lower paid, or may have happened anyway without the grant [1]. Other criteria such as how long jobs last, wages and environmental effects also matter [1]. **[5]**

**4. (a)** 1990: 8,000 / 14,000 x 100 = 57.1% [1]. 2020: 8,600 / 16,500 x 100 = 52.1% [1].

**(b)** The South's GDP per capita rose by 600 US dollars, so living standards improved slightly [1]. But it fell from 57.1% to 52.1% of the core's level, so the relative gap widened [1], and the absolute gap grew from 6,000 to 7,900 US dollars, so the policy was not successful in closing disparities [1]. **[5]**

**5.** Requiring permits for new factories or offices in the core [1] steers investment elsewhere [1]. Relocating government departments or the capital away from the core [1] moves jobs and spending to other regions [1]. Also credit higher taxes or charges on congestion in the core. **[4]**

**6.** Better links make it easier for core firms to sell into the periphery [1], out-competing local producers [1]. They also make it easier for young and skilled workers to migrate to the core, increasing backwash [1]. **[3]**

**7.** Patronage or corruption [1] can divert funds to politically favoured projects rather than the most useful ones [1]. Changes of government [1] can end or reverse policies before they have time to work [1]. Also credit organised crime in contracts or tension between central and regional governments. **[4]**

**8.** Effectiveness is whether the policy achieved its aim, such as narrowing disparities [1]; efficiency is whether it achieved this at reasonable cost, such as cost per job [1]. **[2]**

**9.** Levels-based. Credit: named country with its core region located [1]; peripheral regions named [1]; an economic indicator with figures [1]; a social indicator with figures [1]; change over time [1]; reference to migration or other flows between core and periphery [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Levels-based. Credit: a physical difficulty such as terrain, climate or distance [1] and its effect [1]; an economic difficulty such as agglomeration in the core or weak linkages [1] and its effect [1]; a political difficulty such as corruption or policy changes [1] and its effect [1]; a social difficulty such as brain drain [1]; reference to the named country throughout [1]. **[8]**

**11.** The real exam marks this essay with levels of response. Indicative creditworthy content:

*Introduction:* name the country and its core and periphery [1]; distinguish living standards from relative gaps [1].

*Agree:* infrastructure, services and incomes in the periphery have improved, with evidence [1] [1]; the relative gap has persisted or widened, with evidence [1] [1]; reasons, such as weak linkages or continued backwash [1].

*Disagree:* some peripheral regions or sub-cores have converged [1]; social indicators may have converged more than income [1]; in some periods the gap narrowed [1]; new growth centres such as zones or planned cities have grown in parts of the periphery [1].

*Explanation using theory:* spread versus backwash [1]; cumulative causation in the core continued [1].

*Measures:* relative and absolute measures may disagree [1]; the time scale matters [1].

*Judgement:* a supported view on "how far" [1]; differentiated by indicator or region [1]; linked to evidence [1]; recognition that improving living standards is itself a success [1]; a conclusion that answers the question [1]. **[20]**
*Examiner insight:* This statement separates two aims; the best answers judge each aim separately before reaching an overall view.

**12.** Levels-based. Indicative content:

*Introduction:* name and locate the country and its disparities [1]; state the aims of policy [1].

*Solutions:* describe at least three measures with places and dates [1] [1] [1].

*Evaluation by criteria:* effectiveness, with evidence [1]; efficiency, such as cost or waste [1]; equity, who benefited [1]; sustainability after subsidy [1].

*Difficulties:* how physical, economic and political difficulties limited success [1] [1].

*Unintended effects:* environmental or social costs [1]; dependency on continued subsidy [1].

*Theory:* whether spread effects were created [1] or backwash continued [1].

*Judgement:* overall verdict [1]; varies by measure or period [1]; supported by evidence [1]; comparison with another country's policy for context [1]; a conclusion that answers the question [1]. **[20]**
*Examiner insight:* A criteria-based structure (effectiveness, efficiency, equity, sustainability) keeps every paragraph evaluative.
