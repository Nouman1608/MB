This set tests **3.2.2.5 Employer-employee relations** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Business (9725) specification, Version 1.1 (International AS, Unit 2). Section A has short-answer and calculation questions; Section B has longer, structured questions. Every business and figure is invented. Revise first with the [employer-employee relations study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-employer-employee-relations-9725/) and the [employer-employee relations revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-employer-employee-relations-9725-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Define the term arbitration. **[2]**

**2.** State **two** actions employees might take during a conflict with their employer. **[2]**

**3.** Distinguish between a work to rule and an overtime ban. **[2]**

**4.** A strike stops production at Lowther Glass for 4 days. The factory normally produces 1,500 units a day, and each unit earns a contribution of $8. Calculate the contribution lost because of the strike. **[3]**

**5.** Staff at Carden Print impose an overtime ban for 3 weeks. Carden normally uses 320 hours of overtime a week, producing 12 units an hour, with a contribution of $2.50 per unit. Calculate the contribution lost over the 3 weeks. **[4]**

**6.** Explain **one** advantage to a hotel of consulting its staff before refurbishing its restaurant. **[3]**

**7.** Explain **one** disadvantage to employees of relying on an employee forum rather than a trade union. **[2]**

**8.** Explain **one** risk to a business of paying its employees low wages. **[3]**

## Section B

**9.** Analyse the value of employee involvement in decision making for a manufacturer planning to introduce automated machinery. **[6]**

**10.** Kestwick Buses has told its drivers that from next month they will work longer shifts, with fewer breaks, to cover a shortage of drivers. The drivers' union has threatened a strike.

**(a)** Explain **two** reasons for the conflict at Kestwick Buses. **[4]**

**(b)** Explain how bargaining could help to resolve the conflict. **[2]**

**11.** Explain **two** ways in which remote working could affect employer-employee relations in an insurance company. **[4]**

**12.** Oakhaven Distribution employs 260 warehouse staff, whose union is demanding a 7% pay rise. The annual wage bill for these staff is $6m. Managers have offered 4%. The union has threatened a strike during the peak season, when a strike would cost Oakhaven an estimated $150,000 a day in lost contribution and could lead to penalties from its largest customer. Labour turnover is 22%, and competitors in the area have recently raised pay by about 5%. Managers could accept the union's demand, hold at 4% and risk a strike, or propose that the dispute goes to arbitration. Evaluate how Oakhaven should respond to the union's demand. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** A way of resolving a dispute in which an independent third party hears both sides [1] and recommends or decides a settlement [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any two of: withdraw cooperation [1]; work to rule [1]; overtime ban; strike; leave. **[2]**

**3.** In a work to rule, employees follow every rule and procedure exactly, so work slows down during normal hours [1]; in an overtime ban, employees work normally but refuse to work beyond their contracted hours [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Lost output = 4 × 1,500 = 6,000 units [1]. Lost contribution = 6,000 × 8 [1] = **$48,000** [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Lost output per week = 320 × 12 = 3,840 units [1]. Lost contribution per week = 3,840 × 2.50 = $9,600 [1]. Over 3 weeks = 9,600 × 3 [1] = **$28,800** [1]. **[4]**

**6.** Waiting and kitchen staff know how the restaurant works during busy service [1], so they can point out layout problems, such as a long route from kitchen to tables [1]. Including their ideas avoids costly mistakes and makes staff more willing to accept the changes [1]. **[3]**

**7.** Forum representatives may lack the bargaining power and expertise of an independent trade union [1], so employees' concerns about pay or conditions may be heard but not acted on [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Low pay may lead staff to leave for better-paid jobs [1], raising labour turnover and the costs of recruiting and training replacements [1]. The business may also gain a reputation as an unethical employer, making recruitment harder and harming its brand [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Employees who run the current machines understand the practical problems of production [1], so they can help choose and set up the new machinery to suit the work [1]. Automation may make staff fear job losses; involving them early explains the reasons and plans, such as retraining [1], which reduces resistance and the risk of industrial action [1]. However, involvement takes time and may delay installation [1], and if many jobs will go, involvement cannot remove all opposition, so managers still need a clear plan for redeployment [1]. **[6]**

**10. (a)** Longer shifts with fewer breaks worsen working conditions and may raise safety concerns for drivers [1], since tired drivers are more likely to have accidents [1]. Managers have told drivers about the change rather than consulting them [1], so drivers feel their views have been ignored, which increases resentment [1].

**(b)** Managers and union representatives could negotiate a compromise [1], such as longer shifts for a limited period with extra pay, while Kestwick recruits more drivers [1]. **[6]**

**11.** Staff working from home may feel isolated and less connected to colleagues and managers [1], which can lower engagement and make it harder to spot problems early [1]. Remote working gives staff more control over their time and saves travel [1], which many employees value, improving relations and retention if trust and clear targets are in place [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Credit chains of reasoning and a judgement, for example:

- Accepting 7% costs 0.07 × $6m = $420,000 a year, against $240,000 for 4% [1]: the difference is $180,000 a year [1].
- A strike at peak would cost about $150,000 a day, so just over one day of strike would cost more than the year's difference [1].
- Penalties from the largest customer, and possibly losing the contract, make a strike even more costly [1].
- Competitors have raised pay by about 5%, so 4% may leave Oakhaven's pay behind the local market [1].
- With labour turnover already at 22%, falling behind could make more staff leave, adding recruitment and training costs [1].
- But conceding the full 7% may encourage larger demands in future and raises costs every year, not just this year [1].
- Arbitration avoids a strike while an independent party decides [1], but Oakhaven loses control of the outcome and the award could be close to 7% [1].
- A middle offer, such as 5% to match competitors, linked to productivity improvements, may be acceptable to the union [1].
- Involving staff in the productivity changes through participation would also improve relations [1].
- Judgement: Oakhaven should raise its offer to about the local market rate and negotiate before the peak season, because the cost of even a short strike far exceeds the extra pay, and better pay may also reduce turnover [1]. **[12]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing arbitration with bargaining.
- Forgetting to multiply by the number of weeks or days in industrial action calculations.
- Describing a cause of conflict without linking it to the employees in the case.
- Treating employee involvement as cost-free.
- Evaluating a pay dispute without comparing the cost of conceding with the cost of a strike.
