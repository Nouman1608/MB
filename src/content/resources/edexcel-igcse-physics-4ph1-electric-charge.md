This guide goes deep on sub-topic **2(d) Electric charge**, statements
2.22P to 2.28P of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Physics
(4PH1), Issue 4. Every statement in this sub-topic carries a "P": it is
Physics-only content, assessed on Paper 2P and not part of the
International GCSE in Science (Double Award). For Topic 2 as a whole, see
the [Electricity study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-physics-electricity/).

## What the specification asks

- **2.22P** identify common materials that are electrical conductors or
  insulators, including metals and plastics
- **2.23P** practical: investigate how insulating materials can be charged
  by friction
- **2.24P** explain how positive and negative electrostatic charges are
  produced on materials by the loss and gain of electrons
- **2.25P** know that there are forces of attraction between unlike
  charges and forces of repulsion between like charges
- **2.26P** explain electrostatic phenomena in terms of the movement of
  electrons
- **2.27P** explain the potential dangers of electrostatic charges, e.g.
  when fuelling aircraft and tankers
- **2.28P** explain some uses of electrostatic charges, e.g. in
  photocopiers and inkjet printers

## The one rule: only electrons move

Every atom has a positively charged nucleus and negatively charged
electrons around it. A neutral object has equal amounts of positive and
negative charge. The nuclei are fixed in place; in the processes in this
sub-topic, **only electrons move**.

- An object that **gains** electrons has more negative than positive
  charge: it is **negatively charged**.
- An object that **loses** electrons has more positive than negative
  charge: it is **positively charged**.

Never say that "positive charges moved" or that "protons were rubbed
off". A full answer names electrons and the direction they moved.

## Conductors and insulators (2.22P)

| Conductors | Insulators |
|---|---|
| metals: copper, aluminium, iron, steel | plastics: polythene, acetate, PVC, nylon |
| graphite (carbon) | rubber, glass, wood (dry), wool, paper |
| allow electrons to move through them | electrons cannot move freely through them |

In a metal, some electrons are free to move through the whole material,
so charge given to one part spreads out and flows away if the metal is
touched or connected to earth. In an insulator, electrons cannot move
through the material, so charge stays where it was put. This is why
insulators, not metals held in the hand, are charged by rubbing.

## Charging by friction (2.23P, 2.24P)

When two different insulators are rubbed together, electrons are
transferred from one surface to the other. Which way they go depends on
the materials.

- A **polythene** rod rubbed with a cloth gains electrons from the cloth:
  the rod becomes negative and the cloth positive.
- An **acetate** rod rubbed with a cloth loses electrons to the cloth: the
  rod becomes positive and the cloth negative.

The two charges are equal and opposite, because every electron the rod
gains, the cloth loses. Charge is transferred, not created.

**The practical.**

1. Rub a polythene rod with a dry cloth. Balance it on a watch glass or
   hang it in a paper stirrup from a thread so that it can turn freely.
2. Rub a second polythene rod and bring it near one end of the hanging
   rod without touching. The hanging rod turns away: **repulsion**.
3. Rub an acetate rod and bring it near the hanging polythene rod. The
   hanging rod turns towards it: **attraction**.
4. Conclude that the two rod materials gain opposite charges, that like
   charges repel and unlike charges attract.

Use dry materials and conditions: in damp air, moisture on the surface
lets charge leak away. Hold the rod by one end and do not touch the
rubbed area.

## Forces between charges (2.25P)

**Like charges repel; unlike charges attract.** The forces act on both
objects, are equal and opposite, and act without contact. They are larger
when the charges are closer together.

A charged object can also attract a **neutral** object. A negatively
charged balloon held near a wall repels electrons in the wall's surface
slightly away, so the nearby surface is left slightly positive. The
attraction between the balloon and the nearby positive region is stronger
than the repulsion from the more distant negative region, so the balloon
sticks. Small pieces of paper jump to a charged comb for the same reason.

## Explaining phenomena with electrons (2.26P)

- **Hair standing up after brushing with a plastic brush:** electrons
  transfer between hair and brush, so the strands all gain the same charge
  and repel each other.
- **A shock from a door handle after walking on a nylon carpet:** rubbing
  of shoes on the carpet transfers electrons, so the person becomes
  charged; touching the metal handle lets electrons flow suddenly between
  the person and the earth through the conductor, which is felt as a
  shock and may produce a spark.
- **Clothes clinging after a tumble dryer:** different fabrics rub,
  electrons transfer, and oppositely charged items attract.

Each explanation has three steps: rubbing transfers electrons; the
objects have opposite (or the same) charge; the result is attraction,
repulsion or a flow of electrons (discharge).

## Dangers (2.27P)

**Fuelling aircraft and tankers.** Fuel flowing quickly through a pipe
rubs against it, and electrons are transferred, so the pipe, nozzle or
vehicle can build up a large charge. If the charge jumps to earth as a
**spark** near fuel vapour, it can ignite the vapour and cause an
explosion or fire.

**Prevention:** the aircraft or tanker is connected to earth with a
conducting cable before fuelling, and conducting hoses are used. Electrons
can then flow continuously to or from earth, so charge never builds up
enough to cause a spark.

Other dangers follow the same pattern: sparks in places with flammable
gas or dust, and shocks when touching a highly charged object.

## Uses (2.28P)

**Photocopiers.** A drum coated with a material that conducts when light
falls on it is given a charge over its whole surface. An image of the
page is shone onto the drum: where light from white parts of the page
lands, the coating conducts and the charge leaks away; the dark parts of
the image stay charged. Fine toner powder with the opposite charge is
attracted only to the charged areas. The toner is transferred to a sheet
of paper, which is charged to attract it, and is then heated so that it
melts and sticks.

**Inkjet printers.** Tiny drops of ink are given a charge as they leave
the nozzle. They pass between two charged deflecting plates. The drops
are attracted towards the oppositely charged plate and repelled by the
other, and the size of the charge controls how far each drop is
deflected, so the drops land in the right places to form the letters or
image.

**Spray painting** is a further example: charged paint droplets repel one
another into a fine spray and are attracted to an object given the
opposite charge, giving an even coat with less waste.

## Worked answer: an annotated explanation

*Question (original): Explain why a rubbed polythene comb picks up small
pieces of paper. [3]*

Model answer: "Rubbing transfers electrons onto the comb, so it becomes
negatively charged [1]. When it is held near the paper, electrons in the
paper are repelled slightly away from the comb, so the near side of the
paper becomes positive [1]. Unlike charges attract, and the attraction to
the near side is greater than the repulsion from the far side, so the
paper moves to the comb [1]."

*Why it scores:* each sentence names electrons and their direction, uses
"unlike charges attract", and explains why the neutral paper experiences
a net attraction.

## Common mistakes

- Saying protons or "positive charge" moved. Only electrons move.
- Saying rubbing "creates" charge. It transfers electrons.
- Saying the danger in fuelling is "electricity". Name the charge build-up
  and the spark igniting fuel vapour.
- Saying earthing "removes the static". Say electrons flow to or from
  earth through the cable, so no large charge builds up.

## Exam technique with command words

- **Explain** (2.24P, 2.26P, 2.27P, 2.28P): give a linked sequence, each
  step mentioning electrons, the charge produced and the force or flow.
- **Describe** the practical: apparatus, how the rods are charged, how the
  force is seen, and the conclusion.
- **Identify** conductors and insulators from a list: metals and graphite
  conduct; plastics, rubber, glass and wool insulate.
