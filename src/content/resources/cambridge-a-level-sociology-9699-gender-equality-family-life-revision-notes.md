Condensed notes for section 4.1, *Gender equality and experiences of family life*, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Sociology (9699), Paper 2. Full explanations and a worked answer are in the [4.1 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-gender-equality-family-life/); test yourself with the [4.1 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-gender-equality-family-life-practice/).

## Syllabus checklist

- Liberal, radical and Marxist feminist perspectives on equality and power in the family.
- Conjugal roles and gender equality: housework, childcare, power and emotion work.
- Whether the experience of family life is positive or negative for family members.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Conjugal roles | The roles of partners in a marriage or cohabiting couple |
| Segregated conjugal roles | Separate tasks and leisure (Bott, 1957) |
| Joint conjugal roles | Shared tasks, decisions and leisure (Bott, 1957) |
| Symmetrical family | Similar, though not identical, roles for both partners (Young and Willmott, 1973) |
| March of progress | The view that family life is becoming steadily more equal |
| Dual burden | Doing both paid work and most of the unpaid domestic work |
| Second shift | Hochschild (1989): domestic work done after the paid working day |
| Emotion work | Managing other family members' feelings and well-being |
| Triple shift | Paid work, domestic work and emotion work (Duncombe and Marsden, 1995) |
| Patriarchy | A system of male power over women |
| Allowance system | One partner gives the other a fixed sum for household spending (Pahl, 1989) |
| Pooling | Both partners' money is shared and managed jointly |
| Dark side of the family | Conflict, abuse and harm within families |

## Three feminist perspectives

| | Liberal | Radical | Marxist feminist |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cause | Socialisation, attitudes, laws | Patriarchy | Capitalism |
| Change? | Real, gradual progress | Little | Limited under capitalism |
| Remedy | Reform: equal pay, parental leave | Overturn patriarchal structures | End capitalism and gender roles |
| Name | Equal pay and anti-discrimination law as evidence | Delphy and Leonard (1992) | Ansley (1972); Benston (1969) |
| Criticism | Legal equality is not real equality | Treats men and women as uniform; ignores change | Gender inequality also exists outside capitalism |

## Conjugal roles: the debate

**Towards equality**
- Bott (1957): joint and segregated roles.
- Young and Willmott (1973): the symmetrical family, linked to women's work, smaller families and home-centred leisure.

**Against**
- Oakley (1974): Young and Willmott's measure was too generous (one task a week counted as helping); in her interviews only a minority of husbands took a high share.
- Hochschild (1989): the second shift among dual-earner couples in the USA.
- Childcare: men's involvement has grown but often focuses on enjoyable tasks; mothers keep routine and responsibility.

**Weighing it**: separate attitudes (changed most), time spent (risen for men from a low base) and responsibility or "mental load" (still mostly women).

## Power

- **Decisions**: Edgell (1980), husbands made or had the final say on important decisions; wives made frequent minor ones.
- **Money**: Pahl (1989), allowance and pooling systems; men often had more control over large spending even when money was pooled.
- **Violence**: Dobash and Dobash (1979), violence against wives as a means of male control.

## Emotion work

- Hochschild (1983) developed the idea of emotional labour.
- Duncombe and Marsden (1995): women do most emotion work, a triple shift.

## Positive or negative?

| Positive | Negative |
|---|---|
| Parsons: stabilisation of adult personalities | Leach (1967): the isolated nuclear family turns inward and breeds discontent |
| Fletcher (1966): needs met more fully | Laing (1971): damaging interaction within families |
| Young and Willmott: more equal, home-centred | Domestic violence and child abuse |
| Personal-life views: love, identity, support | Feminist: dual burden, triple shift, unequal power |

Judgement: experience varies by gender, age, class, ethnicity and family type; the same family can be positive for one member and negative for another.

## Exam traps

- **"Feminists think..."** Name the strand.
- **Young and Willmott without Oakley.** Always pair the claim with the critique.
- **Housework only.** Include childcare, power and emotion work.
- **Equality of tasks versus responsibility.** Sharing tasks is not the same as sharing responsibility.
- **All or nothing.** Family life is rarely wholly positive or wholly negative.
- **No context.** Say which society and which group a study looked at.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish joint and segregated conjugal roles.
2. What did Young and Willmott mean by the symmetrical family?
3. How did Oakley criticise Young and Willmott?
4. What is the dual burden?
5. What did Hochschild mean by the second shift?
6. Define emotion work.
7. What is the triple shift?
8. What did Edgell find about decision-making?
9. What did Pahl find about pooled money?
10. How do Dobash and Dobash explain domestic violence?
11. How do liberal and radical feminists differ on change in the family?
12. Give one argument that family life is negative for its members.

### Answers

1. Joint: partners share tasks, decisions and leisure. Segregated: they have separate tasks and leisure.
2. Partners' roles become similar, though not identical, with both working and sharing domestic tasks.
3. Their measure counted a husband as helping if he did one task a week, which exaggerated equality; her own research found few husbands with a high share.
4. Women doing paid work and most of the unpaid housework.
5. The domestic work women do after their paid working day.
6. Managing other family members' feelings and emotional well-being.
7. Paid work, domestic work and emotion work combined.
8. Husbands made, or had the final say on, important decisions; wives made more frequent, less important ones.
9. Even when money was pooled, men often had more control over major spending.
10. As a means of male control over wives, used when husbands feel their authority is challenged.
11. Liberal feminists see real, gradual progress; radical feminists see little change in patriarchal power.
12. Any one: domestic violence and abuse; Leach's view of the inward-looking nuclear family; Laing's damaging interactions; the dual burden and triple shift.
