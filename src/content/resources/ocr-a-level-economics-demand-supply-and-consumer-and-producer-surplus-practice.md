## Questions

**1.** State the type of demand shown in each case. **[4]**

- Builders demand bricks because households demand new houses.
- Households switch from rice to noodles when rice becomes dearer.
- Households buy printers and ink cartridges together.
- Timber is wanted by furniture makers, as fuel and for paper.

**2.** The table shows weekly demand for a good by three households in a small town (illustrative).

| Price (£) | Household A | Household B | Household C |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 12 | 8 | 6 |
| 4 | 9 | 6 | 4 |
| 6 | 6 | 4 | 2 |
| 8 | 3 | 2 | 0 |

**(a)** Calculate market demand at each price. **[2]**
**(b)** Household C leaves the town. State what happens to market demand at £6, and say whether the demand curve itself moves or the market slides along it. Explain. **[3]**

**3.** A consumer's marginal utility, in £, from sandwiches one to five is 6.00, 4.50, 3.00, 1.50 and 0.50.

**(a)** State how many she buys at £2.50 and explain using marginal utility. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate her consumer surplus at £2.50. **[2]**
**(c)** The price falls to £1.00. Calculate her new consumer surplus and the change. **[3]**

**4.** A consumer spends all her income of £240 on one normal good.

**(a)** The price falls from £6 to £4. Calculate how many more units she can afford. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain, using both the substitution and income effects, why quantity demanded rises. **[4]**

**5.** Demand for good Z is Q = 80 − 5P (Q units, P in £).

**(a)** Calculate Q at £6 and at £4, and name the change. **[3]**
**(b)** Incomes rise and demand becomes Q = 100 − 5P. Calculate Q at £6 and name the change. **[2]**
**(c)** State two other factors that could shift demand. **[2]**

**6.** Demand for a good is P = 36 − 6Q (Q in thousands, P in £).

**(a)** Calculate consumer surplus at £18 and at £12. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the change when price falls from £18 to £12. **[1]**
**(c)** Split the change into a rectangle and a triangle, saying who gains each. **[2]**

**7.** Evaluate the claim that a fall in the price of a good always raises consumer surplus. Use P = 28 − 4Q in your answer. **[8]**

**8.** **(a)** Identify the type of supply in each case. **[3]**
- A farm sells lamb and wool from the same flock.
- A bakery switches its ovens between bread and cakes.
- A quarry supplies stone for road building and for housing.

**(b)** The price of lamb rises. Explain the effect on the supply of wool. **[2]**

**9.** Three firms supply 40, 25 and 15 units at £10.

**(a)** State market supply at £10. **[1]**
**(b)** New rules raise costs so each firm supplies 20 per cent less at every price. Calculate new market supply at £10. **[2]**
**(c)** State whether this is a movement or a shift, and explain. **[2]**
**(d)** Instead, a new firm entering supplies 10 units at £10. State market supply. **[1]**

**10.** Supply is P = 5 + 2.5Q (Q in hundreds, P in £).

**(a)** Calculate producer surplus at £15. **[2]**
**(b)** Price rises to £20. Calculate producer surplus. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the change and split it into a rectangle and a triangle. **[3]**

**11.** In the market for good Y (illustrative), demand is P = 26 − 2Q. Supply is first P = 2 + 2Q, and the market trades at £14 and 6 thousand units. A new technology gives supply P = 2 + Q, and the market then trades at £10 and 8 thousand units.

**(a)** Calculate consumer surplus and producer surplus before and after. **[4]**
**(b)** Evaluate whether producers are worse off. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Derived demand [1]; competitive demand [1]; joint demand [1]; composite demand [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the type and tie it to the case in a few words; a label with no link to the stem is hard to credit as showing understanding.

**2. (a)** Add quantities across at each price: £2: 12 + 8 + 6 = 26 [1]; £4: 19; £6: 12; £8: 5 [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Without C, demand at £6 is 12 − 2 = **10** [1]. It is a decrease in demand, a **shift left** [1], because fewer buyers change the quantity wanted at every price, while the price itself has not moved [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the addition at each price so a slip is visible, and say why the cause is not the good's own price.

**3. (a)** She buys **3** [1]: the third sandwich is worth 3.00, at least the price, but the fourth is worth only 1.50, below it [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** (6.00 − 2.50) + (4.50 − 2.50) + (3.00 − 2.50) = 3.50 + 2.00 + 0.50 [1] = **£6.00** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Now she buys 4, since 1.50 exceeds £1.00 but 0.50 does not [1]. Surplus = 5.00 + 3.50 + 2.00 + 0.50 = **£11.00** [1]. Change = **+£5.00** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare each unit's marginal utility with the price one unit at a time, and sum the gaps rather than guessing an area.

**4. (a)** 240 ÷ 6 = 40 units and 240 ÷ 4 = 60 units [1], so **20 more** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Substitution: the good is cheaper relative to others [1], so consumers switch towards it and away from dearer alternatives [1]. Income: the price fall raises real income, as part (a) shows [1], and for a normal good some of this extra purchasing power is spent on more of it [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Treat the two effects as separate chains and say what each one does to quantity; a single sentence covering both is harder to credit.

**5. (a)** At £6, Q = 80 − 30 = **50** [1]. At £4, Q = **60** [1]. This is an **extension** of demand [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** At £6, Q = 100 − 30 = **70** [1]. Demand has **increased**, so the curve moves right [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Any two: price of a substitute or complement, tastes or advertising, population, expectations, credit conditions [1][1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the terms extension and increase for the right cause. A price change alone is never a shift.

**6. (a)** At £18, Q = 3, surplus = ½ × 3 × 18 = **£27 thousand** [1]. At £12, Q = 4, surplus = ½ × 4 × 24 = **£48 thousand** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 48 − 27 = **£21 thousand** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Rectangle 3 × 6 = 18, the gain to existing buyers [1]. Triangle ½ × 1 × 6 = 3, the gain to new buyers [1]. Total 21. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the height of each triangle as the gap between the curve's intercept and the price; this is where slips occur.

**7.** Consumer surplus is the area between the demand curve and the price line [1]. On P = 28 − 4Q, a price of £12 gives Q = 4 and surplus ½ × 4 × 16 = 32. If price falls on an unchanged curve, existing buyers gain a rectangle [1] and new buyers a triangle [1], so surplus rises. But the claim says always. Suppose price falls to £10 because demand decreases to P = 20 − 4Q [1]. Then Q = 2.5 and surplus = ½ × 2.5 × 10 = 12.5, below 32 [1]. So the cause of the fall matters. Cheaper consumption of a harmful good, or a price cut that comes with lower quality, can leave consumers or society worse off [1]. Money measures also treat a pound alike for rich and poor, and demand curves are estimates [1]. **Judgement:** the claim holds when the demand curve stays put, for example after an increase in supply, but not always [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Separate the price fall from its cause, then reach a judgement; listing caveats without a conclusion leaves the evaluation unfinished.

**8. (a)** Joint supply [1]; competitive supply [1]; composite supply [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** A higher lamb price makes it profitable to rear more sheep [1], so more wool is produced and the supply of wool increases (shifts right), which tends to lower the price of wool [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Finish the chain by naming the curve that moves and its direction.

**9. (a)** 40 + 25 + 15 = **80** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Firms supply 32, 20 and 12 [1], so market supply = **64** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** It is a **shift left**, a decrease in supply [1], because costs, not the price, have changed and the quantity at every price changes [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** 80 + 10 = **90** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Work each firm separately before totalling; it makes the source of any error clear.

**10. (a)** At £15, Q = (15 − 5) ÷ 2.5 = 4. Surplus = ½ × 4 × 10 = **£20** [1][1]. **[2]**
**(b)** At £20, Q = 6, surplus = ½ × 6 × 15 = **£45** [1][1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Change = **£25** [1]. Rectangle 4 × 5 = 20 [1]; triangle ½ × 2 × 5 = 5 [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the supply intercept as the base of the height, not zero.

**11. (a)** Before: CS = ½ × 6 × (26 − 14) = **36** [1]; PS = ½ × 6 × (14 − 2) = **36** [1]. After: CS = ½ × 8 × (26 − 10) = **64** [1]; PS = ½ × 8 × (10 − 2) = **32** [1]. **[4]**
**(b)** Producer surplus falls from 36 to 32 [1] even though output rises, because the price falls by more than the extra volume makes up [1]. But surplus is not profit: the cost of installing the technology and fixed costs are not shown [1]. Consumers gain 28 and total surplus rises from 72 to 96 [1]. The result depends on the straight-line curves and on whether all firms adopt the technology, which the figures do not show [1]. **Judgement:** on these figures producers' measured surplus is slightly lower, but their position depends on costs outside the diagram, so "worse off" is not proven [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Support each claim with a figure from part (a), then weigh what the diagram leaves out.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a price-driven change a shift of the curve, or a non-price change a movement.
- Adding prices instead of quantities to build market demand.
- Using the wrong triangle height for surplus.
- Leaving out the rectangle when surplus changes with price.
- Giving only the calculated surplus without saying who gains it.
- Listing evaluative points without a judgement.
- Mixing up composite demand (uses), joint demand (complements) and competitive demand (substitutes).
- Treating producer surplus as the same thing as profit.
