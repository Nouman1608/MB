These notes condense the first three sections of **3.1.2 Socialisation and social control** in
OxfordAQA International AS/A level Sociology (9690), assessed in Unit 2 Section A. In short:
individuals live within cultures of norms, values, roles and statuses, and C. Wright Mills's
sociological imagination links personal troubles to public issues (3.1.2.1); culture is learned
through primary, secondary and re-socialisation by several agencies, which perspectives interpret
differently (3.1.2.2); and identities — individual and collective — are socially constructed and
changing through gender change, diasporas, online life, consumption and globalisation (3.1.2.3).

Full overview: [Exploring Sociology study guide](/resources/a-level-oxfordaqa-sociology-exploring-sociology/).

## Specification coverage

- **3.1.2.1 Individuals and society** — culture: norms and values; roles; status; stereotypes;
  subcultures, including ethnic groups in multicultural societies; the sociological imagination
  (Mills): connecting personal troubles and public issues.
- **3.1.2.2 Socialisation** — primary and secondary socialisation; re-socialisation; inadequate
  socialisation; agencies (families, schools, peers, media, religions, workplace); processes and
  methods; functionalist, Marxist, feminist and social action/interactionist perspectives.
- **3.1.2.3 Social identities and social change** — individual and collective identities; social
  construction through socialisation; identities based on age and generation/cohort, sex and gender,
  race and ethnicity, social class, religion, ability/disability and nationality; gender identities,
  hybrid ethnic identities and diasporas, online identities, consumption and globalisation.

The specification suggests studying Stanley Cohen, Fanon, Goffman, Mead, Mills and Zuboff for
section 3.1.2.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Culture** | The shared, learned way of life of a group |
| **Norms / values** | Specific rules of behaviour / general beliefs about what is worthwhile |
| **Role / status** | Expected behaviour / social position (ascribed or achieved) |
| **Stereotype** | Oversimplified, generalised image of a group |
| **Subculture** | Group with distinctive norms and values within a wider culture |
| **Sociological imagination** | Seeing the link between personal biography and wider social structures (Mills, 1959) |
| **Primary / secondary socialisation** | Early learning in the family / later learning from other agencies |
| **Re-socialisation** | Learning new norms that replace old ones, e.g. in prisons, armies or new jobs |
| **Inadequate socialisation** | Failure to learn society's norms properly |
| **Collective identity** | Sense of belonging to a group (nation, faith, class) |
| **Cohort / generation** | People born in the same period who share experiences |
| **Diaspora** | Dispersed population keeping ties to a homeland |
| **Hybrid identity** | Identity mixing more than one culture |

## 3.1.2.1 Individuals and society

- **Culture** gives the norms, values, roles and statuses that guide behaviour; **stereotypes**
  simplify groups and can justify inequality.
- **Subcultures**, including ethnic groups in multicultural societies, keep distinct languages,
  religions and customs while sharing some wider norms.
- **Mills** (*The Sociological Imagination*, 1959): unemployment of one person is a personal trouble;
  mass unemployment is a public issue rooted in the economy. Sociology connects the two.

## 3.1.2.2 Socialisation

**Agencies and methods**

| Agency | Methods / processes |
|---|---|
| Families | Imitation, role modelling, rewards and punishments, gendered toys and tasks |
| Schools | Formal curriculum, hidden curriculum, rules and sanctions |
| Peers | Acceptance, peer pressure, ostracism |
| Media | Representations, role models, advertising, online feedback |
| Religions | Teachings, rituals, community expectations |
| Workplace | Training, codes of conduct, organisational culture |

**Re-socialisation:** Erving Goffman (*Asylums*, 1961) described "total institutions" such as
psychiatric hospitals and prisons that strip away a person's previous identity ("mortification of
the self") and impose new routines.

**Perspectives**

| Perspective | View of socialisation |
|---|---|
| Functionalist | Passes on shared values; creates social order and consensus (Parsons) |
| Marxist | Transmits ruling-class ideology; prepares a compliant workforce |
| Feminist | Reproduces gender roles and patriarchy (e.g. Oakley on manipulation and canalisation) |
| Social action / interactionist | Socialisation is active; the self develops through interaction — George Herbert Mead's "I" and "me", taking the role of others and the "generalised other" (*Mind, Self and Society*, 1934) |

## 3.1.2.3 Identities and social change

**Bases of identity:** age and generation/cohort; sex and gender; race and ethnicity; social class;
religion; ability/disability; nationality.

- **Ability/disability:** Goffman's *Stigma* (1963) shows how people with "spoiled identities"
  manage others' reactions.
- **Race and ethnicity:** Frantz Fanon's *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952) analysed how colonial racism
  shaped the identities of colonised Black people, who were pressured to see themselves through
  the coloniser's eyes.

**Social change and identity**
- **Gender:** more varied masculinities and femininities as women's education and work expand; but
  traditional expectations persist.
- **Hybrid ethnic identities and diasporas:** migrants and their children combine cultures and keep
  transnational ties.
- **Online identities:** social media and virtual communities allow curated and multiple selves;
  Shoshana Zuboff (*The Age of Surveillance Capitalism*, 2019) warns that platforms collect and
  profit from personal data, shaping behaviour.
- **Consumption and globalisation:** identities increasingly linked to brands, lifestyles and global
  culture; critics argue structural factors still limit choice.

## Worked example: an 8-mark answer outline

*Explain two ways in which social media may influence identity.* (practice)

1. **Curated online identities** — users present selected images and achievements, creating a chosen
   digital self; this allows experimentation but creates pressure to conform to idealised norms. (4)
2. **Virtual communities** — people form collective identities with others who share interests,
   ethnicities or causes across borders, including diaspora members; but platforms' data collection
   (Zuboff) shapes what people see and, potentially, who they become. (4)

## Common mistakes and exam traps

- Treating **re-socialisation** as the same as secondary socialisation — it involves replacing earlier
  norms.
- Describing Mills without the link between **personal troubles** and **public issues**.
- Leaving out the **interactionist** perspective, which the specification names.
- Listing bases of identity without explaining how they are **socially constructed**.
- Forgetting that the specification requires examples from your own or a known society in some
  extended answers.

## Quick self-test

1. Define the sociological imagination.
2. Give one example of re-socialisation.
3. What did Mead mean by the "generalised other"?
4. Name four bases of social identity in the specification.
5. Explain one way globalisation affects identity.

## Frequently asked questions

**How does 3.1.2.3 link to 3.1.2.4 social control?** Stigmatised identities and othering appear in
3.1.2.4, so identity and control are closely connected.

**Which perspective sees individuals as active?** Social action/interactionist theory, which
emphasises meaning and interaction rather than structure.

**Why study Fanon in a socialisation unit?** His work shows how identity is shaped by power and
racism, linking socialisation to inequality.
