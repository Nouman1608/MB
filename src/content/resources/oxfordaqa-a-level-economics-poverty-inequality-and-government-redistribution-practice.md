## Questions

**1.** Household H lives in Country P. It owns a house worth 210,000, savings of 22,000, shares worth 18,000 and a car worth 12,000. It owes a mortgage of 140,000 and a personal loan of 9,000. Its annual income is 31,000.

**(a)** Distinguish between income and wealth. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the household's net wealth and express it as a multiple of annual income, to one decimal place. **[3]**

**2.** Income shares by fifth of households in Country P, poorest to richest. Before tax and benefits: 4%, 8%, 14%, 24%, 50%. After: 6%, 11%, 16%, 25%, 42%.

**(a)** Calculate the cumulative income share of the poorest four fifths before and after tax and benefits. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the ratio of the richest fifth's share to the poorest fifth's share, before and after. **[2]**
**(c)** State which distribution is more equal. **[1]**

**3.** The Gini coefficient for Country P is about 0.43 before tax and benefits and about 0.34 after. Explain what this shows and give two limitations of using the Gini coefficient. **[4]**

**4.** Weekly incomes of ten households in Town Y: 90, 130, 170, 200, 240, 280, 330, 400, 560 and 900. Relative poverty is income below 60% of the median.

**(a)** Find the median and the relative poverty line. **[2]**
**(b)** What percentage of households is in relative poverty? **[1]**
**(c)** A year later the eight lowest incomes rise 4% and the two highest rise 20%. The absolute poverty line is a fixed 132. Show that the number of households in absolute poverty falls but the number in relative poverty does not. **[2]**

**5.** Explain two causes of poverty. **[4]**

**6.** Country R taxes income at 0% up to 10,000, 20% from 10,000 to 40,000 and 40% above 40,000. Calculate the tax and the average tax rate for an earner on 25,000 and an earner on 70,000, and explain what your results show. **[5]**

**7.** A worker receives a means-tested benefit withdrawn at 55p for each extra 1 earned. Income tax is 20% of earnings. The worker earns 120 more. Calculate how much of the extra 120 the worker keeps and explain the effect on incentives. **[4]**

**8.** A weekly benefit of 210 rises by 3.5%. Over the same period the price index rises from 100 to 105.2. Calculate the percentage change in the benefit's real value and comment on the effect on absolute poverty. **[4]**

**9.** Explain one benefit and one cost of a more equal distribution of income. **[6]**

**10.** Explain how excessive inequality can be both a cause and a consequence of market failure. **[6]**

**11.** Evaluate the view that means-tested benefits are better than universal benefits as a way to reduce poverty. **[10]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Income is a flow of earnings and receipts over a period of time [1]; wealth is a stock of assets less debts at a point in time [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Assets = 210,000 + 22,000 + 18,000 + 12,000 = 262,000 [1]. Net wealth = 262,000 − (140,000 + 9,000) = **113,000** [1]. 113,000 / 31,000 = **3.6** times income [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Show assets and debts separately so your working is clear; reporting gross assets as wealth is the usual error.

**2. (a)** Before: 4 + 8 + 14 + 24 = **50%** [1]. After: 6 + 11 + 16 + 25 = **58%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Before: 50 / 4 = **12.5** [1]. After: 42 / 6 = **7** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** The distribution after tax and benefits is more equal, because the ratio is smaller and the poorest fifth's share is higher [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the dimension you compare (ratio, bottom share) in the verdict rather than writing "more equal" alone.

**3.** The fall from 0.43 to 0.34 shows that the Lorenz curve moved closer to the line of equality [1], so the tax and benefit system made income more equally distributed [1]. Limitation: it is a single number, so different distributions can give the same value and it hides where changes occurred [1]. Limitation: it ignores income level, so a country can be equal and poor; it also depends on whether household or individual and pre- or post-tax income is used [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Limitations earn credit when tied to what the Gini cannot show, not when written as generic "data may be inaccurate".

**4. (a)** Sorted incomes are already in order; median = (240 + 280) / 2 = **260** [1]. Line = 0.6 × 260 = **156** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 90 and 130 are below 156, so 2 of 10 = **20%** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** New incomes include 93.6, 135.2, 249.6 and 291.2, so the median is (249.6 + 291.2) / 2 = 270.4 and the line is 162.24; 93.6 and 135.2 are still below, so relative poverty is still 2 households [1]. Against the fixed 132, 90 and 130 were below before but only 93.6 is below now, so absolute poverty falls from 2 households to 1 [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A "show that" works best when you state the new line and compare each relevant income with it, so the reasoning is visible.

**5.** Cause 1: unemployment. Without wages, households rely on benefits that are usually below earnings [1], so income falls below the poverty line, especially for long-term unemployed workers whose skills decay [1]. Cause 2: low skills and education. Workers with few qualifications have low productivity and bargaining power [1], so they receive low wages and may be trapped in poverty across generations as their children lack resources for education [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A cause scores better when followed through to income and poverty; the cause alone is a list item.

**6.** Earner on 25,000: tax = 0.20 × 15,000 = **3,000** [1]; average rate = 3,000 / 25,000 = **12%** [1]. Earner on 70,000: tax = 0.20 × 30,000 + 0.40 × 30,000 = 6,000 + 12,000 = **18,000** [1]; average rate = 18,000 / 70,000 = **25.7%** [1]. The average rate rises with income, so the tax is progressive and narrows the gap in net incomes [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Apply each rate to its band only; applying the top rate to the whole income is a common route to a wrong answer.

**7.** Tax = 0.20 × 120 = 24 [1]. Benefit lost = 0.55 × 120 = 66 [1]. Kept = 120 − 24 − 66 = **30** [1]. The combined marginal rate is 75%, so the extra work is poorly rewarded and the worker may choose not to work more or look for work, which is the poverty trap [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Convert your answer into a rate (here 75%); it makes the incentive point precise.

**8.** New benefit = 210 × 1.035 = 217.35 [1]. Real change = 1.035 / 1.052 − 1 [1] = **−1.6%** [1]. The benefit buys less than before, so recipients near the absolute poverty line may fall below it despite the nominal rise [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Divide the nominal growth factor by the price factor; subtracting the two percentages gives a different figure.

**9.** Benefit: less poverty and higher cohesion [1]. A more equal distribution raises the incomes of poorer households, who spend a larger share on necessities and can afford education and health care [1], so there is wider access to human capital and lower social tension, which may support growth over time [1]. Cost: incentives. Higher marginal tax rates reduce the reward for extra work, saving and enterprise [1], so some workers or firms supply less effort or investment, or relocate or avoid tax [1], which can reduce output and tax revenue and may leave low-income households less well off than predicted [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Each side needs its own chain from cause to effect; a list of effects with no links stays at statement level.

**10.** Cause: poor households cannot afford education, health care or insurance, so merit goods are under-consumed [1]. Low human capital cuts productivity and mobility [1]. Wealth can also give market power or political influence, reducing competition [1]. Consequence: markets reward by ability to pay and ownership, not need [1], and market failures such as monopoly profit, information gaps and discrimination can create unequal incomes [1]. So the two reinforce each other: market failure widens inequality and the inequality deepens market failure [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Give a mechanism in each direction; asserting "inequality is market failure" without a reason is not enough.

**11.** Means-tested benefits direct limited funds to those in greatest need, so the cost per person lifted out of poverty is lower [1] and money can be spent on other priorities [1]. Universal benefits reach everyone, including the poor who do not apply or fail the test, so take-up is higher and stigma lower [1], and administration is simpler [1]. Means-testing creates a poverty trap, as withdrawn benefit plus tax gives high marginal rates [1], which discourages work and can lock households in poverty [1]. Universal benefits spend on households who do not need them, so they need higher taxes that may weaken incentives [1]. Judgement depends on scale and funding: with large budgets and political support for solidarity, universal benefits may suit; with tight budgets and a priority on the poorest, targeting wins [1]. A free-market view favours targeting and low taxes, while an egalitarian view values universal provision for cohesion [1]. A reasoned conclusion could prefer a mix, for example universal child payments plus a tapered top-up for low earners, because it reduces the withdrawal rate [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* A strong evaluation weighs both designs against the same criteria (cost, incentives, take-up) and states the value judgement behind the final choice.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Reading wealth as the same as income, or leaving out debts.
- Not converting shares into cumulative shares before comparing curves.
- Using the median incorrectly with an even number of values.
- Forgetting that a relative line moves when incomes move.
- Applying a marginal rate to the whole of an income.
- Calling the tax system progressive without comparing average rates.
- Treating a nominal benefit rise as a real rise.
- Making a policy point with no consequence or no value judgement.
