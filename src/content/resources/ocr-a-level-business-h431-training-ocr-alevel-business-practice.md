## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by a training needs analysis (TNA). **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between on-the-job and off-the-job training. **[3]**

**3.** Explain **one** reason why a business might provide an induction for new employees. **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** risk to a business of investing in training. **[2]**

**5.** Which **one** of the following is an apprenticeship?

A: A one-day health and safety course.

B: A job combined with structured training leading to a qualification.

C: A degree studied full-time before starting work.

D: An introduction to the staff canteen on the first day.

**[1]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Wilmott Shoes has 24 staff. A TNA finds that 15 need training on new machinery, costing £380 each, and 8 need customer service training, costing £120 each.

**(a)** Calculate the total cost of the training. **[3]**

**(b)** Wilmott estimates that the machinery training will cut faulty shoes costing £9,000 a year by 50%, and the customer service training will cut complaint costs of £2,500 a year by 60%. Calculate the total annual benefit and the payback period, to one decimal place. **[4]**

**(c)** Evaluate the importance of the TNA to Wilmott Shoes and its stakeholders. **[8]**

**7.** Wilmott Shoes is considering taking on two apprentice shoemakers. Each would be paid £15,000 a year for 18 months and spend 20% of their time in off-the-job study.

**(a)** Calculate the total wage cost of one apprentice for the 18 months, and the part of this that is for time spent in off-the-job study. **[3]**

**(b)** Evaluate whether Wilmott Shoes should use apprenticeships to train its future shoemakers. **[9]**

**8.** Analyse **two** ways in which training could improve the performance of a restaurant. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** A TNA is an analysis of the gap between the skills and knowledge the business needs [1] and those its employees already have, so that training can be targeted [1]. **[2]**

**2.** On-the-job training takes place at the workplace while the employee does the job, usually with a supervisor or experienced colleague [1], whereas off-the-job training takes place away from the job, in a course, workshop or college [1]. On-the-job is usually cheaper and more practical, while off-the-job gives access to specialists [1]. **[3]**

**3.** Induction helps new staff to settle in quickly, because they learn the layout, rules and people [1], and it covers legal safety briefings, which reduces the risk of accidents [1]. **[2]**

**4.** A trained employee may leave to work for a competitor [1], so the business loses the money it spent and has to recruit and train a replacement [1]. **[2]**

**5.** **B** [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** Machinery: 15 × £380 = £5,700 [1]. Customer service: 8 × £120 = £960 [1]. Total = **£6,660** [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Faults: 50% × £9,000 = £4,500 [1]. Complaints: 60% × £2,500 = £1,500 [1]. Total = **£6,000** a year [1]. Payback = £6,660 ÷ £6,000 = **1.1 years** [1]. **[4]**

**(c)** Targeting: the TNA shows that 15 of 24 staff need machine training and 8 need customer service training, so Wilmott does not spend money training staff who do not need it [1]. Training all 24 staff in both areas would cost 24 × £380 + 24 × £120 = £12,000, nearly twice the targeted cost of £6,660 [1].
It links training to business results: the payback of 1.1 years is attractive [1], and the figures provide targets against which to check success.
Stakeholders: staff gain skills and confidence for the new machines [1]; customers receive fewer faulty shoes [1].
Limits: the benefits are estimates and may not be reached, for example if the 50% fall in faults does not happen [1]. A TNA takes time and requires honest information from staff, who may fear that gaps will count against them [1].
Judgement: the TNA is very important because it shows where training will pay and avoids waste, so Wilmott should repeat it each year or when new machines arrive [1]. **[8]**

**7. (a)** Wage cost for 18 months = £15,000 × 1.5 = **£22,500** [1] [1]. Time in off-the-job study = 20% × £22,500 = **£4,500** [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Benefits: apprentices earn while they learn, and Wilmott shapes their skills to its own methods [1]. A trained shoemaker can take years to find, so apprenticeships provide a supply of scarce skills [1]. Apprentices are paid less than a qualified shoemaker, so wage costs are lower during training [1]. A qualification motivates the apprentice, who may stay.
Costs and risks: £22,500 per apprentice is a big cost for a small business, and £4,500 of it is for time spent away from work [1]. Supervisors must spend time teaching, so their own output falls [1]. An apprentice may leave after qualifying for a higher wage elsewhere, and the investment is lost [1].
Alternatives: recruiting an experienced shoemaker would be quicker but wages are higher and skilled people are scarce [1]. Short off-the-job courses would cost less but would give fewer skills [1].
Judgement: apprenticeships are worthwhile if Wilmott expects to need skilled shoemakers for many years, since the shortage of skilled staff makes the cost worthwhile; it should offer a contract and career steps to reduce the risk that apprentices leave [1]. **[9]**

**8.** Any two ways, 3 marks each. Service quality: training staff in customer care and menu knowledge [1] means guests are served more politely and accurately [1], which raises satisfaction and repeat bookings [1]. Safety and hygiene: food hygiene training [1] reduces the risk of food poisoning and inspection failures [1], protecting reputation and avoiding fines [1]. Accept: training in new till or ordering technology cutting errors and waiting times, or training cooks to cut waste, with explanation. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing induction with on-the-job training.
- Treating training only as a cost.
- Saying off-the-job training is always better.
- Forgetting indirect costs such as lost output.
- Not saying that trained staff may leave.
