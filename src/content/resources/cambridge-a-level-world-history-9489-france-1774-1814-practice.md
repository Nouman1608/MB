## Section A

**1.** Identify two ways in which the Estate system placed the financial burden of the state on the Third Estate. **[2]**

**2.** State three reasons why Calonne's proposals of 1787 failed. **[3]**

**3.** Explain the significance of the decision on how the Estates-General would vote. **[3]**

**4.** Name two features of the Constitution of 1791 that limited democracy or royal power. **[2]**

**5.** Give three reasons why counter-revolutionary groups failed to reverse the Revolution before 1792. **[3]**

**6.** Identify three measures used by the Committee of Public Safety to defend the Republic in 1793-94. **[3]**

**7.** State four methods Napoleon used to establish authoritarian control as First Consul and Emperor. **[4]**

## Section B

**8.** Explain why the Directory relied increasingly on the army between 1795 and 1799. **[10]**

**9.** "The Concordat was Napoleon's most important domestic reform." How far do you agree? **[20]**

**10.** Read the two invented sources below. Compare Sources A and B as evidence about the flight to Varennes.

*Source A (invented)* -- From a Paris club newspaper, late June 1791. "The King has shown us his heart. He swore loyalty to the constitution with one hand and with the other packed his carriage for the frontier, where the émigrés and the Austrians wait. A man who flees his people has abdicated. Let the Assembly stop pretending that Louis is a friend of liberty."

*Source B (invented)* -- From a speech by a moderate deputy in the National Assembly, July 1791. "We are told the King was carried off. Let us accept it, for the alternative is a republic, and a republic means disorder and war with every crowned head in Europe. The constitution is nearly complete. Shall we throw it away because of one night's error?" **[15]**

**11.** Read the two further invented sources below, together with Sources A and B in Question 10. How far do Sources A to D support the view that the King's own actions were the main reason for the fall of the monarchy by 1792?

*Source C (invented)* -- From the diary of a Paris shopkeeper, August 1792. "The Prussians are on the road, and the Duke of Brunswick threatens to burn Paris if the King is harmed. The King vetoes the decrees against the priests and dismisses the patriot ministers. Who can doubt now that he wishes for the enemy to win?"

*Source D (invented)* -- From the memoirs of a former Girondin deputy, written in exile in 1796. "We wanted war to unmask the court and carry liberty to other peoples. We did not see that defeat would hand the streets to the men who later turned on us. The monarchy fell because the war we made required a victim." **[25]**

**12.** Explain why the March of the Women took place in October 1789. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two, 1 mark each: the clergy and nobility were largely exempt from the *taille*, so it fell on commoners (1); peasants also paid seigneurial dues to lords and the tithe to the Church (1); indirect taxes such as the *gabelle* on salt fell heavily on ordinary consumers (1). **[2]**

**2.** Any three, 1 mark each: the Assembly of Notables, made up largely of privileged men, refused a universal land tax that would end their exemptions (1); the Notables distrusted Calonne after he revealed a deficit that contradicted Necker's *Compte rendu* (1); they claimed they had no authority to approve permanent taxation, pointing towards the Estates-General (1); Louis XVI did not back Calonne and dismissed him in April 1787 (1). **[3]**

**3.** 1 mark each for: voting by order meant the First and Second Estates could outvote the Third two to one (1); the doubling of the Third's deputies was meaningless unless votes were counted by head (1); the deadlock led the Third to declare itself the National Assembly on 17 June, turning a dispute over procedure into a claim to national sovereignty (1). **[3]**

**4.** Any two, 1 mark each: only active citizens who paid a set amount of tax could vote (1); the King kept a suspensive veto, which could delay laws but not block them permanently (1); there was a single-chamber Legislative Assembly the King could not dissolve (1); ministers were chosen by the King but could not sit in the Assembly (1). **[2]**

**5.** Any three, 1 mark each: émigré nobles were outside France and seen as traitors (1); counter-revolutionaries were divided between those wanting the full Ancien Régime and constitutional monarchists (1); the King himself did not lead resistance openly until Varennes, which discredited him (1); foreign powers such as Austria were slow to act; the Declaration of Pillnitz (August 1791) was conditional (1); the August Decrees and Church land sales gave peasants and buyers a stake in the Revolution (1). **[3]**

**6.** Any three, 1 mark each: the *levée en masse* (August 1793) conscripted the nation (1); the Law of Suspects (September 1793) allowed arrest of suspected enemies (1); the General Maximum fixed prices and wages to feed Paris and the army (1); representatives on mission were sent to the provinces and armies (1); revolutionary tribunals tried rebels and opponents (1). **[3]**

**7.** Any four, 1 mark each: prefects appointed to run each department (1); a Council of State and new ministries answerable to him (1); a reorganised police under Fouché, with informers (1); reform of the judiciary with appointed judges (1); the closure of most Paris newspapers and censorship (1); propaganda in art and the official press (1); plebiscites to approve the Consulate, life consulship and Empire (1). **[4]**

**8.** Indicative content. Strong answers explain and link several reasons. **Political:** the Constitution of the Year III gave France annual elections, and voters returned royalists in 1797 and Jacobins in 1798; with no lawful way to break deadlock, the Directors used troops in the coup of Fructidor (1797) and annulled results in Floréal (1798). **Popular unrest:** after the Paris risings of 1795, the regime knew it could not rely on the National Guard, and Bonaparte's suppression of the Vendémiaire rising showed what soldiers could do. **Financial:** conquests in Italy brought money that the Treasury needed while the assignats collapsed. **Military success:** victorious generals gained prestige that the Directors lacked, so dependence grew. The best answers show the connection: the constitution created instability, instability required force, and force made generals indispensable -- culminating in the call to Bonaparte in 1799. **[10]**

**9.** Indicative content. **For the Concordat:** it ended the religious schism opened by the Civil Constitution of the Clergy, removing a cause of royalist revolt in the west; the Pope accepted the loss of Church lands, which reassured the many buyers of national property; bishops were nominated by the state and clergy were paid by it, so the Church became a support of the regime; it widened Napoleon's popularity among Catholic peasants. **Against:** the Civil Code (1804) was more far-reaching -- it fixed legal equality, property rights and the abolition of feudalism across France and much of Europe, though it also reduced the rights of women; the Bank of France (1800) and efficient tax collection gave the regime the financial stability the Directory had lacked; the prefects created a centralised administration that outlived him; the lycées trained officials and officers. **Judgement:** a strong answer sets criteria (permanence, effect on stability, scale). For example, the Concordat was the most important for securing the regime in 1801-02, but the Code was the most important in lasting impact. **[20]**

**10.** Indicative content. **Similarities:** both treat the flight as a test of the King's commitment to the constitution; both recognise that a republic is the logical alternative to keeping Louis. **Differences:** A says the King fled to join the émigrés and Austrians and has in effect abdicated; B adopts the fiction that he was "carried off" and wants the constitution completed with him. A demands that the Assembly stop protecting him; B urges it to do exactly that. **Explaining the difference:** A is from a radical club paper written in the anger of late June, aimed at Paris readers; B is a moderate deputy in July, when the Assembly was deciding to suspend rather than depose Louis, and its motive is fear of disorder and war. Knowledge supports A on facts -- Louis left a declaration criticising the Revolution -- while B is better evidence of the Assembly's policy, which led to the Champ de Mars and the Feuillant split. A judgement on which is more useful, and for what, lifts the answer. **[15]**

**11.** Indicative content. **Support:** A presents the flight as proof of betrayal; C points to the King's vetoes and dismissal of the patriot ministers in 1792 and links him with the enemy. Knowledge: the vetoes on decrees against refractory priests and on a camp of *fédérés* near Paris, and the Brunswick Manifesto, made him look complicit. **Challenge:** B shows the Assembly was willing to keep him after Varennes, so his actions alone did not end the monarchy in 1791; D argues that the Girondins' war was the real cause, because defeat radicalised Paris; C itself shows the role of foreign invasion. Knowledge: the declaration of war in April 1792, the defeats that followed and the rise of the sections and the sans-culottes before 10 August. **Provenance:** D is written in exile with hindsight and some guilt; C is a private diary, possibly reflecting rumour; A is a radical paper with a purpose. **Judgement:** the sources support the view to a large extent for 1791, but suggest that war turned the King's unpopularity into his overthrow. **[25]**

**12.** Indicative content. **Bread:** prices in Paris remained high in autumn 1789 and shortages hit women who queued for bread. **Political fears:** the King had not yet approved the August Decrees or the Declaration of the Rights of Man, and Paris suspected he would resist. **The banquet at Versailles:** reports that officers of the Flanders Regiment had trampled the tricolour cockade at a banquet in early October suggested a counter-revolutionary plot. **Radical leadership:** the Paris press and the districts encouraged action, and the National Guard followed the women to Versailles. **Linking:** hunger gave the march its numbers, while political suspicion gave it a goal -- bringing the King to Paris, where he could be watched. **[10]**
