Learn the content first from the [trade unions study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-trade-unions-cambridge-igcse-economics/) and the [trade unions revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-trade-unions-cambridge-igcse-economics-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define a trade union. **[2]**

**2.** Which of the following is an example of collective bargaining?

A: a worker asks her manager for a pay rise

B: a union negotiates a shorter working week for all its members

C: the government raises the minimum wage

D: a firm advertises for new staff

**[1]**

**3.** Identify **two** areas covered by collective bargaining. **[2]**

**4.** Which factor is most likely to weaken a trade union?

A: low unemployment in the economy

B: members with scarce skills

C: falling demand for the firm's product

D: a high share of workers belonging to the union

**[1]**

**5.** A union has 2,550 members among 3,000 workers. Calculate the membership density. **[1]**

**6.** State **one** way a trade union may try to influence government policy. **[1]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**7.** A factory employs 400 workers on 450 a week. The union claims a 6% pay rise. The firm offers 2%.

**(a)** Calculate the weekly wage bill before any rise. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the extra yearly cost of the union's claim, using 52 weeks. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **two** reasons why the firm might agree to a rise above its offer. **[4]**

**8.** Workers at a packing plant each pack 300 boxes a week for 540. A productivity deal raises pay to 567 and output to 330 boxes.

**(a)** Calculate the labour cost per box before and after the deal. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why both workers and the firm may benefit from this deal. **[3]**

**9.** Explain **two** ways in which trade unions protect employment. **[4]**

**10.** Hospital cleaners and hospital surgeons each have a union. Explain why the surgeons' union is likely to be stronger. **[4]**

**11.** Analyse the possible disadvantages of trade union activity for a government. **[5]**

**12.** Discuss whether workers always benefit from belonging to a trade union. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** An organisation of workers [1]; that aims to protect and improve its members' pay, working conditions and job security [1]. **[2]**

**2.** B [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* A is individual bargaining; C is government policy.

**3.** Any two: wages [1]; working hours [1]; working conditions. **[2]**

**4.** C [1]. **[1]**

**5.** 2,550 / 3,000 x 100 = **85%** [1]. **[1]**

**6.** Any one: lobbying ministers for a higher minimum wage; campaigning publicly for stronger safety laws; giving evidence to official inquiries [1]. **[1]**

**7. (a)** 400 x 450 = **180,000** [1].

**(b)** 180,000 x 0.06 = 10,800 a week [1]; 10,800 x 52 = **561,600** a year [1].

**(c)** A strike would stop production and the firm could lose orders and customers [1], which might cost more than the extra pay [1]. If unemployment is low, workers unhappy with pay may leave for other firms [1], and recruiting and training replacements is costly [1]. (Accept: high profits mean the firm can afford it; a productivity deal offsets the cost, developed.) **[7]**

**8. (a)** Before: 540 / 300 = **1.80** [1]; after: 567 / 330 = **1.72** (to two decimal places) [1].

**(b)** Workers gain a 5% pay rise, from 540 to 567 [1]. Output per worker rises by 10%, faster than pay [1], so labour cost per box falls and the firm can cut prices or raise profit [1]. **[5]**

**9.** Unions negotiate over planned redundancies [1], seeking alternatives such as reduced hours or voluntary early retirement, so fewer members lose jobs [1]. They press for retraining when new technology is introduced [1], so members can move to new tasks in the firm rather than being dismissed [1]. (Accept: representing members facing unfair dismissal, developed.) **[4]**

**10.** Surgeons have scarce skills that take many years to train [1], so a hospital cannot replace them quickly during a dispute [1]. Action by surgeons stops operations, causing serious and visible disruption [1], which puts strong pressure on the employer to settle [1]. Cleaners can be replaced more easily, so their union has less power. **[4]**

**11.** Strikes in essential services such as transport or health care disrupt the wider economy [1], so firms lose output and the public loses services [1]. Large pay rises raise firms' costs [1], which may be passed on as higher prices, making it harder to meet the aim of stable prices [1]. Unions may press for higher public spending or pay for public-sector workers, increasing pressure on the government budget [1]. **[5]**

**12.** Benefits: collective bargaining usually wins higher pay and better conditions than a worker could alone [1]; unions give legal support and representation if a worker is treated unfairly [1].
Costs: members pay subscriptions [1]; strikes mean lost pay [1]; if the union pushes wages well above the market level, the firm may employ fewer workers, so some members lose jobs [1].
Judgement: workers usually benefit where the union is strong and the employer profitable, but not always: in a struggling firm, high pay demands may cost jobs, so the benefit depends on the employer's position [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining a union without stating its purpose.
- Calculating the yearly cost without multiplying by 52 when the wage is weekly.
- Treating every pay rise as raising labour cost per unit, when output per worker may rise too.
- Using general strength factors that do not fit the workers in the question.
- Writing only from the workers' viewpoint when asked about firms or government.
