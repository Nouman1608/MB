## 3.1.1.1 Fundamental particles

An atom has a **nucleus** containing protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons.

| Particle | Where found | Relative charge | Relative mass |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proton | nucleus | +1 | 1 |
| Neutron | nucleus | 0 | 1 |
| Electron | shells around the nucleus | −1 | about 1/1840 (very small) |

The specification asks you to appreciate that ideas about atomic structure have **evolved over time**. Know one
clear chain of cause and effect:

- Dalton pictured atoms as tiny solid spheres.
- The discovery of the electron showed that atoms contain smaller, negatively charged parts. The "plum pudding"
  model followed, with electrons embedded in a ball of positive charge.
- Firing alpha particles at thin gold foil did not fit that model. Nearly all passed undeflected, yet a small
  number bounced off at large angles. The conclusion was a tiny, dense, positive nucleus with
  mostly empty space around it.
- The neutron was identified later, which explained why mass numbers exceed proton numbers.
- Electrons arranged in shells and then in sub-shells and orbitals refined the picture further.

The point to make in an answer: a model is kept until new evidence it cannot explain appears, then it is revised.

## 3.1.1.2 Mass number and isotopes

| Symbol | Name | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| A | Mass number | protons + neutrons |
| Z | Atomic (proton) number | number of protons |

Method for any atom or ion:

1. Protons = Z.
2. Neutrons = A − Z.
3. Electrons = Z − charge. A 3+ ion has lost three electrons; a 2− ion has gained two.

| Species | Protons | Neutrons | Electrons |
|---|---|---|---|
| ⁵⁶Fe³⁺ (Z = 26) | 26 | 30 | 23 |
| ³²S²⁻ (Z = 16) | 16 | 16 | 18 |
| ²⁷Al³⁺ (Z = 13) | 13 | 14 | 10 |
| ⁸¹Br⁻ (Z = 35) | 35 | 46 | 36 |

**Isotopes** are forms of one element whose nuclei hold equal numbers of protons but unequal numbers of neutrons, so
their mass numbers differ. Their electron arrangements match, so their chemical behaviour is the same. Properties
tied to mass, such as density, vary a little.

### Time of flight (TOF) mass spectrometer

The specification limits you to four stages, then data analysis.

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| Ionisation | The sample is vaporised and turned into positive ions, for example by knocking electrons out with a beam of high-energy electrons. |
| Acceleration | An electric field accelerates the ions so that all of them have the same kinetic energy. |
| Ion drift | The ions travel along a flight tube with no field. With equal kinetic energy, lighter ions move faster and arrive sooner. |
| Ion detection | Ions reach a detector, gain electrons and produce a current. A larger current means more ions. |
| Data analysis | A computer converts flight times into mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) and plots abundance against m/z. |

The whole instrument is under vacuum so that ions do not collide with air particles. Learn the reason for each
stage, not just the order.

### Using the spectrum

- Each peak is one isotope. Peak position is its m/z and peak height is its relative abundance. For a 1+ ion, m/z
  equals the relative isotopic mass.
- A spectrum identifies an element because the pattern of isotopes (positions and relative heights) is
  characteristic of that element.
- For a compound, the peak at the highest m/z is the molecular ion. Its m/z gives the relative molecular mass.

**Relative atomic mass (limited to mononuclear ions)**

Ar = Σ(isotopic mass × abundance) / Σ(abundance)

Worked reminder. An element X (invented data) shows peaks at m/z 46, 47 and 48 with relative abundances 8.0, 7.0 and
85.0. The abundances total 100.0.

Ar = (46 × 8.0 + 47 × 7.0 + 48 × 85.0) / 100.0 = 4777 / 100.0 = 47.77

To 3 significant figures that is **47.8**. A sensible answer falls between the smallest and largest isotopic masses, closer to the
most abundant isotope.

Working backwards. An element has isotopes of mass 20 and 22 and Ar = 20.4. If x is the fraction of the 22
isotope: 20(1 − x) + 22x = 20.4, so 2x = 0.4 and x = 0.20. The abundances are **80% and 20%**.

## 3.1.1.3 Electron configuration

### Shells, sub-shells and orbitals

| Sub-shell | Number of orbitals | Capacity (electrons) |
|---|---|---|
| s | 1 | 2 |
| p | 3 | 6 |
| d | 5 | 10 |

Each orbital holds a maximum of two electrons. Shell number n holds up to 2n² electrons (2, 8, 18, 32).

**Filling order** (up to Z = 36): 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p. Within that sequence 4s is occupied ahead of 3d.

| Species | Configuration |
|---|---|
| Ca (Z = 20) | 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s² |
| Cr (Z = 24) | 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d⁵ 4s¹ |
| Cu (Z = 29) | 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d¹⁰ 4s¹ |
| Fe³⁺ (23 electrons) | 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d⁵ |
| S²⁻ (18 electrons) | 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ |

Chromium and copper are the two exceptions to the filling order up to Z = 36: a half-full or full 3d set is
favoured. When a d-block atom forms a positive ion, the **4s electrons are lost first**. For negative ions, add the
extra electrons to the next available space.

### Ionisation energies

**First ionisation energy:** the energy change when a mole of gaseous atoms each lose one electron, giving a mole of
gaseous 1+ ions.

Equations always carry state symbols:

- First: Ba(g) → Ba⁺(g) + e⁻
- Second: Ba⁺(g) → Ba²⁺(g) + e⁻

Three factors set the size: nuclear charge, distance of the outer electron from the nucleus, and shielding by
inner electrons.

| Pattern | Explanation | What it is evidence for |
|---|---|---|
| First ionisation energy falls down Group 2 (Be to Ba) | Extra shells put the outer electron further away and more shielded, outweighing the greater nuclear charge | Electron shells |
| Overall rise across Period 3 (Na to Ar) | Nuclear charge increases while shielding and shell stay the same | Electrons added to the same shell |
| Dip from Mg to Al | Al's outermost electron is held in 3p, a sub-shell a little higher in energy and further out than 3s | Sub-shells |
| Dip from P to S | In S, one 3p orbital holds a pair of electrons that repel one another, so one of them leaves more easily | Electron pairing in orbitals |

### Reading successive ionisation energies

1. Look for the largest jump between consecutive values.
2. The number of electrons removed before that jump is the number in the outer shell. That gives the group.
3. Later jumps mark the next inner shells.

Invented data for a Period 3 element, in kJ mol⁻¹: 540, 1150, 1740, 10900, 13700, 17600, 21500, 25200.
The biggest jump is from the third to the fourth value (1740 to 10900), so there are three outer electrons: Group 3(13).
Removing the fourth electron means breaking into a full inner shell that is closer to the nucleus and less shielded.

## Quick self-test

1. Give the numbers of protons, neutrons and electrons in ⁷⁹Se²⁻ (Z = 34).
2. State two differences between isotopes of an element, and one thing they share.
3. Name the stage of TOF mass spectrometry in which all ions are given the same kinetic energy.
4. Why do lighter ions reach the detector first?
5. An element has two isotopes, mass 35 and 37, in the ratio 3 : 1. Calculate Ar.
6. State what the highest m/z peak in a compound's spectrum represents.
7. Write the configuration of Mn (Z = 25).
8. Give the equation, with state symbols, for removing the second electron from a magnesium atom.
9. Why does strontium need less energy than calcium to lose its outer electron?
10. Successive ionisation energies of an element jump sharply after the second electron. State its group.

### Answers

1. 34 protons, 45 neutrons, 36 electrons.
2. They differ in number of neutrons and in mass number (so in mass-dependent physical properties); they share the same number of protons (and so the same chemical properties).
3. Acceleration.
4. All ions have the same kinetic energy, so the lighter ions have the greater speed and cross the drift region in less time.
5. (35 × 3 + 37 × 1) / 4 = 142 / 4 = **35.5**.
6. The molecular ion, which gives the relative molecular mass.
7. 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d⁵ 4s².
8. Mg⁺(g) → Mg²⁺(g) + e⁻.
9. Strontium's outermost electron occupies a more distant shell and is screened by more inner electrons; this beats the larger nuclear charge, so less energy is needed.
10. Group 2.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing electrons = A − Z for ions, instead of Z minus the charge.
- Describing isotopes as having different numbers of "particles" instead of different numbers of neutrons.
- Saying isotopes have different chemical properties.
- Giving the ionisation stage as "electron bombardment" with no mention that positive ions form.
- Leaving out state symbols, or putting charges in the wrong place, in ionisation equations.
- Defining first ionisation energy without "gaseous", or without a mole of atoms.
- Explaining the Group 2 trend by nuclear charge alone, when shielding and distance decide it.
- Writing 3d before 4s when giving the configuration of an ion, or removing 3d electrons first from a d-block atom.
- Quoting a relative atomic mass outside the span of the isotopic masses, which signals an arithmetic slip.
