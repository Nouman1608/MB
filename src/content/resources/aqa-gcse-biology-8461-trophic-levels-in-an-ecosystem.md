This guide goes deeper into section **4.7.4 Trophic levels in an ecosystem** of the AQA GCSE Biology (8461)
specification, for first teaching from 2016. It covers 4.7.4.1 Trophic levels, 4.7.4.2 Pyramids of biomass and
4.7.4.3 Transfer of biomass. The whole of 4.7.4 is **biology only**: it is in GCSE Biology but not in Combined
Science. It is assessed on Paper 2, at both tiers.

For the whole of Topic 7, start with the [Ecology study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-biology-8461-ecology/). Then
condense this section with the [Trophic levels revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-biology-8461-trophic-levels-in-an-ecosystem-revision-notes/)

Food chains and producers are introduced in the [Organisation of an ecosystem study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-biology-8461-organisation-of-an-ecosystem/).

## What the specification asks you to do

- **Describe the differences** between the trophic levels of organisms within an ecosystem.
- **Construct accurate pyramids of biomass** from appropriate data.
- **Describe** pyramids of biomass and **explain how biomass is lost** between trophic levels.
- **Calculate the efficiency** of biomass transfers between trophic levels by percentages or fractions of mass.
- **Explain how this affects the number of organisms** at each trophic level.

## Numbering the trophic levels

Trophic levels are represented by numbers, starting at level 1 with plants and algae. Further levels are
numbered according to how far the organism is along the food chain.

| Level | Who | Name | How it gets biomass |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Plants and algae | Producers | Make their own food by photosynthesis |
| 2 | Herbivores | Primary consumers | Eat plants or algae |
| 3 | Carnivores that eat herbivores | Secondary consumers | Eat level 2 |
| 4 | Carnivores that eat other carnivores | Tertiary consumers | Eat level 3 |

**Apex predators** are carnivores with no predators. An apex predator may sit at level 3 or level 4 depending on
the food chain: what makes it an apex predator is that nothing eats it.

An organism's level depends on **what it eats in that food chain**. A fox eating rabbits is at level 3; a fox
eating blackberries is at level 2. In a food web, one species can occupy more than one level.

### Decomposers

**Decomposers** break down dead plant and animal matter by **secreting enzymes into the environment**. The
enzymes digest large insoluble molecules outside the decomposer. **Small soluble food molecules then diffuse
into the microorganism.** This is different from a consumer, which takes food inside its body before digesting
it. Decomposers feed on every trophic level, so they are not given a level number in a food chain.

## Pyramids of biomass

**Biomass** is the mass of living material. A pyramid of biomass represents the **relative amount of biomass in
each level** of a food chain. **Trophic level 1 is at the bottom** of the pyramid.

How to construct one accurately:

1. Draw a horizontal bar for each trophic level, level 1 at the bottom.
2. Make the **width of each bar proportional to its biomass**, using a stated scale.
3. Centre the bars above each other so the shape is clear.
4. Label each bar with the trophic level (or organism) and the biomass.

**Worked example 1: choosing a scale.** A pond survey (invented data) gives:

| Trophic level | Organisms | Biomass (g/m²) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Pondweed and algae | 800 |
| 2 | Water snails and mayfly nymphs | 72 |
| 3 | Small fish | 6 |

```
scale chosen: 1 mm = 5 g/m²
level 1 bar: 800 ÷ 5 = 160 mm   (too wide for most pages)
better scale: 1 mm = 10 g/m²
level 1 bar: 800 ÷ 10 = 80 mm
level 2 bar: 72 ÷ 10 = 7.2 mm
level 3 bar: 6 ÷ 10 = 0.6 mm
```

The top bar is very narrow, and that is correct: an accurate pyramid shows how small the higher levels are. Do
not widen the top bar "so it can be seen"; that makes the pyramid inaccurate.

## Why biomass is lost between levels

Producers are mostly plants and algae, which transfer **about 1% of the incident energy from light** for
photosynthesis. After that, **only approximately 10% of the biomass from each trophic level is transferred to the
level above it**. These are approximate figures; real values vary between ecosystems.

The losses named in the specification:

- **Not all the ingested material is absorbed**; some is **egested as faeces**.
- Some absorbed material is **lost as waste**: **carbon dioxide and water in respiration**, and **water and urea
  in urine**.
- **Large amounts of glucose are used in respiration**, transferring energy for movement and, in mammals and
  birds, keeping warm. That glucose does not become new biomass.

Mark schemes also accept that **not every part of an organism is eaten** (for example roots or bones), so that
biomass is not passed on.

## Calculating efficiency

```
efficiency (%) = biomass transferred to the higher level ÷ biomass in the lower level × 100
```

**Worked example 2.** Using the pond data above:

```
level 1 → 2: 72 ÷ 800 × 100 = 9%
as a fraction: 72/800 = 9/100
level 2 → 3: 6 ÷ 72 × 100 = 8.3% (2 s.f.)
as a fraction: 6/72 = 1/12
```

**Worked example 3: working backwards.** A field contains 4500 kg of grass. If about 10% of biomass passes to each
level, estimate the biomass available to the secondary consumers.

```
level 2: 10% of 4500 kg = 450 kg
level 3: 10% of 450 kg = 45 kg
```

**Worked example 4: energy from light.** About 1% of the incident light energy is transferred by producers. If a
field receives 3 000 000 kJ of light energy per m² in a year, about 30 000 kJ per m² is transferred.

## How this affects numbers of organisms

Because only a small fraction of biomass passes from one level to the next:

- each higher level has **less biomass available**, so it can support **fewer organisms** (or a smaller total
  mass of organisms);
- after a few transfers there is too little biomass left to support another level, which is why food chains
  rarely have more than four or five levels;
- apex predators are usually few in number and need large areas to find enough food.

**Annotated model answer**

*Question: Explain why there are fewer owls than mice in a wood. [3]*

> Only about 10% of the biomass in the mice is transferred to the owls [1]. Biomass is lost because some of what
> the owls eat is egested as faeces and much glucose is used in respiration [1]. So there is less biomass at the
> owls' trophic level to support organisms, and fewer owls can survive [1].

## Common mistakes

- Starting the numbering at 0, or calling herbivores level 1.
- Drawing a pyramid with bars not to scale, or with level 1 at the top.
- Saying energy or biomass is "used up" or "destroyed". It is transferred to the surroundings or lost as waste.
- Saying decomposers eat dead material. They secrete enzymes and absorb the soluble products.
- Dividing the lower level by the higher one, giving an efficiency over 100%.
- Confusing **egestion** (faeces, never absorbed) with **excretion** (urea, made in the body).

## Exam technique for AQA command words

- **Construct**: choose a scale, state it, and draw bar widths to that scale.
- **Calculate**: write the formula, substitute, and give the unit (%) or the fraction in simplest form.
- **Explain** biomass loss: name at least two losses and say what happens to the material.
- **Describe** differences between trophic levels: what each level eats and how it obtains biomass.
