Condensed notes on section **4.4 Location decisions** of Cambridge O Level Business Studies 7115 (for examination in 2026). The full teaching and a worked recommendation are in the [location decisions study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-business-7115-location-decisions/); exam-style questions are in the [location decisions practice set](/resources/cambridge-o-level-business-7115-location-decisions-practice/).

## What 4.4.1 covers

The syllabus heading is "the main factors influencing the location and relocation decisions of a business". Four skills follow from it:

1. Factors for **manufacturing** and for **service** businesses.
2. Factors when choosing **which country** to locate operations in.
3. The **role of legal controls** on location.
4. **Recommend and justify** a location in given circumstances.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning to learn |
|---|---|
| Location decision | Choosing where a business will carry out its operations |
| Relocation | Moving an existing operation to a new site |
| Footfall | The number of people passing a site, a key factor for shops and cafes |
| Infrastructure | Transport, power, water and communication networks serving an area |
| Import tariff | A tax on imported goods, which can be avoided by producing inside the market |
| Import quota | A limit on the quantity of a good that may be imported |
| Legal controls | Laws that restrict, add conditions to, or encourage business activity in particular places |
| Government incentive | A grant, tax relief or cheap land offered to attract businesses to an area |

## Manufacturing: which factor dominates?

Ask what is expensive to move or hard to find.

- Inputs much heavier or bulkier than the output: locate **near raw materials**.
- Output bulky, heavy or fragile: locate **near the market**.
- Process needs scarce skills: locate **where skilled labour is**.
- Process uses huge amounts of energy or water: locate **where utilities are reliable**.
- Low-value, high-volume product: **land and wage costs** carry most weight.

Always check: transport links, room to expand, government help. Remember a grant is often paid once, while running costs recur every year.

## Services: sort first, then choose

| Service type | Example | Factors that matter most |
|---|---|---|
| Customers come to it | cafe, clinic, salon | footfall, visibility, parking, public transport, rent |
| It goes to customers | plumber, courier | central position in the area served, travel time, cheap premises |
| Delivered at a distance | online shop, call centre | cost of premises, wages, staff availability, reliable internet |

Competitors nearby can help (shoppers compare in a cluster of similar shops) or hurt (customers are split).

## Choosing a country

Weigh **costs** against **market access** and **risk**:

- labour costs, skills and productivity
- size and growth of the market; producing inside it avoids tariffs and quotas
- exchange rates: changing values make costs and revenues uncertain
- political stability
- infrastructure
- culture and language
- incentives and laws.

## Legal controls

Four effects to remember:

- **prevent** activity in some places (polluting work near homes, protected land)
- **delay** through permission to build or change the use of a site
- **add cost** through conditions on noise, hours, waste or emissions
- **encourage** location in areas needing jobs.

Describe the control and its effect. Do not make up the names of laws.

## Relocation

**Why move:** premises too small, rising rent or lease ending, customers or suppliers have moved, cheaper or better site available.
**One-off costs and risks:** moving costs, lost production, staff who leave, customers who do not follow, temporary damage to service.
Judge: do the ongoing benefits outweigh the one-off costs?

## Recommend and justify: checklist

- Choose one site or country.
- Use figures from the case (work out a total or a difference).
- Link the main factor to the type of business.
- Say why the other option is weaker.
- State what the decision depends on.

## Exam traps

- A generic list of factors with no link to the business in the question.
- "Cheap labour" without any mention of skills or productivity.
- Treating the lowest-rent site as automatically the best for a business that depends on passing trade.
- Forgetting legal controls can also **attract** businesses, through incentives.
- Mixing up tariffs (a tax) with quotas (a quantity limit).
- Ignoring the one-off costs of relocating.
- Copying figures from a table without calculating anything from them.

## Self-test

1. A sawmill turns logs into planks. Which location factor is likely to dominate, and why?
2. A brewery sells bottled drinks across one country. Which factor is likely to dominate?
3. Classify a mobile phone repair van, a dentist and an online bookshop into the three service types.
4. Why might a busy high street be the wrong place for a courier firm?
5. Give one reason a cluster of competitors can help a business.
6. How can locating a factory inside a foreign market help a business avoid trade barriers?
7. Why do exchange rate changes make locating abroad risky?
8. State two ways legal controls can affect a location decision.
9. A business receives a one-off grant of $20,000 to locate at Site X, but transport costs there are $6,000 a year higher than at Site Y. After how many years has the extra transport cost used up the grant?
10. Site P has rent of $3,200 a month. Site Q has rent of $2,450 a month but extra fuel costs of $500 a month. Which is cheaper, and by how much a year?
11. Give two one-off costs of relocation.
12. What should the final sentence of a "recommend and justify" answer do?

### Answers

1. Closeness to raw materials (the forests), because logs are heavier and bulkier than planks, so moving the inputs costs more than moving the output.
2. Closeness to the market, because bottled drinks are heavy and bulky to transport.
3. Repair van: goes to customers. Dentist: customers come to it. Online bookshop: delivered at a distance.
4. Its customers never visit, so it pays high rent for footfall it does not need, and traffic slows its vans.
5. Shoppers are drawn to an area where they can compare, so total customer numbers rise.
6. Goods made inside the country are not imports, so no import tariff is charged and import quotas do not limit them.
7. Costs and revenues in another currency change in value when the rate moves, so profit becomes less predictable.
8. Any two: preventing activity at a site; delaying it through permission; adding costs through conditions; encouraging location through incentives.
9. $20,000 ÷ $6,000 = 3.3, so in the fourth year the extra transport cost overtakes the grant.
10. Q costs $2,450 + $500 = $2,950 a month, $250 less than P; $250 × 12 = **$3,000 a year cheaper**.
11. Any two: moving equipment, lost production during the move, recruiting replacements for staff who leave, informing customers of the new address.
12. State what the decision depends on, such as whether sales forecasts are met.
