These questions cover section **3.1.6 Kinetics** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry
(9620) specification, Version 5.3, for International AS and A-level exams May/June 2020 onwards: points
3.1.6.1 to 3.1.6.5. All of it is International AS content, so nothing here is International A-level only.
The specification assesses it in Unit 2 (Organic 1 and Physical 1), where questions may also draw on
amount of substance (3.1.2).

Learn the ideas first in the [study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-kinetics/) and

## Questions

**1.** Define the term *activation energy*. **[1]**

**2.** State what is meant by the *rate of a reaction*, and give its unit when concentration is measured in
mol dm⁻³ and time in seconds. **[2]**

**3.** Nitrogen and oxygen molecules in the air collide constantly at 25 °C, but they do not react to a
noticeable extent. Explain why most of these collisions do not lead to a reaction. **[2]**

**4.** A student's Maxwell–Boltzmann sketch starts above zero on the y-axis, is symmetrical about its peak,
and meets the energy axis at high energy. Correct each fault, giving a reason. **[3]**

**5.** Curves A and B are drawn on squared paper for one gas sample at two temperatures. Each encloses 1600
small squares. To the right of Ea, curve A encloses 12
squares and curve B encloses 34 squares.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of molecules with E ≥ Ea for curve A. **[1]**
**(b)** State which curve is at the higher temperature, and calculate by what factor the proportion of
molecules with E ≥ Ea has increased. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the rate increases by roughly this factor, and not just by the change in collision
frequency. **[2]**

**6.** Bromine reacts with methanoic acid in aqueous solution, and the brown colour fades:

```
Br₂(aq) + HCOOH(aq) → 2Br⁻(aq) + 2H⁺(aq) + CO₂(g)
```

| Time / s | 0 | 240 | 480 |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Br₂] / mol dm⁻³ | 0.0150 | 0.0102 | 0.0072 |

**(a)** Calculate the mean rate of reaction over the first 240 s. Give the unit. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the mean rate between 240 s and 480 s. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain, in terms of collisions, why the rate in (b) is lower than the rate in (a). **[2]**

**7.** A student adds 0.300 g of zinc to 50 cm³ of sulfuric acid at a fixed temperature and collects the
hydrogen in a gas syringe: one run with 1.0 mol dm⁻³ acid, one with 0.50 mol dm⁻³. The acid is in excess.

```
Zn(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + H₂(g)
```

After 30 s the 1.0 mol dm⁻³ run has produced 54 cm³ of hydrogen and the 0.50 mol dm⁻³ run 29 cm³.

**(a)** Calculate the mean rate over the first 30 s for the 1.0 mol dm⁻³ run. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the same for the 0.50 mol dm⁻³ run. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain the difference between the two rates. **[3]**
**(d)** Calculate the maximum volume of hydrogen, in cm³, that 0.300 g of zinc can produce at 100 kPa and
293 K. Ar of Zn = 65.4; R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. **[3]**
**(e)** Explain why both runs finally collect the same volume of hydrogen. **[1]**

**8.** A gas decomposes slowly on heating, but much faster at the same temperature over a metal surface
that is unchanged at the end. Use a Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution to
explain how the metal increases the rate. **[4]**

**9.** For a gas-phase reaction at a fixed temperature, a distribution curve encloses 2000 small squares.
The area beyond Ea for the uncatalysed reaction is 3 squares. The area beyond Ea for the catalysed route is
57 squares.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of molecules with enough energy to react without and with the catalyst.
**[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the factor by which the catalyst increases this proportion. **[1]**
**(c)** The student weighs 0.250 g of the solid catalyst before the reaction. State the mass she should
recover afterwards and why. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain, using an enthalpy profile, whether the catalyst changes the enthalpy change of the reaction.
**[2]**

**10.** For a reaction with Ea = 60 kJ mol⁻¹, warming the mixture from 293 K to 303 K increases the mean
speed of the molecules by 1.7 % and makes the proportion of collisions with E ≥ Ea 2.25 times larger. A
student says: "The rate more than doubles mainly because the particles collide more often." Evaluate this
statement, using the data and the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution. **[5]**

**11.** A mixture of hydrogen and oxygen can be kept at room temperature without reacting, but it explodes
when a spark passes through it.

**(a)** Explain both observations in terms of collision theory. **[4]**
**(b)** The mixture is compressed to a higher pressure before the spark, at the same temperature. Explain why
the reaction is then faster. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** The minimum energy that particles must have when they collide for a reaction to occur [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers without the word "minimum", or that say "energy to start a reaction", are unlikely to earn this mark.

**2.** The change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time [1]; unit **mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹** [1].
**[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A unit with no time part, such as mol dm⁻³, does not describe a rate, so make sure the s⁻¹ is there.

**3.** Most of the molecules have energy less than the activation energy [1]; so the collisions do not supply
enough energy to break the bonds, and the molecules move apart unchanged [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* This is about energy, not frequency; "they do not collide often enough" misses the point.

**4.** The curve starts at the origin, because no molecule has zero energy [1]; it is not symmetrical, with a
long high-energy tail, so the mean energy lies to the right of the peak [1]; it approaches but never meets
the energy axis, because there is no maximum energy [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Give a reason with each correction; "start at zero" alone is weaker than "no molecule has zero energy".

**5. (a)** 12 ÷ 1600 × 100 = **0.75 %** [1].
**(b)** **Curve B** is at the higher temperature [1]; factor = 34 ÷ 12 = **2.8** [1].
**(c)** B has 2.8 times the proportion of molecules with E ≥ Ea, so 2.8 times as many collisions can
succeed [1]; the rise in collision frequency is tiny by comparison [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b), pick the hotter curve by its larger area beyond Ea, not by peak height; the hotter peak is lower.

**6. (a)** Change in [Br₂] = 0.0150 − 0.0102 = 0.0048 mol dm⁻³; rate = 0.0048 ÷ 240 [1] = **2.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹** [1].
**(b)** (0.0102 − 0.0072) ÷ 240 = 0.0030 ÷ 240 = **1.25 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹** (1.3 × 10⁻⁵) [1].
**(c)** Bromine and methanoic acid are used up, so their concentrations are lower in the second interval [1];
so collisions between reactant particles are less frequent and there are fewer successful collisions per
second [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Rates are positive even though [Br₂] falls; a negative answer or a missing s⁻¹ in the unit can cost credit.

**7. (a)** 54 ÷ 30 = **1.8 cm³ s⁻¹** [1].
**(b)** 29 ÷ 30 = **0.97 cm³ s⁻¹** [1].
**(c)** Each cm³ of the 1.0 mol dm⁻³ acid holds more H⁺ ions [1]; so they strike the zinc
more often [1]; the same fraction of strikes reaches Ea in both runs (same temperature), so more succeed
each second in the stronger acid [1].
**(d)** n(Zn) = 0.300 ÷ 65.4 = 4.59 × 10⁻³ mol, and n(H₂) = n(Zn) = 4.59 × 10⁻³ mol (1 : 1) [1];
V = nRT/p = 4.59 × 10⁻³ × 8.31 × 293 ÷ 100 000 = 1.12 × 10⁻⁴ m³ [1] = **112 cm³** [1].
**(e)** Zinc is the limiting reagent and its amount is the same in both runs, so the same amount of hydrogen
forms [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* In (d), p must be in Pa, so V comes out in m³; remember to multiply by 10⁶ for cm³.

**8.** The metal is a catalyst, offering a different pathway whose energy barrier is lower [1]. With no
change in temperature, a single, unmoved distribution curve applies [1]. Ea(cat) sits at lower energy than
Ea, so a bigger slice of the tail lies beyond it: more molecules can react [1]; hence more successful
collisions per second and a faster rate [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A sketch should show one curve with two Ea lines; a second, shifted curve shows a temperature change.

**9. (a)** Without: 3 ÷ 2000 × 100 = **0.15 %** [1]; with: 57 ÷ 2000 × 100 = **2.85 %** [1].
**(b)** 57 ÷ 3 = **19 times** [1].
**(c)** **0.250 g**, because a catalyst is not changed in amount (or chemical composition) by the reaction [1].
**(d)** No change: the catalysed route has a lower energy hump [1], but it starts and ends at the same
reactant and product enthalpies, so ΔH is the same [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* In (d), draw both routes between the same two levels, or the sketch contradicts your written answer.

**10.** Collision frequency rises by a factor of only about 1.017 [1]; the proportion with E ≥ Ea rises
2.25 times [1]. Overall the rate rises about 1.017 × 2.25 ≈ **2.3 times**, so it does more than double [1].
But the reason given is wrong: almost all of the rise comes from the larger proportion with E ≥ Ea [1].
Ea lies in the tail of the curve, so a shift to higher energy makes the area beyond Ea grow sharply [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* "Evaluate" needs a judgement backed by data: say which part is right (the rate) and which is wrong (the reason).

**11. (a)** At room temperature very few molecules have E ≥ Ea, so almost no collisions succeed [1]. The
spark gives nearby molecules energy above Ea, so they react [1]. The exothermic reaction heats the nearby
gas [1]; so a much larger proportion there have E ≥ Ea, and the reaction spreads rapidly as an explosion [1].
**(b)** At higher pressure there are more molecules per unit volume, so collisions are more frequent [1]; the
proportion with E ≥ Ea is unchanged, but there are more successful collisions per second [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* In (a), link each step with "so" or "because"; separate facts with no chain to rate are harder to credit.

## Where marks are usually lost

- An Ea definition with no "minimum".
- Picking the warmer curve because its peak is taller (it is shorter).
- Putting the temperature effect down to more frequent collisions only.
- Linking concentration or pressure to a change in molecular energy.
- Redrawing the curve for a catalyst instead of just adding a lower Ea marker.
- Omitting the new pathway, or claiming a catalyst alters ΔH.
- Negative rates, or rate units with no time part.
- Leaving a gas volume in m³ when cm³ was asked for.
