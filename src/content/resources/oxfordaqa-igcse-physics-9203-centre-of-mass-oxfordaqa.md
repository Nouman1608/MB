This guide covers **3.1.7 Centre of mass** (statements a to c) of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Physics (9203) specification, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. The section is marked P, so it is assessed in the full Physics award. The [terminal velocity, centre of mass, moments and levers guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-terminal-velocity-centre-of-mass-moments-and-levers/) gives the basic lamina method; this page goes further into why a suspended object hangs as it does, how to use symmetry, how to improve and evaluate the lamina experiment, and how the position of the centre of mass decides stability. Quick recall is in the [Centre of mass revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-centre-of-mass-oxfordaqa-revision-notes/) and exam-style questions are in the [Centre of mass practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-centre-of-mass-oxfordaqa-practice/).

## What the specification asks for

| Statement | Content |
|---|---|
| a | The centre of mass is the point at which the mass of an object may be thought to be concentrated; describe how to find the centre of mass of a thin lamina with an irregular shape |
| b | A freely suspended object comes to rest with its centre of mass directly below the point of suspension |
| c | The centre of mass of a symmetrical object is along the axis of symmetry; the position of the centre of mass affects the stability of objects |

A **lamina** is a thin, flat sheet, such as a piece of card or thin metal. Treating it as flat means its centre of mass lies on the sheet itself, which makes it easy to mark.

## What the centre of mass means

An object is made of many small pieces, each pulled down by gravity. The centre of mass is the single point where all of that mass can be treated as concentrated. In practice, this means the object's **weight acts at its centre of mass**. When you draw a free-body diagram for an extended object, start the weight arrow at the centre of mass.

The centre of mass does not have to be inside the material. A ring, a horseshoe or a boomerang has its centre of mass in the empty space between its arms. The definition still works: the mass behaves as if it were concentrated at that point.

## Using symmetry

Statement c says the centre of mass of a symmetrical object lies **along its axis of symmetry**. If an object has two or more axes of symmetry, the centre of mass must lie on every one of them, so it is where they cross.

| Uniform object | Axes of symmetry | Centre of mass |
|---|---|---|
| Rectangle | Two (through the midpoints of opposite sides) | Where the two axes cross, at the centre |
| Circle | Infinitely many (every diameter) | At the centre |
| Square | Four | At the centre, where the diagonals cross |
| Metre rule | Along its length and across the middle | At the 50 cm mark |

**Uniform** means the mass is spread evenly. A rectangle made half of wood and half of steel is symmetrical in shape but not in mass, so its centre of mass is shifted towards the steel half. Symmetry arguments only work when the mass is symmetrical too.

**Worked example 1: a shop sign.** A uniform rectangular sign is 1.20 m wide and 0.60 m tall. Its centre of mass lies on the vertical axis of symmetry, 0.60 m from each side edge, and on the horizontal axis of symmetry, 0.30 m from the top and bottom edges. Its weight acts at that point.

## Why a suspended object hangs with its centre of mass below the pivot

Hang an object from a point and let it swing freely. Two forces act on it: its weight, acting downwards at the centre of mass, and the upward force from the pivot.

- If the centre of mass is **not** directly below the pivot, the weight's line of action passes to one side of the pivot. The weight then has a turning effect (a moment, section 3.1.8) about the pivot, and the object swings.
- The only position with no turning effect is when the line of action of the weight passes **through** the pivot, which means the centre of mass is directly below it.

So the object settles with the pivot and centre of mass on one vertical line. This is the physics that makes the lamina method work.

**Worked example 2: hanging a sign by one corner.** If the sign from example 1 hangs from a hook at its top-left corner, it rotates until its centre is directly below the hook. The diagonal from that corner to the opposite corner passes through the centre, so the sign hangs with this diagonal vertical.

## Finding the centre of mass of an irregular lamina

1. Make three small holes near the edge of the lamina, well spread out.
2. Clamp a pin or nail horizontally in a stand, and hang the lamina from it through one hole so it swings freely.
3. Hang a plumb line (a thread with a small weight) from the same pin.
4. When both are still, mark two points along the plumb line on the lamina, one near the pin and one near the far edge.
5. Take the lamina down and rule a line through the marks.
6. Repeat from the second and third holes.
7. The centre of mass is where the lines cross.

Two holes are enough to find the crossing point; the third line is a check.

### Evaluating the method

If the three lines do not meet at a single point, they form a small triangle. The centre of mass lies inside it, and the size of the triangle shows how precise the method was.

| Problem | Improvement |
|---|---|
| Lamina sticks on the pin and does not hang freely | Use small, smooth holes on a thin pin; tap the stand gently before marking |
| Plumb line is still swinging | Wait until it is still, or let the plumb bob hang in a beaker of water to slow its swinging |
| Thick pencil line or thick thread | Use a sharp pencil and thin thread |
| Holes close together | Space the holes widely so the lines cross at a large angle |
| Lamina bends | Use stiffer card |

**Worked example 3: interpreting results.** A student's three lines form a triangle about 3 mm across on a lamina 20 cm wide. The best estimate of the centre of mass is the middle of the triangle. Because the triangle is small compared with the lamina, the method was precise; a larger triangle would suggest the lamina did not hang freely.

## Centre of mass and stability

An object rests safely while the vertical line through its centre of mass falls **inside** its base. If the object is tilted, it falls back to its original position as long as that line still lies inside the base. Once the line passes outside the edge of the base, the weight produces a moment that turns the object further over, and it topples (statement 3.1.8 c).

Two features make an object more stable:

- **A low centre of mass:** the object must tilt further before the line of action reaches the edge of the base.
- **A wide base:** the edge is further away from the line of action.

| Example | Design choice | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Racing car | Low body, wide wheelbase | Hard to tip over on bends |
| Bus | Heavy parts and luggage stored low | Lower centre of mass |
| Table lamp | Heavy, wide base | Line of action stays inside the base |
| Tall vase with flowers | High centre of mass, narrow base | Topples easily |

**Worked example 4: loading a van.** Moving heavy boxes from a roof rack to the floor of a van lowers its centre of mass. On a slope or a bend the van can then tilt further before the line of action of its weight passes outside the wheels, so it is less likely to topple.

## Common mistakes

- Saying the centre of mass is always "in the middle". That is true only for uniform symmetrical objects.
- Saying an object topples "because it is top-heavy" without mentioning the line of action of the weight and the base.
- Describing the lamina method without a plumb line or without repeating from a second hole.
- Saying a suspended object hangs with its centre of mass "level with" the pivot.
- Forgetting that the centre of mass can be outside the material.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Describe** the lamina method: numbered steps that someone could follow, naming the plumb line and the repeat.
- **Explain** why an object topples: refer to the line of action of the weight passing outside the base, and the resulting moment.
- **Suggest** improvements: link each one to the problem it solves.
- **State** where the weight acts: at the centre of mass.
