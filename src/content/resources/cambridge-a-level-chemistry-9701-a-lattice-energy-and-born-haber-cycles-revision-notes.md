**Syllabus:** Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027, **A Level** subtopic 23.1.
Born–Haber cycles are limited to +1 and +2 cations and −1 and −2 anions.

## Definitions and equations

| Term | Equation to write | Sign |
|---|---|---|
| ΔH<sub>at</sub> of a metal | Na(s) → Na(g) | + |
| ΔH<sub>at</sub> of a non-metal | ½O₂(g) → O(g); ½Br₂(l) → Br(g) | + |
| IE₁ | Ca(g) → Ca⁺(g) + e⁻ | + |
| IE₂ | Ca⁺(g) → Ca²⁺(g) + e⁻ | + |
| EA₁ | Cl(g) + e⁻ → Cl⁻(g) | − (usually) |
| EA₂ | O⁻(g) + e⁻ → O²⁻(g) | + |
| ΔH<sub>latt</sub> | Ca²⁺(g) + 2F⁻(g) → CaF₂(s) | − |
| ΔH<sub>f</sub> | Ca(s) + F₂(g) → CaF₂(s) | usually − |

Keys: **one mole** of the product species; **gaseous** atoms and ions; lattice energy is **formation** of
the solid from gaseous ions.

## Electron affinity in four points

1. Factors: **nuclear charge**, **distance** of outer shell from nucleus, **shielding**.
2. Down Groups 16 and 17 (from the second member): EA₁ **less exothermic**, because the outer shell is
   further away and more shielded.
3. **F and O are anomalous**: less exothermic than Cl and S, because their small outer shells are crowded
   and the extra electron is strongly repelled.
4. **EA₂ is endothermic**: an electron is forced onto an ion that is already negative.

## The cycle, step by step

    ΔH_f = ΔH_at(metal) + IE(s) + ΔH_at(non-metal) × atoms + EA(s) × atoms + ΔH_latt

| Compound type | Metal steps | Non-metal steps |
|---|---|---|
| MX (NaCl, KBr) | ΔH<sub>at</sub> + IE₁ | ΔH<sub>at</sub> + EA₁ |
| MX₂ (MgCl₂, CaF₂) | ΔH<sub>at</sub> + IE₁ + IE₂ | 2ΔH<sub>at</sub> + 2EA₁ |
| M₂X (Na₂O, K₂S) | 2ΔH<sub>at</sub> + 2IE₁ | ΔH<sub>at</sub> + EA₁ + EA₂ |
| MX (MgO, CaS) | ΔH<sub>at</sub> + IE₁ + IE₂ | ΔH<sub>at</sub> + EA₁ + EA₂ |

Drawing rules: elements at zero; endothermic arrows **up**; exothermic arrows **down**; state symbols on
every level.

## A calculation in four lines (CaO)

Data (kJ mol⁻¹): ΔH<sub>f</sub> = −635; ΔH<sub>at</sub>(Ca) = +178; IE₁ = +590; IE₂ = +1150;
ΔH<sub>at</sub>(O) = +248; EA₁(O) = −141; EA₂(O) = +798.

1. Sum of steps = 178 + 590 + 1150 + 248 − 141 + 798 = +2823
2. ΔH<sub>f</sub> = +2823 + ΔH<sub>latt</sub>
3. ΔH<sub>latt</sub> = −635 − 2823
4. ΔH<sub>latt</sub>(CaO) = **−3458 kJ mol⁻¹**

## Lattice energy: charge and radius

| Change | Effect on magnitude of ΔH<sub>latt</sub> |
|---|---|
| higher ionic charge | larger (more exothermic) |
| smaller ions | larger (more exothermic) |
| larger ions | smaller (less exothermic) |

Order examples: LiF > NaF > KF (cation size); NaF > NaCl > NaBr > NaI (anion size); MgO > NaF (charge).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Born–Haber cycle | an enthalpy cycle, using Hess's law, linking ΔH<sub>f</sub> of an ionic solid to atomisation, ionisation, electron affinity and lattice energy |
| Hess's law | the total enthalpy change for a reaction is independent of the route taken |
| Lattice energy | enthalpy change when 1 mol of an ionic solid forms from its gaseous ions |
| Electron affinity | enthalpy change when 1 mol of gaseous atoms (or 1− ions) each gain one electron |
| Atomisation | formation of 1 mol of gaseous atoms from the element in its standard state |

## Exam traps

- ΔH<sub>at</sub>(Cl) is half the Cl–Cl bond energy (per mole of **atoms**).
- **Double** the right steps for MX₂ or M₂X compounds.
- Include **IE₂** for 2+ cations and **EA₂** for 2− anions.
- EA₂ is **positive**.
- Lattice energy is **negative** in 9701 (formation from gaseous ions).
- Bromine is a liquid: its ΔH<sub>at</sub> includes vaporisation.

## Self-test

1. Define lattice energy as used in 9701.
2. Write the equation for the first electron affinity of sulfur.
3. Explain why the second electron affinity of oxygen is endothermic.
4. Explain why the first electron affinity of chlorine is more exothermic than that of bromine.
5. Explain why the first electron affinity of fluorine is less exothermic than that of chlorine.
6. Write the equation for the enthalpy change of atomisation of iodine, I₂(s).
7. Which steps must be doubled in a Born–Haber cycle for CaCl₂?
8. Which steps must be doubled for K₂O?
9. Given ΔH<sub>f</sub>(NaF) = −574, ΔH<sub>at</sub>(Na) = +107, IE₁(Na) = +494, ΔH<sub>at</sub>(F) = +79 and
   EA₁(F) = −328 (kJ mol⁻¹), calculate ΔH<sub>latt</sub>(NaF).
10. Predict which has the more exothermic lattice energy, KCl or CaCl₂, and explain.
11. Put LiCl, NaCl and KCl in order of decreasing magnitude of lattice energy.
12. Why is ΔH<sub>at</sub> always positive?

**Answers:**

1. The enthalpy change when 1 mol of an ionic compound is formed from its gaseous ions.
2. S(g) + e⁻ → S⁻(g).
3. The electron is added to a negative O⁻ ion, which repels it, so energy must be supplied.
4. Chlorine's outer shell is closer to the nucleus and less shielded, so the added electron is attracted
   more strongly.
5. Fluorine is very small, so the added electron enters a crowded shell and is strongly repelled by the
   electrons already there; this outweighs the greater nuclear attraction.
6. ½I₂(s) → I(g).
7. ΔH<sub>at</sub>(Cl) and EA₁(Cl), because there are two chlorine atoms.
8. ΔH<sub>at</sub>(K) and IE₁(K), because there are two potassium atoms.
9. Sum = 107 + 494 + 79 − 328 = +352; ΔH<sub>latt</sub> = −574 − 352 = −926 kJ mol⁻¹.
10. CaCl₂: Ca²⁺ has a higher charge than K⁺ (and is smaller), so its attraction to Cl⁻ is stronger.
11. LiCl > NaCl > KCl (the cation gets larger).
12. Energy is always needed to break bonds or overcome attractions to produce separate gaseous atoms.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need Born–Haber cycles for compounds like Al₂O₃?**
No. The 9701 syllabus limits cycles to +1 and +2 cations and −1 and −2 anions.

**Do I need to calculate lattice energies from ionic radii?**
No. The effect of ionic charge and ionic radius on lattice energy is required qualitatively only.
