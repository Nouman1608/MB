This guide goes deeper into section **3.12.2 Wave-particle duality** of the AQA AS and A-level Physics specification (7407/7408), part of the A-level only Turning points in physics option assessed in Paper 3 Section B. It has six content statements: 3.12.2.1 Newton's corpuscular theory of light, 3.12.2.2 significance of Young's double slits experiment, 3.12.2.3 electromagnetic waves, 3.12.2.4 the discovery of photoelectricity, 3.12.2.5 wave-particle duality, and 3.12.2.6 electron microscopes.

The story runs in one direction and then turns: light was thought to be particles, then shown to be a wave, then found to behave as particles too; and matter, thought to be particles, was found to behave as waves. Every exam answer in this sub-section is easier if you know where each experiment sits in that sequence.

## 3.12.2.1 Newton's corpuscular theory and Huygens' wave theory

**Newton**: light is a stream of tiny particles (corpuscles).

- **Reflection**: corpuscles bounce elastically off a surface, so the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection.
- **Refraction**: corpuscles are attracted towards the surface of a denser medium, gaining a component of velocity perpendicular to the surface. They bend towards the normal, so Newton's theory **predicts light travels faster in water than in air**.

**Huygens**: light is a wave. Every point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary wavelets, and the new wavefront is the surface touching them all. Refraction occurs because the wavelets travel more slowly in the denser medium, so **light is slower in water**.

**Why Newton's theory was preferred** for over a century:

- Newton's great reputation and scientific authority;
- it explained reflection, refraction and the straight-line travel of light with sharp shadows simply, while waves were expected to spread round obstacles noticeably;
- the deciding test, the speed of light in water, could not be measured at the time.

## 3.12.2.2 The significance of Young's double slits experiment

Light from a single source passes through two narrow, closely spaced slits and forms **alternate bright and dark fringes** on a screen. The explanation in general terms (no calculations are expected here): the slits act as **coherent** sources; where waves arrive **in phase** they superpose constructively, giving bright fringes; where they arrive **in antiphase** they cancel, giving dark fringes.

Significance: **interference** is a wave property that a stream of corpuscles cannot explain, so the experiment was strong evidence for the wave theory. Acceptance was nevertheless **delayed**, largely because Newton's authority was still dominant. The wave theory was firmly established once the speed of light in water was measured and found to be **lower** than in air, as Huygens predicted.

## 3.12.2.3 Electromagnetic waves

**Nature**: an electromagnetic wave consists of an oscillating electric field and an oscillating magnetic field, in phase, perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel. It is transverse and needs no medium.

**Maxwell** predicted that such waves travel in a vacuum at

**c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀)**

where ε₀ (permittivity of free space) relates to the electric field strength due to a charged object in free space and μ₀ (permeability of free space) relates to the magnetic flux density due to a current-carrying wire in free space. Both constants could be measured in laboratory experiments with charges and currents, and the result matched the measured speed of light, suggesting that **light is an electromagnetic wave**.

**Worked example 1.** Using μ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷ H m⁻¹ and c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹, find ε₀.

```
ε₀ = 1/(μ₀c²) = 1/(4π × 10⁻⁷ × 9.00 × 10¹⁶) = 8.84 × 10⁻¹² F m⁻¹
```

**Hertz** produced radio waves using a high-voltage spark gap (the oscillator) and detected them with a loop of wire with a small gap, in which sparks appeared when waves arrived. He showed the waves could be reflected, refracted and polarised. Reflecting them from a metal sheet produced **stationary waves**; moving the detector found nodes, half a wavelength apart. With the oscillator frequency known, **c = fλ** gave the speed of radio waves, equal to the speed of light.

**Worked example 2.** A detector finds nodes 0.30 m apart in front of a reflector, for waves of frequency 500 MHz (illustrative values): λ = 0.60 m, so c = 5.00 × 10⁸ × 0.60 = **3.0 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹**.

**Fizeau** measured the speed of light directly. Light passed through a gap between the teeth of a rotating toothed wheel, travelled to a distant mirror and back. At a certain rotation frequency, the returning light met the next tooth and was blocked. With N teeth and rotation frequency f, the time to move from a gap to the next tooth is t = 1/(2Nf), and c = 2d/t.

**Worked example 3.** N = 400, d = 7.5 km, first eclipse at 25 rev s⁻¹ (illustrative values):

```
t = 1/(2 × 400 × 25) = 5.0 × 10⁻⁵ s
c = 2 × 7500/5.0 × 10⁻⁵ = 3.0 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹
```

**Implication**: the measured speed of light agreed with Maxwell's predicted speed of electromagnetic waves, supporting the conclusion that light is an electromagnetic wave.

## 3.12.2.4 The discovery of photoelectricity

**Black-body radiation and the ultraviolet catastrophe.** A black body absorbs all radiation that falls on it, and its emission spectrum depends only on temperature. Measured curves rise to a peak and fall to zero at short wavelengths. Classical wave theory predicted that the intensity should keep rising as wavelength decreases, without limit: the **ultraviolet catastrophe**.

**Planck's interpretation**: energy is emitted and absorbed only in discrete **quanta** of energy E = hf. High-frequency quanta are large, so few are emitted, and the predicted curve falls at short wavelengths, matching experiment.

**Failure of classical wave theory for photoelectricity.** Wave theory predicted that:

- light of any frequency would release electrons if it were intense enough;
- with weak light there would be a delay while each electron absorbed enough energy;
- greater intensity would give electrons more kinetic energy.

Observations: there is a **threshold frequency** below which no electrons are emitted, however intense the light; emission is **instantaneous**; the maximum kinetic energy depends on **frequency only**, while intensity changes only the number of electrons.

**Einstein's explanation**: light consists of **photons**, each of energy hf. One electron absorbs one photon. It escapes only if hf ≥ φ (the work function), and E_k(max) = hf − φ. **Significance**: electromagnetic radiation has a particle nature as well as a wave nature.

**Worked example 4.** A metal has a work function of 2.3 eV (illustrative): threshold frequency f₀ = φ/h = 2.3 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹/6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ = 5.6 × 10¹⁴ Hz. Photons of 400 nm light have energy 3.1 eV, so E_k(max) = 0.8 eV.

## 3.12.2.5 Wave-particle duality

**de Broglie's hypothesis**: if waves can behave as particles, particles can behave as waves, with wavelength **λ = h/p**. For an electron accelerated from rest through V, eV = p²/2m, so p = √(2meV) and

**λ = h/√(2meV)**

**Worked example 5.** Electrons accelerated through 150 V:

```
λ = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴/√(2 × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 150) = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁰ m
```

This matches the spacing of atoms in a crystal, so crystals can diffract electrons.

**Low-energy electron diffraction.** A beam of electrons directed at a thin crystal (or a thin layer of graphite) produces a diffraction pattern of rings or spots on a fluorescent screen, which only waves can produce. **Effect of changing speed**: increasing the accelerating p.d. increases the speed and momentum, so λ decreases, there is less diffraction and the rings become **smaller**. At four times the p.d. (600 V), λ halves to 5.0 × 10⁻¹¹ m.

## 3.12.2.6 Electron microscopes

To resolve detail of the order of the size of an atom (about 10⁻¹⁰ m), the wavelength must be of a similar size or smaller. Visible light (about 500 nm) is thousands of times too long.

**Worked example 6.** Estimate the anode voltage for λ = 2.0 × 10⁻¹¹ m: V = h²/(2meλ²) = **3.8 × 10³ V**.

**Transmission electron microscope (TEM).** Electrons from a heated filament are accelerated by the anode. A **magnetic condenser lens** forms a parallel beam that passes through a very thin sample. A **magnetic objective lens** forms an image, and a **magnetic projector lens** magnifies it onto a fluorescent screen. Detail is limited because thicker samples slow the electrons (increasing their wavelength and spreading their speeds) and because of lens aberrations.

**Scanning tunnelling microscope (STM).** A very fine conducting tip, ending in a single atom or a few atoms, is held about 1 nm above a conducting surface with a small p.d. between them. Electrons cross the gap by **quantum tunnelling**, a wave effect. The tunnelling current depends very strongly on the gap width. In **constant-height mode** the current changes are recorded as the tip scans; in **constant-current mode** the tip is moved up and down to keep the current fixed, and its movements map the surface atom by atom.

## Common mistakes

- Saying Newton's theory predicted slower light in water.
- Calling Young's experiment "proof of photons".
- Saying intensity affects the kinetic energy of photoelectrons.
- Saying faster electrons give larger diffraction rings.
- Describing the STM as using an electron beam.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** each historical experiment as evidence for or against a model.
- **Compare** Newton and Huygens on the same point (refraction speed).
- **Calculate** λ from the accelerating p.d. with all values in SI units.
