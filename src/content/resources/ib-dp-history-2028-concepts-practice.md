These questions practise the four specified concepts of IB DP History (first assessment 2028). Section A checks the definitions. Section B applies the concepts to short examples, and Section C is written in the style of paper 2, section A (6 marks) using an example you choose from your own thematic study. Answers with marking points follow.

## Section A

**1.** Name the four specified historical concepts and say which two are interrelated pairs. **[3]**

**2.** State the three conceptual understandings of the concept of perspectives. **[3]**

**3.** In cause and consequence, what is the difference between a historical actor and a condition? **[2]**

## Section B

**4.** Read this constructed practice extract (constructed for practice).

> "The war ended in 1918 and the old empire was gone, but the same officials ran the ministries, the same families held the land and the railways ran on the same timetable. For ordinary villagers, the new flag mattered less than the price of bread."

**(a)** Which concept does the extract illustrate? Explain your choice. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain one way in which the extract shows that change was experienced differently by different groups. **[2]**

**5.** A student writes: "The Haitian Revolution was caused by slavery." Explain two ways in which this statement could be improved using the idea that causes are multiple and interrelated. **[4]**

**6.** Explain why historians say that the significance of an event is "constructed". **[3]**

## Section C

**7.** Using one example from a thematic study you have studied, analyse the concept of cause and consequence. **[6]**

**8.** Using one example from a thematic study you have studied, analyse the concept of continuity and change. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Cause and consequence; continuity and change; perspectives; significance **[2]**. The interrelated pairs are cause and consequence, and continuity and change **[1]**.

**2.** Any three: there are always diverse perspectives, held both by those who took part in or observed events and by those looking back later **[1]**; perspectives are not all equally valid and historians decide this by examining the claims **[1]**; perspectives can corroborate or contradict each other, leading to ambiguities that historians try to resolve **[1]**. (Award one mark for each correct understanding, up to 3.)

**3.** A historical actor is a person or group who takes action **[1]**. A condition is a circumstance within which actors operate (for example economic strain, social structures or ideas) **[1]**.

**4. (a)** Continuity and change **[1]**, because the extract shows that political change (the old empire has gone) happened alongside continuity (the same officials, landholders and railways) **[1]**.
**(b)** The extract suggests that the change in government (the "new flag") mattered less to villagers than their daily hardships such as the price of bread **[1]**, so political change was important for some groups and less so for others **[1]**. (Accept a reasoned alternative, such as that officials experienced continuity while villagers saw little benefit.)

**5.** Any two points, two marks each:
- Slavery was a long-term condition, but it needs actors to turn it into a revolution, for example enslaved people's resistance, the leaders of the revolt of 1791 and, later, Toussaint Louverture **[2]**.
- Other causes interrelate with slavery: the example of the French Revolution and its declaration of rights, divisions between planters, free people of colour and the enslaved, and the weakening of French authority **[2]**.
- Alternatively, distinguish short-term triggers (the 1791 rising) from long-term conditions (plantation society) and show links **[2]**.

**6.** Significance is not a fact in the past but a judgement made later **[1]**. Historians decide which people and events to include or leave out of a narrative, and their choices reflect the evidence they have and their own values and those of their society **[1]**. Marginalised people may be excluded and later reinterpreted as significant for what they reveal about the past **[1]**.

**7.** Marking points (up to 6). A strong answer will:
- briefly define cause and consequence as the interplay of multiple reasons for and results of events **[1]**;
- choose a specific, accurate example (for example the Salt March, the Great Leap Forward, the Enabling Act or the American Civil War) with names and dates **[1]**;
- distinguish actors from conditions within the example, with specific detail **[1 to 2]**;
- distinguish short-term and long-term causes or intended and accidental consequences **[1]**;
- show the example was not inevitable, or rank the causes **[1]**.
Award marks by the markband: 1 to 2 for description of the concept with minimal analysis, 3 to 4 for partial analysis with a relevant example, 5 to 6 for clear, accurate analysis effectively supported by a relevant, specific example.

**8.** Marking points (up to 6). A strong answer will:
- define continuity and change as societies becoming different while keeping similarity **[1]**;
- use a specific example with dates and detail, such as the British Industrial Revolution, the Meiji transformation or Pinochet's Chile **[1]**;
- identify at least one change and one continuity at the same time, showing that they are interwoven **[1 to 2]**;
- comment on pace (rapid or gradual) or on who experienced it positively or negatively **[1]**;
- reach a short judgement about the balance of change and continuity **[1]**.
Use the same markband as Question 7.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long should a section A answer be?** There is no fixed length. In the time available, a focused paragraph or two that defines, applies and judges is usually enough.

**Do I need dates?** Yes, accurate dates and names make an example specific, and specific examples are rewarded at the top of the markband.

**Can I reuse the same example for different concepts?** Yes, but analyse the concept in the question, not the whole story.
