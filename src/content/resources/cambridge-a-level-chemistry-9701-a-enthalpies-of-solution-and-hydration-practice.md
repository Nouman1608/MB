## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define the term *enthalpy change of hydration*. **[2]**

**2.** Define the term *enthalpy change of solution*. **[2]**

**3.** Which ion has the most exothermic enthalpy change of hydration?

A: K⁺

B: Ca²⁺

C: Mg²⁺

D: Na⁺

**[1]**

**4.** Which equation represents the enthalpy change of solution of sodium carbonate?

A: Na₂CO₃(s) → Na₂CO₃(aq)

B: 2Na⁺(g) + CO₃²⁻(g) → 2Na⁺(aq) + CO₃²⁻(aq)

C: Na₂CO₃(s) → 2Na⁺(aq) + CO₃²⁻(aq)

D: 2Na⁺(g) + CO₃²⁻(g) → Na₂CO₃(s)

**[1]**

**5.** Describe how water molecules are arranged around a hydrated Na⁺ ion and around a
hydrated Cl⁻ ion, and explain the difference. **[3]**

**6.** Explain why the enthalpy change of hydration of Li⁺ is more exothermic than that of
Na⁺. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** Rubidium chloride dissolves in water. Data: ΔH<sub>latt</sub>(RbCl) = −692 kJ mol⁻¹;
ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Rb⁺) = −301 kJ mol⁻¹; ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Cl⁻) = −364 kJ mol⁻¹.

**(a)** Describe an energy cycle linking these three quantities with ΔH<sub>sol</sub>(RbCl).
Name the species, with state symbols, at each level of the cycle. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate ΔH<sub>sol</sub>(RbCl). **[2]**

**(c)** State what the sign of your answer tells you about the temperature of the solution
as the solid dissolves. **[1]**

**8.** Data: ΔH<sub>sol</sub>(BaCl₂) = −13 kJ mol⁻¹; ΔH<sub>latt</sub>(BaCl₂) =
−2056 kJ mol⁻¹; ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Cl⁻) = −364 kJ mol⁻¹.

**(a)** Write the equation for ΔH<sub>sol</sub>(BaCl₂) in terms of the lattice energy and
hydration enthalpies. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Ba²⁺). **[2]**

**9.** Strontium chloride and magnesium chloride are both soluble in water. Data:
ΔH<sub>latt</sub>(SrCl₂) = −2156 kJ mol⁻¹; ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Sr²⁺) = −1480 kJ mol⁻¹;
ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Mg²⁺) = −1920 kJ mol⁻¹; ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Cl⁻) = −364 kJ mol⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate ΔH<sub>sol</sub>(SrCl₂). **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Sr²⁺) is less exothermic than ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Mg²⁺). **[2]**

**10.** Data: ΔH<sub>sol</sub>(NaI) = −8 kJ mol⁻¹; ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Na⁺) = −406 kJ mol⁻¹;
ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(I⁻) = −293 kJ mol⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate the lattice energy of sodium iodide. **[2]**

**(b)** ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Cl⁻) is −364 kJ mol⁻¹. Explain why ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(I⁻) is less
exothermic. **[2]**

**11.** Predict, with reasons, how the enthalpy change of hydration of Al³⁺ compares with
that of Mg²⁺. **[3]**

**12.** A student is given ΔH<sub>latt</sub>(CaF₂) = −2630 kJ mol⁻¹,
ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Ca²⁺) = −1650 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(F⁻) = −506 kJ mol⁻¹. The student
writes: ΔH<sub>sol</sub> = 2630 − 1650 − 506 = +474 kJ mol⁻¹.

**(a)** Identify the student's error. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the correct value of ΔH<sub>sol</sub>(CaF₂). **[2]**

**13.** 3.73 g of potassium chloride, KCl, is dissolved in 50.0 cm³ of water in an insulated
cup. The temperature falls by 4.1 °C. The specific heat capacity of the solution is
4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ and its density is 1.00 g cm⁻³. (A<sub>r</sub>: K = 39.1, Cl = 35.5)

**(a)** Calculate ΔH<sub>sol</sub>(KCl) in kJ mol⁻¹. **[3]**

**(b)** Given ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(K⁺) = −322 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Cl⁻) =
−364 kJ mol⁻¹, use your answer to (a) to calculate the lattice energy of KCl. **[2]**

**(c)** State one assumption made in the calculation in (a). **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** The enthalpy change when one mole of gaseous ions [1] dissolves in sufficient water to
form an infinitely dilute solution [1].

**2.** The enthalpy change when one mole of an ionic solid (compound) [1] dissolves in
sufficient water to form an infinitely dilute solution [1].

**3.** **C** [1]. Mg²⁺ has the highest charge (shared with Ca²⁺) and the smallest ionic
radius of the four.

**4.** **C** [1]. A is wrong because the aqueous solution contains separate ions; B is the sum
of hydration enthalpies; D is the lattice energy.

**5.** Around Na⁺ the water molecules point their δ⁻ oxygen atoms towards the ion [1]. Around
Cl⁻ they point their δ⁺ hydrogen atoms towards the ion [1]. Opposite partial charges are
attracted to each ion's charge (ion–dipole attraction) [1].

**6.** Li⁺ has a smaller ionic radius than Na⁺, with the same charge [1], so water dipoles get
closer and are attracted more strongly, releasing more energy [1].

**7. (a)** Top level: Rb⁺(g) + Cl⁻(g) [1]. Lattice energy arrow down to RbCl(s); hydration
arrow (ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Rb⁺) + ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Cl⁻)) down to Rb⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) [1].
ΔH<sub>sol</sub> arrow from RbCl(s) to Rb⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) [1].

**(b)** ΔH<sub>sol</sub> = −ΔH<sub>latt</sub> + ΣΔH<sub>hyd</sub> = +692 + (−301) + (−364) [1]
= **+27 kJ mol⁻¹** [1].

**(c)** Positive, so dissolving is endothermic: the solution temperature falls [1].

**8. (a)** ΔH<sub>sol</sub> = −ΔH<sub>latt</sub> + ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Ba²⁺) +
2ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Cl⁻) [1].

**(b)** −13 = +2056 + ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Ba²⁺) + 2(−364) [1], so ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Ba²⁺) =
−13 − 2056 + 728 = **−1341 kJ mol⁻¹** [1].

**9. (a)** ΔH<sub>sol</sub> = +2156 + (−1480) + 2(−364) [1] = **−52 kJ mol⁻¹** [1].

**(b)** Sr²⁺ has the same charge as Mg²⁺ but a larger ionic radius [1], so water molecules are
held further from the ion's centre and attracted less strongly, so less energy is released
[1].

**10. (a)** ΔH<sub>latt</sub> = ΣΔH<sub>hyd</sub> − ΔH<sub>sol</sub> [1] = (−406 − 293) −
(−8) = **−691 kJ mol⁻¹** [1].

**(b)** I⁻ has a larger ionic radius than Cl⁻ (same charge) [1], so its attraction to the δ⁺
hydrogen atoms of water is weaker and less energy is released [1].

**11.** ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Al³⁺) is more exothermic [1]. Al³⁺ has a greater charge than Mg²⁺
[1] and a smaller ionic radius [1], so it has a higher charge density and attracts water
dipoles more strongly.

**12. (a)** The hydration enthalpy of F⁻ has not been doubled, although CaF₂ contains two
fluoride ions [1].

**(b)** ΔH<sub>sol</sub> = +2630 − 1650 − 2(506) [1] = **−32 kJ mol⁻¹** [1].

**13. (a)** Energy absorbed: q = mcΔT = 50.0 × 4.18 × 4.1 = 857 J [1]. Moles of KCl =
3.73 ÷ 74.6 = 0.0500 mol [1]. ΔH<sub>sol</sub> = +857 ÷ 0.0500 = +17 100 J mol⁻¹ =
**+17.1 kJ mol⁻¹** (positive because the temperature fell) [1].

**(b)** ΔH<sub>latt</sub> = ΣΔH<sub>hyd</sub> − ΔH<sub>sol</sub> = (−322 − 364) − 17.1 [1] =
**−703 kJ mol⁻¹** [1].

**(c)** Any one: the solution has the same specific heat capacity as water; the mass of
solution is 50.0 g (the mass of the solid is ignored); no heat is exchanged with the
surroundings [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing the hydration equation from a solid, or the solution equation from gaseous ions.
- Using lattice energy with its original sign in the solution cycle.
- Forgetting to multiply the hydration enthalpy of an ion that appears twice in the formula.
- Giving a negative ΔH<sub>sol</sub> when the temperature of the solution falls.
- Explaining hydration trends with "atomic radius" or "size" instead of ionic radius and
  charge, or leaving out the link to how strongly water dipoles are attracted.
