## What this component actually asks

Since this course studies literary works exclusively, this component goes deeper into literary
technique specifically than the equivalent component in Language A: Language and Literature (which
must split its attention across literary and non-literary material). Focus areas: narrative voice,
poetic form, dramatic structure, and how a writer's specific craft choices shape what a reader
constructs from the text.

## Readers construct meaning -- they don't just receive it

The specification's central idea is that reading is active: two readers can construct genuinely
different, both defensible, interpretations of the same passage based on the meaning they build from a
writer's choices. A strong answer does not just identify a technique (e.g. "the writer uses a first-
person narrator") -- it explains what interpretive possibilities that specific choice opens up or
forecloses for the reader.

## Worked example: narrative voice and reader construction

A novel narrated in the first person by a character whose reliability the text gradually undermines.

```
Technique:        first-person narration
Initial effect:   reader trusts the narrator's account by default,
                  since first-person narration typically implies
                  direct access to the "truth" of events
Later shift:      as inconsistencies emerge, the reader is forced to
                  reconstruct events from a position of doubt rather
                  than trust
Analytical point: the writer's choice of an increasingly unreliable
                  first-person narrator actively shapes HOW a reader
                  constructs meaning over the course of the novel,
                  not just what information the reader receives
```

Explaining the *effect* of a technique on the reader's construction of meaning, not just naming the
technique, is what separates strong from adequate answers in this component.

## Genre and form across literary types

The course's works are selected across the literary forms defined on the IB's prescribed reading
list -- fiction, non-fiction, poetry and drama -- so revise this component's key terms
separately for each form rather than assuming they transfer directly: narrative voice and structure
apply differently to a novel than to a poem's speaker, and dramatic structure (staging, dialogue,
dramatic irony) introduces performance-specific considerations neither prose nor poetry share.

## Building this into Paper 1's guided analysis

Because Paper 1 sets two unseen literary passages for guided analysis -- SL students analysing one
of the two, HL students analysing both -- this component's core skill — explaining how a specific
technique shapes the reader's construction of meaning — is directly transferable exam practice, not
just background theory. When practising with unseen extracts, resist
the temptation to simply list techniques spotted in the passage; instead, select two or three genuinely
significant ones and trace their effect across the whole extract, since guided analysis rewards depth
of connected argument over breadth of feature-spotting.

## Connecting to studied works

For each of your studied works, prepare a short list of the writer's most distinctive techniques
(a particular narrative structure, a recurring type of imagery, a characteristic use of dialogue) along
with a clear account of how each shapes reader interpretation. This preparation serves both Paper 2's
comparative essay, where technique comparison across two works is directly rewarded, and the
individual oral, where close attention to how craft choices shape meaning is assessed throughout.

## Common mistakes

Naming a literary technique without explaining its effect on the reader's construction of meaning.
Applying prose-analysis habits unchanged to poetry or drama, without adjusting for each form's
distinct conventions. Treating "readers construct meaning" as a throwaway opening line rather than the
governing idea that should shape the whole analysis.

## Quick revision checklist

- For each studied work, identify at least one specific technique and explain its effect on how a
  reader constructs meaning, not just what it is called.
- Keep separate technique vocabulary for prose (fiction and non-fiction), poetry and drama rather than
  one undifferentiated list.
- Practise explaining how meaning might shift or develop for a reader across a text, not just at a
  single fixed point.

## A note on assessment weighting

Like the course's other two areas of exploration, Readers, writers and texts is not tested as a
separate, named exam section — its ideas are woven into Paper 1's guided literary analysis, Paper 2's
comparative essay and the individual oral alike. Revision is therefore most effective when it builds
transferable fluency in explaining technique-to-effect relationships, rather than treating this
component as isolated content to be recalled on its own.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the four literary forms in IB Language A: Literature?**
The IB's prescribed reading list defines four literary forms: fiction, non-fiction, poetry and drama. Revise technique vocabulary separately for each form rather than using one generic list.

**How is Readers, writers and texts different in Literature and in Language and Literature?**
Because IB DP Language A: Literature studies literary works only, this area goes deeper into literary technique than the same-named area in Language and Literature, which splits time with non-literary texts.

**How should you prepare studied works for Paper 2 and the oral?**
For each work, list the writer's most distinctive techniques, such as a narrative structure, a recurring type of imagery or a use of dialogue, with a clear account of how each shapes the reader's interpretation.
