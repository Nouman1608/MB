This guide covers topic 1.6 Urban settlements in Theme 1 (Population and settlement) of the Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026. The syllabus asks you to describe and give reasons for the characteristics of, and changes in, land use in urban areas, and to explain the problems of urban areas, their causes and possible solutions. The [Urban Settlements revision notes](/resources/igcse-geography-0460-urban-settlements-revision-notes/) summarise the zones, bid rent and a Rio de Janeiro case study; this guide adds the land use models, how land use changes over time, a problem-by-problem table of named solutions and a second case study built around solutions. Practise with the [Urban Settlements practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-geography-0460-urban-settlements-practice/).

## Models of urban land use

Models simplify real cities so that patterns can be compared. Two are widely taught.

- **Concentric zone model (Ernest Burgess, 1925, based on Chicago)**: rings around the **CBD**, then a zone in transition (old industry and housing), low-quality inner-city housing, medium-quality housing and high-quality suburbs or commuter zone. Land values and building density fall with distance from the centre; building age decreases outwards because the city grew outwards over time.
- **Sector model (Homer Hoyt, 1939)**: wedges or sectors extending from the CBD along transport routes. Industry follows railways, rivers or main roads, low-quality housing grows next to industry, and high-quality housing grows along the most attractive routes, away from noise and pollution.

Real cities usually show elements of both. A strong answer uses a model to explain a pattern and then notes where the real city differs.

## Characteristics of land use zones

| Zone | Characteristics | Reasons |
|---|---|---|
| **CBD** | Tall buildings; shops, offices, banks, hotels; high land values; few residents; heavy traffic by day | Most accessible point in the city, so firms compete for space; high rent means building upwards |
| **Inner city** | Older, high-density housing and former industry; in many high-income cities some areas are redeveloped or gentrified | Built when workers lived close to factories; old industry closed, leaving brownfield land |
| **Suburbs** | Lower-density, newer housing with gardens; local shops and schools; commuters | Built as public transport and car ownership allowed people to live further out on cheaper land |
| **Industrial areas** | Older industry near the centre beside rivers, canals and railways; modern industrial estates and business parks on the edge near main roads | Old industry needed water and rail transport; modern firms need space, cheap land and road access |
| **Rural-urban fringe** | Mix of farmland, out-of-town retail parks, business parks, airports, golf courses, new housing | Cheap, large plots, easy road access and space for expansion |

**Cities in lower-income countries.** The pattern differs in important ways. The CBD often has a modern core of offices and hotels surrounded by older markets and informal traders. Wealthy residents often live close to the CBD or in planned, high-quality areas along main routes, where services and security are best. Industry often follows main roads and railways out of the city. The fastest growth is frequently in informal or squatter settlements on the edge of the city or on unwanted land within it, such as steep slopes, flood plains and land beside railways.

## How land use changes

- **In the CBD**: competition from out-of-town shopping centres and online shopping has led many CBDs to pedestrianise streets, convert offices to housing and add leisure uses to bring people back.
- **In the inner city**: closed factories and docks leave brownfield land. Redevelopment brings new housing and offices; for example, London's former docks were redeveloped from the 1980s, with Canary Wharf becoming a major financial district. **Gentrification**, when better-off people move into and renovate older housing, improves buildings but can raise rents and push out lower-income residents.
- **At the rural-urban fringe**: **urban sprawl** is the uncontrolled spread of a city into surrounding countryside. It causes loss of farmland and habitats, longer commutes, more car use and higher costs of extending services such as water, sewers and buses. Planners respond with **green belts**, zones around a city where most building is restricted (London's green belt has been protected since the mid-twentieth century), and by encouraging building on brownfield sites.
- **With rapid urban growth**: in fast-growing cities, land use changes quickly and often without planning: farmland is built over, informal settlements expand and roads, water supply and waste collection fail to keep up.

## Problems, causes and named solutions

| Problem | Causes | Example of a solution | Evaluation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Traffic congestion** | Rising car ownership; commuting from suburbs; narrow old streets | London congestion charge (from February 2003); Singapore Electronic Road Pricing (from 1998); bus rapid transit in Curitiba, Brazil (from the 1970s) | Charges cut traffic in the zone but are unpopular and can shift congestion elsewhere; good public transport is needed as an alternative |
| **Air pollution** | Vehicle exhaust, industry, burning waste or fuel for cooking | Delhi switched its buses and auto-rickshaws to compressed natural gas in the early 2000s; low-emission zones | Improves one source, but growth in vehicle numbers and other sources (crop burning, construction dust) can outweigh gains |
| **Noise pollution** | Traffic, aircraft, construction, nightlife | Noise barriers beside roads; flight restrictions at night; zoning to separate homes and industry | Effective locally, but expensive and does not remove the source |
| **Water pollution** | Untreated sewage and industrial waste entering rivers; runoff from roads | Sewage treatment works; laws on industrial discharge | Works where enforced; costly for fast-growing cities |
| **Visual pollution** | Litter, graffiti, derelict buildings, advertising hoardings | Regeneration schemes; street cleaning; planning controls | Improves image and investment, but may be cosmetic if causes are not tackled |
| **Inequality and housing** | Low incomes, high land prices, shortage of affordable homes, informal settlements | Public housing; affordable housing quotas; upgrading informal settlements | Depends on cost to residents and whether communities are kept together |
| **Conflicts over land use change** | Competing demands for limited land, for example new housing versus green space or farmland | Planning inquiries and consultation; brownfield-first policies | Rarely satisfies all groups; compromise is usual |

## Case study: Singapore, solutions in a crowded city

Singapore is a small island city-state with very limited land, so it shows how planning can manage urban problems.

- **Housing**: the Housing and Development Board has built high-rise public flats in planned new towns across the island; around four in five residents live in them, most as owners. This has largely removed informal housing.
- **Traffic**: Singapore limits the number of cars through a quota system in which buyers must bid for a certificate of entitlement, and it charges drivers electronically for using busy roads at busy times (Electronic Road Pricing, from 1998). Its Mass Rapid Transit railway, opened in 1987, has been extended to most of the island.
- **Land**: land reclamation has increased the country's land area by about a quarter since independence, providing space for the port, industry and housing.
- **Evaluation**: congestion and informal housing are far lower than in many cities of similar size, but cars and housing are very expensive, and reclamation needs sand imported from other countries and alters coastal ecosystems.

Use Singapore with the Rio de Janeiro case study in your revision notes: Rio shows the problems of rapid growth and inequality; Singapore shows a strongly planned response. The syllabus allows "an urban area or urban areas", so either can be your main example.

## Model answer: explain a land use pattern

*Question: Explain why the CBD has tall buildings and high land values.*

> The CBD is the most accessible part of the city, because roads and public transport routes meet there (knowledge). Shops and offices want to locate where the most customers and workers can reach them, so they compete for the limited land (application). This competition pushes land values and rents up, so firms build upwards to make the most of each plot, which is why the CBD has the tallest buildings (analysis). However, as out-of-town centres and online shopping become more accessible, demand in some CBDs has fallen and land use is changing to housing and leisure (evaluation).

## Common errors

- Describing zones without explaining the reasons for their characteristics.
- Assuming cities in lower-income countries follow the same pattern as high-income ones.
- Naming a solution without a place or any evaluation.
- Confusing urban sprawl with urbanisation (an increase in the proportion of people living in towns and cities).
- Listing "pollution" without saying which type and what causes it.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** land use from a map or photograph: name the zone, give evidence (building height, density, function) and its position.
- **Explain**: link each characteristic to accessibility, land value, age or planning.
- **Suggest solutions**: name a method, say how it works and judge how well it would work.
- **To what extent**: weigh benefits against costs for different groups of people.
