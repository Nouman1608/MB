> **HL only. These are original questions written for Marlbridge** to practise Regional study 2 of Africa and the Middle East. They are not reproduced exam questions, and the real Paper 3 has essay questions only. The questions name Mali, Ashanti and the Zulu Kingdom, but in the exam you may use any one sub-Saharan state. Source extracts are constructed for practice. See the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-precolonial-african-states/) and [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-precolonial-african-states-revision-notes/).

## Section A: short answers

**1.** Identify two commodities that gave the Mali Empire its wealth. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why the Golden Stool mattered to the Ashanti state. **[3]**

**3.** Describe two changes Shaka made to the organisation of the Zulu army. **[3]**

## Section B: source and argument

*Constructed for practice, not a real source:* "The mansa did not need to conquer the desert. He only had to make sure that the caravans were safe on his side of it, and then wait for the gold to come to him." (a modern historian's summary)

**4.** Explain what this extract suggests about how the Mali state made its money. **[3]**

**5.** Explain one limitation of relying on Arab travellers' accounts to understand Mali. **[3]**

**6.** Examine why the Ashanti state was able to resist Britain for much of the nineteenth century before annexation in 1902. **[6]**

## Section C: essay questions

**7.** To what extent was the growth of one sub-Saharan African state due to its control of trade? **[15]**

**8.** Evaluate the view that internal divisions, not foreign pressure, were the main reason for the collapse of one sub-Saharan African state. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** One mark each for two of: gold; salt; copper; kola nuts; cloth; slaves traded north. **[2]**

**2.** One mark for what it was: the symbol of unity of the Akan clans around Kumasi, linked to Osei Tutu and Okomfo Anokye, about 1701. One mark for how it created unity: it was said to hold the soul of the nation, so the Asantehene's authority was sacred and the chiefs owed loyalty to it. One mark for consequence: it gave a shared identity that outlasted individual rulers, and the demand by the British for the Stool in 1900 sparked the War of the Golden Stool. **[3]**

**3.** One mark for each of two changes, with a mark for development of one: age regiments (*amabutho*) of young men under the king's command; training and a new weapon, the short stabbing spear used in close combat; the "buffalo horns" formation that encircled the enemy; discipline and loyalty to the king rather than to the clan; incorporation of defeated warriors. Maximum 3. **[3]**

**4.** One mark for the idea that the state profited from taxing or protecting trade rather than from conquering far lands; one for the link to gold and salt trade across the Sahara; one for noting the state's strength came from controlling the routes and the source regions. **[3]**

**5.** Award one mark for the limitation, one for explanation, one for example. Example: Ibn Battuta visited in 1352 to 1353 for a short time, saw mainly the court and towns, judged customs by the standards of his own Islamic society, and wrote down some details years afterwards; so his view of ordinary people, rural areas and non-Muslim practice is thin. **[3]**

**6.** Up to 6 marks. Credit points such as: the union of clans and the authority of the Asantehene; income from gold and trade that bought firearms; knowledge of forest terrain that made invasion difficult (Britain was defeated at Nsamankow in 1824 and the governor Charles MacCarthy was killed); British hesitation and cost; the Ashanti army's size. Credit counterpoints: the British sack of Kumasi in 1874; annexation in 1896; divisions among Ashanti chiefs and revolt of tributary peoples. Level guide: 1 to 2 for description, 3 to 4 for partial examination, 5 to 6 for a balanced examination with a clear conclusion. **[6]**

**7.** Marking points, up to 15 in total (understanding the argument, evaluation of perspectives, judgement):
- up to 3 for defining control of trade and choosing a state (for example Mali: gold and salt, caravan taxes, Timbuktu);
- up to 4 for evidence that trade drove growth (revenue, towns, ability to pay officials and an army, influence abroad);
- up to 4 for other factors and rival views (military leadership of Sundiata, alliances of clans, agriculture on the Niger, Islam and the court, ideology);
- up to 4 for a reasoned judgement, such as: trade sustained the state but leadership and alliance began it, so control of trade was the central, but not the sole, reason. **[15]**

**8.** Marking points, up to 15 in total:
- up to 3 for understanding the claim and choosing a state (for example the Zulu Kingdom or Ashanti);
- up to 4 for internal divisions (succession quarrels, rival claimants, civil war, disaffected tributaries; for the Zulu, conflict after Cetshwayo's defeat in 1879 and the division of the kingdom);
- up to 4 for foreign pressure (British armies, annexation in 1887 or 1902, loss of trade, settler demand for land);
- up to 4 for evaluation and judgement, including how the two interacted: Britain exploited existing divisions, so a clear answer will argue which was more decisive and why. **[15]**
