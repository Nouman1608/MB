This guide goes deeper into section **3.1.4 Plant tissues, organs and systems** of the OxfordAQA International
GCSE Biology (9201) specification, Version 5.2, for exams in May/June 2018 onwards. The qualification is untiered
and any part of the specification may be assessed on either paper.

The [Organisation study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-biology-organisation/) gives the outline for all of
Topic 1. This page concentrates on plant tissues, with new worked examples on leaf layers, stomata counts and a
dye experiment. Afterwards, use the
[Organisation revision notes](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-biology-organisation-revision-notes/) and the
[Plant tissues, organs and systems practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-9201-plant-tissues-organs-and-systems-practice/).
Transport in the xylem and phloem is developed in 3.2.2; here the focus is on structure and position.

## What 3.1.4 asks you to know

| Statements | Content |
|---|---|
| a | Examples of plant tissues: epidermal tissues, palisade mesophyll, spongy mesophyll, xylem and phloem, with the job of each |
| b | Plant organs include stems, roots and leaves |

The specification adds a limit: details of the internal structure of these organs are limited to **the leaf** and to
**the position of the xylem and phloem in a dicotyledonous primary root and primary stem**. You do not need the
internal structure of a stem or root beyond those positions.

## The plant tissues

| Tissue | What it does (as the specification words it) |
|---|---|
| Epidermal tissues | **Cover** the plant |
| Palisade mesophyll | **Carries out photosynthesis** |
| Spongy mesophyll | Has **air spaces** to facilitate diffusion of gases |
| Xylem and phloem | **Transport substances** around the plant |

Each tissue is a group of cells with similar structure and function, so the tissue name tells you the job of its
cells. The word *mesophyll* means "middle of the leaf"; the two types are named by how they look under the
microscope.

### Linking structure to function

- **Palisade mesophyll** cells are packed with chloroplasts, which absorb light energy to make food. They sit near
  the top of the leaf where the most light arrives.
- **Spongy mesophyll** cells are loosely packed. The gaps (air spaces) allow carbon dioxide and oxygen to diffuse
  through the leaf to and from the cells.
- **Epidermal tissue** covers the surfaces of the leaf. In the lower epidermis, small pores (stomata) allow gases
  to enter and leave.
- **Xylem and phloem** are tubes that run through the plant to transport substances.

## The leaf as an organ

The leaf is an organ because it contains several tissues. From the top of a leaf downwards, a typical section
shows the following.

```
upper epidermis       covers the top surface
palisade mesophyll    photosynthesis
spongy mesophyll      air spaces for gas diffusion
lower epidermis       covers the bottom surface (with stomata)
xylem and phloem      in the veins, running through the mesophyll
```

### Worked example 1: layer thicknesses in a leaf

Invented measurements from a micrograph of a leaf, in micrometres (μm).

| Layer | Thickness (μm) |
|---|---|
| Upper epidermis | 20 |
| Palisade mesophyll | 90 |
| Spongy mesophyll | 110 |
| Lower epidermis | 20 |

```
total thickness = 20 + 90 + 110 + 20 = 240 μm
palisade share = 90 ÷ 240 × 100 = 37.5%
spongy share   = 110 ÷ 240 × 100 = 45.8%  (3 s.f.)
epidermis total = 40 ÷ 240 × 100 = 16.7%  (3 s.f.)
```

Interpretation: the two mesophyll tissues make up most of the thickness, as expected for the tissues that carry
out photosynthesis and gas exchange. The percentages add to 100% (37.5 + 45.8 + 8.3 + 8.3 = 99.9%, due to rounding).

### Worked example 2: counting stomata

Invented data. A student presses nail varnish onto the upper and lower surfaces of a leaf, peels it off and counts
the stomata in a circular field of view with a diameter of 0.50 mm.

| Surface | Stomata counted |
|---|---|
| Upper | 3 |
| Lower | 14 |

```
radius = 0.25 mm
area of field = π × 0.25² = 0.196 mm²  (3 s.f.)
stomata per mm², upper = 3 ÷ 0.196 = 15.3 → about 15
stomata per mm², lower = 14 ÷ 0.196 = 71.3 → about 71
```

Interpretation: there are about five times as many stomata on the lower surface (71.3 ÷ 15.3 = 4.7). The lower
surface is shaded, so there is less evaporation of water than from the sunlit upper surface. The result from
one leaf, one field of view per surface, is not very reliable; taking several fields from several leaves and
calculating means would improve it.

## Stems and roots: the position of xylem and phloem

The specification limits the internal detail of stems and roots to **where the xylem and phloem are** in a
dicotyledonous (dicot) plant, in the primary stem and primary root.

| Organ | Position of xylem and phloem |
|---|---|
| Primary root | Xylem in the **centre**, usually in an X or star shape; phloem lies **between the arms** of the xylem |
| Primary stem | **Vascular bundles** arranged in a **ring** near the outer edge; in each bundle the xylem is on the inside and the phloem is on the outside |

Why does the arrangement differ? A root is pulled and must resist being dragged out of the soil, and a stem must
resist bending in the wind. You do not need the explanation for the exam, but it helps to remember the pattern:
**root, centre; stem, ring**. If you are shown a diagram of a section, check whether the tissues are in a central
star or in separate bundles around the edge, and write the answer from that.

### Worked example 3: working out the organ from the section

Invented stimulus. "In a cross-section, there is a central X-shaped region of xylem with phloem between its arms.
There are no separate bundles near the outside." State the organ and give a reason.

```
central X-shaped xylem, phloem between the arms → primary root of a dicot
separate bundles in a ring would indicate a primary stem instead
```

## Evidence for transport tissue: the dye experiment

A simple way to find the xylem is to stand a freshly cut stem in coloured water and cut sections later. Only the
xylem becomes coloured, because water moves up it.

### Worked example 4: speed of dye movement

A celery stem is placed in red dye at 09:00. At 12:00 the dye has reached 12 cm above the cut end.

```
time = 3 hours
speed = distance ÷ time = 12 ÷ 3 = 4 cm per hour
```

If the same stem were left for 8 hours at the same rate, the dye would be expected to reach 32 cm, provided
the stem is long enough and the conditions stay the same. Rates in plants change with light, temperature and
humidity, so a prediction like this is only approximate.

## Putting the organs and tissues together

| Organ | Tissues you can name | Job |
|---|---|---|
| Leaf | Epidermis, palisade mesophyll, spongy mesophyll, xylem, phloem | Photosynthesis and gas exchange |
| Stem | Xylem, phloem (in vascular bundles) | Transport; supports the plant |
| Root | Xylem, phloem (centre) | Transport; takes in water and ions |

## Common mistakes

- Saying the spongy mesophyll carries out most photosynthesis. The palisade mesophyll does this.
- Confusing xylem and phloem; the specification only asks you to say they **transport substances**, with the
  detailed jobs coming in 3.2.2.
- Placing the xylem outside the phloem in the stem. In a stem bundle, xylem is on the inside, and phloem on the
  outside.
- Describing the root xylem as scattered. It is central in a primary dicot root.
- Writing that the epidermis "carries out photosynthesis". It covers the plant.
- Calling the leaf a tissue. It is an organ.

## Exam technique

- Give the **tissue and its function** as a pair in each answer.
- When shown a leaf section, work from the top down and name each layer before answering.
- For a section of a stem or root, state the position of the xylem and phloem, then decide the organ.
- For rate or percentage questions, show the formula, the numbers and the unit.
