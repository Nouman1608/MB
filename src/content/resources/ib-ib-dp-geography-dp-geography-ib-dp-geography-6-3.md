## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Identify the stakeholders who influence food and health outcomes at different scales.
- Explain what each stakeholder controls, wants and does.
- Evaluate strategies that stakeholders use to improve food security and health.
- Judge who gains and who loses from those strategies.

## A method for analysing stakeholders

Listing stakeholders earns little credit. Analysing them does. For each stakeholder, ask four questions:

1. **What do they control?** Land, money, laws, technology, information or votes.
2. **What do they want?** Profit, re-election, health outcomes, livelihoods, cheap food.
3. **How do they act?** Investment, regulation, campaigning, buying choices.
4. **What limits them?** Budgets, competition, public opinion, international rules.

A **power-interest grid** then sorts stakeholders into four groups: high power and high interest (key players, such as national governments), high power but low interest (for example, investors in a food firm), low power but high interest (smallholder farmers, patients), and low power and low interest. A strong answer explains how low-power, high-interest groups can gain influence, for example by organising into cooperatives or campaigning through NGOs.

## Corporate concentration in the food system

Several stages of the global food system are dominated by a few large firms:

- **Seeds and agrochemicals.** Major mergers in the late 2010s, including Bayer's acquisition of Monsanto in 2018, left a small number of companies controlling much of the world's commercial seed and pesticide market.
- **Grain trading.** A handful of trading companies handle a large share of the world's traded grain and oilseeds, moving them from farm to port to processor.
- **Retail.** In many countries, a few supermarket chains sell most food, giving them strong **buyer power** over suppliers.

Concentration brings investment, research and efficient logistics. It also gives a few firms influence over prices paid to farmers, the varieties farmers can buy and the products consumers see. When evaluating, separate efficiency from fairness: the two can point in opposite directions.

### Contract farming

Under contract farming, a processor or retailer agrees in advance to buy a farmer's crop at an agreed price and quality, often supplying seed and advice. Farmers gain a secure market and access to inputs; they lose some independence and can be dropped if they miss quality standards. Small farmers may be excluded because buyers prefer fewer, larger suppliers.

## Certification: Fairtrade

Fairtrade certification sets a **minimum price** paid to producer organisations, intended to cover the cost of sustainable production when market prices fall, and a **premium** paid on top, which producer groups invest in community projects such as schools, clinics or processing equipment. Strengths: price security and community investment. Limits: certification costs, the fact that only part of a cooperative's crop may be sold on Fairtrade terms, and a focus on export crops rather than local food.

## Comparing forms of food aid

Food aid is not one strategy. Each form has different effects on local food systems.

| Form | How it works | Strength | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| In-kind aid | Donor ships food from its own country | Reliable where markets have collapsed | Slow, costly to ship; may undercut local farmers |
| Local and regional purchase | Agency buys food near the crisis | Faster, cheaper, supports nearby farmers | Can raise local prices if supply is short |
| Cash transfers | Households receive money | Choice and dignity; boosts local traders | Useless if markets have no food; prices may rise |
| Vouchers | Households exchange vouchers at approved traders | Targets spending on food | Administrative cost; limits choice |

Aid that is **tied**, meaning it must be spent on goods or services from the donor country, usually costs more per unit of benefit.

### Worked example: cost per person reached

An agency has 6,000,000 currency units for a food emergency. All figures are invented.

| Option | Cost per tonne of food delivered | Tonnes per person for the season |
|---|---|---|
| In-kind food shipped from donor | 1,200 | 0.1 |
| Food bought regionally | 800 | 0.1 |

- In-kind: cost per person = 1,200 x 0.1 = 120, so 6,000,000 / 120 = **50,000 people**.
- Regional purchase: cost per person = 800 x 0.1 = 80, so 6,000,000 / 80 = **75,000 people**.

Regional purchase reaches 25,000 more people, a 50% increase, for the same budget. The decision still depends on context: if regional markets are also short of food, buying there could push prices up for non-recipients, so in-kind aid may be the right choice despite its cost.

## Health stakeholders and access to medicines

Pharmaceutical firms invest heavily in research and recover the cost through **patents**, which give them exclusive rights for a period. International patent rules were strengthened under the World Trade Organization's agreement on intellectual property in the 1990s. Patents encourage innovation, but they raise prices, which can put medicines beyond the reach of low-income countries.

The tension became clear with antiretroviral drugs for HIV around 2000. Generic manufacturers, many in India, produced far cheaper versions, and in 2001 WTO members agreed the Doha Declaration, confirming that countries could take steps to protect public health, such as compulsory licensing. Prices fell sharply and treatment expanded in Africa. The case shows governments, firms, NGOs and international organisations negotiating over the same product with different goals.

At the other end of the scale, **community health workers**, local people trained to deliver basic care, vaccination and nutrition advice, extend health systems into villages that clinics cannot reach. They show how bottom-up delivery can carry top-down programmes the last mile.

## Annotated model answer

*Examine the role of transnational corporations in global food security.* **(sample paragraph)**

"Transnational corporations control key stages of the food system, from seeds and agrochemicals to grain trading and retail, so their investment decisions shape which crops are grown and how food moves **[what they control]**. Their research and logistics can raise yields and cut losses, supporting availability **[positive role]**. However, concentration gives a few firms buyer power over farmers and influence over prices, which can reduce smallholders' incomes and therefore their access to food **[negative role linked to a dimension of food security]**. Their influence is limited by competition law, consumer campaigns and certification schemes **[limits on power]**, so their overall role depends heavily on how governments regulate them **[judgement]**."

## Common mistakes

- Listing stakeholders without saying what each controls or wants.
- Treating all food aid as the same, or as always harmful or always helpful.
- Assuming corporate efficiency means fairness for farmers.
- Presenting patents only as a barrier, ignoring their role in funding research.
- Ignoring the household and community scale.

## Exam technique

- **Examine** a stakeholder's role with its powers, goals, actions and limits.
- **Discuss** a strategy from more than one stakeholder's viewpoint.
- **To what extent** is a stakeholder responsible: weigh it against others and take a position.
- **Evaluate** using effectiveness, cost, equity and sustainability, with a judgement for a named context.
