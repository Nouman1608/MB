These questions cover section 3.1.2 Resource allocation (markets and allocation of resources, economic sectors, and specialisation, division of labour and exchange) of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Economics (9214) specification, for exams from May/June 2025 onwards. All countries, firms and figures are invented, and money is in unnamed currency units. Revise first with the [resource allocation study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-economics-9214-resource-allocation-oxfordaqa-igcse-economics/) and the [resource allocation revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-economics-9214-resource-allocation-oxfordaqa-igcse-economics-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: multiple choice and short answers

**1.** A market is best described as: **[1]**
A. a building where goods are sold  B. an opportunity for buyers and sellers to interact in order to establish price  C. a list of prices set by government  D. a group of firms making the same product

**2.** In a factor market, households are usually: **[1]**
A. buyers of goods  B. sellers of goods  C. sellers of factors of production  D. buyers of labour

**3.** A car assembly plant is part of the: **[1]**
A. primary sector  B. secondary sector  C. tertiary sector  D. public sector only

**4.** Which of these is a service? **[1]**
A. a bag of rice  B. a bicycle  C. a bus journey  D. a tonne of copper

**5.** The division of labour means: **[1]**
A. a country producing a narrow range of exports  B. splitting production into separate tasks, each done by a different worker  C. paying workers different wages  D. sharing output equally between workers

**6.** Explain how a fall in demand for printed newspapers may lead to resources being reallocated to other uses. **[4]**

**7.** State whether each transaction takes place in a factor market or a product market.

**(a)** A family buys a fridge. **[1]**
**(b)** A hotel hires a cleaner. **[1]**
**(c)** A firm pays rent for a warehouse. **[1]**

**8.** A cotton farm sells cotton to a clothing factory, which sells clothes to a chain of shops.

**(a)** State the sector of the economy each business is in. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why the clothing shop is in the tertiary sector even though it sells goods. **[2]**

**9.** The table shows output in Country T.

| Sector | Year 1 (billion) | Year 20 (billion) |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | 36 | 30 |
| Secondary | 48 | 90 |
| Tertiary | 36 | 180 |
| Total | 120 | 300 |

**(a)** Calculate the primary sector's share of output in Year 1 and in Year 20. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the change in the secondary sector's share, in percentage points. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage change in secondary sector output. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain why the secondary sector's output rose while its share fell. **[2]**

### Section B: extended answers

**10.** Region X and Region Y each have 20 workers. In one day a worker can produce:

| | Rice (sacks) | Cloth (rolls) |
|---|---|---|
| Region X | 6 | 1 |
| Region Y | 1 | 4 |

**(a)** Each region puts half its workers on each product. Calculate the total output of rice and of cloth. **[2]**
**(b)** Each region now specialises in the product it is better at. Calculate the new total output of each product. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why specialisation means the two regions must exchange. **[2]**

**11.** A smartphone factory divides production into 30 separate tasks, each done by a different worker. Analyse the benefits and costs of this division of labour to the workers. **[6]**

**12.** Country V earns 900 million a year from exports, 75% of it from copper. Last year the world price of copper fell by 20%, while the amount exported stayed the same.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage fall in Country V's total export earnings. **[2]**
**(b)** Evaluate whether Country V should continue to specialise in producing copper. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** B [1]
*Examiner insight:* A market need not be a building; online markets count. Option B is the specification's own wording.

**2.** C [1]
*Examiner insight:* Households own the factors and sell them, for example their labour, to firms.

**3.** B [1]
*Examiner insight:* Assembling, processing and manufacturing are all secondary activities.

**4.** C [1]
*Examiner insight:* A bus journey is intangible and is consumed as it is provided.

**5.** B [1]
*Examiner insight:* Option A describes specialisation by a country, which is a wider idea.

**6.** Lower demand means newspaper prices or sales fall, so newspaper firms earn less profit [1]. This signals to producers that consumers want fewer newspapers, so some firms cut output or close [1]. The printing machinery, paper and workers they used are released [1], and they move to uses where demand and profit are rising, for example printing packaging or working for online news firms [1].
*Examiner insight:* Follow the chain from demand, through price and profit, to resources moving. Stopping at "firms lose money" earns only one mark.

**7. (a)** Product market [1]
**(b)** Factor market (labour market) [1]
**(c)** Factor market (for land) [1]
*Examiner insight:* Ask what is being bought: a finished product, or a factor of production used to make something.

**8. (a)** Cotton farm: primary [1]; clothing factory: secondary [1]; shop: tertiary [1].
**(b)** The shop does not make the clothes; it provides the service of selling them [1], such as stocking, displaying and advising customers, which is a tertiary activity [1].
*Examiner insight:* Classify a business by what it does, not by whether a physical product changes hands.

**9. (a)** Year 1: 36 ÷ 120 × 100 = **30%** [1]; Year 20: 30 ÷ 300 × 100 = **10%** [1].
**(b)** Year 1: 48 ÷ 120 × 100 = 40%; Year 20: 90 ÷ 300 × 100 = 30% [1]; change = **−10 percentage points** [1].
**(c)** (90 − 48) ÷ 48 × 100 = **+87.5%** [1].
**(d)** Total output grew from 120 to 300 billion [1], and the tertiary sector grew much faster (from 36 to 180 billion), so the secondary sector became a smaller share of a larger total even though its own output rose [1].
*Examiner insight:* Use "percentage points" for a change in a share and "per cent" for a change in output.

**10. (a)** Rice: 10 × 6 + 10 × 1 = **70 sacks** [1]; cloth: 10 × 1 + 10 × 4 = **50 rolls** [1].
**(b)** Rice: 20 × 6 = **120 sacks** [1]; cloth: 20 × 4 = **80 rolls** [1].
**(c)** Region X now produces no cloth and Region Y no rice [1], so each must trade its surplus for the other product to consume both, and both can end up with more of each than before [1].
*Examiner insight:* Part (c) needs the reason exchange is necessary, which is that each region no longer makes the other good.

**11.** Benefits: each worker repeats one task, so they become quicker and more skilled at it [1]; this raises their output, and in a factory paid partly by output it could raise their pay [1]; a worker needs to learn only one task, so they can start work quickly after short training [1]. Costs: doing one of 30 small tasks all day is repetitive, so workers may become bored and less motivated [1]; their skills are very narrow, so if their task is automated they may struggle to find another job [1]. On balance the benefits are mainly to output and pay in the short run, while the costs grow over time if workers stay on the same task for years [1].
*Examiner insight:* The question asks about workers only; benefits to the firm, such as lower unit costs, earn no credit here.

**12. (a)** Copper earnings = 0.75 × 900 = 675 million; a 20% fall = 135 million [1]; fall in total = 135 ÷ 900 × 100 = **15%** [1].
**(b)** Specialising lets Country V produce copper on a large scale, using its natural resources and skilled miners, so it can sell far more than it needs [1]. Copper earnings of 675 million pay for imports that Country V cannot produce cheaply [1]. However, 75% of exports from one product means a 20% price fall cut total earnings by 15% in a single year [1], so the country's income, and its ability to pay for imports, is very uncertain [1]. Copper is non-renewable, so earnings will fall when the mines are used up [1]. Diversifying into other products, such as farming or tourism, would spread the risk, but it takes time and those industries may be less productive at first [1]. Country V could keep mining while using some copper earnings to develop other industries [1]. Judgement: Country V should continue to specialise in copper for now, because it earns far more from copper than from any alternative, but it should reduce its reliance over time; the right balance depends on how often and how far the copper price falls and how long its reserves will last [1].
*Examiner insight:* Use your answer to (a) as evidence of the risk; a judgement that weighs today's earnings against future risk reaches the top marks.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a market a building or a place only.
- Mixing up buyers and sellers in factor markets.
- Confusing a fall in a sector's share with a fall in its output.
- Giving benefits to the firm when the question asks about workers.
- Not explaining why specialisation makes exchange necessary.
- Ending an evaluation without a judgement.
