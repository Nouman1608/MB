## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define what is meant by a **beanpole family**. **[2]**

**2.** Define what is meant by **living apart together**. **[2]**

**3.** Outline what is meant by a **transnational family**. **[2]**

**4.** Identify and briefly explain **two** reasons for a fall in infant mortality. **[4]**

**5.** Identify and briefly explain **two** effects of an ageing population on families. **[4]**

**6.** Identify and briefly explain **two** ways in which laws or social policies can affect family
structure. **[4]**

**7.** Identify and briefly explain **two** reasons why some couples choose to be child-free. **[4]**

### Section B: longer questions

**8.** Explain **two** ways in which internal migration may affect family life. **[8]**

**9.** Explain **two** criticisms of the view that industrialisation led to the decline of the
extended family. **[8]**

**10.** Explain **two** reasons why some sociologists see friends or pets as family members. **[8]**

**11.** Using examples from a society you know, evaluate the view that individualisation explains
the growth of family diversity. **[20]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that urbanisation has made families smaller and more isolated. **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** A family with several generations alive at the same time but few members in each generation
[1], because of falling fertility and rising life expectancy [1]. **[2]**

**2.** A committed couple in an intimate relationship who keep separate homes [1], by choice or
because of circumstances such as jobs in different cities [1]. **[2]**

**3.** A family whose members live in different countries [1] but keep shared family life through
decisions, care, communication and remittances across borders [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Two reasons, 2 marks each (identify [1], explain [1]):
- Clean water and sanitation [1]: reduce deadly infections such as diarrhoeal disease in babies [1].
- Immunisation [1]: protects infants against diseases such as measles [1].
- Better maternal health care [1]: trained birth attendants and antenatal care reduce deaths around
  birth [1].
- Better nutrition [1]: healthier mothers and babies resist illness [1].
**[4]**

**5.** Two effects, 2 marks each:
- Beanpole structure [1]: more generations alive but fewer siblings and cousins [1].
- Caring responsibilities [1]: the pivot generation cares for older parents as well as children [1].
- Grandparent roles [1]: healthy older people provide childcare and financial help [1].
**[4]**

**6.** Two ways, 2 marks each:
- Divorce law reform [1]: easier divorce increases single-parent and reconstituted families [1].
- Birth policies [1]: China's one-child policy produced many single-child families and a "4-2-1"
  structure [1].
- Welfare and housing [1]: benefits or affordable housing affect whether young adults or lone
  parents can live independently [1].
**[4]**

**7.** Two reasons, 2 marks each:
- Careers [1]: women's education and career opportunities raise the opportunity cost of children [1].
- Cost [1]: housing and education costs make children expensive [1].
- Individualisation [1]: couples prioritise their relationship, travel or personal goals [1].
- Reduced stigma [1]: less social pressure to become parents [1].
**[4]**

**8.** Two ways, 4 marks each (identify [1], explain [1], develop [1], evidence or example [1]):
- **Split households** [1]: parents moving to cities for work may leave children behind [1], so
  grandparents take on daily care and skipped-generation households form [1]; China's "left-behind
  children" of rural migrants are a clear example [1].
- **Changing household size** [1]: migrants to cities often live in smaller, more expensive housing
  [1], encouraging nuclear or single-person households [1], although remittances and visits keep
  extended kin networks alive across city and village [1].
**[8]**

**9.** Two criticisms, 4 marks each:
- **Nuclear households pre-dated industry** [1]: Laslett's research on parish records found nuclear
  households common in pre-industrial England [1], so the extended family was not dominant to begin
  with [1], weakening the idea of a decline caused by industry [1].
- **Extended families grew in early industrial towns** [1]: Anderson's Preston study using the 1851
  census [1] found kin shared housing to cope with poverty, illness and finding work [1], so
  industrialisation could strengthen extended ties [1].
- Credit: Young and Willmott found strong extended kin networks in working-class East London in the
  1950s; modified extended families persist through contact rather than co-residence.
**[8]**

**10.** Two reasons, 4 marks each:
- **Friends perform family roles** [1]: friends give care, emotional support and practical help [1],
  especially where kin are distant or unaccepting [1]; Weeks and colleagues called these families of
  choice, and Pahl and Spencer describe personal communities [1].
- **Pets as kin** [1]: people describe companion animals as family members and grieve for them [1];
  Charles and Davies found pets talked about in kinship terms [1], and Smart's concept of personal
  life includes such bonds [1].
**[8]**

**11.** Levels of response:
- 17–20: detailed knowledge; sustained application to a named society; balanced evaluation and a
  justified conclusion.
- 13–16: good knowledge and application; some evaluation.
- 9–12: reasonable knowledge; limited evaluation.
- 5–8: basic, descriptive.
- 1–4: fragments.

Indicative content. *For:* Beck and Beck-Gernsheim argue traditional scripts have weakened, so
people choose relationships; Giddens' pure relationship explains more divorce and reconstituted
families; LAT and child-free couples reflect personal choice; Stacey's postmodern families show
women creating new forms. *Against:* Smart argues choices remain embedded in relationships, memory
and obligation; demographic change (ageing, low fertility) creates beanpole and pivot families
regardless of choice; laws and policies, such as birth limits or divorce reform, shape structures;
economic pressures, such as housing costs, create boomerang and multigenerational households;
migration creates transnational families for economic reasons; class and culture limit choice.
*Application:* reward specific examples from a named society. *Conclusion:* supported judgement,
for example that individualisation explains some urban, educated groups' choices but structural
forces explain more. **[20]**

**12.** Levels of response as for Question 11.

Indicative content. *For:* city housing is smaller and costly; migrants are separated from rural kin;
urban jobs reward individual mobility (Parsons, Goode); more single-person households in cities
(Klinenberg on living alone); childcare moves from kin to paid services. *Against:* Anderson found
extended households in industrial towns; Young and Willmott found strong kin networks in East
London; modern communication keeps distant kin close; remittances and visits link city and village
families; urban areas host diaspora communities with strong kin and community ties; isolation can
be overstated, as friends and personal communities provide support (Pahl and Spencer). *Conclusion:*
households may have shrunk, but networks have not necessarily become isolated. **[20]**
