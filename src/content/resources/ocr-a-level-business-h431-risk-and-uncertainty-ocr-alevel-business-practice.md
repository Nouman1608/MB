## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain the usual relationship between risk and reward. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between quantifiable and unquantifiable risk. **[3]**

**3.** Keldry Events estimates that a new festival will make a net gain of £45,000 with probability 0.3, £15,000 with probability 0.5, and a loss of £25,000 with probability 0.2. Calculate the expected value. **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** internal cause of uncertainty for a newly opened restaurant. **[2]**

**5.** Which **one** of the following is an example of transferring a risk?

A: Training staff in food hygiene.

B: Taking out insurance against fire damage.

C: Refusing to sell on credit.

D: Keeping a cash reserve. **[1]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Holloway Garden Rooms builds wooden garden offices. It has cash reserves of £35,000 and must choose one option. Figures are net gains over three years.

Option A, a show site near a motorway: £90,000 with probability 0.5, or £30,000 with probability 0.5.

Option B, exporting to Ireland: £200,000 with probability 0.4, £20,000 with probability 0.2, or a loss of £40,000 with probability 0.4.

**(a)** Calculate the expected value of each option. **[4]**

**(b)** Evaluate which option Holloway Garden Rooms should choose. **[8]**

**7.** Brookside Care Home has identified four risks and scored each for likelihood and impact on a scale of 1 to 5.

| Risk | Likelihood | Impact |
|---|---|---|
| Agency staff costs rise sharply | 4 | 3 |
| Failing an official inspection | 2 | 5 |
| A food poisoning outbreak | 1 | 5 |
| Boiler failure in winter | 3 | 3 |

**(a)** Calculate a score for each risk and rank them. **[3]**

**(b)** Recommend and justify a response to the highest-ranked risk. **[4]**

**8.** Owen is leaving a job that pays £45,000 a year to open a bicycle repair shop as a sole trader. He is investing £30,000 of savings and borrowing £20,000 from a bank. He forecasts a profit of £28,000 in the first year. Evaluate the specific risks Owen faces as an entrepreneur. **[9]**

**9.** Tregar Ski Holidays is a UK business that sells skiing holidays in Austria. It sets its prices in pounds a year in advance, but pays hotels in euros. Bookings also depend on how much snow falls.

**(a)** Using Tregar Ski Holidays as an example, explain the difference between risk and uncertainty. **[3]**

**(b)** Analyse how uncertainty affects Tregar's planning and forecasting. **[6]**

**10.** Fenmoor Foods makes ready meals for supermarkets. A failure in its hygiene checks led to a product recall costing £400,000. Its annual profit is usually about £250,000. Two supermarkets have suspended orders. Evaluate the consequences of this poor risk management for Fenmoor Foods and its stakeholders. **[9]**

**11.** Pellow Builders employs 18 people and has been offered a contract to build 40 houses, three times larger than any job it has done before. Evaluate **two** ways in which Pellow Builders could reduce the risk of accepting this contract. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Options with higher potential reward usually carry a higher chance of loss [1], because they involve new markets, new products or more competition, where outcomes are less certain [1]. **[2]**

**2.** A quantifiable risk can be measured from data, so its likelihood or cost can be estimated [1]. An unquantifiable risk has no reliable basis for a number [1]. Quantifiable risks can be insured or provided for; unquantifiable risks can only be managed through flexibility and reserves [1]. **[3]**

**3.** (0.3 × 45,000) + (0.5 × 15,000) + (0.2 × −25,000) = 13,500 + 7,500 − 5,000 [1] = **£16,000** [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Whether new kitchen staff can cook consistently to the standard needed [1], because the owner cannot know how they will perform until the restaurant is busy, so quality and reviews are uncertain [1]. Also accept equipment reliability or the accuracy of cost estimates. **[2]**

**5.** **B** [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** Option A: (0.5 × 90,000) + (0.5 × 30,000) [1] = **£60,000** [1]. Option B: (0.4 × 200,000) + (0.2 × 20,000) + (0.4 × −40,000) = 80,000 + 4,000 − 16,000 [1] = **£68,000** [1]. **[4]**

**(b)** Option B has the higher expected value, by £8,000 [1], and its best outcome, £200,000, is far above anything A offers [1].
But B has a 40% chance of a £40,000 loss [1], which is more than Holloway's cash reserves of £35,000, so the worst case could threaten the business's survival [1]. Option A cannot make a loss on these figures [1].
The probabilities for B relate to a market Holloway has never sold in, so they are less reliable than those for A [1].
Judgement: Holloway should choose Option A, because a small business should not take a 40% chance of a loss larger than its reserves for an extra £8,000 of expected value [1]. This would change if Holloway could test the Irish market with a few orders first, or if it had more reserves [1]. **[8]**

**7. (a)** Agency staff 4 × 3 = 12; inspection 2 × 5 = 10; boiler 3 × 3 = 9; food poisoning 1 × 5 = 5 [1] [1]. Ranking: agency staff costs, inspection, boiler, food poisoning [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Reduce the risk by recruiting more permanent staff or a bank of the home's own relief staff [1], so that fewer shifts need expensive agency cover [1]. This is better than accepting the risk because staff costs are a large, recurring cost and the likelihood is high [1]; the trade-off is that permanent staff are paid even when occupancy is low, so the home should recruit to its usual minimum level rather than its peak [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Income risk: Owen's forecast profit of £28,000 is £17,000 below his salary [1], and it is only a forecast, so he could earn much less in year 1 [1].
Capital risk: his £30,000 of savings could be lost if the shop fails [1].
Liability: as a sole trader he has unlimited liability, so if the business cannot repay the £20,000 loan, his personal assets, possibly his home, could be used to repay it [1].
Personal: he loses employment benefits such as sick pay and a pension, and faces long hours [1].
Rewards to set against these: independence, all profits if the shop grows, and a business that may later be sold [1].
Evaluation: the risks are significant because he has put his savings in and borrowed on top [1]. They would be smaller if he had experience of bicycle repair and evidence of local demand, or if he formed a private limited company [1]. Judgement: the venture is reasonable only if he has savings beyond the £30,000 to live on while the shop builds up, since the first year's income is uncertain [1]. **[9]**

**9. (a)** Risk can be measured: Tregar can use past records to estimate, for example, how many customers cancel [1]. Uncertainty cannot be given a reliable probability [1]: Tregar cannot know next winter's exchange rate or how much snow will fall [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Planning: because prices are fixed a year ahead but hotel costs are paid in euros, a fall in the pound would raise costs without raising revenue [1], so Tregar's budgets may need to plan for several exchange rates [1]; it may fix the exchange rate with its bank in advance to make the cost certain [1].
Forecasting: bookings depend on snow, which is unpredictable [1], so a single sales forecast is unreliable [1]; Tregar is more likely to forecast a range of bookings and plan staffing and hotel contracts flexibly, for example with cancellation terms [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Business: the recall cost of £400,000 is 1.6 times its usual annual profit [1], so Fenmoor is likely to make a loss this year [1]. The suspended orders cut future revenue [1], and its reputation for food safety, which matters greatly to supermarkets, has been damaged [1].
Stakeholders: employees may face reduced hours or redundancy if orders do not return [1]; owners lose profit and possibly the value of the business [1]; supermarkets face empty shelves and their own reputational risk, so they may switch suppliers permanently [1].
Evaluation: the severity depends on whether Fenmoor has reserves to absorb the loss and whether the supermarkets return after improvements [1]. Judgement: the reputational damage is likely to be more serious than the one-off cost, because lost supermarket contracts would reduce revenue for years [1]. **[9]**

**11.** Phasing or staged contracts: Pellow could agree to build the houses in phases, with payment at each stage [1], so cash comes in as costs are paid and a problem in phase one does not commit the firm to all 40 houses [1]. However, the client may not agree, or may pay more to a rival that will take the whole job [1].
Subcontracting part of the work: Pellow could use other firms for specialist tasks such as roofing [1], so it does not need to hire and train many new staff quickly [1]. The limitation is less control over quality and timing, and a lower profit margin [1].
Evaluation: staged payments are more important, because the main risk for a small firm on a job three times its normal size is running out of cash before it is paid [1]. Judgement: Pellow should accept only if staged payments are agreed, combined with subcontracting to cover the skills gap [1]. **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Choosing the option with the highest expected value without checking the worst case.
- Treating risk and uncertainty as the same thing.
- Saying an entrepreneur "could lose everything" without referring to liability.
- Listing ways to reduce risk without saying what each costs.
- Ignoring the size of a loss relative to the business's resources.
