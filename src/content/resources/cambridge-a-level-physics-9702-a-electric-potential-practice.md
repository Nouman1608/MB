## Section A

**1.** Define electric potential at a point. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why the electric potential near an isolated negative point charge is negative. **[2]**

**3.** Calculate the electric potential 0.25 m from a point charge of +5.0 nC, and use your answer to find the electric field strength at the same point. **[3]**

**4.** Two equipotential surfaces 2.0 mm apart differ in potential by 5.0 V. Calculate the average electric field strength between them. **[2]**

**5.** Charges of +2.0 μC and −3.0 μC are 0.15 m apart. Calculate their electric potential energy, and explain the meaning of its sign. **[3]**

**6.** Calculate the work done in bringing a charge of +1.0 nC from infinity to a point where the potential is 450 V. **[1]**

## Section B

**7.** A charge of +6.0 nC is at x = 0 and a charge of −2.0 nC is at x = 0.40 m.

**(a)** Calculate the electric potential at x = 0.20 m. **[2]**

**(b)** Determine the position between the charges where the potential is zero. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the electric field strength is not zero at the point found in (b). **[2]**

**8.** The potential 0.10 m from a small charged sphere is +900 V. The sphere may be treated as a point charge.

**(a)** Calculate the charge on the sphere. **[2]**

**(b)** State the potential 0.30 m from the sphere. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the electric field strength 0.10 m from the sphere. **[2]**

**(d)** Calculate the work done in moving a charge of +3.0 nC from 0.30 m to 0.10 m from the sphere. **[2]**

**9.** An alpha particle (charge +2e) with kinetic energy 6.4 × 10⁻¹³ J travels directly towards a stationary tin nucleus (charge +50e).

**(a)** Explain what happens to the kinetic energy of the alpha particle as it approaches the nucleus. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the distance of closest approach. **[3]**

**10.** Two protons are held at rest 2.0 × 10⁻¹² m apart and then released.

**(a)** Calculate their initial electric potential energy. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the speed of each proton when they are very far apart. **[2]**

**11.** Describe the equipotential surfaces around an isolated point charge, drawn at equal intervals of potential, and explain why their spacing changes with distance from the charge. **[3]**

**12.** An electron is released from rest at a point where the potential is −200 V and moves to a point where the potential is +300 V.

**(a)** Calculate the change in its electric potential energy. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate its speed at the second point. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** The **work done per unit positive charge** [1] in bringing a small test charge **from infinity** to that point [1].

**2.** The negative charge **attracts** a positive test charge [1], so the work done by an external agent bringing it from infinity is **negative**; as potential is zero at infinity, the potential is negative [1].

**3.** V = 8.99 × 10⁹ × 5.0 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ 0.25 [1] = **180 V** [1]. For a point charge E = V/r = 180 ÷ 0.25 = **720 V m⁻¹** [1].

**4.** E = ΔV ÷ Δx = 5.0 ÷ 2.0 × 10⁻³ [1] = **2.5 × 10³ V m⁻¹** [1].

**5.** E_P = 8.99 × 10⁹ × (2.0 × 10⁻⁶)(−3.0 × 10⁻⁶) ÷ 0.15 [1] = **−0.36 J** [1]. Negative: the charges attract, and work must be **done on the system to separate them** to infinity [1].

**6.** W = qV = 1.0 × 10⁻⁹ × 450 = **4.5 × 10⁻⁷ J** [1].

**7. (a)** V = 8.99 × 10⁹ × (6.0 × 10⁻⁹ − 2.0 × 10⁻⁹) ÷ 0.20 [1] = **180 V** [1].

**(b)** 6.0/x = 2.0/(0.40 − x) [1], so 6(0.40 − x) = 2x and x = **0.30 m** from the +6.0 nC charge [1].

**(c)** Potentials add as scalars and can cancel, but fields add as vectors [1]; between the charges both fields point **towards the negative charge**, so they add and E is not zero [1].

**8. (a)** Q = Vr ÷ 8.99 × 10⁹ = 900 × 0.10 ÷ 8.99 × 10⁹ [1] = **1.0 × 10⁻⁸ C** [1].

**(b)** V ∝ 1/r, so **300 V** [1].

**(c)** E = V/r = 900 ÷ 0.10 [1] = **9.0 × 10³ V m⁻¹** [1].

**(d)** W = qΔV = 3.0 × 10⁻⁹ × (900 − 300) [1] = **1.8 × 10⁻⁶ J** [1].

**9. (a)** It is converted to **electric potential energy** as the alpha particle slows against the repulsion, until all of it has been converted at closest approach [1].

**(b)** E_K = Qq/(4πε₀r) [1]; r = 8.99 × 10⁹ × (50 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹)(2 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹) ÷ 6.4 × 10⁻¹³ [1] = **3.6 × 10⁻¹⁴ m** [1].

**10. (a)** E_P = 8.99 × 10⁹ × (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹)² ÷ 2.0 × 10⁻¹² [1] = **1.15 × 10⁻¹⁶ J** [1].

**(b)** Equal masses, so each gains half: ½mv² = 5.75 × 10⁻¹⁷ J [1]; v = √(2 × 5.75 × 10⁻¹⁷ ÷ 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷) = **2.6 × 10⁵ m s⁻¹** [1].

**11.** The equipotentials are **concentric spheres** centred on the charge [1], perpendicular to the radial field lines [1]. They get **further apart** with distance because the field strength (the potential gradient) decreases, so a greater distance is needed for the same change in potential [1].

**12. (a)** ΔE_P = qΔV = (−1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹) × (300 − (−200)) [1] = **−8.0 × 10⁻¹⁷ J** (a decrease) [1].

**(b)** E_K = 8.0 × 10⁻¹⁷ J, so v = √(2 × 8.0 × 10⁻¹⁷ ÷ 9.11 × 10⁻³¹) = **1.3 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹** [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using r² in the potential or potential-energy formula.
- Ignoring the sign of a negative charge when adding potentials.
- Assuming the field is zero where the potential is zero.
- Omitting "from infinity" or "positive" in the definition.
- Forgetting that two equal particles released from rest share the energy equally.
- Getting the sign of the potential-energy change wrong for a negative charge.
