**These notes condense Topic 3 of Cambridge 9489 Paper 3, the origins and development of the Cold War, for fast recall.** The key question is "Who or what was to blame for the Cold War?" For fuller explanation and a worked reading of an extract see the [Cold War interpretations study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-world-history-9489-origins-development-cold-war/); for original extracts to practise on see the [Cold War interpretations practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-world-history-9489-origins-development-cold-war-practice/).

## Syllabus coverage

- Inherent East-West tensions; personalities; ideology, security and economics.
- Traditionalism; revisionism; post-revisionism.
- Russian perspectives compared with Western ones.
- Reinterpretations from new archives; the "New" Cold War history and post-post-revisionism.
- Context: Europe, 1941-49.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Interpretation | What can be inferred about the historian's claims and conclusions |
| Approach | What the historian brings: interests, questions asked, ideology and beliefs |
| Traditionalism (orthodox) | Interpretations blaming the Soviet Union |
| Revisionism | Interpretations blaming the USA |
| Post-revisionism | Interpretations blaming both or neither side |
| Post-post-revisionism | Archive-based work after 1991, often returning weight to Stalin and ideology |
| New Cold War history | Multi-archival, international and ideology-aware study after 1991 |
| Open Door | American pursuit of open markets, central to Williams's revisionism |
| Atomic diplomacy | Using the bomb to pressure the USSR (Alperovitz) |
| Riga and Yalta axioms | Yergin's two US images of the USSR: revolutionary state or traditional great power |
| Revolutionary-imperial paradigm | Zubok and Pleshakov's fusion of ideology and Russian imperial security |
| Security dilemma | One side's defensive measures appear as threats to the other |
| Sub-message | A secondary point in an extract, not its overall interpretation |

| Date | Event | Typically used by |
|---|---|---|

## The schools in one table

| School | Historians and works | Core argument | Approach and context |
|---|---|---|---|
| Traditionalist | Feis; Schlesinger, 1967 | Soviet ideology and expansion; Western response defensive | Western official sources, early Cold War, little Soviet evidence |
| Revisionist | Williams, 1959; Alperovitz, 1965; Kolko, 1968 | US economic expansion and atomic diplomacy; Soviet defensiveness | Vietnam era, New Left, newly opened US archives |
| Post-revisionist | Gaddis, 1972; Yergin, 1977; Leffler, 1992 | Shared responsibility, misperception, national security | Détente, broader US records, systemic analysis |
| Russian / Soviet-era | Official histories | US imperialism and Fulton speech to blame | Party line, closed archives |
| Archive-era | Zubok and Pleshakov, 1996; Mastny, 1996; Roberts, 2006 | Ideology plus security; Stalin's insecurity; or Stalin's wish for co-operation | Soviet and East European archives |
| Post-post-revisionist | Gaddis, *We Now Know*, 1997; Westad, 2005 | Stalin and ideology central; global, multi-archival view | Post-1991 archives, international history |

## How to identify an extract's position

1. **Who acts, who reacts?** If the USSR acts and the West reacts, it leans traditionalist; the reverse leans revisionist.
2. **Which cause dominates?** Ideology, security, economics or personality.
3. **What evidence is cited?** Soviet archival material suggests post-1991 work.
4. **What tone towards blame?** Moral language ("aggression") or structural language ("dilemma", "vacuum").

## Exam traps

- **Labels are not answers:** explain the interpretation and approach; a school label alone earns little.
- **Sub-messages:** do not build the answer on a single sentence of the extract.
- **Context limits:** the syllabus context is Europe, 1941-49.
- **Dating fallacy:** old does not mean wrong; judge the argument and evidence.
- **Naming the author:** not required and not rewarded.
- **Knowledge for its own sake:** AO1 must test the interpretation.

## Self-test

1. What is the over-arching key question of this topic? *Who or what was to blame for the Cold War?*
2. Which school blames the Soviet Union? *Traditionalism (orthodox).*
3. What did Williams mean by the "Open Door"? *A US policy of economic expansion through open markets.*
4. What did Alperovitz argue about the atomic bomb? *That it was used partly to intimidate the USSR and strengthen the US position.*
5. What were Yergin's "Riga axioms"? *A view of the USSR as an ideologically driven revolutionary state.*
6. How did Gaddis's 1972 book explain US policy? *It stressed the constraints of domestic politics and opinion on US leaders.*
7. What is Leffler's main argument? *US officials' broad view of national security led them to seek a preponderance of power.*
8. What did Zubok and Pleshakov call Stalin's outlook? *A revolutionary-imperial paradigm.*
9. Name one new source that became available in the 1990s. *Soviet archival documents such as the Novikov telegram; or the Venona decrypts.*
10. Why is *We Now Know* called post-post-revisionist? *It used new archives to return much of the blame to Stalin and ideology.*
11. How did Soviet-era historians explain the Cold War? *As the result of Western imperialism and hostility, such as the Fulton speech.*
12. What does the syllabus mean by "approach"? *What the historian brings to the topic: interests, questions and beliefs.*
