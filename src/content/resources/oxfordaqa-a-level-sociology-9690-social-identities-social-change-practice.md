## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define what is meant by a **cohort**. **[2]**

**2.** Define what is meant by **banal nationalism**. **[2]**

**3.** Outline what is meant by a **hybrid identity**. **[2]**

**4.** Outline the **social model of disability**. **[2]**

**5.** Identify and briefly explain **two** ways religion may form part of a person's identity.
**[4]**

**6.** Identify and briefly explain **two** ways in which online communities may strengthen collective
identities. **[4]**

**7.** Identify and briefly explain **two** differences between race and ethnicity as bases of
identity. **[4]**

### Section B: longer questions

**8.** Explain **two** reasons why some sociologists claim there is a crisis of masculinity. **[8]**

**9.** Explain **two** ways in which Fanon's work helps sociologists understand ethnic identity.
**[8]**

**10.** Explain **two** ways in which diasporas may maintain or change ethnic identities across
generations. **[8]**

**11.** Using examples from a society you know, evaluate the view that online identities are freely
chosen. **[20]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that social class is no longer an important source of identity. **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** A group of people born in the same period [1] who share formative experiences, such as a
war, economic crisis or technological change, as they grow up [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Everyday, routine reminders of nationhood [1], such as flags on public buildings, national
weather maps or "our" team in sports coverage, which reproduce national identity unnoticed (Billig)
[1]. **[2]**

**3.** An identity that combines elements of more than one culture [1], for example a
second-generation migrant mixing parents' language and religion with the music and fashion of the
society where they grew up [1]. **[2]**

**4.** The view that people are disabled by social, physical and attitudinal barriers [1], such as
inaccessible buildings or discrimination, rather than by their impairment itself (Oliver) [1].
**[2]**

**5.** Two ways, 2 marks each (identify [1], explain [1]):
- Dress and practice [1]: religious clothing, diet and prayer mark identity visibly to self and others [1].
- Community belonging [1]: membership of a congregation gives a collective identity and support [1].
- Moral framework [1]: religious values guide life choices and self-understanding [1].
- Minority or diaspora identity [1]: religion may become more central where a group is a minority [1].
**[4]**

**6.** Two ways, 2 marks each:
- Connecting dispersed people [1]: diaspora members or people with rare conditions find others like
  them across borders [1].
- Shared symbols and language [1]: memes, hashtags and in-group terms reinforce belonging [1].
- Mobilisation [1]: online campaigns create a sense of shared cause and identity [1].
**[4]**

**7.** Two differences, 2 marks each:
- Basis [1]: race is a social category based on perceived physical differences such as skin colour,
  while ethnicity is based on shared culture, history, language or religion [1].
- Imposition vs claim [1]: racial categories are often imposed by others, sometimes in racist ways,
  whereas ethnic identity is more often claimed by group members [1].
- Fluidity [1]: ethnicity can be expressed or played down through cultural choices, whereas racial
  labels based on appearance are harder to escape [1].
**[4]**

**8.** Two reasons, 4 marks each (identify [1], explain [1], develop [1], evidence [1]):
- **Decline of manual work** [1]: loss of industrial jobs removes the male breadwinner role [1] that
  defined working-class masculinity, leaving some young men uncertain of their status [1];
  Mac an Ghaill described this as a crisis of masculinity [1].
- **Changing gender roles** [1]: women's success in education and employment [1] challenges
  hegemonic masculinity (Connell), which stressed male dominance [1]; some men respond with
  exaggerated or defensive masculinities, while others adopt more caring identities [1].
- Credit: boys' relative underachievement in schools; media debates about men's mental health.
**[8]**

**9.** Two ways, 4 marks each:
- **Racism shapes self-image** [1]: Fanon (*Black Skin, White Masks*, 1952) argued colonised Black
  people were pressured to see themselves through the coloniser's eyes [1], so ethnic identity is
  shaped by power, not just culture [1]; this helps explain internalised racism and pressure to
  assimilate [1].
- **Identity as relational and constructed** [1]: Fanon showed "Blackness" took meaning from
  colonial contrast with "whiteness" [1], linking identity to othering [1], which sociologists use to
  understand racialised identities in post-colonial and multi-ethnic societies today [1].
**[8]**

**10.** Two ways, 4 marks each:
- **Maintaining identity** [1]: families, religious institutions and community-language schools
  pass on language, faith and customs [1]; transnational ties, visits and media from the homeland
  keep links alive [1], so the diaspora keeps a strong collective identity [1].
- **Changing identity** [1]: children socialised in the new society's schools and peer groups [1]
  blend cultures, producing hybrid identities [1]; Stuart Hall argued diaspora identity is about
  "becoming" as well as "being", continually reworked [1].
**[8]**

**11.** Levels of response:
- 17–20: detailed knowledge; sustained application to a named society; balanced evaluation;
  justified conclusion.
- 13–16: good knowledge and application; some evaluation.
- 9–12: reasonable knowledge; limited evaluation.
- 5–8: basic, descriptive.
- 1–4: fragments.

Indicative content. *For:* users choose what to post and can create several profiles; anonymity
allows experimentation with identities; virtual communities let people explore identities unavailable
offline; postmodernists see identity as pick-and-mix; Goffman's impression management shows active
performance. *Against:* Zuboff argues platforms harvest data and steer behaviour; algorithms reward
some content and hide other content; peer approval through likes acts as informal control; offline
class, gender and ethnicity shape online presentation and harassment; access depends on income and
infrastructure; governments in some societies monitor or restrict online expression. *Application:*
reward examples from a named society. *Conclusion:* supported judgement, for example that
choice exists within commercially and socially structured limits. **[20]**

**12.** Levels of response as for Question 11.

Indicative content. *For:* decline of manual industry and trade unions; growth of consumption-based
identities (Bauman); Giddens' reflexive self; global and online identities cut across class;
people may describe themselves through lifestyle, ethnicity or religion rather than class.
*Against:* Savage and colleagues show class reworked through economic, cultural and social capital;
class still shapes education, health and life chances; taste and consumption reflect class; class
consciousness may be weak while class inequality remains strong; Marxists distinguish a class "in
itself" from "for itself". *Conclusion:* supported judgement. **[20]**
