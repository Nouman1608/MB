This guide teaches section **3.1.4.1 Enthalpy change** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Papers 1 and 2 (and Paper 3). It lays the groundwork for the rest of Energetics: calorimetry, Hess's law and bond enthalpies. The specification asks for three things: the meaning of enthalpy change and of standard conditions, and definitions of two standard enthalpy changes, combustion and formation. The [Energetics study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-energetics/) covers the whole topic. This page focuses on precision: using the definitions word for word, writing the correct equation for each, and using ΔH to find the energy released for a given amount. Recall is in the [Enthalpy change revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-enthalpy-change-revision-notes/), and questions are in the [Enthalpy change practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-enthalpy-change-practice/).

## Enthalpy change and its sign

Chemical reactions involve energy changes because bonds are broken (which needs energy) and bonds are made (which releases energy). The energy transferred as heat at constant pressure is the **enthalpy change**, ΔH.

ΔH = H(products) − H(reactants)

| Type | Heat | Temperature of surroundings | ΔH | Profile |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Exothermic | Released to surroundings | Rises | Negative | Products lower than reactants |
| Endothermic | Taken in from surroundings | Falls | Positive | Products higher than reactants |

The unit is **kJ mol⁻¹**, which means per mole of reaction **as the equation is written**. If the equation is doubled, ΔH doubles. If it is reversed, ΔH changes sign.

### Enthalpy profile diagrams

A profile shows enthalpy on the vertical axis and progress of reaction on the horizontal axis. For an exothermic reaction the products are drawn below the reactants, with an arrow downwards labelled ΔH (negative). A hump between them shows the activation energy. For an endothermic reaction the products are higher and the arrow points upwards. The arrow for ΔH goes from the reactants level to the products level and is not the same as the activation energy.

## Standard conditions and standard states

**Standard conditions** are a pressure of **100 kPa** and a **stated temperature**, usually 298 K. The temperature is part of the symbol, as in ΔH₂₉₈⦵. The symbol ⦵ (standard) is added to ΔH. Each substance must be in its **standard state**, its physical state at 100 kPa and the stated temperature, and for elements, its most stable form.

| Element | Standard state at 298 K |
|---|---|
| Carbon | C(s, graphite) |
| Oxygen | O₂(g) |
| Hydrogen | H₂(g) |
| Bromine | Br₂(l) |
| Sulfur | S(s) (rhombic) |
| Sodium | Na(s) |

A solution is taken at a concentration of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ when this applies. State symbols are essential. The states H₂O(g) and H₂O(l) differ in enthalpy by the heat needed to vaporise water.

## Standard enthalpy of formation, ΔfH⦵

**The enthalpy change when one mole of a compound is formed from its elements, with all reactants and products in their standard states, under standard conditions.**

Key points for writing the equation:

- Exactly **one mole of the compound** on the right.
- Only **elements** on the left, each in its standard state. Fractions are allowed (½O₂).
- ΔfH⦵ of an element in its standard state is **zero**.

### Worked example 1: writing formation equations

| Compound | Equation |
|---|---|
| Carbon dioxide | C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g) |
| Water (liquid) | H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → H₂O(l) |
| Ethanol | 2C(s) + 3H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → C₂H₅OH(l) |
| Sodium chloride | Na(s) + ½Cl₂(g) → NaCl(s) |
| Ammonia | ½N₂(g) + 1½H₂(g) → NH₃(g) |

Notice that "2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O" is **not** a formation equation: it makes two moles. Formation of nitrogen monoxide is ½N₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → NO(g), with ΔfH⦵ = +90 kJ mol⁻¹. That is a positive value, so formation reactions are not always exothermic.

## Standard enthalpy of combustion, ΔcH⦵

**The enthalpy change when one mole of a substance is completely burned in oxygen, with all reactants and products in their standard states, under standard conditions.**

Key points:

- Exactly **one mole of the substance being burned** on the left.
- Reaction with **excess oxygen** to give complete combustion: CO₂(g) and H₂O(l) for compounds of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen.
- Combustion is always **exothermic**, so ΔcH⦵ is always negative.

### Worked example 2: writing combustion equations

| Substance | Equation |
|---|---|
| Methane | CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l) |
| Ethanol | C₂H₅OH(l) + 3O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 3H₂O(l) |
| Hydrogen | H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → H₂O(l) |
| Carbon (graphite) | C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g) |
| Propane | C₃H₈(g) + 5O₂(g) → 3CO₂(g) + 4H₂O(l) |

### Equations that are both

The same equation can be a formation and a combustion equation:

- C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g) is ΔfH⦵ of carbon dioxide **and** ΔcH⦵ of carbon (graphite).
- H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → H₂O(l) is ΔfH⦵ of water **and** ΔcH⦵ of hydrogen.

Always say which one your values refer to.

## Calculations with ΔH

Energy change = number of moles × ΔH (per mole).

### Worked example 3: energy released

The standard enthalpy of combustion of ethanol is −1367 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the energy released when 2.30 g of ethanol is burned completely. (Mᵣ = 46.0)

```
n = 2.30 ÷ 46.0 = 0.0500 mol
energy released = 0.0500 × 1367 = 68.4 kJ
```

The energy released is a positive quantity of heat; ΔH for the reaction is −68.4 kJ.

### Worked example 4: mass needed

The standard enthalpy of combustion of methane is −890 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the mass of methane that must be burned to release 1000 kJ. (Mᵣ = 16.0)

```
n = 1000 ÷ 890 = 1.124 mol
m = 1.124 × 16.0 = 18.0 g
```

### Worked example 5: scaling and reversing

The enthalpy change for 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l) is 2 × (−286) = −572 kJ mol⁻¹, because two moles of water form. The enthalpy change for H₂O(l) → H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) is +286 kJ mol⁻¹, because the reaction is the reverse of the formation.

### Worked example 6: why the state symbol matters

ΔfH⦵ of H₂O(l) is −286 kJ mol⁻¹ and of H₂O(g) is −242 kJ mol⁻¹. The difference of 44 kJ mol⁻¹ is the energy needed to turn one mole of liquid water into steam. A combustion value is always quoted with liquid water, so a quoted value for burning a hydrocarbon cannot be used for a reaction that produces steam without correcting for it.

## Common mistakes

- Forgetting state symbols, or using the wrong ones.
- Writing a formation equation that makes more than one mole of product, or has a compound on the left.
- Writing a combustion equation that burns two moles, or gives CO or C as a product.
- Writing 2O (atoms) instead of O₂ in an equation.
- Giving ΔH a positive sign for an exothermic reaction.
- Leaving the unit out, or writing kJ instead of kJ mol⁻¹.
- Omitting the condition "standard conditions" or "standard states" from a definition.
- Confusing ΔH with the activation energy in a profile diagram.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Define**: include "one mole", "standard states", "standard conditions" and for combustion "completely burned in oxygen".
- **Write an equation for**: put one mole of the specified substance, with state symbols.
- **Calculate**: show n, then the multiplication by ΔH, with the unit and the sign.
- **Sketch** a profile: label the axes, the reactants and products, the activation energy and the ΔH arrow.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is the enthalpy of formation of an element zero?**
The reaction would be an element forming itself, so there is no change. For example, O₂(g) → O₂(g).

**Is combustion the same as burning in air?**
In enthalpy definitions it is a complete reaction with oxygen. The products for a hydrocarbon are CO₂ and H₂O.

**What does "standard" mean for the temperature?**
The pressure is fixed at 100 kPa, but the temperature is whatever is stated. In practice it is usually 298 K.
