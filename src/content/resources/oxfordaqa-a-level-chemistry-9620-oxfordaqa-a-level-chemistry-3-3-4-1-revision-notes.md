## Specification in brief

- Alkenes are **unsaturated hydrocarbons**.
- Bonding in alkenes involves a **double covalent bond, a centre of high electron density**.
- Consequence (section introduction): the C=C is attacked by **electrophiles**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Hydrocarbon | Compound of carbon and hydrogen only |
| Saturated | Only single carbon–carbon bonds |
| Unsaturated | Contains at least one carbon–carbon double (multiple) bond |
| Alkene | Unsaturated hydrocarbon with a C=C bond; chain alkenes with one C=C fit CₙH₂ₙ |
| Centre of high electron density | Region with a concentration of electrons; in C=C, four electrons between two carbons |
| Electrophile | Electron pair acceptor; positive or δ+, attracted to electron-rich regions |
| Nucleophile | Electron pair donor; attracted to electron-deficient atoms |
| Electrophilic addition | An electrophile attacks C=C and two new single bonds form; no atoms are lost |
| Induced dipole | Temporary polarity caused in a molecule (such as Br₂) by nearby electron density |

## Recall: structure of the C=C bond

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Electrons between the two carbons | 4 (two shared pairs) |
| Shape around each C=C carbon | trigonal planar |
| Bond angle | about 120° |
| Planarity | the two C=C carbons and the four attached atoms lie in one plane |
| Rotation | restricted, so E/Z isomers are possible |
| Mean bond enthalpy (one data set) | C=C 612 kJ mol⁻¹; C–C 348 kJ mol⁻¹ |
| Key comparison | C=C is stronger than C–C but less than twice as strong |

## Recall: reasoning chain for "why are alkenes reactive?"

1. C=C contains four electrons, so it is a **centre of high electron density**.
2. **Electrophiles** (electron pair acceptors) are attracted to it.
3. The electrophile accepts an electron pair from C=C; one carbon–carbon bond is used and two new single bonds form.
4. The reaction is **electrophilic addition**.

## Recall: comparing reactive sites

| Compound | Reactive site | Reagent type | Reaction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alkane | none polar | free radical | substitution |
| Halogenoalkane | δ+ carbon | nucleophile | substitution / elimination |
| Alkene | electron-rich C=C | electrophile | addition |

**Electrophiles met with alkenes in 9620:** δ+ H of HBr; δ+ H of H₂SO₄; δ+ Br of a polarised Br₂.

**Not electrophiles:** OH⁻, CN⁻, NH₃, Cl⁻, H₂O (all lone pair donors).

## Recall: working with formulae

- Chain alkane CₙH₂ₙ₊₂; chain alkene with one C=C CₙH₂ₙ.
- Each C=C **or** each ring removes 2 H from CₙH₂ₙ₊₂.
- Moles of Br₂ that add per mole of hydrocarbon = number of C=C bonds.
- Example: C₆H₁₀ is 4 H short of C₆H₁₄, so it has two rings or double bonds in total. If 1 mol adds 1 mol Br₂, it has one C=C and one ring (for example cyclohexene).

### Worked reminder

Hydrocarbon Z, C₇H₁₂, adds 1 mol of Br₂ per mole. C₇H₁₆ − C₇H₁₂ = 4 H, so Z has two units of unsaturation. One is a C=C (one Br₂ adds); the other must be a ring. A possible structure is methylcyclohexene; a chain diene such as hepta-1,6-diene would add 2 mol of Br₂ and is ruled out.

## Exam traps

- "Unsaturated" means a **carbon–carbon** multiple bond.
- Alkenes are reactive because C=C is **electron rich**, not because it is weak.
- Electrophile = pair **acceptor**; nucleophile = pair **donor**.
- Br₂ is **non-polar**; its dipole is **induced** by the C=C.
- C₆H₁₂ could be hexene **or** cyclohexane.
- Nucleophiles are **repelled** by C=C, so they do not add to alkenes.

## Self-test

1. What is meant by the term *unsaturated*?
2. Give the general formula for chain alkenes with one C=C bond.
3. How many electrons are shared between the two carbon atoms in C=C?
4. State the shape around each carbon of a C=C bond and the bond angle.
5. Define *electrophile*.
6. Explain why alkenes react with electrophiles.
7. Explain how a non-polar Br₂ molecule can act as an electrophile.
8. Why does hydroxide ion not add to ethene?
9. Name the type of reaction that alkenes typically undergo.
10. Name a compound with formula C₅H₁₀ that is not an alkene.
11. A hydrocarbon C₆H₁₀ adds 2 mol of Br₂ per mole. How many C=C bonds and rings does it have?
12. Using C=C 612 and C–C 348 kJ mol⁻¹, explain why converting C=C into C–C needs less energy than breaking a C–C bond.

### Answers

1. Contains at least one **carbon–carbon double (multiple) bond**.
2. **CₙH₂ₙ**
3. **Four** (two shared pairs).
4. **Trigonal planar**, about **120°**.
5. An **electron pair acceptor**.
6. The C=C has **high electron density** (four electrons), which attracts electron pair acceptors.
7. The C=C electron density **repels the electrons in Br–Br**, so the nearer Br becomes δ+ (an **induced dipole**) and accepts a pair.
8. OH⁻ is a **nucleophile**; its negative charge and lone pairs are **repelled** by the electron-rich double bond.
9. **Electrophilic addition.**
10. **Cyclopentane** (or methylcyclobutane).
11. **Two C=C and no rings** (C₆H₁₄ − C₆H₁₀ = 4 H, i.e. two units of unsaturation, both C=C).
12. 612 − 348 = **264 kJ mol⁻¹** is the extra energy in the double bond, which is **less than 348**, so the second bond of C=C is broken more easily than a C–C single bond.
