## How the terms connect

Learning the nine terms as separate definitions is not enough. Questions usually test how a change in one affects the others.

* **Span of control and levels move in opposite directions.** For the same number of staff, wider spans need fewer levels.
* **Levels and chain of command.** More levels make the chain of command longer, so instructions pass through more people and take longer to arrive.
* **Delegation and decentralization.** Decentralization is delegation applied across the organisation: decisions move down to branches, regions or teams.
* **Delayering** removes levels, so it widens spans of control and shortens the chain of command at the same time.
* **Bureaucracy** usually goes with centralization: rules written at the top are applied everywhere.
* **A matrix structure** cuts across the hierarchy: an employee keeps a functional manager and also reports to a project manager.

When a question asks for the effect of delayering, a full answer traces at least two of these links, for example wider spans for the remaining managers and faster communication through a shorter chain.

## Reading and calculating from an organization chart (AO4)

### Worked example: Quenby Software

Quenby Software is an invented company. Its chart, written out in words:

```
Chief executive
  -> Product director
       -> 4 team leaders, each with 6 developers
  -> Sales director
       -> 2 regional managers, each with 9 sales staff
  -> Finance director
       -> 5 accountants
```

Questions an examiner can ask, with answers:

| Question | Working | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| Total employees | 1 + 3 + 4 + 24 + 2 + 18 + 5 | 57 |
| Number of levels | Chief executive, directors, team leaders or regional managers, front-line staff | 4 |
| Span of control of the chief executive | Directors reporting directly | 3 |
| Widest span in the business | Regional managers | 9 |
| Staff below the Sales director | 2 + 18 | 20 |
| Links in the chain from a developer to the chief executive | Developer to team leader, to director, to chief executive | 3 |

Note that the Finance branch has only three levels, because accountants report directly to their director. A chart does not have to be symmetrical, and a question may ask you to spot this.

### What the chart tells you

Quenby's spans range from 3 to 9. The chief executive's span of 3 suggests close contact with each director. The regional managers' span of 9 suggests sales staff work with some independence, which fits field sales. Good AO2 comment goes beyond the number to what it means for supervision, communication and motivation.

## Types of chart: choosing the basis of grouping

| Basis | Best when | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|
| By function | One main product; specialist skills matter | Departments may pursue their own goals (silo thinking) |
| By product | Several distinct product lines with different customers | Roles such as marketing are duplicated in each division |
| By region | Customers or laws differ between places | Regions may become inconsistent with each other |
| Matrix | Work is project-based and needs mixed skills | Two bosses can give conflicting instructions |

A large business may combine bases, for example regions at the top and functions inside each region. Say which basis applies at which level when describing such a chart.

## Appropriateness given a change in external factors (AO3)

A reliable method for this point:

1. **Name the external change** and its source, using a STEEPLE heading if helpful.
2. **State what it demands** of the organisation: speed, local knowledge, cost cuts, tighter control or flexibility.
3. **Compare at least two structures** against that demand, with a weakness for each.
4. **Judge**, and state the condition that would change your judgement.

### Annotated example

*Case: Harcastle Pharmacy, an invented chain of 30 pharmacies, is tall and centralized. A new law lets pharmacists give some vaccinations, and demand varies sharply between towns.*

> The legal change creates a new service whose demand differs by town, so it calls for quick local decisions about staffing and stock. **[demand of the change]** Decentralizing these decisions to store managers would let each pharmacy respond to local demand, but it risks inconsistent clinical standards across 30 stores. **[structure 1 with weakness]** Keeping the centralized, bureaucratic structure would protect consistency, which matters for a regulated clinical service, but head office would react slowly. **[structure 2 with weakness]** On balance, Harcastle should decentralize scheduling and stock decisions while keeping clinical rules centralized, because speed matters for local demand but safety must be uniform. If the law imposed strict national reporting, more centralization would be justified. **[judgement and condition]**

This answer avoids the common claim that flat or decentralized structures are always better. The best structure is the one that fits the demand of the change.

## Delayering in numbers

Suppose Harcastle has 1 managing director, 5 area managers, 30 store managers and 270 other staff. The managing director's span is 5, and each area manager supervises 30 ÷ 5 = 6 store managers. Removing the area-manager level gives the managing director a span of 30. That is the trade-off of delayering: one fewer level and a shorter chain of command, but a span so wide that supervision of store managers becomes very thin. A middle option is to remove some area managers, for example keeping 3 so each supervises 10 stores.

## HL only: changes in organizational structure

### Project-based organization

Teams form around a project, draw people from different functions, then disband when the project ends. It suits work with a clear start and finish, such as building a new product or opening a site. Benefits: mixed skills focused on one goal and fast decisions within the team. Limitations: staff may feel insecure between projects, and functional expertise can weaken if people move often.

### Handy's Shamrock Organization

Charles Handy described an organisation with three "leaves":

* **Core**: permanent, highly skilled staff and managers who are essential to the organisation and are rewarded for commitment.
* **Contractual fringe**: outside firms and specialists paid for results, doing non-core work such as cleaning, payroll or IT support.
* **Flexible labour force**: part-time, temporary or seasonal workers who are brought in when demand rises.

How to use it: Quenby Software might keep its developers as the core, contract out payroll to an accounting firm, and hire temporary support staff for a product launch. Benefits include lower fixed labour costs and quick changes in capacity. Limitations include weaker loyalty in the outer leaves, more effort to manage contracts, and possible ethical questions about job security for flexible workers. The model links to the gig economy factor in section 2.1.

## Common mistakes

* Counting all employees in a branch as the manager's span of control. Span counts direct reports only.
* Saying delayering removes "workers" rather than levels, usually of management.
* Assuming a chart must be symmetrical when counting levels.
* Treating a matrix structure as a type of tall structure. It is a different way of grouping.
* Calling one structure "best" without linking it to the external change in the case.

## Exam technique

* **Calculate** or **determine**: show the addition or division you used from the chart.
* **Explain** or **analyse**: trace the effect through linked terms, such as levels to chain of command to communication speed.
* **Discuss**, **evaluate** or **recommend**: compare two structures against the external change and finish with a conditional judgement.
