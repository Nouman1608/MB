## 1.2.1 Classifying by shared features

Classification means sorting organisms into groups. Organisms are placed in the same group because they **share features**, such as having feathers, having a backbone or having a cellulose cell wall. Large groups are divided into smaller and smaller groups whose members share more and more features. The smallest group in everyday use is the **species**.

Classification is useful because it:

- turns millions of different organisms into a manageable system;
- lets scientists predict features of a newly found organism from the group it belongs to;
- helps biologists communicate, because a group name means the same thing everywhere;
- helps in conservation, by showing which species are rare and how they are related.

## 1.2.2 What is a species?

A **species** is a group of organisms that can reproduce to produce **fertile offspring**.

The word "fertile" is essential. A horse and a donkey can mate and produce a mule, but mules are almost always infertile. So horses and donkeys belong to **different** species, even though they look alike and can interbreed. Two organisms that look quite different, such as a tiny dog and a very large dog, are the **same** species, because they can produce fertile puppies.

Exam answers that say "organisms that can breed together" lose the mark: the offspring must be able to reproduce too.

## 1.2.3 The binomial system

Every species has a two-part scientific name.

| Part | What it shows | Written with |
|---|---|---|
| First word | The **genus**, a group of closely related species | A capital letter |
| Second word | The **species** within that genus | A lower-case letter |

Examples: the lion is *Panthera leo* and the tiger is *Panthera tigris*. They share the genus *Panthera*, so they are closely related, but they are different species. Humans are *Homo sapiens*.

**Rules:** in print the name is in *italics*; when handwritten, underline it. After the first use, the genus can be shortened to its initial, as in *P. leo*.

**Why it is internationally agreed:** common names vary between languages and places. For example, the bird called a robin in Britain is a different species from the bird called a robin in North America. A binomial name refers to one species only, so scientists in every country know exactly which organism is meant.

## 1.2.4 Constructing and using dichotomous keys

A dichotomous key is a series of numbered steps, each offering **two** contrasting statements about an **identifiable feature**. Following the steps leads to the name of the organism.

### Method for constructing a key

1. **List the features** of each organism that you can actually see: number of legs, wings present or absent, shape of leaf edge, and so on.
2. **Choose a feature that splits the group** into two smaller groups. It does not need to split them evenly.
3. **Write a pair of opposite statements**, such as "has wings" / "has no wings". Use features that are clear yes-or-no, or that are measurable. Avoid opinions such as "large", "pretty" or "dark" unless a value is given.
4. **Repeat** for each smaller group until every organism is on its own.
5. **Check:** a key for *n* organisms needs *n* − 1 pairs of statements. Run each organism through the key to test that it arrives at its own name.

### Worked example 1: building a key for five invertebrates

The five animals:

| Animal | Legs | Wings | Body segments visible |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ant | 6 | no | 3 parts |
| Fly | 6 | yes | 3 parts |
| Spider | 8 | no | 2 parts |
| Earthworm | 0 | no | many rings |
| Woodlouse | 14 | no | many plates |

A suitable key:

```
1  Has legs ............................. go to 2
   Has no legs .......................... earthworm
2  Has 6 legs ........................... go to 3
   Has more than 6 legs ................. go to 4
3  Has wings ............................ fly
   Has no wings ......................... ant
4  Has 8 legs ........................... spider
   Has more than 8 legs ................. woodlouse
```

Five animals need four pairs of statements, and each animal has one route. Notice that each step uses a feature you could check on a specimen in front of you.

### Using a key

Start at step 1 every time, choose the statement that fits, and follow the instruction. Write down the route (for example 1 → 2 → 4 → spider) if the question asks you to show your working. If neither statement fits, re-check the specimen rather than guessing.

## 1.2.5 to 1.2.7 Classification and evolution -- Extended only

### Classification reflects evolutionary relationships

Classification systems aim to group organisms by how closely they are related through **evolution**: organisms in the same group should share a **common ancestor**. Features can mislead. A dolphin and a shark both have a streamlined body and fins, but the dolphin is a mammal: it breathes air with lungs and feeds its young on milk. Its similar shape evolved because both live in water, not because they share a recent ancestor.

### DNA base sequences as a means of classification

DNA carries a sequence of bases (A, T, C and G). When a species splits into two, their DNA starts to change independently, so differences build up over time. Therefore:

- groups that share a **more recent** ancestor have **more similar** base sequences;
- groups that share only a **distant** ancestor have **less similar** base sequences.

Comparing base sequences gives evidence of relationships that does not depend on how organisms look.

### Worked example 2: comparing base sequences

The same 20-base section of a gene was sequenced in four species.

```
Species W:  A T G C C T A G G A T C G T A A C G T T
Species X:  A T G C C T A G G T T C G T A A C G T T
Species Y:  A T G A C T A G G T T C G A A A C G C T
Species Z:  T T G A C T A C G T T C G A A T C G C A
```

Compare each with species W, base by base:

| Pair | Bases different | Percentage identical |
|---|---|---|
| W and X | 1 | (20 − 1) ÷ 20 × 100 = 95% |
| W and Y | 4 | (20 − 4) ÷ 20 × 100 = 80% |
| W and Z | 8 | (20 − 8) ÷ 20 × 100 = 60% |

**Conclusion:** X is most closely related to W, because their sequences are most similar, so they share the most recent common ancestor. Z is the most distantly related. In an answer, say "more similar base sequence, so a more recent common ancestor"; do not say one species evolved from the other.

## Common errors

- Defining a species as organisms that "can breed" without **fertile offspring**.
- Writing a binomial name with two capitals (*Panthera Leo*) or not underlining it by hand.
- Saying the second word is the "family" or "surname": it is the **species**.
- Using vague features in a key, or offering three choices at one step.
- (Extended) Saying related species have the "same" DNA. They have **more similar** base sequences.
- (Extended) Saying species X "came from" species W. Both descend from a common ancestor.

## Exam technique for Cambridge command words

- **State:** short fact, for example "the genus".
- **Describe:** give the full meaning, such as the definition of a species including fertile offspring.
- **Construct:** produce a working key, with paired statements and numbered steps.
- **Use:** follow a given key and give the name, often with the route.
- **Explain** (Extended): link more similar DNA to a more recent common ancestor.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why are horses and donkeys different species?** Their offspring, mules, are infertile, so they do not produce fertile offspring together.

**What does the first word of a binomial name show?** The genus, a group of closely related species.

**Why does DNA help classification?** Groups with a more recent common ancestor have more similar base sequences, so DNA shows relationships even when appearance misleads.
