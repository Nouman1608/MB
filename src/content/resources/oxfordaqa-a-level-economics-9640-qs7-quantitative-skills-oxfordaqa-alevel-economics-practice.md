These questions practise quantitative skill QS7, converting money values to real terms, from section 3.5 of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Economics (9640) specification. Every country and figure is invented. Revise first with the [QS7 study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-economics-9640-qs7-quantitative-skills-oxfordaqa-alevel-economics/) and the [QS7 revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-economics-9640-qs7-quantitative-skills-oxfordaqa-alevel-economics-revision-notes/), and see the [course hub](/boards/oxfordaqa/a-level/economics/) for the rest of the course. Give real values to two decimal places or to the nearest whole unit, as the question asks.

## Questions

### Section A

**1.** Explain the difference between a money value and a real value. **[2]**

**2.** A nurse earns 33,000 a year when the price index is 104 and 37,400 a year when it is 118 (base year = 100). Calculate her real salary in base-year prices in each year, and the percentage change in her real salary. **[3]**

**3.** A family paid rent of 1,200 a month in year 1, when the price index was 96. Convert this rent into the prices of year 4, when the index was 120. **[2]**

**4.** Inflation in Country A is 8% in year 2, 10% in year 3 and 5% in year 4. A salary rises from 15,000 in year 1 to 18,000 in year 4. Calculate the price index in year 4 (year 1 = 100) and state, with a reason, whether the salary kept pace with prices. **[3]**

**5.** A bank pays a nominal interest rate of 7% when inflation is 4.5%. Calculate the exact real interest rate and state whether savers gain or lose purchasing power. **[2]**

**6.** Money GDP in a country rises by 9% in a year in which the price level also rises by 9%. Which statement is correct? **[1]**

A: Real GDP rises by 9%.

B: Real GDP is unchanged.

C: Real GDP falls.

D: Real GDP per head must rise.

### Section B

**7.** The table shows government health spending in Country S. The price index has 2018 = 100.

| Year | Money spending (bn) | Price index |
|---|---|---|
| 2016 | 80 | 95 |
| 2018 | 88 | 100 |
| 2022 | 104 | 121 |

**(a)** Calculate real spending in 2018 prices for each year. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in money spending and in real spending from 2016 to 2022. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the real value for 2016 is larger than its money value. **[1]**

**8.** Country T's money GDP rises from 250bn to 280bn while its price index rises from 100 to 108. Its population rises from 10.0 million to 10.4 million.

**(a)** Calculate real GDP in year 2, in year 1 prices. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate real GDP per head in each year and its percentage change. **[3]**

**(c)** Explain why the growth of money GDP gives a misleading view of living standards in Country T. **[2]**

**9.** A saver deposits 5,000 for two years at a nominal interest rate of 3% a year, compounded annually. Inflation is 5% in the first year and 4% in the second.

**(a)** Calculate the money value of the deposit after two years. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate its real value in the prices of the day it was deposited, and the percentage change in its real value. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain one effect of negative real interest rates on the behaviour of households. **[2]**

**10.** A government raises the hourly minimum wage from 6.00 to 7.20 over a period in which the price index rises from 100 to 116. It says workers on the minimum wage have had "a 20% pay rise".

**(a)** Calculate the real percentage rise in the minimum wage. **[2]**

**(b)** Assess the government's claim. **[4]**

**11.** Average pay rises by 5% in a year when the consumer price index rises by 6%. Worker A spends half her income on food, whose prices rose by 9%, and half on other goods, whose prices rose by 4%.

**(a)** Calculate the change in the average worker's real pay. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate Worker A's personal inflation rate and the change in her real pay, assuming her pay also rose by 5%. **[3]**

**(c)** Explain one limitation of using an average price index to measure changes in real income. **[2]**

**12.** Over four years in Country V, average money wages rose by 18% and the consumer price index rose by 14%. Over the same period average weekly hours worked rose slightly, and the share of income going to the top tenth of earners increased. Evaluate the view that the rise in money wages shows that living standards in Country V have improved. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** A money value is measured in the prices of the year it was recorded, so it can rise just because prices rise [1]. A real value is measured in the prices of one chosen year, so it removes the effect of price changes and shows purchasing power [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The key word is purchasing power; say what a real value lets you compare.

**2.** Year 1: 33,000 / 104 x 100 = **31,731** [1]. Later year: 37,400 / 118 x 100 = **31,695** [1]. Real change = (31,695 - 31,731) / 31,731 = **-0.1%** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A 13.3% money rise turns into a small real fall; state both to show you understand the difference.

**3.** 1,200 x 120 / 96 [1] = **1,500** in year 4 prices [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Year 4 prices are higher, so the converted value must be larger; that check catches an inverted fraction.

**4.** Index = 100 x 1.08 x 1.10 x 1.05 = **124.74** [1]. The salary needed to keep pace is 15,000 x 1.2474 = 18,711 [1]. The salary of 18,000 is below this, so it did not keep pace: its real value fell by about 3.8% [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Adding the inflation rates (23%) would give a required salary of 18,450 and the same conclusion only by luck; multiply the factors.

**5.** 1.07 / 1.045 - 1 = **+2.4%** [1]. The real rate is positive, so savers gain purchasing power [1]. **[2]**

**6.** **B** [1]. Money GDP and prices rose by the same proportion, so real output is unchanged. **[1]**

**7. (a)** 2016: 80 / 95 x 100 = **84.21bn** [1]. 2018: **88.00bn** [1]. 2022: 104 / 121 x 100 = **85.95bn** [1].

**(b)** Money spending rose by (104 - 80) / 80 = **30.0%** [1]. Real spending rose by (85.95 - 84.21) / 84.21 = **2.1%** [1].

**(c)** The 2016 index is below 100, so 2016 prices were lower than 2018 prices, and converting into dearer 2018 prices raises the figure [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Real spending also fell between 2018 and 2022 (88.00 to 85.95), which a comparison of only the end years hides.

**8. (a)** 280 / 108 x 100 = **259.26bn** [1].

**(b)** Year 1: 250bn / 10.0m = **25,000** [1]. Year 2: 259.26bn / 10.4m = **24,929** [1]. Change = **-0.3%** [1].

**(c)** Money GDP rose by 12%, but most of that is higher prices (8%) [1], and the population grew by 4%, so real output per person fell slightly and the average person is not better off [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Name both adjustments; leaving out population gives real growth of 3.7% and the wrong conclusion.

**9. (a)** 5,000 x 1.03 x 1.03 = **5,304.50** [1].

**(b)** Real value = 5,304.50 / (1.05 x 1.04) = **4,857.60** [1], a fall of **2.8%** in purchasing power [1].

**(c)** Saving becomes less attractive because deposits lose purchasing power [1], so households may spend more now or borrow more, since debts fall in real value [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Deflate by both years' inflation; dividing by 1.05 alone overstates the real value.

**10. (a)** 7.20 / 1.16 = 6.21 in starting prices [1], a real rise of (6.21 - 6.00) / 6.00 = **3.4%** [1].

**(b)** The money rise is indeed 20% [1], but prices rose by 16%, so the gain in purchasing power is only about 3.4% [1]. The claim is accurate in money terms but misleading about living standards [1]. The average price index may also understate the inflation faced by low-paid workers if food and rent, which take a large share of their spending, rose faster [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* "Assess" needs a judgement: true in one sense, misleading in another, with the figure that shows why.

**11. (a)** 1.05 / 1.06 - 1 = **-0.9%** [1].

**(b)** Personal inflation = (0.5 x 9) + (0.5 x 4) = **6.5%** [1]. Real pay change = 1.05 / 1.065 - 1 [1] = **-1.4%** [1].

**(c)** An average index uses average spending weights [1], so households that spend more on goods whose prices rose fastest face a higher inflation rate than the index shows [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Weighting the two price rises by spending shares is a short calculation that earns credit as application.

**12.** Real wages rose by 1.18 / 1.14 - 1 = **3.5%** over four years [1], so purchasing power per hour rose, but much less than the 18% money rise suggests [1]. That is under 1% a year, a modest gain [1]. Hours worked rose, so part of any rise in weekly pay comes from working longer, which is not a pure gain in well-being [1]. Rising inequality means the median worker may have gained less than the average, or nothing [1]. The price index may not match the spending of every group [1]. Wages also leave out other influences on living standards, such as public services and the environment [1]. Judgement: the data support a small rise in average material living standards, but not a clear improvement for most people [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Convert to real terms first; the evaluation then questions what an average real wage leaves out.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing by the index of the wrong year.
- Adding inflation rates across several years.
- Giving a real figure with no price year.
- Using the approximation when exact working is asked for.
- Forgetting population in "per head" questions.
- Concluding from money values alone.
