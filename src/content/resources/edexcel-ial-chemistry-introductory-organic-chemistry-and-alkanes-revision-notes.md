## Definitions to learn word for word (4.1, 4.4, 4.7–4.10)

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Hazard | Something with the potential to cause harm |
| Risk | The likelihood that a hazard causes harm under the conditions of use |
| Homologous series | Members share a functional group and a general formula; neighbouring members differ by CH₂ |
| Functional group | The part of a molecule that determines its characteristic reactions |
| Hydrocarbon | A compound of carbon and hydrogen only |
| Saturated | Contains single bonds only |
| Free radical | A species with an unpaired electron |
| Electrophile | A species that accepts a pair of electrons to form a new covalent bond |
| Homolytic fission | Each bonded atom keeps one electron of the shared pair; free radicals form |
| Heterolytic fission | One bonded atom keeps both shared electrons; ions form |
| Structural isomers | Same molecular formula, different structural formula |
| Carbon-neutral | No net CO₂ added to the atmosphere over the fuel's whole life cycle |

## Reducing risk (4.2–4.3)

Organic liquids are often volatile, flammable and sometimes toxic, so every practical needs a risk
assessment. The three routes the specification lists:

- smaller scale (a few cm³ or a few grams)
- precautions matched to the hazard (fume cupboard for toxic vapour; water bath or electric heater, no
  flame, for flammable liquids; gloves and goggles for corrosives)
- a different method using less hazardous substances.

Always link the precaution to the named hazard. "Wear goggles" alone does not answer a question about a
flammable solvent.

## Naming, drawing and classifying (4.5–4.6)

**Stems:** meth- C₁, eth- C₂, prop- C₃, but- C₄, pent- C₅, hex- C₆, hept- C₇, oct- C₈, non- C₉, dec- C₁₀.

**Method in steps: naming a branched alkane**

1. Find the longest chain, including any route through a branch.
2. Number from the end that gives the lowest locants.
3. Name branches with positions; di-, tri- for repeats; alphabetical order.
4. Check: no branch should be on carbon 1, and no ethyl should be on carbon 2.

**Three formula types**

| Type | What it shows |
|---|---|
| Structural | Groups in order, e.g. CH₃CH₂CH(CH₃)₂ |
| Displayed | Every atom and every bond |
| Skeletal | Zigzag of C–C bonds; carbons at corners and ends; no C–H drawn |

**Reaction types**

| Type | Signal in the equation |
|---|---|
| Addition | Two reactants, one product |
| Substitution | Atom or group swapped; two products |
| Oxidation | O gained or H lost |
| Reduction | H gained or O lost |
| Polymerisation | n monomers → one long chain |

## Alkanes and isomers (4.9–4.11)

| Series | General formula | First member |
|---|---|---|
| Alkanes | CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ | methane, CH₄ |
| Cycloalkanes | CₙH₂ₙ | cyclopropane, C₃H₆ |

Mᵣ check: alkane Mᵣ = 14n + 2; cycloalkane Mᵣ = 14n.

**Isomer counts to know (structural only)**

- C₄H₁₀: butane, 2-methylpropane
- C₅H₁₂: pentane, 2-methylbutane, 2,2-dimethylpropane
- C₆H₁₄: five isomers (hexane, two methylpentanes, two dimethylbutanes)
- Cycloalkanes C₅H₁₀: cyclopentane, methylcyclobutane, ethylcyclopropane, 1,1-dimethylcyclopropane,
  1,2-dimethylcyclopropane

**Method in steps: finding every isomer**

1. Draw the straight chain.
2. Remove one carbon and place it as a methyl on each possible position (not the end).
3. Remove two carbons: two methyls, or one ethyl if the chain is long enough.
4. Name each one and cross out any duplicates with the same name.
5. For cycloalkanes, repeat for each ring size from the full ring down to three carbons.

## Crude oil and fuels (4.12)

| Process | What it does | Example equation |
|---|---|---|
| Fractional distillation | Separates crude oil into fractions by boiling temperature | Physical, no equation |
| Cracking | Long alkane → shorter alkane + alkene | C₁₂H₂₆ → C₆H₁₄ + 2C₃H₆ |
| Reforming | Straight chain → branched or cyclic, for better combustion in engines | C₆H₁₄ → C₆H₁₂ (cyclohexane) + H₂ |

Why crack: demand for short-chain fuels like petrol exceeds supply, and alkenes are needed for polymers.

Cracking practical (further suggested): heat aluminium oxide strongly, let heat reach paraffin on
mineral wool, collect gas over water, test with bromine water (decolourised). Remove the delivery tube
from the water before heating stops to prevent suck-back.

## Combustion and pollutants (4.13–4.14, 4.17)

**Method in steps: balancing combustion of CₓHᵧ**

1. Carbon: x CO₂ (or x CO for incomplete combustion).
2. Hydrogen: y/2 H₂O.
3. Count oxygen atoms on the right; halve for O₂.
4. A half-integer amount of O₂ means you multiply every coefficient by two.

Example: cyclopentane, 2C₅H₁₀ + 15O₂ → 10CO₂ + 10H₂O.

| Pollutant | Source | Problem |
|---|---|---|
| Carbon monoxide | Incomplete combustion | Binds to haemoglobin more strongly than O₂, reducing oxygen transport |
| Oxides of nitrogen | N₂ + O₂ at high engine temperature | Acidic: NO₂ forms nitric acid, so acid rain |
| Oxides of sulfur | Sulfur impurities in fuel | Acidic: SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃, so acid rain |
| Carbon particulates | Incomplete combustion | Soot |
| Unburned hydrocarbons | Fuel that does not burn | Released into the air |

The specification limits the "problems" to CO toxicity and the acidity of the nitrogen and sulfur
oxides, so learn those two explanations in full.

## Alternative fuels (4.15–4.16)

Reasons to develop them: crude oil is finite (sustainability), and burning fossil fuels adds CO₂, which
is linked to climate change.

| Fuel | Carbon-neutral? | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| Petrol | No | Releases fossil carbon locked away for millions of years |
| Bioethanol | Nearly, not fully | CO₂ from fermentation and combustion was taken in by photosynthesis; farming, processing and transport add CO₂ |
| Hydrogen | Depends on production | Burns to water only; made from methane it releases carbon oxides; from electrolysis it is only as clean as the electricity |

## Free radical substitution (4.17–4.18)

Conditions: alkane + Cl₂ or Br₂, ultraviolet light. Example: cyclohexane and chlorine.

| Stage | Equation | Feature |
|---|---|---|
| Initiation | Cl₂ → 2Cl• | Homolytic fission by UV; two curly half-arrows |
| Propagation 1 | Cl• + C₆H₁₂ → C₆H₁₁• + HCl | Radical in, radical out |
| Propagation 2 | C₆H₁₁• + Cl₂ → C₆H₁₁Cl + Cl• | Cl• regenerated |
| Termination | Cl• + Cl• → Cl₂; C₆H₁₁• + Cl• → C₆H₁₁Cl; 2C₆H₁₁• → C₁₂H₂₂ | Two radicals pair up |

Limited use in synthesis: the product can be substituted further (C₆H₁₀Cl₂ and beyond), and
termination gives side products, so you get a mixture and a low yield of any one compound.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Hazard vs risk**: the property versus the likelihood of harm.
- **Homolytic vs heterolytic**: radicals versus ions; half-arrows versus full arrows.
- **Alkane vs cycloalkane**: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ versus CₙH₂ₙ; a cycloalkane shares its formula with an alkene.
- **Cracking vs reforming**: cracking shortens chains and makes alkenes; reforming keeps the carbon count and changes shape.
- **Complete vs incomplete combustion**: CO₂ versus CO or C.

## Quick self-test

1. Is "ethoxyethane is extremely flammable" a hazard or a risk?
2. Give the stem for nine carbons.
3. An alkane has Mᵣ = 142. Give its molecular formula.
4. A cycloalkane has eight hydrogen atoms. Give its formula and Mᵣ.
5. Name CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₃.
6. Classify C₂H₄ + HBr → C₂H₅Br.
7. How many moles of O₂ burn one mole of heptane, C₇H₁₆, completely?
8. Write an equation for cracking decane into butane and ethene only.
9. Explain in one sentence why carbon monoxide is toxic.
10. Which stage of free radical substitution forms two radicals from one molecule?
11. Calculate the maximum mass of chlorocyclohexane from 8.40 g of cyclohexane (C = 12.0, H = 1.0, Cl = 35.5).
12. Why is hydrogen not automatically a carbon-neutral fuel?

### Answers

1. Hazard (a property of the substance).
2. Non-.
3. 14n + 2 = 142, so n = 10: C₁₀H₂₂.
4. C₄H₈; Mᵣ = 56.
5. 2-methylhexane.
6. Addition.
7. 7 + 16/4 = 11 mol.
8. C₁₀H₂₂ → C₄H₁₀ + 3C₂H₄.
9. It binds to haemoglobin more strongly than oxygen, so the blood carries less oxygen.
10. Initiation.
11. 8.40 ÷ 84.0 = 0.100 mol; 0.100 × 118.5 = 11.9 g.
12. Its production may release CO₂, for example when it is made from methane or by electrolysis using fossil-fuel electricity.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "risk is how dangerous a chemical is". That describes a hazard.
- Giving a generic precaution (goggles) when the question names a different hazard (flammability).
- Naming from the row as drawn when a longer chain runs through a branch.
- Giving 1-methylbutane or 2-ethylpropane: both mean the longest chain was missed.
- Leaving out alkenes as products when balancing a cracking equation.
- Saying CO "reduces oxygen in the air" rather than linking it to haemoglobin.
- Saying oxides of nitrogen "come from the fuel". They form from atmospheric nitrogen at high temperature.
- Using full curly arrows for homolytic fission, or putting the dot on the wrong atom of an alkyl radical.
- Stating that bioethanol is carbon-neutral without the life-cycle qualification.
