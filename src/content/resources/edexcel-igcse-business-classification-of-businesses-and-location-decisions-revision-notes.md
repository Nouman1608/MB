## 1. The three sectors (1.3.1)

| Sector | Specification wording | Typical activities | Clue verbs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary | Extracting raw materials from the earth | Farming, fishing, forestry, mining | grows, catches, digs, drills |
| Secondary | Converting raw materials into finished or semi-finished goods | Manufacturing, construction, food processing | makes, builds, assembles, refines |
| Tertiary | Provision of a wide variety of services | Retail, banking, transport, teaching, repairs | sells, delivers, advises, cleans |

Must-know distinctions:

- A **semi-finished** good (steel sheet, sawn timber, flour) is still secondary output. It is an input for another business.
- A business that **grows tomatoes** is primary. One that **turns them into sauce** is secondary. One that **sells the sauce** is tertiary.
- One business can sit in several sectors. Classify each activity, not the company name.
- Sector share of revenue = revenue from that sector / total revenue x 100.

Method in steps for a sector-share question:

1. Add the revenues to find the total.
2. Divide each sector's revenue by the total.
3. Multiply by 100 and check the shares add to 100%.
4. Say what the biggest share means for the business.

## 2. Location factors at a glance (1.4.1)

| Factor | Why it can pull a business towards a site | Why it can push it away |
|---|---|---|
| Proximity to market | Easy access for customers, low delivery cost, fresh goods | Rent and rates in busy areas are high |
| Proximity to labour | Easy recruitment, right skills, lower wage costs | Competition for staff can raise wages; remote sites mean transport or relocation of staff |
| Proximity to materials | Lower transport cost, less waste or spoilage, reliable supply | Remote or hard-to-reach supply sites; the market may be far away |
| Proximity to competitors | Clusters attract customers and skilled workers | Customers can compare and switch; price pressure |
| Nature of the business activity | Matches the site to what the activity needs (resource, space, footfall) | Some activities have almost no choice (mining) |
| Internet / e-commerce | Cheaper premises, wider market | Delivery and website costs; customers who want to see goods |
| Legal controls | Clear rules, protected neighbourhoods | Restrictions on use of land, noise, pollution; extra compliance costs |
| Trade blocs | Free or easier trade with members; no tariff inside | Tariffs on goods coming from outside |

### Which factor leads?

Use this guide to get started, then check it against the case.

| If the business... | The factor that often leads |
|---|---|
| Handles heavy, bulky or perishable inputs | Materials |
| Sells to walk-in customers | Market |
| Needs particular skills in quantity | Labour |
| Extracts a resource | Nature of the activity |
| Sells small, light goods online | Internet (cheaper premises) |
| Exports to a bloc with a tariff | Trade bloc |

These are tendencies, not rules. A bulky product with a very small customer base may be driven by the market.

## 3. The internet and location (1.4.1)

- **E-commerce** is buying and selling online.
- Benefits: lower-cost premises, wider market, longer opening hours, lower staffing at the point of sale.
- Costs and limits: delivery, packaging, returns, website upkeep, no chance to touch or try goods, dependence on carriers.
- **Fixed premises** still matter when customers want to see goods, receive a personal service or collect quickly.
- Many businesses combine both.

Quick reminder calculation: a business ships 9,000 online orders at £3.20 delivery each. Delivery cost = 9,000 x 3.20 = £28,800.

## 4. Legal controls and trade blocs (1.4.1)

- **Legal controls**: rules that limit where or how a business may operate (use of land, noise, pollution, safety). Effects: a site may be unavailable, costs may rise, or a firm may be pushed away from housing. Describe the effect. Do not invent a statute.
- **Trade bloc**: a group of countries that agree to reduce trade barriers between themselves. This is the standard form of the definition. Inside the bloc, trade is easier. Outsiders may pay a **tariff** (a tax on imports).
- Reminder calculation: a 10% tariff on £80,000 of imports = 0.10 x 80,000 = £8,000.

Compare the cost of locating inside and outside a bloc by adding the tariff to the outside cost, then comparing totals.

## 5. Relocation (1.4.1)

| Reasons a business may move | One-off costs and risks |
|---|---|
| Rent or other costs have risen | Moving and fitting-out costs |
| Business has outgrown its premises | Disruption to production and sales |
| Customers, labour or materials are now elsewhere | Staff who will not move |
| A new legal control restricts the site | Customers who cannot find the new site |
| A trade bloc change affects tariffs | Possible higher transport costs |

Method for a relocation decision:

1. Find the annual saving (or extra cost) of the new site.
2. Subtract extra costs, such as transport.
3. Compare the one-off cost with the annual net saving: one-off cost / annual net saving = years to recover.
4. Weigh the non-financial effects (staff, customers, risk).
5. Make a decision and give a reason.

## Command words for this topic

The specification's command-word table describes the answers as follows.

| Command word | What the table says | How to use it here |
|---|---|---|
| Define | define a term from the specification content | One clear sentence, such as the sector definitions above |
| Outline | two linked points, placed in context | Link cause and effect in the case |
| Explain | a statement of fact with two expansion points, no context | General statement, then two developments |
| Analyse | extended answer, placed in context | Chain of reasoning using case facts |
| Justify | extended answer recommending one of two options | Choose, then say why the other is weaker |
| Evaluate | extended answer reaching a supported conclusion | Weigh both sides, then judge |

## Worked reminders

**Comparing two sites.** A bakery expects site P to cost £92,000 a year and bring in £120,000, and site Q to cost £81,000 and bring in £104,000. Profit at P = 120,000 - 92,000 = £28,000. Profit at Q = 104,000 - 81,000 = £23,000. P is £5,000 better on the figures. Then ask what the forecasts assume and what the figures leave out.

**Reading a trade-off.** Cheap rent far from customers lowers one cost but may cut revenue and raise delivery cost. Always follow a cost saving through to its knock-on effect.

**Writing an analyse paragraph.** Point (the factor), evidence (a fact from the case), consequence (what it does to cost, sales or convenience), link (why that matters for the decision).

## Quick self-test

1. Which sector does an oil-extraction company belong to?
2. Which sector does a car assembly plant belong to?
3. Which sector does an accountancy firm belong to?
4. A firm has total revenue of £150,000, of which £45,000 comes from farming. What percentage is primary?
5. Name the four proximity factors in 1.4.1.
6. Why is the location of a quarry largely fixed?
7. A town's wage is £18,000 and another's is £15,300. Calculate the percentage fall in wage cost.
8. State one cost that rises when a shop moves online.
9. A bloc charges a 10% tariff on goods worth £80,000. State the tariff.
10. A move costs £90,000 and saves £30,000 a year net. How many years to recover the cost?
11. Define "trade bloc".

### Answers

1. Primary.
2. Secondary.
3. Tertiary.
4. 45,000 / 150,000 x 100 = 30%.
5. Market, labour, materials, competitors.
6. The activity must take place where the resource is.
7. (18,000 - 15,300) / 18,000 x 100 = 15%.
8. Delivery (or packaging, returns, or website upkeep).
9. £8,000.
10. 90,000 / 30,000 = 3 years.
11. A group of countries that agree to reduce barriers to trade between themselves.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving a sector without a reason when the question asks you to link the activity to its definition.
- Classifying the company by its name rather than the activity described.
- Listing factors without saying how they change cost, revenue or convenience for the business in the case.
- Treating competitors as only a threat.
- Writing that the internet means a business needs no premises.
- Forgetting to add the tariff to the outside cost before comparing.
- Leaving out one-off costs in a relocation decision.
- Stopping at figures in an evaluate or justify answer, with no judgement.
- Describing legal controls in detail that is not in the question, or naming a law you are unsure of.
