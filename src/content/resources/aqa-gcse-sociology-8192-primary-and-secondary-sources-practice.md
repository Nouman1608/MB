## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Which of the following is a secondary source for a sociologist studying school exclusions?

A. Interviews she carries out with excluded pupils
B. Her observation of a pupil referral unit
C. Government figures on exclusions
D. A questionnaire she gives to teachers

**[1]**

**2.** The systematic study of documents or media content is called:

A. Ethnography
B. Content analysis
C. Triangulation
D. A longitudinal study

**[1]**

**3.** Explain the difference between primary and secondary data. **[2]**

**4.** Give **two** examples of personal documents. **[2]**

**5.** Identify and explain **one** limitation of using previous research as a secondary source. **[3]**

**6.** Identify and explain **one** reason why a researcher might ask whether a personal diary is believable. **[3]**

### Section B: longer and structured questions

**7.** A researcher carried out a content analysis of 40 fictional children's picture books. The data are fictional.

| Character shown | Cooking or cleaning | Fixing or building things |
|---|---|---|
| Female adult | 36 | 6 |
| Male adult | 9 | 30 |

**(a)** What percentage of the cooking or cleaning scenes showed a female adult? **[1]**
**(b)** Explain what these data suggest about gender socialisation. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain **one** limitation of content analysis for studying how children learn gender roles. **[2]**

**8.** A sociologist wants to study how family life in a northern town has changed since the 1950s. Explain **one** secondary source and **one** primary source she could use, and why each would be useful. **[4]**

**9.** Read the item.

> Linwood Academy (a fictional school) publishes a "Student Wellbeing Policy" on its website. It states that "every pupil feels safe and listened to". A researcher wants to study pupils' experiences of bullying at the school.

Using the item, explain **two** reasons why the policy would be of limited use for this research. **[4]**

**10.** Explain **two** advantages of using secondary sources rather than primary research for a study of poverty across the whole UK. **[4]**

**11.** Discuss how far secondary sources are more useful than primary sources for studying crime. **[12]**

**12.** A sociologist wants to study how newspapers represent young people. Describe and explain how she could use content analysis, and assess its usefulness. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** C. Government figures on exclusions [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* the other three are collected first-hand by the researcher, so they are primary.

**2.** B. Content analysis [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* ethnography studies a way of life by observation, not documents.

**3.** Primary data are collected first-hand by the researcher for their own study [1], whereas secondary data already exist and were produced by someone else, usually for a different purpose [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* the contrast "whereas" plus "who collected it" earns both marks.

**4.** One correct example, such as a diary [1], and a second, such as a letter, an autobiography or a personal blog [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* government reports are public, not personal, documents.

**5.** Previous research was carried out for a different aim [1], so its questions and definitions may not match what the new researcher wants to know [1]. For example, an older study of housework may not have asked about childcare or emotional work, so it cannot answer a new question about these [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "it may be out of date" is also valid but needs an explanation of why that matters.

**6.** The writer may not have been sincere or accurate [1], for example exaggerating events or leaving out things that show them badly [1]. If the diary was written with a reader in mind, or long after the events, it may give a distorted picture, reducing its validity as evidence [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "believable" is about the author's honesty and accuracy, not whether the document is a fake.

**7. (a)** 36 + 9 = 45 cooking or cleaning scenes; 36 ÷ 45 × 100 = 80% [1]
**(b)** Female adults are mostly shown cooking or cleaning and male adults fixing or building [1], so children reading these books may learn that domestic work is for women and practical work for men, reinforcing traditional gender roles [1].
**(c)** Content analysis shows what is in the books but not how children interpret them [1], so children may ignore, question or not notice the gender messages, meaning the effect on their gender roles cannot be assumed [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* in (b), describe the pattern and then explain its meaning; both steps carry a mark.

**8.** Secondary source: official records or earlier studies of family life in the town [1], useful because they show what families were like in the 1950s, which primary research cannot reach directly [1]. Primary source: unstructured interviews with older residents who grew up in the town [1], useful because they can describe in their own words how family life has changed over their lifetime [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* each source needs a reason linked to studying change over time.

**9.** First, the policy was written by the school for its own purpose [1], probably to present a positive image to parents and inspectors, so the claim that "every pupil feels safe" may not reflect reality [1]. Second, the policy describes how the school intends things to work, not what pupils actually experience [1], so it cannot show how often bullying happens or how pupils feel about it; interviews with pupils would be needed [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* quote the item's claim and question whether it is believable.

**10.** First, official statistics on income and benefits cover the whole country [1], which no single researcher could afford to collect through primary research [1]. Second, secondary data are usually free and quick to obtain [1], and data collected regularly allow the researcher to compare poverty over many years, which a new study could not do [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "cheap" alone is one mark; explain why that matters for a national study.

**11.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Secondary sources include official crime statistics, court records, earlier studies and media reports [1].
- Official statistics cover the whole country and show trends over time [1].
- Victim surveys produced by government agencies estimate crime not reported to the police [1].
- Secondary sources avoid some ethical and safety risks of studying offenders directly [1].
- They are quick and cheap [1].
- Against: recorded crime misses the dark figure of crime [1].
- Statistics are shaped by police recording decisions, so interactionists see them as social constructs [1].
- Media reports are selective and may exaggerate some crimes [1].
- Primary sources, such as Carlen's unstructured interviews with women convicted of crimes, reveal motives and experiences [1].
- Primary research can be designed for the exact aim, but access to offenders and ethical issues are difficult [1].
- Mixed sources allow triangulation between statistics and interviews [1].
- Judgement: secondary sources are more useful for measuring crime patterns, but primary sources are more useful for explaining why people offend, so usefulness depends on the research aim [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* name at least one primary and one secondary example by name; abstract answers struggle to reach the top level.

**12.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Select a sample of newspapers over a set period, such as several titles over three months [1].
- Design a coding sheet with categories, such as young people shown as victims, offenders or achievers [1].
- Count how often each category appears to produce quantitative data [1].
- Add qualitative analysis of the words used in headlines to describe young people [1].
- Strength: newspapers are easy to access and cheap to study [1].
- Strength: the coding sheet can be reused by another researcher, making the findings fairly reliable [1].
- Limitation: deciding which category a story fits involves the researcher's judgement, so bias is possible [1].
- Limitation and judgement: content analysis shows how young people are represented but not how readers interpret it, so it is useful for representation but should be combined with audience research [1]. **[8]**

*Examiner insight:* "describe and explain how" needs practical steps; "assess" needs a judgement at the end.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining primary data as "from people" and secondary as "from books".
- Saying secondary data are always less reliable.
- Not quoting the item when judging a document.
- Forgetting that content analysis shows content, not audience effects.
- Choosing sources without explaining their link to the area of research.
