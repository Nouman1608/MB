Section 3.2.2.6 of the AQA GCSE Economics (8136) specification has four content points: "Income and wealth inequality", "Causes of income and wealth inequality", "Consequences of income and wealth inequality" and "Government policies to redistribute income and wealth and their consequences". The additional guidance asks you to understand "the distribution of income in the UK", "how inequality in the distribution of income can occur" and "how redistribution of income and wealth can be achieved through taxation and government spending". This guide follows a household's income through each stage of the tax and benefit system, which is the clearest way to see redistribution at work.

The section sits in 3.2 How the economy works, assessed on Paper 2, with no tiers. Figures are invented. ## Income, wealth and how the UK measures them

**Income** is a flow received over a period: wages and salaries, profit from running a business, rent, interest, dividends, pensions and benefits. **Wealth** is a stock of assets owned at a point in time: houses, savings, shares and pension funds. Wealth produces income (shares pay dividends, property earns rent), and saving income adds to wealth, so the two feed each other.

The Office for National Statistics ranks UK households from the lowest income to the highest and divides them into five equal groups (**quintiles**) or ten (**deciles**). It then traces each group's income through a series of stages:

| Stage | How it is reached |
|---|---|
| Original income | Income from employment, self-employment, investments and private pensions |
| Gross income | Original income plus cash benefits, such as the State Pension and Universal Credit |
| Disposable income | Gross income minus direct taxes, such as income tax and National Insurance |
| Post-tax income | Disposable income minus indirect taxes, such as VAT and fuel duty |
| Final income | Post-tax income plus the value of benefits in kind, such as state education and the NHS |

In the UK, as in most countries, original income is very unequal, and the gap narrows at each later stage. Wealth is more unequally distributed than income.

### Worked example: two households through the stages (invented)

| Stage | Household L | Household H | H as a multiple of L |
|---|---|---|---|
| Original income | £10,000 | £90,000 | 9.0 |
| + cash benefits | £6,000 | £500 | |
| − direct taxes | £500 | £22,000 | |
| Disposable income | £15,500 | £68,500 | 4.4 |
| − indirect taxes | £2,500 | £6,000 | |
| + benefits in kind | £8,000 | £4,000 | |
| Final income | £21,000 | £66,500 | 3.2 |

Three lessons come from the table:

1. **Cash benefits and direct taxes do most of the narrowing.** The gap falls from 9 times to about 4.4 times.
2. **Indirect taxes work the other way.** Household L pays £2,500 ÷ £15,500 = 16.1% of its disposable income in indirect taxes; Household H pays £6,000 ÷ £68,500 = 8.8%. Indirect taxes take a larger share of low incomes, so they are **regressive**.
3. **Benefits in kind matter.** Services such as schools and healthcare are worth more relative to income for poorer households, which narrows the gap further to about 3.2 times.

## Why inequality occurs

**Causes of income inequality**

- **Wage differences.** Workers with scarce skills and high productivity earn more; low-skilled jobs with many applicants pay less.
- **Hours and security of work.** Part-time, temporary and zero-hours work bring lower and less certain earnings.
- **Unemployment and inactivity.** Households with nobody in work depend on benefits.
- **Returns on wealth.** People who own property and shares receive rent, dividends and interest on top of earnings.
- **Discrimination.** Unequal treatment by gender, ethnicity or disability can lower some groups' pay.
- **Region.** Pay differs between parts of the UK.

**Causes of wealth inequality**

- **Inheritance.** Wealth passes between generations, so differences persist.
- **Saving out of high incomes.** People on high incomes can save more each year, and savings grow over time.
- **Rising asset prices.** When house and share prices rise, people who already own them gain; people who do not are left further behind.

## Consequences of inequality

| Possible costs | Possible benefits |
|---|---|
| Poverty and poorer health among low-income groups | Higher rewards give incentives to train, work and take risks |
| Children from poorer homes have fewer opportunities, so inequality lasts across generations | Successful entrepreneurs create jobs and pay taxes |
| Social tension and lower trust | Savings by higher earners can fund investment |
| Low-income households spend less, which limits demand | |
| More government spending on benefits | |

Whether inequality is "too high" is a **value judgement**. Economics can show the effects; it cannot settle what is fair.

## How taxes and spending redistribute

**Through taxation**

- A **progressive** tax takes a higher percentage of income as income rises. UK income tax is progressive because higher bands of income are taxed at higher rates.
- A **proportional** tax takes the same percentage from everyone.
- A **regressive** tax takes a higher percentage from people on low incomes, as indirect taxes do in the example above.
- Taxes on wealth and its transfer, such as inheritance tax and capital gains tax, target wealth inequality directly.

**Through government spending**

- **Means-tested benefits**, such as Universal Credit, go only to households below an income threshold, so they are targeted at the poorest.
- **Universal benefits** are paid regardless of income, so they are simpler to run but less targeted.
- **Benefits in kind**, such as state education and the NHS, provide services free at the point of use.
- Spending on **education and training** raises the future earning power of people from low-income families, tackling a cause of inequality rather than its outcome.

## Consequences of redistribution

- **Reduced poverty and a fairer spread of opportunities**, which may raise skills and productivity over time.
- **Incentive effects.** Very high tax rates may discourage extra work or encourage tax avoidance. Means-tested benefits are withdrawn as earnings rise, which can reduce the reward from working more hours.
- **Cost.** Benefits and public services must be funded by taxes or borrowing.
- **Conflict with other objectives.** If incentives weaken, growth could be slower.

## Annotated model answer

*Question (original):* Explain why a rise in VAT may increase income inequality. **[3]**

*Model answer:* VAT is an indirect tax paid as part of the price of goods and services [identifies the tax]. Low-income households spend a larger share of their income than high-income households, who save more [the key mechanism]. So VAT takes a higher percentage of low incomes, which makes it regressive, and a rise widens the gap in income after tax [links to inequality].

## Common mistakes

- Saying a tax is progressive because rich people pay more money. It must take a larger **percentage** of income.
- Confusing income with wealth, or using "wealthy" and "high-income" as if they always mean the same person.
- Describing redistribution only through taxes and forgetting government spending.
- Listing costs of inequality with no benefit, or giving no judgement.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate** questions: give tax as a percentage of income and compare; state whether the result is progressive or regressive.
- **Explain** questions: always link a policy to a change in the **gap** between groups.
- **Evaluate** questions: weigh fairness against incentives and cost, and say which policy tackles the cause and which the outcome.
