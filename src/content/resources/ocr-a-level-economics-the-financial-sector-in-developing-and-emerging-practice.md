## Questions

**1.** An economy has a savings ratio of 18% and a capital-output ratio of 4.5. Use the Harrod-Domar model to calculate its growth rate. **[2]**

**2.** Country Delta has national income of 300 units, a savings ratio of 14% and a capital-output ratio of 2.8. Its target growth rate is 6%.

**(a)** Calculate the growth rate the model predicts. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the investment needed for the target and the savings gap. **[2]**
**(c)** Better infrastructure cuts the capital-output ratio to 2.1. Calculate the new predicted growth rate and comment. **[2]**

**3.** Explain two limitations of the Harrod-Domar model for a developing economy. **[4]**

**4.** **(a)** Explain what is meant by microfinance. **[2]**
**(b)** A trader borrows 500 units for a year. A microfinance lender charges a flat 30% a year. An informal moneylender charges 8% a month on the original sum. Calculate the interest under each and compare the annual rates. **[3]**

**5.** Evaluate the role of microfinance in supporting economic development. **[6]**

**6.** Country E has national income of 800 units. In year 1 it receives foreign direct investment of 24, equity investment of 16, bond purchases of 12 and bank loans of 8. In year 2 foreign direct investment is 22, portfolio investment is −10 and bank lending is −6.

**(a)** Calculate year 1 net inflows and their share of national income. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate year 2 net flows and the change from year 1. **[2]**

**7.** Evaluate the extent to which international financial markets have influenced capital flows into and out of developing and emerging economies. **[6]**

**8.** Country A has public debt of 45 units, national income of 90, export earnings of 30 and annual debt repayments of 6.

**(a)** Calculate the debt-to-GDP ratio and the debt service ratio. **[2]**
**(b)** Firms in Country A borrowed 20 units of foreign currency when the rate was 5 local units per unit of foreign currency. It is now 7. Calculate the percentage rise in the local-currency cost. **[3]**

**9.** Country G has a debt ratio of 50%, an interest rate of 5% and a primary balance of zero.

**(a)** Calculate next year's ratio if national income grows by 4%. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate it if growth is 1%. **[1]**
**(c)** State what the results show. **[1]**

**10.** Evaluate the impact of public and private sector debt on developing and emerging economies. **[6]**

**11.** Country H has national income of 250 units, exports of 80 and receives remittances of 20.

**(a)** Explain what is meant by remittances. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate remittances as a share of national income and of exports. **[2]**
**(c)** Fees take 8% of each transfer. Calculate the extra amount households receive if fees fall to 4%. **[1]**
**(d)** Assess one benefit and one limitation of remittances for Country H. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** g = s / k [1] = 0.18 / 4.5 = **4%** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Convert percentages to decimals and show the formula; a bare number leaves nothing to credit if it is wrong.

**2. (a)** g = 0.14 / 2.8 = **5%** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Investment needed = 0.06 × 2.8 × 300 = 50.4 [1]. Saving = 0.14 × 300 = 42, so the gap is **8.4 units** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** g = 0.14 / 2.1 = **6.7%** [1], which exceeds the 6% target, so no gap remains; lowering the capital-output ratio can substitute for extra saving [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Part (c) invites a comment, so link the number to the target rather than stopping at the calculation.

**3.** Any two, each with explanation. All saving is assumed to be invested, but in practice funds may be held as cash, lent abroad or lost in a financial crisis [1], so the predicted growth overstates the result [1]. Capital is not the only constraint; skills, institutions and infrastructure also limit growth [1], so raising saving alone may not deliver it [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A limitation scores when it is paired with its effect on the model's prediction, not when it is merely named.

**4. (a)** Small loans, savings or insurance [1] offered to poor people and tiny firms that formal banks usually exclude [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Microfinance interest = 0.30 × 500 = 150 [1]. Moneylender = 0.08 × 500 × 12 = 480 [1]. Annual rates are 30% against 96%, so the informal loan costs more than three times as much [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Say whether each rate is monthly or annual before comparing, because mixed periods are the usual slip.

**5.** Benefits: credit without collateral lets poor households invest in tools or stock [1], and savings and insurance smooth income when shocks hit [1]. Limits: rates are often high, so borrowers can become over-indebted [1], and loans may fund consumption, not production [1]. Tiny firms may not grow because local markets are small [1]. Judgement: it helps where project returns exceed the interest rate, but it complements rather than replaces infrastructure, education and jobs [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Close with a judgement that names the condition it depends on, for example the return on the project against the interest rate.

**6. (a)** Net inflow = 24 + 16 + 12 + 8 = 60 [1]. Share = 60 / 800 = **7.5%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Net flow = 22 − 10 − 6 = **6** [1]. Change = 6 − 60 = **−54 units** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep the signs: outflows are negative, and adding them as positive figures gives the wrong net result.

**7.** Support: global interest rates and investor appetite for risk push funds in and out [1], and portfolio and bank flows can reverse quickly, as in a sudden stop [1]. Currency and interest rates then respond [1]. Against: pull factors such as institutions, policy credibility and growth prospects decide who attracts funds [1], and FDI is steadier because it is tied to productive assets [1]. Judgement: global markets strongly affect the timing and size of flows, but domestic conditions determine the effect [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Separate FDI from portfolio flows; treating all capital flows alike limits the evaluation.

**8. (a)** Debt-to-GDP = 45 / 90 = **50%** [1]. Debt service ratio = 6 / 30 = **20%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Old cost = 20 × 5 = 100 [1]. New cost = 20 × 7 = 140 [1]. Rise = 40 / 100 = **40%** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the old and new local-currency cost before the percentage, so the working can be followed.

**9. (a)** Next ratio = 0.50 × 1.05 / 1.04 [1] = **50.5%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 0.50 × 1.05 / 1.01 = **52.0%** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** The ratio rises whenever the interest rate exceeds growth, and rises faster when growth is slow [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the interest rate and the growth rate in the comment; the ratio alone does not explain the change.

**10.** Public debt can fund infrastructure that raises growth [1], but heavy debt service crowds out health and education spending [1] and may force tax rises or cuts [1]. Private debt can fuel booms and banking crises, and governments may absorb the losses [1]. Foreign-currency debt is exposed to depreciation [1]. Judgement: debt is harmful when its cost exceeds the growth it creates, so use, currency and terms matter [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Cover both public and private debt, since the question names both.

**11. (a)** Money sent home by migrants working abroad [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 20 / 250 = **8%** of national income [1]; 20 / 80 = **25%** of exports [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Fees fall from 1.6 to 0.8, so households receive **0.8 units** more [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** Benefit: raises household income and can fund education, health or small businesses [1], with a steady flow that supports the balance of payments [1]. Limitation: a large inflow can appreciate the currency and hurt exporters, or families may come to depend on it [1]. Judgement: at 8% of income it is significant, but its value depends on whether it is spent productively [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the 8% and 25% figures in your answer; generic comments miss what the data show.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing s × k instead of s / k, or leaving percentages as whole numbers.
- Stating the savings gap without the investment needed beside it.
- Mixing monthly and annual interest rates.
- Treating FDI, portfolio flows and bank lending as one thing.
- Judging debt by the debt-to-GDP ratio alone.
- Omitting the sign on outflows.
- Describing remittances as aid.
- Ending evaluation with "it depends" and no condition.
