## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by the product life cycle. **[2]**

**2.** Explain **one** way in which a marketing manager could use the product life cycle. **[3]**

**3.** Explain the purpose of an extension strategy. **[3]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**4.** Crisp Peel makes a vegetable snack. Its annual sales are shown below.

| Year | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 | 9 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sales (£000) | 40 | 150 | 420 | 780 | 940 | 975 | 960 | 890 | 720 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in sales between year 4 and year 5. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in sales between year 8 and year 9. **[2]**

**(c)** Identify the stage of the life cycle in year 3 and in year 9. Justify each answer using the data. **[4]**

**(d)** Analyse how Crisp Peel's marketing mix should change between year 5 and year 8. **[6]**

**5.** In year 9, Crisp Peel considers launching a new spicy flavour. It will cost £80,000 and is forecast to add £260,000 of sales a year at a margin of 25%.

**(a)** Calculate the extra contribution each year. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the payback period in years, to one decimal place. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the payback period if extra sales are only half the forecast, to one decimal place. **[2]**

**(d)** Evaluate whether Crisp Peel should launch the new flavour. **[9]**

**6.** Brecon Eyewear sells fashion sunglasses whose styles change every year. Sunrise Equipment makes industrial ovens that are sold for twenty years or more. Evaluate the usefulness of the product life cycle to each business. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** A model showing how the sales of a product change over time [1] from launch through growth and maturity to decline [1]. **[2]**

**2.** To choose the marketing mix for the stage the product is in [1], for example heavy promotion to build awareness at introduction [1], and cost-cutting or withdrawal in decline, which helps to use the budget where it earns most [1]. Accept other valid uses such as forecasting cash flow or timing a new product. **[3]**

**3.** To lengthen the life of a product that is mature or declining [1] by renewing demand, for example with a new flavour or market [1], which is usually cheaper than developing a new product because the brand and customers already exist [1]. **[3]**

**4. (a)** (940 ÷ 780 − 1) × 100 [1] = **20.5%** (to one decimal place) [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** (720 ÷ 890 − 1) × 100 [1] = **−19.1%** [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** Year 3: growth [1], because sales rose by 180% (150 to 420) and are rising fast [1]. Year 9: decline [1], because sales have fallen for three years in a row, from 975 to 720, and the fall in year 9 is the largest [1]. **[4]**

**(d)** Year 5: sales are still growing (20.5%) but slowing, so Crisp Peel should widen distribution and build brand preference as rivals enter [1]. Years 6 to 7: sales have peaked, with growth of 3.7% then a fall of 1.5% [1], so the focus should change to defending share through promotions, loyalty offers or product variations [1]. Price: competition is likely to increase in maturity, so the firm may need to hold prices or offer discounts, which reduces margin [1]. Year 8: sales fall by 7.3% [1], so Crisp Peel should reduce spending on promotion for the core product and look at an extension strategy or a new product, since decline is starting [1]. **[6]**

**5. (a)** £260,000 × 25% [1] = **£65,000** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** £80,000 ÷ £65,000 [1] = **1.2 years** [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** Contribution = £32,500 [1]; £80,000 ÷ £32,500 = **2.5 years** [1]. **[2]**

**(d)** For: the payback of 1.2 years is short, and even at half the forecast it is 2.5 years [1]; the brand and distribution already exist, so launching a flavour is cheaper than a new product [1]; the sales boost could slow the decline [1].
Against: the snack is already declining (down 19.1% in year 9), so a flavour may not reverse the trend [1]; the new flavour may take sales from existing flavours, reducing the net gain [1]; the forecast is uncertain and a trial has not been done [1].
Further: the money might instead develop a new product, which is needed in the long run [1].
Judgement: launching the flavour is worthwhile if a small trial confirms demand, because the cost is low and the payback is short, but it should be part of a plan that includes a new product [2]. **[9]**

**6.** Brecon (fashion): the life cycle is short, so the model is useful to warn that sales will fall quickly and to plan the next range [1]; but each year's style is a new product, so the curve may be too short and unpredictable to forecast with accuracy [1]; promotion should be heavy at launch and stock cleared in decline [1].
Sunrise (industrial): a long life gives a clearer pattern of introduction, growth, maturity and decline over years, so the model helps to plan investment, replacement models and spare-parts income [1]; but the stages are long and affected by the economy and technology, so the curve may not show the true position [1]; customers are few, and their needs may matter more than a general curve [1].
General limits: the model is descriptive, not predictive, and ignores events outside the business [1].
Judgement: the model is useful for both but needs to be supported by other data. It is probably more helpful to Sunrise, where stages last long enough to plan around, while Brecon needs quick market feedback [2]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming the stage without quoting figures.
- Using the size of sales rather than the trend in growth.
- Treating the extension strategy as certain to work.
- Ignoring the cost and payback.
