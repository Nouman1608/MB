## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define the term *conjugal roles*. **[2]**

**2.** Identify **two** types of feminism named in the syllabus for this topic. **[2]**

**3.** Distinguish between *joint* and *segregated* conjugal roles. **[4]**

**4.** Explain what is meant by *emotion work*. **[3]**

**5.** Explain **one** way in which men may have more power than women in family decision-making. **[3]**

**6.** Explain what is meant by the *dual burden*. **[3]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** Explain **two** reasons why conjugal roles may have become more equal. **[6]**

**8.** Explain **two** ways in which family life may be negative for women. **[6]**

**9.** Evaluate the radical feminist theory of the family. **[8]**

**10.** Evaluate the liberal feminist view of gender equality in the family. **[8]**

**11.** *"Men and women now share housework and childcare equally."*

- **(a)** Explain the view expressed in the quotation. **[6]**
- **(b)** Using sociological material, give **two** arguments against the view expressed in the quotation. **[10]**

**12.** "The family is a positive experience for all its members." Evaluate this view. **[26]**

## Answers

### 1. Conjugal roles [2]

The roles played by partners in a marriage or cohabiting relationship [1], including how they divide paid work, housework, childcare, decisions and leisure [1]. **[2]**

### 2. Types of feminism [2]

Any two, one mark each: liberal feminism, radical feminism, Marxist feminism [1] [1]. **[2]**

### 3. Joint and segregated roles [4]

- Joint conjugal roles: partners share tasks, decisions and leisure [1], for example both cooking and both handling money (Bott, 1957) [1].
- Segregated conjugal roles: partners have separate tasks and leisure [1], for example a husband as breadwinner who spends leisure with male friends and a wife responsible for home and children [1]. **[4]**

### 4. Emotion work [3]

The work of managing other family members' feelings and emotional well-being [1], such as listening to worries, calming conflict or remembering family occasions [1]; Duncombe and Marsden (1995) found women do most of it, adding a "triple shift" to paid and domestic work [1]. **[3]**

### 5. Power in decision-making [3]

Way identified [1], explained [1] and supported [1]. For example: men may make the most important decisions [1], such as moving house or major spending, while women make frequent minor ones [1]; Edgell (1980) found this pattern among middle-class couples [1]. Also credit control of money (Pahl, 1989) or violence as control (Dobash and Dobash, 1979). **[3]**

### 6. Dual burden [3]

Women in paid employment continuing to do most of the unpaid domestic work [1], so they effectively carry two jobs [1]; Hochschild (1989) called the domestic part a "second shift" [1]. **[3]**

### 7. Reasons for more equal roles [6]

Up to 3 marks per reason.

- **Women's employment:** women's earnings give them more bargaining power, and couples must share tasks when both work (Young and Willmott, 1973).
- **Changing attitudes:** liberal feminist ideas and changing gender socialisation make men more willing to share.
- **Smaller families and home-centred leisure:** couples spend more time together at home.
- **Social policy:** parental leave for fathers and equal pay laws make shared roles easier. **[6]**

### 8. Family life negative for women [6]

Up to 3 marks per way.

- **Dual burden and triple shift:** women do most housework and emotion work alongside paid work, causing stress and fatigue.
- **Domestic violence:** Dobash and Dobash (1979) showed violence used by men to control wives.
- **Economic dependence:** women who reduce paid work for childcare lose income, pensions and power over money (Pahl, 1989).
- **Exploitation:** radical feminists (Delphy and Leonard, 1992) argue men benefit from women's unpaid work. **[6]**

### 9. Evaluating radical feminism [8]

Up to 4 marks for strengths and up to 4 for limitations.

**Strengths:**
- Highlights the persistence of women's greater share of housework and emotion work [1].
- Draws attention to domestic violence as an expression of male power (Dobash and Dobash, 1979) [1].
- Delphy and Leonard (1992) show how the family operates as an economic system benefiting men [1].
- Challenges the idealised functionalist view of the family [1].

**Limitations:**
- Treats all men and all women as uniform groups, ignoring class, ethnicity and individual differences [1].
- Ignores evidence of change, such as men's increased involvement in childcare [1].
- Liberal feminists argue reform is working [1].
- Underestimates women's agency, as many women choose and value family life [1]. **[8]**

### 10. Evaluating liberal feminism [8]

**Strengths:**
- Supported by evidence that men's share of housework and childcare has risen [1].
- Explains change through women's education, employment and legal reform [1].
- Recognises that socialisation can change, so inequality is not fixed [1].
- Has influenced policies such as equal pay and parental leave [1].

**Limitations:**
- Radical feminists argue legal equality has not changed patriarchal power [1].
- Oakley (1974) and Hochschild (1989) show inequality persisting in practice [1].
- Focuses on attitudes and laws rather than deeper structures such as the economy [1].
- Based mainly on the experience of educated, middle-class women in Western societies [1]. **[8]**

### 11(a). Explaining the view [6]

- Young and Willmott (1973) argued the family has become symmetrical [1].
- Women's employment means both partners earn and share domestic tasks [1].
- Joint conjugal roles have replaced segregated ones in many couples (Bott's distinction) [1].
- Fathers are more involved in childcare than in earlier generations [1].
- Liberal feminists see gender socialisation and attitudes changing [1].
- Policies such as paternity leave encourage shared parenting [1]. **[6]**

### 11(b). Arguing against the view [10]

Up to 5 marks per argument.

- **Oakley (1974):** Young and Willmott's measure exaggerated men's role; few husbands took a high share of housework.
- **Dual burden and second shift:** Hochschild (1989) found employed women still doing most domestic work after their paid day.
- **Childcare quality, not just quantity:** men often take the enjoyable tasks while women keep responsibility and the "mental load".
- **Emotion work:** Duncombe and Marsden (1995) found women doing most emotion work, a triple shift.
- **Power:** Edgell (1980) and Pahl (1989) show men controlling major decisions and money.

Credit any two developed arguments. **[10]**

### 12. Essay: is family life positive for all members? [26]

Marked by levels across AO1, AO2 and AO3.

**Indicative content for the view:**
- Functionalists: Parsons' stabilisation of adult personalities; Murdock's functions.
- Fletcher (1966): families meet needs more fully.
- Young and Willmott: symmetrical, home-centred family.
- Personal-life views: love, identity and support, including wider kin and chosen family.

**Indicative content against the view:**
- Leach (1967): the inward-looking nuclear family as a source of discontent.
- Laing (1971): damaging interaction.
- Radical feminists: patriarchal exploitation; Dobash and Dobash on violence.
- Dual burden, triple shift, unequal power over money and decisions.
- Child abuse and the experience of children (link to 4.2).
- Marxist feminists: the family serves capitalism at women's expense.

**Evaluation:** "all its members" invites comparison of men, women and children; experience varies by class, ethnicity and family type; positive and negative can coexist in the same family.

**Level guidance:** top-level answers compare perspectives and members' experiences with accurate evidence and reach a supported judgement; middle-level answers present both sides with limited evaluation; low-level answers describe one view.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "feminists" without naming liberal, radical or Marxist.
- Using Young and Willmott without Oakley's critique.
- Answering about housework when the question is about power or emotion work.
- Ignoring the word "all" in the essay.
- Evaluating a theory with only limitations.
