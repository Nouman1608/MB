These questions cover section 3.8.1, Coulomb's law, of OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630), which is International A-level content. Section A has short questions; Section B has longer, structured questions. If a point is unfamiliar, read the [Coulomb's law study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-coulombs-law-oxfordaqa/) or the [Coulomb's law revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-coulombs-law-oxfordaqa-revision-notes/) first.

Use 1/(4πε₀) = 8.99 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻², e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻², proton mass 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg and g = 9.81 N kg⁻¹. Treat air as a vacuum.

## Questions

### Section A

**1.** State Coulomb's law. **[2]**

**2.** Two point charges exert a force of 6.0 μN on each other. Which change gives a force of 1.5 μN?

A: doubling one charge

B: doubling the separation

C: halving the separation

D: halving both charges and halving the separation

**[1]**

**3.** The force between two point charges is F. Both charges are tripled and the separation is doubled. Find the new force in terms of F. **[2]**

**4.** Calculate the force between charges of +12 nC and +8.0 nC whose centres are 0.15 m apart, and state whether it is attractive or repulsive. **[3]**

**5.** Use Coulomb's law to show that the unit of ε₀ can be written as C² N⁻¹ m⁻². **[2]**

**6.** A conducting sphere of radius 1.0 cm carries +25 nC. A point charge of −4.0 nC is placed 4.0 cm from the surface of the sphere.

**(a)** State the distance you must use in Coulomb's law, and why. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the force on the point charge. **[2]**

### Section B

**7.** Three charges lie on a straight line. A (+2.0 nC) is at x = 0, B (+6.0 nC) at x = 0.12 m and C (−3.0 nC) at x = 0.20 m. Calculate the resultant force on C and state its direction. **[4]**

**8.** Charges of +9.0 nC and +1.0 nC are fixed 0.40 m apart.

**(a)** Calculate the distance from the +9.0 nC charge of the point between them where a third charge would experience no resultant force. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why the answer to (a) does not depend on the size or sign of the third charge. **[1]**

**9.** Two protons are 3.0 × 10⁻¹⁵ m apart.

**(a)** Calculate the electrostatic force between them. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the gravitational force between them. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the ratio of the two forces and comment on what it shows. **[2]**

**10.** Two identical small spheres, each of mass 0.80 g, hang from the same point on insulating threads 0.25 m long. They carry equal charges and come to rest with each thread at 8.0° to the vertical.

**(a)** Calculate the separation of the centres of the spheres. **[1]**

**(b)** Show that the electric force on each sphere is about 1.1 × 10⁻³ N. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the charge on each sphere. **[2]**

**11.** A charge of +5.0 nC sits at point P. A second +5.0 nC charge is 0.080 m due east of P and a third +5.0 nC charge is 0.060 m due north of P. Calculate the magnitude and direction of the resultant force on the charge at P. **[4]**

**12.** Two small charged spheres 0.12 m apart repel each other with a force of 2.0 mN.

**(a)** Calculate the separation at which the force would be 0.50 mN. **[2]**

**(b)** The charge on one sphere is then reversed in sign, at this new separation. State the size of the force and how its direction changes. **[1]**

**13.** A student says: "The +40 nC charge pushes the +2.0 nC charge twenty times harder than the +2.0 nC charge pushes it back." Explain why the student is wrong. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** The force between two point charges is directly proportional to the product of the charges [1] and inversely proportional to the square of their separation [1]. **[2]**

**2.** **B** [1]. Doubling r divides F by 4, giving 1.5 μN. **[1]**

**3.** Charges give a factor 3 × 3 = 9 [1]; doubling r divides by 4, so the new force is **9F/4** [1]. **[2]**

**4.** F = 8.99 × 10⁹ × 12 × 10⁻⁹ × 8.0 × 10⁻⁹ / 0.15² [1] = **3.8 × 10⁻⁵ N** [1]; repulsive, because the charges are alike [1]. **[3]**

**5.** ε₀ = Q₁Q₂ / (4πFr²) [1]; units = C² / (N × m²) = **C² N⁻¹ m⁻²** (4π has no unit) [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** 5.0 cm (0.050 m), measured from the centre of the sphere [1], because the charge on a sphere may be treated as if it were at its centre [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** F = 8.99 × 10⁹ × 25 × 10⁻⁹ × 4.0 × 10⁻⁹ / 0.050² [1] = **3.6 × 10⁻⁴ N**, attractive [1]. **[2]**

**7.** Force from A: r = 0.20 m, F = 8.99 × 10⁹ × 2.0 × 10⁻⁹ × 3.0 × 10⁻⁹ / 0.20² = 1.35 × 10⁻⁶ N [1]. Force from B: r = 0.080 m, F = 8.99 × 10⁹ × 6.0 × 10⁻⁹ × 3.0 × 10⁻⁹ / 0.080² = 2.53 × 10⁻⁵ N [1]. Both are attractions, so both point towards A and B, in the −x direction [1]. Resultant = **2.7 × 10⁻⁵ N in the −x direction** (towards B) [1]. **[4]**

**8. (a)** 9.0/x² = 1.0/(0.40 − x)² [1]; 3/x = 1/(0.40 − x), so 1.20 − 3x = x [1]; **x = 0.30 m** [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** The third charge multiplies both forces equally, so it cancels from both sides of the equation; if its sign changes, both forces reverse together and still balance [1]. **[1]**

**9. (a)** F = 8.99 × 10⁹ × (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹)² / (3.0 × 10⁻¹⁵)² [1] = **26 N** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** F = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × (1.67 × 10⁻²⁷)² / (3.0 × 10⁻¹⁵)² [1] = **2.1 × 10⁻³⁵ N** [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** Ratio = **1.2 × 10³⁶** [1]; the gravitational force is negligible compared with the electrostatic force between subatomic particles [1]. **[2]**

**10. (a)** r = 2 × 0.25 × sin 8.0° = **0.070 m** [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** Horizontal and vertical equilibrium give F = mg tan θ [1]; F = 0.80 × 10⁻³ × 9.81 × tan 8.0° = 1.10 × 10⁻³ N [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** Q² = F r² / (8.99 × 10⁹) = 1.10 × 10⁻³ × 0.0696² / 8.99 × 10⁹ [1]; Q = **2.4 × 10⁻⁸ C** (24 nC) [1]. **[2]**

**11.** Force from the east charge: 8.99 × 10⁹ × (5.0 × 10⁻⁹)² / 0.080² = 3.51 × 10⁻⁵ N, westwards [1]. Force from the north charge: 8.99 × 10⁹ × (5.0 × 10⁻⁹)² / 0.060² = 6.24 × 10⁻⁵ N, southwards [1]. Resultant = √(3.51² + 6.24²) × 10⁻⁵ = **7.2 × 10⁻⁵ N** [1], at tan⁻¹(6.24/3.51) = **61° south of west** [1]. **[4]**

**12. (a)** F falls by a factor 4, so r doubles [1]: **0.24 m** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Still **0.50 mN**, but now attractive rather than repulsive [1]. **[1]**

**13.** The two forces form a Newton's third law pair, so they are equal in size and opposite in direction [1]. Coulomb's law gives one value of F that depends on the product of both charges, and that value applies to each charge [1]. **[2]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Measuring to the surface of a sphere rather than its centre.
- Leaving nanocoulombs or centimetres unconverted.
- Adding force magnitudes that act in different directions.
- Giving a direction as "attractive" when a compass direction or axis direction was asked for.
- Using the thread length rather than the separation in a hanging-sphere question.
- Copying the value in a "show that" without an unrounded answer to one more significant figure.
