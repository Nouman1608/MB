This guide covers section **3.3.3 The concept of quality** of OxfordAQA International GCSE Business (9225), part of Topic 3, Business operations. The specification asks you to understand **customer expectations of appropriate quality** for goods and services; **how businesses identify and measure quality problems** and the **consequences of poor quality**; the methods of maintaining consistent quality, especially **total quality management (TQM)**, and its advantages; the **costs and benefits of maintaining quality**; and the **quality issues that arise as businesses grow**, particularly when **outsourcing and franchising** are used. All businesses and figures in this guide are invented.

For the whole topic, see the [Business Operations study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-business-operations/). Condensed notes are in the [revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-the-concept-of-quality-revision-notes/) and questions are in the [practice set](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-the-concept-of-quality-practice/).

## What is quality?

**Quality** is how well a good or service meets the needs and expectations of the customer. A high-quality product is reliable, safe, lasts a reasonable time and does what it promises. Quality is not the same as luxury. A cheap pair of scissors is high quality if it cuts well and lasts, and an expensive watch is low quality if it stops after a month.

### Customer expectations of appropriate quality

Customers judge quality against what they expect, which depends on the price paid and the type of product.

| Product | What customers expect |
|---|---|
| Budget bus ticket | Safe, on time and clean; not luxury seating |
| Premium restaurant meal | Fresh ingredients, careful cooking, attentive service and a pleasant setting |
| Smartphone | Works reliably, a good battery, regular software updates |
| Hospital | Safe, correct treatment, clean, respectful staff |

**For goods**, quality usually includes the materials, how well the product is made, durability, safety and how closely it matches the description. **For services**, quality includes reliability, speed, friendly and knowledgeable staff, accuracy and how problems are handled. Because services are produced and used at the same time, a mistake cannot be corrected before the customer sees it, so training and standards are especially important.

A business must match its quality to its market. Too little quality loses customers. Too much may raise costs and prices above what its customers will pay.

## Identifying and measuring quality problems

Businesses use several ways to find out whether quality is acceptable.

| Method | How it measures quality |
|---|---|
| Inspection and testing | Checking samples or every item for faults before they leave the factory |
| Defect rate | Percentage of items found faulty |
| Returns and refunds | The number of products customers send back |
| Complaints | The number and type of customer complaints |
| Customer feedback | Surveys, reviews and ratings |
| Mystery shoppers | Researchers pose as customers to rate service |
| Repeat purchases | Whether customers come back |

**Worked example 1: defect rate.** A toy factory inspects a batch of 15,000 toys and finds 450 faulty.

```
Defect rate = 450 / 15,000 x 100 = 3%
```

**Worked example 2: complaint rate.** A cinema had 2,400 customers in a week, and 120 complained about the sound.

```
Complaint rate = 120 / 2,400 x 100 = 5%
```

Measures such as these show whether quality is improving or getting worse and let managers set targets.

## Consequences of poor quality

Poor quality hurts a business in several ways.

- **Lost sales:** unhappy customers go elsewhere and tell others.
- **Damaged reputation:** it takes a long time to rebuild trust.
- **Extra costs:** refunds, repairs, replacements, wasted materials and rework.
- **Legal action:** unsafe or misleading products can lead to fines or compensation.
- **Lower staff morale:** staff dislike dealing with complaints and may leave.
- **Weaker bargaining power:** retailers may refuse to stock the product.

**Worked example 3: the cost of defects.** A factory makes 180,000 units a year. At a 3% defect rate, each faulty unit costs $18 to replace and handle.

```
Faulty units = 3% x 180,000 = 5,400
Cost of defects = 5,400 x $18 = $97,200 a year
```

At 1% the cost would be 1,800 x $18 = $32,400, so cutting the defect rate by 2 percentage points saves $64,800 a year.

## Methods of maintaining consistent quality

### Quality control

**Quality control** means inspecting products at the end of production, or at stages along the way, and rejecting those that are faulty. It finds faults but does not stop them happening, and inspection takes time and money.

### Quality assurance

**Quality assurance** focuses on preventing faults by setting standards for every stage of production and checking that procedures are followed.

### Total quality management (TQM)

**Total quality management (TQM)** is an approach in which **every employee** in every department is responsible for quality, and the aim is to get things right first time. Instead of relying on inspectors to find faults at the end, the business aims to prevent faults. TQM usually involves:

- training all staff in quality methods
- involving employees in finding and solving problems
- checking quality at every stage of production, from supplies to delivery
- listening to customers, and treating each department as the customer of the department before it
- continuous improvement.

| Advantages of TQM | Disadvantages of TQM |
|---|---|
| Fewer faults, so lower costs of waste, rework and returns | Training and set-up costs are high |
| Better reputation and more repeat business | Takes time to see results; needs commitment from managers |
| Higher staff motivation as employees are involved | Staff may resist change or see it as extra work |
| Less need for inspection staff | Hard to maintain if staff turnover is high |

## Costs and benefits of maintaining quality

| Costs | Benefits |
|---|---|
| Better materials and components | Fewer returns, refunds and repairs |
| Training and quality systems | Stronger brand and reputation |
| Inspection and testing equipment | Customer loyalty and repeat purchases |
| Slower production if checks are thorough | Ability to charge a higher price |
| Possibly higher prices to customers | Higher staff morale |

A business should compare the cost of preventing faults with the cost of poor quality.

**Worked example 4: is TQM worthwhile?** For the factory in Worked example 3, TQM costs $40,000 a year in training and systems and reduces the defect rate from 3% to 1%.

```
Saving on defects = $97,200 - $32,400 = $64,800
Net benefit       = $64,800 - $40,000 = $24,800 a year
```

The factory also gains a better reputation, which is not in the figures. The saving only holds if the defect rate really falls to 1%, so the decision carries some risk.

## Quality issues as a business grows

As a business expands, keeping quality consistent gets harder, especially when it uses **outsourcing** or **franchising**.

- **Outsourcing:** another firm does some of the work. The business has less direct control, so it must set clear standards in the contract, inspect samples and choose reliable partners. If the supplier cuts corners, the customer blames the brand, not the supplier.
- **Franchising:** franchisees run outlets using the brand. If one outlet is dirty or slow, customers may think the whole chain is poor. The franchisor needs training, standards, inspections and the power to end a franchise agreement.
- **General growth:** more staff and more locations make it harder to train everyone, communicate standards and spot problems early. Rapid growth can strain systems and lead to mistakes.

## Worked exam-style answer

**Question (6 marks):** Explain the consequences for a toy maker of poor quality.

**Answer:** If 3% of 15,000 toys are faulty, that is 450 toys that customers may return, costing refunds and replacements. Parents may leave bad reviews and buy from rivals, so sales fall. Shops may stop stocking the toys, which reduces sales even more. If a toy is unsafe, the business may face fines and compensation claims. All of these hurt profit and take a long time to repair. The toy maker would therefore gain from spending on testing and training, if the cost is lower than the cost of faults.

## Common mistakes

- Equating quality with high price. Quality means meeting customer expectations for that product.
- Confusing quality control (checking at the end) with TQM (everyone responsible for preventing faults).
- Describing poor quality without explaining its cost, such as returns, lost reputation or legal action.
- Forgetting that quality for a service depends on people, not just products.
- Ignoring the costs of quality, which include training and better materials.
- Treating outsourcing and franchising as quality-free ways to grow.

## Exam technique for this section

For **define** questions, use customer expectations. For **explain** questions, give a benefit or consequence and a reason: "poor quality leads to returns, which cost the business refunds and a damaged reputation". For **calculate** questions, show the percentage or cost step by step. For **evaluate** questions, such as whether a business should invest in TQM, compare the cost of the scheme with savings and long-term benefits and decide using the facts.
