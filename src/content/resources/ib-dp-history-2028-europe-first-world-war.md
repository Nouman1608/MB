**This page is for HL only.** Regional study 6, Europe and the First World War (1871 to 1923), is one of twelve studies in the Europe option examined on HL Paper 3, where you write two essays from different regional studies. The guide has six inquiry topics: maintenance of peace and the changing balance of power after 1871; German foreign policy before the war; reasons for the war; reasons for the defeat of the Central Powers; the impact of the war on **one European country** (to 1923); and the Paris Peace Treaties (1919 to 1923). This guide uses Britain as the country case.

## Peace, stability and the balance of power after 1871

The unification of Germany in 1871 created a powerful state at the centre of Europe. Its chancellor, **Bismarck**, then tried to keep France isolated and avoid a war on two fronts. His system of alliances is one of the key topics.

*Impact of imperial expansion on diplomacy.* European powers competed for colonies in Africa and Asia. Bismarck first used colonial questions to keep the powers busy and to draw France away from revenge for Alsace-Lorraine; the Berlin Conference of 1884 to 1885 set rules for the division of Africa. Later rivalries, such as Fashoda (1898) between Britain and France, created tension, but also showed that colonial disputes could be settled by negotiation.

*Congress of Berlin (1878) and the Balkans.* After Russia defeated the Ottomans in 1877 to 1878, Bismarck acted as "honest broker" at Berlin. Large Bulgaria, created by the Treaty of San Stefano, was cut down; Austria-Hungary gained the right to occupy Bosnia-Herzegovina; Serbia, Montenegro and Romania were recognised as independent; Britain received Cyprus. Russia felt cheated and its relations with Germany and Austria soured.

*Decline of the Ottoman Empire.* Nationalist movements in the Balkans, military defeats and financial weakness made the Ottoman Empire the "sick man of Europe". Its decline created a power vacuum which Austria-Hungary and Russia both wanted to fill, and gave the Balkans their explosive reputation.

*Alliance system (1871 to 1890).* Bismarck created the Three Emperors' League (1873, renewed later), the **Dual Alliance** with Austria-Hungary (1879), the **Triple Alliance** with Austria-Hungary and Italy (1882), and the Reinsurance Treaty with Russia (1887). These were defensive and aimed to keep France isolated. Historians judge them either as masterful management of peace or as a complicated structure that only Bismarck could handle.

## German foreign policy before the First World War

*Domestic conditions.* Germany was an industrial giant with a rapidly growing population, but its political system gave great power to the Kaiser, the army and the Prussian landowning elite. The Social Democratic Party (SPD) grew into the largest party in the 1912 election, alarming conservatives. Some historians (the "primacy of domestic policy" school) argue that leaders used an aggressive foreign policy and navalism to distract from internal tensions and to unify the right.

*Role of Wilhelm II.* The Kaiser dismissed Bismarck in 1890 and let the Reinsurance Treaty lapse. He was impulsive and tactless, as shown in the Kruger Telegram (1896) and the *Daily Telegraph* affair (1908). Historians debate how far he controlled policy; many point to the influence of the army and the chancellors.

*Weltpolitik.* From the late 1890s Germany sought a "place in the sun" as a world power, with colonies, trade and a **large navy**. Admiral Tirpitz's Navy Laws (1898, 1900) expanded the fleet. The naval race with Britain was a major reason the British moved away from isolation.

*Responses of other countries.* **Britain** reached the Entente Cordiale with France (1904) and an agreement with Russia (1907), forming the Triple Entente. **France** sought security through the Franco-Russian Alliance (1894) and then Britain. **Russia** saw Germany as backing Austria in the Balkans. **Austria-Hungary** relied on German support but feared decline. German actions in Morocco (1905, 1911) brought the Entente closer together.

## Reasons for the First World War

*Austria-Hungary, Russia and Balkan nationalism.* The Dual Monarchy contained many Slavs, and Serbian nationalism threatened it. Russia saw itself as protector of Slavs and wanted influence over the Straits. Austria's annexation of Bosnia in 1908 and the Balkan Wars of 1912 to 1913 raised tensions.

*Arms race.* The naval race and expanding armies increased fear. German war plans (the Schlieffen Plan) required a quick strike on France through Belgium, which meant a war with Russia would also be a war with France.

*Diplomatic crises.* Morocco (1905 to 1906, 1911), Bosnia (1908) and the Balkan Wars showed how close war was. Each crisis taught leaders that bluffing sometimes worked and that backing down was costly.

*Alliance system and the July Crisis.* On 28 June 1914 Gavrilo Princip, a Bosnian Serb, shot Archduke Franz Ferdinand at Sarajevo. Germany gave Austria a "blank cheque" of support in early July. Austria's ultimatum to Serbia on 23 July was rejected in part, and Austria declared war on 28 July. Russia mobilised, Germany declared war on Russia (1 August) and France (3 August), and the invasion of Belgium brought Britain in on 4 August.

*Debate.* Fritz Fischer argued in 1961 that Germany pursued a deliberate bid for world power and bore main responsibility. Christopher Clark's *The Sleepwalkers* (2012) stressed shared responsibility and the multi-sided decisions of leaders in several capitals. A strong essay weighs long-term causes against the July decisions, and ranks responsibility.

## Reasons for the defeat of the Central Powers

*Strategy.* The Schlieffen Plan failed at the Marne in September 1914, leaving Germany in a long two-front war. Attrition on the Western Front, such as Verdun and the Somme in 1916, wore out both sides. Germany's last offensives in spring 1918 failed to win a decision.

*Economic and technological factors.* The Allied naval blockade cut Germany's imports, causing food and material shortages, such as the "turnip winter" of 1916 to 1917. The Allies had access to global resources and credit. New technology, such as tanks and aircraft, and Allied improvements in artillery and tactics in 1918, helped.

*Entry of the USA.* Germany's resumption of unrestricted submarine warfare and the Zimmermann Telegram pushed the USA to declare war in April 1917. American loans, supplies and fresh troops, which reached France in large numbers by mid-1918, tilted the balance as Russia left the war.

*Mobilization of the home fronts.* The Central Powers' home fronts suffered most. German strikes, hunger and war weariness grew, and Austria-Hungary faced national revolts. The Allies, with better supplies, were able to keep up output. Germany's allies collapsed one by one in autumn 1918, and the Kaiser abdicated on 9 November. The Armistice came on 11 November 1918.

## Impact of the war on one country: Britain

*Social impact.* The war killed about three-quarters of a million British servicemen and left many disabled and bereaved. The state took unprecedented powers through the Defence of the Realm Act (1914), and conscription was introduced in 1916.

*Economic impact.* Britain borrowed heavily, including from the USA, and the national debt rose sharply. Old export industries lost markets, and trade unions and state controls grew.

*Experiences of women.* Many women worked in munitions, transport, farming and nursing. In 1918 women over thirty who met property conditions gained the vote. Historians debate whether the war changed women's status permanently; many left their jobs when men returned.

*Marginalized groups.* Conscientious objectors faced prison or hard labour. People of German origin were attacked or interned, particularly after the sinking of the *Lusitania* in 1915. The Easter Rising in Ireland in 1916 and its harsh repression pushed Irish opinion toward independence.

## Impact of the Paris Peace Treaties (1919 to 1923)

*Aims of the Big Three.* **Wilson** wanted a just peace based on his Fourteen Points, self-determination and a League of Nations. **Clemenceau** wanted security against Germany. **Lloyd George** wanted to protect British interests and avoid crippling Germany completely. The result was a compromise.

*Terms.* The Treaty of **Versailles** (June 1919) with Germany included the war guilt clause (Article 231), reparations (fixed in 1921), loss of territory including Alsace-Lorraine, limits of 100,000 soldiers, and a demilitarised Rhineland. The Treaty of **St Germain** (Austria) forbade union with Germany. **Neuilly** (Bulgaria), **Trianon** (Hungary, which lost about two-thirds of its territory) and **Sèvres** (Ottoman Empire) completed the settlement. Sèvres was replaced by the Treaty of **Lausanne** (1923) after the Turkish nationalist victory under Mustafa Kemal.

*Reactions.* Germans called Versailles a *Diktat* and resented the war guilt clause. Hungary resented Trianon. Italy felt its gains were a "mutilated victory". The US Senate refused to ratify the treaty.

*Economic and political impact.* Reparations and the French occupation of the Ruhr in 1923 contributed to German hyperinflation. New states in eastern Europe were created, many with minorities. Historians debate whether the settlement was too harsh, too weak, or simply impossible to enforce.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need a different country for the war's impact?**
No, but questions refer to one, so choose one and know it in depth.

**Is Germany solely to blame?**
Historians disagree, and the best essays weigh several countries' roles.
