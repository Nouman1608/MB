These questions cover sub-topic 4(a), **The organism in the environment** (specification points 4.1 to 4.5), of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Biology (4BI1) specification, Issue 3. The qualification is untiered. Points 4.1, 4.2 and 4.5 can be examined on either paper. Questions labelled **(Paper 2 only)** test the bold **B** points 4.3B and 4.4B. The set applies to the June and November series examined under Issue 3. A calculator may be used.

Learn the content first with the [organism in the environment study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-biology-4bi1-organism-in-the-environment/). The course hub is [Edexcel IGCSE Biology](/boards/edexcel/igcse/biology/).

## Questions

**1.** A pond contains frogs, fish, water lilies, pond snails and bacteria.

**(a)** State what is meant by the term habitat. **[1]**
**(b)** All the frogs in the pond are a population. Explain why all the organisms in the pond are not a population. **[2]**
**(c)** Name the term for all the living organisms in the pond together with the non-living surroundings. **[1]**

**2.** A student estimates the number of clover plants in a field of 60 m by 40 m. She places ten 1 m² quadrats at random. The counts are 12, 9, 15, 11, 8, 14, 10, 13, 12 and 6.

**(a)** Calculate the mean number of clover plants per quadrat. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the estimated number of clover plants in the field. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the quadrats were placed at random. **[1]**
**(d)** Suggest **one** way to make the estimate more reliable. **[1]**

**3.** A second student uses a 0.5 m by 0.5 m quadrat in a lawn that measures 24 m by 20 m. Eight quadrats give these counts of daisies: 3, 5, 2, 0, 4, 6, 1, 3.

**(a)** Calculate the area of the quadrat. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the mean number of daisies per m². **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the estimated population of daisies in the lawn. **[2]**

**4.** Two students want to compare the number of woodlice under logs in a wood and in an open field.

**(a)** State **three** things they should keep the same in both areas. **[3]**
**(b)** The wood has a mean of 14 woodlice per m² and the field has a mean of 3.5 per m². Calculate how many times greater the density is in the wood. **[1]**
**(c)** Suggest **one** abiotic factor and **one** biotic factor that could explain the difference. **[2]**

**5.** (Paper 2 only) Two meadows, A and B, each have a total of 100 plants in the sampled quadrats.

| Species | Meadow A | Meadow B |
|---|---|---|
| Grass | 25 | 90 |
| Clover | 20 | 6 |
| Buttercup | 20 | 4 |
| Daisy | 20 | 0 |
| Plantain | 15 | 0 |

**(a)** State what is meant by the term biodiversity. **[1]**
**(b)** State which meadow has the higher biodiversity and give **two** reasons. **[3]**

**6.** (Paper 2 only) A tape measure is laid from the edge of a wood into an open field. A quadrat is placed every 5 m along it.

**(a)** Explain why the quadrats are placed at regular intervals along the tape and not at random. **[2]**
**(b)** The student records percentage cover of moss. Moss covers 38 of the 100 squares in a grid quadrat. State the percentage cover. **[1]**
**(c)** The cover of moss falls from 38% to 2% as the quadrats move into the field. Suggest **one** abiotic factor that could explain this, with a reason. **[2]**

**7.** Explain how each factor could reduce the size of a population of rabbits on a hillside.

**(a)** A lack of grass in a dry summer **[2]**
**(b)** An increase in the number of foxes **[2]**
**(c)** An outbreak of disease **[2]**

**8.** (Paper 2 only) Describe how you would measure and compare the biodiversity of two areas of grassland using quadrats. **[5]**

**9.** On a rocky shore, one species of seaweed grows only at the top of the shore and a different species only at the bottom. Explain two factors that may cause this pattern of distribution. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** **(a)** The place where an organism lives [1]
**(b)** A population is all the organisms of one species [1]; the pond contains many different species, so together they form a community [1]
**(c)** Ecosystem [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (b), the contrast between one species and many species is the point. "It is too big" earns nothing.

**2.** **(a)** Total = 110 [1]; mean = 110 ÷ 10 = **11 plants per quadrat** [1]
**(b)** Mean per m² = 11 (the quadrat is 1 m²) and field area = 60 × 40 = 2400 m² [1]; 11 × 2400 = **26 400 plants** [1]
**(c)** To avoid bias, so that the sample is representative of the whole field [1]
**(d)** Any one of: use more quadrats, repeat the survey, sample at different times [1]
*Examiner insight:* A common slip is to multiply by the field area in cm² or to forget the quadrat area. Check the units at each step.

**3.** **(a)** 0.5 × 0.5 = **0.25 m²** [1]
**(b)** Total = 24, mean per quadrat = 24 ÷ 8 = 3 [1]; 3 ÷ 0.25 = **12 per m²** [1]
**(c)** Lawn area = 24 × 20 = 480 m² [1]; 12 × 480 = **5760 daisies** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Dividing by 0.25 converts a count in one small quadrat into a count per square metre. Multiplying by 0.25 gives a smaller number and is wrong.

**4.** **(a)** Any three of: quadrat (or log) size, number of quadrats, method of placing them, time of day / date, the way of counting [3]
**(b)** 14 ÷ 3.5 = **4 times** [1]
**(c)** Abiotic: for example moisture, temperature or light intensity [1]; biotic: for example predators or food supply [1]
*Examiner insight:* Only one factor should change between the two areas, apart from the area itself, for the comparison to be fair.

**5.** **(a)** The variety of living organisms (species) in an area [1]
**(b)** Meadow A [1]; it has more species (five against three) [1]; its numbers are spread more evenly between species (in B, grass makes up 90 of the 100 plants) [1]
*Examiner insight:* Both the number of species and the evenness are needed for full marks. "More plants" is not a valid reason because both meadows have 100.

**6.** **(a)** A transect shows how the species change along a gradient of conditions [1]; random placement would not follow the gradient in order / regular spacing allows comparison at known distances [1]
**(b)** **38%** [1]
**(c)** Light intensity (accept moisture) [1]; moss lives in the wood where there is shade and damp, so it does less well in the brighter, drier field (accept a reasonable link) [1]
*Examiner insight:* Name the factor, then connect it to the observation. "The environment changes" is not enough.

**7.** **(a)** Less food, so fewer rabbits survive / more starve [1]; rabbits also have fewer offspring, so the population falls [1]
**(b)** More foxes eat (predate) more rabbits [1]; so deaths rise and the rabbit population falls [1]
**(c)** Disease spreads between rabbits (accept especially in crowded conditions) [1]; infected rabbits die, so the population falls [1]
*Examiner insight:* Each answer needs a cause and an effect on numbers. Writing "the rabbits die" is only half of the explanation.

**8.** Choose the two areas and use the same quadrat size and the same number of quadrats in each [1]; place the quadrats at random [1]; in each quadrat identify every species and count the number of each (or estimate the percentage cover) [1]; total the number of species in each area and compare how evenly the numbers are spread [1]; the area with more species, more evenly spread, has the higher biodiversity [1]
*Examiner insight:* A good answer says "record every species". Counting only one species gives a population estimate, not biodiversity.

**9.** Any two factors, each with an explanation, for example: exposure to air and drying out at the top of the shore [1]; some species tolerate drying out and others do not [1]; competition or grazing by animals such as limpets or snails limits where each species can grow [1]; so each species is found where its conditions and competition suit it [1]
*Examiner insight:* A complete answer uses one abiotic factor (such as water availability) and one biotic factor (such as grazing).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing population with community.
- Dividing by the wrong area when scaling up a quadrat count.
- Placing quadrats where the plants look dense.
- Treating biodiversity as the total number of individuals.
- Writing factors without the effect on the population.
- Forgetting to keep the quadrat size and number the same in both areas.
