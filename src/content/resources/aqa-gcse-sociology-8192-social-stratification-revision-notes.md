## Key terms

All of these appear in the specification's key terms list for social stratification (Appendix A, 6.4).

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Social stratification | Division of society into unequal layers of wealth, income, status and power |
| Functionally important roles | Positions that need rare skills or carry high responsibility (Davis and Moore) |
| Meritocracy | A system where rewards depend on ability and effort, not background |
| Ascribed status | Status fixed at birth, such as an aristocratic title |
| Achieved status | Status gained through effort, such as a qualification |
| Means of production | Land, capital, labour power, buildings and machinery (Marx) |
| Bourgeoisie / proletariat | Owners of the means of production / workers who sell their labour |
| False class consciousness | Workers accepting a system that exploits them |
| Ruling class ideology | Ideas that make capitalism seem normal and natural |
| Market situation | A person's position in the market for skills and pay (Weber) |
| Life chances | Chances of good outcomes such as health, education, work and housing |
| Embourgeoisement | The idea that affluent workers become middle class in lifestyle and values |
| Relative deprivation | Lacking resources for the diet, activities and living conditions widely available (Townsend) |
| Underclass | A group below the working class, defined by Murray through behaviour and dependency |
| Culture of dependency | Reliance on benefits as a way of life (New Right) |
| Poverty trap | Earning more leads to losing benefits, so people gain little from work |
| Means testing | Checking income before paying a benefit |
| Authority | Power accepted as legitimate |
| Pluralism | The view that power is spread among competing groups |
| Patriarchy | Male domination of society |
| Glass ceiling | An invisible barrier that stops women reaching top jobs |
| Social mobility | Movement up or down the class structure, within a lifetime or between generations |
| Subjective poverty | Whether people see themselves as poor |
| Universal benefits | Benefits paid to everyone in a group, whatever their income |
| Ageism | Prejudice or discrimination on the grounds of age |
| Institutional racism | Racism built into the routines and rules of an organisation |

## Sociologists at a glance

| Who | What | Finding or argument | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Davis and Moore (1945) | Functionalist theory | Stratification is a "universal necessity": unequal rewards attract the most able into functionally important roles | Low-paid nurses are vital, so pay may follow power, not importance |
| Marx | Class conflict | Bourgeoisie exploit proletariat; ruling class ideology; class struggle; polarisation leading to revolution | The middle class grew instead of shrinking; no revolution in Britain |
| Weber | Class, status, party; authority | Class is market situation; middle class expands; status and party are separate; three types of authority | Gives a more detailed picture, but Marxists say it hides the basic owner-worker divide |
| Devine (1992) | Affluent workers revisited, Vauxhall, Luton | No evidence for privatised instrumentalism; rejected the "new working class" idea; workers resented inherited wealth | One plant in one town, so hard to generalise |
| Townsend (1979) | Relative deprivation | Deprivation index; over 22% in poverty in 1968 to 1969, against just over 6% on the state standard | The index may record choice, not poverty |
| Murray (1984) | Underclass, New Right | Welfare creates dependency; signs in Britain: illegitimacy, crime, unwillingness to work | Ignores economic causes; most poor people hold conventional values |
| Walby (1990) | Patriarchy, feminist | Six structures; shift from private to public patriarchy | Less attention to differences between women by class and ethnicity |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Marx versus Weber on class.** Marx: two main classes defined by ownership; polarisation. Weber: owners versus non-owners, but non-owners split by market situation; middle class grows.
- **Class versus status.** Class is economic. Status is prestige, which can come from occupation, ethnicity, religion or lifestyle (Weber).
- **Absolute versus relative poverty.** Survival basics versus the normal standard of a society.
- **Townsend's three measures.** State standard, relative income standard, relative deprivation (his preferred measure).
- **Culture of poverty versus material deprivation.** Values passed on versus lack of money and resources.
- **Power versus authority.** Getting your way versus power accepted as legitimate.
- **Traditional, charismatic, rational-legal.** Custom and inheritance; exceptional personal qualities; impersonal rules.
- **Formal versus informal power.** Official position or law versus personal influence, peer pressure or money.
- **Private versus public patriarchy.** Control by fathers and husbands versus segregation in low-paid public work.
- **Universal versus selective benefits.** Paid to all in a group versus means-tested.

## Perspectives grid

| Issue | Functionalist | Marxist | Feminist |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stratification | Necessary; motivates talent | Exploitation by owners | Ignores unpaid work and gender inequality |
| Class | Reflects merit; mobility possible | Based on ownership of means of production | Class schemes hid women's own position |
| Life chances | Differences follow ability and effort | Class is the root inequality | Patriarchy limits women's chances |
| Poverty | Gans: poverty has functions for the better-off | Built into capitalism; cheap labour | Women at higher risk through caring and low pay |
| Power | Used for collective goals; legitimate | Follows ownership; state serves ruling class | Male domination of politics and work |

## Connections across the course

- **Education.** Meritocracy links Davis and Moore to Parsons on schools. Class gaps in achievement are a life chances point.
- **Crime and deviance.** Murray connects the underclass to rising crime. Marxists link crime to inequality.
- **Families.** Walby's patriarchal relations of production overlap with the domestic division of labour.
- **Methods.** Townsend used questionnaires and a deprivation index; Devine used interviews. Both make good examples of how method shapes findings.

## Mini essay plan

**Question type:** "Discuss how far sociologists would agree that welfare dependency is the main reason poverty persists in the UK." (extended response)

1. **Define and frame.** Poverty: absolute and relative. The claim matches New Right views and links to the culture of poverty.
2. **For: Murray.** Underclass; generous welfare creates dependency; signs he named in Britain. Link to the culture of poverty and the cycle of deprivation.
3. **Apply.** Use a realistic example, such as long-term unemployment across two generations of one family.
4. **Against: Townsend.** Relative deprivation shows poverty as lack of resources. His measure found far more poverty than the state standard.
5. **Against: structural causes.** Material deprivation, the poverty trap, loss of industrial jobs through globalisation.
6. **Perspectives.** Marxist: poverty built into capitalism. Feminist: women's poverty linked to caring and low pay.
7. **Evaluate evidence.** Murray's evidence base is criticised; Townsend's index may confuse choice with poverty.
8. **Judgement.** Reach your own conclusion from the evidence you have weighed. New Right writers stress dependency and values; their critics stress low pay, job loss and lack of resources.

## Quick self-test

1. What four things did Davis and Moore say a society needs to happen?
2. Name two Marxist terms for the classes in capitalist society.
3. What did Weber mean by "market situation"?
4. Which idea of Lockwood's did Devine test?
5. Give two of Townsend's three ways of defining poverty.
6. Which perspective is Murray linked to?
7. What is a poverty trap?
8. Name Weber's three types of authority.
9. What is the difference between formal and informal sources of power?
10. What is public patriarchy?
11. Give two factors affecting life chances, other than class, listed in the specification.

### Answers

1. All roles filled; filled by those best able; necessary training takes place; roles performed conscientiously.
2. Bourgeoisie and proletariat (also petty bourgeoisie, lumpenproletariat).
3. A group's position in a market economy, which shapes pay and life chances.
4. Privatised instrumentalism.
5. Any two: state standard, relative income standard, relative deprivation.
6. The New Right.
7. Earning more leads to losing means-tested benefits, so people are little better off.
8. Traditional, charismatic, rational-legal.
9. Formal power comes from official positions or laws; informal power from personal influence, peer pressure or money.
10. Women are in public life but segregated into low-paid, low-status jobs (Walby).
11. Any two: gender, race and ethnicity, sexuality, age, disability, religion and belief.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a theorist but not their perspective. Always say "the functionalists Davis and Moore" or "the feminist Walby".
- Treating Weber as a Marxist. He shared the owner versus non-owner idea but rejected polarisation and inevitable revolution.
- Quoting Townsend's 22% without saying it came from his own relative deprivation measure for 1968 to 1969.
- Presenting Murray's underclass as fact. It is a contested New Right argument.
- Reporting Devine as supporting embourgeoisement. Her findings do not support it.
- Giving a type of authority without an example, or using "power" and "authority" as if they mean the same.
- In "Discuss" questions, covering one side only. Extended answers need a contrasting view and a judgement.
- Ignoring the item or scenario. Application marks need its details, not a general definition.
- Forgetting the feminist view in 3.6.2 to 3.6.6. Each sub-section names functionalist, feminist and Marxist views.
