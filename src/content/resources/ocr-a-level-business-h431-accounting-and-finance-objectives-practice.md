## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by an accounting and finance objective. **[2]**

**2.** Identify **two** accounting and finance objectives a business might set. **[2]**

**3.** A business has revenue of £2,000,000 and net profit of £130,000. Calculate its net profit margin. **[2]**

**4.** Which **one** of the following is a SMART objective?

A: Increase profit.

B: Be the best in the industry.

C: Raise the net profit margin from 6.5% to 8% by the end of next year.

D: Control costs more carefully. **[1]**

**5.** Explain **one** reason why a business needs clear finance objectives. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Ravenscar Tiles has these figures this year: revenue £2,000,000; net profit £130,000; operating profit £170,000; capital employed £1,000,000; non-current liabilities £350,000. Its objectives are a net profit margin of at least 8%, ROCE of at least 12% and gearing of no more than 40%.

**(a)** Calculate the net profit margin, ROCE and gearing. **[3]**

**(b)** State which objectives have been met. **[3]**

**(c)** Calculate the net profit needed to meet the margin objective at the current revenue. **[2]**

**(d)** Ravenscar wants a £200,000 loan to buy a new kiln. Evaluate the effect of this loan on its ability to meet its gearing objective. Assume the loan adds to capital employed. **[6]**

**7.** Explain how a manager could use a finance objective to improve decisions about spending. **[4]**

**8.** Evaluate the usefulness of finance objectives to a business such as a cinema chain and to its stakeholders. **[9]**

**9.** Larkhill Bakery has revenue of £1,500,000 and operating profit of £150,000. The owner wants to open a second shop in two years and the bank will lend only if the business shows steady profits and good cash. Recommend and justify **two** accounting and finance objectives for Larkhill Bakery. **[8]**

**10.** The sales director of a clothing company is rewarded for revenue growth. The finance director is rewarded for cutting costs. Analyse how these objectives may conflict. **[5]**

## Answers

**1.** An accounting and finance objective is a measurable target set for the finance function [1], for example a profit margin or cash balance the business aims to reach in a given period [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any two from: a net profit margin; ROCE; a minimum cash balance; a limit on gearing; a dividend per share; a cost reduction [1] [1]. **[2]**

**3.** 130,000 ÷ 2,000,000 × 100 [1] = **6.5%** [1]. **[2]**

**4.** **C** [1]. **[1]**

**5.** Without clear objectives, managers have no common target and may pull in different directions [1], and the business cannot judge whether its performance is good or bad [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** Net profit margin = 130,000 ÷ 2,000,000 × 100 = **6.5%** [1]. ROCE = 170,000 ÷ 1,000,000 × 100 = **17%** [1]. Gearing = 350,000 ÷ 1,000,000 × 100 = **35%** [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Margin objective not met (6.5% is below 8%) [1]. ROCE objective met (17% is above 12%) [1]. Gearing objective met (35% is below 40%) [1]. **[3]**

**(c)** 8% × £2,000,000 [1] = **£160,000**, so £30,000 more than the current £130,000 [1]. **[2]**

**(d)** New gearing = (350,000 + 200,000) ÷ (1,000,000 + 200,000) × 100 [1] = **45.8%** [1], which is above the 40% limit, so the objective would be broken [1].
The most Ravenscar can borrow while keeping gearing at 40% is x, where (350,000 + x) ÷ (1,000,000 + x) = 0.4, so x = £83,333 [1].
Options: borrow less and fund the rest from retained profit or a new share issue [1].
Judgement: the loan should not go ahead as proposed, because the objective is there to limit risk from interest, but the limit could be reviewed if the kiln is expected to raise profit significantly [1]. **[6]**

**7.** A manager can set a target such as a net profit margin of 8% [1]. This tells the manager how much cost the business can afford for each pound of revenue [1], so a spending proposal that would push the margin below target can be rejected or reduced [1]. Spending that raises revenue enough to keep the margin can be approved [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Usefulness to the business: objectives give clear targets, for example profit per screen or cash held, so managers know what is expected [1]; they allow performance to be compared with a plan and variances investigated [1]; they guide funding decisions [1].
Stakeholders: owners can see whether the return is acceptable [1]; lenders can judge risk from gearing and liquidity targets [1]; employees may be affected by cost-cutting pressure [1].
Limits: figures are based on forecasts, which are uncertain for a cinema chain depending on film releases [1]; targets can encourage short-term cuts in maintenance or staff [1].
Judgement: finance objectives are very useful when few and clear, but should be used with non-financial measures such as customer satisfaction [1]. **[9]**

**9.** Objective 1: raise the operating profit margin from 10% (150,000 ÷ 1,500,000) to 12% within 18 months [1]. At the same revenue, that is operating profit of £180,000, which is £30,000 more [1]. This builds the steady profit the bank wants to see [1].
Objective 2: keep a cash balance of at least £40,000 at every month end [1]. This protects the business from a shortage while it opens the second shop [1].
Risk: a profit target could tempt the owner to cut ingredient quality, so a customer satisfaction target should be added [1]. A gearing limit is an alternative, but cash is more urgent at the start of an expansion [1].
Judgement: both objectives are specific, measurable and tied to the bank's concerns [1]. **[8]**

**10.** Revenue growth may need discounts, advertising and more stock, all of which raise costs [1]. Cost cutting may mean lower advertising, fewer sales staff or cheaper stock, which reduces revenue [1]. Each director is rewarded for a different outcome, so they may act against each other [1]. The result may be neither revenue nor profit improving [1]. The business should set a joint objective, such as net profit margin or ROCE, that both directors share [1]. **[5]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing objectives without numbers or dates.
- Confusing business objectives with finance objectives.
- Not showing the calculation when testing an objective.
- Assuming a loan does not change capital employed.
- Stopping at one stakeholder or giving no judgement.
