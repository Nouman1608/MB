Section 9.1 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699), *Traditional media and the new
media*, opens the Media section of Paper 4 under heading 9, *Ownership and control of media*. The
syllabus lists four points: trends affecting the organisation of the media, including cross-media
ownership, digitalisation, media conglomerates and social media; debates about who controls the
media; differences between the traditional media and the new media; and the debate about whether
the traditional media has been undermined by the growth of the new media. Heading 9 links these to
*Power, control and resistance* and *Social change and development*.

## Trends in the organisation of the media

### Concentration and integration

Media companies grow in recognisable ways, and naming the type of growth makes an answer precise.

| Type of growth | Meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Horizontal integration** | Buying companies at the same stage in the same industry, such as one newspaper group buying another | Fewer independent voices in that medium |
| **Vertical integration** | Owning several stages of production and distribution, such as a film studio that also owns cinemas or a streaming service | Control from making content to delivering it |
| **Cross-media ownership** | Owning different kinds of media: press, television, film, online | Influence spreads across formats; content can be promoted across them |
| **Diversification** | Expanding into non-media businesses | Media become one part of wider corporate interests |

**Media conglomerates** are the result: very large corporations with interests across many media
and often beyond them. The Walt Disney Company's purchase of most of 21st Century Fox, completed in
2019, is a recent example of a merger that combined film studios, television channels and
streaming. Conglomerates seek **synergy**, where one part of the business promotes another, as when
a film, its soundtrack, a theme-park ride and merchandise are marketed together.

### Digitalisation and convergence

**Digitalisation** turns text, sound and images into the same digital code. This makes
**convergence** possible in three senses:

- **Technological:** one device (a smartphone) carries news, film, music, games and messaging.
- **Economic:** companies once in separate industries (telephone, broadcasting, computing) now
  compete in the same markets.
- **Cultural:** audiences move between platforms and also create and share content.

### Social media and platform companies

Social media are platforms on which users create, share and comment on content. A small number of
platform companies now operate on a global scale, for example Alphabet (owner of Google and
YouTube) and Meta, which bought Instagram in 2012 and WhatsApp in 2014. These firms mainly
distribute and rank content rather than produce it, but their **algorithms** decide what billions
of people see, so they have become a new kind of gatekeeper.

## Differences between traditional and new media

Martin Lister and colleagues' *New Media: A Critical Introduction* (2003) identify features that
distinguish new media, including **digitality**, **interactivity**, **hypertextuality** (linked
content that users navigate in their own order) and **virtuality** (immersive, simulated
environments such as online games). Production and consumption are also spread across many people
and places rather than concentrated in a few organisations.

| Dimension | Traditional media | New media |
|---|---|---|
| Direction of communication | One-to-many | Many-to-many; users reply and share |
| Who produces | Professional organisations | Professionals plus users (user-generated content) |
| Gatekeeping | Editors and producers | Algorithms, moderators and users' own sharing |
| Time | Scheduled | On demand and continuous |
| Reach | Mostly national | Global by default |
| Regulation | National regulators and laws | Harder to regulate across borders |
| Revenue | Sales, licence fees, advertising | Targeted advertising based on user data; subscriptions |

Use the table critically: the line is blurred. Newspapers publish online, broadcasters run
streaming services, and social media carry professional content. Andrew Chadwick's *The Hybrid
Media System* (2013) argues that older and newer media logics now operate together, so the useful
question is how they combine rather than which one has won.

## Debates about who controls the media

- **Owners (instrumental Marxist view):** proprietors use ownership to shape content. The closure
  of the *News of the World* in 2011, after revelations of phone hacking, led to public scrutiny of
  the power of Rupert Murdoch's News International and its relationships with politicians.
- **Managers and journalists:** in large corporations day-to-day decisions are taken by editors
  and managers; professional norms of journalism limit owners' interference.
- **Audiences (pluralist view):** in a competitive market, audiences "vote" with their attention.
- **The state:** governments license broadcasters, set laws and, in some countries, censor heavily;
  China's internet controls, often called the "Great Firewall", block many foreign platforms.
  Regulators such as Ofcom in the United Kingdom (established 2003) set standards for broadcasters.
- **Platforms and algorithms:** a newer answer is that control lies with the companies whose
  software ranks content, and with advertisers whose spending funds it.
- **Users:** Axel Bruns described **gatewatching** (2005): users select and share material from
  many sources, so audiences take on some of the gatekeeping role.

A strong essay shows that these are not exclusive: owners, states and platforms may all exercise
control, and the balance differs by country and medium.

## Has the traditional media been undermined?

### Arguments that it has

- Print circulation and scheduled television audiences have fallen in many countries, and
  advertising has moved to search and social platforms.
- News is increasingly encountered through social media feeds, so traditional organisations lose
  direct contact with audiences.
- Citizen journalism and smartphone footage can break stories before professional newsrooms.
- Trust in traditional news organisations has fallen in some societies.

### Arguments that it has not

- James Curran, Natalie Fenton and Des Freedman's *Misunderstanding the Internet* (2012) argued
  that early predictions of an internet revolution were exaggerated: established companies
  adapted, and the most-used news sites are often run by traditional organisations.
- Traditional media still set much of the agenda that social media discuss; many shared stories
  start in newspapers or broadcast news.
- Concentration has moved rather than disappeared: the largest new-media companies are themselves
  conglomerates.
- Traditional organisations have adopted new media techniques (paywalls, podcasts, streaming).

### Reaching a judgement

The hybrid media system idea gives a useful conclusion: traditional media have been **weakened
economically** and have lost their monopoly over gatekeeping, but they have not been replaced.
Their content, brands and agenda-setting power remain central inside a system that now includes
platforms and users.

## Annotated model paragraph

*Question focus: evaluate the view that the new media has undermined the traditional media.*

> Supporters of the view point to the movement of advertising from print and television to search
> and social platforms, which has weakened the finances of many newspapers. **[AO1: knowledge of
> trend]** However, Curran, Fenton and Freedman (2012) argue that established organisations
> adapted by moving online, and their websites remain among the most visited news sources.
> **[AO3: counter-evidence]** Chadwick's idea of a hybrid media system (2013) suggests that the
> old and the new now depend on each other, as when a broadcaster's investigation is spread through
> social media. **[AO2: application]** The traditional media has therefore been weakened as a
> business more than it has been undermined as a source of news. **[AO3: judgement]**

## Common mistakes

- Defining cross-media ownership as "owning lots of media"; specify *different types* of media.
- Treating "new media" as only social media; the term also covers streaming, online news and
  digital games.
- Describing differences in a list without evaluating whether the distinction still holds.
- Assuming that because anyone can publish, everyone is heard; attention is still concentrated.
- Using circulation or audience figures you cannot source.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** questions on trends: name the trend, explain the process and give its consequence
  for control or content.
- **Evaluate** questions on undermining: give both sides, then a judgement such as the hybrid
  system argument.
- Connect 9.1 to 9.2: ownership trends are evidence for Marxist theories, while audience choice and
  gatewatching support pluralism.
- Connect to 9.3: if traditional media are weakened, ask whether that challenges existing power
  structures or simply shifts power to platform companies.
