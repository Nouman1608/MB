This guide covers section 4.2, The macroeconomic aims of government, of the Cambridge IGCSE Economics 0455 syllabus for examination in 2026. Part 4.2.1 names five aims ("economic growth, full employment/low unemployment, stable prices/low inflation, balance of payments stability, redistribution of income") and asks for the "reasons behind the choice of aims and the criteria that governments set for each aim". Part 4.2.2 names three "possible conflicts between aims: full employment versus stable prices; economic growth versus balance of payments stability; and full employment versus balance of payments stability". All countries and figures below are invented.

The combined [role of government and macroeconomic aims guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-the-role-of-government-and-macroeconomic-aims/) defines each aim with a sample target. This page concentrates on what data questions test: judging a country's performance against its targets, explaining why targets differ between countries, and tracing each conflict step by step with numbers. Recall the content with the [macroeconomic aims revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-the-macroeconomic-aims-of-government-revision-notes/) and practise with the [macroeconomic aims practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-the-macroeconomic-aims-of-government-practice/).

## The five aims and how each is measured

| Aim | Usual measure | Form of a target |
|---|---|---|
| Economic growth | Percentage change in real GDP | A steady rate of growth each year |
| Full employment / low unemployment | Unemployment rate: unemployed as a percentage of the labour force | A rate at or below a chosen level |
| Stable prices / low inflation | Percentage change in a consumer price index | A low, positive rate, often a range |
| Balance of payments stability | Current account balance, often as a percentage of GDP | No large or lasting deficit or surplus |
| Redistribution of income | Gap between high and low incomes; numbers in poverty | A narrower gap; fewer people below a poverty line |

```
Unemployment rate = unemployed / labour force x 100
Labour force = employed + unemployed
Current account as % of GDP = current account balance / GDP x 100
```

## Reasons behind the choice of aims

Each aim is chosen because missing it causes real harm.

- **Growth** raises incomes and living standards, creates jobs and raises tax revenue for public services.
- **Low unemployment** avoids wasted resources, lost output and income, hardship for families and higher spending on benefits.
- **Low, stable inflation** protects the purchasing power of money and savings, helps firms plan, and keeps exports competitive.
- **Balance of payments stability** avoids the need to borrow from abroad or sell assets to pay for imports, and avoids pressure on the exchange rate.
- **Redistribution** reduces poverty and can improve health, education and social stability.

### Why targets differ between countries

The syllabus asks about the criteria governments set. They vary because economies differ.

| Situation | Effect on targets (illustrative) |
|---|---|
| Low-income, fast-developing economy | May aim for faster growth to raise living standards quickly |
| High-income, mature economy | May accept slower growth as normal |
| History of very high inflation | May set a strict, low inflation target to rebuild trust in money |
| Large share of workers in seasonal farming | Unemployment target may allow for seasonal job changes |
| Economy heavily dependent on borrowing from abroad | May give priority to reducing a current account deficit |

Targets also change over time: a government facing a recession may put jobs and growth first, while one facing rising prices may put inflation first. This is a **choice of priorities**, because aims can conflict.

## Judging performance against targets

**Worked example 1 (invented).** Country G sets these targets: real growth of at least 2.5%, unemployment no higher than 5%, inflation between 1% and 3%, and a current account deficit no larger than 2% of GDP.

| | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Real GDP (billion) | 400 | 412 | 426.4 |
| Unemployed (million) | 1.8 | 1.5 | 1.2 |
| Labour force (million) | 30 | 30 | 30 |
| Price index | 100 | 102 | 106.1 |
| Current account balance (billion) | -6 | -9.9 | -12.8 |

Calculations:

```
Growth Y2 = (412 - 400) / 400 x 100 = 3.0%
Growth Y3 = (426.4 - 412) / 412 x 100 = 3.5%
Unemployment Y1 = 1.8 / 30 = 6.0%;  Y2 = 5.0%;  Y3 = 4.0%
Inflation Y2 = (102 - 100) / 100 x 100 = 2.0%
Inflation Y3 = (106.1 - 102) / 102 x 100 = 4.0%
Current account % GDP: Y1 -1.5%;  Y2 -2.4%;  Y3 -3.0%
```

| Aim | Year 3 result | Target met? |
|---|---|---|
| Growth | 3.5% | Yes |
| Unemployment | 4.0% | Yes |
| Inflation | 4.0% | No: above the 1% to 3% range |
| Current account | Deficit of 3.0% of GDP | No: larger than 2% |

**Interpretation.** As growth sped up and unemployment fell, inflation rose above its range and the current account deficit widened. The data shows two of the three conflicts the syllabus names. A data answer should quote the figures, state which targets were met, and suggest a reason linking them.

## 4.2.2 The three named conflicts

### Full employment versus stable prices

1. To cut unemployment, the government raises spending or cuts taxes (or interest rates fall).
2. Households and firms spend more, so firms hire more workers.
3. As unemployment falls, workers become scarce; firms compete for them and wages rise.
4. Higher wages raise firms' costs, and strong spending lets firms raise prices.
5. Inflation rises.

The reverse also applies: policies to reduce inflation, such as higher interest rates, reduce spending and can raise unemployment.

### Economic growth versus balance of payments stability

1. Rising incomes during growth increase spending on all goods, including imports.
2. Firms also import more raw materials and machinery to expand output.
3. If imports rise faster than exports, the current account moves towards deficit.

**Worked example 2 (invented).** Country H's GDP rises from 500 to 530 billion (6%). Imports are 20% of GDP; exports stay at 98 billion.

```
Imports before = 0.20 x 500 = 100; balance on trade = 98 - 100 = -2
Imports after  = 0.20 x 530 = 106; balance on trade = 98 - 106 = -8
```

The trade deficit quadruples, from 2 to 8 billion, although exports did not fall.

### Full employment versus balance of payments stability

1. Policies that cut unemployment raise the incomes of people who find work.
2. Part of the extra income is spent on imported goods.
3. Higher inflation from a tight labour market (see the first conflict) can also make exports less competitive.
4. The current account worsens.

In reverse, cutting spending to reduce a current account deficit lowers demand for domestic goods as well as imports, so unemployment may rise.

### Conflicts are possible, not certain

Each conflict depends on conditions. If there is plenty of spare capacity, growth and falling unemployment may not raise inflation. If growth comes from exports or from supply-side improvements, the current account may improve rather than worsen. Strong answers state the condition.

## Annotated model answer

**Question (invented):** Explain why achieving full employment may conflict with stable prices. [4]

> To reduce unemployment, the government may increase its spending, so demand for goods and services rises [policy and first effect]. Firms hire more workers to meet demand; as unemployment falls, workers become scarce and firms raise wages to attract them [labour market link]. Higher wage costs, together with strong demand, lead firms to raise their prices [cost and demand link]. So the general price level rises, and the aim of stable prices is harder to meet [conflict stated].

## Common mistakes

- Calculating the unemployment rate with the population, not the labour force, as the base.
- Treating "stable prices" as "zero inflation". Most targets are a low, positive range.
- Describing a conflict without the steps that link the two aims.
- Inventing conflicts outside the three the syllabus names, instead of developing those three.
- Saying a current account deficit is always bad. A small, temporary deficit may cause no problem.
