This guide covers **section 2.3, The individual**, the last of the three sections of Paper 2 (The Law in Action) in Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Level Law, Issue 4. The [Law in Action guide](/resources/a-level-edexcel-law-the-law-in-action/) sets out the statutes. This page adds the cases, explains how the rights and torts interact, and shows how to apply them to a scenario.

## What section 2.3 covers

- **Freedom of speech and defamation (2.3.1)**: the **Defamation Act 2013**, ss 1, 2, 3, 4 and 15; libel, slander, defences and remedies.
- **Human Rights Act 1998 (2.3.2 to 2.3.4)**: **Article 10** (expression) and **Article 11** (assembly); appeals to the **European Court of Human Rights**; the effect on government of successful appeals.
- **Right to privacy (2.3.5, 2.3.6)**: the **Data Protection Act 2018** (ss 1 to 7, 43, 45 and 170); **Article 8**; the **Freedom of Information Act 2000**.
- **Occupiers' liability and trespass (2.3.7 to 2.3.9)**: the **Occupiers' Liability Acts 1957 and 1984**; trespass to land.
- **Remedies for the individual (2.3.10)**.

## Defamation

A claimant must show a statement that is **defamatory** (it tends to lower them in the estimation of right-thinking members of society: *Sim v Stretch* [1936]), that **refers to them** and that is **published** to a third party. **Libel** is in permanent form, including broadcasts and online posts; **slander** is transient, such as speech, and usually needs proof of financial loss.

**Section 1, serious harm.** The statement must have caused, or be likely to cause, **serious harm** to reputation; for a business trading for profit, that means serious financial loss. In *Lachaux v Independent Print* [2019] the Supreme Court held that this must be proved on the facts, not presumed. In *Monroe v Hopkins* [2017], tweets wrongly suggesting that the claimant had approved vandalism of a war memorial were held to have caused serious harm.

**Defences:**

| Section | Defence | Key requirement |
|---|---|---|
| s 2 | Truth | The imputation is substantially true |
| s 3 | Honest opinion | A statement of opinion, indicating its basis, which an honest person could have held on facts that existed |
| s 4 | Public interest | The statement was on a matter of public interest and D reasonably believed publishing it was in the public interest |

The 2013 Act replaced the common law defences of justification, fair comment and the *Reynolds* defence. **Section 15** defines terms used in the Act, including "publish" and "statement". **Remedies** are damages and injunctions; the court may also order the defendant to publish a summary of its judgment.

## Articles 10 and 11

Both are **qualified rights**: the state may restrict them if the restriction is **prescribed by law**, pursues a **legitimate aim** (such as national security, public safety, preventing disorder or crime, or protecting the rights and reputation of others) and is **necessary in a democratic society**, meaning proportionate.

- **Article 10** protects ideas that "offend, shock or disturb" (*Handyside v UK* [1976]). In *Sunday Times v UK* [1979] an injunction stopping articles about the thalidomide litigation was held to breach Article 10.
- **Article 11** protects **peaceful** assembly. A peaceful, non-obstructive assembly on the highway can be lawful (*DPP v Jones* [1999]), and when deciding whether protesters obstructing a road have a lawful excuse, the court must weigh proportionality (*DPP v Ziegler* [2021]). Domestic controls include the conditions police can impose on processions and assemblies under the Public Order Act 1986.

Under the Human Rights Act 1998, **public authorities** must act compatibly with Convention rights (s 6), courts must interpret legislation compatibly where possible (s 3), and higher courts may issue a declaration of incompatibility (s 4).

## The European Court of Human Rights

The Court sits in **Strasbourg** and is part of the Council of Europe, not the EU.

1. The applicant must have **exhausted domestic remedies**.
2. The application must be made within the time limit (now **four months** from the final domestic decision).
3. The Court decides **admissibility**, then the merits, sitting as a single judge, committee, chamber or Grand Chamber.
4. If it finds a violation it may award **just satisfaction** (compensation).
5. The **Committee of Ministers** supervises whether the state carries out the judgment.

**Effect on government (2.3.4).** A judgment binds the UK in international law but does not itself change UK law. In practice governments have often responded with legislation: *Malone v UK* [1984] (telephone tapping) led to the Interception of Communications Act 1985; *Sunday Times v UK* to the Contempt of Court Act 1981; *Goodwin v UK* [2002] to the Gender Recognition Act 2004. *Hirst v UK (No 2)* [2005], on prisoners' voting, shows the limits: the UK responded only with a limited administrative change many years later.

## Privacy and confidentiality

**Article 8** protects respect for private and family life, home and correspondence. English law protects it mainly through the tort of **misuse of private information**, developed from breach of confidence: in *Campbell v MGN* [2004] publishing photographs of a model leaving a Narcotics Anonymous meeting was a misuse. Courts balance Article 8 against Article 10, asking whether the claimant had a **reasonable expectation of privacy** and whether publication was justified. *Mosley v News Group* [2008] and *PJS v News Group* [2016] show that privacy can outweigh press freedom where there is no genuine public interest.

**The Data Protection Act 2018**, with the UK GDPR, regulates the processing of personal data. The early sections set out the Act's structure and key terms (such as personal data, processing, controller and data subject); ss 43 and 45 concern a data subject's rights, including the right of access to data held about them, in the part of the Act dealing with law enforcement processing; **s 170** makes it an **offence** knowingly or recklessly to obtain or disclose personal data without the controller's consent.

**The Freedom of Information Act 2000** gives a right to request recorded information held by public authorities, who must generally respond within **20 working days**, subject to exemptions (such as national security). Requests for your own personal data go through the data protection subject access route instead.

## Occupiers' liability

**OLA 1957: lawful visitors.** The occupier (whoever has control of the premises: *Wheat v E Lacon & Co* [1966]) owes the **common duty of care** (s 2(2)): to take reasonable care to see the visitor is reasonably safe for the purposes of the visit.

- **Children**: occupiers must be prepared for children to be less careful (s 2(3)(a); *Glasgow Corporation v Taylor* [1922], poisonous berries), though parents are expected to supervise young children (*Phipps v Rochester Corporation* [1955]).
- **Skilled visitors** are expected to guard against risks of their trade (s 2(3)(b); *Roles v Nathan* [1963]).
- **Warnings** may discharge the duty if enough to keep the visitor safe (s 2(4)(a)).
- **Independent contractors**: the occupier is not liable if they acted reasonably in choosing and checking the contractor (s 2(4)(b); *Haseldine v Daw* [1941]).

**OLA 1984: non-visitors.** A duty arises only if the occupier is aware of the danger or has reasonable grounds to believe it exists, knows or has reasonable grounds to believe someone may come near it, and the risk is one they may reasonably be expected to protect against (s 1(3)). The duty covers **personal injury only**, not property (s 1(8)). In *Tomlinson v Congleton BC* [2003] there was no liability to a man who dived into a shallow lake, because the danger was obvious; see also *Ratcliff v McConnell* [1999].

## Trespass to land

Trespass is a **direct and intentional** interference with land in another's possession, **actionable without proof of damage**. It includes entering (even by mistake about whose land it is), remaining after permission ends, and placing things on land. It extends to airspace at a height needed for ordinary use (*Kelsen v Imperial Tobacco* [1957], an overhanging sign) but not to aircraft at a reasonable height (*Bernstein v Skyviews* [1978]). A **continuing trespass**, such as leaving an object on the land, gives a fresh cause of action each day. Defences include permission (licence) and lawful authority.

## Remedies for the individual (2.3.10)

Damages (compensatory, and sometimes aggravated); **injunctions**, including interim injunctions in privacy cases; declarations; orders to publish a correction or summary in defamation; possession orders and reasonable self-help for trespass.

## Worked example: occupiers' liability

*Zara, 9, climbs through a gap in a fence into a disused quarry and is injured falling from a ledge. The owner knew children often came in.*

1. Zara is a **trespasser**, so the **1984 Act** applies.
2. The owner knew of the danger and that children came in: s 1(3)(a) and (b) are met.
3. Risks to children from a quarry are ones the owner could reasonably be expected to guard against: s 1(3)(c).
4. Mending the fence was cheap and practical, so a court is likely to find breach. Damages cover her personal injury.

## Common mistakes

- Forgetting the serious harm requirement in s 1.
- Treating Articles 10 and 11 as absolute rights.
- Saying an ECtHR judgment automatically changes UK law.
- Applying the 1957 Act to a trespasser, or claiming property damage under the 1984 Act.
- Saying trespass requires damage.
