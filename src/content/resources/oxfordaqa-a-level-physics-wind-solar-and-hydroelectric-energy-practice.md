## Questions

**1.** A wind turbine has blades of length 18.0 m. Air of density 1.2 kg m⁻³ moves at 6.5 m s⁻¹ perpendicular to the rotor. Calculate the maximum power available from the wind. **[3]**

**2.** The wind speed at a turbine falls from 12 m s⁻¹ to 9.0 m s⁻¹.
**(a)** Calculate the fraction of the original maximum power now available. **[2]**
**(b)** At a steady wind speed, the blade length is increased by 30%. State the factor by which the maximum power changes. **[2]**

**3.** **(a)** Explain why the electrical power from a wind turbine is always less than the maximum power available in the wind. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why turbines in a wind farm are not placed close together in line with the prevailing wind. **[3]**

**4.** Give two advantages and two disadvantages of using a wind farm to supply electricity. **[4]**

**5.** The Sun radiates 3.8 × 10²⁶ W. Mars is 2.28 × 10¹¹ m from the Sun.
**(a)** Calculate the intensity of sunlight at Mars. **[3]**
**(b)** A panel of area 6.0 m² faces the Sun squarely at Mars's distance and is 15% efficient. Calculate its output. **[2]**

**6.** A small lamp radiates equally in all directions. The intensity is 3.2 W m⁻² at 1.5 m.
**(a)** Calculate the power radiated. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the intensity at 4.0 m. **[2]**

**7.** A solar cell of area 1.4 cm² is lit by an intensity of 1.0 × 10³ W m⁻². Its readings are:

| V / V | 0.10 | 0.30 | 0.40 | 0.45 | 0.50 | 0.55 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I / mA | 48 | 47 | 45 | 41 | 30 | 0 |

**(a)** Calculate the power at each voltage. **[2]**
**(b)** State the maximum power and the voltage at which it occurs. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the load resistance that gives maximum power. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the efficiency of the cell at maximum power. **[2]**

**8.** Each cell in a solar array gives 0.55 V and 90 mA at maximum power. The array must give 4.4 V and 270 mA. State the number of cells in each series branch, the number of parallel branches and the total number of cells, and calculate the power of the array. **[4]**

**9.** Describe an experiment with a small lamp and a light-dependent resistor to test whether intensity follows an inverse square law, and how you would use your results to decide whether the law holds. **[6]**

**10.** **(a)** Water falls through a height of 85 m at 22 m³ s⁻¹ in a hydroelectric station that is 82% efficient overall. Calculate the electrical output. **[3]**
**(b)** A turbine of blade radius 1.8 m stands in a river flowing at 2.5 m s⁻¹. Calculate the maximum power available from the water. **[3]**

**11.** **(a)** Distinguish between a base-power station and a back-up power station. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how a pumped storage system works and why it is useful. **[3]**
**(c)** The upper reservoir holds 1.2 × 10⁶ m³ at a mean height of 250 m above the turbines. Generation is 85% efficient. Calculate the electrical energy it can deliver, in MWh. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** P = ½πr²ρv³ [1]; P = ½ × π × 18.0² × 1.2 × 6.5³ [1]; P = **1.68 × 10⁵ W (168 kW)** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Show the substitution before the number so your method is visible even if you slip, and give the unit, since leaving it out can weaken an otherwise correct answer.

**2. (a)** Power ∝ v³, so fraction = (9.0/12)³ [1] = **0.42** [1]
**(b)** Power ∝ r², factor = 1.3² [1] = **1.69** (an increase of 69%) [1]
*Examiner insight:* A ratio method needs the correct power of each variable; writing 0.75 or 1.3 alone is the typical way to lose both marks.

**3. (a)** The air must leave the turbine still moving, so not all its kinetic energy can be extracted [1]; further energy is lost to friction, turbulence and generator or gearbox heating [1]
**(b)** A turbine produces slower, more turbulent air downwind (a wind shadow) [1]; a turbine in that shadow meets a lower wind speed [1]; power depends on v³, so the loss in output is large, and spacing avoids it [1]
*Examiner insight:* "Loss of energy" alone is too vague for part (a); naming where the energy goes, and linking the shadow to v³ in part (b), is what separates the marks.

**4.** Advantages, any two: no carbon dioxide emitted when operating; no fuel needed; low running cost [2]. Disadvantages, any two: intermittent output; noise or visual impact; risk to birds and bats; large area needed [2]
*Examiner insight:* Two points in the same bullet, such as "noise" and "sound", count once; give four distinct ideas.

**5. (a)** I = P/4πr² [1]; I = 3.8 × 10²⁶ / (4π × (2.28 × 10¹¹)²) [1]; I = **5.82 × 10² W m⁻²** [1]
**(b)** Light power = 5.82 × 10² × 6.0 = 3.49 × 10³ W [1]; output = 0.15 × 3.49 × 10³ = **5.2 × 10² W** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Squaring the whole bracket 4π × r², not only r, is a frequent slip; carrying the unrounded value into part (b) avoids drift in the last figure.

**6. (a)** P = 4πr²I = 4π × 1.5² × 3.2 [1] = **90.5 W** [1]
**(b)** I = 3.2 × (1.5/4.0)² [1] = **0.45 W m⁻²** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Using the ratio in part (b) shows the inverse square law directly; either route should end with the same value, which is a useful check.

**7. (a)** P = IV: 4.8, 14.1, 18.0, 18.5, 15.0 and 0 mW [2]
**(b)** Maximum **18.5 mW at 0.45 V** [1]
**(c)** R = 0.45 / 0.041 = **11 Ω** [1]
**(d)** Light power = 1.0 × 10³ × 1.4 × 10⁻⁴ = 0.14 W [1]; efficiency = 0.0185 / 0.14 = **13%** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Maximum power must come from computed products, not the largest current or voltage; also convert mA and cm² before comparing powers.

**8.** Cells per branch = 4.4 / 0.55 = **8** [1]; branches = 270 / 90 = **3** [1]; total = 8 × 3 = **24 cells** [1]; power = 4.4 × 0.27 = **1.19 W** [1]
*Examiner insight:* State which quantity each arrangement adds; the reasoning is as important as the count.

**9.** Use a small lamp as the point source in a darkened room, with the LDR on a metre rule in line with it [1]; record the LDR resistance (or conductance) at several distances, repeating each [1]; subtract the reading with the lamp off [1]; measure to the filament and allow for an offset x₀ in the distance, a systematic error [1]; plot 1/√G against d (or check that G × distance² is constant) [1]; if the law holds the graph is straight with a negative d-axis intercept equal to −x₀, within the range tested, because the LDR may not respond exactly in proportion to intensity [1]
*Examiner insight:* Plans that name the variable controlled, the repeat, the background and the graph test tend to gain more credit than ones describing only equipment.

**10. (a)** P = ρQgh = 1000 × 22 × 9.81 × 85 [1] = 1.83 × 10⁷ W; output = 0.82 × 1.83 × 10⁷ [1] = **1.5 × 10⁷ W (15 MW)** [1]
**(b)** P = ½πr²ρv³ [1] = ½ × π × 1.8² × 1000 × 2.5³ [1] = **7.95 × 10⁴ W (about 80 kW)** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Part (a) uses a head of water, part (b) a moving stream; choosing the equation that matches the situation is the first mark.

**11. (a)** A base-power station runs continuously at a steady output to meet constant demand [1]; a back-up station starts quickly to meet extra demand at peak times [1]
**(b)** Water is pumped to a high reservoir when demand is low, using surplus electricity [1]; stored as gravitational potential energy [1]; released through turbines to generate at peak demand, so it supplies back-up power quickly (it is a store, not a source) [1]
**(c)** E = ρVgh = 1000 × 1.2 × 10⁶ × 9.81 × 250 = 2.94 × 10¹² J [1]; = 2.94 × 10¹² / 3.6 × 10⁹ = 817.5 MWh [1]; output = 0.85 × 817.5 = **695 MWh** [1]
*Examiner insight:* "Stores energy" with no mention of when it is pumped and when it is released is a common half-answer; include the demand condition.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using diameter, or forgetting the square on r, in the wind power equation.
- Forgetting that v is cubed.
- Giving the maximum power as the power delivered.
- Squaring r but not the factor 4π in the inverse square law.
- Reading the best power from the table without multiplying current by voltage.
- Not converting mA, cm² or J to W, m² or MWh.
- Adding currents in series or voltages in parallel.
- Describing pumped storage as a source of energy.
