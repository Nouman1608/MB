This guide goes deeper on one section of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Physics (9203) specification, for exams May/June 2018 onwards: **3.8.2 Solar system and orbital motion**. The qualification is untiered. The specification states that the equation for centripetal force is not required, so every orbit question here is answered with reasoning plus speed = distance ÷ time.

The wider [solar system, orbits, red shift and Big Bang guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-solar-system-orbits-red-shift-and-the-big-bang/) introduces the main facts and the two satellite orbits. This page adds the comparisons, reasoning chains and design arguments that longer questions demand. Afterwards, use the [solar system and orbits revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-solar-system-and-orbital-motion-oxfordaqa-revision-notes/) and the [solar system and orbits practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-solar-system-and-orbital-motion-oxfordaqa-practice/).

## What the specification asks

| Statement | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| 3.8.2 a | Describe the solar system; compare planets, moons, the Sun, comets and asteroids by relative size and motion |
| 3.8.2 b | Describe the universe as thousands of millions of galaxies, each of thousands of millions of stars; place the Sun in the Milky Way |
| 3.8.2 c | Distinguish planets, moons and artificial satellites; name geostationary and low polar orbits |
| 3.8.2 d, e | Explain that gravity provides the centripetal force; explain why it changes velocity but not speed |
| 3.8.2 f, g | Link separation, force and orbital speed; explain that a change in orbital speed changes the orbital radius; apply this to placing satellites |

## Relative size: putting numbers on "bigger"

The specification asks for differences "in terms of relative size and motion". Approximate diameters make the comparison concrete.

| Body | Approximate diameter | Compared with Earth |
|---|---|---|
| Sun (a medium-sized star) | 1 390 000 km | about 109 times wider |
| Jupiter (largest planet) | 140 000 km | about 11 times wider |
| Earth | 12 700 km | 1 |
| Earth's Moon | 3 500 km | just over a quarter as wide |
| Pluto (dwarf planet) | 2 400 km | about a fifth as wide |
| Largest asteroids | about 500 km | under a twentieth as wide |
| Comet nucleus | a few km to tens of km | tiny by comparison |

Three points come out of this table. The Sun dwarfs everything else. Some moons are larger than dwarf planets, so "a moon is smaller than a planet" is true of the planet it orbits, not of every object. Asteroids and comets are the smallest bodies listed.

## Relative motion: what orbits what

Size alone does not define an object; what it orbits does.

- **Planets** orbit the Sun on near-circular paths.
- **Dwarf planets** also orbit the Sun but are much smaller than planets.
- **Moons** orbit planets. Because the planet orbits the Sun, the moon travels round the Sun as well.
- **Asteroids** are small rocky bodies orbiting the Sun, mostly in a belt between Mars and Jupiter.
- **Comets** orbit the Sun on long, stretched paths. They spend most of the orbit far from the Sun and pass close to it only briefly, moving fastest when nearest.

A strong "describe the differences" answer pairs a size point with a motion point. For example: "A moon is smaller than the planet it orbits and moves round that planet, whereas the planet is larger and moves round the Sun."

## Scale beyond the solar system (3.8.2 b)

Build a nested picture, from small to large: a moon orbits a planet; planets orbit a star; our star, the Sun, is one of thousands of millions of stars in the Milky Way galaxy; the universe contains thousands of millions of galaxies. "Thousands of millions" means at least 10⁹. A common slip is to write that the solar system is a galaxy, or that the Milky Way is the universe.

## Why an orbit needs a force (3.8.2 d, e)

Picture a ball whirled on a string. The string pulls the ball towards your hand. If the string snaps, the ball flies off in a straight line along the direction it was moving at that instant. The inward pull is what bends the path into a circle.

In space there is no string. Gravity does the same job: it provides the centripetal force. It always acts towards the centre of the orbit.

The reasoning chain the specification expects:

1. Gravity acts towards the centre, at right angles to the direction of motion.
2. A force at right angles to the motion changes the direction but not the speed.
3. Velocity is speed in a given direction, so the velocity changes continuously.
4. A changing velocity is an acceleration, so an orbiting body accelerates towards the centre.
5. The force is unbalanced, which is why there is an acceleration at all.

Steps 3 and 4 trip many candidates: "constant speed" and "accelerating" are both true at once.

## Separation, force and speed (3.8.2 f)

The gravitational force between two masses decreases as their separation increases. A body further out feels a weaker inward force, so it can only stay on its circle if it moves more slowly. The planets show the pattern clearly.

| Planet | Mean orbital radius (million km) | Mean orbital speed (km/s) |
|---|---|---|
| Mercury | 58 | 47.9 |
| Earth | 150 | 29.8 |
| Mars | 228 | 24.1 |
| Jupiter | 779 | 13.1 |
| Neptune | 4 500 | 5.4 |

As radius increases, speed decreases. The time for one orbit therefore rises steeply: an outer planet has a longer path and travels it more slowly.

**Worked example 1: Mars from its period.** Mars orbits at 2.28 × 10¹¹ m and takes 687 days.

1. Time in seconds = 687 × 86 400 = 5.94 × 10⁷ s.
2. Distance in one orbit = 2πr = 2π × 2.28 × 10¹¹ = 1.43 × 10¹² m.
3. Speed = distance ÷ time = 1.43 × 10¹² ÷ 5.94 × 10⁷ = 2.41 × 10⁴ m/s, or 24.1 km/s.

This agrees with the table and is lower than Earth's 29.8 km/s, as expected for a larger radius.

## Speed sets the radius (3.8.2 g)

At a particular separation, the centripetal force produces one particular orbital speed. To stay in a stable orbit at a given distance, a planet or satellite must move at that speed. If the speed changes, the radius changes.

This is the principle behind placing satellites. Engineers choose the final orbit first, then give the satellite the speed that matches that radius.

**Worked example 2: geostationary speed.** A geostationary satellite must orbit once every 24 hours above the equator. That period is only possible at a radius of about 4.22 × 10⁷ m from the centre of the Earth.

1. Time = 24 × 3600 = 86 400 s.
2. Distance = 2π × 4.22 × 10⁷ = 2.65 × 10⁸ m.
3. Speed = 2.65 × 10⁸ ÷ 86 400 = 3.07 × 10³ m/s, about 3.1 km/s.

Compare a low polar satellite at about 6.8 × 10⁶ m from the centre: it travels at roughly 7.7 km/s and completes about 15 orbits a day. Lower orbit, stronger pull, faster speed, shorter period. That speed and short period are what let it scan the whole surface as Earth turns beneath it.

## Annotated model answer: satellite design

**Question:** Engineers want a communications satellite to stay above one point on the equator. Explain why it must be placed at one particular height and travel at one particular speed. [4 marks]

**Answer:** To stay above one point it must orbit once every 24 hours, the same time the Earth takes to spin (1). Gravity provides the centripetal force, and its strength depends on the distance from the Earth (1). At each radius there is only one speed that gives a stable orbit (1), so only one radius gives a 24-hour period, and the satellite must be given the speed for that radius; at any other speed its orbital radius would change (1).

*Why it scores:* it links the job (fixed position) to a period, the period to a radius, and the radius to a single speed, which is the chain in 3.8.2 g. Answers that only say "it must be high up" miss three of the four links.

## Common mistakes

- Writing that a satellite in a circular orbit is "not accelerating" because its speed is constant.
- Saying gravity is weaker so the satellite "falls behind". Use the specification idea: weaker force, lower orbital speed.
- Claiming that a body must keep pushing forward to stay in orbit. No forward force is needed.
- Describing a comet's orbit as circular.
- Treating "moon" as any small object. A moon is a natural satellite of a planet.
- Confusing radius from Earth's centre with height above the surface in calculations: radius = Earth's radius + height.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** the solar system: name the Sun, eight planets, dwarf planets, asteroids, comets and moons, with what each orbits.
- **Explain** orbital motion: include direction of the force, effect on direction, velocity change, acceleration.
- **Compare** orbits: use paired statements with "whereas", and quote speeds or periods if data are given.
- **Calculate**: convert days or hours to seconds, use 2πr for the distance, and give units.
- **Suggest** or **apply** in a design context: state the required period or coverage first, then the radius, then the speed.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does a planet further from the Sun move more slowly?**
Gravity weakens as separation increases, so the centripetal force is smaller. A smaller inward force keeps a body on a circle only if it moves more slowly.

**Do I need the centripetal force equation for OxfordAQA 9203?**
No. The specification says the equation is not required. Explain orbits in words and use speed = distance ÷ time for calculations.
