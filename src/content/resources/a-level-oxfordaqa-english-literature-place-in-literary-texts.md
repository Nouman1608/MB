This guide covers **Unit 2 Place in Literary Texts**, the second of two
units forming the International AS half of OxfordAQA International AS &
A-Level English Literature (9675), Version 4.1. Where Unit 1 organises
texts around the mainstream genre of tragedy, Unit 2 takes a less
conventional approach: it connects a prose text and a poetry text
through the shared cultural genre of *place* -- how specific settings
shape a text's story, structure, worldview and meaning.

## Where this fits in 9675

Unit 2 is an open-book written exam lasting 2 hours, worth 50 marks and
50% of the International AS grade (20% of the full International
A-level once combined with the A2 units). Candidates study **one prose
text and one poetry text** and answer two essay questions, one from
each section. Because the exam is open book, the emphasis in
preparation should be on building a genuine analytical framework for
"place" that can be applied under exam conditions with the text in
hand, rather than on memorising quotations.

## Syllabus coverage

**OXFORDAQA A-LEVEL ENGLISH LITERATURE (9675) — UNIT 2: PLACE IN LITERARY TEXTS**

The specification does not give an exhaustive list of what "place" can
mean in a text, but identifies areas worth exploring, including:

- Specific geographical locations and their significance, from
individual houses and villages to whole countries
- The locations, and sometimes the time periods, in which a story is
set
- The significance of the natural world -- its beauty, and sometimes
its despoliation or destruction
- The representation of social identity and how people are positioned
within society
- Ideas of social class as expressed through place
- Place as a setting for personal and human relationships
- Place as a political space
- The idea of home and homeland
- The language a text uses to represent place

**Section A: Prose** — candidates study **one** text, chosen by their
centre from: Joseph Conrad's *Heart of Darkness*, F. Scott Fitzgerald's
*The Great Gatsby*, Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie's *Americanah*, Andrea
Levy's *Small Island*, Aravind Adiga's *Last Man in Tower*, or Kazuo
Ishiguro's *The Remains of the Day*.

**Section B: Poetry** — candidates study **one** poet's selection,
chosen from: a William Wordsworth selection (including *Lines Composed
a Few Miles Above Tintern Abbey*, *Michael*, *Composed Upon Westminster
Bridge* and *Daffodils*); a Robert Frost selection (including *Mending
Wall*, *The Road Not Taken*, *Birches* and *Stopping by Woods on a
Snowy Evening*); a Thomas Hardy selection (including *Neutral Tones*,
*The Darkling Thrush*, *The Convergence of the Twain* and *The Oxen*);
or a Seamus Heaney selection (including *Mid-Term Break*, *Bogland*,
*The Tollund Man* and *Punishment*).

## Exam structure

The 2-hour paper has two sections, each worth 25 marks:

- **Section A** — one essay question on the candidate's prose set text.
- **Section B** — one essay question on the candidate's poetry
selection.

## How to approach it

Because "place" is deliberately not defined as a fixed checklist, the
strongest preparation is building a personal bank of textual evidence
organised by the specification's own suggested angles -- geography,
the natural world, social identity and class, relationships, politics,
home and homeland, and language -- rather than a single generic essay
plan. For the prose text, track how the setting changes (or fails to
change) across the narrative, and what that shift reveals about
characters or themes. For the poetry selection, since candidates study
a body of poems by one writer rather than a single poem, practise
comparing how place functions differently across two or three poems in
the selection, since strong answers tend to range across the set poems
rather than relying on just one. Because the exam is open book, spend
revision time deepening understanding of the text's use of place-related
language and imagery, since these are quick to locate in the exam but
easy to under-analyse without prior practice identifying them.

## Worked example: reading place across a text

Take *The Great Gatsby* as an example of how the specification's angles
combine in a single prose text. The geographical contrast between West
Egg and East Egg maps directly onto social class (old money versus new
money), while the Valley of Ashes sits between them as a third space
representing the human cost of that divide. The green light across the
water functions as a place-based symbol of an unreachable home or
future, tying the "home and homeland" angle to the novel's central
longing. A strong Unit 2 essay does not simply describe these locations
but explains how Fitzgerald's language constructs them -- the recurring
imagery of light, water and distance -- and connects that construction
to the text's wider concerns about class and aspiration. The same
method applies to the poetry selection: a Thomas Hardy essay on place
might trace how a poem's physical landscape becomes inseparable from
memory and loss, using the specification's own "natural world" and
"language and representation of place" angles as the analytical spine
of the response, rather than treating setting as background detail.

## Common mistakes

Treating "place" as if it only meant physical geography, and missing
the specification's broader angles -- social identity, political space,
home and homeland, and the language used to represent place. Writing
about the poetry selection using only one poem, when strong answers
typically draw on the breadth of the set poems. Bringing an annotated
copy of the set text into the open-book exam, which is not permitted.
Spending equal revision time on both sections without checking that
each is genuinely worth the same 25 marks -- both deserve full
preparation.

## Quick revision checklist

- Know which prose text and which poet's selection your centre has
chosen for Unit 2.
- Build evidence under each of the specification's suggested "place"
angles: geography, the natural world, social identity/class,
relationships, politics, home/homeland, and language.
- For the poetry section, practise comparing place across multiple
poems in the selection, not just one.
- Bring a clean, unannotated copy of both set texts to the open-book
exam.
- Revise Unit 1 and Unit 2 separately -- they assess different genres
(tragedy vs place) with different text types (drama vs prose/poetry).
