This guide goes deeper on one section of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Biology (9610) specification, Version 5.1: **3.1.9 Genetic diversity may arise by meiosis**, in Unit 1, The diversity of living organisms. Unit 1 is part of the International AS and counts towards the full International A-level.

The specification asks you to study meiosis "only in sufficient detail to show how" three things happen. That phrase tells you what to prioritise: the numbers of cells and chromosomes, the behaviour of homologous chromosomes, and the two sources of variation that arise during the process. Test yourself afterwards with the [meiosis practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-biology-9610-genetic-diversity-by-meiosis-practice/).

## Key terms

- **Diploid (2n):** a cell with two sets of chromosomes, one from each parent. Human body cells have 46 chromosomes.
- **Haploid (n):** a cell with one set of chromosomes. Human gametes have 23.
- **Homologous chromosomes:** a pair of chromosomes, one maternal and one paternal, that carry the same genes at the same loci, although they may carry different alleles.
- **Chromatid:** one of the two identical copies of a chromosome joined at the centromere after DNA replication.
- **Allele:** a version of a gene.
- **Gamete:** a haploid sex cell.

## Why meiosis is needed

If gametes were diploid, the chromosome number would double at each fertilisation. Meiosis halves the chromosome number, so that the number is restored at fertilisation. It also produces genetically different cells, which is the main reason why offspring differ from their parents and from each other.

## Meiosis: the process

Before meiosis, the DNA replicates, so each chromosome consists of two identical sister chromatids. Meiosis then involves **two nuclear divisions**, which produce **four** haploid cells from one diploid parent cell.

### Meiosis I: homologous chromosomes separate

1. **Prophase I.** The chromosomes condense. Homologous chromosomes pair up. Non-sister chromatids of a homologous pair may cross over and exchange sections (crossing over). The pairs are held together at points called chiasmata.
2. **Metaphase I.** The pairs of homologous chromosomes line up at the equator of the cell. Each pair lines up independently of all the other pairs, with the maternal or paternal chromosome of a pair facing either pole at random.
3. **Anaphase I.** The two chromosomes of each homologous pair are pulled to opposite poles. The chromatids stay together.
4. **Telophase I.** Two cells form, each with half the number of chromosomes of the parent. Each cell is now haploid, although each chromosome still has two chromatids.

### Meiosis II: chromatids separate

5. **Metaphase II.** The chromosomes line up at the equator of each cell.
6. **Anaphase II.** The centromeres divide and the chromatids are pulled apart to opposite poles.
7. **Telophase II.** Four haploid cells form. Each has a single set of chromosomes, and in most cases the four are genetically different from each other.

**The key difference from mitosis:** in meiosis I homologous chromosomes separate, whereas in mitosis (and meiosis II) chromatids separate. Mitosis produces two genetically identical diploid cells; meiosis produces four genetically different haploid cells.

## Two sources of variation in meiosis

### Independent segregation of homologous chromosomes

In metaphase I, each homologous pair lines up at the equator independently of the others. For each pair, either the maternal chromosome or the paternal chromosome can go to a given pole. This produces gametes with different **combinations of maternal and paternal chromosomes**.

The number of possible combinations is 2^n, where n is the haploid number of chromosomes (ignoring crossing over).

**Worked example 1.** A species has a diploid number of 8, so a haploid number of 4. Calculate the number of different gametes from independent segregation.

1. Haploid number n = 8 / 2 = 4.
2. Combinations = 2^4 = **16** different gametes.

**Worked example 2.** Humans have 23 pairs of chromosomes.

1. Combinations of chromosomes in a gamete = 2^23 = **8,388,608**.
2. For one parent, there are over 8 million genetically different gametes, before any crossing over.

### Crossing over

In prophase I, non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes break at the same positions and rejoin to each other's chromosome. This exchange of sections gives new combinations of alleles on the same chromosome. Two alleles that were previously on different chromosomes can now be on the same chromatid. This is further genetic variation among daughter cells.

For a gene with alleles A and a on one pair of chromosomes and another gene with alleles B and b on the same pair, a parent with chromosomes carrying AB and ab can produce AB and ab gametes without crossing over. With crossing over between the two genes, it can also produce Ab and aB gametes.

## Random fertilisation

At fertilisation, any male gamete can fuse with any female gamete, at random. This **random fertilisation** of haploid gametes further increases genetic variation within a species.

**Worked example 3.** Calculate the number of genetically different zygotes that two human parents could produce, from independent segregation alone.

1. Each parent produces 2^23 = 8,388,608 types of gamete.
2. Any male gamete can fuse with any female gamete: 8,388,608 x 8,388,608 = 2^46.
3. 2^46 = **70,368,744,177,664** possible combinations, over seventy trillion, before crossing over is considered.

## Describing diagrams and photographs

The specification asks you to describe and explain chromosome behaviour from drawings or photographs. A method that works:

1. **Count the chromosomes** in each cell. Decide whether the cell is diploid or haploid.
2. **Look at the arrangement.** Pairs of homologous chromosomes side by side at the equator indicate metaphase I. Single chromosomes lined up individually, each with two chromatids, indicate metaphase II (or mitosis).
3. **Look at what is being pulled apart.** Whole chromosomes moving to poles with their chromatids still joined indicates anaphase I. Separated chromatids moving to the poles indicates anaphase II.
4. **Name the stage and state the event.** For example: "anaphase I, because the homologous chromosomes have separated, and each still has two chromatids; this halves the chromosome number."

## Common mistakes

- Saying that homologous chromosomes are identical. They carry the same genes, but may have different alleles.
- Saying that chromatids separate in meiosis I. Homologous chromosomes separate.
- Writing "crossing over between sister chromatids". It occurs between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes.
- Describing independent segregation as "genes separating independently". It is whole homologous chromosomes, maternal and paternal, that segregate independently of other pairs.
- Writing that meiosis "produces two cells" or that the daughter cells are identical.
- Giving variation due to mutation as part of this section. It is a different source of variation.
- Saying that random fertilisation happens in meiosis. It occurs after meiosis, when gametes fuse.

## Exam technique

- **Give the numbers.** State "diploid to haploid" and "four cells".
- **Name the stage** when explaining where variation arises: prophase I (crossing over) and metaphase I (independent segregation).
- **Calculation questions:** the formula is 2^n, with n the haploid number. For fertilisation, square the number of gamete types for equal parents.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to name every stage of meiosis?**
The specification wants the process "only in sufficient detail to show how" the three events occur. Know the sequence, and be able to place the three events in the right stage.

**Does meiosis always produce four different cells?**
Because of crossing over and independent segregation, the four cells from one meiosis are usually genetically different. Only in special cases, such as the absence of any crossing over and homozygosity at every gene, could two of them be the same.
