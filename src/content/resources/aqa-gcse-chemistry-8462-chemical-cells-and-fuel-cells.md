This guide covers **4.5.2 Chemical cells and fuel cells (chemistry only)** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462)
specification, for teaching from September 2016. This section is **chemistry only**, so it is not assessed in
the combined science qualification. It is assessed on Paper 1. Sections 4.5.2.1 and 4.5.2.2 are for both
tiers, apart from writing the half equations for a hydrogen fuel cell, which is **Higher tier only**.

Use the [chemical cells and fuel cells revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-chemical-cells-and-fuel-cells-revision-notes/)

## 4.5.2.1 Cells and batteries

A **cell** contains chemicals that react to produce **electricity**. The chemical reaction transfers energy,
and some of that energy is delivered as an electric current instead of only as heat.

### A simple cell

A simple cell can be made by connecting **two different metals** in contact with an **electrolyte**.

1. Place two different metals (for example magnesium and copper) as electrodes in an electrolyte such as
   salt solution or dilute sulfuric acid.
2. Connect the electrodes with a wire and a voltmeter.
3. The more reactive metal tends to lose electrons and forms positive ions. Electrons flow through the wire
   from the more reactive metal to the less reactive one.

The voltage depends on **the type of electrode and the electrolyte**. The most important factor in the
specification is the **difference in reactivity** of the two metals: the further apart they are in the
reactivity series, the **larger the voltage**. Two electrodes of the same metal give no voltage.

### Worked example 1: interpreting data

*A student connects different metals with copper in the same electrolyte and measures the voltage.*

| Metal paired with copper | Voltage / V |
|---|---|
| Magnesium | 2.7 |
| Zinc | 1.1 |
| Iron | 0.8 |
| Copper | 0.0 |

1. Order the metals by reactivity: magnesium, zinc, iron, copper (most to least reactive). The voltage falls in
   that order.
2. The copper-copper pair gives 0.0 V, so a voltage needs **two different metals**.
3. The bigger the difference in reactivity from copper, the larger the voltage.
4. A magnesium-zinc cell would give a smaller voltage than a magnesium-copper cell, because zinc is closer to
   magnesium in reactivity than copper is.

Typical values vary slightly with the electrolyte and conditions, so always base your answer on the data you
are given.

### Batteries

A **battery** is **two or more cells connected in series**. The voltages add up, so a battery gives a greater
voltage than one cell. Two 1.5 V cells in series give 3.0 V.

### Non-rechargeable and rechargeable

| | Non-rechargeable | Rechargeable |
|---|---|---|
| Example | Alkaline batteries | Lithium-ion batteries in phones |
| What happens | Reactions stop when one reactant is **used up** | Reactions are **reversed** when an external electrical current is supplied |
| Use | Torches, remote controls, smoke alarms | Phones, laptops, electric vehicles |
| After use | Thrown away or recycled | Recharged many times |

When an alkaline battery goes flat, the chemicals have been used up and cannot be restored. In a rechargeable
cell the products are changed back into reactants by passing a current through, which is why such cells can be
reused. They do wear out after many cycles.

### Evaluating cells and batteries

The specification asks you to **evaluate the use of cells**. Points to compare:

- **Cost**, including cost over its lifetime
- **Size and mass** for the energy supplied
- **Voltage** and how long it lasts
- **Whether it can be recharged**
- **Safety** and **environmental impact**, including disposal and the metals used.

## 4.5.2.2 Fuel cells

A **fuel cell** is supplied by an **external source of fuel**, such as hydrogen, and by **oxygen or air**. The
fuel is **oxidised electrochemically** within the cell to produce a potential difference (voltage). Unlike a
battery, a fuel cell does not run flat while the fuel keeps being supplied, and it does not need recharging.

In a **hydrogen fuel cell**, the overall reaction is the **oxidation of hydrogen to produce water**:

**2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O**

The only waste product at the point of use is water. There is no burning in a flame; the energy is delivered
as electricity.

### Higher tier only: half equations in the hydrogen fuel cell

With an acidic electrolyte, the electrode reactions are:

- **Negative electrode (hydrogen):** 2H₂ → 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ (oxidation: hydrogen loses electrons)
- **Positive electrode (oxygen):** O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O (reduction: oxygen gains electrons)

Adding them: the 4H⁺ and 4e⁻ cancel, leaving 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. You can also meet the alkaline form, in which
hydrogen reacts with hydroxide ions (2H₂ + 4OH⁻ → 4H₂O + 4e⁻) and oxygen reacts with water (O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ →
4OH⁻). Both versions balance and both give the same overall equation. Check atoms and charge on each side.

### Evaluating hydrogen fuel cells against rechargeable batteries

| Point | Hydrogen fuel cell | Rechargeable cell or battery |
|---|---|---|
| Waste at point of use | Only water | None while in use |
| Recharging | None, refuel with hydrogen (a few minutes) | Plug in, often hours |
| Running time | Continues while fuel is supplied | Limited by stored charge |
| Fuel | Hydrogen is flammable, hard to store and often made using fossil fuels | Uses electricity from the grid |
| Cost | High cost of the cell and refuelling network | Lower for small devices |
| Ageing | Fewer chemicals to dispose of | Capacity falls after many cycles; disposal needed |

A balanced answer includes advantages and disadvantages of each and a conclusion. For example, fuel cells
suit vehicles needing quick refuelling and long range, while rechargeable batteries suit small portable devices
and the grid already supplies the electricity.

### Worked example 2: a model evaluation answer

*Evaluate the use of a hydrogen fuel cell, rather than a rechargeable battery, to power a delivery van.*

> A hydrogen fuel cell can be **refuelled in minutes**, whereas a rechargeable battery may need hours on
> charge, so the van spends more time working. The only product at the point of use is **water**, so there
> are no exhaust pollutants. However, hydrogen is **flammable and difficult to store**, there are few
> refuelling stations, and the hydrogen is often made from fossil fuels, which gives carbon dioxide
> emissions elsewhere. A battery van can be charged from the grid overnight and is simpler. Overall, the
> fuel cell is better for long routes with little time to stop, but the battery is better where charging
> points are available.

The answer has an advantage, a disadvantage for each option and a **conclusion that depends on the
situation**. Those three features are what an evaluation mark scheme looks for.

### Worked example 3: building a simple cell

*Describe how to make a simple cell and explain how to increase its voltage.*

1. Put a strip of magnesium and a strip of copper in a beaker of salt solution without letting them touch.
2. Connect the strips to a voltmeter with wires. The voltmeter reads the voltage.
3. To **increase the voltage**, choose a pair of metals further apart in reactivity, for example magnesium
   with copper instead of zinc with copper.
4. To get a **greater voltage still**, connect several cells in series to make a battery.

Changing the electrolyte also changes the voltage, but the data you meet in the exam usually concentrates on
the choice of metals.

## Exam technique

- In data questions, link the **voltage** to the **difference in reactivity** of the metals.
- State that a battery is **two or more cells in series**.
- For "explain why a battery stops working", say the reactants have been **used up** (non-rechargeable).
- In evaluations, give **at least one advantage and one disadvantage** for each option and then reach a
  judgement.
- For hydrogen fuel cells, name the **product** (water) and say the hydrogen is **oxidised**.

## Common mistakes

- Using two electrodes of the same metal in a diagram of a simple cell.
- Saying a fuel cell "burns" hydrogen in a flame.
- Saying a fuel cell produces no pollution at all, without noting how the hydrogen is made.
- Confusing a **cell** (one unit) with a **battery** (two or more cells).
- Saying a rechargeable battery "makes" electricity from nothing when plugged in. Electricity is used to
  reverse the reactions.
- Writing electrons on the wrong side in half equations (oxidation: electrons on the right).
