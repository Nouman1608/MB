This study guide teaches sub-topic 1(b), **Variety of living organisms** (specification points 1.2 to 1.4), of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Biology (4BI1) specification, Issue 3. The qualification is untiered. None of these points is bold-referenced, so all of them can be examined on **either paper**. It applies to the June and November series examined under Issue 3.

Use it with the [Topic 1 revision notes](/resources/igcse-edexcel-biology-nature-variety-revision-notes/) and the [Topic 1 practice questions](/resources/igcse-edexcel-biology-nature-variety-practice/). The full course is on the [Edexcel IGCSE Biology hub](/boards/edexcel/igcse/biology/), and you can tick off each point on the [printable checklist](/checklists/edexcel/igcse/biology/).

## What this sub-topic covers

| Spec point | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| 1.2 | Describe the common features of eukaryotic organisms: plants, animals, fungi and protoctists |
| 1.3 | Describe the common features of prokaryotic organisms such as bacteria |
| 1.4 | Understand the term pathogen and know that pathogens may include fungi, bacteria, protoctists or viruses |

The big idea is a sorting system. You are given a list of features and must decide which group an organism belongs to. The features that separate the groups are: **cell type** (eukaryotic or prokaryotic), **number of cells**, **cell wall material**, **chloroplasts**, **how the organism feeds** and **how it stores carbohydrate**.

**Eukaryotic** cells have a true nucleus that contains the genetic material, surrounded by a membrane. **Prokaryotic** cells have no nucleus. Plants, animals, fungi and protoctists are eukaryotes. Bacteria are prokaryotes. Viruses are neither, because they are not cells at all.

## 1.2 Eukaryotic organisms

### Plants

- Multicellular.
- Cells contain **chloroplasts** and carry out **photosynthesis**.
- Cells have **cellulose** cell walls.
- Store carbohydrate as **starch** or **sucrose**.
- Examples in the specification: flowering plants such as a cereal (for example, maize) and a herbaceous legume (for example, peas or beans).

### Animals

- Multicellular.
- Cells have **no chloroplasts** and cannot photosynthesise.
- Cells have **no cell walls**.
- Usually have **nervous co-ordination** and can move from one place to another.
- Often store carbohydrate as **glycogen**.
- Examples: mammals (for example, humans) and insects (for example, the housefly and mosquito).

### Fungi

- Cannot photosynthesise.
- The body is usually a **mycelium**, made of thread-like **hyphae** that contain many nuclei. Some fungi, such as yeast, are single-celled.
- Cell walls are made of **chitin**.
- Feed by releasing digestive enzymes onto food and absorbing the products. This is **extracellular secretion** of enzymes, and the feeding method is called **saprotrophic nutrition**.
- May store carbohydrate as glycogen.
- Examples: *Mucor* (typical hyphal structure) and yeast (single-celled).

### Protoctists

- Microscopic and single-celled.
- Variable features. Some, such as *Amoeba* from pond water, have features like an animal cell. Others, such as *Chlorella*, have chloroplasts and are more like plants.
- A pathogenic example is *Plasmodium*, which causes malaria.

### Comparison table

| Feature | Plants | Animals | Fungi | Protoctists |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Number of cells | Many | Many | Many (hyphae) or one (yeast) | One |
| Chloroplasts | Yes | No | No | Some have them (*Chlorella*) |
| Cell wall | Cellulose | None | Chitin | Varies |
| Carbohydrate store | Starch or sucrose | Often glycogen | May be glycogen | Not specified |
| Feeding | Photosynthesis | Takes in food | Extracellular digestion, absorption | Varies |

## 1.3 Prokaryotic organisms: bacteria

**Bacteria** are microscopic, single-celled organisms. A bacterial cell has:

- a **cell wall**
- a **cell membrane**
- **cytoplasm**
- **plasmids**, small rings of extra DNA
- a **circular chromosome** of DNA, lying free in the cytoplasm.

It has **no nucleus**. This is the feature that most often appears in exam questions.

Most bacteria feed on other living or dead organisms. Some can photosynthesise.

The specification gives two examples:

- ***Lactobacillus bulgaricus***, a rod-shaped bacterium used to make yoghurt from milk.
- ***Pneumococcus***, a spherical bacterium that causes the disease pneumonia.

Do not describe bacterial DNA as being "in a nucleus". Say that the circular chromosome lies in the cytoplasm.

## 1.4 Viruses and pathogens

A **pathogen** is an organism or particle that causes disease. The specification says pathogens may include **fungi, bacteria, protoctists or viruses**. Examples already met: *Plasmodium* (a protoctist) causes malaria and *Pneumococcus* (a bacterium) causes pneumonia.

**Viruses** have the following features:

- They are **not living organisms**.
- They are **small particles**, smaller than bacteria.
- They are **parasitic** and can reproduce only **inside living cells**.
- They infect **every type of living organism**.
- They have a **wide variety of shapes and sizes**.
- They have **no cellular structure**. They have a **protein coat** and contain **one type of nucleic acid**, either DNA or RNA.

Examples in the specification: the **tobacco mosaic virus**, which discolours the leaves of tobacco plants by preventing the formation of chloroplasts; the **influenza virus**, which causes flu; and **HIV**, which causes AIDS.

Viruses fail the test for living organisms in point 1.1. They do not respire or excrete, and they cannot reproduce on their own.

## Worked examples

### Worked example 1: classify from a feature list

*Organism X is multicellular. Its cell walls contain chitin. It has no chloroplasts, and it feeds by releasing enzymes onto bread and absorbing the digested products. Name the group and give two supporting features.*

1. Chitin cell walls point to a fungus. Plants have cellulose, and animals have no walls.
2. No chloroplasts means it cannot photosynthesise, so it is not a plant.
3. Extracellular digestion followed by absorption is saprotrophic nutrition, a fungal feature.

**Answer:** fungus. Supporting features: cell walls made of chitin, and extracellular secretion of enzymes onto food.

Organism X is probably *Mucor*, a bread mould with the typical hyphal structure.

### Worked example 2: plant or protoctist?

*A single-celled organism lives in pond water. It has chloroplasts and a cell wall. Which group does it belong to, and why is the answer not "plant"?*

1. It has chloroplasts, which suggests plant-like features.
2. But plants are **multicellular**, and this organism has **one cell**.
3. Microscopic, single-celled eukaryotes belong with the **protoctists**. *Chlorella* is the specification's example of a protoctist with chloroplasts.

**Answer:** protoctist. The number of cells is the deciding feature.

### Worked example 3: eukaryote or prokaryote?

*A microscope image shows a cell with cytoplasm, a cell wall and a loop of DNA, but no nucleus. Name the cell type and give a possible organism.*

1. The absence of a nucleus shows a **prokaryote**.
2. A loop of DNA is the **circular chromosome**.
3. The organism is a **bacterium**, such as *Lactobacillus*.

### Worked example 4: size comparison

*A bacterium is 2 µm long. A virus particle is 100 nm long. How many times longer is the bacterium?*

```
1 µm = 1000 nm
2 µm = 2 × 1000 = 2000 nm
2000 ÷ 100 = 20
```

The bacterium is 20 times longer. The sizes are illustrative, but the statement that viruses are smaller than bacteria is in the specification. Always convert both lengths into the same unit before you divide.

## Yeast and Mucor: two fungi

The specification names two fungal examples. They look different but share the same feeding method.

| Feature | *Mucor* | Yeast |
|---|---|---|
| Body | Mycelium of hyphae with many nuclei | Single cell |
| Cell wall | Chitin | Chitin |
| Feeding | Extracellular enzymes, then absorption | Extracellular enzymes, then absorption |
| Place in the course | Typical hyphal structure | Used in bread making (Topic 5) |

When you meet yeast later, remember that it is a fungus, not a bacterium and not a plant.

## Common mistakes

- Saying viruses are living, or calling a virus a "microbe with a nucleus". A virus has no cell structure at all.
- Saying bacteria have "a nucleus with a circular chromosome". Bacteria have no nucleus.
- Saying fungi have cellulose cell walls. Fungal walls are chitin, and plant walls are cellulose.
- Describing fungi as plants because they do not move. Fungi cannot photosynthesise.
- Writing that all protoctists are plant-like. Some, such as *Amoeba*, are animal-like.
- Mixing up *Plasmodium* (the protoctist that causes malaria) with a bacterium.
- Writing "bacteria cause all diseases". Fungi, protoctists and viruses can also be pathogens.

## Self-check

1. Name the carbohydrate store in an animal cell.
2. Name the material in the cell wall of a fungus.
3. State two ways in which a bacterial cell differs from a plant cell.
4. Why are viruses described as parasitic?
5. Name one pathogen that is a protoctist, one that is a bacterium and one that is a virus.

**Answers:** (1) Glycogen. (2) Chitin. (3) A bacterium has no nucleus and has plasmids and a circular chromosome, whereas a plant cell has a nucleus, chloroplasts and a cellulose wall. (4) They can reproduce only inside living cells. (5) Protoctist: *Plasmodium*. Bacterium: *Pneumococcus*. Virus: influenza virus (or HIV, or tobacco mosaic virus).

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the four groups of eukaryotic organisms in the specification?**
Plants, animals, fungi and protoctists.

**What is a plasmid?**
A small ring of extra DNA in a bacterial cell, separate from the circular chromosome.

**Why are viruses not classed as living organisms?**
They have no cellular structure and can reproduce only inside living cells.

**What is saprotrophic nutrition?**
Feeding by releasing digestive enzymes onto food material and absorbing the products. It is shown by fungi.
