## What section 15.2 covers

| Syllabus strand | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| Types | Describe desktop, web-based and mobile software, and personal, single-user and collaborative software |
| Uses | Planning, scheduling tasks, allocating resources, costings, communication, collaborative working and decisions |
| Evaluation | Give advantages and disadvantages of the types, and of using project management software to support projects |

**Project management software** is a set of tools that helps people plan, organise, monitor and report on a project. Typical features are task lists, calendars, Gantt charts, resource and cost tables, progress tracking, document sharing and messaging.

## Types of software

The syllabus groups software in two ways: by **where it runs** and by **who uses it**.

### By where it runs

**Desktop software** is installed on a computer and runs locally. Files are normally stored on that computer or the organisation's network.

- *Advantages:* works without an internet connection; fast; usually has the most complete set of features; data stays under local control.
- *Disadvantages:* must be installed and updated on every computer; may need a licence for each; sharing means sending files, which can create several different versions; tied to the machine.

**Web-based software** runs in a browser and the data is stored on the provider's servers, often in the cloud.

- *Advantages:* nothing to install; accessible from any device with a browser; updates are automatic; easy to share, as everyone sees the same live plan.
- *Disadvantages:* needs an internet connection; monthly subscription costs; data is held by a third party, so security and privacy matter; features and speed depend on the provider; difficult to move data to another service.

**Mobile software** is an app on a phone or tablet.

- *Advantages:* update tasks, check progress and receive alerts anywhere, even on site; uses phone features such as the camera for photographs of progress, and notifications for deadlines.
- *Disadvantages:* small screen makes charts and complex plans hard to see or edit; fewer features than desktop software; battery and signal limits; risk of a lost or stolen device holding project data.

### By who uses it

**Personal software** is for managing one person's own tasks and goals, such as a to-do list or a calendar. It is simple and cheap, but does not handle teams, dependencies or costs well.

**Single-user software** is a full project management package used by one person, such as a project manager who builds and updates the plan on one machine. It has advanced features such as Gantt charts, dependencies and budgets, but others cannot work on the plan at the same time; the manager must pass information to the team.

**Collaborative software** allows many users to work on the same project at once: team members update their own tasks, comment, attach files and see changes immediately. Usually web-based.

- *Advantages:* shared, up-to-date plan; less time spent collecting updates; better communication; decisions are recorded.
- *Disadvantages:* needs user accounts, training and an internet connection; costs per user; data can be changed by mistake; permissions must be set carefully; too many notifications can be distracting.

## Uses for supporting projects

### Supporting planning

Tasks can be broken down into a **work breakdown structure**, with sub-tasks and milestones. The software can store objectives, scope, risks and documents in one place, and use templates from earlier projects.

### Scheduling of tasks

The user enters the **duration** of each task and the **dependencies** (task B cannot start until task A finishes). The software draws a Gantt chart, works out start and finish dates and finds the critical path. If a task is delayed, the software **recalculates** the dates of the tasks that depend on it.

**Worked example 1: automatic rescheduling.** Task A (5 days) must finish before Task B (4 days) and Task C (3 days), which can run side by side, and both must finish before Task D (2 days). Without delay, the longest path is A, B, D = 5 + 4 + 2 = 11 days. If task A overruns by 3 days to 8 days, the software recalculates: 8 + 4 + 2 = **14 days**, and shows the new finish date for the project and every dependent task. Doing this by hand across hundreds of tasks would be slow and error-prone.

### Allocation of resources

People, equipment and rooms are assigned to tasks. The software shows each resource's workload and warns of **over-allocation**, where someone is assigned more work than their available time.

**Worked example 2: spotting over-allocation.** A developer works 40 hours a week. In the same week, she has been given three tasks of 30, 25 and 15 hours. Total = 30 + 25 + 15 = 70 hours. Utilisation = 70 ÷ 40 = **175%**. The software highlights this, so the manager can move a task to someone else, extend the schedule, or add staff.

### Costings

The software holds costs per resource (for example a daily rate), and adds up the costs of tasks to give a total budget. It compares **planned costs with actual costs** and warns when spending is over budget. This supports forecasting: what the final cost is likely to be at the present rate.

### Communication

Built-in messaging, comments on tasks, notifications of deadlines and changes, shared calendars and automatic status reports keep stakeholders informed. Everyone sees the same information, which reduces misunderstandings.

### Collaborative working and decisions

Team members share documents, update their own tasks and discuss problems in one place. Decisions are recorded with a date and a name, giving an audit trail. Managers use up-to-date data and reports (such as progress, cost and risk) to make decisions quickly, such as moving resources to a late task.

## Advantages and disadvantages of using project management software

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Saves time on calculation and updating of schedules and costs | Cost of software, licences and training |
| Keeps all project information in one place | Time to set up the project and enter data |
| Warns of clashes, delays and overspending early | The plan is only as good as the data entered; out-of-date data gives false confidence |
| Standard reports and charts are produced automatically | Staff may resist change or not use it properly |
| Supports teams in different places | Complex packages can be hard to learn |
| Past projects can be reused as templates | Over-reliance on the software, instead of judgement |
| Records give an audit trail | Security and privacy risks, especially when stored online |

**Worked example 3: cost of collaborative software.** A team of 15 uses a web-based collaborative tool at £8 per user per month. Monthly cost = 15 × 8 = £120. Yearly cost = 120 × 12 = **£1,440**. Over 5 years = **£7,200**. A desktop package at £600 per licence for 15 users costs 15 × 600 = **£9,000** at the start. The subscription is cheaper over 5 years, spreads the payment, and includes updates and sharing, but the costs continue as long as the tool is used, and the data depends on the provider. The decision should include training, support and features, not just price.

## Choosing software for a scenario

| Scenario | Suitable choice | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| A student plans revision tasks | Personal software | Simple and free |
| A freelance consultant builds detailed plans for clients | Single-user desktop | Full features, works offline |
| A team of 20 across three countries | Collaborative web-based | Shared, live plan |
| A site manager checking progress on a building site | Mobile | Updates and photographs from anywhere |

## Common mistakes

- Stating that the software "manages the project". It supports the manager, who still has to make decisions.
- Confusing the two groupings (desktop, web-based, mobile against personal, single-user, collaborative).
- Writing "it is easy to use" or "it saves time" without saying how.
- Forgetting the internet dependency of web-based and collaborative tools.
- Giving only advantages, when the syllabus asks for both advantages and disadvantages.
- Not linking a feature to a use, such as dependencies to automatic rescheduling.

## Exam technique

- **Describe a type** by where it runs or who uses it, and give a feature.
- **Explain a use** by naming the feature and the benefit to the project.
- **Advantages and disadvantages**: give a reason for each, using the scenario.
- **Recommend** software for a scenario by comparing team size, location, budget and the need for offline use.
