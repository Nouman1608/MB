This guide covers sub-topic **14.2 Sense organs** of the Cambridge IGCSE Biology 0610 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. Outcomes 1 to 4 are Core: sense organs, eye structures and functions, and the pupil reflex in terms of light and pupil size. Outcomes 5 to 9 are **Extended only**: the iris muscles, accommodation, rods and cones, and the fovea.

The [coordination and response study guide](/resources/igcse-biology-0610-coordination-and-response/) gives the eye tables in brief. This page goes further: the pupil reflex as a reflex arc, reading pupil data, a step-by-step method for accommodation questions, and applying rods and cones to unfamiliar situations. Afterwards, use the [sense organs revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-sense-organs-cambridge-igcse-biology-revision-notes/) and the [sense organs practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-sense-organs-cambridge-igcse-biology-practice/).

## 14.2.1 Sense organs

A **sense organ** is a **group of receptor cells** responding to a **specific stimulus**. The syllabus names five stimuli:

| Stimulus | Example of a sense organ containing the receptors |
|---|---|
| Light | eye (receptors in the retina) |
| Sound | ear |
| Touch | skin |
| Temperature | skin |
| Chemicals | tongue and nose |

Receptor cells convert the stimulus into electrical impulses, which travel along sensory neurones to the CNS, as in [coordination and response](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-coordination-and-response-cambridge-igcse-biology/).

## 14.2.2 and 14.2.3 The eye

Follow light through the eye in order; it makes both the diagram and the functions easier to remember.

| Order | Structure | Function (syllabus wording) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cornea | refracts light |
| 2 | Pupil | the hole in the iris through which light enters |
| 3 | Iris | controls how much light enters the pupil |
| 4 | Lens | focuses light on to the retina |
| 5 | Retina | contains light receptors, some sensitive to light of different colours |
| 6 | Optic nerve | carries impulses to the brain |
| – | Blind spot | where the optic nerve leaves the eye; there are no light receptors, so light falling here is not detected |

**Diagram tips:** the cornea is the clear, curved front surface. The iris is the coloured ring; the pupil is the gap in its centre. The lens sits just behind the pupil. The retina lines the back of the eye. The optic nerve leaves from the back, and the blind spot is exactly where it joins the retina.

## 14.2.4 and 14.2.5 The pupil reflex

**Core.** The pupil reflex changes the pupil diameter when light intensity changes:

- **Bright light:** pupil diameter **decreases**, so less light enters, protecting the retina from damage.
- **Dim light:** pupil diameter **increases**, so more light enters, so there is enough light to see.

It is a reflex action: automatic and rapid. As a reflex arc: light receptors in the retina → sensory neurone (optic nerve) → brain → motor neurone → **iris muscles** (effector).

**Extended only.** The iris contains two sets of muscles that work **antagonistically**: when one contracts, the other relaxes.

| Condition | Circular muscles | Radial muscles | Pupil |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bright light | contract | relax | constricts (smaller) |
| Dim light | relax | contract | dilates (larger) |

**Memory hook:** circular muscles form a ring; when a ring tightens, the hole in the middle gets smaller.

### Worked example 1: pupil data

A student measures her partner's pupil diameter under different light intensities. (Example data written for this guide.)

| Light intensity / arbitrary units | 1 | 2 | 4 | 8 | 16 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pupil diameter / mm | 7.0 | 6.0 | 4.5 | 3.0 | 2.5 |

- **Describe:** as light intensity increases, pupil diameter decreases, from 7.0 mm to 2.5 mm. The decrease is smaller at the highest intensities.
- **Calculate the percentage decrease from intensity 1 to 16:** (7.0 − 2.5) ÷ 7.0 × 100 = 64.28...% = **64%**.
- **Explain (Extended):** as light gets brighter, circular muscles contract and radial muscles relax, so the pupil constricts and less light reaches the retina.

## 14.2.6 Accommodation (Extended only)

Accommodation is how the eye changes the shape of the lens to focus light from near or distant objects on to the retina. Work through the chain in this order every time: **ciliary muscles → suspensory ligaments → lens shape → refraction**.

| | Near object | Distant object |
|---|---|---|
| Ciliary muscles | contract | relax |
| Suspensory ligaments | slacken (less tension) | pulled tight (more tension) |
| Lens shape | fatter, more rounded | thinner, flatter |
| Refraction of light | more | less |

Light from a near object is spreading out more as it reaches the eye, so it needs to be refracted more to focus on the retina.

### Model answer: accommodation for a near object

**Question:** Explain how the eye focuses on a book held close to the face. [4]

**Answer:** The ciliary muscles contract (1). The tension in the suspensory ligaments decreases, so they slacken (1). The lens becomes fatter and more rounded (1). Light is refracted more, so it is focused on the retina (1).

The most common error is saying the **ligaments contract**. Ligaments are not muscles; they only change in tension.

## 14.2.7 to 14.2.9 Rods, cones and the fovea (Extended only)

| | Rods | Cones |
|---|---|---|
| Distribution | across most of the retina, more towards the edges; none at the fovea | concentrated in the fovea; fewer elsewhere |
| Function | greater sensitivity to light, so **night vision** | **colour vision**: three kinds, each absorbing light of different colours |
| Works in dim light? | yes | need brighter light |

The **fovea** is the part of the retina **directly opposite the lens**, on the line of sight. Its function is to give the most **detailed** and **sharpest** image, in colour, because it is packed with cones. Neither rods nor cones are found at the **blind spot**.

### Worked example 2: applying rods and cones

*At night, a person sees a dim star more clearly if they look slightly to one side of it. Suggest why.*

- Looking directly at the star focuses its light on the fovea, which contains cones but no rods.
- Cones need brighter light, so the dim light is hardly detected.
- Looking to one side focuses the light on part of the retina with many rods, which are more sensitive, so the star becomes visible.

*Why do colours seem to fade at dusk?* As light becomes dim, cones stop responding and vision depends on rods, which do not give colour vision.

## Common mistakes

- Saying the lens is the only structure that refracts light; the cornea refracts light too.
- Saying the iris "lets light in"; the pupil is the opening, and the iris controls its size.
- Pupil reflex answers with no reason: add "to protect the retina" or "so enough light reaches the retina".
- (Extended) Circular and radial muscle actions swapped.
- (Extended) "The suspensory ligaments contract."
- (Extended) Saying the lens becomes thinner for near objects.
- (Extended) Placing rods in the fovea, or saying rods detect colour.

## Exam technique for 14.2 command words

- **Identify**: label structures with their exact syllabus names.
- **Describe** functions: use the syllabus verbs: refracts, controls, focuses, contains, carries.
- **Explain** the pupil reflex: light change → pupil change → effect on light reaching the retina; Extended answers add the two muscle sets.
- **Explain** accommodation: the four-step chain, in order.
- **Outline** rods and cones: one function each, with the three kinds of cone.
