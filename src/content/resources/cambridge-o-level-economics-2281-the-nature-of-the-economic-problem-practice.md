These questions cover section 1.1, *The nature of the economic problem*, of the Cambridge O Level Economics 2281 syllabus for examination in 2026: finite resources and unlimited wants for consumers, workers, producers and governments, and economic and free goods. All people, firms and figures are invented. Revise first with the [study guide on the nature of the economic problem](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-the-nature-of-the-economic-problem/) and the [revision notes on the nature of the economic problem](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-the-nature-of-the-economic-problem-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** What is the basic economic problem? **[1]**

A: Some people have more income than others

B: Resources are finite but wants are unlimited

C: Prices rise faster than wages

D: Firms do not produce enough goods

**2.** Which **one** of the following is a free good? **[1]**

A: Electricity from solar panels

B: Bottled spring water

C: Air in an open mountain valley

D: A free sample of chocolate

**3.** Why is a book borrowed at no charge from a public library an economic good? **[1]**

A: It is used by many people

B: Scarce resources were used to produce and lend it

C: It has no price

D: It is provided by the government

**4.** Define the term *scarcity*. **[2]**

**5.** State **two** reasons why human wants are unlimited. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why a free good has no opportunity cost. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**7.** Leila is a student. She receives 120 cu a month. This month she wants a pair of trainers (75 cu), mobile phone credit (25 cu), a book (30 cu) and a cinema trip with friends (20 cu).

**(a)** Calculate the difference between the cost of Leila's wants and her income. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain how Leila's situation illustrates the economic problem. **[3]**

**8.** A furniture firm has 12 workers, one workshop and a limited supply of timber.

**(a)** Identify **two** finite resources available to the firm. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain **one** choice the firm must make because of the economic problem. **[2]**

**9.** A government has a budget of 40 billion cu. Its ministries request 25 billion cu for health, 14 billion cu for education and 9 billion cu for transport.

**(a)** Calculate by how much the requests exceed the budget. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain **two** ways the government could respond to the economic problem it faces. **[4]**

**10.** Ravi works in a hospital. He can work up to 20 hours of overtime a month, or use that time to study for a qualification.

Explain how the economic problem affects Ravi as a worker. **[3]**

**11.** In a dry region, villagers once collected water freely from a large lake. After several years of low rainfall, the lake shrank and water now has to be pumped and rationed.

**(a)** Explain whether the lake water was a free good before the low rainfall. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why the water has now become an economic good. **[2]**

**12.** Discuss whether a medical service provided at no charge to patients should be described as a free good. **[5]**

## Answers

**1.** B [1]. **[1]**

**2.** C [1]. **[1]**

**3.** B [1]. **[1]**

*Examiner insight:* in multiple-choice questions, "no price" is a distractor. The test is whether scarce resources were used.

**4.** Scarcity is the situation in which resources are finite [1] and are not enough to satisfy all wants, so choices must be made [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Any two, one mark each: satisfying one want leads to new wants [1]; rising incomes create new wants [1]; new products create new wants; people want better quality. **[2]**

**6.** A free good is not scarce and uses no scarce resources [1], so using it means nothing else has to be given up [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** 75 + 25 + 30 + 20 = 150 cu; 150 − 120 = **30 cu** [1].

**(b)** Leila's income is a finite resource [1] while her wants are greater than her income (and will continue after these are met) [1], so she must choose which wants to satisfy, for example by giving up the cinema trip and the book or the trainers [1]. **[4]**

**8. (a)** Any two, one mark each: workers (labour) [1]; the workshop (capital) [1]; timber (land, a natural resource); finance.

**(b)** The firm must choose what to make with its limited timber and labour, such as tables or chairs [1]; using the timber for tables means there is less for chairs, so it cannot make as much of everything as it would like [1]. **[4]**

**9. (a)** 25 + 14 + 9 = 48 billion cu; 48 − 40 = **8 billion cu** [1].

**(b)** Two from, two marks each: the government could cut one or more of the requests [1], giving priority to services it values most, so some wants go unmet [1]; it could raise taxes [1], which leaves households with less income for their own wants, so the scarcity is shifted rather than removed [1]; it could borrow, which has to be repaid later and so limits future spending. **[5]**

*Examiner insight:* "explain" needs a reason with each response. Stating "raise taxes" alone earns at most one mark.

**10.** Ravi's time is finite: hours spent on overtime cannot be spent studying [1]. He wants both more income now and a qualification that may raise his pay later [1], so he must choose how to divide his time between them [1]. **[3]**

**11. (a)** Yes [1]: the lake held enough water for everyone at zero price, so taking water did not leave less for others and used no scarce resources [1].

**(b)** Water is now scarce relative to the villagers' wants [1], and pumping and rationing use scarce resources such as pumps, energy and labour, so supplying it has an opportunity cost [1]. **[4]**

**12.** The service has no price to the patient, which is why some people call it free [1]. However, doctors, nurses, buildings and medicines are scarce resources [1], paid for through taxes or other funds, so the resources could have been used elsewhere [1]. Providing the service therefore has an opportunity cost, which a free good does not [1]. Conclusion: it is an economic good provided at zero price, not a free good [1]. **[5]**

*Examiner insight:* a "discuss" answer needs both the reason someone might call it free and the economic reason it is not, followed by a conclusion.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "a lack of money" instead of finite resources.
- Giving a consumer example when the question asks about a worker or government.
- Confusing a zero price with a free good.
- Stating a response to scarcity with no explanation of why it helps or what it costs.
- Leaving out the conclusion in a "discuss" answer.
