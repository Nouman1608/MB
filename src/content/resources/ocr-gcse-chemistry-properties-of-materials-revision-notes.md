## C2.3a and C2.3b: why carbon is special

- Carbon has **four outer electrons** and forms **four covalent bonds**.
- Carbon bonds strongly to **other carbon atoms**, so it forms **chains** (straight or branched), **rings** and **families of similar compounds** where each member differs by CH₂.
- Result: a vast number of natural compounds (in living things) and synthetic compounds (plastics, medicines, fuels).

**Answer pattern for "explain why there are so many carbon compounds":** four bonds per carbon + bonds to other carbons + chains and rings of different lengths + families of similar compounds.

## C2.3c: forms of carbon

| | Diamond | Graphite | Graphene | Fullerenes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bonds per C | 4 | 3 | 3 | 3 |
| Structure | Giant 3D tetrahedral network | Giant; stacked hexagonal sheets | A lone graphite sheet | Molecules: hollow spheres (C₆₀) or tubes |
| Conducts? | No | Yes, along layers | Yes | Nanotubes yes |
| Hardness | Very hard | Soft, slippery | Very strong, flexible | C₆₀ soft; nanotubes strong |
| Key reason | No delocalised electrons; every bond strong | Weak forces between layers; one delocalised electron per C | Strong bonds across sheet; delocalised electrons | C₆₀: weak forces between molecules |

**CM2.3i reminder.** In a flat drawing of diamond, a carbon with four lines is a tetrahedron in 3D. In graphite, the space between sheets in a drawing is a region of weak forces, not empty "nothing". To identify a structure from a model, count bonds on one carbon and check whether it continues (giant) or closes into a cage (molecule).

## C2.3d: changes of state

Method in steps for "explain why X has a higher boiling point than Y":

1. Name the structure of each substance.
2. Say what must be overcome: intermolecular forces (simple molecules) or strong bonds (giant structures).
3. Compare strength: stronger forces or bonds need more energy.
4. Conclude: more energy needed, so a higher temperature.

Must-know facts:

- During melting or boiling, the temperature stays constant while energy overcomes forces.
- Boiling a simple molecular liquid does **not** break covalent bonds inside its molecules.
- Bigger simple molecules: stronger intermolecular forces, higher boiling point.
- Freezing and condensing transfer energy **out** to the surroundings.

## C2.3e: predicting states

| Temperature compared with data | State |
|---|---|
| Below mp | Solid |
| Between mp and bp | Liquid |
| Above bp | Gas |

**Small reminder.** Use these values: mp 80 °C, bp 218 °C. At 150 °C the substance is above its mp but below its bp, so it is a **liquid**. Range over which it is liquid: 218 − 80 = 138 °C.

Watch negative numbers. −50 °C is **higher** than −120 °C. A substance with bp −35 °C is a gas at −20 °C.

## C2.3f: bulk properties from bonding

| Structure | What holds it together | Melting point | Conducts? | Other |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ionic | Strong electrostatic attraction between ions in a giant lattice | High | Only molten or in solution | Brittle |
| Simple molecular | Weak intermolecular forces between molecules | Low | No | Often gas or liquid at room temperature |
| Giant covalent | Strong covalent bonds throughout | Very high | No (except graphite, graphene) | Hard (diamond) |
| Polymer | Covalent bonds in long chains; forces between chains | Usually solid at room temperature | No | Longer chains, stronger forces between chains |
| Metal | Metal cations with delocalised electrons around them | High | Yes, solid or liquid | Malleable |

**Must-know distinction:** atoms do not have bulk properties. A single atom of a metal does not conduct or bend; one carbon atom is neither hard nor soft. Properties come from many atoms bonded and arranged together.

## C2.3g: nano dimensions

| Object | Typical size |
|---|---|
| Atom | radius about 0.1 nm (1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m) |
| C₆₀ molecule | about 1 nm across |
| Nanoparticle | 1 nm to 100 nm |
| Unit conversion | 1 nm = 1 × 10⁻⁹ m; 1 µm = 1000 nm |

**Method in steps for a size comparison:**

1. Convert both sizes to the same unit (often metres in standard form).
2. Divide the larger by the smaller.
3. State "about ... times larger", with the number to a sensible precision.

**Small reminder.** A particle 16.8 nm across, atoms 0.28 nm across: 16.8 ÷ 0.28 = **60 atoms** across.

## C2.3h: surface area to volume

| Quantity for a cube of side x | Formula |
|---|---|
| Surface area | 6x² |
| Volume | x³ |
| Surface area : volume | 6 ÷ x |

- Smaller particles: **larger** surface area to volume ratio.
- Side 10 times smaller: ratio 10 times larger.
- More surface exposed means faster reactions and dissolving, so nanoparticles can make better catalysts.

**Small reminder.** Cube of side 4 nm: area = 6 × 16 = 96 nm², volume = 64 nm³, ratio = 96 ÷ 64 = **1.5 nm⁻¹**.

**Dissolving tablets (PAG C8):** independent variable is particle size (whole, broken, powdered); dependent variable is time to dissolve; control mass of tablet, volume of water and temperature. Repeat and take a mean, leaving out anomalies. Powdered tablets dissolve fastest.

## C2.3i and C2.3j: uses and risks

| Nanoparticle | Property | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Silver | Antibacterial | Wound dressings, socks |
| Titanium dioxide, zinc oxide | Block UV; no visible white layer | Sunscreens |
| Carbon nanotubes | Strong, light, conduct | Sports equipment, electronics |
| Many catalysts | High surface area to volume | Faster reactions with less material |
| Fullerene cages | Can hold other molecules | Drug delivery (in development) |

Risks to quote, each linked to a property:

- Very small, so may be inhaled or pass through skin into cells.
- Large surface area, so may be more reactive or toxic than the bulk material.
- New materials, so long-term effects are not fully known.
- May reach the environment (for example in waste water) and harm organisms.

## Quick self-test

1. How many covalent bonds does a carbon atom form?
2. Give two features of carbon compounds that explain why there are so many.
3. Why does graphite conduct electricity?
4. Why is C₆₀ softer than diamond?
5. Use these values: mp −39 °C, bp 357 °C. State the substance's state at 0 °C.
6. When water boils, what is overcome?
7. Write 8 nm in metres, in standard form.
8. Is a particle 3.0 × 10⁻⁷ m across a nanoparticle? Show why.
9. A cube has edges of 8 nm. Find its surface area to volume ratio.
10. How many times larger is the surface area to volume ratio of a 3 nm cube than that of a 75 nm cube?
11. Why does a single metal atom not conduct electricity?
12. Give one property-linked risk of nanoparticles in sunscreen.

### Answers

1. Four.
2. Any two: carbon bonds to other carbon atoms; forms chains; forms rings; forms families of similar compounds.
3. Each carbon bonds to three others, leaving one delocalised electron per carbon that can move along the layers.
4. C₆₀ is made of separate molecules held by weak intermolecular forces; diamond is a giant network of strong covalent bonds.
5. Liquid (0 °C is between −39 °C and 357 °C).
6. The intermolecular forces between water molecules, not the covalent bonds inside them.
7. 8 × 10⁻⁹ m.
8. 3.0 × 10⁻⁷ m = 300 nm, which is more than 100 nm, so **no**.
9. 384 nm² ÷ 512 nm³ = **0.75 nm⁻¹**.
10. Ratios are 6 ÷ 3 = 2 and 6 ÷ 75 = 0.08; 2 ÷ 0.08 = **25 times**.
11. Conduction is a bulk property: it needs a lattice of many metal ions with delocalised electrons moving through it. One atom has no such structure.
12. The particles are small enough to pass through skin or be inhaled, and their effects in the body are not fully known.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "bonds break" for boiling a simple molecular substance, instead of "intermolecular forces are overcome".
- Saying graphite has "free ions" or "free atoms" instead of delocalised electrons.
- Describing C₆₀ as giant covalent; it is molecular.
- Giving only the property in a "use" question without linking it to why it suits the use.
- Writing "nanoparticles are dangerous" without naming a property (size, surface area) that causes the risk.
- Reading negative temperatures the wrong way round when predicting states.
- Mixing nm and m, or forgetting that 1 nm is 10⁻⁹ m, not 10⁻⁶ m.
- Stating that a smaller particle has a bigger surface area. It is the ratio (or the total area of many small pieces) that is bigger.
- Saying ionic compounds conduct when solid.
