All businesses and figures are invented, and amounts are in dollars ($). The syllabus says the expected formats are in its Teacher Guidance document, so check the layouts here against your teacher's copy. ## Adjustments to a trial balance (1.5.1)

The matching/accruals concept says each period carries the income earned and expenses incurred in it, whenever cash moves. So each adjustment to the trial balance affects both statements.

| Adjustment | Effect on profit or loss | Effect on financial position |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Expense accrued (owed) | Add to expense | Other payable (current liability) |
| Expense prepaid | Deduct from expense | Prepayment (current asset) |
| Income accrued (earned, not received) | Include in income | Current asset |
| Income received in advance | Remove from this year's income | Current liability |
| Irrecoverable debt | Expense | Cut trade receivables |
| Irrecoverable debt recovered | Income | Cash received |
| Allowance for irrecoverable debts | Expense for the increase, or credit for the decrease | Deducted from trade receivables |
| Depreciation | Expense | Added to accumulated depreciation |
| Closing inventory | Deducted in cost of sales | Current asset |

For the allowance, write off irrecoverable debts first, apply the percentage to the receivables that remain, then compare with the existing allowance: only the change goes to the statement of profit or loss. Prudence requires closing inventory to be shown at cost or net realisable value, whichever is lower. Items that cost 4,000 but can only be sold for 3,100 net are valued at 3,100, so inventory and profit both fall by 900. Depreciation methods are in the [non-current assets guide](/resources/accounting-non-current-assets/).

**Correction of errors.** An error found after the trial balance is corrected by journal. Decide whether it changes profit, assets or liabilities: repairs debited to a van, for example, change both profit and the asset. Errors that unbalance the trial balance go through a suspense account first: see the [1.4 guide](/resources/accounting-reconciliation-and-verification/).

Opening assets 15,000 less liabilities 2,600 give opening capital 12,400; closing assets 17,900 less liabilities 2,200 give 15,700. ## Partnerships (1.5.3)

**Agreement and the Act.** A partnership agreement usually sets out: capital each partner contributes, profit-sharing ratio, partners' salaries, interest on capital, interest on drawings, interest on loans, and what happens when a partner joins or leaves. Advantages: fewer disputes, written rules, rewards matching capital and effort. Disadvantages: changing it needs everyone to agree, drafting costs time, and terms can become unfair. Without an agreement, the Partnership Act 1890 applies, and it divides profits and losses equally; there are no partners' salaries; no interest on capital; no interest on drawings; and a partner's loan to the firm earns 5% a year.

**Capital and current accounts.** Fixed capital accounts show the permanent investment and change only by agreement. Current accounts record the yearly flow: profit share, salary and interest on capital are credited; drawings and interest on drawings are debited. This keeps investment apart from day-to-day withdrawals. A debit balance on a current account shows a partner has drawn more than earned.

Hana Soames and Tomas Kasprzak run a physiotherapy practice. Their agreement: profits shared 3 : 2, 5% interest on capital (Soames 60,000, Kasprzak 40,000), a salary of 14,000 for Kasprzak and 4% interest on drawings. Kasprzak also lent the firm 20,000 at 5%. Drawings were Soames 18,000 and Kasprzak 15,000, taken evenly through the year, so interest runs for half a year: 360 and 300. Fees were 214,300, expenses 154,960 and loan interest 1,000, which is an expense, not an appropriation.

Profit for the year: 214,300 − 154,960 − 1,000 = **58,340**.

## Limited companies (1.5.4)

**Ordinary shares** carry votes and a dividend that varies with profit; holders rank last and bear most risk. The syllabus says questions will not be set on preference shares. A **dividend** is paid out of profit, so it is shown in the statement of changes in equity and never as an expense when finding profit. **Debentures** are long-term loans at fixed interest, paid whether or not there is profit, so the interest is charged as a finance cost against profit and the debenture is a liability. **Reserves** are the shareholders' stake beyond share capital. **Capital reserves** (share premium and revaluation reserve) arise from capital events, not trading, and are not normally used for cash dividends. **Revenue reserves** (retained earnings and general reserve) come from profits kept back.

**Ledger accounts.** Issue at par: debit bank, credit ordinary share capital. At a premium: debit bank with the full cash, credit share capital with the nominal value and share premium with the excess. A **rights issue** lets current shareholders buy new shares pro rata to what they already hold, usually at a discount. Hartsmere Cycles Ltd has 200,000 ordinary shares of 1 each and issues them 1 for 4 at 1.50. That is 50,000 shares:

| Account | Debit | Credit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bank (50,000 × 1.50) | 75,000 | |
| Ordinary share capital (50,000 × 1.00) | | 50,000 |
| Share premium (50,000 × 0.50) | | 25,000 |

A **bonus issue** gives free shares by capitalising reserves, with no cash received. A 1 for 10 bonus on 250,000 shares is 25,000 shares: debit retained earnings 25,000, credit ordinary share capital 25,000. The syllabus says the revaluation reserve is not used for a bonus issue. Total equity is unchanged.

## Evaluating share issues and finance

| Option | For the company | For the shareholders |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bonus issue | No cash paid out; lower share price aids trading | More shares, same total value and percentage; no cash raised |
| Rights issue | Raises cash cheaply, no new owners, no interest | Discounted shares and kept percentage; must find cash or be diluted |
| Issuing shares | No fixed charge or repayment | Dilutes control and dividends |
| Issuing debentures | No loss of control; interest is an expense | Fixed interest and priority, but no vote |

Disadvantages to remember: a bonus issue raises no cash and turns reserves into capital; a rights issue needs shareholders to pay; debentures need interest and repayment even in a poor year.

**Sources of finance for purposes.** Match the finance to the need and its time span. Buying a building: a share or rights issue, debentures or retained earnings. A seasonal inventory build-up: an overdraft or trade credit. Check cost, repayment, control and risk.

## Common errors

- Charging the whole allowance instead of the change.
- Treating loan or debenture interest as an appropriation.
- Recording cash in a bonus issue.
