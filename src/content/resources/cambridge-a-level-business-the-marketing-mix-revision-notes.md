## The big picture

The mix is Product, Price, Promotion, Place (distribution channels). The skill is to recommend a mix that suits one business in one context. In any answer, name the method, tie it to a detail in the case, then weigh it up.

## 3.3.2 Product: definitions

| Term | Meaning | Quick example |
|---|---|---|
| Good | Physical item you can touch and store | A lamp |
| Service | Activity; not stored | A boiler repair |
| Tangible attribute | Can be seen or touched | Materials, size, packaging |
| Intangible attribute | Cannot be touched | Reputation, status, friendliness |
| Product development | Creating new or improved products | A lighter, quieter model |
| Differentiation | Making a product stand out from rivals | Design, quality, service, image |
| USP | The feature rivals lack that customers value | A two-year no-quibble guarantee |

**Why development matters:** tastes and technology change, rivals copy, it defends share and spreads risk. **Cost:** money, time and the chance of failure.

**Differentiation supports** a higher price and loyalty, but only if customers notice and value the difference.

## 3.3.3 Product life cycle and extension strategies

Stages: (development) then introduction, growth, maturity, decline.

| Stage | What happens | Typical marketing |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | Low sales, high costs | Awareness; skimming or penetration |
| Growth | Sales rise quickly; rivals arrive | Widen distribution; build brand |
| Maturity | Sales flatten | Defend share; differentiate; competitive pricing |
| Decline | Sales fall | Cut costs, drop it or extend it |

**Extension strategies:** new packaging, product modification, new uses, new markets, extra promotion, price changes, new channels. They succeed only where a real opportunity exists.

**Method: read a life cycle table.**
1. Work out the percentage change in sales each period.
2. Look for growth slowing (maturity) or turning negative (decline).
3. Link the stage to a sensible response, then check what the case says about costs and competitors.

## 3.3.3 Boston Matrix

| | High market share | Low market share |
|---|---|---|
| High growth | Star | Question mark (problem child) |
| Low growth | Cash cow | Dog |

Standard form of relative market share: own share divided by the share of the largest rival. Above 1 means market leader.

| Position | Role | Typical decision |
|---|---|---|
| Star | Earns, but needs funding to hold share | Invest to keep the lead |
| Cash cow | Surplus cash | Milk it; fund others |
| Question mark | Cash drain; uncertain | Invest to turn into a star, or stop |
| Dog | Low earnings | Drop, harvest, or keep if it helps the range |

**Uses:** shows balance of the portfolio, guides spending, flags products to drop, shows future cash needs. **Limits:** two measures only, market definition matters, a snapshot, ignores why a market is growing, and the high/low boundary is a choice.

**Must-know distinction.** The Boston Matrix looks at market share and growth across the portfolio. The life cycle follows one product's sales over time. A dog may be in decline but need not be. A cash cow is often in maturity but not always.

**Worked reminder.** A product has 8% of a market where the largest rival has 16%: relative share is 8 / 16 = 0.5. In a market growing 14% a year, it is a question mark. In a market growing 2%, it is a dog.

## 3.3.4 Pricing methods

| Method | Remember it as | Best when | Watch out |
|---|---|---|---|
| Competitive | Price against rivals | Many similar products | Price wars |
| Penetration | Low start, win share | New entrant; elastic demand | Low early profit; raising price later |
| Skimming | High start, then fall | Novel product; few rivals | Rivals enter; slower share growth |
| Price discrimination | Different prices to different groups | Segments can be kept apart | Complaints; leakage |
| Dynamic | Price moves with demand or stock | Online; perishable capacity | Seen as unfair |
| Cost-based | Cost plus a margin | Quick; secure return | Ignores demand |
| Psychological | Looks cheaper, e.g. 19.99 | Customers notice price points | Small effect |

**Method: cost-plus.** Price = unit cost x (1 + mark-up). Mark-up is measured against cost, whereas margin is measured against the selling price.

Brindle Wharf Audio pays £62 for a speaker. A 30% mark-up gives £80.60; a 50% mark-up gives £93.00. A 30% mark-up on cost is a margin of 23.1% of the price (18.60 / 80.60).

**Price discrimination reminder.** A 120-seat hall charges £14 for 80 peak seats and £9 for 40 off-peak seats: revenue £1,480. A single £12 price on all 120 seats gives £1,440. Discrimination wins by £40, only if the seats are filled at each price and the groups cannot swap.

**Psychological reminder.** £19.99 is only 1p below £20, yet buyers may read it as "under £20".

**Choosing a method: three questions.** What is the objective (profit, share, survival, image)? How do customers react to price? What are rivals likely to do?

## 3.3.5 Promotion methods

| Method | Objective | Use | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Advertising | Inform, persuade, remind; build image | Mass reach | Costly; hard to measure |
| Sales promotion | Short-term sales; trial | Discounts, samples, BOGOF, loyalty points | Cuts margins; trains customers to wait |
| Direct promotion | Reach named customers | Mail, email, telesales, personal selling | Can annoy; higher cost per contact |
| Digital | Targeted, measurable contact | Social media, search, email, influencers, apps | Bad reviews spread; ad avoidance |

**Packaging:** protects, attracts, shows brand, informs, signals quality. **Branding:** recognition, differentiation, loyalty, price support. A brand is costly to build and can be damaged quickly.

**Method: cost per new customer.** Cost of the campaign divided by customers won. Cobalt Fen Skincare spends £3,200 on digital promotion and wins 160 customers (£20 each), against £2,700 on leaflets for 90 customers (£30 each). Digital is cheaper per customer here. Say what the figure leaves out: quality of customers and repeat sales.

**Buy 2 get 1 free.** The customer pays for two items and gets three, so each costs two-thirds of the normal price (one-third off).

## 3.3.6 Place

Channels: direct; via retailer; via wholesaler then retailer.

| Channel | Gain | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Direct | Keeps whole price; brand control; data | Producer bears selling and delivery |
| Retailer | Reach; display; less work | Retailer margin; less control |
| Wholesaler | Bulk-breaking; many outlets | Extra margin |

**Digital v physical:** physical suits products customers want to see, try or take away now; digital widens reach and works for digital goods, but means delivery, returns and site costs. Many businesses use both.

**Retailer margin reminder.** A retailer buys at £40 and sells at £65: margin £25, or 38.5% of the selling price.

## Quick self-test

1. State two tangible attributes and two intangible attributes of a coffee shop's drink.
2. Define a unique selling point.
3. A product's sales (units) are 500, 700, 840, 850 across four years. Calculate the growth in each year and name the likely stage.
4. A product has 9% of a market; the largest rival has 18%. Calculate relative market share.
5. Name the Boston Matrix category for low share in a fast-growing market.
6. Calculate the cost-plus price for a unit cost of £25 and a 40% mark-up.
7. Name the pricing method of charging students less than other adults for the same ticket.
8. Give one advantage of skimming for a novel product.
9. Calculate the cost per new customer: £2,000 spent, 80 customers won.
10. Give one disadvantage of selling through a retailer rather than direct.

### Answers

1. Tangible: size of cup, taste or temperature, packaging. Intangible: friendly service, brand reputation, atmosphere (any two of each).
2. A feature of a product that rivals do not offer and that customers value.
3. 40%, 20%, 1.2%. Likely maturity (growth slowing sharply).
4. 9 / 18 = 0.5.
5. Question mark (problem child).
6. £25 x 1.40 = £35.00.
7. Price discrimination.
8. Early buyers pay a high price, so the business recovers development costs quickly.
9. £2,000 / 80 = £25.
10. The retailer takes a margin, so the producer receives less per unit and has less control over how it is sold.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining a tool but never tying it to the firm in the stem.
- Mixing up mark-up (on cost) and margin (on price).
- Confusing the Boston Matrix with the life cycle.
- Calling every low-priced launch penetration pricing without reasoning about share.
- Describing a promotion method with no judgement of cost against likely sales.
- Forgetting that psychological pricing is about perception, not cost.
- Ignoring the target segment when choosing a channel.
- Giving evaluation as a list of generic pros and cons rather than a conclusion based on the case.
