## Section A

**1.** Which case set the test for fraudulent misrepresentation? A: Derry v Peek. B: Redgrave v Hurd. C: Bisset v Wilkinson. D: Long v Lloyd. **[1]**

**2.** Under the Misrepresentation Act 1967, on whom does the burden of proof lie in a claim under s2(1)? A: the claimant, who must prove negligence. B: the defendant, who must prove reasonable grounds for belief. C: the court. D: neither party. **[1]**

**3.** State two situations in which silence may amount to a misrepresentation. **[2]**

**4.** Identify two bars to rescission. **[2]**

**5.** State the three elements of economic duress identified in DSND Subsea v Petroleum Geo-Services (2000). **[3]**

## Section B

**6.** Explain the difference between a statement of fact and a statement of opinion in the law of misrepresentation, referring to two cases. **[4]**

**7.** Explain how damages are assessed for fraudulent and for negligent misrepresentation. **[4]**

**8.** Explain the factors a court considers in deciding whether a party had no practical choice but to agree to a variation of a contract. **[4]**

**9.** *Nadia buys a vintage watch from Oskar, a dealer, for £6,000. Oskar says it is "an original 1960s model". He had read this in an auction catalogue, but the serial number, which he never checked, shows it is a later copy worth £800. Nadia discovers the truth seven months later, having worn the watch daily since she noticed that the mechanism looked unusual two months after buying it.* Suggest what remedies Nadia may have. **[6]**

**10.** *Brightbuild Ltd agrees to build an extension for Coastline Hotels for £200,000, to be finished before the summer season. Halfway through, Brightbuild says it will stop work unless Coastline pays an extra £40,000, although costs have not risen. Coastline protests in writing, cannot find another builder at short notice and pays. The extension is finished in May. In December Coastline asks for the £40,000 back.* Advise Coastline. **[8]**

**11.** *Ella buys a holiday cottage from Ray. During negotiations Ray says the cottage "has never flooded". This was true when he said it, but a week before the sale the cottage flooded and Ray says nothing. He also tells Ella that "the area is lovely and peaceful". Ella did not commission a survey.* Consider whether Ray has made any actionable misrepresentation. **[6]**

**12.** Discuss whether the law on misrepresentation provides fair remedies for both parties. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** A: Derry v Peek. **[1]**

**2.** B: the defendant, who must prove reasonable grounds for belief. **[1]**

**3.** Any two of: a **half-truth** that gives a misleading impression (Dimmock v Hallett, 1866) [1]; a statement that was true when made but **becomes untrue** before the contract and is not corrected (With v O'Flanagan, 1936); a contract of the **utmost good faith**, such as insurance [1].

**4.** Any two of: **affirmation** [1]; **lapse of time**; **third-party rights**; **restitution impossible** [1].

**5.** Pressure whose practical effect is **compulsion or lack of practical choice** [1]; pressure that is **illegitimate** [1]; pressure that is a **significant cause** of entering the contract [1].

**6.** A misrepresentation must be a statement of **fact**; an honest statement of **opinion** is not enough [1]. In **Bisset v Wilkinson (1927)** a seller's view that land could carry 2,000 sheep was opinion, as neither party knew it had been used for sheep [1]. But if the maker has **superior knowledge**, an opinion may carry an implied statement of fact [1]: in **Smith v Land and House Property (1884)** calling a tenant "most desirable" when he was in arrears was a misrepresentation [1].

**7.** Fraudulent misrepresentation is actionable in the **tort of deceit** [1], and the claimant recovers **all losses flowing directly** from the fraud, whether or not foreseeable (Doyle v Olby, 1969) [1]. Negligent misrepresentation under **s2(1) Misrepresentation Act 1967** gives damages [1] assessed **as if the misrepresentation were fraudulent**, following **Royscot Trust v Rogerson (1991)** [1].

**8.** From **Pao On v Lau Yiu Long (1980)**: whether the victim **protested** at the time [1]; whether an **alternative course** of action, such as another supplier or a legal remedy, was open [1]; whether the victim had **independent advice** [1]; and whether, after entering the contract, the victim **took steps to avoid it** [1].

**9.** "An original 1960s model" is a statement of **fact** that induced Nadia to pay £6,000 [1]. Oskar is a dealer who relied on a catalogue without checking the serial number, so under **s2(1)** he is unlikely to prove **reasonable grounds** for belief, as in Howard Marine v Ogden [1]. Damages under s2(1) are assessed on the **fraud measure** (Royscot), so she can recover her direct loss, at least the £5,200 difference between price and value [1]. Rescission may be **barred by affirmation**: she kept wearing the watch after noticing the unusual mechanism (Long v Lloyd) [1]. It may also be barred by **lapse of time**, though seven months is far shorter than in Leaf v International Galleries [1]. Conclusion: damages under s2(1) are her safest remedy; rescission is doubtful [1].

**10.** The issue is **economic duress**, which makes the variation **voidable** [1]. Brightbuild threatened to **break the contract** without any increase in costs, which is **illegitimate pressure** [1], as in **Atlas Express v Kafco (1989)** [1]. Coastline had **no practical choice**: no other builder at short notice and the season approaching [1]. Coastline **protested**, which is evidence of coercion under **Pao On** [1]. The pressure was a **significant cause** of payment (DSND Subsea) [1]. However, Coastline waited from May to December before seeking repayment, which may be **affirmation**, as in **The Atlantic Baron (1979)** [1]. Conclusion: Coastline has a strong claim to rescind the variation and recover £40,000, but must explain the delay [1].

**11.** "Has never flooded" was true when made, but the cottage **flooded before the sale** and Ray failed to correct it, so it became a misrepresentation (**With v O'Flanagan, 1936**) [1]. It is a statement of **fact** and is **material** to buying a cottage [1]. Ella did not survey, so she relied on Ray's statement; even if she could have discovered the truth she is still induced (**Redgrave v Hurd, 1881**) [1]. Ray knew of the flood, so this is likely **fraudulent** (Derry v Peek) [1]. "Lovely and peaceful" is vague **opinion or sales talk**, not fact (Bisset v Wilkinson) [1]. Conclusion: the flooding statement is actionable, giving rescission and damages in deceit; the description of the area is not [1].

**12.** Award one mark per developed point, to a maximum of 8:
- **Rescission** is available for all types, restoring both parties, which is fair to the innocent party [1].
- But the **bars**, especially lapse of time in Leaf, can leave a claimant without rescission before they could reasonably discover the truth [1].
- **s2(1)** shifts the burden to the maker, which is fair because the maker controls the information (Howard Marine) [1].
- **Royscot** gives fraud-measure damages for negligence, which may be **unfair to defendants** who were careless, not dishonest [1].
- **s2(2)** lets courts award damages instead of rescission for minor misrepresentations, protecting defendants from disproportionate outcomes [1].
- Innocent misrepresentors have **no liability in damages as of right**, which balances fault and outcome [1].
- The fact or opinion line (Bisset; Smith) can be **uncertain**, but protects honest sellers [1].
- A **reasoned conclusion**, for example that remedies are broadly fair, though Royscot over-compensates and the bars can be harsh [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Not identifying whether a statement is fact, opinion or intention before classifying it.
- Forgetting that a true statement can become false before the contract.
- Placing the burden of proof on the claimant under s2(1).
- Ignoring delay and continued use as bars to rescission.
- Treating any demand for more money as duress without applying the DSND elements.
