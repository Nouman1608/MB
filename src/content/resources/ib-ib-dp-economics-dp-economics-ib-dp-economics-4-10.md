This guide goes deeper on sub-topic **4.10 Economic growth and/or economic development strategies**, the last sub-topic of Unit 4 in the IB Diploma Programme *Economics guide* (first assessment 2022). It is SL and HL content. The [guide to barriers and strategies](/resources/ib-dp-economics-barriers-and-strategies-for-growth-and-development/) defines every strategy; this page shows how to **choose** between strategies by matching them to the barrier a country faces, works through the numbers behind FDI, aid and microfinance, and models the market-versus-intervention judgement. Consolidate with the [4.10 revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-economics-dp-economics-ib-dp-economics-4-10-revision-notes/) and practise with the [4.10 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-economics-dp-economics-ib-dp-economics-4-10-practice/). The barriers themselves are in [4.9](/resources/ib-ib-dp-economics-dp-economics-ib-dp-economics-4-9/).

All countries, firms and figures are invented. The main example is Sundrel, whose currency is the sund.

## The strategies in 4.10

| Group | Strategies |
|---|---|
| Trade strategies | Import substitution; export promotion; economic integration |
| Diversification | Spreading production and exports across more products and markets |
| Social enterprise | Businesses pursuing social or environmental aims, reinvesting most profit |
| Market-based policies | Trade liberalisation; privatisation; deregulation |
| Interventionist policies | Redistribution (progressive taxes, transfers, minimum wages); merit goods (education and health programmes); infrastructure |
| Inward foreign direct investment | Investment by multinational firms in production facilities |
| Foreign aid | Humanitarian aid; development aid; Official Development Assistance; non-governmental organisations; debt relief |
| Multilateral development assistance | World Bank; International Monetary Fund |
| Institutional change | Access to banking (microfinance, mobile banking); women's empowerment; reducing corruption; property and land rights |

You also need to evaluate the **strengths and limitations** of strategies, weigh **market-oriented** against **interventionist** approaches, and judge progress towards selected **Sustainable Development Goals** using data.

## Step one: match the strategy to the barrier

No strategy is good or bad in the abstract. The first question is always: **which barrier does it remove?**

| Barrier (4.9) | Strategies that target it |
|---|---|
| Dependence on primary production | Diversification; export promotion of manufactures; import substitution (with care) |
| Low human capital | Merit goods: education and health programmes; development aid for schools and clinics |
| Lack of infrastructure | Public infrastructure investment; World Bank project lending; FDI in power or telecoms |
| Indebtedness | Debt relief; IMF support; export growth to raise earnings |
| Weak banking and property rights | Microfinance; mobile banking; land titling |
| Lack of access to international markets | Economic integration; trade agreements; infrastructure to the border |
| Corruption and weak governance | Institutional change: transparency, independent courts |
| Gender inequality | Women's empowerment: education for girls, equal property and credit rights |

Matching first prevents the most common weakness in 4.10 answers: praising a strategy that does not address the country's actual problem.

## Inward FDI: measuring the net gain

A foreign electronics firm opens an assembly plant in Sundrel. To attract it, Sundrel offers a tax holiday worth 5 million sunds a year in forgone revenue.

| Item | Million sunds a year |
|---|---|
| Wages: 1,500 workers × 6,000 sunds | 9 |
| Inputs bought from Sundrel suppliers | 12 |
| Profit | 40 |
| Profit sent to the parent company abroad (80%) | 32 |
| Profit kept in Sundrel (20%) | 8 |

```
Money retained in Sundrel = wages + local inputs + retained profit = 9 + 12 + 8 = 29 million
Less tax forgone = 5 million
Net gain to Sundrel = about 24 million sunds a year
```

The calculation shows why **linkages** matter: local purchases of 12 million are worth more to Sundrel than the plant's retained profit. Further benefits that the numbers do not capture include skills and technology transfer. Limitations: most profit leaves, the firm may relocate when the tax holiday ends, and the plant may use capital-intensive methods that create few jobs. FDI is most valuable where it builds links with local suppliers and trains local workers.

## Aid: what kind, for what barrier?

- **Humanitarian aid** (food, shelter, medicine after a disaster) saves lives in the short term but does not address long-run barriers.
- **Development aid** (funding for schools, clinics, irrigation) targets human capital and infrastructure.
- **Official Development Assistance (ODA)** is aid from governments; **NGOs** run projects directly and often reach remote communities.
- **Debt relief** frees revenue for public spending, but only helps if the government uses it well.

**Evaluating aid.** Strengths: it fills savings and foreign-exchange gaps and can fund merit goods a poor government cannot afford. Limitations: it may be tied to the donor's goods, create dependence, be lost to corruption, or reflect the donor's priorities more than the recipient's. Effectiveness depends heavily on governance: the same aid achieves more where institutions are strong.

## Multilateral assistance

The **World Bank** lends for development projects and gives technical advice. The **IMF** lends to countries facing balance of payments difficulties. Both can unlock finance a country could not raise alone. Loans often carry **conditions**, such as cuts in budget deficits or market reforms; supporters argue conditions encourage sound policy, while critics argue they can cut spending on health and education and limit a government's choices.

## Institutional change: microfinance in numbers

A market trader in Sundrel borrows 500 sunds at 18% a year to buy a refrigerated display, which lets her sell fresh fish and raises her income by 60 sunds a month.

```
Interest = 500 x 0.18 = 90 sunds
Extra income over the year = 60 x 12 = 720 sunds
Left after interest and repaying the 500 = 720 - 90 - 500 = 130 sunds
```

The loan pays for itself within a year and leaves her with a productive asset. Limitations: microloans are small, interest rates can be high, repayments may start before income rises, and they suit trading activities more than larger-scale industry. Mobile banking lowers the cost of saving and receiving payments, especially in rural areas.

## Market-oriented versus interventionist approaches

| | Market-oriented | Interventionist |
|---|---|---|
| Main tools | Trade liberalisation, privatisation, deregulation, attracting FDI | Merit goods, infrastructure, redistribution, import substitution |
| Strengths | Efficiency, competition, access to foreign capital and markets, lower fiscal cost | Provides goods markets under-supply; reduces inequality; can build human capital and infrastructure |
| Limitations | Under-provision of merit goods; rising inequality; private monopolies; exposure to world shocks | Government failure, corruption, high fiscal cost, protected industries may stay inefficient |

**Model judgement.** "A country with weak human capital and poor infrastructure needs government investment that markets will not provide, but a country with an efficient public sector and a broad tax base is better placed to make that investment succeed. The most effective approach is usually a combination: market-oriented policies for tradable goods and competition, alongside government provision of education, health and infrastructure, with the balance depending on the strength of the country's institutions."

## Judging progress towards the Sustainable Development Goals

The United Nations adopted 17 Sustainable Development Goals in 2015, with a target date of 2030. Progress should be judged with data, comparing countries and allowing for starting points.

| Indicator (invented data) | Sundrel, 10 years ago | Sundrel, now | Varkos, 10 years ago | Varkos, now |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Primary school completion (Goal 4, quality education) | 78% | 92% | 81% | 85% |
| Population with safely managed drinking water (Goal 6) | 40% | 64% | 62% | 70% |

- Sundrel improved by 1.4 percentage points a year on schooling and 2.4 on water; Varkos by 0.4 and 0.8.
- Sundrel started lower and has now overtaken Varkos on schooling, but remains behind on water.
- A judgement should note that rapid early gains are often easier than the last steps to universal coverage, and that indicators say nothing about the quality of schooling.

## Common mistakes

- Describing strategies without linking them to a barrier.
- Treating FDI as pure gain without counting profit outflows or tax incentives.
- Writing that aid "always" or "never" works instead of naming the conditions.
- Presenting market-oriented and interventionist approaches as opposites with no middle ground.
- Comparing SDG indicators without allowing for different starting points.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** a strategy in three links: what it does, which barrier it removes, how that raises growth or development.
- **Evaluate** or **discuss**: one strength, one limitation, and a judgement that depends on country conditions (institutions, size of the barrier, time horizon).
- **Calculate** in Paper 2: net FDI gains, microfinance returns or changes in an indicator; then say what the figure means.
- In Paper 1 part (b), use real-world examples of strategies you have studied and state where they worked or failed and why.
