This guide covers **section 1.2, The sources of English law, its enforcement and administration**, the larger of the two sections of Paper 1 in Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Level Law, Issue 4. It has twenty-four content areas, so this page does not repeat the outline in the [Paper 1 Underlying Principles guide](/resources/a-level-edexcel-law-underlying-principles/) or the law-making detail in the [underlying principles revision notes](/resources/a-law-underlying-principles-revision-notes/). Instead it fills in the parts those pages treat briefly.

## What section 1.2 covers

- **Legislation (1.2.1 to 1.2.3):** how an Act is made, including the **Parliament Acts 1911 and 1949** and the **Royal Assent Act 1967**; influences on law reform; statutory interpretation, including the **Interpretation Act 1978** and the effect of the **Human Rights Act 1998**.
- **Delegated legislation (1.2.4 to 1.2.6):** orders in council, statutory instruments and bylaws, their control (including the **Legislative and Regulatory Reform Act 2006**), advantages and disadvantages.
- **Judicial law making (1.2.7 to 1.2.9):** precedent, equity's remedies in contract, and the case for and against judges making law.
- **EU law (1.2.10 to 1.2.12):** directives, regulations, the Court of Justice and the Commission.
- **Civil dispute resolution (1.2.13 to 1.2.19):** the civil courts and Civil Procedure Rules, tribunals, and arbitration, conciliation, mediation, negotiation and ombudsmen.
- **People in the law (1.2.20 to 1.2.22):** judges, solicitors and barristers, and lay people.
- **Costs and funding (1.2.23 to 1.2.24).**

## The Parliament Acts and Royal Assent

Normally a Bill must pass both Houses. The **Parliament Act 1911** removed the House of Lords' power to veto most public Bills, leaving a power to delay, and the **Parliament Act 1949** cut that delay to roughly one year. A money Bill can become law without the Lords' consent after one month. The Acts have been used only rarely, for example for the **War Crimes Act 1991** and the **Hunting Act 2004**; in *Jackson v Attorney General* [2005] the House of Lords upheld the 1949 Act as valid. The **Royal Assent Act 1967** allows Royal Assent to be notified to each House rather than given in person by the monarch.

**Exam use:** the Parliament Acts show that the elected House has the final say, which supports the democratic argument for Acts of Parliament.

## Influences on law reform

| Influence | Example | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Political parties** | Manifesto commitments carried out by a government with a majority | Democratic mandate | Reform can follow party advantage; may be reversed by the next government |
| **Pressure groups** | The Snowdrop campaign after the Dunblane shooting, followed by the Firearms (Amendment) Act 1997 | Raise issues politicians ignore | Small, well-funded groups can gain influence out of proportion to support |
| **Law Commission** | Set up by the Law Commissions Act 1965; its work fed into the Fraud Act 2006 | Expert, independent, research-based | Many reports wait years for parliamentary time |
| **Judges** | *R v R* [1991] removed the marital rape exemption before Parliament legislated | Respond quickly to injustice in a real case | Unelected; can only act when a case arises |
| **Media** | Newspaper campaigning after the murder of Sarah Payne led to the child sex offender disclosure scheme ("Sarah's Law") | Mobilises public opinion | Can push hurried, emotional law making |

## Statutory interpretation: the extra pieces

The rules (literal, golden, mischief) and the purposive approach are covered in the Paper 1 materials. The specification also lists:

- **Ejusdem generis**: general words after a list take their meaning from the list. In *Powell v Kempton Park Racecourse* [1899] "house, office, room or other place" was held to mean indoor places, so an outdoor enclosure was not covered.
- **Presumptions**: for example, that criminal offences require *mens rea* unless Parliament clearly says otherwise (*Sweet v Parsley* [1970]), and that statutes do not operate retrospectively.
- **Intrinsic aids** (the long title, definition sections, schedules) and **extrinsic aids** (dictionaries, Law Commission reports, *Hansard* since *Pepper v Hart* [1993]).
- **The Interpretation Act 1978**: unless the contrary intention appears, words importing the masculine include the feminine, and the singular includes the plural.
- **The Human Rights Act 1998, s 3**: legislation must be read and given effect compatibly with Convention rights "so far as it is possible to do so". In *Ghaidan v Godin-Mendoza* [2004] this allowed "as his or her wife or husband" to include a same-sex partner. Where that is impossible, a higher court may issue a declaration of incompatibility under s 4.

## Delegated legislation: the 2006 Act

Beyond affirmative and negative resolution and judicial review for *ultra vires*, the specification names the **Legislative and Regulatory Reform Act 2006**. It lets ministers make orders to reduce burdens created by legislation, subject to preconditions and parliamentary procedures. Critics see it as widening ministerial power to change Acts by delegated legislation; supporters see a faster route to removing outdated regulation.

## Equity in contract

Equity supplies discretionary remedies where damages would not be adequate. **Specific performance** orders a party to carry out the contract, typically for unique items such as land; it was ordered in *Beswick v Beswick* [1968]. **Rescission** sets the contract aside and returns the parties to their original positions, but it can be lost through delay, as in *Leaf v International Galleries* [1950]. Because these remedies are discretionary, a claimant who has behaved unfairly may be refused them ("he who comes to equity must come with clean hands").

## EU law

The specification asks about **regulations** (directly applicable in member states without further legislation), **directives** (binding as to the result, leaving each state to choose how to implement them) and the roles of the **Court of Justice** and the **European Commission**. Classic examples include *Marshall v Southampton Health Authority* [1986], where a directive was enforced against a state body, and *Francovich v Italy* [1991], which allowed damages against a state for failing to implement a directive. The UK left the EU in 2020, so EU law now matters mainly as history and through retained law; check with your teacher how this content is being examined.

## Civil courts, tribunals and ADR

**The courts.** Most civil claims start in the **County Court** or the **High Court**, with appeals to the Court of Appeal and the Supreme Court. Under the **Civil Procedure Rules 1998**, introduced after Lord Woolf's review, claims are allocated to a track by value and complexity: **small claims** (generally up to £10,000), **fast track** (up to £25,000) and **multi-track**. An intermediate track was added in 2023 for some claims above £25,000. The rules' **overriding objective** is to deal with cases justly and at proportionate cost.

**Tribunals.** The **Tribunals, Courts and Enforcement Act 2007** created a two-tier structure: the **First-tier Tribunal**, divided into chambers, and the **Upper Tribunal**, which hears appeals. A panel is usually a tribunal judge, sometimes sitting with specialist lay members.

**ADR.**

| Method | Who decides | Binding? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Negotiation** | The parties themselves | Only if they agree | Direct settlement between solicitors |
| **Mediation** | The parties, helped by a neutral mediator | Only if they agree | Family and neighbour disputes |
| **Conciliation** | The parties; the conciliator may suggest terms | Only if they agree | ACAS in employment disputes |
| **Arbitration** | An arbitrator | Yes, under the Arbitration Act 1996 | Commercial and consumer scheme disputes |
| **Ombudsman** | The ombudsman investigates complaints | Depends on the scheme | Financial Ombudsman Service |

## People in the law

- **Judges**: appointed through the **Judicial Appointments Commission** under the **Constitutional Reform Act 2005**; the **Courts and Legal Services Act 1990** widened eligibility, for example to solicitor-advocates. Criticisms centre on a lack of diversity.
- **Solicitors and barristers**: solicitors deal directly with clients and are regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority; barristers specialise in advocacy and advice and are regulated by the Bar Standards Board.
- **Magistrates**: unpaid lay justices, usually sitting as a bench of three with a legally qualified adviser. **Jurors**: selected under the **Juries Act 1974** at random from the electoral register.
- **Other lay roles**: tribunal members, mediators, conciliators, arbitrators and ombudsmen bring specialist or everyday experience.

## Costs and funding

**Costs** include court fees, lawyers' fees, the usual rule that the loser pays the winner's costs, and hidden costs such as damage to reputation. **Funding** routes: legal expenses **insurance**; **state funding**, sharply narrowed for civil cases by the **Legal Aid, Sentencing and Punishment of Offenders Act 2012**; **conditional fee agreements** ("no win, no fee"); **trade union** support; **Citizens Advice**; and **pro bono** work.

## Worked example: advising on dispute resolution

*Amira's builder has left her kitchen half-finished and refuses to return. She wants £4,000 back. Advise her.*

```
1. Negotiation first: a letter before action is cheap and may settle it.
2. If not: mediation (quick, private, keeps costs low).
3. Court: about £4,000 is within the small claims track, designed
   for litigants without lawyers; costs recoverable are limited.
4. Funding: legal aid is unlikely for this claim after LASPO 2012;
   she could check home insurance for legal expenses cover.
```

A strong answer applies each method to Amira's facts, not just describes it.

## Common mistakes

- Saying the House of Lords can block any Bill indefinitely.
- Treating ADR methods as the same, rather than contrasting who decides and whether the outcome is binding.
- Describing EU law as though the UK were still a member state.
- Forgetting that a declaration of incompatibility does not invalidate an Act.
