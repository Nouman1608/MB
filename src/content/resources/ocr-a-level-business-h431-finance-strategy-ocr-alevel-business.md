## What a finance strategy is

A **strategy** is a long-term plan for achieving the business's objectives. A **finance strategy** is the long-term plan for how the business will raise, use and control its money so that it can achieve its objectives. It differs from day-to-day finance work (paying invoices, preparing monthly accounts) because it looks several years ahead and asks: *how much money will we need, where will it come from, what will we spend it on, and how will we make sure we stay solvent and profitable?*

### What it contains

| Component | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| Finance objectives | What returns, margins, cash and gearing levels do we aim for? |
| Funding | Which mix of internal and external, short- and long-term sources? |
| Investment | Which projects will we spend on, and how will we appraise them? |
| Budgeting and control | How will we plan spending and monitor variances? |
| Cash and working capital | How will we keep enough cash and manage the working capital cycle? |
| Dividend policy | How much profit do we pay out and how much do we retain? |
| Risk management | How will we deal with interest rate, exchange rate and bad debt risk? |
| Reporting and compliance | How will we meet legal duties and inform stakeholders? |

### Fit

A strategy works only if it fits. It should **support the business objectives** (growth needs funding and investment; a stable-profit objective suggests low gearing); the **parts must be consistent** (a plan for fast growth with a policy of paying out most profit as dividends contradicts itself); and it must suit the business's **size, legal form and the external environment** (interest rates, availability of credit). A private limited company cannot sell shares to the public; a sole trader has a limited capacity to borrow.

## Worked example 1: comparing two funding strategies

Stanton Leisure runs gyms. It has 400,000 shares in issue, equity of £2,000,000, non-current liabilities of £800,000 (6% loan), and operating profit of £450,000. Tax is 20%. It wants to open three new gyms for £1,200,000, and expects them to add £210,000 to operating profit. It has £300,000 of cash available from retained profit. Two strategies are proposed for the remaining £900,000.

- **Strategy A (debt):** borrow the full £900,000 at 7%.
- **Strategy B (mixed):** borrow £500,000 at 7% and issue 80,000 new shares at £5 (raising £400,000).

| | Before expansion | Strategy A | Strategy B |
|---|---|---|---|
| Operating profit | £450,000 | £660,000 | £660,000 |
| Interest | £48,000 | £111,000 | £83,000 |
| Profit before tax | £402,000 | £549,000 | £577,000 |
| Profit after tax (20% tax) | £321,600 | £439,200 | £461,600 |
| Shares in issue | 400,000 | 400,000 | 480,000 |
| Earnings per share | 80.4p | 109.8p | 96.2p |
| Non-current liabilities | £800,000 | £1,700,000 | £1,300,000 |
| Equity | £2,000,000 | £2,000,000 | £2,400,000 |
| Gearing (NCL ÷ capital employed) | 28.6% | 45.9% | 35.1% |
| Interest cover | 9.4 times | 5.9 times | 8.0 times |
| Existing owners' share | 100% | 100% | 83.3% |

Working for A: interest = £48,000 + 7% × £900,000 = £111,000; profit before tax = £660,000 − £111,000 = £549,000; tax £109,800; profit after tax £439,200; EPS = £439,200 ÷ 400,000 = 109.8p. Gearing = £1,700,000 ÷ (£2,000,000 + £1,700,000) = 45.9%. For B: interest = £48,000 + 7% × £500,000 = £83,000; profit after tax = £461,600; EPS = £461,600 ÷ 480,000 = 96.2p; gearing = £1,300,000 ÷ £3,700,000 = 35.1%.

**What if the gyms disappoint?** If they add only £80,000 to operating profit (not £210,000), operating profit is £530,000. Under A: interest £111,000, profit after tax £335,200, EPS 83.8p, interest cover 4.8 times. Under B: interest £83,000, profit after tax £357,600, EPS 74.5p, interest cover 6.4 times.

**Evaluation.**

- Strategy A gives the higher earnings per share in both cases (109.8p against 96.2p; 83.8p against 74.5p) and keeps all control with existing owners.
- But A raises gearing to 45.9% and cuts interest cover to 5.9 times (4.8 times if the gyms disappoint). If the bank has set a covenant (a condition of the loan) limiting gearing to 40%, A breaks it, while B (35.1%) meets it.
- B lowers EPS by 13.6p compared with A, and existing owners' share falls to 83.3%, but the business is safer.
- Both beat the 80.4p before expansion, so the expansion adds value either way, if the forecast profit arrives.

**Judgement.** Strategy B is the better choice for a business with a covenant at 40% and uncertain demand for new gyms, because the extra earnings under A do not justify breaking the lender's limit. If there were no covenant, A's higher return might suit owners who want to keep control and accept more risk. The judgement would change if interest rates were expected to rise, since that would hit A harder.

## Impact and importance

**Impact** is the effect the strategy has on the business and its stakeholders; **importance** is how much it matters to success. A sound finance strategy:

- **Secures the funds** needed for the business objectives at an acceptable cost.
- **Controls risk**, through limits on gearing and policies on cash reserves.
- **Improves returns** through good investment choices (the Stanton expansion raises EPS from 80.4p to between 96.2p and 109.8p).
- **Gives control and early warning** through budgets, forecasts and ratios.
- **Builds confidence** among lenders and investors, reducing the cost of future finance.

| Stakeholder | Effect of a good strategy | Effect of a poor or missing strategy |
|---|---|---|
| Owners | Better returns at acceptable risk | Wasted funds; risk of failure |
| Lenders | Confidence in repayment | Missed payments; higher interest |
| Employees | Job security and investment | Redundancies if cash runs out |
| Suppliers | Prompt payment | Late payment, lost supply |
| Customers | Reliable supply and service | Disruption if the business fails |
| Government | Tax and employment | Tax losses and unemployment |

Importance is greatest when the business is growing, has high fixed costs or borrowings, operates in a volatile market (exchange or interest rates) or is close to its limits on cash. It is lower for a small stable business with no borrowing, where an informal approach may be enough. A strategy is only as good as its assumptions: forecasts may prove wrong, and external changes can make a plan out of date, so it needs regular review.

## Recommending a strategy

To **recommend and justify**:

1. **Diagnose** the objective and the financial position (use ratios and cash forecasts).
2. **Choose** the main elements: funding mix, investment priorities, controls, dividend policy.
3. **Justify** with figures (gearing, interest cover, EPS, payback or NPV, cash balance).
4. **Weigh** risks and an alternative.
5. **Judge**, and say what would change your view.

### Worked example 2: a short recommendation

Larchfield Bakery (sales £3,000,000) wants to double output in three years. Its gearing is 20%, interest cover is 12 and cash is tight in the winter months.

*Recommendation.* A three-part strategy: (1) fund the new ovens (£400,000) with a five-year bank loan, which keeps gearing below 40% (the lender's likely limit); (2) arrange a £50,000 overdraft facility to cover the winter cash gap; (3) hold dividends at 30% of profit to retain funds for growth, with quarterly budget reviews.

*Justification.* The ovens last ten years, so matching with a five-year loan keeps the cost certain. Low existing gearing gives room to borrow. The overdraft deals with a short-term problem with short-term finance. Retained profit reduces future borrowing.

*Risks and alternatives.* Higher interest rates would raise costs, so the loan could have a fixed rate. Issuing shares would avoid interest but dilute control of a family business. Leasing the ovens would spread the cost but cost more in total.

*Judgement.* The loan-based strategy is right because the business is stable, gearing is low and owners wish to keep control. It would be reviewed if profit forecasts fell or if interest cover dropped near 4.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the strategy as a list of finance tasks with no link to objectives.
- Recommending a funding source without figures on gearing or interest cover.
- Ignoring risk and the effect on control.
- Forgetting that the strategy needs regular review.
- Giving a recommendation with no alternative considered.

## Exam technique

For **evaluate**, give the impact on the business and at least two stakeholders, use figures, weigh short- and long-term effects and conclude. For **recommend and justify**, make a clear choice, justify it with case evidence and numbers, consider an alternative and give a judgement.
