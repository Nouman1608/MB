These notes condense section **3.5.7 Diffraction** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630) specification: the "appearance of the diffraction pattern from a single slit using monochromatic and white light", the "qualitative treatment of the variation of the width of the central diffraction maximum with wavelength and slit width", the plane transmission grating at normal incidence with the "derivation of d sin θ = nλ", and "applications of diffraction gratings". The intensity-angle graph and the spectrometer are not tested.

For full explanations and worked examples, read the [diffraction study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-diffraction/). Test yourself afterwards with the [diffraction practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-diffraction-practice/).

## Core recall

- Diffraction is the spreading of a wave through a gap or around an obstacle. It is most noticeable when the gap is similar in size to the wavelength.
- Wavelength, frequency and speed are unchanged by diffraction.
- Single slit, monochromatic light: very bright central maximum, twice the width of the others; dimmer side maxima; dark minima between.
- Single slit, white light: white centre; side maxima are spectra with violet inside and red outside.
- Central maximum gets wider when the slit is narrower or the wavelength is longer.
- Grating at normal incidence: d sin θ = nλ, with d = 1/N.
- Grating maxima are sharp and bright, so angles, and hence wavelengths, can be measured precisely.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Diffraction | Spreading of waves on passing through a gap or past an edge |
| Central maximum | The bright region straight ahead in a single-slit pattern |
| Subsidiary maxima | The dimmer bright regions on either side of the central maximum |
| Plane transmission grating | A plate with many equally spaced parallel slits that light passes through |
| Grating spacing d | Distance between centres of adjacent slits; d = 1/N |
| Order n | The whole number labelling each maximum; n = 0 is straight through |
| Normal incidence | Light striking the grating at 90° to its surface |
| Line spectrum | A set of separate wavelengths emitted by a particular element |

## Single slit: the trends

| Change | Width of central maximum |
|---|---|
| Slit narrower | Increases |
| Slit wider | Decreases (and pattern brighter) |
| Red light instead of blue | Increases |
| Blue light instead of red | Decreases |
| Slit very much wider than λ | Hardly any spreading |

Phrase it as: more spreading when λ is larger compared with the slit width.

## Grating: method and checks

1. Convert to lines per metre: N (per mm) × 1000.
2. d = 1/N.
3. sin θ = nλ/d, with θ measured from the straight-through direction.
4. If you measure across both sides, halve the angle.
5. Highest order: n ≤ d/λ, rounded down. Number of maxima = 2n(max) + 1.
6. If d/λ is a whole number, that order would be at 90° and is not seen.

## Derivation points

- Normal incidence, so the waves leave all slits in phase.
- Two adjacent slits, waves at angle θ to the normal.
- Path difference between them = d sin θ (right-angled triangle, hypotenuse d).
- Maximum when path difference = nλ, so d sin θ = nλ.

## White light through a grating

- Zero order: white, straight through.
- Each other order is a full spectrum, violet nearest the centre, red furthest out.
- Second- and third-order spectra always overlap, because 2 × 700 nm is greater than 3 × 400 nm.
- Higher orders spread close wavelengths further apart.

## Applications

- Precise measurement of wavelengths.
- Identifying elements from line spectra, including in starlight.
- Analysing the composition of gases and flames.
- X-ray diffraction: crystal layers act like a grating, revealing atomic arrangement.

## Quick reminders with numbers

- 500 lines per mm, λ = 550 nm: d = 2.0 × 10⁻⁶ m; first order at 16.0°; d/λ = 3.6, so the highest order is 3 and there are 7 maxima.
- First orders measured at 44.6° apart with a 633 nm laser: θ = 22.3°, so the grating has 600 lines per mm.
- 300 lines per mm with white light: first-order spectrum runs from 6.9° to 12.1°.

## Exam traps

- A narrower slit gives a wider central maximum, not a narrower one.
- Red is on the outside of each coloured fringe, not the inside.
- Lines per mm is not d: take the reciprocal after converting to lines per metre.
- Halve the angle between left and right maxima of the same order.
- Never round the highest order up.
- Only whole-number orders exist.
- Do not draw or describe the intensity-angle graph unless asked; it is not required.

## Self-test

1. Define diffraction.
2. Describe the single-slit pattern for monochromatic light.
3. What happens to the central maximum when the slit is made narrower?
4. In a white-light single-slit pattern, which colour is on the outer edge of each side maximum?
5. A grating has 250 lines per mm. Find d.
6. For the grating in question 5, find the angle of the second order for 650 nm light.
7. A grating has d = 1.25 × 10⁻⁶ m. How many maxima are seen with 532 nm light?
8. Why are grating maxima sharper than double-slit fringes?
9. State the condition for a maximum in the grating derivation.
10. The two first-order maxima are 36.0° apart. What angle do you use in d sin θ = nλ?
11. Give two applications of diffraction gratings.
12. Explain why the second- and third-order white-light spectra overlap for any grating.

### Answers

1. The **spreading** of a wave as it passes through a gap or around an obstacle.
2. A **bright, wide central maximum** with dimmer, narrower maxima on each side, separated by dark minima.
3. It becomes **wider**.
4. **Red**.
5. d = 1/(250 × 10³) = **4.0 × 10⁻⁶ m**.
6. sin θ = 2 × 650 × 10⁻⁹/4.0 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.325, so θ = **19.0°**.
7. d/λ = 2.35, so n(max) = 2: **5 maxima** (n = 0, ±1, ±2).
8. With many slits, a small change of angle puts the waves from widely separated slits out of step, so they cancel except at the **exact angles** of the maxima.
9. Path difference between adjacent slits equals a **whole number of wavelengths**: d sin θ = nλ.
10. **18.0°**, half the angle between them.
11. Any two: measuring wavelengths; identifying elements from line spectra; analysing starlight or gases; X-ray diffraction to study crystals.
12. The red end of the second order needs sin θ = 1400 nm/d, which is larger than the violet end of the third order, 1200 nm/d, **whatever d is**.
