These notes condense section 1.2.6, Government intervention, of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Economics (4EC1) specification, Issue 3, February 2026, examined in Paper 1 with a single tier of entry. All figures are invented. For explanations and worked cases, read the [government intervention study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-economics-4ec1-government-intervention/); to practise, use the [government intervention practice questions](/resources/edexcel-igcse-economics-4ec1-government-intervention-practice/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Indirect tax | Tax on spending on a good, raising firms' costs and the price |
| Subsidy | Government payment that lowers producers' costs (or the price to consumers) |
| Fine | Penalty for breaking a rule |
| Regulation | Rules that limit or ban an activity |
| Pollution permit | Permission to emit a set amount of pollution; can be traded |
| Cap | The total amount of pollution allowed by all permits |
| Merger | Two firms joining to form one |
| Takeover | One firm buying control of another |
| Minimum wage | The lowest wage per hour employers may legally pay |
| Surplus of labour | More workers supplied than demanded at a wage above equilibrium (unemployment) |

## Five externality policies

| Policy | How it works | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| Taxation | Raises cost and price of goods with external costs; less bought | Polluter pays; revenue raised; incentive to switch | Hard to set the right level; little effect if demand is inelastic; can hit poorer households |
| Subsidies | Lowers cost and price; more bought | Encourages goods with external benefits and cleaner alternatives | Costs taxpayers; opportunity cost; dependency; may boost profits rather than cut prices |
| Fines | Penalty for breaking pollution rules | Clear; punishes offenders; some revenue | Must catch offenders; harm already done; too small to deter if saving is larger |
| Regulation | Limits or bans the harmful activity | Certain outcome; quick for dangerous harms | Enforcement cost; raises firms' costs; no incentive to go beyond the limit |
| Pollution permits | Cap on total pollution; permits traded | Certain total; cuts made where cheapest; cap can be tightened | Hard to set cap; monitoring cost; too many permits means little effect |

**Matching rule:** taxes, fines, regulation and permits target **external costs**; subsidies are for **external benefits** (or cleaner substitutes).

## Calculation drills (invented)

- **Tax revenue:** tax per unit × quantity after the tax. 0.50 × 30 000 = **15 000**.
- **Tax burden:** price rises from 2.00 to 2.30 with a 0.50 tax → buyers pay 0.30, sellers bear 0.20.
- **Subsidy cost:** subsidy per unit × quantity after. 3 × 12 000 = **36 000**.
- **Expected fine:** fine × chance of being caught. 80 000 × 0.25 = **20 000**; deters only if greater than the money saved.
- **Permit cut:** emissions without permits − permits issued. 500 − 400 = **100 tonnes (20%)**.

## Regulating competition: four aims

| Aim | Example action |
|---|---|
| Promote competition | Remove barriers to entry; help new firms |
| Limit monopoly power | Ban cartels and price fixing; cap prices of dominant firms |
| Protect consumer interests | Safety standards; honest advertising and labelling; refund rights |
| Control mergers and takeovers | Investigate large combined market shares; block or set conditions |

*Drill:* merging firms with 28% and 17% → combined share **45%**; a regulator may block it or require the sale of some outlets.

**Evaluation:** enforcement costs; slow investigations; blocking a merger may lose economies of scale.

## The minimum wage

**Reasons:** reduce poverty; prevent exploitation; make work pay more than benefits; reduce inequality.

**Introducing (diagram):** horizontal line above equilibrium wage → quantity demanded falls, quantity supplied rises → surplus of labour (unemployment) = Qs − Qd. Below equilibrium → no effect.

**Increasing (diagram):** higher line → demand falls further, supply rises → wider surplus.

*Drill (invented schedule):*

| Wage | Demanded | Supplied |
|---|---|---|
| 7 | 4 400 | 4 400 |
| 8 | 4 000 | 5 000 |
| 9 | 3 600 | 5 600 |

- Equilibrium: 7, employing 4 400.
- Minimum wage 8: employment 4 000; surplus **1 000**.
- Raised to 9: employment 3 600; surplus **2 000**.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Higher pay for those in work | Possible unemployment |
| Less poverty and inequality | Higher costs, prices or lower profit |
| Better motivation and productivity | Firms substitute machines |
| More spending | Informal work |

**Deciding factor:** how far above equilibrium it is set, and whether productivity rises.

## Exam traps

- A minimum wage **below** equilibrium has no effect.
- Use subsidies for **external benefits**, not external costs.
- Fines and regulation **need enforcement**.
- Permits work only if the **cap is low enough**.
- Each policy needs **one advantage and one disadvantage** when asked.
- Competition answers need an **action**, not just an aim.
- Policy answers with no advantage or disadvantage.
- Using the wrong policy for the type of externality.
- Minimum wage diagrams with the line below equilibrium.
- Forgetting to multiply by quantity in tax and subsidy calculations.
- Evaluations without a deciding factor.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the five policies in the specification for dealing with externalities.
2. Give one advantage of a tax on a polluting good.
3. Why might a tax not reduce pollution much?
4. Which policy would best raise vaccination rates?
5. Give one disadvantage of fines.
6. How do pollution permits ensure a certain total of pollution?
7. State the four aims of government regulation of competition.
8. Two firms with 31% and 14% of a market merge. Calculate their combined share.
9. Give two reasons for a minimum wage.
10. What does a minimum wage set below the equilibrium wage do?
11. At a minimum wage, 6 200 workers are supplied and 5 400 demanded. Calculate the surplus.
12. Give one disadvantage of raising the minimum wage.

### Answers

1. Taxation, subsidies, fines, regulation, pollution permits.
2. The polluter pays (or: raises revenue; encourages switching to cleaner goods).
3. If demand is price inelastic, quantity bought falls only slightly.
4. A subsidy.
5. Offenders must be caught (or: the harm is already done; too small to deter).
6. The government sets a cap on the number of permits, so total emissions cannot exceed it.
7. Promote competition; limit monopoly power; protect consumer interests; control mergers and takeovers.
8. 31 + 14 = **45%**.
9. Any two: reduce poverty; prevent exploitation; make work pay; reduce inequality.
10. Nothing: the market wage is already higher.
11. 6 200 − 5 400 = **800**.
12. More unemployment (or: higher costs for firms, higher prices, substitution by machines).
