## 3.2.5.1 The principles of immunology

### Cell-surface molecules and identity

Every cell carries specific molecules on its surface, many of them proteins. These act as an identity badge. The immune system uses them to recognise:

- pathogens and toxins
- cells from other organisms of the same species (for example, transplanted tissue from a donor who is not genetically identical)
- abnormal body cells, such as cells infected by a virus or cancer cells.

Your own cells carry "self" molecules, so the response is aimed at "non-self" molecules.

### Antigens

An **antigen** is a molecule, usually a protein, that is recognised as foreign and stimulates an immune response. Antigens sit on the surface of pathogens, are part of toxins, and are found on foreign or abnormal cells. Each antigen has a specific three-dimensional shape, which is why each lymphocyte and antibody recognises only one.

### Antigen variability

Some pathogens change their antigens. This matters in two ways.

1. **Disease.** If a pathogen's antigen changes by mutation, memory cells made against the old antigen no longer bind it. The person can be infected again with the "new" strain. Influenza is the standard example: its surface antigens change, so new strains keep appearing.
2. **Prevention.** A vaccine contains specific antigens. If the circulating pathogen has different antigens, the vaccine gives little protection. Influenza vaccines are therefore updated and given again, and a vaccine against a very variable pathogen such as HIV is hard to design.

### T cells and B cells

Both are lymphocytes. The two types of response work together.

| Feature | Cell-mediated immunity | Humoral immunity |
|---|---|---|
| Main cells | T cells | B cells |
| Acts on | Body cells that carry foreign antigens, for example virus-infected cells | Antigens free in blood and tissue fluid |
| Main product | Responses by T cells themselves | Antibodies, released into the blood |

Humoral immunity is called that because it works through antibodies carried in body fluids ("humours"). B cells are *primarily* responsible for it. T cells are needed to start the B cell response, as section 3.2.5.2 explains.

### Phagocytosis and lysosomes

Phagocytes such as neutrophils and macrophages engulf and digest pathogens.

1. Chemicals from the pathogen attract the phagocyte, and receptors on its surface bind to the pathogen.
2. The cell surface membrane folds round the pathogen and engulfs it, forming a vacuole called a **phagosome**.
3. **Lysosomes** fuse with the phagosome and release hydrolytic enzymes (including lysozyme) into it.
4. The enzymes digest the pathogen. Useful products are absorbed into the cytoplasm.

Lysosomes are membrane-bound organelles from the Golgi apparatus that hold the enzymes safely away from the rest of the cell. A macrophage then displays pathogen antigens on its own surface, which leads into the next section.

## 3.2.5.2 The response of B cells to a foreign antigen

### Antigen-presenting cells

An **antigen-presenting cell** displays foreign antigens on its surface after taking in and processing a pathogen. A macrophage is a typical example, and so is a body cell infected by a virus, which displays viral antigens. T cells have receptors that bind to a particular antigen displayed this way. This step links the non-specific response (phagocytosis) to the specific response.

### Sequence of the B cell response

1. A pathogen is engulfed by a macrophage, which presents its antigen.
2. A T cell whose receptor is complementary to that antigen attaches to the presenting cell and becomes activated. These T cells then help to stimulate B cells.
3. A B cell with a surface antibody complementary to the same antigen binds it. Each B cell makes one type of antibody, so only a few B cells fit a given antigen.
4. The selected B cell divides repeatedly by mitosis (**clonal expansion**), giving many identical cells.
5. Most of the clones become **plasma cells**, which secrete large amounts of one antibody. Some become **memory cells** (section 3.2.5.3).

### Antibodies

An **antibody** is a protein (an immunoglobulin) made by plasma cells that binds specifically to one antigen.

Structure of a typical antibody:

- Four polypeptide chains (two identical long **heavy chains**, two identical shorter **light chains**) joined by disulfide bridges.
- The molecule is Y-shaped. The two arms each end in a **variable region**. Its amino acid sequence differs between antibodies, so its tertiary structure forms an **antigen-binding site** complementary to one antigen. There are two identical binding sites per antibody.
- A **constant region**, which does not vary within a class of antibodies.
- A flexible **hinge region** at the junction of the arms.

A different primary structure in the variable region gives a different binding-site shape, so each antibody is specific.

### The antigen-antibody complex

When an antibody binds its antigen it forms an **antigen-antibody complex**. For bacterial cells, this leads to destruction in two linked ways.

- **Agglutination.** An antibody has two binding sites and can therefore attach to two bacterial cells at the same time. Many antibodies link many cells into clumps. Clumped cells cannot spread easily and are easier to engulf.
- **Phagocytosis.** Phagocytes bind to the antibodies coating the bacteria and engulf them, and the cells are digested by lysosomes as above.

## 3.2.5.3 Vaccination

### Primary and secondary response

The first time you meet an antigen, the **primary response** is slow. Few B cells fit the antigen, so there is a lag before antibody concentration rises, and the peak is low. Some clones become **memory cells**, which persist for years.

On the next exposure, memory cells recognise the antigen quickly, divide rapidly and form plasma cells. The **secondary response** therefore starts sooner, rises faster, reaches a much higher peak and lasts longer. The pathogen is usually destroyed before symptoms appear.

| Cell | Role |
|---|---|
| Plasma cell | Secretes large quantities of antibody; short-lived |
| Memory cell | Long-lived; gives the rapid, larger secondary response |

### Vaccines and herd immunity

A **vaccine** contains antigens from a pathogen (a weakened or killed whole organism, or purified antigens) but cannot cause the disease. It triggers the primary response and creates memory cells, so a later infection triggers a secondary response. Booster doses strengthen memory.

**Herd immunity.** When a high proportion of a population is immune, a pathogen cannot pass easily between susceptible people. This protects people who are not immune, such as babies too young to be vaccinated and people who cannot be vaccinated for medical reasons. Protection of a population depends on how many people are vaccinated and how well the vaccine works.

### Active and passive immunity

| | Active immunity | Passive immunity |
|---|---|---|
| Source of antibodies | Your own plasma cells | Another individual |
| Memory cells | Formed | Not formed |
| Onset | Slow | Immediate |
| Duration | Long term | Short term, as antibodies are broken down |
| Examples | Infection; vaccination | Antibodies crossing the placenta or in breast milk; injected antibodies |

### Evaluating vaccine trials

You may be asked to judge methodology, evidence and data. Points to look for:

- **Control.** A placebo group, or a group given an existing vaccine, lets you attribute differences to the vaccine.
- **Randomisation and blinding.** Random allocation avoids bias in who gets the vaccine; double-blind testing stops expectations affecting results.
- **Sample.** Large, representative groups make results more reliable and let you generalise. Check age, sex and health status.
- **Outcome measured.** Antibody concentration shows an immune response; fewer cases of disease shows protection. Disease incidence is the stronger evidence.
- **Duration.** Short follow-up cannot show how long protection lasts.
- **Safety.** Compare side effects between groups.
- **Statistics.** A difference needs a suitable statistical test before you can call it significant.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: vaccine efficacy

In a trial, 6,000 people received a vaccine and 9 developed the disease. In a placebo group of 6,000 people, 72 developed it. Calculate the efficacy.

```
Attack rate, vaccine  = 9 / 6000 x 100  = 0.15 %
Attack rate, placebo  = 72 / 6000 x 100 = 1.2 %
Efficacy = (1.2 - 0.15) / 1.2 x 100     = 87.5 %
```

The vaccine reduced the risk of disease by 87.5%.

### Example 2: how much of a population must be immune?

A simple model gives the minimum immune fraction as 1 - 1/R, where R is the mean number of new infections caused by a single case when nobody is immune. If R = 6:

```
1 - 1/6 = 0.8333, so 83.3 % must be immune.
```

If the vaccine protects only 90 % of those given it, coverage must be 83.3 / 0.9 = **92.6 %**.

### Example 3: reading an antibody graph

A person's antibody concentration peaks at 12 arbitrary units after a first exposure and at 480 units after a second.

```
Fold increase = 480 / 12 = 40
```

The secondary peak is 40 times higher. Explain: memory cells from the first exposure divide quickly and form many plasma cells.

## Common errors

- Writing that antibodies "kill" bacteria. They bind, cause agglutination and mark cells for phagocytosis.
- Saying a vaccine contains antibodies. It contains antigens.
- Saying memory cells make antibodies directly. They divide and become plasma cells on re-exposure.
- Describing herd immunity as everybody being immune.
- Calling a placebo group "unvaccinated and untreated". It receives an inactive injection so participants cannot tell which group they are in.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is an antigen?**
An antigen is a molecule, usually a protein, that is recognised as foreign and stimulates an immune response. Antigens are found on pathogens, as part of toxins, and on foreign or abnormal cells.

**What is the difference between cell-mediated and humoral immunity?**
Cell-mediated immunity is carried out by T cells and acts on body cells that carry foreign antigens, such as virus-infected cells. Humoral immunity is mainly carried out by B cells and works through antibodies released into body fluids.

**How do antibodies lead to the destruction of bacteria?**
Each antibody has two binding sites, so it can attach to two bacterial cells at once, clumping many cells together by agglutination. Phagocytes then bind to the antibody-coated bacteria, engulf them and digest them with lysosome enzymes.

**What is herd immunity?**
Herd immunity is when a high proportion of a population is immune, so a pathogen cannot pass easily between susceptible people. It protects people who are not immune, such as babies too young to be vaccinated.
