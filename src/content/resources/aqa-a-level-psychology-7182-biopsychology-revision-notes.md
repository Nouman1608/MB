**Biopsychology in AQA A-level Psychology (7182) covers how the nervous system, hormones and brain produce behaviour, and how the brain is studied.** It is section 4.2.2 and topic 6 of the AQA AS and A-level Psychology (7181, 7182) specification, Version 1.4 (1 September 2025), and is assessed on Paper 2: Psychology in Context (2 hours, 96 marks, 33.3% of the A-level). These notes give you the facts, the studies and the evaluation points in a form you can test yourself on.

For full explanations, read the [Biopsychology study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-psychology-7182-biopsychology/) first. When you are ready, try the [Biopsychology practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-psychology-7182-biopsychology-practice/).

## What the specification lists

1. The divisions of the nervous system: central and peripheral (somatic and autonomic).
2. The structure and function of sensory, relay and motor neurons; synaptic transmission, neurotransmitters, excitation and inhibition.
3. The function of the endocrine system: glands and hormones.
4. The fight or flight response, including the role of adrenaline.
5. Localisation of function and hemispheric lateralisation: motor, somatosensory, visual, auditory and language centres; Broca's and Wernicke's areas; split-brain research. Plasticity and functional recovery after trauma.
6. Ways of studying the brain: fMRI; EEG and ERPs; post-mortem examinations.
Biological rhythms (circadian, infradian and ultradian rhythms, pacemakers and zeitgebers) were in earlier editions but are **not** in section 4.2.2 of Version 1.4, so they are not examined.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| CNS | Brain and spinal cord |
| Somatic nervous system | Voluntary movement and sensory information |
| Autonomic nervous system | Involuntary functions; sympathetic (arousal) and parasympathetic (rest) |
| Sensory / relay / motor neuron | Receptor to CNS / within CNS / CNS to effector |
| Synapse | Gap between neurons where neurotransmitters carry the signal |
| Excitation / inhibition | Neurotransmitter makes postsynaptic firing more / less likely |
| Summation | Combined effect of excitatory and inhibitory inputs decides firing |
| Hormone | Chemical messenger carried in the blood |
| Adrenaline | Hormone from the adrenal medulla that drives fight or flight |
| Localisation of function | Specific brain areas have specific functions |
| Lateralisation | The two hemispheres specialise in different functions |
| Plasticity | The brain's ability to change with experience |
| Functional recovery | Undamaged areas take over functions after trauma |
| Spatial resolution | How precisely a method shows where activity happens |
| Temporal resolution | How precisely a method shows when activity happens |

## Neuron types compared

| Neuron | Location | Dendrites | Axon | Function |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sensory | PNS, receptors to CNS | Long | Short | Carries sensory information to the CNS |
| Relay | CNS | Short | Short | Links sensory and motor neurons |
| Motor | CNS to effectors | Short | Long | Carries commands to muscles and glands |

## Synaptic transmission in six steps

1. Action potential reaches the presynaptic terminal.
2. Synaptic vesicles release neurotransmitter into the cleft.
3. Neurotransmitter diffuses across the synapse.
4. It binds to receptors on the postsynaptic neuron.
5. The postsynaptic effect is excitatory or inhibitory; summation decides whether it fires.
6. Neurotransmitter is broken down or reabsorbed (reuptake).

## Fight or flight in five steps

1. Amygdala detects threat.
2. Hypothalamus activates the sympathetic nervous system.
3. Adrenal medulla releases adrenaline.
4. Heart rate, breathing and blood glucose rise; digestion slows; pupils dilate.
5. Parasympathetic system restores rest when the threat has gone.

## Studies at a glance

| Study | Finding | Use it for |
|---|---|---|
| Broca (Tan) | Damage to left frontal area; could not produce speech | Localisation of speech production |
| Petersen et al. (1988) | PET: Wernicke's area active when listening, Broca's when reading | Scan evidence for localisation |
| Lashley (1950) | Rats' learning not lost by removing any single cortical area | Equipotentiality, challenges localisation |
| Sperry (1968) | Split-brain patients named objects shown to the right visual field only | Lateralisation of language |
| Maguire et al. (2000) | Taxi drivers had more posterior hippocampal grey matter | Plasticity |
| Taylor et al. (2000) | Females may "tend and befriend" | Gender bias in fight or flight |
| Dement and Kleitman (1957) | EEG showed REM sleep linked to dreaming | EEG as a way of studying the brain |

## Brain-study methods compared

| Method | Spatial resolution | Temporal resolution | Invasive? | Key limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| fMRI | High | Low (seconds) | No | Correlational; costly |
| EEG | Low | High (milliseconds) | No | Cannot locate deep activity |
| ERP | Low | High | No | Needs many trials to average out noise |
| Post-mortem | Very high (direct) | None | After death | Cause of damage unclear; no live behaviour |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Somatic vs autonomic**: voluntary vs involuntary.
- **Sympathetic vs parasympathetic**: arousal vs rest.
- **Nervous vs endocrine**: fast, short-lived electrical/chemical signals vs slower, longer-lasting hormones in the blood.
- **Localisation vs lateralisation**: a function in a specific area vs a function in one hemisphere.
- **Plasticity vs functional recovery**: general change with learning vs change to compensate for damage.
- **Spatial vs temporal resolution**: how precisely a method locates activity vs how precisely it times it.

## Mini essay plan: "Discuss localisation of function in the brain" [16]

1. **AO1**: motor, somatosensory, visual, auditory areas; Broca's (production) and Wernicke's (comprehension) areas.
2. **AO3 support**: Broca's Tan; Petersen et al. (1988) scans.
3. **AO3 challenge**: Lashley (1950) equipotentiality; plasticity shows functions can move.
4. **AO3 method**: case studies of brain damage are unique and may not generalise; scans show correlation, not cause.
5. **Issue**: localisation is reductionist; complex behaviours involve networks across the brain.
6. **Conclusion**: basic functions are localised; higher functions are more distributed.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which paper assesses biopsychology?**
Paper 2, Psychology in Context, together with approaches and research methods: 2 hours, 96 marks, 33.3% of the A-level.

**What is the difference between localisation and lateralisation?**
Localisation means specific areas of the brain have specific functions; lateralisation means some functions are processed mainly in one hemisphere, such as language in the left in most people.

**Do I need to know scanning techniques in detail?**
You need fMRI, EEG, ERPs and post-mortem examinations: how each works, what it shows, and its main strengths and limitations, especially spatial and temporal resolution.

## Quick self-test

1. Which division of the nervous system controls voluntary movement?
2. Which neuron has a long axon and short dendrites?
3. Why is synaptic transmission one-directional?
4. Which gland releases adrenaline?
5. Which hemisphere usually controls language?
6. In split-brain research, which visual field's objects could be named?
7. Which brain method has the best temporal resolution?
8. Which gland releases melatonin, and which part of the adrenal gland releases cortisol?

**Answers:** (1) somatic; (2) motor neuron; (3) vesicles are only presynaptic and receptors only postsynaptic; (4) adrenal medulla; (5) left; (6) right visual field; (7) EEG (or ERP); (8) the pineal gland; the adrenal cortex.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Labelling diagrams of neurons wrongly; learn the dendrite and axon lengths.
- Writing "adrenaline is a neurotransmitter released by the hypothalamus" in a fight-or-flight answer; it is a hormone from the adrenal medulla.
- Giving "fMRI is expensive" as the only evaluation; compare spatial and temporal resolution.
- Revising biological rhythms from an older textbook; they are not in Version 1.4.
