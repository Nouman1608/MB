Work through these coordinate geometry questions in order. They start with short skills and build to multi-step problems. Questions marked *(no calculator)* are in the style of Paper 1; the rest allow a calculator, as in Paper 2. Show every step, because method marks are awarded even when the final answer is wrong.

## Questions

**1.** *(no calculator)* Rearrange 4y + 3x = 12 to make y the subject, and state the gradient of the line. **[2]**

**2.** *(no calculator)* Write down the equation of the horizontal line and the equation of the vertical line that both pass through (3, −4). **[2]**

**3.** *(no calculator)* A is the point (−2, 5) and B is the point (6, −1). Find the midpoint of AB and the length of AB. **[3]**

**4.** *(no calculator)* Find the equation of the straight line through (1, 4) and (5, 12). **[3]**

**5.** *(no calculator)* Show that the lines y = 3x − 2 and 6x − 2y = 7 are parallel. **[2]**

**6.** *(no calculator)* Line P is perpendicular to the line 4y + 3x = 12 and passes through (3, 2). Find the equation of line P. **[3]**

**7.** *(no calculator)* The line through (1, k) and (5, 9) is parallel to the line 4y = 3x + 1. Find the value of k. **[4]**

**8.** *(no calculator)* The points A(−1, −2), B(2, 4) and C(5, 10) are given. Show that A, B and C lie on one straight line. **[4]**

**9.** The line 3x + 5y = 30 crosses the x-axis at A and the y-axis at B. Find the coordinates of A and B, and calculate the length of AB, giving your answer correct to 3 significant figures. **[4]**

**10.** P is the point (−2, 1) and Q is the point (4, 5). Find the equation of the perpendicular bisector of PQ. Give your answer in the form ax + by = c, where a, b and c are integers. **[5]**

**11.** The points A, B and C are (1, 1), (7, 3) and (6, 6).

(a) Show that angle ABC is a right angle. **[3]**

(b) Find the exact lengths of AB and BC, and hence the area of triangle ABC. **[3]**

(c) D is the midpoint of AC. Show that BD = AD. **[4]**

**12.** The line l passes through A(0, 8) and B(6, 0). The line m is perpendicular to l and passes through the origin O.

(a) Find the equation of l. **[3]**

(b) Find the equation of m. **[2]**

(c) The lines l and m meet at P. Find the coordinates of P. **[3]**

(d) Calculate the distance OP. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** 4y = 12 − 3x, so **y = 3 − ¾x** [1]. Gradient = **−¾** [1].

*Examiner insight:* the gradient is the coefficient of x only once y is on its own, so candidates who read off 3 or 4 from the original equation lose the second mark.

**2.** Horizontal line: **y = −4** [1]. Vertical line: **x = 3** [1].

*Examiner insight:* a horizontal line has every point at the same height, so the equation names y. Swapping the two letters is the usual way to lose both marks.

**3.** Midpoint = ((−2 + 6) ÷ 2, (5 + (−1)) ÷ 2) = **(2, 2)** [1]. Length = √(8² + 6²) [1] = √100 = **10 units** [1].

*Examiner insight:* write the differences in brackets before squaring. A slip such as 5 − 1 instead of 5 − (−1) in the y-difference is a common error.

**4.** Gradient = (12 − 4) ÷ (5 − 1) = 2 [1]. y − 4 = 2(x − 1) [1], so **y = 2x + 2** [1].

*Examiner insight:* substitute the second point into your final equation. 12 = 2 × 5 + 2 confirms it in seconds and catches a wrong intercept.

**5.** 6x − 2y = 7 gives 2y = 6x − 7, so y = 3x − 3.5 [1]. Both lines have gradient 3, and the intercepts differ, so they are parallel and distinct [1].

*Examiner insight:* "show that" needs a stated conclusion. Writing the two gradients without saying that equal gradients mean parallel lines can lose the final mark.

**6.** Gradient of 4y + 3x = 12 is −¾, so gradient of P = 4/3 [1]. y − 2 = 4/3(x − 3) [1], so **y = 4/3 x − 2** [1].

*Examiner insight:* flip the fraction and change the sign, and do both. Check with a product: −¾ × 4/3 = −1.

**7.** 4y = 3x + 1 gives gradient ¾ [1]. Parallel lines have equal gradients [1]. (9 − k) ÷ (5 − 1) = ¾ [1]. 9 − k = 3, so **k = 6** [1].

*Examiner insight:* subtract the coordinates in the same order on top and bottom. Reversing one of them flips the sign of the gradient and gives k = 12.

**8.** Gradient AB = (4 − (−2)) ÷ (2 − (−1)) = 6 ÷ 3 = 2 [1]. Gradient BC = (10 − 4) ÷ (5 − 2) = 2 [1]. The gradients are equal [1]. B is common to both segments, so A, B and C are on one straight line [1].

*Examiner insight:* equal gradients alone are not enough. The shared point B is what turns "parallel" into "collinear", and the mark is for saying so.

**9.** At A, y = 0: 3x = 30, so **A = (10, 0)** [1]. At B, x = 0: 5y = 30, so **B = (0, 6)** [1]. AB = √(10² + 6²) [1] = √136 = **11.7 units** (3 s.f.) [1].

*Examiner insight:* keep √136 on your calculator display until the end. Rounding 11.66 to 11.7 only at the last step avoids losing the accuracy mark.

**10.** Midpoint of PQ = (1, 3) [1]. Gradient of PQ = (5 − 1) ÷ (4 − (−2)) = 4/6 = ⅔ [1]. Perpendicular gradient = −3/2 [1]. y − 3 = −3/2(x − 1) [1]. Multiply by 2 and rearrange: **3x + 2y = 9** [1].

*Examiner insight:* the bisector passes through the midpoint, not through P or Q. Using an end point instead loses most of the marks.

**11.** (a) Gradient AB = (3 − 1) ÷ (7 − 1) = ⅓ [1]. Gradient BC = (6 − 3) ÷ (6 − 7) = −3 [1]. ⅓ × (−3) = −1, so AB is perpendicular to BC and angle ABC = 90° [1].

(b) AB = √(6² + 2²) = √40 [1]. BC = √((−1)² + 3²) = √10 [1]. Area = ½ × √40 × √10 = ½ × √400 = **10 square units** [1].

(c) D = ((1 + 6) ÷ 2, (1 + 6) ÷ 2) = (3.5, 3.5) [1]. BD² = (7 − 3.5)² + (3 − 3.5)² = 12.25 + 0.25 = 12.5 [1]. AD² = (3.5 − 1)² + (3.5 − 1)² = 6.25 + 6.25 = 12.5 [1]. BD² = AD², so BD = AD [1].

*Examiner insight:* in a multi-part question, use the result you have just shown. The right angle at B in (a) is the reason the area in (b) is half the product of the two shorter sides.

**12.** (a) Gradient of l = (0 − 8) ÷ (6 − 0) = −4/3 [1]. y-intercept = 8 [1]. **y = −4/3 x + 8** [1].

(b) Gradient of m = ¾ [1]. m passes through the origin, so **y = ¾x** [1].

(c) ¾x = −4/3 x + 8 [1]. Multiply by 12: 9x = −16x + 96, so 25x = 96 and x = 3.84 [1]. y = ¾ × 3.84 = 2.88, so **P = (3.84, 2.88)** [1].

(d) OP = √(3.84² + 2.88²) [1] = √23.04 = **4.8 units** [1].

*Examiner insight:* clear fractions before solving simultaneous equations to avoid arithmetic slips. Keep x and y as exact decimals until the end, so that OP comes out as a clean 4.8.

These last two questions are structured so that each part feeds the next. In the real exam, a wrong answer in an early part can still earn follow-through marks later, provided your method is clear.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the same gradient instead of the negative reciprocal for a perpendicular line, or changing the sign without inverting the fraction.
- Making a sign error when subtracting negative coordinates in gradient, length and midpoint questions.
- Reading the gradient from 3x + 4y = 12 as 3 or 4 instead of rearranging to y = mx + c first.
- Subtracting coordinates in a different order on the numerator and denominator of the gradient fraction.
- Writing y = k for a vertical line, or x = k for a horizontal line.
- Using an end point instead of the midpoint when finding a perpendicular bisector.
- Stopping at "the gradients are equal" in a "show that" question without writing the conclusion.
- Leaving the answer as y = mx + c when the question asks for the form ax + by = c with integer values.
- Rounding too early in a length calculation, or forgetting the square root.
