## Questions

**1.** Explain how a hazard differs from a risk. **[2]**

**2.** This question is about bond breaking.

**(a)** Define the term free radical. **[1]**
**(b)** Define the term electrophile. **[1]**
**(c)** In ultraviolet light, the Cl–Cl bond breaks. State the type of bond fission and identify the products. **[2]**

**3.** Classify each reaction as addition, substitution, oxidation, reduction or polymerisation.

**(a)** CH₂=CHCH₃ + Br₂ → CH₂BrCHBrCH₃ **[1]**
**(b)** C₃H₈ + Cl₂ → C₃H₇Cl + HCl **[1]**
**(c)** n CH₂=CHCl → –(CH₂–CHCl)ₙ– **[1]**
**(d)** CH₃CH₂CH₂OH + [O] → CH₃CH₂CHO + H₂O **[1]**

**4.** This question is about naming alkanes.

**(a)** Give the IUPAC name of CH₃CH₂CH(CH₃)CH(CH₃)CH₃. **[1]**
**(b)** A student names a compound 2-ethylhexane. Explain why this name is wrong and give the correct name. **[2]**
**(c)** Draw the displayed formula of 2,2-dimethylpropane. **[1]**

**5.** This question is about structural isomers.

**(a)** State what is meant by structural isomers. **[1]**
**(b)** Name the five cycloalkanes with the molecular formula C₅H₁₀. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why no alkane has the formula C₅H₁₀. **[1]**

**6.** A refinery processes the fractions from crude oil.

**(a)** Undecane, C₁₁H₂₄, is cracked to give heptane and ethene only. Write the equation. **[1]**
**(b)** Give two reasons why refineries crack heavy fractions. **[2]**
**(c)** Pentane can be reformed into cyclopentane. Write the equation and state why reformed hydrocarbons are useful in petrol. **[2]**

**7.** Ethylcyclohexane, C₈H₁₆, is a cycloalkane that can be burned as a fuel.

**(a)** Write an equation for the complete combustion of ethylcyclohexane. **[1]**
**(b)** In a limited supply of oxygen, ethylcyclohexane burns to give carbon monoxide and water only. Write the equation. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why carbon monoxide is toxic. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain how oxides of nitrogen form when ethylcyclohexane is burned in an engine, and why they are harmful to the environment. Include an equation. **[3]**

**8.** A standby generator burns 3.42 kg of 2,2,4-trimethylpentane, C₈H₁₈, completely.

**(a)** Write the equation for the complete combustion of C₈H₁₈. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the mass of carbon dioxide produced, in kg, to three significant figures. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the volume of oxygen used, in dm³. Take the molar volume of a gas as 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹. **[2]**

**9.** A city council is comparing petrol, bioethanol and hydrogen as fuels for its buses. Discuss the extent to which each fuel can be described as carbon-neutral. Include equations for the formation and the combustion of bioethanol. **[6]**

**10.** 2-methylpropane, (CH₃)₃CH, reacts with chlorine in ultraviolet light. One product is 2-chloro-2-methylpropane, (CH₃)₃CCl.

**(a)** Write an equation for the initiation step and state what the ultraviolet light does. **[2]**
**(b)** Write the two propagation steps that form 2-chloro-2-methylpropane. **[2]**
**(c)** Write a termination step that forms an alkane with eight carbon atoms, and name that alkane. **[1]**
**(d)** A student reacts 5.80 g of 2-methylpropane and obtains 3.70 g of 2-chloro-2-methylpropane. Calculate the percentage yield. **[3]**
**(e)** Give two reasons why this reaction has limited use for making 2-chloro-2-methylpropane. **[2]**

**11.** A student cracks liquid paraffin by passing its vapour over heated aluminium oxide and collects the gas over water.

**(a)** Describe a test that shows the gas contains an alkene, giving the result. **[2]**
**(b)** The student must lift the delivery tube out of the water before stopping heating. Explain why. **[2]**
**(c)** Identify one other hazard in this experiment and suggest how the risk can be reduced. **[2]**
**(d)** One alkane in liquid paraffin is C₁₈H₃₈. Write an equation for its cracking into decane and butene only. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** A hazard is something with the potential to cause harm [1]. A risk is the likelihood that the hazard causes harm in the way the substance is used [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* An answer that only says "risk is how dangerous it is" tends to lose the second mark, because that describes the hazard; the word likelihood (or chance) carries the point.

**2. (a)** A species with an unpaired electron [1].
**(b)** A species that accepts a pair of electrons to form a new covalent bond [1].
**(c)** **Homolytic fission** [1], giving two chlorine free radicals, 2Cl• [1].
*Examiner insight:* "Accepts electrons" is too vague for an electrophile; include the word pair, because that is what makes the definition complete.

**3. (a)** **Addition** [1]
**(b)** **Substitution** [1]
**(c)** **Polymerisation** [1]
**(d)** **Oxidation** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Give one type per equation as asked; listing two types for the same equation can be read as a contradiction and lose the mark.

**4. (a)** **2,3-dimethylpentane** [1]
**(b)** The longest chain has seven carbons, running through the ethyl group, so hexane is not the parent chain [1]. The correct name is **3-methylheptane** [1].
**(c)** All atoms and bonds shown, with four CH₃ groups on a central carbon [1]:

```
         H
         |
      H--C--H
    H    |    H
    |    |    |
 H--C----C----C--H
    |    |    |
    H    |    H
      H--C--H
         |
         H
```

*Examiner insight:* A displayed formula must show every C–H bond; writing CH₃ on the diagram turns it into a structural formula and usually loses the mark.

**5. (a)** They share a molecular formula but differ in structural formula [1].
**(b)** Cyclopentane and methylcyclobutane [1]; ethylcyclopropane [1]; 1,1-dimethylcyclopropane and 1,2-dimethylcyclopropane [1].
**(c)** An alkane with five carbons has the formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂, which is C₅H₁₂ [1].
*Examiner insight:* For the isomer list, two names for the same structure (such as dimethylcyclopropane twice with no locants) count once, so always give the numbers.

**6. (a)** **C₁₁H₂₄ → C₇H₁₆ + 2C₂H₄** [1]
**(b)** Demand for short-chain fractions such as petrol is greater than the supply from distillation [1]. Cracking also produces alkenes, which are used to make polymers [1].
**(c)** **C₅H₁₂ → C₅H₁₀ + H₂** [1]. Cyclic and branched hydrocarbons burn more smoothly in petrol engines, so they promote efficient combustion [1].
*Examiner insight:* "Cracking makes more useful products" alone is unlikely to score; name the product and its use, and tie it to demand.

**7. (a)** **C₈H₁₆ + 12O₂ → 8CO₂ + 8H₂O** [1]
**(b)** **C₈H₁₆ + 8O₂ → 8CO + 8H₂O** [1]
**(c)** Carbon monoxide binds to the haemoglobin in red blood cells, more strongly than oxygen does [1]. This reduces the amount of oxygen carried to the body's cells [1].
**(d)** The engine's very high temperature makes nitrogen from the air combine with oxygen [1]: N₂ + O₂ → 2NO [1]. Oxides of nitrogen are acidic; NO₂ dissolves in rain to form nitric acid, causing acid rain [1].
*Examiner insight:* For part (d), answers that say the nitrogen comes from the fuel lose credit; the nitrogen is from air, and the high temperature is the condition you must state.

**8. (a)** **2C₈H₁₈ + 25O₂ → 16CO₂ + 18H₂O** [1]
**(b)** Mᵣ of C₈H₁₈ = 114.0; amount = 3420 ÷ 114.0 = 30.0 mol [1]. Each mole gives 8 mol CO₂, so 240 mol CO₂ [1]. Mass = 240 × 44.0 = 10 560 g = **10.6 kg** [1].
**(c)** O₂ needed = 30.0 × 12.5 = 375 mol [1]. Volume = 375 × 24.0 = **9000 dm³** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Convert kg to g before dividing by Mᵣ; using 3.42 instead of 3420 gives an answer a thousand times too small, and clear working gives you the best chance of credit for the steps that are right.

**9.** Petrol is not carbon-neutral: it releases carbon that was stored underground for millions of years, adding to atmospheric CO₂ [1]. Bioethanol: plants take in CO₂ by photosynthesis, 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ [1]. Fermentation, C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂, and combustion, C₂H₅OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O, release CO₂ [1]. Six moles of CO₂ are taken in and six released for each mole of glucose, so in theory there is no net release [1]. In practice, energy for farming, fertiliser, distillation and transport often comes from fossil fuels, so bioethanol is not fully carbon-neutral [1]. Hydrogen produces only water when it burns, but it is carbon-neutral only if it is made without releasing CO₂, for example by electrolysis using renewable electricity, not from methane [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A discussion question rewards a judgement on each fuel; three descriptions with no conclusion about how carbon-neutral each one is tend to score well below the full mark.

**10. (a)** **Cl₂ → 2Cl•** [1]. The UV supplies enough energy for homolytic fission of the Cl–Cl bond [1].
**(b)** Cl• + (CH₃)₃CH → (CH₃)₃C• + HCl [1]; (CH₃)₃C• + Cl₂ → (CH₃)₃CCl + Cl• [1].
**(c)** 2(CH₃)₃C• → (CH₃)₃CC(CH₃)₃, **2,2,3,3-tetramethylbutane** [1].
**(d)** Amount of 2-methylpropane = 5.80 ÷ 58.0 = 0.100 mol [1]. Theoretical mass of C₄H₉Cl = 0.100 × 92.5 = 9.25 g [1]. Percentage yield = 3.70 ÷ 9.25 × 100 = **40.0%** [1].
**(e)** Further substitution gives dichloro and more highly substituted products [1]. Substitution at a CH₃ carbon gives the isomer 1-chloro-2-methylpropane, so a mixture forms that must be separated [1].
*Examiner insight:* In propagation, the radical dot must sit on the carbon, (CH₃)₃C•, and the first step must form HCl, not H•; a step that makes a hydrogen radical is wrong chemistry, so it cannot gain credit.

**11. (a)** Shake the gas with bromine water [1]. The colour changes from orange to colourless [1].
**(b)** If heating stops while the tube is in the water, the gas in the apparatus cools and contracts [1]. Cold water is sucked back into the hot boiling tube, which can crack it [1].
**(c)** The alkene products are flammable [1], so keep the collected gas away from naked flames and work on a small scale [1].
**(d)** **C₁₈H₃₈ → C₁₀H₂₂ + 2C₄H₈** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Write colourless rather than "clear", because a clear solution can still be coloured; the full result is orange (or brown) to colourless.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing risk as the dangerous property of a substance instead of the likelihood of harm.
- Defining an electrophile as an "electron acceptor" without the word pair.
- Naming an alkane from the row as drawn when a longer chain runs through a branch.
- Writing CH₃ inside a "displayed" formula.
- Listing two cycloalkanes that are really the same structure because no locants were given.
- Unbalanced cracking equations, often from forgetting how many alkene molecules are needed.
- Linking CO to "less oxygen in the air" rather than to haemoglobin.
- Saying oxides of nitrogen come from the fuel.
- Calling bioethanol or hydrogen carbon-neutral with no condition attached.
- Dividing a mass in kg by Mᵣ without converting to grams.
