## What 1.3.2 asks you to know

| Strand | Specification wording |
|---|---|
| Concept and calculation | revenue; fixed and variable costs; total costs; profit and loss; interest; break even level of output; margin of safety |
| Interpretation of break even diagrams | the impact of changes in revenue and costs; break even level of output; margin of safety; profit and loss |

## The Appendix 3 formulae used here

- Revenue = price × quantity
- TC = TFC + TVC
- Interest (on loans) in % = (total repayment − borrowed amount) ÷ borrowed amount × 100
- Break even point in units = fixed cost ÷ (sales price − variable cost)
- Break even point in costs/revenue = break even point in units × sales price
- Margin of safety = actual or budgeted sales − break even sales

Profit or loss = revenue − total costs is the standard form.

## Part 1: classifying awkward costs

Most costs are easy to sort. The ones that catch students out depend on how they are paid, so read the case carefully.

| Cost | Fixed or variable? | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Staff on a monthly salary | Fixed | Paid the same whatever the output |
| Staff paid per item made | Variable | Rises with each item |
| Electricity for machines running only when producing | Variable (mostly) | Rises with output |
| Rent for a unit | Fixed | Same each month |
| Delivery charge per parcel | Variable | One charge per sale |
| Loan interest payments | Fixed | Due whatever the sales |
| Card payment fee per sale | Variable | Charged on each transaction |

Fixed costs are fixed only in the short term and within a range of output. If a business doubles in size and needs a second unit, rent rises.

## Part 2: a full worked example

### Puffin Rock Kayak Tours

Puffin Rock charges £35 a person for a guided tour. Variable cost is £8 a person (insurance per head, snacks, wetsuit cleaning). Fixed costs are £10,800 for the season (boat storage, guides' salaries, the website). It budgets for 600 customers.

| Step | Working | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | £35 × 600 | £21,000 |
| Total variable costs | £8 × 600 | £4,800 |
| Total costs | £10,800 + £4,800 | £15,600 |
| Profit | £21,000 − £15,600 | £5,400 |
| Contribution per customer | £35 − £8 | £27 |
| Break even (customers) | £10,800 ÷ £27 | 400 customers |
| Break even revenue | 400 × £35 | £14,000 |
| Margin of safety (customers) | 600 − 400 | 200 customers |
| Margin of safety (revenue) | £21,000 − £14,000 | £7,000 |

The margin of safety can be given in units or in pounds, because the formula uses "sales", which can mean either. Read which the question asks for. As a standard extra, 200 ÷ 600 × 100 = 33.3% of budgeted sales could be lost before Puffin Rock makes a loss.

A quick check: profit = margin of safety in units × contribution. 200 × £27 = £5,400, which matches. Every customer beyond break even adds £27 of profit.

## Part 3: interest forwards and backwards

**Forwards (the Appendix 3 formula).** Puffin Rock borrows £12,000 for a new boat and repays £14,160 in total. Interest = (£14,160 − £12,000) ÷ £12,000 × 100 = £2,160 ÷ £12,000 × 100 = **18%**.

**Backwards.** If you are told the interest rate and the loan, rearrange: total repayment = borrowed amount × (1 + rate ÷ 100). £12,000 × 1.18 = £14,160. Over 24 equal monthly payments, each is £14,160 ÷ 24 = **£590**.

The interest itself (£2,160) is a cost. Monthly loan repayments are usually treated as a fixed cost, so borrowing raises the break even level of output.

## Part 4: working backwards from break even

Some questions give the break even output and ask for a missing figure. Rearrange the formula.

- Fixed costs = break even units × (price − variable cost)
- Contribution per unit = fixed costs ÷ break even units

**Example.** A stall breaks even at 250 bags. It sells each bag for £20 and the variable cost is £12. Fixed costs = 250 × (£20 − £12) = 250 × £8 = **£2,000**.

## Part 5: two changes at once

Questions may change more than one figure. Work out the new contribution first.

Puffin Rock raises its price to £40 and adds a hot drink, so variable cost rises to £10. Fixed costs stay at £10,800.

- New contribution = £40 − £10 = £30
- New break even = £10,800 ÷ £30 = **360 customers** (down from 400)
- If 600 customers still book: profit = (£40 × 600) − (£10,800 + £10 × 600) = £24,000 − £16,800 = **£7,200**
- New margin of safety = 600 − 360 = **240 customers**

Both changes moved the lines on a break even diagram: the revenue line is steeper (higher price) and the total costs line is also steeper (higher variable cost). Because contribution rose overall from £27 to £30, break even fell. The key risk is the assumption that 600 people still book at the higher price.

## Part 6: interpreting a break even diagram without drawing it

You may be given a diagram and asked to read it. Use these rules.

- Break even output: read **down** from where total revenue and total costs cross.
- Profit or loss at an output: the **vertical** gap between the revenue and total costs lines at that output.
- Margin of safety: the **horizontal** distance from break even output to actual or budgeted output.
- Fixed costs: the height where the total costs line meets the vertical axis.
- Price: revenue at any output ÷ that output.

## Annotated model answer

**Question (Analyse):** Analyse the impact on Puffin Rock Kayak Tours of raising its price to £40 while adding a hot drink. **[6]**

> Contribution per customer rises from £27 to £30, because the £5 price rise is bigger than the £2 rise in variable cost (calculation in context). Break even falls from 400 to 360 customers, so the business needs fewer bookings to cover its £10,800 of fixed costs (consequence). If 600 customers still book, profit rises from £5,400 to £7,200 and the margin of safety widens to 240 customers (development). However, the higher price may put some customers off (counter-point). If bookings fell below 540, profit would be lower than before (development: 540 × £30 − £10,800 = £5,400). The change is worth making only if Puffin Rock's customers value the drink enough to accept the price (conclusion).

## Common mistakes

- Treating salaried wages as variable, or piece-rate wages as fixed.
- Giving margin of safety in units when pounds were asked for, or the other way round.
- Dividing interest by the total repayment.
- Rounding break even down; round up, because part-units cannot be sold.
- Changing price and forgetting that a variable cost change also moves the total costs line.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Calculate** and **Complete the table:** show every step with units.
- **Identify:** read values from a diagram or table.
- **Analyse:** give the new figures, then the effect on risk and profit.
- **Justify:** compare two plans using profit, break even and margin of safety.
