These notes condense **2.10 Market failure: asymmetric information**, which is **HL only** in the IB Diploma Programme *Economics guide* (first assessment 2022). For full explanations read the [2.10 study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-economics-dp-economics-ib-dp-economics-2-10/); then test yourself with the [2.10 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-economics-dp-economics-ib-dp-economics-2-10-practice/). Places and figures are invented.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Asymmetric information | One party to a transaction has more relevant information than the other |
| Adverse selection | Hidden information about quality or risk **before** a transaction drives out better-quality goods or lower-risk buyers |
| Moral hazard | A party changes its behaviour **after** a transaction because it no longer bears the full cost, and the other party cannot observe this |
| Signalling | The informed party takes a costly action that reveals its quality |
| Screening | The uninformed party designs a test or choice that makes the informed party reveal information |
| Licensing | A legal requirement to meet standards before supplying a service |
| Mandatory disclosure | A legal duty to reveal relevant information to the other party |
| Excess | The part of a loss the insured person pays themselves |

## Adverse selection vs moral hazard

| | Adverse selection | Moral hazard |
|---|---|---|
| Timing | Before | After |
| Hidden | Characteristics | Actions |
| Insurance case | Unhealthy people more likely to buy | Insured people take less care |
| Jobs case | Employer cannot see ability when hiring | Employee shirks when effort is unobserved |
| Credit case | Riskier borrowers accept high interest rates | Borrower takes bigger risks with the loan |

## Method: the unravelling chain

1. The uninformed side cannot tell types apart, so charges one average price.
2. Calculate the average: weight each type's cost by its share.
3. Compare each type's maximum willingness to pay with that price.
4. The better types leave; the average worsens; the price rises.
5. Count the welfare lost: the gains the departing types would have had.

**Worked reminder.** 70 careful drivers (expected claim 300 tals) and 30 risky drivers (expected claim 1,300 tals). Average premium = (70 × 300 + 30 × 1,300) / 100 = (21,000 + 39,000) / 100 = **600 tals**. If careful drivers will pay at most 450, they leave, the premium rises to 1,300, and only risky drivers stay insured.

## Method: moral hazard with an excess

Expected cost to the insured = probability of loss × amount the insured pays + cost of taking care (if any). Compare careful and careless choices.

**Worked reminder.** Loss 2,000; probability 3% with care, 10% without; care costs 30. Excess of 500: careless 0.10 × 500 = 50; careful 30 + 0.03 × 500 = 45. Care is cheaper, so the excess removes the moral hazard. With no excess, careless costs 0 and careful costs 30, so care is not taken.

## Responses at a glance

| Response | Type | Targets | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Qualifications, guarantees | Private: signalling | Adverse selection | Costly; must be hard to fake |
| Questionnaires, probation, contract menus | Private: screening | Adverse selection | Costly; some priced out |
| Excess, co-payments, performance pay | Private: contract design | Moral hazard | Shifts risk back to the individual |
| Licensing, minimum standards | Government: regulation | Adverse selection | Enforcement cost; restricts entry |
| Disclosure laws | Government: legislation | Adverse selection | Hard to enforce; complex fine print |
| Compulsory insurance | Government: legislation | Adverse selection | Low risks subsidise high risks |
| Ratings, inspection reports, labels | Government: information | Adverse selection | May be ignored or out of date |

## Building an evaluation paragraph

1. Name the problem (adverse selection or moral hazard) and the hidden element.
2. Name the response and explain how it changes incentives or information.
3. Give one limitation linked to cost, enforcement or fairness.
4. Judge: the response suits this market if information is cheap to verify, or if pooling risk is accepted as fair.

## Exam traps

- Asymmetric means **one side knows more**, not "nobody knows".
- Adverse selection is about **who** enters the deal; moral hazard is about **what they do** after.
- Say which problem a response solves.
- Compulsory insurance raises an equity point as well as efficiency.
- 2.10 is HL only: keep it out of SL answers.

## Quick self-test

1. Define asymmetric information.
2. Is a borrower taking a riskier investment after receiving a loan adverse selection or moral hazard?
3. Why might only lower-quality used cars be offered for sale?
4. Fifty low-risk people (expected cost 400) and fifty high-risk people (expected cost 1,200) seek cover. Calculate the pooled premium.
5. In question 4, low-risk people will pay at most 550. What happens?
6. Give one example of signalling in a labour market.
7. Explain why a signal must be hard to copy.
8. Give an example of screening by an insurer.
9. How does an excess reduce moral hazard?
10. Give one strength and one limitation of licensing.
11. Why does compulsory insurance stop adverse selection?
12. Name one government response based on providing information.

### Answers

1. One party to a transaction has more relevant information than the other.
2. Moral hazard: it is an action after the deal.
3. Buyers pay only an average price, so owners of good cars withdraw them, leaving lower-quality cars (adverse selection).
4. (50 × 400 + 50 × 1,200) / 100 = **800**.
5. 550 is below 800, so low-risk people do not buy; the premium rises towards 1,200 and only high-risk people stay insured.
6. Earning a qualification that is easier for high-ability workers to obtain.
7. If low-quality types can copy it cheaply, it no longer separates the types, so it reveals nothing.
8. A medical questionnaire, or a menu of policies with different excesses.
9. The insured person bears part of any loss, so taking care becomes worthwhile.
10. Strength: sets enforceable minimum standards. Limitation: enforcement costs or restricted entry that raises prices.
11. Low-risk people cannot leave the pool, so the average risk and the premium do not spiral upwards.
12. Publishing restaurant hygiene ratings (or school inspection reports, or energy labels).
