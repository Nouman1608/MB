They cover Topic 3.5 Energy from the Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026, Version 2 (published November 2022). The topic is examined in Paper 1 Geographical Themes, and the syllabus lists energy among the things graphs in Paper 2 Geographical Skills may show. When you are ready, test yourself with the [energy practice questions](/resources/igcse-geography-0460-energy-practice/). ## What the syllabus asks

- **Describe the importance** of non-renewable fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas), renewable supplies (geothermal, wind, HEP, wave and tidal, solar, biofuels), nuclear power and fuelwood -- globally and in countries at different levels of development.
- **Evaluate** the benefits and disadvantages of nuclear power and renewable sources.
- **Case study:** energy supply in a country or area.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning in one line |
|---|---|
| Non-renewable | A stock that runs out: coal, oil, gas, uranium |
| Renewable | A flow that is naturally replaced: sun, wind, water, Earth's heat |
| Fossil fuel | Coal, oil or gas formed from ancient plant and animal remains |
| Fuelwood | Wood or charcoal burned for cooking and heating |
| Nuclear power | Electricity from the heat of splitting uranium atoms |
| HEP | Electricity from water flowing through turbines, usually below a dam |
| Geothermal | Heat from hot rocks underground, used for steam or direct heating |
| Biofuel | Fuel from plant or animal material, such as sugarcane ethanol |
| Energy mix | The share of energy from each source in one country |
| Intermittent | Not available all the time (solar at night, wind on calm days) |
| Energy security | A reliable, affordable supply not dependent on one source or supplier |

## Global picture in four numbers

- About **80%** of world energy consumption came from fossil fuels (2022).
- Nuclear supplied about **4.3%** of world primary energy (2020).
- Hydro, wind and solar together generated about **29%** of world electricity in 2024 (hydro 14.4%, wind 8.1%, solar 6.9%).
- About **2.1 billion** people cook with polluting fuels such as wood, dung, crop waste, kerosene or coal (WHO).

## Sources at a glance

| Source | Needs or suits | One benefit | One disadvantage | Real example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Coal | Large reserves; power stations | Cheap, reliable | Most carbon dioxide per unit of energy | Main electricity fuel worldwide (34.4% in 2024) |
| Oil | Transport and chemicals | Easy to store and move | Price shocks for importers | 1973 oil crisis pushed France into nuclear |
| Natural gas | Pipelines or shipping | Less carbon dioxide than coal | Still a fossil fuel; supply can be cut | -- |
| Fuelwood | Rural areas with no grid | Free to collect | Indoor smoke; deforestation | WHO: 2.9 million deaths linked to household air pollution (2021) |
| Nuclear | Money, skills, cooling water | Large, steady, low-carbon | Waste; accident risk; cost | France, about 72% of electricity (2018) |
| Geothermal | Plate boundaries, volcanic areas | Steady day and night | Few suitable places; gases | Hellisheiði, Iceland, 303 MW; Olkaria, Kenya |
| Wind | Strong, steady winds; coasts, uplands, offshore | No fuel cost | Intermittent; visual impact | -- |
| HEP | High rainfall, steady flow, deep narrow valley | Large, controllable output | Floods land; displaces people | Three Gorges, China, about 22,500 MW |
| Wave | Exposed coasts | Strong resource | Mostly at trial stage | -- |
| Tidal | Large tidal range, estuary | Predictable | Few sites; habitat change | La Rance, France, 240 MW, 1966 |
| Solar | Sunny, low-latitude areas | Works off-grid on single homes | No output at night | -- |
| Biofuel | Land for crops such as sugarcane | Replaces petrol | Competes with food crops | Brazil, ethanol programme from 1975 |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Renewable vs low-carbon.** Nuclear is low-carbon but not renewable. Biofuels are renewable but release carbon dioxide when burned.
- **Energy vs electricity.** A country can make nearly all its electricity from renewables and still import oil for transport (Iceland).
- **Intermittent vs controllable.** Wind and solar depend on the weather; HEP, geothermal and nuclear can run steadily.
- **Predictable vs reliable.** Tides are predictable to the minute, but output still stops for a while at each slack water around high and low tide.
- **Describe vs evaluate.** Describe importance = what it supplies, where and for whom. Evaluate (the syllabus wording) = weigh benefits against disadvantages and reach a judgement; in the exam this often appears as "To what extent ...?" or "How far do you agree ...?".

## Levels of development in one table

| | Low income | Middle income | High income |
|---|---|---|---|
| Energy per person | Low | Rising fast | High |
| Typical main sources | Fuelwood, some oil, HEP | Coal and oil, growing renewables | Oil, gas, nuclear, renewables |
| Main problem | No grid; smoke; deforestation | Coal pollution; demand growth | Cutting emissions |

In 2018 the USA used 4.4 tonnes of oil equivalent per person and India 0.4, against a world average of 1.2.

## Case study card: Iceland

- On the Mid-Atlantic Ridge (constructive boundary) -> geothermal heat; high rainfall and glaciers -> HEP.
- 2016: about 85% of primary energy from domestic renewables (geothermal about 65%, hydro 20%); most of the rest imported fossil fuel.
- 2025: electricity 70.5% hydro, 29.4% geothermal.
- About 90% of homes heated by geothermal energy.
- Hellisheiði geothermal plant: 303 MW; gases reinjected underground since 2014 to cut hydrogen sulphide.
- Kárahnjúkar HEP: 690 MW, 193 m dam, built to power Alcoa's Fjarðaál aluminium smelter; protests over flooded highland.
- 2005: 72% of electricity used by power-intensive industry.
- Still imports oil for vehicles, ships and aircraft.

## Mini essay plan

**Question type:** "To what extent is renewable energy a better choice than nuclear power for reducing a country's use of fossil fuels?"

1. **Define and frame.** Both are low-carbon; only renewables are renewable. The answer depends on the country's physical geography and wealth.
2. **For renewables.** No fuel cost; no radioactive waste; can be small and local (solar in villages). Example: Iceland's hydro and geothermal.
3. **Against renewables.** Wind and solar are intermittent; HEP and tidal floods or alter land and estuaries. Example: Three Gorges displacement.
4. **For nuclear.** Large, steady supply. Example: France's reactors after the 1973 oil crisis.
5. **Against nuclear.** Cost, waste and accident risk (Chernobyl 1986, Fukushima 2011); rarely possible in low-income countries.
6. **Judgement.** Renewables are the better choice where conditions suit them and for poorer countries; nuclear can provide steady base load in wealthy countries. Many countries use both.

## Model evaluation paragraph

"Tidal power is predictable because tides follow a known daily pattern (knowledge). La Rance in Brittany has generated electricity since 1966 from a tidal range of about 8 metres, with a capacity of 240 MW (application). However, the barrage has caused silting of the estuary, and few coasts have a large enough tidal range, so tidal power can only ever supply a small share of most countries' needs (analysis). It is a reliable low-carbon source in the right place, but not a national solution for most countries (evaluation)."

Use the same four steps for any source: what it is, a real place, a consequence, then a judgement that says *where* or *for whom* it works.

## Data skills to rehearse

- **Percentages.** Share of a source = source ÷ total × 100. Check that the shares in a table add up to the total before you start.
- **Percentage change.** (new − old) ÷ old × 100. Say whether it is an increase or a decrease.
- **Capacity vs output.** Capacity is in MW (power). Output over time is in MWh, GWh or TWh (energy). Output = capacity × hours × the share of time it runs.
- **Describing a graph.** Give the overall trend, the highest and lowest values, any anomaly, and quote figures with units.
- **Divided bars and pie graphs.** Compare the biggest segment in each country first, then the most surprising difference.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the three fossil fuels in the syllabus.
2. Why is nuclear power non-renewable?
3. State two physical conditions for a good HEP site.
4. What does "intermittent" mean? Give an example.
5. Which renewable source depends on plate boundaries?
6. Give two problems caused by relying on fuelwood.
7. State one benefit and one disadvantage of tidal power.
8. Why do biofuels raise food-supply concerns?
9. In which year did La Rance open?
10. What share of Iceland's homes are heated by geothermal energy?
11. Why does energy use per person rise with income?

### Answers

1. Coal, oil and natural gas.
2. It uses uranium, a mineral stock that runs out.
3. High, reliable rainfall or river flow; a deep, narrow valley to dam (also impermeable rock).
4. Not available all the time, such as solar at night.
5. Geothermal.
6. Health damage from indoor smoke; deforestation and soil erosion; time spent collecting wood.
7. Benefit: tides are predictable. Disadvantage: few suitable sites, and barrages change estuary habitats.
8. Land used for fuel crops such as sugarcane could grow food instead.
9. 1966.
10. About 90%.
11. Richer people own more cars and appliances, heat or cool their homes more, and their economies have more industry and services.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Listing renewables with no conditions or examples, when the question asks to describe importance.
- Calling nuclear power renewable.
- Leaving out fuelwood, which the syllabus names separately.
- "Evaluate" answers that only list advantages, or never reach a judgement.
- Stating that renewables have no environmental impact; HEP, tidal and biofuels all do.
- Confusing a country's electricity with its total energy use.
- Generic case-study answers with no named plants, figures or physical reasons.
- Ignoring the "different levels of development" part of a question.
- Misreading graph units, such as reading MW (capacity) as if it were energy produced.
