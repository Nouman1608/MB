## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** State the five fundamental principles of professional ethics. **[5]**

**2.** Describe the principle of confidentiality. **[2]**

**3.** For each situation, identify the principle of professional ethics that is mainly at risk.

**(a)** A bookkeeper is asked to prepare a partnership appropriation account but has never studied partnerships. **[1]**
**(b)** An accountant claims on a website to hold a qualification she has not passed. **[1]**
**(c)** A sales ledger clerk is told by the owner to delete an invoice so that a favoured customer owes nothing. **[1]**
**(d)** An auditor is asked to check the accounts of a business owned by his uncle. **[1]**

**4.** Explain what is meant by the public interest in accounting. **[2]**

**5.** Give two groups outside a business who rely on its financial statements, and state one decision each group might make using them. **[4]**

**6.** Distinguish between the principles of integrity and objectivity. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** Amira Haddad is the payroll clerk at Brookvale Joinery. A colleague asks her how much the new workshop manager is paid. Later, Amira leaves Brookvale to work for a rival firm, whose owner asks her for a list of Brookvale's customers.

**(a)** Identify the principle at risk in both requests. **[1]**
**(b)** Apply the principle to each request and state what Amira should do. **[4]**

**8.** Joel Marsh is the accounts assistant at Kestrel Office Supplies. The owner tells him that his annual bonus will be 2% of the profit for the year. At the year end Joel must decide whether to make a provision for irrecoverable debts of 1,800.

**(a)** Calculate how much Joel's bonus would rise if he left out the provision. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the bonus is a threat to Joel's objectivity. **[2]**
**(c)** State one safeguard the business could use. **[1]**

**9.** Fern Okafor works as a bookkeeper for several small businesses. Explain how professional competence and due care applies to each of the following.

**(a)** A new accounting software package is introduced by one client. **[2]**
**(b)** Fern has too many clients to finish all their bank reconciliations by the deadline. **[2]**

**10.** Halden Garden Centre is applying to a bank for a loan of 40,000. The owner asks the accountant, Sam Petrov, to show a delivery van that the business has sold as still owned, so that non-current assets look larger.

**(a)** Identify and explain two principles that Sam would breach if he agreed. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain how the public interest applies to Sam's decision. **[3]**
**(c)** State what Sam should do. **[1]**

**11.** Explain how the public interest applies to each of the following accounting roles.

**(a)** A payroll clerk who calculates deductions from employees' pay. **[2]**
**(b)** An auditor who reports on a company's financial statements. **[2]**
**(c)** An adviser who prepares a client's tax figures. **[2]**

**12.** Nadia Clarke is the accountant for Marlow Bakehouse. The owner has instructed her to keep the business's records confidential. Nadia discovers that the owner has been recording private household bills as business expenses for three years, reducing the tax the business pays.

Evaluate whether Nadia's duty of confidentiality to her employer should prevent her from acting on what she has found. Refer to the principles of professional ethics and the public interest, and reach a conclusion. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Integrity [1]. Objectivity [1]. Professional competence and due care [1]. Confidentiality [1]. Professional behaviour [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* "Competence and due care" is one principle; writing "competence" and "due care" as two separate answers still leaves one principle missing.

**2.** Information gained through professional work must not be disclosed without proper authority [1], and must not be used for the personal advantage of the accountant or anyone else [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Both halves are needed: not telling, and not using.

**3.** (a) Professional competence and due care [1]. (b) Professional behaviour (integrity is also acceptable, as the claim is false) [1]. (c) Integrity [1]. (d) Objectivity [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* In (d) nothing has gone wrong yet; the family link is the threat to judgement.

**4.** The wellbeing of all the people and organisations that rely on accounting information [1], not only the employer or client who pays the accountant, so information must be honest and accurate for them too [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Any two groups, one mark for the group and one for a linked decision. Lenders (bank) [1], deciding whether to lend or at what interest rate [1]. Suppliers [1], deciding whether to give credit and how much [1]. Also acceptable: tax authorities assessing tax due; investors deciding whether to invest; employees judging job security. **[4]**

**6.** Integrity is about honesty: the accountant must not be associated with false or misleading information [1]. Objectivity is about judgement: decisions must not be influenced by bias, conflicts of interest or pressure from others [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Confidentiality [1].
**(b)** Colleague's request: pay details were learned through her payroll job [1], so she must not disclose them and should politely refuse [1]. Rival's request: the duty of confidentiality continues after she leaves an employer [1], so she must not hand over Brookvale's customer list, which would also give the rival an unfair advantage, and should refuse [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* The second request tests whether you know the duty continues after a job ends; say so directly.

**8. (a)** Leaving out the provision raises profit by 1,800 [1]. Bonus rises by 1,800 × 2% = **36** [1].
**(b)** Joel gains personally from a higher reported profit [1], so he may be tempted to leave out or reduce the provision, letting self-interest override his professional judgement [1].
**(c)** Any one: the owner or another person reviews and approves the provision; the bonus is not based on profit; the provision is set using a fixed policy such as a percentage of trade receivables [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* The bonus is small, but the threat exists whatever its size. Objectivity is about the risk of bias, not the amount.

**9. (a)** Fern must have the skill to use the software correctly [1], so she should train or seek help before entering the client's transactions, otherwise errors could be posted [1].
**(b)** Due care means working thoroughly and on time [1]; she should not rush and skip checks, but should tell the clients, prioritise, or take on fewer clients so each reconciliation is done properly [1]. **[4]**

**10. (a)** Integrity [1]: showing a sold van as an asset makes the statement of financial position false and misleading [1]. Objectivity [1]: Sam would be letting the owner's pressure override his own judgement about which assets exist [1]. (Professional behaviour is an acceptable alternative for one principle.)
**(b)** The bank relies on the statements to decide whether to lend 40,000 [1]. Overstated assets would make the business look a safer borrower than it is [1], so the bank could lend money it may not get back, and other creditors could also be misled [1].
**(c)** Refuse to include the van, prepare accurate statements and explain his reasons to the owner [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the public interest to the named outsider in the scenario, here the bank, rather than to "the public" in general.

**11. (a)** Deductions are owed to the tax authority and others [1], so they must be calculated correctly and paid over, protecting the authority and the employees whose records depend on them [1].
**(b)** Shareholders, lenders and others outside the company rely on the auditor's report [1], so the auditor must report honestly, even if the directors disagree [1].
**(c)** The tax authority and other taxpayers rely on accurate figures [1], so the adviser must not understate income or help the client hide it [1]. **[6]**

**12.** Up to 6 marks for a balanced, applied evaluation:
- Confidentiality normally means Nadia should not disclose the business's information [1].
- But confidentiality does not require an accountant to take part in, or conceal, wrongdoing; it can be overridden where the law requires disclosure [1].
- Integrity: if she continues to prepare accounts that include private bills as expenses, she is associated with false records [1].
- Business entity: the private bills are drawings, so profit and tax are understated [1].
- Public interest: the tax authority and other taxpayers are harmed, so her duty is wider than her employer [1].
- Conclusion: confidentiality should not stop her acting; she should raise the matter with the owner, correct the records so the private bills are treated as drawings and, if the owner refuses, seek professional advice on her obligations [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* The top mark needs a conclusion that follows from the arguments. Explaining both sides without deciding leaves the evaluation unfinished.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Listing principles without linking them to the facts in the scenario.
- Leaving out what the accountant should do.
- Treating "competence" and "due care" as separate principles.
- Saying confidentiality ends when an employee leaves.
- Using "the public interest" to mean what is popular or newsworthy.
- Writing an evaluation with no conclusion.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I answer an "apply" question on professional ethics?**
Pick out the fact that creates the risk, name the principle, explain why it is at risk and who could be harmed, then state what the accountant should do.

**Does confidentiality continue after an accountant leaves a job?**
Yes. Information learned in one job must not be disclosed or used for advantage after the accountant moves to another employer.
