This guide goes deeper into section **4.4.1 Photosynthesis** of the AQA GCSE Biology (8461) specification, for
first teaching from 2016: 4.4.1.1 Photosynthetic reaction, 4.4.1.2 Rate of photosynthesis and 4.4.1.3 Uses of

specification marks (HT only) is labelled **Higher tier only**.

The [Bioenergetics study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-biology-8461-bioenergetics/) introduces the equation, the
four factors and the practical. This page concentrates on the skills examiners test with them: reading data,
deciding which factor is limiting, analysing the practical and handling the calculations. Condense it with the
[Photosynthesis revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-biology-8461-photosynthesis-aqa-gcse-biology-revision-notes/)
and test yourself with the [Photosynthesis practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-biology-8461-photosynthesis-aqa-gcse-biology-practice/).

## The reaction: what each part means

```
                        light
carbon dioxide + water  ------>  glucose + oxygen
     CO₂          H₂O             C₆H₁₂O₆     O₂
```

- **Where:** chloroplasts in plant cells and in algae; chlorophyll absorbs the light.
- **Energy:** the reaction is **endothermic**. Energy is transferred **from the environment to the chloroplasts
  by light**, and is stored in the glucose.
- **Raw materials:** carbon dioxide enters leaves by diffusion through the stomata; water comes from the roots
  through the xylem.
- **Products:** oxygen diffuses out of the leaf or is used in respiration; glucose is used or converted.

## Thinking about rate as a supply chain

The rate of photosynthesis can only be as fast as its slowest input allows. Each of the four factors supplies
something different:

| Factor | What it supplies | What happens when it is too low |
|---|---|---|
| Light intensity | Energy for the reaction | Too little energy transferred; rate falls |
| Carbon dioxide concentration | A reactant | Too little reactant; rate falls |
| Temperature | Kinetic energy for enzyme-controlled reactions | Reactions slow down |
| Amount of chlorophyll | Absorption of light | Less light absorbed even if light is bright |

Temperature is the odd one out because it has an **optimum**. Above it, the enzymes controlling photosynthesis
are **denatured**, so the rate falls steeply, whereas extra light or CO₂ beyond the limiting point simply has no
further effect.

## Reading one-factor data

**Worked example 1** (invented data). A student measures the rate of oxygen production by an alga at
different CO₂ concentrations, keeping light and temperature constant.

| CO₂ concentration (%) | 0.00 | 0.02 | 0.04 | 0.06 | 0.08 | 0.10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rate (cm³ O₂ per hour) | 0 | 3.0 | 6.0 | 8.5 | 9.0 | 9.0 |

- **Describe:** the rate increases from 0 to 9.0 cm³ per hour as CO₂ rises from 0 to 0.08%, then stays at 9.0.
- **Explain:** up to about 0.06%, CO₂ is the limiting factor, because increasing it increases the rate. At 0.08%
  and above, raising CO₂ has no effect, so another factor (light intensity, temperature or chlorophyll) is
  limiting.
- Note the rate at 0% CO₂ is zero: with no reactant, no photosynthesis occurs.

When you plot this, CO₂ concentration is the independent variable and goes on the x-axis. Choose a scale that
uses at least half the grid and join the points with a smooth curve.

## Interacting factors (Higher tier only)

**Worked example 2** (invented data). Rate of photosynthesis against light intensity at two temperatures, with
CO₂ high and constant.

| Light intensity (arbitrary units) | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rate at 15 °C | 8 | 16 | 20 | 21 | 21 | 21 |
| Rate at 25 °C | 8 | 16 | 24 | 30 | 34 | 34 |

- At light intensity 1 and 2, the two rows are identical. Changing temperature makes no difference, and raising
  light raises the rate, so **light intensity is limiting**.
- At light intensity 5 at 15 °C, the rate has levelled off, but the 25 °C rate is higher. Raising the temperature
  would raise the rate, so **temperature is limiting** there.
- At light intensity 6 at 25 °C, the rate has levelled again. The data cannot tell you which factor limits it;
  you could suggest CO₂ concentration or chlorophyll, and say that it would need testing.

The test is always the same: **if increasing a factor would increase the rate at that point, that factor is
limiting.**

## Required practical 6: analysing it well

The practical investigates the effect of light intensity on the rate of photosynthesis using an aquatic
organism such as pondweed. Most marks come from understanding the design, not from recalling the steps.

| Feature | Reason |
|---|---|
| Independent variable: distance of lamp (light intensity) | The factor being investigated |
| Dependent variable: volume of oxygen, or bubbles, per unit time | Measures the rate |
| Control: temperature (heat shield, thermometer) | The lamp heats the water; temperature also affects rate |
| Control: CO₂ (same sodium hydrogencarbonate solution) | CO₂ also affects rate |
| Control: same piece of pondweed | Different pieces have different amounts of chlorophyll |
| Leave to adjust at each distance | The rate takes time to settle at a new light intensity |
| Repeat readings and calculate a mean | Makes results more repeatable and helps spot anomalies |

**Improving accuracy.** Counting bubbles is less accurate than collecting gas, because bubbles vary in size.
Collecting the gas in a gas syringe or measuring cylinder measures volume directly.

**Worked example 3.** A student collects 4.2 cm³ of oxygen in 3 minutes, then repeats and collects 3.9 cm³ and
4.5 cm³ in 3 minutes.

```
mean volume = (4.2 + 3.9 + 4.5) ÷ 3 = 12.6 ÷ 3 = 4.2 cm³
rate        = 4.2 ÷ 3 = 1.4 cm³ per minute
```

## The inverse square law (Higher tier only)

```
light intensity ∝ 1 / distance²
```

**Worked example 4.** A lamp is moved from 5 cm to 15 cm from the pondweed.

```
distance × 3  ->  light intensity × 1/3² = × 1/9
```

To plot relative light intensity, calculate 1/d² for each distance:

| Distance d (cm) | 10 | 20 | 25 | 40 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1/d² (cm⁻²) | 0.0100 | 0.0025 | 0.0016 | 0.000625 |

A graph of rate against 1/d² is a graph of rate against light intensity, which is often easier to interpret
than rate against distance.

## Greenhouse decisions (Higher tier only)

The rule: adding heat, light or CO₂ is cost effective only if the value of the extra crop is greater than the
cost, and only the **limiting** factor can increase the crop.

**Worked example 5** (invented figures). Tomatoes sell for £3.60 per kg.

```
CO₂ enrichment: costs £80 a week; yield +25 kg
  extra income = 25 × 3.60 = £90     gain = 90 − 80 = £10 a week

Extra heating:  costs £200 a week; yield +30 kg
  extra income = 30 × 3.60 = £108    gain = 108 − 200 = −£92 a week
```

CO₂ enrichment is just worth it. Heating loses money, so temperature is probably close to its best already.

## 4.4.1.3 Uses of glucose, and why each matters

| Use | Why the plant needs it |
|---|---|
| Respiration | Releases energy for living processes |
| Converted to insoluble starch | Starch does not affect osmosis, so it can be stored in large amounts |
| Fat or oil for storage | Stored especially in seeds |
| Cellulose | Strengthens the cell wall |
| Amino acids | Built into proteins; plants also need **nitrate ions** from the soil |

## Common mistakes

- Saying a factor is limiting where the line is already flat.
- Writing "the lamp gets hotter so the rate increases" when explaining the effect of light.
- Treating bubbles per minute as perfectly accurate.
- Writing 1/d instead of 1/d² (Higher tier only).
- Forgetting that plants need nitrate ions, not just glucose, to make proteins.

## Exam technique for AQA command words

- **Describe** a graph: trend plus figures from the data.
- **Explain** a graph: identify the limiting factor and give the reason.
- **Suggest** an improvement to the practical: name the change and how it improves the results.
- **Calculate**: show the working and give the unit.
