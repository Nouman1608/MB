This guide covers subtopic **9.2, Periodicity of chemical properties of the elements in Period 3**, from Topic 9, The Periodic Table: chemical periodicity, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is AS Level content.

The [Periodicity Across Period 3](/resources/as-periodicity-across-period-3/) guide sets out the basic equations for Topic 9. This page goes further into 9.2: what you see in each reaction, why the oxidation numbers take the values they do, the reactions of the oxides and hydroxides with sodium hydroxide as well as with acid, the aqua-ion explanation of acidic chloride solutions, and how to deduce bonding from observations. Condensed notes are in the [9.2 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-as-periodicity-of-chemical-properties-of-the-elements-revision-notes/), and the [9.2 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-as-periodicity-of-chemical-properties-of-the-elements-practice/) test it.

## What 9.2 asks you to do

The seven learning outcomes cover: the reactions of the elements with **oxygen** (to give Na₂O, MgO, Al₂O₃, P₄O₁₀, SO₂), **chlorine** (to give NaCl, MgCl₂, AlCl₃, SiCl₄, PCl₅) and **water** (Na and Mg only); the **oxidation numbers** in the listed oxides and chlorides; the reactions of the oxides with water and the likely pH; the **acid/base behaviour** of the oxides and of NaOH, Mg(OH)₂ and Al(OH)₃, including amphoteric behaviour with acids and **sodium hydroxide only**; the reactions of the chlorides with water and the likely pH; explaining all of these in terms of **bonding and electronegativity**; and **suggesting the bonding** from observations.

## The elements: what you see

| Reaction | Observation |
|---|---|
| Na burning in oxygen | Bright yellow-orange flame; white solid (Na₂O) |
| Mg burning in oxygen | Brilliant white flame; white powder (MgO) |
| Al powder heated in oxygen | Burns brightly; white solid (Al₂O₃) |
| Phosphorus burning in oxygen | Bright flame; dense white smoke (P₄O₁₀) |
| Sulfur burning in oxygen | Blue flame; colourless, choking gas (SO₂) |
| Na heated in chlorine | Bright yellow-orange flame; white solid (NaCl) |
| Si heated in chlorine | Colourless liquid forms (SiCl₄) |
| Na with cold water | Floats, melts into a ball, fizzes, moves about and disappears |
| Mg with cold water | Very slow; a few bubbles over a long time |
| Mg heated in steam | Bright white glow; white solid (MgO) |

The equations for magnesium with water depend on conditions:

- Cold water (very slow): Mg + 2H₂O → Mg(OH)₂ + H₂
- Steam: Mg + H₂O → MgO + H₂

Aluminium is not in the water outcome. In everyday use aluminium is protected by a thin, unreactive surface layer of Al₂O₃, which is why aluminium objects do not react visibly with water.

## Oxidation numbers: why they rise

| Compound | Na₂O | MgO | Al₂O₃ | P₄O₁₀ | SO₂ | SO₃ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Oxidation number | +1 | +2 | +3 | +5 | +4 | +6 |

| Compound | NaCl | MgCl₂ | AlCl₃ | SiCl₄ | PCl₅ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Oxidation number | +1 | +2 | +3 | +4 | +5 |

Oxygen and chlorine are more electronegative than every Period 3 element in these compounds, so the Period 3 element always takes the positive oxidation number. The **maximum** oxidation number equals the number of **outer-shell (valence-shell) electrons**: one for Na, two for Mg, and so on up to six for S. When all the outer electrons are used in bonding, the element reaches its group maximum (Na₂O to P₄O₁₀, SO₃, and all five chlorides). SO₂ is the exception in the list: sulfur uses only four of its six outer electrons, so its oxidation number is +4.

## Oxides: water, acids and sodium hydroxide

The oxides fall into three groups, and the explanation is the change in bonding.

**Basic oxides: Na₂O and MgO.** These are ionic. The oxide ion, O²⁻, is a strong base. It accepts protons from water or from an acid:

O²⁻ + H₂O → 2OH⁻   and   O²⁻ + 2H⁺ → H₂O

With water, Na₂O gives a strongly alkaline solution (pH about 13–14). MgO gives only a weakly alkaline one (about 9–10) because Mg(OH)₂ is sparingly soluble. Both react with acids:

Na₂O + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H₂O

MgO + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂O

The hydroxides behave the same way: NaOH and Mg(OH)₂ react with acids and not with sodium hydroxide.

Mg(OH)₂ + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + 2H₂O

**Amphoteric: Al₂O₃ and Al(OH)₃.** Neither dissolves in water. Both react with acids **and** with sodium hydroxide:

Al₂O₃ + 6HCl → 2AlCl₃ + 3H₂O

Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH + 3H₂O → 2NaAl(OH)₄

Al(OH)₃ + 3HCl → AlCl₃ + 3H₂O

Al(OH)₃ + NaOH → NaAl(OH)₄

The product NaAl(OH)₄ is sodium aluminate. Some sources write the anhydrous form NaAlO₂ for the oxide reaction; balance whichever formula you use. The amphoteric behaviour comes from the bonding: Al³⁺ is small and highly charged, so it **polarises** the oxide ion strongly. Al₂O₃ is ionic with considerable covalent character, between the ionic basic oxides and the covalent acidic ones.

**Acidic oxides: P₄O₁₀, SO₂ and SO₃.** These are simple molecular and covalent. They react with water to form acids, and they react with sodium hydroxide:

P₄O₁₀ + 6H₂O → 4H₃PO₄ (pH about 0–2)

SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃ (pH about 2–3); SO₃ + H₂O → H₂SO₄ (pH about 0–1)

P₄O₁₀ + 12NaOH → 4Na₃PO₄ + 6H₂O

SO₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₃ + H₂O

SO₃ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + H₂O

**Silicon dioxide** appears only in the "with water" outcome: it is a giant covalent solid and does not react with water, so water stays at pH 7.

**Worked example: a neutralisation calculation.** 1.42 g of P₄O₁₀ is added to water. Calculate the volume of 2.00 mol dm⁻³ NaOH needed to neutralise the acid formed completely. (Aᵣ: P = 31.0, O = 16.0)

Mᵣ(P₄O₁₀) = 4(31.0) + 10(16.0) = 284. Amount = 1.42 ÷ 284 = 0.00500 mol.

From the overall equation P₄O₁₀ + 12NaOH → 4Na₃PO₄ + 6H₂O, amount of NaOH = 12 × 0.00500 = 0.0600 mol.

Volume = 0.0600 ÷ 2.00 = 0.0300 dm³ = **30.0 cm³**.

## Chlorides with water: dissolving or hydrolysis

| Chloride | Bonding | With water | Likely pH |
|---|---|---|---|
| NaCl | Ionic | Dissolves; ions are hydrated, no reaction | 7 |
| MgCl₂ | Ionic | Dissolves; very slightly acidic | about 6.5 |
| AlCl₃ | Ionic with much covalent character | Dissolves with hydrolysis of the aqua ion; with a little water, fumes of HCl | about 3 |
| SiCl₄ | Covalent | Hydrolysed completely; white solid and misty fumes | about 1–2 |
| PCl₅ | Covalent | Hydrolysed completely; misty fumes | about 1–2 |

**Why AlCl₃ solution is acidic.** In water, the aluminium ion is surrounded by six water molecules, [Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺. The small, highly charged Al³⁺ ion pulls electron density from the O–H bonds of these water molecules, so one of them can lose H⁺:

[Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺ ⇌ [Al(H₂O)₅(OH)]²⁺ + H⁺

Mg²⁺ has a lower charge and a larger radius, so it does this only very slightly. Na⁺ does not do it at all.

**The covalent chlorides** react directly with water, breaking the Si–Cl and P–Cl bonds:

SiCl₄ + 2H₂O → SiO₂ + 4HCl

PCl₅ + 4H₂O → H₃PO₄ + 5HCl

## Linking it all to electronegativity

Across the period, the electronegativity of the element increases while that of oxygen and chlorine stays the same. The electronegativity **difference** therefore decreases, and the bonding changes from ionic (Na, Mg) through ionic with covalent character (Al) to covalent (Si, P, S). That single change explains all four patterns: basic → amphoteric → acidic oxides; strongly alkaline → neutral → acidic oxide solutions; dissolving → hydrolysing chlorides; and the rise in oxidation number as more outer electrons are drawn into bonding.

## Suggesting bonding from observations

| Observation | Suggests |
|---|---|
| High melting point; conducts when molten; dissolves to give a neutral solution | Ionic (for example NaCl) |
| Very high melting point; insoluble; does not conduct when molten | Giant covalent (SiO₂) |
| Low boiling point liquid; fumes in moist air; hydrolysed to an acidic solution | Simple molecular covalent (SiCl₄) |
| Sublimes at a fairly low temperature; acidic solution | Significant covalent character (AlCl₃) |

## Common mistakes

- **Writing Mg + H₂O → MgO + H₂ for cold water.** Cold water gives Mg(OH)₂; steam gives MgO.
- **Showing MgO or Mg(OH)₂ reacting with sodium hydroxide.** They are basic only; in Period 3, only the aluminium compounds are amphoteric.
- **Showing Al₂O₃ reacting with water.** It is insoluble.
- **Giving SO₂ an oxidation number of +6.** It is +4.
- **Saying NaCl "hydrolyses".** It simply dissolves.
- **Explaining acidic AlCl₃ solutions with "AlCl₃ is acidic".** Explain the polarising Al³⁺ ion and the release of H⁺.

## Exam technique: command words in 9.2

- **Describe**: give observations and balanced equations, with state symbols if asked.
- **State and explain** (oxidation number): give the value and link it to the number of outer-shell electrons used in bonding.
- **Explain** (trends): use the decreasing electronegativity difference and the change from ionic to covalent bonding.
- **Suggest** (bonding): use the evidence in the question, such as melting point, conductivity and reaction with water.
