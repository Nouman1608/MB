These questions cover **topic 6, The digital divide** (section 6.1) of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Information Technology 9626 syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027 (version 3). This is AS Level content, part of both the AS Level and the full A Level. It is examined on Paper 1 (Theory), which is based on sections 1–11; Paper 2 (Practical) candidates apply knowledge and understanding from sections 1–7. All questions can be answered on paper.

Learn the content first with the [study guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-ict-the-digital-divide/) and the [revision notes](/resources/a-level-cambridge-ict-the-digital-divide-revision-notes/). ## Questions

**1.** Define the term *digital divide*. **[2]**

**2.** Identify **three** groups of people who may be affected by the digital divide. **[3]**

**3.** Describe what is meant by *digital literacy*. **[2]**

**4.** Amira has a fast Wi-Fi connection at home, but her only computer is an old, low performance laptop. Explain **two** ways this could restrict her access to technology and information. **[4]**

**5.** Explain why people in different socioeconomic groups may be affected differently by the digital divide. **[4]**

**6.** A college wants to make sure students with accessibility barriers are not disadvantaged.

**(a)** Describe **two** barriers that could restrict these students' access to technology. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest **two** ways the college could reduce these barriers. **[2]**

**7.** A fictional regional survey records households with a home broadband connection.

| District | Households surveyed | Households with broadband |
|---|---|---|
| Urban | 4,800 | 4,128 |
| Rural | 2,500 | 1,150 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of households with broadband in each district. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the difference between the two districts in percentage points. **[1]**
**(c)** The regional authority sets a target that 75% of rural households have broadband. Calculate how many more rural households need to be connected to meet the target. **[2]**
**(d)** Suggest **two** reasons for the difference between the districts. **[2]**

**8.** Explain how inequality of access to internet services and technology can affect people living in a less industrially developed nation. **[4]**

**9.** A charity runs free digital literacy classes for adults who left school with few qualifications. Explain how these classes could reduce the effects of the digital divide for this group. **[3]**

**10.** A government plans to make all applications for financial support available **only** online. Discuss the impact of this plan on groups affected by the digital divide. **[8]**

**11.** A secondary school finds that some pupils cannot complete online homework. Evaluate **three** measures the school could take to reduce the effects of the digital divide on these pupils. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** The gap between people or regions that have access to modern technology and information [1] and those that have restricted or no access to it. [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The second mark needs "restricted **or** no" access, or an equivalent; an answer that only says "people without computers" usually gains one mark at most.

**2.** Any three from the syllabus groups, for example: people in different age groups (such as some older people) [1]; people in rural areas compared with people in cities [1]; people in lower socioeconomic groups [1]. Also accept people with differing levels of education, people with accessibility barriers, and people in less industrially developed nations. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Identify" needs only the group named clearly; three versions of the same group (for example "old people", "pensioners", "elderly") earn one mark, not three.

**3.** Having the skills to find, use and create information using digital technology [1], including judging whether information is reliable and using technology safely. [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Being good with computers" is too vague; the second mark needs a second distinct feature, such as evaluating information or staying safe online.

**4.** The laptop may not be able to run current software or modern web browsers [1], so Amira cannot use online learning platforms or applications that others can use. [1] It may struggle with demanding tasks such as video calls or streaming [1], so she cannot take part in live online lessons or meetings despite her fast connection. [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* On a two-way "Explain" each point earns a mark for the limitation and a mark for its consequence; repeating "it is slow" for both ways earns credit only once.

**5.** Devices, internet connections and data plans cost money [1], so people in lower-income households are less likely to afford them. [1] They may have no device, or only a shared, low performance device with a limited data plan [1], so they have restricted access to services such as online learning, job applications and online shopping, while higher-income groups have full access. [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Marks go to the chain cost → affordability → type of access → effect; a bare statement that "poor people are affected" earns nothing on its own.

**6. (a)** A student with a visual impairment may be unable to read a standard screen [1]; a student with a physical impairment may be unable to use a standard keyboard or mouse. [1] Also accept a hearing impairment (no captions on videos) or a learning difficulty (complex sites or long forms hard to follow).
**(b)** Provide assistive technology such as screen readers, screen magnification or alternative input devices [1]; make sure the college's websites and course materials are designed to be accessible, for example with captions and clear layouts. [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each barrier must be linked to a specific impairment and a specific difficulty; in (b) two different measures are needed, not two examples of the same device.

**7. (a)** Urban: 4,128 ÷ 4,800 × 100 = **86%** [1]; rural: 1,150 ÷ 2,500 × 100 = **46%**. [1]
**(b)** 86 − 46 = **40 percentage points**. [1]
**(c)** 75% of 2,500 = 0.75 × 2,500 = 1,875 households [1]; 1,875 − 1,150 = **725 more households**. [1]
**(d)** Rural areas may have weaker or no wireless and broadband coverage, because infrastructure is costly to build for few users [1]; rural households may have lower incomes or fewer providers, so connections may cost more or be harder to obtain. [1] **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b) "40%" is not the same as "40 percentage points"; in (c) the method mark is for finding 75% of the rural total, so an answer using the urban figure gains nothing.

**8.** Less industrially developed nations may have less infrastructure, such as reliable electricity, broadband and mobile networks [1], and fewer affordable devices or lower technological awareness. [1] So people there may be unable to use online education, online banking or access to global information [1], which can widen the gap with more developed nations, for example in education, trade and job opportunities. [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The question asks about effects, so at least half the marks need consequences for people, not only a description of missing infrastructure.

**9.** The classes teach skills such as using a device, searching for and judging information, and completing online forms [1], which this group may lack because of fewer opportunities to learn them in education. [1] With these skills and more confidence, they can use online services such as job applications and government services, reducing the gap in access. [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The answer must link the classes to the specific group named (differing levels of education); a general description of a class with no link to that group's problem limits the mark.

**10.** Indicative points; credit any eight valid, developed points. Eight shown:
- Some older people may lack the skills or confidence to apply online [1], so they could miss support they are entitled to. [1]
- People in rural areas may have weak or no wireless coverage [1], so they may be unable to submit applications or may need to travel to find a connection. [1]
- People in lower socioeconomic groups, who may most need financial support, are less likely to own a suitable device or connection [1], so the plan could exclude the people it is meant to help. [1]
- People with accessibility barriers may be unable to use the forms unless they are accessible and compatible with assistive technology. [1]
- Benefits: online applications can be submitted at any time and may be processed faster; the plan would be fairer if paired with digital literacy teaching, public access points or a paper or phone alternative. [1] **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* "Discuss" needs points developed in a structured way; listing groups without explaining the impact on each tends to cap the mark well below full, and a balancing point (benefits or mitigation) is expected.

**11.** Indicative points; for each measure, one mark for a benefit and one for a limitation.
- **Lending laptops to pupils**: removes the problem of having no device, or only a low performance one [1], but pupils without a home connection still cannot get online. [1]
- **Opening the school computer room after lessons with free Wi-Fi**: gives access to devices and a connection [1], but pupils who live far away or have duties at home may not be able to stay. [1]
- **Digital literacy sessions for pupils and parents**: build skills so pupils and families can use the homework platform confidently [1], but they do not help if the barrier is cost or coverage rather than skills. [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* "Evaluate" needs a judgement on each measure; three benefits with no limitations would usually earn about half the marks.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving "restricted" out of the definition, so partial access is ignored.
- Writing the same group three times in different words on an "Identify" question.
- Describing a device's weakness without saying what the user then **cannot do**.
- Writing "40%" for the gap between two percentages instead of "40 percentage points".
- Using the wrong total when calculating a target, such as the urban figure in a rural question.
- Proposing a measure that does not match the cause: free Wi-Fi for a skills gap, or digital literacy classes for no signal.
- Missing a balancing point on "Discuss" or "Evaluate" questions.
- Treating accessibility barriers as only physical, and forgetting learning difficulties and sensory impairments.
