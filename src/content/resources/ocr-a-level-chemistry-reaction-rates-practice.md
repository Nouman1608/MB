## Questions

**1.** Which change increases the proportion of molecules with energy of at least Ea?

A. Increasing the pressure of a gas at constant temperature
B. Increasing the concentration of a solution
C. Increasing the temperature
D. Using a larger flask for the same reaction mixture **[1]**

**2.** Zinc powder reacts with aqueous copper(II) sulfate.

**(a)** State what is meant by the term activation energy. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why the reaction is faster with 0.50 mol dm⁻³ copper(II) sulfate than with 0.20 mol dm⁻³ copper(II) sulfate. **[2]**

**3.** Excess iron powder reacts with dilute sulfuric acid. The hydrogen is collected in a gas syringe.

| t / s | 0 | 30 | 60 | 90 | 120 | 150 | 180 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| V(H₂) / cm³ | 0 | 42 | 72 | 92 | 104 | 110 | 112 |

**(a)** Calculate the mean rate over the first 60 s. **[1]**
**(b)** A tangent to the curve at 90 s passes through (30 s, 59 cm³) and (150 s, 125 cm³). Calculate the rate at 90 s. **[2]**
**(c)** Convert your answer to (b) into mol s⁻¹. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why the gas is produced more slowly as time goes on. **[2]**
**(e)** The experiment is repeated with a different concentration of acid. State one variable, other than temperature, that must be kept the same. **[1]**

**4.** Zinc carbonate powder, ZnCO₃, is added to excess dilute sulfuric acid in a conical flask on a balance.

ZnCO₃(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)

**(a)** The mass falls from 85.40 g to 85.04 g in the first 20 s. Calculate the mean rate of CO₂ production in mol s⁻¹. **[2]**
**(b)** State why a plug of cotton wool is placed in the neck of the flask. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why this method would be unsuitable for following the reaction of zinc metal with the same acid. **[1]**
**(d)** 1.881 g of zinc carbonate was used. Calculate the total mass loss when the reaction is complete. Mr of ZnCO₃ = 125.4. **[2]**

**5.** A reaction has ΔH = +48 kJ mol⁻¹ and an uncatalysed activation energy of 210 kJ mol⁻¹. A catalyst lowers the activation energy by 75 kJ mol⁻¹.

**(a)** Sketch a labelled enthalpy profile diagram showing the catalysed and uncatalysed routes. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the activation energy of the catalysed reverse reaction. **[2]**
**(c)** State the value of ΔH for the catalysed reaction. **[1]**

**6.**

**(a)** Explain the difference between a homogeneous catalyst and a heterogeneous catalyst. **[2]**
**(b)** Classify each catalyst as homogeneous or heterogeneous.
(i) Iron in the reaction of nitrogen gas with hydrogen gas
(ii) Sulfuric acid in a solution of ethanol and ethanoic acid
(iii) Nickel in the reaction of gaseous ethene with hydrogen
(iv) Chlorine radicals in the breakdown of ozone in the stratosphere **[2]**

**7.** A sample of gas is heated from temperature T₁ to a higher temperature T₂.

**(a)** Sketch Boltzmann distributions for the gas at T₁ and T₂ on the same axes. Label the axes. **[3]**
**(b)** Use your sketch to explain why the rate of reaction increases. **[3]**

**8.** Explain, with reference to a Boltzmann distribution, why adding a catalyst makes a reaction faster. **[3]**

**9.** A new catalyst lets a process run cooler, saving 1.11 × 10⁹ kJ of heat per day that was supplied by burning propane. Use these values: ΔcH of propane = −2220 kJ mol⁻¹; Mr of CO₂ = 44.0.

C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O

**(a)** Calculate the mass of CO₂, in tonnes, that is no longer released each day. **[3]**
**(b)** The new catalyst contains a toxic metal compound. Evaluate whether switching to it improves the sustainability of the process. **[2]**

**10.** Plan an experiment to find out how the concentration of nitric acid affects the rate of its reaction with copper(II) carbonate powder, by following the loss in mass. **[6]**

**11.** In a gas-phase reaction, the concentration of product P was measured over time.

| t / s | 0 | 50 | 100 | 150 | 200 | 300 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| [P] / mol dm⁻³ | 0 | 0.0240 | 0.0410 | 0.0530 | 0.0610 | 0.0700 |

**(a)** Calculate the mean rate of formation of P over the first 100 s. **[1]**
**(b)** A tangent at t = 0 passes through (0 s, 0) and (60 s, 0.0330 mol dm⁻³). Calculate the initial rate. **[2]**
**(c)** The experiment is repeated at the same temperature but at twice the pressure. Predict and explain the effect on the initial rate. **[2]**
**(d)** The experiment is repeated at the original pressure but at a higher temperature. Explain, with reference to the Boltzmann distribution, the effect on the initial rate. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** **C** [1]
*Examiner insight:* At a fixed temperature the energy distribution is unchanged, so A, B and D alter only collision frequency.

**2. (a)** The **minimum energy colliding particles need in order to react** [1]
**(b)** The more concentrated solution has more Cu²⁺ ions per unit volume [1], so the ions hit the zinc surface more often and more successful collisions happen per second [1].
*Examiner insight:* Saying the particles "have more energy" in (b) contradicts the chemistry; concentration does not change collision energy.

**3. (a)** 72 ÷ 60 = **1.2 cm³ s⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** Gradient = (125 − 59) ÷ (150 − 30) [1] = 66 ÷ 120 = **0.55 cm³ s⁻¹** [1]
**(c)** 0.55 ÷ 24 000, using 24 000 cm³ mol⁻¹ [1] = **2.3 × 10⁻⁵ mol s⁻¹** (2.29 × 10⁻⁵) [1]
**(d)** The sulfuric acid is consumed, lowering its concentration [1]; collisions with the iron happen less often [1].
**(e)** Any one: **mass of iron powder**, its particle size, or the volume of acid [1].
*Examiner insight:* In (c), dividing by 24.0 instead of 24 000 gives an answer a thousand times too large.

**4. (a)** 0.36 g ÷ 20 s = 0.018 g s⁻¹ [1]; 0.018 ÷ 44.0 = **4.1 × 10⁻⁴ mol s⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** It lets CO₂ out but stops acid spray escaping and adding to the mass loss [1].
**(c)** The gas would be hydrogen, whose small Mr gives too small a mass change to measure precisely [1].
**(d)** n(ZnCO₃) = 1.881 ÷ 125.4 = 0.0150 mol = n(CO₂) [1]; mass = 0.0150 × 44.0 = **0.660 g** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Stopping at 0.018 g s⁻¹ in (a) answers only half the question.

**5. (a)** Axes labelled enthalpy and progress of reaction, with products drawn above reactants [1]; two humps, the catalysed one lower [1]; Ea, Ea(cat) and ΔH shown with correctly directed arrows [1].
**(b)** Catalysed forward Ea = 210 − 75 = 135 kJ mol⁻¹ [1]; reverse = 135 − 48 = **87 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(c)** **+48 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Each Ea arrow runs from the reactant level to a peak; one drawn from the products shows the reverse Ea.

**6. (a)** Homogeneous: the catalyst is in the same physical state as the reactants [1]. Heterogeneous: the catalyst is in a different physical state from the reactants [1].
**(b)** (i) heterogeneous; (ii) homogeneous; (iii) heterogeneous; (iv) homogeneous. All four correct [2]; two or three correct earn one mark.
*Examiner insight:* "Physical state" or "phase" is needed; "different substance" does not explain the distinction.

**7. (a)** Axes: number of molecules (y) and energy (x) [1]; both curves start at the origin and do not meet the energy axis at high energy [1]; the T₂ curve has a lower peak displaced to higher energy, with the same area [1].
**(b)** Ea marked: the area to the right of Ea is larger at T₂ [1], so a greater proportion of molecules have energy of at least Ea [1], giving more successful collisions per second [1].
*Examiner insight:* A T₂ curve lying wholly above the T₁ curve shows more molecules, not a higher temperature.

**8.** With the catalyst the reaction follows a different pathway of lower activation energy, so Ea sits at a lower energy on the same curve [1]. A greater proportion of molecules have energy of at least the catalysed Ea [1]. More collisions per second are successful, so the rate increases [1].
*Examiner insight:* Redrawing the curve for a catalyst is wrong; the temperature has not changed, so only the Ea marker moves.

**9. (a)** n(C₃H₈) = 1.11 × 10⁹ ÷ 2220 = 5.0 × 10⁵ mol [1]; n(CO₂) = 3 × 5.0 × 10⁵ = 1.5 × 10⁶ mol [1]; mass = 1.5 × 10⁶ × 44.0 = 6.6 × 10⁷ g = **66 tonnes** [1]
**(b)** Benefit: less fuel burned, so less CO₂ released and fewer fossil resources used [1]. Against: the toxic catalyst must be contained, recovered and disposed of safely, so sustainability improves only if its escape is prevented [1].
*Examiner insight:* An "evaluate" answer that lists only benefits is one-sided; give a point on each side and a conclusion.

**10.** Six points such as:
- Weigh the same mass of copper(II) carbonate powder, of the same particle size, and measure the same volume of acid for every run [1].
- Make several nitric acid concentrations by diluting a stock solution with distilled water [1].
- Keep the temperature constant, for example by standing the acid in a water bath first and checking with a thermometer [1].
- Stand a conical flask of acid on a balance; tip in the carbonate, add a cotton wool plug and start the timer at once [1].
- Record the mass at regular intervals, such as every 15 s, until it stops changing [1].
- Plot mass lost against time, draw a tangent at t = 0 for each initial rate, and compare with concentration, repeating runs to check reliability [1].

*Examiner insight:* State how each control variable is controlled; "keep it a fair test" alone earns nothing.

**11. (a)** 0.0410 ÷ 100 = **4.1 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** 0.0330 ÷ 60 [1] = **5.5 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹** [1]
**(c)** The initial rate **increases** [1]: doubling the pressure doubles the gas concentrations, so collisions are more frequent [1].
**(d)** At the higher temperature, the Boltzmann curve is flatter and shifted to higher energy [1]. A greater proportion of molecules have energy of at least Ea [1]. More successful collisions per second, so the initial rate **increases** [1].
*Examiner insight:* In (c) the temperature is fixed, so answers about Ea or the Boltzmann curve gain nothing.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving concentration or pressure an effect on collision energy, as in questions 2 and 11.
- Taking a tangent gradient from points on the curve instead of on the drawn line.
- Mixing cm³ and dm³ when converting a gas volume rate to moles, as in question 3.
- Stopping at g s⁻¹ when mol s⁻¹ was asked for.
- Moving the Boltzmann curve for a catalyst, or giving the hotter curve a higher peak.
- Using the wrong mole ratio for CO₂ from a fuel, as in question 9.
- Listing control variables in a plan without saying how each is controlled.
