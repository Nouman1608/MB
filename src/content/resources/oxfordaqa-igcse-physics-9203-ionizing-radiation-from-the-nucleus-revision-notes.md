These notes condense section **3.7.2 Ionizing radiation from the nucleus** (statements a to j) of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Physics (9203) specification, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. Statements h to j are marked P (full Physics award only). For worked examples read the [radiation study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-ionizing-radiation-from-the-nucleus/), then test yourself with the [radiation practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-ionizing-radiation-from-the-nucleus-practice/).

## Condensed recall

**Decay (a, b).** An unstable nucleus emits particles or radiation, changes to a different element's nucleus and becomes more stable. Energy is emitted by changes in the nucleus.

**What is emitted (c).** Alpha particles, beta particles, neutrons, and gamma rays.

**Random and unaffected (c).** No one can predict when a particular nucleus will decay. Chemical and physical processes do not affect decay.

**Background (d).** Always present. Sources: radioactive substances in the environment, space, and devices such as hospital X-ray machines. Corrected count rate = measured count rate − background count rate.

**The radiations (e, g).**

| | Alpha | Beta | Gamma |
|---|---|---|---|
| What it is | Helium nucleus: 2p + 2n | Fast electron from the nucleus (neutron → proton) | Electromagnetic radiation |
| Ionising power | Strongest | Moderate | Weakest |
| Stopped by | Paper | A few mm of aluminium | Reduced by thick lead or concrete |
| Range in air | A few cm | Tens of cm to a few m | Very long |

**Nuclear equations (f).** Single alpha or beta decays only; balance mass numbers and atomic numbers. Naming the new element is not required.

| | Mass number | Atomic number |
|---|---|---|
| Alpha (⁴₂He) | −4 | −2 |
| Beta (⁰₋₁e) | 0 | +1 |

**Half-life (h, P).** Average time for the number of nuclei of an isotope in a sample to halve; or time for the count rate to fall to half its initial level.

**Contamination and irradiation (i, P).**

- Contamination: unwanted radioactive atoms on or in a material; hazard continues while they decay; depends on radiation type.
- Irradiation: exposure to radiation; the object does **not** become radioactive.

**Half-life and hazard (j, P).** Very unstable → short half-life → lots of radiation quickly. Least unstable → long half-life → little radiation per second, for a long time.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Unstable nucleus | A nucleus that can decay by emitting particles or radiation |
| Radioactive decay | Random process in which an unstable nucleus emits radiation and changes |
| Background radiation | Radiation present all the time from natural and artificial sources |
| Count rate | Number of decays detected per second or per minute |
| Ionising power | How strongly radiation removes electrons from atoms |
| Penetration | How far radiation passes through materials |
| Half-life | Time for the number of nuclei, or the count rate, to halve |
| Contamination | Unwanted presence of radioactive atoms on or in other materials |
| Irradiation | Exposure of an object to ionising radiation |

## Methods

**Half-life sums.** Number of half-lives = time ÷ half-life. Halve once per half-life. For example, 6.4 × 10⁶ nuclei with a half-life of 5 days: after 15 days (3 half-lives), 6.4 × 10⁶ → 3.2 × 10⁶ → 1.6 × 10⁶ → 8.0 × 10⁵.

**Absorber data.** Correct for background first. A big drop with paper means alpha; a big drop with aluminium means beta; what is left after aluminium and reduced by lead is gamma.

## Answer templates

- **Random:** "It is impossible to predict when a particular nucleus will decay."
- **Unaffected:** "Decay depends on the nucleus, so chemical and physical processes such as heating do not change it."
- **Short range of alpha:** "Alpha is strongly ionising, so it transfers its energy quickly and stops within a few centimetres of air."
- **Irradiation:** "The object is exposed to radiation but does not become radioactive."

## Exam traps

- Beta comes from the nucleus, not the electron shells.
- Heating does not change decay.
- Convert counts to rates before subtracting background.
- Beta decay increases the atomic number.
- Lead reduces gamma; it does not stop it completely.
- An irradiated object is not radioactive.

## Quick self-test

1. What happens to an unstable nucleus when it decays?
2. Give two features of radioactive decay stated in the specification.
3. Name three sources of background radiation.
4. What is an alpha particle?
5. How is a beta particle produced?
6. Which radiation has the longest range in air?
7. Complete: ²¹⁰₈₄Po → ᴬ_Z X + ⁴₂He.
8. Complete: ¹³¹₅₃I → ᴬ_Z X + ⁰₋₁e.
9. Background is 25 counts per minute. A source gives 3150 counts in 3 minutes. Find the corrected count rate.
10. A count rate falls from 1600 to 200 counts per minute in 36 hours. Find the half-life.
11. Which is more hazardous inside the body, an alpha emitter or a gamma emitter? Why?
12. Does an irradiated object become radioactive?

### Answers

1. It emits particles or radiation and changes into the nucleus of a different element, becoming more stable.
2. It is random, and it is not affected by chemical or physical processes.
3. Radioactive substances in the environment, radiation from space, devices such as hospital X-ray machines.
4. Two protons and two neutrons (a helium nucleus).
5. A neutron in the nucleus turns into a proton and a high-speed electron is ejected.
6. Gamma.
7. A = 206, Z = 82.
8. A = 131, Z = 54.
9. 3150 ÷ 3 = 1050; 1050 − 25 = **1025 counts per minute**.
10. 1600 → 800 → 400 → 200 is 3 half-lives, so half-life = 36 ÷ 3 = **12 hours**.
11. The alpha emitter: alpha is the most strongly ionising, and inside the body it is not stopped by skin.
12. No.
