Key question 2.2 of Cambridge O Level Sociology (2251) asks *How does society control us?* Most people
obey most rules most of the time, and sociologists want to know why. The answer combines internal
control (we have learned to want to conform) with external pressure from formal agencies such as the
police and informal agencies such as the family. The syllabus also asks you to judge how effective
each agency is, to compare consensus and conflict theories, and to explain why some people resist
control through protest groups and subcultures. This is Paper 1 Question 2 content.

## What the syllabus covers

- **2.2.1** Formal and informal social control; positive and negative sanctions; their effectiveness in
  producing social conformity.
- **2.2.2** Consensus and conflict views: functionalism, Marxism, feminism.
- **2.2.3** The effectiveness of formal agencies: government, police, courts, penal system, armed
  forces.
- **2.2.4** Methods used by formal agencies, e.g. law-making, coercion, digital surveillance, arrest,
  sentencing, imprisonment.
- **2.2.5** The effectiveness of informal agencies: family, education, peer group, media, religion,
  workplace.
- **2.2.6** Methods used by informal agencies: positive and negative sanctions, e.g. ostracism,
  parental rewards, social media "likes", religious rewards/sanctions, school punishments, dismissal
  from a job.
- **2.2.7** Resistance through protest groups and subcultures: why people join; deviant and
  non-conformist behaviour; examples of protest groups and online, youth and religious subcultures.

## Formal and informal control

**Social control** is every way in which a society encourages conformity to its norms and values.

- **Formal control** is exercised by official agencies of the state, using written rules (laws and
  regulations) and official penalties.
- **Informal control** is exercised by the people around us through everyday approval and
  disapproval, based on unwritten norms.

Both rely on **sanctions**. A **positive sanction** rewards conformity (a promotion, praise, a medal);
a **negative sanction** punishes deviance (a fine, a detention, ostracism). Sanctions can be formal or
informal, so there are four combinations: a court sentence is a formal negative sanction, a
bravery award is a formal positive sanction, a parent's praise is an informal positive sanction and
being ignored by friends is an informal negative sanction.

The strongest control of all is **internalised**: through socialisation we come to accept norms as
right, so we feel guilt or shame before anyone else reacts. External sanctions mainly deal with the
cases where internal control fails.

## Formal agencies and their methods

| Agency | Main methods | Effective when… | Less effective when… |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Government** | Law-making; regulation; funding of other agencies | Laws reflect widely shared values | Laws lack public support or are poorly enforced |
| **Police** | Arrest; patrol; digital surveillance; stop and search | Trusted by the public, who then report crime | Crime goes unreported; corruption or low resources |
| **Courts** | Sentencing; fines; orders | Penalties are seen as fair and consistent | Long delays; unequal access to legal help |
| **Penal system** | Imprisonment; probation; rehabilitation | Offenders are reformed and do not reoffend | Prisons spread criminal skills and labels |
| **Armed forces** | Coercion in emergencies, civil unrest or conflict | Order must be restored quickly | Force without legitimacy produces resistance |

**Coercion** means force or the threat of force. It is the last resort of formal control, and
societies that rely on it heavily are often less stable, because people conform out of fear rather
than agreement.

**Digital surveillance** extends formal control: CCTV, facial recognition, number-plate cameras,
phone location data and monitoring of online activity. In *Discipline and Punish* (1975),
Michel Foucault drew on the **panopticon**, a design for a prison with a central watchtower: because
prisoners could never tell when a guard was looking, they behaved as though they were always
observed. Surveillance works in the same way: knowing you might be watched changes behaviour.
Critics point to privacy, the risk of misuse by governments and the fact that some groups are
watched more closely than others.

## Informal agencies and their methods

The syllabus names six informal agencies; their power comes from relationships people value.

- **Family:** parental rewards (treats, freedom) and sanctions (grounding, disapproval); strongest when
  children are young.
- **Education:** merits, prizes and school punishments such as detention or exclusion; teachers'
  approval also matters.
- **Peer group:** acceptance or **ostracism** (being excluded); powerful in adolescence, but peer
  norms may encourage deviance rather than conformity.
- **Media:** social media "likes", shares and followers reward approved behaviour; public shaming and
  hostile comments punish breaches of norms.
- **Religion:** religious rewards and sanctions, including the promise of reward or punishment after
  death, respect within the community, or exclusion from it.
- **Workplace:** pay rises and promotion; warnings and **dismissal from a job**.

Informal control is usually more effective in everyday life because it is constant and comes from
people whose opinion matters. It is weaker in large, anonymous cities, where people do not know their
neighbours, and among people who do not care about the group's approval.

## The theoretical debate

**Functionalism (consensus).** Social control protects shared values that benefit everyone. Émile
Durkheim argued that punishing deviance reinforces the boundaries of acceptable behaviour: when a
wrongdoer is punished, everyone else is reminded of the rules and united in disapproval. Agencies of
control therefore help maintain social order and cohesion.

**Marxism (conflict).** Social control serves the ruling class. Laws protect private property and the
capitalist system; the police, courts and armed forces enforce them. Althusser distinguished
**repressive state apparatuses** (police, courts, army), which control by force, from **ideological
state apparatuses** (education, media, religion), which control by persuading people that inequality
is fair. Ideological control is cheaper and more effective, because people police themselves.

**Feminism (conflict).** Social control maintains patriarchy. Frances Heidensohn's *Women and Crime*
(1985) argued that women are controlled more tightly than men: at home through domestic
responsibilities, in public through fear of male violence and concern for reputation, and at work by
male supervisors. This helps explain why women commit fewer crimes, and shows informal control
falling unequally on women.

## Resistance: protest groups and subcultures

Not everyone conforms. **Deviant behaviour** breaks a norm and attracts negative sanctions;
**non-conformist behaviour** rejects mainstream expectations without necessarily breaking the law,
such as unusual dress or lifestyle.

- **Protest groups** campaign for change: environmental groups such as the school climate strikes
  begun by Greta Thunberg in Sweden in 2018, or civil rights and workers' movements. Most act
  legally through marches and petitions; some use civil disobedience.
- **Youth subcultures** have distinctive styles and values: Dick Hebdige's *Subculture: The Meaning of
  Style* (1979) interpreted punk style as a symbolic challenge to mainstream society.
- **Online subcultures** form around gaming, music, fandoms or political views, and can link people
  across countries.
- **Religious subcultures** follow beliefs and practices different from the majority, often with
  strict internal control.

**Why people join:** a sense of belonging and identity; shared grievances and a wish for change;
status unavailable elsewhere (Albert Cohen's idea of status frustration among working-class boys,
1955); excitement and rebellion; and the influence of friends. Note the irony: subcultures resist
mainstream control but usually impose strong informal control of their own.

## Worked example: an evaluation plan (practice)

*Evaluate the view that social control mainly benefits the powerful.*

- **For (conflict):** Marxists argue laws protect property and the capitalist class, while crimes of
  the powerful are rarely punished; ideological state apparatuses persuade the working class to
  accept inequality; feminists such as Heidensohn show control falling more heavily on women.
- **Against (consensus):** functionalists argue control protects shared values, such as laws against
  violence that benefit everyone; Durkheim saw punishment as uniting society; informal control in
  families and religious communities often reflects genuine agreement.
- **Judgement:** some control clearly protects everyone, but who makes the rules and who is
  watched most closely suggests that the powerful benefit more.

## Common mistakes

- Treating "formal" as "serious": a school detention is informal social control in this syllabus,
  as school is an informal agency.
- Giving sanctions without saying whether they are positive or negative.
- Describing agencies without judging their effectiveness.
- Assuming subcultures are always criminal; many are non-conformist rather than deviant.

## Exam technique

- **Explain three** (parts c and d): agency, method, example, then why it produces conformity.
- **Discuss** (part e): for "why people join subcultures", give several reasons with named evidence.
- **Evaluate** (part f): set consensus against conflict views and conclude.
