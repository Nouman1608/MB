> **Syllabus edition note.** This guide follows the Cambridge O Level Environmental Management 5014
> syllabus for exams in 2027, 2028 and 2029.

Sub-topic 1.1 is the geology the rest of Topic 1 is built on. Ores are dug out of rocks, fossil fuels
are trapped in them, and the permeability of a rock decides where ground water can be stored and why
shale gas can only be reached by fracking. The
[Topic 1 rocks, ores and minerals guide](/resources/o-level-cambridge-environmental-management-rocks-ores-and-minerals/)
summarises the named rocks in a table; this page explains each formation step by step, shows how to
read an unfamiliar rock cycle diagram, and links permeability to the environmental questions it answers.

## What the syllabus asks

- **1.1.1** Describe the formation of named rocks, limited to igneous (granite and basalt), sedimentary
  (limestone, sandstone and shale) and metamorphic (marble and slate).
- **1.1.2** Describe and interpret the rock cycle, limited to weathering, erosion, transportation,
  sedimentation, compaction, cementation, crystallisation and deposition.
- **1.1.3** Define permeability as "the ability of water to pass through the pore spaces of rock and
  soil".
- **1.1.4** Classify rocks as permeable (most sedimentary rocks: limestone and sandstone) or impermeable
  (igneous, metamorphic and some sedimentary rocks: shale).

Seven rocks, eight processes and one property: the list is short, so examiners expect the exact process
for the exact rock.

## Igneous rocks: from molten rock to crystals

Igneous rock forms when molten rock cools and minerals **crystallise** from it. Molten rock underground is
**magma**; molten rock that erupts at the surface is **lava**. The rate of cooling controls the size of
the crystals, and that is the difference between the two named igneous rocks.

**Granite**

1. Magma rises into the crust but stays deep underground.
2. Surrounded by hot rock, it loses heat very slowly, over a very long time.
3. Minerals have time to grow, so granite has **large, interlocking crystals** that are visible without a
   lens.
4. Granite reaches the surface only when the rocks above it are worn away.

**Basalt**

1. Lava erupts at the surface, for example from a volcano or at a crack in the ocean floor.
2. Exposed to air or water, it cools quickly.
3. Crystals have little time to grow, so basalt has **small crystals**, often too small to see.

Because the crystals interlock, there are almost no pore spaces between them, which is why igneous rocks
are impermeable.

## Sedimentary rocks: layer by layer

Sedimentary rock is made from pieces of older rock, or from the remains of living things, that are laid
down in layers and then hardened. Three of the rock cycle processes do the hardening:

- **sedimentation**: sediment settles and builds up in layers, usually on a sea or lake bed;
- **compaction**: the weight of the layers above squeezes the lower layers and pushes out water;
- **cementation**: minerals carried in the water crystallise between the grains and glue them together.

| Rock | What it is made from | Where the sediment collects | Resulting rock |
|---|---|---|---|
| Limestone | Mainly calcium carbonate, often from the shells and skeletons of sea creatures | Warm, shallow seas | Layers with pore spaces; water also moves along its cracks |
| Sandstone | Sand grains | River beds, beaches, sea floors and deserts | Grains with pore spaces between them |
| Shale | Very fine mud and clay | Calm water, such as lakes and deep sea floors | Thin layers; particles packed so tightly that water cannot pass |

The grain size of the sediment explains the permeability. Sand grains are large, so the gaps between them
are large and connected; clay particles are tiny and flat, so the gaps are too small and poorly connected
for water to flow.

## Metamorphic rocks: changed without melting

Metamorphic rock forms when an existing rock is changed by **heat and/or pressure** while staying solid.
If it melted, it would become magma and then igneous rock.

- **Marble** forms from **limestone**. Heat, for example from nearby magma, and pressure make the
  calcium carbonate recrystallise into a hard rock of interlocking crystals. Fossils in the original
  limestone are destroyed.
- **Slate** forms from **shale**. Great pressure, for example where rocks are squeezed during mountain
  building, lines up the flat clay minerals. Slate is harder than shale and splits into thin, flat sheets,
  which is why it is used for roofing.

Recrystallisation removes pore spaces, so both marble and slate are impermeable.

## The rock cycle: processes in order

The cycle has no fixed start, but a sedimentary route is the easiest to describe because it uses six of
the eight named processes in order:

**weathering → erosion → transportation → deposition → sedimentation → compaction → cementation**

- **Weathering** breaks rock down where it is, by physical, chemical or biological action.
- **Erosion** removes the broken material.
- **Transportation** carries it away by rivers, wind, ice or the sea.
- **Deposition** drops it when the transporting agent loses energy.

The eighth process, **crystallisation**, makes igneous rock from magma or lava. Any rock exposed at the
surface can be weathered, and any rock buried deeply enough can be changed by heat and pressure or melted,
so arrows in a rock cycle diagram can branch in several directions.

### Interpreting a rock cycle diagram

Exam diagrams often replace process names with letters. Use the two boxes each arrow joins:

| Arrow goes from... | ...to | The process is |
|---|---|---|
| Magma or lava | Igneous rock | Crystallisation |
| Rock at the surface | Loose fragments | Weathering |
| Fragments in place | Fragments moved away | Erosion, then transportation |
| Moving sediment | Sediment on the sea bed | Deposition |
| Loose layers | Sedimentary rock | Compaction and cementation |
| Any rock, buried | Metamorphic rock | Heat and/or pressure (metamorphism) |
| Any rock, buried deeper | Magma | Melting |

Melting and metamorphism are not in the 1.1.2 list, but you need them to explain how igneous and
metamorphic rocks form, so use them where a diagram shows those arrows.

## Permeability and why it matters

**Definition (1.1.3):** permeability is the ability of water to pass through the pore spaces of rock and
soil. Learn this wording: a definition question rewards the exact meaning.

**Classification (1.1.4):**

| Permeable | Impermeable |
|---|---|
| Most sedimentary rocks, including limestone and sandstone | Igneous rocks (granite, basalt) |
| | Metamorphic rocks (marble, slate) |
| | Some sedimentary rocks, including shale |

Permeability connects 1.1 to the rest of the course:

- Water collects underground in permeable rock above an impermeable layer, forming an aquifer that wells
  can tap.
- Reservoirs are best built where impermeable rock stops water draining away.
- Oil and gas rise through permeable rock until an impermeable layer traps them; gas held in shale cannot
  flow out by itself, which is why fracking (1.6) is used.
- Rain falling on impermeable rock runs off the surface quickly, which affects flooding.

## Worked example: data from a permeability test

*Invented data.* A student pours 100 cm³ of water onto equal-sized samples of four rocks and measures the
volume that passes through in 10 minutes.

| Rock | Volume passing through (cm³) |
|---|---|
| Sandstone | 42 |
| Limestone | 18 |
| Shale | 0 |
| Granite | 0 |

*Question (original):* Calculate the percentage of water that passed through the sandstone, and explain
the difference between sandstone and shale. [4]

*Model answer:* 42 / 100 x 100 = **42%** [1]. Sandstone is made of large sand grains with connected pore
spaces between them [1], so water can pass through it [1]. Shale is made of tiny clay particles packed
tightly together, so its pore spaces are too small for water to pass, and it is impermeable [1].

## Common mistakes

- Saying granite cools slowly "because it is in the sea" or "on the surface". It cools slowly because it
  is deep underground.
- Saying metamorphic rocks form by melting.
- Calling every sedimentary rock permeable: shale is impermeable.
- Using weathering and erosion as if they meant the same thing.
- Leaving out cementation, so the sediment never becomes rock.
- Pairing the wrong parent rocks: marble comes from limestone and slate from shale.
- Defining permeability as "how much water a rock holds". It is about water **passing through**.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** the formation of a rock: give the starting material, the process and the result, in order.
- **Interpret** the rock cycle: name the process each labelled arrow represents, using the start and end
  boxes.
- **Define**: give the syllabus definition.
- **Explain** a difference in permeability: link it to crystals or grain size and pore spaces.
