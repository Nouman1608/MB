This guide covers sub-topic **11.1 The immune system** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Biology 9700 syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027. It is AS Level content, examined on Paper 1 and Paper 2 and assumed on Paper 4.

The [immunity study guide](/resources/a-level-biology-9700-immunity/) sets out the cells and the order of events. This page explains why each event happens, separates what each cell does so you never mix them up, models a full-mark sequence answer, and uses a simple growth model to show why the secondary response is faster. Condense it with the [immune system revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-biology-9700-the-immune-system-cambridge-alevel-biology-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [immune system practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-biology-9700-the-immune-system-cambridge-alevel-biology-practice/).

## What 11.1 asks you to do

| Outcome | Content |
|---|---|
| 11.1.1 | Describe the mode of action of phagocytes (macrophages and neutrophils) |
| 11.1.2 | Explain what is meant by an antigen; state the difference between self and non-self antigens |
| 11.1.3 | Describe the primary immune response, with the roles of macrophages, B-lymphocytes (including plasma cells), and T-helper and T-killer cells |
| 11.1.4 | Explain the role of memory cells in the secondary immune response and in long-term immunity |

The syllabus limits T-lymphocytes to **T-helper** and **T-killer** cells. You do not need other types.

## Two kinds of response

- **Non-specific**: phagocytes engulf any pathogen they meet. Fast, but the same every time and leaves no memory.
- **Specific**: lymphocytes respond only to particular antigens. Slow the first time, much faster afterwards, because of memory cells.

Macrophages connect the two: they engulf non-specifically and then present antigens that start the specific response.

## Phagocytes in action

Both neutrophils and macrophages are made in the bone marrow and work in the same way:

1. **Attraction**: pathogens and damaged cells release chemicals; the phagocyte moves towards the highest concentration (chemotaxis).
2. **Recognition**: receptors on the phagocyte's cell surface membrane bind to molecules on the pathogen's surface. If antibodies are already attached, binding is faster, because phagocytes have receptors for the antibody's constant region.
3. **Engulfing**: the membrane extends around the pathogen and fuses, taking it in by **endocytosis** in a **phagocytic vacuole**.
4. **Digestion**: **lysosomes** fuse with the vacuole and release **hydrolytic enzymes**, which break down the pathogen.
5. **Afterwards**: soluble products are absorbed or released. A neutrophil soon dies. A macrophage keeps fragments of the pathogen's antigens and places them on its cell surface membrane, becoming an **antigen-presenting cell**.

| | Neutrophil | Macrophage |
|---|---|---|
| Timing | First to arrive, in large numbers | Arrives later; long-lived |
| Where | Circulates in blood, leaves at infection sites | Develops from monocytes; stays in tissues and lymph nodes |
| After digestion | Dies | Presents antigens to lymphocytes |

## Antigens: self and non-self

An **antigen** is a molecule that the immune system recognises and that can stimulate an immune response. Most are **proteins or glycoproteins**, sometimes polysaccharides, on cell surfaces (the cell surface antigens of topic 4) or free in body fluids, such as toxins.

- **Self antigens** are on the body's own cells. While lymphocytes develop, those whose receptors bind strongly to self antigens are destroyed, so the remaining lymphocytes normally do not attack the body.
- **Non-self antigens** are any that are not the body's own: on pathogens, on transplanted organs, on transfused red blood cells of a different blood group, and on toxins. These are recognised as foreign and trigger a response.

## The primary immune response

Each lymphocyte has receptors of **one shape only**. Among the millions of lymphocytes, a few will have receptors complementary to any given antigen. The response is about **finding** those few and **multiplying** them.

### Who does what

| Cell | Role in the primary response |
|---|---|
| Macrophage | Engulfs the pathogen; presents its antigens |
| T-helper cell | Binds to the presented antigen; divides; releases **cytokines** that activate matching B cells and T-killer cells |
| B-lymphocyte | Binds the antigen with its receptor; when stimulated by cytokines, divides by mitosis |
| Plasma cell | Formed from B cells; **secretes antibodies** specific to the antigen |
| T-killer cell | Activated by cytokines; binds to infected body cells displaying the antigen and kills them |
| Memory cell | Formed from both B and T clones; remains long after the infection |

### Annotated model answer

*Question: Describe the sequence of events in a primary immune response to a bacterium.* **[6]**

> A macrophage engulfs the bacterium and displays its antigens on its surface **(1)**. A T-helper cell with a complementary receptor binds to the antigen (clonal selection) **(1)** and divides by mitosis **(1)**. It releases cytokines, which stimulate B-lymphocytes with complementary receptors to divide **(1)**. Many of these B cells become plasma cells, which secrete antibodies specific to the antigen **(1)**. Some B and T cells become memory cells **(1)**.

Notice that each sentence names a cell and the action it performs. Answers that describe "the immune system" doing things without naming the cell lose marks.

### Why it is slow

Days pass before antibody levels rise because there are very few matching lymphocytes at first; they must be selected, then divide many times, and B cells must then differentiate into plasma cells. Meanwhile the pathogen multiplies, which is why symptoms usually appear in a first infection.

Plasma cells are adapted for antibody secretion: they have extensive **rough endoplasmic reticulum** to make the protein, a large **Golgi apparatus** to modify and package it, and many **mitochondria** to supply ATP.

## Memory cells and the secondary response

Memory cells are **long-lived** and far more numerous than the original matching lymphocytes. If the same antigen enters again:

- memory B cells divide quickly and become plasma cells, so antibodies appear sooner;
- memory T cells quickly form T-helper and T-killer cells;
- more antibody is produced, and it stays at a higher level for longer.

The pathogen is usually destroyed before it can multiply enough to cause symptoms. The person is **immune**, and because memory cells last, the immunity is **long-term**.

### Worked example: a simple growth model

Assume (for this model only) that matching cells double every 12 hours and that 100 000 plasma cells are needed to control the infection. In a first infection there are 10 matching B cells; after it, 1000 memory B cells.

```
primary:   10 × 2ⁿ = 100 000 → 2ⁿ = 10 000 → n = log 10 000 ÷ log 2 = 13.3 doublings
           13.3 × 12 h = 160 h ≈ 6.6 days
secondary: 1000 × 2ⁿ = 100 000 → 2ⁿ = 100 → n = 6.6 doublings
           6.6 × 12 h = 80 h ≈ 3.3 days
```

Starting with 100 times more matching cells halves the time needed, even before counting the time saved because memory cells do not need to be selected and activated from scratch. The model is simplified, but it shows why the number of memory cells is the key idea.

## Common mistakes

- Saying phagocytes are specific, or that lymphocytes engulf pathogens.
- Saying B cells secrete antibodies: **plasma cells** do.
- Saying T-killer cells kill bacteria directly in the blood; they kill **infected body cells** displaying the antigen.
- Writing that memory cells "remember" the antigen; they **recognise** it because their receptors are complementary.
- Omitting cytokines when describing how T-helper cells activate B cells.
- Calling the antigen "the pathogen"; the antigen is a molecule on or from the pathogen.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Describe** (phagocytes or primary response): sequence of steps, each naming the cell and its action.
- **Explain** (antigen; memory cells): definition plus reason, or cause plus effect.
- **State** (self vs non-self): one clear contrast.
- **Compare** (primary vs secondary): use matching terms: speed, amount of antibody, duration, symptoms.
