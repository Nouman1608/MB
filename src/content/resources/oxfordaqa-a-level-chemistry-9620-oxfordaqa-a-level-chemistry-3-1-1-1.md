This guide goes deeper into **3.1.1.1 Fundamental particles**, the opening subtopic of section 3.1.1 Atomic structure in the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, assessed in Unit 1 and available again in the synoptic Unit 5. The subtopic is short, but every later idea in the course, from isotopes to bonding, assumes you can describe the particles in an atom and say what each one contributes to charge and mass.

When you have read the guide, condense it with the [fundamental particles revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-1-1-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [fundamental particles practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-1-1-practice/). For the whole of section 3.1.1, including mass spectrometry and ionisation energies, use the [atomic structure study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-atomic-structure/).

## What the specification asks for

The subtopic has three statements:

- appreciate that knowledge and understanding of atomic structure has evolved over time
- protons, neutrons and electrons: relative charge and relative mass
- an atom consists of a nucleus containing protons and neutrons surrounded by electrons.

Nothing here involves calculation in the way that 3.1.1.2 does, but questions often ask you to *use* the particle data: to work out the overall charge of a species, to explain how a beam of particles behaves in an electric field, or to link an experimental observation to a change in the model of the atom.

## The three particles

| Particle | Location | Relative charge | Relative mass |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proton | nucleus | +1 | 1 |
| Neutron | nucleus | 0 | 1 |
| Electron | around the nucleus, in shells | −1 | about 1/1840 |

### Why "relative" values?

The actual mass of a proton is about 1.673 × 10⁻²⁷ kg and its charge is about 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. Numbers this small are awkward, so chemists compare the particles with one another. The proton is given a relative mass of 1 and a relative charge of +1; the other particles are expressed on the same scales. The neutron's actual mass is very slightly greater than the proton's, but the difference is far too small to matter at this level, so both are given a relative mass of 1. The electron's charge is equal in size to the proton's but opposite in sign, so its relative charge is −1.

The electron's relative mass is the one students most often get wrong. It is not zero. The ratio of proton mass to electron mass is about 1836, which is why you will see the electron's relative mass written as 1/1836 or rounded to about 1/1840. Either form is acceptable; "negligible" on its own is too vague when a question asks for a value.

### What each particle contributes

- **Charge.** Only protons and electrons carry charge. In a neutral atom the number of electrons equals the number of protons, so the charges cancel. An ion forms when this balance is broken: losing electrons leaves a positive ion; gaining electrons gives a negative ion. The nucleus itself is always positive, with a relative charge equal to its number of protons.
- **Mass.** Almost all the mass of an atom is in the nucleus, because protons and neutrons are each roughly 1840 times heavier than an electron. This is why the mass number counts only protons and neutrons.
- **Size.** The nucleus is tiny compared with the whole atom; most of the atom's volume is space occupied by the electrons.

### Worked example 1: how much mass do electrons carry?

**Question.** A carbon-12 atom has 6 protons, 6 neutrons and 6 electrons. Use relative masses to estimate the percentage of the atom's mass that is due to its electrons.

**Method.**

1. Mass of nucleus = 6 × 1 + 6 × 1 = 12.
2. Mass of electrons = 6 × (1/1836) = 0.00327.
3. Percentage = 0.00327 / (12 + 0.00327) × 100 = **0.027%**.

**Comment.** Less than three hundredths of one per cent of the mass comes from electrons. That is the quantitative reason the mass number ignores them, and why the mass of an ion is taken to be the same as the mass of the atom it came from.

## How the model of the atom developed

The specification asks you to *appreciate* that understanding has evolved. In an exam this usually means linking a piece of evidence to the change it forced. Learn the sequence as cause and effect rather than a list of names.

| Stage | Model | Evidence that led to it, or problem it left |
|---|---|---|
| Early 1800s, John Dalton | Atoms as tiny, solid, indivisible spheres; each element has its own kind of atom | Explained fixed combining ratios in compounds, but had no internal structure |
| 1897, J. J. Thomson | Discovered the electron from cathode-ray experiments; proposed the "plum pudding" model of electrons embedded in a sphere of positive charge | Atoms are neutral, so if they contain negative electrons they must also contain positive charge |
| 1909 to 1911, Ernest Rutherford, with Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden | Nuclear model: a tiny, dense, positive nucleus with electrons around it | Alpha particles fired at thin gold foil mostly passed straight through, but a few were deflected through large angles |
| 1913, Niels Bohr | Electrons occupy fixed energy levels (shells) | Elements emit light only at particular frequencies, so electron energies must be fixed |
| 1932, James Chadwick | Discovered the neutron | The mass of most nuclei was more than the mass of their protons alone |
| Later refinement | Electrons in sub-shells and orbitals | Patterns in ionisation energies (see 3.1.1.3) cannot be explained by shells alone |

Two general points earn credit in "evolved over time" questions:

1. **A model is kept until evidence appears that it cannot explain.** The plum pudding model predicted that alpha particles would pass through gold foil with only small deflections. Large deflections contradicted it, so it was replaced.
2. **Each new model kept what still worked.** The nuclear model kept Thomson's electrons; Bohr kept Rutherford's nucleus; the orbital model kept Bohr's idea of fixed energy levels.

### Worked example 2: annotated model answer

**Question.** Explain why the discovery of the neutron was needed to complete the nuclear model of the atom. (3 marks)

**Model answer.**

"A helium nucleus has a relative charge of +2, so it contains two protons ✔. However, its relative mass is about 4, which is twice the mass of two protons ✔. The extra mass must come from particles in the nucleus that have mass but no charge; these are neutrons, which have a relative mass of 1 and a relative charge of 0 ✔."

**Why it scores.** It uses a specific nucleus as evidence, compares charge with mass, and states the properties of the particle that resolves the mismatch. An answer that just says "neutrons add mass" misses the link to the evidence.

## Using particle data in unfamiliar contexts

### Overall charge of a species

Add the charges: each proton +1, each electron −1, each neutron 0. A species with 26 protons, 30 neutrons and 23 electrons has a charge of 26 − 23 = +3. Neutrons never affect the charge, so a common trap is to include them.

### Particles in an electric field

A favourite unfamiliar context sends beams of the three particles, at the same speed, between two oppositely charged plates.

- The **neutron** beam is not deflected, because neutrons have no charge.
- The **proton** beam bends towards the negative plate.
- The **electron** beam bends towards the positive plate, and is deflected much more than the proton beam, because its charge is the same size but its mass is about 1840 times smaller.

Always give both parts of the reason: the direction depends on the sign of the charge; the amount of deflection depends on the mass (for equal charge size).

## Common mistakes

- Giving the electron's relative mass as 0, or its relative charge as 0.
- Placing electrons in the nucleus, or describing the nucleus as "containing all the particles".
- Saying the neutron has a charge of −1, or that it was discovered by the gold foil experiment.
- Describing the gold foil results without the conclusions: "most went through" needs "so most of the atom is empty space"; "a few bounced back" needs "so the positive charge and mass are concentrated in a small nucleus".
- Including neutrons when working out the charge of an ion.
- Writing "atoms are made of protons, neutrons and electrons" for hydrogen-1, which has no neutrons.

## Exam technique for OxfordAQA command words

- **State** or **Give**: a short fact is enough, such as "relative mass 1/1840". No explanation is needed.
- **Describe**: say what happens or what something is like, in order. For the nuclear model: a small, positive nucleus containing protons and neutrons, with electrons around it.
- **Explain**: give a reason that links cause to effect, usually with "because" or "so". In model-history questions, every observation needs its conclusion.
- **Suggest**: apply the particle data to a context you have not met, such as the deflection of an unfamiliar ion, and give a reason based on charge or mass.
- **Deduce**: work something out from information in the question, such as the charge of a species from its numbers of protons and electrons.

Keep to the specification's wording. "Relative charge" and "relative mass" are the terms examiners look for, and "nucleus" is preferred to "centre of the atom".
