These questions cover **3.1.6.3 Effect of temperature on reaction rate** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, examined in Unit 2. Section A has short recall and application questions; Section B has longer, structured questions. All experimental results and percentages are illustrative values invented for these questions. If a point is unfamiliar, read the [temperature and rate study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-6-3/) or the [temperature and rate revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-6-3-revision-notes/) first.

## Questions

### Section A

**1.** In a reaction, the concentration of product C rises from 0 to 0.036 mol dm⁻³ in the first 45 s. Calculate the mean rate of formation of C, with its unit. **[2]**

**2.** A rate is quoted as 1.8 × 10⁻² mol dm⁻³ min⁻¹. Convert it into mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹. **[1]**

**3.** At 25 °C a reaction takes 75 s to reach a fixed end point; at 35 °C it takes 30 s. Calculate the relative rate (1/t) at each temperature and the factor by which the rate increases. **[2]**

**4.** State the effect of lowering the temperature on the rate of a reaction, and give the main reason. **[2]**

**5.** State two effects that increasing the temperature has on the molecules in a reacting gas. **[2]**

**6.** A student explains the effect of temperature by writing only: "The particles move faster, so they collide more often." Explain why this answer is incomplete. **[2]**

### Section B

**7.** Marble chips (calcium carbonate) react with excess hydrochloric acid, giving off carbon dioxide. A student measures the loss in mass in the first 60 s at three temperatures.

| Temperature / °C | 20 | 30 | 40 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Loss in mass in 60 s / g | 0.33 | 0.62 | 1.18 |

**(a)** Calculate the mean rate of mass loss, in g s⁻¹, at each temperature. **[2]**

**(b)** Describe the pattern in the rates. **[1]**

**(c)** State two variables the student must keep constant. **[2]**

**(d)** Explain the pattern in terms of collisions and energy. **[3]**

**8.** Use a Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution to explain why a small increase in temperature can lead to a large increase in the rate of a gas reaction. Include a labelled sketch. **[6]**

**9.** For a gas reaction, 0.050 % of collisions have energy ≥ Ea at 400 K and 0.090 % at 410 K. Over the same change the collision frequency rises by about 1.2 %.

**(a)** Calculate the factor by which the proportion of collisions with energy ≥ Ea increases. **[1]**

**(b)** State which of the two changes is mainly responsible for the increase in rate. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why the rate increases by roughly 1.8 times rather than by about 1.2 %. **[1]**

**10.** A carton of milk sours in about 2 days at 25 °C but takes about 10 days in a refrigerator at 5 °C.

**(a)** Calculate how many times faster the souring is at 25 °C, treating the time to sour as the time for a fixed amount of reaction. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why keeping milk cold slows the souring reactions. **[3]**

**11.** Each statement is incorrect. Correct it.

**(a)** "Heating a reaction lowers its activation energy." **[1]**

**(b)** "Heating a gas increases the number of molecules, so the area under the Maxwell–Boltzmann curve gets bigger." **[1]**

**(c)** "The rate of a reaction is the time it takes to finish." **[1]**

**12.** Hydrogen peroxide decomposes with a catalyst at 30 °C. The volume of oxygen collected is measured.

| Time / s | 0 | 20 | 40 | 60 | 80 | 100 | 120 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Volume of O₂ / cm³ | 0 | 18 | 32 | 42 | 48 | 50 | 50 |

**(a)** Calculate the mean rate of oxygen production over the first 40 s. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the mean rate between 40 s and 80 s. **[1]**

**(c)** The experiment is repeated at 40 °C with the same amounts of every substance. Describe how the volume–time graph would differ, and why the final volume is the same. **[2]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** 0.036 ÷ 45 [1]; = **8.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹** [1] **[2]**

**2.** 1.8 × 10⁻² ÷ 60 = **3.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹** [1] **[1]**

**3.** 1/75 = 0.0133 s⁻¹ and 1/30 = 0.0333 s⁻¹ [1]; the rate increases by 75 ÷ 30 = **2.5 times** [1] **[2]**

**4.** The rate decreases [1]; a much smaller proportion of molecules have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy [1] **[2]**

**5.** The molecules move faster, so they collide slightly more often [1]; a much greater proportion of the molecules have energy ≥ Ea [1] **[2]**

**6.** More frequent collisions account for only a small part of the increase [1]; the answer must say that a much greater proportion of collisions have energy ≥ Ea, so more collisions are successful [1] **[2]**

### Section B

**7. (a)** 20 °C: 0.33 ÷ 60 = 0.0055 g s⁻¹; 30 °C: 0.62 ÷ 60 = 0.0103 g s⁻¹ [1]; 40 °C: 1.18 ÷ 60 = 0.0197 g s⁻¹ [1]

**(b)** The rate roughly doubles for each 10 °C rise [1]

**(c)** The concentration and volume of the acid [1]; the mass and size of the marble chips (their surface area) [1]

**(d)** At higher temperature the particles have more energy on average [1]; a much greater proportion of collisions between acid particles and the marble have energy ≥ Ea [1]; so there are more successful collisions per second (collisions are also slightly more frequent) [1] **[8]**

**8.** Sketch with axes labelled energy and number of molecules, and two curves starting at the origin [1]; higher-temperature curve with a lower peak shifted to the right, crossing the other curve, with similar area [1]; Ea marked to the right of both peaks, with the area beyond it larger for the higher temperature [1]; at the higher temperature a much greater proportion of molecules have energy ≥ Ea, because Ea is in the tail and the area beyond it grows by a large factor [1]; so many more collisions per second are successful [1]; collision frequency rises only slightly, so this is the minor effect [1] **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* "More molecules have the activation energy" is not precise enough for the fourth mark; write "energy equal to or greater than Ea".

**9. (a)** 0.090 ÷ 0.050 = **1.8** [1]

**(b)** The increase in the proportion of collisions with energy ≥ Ea [1]

**(c)** The rate depends on the number of successful collisions per second; the proportion of successful collisions rises 1.8-fold, far more than the 1.2 % rise in collision frequency [1] **[3]**

**10. (a)** 10 ÷ 2 = **5 times** faster [1]

**(b)** At the lower temperature the molecules have less energy on average [1]; a much smaller proportion of collisions have energy ≥ Ea [1]; so there are far fewer successful collisions per second in the souring reactions [1] **[4]**

**11. (a)** Heating does not change Ea; it increases the proportion of molecules with energy ≥ Ea [1]

**(b)** Heating does not change the number of molecules; the area stays the same and the curve shifts to higher energy [1]

**(c)** Rate is the change in concentration (or amount) of a reactant or product per unit time [1] **[3]**

**12. (a)** 32 ÷ 40 = **0.80 cm³ s⁻¹** [1]

**(b)** (48 − 32) ÷ 40 = **0.40 cm³ s⁻¹** [1]

**(c)** The curve is steeper at the start and levels off sooner [1]; the final volume is still 50 cm³ because the same amount of hydrogen peroxide decomposes; temperature changes the rate, not the amount of product [1] **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Rates given without units, or as negative numbers.
- Treating 1/t as an absolute rate in mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹.
- Explaining the temperature effect only by more frequent collisions.
- "More molecules have the activation energy" instead of "energy ≥ Ea".
- Hotter Maxwell–Boltzmann curves with a taller peak or more area.
- Claiming heating changes Ea or the amount of product formed.
