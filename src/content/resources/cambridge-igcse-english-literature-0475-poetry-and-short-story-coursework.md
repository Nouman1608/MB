**This guide covers coursework assignments based on poetry or short stories in Component 5.** The
syllabus states: "If poetry or short stories are used for an assignment, candidates must cover a
minimum of two poems or stories. Candidates are not required to compare poems or stories within the
assignment, as it is assumed that the assignment is based on the study of a wider selection of poems
or stories broadly equivalent to a poetry or short stories set text." The
[Component 5 coursework study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-english-literature-component-5-coursework-study-guide/)
covers the portfolio as a whole; this page explains how to build an assignment of 600–1200 words that
discusses two or more short texts well.

## What the rule means

Three ideas sit inside the syllabus wording:

- **A minimum of two.** An assignment on a single poem or story does not meet the requirement,
  however good it is.
- **No comparison required.** You may discuss each piece in turn. Comparison is allowed, and can
  help, but it is not demanded.
- **A wider selection behind it.** The two or more pieces you write about stand for a body of study
  "broadly equivalent to a poetry or short stories set text". Your teacher plans the course so that
  you read widely; the assignment then focuses on a few pieces from that reading.

The texts must also have been "originally written in English" and be "of a quality appropriate for
study at Cambridge IGCSE". Translations do not qualify.

## Choosing the pieces

Choose pieces that share a focus but treat it differently. A shared focus gives the assignment a
single argument; differences give it depth. Useful links include:

- a theme approached from different angles (memory as comfort and as burden);
- a type of speaker or narrator (children, outsiders, people looking back);
- a recurring method (a final twist, a single extended image, a dramatic monologue).

Two pieces usually suit 600–1200 words best. Three are possible, but each then gets less space, and
the analysis can become thin.

## Framing the title

The syllabus requires that each title "must be phrased to allow scope for the assessment of all four
assessment objectives". A good title for this kind of assignment:

- names the focus (the theme, the speaker, the method);
- invites analysis of how the writer achieves effects (AO3);
- invites a personal judgement (AO4);
- names the pieces, or says "two poems you have studied", so the scope is clear.

Weak: "Write about two poems by [poet]." There is no focus and nothing to argue.
Stronger: "How do two poems you have studied by [poet] present memory as both comforting and
painful?"

## Structuring without comparison

Two structures both work.

**Sequential.** Introduction; two or three paragraphs on the first piece; two or three on the second;
conclusion. Simple and clear. To avoid two separate mini-essays, open the second half with a sentence
that connects it to the first ("Where the first poem finds comfort in the past, the second…"), and
let the conclusion draw both together.

**Integrated.** Introduction; paragraphs organised by aspects of the focus, each discussing both
pieces. This produces a more developed argument, but demands control; it is a form of comparison,
which the syllabus allows without requiring.

## Worked example: two original poems

The two poems below were written for this guide. Imagine them as two pieces from a wider collection
studied in class.

> **Grandmother's Coat** *(original poem)*
>
> It hangs behind the door and keeps
> the shape of her, the slope of her shoulders,
> the pocket where she kept the sweets.
> I put my hand in. Paper. Warm.

> **Moving Day** *(original poem)*
>
> We left the house the way you leave a room
> you mean to come back to: lights on,
> the radio talking to itself. Ten years.
> I still dream the stairs. I still count them.

**Title:** How do the poets present memory in two poems you have studied?

**Plan (sequential, about 900 words):**
1. *Introduction (100 words):* both poems present memory through physical things; one finds comfort,
   the other an inability to let go.
2. *"Grandmother's Coat" (two paragraphs, about 300 words):* the coat that "keeps / the shape of
   her"; the line break placing "keeps" at the end of the line; the minor sentences "Paper. Warm."
   making memory sensory and immediate.
3. *Linking sentence:* where the coat brings the grandmother close, the second poem shows memory that
   will not stay in the past.
4. *"Moving Day" (two paragraphs, about 300 words):* the simile of leaving "a room / you mean to come
   back to"; the personified radio "talking to itself"; "Ten years." as a minor sentence marking time;
   repetition of "I still" showing memory as habit.
5. *Conclusion (about 150 words):* memory in both is held in objects and places; a personal judgement
   on which presentation is more striking and why.

**Model paragraph (on the second poem):**

> In "Moving Day" the poet presents memory as something that refuses to be left behind. The family
> left the house "the way you leave a room / you mean to come back to", and the line break after
> "room" makes the reader expect a simple departure before the next line reveals an intention, perhaps
> a hope, of return. Leaving the "radio talking to itself" turns the empty house into something still
> alive, as though it carries on without them. The repetition of "I still" in the final line, "I still
> dream the stairs. I still count them.", is what makes the poem haunting for me: after "Ten years",
> memory has become a habit of the body, as automatic as counting steps in the dark.

## Quotation and referencing

The syllabus asks centres to teach "the academic conventions governing quotation and reference to the
work of others". In practice:

- Quote short phrases and embed them in your sentences.
- Use line breaks marked with "/" when quoting across lines of poetry.
- Give the title of each poem or story in quotation marks.
- If you use any other source, reference it fully: "author, date and page number", and for a website
  "the website address and the date the website was accessed".

## Word budget

The word count includes quotations but excludes references and bibliography. With two pieces in about
900–1200 words, aim for an equal share for each, plus a short introduction and conclusion. The
syllabus warns that candidates "may disadvantage themselves if their assignments are significantly
over or under the word count guidance".

## Common mistakes

- **One piece only.** It does not meet the minimum of two.
- **Two mini-essays.** No link between the halves and no shared argument.
- **Forced comparison.** Weak "similarly… however…" sentences added for their own sake.
- **Three or four pieces** in 1,000 words, each treated thinly.
- **A title with no focus**, giving no scope for argument or judgement.
- **A translated text**, which does not meet the requirement that texts were originally written in
  English.
- **Ignoring form.** Line breaks, stanza shape and endings are central in short texts.
