This guide goes deeper into section 5.4 of the Cambridge IGCSE Economics 0455 syllabus for examination in 2026. Section 5.4.1 asks for the causes and impacts of differences between countries in seven areas: income; productivity; population growth; the size of the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors; saving and investment; education; and healthcare. Economics 0455 is not tiered. Every country and figure below is invented.

The combined unit on [population and differences in development](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-population-and-differences-in-development-between/) sets out each factor briefly. This page shows how the factors feed into one another, works through new calculations on sector structure and health, and models a full evaluation. Afterwards, use the [development differences revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-differences-in-economic-development-between-countries-revision-notes/) and the [development differences practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-differences-in-economic-development-between-countries-practice/).

## What "economic development" means

**Economic development** is a sustained improvement in the living standards of a country's people: higher real income, but also better health, education and opportunities. **Economic growth** (a rise in real GDP) is part of it, but a country can grow without developing if the extra output reaches few people or damages health and the environment.

For every factor, the syllabus wants two things: the **cause** of the difference between countries and its **impact**. A strong answer gives both and links them in a chain.

Use the table to describe the pattern: as income rises, productivity rises, population growth slows, workers move out of the primary sector, schooling lengthens and people live longer. Country L has the highest saving rate, which suggests it is investing heavily to catch up.

## The seven factors: cause and impact

### 1. Income

- **Cause of the difference:** differences in productivity, resources, capital and the types of goods produced.
- **Impact:** low income limits spending on food, housing and services, limits saving, and gives the government a small tax base, so it can fund few schools, clinics or roads.

### 2. Productivity

- **Cause:** capital per worker, technology, skills, health and the sector in which people work.
- **Impact:** output per worker sets the wages firms can pay. Low productivity means low wages and low income.

### 3. Population growth

- **Cause:** high birth rates in low-income countries (see section 5.3) and falling death rates.
- **Impact:** if population grows almost as fast as output, income per head barely rises. A young, fast-growing population also needs more schools and, later, more jobs. Slow growth in high-income countries brings ageing instead.

### 4. Size of the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors

- **Primary:** farming, fishing, forestry, mining. **Secondary:** manufacturing and construction. **Tertiary:** services.
- **Cause:** low-income countries have little capital and many people farming small plots; as capital and skills grow, workers move into manufacturing and services.
- **Impact:** output per worker is usually lowest in small-scale farming, so a large primary sector holds down average productivity. Countries relying on primary exports also face large swings in world prices.

**Worked example 1: moving workers between sectors.** Country K's output per worker (invented) is 2,000 in the primary sector, 8,000 in the secondary sector and 6,000 in the tertiary sector.

1. Average output per worker now = (0.65 × 2,000) + (0.10 × 8,000) + (0.25 × 6,000) = 1,300 + 800 + 1,500 = **3,600**.
2. Suppose 20% of all workers move from the primary to the secondary sector, giving shares of 45 / 30 / 25.
3. New average = (0.45 × 2,000) + (0.30 × 8,000) + (0.25 × 6,000) = 900 + 2,400 + 1,500 = **4,800**.
4. Increase = (4,800 − 3,600) ÷ 3,600 × 100 = **33.3%**.

No worker became more productive in their own sector; average productivity rose because labour moved to where each worker produces more. This is why a shift in sector structure is part of development.

### 5. Saving and investment

- **Cause:** with low income, most is spent on necessities, leaving little to save. Weak banks and low confidence also reduce saving.
- **Impact:** saving provides funds for investment in machinery, roads and power. Little investment means little capital per worker, so productivity and income stay low. This is the cycle that keeps low-income countries poor: low income → low saving → low investment → low productivity → low income.

### 6. Education

- **Cause:** low incomes and tax revenue mean fewer schools and teachers; children may work instead of attending.
- **Impact:** fewer skills, so lower productivity and less ability to use new technology; fewer people qualified to start firms or work in higher-value industries.

### 7. Healthcare

- **Cause:** limited funding, few trained staff, poor sanitation and clean water.
- **Impact:** shorter lives, more days lost to sickness, weaker learning by children who are often ill.

**Worked example 2: days lost to illness.** In Country K (invented) a worker is available for 250 days a year but loses 15 days to illness, so works 235 days. Better healthcare cuts this to 5 days, so the worker works 245 days.

Increase in days worked = (245 − 235) ÷ 235 × 100 = **4.3%**. If output per day is unchanged, output rises by the same 4.3%, before counting any gain from healthier workers producing more each day.

## How the factors connect

```
Low income -> low saving -> low investment -> low productivity -> low income
Low income -> little tax revenue -> few schools and clinics -> poor skills and health -> low productivity
Low income -> children as labour and old-age support -> high birth rate -> rapid population growth -> slow rise in income per head
```

Breaking any link helps the others. Investment raises productivity, which raises income and saving; better education raises productivity and tends to lower birth rates. In an answer, show that you understand factors reinforce each other rather than acting alone.

## Annotated model answer

**Question (original):** Discuss whether improving education is the most important way for a low-income country to increase its economic development. **[8]**

> Education raises workers' skills, so each worker produces more and can earn higher wages. Higher productivity also lets firms adopt new technology and move into manufacturing and services, where output per worker is higher. *(Chain from cause to development.)*
>
> Education also has wider effects: educated women tend to have fewer children, slowing population growth, so income per head rises faster. *(A second, developed benefit.)*
>
> However, education takes many years to affect productivity, and skilled workers need capital and jobs to use their skills. Without investment, graduates may be unemployed or emigrate. *(Limits.)*
>
> Healthcare may matter more where disease is common, because sick children cannot learn and sick workers cannot work. Investment in power and roads may be needed first for firms to grow. *(Alternatives.)*
>
> Education is likely to be the most important long-term factor, because it raises productivity and slows population growth. In the short term, and where health is very poor, healthcare and investment may matter more. The best results come when they are improved together. *(Judgement with conditions.)*

## Exam technique

- **Identify** or **State:** pick a factor from data, such as "Country K has the largest primary sector".
- **Explain:** give a cause and an impact, joined by "so" or "which means".
- **Analyse:** build a chain of at least three links.
- **Discuss:** weigh at least two factors and reach a judgement that says what it depends on.
- **Calculate:** show weighted averages and percentage changes step by step.

## Common mistakes

- Treating growth and development as the same thing.
- Describing a factor without giving its impact.
- Saying a large primary sector "causes poverty" without the link through productivity or price swings.
- Ignoring how factors reinforce one another.
- Comparing countries on total GDP instead of per-head figures.
