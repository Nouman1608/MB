## Political factors: recall

- Three levels: **local** (planning, licensing, local charges), **national** (tax, minimum wage, regulation, spending, trade policy), **international** (trade deals, tariffs, sanctions, standards).
- **Uncertainty**: rules may change; firms delay investment and hiring.
- **Instability**: the system itself is disrupted; trade can stop because contracts, borders and payments become unreliable.
- **Stability** benefits: long-term planning, cheaper borrowing, confident consumers, inward investment.

## Economic factors: recall

**Five key indicators:** GDP growth, inflation, unemployment, interest rates, exchange rates.

**UK taxes.**

| Direct (paid on income or profit) | Indirect (added to spending) |
|---|---|
| Income tax | VAT |
| National Insurance contributions | Excise duties (fuel, alcohol, tobacco) |
| Corporation tax | |

Business rates are a tax on non-domestic property.

**Government spending.** Current (wages, benefits, running costs) and capital (roads, rail, buildings). Purposes: public services, redistribution, supporting growth.

**Subsidy.** A payment from government that lowers a firm's costs or encourages an activity. Benefits: lower costs and break-even, room to cut price or invest, survival in hard times. Risks: dependence, withdrawal, conditions attached.

**Policies.**
- Monetary: interest rates, set by the Bank of England.
- Fiscal: taxation and government spending.
- Supply-side: raising productive capacity through training, infrastructure, deregulation and investment incentives.

**Interest rates and exchange rates.** Higher rates → overseas savers buy pounds → pound appreciates → exports dearer abroad, imports cheaper at home. Lower rates tend to do the reverse.

**Business cycle.** Boom → downturn (recession) → slump → recovery. Recession is commonly defined as two successive quarters of falling real GDP.

## Social and technological factors: recall

- **Social:** population size and age, migration, household make-up, lifestyle, attitudes, fashion. Usually slow and persistent.
- **Technological:** new products, automation and robotics, CAD/CAM, online selling, stock and customer databases, cloud systems.
- Hardware and software: **advantages** are speed, accuracy, lower unit labour cost, better information; **disadvantages** are purchase, training and upgrade costs, breakdown and cyber risk, job losses, resistance from staff.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Political uncertainty | Doubt about future government policy |
| Political instability | Disruption to the system of government itself |
| Direct tax | Tax paid by the person or firm on whose income or profit it falls |
| Indirect tax | Tax added to the price of goods and services |
| Subsidy | Government payment that reduces a firm's costs |
| Monetary policy | Changes in interest rates to influence spending |
| Fiscal policy | Changes in taxation and government spending |
| Supply-side policy | Measures to raise the economy's productive capacity |
| GDP | The value of all output produced in a country over a period |
| Business cycle | The recurring pattern of rising and falling economic activity |
| Appreciation | A rise in the value of a currency against others |

## Quick calculations

- **Interest cost**: loan × rate. £300,000 at 4% = £12,000; at 5.5% = £16,500.
- **Exchange rate conversion**: foreign price = sterling price × rate. A £12,000 order at $1.30 = $15,600. If the pound falls to $1.20, keeping the dollar price of $15,600 earns 15,600 ÷ 1.20 = £13,000.
- **Technology payback**: cost ÷ annual saving. £24,000 ÷ £9,600 = 2.5 years.

## Who is hit hardest in a downturn?

- Sellers of luxuries and durable goods, whose purchases can be postponed.
- Firms with high fixed costs or high gearing.
- Businesses depending on credit-funded purchases.
- Possible gainers: discount retailers, repair services, low-cost brands.

## Exam traps

- Calling VAT or excise duty a direct tax.
- Stating that higher interest rates only raise loan costs; they also cut customer demand and strengthen the pound.
- Saying a weaker pound is bad for all firms; it helps exporters.
- Writing "the economy" as a factor without naming the indicator or policy.
- Ignoring stakeholders other than owners when the question asks about stakeholders.
- Evaluating with no judgement of importance compared with other factors.

## Self-test

1. Give one local, one national and one international political decision that affects a business.
2. Distinguish political uncertainty from political instability.
3. Name two direct and two indirect UK taxes.
4. Distinguish current from capital government spending.
5. Who sets UK interest rates?
6. Explain the link from higher UK interest rates to a firm's exports.
7. Name the four phases of the business cycle.
8. Why does a furniture retailer suffer more than a supermarket in a downturn?
9. A firm with a £400,000 variable loan sees its rate rise from 3% to 4.5%. What is the rise in annual interest?
10. A £30,000 robot saves £12,000 a year. What is its payback?
11. Give one social factor and one effect it could have on a gym chain.
12. Give one stakeholder who may lose from new automation and one who may gain.

### Answers

1. Local: refusal of planning permission. National: a rise in corporation tax. International: a new tariff on imports.
2. Uncertainty is doubt about future policy while the system works; instability is disruption to the system itself, such as unrest.
3. Direct: income tax, corporation tax (or National Insurance). Indirect: VAT, excise duty.
4. Current spending covers day-to-day costs such as wages and benefits; capital spending is long-term investment such as roads.
5. The Bank of England.
6. Higher rates attract overseas savings, the pound appreciates, so exports become dearer abroad and sales may fall.
7. Boom, downturn (recession), slump, recovery.
8. Furniture is a durable purchase that can be postponed; food is a necessity.
9. £18,000 − £12,000 = £6,000.
10. 30,000 ÷ 12,000 = 2.5 years.
11. An ageing population: more demand for low-impact classes and daytime sessions.
12. Loses: production staff whose hours are cut. Gains: shareholders, if unit costs fall and profit rises.
