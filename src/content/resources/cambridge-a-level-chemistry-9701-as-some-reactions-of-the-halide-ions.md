This guide covers subtopic **11.3, Some reactions of the halide ions**, from Topic 11, Group 17, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is AS Level content.

The [Group 17: The Halogens](/resources/as-group-17-the-halogens/) guide introduces both halide tests. This page goes further into 11.3: why the reducing power trend runs the way it does, why the silver halides respond differently to ammonia, and, because the syllabus asks for **balanced chemical equations** with concentrated sulfuric acid, how to build each of those equations from half-equations rather than memorising them. Condensed notes are in the [11.3 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-as-some-reactions-of-the-halide-ions-revision-notes/), and the [11.3 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-as-some-reactions-of-the-halide-ions-practice/) test it.

## What 11.3 asks you to do

1. Describe the relative reactivity of halide ions as **reducing agents**.
2. Describe and explain the reactions of halide ions with:
   - (a) aqueous silver ions followed by aqueous ammonia (the formation and formula of the complex formed with ammonia is **not required**);
   - (b) concentrated sulfuric acid, **to include balanced chemical equations**.

## Halide ions as reducing agents

A halide ion acts as a reducing agent by losing electrons. It is itself oxidised:

2X⁻ → X₂ + 2e⁻

Reducing power **increases** down the group: I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻.

- Down the group the ion is **larger**, so the outer electrons are further from the nucleus.
- There is **more shielding** by inner shells.
- So the outer electrons are held less strongly and are **lost more easily**.

This is the mirror image of the halogens' oxidising power (11.2). The strongest oxidising agent, Cl₂, forms the weakest reducing agent, Cl⁻; the weakest oxidising agent, I₂, forms the strongest reducing agent, I⁻.

## Silver ions, then ammonia

### The method

1. Dissolve the sample in water.
2. Add **dilute nitric acid**. This removes ions such as carbonate that would also give a precipitate with silver ions. Nitric acid is used because it adds no halide ions.
3. Add **aqueous silver nitrate** and note the colour of the precipitate.
4. Add **aqueous ammonia** to the precipitate, first dilute, then concentrated.

### The results

| Halide | Precipitate | Ionic equation | Dilute NH₃(aq) | Concentrated NH₃(aq) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cl⁻ | White, AgCl | Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s) | Dissolves | Dissolves |
| Br⁻ | Cream, AgBr | Ag⁺(aq) + Br⁻(aq) → AgBr(s) | Does not dissolve (at most slightly) | Dissolves |
| I⁻ | Yellow, AgI | Ag⁺(aq) + I⁻(aq) → AgI(s) | Does not dissolve | Does not dissolve |

### Explaining the ammonia results

The solubility of the silver halides in water **decreases** from AgCl to AgI. In each case a very small amount of the solid is in equilibrium with its ions in solution:

AgX(s) ⇌ Ag⁺(aq) + X⁻(aq)

Ammonia combines with Ag⁺(aq) ions to form a soluble complex, which removes Ag⁺ ions from the solution. The equilibrium shifts to the right and more solid dissolves.

- AgCl is the most soluble of the three, so even dilute ammonia removes enough Ag⁺ for the precipitate to dissolve.
- AgBr is less soluble. Only concentrated ammonia lowers [Ag⁺] enough.
- AgI is so insoluble that even concentrated ammonia cannot lower [Ag⁺] enough to dissolve it.

You do not need the formula of the silver–ammonia complex. Describe it as "a soluble complex formed between silver ions and ammonia".

## Halides with concentrated sulfuric acid

### Step 1: acid–base, for every halide

Concentrated sulfuric acid first protonates the halide ion, giving the hydrogen halide. This is **not** a redox reaction. Oxidation numbers do not change.

NaCl + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HCl

NaBr + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HBr

NaI + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HI

All three give **steamy (misty) fumes** of hydrogen halide in moist air.

### Step 2: redox, for bromide and iodide only

Br⁻ and I⁻ are strong enough reducing agents to reduce the sulfur in H₂SO₄ (oxidation number +6). Cl⁻ is not, so chloride stops at step 1.

The sulfur-containing products, with their oxidation numbers and the half-equations that form them:

| Product | Oxidation number of S | Half-equation |
|---|---|---|
| SO₂ | +4 | H₂SO₄ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → SO₂ + 2H₂O |
| S | 0 | H₂SO₄ + 6H⁺ + 6e⁻ → S + 4H₂O |
| H₂S | −2 | H₂SO₄ + 8H⁺ + 8e⁻ → H₂S + 4H₂O |

- **Bromide** reduces sulfur only as far as **SO₂** (+6 → +4).
- **Iodide** reduces sulfur to **SO₂, S and H₂S** (+6 → +4, 0 and −2). The deepest reduction, to H₂S, shows that iodide is the strongest reducing agent.

### Building the balanced equations

**Worked example: bromide.** Combine the oxidation half-equation with the SO₂ half-equation so that the electrons cancel:

2HBr → Br₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻

H₂SO₄ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → SO₂ + 2H₂O

Add, cancelling 2H⁺ and 2e⁻:

**2HBr + H₂SO₄ → Br₂ + SO₂ + 2H₂O**

Check: H 4 = 4; Br 2 = 2; S 1 = 1; O 4 = 4.

Combining step 1 (twice) with step 2 gives an overall equation starting from the solid salt:

**2NaBr + 3H₂SO₄ → 2NaHSO₄ + Br₂ + SO₂ + 2H₂O**

Check: Na 2 = 2; Br 2 = 2; S 3 = 2 + 1; H 6 = 2 + 4; O 12 = 8 + 2 + 2.

**Worked example: iodide to hydrogen sulfide.** Eight electrons must be transferred to reduce one sulfur from +6 to −2, so eight HI are oxidised:

8HI → 4I₂ + 8H⁺ + 8e⁻

H₂SO₄ + 8H⁺ + 8e⁻ → H₂S + 4H₂O

Add: **8HI + H₂SO₄ → 4I₂ + H₂S + 4H₂O**

The same method gives the other two iodide equations:

**6HI + H₂SO₄ → 3I₂ + S + 4H₂O**

**2HI + H₂SO₄ → I₂ + SO₂ + 2H₂O**

The numbers in front of HI (2, 6, 8) are the numbers of electrons needed to reduce one sulfur atom by 2, 6 and 8 oxidation states.

### Observations

| Halide | What you see | Products |
|---|---|---|
| Cl⁻ | Steamy fumes | HCl |
| Br⁻ | Steamy fumes; orange-brown vapour; a colourless, choking gas | HBr, Br₂, SO₂ |
| I⁻ | Some steamy fumes; purple vapour or a black solid; a yellow solid; a smell of bad eggs | HI, I₂, SO₂, S, H₂S |

Very little HI escapes, because most of it is oxidised to iodine as soon as it forms.

This also explains why concentrated sulfuric acid is a poor reagent for preparing HBr or HI: much of the hydrogen halide is oxidised. Concentrated phosphoric(V) acid, which is not an oxidising agent, is used instead.

## Common mistakes

- **Saying "reducing power decreases down the group".** That is the halogens' oxidising power. Halide reducing power increases.
- **Acidifying with hydrochloric acid** before silver nitrate. It adds chloride ions.
- **Describing AgBr as white or yellow.** It is cream.
- **Treating step 1 as redox.** Oxidation numbers do not change.
- **Writing chloride reducing sulfuric acid to SO₂.** Chloride does not reduce it.
- **Giving H₂S as the only product with iodide.** SO₂ and S also form.
- **Unbalanced equations.** Check every element, and that electrons lost equal electrons gained.

## Exam technique: command words in 11.3

- **Describe** (tests): give reagents in order, observations, and ionic equations.
- **Explain** (ammonia results): decreasing solubility of AgX, and ammonia removing Ag⁺ ions.
- **Describe and explain** (sulfuric acid): observations, the acid–base step, how far sulfur is reduced, and the link to reducing power.
- **Construct** (equations): combine half-equations so that electrons cancel, then check atoms.
