These questions practise key question 6.2 of Cambridge IGCSE Sociology (0495), *What are the
patterns of and explanations for crime and deviance?* They follow the six-part (a)–(f) structure
the syllabus describes for each Paper 2 question, followed by extra short questions on the theories.

## Questions

**(a)** Define the terms:
- (i) *status frustration* **[2]**
- (ii) *institutional racism* **[2]**

**(b)** Give **two** reasons why women may commit less recorded crime than men. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **three** reasons why young people may appear more often in crime statistics.
**[6]**

**(d)** Explain **three** reasons why working-class people may be more likely to be convicted of
crime. **[6]**

**(e)** Discuss how interactionists explain crime and deviance. **[8]**

**(f)** Evaluate the view that crime is mainly caused by material deprivation. **[14]**

### Extra practice on theories (6.2.6 and 6.2.7)

**(g)** Explain the Marxist view of crime. **[4]**

**(h)** Explain **one** strength and **one** limitation of strain theory. **[4]**

**(i)** Explain the postmodernist view of crime. **[4]**

## Answers and mark guidance

**(a)(i) [2]** The frustration felt by people, especially working-class young males, who cannot gain
status through legitimate routes such as school, so they seek it through deviant subcultures
(Albert Cohen, 1955) (2).

**(a)(ii) [2]** Racism built into the policies, practices and culture of an organisation, such as a
police force, producing unfair outcomes even without individual intent (2). The Macpherson Report
(1999) applied this term to the Metropolitan Police.

**(b) [2]** Any two: greater social control of girls and women at home and in public (Heidensohn,
1985); socialisation into caring, less aggressive roles; fewer opportunities; chivalry thesis —
lenient treatment means fewer women recorded.

**(c) [6]** Three reasons, 2 marks each:
- **Police targeting / stop and search** — police focus on public spaces where young people gather.
- **Stereotyping and self-fulfilling prophecy** — labelled as troublemakers, some young people
  act out the label.
- **Status frustration and lack of opportunity** — low status and few jobs encourage seeking status
  through deviance.
- **Peer pressure and gang culture** — risk-taking to gain group acceptance.
- **Weaker social control** — less tied to work, marriage and mortgages.

**(d) [6]** Three reasons, 2 marks each:
- **Material and relative deprivation** — poverty and comparing oneself with others create motives.
- **Police targeting** — working-class areas are policed more heavily.
- **Power inequalities** — middle-class white-collar crime is harder to detect and often dealt with
  outside courts.
- **Social exclusion** — lack of stake in society.
- **Inadequate socialisation** (New Right) — underclass families blamed for weak norms.

**(e) [8]** Levels: 7–8 clear, developed and well applied; 4–6 some development; 1–3 basic.
- Deviance is not a quality of the act but a **label** applied by others (Becker, 1963).
- Agents of social control (police, courts, teachers) have power to make labels stick.
- Labels can become a **master status**, leading to a **self-fulfilling prophecy** and deviant
  career.
- **Moral panics** (Stanley Cohen, 1972): media exaggeration creates folk devils and leads to more
  control and possibly more deviance.
- Evaluation: explains why some groups are criminalised, but does not explain why people commit
  the first deviant act, and may neglect victims.

**(f) [14]** Levels: 11–14 balanced, developed, evaluated, conclusion; 7–10 developed but less
balanced; 4–6 limited; 1–3 basic.

*For the view:*
- Statistics show more recorded crime among poorer groups.
- Material deprivation creates motives for property crime.
- Relative deprivation — comparing oneself with richer groups — can motivate crime even where
  absolute poverty is low.
- Marxists argue capitalism's inequality drives crime.
- Social exclusion and lack of opportunity.

*Against the view:*
- White-collar and corporate crimes are committed by the wealthy.
- Most poor people do not commit crime.
- Labelling: statistics reflect policing of the poor rather than real offending.
- Other causes: masculinity, peer and gang culture, inadequate socialisation, status frustration.
- Feminist: gender, not poverty, is the strongest predictor of offending.
- Postmodernism: crime is individual and varied, linked to excitement and consumer desires.

*Conclusion:* deprivation helps explain some property crime but not crime of the powerful; credit a
reasoned judgement.

**(g) [4]** Laws reflect ruling-class interests and protect property (1–2); the working class is
policed and punished more heavily, while crimes of the powerful are under-policed (1–2); capitalism
encourages greed and competition, motivating crime across all classes (1–2). Max 4.

**(h) [4]** Strength (2): explains why working-class people with blocked opportunities may turn to
crime to reach shared goals such as wealth (Merton, 1938). Limitation (2): does not explain
non-utilitarian crime such as vandalism, or crime by the wealthy; assumes everyone shares the same
goals.

**(i) [4]** Postmodernists argue crime is now diverse and individualised, not explained by one big
theory (1–2); consumer culture creates new desires and identities that some pursue through crime
(1–2); new forms of crime such as cyber crime and hate crime reflect a fragmented society (1–2).
Max 4.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between material and relative deprivation?** Material deprivation is
lacking basic resources; relative deprivation is feeling deprived compared with others.

**Do I need named sociologists?** They are not required, but accurate names (Becker, Merton, Albert
Cohen, Heidensohn) add precision and credibility.

**How do I evaluate in (f)?** Set deprivation against at least two alternative explanations and
question the statistics behind the claim.
