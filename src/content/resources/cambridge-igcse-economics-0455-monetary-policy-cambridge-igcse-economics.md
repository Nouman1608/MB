## 4.4.1 Definitions

**Money supply:** the total amount of money in an economy, including notes and coins and bank deposits that can be spent.

**Monetary policy:** the use of changes in interest rates, the money supply and the foreign exchange rate to influence the economy and help achieve the government's macroeconomic aims. In many countries the central bank carries it out, sometimes independently of the government.

Two directions:

| | Expansionary (loose) monetary policy | Contractionary (tight) monetary policy |
|---|---|---|
| Interest rates | Lowered | Raised |
| Money supply | Increased | Reduced |
| Exchange rate | Allowed or encouraged to fall | Allowed or encouraged to rise |
| Aim | Raise spending, output and employment | Reduce spending to control inflation |

## 4.4.2 Measure 1: changes in interest rates

### Five routes from the interest rate to spending

When the central bank raises its rate, commercial banks raise the rates they charge and pay. The effect reaches the economy by several routes:

1. **Borrowing for spending:** loans for cars, furniture and holidays cost more, so households borrow and spend less.
2. **Existing debts:** households and firms with variable-rate loans pay more interest, leaving less to spend.
3. **Saving:** saving earns more, so some households save more and spend less.
4. **Investment:** firms' projects must earn more than the cost of borrowing, so fewer projects go ahead.
5. **Exchange rate:** higher rates attract savings from abroad; foreign savers buy the currency, its value rises, exports become dearer and imports cheaper.

A cut in interest rates works the same routes in reverse.

### How investment responds

**Worked example 1 (invented).** A firm considers four projects, each costing 100,000 and financed by a loan. The yearly return is the extra profit expected each year, before interest.

| Project | Expected yearly return | Goes ahead at 4% interest? | Goes ahead at 7% interest? |
|---|---|---|---|
| A: new delivery van | 9,000 (9%) | Yes | Yes |
| B: new oven | 6,500 (6.5%) | Yes | No |
| C: shop refit | 5,000 (5%) | Yes | No |
| D: website upgrade | 3,000 (3%) | No | No |

At 4%, projects A, B and C go ahead: investment of 300,000. At 7%, only A goes ahead: investment of 100,000. The rise of 3 percentage points cuts this firm's investment by two thirds. Lower investment means fewer orders for machinery and building firms, and less extra capacity for the future.

### How households respond differently

**Worked example 2 (invented).** The interest rate falls from 6% to 4%.

- Household K owes 90,000 on a variable-rate, interest-only home loan: interest falls from 5,400 to 3,600 a year, freeing 1,800 a year (150 a month) to spend.
- Household L has 30,000 in savings: interest income falls from 1,800 to 1,200 a year, a loss of 600.

The cut helps borrowers more than it hurts savers in this example, so spending is likely to rise overall. The size of the effect depends on how much households owe compared with how much they save.

## Measure 2: changes in the money supply

The central bank can increase the money supply, for example by buying government bonds and paying with newly created money, or by allowing commercial banks to keep less in reserve so they can lend more. It can reduce the money supply by doing the opposite.

- **More money and easier credit:** households and firms can borrow more easily, so spending rises; interest rates tend to fall.
- **Risk:** if the money supply rises much faster than the output of goods and services, more money chases the same goods and prices rise.

## Measure 3: changes in the foreign exchange rate

A central bank can influence the exchange rate by changing interest rates, or by buying or selling its own currency in exchange for foreign currency.

**Worked example 3 (invented).** Country P's currency, the P-pound, falls from 1 P-pound = 2.00 Q-dollars to 1 P-pound = 1.60 Q-dollars, a depreciation of 20%.

```
P-made bicycle at 150 P-pounds:
  price abroad before = 150 x 2.00 = 300 Q-dollars
  price abroad after  = 150 x 1.60 = 240 Q-dollars (20% cheaper)
Q-made laptop at 800 Q-dollars:
  price in P before = 800 / 2.00 = 400 P-pounds
  price in P after  = 800 / 1.60 = 500 P-pounds (25% dearer)
```

Exports become cheaper for foreign buyers and imports dearer at home. This can raise export sales, reduce imports, and help output and jobs; but dearer imports, including raw materials and fuel, push up prices in Country P.

## 4.4.3 Matching measures to aims

| Problem | Suitable monetary measure | Chain to the aim |
|---|---|---|
| Recession, high unemployment | Cut interest rates; increase money supply | Cheaper borrowing → more spending and investment → firms produce more and hire more |
| High inflation | Raise interest rates; reduce money supply; allow a higher exchange rate | Less borrowing and spending → less pressure on prices; cheaper imports lower costs |
| Current account deficit | Lower exchange rate | Exports cheaper and imports dearer → exports rise, imports fall |
| Slow growth | Lower interest rates | More investment → more capital → higher output now and greater capacity later |

Monetary policy is a weak tool for **redistribution of income**: rate changes shift income between borrowers and savers but do not target the poor directly.

## Evaluation: why monetary policy may not work as planned

- **Time lags:** households and firms take months to change plans, so the full effect may arrive after conditions have changed.
- **Confidence:** in a deep recession, firms may not borrow even at very low rates if they expect weak sales.
- **Banks' behaviour:** commercial banks may not pass on a rate cut fully, or may refuse to lend to risky borrowers.
- **Conflicts between aims:** a rate cut to reduce unemployment may raise inflation; a lower exchange rate to help exports raises import prices.
- **Fixed-rate borrowers:** households on fixed-rate loans are unaffected until their deals end.
- **Scale of debt:** in economies where few households borrow, rate changes have smaller effects.

**Question (invented):** Explain how a cut in interest rates may help a government reduce unemployment. [4]

> A lower interest rate reduces the cost of borrowing [measure]. Households take out more loans to buy goods such as cars, and firms find more investment projects profitable [effect on spending]. Higher spending means firms receive more orders and must produce more output [link to production]. To produce more, firms hire additional workers, so unemployment falls [link to the aim].

## Common mistakes

- Confusing monetary policy (interest rates, money supply, exchange rate) with fiscal policy (taxes and government spending).
- Saying a higher interest rate increases spending because savers earn more. The overall effect is usually lower spending.
- Getting exchange-rate effects backwards: a lower exchange rate makes exports cheaper, not dearer.
- Explaining the measure without linking it to a named macroeconomic aim.
- Relying on aggregate demand and aggregate supply diagrams, which the syllabus does not include.
