## Core definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Market failure | The market quantity is above or below the level that is best for society |
| Symmetric information | Buyer and seller know the same about the product |
| Asymmetric information | One side knows more than the other |
| Information gap | A shortfall in what a decision maker knows, such as quality, price, risk or long-run benefit |
| Moral hazard | Taking more risk because someone else bears some of the cost |
| Speculation | Buying an asset mainly to sell later at a higher price |
| Market bubble | A sustained rise in price well above fundamental value, driven by expectations of further rises |

## Item 4: imperfect information

**Why it causes misallocation.** Decisions rest on perceived costs and benefits. If perceptions are wrong, the market quantity is not the socially optimal quantity. Describe it in words: perceived marginal benefit sits below (or above) true marginal social benefit, so the equilibrium quantity is too low (or too high), and there is a welfare loss.

| Context | Typical information gap | Likely result |
|---|---|---|
| Healthcare | Patients cannot judge diagnosis; they underestimate long-run risk | Too little prevention; possible overtreatment |
| Education | Quality and future pay are uncertain | Underinvestment in courses |
| Pensions | Long horizon, complex products, delay | Under-saving; unsuitable purchases |
| Insurance | Insurer cannot see each customer's risk | Premiums rise; low-risk customers leave; too little cover |

**Method for a misallocation answer**
1. Name who lacks information and what they do not know.
2. Say what choice they make as a result.
3. State the direction: too much or too little compared with the optimum.
4. Give the consequence for individuals and society.

**Reminders (illustrative).**
- Health check costs 90. Late treatment costs 3,000, with a true chance of 5%. True expected cost: 0.05 × 3,000 = 150. A person who believes the chance is 1% sees 30, below 90, so skips the check. The 60 difference between true expected cost and the check price is lost.
- An insurance pool: 400 low-risk (expected claims 150) and 100 high-risk (900). Average cost: (400 × 150 + 100 × 900)/500 = 300. If low-risk people value cover at 220, they leave at 300. Each lost policy wastes 220 − 150 = 70; 400 × 70 = 28,000.
- Education: perceived pay premium 2,000 a year for 25 years (50,000) against a cost of 70,000, so the student declines. True premium 6,000 a year (150,000), so a gain of 80,000 is missed.
- Pensions: saving 2,400 a year at 4%. Thirty years gives 134,603.85. Twenty years gives 71,467.39. Delay costs 63,136.46, while contributions differ by only 24,000.

## Item 5: moral hazard

**How it occurs.** Protection plus unobservable behaviour. After a contract, one side takes more risk because they do not bear all the cost, and the other side cannot see it.

**Do not confuse.** Information gap before the deal (the insurer cannot see risk type) is different from changed behaviour after the deal (moral hazard).

| Setting | The protection | The behaviour | Who pays |
|---|---|---|---|
| Insurance | Cover for losses | Less care | Insurer, then other customers |
| Banking | Deposit guarantee or expected rescue | Riskier lending | Depositors, taxpayers |

**Four groups (insurance and banking)**

| Group | Insurance | Banking |
|---|---|---|
| Consumers | Higher premiums; limits on cover | Credit boom then squeeze; weaker monitoring by depositors |
| Producers | Higher claims and monitoring costs | Cheap credit, then credit shortage |
| Workers | Less care at work; job cuts at weak insurers | Bonuses reward risk; job losses if banks fail |
| Governments | Pay for uninsured risks | Bailout costs, higher debt or taxes, tighter regulation |

**Reminders (illustrative).**
- Household contents insurer assumes a 2% claim chance and an average claim of 4,000: expected claim 80. After cover, behaviour lifts the chance to 3%: 120. Premium must rise 50% to break even.
- An excess of 500 might lead to a 2.5% chance with payouts of 3,500: 0.025 × 3,500 = 87.5. Excess, no-claims discounts and monitoring all restore incentives.
- Bank test: risky lending returns +80 with probability 0.7 and −50 with probability 0.3: expected 0.7 × 80 + 0.3 × (−50) = 41. With losses capped at 30 for the bank because the state covers the rest: 0.7 × 80 + 0.3 × (−30) = 47. The state absorbs expected cost 0.3 × 20 = 6, and 47 − 6 = 41. The bank gains from risk the economy does not.

## Item 6: speculation and bubbles

**How a bubble builds**
1. A trigger: good news or low borrowing costs lifts price.
2. Early buyers profit and are noticed.
3. Herd behaviour and expectations of further rises add demand for resale.
4. Easy credit lets more buyers in.
5. Price detaches from fundamental value, for example rent for housing or earnings for shares.
6. Confidence breaks, sales rise, loss-making holders are forced to sell, and prices fall quickly.

**Percentage arithmetic.** A rise of 50% takes an index from 100 to 150. A 40% fall then gives 150 × 0.6 = 90, which is 10% below the start. After a 40% fall, a rise of 1/0.6 − 1 = 66.7% is needed to recover.

**Housing (illustrative).** Price-to-rent ratio: 300,000/12,000 = 25. If price reaches 540,000, ratio is 45, and rental yield falls from 4% to 2.22%. A buyer pays 240,000 with a 10% deposit (24,000) and a loan of 216,000. After a 25% fall, value is 180,000, so equity is 180,000 − 216,000 = −36,000.

**Shares (illustrative).** Price 300 with earnings per share of 15 gives a price-earnings ratio of 20. At price 90 the ratio is 6. Buying at 300 and selling at 90 is a loss of 70%.

| Group | Housing | Shares |
|---|---|---|
| Consumers | Negative equity; wealth effect on spending; first-time buyers priced out | Savings and pensions fall; late buyers lose most |
| Producers | Construction boom then unsold stock | Cheap funds then scarce funds; over-investment |
| Workers | Construction jobs rise then fall; limited mobility | Lower pension values; redundancies |
| Governments | Tax receipts rise then fall; welfare and bank support costs | Gains tax receipts swing; weaker growth and confidence |

**Evaluation points.** Bubbles are hard to identify in real time. Some price rises reflect real improvements. Speculation can add information and liquidity. Harm depends on how far debt financed the boom. Effects differ by group and time.

## Quick self-test

1. State the difference between symmetric and asymmetric information.
2. Explain why imperfect information can cause too little consumption of a good.
3. A health check costs 90, late treatment costs 3,000, and the true chance is 5%. Calculate the true expected cost without the check.
4. In the insurance pool above, what is the average cost per customer if 400 customers cost 150 and 100 cost 900?
5. Define moral hazard.
6. Why does an excess reduce moral hazard?
7. A claim chance moves from 2% to 3% with an average claim of 4,000. Calculate the percentage rise in expected claims.
8. Give one way moral hazard in banking affects a government.
9. An index rises from 100 to 150, then falls 40%. State its final value.
10. Give one effect of a housing bubble bursting on workers.

### Answers

1. Symmetric: both sides know the same. Asymmetric: one knows more.
2. Consumers underestimate benefits or overestimate costs, so the market quantity is below the optimum.
3. 0.05 × 3,000 = 150.
4. (400 × 150 + 100 × 900)/500 = 300.
5. Taking more risk because another party bears part of the cost.
6. The insured bears part of any loss, so care pays.
7. 80 to 120 is a 50% rise.
8. It may have to fund bailouts or guarantees, which raises debt or taxes.
9. 90.
10. Construction job losses, or reduced mobility if homes cannot be sold.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming moral hazard but not saying who bears the cost.
- Using the pre-deal information gap as if it were moral hazard.
- Covering only consumers when four groups are asked for.
- Describing a housing effect when asked about stocks and shares.
- Not saying whether too much or too little is consumed.
- Defining a bubble without mentioning fundamental value or expectations.
- Calculating a percentage fall from the wrong base.
- Listing effects without a chain of reasoning, and evaluating without a conclusion.
