Condensed notes for the reading section of Component 1 in Pearson Edexcel International GCSE English
Language A (4EA1). For full explanations and a worked example, use the
[Section A Non-fiction Texts study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-english-language-4ea1-section-a-non-fiction-texts/).

## The section in one box

| Detail | Section A: Non-fiction texts |
|---|---|
| Texts | One Part 1 anthology text (printed in the exam) and one previously unseen extract |
| Questions | A mixture of short- and long-answer questions; answer all of them |
| Marks | 45 of the paper's 90 |
| Advised time | About 1 hour 30 minutes, including reading time |
| AOs | AO1 (11 marks), AO2 (12 marks), AO3 (22 marks) |

The paper lasts 2 hours 15 minutes in total; Section B transactional writing takes the remaining
45 minutes, so overrunning Section A costs writing marks.

## Part 1 anthology texts to know

| Text | Writer | Form to remember |
|---|---|---|
| *The Danger of a Single Story* | Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie | Speech (talk) |
| *A Passage to Africa* | George Alagiah | Journalist's memoir |
| *The Explorer's Daughter* | Kari Herbert | Travel writing / memoir |
| *Explorers, or boys messing about? Either way, taxpayer gets rescue bill* | Steven Morris | Newspaper article |
| *127 Hours: Between a Rock and a Hard Place* | Aron Ralston | Autobiography |
| *Young and Dyslexic? You've got it going on* | Benjamin Zephaniah | Newspaper article (personal) |
| *A Game of Polo with a Headless Goat* | Emma Levine | Travel writing |
| *Beyond the Sky and Earth: A Journey into Bhutan* | Jamie Zeppa | Travel memoir |
| *H is for Hawk* | Helen Macdonald | Memoir / nature writing |
| *Chinese Cinderella* | Adeline Yen Mah | Autobiography |

For each text, revise: the writer's **perspective**, the **form**, two or three **methods**, and one idea
it shares with at least two other texts. The list is fixed by specification issue, so check the current
one with your teacher.

## The three skills

**AO1: select and interpret.** Find explicit information; infer implicit attitudes. Keep to the line
range. One precise point per piece of evidence.

**AO2: analyse language and structure.** Method, short quotation, effect on the reader, link to purpose.
Cover structure (openings, shifts, endings, chronology) as well as word choice.

**AO3: compare ideas and perspectives.** The specification adds "as well as how these are conveyed",
so compare **methods** too. Integrate both texts in every paragraph.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning | Why it matters in Section A |
|---|---|---|
| Explicit | Stated directly | Basis of straightforward AO1 answers |
| Implicit | Suggested; must be inferred | Basis of higher AO1 answers |
| Perspective | The writer's viewpoint or position | Central to AO1 and AO3 |
| Purpose | What the text sets out to do (inform, persuade, entertain, reflect) | Explains *why* a method is used |
| Register | Level of formality and tone | Shifts often mark a change in purpose |
| Anecdote | A short personal story | Makes an argument vivid and credible |
| Juxtaposition | Placing contrasting ideas together | Sharpens a judgement |
| Hyperbole | Deliberate exaggeration | Creates humour or emphasis |
| Rhetorical question | A question needing no answer | Steers the reader's conclusion |
| Cyclical structure | Ending returns to the opening | Shows change or reinforces a message |
| Retrospective narration | Looking back on past events | Lets the writer comment with hindsight |
| Connective | Linking word such as *whereas* or *similarly* | Makes comparison visible |

## Methods worth spotting in any non-fiction text

- **Openings:** a scene, a question, a statistic, a bold claim.
- **Viewpoint:** first person (intimacy, honesty) or third person (distance, authority).
- **Time:** present tense for immediacy; past tense for reflection; flashback for contrast.
- **Voices:** quotations from other people add authority or a second perspective.
- **Sentence variety:** a short sentence after a long one lands as a judgement or a shock.
- **Endings:** a reflection, a call to the reader, or an image left unexplained.

## A comparison plan in three rows

| Row | Text 1 | Text 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Shared idea | e.g. facing danger | e.g. facing danger |
| Perspective | participant, reflective | observer, sceptical |
| Methods | anecdote, short sentences | headline, quotation, contrast |

Three or four rows like this become three or four integrated paragraphs.

## Exam traps

- Quoting long passages: short, precise quotations are easier to analyse.
- Spending too long on the short AO1 questions, which carry fewer marks than the comparison.
- Naming a device without its effect.
- Writing about structure as "the text uses paragraphs".
- Comparing only the topic and not the writers' perspectives and methods.
- Leaving the comparison until the last ten minutes: it is worth almost half the section.
- Forgetting the anthology text is printed in the paper: re-read the relevant part rather than writing
  from memory.

## Self-test

1. How many marks is Section A worth, and how long are you advised to spend on it?
2. Which two texts appear in Section A?
3. What is the AO1, AO2 and AO3 split in marks?
4. What is the difference between an explicit and an implicit point?
5. Name two non-fiction forms the specification expects you to have read.
6. Which text types does the specification say will not be assessed?
7. What does "perspective" mean?
8. What does AO3 add after "ideas and perspectives"?
9. Give the four steps of a mark-earning AO2 point.
10. Name two structural features you could analyse in a non-fiction text.
11. Why is a "Text 1 then Text 2" essay weak for AO3?
12. What should you revise about each Part 1 anthology text?

**Answers:** 1. 45 marks; about 1 hour 30 minutes including reading time. 2. One non-fiction text from
Part 1 of the anthology and one previously unseen extract. 3. AO1 11, AO2 12, AO3 22. 4. An explicit
point is stated directly; an implicit point is inferred from clues. 5. Any two of journalism (articles,
reviews), speeches, journals, reference-book extracts, autobiography, letters, obituaries, travel
writing. 6. Transient texts such as instant news feeds and advertisements. 7. The writer's viewpoint
or position on the subject. 8. "As well as how these are conveyed", so methods must be compared too.
9. Name the method, give short evidence, explain the effect, link it to the writer's purpose. 10. Any two
of the opening, a shift in time or focus, chronology, sentence length, the ending, a cyclical structure.
11. Comparison appears only at the end, so connections are not explored throughout. 12. Perspective,
form, two or three methods, and ideas it shares with other texts.
