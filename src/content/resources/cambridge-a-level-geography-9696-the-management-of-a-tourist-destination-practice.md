These questions cover section 13.4 of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026: the case study of one tourist area or resort, its growth and development, its sustainability issues and the impacts of tourism on its environment, society and economy. All places and data in Questions 1 to 8 are fictional. Questions 9 to 12 ask you to use your own case study. Revise first with the [13.4 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-management-of-a-tourist-destination/) and the [13.4 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-management-of-a-tourist-destination-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Define the term "perceptual carrying capacity". **[2]**

**2.** Explain why a seasonal closure can make a tourist destination more environmentally sustainable but less economically sustainable. **[4]**

**3.** Outline two ways in which leakage reduces the benefit of tourism to a resort. **[4]**

**4.** The table shows data for the fictional island resort of Kelara.

| Year | Overnight visitors (thousands) | Day visitors (thousands) | Resident population | Share of hotel beds owned by firms based off the island (%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2005 | 40 | 20 | 6,200 | 25 |
| 2015 | 110 | 180 | 5,900 | 48 |
| 2025 | 120 | 390 | 5,100 | 61 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage increase in day visitors between 2005 and 2025. Show your working. **[2]**

**(b)** Describe how the character of tourism on Kelara changed between 2005 and 2025. **[3]**

**(c)** Suggest two sustainability issues that the data indicate for Kelara. **[4]**

**5.** Kelara's council proposes three options: a daily cap of 2,000 day visitors; a tourist tax of a fixed sum per day visitor; a ban on new hotel building.

**(a)** For one option, identify one stakeholder group likely to support it and one likely to oppose it, giving a reason for each. **[4]**

**(b)** Suggest why the council might combine a cap with a tax rather than use either alone. **[3]**

**6.** Explain how boardwalks and fixed moorings can reduce environmental damage in a coastal destination. **[4]**

**7.** Explain what is meant by displacement in the management of tourism, and why it makes management harder to evaluate. **[3]**

**8.** Kelara's coral reef survey recorded live coral cover of 46% in 2010 and 29% in 2020. A no-anchoring zone was introduced in 2021, and cover was 34% in 2025.

**(a)** Calculate the mean annual change in coral cover between 2010 and 2020. **[2]**

**(b)** Assess whether the data prove that the no-anchoring zone was successful. **[4]**

## Section B

**9.** For one tourist area or resort you have studied, describe its growth and development. **[6]**

**10.** For one tourist area or resort you have studied, explain the sustainability issues it faces. **[8]**

**11.** "The management of tourist destinations protects the environment at the expense of local people." With reference to one tourist area or resort you have studied, how far do you agree? **[20]**

**12.** Evaluate the impacts of tourism on the environment, society and economy of one tourist area or resort you have studied. **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** The level of crowding or visitor numbers that visitors or residents will accept [1] before the experience, or local support for tourism, declines [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Separate it from physical capacity (space) and ecological capacity (damage to ecosystems).

**2.** Closing for part of the year removes trampling, boats and waste [1], giving ecosystems time to recover, for example coral or dune vegetation [1]. But businesses and workers earn nothing during the closure [1], and visitors may switch permanently to other destinations, reducing future income [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Explain" needs both sides linked to the same measure, with a reason for each.

**3.** Profits from hotels or tour firms owned outside the resort are sent back to the owners' home areas [1], so less income circulates locally and the multiplier is smaller [1]. Food, fuel or goods for tourists may be imported [1], so spending on them leaves the local economy rather than supporting local suppliers [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each way needs a mechanism and an effect; "money leaves the area" twice earns two marks at most.

**4. (a)** (390 - 20) / 20 x 100 [1] = 1,850% increase [1].

**(b)** Day visitors grew far faster than overnight visitors, rising from fewer than overnight visitors in 2005 to more than three times as many in 2025 [1]. Overnight visitors almost tripled to 2015 but then levelled off at 110,000 to 120,000 [1]. Ownership became increasingly external, with the share of beds owned off the island rising from 25% to 61% [1].

**(c)** Falling resident population (6,200 to 5,100) suggests residents are being displaced or leaving, a social issue [1], possibly because housing is converted to tourist use or living costs rise [1]. Rising external ownership means more leakage of profits [1], so the island gains less income per visitor, an economic issue [1]. Also credit: fast growth in day visitors suggests crowding at peak times and pressure on fragile environments, with little spending per visitor. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the figures; "Suggest" rewards plausible reasons tied to the data.

**5. (a)** Example for the daily cap: residents may support it [1] because it reduces crowding and pressure on services [1]. Day-trip boat operators may oppose it [1] because fewer passengers means lower income [1]. Accept any valid groups and reasons for the tax or the hotel ban.

**(b)** A cap limits numbers but raises no money [1]. A tax raises money for conservation or services but a small tax may not cut numbers [1]. Together they control numbers and fund management, such as enforcement of the cap [1]. **[7]**

**6.** A boardwalk concentrates people on one surface [1], so trampling of dunes or vegetation is reduced and plants can recover [1]. Fixed moorings let boats tie up without dropping anchors [1], so anchors and chains do not break coral or scour seagrass [1]. **[4]**

**7.** Displacement is when pressure moves from a managed site to nearby sites that are not managed [1]. The managed site may improve [1], but the total impact on the region may be unchanged or worse, so judging success at one site alone can overstate it [1]. **[3]**

**8. (a)** 29 - 46 = -17 percentage points over 10 years [1]; mean change = -1.7 percentage points per year [1].

**(b)** Cover rose from 29% to 34% after the zone was introduced, reversing the earlier decline [1]. But the data show correlation in time, not cause [1]; other factors such as cooler sea temperatures or fewer visitors could explain recovery [1]. Only two survey points after 2020 and no control site without the zone means the data support, but do not prove, success [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* "Assess" needs a conclusion; say what extra evidence would make the judgement firmer.

**9.** Levels-based. Credit: a named, located area or resort [1]; early visitors and why they came [1]; the trigger for rapid growth, for example transport links, a film, marketing or cheap flights [1]; dated stages of development [1]; change in the type of tourism, for example from independent travellers to mass day trips or cruises [1]; reference to stages of a life cycle model where appropriate [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Levels-based. Credit: named environmental pressure with place detail, for example coral damage, erosion, waste, water shortage [1]; explanation of why it arises from visitor numbers or behaviour [1]; a social issue such as displacement of residents, loss of culture or crowding [1]; its cause, for example conversion of homes to short-term lets [1]; an economic issue such as seasonality, low wages, leakage or over-dependence on one sector [1]; its cause [1]; a future-oriented point, for example climate change or sea level rise threatening the attraction [1]; reference to carrying capacity being exceeded [1]. **[8]**

**11.** The real exam marks this essay with levels of response. Indicative creditworthy content:

*Introduction:* define management and locate the case study [1]; state a line of argument [1].

*Agree:* strict measures such as closures, caps or vessel bans cut income for workers and small businesses [1], with named evidence from the case study [1]; fees and caps may price out lower-income visitors, including domestic tourists [1]; jobs may be seasonal or lost [1].

*Disagree:* environmental protection preserves the attraction on which local jobs depend [1]; revenue from fees can fund local services [1]; some measures target external operators, protecting residents [1]; in lived-in destinations, limits on short-term lets or crowds directly protect residents [1].

*Stakeholder analysis:* distinguish residents, workers, local owners and external firms [1]; show that costs often fall on external operators and benefits on the environment and residents, or the reverse, using case-study detail [1].

*Scale and time:* short-term costs versus long-term benefits [1]; displacement to other sites [1].

*Judgement:* a supported view on "how far" [1] that varies by group [1], refers back to evidence [1], and links to sustainability in all three dimensions [1]; a conclusion that answers the question directly [1]; reference to whether the destination type made the outcome likely [1]. **[20]**
*Examiner insight:* The strongest answers separate "local people" into groups; it is rarely true that all local people lose or all gain.

**12.** Levels-based. Indicative content:

*Environment:* positive impacts such as conservation funded by fees [1]; negative impacts such as habitat damage, pollution and waste with named evidence [1]; judgement on balance [1].

*Society:* positive impacts such as investment in heritage and services [1]; negative impacts such as crowding, rising housing costs and population loss [1]; differences between groups [1]; judgement [1].

*Economy:* jobs, foreign exchange and multiplier effects [1]; leakage, seasonality and dependence [1]; who captures spending [1]; judgement [1].

*Management:* how management has changed the balance of impacts [1], with evidence [1]; limits of management [1].

*Synthesis:* links between dimensions, for example environmental damage threatening future income [1]; use of a model such as Butler's or carrying capacity to frame the evaluation [1]; change over time [1]; overall judgement on whether impacts are net positive [1], for whom [1]; a conclusion that addresses all three dimensions [1]. **[20]**
*Examiner insight:* Organise by dimension, but end each section with a judgement; a final paragraph that only summarises earns little.
