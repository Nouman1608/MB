This guide covers section **2.3.4 Resource management** in Unit 2 (Managing business activities) of the Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Subsidiary/Advanced Level in Business, Issue 1. Resource management is about using people, machines, materials and time so that goods or services reach customers at the right quality and the right cost. All businesses and figures in this guide are invented.

For the unit as a whole, see the [Managing Business Activities study guide](/resources/a-level-edexcel-business-managing-business-activities/). Condensed notes are in the [Resource management revision notes](/resources/edexcel-a-level-business-ybs11-resource-management-revision-notes/) and questions in the [Resource management practice questions](/resources/edexcel-a-level-business-ybs11-resource-management-practice/).

## What the specification lists

1. **Production, productivity and efficiency:** methods of production (job, batch, flow, cell); productivity as output per unit of input per time period, what influences it, its link to competitiveness and ways to improve it; efficiency as production at minimum average cost; labour-intensive versus capital-intensive production; competitive advantage from short product lead-in times.
2. **Capacity utilisation:** current output divided by maximum possible output, multiplied by 100; the implications of under- and over-utilisation; ways of improving utilisation in each case.
3. **Inventory control:** interpreting an inventory control diagram; buffer inventory; the implications of poor inventory control; just in time (JIT); waste minimisation; competitive advantage from lean production.
4. **Quality management:** quality control, quality assurance and quality circles; Total Quality Management (TQM); continuous improvement (Kaizen); competitive advantage from quality management.

Unit 2 is a two-hour paper with 80 marks, built from short and extended responses based on sources. Resource management questions often ask you to calculate productivity or capacity utilisation and then to analyse or assess what the figure means for the business in the source.

## Methods of production

| Method | What it means | Suits | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Job | One item made to a customer's order | Bespoke goods such as a wedding dress or a bridge | Slow, skilled labour, high unit cost |
| Batch | A group of identical items made together, then the process is reset | Bakery loaves, paint colours, clothing ranges | Machines idle between batches; work in progress builds up |
| Flow | Continuous production of a standard product, often on a line | Cars, bottled drinks, tinned food | Large capital cost; inflexible; a breakdown stops everything |
| Cell | Small teams each responsible for a complete part of the product | Electronics assembly, furniture components | Needs multi-skilled staff and careful training |

The choice depends on demand and variety. Flow production gives the lowest unit cost but needs high, steady demand. Job production gives the highest price per item but little scale. **Cell production** is a halfway house that raises flexibility and staff involvement, because each team owns its output and can spot problems early.

## Productivity

**Productivity** measures output per unit of input per time period. The most common form is labour productivity:

```
Labour productivity = output / number of employees (or labour hours)
```

**Worked example 1.** Ardfield Plastics made 18,000 units using 600 labour hours in March. In April it made 21,000 units using 640 labour hours.

- March: 18,000 / 600 = **30 units per hour**.
- April: 21,000 / 640 = **32.8 units per hour** (to one decimal place).
- Change: (32.8125 - 30) / 30 x 100 = **9.4% increase**.

Output rose by 16.7% but hours rose by only 6.7%, so each hour became more productive. A common mistake is to say productivity rose because output rose. If hours had risen by the same percentage as output, productivity would not have changed.

**Factors influencing productivity** include the skills and motivation of staff, the quality and age of machinery, the layout of the workplace, the quality of materials and management. **Ways to improve it** include training, better equipment, financial and non-financial motivation, improved layouts and removing wasted steps. Higher productivity gives lower unit labour costs, which can allow lower prices or higher margins. That is the link to **competitiveness**: a business with higher productivity than its rivals can undercut them or earn more per sale.

## Efficiency

**Efficiency** means producing at minimum average cost. Productivity measures only the physical relationship between inputs and outputs, while efficiency brings in the cost of those inputs.

**Worked example 2.** Using the same firm, total costs in March were $90,000 and in April $99,750.

- March average cost: 90,000 / 18,000 = **$5.00** per unit.
- April average cost: 99,750 / 21,000 = **$4.75** per unit.

Average cost fell by 5%, so the firm became more efficient. Efficiency can be improved by cutting waste, negotiating cheaper materials, using machinery fully and spreading fixed costs over more units. Be careful with the trade-offs: cheaper materials may cut quality, and heavy cost-cutting may damage morale and productivity.

## Labour-intensive and capital-intensive production

A **labour-intensive** business relies mainly on people (a hair salon, a consultancy, a hand-finished furniture workshop). A **capital-intensive** business relies mainly on machinery (a car plant, a bottling line). Labour-intensive production is flexible and suits differentiated, personal products, but wage costs rise with output. Capital-intensive production has high fixed costs and low variable costs per unit, so it needs high volumes to pay off. Rising wages and falling machine prices push many businesses towards automation, though the loss of jobs and the cost of finance are risks.

## Short product lead-in times

The **lead-in time** is the time from the idea for a product to its launch. A shorter lead-in gives competitive advantage because the business reaches the market before rivals, can charge a premium price for a while, and can respond to changing fashions. Flexible production such as cell production, strong links with suppliers and good project management all help to shorten it.

## Capacity utilisation

```
Capacity utilisation = current output / maximum possible output x 100
```

**Worked example 3.** A factory can make at most 25,000 units a month. It currently makes 20,000. Fixed costs are $50,000 a month.

- Utilisation = 20,000 / 25,000 x 100 = **80%**.
- Fixed cost per unit = 50,000 / 20,000 = **$2.50**.

A new order lifts output to 24,500 units.

- Utilisation = 24,500 / 25,000 x 100 = **98%**.
- Fixed cost per unit = 50,000 / 24,500 = **$2.04**.

Spreading the fixed costs saved $0.46 a unit.

**Under-utilisation** means spare capacity: higher unit costs, wasted resources and possible job losses, but there is room to meet an extra order. Ways to improve it include finding new customers or markets, cutting prices, rationalising (closing a plant or selling equipment), renting out spare capacity or subcontracting work in. **Over-utilisation** (running at or near 100%) brings overtime costs, machine breakdowns, tired staff, falling quality and late deliveries, and no room for a surge in demand. Ways to relieve it include extra shifts, new equipment or premises, outsourcing or raising prices to slow demand.

## Inventory control

A business holds **inventory** (raw materials, work in progress and finished goods) so that it does not run out. Holding too much ties up cash, needs storage and risks damage or obsolescence. Holding too little risks stock-outs, idle production and lost customers.

An **inventory control diagram** plots inventory level against time. Four values matter:

- **Maximum inventory level:** the most the business wants to hold.
- **Buffer inventory:** the minimum level, held as a safety net against late deliveries or sudden demand.
- **Reorder level:** the level at which a new order is placed.
- **Lead time:** the time between ordering and delivery.

**Worked example 4.** A bakery has a maximum inventory of 600 kg of flour and a buffer of 150 kg. It uses 50 kg a day and the supplier takes 4 days to deliver.

- Usage during the lead time = 50 x 4 = 200 kg.
- Reorder level = 150 + 200 = **350 kg**, so that stock falls to the buffer just as the delivery arrives.
- Order quantity = 600 - 150 = **450 kg**.
- Time between orders = 450 / 50 = **9 days**.

On the diagram, inventory slopes down steadily at 50 kg a day, drops to 350 kg on day 5 of each cycle (when the order is placed), reaches 150 kg when the order arrives, and jumps back to 600 kg. If usage rises, the line is steeper and the buffer is reached sooner than planned. **Poor inventory control** causes stock-outs, lost sales, rushed and costly ordering, high storage costs, waste and tied-up cash.

## Just in time and lean production

**Just in time (JIT)** means receiving materials only as they are needed, with minimal inventory. Benefits: lower storage costs, less cash tied up, less waste and a quicker response to changes. Risks: no cushion if a supplier is late, higher ordering costs, and loss of bulk discounts. It requires reliable suppliers and accurate forecasting.

**Waste minimisation** removes anything that does not add value: overproduction, waiting, unnecessary movement, defects and excess inventory. **Lean production** combines JIT, waste minimisation, continuous improvement and high quality to do more with fewer resources. The competitive advantage comes from lower costs, faster delivery and fewer defects.

## Quality management

- **Quality control** checks products at the end of a process (or at stages) and rejects faulty ones. It finds mistakes but does not prevent them, and inspection costs money.
- **Quality assurance** builds quality into every stage, setting standards for suppliers, processes and staff so that faults do not occur.
- **Quality circles** are small groups of employees who meet regularly to suggest improvements. They draw on staff knowledge and improve motivation, but take time and managers must act on the ideas.
- **Total Quality Management (TQM)** makes quality the responsibility of everyone in the business, with customer satisfaction as the goal.
- **Continuous improvement (Kaizen)** means constant small changes by all employees, rather than occasional big projects.

Competitive advantage follows from fewer defects and returns, lower waste, a stronger reputation and customer loyalty. Quality management has costs, including training and time, and TQM works only if managers and staff commit to it.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a rise in output as a rise in productivity without checking the inputs.
- Confusing productivity (inputs and outputs) with efficiency (cost per unit).
- Saying that a lower reorder level is always better; it saves money but raises the risk of a stock-out.
- Describing JIT without its main risk: reliance on suppliers.
- Mixing up quality control (checking) and quality assurance (preventing).
- Giving a capacity utilisation figure with no comment on what it means for costs or the ability to meet orders.

## Exam technique

**Calculate** questions need the formula, the figures and a unit or percentage. For **explain**, give one cause or effect and develop it with a specific detail from the source. **Analyse** needs a chain of reasoning, such as "higher utilisation, lower fixed cost per unit, higher profit margin". **Discuss** and **assess** need both sides and a judgement that depends on the business in the source: for instance, JIT suits a business with reliable local suppliers and predictable demand, but not one that depends on distant suppliers.
