This guide covers subtopic **8.2 Human digestive system** of Cambridge O Level Biology 5090, 2026-2028 series. The syllabus asks you to identify the main regions of the digestive system, to explain why most foods must be digested before absorption, to describe physical and chemical digestion, to identify the types of teeth and describe their structure and functions, to describe the functions of each region, to describe the functions of amylase, maltase, protease and lipase with their substrates and end-products, to describe the function of hydrochloric acid, to understand that different proteases work best at different pH levels, to outline the role of bile, and to describe peristalsis.

## Why food must be digested

Food contains large molecules such as starch, protein and fats. They are **insoluble** and too big to pass through the walls of the gut into the blood. **Digestion** breaks them into small, soluble molecules that can be absorbed. Without digestion, the body would get no glucose, amino acids, fatty acids or glycerol from a meal.

## The main regions

Food travels along the **alimentary canal**, with the help of several organs that make secretions.

| Region | Role |
|---|---|
| Mouth | Ingestion (taking in food), physical digestion by teeth, mixing with saliva |
| Salivary glands | Make saliva containing amylase |
| Oesophagus | Muscular tube that carries food to the stomach |
| Stomach | Physical digestion by churning; hydrochloric acid and protease |
| Small intestine: duodenum | Receives bile and pancreatic juice; chemical digestion |
| Small intestine: ileum | Chemical digestion; absorption of nutrients |
| Pancreas | Makes alkaline pancreatic juice with amylase, protease and lipase |
| Liver | Makes bile; stores glycogen |
| Gall bladder | Stores bile |
| Large intestine: colon | Absorbs water (and some mineral ions) |
| Rectum | Stores faeces |
| Anus | Egestion of faeces |

**Egestion** is the removal of undigested food (faeces) from the body. Do not confuse it with excretion, which is the removal of metabolic waste such as urea.

## Physical and chemical digestion

**Physical digestion** is the breakdown of food into smaller pieces **without chemical change** to the food molecules. It is done by the teeth, and by the churning of the stomach wall. It increases the **surface area** of the food, so enzymes can act on it more quickly.

**Chemical digestion** is the breakdown of **large molecules into small molecules** by enzymes.

## Teeth

### Types and functions

| Type | Shape | Function in physical digestion |
|---|---|---|
| Incisors | Chisel-shaped, sharp edge | Bite and cut food |
| Canines | Pointed | Tear and grip food |
| Premolars | Broad, with ridges (cusps) | Crush and grind food |
| Molars | Broad, with large cusps | Crush and grind food |

### Structure of a tooth

A tooth has a visible **crown** and a **root** in the jaw. It is embedded in the **gum**.

- **Enamel**: the very hard outer layer of the crown; protects the tooth from wear.
- **Dentine**: a bone-like layer under the enamel; it is softer than enamel.
- **Pulp**: the central cavity, containing **nerves** and blood vessels.
- **Cement**: a bony layer around the root that fixes the tooth in the jaw.

## Chemical digestion: the enzymes

Enzymes in digestion are proteins that act as biological catalysts. Each enzyme works on one type of substrate.

| Enzyme | Substrate | End-product | Where it acts |
|---|---|---|---|
| Amylase | Starch | Maltose | Mouth (from saliva); small intestine (from pancreas) |
| Maltase | Maltose | Glucose | Small intestine |
| Protease (pepsin) | Protein | Amino acids (smaller polypeptides first) | Stomach |
| Protease (trypsin) | Protein | Amino acids | Small intestine |
| Lipase | Lipids | Fatty acids and glycerol | Small intestine |

A helpful rule is that the **name of the enzyme starts like the substrate**: amylase acts on starch (amylose), protease on protein and lipase on lipid.

### Hydrochloric acid in the stomach

The stomach produces **hydrochloric acid**. It **kills bacteria** that are swallowed with the food. It also gives the stomach contents a low pH for the protease called pepsin.

### Different pH values for different proteases

The proteases in the stomach and the duodenum work best at **different pH levels**.

- **Pepsin** in the stomach works best in **acid** conditions (about pH 2).
- **Trypsin** in the duodenum works best in **alkaline** conditions (about pH 8).

The alkaline pancreatic juice, and the bile, neutralise the acid from the stomach as the food enters the duodenum. This gives trypsin the right pH. If the pH is wrong for an enzyme, its shape changes and it stops working (denaturation).

## Bile and emulsification

Bile is made in the **liver**, stored in the **gall bladder**, and passes into the duodenum. It does not contain enzymes. Bile is **alkaline**, and it **emulsifies fats**, which means that it breaks large fat droplets into many tiny droplets. This increases the **surface area** of the fat, so lipase can digest it faster to fatty acids and glycerol.

### Worked example: surface area from emulsification

*A fat droplet is a cube of side 1 cm. Bile breaks it into 1000 cubes of side 1 mm (0.1 cm). Compare the total surface area before and after.*

1. Before: surface area of one cube = 6 x 1 x 1 = **6 cm squared**.
2. After: each small cube has a surface area of 6 x 0.1 x 0.1 = 0.06 cm squared. For 1000 cubes: 1000 x 0.06 = **60 cm squared**.
3. The total surface area is 60 divided by 6 = **10 times** greater.
4. **Explanation**: more fat is in contact with lipase, so the chemical digestion is faster, although the amount of fat is the same.

## Peristalsis

**Peristalsis is waves of contraction of the longitudinal and circular muscles in the wall of the gut, which push food along.**

- Circular muscles contract behind the food, narrowing the tube and squeezing the food forwards.
- Longitudinal muscles also contract, which shortens that section of the gut.
- The muscles relax after the wave passes, and the next wave follows.

Peristalsis moves food along the oesophagus, stomach, small intestine and large intestine. It moves food even when a person is upside down.

## Putting the journey together: a piece of bread

1. **Mouth**: teeth chew the bread (physical digestion); saliva with amylase begins to digest starch to maltose.
2. **Oesophagus**: peristalsis moves the food to the stomach.
3. **Stomach**: churning and hydrochloric acid; protease starts protein digestion.
4. **Duodenum**: bile and pancreatic juice arrive; amylase, protease and lipase act; starch continues to be digested.
5. **Ileum**: maltase finishes digesting maltose to glucose; nutrients are absorbed (see subtopic 8.3).
6. **Colon**: water is absorbed. **Rectum and anus**: undigested material is egested.

## Reading data about digestive enzymes

Digestion questions often give a table or graph of enzyme activity against pH or time. Use these steps.

1. **Find the peak or the fastest result**. For a table of time taken, the *shortest* time is the fastest digestion. For a graph of rate, the highest point is the optimum.
2. **Name the optimum pH** and link it to a region. An optimum near pH 2 matches pepsin in the stomach. An optimum near pH 8 matches trypsin in the duodenum.
3. **Compare rates correctly**. If digestion took 6 minutes at one pH and 24 minutes at another, the first is 24 divided by 6 = 4 times faster. Rate is the inverse of time.
4. **Explain the fall** on either side of the optimum. Away from the optimum pH, the shape of the enzyme (including its active site) changes, the substrate no longer fits, and the enzyme is denatured.

For a rate measured as "amount digested per minute", use rate = amount divided by time. Always include the unit.

## Why each region has its own enzymes

Each enzyme is specific to one type of substrate because of the shape of its active site. That is why the gut needs several enzymes and why the food is digested in stages.

| Stage | What is digested | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Mouth | Starch partly digested by salivary amylase | Some maltose |
| Stomach | Protein partly digested by pepsin | Short chains of amino acids |
| Duodenum and ileum | Starch, protein and fat completed by pancreatic and intestinal enzymes | Maltose, then glucose; amino acids; fatty acids and glycerol |

The small intestine is where digestion is **completed**, because all three types of food molecule meet enzymes there. The products are then ready for absorption (subtopic 8.3).

## Common mistakes

- Saying that bile digests fat. It emulsifies it; **lipase** digests it.
- Saying that the stomach is where most digestion and absorption happen. The small intestine does most of both.
- Mixing up egestion and excretion.
- Writing that amylase produces glucose. It produces **maltose**; maltase makes glucose.
- Saying that all enzymes work best at the same pH.
- Saying that physical digestion changes the chemical structure of food.
- Forgetting the surface-area reason for chewing and for bile.

## Command words

- **Identify**: name a region or tooth on a diagram.
- **Describe**: state the functions of each region or the structure of a tooth.
- **Explain**: give the reason, such as why digestion is needed or why bile helps.
- **Outline**: set out the main points of the role of bile or peristalsis.
