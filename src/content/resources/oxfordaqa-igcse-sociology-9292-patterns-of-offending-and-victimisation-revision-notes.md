**These notes cover "Patterns of offending and victimisation by: gender; social class; age;
ethnicity; explanations of these patterns"** in 3.2.2.4 of OxfordAQA International GCSE Sociology
(9292). They condense the [patterns of offending and victimisation study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-sociology-9292-patterns-of-offending-and-victimisation/).
How crime is measured is condensed in the [research methods (Paper 2) revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-sociology-9292-research-methods-paper2-revision-notes/).

## Two cautions

- Official statistics show **recorded** crime: patterns may partly reflect **who is policed**.
- Patterns **intersect** (Crenshaw): gender, class, age and ethnicity combine.

## Patterns at a glance

| Factor | Offending pattern | Victimisation pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Gender | Men are the large majority of recorded offenders and prisoners, especially for violence | Women more often victims of domestic abuse and sexual violence; men more often victims of public and stranger violence and of homicide |
| Social class | Working class over-represented for street and property crime; crimes of the powerful under-recorded | Poorer areas experience more burglary and violence |
| Age | Peaks in late teens and early twenties, then falls (age-crime curve) | Young people more often victims of violence; older people targeted by fraud, high fear of crime |
| Ethnicity | Some minority groups over-represented in stop and search and prison in some societies | Racially motivated crime and harassment, often under-reported |

## Explanations by factor

### Gender

- **Socialisation and masculinity:** Connell's **hegemonic masculinity**.
- **Control of women:** Heidensohn (1985): home, public, work.
- **Chivalry thesis:** Pollak (1950): leniency to women. Counter: **double deviance**.
- **Liberation thesis:** Adler (1975): more freedom, more female crime (disputed).

### Social class

- **Strain:** Merton (1938): goals without legitimate means → **innovation**.
- **Subculture:** Albert Cohen (1955): **status frustration**.
- **Social bonds:** Hirschi (1969): attachment, commitment, involvement.
- **Marxist:** selective law enforcement; crimes of the powerful under-recorded.
- **Labelling:** Cicourel (1968): the "typical delinquent" as working class.

### Age

- Peer groups and status-seeking.
- Fewer commitments, less to lose (Hirschi).
- Visibility in public; police attention; youth moral panics (Stanley Cohen).

### Ethnicity

- **Institutional racism:** Macpherson Report (1999), after the racist murder of Stephen Lawrence
  (1993).
- **Stereotypes and moral panics:** Hall and colleagues, *Policing the Crisis* (1978), on "mugging";
  Gilroy's "myth of black criminality".
- **Socio-economic disadvantage** and **marginalisation**.
- Over-representation in the criminal justice system of England and Wales examined in the **Lammy
  Review** (2017).

## Evaluation points

- Victim surveys and self-report studies can **check** official patterns.
- Patterns are **real but shaped** by reporting, recording and policing.
- Explanations often **combine**: class and ethnicity overlap through disadvantage.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Victimisation | Being the victim of a crime |
| Age-crime curve | The rise of offending to a peak in late teens or early twenties and decline after |
| Hegemonic masculinity | The dominant ideal of manhood in a society (Connell) |
| Chivalry thesis | The claim that the justice system treats women more leniently (Pollak) |
| Double deviance | Women judged both for a crime and for breaking gender norms |
| Strain | The gap between approved goals and legitimate means (Merton) |
| Status frustration | Lack of status leading to delinquent subcultures (Albert Cohen) |
| Institutional racism | Racism built into the practices of an organisation (Macpherson) |

## Model paragraph

"Official statistics show working-class young men are over-represented among offenders, which strain
theory explains as a response to blocked opportunities. However, Cicourel's research suggests that
police officers' image of the 'typical delinquent' made working-class youths more likely to be
arrested and charged, so the statistics may partly reflect policing. In [a named society], comparing
police records with self-report studies would help judge how far the pattern is real."

## Exam traps

- Treating official statistics as the full picture.
- Patterns with no explanation, or explanations with no sociologist.
- Forgetting **victimisation**.
- Confusing **Albert Cohen** (subcultures, 1955) with **Stanley Cohen** (moral panics, 1972).
- Implying any ethnic group is naturally more criminal instead of analysing policing, disadvantage
  and labelling.
- No named society in an extended answer.

## Self-test

1. Why may official statistics not show the true pattern of offending?
2. State the gender pattern of recorded offending.
3. Give two explanations of the gender pattern.
4. What is the chivalry thesis, and who proposed it?
5. What is double deviance?
6. Explain Merton's idea of innovation.
7. What did Albert Cohen mean by status frustration?
8. What did Cicourel find?
9. Describe the age-crime curve.
10. What did the Macpherson Report conclude?
11. Give one pattern of victimisation by gender.
12. How could intersectionality be applied to patterns of offending?

**Answers:** 1. They record only crime that is reported, recorded and prosecuted, which depends partly
on policing. 2. Men make up the large majority of recorded offenders, especially for violent crime.
3. Any two: gender socialisation and hegemonic masculinity; greater social control of women; the
chivalry thesis; differences in opportunity. 4. The claim that a male-dominated justice system treats
women more leniently; Pollak (1950). 5. Women being judged both for their crime and for breaking
gender norms. 6. Using illegal means to achieve socially approved goals, such as wealth, when
legitimate means are blocked. 7. Working-class boys who fail to gain status at school form
subcultures that reward delinquency instead. 8. Police and juvenile justice officers held an image of
the "typical delinquent" that matched working-class youths, so they were more likely to be defined as
delinquent. 9. Offending rises to a peak in the late teens and early twenties and then declines.
10. That the Metropolitan Police was institutionally racist. 11. For example, women are far more
likely to be victims of domestic abuse and sexual violence. 12. By showing how gender, class, age and
ethnicity combine, so a young working-class man from a minority group may be affected by several
patterns, such as more frequent police stops, at once.
