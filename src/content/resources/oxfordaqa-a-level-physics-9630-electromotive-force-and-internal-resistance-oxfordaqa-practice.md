Learn the content first with the [emf and internal resistance study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-electromotive-force-and-internal-resistance-oxfordaqa/) and the [emf and internal resistance revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-electromotive-force-and-internal-resistance-oxfordaqa-revision-notes/). Treat meters as ideal.

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.**

**(a)** Define the emf of a source. **[1]**

**(b)** State how the terminal pd of the source differs in meaning from its emf. **[1]**

**2.** A battery of emf 4.5 V and internal resistance 0.30 Ω is connected to a 1.5 Ω resistor.

**(a)** Calculate the current. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the terminal pd. **[1]**

**3.** Explain why the terminal pd of a cell is less than its emf when it supplies a current. **[2]**

**4.** A cell has a terminal pd of 2.70 V when it supplies 0.50 A, and 2.40 V when it supplies 1.10 A. Calculate its emf and internal resistance. **[3]**

**5.** A 12 V battery has internal resistance 0.020 Ω. Calculate its short-circuit current and explain why a short circuit across it is dangerous. **[2]**

**6.** Four identical cells, each of emf 1.5 V and internal resistance 0.40 Ω, are connected in parallel. State the emf and calculate the internal resistance of the combination. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** In Required practical 3 a student obtains these readings for a battery.

| I / A | 0.20 | 0.40 | 0.60 | 0.80 | 1.00 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| V / V | 2.84 | 2.68 | 2.52 | 2.36 | 2.20 |

**(a)** Explain how the emf and internal resistance are found from a graph of V against I. **[2]**

**(b)** Determine the emf and internal resistance. **[2]**

**(c)** Give two reasons why the student should open the switch between readings. **[2]**

**8.** Four rechargeable cells, each of emf 1.2 V and internal resistance 0.30 Ω, are connected in series to a 6.0 Ω resistor.

**(a)** State the total emf and total internal resistance. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the current and the terminal pd. **[2]**

**(c)** The cells are rearranged as two parallel pairs connected in series. Calculate the new current in the 6.0 Ω resistor. **[2]**

**9.** A phone battery has emf 3.7 V and internal resistance 0.15 Ω. During heavy use it supplies a current of 2.0 A.

**(a)** Calculate the terminal pd. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the power wasted in the internal resistance. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the total power supplied by the battery. **[1]**

**(d)** Explain why the battery warms during heavy use. **[1]**

**10.** A source of emf 12 V and internal resistance 2.0 Ω is connected to an 8.0 Ω resistor.

**(a)** Calculate the current. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the power delivered to the resistor. **[1]**

**(c)** Show that the fraction of the source's power delivered to the resistor is R/(R + r), and evaluate it. **[2]**

**11.** A student uses a resistance box R and an ammeter with a cell, and plots R (vertical) against 1/I (horizontal). The line has gradient 4.5 V and meets the R axis at −1.5 Ω.

**(a)** Show that R = ε(1/I) − r. **[2]**

**(b)** State the emf and internal resistance of the cell. **[2]**

**12.** A laboratory EHT supply has an emf of 5.0 kV and an internal resistance of 20 MΩ.

**(a)** Calculate the short-circuit current. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why the large internal resistance makes the supply safer. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why this supply could not be used to run a device that needs a large current. **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** The energy transferred to electrical energy per unit charge passing through the source (ε = E/Q) [1]

**(b)** The terminal pd is the energy per unit charge delivered to the external circuit, which is less than the emf by the energy per unit charge transferred in the internal resistance [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* Avoid "the voltage of the cell": define emf in terms of energy per unit charge.

**2. (a)** I = ε/(R + r) = 4.5 / 1.8 = 2.5 A [1]

**(b)** V = IR = 2.5 × 1.5 = 3.75 V (or 4.5 − 2.5 × 0.30) [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* Using I = 4.5/1.5 = 3.0 A ignores the internal resistance and loses both marks.

**3.** Charges passing through the internal resistance transfer some energy to heating inside the cell [1]; so the energy per coulomb delivered to the external circuit is less than the emf by Ir, the lost volts [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* Say where the energy goes; "voltage is lost" without a mechanism earns little.

**4.** 2.70 = ε − 0.50r and 2.40 = ε − 1.10r [1]; subtracting: 0.30 = 0.60r, so r = 0.50 Ω [1]; ε = 2.70 + 0.50 × 0.50 = 2.95 V [1] **[3]**

*Examiner insight:* Two unknowns need two equations; write both before solving.

**5.** I = ε/r = 12 / 0.020 = 600 A [1]; such a large current heats the wire and the battery rapidly, risking fire or damage [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* The small internal resistance that suits a car battery is exactly what makes a short circuit dangerous.

**6.** emf = 1.5 V [1]; r = 0.40 / 4 = 0.10 Ω [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* Identical cells in parallel keep the emf of one cell; only the internal resistance changes.

**7. (a)** V = ε − rI, so the line is straight [1]; the intercept on the V axis is ε and the gradient is −r [1]

**(b)** Gradient = (2.20 − 2.84) / (1.00 − 0.20) = −0.80 V A⁻¹, so r = 0.80 Ω [1]; ε = 2.84 + 0.20 × 0.80 = 3.0 V [1]

**(c)** To stop the battery warming, which would change its internal resistance [1]; to stop it running down, which would lower its emf during the experiment [1] **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* "To save the battery" is too vague; link each reason to a change in ε or r.

**8. (a)** ε = 4 × 1.2 = 4.8 V and r = 4 × 0.30 = 1.2 Ω [1]

**(b)** I = 4.8 / (6.0 + 1.2) = 0.67 A [1]; V = 0.667 × 6.0 = 4.0 V [1]

**(c)** Each pair: 1.2 V, 0.15 Ω; two pairs in series: 2.4 V, 0.30 Ω [1]; I = 2.4 / 6.3 = 0.38 A [1] **[5]**

*Examiner insight:* Work out each pair first, then combine the pairs in series.

**9. (a)** V = 3.7 − 2.0 × 0.15 = 3.4 V [1]

**(b)** P = I²r = 2.0² × 0.15 = 0.60 W [1]

**(c)** P = εI = 3.7 × 2.0 = 7.4 W [1]

**(d)** Energy is transferred to heating in the internal resistance at 0.60 W, and this rises as I² for larger currents [1] **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* The useful power, 6.8 W, plus the wasted power, 0.60 W, equals the total; check this.

**10. (a)** I = 12 / 10 = 1.2 A [1]

**(b)** P = I²R = 1.2² × 8.0 = 11.5 W [1]

**(c)** Fraction = I²R / (I²R + I²r) = R/(R + r) [1]; = 8.0 / 10 = 0.80 (80%) [1] **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* The fraction is close to 1 only when R is much larger than r.

**11. (a)** ε = I(R + r), so ε/I = R + r [1]; R = ε(1/I) − r, which has the form y = mx + c [1]

**(b)** ε = gradient = 4.5 V [1]; r = 1.5 Ω (the intercept is −r) [1] **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* A negative intercept gives a positive internal resistance; quoting r = −1.5 Ω loses the mark.

**12. (a)** I = 5000 / 20 × 10⁶ = 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ A [1]

**(b)** Even if the terminals are short-circuited (for example through a person), the current cannot exceed this small value [1]

**(c)** Any appreciable current would give lost volts Ir so large that almost no pd would remain across the device [1] **[3]**

*Examiner insight:* For (c), a numerical example helps: a current of only 1 mA would give lost volts of 20 kV, more than the emf, which is impossible.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving out r when finding the current.
- Defining emf vaguely as a voltage.
- Reading the V-I gradient as +r.
- Adding internal resistances for parallel cells.
- Reasons for switching off that do not mention a change in ε or r.
- Quoting a negative internal resistance from an intercept.
