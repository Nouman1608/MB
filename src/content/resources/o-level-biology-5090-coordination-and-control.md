## 14.1 The mammalian nervous system

The nervous system (the **brain**, **spinal cord** and **nerves**) coordinates and regulates body functions. It has two parts:

- **Central nervous system (CNS):** the brain and the spinal cord.
- **Peripheral nervous system (PNS):** the nerves outside the brain and spinal cord.

**Electrical impulses** travel along neurones (nerve cells). A nerve is a bundle of many neurones.

### Three types of neurone

| Neurone | Carries impulses | How to identify it on a diagram |
|---|---|---|
| Sensory | From a receptor to the CNS | Long fibre on both sides of the cell body; the cell body sits on a short side branch, outside the spinal cord |
| Relay | Within the CNS, from sensory to motor neurone | Short, with many branches, drawn entirely inside the spinal cord or brain |
| Motor | From the CNS to an effector | Cell body at one end, inside the CNS, with many short branches; one long fibre runs to a muscle or gland |

### Reflex arcs and reflex actions

A **reflex action** is a rapid and automatic response to a stimulus. The pathway is a **reflex arc**:

`stimulus → receptor → sensory neurone → relay neurone (in CNS) → motor neurone → effector → response`

The **effector** is a muscle (which contracts) or a gland (which secretes).

**Worked example.** *Describe the reflex arc when your hand touches a hot pan.*

1. Temperature receptors in the skin of the hand detect the heat (the stimulus).
2. They start impulses in a **sensory neurone**, which carries them to the spinal cord.
3. In the spinal cord, a **relay neurone** passes the impulses to a **motor neurone**.
4. The motor neurone carries impulses to the **effector**, a muscle in the arm.
5. The muscle contracts and the hand is pulled away, quickly and automatically.

### Synapses

A **synapse** is a junction between two neurones. Its structure:

```
  neurone 1 ending                         neurone 2
 ┌──────────────────┐                  ┌──────────────
 │ vesicles (o o o) │   synaptic gap   │ receptor proteins
 │ of neuro-        │ ←── (tiny) ──→   │ on the membrane
 │ transmitter      │                  │
 └──────────────────┘                  └──────────────
        impulse arrives  ──────────────►  new impulse
```

Events at a synapse, in order:

1. An impulse arrives and stimulates the release of **neurotransmitter** molecules from **vesicles** into the **synaptic gap**.
2. The neurotransmitter molecules **diffuse** across the gap and **bind with receptor proteins** on the next neurone.
3. An impulse is stimulated in the next neurone.

Synapses ensure that impulses travel in **one direction only**. Vesicles are only on one side of the gap and receptor proteins are only on the other, so the signal cannot pass backwards.

## 14.2 Mammalian sense organs

A **sense organ** is a group of receptor cells responding to a specific stimulus: **light** (eyes), **sound** (ears), **touch** and **temperature** (skin), and **chemicals** (nose and tongue).

### Parts of the eye and their functions

| Part | Function |
|---|---|
| Cornea | Transparent front layer; refracts light |
| Iris | Coloured ring of muscle; controls how much light enters the pupil |
| Pupil | The hole in the centre of the iris that light passes through |
| Lens | Focuses light onto the retina |
| Ciliary muscles and suspensory ligaments | Control the shape of the lens |
| Retina | Contains light receptors, some sensitive to light of different colours |
| Fovea | Part of the retina with the greatest density of light receptors |
| Optic nerve | Carries impulses to the brain |
| Blind spot | Where the optic nerve leaves the eye; it has no light receptors |

On a diagram, the ciliary muscles form a ring around the lens, the suspensory ligaments join them to the lens, the fovea is opposite the lens on the retina, and the blind spot is where the optic nerve joins.

### The pupil reflex

The iris has two sets of muscles that work **antagonistically** (one contracts while the other relaxes):

| Light intensity | Circular muscles | Radial muscles | Pupil |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bright | Contract | Relax | Becomes smaller (constricts), so less light enters and the retina is protected |
| Dim | Relax | Contract | Becomes wider (dilates), so more light enters and you can see |

This is a reflex: light receptors in the retina are the receptors, and the iris muscles are the effectors.

### Accommodation

**Accommodation** is changing the shape of the lens to focus on near or distant objects.

| | Near object | Distant object |
|---|---|---|
| Ciliary muscles | Contract | Relax |
| Suspensory ligaments | Slacken (less tension) | Pulled tight (more tension) |
| Lens shape | Fatter, more convex | Thinner, less convex |
| Refraction of light | More | Less |

**Worked example.** *You look up from a book to a bird in a distant tree. Explain how your eye focuses on the bird.*

1. The ciliary muscles **relax**.
2. The suspensory ligaments are pulled **tight**, so tension in them increases.
3. They pull the lens into a **thinner** shape.
4. Light is **refracted less**, so it is focused on the retina.

## 14.3 Mammalian hormones

A **hormone** is a chemical substance, produced by a gland and carried by the blood, which alters the activity of one or more specific target organs.

| Endocrine gland | Position (on a diagram) | Hormone(s) |
|---|---|---|
| Pituitary gland | Base of the brain | Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinising hormone (LH) |
| Adrenal glands | On top of the kidneys | Adrenaline |
| Pancreas | Below the stomach | Insulin and glucagon |
| Testes | Scrotum (male) | Testosterone |
| Ovaries | Lower abdomen (female) | Oestrogen and progesterone |

### Adrenaline

The adrenal glands release adrenaline in frightening, stressful or exciting situations, such as being chased by a dog, waiting to sit an exam or the start of a race. It:

- **increases the blood glucose concentration**, so more glucose is available for respiration in muscles;
- **increases the heart rate**, so glucose and oxygen reach the muscles faster.

More respiration releases more energy for muscle contraction.

### Nervous and hormonal control compared

| | Nervous control | Hormonal control |
|---|---|---|
| Speed of action | Fast | Slower |
| Duration of effect | Short | Longer lasting |

## 14.4 Homeostasis

**Homeostasis** is the maintenance of a constant internal environment, such as body temperature and blood glucose concentration.

**Negative feedback** works around a **set point** (the normal value):

```
 value rises above set point → detected → response brings value DOWN
 value falls below set point → detected → response brings value UP
```

The change itself switches on a response that reverses it, so the value stays close to the set point.

## 14.5 Temperature control

### The skin

On a diagram of the skin you must identify: **hairs**, **hair erector muscles**, **sweat glands**, **receptors**, **sensory neurones**, **blood vessels** and **fatty tissue** (the layer at the bottom).

### Insulation

Mammals keep a constant internal temperature, around 37 °C in humans. **Insulation** reduces heat loss to cold surroundings. The **fatty tissue** under the skin is a poor conductor of heat, and a layer of **air trapped by hairs** or fur insulates too.

### The hypothalamus and skin receptors

**Temperature receptors in the skin** detect changes in the temperature outside and send impulses along sensory neurones to the brain. The **hypothalamus** in the brain acts as the control centre: it monitors the temperature of the blood flowing through it, compares it with the set point and coordinates the responses below.

### The responses

| Response | When | How it helps |
|---|---|---|
| Sweating | Too hot | Sweat glands secrete sweat; water evaporates from the skin surface, transferring heat away from the body |
| Shivering | Too cold | Rapid, involuntary muscle contractions; the extra respiration releases heat |
| Hair erector muscles contract | Too cold | Hairs stand up and trap a thicker layer of air, which insulates |
| Vasodilation | Too hot | Arterioles supplying skin surface capillaries widen; more blood flows near the surface, so more heat is lost by radiation |
| Vasoconstriction | Too cold | Arterioles supplying skin surface capillaries narrow; less blood flows near the surface, so less heat is lost |

**Worked example.** *A swimmer gets out of a cold lake. Explain two ways her body reduces heat loss.*

1. Skin receptors detect the cold and the hypothalamus detects a fall in blood temperature below the set point.
2. **Vasoconstriction:** arterioles supplying the skin surface capillaries narrow, so less warm blood flows near the skin surface and less heat is lost by radiation.
3. **Hair erector muscles contract:** hairs stand up and trap a layer of air, which insulates the skin.

Shivering produces heat rather than reducing heat loss, so it does not answer this question.

## 14.6 Blood glucose control

### Why control is needed

Cells need a steady supply of glucose for respiration. Too little and cells, especially brain cells, cannot release enough energy. Too much glucose in the blood lowers its water potential, so water moves out of cells by osmosis.

### The liver and the pancreas

The **pancreas** detects the blood glucose concentration and releases the right hormone. The **liver** is the target organ that stores or releases glucose.

| Blood glucose | Pancreas releases | Effect on the liver | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Too high (after a meal) | Insulin | Converts glucose to glycogen and stores it | Blood glucose falls |
| Too low (exercise, fasting) | Glucagon | Converts glycogen to glucose and releases it | Blood glucose rises |

This is negative feedback around a set point.

**Worked example.** *The table gives one person's blood glucose concentration after a meal eaten at 0 minutes.*

| Time / min | 0 | 30 | 60 | 90 | 120 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Blood glucose / mg per 100 cm³ | 85 | 135 | 115 | 95 | 85 |

*Calculate the percentage increase from 0 to 30 minutes and explain the change after 30 minutes.*

```
increase = 135 − 85 = 50 mg per 100 cm³
percentage increase = 50 ÷ 85 × 100 = 58.8 % ≈ 59 %
```

Explanation: glucose from digested food is absorbed, so the concentration rises above the set point. The pancreas detects this and releases more insulin. Insulin makes the liver convert glucose into glycogen, so the concentration falls back to 85 by 120 minutes.

### Type 1 diabetes

In Type 1 diabetes the pancreas does not produce enough insulin. The signs you need are:

- **increased blood glucose concentration**;
- **glucose in the urine**.

The treatment is the **administration of insulin**, usually by injection.

## Common errors

- Writing that neurotransmitter "travels as an impulse" across the gap; it **diffuses**.
- Saying the ciliary muscles relax for near vision. They **contract**, and the ligaments slacken.
- Mixing up circular and radial muscles in the pupil reflex.
- Saying blood vessels "move closer to the skin". Arterioles **widen or narrow**; nothing moves.
- Saying sweat cools you by being produced. Cooling happens when water **evaporates**.
- Writing "glucagon" when you mean "glycogen". Glucagon is a hormone; glycogen is the stored carbohydrate.
- Saying a hormone travels along nerves. Hormones are carried in the **blood**.
