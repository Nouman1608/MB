## Section B

**4.** A therapist treats a client's phobia using systematic desensitisation.

**(a)** Identify the approach this treatment comes from. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain how classical and operant conditioning together explain the phobia's acquisition and maintenance. **[4]**

**5.** Evaluate the psychodynamic approach. **[6]**

**(a)** Name the three types of determinism and the approach associated with each. **[3]**

## Answers

**1. Negative reinforcement** — removing an **unpleasant** stimulus, which **increases** the behaviour [1]; e.g. taking a painkiller to remove a headache makes you more likely to take one again [1].
**Punishment** — adding an unpleasant stimulus, which **decreases** the behaviour [1]; e.g. being fined for speeding makes speeding less likely [1].
*Both involve something unpleasant — the difference is whether it is removed or added, and whether behaviour increases or decreases.*

**2.** Attention [1], retention [1], motor reproduction [1], motivation [1].

**3. Genotype** — the individual's actual **genetic make-up** [1]. **Phenotype** — the **observable characteristics**, resulting from the genotype interacting with the environment [1].

**4. (a)** The **behaviourist** approach [1].

**(b)** **Classical conditioning** explains **acquisition**: a neutral stimulus is associated with something frightening and becomes a conditioned stimulus [1] [1].
**Operant conditioning** explains **maintenance**: avoiding the phobic object **reduces anxiety**, which **negatively reinforces** the avoidance [1] [1].

**(c)** Because avoidance is what maintains the phobia [1], the treatment works by **exposing the client to the stimulus while preventing avoidance**, so the reinforcement cycle is broken and the association is extinguished [1].

**5. Strengths:** it was the **first approach to explain behaviour psychologically** rather than physically, and introduced psychotherapy [1]; it highlighted the influence of **childhood experience** on adult personality, which remains influential [1]; it has real explanatory power for defence mechanisms and repression [1].
**Weaknesses:** its concepts are **unfalsifiable** — the unconscious cannot be empirically tested, so by Popper's criterion the theory is unscientific [1]; it relies on **subjective case studies of a small, unrepresentative sample** of Freud's patients [1]; it is **psychically determinist**, denying free will [1].

**6. (a)** **Biological** determinism — the biological approach [1]. **Environmental** determinism — the behaviourist approach [1]. **Psychic** determinism — the psychodynamic approach [1].

**(b)** Behaviour is **caused**, but people can still exercise **conscious choice within those constraints** [1]; held by the **cognitive** approach [1].

**(c)** It undermines **legal and moral responsibility** [1]: if a person could not have acted otherwise, holding them blameworthy is difficult to justify [1]. However, determinism is also what makes psychology **scientific**, since science assumes causation and prediction — so the position has both costs and benefits [1].

**7. (a)** A measure or theory developed **within one culture** [1] and then applied to other cultures **as though it were universal** [1].

**(b)** The Strange Situation was developed using **American** child-rearing norms [1]. In Germany, higher rates of insecure-avoidant attachment reflect a **cultural value placed on early independence** [1], so the behaviour is **misclassified as insecure** when it may be culturally appropriate [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing negative reinforcement with punishment.
- Saying Freud was "unscientific" without explaining unfalsifiability.
- Describing a debate without attaching it to a named approach.
- Giving strengths only, or weaknesses only, in an evaluation.
- Confusing genotype with phenotype.
