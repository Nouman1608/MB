## Section A

**1.** State two inputs and two outputs of the atmospheric system. **[2]**

**2.** Outline why free oxygen did not build up in the atmosphere as soon as photosynthesis began. **[2]**

**3.** State why water vapour is not included in the composition of dry air. **[1]**

**4.** Distinguish between the effects of sulfate aerosols and soot on the absorption of sunlight. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why nitrogen, despite making up about 78% of dry air, is not a greenhouse gas. **[2]**

**6.** Define *albedo*. **[1]**

## Section B

**7.** (Data response) A weather balloon recorded the following temperatures over a fictional site.

| Height / km | 0 | 4 | 8 | 12 | 20 | 30 | 45 | 60 | 75 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Temperature / °C | 15 | −11 | −37 | −56 | −56 | −46 | −8 | −20 | −60 |

**(a)** Calculate the mean lapse rate between 0 and 12 km. **[2]**
**(b)** Identify the approximate heights of the tropopause and the stratopause from the data. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain the trend in temperature between 20 km and 45 km. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why the trend reverses above about 45–50 km. **[2]**

**8.** (Data response) A fictional catchment has the land cover shown.

| Cover | Share of area / % | Albedo |
|---|---|---|
| Snow | 50 | 0.85 |
| Coniferous forest | 40 | 0.12 |
| Bare rock | 10 | 0.30 |

**(a)** Calculate the overall albedo of the catchment. **[2]**
**(b)** In summer the snow melts to reveal grassland with an albedo of 0.22. Calculate the new overall albedo. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage increase in the fraction of sunlight absorbed between winter and summer. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain how earlier spring snowmelt could act as a positive feedback on warming. **[2]**

**9.** Outline the sequence of events by which the atmosphere changed from its early composition to one containing an ozone layer. **[4]**

**10.** Explain why weather occurs almost entirely in the troposphere. **[3]**

**11.** Explain why a lapse-rate calculation may overestimate the temperature fall in moist, rising air. **[2]**

**12.** Discuss the extent to which living organisms have controlled the composition of Earth's atmosphere. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Inputs, any two: solar radiation; gases from volcanoes, respiration, decomposition or combustion [1]. Outputs, any two: radiation emitted to space; gases absorbed by oceans, rocks or photosynthesising organisms [1]. **[2]**

**2.** The oxygen released first reacted with iron and other substances in the oceans and rocks [1]; only when these oxygen sinks were filled could free oxygen accumulate in the air [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Its concentration varies widely with temperature and place, so it is excluded to allow a fixed comparison of the other gases [1]. **[1]**

**4.** Sulfate aerosols mainly reflect and scatter sunlight back to space, cooling the surface [1], whereas soot absorbs sunlight, warming the air around it [1]. **[2]**

**5.** A nitrogen molecule is made of two identical atoms [1], so its vibrations do not change the distribution of charge and it cannot absorb infrared radiation [1]. **[2]**

**6.** The fraction (or percentage) of incoming solar radiation reflected by a surface [1]. **[1]**

**7. (a)** Fall = 15 − (−56) = 71 °C [1]; 71 ÷ 12 = **5.9 °C per km** (accept 5.92) [1].
**(b)** Tropopause: about 12–20 km, where temperature stops falling [1]. Stratopause: about 45 km, where temperature peaks [1].
**(c)** Temperature rises from −56 °C to −8 °C [1] because ozone in the stratosphere absorbs ultraviolet radiation, which heats the air [1].
**(d)** Above the stratopause there is little ozone to absorb UV [1], and the air is very thin, so temperature falls with height through the mesosphere [1]. **[8]**

**8. (a)** (0.50 × 0.85) + (0.40 × 0.12) + (0.10 × 0.30) = 0.425 + 0.048 + 0.030 [1] = **0.503** [1].
**(b)** (0.50 × 0.22) + 0.048 + 0.030 = 0.110 + 0.048 + 0.030 [1] = **0.188** [1].
**(c)** Absorbed: winter 1 − 0.503 = 0.497; summer 1 − 0.188 = 0.812 [1]; increase = (0.812 − 0.497) ÷ 0.497 × 100 = **63.4%** [1].
**(d)** Earlier melting exposes darker surfaces sooner, so more sunlight is absorbed [1]; the extra warming melts more snow and ice, further lowering albedo and amplifying the original change [1]. **[8]**

**9.** Volcanic outgassing produced an early atmosphere of water vapour, carbon dioxide and nitrogen, with almost no oxygen [1]. Water vapour condensed into oceans, which dissolved carbon dioxide, much of it locked into carbonate rocks [1]. Cyanobacteria released oxygen by photosynthesis, which accumulated once oxygen sinks were filled (about 2.4 billion years ago) [1]. Ultraviolet radiation acting on oxygen in the upper atmosphere formed ozone, creating a layer that screens the surface [1]. **[4]**

**10.** The troposphere contains most of the mass of the atmosphere [1] and almost all its water vapour, so clouds and precipitation form there [1]. It is heated from below, so warm air rises and convection drives vertical movement; the warmer stratosphere above acts as a lid that limits this mixing [1]. **[3]**

**11.** As moist air rises and cools, water vapour condenses, releasing latent heat [1], which warms the air, so it cools more slowly than the average lapse rate suggests [1]. **[2]**

**12.** Indicative content. Extended answers in the real exam are marked holistically against the IB's criteria, so treat these points as a guide:
- Photosynthesis by cyanobacteria produced the oxygen in today's atmosphere [1].
- Oxygen made the ozone layer possible, which allowed life to colonise land [1].
- Photosynthesis and burial of organic matter remove CO₂; respiration and decomposition return it [1].
- Marine organisms build carbonate shells that are buried as rock, storing carbon long term [1].
- Bacteria fix and denitrify nitrogen, controlling its cycling between air, soil and water [1].
- However, non-living processes also matter: volcanic outgassing supplied the original gases [1].
- Chemical weathering of rocks removes CO₂ over geological time, independent of life [1].
- Humans are now changing composition faster than natural sinks can respond [1].
- Judgement: life has been a major control on oxygen and carbon dioxide, but always alongside geological processes, and now human activity is the dominant short-term influence [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving the lapse rate without units, or subtracting temperatures with the wrong sign.
- Forgetting to weight each albedo by its share of the area.
- Calculating the change in albedo when the question asks about absorption.
- Explaining stratospheric warming without mentioning ozone and UV.
- Discussing the atmosphere's evolution without any non-living processes.
