## 6.2.4(a): Why make C–C bonds?

Oxidising an alcohol, hydrolysing a haloalkane or reducing a carbonyl compound all keep the same carbon skeleton. If a target has more carbon atoms than your starting material, you need a step that joins carbon to carbon.

This section gives you three such steps:

- a haloalkane plus cyanide ions adds one carbon (as –C≡N) in place of the halogen
- an aldehyde or ketone plus HCN adds one carbon (as –C≡N) to the carbonyl carbon
- benzene plus a haloalkane or acyl chloride, with a halogen carrier, joins a whole carbon chain to the ring

The nitrile can then be reduced to a primary amine (–CH₂NH₂) or hydrolysed to a carboxylic acid (–COOH). Either way the nitrile carbon stays in the chain.

**Counting rule.** Count the carbons in the target and the starting material. One extra carbon at a chain end usually means a nitrile step; a chain joined directly to a benzene ring usually means a Friedel–Crafts step.

## 6.2.4(b)(i): Haloalkanes with cyanide ions

**Reagent and conditions:** sodium or potassium cyanide dissolved in ethanol, heated under reflux.

General equation: R–X + CN⁻ → R–CN + X⁻ (X = Cl, Br or I)

The cyanide ion is a nucleophile (an electron pair donor); the lone pair on its carbon atom forms the new C–C bond.

**Naming nitriles.** The carbon of the –C≡N group counts as carbon 1 of the chain. So a nitrile always has one more carbon in its stem name than the haloalkane it came from. Bromoethane gives propanenitrile, CH₃CH₂CN.

### The nucleophilic substitution mechanism

The pattern is the one you learned for hydrolysis of a primary haloalkane with hydroxide ions (4.2.2 c), with CN⁻ as the nucleophile.

1. Show the C–X bond as polar: Cδ+ and Xδ−, because the halogen is more electronegative.
2. Start a curly arrow at the **carbon** lone pair of :CN⁻ and end it on the δ+ carbon.
3. Draw a curly arrow from the middle of the C–X bond to the halogen atom. The bond breaks heterolytically.
4. Show the products: the nitrile and a halide ion, X⁻.

### Worked example 1: a nitrile and its yield

8.25 g of 1-bromo-3-methylpentane, CH₃CH₂CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₂Br, is heated under reflux with excess KCN in ethanol. 4.12 g of nitrile is collected. Name the product and calculate the percentage yield. Use Mᵣ values C₆H₁₃Br = 164.9 and C₇H₁₃N = 111.0.

1. Product: CH₃CH₂CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₂CN. Number from the nitrile carbon: C1 is CN, C2 and C3 are CH₂, C4 carries the methyl. Name: **4-methylhexanenitrile**.
2. Equation: CH₃CH₂CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₂Br + KCN → CH₃CH₂CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₂CN + KBr
3. Amount of haloalkane = 8.25 ÷ 164.9 = 0.0500 mol. The ratio is 1 : 1.
4. Theoretical mass of nitrile = 0.0500 × 111.0 = 5.55 g
5. Percentage yield = 4.12 ÷ 5.55 × 100 = **74.2%**

The methyl branch moves from C3 in the haloalkane to C4 in the nitrile, because the chain gained a carbon at the end.

## 6.2.4(b)(ii): Carbonyl compounds with HCN

**Reagent:** HCN, supplied in practice as NaCN(aq) with H⁺(aq). The product is a hydroxynitrile: the former carbonyl carbon now carries both –OH and –C≡N.

General equation: R₂C=O + HCN → R₂C(OH)CN

This works for aldehydes and ketones, and again the chain grows by one carbon.

### The nucleophilic addition mechanism

1. Show the C=O bond as polar: Cδ+ and Oδ−.
2. Start an arrow at the cyanide's carbon lone pair; end it on the carbonyl carbon.
3. Curly arrow from the C=O double bond onto the oxygen. This gives an intermediate with O⁻.
4. The O⁻ is then protonated. The specification allows either H₂O (arrow from an O⁻ lone pair to an H of water, with an arrow from the O–H bond onto the oxygen, releasing OH⁻) or H⁺ (one arrow from an O⁻ lone pair to H⁺).

### Worked example 2: addition to a branched ketone

3-Methylbutan-2-one, CH₃COCH(CH₃)₂, reacts with NaCN(aq)/H⁺(aq). Give the product and describe the mechanism.

1. The C=O carbon is C2 of the ketone. CN adds to it and H adds to the oxygen.
2. Product: CH₃C(OH)(CN)CH(CH₃)₂, molecular formula C₆H₁₁NO.
3. Name it from the nitrile carbon: C1 is CN, C2 carries OH and a methyl, C3 carries a methyl, C4 is the end CH₃. Name: **2-hydroxy-2,3-dimethylbutanenitrile**.
4. Mechanism: :CN⁻ carbon lone pair attacks the δ+ carbonyl carbon; the π pair moves onto O; the intermediate is CH₃C(O⁻)(CN)CH(CH₃)₂; the O⁻ takes H⁺ from water or from H⁺(aq).

## 6.2.4(c): Taking the nitrile further

### (c)(i) Reduction to amines

**Reagent and conditions:** hydrogen gas with a nickel catalyst.

R–C≡N + 2H₂ → R–CH₂NH₂

Two H₂ molecules add across the triple bond. The nitrile carbon becomes the CH₂ next to NH₂, and the amine is numbered from that carbon.

### (c)(ii) Acid hydrolysis to carboxylic acids

**Reagent and conditions:** heat under reflux with a dilute acid, such as dilute hydrochloric acid.

R–C≡N + 2H₂O + H⁺ → R–COOH + NH₄⁺

With hydrochloric acid, write it as R–CN + 2H₂O + HCl → R–COOH + NH₄Cl. The nitrogen leaves as ammonium ions, and the nitrile carbon becomes the –COOH carbon.

### Worked example 3: two products from one nitrile

Take the 4-methylhexanenitrile from worked example 1.

**(a) Reduction.** 3.33 g of the nitrile (Mᵣ 111.0) is reduced completely with H₂/Ni. Give the product and the volume of hydrogen used at room temperature and pressure, where 1 mol of gas occupies 24.0 dm³.

1. CH₃CH₂CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₂CN + 2H₂ → CH₃CH₂CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₂CH₂NH₂
2. Name: **4-methylhexan-1-amine**, C₇H₁₇N.
3. Amount of nitrile = 3.33 ÷ 111.0 = 0.0300 mol
4. Amount of H₂ = 2 × 0.0300 = 0.0600 mol
5. Volume = 0.0600 × 24.0 = **1.44 dm³**

**(b) Hydrolysis.** Give the equation for hydrolysis with dilute hydrochloric acid.

CH₃CH₂CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₂CN + 2H₂O + HCl → CH₃CH₂CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₂COOH + NH₄Cl

The product is **4-methylhexanoic acid**, C₇H₁₄O₂: a five-carbon chain has become a six-carbon chain.

## 6.2.4(d): Friedel–Crafts reactions

A benzene ring is too stable to react with a haloalkane or acyl chloride alone. A halogen carrier, such as AlCl₃, FeCl₃ or FeBr₃, generates a strong electrophile. Keep the conditions dry, because aluminium chloride reacts with water.

**Alkylation** uses a haloalkane and adds an alkyl group:

C₆H₆ + R–Cl → C₆H₅–R + HCl

Electrophile formation: R–Cl + AlCl₃ → R⁺ + AlCl₄⁻

**Acylation** uses an acyl chloride and adds an acyl group, giving an aromatic ketone:

C₆H₆ + R–COCl → C₆H₅–COR + HCl

Electrophile formation: R–COCl + AlCl₃ → RCO⁺ + AlCl₄⁻

The carrier is regenerated: H⁺ + AlCl₄⁻ → AlCl₃ + HCl. The electrophile attacks the ring as in the halogenation mechanism from 6.1.1: π electrons bond to the electrophile, then the ring loses H⁺. Outcome 6.1.1(g) expects you to predict mechanisms like this for unfamiliar electrophiles.

### Worked example 4: alkylation and acylation

**(a)** Benzene reacts with chlorocyclopentane and AlCl₃. Write the equation and name the product.

C₆H₆ + C₅H₉Cl → C₆H₅–C₅H₉ + HCl. Product: **cyclopentylbenzene**, C₁₁H₁₄. The electrophile is the cyclopentyl cation, C₅H₉⁺.

**(b)** 5.46 g of benzene reacts with excess 2-ethylbutanoyl chloride, (CH₃CH₂)₂CHCOCl, and AlCl₃. 8.87 g of ketone is obtained. Find the percentage yield. Mᵣ: benzene 78.0, C₁₂H₁₆O 176.0.

1. C₆H₆ + (CH₃CH₂)₂CHCOCl → C₆H₅COCH(CH₂CH₃)₂ + HCl
2. Product name: **2-ethyl-1-phenylbutan-1-one**.
3. Amount of benzene = 5.46 ÷ 78.0 = 0.0700 mol
4. Theoretical mass = 0.0700 × 176.0 = 12.32 g
5. Percentage yield = 8.87 ÷ 12.32 × 100 = **72.0%**

## Putting it together: a route that adds a carbon

### Worked example 5: planning and overall yield

Plan a route from pentan-2-ol to 2-hydroxy-2-methylpentanoic acid, CH₃CH₂CH₂C(OH)(CH₃)COOH. The steps have yields of 85%, 70% and 62%.

1. Pentan-2-ol has five carbons and the target six, so one C–C bond is needed.
2. The target has OH and COOH on the same carbon, which points to a hydroxynitrile made from a ketone.
3. Step 1: oxidise pentan-2-ol to pentan-2-one with acidified dichromate under reflux.
4. Step 2: add HCN (NaCN(aq)/H⁺(aq)) to give 2-hydroxy-2-methylpentanenitrile.
5. Step 3: heat under reflux with dilute HCl to hydrolyse the nitrile to the acid.
6. Overall yield = 0.85 × 0.70 × 0.62 × 100 = **36.9%**

To make 5.00 g of the acid (Mᵣ 132.0): 5.00 ÷ 132.0 = 0.03788 mol, so 0.03788 ÷ 0.3689 = 0.1027 mol of pentan-2-ol (Mᵣ 88.0), which is **9.04 g**.

## Common errors

- Starting the cyanide curly arrow at nitrogen or at the minus sign. It starts at the lone pair on carbon.
- Using aqueous KCN for the haloalkane reaction. The specification pairs CN⁻ with ethanol.
- Naming the nitrile with the haloalkane's carbon count. The nitrile carbon is carbon 1, so the stem gains one carbon.
- Writing RCN + H₂ → RCH₂NH₂. Two H₂ are needed for the triple bond.
- Leaving out NH₄⁺ or NH₄Cl from the hydrolysis equation, or showing NH₃ as the product under acid conditions.
- Writing Friedel–Crafts equations without the HCl product, or forgetting to regenerate the AlCl₃.
- Calling the acylation product an aldehyde. It is a ketone.
