## Questions

**1.** Distinguish between average product and marginal product. **[3]**

**2.** A print shop in Country C adds operators to a fixed set of printers. Output is pages an hour.

| Operators | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Total product | 30 | 70 | 120 | 150 | 165 | 168 |

**(a)** Calculate the marginal product of the fourth operator and the average product with four operators. **[2]**
**(b)** After which operator do diminishing returns begin? Explain. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why average product falls when the fourth operator is hired. **[2]**

**3.** A firm has fixed costs of 240. Its total cost is 600 at 30 units, 720 at 40 units and 930 at 50 units.

**(a)** Calculate average variable cost and average total cost at 30 units. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate marginal cost over the range 40 to 50 units. **[1]**
**(c)** Average total cost is 18.0 at 40 units and 18.6 at 50 units. Use your answer to (b) to explain this change. **[3]**
**(d)** Fixed costs rise to 300. Calculate the new average total cost at 40 units and state the effect on marginal cost. **[2]**

**4.** A bottling plant in Country D uses sets of inputs, each costing 300 a day, with constant input prices. One set makes 80 bottles, two sets make 200 and three sets make 270.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in output when sets rise from 1 to 2 and from 2 to 3. **[2]**
**(b)** State the type of returns to scale in each case. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate long-run average cost with 1, 2 and 3 sets. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why this is not an example of the law of diminishing returns. **[2]**

**5.** A bakery can use method A (6 workers, 1 oven) or method B (2 workers, 4 ovens) for a daily batch. An oven costs 70 a day.

**(a)** Find the cheaper method when the wage is 45 a day. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the cheaper method when the wage is 60 a day. **[2]**
**(c)** Find the daily wage that would make the two methods equally costly. **[2]**

**6.** Five workers on a wage of 48 a day make 60 units. Training raises their output to 75 units.

**(a)** Calculate average variable cost before and after training. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change. **[1]**
**(c)** The wage then rises by 10%. Calculate the new average variable cost and compare it with the original. **[2]**

**7.** Short-run average cost (cost per unit) for three plants:

| Output | 200 | 400 | 600 | 1,000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Plant X | 5.40 | 7.05 | 9.20 | 13.80 |
| Plant Y | 6.30 | 4.85 | 4.90 | 5.90 |
| Plant Z | 13.04 | 7.28 | 5.52 | 4.40 |

**(a)** State the long-run average cost and the plant used at each output. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why the long-run average cost curve is never above a short-run average cost curve. **[2]**

**8.** A long-run average cost schedule (thousands of units): output 20 costs 12.0 a unit; 40 costs 8.0; 60 costs 6.5; 80 costs 6.0; 100 costs 5.8; 120 costs 5.8; 160 costs 5.8.

**(a)** Identify the output at which the minimum efficient scale is reached. **[1]**
**(b)** By what percentage is unit cost at output 40 higher than at the minimum efficient scale? **[2]**
**(c)** Market demand is 450. How many firms could the market support if each produced at the minimum efficient scale? **[2]**
**(d)** Explain how the minimum efficient scale creates a barrier to entry. **[2]**

**9.** Explain why a firm's long-run average cost curve may be L-shaped. **[4]**

**10.** "Once diminishing returns set in, a firm should stop hiring workers." Evaluate this view. **[9]**

**11.** Assess whether a high minimum efficient scale is likely to produce a concentrated industry. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Average product is total product divided by the number of units of the variable input. [1] Marginal product is the extra output from one more unit of that input. [1] When marginal product is above average product the average rises, and when it is below, the average falls. [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Define both terms, then add the link between them; two bare definitions leave out the relationship.

**2. (a)** MP = 150 - 120 = 30. [1] AP = 150 ÷ 4 = 37.5. [1] **[2]**
**(b)** After the third operator. [1] Marginal product peaks at 50 for the third operator, then falls to 30, 15 and 3. [1] **[2]**
**(c)** The fourth operator adds 30 pages, below the previous average of 40 (120 ÷ 3). [1] A marginal value below the average pulls the average down, to 37.5. [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Diminishing returns begin where marginal product starts to fall, not where total product stops rising; quote the figures.

**3. (a)** VC = 600 - 240 = 360, so AVC = 360 ÷ 30 = 12. [1] ATC = 600 ÷ 30 = 20. [1] **[2]**
**(b)** MC = (930 - 720) ÷ 10 = 21. [1] **[1]**
**(c)** MC of 21 is above the average of 18.0. [1] Each extra unit costs more than the average, so it pulls average cost up [1] to 18.6; this happens because diminishing returns raise the cost of extra units. [1] **[3]**
**(d)** TC at 40 = 720 + 60 = 780, so ATC = 780 ÷ 40 = 19.5. [1] Marginal cost is unchanged, because fixed costs do not vary with output. [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the subtraction and division, and in (c) compare marginal cost with the average instead of restating that the average rose.

**4. (a)** 1 to 2 sets: (200 - 80) ÷ 80 = +150%. [1] 2 to 3 sets: (270 - 200) ÷ 200 = +35%. [1] **[2]**
**(b)** Inputs rise 100%, output 150%: increasing returns to scale. [1] Inputs rise 50%, output 35%: decreasing returns to scale. [1] **[2]**
**(c)** LRAC = 300 ÷ 80 = 3.75, [1] then 600 ÷ 200 = 3.00 and 900 ÷ 270 = 3.33. [1] **[2]**
**(d)** The law of diminishing returns applies when at least one input is fixed and only one is varied. [1] Here every input changes together, so this is a long-run returns-to-scale effect. [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare the percentage change in output with that in inputs at each step; raw output figures alone do not show the type of returns.

**5. (a)** A = 6(45) + 70 = 340. [1] B = 2(45) + 280 = 370; A is cheaper. [1] **[2]**
**(b)** A = 6(60) + 70 = 430. [1] B = 2(60) + 280 = 400; B is cheaper. [1] **[2]**
**(c)** 6w + 70 = 2w + 280. [1] 4w = 210, so w = 52.5. [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* State which method is cheaper, not only the costs; the choice is the economic conclusion.

**6. (a)** VC = 5 × 48 = 240. Before: 240 ÷ 60 = 4.00. [1] After: 240 ÷ 75 = 3.20. [1] **[2]**
**(b)** (3.20 - 4.00) ÷ 4.00 = -20%. [1] **[1]**
**(c)** New wage 52.8, VC = 264, AVC = 264 ÷ 75 = 3.52. [1] This is 12% below the original 4.00, so the productivity gain outweighs the pay rise. [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Give the sign of a percentage change and say what it means for unit costs.

**7. (a)** 200: 5.40 (X); 400: 4.85 (Y). [1] 600: 4.90 (Y). [1] 1,000: 4.40 (Z). [1] **[3]**
**(b)** In the long run the firm can pick the plant with the lowest cost for each output. [1] So LRAC can only equal or be below any one plant's SRAC at a given output. [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Read the lowest figure in each column and name the plant used.

**8. (a)** 100 thousand units. [1] **[1]**
**(b)** (8.0 - 5.8) ÷ 5.8 [1] = 37.9%. [1] **[2]**
**(c)** 450 ÷ 100 = 4.5. [1] So at most 4 firms. [1] **[2]**
**(d)** A new entrant must produce about 100 thousand units to match established costs. [1] Smaller entrants face higher unit costs and need large finance and sales to compete. [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Say "at most 4" and not "4.5 firms"; a number of firms must be whole, and the reasoning should refer to the market size.

**9.** At low output, LRAC falls because of increasing returns to scale, such as specialist equipment. [1] At larger output, a firm can copy an efficient plant instead of enlarging it. [1] Management can reuse the same methods, so diseconomies are small. [1] LRAC therefore flattens instead of rising sharply, giving an L shape. [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each point should be a link in a chain (cause, effect on cost, effect on the curve) rather than a list of terms.

**10.** Diminishing returns mean marginal product falls, not that it is zero or negative. [1] Marginal cost rises, but output still grows. [1] A firm gains from hiring another worker while the extra revenue exceeds marginal cost. [1] Example: a wage of 60 and marginal product of 10 units gives marginal cost of 6 a unit. [1] Total product only falls once marginal product is negative, so stopping early gives up output. [1] Evaluation: it depends on the price, which fixes extra revenue per unit. [1] In the long run, adding capital can lift marginal product again. [1] Training or technology can raise the product curve. [1] Judgement: the view is wrong as a general rule; a firm should stop where the extra cost of a unit exceeds the extra revenue. [1] **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Define the term, apply a figure, then reach a judgement with a condition; a flat "true" or "false" leaves the evaluation thin.

**11.** MES is the lowest output at which LRAC is minimised. [1] If MES is large compared with demand, few firms can operate at the lowest cost. [1] Example: MES of 100 and demand of 450 allows at most 4 such firms. [1] Smaller firms have higher unit costs, so they are undercut and leave or never enter. [1] The cost gap is a barrier to entry, which supports concentration. [1] Limits: the market may grow, raising the number of efficient firms. [1] Niche products or leased capacity can let small firms survive. [1] Judgement: a high MES makes concentration likely, but the outcome depends on market size relative to MES. [1] **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Always compare MES with market demand; MES alone does not decide industry structure.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing a cost by the number of workers when the question wants cost per unit of output.
- Confusing marginal product with average product, and marginal cost with average cost.
- Saying diminishing returns means total output falls.
- Using returns to scale for a case where one input is fixed.
- Giving a percentage change without the sign, or using the wrong base year.
- Giving a judgement with no condition or reason.
