## The strategic thread

An operations strategy decides how the business will produce: at what scale, how flexibly, with which systems and how lean. Each choice involves a trade-off between **cost** and **responsiveness**. Lean production and high capacity utilisation cut cost but leave little slack; flexibility needs spare capacity, multi-skilled staff or adaptable machines, which cost money. The right balance depends on the market: a business selling standard products on price leans towards efficiency; one selling customised products at a premium leans towards flexibility.

## 9.3.1 Resource availability shapes operations decisions

**Worked decision: should Harlow Mouldings accept a large order?** A retailer offers Harlow a contract for 50,000 plastic storage boxes over three months, 40% more than its normal output.

- **Human resources.** Harlow's machine operators are already working overtime. It would need to recruit and train 12 temporary operators, which takes four weeks. Without them, the order cannot be met.
- **Marketing.** The retailer is a well-known brand; supplying it raises Harlow's profile. But if the order squeezes out regular customers, Harlow risks losing them.
- **Finance.** Harlow must buy plastic before the retailer pays on 60-day terms, so working capital is stretched. A bank overdraft or invoice finance may be needed.

The decision depends on whether all three resources can be secured in time. This is the pattern the syllabus wants: an operations decision is never purely an operations decision.

**The changing role of IT and AI.** IT first automated tasks: computer-aided design, computer-controlled machines, stock records. AI adds **prediction and judgement**:

- Forecasting demand from sales, weather and promotion data.
- Predictive maintenance: sensors flag when a machine is likely to fail, so it is serviced before it breaks.
- Visual inspection: cameras spot defects faster than people.
- Scheduling: matching orders to machines and staff.

The role of operations managers is shifting from collecting information to judging what the systems recommend. Risks include cost, dependence on data quality, cyber-security and job changes that staff may resist.

## 9.3.2 Flexibility and process innovation

| Flexibility of | Meaning | How achieved | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Volume | Raising or cutting output with demand | Temporary staff, overtime, subcontracting, spare capacity | Higher unit cost at peaks; idle capacity at troughs |
| Delivery time | Meeting short or changed deadlines | Inventory of finished goods, fast set-ups, priority scheduling | Inventory holding cost; disruption to other orders |
| Specification | Changing the product to suit customers | Multi-skilled staff, flexible machines, modular design | Training and equipment cost; lower economies of scale |

**Process innovation** means changing current processes or adopting new ways of producing or delivering. Examples: a restaurant moving to tablet ordering; a printer adopting digital presses that make short runs economic; a bank processing loans online. Process innovation can raise all three flexibilities at once. The digital press, for example, allows short runs (volume), quick turnaround (delivery) and personalised designs (specification). Its risks are capital cost, disruption while staff learn, and the chance that the new process does not deliver the expected gains.

## 9.3.3 ERP: following one order through the system

**Main features:** one shared database; linked modules for sales, purchasing, inventory, production, finance and HR; real-time updating; automatic data transfer between functions; reporting tools.

**Example: Ferris Furniture with ERP.** A customer orders 20 oak tables.

1. The sales module records the order; the system checks inventory and production capacity and gives a delivery date at once (**responses to change**).
2. Missing timber is ordered automatically from the supplier (**inventory control**).
3. The production schedule fits the job into spare machine time (**capacity utilisation**).
4. Labour and material costs are recorded as they occur, so the true cost of each table is known (**costing and pricing**).
5. The system shows which skilled workers are free, so they can be moved to the job (**workforce flexibility**).
6. Managers see orders, costs and profit margins on one dashboard (**management information**).

Without ERP, information passes by hand between departments, causing delays and errors. **Limits:** high cost, months of disruption, staff training, and "garbage in, garbage out": the system is only as good as the data entered.

## 9.3.4 Lean production as a system

Lean production aims to use the minimum of resources (materials, time, space, labour, inventory) to meet customer needs at the required quality. Its strategies support one another:

- **JIT** cuts inventory, but works only if quality is reliable, because there is no buffer stock to replace faulty parts. So JIT needs **kaizen** and **quality circles** to keep defects low.
- **Cell production** gives teams a whole task, which raises responsibility and motivation and supports kaizen suggestions.
- **Simultaneous engineering** shortens product development by running design, testing and production planning together.
- **Waste management** targets defects, waiting time, excess movement, over-production and excess stock.

**Links the syllabus names:** inventory (low under JIT), quality (built in, right first time), employee roles (multi-skilled, involved, responsible for quality), capacity management (high utilisation, little slack) and efficiency (lower unit costs).

**Limitations:** dependence on reliable suppliers; vulnerability to sudden demand spikes or supply disruption; high training cost; staff may feel pressure from constant improvement; and benefits are slow to arrive.

## 9.3.5 Operations planning: a fuller CPA example

**Brookfield Events** is building a temporary exhibition stand. Durations are in days.

| Activity | Description | Follows | Duration |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Agree design | – | 3 |
| B | Build frame | A | 5 |
| C | Order graphics | A | 4 |
| D | Install lighting | B | 6 |
| E | Print graphics | C | 2 |
| F | Train stand staff | C | 4 |
| G | Fit graphics | D, E | 3 |
| H | Final inspection | F, G | 2 |

**Network:** A runs from node 1 to 2; B from 2 to 3; C from 2 to 4; D from 3 to 5; E from 4 to 5; F from 4 to 6; G from 5 to 6; H from 6 to 7. No dummy is needed because every dependency is shown by the nodes. (A dummy, with zero duration, would be needed only if one activity depended on part, but not all, of the activities entering a node.)

**Forward pass (earliest start times):**

```
Node 1: 0
Node 2: 0 + 3 = 3
Node 3: 3 + 5 = 8
Node 4: 3 + 4 = 7
Node 5: larger of 8 + 6 = 14 and 7 + 2 = 9  -> 14
Node 6: larger of 14 + 3 = 17 and 7 + 4 = 11 -> 17
Node 7: 17 + 2 = 19
```

**Backward pass (latest finish times):** node 7 = 19; node 6 = 17; node 5 = 14; node 4 = smaller of 14 − 2 = 12 and 17 − 4 = 13, so 12; node 3 = 8; node 2 = smaller of 8 − 5 = 3 and 12 − 4 = 8, so 3; node 1 = 0.

**Minimum duration:** 19 days. **Critical path:** A, B, D, G, H (3 + 5 + 6 + 3 + 2 = 19).

**Floats** (total float = LFT at end − EST at start − duration; free float = EST at end − EST at start − duration):

| Activity | Total float | Free float |
|---|---|---|
| C | 12 − 3 − 4 = 5 | 7 − 3 − 4 = 0 |
| E | 14 − 7 − 2 = 5 | 14 − 7 − 2 = 5 |
| F | 17 − 7 − 4 = 6 | 17 − 7 − 4 = 6 |

**Using the results.** C can run up to 5 days late without delaying the stand, but any delay to C pushes back the earliest start of E and F (free float 0). F has 6 days of total float, so staff training can be moved later if the people running it are needed on critical work, without delaying the opening. If the frame (B) runs 2 days late, the whole project finishes on day 21. Managers watch critical activities most closely and schedule resources around the floats.

**Benefits of CPA:** shows the minimum duration, the activities that must not slip, and where resources can be moved; supports just-in-time ordering of materials for each activity. **Limitations:** durations are estimates; it ignores cost unless added; resource limits (one crane, two electricians) are not shown; and the network must be redrawn when plans change.

## Common mistakes

- Treating flexibility and lean production as free; both have costs and they can conflict.
- Describing ERP features without linking them to a named efficiency area.
- In CPA, taking the smaller value on the forward pass or the larger on the backward pass.
- Confusing total float (delay without delaying the project) with free float (delay without delaying the next activity).

## Exam technique

For **calculate** questions, show the forward and backward passes. For **analyse**, link an operations choice to cost, quality or speed and then to profit or competitiveness. For **evaluate** and **assess**, weigh benefits against cost, disruption and staff reaction, then judge what success depends on, such as reliable suppliers, accurate data or the nature of demand.
