This guide covers section 3.1.1, The purpose and nature of businesses, of the AQA GCSE Business (8132) specification, for first teaching from September 2017. It sits in Topic 1, Business in the real world, which is assessed on both Paper 1 and Paper 2. The [study guide for 3.1.1 and 3.1.2](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-the-purpose-and-nature-of-businesses-and-business-ownership/) gives the definitions and one example of each idea. This page concentrates on what the exam actually rewards: applying those ideas to a business you have never seen before, using clues from a case study.

Use it with the [3.1.1 revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-8132-purpose-and-nature-of-businesses-revision-notes/) and the [3.1.1 practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-8132-purpose-and-nature-of-businesses-practice/). ## What the specification asks

The specification says students should be able to:

| Statement | What it means in practice |
|---|---|
| Understand what a business is and the reasons for starting a business | Spot the reason in a case: producing goods, supplying services, distributing products, fulfilling a business opportunity, providing a good or service to benefit others |
| Understand the difference between goods and services, needs and wants | Classify what a business sells and explain why it matters |
| Understand the factors of production: land, labour, capital, enterprise | Identify each factor in a given business |
| Define opportunity cost | Name the single next best alternative given up, often with figures |
| Define the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors and give examples | Classify activities, including businesses that work in more than one sector |
| Understand enterprise and what is meant by an entrepreneur | Use both terms precisely |
| Outline the characteristics and objectives of an entrepreneur | Pick the ones a case shows and explain them |
| Understand that businesses face a constantly changing environment | Link changes in technology, the economic situation, legislation and environmental expectations to effects on a business |

## Reading the reason for starting a business from a case

Exam cases rarely say "she started the business to fulfil a business opportunity". They give clues. Learn to match them.

| Clue in the case | Reason for starting |
|---|---|
| "He makes hand-built bicycles in his garage" | Producing goods |
| "She offers dog walking and pet sitting" | Supplying services |
| "The firm buys from farms and delivers to restaurants" | Distributing products |
| "No one in the town sold vegan takeaway food" | Fulfilling a business opportunity |
| "Profits fund free sports coaching for local children" | Providing a good or service to benefit others |

A business can have more than one reason. A community bakery that bakes bread (producing goods) and gives unsold loaves to a food bank (benefiting others) shows two. If a question asks for one reason, choose the one with the strongest evidence and quote it.

## Goods, services, needs and wants: why the distinction matters

Classifying is only the first step; higher marks come from explaining why it matters to the business.

- **Goods** can be stored, so a business can make them in advance and hold stock. **Services** usually cannot be stored: an unsold hairdressing appointment is lost for good. Service businesses therefore worry about filling every slot.
- **Needs** are bought even when money is short; **wants** are cut first. A business selling wants is more exposed when the economic situation worsens, which links this point to the changing business environment.

Many businesses sell both goods and services. A garden centre sells plants (goods) and offers garden design (a service). Say which part of the business you are discussing.

## Factors of production in a real business

Every business needs all four factors, and a shortage of one limits what the others can achieve.

**Example: Lochan Ice Cream**, a fictional parlour that makes its own ice cream.

- **Land:** the premises, and the milk and fruit that come originally from farms.
- **Labour:** the two staff who serve customers and the owner's partner who makes the ice cream.
- **Capital:** freezers, mixers, the serving counter, the delivery van.
- **Enterprise:** the owner, who had the idea, borrowed the money and takes the risk.

If a freezer breaks in July, labour and land are still available but output falls because one piece of capital is missing. That kind of linked reasoning is what "understand" questions reward.

## Opportunity cost in business decisions

Opportunity cost is the **next best alternative given up** when a choice is made. Businesses face it whenever money, time or space is limited.

### Worked example

Marlowe's Café (fictional) has £12,000 to spend. The owner's estimates:

| Option | Extra profit per year |
|---|---|
| A new coffee machine that serves customers faster | £4,000 |
| New seating so more customers can sit in | £3,200 |
| Leaving the money in a savings account at 4% interest | £12,000 × 0.04 = £480 |

1. The best option is the coffee machine (£4,000).
2. The next best alternative is the new seating (£3,200).
3. So the opportunity cost of buying the machine is **the new seating and the £3,200 a year of extra profit it would have earned**.

The opportunity cost is not £480 (the third option) and not the total of the other two. Time works the same way: an hour the owner spends on paperwork is an hour not spent serving or planning promotions.

## Sectors and the chain of production

The three sectors are linked. Follow one product through them:

```
Primary:   a forestry business grows and fells trees
Secondary: a sawmill and furniture maker turns the timber into tables
Tertiary:  a furniture shop sells the tables and a firm delivers them
```

Two exam points follow. First, classify the **activity**, not the business name: a farm that sells jam made from its own fruit in a farm shop does primary work (growing), secondary work (making jam) and tertiary work (selling). Second, the sectors depend on each other. A poor harvest or a shortage of timber raises costs for every business further along the chain.

## Entrepreneurs: characteristics versus objectives

These two lists are often confused.

- A **characteristic** is what the entrepreneur is like: hard working, innovative, organised, willing to take a risk.
- An **objective** is what the entrepreneur wants: to be their own boss, flexible working hours, to pursue an interest, to earn more money, to identify a gap in the market, or because of dissatisfaction with their current job.

Case evidence points to one or the other. "She works twelve-hour days in the first year" shows a characteristic (hard working). "She wanted to fit work around her children" shows an objective (flexible working hours). Answer the one the question asks for.

## A constantly changing business environment

The specification names four sources of change. For each, an answer should state the change, its effect on the business and which functional area it hits.

| Change | Example | Effect on the business | Functional area |
|---|---|---|---|
| Technology | Customers expect to order and pay by app | Must invest in an app or join a delivery platform | Operations, marketing |
| Economic situation | Household incomes fall | Fewer sales of wants; may need lower prices or cheaper ranges | Marketing, finance |
| Legislation | A higher legal minimum wage | Wage costs rise, squeezing profit | Human resources, finance |
| Environmental expectations | Customers reject plastic packaging | Must switch to dearer packaging or lose customers | Operations, marketing |

## Exam technique

Papers 1 and 2 each have a Section A of multiple-choice and short-answer questions, then two case-study sections. This section is tested in all three.

- **Define:** give the precise meaning. For opportunity cost, the words "next best alternative" are essential.
- **Identify or state:** a word or phrase, with no explanation.
- **Explain:** make a point, then develop it with "because" or "so", applied to the business.
- **Analyse:** build a chain of consequences, for example change → effect → result for profit.
- **Evaluate:** weigh both sides and reach a justified judgement.

**Model "explain" answer.** *Explain one reason why a business selling wants may lose sales when the economy weakens.* "When incomes fall, customers prioritise needs such as food and rent, so they cut back on wants such as Lochan's ice cream. This lowers Lochan's revenue, especially in a month when it relies on passing trade." The point is made, developed and applied.

## Common mistakes

- Giving a reason for starting a business with no evidence from the case.
- Treating capital as money rather than equipment and machinery.
- Adding up all the options given up instead of naming the next best one.
- Classifying a business by its name rather than by each activity it carries out.
- Mixing up characteristics and objectives of an entrepreneur.
- Describing a change in the environment without its effect on the business.
