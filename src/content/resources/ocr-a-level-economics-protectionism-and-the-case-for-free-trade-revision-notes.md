## Methods of protectionism

| Method | What it does | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| Specific tariff | Fixed tax per imported unit | Tariff ÷ price = ad valorem equivalent |
| Ad valorem tariff | Percentage of the import price | Amount rises with the price |
| Quota | Cap on units allowed in | Rectangle is quota rent |
| Subsidy | Payment to domestic producers | Taxpayers pay; consumer price unchanged |
| Embargo | Total prohibition | Extreme; price rises to the no-trade level |
| Voluntary export restraint | Exporter caps its own sales | Agreed under pressure |
| Administrative barriers | Standards, licences, delays | No revenue; hard to measure |
| Local content rules | Required share of domestic inputs | Raises costs for assemblers |

## Tariff diagram: method in steps

1. Sketch demand D, supply S and a flat world price line (a small country), with price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal.
2. Add the tariff to get the new price line.
3. Read quantities demanded and supplied at both prices. Imports are the gap between them.
4. Label four areas between the price lines:
   - a is the producer surplus gain.
   - b is the production triangle, the extra output at higher domestic cost.
   - c is the revenue rectangle: tariff times imports.
   - d is the consumption triangle, the lost demand.
5. Consumer surplus falls by a + b + c + d. The net welfare loss is b + d.

**Worked reminder (illustrative).** World price 10, Qd = 80 − 2P, Qs = 3P − 20. Free trade: Qd = 60, Qs = 10, imports 50. A tariff of 2 gives a price of 12, Qd = 56, Qs = 16, imports 40, revenue 80. Consumers lose 116; producers gain 26; revenue is 80; so the net loss is 116 − 26 − 80 = 10, made of 6 (production) and 4 (consumption).

## Tariff versus quota versus subsidy

| Feature | Tariff | Quota | Subsidy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fixes | Price | Quantity | Producer price |
| Government revenue | Yes | Only if licences are sold | None; it pays |
| If demand rises | Imports rise, price fixed | Price rises, imports fixed | Imports rise |
| Consumer price | Higher | Higher | Unchanged |
| Net welfare loss | Two triangles | Two triangles | Production triangle only |

Quota rent = (domestic price − world price) × quota. With a quota of 40 and a price of 12 against a world price of 10, rent is 80.

## Effectiveness: what to weigh

- **Elasticity** of demand and supply: inelastic home supply means few jobs saved.
- **Retaliation** and trade wars.
- **Size**: a large importer can pass part of the tariff to exporters; a small one cannot.
- **Aim**: revenue, infant industry or deficit reduction conflict with one another.
- **Time**: temporary and declining beats permanent.
- **Inputs**: tariffs on components raise domestic costs.
- **Cost per job saved**: net welfare loss or consumer loss divided by jobs protected.
- **Prohibitive point**: when the tariff cancels all imports, revenue is zero.

## Quota diagram: method in steps

1. Draw demand, supply and the world price line as before.
2. Treat the quota as the only gap allowed between quantity demanded and quantity supplied.
3. Find the price at which the horizontal distance between supply and demand equals the quota. That price is the new domestic price.
4. Read the quota rent as (new price − world price) × quota.
5. If demand later rises, the gap stays fixed, so the price climbs further.

**Reminder.** With the same market and demand raised to Qd = 90 − 2P, a quota of 30 solves 90 − 2P = 3P − 20 + 30, so P = 16, Qd = 58, Qs = 28, imports 30 and rent (16 − 10) × 30 = 180.

## Tariff schedule: the revenue pattern

Same market (world price 10, Qd = 80 − 2P, Qs = 3P − 20):

| Tariff | Price | Imports | Revenue |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 10 | 50 | 0 |
| 2 | 12 | 40 | 80 |
| 5 | 15 | 25 | 125 |
| 8 | 18 | 10 | 80 |
| 10 | 20 | 0 | 0 |

Revenue rises, peaks and falls to zero. The no-trade price is 20 (where 80 − 2P = 3P − 20), so a tariff of 10 or more is prohibitive. Use this to explain why protection and revenue conflict.

## Large country reminder

If a large importer's tariff pushes the world price down from 10 to 9, the exporter bears part of the tax. The importer gains from better terms of trade, but the gain is at the exporter's expense, so retaliation is likely and may cancel it.

## Writing an evaluation chain

- Point: name the effect, such as higher prices for consumers.
- Reason: tie it to the diagram, such as the consumption triangle.
- Context: use the country, product and elasticity in the stem.
- Counter: say who gains, such as producers or the government.
- Judgement: state the condition that tips the balance, such as temporary protection or likely retaliation.

## Case for and against protectionism

| For | Against |
|---|---|
| Infant industries | Welfare loss and higher prices |
| Anti-dumping | Retaliation and trade wars |
| Strategic and security goods | Less competition, so X-inefficiency |
| Slows structural unemployment | Hurts exporters and users of inputs |
| Revenue | High cost per job; lobbying |
| Trade deficit and diversification | Infant industries that stay dependent |
| Optimal tariff for a large country | Regressive effect on poor households |
| Standards on safety and environment | Rules can restrict use |

**Judgement phrases:** it depends on whether the industry is new or mature, the protection temporary, the country large, and partners likely to retaliate.

## Case for and against free trade

| For | Against |
|---|---|
| Comparative advantage raises world output | Structural unemployment; uneven losses |
| Economies of scale in bigger markets | Over-specialisation and exposure to price falls |
| Competition, efficiency, variety | Primary exporters may face weak terms of trade |
| Cheaper imports, access to technology | Environmental costs and low-wage competition |
| Gains exceed losses in total | Gains need mobile resources and compensation |

## International competitiveness

- Unit labour cost = wage cost per unit of output = wage ÷ productivity.
- Growth: (1 + wage growth) ÷ (1 + productivity growth) − 1.
- Index: ULC index = wage index ÷ productivity index × 100. Wages index 110 and productivity index 104 gives 105.8.

| Policy | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Training and education | Lifts productivity | Slow, costly |
| Infrastructure and R&D | Lowers costs, raises quality | Long lags |
| Lower tax, deregulation | Encourages investment | Revenue loss, side effects |
| Competition policy, flexible labour | Cost pressure | Inequality, insecurity |
| Subsidies | Quick | Opportunity cost, retaliation |

## Must-know distinctions

- Transfer (revenue, producer gain) versus deadweight loss (two triangles).
- Quota rent versus tariff revenue.
- Specific versus ad valorem.
- Fixed price (tariff) versus fixed quantity (quota).
- Protecting an industry versus protecting a country's welfare.
- Short-run measures versus supply-side policy.

## Quick self-test

1. A specific tariff of 5 is put on a good with a price of 25. State the ad valorem equivalent.
2. An ad valorem tariff of 15% applies to a good priced at 40. Find the tariff and the price paid.
3. Using world price 10, Qd = 80 − 2P and Qs = 3P − 20, find imports under free trade.
4. With a tariff of 2 in question 3, find imports and revenue.
5. In question 4, state the net welfare loss.
6. A quota of 40 raises the price from 10 to 12. Find the quota rent.
7. A firm pays 18 an hour and produces 6 units an hour. Find the labour cost per unit.
8. Wages index 110, productivity index 104. Find the unit labour cost index.
9. Give one reason the net welfare loss from a subsidy is smaller than from a tariff, and one cost to set against it.
10. State two conditions that make the infant-industry case stronger.

### Answers

1. 5 ÷ 25 = 20%.
2. Tariff = 0.15 × 40 = 6; price paid = 46.
3. Qd = 60, Qs = 10, imports = 50.
4. Qd = 56, Qs = 16, imports = 40; revenue = 2 × 40 = 80.
5. 0.5 × 2 × 6 + 0.5 × 2 × 4 = 10.
6. (12 − 10) × 40 = 80.
7. 18 ÷ 6 = 3.
8. 110 ÷ 104 × 100 = 105.8.
9. Consumers still pay the world price, so no consumption triangle; but taxpayers fund the cost.
10. The protection is temporary or declining, and the industry has a realistic route to reaching scale.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Not recalculating both quantities after the price changes.
- Treating revenue as a deadweight loss.
- Saying a quota raises government revenue without saying who gets the rent.
- Giving a list of arguments with no judgement about context.
- Ignoring retaliation when evaluating protection.
- Forgetting that productivity is part of unit labour costs.
- Describing the gains from free trade without saying who loses.
- Using the same example for every country in the stem.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the infant industry argument for protection?**
New firms may need time to reach scale before they can compete with mature foreign rivals, so temporary protection can help them grow. It is more likely to work if the protection is temporary and declining rather than permanent.

**Why might a tariff save few jobs?**
If domestic supply is price inelastic, home firms cannot expand output much when the price rises, so few extra jobs are created. Retaliation by trading partners can also cost jobs in export industries.

**What is a prohibitive tariff?**
A prohibitive tariff is high enough to remove all imports, so the domestic price rises to the no-trade level and the tariff raises no revenue.

**How do you measure the cost per job saved by protection?**
Divide the net welfare loss, or the loss to consumers, by the number of jobs protected. This figure is a useful evaluation point in OCR A Level Economics (H460) answers on tariffs.
