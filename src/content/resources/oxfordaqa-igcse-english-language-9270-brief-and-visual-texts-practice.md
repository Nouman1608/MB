## The source pack: food waste (all original)

**Source A (poster, described).** A photograph fills the poster: a full supermarket trolley tipped on
its side in a rubbish skip, apples and bread spilling out. It is taken from above, looking down into
the skip. The colours are bright and fresh in the trolley, grey and dirty in the skip. At the top, in
large black capitals: "YOU PAID FOR THIS." At the bottom, in smaller type: "Plan. Buy less. Waste
less."

**Source B (infographic, described).** Titled "Where does our food go?", it shows a large plate
divided into three segments. The biggest segment, coloured green, is labelled "Eaten". A smaller red
segment is labelled "Thrown away at home", and a thin grey segment is labelled "Lost before it
reaches the shop". Each segment has an icon: a smiling face, a bin, a lorry. No figures are given.

**Source C (social media post).** "Made soup from the 'ugly' veg nobody wanted at the market. Best
lunch all week. #WonkyIsWonderful"

**Source D (notice in a school canteen).** "Take what you'll eat. Come back if you're still hungry."

## Section A — Short questions

**1.** In Source A, what is the most salient feature, and why do you think the designer made it
dominant? **[2]**

**2.** Explain the effect of the high camera angle in Source A. **[2]**

**3.** Explain how the slogan "YOU PAID FOR THIS." changes the meaning of the photograph in Source A.
**[3]**

**4.** In the smaller slogan in Source A, "Plan. Buy less. Waste less.", identify **two** language
features and explain the effect of **one**. **[3]**

**5.** In Source B, explain why the designer might have chosen the colours green and red for the two
main segments. **[2]**

**6.** Explain the effect of the inverted commas around "ugly" in Source C. **[2]**

## Section B — Longer questions

**7.** How does Source A use presentational features to influence the reader? **[8]**

**8.** Source B gives no figures. Explain how it still presents a perspective on food waste. **[5]**

**9.** Analyse how Source C uses language to present an attitude to food waste in very few words.
**[5]**

**10.** Compare the tone of Source A and Source D. **[6]**

**11.** Compare how Source A and Source C try to change readers' behaviour. **[8]**

**12.** Write a precise description of Source A of no more than two sentences, suitable for use as
evidence in an answer. Then explain why it is better evidence than "It shows a trolley in a bin."
**[3]**

## Answers

**How these are marked.** Short questions are point-marked as shown. For the longer questions, the
bracketed credits show one way to reach the tariff; other valid points of the same quality earn the
same credit.

**1.** The tipped trolley of food spilling into the skip [1]; it is dominant so that the waste of food
is the first and strongest message the reader receives [1].

**2.** Looking down into the skip makes the reader seem to stand over the waste [1], which can create
a sense of judgement or responsibility, as if the reader is inspecting what has been thrown away [1].

**3.** Without the slogan, the image could simply show rubbish [1]; the direct address "YOU" makes the
reader responsible [1], and "PAID" turns the waste into the reader's own lost money, anchoring the
image as a personal warning [1].

**4.** Two features [1 each]: imperatives; short sentences with full stops; repetition of "less"; a
list of three. Effect of one [1], for example: the imperatives turn the message into simple
instructions the reader can follow at once.

**5.** Green suggests health, freshness and approval, so eating the food seems good [1]; red suggests
danger or warning, so throwing food away at home seems a problem [1].

**6.** They suggest the writer does not accept the label [1], mocking the idea that vegetables are
worthless because of their appearance [1].

**7.** Credit up to 8: the salient trolley makes waste the main message [2]; the high angle places the
reader above the skip, implying judgement [2]; the contrast between fresh, bright food and the grey,
dirty skip shows the shock of good food spoiled [2]; the large capital slogan with direct address,
followed by the smaller practical slogan, moves the reader from guilt to action [2].

**8.** Credit up to 5: the title "our food" includes the reader [1]; the red home segment is larger than
the grey segment for food lost before shops, which places responsibility on households [1]; the bin
icon makes home waste concrete [1]; the smiling face links eating with happiness [1]; the absence of
figures keeps the message simple and emotional rather than factual [1].

**9.** Credit up to 5: the past-tense, first-person account gives a personal example [1]; "nobody
wanted" sets the writer apart from wasteful shoppers [1]; the inverted commas around "ugly" challenge
the label [1]; the minor sentence "Best lunch all week" shows enthusiasm briefly [1]; the hashtag's
alliteration and positive adjective "Wonderful" invite others to join a campaign [1].

**10.** Credit up to 6: Source A is accusing and dramatic, using capitals and direct blame [2]; Source
D is friendly and reassuring, using contracted, conversational language [2]; both use imperatives and
the second person, but Source D's "Come back if you're still hungry" removes the fear of going
without, whereas Source A works through guilt [2].

**11.** Credit up to 8: Source A uses shock and guilt through image and slogan [2], while Source C uses
a positive personal example [2]; Source A addresses the reader directly as responsible [2], whereas
Source C models the behaviour and invites joining through the hashtag [2].

**12.** A precise description naming at least two of position, angle and colour [2], for example: "A
full supermarket trolley lies tipped on its side in a skip, photographed from above, with bright
apples and bread spilling into the grey rubbish." Explanation [1]: it identifies the exact features
being analysed, so the effects can be explained.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing Source A without analysing the effect of angle, colour or slogan.
- Adding figures to Source B that it does not give.
- Writing only a line on Sources C and D because they are short.
- In Questions 10 and 11, writing about each source separately instead of comparing.
