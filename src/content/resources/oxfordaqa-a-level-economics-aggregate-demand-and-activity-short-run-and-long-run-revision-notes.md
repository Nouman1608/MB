## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Aggregate demand (AD) | Total planned spending on a country's output at each price level |
| Level of economic activity | Output, employment and incomes in the economy, strongly influenced by AD |
| Injection | Spending entering the flow from outside household spending: investment, government spending, exports |
| Withdrawal (leakage) | Income not re-spent on domestic output: saving, tax, imports |
| Multiplier (k) | How many times larger the final rise or fall in income is than the first injection |
| SRAS | Real output firms will supply at each price level, with production costs given |
| LRAS | Vertical curve at the normal capacity level of output |
| Normal capacity output | The level of output shown by the position of the vertical LRAS curve |

## Multiplier: formulas and method

| Version | Formula |
|---|---|
| From income | k = final change in Y ÷ first change in injection |
| From MPC | k = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC), where MPC is the fraction of extra income that goes to domestic output |
| From withdrawals | k = 1 ÷ MPW, with MPW = MPS + MPT + MPM |

Working order:

1. Find MPW by adding MPS, MPT and MPM (or take 1 − MPC).
2. Divide 1 by that figure to find k.
3. Multiply the change in injection by k. A fall in injection gives a negative answer.
4. To find the injection needed for a target, divide the gap by k.
5. Comment: leakages, spare capacity, time lags.

Worked reminders (all invented):

- MPC = 0.75 so k = 1 ÷ 0.25 = 4. An investment rise of 12 gives 12 × 4 = 48. A fall in exports of 9 gives −36.
- MPS = 0.04, MPT = 0.16, MPM = 0.30, so MPW = 0.50 and k = 2. An injection of 25 raises income by 50.
- Output gap of 80 with k = 5 needs an injection of 80 ÷ 5 = 16.
- A local multiplier works the same way. If residents spend 40% of extra income locally, local k = 1 ÷ 0.60 = 1.67.

Why the real effect can be smaller than the formula:

- Higher leakages mean a smaller multiplier.
- Near normal capacity, extra demand raises prices, not real output.
- Rounds of spending take time.
- Expectations can change saving or tax behaviour.

## AD and the level of economic activity

| Position | What a rise in AD mainly does |
|---|---|
| Well below normal capacity (negative output gap) | Raises output and employment |
| Close to normal capacity | Raises the price level |
| Persistent weak AD | Can leave demand-deficient (cyclical) unemployment |

Output gap (% of capacity) = (actual − capacity) ÷ capacity × 100. If actual output is 450 and capacity is 500, the gap is −10.0%.

## SRAS: determinants

The price level and production costs are the main determinants. Remember the rule: price level changes mean a movement along; cost changes mean a shift.

| Change in cost factor | SRAS | Typical effect |
|---|---|---|
| Money wage rates rise | Shifts left | Higher prices, lower output |
| Raw material prices rise | Shifts left | Cost-push pressure |
| Indirect taxes rise | Shifts left | Higher costs at each price level |
| Productivity rises | Shifts right | Lower cost per unit |
| Raw material prices or wage growth fall | Shifts right | Lower prices, higher output |

Labour cost per unit of output = hourly wage ÷ units produced per hour. If wages and productivity both rise 5%, labour cost per unit is unchanged (1.05 ÷ 1.05 = 1.00). If wages rise 3% and productivity 1%, it rises by 1.03 ÷ 1.01 − 1 = 2.0%.

## LRAS: determinants

LRAS is assumed vertical, and its position is the normal capacity level of output. A rightward shift is underlying economic growth.

| Determinant | Link to capacity |
|---|---|
| Capital stock | More equipment per worker |
| Technology | More output from given inputs |
| Working population | More workers available |
| Productivity | More output per worker |
| Attitudes | Work ethic and openness to change |
| Enterprise | More new firms and risk-taking |
| Factor mobility | Easier movement of labour and capital to where they are needed |
| Economic incentives | Rewards that encourage work, saving and investment |

Capacity growth compounds. If the workforce grows 2% and productivity 3%, capacity grows 1.02 × 1.03 − 1 = 5.06%, not 5%.

## Diagrams in words

Label the vertical axis price level and the horizontal axis real output.

- **Spare capacity.** AD and SRAS cross to the left of the vertical LRAS line. The distance to LRAS is the negative output gap. Shift AD right and the equilibrium moves along SRAS towards LRAS, mostly raising output.
- **Cost-push shock.** SRAS shifts left. The price level ends up higher and real output lower, possibly further left of LRAS.
- **Productivity gain.** SRAS shifts right and, if it lasts, LRAS also shifts right. Price level falls or rises less and output rises.
- **Underlying growth.** LRAS shifts right. Mark the old and new normal capacity on the horizontal axis.
- **Keynesian stuck point.** AD and SRAS cross well left of LRAS. Show that no force in the diagram moves the equilibrium quickly.

## Evaluation prompts

Ask these questions to move from describing to judging.

| Claim | Ask |
|---|---|
| A rise in injections will raise income by k times | How large are leakages, and how close is the economy to capacity? |
| A rise in AD closes the output gap | Was the gap measured correctly, and how long will the rounds take? |
| Lower costs will shift SRAS right | Is the change temporary (a one-off price fall) or lasting (productivity)? |
| Raising capital stock raises LRAS | How long does it take, and is there enough demand to use the new capacity? |
| The economy will return to normal capacity by itself | Do wages and prices adjust quickly, or can it stay below for years? |

## Mini worked chain: why one shock matters

Suppose raw material prices rise in Country A (invented). Write the chain in this order:

1. Firms face higher costs at each price level.
2. SRAS shifts left.
3. The equilibrium price level rises and real output falls.
4. Employment tends to fall, so incomes and AD may weaken.
5. Judgement: the effect is larger if the economy has little spare capacity, and temporary if the price rise reverses.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Injection versus withdrawal.** Injections raise the flow, withdrawals take from it. The multiplier depends on how much leaks at each round.
- **Movement along versus shift.** The price level moves you along SRAS. Costs shift it.
- **SRAS versus LRAS.** SRAS depends on costs given in the short run. LRAS depends on productive capacity.
- **Actual versus potential growth.** A rise in AD can raise actual output (if there is spare capacity). Only a rightward LRAS shift raises normal capacity.
- **Keynesian view.** An economy can stay well below normal capacity for many years, so waiting for wages and prices to adjust may be too slow. The counter-argument is that stimulus can be inflationary if the output gap has been overstated.

## Quick self-test

1. Define the multiplier in your own words.
2. If 75% of extra income is re-spent on domestic output, what is k?
3. With that k, what is the effect of an injection of 12?
4. Exports fall by 9 and k = 4. What happens to income?
5. MPS = 0.04, MPT = 0.16 and MPM = 0.30. Find k.
6. Output gap is 80 and k = 5. What injection is needed?
7. Actual output is 450 and capacity is 500. Find the output gap as a percentage.
8. A rise in AD raises the price level. Is that a shift or a movement for SRAS?
9. Wages rise 3% and productivity 1%. What happens to labour cost per unit?
10. What does the position of the vertical LRAS curve represent?
11. State two determinants of LRAS.
12. Give the Keynesian view about normal capacity.

### Answers

1. How many times larger the final change in income is than the first injection.
2. k = 1 ÷ (1 − 0.75) = 4.
3. 12 × 4 = 48.
4. It falls by 9 × 4 = 36.
5. MPW = 0.50, so k = 2.
6. 80 ÷ 5 = 16.
7. (450 − 500) ÷ 500 × 100 = −10.0%.
8. A movement along SRAS.
9. It rises by about 2.0%, so SRAS shifts left.
10. The normal capacity level of output.
11. Any two from capital stock, technology, working population, productivity, attitudes, enterprise, factor mobility, economic incentives.
12. An economy can get stuck well below its normal capacity level of output for many years.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using 1 ÷ MPC instead of 1 ÷ (1 − MPC).
- Forgetting that tax and imports are leakages as well as saving.
- Multiplying by k and calling the answer the multiplier, not the change in income.
- Drawing a shift of SRAS for a change in the price level.
- Shifting LRAS because AD rose.
- Naming a determinant of LRAS but not saying why it raises capacity.
- Stating the multiplier effect without a comment on spare capacity or leakages when the question asks for evaluation.
- Rounding percentages too early in a compounding calculation.
