This guide covers section 3.2.3.2, Population and communication, in Section C (Global issues) of Unit 2 of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Geography (9230) specification, Version 4.3. In Section C, students study **either** Water and energy resources **or** Population and communication, so you only need this theme if your class chose the population option. It has four key ideas: global population is increasing and strategies manage this; there are reasons for international migration, with significant impacts; developments in ocean ports and airports offer opportunities and challenges; and the development of ICT has led to worldwide opportunities. The specification gives only some examples, so this guide offers them as a framework. If your class used other places, keep the headings.

## What the specification lists

- The change in **world population over time**.
- **Location and causes** of global population growth: improved medical care, improved sanitation, the role of aid, religion, the status of women, the role of children, age structure and education.
- **Examples** of the economic, environmental and social impacts of population change, and of strategies to manage population growth (contrasting approaches to reducing the birth rate).
- **International migration** in the twenty-first century: causes (refugees, asylum seekers, economic migrants) and demographic, economic, social and political impacts.
- **Ocean shipping, ports and airports:** development, global patterns, advantages for manufacturing, trade and tourism, and a **case study of airport expansion**.
- **ICT:** developments, access to the internet and phones, cause and pattern; the role of ICT in economic growth in NEEs (call centres, TNC investment, trade, tourism).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Birth rate | Live births per 1 000 people per year |
| Fertility rate | Average number of children born per woman |
| Natural increase | Birth rate minus death rate |
| Dependency | People too young or too old to work, who rely on the working population |
| Refugee | Someone forced to flee their country because of war or persecution |
| Asylum seeker | Someone who has asked another country for protection and is waiting for a decision |
| Economic migrant | Someone who moves to find work or better living standards |
| Remittances | Money sent home by migrants |
| Containerisation | Shipping goods in standard-size steel boxes |
| ICT | Information and communication technology |
| Digital divide | The gap between people with and without access to ICT |

## World population change

World population grew very slowly for thousands of years. It reached 1 billion in about 1800 and 2 billion in the late 1920s. Then it accelerated: 3 billion around 1960, 7 billion in 2011 and 8 billion in 2022. Growth is now slowing, and the United Nations projects a peak of around 10 billion later this century.

Growth is fastest in LICs, especially in sub-Saharan Africa and parts of South Asia. Many HICs and some NEEs now have low growth or falling populations.

### Causes of rapid growth

- **Improved medical care:** vaccination and treatment reduce deaths, particularly of children.
- **Improved sanitation and clean water:** reduce disease, so death rates fall.
- **Role of aid:** funds clinics, vaccination programmes and water projects.
- **Religion:** some religious teachings encourage large families or discourage contraception.
- **Status of women:** where women have little education or independence, they marry early and have more children.
- **Role of children:** in poor, rural areas, children work on farms and care for parents in old age; high infant mortality encourages large families.
- **Age structure:** a young population has many women of childbearing age, so births continue to rise even when family size falls (population momentum).
- **Education:** educated women usually have fewer children.

Death rates fall first and birth rates fall later, which is why growth can be rapid for a time.

## Impacts of population change

| Type | Impact |
|---|---|
| Population and resource balance | Too many people for resources (overpopulation) leads to shortages; too few leads to under-use |
| Economic | A young population provides labour and a growing market, but needs schools and jobs; unemployment can rise |
| Age structure | A high proportion of children brings dependency; a growing elderly population strains pensions and healthcare |
| Food supply | More mouths to feed; farmland may be over-used; imports may increase |
| Environmental degradation | Deforestation, soil erosion, pollution and pressure on water |

Fast growth can also be a benefit if jobs and investment keep pace, giving a "demographic dividend".

## Strategies to reduce the birth rate

| Approach | Example | How it worked | Issues |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strict government control | China's one-child policy, 1979 to 2015 | Limits on family size, with fines and incentives | Slowed growth quickly, but led to an ageing population, fewer women than men and human rights concerns; the limit was later raised and relaxed |
| Voluntary programmes and education | Thailand, from the 1970s | Widespread contraception, health workers, public campaigns and education | Fertility fell from about six children per woman in the 1960s to around two by the 1990s without compulsion; needs time, funding and cooperation |

Other approaches include educating girls, improving health care and child survival, and giving women jobs. These reduce the birth rate by changing attitudes and costs.

## International migration

Migration is the movement of people. International migration is movement between countries. In the twenty-first century, large movements have been caused by conflict, poverty, jobs and political change.

### Causes

| Type | Cause | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Refugees | War, persecution | Millions fled the civil war in Syria from 2011, mostly to neighbouring Turkey, Lebanon and Jordan, with others reaching Europe |
| Asylum seekers | Seek protection and wait for a decision | Many Syrians applied for asylum in Germany and Sweden, especially in 2015 |
| Economic migrants | Seek work and higher income | Workers from South Asia to the Gulf states; Mexicans to the USA; Poles to the UK after 2004 |

Use **push and pull** ideas: push factors include war, poverty and lack of jobs; pull factors include safety, jobs and higher wages.

### Impacts

| Impact | Country of origin | Destination |
|---|---|---|
| Demographic | Loss of young adults; skewed age structure | Younger population; more workers; possible population growth |
| Economic | Remittances; loss of skilled workers (brain drain) | Labour for farms, construction and services; costs for services |
| Social | Families separated | Cultural diversity; pressure on housing and schools; possible tension |
| Political | Governments rely on remittances | Debate on immigration and border control; integration policies |

In some Gulf states, migrants make up a large majority of the population, filling construction and service jobs.

## Ocean shipping, ports and airports

- **Development:** containerisation made shipping cheaper and faster. Ships have become very large, so deep-water ports and expanded canals such as Panama (widened in 2016) were built. Air travel and air freight have grown, and airports have become global hubs.
- **Global patterns:** the busiest container ports are mainly in East Asia, including Shanghai and Singapore, and Rotterdam is the largest in Europe. Major air hubs include Dubai, London Heathrow and Atlanta. Sea routes carry most world trade by volume.
- **Advantages:** cheaper movement of raw materials and goods supports **manufacturing** (global supply chains); trade grows; **tourism** grows as flights become cheaper; ports and airports create jobs and attract investment.

### Case study of airport expansion: London Heathrow

Heathrow is one of Europe's busiest airports by passenger numbers and operates close to capacity. Proposals for a third runway have been debated for many years. Check the current position in your notes.

| Reasons for expansion and benefits | Challenges |
|---|---|
| More flights, links to growing markets and increased trade | Aircraft noise over densely populated west London |
| Jobs and economic growth | Air pollution and greenhouse gas emissions |
| Competition with other European hubs | Homes and land lost to the new runway; road traffic |
| Reduced delays | Very high cost; public opposition; legal challenges |

## ICT

**Developments.** The internet, mobile phones, satellites, fibre-optic cables, cloud services and social media have made it faster and cheaper to communicate across the world. More than half the world's people now use the internet, and mobile subscriptions now roughly equal the number of people.

**Cause and pattern.** Causes include falling costs, new technology, investment by TNCs and government policy. Access is highest in HICs and lowest in LICs, particularly in rural areas, so there is a **digital divide**. Access is growing fastest in NEEs and LICs, driven by mobile phones.

### ICT and economic growth in NEEs: India

India's cities, such as Bangalore, Hyderabad and Gurgaon, have grown as centres of ICT services. Features include:

- **Call centres and business process outsourcing:** TNCs from HICs outsource customer service and support to India, where English-speaking graduates cost less.
- **Investment by TNCs:** global IT firms have set up development centres.
- **Trade:** software and IT services are major exports, and online shopping connects small businesses to customers.
- **Tourism:** online booking and promotion attract visitors.

The Philippines is another example for call centres. The benefits are jobs, higher incomes and foreign currency; the problems include shift work, competition from other countries, dependence on foreign firms, and uneven access.

## Worked example: a 4-mark answer

**Question:** Explain how improved medical care and the role of children have caused rapid population growth in some LICs. [4 marks]

**Model answer:** "Vaccines, clinics and better treatment reduce deaths, especially of babies and children, so more people survive and the death rate falls. However, birth rates stay high. In poor rural areas, children help on farms and look after parents in old age, and where infant mortality is high, families have more children to make sure some survive. So births greatly exceed deaths and population grows rapidly."

The answer links each cause to the outcome and explains why birth rates stay high.

## Common mistakes

- **Saying population growth is always a problem.** Impacts depend on resources and jobs.
- **Mixing up refugees, asylum seekers and economic migrants.** Learn the definitions.
- **Describing China's policy without any evaluation.** Give both effects and problems.
- **Missing the link between ICT and economic growth.** Show how jobs, trade and investment follow.
- **Case-study answers without a named airport and facts.**
- **Ignoring the digital divide.**
