This guide goes deeper into content item 1.1.2, Risk and reward, in Topic 1.1 Enterprise and entrepreneurship of the Pearson Edexcel Level 1/Level 2 GCSE (9-1) in Business (1BS0) specification, Issue 2, July 2022. It sits in Theme 1, Investigating small business, assessed in Paper 1 (1BS0/01). ## What 1.1.2 asks you to know

The specification heading is "The impact of risk and reward on business activity". It lists:

| Side | Specification wording |
|---|---|
| Risk | business failure, financial loss, lack of security |
| Reward | business success, profit, independence |

The word "impact" matters. You are not only expected to define the six terms but to show how the balance between them affects what entrepreneurs and businesses decide to do: whether to start up, how big to start, whether to expand, and how to fund it.

## Part 1: the three risks as a chain

The three risks are linked, and strong answers show the links.

1. **Lack of security.** An employee normally receives a regular wage, sick pay and holiday pay. An entrepreneur's income depends on how well the business does each month. There is no guaranteed income and no guaranteed future.
2. **Financial loss.** If costs are higher than revenue, the business makes a loss. The entrepreneur may also lose money put into the business, such as savings spent on equipment that sells for much less than it cost.
3. **Business failure.** If losses continue, or cash runs out, the business closes.

So lack of security is about the owner's income, financial loss is about money lost, and business failure is the end of the business. A single poor season can bring the first two; repeated poor seasons bring the third.

## Part 2: the three rewards

1. **Business success.** The business achieves what its owner set out to do: it survives, gains loyal customers, grows, or builds a good name.
2. **Profit.** Revenue is greater than total costs. Profit belongs to the owner, so it is the entrepreneur's financial reward for taking the risk.
3. **Independence.** The owner is their own boss: they choose what to sell, how to work and when.

Not all rewards are financial. Some owners value independence so highly that they accept a lower income than they could earn as an employee.

## Part 3: why risk and reward usually rise together

Bigger possible rewards usually come with bigger risks. An entrepreneur who invests more money, gives up a secure job or launches an untested idea has more to lose, but also has the chance of a larger profit if it works. Someone who starts small, keeps a part-time job and sells a proven product takes less risk, and the possible reward is usually smaller too.

This is why entrepreneurs make different decisions with the same information: they differ in how much they can afford to lose and how much the rewards matter to them.

## Worked example: Bramble and Bone Grooming

Asha earns £24,000 a year as an employee at a pet shop. She is thinking of leaving to open a dog-grooming salon. She would use £18,000 of savings for equipment and fitting out the unit. She plans to charge £45 a groom, and expects total costs of £31,500 a year (rent, products, insurance and equipment upkeep). Profit is revenue minus total costs.

| Outcome | Grooms in a year | Revenue (£) | Profit or loss (£) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Poor | 600 | 27,000 | −4,500 |
| Expected | 1,000 | 45,000 | 13,500 |
| Strong | 1,400 | 63,000 | 31,500 |

Working for the expected row: revenue = 1,000 × £45 = £45,000; profit = £45,000 − £31,500 = £13,500.

**Reading the table for risk:**
- In the poor year she makes a **loss** of £4,500 and has no wage at all: financial loss and lack of security together.
- Even in the expected year her profit of £13,500 is £10,500 less than her current salary.
- If poor years continue, the business could fail and much of the £18,000 could be lost, because second-hand equipment sells for less than it cost.

**Reading the table for reward:**
- In the strong year her profit of £31,500 is £7,500 more than her salary.
- To match her salary she needs profit of £24,000, so revenue of £24,000 + £31,500 = £55,500. That is £55,500 ÷ £45 = 1,233.3 grooms, so **1,234 grooms** (rounded up, because a part-groom cannot be sold).
- She would also gain independence: she decides her prices, hours and services.

**What this shows.** Asha's decision depends on how likely the strong outcome is and how much the non-financial rewards matter to her. Market research into local demand would help, because it reduces (but does not remove) the risk. She could also lower the risk by starting with one day a week while keeping part of her job, accepting a slower route to the reward.

## Part 4: the impact on business activity

The balance of risk and reward shapes decisions throughout a business's life, not only at start-up.

| Decision | Risk side | Reward side |
|---|---|---|
| Leaving a job to start up | Loss of a secure wage | Profit and independence |
| Using savings rather than borrowing | Savings could be lost | No repayments; full control kept |
| Opening a second branch | Higher costs, possible loss if it fails | Larger profit and success |
| Launching a new product | It may not sell | Extra sales and profit |

## Annotated model answer

**Question (Analyse, in context):** Asha is deciding whether to leave her job to open Bramble and Bone Grooming. Analyse the risks to Asha of starting the business. **[6]**

> Asha would give up a secure salary of £24,000, so she faces a lack of security (risk named, tied to the case). Her income would depend on how many dogs she grooms, and in a poor year of 600 grooms she would make a loss of £4,500 and receive no income at all (developed with figures). She is also putting £18,000 of savings into the business (financial loss). If the business fails, she may only get back a fraction of this by selling the equipment, so she could lose most of her savings (consequence). Because her expected profit of £13,500 is below her salary, she would need over 1,200 grooms a year just to earn what she earns now (further development). This means the business could struggle to give her enough to live on during its first year (conclusion of the chain).

Why it works: each risk from the specification is named, tied to Asha and developed with a consequence. Analyse answers are placed in context by the question, so general points about "businesses" earn less.

## Common mistakes

- Treating profit as the only reward; success and independence are also in the specification.
- Writing "risk of losing money" for every risk. Separate financial loss from lack of security and failure.
- Saying market research removes risk. It reduces it.
- Ignoring the owner's personal position: savings, family, existing job.
- Concluding "it depends" without saying what it depends on and what the owner should do.
- Mixing up a loss for one year with business failure. A business can survive a loss if it has cash or savings to fall back on.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Explain** (no context): one risk or reward, then two linked expansions.
- **Outline:** two linked points about the named business.
- **Analyse:** use figures from the case and follow each risk or reward through to its consequence.
- **Evaluate / Justify:** set the risks against the rewards for this owner. Decide, and say what would change the decision, such as stronger demand or a smaller investment.

## Frequently asked questions

**What risks does the Edexcel GCSE Business specification list?**
Business failure, financial loss and lack of security.

**What rewards does it list?**
Business success, profit and independence.

**Is a loss the same as business failure?**
No. A loss means costs were higher than revenue in a period. Failure means the business closes, which can follow repeated losses or running out of cash.
