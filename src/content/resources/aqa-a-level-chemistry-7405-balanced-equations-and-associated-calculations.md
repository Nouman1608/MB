This guide teaches section **3.1.2.5 Balanced equations and associated calculations** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Papers 1 and 2 (and Paper 3). It is the point where the mole, the ideal gas equation and concentration come together. Earlier pages cover each tool on its own, for example the [Mole and Avogadro constant study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-the-mole-and-the-avogadro-constant/) and the [Ideal gas equation study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-the-ideal-gas-equation/). This page shows how to choose between them, how to build a balanced equation from scratch, and how to judge a reaction by yield and atom economy. Recall is in the [Balanced equations revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-balanced-equations-and-associated-calculations-revision-notes/), and questions are in the [Balanced equations practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-balanced-equations-and-associated-calculations-practice/).

## Balancing an unfamiliar equation

The method is the same every time. Count each element on both sides, change only the big numbers in front of formulas (never the small numbers inside a formula), and finish with the element that appears in the most places.

**Aluminium and iron(III) oxide.** Reactants Al and Fe₂O₃, products Al₂O₃ and Fe.

```
Al + Fe₂O₃ → Al₂O₃ + Fe
```

Oxygen is the awkward element: three O on the left and three O on the right already match. Iron: two Fe on the left, so put 2 in front of Fe on the right. Aluminium: two Al on the right, so put 2 in front of Al on the left.

```
2Al + Fe₂O₃ → Al₂O₃ + 2Fe
```

Check each element: Al 2 and 2, Fe 2 and 2, O 3 and 3. Balanced.

### Ionic equations

An ionic equation shows only the species that change. Take the precipitation of barium sulfate.

```
BaCl₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2NaCl(aq)
Ba²⁺(aq) + 2Cl⁻(aq) + 2Na⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2Na⁺(aq) + 2Cl⁻(aq)
Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s)
```

Write soluble ionic compounds as separate ions, keep solids, liquids and gases whole, then cancel the **spectator ions** that appear unchanged on both sides (Na⁺ and Cl⁻ here). An ionic equation must balance for atoms **and** for charge. Here the left has charge +2 and −2, which totals zero, and the right is neutral.

## Choosing a route: the mole bridge

Every calculation from an equation follows one pattern.

```
quantity of A  →  moles of A  →  (mole ratio)  →  moles of B  →  quantity of B
```

The quantity can be a mass (n = m ÷ Mᵣ), a gas volume (pV = nRT), or a solution (n = c × V with V in dm³). The mole ratio from the **balanced** equation is the only link between two different substances. Never compare masses directly.

### Worked example 1: reacting masses

Iron is made in the blast furnace: Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂. Calculate the maximum mass of iron from 1.00 kg of iron(III) oxide. (Aᵣ Fe = 55.8, O = 16.0)

```
Mᵣ(Fe₂O₃) = 2 × 55.8 + 3 × 16.0 = 159.6
n(Fe₂O₃)  = 1000 ÷ 159.6 = 6.266 mol
n(Fe)     = 2 × 6.266 = 12.53 mol       (ratio 1 : 2)
m(Fe)     = 12.53 × 55.8 = 699 g
```

The theoretical maximum is 699 g. The answer is just under 70 per cent of the starting mass, which is sensible because iron makes up 111.6 of the 159.6 in the oxide.

### Worked example 2: gas volumes

Gas volumes at the **same temperature and pressure** are in the same ratio as the moles, so equations can be used directly. 20.0 cm³ of propane burns completely in excess oxygen: C₃H₈(g) + 5O₂(g) → 3CO₂(g) + 4H₂O(l). Find the volume of oxygen used and of carbon dioxide formed.

```
V(O₂)  = 20.0 × 5 = 100 cm³
V(CO₂) = 20.0 × 3 =  60 cm³
```

Water is a liquid at room temperature, so it contributes no gas volume. The shortcut works only for gases measured under the same conditions. If a question gives a mass of reactant and asks for a gas volume, use n then pV = nRT, as in the ideal gas guide.

## Percentage yield

The **theoretical yield** assumes complete reaction and no losses. The **actual yield** is what you obtain.

```
percentage yield = (actual mass ÷ theoretical mass) × 100
```

### Worked example 3

4.80 g of magnesium is burned in air to make magnesium oxide. 6.50 g of oxide is collected. Calculate the percentage yield. (Aᵣ Mg = 24.3, O = 16.0)

```
2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO
n(Mg) = 4.80 ÷ 24.3 = 0.1975 mol
n(MgO) = 0.1975 mol                      (ratio 1 : 1)
theoretical m(MgO) = 0.1975 × 40.3 = 7.96 g
percentage yield = 6.50 ÷ 7.96 × 100 = 81.7%
```

Reasons a yield can be below 100 per cent include incomplete reaction, side reactions, loss during transfer, filtering and purification, and (in this case) magnesium oxide escaping as smoke. A yield above 100 per cent means the product is impure or still wet.

A yield can be used in reverse. If a question gives the percentage yield and the mass you want, work out the **theoretical** mass first, then divide by the yield fraction to find how much reactant to start with.

## Percentage atom economy

Atom economy is a property of the **equation**, not of the experiment. It measures how much of the mass of the reactants ends up in the desired product.

```
atom economy = (Mᵣ of desired product ÷ sum of Mᵣ of all reactants) × 100
```

Use the stoichiometric numbers in the balanced equation: if the equation shows 2Fe, the product mass is 2 × 55.8.

### Worked example 4: comparing two routes to ethanol

Hydration of ethene: C₂H₄ + H₂O → C₂H₅OH

```
atom economy = 46.0 ÷ (28.0 + 18.0) × 100 = 100%
```

Fermentation of glucose: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂

```
atom economy = (2 × 46.0) ÷ 180.0 × 100 = 51.1%
```

### Worked example 5: a lower atom economy

For the blast furnace, the desired product is iron only.

```
atom economy = (2 × 55.8) ÷ (159.6 + 3 × 28.0) × 100 = 111.6 ÷ 243.6 × 100 = 45.8%
```

### Why high atom economy matters

| Type of advantage | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Economic | Fewer raw materials end up as waste, so less feedstock is bought, and less money is spent separating and disposing of by-products |
| Environmental | Less waste to treat or release, and less raw material extracted; fewer resources are used per tonne of product |
| Ethical and social | Conserves finite resources for future generations and limits pollution that affects local communities |

A high atom economy is not the same as a high yield. A reaction can have a 95 per cent yield and a poor atom economy, if most of the reactant mass ends up in an unwanted by-product. A by-product with a use (for example, sold on) improves the real economics even when the atom economy is low.

## Solutions and titrations

For a solution, n = c × V, with V in dm³ (cm³ ÷ 1000). This links to Required practical 1.

### Worked example 6: finding a concentration

25.0 cm³ of sodium hydroxide solution is titrated with 0.100 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid. The concordant titres are 21.40 cm³ and 21.40 cm³. Calculate the concentration of the sodium hydroxide.

```
NaOH + HCl → NaCl + H₂O                     (1 : 1)
n(HCl) = 0.100 × 21.40 ÷ 1000 = 2.140 × 10⁻³ mol
n(NaOH) = 2.140 × 10⁻³ mol
c(NaOH) = 2.140 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.0250 = 0.0856 mol dm⁻³
```

### Worked example 7: a 1 : 2 ratio

25.0 cm³ of 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ sulfuric acid is neutralised by sodium hydroxide of concentration 0.200 mol dm⁻³. Calculate the volume of alkali.

```
H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O
n(H₂SO₄) = 0.0500 × 0.0250 = 1.25 × 10⁻³ mol
n(NaOH)  = 2 × 1.25 × 10⁻³ = 2.50 × 10⁻³ mol
V(NaOH)  = 2.50 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.200 = 0.0125 dm³ = 12.5 cm³
```

Choose concordant titres (within 0.10 cm³) and average only those. Outliers are left out.

## Water of crystallisation

A hydrated salt contains a fixed number of water molecules per formula unit. Heating to constant mass drives off the water.

### Worked example 8

3.69 g of hydrated magnesium sulfate, MgSO₄·xH₂O, is heated until the mass stops changing. The anhydrous solid has a mass of 1.80 g. Find x. (Mᵣ MgSO₄ = 120.4)

```
n(MgSO₄) = 1.80 ÷ 120.4 = 0.01495 mol
m(H₂O)   = 3.69 − 1.80 = 1.89 g
n(H₂O)   = 1.89 ÷ 18.0 = 0.105 mol
ratio    = 0.105 ÷ 0.01495 = 7.02, so x = 7
```

Heating to constant mass shows that all the water has gone. If the final mass is still falling, x will be calculated as too small.

## Uncertainty and significant figures

A titre is the difference between two burette readings, each with an uncertainty of ±0.05 cm³, so the titre has an uncertainty of ±0.10 cm³. The percentage uncertainty is (0.10 ÷ 21.40) × 100 = 0.47%. Report the final answer to the same number of significant figures as the least precise piece of data.

## Common mistakes

- Changing a subscript inside a formula to balance an equation.
- Comparing masses of different substances directly instead of going through moles.
- Using the stoichiometric number of the **reactant** instead of the product when finding a ratio.
- Leaving cm³ in n = cV, so the answer is a thousand times too large.
- Counting a by-product in the numerator of atom economy.
- Quoting an ionic equation that balances for atoms but not for charge.
- Averaging titres that are not concordant.
- Treating a high yield as proof of a high atom economy.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Calculate**: show each stage (moles, ratio, final quantity) and give units.
- **Deduce** or **balance**: check atoms and charge at the end; marks are lost for a final unchecked number.
- **Explain** why a yield is less than 100 per cent: name a specific loss, such as product left on filter paper, not simply "human error".
- **Suggest** an advantage of high atom economy: link the idea (less waste) to a consequence (lower disposal cost, or fewer resources used).

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need state symbols in an ionic equation?**
Yes. Ionic equations carry state symbols, because they show what the ions are doing, such as joining to form a solid.

**Is atom economy always based on the total of all reactants?**
Yes, in the AQA definition it uses the sum of the molecular masses of all reactants, counted with their stoichiometric numbers.

**Why is a yield of 100 per cent rare?**
Reactions may be reversible, may give side products, and always lose some material in separation. These are real chemical and practical limits, not mistakes in the sum.
