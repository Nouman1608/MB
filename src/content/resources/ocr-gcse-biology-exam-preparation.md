OCR GCSE (9-1) Biology A (Gateway Science, J247) is tiered and linear, assessed by two papers per
tier. Foundation candidates sit Papers 1 and 2; Higher candidates sit Papers 3 and 4. Every paper is 1
hour 45 minutes, 90 marks and 50% of the qualification. The content split is the same at both tiers:
**Paper 1/3 assesses topics 1-3 plus topic 7 (practical skills); Paper 2/4 assesses topics 4-6 plus
topic 7, with assumed knowledge of topics 1-3 and synoptic assessment across the specification.**
These notes complement the site's guides to
[Cell Level Systems](/resources/ocr-gcse-biology-cell-level-systems/) and
[Scaling Up](/resources/ocr-gcse-biology-scaling-up/).

## The second paper is not a fresh start -- topics 1-3 are assumed knowledge

This is the most consequential structural fact about the specification. Paper 2 (or 4) formally
assesses topics 4-6, but topics 1-3 are assumed and can be drawn on synoptically. **Exam-preparation
priority**: do not close the book on topics 1-3 once the first paper is sat. Cell structure, transport
and enzyme chemistry underpin the digestion, ecology and inheritance content of the later topics, and
a synoptic question can require both.

## Topic 7 practical skills appear on every paper

Practical skills are examined on both papers at both tiers rather than being confined to one.
**Exam-preparation priority**: treat the required practical activities as examinable content of equal
standing with the biology topics. For each one, know the variables, the measurements taken, the main
source of error, and one specific improvement -- this set of four facts answers most practical
questions regardless of which paper they appear on.

## The tier decision should rest on full-paper evidence

Both tiers use the same paper length and mark total, but the question demand differs. **Exam-
preparation priority**: base the tier conversation on recent timed full papers at both tiers rather
than on topic confidence. Marks lost to questions that cannot be attempted at all are the most
expensive kind, and a paper that is accessible throughout usually produces a better outcome than one
where a third of the questions are out of reach.

## Ninety marks in 105 minutes is a little over a minute a mark

**Exam-preparation priority**: practise complete papers rather than question sets. Extended-response
questions typically sit later in the paper, so pace has to be banked early. A candidate who reaches
the six-mark questions with eight minutes left has lost marks to timing rather than to biology.

## Extended-response questions are marked on linked reasoning

The longer questions reward a connected explanation rather than a list of relevant facts.
**Exam-preparation priority**: practise writing in chains -- "because... which means... so..." -- and
check each practice answer by asking whether each sentence follows from the one before. A list of
true statements about a topic is a common way to score half marks on a six-mark question.

## Worked routine: a practical-based question

The routine below is an original model written for this resource, not a reproduction of any official
past paper or mark scheme.

```
Step 1 - name the independent and dependent variables:
What was deliberately changed, and what was measured. Getting
these round the wrong way undermines everything after it.

Step 2 - name TWO control variables and say why each matters:
"Temperature was kept constant because enzyme activity changes
with temperature" -- the reason is the mark, not the variable.

Step 3 - identify one specific source of error:
Not "human error". Name the measurement that was hard to take
precisely and say why.

Step 4 - give an improvement that targets THAT error:
A data logger for a timing judged by eye; a water bath for
temperature drift. General "repeat more times" only addresses
random error, so say so if that is your point.

Step 5 - state the conclusion the data supports, and its limit:
"These results support X within the range tested" is more accurate,
and more creditable, than an unqualified generalisation.
```

Step 2 is where the readily available marks are: naming a control variable is worth little on its own,
while naming it and explaining why it had to be controlled is a complete answer.

## Before/during exam checklist

- **Before the exams**: keep topics 1-3 revised after the first paper, since they are assumed
  knowledge on the second; learn four facts per required practical (variables, measurements, main
  error, targeted improvement); settle the tier decision on timed full-paper evidence; practise
  complete papers to build pacing.
- **During either paper**: bank time early so the extended-response questions are not rushed.
- **On extended responses**: write in linked chains rather than lists.
- **On practical questions**: name variables, explain why controls mattered, name a specific error,
  give a targeted improvement, and qualify the conclusion.

## Self-test

1. Which topics does each paper assess, and what is assumed on the second?
2. Where do practical skills appear in the assessment?
3. What makes a control-variable answer complete?
4. Why does an unqualified conclusion score below a qualified one?

**Answers:** 1. Paper 1/3 assesses topics 1-3 plus topic 7; Paper 2/4 assesses topics 4-6 plus topic
7, with topics 1-3 assumed as prior knowledge and available for synoptic questions. 2. Topic 7
practical skills are examined on every paper at both tiers, not confined to one. 3. Naming the
variable *and* explaining why it had to be held constant -- the explanation is where the mark sits.
4. Because the data supports a conclusion only within the range and conditions actually tested, and
recognising that limit is itself creditable.
