## Section A and Section B are one connected task, not two

The single most valuable exam-technique skill for this unit is reading
Section A's source material **with Section B already in mind** —
noting, while analysing, which observations could support an academic
argument later. A Section B essay that restates the Section A analysis
rather than building on it as evidence is the most common cause of lost
marks.

## Four social functions — name them precisely

| Function | What to look for |
|---|---|
| **Group rapport / shared perspectives** | Language building solidarity around shared interests or affiliations |
| **Power and status** | Closed questions, topic control, formal register, politeness/hedging strategies |
| **Identity and relationship** | Word choices constructing a persona; first-person narrative building credibility |
| **Play and performance** | Wordplay, in-group banter, creative or performative language use |

Naming the specific function a feature demonstrates — not just
describing what a text says — is what Section A is assessed against.

## Practise across all three modes

Section A source material can be **spoken, written or multimodal**.
Revision that only uses written prose leaves a gap: practise an
interview transcript (spoken), a piece of persuasive writing (written),
and an example of informal banter or wordplay (multimodal/informal), so
an unfamiliar text type in the exam is not a surprise.

## A worked structure for a spoken transcript

```
POWER AND STATUS:      closed questions and topic control by one
                        speaker; formal register maintained by both;
                        politeness/hedging by the less powerful speaker
IDENTITY/RELATIONSHIP: word choices constructing a professional
                        persona; first-person narrative building
                        credibility
LINK TO SECTION B:      these observations about power and
                        self-presentation in institutional talk could
                        support an essay on how language reflects or
                        reinforces power relationships in interaction
```

A weak answer would describe the transcript's topic (what job, what
questions) without naming the specific language features doing this
social work.

## Weighting tells you where to invest revision time

AO1 (understanding of methods, terminology, concepts and issues) is
weighted more heavily in Unit 2 than in Unit 1, where AO3 (using
English in different ways) dominates. This signals that **precise
linguistic terminology** matters at least as much as fluent writing
style for this unit — vague description costs marks that precise,
named terminology would earn.

## Distinguishing Unit 2's Section B from Unit 1's

Unit 2's Section B is an **academic essay** using Section A's analysis
as evidence — it is not a transformed or creative writing task, which
is what Unit 1's Directed Writing asks for. Treating Unit 2's Section B
as a creative exercise, rather than an evidence-based argument, misreads
the task entirely.

## How Unit 2 fits the wider International AS/A-level

Units 1 and 2 together make up the International AS, each contributing
20% to the full International A-level (50% each of the AS). Because
both units are weighted identically at the AS stage, revision time
across them should be balanced evenly rather than skewed toward
whichever unit was taught most recently — a candidate strong on Unit 1
but under-prepared on Unit 2's social-function vocabulary has not
actually balanced their AS preparation, even where overall confidence
feels high.

## Exam traps

- Writing Section B as a restatement of Section A rather than an
academic argument built on it.
- Describing content without naming the specific language feature and
its social function.
- Treating "power and status" and "identity" as interchangeable
categories.
- Revising only written prose, neglecting spoken and multimodal data.
- Confusing Unit 2's academic-essay Section B with Unit 1's
transformed/creative Directed Writing task.

## Building a genuinely mixed-mode revision folder

Because Section A source material is drawn from spoken, written and
multimodal texts about power, identity and play, keep a revision folder
that genuinely mixes these formats rather than one dominated by written
articles simply because they are easiest to find and annotate. An
interview transcript sourced from a real broadcast, a piece of online
persuasive writing, and an example of informal banter or wordplay
together give more realistic preparation than three examples of the
same written-prose format with different topics.

## Self-test

1. What is the single most valuable exam-technique skill for this
unit, connecting its two sections?
2. Name the four social functions of language this unit assesses.
3. Why should revision include spoken and multimodal texts, not just
written prose?
4. Which assessment objective is weighted more heavily in Unit 2 than
Unit 1, and what does that signal for revision priorities?
5. How does Unit 2's Section B differ in task type from Unit 1's
Section B?

**Answers:** 1. Reading Section A's source material with an eye to what
argument it could support in Section B, rather than treating the two
sections as unrelated tasks. 2. Group rapport/shared perspectives,
power and status, identity and relationship, and play/performance. 3.
Because Section A's source material is explicitly drawn from spoken,
written and multimodal texts, so revision confined to written prose
leaves genuine gaps. 4. AO1 (understanding of methods, terminology,
concepts and issues); this signals that precise linguistic terminology
matters at least as much as fluent writing style. 5. Unit 2's Section B
is an academic essay building an argument from Section A's analysis;
Unit 1's Section B is a transformed or creative Directed Writing task,
not an academic essay.
