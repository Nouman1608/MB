## Section A

**1.** State what is meant by a tracer. **[1]**

**2.** State why the radioactive isotope in a PET tracer must decay by β⁺ emission. **[1]**

**3.** Carbon-11 (proton number 6) decays by β⁺ emission to an isotope of boron. Write the decay equation, including all the particles emitted. **[2]**

**4.** State **two** quantities that are conserved when an electron and a positron annihilate. **[2]**

**5.** An electron and a positron, both at rest, annihilate.

**(a)** Calculate the energy of each gamma-ray photon produced, in MeV. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the frequency of each photon. **[1]**

## Section B

**6.** Explain why the annihilation of a slow-moving positron with an electron in tissue produces two gamma-ray photons travelling in opposite directions, rather than a single photon. **[3]**

**7.** In a PET scanner, an annihilation occurs on the line between two opposite detectors, 5.0 cm from the midpoint of that line.

**(a)** Calculate the difference in the arrival times of the two photons at the detectors. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest why the electronic timing of the detectors must be very precise. **[1]**

**8.** In a laboratory experiment, an electron and a positron each have kinetic energy 0.10 MeV and move directly towards each other with equal speeds. They annihilate.

**(a)** Calculate the total energy available for the photons, in joules. **[2]**

**(b)** The photons travel in opposite directions with equal energies. Calculate the energy of each photon in joules. **[1]**

**9.** A patient is injected with a fluorine-18 tracer of activity 300 MBq. Fluorine-18 has a half-life of 110 minutes. Assume every decay produces a positron.

**(a)** Calculate the activity of the tracer when the scan begins, 60 minutes after injection. **[2]**

**(b)** State the number of annihilation photons produced per second at this time. **[1]**

**10.** Describe how a PET scanner uses the gamma-ray photons to produce an image of the concentration of the tracer in the body. **[4]**

**11.** Two isotopes that decay by β⁺ emission are considered for a tracer. Isotope P has a half-life of 2 minutes and isotope Q has a half-life of 3 days. Explain why neither is ideal. **[2]**

**12.** A student writes: "The two gamma-ray photons go in opposite directions because they repel each other, like two electrons."

Explain why this is wrong, and give the correct reason. **[2]**

**13.** An annihilation produces two photons of energy 8.20 × 10⁻¹⁴ J each.

**(a)** Calculate the momentum of each photon. **[1]**

**(b)** Show that the total momentum of the two photons is zero. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** A substance containing radioactive nuclei that is introduced into the body and is absorbed by the tissue being studied [1].

**2.** The scan depends on positrons annihilating with electrons to produce pairs of gamma-ray photons, so the isotope must emit positrons [1].

**3.** ¹¹₆C → ¹¹₅B + ⁰₊₁e [1] + ν (electron neutrino) [1].

**4.** Any two [1 each]: mass-energy (total energy); momentum; charge.

**5. (a)** E = mₑc² = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × (3.00 × 10⁸)² = 8.20 × 10⁻¹⁴ J [1]; ÷ 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ = 0.51 MeV [1].

**(b)** f = E/h = 8.20 × 10⁻¹⁴ ÷ 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ = 1.24 × 10²⁰ Hz [1].

**6.** The positron and electron are almost at rest, so their total momentum before annihilation is (nearly) zero [1]. Any photon carries momentum (p = E/c), so a single photon would have non-zero momentum and momentum would not be conserved [1]. Two photons of equal energy travelling in opposite directions have equal and opposite momenta, so the total is zero [1].

**7. (a)** Path difference = 2 × 0.050 = 0.10 m [1]; Δt = 0.10 ÷ 3.00 × 10⁸ = 3.3 × 10⁻¹⁰ s [1].

**(b)** The time differences are fractions of a nanosecond; a timing error of even 0.1 ns corresponds to an error of 1.5 cm in position [1].

**8. (a)** Rest-mass energy = 2 × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × (3.00 × 10⁸)² = 1.64 × 10⁻¹³ J [1]; total = 1.64 × 10⁻¹³ + 2 × 0.10 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ = 1.96 × 10⁻¹³ J [1].

**(b)** 1.96 × 10⁻¹³ ÷ 2 = 9.8 × 10⁻¹⁴ J [1].

**9. (a)** λ = 0.693 ÷ 110 = 6.30 × 10⁻³ min⁻¹ [1]; A = 300 × e^(−6.30 × 10⁻³ × 60) = 210 MBq (206 MBq) [1].

**(b)** Each decay gives one annihilation and two photons, so about 4.1 × 10⁸ photons per second [1].

**10.** The patient is surrounded by a ring of gamma-ray detectors [1]. Two photons detected at almost the same time on opposite sides are taken to come from one annihilation, which must lie on the line joining the two detectors [1]. The difference in their arrival times gives the position of the annihilation along that line (x = cΔt/2 from the midpoint) [1]. A computer processes the arrival times of very many photon pairs to build an image showing where the annihilations occur, and so the concentration of tracer in the tissue [1].

**11.** P decays too quickly: much of its activity would be lost while the tracer is prepared and injected, and it may not reach the tissue in time [1]. Q decays too slowly: it would remain active in the body long after the scan, giving the patient an unnecessary dose (and a low activity for a given number of nuclei) [1].

**12.** Photons have no charge, so they do not repel each other [1]. They travel in opposite directions because momentum must be conserved: the electron-positron pair had (nearly) zero momentum, so the photons' momenta must be equal and opposite [1].

**13. (a)** p = E/c = 8.20 × 10⁻¹⁴ ÷ 3.00 × 10⁸ = 2.73 × 10⁻²² kg m s⁻¹ [1].

**(b)** The two momenta are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, so (+2.73 × 10⁻²²) + (−2.73 × 10⁻²²) = 0 [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Stating that the tracer itself emits gamma rays.
- Giving each photon the total energy 2mₑc².
- Explaining the opposite directions without referring to conservation of momentum.
- Missing the factor of 2 when converting an arrival-time difference into a distance.
- Forgetting the neutrino in a β⁺ decay equation.
- Describing the PET image as showing structure, rather than tracer concentration.
