## What makes this section different

Much of 4.7.3 is assessed through **evaluation**: you are given information about a human activity or a
conservation scheme and asked to weigh up its effects. The biology is not hard, but marks depend on linking each
human action to a specific effect on organisms, and on recognising conflicting needs, such as food and jobs
against habitats.

## Biodiversity and stability

**Biodiversity** is the variety of all the different species of organisms on Earth, or within an ecosystem.

The specification explains why it matters: **a great biodiversity ensures the stability of ecosystems by
reducing the dependence of one species on another** for food, shelter and the maintenance of the physical
environment.

**Worked example 1: why more species means more stability.** Compare two food webs.

```
Low biodiversity:    grass → rabbit → fox
High biodiversity:   grass, clover, seeds → rabbits, voles, mice, beetles → fox
```

If disease kills most rabbits, the fox in the first web loses its only food and its population crashes. In the
second web, the fox can switch to voles and mice, so its population changes much less. Each species depends less
on any single other species, so the community is more stable.

The future of the human species on Earth relies on us maintaining a good level of biodiversity. Many human
activities are reducing it, and only recently have measures been taken to try to stop the reduction.

## Waste management and pollution

**Rapid growth in the human population** and an **increase in the standard of living** mean that more resources
are used and more waste is produced. Unless waste and chemical materials are properly handled, more pollution is
caused.

| Where | Sources named in the specification | How it can harm organisms |
|---|---|---|
| Water | Sewage, fertiliser, toxic chemicals | Toxic chemicals poison organisms; sewage and fertiliser can lower dissolved oxygen, killing fish and invertebrates |
| Air | Smoke, acidic gases | Acidic gases dissolve in rain, damaging leaves and making lakes and soils more acidic |
| Land | Landfill, toxic chemicals | Landfill covers habitats; toxic chemicals soak into soil and water |

**Pollution kills plants and animals, which can reduce biodiversity.** When you explain a pollution example,
finish the chain with **fewer species** or **fewer organisms**, not just "it damages the environment".

## Land use

Humans reduce the amount of land available for other animals and plants by **building, quarrying, farming and
dumping waste**.

### Peat bogs

Peat bogs are destroyed, and other areas of peat dug up, to produce **garden compost**. This has two effects you
must explain:

1. It **reduces the area of this habitat**, and so the variety of plant, animal and microorganism species that
   live there (biodiversity).
2. The **decay or burning of the peat releases carbon dioxide** into the atmosphere, which contributes to global
   warming.

The specification also wants you to understand the **conflict**: cheap compost helps increase food production,
but peatlands need to be conserved as habitats and to reduce carbon dioxide emissions. Peat-free composts are one
way of reducing the conflict.

## Deforestation

Large-scale deforestation in tropical areas has occurred to:

- provide land for **cattle and rice fields**;
- grow **crops for biofuels**.

When you evaluate its environmental implications, use these links:

```
trees removed → habitats lost → fewer species (biodiversity falls)
trees removed → less photosynthesis → less carbon dioxide removed from the air
trees burned or left to decay → carbon dioxide released
```

On the other side are the reasons people deforest: food production for a growing population, income and jobs,
and biofuels that may replace some fossil fuels.

**Worked example 2: percentage change** (invented figures). The forest area in a region falls from 46 000 km²
to 31 000 km² over 20 years.

```
decrease = 46 000 − 31 000 = 15 000 km²
percentage decrease = 15 000 ÷ 46 000 × 100 = 33% (2 s.f.)
mean rate of loss = 15 000 ÷ 20 = 750 km² per year
```

## Global warming

**Levels of carbon dioxide and methane in the atmosphere are increasing**, and they contribute to global
warming. You must describe some **biological consequences**, for example:

- species' **distribution changes**: some move towards the poles or to higher altitudes where it is cooler;
- **loss of habitat**, for example where ice melts or rising sea levels flood low-lying land;
- **changes in migration patterns** of birds and other animals;
- some species may become **extinct** if they cannot move or adapt;
- pests and pathogens may spread to new areas as they become warmer.

The scientific consensus about global warming and climate change is based on **systematic reviews of thousands of
peer-reviewed publications**. Evidence for the effects on particular species can still be uncertain, because many
factors change at once in a complex system.

## Maintaining biodiversity

Scientists and concerned citizens have put in place programmes to reduce the negative effects of humans on
ecosystems and biodiversity. For each one, know how it helps and what it costs or conflicts with:

| Programme | How it helps biodiversity | Possible conflict or cost |
|---|---|---|
| Breeding programmes for endangered species | Increases numbers; animals may be released into the wild | Expensive; small captive populations have little genetic variation |
| Protection and regeneration of rare habitats | Keeps space and resources for the species that live there | Land cannot be used for building or farming |
| Field margins and hedgerows where farmers grow one crop | Provide habitats and food for insects, birds and small mammals | Less land for crops; time and money to maintain |
| Reduction of deforestation and CO₂ emissions by some governments | Protects forest habitats; slows global warming | May reduce income or jobs from farming, logging or industry |
| Recycling resources rather than dumping in landfill | Less land used for landfill; fewer new resources extracted | Collection and processing cost money and energy |

**Annotated model answer**

*Question: A farmer grows only wheat in large fields. A conservation group asks her to replant hedgerows around
each field. Evaluate this proposal. [6]*

> Hedgerows would provide habitats and food for many species, such as insects, birds and small mammals, so
> biodiversity would increase [1]. Growing only wheat gives very few habitats, so the hedges add variety [1].
> Some of the insects that live in hedges may eat crop pests or pollinate other plants [1]. However, the hedges
> take up land that could grow wheat, so the farmer's yield and income may fall [1]. Planting and cutting the
> hedges also costs time and money [1]. Overall, I think the proposal is worthwhile if the farmer receives some
> support for the cost, because the gain in biodiversity is large while the loss of land is small [1].

The final sentence earns credit because it is a **judgement linked to the points made**, not just "it is good".

## Common mistakes

- Defining biodiversity as "the number of organisms". It is the variety of different **species**.
- Writing "pollution harms animals" without naming the pollutant or the harm.
- Explaining peat destruction only as habitat loss and forgetting the **carbon dioxide** released.
- Saying deforestation "removes oxygen". The specification's links are habitat loss, less carbon dioxide
  absorbed and more released.
- Listing conservation methods without explaining how each one helps.
- Evaluations with no disadvantage or no conclusion.

## Exam technique for AQA command words

- **Describe** a biological consequence of global warming: name the change and the organisms affected.
- **Explain** the effect of a human activity: activity → effect on habitat or organisms → effect on biodiversity.
- **Evaluate**: positives, negatives (including cost and human needs), then a justified judgement.
- **Calculate**: show the subtraction before the percentage; give units for rates.
