This guide covers section 4.3, Scale drawings, in the Geometry topic of the Cambridge O Level Mathematics (Syllabus D) 4024 syllabus for 2025, 2026 and 2027. The syllabus asks you to **draw and interpret scale drawings** (a ruler must be used for all straight edges) and to **use and interpret three-figure bearings**, including the terms north, east, south and west. After this guide, use the [scale drawings revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-mathematics-4024-scale-drawings-revision-notes/) and the [scale drawings practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-mathematics-4024-scale-drawings-practice/).

## What section 4.3 asks for

1. Draw a scale drawing from given measurements, and read lengths and angles from one.
2. Convert between drawn lengths and real lengths.
3. Measure and write bearings: angles measured clockwise from north, from 000° to 360°, always with three figures.
4. Find a back bearing, for example the bearing of A from B when the bearing of B from A is given.
5. Understand that due east means directly east of a point.

## Scales

A scale links a length on the drawing to the real length it stands for. It may be given as a ratio or in words.

- A ratio such as **1 : 50 000** means that 1 unit on the drawing is 50 000 of the same units in real life.
- A statement such as **1 cm represents 5 m** says the same thing in words.

To find a real length, multiply the drawn length by the scale. To find a drawn length, divide the real length by the scale. Always convert units at the end.

### Worked example 1

A map has scale 1 : 50 000.

(a) Two towns are 6.4 cm apart on the map. How far apart are they?

(b) A road is 7.5 km long. How long is it on the map?

```
(a) 6.4 × 50 000 = 320 000 cm
    320 000 cm = 3 200 m = 3.2 km
(b) 7.5 km = 750 000 cm
    750 000 ÷ 50 000 = 15 cm
```

A handy fact: on a 1 : 50 000 map, 1 cm stands for 500 m. A 1 : 25 000 map has 1 cm for 250 m.

### Worked example 2

A scale drawing uses 1 cm to represent 8 m. A building is 36 m long. A path measures 7.25 cm on the drawing.

```
building: 36 ÷ 8 = 4.5 cm on the drawing
path: 7.25 × 8 = 58 m in real life
```

### Scales and area

A scale applies to lengths. An area scales by the square of the length scale. If 1 cm represents 250 m, then 1 cm² represents 250 × 250 = 62 500 m².

### Worked example 3

On a 1 : 200 000 map, a lake has area 3.5 cm². Find its real area.

```
1 cm represents 200 000 cm = 2 km
1 cm² represents 2 × 2 = 4 km²
area = 3.5 × 4 = 14 km²
```

A very common error is multiplying the area by 2 (the length scale) instead of 4.

## Drawing a scale drawing

1. Decide on a convenient scale so that the drawing fits the paper.
2. Calculate the length of each line on the drawing.
3. Use a ruler for straight edges and a protractor for angles.
4. Label every point and write the scale beside the drawing.
5. Measure the quantity asked for and convert it with the scale.

Because measuring is never exact, small differences in your answer are accepted. A scale drawing can only be as accurate as your pencil and protractor.

## Compass directions and bearings

The four main compass points are north (N), east (E), south (S) and west (W). Between them are north-east, south-east, south-west and north-west.

| Direction | Bearing |
|---|---|
| North | 000° |
| North-east | 045° |
| East | 090° |
| South-east | 135° |
| South | 180° |
| South-west | 225° |
| West | 270° |
| North-west | 315° |

A **bearing** is measured:

- from the **north line** at the point you are travelling from or looking from,
- **clockwise**,
- and written with **three figures**, so 45° is written 045°.

When a question says "the bearing of B from A", put your protractor at A, take north as the zero line there, and turn clockwise until you point to B.

## Back bearings

The bearing of A from B and the bearing of B from A differ by 180°. The two north lines are parallel, so the angles between the north lines and the line AB are co-interior angles that add up to 180°.

```
back bearing = bearing + 180°   (if the bearing is less than 180°)
back bearing = bearing − 180°   (if the bearing is 180° or more)
```

### Worked example 4

The bearing of B from A is 072°. Find the bearing of A from B.

```
072° + 180° = 252°
```

### Worked example 5

The bearing of D from C is 248°. Find the bearing of C from D.

```
248° − 180° = 068°
```

Notice that the answer is written 068°, with its leading zero.

## Bearings in a triangle

A bearing question often forms a triangle. Mark north lines at each point, mark the angles, and use the angle facts you know.

### Worked example 6

A ship sails 12 km from P on a bearing of 040° to Q. It then sails 9 km on a bearing of 130° to R. Find the distance PR and the bearing of R from P.

```
bearing of P from Q = 040° + 180° = 220°
angle PQR = 220° − 130° = 90°
PR² = 12² + 9² = 225, so PR = 15 km
angle QPR: tan(QPR) = 9/12, so QPR = 36.9°
bearing of R from P = 040° + 36.9° = 76.9°, written 077° to the nearest degree
bearing of P from R = 76.9° + 180° = 256.9°, written 257° to the nearest degree
```

The right angle comes from the bearings: 130° is exactly 90° less than 220°, so the legs meet at a right angle. If you do the same problem as a scale drawing with 1 cm for 3 km, then PQ is 4 cm, QR is 3 cm and PR should measure about 5 cm.

### Worked example 7

The bearing of B from A is 040° and AB = 10 km. The bearing of C from B is 130° and BC = 10 km. Find the bearing of A from C.

```
angle ABC = (040° + 180°) − 130° = 90°
triangle ABC is right-angled and isosceles, so angle BCA = 45°
bearing of B from C = 130° + 180° = 310°
bearing of A from C = 310° − 45° = 265°
```

You can check by drawing: A is to the west of C, slightly south, which matches 265°.

## Interpreting a scale drawing

A scale drawing may show a situation such as two ships, a tree and its shadow, or a ladder against a wall. To interpret it, find the quantity asked for, measure it, and use the scale factor.

- If it asks for the real distance between two objects, measure the drawn distance and multiply.
- If it asks for the bearing of one object from another, measure the angle clockwise from north at the starting object.
- If it asks which of two routes is shorter, convert both lengths before comparing.

### Worked example 8

A tower stands on level ground. From a point 40 m from its base, the angle of elevation of the top is 35°. Use a scale drawing with 1 cm representing 10 m to estimate the height of the tower.

```
horizontal line: 40 m ÷ 10 = 4 cm
draw an angle of 35° at the end of the line, and a vertical line at the other end
the two lines cross; the vertical line measures about 2.8 cm
height ≈ 2.8 × 10 = 28 m
```

A calculation gives 40 × tan 35° = 28.0 m, so the drawing agrees. This is a useful check on your accuracy. A slip of one degree on the protractor changes the answer by about half a metre here, so work carefully and use a sharp pencil.

## Common mistakes

- Measuring the angle anticlockwise or from the wrong north line.
- Writing a bearing with two figures, such as 72° instead of 072°.
- Adding 180° to a bearing larger than 180° without subtracting 360°. For example 248° + 180° = 428°, which is the same as 068°.
- Using the scale the wrong way round (dividing when you should multiply).
- Forgetting to convert units: cm to m or km.
- Multiplying an area by the length scale instead of its square.
- Not using a ruler for straight edges.

## Exam technique

- **Draw** needs a ruler and a protractor, and your lines must be clear.
- **Calculate** the real distance: show the measurement and the multiplication.
- **Measure** gives an answer read from a diagram.
- Label north lines and angles on your sketch before you calculate.
- Check bearings with common sense: south-west must be near 225°.
