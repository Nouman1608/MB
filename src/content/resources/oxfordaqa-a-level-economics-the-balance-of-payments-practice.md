## Questions

**1.** Country M has these figures, in billions of M-units: goods credits 140 and debits 165; services credits 55 and debits 38; primary income credits 20 and debits 31; secondary income credits 8 and debits 12. GDP is 500.

**(a)** Find the combined balance on goods and services. **[2]**
**(b)** Express the current account balance as a share of GDP. **[2]**

**2.** In Country M the capital account is +3 and the financial account is +17. Using your answer to question 1, calculate net errors and omissions, and state what this item represents. **[3]**

**3.** Compare foreign direct investment with portfolio investment, using an illustration of each. **[4]**

**4.** In Country N, each 1 rise in national income adds 0.15 to imports. The government wants imports to fall by 9 billion using expenditure-reducing policy. The multiplier is 2.5.

**(a)** Calculate the fall in income needed. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the cut in aggregate demand needed. **[2]**

**5.** Country M has exports of 180 and imports of 200 (billions of M-units, goods and services). Its currency falls 5%. Export prices in M-units are unchanged and import prices in M-units rise 5%.

**(a)** Calculate the new trade balance if export PED is 2.0 and import PED is 0.8. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the new trade balance if export PED is 0.4 and import PED is 0.3. **[3]**

**6.** A country runs a current account deficit of 3.5% of GDP each year. Nominal GDP grows 5% a year. Using deficit divided by growth, calculate the long-run level of foreign liabilities as a percentage of GDP, then the level if growth falls to 3.5%. Comment on what this shows. **[4]**

**7.** Explain two consequences of a persistent current account deficit. **[4]**

**8.** A foreign firm invests 90 million M-units in a plant in Country M. Each year it exports 25 million of output, imports 9 million of inputs and sends home profit equal to an 8% return on the investment.

**(a)** Calculate the yearly net effect on the current account. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain one reason why FDI may be a more stable source of finance for a deficit than portfolio investment. **[2]**

**9.** The government of Country N imposes a 10% tariff on imported goods. Import demand has PED of 1.2 and the goods import bill, before tariff, was 300 billion N-units.

**(a)** Calculate the change in import volume and the new bill before tariff. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain one reason why the tariff may not improve the balance of payments as much as this suggests. **[1]**

**10.** Using an illustration of each, say how policies that reduce expenditure differ from policies that switch it. **[4]**

**11.** Explain why a large and persistent current account surplus may be a problem for the country running it. **[4]**

**12.** Country M has a current account deficit of 40 billion M-units (5% of GDP), unemployment of 9%, inflation of 1.5% and growth of 0.8%. In the short run its export PED is estimated at 0.4 and import PED at 0.3. The government is deciding between cutting spending and raising taxes, a fall in the exchange rate, or supply-side policy. Evaluate which policy, or mix of policies, it should choose. **[8]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Goods = 140 - 165 = -25 [1]; services = 55 - 38 = +17, so trade balance = -25 + 17 = **-8** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Primary income = 20 - 31 = -11 and secondary income = 8 - 12 = -4, so current account = -8 - 11 - 4 = **-23** [1]; -23 / 500 × 100 = **-4.6%** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show each balance before adding, and keep the signs. A bare total with no signs is hard to credit if one balance is wrong.

**2.** Current + capital + financial = -23 + 3 + 17 = -3 [1]; net errors and omissions = **+3** [1]. It is the balancing item that makes the accounts sum to zero, covering mistakes and unrecorded transactions [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers gain credit for explaining why the item exists, not only for the number. State that the whole balance of payments sums to zero.

**3.** FDI is investment in real business operations abroad with a lasting interest and control [1], for example a firm building a factory [1]. Portfolio investment is the purchase of financial assets such as shares or bonds with no control [1], for example a pension fund buying government bonds [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A distinction needs both sides compared. The key contrast is control and permanence, so make it explicit.

**4. (a)** Fall in income = 9 / 0.15 = **60 billion** [1] (method: fall in imports divided by the marginal propensity to import [1]). **[2]**
**(b)** Cut in AD = 60 / 2.5 = **24 billion** [1] (method: income fall divided by the multiplier [1]). **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the step you are doing: income first, then AD. Dividing 9 by 2.5 directly is a common slip.

**5. (a)** Exports = 180 × (1 + 2.0 × 0.05) = 180 × 1.10 = **198** [1]; imports = 200 × 1.05 × (1 - 0.8 × 0.05) = 200 × 1.05 × 0.96 = **201.60** [1]; balance = 198 - 201.60 = **-3.60** (an improvement of 16.40) [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Exports = 180 × (1 + 0.4 × 0.05) = **183.60** [1]; imports = 200 × 1.05 × (1 - 0.3 × 0.05) = 200 × 1.05 × 0.985 = **206.85** [1]; balance = 183.60 - 206.85 = **-23.25** (worse than -20) [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the volume change and the price change separately for imports. Comparing the two balances with the starting -20 makes the role of elasticity clear.

**6.** 3.5 / 5 × 100 = **70% of GDP** [1]; at 3.5% growth, 3.5 / 3.5 × 100 = **100%** [1]. Slower growth raises the long-run burden of the same deficit [1], so a deficit should be judged against growth in income, not as a fixed sum [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Finish with the interpretation. The idea is that sustainability depends on the deficit relative to income and growth.

**7.** Consequence one: the deficit has to be paid for by foreign lending or asset sales [1], so foreign claims rise and income flows abroad as interest and profit, which worsens the primary income balance [1]. Consequence two: if lenders lose confidence, inflows slow [1], which may force higher interest rates or a falling currency, with lower growth [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers chain the argument (cause, then effect, then result) and do not just list. One developed consequence is worth more than two bare ones.

**8. (a)** Profit sent abroad = 8% × 90 = **7.2 million** [1]; net effect = 25 - 9 - 7.2 [1] = **+8.8 million** a year [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** FDI is tied to physical operations and a long-term commitment [1], so it is hard to withdraw quickly, whereas portfolio investors can sell and leave at short notice [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Include the profit outflow in the current account calculation. Leaving it out gives +16, which ignores the primary income debit.

**9. (a)** Volume change = -1.2 × 10 = **-12%** [1]; new bill before tariff = 300 × 0.88 = **264 billion** [1]; a fall of 36 billion [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Trading partners may retaliate with their own tariffs, cutting the country's exports [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* The tariff itself is not part of the foreign-currency bill, so state that your figure is before tariff. Any one sound limitation earns the mark.

**10.** Expenditure-reducing policy cuts total spending [1], for example higher taxes or interest rates, so that fewer imports are bought [1]. Expenditure-switching policy moves spending from imports to home-produced goods [1], for example a fall in the exchange rate or a tariff [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The distinction is about what happens to spending. Say whether total demand falls or only changes direction.

**11.** A surplus may reflect weak domestic demand and low consumption, so households do not enjoy the gains from trade [1]. Export-led demand can raise AD and cause inflation when the economy is near capacity [1]. Trading partners with the matching deficits may be pushed into debt or may respond with trade barriers [1]. Pressure for a higher currency can erode competitiveness [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Surplus questions often tempt a one-sided "surplus is good" answer. Answers that explain a cost with a mechanism gain more than assertions.

**12.** Cutting spending or raising taxes (expenditure-reducing) would lower imports [1], but with unemployment at 9% and growth of 0.8% it would deepen the weakness and conflict with employment [1]. A fall in the exchange rate raises import prices and lowers export prices [1]. With PEDs of 0.4 and 0.3 in the short run, the import bill rises more than export volume, so the deficit may worsen at first [1], though the effect improves if elasticities rise over time [1]. Inflation of only 1.5% leaves room for some imported inflation [1]. Supply-side policy raises productivity and quality, so competitiveness improves without cutting demand, but it is slow and costly and cannot fix the deficit quickly [1]. Judgement: with spare capacity and low inflation, a gentle depreciation combined with supply-side measures suits best and spending cuts should be avoided, though the choice depends on how quickly elasticities rise and whether the deficit is easy to finance [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers use the data given (unemployment, inflation, the elasticities) and reach a clear judgement with a reason. A list of policies without linking to the data is the weaker route.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a deficit without saying which account or giving its sign.
- Leaving out profit outflows when judging the effect of FDI on the current account.
- Applying elasticity to the wrong price: import prices rise in home currency when the currency falls.
- Dividing by the wrong figure when finding income or AD changes.
- Describing a policy without saying whether it cuts spending or switches it.
- Treating a currency fall as always successful when demand is inelastic in the short run.
- Ignoring the state of the economy, such as spare capacity, when choosing between policies.
- Giving one-sided answers on deficits and surpluses, with no judgement.
