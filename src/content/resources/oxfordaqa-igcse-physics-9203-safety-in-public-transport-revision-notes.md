Condensed recall for **3.1.5 Safety in public transport** in OxfordAQA International GCSE Physics (9203), for exams May/June 2018 onwards. Worked examples are in the [Safety in public transport study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-safety-in-public-transport/); exam-style questions are in the [Safety in public transport practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-safety-in-public-transport-practice/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Driving force | The forward force on a vehicle from its engine, through the wheels |
| Resistive forces | Forces opposing motion: air resistance and friction |
| Braking force | The backward force produced when the brakes are applied |
| Deceleration | Negative acceleration: the rate at which velocity decreases |
| Reaction time | Time between the driver seeing a hazard and applying the brakes |
| Thinking distance | Distance travelled during the reaction time |
| Braking distance | Distance travelled while the braking force acts, until the vehicle stops |
| Stopping distance | Thinking distance + braking distance |
| Adverse road conditions | Wet or icy roads (as the specification defines them) |
| Poor vehicle condition | Worn brakes or tyres (as the specification defines it) |

## Condensed recall

**Steady speed (a).** Driving force = resistive forces, so the resultant force is zero and the velocity is constant.

**Braking force (b).**
- Bigger braking force → bigger deceleration (a = F ÷ m).
- Faster vehicle → bigger braking force needed to stop in a given distance.
- For a given braking force, faster vehicle → longer stopping distance.
- Large decelerations → brakes overheat (less effective) and/or wheels skid (loss of control).

**Stopping distance (c).**
- Stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance.
- Thinking distance = speed × reaction time, so it is proportional to speed.
- Reaction time is increased by tiredness, distractions, drugs and alcohol.

**Work done by brakes (d).**
- Friction between brakes and wheel does work on the vehicle.
- This work reduces the vehicle's kinetic energy.
- The brakes' temperature rises; energy then spreads to the surroundings.
- With a constant braking force: braking force × braking distance = ½ m v².

**Braking distance factors (e).** Wet or icy roads; worn brakes; worn tyres. All reduce the braking force available or the grip, so the vehicle travels further while braking.

## Which factor affects which distance

| Factor | Thinking distance | Braking distance |
|---|---|---|
| Higher speed | Increases (in proportion) | Increases (with speed squared) |
| Tiredness | Increases | No effect |
| Alcohol or drugs | Increases | No effect |
| Distraction | Increases | No effect |
| Wet or icy road | No effect | Increases |
| Worn tyres | No effect | Increases |
| Worn brakes | No effect | Increases |

## Speed scaling

| Speed | ×2 | ×3 |
|---|---|---|
| Thinking distance | ×2 | ×3 |
| Kinetic energy | ×4 | ×9 |
| Braking distance (same braking force) | ×4 | ×9 |

## Equations used together

```
a = F / m
thinking distance = speed x reaction time
Ek = 1/2 m v^2
work done = force x distance
```

## Calculation chain for a stopping question

1. Thinking distance = speed × reaction time (reaction time in seconds).
2. Kinetic energy = ½ × mass × speed².
3. Braking distance = kinetic energy ÷ braking force (constant force does all the stopping work).
4. Stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance.
5. If asked for deceleration: a = braking force ÷ mass.

Short example: a 1500 kg car at 16 m/s, reaction time 0.60 s, braking force 6000 N. Thinking distance = 9.6 m. Kinetic energy = 192 000 J. Braking distance = 32 m. Stopping distance = 41.6 m. Deceleration = 4.0 m/s².

## Exam traps

- **Mixing up the two distances.** Driver factors affect thinking distance only.
- **Doubling braking distance.** Doubling speed makes braking distance four times longer, not twice.
- **"Uses up energy".** Say kinetic energy is transferred to thermal energy in the brakes.
- **Constant speed is not zero force.** There are forces; they are balanced.
- **Square the speed.** ½ × 1000 × 20² is 200 000 J, not 10 000 J.
- **Statement b direction.** A larger braking force gives a larger deceleration, so a shorter braking distance, but greater risk of skidding.

## Self-test

**1.** A tram moves at a steady 12 m/s with a driving force of 4500 N. State the total resistive force.

**2.** Define stopping distance.

**3.** A driver's reaction time is 0.70 s. Find the thinking distance at 20 m/s.

**4.** Name two factors that increase thinking distance.

**5.** Name two factors that increase braking distance.

**6.** A 2000 kg minibus has a braking force of 8000 N. Find its deceleration.

**7.** Find the kinetic energy of a 2000 kg minibus at 14 m/s.

**8.** Using questions 6 and 7, find the braking distance.

**9.** Speed doubles with the same braking force. What happens to braking distance?

**10.** Describe the energy transfer when a vehicle brakes to rest.

**11.** Give two dangers of a very large deceleration.

**12.** Why does a passenger standing on a bus lurch forwards when the bus brakes?

## Self-test answers

**1.** 4500 N (forces are balanced at steady speed).

**2.** The sum of the thinking distance and the braking distance.

**3.** 20 × 0.70 = 14 m.

**4.** Any two: tiredness, distractions, drugs, alcohol, higher speed.

**5.** Any two: wet road, icy road, worn tyres, worn brakes, higher speed.

**6.** a = 8000 ÷ 2000 = 4.0 m/s².

**7.** ½ × 2000 × 14² = 196 000 J.

**8.** 196 000 ÷ 8000 = 24.5 m.

**9.** It becomes four times longer.

**10.** Friction between brakes and wheels does work, reducing the kinetic energy of the vehicle; the brakes heat up and energy is transferred to the surroundings.

**11.** The brakes can overheat; the vehicle can skid and the driver loses control.

**12.** The bus is slowed by the braking force, but no force acts at first on the passenger, who continues moving forwards at the original speed.
