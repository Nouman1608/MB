These notes condense **1.2 How do economists approach the world?** from the IB Diploma Programme *Economics guide* (first assessment 2022). It is SL and HL content. For full explanations read the [1.2 study guide](/resources/ib-dp-economics-1-2/), then test yourself with the [1.2 practice questions](/resources/ib-dp-economics-1-2-practice/). Figures are invented.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Economic methodology | The methods economists use to build and test explanations |
| Positive statement | A statement about what is, was or will be, testable against evidence (it may be false) |
| Normative statement | A statement containing a value judgement about what ought to be |
| Value judgement | A view based on beliefs about what is good or fair, not on evidence alone |
| Hypothesis | A testable statement of a relationship between variables |
| Model | A simplified representation of reality that isolates key relationships |
| Theory | A set of related ideas that explains and predicts, supported by evidence so far |
| Ceteris paribus | "Other things being equal": holding all variables but one constant |
| Empirical evidence | Data and observation used to test a hypothesis |
| Refutation | Rejecting or revising a hypothesis when evidence contradicts it |
| Laissez faire | The view that the state should interfere little in markets |
| Margin | The extra benefit or cost of one more unit |

## Method in one chain

Assumptions and logic → hypothesis → model → test against empirical evidence (with ceteris paribus) → supported for now, or refuted and revised.

- Economics cannot easily run controlled experiments on whole economies.
- Many variables change at once, so ceteris paribus rarely holds in real data.
- Conclusions are therefore provisional and conditional.

## Positive or normative: quick tests

- Look for "should", "ought", "too", "fair", "unfair", "better", "worse": normative.
- Ask: could data, in principle, show it to be right or wrong? If yes: positive.
- A false statement can still be positive.
- A statement with numbers can still be normative ("should cut debt to 60% of GDP").
- The same issue can produce both types.

## Models: strengths and limits

| Strength | Limit |
|---|---|
| Isolate one relationship | Rest on assumptions that may not hold |
| Make predictions that can be tested | Ceteris paribus rarely holds in reality |
| Give a shared language and diagrams | Human behaviour changes; values differ |

Judge a model by how well it predicts or explains for the purpose, not by realism alone.

## Economic thought timeline

| Era | Idea | Key name(s) | Course link |
|---|---|---|---|
| 18th century | Laissez faire; the "invisible hand" of self-interest in markets | Adam Smith, *The Wealth of Nations* (1776) | Market efficiency, Unit 2 |
| 19th century | Say's law: supply creates its own demand | Jean-Baptiste Say | Self-correcting economy |
| 19th century | Utility and marginal analysis (1870s) | Jevons, Menger, Walras; Marshall's *Principles* (1890) | Marginal utility, demand and supply |
| 19th century | Critique of capitalism: class conflict, crises | Karl Marx | Inequality, role of the state |
| 20th century | Demand can be too low for long periods; active policy | John Maynard Keynes, *General Theory* (1936) | Keynesian AS, demand management |
| 20th century | Money supply and inflation; rational expectations; economy returns to potential | Milton Friedman; Robert Lucas | Vertical LRAS |
| 21st century | Biases and rules of thumb in decisions | Kahneman and Tversky; Thaler | Nudges (HL 2.4) |
| 21st century | Economy within environmental limits; circular economy; the "doughnut" | Kate Raworth | Sustainability, 4.7 |

## Using 1.2 in evaluation across the course

- **Name the assumption:** "if firms maximise profit…", "if consumers respond to price…".
- **Separate evidence from values:** say what data show, then which value judgement decides the policy choice (for example efficiency against equity).
- **Contrast schools:** a Keynesian expects demand-side policy to cut unemployment; a new classical economist expects output to return to potential anyway.
- **Flag ceteris paribus:** real outcomes may differ because other factors changed at the same time.

## Exam traps

- Calling a statement positive because it contains a statistic.
- Calling a statement normative because it seems wrong.
- Naming a thinker without the idea and why it matters.
- Writing that a model is "unrealistic, so useless".
- Forgetting that evidence can fail to refute a hypothesis because other things changed, not because the hypothesis is true.

## Quick self-test

1. Define a normative statement.
2. Classify: "Velsa's inflation rate will be 4% next year."
3. Classify: "Velsa's government should spend 5% of GDP on health."
4. Why can a false statement still be positive?
5. What does ceteris paribus mean, and why is it used?
6. Give one reason why economists find it hard to test hypotheses.
7. A fare cut is followed by fewer bus journeys, but a new railway line opened the same month. Should the hypothesis "lower fares raise journeys" be refuted? Explain.
8. What was the policy implication of Adam Smith's ideas?
9. State Say's law.
10. Which economist argued that aggregate demand can stay too low, leaving lasting unemployment?
11. How did the monetarist and new classical view differ from the Keynesian view?
12. Name one 21st-century development in economic thought and its course link.

### Answers

1. A statement containing a value judgement about what ought to be.
2. Positive: it is a prediction that data can test.
3. Normative: "should" signals a value judgement.
4. Positive means testable, not true; evidence could show it to be wrong.
5. "Other things being equal": holding other variables constant so the effect of one can be isolated.
6. Many variables change at once in real economies, and controlled experiments on whole economies are rarely possible.
7. Not yet: ceteris paribus did not hold, because the railway gave travellers a substitute. More data are needed.
8. Laissez faire: limited state interference in markets.
9. Supply creates its own demand, so general overproduction should not last.
10. John Maynard Keynes.
11. They argued the economy tends to return to potential output and that demand management cannot permanently cut unemployment; Keynes argued active policy was needed.
12. Behavioural economics (link: HL 2.4 nudges) or the circular economy and environmental limits (link: sustainable development).
