These revision notes condense the [multimodal texts study guide](/resources/ib-myp-language-acquisition-multimodal-texts-and-visuals/) -- go there for full explanations and worked examples. They cover IB MYP Language Acquisition and are aligned to the International Baccalaureate Organization, Middle Years Programme Subject Brief -- *Language acquisition*, from 2020 (first eAssessment May 2023/November 2023). They suit MYP years 4 and 5 (no SL/HL split) and apply to school units and to eAssessment from the May 2023 and November 2023 sessions onwards.

MYP has no prescribed content list -- schools design their own units. These notes cover a skill named in the brief's criteria: working with multimodal texts. Notes are in English; practise every frame in your target language.

Links: [practice questions](/resources/ib-myp-language-acquisition-multimodal-texts-and-visuals-practice/), [course hub](/boards/ib/ib-myp/myp-language-acquisition/), [printable checklist](/checklists/ib/ib-myp/myp-language-acquisition/), [criteria in practice revision notes](/resources/ib-myp-language-acquisition-criteria-in-practice-revision-notes/).

## Key definitions

- **Mode** -- one channel of meaning: written, spoken, visual, spatial, aural.
- **Multimodal text** -- a text that combines two or more modes (poster, web page, video, infographic, comic, social media post).
- **Spatial aspects** -- layout: position, size, order, grouping and white space.
- **Denotation** -- what is literally shown or heard.
- **Connotation** -- what a detail suggests or makes you feel.
- **Interplay** -- the brief's idea that the aspects of a text work together to convey ideas, values and attitudes.
- **Multiliteracy** -- the brief's term for skills across many kinds of text; one of the stated aims of the subject.

## What the brief says each criterion expects (paraphrased)

| Criterion | Multimodal focus |
|---|---|
| A Listening | Meaning from spoken text, and how images and spatial aspects shown with it convey ideas, values and attitudes |
| B Reading | Meaning from written text, and how its spatial and visual aspects convey ideas, values and attitudes |
| C Speaking | Interacting on personal, local and global topics, supported by spoken, written and visual texts |
| D Writing | Language suited to audience and purpose; understanding of language, form, mode and medium |

Each criterion: eight achievement levels (1-8) in four bands. Your teacher will share the task-specific clarifications.

## eAssessment facts from the brief

| Component | Objective | Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|---|---|
| On-screen examination | Comprehending spoken multimodal texts | A | 24 |
| On-screen examination | Comprehending written multimodal texts | B | 24 |
| On-screen examination | Writing in response to multimodal texts | D | 24 |
| Speaking examination | Speaking in response to multimodal texts | C | 24 |

- On-screen examination: three tasks; receptive (A, B) and productive (D).
- Marks per task may vary by no more than three from the blueprint (so 21 to 27 around each 24).
- Speaking: interactive, internally assessed by your class teacher, sample moderated by the IB.
- Courses assessed at one of three proficiency levels: emergent, capable, proficient.
- Timing and exact task format: confirm with your school.

## Visual vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Typical effect |
|---|---|---|
| Close-up | Face or object fills the frame | Emotion, intimacy, detail |
| Long shot | Whole figure plus setting | Context, isolation, scale |
| Low angle | Camera looks up | Subject seems strong or important |
| High angle | Camera looks down | Subject seems small or vulnerable |
| Direct gaze | Subject looks at viewer | Demands a response, creates contact |
| Indirect gaze | Subject looks away | Viewer observes, feels outside the scene |
| Foreground | Nearest part of image | What the designer puts first |
| Salience | What stands out most (size, colour, contrast) | Where the eye lands first |
| Typography | Font, size, capitals, weight | Tone: shouting, calm, playful, official |
| White space | Empty area around elements | Calm, focus, a premium feel |
| Icon/symbol | Simple image standing for an idea | Fast meaning across languages |

Sound vocabulary for Criterion A: tone of voice, pace, pause, volume, music (mood, change of track), sound effects (setting), silence (emphasis).

## Four text-image relationships

| Relationship | What happens | Quick example |
|---|---|---|
| Anchor | Words fix the meaning of an open image | Caption tells you a smiling crowd is celebrating, not protesting |
| Reinforce | Words and image say the same thing | "Clean beaches" over a photo of a clean beach |
| Extend | One mode adds what the other leaves out | Chart shows the numbers, text gives the reason |
| Contrast | Words and image pull against each other | "Relaxing holiday?" over a packed, noisy airport |

## Method in steps: Notice -- Connect -- Explain

```
1. NOTICE   one precise detail (denotation)      "In the foreground..."
2. CONNECT  it to the words / sound / layout      "...which contrasts with the headline..."
3. EXPLAIN  effect + idea, value or attitude      "...so the reader feels... This shows the text values..."
```

Repeat the cycle for two or three details, not ten. Depth beats a list.

## Method in steps: responding to a multimodal stimulus in writing (D)

```
1. Identify the task's text type, audience and purpose.
2. Pick 3-4 facts or ideas from the stimulus that you need.
3. Rephrase them -- do not copy whole phrases.
4. Add your own opinion, reason or example.
5. Check register (formal / informal) and text-type conventions.
```

## Method in steps: talking about an image (C)

```
1. Overview: what kind of text, who it is for.         (1 sentence)
2. Describe: 2-3 key details, foreground first.         (2-3 sentences)
3. Interpret: purpose, message, values.                 (2-3 sentences)
4. Personal link: your view or experience.              (1-2 sentences)
5. Be ready for a follow-up question on any of these.
```

## Phrase bank (translate into your target language)

- *At first sight... / The first thing I notice is...*
- *On the left / right / at the top / at the bottom...*
- *The colour ... creates a ... mood.*
- *The headline anchors the photo by...*
- *The image adds something the text does not say:...*
- *The target audience seems to be... because...*
- *The text suggests that... / It implies that...*
- *In my culture this colour would mean..., but here...*

## Worked reminders

- **Weak:** "The poster is blue. It is nice."
- **Better:** "The poster is mainly pale blue, which suggests calm. This fits the headline 'Take a breath', so the reader feels invited to relax."
- **Listening note-taking:** two columns -- *heard* / *saw*. "Heard: 'everyone welcome' / Saw: people of all ages at one table" -> reinforce.

## The same detail at three levels (rough guide only)

| Level | What an answer about one detail might look like |
|---|---|
| Emergent | "The girl is smiling. She is happy." -- accurate description, one simple idea |
| Capable | "The girl is smiling because the advert wants volunteering to look fun." -- description linked to purpose |
| Proficient | "Her direct smile at the camera invites the reader in, so the advert feels personal rather than a request from an organisation." -- effect on the audience and an implied attitude |

This is not an IB descriptor; your teacher will tell you what your level's tasks expect.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Describe vs interpret:** describing names what is there; interpreting explains what it means and why it is there.
- **Denotation vs connotation:** literal content vs suggested meaning.
- **Receptive vs productive vs interactive:** A and B receptive; D productive; C interactive (the brief's wording).
- **Explicit vs implicit:** a stated message ("Recycle!") vs an attitude you infer (the text assumes the reader is careless).
- **Layout vs content:** *where* something is placed is meaning too, not just *what* it is.
- **Proficiency level vs criterion level:** the proficiency level (emergent, capable, proficient) sets the course; the criterion level (1-8) judges your performance in it.

## Quick self-test

1. Name five modes a text can use.
2. What is the difference between denotation and connotation?
3. A caption tells you a photo of an empty street shows a curfew, not an early morning. Which text-image relationship is this?
4. What effect does a low camera angle usually create?
5. How many marks does the brief's blueprint give the on-screen examination in total?
6. By how many marks may the marks in an on-screen task vary from the blueprint?
7. Which criterion is assessed in the internally assessed, IB-moderated examination?
8. What are the three steps of Notice -- Connect -- Explain?
9. Why should you be careful when interpreting colour?
10. In a listening task, what two things should you note side by side?
11. Name the three proficiency levels.

### Answers

1. Written, spoken, visual, spatial, aural (non-speech sound).
2. Denotation is what is literally there; connotation is what it suggests.
3. Anchor -- the words fix the meaning of an open image.
4. The subject looks strong, powerful or important.
5. 72 marks (24 each for A, B and D).
6. By no more than three marks.
7. Criterion C: Speaking.
8. Notice a detail, connect it to the words, sound or layout, explain its effect and the idea, value or attitude.
9. Colour meanings depend on culture, so link them to the community the text was made for.
10. What you heard and what you saw at the same moment.
11. Emergent, capable, proficient.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Answering a reading question about a poster using only the written words.
- Naming a feature ("there is a close-up") without saying what it does.
- Interpreting with no evidence -- every "this suggests" needs a named detail before it.
- Listing six visual features at surface level instead of explaining two in depth.
- Missing a contrast between words and image, and so missing irony or humour.
- In listening, forgetting the on-screen images and on-screen text.
- In writing, lifting whole sentences from the stimulus instead of rephrasing.
- In writing, ignoring the text type and register the task sets.
- In speaking, describing the image for too long and never giving an interpretation or opinion.
- Assuming a colour or gesture means the same in every culture.
