The focused study **Conflict and displacement** is one of the five focused studies in IB DP History (first assessment 2028). Both of its examples must be studied: **post-war displacement in Europe (1945 to 1960)** and **the Indochina refugee crisis (1975 to 1990)**. There are three inquiry questions. Paper 1 assesses the study through sources, so this guide supplies the historical context and shows how to use sources about displaced people. It applies to SL and HL. Because the subject involves suffering, persecution and death, treat the material with care and precision.

## How paper 1 uses this study

Paper 1 (1 hour 15 minutes, 24 marks) gives three sources (A, B and C) and one inquiry question. The three static questions ask you to explain how the content of sources A and B can be used (6 marks), analyse how the context of source C influences its use (6 marks), and examine the perspectives in all the sources (12 marks). The grounding content provides the context.

Some key terms. A **refugee** is a person outside their country who has fled persecution or conflict. A **displaced person** is someone forced to leave home, and in 1945 the term "displaced persons" (DPs) was used for people outside their country of origin as a result of the war. A **stateless person** has no recognised nationality. **Repatriation** is return to the country of origin; **resettlement** is movement to a new country.

## Inquiry question 1: What were the conditions that led to mass displacement?

### Post-war Europe

The grounding content names combat operations and Allied victory, persecution and fear of reprisals, and economic factors.

The Second World War uprooted millions of people. Armies advanced through Eastern and Central Europe, destroying homes and cities. When Germany surrendered in 1945, the Allies found millions of people outside their home countries: prisoners of war, people brought to Germany as forced labourers, survivors of concentration camps and refugees. Authorities estimated that there were several million displaced persons in Germany and Austria alone in 1945. (Figures vary according to source and date, so say "millions".)

Persecution and fear of reprisals also drove movement. Many Jewish survivors did not want to return to places where their communities had been destroyed and where there had been postwar violence. People who had collaborated with the occupying forces, or feared Soviet rule, hesitated to go home. Millions of ethnic Germans were expelled or fled from Poland, Czechoslovakia and other eastern countries, in part because of reprisals for German occupation and under the terms the Allies accepted at Potsdam in 1945. Economic factors mattered too: ruined cities, shortages of food and housing, and collapsed industry gave people little reason to stay or return.

### The Indochina refugee crisis

The grounding content names combat operations and Communist victory, persecution and fear of reprisals, and economic factors.

The Vietnam War ended in April 1975 with the fall of Saigon, and the communist Pathet Lao took power in Laos later that year. In Cambodia the Khmer Rouge took power in April 1975. In the first wave, tens of thousands of people connected with the former South Vietnamese government and the American presence left, many in the final days. Later waves followed as people feared punishment or "re-education" camps, lost property and faced economic hardship under new collectivisation policies. In Cambodia, the Khmer Rouge emptied the cities, and historians estimate that well over a million people died from execution, forced labour, hunger and disease (estimates vary) before its overthrow in January 1979. In Laos, many highland people who had supported the anti-communist forces fled. From 1978 to 1979, tensions between Vietnam and China, and Vietnamese policies, pushed many ethnic Chinese (the Hoa) to leave.

## Inquiry question 2: What was the national and international response to displacement?

### Post-war Europe

The grounding content names displaced persons camps, migration, emigration and repatriation; the roles of Allied governments and non-governmental organisations, including the International Red Cross; and the roles of the UN, including UNRRA and the IRO.

The Allies set up assembly centres and displaced persons camps, many in the former concentration camp or military sites. The United Nations Relief and Rehabilitation Administration (UNRRA), founded in 1943, worked with the military to feed, house and repatriate people. Millions returned home in 1945, many of them quickly. Others stayed, particularly Jewish survivors, and people from the Baltic states and Eastern Europe unwilling to return to Soviet-controlled countries. The Soviet Union pressed for the return of its citizens, and some repatriation was forced. In 1947 the International Refugee Organization (IRO) replaced UNRRA, and over the next years helped hundreds of thousands to resettle in the United States, Canada, Australia, Israel and elsewhere. Voluntary organisations such as the Red Cross and religious and Jewish relief groups gave aid. The IRO was succeeded in the early 1950s by the office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees.

### The Indochina refugee crisis

The grounding content names displaced persons camps, migration, emigration and repatriation; the roles of the USA and the Vietnamese government; and the role of the UNHCR, including the Orderly Departure Program (1979).

Refugees fled by land and by sea, and the so-called "boat people" faced storms, piracy and rejection. Neighbouring countries such as Thailand, Malaysia, Indonesia, Hong Kong and the Philippines hosted camps. In 1979 an international conference in Geneva secured pledges for resettlement from countries including the United States, and a memorandum between the UNHCR and the Vietnamese government created the **Orderly Departure Program**, which allowed people to leave Vietnam legally rather than by boat. The United States admitted large numbers under its refugee legislation, and other countries such as Canada, Australia and France also accepted large numbers. By the early 1990s, well over a million people had been resettled from the region. The Vietnamese government's role was contested: it allowed some departures, but it was also criticised for the policies that caused people to flee. In 1989 a Comprehensive Plan of Action tried to manage continuing flows, including screening and return.

## Inquiry question 3: How was displacement experienced by different groups?

### Post-war Europe

The grounding content names refugees and stateless persons; concentration camp survivors, including Jews, Roma and prisoners of war; and former forces under German command.

Different groups had very different experiences. Stateless persons, whose country had disappeared or who had lost citizenship, had no government to protect them. Concentration camp survivors, including Jews and Roma, were often ill and bereaved and had lost their homes. Many Jewish survivors lived in DP camps for years, where they organised schools, newspapers and political groups, and many wished to emigrate to Palestine, and later Israel after 1948. Roma survivors received little attention. Former members of units that had served under German command, including volunteers from occupied countries, raised difficult questions: some were screened and resettled, while others were repatriated, and the question of who counted as a victim and who as a collaborator was disputed.

### The Indochina refugee crisis

The grounding content names the Vietnamese, including the Hoa and the Montagnard; Cambodians fleeing the Khmer Rouge, including the Chams; and Laotians and highland people of Laos, including the Hmong.

The Hoa left in large numbers from 1978 to 1979, often paying to leave. The Montagnard, highland peoples of Vietnam who had worked with American forces, faced discrimination and were also displaced. Cambodians who fled the Khmer Rouge crossed into Thailand and Vietnam. The Cham, a Muslim minority, suffered particular persecution. In Laos, the Hmong, many of whom had fought with American-backed forces, fled across the Mekong river to Thailand and many later resettled in the United States and elsewhere. Camps like Khao-I-Dang in Thailand became large, long-term homes. Women and children were exposed to violence and exploitation on the journey.

## Using sources

- Typical sources include UNRRA and UNHCR reports, photographs of camps, memoirs of survivors, government statements and statistics. Memoirs are valuable for experience but record individual memory. Government documents give policy but not feelings.
- Always ask about the author's position: an aid worker, a government official, a refugee, a historian. Each saw a different part of the story.
- Figures need care. Say what the number counts and who produced it.

## Linking to the concepts

- **Cause and consequence:** conflict as a cause of displacement, and displacement as a consequence with its own consequences.
- **Continuity and change:** policies and institutions (UNRRA, IRO, UNHCR) changed, but the problems of statelessness and camps continued.
- **Perspectives:** refugees, governments, host communities, aid agencies.
- **Significance:** why these cases shaped the international refugee system.

## Common mistakes

- Giving precise figures with no source or date.
- Treating all displaced people as one group.
- Mixing up UNRRA, IRO and UNHCR.
- Writing about the war instead of how sources can be used.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which organisation came first, UNRRA or the IRO?** UNRRA was founded in 1943 and the IRO replaced it in 1947. The UNHCR followed in the early 1950s.

**What is the Orderly Departure Program?** An arrangement from 1979 allowing Vietnamese to emigrate legally, instead of by boat.
