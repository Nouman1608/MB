These questions practise key question 2.2 of Cambridge O Level Sociology (2251), *How does society
control us?* Section A follows the six-part (a)–(f) shape the syllabus describes for Paper 1
Question 2. Section B gives extra practice on agencies, theories and resistance.

## Section A — Social control question

**(a)** Define the terms:
- (i) *positive sanction* **[2]**
- (ii) *coercion* **[2]**

**(b)** Give **two** examples of informal negative sanctions. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **three** ways the family controls children's behaviour. **[6]**

**(d)** Explain **three** reasons why some people join protest groups. **[6]**

**(e)** Discuss the Marxist view of social control. **[8]**

**(f)** Evaluate the view that imprisonment is the most effective method of formal social control.
**[14]**

## Section B — Agencies, theories and resistance

**(g)** Explain the difference between deviant and non-conformist behaviour, giving an example of
each. **[4]**

**(h)** Explain **two** ways social media acts as an agency of informal social control. **[4]**

**(i)** Explain **two** ways the armed forces may be used as an agency of social control. **[4]**

**(j)** Explain the feminist view of social control. **[4]**

**(k)** Explain **two** reasons why formal social control may be less effective in some societies.
**[4]**

## Answers

**(a)(i) [2]** A reward given for conforming to norms or rules (1), such as praise from a parent, a
school prize or a promotion at work (1).

**(a)(ii) [2]** The use of force or the threat of force to make people conform (1), such as police
using physical restraint or the army enforcing a curfew (1).

**(b) [2]** Any two (1 each): ostracism; teasing or gossip; grounding by parents; withdrawal of pocket
money; a school detention; disapproving looks; hostile comments online; dismissal from a job;
religious disapproval.

**(c) [6]** Three ways, 2 marks each:
- **Parental rewards** such as treats, praise or extra freedom encourage children to repeat approved
  behaviour (2).
- **Negative sanctions** such as grounding or loss of privileges discourage disapproved behaviour (2).
- **Role modelling:** parents show norms through their own behaviour, which children copy and
  internalise (2).
- **Supervision:** parents monitor where children go and who their friends are, limiting
  opportunities for deviance (2).

**(d) [6]** Three reasons, 2 marks each:
- **Shared grievance:** people who feel strongly about an issue, such as climate change, want to
  bring about change and feel stronger acting together (2).
- **Belonging and identity:** membership gives a sense of community and a clear sense of who they are
  (2).
- **Lack of other channels:** people who feel ignored by governments or political parties see protest
  as the only way to be heard (2).
- **Peer influence and online networks:** friends or social media draw people in, as with school
  climate strikes that spread from 2018 (2).

**(e) [8]** Levels: 7–8 developed points with evidence and some evaluation; 4–6 some development;
1–3 basic points.
- Marxists see social control as serving the interests of the ruling class, not society as a whole.
- Laws protect private property and the capitalist system; police and courts enforce them most
  strongly against the working class.
- Althusser: **repressive state apparatuses** (police, courts, army) control by force when needed;
  **ideological state apparatuses** (education, media, religion) persuade people that inequality is
  fair.
- Ideological control produces false consciousness, so most people conform without force.
- Crimes of the powerful are less often detected or punished, showing control is unequal.
- **Evaluation:** functionalists argue many laws, such as those against violence, protect everyone;
  feminists argue Marxism ignores patriarchal control; many working-class people recognise and
  challenge inequality.

**(f) [14]** Levels: 11–14 balanced, developed, evaluated with a supported conclusion; 7–10 developed
but less balanced; 4–6 limited; 1–3 basic.

*For imprisonment:*
- **Incapacitation:** prisoners cannot offend in the community while inside.
- **Deterrence:** the threat of prison may discourage others.
- Satisfies public demand for justice, supporting the legitimacy of the law.
- A prison sentence signals society's strong disapproval, reinforcing the boundaries of acceptable
  behaviour for everyone (Durkheim).

*Against:*
- Prisons can act as "schools for crime" where offenders learn skills and make criminal contacts.
- The label of ex-prisoner makes finding work harder, increasing the chance of reoffending.
- It is expensive and breaks family ties, weakening informal control after release.
- Other formal methods may be more effective: digital surveillance deters and detects; law-making
  shapes behaviour before offences occur; community sentences and probation keep offenders in work.
- Most conformity comes from internalised norms and informal control, not formal punishment.

*Conclusion:* imprisonment protects the public from dangerous offenders, but its high reoffending
risks and cost mean it is not the most effective method overall; it works best combined with other
methods.

**(g) [4]** Deviant behaviour breaks a norm and attracts negative sanctions (1), e.g. shoplifting or
cheating in an examination (1). Non-conformist behaviour rejects mainstream expectations without
necessarily breaking rules or laws (1), e.g. adopting an unusual style of dress or choosing not to
marry (1).

**(h) [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each:
- **"Likes", shares and followers** reward content and behaviour that fits group norms, encouraging
  people to present themselves in approved ways (2).
- **Public shaming and hostile comments** punish people who break norms, deterring others (2).
- **Fear of exposure:** people behave carefully because images or comments may be shared widely (2).

**(i) [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each:
- Restoring order during riots, civil unrest or natural disasters when the police cannot cope (2).
- Enforcing curfews or emergency rule, using coercion or its threat (2).
- Protecting the state and its borders; in some societies, used against political opposition, which
  conflict theorists see as protecting those in power (2).

**(j) [4]** Feminists argue that social control maintains patriarchy, keeping women subordinate to men
(1). Heidensohn (1985) argued women are controlled more tightly than men (1): at home by domestic and
childcare responsibilities, in public by fear of male violence and concern for their reputation (1),
and at work by male supervisors and harassment (1).

**(k) [4]** Two reasons, 2 marks each:
- **Low trust or legitimacy:** where police or courts are seen as corrupt or unfair, people do not
  report crime or cooperate (2).
- **Limited resources:** too few officers or courts mean many offences are not detected or dealt with
  quickly (2).
- **Weak informal control:** in large, anonymous cities, formal agencies lack the support of
  community pressure (2).

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I use examples from my own society in part (d)?** Yes. Any protest group you can describe
accurately is relevant; explain the reason for joining, not just the group's aims.

**Does part (f) need theory?** Theory strengthens it: Durkheim's view of punishment and the labelling
of ex-prisoners both add explanation to the arguments.
