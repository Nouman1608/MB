These questions practise the "Research log" section of Component 4, the Cambridge Research Report, in Cambridge International AS & A Level Global Perspectives & Research (9239), 2026-2028, which requires candidates to use a log "throughout the research process to record information, plan actions and reflect on research findings and decisions". For the component as a whole, see the [Cambridge Research Report study guide](/resources/a-level-global-perspectives-9239-component-4-cambridge-research-report/).

> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge.** The log is coursework, so these questions practise writing and judging log entries; your own log must record your own research. All projects, sources and entries below are invented. Marks are for practice only.

Revise first with the [research log study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-global-perspectives-9239-research-report-research-log/) and the [research log revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-global-perspectives-9239-research-report-research-log-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** State how many marks the research log carries and whether it counts towards the report's 5000-word limit. **[2]**

**2.** Name the **three** activities the first log assessment point says the log is used for. **[3]**

**3.** Apart from decisions, name **two** things the syllabus says the log could contain. **[2]**

## Section B

**4.** Label each part of this entry as record, plan or reflect. **[3]**

> **(i)** "Read a national survey of commuting times." **(ii)** "It shows longer commutes in the suburbs, which supports the perspective that housing policy, not transport, is the real issue." **(iii)** "Next: find data on house prices by distance from the centre."

**5.** Rewrite this source entry so that it includes an evaluation: "Read an article by Green Futures about electric buses." **[3]**

**6.** A teacher tells a candidate that her question "Is tourism bad for coral reefs?" may only allow one perspective. Write a feedback entry recording this and her response. **[3]**

**7.** A candidate researching "Should cities replace parking spaces with housing?" had planned a survey of drivers but realises it would only show drivers' views. Write a decision entry about her choice of methods. **[4]**

**8.** A candidate pastes 900 words of polished argument from an earlier draft into his log because his report is over the word limit. Explain **three** problems with this. **[3]**

**9.** A candidate's two main sources disagree: a government report says a recycling scheme cut household waste, and a university study says waste moved to illegal dumping. Write a finding entry. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how log entries can help a candidate write the introduction and the conclusion of the report. **[4]**

**11.** A candidate writes all his log entries in the final week, from memory. Evaluate this approach. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate this sequence of entries against the log's assessment points. **[6]**

> **3 Oct.** Question: "Should schools teach financial literacy?" Read two articles.
> **17 Oct.** Teacher meeting. Talked about my question.
> **2 Nov.** Survey done, 30 replies from my year group. Most want financial lessons.
> **20 Nov.** Started writing report.

## Answers

**1.** 10 marks [1]; it does not count towards the 5000-word limit, but must not be used to extend it [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Record [1]; plan [1]; reflect [1]. **[3]**

**3.** Any two: reminders of things to check [1]; ongoing evaluation of the research [1]; reflections on how thinking or the research question changed as a result of teacher feedback [1]. **[2]**

**4.** (i) record [1]; (ii) reflect [1]; (iii) plan [1]. **[3]**

**5.** For example: "Read an article by Green Futures, a campaign group for clean transport, on electric buses (record) [1]. It reports lower running costs but cites only manufacturers' figures, and the group has an interest in promoting electric vehicles (evaluation) [1]. Use it for the advocates' perspective; look for an independent cost comparison (plan) [1]." **[3]**

**6.** For example: "Teacher said 'Is tourism bad for coral reefs?' assumes harm and may allow only one perspective (record) [1]. Agree: most sources I have found already assume damage (reflect) [1]. Reworded to 'Should coral reef areas limit tourist numbers?', which sets conservation against local livelihoods; check with teacher next week (plan) [1]." **[3]**

**7.** Credit an entry that records the original plan and its problem [1], states the new choice [1], gives the reason linked to the question [1] and notes a methodology point or next step [1]. For example: "Planned a survey of drivers, but it would show only one group's views, while the question is about the best use of land. Switching to a literature review on housing need and parking demand, plus council data on parking-space use. Planning questions are usually argued from land-use data and policy research. Next: request occupancy figures for three car parks." **[4]**

**8.** The syllabus says the log "should not be seen as a way to extend that word limit" [1]. Polished argument is not record, plan or reflection, so it would not meet the log's assessment points [1]. The report itself would still lack that argument, so its own marks would not benefit [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Credit an entry that records both findings with their sources [1], evaluates the conflict, for example that the government report measures collected waste only [1], reflects on what it means for the argument [1] and plans a next step [1]. For example: "Government report: household waste collections fell after the scheme. University study: fly-tipping rose in the same area. The report measures only what the council collects, so a fall could hide waste moved elsewhere. This weakens the 'scheme works' perspective. Next: find fly-tipping figures for nearby areas without the scheme." **[4]**

**10.** Introduction: the report must explain "how and why the question was developed" [1], and dated entries on topic choice and teacher feedback provide that account [1]. Conclusion: entries on findings that changed the candidate's view provide evidence for reflecting on how alternative perspectives influenced the argument [1], and the final reflection entry records limits to discuss [1]. **[4]**

**11.** It may produce a tidy log [1], but entries from memory lose the detail and reasons of decisions made at the time [1]. The syllabus says the log is used "throughout the research process", so it cannot show planning that guided the research [1]. Judgement: weak, because the log would record a reconstruction rather than the process, and it could not have been used in teacher discussions to monitor progress [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Indicative content:
- Entries are dated, which shows the timeline of the project [1].
- Records are thin: the articles are not identified or evaluated [1].
- The teacher meeting is recorded without the feedback or any decision [1].
- The survey entry records a result, but does not reflect that 30 students from one year group cannot show whether schools should teach the subject [1].
- There are no plans for next steps and no record of whether the question changed [1].
- Judgement: the log records some activity but shows almost no planning or reflection on findings and decisions, so it meets the assessment points only weakly [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Labelling evaluation as "record" because it mentions a source.
- Source entries that summarise without judging credibility.
- Feedback entries that note the teacher's comment but not the response.
- Decision entries without the reason for the change.
- Judging a log by its neatness rather than by record, plan and reflect.
