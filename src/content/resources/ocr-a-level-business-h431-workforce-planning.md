## What workforce planning is

**Workforce planning** is the process of forecasting how many people, with which skills, the business will need in the future, comparing this with the people it will have, and deciding how to close any gap. The aim is the right people, in the right place, at the right time, at an acceptable cost.

It has four steps.

1. **Forecast demand for labour.** Use the business plan (expected sales or output), productivity, planned changes in technology or location, and the skills required.
2. **Forecast supply.** Start with current staff, then allow for leavers (labour turnover, retirement), promotions or transfers, and the expected supply from the labour market.
3. **Identify the gap.** A shortage means recruit, train or use flexible labour; a surplus means redeploy, stop recruiting, retrain or, as a last resort, make redundancies.
4. **Act and review.** Carry out the plan, monitor results and update as conditions change.

Forecasts are uncertain. Sales may differ from the forecast, staff leave unexpectedly and the labour market changes. A plan should be reviewed regularly and should include flexibility.

### Worked example 1: a workforce plan

Marsden Contact Centre (invented) expects 1,200,000 calls next year. One full-time handler can deal with 8,000 calls a year. It has 138 handlers. Labour turnover is expected to be 15%. Four handlers will be promoted to team leader posts elsewhere in the business.

**Demand:** 1,200,000 ÷ 8,000 = **150 handlers**.

**Supply:** Leavers = 15% × 138 = 20.7, so about **21**. Staff left = 138 − 21 = **117**.

**Gap:** 150 − 117 = **33** handlers short.

Four of the 33 posts can be filled by internal transfers from other departments, so **29** must be recruited externally. At £2,800 per external hire (advertising, selection, induction), the cost is 29 × £2,800 = **£81,200**.

Evaluation:

- If call volumes are 10% lower than forecast (1,080,000 calls), demand falls to 135 handlers and the gap to 18. The plan would then over-recruit by 15 staff; each costs £28,000 a year in wages, so the risk is 15 × £28,000 = £420,000 a year.
- If turnover is 25% instead of 15%, leavers would be 34.5, so supply falls to about 103 and the gap is 47. The plan must therefore include retention measures.
- A mix of permanent and flexible staff reduces the risk of over-recruiting.
- Training new recruits takes time, so the plan must allow for the lag between recruitment and full productivity.

**Recommendation.** Recruit 29 permanent handlers in stages, with half starting before the busy season, and use part-time or annualised-hours contracts for the remainder, so that numbers can be adjusted if volumes change.

## Impact and importance of workforce planning

Benefits:

- Right numbers and skills avoid shortages that hurt service and surpluses that waste wages.
- Recruitment and training can be planned and budgeted.
- It supports business strategy: growth needs staff, closure needs redeployment.
- It helps control labour costs.
- It reduces the need for sudden redundancies, which are costly and damage morale.
- Employees gain from clearer career paths and training.

Limits:

- It depends on forecasts, which may be wrong.
- It takes time and expertise, which small businesses may lack.
- Unexpected events (a sudden loss of a contract, a pandemic) may overturn the plan.
- Rigid plans may reduce a business's ability to adapt.

Stakeholders: employees (job security, training), owners (cost control), customers (service levels), and the community (jobs).

## Flexible working practices

Flexible working practices let a business and its staff vary when, where or how much work is done.

| Practice | Meaning | Benefit to business | Drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Part-time | Fewer hours than a full-time contract | Staff available at busy times; lower cost per post | More staff to manage and train |
| Flexi-time | Staff choose start and finish times within limits, working core hours | Better morale; fewer late arrivals | Hard to cover all times |
| Compressed hours | Full-time hours in fewer days (such as four 10-hour days) | Fewer commutes; staff like longer breaks | Tiredness; cover for off days |
| Home or remote working | Working away from the business premises | Lower office costs; wider choice of recruits | Weaker supervision and teamwork; isolation |
| Annualised hours | Total hours agreed for the year, spread to match demand | Hours match busy periods; less overtime | Complex to plan; long weeks at peak |
| Term-time working | Working during school terms only | Attracts parents | Cover needed in holidays |
| Structured time off in lieu (TOIL) | Extra hours worked are taken back as time off | Avoids overtime pay | Absence at awkward times; record keeping |
| Job sharing | Two people share one full-time job | Two sets of skills; cover for absence | Handover problems; cost of two sets of training |
| Zero hours contract | No guaranteed hours; work offered as needed | Lowest cost in quiet times; very flexible | Insecurity, high turnover, poor morale; weaker staff loyalty |

### Worked example 2: annualised hours against standard contracts

Dene Garden Centre (invented) needs 600 staff hours a week for 40 weeks and 960 hours a week for the 12 peak weeks. Total hours = 40 × 600 + 12 × 960 = 24,000 + 11,520 = **35,520** a year. The hourly wage is £14. Overtime is paid at 1.5 times (£21).

**Standard contracts.** Employ 16 full-time staff, each working 1,950 hours a year (37.5 hours × 52 weeks), giving 600 hours a week. Annual wage cost = 16 × 1,950 × £14 = **£436,800**. In the 12 peak weeks, 360 extra hours a week are covered by overtime: 360 × 12 × £21 = **£90,720**. Total = £436,800 + £90,720 = **£527,520**.

**Annualised hours.** Pay for the 35,520 hours actually needed: 35,520 × £14 = **£497,280**. This needs 35,520 ÷ 1,950 = about 18.2 full-time equivalents.

Saving = £527,520 − £497,280 = **£30,240** a year.

Evaluation:

- The saving of about 5.7% of the wage cost (30,240 ÷ 527,520) comes from avoiding overtime premiums, and from not paying for idle hours in the quiet weeks.
- Staff work long weeks in peak season but lighter weeks otherwise; this suits some people (students, parents) better than others.
- The business must keep accurate records and agree the system in the contracts.
- Absence at peak times is more costly.
- Staff may prefer overtime pay because it raises earnings; some may leave.

**Judgement.** Annualised hours suit Dene because demand is seasonal and predictable. It should consult staff, offer a fair spread of hours and review after a year.

## Evaluating flexible practices

Consider:

1. **Fit with demand.** Predictable seasonal demand suits annualised hours; unpredictable demand may suit zero hours or part-time.
2. **Type of work.** Remote working suits office or data tasks, not customer-facing shop work.
3. **Staff.** Parents, students and older workers may value flexibility; others want security.
4. **Cost.** Overtime, training, recruitment and administration.
5. **Service and quality.** Continuity of service and teamwork.
6. **Law and fairness.** Rights to request flexible working, equal treatment of part-time staff, and the effect of zero-hours contracts on staff security.
7. **Judgement.** Which practices, and for which roles, are best.

## Recommending

A strong recommendation for a workforce plan or flexible practices links to the case, uses numbers, states the risk, and shows what the plan depends on. For example: "Recruit 29 permanent handlers in two stages and offer flexi-time to retain staff, as this meets the forecast and reduces turnover, provided call volume is confirmed by the spring."

## Common mistakes

- Giving a definition of workforce planning with no steps (demand, supply, gap, action).
- Forgetting to allow for leavers when forecasting supply.
- Assuming flexibility is always good for staff. Zero hours contracts can mean insecurity.
- Confusing part-time with job sharing, or flexi-time with compressed hours.
- Producing a plan that ignores the risk that forecasts are wrong.

## Exam technique

When asked to recommend a plan, show demand, supply and the gap with figures, then give the action and its cost. When asked about flexible working, name the practice, explain it briefly, then give a benefit and a drawback to both business and employees. Finish with a judgement.
