Questions 6 to 9 use the data below for Cobbett Fabrics Ltd (£000).

| Item | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 6,000 | 6,600 |
| Cost of sales | 3,900 | 4,620 |
| Operating profit | 720 | 594 |
| Inventory | 650 | 990 |
| Receivables | 900 | 1,210 |
| Payables | 520 | 700 |
| Current assets | 1,900 | 2,300 |
| Current liabilities | 1,250 | 1,850 |
| Non-current assets | 3,800 | 4,400 |
| Non-current liabilities | 1,200 | 1,900 |

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** A business has current assets of £450,000 and current liabilities of £300,000. What is its current ratio? **[1]**

A: 0.67 : 1

B: 1.5 : 1

C: 2 : 1

D: 150 : 1

**2.** Which **one** of the following would raise a business's gearing, other things being equal? **[1]**

A: Repaying a long-term loan

B: Issuing new shares

C: Taking out a new long-term loan

D: Retaining more profit

**3.** A business has operating profit of £63,000 and capital employed of £420,000. Calculate its return on capital employed. **[2]**

**4.** A business has cost of sales of £720,000 and inventory of £90,000. Calculate its inventory turnover and state what it shows. **[3]**

**5.** Explain why a business might want to increase its payables days. **[3]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**6.** Calculate for Cobbett Fabrics:

**(a)** capital employed in Year 1 and in Year 2 **[2]**

**(b)** return on capital employed in Year 2, to one decimal place **[2]**

**(c)** the current ratio in Year 2, to two decimal places **[2]**

**7.** Calculate gearing for Year 1 and Year 2, to one decimal place. **[3]**

**8.** Calculate receivables days and inventory turnover for Year 2, rounding receivables days to the nearest day and inventory turnover to one decimal place. **[4]**

**9.** Using the ratios from questions 6 to 8 and the Year 1 ratios (ROCE 16.2%, current ratio 1.52 : 1, gearing 27.0%, receivables days 55, inventory turnover 6.0), evaluate Cobbett Fabrics' financial position. **[12]**

**10.** Explain **two** limitations of using ratio analysis to judge the performance of a business. **[4]**

**11.** Analyse why a business might have a high gearing ratio but still be financially secure. **[6]**

**12.** A retailer's current ratio is 0.8 : 1, while a manufacturer's is 1.9 : 1. Assess whether the retailer is in a weaker financial position. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. 450,000 ÷ 300,000 = 1.5. **[1]**

**2.** **C** [1]. A new long-term loan raises non-current liabilities relative to capital employed; the other options reduce gearing. **[1]**

**3.** ROCE = operating profit ÷ capital employed × 100 [1] = 63,000 ÷ 420,000 × 100 = **15%** [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Inventory turnover = cost of sales ÷ inventory [1] = 720,000 ÷ 90,000 = **8 times** [1]. It shows that stock is sold and replaced eight times a year [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Paying suppliers later keeps cash in the business for longer [1], which helps cash flow and reduces the need for an overdraft or loan [1]. The business can use the cash to pay wages or invest, though if payment is delayed too far suppliers may withdraw credit or raise prices [1]. **[3]**

**6. (a)** Year 1: 3,800 + 1,900 − 1,250 = **£4,450k** [1]; Year 2: 4,400 + 2,300 − 1,850 = **£4,850k** [1].

**(b)** 594 ÷ 4,850 × 100 [1] = **12.2%** [1].

**(c)** 2,300 ÷ 1,850 [1] = **1.24 : 1** [1]. **[6]**

**7.** Year 1: 1,200 ÷ 4,450 × 100 = **27.0%** [1]. Year 2: 1,900 ÷ 4,850 × 100 [1] = **39.2%** [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Receivables days: 1,210 ÷ 6,600 × 365 [1] = **67 days** [1]. Inventory turnover: 4,620 ÷ 990 [1] = **4.7 times** [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Profitability: ROCE fell from 16.2% to 12.2% [1] because operating profit fell 17.5% while capital employed rose 9% [1]; the extra capital has not brought extra profit. Liquidity: the current ratio fell from 1.52 to 1.24 [1], so there is less cover for short-term debts. Gearing: it rose from 27.0% to 39.2% [1], showing the expansion was funded by borrowing, which increases interest costs and risk when profit is falling [1]. Efficiency: customers take 12 days longer to pay [1] and inventory turns less often, 4.7 against 6.0 times [1], so cash is tied up in stock and debts. Overall judgement: the position is weaker on every measure [1], and the trend, not any single ratio, is the concern [1]. Limits: two years of data may not show whether this is a one-off investment year [1], and we need competitors' ratios to judge whether the business is out of line with the industry. Recommendation: tighten credit control, reduce stock, and check whether new capacity is bringing sales [1]. **[12]**

**10.** Limitation 1: ratios are based on past data [1], so they may not predict future performance if conditions change [1]. Limitation 2: they ignore non-financial factors such as staff morale, brand image or product quality [1], which can be crucial to success but do not appear in the accounts [1]. (Credit also: accounting policies differ; balance sheet shows one date only.) **[4]**

**11.** Gearing shows only the proportion of long-term funds that is borrowed [1], not whether the business can afford it. A business with steady, high profit can easily pay the interest [1], for example a business with long-term contracts and stable income [1]. If interest rates are fixed, the cost is predictable [1]. Borrowing may also be secured on valuable assets that reassure lenders [1]. However, high gearing leaves little room for error if sales fall or rates change [1]. **[6]**

**12.** For: a current ratio of 0.8 : 1 means current liabilities exceed current assets [1], so the retailer might struggle to pay its debts as they fall due [1]. If it cannot raise cash quickly it risks a cash crisis or relying on overdrafts [1]. Against: retailers sell largely for cash and have fast inventory turnover [1], while their suppliers give credit, so low ratios are normal [1]. A manufacturer holds raw materials and gives credit to customers, so it needs a higher ratio [1]. Other factors: the retailer's cash flow forecast and available borrowing, its trend over time, and rivals' ratios [1]. Judgement: not necessarily weaker; the figure alone is not enough [1], and the answer depends on whether 0.8 is typical for the sector and stable over time [1]. **[9]**

*Examiner insight:* High-scoring evaluations pick out the trend across several ratios and say what else they would need to know.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the wrong figure in a formula, especially revenue and cost of sales.
- Leaving out units, or the "x : 1" form of the current ratio.
- Stating that a number changed without explaining what it means for the business.
- Treating every ratio change as good or bad regardless of context.
- Judging a business on one ratio.
