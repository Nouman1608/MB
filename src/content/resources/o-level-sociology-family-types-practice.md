These questions practise key question 4.1 of Cambridge O Level Sociology (2251), *What are the
different types of families?* They follow the six-part (a)–(f) structure the syllabus describes for
each Paper 2 question, with extra short questions at the end.

## Questions

**(a)** Define the terms:
- (i) *modified extended family* **[2]**
- (ii) *empty shell marriage* **[2]**

**(b)** Give **two** examples of alternatives to the family. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **three** strengths of the extended family. **[6]**

**(d)** Explain **three** ways family life may vary between social classes. **[6]**

**(e)** Discuss New Right views of family diversity. **[8]**

**(f)** Evaluate the view that the nuclear family is still the most common family type. **[14]**

### Extra practice

**(g)** Distinguish between monogamy and serial monogamy. **[4]**

**(h)** Explain **two** limitations of lone-parent families. **[4]**

**(i)** Explain **two** ways ethnicity may affect family structure. **[4]**

## Answers and mark guidance

**(a)(i) [2]** A family in which relatives live in separate households, often at a distance, but keep
in regular contact and give each other support (2). "Relatives living apart" alone: 1.

**(a)(ii) [2]** A marriage in which the couple stay together legally and outwardly, but love and
emotional closeness have gone; they may stay for children, money or reputation (2).

**(b) [2]** Any two: single-person households; shared households; friends as family.

**(c) [6]** Three strengths, 2 marks each:
- **Shared childcare** — grandparents and relatives help raise children while parents work.
- **Economic support** — pooled incomes and shared housing reduce costs.
- **Care for the elderly and sick** within the family.
- **Passing on traditions** — language, religion and customs across generations.
- **Emotional support** from a wider network.

**(d) [6]** Three ways, 2 marks each:
- **Resources** — middle-class families can afford larger homes, private education and leisure.
- **Geographical mobility** — middle-class careers may require moving away from kin, producing more
  nuclear or modified extended families.
- **Kin support** — working-class families may rely more on local relatives for childcare and help.
- **Parenting styles and expectations** about education may differ.
- **Age of marriage and childbirth** may differ with length of education.

**(e) [8]** Levels: 7–8 clear, developed, applied; 4–6 some development; 1–3 basic.
- The New Right sees the married two-parent nuclear family as the ideal, providing stable
  socialisation and clear gender roles.
- Lone-parent families, especially those without fathers, are blamed for poor discipline and
  under-achievement; Charles Murray linked them to an underclass.
- Diversity and rising divorce are seen as signs of moral decline; welfare is said to encourage lone
  parenthood.
- Evaluation: critics argue the problems of lone-parent families are mainly caused by poverty, not
  family structure; the view ignores abuse within some nuclear families.

**(f) [14]** Levels: 11–14 balanced, developed, evaluated with conclusion; 7–10 developed but less
balanced; 4–6 limited; 1–3 basic.

*For the view:*
- Many households at any time contain a couple and their children.
- Chester (1985): most people experience a nuclear family at some point in their lives — the
  "neo-conventional" family.
- Nuclear-family ideals remain strong in media and religion.
- Urbanisation and geographical mobility favour smaller households.

*Against the view:*
- Rising numbers of lone-parent, reconstituted and single-person households in many societies.
- In much of South Asia and Africa, extended and joint families remain common.
- The Rapoports (1982) showed organisational, cultural and class diversity.
- Postmodernists (Stacey, 1990) argue there is no longer one dominant type.
- Beanpole families are increasing as life expectancy rises.

*Conclusion:* the nuclear family remains important, but whether it is "most common" depends on the
society and on whether we count households at one moment or families across a lifetime.

**(g) [4]** Monogamy is marriage to one person at a time (2); serial monogamy is having a sequence of
monogamous marriages, one after another, following divorce or death (2).

**(h) [4]** Two limitations, 2 marks each: higher risk of poverty because there is one income;
the parent may struggle to combine work and childcare; social stigma in some societies; children may
have less contact with one parent.

**(i) [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each: some ethnic groups maintain extended or joint families because of
cultural values about duty to elders; migrant families may rely on kin networks for support in a new
country; religious beliefs may shape marriage type and family size; some groups have higher rates of
lone parenthood for historical and economic reasons.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need sociologists' names for 4.1?** Not always, but references such as the Rapoports, Chester
or Stacey strengthen (e) and (f) answers.

**Can I write about polygamous families?** The syllabus says such family types may be taught where
appropriate but are not assessed; you may use them to support an answer where relevant.

**What earns the conclusion mark in (f)?** A judgement that answers the question directly and is
supported by the arguments you have made.
