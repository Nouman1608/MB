These revision notes cover the individual oral in IB Diploma Programme Language A: Language and
Literature: choosing a global issue, pairing a literary and a non-literary extract, structuring the
oral and preparing with your learner portfolio. They draw on the areas of exploration **Readers,
writers and texts** and **Intertextuality: connecting texts**, at both SL and HL. They follow the
current IB subject brief (first assessments for SL and HL 2021).

For full explanations and a worked example, read the
[study guide](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-language-literature-individual-oral-global-issues/). Then
try the [practice questions](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-language-literature-individual-oral-global-issues-practice/).

## The oral in one table

These facts come from the subject brief.

| Feature | What the brief says |
|---|---|
| Type | Internal assessment |
| Format | Prepared oral response |
| Texts | One literary work and one non-literary body of work, both studied in class |
| Focus | The way both have approached a common global issue |
| Weighting | 30% of the final grade at SL; 20% at HL |
| Levels | Taken at both SL and HL |

**Not in the brief -- confirm with your teacher:** the timing, the extract length, whether you may
use notes, any follow-up questions, and the assessment criteria.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning for the oral |
|---|---|
| Global issue | A problem or tension that matters widely, crosses borders and shows up in local life |
| Topic | A subject area ("technology", "family"). Too wide to argue on its own |
| Angle | The specific question or claim you trace through both texts |
| Literary work | A studied novel, play, poem collection or other literary text |
| Non-literary body of work | A group of related non-literary texts, e.g. several adverts from one campaign; your teacher confirms what counts |
| Extract | The short passage from each text that anchors your analysis |
| Whole work | Everything beyond the extract that you refer to briefly for support |
| Thesis | One sentence that answers how both texts approach the issue |
| Learner portfolio | Your own collection of notes, reflections and responses built during the course |

## Method in steps

### Choosing the issue

1. List the texts you have studied.
2. For each, note two or three global issues it touches (from your portfolio issue log).
3. Find issues that appear in **one** literary and **one** non-literary text.
4. Narrow each shared issue: topic → issue → angle.
5. Test the angle: can you quote at least three specific choices from each extract that bear on it?
6. Keep the angle that gives the sharpest **contrast in method**.

### Analysing an extract

1. State what the extract shows about the issue.
2. Pick two or three choices (word, image, sentence form, structure, layout, typography).
3. For each: quote → name the choice → explain the effect → tie it to the issue.
4. Add one precise link to the whole work or body of work.

### Building the oral

1. Introduction: issue as an angle, both texts, thesis, route.
2. Literary extract: focused points with quotations and effects.
3. Link to the whole literary work.
4. Non-literary extract: focused points.
5. Link to the whole body of work.
6. Comparison and conclusion that answers the thesis.

Weaving comparison through each section also works. Balance is what matters.

### Pairing checklist

A strong pair of extracts:

- shows the global issue clearly in a short passage;
- is dense with choices you can name and explain;
- connects to the rest of its work or body of work;
- differs in **method** from the other extract, so the comparison says something.

## Learner portfolio: four entries

| Entry | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| Issue log | Two or three global issues per studied text, with page or post references |
| Technique bank | Short quotations, the choice named, the effect in one line |
| Pairing trials | Two or three possible pairs, each with a draft thesis |
| Rehearsal reflections | After each spoken run-through: what ran long, what was vague, what to cut |

Start the issue log when you start each text, not when the oral is announced.

## Worked reminders

### Narrowing ladder

| Level | Example |
|---|---|
| Topic | Technology |
| Issue | Digital surveillance |
| Angle | How constant monitoring is presented as care rather than control |

The angle names a mechanism ("presented as care") that you can find in language: soft verbs,
reassuring second-person address, images of family.

### Thesis template

> "Both [literary work] and [body of work] present [angle], but where the first uses [method] to
> [effect], the second uses [method] to [effect]."

### Point template

> Claim about the issue → "quotation" → name the choice → effect on the reader → link back to the
> issue.

### Linking phrases

- Comparing: "Where the poem slows time down, the advert speeds it up."
- Moving to the whole work: "This pattern recurs later, when..."
- Signposting: "My second point about the campaign..."

## Must-know distinctions

| This | Not this |
|---|---|
| Global issue as an arguable angle | A broad topic label |
| Analysing how texts present the issue | Giving a speech about the issue |
| Literary work **and** non-literary body of work | Two literary texts, or two non-literary texts |
| Extract as the anchor | Summarising the whole plot |
| Effect of a choice | Naming a technique only |
| Balanced time on both texts | One text treated in full, the other rushed |
| Non-literary form (layout, image, typography) | Treating adverts as words only |
| Spoken signposts | A script read aloud |

## Technique bank

| Literary texts | Non-literary texts |
|---|---|
| Imagery, simile, metaphor | Imperatives and direct address ("you") |
| Narrative voice and point of view | Headline and slogan structure |
| Pace, sentence length, repetition | Statistics and numbers as persuasion |
| Symbol and motif | Image, colour, layout and white space |
| Dialogue and silence | Passive and agentless phrasing |
| Structure and turning points | Typography and font size |

The same choice can appear in both columns. Numbers, for example, can build persuasion in an advert
and build tension in a story. Shared features give you the strongest comparisons.

## Quick self-test

1. What percentage of the final grade is the individual oral worth at SL?
2. What percentage is it worth at HL?
3. Is the individual oral internally or externally assessed?
4. Which two kinds of text must the oral connect?
5. Is "family" a topic or a global issue?
6. Narrow "the environment" into an angle suitable for an oral.
7. Original text written for Marlbridge: "Your data keeps your family safe." Name one language
   choice and its effect in relation to surveillance.
8. Original text written for Marlbridge: "The clock in the ward ticked louder than the nurses
   spoke." Name the technique and its effect.
9. Give one reason to link an extract to the whole work.
10. What is the difference between block comparison and woven comparison?
11. Name two entries you could keep in your learner portfolio to prepare for the oral.

### Answers

1. **30%.**
2. **20%.**
3. **Internally assessed.**
4. **One literary work and one non-literary body of work**, both studied.
5. **A topic.** It needs a problem or tension to become an issue, e.g. how caring for relatives
   falls unequally on daughters.
6. Any angle that names a mechanism, e.g. **how products are marketed as "green" to reduce buyers'
   guilt about waste.**
7. **Second-person address ("Your")** makes the reader personally responsible; **"keeps ... safe"**
   presents monitoring as protection, so control is framed as care. (Either choice with its effect.)
8. **Auditory imagery built on a comparison** ("ticked louder than the nurses spoke"): the clock
   dominates the human voices, suggesting neglect and a system that runs on time rather than care.
9. It shows the extract is **typical of the whole text**, not an isolated moment, so your argument
   about the issue holds for the work as a whole.
10. **Block** treats one text fully, then the other, and compares at the end. **Woven** compares the
    two texts point by point throughout.
11. Any two of: **issue log, technique bank, pairing trials with draft theses, rehearsal
    reflections.**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Choosing a topic ("work", "identity") instead of an arguable angle, so the oral drifts into
  description.
- Talking about the real-world issue with facts and opinions that are not tied to any choice in the
  texts.
- Summarising the plot of the literary work instead of analysing the extract closely.
- Naming techniques ("there is a metaphor") without explaining the effect on the reader.
- Treating the non-literary texts as words only and ignoring layout, image and typography.
- Giving far more time to one text than the other.
- Linking to the whole work with vague claims ("this happens a lot") instead of one named moment.
- Saving all comparison for the last few sentences, so the two texts never really meet.
- Reading from a full script, which makes the delivery flat and hard to follow.
- Leaving the choice of pairing to the last minute, with no portfolio evidence to test it against.
