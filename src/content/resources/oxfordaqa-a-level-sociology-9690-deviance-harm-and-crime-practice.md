## Questions

**1.** Define what is meant by **green crime**. **[2]**

**2.** Outline **one** example of an action that causes social harm but is not usually treated as a
crime. **[2]**

**3.** Identify and briefly explain **two** ways in which definitions of crime and deviance differ
between societies or over time. **[4]**

**4.** Identify and briefly explain **two** reasons why corporate crime may be under-represented in
official statistics. **[4]**

**5.** Explain **two** strengths of self-report studies as a way of researching crime. **[8]**

**6.** Using examples from a society you know, evaluate the view that official statistics give an
accurate picture of patterns of offending by gender, social class, age and ethnicity. **[20]**

### Extra practice

**7.** Distinguish between white-collar crime and state crime. **[4]**

**8.** Explain **one** reason why some crimes are described as "victimless" and **one** criticism of
this label. **[4]**

**9.** Identify and briefly explain **two** problems sociologists face in researching cybercrime.
**[4]**

## Mark schemes

**1. [2]** Crime, and wider harm, against the natural environment and other species — for example
illegal dumping of toxic waste, illegal logging or wildlife trafficking (2). Partial: "crime against
nature" (1).

**2. [2]** Any clearly explained example (2): deaths from unsafe but legal working conditions;
pollution within legal limits that damages health; marketing harmful but legal products; poverty
resulting from government policy. Credit reference to the social harm approach (Hillyard and Tombs,
2004).

**3. [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each: alcohol or gambling legal in some societies and illegal in others;
behaviours criminalised over time, such as new online offences; behaviours decriminalised as
attitudes change; different norms about dress, sexuality or religion; moral panics change what is
seen as deviant at particular times.

**4. [4]** Two reasons, 2 marks each: victims often do not know they have been harmed (e.g.
price-fixing); complex offences are hard to detect and investigate; companies have resources to
avoid prosecution; offences are often handled by regulators rather than police; corporate harm may
be treated as an accident rather than a crime.

**5. [8]** Two strengths, 4 marks each (identify, explain, develop, link to evidence):
- **Reveal the dark figure** — they uncover offences never reported or recorded, giving a fuller
  picture of how much crime there is.
- **Information about offenders** — they show offending among groups rarely arrested, testing whether
  official statistics over-represent some groups (for example by class).
- **Anonymity** can encourage honesty about minor offending.
- **Large samples and standardised questions** allow comparison between groups and over time.

**6. [20]** Levels: 17–20 detailed, analytical, a range of evidence and examples from a named
society, sustained evaluation, justified conclusion; 13–16 good knowledge, some evaluation; 9–12
reasonable knowledge, limited evaluation; 5–8 basic; 1–4 fragments.

*For the view:*
- Official statistics are large-scale and regular, showing consistent patterns — more male than
  female offending, a peak in young adulthood — that also appear in self-report studies.
- Serious crimes such as homicide are usually recorded.
- Statistics allow comparison over time and between areas.

*Against the view:*
- The dark figure: many crimes are never reported or recorded.
- **Class:** white-collar and corporate crime is under-recorded, so statistics exaggerate the share
  of working-class offenders.
- **Gender:** possible lenient treatment of women; domestic and sexual violence heavily
  under-reported, affecting victimisation patterns.
- **Age:** young people are more visible in public spaces and more often targeted by police.
- **Ethnicity:** over-representation may reflect discriminatory policing as well as offending.
- Interactionists: statistics are socially constructed by decisions of victims, police and courts;
  moral panics (Stanley Cohen, 1972) can increase recording of particular groups.
- Victim surveys and self-report studies give different pictures.

*Application:* reward specific examples from a named society — for example how statistics are
collected there, or a recorded pattern.

*Conclusion:* reward a supported judgement, for example that statistics show some genuine patterns
but distort class and ethnic patterns most.

**7. [4]** White-collar crime is committed by individuals in high-status jobs during their work, for
their own benefit (e.g. embezzlement) (2); state crime is committed by governments or state agencies,
often on a large scale (e.g. torture or genocide) (2).

**8. [4]** "Victimless" because there is no obvious individual victim who reports the offence, as
with some drug use or consensual gambling (2); criticism: harm may still fall on families, communities
or the offender's health, and such crimes can fund organised crime (2).

**9. [4]** Two problems, 2 marks each: offences cross national borders, making data hard to
collect; many victims (individuals and companies) do not report cyber offences; offenders are
anonymous and hard to identify; definitions and laws change quickly with technology.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which sociologists help with 3.1.2.5?** The specification suggests Stanley Cohen for section
3.1.2; Hillyard and Tombs are useful on social harm, and Sutherland on white-collar crime.

**Must every 20-mark answer include my own society?** Practise doing so; the specification requires
examples from students' own or a known society in selected extended responses.

**How do I show evaluation in question 6?** Compare official statistics with victim surveys and
self-report studies, and apply interactionist arguments about how statistics are constructed.
