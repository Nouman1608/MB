A **clone** is an organism (or cell) that is **genetically identical** to its parent. Identical twins are natural clones. Cloning is a form of **asexual reproduction**, because there is no fusion of gametes and so no mixing of genes.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Explant** | A small piece of tissue taken from a plant and used to start a culture |
| **In vitro** | "In glass": in a laboratory container, not in the whole organism |
| **Tissue culture** | Growing cells or tissues in a sterile nutrient medium |
| **Micropropagation** | Making many new plants from small pieces of a plant by tissue culture |
| **Enucleated** | Having had the nucleus removed |
| **Diploid** | Having two sets of chromosomes, as in body cells |
| **Transgenic** | Containing genetic material from a different species |

## 5.17B Micropropagation (tissue culture)

### The stages

1. **Choose the parent plant.** Select a plant with the desired characteristics, for example high yield, disease resistance or attractive flowers.
2. **Take explants.** Cut small pieces of tissue, often from the tip of a shoot, which contains actively dividing cells.
3. **Sterilise.** Treat the explants so that no bacteria or fungi are present. All equipment and containers are also sterile (**aseptic technique**), because microorganisms would grow rapidly on the rich medium and ruin the culture.
4. **Culture in vitro.** Place each explant on a sterile **nutrient medium**, such as agar jelly, containing sugars, minerals and plant growth substances (hormones).
5. **Growth.** The cells divide by mitosis and form a mass of cells. A change of growth substances makes the cells develop into small shoots and roots (**plantlets**). Often the shoots are divided again and the cycle repeated to increase numbers.
6. **Transfer.** The plantlets are moved to compost and gradually acclimatised to normal conditions, then grown on to full size.

All the plants are produced by mitosis, so they have the **same genes** as the parent.

### Worked example 1: multiplication

*One explant produces 4 shoots in 4 weeks. Each shoot is cut and cultured again, producing 4 new shoots in the next 4 weeks. How many plantlets are there after five cycles?*

```
number after n cycles = 4ⁿ
4⁵ = 4 × 4 × 4 × 4 × 4 = 1024
5 cycles × 4 weeks = 20 weeks
```

One explant could give 1024 plantlets in 20 weeks. That is how commercial growers make thousands of plants from one parent.

## 5.18B Why micropropagation is used commercially

### Advantages

- Produces **large numbers** of plants quickly from a very small amount of tissue.
- All plants are **genetically identical** to the parent, so the **desirable characteristics** (such as flower colour or yield) are kept. Seeds from sexual reproduction would show variation.
- Can be done **all year round**, in a small space, under controlled conditions.
- Can produce **disease-free** plants if tissue from a shoot tip is used.
- Can **save rare or endangered species** and reproduce plants that are difficult to grow from seed, such as some orchids.

### Disadvantages

- All the plants have the same genes, so a single new disease, pest or change in conditions could affect all of them. There is no genetic variation.
- Needs a sterile laboratory, trained staff and equipment, which is costly.
- If contamination occurs, a whole batch may be lost.

### Worked example 2: planning production

*A nursery wants 10 000 plantlets. Each shoot produces 6 new shoots per 4-week cycle. How many cycles are needed starting from one explant?*

```
after 1 cycle: 6
after 2 cycles: 6² = 36
after 3 cycles: 6³ = 216
after 4 cycles: 6⁴ = 1296
after 5 cycles: 6⁵ = 7776
after 6 cycles: 6⁶ = 46 656
```

After 5 cycles there are 7776, which is less than 10 000. After 6 cycles there are 46 656, which is more than enough. So **6 cycles**, which take 6 × 4 = **24 weeks**. The nursery could also start with more than one explant to shorten this.

## 5.19B Cloning mammals: Dolly the sheep

In animals, body cells cannot be grown into whole organisms as easily as plant cells, so a different method is used. It transfers a **diploid nucleus from a mature body cell** into an **egg cell that has had its own nucleus removed**.

### The stages (Dolly the sheep)

1. **Donor cell.** A body cell (from the udder) is taken from an adult sheep, the donor, of one breed (Finn Dorset). Its nucleus is **diploid**, containing the full set of genes.
2. **Egg cell.** An unfertilised egg cell is taken from a second sheep of another breed (Scottish Blackface). Its nucleus is removed (the egg is **enucleated**), so it has no genes of its own from the egg donor.
3. **Fusion.** The diploid nucleus from the donor cell is put into the enucleated egg cell. A mild electric shock is used to fuse them and to stimulate the cell to start dividing.
4. **Embryo.** The cell divides by mitosis to form a ball of cells, an early **embryo**.
5. **Implantation.** The embryo is placed in the uterus of a **surrogate mother** sheep (a Scottish Blackface), where it develops.
6. **Birth.** The lamb, Dolly, is born. She is genetically identical to the donor sheep, because her nucleus came from the donor cell. She looked like the Finn Dorset sheep, and not like the surrogate mother.

The key idea is that all the genetic information for Dolly came from the **nucleus** that was transferred. The egg cell donor and the surrogate mother contributed no nuclear genes.

### Worked example 3: who is the clone?

*Egg cells from sheep A have their nuclei removed. A nucleus from a body cell of sheep B is inserted. The embryo is placed in sheep C. Which sheep is the lamb genetically identical to, and why?*

The lamb is identical to **sheep B**, because its nucleus (and so its genes) came from sheep B's body cell. Sheep A supplied only the cytoplasm of the egg. Sheep C only provided the uterus and nutrients.

### Uses and concerns

- Uses: producing many copies of animals with desirable features (such as high milk yield), conservation of endangered species and research.
- Concerns: low success rate (many embryos fail), cloned animals may have health problems, loss of genetic variation and ethical objections. The specification asks you to describe the method, so know the stages in order.

## 5.20B Cloned transgenic animals and human proteins

Some medicines are proteins that humans make naturally. They can be made by **transgenic** animals that carry a **human gene**.

### The stages

1. The **human gene** for the protein is inserted into a **sheep cell** (or the cell of another mammal), using genetic engineering.
2. The gene is arranged so that it is active in the **mammary gland**, so the protein is made in the animal's **milk**.
3. The nucleus of the modified cell is put into an enucleated egg cell, as in the Dolly method, and the embryo is placed in a surrogate mother.
4. The offspring is a **transgenic clone**, and all its cells carry the human gene.
5. The animal makes the human protein in its milk. The protein is **extracted and purified**.
6. Cloning more animals from the same cells gives a **herd** of identical animals, each making the protein.

An example is a protein used to treat a blood-clotting disorder or an inherited lung disease. The cloned herd gives a reliable, large supply.

### Worked example 4

*Explain why cloning is used after the human gene has been added to a sheep cell.*

1. Adding the gene to an animal by genetic engineering is difficult and does not work every time.
2. When it works in one cell, that cell's nucleus can be cloned to produce many animals that all carry the gene.
3. So the farmer gets a whole flock making the protein, without needing to repeat the difficult genetic engineering step each time.

## Comparing the three methods

| Feature | Micropropagation | Mammal cloning (nuclear transfer) | Selective breeding |
|---|---|---|---|
| Organism | Plants | Mammals | Plants and animals |
| Parents | One | One donor of the nucleus (plus an egg cell and a surrogate) | Two |
| Offspring | Genetically identical | Genetically identical to the nucleus donor | Variation |
| Speed | Fast | Slow, low success rate | Many generations |

## Common mistakes

- Saying that Dolly's genes came from the egg cell or from the surrogate mother. They came from the donor nucleus.
- Forgetting that the egg cell's own nucleus is removed (enucleated).
- Saying that the nucleus was haploid. It is diploid, from a body cell.
- Writing that explants are placed in water. They are placed on a sterile nutrient medium.
- Leaving out aseptic technique in micropropagation.
- Stating that cloned plants show variation. They are genetically identical.
- Confusing cloning with genetic modification. A clone has the same genes as the parent, with no new gene added.

## Self-check

1. What is an explant?
2. Why is aseptic technique used?
3. Name the type of cell division that produces the plantlets.
4. Why was the egg cell's nucleus removed in the Dolly method?
5. Why is a cloned transgenic sheep useful?

**Answers:** (1) A small piece of tissue used to start a culture. (2) To prevent bacteria and fungi growing on the nutrient medium. (3) Mitosis. (4) So that all the genes of the lamb come from the donor nucleus. (5) It makes a human protein in its milk, and cloning gives many such animals.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is cloning examined on Paper 1?**
No. Every point in this sub-topic is bold with a B reference, so it is Paper 2 only.

**Is a clone always identical in every way?**
Genetically it is identical, but environment can still affect features such as size.

**What is the difference between micropropagation and taking cuttings?**
Both make genetically identical plants. Micropropagation uses tiny pieces of tissue grown in sterile laboratory conditions, so many more plants can be made.
