## Section B

**6.** Compound S, C₃H₈O, has a strong, broad absorption at 3350 cm⁻¹ and a strong absorption at
1050 cm⁻¹. There is no absorption between 1640 and 1750 cm⁻¹.

**(a)** Identify the functional group in S, giving the bonds responsible for both absorptions. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest two possible structures for S. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the infrared spectrum alone may not distinguish between your two structures. **[1]**

**7.** Compound T, C₄H₈O, has a strong absorption at 1720 cm⁻¹. There is no absorption between 3200 and
3600 cm⁻¹ and none between 2500 and 3000 cm⁻¹ apart from C–H.

**(a)** Identify the type of functional group in T. **[1]**

**(b)** Suggest two possible structures for T. **[2]**

**(c)** Describe a chemical test that would show whether T is an aldehyde or a ketone. **[2]**

**8.** Butan-2-ol is heated with acidified potassium dichromate(VI).

**(a)** Name the organic product. **[1]**

**(b)** Describe two changes between the IR spectrum of butan-2-ol and that of the product. **[2]**

**9.** The spectra of three compounds, propanoic acid, methyl ethanoate and propanal, are summarised.

- Spectrum X: strong absorptions at 1740 cm⁻¹ and 1240 cm⁻¹; no O–H absorption.
- Spectrum Y: strong absorption at 1715 cm⁻¹ and a very broad absorption from 2500 to 3000 cm⁻¹.
- Spectrum Z: strong absorption at 1730 cm⁻¹; no O–H absorption and no strong absorption in the
  1040–1300 cm⁻¹ range.

Match each spectrum to its compound. **[3]**

**10.** 1-bromobutane is converted into pentanenitrile, which is then hydrolysed to pentanoic acid.

**(a)** State the absorption that appears when 1-bromobutane is converted into pentanenitrile. **[1]**

**(b)** Describe the changes in the IR spectrum when pentanenitrile is hydrolysed to pentanoic acid. **[2]**

**11.** Propene is reacted with steam in the presence of H₃PO₄. Explain how IR spectroscopy would show that
an alcohol has been formed and that no propene remains. **[2]**

**12.** Compound U, C₃H₇NO, has absorptions at 3350 cm⁻¹ (medium) and 1665 cm⁻¹ (strong), and no broad
absorption between 2500 and 3000 cm⁻¹.

**(a)** Identify the bonds responsible for each absorption. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest the functional group present in U. **[1]**

**13.** Ethanal reacts with HCN, with a KCN catalyst, to form 2-hydroxypropanenitrile.

**(a)** Give two absorptions, with their ranges, that would appear in the spectrum of the product but not
in that of ethanal. **[2]**

**(b)** Give one absorption in the spectrum of ethanal that would be absent from the product. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** cm⁻¹ (reciprocal centimetres) [1].

**2.** C≡N (nitrile) [1].

**3.** Carboxyl group (carboxylic acid) [1].

**4.** The O–H groups are hydrogen bonded to each other, so a range of slightly different O–H stretching
frequencies is absorbed [1].

**5.** C=C (alkene) [1].

**6. (a)** Alcohol (hydroxy) [1]: O–H at 3350 cm⁻¹ and C–O at 1050 cm⁻¹ [1].

**(b)** Propan-1-ol, CH₃CH₂CH₂OH [1]; propan-2-ol, CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ [1].

**(c)** Both have the same functional group and the same bonds, so they absorb in the same ranges [1].

**7. (a)** Carbonyl group (aldehyde or ketone) [1].

**(b)** Any two of: butanal, CH₃CH₂CH₂CHO; butanone, CH₃COCH₂CH₃; 2-methylpropanal, (CH₃)₂CHCHO [2].

**(c)** Warm with Tollens' reagent (or Fehling's solution) [1]; an aldehyde gives a silver mirror (or a
red-brown precipitate with Fehling's), a ketone gives no change [1].

**8. (a)** Butanone [1].

**(b)** The broad O–H absorption at 3200–3600 cm⁻¹ disappears [1]; a strong C=O absorption appears at
1670–1740 cm⁻¹ [1].

**9.** X is methyl ethanoate (ester C=O and C–O, no O–H) [1]; Y is propanoic acid (C=O and very broad
carboxyl O–H) [1]; Z is propanal (C=O only) [1].

**10. (a)** A sharp C≡N absorption at 2200–2250 cm⁻¹ [1].

**(b)** The C≡N absorption at 2200–2250 cm⁻¹ disappears [1]; a C=O absorption at 1670–1740 cm⁻¹ and a very
broad O–H absorption at 2500–3000 cm⁻¹ appear [1].

**11.** A broad O–H absorption at 3200–3600 cm⁻¹ (with C–O at 1040–1300 cm⁻¹) shows an alcohol is present
[1]; the absence of a C=C absorption at 1500–1680 cm⁻¹ shows no propene remains [1].

**12. (a)** 3350 cm⁻¹: N–H [1]; 1665 cm⁻¹: C=O of an amide [1].

**(b)** Amide (for example propanamide, CH₃CH₂CONH₂) [1].

**13. (a)** Broad O–H at 3200–3600 cm⁻¹ [1]; C≡N at 2200–2250 cm⁻¹ [1].

**(b)** C=O at 1670–1740 cm⁻¹ [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Quoting a wavenumber without naming the bond, or naming a bond without the functional group.
- Assigning the 2850–2950 cm⁻¹ C–H peaks to O–H.
- Mixing up the alcohol and carboxylic acid O–H ranges.
- Calling a compound an ester from C=O alone, without showing that O–H is absent.
- Claiming IR distinguishes aldehydes from ketones, or positional isomers from each other.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I separate a carboxylic acid from an ester by IR?**
Both have C=O and C–O absorptions, but only the carboxylic acid also shows the very broad O–H absorption
at 2500–3000 cm⁻¹. An ester has no O–H absorption at all.

**What does the disappearance of an absorption tell me?**
It shows that the bond responsible has been lost in the reaction. For example, losing the 3200–3600 cm⁻¹
O–H band when an alcohol is oxidised to a ketone shows that no alcohol remains.
