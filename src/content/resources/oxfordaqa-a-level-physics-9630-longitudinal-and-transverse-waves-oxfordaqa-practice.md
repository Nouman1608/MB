These questions cover section **3.5.4 Longitudinal and transverse waves** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630) specification, including the "use of ultrasound in medicine" and "polarisation as evidence for the nature of transverse waves". Malus's law is not needed. Marks are shown for each part. All data are invented for practice; take the speed of electromagnetic waves in a vacuum (and in air) as 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.

Revise first with the [longitudinal and transverse waves study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-longitudinal-and-transverse-waves-oxfordaqa/) and the [longitudinal and transverse waves revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-longitudinal-and-transverse-waves-oxfordaqa-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Describe the difference between a longitudinal wave and a transverse wave. **[2]**

**2.** Which of these waves cannot be polarised?

A: microwaves

B: radio waves

C: sound waves

D: visible light **[1]**

**3.** A microwave oven uses electromagnetic waves of frequency 2.45 GHz. Calculate their wavelength in a vacuum. **[2]**

**4.** State two differences between sound waves in air and light waves, other than their speeds. **[2]**

**5.** At the centre of a compression in a sound wave, the displacement of the air particles is

A: a maximum in the direction of travel

B: a maximum opposite to the direction of travel

C: zero

D: equal to half the amplitude **[1]**

**6.** Explain why a gel is spread on the skin before an ultrasound scan. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** A sound wave travels in the +x direction through air at 330 m s⁻¹. On its displacement-distance graph, where positive displacement means displacement in the +x direction, the displacement is zero at x = 0, x = 0.825 m and x = 1.65 m. It is a positive maximum at x = 0.41 m and a negative maximum at x = 1.24 m.

**(a)** Calculate the frequency of the sound. **[2]**

**(b)** State which of the three zero-displacement points is the centre of a compression, and explain how you know. **[2]**

**(c)** State the distance between a compression and the nearest rarefaction. **[1]**

**8.** During an ultrasound scan, echoes from the front and back surfaces of an organ return to the probe 65 μs and 104 μs after a pulse is emitted. Take the speed of ultrasound in soft tissue as 1540 m s⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate the depth of the front surface below the probe. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the thickness of the organ. **[2]**

**(c)** The operator can choose a 3.5 MHz probe or a 10 MHz probe. Calculate the wavelength in tissue for each, and suggest which is better for this organ. **[3]**

**9.**

**(a)** Explain how the polarisation of light provides evidence that light is a transverse wave. **[3]**

**(b)** Unpolarised light passes through two Polaroid sheets. The second sheet is rotated slowly through 360° while the first is kept still. Describe what an observer sees through the second sheet. **[2]**

**10.** A television transmitter uses a vertical rod aerial.

**(a)** State how a receiving rod aerial should be aligned, and explain why. **[2]**

**(b)** Describe how the received signal changes as the receiving rod is tilted slowly from vertical to horizontal in a plane facing the transmitter. **[2]**

**11.**

**(a)** A radio station broadcasts at 100 MHz. Calculate the wavelength. **[1]**

**(b)** Lightning strikes 1.5 km from an observer with a radio. Light and the radio crackle from the flash arrive at almost the same instant, but the thunder arrives later. Taking the speed of sound as 340 m s⁻¹, calculate the time taken by the light and by the thunder, and explain the difference in terms of the nature of the waves. **[3]**

**12.** A microwave transmitter and a receiver face each other 0.40 m apart. Describe how you would use them to show that the microwaves are plane-polarised, and state the results you would expect. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** In a longitudinal wave the particles oscillate parallel to the direction of energy transfer [1]; in a transverse wave the oscillation is perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer [1].

**2.** **C** [1]. Sound is longitudinal.

**3.** λ = c/f = 3.00 × 10⁸/2.45 × 10⁹ [1] = **0.122 m** [1]

**4.** Sound is longitudinal but light is transverse [1]; sound needs a medium but light can travel through a vacuum [1]. Also accept: light can be polarised but sound cannot.

**5.** **C** [1]

**6.** The gel removes the air layer between the probe and the skin [1]; at an air boundary almost all the ultrasound would be reflected, so very little would enter the body [1].

**7.**

**(a)** λ = 1.65 m [1]; f = c/λ = 330/1.65 = **200 Hz** [1]

**(b)** **x = 0.825 m** [1]; particles just before it are displaced forwards and particles just after it are displaced backwards, so they crowd together there [1].

**(c)** Half a wavelength: **0.825 m** [1]

**8.**

**(a)** d = ct/2 = 1540 × 65 × 10⁻⁶/2 [1] = **0.050 m (5.0 cm)** [1]

**(b)** Back surface: 1540 × 104 × 10⁻⁶/2 = 0.080 m [1]; thickness = 0.080 − 0.050 = **0.030 m (3.0 cm)** [1]

**(c)** 3.5 MHz: λ = 1540/3.5 × 10⁶ = 0.44 mm; 10 MHz: λ = 0.15 mm [1]; the 10 MHz probe gives finer detail but is absorbed more strongly [1]; the organ lies 5 to 8 cm deep, so the **3.5 MHz** probe is the better choice because it penetrates further [1]. Accept 10 MHz only if justified by a need for detail with a statement that depth is a concern.

**9.**

**(a)** In a transverse wave, the oscillation is perpendicular to the direction of travel and can be in many directions [1]; a polariser transmits only the oscillation in one direction, so it can block or pass the wave depending on its orientation [1]; a longitudinal wave oscillates along its direction of travel, so a polariser would have no effect; since rotating a polariser does change the intensity of light, light must be transverse [1].

**(b)** The brightness rises and falls as the sheet turns [1]; there are two positions of maximum brightness and two of near-darkness, 90° apart from each other, in each full rotation [1].

**10.**

**(a)** The receiving rod should be **vertical** [1], because the transmitted waves are polarised with the electric field vertical and the rod responds to the component of the field along its length [1].

**(b)** The signal is strongest when the rod is vertical [1] and falls steadily as the rod tilts, becoming very weak when the rod is horizontal [1].

**11.**

**(a)** λ = 3.00 × 10⁸/100 × 10⁶ = **3.0 m** [1]

**(b)** Light: t = 1500/3.00 × 10⁸ = **5.0 × 10⁻⁶ s** [1]; thunder: t = 1500/340 = **4.4 s** [1]; light and radio are both electromagnetic waves, which share the same very high speed, while thunder is a longitudinal sound wave carried by the air particles at a far lower speed [1].

**12.** Point the transmitter at the receiver and note the signal on a meter [1]; rotate the receiver slowly about the line joining it to the transmitter, keeping the distance fixed [1]; the reading is a maximum when the receiver is aligned with the transmitter [1] and falls to nearly zero when it has turned through 90°, with two maxima and two minima per full turn, which shows the waves oscillate in one plane [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing transverse waves as "up and down" with no reference to the direction of energy transfer (Question 1).
- Putting a compression at a point of maximum displacement (Questions 5 and 7).
- Forgetting to halve the echo time (Question 8).
- Choosing an ultrasound frequency without mentioning both detail and depth (Question 8).
- Stating only that "transverse waves can be polarised" without explaining why longitudinal waves cannot (Question 9).
- Rotating the aerial about its own axis instead of tilting it (Question 10).
- Converting GHz or MHz incorrectly (Questions 3 and 11).
