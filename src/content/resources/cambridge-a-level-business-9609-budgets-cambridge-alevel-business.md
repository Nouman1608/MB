## What a budget is, and what it is not

A **budget** is a financial plan for a future period, agreed in advance, setting out expected revenue and permitted spending. It differs from a **forecast**, which is a prediction of what is likely to happen. A forecast says "we expect sales of $500 000"; a budget turns that into targets and spending limits that someone is responsible for meeting.

Each budget usually has a **budget holder**: the manager accountable for keeping to it. That link between a figure and a person is what makes budgets useful for control.

## How a budget is built

Most businesses start with the **sales budget**, because almost every other figure depends on the expected level of sales.

1. **Sales budget**: expected units and revenue, based on market research and past trends.
2. **Production or purchasing budget**: how much must be made or bought to meet sales.
3. **Cost budgets**: materials, labour and overheads for that level of output.
4. **Department budgets**: marketing, HR and administration spending limits.
5. **Overall budget**: combines these into planned profit, linked to the business's objectives.

**Worked example.** Wrenfield Juices expects to sell 50 000 bottles next quarter at $2.40. Each bottle needs $0.90 of ingredients and packaging and $0.30 of production labour. Overheads are budgeted at $28 000.

- Sales budget = 50 000 × 2.40 = $120 000.
- Materials budget = 50 000 × 0.90 = $45 000.
- Labour budget = 50 000 × 0.30 = $15 000.
- Budgeted profit = 120 000 − 45 000 − 15 000 − 28 000 = **$32 000**.

If the sales forecast is wrong, every other figure is wrong too. That is a key limitation.

## The four uses of budgets, applied

| Use | What it means | Example at Wrenfield |
|---|---|---|
| Measuring performance | Actual results compared with the plan | Did the production manager keep labour within $15 000? |
| Allocating resources | Deciding how much each area can spend | Marketing receives $6 000 of the overheads for a launch |
| Controlling | Limiting spending to agreed amounts | Purchases above budget need approval |
| Monitoring | Checking progress during the period | Monthly reports show whether sales are on track |

## Benefits and drawbacks, with depth

**Benefits**

- **Direction and coordination**: departments work to the same plan, so production makes what marketing expects to sell.
- **Control**: overspending is spotted early.
- **Motivation**: if managers help set their own budgets, they are more likely to be committed to them.
- **Accountability**: each figure has a named holder.

**Drawbacks**

- **Time and cost** to prepare.
- **Inaccuracy** if forecasts are wrong, especially in fast-changing markets.
- **Rigidity**: a manager may turn down a good opportunity because it was not in the budget.
- **Budget slack**: managers may ask for more than they need, or set easy sales targets, so that they can meet them comfortably.
- **"Spend it or lose it"**: departments may spend money at the end of the year to avoid a cut next year.
- **Demotivation** if budgets are imposed and unrealistic.

Several of these drawbacks depend on how the budget is set, which leads to the choice of method.

## Choosing a budgeting method

| Method | How it works | Suits | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Incremental | Last period's budget plus or minus a percentage | Stable organisations where activities change little | Carries forward past waste; encourages "spend it or lose it" |
| Flexible | Variable cost budgets adjusted to the actual level of output | Businesses where output varies, such as manufacturers or seasonal firms | More complex; needs costs split into fixed and variable |
| Zero budgeting | Every item starts at zero and must be justified | Organisations reviewing spending or cutting costs; new projects | Time-consuming; managers may need training |

**Worked example: zero budgeting.** A college marketing department's incremental budget would be last year's $40 000 plus 3% = $41 200. Under zero budgeting, each item is justified from scratch:

| Item | Justified amount |
|---|---|
| Open days | $14 000 |
| Online advertising | $12 000 |
| Printed prospectus | $5 000 (reduced; most applicants now apply online) |
| School visits | $6 000 |
| **Total** | **$37 000** |

Zero budgeting saves $4 200 against the incremental budget by removing spending that was carried forward out of habit, but it took many hours of work to reach.

## Variances: the meaning

A **variance** is the difference between a budgeted figure and the actual figure.

- **Favourable (F)**: improves profit. Revenue above budget, or costs below budget.
- **Adverse (A)**: reduces profit. Revenue below budget, or costs above budget.

Always label every variance F or A, and remember that for costs, a *higher* actual figure is *adverse*.

## Interpreting variances: four cautions

### 1. Favourable is not always good

A favourable materials variance could come from cheaper, lower-quality ingredients, leading to complaints and lost sales later. A favourable labour variance could come from short staffing that lengthened waiting times.

### 2. Adverse is not always bad

An adverse materials variance might be caused by **selling more** than planned. Higher sales need more materials; profit may still rise.

### 3. Flex before judging

**Worked example.** Wrenfield actually sells 56 000 bottles.

| | Original budget (50 000) | Flexed budget (56 000) | Actual | Variance against flexed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $120 000 | $134 400 | $131 600 | $2 800 A |
| Materials | $45 000 | $50 400 | $49 000 | $1 400 F |
| Labour | $15 000 | $16 800 | $18 200 | $1 400 A |
| Overheads | $28 000 | $28 000 | $28 500 | $500 A |
| Profit | $32 000 | $39 200 | $35 900 | $3 300 A |

Against the original budget, profit is $3 900 *favourable* ($35 900 against $32 000). Against the flexed budget, it is $3 300 *adverse*. The flexed view tells the more useful story: volume was better than planned, but the price achieved was lower (revenue per bottle was $2.35, not $2.40) and labour was not controlled, perhaps because of overtime to meet the extra demand.

### 4. Look at the size, not just the direction

A $500 adverse overhead variance is under 2% of budget and may not need action. A variance that grows each month, or exceeds a set percentage, deserves investigation.

## From variance to action

| Variance | Possible causes | Possible action |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue adverse | Lower price, fewer sales, new competitor | Review pricing or promotion |
| Materials adverse | Supplier price rise, waste, more output | Negotiate, reduce waste, check volume |
| Labour adverse | Overtime, higher wage rates, inefficiency | Review rotas, training |
| Overheads adverse | Energy price rise, unexpected repairs | Investigate; may be outside the manager's control |

A manager should not be blamed for variances caused by factors outside their control, such as a national rise in energy prices.

## Common mistakes

- Calling a higher-than-budget cost "favourable".
- Judging cost variances without flexing for a change in output.
- Assuming every favourable variance is good news.
- Confusing a budget with a forecast.
- Recommending zero budgeting without mentioning the time it takes.

## Exam technique: command words

- **Calculate**: show budget, actual and variance, and label each F or A.
- **Explain**: give a likely cause of a variance using the case.
- **Analyse**: trace how a variance affects profit and what it suggests about performance.
- **Evaluate** or **Assess**: weigh the usefulness of budgets or a budgeting method for this organisation, considering its size and how stable its activities are.
- **Advise**: recommend a budgeting method or action on a variance, justified from the case.
