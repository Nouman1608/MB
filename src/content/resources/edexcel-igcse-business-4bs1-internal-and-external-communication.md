This guide covers section 2.1, Internal and external communication, in Topic 2 (People in business) of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Business (4BS1) specification, Issue 2, February 2026. The specification lists:

- 2.1.1 the importance of good communication and the problems of ineffective communication, including the different communication methods used, including information technology (IT), and the benefits and limitations of each method
- 2.1.2 barriers to communication: how they arise, the problems of ineffective communication and how they can be removed.

Paper 1 is set on a small business, where most communication is face to face. Paper 2 is set on a large one with more than 250 employees, often in several countries, where messages pass through more people and rely more on IT. The first overview, with recruitment and selection, is in the [communication, recruitment and selection guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-communication-recruitment-and-selection/), which has a full table of methods. Companion pages: the [internal and external communication revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-internal-and-external-communication-revision-notes/) and the [internal and external communication practice questions](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-internal-and-external-communication-practice/). Businesses and figures are invented; amounts are in dollars. The specification does not list particular methods or barriers, so those here are standard examples.

## 1. How communication works

Communication is successful when the receiver understands the message as the sender meant it. Five parts are involved:

1. **Sender**: the person with the message.
2. **Message**: the information, instruction or idea.
3. **Method**: how it is sent (meeting, letter, email, phone, video call, website).
4. **Receiver**: the person it is for.
5. **Feedback**: the receiver's reply, which shows whether the message was understood.

A barrier can appear at any of the five points, which is the easiest way to explain where a problem came from.

**Internal communication** is within the business: between managers and staff, between departments, between sites. **External communication** is with people outside: customers, suppliers, banks, the government and the public.

| Audience | Typical messages | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Staff (internal) | Instructions, targets, changes, safety rules | Work is done correctly; staff feel informed and valued |
| Other departments (internal) | Orders, stock levels, budgets | Departments coordinate, so customers get what they ordered |
| Customers (external) | Prices, offers, order updates, responses to complaints | Sales, satisfaction and loyalty |
| Suppliers (external) | Orders, delivery dates, specifications | The right materials arrive on time |
| Lenders and investors (external) | Financial results, plans | Finance on good terms |

## 2. Choosing the right method

No method is best for every message. Use these questions:

- **Urgency:** does the receiver need it now?
- **Detail:** is it complex, with figures or diagrams?
- **Record:** will anyone need proof later?
- **Two-way:** does the sender need questions and feedback?
- **Sensitivity:** is it personal or confidential?
- **Number of receivers:** one person or thousands?
- **Cost and access:** what equipment does the receiver have?

### Worked example 1: matching message to method

| Message | Best method | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Telling a worker his job is at risk | Face-to-face meeting, confirmed in writing | Sensitive, needs two-way discussion, then a record |
| New price list for 500 trade customers | Email with an attachment | Fast, cheap, detailed, gives a record |
| A fire exit is blocked today | Spoken announcement and notice | Urgent and must reach everyone at once |
| Weekly sales figures to four regional managers | Shared spreadsheet or intranet page | Detailed, updated often, available any time |
| Responding to a complaint posted publicly online | Public reply followed by private message | Shows other customers the business cares; details kept private |

## 3. The cost of ineffective communication

Poor communication is not just an annoyance; it costs money and customers. Linking the fault to a cost is what turns a point into analysis.

### Worked example 2: errors in orders

Westbrook Supplies handles 12,000 orders a year. Because sales staff pass orders to the warehouse by handwritten notes, 3% are sent out wrongly. Each wrong order costs 40 to collect and resend.

- Wrong orders: 3% × 12,000 = 360.
- Cost: 360 × 40 = **14,400** a year.
- Ordering software costs 5,000 a year and is expected to cut errors to 0.5%: 60 errors, costing 2,400.
- Saving: 14,400 − 2,400 − 5,000 = **7,000** a year.

The figures leave out lost customers, who may not order again after a wrong delivery, so the real benefit is probably larger.

Other problems of ineffective communication:

- Staff do not know what is expected, so mistakes and wasted time increase.
- Rumours fill the gap when staff are not told about changes, and morale falls.
- Managers make poor decisions because information from the shop floor is late or wrong.
- Suppliers deliver the wrong quantity, so production stops or stock piles up.

## 4. Barriers: how they arise

Group barriers by where they arise in the process.

| Where it arises | Barrier | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Sender | Jargon, unclear or incomplete message | An engineer's technical email to sales staff |
| Method | Wrong method; technology failure | A complex change explained in a short text message; email server down |
| Receiver | Information overload; not listening; different first language | A manager with 300 unread emails misses an urgent one |
| Organisation | Too many levels; staff in different places or time zones | A message from head office passes through four managers before reaching shop staff |
| Attitude | Distrust between staff and managers | Staff ignore a notice because they do not believe it |

### Worked example 3: a message passed down

A head office instruction passes through four levels before reaching shop staff. If each level loses or changes 10% of the detail, the share left is 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.9 = **0.66**, about two-thirds. Sending the message directly to every shop by email or video briefing keeps all of it.

This is why barriers tend to be greater in a large business, with more levels and more sites, than in a small one where the owner speaks to everyone daily.

## 5. Removing barriers

| Barrier | How it can be removed |
|---|---|
| Jargon | Plain language; explain terms; know the audience |
| Wrong method | Choose the method using the questions in section 2 |
| Technology failure | Back-up methods; reliable systems; training |
| Overload | Short messages; clear subject lines; one action per message |
| Too many levels | Direct messages from the source; fewer levels |
| Language | Translations, simple wording, pictures or symbols |
| Distrust | Honest, regular updates; two-way meetings where staff can ask questions |

Feedback is the check that works for all of them: ask the receiver to confirm what they will do.

## 6. Annotated model answers

**Explain one benefit of using email for external communication.**
*Email is fast and cheap (statement). A business can send the same message, such as a price change, to hundreds of customers at once (expansion 1), and it keeps a written record of what was sent and when, which helps if there is a dispute later (expansion 2).*

**Analyse how ineffective communication could affect a large retailer with 300 stores** (Paper 2 style).
*If head office sends price changes by email to store managers who are overloaded, some stores may miss them. Those stores charge the old prices, so customers find different prices in different shops and complain. The retailer loses revenue where prices are too low and goodwill where they are too high, and staff time is wasted dealing with complaints.*

## Common mistakes

- Listing methods without a benefit and a limitation for each.
- Describing a barrier without saying how it arises or how to remove it.
- Ignoring the receiver: a method is only effective if they have access and understand it.
- Writing "communication is important" with no consequence for costs, sales or morale.
- Treating external communication as advertising only; suppliers, lenders and the government are external too.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between internal and external communication?**
Internal communication happens within the business, for example between managers and staff. External communication is with people outside it, such as customers, suppliers and lenders.

**Why are barriers usually greater in a large business?**
Messages pass through more levels and sites, staff may work in different places or languages, and managers receive more information, so detail is lost or missed.
