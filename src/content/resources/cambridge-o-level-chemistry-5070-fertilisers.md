Section 10.2 of the Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 syllabus (2026–2028) has two learning outcomes. You must be able to "state that ammonium salts and nitrates are used as fertilisers" and "describe the use of NPK fertilisers to provide the elements nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium for improved plant growth". 5070 has no Core/Extended split, so both outcomes are examined for every candidate.

Two short outcomes do not mean short questions. Fertiliser questions are a favourite context for linking other parts of the syllabus: reading formulae (section 3.1), percentage composition by mass (section 3.3), making salts from ammonia and acids (sections 7.1 and 7.3), the Haber process (section 6.3), tests for ammonium and nitrate ions (section 12.5) and water pollution (section 10.1). The broader [Chemistry of the Environment guide](/resources/chemistry-of-the-environment/) gives the basic facts in one paragraph; this guide works through the linked skills with worked examples.

## Why plants need fertilisers

Plants take in the elements they need as ions dissolved in soil water, through their roots. Three elements are needed in large amounts: **nitrogen (N)**, **phosphorus (P)** and **potassium (K)**. When crops are harvested, these elements are carried away with the plants instead of returning to the soil, so the soil gradually runs short of them. Fertilisers replace them, giving **improved plant growth** and higher crop yields.

The syllabus does not ask for the separate job of each element inside the plant. It asks you to describe that NPK fertilisers supply all three elements.

## Ammonium salts and nitrates

Most nitrogen fertilisers are **ammonium salts** (containing the NH₄⁺ ion) or **nitrates** (containing the NO₃⁻ ion), or both.

| Fertiliser | Formula | Elements supplied |
|---|---|---|
| Ammonium nitrate | NH₄NO₃ | N (from both ions) |
| Ammonium sulfate | (NH₄)₂SO₄ | N |
| Ammonium phosphate | (NH₄)₃PO₄ | N and P |
| Potassium nitrate | KNO₃ | K and N |

Two properties make these compounds suitable.

1. **They are soluble in water.** The solubility rules in section 7.3 state that all ammonium salts and all nitrates are soluble. Dissolved ions can be absorbed through roots.
2. **They contain nitrogen in a form plants can use.** Plants cannot use nitrogen gas, N₂, directly from the air, even though air is about 78% nitrogen. Converting N₂ into ammonia in the Haber process, and then into ammonium salts and nitrates, turns it into a usable form.

The same solubility has a downside. Rain dissolves excess fertiliser and washes it off fields into rivers and lakes, which is why section 10.1 lists "nitrates from fertilisers" and "phosphates from fertilisers and detergents" among the substances that lead to deoxygenation of water and damage to aquatic life.

## NPK fertilisers

An **NPK fertiliser** is a mixture of compounds chosen so that, together, they supply nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium. A typical mixture might combine ammonium nitrate (for N), ammonium phosphate (for N and P) and a potassium salt such as potassium chloride or potassium sulfate (for K).

To decide which elements a compound supplies, read its formula and look for N, P and K.

**Worked example 1.** A farmer has three compounds: ammonium sulfate, (NH₄)₂SO₄; potassium nitrate, KNO₃; and ammonium phosphate, (NH₄)₃PO₄. Which **two** should be mixed to make an NPK fertiliser? Explain your choice.

*Answer.* Potassium nitrate and ammonium phosphate [1]. Potassium nitrate supplies K and N; ammonium phosphate supplies N and P; together they provide all three elements [1]. Ammonium sulfate supplies only N, so it cannot replace either compound.

A common error is to think "sulfate" or "phosphate" contains potassium because the name ends in a familiar way. Always check the formula: the symbol for potassium is K, and phosphorus is P.

## Making ammonium salt fertilisers

Ammonia is a base. It is neutralised by acids to form ammonium salts:

```
ammonia + nitric acid    → ammonium nitrate
NH3     + HNO3           → NH4NO3

ammonia + sulfuric acid  → ammonium sulfate
2NH3    + H2SO4          → (NH4)2SO4
```

No water is formed in these equations, because ammonia accepts a proton (H⁺) from the acid to become the ammonium ion. In the laboratory a soluble salt like ammonium sulfate is made by titration, as described in section 7.3, and then crystallised.

The ammonia itself comes from the Haber process (section 6.3), using nitrogen from the air and hydrogen from methane. This is the chain that links the air to the farmer's field:

```
N2 (air) + H2 (methane) → NH3 → ammonium salts and nitrates → fertiliser
```

### Why ammonium fertilisers are not spread with lime

Section 7.1 says bases react with ammonium salts. Farmers add lime (calcium hydroxide) to soil that is too acidic. If lime and an ammonium fertiliser are added together, they react and **ammonia gas is given off**:

```
calcium hydroxide + ammonium sulfate → calcium sulfate + water + ammonia
Ca(OH)2 + (NH4)2SO4 → CaSO4 + 2H2O + 2NH3
```

The nitrogen escapes into the air as ammonia instead of staying in the soil, so the fertiliser is wasted. This reaction is also the basis of the laboratory test for the ammonium ion: warm with aqueous sodium hydroxide, and ammonia is given off, which turns damp red litmus paper blue.

## Percentage of nitrogen by mass

Section 3.3 asks you to calculate percentage composition by mass. Fertilisers are the classic context, because the percentage of nitrogen tells a farmer how much nitrogen each kilogram supplies.

```
% of element = (number of atoms of element × Ar) ÷ Mr × 100
```

**Worked example 2.** Calculate the percentage by mass of nitrogen in ammonium nitrate, NH₄NO₃, and in ammonium sulfate, (NH₄)₂SO₄. Use Ar values N = 14, H = 1, O = 16, S = 32. State which supplies more nitrogen per kilogram.

*Answer.*

- NH₄NO₃: Mr = 14 + (4 × 1) + 14 + (3 × 16) = 80. There are **2** nitrogen atoms. % N = (2 × 14) ÷ 80 × 100 = **35.0%**.
- (NH₄)₂SO₄: Mr = 2 × (14 + 4) + 32 + (4 × 16) = 132. There are **2** nitrogen atoms. % N = (2 × 14) ÷ 132 × 100 = **21.2%** (to 3 significant figures).

Ammonium nitrate supplies more nitrogen per kilogram [1].

The most common error is counting only one nitrogen atom in ammonium nitrate. There is one N in the NH₄⁺ ion and one in the NO₃⁻ ion.

**Worked example 3.** A field needs 14 kg of nitrogen. Calculate the mass of ammonium sulfate needed.

*Answer.* 132 kg of ammonium sulfate contains 28 kg of nitrogen. So 14 kg of nitrogen is in 132 × 14 ÷ 28 = **66 kg** of ammonium sulfate.

## Testing a fertiliser

Section 12.5 tests can identify the ions in a fertiliser sample dissolved in water:

- **Ammonium ion**: add aqueous sodium hydroxide and warm; ammonia is produced, turning damp red litmus blue.
- **Nitrate ion**: add aqueous sodium hydroxide and aluminium foil and warm; ammonia is produced, turning damp red litmus blue.
- **Potassium ion**: flame test, lilac flame.

If a sample gives ammonia with sodium hydroxide alone, it contains ammonium ions. If ammonia appears only after aluminium foil is added, the nitrogen is present as nitrate.

## Common mistakes

- Writing that plants absorb nitrogen gas from the air. They need it in soluble compounds such as nitrates and ammonium salts.
- Calling ammonium "ammonia" in a formula or name. Ammonia is NH₃, a gas; the ammonium ion is NH₄⁺.
- Counting only one N atom in NH₄NO₃, or forgetting the bracket in (NH₄)₂SO₄ when finding Mr.
- Confusing P (phosphorus) with K (potassium).
- Saying ammonium fertilisers react with acids to give ammonia. It is **bases**, such as lime, that release ammonia.
- Forgetting that the solubility of fertilisers is the reason they wash into rivers.

## Exam technique: the command words

- **State**: name the type of compound ("ammonium salts and nitrates") or the three elements.
- **Describe** (NPK): say the fertiliser is a mixture supplying nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium, and that this improves plant growth.
- **Calculate**: show Mr, the number of atoms of the element, and the final percentage; give units and sensible significant figures.
- **Explain** (lime and ammonium salts): name the reaction (a base with an ammonium salt), the product (ammonia gas) and the consequence (nitrogen lost from the soil).
- **Suggest**: apply the ideas to an unfamiliar compound, using its formula.
