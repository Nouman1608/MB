## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Which Act introduced irretrievable breakdown as the single ground for divorce in England and Wales?

A. Legal Aid and Advice Act 1949
B. Divorce Reform Act 1969
C. Matrimonial and Family Proceedings Act 1984
D. Divorce, Dissolution and Separation Act 2020

**[1]**

**2.** What is secularisation?

A. The growth of cohabitation instead of marriage
B. The declining influence of religion in society
C. The separation of a married couple without divorce
D. The spread of changes from higher to lower social classes

**[1]**

**3.** Identify **two** possible consequences of divorce for children. **[2]**

**4.** Describe **one** way in which divorce law in England and Wales changed in 2022. **[2]**

**5.** Study the table. The data are fictional.

> **Northmere (a fictional country): divorces, selected years**

| Year | Number of divorces (thousands) | Divorce rate (per 1,000 married people) |
|---|---|---|
| 1960 | 25 | 2.0 |
| 1975 | 70 | 5.5 |
| 1990 | 95 | 7.5 |
| 2005 | 80 | 7.0 |
| 2020 | 55 | 6.5 |

- **(a)** In which year shown was the number of divorces highest? **[1]**
- **(b)** Calculate the percentage increase in the number of divorces between 1960 and 1975. Show your working. **[2]**
- **(c)** Between 1990 and 2020 the number of divorces fell by much more than the divorce rate. Explain why this could happen. **[2]**

**6.** Identify and explain **one** way in which changes in social attitudes and values may have contributed to the rise in divorce. **[4]**

**7.** Explain **one** consequence of divorce for the extended family. **[3]**

**8.** Explain why a rise in divorces soon after a change in the law may not mean that marriages had become less happy. **[3]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**9.** Read Item A.

> **Item A (fictional).** A researcher wanted to understand how divorce affects children. With the head teacher's permission, she held group interviews with 24 pupils aged 8 to 11 at one primary school, all of whose parents had divorced in the past year. Several children said little in front of their classmates.

- **(a)** From Item A, examine **one** weakness of the research. **[2]**
- **(b)** Explain **one** advantage of using a longitudinal study to investigate the consequences of divorce for children. **[4]**

**10.** Explain **two** consequences of divorce for women. **[4]**

**11.** Discuss how far sociologists would agree that changes in the law are the main reason for the rise in divorce since 1945. **[12]**

**12.** Discuss how far sociologists would agree that divorce has mainly negative consequences for family members. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** B [1]. It came into force in 1971. **[1]**

**2.** B [1]. Secularisation is one of the four reasons for the rise in divorce named in the specification. **[1]**

**3.** Moving home or school [1]; less contact with the non-resident parent [1]. *Also credit:* living in a reconstituted family with step-parents or step-siblings; emotional distress; dividing time between two households; lower household income. **[2]**

**4.** The Divorce, Dissolution and Separation Act 2020 introduced no-fault divorce from April 2022 [1], so couples no longer had to blame each other or prove a long separation, and could apply jointly [1]. **[2]**

**5. (a)** 1990 [1].

**(b)** Increase = 70 − 25 = 45 thousand [1]; 45 ÷ 25 × 100 = 180% [1].

**(c)** The divorce rate is measured per 1,000 married people, so it takes account of how many people are married [1]. If fewer people marry, for example because more couples cohabit, the number of divorces can fall sharply (here from 95 to 55 thousand, about 42%) while the rate falls only a little (from 7.5 to 6.5, about 13%) [1]. **[5]**

**6.** Identify: reduced stigma attached to divorce [1]. Explain: in the 1950s divorced people, especially women, faced disapproval [1]; as divorce has become more common and accepted, including in the media, people are less afraid of how others will see them [1]. So a couple in an unhappy or empty shell marriage are more willing to end it rather than stay together for appearances [1]. *Also credit:* higher expectations of marriage as a source of personal happiness, so people are less willing to tolerate an unsatisfying relationship. **[4]**

**7.** Grandparents may lose regular contact with their grandchildren, especially on the side of the parent who no longer lives with the children [1]. This can weaken ties between generations [1], and some grandparents may turn to the courts or informal arrangements to keep in touch [1]. *Also credit:* grandparents may give more childcare and money to support a lone parent; kin networks become more complex with step-relatives. **[3]**

**8.** A new law may allow couples who were already separated, or in empty shell marriages, to divorce [1], so there is a backlog of divorces as these marriages are legally ended [1]. The rise therefore reflects the law making divorce possible, not a sudden increase in unhappy marriages [1]. **[3]**

**9. (a)** One weakness, examined. Group interviews with classmates present meant some children said little [1], so the data may not show how they really feel and lack validity; individual interviews might reveal more [1]. *Also credit:* one school and a sample of 24 pupils is small, so findings cannot be generalised. **[2]**

**(b)** A longitudinal study follows the same people over a long period [1]. This allows the researcher to see how children's lives change before, during and after the divorce [1], for example whether early distress fades over time [1], so it can separate short-term effects from long-term consequences, which a single interview cannot do [1]. **[6]**

**10.** First, women's income often falls after divorce, especially if their children live mainly with them [1], so they face a higher risk of poverty as lone parents, juggling childcare with paid work [1]. Second, divorce can bring relief and independence [1]: a woman leaving an unhappy or abusive marriage gains control over her own life and money, which feminists see as a positive outcome [1]. **[4]**

**11.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- The Legal Aid and Advice Act 1949 made divorce more affordable for people on lower incomes [1].
- The Divorce Reform Act 1969, in force from 1971, introduced irretrievable breakdown, followed by a sharp rise in divorces [1].
- The 1984 Act allowed divorce after one year of marriage [1].
- No-fault divorce from 2022 removed the need to blame a partner [1].
- Against: laws make divorce possible, but do not explain why people want to divorce [1].
- Laws often follow changes in attitudes rather than cause them [1].
- Changing social attitudes have reduced stigma [1].
- Higher expectations of marriage mean empty shell marriages are less tolerated, as functionalists argue [1].
- Secularisation means fewer people see marriage as a lifelong, sacred vow [1].
- Women's paid work and equality laws reduce financial dependence; most petitions are brought by wives [1].
- Feminists argue women's greater independence and rejection of patriarchal marriage is the key reason [1].
- Judgement: law changes explain the timing of rises, but social changes explain the underlying demand, so the law is a necessary rather than the main cause [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* Strong answers show how the reasons interact; for example, the 1969 Act reflected changing attitudes as well as changing them.

**12.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Divorce can cause financial hardship; two households cost more than one [1].
- Women's incomes often fall more, and lone parent families face a higher risk of poverty [1].
- Children may move home or school and lose regular contact with one parent [1].
- Functionalists worry that divorce may disrupt primary socialisation and stability [1].
- Grandparents may lose contact with grandchildren [1].
- Against: ending an abusive or high-conflict marriage can benefit both adults and children [1].
- Many sociologists argue conflict between parents harms children more than divorce itself [1].
- Feminists see divorce as a route for women to escape patriarchal marriages [1].
- Remarriage and reconstituted families can give children supportive step-relationships [1].
- Grandparents may become more involved, strengthening some kin ties [1].
- Marxists would argue the strain comes from capitalism's financial pressures, not divorce itself [1].
- Judgement: divorce has real costs, especially financial ones for women and children, but its consequences depend on the marriage it ends, so "mainly negative" is too simple [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* The specification names three groups (husband and wife, children, extended family); cover all three to show range.

## Where marks are usually lost

- In 5(b), dividing by the new figure instead of the original one.
- In 5(c), not explaining that the rate takes account of how many people are married.
- Describing the divorce trend as a steady rise since 1945.
- In Question 11, describing each reason without weighing the law against the others.
