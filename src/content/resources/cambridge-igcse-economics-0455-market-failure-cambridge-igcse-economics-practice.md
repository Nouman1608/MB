Learn the content first from the [market failure study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-market-failure-cambridge-igcse-economics/) and the [market failure revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-market-failure-cambridge-igcse-economics-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define external cost. **[2]**

**2.** Which of the following is a public good?

A: a seat on a commuter train

B: a lighthouse warning ships away from rocks

C: a place at a private school

D: a bottle of drinking water

**[1]**

**3.** State the formula for social cost. **[1]**

**4.** A paint factory has private costs of 54,000 a month and its social costs are 66,000 a month. Calculate the external cost. **[1]**

**5.** Identify **two** characteristics of a public good. **[2]**

**6.** Which of the following best explains why a merit good is under-consumed in a free market?

A: it is non-excludable

B: consumers underestimate the benefit they gain from it

C: it is always provided by the government

D: it creates external costs

**[1]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**7.** In Town K, 400 households would each benefit from a new storm drain that costs 32,000 to build. A private firm offers to build it if households pay voluntarily.

**(a)** Calculate the amount each household would pay if the cost were shared equally. **[1]**

**(b)** Only 90 households agree to pay this amount. Calculate the revenue raised and the shortfall. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why so few households agree to pay. **[3]**

**8.** A swimming course costs 150 per child. The table shows the invented private and external benefits for three groups of children.

| Group | Children | Private benefit each | External benefit each |
|---|---|---|---|
| X | 200 | 180 | 40 |
| Y | 300 | 120 | 40 |
| Z | 100 | 90 | 40 |

**(a)** Calculate how many children enrol if families consider only private benefit. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate how many children should enrol for the best outcome for society. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **one** consequence of this market outcome. **[2]**

**9.** Explain how factor immobility can cause market failure. **[4]**

**10.** A cement company is the only supplier in Country M. It cuts output by 20% and raises its price by 35%.

**(a)** Explain why this is described as an abuse of monopoly power. **[3]**

**(b)** Analyse how this abuse leads to a misallocation of resources. **[4]**

**11.** Analyse why a free market is likely to over-produce goods whose production creates external costs. **[5]**

**12.** Discuss whether health care is better described as a merit good or as a public good. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** A cost that falls on third parties [1]; who are not part of the decision and are not compensated by the person or firm causing it [1]. **[2]**

**2.** B [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* A, C and D are all excludable and rival. A lighthouse's warning reaches every ship, whether or not it paid.

**3.** Social cost = private cost + external cost [1]. **[1]**

**4.** 66,000 - 54,000 = **12,000** a month [1]. **[1]**

**5.** Non-excludable: non-payers cannot be stopped from benefiting [1]. Non-rival: one person's use does not reduce the amount available to others [1]. **[2]**

**6.** B [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Option A describes a public good and option D describes a reason for over-consumption.

**7. (a)** 32,000 / 400 = **80** per household [1].

**(b)** Revenue = 90 x 80 = **7,200** [1]; shortfall = 32,000 - 7,200 = **24,800** [1].

**(c)** The drain is non-excludable, so once built it protects households that did not pay [1]; each household has an incentive to free ride on others' payments [1]; so the firm cannot raise enough revenue and the drain may not be built, although society would benefit [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** Only group X has private benefit (180) above the price (150), so **200** enrol [1].

**(b)** Social benefits: X 220, Y 160, Z 130 [1]; X and Y are above 150, so **500** should enrol [1].

**(c)** Under-consumption of 300 places [1]; so too few resources are allocated to swimming lessons and third parties lose external benefits such as fewer drownings needing rescue services [1]. **[5]**

**9.** Workers may be occupationally immobile because they lack the skills for jobs that are available [1]; or geographically immobile because high housing costs or family ties stop them moving [1]. As a result some workers stay unemployed while vacancies elsewhere are unfilled [1]; so resources are idle and output is lower than the economy could produce, a misallocation of resources [1]. **[4]**

**10. (a)** As the only supplier, the firm faces no competition [1]; buyers cannot switch to rivals, so it can raise price [1]; it deliberately restricts output to raise price and profit at consumers' expense [1].

**(b)** Output falls by 20%, so fewer resources are used in cement production than consumers would want at a competitive price [1]. The higher price means some buyers, such as small builders, can no longer afford cement and building projects are cancelled [1]. Consumers pay more and have less choice, while the firm earns higher profit [1]. With no competitive pressure the firm may also become inefficient, wasting resources [1]. **[7]**

**11.** The producer bases its output decision on private costs only [1]; it does not pay for the external costs it causes, such as pollution damage to farms or health [1]. So the price charged is below the full social cost of production [1]. At this lower price consumers buy more than they would if the price reflected social cost [1]. Output and consumption are therefore greater than the level best for society, and too many resources are allocated to the good [1]. **[5]**

**12.** Merit good case: health care is excludable, because a clinic can refuse treatment to people who do not pay [1]; and rival, because a doctor's time with one patient cannot be used for another [1]. People may underestimate the benefit of check-ups and treatment, so they consume too little, which is the defining feature of a merit good [1].
Public good case: some health services, such as campaigns to control disease-carrying mosquitoes, benefit everyone in an area and cannot exclude non-payers [1].
Judgement: most health care is a merit good because it is excludable and rival [1]; only a few public-health activities have public good features, so "merit good" is the better general description [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Being provided free by the government does not make a good a public good. Use the two tests.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a cause of market failure but not stating whether consumption is too high or too low.
- Calling a good "public" because the government provides it.
- Treating the firm's own costs, such as wages and fuel, as external costs.
- In calculations with groups, using private benefit to decide what is best for society.
- Leaving out the third party when explaining an external cost or benefit.
- In a "discuss" question, giving two sides with no final judgement.
