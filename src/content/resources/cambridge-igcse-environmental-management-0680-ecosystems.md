Sub-topic 5.1 of Cambridge IGCSE Environmental Management (0680) is the foundation of Topic 5. It has nine syllabus points: ecosystem components, biotic and abiotic components, biotic interactions, pollination, photosynthesis, energy flow, ecological pyramids, aerobic respiration and the carbon cycle. The [Ecosystems, biodiversity and fieldwork study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-environmental-management-ecosystems-biodiversity-and-fieldwork-study-guide/) gives an overview of the whole topic; this page goes further into each point, with an emphasis on applying terms to unfamiliar ecosystems and interpreting pyramids.

## Components of an ecosystem (5.1.1)

The syllabus lists eight terms. A useful way to fix them is to apply them to one example, here a savannah grassland.

| Term | Meaning | Savannah example |
|---|---|---|
| Habitat | The place where an organism lives | Open grassland with scattered trees |
| Species | A group of organisms that can breed together to produce fertile offspring | Zebras |
| Population | All the organisms of one species living in an area at the same time | All the zebras in one reserve |
| Community | All the populations of different species living in an area | Grasses, trees, zebras, lions, termites, birds |
| Niche | The role of a species in its ecosystem, including what it eats and where and when it feeds | Zebra: daytime grazer of tall grasses |
| Prey | An animal that is hunted and eaten | Zebra |
| Predator | An animal that hunts and eats other animals | Lion, hyena |
| Apex predator | A predator at the top of a food chain with no natural predators of its own | Lion |

Exam questions often give a short description of an ecosystem you have not studied and ask you to identify a population or a niche from it. Practise by applying the table to a pond, a coral reef or a rainforest.

## Biotic and abiotic components (5.1.2)

- **Biotic (living)**: producers, primary consumers, secondary consumers, tertiary consumers and decomposers.
- **Abiotic (non-living)**: temperature, water, oxygen, carbon dioxide, salinity, light, pH.

Abiotic factors limit where species can live. Salinity controls which plants grow in an estuary; light controls how deep seaweeds grow; pH controls which invertebrates survive in a stream. When a question asks you to explain a distribution, link one abiotic factor to one biological need, such as light for photosynthesis.

## Biotic interactions (5.1.3)

- **Native and invasive species**: a native species occurs naturally in an area; an invasive species has been introduced, spreads rapidly and harms native species, often because it has no natural predators there.
- **Competition**: organisms struggle for the same limited resource, such as food, light, water, space or mates. It can be between members of the same species or between different species.
- **Predation**: one animal hunts and eats another. Predator and prey numbers are linked: more prey supports more predators, which then reduce prey, so predators later decline.
- **Pollination**: the interaction between plants and animal pollinators, described below.

## Pollination (5.1.4)

Pollination is **the transfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma**. Knowledge of the structure and naming of other flower parts is not required.

- **Insect pollination**: an insect pollinator such as a bee visits a flower for nectar, picks up pollen on its body and carries it to the stigma of another flower. These flowers tend to be brightly coloured and scented.
- **Wind pollination**: pollen is light and produced in large amounts, and is blown by the wind to a stigma. Grasses are common examples.

Pollination leads to **fertilisation, followed by seed and fruit formation**. This is why pollinator decline matters to food production: many crops need pollinators to produce fruit and seeds.

## Photosynthesis (5.1.5)

Photosynthesis is the **synthesis of glucose using carbon dioxide and water**, with **sunlight as the source of energy**. **Chlorophyll**, the green pigment in leaves, **captures light energy**.

Word equation: **carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen**

Producers make all the food energy available to an ecosystem, so anything that reduces photosynthesis, such as shading, drought or loss of leaves, reduces the energy available to every consumer.

## Aerobic respiration (5.1.8)

Aerobic respiration is the **breakdown of glucose in the presence of oxygen to release energy, with carbon dioxide and water as waste products**.

Word equation: **glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water**

It is the reverse of photosynthesis in terms of reactants and products. All living organisms respire, including plants, day and night; photosynthesis happens only in light.

## Energy flow (5.1.6)

Energy enters as sunlight, is captured by producers, and passes along food chains through trophic levels: producer, primary consumer, secondary consumer, tertiary consumer. The syllabus figure is that **10% of energy is passed between trophic levels**. The rest is lost during life processes: **movement, respiration, digestion and excretion**. Respiration releases energy that ends up as heat; undigested food leaves as faeces; excretion removes waste such as urine.

## Ecological pyramids (5.1.7)

### Pyramid of numbers

Each bar shows the number of organisms at a trophic level. Usually the bars get smaller upwards, but a pyramid of numbers can be **inverted** when a single large producer supports many small consumers. For example, one tree may feed thousands of caterpillars, which feed a few birds, so the producer bar is the narrowest at the bottom.

### Pyramid of energy

Each bar shows the energy at a trophic level, usually per square metre per year. Because only about 10% passes on each time, a pyramid of energy is **always pyramid-shaped**; it cannot be inverted. This makes it the more reliable way to compare trophic levels.

## The carbon cycle (5.1.9)

| Process | Carbon moves from | Carbon moves to |
|---|---|---|
| Photosynthesis | Carbon dioxide in air or water | Glucose in producers |
| Respiration | Glucose in all organisms | Carbon dioxide in air or water |
| Feeding | One organism | The organism that eats it |
| Decomposition | Dead organisms and waste | Carbon dioxide, via respiration of decomposers |
| Formation of fossil fuels | Dead organisms buried without decomposition over millions of years | Coal, oil, natural gas |
| Combustion | Fossil fuels and wood | Carbon dioxide in the air |

The cycle links Topic 5 to climate change in Topic 4: combustion and deforestation move carbon into the atmosphere faster than photosynthesis removes it.

## Worked example 1: energy through a food chain

A food chain in a pond: algae → water flea → small fish → heron. The algae capture 60,000 kJ per square metre per year.

1. Water fleas receive 60,000 × 10 / 100 = 6,000 kJ.
2. Small fish receive 6,000 × 10 / 100 = 600 kJ.
3. Herons receive 600 × 10 / 100 = 60 kJ.
4. Energy lost between algae and water fleas = 60,000 − 6,000 = 54,000 kJ, through respiration, movement, digestion and excretion.

## Worked example 2: calculating efficiency from data

The figures are invented for practice. In a grassland, primary consumers receive 3,200 kJ per square metre per year and secondary consumers receive 256 kJ.

Efficiency = 256 / 3,200 × 100 = 8%.

This is a little below the 10% guide figure. A sensible suggestion: the secondary consumers are active hunters that use a lot of energy in movement, or much of the prey (bones, fur) is not eaten or digested.

## Worked example 3: interpreting a pyramid of numbers

A woodland pyramid of numbers shows 1 oak tree, 2,000 caterpillars, 40 small birds and 2 hawks. Question: explain why the bottom bar is narrower than the one above it, and what a pyramid of energy would show.

Answer: the oak is a single large producer that provides enough food for thousands of small caterpillars, so the number of producers is smaller than the number of primary consumers. A pyramid of energy would be a true pyramid, with the most energy in the oak, because energy is lost at each step.

## Common mistakes

- Defining a population as "all the animals in an area". It is one species.
- Describing a niche as a habitat. The habitat is where; the niche is the role.
- Saying energy is recycled. Carbon is recycled; energy flows through and is lost as heat.
- Writing that plants respire only at night. They respire all the time.
- Saying pyramids of energy can be inverted.

## Exam technique

**Describe** questions on components want the term applied to the organisms in the question. **Describe and interpret** questions on food webs and pyramids reward reading values, calculating percentages and stating trends. **Explain** questions on pyramids want the reason, usually the 10% transfer and named energy losses. Always give units, such as kJ per square metre per year.
