## Animal studies

**Lorenz (1935)** divided a clutch of goose eggs: half hatched with their mother, half in an incubator where Lorenz was the first moving object they saw. The incubator goslings followed Lorenz and, when the groups were mixed, still chose him. This **imprinting** happened only in a brief **critical period** shortly after hatching. Lorenz also described **sexual imprinting**: birds later directed courtship at the kind of object they had imprinted on.

*Evaluation.* Generalising from birds to humans is risky, because mammals show more emotional attachment and more flexibility. Guiton (1966) found that chickens imprinted on yellow washing-up gloves tried to mate with them at first, but later learned to prefer other chickens, suggesting imprinting is less permanent than Lorenz claimed.

**Harlow (1958)** reared infant rhesus monkeys with two wire "mothers": one plain wire, one covered in soft cloth. Even when only the wire mother gave milk, the monkeys spent most time with the cloth mother and ran to it when frightened. **Contact comfort**, not food, drove attachment. Monkeys reared without real mothers became aggressive and less sociable as adults, bred less often and were poor mothers, some neglecting or harming their young. Harlow concluded there was a critical period: damage was hard to reverse unless a monkey had a mother figure within about 90 days.

*Evaluation.* Harlow's work changed practice by showing that comfort matters as well as feeding, for example in care for children in hospitals and institutions. However, monkeys differ from humans, and the severe and lasting harm raises ethical objections, although the work was legal at the time.

## Explanations of attachment

**Learning theory** (Dollard and Miller, 1950), often called "cupboard love":
- *Classical conditioning*: food (unconditioned stimulus) produces pleasure (unconditioned response). The caregiver, repeatedly present at feeding, becomes a conditioned stimulus that produces pleasure on its own.
- *Operant conditioning*: hunger is an uncomfortable drive. Feeding reduces it, so food is a primary reinforcer and the caregiver, associated with it, becomes a secondary reinforcer. The infant's crying is also reinforced when it brings care.

*Evaluation.* Harlow's monkeys attached to the cloth mother, not the food source, which contradicts the central role of feeding. Human research also finds that many infants' strongest attachment is not to the person who feeds them, but to the person most responsive to them.

**Bowlby's monotropic theory** (1969) argues attachment is innate and adaptive, because staying close to a caregiver kept infants safe.
- **Monotropy**: one attachment, usually to the mother, is more important than all others. The *law of continuity* says consistent care improves attachment quality; the *law of accumulated separation* says repeated separations add up.
- **Social releasers**: innate behaviours such as crying, cooing and smiling trigger caregiving.
- **Critical period**: about the first two and a half years; if attachment has not formed by then, it is much harder to form later.
- **Internal working model**: the primary attachment forms a mental template of what relationships are like, which guides later relationships and the person's own parenting.

*Evaluation.* The idea of a critical period has been softened to a *sensitive period*, since some late-adopted children do form attachments. Monotropy is disputed: many infants form multiple attachments early, and attachment to a father or other caregiver can predict later outcomes. The theory has also been criticised for placing responsibility on mothers.

## Ainsworth's Strange Situation

A **controlled observation** of infants aged roughly 12 to 18 months in a laboratory playroom, in eight episodes of about three minutes each, including: the caregiver and infant alone; a stranger enters; the caregiver leaves; the caregiver returns; the infant alone; the stranger returns; the caregiver returns. Observers record **proximity seeking**, **exploration and secure-base behaviour**, **stranger anxiety**, **separation anxiety** and **response to reunion**.

| Type | Behaviour | Approximate proportion in Ainsworth's sample |
|---|---|---|
| Secure | Explores using caregiver as a secure base; moderate distress on separation and stranger; comforted quickly at reunion | About two-thirds |
| Insecure-avoidant | Explores freely; little separation or stranger anxiety; little response or avoidance at reunion | About a fifth |
| Insecure-resistant | Explores little; strong stranger and separation anxiety; seeks and resists contact at reunion | About an eighth |

*Evaluation.* Standardised procedures and trained observers give good inter-rater reliability. But the procedure may be culture-bound (see below), and some infants fit none of the three types, which led later researchers to add a disorganised type that the specification does not require.

## Cultural variations

**Van IJzendoorn and Kroonenberg (1988)** combined 32 Strange Situation studies from eight countries. Secure attachment was the most common type in every country. Insecure-avoidant attachment was relatively common in West Germany, and insecure-resistant was relatively common in Israel and Japan. Variation **within** countries was about one and a half times greater than variation **between** them.

*Evaluation.* The large combined sample increases confidence in the overall pattern. However, "country" is not the same as culture, and most samples came from a few countries. Applying a procedure developed in the USA to other cultures assumes that behaviours mean the same everywhere, an **imposed etic**. For example, Japanese infants are rarely separated from their mothers, so high distress on separation may reflect unfamiliarity rather than insecure attachment.

## Maternal deprivation and institutionalisation

**Bowlby's theory of maternal deprivation** (1951) claims that prolonged separation from the mother during the critical period, without substitute emotional care, causes lasting harm: intellectual delay and emotional problems such as **affectionless psychopathy**, a lack of guilt and empathy. In his **44 thieves study** (1944), 14 of the thieves were diagnosed as affectionless psychopaths, and 12 of these had experienced prolonged early separation, compared with only 5 of the other 30 thieves.

*Evaluation.* The 44 thieves data are retrospective and correlational, and Bowlby carried out the interviews and diagnoses himself, so his expectations may have biased them. Rutter distinguished **deprivation** (losing an attachment) from **privation** (never forming one), arguing that much of the harm Bowlby described results from privation.

**The English and Romanian Adoptees project** (Rutter and colleagues) followed 165 Romanian children raised in poor-quality institutions and adopted by British families, compared with British children adopted at a similar age. Children adopted after about six months were more likely to show **disinhibited attachment** (indiscriminate friendliness to strangers) and lower intellectual ability on average than those adopted earlier, who largely caught up.

*Evaluation.* The longitudinal design tracks real outcomes over many years. Children were not randomly allocated to late or early adoption, so differences in the children themselves could affect who was adopted first. The findings have informed adoption practice, supporting early placement.

## Early attachment and later relationships

The internal working model predicts that early attachment type shapes **childhood relationships**, such as friendships, and **adult relationships**, including romantic partnerships and parenting. **Hazan and Shaver (1987)** analysed replies to a "love quiz" in a newspaper. Adults' self-reported attachment types occurred in roughly similar proportions to those found in infants, and those classed as secure reported longer-lasting, happier relationships.

*Evaluation.* The evidence is largely correlational and often relies on adults recalling childhood, which may be inaccurate. Many people with insecure early attachment form happy adult relationships, so the internal working model is a tendency, not a determinant.

## Applied model answer

*Original scenario.* In a Strange Situation, Theo, aged 14 months, cries loudly when his father leaves. When his father returns, Theo reaches to be picked up, then pushes him away and keeps crying. *Identify Theo's attachment type and explain your answer.* [3]

> Theo shows insecure-resistant attachment. His strong separation anxiety, crying loudly when his father leaves, is typical of this type. At reunion he seeks contact by reaching out but also resists it by pushing his father away, which is the mixed response to reunion that defines insecure-resistant attachment.

The answer names the type, then supports it with two behaviours from the scenario.

## Common mistakes

- Calling Harlow's monkeys "attached to food": the point is the reverse.
- Treating Bowlby's monotropic theory and his maternal deprivation theory as the same theory.
- Describing the Strange Situation as an experiment; it is a controlled observation.
- Saying van IJzendoorn found insecure attachment most common in Japan; secure was most common everywhere.
- Confusing deprivation with privation.
- Presenting the internal working model as determining adult relationships.

## Exam technique

- **Outline** a study: aim or procedure, then findings, then a conclusion, briefly.
- **Explain** a concept, such as the internal working model: define it and show how it works or what it predicts.
- **Apply**: identify the concept, then justify with specific details from the scenario.
- **Evaluate** and **discuss**: point, evidence, explanation of what it means for the theory, and a final judgement.
