Take c = 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ for water and all solutions, and assume 1.00 cm³ of solution has a mass of
1.00 g.

## Questions

**1.** Define the term *standard enthalpy of combustion*. **[3]**

**2.** This question is about standard conditions.

**(a)** State the pressure used for standard enthalpy changes. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why ΔfH⦵ of Br₂(l) is zero but ΔfH⦵ of Br₂(g) is not. **[2]**

**3.** Write equations, with state symbols, for:

**(a)** the standard enthalpy of formation of propan-1-ol, CH₃CH₂CH₂OH(l) **[2]**
**(b)** the standard enthalpy of combustion of ethane, C₂H₆(g). **[2]**

**4.** Excess zinc powder is added to 25.0 cm³ of 0.400 mol dm⁻³ copper(II) sulfate solution in a
polystyrene cup. The extrapolated temperature rise is 20.5 K.
Zn(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)
Calculate the enthalpy change for this reaction in kJ mol⁻¹. **[4]**

**5.** A spirit burner containing propan-1-ol (Mr = 60.0) is used to heat 100 g of water in a copper
can. The burner's mass falls from 64.37 g to 63.89 g. The water temperature rises from 19.5 °C to
42.0 °C.

**(a)** Calculate the enthalpy of combustion of propan-1-ol. Give your answer to an appropriate number
of significant figures. **[4]**
**(b)** Suggest two reasons why your answer is less exothermic than a data-book value. **[2]**

**6.** Use the data to calculate the standard enthalpy of formation of butane, C₄H₁₀(g).
ΔcH⦵ (kJ mol⁻¹): C(s) −394, H₂(g) −286, C₄H₁₀(g) −2877. **[3]**

**7.** Calculate ΔH for 4NH₃(g) + 5O₂(g) → 4NO(g) + 6H₂O(g).
ΔfH⦵ (kJ mol⁻¹): NH₃(g) −46, NO(g) +90, H₂O(g) −242. **[3]**

**8.** Chloroethane forms in the reaction C₂H₆(g) + Cl₂(g) → C₂H₅Cl(g) + HCl(g).
Mean bond enthalpies (kJ mol⁻¹): C–H 412, Cl–Cl 243, C–Cl 338, H–Cl 432, C–C 348.

**(a)** Use the mean bond enthalpies to calculate ΔH for the reaction. **[3]**
**(b)** All the species are gases. Explain why your answer may still differ from a value calculated
from enthalpies of formation. **[2]**

**9.** For H₂(g) + F₂(g) → 2HF(g), ΔH = −542 kJ mol⁻¹. The mean bond enthalpies of H–H and F–F are
436 and 158 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the H–F bond enthalpy. **[3]**

**10.** A student finds the enthalpy change for MgSO₄(s) + 7H₂O(l) → MgSO₄·7H₂O(s) using two
experiments. Ar: Mg 24.3, S 32.1, O 16.0, H 1.0.

- Experiment 1: 3.01 g of anhydrous MgSO₄ dissolves in 50.0 g of water. The temperature rises by 10.9 K.
- Experiment 2: 6.16 g of MgSO₄·7H₂O dissolves in 50.0 g of water. The temperature falls by 1.9 K.

Use 50.0 g as the mass that changes temperature in both experiments.

**(a)** Explain why this enthalpy change cannot be measured directly by calorimetry. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate ΔH₁, the enthalpy change for dissolving anhydrous MgSO₄. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate ΔH₂, the enthalpy change for dissolving MgSO₄·7H₂O. **[3]**
**(d)** Use Hess's law to calculate the enthalpy change for the hydration reaction. **[2]**

**11.** This question compares two ways of finding ΔH for the combustion of methanol.

**(a)** Calculate ΔcH⦵ for CH₃OH(l) + 1½O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l).
ΔfH⦵ (kJ mol⁻¹): CH₃OH(l) −239, CO₂(g) −394, H₂O(l) −286. **[3]**
**(b)** Use mean bond enthalpies to calculate ΔH for CH₃OH(g) + 1½O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(g).
(kJ mol⁻¹): C–H 412, C–O 360, O–H 463, O=O 496, C=O 743. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why your answers to (a) and (b) are different. **[2]**

**12.** In Required practical 2, a student records the temperature every 30 seconds before and after
mixing two solutions.

**(a)** Explain why the student plots temperature against time and extrapolates back to the time of
mixing. **[2]**
**(b)** The student repeats the experiment in a glass beaker instead of a polystyrene cup. State and
explain the effect on the calculated value of ΔH. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** The enthalpy change when 1 mole of a substance [1] is completely burned in oxygen [1] under
standard conditions, with all reactants and products in their standard states [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* each idea is a separate mark, so "burned in air" or leaving out "1 mole" costs a
mark even when the rest is perfect.

**2. (a)** **100 kPa** [1]
**(b)** Br₂(l) is the standard state of bromine, so forming it from the element involves no change [1].
Br₂(g) is not the standard state, so its value includes the enthalpy change of turning Br₂(l) into
Br₂(g) [1].
*Examiner insight:* the mark needs the idea of a standard state; "because it is an element" alone
does not explain why Br₂(g) is different.

**3. (a)** 3C(s) + 4H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → CH₃CH₂CH₂OH(l): elements on the left, 1 mol of product [1];
balanced with correct state symbols [1].
**(b)** C₂H₆(g) + 3½O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 3H₂O(l): 1 mol of ethane, balanced [1]; state symbols with
H₂O as a liquid [1].
*Examiner insight:* doubling the equation to avoid fractions loses the mark, because the definitions
require exactly 1 mole of the named substance.

**4.** q = 25.0 × 4.18 × 20.5 = 2142 J [1]
n(CuSO₄) = 0.400 × 0.0250 = 0.0100 mol [1]
ΔH = −2.142 ÷ 0.0100 [1]
= **−214 kJ mol⁻¹** [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* the final mark needs the negative sign and kJ mol⁻¹; an answer of −214 000 shows
the J to kJ conversion was missed and loses the last mark.

**5. (a)** Mass burned = 0.48 g; n = 0.48 ÷ 60.0 = 0.0080 mol [1]
q = 100 × 4.18 × 22.5 = 9405 J [1]
ΔcH = −9.405 ÷ 0.0080 = −1176 kJ mol⁻¹ [1]
= **−1200 kJ mol⁻¹** to 2 s.f., because the mass change has only 2 significant figures [1]
**(b)** Any two of: heat lost to the surroundings or to the can; incomplete combustion; evaporation
of propan-1-ol from the wick. One mark each [1] [1]
*Examiner insight:* using 0.48 g as m in q = mcΔT is a common error that loses the q mark, but later
marks can still be awarded as error carried forward.

**6.** Correct cycle or expression: ΔfH = ΣΔcH(reactants) − ΔcH(butane) [1]
4(−394) + 5(−286) = −3006 [1]
ΔfH⦵ = −3006 − (−2877) = **−129 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
*Examiner insight:* +129 kJ mol⁻¹ shows the subtraction was reversed and usually scores only the
first mark.

**7.** Products: 4(+90) + 6(−242) = −1092 [1]
Reactants: 4(−46) + 5(0) = −184 [1]
ΔH = −1092 − (−184) = **−908 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
*Examiner insight:* forgetting to multiply by the coefficients gives a wrong sum; giving O₂ a
non-zero value loses the reactants mark.

**8. (a)** Bonds broken (after cancelling identical bonds): one C–H + one Cl–Cl = 412 + 243 = 655 [1]
Bonds made: one C–Cl + one H–Cl = 338 + 432 = 770 [1]
ΔH = 655 − 770 = **−115 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** Mean bond enthalpies are averages over a range of compounds [1], so the actual bond
enthalpies in C₂H₆ and C₂H₅Cl differ from these values [1].
*Examiner insight:* the C–C value is not needed because that bond is unchanged; in (b) the
"not gaseous" reason earns nothing here because the question says all species are gases.

**9.** −542 = (436 + 158) − 2x [1]
2x = 594 + 542 = 1136 [1]
x = **568 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
*Examiner insight:* two H–F bonds form, so dividing by 2 is essential; 1136 scores only the first two
marks.

**10. (a)** It is not possible to add exactly the right amount of water to the solid and measure its
temperature change accurately [1].
**(b)** Mr(MgSO₄) = 120.4, so n = 3.01 ÷ 120.4 = 0.0250 mol [1]
q = 50.0 × 4.18 × 10.9 = 2278 J [1]
ΔH₁ = −2.278 ÷ 0.0250 = **−91.1 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(c)** Mr(MgSO₄·7H₂O) = 246.4, so n = 6.16 ÷ 246.4 = 0.0250 mol [1]
q = 50.0 × 4.18 × 1.9 = 397 J [1]
ΔH₂ = +0.397 ÷ 0.0250 = **+15.9 kJ mol⁻¹** (+16 to 2 s.f.) [1]
**(d)** ΔH₁ = ΔH(hydration) + ΔH₂ [1]
ΔH(hydration) = −91.1 − (+15.9) = **−107 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
*Examiner insight:* the sign of ΔH₂ must be positive because the temperature fell; using −15.9 gives
−75.2 kJ mol⁻¹, which can earn the cycle mark in (d) but not the final answer.

**11. (a)** Products: −394 + 2(−286) = −966 [1]
Reactants: −239 (O₂ is zero) [1]
ΔcH⦵ = −966 − (−239) = **−727 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** Broken: 3(412) + 360 + 463 + 1½(496) = 2803 [1]
Made: 2(743) + 4(463) = 3338 [1]
ΔH = 2803 − 3338 = **−535 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(c)** Mean bond enthalpies are averages, not the values for the bonds in these particular molecules
[1]. In (a) methanol and water are liquids, so the enthalpy changes of state are included there but
not in (b) [1].
*Examiner insight:* the 1½ O=O bonds are easy to miss in (b); the bonds-made total must include both
C=O bonds in CO₂ and all four O–H bonds in two water molecules.

**12. (a)** Heat is lost to the surroundings while the temperature is rising [1]. Extrapolating back
gives the temperature at the moment of mixing as if no heat had been lost, so ΔT is more accurate [1].
**(b)** Glass is a better conductor, so more heat is lost and the measured ΔT is smaller [1]. The
calculated ΔH is therefore smaller in magnitude (less exothermic or less endothermic) [1].
*Examiner insight:* "the result is less accurate" gains nothing; the marks need the direction of the
change in ΔT and in ΔH.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the mass of fuel or solid instead of the mass of water as m in q = mcΔT.
- Not converting J to kJ, giving answers 1000 times too large.
- Missing signs: temperature rise means negative ΔH, temperature fall means positive ΔH.
- Quoting too many significant figures when the data are only to 2 s.f.
- Reversing the subtraction in combustion-data Hess's law calculations.
- Not multiplying ΔfH⦵, ΔcH⦵ or bond enthalpies by the coefficients in the equation.
- Leaving "1 mole" or "standard states" out of definitions.
- Giving the states reason for a bond enthalpy discrepancy when every species is already a gas.
