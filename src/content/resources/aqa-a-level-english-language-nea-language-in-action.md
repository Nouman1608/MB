This guide covers **3.3 Language in Action**, the non-exam assessment (NEA) component of AQA A-level
English Language (7702), worth 20% of the qualification. Unlike Papers 1 and 2, it is coursework:
independent research and original writing, developed and drafted over time rather than produced under
timed exam conditions. These notes complement the site's guides to
[Paper 1](/resources/aqa-a-level-english-language-paper-1/) and
[Language Diversity and Change](/resources/a-level-aqa-english-language-diversity-and-change/).

## Where this fits in 7702

Papers 1 and 2 test analysis of given or unseen material; the NEA instead asks students to generate
their own object of study -- and every student completes both tasks: a piece of independent language
research (the investigation) and a piece of original writing reflected on through commentary. Both tasks draw on the same underlying methods of language
analysis taught across the whole specification, applied here to material the student has chosen rather
than material set by the exam board.

## Syllabus coverage

**AQA A-LEVEL ENGLISH LANGUAGE (7702) -- 3.3 LANGUAGE IN ACTION**

- **3.3.1 Language investigation** (2,000 words, excluding data and quotations): a student-led enquiry
  into an aspect of language, drawing on spoken, written or multimodal data, or a mix of text types;
  students are not restricted to formally taught areas -- any area yielding interesting questions about
  language in use may be chosen, generated by a genre-based, function/use-based, attitudes-based or
  user-based investigation question. The investigation must contain: an introduction (reasons for the
  focus, aims), a methodology (an evaluative account of data collection and organisation, approach to
  analysis), an analysis (interpretation of findings, relevant concepts and issues, contextual
  influences), a conclusion (linked back to the aim), references, and appendices (clean copies of data)
- **3.3.2 Original writing** (750 words, plus a 750-word commentary): one piece of original writing based
  on one of three areas -- The Power of Persuasion, The Power of Storytelling, or The Power of
  Information -- informed by studying a range of style models and analysing one in detail, plus a
  reflective commentary evaluating the style model, the writing process, and the effectiveness of the
  final piece. The submitted folder contains the writing, an annotated style model, the commentary, and
  references
- **3.3.3 Methods of language analysis**: both tasks draw on the same underlying language levels --
  phonetics, phonology and prosodics; graphology; lexis and semantics; grammar (including morphology);
  pragmatics; and discourse

## How to approach it

The single most important early decision is choosing a genuinely specific investigation focus -- "the
language used in three different advertisements for a particular product" is workable; "advertising
language" alone is not. A precise, narrow focus makes every later stage (methodology, analysis,
conclusion) easier, because the investigation has a clear boundary. For the original writing, the style
model analysis done in preparation directly feeds the final commentary, so treat that early research
stage as building material for the commentary, not as separate, disposable groundwork.

## Choosing an investigation focus

A strong investigation question falls into one of four types: genre-based (what are the distinctive
features of this type of language use?), function/use-based (what is the language used to do?),
attitudes-based (how do people feel about this language?), or user-based (who uses this type of
language?). Whichever type is chosen, the investigation needs a specific, bounded focus -- a named
dialect surveyed in a specific way, a specific set of texts, a specific comparison -- not a broad topic
area that could support a whole book.

## The investigation's five required sections

Introduction states why the focus was chosen and what the investigation aims to find out. Methodology
gives an evaluative account -- not just a description -- of how data was collected and why that approach
was chosen. Analysis interprets the findings against the stated aim, considers relevant concepts and
issues, and examines contextual influences on the data. Conclusion draws the analysis back to the
original aim. References and appendices support the whole piece with sources and clean copies of the raw
data.

## Original writing: three power categories

The Power of Persuasion (investigative journalism, a speech on a controversial topic, a letter to an MP),
the Power of Storytelling (a short story, a biography extract, a dramatic monologue), and the Power of
Information (travel journalism, a social-issues blog, local history writing) each require studying a
style model before drafting original work in that mode, then reflecting on the process in the
commentary.

## Worked example: from broad interest to bounded investigation question

```
Too broad: "How does social media affect language?"

Step 1 - narrow the mode:
Which platform, or which specific feature (hashtags, emoji use,
abbreviation)?

Step 2 - narrow the population:
Whose language -- a specific age group, a specific online
community?

Step 3 - narrow the comparison or question type:
Genre-based ("what features characterise X"), or attitudes-based
("how do users feel about X")?

Result: "How do abbreviation and emoji use differ between two
specific age groups' text messages, and what attitudes do each
group express towards the other's style?"
```

This final version has a clear data-collection plan, a clear analytical framework, and a clear boundary
-- all missing from the original broad question.

## Common mistakes

Choosing an investigation topic too broad to analyse in 2,000 words. Treating the methodology section as
a simple description rather than an evaluative account of the approach taken. Writing an original piece
without first analysing a specific style model in enough depth to inform the commentary. Applying
language-level analysis (phonetics, lexis, grammar, pragmatics) inconsistently, missing levels relevant
to the chosen data type.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long is the 7702 NEA?**
3,500 words in total: a 2,000-word investigation excluding data, and original writing and a commentary of 750 words each.

**How is the NEA marked?**
It is worth 100 marks and 20% of the A-level, assessed by teachers and moderated by AQA.

**How are the marks split?**
The investigation carries 50 marks, the original writing 25 and the commentary 25.


## Quick revision checklist

- State the exact word limits and required sections for both the investigation and the original
  writing/commentary.
- Practise narrowing a broad interest into a bounded, analysable investigation question.
- Identify which of the four investigation question types (genre, function/use, attitudes, user-based)
  best suits a given research interest.
- Explain the relationship between style-model analysis and the final reflective commentary.
- Apply the six methods of language analysis (phonetics/phonology, graphology, lexis/semantics, grammar,
  pragmatics, discourse) relevant to a given data type.
