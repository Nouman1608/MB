This guide goes deeper into section **6.5 Limitations of accounting statements** of the Cambridge O Level Accounting 7707 syllabus for examination in 2027, 2028 and 2029. Candidates should understand "the limitations of accounting statements due to the following factors: historic cost; application of accounting policies; non-financial aspects for example, skill of the workforce, location of the business, economic climate".

The shared [inter-business comparison, interested parties and limitations guide](/resources/igcse-accounting-inter-business-comparison-interested-parties-and-limita/) introduces the three factors. This page measures their effect with figures, so that you can explain a limitation precisely instead of naming it. Recall the points with the [limitations revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-limitations-of-accounting-statements-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [limitations practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-limitations-of-accounting-statements-practice/). All businesses and figures are invented, and amounts are in dollars.

## Why statements have limits

Financial statements are a careful record, but they are a record of past transactions, measured in money, using methods the business has chosen. Each of those features is useful and each creates a limitation. The three factors in 6.5 match them: historic cost (the past), accounting policies (the methods chosen) and non-financial aspects (what money cannot measure).

## 1. Historic cost

Under the historic cost concept, assets are recorded at what they cost, less depreciation, not at what they are worth today.

**Effect on the statement of financial position.** Moorgate Printers bought its premises years ago for $90,000. After $27,000 of depreciation, the statement of financial position shows them at $63,000. A valuer says they would sell for $240,000. The statement understates the assets the business controls by $177,000.

**Effect on ratios.** Moorgate's profit before interest is $30,000 and its capital employed is $150,000, giving ROCE of 20.00%. If the premises were shown at their current value, capital employed would be 150,000 + 177,000 = $327,000, and ROCE would be 30,000 / 327,000 x 100 = 9.17%. The historic cost figure makes the return look more than twice as good. A rival that bought similar premises last year at today's price would show a much lower ROCE for the same trading.

**Effect of rising prices over time.** Moorgate's revenue rose from $200,000 to $212,000, a 6% increase. If its selling prices rose by 8% during the year, then in last year's prices the revenue is 212,000 / 1.08 = $196,296, so it actually sold about 1.85% less. Statements in money terms do not separate price changes from changes in the amount of business done.

## 2. Application of accounting policies

Businesses choose methods and make estimates: the depreciation method and rate, the useful life of assets, how inventory is valued, the percentage used for the allowance for irrecoverable debts and the limit below which items are treated as expenses. Different reasonable choices give different profits from the same transactions.

**Example.** Cranleigh Supplies has trade receivables of $60,000 at its year end. If it sets the allowance for irrecoverable debts at 2%, the allowance is $1,200; at 5% it is $3,000. Profit (in the first year of the allowance) differs by $1,800, and so does the value of net current assets. Neither figure is wrong: both are estimates.

| Policy choice | Effect on the figures |
|---|---|
| Higher depreciation rate | Lower profit, lower asset values |
| Reducing balance instead of straight line | Higher charges early in an asset's life, lower later |
| Larger allowance for irrecoverable debts | Lower profit and lower net trade receivables |
| Lower inventory valuation | Lower gross profit this year, higher next year |

Consistency means a business keeps its policies from year to year, which makes its own trend reliable. But users comparing two businesses cannot assume they made the same choices, and a business that changes a policy can move its profit without any change in trading.

## 3. Non-financial aspects

The money measurement concept records only items that can be measured reliably in money. Many things that decide a business's future cannot.

- **Skill of the workforce.** Two garages may have identical statements, but one has qualified mechanics who attract loyal customers and the other relies on untrained staff. Neither statement shows this.
- **Location of the business.** A shop on a busy high street and one on a declining street may have had the same profit last year, but their prospects differ. A new road that diverts traffic is not in the accounts.
- **Economic climate.** In a recession customers spend less; when interest rates rise, loans cost more. Last year's statements do not show how the business will cope with the conditions ahead.

Other non-financial factors include the business's reputation, customer loyalty, the strength of competitors, staff morale and its effect on the environment.

## Which limitation matters to which user

| User and decision | Limitation that matters most | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Bank deciding on a loan secured on premises | Historic cost | The premises may be worth far more, or less, than their book value |
| Investor comparing two companies | Accounting policies | Profit and ROCE may differ because of choices, not performance |
| Buyer of a business | Non-financial aspects | Staff skill, location and reputation decide future profit |
| Owner comparing this year with years ago | Historic cost and rising prices | Higher revenue may come from higher prices, not more sales |
| Supplier deciding on credit | Economic climate | Last year's liquidity may not hold if trading conditions worsen |

Matching the limitation to the user's decision turns a list into an explanation, which is what longer questions reward.

## Worked example: buying a café

Hamid is choosing between two cafés with almost identical statements: profit for the year of about $40,000, similar ratios and similar net assets. A good answer to "explain the limitations of using these statements to choose" would make three linked points.

1. **Historic cost**: café A's premises were bought ten years ago and appear at a low book value; café B leases its premises. The net assets and ROCE are therefore not comparable.
2. **Accounting policies**: café B depreciates its kitchen equipment over ten years, café A over five. B's profit is higher partly because of the longer estimated life.
3. **Non-financial aspects**: a large office near café A is due to close, which will cut its lunchtime trade; café B has an experienced chef with a strong local reputation.

Hamid should use the statements as a starting point, then visit both cafés, ask about future plans in each area and look at more than one year's results.

## Common mistakes

- Naming "historic cost" without explaining that it means current values are not shown.
- Writing "accounting policies are different" without giving a policy and its effect on profit or assets.
- Listing non-financial aspects without linking them to the business in the question.
- Treating limitations as errors: they are features of how statements are prepared.

## Exam technique

Questions may use command words such as **explain**, **discuss** or **evaluate**. For each limitation: name it, explain what the statements do not show because of it, and state the effect on the decision in the question. In an evaluation, balance the limitations against what the statements do show reliably, such as actual revenue and expenses for the year.
