## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between a "total war" and a "limited war". **[2]**

**2.** Identify **three** long-term causes of the First World War. **[3]**

**3.** State **two** forms of foreign involvement in the Spanish Civil War. **[2]**

**4.** Outline **two** reasons why the Argentine junta invaded the Falkland Islands in 1982. **[2]**

**5.** Identify **three** technological developments used in the First World War. **[3]**

**6.** Outline **two** economic or demographic effects of the Iran-Iraq War. **[2]**

---

## Section B

**7.** **(a)** Explain why the assassination of Franz Ferdinand led to a general European war within six weeks. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain **one** argument that long-term causes were more important than the assassination. **[2]**

**8.** **(a)** Explain why the First World War became a total war. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain how the mobilization of resources affected the outcome of the war. **[4]**

**9.** **(a)** Explain the importance of air and sea power in the outcome of the Falklands War. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain **two** political effects of the war. **[4]**

**10.** Explain why the policy of non-intervention damaged the Spanish Republic. **[4]**

**11.** Evaluate the impact of the First World War on the role and status of women in **one** country. **[6]**

**12.** "Foreign intervention was the decisive factor in the outcome of civil wars." With reference to **two** civil wars, each chosen from a different region, to what extent do you agree with this statement? **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** 1 mark: a total war mobilizes the whole of a society's population and economy and treats civilians and industry as targets. 1 mark: a limited war is restricted in its aims, geographical area or means, and does not seek the complete defeat of the enemy, for example the Falklands War. **[2]**

**2.** Any three, 1 mark each: the alliance system dividing Europe into two blocs; the Anglo-German naval race; imperial rivalry, including the Moroccan crises (1905, 1911); Balkan nationalism and Austro-Serbian hostility; militarism and inflexible war plans such as the Schlieffen Plan. **[3]**

**3.** Any two, 1 mark each: German aid to the Nationalists, including the Condor Legion; Italian troops and aircraft for the Nationalists; Soviet arms and advisers for the Republic; the International Brigades of foreign volunteers; the Non-Intervention Agreement and committee led by Britain and France. **[2]**

**4.** Any two, 1 mark each: to restore a long-standing Argentine claim to the islands (the Malvinas); to divert attention from economic crisis and growing opposition at home; a belief that Britain would not fight, because of signs of reduced British commitment to the South Atlantic. **[2]**

**5.** Any three, 1 mark each: machine guns; heavy artillery; poison gas (first large-scale use at Ypres, 1915); tanks (first used at the Somme, 1916); aircraft for reconnaissance and bombing; submarines; airships. **[3]**

**6.** Any two, 1 mark each: very heavy casualties on both sides; destruction of oil installations and cities, including in the "war of the cities"; Iraq's large war debts to Gulf states; disruption of Gulf shipping through attacks on tankers; refugees and displacement in border regions. **[2]**

**7.** **(a)** Up to 4 marks: Austria-Hungary, backed by Germany's "blank cheque", sent Serbia an ultimatum and declared war (28 July 1914) (1); Russia mobilized to support Serbia (1); Germany's Schlieffen Plan required an immediate attack on France through Belgium, so Germany declared war on Russia and France (1); the invasion of Belgium brought Britain into the war on 4 August (1). **[4]**
**(b)** 2 marks for a developed argument: the alliances, arms race and war plans meant a local crisis could escalate rapidly, and earlier crises (Morocco, Bosnia, the Balkan Wars) had already raised tension; the assassination was the trigger, not the reason a general war was possible. **[2]**

**8.** **(a)** 1 mark each: trench deadlock on the Western Front made it a long war of attrition; governments needed mass armies, so conscription was introduced (Britain, 1916); economies were controlled for war production (for example by the Defence of the Realm Act in Britain); civilians became targets through blockade, submarine warfare and air raids. **[4]**
**(b)** 2 marks for each developed point: the British naval blockade caused severe shortages in Germany by 1917-18, weakening morale; American entry in 1917 added vast financial and industrial resources and fresh troops; the Allies' greater capacity to replace losses meant that the failure of Germany's 1918 offensive could not be recovered. **[4]**

**9.** **(a)** Up to 4 marks: the sinking of the *General Belgrano* by a British submarine (2 May 1982) led the Argentine navy to remain in port (1); British carrier-based aircraft provided air cover for the task force (1); Argentine aircraft sank several British ships, showing that air power nearly offset British naval strength (1); British control of the sea allowed landings at San Carlos and the advance to Stanley (1). **[4]**
**(b)** 2 marks for each developed effect: in Argentina, defeat discredited the military junta, Galtieri was replaced, and civilian rule returned with elections in 1983; in Britain, victory strengthened Thatcher's government before her re-election in 1983; continued British commitment to defending the islands. **[4]**

**10.** Up to 4 marks: it prevented the legal government from buying arms from Britain and France (1); Germany and Italy signed but ignored the agreement, so the Nationalists continued to receive aid (1); the Republic became dependent on the Soviet Union, which had to be paid in gold and which increased communist influence (1); that influence deepened divisions within the Republic, as in Barcelona in May 1937 (1). **[4]**

**11.** Up to 6 marks: 2 for wartime change, 2 for lasting change and limits, 2 for a supported judgement. *Britain as an example:* women took jobs in munitions factories, transport and offices, and joined auxiliary services; the Representation of the People Act (1918) gave the vote to women over 30 who met a property qualification; the Sex Disqualification (Removal) Act (1919) opened some professions. Limits: many women were dismissed after demobilization; the vote was not equal until 1928; pre-war suffrage campaigns also explain the 1918 Act. Judgement: an important but partial and uneven change. **[6]**

**12.** Marked holistically using the 15-mark markbands; the indicative content is a guide, not a checklist. *Spanish Civil War (Europe) and Chinese Civil War (Asia and Oceania), for example.* For: in Spain, German and Italian aid and non-intervention gave the Nationalists decisive material superiority; in China, Soviet handover of Manchuria and captured Japanese arms helped the communists in 1945-46. Against: in Spain, Nationalist unity and Republican division; in China, the communists' land reform and peasant support, Guomindang corruption, inflation and military errors, and Mao and Lin Biao's strategy; US aid to the Guomindang was large but did not prevent defeat. A strong answer compares the two wars in each paragraph and judges, for example, that foreign intervention was decisive in Spain but secondary in China, where internal factors determined the outcome. **[15]**

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I use the First World War and the Second World War together?**
Both involved several regions, but check whether the question's regional requirement is met by the theatres you discuss; safer pairs come from clearly different regions.

**Do I need casualty figures?**
Only ones you are sure of. A precise policy, date or event is better evidence than an uncertain number.
