## Section A: knowledge and concepts

**1.** Give two differences between the role of revenge in Jacobean revenge tragedy and its role in
later domestic tragedy. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why a playwright might keep a death offstage, and give two possible effects on an
audience. **[3]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by economic or institutional violence, with an example from one Section
B play. **[3]**

**4.** For one Section B play, identify one prop or sound connected with violence and explain its
significance. **[3]**

**5.** "In *Waiting for Godot*, the violence has no meaning." Give one argument for and one against
this view. **[2]**

## Section B: an original scene, arguments and plans

**Extract (original).** In this invented play, Carol has looked after her brother's farm for twenty
years. He has sold it to a developer without telling her.

> *The farmhouse kitchen. Evening. A shotgun hangs above the door, as it has all play. CAROL is
> washing a single cup. FRANK enters with papers.*
> **FRANK:** It's done. They sign on Monday.
> **CAROL:** *(still washing)* You'll want your supper.
> **FRANK:** Carol, did you hear me?
> **CAROL:** I heard. I hear everything in this house. I heard Dad tell you it was yours.
> *(She dries the cup, carefully, and places it on the shelf beneath the gun.)*
> **FRANK:** You'll have money. More than you've ever had.
> **CAROL:** I've had a farm. *(Pause.)* Go to bed, Frank. Lock the door behind you. I always do.

**6.** Analyse how the playwright creates a sense of threatened violence in the extract. **[6]**

**7.** Explain how the extract presents the idea that the past has already wronged Carol. **[4]**

**8.** A student argues that Carol's final lines are an act of revenge. Give one point in support and
one against. **[2]**

**9.** "Violence in later tragedy is always a sign of powerlessness." Plan three paragraphs on your
own set play to test this view, naming one moment for each paragraph. **[6]**

**10.** Read this student paragraph on *Hedda Gabler*: "Hedda is very violent. She shoots at Brack,
burns the manuscript and shoots herself. This shows she is evil." Explain two weaknesses and rewrite
the final sentence. **[4]**

**11.** Choose one Section B play. Explain how violence in the past shapes the protagonist's tragic
journey. **[4]**

**12.** List four pieces of evidence, from different parts of your own set play, that you would learn
for an essay on violence and revenge, and state what each would show. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two, [1] each: revenge is a sacred or public duty in Jacobean tragedy but becomes private
spite or humiliation later; Jacobean revenge drives the plot to a bloody climax, while later revenge
may be small, hidden or absent; the older avenger is often the protagonist, while later protagonists
are more often victims of others' grievances or of unseen forces. **[2]**

**2.** Reason [1]: for example, to make the audience imagine the act, or to keep attention on those
left behind. Two effects, [1] each: suspense or shock when the sound or report arrives; pity focused
on the survivors' reactions; a sense that the death is sordid or anticlimactic rather than heroic;
unease because the audience is denied a clear view. **[3]**

**3.** Definition [1]: harm done by systems such as employers, markets, the state or an empire rather
than by an individual's blow. Example [2] for a precise reference and explanation, for example: in
*Death of a Salesman*, Howard dismisses Willy after decades of work [1], and the scene shows a
company discarding a worker as worn out, which drives Willy's despair [1]; or in *Translations*, the
army's renaming of places damages a community's language and memory without a shot being fired. **[3]**

**4.** Prop or sound identified [1]; significance explained [2]. For example: General Gabler's
pistols in *Hedda Gabler* [1] link Hedda to her father's military power [1] and, once she fires at
Brack, warn the audience that the play will end with a shot [1]. Credit: the rubber tube or the car
sound in *Death of a Salesman*; the radio or the Varsouviana in *Streetcar*; Lucky's rope in *Godot*.
**[3]**

**5.** For [1]: Estragon's beatings have no stated cause and no consequence, so violence appears
random. Against [1]: the violence is part of the play's meaning, showing a world in which suffering
has no explanation; or Pozzo's whip shows violence as a relationship of power and dependence. **[2]**

**6.** Any six, [1] each:

- The shotgun "as it has all play" is a loaded symbol the audience has been waiting to see used.
- Placing the cup "on the shelf beneath the gun" draws the eye to the weapon without any line
  mentioning it.
- Washing "a single cup" and drying it "carefully" suggests a suppressed fury expressed as control.
- Carol's evasive reply ("You'll want your supper") refuses to engage, building tension through
  what is not said.
- "I hear everything in this house" sounds like a threat as well as a complaint.
- The short sentence "I've had a farm" sets the past tense against Frank's future money, a
  loss that cannot be repaid.
- The pause gives the audience time to fear what she will do.
- "Lock the door behind you. I always do" is ambiguous: care, warning or menace.

**7.** Any four, [1] each: "I heard Dad tell you it was yours" reveals an older injustice in the
inheritance; her twenty years of work have gone unrewarded; "I've had a farm" shows that what she
valued was never legally hers; her routines ("I always do") show a life shaped by service to the
house; the sale completes a wrong that began before the play. **[4]**

**8.** For [1]: the ambiguous instruction to lock the door can unsettle Frank and leave him afraid in
his own house, a small act of power. Against [1]: it may be habit and care, showing that she is
trapped by the role of carer rather than taking revenge. **[2]**

**9.** Three paragraphs, each with a relevant moment [1] and a point that tests the view [1]. For
example, on *A Streetcar Named Desire*: Stanley's violence on poker night, when the women's talk and the radio interrupt his game [1],
shows anger at a loss of control, which supports the view [1]; his rape of Blanche [1] is the act of
the man who holds power in the house, which challenges it [1]; Blanche's past cruelty to her husband
[1] shows that verbal violence can come from someone who is, at that moment, powerful [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Weakness one [1]: the paragraph lists actions without analysing how they are staged or why.
Weakness two [1]: "evil" is a moral label that ignores the pressures of marriage, class and gender
that the play presents. Rewritten sentence [2] for an argued, precise judgement, [1] for a partial
improvement. Example: "Each act is a bid for control in a life where Hedda has little, so her
violence measures how trapped she is rather than simply how cruel." **[4]**

**11.** Past event identified accurately [1]; explanation of how it shapes the journey [3]. For
example: in *Streetcar*, the suicide of Blanche's husband after her public cruelty [1] leaves her
haunted by guilt, heard in the polka music [1]; it drives her flight into illusion and drink [1], so
the past violence becomes a cause of her collapse when Stanley exposes her [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Four pieces of evidence from different parts of the play, each with a stated purpose, [1]
each. For example, on *Death of a Salesman*: Ben's sparring with Biff (the brutal ideal of success);
Linda's discovery of the rubber tube (suicide planned early); Willy's dismissal by Howard (economic
violence); the offstage car and the Requiem (death that brings no recognition). **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Retelling violent events without analysing staging, sound or report.
- Treating offstage violence as unimportant because it is not seen.
- Using moral labels instead of examining power and pressure.
- Arguing only one side of a view about violence when the question asks you to weigh it.

For a full essay, choose one of the arguments in Question 9 and write for about an hour without
notes, as in the closed-book exam.
