This guide covers section 4.5, Symmetry, in the Geometry topic of the Cambridge O Level Mathematics (Syllabus D) 4024 syllabus for 2025, 2026 and 2027. The syllabus asks you to **recognise line symmetry and the order of rotational symmetry in two dimensions**, including the properties of triangles, quadrilaterals and polygons that are directly related to their symmetries, and to **recognise the symmetry properties of prisms, cylinders, pyramids and cones**, for example by identifying planes and axes of symmetry. After this guide, use the [symmetry revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-mathematics-4024-symmetry-revision-notes/) and the [symmetry practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-mathematics-4024-symmetry-practice/).

## What section 4.5 asks for

1. Find lines of symmetry in a plane shape and state how many there are.
2. State the order of rotational symmetry of a plane shape.
3. Use symmetry to explain facts about triangles, quadrilaterals and polygons, such as the equal base angles of an isosceles triangle.
4. Identify planes of symmetry and axes of symmetry of simple solids.

## Line symmetry

A shape has **line symmetry** if a line (called a **mirror line** or line of symmetry) divides it into two parts, each the mirror image of the other. If you folded the shape along the line, one half would fit exactly on the other.

Tips for finding lines of symmetry:

- Imagine a mirror along a possible line, or trace the shape and fold the tracing.
- Check lines that join midpoints of opposite sides and lines through opposite vertices.
- A line of symmetry is not simply a line that cuts the shape into two equal areas. A rectangle cut along its diagonal gives two equal halves, but the diagonal is not a line of symmetry.

## Rotational symmetry

A shape has **rotational symmetry** if it looks exactly the same after a turn of less than 360° about a point, the centre of rotation. The **order of rotational symmetry** is the number of different positions in which the shape fits onto itself during one full turn, counting the starting position.

```
order = 360° ÷ smallest angle of turn that fits the shape on itself
```

A shape with no rotational symmetry has order 1: it only fits itself after a complete turn.

| Smallest turn | Order |
|---|---|
| 180° | 2 |
| 120° | 3 |
| 90° | 4 |
| 72° | 5 |
| 60° | 6 |

## Triangles

| Triangle | Lines of symmetry | Order of rotational symmetry |
|---|---|---|
| Equilateral | 3 | 3 |
| Isosceles | 1 | 1 |
| Scalene | 0 | 1 |

The single line of symmetry of an isosceles triangle goes from the apex to the midpoint of the base. It meets the base at a right angle. This explains the properties that the base angles are equal and that the line bisects the apex angle.

### Worked example 1

Triangle ABC is isosceles with AB = AC and angle BAC = 40°. Find angle ABC.

```
the line of symmetry through A makes angle ABC = angle ACB
angle sum: 40° + 2x = 180°
x = 70°
```

So angle ABC = 70°.

## Quadrilaterals

| Quadrilateral | Lines of symmetry | Order of rotational symmetry |
|---|---|---|
| Square | 4 | 4 |
| Rectangle | 2 | 2 |
| Rhombus | 2 | 2 |
| Parallelogram | 0 | 2 |
| Kite | 1 | 1 |
| Isosceles trapezium | 1 | 1 |
| Trapezium (general) | 0 | 1 |

Symmetry explains the properties:

- The two lines of symmetry of a **rectangle** join the midpoints of opposite sides. That is why opposite sides are equal and the diagonals have equal length.
- The two lines of symmetry of a **rhombus** are its diagonals. That is why the diagonals bisect each other at right angles and bisect the vertex angles.
- A **kite** has one line of symmetry along a diagonal. That is why one pair of opposite angles are equal, and the diagonals cross at right angles.
- A **parallelogram** has rotational symmetry of order 2 about the centre where its diagonals meet. That is why opposite sides and angles are equal and the diagonals bisect each other.

## Regular polygons

A regular polygon with n sides has n lines of symmetry and rotational symmetry of order n.

| Polygon | Lines | Order | Smallest turn |
|---|---|---|---|
| Regular pentagon | 5 | 5 | 72° |
| Regular hexagon | 6 | 6 | 60° |
| Regular octagon | 8 | 8 | 45° |
| Regular decagon | 10 | 10 | 36° |

An irregular polygon may have fewer symmetries, or none. A circle has infinitely many lines of symmetry and every angle of turn about its centre works.

## Symmetry on a grid

### Worked example 2

The points (1, 2) and (2, 5) are on one side of a vertical line of symmetry x = 3. Find the matching points on the other side.

```
reflection in x = 3: x → 6 − x, y stays
(1, 2) → (5, 2)
(2, 5) → (4, 5)
```

### Worked example 3

The point (2, 1) is part of a shape with rotational symmetry of order 4 about the origin. Find the other three matching points.

```
a quarter turn takes (x, y) to (−y, x)
(2, 1) → (−1, 2) → (−2, −1) → (1, −2)
```

The four points are (2, 1), (−1, 2), (−2, −1) and (1, −2).

## Symmetry in solids

A **plane of symmetry** divides a solid into two parts that are mirror images of each other. An **axis of symmetry** is a line through the solid about which the solid can be turned through less than 360° and fit onto itself.

| Solid | Planes of symmetry | Axes of symmetry |
|---|---|---|
| Cuboid (all edges different) | 3 | 3 axes, each of order 2 |
| Cube | 9 | 13 axes (3 of order 4, 4 of order 3, 6 of order 2) |
| Square-based pyramid (apex above centre) | 4 | 1 axis of order 4 |
| Cone | infinitely many through the axis | 1 axis |
| Cylinder | infinitely many through the axis, and 1 halfway along its length | 1 main axis, and infinitely many order 2 axes at right angles |
| Prism with an equilateral triangle cross-section | 4 | 1 axis of order 3 along its length, and 3 axes of order 2 |
| Prism with a square cross-section | 5 | 1 axis of order 4, and 4 axes of order 2 |
| Sphere | infinitely many | infinitely many |

For prisms, count the planes by separating them into two kinds. A plane through the length that matches a line of symmetry of the cross-section, and a plane at right angles to the length halfway along it. An equilateral triangular prism has 3 of the first kind and 1 of the second, giving 4.

### Worked example 4

How many planes of symmetry has a cuboid that measures 5 cm by 3 cm by 2 cm?

```
one plane parallel to each pair of faces, halfway between them
number = 3
```

If two of the three measurements are equal, such as 5 by 3 by 3, the cuboid has a square cross-section and 5 planes: the three above and two diagonal planes.

## Common mistakes

- Counting a diagonal of a rectangle or a parallelogram as a line of symmetry.
- Saying the order of rotational symmetry is zero. If there is none, it is 1.
- Forgetting that a parallelogram has rotational symmetry of order 2 but no line symmetry.
- Finding the angle of turn for a regular polygon with the wrong formula, for example 180° ÷ n. It is 360° ÷ n.
- Giving a single plane of symmetry for a cylinder, a cone or a pyramid, which has several.
- Mixing up planes of symmetry (flat surfaces) with axes (lines).

## Exam technique

- **State** the number of lines or the order. A number is enough.
- **Draw** a line of symmetry: use a ruler.
- **Complete** a diagram: check by folding, or by working out the reflected coordinates.
- Explain a property by naming the symmetry that causes it.
