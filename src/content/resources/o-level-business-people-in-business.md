Every business decision studied under Topic 1 -- what to produce, how
to organise it, who owns it -- eventually comes down to people getting
the work done. Topic 2 **People in business** is where 7115 turns to
the human resources side of running a business: how a workforce is
motivated, structured, staffed and kept informed. It is examined
across Paper 1 (Short Answer and Data Response) and Paper 2 (Case
Study), both worth 50% of the final grade, since Business Studies at
7115 is not tiered and content is drawn from the entire syllabus.

## Where this fits in 7115

Note for candidates comparing notes with friends on Cambridge IGCSE
Business Studies (0450): Cambridge explicitly builds this O Level on
shared content with the IGCSE syllabus, so the underlying human
resources theory is the same course either way, and the assessment
structures are identical -- the same two papers, timings, weightings,
question descriptions and assessment-objective weightings. What
differs is the qualification code, the grade range (A* to G for the
IGCSE against A* to E for the O Level), and the series and zone in
which each qualification is available.

A business cannot rely on unmotivated staff to deliver good service or
high productivity, so this sub-topic starts with **why** motivation
matters before turning to **how** it is achieved.

The benefits of a well-motivated workforce named in the syllabus are
specific: higher labour productivity, reduced absenteeism and lower
labour turnover. To explain why motivation produces these effects, you
need two named theoretical frameworks:

- **Maslow's hierarchy of needs** -- the idea that human needs are
  arranged in levels, and a business must address lower-level needs
  (pay, security) before higher-level needs (belonging, esteem,
  self-actualisation) will motivate an employee.
- **Taylor and Herzberg** -- two named motivational theorists whose
  contrasting views (Taylor's focus on pay as the primary motivator
  versus Herzberg's distinction between "hygiene factors" that prevent
  dissatisfaction and genuine motivators) are both examinable.

Methods of motivation split into two categories: **financial rewards**
(wage, salary, bonus, commission, profit sharing) and **non-financial
methods** (job enrichment, job rotation, teamworking, training,
opportunities for promotion). Exam questions frequently ask you to
recommend and justify a specific method for a given business scenario
-- a decision that should draw on which category of need Maslow's
hierarchy suggests is currently unmet for that workforce.

## 2.2 Organisation and management

This sub-topic covers how a business is structured and led once its
people are in place.

- **Organisational charts**: you must be able to draw, interpret and
  understand simple hierarchical structures, using the vocabulary of
  levels of hierarchy, span of control and chain of command (including
  the distinction between a "tall" structure, with many levels and
  narrow spans of control, and a "short" structure, with fewer levels
  and wider spans).
- **Roles and responsibilities**: directors, managers, supervisors and
  other employees, and how these roles relate to one another.
- **The role of management**: the functions of management -- planning,
  organising, coordinating, commanding and controlling -- plus the
  importance of delegation and the trade-off between trust and
  control.
- **Leadership styles**: the features of autocratic, democratic and
  laissez-faire leadership, with the expectation that you can
  recommend and justify an appropriate style for given circumstances
  (an autocratic style may suit a business needing fast decisions in a
  crisis; a democratic style may suit a business drawing on staff
  expertise for complex decisions).
- **Trade unions**: what a trade union is and the effects on a
  business of its employees being union members.

## 2.3 Recruitment, selection and training of employees

This sub-topic follows the employee lifecycle from hiring through to,
where necessary, downsizing.

**Recruitment and selection** covers the methods used to recruit and
select staff, the difference between internal recruitment (filling a
vacancy from within the existing workforce) and external recruitment
(hiring from outside), the main stages of the process, and the
benefits and limitations of employing part-time versus full-time
staff.

**Training** covers why training matters to both the business and the
employee, and the benefits and limitations of three named types:
induction training (for new starters), on-the-job training (learning
while working) and off-the-job training (learning away from the normal
work environment, e.g. a course).

**Reducing workforce size** requires you to distinguish **dismissal**
(an employee is removed for a specific, employee-related reason) from
**redundancy** (a role is removed, typically because it is no longer
needed, for reasons unrelated to the individual's performance), and to
understand situations where downsizing might be necessary, such as
automation or falling demand.

**Legal controls** close this sub-topic: employment contracts, unfair
dismissal, discrimination, health and safety, and the legal minimum
wage, and how each affects both employers and employees.

## 2.4 Internal and external communication

The final sub-topic asks why effective communication matters to a
business and what can go wrong. You need to know the benefits and
limitations of different communication methods, including those based
on information technology, and be able to recommend and justify which
method suits a given circumstance. You also need to demonstrate
awareness of **communication barriers** -- how they arise, the
problems caused by ineffective communication, and how such barriers
can be reduced or removed.

## How to approach it

Because Topic 2 is assessed through scenario-based questions on both
papers, the highest-value preparation is practising "recommend and
justify" answers rather than memorising definitions in isolation --
for every named method (a motivation method, a leadership style, a
recruitment approach, a communication channel), be ready to explain
which specific business circumstance makes it the better choice, and
which makes an alternative better instead. Keep the theorists (Maslow,
Taylor, Herzberg) tied to concrete workplace examples rather than
abstract descriptions, since application (AO2) and analysis (AO3)
carry more combined weighting than pure knowledge (AO1) alone across
the qualification as a whole, and especially on the Paper 2 case study
-- though on Paper 1 itself, knowledge still carries the larger single
share.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is O Level Business 7115 the same as IGCSE Business Studies 0450?**
Cambridge builds this O Level on content shared with the IGCSE syllabus, so the human resources theory is the same. The qualification code, the grade range (A* to E for the O Level against A* to G for the IGCSE) and the series in which each is available differ.

**What is the difference between dismissal and redundancy?**
Dismissal is when an employee is removed for a specific, employee-related reason. Redundancy is when a role is removed because it is no longer needed, for example because of automation or falling demand, for reasons unrelated to the individual's performance.

**What are the three types of training in Cambridge O Level Business 7115?**
Induction training for new starters, on-the-job training while working, and off-the-job training away from the normal work environment, such as a course. Know a benefit and a limitation of each.

**What legal controls over employment are in Topic 2?**
Employment contracts, unfair dismissal, discrimination, health and safety, and the legal minimum wage, together with how each affects employers and employees.

**Which assessment skills matter most in People in business?**
Application (AO2) and analysis (AO3) carry more combined weighting than knowledge (AO1) across the qualification, especially on the Paper 2 case study, although on Paper 1 knowledge still has the largest single share.
