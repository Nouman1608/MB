## Section A: Short questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following is most likely to follow a rise in Bank Rate? **[1]**

A: An increase in house prices

B: A depreciation of the pound

C: A fall in consumer borrowing

D: A rise in investment

**2.** Quantitative easing involves the central bank: **[1]**

A: Selling government bonds to banks

B: Creating money to buy financial assets such as government bonds

C: Raising Bank Rate to reduce the money supply

D: Lending directly to the government to finance its deficit

**3.** State who sets the UK's inflation target and who decides Bank Rate. **[2]**

**4.** A household has a 180,000 interest-only mortgage. Its rate rises from 3.5% to 5.0%. Calculate the change in annual interest payments. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why an inflation target may help to keep inflation low. **[2]**

**6.** Explain, with reference to a money market diagram, the effect of an increase in the money supply on the interest rate. **[3]**

## Section B: Structured and extended questions

**7.** The exchange rate of Country Q's currency, the crown, changes from 1 crown = 2.00 dollars to 1 crown = 2.20 dollars after a rise in interest rates.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in the value of the crown. **[1]**

**(b)** A Country Q export costs 50 crowns. Calculate its dollar price before and after the change. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain two ways in which this change could reduce inflation in Country Q. **[4]**

**8.** Explain, with the aid of an AD/AS diagram, how a cut in interest rates could affect real output and the price level. **[4]**

**9.** Explain how quantitative easing can increase aggregate demand when interest rates are already close to zero. **[4]**

**10.** A firm expects a new warehouse to earn a return of 7% a year. Explain how a rise in its borrowing rate from 6% to 8% could affect its decision, and what this implies for aggregate demand. **[3]**

**11.** Inflation in Country R has risen to 6% because of a sharp rise in imported energy prices, while unemployment is rising. Explain why raising interest rates may not be the best response. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate the effectiveness of monetary policy in achieving low and stable inflation. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** C [1]. Borrowing becomes dearer, so households borrow less. **[1]**

**2.** B [1]. QE creates central bank money to buy assets. **[1]**

**3.** The government (the Chancellor) sets the target [1]; the Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee decides Bank Rate [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Before: 3.5% x 180,000 = 6,300. After: 5.0% x 180,000 = 9,000 [1]. Annual interest rises by **2,700** [1]. **[2]**

**5.** A clear target anchors expectations: workers and firms expect about 2% inflation [1], so they set wages and prices consistent with it, which helps inflation stay near target [1]. **[2]**

**6.** The diagram has the interest rate on the vertical axis and the quantity of money on the horizontal, with a downward-sloping demand for money and a vertical supply [1]. An increase in money supply shifts the supply curve right [1], so the equilibrium interest rate falls [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** (2.20 - 2.00) / 2.00 x 100 = **+10%** [1].

**(b)** Before: 50 x 2.00 = **100 dollars**. After: 50 x 2.20 = **110 dollars** [1].

**(c)** Way 1: exports become dearer abroad and imports cheaper, so net exports fall [1], reducing AD and demand-pull pressure [1]. Way 2: imported goods and raw materials cost less in crowns [1], lowering firms' costs and the prices of imported consumer goods directly [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Lower interest rates reduce the cost of borrowing and the reward for saving [1], so consumption and investment rise [1]. AD shifts right from AD1 to AD2 on the diagram, with price level and real output axes [1]. Real output and the price level both rise; the split depends on spare capacity [1]. **[4]**

**9.** The central bank creates money to buy government bonds [1]. Higher bond prices mean lower yields, so long-term interest rates fall [1]. Sellers use the money to buy other assets, raising asset prices and wealth [1]. Cheaper long-term borrowing and higher wealth encourage consumption and investment, shifting AD right [1]. **[4]**

**10.** At 6% the expected return exceeds the cost of borrowing, so the warehouse is worth building [1]. At 8% the cost exceeds the 7% return, so the project is likely to be cancelled or delayed [1]. If many firms respond like this, investment falls and AD shifts left [1]. **[3]**

**11.** The inflation is cost-push, from imported energy prices, so it is not caused by excess demand [1]. Raising rates reduces AD, which lowers output further and adds to rising unemployment [1]. It does not lower energy prices, so inflation may fall only slowly [1]. If the energy shock is temporary, inflation may fall back without action, so the Bank might hold rates unless expectations of higher inflation start to rise [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Award up to 10 marks across knowledge, application, analysis and evaluation.
- Raising rates reduces C, I and net exports, shifting AD left and lowering demand-pull inflation [1].
- An appreciation lowers import prices, reducing inflation directly [1].
- An independent central bank with a clear 2% target is credible, anchoring expectations [1].
- Rates can be changed quickly at regular meetings [1].
- Time lags of a year or more mean policy depends on forecasts [1].
- The effect is weaker when many borrowers are on fixed rates or confidence is low [1].
- Against cost-push inflation, rate rises reduce output and jobs without removing the cause [1].
- At the lower limit for rates, policy relies on QE, whose effects are less certain [1].
- Side effects on borrowers, savers, exporters and asset prices may be large [1].
- Judgement: monetary policy is effective at keeping inflation low and stable when inflation is demand-led and expectations are anchored, but less effective against large supply shocks, so it works best alongside fiscal and supply-side policy [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Separate demand-pull from cost-push inflation; that distinction drives the judgement.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying the government sets interest rates.
- Calling QE printing banknotes.
- Ignoring the time lag.
- Missing the exchange rate channel.
- Treating every kind of inflation the same.
- Drawing the money market with unlabelled axes.
