## 6.4.1 Structure and calculations

**Current account:** a country's yearly record of trade in goods and services plus income and gift flows with other countries. A balance is money in minus money out.

| Component | In (credit) | Out (debit) |
|---|---|---|
| Trade in goods | Goods exported | Goods imported |
| Trade in services | Services sold abroad (for example foreign tourists) | Services bought from abroad |
| Primary income | Wages, interest, profit, dividends earned abroad | The same paid to foreigners |
| Secondary income | Gifts, aid, remittances received | Gifts, aid, remittances sent |

| Formula | Use |
|---|---|
| Component balance = money in - money out | Each line |
| Current account balance = sum of the four component balances | Overall |
| Balance on goods and services = goods balance + services balance | Trade balance |
| Balance ÷ GDP × 100 | Size relative to the economy |

### Method in steps

1. Work out each component's balance with its sign.
2. Add the four balances.
3. Positive means surplus; negative means deficit. State which.
4. To compare with the size of the economy, divide the balance by GDP and multiply by 100.
5. For a missing component, subtract the known balances from the total.

### Worked reminder

Country V, one illustrative year: goods in 310, out 275; services in 60, out 85; primary income in 18, out 40; secondary income in 30, out 12. Balances: +35, -25, -22, +18. Total: 35 - 25 - 22 + 18 = **+6**, a small surplus. Goods and services alone: 35 - 25 = +10. With GDP of 480, the surplus is 6 ÷ 480 × 100 = **1.25%** of GDP.

## 6.4.2 Causes

| Deficit | Surplus |
|---|---|
| Home prices climb faster than rivals' | Goods that sell well: lower prices or better quality |
| High exchange rate | Low exchange rate |
| Rising incomes pull in imports | High saving, low spending on imports |
| Exports are a few primary products with falling prices | Valuable raw material or energy exports |
| Profits and interest paid to foreign owners and lenders | Income earned on overseas assets |
| Recession abroad cuts demand for exports | Strong growth among trading partners |
| Remittances, gifts or aid sent abroad | Remittances or aid received |

Competitiveness reminder: if home prices rise 8% and a rival's rise 5%, the relative price is 1.08 ÷ 1.05 = 1.0286, so home goods are about **2.9% dearer** than before.

## 6.4.3 Consequences

| Area | Deficit | Surplus |
|---|---|---|
| GDP | Net exports fall; demand leaks abroad; GDP lower than it would be | Net exports rise; demand and GDP higher |
| Employment | Job losses where firms lose sales to imports | More jobs in export industries |
| Inflation | Lower demand eases pressure; but a falling currency raises import prices | More demand may cause demand-pull inflation if capacity is nearly full |
| Exchange rate | Floating: more currency supplied than demanded, so it falls. Fixed: reserves run down | Floating: more demand for the currency, so it rises. Reserves build up |

**Chain for a deficit (practise writing it):** imports exceed exports, so net exports fall, so total demand for home output falls, so output and jobs fall. If the currency then falls, imports cost more, so cost-push inflation may follow.

**A deficit is not always harmful.** It matters less if it is small relative to GDP, is temporary, or pays for machinery that raises future output. It matters more if it is large, lasts for years, and pays for consumption.

**Reserves sum:** years of cover = reserves ÷ annual deficit. Reserves of 90 and a deficit of 15 a year give 90 ÷ 15 = 6 years.

### Exchange rate, PED and trade (6.3.4 link)

Worked reminder: the zel rises from 1 zel = 2.00 marn to 1 zel = 2.50 marn. A jacket priced at 100 zel goes from 200 to 250 marn, a rise of 25%. A machine priced at 300 marn goes from 150 to 120 zel, a fall of 20%.

If PED for exports is 0.5, quantity falls 0.5 × 25 = 12.5%. Revenue in marn: 1.25 × 0.875 = 1.094, up about 9.4%. Revenue in zel: price unchanged, quantity down 12.5%, so down 12.5%. The home exporter earns fewer zel even though the foreign-currency total rose.

Rule: with **elastic** demand, cutting the price lifts revenue. With **inelastic** demand, cutting it lowers revenue, while raising it lifts revenue.

## 6.4.4 Policies

| Policy | Mechanism | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deflationary fiscal (higher taxes, lower spending) | Cuts incomes and import spending | Can act fairly fast | Lower growth and jobs |
| Higher interest rates | Cuts borrowing and spending | May attract foreign savings | Raises firms' costs |
| Tariffs and quotas | Make imports dearer or scarcer | Direct effect on imports | Retaliation, higher prices, small effect if PED is low |
| Subsidies | Lower costs of exporters or import-competing firms | Helps targeted industries | Costs government money |
| Lower exchange rate | Cheaper exports, dearer imports | Boosts export demand | Depends on PED; higher import costs |
| Supply-side (training, infrastructure, research) | Raises productivity and quality | Tackles the cause | Slow, uncertain |

Worked reminder (tariff): imports of 500 units at 40 zel with a 15% tariff cost buyers 40 × 1.15 = 46 zel each. If PED is 0.4, quantity falls 0.4 × 15 = 6%, to 470. Payments to foreign sellers fall from 20,000 to 470 × 40 = 18,800, a saving of 1,200. The tariff revenue (470 × 6 = 2,820) goes to the government.

Conflicts to mention (syllabus 4.2.2): economic growth against balance of payments stability, and full employment against balance of payments stability. Policies that cut a deficit by cutting demand work against both aims.

**Evaluation checklist:** cause of the deficit, size, PED, time lags, retaliation, side effects, then a judgement.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Current account vs trade balance.** The trade balance is goods and services only.
- **Primary vs secondary income.** Primary income is earned from work or ownership abroad. Secondary income is a gift with nothing received in return.
- **Deficit vs debt.** A deficit is a yearly flow. Debt is an accumulated stock.
- **Depreciation under floating vs devaluation under fixed.** The market causes the first. The government or central bank chooses the second.
- **Tariff vs quota.** A tariff taxes imports; a quota limits quantity.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the component that records profit paid by a foreign-owned factory to its overseas owners.
2. Name the component that records money spent by foreign tourists.
3. Calculate the current account balance: goods +45, services -60, primary income +12, secondary income -8.
4. An economy with GDP of 900 has a deficit of 18. What share of GDP is that?
5. The current account is +20. Goods are +50, services -30, primary income -12. Find the secondary income balance.
6. State the relative price change if home prices rise 8% and a rival's rise 5%.
7. At 1 zel = 2.00 marn, convert 500 marn to zel. Repeat at 1 zel = 2.50 marn.
8. Reserves are 90 and the deficit is 15 a year. How many years can reserves cover it?
9. A 15% tariff is placed on an import priced at 40 zel. What do buyers pay?
10. Export prices abroad fall 10% and PED is 1.6. Find the change in quantity and in revenue in the foreign currency.
11. State one weakness of using deflationary policy to cut a deficit.

### Answers

1. Primary income (an outflow).
2. Trade in services (an inflow).
3. 45 - 60 + 12 - 8 = **-11**, a deficit.
4. 18 ÷ 900 × 100 = **2.0%**.
5. Known: 50 - 30 - 12 = 8. Secondary income = 20 - 8 = **+12**.
6. 1.08 ÷ 1.05 = 1.0286, about **2.9% dearer**.
7. 500 ÷ 2.00 = **250 zel**; 500 ÷ 2.50 = **200 zel**.
8. 90 ÷ 15 = **6 years**.
9. 40 × 1.15 = **46 zel**.
10. Quantity +16%. Revenue: 0.90 × 1.16 = 1.044, so **+4.4%**.
11. It lowers output and employment, and may cut growth.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Reporting a component balance with the wrong sign.
- Placing remittances or aid in trade in services rather than secondary income.
- Forgetting that profit paid abroad is a primary income outflow.
- Claiming a depreciation must cure a deficit without any mention of PED.
- Stopping a chain of reasoning after one step, for example "net exports fall" with no link to GDP or jobs.
- Treating the tariff as money paid to the foreign seller.
- Listing policies with no judgement of how effective they might be.
- Calling every deficit harmful without discussing cause and size.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the four components of the current account?**
The current account is made up of trade in goods, trade in services, primary income (wages, interest, profit and dividends) and secondary income (gifts, aid and remittances).

**How do you measure the size of a current account balance?**
Divide the balance by GDP and multiply by 100 to express it as a percentage of GDP, which shows how large it is relative to the economy.

**How does a deficit affect the exchange rate under a floating system?**
A deficit means more of the home currency is supplied to buy foreign currency for imports than is demanded by foreigners buying exports, so the currency tends to fall.

**Will a depreciation always cure a deficit?**
No. The outcome depends on the price elasticity of demand for exports and imports, and buyers often take time to change habits. If both are very inelastic, the deficit could widen.
