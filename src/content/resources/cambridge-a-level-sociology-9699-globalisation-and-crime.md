Section 8.3 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699), *Globalisation and crime*, is the
last sub-topic of the Globalisation section of Paper 4. The syllabus names three areas: reasons
for the emergence of global crimes, such as human trafficking, corporate crime and crimes against
the environment; explanations of who benefits from global crime, including Marxist and feminist
perspectives; and policing and prosecuting global crime, including the benefits and challenges
resulting from globalisation and new technology. Heading 8 links this content to the key concepts
*Power, control and resistance* and *Structure and human agency*.

introduces the main examples. This page explains the mechanisms behind them in more depth. Use it

## What makes a crime "global"?

A crime is global when its organisation, its victims or its harm cross national borders. Three
features matter:

- **Transnational organisation.** Offenders, money and goods move between states. Dick Hobbs and
  Colin Dunnighan (1998) used the term **glocal** organised crime: networks are global in reach but
  still rooted in local places, contacts and markets.
- **Harm that ignores borders.** Pollution, illegal logging and toxic waste damage people far from
  where decisions were made.
- **Gaps between legal systems.** An act may be legal in one country and criminal in another, so
  offenders can exploit differences in law and enforcement.

## Why have global crimes emerged?

- **Time–space compression.** David Harvey's *The Condition of Postmodernity* (1989) described how
  faster transport and communication shrink the effective distance between places. The same
  changes that let firms trade worldwide let criminal networks move goods, people and money.
- **Deregulated finance.** Electronic transfers, offshore financial centres and shell companies
  make it easier to hide the source of money (money laundering) and to avoid or evade tax. The
  Panama Papers leak (2016), from the law firm Mossack Fonseca, exposed how offshore companies were
  used to conceal wealth.
- **Inequality and insecurity.** Ian Taylor (1997) argued that neo-liberal globalisation increased
  job insecurity and inequality while promoting a consumer culture, encouraging crime both among
  the poor who are excluded and among elites who find new opportunities.
- **Global demand.** Demand in richer countries for drugs, cheap labour, exotic wildlife or sexual
  services creates markets that criminal networks supply from poorer ones.
- **Weak or corrupt states.** Where policing is under-resourced or officials can be bribed, global
  networks can operate with less risk.
- **New technology.** The internet makes possible fraud, data theft and online exploitation
  across borders, and encrypted communication reduces the risk of detection.

## Three global crimes in more detail

### Human trafficking

The UN Trafficking Protocol (2000), part of the Palermo Protocols, defines trafficking through
three elements:

| Element | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Act** | Recruitment, transport, transfer, harbouring or receipt of people |
| **Means** | Threat or use of force, coercion, abduction, fraud, deception, abuse of power or of vulnerability |
| **Purpose** | Exploitation, including sexual exploitation, forced labour, slavery or removal of organs |

For children, the "means" element is not required: moving a child for exploitation is trafficking
even without force or deception.

**Trafficking is not the same as people smuggling.** Smuggling is helping someone cross a border
illegally, usually for a fee; the relationship normally ends on arrival. Trafficking is about
exploitation, which may continue for years and may not involve crossing a border at all. In
practice the two can overlap, for example when a smuggled migrant is forced to work off a debt.

### Corporate crime

Transnational corporations (TNCs) can locate production where regulation is weakest, sometimes
called **regulatory arbitrage**. Frank Pearce's *Crimes of the Powerful* (1976) argued that
corporate offences cause great harm but are rarely treated as "real crime". The Volkswagen
emissions scandal (2015), in which software was used to cheat vehicle emissions tests, shows a
corporate offence with global effects: cars sold in many countries emitted more pollution than
tests showed.

### Crimes against the environment

Green criminology studies harms to the environment, whether or not they are illegal everywhere.
Eamonn Carrabine and colleagues (2004) distinguished:

- **Primary green crimes:** direct destruction, such as air and water pollution, deforestation and
  the decline of species.
- **Secondary green crimes:** crimes that arise from flouting rules meant to prevent environmental
  harm, or from state violence against environmental groups. The sinking of the Greenpeace ship
  *Rainbow Warrior* by French intelligence agents in Auckland harbour (1985) is a classic example.

The dumping of toxic waste from the ship *Probo Koala*, chartered by the trading company
Trafigura, in Abidjan, Côte d'Ivoire (2006) illustrates the global dimension: waste generated in
one part of the world was disposed of in a poorer country, harming local residents.

## Who benefits from global crime?

### Marxist explanations

- Global crime is a product of capitalism's search for profit; corporations and elites commit the
  most harmful crimes but are policed lightly, often through civil fines rather than criminal
  courts.
- Laws themselves reflect ruling-class interests: tax avoidance through offshore structures may be
  legal, while small-scale benefit fraud is prosecuted.
- Poor people are drawn into the lowest-paid, most dangerous roles (drug couriers, small-time
  smugglers) and are the ones most often caught.

*Evaluation:* Marxism explains corporate and financial crime well but says less about trafficking
networks run by people who are not part of any ruling class, or about crimes whose victims are
corporations.

### Feminist explanations

- Women and girls are disproportionately victims of trafficking for sexual exploitation and
  domestic servitude; patriarchal demand for sexual services sustains the trade.
- Saskia Sassen (2000) described a **feminisation of survival**: as households, communities and
  even governments in poorer countries come to depend on women's earnings abroad, women are pushed
  into risky migration and informal work where exploitation is common.
- Feminists also criticise state responses: trafficked women may be treated as irregular migrants
  and deported rather than protected as victims.

*Evaluation:* Laura Agustín's *Sex at the Margins* (2007) argued that campaigns against trafficking
can deny migrant women's agency by treating all who sell sex as passive victims. This is a useful
counterpoint because it shows that feminists themselves disagree about the issue.

## Policing and prosecuting global crime

**Benefits from globalisation and new technology**
- **International cooperation:** Interpol circulates notices, including Red Notices asking police
  worldwide to locate a wanted person; regional bodies such as Europol coordinate investigations.
- **International law:** the Rome Statute (1998) created the International Criminal Court, which
  began work in 2002; the Council of Europe's Budapest Convention on Cybercrime (2001) set common
  rules for cybercrime investigations.
- **Financial tracking:** the Financial Action Task Force, set up in 1989, sets anti-money
  laundering standards; banks must report suspicious transactions.
- **Technology:** digital forensics, data sharing and surveillance help identify networks.

**Challenges**
- **Jurisdiction and sovereignty:** each state controls its own police and courts; extradition can
  be slow or refused.
- **Uneven capacity:** some states lack resources; corruption can protect offenders.
- **Technology helps offenders too:** encryption, anonymising networks and cryptocurrencies make
  tracing harder.
- **Power of offenders:** TNCs can afford lawyers, lobby governments and relocate; harms such as
  pollution may be treated as regulatory breaches, not crimes.
- **Definitions:** a harm that is legal in one country may be a crime elsewhere, so offenders
  choose where to operate.

## Common mistakes

- Using "trafficking" and "smuggling" as if they meant the same.
- Treating all global crime as organised crime; the syllabus also names corporate and
  environmental crime.
- Writing about Marxist and feminist views in general terms without linking them to *who benefits*,
  which is the syllabus wording.
- Ignoring the second half of the policing bullet: technology brings both benefits and challenges.
- Inventing statistics on the size of global crime. Use named cases and studies instead.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** questions: give the reason, the mechanism and an example. For instance, "deregulated
  finance" → "shell companies hide ownership" → "Panama Papers".
- **Evaluate** questions: contrast Marxist and feminist views of who benefits, or the benefits and
  challenges of policing, then judge by crime type.
- Bring in heading 7: global social movements (7.3) campaign against corporate and environmental
  harm, and the debate about the nation state's role in tackling global problems applies directly
  to policing.
