## Section A

**1.** Define resource stewardship. **[1]**

**2.** Put these waste options in order from most to least preferred: recycle, dispose, prevent, recover, reuse. **[2]**

**3.** Distinguish between the views of Malthus and Boserup on population and food supply. **[2]**

**4.** Outline what is meant by a payment for ecosystem services. **[2]**

**5.** State one Sustainable Development Goal that relates directly to resource stewardship. **[1]**

## Section B

**6.** A region compares two strategies to reduce food waste over five years.

| | Cost (million dollars) | Food waste avoided (tonnes) |
|---|---|---|
| Strategy P: household education campaign | 2.4 | 8,000 |
| Strategy Q: redistribution of surplus food from retailers | 6.0 | 15,000 |

**(a)** Calculate the cost per tonne avoided for each strategy. **[2]**

**(b)** Using the waste hierarchy, suggest why Strategy P might be preferred even if its cost per tonne were higher. **[2]**

**7.** Explain how the Machakos district of Kenya provides evidence for Boserup's view, and one limitation of using it as proof. **[4]**

**8.** Explain why the rebound effect can limit the success of efficiency-based stewardship. **[3]**

**9.** Explain why a national ban on a product may be easier to enforce in some countries than others. **[3]**

## Section C

**10.** Evaluate the circular economy as a strategy for resource stewardship. **[10]**

**11.** "Population growth is the greatest threat to future resource security." To what extent do you agree? **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Careful and responsible management of resources so that they remain available for future generations [1]. **[1]**

**2.** Prevent, reuse, recycle [1], recover, dispose [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Malthus argued that population tends to grow faster than food supply, so growth is checked by famine, disease or war [1]; Boserup argued that population pressure stimulates new farming methods that raise food output [1]. **[2]**

**4.** A scheme in which landowners are paid to manage land in ways that provide benefits to others, such as protecting forests that store carbon or safeguard water supplies [1], as in Costa Rica's national programme begun in 1997 [1]. **[2]**

**5.** For example Goal 12, responsible consumption and production, or Goal 6, clean water and sanitation [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** P: 2,400,000 / 8,000 = **300 dollars per tonne** [1]. Q: 6,000,000 / 15,000 = **400 dollars per tonne** [1].

**(b)** An education campaign prevents waste from being created in the first place, which is the highest level of the hierarchy [1], whereas redistribution deals with surplus food once it already exists, a form of reuse that ranks lower and depends on continued over-supply [1]. **[4]**

**7.** In the 1930s Machakos was described as overpopulated and eroded, but by the time of a study published in 1994 its population had grown several times over while erosion was controlled and output per hectare rose [1]. Farmers responded to pressure by terracing, water harvesting and tree planting, which supports Boserup's idea that population pressure stimulates innovation [1]. However, the outcome also depended on access to Nairobi's markets, investment and land rights [1], so it shows that population pressure can lead to innovation under certain conditions rather than proving that it always will [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Efficiency reduces the cost of using a resource, for example fuel per kilometre [1]. Lower costs encourage people to use more of it, such as by driving further [1], so part, or occasionally all, of the expected saving is lost, as Jevons observed for coal in the 19th century [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Enforcement depends on the state's capacity to inspect, fine and control borders [1]. A country with strong institutions and centralised control, as Rwanda has shown with its plastic bag ban since 2008, can enforce rules nationally [1], whereas a country with large informal markets, long borders or weak administration may find smuggling and non-compliance hard to stop [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Indicative content; award one mark per valid, developed point up to 10:

- Defines the circular economy as keeping materials in use and designing out waste, in contrast to take-make-dispose [1].
- Strength: reduces demand for raw materials and the environmental impacts of extraction [1].
- Strength: prevention and reuse save energy as well as materials [1].
- Strength: can create local jobs in repair, remanufacturing and recycling [1].
- Example: deposit return schemes or European Union circular economy plans [1].
- Limitation: recycling loses material and quality, so circularity is never complete [1].
- Limitation: it does not by itself reduce total consumption, and the rebound effect can offset gains [1].
- Limitation: it needs infrastructure and regulation that many lower-income countries lack [1].
- Uses the waste hierarchy to distinguish strong from weak circular strategies [1].
- Conclusion: a supported judgement, for example that it is valuable but works best combined with reduced consumption [1]. **[10]**

**11.** Indicative content; award one mark per valid, developed point up to 10:

- Neo-Malthusian argument: more people increase total demand for food, water and energy [1].
- Population growth is concentrated in some regions where resources are already stressed [1].
- Counter: consumption per person, driven by rising affluence, often matters more than numbers of people [1].
- Counter: high-income countries with slow population growth have the largest footprints per person [1].
- Counter: Boserup and resource optimists argue that innovation raises supply, with evidence such as the Green Revolution and Machakos [1].
- Other threats: climate change, conflict, poor governance and unequal access [1].
- Waste is significant: around a third of food is lost or wasted, according to the UN Food and Agriculture Organization [1].
- Scale and place matter: population pressure can be decisive locally, but not globally [1].
- Uses accurate examples on both sides [1].
- Conclusion: a supported judgement, for example that population growth contributes but is not the greatest threat compared with consumption patterns and governance [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* For "to what extent", rank the factors. Saying "many factors matter" without deciding which matters most earns less credit than a clear, justified ranking.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Placing recycling at the top of the waste hierarchy.
- Dividing cost by tonnes without converting millions to dollars first.
- Describing Machakos as proof of Boserup without noting the conditions that made it work.
- Ignoring consumption per person when discussing population and resources.
