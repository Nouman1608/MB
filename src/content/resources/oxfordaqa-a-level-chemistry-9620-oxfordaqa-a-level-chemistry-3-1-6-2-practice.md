These questions cover **3.1.6.2 Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, examined in Unit 2. Section A has short recall and application questions; Section B has longer, structured questions. Percentages and curve readings are invented for these questions. If a point is unfamiliar, read the [Maxwell–Boltzmann study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-6-2/) or the [Maxwell–Boltzmann revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-6-2-revision-notes/) first.

## Questions

### Section A

**1.** State the labels for the two axes of a Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution. **[2]**

**2.** State what is meant by the most probable energy and where it appears on the curve. **[1]**

**3.** Explain why the mean energy of the molecules is greater than the most probable energy. **[1]**

**4.** State what the total area under the curve represents, and explain why it does not change when the gas is heated. **[2]**

**5.** For one gas sample, 1.2 % of molecules have energy ≥ Ea at 300 K and 4.5 % at 330 K. Calculate the factor by which the proportion increases, and explain why a 30 K rise can produce such a large factor. **[2]**

**6.** A student states: "When a gas is heated, more molecules have the most probable energy." Explain why this is incorrect. **[2]**

### Section B

**7.** This question is about sketching distributions.

**(a)** Sketch a Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution for a gas at temperature T₁. Label the axes and mark a typical activation energy, Ea. **[3]**

**(b)** On the same axes, sketch the curve for the same gas at a higher temperature T₂. **[3]**

**(c)** Shade the region that represents the extra molecules that can react at T₂ but not at T₁. **[1]**

**8.** Two curves, X and Y, are drawn for the same gas sample. X has its peak at 4.0 energy units with a height of 100 units. Y has its peak at 5.5 energy units with a height of 75 units.

**(a)** State which curve is for the higher temperature, giving two pieces of evidence. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why curve Y has a lower peak. **[2]**

**(c)** State which curve is higher at an energy well beyond both peaks. **[1]**

**9.** More of the same gas is pumped into a sealed flask, and the temperature is returned to its original value.

**(a)** Describe how the new curve compares with the original. **[2]**

**(b)** State the effect on the fraction, and on the number, of molecules with energy ≥ Ea. **[2]**

**10.** A gas is cooled from 350 K to 300 K.

**(a)** Describe three changes to its Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution. **[3]**

**(b)** State the effect on the number of molecules with energy ≥ Ea. **[1]**

**11.** A student's sketch of two curves for one gas sample has these features. Explain what is wrong with each.

**(a)** The tail of each curve touches the energy axis. **[1]**

**(b)** The cooler curve encloses a smaller area than the hotter curve. **[1]**

**(c)** Ea is marked to the left of both peaks, for a reaction that is slow at room temperature. **[2]**

**12.** For a gas at 300 K, 2.0 % of molecules have energy ≥ 40 kJ mol⁻¹ and 0.10 % have energy ≥ 60 kJ mol⁻¹. Two reactions of this gas have Ea = 40 kJ mol⁻¹ and Ea = 60 kJ mol⁻¹, and the same collision frequency.

**(a)** State which reaction is faster and by roughly what factor. **[2]**

**(b)** Describe how you would show the two activation energies, and the molecules able to react in each case, on a single sketch. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why both percentages would increase if the gas were heated. **[1]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** Horizontal: energy [1]; vertical: number (or fraction) of molecules with that energy [1] **[2]**

**2.** The energy possessed by the largest number of molecules, at the peak of the curve [1] **[1]**

**3.** The curve has a long tail at high energy, which pulls the average up [1] **[1]**

**4.** The total number of molecules [1]; heating does not change the number of molecules [1] **[2]**

**5.** 4.5 ÷ 1.2 = **3.75**, about 3.8 times [1]; Ea lies far out in the tail, so when the curve shifts to higher energy the small area beyond Ea grows by a large factor [1] **[2]**

**6.** On heating the peak is lower, so fewer molecules have the most probable energy [1]; the same number of molecules is spread over a wider range of energies [1] **[2]**

### Section B

**7. (a)** Axes labelled energy and number of molecules [1]; curve starting at the origin, rising to one peak and with a long tail that does not touch the axis [1]; Ea marked on the energy axis well to the right of the peak [1]

**(b)** Peak lower than the T₁ peak [1]; peak to the right of the T₁ peak [1]; curves cross once, with the T₂ curve above T₁ at high energies and similar total area [1]

**(c)** The region beyond Ea that lies between the T₂ curve and the T₁ curve [1] **[7]**

*Examiner insight:* In (b), a T₂ curve that is higher everywhere loses the third mark, because it would enclose more molecules.

**8. (a)** Y [1]; its peak is at higher energy and its peak is lower [1]

**(b)** The number of molecules is the same, so the areas are equal [1]; at the higher temperature the energies are spread over a wider range, so fewer molecules have any single energy [1]

**(c)** Y [1] **[5]**

**9. (a)** The peak is at the same energy and the shape is the same [1]; the curve is higher at every energy, enclosing a larger area [1]

**(b)** The fraction with energy ≥ Ea is unchanged [1]; the number with energy ≥ Ea increases [1] **[4]**

**10. (a)** The peak moves to lower energy [1]; the peak becomes higher [1]; the curve becomes narrower, with less of it at high energy (area unchanged) [1]

**(b)** It decreases [1] **[4]**

**11. (a)** There is no maximum energy, so the tail should approach the axis without touching it [1]

**(b)** The sample has the same number of molecules at both temperatures, so the areas must be equal [1]

**(c)** If Ea were left of the peaks, most molecules would have energy ≥ Ea [1]; the reaction would then be fast, so for a slow reaction Ea must lie in the tail, well to the right of the peaks [1] **[4]**

**12. (a)** The reaction with Ea = 40 kJ mol⁻¹ [1]; about 2.0 ÷ 0.10 = **20 times** faster [1]

**(b)** Draw one curve (one temperature) with two vertical lines on the energy axis at 40 and 60 kJ mol⁻¹ [1]; the area to the right of each line represents the molecules that can react in that reaction [1]

**(c)** On heating the curve shifts to higher energies, so more of its area lies beyond each fixed Ea [1] **[5]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Labelling the vertical axis "energy" or "rate".
- Drawing a bell-shaped curve, or one that starts above zero.
- A hotter curve with a taller peak, or more area than the cooler curve.
- Moving Ea when the temperature changes.
- Interpreting with the height at Ea rather than the area beyond Ea.
