Recall notes for section 3.5 of OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics (J205, Version 2.0, June 2026), examined in Component 02. Full explanations and worked examples are in the [fiscal policy study guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-fiscal-policy/); apply them with the [fiscal policy practice questions](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-fiscal-policy-practice/). All figures are invented.

## What the specification asks

- Explain **purposes of government spending** and **sources of revenue**, including **direct and indirect taxes**.
- Explain a **balanced budget**, **budget surplus** and **budget deficit**.
- Explain **fiscal policy** and how it achieves economic objectives.
- **Calculate and analyse** how taxes and spending affect markets and the whole economy.
- **Evaluate** costs (including **opportunity cost**) and benefits of fiscal policy.
- **Evaluate** redistribution of income and wealth, including **progressive taxes**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Fiscal policy | Changing government spending and taxation to influence the economy |
| Direct tax | Tax on income or wealth, paid directly to the government |
| Indirect tax | Tax on spending, collected by sellers |
| Balanced budget | Revenue equals spending |
| Budget surplus | Revenue exceeds spending |
| Budget deficit | Spending exceeds revenue; the gap is borrowed |
| National debt | Total government borrowing outstanding from past deficits |
| Expansionary fiscal policy | Higher spending and/or lower taxes to raise demand |
| Contractionary fiscal policy | Lower spending and/or higher taxes to reduce demand |
| Average tax rate | Tax paid ÷ income × 100 |
| Progressive tax | Average rate rises with income |
| Regressive tax | Average rate falls as income rises |
| Opportunity cost | The next best alternative given up |

## Direct or indirect?

| Direct | Indirect |
|---|---|
| Income tax | VAT |
| National insurance | Fuel duty |
| Corporation tax | Alcohol and tobacco duties |

## Purposes of spending

- Services: health, education, defence, police, transport.
- Income support: benefits and state pensions.
- Managing the economy: infrastructure, training, support in a downturn.

## Deficit versus debt

- **Deficit** = one year's spending − revenue (a flow).
- **Debt** = all past borrowing still owed (a stock).
- Deficits add to debt; debt brings interest payments, an opportunity cost.
- Compare deficits as a **share of GDP**.

## Fiscal policy and objectives

| Objective | Fiscal tool | Chain |
|---|---|---|
| Growth | Infrastructure spending | Lower costs for firms, more capacity, more output |
| Low unemployment | Tax cuts or more spending | More demand, more output, more jobs |
| Price stability | Higher taxes or less spending | Less demand, less pressure on prices |
| Fair distribution | Progressive taxes, benefits | Narrower gap in after-tax income |

**Trade-off:** expansion cuts unemployment but can raise inflation and the deficit; contraction does the reverse.

## Worked reminders

**Average rates.** Bands: 0% to £10,000, 20% to £45,000, 40% above. On £32,000: 22,000 × 0.2 = **£4,400**, average rate **13.75%**.

**Regressive VAT.** £18,000 earner pays £1,620 VAT (**9%**); £90,000 earner pays £5,400 (**6%**).

**Deficit share.** Deficit £45bn, GDP £1,800bn: **2.5%** of GDP.

## Evaluation checklist

- **Opportunity cost**: what else the money could fund.
- **Borrowing**: deficits raise debt and future interest.
- **Time lags**: projects take years.
- **Incentives**: high rates may discourage work, investment and enterprise.
- **Type of unemployment**: demand policy helps cyclical, not structural.
- **State of the economy**: expansion near full capacity mainly raises prices.

## Redistribution: two sides

- **For:** reduces poverty and inequality; low-income households spend more of extra income; funds services.
- **Against:** may weaken incentives; avoidance; skilled workers may move; cost to taxpayers.

## Exam traps

- National insurance is **direct**.
- Progressive is about the **average rate**, not the amount paid.
- A deficit is not the debt.
- Always mention **opportunity cost** when evaluating spending.
- Fiscal = government spending and tax; interest rates are **monetary** policy.

## Self-test

1. Define fiscal policy.
2. State one difference between a direct and an indirect tax.
3. Give two purposes of government spending.
4. A government raises £640bn and spends £610bn. Is the budget in surplus or deficit, and by how much?
5. A deficit is £45bn and GDP is £1,800bn. Express the deficit as a percentage of GDP.
6. Using bands of 0% to £10,000, 20% to £45,000 and 40% above, calculate the tax and average tax rate on £32,000.
7. Classify each as direct or indirect: VAT, corporation tax, fuel duty, national insurance.
8. A person on £18,000 pays £1,620 in VAT and a person on £90,000 pays £5,400. Is VAT progressive or regressive here? Show why.
9. A government spends £4bn on a new hospital. State an opportunity cost.
10. When would a government use expansionary fiscal policy?
11. Give one reason a tax cut may not reduce unemployment.
12. Explain the difference between the budget deficit and the national debt.

### Answers

1. Using **government spending and taxation** to influence the economy and meet objectives.
2. A direct tax is on **income or wealth** paid directly to the government; an indirect tax is on **spending**, collected by sellers.
3. Any two: **health, education, defence, infrastructure, benefits and pensions**.
4. **Surplus of £30bn.**
5. 45 ÷ 1,800 × 100 = **2.5%**.
6. 22,000 × 0.2 = **£4,400**; 4,400 ÷ 32,000 × 100 = **13.75%**.
7. VAT **indirect**; corporation tax **direct**; fuel duty **indirect**; national insurance **direct**.
8. **Regressive**: 9% of the lower income against 6% of the higher income.
9. For example, **schools or roads** that could have been built, or a tax cut not made.
10. In a **downturn**, when demand is weak and unemployment is high.
11. Any one: households **save** the extra income or buy **imports**; unemployment is **structural**; confidence is low.
12. The deficit is the **gap in one year**; the debt is the **total owed** from all past borrowing.
