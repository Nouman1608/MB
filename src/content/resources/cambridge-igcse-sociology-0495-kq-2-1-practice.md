identity?* Parts (a) to (f) follow the six-part shape the syllabus describes for Paper 1 Question 2:

## Questions

**(a)** Define the terms:
- (i) *norms* **[2]**
- (ii) *ascribed status* **[2]**

**(b)** Give **two** examples of customs. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **three** processes used by agencies of socialisation. **[6]**

**(d)** Explain **three** ways the peer group socialises young people. **[6]**

**(e)** Discuss Marxist and feminist views of socialisation. **[8]**

**(f)** Evaluate the view that human behaviour is mainly the result of nurture rather than nature.
**[14]**

### Extra practice (2.1.1 to 2.1.5)

**(g)** Explain **two** ways stereotypes can influence a person's identity. **[4]**

**(h)** Explain the difference between a role and a status. **[4]**

**(i)** Explain **two** limitations of the family as an agency of socialisation. **[4]**

**(j)** Explain the functionalist view of the part education plays in socialisation. **[4]**

**(k)** Explain how religion acts as an agency of secondary socialisation. **[4]**

## Answers and mark guidance

**(a)(i) [2]** Specific rules or expectations about how people should behave in particular situations
(1), for example queuing or greeting elders in a certain way (1).

**(a)(ii) [2]** A social position given at birth rather than earned (1), for example sex, caste or a
royal title (1).

**(b) [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: wedding or funeral rituals; festival meals or gifts; ways of
greeting such as bowing or shaking hands; coming-of-age ceremonies; traditional dress on special
occasions.

**(c) [6]** Three processes, 2 marks each (identify + explain how it socialises):

- **Canalisation** — parents channel children towards gender-typed toys and activities, so children
  learn what is expected of their sex (Oakley, 1972).
- **Hidden curriculum** — school organisation teaches obedience, punctuality and respect for
  hierarchy without formal lessons.
- **Media representations** — repeated images of groups shape what audiences see as normal, for
  example body ideals in advertising.
- **Peer pressure** — friends reward conformity with acceptance and punish difference with ridicule.
- **Religious teachings** — sacred texts and sermons teach moral codes.
- **Role modelling** — people copy admired figures such as parents or celebrities.
- **Workplace training** — induction teaches occupational norms.

**(d) [6]** Three ways, 2 marks each:

- **Acceptance and rejection** — teenagers who follow the group's norms gain belonging; those who do
  not may be teased or excluded, so they adjust their behaviour.
- **Shared tastes and style** — peer groups set norms of fashion, music and language that become part
  of members' identity.
- **Role models within the group** — younger members copy older or popular members.
- **Gender norms** — peers police masculinity and femininity, for example mocking boys who show
  emotion.
- **Online peer groups** — group chats and social media extend peer influence beyond school.

**(e) [8]** Levels: 7–8 a clear, developed account of both perspectives with comparison; 4–6 some
explanation of both or good explanation of one; 1–3 basic points.

- **Marxism:** socialisation transmits capitalist ideology so that inequality seems fair; Althusser
  (1971) saw schools, religion and media as ideological state apparatuses; Bowles and Gintis (1976)
  argued the hidden curriculum produces obedient workers, reproducing social class.
- **Feminism:** gender role socialisation teaches boys masculinity and girls femininity, maintaining
  patriarchy; Oakley's canalisation and manipulation; Sharpe's study of girls' priorities (1976).
- **Comparison:** both are conflict views, seeing socialisation as serving a powerful group, but
  Marxists stress class while feminists stress gender. Credit criticisms, such as both treating
  people as passive, or Sharpe's later findings showing change.

**(f) [14]** Levels: 11–14 balanced, developed and evaluated with a conclusion; 7–10 developed but
less balanced; 4–6 limited; 1–3 basic.

- *For nurture:* isolated children such as "Genie" (found 1970) failed to develop language without
  early socialisation; cultural relativity — norms and gender roles vary between societies (Mead,
  1935); norms change within one society faster than biology could; agencies such as family, school
  and media can be seen shaping behaviour through canalisation, the hidden curriculum and media
  representations.
- *For nature / against the view:* biological maturation sets limits on what children can learn and
  when; some behaviour such as basic reflexes is inborn; feral cases are rare and may involve other
  factors such as disability; Mead's research has been questioned.
- *Conclusion:* most sociologists conclude that biology provides capacities, but how they are
  expressed and what they mean is learned; credit a reasoned judgement.

**(g) [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each:

- **Self-image** — people who are repeatedly stereotyped may come to see themselves that way, for
  example girls told they are "not good at physics" losing confidence.
- **Limiting choices** — stereotypes shape others' expectations, steering people into or away from
  roles and careers.
- **Reaction against** — some people build their identity in opposition to a stereotype.

**(h) [4]** A **status** is a position in society with a level of prestige (1), for example being a
teacher (1). A **role** is the behaviour expected of someone holding that status (1), for example a
teacher being expected to prepare lessons and treat students fairly (1).

**(i) [4]** Two limitations, 2 marks each:

- **Weakening influence** — in adolescence, peers and media may become more influential than parents.
- **Inadequate socialisation** — some families fail to pass on mainstream norms, for example through
  neglect.
- **Diversity of families** — families teach different values, so the family does not produce a
  single shared culture.
- **Competing messages** — children receive conflicting norms from school, religion and media.

**(j) [4]** Functionalists such as Durkheim see education as passing on society's shared values (1),
creating social cohesion and a sense of belonging to the wider society (1). Schools teach children to
follow universal rules that apply to everyone, preparing them for adult roles (1), which helps
maintain value consensus and social order (1).

**(k) [4]** Religion teaches moral codes through **religious teachings**, sacred texts and sermons
(1), for example rules about honesty or family duty (1). Rituals and festivals strengthen a shared
identity among believers (1), and **religious rewards and sanctions**, such as approval from the
community or fear of sin, encourage conformity (1).

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need named sociologists for (c) and (d)?** They are not required, but one accurate reference,
such as Oakley on canalisation, strengthens development.

**How much should I write for (e)?** Two or three developed paragraphs: one on each perspective and a
short comparison.
