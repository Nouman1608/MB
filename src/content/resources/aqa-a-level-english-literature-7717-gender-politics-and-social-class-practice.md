## Section B: an unseen extract and essay skills

**Unseen extract (original prose).** A housekeeper, Mrs Dunn, has worked for a mill owner's family for
twenty years. The owner's wife is giving a dinner.

> "You'll serve from the left, Dunn, as always." Mrs Harcourt did not look up from the place cards.
> She was writing the names of the guests in a hand she had learned at a school in Bath, where girls
> were taught to write so that no one would ever mistake them for clerks. Mrs Dunn had learned to
> write at the mill school, between shifts, from a man who cared only that the letters could be read.
> She could read the place cards upside down. Mr Harcourt, Mr Ashby, Mr Lyle: the men who decided
> what the mill would pay. Their wives' cards said only *Mrs*, followed by their husbands' names, as
> if each woman had come to dinner wearing her husband like a coat.
> "Shall I put the flowers in the centre, madam?"
> "Where else would you put them?"
> Mrs Dunn did not answer. She had been asked a question that was not a question, and she had been in
> service long enough to know the difference.

## Answers

**1.** A system has beneficiaries, justifications and methods of control, so analysing it explains how
power is built and kept [1]; a theme approach tends to reduce the text to a message, whereas AQA's
option is about "issues of power and powerlessness" [1].

**2.** Nora has borrowed the money and been secretly repaying it, showing intelligence and courage [1]; she had to
forge her father's signature because the law did not let a wife borrow without her husband's consent
[1]; so the act that proves her capable is also a crime created by the system that denies her
agency [1].

**3.** Women are assigned ranks by function, such as Wife, Handmaid, Martha and Econowife [1]; each rank
is marked by colour and has a different status [1]; so a woman's gender role decides her class
position, and women of different ranks are divided from each other [1].

**4.** Stephen is trapped in a marriage he cannot afford to end [1]; Bounderby tells him that the law's
remedy is available only to the rich [1]; so the scene exposes a legal system with one rule for those
with money and another for the "Hands" [1].

**5.** The poet's northern working-class accent is corrected as unfit for poetry [1], and the poem
claims his right to use his own voice, turning pronunciation into a contest over who may speak in
literature [1].

**6.** Any eight, [1] each:

- Mrs Harcourt's command "as always" assumes obedience and routine.
- Calling her "Dunn" without a title marks her lower status.
- "Did not look up" shows power through withheld attention.
- The Bath school handwriting shows class marked even in the body's habits.
- "So that no one would ever mistake them for clerks" reveals class anxiety and the policing of
  boundaries.
- The contrast with the mill school "between shifts" shows unequal education.
- Mrs Dunn's reading upside down shows hidden knowledge and capability.
- The list of men "who decided what the mill would pay" links the dinner to economic power.
- The wives' cards erase their names, presenting marriage as absorption.
- "Where else would you put them?" dismisses Mrs Dunn's question and her judgement.
- The final paragraph's "question that was not a question" shows power working through language.

**7.** Mrs Harcourt has class power over Mrs Dunn [1], but she is herself named only through her
husband, as the place cards show [1], so women of both classes are subordinated to men, while class
divides them from each other [1].

**8.** It presents the wives' identities as borrowed and external, something put on for public
display [1], and suggests that marriage gives them status only as an extension of their husbands [1].

**9.** Mrs Dunn's silence is a form of survival learned through long service [1]; her recognition of
the "question that was not a question" shows she understands the power at work [1]; so resistance is
internal and unspoken, which the reader notices even though Mrs Harcourt does not [1].

**10.** Two developments, [2] each for a developed point or [1] for a partial one: show that women
also enforce the regime (the Aunts, Serena Joy), so gender alone does not explain power; show how
class divides women (Wives and Econowives); show that men are also controlled (Guardians, Nick); use
the Historical Notes to discuss who controls the story of oppression.

**11.** Any four, [1] each: a thesis on "divided against each other"; two named texts different from
the Section B text; a paragraph comparing divisions among the powerless (for example, Wives and
Handmaids; Amir and Hassan); a paragraph on solidarity that resists division; contexts for each
text; an alternative interpretation; a comparative judgement.

**12.** A context identified [1] and tied to a specific scene or line [1]: for example, the cost of
divorce in the period links to Stephen Blackpool's interview with Bounderby, explaining why the law
offers him no escape.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating gender or class as a message rather than a system.
- Ignoring the intersection of gender and class.
- Unseen answers that list techniques without an argument about power.
- In Section C, reusing the Section B text.

For full practice, write a timed Section A answer of about an hour on the unseen extract above.
