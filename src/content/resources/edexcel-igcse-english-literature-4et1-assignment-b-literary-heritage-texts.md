This guide focuses on the second half of Component 3 of Pearson Edexcel International GCSE English
Literature (4ET1): **Assignment B, Literary Heritage Texts**. For the rules that apply to both
coursework assignments, start with the
[Component 3 study guide](/resources/igcse-edexcel-english-literature-component-3/).

## What Assignment B covers

Assignment B is **one coursework essay of about 650–800 words on a literary heritage set text**,
marked out of 30: AO1 10, AO2 10 and AO4 10. It is the only part of Component 3 where context is
assessed. The task is set by your teacher, and you may help to choose or shape it. Your centre marks
the work and Pearson moderates a sample.

Under the Issue 3 specification the literary heritage texts are *Romeo and Juliet*, *Macbeth* and *The
Merchant of Venice* (Shakespeare), *Pride and Prejudice* (Jane Austen), *Great Expectations* (Charles
Dickens) and *The Scarlet Letter* (Nathaniel Hawthorne). Lists can change, so always check the
specification for your entry.

| AO | Marks | In a heritage essay this means |
|---|---|---|
| AO1 | 10 | Close knowledge of the whole text and an informed personal response to the task, in a critical style |
| AO2 | 10 | Analysis of language, form and structure and their effects |
| AO4 | 10 | The relationship between the text and the contexts in which it was written and received |

## How Assignment B differs from the exam route

The same texts can be studied for the Component 2 exam, but coursework changes the challenge:

| | Component 2 Section B (exam) | Component 3 Assignment B (coursework) |
|---|---|---|
| Time | About 45 minutes | Weeks of preparation and drafting |
| Question | Chosen from two on the day | A set task known in advance |
| Context | Recalled from memory | Can be researched, but must be selected and acknowledged |
| Length | Whatever you can write well in the time | 650–800 words advisory |
| Main risk | Forgetting context under pressure | Including too much context and squeezing out analysis |

Because you have time to research, the danger is not too little context but **too much of the wrong
kind**. With only about 800 words, every contextual sentence has to earn its place.

## Key ideas

### 1. Choose context that fits the task

Do not research "the Victorians" or "Elizabethan England" in general. Start with the task and ask
which two or three contexts would change how a reader understands your chosen moments. For a task on
city life in *Great Expectations* (the specification's own model), the relevant contexts are about the
growing Victorian city, crime and the law, and money and class: not, for instance, the history of the
railway.

### 2. Use more than one kind of context

The specification names the writer's situation (where relevant), historical setting, social and
cultural attitudes, literary context and reception by different audiences. Top answers often combine
two kinds in one argument, for example a social attitude and how a modern audience responds to it.

### 3. Literary context is often forgotten

Genre and tradition count as context and are often the quickest way to link AO2 and AO4:

| Text | Literary context to consider |
|---|---|
| *Romeo and Juliet* | A tragedy that begins with features of comedy; the sonnet as the language of love |
| *Macbeth* | Tragedy of a hero brought down by a flaw; the soliloquy as a window into the mind |
| *The Merchant of Venice* | A comedy with a dark central plot, often called a problem play |
| *Pride and Prejudice* | Comedy of manners centred on courtship; the narrator's irony |
| *Great Expectations* | A novel of growing up, first published in weekly parts |
| *The Scarlet Letter* | A work Hawthorne called a romance, rich in symbolism |

### 4. Reception is context too

How different audiences respond is part of AO4. A sentence such as "a twenty-first-century audience
may find this harder to accept than Shakespeare's first audience" is valid context, provided you
explain why.

### 5. Acknowledge sources

Coursework must be your own. If an idea or fact comes from a book, website or teacher handout, say so
in the way your centre asks. Essays copied or generated from online material put the whole submission
at risk.

## Worked example 1: building a context map

**Task:** How does Hawthorne present the Puritan community in *The Scarlet Letter*? You must consider
language, form and structure and refer to the context of the novel.

| Moment | Method (AO2) | Context (AO4) |
|---|---|---|
| The crowd at Hester's first public punishment | The narrator's ironic description of the stern spectators | Seventeenth-century Boston, where religious and civil law overlapped |
| The town's changing reading of the letter as Hester serves the poor | The symbol's shifting meaning | Hawthorne, writing in 1850, looking back critically on his ancestors' society |
| Dimmesdale's confession on the scaffold | The scaffold scenes as a structural frame | Public shame as a tool of community control; modern readings of the novel as a critique of moral hypocrisy |

Three rows give three paragraphs, each with a moment, a method and a context. The plan covers
beginning, middle and end, and uses three kinds of context.

## Worked example 2: from Level 3 to Level 5

**Task:** How does Shakespeare present Shylock in *The Merchant of Venice*? You must consider language,
form and structure and refer to the context of the play.

**Level 3 direction:** "Shylock is treated badly by Antonio, who has insulted him in public. In
Shakespeare's time Jewish people faced prejudice. This makes the audience feel sorry for him."

The knowledge is sound, but the context is general and placed next to the point.

**Level 5 direction:** "Shakespeare gives Shylock some of the play's most forceful prose when he lists
the insults he has received, and the rhythm of repeated questions forces the audience to see him as
a person, not a stage villain. Many in an Elizabethan audience, living in a country from which Jewish
people had been expelled centuries earlier, may have expected only a comic figure of greed; a modern
audience, aware of the long history of antisemitism, is more likely to hear the speech as an
accusation against the Christians on stage. Because the same speech ends
in Shylock's demand for revenge, Shakespeare keeps both readings open."

The stronger version links specific language (AO2) to two kinds of context (social attitude and
reception) and builds an argument about the play's double effect (AO1).

## A word budget for 800 words

| Section | Words | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | about 100 | Answer the task directly; name the main context you will use |
| Three paragraphs | about 200 each | Moment, method, context, link to task |
| Conclusion | about 100 | A judgement that draws the contexts together |

If a context sentence could be removed without weakening the paragraph, cut it and use the words for
analysis.

## The coursework process

Plan from the task, gather evidence and context, draft, receive general feedback, revise and submit.
Your teacher may suggest general improvements at draft stage but may not correct the work. It must not
be rewritten after marking, and you and your teacher sign to confirm it is your own.

## Command words

- **How does [writer] present...** Keep the writer's choices at the centre of each paragraph.
- **Explore the ways in which...** Show range across methods and across the text.
- **To what extent...** Weigh a view and reach a judgement; context often helps you decide.

## Common mistakes

- A long opening paragraph of history or biography.
- Context collected for its own sake rather than chosen for the task.
- Only one kind of context, repeated in every paragraph.
- Losing AO2 because context takes over the word count.
- Judging characters by modern values without presenting it as a reading.
- Unacknowledged material from websites or study guides.
