## 1. Fixed and variable costs

A firm's costs are split by how they behave when output changes.

- **Fixed costs** do not change with output in the short run: rent, insurance, loan interest, salaries of permanent managers. They must be paid even if output is zero.
- **Variable costs** change with output: raw materials, energy used in production, wages of hourly staff, packaging.

The **short run** is the period in which at least one factor of production is fixed, usually capital such as buildings and machinery. In the **long run** all factors can be varied, so there are no fixed costs: the firm can move to a larger or smaller site.

Classifying a cost depends on the firm and the time period. Labour on a long contract behaves like a fixed cost in the short run; agency staff hired by the day are variable.

## 2. Total and average cost

| Measure | Formula |
|---|---|
| Total cost (TC) | Total fixed cost + total variable cost |
| Average cost (AC) | TC ÷ output |
| Average fixed cost (AFC) | Fixed cost ÷ output |
| Average variable cost (AVC) | Variable cost ÷ output |

**Worked example 1 (invented Harbour Kayaks).** The firm hires out kayaks. Fixed costs are £1,200 a week (boathouse rent, insurance and the manager's salary). Each hire costs £3 in cleaning, repairs and booking fees.

| Hires a week | Fixed cost (£) | Variable cost (£) | TC (£) | AC (£) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 100 | 1,200 | 300 | 1,500 | 15.00 |
| 200 | 1,200 | 600 | 1,800 | 9.00 |
| 300 | 1,200 | 900 | 2,100 | 7.00 |
| 400 | 1,200 | 1,200 | 2,400 | 6.00 |
| 500 | 1,200 | 1,500 | 2,700 | 5.40 |
| 600 | 1,200 | 1,800 | 3,000 | 5.00 |
| 700 | 1,200 | 2,100 | 3,300 | 4.71 |

AC falls throughout because the £1,200 of fixed cost is **spread** over more hires: AFC is £12 at 100 hires but only about £1.71 at 700. Here AVC is constant at £3, so AC falls towards £3 but never reaches it.

In most firms AVC eventually rises as output approaches the capacity of the fixed factors (staff get in each other's way, machines are overworked), which is why the typical short-run AC curve is U-shaped. The lowest point of the AC curve is the productively efficient output.

## 3. Total and average revenue

- **Total revenue (TR)** = price × quantity sold.
- **Average revenue (AR)** = TR ÷ quantity, which equals the price when every unit sells at the same price. The AR curve is therefore the firm's demand curve.
- **Marginal revenue (MR)** is the change in TR from selling one more unit. When a firm must cut its price to sell more, MR is less than price, because the lower price applies to all the units it sells.

**Worked example 2 (Harbour Kayaks' demand).**

| Price (£) | 10 | 9 | 8 | 7 | 6 | 5 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hires a week | 100 | 200 | 300 | 400 | 500 | 600 | 700 |
| TR (£) | 1,000 | 1,800 | 2,400 | 2,800 | 3,000 | 3,000 | 2,800 |

Each cut of £1 adds 100 hires. TR rises by £800, then £600, £400 and £200, then stops rising and finally falls by £200. Per extra hire, MR is £8, £6, £4, £2, £0 and −£2.

## 4. PED and total revenue

The table shows the AS rule at work. Between £7 and £6, price falls by 14.3% and hires rise by 25%, so PED = 25 ÷ −14.3 = **−1.75**: demand is elastic and TR rises (£2,800 to £3,000). Between £5 and £4, price falls by 20% and hires rise by 16.7%, so PED = **−0.83**: demand is inelastic and TR falls (£3,000 to £2,800).

TR is greatest where demand changes from elastic to inelastic, which is where MR is zero. A firm whose only aim was revenue would choose a price of £5 or £6 here.

## 5. Profit and the firm's objectives

**Profit = TR − TC.** Combining the two tables:

| Price (£) | Hires | TR (£) | TC (£) | Profit (£) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | 100 | 1,000 | 1,500 | −500 |
| 9 | 200 | 1,800 | 1,800 | 0 |
| 8 | 300 | 2,400 | 2,100 | 300 |
| 7 | 400 | 2,800 | 2,400 | **400** |
| 6 | 500 | 3,000 | 2,700 | 300 |
| 5 | 600 | 3,000 | 3,000 | 0 |
| 4 | 700 | 2,800 | 3,300 | −500 |

- **Profit maximisation:** price £7, 400 hires, profit £400.
- **Revenue maximisation:** price £5 or £6, TR £3,000, profit £300 or zero.
- **Covering costs only** (for example a community-run hire scheme): it could charge as little as £5 for 600 hires and still break even.

The profit-maximising output is lower than the revenue-maximising output. Moving from 400 to 500 hires adds £200 of revenue but £300 of cost, so profit falls. Each extra hire is worth producing only while it adds more to revenue than to cost.

Which output a firm actually chooses depends on its objectives. A firm run by its owners may aim for profit; one run by managers whose pay depends on sales may aim for revenue; a social enterprise may aim to serve as many customers as possible while covering costs.

## Annotated model answer

*Question:* "Using the data, explain why Harbour Kayaks' profit falls if it cuts its price from £7 to £6."

> At £7 the firm makes 400 hires, earning TR of £2,800 against TC of £2,400, a profit of £400 *[data]*. Cutting the price to £6 raises hires to 500 because demand is price elastic over this range (PED −1.75), so TR rises by £200 to £3,000 *[links PED to revenue]*. But the extra 100 hires add £300 of variable cost at £3 each *[cost side]*. Because cost rises by more than revenue, profit falls by £100 to £300 *[conclusion with figures]*.

## Common mistakes

- Calling a cost "fixed" because it is large; fixed means it does not vary with output.
- Saying there are fixed costs in the long run.
- Treating AR and TR as the same thing.
- Assuming higher revenue means higher profit.
- Forgetting that a price cut applies to all units, so MR is below price.
- Leaving units (£, hires a week) off answers.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate:** set out TR = P × Q and AC = TC ÷ Q row by row; give units and round money to two decimal places.
- **Explain:** link the cost or revenue idea to the context and complete the chain, for example from PED to the change in TR, then to profit.
- **Evaluate:** when asked whether a firm should change its price, weigh the revenue effect (PED) against the cost effect and the firm's objectives, and reach a judgement.
