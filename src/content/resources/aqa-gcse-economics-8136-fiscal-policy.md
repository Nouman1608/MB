Section 3.2.3.1 of the AQA GCSE Economics (8136) specification has two content points, "Fiscal policy" and "The government budget". The additional guidance asks you to understand "how fiscal policy can affect levels of income and expenditure within the economy", "how fiscal policy can be used to achieve government objectives" and "the meaning of a balanced budget" and the "consequences of operating a budget surplus and deficit". This guide traces how a change in tax or spending spreads through the economy, separates the deficit from the debt with worked numbers, and sets out the limits examiners expect you to weigh.

## What fiscal policy uses

Fiscal policy is the use of **government spending** and **taxation** to influence the economy. In the UK, the Chancellor of the Exchequer sets out tax and spending plans in the Budget, and the independent Office for Budget Responsibility publishes forecasts of the public finances.

Government spending takes three forms:

- **Current spending:** day-to-day costs such as salaries of nurses and teachers, and medicines.
- **Capital spending:** investment in long-lasting assets such as roads, railways, schools and hospitals.
- **Transfer payments:** benefits and the State Pension, which move income from taxpayers to recipients without buying any output.

Taxes are **direct** (on income and profit, such as income tax and corporation tax) or **indirect** (on spending, such as VAT and excise duties).

## How fiscal policy changes income and expenditure

Spending by the government adds to total demand in the economy; taxes take money out of households' and firms' hands.

**Expansionary fiscal policy** (more spending or lower taxes) raises demand.

Government spends more on new rail lines → construction firms win contracts and hire workers → those workers earn wages and spend them in shops and restaurants → those firms sell more and may hire more staff → total income and expenditure rise by more than the first round of spending.

A cut in income tax works through households instead: disposable income rises, so consumer spending rises, and firms respond by producing more.

**Contractionary fiscal policy** (less spending or higher taxes) reduces demand. A rise in VAT raises prices of most goods, so households buy less; firms sell less and may cut output and jobs.

The knock-on effect is weaker when people save much of any extra income, or when they spend it on imports, because that money does not become income for UK firms.

### Automatic and deliberate changes

Some changes happen without any decision. In a recession, unemployment rises, so benefit spending increases and income tax and VAT receipts fall. This supports spending just when demand is weak. In a boom the reverse happens. Other changes are **deliberate**, announced in the Budget, such as a new road programme or a change to income tax rates.

## Using fiscal policy to meet objectives

| Objective | Fiscal policy | How it helps |
|---|---|---|
| Economic growth | More capital spending on infrastructure; lower taxes | Raises demand now and productive capacity later |
| Full employment | More government spending, lower taxes | More demand means firms need more workers |
| Price stability | Higher taxes or lower spending | Less demand eases demand-pull inflation |
| Balance of payments | Higher taxes | Lower spending on imports narrows a current account deficit |
| Fairer distribution of income | Progressive taxes, benefits, free public services | Narrows the gap between high and low incomes |
| The environment | Taxes on pollution; subsidies for clean energy | Changes behaviour of producers and consumers |

The same policy can help one objective and harm another. Expansionary policy that reduces unemployment can raise inflation and draw in imports; contractionary policy that cuts inflation can raise unemployment.

## The government budget

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Balanced budget | Government spending equals government revenue |
| Budget surplus | Revenue is greater than spending |
| Budget deficit | Spending is greater than revenue, so the government borrows the difference |
| National debt | The total the government owes from all past borrowing |

### Worked example: deficit and debt (invented)

Country V's government receives 1,050 billion units in revenue and spends 1,134 billion.

- Budget balance = 1,050 − 1,134 = **−84**, a deficit of 84 billion.
- With GDP of 2,800 billion, the deficit is 84 ÷ 2,800 × 100 = **3.0%** of GDP.

Its national debt starts at 1,800 billion. If it runs deficits of 84, 70 and 56 billion in three successive years, debt rises to 1,800 + 84 + 70 + 56 = **2,010 billion**. The deficit fell each year, but the debt still grew, because any deficit adds to the debt. At an average interest rate of 4%, yearly interest on that debt is 0.04 × 2,010 = **80.4 billion**, money that cannot be spent on public services.

## Consequences of a deficit and a surplus

**A budget deficit**

- In a recession, a deficit supports demand and protects jobs while households and firms cut back.
- It adds to the national debt, so interest payments rise.
- A long-running deficit may require tax rises or spending cuts later, which can hold back growth at that point.
- If it is used for capital spending, such as transport links, it can raise future output and tax revenue.
- If demand is already strong, extra borrowing and spending can cause inflation.

**A budget surplus**

- Debt can be repaid, reducing future interest payments.
- It gives the government room to borrow in a future downturn.
- It takes more out of the economy in tax than it returns in spending, which reduces demand and can slow growth or raise unemployment.
- It may be achieved by cutting public services or investment, with long-term costs.

The consequence depends on **the state of the economy**: a deficit is more defensible in a recession, and a surplus is more defensible in a boom.

## Limits of fiscal policy

- **Time lags:** spending projects take time to plan and build; tax changes usually take effect at set dates.
- **Uncertain responses:** households may save a tax cut rather than spend it, especially if they are worried about jobs.
- **Leakage into imports:** extra spending may go on foreign goods.
- **Political pressure:** tax rises and spending cuts are unpopular, so contractionary policy may be delayed.
- **Debt:** high existing debt limits how much more a government can borrow.

## Annotated model answer

*Question (original):* Explain how an increase in government capital spending could help achieve economic growth. **[4]**

*Model answer:* Capital spending, such as building new railways, raises total demand directly because construction firms receive contracts [first effect]. They hire workers, who spend their wages, so other firms' sales and output rise too [knock-on effect on income and spending]. In the long run the railway cuts journey times and transport costs for businesses [second, supply-side effect], so firms can produce more, which raises real GDP over time [links to growth].

## Common mistakes

- Treating fiscal policy as interest rates; that is monetary policy.
- Confusing the deficit (one year's gap) with the debt (the running total).
- Saying a falling deficit means falling debt.
- Explaining only the first effect of a spending rise and not the knock-on spending.
- Judging a deficit as "always bad" without asking whether the economy is in a recession or a boom.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate** questions: show revenue minus spending, keep the sign, then state deficit or surplus.
- **Explain** questions: build a chain from the tax or spending change to households' income, then spending, then output and jobs.
- **Evaluate** questions: weigh the effect on the target objective against a conflict with another objective, and use the state of the economy in the case to judge.
