These questions cover section 3.5 Processing Results of Unit 3: Practical Skills in Physics I, an International AS (IAS) unit, in the Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Subsidiary/Advanced Level in Physics (YPH11) specification, Issue 3. As the specification does not expect compounding of percentage uncertainties, none is asked for. ## Questions

**1.** A voltmeter reads 4.8 V and an ammeter reads 0.250 A for the same component. Calculate its resistance, giving your answer to a suitable number of significant figures. **[2]**

**2.** Calculate the percentage uncertainty in each reading, using half the resolution of the instrument.

**(a)** A rule with 1 mm divisions is used to measure a length of 18.5 cm. **[2]**
**(b)** A micrometer with resolution 0.01 mm is used to measure a wire of diameter 0.82 mm. **[2]**

**3.** A student times 10 oscillations of a mass on a string. Five repeats, in seconds, are 14.8, 15.1, 14.9, 15.3 and 14.7.

**(a)** Calculate the mean time for 10 oscillations and the period. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage uncertainty in the mean time, using the half range. **[2]**

**4.** A student plots extension against force on a grid 16 cm wide and 12 cm tall. The force runs from 0 to 9.0 N and the extension from 0 to 126 mm.

**(a)** State a suitable scale for each axis, with extension on the horizontal axis. **[2]**
**(b)** The student suggests 7 mm per centimetre for the extension axis. Give two reasons why this is a poor choice. **[2]**

**5.** A spring is loaded and the extension x is measured. The best-fit line of force F (in N) against x (in mm) passes through the points (10, 2.0) and (60, 12.0).

**(a)** Calculate the gradient of the line, with its unit. **[2]**
**(b)** Convert the gradient to N m⁻¹. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why points this far apart were chosen. **[1]**
**(d)** The line also passes through (0, 0). State what this shows about F and x. **[1]**

**6.** A ball is released from rest and the distance fallen h is measured for different fall times t.

| t / s | 0.20 | 0.30 | 0.40 | 0.50 | 0.60 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| h / m | 0.22 | 0.47 | 0.81 | 1.25 | 1.79 |

**(a)** Calculate t² for each time, to a suitable number of significant figures. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why a graph of h against t² is expected to be a straight line. **[1]**
**(c)** The best-fit line of h against t² passes through (0.05, 0.27) and (0.35, 1.74). Determine the gradient and use it to find the acceleration of free fall. **[3]**
**(d)** The line meets the h-axis at +0.025 m. Suggest one cause. **[1]**

**7.** A student measures the terminal potential difference V of a cell for several currents I. The best-fit line of V against I passes through (0, 1.52) and (0.50, 0.82), with V in volts and I in amperes.

**(a)** State what the intercept on the V-axis represents and give its value. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the gradient, with its unit. **[2]**
**(c)** Use V = ε − Ir to find the internal resistance of the cell. **[1]**
**(d)** A classmate says the line should be forced through the origin. Explain why this is wrong. **[1]**

**8.** A micrometer is closed with nothing between the jaws and reads +0.02 mm. A wire is then measured and the reading is 0.48 mm.

**(a)** Name the type of error and give the correct diameter. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why repeating the reading and taking a mean does not remove this error. **[1]**
**(c)** Give one source of random error when timing by hand, and one way to reduce its effect. **[1]**

**9.** A student investigates how the period of a pendulum depends on its length. Each period is found by timing a single oscillation with a hand stopwatch. The student starts and stops the watch as the bob passes any convenient point. Only three lengths, between 0.30 m and 0.40 m, are used. The length is measured with a rule held by hand. Suggest five realistic modifications, each with the reason it helps. **[5]**

**10.** A student measures the Young modulus of a wire of length 2.00 m. Five micrometer readings of its diameter, in mm, are 0.46, 0.47, 0.45, 0.46 and 0.46. The best-fit line of extension e against load F passes through (0, 0) and (12.0 N, 0.75 mm).

**(a)** Calculate the mean diameter, the half range and the percentage uncertainty in the diameter. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the cross-sectional area of the wire. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the gradient of the line in m N⁻¹. **[1]**
**(d)** Use E = FL / (Ae) to find the Young modulus. **[2]**
**(e)** The metre rule used for the length has 1 mm divisions. Compare its percentage uncertainty with that of the diameter and suggest one improvement. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** R = V / I = 4.8 / 0.250 = 19.2 Ω [1]. The voltage has 2 s.f., so the answer is **19 Ω** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Give the final answer to the s.f. of the least precise value, and keep the extra digit only in the working.

**2. (a)** Uncertainty = 0.5 mm [1]; 0.5 / 185 × 100 = **0.27%** [1].
**(b)** Uncertainty = 0.005 mm [1]; 0.005 / 0.82 × 100 = **0.61%** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Write the uncertainty first and then the percentage. Using the whole resolution, or forgetting to convert 18.5 cm to 185 mm, is a slip that changes the answer.

**3. (a)** Mean = 74.8 / 5 = 14.96 s [1]. Period = 14.96 / 10 = **1.50 s** [1].
**(b)** Half range = (15.3 − 14.7) / 2 = 0.3 s [1]. 0.3 / 14.96 × 100 = **2.0%** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show the largest and smallest values you used, then divide by 2.

**4. (a)** Extension: 10 mm per 1 cm, so 126 mm takes 12.6 cm of the 16 cm [1]. Force: 1 N per 1 cm, so 9.0 N takes 9.0 cm of the 12 cm [1].
**(b)** 126 / 7 = 18 cm, which is longer than the 16 cm grid, so the points do not fit [1]. Steps of 7 are awkward to subdivide and read, so plotting is slow and error-prone [1].
*Examiner insight:* A scale answer is stronger when you state how much of the grid the data fill.

**5. (a)** Gradient = (12.0 − 2.0) / (60 − 10) [1] = **0.200 N mm⁻¹** [1].
**(b)** 0.200 N per 10⁻³ m = **200 N m⁻¹** [1].
**(c)** A long baseline means a reading error of a fraction of a small square is a small fraction of the change, so the gradient is more reliable [1].
**(d)** The line through the origin shows F is directly proportional to x [1].
*Examiner insight:* Include the unit in the gradient before converting. Converting mm to m in the wrong direction, giving 0.0002, is an easy error to make.

**6. (a)** **0.040, 0.090, 0.16, 0.25, 0.36 s²** (2 s.f.) [1].
**(b)** From rest, h = ½gt², which has the form y = mx with y = h and x = t² [1].
**(c)** Gradient = (1.74 − 0.27) / (0.35 − 0.05) [1] = 4.90 m s⁻² [1]. The gradient is g/2, so g = 2 × 4.90 = **9.80 m s⁻²** [1].
**(d)** A systematic error, for example the timer started a little after release, or h was measured from a point 2.5 cm above the true release point [1].
*Examiner insight:* After finding the gradient, say what it equals. Stopping at 4.90 without doubling it leaves the answer incomplete.

**7. (a)** The intercept is the emf of the cell [1], **1.52 V** [1].
**(b)** Gradient = (0.82 − 1.52) / (0.50 − 0) [1] = **−1.40 V A⁻¹** (that is, −1.40 Ω) [1].
**(c)** V = ε − Ir, so the gradient is −r and **r = 1.40 Ω** [1].
**(d)** When I = 0 the terminal pd equals the emf, which is not zero [1].
*Examiner insight:* Keep the minus sign in the gradient, then say that r is positive.

**8. (a)** A **systematic (zero) error** [1]; correct diameter = 0.48 − 0.02 = **0.46 mm** [1].
**(b)** Each reading is out by the same offset, so the mean carries that offset as well [1].
**(c)** Reaction time varies from one timing to the next [1]; repeating and taking a mean, or using a light gate, reduces its effect. Credit one source with a matching remedy.
*Examiner insight:* Part (c) needs a source and a remedy that fit each other.

**9.** Any five of these, each with its reason, one mark each:
- Time 20 oscillations and divide by 20, so the reaction-time uncertainty is a smaller percentage of the total [1].
- Use a fiducial marker at the centre of the swing as the start and stop point, so the same point is used each time [1].
- Repeat each timing and take a mean, to reduce random error [1].
- Use many more lengths over a wider range, such as 0.20 m to 1.00 m, so a trend is clear on the graph [1].
- Clamp the rule vertically, check with a set square, and read at eye level to reduce parallax [1].
*Examiner insight:* "Use more accurate equipment" gains nothing. Name the change, and say which error it reduces.

**10. (a)** Mean = 2.30 / 5 = **0.460 mm** [1]; half range = (0.47 − 0.45) / 2 = **0.010 mm** [1]; 0.010 / 0.460 × 100 = **2.2%** [1].
**(b)** A = π × (0.230 × 10⁻³)² [1] = **1.66 × 10⁻⁷ m²** [1].
**(c)** Gradient = 0.75 × 10⁻³ / 12.0 = **6.25 × 10⁻⁵ m N⁻¹** [1].
**(d)** E = FL / (Ae), so e / F = L / (AE) and E = L / (A × gradient) [1] = 2.00 / (1.66 × 10⁻⁷ × 6.25 × 10⁻⁵) = **1.93 × 10¹¹ Pa** [1].
**(e)** Rule: 0.5 / 2000 × 100 = 0.025%, far smaller than the 2.2% in the diameter [1]. Improve the diameter by measuring at several points along the wire and at right angles to each other, and averaging [1].
*Examiner insight:* Convert mm to m before using the formula. In part (e) a comparison of two figures is more convincing than a statement that one is larger.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving a final answer with more significant figures than the data support.
- Dividing the range by 2 in the wrong place, or forgetting to halve it at all.
- Using the resolution, not half the resolution, as the uncertainty of one reading.
- Mixing millimetres and metres in gradients and areas.
- Gradient triangles that are small, or that use plotted points not on the line.
- Stopping at the gradient and not equating it to the physical quantity.
- Calling an offset or intercept random error when it is systematic.
- Vague improvements that name no instrument and no error. Missing units on axes and gradients.
