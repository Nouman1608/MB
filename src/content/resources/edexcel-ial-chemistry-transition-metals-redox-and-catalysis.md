## Vanadium: four oxidation states (17.18-17.19)

| Ion in acid | Name | Oxidation number of V | Colour |
|---|---|---|---|
| VO₂⁺ | dioxovanadium(V) | +5 | yellow |
| VO²⁺ | oxovanadium(IV) | +4 | blue |
| V³⁺ | vanadium(III) | +3 | green |
| V²⁺ | vanadium(II) | +2 | violet |

Zinc in acid takes vanadium from +5 down to +2 one step at a time. While yellow VO₂⁺ and blue VO²⁺ are both present, the solution looks green, so green appears twice: once as a mixture, then as V³⁺.

Use these electrode potentials on this page:

| Half-equation | E⦵ / V |
|---|---|
| VO₂⁺ + 2H⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ VO²⁺ + H₂O | +1.00 |
| VO²⁺ + 2H⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ V³⁺ + H₂O | +0.34 |
| V³⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ V²⁺ | −0.26 |
| Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Zn | −0.76 |
| Sn⁴⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Sn²⁺ | +0.15 |

A reducing agent can push vanadium down one step only if E⦵cell = E⦵(vanadium couple) − E⦵(reductant couple) is positive. Zinc gives +1.76 V, +1.10 V and +0.50 V for the three steps, so it reaches V²⁺.

### Worked example 1: how far does tin(II) go?

Acidified ammonium vanadate(V) is shaken with excess Sn²⁺ ions.

1. +5 to +4: E⦵cell = 1.00 − 0.15 = +0.85 V. Feasible.
2. +4 to +3: E⦵cell = 0.34 − 0.15 = +0.19 V. Feasible.
3. +3 to +2: E⦵cell = −0.26 − 0.15 = −0.41 V. Not feasible.
4. The final species is V³⁺, so the solution ends green.
5. Equation for step 2, with electrons balanced (two VO²⁺ per Sn²⁺):
   2VO²⁺ + 4H⁺ + Sn²⁺ → 2V³⁺ + 2H₂O + Sn⁴⁺

A positive E⦵cell shows feasibility only. It says nothing about rate.

## Chromium: dichromate(VI), Cr³⁺ and Cr²⁺ (17.20-17.21)

**Reduction with zinc in acid.** Orange Cr₂O₇²⁻ is reduced first to green Cr³⁺, then to blue Cr²⁺. Data: Cr₂O₇²⁻/Cr³⁺ +1.33 V; Cr³⁺/Cr²⁺ −0.41 V.

Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 3Zn → 2Cr³⁺ + 7H₂O + 3Zn²⁺   E⦵cell = 1.33 − (−0.76) = +2.09 V

2Cr³⁺ + Zn → 2Cr²⁺ + Zn²⁺   E⦵cell = −0.41 − (−0.76) = +0.35 V

Keep Cr²⁺ away from air: with O₂/H₂O at +1.23 V, oxygen oxidises it back to Cr³⁺ (E⦵cell = +1.64 V).

**Oxidation of Cr³⁺ with hydrogen peroxide.** Add excess NaOH(aq) to Cr³⁺(aq): the grey-green precipitate redissolves to give dark green [Cr(OH)₆]³⁻. Warm with H₂O₂ and the solution turns yellow as chromate(VI) forms:

2[Cr(OH)₆]³⁻ + 3H₂O₂ → 2CrO₄²⁻ + 2OH⁻ + 8H₂O

Alkaline conditions matter. Use these values: CrO₄²⁻ + 4H₂O + 3e⁻ ⇌ [Cr(OH)₆]³⁻ + 2OH⁻, E⦵ = −0.13 V; H₂O₂ + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2OH⁻, E⦵ = +0.88 V. E⦵cell = 0.88 − (−0.13) = +1.01 V. In acid, the Cr(VI)/Cr(III) potential is far more positive (+1.33 V): chromium(III) is much harder to oxidise, and acidified dichromate(VI) oxidises hydrogen peroxide instead. So boil off leftover H₂O₂ first, then acidify: the yellow solution turns orange as dichromate(VI) forms.

**The dichromate/chromate equilibrium (17.21).**

Cr₂O₇²⁻ + H₂O ⇌ 2CrO₄²⁻ + 2H⁺
orange ⇌ yellow

Adding OH⁻ removes H⁺, so the position moves right and the colour goes yellow. Adding acid moves it left, back to orange. Chromium is +6 on both sides, so this is an acid-base equilibrium, not redox.

## Aqua ions with NaOH(aq) and NH₃(aq) (17.22-17.23)

Both reagents first remove H⁺ from coordinated water molecules (deprotonation), giving a neutral hydroxide precipitate. What happens in excess is the part to learn.

| Ion (solution colour) | Few drops NaOH or NH₃ | Excess NaOH | Excess NH₃ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cr³⁺ (green) | grey-green ppt | dissolves: dark green [Cr(OH)₆]³⁻ | dissolves: purple [Cr(NH₃)₆]³⁺ |
| Mn²⁺ (very pale pink) | off-white ppt, darkens to brown in air | no change | no change |
| Fe²⁺ (pale green) | green ppt, turns brown at the surface in air | no change | no change |
| Fe³⁺ (yellow-brown) | red-brown ppt | no change | no change |
| Co²⁺ (pink) | blue ppt | no change | dissolves: straw-coloured [Co(NH₃)₆]²⁺, darkens in air |
| Ni²⁺ (green) | green ppt | no change | dissolves: blue [Ni(NH₃)₆]²⁺ |
| Cu²⁺ (blue) | pale blue ppt | no change | dissolves: deep blue [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺ |
| Zn²⁺ (colourless) | white ppt | dissolves: colourless [Zn(OH)₄]²⁻ | dissolves: colourless [Zn(NH₃)₄]²⁺ |

The darkening of Mn(OH)₂, Fe(OH)₂ and the cobalt ammine is oxidation by air to a higher oxidation state.

### Three kinds of equation (17.23)

**Deprotonation** (NaOH or a little NH₃, which acts as a base):
[Mn(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → [Mn(OH)₂(H₂O)₄] + 2H₂O
[Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 2NH₃ → [Fe(OH)₂(H₂O)₄] + 2NH₄⁺

**Amphoteric behaviour**: a hydroxide that reacts with both acid and alkali.
[Zn(OH)₂(H₂O)₄] + 2H⁺ → [Zn(H₂O)₆]²⁺
[Zn(OH)₂(H₂O)₄] + 2OH⁻ → [Zn(OH)₄]²⁻ + 4H₂O
[Cr(OH)₃(H₂O)₃] + 3OH⁻ → [Cr(OH)₆]³⁻ + 3H₂O

**Ligand exchange** in excess ammonia (NH₃ now acts as a ligand):
[Ni(OH)₂(H₂O)₄] + 6NH₃ → [Ni(NH₃)₆]²⁺ + 2OH⁻ + 4H₂O

A neutral precipitate has as many OH⁻ ligands as the metal's charge.

## Ligand exchange and colour change (17.24)

**Copper with ammonia.** Pale blue [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ gives a pale blue precipitate, [Cu(OH)₂(H₂O)₄], then a deep blue solution:

[Cu(OH)₂(H₂O)₄] + 4NH₃ → [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺ + 2OH⁻ + 2H₂O

Overall, four NH₃ replace four of the six water ligands of [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺. Coordination number stays 6.

**Chloride ligands.** Concentrated hydrochloric acid supplies a high Cl⁻ concentration:

[Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 4Cl⁻ ⇌ [CuCl₄]²⁻ + 6H₂O   (blue to yellow, often seen as green part-way)
[Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 4Cl⁻ ⇌ [CoCl₄]²⁻ + 6H₂O   (pink to blue)

Cl⁻ is larger than H₂O, so only four fit: the shape changes from octahedral to tetrahedral and the coordination number from 6 to 4. Both colour changes come from a change of ligand and coordination number. Adding water reverses them.

## Why chelating ligands win (17.25)

When a bidentate or hexadentate ligand replaces monodentate ones, the number of particles in solution rises. For example, [Mn(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + EDTA⁴⁻ → [Mn(EDTA)]²⁻ + 6H₂O goes from 2 particles to 7, so ΔSsystem is large and positive. The bonds broken and made are similar metal-ligand bonds, so ΔH is small and ΔSsurroundings (= −ΔH/T) is small too. ΔStotal is therefore positive, and since ΔStotal = R ln K, K is large: the chelated complex is more stable.

### Worked example 2: an endothermic substitution that still happens

Use these values for the Mn²⁺/EDTA⁴⁻ reaction above at 298 K: ΔH = +4.0 kJ mol⁻¹, ΔSsystem = +190 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.

1. ΔSsurroundings = −ΔH/T = −4000/298 = −13.4 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
2. ΔStotal = 190 + (−13.4) = **+176.6 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹**.
3. Positive, so the reaction is feasible even though it is endothermic. The entropy gain of the system drives it.

## Catalysis (17.26-17.32)

Transition metals and their compounds catalyse reactions because they have variable oxidation numbers and can form bonds to reactant molecules.

**Heterogeneous catalysts** (17.27) are not in the same phase as the reactants, and the chemistry takes place on the catalyst surface: reactants adsorb, their bonds weaken, they react, and products desorb.

**V₂O₅ in the Contact process** (17.28), for 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃:

SO₂ + V₂O₅ → SO₃ + V₂O₄   (V: +5 to +4)
V₂O₄ + ½O₂ → V₂O₅   (V: +4 to +5)

V₂O₅ is regenerated each cycle.

**Catalytic converters** (17.29) remove CO and NO from engine exhaust:

2CO + 2NO → 2CO₂ + N₂

CO and NO adsorb onto the metal surface (platinum, rhodium or palladium on a ceramic honeycomb), which weakens their bonds so they react. CO₂ and N₂ then desorb, freeing the surface. The honeycomb gives a large surface area for a small mass of metal.

**Homogeneous catalysts** (17.30) are in the same phase as the reactants. The reaction goes via an intermediate species, giving two steps, each with a lower activation energy than the uncatalysed route.

**Fe²⁺ with peroxodisulfate and iodide** (17.31). S₂O₈²⁻ + 2I⁻ → 2SO₄²⁻ + I₂ is slow: both reactants carry negative charges and repel each other, which raises the activation energy. With Fe²⁺:

S₂O₈²⁻ + 2Fe²⁺ → 2SO₄²⁻ + 2Fe³⁺   E⦵cell = 2.01 − 0.77 = +1.24 V
2Fe³⁺ + 2I⁻ → 2Fe²⁺ + I₂   E⦵cell = 0.77 − 0.54 = +0.23 V

Each step involves oppositely charged ions. Fe³⁺ is the intermediate and Fe²⁺ is regenerated. Fe³⁺ works too, with the steps reversed.

**Mn²⁺ as an autocatalyst** (17.32). In 2MnO₄⁻ + 16H⁺ + 5C₂O₄²⁻ → 2Mn²⁺ + 10CO₂ + 8H₂O, the product Mn²⁺ catalyses the reaction. Mn²⁺ is oxidised to Mn³⁺ by MnO₄⁻, then Mn³⁺ oxidises C₂O₄²⁻ and returns to Mn²⁺. A plot of [MnO₄⁻] against time falls slowly at first, then steeply as Mn²⁺ builds up, then levels off as reactants run out.

## Core Practical 14: preparing a complex (17.33)

The specification names it "the preparation of a transition metal complex". A preparation that links to 17.20 is chromium(II) ethanoate:

1. Dissolve potassium dichromate(VI) in water and add zinc granules and concentrated hydrochloric acid.
2. Limit air entry. The colour goes orange, then green, then blue (Cr²⁺).
3. Push the blue solution into saturated sodium ethanoate solution. A red precipitate of chromium(II) ethanoate forms.
4. Filter quickly under reduced pressure and wash with water that has been boiled to remove oxygen, then ethanol.

### Worked example 3: percentage yield

1.47 g of K₂Cr₂O₇ (Mr 294.2) gives 1.20 g of Cr₂(CH₃COO)₄(H₂O)₂ (Mr 376.0). One K₂Cr₂O₇ supplies two Cr, which is one formula unit of product.

1. n(K₂Cr₂O₇) = 1.47/294.2 = 4.997 × 10⁻³ mol.
2. Theoretical mass = 4.997 × 10⁻³ × 376.0 = 1.879 g.
3. Yield = 1.20/1.879 × 100 = **63.9%**.

Losses come from air oxidation of Cr²⁺, product left in solution and transfer losses.

## Common errors

- Writing VO²⁺ for +5 or VO₂⁺ for +4. Count: in VO₂⁺, V + 2(−2) = +1, so V is +5.
- Stopping zinc at Cr³⁺ or V³⁺ without checking the next E⦵ step.
- Calling Cr₂O₇²⁻ ⇌ CrO₄²⁻ a redox change.
- Saying excess NaOH dissolves Cu(OH)₂. Only the Cr and Zn hydroxides dissolve in excess NaOH.
- Saying [CuCl₄]²⁻ keeps coordination number 6.
- Explaining the chelate effect by "stronger bonds" rather than ΔSsystem.
- Describing a heterogeneous catalyst without both adsorption and desorption.
