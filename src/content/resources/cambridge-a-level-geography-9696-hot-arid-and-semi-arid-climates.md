## Distribution: a pattern with reasons

Hot deserts and their semi-arid margins form a broken belt on either side of the tropics.

- **Subtropical deserts** under the descending limb of the Hadley cell: the Sahara, Arabian, Kalahari and much of the Australian desert.
- **West-coast deserts** reinforced by cold currents, which extend unusually close to the equator: the Atacama-Peruvian desert (Humboldt or Peru Current) and the Namib (Benguela Current).
- **Rain shadow and continental deserts** where mountains or distance block moist air: the Patagonian desert east of the Andes and the Great Basin behind the Sierra Nevada. The Thar desert of India and Pakistan lies at the dry western margin of the South Asian monsoon.
- **Semi-arid belts** fringe the hot deserts: the Sahel to the south of the Sahara, the margins of the Kalahari, and much of interior Australia and the south-western USA.

The broad rule is that aridity is greatest where two or more causes overlap. Exceptions repay attention: east coasts at the same latitude, such as eastern Australia or south-east Brazil, are wetter because onshore trade winds bring moist air from warm seas.

## Defining aridity

Rainfall alone does not define a desert, because the same rainfall is more effective in a cool place than a hot one. Definitions therefore compare water supply with demand.

- **UNEP aridity index (P/PET):** precipitation divided by potential evapotranspiration. Hyper-arid below 0.05, arid 0.05-0.20, semi-arid 0.20-0.50.
- **Köppen classification:** dry climates (group B) are defined by a threshold of rainfall that rises with mean temperature. **BWh** is hot desert and **BSh** is hot steppe (semi-arid). The letter *h* indicates a hot climate.
- **Practical definitions** often use about 250 mm of rainfall a year for arid and about 250-500 mm for semi-arid. They are simple but ignore evaporation.

**Worked comparison.** A station with 300 mm of rain and a PET of 1,800 mm has P/PET of 0.17, so it is arid. A cooler station with the same rainfall but a PET of 600 mm has 0.5, at the semi-arid upper limit. The same rainfall gives different aridity.

## Causes of aridity, examined more closely

**1. Pressure and wind systems.** In the Hadley cell, air that has risen and lost most of its moisture at the ITCZ moves poleward at high level and sinks at about 20-30°. Sinking air is compressed and warms at the dry adiabatic lapse rate, so its relative humidity falls. This produces a **subsidence inversion**, a warm layer aloft that caps convection. Clouds can form beneath it but rarely grow tall enough to produce rain. The trade winds blowing out of the subtropical highs are dry over land. In semi-arid margins, the seasonal migration of the ITCZ brings a short wet season when it moves overhead and a long dry season when the subtropical high returns. In the Sahel, this gives rain mainly between about June and September.

**2. Cold ocean currents.** Along west coasts, cold currents and **upwelling** of deep water cool the lowest layer of air. Cool air beneath warmer air is stable, so it does not rise. Moisture condenses as **fog** or low stratus instead of rain. The Namib and Atacama fogs supply moisture that some plants and animals use, yet rainfall remains extremely low.

**3. Rain shadow.** Moist air forced over mountains cools adiabatically, condenses and releases rain on the windward side. On the lee side it descends and warms at the dry rate, so it arrives warmer and drier than it started. The effect is strongest where high ranges lie across the path of prevailing winds.

**4. Continentality** (a contributing factor): distance from the sea means air has lost moisture by the time it arrives, as in parts of interior Australia.

## Key feature: temperature variation

**Diurnal range is large** because of the energy budget:

- By day, clear skies and dry air let most shortwave radiation reach the ground. Little water is available for evaporation, so little energy is used as latent heat; most heats the ground and the air above it.
- By night, the lack of cloud and water vapour lets longwave radiation escape freely, so the surface cools rapidly.

The air diurnal range often exceeds 20°C, and rock and sand surfaces vary much more than the air. Frost can occur on winter nights in deserts away from the equator.

**Seasonal range** increases with latitude and distance from the sea. Deserts near 30° have hot summers and mild or cool winters; those near the tropics vary less.

## Key feature: precipitation variation

Desert rainfall is not only low but **highly variable**:

- **Interannual variability:** the coefficient of variation of annual rainfall (standard deviation ÷ mean × 100) rises as mean rainfall falls. In very dry places, one storm can deliver more than the "average" year's rain, while several years pass with almost none.
- **Spatial variability:** convective storms are local, so one valley floods while the next stays dry.
- **Seasonal concentration:** semi-arid areas have short wet seasons linked to the ITCZ or to winter depressions on their poleward margins.
- **Intensity:** rain often falls in short, intense bursts, so much runs off rather than soaking in. Some evaporates before it reaches the ground, visible as **virga**.

## Key feature: high wind energy

Deserts are **high wind energy environments** because:

- strong daytime heating creates steep temperature and pressure gradients and vigorous convection, so gusty winds develop by day;
- sparse vegetation means the ground surface offers little friction or shelter;
- dry, loose sediment is easily lifted.

Named winds illustrate this. The **harmattan** is a dry north-easterly wind blowing from the Sahara across West Africa, roughly between November and March, carrying dust far over the Gulf of Guinea. **Haboobs** are dust storms driven by the cold outflow from collapsing thunderstorms, known in Sudan and the south-western USA. The **shamal** is a north-westerly wind over Iraq and the Gulf that raises dust storms.

## Worked example

*Explain why the diurnal temperature range is greater in hot deserts than in humid tropical environments. [5]*

> Desert skies are usually clear, so more incoming shortwave radiation reaches the surface by day [1]. Dry ground has little water to evaporate, so less energy is used as latent heat and more heats the surface [1]. At night, the lack of cloud and water vapour allows longwave radiation to escape freely [1], so the surface cools rapidly [1]. In humid tropical areas, cloud and humid air reduce both daytime heating and night-time heat loss, narrowing the range [1].

## Common mistakes

- Defining a desert by rainfall alone with no reference to evaporation.
- Saying sinking air is "cold"; it warms as it descends.
- Treating cold currents as causing rain shadow; they cause stability and fog.
- Describing rainfall as "low" without variability and intensity.
- Explaining the diurnal range without both daytime and night-time energy flows.

## Exam technique

When given a climate graph, **describe** with figures: annual total, wettest month, range of temperature. Then **explain** by naming the cause of aridity that fits the location. For **assess** questions on the relative importance of causes, weigh them for specific deserts, since most have more than one cause.
