## Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Monetary policy | Use of interest rates, the money supply, quantitative easing and the exchange rate to influence AD and meet macro objectives |
| Policy rate | The rate set by the central bank, which commercial rates follow |
| Money supply | The quantity of money and credit in the economy |
| Quantitative easing (QE) | The central bank creates electronic money to buy assets, usually government bonds, from financial institutions |
| Liquidity trap | Rates so low that further cuts fail to raise spending, as people hold cash |
| Inflation rate targeting | A published numerical inflation target that guides policy |
| Symmetric target | Misses above and below count equally |
| Asymmetric target | One direction of miss counts as worse, for example a ceiling |
| Real interest rate | Nominal rate adjusted for inflation |

## Formulas

| Quantity | Formula |
|---|---|
| Inflation rate | change in index ÷ old index × 100 |
| Real interest rate (approx.) | nominal rate − inflation rate |
| Real interest rate (exact) | (1 + nominal rate) ÷ (1 + inflation rate) − 1 |
| Bond yield | coupon/price × 100 |
| Percentage change in exchange rate | (new − old)/old × 100 |
| Foreign price of an export | home price × units of foreign currency per home unit |
| Home price of an import | foreign price ÷ units of foreign currency per home unit |
| Multiplier | 1/marginal propensity to withdraw |
| Final change in output | initial change in AD × multiplier |

## Quick worked reminders

*Inflation.* An index rises from 108.0 to 110.7. Inflation = 2.7/108.0 × 100 = 2.5%.

*Real rate.* Nominal 3.5% and inflation 2.0%. Approximate real rate = 1.5%. Exact = 1.035/1.02 − 1 = 1.47%.

*Bond yield.* A coupon of 4 on a bond priced at 100 gives 4.00%. After QE the price is 110, so the yield is 4/110 = 3.64%. Higher price means lower yield.

*Exchange rate.* The mu falls from 1.50 to 1.35 foreign units (fu). Change = −0.15/1.50 = −10%. An export priced at 80 mu costs 120 fu before and 108 fu after, so it is cheaper abroad. An import costing 60 fu costs 40 mu before and 60/1.35 = 44.44 mu after, up 11.1%.

*Multiplier.* A rate cut adds 1.5 billion mu to AD. With a marginal propensity to withdraw of 0.5, the multiplier is 2, so output rises by 3.0 billion mu.

## The instruments in one table

| Instrument | Direct effect | Effect on AD | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Higher interest rate | Dearer borrowing, better saving returns | C and I fall, exchange rate rises so net exports fall | Slower growth, higher unemployment |
| Lower interest rate | Cheaper borrowing | C and I rise, net exports rise | Demand-pull inflation, asset bubbles |
| Tighter money supply | Less credit | AD falls | Credit shortage for sound firms |
| QE | Lower bond yields, more money | C, I and wealth rise | Asset price inflation, unequal gains |
| Lower exchange rate | Cheaper exports, dearer imports | Net exports rise | Imported inflation |
| Higher exchange rate | Dearer exports, cheaper imports | Net exports fall | Loss of competitiveness |

## Method in steps: a transmission chain

1. State the change in the instrument.
2. Say which component of AD it affects: C, I or net exports.
3. Show the initial shift of AD, then add the multiplier.
4. State the effect on real output, employment and the price level using AD/AS.
5. Add a second-round effect, for example on the exchange rate or house prices.
6. Evaluate size and timing.

## Liquidity trap checklist

- Interest rates are close to zero.
- The demand for money is very elastic, so people hold cash.
- Banks and borrowers do not respond to a further cut.
- Deflation expectations keep real rates high.
- Alternatives are QE, forward guidance and fiscal policy. Fiscal policy is covered in other units.

## Targeting: symmetric versus asymmetric

| Feature | Symmetric | Asymmetric |
|---|---|---|
| Concern | Both too high and too low | Mainly one direction |
| Deflation risk | Taken seriously | May be tolerated |
| Example | 2% point target treated equally | A ceiling of 2% |
| Policy bias | Neutral | Towards tighter policy if the ceiling is the limit |

The UK target is a CPI target of 2%, and the Bank of England has described it as symmetric.

Credible targets anchor expectations. Workers claim less, firms plan investment with confidence, lenders accept lower long-term rates, and the bank needs smaller rate moves.

## Evaluation toolkit

| Point | Why it limits or strengthens monetary policy |
|---|---|
| Time lags | Effects take a year or more, so policy can be mistimed |
| Cost-push | Higher rates do not directly lower input costs; a stronger currency helps only partly |
| Liquidity trap | Cuts cannot go below the floor |
| Household debt | Raises the impact of rate rises, lowers the impact of cuts if confidence is low |
| Savers and borrowers | Gains and losses differ across groups |
| Exchange rate | Exporters lose when it rises |
| Independence | Reduces political pressure, but needs accountability |
| Flexibility | Can be changed quickly, unlike most fiscal measures |
| Global factors | Open economies face foreign shocks that domestic rates do not affect |
| Judgement | Depends on cause of inflation, level of rates, credibility and spare capacity |

## How a central bank works to achieve its aims

1. Gather data and forecast inflation and output.
2. Compare the forecast with the target.
3. A committee votes on the policy rate.
4. Announce and explain the decision.
5. Use market operations to bring market rates into line, or QE if rates are at the floor.
6. Review outcomes and account for misses.

## Quick self-test

1. Inflation is 2.5% against a symmetric target of 2%. State the gap.
2. Calculate the real interest rate (approximate) for 4.0% nominal and 2.5% inflation.
3. A bond with a coupon of 6 is priced at 120. Calculate the yield.
4. The mu moves from 2.00 to 2.50 fu. Calculate the percentage change.
5. A home product priced at 40 mu is sold abroad at that rate before the change in question 4. State the foreign price before and after.
6. An initial AD rise of 2 billion and a marginal propensity to withdraw of 0.25. Calculate the final change in output.
7. State one reason QE might fail to raise spending.
8. Distinguish symmetric from asymmetric targeting.
9. Explain why a credible target may lower wage claims.
10. State one reason why a rate rise may fail to reduce cost-push inflation.

### Answers

1. 2.5 − 2.0 = **0.5 percentage points above target**.
2. 4.0 − 2.5 = **1.5%**.
3. 6/120 × 100 = **5.0%**.
4. (2.50 − 2.00)/2.00 = **+25%** (appreciation).
5. Before: 40 × 2.00 = 80 fu. After: 40 × 2.50 = 100 fu. **80 fu rises to 100 fu**.
6. Multiplier = 1/0.25 = 4. Output rises by 2 × 4 = **8 billion**.
7. For example, banks may hold the extra reserves rather than lend, or firms may not borrow because confidence is low.
8. Symmetric: misses above and below the target count equally. Asymmetric: misses in one direction count as worse.
9. Expected inflation is a component of wage claims, so when people trust the target they expect lower inflation and ask for smaller rises.
10. The shock comes from supply (such as imported inputs), so cutting AD lowers output and jobs but does not reduce those costs.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Stating that rates "reduce inflation" with no chain through spending and AD.
- Confusing a movement along AD with a shift.
- Using the wrong direction for exchange rate effects on exports.
- Giving the real interest rate as nominal plus inflation.
- Describing QE as the government printing cash.
- Listing limits of monetary policy without weighing them.
- Forgetting to apply the evaluation to the context in the question.
- Presenting the liquidity trap as a rate that cannot be changed rather than one where changes have no effect.
- Omitting units, or percentage points versus percent, in calculations.

## Applying the ideas to a context

- Identify the cause of the inflation or slowdown before choosing a tool. Demand-pull cases suit a higher policy rate. Cost-push cases limit what rates can do.
- Check the level of rates. Well above zero, conventional changes work. Near zero, think of QE and expectations.
- Check the exchange rate channel. A small, open economy feels it strongly, because imports and exports are a large share of spending.
- Check credibility. If people trust the target, a small rate move may be enough because expectations do the work.
- Close with a judgement and name the condition it rests on.
