This guide covers **Component 2 – Essay**, for Cambridge International AS
& A Level Global Perspectives & Research (9239), 2026–2028 series. Worth
40 marks and externally marked, it is the one component of 9239 that
candidates complete almost entirely independently, on a question of
their own choosing.

## Where this fits in 9239

Global Perspectives & Research is built around Cambridge's "Critical
Path" — a sequence of research, reasoning and communication skills
rather than a numbered content syllabus — and is assessed across up to
four components. Component 1 (Written Exam) and Component 3 (Team
Project) are compulsory for the AS award; the Essay sits alongside them
as the component where a candidate demonstrates independent research and
argument on a self-chosen global issue, without a teacher-set question or
a team to share the work with.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE A-LEVEL GLOBAL PERSPECTIVES & RESEARCH (9239) — COMPONENT 2 ESSAY**

- Length and format — a continuous-prose essay of 1750–2000 words on a
global issue of the candidate's own choice, drawn from topics studied
during the course; an accurate word count (excluding title, references
and citations) must be stated on the essay, and work beyond 2000 words
is not marked
- Choosing the question — the essay title must be phrased as a single
question, not a statement or generic topic area, and must create genuine
opportunities to develop globally contrasting perspectives; a question
focused on an exclusively local or national issue is unlikely to be
appropriate
- Research and sourcing — candidates identify and explore the context
and basis of arguments responding to the question from different global
perspectives, select a range of sources, and assess and justify why
those particular sources were chosen
- Evaluation of perspectives — candidates compare and evaluate
contrasting perspectives, reflect on how alternative perspectives affect
their own viewpoint, and reach a personal, supported conclusion on the
question
- Referencing — quotations must be fully referenced and the essay must
include a list of sources used; referencing accuracy is assessed as part
of AO3
- Assessment objectives — the essay is marked against AO1 (analysing the
question, synthesising arguments into contrasting perspectives,
selecting and justifying sources, comparing and evaluating perspectives),
AO2 (reflecting on how alternative perspectives affect the candidate's
own view, and suggesting further relevant research) and AO3 (writing a
clear, structured essay with effective referencing)
- The role of the teacher — teachers may lead whole-class background
research into a general issue, but each candidate must devise their own
question individually; teachers support planning, source citation and
word-count discipline but must not give detailed advice on how a
specific draft could be improved to meet the mark scheme, and must not
correct or edit draft coursework directly

## How to approach it

Spend real time on the question before starting to research — a weak
question (one that only supports a single perspective, or that is really
a local issue dressed up as a global one) makes every later stage
harder, however good the research is. A strong Global Perspectives essay
question is phrased as a genuine question, allows at least two globally
contrasting perspectives to be developed in real depth, and is
interesting enough to the candidate to sustain 1750–2000 words of
engaged argument rather than padded restatement.

Because AO1 rewards justifying source selection, not just citing sources,
keep a brief running note while researching on *why* each source was
chosen — its authority, its standpoint, and what perspective it
represents — rather than reconstructing that justification from memory
once drafting starts. Perspectives should be drawn from genuinely
different standpoints (for example, differing by country, by economic
interest, or by cultural or religious position), not merely restated
versions of the same underlying view with different wording.

Draft with the word limit in mind from the start rather than writing
long and cutting later: at 1750–2000 words there is only room for two or
three perspectives developed with real evidence, not four or five
sketched briefly. Reserve the closing paragraphs for the personal,
supported view AO2 requires — an essay that ends by simply summarising
the perspectives already given, without the candidate's own reasoned
judgement, has not completed the task.

## Worked example: turning a topic into a question

A candidate interested in the topic "plastic pollution" might start with
a statement like *"Plastic pollution is a global problem."* This is not
usable as an essay title — it is a claim, not a question, and it does
not open up contrasting perspectives.

```
Weak    "Plastic pollution is a global problem."
Better  "Should developing economies bear the same responsibility as
         developed economies for reducing single-use plastic?"
```

The improved version is phrased as a single question, and it structurally
requires at least two globally contrasting perspectives to answer well —
for example, a perspective centred on historical responsibility and
manufacturing-capacity differences between developed and developing
economies, set against a perspective centred on present-day consumption
volumes and the urgency of environmental harm regardless of a country's
development status. Both are legitimate, evidence-based positions, which
is exactly what AO1's "globally contrasting perspectives" requirement is
looking for.

## Common mistakes

Choosing a question that only really supports one side, then padding out
a second "perspective" that is not genuinely contrasting. Citing sources
without explaining why they were selected or what makes them credible.
Describing perspectives without comparing or evaluating them against each
other. Ending the essay with a restated summary instead of a personal,
supported conclusion. Missing the word count statement, or exceeding
2000 words, which stops the excess from being marked at all.
