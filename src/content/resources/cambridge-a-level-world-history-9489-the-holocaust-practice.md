## Section A

**1.** Explain the difference between structuralist and functionalist interpretations of the Holocaust. **[3]**

**2.** Identify two pieces of evidence used by intentionalist historians. **[2]**

**3.** State three ways in which the war against the Soviet Union changed Nazi policy towards the Jews. **[3]**

**4.** Explain why Goldhagen's *Hitler's Willing Executioners* was controversial. **[3]**

**5.** Identify two reasons why historians disagree about when the decision for the "Final Solution" was taken. **[2]**

**6.** Give three examples of Jewish resistance, armed or unarmed. **[3]**

**7.** Explain how the opening of archives in eastern Europe after 1989-91 affected study of the perpetrators. **[2]**

**8.** Explain two arguments in the debate about whether Britain and the USA could have done more to help Jews during the war. **[4]**

## Section B

**9.** *Constructed extract (Marlbridge).*

> It is tempting to describe the murder of the Jews as the product of chaos, of rival officials improvising in the confusion of war. The record does not support it. From his first political statements Hitler spoke of the Jews as a poison to be removed, and he never wavered. What changed between 1933 and 1941 was not the aim but the opportunity. In peace, with the world watching and Germany weak, persecution had to be limited to laws, boycotts and pressure to emigrate. Hitler told the Reichstag in January 1939 what war would mean for the Jews, and when war came he kept his word. The rivalries inside the regime were real, but they were rivalries over who would carry out the leader's will most effectively, not over what that will was. Without Hitler there would have been antisemitism in Germany; there would not have been Auschwitz.

What can you learn from this extract about the interpretation and approach of the historian who wrote it? Use the extract and your knowledge of the Holocaust to explain your answer. **[40]**

**10.** *Constructed extract (Marlbridge).*

> The men who shot Jews in the forests of Poland and the Soviet Union were not, for the most part, fanatics. They were policemen, clerks and labourers, many too old for front-line service. They had grown up in a society in which hostility to Jews was common, and the regime had spent years teaching them that Jews were enemies. But when they were offered the chance to step aside, few did, and those who refused were rarely punished. What held the others was the fear of seeming weak before their comrades, the habit of obedience and the sense that refusing would leave the dirty work to others. Nor were they alone. Across occupied Europe, local police, militias and neighbours joined in, often with an eagerness that shocked even German officers. The Holocaust was the work of a regime, but it required the participation of a very large number of people.

What can you learn from this extract about the interpretation and approach of the historian who wrote it? Use the extract and your knowledge of the Holocaust to explain your answer. **[40]**

**11.** *Constructed extract (Marlbridge).*

> By the end of 1942 the governments in London and Washington knew, in outline, what was happening to the Jews of Europe. They said so publicly. Yet the conference they called at Bermuda the following spring was held on a remote island, away from the press, and agreed almost nothing. Immigration quotas stayed in place; the doors of Palestine stayed nearly closed; rescue was treated as a distraction from winning the war. It was not until 1944 that Washington created an agency dedicated to rescue, and its achievements in a few months suggest what might have been done earlier. Requests to bomb the railways to Auschwitz were turned down on grounds that do not survive close examination. Victory was the indispensable condition of saving the remaining Jews, but it was not a reason to do nothing in the meantime.

What can you learn from this extract about the interpretation and approach of the historian who wrote it? Use the extract and your knowledge of the Holocaust to explain your answer. **[40]**

## Answers

**1.** 1 mark each: structuralists explain the Holocaust through the nature of the Nazi state -- its competing agencies and chaotic decision-making (1); functionalists explain it through contingent circumstances, above all the war and the failure of earlier plans (1); both reject the idea of a long-planned programme decided by Hitler alone, but differ on whether the cause lay in institutions or in events (1). **[3]**

**2.** Any two, 1 mark each: Hitler's 1919 letter calling for the "removal" of the Jews (1); statements in *Mein Kampf* (1); the Reichstag "prophecy" of 30 January 1939 (1); Hitler's later references to fulfilling that prophecy (1). **[2]**

**3.** Any three, 1 mark each: Einsatzgruppen began mass shootings of Jews behind the advancing front (1); the war was presented as a war of annihilation against "Judeo-Bolshevism", removing restraints (1); millions more Jews came under German control (1); Göring's authorisation to Heydrich of 31 July 1941 to prepare a "total solution" (1); killing was extended from men to women and children by late summer 1941 (1). **[3]**

**4.** 1 mark each: it argued that ordinary Germans were driven by a long-standing "eliminationist antisemitism", implying a national character (1); most historians judged his use of evidence selective and his comparison with other countries inadequate (1); it was a bestseller and prompted fierce public debate, especially in Germany, about collective guilt (1). **[3]**

**5.** Any two, 1 mark each: no written order from Hitler has been found (1); decisions were often communicated orally or in euphemistic language (1); policy developed in stages in different regions, so historians define "the decision" differently (1). **[2]**

**6.** Any three, 1 mark each: the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising, April-May 1943 (1); revolts at Treblinka and Sobibór, 1943 (1); partisan groups such as the Bielski brothers' (1); the Oneg Shabbat archive recording ghetto life (1); smuggling food and running secret schools (1); religious and cultural life maintained in the ghettos (1). **[3]**

**7.** 1 mark each: local records and testimony showed the scale of killing by shooting and the role of local collaborators (1); this widened research from German decision-makers to perpetrators across occupied Europe, as in work on Jedwabne (1). **[2]**

**8.** Two arguments, 2 marks each: some historians (Wyman, Wasserstein) argue that immigration restrictions, the failure of Bermuda and the late creation of the War Refugee Board show a lack of will (2); others (Rubinstein) argue that once the killing began, the Allies had little practical means of rescue, and victory was the only real way to save lives (2); the debate over bombing Auschwitz in 1944 shows both arguments -- whether it was militarily possible and whether it would have saved lives (2). **[4]**

**9.** Indicative content. **Main interpretation:** the Holocaust was the result of Hitler's long-held intention to remove the Jews; circumstances changed only the opportunity, and without Hitler there would have been no genocide. **Approach:** intentionalist and Hitler-centred, in the tradition of Dawidowicz and Fleming; it treats rivalry inside the regime as a matter of method, not aim, and explicitly rejects structuralist "chaos". **Knowledge to support:** the 1919 letter; *Mein Kampf*; the January 1939 prophecy; Hitler's direct involvement in key decisions, such as authorising the killing of the disabled in 1939; the speed with which killing spread after June 1941; Wannsee. **Knowledge to challenge:** policy in the 1930s aimed at emigration; the Madagascar and Nisko plans suggest no fixed plan for murder; no written order has been found; Broszat and Mommsen showed initiative from regional officials and cumulative radicalisation; synthesis historians (Browning, Kershaw) argue the decision developed in stages in 1941. **Avoid:** treating the extract's admission that rivalries were "real" as its main message. **[40]**

**10.** Indicative content. **Main interpretation:** the Holocaust depended on the participation of large numbers of ordinary people, motivated less by fanaticism than by conformity, obedience and peer pressure within an antisemitic environment, and on non-German collaboration across Europe. **Approach:** a "history from below" of perpetrators, studying the behaviour of ordinary men, close to Browning's *Ordinary Men*, and extended to non-German participation (as in Gross's work); it acknowledges ideology but places situational factors first. **Knowledge to support:** Reserve Police Battalion 101 at Józefów in 1942, where men were offered the chance to step aside; most killing in the east by shooting; local militias in Lithuania, Latvia and Ukraine; Jedwabne in 1941; Romanian killings. **Knowledge to challenge:** Goldhagen used the same battalion to argue that antisemitic conviction drove the killing; the SS and Einsatzgruppen leaders were often committed ideologues; years of propaganda and the Wehrmacht's role suggest ideology mattered more than the extract allows; central decisions in Berlin set the framework. **Avoid:** focusing only on the non-German point, which is part of the wider argument about broad participation. **[40]**

**11.** Indicative content. **Main interpretation:** Britain and the USA knew about the killing by late 1942 and could have done more to rescue Jews; their failure was a failure of will, not merely of means. **Approach:** a study of bystanders, focusing on Allied governments' decisions and moral responsibility, close to Wyman and Wasserstein; it rejects the argument that victory alone was possible. This extract should not be labelled intentionalist or functionalist. **Knowledge to support:** the Riegner telegram (August 1942); the Allied declaration of December 1942; the Bermuda Conference (April 1943); US quotas and the British White Paper of 1939; the War Refugee Board (January 1944) and its rescue work, for example in Hungary; refusal of requests to bomb Auschwitz in 1944. **Knowledge to challenge:** Rubinstein's argument that few Jews could have been saved once the killing was under way; Allied military priorities and the technical difficulty of accurate bombing; the Kindertransport and other pre-war admissions; the Nazis controlled the victims, so only defeat of Germany could stop the killing. **Avoid:** treating the extract's concession that victory was "indispensable" as the main argument. **[40]**
