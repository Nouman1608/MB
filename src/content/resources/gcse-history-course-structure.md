An overview of how AQA GCSE History (8145) is put together, for students
starting the course and for anyone deciding whether it is the right
specification for them.

## Two components

Paper 1 (Understanding the Modern World) and Paper 2 (Shaping the
Nation) carry equal weight, and each splits into two equally weighted
sections, A and B -- so no single component or section dominates
revision time by default. AQA organises GCSE History subject content
into two components:

- **Understanding the Modern World** — study of the wider world and how
change happens over time.
- **Shaping the Nation** — study focused more specifically on Britain and
national development.

Within these components, schools choose from AQA's set of period studies,
wider-world depth studies, thematic studies and British depth studies —
so two schools both teaching AQA GCSE History may cover genuinely
different content depending on which specific studies they select, while
still being assessed on the same specification.

## The historic environment study

One required element is a **historic environment study**, based on a
specified list of historic sites tied to whichever British depth study a
school has chosen. AQA refreshes the specified sites periodically for
each two-year examination cycle, so the exact sites in scope depend on
your examination series — always confirm the current list with your
teacher or AQA directly rather than relying on a previous year's list.

## Why the subject is structured this way

AQA designed the specification so students engage with key issues such as
conflict, and understand what drives change and how the past continues to
influence the present — building on the historical skills introduced at
Key Stage 3, and preparing students who go on to study History further.

## Study approach

Because the specific period, depth and thematic studies your school
teaches are chosen by the school (not fixed nationally), the single most
important first step is confirming exactly which studies you are being
examined on — revision guides and past papers are only useful once you
know which specific studies apply to you. From there, the recurring
skills AQA GCSE History rewards are: using sources as evidence,
explaining causation and consequence, and analysing change and continuity
over time — all worth practising against whichever specific content your
course covers, since these skills transfer across every period and
depth study AQA offers.

## The three assessment objectives

Across both papers, AQA GCSE History assesses three objectives: AO1
(knowledge and understanding of the period studied), AO2 (explaining
and analysing key concepts including causation, change, continuity,
consequence and significance), and AO3 (analysing and evaluating
source material and historical interpretations). Structured exam
questions typically move from a lower-tariff AO1 question testing
recall through to a higher-tariff AO2 or AO3 extended-writing question
on the same content, which means a revision session built only around
drilling facts leaves the highest-value marks unpractised -- pairing
every content-recall session with at least one timed extended-writing
question on that same material is a more effective use of revision
time than treating recall and extended writing as separate stages of
preparation.

## The recurring skills across every option

Whichever specific period study, depth study, thematic study or
British depth study a school selects, the same handful of transferable
skills are tested throughout: using sources as evidence by identifying
what a source shows and considering its origin and purpose before
judging its usefulness or reliability; explaining causation by sorting
causes into long-term underlying conditions, short-term events in the
run-up, and the specific trigger, then explaining how the three
reinforced each other rather than simply listing them; and analysing
change and continuity by weighing what genuinely changed against what
stayed the same or changed only superficially, before reaching an
explicit judgement rather than presenting change alone. Some Paper 1
tasks add a further skill on top of source evaluation: comparing two
differing historical interpretations of the same event and explaining
why historians might reach different conclusions from broadly similar
evidence, whether through differences in the evidence each emphasises,
the specific questions each set out to answer, or the context in which
each was writing.

## How the two components share this structure

Both components tend to follow a similar shape at the level of
individual studies: an opening phase covering the establishment of or
aftermath following some change, a middle phase covering crisis,
upheaval or diplomatic effort, and a final phase covering lived
experience or a breakdown into renewed conflict or change. Revising a
chosen period, depth or thematic study evenly across all three phases,
rather than concentrating only on the most dramatic middle section,
is a more reliable way to cover a study's full range than focusing
disproportionately on the events students find most memorable.

## Building a revision timetable around your school's choices

Because Paper 1 and Paper 2 between them draw on several genuinely
separate bodies of content -- distinct period studies, depth studies
and a British-focused historic environment site -- it helps to avoid
revising all of it as a single undifferentiated block of "history
content." Allocating distinct revision sessions to each chosen study,
and within each session revising the same recurring skills applied to
that specific content, reinforces the transferable AO2 and AO3 skills
while keeping the separate bodies of AO1 factual knowledge
appropriately distinct in memory rather than blurring together.
