## Definitions

- **Contingency plan**: a set of actions prepared in advance for an unexpected event that could disrupt the business. Standard definition.
- **Contingency planning**: the process of identifying such events, assessing them and preparing responses.
- **Crisis**: a serious, usually sudden event that threatens survival, reputation or normal running and needs urgent decisions. Standard definition.
- **Crisis management**: how a business responds during and after a crisis.
- **Stakeholders**: individuals or groups with an interest in the business, such as owners, employees, customers, suppliers, lenders and the local community.

## Must-know distinctions

| | Contingency planning | Crisis management |
|---|---|---|
| When | Before the event | During and after the event |
| Main activity | Predicting, preparing, rehearsing | Deciding, communicating, containing, recovering |
| Needs | Time, money, imagination | Speed, clear leadership, honest messages |
| Weakness | Event may not match the plan | Poor decisions under pressure if unprepared |
| Link | A good plan makes the response faster | Lessons feed back into the plan |

Also keep these apart:

- **Risk that can be measured** and **uncertainty that cannot**. Contingency plans are partly a response to the second.
- **Prevention** (stop the event) and **mitigation** (reduce its effect). Insurance **transfers** cost but does not repair reputation or keep customers.
- **Impact** (what difference the plan makes) and **importance** (how much it matters).

## Method: preparing a contingency plan (standard approach)

1. Identify possible disruptive events.
2. Rate each for likelihood and severity.
3. Choose a response: prevent, reduce, transfer or accept.
4. Assign roles, resources and a trigger for action.
5. Plan communication with each stakeholder group.
6. Test, review and update.

## Advantages and disadvantages: recall list

**Advantages**: quicker, calmer response; less lost sales revenue; protects customers and contracts; protects jobs; may help with insurance or customer requirements; reveals weak points.

**Disadvantages**: cost and management time every year; cannot cover every event; may go out of date; may not be tested; false confidence; opportunity cost.

**Stakeholder angle**: link each group to a specific effect. Owners (value protected, profit reduced by cost), employees (security, extra training), customers (continuity, possible higher prices), suppliers (stable orders, new demands), lenders (lower default risk).

## Impact and importance: the checklist

Ask these five questions of any case:

1. How likely and how severe is the event?
2. How dependent is the business on one site, supplier, system or person?
3. How large and well resourced is the business?
4. Are people's safety or perishable goods involved?
5. Is the plan realistic, tested and understood by those who must use it?

## Small worked reminder: expected loss

A standard comparison is **expected loss = probability x loss**. It is not an OCR formula, so state it as a standard approach.

| Event | Probability per year | Loss if it happens | Expected loss |
|---|---|---|---|
| A: supplier failure | 10% | £30,000 | £3,000 |
| B: warehouse fire | 2% | £400,000 | £8,000 |
| C: payment-system outage | 25% | £8,000 | £2,000 |

Event B has the highest expected loss, although it is the least likely. Suppose a £5,500-a-year plan cuts B's loss to £150,000. The new expected loss is 0.02 x £150,000 = £3,000. The saving is £8,000 - £3,000 = £5,000, which is £500 less than the plan's cost. Numbers alone would not support the plan, but a fire could end the business, so a judgement might still favour a cheaper version.

Always follow the sum with a comment on what it leaves out: reputation, safety, the accuracy of the probability, and whether the loss could be larger than estimated.

## Events and typical responses

Use this table to generate specific ideas for a "recommend" answer. Adapt each to the case; do not copy it.

| Event | Prevent or reduce | Prepare to respond |
|---|---|---|
| Fire at the only site | Fire detection, safe storage | Alternative premises, copies of records off site, insurance |
| Supplier fails or is delayed | A second approved supplier, buffer stock | Pre-agreed emergency orders, customer messages |
| Cyber-attack | Staff training, access controls | Tested backups, a named recovery contact |
| Key person leaves suddenly | Shared knowledge, documented procedures | Named deputy, interim cover |
| Faulty product | Quality checks, traceability | Recall procedure, helpline, refund policy |

## Communication in a crisis

Each stakeholder group needs a different message, so a plan should say who tells whom.

- **Employees**: what has happened, what to do, who to ask. Hearing it from the business first limits rumour.
- **Customers**: what is affected, what the business is doing, what they should do.
- **Suppliers and lenders**: how payments and orders will be handled, so they stay supportive.
- **Media and the public**: one spokesperson, factual statements, no guessing about causes.

## Method: recommend and justify a plan

1. Choose the most serious realistic risk in the case.
2. Name specific actions (what, who, how quickly, at what cost).
3. Use case evidence to justify: figures, dependence, customer expectations.
4. Compare with an alternative and say why you reject it.
5. Note a limit or risk.
6. Give a one-sentence judgement.

## Method: evaluate crisis management

Score the response against these seven criteria, using evidence from the case:

- Speed
- Honesty and clarity of communication
- Safety and people first
- Clear control and roles
- Cost against harm avoided
- Speed and extent of recovery
- Learning afterwards

Then decide how effective it was overall, and say what depends on the time frame. A response can look expensive now yet protect the business over several years. Avoid judging only by outcome.

## Quick self-test

1. Define a contingency plan.
2. State whether contingency planning takes place before or after an event.
3. Give two examples of events a business might plan for.
4. Give one advantage and one disadvantage of contingency planning to a business.
5. Name two stakeholder groups and one way a plan affects each.
6. A business estimates a 5% chance of a £120,000 loss each year. Calculate the expected annual loss.
7. A plan would cut that loss to £30,000 and costs £2,500 a year. Calculate the net annual benefit using expected loss.
8. Give one reason why a plan might be less valuable to a business than the figures suggest.
9. State two things that good crisis management usually involves.
10. Give two criteria for judging how well a business handled a crisis.

### Answers

1. A set of actions prepared in advance for an unexpected event that could disrupt the business.
2. Before.
3. For example fire, cyber-attack, supplier failure, loss of a key person, product fault.
4. For example: faster recovery (advantage); cost and time to prepare and keep up to date (disadvantage).
5. For example: employees, who gain security or clearer instructions; customers, who keep receiving orders.
6. 0.05 x £120,000 = **£6,000**.
7. Expected loss after: 0.05 x £30,000 = £1,500. Saving £4,500. Less cost £2,500 gives **£2,000** net benefit per year.
8. For example: the probability is only an estimate; the plan may be untested or out of date; the event may not match the plan.
9. For example: quick, honest communication; clear leadership; putting people's safety first; containing the problem.
10. For example: speed of response; honesty of communication; recovery time; cost against harm avoided.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing that a contingency plan is used after the event.
- Describing contingency planning in general without using the business in the case.
- Listing advantages and disadvantages with no weighing or judgement.
- Naming stakeholders but not saying how they are affected.
- Calculating expected loss and stopping, with no comment on what the figure leaves out.
- Recommending "insurance and a backup" for every business without saying which, how and why.
- Judging crisis management only by whether things turned out well.
- Praising a response without stating a criterion, such as speed or honesty.
- Ignoring the size of the business when judging how affordable a plan is.
- Giving a conclusion that repeats the question instead of answering it.
