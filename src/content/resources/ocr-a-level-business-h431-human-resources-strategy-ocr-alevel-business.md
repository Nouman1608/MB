## What the specification asks for

| Learners should be able to | Covered below |
|---|---|
| evaluate the impact and importance of a human resources strategy for a business and its stakeholders | Impact, Worked example 1 and Importance |
| recommend and justify a human resources strategy for a business | Recommending a strategy and Worked example 2 |

Both statements are higher-order: the command words are **evaluate** and **recommend and justify**, so definitions alone score little. The strategy draws on everything in the human resources topic, including objectives, structure, recruitment, training, motivation, leadership, relations and external influences.

## What a human resources strategy is

A **strategy** is a long-term plan for achieving the business's objectives. A **human resources strategy** is the long-term plan for how the business will obtain, develop, reward and keep the workforce it needs. It differs from day-to-day human resource tasks (running payroll, filling one vacancy) because it looks several years ahead and starts from the question: *what workforce does the business need to achieve its objectives, and how will it get it?*

A strategy normally contains:

| Component | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| Workforce plan | How many people, with what skills, and when? |
| Recruitment and selection | Where will they come from, and how will the business choose? |
| Training and development | How will skills be built and maintained? |
| Reward and motivation | How will pay, benefits and recognition attract and keep staff? |
| Organisation and structure | How will jobs and reporting lines be arranged? |
| Leadership and culture | What kind of management style and values does the business want? |
| Employee relations | How will it communicate, consult and deal with unions? |
| Exit | How will redundancy, retirement and dismissal be handled? |

## Fit: why a strategy needs to match the business

A human resources strategy is effective only if it **fits** the business. There are two kinds of fit.

- **Vertical fit** means the strategy supports the business's overall objectives. A business aiming at rapid growth needs a strategy built around recruitment and training; a business aiming at low costs may stress productivity and flexible contracts.
- **Horizontal fit** means the parts of the strategy work together. Hiring skilled staff but paying below the market rate, or training staff and then giving them no route to promotion, wastes money.

It must also fit the **external environment**: labour supply, law and technology (see [external influences on human resources](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-h431-external-influences-on-human-resources-ocr-alevel-business/)), and the business's **resources**: finance, existing skills and management ability.

## Measuring impact

Impact can be judged against both people measures and business measures.

| Measure | How it is found | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| Labour turnover | Leavers ÷ average staff × 100 | Retention and morale |
| Labour productivity | Output or revenue ÷ number of employees | Efficiency of the workforce |
| Labour cost as a share of revenue | Labour cost ÷ revenue × 100 | Affordability |
| Absence rate | Days lost ÷ days possible × 100 | Engagement and health |
| Cost per hire | Total recruitment cost ÷ number hired | Efficiency of recruitment |

### Worked example 1: the impact of a strategy

Kestrel Home Care employs 120 care workers. Revenue is £6,000,000 a year and labour cost is £3,000,000.

**Position before the strategy.**

- Revenue per employee = £6,000,000 ÷ 120 = **£50,000**.
- Labour cost as a share of revenue = £3,000,000 ÷ £6,000,000 × 100 = **50%**.
- 30 workers leave a year, so turnover = 30 ÷ 120 × 100 = **25%**.
- Each leaver costs £5,500 (recruitment, induction and agency cover), so turnover costs 30 × £5,500 = **£165,000**.

**The strategy.** A three-part plan: training worth £400 per employee, a 2% pay rise, and a coordinator (salary £38,000) to run career pathways. The aim is to cut turnover to 15% and to raise revenue by 5% through better care and fewer gaps in service. Extra revenue earns a contribution of 25%.

**Year 1 costs.**

- Training = 120 × £400 = £48,000.
- Pay rise = 2% × £3,000,000 = £60,000.
- Coordinator = £38,000.
- Total = **£146,000**.

**Year 1 benefits.**

- Leavers fall to 15% × 120 = 18, saving 12 × £5,500 = **£66,000**.
- Extra revenue = 5% × £6,000,000 = £300,000; contribution = 25% × £300,000 = **£75,000**.
- Total = **£141,000**.

**Result.** Net effect in year 1 = £141,000 − £146,000 = **−£5,000**, almost break-even. In year 2, suppose training falls to half (£24,000) while other costs stay: costs are £24,000 + £60,000 + £38,000 = £122,000, and net benefit rises to **+£19,000**. Revenue per employee rises to £6,300,000 ÷ 120 = £52,500, a 5% gain.

**Evaluation.** The strategy pays for itself by year 2 on these figures, but the numbers rest on assumptions: turnover may fall less than hoped and care quality is hard to put in money. Non-financial benefits count too: staff who stay build relationships with clients, and the business gains a reputation as a good employer. A fair judgement says the strategy is worthwhile if the business can fund a first year with no profit gain, and if it tracks turnover and client satisfaction every quarter.

## Importance for the business and its stakeholders

A strategy is important because it:

- **aligns** hiring, training and reward with the business's objectives, so effort is not wasted
- **controls cost**, since labour is often the largest cost
- **builds a skilled, committed workforce** that is hard for competitors to copy
- **prepares for change**, such as retirements, new technology or growth
- **reduces risk** from legal breaches, shortages and sudden redundancies.

| Stakeholder | Effect of a good strategy | Effect of a poor or missing one |
|---|---|---|
| Owners | Better profit and lower risk | Wasted cost; skills shortages |
| Employees | Training, clear careers, fair reward | Uncertainty, low morale |
| Customers | Consistent service | Poor service, delays |
| Managers | Clear direction | Constant fire-fighting |
| Community | Stable local jobs | Job losses |

The importance is greatest where labour is a large cost, skills are scarce, the business is growing or changing quickly, or reputation depends on staff. It is lower in a small business with a handful of staff and stable tasks, where an informal approach may be enough.

## Recommending a strategy

To **recommend and justify**, follow five steps.

1. **Diagnose** the business's objectives and its workforce problem.
2. **Choose** a direction and the main components.
3. **Support** it with evidence from the case: figures, labour market and resources.
4. **Weigh** costs, risks and alternatives.
5. **Conclude** with a judgement, and say what would change it.

### Worked example 2: a recommendation

Marlow Engineering has 80 staff and revenue of £4,000,000. It wants to double output in four years by winning export orders. Its problems: the average age of its toolmakers is 56, local unemployment is low, and training has been cut. A rival is offering higher pay.

*Recommendation.* Adopt a growth and skills strategy with three parts: (1) an apprenticeship programme to train younger toolmakers; (2) a retention package for older toolmakers, including phased retirement, so that their knowledge is passed on; and (3) a pay review to bring the lowest-paid skilled roles close to the market rate.

*Justification.* The growth objective needs more skilled staff than the local market can supply, so the business must train its own (vertical fit). Phased retirement and apprenticeships work together (horizontal fit). With low unemployment, buying skills at the market rate would cost more than building them.

*Risks and alternatives.* Apprentices take years to become fully productive, so output may lag demand. An alternative of recruiting skilled toolmakers from abroad is quicker but costs more and depends on immigration rules. Automation could cut the number of toolmakers needed, but it requires investment.

*Judgement.* The skills strategy is the better choice because growth depends on skills that are scarce and slow to build, and a rival's pay offer means delay would cost staff. It would be reviewed if export orders did not appear, since the commitment to training would then be too large.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the strategy as a list of human resource activities with no link to objectives.
- Recommending training or pay rises with no cost.
- Giving a recommendation with no alternative considered.
- Missing the horizontal fit: parts that contradict each other.
- Forgetting that benefits are partly non-financial and take time.

## Exam technique

For **evaluate**, give the impact on the business and at least two stakeholders, use a measure, weigh short- and long-term effects and conclude. For **recommend and justify**, state a clear strategy, justify with case evidence, show you considered an alternative and give a judgement.
