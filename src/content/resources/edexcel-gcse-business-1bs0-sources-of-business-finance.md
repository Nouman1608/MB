## What 1.3.4 asks you to know

The heading is "Sources of finance for a start-up or established small business".

| Strand | Specification wording |
|---|---|
| Short-term sources | overdraft and trade credit |
| Long-term sources | personal savings, venture capital, share capital, loans, retained profit and crowd funding |

The phrase "start-up or established small business" matters: some sources are open to one but not the other.

## Part 1: six questions for choosing a source

When a case asks which source a business should use, work through these questions. They turn a description of sources into an argued choice.

1. **How much is needed?** Personal savings and trade credit suit small sums; venture capital and share capital suit large ones.
2. **What is it for, and for how long?** Match the source's length to the need. A temporary cash gap needs a short-term source; equipment or premises used for years need a long-term source.
3. **What does it cost?** Loans and overdrafts charge interest. Shares and venture capital cost a share of future profits. Savings and retained profit have no interest but mean the owner gives up other uses of that money.
4. **Who controls the business afterwards?** Selling shares or taking venture capital gives others part-ownership and often a say in decisions. Borrowing keeps ownership with the owner.
5. **Is it available to this business?** A start-up has no retained profit and no trading record, so lenders and suppliers may be cautious. An established business can show past accounts.
6. **What happens if things go badly?** Loan repayments must be made even in a poor month. Share capital does not have to be repaid.

## Part 2: short-term sources in detail

### Overdraft
- The bank agrees a limit below zero on the current account.
- Interest is charged only on the amount overdrawn and only while it is used, which makes it cheap for short, small gaps.
- Rates are usually higher than on a loan, and the bank can reduce or withdraw the limit.
- Best for: cash-flow gaps that the forecast shows will close within weeks or months.

### Trade credit
- A supplier lets the business pay for goods after delivery, often after 30 days or more.
- It costs nothing if the business pays on time, and it lets the business sell stock before paying for it.
- New businesses may be refused credit until they have a record; paying late damages the relationship.
- Best for: buying stock and materials.

## Part 3: long-term sources compared

| Source | Repaid? | Ownership given up? | Main cost | Available to a start-up? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Personal savings | No | No | Owner risks losing the money | Yes, if the owner has savings |
| Loans | Yes, with interest | No | Interest; fixed repayments | Yes, but may need a business plan or security |
| Retained profit | No | No | Profit not paid to owners | No: needs past profits |
| Share capital | No | Yes, to shareholders | Share of future profits (dividends) | Only if set up as a company |
| Venture capital | No (usually) | Yes, to the investor | Share of future profits and control | Possible, for high-growth ideas |
| Crowd funding | Depends on terms | Sometimes | Rewards, shares or interest, depending on the platform | Yes, if the idea attracts backers |

Share capital requires the business to be a company, which links to business ownership in 1.4.1.

## Part 4: putting figures on the choice

### Worked example: Marram Surf School

Marram Surf School has traded for three years as a private limited company. It needs £30,000 for a new beach hut and boards. It has £12,000 of retained profit. The owner expects profit of £48,000 a year after the expansion.

**Option A: bank loan of £30,000.** Total repayment £36,600 over five years.
- Interest = (£36,600 − £30,000) ÷ £30,000 × 100 = £6,600 ÷ £30,000 × 100 = **22%**
- Monthly repayment = £36,600 ÷ 60 = **£610**

**Option B: venture capital.** An investor provides £30,000 for 25% of the shares.
- Investor's share of expected profit = 25% × £48,000 = **£12,000 a year**
- Over five years this would be £60,000, against £6,600 of loan interest, and the investor keeps their share after that.

**Option C: combine retained profit and a smaller loan.** Use £12,000 of retained profit and borrow £18,000 on the same terms.
- Total repayment = £18,000 × 1.22 = £21,960; interest = **£3,960**
- Monthly repayment = £21,960 ÷ 60 = **£366**

**Weighing them.**
- Option C costs the least in interest and keeps full ownership, but it uses up the reserve that protects Marram against a bad summer.
- Option A keeps the reserve but adds £610 a month of fixed repayments, which matters for a seasonal business with little winter income.
- Option B has no repayments, which suits seasonal cash flow, and the investor may bring advice; but it is the most expensive if profits are as forecast, and the owner loses some control.

A reasonable judgement is Option C, provided the cash-flow forecast shows that £366 a month can be paid in winter. If winter income is very low, the owner might keep some retained profit as a cushion and borrow a little more.

## Part 5: start-up or established?

| | Start-up | Established small business |
|---|---|---|
| Likely sources | Personal savings, loans, crowd funding, venture capital (if high-growth) | Retained profit, loans, overdraft, trade credit, share capital |
| Why | No record of profits; lenders and suppliers see more risk | Past accounts and a payment record make finance easier to obtain |
| Trade credit | May be refused or limited at first | Usually offered |

## Annotated model answer

**Question (Explain):** Explain one disadvantage to a business owner of using venture capital. **[3]**

> A venture capitalist provides money in return for a share of the business (statement). This means the owner gives up part of the ownership and the investor will take a share of future profits (expansion). The investor may also want a say in decisions, so the owner loses some control over how the business is run (second expansion).

## Common mistakes

- Recommending an overdraft for equipment that will last years.
- Saying retained profit is available to a start-up.
- Calling share capital a loan; it is not repaid.
- Ignoring the effect of loan repayments on cash flow.
- Choosing a source without saying why it suits this business's amount, purpose and situation.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Calculate:** interest percentages, repayments and profit shares; show workings.
- **Outline:** one source with a reason it suits the business in the case.
- **Analyse:** apply the six questions above to the business.
- **Justify:** compare the two sources given, with figures where possible, and recommend one.
