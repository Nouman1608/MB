## Section A

**1.** Identify **three** features of the Weimar constitution of 1919. **[3]**

**2.** State **two** measures taken by Stresemann's governments to end the crisis of 1923. **[2]**

**3.** Outline **two** ways in which India's 1950 constitution protected disadvantaged groups. **[2]**

**4.** Define "proportional representation" and give **one** disadvantage of it. **[2]**

**5.** Identify **two** actions taken by Indira Gandhi's government during the Emergency of 1975-77. **[2]**

**6.** Outline the purpose of South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** **(a)** Explain **two** reasons why the Weimar Republic survived the crises of 1919-1923. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain why the Depression after 1929 proved more dangerous to the republic than the crisis of 1923. **[4]**

**8.** **(a)** Explain **two** challenges faced by India's democracy in its first decade. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain why Indian democracy survived the Emergency. **[3]**

**9.** **(a)** Explain the economic challenges facing South Africa's government after 1994. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain **one** way in which the government's economic strategy changed in 1996, and why. **[3]**

**10.** Compare and contrast the role of the constitution in the history of democracy in Weimar Germany and India. **[6]**

**11.** Evaluate the impact of the policies of **one** democratic state on women. **[6]**

**12.** "Economic crisis was the greatest challenge faced by democratic states." With reference to **two** democratic states, each chosen from a different region, to what extent do you agree with this statement? **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Any three, 1 mark each: proportional representation for elections to the Reichstag; votes for men and women aged 20 and over; a president elected directly for seven years; Article 48 emergency powers for the president; a federal system with state governments; a statement of basic rights. **[3]**

**2.** Any two, 1 mark each: ending passive resistance in the Ruhr (September 1923); introducing the Rentenmark (November 1923); negotiating the Dawes Plan (1924) to reschedule reparations and attract American loans. **[2]**

**3.** Any two, 1 mark each: abolition of untouchability; reserved seats in legislatures and posts in public employment for Scheduled Castes and Tribes; equality before the law and prohibition of discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth; universal adult suffrage regardless of literacy or property. **[2]**

**4.** 1 mark for the definition: a system in which parties win seats in proportion to their share of the vote. 1 mark for a disadvantage: it can produce many parties and unstable coalitions, or give small extremist parties a parliamentary platform. **[2]**

**5.** Any two, 1 mark each: imprisonment of opposition leaders without trial; censorship of the press; postponement of elections; a forced sterilization campaign associated with Sanjay Gandhi; demolition of slum housing in Delhi. **[2]**

**6.** 1 mark each: to investigate gross human rights violations committed under apartheid between 1960 and 1994; to let victims give testimony and be acknowledged; to grant amnesty to perpetrators who made full disclosure of politically motivated crimes, in the interest of reconciliation rather than retribution. **[3]**

**7.** **(a)** 2 marks for each developed reason: the army and *Freikorps* were willing to crush left-wing risings such as the Spartacists (1919), and workers' general strike defeated the right-wing Kapp Putsch (1920); the extremes were divided and their coups badly organized, as at Munich in 1923; Stresemann's measures and the Dawes Plan restored stability; the moderate parties supporting the republic still held most Reichstag seats in the early 1920s. **[4]**
**(b)** 2 marks for each developed reason: unemployment reached around six million by early 1932, far longer-lasting than the 1923 crisis; the recall of American loans removed the support on which recovery had depended; the grand coalition collapsed (1930), so government passed to presidential decree, weakening parliament; voters turned to the Nazis and Communists, so by July 1932 anti-republican parties held a majority of seats. **[4]**

**8.** **(a)** 2 marks for each developed challenge: the aftermath of partition, including refugees and communal violence; integrating the princely states, including the dispute over Kashmir; demands for states based on language, met by reorganization in 1956; mass poverty and illiteracy in an electorate of millions voting for the first time. **[4]**
**(b)** 1 mark each: the Emergency was ended by Indira Gandhi's own decision to call elections in 1977; an opposition coalition, the Janata Party, won those elections and power transferred peacefully; the institutions of the constitution, including the electoral system, remained in place and were restored. **[3]**

**9.** **(a)** Up to 4 marks: extreme inequality inherited from apartheid, with wealth concentrated among white South Africans (1); high unemployment, especially among Black South Africans (1); the need to provide housing, water, electricity and education quickly (1); the need to reassure investors and avoid capital flight while meeting expectations of redistribution (1). **[4]**
**(b)** 1 mark for identifying the change: from the Reconstruction and Development Programme to GEAR. 1 mark for its nature: emphasis on growth, fiscal discipline, privatization and attracting investment. 1 mark for the reason: concern about the budget deficit, currency weakness and the need for foreign investment. **[3]**

**10.** Up to 6 marks; both similarities and differences are needed. Similarities: both constitutions established universal suffrage and legal rights; both contained emergency powers (Article 48 in Germany; the emergency provisions used by Indira Gandhi in 1975). Differences: in Germany, Article 48 became the normal means of government after 1930 and helped open the way to Hitler, while India's Emergency ended with elections and the restoration of normal rule; Weimar's proportional representation fragmented parliament, while India's first-past-the-post system produced large Congress majorities; India's constitution addressed social inequality directly through reservations. The top mark needs a judgement, such as that the constitutions mattered less than the political conditions in which they operated. **[6]**

**11.** Up to 6 marks: 2 marks for identifying policies and their aims; 2 marks for evaluating their results; 2 marks for a supported judgement on extent. *India as an example:* the constitution gave women the vote and equality before the law; the Hindu Code Bills (mid-1950s) gave Hindu women rights of divorce and inheritance. Results: legal rights were real, but social practice, including dowry and limited access to education, changed slowly, and the reforms did not apply to all communities. Judgement: significant legal change with limited social change in the short term. **[6]**

**12.** Marked holistically using the 15-mark markbands; the indicative content is a guide, not a checklist. *Weimar Germany and South Africa, for example.* For the claim: Weimar's hyperinflation (1923) and the Depression destroyed confidence, and the Depression drove voters to the extremes and parliament to rule by decree; South Africa's inequality and unemployment shaped every government after 1994. Against: political challenges such as coup attempts, extremist parties and hostile elites in Germany; the legacy of racial division, political violence before 1994 and the need for reconciliation in South Africa. A strong answer shows how economic crisis interacted with political structures (Article 48, proportional representation) and concludes, for example, that economic crisis was decisive in Germany because it struck a republic with weak institutions, while South Africa's stronger constitution and political leadership allowed democracy to survive economic difficulty. **[15]**

## Frequently asked questions

**Is Weimar Germany a democratic state or an example of authoritarianism?**
It is a democratic state for this topic. Its collapse in 1933 is part of its story here; Nazi rule after 1933 belongs to the authoritarian states topic.

**Can I use a nineteenth-century case?**
Yes, if you studied one: the topic begins in 1848.
