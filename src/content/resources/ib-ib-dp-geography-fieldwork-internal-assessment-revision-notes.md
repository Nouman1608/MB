Condensed recall for the **fieldwork internal assessment** (IA). For the full explanation and the
worked Spearman example, read the
[Fieldwork study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-fieldwork-study-guide/). Always check the current subject guide and your teacher's instructions for exact requirements,
word limit and criteria; they take priority.

## What the IA is

An individual investigation: identify a geographical question, collect **primary data**, process and
present it, analyse it and evaluate the whole investigation in a written report. Small, well-designed
local studies usually beat ambitious ones that cannot be done carefully.

## Planning

1. A focused, answerable **question** linked to course ideas.
2. State the geographical **context** and theory.
3. **Hypothesis** (predicts a relationship or difference) and **null hypothesis** (no relationship or
   difference), which is what a statistical test examines.
4. List variables, measures and units.
5. **Sampling:** random avoids bias; systematic uses regular intervals; stratified represents each
   sub-group in proportion. Justify the method and give sample size and limits.
6. **Pilot** to expose problems; **risk assessment** and ethics (consent, respect for property and
   wildlife).

## From weak to strong questions

| Weak | Stronger |
|---|---|
| "What is the river like?" | "How do channel width, depth and velocity change downstream along a named stretch of river?" |
| "Is tourism good for the town?" | "How do residents' perceptions of tourism vary with distance from the main attraction?" |
| "What is the CBD like?" | "How does environmental quality change with distance from the centre of a named town?" |

A strong question names a place, contains measurable variables and links to a theory or model
(for example the Bradshaw model of downstream change or the bid-rent idea in urban geography).

## Sampling in practice

- **Point, line and area sampling:** individual spots, points along a transect, or quadrats.
- A **transect** is a line along which data are collected at intervals, used to show change with
  distance (from a city centre, across a beach, up a valley).
- **Sample size:** larger samples reduce the effect of chance, but must be achievable in the time.
- Combining methods is common: **stratified systematic** sampling takes regular samples within each
  sub-group, such as each zone of a town.

## Collecting and presenting data

Record on prepared sheets with units, times, dates and exact locations; note conditions; repeat
readings; keep raw data.

- Graphs: scatter (relationships), bar (categories), line (change).
- Maps, annotated photographs and sketches.
- Summary statistics: mean, median, range, interquartile range.
- Every figure: **title, labelled axes with units, source**, and referred to in the text.

Typical primary methods:

| Method | Notes |
|---|---|
| Questionnaire | Closed questions give countable data; open questions give depth; a Likert scale (for example 1 to 5 from strongly disagree to strongly agree) turns opinions into numbers |
| Environmental quality survey | A bipolar scale (for example -2 to +2) for litter, noise or building condition; subjective, so use the same observer or train observers |
| Pedestrian or traffic counts | Fixed time periods at each site, at the same time of day |
| River measurements | Width, depth at regular intervals, velocity (flow meter or float), sediment size and roundness |
| Land-use mapping | Classify each building's use along a transect |

Ethics: get consent for questionnaires, keep respondents anonymous, avoid private land without
permission and do not damage the environment you study.

## Statistical tests

| Test | Use |
|---|---|
| Spearman's rank | Strength of association between two ranked variables |
| Chi-squared | Observed against expected frequencies in categories |
| Mann-Whitney U | Compare two independent samples |

Spearman: rs = 1 - 6(sum of d squared) / n(n squared - 1). Worked example from the study guide
(invented data): sum of d squared = 166, n = 8, rs = 1 - 996 / 504 = -0.976, a very strong negative
correlation, past the critical value 0.738 at the 5 per cent level (two-tailed). Check the table
in your own materials.

**Chi-squared worked example (invented data):** 60 visitors are counted across three zones of a
park. If use were even, the expected value would be 20 in each zone. Observed: 30, 18 and 12.

- Chi-squared = sum of (O - E) squared / E.
- (30 - 20) squared / 20 = 5.0; (18 - 20) squared / 20 = 0.2; (12 - 20) squared / 20 = 3.2.
- Total = 8.4. Degrees of freedom = number of categories - 1 = 2.
- The critical value at the 5 per cent level for 2 degrees of freedom is 5.99, so 8.4 exceeds it:
  reject the null hypothesis that visitors are spread evenly.

Other useful tools: the **nearest neighbour index** for point patterns (values near 0 suggest
clustering, near 1 a random pattern, and higher values a more regular pattern), and **standard
deviation** to show how spread out data are around the mean.

## Analysis and evaluation

Describe patterns, then explain; interpret significance as the chance of such data if the null
hypothesis were true, **not proof**. Evaluate reliability and accuracy, sample size, sampling bias,
validity, limits of time, access and equipment, and realistic improvements.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Accuracy | How close a measurement is to the true value |
| Reliability | Whether repeating the method would give similar results |
| Validity | Whether the method measures what the question needs |
| Anomaly | A result that does not fit the pattern; explain it rather than delete it |

**Model evaluation sentence:** *"Velocity was measured with a float over only three runs per site,
so readings were affected by wind and surface eddies; using a flow meter at 60 per cent of depth with
five readings per site would have improved accuracy and reliability."*

Your **conclusion** should return to the question, state whether each hypothesis is supported, refer
to the key evidence and link back to the theory you used.

## Exam traps

- A question too broad to answer with the data you can collect.
- Methods described but not justified.
- Treating correlation as proof of cause.
- A long method with a thin evaluation.
- Deleting anomalies instead of discussing them.
- Generic improvements ("collect more data") with no explanation of how they would help.

## Self-test

1. Why write a null hypothesis?
2. Distinguish systematic from stratified sampling.
3. Why run a pilot study?
4. Which test examines association between two ranked variables?
5. What must every graph include?
6. What is a transect?
7. Distinguish accuracy from reliability.
8. Calculate chi-squared for observed values 25 and 15 when 20 is expected in each.
9. What does a nearest neighbour value close to 0 suggest?
10. What is a Likert scale used for?
11. What should a conclusion do?

**Answers:** 1. Statistical tests examine whether data are consistent with no relationship or
difference. 2. Systematic uses regular intervals; stratified represents each sub-group in
proportion. 3. To find problems in methods, timing and equipment before main collection. 4.
Spearman's rank. 5. A title, labelled axes with units, and a source, referred to in the text.
6. A line along which data are collected at intervals to show change with distance. 7. Accuracy is
closeness to the true value; reliability is consistency when repeated. 8. (5 squared / 20) + (5 squared
/ 20) = 1.25 + 1.25 = 2.5. 9. A clustered pattern. 10. Turning opinions into numerical data. 11. Answer
the question, state whether hypotheses are supported, cite key evidence and link to theory.
