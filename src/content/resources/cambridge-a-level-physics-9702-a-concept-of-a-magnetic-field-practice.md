## Section A

**1.** State what is meant by a magnetic field. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Gravitational, electric and magnetic fields are all fields of force.

**(a)** Explain what is meant by a field of force. **[1]**

**(b)** For each of the three fields, state what produces the field and what it acts on. **[3]**

**(c)** State one way in which the field lines of a magnetic field differ from those of the electric field due to an isolated positive point charge. **[1]**

**8.** A single bar magnet lies on a sheet of paper.

**(a)** Sketch (or describe) the magnetic field around it, showing at least four field lines. **[3]**

**(b)** State where the field is strongest and how your diagram shows this. **[2]**

**9.** Two identical bar magnets are placed in a line with their north poles facing each other.

**(a)** Describe the pattern of field lines in the region between the poles. **[2]**

**(b)** State what is meant by a neutral point and state where it is in this arrangement. **[2]**

**(c)** A small compass is placed at the neutral point. State and explain the direction in which it points. **[2]**

**10.** A uniform magnetic field acts at right angles to this page, directed into the page.

**(a)** Describe how this field is represented on a diagram. **[2]**

**(b)** The field is made twice as strong. State how the diagram should change. **[1]**

**11.** A student says: "A magnetic field is made only by magnets, so a wire can only feel a force in a magnetic field if it is made of iron."

**(a)** Identify **two** errors in the student's statement. **[2]**

**(b)** Describe a simple observation that shows a current produces a magnetic field. **[2]**

**12.** The flat pole faces of a large U-shaped magnet are parallel and 3.0 cm apart.

**(a)** Describe the field between the pole faces near the centre of the gap. **[2]**

**(b)** Describe how the field differs near the edges of the pole faces. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest why questions on forces and induction usually specify that a wire or coil is "near the centre" of such a magnet. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** A region in which a moving charge (or a current-carrying conductor) [1] or a magnetic material experiences a force [1].

**2.** Moving charges (including electric currents) [1]; permanent magnets [1].

**3.** There is no magnetic force [1], because a magnetic field exerts a force only on a moving charge, and the proton is stationary [1].

**4.** The direction of the field: the tangent to the line, with the arrow showing the direction a compass north pole points [1]. The strength of the field: the closer together the lines, the stronger the field [1].

**5.** The field has only one direction at any point, and crossing lines would give two directions at the crossing point [1].

**6.** B [1].

**7. (a)** A region in which an object experiences a force without contact [1].

**(b)** Gravitational: produced by mass, acts on mass [1]. Electric: produced by charge, acts on charge whether moving or not [1]. Magnetic: produced by moving charges or permanent magnets, acts on moving charges (currents) and magnetic materials [1].

**(c)** Magnetic field lines form closed loops, whereas the electric lines start on the positive charge and go out to infinity (or end on negative charge) [1].

**8. (a)** Lines leave the north pole and enter the south pole, curving round the magnet [1]; arrows on each line point from N to S outside the magnet [1]; the pattern is symmetrical and no lines cross [1].

**(b)** At (near) the poles [1]; the field lines are closest together there [1].

**9. (a)** Lines from each north pole curve away from the line joining the magnets (bending outwards to the south poles) [1]; the lines from the two poles do not meet or cross, so the pattern is symmetrical about the midpoint [1].

**(b)** A point where the magnetic fields cancel, so the resultant flux density is zero [1]. It is midway between the two north poles [1].

**(c)** It points along the Earth's magnetic field (approximately north) [1], because the fields of the two magnets cancel there and only the background (Earth's) field remains [1].

**10. (a)** By a pattern of crosses (×) [1], evenly spaced in a regular grid to show the field is uniform [1].

**(b)** The crosses are drawn closer together (more crosses per unit area) [1].

**11. (a)** Magnetic fields are also produced by moving charges (currents), not only by magnets [1]. A current-carrying conductor of any material (such as copper) experiences a force in a magnetic field; it need not be iron [1].

**(b)** A compass placed near a wire is deflected when a current is switched on [1], and the deflection reverses when the current is reversed (or disappears when the current is switched off) [1].

**12. (a)** The field is uniform [1]: the lines are straight, parallel and equally spaced, running from the N face to the S face [1].

**(b)** Near the edges the lines bulge outwards [1], so they are further apart and the field is weaker (and not uniform) [1].

**(c)** So that the flux density can be taken as uniform, with a single value of B for the whole wire or coil [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving "moving" out of the definition of a magnetic field.
- Drawing field lines without arrows, or with arrows from S to N outside a magnet.
- Drawing lines that cross or that stop in mid-air.
- Saying a compass at a neutral point "spins" or "has no direction", instead of naming the background field.
- Showing a uniform field with uneven spacing.
- Writing that a magnetic field acts on stationary charges.
