## Core facts

- Weak acids and weak bases **dissociate only slightly** in aqueous solution; the equilibrium lies well to the left.
- Most of a weak acid stays as undissociated HA molecules (or ions such as NH₄⁺).
- A weak base such as ammonia accepts protons from water only to a small extent: NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻.
- The specification uses a dissociation constant for weak acids only (Ka). No calculation with a constant for weak bases is listed.
- Ka depends only on temperature. Changing the concentration changes the pH and the fraction dissociated, never Ka.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Weak acid | An acid that dissociates only slightly in aqueous solution |
| Strong acid | An acid that dissociates completely in aqueous solution |
| Ka | The acid dissociation constant: Ka = [H⁺][A⁻] / [HA], units mol dm⁻³ |
| pKa | −log₁₀ Ka; no units |
| Concentrated / dilute | How many moles of acid are dissolved per dm³; unrelated to strength |
| Fraction dissociated | [H⁺] ÷ initial [HA] for a weak acid on its own |

## The expression and the rearrangement

```
HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻
Ka = [H⁺][A⁻] / [HA]
```

Approximations for a weak acid **on its own** in water:

1. [H⁺] = [A⁻], because H⁺ from water is negligible.
2. [HA]equilibrium ≈ [HA]initial, because so little dissociates.

So:

```
Ka = [H⁺]² / [HA]
[H⁺] = √(Ka × [HA])
[HA] = [H⁺]² / Ka
```

## Conversions

| From | To | Operation |
|---|---|---|
| Ka | pKa | pKa = −log₁₀ Ka |
| pKa | Ka | Ka = 10^(−pKa) |
| pH | [H⁺] | [H⁺] = 10^(−pH) |
| [H⁺] | pH | pH = −log₁₀[H⁺] |

**Strength check:** larger Ka = smaller pKa = stronger acid. A change of 1 in pKa is a tenfold change in Ka.

## Patterns worth knowing

- **Dilution:** a tenfold dilution raises the pH of a weak acid by about 0.5 (because [H⁺] ∝ √c), compared with 1.0 for a strong acid. A hundredfold dilution raises it by about 1.0.
- **Dilution increases the fraction dissociated**: fewer particles per dm³ favours the side with two particles.
- **Same concentration, different acids:** the pH difference is half the pKa difference.
- **Sense check:** a weak acid always has a higher pH than a strong acid of the same concentration.

## Exam traps

- Writing the Ka expression upside down, or putting water in it.
- Giving Ka with units of mol² dm⁻⁶ (that is Kw).
- Rounding [H⁺] to two significant figures before squaring, which shifts Ka in the second figure.
- Using √(Ka × [HA]) for a solution that also contains the salt NaA. That is a buffer, so [A⁻] ≠ [H⁺].
- Calling a weak acid "dilute" or a strong acid "concentrated".
- Saying that diluting a weak acid changes Ka.
- Stating only one approximation when the question asks for two.

## Quick self-test

1. Define a weak acid.
2. Write the Ka expression for hydrocyanic acid, HCN.
3. Explain why Ka has units of mol dm⁻³.
4. An acid has pKa 3.86. Calculate Ka.
5. An acid has Ka = 4.5 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³. Calculate pKa.
6. HCN has Ka = 6.2 × 10⁻¹⁰ mol dm⁻³. Calculate the pH of a 0.150 mol dm⁻³ solution.
7. A 0.0800 mol dm⁻³ solution of a weak acid has pH 2.95. Calculate Ka.
8. Ethanoic acid has pKa 4.76. What concentration of ethanoic acid has pH 3.40?
9. Acid X has pKa 4.20 and acid Y has pKa 4.87. Which is stronger, and by what factor is its Ka larger?
10. By how much does the pH of a weak acid change when it is diluted by a factor of 100?
11. State the two approximations used when calculating the pH of a weak acid from Ka.
12. An acid has Ka = 1.4 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³. Use the usual method to estimate the percentage dissociated in a 0.0100 mol dm⁻³ solution, and comment on the result.

### Answers

1. An acid that **dissociates only slightly** (partially) in aqueous solution.
2. **Ka = [H⁺][CN⁻] / [HCN]**
3. Two concentration terms on top and one below: (mol dm⁻³)² ÷ mol dm⁻³ = **mol dm⁻³**.
4. Ka = 10^(−3.86) = **1.38 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³**
5. pKa = −log₁₀(4.5 × 10⁻⁴) = **3.35**
6. [H⁺] = √(6.2 × 10⁻¹⁰ × 0.150) = 9.64 × 10⁻⁶ mol dm⁻³, so pH = **5.02**
7. [H⁺] = 10^(−2.95) = 1.12 × 10⁻³; Ka = (1.12 × 10⁻³)² / 0.0800 = **1.57 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³**
8. Ka = 1.74 × 10⁻⁵; [H⁺] = 3.98 × 10⁻⁴; [HA] = [H⁺]² / Ka = **9.12 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³**
9. **X** (lower pKa). The difference is 0.67, so its Ka is 10^0.67 ≈ **4.7 times** larger.
10. [H⁺] falls by √100 = 10, so the pH **rises by 1.00**.
11. [H⁺] = [A⁻] (H⁺ from water ignored); the equilibrium [HA] equals the starting concentration (dissociation is slight).
12. [H⁺] = √(1.4 × 10⁻⁵) = 3.74 × 10⁻³, which is **37 %** of 0.0100. That is not "slight", so approximation 2 is poor here and the estimate is unreliable.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does diluting a weak acid change Ka?**
No. Ka is an equilibrium constant and depends only on temperature. Dilution changes the pH and increases the fraction of acid dissociated, but Ka stays the same.

**How do I rank acids given a mix of Ka and pKa values?**
Convert every value to the same form first. Then the largest Ka, or the smallest pKa, is the strongest acid.

**Is ammonium a weak acid?**
Yes. NH₄⁺ ⇌ NH₃ + H⁺, so Ka = [NH₃][H⁺] / [NH₄⁺]. It is the conjugate acid of the weak base ammonia.
