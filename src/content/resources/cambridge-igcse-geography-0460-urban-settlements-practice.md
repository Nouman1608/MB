These questions cover topic 1.6 Urban settlements in Theme 1 (Population and settlement) of the Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026. All towns, cities and survey data in the data questions are fictional and the data are invented.

Revise first with the [Urban Settlements study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-geography-0460-urban-settlements/) and the [Urban Settlements revision notes](/resources/igcse-geography-0460-urban-settlements-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** State what the letters CBD stand for, and give two characteristics of a CBD. **[3]**

**2.** Define the term "urban sprawl". **[2]**

**3.** Give two reasons why modern industrial estates are often located on the edge of cities. **[2]**

**4.** Define the term "gentrification". **[2]**

**5.** Name two types of pollution found in urban areas and give one cause of each. **[4]**

## Section B

**6.** Students recorded building heights and land values along a transect from the centre of a fictional city.

| Distance from city centre (km) | Average building height (storeys) | Land value index (centre = 100) |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 22 | 100 |
| 1 | 9 | 54 |
| 2 | 4 | 31 |
| 4 | 2 | 18 |
| 8 | 2 | 12 |

**(a)** Describe how building height changes with distance from the city centre. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage fall in the land value index between 0 km and 2 km. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why land values change with distance from the city centre. **[3]**

**7.** The council of the fictional town of Hartwell plans to build 2,000 new homes on farmland at the rural-urban fringe, rather than on a former factory site in the inner city.

**(a)** Give two advantages of building on the farmland site. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why some groups of people may oppose the plan. **[3]**

**8.** Explain how the pattern of land use in cities in lower-income countries can differ from that in cities in high-income countries. **[4]**

**9.** Describe and explain one method that a city can use to reduce traffic congestion. **[4]**

**10.** For a named urban area you have studied, describe its problems and explain the solutions that have been tried. **[7]**

**11.** Explain the effects of urban sprawl on people and on the natural environment. **[5]**

**12.** "Good planning can solve the problems of urban areas." To what extent do you agree? Use examples. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Central Business District [1]. Tall buildings such as office blocks [1]. High land values, with shops, offices and banks competing for space [1]. Also credit few residents or heavy traffic during the day. **[3]**

**2.** The uncontrolled spread of a city outwards [1] into the surrounding countryside, often as low-density housing and roads [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Land is cheaper and plots are larger, allowing space for big buildings and parking [1]. There is easy access to main roads and motorways for lorries and workers [1]. **[2]**

**4.** The process by which better-off people move into an older, lower-income area and renovate housing [1], which can raise property prices and rents so that original residents are pushed out [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Air pollution [1], caused by vehicle exhaust or factory emissions [1]. Noise pollution [1], caused by traffic, aircraft or construction [1]. Also credit water pollution (untreated sewage or industrial waste) or visual pollution (litter, graffiti, derelict buildings). **[4]**

**6. (a)** Building height falls sharply with distance, from 22 storeys at the centre to 4 storeys at 2 km [1]. Beyond 4 km it stays at 2 storeys [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** (100 − 31) ÷ 100 × 100 = 69% [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** The centre is the most accessible point in the city, where transport routes meet [1]. Shops and offices compete for space there, so land values are highest [1]. Further out, land is less accessible and more plentiful, so there is less competition and values fall [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Accessibility is the key idea; "the centre is busy" is a description, not an explanation.

**7. (a)** Greenfield land is cheaper to build on because it does not need clearing or decontaminating [1]. There is more space for gardens, roads and parking, which may attract buyers [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Farmers and environmental groups may object to the loss of farmland and wildlife habitats [1]. Residents of nearby villages may fear more traffic, noise and pressure on local schools [1]. Others may argue the inner-city site should be used first, to regenerate a derelict area and reduce urban sprawl [1]. **[3]**

**8.** In lower-income countries, wealthy people often live close to the CBD or along main routes, whereas in high-income countries many better-off people live in the suburbs [1]. Large informal or squatter settlements grow on the edge of the city [1], and on hazardous land such as steep slopes and flood plains [1], whereas the edge of high-income cities is mostly planned suburbs and retail or business parks [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Method: a congestion charge, such as London's from 2003 [1]. Drivers pay a daily fee to enter the central zone at busy times [1]. This makes some people switch to public transport, cycling or walking, or travel at other times [1], so fewer vehicles enter the zone and journeys are faster, although it is unpopular with some drivers and businesses [1]. Also credit a bus rapid transit system or a road pricing scheme. **[4]**

**10.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point. Indicative creditworthy content, for example for Singapore:

Named city with context: a small island city-state with limited land [1]. Problem: shortage of land for housing and growth [1]. Problem: risk of severe traffic congestion as incomes rise [1]. Solution: public high-rise flats built in planned new towns, now housing around four in five residents [1]. Solution: a quota system for cars and Electronic Road Pricing from 1998 [1]. Solution: the Mass Rapid Transit railway from 1987 and land reclamation [1]. Explanation of how a solution works, for example road pricing makes drivers pay more at busy times so some switch to the railway [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Top answers explain how each solution tackles its problem; a list of schemes without explanation stays in the middle level.

**11.** People: longer commuting distances and times, so more car use and congestion [1]. People: higher costs of extending water, sewers, roads and bus services to new areas [1]. People: inner-city areas may decline as investment moves outwards [1]. Environment: loss of farmland, woodland and habitats [1]. Environment: more air pollution and surface runoff from roads and roofs, increasing flood risk [1]. **[5]**

**12.** Indicative content:

Agree: planning can reduce congestion, as Singapore's road pricing and car quotas show [1]. Agree: public housing programmes can replace informal settlements, as in Singapore [1]. Agree: green belts and brownfield-first policies can limit urban sprawl [1]. Disagree: solutions are expensive, and many fast-growing cities cannot afford them [1]. Disagree: rapid in-migration can outpace planning, so informal settlements grow faster than housing can be built [1]. Disagree: solutions can create new problems, such as high housing costs or gentrification displacing residents [1]. Disagree: some problems, such as air pollution, have causes outside the city's control [1]. Judgement: planning can greatly reduce urban problems where governments have money and control of land, but it rarely solves them completely [1]. **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing zones with no reasons, when the question says "explain".
- Using percentage change formulae with the wrong starting value.
- Solutions given with no named place and no evaluation.
- Confusing urban sprawl with urbanisation.
- One-sided answers to "to what extent" questions.
