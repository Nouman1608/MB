Section B of Pearson Edexcel International GCSE English Literature (4ET1) Component 2 is **one
30-mark essay, chosen from two questions, on your literary heritage text**, in an open-book exam with
an unmarked prescribed edition. Allow about 45 minutes. For the full explanation, use the
[Section B literary heritage study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-english-literature-4et1-section-b-literary-heritage-texts/).

## The essentials

| Detail | Section B |
|---|---|
| Marks | 30 (AO1 10, AO2 10, AO4 10) |
| Choice | One of two questions on your text |
| Book | Prescribed edition, completely unmarked |
| Time | About 45 of the paper's 90 minutes |
| Unique feature | AO4 context: not assessed in Section A |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning for this essay |
|---|---|
| AO4 | Understanding the relationship between a text and the contexts of its writing and reception |
| Integrated context | A contextual point that explains a specific moment or method in the same sentence or paragraph |
| Bolt-on context | A fact placed next to analysis without being connected to it |
| Reception | How different audiences or readers, then and now, respond to the text |
| Literary context | The genre or tradition a text belongs to, such as tragedy or the novel of growing up |
| Time frames | The gap between when a text is set, when it was written and when it is read |

## A context bank for each set text

Learn two or three of these for your text and practise linking each to a specific moment.

| Text | Context points to connect to the text |
|---|---|
| *Romeo and Juliet* | A father's authority over his daughter's marriage; honour and public violence between rival households; tragedy as a form that announces its ending in the Prologue |
| *Macbeth* | Belief that kings ruled by God's will, so regicide disturbs nature; James I's interest in witchcraft; written soon after the Gunpowder Plot of 1605 |
| *The Merchant of Venice* | Widespread prejudice against Jewish people in Elizabethan England; Christian disapproval of lending money at interest; Venice as a city of trade and law; changed modern reception of Shylock |
| *Pride and Prejudice* | Property passing to male heirs (the Longbourn entail); marriage as women's main route to security; rank and manners among the landed gentry; published 1813 |
| *Great Expectations* | Rigid Victorian ideas of class and the "gentleman"; convicts transported to Australia; first published in weekly parts, which shaped its cliffhangers |
| *The Scarlet Letter* | Seventeenth-century Puritan Boston where religious and civil law overlapped; Hawthorne writing in 1850 about his ancestors' society; public punishment as community spectacle |

## Paragraph formula

**Point** (answers the question) + **method** (how the writer shows it) + **context** (why that method
matters for the period or a later reader) + **link back** to the question. Every paragraph, not just
the last one.

## Choosing the question in two minutes

- Can I name three moments from different parts of the text for this question?
- Can I attach at least two different contexts?
- Does the wording ("explore the ways", "how does") let me write about methods, not just events?

If one option ticks all three, take it.

## Timing reminder

2 minutes choose, 5 plan, 3 locate passages, about 32 write, 3 check. If you are behind, cut a
paragraph, not the context.

## Exam traps

- A history paragraph at the start, unlinked to the text.
- Context only in the conclusion.
- Biography that does not explain anything in the text.
- Copying long quotations from the open book instead of analysing a few words.
- Calling characters "real people" instead of the writer's constructions.

## Self-test

1. How many of Section B's 30 marks are for AO4?
2. What is the difference between bolt-on and integrated context?
3. Name two time frames that matter for *The Scarlet Letter*.
4. Give one social context for *Pride and Prejudice* and one moment it explains.
5. Why should you aim to use the beginning, a turning point and the ending of the text?
6. What may and may not be in your exam copy of the set text?
7. What is "reception" context? Give one example.

**Answers:** 1. Ten. 2. Bolt-on context sits beside the analysis without explaining it; integrated
context explains why a specific moment or method matters. 3. The seventeenth-century Puritan setting
and the time of writing, 1850 (a modern reading is a third). 4. For example, the entail of Longbourn
to Mr Collins explains Mrs Bennet's urgency to see her daughters married. 5. It shows knowledge of
the whole text and awareness of structure (AO1 and AO2). 6. The prescribed edition may be used, but
it must be completely unmarked: no notes or highlighting. 7. How different audiences respond
at different times, for example a modern audience reading Shylock as a victim of prejudice more
readily than many Elizabethan audiences might have.
