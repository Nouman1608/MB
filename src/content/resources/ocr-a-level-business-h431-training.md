## What training is

**Training** is the process of teaching employees the skills, knowledge and attitudes they need to do their jobs well, now or in the future. It differs from **development**, which is the broader growth of an employee's abilities for future roles, but the two overlap and exam answers often use them together.

Businesses train for several reasons: to bring new staff up to standard, to introduce new technology or procedures, to meet legal requirements (such as health and safety or food hygiene), to improve quality and productivity, to prepare staff for promotion, and to motivate and retain people.

## Methods of training

| Method | Meaning | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Induction | Introduction of a new employee to the business: layout, rules, safety, people, procedures | Settles new staff quickly; covers legal safety briefings | Takes time; can overwhelm with information; short-lived |
| On-the-job | Learning while doing the job, with a supervisor or experienced colleague | Cheap; relevant; trainee produces something | Quality depends on the trainer; bad habits passed on; slows the trainer |
| Off-the-job | Training away from the workplace: courses, workshops, e-learning, conferences | Specialist trainers; new ideas; no pressure of work | Costly; trainee absent; skills may not transfer to the job |
| Academic | Education leading to formal qualifications, such as a degree or A level | Recognised qualification; broad knowledge | Long; costly; less practical |
| Vocational | Training for a specific job or trade, such as plumbing or catering | Directly relevant; practical | Narrow; may limit mobility |
| Apprenticeship | A job combined with structured training and a qualification, usually over one to several years | Trainee earns while learning; employer shapes the skills | Long; costs of wages and supervision; trainee may leave afterwards |

Business may use a mix: induction first, on-the-job training for the daily tasks, off-the-job for specialist skills and an apprenticeship for the long-term supply of skilled staff.

## Training needs analysis (TNA)

A **training needs analysis** identifies the gap between the skills and knowledge employees have and those the business needs, so training can be targeted. Its steps are:

1. Identify the skills the business needs, now and in future (from the business plan, new technology, standards).
2. Assess the skills employees have (appraisals, skills audits, performance data, observation, surveys).
3. Find the gaps, by individual, team and business.
4. Rank the gaps by importance and cost.
5. Choose training methods and plan them.
6. Evaluate the results afterwards.

### Worked example 1: a TNA and its payback

Haddow Print (invented) has 30 staff. A skills audit finds that 18 staff lack the digital skills needed for a new press, and 9 staff have not had recent health and safety training.

Costs: digital training £420 each, health and safety training £150 each.

- Digital: 18 × £420 = **£7,560**.
- Health and safety: 9 × £150 = **£1,350**.
- Total = **£8,910**.

Benefits (estimates): errors on the new press currently cost £14,000 a year, and training should cut them by 40%: 40% × £14,000 = **£5,600**. Accidents cost £4,200 a year and training should cut them by 50%: 50% × £4,200 = **£2,100**. Total annual benefit = **£7,700**.

Payback = £8,910 ÷ £7,700 = **1.2 years**, about 14 months.

Without the TNA, Haddow might have trained all 30 staff in both areas: 30 × £420 + 30 × £150 = £12,600 + £4,500 = £17,100, almost twice the targeted cost, and much of it wasted on staff who did not need it.

Evaluation of the TNA:

- It focuses spending on the real gaps and saves £8,190 compared with training everyone (£17,100 − £8,910).
- It links training to the business's aims and to measurable benefits.
- The benefits are estimates. If errors fall by only 20%, the saving is £2,800 and the annual benefit £4,900, so payback is 1.8 years.
- A TNA takes time and needs honest information from staff, who may fear that gaps will be used against them.
- Stakeholders: employees gain relevant skills and confidence; customers get fewer errors; owners spend more efficiently; managers get a basis for planning.

## Comparing on-the-job and off-the-job training

### Worked example 2: costing two methods

Haddow trains new press operators. Two methods are compared for one trainee.

**On-the-job.** A supervisor spends 16 hours at £22 an hour = **£352**. The trainee is only 60% productive for three weeks. A full week's output is worth £450, so the lost output is 40% × 3 × £450 = **£540**. Total = £352 + £540 = **£892**.

**Off-the-job.** A course costs £650, the trainee is away for 3 days and each day of lost output is worth £90 (3 × £90 = £270), and travel costs £60. Total = £650 + £270 + £60 = **£980**.

Off-the-job costs £88 more per trainee (£980 − £892).

Evaluation:

- On-the-job is slightly cheaper and gives practical experience on the actual machine, but it relies on the supervisor, who loses 16 hours of their own work.
- Off-the-job gives specialist teaching and a recognised certificate, which may matter for safety or customers, but the trainee is away from the machine and may find it hard to apply what was taught.
- For 12 trainees the difference is 12 × £88 = **£1,056**, small relative to the importance of competence on a machine that is expensive.

**Judgement.** A mix is best: a short off-the-job course for the safety and theory, then on-the-job coaching on the press. The cost difference is small compared with the cost of errors on the machine.

## Impact and importance of training

Benefits to the business:

- higher productivity and quality;
- fewer accidents and less waste;
- flexibility, since staff can do several jobs;
- motivation and loyalty, so lower turnover;
- a supply of skilled staff for promotion;
- compliance with the law and standards;
- a better reputation, which helps recruitment.

Costs and risks:

- direct costs (fees, trainers, materials) and indirect costs (lost output while training);
- trained staff may leave and take the skills to a competitor ("poaching");
- training may not change behaviour if the working environment does not support it;
- the benefit is hard to measure.

Stakeholders: employees (skills, pay, security, but time pressure), owners (cost and return), customers (quality and service), managers (a more capable team), the community (a skilled workforce and local jobs).

Training matters most where technology changes quickly, where safety is critical and where customer service is the competitive edge.

## Recommending training methods

A good recommendation:

1. starts from the gap identified by a TNA;
2. matches the method to the skill (practical skills on the job, theory or certificates off the job, long-term trade skills by apprenticeship);
3. considers numbers (many trainees suggest in-house group training; few suggest external courses);
4. considers cost and the effect on production;
5. says how success will be measured, such as error rates or accidents;
6. states the main risk (for example that trained staff leave) and how to reduce it (for instance by a commitment to stay, or career prospects).

## Common mistakes

- Treating training as a cost only, with no benefit.
- Confusing induction with on-the-job training. Induction is introduction to the business; on-the-job is learning the job by doing it.
- Saying that off-the-job training is always better because it uses experts.
- Forgetting indirect costs such as lost output.
- Ignoring the TNA, which should come first.
- Assuming that trained staff will stay.

## Exam technique

When asked to distinguish between methods, give a defining feature of each. When evaluating, quantify cost and benefit if the case gives figures, name the stakeholders affected and conclude with a judgement that depends on the case, for example staff turnover or how quickly technology changes.
