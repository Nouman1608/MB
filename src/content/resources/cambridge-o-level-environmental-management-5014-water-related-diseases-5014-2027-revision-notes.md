Condensed notes for sub-topic 3.3 Water-related diseases in the Cambridge O Level Environmental Management (5014) syllabus for 2027 to 2029. The [water-related diseases study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-environmental-management-5014-water-related-diseases-5014-2027/) explains each strategy in full; the [water-related diseases practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-environmental-management-5014-water-related-diseases-5014-2027-practice/) let you apply them.

## Two diseases, two routes

| | Malaria | Cholera |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | Plasmodium parasite | Bacteria |
| How it reaches people | Bite of an infected female Anopheles mosquito (vector) | Water or food contaminated with faeces |
| Link to water | Mosquitoes breed in still water | Bacteria travel in water |
| Control aims | Stop bites; reduce mosquito numbers | Break the faeces-to-mouth route |

## 3.3.1 Malaria transmission in three steps

1. Infected female Anopheles mosquito bites a human → Plasmodium passes into the blood.
2. The human is infected.
3. A non-infected mosquito feeds on the infected human's blood → takes in the parasite → can infect the next person.

Extra detail that helps explanations:
- Only females bite; they need blood to produce eggs.
- Eggs, larvae and pupae develop in still water: puddles, tanks, tyres, blocked drains, irrigation channels.
- Malaria often rises after heavy rain or near new reservoirs and irrigation schemes.

## 3.3.2 Malaria control: eight strategies

**Personal protection**: stops a person being bitten or infected.
- Nets, best treated with insecticide; used at night.
- Insect repellent on skin or clothing.
- Vaccination, given to young children in some countries.
- Antimalarial drugs, to prevent or treat infection.

**Vector control**: reduces the number of mosquitoes.
- Cover or drain breeding areas.
- Spray large areas with insecticides.
- Sterilise male mosquitoes and release them.
- Biological control, for example fish that eat larvae.

## 3.3.3 Cholera control: four strategies

- Handwashing with soap.
- Adequate sanitation and sewage treatment.
- Potable water supply by boiling and chlorination.
- Vaccination.

## 3.3.4 Benefits and limitations in brief

- **Nets**: cheap and effective while asleep / must be used nightly; no protection outdoors in the evening.
- **Repellent**: protects outdoors / needs reapplying; ongoing cost.
- **Vaccines**: cut severe illness / partial protection; several doses; cost and supply.
- **Antimalarial drugs**: prevent or cure / cost; side effects; parasite resistance.
- **Draining sites**: no chemicals / labour; impossible in rainy season; wetland loss.
- **Insecticide spraying**: fast, large area / harms other species; resistance; repeated cost.
- **Sterile males**: targets only mosquitoes / expensive, repeated releases.
- **Biological control**: chemical-free / introduced predators may harm native species.
- **Handwashing**: very cheap / needs soap, water and habit change.
- **Sanitation and sewage treatment**: removes the source / high cost.
- **Boiling and chlorination**: immediate / fuel and chemical supply needed.
- **Cholera vaccine**: rapid protection / fades with time; does not remove contamination.

General judgement: combine strategies; long-term measures cost more at first but less over time.

## Data skills

- **Seasonal pattern**: malaria cases usually peak a few weeks after the wettest months, because larvae need time to develop in new pools and the parasite needs time to spread between people and mosquitoes.
- **Percentage change** = (before − after) ÷ before × 100. For example, cases falling from 300 to 210 per 1,000 people is a 30% fall.
- **Comparison groups**: a village without the new strategy shows whether a fall was caused by the strategy or by something else, such as a dry year.
- **Cholera outbreaks** often follow floods or damage to water and sewage systems; link the date of the event to the rise in cases.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Vector | An organism that transmits a disease-causing organism between hosts |
| Anopheles | The type of mosquito whose females transmit malaria |
| Plasmodium | The parasite that causes malaria |
| Parasite | An organism that lives in or on a host and harms it |
| Breeding site | Still water where mosquito eggs and larvae develop |
| Insecticide-treated net | A net that blocks and kills mosquitoes |
| Antimalarial drug | Medicine that prevents or treats malaria |
| Sterile male release | Releasing males that cannot fertilise eggs, so fewer mosquitoes hatch |
| Biological control | Using a natural enemy to reduce a pest population |
| Sanitation | Safe disposal of human waste |
| Chlorination | Adding chlorine to water to kill microorganisms |
| Resistance | When a pest or parasite is no longer killed by a chemical or drug |

## Exam traps

- Malaria is not water-borne; never write "drinking dirty water causes malaria".
- The mosquito is the vector, not the cause.
- Always include step 3 of transmission (non-infected mosquito feeds on infected person).
- Only **female** Anopheles mosquitoes transmit malaria.
- Keep the two lists separate: nets and spraying are for malaria; handwashing and chlorination are for cholera.
- Vaccination appears in **both** lists.
- A "discuss" answer without limitations cannot reach the top marks.

## Self-test

1. Name the vector of malaria.
2. Name the parasite that causes malaria.
3. Why do only female mosquitoes bite?
4. How does a non-infected mosquito become infected?
5. Give two examples of mosquito breeding sites around a home.
6. Name the two groups of malaria control strategy.
7. How does releasing sterile male mosquitoes reduce mosquito numbers?
8. Give one limitation of spraying insecticides over large areas.
9. State the four cholera control strategies.
10. Give two ways of making water potable at home.
11. Why might antimalarial drugs become less effective over time?
12. Which strategy appears in both the malaria and the cholera lists?

## Answers

1. The female Anopheles mosquito.
2. Plasmodium.
3. They need a blood meal to produce eggs.
4. It feeds on the blood of an infected person and takes in the parasite.
5. Any two of: uncovered water tanks, discarded tyres or containers, puddles, blocked drains.
6. Personal protection and vector control.
7. Females that mate with them lay eggs that do not hatch, so the next generation is smaller.
8. It kills other insects and harms food chains, or mosquitoes become resistant, or it must be repeated at a cost.
9. Handwashing; adequate sanitation and sewage treatment; potable water supply by boiling and chlorination; vaccination.
10. Boiling and adding chlorine.
11. The parasite can become resistant to the drug.
12. Vaccination.
