## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define allocative efficiency. **[2]**

**2.** Which of the following best describes productive efficiency?

A: Producing the goods consumers value most

B: Producing at the lowest possible average cost

C: Producing where total revenue is greatest

D: Producing the largest output a firm can sell **[1]**

**3.** A firm's total costs are shown below (invented data).

| Output (units) | 50 | 100 | 150 | 200 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Total cost (£) | 600 | 900 | 1,200 | 1,800 |

**(a)** Calculate the average cost at each output. **[2]**

**(b)** State the productively efficient output. **[1]**

**4.** Country T's PPC passes through (50 consumer goods, 10 capital goods) and (35 consumer goods, 20 capital goods), in thousand units. Country T produces 35 thousand consumer goods and 10 thousand capital goods. Calculate how many more consumer goods it could produce without reducing capital goods, and state what this shows. **[2]**

**5.** State the condition for allocative efficiency in a single market with no externalities. **[1]**

**6.** Explain why a point on a PPC may be productively efficient but not allocatively efficient. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**7.** In the market for invented garden tools, demand is Qd = 300 − 20P and supply is Qs = 20P − 60, where P is in pounds.

**(a)** Calculate the equilibrium price and quantity. **[2]**

**(b)** Demand meets the price axis at £15 and supply at £3. Calculate consumer surplus and producer surplus. **[2]**

**(c)** A rule limits sales to 100 units. Calculate the deadweight loss and explain what it shows. **[3]**

**8.** Explain, with the aid of a diagram, why a recession is likely to cause productive inefficiency in an economy. **[4]**

**9.** Explain two reasons why centrally planned economies have often been productively inefficient. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how the price mechanism can lead to allocative efficiency. **[4]**

**11.** Explain why the free market output of a good that causes pollution is not allocatively efficient. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that a market economy allocates resources more efficiently than a planned economy. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Producing the combination of goods and services that society values most [1], where for each good price (marginal benefit) equals marginal cost [1]. **[2]**

**2.** **B** [1]. Option A describes allocative efficiency, and C and D describe other objectives. **[1]**

**3. (a)** AC = £12 and £9 at 50 and 100 units [1]; £8 and £9 at 150 and 200 units [1].

**(b)** **150 units**, where AC is lowest at £8 [1]. **[3]**

**4.** At 10 thousand capital goods the PPC allows 50 thousand consumer goods, so Country T could produce **15 thousand** more [1]. Its point is inside the PPC, so it is productively inefficient, with unemployed or wasted resources [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Price equals marginal cost (marginal benefit equals marginal cost) [1]. **[1]**

**6.** On the curve no resources are wasted, so more of one good needs less of the other [1]; but the mix produced may not match consumers' preferences, so only one point on the curve is allocatively efficient [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** 300 − 20P = 20P − 60, so 40P = 360 and P = **£9** [1]; Q = 300 − 180 = **120** [1].

**(b)** Consumer surplus = ½ × 120 × (15 − 9) = **£360** [1]. Producer surplus = ½ × 120 × (9 − 3) = **£360** [1].

**(c)** At 100 units buyers would pay (300 − 100) ÷ 20 = £10 and marginal cost is (100 + 60) ÷ 20 = £8 [1]. Deadweight loss = ½ × 20 × 2 = **£20** [1]. The 20 units not sold were worth more to buyers than they cost to make, so welfare is lost and the market is allocatively inefficient [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Read both prices at the restricted quantity; using the equilibrium price for either side gives the wrong triangle.

**8.** A PPC diagram with a point on the curve before the recession and a point inside it during the recession [1]. In a recession, falling aggregate demand leads firms to cut output and lay off workers, and machines stand idle [1]. The economy then produces less of both goods than its resources allow [1], so it is inside the PPC and productively inefficient; the PPC itself has not moved, because resources still exist [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Weak incentives: managers and workers do not keep profits from cutting costs, so there is little pressure to avoid waste [1], which raises the resources used per unit [1]. Output targets: plants judged on quantity may ignore cost and quality [1], for example by over-using raw materials to hit a target [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Prices act as signals: a rise in price shows that consumers want more of a good [1]. They act as incentives: higher prices raise profit, so firms move resources into making it [1]. They ration: goods go to those who value them enough to pay [1]. Output adjusts until price equals marginal cost, where the value of the last unit equals its cost, which is allocative efficiency [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Producers base output on marginal private cost and ignore the cost of pollution to third parties [1]. Marginal social cost is therefore above marginal private cost [1]. The market produces where marginal private benefit equals marginal private cost, which is more than where marginal social benefit equals marginal social cost [1]. The last units cost society more than they are worth, so there is overproduction and a welfare loss [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Award up to 10 marks across knowledge, application, analysis and evaluation.
- Market economies use the price mechanism to signal what consumers want [1], giving allocative efficiency where markets work well [1].
- The profit motive and competition push firms to cut costs, supporting productive efficiency [1].
- Planned economies lack the information that prices provide, so shortages and surpluses appear [1], and weak incentives lead to waste [1].
- But markets fail: externalities lead to over or underproduction [1], and goods with wide social benefits may be underprovided [1].
- Markets may also be dominated by a few firms that keep prices high, reducing efficiency [1].
- A planned economy can direct resources to priorities quickly, for example in wartime or for basic health care [1].
- Judgement: markets are usually more efficient for most consumer goods, but where externalities or poor information are large, some government role, as in a mixed economy, gives a more efficient result [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the condition under which each system does better; a judgement that "it depends" without saying on what earns little.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "producing a lot" for productive efficiency.
- Saying all points on the PPC are allocatively efficient.
- Reading the deadweight triangle from the wrong prices.
- Forgetting units on surplus answers.
- Evaluating economic systems on only one kind of efficiency.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between productive and allocative efficiency?**
Productive efficiency is producing with the least resources per unit, so an economy is on its PPC and firms are at their lowest average cost. Allocative efficiency is producing the mix and quantities of goods society values most, where price equals marginal cost.

**Is market equilibrium always allocatively efficient?**
Only when there are no externalities and buyers and sellers are well informed. Otherwise private costs and benefits differ from social ones and the market produces too much or too little.
