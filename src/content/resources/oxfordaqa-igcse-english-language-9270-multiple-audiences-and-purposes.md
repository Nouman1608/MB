**This guide covers the central demand of Paper 2 Section B in OxfordAQA International GCSE English
Language (9270).** The specification says "the focus of the writing task will be writing for
audience and purpose, which could be both multiple and diverse. The most successful responses will
demonstrate the students' ability to respond to this demand." The
[Paper 2 Section B writing study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-english-language-9270-section-b-writing/)
explains how to decode a task, use the sources and match form conventions; this page focuses on the
"multiple and diverse" demand itself: how to recognise it, plan for it, and show the examiner you
have met it.

## What "multiple" and "diverse" mean

**Multiple** means more than one. A task may name two or more audiences, or two or more purposes.

**Diverse** means different from each other: audiences with different interests, knowledge or
power, or purposes that pull in different directions. Writing for parents and students is multiple;
writing for parents who want stricter rules and students who want fewer is multiple **and** diverse.

The specification's sentence ends with a judgement: "the most successful responses" respond to this
demand. A response that serves one audience well and ignores the other cannot show that skill.

## Recognising the demand in a task

Underline every audience and every purpose verb. Then ask four questions.

1. **Who reads it first, and who else will read it?** A letter to a head teacher may be shared with
   governors; an article in a school magazine is read by students, staff and parents.
2. **Do the audiences agree?** If not, which will be hardest to win over?
3. **How many purposes are there, and in what order?** "Explain the problem and propose a solution"
   implies explanation first.
4. **Which purpose is the main one?** Usually the last verb in the task, or the one tied to an
   outcome (a vote, a decision, a change in behaviour).

## Kinds of multiple audience

| Kind | Example (original task wording) | What it demands |
|---|---|---|
| Two named groups | "residents and local businesses" | Address each group's concerns |
| Primary and secondary | A letter to the council that will be published in the local paper | Formal for the council; clear for the public |
| Expert and non-expert | An article for a science magazine read by students and teachers | Explain terms without talking down |
| Sympathetic and sceptical | A speech to supporters and doubters of a scheme | Encourage one, answer the other |
| Different ages | A leaflet for children and their parents | Clear, friendly tone that respects adults |

## Kinds of multiple purpose

| Combination | Order that usually works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Inform and persuade | Inform, then persuade | Explain what a scheme involves, then argue for it |
| Explain and advise | Explain, then advise | Explain why exam stress happens, then suggest strategies |
| Describe and evaluate | Describe, then evaluate | A review: what the place is like, then how good it is |
| Argue and reassure | Argue, with reassurance woven in | Support a change while addressing fears |

## Techniques for meeting the demand

**Segmented address.** Speak to each audience directly at the right moment: "If you are a parent,
you may be wondering… If you are a student, you have probably heard…". Use it sparingly, two or three
times, so it signals control rather than becoming a list.

**Register for the most powerful reader.** Set the register by the audience with most authority or
most to lose, then warm it slightly for the others. A letter to a head teacher and students stays
formal, but can include an inclusive "we" when addressing students.

**Anticipate the sceptic.** Diverse audiences mean someone disagrees. Name their concern fairly, then
answer it: "Business owners have every reason to worry about parking; the plan's answer is…".

**Choose examples for each audience.** An example that persuades students (a shorter queue at lunch)
may not move parents (the cost of food). Use one for each where the task names both.

**Signpost the purposes.** Let the structure show the shift from one purpose to another: a paragraph
that ends the explanation with "So what can be done?" moves the reader into the proposal.

**Use form features to divide audiences.** Sub-headings in a leaflet or article ("For families", "For
local shops") let different readers find what concerns them, while the introduction and conclusion
speak to all.

## Using the sources for different audiences

Because Section B is "linked with the reading sources from Section A", the sources can supply
material for each audience: a statistic from a report for an official reader, an anecdote from a
blog for a general reader. Rework the material in your own words and select only what serves a
named audience and purpose. Never copy whole sentences.

## Worked example: plan and paragraph

**Task (original):** *Write an article for your school website in which you explain the proposal to
start the school day later, and persuade both students and parents to support it.*

```
Audiences: students (likely to support) AND parents (may worry about work and childcare)
Purposes:  explain (first), then persuade (main)
Form:      website article -> headline, short paragraphs, sub-headings possible
Register:  semi-formal; respectful to parents, warm to students

Headline: "Half an Hour That Could Change the School Day"
1. Explain: what is proposed, when, and why it has been suggested
2. Students: better concentration in morning lessons (idea from a source, reworked)
3. Parents: address the work and childcare worry directly; breakfast club idea
4. Both: calmer mornings at home -> shared benefit
5. Close: how to give views; inclusive call to action
```

**Model paragraph (section 3):**

> Parents may reasonably ask what a later start means for them. If you leave for work at half past
> seven, a nine o'clock bell is not a gift; it is a problem. The proposal recognises this. The school
> plans to keep the gates open from the usual time, with a supervised breakfast club in the dining
> hall, so no child need wait outside and no parent need change their working day. For many
> families, the change would simply mean a calmer morning: a teenager who is awake, fed and ready,
> instead of one who is still half asleep at the first lesson.

*Why it works:* it addresses the sceptical audience directly and fairly; the short sentence "The
proposal recognises this" marks the turn from concern to reassurance; the final image offers a
benefit shared by parents and students, so the diverse audiences are brought together.

## Applying the writing objectives

- **W1:** forms, register and vocabulary adapted to each audience and purpose.
- **W2:** varied sentences: a short one to mark a turn, a longer one to explain a solution.
- **W3:** structure that sequences the purposes and signposts the move between audiences.
- **W4:** accuracy in every section, including direct-address sentences and sub-headings.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing for one audience only**, usually the one most like yourself.
- **Switching register wildly** between audiences.
- **Listing audiences** ("Dear parents, students, teachers and governors") and then ignoring them.
- **Mixing purposes randomly** instead of ordering them.
- **Copying source material** without adapting it to an audience.
- **Forgetting the sceptic**, so the persuasion never meets resistance.

## Exam technique

Read both tasks before choosing. In your plan, write the audiences and purposes at the top, mark
which audience is hardest to win, and give that audience its own paragraph. Check your draft
against each named audience: would each reader feel addressed?
