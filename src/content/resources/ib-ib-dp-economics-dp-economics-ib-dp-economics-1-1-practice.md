These questions cover sub-topic 1.1, What is economics?, from the IB Diploma Programme *Economics guide* (first assessment 2022). All of it is SL and HL. Countries and figures are invented. The [1] points in the answers are a revision aid, not IB marks. Revise first with the [1.1 study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-economics-dp-economics-ib-dp-economics-1-1/) or the [1.1 revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-economics-dp-economics-ib-dp-economics-1-1-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Define *opportunity cost*. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between microeconomics and macroeconomics, giving one example of a question each would study. **[3]**

**3.** For each resource, state the factor of production it belongs to, or state that it is not a factor of production. **[4]**

- A copper deposit beneath a hillside.
- A coffee roasting machine in a café.
- The coding skills of a software engineer.
- A sum of money held by a firm in a savings account.

**4.** Explain why a free good has no opportunity cost. **[2]**

**5.** Leakages from the circular flow of the fictional economy of Corvane are 210 billion corves and injections are 235 billion corves. Explain what will tend to happen to the flow of income. **[2]**

**6.** State the three basic economic questions every economy must answer. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** Corvane can produce the following maximum combinations of medical scanners and bicycles (both in thousands).

| Point | Scanners | Bicycles |
|---|---|---|
| A | 0 | 120 |
| B | 5 | 112 |
| C | 10 | 96 |
| D | 15 | 70 |
| E | 20 | 0 |

**(a)** Calculate the opportunity cost of one thousand scanners between points B and C and between points D and E. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why the opportunity cost changes as more scanners are produced. **[2]**

**(c)** Corvane is producing 10 thousand scanners and 80 thousand bicycles. Calculate how many bicycles are being lost and state one possible cause. **[2]**

**(d)** State whether 15 thousand scanners and 90 thousand bicycles is attainable, with a reason. **[1]**

**8.** Draw a PPC diagram to show the effect of an improvement in bicycle-making technology only, and explain the change. **[4]**

**9.** Explain, using a PPC diagram, why an economy that chooses to produce more capital goods today may achieve faster growth in the future. **[4]**

**10.** The fictional economy of Lisorra has the following annual figures, in billions of lisors: saving 50, taxes 140, imports 90, investment 70, government spending 130, exports 75.

**(a)** Calculate total leakages and total injections. **[2]**

**(b)** The government raises spending so that the flow of income is stable. Calculate the new level of government spending. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why the income flow can be stable even though saving does not equal investment. **[2]**

**11.** A city council has 40 million corves to spend. It ranks the options as: a flood barrier first, a sports stadium second and a bus depot third. It builds the flood barrier.

**(a)** Identify the opportunity cost of the decision. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why the bus depot is not part of the opportunity cost. **[2]**

**12.** Discuss whether a free market economy is the best way to answer the basic economic questions. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** The value of the next best alternative [1] given up when a choice is made [1].

**2.** Microeconomics studies individual decision-makers and markets, such as how a rise in coffee prices affects coffee demand [1]. Macroeconomics studies the economy as a whole, such as why national unemployment rose [1]. The difference is the scale of analysis: one market or agent against the whole economy [1].

**3.** Copper deposit: land [1]. Roasting machine: capital [1]. Coding skills: labour [1]. Money in a savings account: not a factor of production, because money produces nothing itself [1].

**4.** Using a free good does not reduce the amount available to anyone else [1], so no alternative use is given up and nothing is sacrificed [1].

**5.** Injections exceed leakages by 235 - 210 = 25 billion corves [1], so the flow of income tends to rise until leakages grow to match injections [1].

**6.** What (and how much) to produce [1]; how to produce it [1]; for whom to produce it [1].

**7. (a)** B to C: (112 - 96) / 5 = **3.2 thousand bicycles** per thousand scanners [1]. D to E: (70 - 0) / 5 = **14 thousand bicycles** per thousand scanners [1].

**(b)** Resources are not equally suited to both goods [1]; the last resources moved into scanners are specialist bicycle workers and machines, so each extra scanner costs more bicycles [1].

**(c)** Point C shows 96 thousand bicycles are possible with 10 thousand scanners, so 96 - 80 = **16 thousand bicycles** are lost [1]. Cause: unemployed workers or inefficient use of resources [1].

**(d)** Not attainable: point D shows a maximum of 70 thousand bicycles with 15 thousand scanners, so the point lies outside the curve [1].

**8.** Axes labelled with the two goods (for example scanners and bicycles) and the original PPC drawn [1]. The curve pivots outwards on the bicycle axis [1] while the scanner intercept stays fixed [1]. Explanation: better technology raises the maximum number of bicycles for any given resources, but maximum scanner output is unchanged [1].

**9.** PPC with capital goods on one axis and consumer goods on the other, and a point chosen with relatively more capital goods [1]. More capital goods add to the stock of machines and infrastructure [1], which raises productive capacity, shown by a larger outward shift of the PPC later [1]. The opportunity cost is fewer consumer goods now [1].

**10. (a)** Leakages = 50 + 140 + 90 = **280** [1]. Injections = 70 + 130 + 75 = **275** [1].

**(b)** Injections must rise by 5, so government spending = 130 + 5 = **135 billion lisors** [1].

**(c)** Stability requires total leakages to equal total injections [1]; a gap between saving and investment can be offset by gaps in the other pairs, such as taxes and government spending [1].

**11. (a)** The sports stadium, the next best alternative [1].

**(b)** Opportunity cost is only the single next best alternative [1]; the council could only ever have chosen one of the three, so the third-ranked bus depot was never the option given up [1].

**12.** For: prices signal what consumers want, so resources move to goods with most demand (what) [1]; competition gives firms an incentive to use the cheapest methods (how) [1]; incentives encourage innovation and effort [1]. Against: output goes to those with income, so people on low incomes may go without essentials (for whom) [1]; some goods may be under-provided or not provided at all [1]; prices may ignore costs to third parties [1]. This is why all real economies are mixed [1]. Judgement: a market answers "what" and "how" efficiently in many cases, but government is needed where "for whom" raises equity concerns, so the best answer is usually a mixed economy whose balance depends on the country's goals [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Listing two or three alternatives as the opportunity cost.
- Forgetting units in PPC calculations.
- Shifting the whole PPC when only one industry's technology improves.
- Comparing saving with investment alone instead of total leakages with total injections.
- Giving a one-sided answer to a "discuss" question.
