These notes condense content item 2.2.2 Price from Topic 2.2 Making marketing decisions in Theme 2 of the Pearson Edexcel GCSE (9-1) Business (1BS0) specification, Issue 2, July 2022. Worked examples are in the [price study guide](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-1bs0-price/) and exam-style questions in the [price practice questions](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-1bs0-price-practice/). Businesses and figures are invented.

## The specification in one view

- Pricing strategies (not listed in the specification; the standard ones are below).
- Influences on pricing strategies: **technology, competition, market segments, product life cycle**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Pricing strategy | The overall approach a business uses to set its price |
| Penetration pricing | A low launch price to win customers and market share quickly |
| Price skimming | A high launch price for a new product, lowered over time |
| Competitive pricing | Setting a price close to rivals' prices |
| Cost-plus pricing | Unit cost plus a percentage mark-up |
| Psychological pricing | A price just below a round number, such as £4.99 |
| Promotional pricing | A temporary price cut or deal |
| Price-sensitive | Customers whose buying changes a lot when price changes |

## Formulae and quick methods

These are standard forms; only revenue = price × quantity appears in Appendix 3.

- Revenue = price × quantity sold
- Contribution per unit = price − variable cost per unit
- Cost-plus price = unit cost + (unit cost × mark-up %)
- Percentage change in price = (new price − old price) ÷ old price × 100

**Quick calculation.** Fenby Soaps' unit cost is £2.40 and it uses a 75% mark-up. Mark-up = £2.40 × 0.75 = £1.80, so price = **£4.20**. A psychological price would be £3.99 or £4.19, depending on whether the owner will give up some profit to look cheaper.

## Matching strategy to situation

| Situation in the case | Likely strategy |
|---|---|
| New entrant in a crowded market | Penetration or competitive |
| Innovative product with no rivals yet | Skimming |
| Many near-identical products | Competitive |
| Owner wants a guaranteed profit on each unit | Cost-plus |
| Supermarket everyday goods | Psychological |
| Excess stock or a quiet season | Promotional |

## The four influences

**Technology:** price comparison sites push prices together; automation and online selling lower costs; online prices can change quickly by demand or time; new technology products can be skimmed.

**Competition:** many rivals limit price freedom; differentiation or few rivals allow higher prices; a rival's price cut forces a decision to match or not.

**Market segments:** different groups pay different prices: concessions for students and older people, off-peak and early-booking prices, basic and premium versions.

**Product life cycle:** skim or penetrate in introduction; prices adjust in growth as rivals arrive; competitive and promotional pricing in maturity; clearance cuts in decline.

## Price and revenue

A price cut raises revenue only if sales rise by a large enough percentage. Example: Corley Candles sells 500 candles at £12 (£6,000). At £10 it would need more than 600 sales to earn more: 600 × £10 = £6,000 exactly. If sales rise only to 560, revenue falls to £5,600.

## Responding to a rival's price cut

| Response | Gain | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Match the cut | Keeps customers who compare prices | Lower profit on every unit sold |
| Hold the price and promote quality or service | Keeps profit per unit | Some price-sensitive customers leave |
| Add value instead (a free extra) | Avoids a price war | Extra cost per unit; rivals may copy |

Which is best depends on how differentiated the product is and how price-sensitive its segment is. A strongly branded product can usually hold its price; a near-identical one usually cannot.

## Exam traps

- Defining a strategy with no reason it suits the business.
- Mixing up penetration with promotional pricing.
- Assuming lower prices always raise revenue or profit.
- Forgetting that cost-plus ignores customers and rivals.
- Listing all four influences instead of applying the one the case points to.

## Self-test

1. Define price skimming.
2. Give one situation in which penetration pricing is suitable.
3. Name the four influences on pricing strategies in the specification.
4. A product costs £8 to make. Calculate the cost-plus price with a 50% mark-up.
5. Why might a cinema charge students less than other customers?
6. State one way technology influences pricing.
7. A price rises from £16 to £20. Calculate the percentage increase.
8. Which pricing strategy would suit a mature product in a market with many similar rivals?
9. A café sells 400 drinks a day at £3. It cuts the price to £2.50 and sells 460. Calculate the change in daily revenue.
10. Give one risk of penetration pricing.
11. How might the price of an innovative product change as it moves from introduction to maturity?
12. Give one weakness of cost-plus pricing.

### Answers

1. Setting a high price when a new product is launched, then lowering it over time.
2. Entering a market with established rivals, where customers switch easily and the business wants market share quickly.
3. Technology, competition, market segments, product life cycle.
4. £8 + (£8 × 0.5) = **£12**.
5. Students are a segment with lower incomes; a lower price fills seats that might otherwise be empty while other customers pay full price.
6. Price comparison sites push prices together; automation lowers costs; online prices can change quickly; new technology can be skimmed.
7. (£20 − £16) ÷ £16 × 100 = **25%**.
8. Competitive pricing (with promotional offers to defend share).
9. Before: 400 × £3 = £1,200. After: 460 × £2.50 = £1,150. Revenue falls by **£50** a day.
10. Low profit per unit, or customers leave when the price later rises.
11. A high skimmed price at launch falls as rivals enter in growth, ending near rivals' prices in maturity.
12. It ignores what customers are willing to pay and what competitors charge.
