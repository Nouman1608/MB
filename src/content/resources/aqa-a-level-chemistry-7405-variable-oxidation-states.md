## Why oxidation states vary

In a transition metal, the 4s and 3d electrons are close in energy, so several different numbers of electrons can be lost. This gives ions with different oxidation states, such as Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺, or Mn²⁺, Mn⁴⁺ and Mn⁷⁺ (in MnO₄⁻). Main-group metals are different: calcium loses two electrons to reach a noble gas configuration, and removing a third from an inner shell needs far more energy.

## Vanadium: four oxidation states

Vanadate(V) ions are reduced stepwise when zinc is added to an acidified solution. The solution changes colour as each oxidation state forms.

| Oxidation state | Species in acid | Colour |
|---|---|---|
| +5 | VO₂⁺ (from vanadate(V), VO₃⁻) | Yellow |
| +4 | VO²⁺ | Blue |
| +3 | V³⁺ | Green |
| +2 | V²⁺ | Violet |

The colours seen in the flask are often mixed (for example green during the change from blue to green because yellow and blue overlap), so the sequence of colour changes you observe is yellow → green → blue → green → violet.

### Half-equations

```
VO₂⁺ + 2H⁺ + e⁻ → VO²⁺ + H₂O      (+5 to +4)
VO²⁺ + 2H⁺ + e⁻ → V³⁺ + H₂O       (+4 to +3)
V³⁺ + e⁻ → V²⁺                    (+3 to +2)
Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻
```

### Why zinc reduces all the way to V²⁺

Zinc is a strong enough reducing agent. The standard electrode potentials (illustrative values) are:

| Half-equation | E / V |
|---|---|
| Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Zn | –0.76 |
| V³⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ V²⁺ | –0.26 |
| VO²⁺ + 2H⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ V³⁺ + H₂O | +0.34 |
| VO₂⁺ + 2H⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ VO²⁺ + H₂O | +1.00 |

Zinc has the most negative potential, so it can reduce each of the vanadium species in turn. A reaction is feasible when E(cell) = E(reduced half) – E(oxidised half) is positive. For zinc and V³⁺: –0.26 – (–0.76) = +0.50 V, so Zn reduces V³⁺ to V²⁺.

### Worked example 1: overall equation

Write the equation for the reduction of VO₂⁺ to VO²⁺ by zinc.

Multiply the vanadium half-equation by 2 so that 2 electrons are used: 2VO₂⁺ + 4H⁺ + 2e⁻ → 2VO²⁺ + 2H₂O. Add Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻:

```
2VO₂⁺ + 4H⁺ + Zn → 2VO²⁺ + 2H₂O + Zn²⁺
```

## The effect of pH and ligand on redox potential

The tendency of a metal ion to be reduced depends on its environment, so the electrode potential changes with pH and with the ligand.

### The effect of pH

Many half-equations involve H⁺ or OH⁻. In the vanadium half-equations, H⁺ is a reactant, so a higher [H⁺] (lower pH) pushes the equilibrium to the right, making the reduction more favourable (E more positive). Another example is iron:

| Half-equation | E / V |
|---|---|
| Fe³⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Fe²⁺ (acidic) | +0.77 |
| Fe(OH)₃ + e⁻ ⇌ Fe(OH)₂ + OH⁻ (alkaline) | –0.56 |

In acid, Fe²⁺ is stable towards oxidation by air. In alkali, Fe(OH)₂ is oxidised easily, which is why a green precipitate of iron(II) hydroxide turns brown on standing in air.

### The effect of the ligand

Different ligands bind to different oxidation states with different strengths, and this changes E.

| Half-equation | E / V |
|---|---|
| [Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ [Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺ | +0.77 |
| [Fe(CN)₆]³⁻ + e⁻ ⇌ [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻ | +0.36 |

The cyanide ligand stabilises the iron(III) state more than the iron(II) state, so it is harder to reduce [Fe(CN)₆]³⁻ than [Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺, and E is less positive.

## Tollens' reagent

Tollens' reagent contains the diamminesilver(I) ion, [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺, made by adding ammonia to silver nitrate until the precipitate just dissolves, in alkaline solution. It is used to distinguish aldehydes from ketones. When warmed with an aldehyde, silver(I) is reduced to silver metal, which forms a silver mirror on the tube.

```
[Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ + e⁻ → Ag + 2NH₃
RCHO + 2[Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ + 3OH⁻ → RCOO⁻ + 2Ag + 4NH₃ + 2H₂O
```

The aldehyde is oxidised to a carboxylate ion. A ketone does not reduce [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺, so no silver mirror forms. This test relies on the silver(I) complex being a mild oxidising agent, strong enough to oxidise an aldehyde but not a ketone.

## Redox titrations with manganate(VII)

Acidified potassium manganate(VII) is a strong oxidising agent. The purple MnO₄⁻ ion is reduced to almost colourless Mn²⁺:

```
MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O
```

Because the product is nearly colourless and the reagent is purple, the reaction is **self-indicating**: the end point is the first permanent pale pink colour from one excess drop of MnO₄⁻.

**Practical points:**

- Acidify with **dilute sulfuric acid**, in excess. Hydrochloric acid cannot be used because MnO₄⁻ would oxidise Cl⁻ to Cl₂. Nitric acid is itself an oxidising agent.
- Read the burette at the top of the meniscus, since the purple solution is opaque.
- For ethanedioate, warm to about 60 °C to start the reaction, since it is slow at first. Mn²⁺ then acts as a catalyst.

### Iron(II) with manganate(VII)

```
Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + e⁻
MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5Fe²⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O + 5Fe³⁺         ratio MnO₄⁻ : Fe²⁺ = 1 : 5
```

### Ethanedioate with manganate(VII)

```
C₂O₄²⁻ → 2CO₂ + 2e⁻
2MnO₄⁻ + 16H⁺ + 5C₂O₄²⁻ → 2Mn²⁺ + 8H₂O + 10CO₂         ratio MnO₄⁻ : C₂O₄²⁻ = 2 : 5
```

### Worked example 2: iron tablet

An iron tablet is dissolved in dilute sulfuric acid and made up to 100 cm³. A 20.0 cm³ portion needs 18.50 cm³ of 0.00500 mol dm⁻³ KMnO₄. Calculate the mass of iron in the tablet (Aᵣ Fe = 55.8).

```
n(MnO₄⁻) = 0.00500 × 0.01850 = 9.25 × 10⁻⁵ mol
n(Fe²⁺) in 20.0 cm³ = 5 × 9.25 × 10⁻⁵ = 4.625 × 10⁻⁴ mol
n(Fe²⁺) in 100 cm³ = 4.625 × 10⁻⁴ × (100 ÷ 20.0) = 2.3125 × 10⁻³ mol
mass of Fe = 2.3125 × 10⁻³ × 55.8 = 0.129 g
```

### Worked example 3: Mᵣ and water of crystallisation

1.575 g of hydrated ethanedioic acid, H₂C₂O₄·xH₂O, is dissolved to make 250 cm³ of solution. A 25.0 cm³ portion needs 25.00 cm³ of 0.0200 mol dm⁻³ KMnO₄. Find x.

```
n(MnO₄⁻) = 0.0200 × 0.02500 = 5.00 × 10⁻⁴ mol
n(C₂O₄²⁻) in 25.0 cm³ = 5.00 × 10⁻⁴ × 5/2 = 1.25 × 10⁻³ mol
n in 250 cm³ = 1.25 × 10⁻² mol
Mᵣ = 1.575 ÷ 1.25 × 10⁻² = 126.0
Mᵣ of H₂C₂O₄ = 90.0, so x = (126.0 – 90.0) ÷ 18.0 = 2
```

The formula is H₂C₂O₄·2H₂O.

### Worked example 4: percentage iron in steel

2.000 g of steel is dissolved in acid and made up to 250 cm³. A 25.0 cm³ portion needs 35.10 cm³ of 0.0200 mol dm⁻³ KMnO₄. Calculate the percentage of iron.

```
n(MnO₄⁻) = 0.0200 × 0.03510 = 7.02 × 10⁻⁴ mol
n(Fe²⁺) in 25.0 cm³ = 5 × 7.02 × 10⁻⁴ = 3.51 × 10⁻³ mol
n in 250 cm³ = 3.51 × 10⁻² mol
mass = 3.51 × 10⁻² × 55.8 = 1.959 g
percentage = 1.959 ÷ 2.000 × 100 = 97.9 %
```

## Common mistakes

- Using the ratio 1 : 1 for MnO₄⁻ and Fe²⁺. It is 1 : 5.
- Using 1 : 5 for ethanedioate. It is 2 : 5.
- Forgetting to scale up from the aliquot to the whole solution.
- Using hydrochloric acid for the titration.
- Calling the end point colourless. It is the first permanent pink.
- Writing the vanadium colour sequence in the wrong order.
- Mixing up which half-equation is reversed: the manganate half-equation is reduction; the iron or ethanedioate is oxidation.
- Saying the E value changes with pH because the oxidation state changes. It changes because H⁺ or OH⁻ is in the half-equation, or because a ligand binds differently.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Write an equation**: combine half-equations so that electrons cancel; check atoms and charge.
- **Calculate**: moles of titrant, ratio, scale for aliquot, then mass or concentration.
- **Explain the end point**: the first excess drop of MnO₄⁻ gives a permanent pink colour.
- **Explain why E changes**: name the factor (pH or ligand) and the species affected.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is no indicator needed for manganate(VII) titrations?**
The MnO₄⁻ ion is purple and Mn²⁺ is almost colourless, so the first drop in excess colours the solution pink.

**Why are zinc and acid used for vanadium?**
Zinc has a very negative electrode potential, so it can reduce all the way to V²⁺, and acid provides the H⁺ needed in the half-equations.

**Is Tollens' reagent acidic?**
No. It is made in alkaline conditions, with ammonia, so the silver stays as the complex ion.
