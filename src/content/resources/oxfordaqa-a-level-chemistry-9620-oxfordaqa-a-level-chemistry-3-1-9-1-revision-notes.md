## Core recall

- **IUPAC convention**: electrode half-equations are written as **reductions** (electrons on the left, ⇌).
- Electrode potentials are measured against the **standard hydrogen electrode**, E⦵ = 0.00 V.
- **Standard conditions**: 298 K, 100 kPa, 1.00 mol dm⁻³ solutions of ions.
- Listing E⦵ values in order gives the **electrochemical series**.
- **EMF = E⦵(right) − E⦵(left) = E⦵(positive) − E⦵(negative)**.
- Conditions change E (direction only; Nernst equation not required).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Half-cell | An electrode in contact with a solution containing both forms of a redox pair |
| Standard hydrogen electrode | H₂ at 100 kPa over Pt in 1.00 mol dm⁻³ H⁺ at 298 K; defined as 0.00 V |
| Standard electrode potential, E⦵ | EMF of a half-cell against the SHE under standard conditions, with the polarity of the half-cell |
| EMF | The potential difference of a cell when no current flows |
| Electrochemical series | Half-equations listed in order of E⦵ |
| Salt bridge | Allows ions to move between half-cells to complete the circuit; electrons do not pass through it |
| Oxidising agent | Species that gains electrons (is reduced) |
| Reducing agent | Species that loses electrons (is oxidised) |

## Reading the electrochemical series

| Position | Strong... | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Most positive E⦵ | Oxidising agent: species on the **left** | MnO₄⁻ (+1.51 V) |
| Most negative E⦵ | Reducing agent: species on the **right** | Mg (−2.37 V) |

## Conventional cell representation

```
negative electrode | its solution || other solution | positive electrode
```

- More negative half-cell on the **left** (oxidation); more positive on the **right** (reduction).
- Left: reduced form | oxidised form. Right: oxidised form | reduced form.
- `|` = phase boundary; `||` = salt bridge; `,` = species in the same phase.
- No metal in a half-reaction → **Pt(s)** on the outside.
- Include state symbols.

**Examples**

- Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(aq) || Cu²⁺(aq) | Cu(s), EMF = +1.10 V
- Pt(s) | I⁻(aq), I₂(aq) || Fe³⁺(aq), Fe²⁺(aq) | Pt(s), EMF = +0.23 V
- Pt(s) | H₂(g) | H⁺(aq) || Ag⁺(aq) | Ag(s), EMF = +0.80 V

## Method: predicting a reaction

1. Find the half-equation for the proposed **oxidising agent** (as written, on the left).
2. Compare its E⦵ with that of the proposed **reducing agent**'s half-equation.
3. Oxidising agent's E⦵ **more positive** → feasible; EMF = difference.
4. Reverse the less positive half-equation, balance electrons, add.
5. Never multiply E⦵ values.

## Method: finding an unknown E⦵

- EMF = E⦵(positive) − E⦵(negative).
- Use the **polarity** given to decide which is which, then rearrange.
- Against the SHE: E⦵ = +EMF if the half-cell is positive; −EMF if negative.

## Conditions

- Each electrode is an equilibrium: oxidised form + ne⁻ ⇌ reduced form.
- More oxidised form (or less reduced form) → shifts right → E **more positive**.
- Less oxidised form → E **less positive**.
- Effect on EMF: raise the positive electrode's E or lower the negative electrode's E → EMF **increases**.
- Predictions with a very small EMF may not hold away from standard conditions; E⦵ says nothing about rate.

## Required practical 6 checklist

- Clean electrodes (emery paper).
- 1.00 mol dm⁻³ solutions, about 298 K.
- Salt bridge: filter paper soaked in unreactive salt solution, such as KNO₃(aq).
- High-resistance voltmeter, so negligible current flows.

## Exam traps

- Oxidation half-equations written as electrode equations.
- Negative EMF from putting the more positive half-cell on the left.
- E⦵ multiplied when balancing.
- Pt(s) or the comma missing in non-metal half-cells.
- "Electrons flow through the salt bridge."
- Sign of E⦵ taken from the voltmeter reading instead of the polarity.
- Forgetting H⁺ and H₂O in manganate(VII) half-equations.

## Quick self-test

Use: Mg²⁺/Mg −2.37; Zn²⁺/Zn −0.76; Fe²⁺/Fe −0.44; Ni²⁺/Ni −0.25; Cu²⁺/Cu +0.34; Ag⁺/Ag +0.80; Br₂/Br⁻ +1.09; Cl₂/Cl⁻ +1.36 (all V).

1. State the three standard conditions.
2. Why is platinum used in the SHE?
3. Write the conventional representation for a magnesium–copper cell and calculate its EMF.
4. For an iron–silver cell, calculate the EMF and write the overall equation.
5. A cadmium half-cell connected to the SHE gives 0.40 V, with cadmium negative. State E⦵(Cd²⁺/Cd).
6. Will zinc reduce Ni²⁺(aq) under standard conditions? Give the EMF.
7. Will copper react with 1.00 mol dm⁻³ H⁺(aq)? Explain.
8. Will chlorine oxidise bromide ions? Write the equation.
9. Which is the strongest reducing agent in the list?
10. In the magnesium–copper cell, in which direction do electrons flow in the external wire?
11. The Mg²⁺ solution in that cell is diluted. What happens to the EMF?
12. Why must the voltmeter in required practical 6 have a high resistance?

### Answers

1. 298 K; 100 kPa; 1.00 mol dm⁻³ solutions of ions.
2. It is inert and conducts electrons, providing a surface for the H⁺/H₂ equilibrium.
3. Mg(s) | Mg²⁺(aq) || Cu²⁺(aq) | Cu(s); EMF = 0.34 − (−2.37) = **+2.71 V**.
4. EMF = 0.80 − (−0.44) = **+1.24 V**; **Fe + 2Ag⁺ → Fe²⁺ + 2Ag**.
5. **−0.40 V**.
6. **Yes**: −0.25 V is more positive than −0.76 V; EMF = **+0.51 V**.
7. **No**: E⦵(H⁺/H₂) = 0.00 V is less positive than E⦵(Cu²⁺/Cu) = +0.34 V, so H⁺ cannot oxidise Cu.
8. **Yes**: Cl₂ + 2Br⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + Br₂ (EMF = +0.27 V).
9. **Mg**.
10. From the **magnesium** (negative) electrode to the **copper** (positive) electrode.
11. Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Mg shifts left, so E(Mg) becomes more negative and the **EMF increases**.
12. So that almost no current flows; the reading then equals the EMF.
