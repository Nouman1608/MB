This study guide teaches section **4.2.2.3 Blood** of the AQA GCSE Biology (8461) specification, for teaching from September 2016 and exams from 2018 onwards. It is Paper 1 content at Foundation and Higher Tier, and nothing in 4.2.2.3 is marked (HT only).

The [blood revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-biology-8461-blood-revision-notes/) already list the components, what plasma carries and how to work out actual size. This guide is the teaching companion to them: it explains why each adaptation works, how to read a micrograph systematically, how the components work together at a wound, and how to build an evaluation of blood products. When you are ready, test yourself with the [blood practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-biology-8461-blood-aqa-gcse-biology-practice/). Course hub: [AQA GCSE Biology](/boards/aqa/gcse/biology/).

## What the specification asks for

The content of 4.2.2.3 is short:

- "Blood is a tissue consisting of plasma, in which the red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets are suspended."
- "Students should know the functions of each of these blood components."
- Students should "recognise different types of blood cells in a photograph or diagram, and explain how they are adapted to their functions."

The skills column adds AT 7, "Observing and drawing blood cells seen under a microscope", and WS 1.5, "Evaluate risks related to use of blood products."

## Why blood counts as a tissue

A tissue is a group of cells with a similar structure and function. Blood is unusual because its cells are not joined together; they float in a liquid. It is still one tissue with one overall function, **transport**, carried out by its parts:

- plasma transports dissolved substances and the cells;
- red blood cells transport oxygen;
- white blood cells are transported to wherever pathogens are found;
- platelets are transported to wounds.

## Explaining adaptations: feature, effect, function

The specification uses the word *explain*, so a list of features is not enough. Each feature needs its effect.

### Red blood cells

- **Biconcave disc shape** → large surface area compared with volume → oxygen diffuses in and out quickly.
- **No nucleus** → more space inside → more haemoglobin, so each cell carries more oxygen.
- **Haemoglobin** → combines with oxygen where oxygen concentration is high (the lungs) and releases it where it is low (respiring tissues).
- **Small and flexible** → passes through narrow capillaries, close to every cell.
- **Very numerous** → a large total oxygen-carrying capacity.

### White blood cells

White blood cells are part of the immune system. They all have a **nucleus**, which makes them easy to identify. Their functions link to Topic 3:

- some engulf and digest pathogens (phagocytosis), so they can change shape to surround a pathogen;
- some produce **antibodies**, which help destroy specific pathogens;
- some produce **antitoxins**, which counteract the toxins that bacteria release.

### Platelets

Platelets are **fragments of cells** with no nucleus. At a wound they help the blood to clot. The clot seals the wound, which stops blood loss and stops pathogens entering.

## The components working together at a cut

A cut finger shows all four components acting at once:

1. Blood escapes; platelets help form a clot that seals the wound.
2. Pathogens that got in are engulfed by some white blood cells; others produce antibodies or antitoxins.
3. Red blood cells keep supplying oxygen to the cells repairing the damage, so they can respire.
4. Plasma brings glucose and amino acids to those cells and carries away carbon dioxide.

Linking components like this is a good way to answer "describe the role of blood" questions in more than one mark.

## Recognising cells in a photograph or diagram

Work through a micrograph in a fixed order:

1. **Find the most common cell.** It will be the red blood cell: round, pale in the centre, no nucleus.
2. **Look for stained nuclei.** Any cell with a nucleus is a white blood cell; it is usually larger than a red cell.
3. **Look for tiny specks** between the cells: platelets.
4. **Everything else is plasma**, which you cannot see as cells.

### Worked example 1: counting cells in a field of view

A student counts the cells in one field of view of a stained blood smear (invented data).

| Component | Count |
|---|---|
| Red blood cells | 640 |
| White blood cells | 4 |

```
Ratio of red to white = 640 : 4 = 160 : 1
```

Red cells hugely outnumber white cells. One field of view is a small sample, so the student should count several fields and calculate a mean before drawing a conclusion. The specification links this kind of handling of data to working scientifically.

### Worked example 2: using magnification

On a micrograph taken at × 300, a white blood cell measures 3.6 mm across. Use actual size = image size ÷ magnification.

```
Actual size = 3.6 / 300 = 0.012 mm
0.012 mm x 1000 = 12 um
```

Comparing it with a red blood cell is then easy: if a red cell in the same image measures 2.1 mm, its actual size is 2.1 ÷ 300 = 0.007 mm, or 7 µm, so the white cell is wider.

## AT 7: observing and drawing blood cells

When you describe the method:

- use a prepared, **stained** blood smear, so the nuclei of white cells show clearly;
- start on the **lowest-power** objective, focus, then move to higher power and use the fine focus;
- draw a few cells of each type, in pencil, with **clear continuous outlines** and **no shading**;
- keep sizes in **proportion** (white cells larger than red);
- add **labels with ruled lines** and record the **magnification**.

## WS 1.5: evaluating the use of blood products

Blood products include whole blood from donors and separated parts such as plasma or platelets. An evaluation weighs risks against benefits and reaches a judgement.

| Consider | Points to make |
|---|---|
| Benefit | Replaces blood lost in surgery or an accident; separated products can be given only to the patients who need that part |
| Risk: infection | A pathogen in donated blood could be passed on |
| How the risk is reduced | Donors are questioned and donations are tested |
| Risk: reaction | If donated blood is not matched to the patient, the patient's immune system may react against it |
| How the risk is reduced | Blood is matched to the recipient before use |
| Supply | Depends on enough people donating; blood must be stored correctly |

### Annotated model answer

*Question: Evaluate the use of a blood transfusion for a patient who has lost a lot of blood in an accident.*

> A transfusion replaces the lost blood quickly, restoring the supply of red blood cells so the patient's cells receive enough oxygen (**benefit, linked to function**). There is a small risk that a pathogen is passed on in the donated blood, but donations are tested, which reduces this risk (**risk, then how it is managed**). The blood could cause an immune reaction if it is not matched to the patient, so it must be checked before use (**second risk**). Because the patient could die without enough blood, the benefit outweighs the small, controlled risks, so the transfusion should be given (**judgement with reason**).

## Exam technique

- **Name** or **identify**: one or two words, such as "platelet".
- **Describe** a function: what the component does, such as "carries oxygen from the lungs to the tissues".
- **Explain** an adaptation: feature + effect + link to function, as above.
- **Calculate** actual size: formula, unit conversion to µm, answer with units.
- **Evaluate**: both sides, then a judgement for the situation given.

## Common errors

- Saying red blood cells have no nucleus "so they can carry more oxygen" without mentioning haemoglobin.
- Calling platelets cells.
- Writing "white blood cells fight disease" without naming how.
- Calling plasma "blood" or calling blood an organ.
- Evaluating a blood product with benefits only, or without a final judgement.
- Leaving actual sizes in millimetres when micrometres are more sensible.
