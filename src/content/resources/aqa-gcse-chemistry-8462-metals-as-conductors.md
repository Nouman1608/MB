This guide covers **4.2.2.8 Metals as conductors** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) specification, for teaching
from September 2016 onwards (version 1.2). It is part of topic 4.2, Bonding, structure, and the properties of
matter, examined on Paper 1 at Foundation and Higher Tier. No statement in it is marked (HT only), and there is
no required practical. It is a short subsection, so this guide spends time on the reasoning that exam questions
reward.

For quick recall use the [metals as conductors revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-metals-as-conductors-revision-notes/),
and test yourself with the [metals as conductors practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-metals-as-conductors-practice/).
How metallic bonding forms is in the [metallic bonding study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-metallic-bonding/).

## What the specification asks for

| Statement | What to be ready for |
|---|---|
| Metals are good conductors of electricity because the delocalised electrons in the metal carry electrical charge through the metal | Explaining electrical conduction |
| Metals are good conductors of thermal energy because energy is transferred by the delocalised electrons | Explaining thermal conduction |

## The structure that makes conduction possible

In a metal, each atom gives up its outer-shell electrons to a shared "sea". What remains is a regular arrangement
of **positive ions**. The outer electrons are **delocalised**, which means they do not belong to any one atom.
They move freely through the whole structure. The positive ions stay in fixed positions, vibrating about their
places in the lattice, and are held together by the attraction between them and the sea of electrons: this is
**metallic bonding**.

```
  +   +   +   +
    e-  e-  e-
  +   +   +   +      (+ = positive metal ion, e- = delocalised electron)
    e-  e-  e-
  +   +   +   +
```

It is the **delocalised electrons that are free to move**, not the ions.

## Electrical conduction

An electric current is a flow of charge. A material conducts electricity if it contains charged particles that
are free to move. In a metal, the free-moving charged particles are the **delocalised electrons**.

When a metal wire is connected into a circuit, the electrons are pushed along the wire from the negative terminal
towards the positive terminal. Because they move through the whole structure, a **current** flows. The metal
conducts in the **solid state**, and it also conducts when molten.

| Statement | Correct? |
|---|---|
| The delocalised electrons carry the charge | Yes |
| The positive ions carry the charge in a solid metal | No. They are fixed in position |
| Metals conduct when solid | Yes |
| Metals conduct because they contain ions | No. The point is the free electrons |

## Thermal conduction

Metals are also good **conductors of thermal energy**. When one end of a metal is heated, the particles at that
end gain energy. The delocalised electrons gain kinetic energy too. Because they can move through the whole
structure, they carry this energy to the cooler parts of the metal, where they pass it on by colliding with
ions and other electrons. This is quicker than the energy being passed only from ion to ion by vibration.
Metals are therefore much better thermal conductors than non-metals such as plastic or wood.

## Comparing metals with other substances

| Substance type | Free-moving charged particles | Electrical conduction |
|---|---|---|
| Metal | Delocalised electrons | Yes, solid and molten |
| Ionic compound | Ions, only when molten or dissolved | Only when molten or dissolved |
| Small molecules | None | No |
| Giant covalent (diamond, silicon dioxide) | None | No |
| Graphite | Delocalised electrons | Yes (see the graphite subsection) |

The statement in the specification that "graphite is similar to metals in that it has delocalised electrons"
links this subsection to graphite.

## Uses linked to conduction

| Use | Property used | Why a metal |
|---|---|---|
| Electrical cables | Good electrical conductor; can be drawn into wires | Delocalised electrons carry the current, and layers slide so the metal can be drawn into wire |
| Cooking pans | Good thermal conductor | Energy transfers quickly from the cooker to the food |
| Radiators and heat sinks | Good thermal conductor | Heat passes quickly through the metal |
| Handles of pans | Poor conductor needed | A plastic or wood handle is used because they do not conduct heat well |

## Worked examples

### Worked example 1: explaining electrical conduction

*Explain why copper conducts electricity.*

```
1. Copper has metallic bonding: positive ions in a sea of delocalised electrons.
2. The delocalised electrons are free to move through the metal.
3. When a potential difference is applied, the electrons carry electrical charge through the metal.
```

### Worked example 2: explaining thermal conduction

*Explain why a metal spoon in hot soup gets hot at the handle.*

```
1. The delocalised electrons in the bowl end of the spoon gain energy from the soup.
2. They move through the metal and transfer the energy to the cooler handle.
3. So energy is transferred along the spoon by the delocalised electrons.
```

### Worked example 3: sodium chloride versus sodium

*Explain why solid sodium conducts electricity but solid sodium chloride does not.*

```
Sodium:           metallic; delocalised electrons free to move -> conducts
Sodium chloride:  ionic; ions fixed in the lattice; no free electrons -> does not conduct as a solid
```

### Worked example 4: a table of data

*A student tests four solids in a simple circuit with a bulb.*

| Solid | Bulb lights? | Melting point / °C |
|---|---|---|
| A | Yes | 1085 |
| B | No | 801 |
| C | No | 114 |
| D | No | 1710 |

Only A lights the bulb, so it is a metal: it conducts as a solid and has a high melting point. The other three
do not conduct as solids. B (high melting point), C (low melting point) and D (very high melting point) can be
ionic, small molecules and giant covalent, respectively. More tests, such as conduction when molten, would
confirm B.

## Testing conduction in a simple circuit

A simple way to test whether a solid conducts electricity is to place it in a series circuit with a cell and a
bulb (or an ammeter).

1. Connect the cell, the bulb and two leads in a loop with a gap.
2. Touch the two leads onto opposite ends of the sample, or clip the sample into the gap.
3. Watch the bulb or read the ammeter.
4. Repeat with other samples, keeping everything else the same.

If the bulb lights, or the ammeter shows a current, the sample conducts. The test works for solids. To test an
ionic compound that has melted or dissolved, use a different arrangement with two electrodes dipped into the
liquid. Results are best displayed in a table, with each sample marked "conducts" or "does not conduct".

### Worked example 5: a six-mark explanation

*Explain how the structure and bonding of a metal allow it to conduct electricity and thermal energy.*

Indicative content:

- A metal is a giant structure of positive ions in a regular arrangement.
- Outer-shell electrons from each atom are delocalised and form a sea of electrons.
- The positive ions are held in place by attraction to the delocalised electrons (metallic bonding).
- The delocalised electrons are free to move through the metal.
- When a voltage is applied, the electrons carry charge through the metal, so it conducts electricity.
- When one part is heated, the electrons gain energy and transfer it to other parts, so it conducts thermal energy.

A strong answer links each property to the same cause: the delocalised electrons.

## Why non-metals are usually poor conductors

Most non-metal elements and their compounds have no particles that can move freely.

| Material | Reason it does not conduct |
|---|---|
| Plastic and wood | Electrons are held in covalent bonds; no ions or free electrons |
| Sulfur | Small molecules with no overall charge |
| Diamond | All four outer electrons of each carbon are used in bonds |
| Solid sodium chloride | Ions are fixed in the lattice |
| Pure water | Almost no ions present |

These materials are also poor conductors of thermal energy, which is why plastic and wood are used for
handles. The exception is graphite, which has delocalised electrons.

## Common mistakes

- Saying metals conduct because they contain "free ions". It is the delocalised electrons.
- Saying that "the electrons are shared between two atoms". In a metal they are delocalised across the whole
  structure.
- Writing that positive ions move to carry the current in a solid metal.
- Failing to mention thermal energy transfer by electrons when asked about thermal conduction.
- Saying all conductors are metals. Graphite also conducts.
- Writing "electricity flows" without saying what carries the charge.

## Exam technique

- **Explain electrical conduction in a metal:** delocalised electrons, free to move, carry charge.
- **Explain thermal conduction:** delocalised electrons, transfer energy through the metal.
- **Compare with ionic compounds:** ions are fixed in the solid, so no conduction; molten ions can move.
- **Use the word "delocalised".** It is the key word the mark scheme is looking for.
