## Section A

**1.** Give **one** way in which digital technology allows an organisation to monitor people's behaviour. **[1]**

**2.** State what is meant by algorithmic bias. **[1]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by a cultural issue arising from the worldwide use of a social media service. **[2]**

**4.** State **two** reasons why laws about computing are difficult to enforce across the internet. **[2]**

**5.** State what the Computer Misuse Act 1990 makes it an offence to do, giving **one** example. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** A fitness app records the location of runners and shares route maps publicly by default.

**(a)** Give **one** opportunity and **one** risk of this feature. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe **two** safeguards the developers could introduce. **[2]**

**7.** A bank uses a program trained on past decisions to decide who should be offered a loan. Applicants from one area are refused much more often than others with similar incomes.

**(a)** Explain how the program could have developed this bias. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain **two** responsibilities the developers have. **[2]**

**8.** Explain what is meant by the statement "software embeds moral and cultural values", using an example. **[3]**

**9.** Explain why the issue of scale matters to the developer of an app that will be used in many countries. **[3]**

**10.** A school plans to install cameras in all corridors that record continuously and use face recognition to take the register.

**(a)** Describe **two** benefits of this system. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe **two** risks to students. **[2]**
**(c)** State **one** legal requirement that the school must consider. **[1]**

**11.** Explain **three** challenges that legislators face in making laws about computing. **[3]**

**12.** A company collects customers' purchase histories to improve its shop. It then sells the data to an advertising firm without telling customers.

Discuss the moral, ethical and legal issues this raises. **[6]**

**13.** A town council plans to publish an online map showing the home addresses of everyone who has applied for a particular benefit, so that "neighbours can offer help".

Discuss whether the council should go ahead. Your answer should consider opportunities, risks, safeguards and a conclusion. **[9]**

---

## Answers

**1.** Any one from: CCTV with face or number plate recognition; tracking a phone's location; recording web browsing or purchases; monitoring staff keystrokes or email [1].

**2.** Systematically unfair outcomes produced by an algorithm, often because of unrepresentative or biased training data [1].

**3.** A cultural issue arises when different communities have different values, customs or expectations [1], so content or practices that are acceptable in one culture (for example about privacy, images or speech) may offend or be unlawful in another [1].

**4.** Any two from: the internet crosses national borders so it is unclear whose law applies; it is hard to identify who carried out an action (anonymity); evidence can be changed or deleted; the scale of activity is too large to monitor; laws differ between countries [1 each].

**5.** It makes unauthorised access to a computer an offence [1]; one example, such as guessing a password to read another person's files, or spreading malware that changes files without permission [1].

**6. (a)** Opportunity: runners can share routes and find new ones, building a community [1]. Risk: public routes may reveal where someone lives or when they are away, which could put them at risk [1].
**(b)** Any two from: make sharing off by default and require the user to opt in; hide the start and end of routes; allow sharing only with approved friends; warn users about the risk before they share [1 each].

**7. (a)** The program learns from past decisions [1]; if past decisions were biased against that area, the program learns and repeats the pattern [1]; the training data may also not represent all groups fairly, or may contain postcode as a stand-in for other characteristics [1].
**(b)** Test the program for unfair outcomes across groups before and after release [1]; make sure a person can review and explain a refusal, or correct the data and training [1].

**8.** Every design decision reflects a judgement about what matters [1]. For example, a recommendation system built to maximise time spent on the app values attention over the user's wellbeing [1], or a face recognition system trained on limited data embeds the assumption that those images are typical [1]. (Other valid examples accepted.)

**9.** A flaw would be repeated across every user, so it could harm millions at once [1]; a benefit, such as giving access to information, can also reach people everywhere [1]; so the developer must consider the effect on different cultures and laws, and test thoroughly before wide release, because a small team's decisions have a large effect [1].

**10. (a)** Any two from: the register is taken quickly, saving lesson time; fewer errors in attendance records; the recordings help to deter or investigate bullying or theft; staff can see who is in the building in an emergency [1 each].
**(b)** Any two from: constant monitoring and loss of privacy; students' faces form a sensitive data store that could be breached; recognition might be less accurate for some groups, so some students are wrongly marked absent; data might be used for other purposes later [1 each].
**(c)** Any one from: the school must comply with data protection law (lawful purpose, security, limited retention); it must tell students and parents how data is used; it must be able to show the monitoring is necessary and proportionate [1].

**11.** Any three, each with a short explanation [1 each]: technology changes faster than laws are made, so laws become out of date; the internet crosses borders, so jurisdiction and enforcement are unclear; it is difficult to identify and prove who carried out an action; rights must be balanced, for example privacy against security; lawmakers need enough technical understanding; different cultures disagree about what should be allowed.

**12.** Indicative content (up to 6 marks). Moral: selling the data without telling customers is dishonest and breaches trust, and customers have not given consent [1]; the individuals at the company who decide to sell are responsible for the choice [1]. Ethical: customers lose control of their data and may be profiled and targeted by advertisers without knowing [1]; the data might reveal sensitive facts such as health or financial position [1]. Legal: data protection law requires personal data to be used fairly and for the purpose for which it was collected, with the individual informed [1]; selling it without consent is likely to breach this and expose the company to penalties [1]. A balanced answer may also note that the company's original use (improving the shop) was reasonable. (Marks: 5 to 6 for a developed discussion of all three; 3 to 4 for two with development; 1 to 2 for a list.)

**13.** Indicative content (9 marks in total).
- Opportunity (1 mark): neighbours might offer practical help, and this could reduce isolation for people in need.
- Risks (maximum 4 marks, 1 each): publishing addresses discloses that a household receives a benefit, which is sensitive personal information; the households may be stigmatised, harassed or targeted for crime; the data cannot easily be taken back once published; people applied in confidence and have not consented to this use, which may breach data protection law.
- Safeguards (maximum 2 marks, 1 each): ask for consent from each household; offer help through a private service that does not publish addresses; let people opt out; show details only to approved volunteers.
- Scale and responsibility (1 mark): the council has power and responsibility because publication reaches many people at once; the aim is good but the method does not match the aim.
- Conclusion (1 mark): a clear judgement that follows from the points, for example that the map should not be published and a private, consent-based scheme should be used instead.

A balanced, developed answer with safeguards and a justified conclusion can be awarded the top marks; a list of points with no development or a one-sided view should be capped at the lower marks.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving only risks, or only benefits.
- Writing generally about privacy without linking to the scenario.
- Treating "legal" as the same as "right".
- Ending without a conclusion.
- Stating details of an Act that are not certain instead of describing the principle.
