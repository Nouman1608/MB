This set covers section 11.1 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699) Paper 4: ways of
defining religion, difficulties in measuring belief, social groups and religiosity, and religion
and other belief systems. Section A tests concepts; Section B asks for developed explanations and
essays.

## Section A

**1.** Define the term *religiosity*. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between exclusive and inclusive definitions of religion. **[4]**

**3.** Explain why the choice of definition affects conclusions about religious decline. **[3]**

**4.** Explain what Davie meant by *believing without belonging*. **[2]**

**5.** Explain **one** reason why self-report surveys may overstate religious practice. **[3]**

**6.** Distinguish between the ageing effect and the generational effect in explaining age
differences in religiosity. **[4]**

**7.** Explain what Stark and Bainbridge meant by *compensators*. **[2]**

**8.** Explain what is meant by a *closed belief system*, with an example. **[3]**

## Section B

**9.** Explain **two** reasons why women may show higher religiosity than men in some societies.
**[8]**

**10.** Explain **two** ways in which religion may be compared with science as a belief system.
**[8]**

**11.** "Ethnicity is the most important influence on religiosity." Evaluate this view. **[35]**

**12.** "Functional definitions of religion are more useful than substantive definitions."
Evaluate this view. **[35]**

## Answers

**1.** The extent to which people are religious [1], measured through belief, belonging and practice
[1]. **[2]**

**2.** Exclusive definitions draw a tight boundary around religion [1], usually requiring belief in
the supernatural, which makes comparison and counting easier [1]. Inclusive definitions include
anything that performs religious functions [1], so nationalism or sport could count, making
religion appear universal [1]. **[4]**

**3.** Substantive, exclusive definitions measure belief in the supernatural, so falling belief
shows decline [1]. Functional, inclusive definitions count anything that performs religious
functions [1], so religion may appear to have moved into new forms rather than declined [1].
**[3]**

**4.** Many people hold some religious beliefs [1] without belonging to or attending a religious
organisation, so measures of membership understate belief [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Identify [1], explain [1], evidence [1]: social desirability [1] — in societies where religion
is valued, respondents give the answer that seems approved [1]; Hadaway, Marler and Chaves (1993)
found that Americans' reported attendance substantially exceeded actual counts [1]. **[3]**

**6.** The ageing effect is that individuals become more religious as they get older [1], for example
as they approach death [1]. The generational effect is that each generation is less religious than
the one before [1], so older people are more religious because they grew up in more religious times;
Voas and Crockett (2005) argued this drives decline in Britain [1]. **[4]**

**7.** Promises of future rewards, such as salvation [1], offered by religion to people who lack
rewards in this life, which helps explain the appeal of some sects to the deprived [1]. **[2]**

**8.** A belief system that claims absolute truth [1] and explains away evidence that contradicts it
rather than abandoning its claims [1]; for example, Evans-Pritchard's account of the Azande
explaining failures of the poison oracle by blaming incorrect procedure [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Up to 4 marks per reason: identify [1], explain [1], develop with a study or concept [1],
evaluate [1].
- **Risk aversion** [1]: women may be more risk-averse than men [1], and not being religious can be
  seen as a risk, as Miller and Hoffmann (1995) argued [1]; but this may describe socialisation
  rather than a fixed trait [1].
- **Caring roles and structural location** [1]: women's greater involvement in child-rearing and
  caring connects them with rites of passage and religious communities [1], and part-time work or
  work at home has given more time for involvement [1]; as women's employment rises, this
  explanation predicts a narrowing gap [1].
- Also creditable: socialisation into values associated with religion; religious organisations as
  sources of community. Evaluation: patterns differ by faith; some religions require men's public
  worship. **[8]**

**10.** Up to 4 marks per way: identify [1], explain [1], develop [1], evaluate [1].
- **Open versus closed** [1]: Popper argued science is open to falsification while religion claims
  absolute truth [1]; Evans-Pritchard's Azande show a belief system explaining away failures [1];
  but Polanyi argued science also protects its core beliefs [1].
- **Both explain and predict** [1]: Horton (1967) argued that traditional religious thought and
  science both try to explain and predict events [1]; science is more open to alternative theories
  [1]; Kuhn (1962) suggests scientists also resist challenges to their paradigm [1].
- Also creditable: both as functional sources of meaning; ideologies as functional alternatives.
  **[8]**

**11.** Marked in levels. The top level explains the link between ethnicity and religiosity, then
weighs it against gender, age and class, and evaluates measurement. Indicative content:
- **For the view:** many minority and migrant groups show high religiosity; Bruce's cultural
  defence and cultural transition; religious organisations as community support, networks and
  identity; religion as a marker of ethnic identity.
- **Against the view:** gender differences are found across many societies (Miller and Hoffmann,
  1995; caring roles); age and generation (Voas and Crockett, 2005); class and compensators (Stark
  and Bainbridge, 1987); differences within ethnic groups by generation, gender and faith.
- **Measurement:** belief, belonging and practice may tell different stories (Davie, 1994);
  ethnic identity may raise belonging more than belief.
- **Evaluation:** influences interact (for example, older women in a migrant community); the most
  important influence may vary by society.
- **Judgement:** for example, ethnicity is a strong influence in migrant and minority contexts, but
  no single factor is most important everywhere. **[35]**

**12.** Marked in levels. The top level explains both types with named definitions, applies them to
research problems and judges usefulness for particular purposes. Indicative content:
- **Functional definitions:** Durkheim's moral community (also partly substantive); religion as
  integration, meaning and coping; inclusive, so they capture new forms and functional
  alternatives such as nationalism.
- **Strengths of functional:** cross-cultural reach; identify religion where there is no god.
- **Weaknesses of functional:** too broad; sport or politics might count; make secularisation
  impossible to test; assume religion is always beneficial.
- **Substantive definitions:** belief in the supernatural (Weber's approach); exclusive and
  measurable; allow testing of decline.
- **Weaknesses of substantive:** Western bias; may exclude belief systems without a god.
- **Interpretive alternative:** Geertz (1966) on symbols and meaning; social constructionist
  definitions based on what members say.
- **Judgement:** for example, usefulness depends on the research question: substantive for
  measuring decline, functional for studying what religion does. **[35]**
