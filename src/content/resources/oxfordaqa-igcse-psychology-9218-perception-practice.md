## Section A — Short questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following is a binocular depth cue?
A Occlusion
B Linear perspective
C Convergence
D Height in plane **[1]**

**2.** Explain the difference between sensation and perception. **[2]**

**3.** A painting shows a road narrowing towards the horizon, with a tall tree partly hiding a cottage. Identify **two** monocular depth cues the artist has used and say where each appears in the painting. **[4]**

**4.** Name the explanation for visual illusions that accounts for **(a)** the Kanizsa triangle **(b)** the Necker cube. **[2]**

**5.** Outline what is meant by size constancy. **[2]**

**6.** Identify the factor affecting perception that was investigated by Gilchrist and Nesberg. **[1]**

## Section B — Longer questions

**7.** Explain the role of motion parallax in Gibson's direct theory of perception. Use an everyday example in your answer. **[4]**

**8.** Explain why the person in one corner of the Ames Room appears much smaller than a person of the same height in the other corner. **[3]**

**9.** Describe Bruner and Minturn's study of perceptual set. **[4]**

**10.** Ayesha has not eaten all day. Walking home, she notices every food advert and thinks the pictures look unusually vivid. Using a named study, explain Ayesha's experience. **[3]**

**11.** Outline the findings of **one** study showing the effect of emotion on perception **and** **one** study showing the effect of culture on perception. **[4]**

**12.** Compare Gibson's direct theory and Gregory's constructivist theory of perception. **[4]**

**13.** Describe **and** evaluate Gregory's constructivist theory of perception. Refer to **at least one** visual illusion in your answer. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** **C** [1].

**2.** Sensation is the **detection of physical energy** such as light by the sense organs [1]. Perception is the **brain's interpretation** of that sensory information, giving it meaning [1].

**3.** **Linear perspective** [1]: the edges of the road converge towards the horizon [1]. **Occlusion** [1]: the tree partly hides the cottage, so the tree is seen as nearer [1]. (Accept height in plane if linked to objects nearer the horizon appearing further away.)

**4.** **(a)** Fiction [1]. **(b)** Ambiguity [1].

**5.** Perceiving an object as **staying the same size** [1] even though the size of its **image on the retina changes** as its distance changes [1].

**6.** **Motivation** (accept hunger as a motivation) [1].

**7.** Gibson argued that the environment provides **enough information for direct perception**, without inference [1]. Motion parallax is the way **near objects appear to move faster** across our view than **distant objects** as we move [1]. For example, from a moving car, nearby lampposts rush past while distant mountains seem to move slowly [1]. The difference in apparent speed gives **direct information about distance**, so depth is perceived without needing past experience [1].

**8.** The room is a distorted shape, but from the viewing point it looks like an ordinary **rectangular room** [1]. The brain assumes both people are at the **same distance** [1], so it applies **size constancy** to their retinal images: the person further away has a smaller retinal image and is therefore perceived as smaller [1].

**9.** Participants were shown an **ambiguous figure** that could be read as the letter **B** or the number **13** [1]. Some saw it in a sequence of **letters** (such as A and C) and some in a sequence of **numbers** (such as 12 and 14) [1]. Those in the letter context tended to report seeing a **B** [1], while those in the number context tended to report seeing **13** [1], showing that expectation created by context affects perception.

**10.** In Gilchrist and Nesberg's study, **hungry** participants judged food pictures to be **brighter** than participants who were not deprived of food did [1]. This shows that **motivation** (here, hunger) creates a perceptual set [1]. Because Ayesha is hungry, she is primed to notice food and perceive food images as more vivid [1].

**11.** **Emotion — Kunst-Wilson and Zajonc:** shapes were flashed **too briefly to be recognised** [1]; participants later **preferred** the shapes they had been shown even though they could not identify them, so liking formed without conscious recognition [1]. **Culture — Nisbett and Miyamoto:** East Asian participants, such as those from Japan, took in **the whole scene**, including the setting and how objects related to each other [1], while Western participants, such as Americans, concentrated on **the central object** and its features [1].

**12.** Gibson's theory is **bottom-up**, whereas Gregory's is **top-down** [1]. Gibson says sensory information is **sufficient** on its own, while Gregory says the brain must make **inferences from past experience** [1]. Gibson's theory explains **accurate, fast perception** in the real world well [1], whereas Gregory's explains **illusions and perceptual set** better [1].

**13.** *Description (up to 3):* perception is **top-down** — the brain builds a model of reality using **inferences** [1] from visual cues and **past experience** [1]. In the **Müller-Lyer** illusion, the fins-out line resembles an inside corner of a room (further away), so the brain scales it up and it is perceived as longer [1].
*Evaluation (up to 3):* a strength is that it **explains illusions** and perceptual set studies such as Bruner and Minturn [1]. A limitation is that much of the evidence uses **artificial images** shown briefly, which may not reflect everyday viewing [1]. If perception relied on hypotheses, we would expect **frequent errors** in everyday life, yet perception is usually accurate [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a depth cue without saying where it appears in the scenario.
- Explaining the Ames Room as ambiguity instead of size constancy.
- Describing Bruner and Minturn's figure without saying what each context group reported.
- Listing differences between Gibson and Gregory as separate descriptions, with no comparison words such as "whereas".
- Describing the Müller-Lyer illusion without explaining how it supports inference.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which illusions must I know for 9218?**
The Ponzo, the Müller-Lyer, Rubin's vase, the Ames Room, the Kanizsa triangle and the Necker cube, each with its explanation from ambiguity, misinterpreted depth cues, fiction or size constancy.

**How do I tell which perceptual set study to use?**
Match the factor: expectation is Bruner and Minturn, motivation is Gilchrist and Nesberg, emotion is Kunst-Wilson and Zajonc, and culture is Nisbett and Miyamoto.

**What makes a good comparison answer?**
Each point should compare the two theories on the same feature, such as direction of processing or what they explain, using a linking word such as "whereas".
