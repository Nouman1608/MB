This guide teaches section **3.2.5.3 Shapes of complex ions** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is A-level only content, examined on Paper 1 and Paper 3. The [Transition metals study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-transition-metals/) summarises the shapes. This page goes deeper: which shape goes with which ligand, the bond angles, how to decide whether a complex has cis–trans or optical isomers, how to draw them, and why cisplatin matters. Recall is in the [Shapes of complex ions revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-shapes-of-complex-ions-revision-notes/), and questions are in the [Shapes of complex ions practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-shapes-of-complex-ions-practice/). You should know about ligands from the [Substitution reactions study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-substitution-reactions/) and E–Z isomerism from organic chemistry.

## What the specification asks

| Content | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| Transition metal ions commonly form octahedral complexes with small ligands (e.g. H₂O and NH₃) | State and draw the octahedral shape |
| Octahedral complexes can show cis–trans isomerism (a special case of E–Z isomerism) with monodentate ligands and optical isomerism with bidentate ligands | Draw the isomers |
| Transition metal ions commonly form tetrahedral complexes with larger ligands (e.g. Cl⁻) | State and draw the tetrahedral shape |
| Square planar complexes are also formed and can display cis–trans isomerism; cisplatin is the cis isomer | Draw cis and trans forms |
| Ag⁺ forms the linear complex [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ as used in Tollens' reagent | State the shape |

## The four shapes

| Co-ordination number | Shape | Bond angle | Typical ligands | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6 | Octahedral | 90° | Small ligands: H₂O, NH₃, CN⁻, bidentate ligands | [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺, [Co(NH₃)₆]³⁺ |
| 4 | Tetrahedral | 109.5° | Larger ligands: Cl⁻ | [CuCl₄]²⁻, [CoCl₄]²⁻ |
| 4 | Square planar | 90° | Specific d⁸ ions such as Pt²⁺ | [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂] |
| 2 | Linear | 180° | Ag⁺ with NH₃ | [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ |

The shape is a result of the ligands repelling each other as far as possible, and, for square planar complexes, of the electronic structure of the metal ion. The size of the ligand sets how many fit round the metal: six small ligands, but only four large ones such as chloride.

In a drawing, show the co-ordinate bonds as lines or arrows from the ligand to the metal, using wedge-shaped lines for bonds coming towards you and dashed lines for bonds going away. Give the charge on the complex outside square brackets.

### Octahedral

Six ligands sit at the corners of an octahedron, with the metal at the centre. Four ligands are in a plane, one is above and one is below. Every bond angle is 90° (or 180° across the centre). Examples: [Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺, [Cr(NH₃)₆]³⁺.

### Tetrahedral

Four ligands are arranged so that the bond angles are 109.5°. This is the common shape for chloride complexes: the chloride ions are too large for six to fit. Examples: [CuCl₄]²⁻, [CoCl₄]²⁻, [FeCl₄]⁻.

### Square planar

Four ligands lie in a plane with 90° between neighbouring bonds. The best-known example is the platinum(II) complex [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂] (cisplatin).

### Linear

Two ligands lie on opposite sides of the metal ion, 180° apart. [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ is the diamminesilver(I) ion, the active ion in Tollens' reagent.

## Cis–trans isomerism

Cis–trans isomerism is a special case of E–Z isomerism. It occurs when two different ligands can be arranged either on the same side or on opposite sides.

- **cis**: two identical ligands next to each other (90° apart).
- **trans**: two identical ligands opposite each other (180° apart).

### In square planar complexes

[Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂] has two NH₃ and two Cl⁻ ligands, arranged as cis or trans:

```
   cis                      trans
 Cl      Cl              Cl      NH₃
   \    /                  \    /
     Pt                      Pt
   /    \                  /    \
 H₃N     NH₃            H₃N     Cl
```

In the cis isomer, the two chloride ligands are adjacent (90°). In the trans isomer, they are across from each other (180°). **Cisplatin is the cis isomer.**

Cisplatin is used in cancer treatment. It binds to the DNA of cancer cells and stops the cells from dividing. The trans isomer does not have the same effect. The specification only requires you to know that cisplatin is the cis isomer, but the example shows why the shape of a complex matters in real life.

### In octahedral complexes with monodentate ligands

[Co(NH₃)₄Cl₂]⁺ has four NH₃ and two Cl⁻ ligands. There are two arrangements:

- **trans**: the two chloride ligands are on opposite sides of the cobalt, along one axis (180°), with the four NH₃ in the plane between them.
- **cis**: the two chloride ligands are next to each other (90°).

### Tetrahedral complexes

Tetrahedral complexes do not show cis–trans isomerism: all four positions are adjacent to each other, so any two ligands are always 109.5° apart.

## Optical isomerism in octahedral complexes

Optical isomers are stereoisomers that are non-superimposable mirror images of each other, like left and right hands. They occur when a complex has no plane of symmetry.

The best-known case in this specification is an octahedral complex with **three bidentate ligands**, such as [Ni(H₂NCH₂CH₂NH₂)₃]²⁺ or [Cr(C₂O₄)₃]³⁻. The three bidentate ligands can be wound around the metal ion in a left-handed or a right-handed arrangement. The two forms are mirror images that cannot be superimposed.

Another case is the cis isomer of a complex with two bidentate ligands and two monodentate ligands, such as cis-[Co(en)₂Cl₂]⁺. Its trans form has a plane of symmetry, so it does not show optical isomerism.

When drawing optical isomers, draw the first complex, draw a mirror line next to it, and draw the reflection. Represent each bidentate ligand as an arc linking two donor atoms, for example N∩N.

## How to decide what isomerism a complex shows

1. Find the shape from the co-ordination number and ligand size.
2. If it is tetrahedral or linear, no stereoisomers.
3. If it is square planar or octahedral, check whether there are at least two different monodentate ligands that can be arranged cis or trans.
4. If it is octahedral with bidentate ligands, check for a lack of symmetry (optical isomerism).

### Worked example 1: deduce the shape

Deduce the shape of [CoCl₄]²⁻. It has four chloride ligands, so the co-ordination number is 4. Chloride is large, so the complex is tetrahedral with bond angles of 109.5°. There are no stereoisomers.

### Worked example 2: cis–trans isomers

Draw and name the isomers of [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂]. See the drawings above: cis (cisplatin) has the two chlorides 90° apart; trans has them 180° apart.

### Worked example 3: does it show isomerism?

Does [Pt(NH₃)₃Cl]⁺ show cis–trans isomerism? It is square planar with three NH₃ and one Cl⁻. Wherever Cl⁻ is placed, the arrangement is the same, so there is no isomerism.

### Worked example 4: optical isomerism

Explain why [Ni(H₂NCH₂CH₂NH₂)₃]²⁺ has optical isomers. It is octahedral with three bidentate ligands. The ligands can be arranged in two ways that are mirror images of each other and cannot be superimposed, so it has two optical isomers.

## Common mistakes

- Giving the shape of [CuCl₄]²⁻ as octahedral or square planar.
- Giving the angle in tetrahedral complexes as 90°.
- Calling [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂] tetrahedral. It is square planar, and the cis and trans forms are only possible because it is square planar.
- Saying cis means "same side" without saying which ligands.
- Drawing optical isomers that are the same molecule rotated rather than mirror images.
- Writing that cisplatin is the trans isomer.
- Forgetting the charge on the complex.
- Forgetting that cis–trans isomerism is a special case of E–Z isomerism: it comes from different arrangements of ligands around a fixed point.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **State the shape**: give the name and the bond angle.
- **Draw a complex**: show the central ion, ligands with donor atoms linked to the metal, a bracket and the charge.
- **Explain cis–trans isomerism in a complex**: say there are two different ligands that can be arranged next to each other or opposite each other.
- **Explain optical isomerism**: non-superimposable mirror images; no plane of symmetry.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is Tollens' reagent linear?**
Silver(I) forms a complex with only two ammonia ligands. Two ligands repel each other and sit 180° apart.

**Are cis and trans isomers different chemicals?**
Yes. They have the same formula and bonds but different spatial arrangement, so they have different properties.

**Can a tetrahedral complex show optical isomerism?**
Only if it has four different ligands, which is beyond this specification.
