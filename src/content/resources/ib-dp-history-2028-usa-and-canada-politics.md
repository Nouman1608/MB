**HL only.** This is the twelfth and last study in the Regional study for the Americas, examined on Paper 3 at Higher Level. Paper 3 asks you to evaluate an argument and reach a judgement. The study covers the domestic politics of the **United States** (three periods) and **Canada** (three topics, including the Quiet Revolution). Questions will name leaders and events from the guide. For recent politics, aim for balance: explain what supporters and critics said, and judge by evidence.

## The six lines of inquiry

The six topics are: US politics 1961 to 1977; US politics 1977 to 2001; US politics 2001 to 2020; Canadian politics 1963 to 1993; the causes and effects of the Quiet Revolution; and Canadian politics 1993 to 2020. Concepts of **continuity and change**, **cause and consequence** and **significance** apply. See the [four concepts guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-four-concepts/).

## Key chronology

- 1961 to 1963: Kennedy. 1963 to 1969: Johnson. 1969 to 1974: Nixon. 1974 to 1977: Ford.
- 1977 to 1981: Carter. 1981 to 1989: Reagan. 1989 to 1993: George H. W. Bush. 1993 to 2001: Clinton.
- 2001 to 2009: George W. Bush. 2009 to 2017: Obama. 2017 to 2021: Trump.
- 1960 to 1966: Quiet Revolution begins under Lesage. 1970: October Crisis. 1976: Parti Québécois elected.
- 1963 to 1968: Pearson. 1968 to 1979 and 1980 to 1984: Pierre Trudeau. 1984 to 1993: Mulroney. 1993 to 2003: Chrétien. 2006 to 2015: Harper. 2015 onwards: Justin Trudeau.

## The USA, 1961 to 1977

**Social policies: New Frontier and Great Society.** Kennedy's New Frontier brought the Peace Corps, a tax cut proposal and a space programme, but Congress passed little before his assassination in November 1963. Johnson, using that memory and his skill in Congress, passed the **Great Society**: the Civil Rights Act (1964), the Economic Opportunity Act (the "War on Poverty"), Medicare and Medicaid (1965), federal aid to education and the Voting Rights Act (1965). Poverty rates fell, and health coverage grew, but the Vietnam War drained resources and public trust.

**Watergate.** In June 1972, burglars linked to Nixon's re-election committee were caught at the Democratic headquarters in the Watergate building. Nixon won re-election in a landslide, but Senate hearings in 1973, the "Saturday Night Massacre" (when he dismissed the special prosecutor) and the Supreme Court's order to release tapes (*United States v. Nixon*, 1974) forced his resignation on 9 August 1974, the only presidential resignation in US history. President Ford pardoned him in September 1974. Many saw the pardon as a bargain, and it damaged Ford. Watergate reduced trust in government and led to reforms on campaign finance and congressional oversight.

**Parties and elections.** The Democratic coalition split over civil rights and Vietnam. In 1968, after the murder of Robert Kennedy and a violent Democratic convention in Chicago, Nixon won with a "law and order" campaign. His "Southern strategy" helped Republicans win white southern voters, a shift that continued for decades. In 1972, the Democrats nominated the anti-war George McGovern, who lost heavily. The Republican Party saw a divide between moderates and a growing conservative wing, which backed Goldwater in 1964.

**Roles of leaders.** Kennedy: inspiration more than legislation. Johnson: major domestic reform, ruined by Vietnam. Nixon: détente, the Environmental Protection Agency, but abuse of power. Ford: restoring calm, but weak against inflation and the pardon controversy.

## The USA, 1977 to 2001

**Economic policies, including Reaganomics.** Carter faced stagflation (high inflation and unemployment), an energy crisis and the Iran hostage crisis, and lost in 1980. Reagan promoted lower taxes, less regulation, reduced domestic spending and more military spending. The Economic Recovery Tax Act (1981) and the Tax Reform Act (1986) cut top income tax rates sharply. After a deep recession in 1981 to 1982, the economy grew and inflation fell, but federal deficits and national debt rose, and inequality widened. Supporters credit supply-side policy and Paul Volcker's high interest rates at the Federal Reserve. Critics argue that benefits went to the wealthy.

**Social policies, including the War on Drugs.** Nixon had begun the "war on drugs"; Reagan expanded it, and the Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1986 set harsh sentences, including a sentencing gap between crack and powder cocaine. The policy raised imprisonment rates, especially among Black and Latino Americans.

**Economic growth in the 1990s.** Under George H. W. Bush, the Gulf War (1991) was a success but he broke his tax pledge in 1990 and lost in 1992. Clinton's 1993 budget, the North American Free Trade Agreement, the technology boom and falling unemployment produced growth and a budget surplus by 1998. Republicans won Congress in 1994 under Newt Gingrich's "Contract with America", and Clinton signed welfare reform in 1996. He was impeached by the House in 1998 for lying about an affair, and acquitted by the Senate.

**Roles of leaders.** Carter: honesty and human rights but weak with Congress and the economy. Reagan: communicator who shifted politics to the right. Bush: foreign policy skill. Clinton: centrist "New Democrat", economically successful, scandal-prone.

## The USA, 2001 to 2020

**The 2001 terrorist attacks.** On 11 September 2001, nearly 3,000 people were killed. George W. Bush launched a "war on terror", invading Afghanistan (2001) and Iraq (2003), created the Department of Homeland Security and signed the Patriot Act, expanding surveillance. Initial national unity gave way to division, especially over Iraq, where weapons of mass destruction were not found.

**The 2008 financial crisis.** The collapse of the housing bubble and the failure of Lehman Brothers (September 2008) led to a deep recession. Bush and Congress passed a $700 billion bank rescue (TARP). Barack Obama, elected in 2008, passed a stimulus (2009), the Affordable Care Act (2010) and the Dodd-Frank financial reforms. Critics on the right formed the Tea Party movement.

**Growth of political division.** Polarisation increased along lines of party, region, race and education. Factors included partisan media, social media, gerrymandering, campaign money after *Citizens United* (2010) and disputes over health care, immigration and race. Donald Trump, elected in 2016, ran against the political establishment, cut taxes (2017), tightened immigration and was impeached in 2019, but acquitted in 2020. The COVID-19 pandemic and protests over racial injustice marked 2020.

**Roles of leaders.** Bush: security and tax cuts, undermined by Iraq and the crisis. Obama: first Black president, reform of health care, but facing opposition. Trump: populist style and sharp polarisation.

## Canada, 1963 to 1993

**Social policies.** Lester Pearson's minority Liberal government built the welfare state: the Canada Pension Plan (1965), national medicare (1966), student loans and a new flag (1965). Pierre Trudeau's Official Languages Act (1969) and multiculturalism policy (1971) addressed Canada's diversity. The Constitution Act (1982) brought the constitution home from Britain and added a Charter of Rights and Freedoms, though Quebec did not sign.

**Economic policies.** Pearson signed the Auto Pact (1965) with the US. Trudeau faced inflation, introduced wage and price controls (1975) and the National Energy Program (1980), which angered the West. Brian Mulroney negotiated the Canada-US Free Trade Agreement (1988), privatised public firms and introduced the Goods and Services Tax (1991), which was unpopular.

**Collapse of the Progressive Conservative Party.** Joe Clark's minority government (1979 to 1980) fell within months. Mulroney won a huge majority in 1984 but his party was damaged by failed constitutional deals (the Meech Lake and Charlottetown Accords), the GST, recession and regional division. In 1993 the Progressive Conservatives fell to two seats, as Reform (West) and the Bloc Québécois (Quebec) rose.

**Roles of leaders.** Pearson: consensus-building. Trudeau: intellectual, strong on national unity and rights. Clark: short-lived. Mulroney: ambitious, controversial.

## Causes and effects of the Quiet Revolution

**Causes.** Quebec was largely rural and Catholic, with English-speaking minorities controlling much of the economy. Post-war urbanisation, television and education created a new French-speaking middle class that wanted change. Premier Jean Lesage's Liberals (elected 1960) promised to become "masters in our own house" (*maîtres chez nous*).

**Effects.** Government took over education and health from the Church. Hydro-Québec was nationalised (1962 to 1963), and the state became an economic actor. Church attendance and the birth rate fell. The rise of **Quebec nationalism** led to demands for a special status or independence. The Parti Québécois, led by René Lévesque, was founded in 1968 and won in 1976, introducing Bill 101 (1977) to protect French.

**The FLQ and the October Crisis (1970).** The Front de libération du Québec carried out bombings from 1963. In October 1970, it kidnapped British trade commissioner James Cross and Quebec minister Pierre Laporte, who was found dead. Trudeau invoked the War Measures Act, with troops and about 450 arrests. Public opinion largely supported him at the time, but later critics condemned the use of emergency powers. The FLQ lost support, and separatism moved into the electoral arena.

**Separatism.** In the 1980 referendum, about 60 per cent voted No. In 1995, the No side won by a margin of about 1 per cent.

## Canada, 1993 to 2020

**Emergence of the Conservative Party.** The right was split in the 1990s between the Progressive Conservatives and the Reform party (later the Canadian Alliance). In 2003 they merged into the Conservative Party, led by Stephen Harper, who formed minority governments in 2006 and 2008 and a majority in 2011.

**Truth and Reconciliation Commission.** After the Indian Residential Schools Settlement Agreement (2007) and a formal apology by Harper (2008), the commission began work in 2008 and reported in 2015 with 94 Calls to Action. It documented abuse and cultural harm in the residential school system. Progress on implementing the calls has been debated.

**The 2008 financial crisis.** Canada's banks were well regulated and avoided the worst, though it suffered a recession. Harper's government used stimulus spending.

**Roles of leaders.** Jean Chrétien (Liberal, 1993 to 2003) cut the deficit, passed the Clarity Act (2000) after the near-loss in 1995 and stayed out of the Iraq war; Paul Martin was harmed by the sponsorship scandal; Harper emphasised fiscal conservatism and a strong foreign policy; Justin Trudeau (from 2015) stressed reconciliation, climate policy and cannabis legalisation.

## Arguments for Paper 3

- Was the Great Society a success? Poverty fell, but costs and the Vietnam War limited it.
- Did Watergate show the strength or weakness of US institutions?
- Was Reaganomics the cause of growth or merely coincident?
- What explains rising polarisation?
- Was the Quiet Revolution a revolution, or the acceleration of existing change?
- Was the use of the War Measures Act justified?

## Common mistakes

- Giving a list of presidents and acts without judgement.
- Taking sides in recent politics without evidence.
- Confusing the FLQ, the Parti Québécois and the Bloc Québécois.
