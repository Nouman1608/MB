## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Issue | (Syllabus definition) an important subject or problem for discussion |
| Perspective | (Syllabus definition) a viewpoint on an issue based on evidence and reasoning |
| Evidence | (Syllabus definition) information about a global issue that helps to develop understanding or prove that something is true or false |
| Argument | (Syllabus definition) a series of statements containing reasons and evidence which support a claim about a global issue |
| Conflict | A serious disagreement or struggle between people, groups or states, from local disputes to war |
| Peacemaking | Bringing sides to an agreement through negotiation or mediation |
| Peacekeeping | Neutral forces or observers helping to stop fighting restarting |
| Peacebuilding | Long-term work on the causes of conflict: trust, institutions, the economy |
| Mediation | A neutral third person helps the sides reach their own agreement |
| Reconciliation | Rebuilding relationships between groups after conflict |
| Vested interest | The writer gains if readers accept their view |
| Generalisation | A broad statement about a whole group based on limited cases |

## The source-judging toolkit

Section 4 lists eight terms candidates benefit from understanding: **bias, claim, fact, generalisation, opinion, prediction, value and vested interest**. Learn a conflict example for each so you can spot them fast.

- **Claim:** "Peer mediation has stopped fights at our school."
- **Fact:** "The agreement was signed by both sides."
- **Opinion:** "The treaty is pointless."
- **Generalisation:** "Soldiers never want peace."
- **Prediction:** "Fighting will return once the peacekeepers leave."
- **Value:** "Dialogue is always better than force."
- **Bias:** A report quoting only one community's view of a clash.
- **Vested interest:** An arms company saying more defence spending keeps the peace.

**Reliability checklist:** author and expertise; purpose and vested interest; method (sample size, who was asked, self-reported or measured); date; support from independent sources.

## Perspectives at a glance

Use "who, what, why, one evaluation point" for each perspective.

| Who | What they argue (example issue) | Why | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Victims' group | Those responsible for abuses must face trial | Wants justice and recognition | Trials can take years and reach few cases |
| Government after a civil conflict | A truth commission helps the country move on | Needs stability and cooperation from former opponents | May be accused of protecting powerful people |
| Peace campaign group | Arms sales to states at war should stop | Values non-violence | Ignores the argument that states need weapons for defence |
| Defence industry | Arms exports are legal and support jobs | Profits and employment depend on sales | Clear vested interest; check its job figures independently |
| Local school leader | Peer mediation reduces fights | Wants a calm, safe school | Evidence may come from the school's own records |

## Courses of action at a glance

Real examples to show *how to evaluate* an action. They are not content to memorise.

| Action | Level | What it does | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mine Ban (Ottawa) Treaty, 1997 | Global | Bans anti-personnel landmines for states that join | Binds only states that sign; some major military powers have not |
| UN peacekeeping missions | Global | Neutral forces help keep ceasefires | Depends on troop contributions, funding and the sides' consent |
| Good Friday (Belfast) Agreement, 1998 | National | Shared government in Northern Ireland | Institutions can stall when parties disagree |
| South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission | National | Public testimony about abuses under apartheid | Amnesty for those who told the truth left some victims feeling denied justice |
| Peer mediation in schools | Local | Trained students help settle disputes | Unsuitable where one side holds much more power |

**Five criteria for assessing any action:** effectiveness, feasibility, acceptability to all sides, sustainability, unintended consequences.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Topic vs issue.** "Conflict and peace" is a topic. "Should countries sell weapons to states at war?" is an issue.
- **Opinion vs perspective.** An opinion can be unsupported. A perspective rests on evidence and reasoning.
- **Peacemaking vs peacekeeping vs peacebuilding.** Reaching agreement; holding the ceasefire; tackling causes over time.
- **Cause vs consequence.** Why the conflict started or continues vs what it leads to. Link them in chains.
- **Bias vs vested interest.** Bias is one-sided presentation. Vested interest is gaining from the outcome. A source can have one without the other.
- **Claim vs evidence.** The claim is what is said. Evidence is what supports it.
- **Local, national, global.** The Individual Report needs local and/or national perspectives and global perspectives. The Team Project needs a local issue.
- **Evaluating an action vs describing it.** Description says what happened; evaluation judges how well it worked and why.

## Mini essay plan: assessing actions

Typical extended question (in the style of Question 4, which asks candidates to "assess actions in response to the global issue"): *"Using the sources, assess which action would do most to reduce conflict between two communities."*

1. **Introduction (2-3 sentences).** Name the issue and the two or three actions in the sources. State the criteria you will use.
2. **Action 1.** What it is; evidence from the sources that it works; who would need to agree; limitation or unintended consequence.
3. **Action 2.** Same structure. Compare directly with Action 1 using the same criteria.
4. **Quality of the evidence.** Which action has stronger support? Point out vested interests, small samples or predictions presented as facts.
5. **Judgement.** Choose the action that does most, explain why it beats the alternative on your criteria, and say what would make you more confident (for example, independent evidence).

Keep each paragraph tied to the sources. Prior knowledge of the conflict earns nothing.

## Quick self-test

1. What does the syllabus say about knowledge of content in 0457?
2. Give the syllabus definition of a perspective.
3. Turn the topic Conflict and peace into one local issue.
4. What is the difference between peacekeeping and peacebuilding?
5. Classify: "If peacekeepers leave, fighting will start again within a year."
6. Classify: "Young people always join armed groups for money."
7. Why does a vested interest not automatically make a source wrong?
8. Name three questions to ask when judging a survey's reliability.
9. Which perspectives must the Individual Report include?
10. Give one way a Team Project on school disputes could measure the success of its action.
11. Name one limitation of a treaty as a course of action.

### Answers

1. It is not assessed; the topics are a context for developing skills.
2. A viewpoint on an issue based on evidence and reasoning.
3. For example: how should our school deal with arguments between year groups over shared spaces?
4. Peacekeeping holds a ceasefire in place; peacebuilding works on the causes of conflict over the long term.
5. A prediction.
6. A generalisation.
7. The evidence may still be accurate; vested interest is a reason to check it against independent sources.
8. Who ran it; how many people were asked and who they were; whether answers were self-reported.
9. Local and/or national perspectives, and global perspectives.
10. Compare the number of reported incidents before and after, or use a before-and-after questionnaire.
11. It binds only the states that join it.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Bringing in background knowledge about a real conflict when the question asks you to use the sources.
- Naming a source as "biased" without saying what is left out or who gains.
- Identifying a perspective but not explaining why that group holds it.
- Listing causes and consequences separately instead of linking them.
- Treating a prediction or a claim in a source as if it were an established fact.
- Assessing only one action when the question asks you to compare.
- Giving a judgement with no criteria, or criteria that do not appear in the paragraphs.
- In the Individual Report, choosing a title that does not divide opinion or is too broad to research.
- In the Team Project, choosing an action with no way to measure success.
