## Questions

**1.** Define scarcity. **[2]**

**2.** A tailor can make 16 shirts or 4 suits in a day, using all her time either way. Assume the trade-off is constant. Calculate the opportunity cost of one suit, and the number of shirts she can still make if she makes 3 suits. **[3]**

**3.** A government collects very high tax revenue. Explain why it still faces the problem of scarcity. **[3]**

**4.** A firm has 80,000 to invest. Project X adds 9,000 a year to profit, project Y adds 7,500, project Z adds 6,000, and a bank deposit pays 4% a year.

**(a)** State the opportunity cost of choosing project X. **[1]**
**(b)** A manager says the opportunity cost of X is 16,700. Explain the error. **[2]**
**(c)** X is expected to add only 7,000 instead. State which project the firm should now choose and its opportunity cost. **[2]**

**5.** Identify which of the three basic questions each decision mainly answers.

**(a)** A farm replaces 20 fruit pickers with one harvesting machine. **[1]**
**(b)** A country devotes more land to housing and less to golf courses. **[1]**
**(c)** A country gives free school meals only to low-income households. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain why the farm in (a) might keep its pickers if wages for pickers fell sharply. **[2]**

**6.** A workshop must make 200 units a day. Method P uses 5 workers at 80 cu a day each plus equipment costing 60 cu a day. Method R uses 2 workers and a robot costing 250 cu a day.

**(a)** Calculate the cost per unit of each method and state which the workshop should choose. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the daily wage at which the two methods cost the same. **[2]**
**(c)** State the cheaper method if the wage falls to 30 cu a day. **[1]**

**7.** A bike-hire stall cuts its price from 8 to 6. Daily hires rise from 150 to 180, but the weather also improved.

**(a)** Define ceteris paribus. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in price and in hires. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why the stall cannot conclude that the price cut alone raised hires by 20%. **[2]**

**8.** Classify each statement as positive or normative.

**(a)** The average rent in City D rose by 7% last year. **[1]**
**(b)** Rents in City D should be capped. **[1]**
**(c)** A rent cap reduces the supply of rented homes. **[1]**
**(d)** Inequality in City D is unfairly high. **[1]**
**(e)** Explain why statement (c) can be positive even if economists disagree about whether it is true. **[2]**

**9.** A small brewery expects demand to rise permanently. Explain how its possible responses differ in the short run, the long run and the very long run. **[6]**

**10.** Evaluate whether economics can be described as a science. **[8]**

**11.** Country E has a fixed budget of 420 million. A clinic network costs 105 million and a rail upgrade costs 70 million.

**(a)** Calculate the opportunity cost of one clinic network in rail upgrades. **[2]**
**(b)** The government builds two clinic networks. Calculate how many rail upgrades it can still fund. **[2]**
**(c)** Evaluate how useful the idea of opportunity cost is to the government in choosing between these projects. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Scarcity is the problem that human wants are unlimited [1] while the resources to satisfy them are limited [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition usually needs both halves, unlimited wants and limited resources. Saying only "not enough" is vague and does not show the contrast.

**2.** Opportunity cost of one suit = 16 / 4 = **4 shirts** [1]. Three suits use 3 x 4 = 12 shirts' worth of time [1]. Shirts left = 16 - 12 = **4 shirts** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* State the unit with the answer (shirts). Show the ratio first so that a slip in the later step is easy to trace.

**3.** Revenue, however large, is finite [1]. Demands on it, such as health, education and defence, exceed what it can fund [1]. So spending more on one use means giving up another, which is an opportunity cost [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A common weaker answer says rich countries have no scarcity. Link the finite budget to the choice between competing uses.

**4.** **(a)** The next best alternative is project Y, so the opportunity cost is **7,500 a year** [1].
**(b)** Opportunity cost counts only the next best alternative [1]. The manager added Y (7,500), Z (6,000) and the deposit (4% x 80,000 = 3,200), which gives 16,700, but the firm could only have chosen one of them [1].
**(c)** Y (7,500) now beats X (7,000), so the firm should choose **Y** [1]. Its opportunity cost is **7,000, the forgone return on X** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers that explain the error name the rule (next best only) and also show why adding the alternatives is wrong.

**5.** **(a)** **How** to produce [1].
**(b)** **What** to produce [1].
**(c)** **For whom** to produce [1].
**(d)** Cheaper labour makes pickers less costly relative to the machine [1]. The labour-intensive method may then give the lower cost per unit, so the farm keeps its pickers [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* In part (d), link the change in a relative price to the cost per unit. Saying only "pickers are cheap" leaves the choice unexplained.

**6.** **(a)** P: 5 x 80 + 60 = 460, so 460 / 200 = **2.30 per unit** [1]. R: 2 x 80 + 250 = 410, so 410 / 200 = **2.05 per unit** [1]. Choose **R**, the cheaper method [1].
**(b)** 5w + 60 = 2w + 250 [1], so 3w = 190 and w = **63.33 cu a day** [1].
**(c)** P costs 5 x 30 + 60 = 210 and R costs 2 x 30 + 250 = 310, so **P** is cheaper [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Show total cost and then cost per unit. An unlabelled final figure is harder to credit than one with working and a unit.

**7.** **(a)** Ceteris paribus means all other things being equal, so only one variable changes [1].
**(b)** Price change = (6 - 8) / 8 = **-25%**, and hires change = (180 - 150) / 150 = **+20%** [1].
**(c)** The weather also improved [1], so part of the rise may come from better weather. The price effect on its own cannot be identified unless other influences are held constant [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the specific other factor from the stem. Generic remarks such as "other things might change" earn less than a reference to the weather.

**8.** **(a)** **Positive**, as it can be checked against rent data [1].
**(b)** **Normative**, as "should" expresses a value judgement [1].
**(c)** **Positive**, as it is a testable claim about cause and effect [1].
**(d)** **Normative**, as "unfairly" is a judgement [1].
**(e)** A positive statement is one that can be tested, not one that is true [1]. Evidence could be used to test whether supply falls, so economists can disagree about the result while the statement stays positive [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Give a reason with each label, not only the label. Judging statements by whether they sound true is a frequent error.

**9.** In the short run some factor cannot be changed [1], so the brewery can only change variable inputs, such as hiring more staff, buying more grain or running extra shifts [1]. In the long run all factors can vary [1], so it can build more vats or larger premises [1]. In the very long run technology and the quality of factors can change [1], so it can adopt a new brewing process or train workers in new skills [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Define each period by what can change, then pair it with an example from the brewery. Fixed numbers of months are not part of the definitions.

**10.** Support: economics uses the scientific method [1]. It builds models and tests hypotheses against data, such as whether a price rise reduces quantity demanded [1]. Limits: ceteris paribus cannot be held in real economies [1], so results are tendencies, not exact laws [1]. Economists rarely run controlled experiments, and behaviour changes with expectations [1]. Policy advice also involves value judgements, which are normative and cannot be tested [1]. Judgement: economics is best described as a social science that applies scientific methods with less precision than the natural sciences [1]. The positive parts are closest to science, and the normative parts fall outside it [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation answers tend to gain credit when they reach a supported judgement, not just two lists. Tie your conclusion to the positive and normative distinction.

**11.** **(a)** 105 / 70 [1] = **1.5 rail upgrades** [1].
**(b)** Two networks cost 2 x 105 = 210, leaving 420 - 210 = 210 [1]. 210 / 70 = **3 rail upgrades** [1].
**(c)** Opportunity cost makes the trade-off explicit, such as 1.5 upgrades per network [1]. Numbers let the public and ministers scrutinise the choice [1]. However, benefits are not measured in the same units, for example lives saved against time saved [1], so the cost ratio does not show which is more valuable [1]. Benefits arrive over time and are uncertain [1]. Overall, it is useful for showing what is given up, but the final choice rests on value judgements about priorities [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* In part (c), use the figures from parts (a) and (b), then weigh them against what the numbers leave out. A judgement should say how far, not just whether, the idea helps.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "the cost of the thing" instead of the value of the next best alternative.
- Adding all alternatives together to find opportunity cost.
- Giving a ratio without units, or using the wrong base for the ratio.
- Treating scarcity as only a poor-country or shortage problem.
- Labelling a statement as positive because it sounds true, or normative because it names a policy.
- Defining ceteris paribus without identifying which other factors change in the stem.
- Defining the short run as a fixed number of months or years.
- Giving two lists in an evaluation answer and no supported judgement.
