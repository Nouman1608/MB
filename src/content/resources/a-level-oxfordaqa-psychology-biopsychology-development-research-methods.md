This guide covers **Unit 2 Biopsychology, Development and Research
Methods 1**, the second of two units forming the International AS in
OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Psychology (9685), first
teaching September 2018. Together with Unit 1, it makes up 50% of the
full A-level content and 40% of its final marks. It is assessed by a
single 1 hour 30 minute written exam worth 90 marks.

## Where this fits in 9685

Unit 1 covers cognitive, social and clinical psychology through
memory, social influence and psychopathology. Unit 2 shifts to
biological and developmental psychology, then introduces the first of
two research methods components -- Research Methods 1 here, extended
by Research Methods 2 at A2. Every topic in Unit 2 is compulsory and
assessed in a dedicated 30-mark section.

## Syllabus coverage

**OXFORDAQA INTERNATIONAL A-LEVEL PSYCHOLOGY (9685) — UNIT 2
BIOPSYCHOLOGY, DEVELOPMENT AND RESEARCH METHODS 1**

- 3.2.1 Biopsychology — the divisions of the nervous system (central
and peripheral, including somatic and autonomic); the structure and
function of sensory, relay and motor neurons and the process of
synaptic transmission, including neurotransmitters, excitation and
inhibition; the function of the endocrine system (glands and
hormones) and the fight-or-flight response, including adrenaline; and
localisation of function and hemispheric lateralisation in the brain,
including motor, somatosensory, visual, auditory and language
centres, Broca's and Wernicke's areas, split-brain research, and
plasticity and functional recovery after trauma
- 3.2.2 Cognitive development — Piaget's theory of cognitive
development, including schemas, assimilation, accommodation,
equilibration and the stages of intellectual development, with their
characteristics (object permanence, conservation, egocentrism, class
inclusion); Baillargeon's violation-of-expectation research;
Vygotsky's theory, including the zone of proximal development and
scaffolding; and social cognition, including theory of mind, the
Sally-Anne studies, and the role of the mirror neuron system
- 3.2.3 Research methods 1 — experimental methods (laboratory, field,
natural); observation techniques (naturalistic, controlled, covert,
overt, participant, non-participant); self-report techniques
(questionnaires, interviews); correlations; scientific processes
including aims and hypotheses, sampling techniques and their
implications, pilot studies, experimental designs (repeated measures,
independent groups, matched pairs), random allocation and
counterbalancing, variables and demand characteristics, and research
ethics; and data handling and analysis, including quantitative versus
qualitative data, descriptive statistics (mean, median, mode, range,
standard deviation), data presentation, and normal and skewed
distributions

## How to approach it

Unit 2 introduces genuinely new territory for most students --
biopsychology in particular relies on precise anatomical and
biochemical vocabulary that rewards labelled-diagram revision (the
divisions of the nervous system, a neuron with its synapse, and the
brain's localised regions) rather than prose alone. Cognitive
development is best revised by contrasting Piaget and Vygotsky
directly: both concern how children's thinking develops, but Piaget
frames this as an internal, stage-based process while Vygotsky frames
it as socially and culturally driven -- exam questions frequently ask
students to compare the two rather than describe either alone. Research
Methods 1 is cumulative rather than a one-off topic: everything learned
here about sampling, experimental design and ethics is assumed
knowledge for Research Methods 2 at A2, so building genuinely secure
foundations now, including being able to design a simple study from
scratch, pays off across the whole qualification.

## Common mistakes

Confusing the central and peripheral nervous systems, or forgetting
that the autonomic nervous system is itself a division of the
peripheral nervous system, not a separate category. Describing
Piaget's stages without their defining features (for example, naming
"the concrete operational stage" without being able to explain
conservation). Treating correlation as a type of experiment rather than
a distinct, non-experimental method -- correlations measure a
relationship between co-variables without manipulating an independent
variable. Confusing a population with a sample, or describing a
sampling technique without stating one implication (bias or
generalisability) of using it.

## Worked example

A study finds a correlation coefficient of -0.7 between hours spent
on a screen before bed and hours of sleep obtained. How should a
student interpret this in a Research Methods 1 answer?

```
COEFFICIENT: -0.7 indicates a strong NEGATIVE correlation -- as
             screen time increases, sleep duration tends to decrease.

WHAT IT DOES NOT SHOW: correlation identifies a relationship between
    two co-variables; it does not establish that screen time CAUSES
    reduced sleep. A third variable (for example, stress) could
    plausibly cause both increased screen use and reduced sleep.

STRENGTHS/LIMITATIONS: correlations can identify relationships
    efficiently and ethically, without manipulating variables, but
    cannot support causal claims and can be affected by unmeasured
    third variables.
```

Exam answers that describe a correlation strength and direction
correctly but never address causation, or never propose a plausible
third variable, typically miss the higher mark bands on this kind of
question. Always name a specific, plausible third variable rather than gesturing vaguely at "other factors" affecting the result.

## Quick revision checklist

- Sketch the divisions of the nervous system (central/peripheral,
somatic/autonomic) from memory.
- Label a neuron and describe the stages of synaptic transmission.
- Contrast Piaget's and Vygotsky's theories of cognitive development,
including one named study or concept from each.
- Explain theory of mind and describe the Sally-Anne study.
- Distinguish an experiment from a correlation, and state one strength
and one limitation of each.
- Practise identifying a sampling technique from a scenario and
stating one implication (bias or generalisability) of using it.
- List the ethical guidelines for psychological research and be ready
to apply them to a novel study description.
