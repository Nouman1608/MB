These questions practise key questions 2.2 (*How does society control us?*) and 2.3 (*What
influences our social identity?*) from Cambridge IGCSE Sociology (0495). They follow the six-part
(a)–(f) shape the syllabus describes for Paper 1 Question 2: short answers, explanations, a
discussion and a short essay.

---

## Question set A — Social control (2.2)

**(a)** Define the terms:
- (i) *informal social control* **[2]**
- (ii) *coercion* **[2]**

**(b)** Give **two** examples of informal sanctions used by the peer group. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **three** methods used by formal agencies to achieve social conformity. **[6]**

**(d)** Explain **three** reasons why some people join protest groups. **[6]**

**(e)** Discuss the differences between consensus and conflict views of social control. **[8]**

**(f)** Evaluate the view that the family is the most effective agency of social control. **[14]**

## Question set B — Social identity (2.3)

**(a)** Define the terms:
- (i) *hybrid identity* **[2]**
- (ii) *digital self* **[2]**

**(b)** Give **two** examples of how globalisation may influence identity. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **three** ways social class may influence a person's identity. **[6]**

**(d)** Explain **three** negative impacts of social media on identity. **[6]**

**(e)** Discuss the positive and negative impacts of multiculturalism on identity. **[8]**

**(f)** Evaluate the postmodernist view that identity is now chosen rather than given. **[14]**

## Answers — Question set A

**(a)(i) [2]** Control exercised through unofficial, everyday means — the reactions, rewards and
punishments of family, friends, school, media, religion or workplace (2). "Control by family" alone: 1.

**(a)(ii) [2]** Using force or the threat of force to make people conform, for example arrest or
military action (2).

**(b) [2]** Any two: teasing, ostracism (exclusion), gossip, praise and acceptance, online "likes" or
negative comments.

**(c) [6]** Three methods, 2 marks each (identify + explain): **law-making** sets rules backed by
punishment; **arrest** removes suspects and signals consequences; **digital surveillance** such as
CCTV and data monitoring deters and detects offences; **sentencing** by courts punishes and deters;
**imprisonment** incapacitates offenders.

**(d) [6]** Three reasons, 2 marks each: a shared grievance or injustice they want changed (for
example environmental damage); sense of belonging and identity; influence of friends or family
already involved; feeling ignored by formal politics; belief that collective action is effective.

**(e) [8]** Levels: 7–8 a clear, developed comparison; 4–6 some explanation of both; 1–3 basic
points.

- **Consensus (functionalism):** control maintains shared values and order, benefiting everyone;
  sanctions reinforce value consensus.
- **Conflict (Marxism):** control serves the ruling class — laws protect property and the police
  control the working class.
- **Conflict (feminism):** control maintains patriarchy — women are controlled through domestic
  roles, reputation and fear of violence.
- Difference: consensus views see control as neutral and beneficial; conflict views see it as
  serving powerful groups.

**(f) [14]** Levels: 11–14 balanced, developed and evaluated with a conclusion; 7–10 developed but
less balanced; 4–6 limited; 1–3 basic.

- *For:* primary socialisation and early sanctions create lasting conscience; parental rewards and
  punishments; emotional ties make children want to conform; family supervision of teenagers.
- *Against:* peers become more influential in adolescence; schools use formal rules and the hidden
  curriculum; media and social media shape norms; for serious crime, formal agencies (police,
  courts) are needed; some families fail to control or even encourage deviance.
- *Conclusion:* the family may be most effective early in life, but its effectiveness depends on age,
  the type of behaviour and support from other agencies.

## Answers — Question set B

**(a)(i) [2]** An identity that combines elements of two or more cultures, for example a young
person of migrant heritage blending the culture of their parents' country and the country they live
in (2).

**(a)(ii) [2]** The identity a person creates and presents online through profiles, posts, images
and interactions (2).

**(b) [2]** Any two: wearing global brands and fashions; following global music or sport; adopting
hybrid identities; strengthening local or religious identity as cultural defence; using global
social media platforms.

**(c) [6]** Three ways, 2 marks each: tastes and leisure (for example cultural capital passed on in
middle-class families — Bourdieu); accent and language; aspirations and expectations about education
and careers; values (such as attitudes to money and saving); sense of community in working-class
neighbourhoods.

**(d) [6]** Three impacts, 2 marks each: pressure to present an idealised image leading to low
self-esteem; cyberbullying; filter bubbles narrowing views; loss of privacy; reduced face-to-face
intimacy (Turkle, 2011).

**(e) [8]** Positive: groups keep their culture while sharing citizenship; diversity enriches
identity; confidence for minorities. Negative: possible separation between communities; prejudice
and stereotyping; pressure on young people caught between cultures. Credit balance and examples.

**(f) [14]**

- *For (postmodernism):* identities built through consumption and lifestyle in a "pick and mix"
  society; digital identities allow multiple selves; Bauman's "liquid" identity; decline of
  traditional class communities; global culture offers many options.
- *Against:* choice limited by money, class and access to technology; gender and ethnicity often
  ascribed and reinforced by others; strong inherited identities based on family and religion;
  cultural defence shows people holding on to traditional identities.
- *Conclusion:* identity involves both choice and constraint; credit a reasoned judgement.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long should a 14-mark answer be?** Several developed paragraphs with arguments on both sides
and a short conclusion.

**Can I use local examples?** Yes — the syllabus encourages examples from your own society.

**What earns the top level in (e)?** Clear, developed points on both sides with examples and an
explicit link to the question.
