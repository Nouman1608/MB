These questions cover "Aims and types of punishment" and "Crime prevention: types of policing,
surveillance" in 3.2.2.3, part of Section B of Paper 2.

---

## Section A: short questions

**1.** Identify **two** aims of punishment. **[2]**

**2.** What is meant by **general deterrence**? **[2]**

**3.** What is meant by **restorative justice**? **[2]**

**4.** Identify **two** forms of surveillance used in crime prevention. **[2]**

**5.** Study **Item A** and answer the questions that follow.

> **Item A** — Recorded thefts from cars in two neighbouring districts, before and after CCTV was
> installed in District P only
>
> *Illustrative data, invented for this practice question.*
>
> | District | Year before CCTV | Year after CCTV |
> |---|---|---|
> | P (CCTV installed) | 400 | 260 |
> | Q (no CCTV) | 300 | 410 |

**(a)** Using Item A, calculate the change in recorded thefts in District P, and state it as a
percentage of the year-before figure. **[2]**

**(b)** Using Item A, explain **one** reason why the data may not show that CCTV reduced crime
overall. **[3]**

## Section B: longer questions

**6.** Explain the difference between **retribution** and **rehabilitation** as aims of punishment.
**[4]**

**7.** Describe what is meant by **zero-tolerance policing**. **[4]**

**8.** Explain how Durkheim's view of punishment differs from a Marxist view. **[6]**

**9.** Explain Foucault's idea of the panopticon and how it applies to surveillance in modern
society. **[4]**

**10.** Explain **two** criticisms of predictive or intelligence-led policing. **[6]**

**11.** Discuss how far prison achieves the aims of punishment. Use examples from your own society
or a society you know about. **[12]**

## Answers: Section A

**A1** [2 marks — 1 each]

Any two: retribution; deterrence; rehabilitation; incapacitation or public protection; reparation;
denunciation.

**A2** [2 marks]

Punishing an offender in order to **warn other people** [1], so that fear of the same punishment
discourages them from committing the crime [1].

**A3** [2 marks]

An approach that brings **offender and victim together** [1] so the offender understands the harm
caused and **makes amends**, for example by apologising or repairing damage [1].

**A4** [2 marks — 1 each]

Any two: CCTV; number-plate recognition; facial recognition; monitoring of online activity; data from
phones, payment cards or apps; neighbourhood watch schemes.

**A5 (a)** [2 marks]

A fall of **140** thefts (400 − 260) [1], which is a **35%** fall (140 ÷ 400 × 100) [1].

**A5 (b)** [3 marks]

**Displacement** [1]: thefts in District Q, which had no CCTV, rose from 300 to 410, an increase of
110 [1], suggesting that some offenders may have moved to the unprotected district rather than
stopped offending [1]. Or: recorded crime depends on reporting and police activity [1], so changes in
recording may explain part of the difference [1], and the data covers only one year either side [1].

## Answers: Section B

**A6** [4 marks]

**Retribution** means the offender is punished because they deserve it [1], in proportion to the
seriousness of the crime [1]. **Rehabilitation** aims to **reform** the offender [1], for example
through education, training or treatment, so they do not reoffend [1].

**A7** [4 marks]

A style of policing that strictly enforces the law against **even minor offences** [1], such as
graffiti or begging [1]. It is linked to the **"broken windows"** idea of Wilson and Kelling (1982) [1],
that visible disorder signals that no one is in control and encourages more serious crime [1].

**A8** [6 marks]

Durkheim saw punishment as **functional for society as a whole** [1]: it expresses collective outrage
[1], reaffirms shared values and marks the boundaries of acceptable behaviour [1]. Marxists see
punishment as **serving the ruling class** [1]: it falls most heavily on the working class [1], while
crimes of the powerful, such as corporate crime, are less often prosecuted or are punished by fines
rather than prison [1].

**A9** [4 marks]

The panopticon was a prison design by Bentham in which inmates could always be watched without
knowing when [1]. Foucault argued that people who might be observed at any time start to **watch and
discipline themselves** [1]. In modern society, CCTV and online monitoring mean people may always be
observed [1], so they regulate their own behaviour even when no one is actually watching [1].

**A10** [6 marks — up to 3 per criticism]

Two from: **bias in data** [1] — if past policing focused on certain areas or groups [1], predictions
send more officers there, reproducing the same bias [1]; **self-fulfilling prophecy** [1] — more
policing in an area [1] leads to more recorded crime there, which seems to confirm the prediction [1];
**privacy and civil liberties** [1] — collecting data on people who have not offended [1] raises
concerns about surveillance and fairness [1]; **neglect of hidden crime** [1] — data-driven targeting
focuses on visible street crime [1], while white-collar and corporate crime receive less attention [1].

**A11** [12 marks — levels]

| Level | Marks | Descriptor |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | 10–12 | Detailed knowledge; well applied to a named society; balanced discussion with a clear, justified conclusion |
| 3 | 7–9 | Good knowledge; some application; discussion with a conclusion |
| 2 | 4–6 | Some relevant knowledge; limited application or one-sided |
| 1 | 1–3 | Basic, mainly descriptive points |

Indicative content:

- **Prison achieves some aims:** incapacitation while in custody; retribution; denunciation;
  deterrence for some; rehabilitation where education and treatment are available.
- **Prison fails some aims:** reoffending after release; stigma and master status (Goffman, Becker)
  limiting legitimate opportunities; learning criminal skills from other prisoners; short sentences
  disrupting work and family without reform.
- **Perspectives:** Durkheim on boundary maintenance; Marxist view of whom prisons hold; Foucault on
  discipline.
- **Alternatives:** community sentences, restorative justice.
- **Application:** a named society, for example its use of short sentences or prison education.
- **Judgement:** for example, prison is effective at incapacitation and denunciation, but often weak at
  rehabilitation and long-term deterrence.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating a change without stating the percentage when asked, or dividing by the wrong figure.
- Confusing deterrence and incapacitation.
- Describing zero tolerance without its rationale.
- Naming Foucault without explaining self-discipline.
- An extended answer with no named society.
