## States of matter (2.1.1)

| Property | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shape | fixed | takes the shape of its container | fills its container |
| Volume | fixed | fixed | fills its container |
| Can it flow? | no | yes | yes |
| Compressible? | almost not | almost not | easily |

**Changes of state:** solid → liquid is **melting**; liquid → solid is **solidification** (freezing); liquid → gas is **boiling** or **evaporation**; gas → liquid is **condensation**. Direct gas ↔ solid changes are not required.

## The particle model (2.1.2)

| | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arrangement | regular pattern (lattice) | irregular, random | random |
| Separation | very close, touching | close, touching | far apart (on average many particle diameters) |
| Motion | vibrate about fixed positions | move around each other, sliding past | move quickly in random directions, in straight lines between collisions |

**Drawing particle diagrams:** for a solid, rows of touching circles of equal size in a regular pattern; for a liquid, touching circles with no pattern and a few gaps (most circles still touching); for a gas, a few circles widely and randomly spaced. Use the same size of circle for all three: the particles themselves do not change size.

The model explains the properties above: a gas is easily compressed because its particles are far apart; a liquid flows because its particles can move past each other; a solid keeps its shape because its particles are held in fixed positions.

### Temperature and particle motion

The higher the temperature, the faster the particles move (or vibrate), on average. Cooling slows them down. There is a lowest possible temperature, **−273 °C**, called **absolute zero**, at which the particles have the least kinetic energy they can have.

### Forces and distances (Extended only)

The particles of matter (atoms, molecules, ions and electrons) attract each other strongly when close together. The **forces and distances** between particles, together with their **motion**, decide the properties:

- In a solid, strong forces hold particles close together in fixed positions: fixed shape and volume.
- In a liquid, the forces still keep the particles close (fixed volume) but are not strong enough to hold them in place against their motion: it flows.
- In a gas, the particles are far apart and the forces between them are very weak: the particles move freely and fill the container, and the gas is easily compressed.

## Gas pressure

**Core.** Gas particles move randomly and collide with the walls of their container. Each collision pushes on the wall, and the huge number of collisions every second produces the **pressure** of the gas. More frequent or harder collisions mean a larger pressure.

**Extended only.** When a particle hits a wall and rebounds, its momentum changes, so the wall exerts a force on it and it exerts an equal force on the wall. The total force from all the collisions, divided by the area of the wall, is the pressure: **pressure is the force per unit area** created by particles colliding with the surface.

## Brownian motion

Place smoke in a small glass cell, light it from the side and look through a microscope. The smoke particles are seen as tiny bright specks that move in a **random, jerky, zigzag** way and never stop.

**Explanation.** The air molecules are far too small to see. They move quickly and randomly, colliding with each smoke particle from all sides. At any instant more collisions happen on one side than the other, so the smoke particle is pushed in a random direction, then another. The random motion of microscopic particles in a suspension is **evidence for the kinetic particle model**: it shows that the fluid around them is made of moving particles.

**Extended only.** The smoke particles are **microscopic particles**, large enough to see in a microscope; the air particles hitting them are **molecules** (or atoms). Use these words carefully: "the molecules move randomly" is right, and "the smoke molecules move randomly" is wrong. A microscopic particle is much more massive than a molecule, but because the molecules are **light and moving fast**, their collisions can still move it.

## Gases and the absolute scale of temperature (2.1.3)

### Pressure, temperature and volume (qualitative)

For a fixed mass of gas:

- **Temperature rises at constant volume:** the particles move faster, so they hit the walls more often and harder; the pressure **increases**. (This is why an aerosol can must not be heated.)
- **Volume decreases at constant temperature:** the particles move at the same speeds but have less space, so they hit each unit area of wall more often; the pressure **increases**.

### The kelvin scale

```
T (in K) = θ (in °C) + 273
```

| °C | K |
|---|---|
| −273 (absolute zero) | 0 |
| 0 (water freezes) | 273 |
| 37 (body temperature) | 310 |
| 100 (water boils) | 373 |
| −196 | 77 |

A temperature **change** is the same size on both scales: a rise of 25 °C is a rise of 25 K.

### pV = constant (Extended only)

For a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature, **pressure × volume = constant**, so p₁V₁ = p₂V₂.

**Worked example 1.** Air in a sealed syringe has a volume of 60 cm³ at 100 kPa. The plunger is pushed in slowly (so the temperature stays constant) until the volume is 24 cm³. Find the new pressure.

```
p₁V₁ = p₂V₂
100 × 60 = p₂ × 24
p₂ = 6000 / 24 = 250 kPa
```

**Worked example 2.** A diver breathes out a bubble of volume 2.0 cm³ where the pressure is 3.0 × 10⁵ Pa. It rises to the surface, where the pressure is 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa. Assuming the temperature is constant, find its volume at the surface.

```
V₂ = p₁V₁ / p₂ = (3.0 × 10⁵ × 2.0) / 1.0 × 10⁵ = 6.0 cm³
```

Pressure and volume can be in any units, provided each is in the same units on both sides.

**The graph.** A graph of p against V is a curve that falls steeply and then levels off, never touching either axis: halving the volume doubles the pressure. A graph of p against 1/V is a **straight line through the origin**, which shows that p is inversely proportional to V. To test results, multiply each pair of p and V: the products should be the same (within experimental error).

## Common mistakes

- Drawing gas particles smaller than liquid particles, or liquid particles widely spaced.
- Saying particles "expand" when heated: they move faster; the particles themselves do not get bigger.
- Saying Brownian motion is caused by the smoke particles "being alive" or by convection.
- Explaining pressure by "particles hitting each other"; it is collisions with the **walls**.
- Converting kelvin to Celsius by adding 273 (subtract it).
- *(Extended)* Using pV = constant when the temperature changes.
- *(Extended)* Calling smoke particles "molecules".

## Exam technique: command words

- **State** the properties of a state: give shape, volume and compressibility.
- **Describe** the particle structure: give arrangement, separation **and** motion; diagrams can earn the marks if neat and labelled.
- **Describe and explain** Brownian motion: what is seen (random, jerky motion of bright specks), then the cause (random collisions with fast-moving air molecules).
- **Explain** a pressure change: describe particle speed, frequency of collisions with the walls and the force of collisions.
- **Calculate** *(Extended)*: write p₁V₁ = p₂V₂, substitute, give the unit.
