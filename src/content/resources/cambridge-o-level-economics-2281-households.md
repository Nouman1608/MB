This guide covers section 3.2 **Households** of the Cambridge O Level Economics 2281 syllabus for examination in 2026. It has one content point: "the influences on spending, saving and borrowing", which the syllabus says should include "income, the rate of interest and confidence – between different households and over time".

The combined [money, banking and household spending guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-money-banking-and-household-spending-saving-and/) explains each influence with saving-ratio and loan calculations. This page goes further on the parts of the content point that answers often handle weakly: why the **same** change affects different households in opposite ways, how wealth and access to credit work alongside income, how one household's choices change through its life and through the economic cycle, and how to judge which influence matters most. Companion pages: [households revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-households-revision-notes/) and [households practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-households-practice/). All households and figures are invented.

## What the syllabus asks for

| Part of the content point | You must be able to |
|---|---|
| Influences on spending | Explain how income, interest rates and confidence (and other influences) change household spending |
| Influences on saving | Explain why households save and why the amount saved changes |
| Influences on borrowing | Explain why households borrow and what makes borrowing more or less attractive |
| Between different households | Compare how influences affect rich and poor, young and old, borrowers and savers |
| Over time | Explain how one household's choices change through its life and as conditions change |

## The basic identity

A household's **disposable income** is its income after direct taxes, plus any state benefits. It can do three things with it: **spend** it on goods and services, **save** it, or use it to **repay debt**. **Borrowing** adds to what can be spent now, at the cost of lower spending later when the debt and interest are repaid. So every influence in this section works by changing the balance between spending now and spending later.

## Influence 1: income

As income rises, households spend more in total, but the **share** spent usually falls and the share saved rises. A household on a low income must spend almost everything on food, housing and transport; a household on a high income has covered its needs and can put more aside.

| Household | Monthly disposable income ($) | Spending ($) | Saving ($) | Share saved |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 800 | 792 | 8 | 1% |
| B | 2,000 | 1,800 | 200 | 10% |
| C | 5,000 | 3,750 | 1,250 | 25% |

Household C earns 6.25 times as much as Household A but saves over 150 times as much. This explains why poorer households are more likely to borrow for an unexpected bill: they have almost no savings to fall back on.

**Wealth is different from income.** Income is a flow received over a period; wealth is a stock of assets owned at a moment, such as savings, shares and property. A household whose house rises in value may spend more and save less even if its income has not changed, because it feels better off and can borrow against the house.

## Influence 2: the rate of interest

The interest rate is the reward for saving and the cost of borrowing. A rise in interest rates usually:

- **encourages saving**, because each dollar saved earns more
- **discourages new borrowing**, because loans cost more to repay
- **reduces spending on items bought on credit**, such as cars, furniture and houses
- **cuts the disposable income of households with variable-rate debts**, because their repayments rise

### Worked example 1: one rise, two households

Interest rates rise from 4% to 6%.

**Household D** (borrower) owes $60,000 on a variable-rate mortgage, paying interest only for simplicity. Its monthly disposable income is $2,500.

1. Interest at 4%: 60,000 × 0.04 = $2,400 a year, or $200 a month.
2. Interest at 6%: 60,000 × 0.06 = $3,600 a year, or $300 a month.
3. Interest now takes 300 ÷ 2,500 × 100 = 12% of income instead of 8%, leaving $100 a month less for other spending.

**Household E** (saver) has $20,000 in a savings account.

1. Interest at 4%: $800 a year. At 6%: $1,200 a year.
2. Its income rises by $400 a year, and it may spend some of that.

The same policy cuts D's spending and may raise E's. This is the heart of "between different households": a change in interest rates moves income from borrowers to savers. Younger households, who are more likely to have mortgages, tend to lose; older households, who are more likely to hold savings, tend to gain.

## Influence 3: confidence

**Confidence** is how secure households feel about their future jobs, incomes and prices. It matters because spending and borrowing are decisions about the future.

- **High confidence**: households expect their income to hold up, so they borrow for big purchases and save less.
- **Low confidence**: households fear job losses, so they build up **precautionary saving**, delay buying cars and furniture, and avoid new debt, even if their current income has not changed.

Confidence can change faster than income. News of factory closures can cut spending across a whole town before a single job has been lost. It also affects lenders: banks lend less when they fear borrowers will not repay.

## Other influences worth using

The syllabus says "including", so other relevant influences earn credit when explained.

- **Availability of credit**: if banks relax lending rules, more households can borrow, whatever the interest rate.
- **Expected price changes**: if prices are expected to rise, households buy durable goods sooner; if they are expected to fall, they wait.
- **Household size and age**: a family with young children spends a larger share on essentials.
- **Taxes and benefits**: higher income tax reduces disposable income, cutting both spending and saving.
- **Attitudes and habits**: some households and cultures place more value on saving or on avoiding debt.

## Over time: the life cycle and the economic cycle

| Stage | Typical pattern | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Young adult | Borrows; saves little | Low income now; borrows for study, a car or a first home, expecting income to rise |
| Middle age | Repays debts; saves more | Income near its peak; saving for children's education and retirement |
| Retired | Spends savings (dissaves) | Income falls; savings and pensions finance spending |

The economy also moves through periods of growth and recession. In a boom, rising incomes and confidence raise spending and borrowing. In a recession, falling incomes and confidence raise precautionary saving, which can make the downturn worse because firms sell less.

### Worked example 2: one household over a year

Household F has a monthly disposable income of $3,000 and spends $2,700. During the year: the main earner hears that the firm may cut jobs; the interest rate on its car loan rises; and its rent rises by $150 a month.

- Confidence falls, so the family moves $200 a month from spending to saving.
- The car loan repayment rises by $50 a month.
- Rent rises by $150, which is essential spending.

Saving rises from $300 to $500 a month, and rent and the loan take $200 more, so all its other spending falls by 200 + 50 + 150 = $400 a month. That cut equals 400 ÷ 2,700 × 100 = 14.8% of its original spending, even though its income has not changed.

## Judging which influence matters most

Good answers do not simply list influences. They judge:

- **Income** is usually the strongest long-run influence, because it sets the limit on what a household can do.
- **Interest rates** matter most for households with large debts, and for spending on items bought on credit.
- **Confidence** can dominate in the short run, especially when unemployment is rising.
- The effect of one influence depends on the others: a cut in interest rates may not raise spending if confidence is very low.

## Common mistakes

- Treating saving as "money kept in a bank" only. Saving is any income not spent; repaying debt is also a use of income.
- Saying a rise in interest rates always reduces spending for everyone. Savers may spend more.
- Confusing income (a flow) with wealth (a stock).
- Writing about "households" in general when the question asks you to compare two types.
- Explaining "over time" with no reference to life stages or changing economic conditions.

## Exam technique for 3.2

- **Explain** an influence as a chain: the change, what it does to the household's choices, and the result for spending, saving or borrowing.
- **Analyse** differences between households by naming two types and showing why the same change affects them differently.
- **Calculate**: shares of income, interest costs and percentage changes, with the base clearly stated.
- **Discuss** whether a change (for example an interest rate cut) will raise household spending: weigh the effect on borrowers and savers, the level of confidence and the availability of credit, then judge.
