These questions test the section 3.2 row on theory in criminal law: harm, autonomy, fault and individual responsibility, and the four principles. Most marks go to applying a principle to a named offence or case.

---

## Section A

**1.** State the harm principle and identify the writer associated with it. **[2]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by the principle of fair labelling. **[2]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by the principle of correspondence. **[2]**

**4.** Explain what is meant by the principle of maximum certainty, and identify the Article of the European Convention on Human Rights that reflects it. **[3]**

**5.** Explain how the rule on omissions reflects the idea of autonomy. **[3]**

**6.** Explain why strict liability is said to conflict with the principle of fault, using **one** case. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain how *R v Kennedy (No 2)* (2007) reflects the idea of individual responsibility. **[4]**

**8.** Explain why *R v Brown* (1993) is controversial when judged against the harm principle. **[5]**

**9.** Explain how the law of murder raises issues of fair labelling. **[5]**

**10.** Explain how *R v R* (1991) raises the principle of no retrospective liability, and how the European Court of Human Rights responded. **[5]**

**11.** Explain how the law on gross negligence manslaughter and the law on dishonesty in theft each raise issues of maximum certainty. **[6]**

**12.** Evaluate the extent to which the law of homicide reflects the principle that criminal liability should be based on fault. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** The only purpose for which power can rightfully be exercised over a member of a civilised community against their will is to prevent harm to others [1]. It is associated with John Stuart Mill, *On Liberty* (1859) [1].

**2.** The name of an offence should fairly describe the nature and seriousness of the wrongdoing [1], so that the label communicates to the public and to the court what the defendant did and distinguishes between more and less serious wrongs [1].

**3.** The mens rea of an offence should match its actus reus [1], so that a defendant is liable only for a consequence they intended or foresaw [1].

**4.** Criminal offences should be defined clearly enough that people can know in advance what conduct is forbidden [1], and judges and juries can apply them consistently [1]. This is reflected in Article 7 ECHR, no punishment without law [1].

**5.** There is no general duty to act in English criminal law [1], so a person who chooses not to help another is not normally liable [1]. This respects individual freedom of choice; liability arises only where a recognised duty exists, such as from a contract or a special relationship [1].

**6.** The fault principle holds that people should be punished only if they are blameworthy [1]. Strict liability offences require no mens rea for at least one element, so a person who took reasonable care can be convicted [1], as in *Harrow LBC v Shah* (1999), where a newsagent was convicted when an employee sold a lottery ticket to a child under 16 despite precautions [1].

**7.** Kennedy supplied heroin to the victim, who injected himself and died [1]. The House of Lords held that the victim's free, deliberate and informed act of self-injection broke the chain of causation [1], so Kennedy was not guilty of unlawful act manslaughter [1]. This reflects individual responsibility: an autonomous adult is responsible for his own free choices, and others are not liable for the consequences of those choices [1].

**8.** The defendants consented to the injuries, which were inflicted in private [1]. On Mill's principle, harm to consenting adults who are not harming others should not be criminal [1]. The House of Lords nevertheless upheld the convictions under s47 and s20 [1], with Lord Templeman saying society is entitled to protect itself against a cult of violence [1]. Critics argue this is legal moralism or paternalism rather than prevention of harm to others, especially when compared with *R v Wilson* (1996), where consensual branding was lawful [1].

**9.** Murder requires an intention to kill or to cause GBH (*Vickers*, 1957) [1], so a defendant who meant only serious harm carries the same label as one who meant to kill [1]. The label covers very different killings, such as a planned contract killing and a mercy killing of a terminally ill relative [1]. All attract the mandatory life sentence, so the court cannot reflect the difference in the label or in the sentence type [1]. The partial defences reduce some killings to manslaughter, but this creates the opposite problem of a very wide label for manslaughter [1].

**10.** Until 1991 a husband could not in law be guilty of raping his wife [1]. In *R v R* the House of Lords held that the marital exemption no longer formed part of the law and upheld the husband's conviction [1], even though his conduct took place before the decision, which appears retrospective [1]. In *SW v UK* (1995) the European Court of Human Rights held there was no breach of Article 7 [1], because the change was a reasonably foreseeable development of the law and removing the exemption respected human dignity [1].

**11.** In gross negligence manslaughter, the jury decides whether the negligence was so bad as to be criminal (*R v Adomako*, 1995) [1]; this is circular, since the jury decides whether conduct is a crime by asking whether it is criminal [1], although *R v Misra and Srivastava* (2004) held the offence compatible with Article 7 [1]. In theft, dishonesty is judged by the standards of ordinary decent people (*Ivey v Genting Casinos*, 2017; *R v Barton and Booth*, 2020) [1]. There is no single fixed definition, so different juries may reach different views on similar facts [1], and a defendant cannot be sure in advance whether conduct will be judged dishonest [1].

**12.** Levels-based. **Reflects fault:** murder requires intention, the highest form of fault [1]; oblique intention requires foresight of virtual certainty (*Woollin*, 1998), so foresight of mere probability is not enough [1]. The partial defences recognise reduced fault: loss of control (s54 Coroners and Justice Act 2009) [1] and diminished responsibility (s2 Homicide Act 1957) [1]. Gross negligence manslaughter requires a serious and obvious risk of death and a gross breach (*Adomako*; *R v Rose*, 2017) [1]. **Departs from fault:** murder can be committed with intention only to cause GBH, without foresight of death [1]. Unlawful act manslaughter requires only the mens rea of the unlawful act and objective dangerousness (*R v Church*, 1966) [1], with no need to foresee harm (*DPP v Newbury and Jones*, 1977) [1], so a single punch causing an unforeseeable death can lead to a manslaughter conviction [1]. Gross negligence is an objective test, so a defendant who did not personally foresee the risk can be convicted [1]. **Counter-argument:** a person who chooses to commit a dangerous unlawful act accepts responsibility for its consequences [1]. A reasoned conclusion, for example that homicide reflects fault at the top of the scale but relies on constructive liability lower down [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining a principle without applying it to any offence.
- Describing *Brown* without saying why it is controversial.
- Treating *R v R* as a breach of Article 7 when Strasbourg found none.
- Giving a one-sided evaluation with no counter-argument.
