## Texts

**Prose passage (original, written for practice).**

> At five I believed the corridor outside my bedroom grew at night. In daylight it was eleven steps from my door to the bathroom; after the lamp went out, I counted twenty, thirty, and still the bathroom light had not come closer. My grandmother, who slept in the room beside mine and who counted everything, said the dark was only the day with its coat on. I did not believe her, but I held the sentence like a banister and walked.

**Poem (original, written for practice).**

> **Night Shift**
>
> He leaves when the streetlamps blink awake,
> his lunchbox rattling like a small, tame engine.
> We learn the house by its daytime silence:
> the folded sleeves, the boots set heel to heel,
> the sleep he wears like a heavy borrowed coat.
> Mother moves round him on her toes.
> What he earns arrives in envelopes, in coins,
> in the careful way she counts them twice.
> At six, the key. We pretend to be asleep
> so he can pretend he does not hear us pretend.

## Answers

Marking guidance is Marlbridge's own. Award marks for the points listed or for equally valid alternatives.

**1.** 2 hours 30 minutes [1]; 50 marks [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Childhood; war; women and men; race, class and culture [2 for all four; 1 for two or three]. **[2]**

**3.** Students are not expected to draw on extensive contextual knowledge of the topic (nor on wider reading) [1]. **[1]**

**4.** Analyse how meanings are shaped in literary texts [1] with close attention to authorial methods, that is, the choices a writer makes [1]. **[2]**

**5. (a)** "Analysis" tells you to examine methods in detail [1]. "Significance" tells you to weigh up what the representation means, not just describe it [1]. "Childhood" is the area, so the focus is on how the child's experience is shown [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Award 2 marks for each complete line of argument (quotation, method, effect), up to 6. Examples:

- Fear of the dark: "I counted twenty, thirty, and still the bathroom light had not come closer". The method is repetition and a growing count. The effect is to show how time and distance stretch for a frightened child, so childhood is represented as a place of large, private fears [2].
- The adult's wisdom: "the dark was only the day with its coat on". The method is personification and a domestic metaphor. The effect is that the grandmother turns fear into something familiar, so adults are shown as translating the world for children [2].
- Coping through language: "I held the sentence like a banister and walked". The method is a simile that makes words physical. The effect is that the child takes support from an adult's words even without belief, showing trust as stronger than understanding [2].

**(c)** Award up to 6: a direct answer to the question in the first sentence [1]; a clear sense of what the passage represents about childhood [1]; at least one short, accurate quotation [1]; a method named and linked to an effect [1]; an indication that other readings are possible [1]; accurate, controlled writing [1]. A model: "The passage represents childhood as a time when ordinary space becomes uncertain and adults provide the means to cope. The narrator's count of 'twenty, thirty' steps in a corridor that is 'eleven steps' by day shows how fear distorts distance, so the child's world is shaped by imagination as much as by fact. Yet the grandmother's phrase, 'the dark was only the day with its coat on', gives the child something to hold. The passage might be read as a gentle picture of reassurance, or as a more guarded one in which the child must walk alone with a sentence she does not believe." **[6]**

**6. (a)** The voice is a collective first-person plural: "We learn the house by its daytime silence", "We pretend to be asleep" [1]. The plural shows the children as a group who share the knowledge of the father's routine [1]. It positions the reader inside the family, seeing the father at a distance, which makes him a respected but remote figure [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** The line "so he can pretend he does not hear us pretend" repeats "pretend" [1], so that father and children share a pretence out of tenderness [1], which suggests that class and work create a silent understanding in which love is shown through what is not said [1]. **[3]**

**(c)** One reading: the careful counting shows the mother's competence and loving care for the family's limited means [1]. Another reading: it shows the pressure of money, and a fear that each coin matters, so the family's class position limits their lives [1]. Both readings are supported by "the careful way she counts them twice", where "careful" can mean tender or anxious [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** The extract summarises the content ("is about") instead of analysing the poem [1]. It names a method (simile) without a quotation, an effect on the representation or any link to class or men [1]. (Also accept: "sad" is vague and unsupported.) **[2]**

**(b)** Award marks for: a clear claim linked to the task [1]; a short quotation [1]; a method named with its effect [1]; a hint at another reading or a link to class [1]. A model: "The poem represents the working father as present only through his absence. The simile 'his lunchbox rattling like a small, tame engine' makes him part of a machine-like routine, and 'tame' suggests that his energy is controlled by the demands of the job. This could be read as respect for his labour, or as a quiet criticism of a life ordered by work." **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Summarising or paraphrasing the text.
- Naming a method with no effect.
- A single reading, with no sense that others are possible.
- Weak or no quotation.
- Neglecting the aspect the question names, in this case childhood or men and class.
- Using prepared context that the passage does not support.
