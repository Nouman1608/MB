## Questions

**1.** Define *genetic diversity* and explain why it matters for the survival of a population.
**[3]**

**2.** Outline **two** sources of heritable variation in a population. **[2]**

**3.** A snail species has banded and unbanded shells. Samples were collected from two habitats.

| Habitat | Banded snails | Total snails |
|---|---|---|
| Woodland (uniform brown leaf litter) | 45 | 180 |
| Rough grassland (varied stems and shadows) | 132 | 220 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of banded snails in each habitat. **[2]**
**(b)** Song thrushes hunt these snails by sight. Suggest an explanation for the difference in
percentages. **[3]**

**4.** Explain how a population of bacteria can become resistant to an antibiotic. **[4]**

**5.** Distinguish between geographical isolation and behavioural isolation. **[2]**

**6.** Explain how a new species could form without any physical barrier separating populations.
**[3]**

**7.** Outline what is meant by adaptive radiation, using a named example. **[3]**

**8.** Outline how the break-up of Gondwana has influenced the animals found in Australia today.
**[2]**

**9.** A fossil study counted families of marine animals.

| Time (million years ago) | 260 | 252 | 230 | 200 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Families recorded | 500 | 250 | 380 | 500 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of families lost between 260 and 252 million years ago. **[1]**
**(b)** State how long it took for the number of families to return to 500. **[1]**
**(c)** Suggest **two** reasons why recovery after a mass extinction takes so long. **[2]**

**10.** Compare the end-Cretaceous mass extinction with present-day biodiversity loss. **[4]**

**11.** Distinguish between gradualism and punctuated equilibrium. **[2]**

**12.** Discuss whether present-day biodiversity loss should be described as a sixth mass
extinction. **[7]**

## Answers

**1.** Genetic diversity is the range of different genes or alleles within a species or population
[1]. A diverse population is more likely to include individuals with alleles that allow survival
when conditions change, such as a new disease [1], so the population can adapt rather than be wiped
out [1]. **[3]**

**2.** Mutation creates new alleles through random changes in DNA [1]; sexual reproduction (meiosis
and fertilisation) creates new combinations of alleles in offspring [1]. **[2]**

**3. (a)** Woodland: 45 / 180 × 100 = **25%** [1]; grassland: 132 / 220 × 100 = **60%** [1]
**(b)** On uniform woodland litter, unbanded shells match the background, so thrushes find more of
the banded snails [1]; among varied grass stems and shadows, bands break up the shell's outline, so
banded snails are harder to see [1]; the less visible form survives and reproduces more in each habitat, so its frequency rises
there (selective predation) [1]. **[5]**

**4.** The population shows variation, and a few bacteria carry an allele giving resistance, arising
by mutation [1]. The antibiotic kills susceptible bacteria [1]. Resistant bacteria survive and
reproduce rapidly, passing on the resistance allele [1]. Over many generations the resistant form
becomes common, especially when the antibiotic is used repeatedly [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Geographical isolation separates populations by a physical barrier such as a sea or mountain
range [1]; behavioural isolation occurs when populations in the same area stop interbreeding because
of differences in courtship behaviour, songs or displays [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Populations in the same area become reproductively isolated, for example by breeding at
different times (temporal) or by different courtship signals (behavioural) [1]. Gene flow between
them stops [1]. Different selection pressures and random changes cause allele frequencies to diverge
until they can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring (sympatric speciation) [1]. **[3]**

**7.** One ancestral species diversifies rapidly into many species [1], each adapted to a different
niche, often after colonising an area with many unoccupied niches [1]. Example: Darwin's finches on
the Galápagos Islands evolved different beak shapes for different foods [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Australia was isolated for a very long time after Gondwana split [1], so marsupials diversified
there with little competition from placental mammals, producing many species found nowhere else [1].
**[2]**

**9. (a)** (500 − 250) / 500 × 100 = **50%** [1]
**(b)** From 252 to 200 million years ago: **52 million years** [1]
**(c)** Any two: new species evolve slowly through speciation [1]; ecosystems and food webs must
rebuild before many niches become available [1]; environmental conditions may remain hostile for a
long time after the event [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Up to 4 marks; at least one similarity and one difference.
- *Similarity:* both involve loss of a large share of species over a geologically short time [1];
  both cause rapid environmental change, including climate change [1].
- *Differences:* the end-Cretaceous event was caused mainly by an asteroid impact, the present loss by
  the activities of one species [1]; the present loss is happening over centuries [1]; present causes
  are multiple and continuing (habitat loss, overexploitation, invasive species, pollution, climate
  change) [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Gradualism describes slow, steady evolutionary change over long periods [1]; punctuated
equilibrium describes long periods of little change interrupted by short bursts of rapid change [1].
**[2]**

**12.** Award up to 7: arguments for, up to 3; arguments against or caveats, up to 3; a judgement
[1].
- *For:* current extinction rates are widely reported to be far above background rates [1]; losses
  span many groups (amphibians, corals, freshwater fish, island birds) [1]; drivers such as habitat
  loss and climate change are intensifying [1]; past mass extinctions also involved rapid
  environmental change [1].
- *Against or caveats:* past mass extinctions removed a very large share of all species, and present
  total losses are smaller so far [1]; many species are undescribed, so rates are uncertain [1];
  extinction can be slowed by conservation, so the outcome is not yet fixed [1].
- *Judgement:* for example, the term is justified as a warning about the direction and rate of
  change, even if present losses have not yet reached the scale of the five past events [1]. **[7]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing that organisms "adapt in order to survive".
- Comparing raw counts when sample sizes differ.
- Leaving "gene flow stops" out of speciation answers.
- Answering "discuss" with arguments on one side only.
