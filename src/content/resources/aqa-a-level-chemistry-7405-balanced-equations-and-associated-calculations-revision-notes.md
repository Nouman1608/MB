These notes condense section **3.1.2.5 Balanced equations and associated calculations** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Papers 1 and 2 (and Paper 3). The [Balanced equations study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-balanced-equations-and-associated-calculations/) explains each method with worked examples, and the [Balanced equations practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-balanced-equations-and-associated-calculations-practice/) give exam-style practice.

## What the specification asks

- Write **full and ionic equations**, including balancing for unfamiliar reactions when reactants and products are given.
- Use balanced equations to calculate **masses, gas volumes, percentage yields, percentage atom economies**, and **concentrations and volumes of solutions**.
- Know the **economic, ethical and environmental** advantages of a high atom economy.
- Required practical 1: make up a volumetric solution and carry out a simple acid-base titration.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Balanced equation | Same number of each type of atom (and the same total charge) on both sides |
| Ionic equation | Shows only the particles that change; spectator ions are left out |
| Spectator ion | An ion present unchanged on both sides |
| Mole ratio | The ratio of the stoichiometric numbers in the balanced equation |
| Theoretical yield | Maximum mass from the equation, assuming complete reaction |
| Percentage yield | (actual mass ÷ theoretical mass) × 100 |
| Atom economy | (Mᵣ of desired product ÷ sum of Mᵣ of all reactants) × 100 |
| Concordant titres | Titres within 0.10 cm³ of each other |
| Water of crystallisation | Water molecules held in a fixed ratio in a crystal |

## Balancing method

1. Write correct formulae. Never change a subscript.
2. Count each element on both sides.
3. Add big numbers in front of formulas, finishing with the element that appears in most places.
4. Check atoms again. For an ionic equation, check **charge** too.

**Ionic equation steps.** Split soluble ionic compounds into ions. Keep solids, liquids, gases and weak electrolytes whole. Cancel spectators. Keep state symbols.

Example: Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2KI(aq) → PbI₂(s) + 2KNO₃(aq) gives Pb²⁺(aq) + 2I⁻(aq) → PbI₂(s).

## The mole bridge

```
quantity A → mol A → × ratio → mol B → quantity B
mass:      n = m ÷ Mᵣ
gas:       n = pV ÷ RT      (SI units)
solution:  n = c × V        (V in dm³)
```

Gases measured at the **same** temperature and pressure react in the volume ratio of the equation. No conversion to moles is needed.

## Yield and atom economy

| Quantity | Formula | Remember |
|---|---|---|
| Percentage yield | actual ÷ theoretical × 100 | Needs an experiment; depends on losses |
| Atom economy | Mᵣ desired product ÷ Σ Mᵣ reactants × 100 | Needs only the equation; independent of yield |

- Use stoichiometric numbers: 2Fe counts as 2 × 55.8.
- A reaction with only one product has an atom economy of 100%.
- Yield is below 100% because of incomplete or reversible reactions, side reactions and losses in transfer, filtering and purification.
- Yield above 100% means the product is impure or wet.

### Why high atom economy is an advantage

- **Economic**: less waste to separate and dispose of, and more of the purchased feedstock becomes saleable product.
- **Environmental**: fewer resources used and less waste released.
- **Ethical**: conserves finite resources and reduces pollution for communities and future generations.

## Titration reminders

- Use a pipette for a fixed volume and a burette for the variable volume.
- Rinse with the solution going in, not water, except for the conical flask.
- Take the mean of **concordant** titres only. Leave out outliers.
- Titre uncertainty: two readings of ±0.05 cm³ give ±0.10 cm³; percentage uncertainty = 0.10 ÷ titre × 100.
- Check the ratio. H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH needs ×2; Na₂CO₃ + 2HCl needs ×2.

## Water of crystallisation

Heat to **constant mass**. m(water) = hydrated mass − anhydrous mass. x = n(H₂O) ÷ n(anhydrous salt), rounded to a whole number. A mass that is still falling means water remains and x will be calculated too small.

## Worked reminders

**Gas volume from a mass.** 0.500 g of CaCO₃ (Mᵣ 100.1) with excess acid at 298 K and 100 kPa.
n(CO₂) = 0.500 ÷ 100.1 = 4.995 × 10⁻³ mol.
V = 4.995 × 10⁻³ × 8.31 × 298 ÷ 1.00 × 10⁵ = 1.237 × 10⁻⁴ m³ = **124 cm³**.

**Titration.** 25.0 cm³ of Na₂CO₃ needs 22.50 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ HCl.
n(HCl) = 2.250 × 10⁻³ mol; n(Na₂CO₃) = 1.125 × 10⁻³ mol (ratio 1 : 2).
c = 1.125 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.0250 = **0.0450 mol dm⁻³**.

**Atom economy.** Making ammonia: N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃.
Atom economy = 34.0 ÷ (28.0 + 6.0) × 100 = **100%**.

## Common mistakes

- Altering a subscript to balance an equation.
- Using masses instead of moles to compare substances.
- Forgetting ÷ 1000 for cm³ in n = cV.
- Using the wrong substance's coefficient in the ratio.
- An ionic equation that balances for atoms but not for charge.
- Mixing up yield (experimental) and atom economy (from the equation).
- Quoting "human error" as a reason for a low yield.

## Self-test

1. Balance: Fe + Cl₂ → FeCl₃.
2. Write the ionic equation for silver nitrate solution mixed with sodium chloride solution.
3. Calculate the volume of CO₂, in cm³, from 2.00 g of CaCO₃ (Mᵣ 100.1) with excess acid at 298 K and 100 kPa.
4. Calculate the atom economy for making CaO from CaCO₃ (CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂), where CaO is the desired product (Mᵣ CaO = 56.1).
5. The theoretical yield is 8.40 g and 6.72 g is obtained. Calculate the percentage yield.
6. 25.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ Na₂CO₃ is neutralised by 0.200 mol dm⁻³ HCl. Calculate the volume of acid.
7. 2.50 g of hydrated copper(II) sulfate, CuSO₄·xH₂O, gives 1.60 g of anhydrous solid on heating. Find x. (Mᵣ CuSO₄ = 159.6)
8. 50.0 cm³ of methane burns in excess oxygen, CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O(l). Give the volume of oxygen used and carbon dioxide formed, at the same conditions.
9. Suggest why a measured percentage yield could be above 100%.
10. Define percentage atom economy.
11. Give two reasons why a high atom economy is an advantage.
12. State why a reaction can have a high yield and a low atom economy.

### Answers

1. 2Fe + 3Cl₂ → 2FeCl₃.
2. Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s).
3. n = 2.00 ÷ 100.1 = 0.01998 mol; V = 0.01998 × 8.31 × 298 ÷ 1.00 × 10⁵ = 4.95 × 10⁻⁴ m³ = **495 cm³**.
4. 56.1 ÷ 100.1 × 100 = **56.0%**.
5. 6.72 ÷ 8.40 × 100 = **80.0%**.
6. n(Na₂CO₃) = 2.50 × 10⁻³ mol; n(HCl) = 5.00 × 10⁻³ mol; V = 5.00 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.200 = 0.0250 dm³ = **25.0 cm³**.
7. n(CuSO₄) = 1.60 ÷ 159.6 = 0.01003 mol; n(H₂O) = 0.90 ÷ 18.0 = 0.0500 mol; ratio 4.99, so **x = 5**.
8. Oxygen **100 cm³**, carbon dioxide **50.0 cm³**.
9. The product is impure, or it is still wet with solvent or water.
10. (Mᵣ of the desired product ÷ sum of the Mᵣ of all reactants) × 100.
11. Any two of: less waste, lower disposal cost, fewer raw materials used, less pollution, conserves finite resources.
12. Most of the reactant mass can end up in an unwanted by-product even if the reaction itself goes almost to completion.
