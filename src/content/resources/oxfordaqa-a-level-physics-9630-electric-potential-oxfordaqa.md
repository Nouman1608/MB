## Potential and potential difference

**Absolute electric potential** at a point is the work done per unit positive charge in bringing a small test charge from infinity to that point. Choosing infinity as zero makes sense because the force from any charge falls to nothing there.

**Potential difference** between two points is the work done per unit charge in moving a positive charge from one point to the other. It depends only on the two end points, not on the route.

Potential is a property of a point in the field. Potential energy belongs to a charge placed at that point and equals QV. The same point has one potential, but a +2 nC and a −2 nC charge placed there have potential energies of opposite sign.

Sign rules, with r measured from the centre of the source charge:

- Near a positive charge V is positive and falls towards zero with distance. A positive test charge must be pushed in, so work is done on it.
- Near a negative charge V is negative and rises towards zero with distance. A positive test charge is pulled in, so the field does the work.
- A positive charge released from rest moves towards lower potential; a negative charge moves towards higher potential. In both cases the potential energy falls and the kinetic energy rises.

## ΔW = QΔV in a radial field

### Worked example 1: moving a charge closer to a sphere

A sphere carries +50 nC. How much work must be done to move a +2.0 nC charge from 0.40 m to 0.10 m from its centre?

V at 0.40 m = 8.99 × 10⁹ × 50 × 10⁻⁹ / 0.40 = 1124 V.

V at 0.10 m = 8.99 × 10⁹ × 50 × 10⁻⁹ / 0.10 = 4495 V.

ΔV = 3371 V, so ΔW = QΔV = 2.0 × 10⁻⁹ × 3371 = **6.7 × 10⁻⁶ J**.

The force on the small charge changes all the way in, so you could not find this as force × distance with one force value. Potential handles the changing force for you.

### Worked example 2: distance of closest approach

An alpha particle (charge +2e) with kinetic energy 5.0 MeV is fired straight at a gold nucleus (charge +79e). Estimate how close it gets.

The alpha particle slows as it climbs to higher potential. At closest approach all its kinetic energy has become electric potential energy:

Ek = (2e) × V, where V = (79e) / (4πε₀r)

Ek = 5.0 × 10⁶ × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 8.0 × 10⁻¹³ J.

r = 8.99 × 10⁹ × 2 × 79 × (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹)² / 8.0 × 10⁻¹³ = **4.5 × 10⁻¹⁴ m**.

The nucleus is treated as a point charge that does not recoil. The answer is an upper limit on the nuclear radius: the alpha particle has not touched the nucleus.

### Accelerating an electron

An electron accelerated from rest through 2.0 kV gains eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 2000 = 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁶ J, so v = √(2eV/m) = **2.7 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹**. Only the p.d. matters, not the shape of the field between the electrodes.

## Several charges: add potentials as numbers

Potential is a scalar. To find V at a point near several charges, calculate Q/(4πε₀r) for each, keeping the sign of each charge, and add.

### Worked example 3: centre of a square

Charges of +2.0 nC, +2.0 nC, −1.0 nC and +3.0 nC sit at the corners of a square of side 0.10 m. Find the potential at the centre and the work needed to bring a +1.0 nC charge there from infinity.

Every corner is 0.10/√2 = 0.0707 m from the centre, so the potentials add to:

V = 8.99 × 10⁹ × (2.0 + 2.0 − 1.0 + 3.0) × 10⁻⁹ / 0.0707 = **763 V**.

Work from infinity = QV = 1.0 × 10⁻⁹ × 763 = **7.6 × 10⁻⁷ J**.

Field strength at the same point would need four vectors resolved and added. That contrast, scalar against vector, is a favourite short question.

## Equipotential surfaces

An equipotential surface joins points of equal potential. Moving a charge along it involves ΔV = 0, so ΔW = QΔV = 0. The field therefore has no component along the surface: field lines cross equipotentials at right angles.

### Worked example 4: spacing round a point charge

Find the radii of the equipotentials at 600 V, 450 V, 300 V and 150 V around a +20 nC charge.

r = Q / (4πε₀V), with Q/(4πε₀) = 8.99 × 10⁹ × 20 × 10⁻⁹ = 179.8 V m.

| V / V | 600 | 450 | 300 | 150 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| r / m | 0.30 | 0.40 | 0.60 | 1.20 |

Equal steps of 150 V give gaps of 0.10 m, 0.20 m and 0.60 m. The surfaces spread out with distance because the field weakens. Between parallel plates, where the field is uniform, equal steps of potential give equally spaced planes.

## Graphs of E and V against r

For a positive point charge, both graphs fall towards zero, but V ∝ 1/r and E ∝ 1/r², so the E curve falls more steeply.

**Gradient of the V–r graph.** E = ΔV/Δr in magnitude. The field points in the direction in which V falls, so a downhill V–r graph means a field pointing outwards. For a curve, draw a tangent and find its gradient.

For the +20 nC charge at r = 0.30 m, E = 179.8 / 0.30² = 2.0 × 10³ V m⁻¹. A tangent drawn at 0.30 m on the V–r graph should have a gradient of about −2.0 × 10³ V m⁻¹, a useful check on your tangent.

**Area under the E–r graph.** Between two radii the area equals ΔV. For the same charge between 0.30 m and 0.60 m the area is 600 − 300 = 300 V. On a printed graph, count squares or split the area into trapezia, and multiply by the value of one square (V m⁻¹ × m = V).

## Electric and gravitational potential compared

| Feature | Electric | Gravitational |
|---|---|---|
| Zero | At infinity | At infinity |
| Sign | Positive or negative, depending on the source charge | Always negative |
| Radial equation | V = Q/(4πε₀r) | V = −GM/r |
| Work | ΔW = QΔV | ΔW = mΔV |

## Common mistakes

- Writing V = Q/(4πε₀r²): potential has r, not r².
- Adding potentials as vectors, or dropping the sign of a negative charge.
- Confusing potential (J C⁻¹) with potential energy (J).
- Treating equipotentials around a point charge as equally spaced.
- Quoting the gradient of a chord when a tangent is needed for E at a point.
- In closest-approach problems, using the charge of the alpha particle in V instead of the charge of the nucleus.

## Exam technique

- **Define** absolute potential: work done per unit positive charge, bringing a small test charge from infinity to the point.
- **Explain** why no work is done along an equipotential: ΔV = 0, so QΔV = 0; the force is at right angles to the motion.
- **Sketch** E and V graphs on the same r axis: label which is which, start both outside the charge, and make E fall faster.
- **Calculate** from a graph: show the tangent or the counted area, with units of one square.
