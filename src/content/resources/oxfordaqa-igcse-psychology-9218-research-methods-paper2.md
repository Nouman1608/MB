**3.2.4 Research methods** is Section D of Paper 2 (Social Context and Behaviour) in OxfordAQA
International GCSE Psychology (9218), Version 1.1. The specification states that its content is the
same as 3.1.4 in Paper 1, including data handling. This guide concentrates on the **non-experimental
methods** and **data handling**, using Paper 2 topics (communication, social influence and mental
health) as examples. For hypotheses, experimental designs, controls and sampling, use the
[Research Methods (Paper 1) study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-psychology-9218-research-methods/).

## What this guide covers from the specification

- **Interviews**, **questionnaires**, **case studies** and **observation studies** (including
  **categories of behaviour** and **inter-observer reliability**), with strengths, weaknesses and
  suitable uses
- **Correlation** and **scatter diagrams**, with strengths and weaknesses (no calculation of
  formulae)
- Planning research with attention to the reliability and validity of **quantitative and qualitative
  data**
- Ethical issues and ways of dealing with them, applied to sensitive topics
- **Data handling:** quantitative and qualitative data; primary and secondary data; decimals, ratios,
  fractions, percentages, estimation, significant figures; **mean, median, mode and range**;
  frequency tables, **bar charts, histograms** and scatter diagrams; the **normal distribution**

## Self-report: interviews and questionnaires

Self-report methods ask people directly about their thoughts, feelings and behaviour.

**Questionnaires** are written sets of questions. **Closed questions** have fixed answers (yes/no,
tick boxes, rating scales) and give **quantitative** data that is easy to compare. **Open questions**
let people answer in their own words and give **qualitative** data with more detail.

**Interviews** are spoken. A **structured interview** uses the same questions in the same order for
everyone, so it is easy to repeat and compare. An **unstructured interview** is more like a
conversation, and the interviewer can follow up interesting answers.

| | Strength | Weakness | Paper 2 example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Questionnaire** | Quick to collect data from many people; anonymous answers may be more honest | Respondents may give socially desirable answers; closed questions limit detail | Asking students how often they feel isolated, as a challenge of modern living |
| **Interview** | Rich detail; misunderstandings can be clarified | Time-consuming; interviewer's manner may influence answers | Asking people with OCD what their obsessions feel like |

**Social desirability bias** is answering in a way that makes you look good. It threatens validity in
both methods, especially on topics such as helping behaviour or mental health.

## Case studies

A **case study** is an in-depth investigation of one person, or a small group, often using several
methods such as interviews, observation and records. It is suitable for **rare or unusual cases**
that could not be created for ethical reasons, such as a person who develops PTSD after a rare
disaster.

- **Strengths:** very detailed, often qualitative, data; can reveal things that challenge existing
  theories.
- **Weaknesses:** results may not **generalise** to others; the researcher may become too involved
  and interpret data subjectively; the case often relies on memories of the past, which may be
  inaccurate.

## Observation studies

The researcher watches and records behaviour. To make an observation systematic, behaviour is broken
into **categories of behaviour**: clear, observable actions that can be tallied.

**Example: observing personal space in a library.** Categories might be: *sits at an empty table*,
*sits next to a stranger*, *sits one seat away from a stranger*, *moves after a stranger sits down*.
Each category describes something an observer can see, not something they have to guess, such as
"feels uncomfortable".

**Inter-observer reliability** means checking that two or more observers record the same behaviour
in the same way. They observe the same people at the same time, record independently using the same
categories, then compare their tallies. A high level of agreement means the observation is
**reliable**. If agreement is low, the categories need to be made clearer and the observers trained.

- **Strengths:** records what people actually do rather than what they say; behaviour in a natural
  setting is true to life.
- **Weaknesses:** if people know they are being watched they may change their behaviour; observers
  may be biased; the reasons behind behaviour cannot be seen.

## Correlation

A **correlation** is an association between two **co-variables**, each measured for every
participant. It is shown on a **scatter diagram**, with one variable on each axis and one point per
person.

| Type | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Positive** | As one rises, the other rises | Hours spent on social media and a loneliness score |
| **Negative** | As one rises, the other falls | Number of close friends and an anxiety score |
| **No correlation** | No pattern | Shoe size and conformity score |

**Strengths:** shows whether a relationship exists and how strong it is; allows study of variables
that could not be manipulated ethically, such as stress and mental health. **Weaknesses:** it **cannot
show cause and effect**; a **third variable** may explain both co-variables (for example, a stressful
job might increase both social media use and loneliness).

## Reliability and validity of data

- **Quantitative data** (numbers) is easy to analyse and compare, and tends to be more **reliable**,
  but can lack the depth needed to be fully **valid** about feelings and experiences.
- **Qualitative data** (words, descriptions) is detailed and often more **valid**, but it is harder
  to analyse and compare, and interpretation can be subjective, which reduces **reliability**.

**Primary data** is collected by the researcher for the current study; it fits the aim exactly but
takes time. **Secondary data** was collected by someone else, such as government statistics on
mental health diagnoses; it is quick to use but may not match the researcher's aim, and its quality
is harder to check.

## Ethics with sensitive Paper 2 topics

Mental health and social influence research raise particular risks. **Protection from harm** may
mean avoiding questions that bring back traumatic memories and offering contact details for support.
**Confidentiality** is vital when collecting information about diagnoses. **Deception**, common in
conformity and bystander research, must be justified and followed by a full **debrief**.

## Data handling

**Measures of central tendency and dispersion.** Ten participants rated how anxious they felt
speaking in public (1-10): 3, 5, 5, 6, 6, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10.

```
Mean   = sum ÷ number of scores = 65 ÷ 10 = 6.5
Median = middle value of ordered scores
         (10 scores, so average the 5th and 6th) = (6 + 6) ÷ 2 = 6
Mode   = most frequent score = 6
Range  = highest − lowest = 10 − 3 = 7
```

The **mean** uses every score but is distorted by extreme scores; the **median** is not affected by
extremes; the **mode** suits categories; the **range** shows spread but depends only on the two most
extreme scores.

**Percentages, ratios and fractions.** If 18 of 24 bystanders helped: fraction = 18/24 = 3/4;
percentage = 75%; ratio of helpers to non-helpers = 18:6 = 3:1. **Significant figures:** a mean of
6.4583 is 6.5 to two significant figures. **Estimating** a result first (18 out of 24 is a bit less
than 80%) helps you check a calculation.

**Choosing a graph.**

| Graph | Use it for | Key feature |
|---|---|---|
| **Frequency table** | Tallying how often each score or category occurs | Columns for score and frequency |
| **Bar chart** | Separate categories, such as the mean of each condition | Gaps between bars |
| **Histogram** | Continuous data in intervals, such as reaction times | Bars touch; no gaps |
| **Scatter diagram** | Correlations between two co-variables | One point per participant |

**The normal distribution** is a symmetrical, bell-shaped curve. Most scores cluster around the
middle and fewer occur at the extremes, and the **mean, median and mode are all at the centre**. Many
human characteristics, such as height, are roughly normally distributed.

## Common mistakes

- Concluding that one co-variable causes the other.
- Writing vague behaviour categories ("being friendly") that two observers could interpret
  differently.
- Drawing a histogram with gaps, or a bar chart with no gaps.
- Calculating the median without putting the scores in order first.
- Saying qualitative data is always better or always worse; each has different strengths.
