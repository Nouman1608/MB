This guide goes deeper into sub-topic 8.2 of Unit 1, Changing population, in IB Diploma Programme Geography (first assessments 2019): changing populations and places. It is part of the core studied by every SL and HL student and examined in Paper 2. The [8.2 revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-changing-populations-and-places-revision-notes/) already define the main demographic indicators, explain how to read pyramids and their shapes, distinguish voluntary and forced migration, and outline Lee's model and the Syrian displacement. This page adds what those notes leave short: how to calculate migration rates and combine them with natural change, why populations keep growing after fertility falls, what patterns migration tends to follow, a second forced-migration case, and how population change transforms the character of places. Test yourself with the [8.2 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-dp-geography-ib-dp-geography-8-2-practice/); the [Population distribution study guide](/resources/ib-dp-geography-population-distribution/) gives the wider picture of the unit.

## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Explain how population changes through natural change and migration.
- Interpret population structure and how it changes over time.
- Explain the causes and consequences of voluntary and forced migration.
- Explain how population change affects places, including source and destination areas.

## Rates, not just counts

Natural change and migration are easiest to compare when both are expressed per 1,000 people.

- **Rate of natural change** = crude birth rate - crude death rate.
- **Net migration rate** = (immigrants - emigrants) / population x 1,000.
- **Growth rate** (per 1,000) = rate of natural change + net migration rate.

### Worked example 1: two invented regions

| | Region A | Region B |
|---|---|---|
| Population | 2,000,000 | 500,000 |
| Crude birth rate (per 1,000) | 9 | 22 |
| Crude death rate (per 1,000) | 12 | 7 |
| Immigrants in the year | 30,000 | 2,000 |
| Emigrants in the year | 10,000 | 9,500 |

- Region A: natural change = 9 - 12 = -3 per 1,000. Net migration = (30,000 - 10,000) / 2,000,000 x 1,000 = +10 per 1,000. Growth = -3 + 10 = **+7 per 1,000** (0.7% a year).
- Region B: natural change = 22 - 7 = +15 per 1,000. Net migration = (2,000 - 9,500) / 500,000 x 1,000 = -15 per 1,000. Growth = 15 - 15 = **0 per 1,000**.

Region A grows despite having more deaths than births, because of immigration. Region B has a youthful population with high natural increase, but loses as many people through emigration. Two places can have the same headline growth for completely different reasons, with very different consequences: an ageing population sustained by migrants in A, and a young population losing workers in B.

## Demographic momentum

A population can keep growing for decades after fertility falls to replacement level. The reason is age structure: a youthful population has many children who have not yet reached child-bearing age.

### Worked example 2: momentum in action

An invented country has 24 million women aged 15-44 and 20 million girls aged 0-14. Assume, for simplicity, that each cohort survives and fertility is now at replacement.

- In 15 years, today's 0-14 girls enter the 15-44 group, while today's 30-44 women leave it. If 9 million women are now aged 30-44, the 15-44 group becomes 24 - 9 + 20 = **35 million**.
- At the same birth rate per woman, annual births rise by a factor of 35 / 24 = **1.46**, even though each woman is having fewer children than her mother did.

Momentum explains why many countries in later stages of demographic transition still have growing populations, and why population projections for youthful countries rise even under falling fertility. Ageing countries face the reverse, **negative momentum**: small young cohorts mean fewer future parents, so decline continues even if fertility recovers.

## Patterns in migration

### Ravenstein's laws

E. G. Ravenstein, writing in the 1880s from British census data, proposed general patterns in migration, including:

- most migrants travel short distances;
- migration often proceeds in steps, from village to town to city;
- long-distance migrants tend to move to large centres of commerce and industry;
- each migration stream produces a smaller counter-stream;
- economic motives are the main cause of movement.

Many still hold, especially distance decay and step migration, but forced migration, cheap long-distance transport and international labour recruitment break several of them.

### Zelinsky's mobility transition

Wilbur Zelinsky (1971) linked migration to the demographic transition. As a society develops, the dominant type of movement changes:

1. **Pre-modern:** little migration; local circulation only.
2. **Early transitional:** heavy rural-urban migration and emigration, as death rates fall and rural populations grow.
3. **Late transitional:** rural-urban migration slows; circulation such as commuting grows.
4. **Advanced:** movement mainly between and within cities; immigration of workers from less developed countries.
5. **Super-advanced:** communication technology may replace some movement.

The model helps explain why middle-income countries often have strong internal migration to megacities, while high-income countries attract international labour migrants. Like the demographic transition model, it generalises from a few countries' histories.

## A second forced-migration case: the Rohingya

In August 2017, violence against the Rohingya, a largely Muslim minority in Rakhine State in Myanmar, led more than 700,000 people to flee across the border into the Cox's Bazar district of Bangladesh. Kutupalong and the surrounding camps became one of the largest refugee settlements in the world.

Consequences for places:

- **In the host area:** rapid clearance of forested hills for shelters and fuel, raising landslide risk in the monsoon; pressure on water and sanitation; competition with local workers for jobs; dependence on international aid.
- **In the source area:** depopulation of villages and the loss of a community's land and livelihoods.
- **For the displaced:** many remain stateless, with limited rights to work, move or study, so the situation becomes protracted.

The case contrasts with voluntary migration: the movement was sudden, the destination was chosen by proximity rather than opportunity, and return depends on political change.

## How population change reshapes places

Population change alters the character of places, not just their size.

- **Rural depopulation** removes young adults, so schools and shops close, services become harder to justify, and the remaining population ages faster.
- **Retirement migration** to coasts and attractive countryside raises the median age of destination areas and increases demand for health care.
- **Immigrant neighbourhoods** in cities develop businesses, places of worship and cultural institutions that change the identity of districts, and can become entry points for later migrants (chain migration).
- **Sex ratios** shift where migration is selective, for example male-dominated labour migration leaves source villages with more women and older people.

When analysing a place, link the demographic change to these consequences explicitly: who left or arrived, and what that changes.

## Annotated model answer

*Examine the consequences of migration for source and destination places.* **(sample paragraph)**

"In source areas, the departure of young adults leaves an older population and fewer workers, so farms may be abandoned and services such as schools close **[demographic change linked to place]**. However, remittances can raise household incomes and fund housing and education, partly offsetting the loss **[counter-effect]**. In destination areas, migrants add to the workforce and can slow ageing, but sudden arrivals, as in Cox's Bazar after 2017, can overwhelm water, sanitation and land **[contrasting destination outcomes]**. The consequences therefore depend on whether migration is voluntary and gradual or forced and sudden, and on the capacity of each place to adapt **[judgement]**."

## Common mistakes

- Comparing migration counts between places of very different size instead of rates.
- Assuming population stops growing as soon as fertility reaches replacement.
- Treating Ravenstein or Zelinsky as laws that every country follows.
- Describing a forced-migration case without its consequences for places.
- Discussing population change only in numbers, not in its effects on the character of places.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate** rates per 1,000 before comparing places.
- **Explain** population change by separating natural change and migration.
- **Examine** consequences for both source and destination, and for different groups.
- In data-response parts, quote figures with units, then interpret what they mean for the place.
