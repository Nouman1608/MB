## Questions

**1.** State two reasons why a business should identify and satisfy customer needs. **[2]**

**2.** Plumtrey Fitness runs gyms in two towns. Explain how it could use segmentation by age and by income to target customers. **[4]**

**3.** Trebarwith Toys is a small family toy shop deciding whether to stock a new range of wooden puzzles.

**(a)** Identify one primary and one secondary method of market research it could use. **[2]**
**(b)** Distinguish between qualitative and quantitative research. **[2]**
**(c)** Recommend one method of research for Trebarwith Toys and justify your choice. **[4]**

**4.** Netherwick Sports sells running kit. The running kit market was worth £8.0 million in 2023 and £8.8 million in 2024. Netherwick's sales revenue was £0.72 million in 2023 and £0.88 million in 2024.

**(a)** Calculate the growth in the market between 2023 and 2024, as a percentage. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate Netherwick's market share in 2024. **[2]**
**(c)** Netherwick's market share in 2023 was 9%. Explain what the change in share suggests about its marketing. **[2]**

**5.** Plumtrey Planners makes diaries. Variable cost is £7.50 a diary and fixed costs are £90,000 a year. It expects to sell 30,000 diaries and uses cost-plus pricing with a 30% mark-up.

**(a)** Calculate the unit cost. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the selling price. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain one drawback of cost-plus pricing. **[2]**

**6.** Analyse why price penetration might be a suitable pricing method for a new brand of bottled iced tea entering a market with many well-known rivals. **[5]**

**7.** A small bakery sells a standard loaf for £2.40 as a loss leader. The loaf costs £3.00 to make. On one Saturday 5,000 loaves are sold.

**(a)** Calculate the total loss on the loaf that day. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the bakery might still choose to do this. **[2]**

**8.** Plumtrey Planners sells a "Focus" planner. Its sales (thousands of planners) over six years were:

| Year | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sales | 12 | 31 | 52 | 53 | 41 | 29 |

**(a)** Identify the stage of the product life cycle reached in year 6. **[1]**
**(b)** Evaluate whether Plumtrey Planners should use a price reduction as an extension strategy for the Focus planner. **[6]**

**9.** Trebarwith Toys sells four product lines.

| Line | Market share | Market growth |
|---|---|---|
| Wooden trains | High | Low |
| Craft kits | Low | High |
| Plush animals | High | High |
| Board games | Low | Low |

**(a)** Using the Boston Matrix, name the category of each line. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain what Trebarwith Toys might do with the board games line. **[2]**

**10.** Netherwick Sports sells running shoes online for £15 a pair. A coupon gives 20% off. The shoes cost Netherwick Sports £9 to supply.

**(a)** Calculate the price paid and the profit per pair when a coupon is used. **[2]**
**(b)** Netherwick has a small budget and aims at 16 to 25 year olds. Analyse which promotion methods suit it. **[5]**

**11.** Netherwick Sports made £560,000 of sales last year, of which £140,000 were online.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of sales made online. **[2]**
**(b)** State two channels of distribution it could use besides its own website. **[2]**
**(c)** Analyse one benefit and one drawback of using m-commerce to reach international customers. **[5]**

**12.** Plumtrey Planners plans to launch a smart notebook that costs £14 to make. A rival product sells for £22. The marketing team compares two pricing methods.

| Method | Price | Forecast first-year sales |
|---|---|---|
| Price skimming | £30 | 4,000 |
| Price penetration | £19 | 9,000 |

**(a)** Calculate the total contribution (price minus the £14 cost, multiplied by sales) for each method. **[4]**
**(b)** Evaluate which pricing method Plumtrey Planners should choose and justify your recommendation. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two: it helps the business provide a product customers will buy [1]; increase sales [1]; select the correct marketing mix; avoid costly mistakes; stay competitive. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* For "state", short points are enough, but two phrasings of the same idea are unlikely to count as two reasons.

**2.** Age: it could offer off-peak, low-cost memberships and social classes to under-25s, and daytime classes to older customers [1], and advertise each where those groups look [1]. Income: it could offer a premium gym with a pool and personal trainers at a high price to high-income customers [1] and a no-frills budget gym to lower-income customers, so the price fits what each group can afford [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Treat both named types separately and say what the business does differently for each group.

**3. (a)** Primary: for example a questionnaire given to customers in the shop [1]. Secondary: for example internet research on toy sales trends [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Quantitative research produces numerical data that can be counted [1]. Qualitative research gives opinions, feelings and reasons [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** A short questionnaire at the till [1] is cheap and quick for a small shop [1], and it reaches the people who already buy from Trebarwith [1], so the owner gets direct evidence of demand for puzzles. It only reaches existing customers, a drawback [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* When asked to recommend and justify, a method named without a reason about this business usually earns little. Point to the size, budget or customers of the shop.

**4. (a)** Growth = (8.8 − 8.0) ÷ 8.0 × 100 [1] = **10%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Market share = 0.88 ÷ 8.8 × 100 [1] = **10%** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Share rose from 9% to 10% [1], so Netherwick grew faster than the market, suggesting its marketing mix is attracting customers from rivals [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the formula before the numbers.

**5. (a)** Fixed cost per diary = 90,000 ÷ 30,000 = £3.00 [1]. Unit cost = 7.50 + 3.00 = **£10.50** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Mark-up = 30% × 10.50 = £3.15 [1]. Price = 10.50 + 3.15 = **£13.65** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** The method ignores what customers are willing to pay [1], so the price might be too high and demand fall, or too low and profit is lost [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Part (b) builds on part (a), so keep the unit cost visible. Cost-plus drawbacks are developed when they say what could happen to sales or profit.

**6.** Penetration means setting a low price at launch [1]. A new brand with no reputation needs to gain customers from well-known rivals [1], and a lower price gives buyers a reason to try it [1]. If many buy it, the brand wins a large market share quickly [1]. The risk is a low profit per unit at first, worth accepting if sales grow [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Analysis chains a feature of the method to a feature of the business. A bare definition gives little credit on "analyse".

**7. (a)** Loss per loaf = 3.00 − 2.40 = £0.60 [1]. Total = 0.60 × 5,000 = **£3,000** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The cheap loaf draws customers into the shop [1], and they are likely to buy other products at normal prices, which may make up the loss [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The "why" answer must say customers buy other things. Saying that low prices are popular does not explain why the business accepts the loss.

**8. (a)** **Decline** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** For: a lower price may attract price-sensitive buyers and raise sales, since demand is likely to rise as price falls [1]. It is quick to put in place and cheaper than redesigning the product [1]. Against: the profit per planner falls, and sales may not rise enough to make up for it [1]. Rivals can copy the price cut [1]. Sales have fallen from 53 to 29 thousand over two years, so a cut alone may not reverse a decline in interest, and adding features or changing target market might work better [1]. Judgement: a price reduction is a short-term boost that is worth using only if costs are low and combined with another strategy [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers on "evaluate" gain credit when they weigh both sides using the figures and finish with a reasoned judgement. A one-sided list tends to cap the marks.

**9. (a)** Wooden trains: cash cow [1]. Craft kits: question mark [1]. Plush animals: star [1]. Board games: dog [1]. **[4]**
**(b)** The line has a low share in a slow-growing market [1], so Trebarwith might stop selling it and use the shelf space and money on the stars or craft kits [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Classify by reading both columns, share first and growth second. High share with low growth is the pairing most often swapped.

**10. (a)** Price = 15 × 0.8 = **£12** [1]. Profit = 12 − 9 = **£3** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Social media suits the 16 to 25 age group, who use it daily, and it costs little [1], so it fits a small budget [1]. Online coupons could encourage trial [1]. Television is too costly for a small budget [1] and not as well targeted [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Analysis of promotion needs the factor (finance, target market) linked to the method. A list of methods without a suitability reason usually earns little.

**11. (a)** 140,000 ÷ 560,000 × 100 [1] = **25%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Any two: retailers [1]; wholesalers [1]; telesales. **[2]**
**(c)** Benefit: a mobile app or site reaches customers abroad without opening shops [1], so the market is larger [1]. Drawback: delivery costs and delays rise with distance [1], and returns are harder to handle [1], which may put off buyers or reduce profit [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Take the benefit and the drawback in turn, and keep both about m-commerce and international customers, not shops in general.

**12. (a)** Skimming: (30 − 14) × 4,000 = 16 × 4,000 = **£64,000** [2]. Penetration: (19 − 14) × 9,000 = 5 × 9,000 = **£45,000** [2]. **[4]**
**(b)** Skimming gives £19,000 more contribution (64,000 − 45,000) in year one [1] and suits a smart product with a USP that early buyers value [1]. But £30 is well above the rival's £22, so the forecast of 4,000 may be too high if buyers see no extra value [1]. Penetration at £19 undercuts the rival, builds market share and brand image quickly [1], but the profit per unit is low and a later price rise could upset customers [1]. Judgement: choose skimming if the product's unique features are strong and tested in research; otherwise start lower and raise the price as the brand grows [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Use your part (a) figures in part (b). Stronger recommendations say when the other method would win instead.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving market share as a decimal or without a percentage sign.
- Writing the definition of a method (such as price skimming) and stopping, when the question asks for analysis.
- Using the wrong category in the Boston Matrix by reading growth before share.
- Calculating mark-up on the selling price instead of on the cost.
- Evaluation answers with no judgement at the end.
