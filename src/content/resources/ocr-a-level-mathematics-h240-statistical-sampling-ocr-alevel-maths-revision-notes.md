## What is in scope

| Ref | Skill |
|---|---|
| 2.01a | Use the terms population and sample |
| 2.01b | Use samples to make informal inferences about the population |
| 2.01c | Use sampling techniques, including simple random and opportunity sampling. For random samples, assume sampling without replacement from a large population unless told otherwise |
| 2.01d | Select or critique techniques in context; different samples can lead to different conclusions. Systematic, stratified, cluster and quota: critique, not carry out |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Population | Every member of the group of interest |
| Sample | The members actually observed |
| Census | Observing every member of the population |
| Sampling unit | A single member of the population |
| Sampling frame | List of the population from which the sample is drawn |
| Statistic | A value calculated from a sample, used to estimate the population value |
| Sampling variability | Natural differences between samples caused by chance |
| Bias | A systematic tendency of a method to over- or under-estimate |

## Sample or census?

| Census | Sample |
|---|---|
| True value, no sampling variability | Estimate only |
| Expensive, slow | Cheaper, quicker |
| Impossible if testing destroys items | Leaves stock to sell |
| Hard for large or unlisted populations | Practical |

## Bias versus variability

- **Variability:** estimates scatter by chance; **larger samples reduce it**.
- **Bias:** estimates consistently off in one direction; **only a better method removes it**.

Model sentence: "A larger sample would make the estimate more reliable, but would not remove the bias caused by sampling only weekday shoppers."

## Informal inference in steps

1. Calculate the sample statistic (mean, proportion).
2. Scale if needed: estimated count = sample proportion × population size.
3. Write "approximately" or "about", never "exactly".
4. Note that another sample would probably give a slightly different value.

**Example.** 14 out of a random sample of 40 households own a dog; the town has 6000 households. Estimate: 14/40 × 6000 = **2100 households**, approximately.

## Simple random sampling: describe it fully

1. Sampling frame of all N members.
2. Number them 1 to N (or 001 to N).
3. Generate random numbers in the range (calculator, computer or tables).
4. Reject out-of-range numbers and repeats.
5. Stop at n different members.

Property: every possible sample of size n is equally likely, so every member is equally likely to be chosen.

## Opportunity sampling

Take whoever is available and willing. Quick, cheap, no frame; **not random**, likely biased towards people present at that time and place.

## Critique-only methods

| Method | One strength | One weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Systematic (every kth after a random start) | Easy; spread through the list | Periodic pattern in the list can bias it |
| Stratified (random from each group, in proportion) | Every group represented | Needs group information for the whole frame |
| Cluster (whole groups chosen at random) | Cheap for spread-out populations | Clusters may be unrepresentative |
| Quota (interviewer fills group numbers) | Quick, no frame | Interviewer's choice not random |

## Critique checklist

1. Random or not? Who chooses?
2. Frame complete and current?
3. Who is missed or over-represented, in this context?
4. Direction of the effect on the estimate.
5. A specific improvement.

## Choosing a method: quick rules

- Groups with different views and a labelled frame → stratified.
- Full frame, no obvious groups → simple random.
- No frame, need speed → quota or opportunity, with the bias risk stated.
- Population spread across many sites → cluster, noting the risk.

## Exam traps

- "Population: people." Name the group in context.
- Population confused with the frame.
- "Randomly picked by the interviewer" called simple random sampling.
- Repeats or out-of-range numbers not rejected.
- "A bigger sample removes bias."
- Critique with no reference to the context.
- Improvement that does not address the weakness named.
- Writing out a calculation for stratified numbers when only a critique is asked for.

## Self-test

1. Define sampling frame.
2. Give two reasons why a sample might be used instead of a census.
3. What property defines a simple random sample?
4. Why are repeated random numbers rejected?
5. A random sample of 50 of a firm's 800 employees finds 12 cycle to work. Estimate the number of cyclists.
6. Two random samples give different means. Does this show either sample is biased?
7. What effect does increasing the sample size have on sampling variability?
8. What effect does increasing the sample size have on bias?
9. Give one weakness of opportunity sampling.
10. Name the method: every 20th name on a list after a random start.
11. Why might quota sampling give a biased sample?
12. When would stratified sampling be a good choice?

### Answers

1. A list of the members of the population from which the sample is taken.
2. Any two: cheaper; quicker; testing may destroy items; population too large or hard to list.
3. Every possible sample of the chosen size is equally likely to be selected.
4. Sampling is without replacement, so no member can be chosen twice.
5. 12/50 × 800 = **192** employees, approximately.
6. No; random samples differ by chance (sampling variability).
7. It reduces it: estimates vary less between samples.
8. None: a biased method stays biased.
9. Not random, so it may over-represent people available at that time and place.
10. Systematic sampling.
11. The interviewer chooses who to ask, so the choice is not random.
12. When the population has groups that may differ and a frame records each member's group.
