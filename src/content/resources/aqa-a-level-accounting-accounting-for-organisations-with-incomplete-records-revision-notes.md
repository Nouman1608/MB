## 3.14 at a glance

- **Calculate profit** when records are too poor for an income statement: use statements of affairs.
- **Find missing figures** with control/total accounts, cash accounts, bank accounts, ratios, depreciation, profit or loss on disposal, and the financial statements themselves.
- **Evaluate** single entry against double entry record keeping.

## Key terms

- **Incomplete records:** records that do not follow the full double entry system, so no trial balance can be extracted.
- **Single entry:** each transaction recorded once, usually only in a cash book or bank statement.
- **Statement of affairs:** a list of assets and liabilities at a date, set out like a statement of financial position; the balancing figure is capital.
- **Total account:** a control account built from totals (receivables or payables) to find a missing figure such as credit sales.
- **Capital introduced:** money or assets the owner puts into the business during the year.

## Formulae to know

| Purpose | Formula |
|---|---|
| Capital | Assets - Liabilities |
| Profit from capital | Closing capital - Opening capital + Drawings - Capital introduced |
| Credit sales | Receipts from customers + Irrecoverable debts + Closing receivables - Opening receivables (adjust for any other items in the account) |
| Credit purchases | Payments to suppliers + Discounts received + Closing payables - Opening payables |
| Cost of sales | Opening inventory + Purchases - Closing inventory |
| Mark-up | Gross profit / Cost of sales x 100 |
| Gross profit margin | Gross profit / Revenue x 100 |
| Rate of inventory turnover | Cost of sales / Average inventory |
| Profit in relation to revenue | Profit for the year / Revenue x 100 |
| Expense for the year | Paid + Closing accrual - Opening accrual - Closing prepayment + Opening prepayment |

The four ratio formulae are listed in the specification's appendix of accounting ratios, which states that you must know them.

## Method in steps

### Profit by the capital method

```
1. Opening statement of affairs -> opening capital
2. Closing statement of affairs -> closing capital
3. Change in capital = closing - opening
4. + drawings (cash, goods and any personal bills paid by the business)
5. - capital introduced
6. = profit (negative = loss)
```

### Missing figure from an account

```
1. Write the account with both sides.
2. Enter every known figure on the correct side.
3. The difference between the two sides is the missing figure.
4. Label it "(bal)" or "(missing figure)" so the examiner can follow.
```

Small reminder: payables of £3,000 at the start and £3,600 at the end, payments £30,000, discounts received £250 and a contra of £400 give credit purchases of 30,000 + 250 + 400 + 3,600 - 3,000 = £31,250. All three reductions in payables (payments, discounts, contra) sit on the debit side with the closing balance.

### Mark-up and margin

```
Build from 100 on the base:
  mark-up -> cost = 100       margin -> revenue = 100
Mark-up m%: margin = m / (100 + m)
Margin g%:  mark-up = g / (100 - g)
```

Small reminder: mark-up 50% gives revenue 150 on cost 100, so the margin is 50/150 = 33.33%.

### Inventory lost (fire, flood or theft)

```
Cost of sales from sales and the ratio
Expected closing inventory = opening + purchases - cost of sales
                             (- goods taken by owner, if any)
Loss = expected closing - inventory saved (at cost)
```

### Depreciation and disposals

```
Carrying amount of asset sold = cost - accumulated depreciation on it
Profit (loss) on disposal     = proceeds - carrying amount
Missing cost of new asset     = closing cost - (opening cost - cost of asset sold)
Closing accumulated depreciation
   = opening - depreciation on asset sold + charge for the year
Reducing balance, working back:
   carrying amount before charge = carrying amount after / (1 - rate)
```

Read the policy every time: is the charge on cost or carrying amount, and is there a charge in the year of purchase or disposal? A profit on disposal is added to gross profit as other income; a loss is listed with the expenses.

### Which tool finds which figure?

| Missing figure | Where to find it |
|---|---|
| Opening capital | Opening statement of affairs |
| Credit sales | Total trade receivables account |
| Credit purchases | Total trade payables account |
| Cash takings, or drawings from the till | Cash account |
| Closing bank balance, or a missing payment | Bank summary |
| Cost of sales, revenue or gross profit | Mark-up or gross profit margin |
| Opening or closing inventory | Rate of inventory turnover, or the cost of sales calculation |
| Total expenses | Profit in relation to revenue, then gross profit - profit |
| Cost of a new non-current asset | Non-current asset account at cost |
| Closing capital | Opening capital + profit - drawings (+ capital introduced) |

### Full statements from incomplete records

```
1. Opening statement of affairs   -> opening capital
2. Cash account and bank summary  -> missing takings, drawings or balance
3. Total accounts                 -> credit sales, credit purchases
4. Accruals and prepayments       -> expense for the year
5. Depreciation and disposals
6. Income statement, then statement of financial position
```

## Must-know distinctions

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| Mark-up vs margin | Mark-up is on cost | Margin is on revenue; for the same goods margin is always the smaller % |
| Statement of affairs vs statement of financial position | Built from lists and valuations | Built from ledger balances under double entry |
| Capital method vs full statements | Gives one profit figure only | Gives revenue, gross profit and each expense |
| Total account vs ledger account | Uses totals for the year | Records each transaction |
| Drawings vs capital introduced | Added back to the change in capital | Subtracted from the change in capital |

## Single entry vs double entry

- **Benefits of single entry:** cheap, quick, simple; may suit a tiny cash business with few assets.
- **Limitations of single entry:** no trial balance check; errors and theft go unnoticed; profit may be a single total; weak basis for ratio analysis, budgeting, tax returns or a loan application; the accountant must rebuild the records each year.
- **Benefits of double entry:** an arithmetic check; full statements; control over receivables, payables and inventory.
- **Limitations of double entry:** more time, skill and cost; some errors (omission, compensating errors) still pass the trial balance.

A good evaluation links the choice to the business: size, credit trading, staff, borrowing and the owner's skill.

## Quick self-test

1. Opening capital £24,000, closing capital £31,500, drawings £15,600, capital introduced £2,000. Calculate profit.
2. Mark-up is 25%. State the gross profit margin.
3. Gross profit margin is 40%. Calculate the mark-up.
4. Cost of sales £45,000; mark-up 50%. Calculate revenue.
5. Revenue £72,000; gross profit margin 30%. Calculate cost of sales.
6. Trade receivables: opening £2,500, closing £3,100; receipts £28,700; irrecoverable debts £200. Calculate credit sales.
7. Trade payables: opening £4,200, closing £3,800; payments £36,900; discounts received £300. Calculate credit purchases.
8. Cash in hand: opening £120, closing £90. Banked £18,400; wages paid in cash £5,200; drawings from the till £3,000. Calculate cash takings.
9. Cost of sales £54,000; rate of inventory turnover 9 times; opening inventory £5,400. Calculate closing inventory.
10. An asset cost £12,000 with accumulated depreciation of £7,500. It is sold for £5,000. Calculate the profit or loss on disposal.
11. Assets £18,400 and liabilities £3,900. Calculate capital.
12. Revenue £60,000; gross profit £21,000; profit in relation to revenue 15%. Calculate total expenses.

### Answers

1. 31,500 - 24,000 + 15,600 - 2,000 = **£21,100**
2. 25 / 125 = **20%**
3. 40 / 60 = **66.67%**
4. 45,000 x 150/100 = **£67,500**
5. 72,000 x 70% = **£50,400**
6. 28,700 + 200 + 3,100 - 2,500 = **£29,500**
7. 36,900 + 300 + 3,800 - 4,200 = **£36,800**
8. 18,400 + 5,200 + 3,000 + 90 - 120 = **£26,570**
9. Average = 54,000 / 9 = 6,000; closing = 12,000 - 5,400 = **£6,600**
10. Carrying amount 4,500; 5,000 - 4,500 = **£500 profit**
11. 18,400 - 3,900 = **£14,500**
12. Profit = 60,000 x 15% = 9,000; expenses = 21,000 - 9,000 = **£12,000**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving drawings out of the capital method, or adding capital introduced instead of subtracting it.
- Missing drawings in kind: goods taken by the owner, or private bills paid from the business bank account.
- Applying a mark-up % to sales, which treats it as a margin.
- Leaving out cash spent from takings before banking, so cash sales are too low.
- Using closing inventory, not average inventory, in the inventory turnover ratio.
- Posting a contra or dishonoured cheque on the wrong side of the total receivables account.
- Taking the cash paid for an expense as the expense, without the accrual and prepayment adjustments.
- Not labelling a balancing figure, so the method is hard to credit if the answer is wrong.
- Writing generic evaluation of single entry that is not linked to the business in the question.
