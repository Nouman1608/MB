Section A of Paper 4 Language Topics in Cambridge International AS & A Level English Language (9093) asks you to
discuss the role and status of English around the world. You respond to an unseen text and write an essay that
connects it to your wider study. This guide sets out the three knowledge areas in the syllabus, the theories and
studies that support each, and a method for answering the question you will actually face.

## What the question asks

Section A is one compulsory question worth 25 marks, half of Paper 4. You read about **400-500 words of text** on the
topic and write an essay discussing **the most important issues the text raises** in relation to **a specified aspect**
of the role and status of English in the world. You must refer to specific details from the text and relate them to
ideas and examples from your wider study. The question assesses **AO1** (reading and understanding), **AO2** (writing
effectively) and **AO4** (linguistic issues, concepts, methods and approaches).

Two phrases deserve attention. "Most important issues" means you select and rank; you do not summarise everything.
"A specified aspect" means the question narrows the topic, for example to education, identity or the future of English,
and your essay must stay on that aspect.

## Area 1: the historical development of English as a "global" language

The syllabus examples are colonialism, cultural influence and effects, the concentric circles model, multilingualism
and the future of English.

**Two waves of spread.** English first spread through migration and colonial settlement, which created
first-language communities in North America, Australia and New Zealand, and through colonial administration in parts
of Africa and Asia, where English became a language of government, law and education alongside local languages. In the
twentieth century, the economic and cultural influence of the United States, followed by global media, science,
business and the internet, extended English into places with no colonial link to Britain.

David Crystal, in *English as a Global Language* (1997), argues that a language becomes global because of the power of
its speakers, political, economic and cultural, not because of anything in the language itself. This is a useful
counter to claims that English spread because it is "simple" or "flexible".

**The concentric circles model.** Braj Kachru's model (1985) describes an **inner circle** where English is the main
first language, an **outer circle** where it has an institutional role after colonial history, and an **expanding
circle** where it is learned as a foreign language for international use. It is valuable because it treats outer-circle
Englishes as varieties in their own right. Common criticisms: it is based on nations rather than speakers; many
countries contain speakers of every type; and it implies the inner circle remains the centre.

**Schneider's Dynamic Model.** Edgar Schneider proposes that postcolonial Englishes pass through five phases:
**foundation**, **exonormative stabilisation** (norms imported from outside), **nativisation** (local features
develop), **endonormative stabilisation** (local norms are accepted as standard) and **differentiation** (internal
varieties emerge). It is useful for explaining why attitudes to a local variety change over time.

**Multilingualism.** Most users of English worldwide are multilingual. English typically occupies particular
**domains** (higher education, business, the internet) while other languages hold home, religion and local community.
Code-switching between English and other languages is normal, skilled behaviour, not a failure of either language.

**The future of English.** Arguments diverge. One view expects English to keep its role as the main international
lingua franca. Another, associated with David Graddol's work for the British Council, suggests that as more people use
English as an additional language, monolingual English speakers may lose their advantage, and that other languages
may grow in international importance. A third expects English to fragment into mutually less intelligible varieties,
as Latin did; critics answer that global media and communication make this less likely.

## Area 2: varieties of English

The syllabus examples are standard and non-standard "Englishes", creolisation, the sociolect continuum, and official
and unofficial attitudes and policies.

**Standard and non-standard Englishes.** Several countries now have codified standards with their own dictionaries and
usage, such as Australian or Indian English. Within each, non-standard varieties carry local identity. The plural
"Englishes" signals a pluralist view: no single variety owns the language.

**Creolisation.** When speakers of different languages need to communicate, a **pidgin** may form: a reduced contact
language with no native speakers. When children grow up speaking it as a first language, it **creolises**, expanding
its grammar and vocabulary into a full language. Tok Pisin, now an official language of Papua New Guinea, is a
well-known example of a pidgin that has creolised.

**The sociolect continuum.** In many creole-speaking communities, speech varies along a scale from the **basilect**
(furthest from the standard), through **mesolects**, to the **acrolect** (closest to the standard). Speakers move
along it according to setting, audience and purpose, which makes the continuum a model of style-shifting as well as of
social difference.

**Attitudes and policies.** Governments and institutions take positions on English and its local forms.
Singapore's Speak Good English Movement, launched in 2000, promoted standard English over Singlish, while many
Singaporeans value Singlish as a marker of identity. France's 1994 Toubon Law requires French in certain public and
commercial contexts, a policy partly aimed at limiting English. In India, English is used alongside Hindi for
official purposes of the national government. "Unofficial" attitudes matter too: employers, parents, schools and
media often rank accents and varieties, which shapes how speakers are treated.

## Area 3: ethical considerations

The syllabus examples are language shift and death, cultural imperialism, equality of opportunity and global
cooperation.

- **Language shift and death.** When a community moves to a dominant language in more and more domains, younger
  generations may stop learning the heritage language. English is one of several dominant languages involved, along
  with national languages.
- **Cultural imperialism.** Robert Phillipson's *Linguistic Imperialism* (1992) argues that the spread of English has
  been actively promoted and serves the interests of powerful nations. The Kenyan writer Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o, in
  *Decolonising the Mind* (1986), explained his decision to write in Gikuyu rather than English. Critics of
  Phillipson argue that people choose English for their own purposes and adapt it.
- **Equality of opportunity.** English can open access to education, employment and information, but where it is a
  gatekeeper, those without access to good English teaching are excluded. The same language can widen and narrow
  opportunity.
- **Global cooperation.** A shared lingua franca supports science, diplomacy and safety-critical communication; English
  is the standard language of international aviation, for example.

## Worked example: using the text

**Extract from a stimulus** (original, written for this guide):

> *In our city's best schools, children are now forbidden to speak their home language in class. Teachers say it is
> for the pupils' own good: "English is the language of the future." Yet my daughter came home last week unable to
> tell her grandmother what she had learned.*

**Specified aspect:** English and education.

**Model paragraph:**

> The writer's most significant issue is the cost of English-medium education to family and heritage languages. The
> teachers' claim that English is "the language of the future" reflects the equality-of-opportunity argument: English
> can give access to higher education and international work. But the anecdote of the daughter "unable to tell her
> grandmother" shows the mechanism of language shift, in which the heritage language loses a domain, here the
> classroom, and with it the vocabulary to discuss new knowledge. Phillipson would read the ban as evidence of English
> being imposed as superior; a pluralist position, closer to the multilingual reality most English users live in,
> would ask why the school cannot add English without subtracting the home language.

The paragraph quotes the text precisely, names the issue, applies two concepts, and evaluates rather than simply
agreeing with the writer.

## Planning the essay

1. Underline the **specified aspect**. Read the text once for its argument and once for issues relevant to that
   aspect.
2. Choose **three or four issues** and rank them. Each becomes a paragraph.
3. For each issue: quote the text, explain the issue, connect it to a theory, study, model or real example, and
   evaluate.
4. Conclude with a judgement that answers the question rather than restating points.

## Common mistakes

- Summarising the text paragraph by paragraph.
- Writing a general essay on global English that drifts from the specified aspect.
- Describing Kachru's circles without applying or evaluating them.
- Treating outer-circle or creole varieties as "broken" English.
- Presenting the spread of English as wholly positive or wholly negative.
- Inventing statistics. Use well-established examples instead.

## Exam technique

The command word is usually **discuss**: write about issues in depth in a structured way. Aim for balance and
judgement. Use the text as a springboard, never as the whole answer: each paragraph should show reading of the text
(AO1), knowledge of concepts (AO4) and controlled, cohesive writing (AO2).
