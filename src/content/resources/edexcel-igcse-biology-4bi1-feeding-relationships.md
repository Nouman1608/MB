## 4.6 Trophic levels

A **trophic level** is a feeding position in a food chain. Each organism is placed by what it eats.

| Trophic level | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Producer | Makes its own food by photosynthesis | Grass, algae |
| Primary consumer | Eats producers (a herbivore) | Grasshopper, rabbit |
| Secondary consumer | Eats primary consumers | Frog, shrew |
| Tertiary consumer | Eats secondary consumers | Snake, owl |
| Decomposer | Feeds on dead organisms and waste, returning nutrients | Bacteria, fungi |

Producers are the first trophic level because they bring energy into the living world. The energy comes from sunlight, absorbed by chlorophyll in the producer. Every other trophic level depends on producers for food.

An **omnivore** eats both plants and animals. It can sit at more than one trophic level. A **carnivore** eats animals and a **herbivore** eats plants.

## 4.7 Food chains, food webs and pyramids

### Food chains

A **food chain** shows who eats whom in a straight line. The **arrows point from the food to the feeder**, and so show the direction in which energy moves.

grass → grasshopper → frog → snake → hawk

In this chain, grass is the producer, grasshopper is the primary consumer, frog is the secondary consumer, snake is the tertiary consumer and hawk is the next consumer along (a quaternary consumer). Decomposers act on all of the organisms when they die.

### Food webs

A **food web** links many food chains. It is more realistic because most animals eat more than one thing. In a food web:

- one animal can occupy **two trophic levels**, for example an owl that eats a shrew (secondary consumer) and a mouse (primary consumer) is a secondary and a tertiary consumer;
- removing or adding one species affects others.

#### Worked example 1: predicting an effect

*In a meadow food web, grass is eaten by rabbits and by grasshoppers. Rabbits and frogs (which eat grasshoppers) are both eaten by foxes. A disease kills most of the rabbits. Predict and explain two changes.*

1. **Foxes** lose a food source, so some fox numbers fall, or they eat more frogs. More predation on frogs means fewer frogs.
2. **Grass** is eaten by fewer rabbits, so the amount of grass may rise. The grasshoppers have more food, so their numbers may rise, but fewer frogs would mean fewer grasshoppers are eaten.

Prediction questions reward a chain of reasoning. State the first effect, then the knock-on effect, and use the words "so" or "because".

### Pyramids

Pyramids show the feeding levels as stacked bars. Each bar is one trophic level, and the producers are at the bottom.

| Pyramid | What the width shows | Shape |
|---|---|---|
| Number | Number of individuals at each level | Can be an odd shape |
| Biomass | Mass of living material (usually dry mass) at each level | Usually narrows upward |
| Energy transfer | Energy passed to each level per unit area per year | Always narrows upward |

**Pyramid of numbers.** One oak tree may support thousands of caterpillars, so the bottom bar is narrow and the middle bar wide. Large producers or parasites can give an unusual shape.

**Pyramid of biomass.** Biomass is measured as **dry mass**, because the water content of living things varies. It takes account of size, so one oak tree is represented by its large mass. A snapshot biomass pyramid in a water habitat can look inverted if the producers are tiny algae that reproduce very fast.

**Pyramid of energy transfer.** This is always pyramid-shaped, because energy is lost at every transfer.

#### Worked example 2: drawing a pyramid from data

*Dry biomass in a meadow: grass 8000 kg, rabbits 800 kg, foxes 40 kg. Describe the pyramid of biomass.*

1. Producer bar (grass): widest, 8000 kg.
2. Primary consumers (rabbits): 800 kg, one tenth of the bar below.
3. Secondary consumers (foxes): 40 kg.
4. Each level is narrower than the one before, so the pyramid is a true pyramid shape.

When bars are drawn to scale, a ratio of 8000 : 800 : 40 means the bottom bar is 200 times as wide as the top.

## 4.8 Transfer of substances and energy

Two different things move along a food chain.

- **Substances** such as carbon compounds and nitrogen compounds move when one organism eats another. They are built into the body of the feeder. Decomposers return them to the soil and air, so they are **recycled**.
- **Energy** enters as sunlight, is captured by producers by photosynthesis and is passed on as chemical energy in food. It leaves each organism as heat. Energy is **not recycled**, so there must be a continuous supply from the Sun.

This difference between recycling of substances and one-way flow of energy is a very common exam point.

## 4.9 Why only about 10% of energy is transferred

On average, only about **10%** of the energy in one trophic level reaches the next. The rest is lost.

Reasons for the loss:

- Much of the food is used in **respiration**, which releases energy as **heat** to the surroundings. Respiration provides the energy for movement, growth and keeping warm.
- Some parts are **not eaten**, such as roots, bones, shells and hair.
- Some food is **not digested** and leaves the body as **faeces**.
- Some energy is lost in **excretion**, for example in urea.
- Warm-blooded animals (birds and mammals) lose more heat because they maintain a high body temperature.

Because so much energy is lost at each transfer, food chains rarely have more than four or five trophic levels. There is not enough energy left to support another level. It also explains why there are many more plants than herbivores and many more herbivores than carnivores.

### Worked example 3: the energy calculation

*A food chain starts with producers that capture 18 000 kJ per m² per year. The primary consumers receive 1440 kJ and the secondary consumers receive 144 kJ. Calculate the percentage of energy transferred at each step.*

```
efficiency = energy passed on ÷ energy available × 100

producers → primary:   1440 ÷ 18 000 × 100 = 8.0%
primary → secondary:   144 ÷ 1440 × 100    = 10.0%
```

Both values are close to the 10% rule. Use the energy of the **lower** level as the divisor.

### Worked example 4: applying the 10% rule

*Grass in a field contains 20 000 kJ of energy. Assuming 10% is transferred at each stage, calculate the energy that reaches the secondary consumer in the chain grass → grasshopper → frog.*

```
grass:                         20 000 kJ
grasshopper (10% of 20 000) =   2 000 kJ
frog (10% of 2 000)         =     200 kJ
```

The frog (secondary consumer) receives **200 kJ**. Show each stage, because marks are given for the method.

### Worked example 5: where did the energy go?

*A calf is fed 100 units of energy in food. 40 units are lost in faeces, 45 units are released in respiration, and 5 units are lost in urine. How much remains for growth?*

```
100 − 40 − 45 − 5 = 10 units
10 ÷ 100 × 100 = 10% is stored as new biomass (growth)
```

This 10 units of energy in the calf's growth is the energy available to the next feeder, which is why the 10% figure appears again.

## Why humans eat low in the food chain

Because 90% of energy is lost at each step, a field of crops can feed more people when people eat the crop directly than when they eat animals that were fed the crop. This is an exam favourite for "suggest why" questions. The answer is: less energy is lost between producer and human, so a greater proportion of the energy captured by the plants reaches people.

## Common mistakes

- Drawing arrows from the feeder to the food. Arrows point from the food to the feeder.
- Saying decomposers are a trophic level that is "last" in the chain. They feed on all levels.
- Saying "energy is used up" or "destroyed". Energy is transferred to the surroundings as heat, and the total is conserved.
- Saying only 10% of energy is lost. Only 10% is **passed on**, and 90% is lost.
- Describing a pyramid of numbers as always shaped like a pyramid.
- Using fresh mass instead of dry mass in a biomass pyramid.
- Writing "energy is recycled".

## Self-check

1. Name the producer in the chain: lettuce → snail → thrush → hawk.
2. State which organism is the tertiary consumer in that chain.
3. Calculate 10% of 5000 kJ.
4. Give two reasons why not all the energy in food is passed to the next trophic level.

**Answers:** (1) Lettuce. (2) Hawk. (3) 500 kJ. (4) Any two of: lost as heat from respiration, some parts not eaten, faeces, excretion.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between a food chain and a food web?**
A food chain is a single line of feeding. A food web joins many chains together.

**Why do arrows in a food chain point towards the feeder?**
They show the direction in which energy and substances move.

**Why is a pyramid of energy always pyramid-shaped?**
Energy is lost as heat and in waste at every transfer, so less energy is available at each higher level.
