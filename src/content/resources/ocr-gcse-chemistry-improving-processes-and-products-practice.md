These questions cover sub-topic **C6.1 Improving processes and products** from OCR's Chemistry A (Gateway Science) specification for GCSE (9–1), code J248, in its Version 4.0 issue of August 2026: learning outcomes C6.1a to C6.1s, mathematical learning outcomes CM6.1i to CM6.1iv, and the practicals PAG C1, PAG C2 and PAG C6. Questions on C6.1c to C6.1g are labelled **Higher Tier only**; all others suit both tiers. The content is examined in Paper 2 (Foundation) or Paper 4 (Higher).

For the rest of J248, use the [course hub](/boards/ocr/gcse/chemistry/) and the [printable statement checklist](/checklists/ocr/gcse/chemistry/).

## Questions

**1.** Tin is extracted from tin(IV) oxide, SnO₂, by heating it with carbon: SnO₂ + 2C → Sn + 2CO. Relative atomic masses: Sn = 119, O = 16.

**(a)** Explain, using the reactivity series, why carbon can extract tin. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage by mass of tin in SnO₂. **[2]**
**(c)** A crushed ore contains 3.0% SnO₂ by mass. Calculate the maximum mass of tin from 2000 kg of this ore. **[1]**
**(d)** The plant obtains 41.2 kg of tin from 2000 kg of ore. Calculate the percentage yield. **[1]**

**2.** In PAG C1, a student heats a mixture of black copper(II) oxide and carbon powder in a crucible with a lid.

**(a)** Describe what the student sees when the reaction is complete. **[1]**
**(b)** Identify the reducing agent. **[1]**
**(c)** Suggest why the lid is kept on the crucible while it cools. **[1]**

**3.** Aluminium is extracted by electrolysis of aluminium oxide dissolved in molten cryolite.

**(a)** Explain why aluminium is not extracted by heating its oxide with carbon. **[1]**
**(b)** Write an equation showing how aluminium ions become aluminium atoms. **[1]**
**(c)** Suggest why aluminium smelters are often built near hydroelectric power stations. **[2]**

**4.** In PAG C2 a student electrolyses copper sulfate solution with inert electrodes, weighing the cathode every 5 minutes.

| Time / min | 0 | 5 | 10 | 15 | 20 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mass gained / g | 0 | 0.07 | 0.15 | 0.21 | 0.28 |

**(a)** Use a line of best fit through the origin to calculate the rate at which copper is deposited. Give the unit. **[2]**
**(b)** Predict the mass gained after 30 minutes, assuming the rate stays the same. **[1]**
**(c)** Name the gas formed at the anode and describe a test for it. **[2]**
**(d)** Sodium chloride solution is electrolysed next. Name the cathode product and explain why sodium does not form. **[2]**

**5.** *(Higher Tier only)* A waste tip of rock containing 0.3% copper lies next to farmland and a river. Evaluate bacterial extraction and phytoextraction for this site. **[4]**

**6.** *(Higher Tier only)* In the Haber process, nitrogen and hydrogen form ammonia: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃. The forward reaction is exothermic. Use these values for a model plant:

| Conditions | 350 °C, 200 atm | 450 °C, 200 atm | 550 °C, 200 atm | 450 °C, 100 atm | 450 °C, 300 atm |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Equilibrium yield / % | 48 | 28 | 14 | 16 | 36 |

**(a)** Use the data to describe how temperature affects the yield, then explain the pattern. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why 450 °C is used rather than 350 °C. **[2]**
**(c)** Going from 200 to 300 atm adds 8 percentage points of yield. Suggest why the plant stays at 200 atm. **[2]**
**(d)** In the Contact process, 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃, the plant runs close to atmospheric pressure. Suggest why. **[1]**

**7.** *(Higher Tier only)* A volume-of-gas against time curve is drawn for a reaction.

**(a)** A tangent at 10 s passes through (0 s, 12 cm³) and (25 s, 62 cm³). Calculate the rate at 10 s. **[2]**
**(b)** The initial rate is 3.6 cm³/s. Rate is directly proportional to the pressure of the reacting gas. Predict the initial rate when the pressure is doubled. **[1]**
**(c)** Describe how the curve changes at a higher temperature with the same amounts of reactants. **[1]**

**8.** Ammonium phosphate, (NH₄)₃PO₄, is used in fertilisers. Relative atomic masses: N = 14, H = 1, P = 31, O = 16.

**(a)** Give the three elements supplied by NPK fertilisers, and one property every fertiliser compound must have. **[2]**
**(b)** Work out the nitrogen content of ammonium phosphate as a percentage by mass. **[2]**
**(c)** Describe how a factory site makes ammonium sulfate from air, natural gas and sulfur. **[2]**
**(d)** Give two differences between making ammonium sulfate in a school laboratory and in industry. **[2]**

**9.** A council compares two garden benches using these illustrative energy values (MJ).

| Stage | Timber bench | Aluminium bench |
|---|---|---|
| Raw materials and manufacture | 600 | 2400 |
| Transport | 40 | 30 |
| Use (wood treatment over its life) | 150 | 0 |
| Disposal | 20 | −900 (recycled) |
| Expected lifetime | 10 years | 25 years |

**(a)** Calculate the total energy for each bench. **[2]**
**(b)** Use the lifetimes to decide which bench uses less energy per year. **[2]**
**(c)** Give one reason not to decide on energy figures alone. **[1]**

**10.** Old car tyres can be shredded into rubber crumb for children's play-area surfaces.

**(a)** Explain why this is a viable use for old tyres. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest two factors a council would consider before starting a tyre-recycling scheme. **[2]**

**11.** This question is about alloys and corrosion.

**(a)** A 250 g brass fitting is 70% copper. Name the other metal and calculate its mass. **[2]**
**(b)** A bicycle chain left outside rusts. State the two conditions needed and explain how oiling prevents it. **[2]**
**(c)** Magnesium blocks are wired to a buried steel pipe. Explain how they protect it. **[2]**
**(d)** Name the alloy used in aircraft bodies that is mainly aluminium with about 4% copper. **[1]**

**12.** A delivery drone needs arms that are strong, light and will not shatter in a hard landing.

| Material | Density / g/cm³ | Tensile strength / MPa | Brittle? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nylon (polymer) | 1.15 | 75 | no |
| Glass-fibre composite | 1.9 | 300 | no |
| Aluminium alloy | 2.7 | 270 | no |
| Glass | 2.5 | 40 | yes |
| Clay ceramic | 2.3 | 10 | yes |

**(a)** Calculate strength ÷ density for the three materials that are not brittle. **[2]**
**(b)** Recommend a material and justify your choice using the data. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Carbon is **more reactive than tin**, so it can take the oxygen from tin(IV) oxide [1].
**(b)** Mr = 119 + 32 = 151 [1]; 119 ÷ 151 × 100 = **78.8%** [1]
**(c)** SnO₂ = 0.030 × 2000 = 60.0 kg; tin = 60.0 × 119 ÷ 151 = **47.3 kg** [1]
**(d)** 41.2 ÷ 47.3 × 100 = **87.1%** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Carry unrounded values forward; rounding 78.8% to 79% early shifts the yield.

**2. (a)** Pink-brown (copper-coloured) solid among the black powder [1].
**(b)** **Carbon** [1]
**(c)** Keeps air out so the hot copper is not oxidised back to copper oxide [1].
*Examiner insight:* "Copper forms" is not an observation; describe the colour seen.

**3. (a)** Aluminium is **above carbon** in the reactivity series; carbon cannot take oxygen from its oxide [1].
**(b)** **Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al** [1]
**(c)** Electrolysis needs huge amounts of electricity [1]; hydroelectric power supplies it cheaply [1].
*Examiner insight:* Electrons are gained at the cathode and charges must balance; a reversed equation is unlikely to gain credit.

**4. (a)** Gradient = 0.28 ÷ 20 [1] = **0.014 g/min** [1]
**(b)** 0.014 × 30 = **0.42 g** [1]
**(c)** **Oxygen** [1]; relights a glowing splint [1].
**(d)** **Hydrogen** [1]; sodium is more reactive than hydrogen, so hydrogen ions are discharged instead [1].
*Examiner insight:* Give g/min with the value in (a); a bare number leaves the rate incomplete.

**5.** Bacterial extraction works on ore this low grade, with little energy and no new mine [1]. But toxic leachate could escape into the river or farmland [1]. Phytoextraction produces no liquid waste and cleans the tip, but is slow, and burning the plants releases carbon dioxide [1]. Judgement: phytoextraction is safer beside a river, if the company can wait [1].
*Examiner insight:* "Evaluate" needs a judgement about the stated site; pros and cons with no conclusion limit the credit.

**6. (a)** Yield **falls** as temperature rises [1]; the forward reaction is exothermic, so higher temperature shifts equilibrium to the left [1].
**(b)** At 350 °C the yield is higher but the rate is too slow [1]; 450 °C is a compromise: acceptable yield at a faster rate [1].
**(c)** Higher pressure needs more energy for compression [1] and stronger, costlier equipment, so the extra yield does not pay; unreacted gas is recycled anyway [1].
**(d)** Yield is already high at low pressure, so extra pressure costs more than it gains [1].
*Examiner insight:* Compromise answers need both yield and rate; one side alone rarely earns full credit.

**7. (a)** (62 − 12) ÷ 25 [1] = **2.0 cm³/s** [1]
**(b)** 2 × 3.6 = **7.2 cm³/s** [1]
**(c)** Steeper start, levels off sooner, **same final volume** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Use the tangent's own coordinates; two points on the curve give an average rate instead.

**8. (a)** Nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium [1]; **soluble** in water [1].
**(b)** Mr = 3 × 18 + 31 + 64 = 149 [1]; 42 ÷ 149 × 100 = **28.2%** [1]
**(c)** Nitrogen (air) and hydrogen (natural gas) make ammonia in the Haber process [1]; sulfur gives sulfuric acid in the Contact process, which reacts with the ammonia [1].
**(d)** Any two: laboratory batch, industry continuous [1]; laboratory uses bought dilute solutions, industry makes concentrated reactants on site; industry reuses reaction heat [1].
*Examiner insight:* The formula has three nitrogen atoms; using 14 instead of 42 gives a wrong answer.

**9. (a)** Timber: 600 + 40 + 150 + 20 = **810 MJ** [1]; aluminium: 2400 + 30 + 0 − 900 = **1530 MJ** [1]
**(b)** Timber: 810 ÷ 10 = 81 MJ per year; aluminium: 1530 ÷ 25 = 61.2 MJ per year [1]. The **aluminium bench** uses less per year [1].
**(c)** Energy misses impacts such as forest loss or mining damage, which need value judgements [1].
*Examiner insight:* Subtract the negative disposal value; adding 900 reverses the conclusion.

**10. (a)** Large numbers of tyres give a reliable supply [1]; the surface does not need pure rubber, and tyres stay out of landfill [1].
**(b)** Any two: cost of collection and transport [1]; energy needed to shred; market demand for the crumb; landfill charges saved [1].
*Examiner insight:* "Good for the environment" is too vague; name the saving, such as landfill space.

**11. (a)** **Zinc** [1]; 0.30 × 250 = **75 g** [1]
**(b)** Oxygen and water [1]; oil is a barrier keeping them off the iron [1].
**(c)** Magnesium is **more reactive** than iron [1], so it oxidises in place of the steel (sacrificial protection) [1].
**(d)** **Duralumin** [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (c), calling magnesium "a coating" misses the point; credit is for reactivity and for magnesium corroding instead.

**12. (a)** Nylon 75 ÷ 1.15 = 65.2; composite 300 ÷ 1.9 = 157.9 [1]; aluminium alloy 270 ÷ 2.7 = 100 [1]
**(b)** **Glass-fibre composite** [1]: highest strength for its density (157.9 against 100 and 65.2) and not brittle [1].
*Examiner insight:* Quote the numbers; "it is strongest" does not use the data.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving electrons on the wrong side of an electrode half equation.
- (Higher Tier) Saying a catalyst raises the yield of ammonia.
- (Higher Tier) Explaining a compromise with yield only.
- Using one nitrogen atom when the formula contains three.
- Adding a negative LCA value instead of subtracting it.
- Giving one rusting condition instead of two.
