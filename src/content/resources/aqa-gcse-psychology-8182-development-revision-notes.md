## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Autonomic functions | Automatic body processes such as breathing and heart rate |
| Schema | A mental framework of knowledge about something |
| Assimilation | Fitting new information into an existing schema |
| Accommodation | Changing a schema, or making a new one, when information does not fit |
| Object permanence | Knowing an object still exists when out of sight |
| Egocentrism | Being unable to see a situation from another person's point of view |
| Conservation | Understanding that quantity stays the same when appearance changes |
| Fixed mindset | Belief that ability cannot be changed |
| Growth mindset | Belief that ability develops through effort and strategy |
| Self-efficacy | Belief in your ability to succeed at a particular task |
| Verbaliser | Someone who prefers to learn through words |
| Visualiser | Someone who prefers to learn through images |

## Brain development: structure to function

```
brain stem  -> autonomic functions (breathing, heart rate)   first
thalamus    -> sensory processing (relays senses to cortex)
cerebellum  -> movement, balance, co-ordination
cortex      -> cognition (thinking, language, planning)       last; matures into adolescence
```

**Nature:** genes set the order. **Nurture:** diet and health in pregnancy, avoiding harmful substances, stimulation and interaction after birth. Development is an **interaction** of both.

**Why the order matters:** a newborn must breathe, keep its heart beating and feed from birth, so the brain stem is well developed first. The thalamus relays information from the senses to the cortex, so sensory processing develops early. The cerebellum's control of balance and co-ordination develops as the infant learns to sit, crawl and walk. The **cortex**, responsible for higher thinking, develops gradually through childhood and adolescence, which fits Piaget's idea that abstract reasoning comes last.

**Evaluating nature and nurture:** genes direct the timetable of brain growth, but the environment affects how well it unfolds. Poor nutrition, alcohol or drugs in pregnancy can harm development, while a stimulating, responsive environment after birth supports it. A full answer says how the two **interact** rather than choosing one.

## Piaget's stages

| Stage | Age | Can do | Cannot yet do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sensorimotor | 0-2 | Learn through senses and action; object permanence by the end | Use symbols fully |
| Pre-operational | 2-7 | Language, pretend play | Conserve; decentre (egocentric) |
| Concrete operational | 7-11 | Conserve; logical thinking about real objects | Reason about abstract ideas |
| Formal operational | 11+ | Abstract, hypothetical reasoning | -- |

**How schemas change:** when new information fits an existing schema, the child **assimilates** it and is in a state of balance. When it does not fit, the child feels imbalance and must **accommodate**, changing the schema or forming a new one (a child who calls every four-legged animal "dog" learns that some are "cats"). This drive to restore balance moves the child through the stages.

**Piaget's own evidence:** hiding a toy under a blanket tested **object permanence**; pouring liquid from a short, wide glass into a tall, thin one tested **conservation** (pre-operational children said the tall glass held more); the **three mountains task**, where children chose the view a doll would see, tested **egocentrism** (younger children often chose their own view).

**In education:** readiness (match the stage), discovery learning, concrete materials for younger pupils, teacher as facilitator.

**Evaluating Piaget:** a strength is that his theory changed education, encouraging active, discovery-based learning matched to the child's level. Weaknesses: his tasks may have confused children, so he underestimated what they could do (see the two studies below); the stage ages are not fixed; and his theory gives little weight to the role of other people and language in learning.

## Studies that challenge Piaget

**McGarrigle and Donaldson (naughty teddy)** -- number conservation with two rows of counters; more young children conserved when "naughty teddy" messed up a row by accident than when an adult changed it on purpose. **Shows:** children conserve earlier than Piaget claimed. **But:** teddy may have distracted children from the change.

**Hughes (policeman doll)** -- children hid a boy doll from two policeman dolls in a cross-shaped model; most succeeded. **Shows:** egocentrism reduces earlier than Piaget claimed when a task makes human sense. **But:** hiding may be easier than knowing exactly what someone else sees.

**Why the studies matter:** in Piaget's version of conservation, an adult asks the same question twice, before and after deliberately changing the row, so a child may think a different answer is expected. In the naughty teddy version the change looks accidental, so the second question no longer hints that something important has changed. In the mountains task, children are asked about an unfamiliar adult problem; hiding from a policeman is a game they understand. Both studies suggest that **task design**, not just stage, explains some of Piaget's results.

**Evaluating the studies:** both used controlled conditions with young children, so they are easy to repeat. But they used artificial tasks with toys, and children's eagerness to please an adult may still have affected answers. They criticise the **age** at which abilities appear more than the order of Piaget's stages.

## Learning and development

- **Dweck:** fixed vs growth mindset. Praise **effort and strategy**, not ability, to build a growth mindset and self-efficacy. *For:* practical, easy to apply in schools. *Against:* later research suggests effects on grades may be smaller than first thought.
- **Learning styles:** verbalisers prefer words; visualisers prefer images.
- **Willingham:** little evidence that matching teaching to a preferred style improves learning; match the method to the **content**, and help pupils think about **meaning**.

**Dweck in more detail:** pupils with a **fixed mindset** see failure as proof they lack ability, so they avoid challenges and give up quickly. Pupils with a **growth mindset** see failure as information about what to work on, so they persist. In research by Mueller and Dweck (1998), children praised for being clever tended afterwards to choose easier tasks, whereas children praised for their effort tended to choose more challenging ones. Praise for effort and strategy also builds **self-efficacy**, the belief that you can succeed at a particular task, which encourages effort.

**Willingham in more detail:** he argues that everyone learns best when the method suits what is being learned (a map visually, a foreign-language accent by listening), that we remember what we have thought about in terms of **meaning**, and that becoming skilled at any physical or mental task needs extended **practice**. For teaching, this means planning lessons around the content and making pupils think hard about it, not sorting them into "visual" or "verbal" learners.

**Applying both to a scenario:** a pupil who says "I'm just not a maths person, and I'm a visual learner so I can't learn from worked examples" shows a **fixed mindset** (Dweck) and a belief in learning styles that Willingham would challenge. A teacher could praise the strategies the pupil uses and present maths in the way the content requires, with practice.

## Exam traps

- Assimilation **fits**; accommodation **changes**.
- Piaget **underestimated** young children; do not just say he was wrong.
- Dweck (mindsets) and Willingham (learning styles) are separate theories.
- Link every brain structure to a function.
- Piaget's stages are in a fixed **order**; the ages are approximate.
- The naughty teddy study is about **conservation**; the policeman doll study is about **egocentrism**.
- Praising intelligence ("You're so clever") encourages a **fixed** mindset, not a growth mindset.
- Verbaliser and visualiser describe **preferences**; Willingham does not deny people have them, only that matching them improves learning.

## Self-test

1. Which brain structure controls breathing and heart rate?
2. A child calls a zebra a "stripy horse". Is this assimilation or accommodation?
3. At which stage does conservation develop, according to Piaget?
4. Why did more children conserve in the naughty teddy condition?
5. What did Hughes' policeman doll study suggest about egocentrism?
6. Give an example of praise that encourages a growth mindset.
7. State Willingham's main criticism of learning styles.
8. Which brain structure relays sensory information to the cortex?
9. What did Piaget's three mountains task test?
10. Give one application of Piaget's theory to education.
11. Give one weakness of the naughty teddy study.
12. What did Mueller and Dweck find about praise?

**Answers:**
1. The brain stem.
2. Assimilation: the zebra is fitted into the existing "horse" schema.
3. The concrete operational stage (about 7-11).
4. The accidental change did not suggest that the answer should change, so children answered on the basis of what they understood.
5. That young children can see from another's point of view earlier than Piaget claimed, when the task makes sense to them.
6. Any praise of effort or strategy, such as "You worked really hard on that and tried a new method."
7. There is little evidence that matching teaching to a preferred style improves learning.
8. The thalamus.
9. Egocentrism: whether a child could choose the view of the mountains seen by a doll in another position.
10. Any one: readiness (teach at the right stage); discovery learning; concrete materials for younger pupils; teacher as facilitator.
11. The teddy may have distracted children so they did not notice the change, or the task was artificial.
12. Children praised for intelligence tended to choose easier tasks afterwards, while those praised for effort chose more challenging ones.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between assimilation and accommodation?**
Assimilation fits new information into an existing schema; accommodation changes the schema or creates a new one when the information does not fit.

**What is self-efficacy in Dweck's Mindset Theory?**
It is a person's belief in their ability to succeed at a task, which praise for effort can strengthen.
