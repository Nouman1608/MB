This guide covers section 8.01, Conventions, notation and terms, of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Mathematics J560 specification, Version 2.1 (August 2026), for first assessment in 2017. The specification says learners "will be expected to be familiar with the following geometrical skills, conventions, notation and terms, which will be assessed in questions at both tiers". So everything here is for **both tiers**, and it is the language every other geometry question is written in.

The [geometry notation, terms, instruments and 3D solids guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-mathematics-geometry-notation-and-terms-instruments-3d-solids/) lists the terms and gives first examples. This page goes further on 8.01: the marks used on diagrams, why angles need three letters, what the order of letters in a shape's name tells you, the family tree of quadrilaterals, turning a written description into an accurate sketch, and using coordinates to name and check shapes. ## Marks on a diagram

Diagrams use a small set of symbols. Read them as carefully as the words.

| Mark | Meaning |
|---|---|
| A small square in a corner | A right angle (90°) |
| Matching arrows on two lines | The lines are parallel; a second pair of parallel lines gets double arrows |
| Matching dashes (hatch marks) on two sides | The sides are equal in length; a second equal pair gets double dashes |
| Matching arcs in two angles | The angles are equal |
| AB ∥ CD | Line AB is parallel to line CD |
| AB ⊥ CD | Line AB is perpendicular to line CD |

Two warnings. First, a diagram may say "not drawn accurately", so never measure it to get an answer; work from the marks and facts. Second, a line that looks perpendicular is not perpendicular unless there is a right-angle mark or the question says so.

**Line or line segment?** A line goes on for ever in both directions. A line segment has two end points. In everyday questions "the line AB" usually means the segment from A to B, and "AB" can also mean its length, as in AB = 6 cm.

## Naming angles

An angle is named by three letters with the vertex in the middle: angle ABC is at B, between the arms BA and BC. Angle CBA is the same angle. You may see it written with the angle symbol before the letters.

**Worked example 1.** Lines BA, BC and BD all start at point B, with D between A and C. Angle ABD = 35° and angle DBC = 50°. Explain why "angle B" is not a good name, and find angle ABC.

At B there are three angles: ABD, DBC and the whole angle ABC. "Angle B" could mean any of them. With three letters there is no doubt.

```
angle ABC = angle ABD + angle DBC = 35° + 50° = 85°   (acute)
```

A single letter is acceptable only when exactly one angle sits at that vertex, as in a triangle.

**Angle types.** Acute: between 0° and 90°. Right: exactly 90°. Obtuse: between 90° and 180°. Reflex: between 180° and 360°. A straight angle (180°) and a full turn (360°) are named in words. At any vertex, the angle and the reflex angle on the other side add up to 360°.

**Side labels in a triangle.** In triangle ABC, the side opposite angle A is called a, so a = BC. Lower-case side, capital angle, matching letters, on opposite sides of the triangle. This is the labelling the Higher tier sine and cosine rules use.

## The order of letters in a shape's name

A polygon is named by listing its vertices **in order around the shape**, either way round. So rectangle ABCD has sides AB, BC, CD and DA, and its diagonals are AC and BD. Rectangle ABDC would be a different arrangement, with AD as a side.

This one convention answers many questions. In parallelogram PQRS, the side parallel to PQ is SR, and angle P is opposite angle R.

## Polygon names and the quadrilateral family

A **regular** polygon has all sides equal **and** all angles equal. Both conditions are needed:

- A rhombus has equal sides but, unless it is a square, unequal angles. It is not regular.
- A rectangle has equal angles but, unless it is a square, unequal sides. It is not regular.
- An equilateral triangle and a square are the regular 3-sided and 4-sided polygons.

Quadrilateral names overlap, because a special shape keeps every property of the more general one.

| Shape | Is also a ... |
|---|---|
| Square | rectangle, rhombus, parallelogram, kite |
| Rectangle | parallelogram |
| Rhombus | parallelogram, kite |
| Parallelogram | quadrilateral |

When a question asks for "the mathematical name", give the most specific name the facts support.

## Drawing diagrams from written descriptions

**Method.** Read one fact at a time. Draw a rough sketch, place the vertices in the order the name gives, then add every mark: equal sides, parallel arrows, right angles and given lengths. Finally, write the unknown you need on the diagram.

**Worked example 2.** ABCD is a parallelogram with AB = 8 cm, AD = 5 cm and angle DAB = 60°. E is the point on DC with DE = 3 cm. Sketch the diagram and describe triangle BCE fully.

```
Order A, B, C, D round the shape: AB ∥ DC and AD ∥ BC
DC = AB = 8 cm, so EC = 8 − 3 = 5 cm
BC = AD = 5 cm
angle BCD = angle DAB = 60°   (opposite angles of a parallelogram)
```

Triangle BCE has BC = CE = 5 cm with the 60° angle between them. Its other two angles are equal and add to 120°, so each is 60°. Triangle BCE is **equilateral**, and BE = 5 cm. The sketch made this visible: without placing E on DC in the right order, the link is easy to miss.

## Using instruments

- **Ruler:** measure from the zero mark; give lengths to the nearest millimetre.
- **Protractor:** centre on the vertex, base line on one arm, read the scale that starts at 0° on that arm. Decide first whether the angle is acute or obtuse, and check your reading against that.
- **Compasses:** set the radius on a ruler; keep the setting fixed while you draw.

**Worked example 3.** Construct triangle PQR with PQ = 6 cm, PR = 8 cm and angle QPR = 50°. Measure QR.

```
1. Draw PQ = 6 cm.
2. At P, measure 50° from PQ and draw a long arm.
3. Mark R on that arm, 8 cm from P.
4. Join QR and measure it.
```

QR should measure about 6.2 cm, and angle PQR about 82°. On the Higher tier you can check with the cosine rule, but the question only asks you to measure. For a reflex angle such as 250°, draw 360° − 250° = 110° and mark the outside angle.

## Coordinates in geometry

Coordinates turn geometry into arithmetic. Horizontal lengths come from subtracting x-coordinates, vertical lengths from subtracting y-coordinates.

**Worked example 4.** A(1, 1), B(7, 1), C(9, 4) and D(3, 4). Give the mathematical name of ABCD.

```
AB: from (1, 1) to (7, 1), horizontal, length 6
DC: from (3, 4) to (9, 4), horizontal, length 6
AD: 2 right and 3 up;  BC: 2 right and 3 up
```

AB and DC are parallel and equal, and AD and BC are parallel and equal, so ABCD is a parallelogram. AD goes 2 across and 3 up, so it is not horizontal or vertical and the corners are not right angles: it is not a rectangle. Its length is not 6 (a 2-by-3 step is far shorter), so it is not a rhombus. The most specific name is **parallelogram**.

**Worked example 5 (transformations of simple shapes).** Triangle T has vertices (1, 2), (4, 2) and (1, 6). Reflect it in the line y = x and state what stays the same.

Reflecting in y = x swaps the coordinates of every point: (1, 2) → (2, 1), (4, 2) → (2, 4), (1, 6) → (6, 1). The image has the same side lengths, angles and area (½ × 3 × 4 = 6 square units). Only its position and orientation change.

## Common mistakes

- Naming an angle with one letter when several angles meet at that point.
- Putting the vertex letter first or last instead of in the middle.
- Listing a polygon's vertices out of order, which swaps sides and diagonals.
- Calling a rhombus or rectangle "regular".
- Measuring a diagram marked "not drawn accurately".
- Reading the wrong protractor scale, so 40° is recorded as 140°.
- Giving "quadrilateral" when a more specific name is supported.

## Exam technique for OCR command words

- **Write down the mathematical name**: give the most specific correct name.
- **Measure**: give the value with units, to the nearest millimetre or degree.
- **Draw** or **Construct**: use instruments, and leave construction lines visible.
- **Sketch**: neat but not accurate; label every given fact.
- **Give a reason**: quote the property or convention, for example "opposite angles of a parallelogram are equal".
