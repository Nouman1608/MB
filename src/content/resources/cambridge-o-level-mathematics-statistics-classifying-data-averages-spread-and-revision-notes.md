## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Categorical data | Described in words (colour, transport type) |
| Discrete data | Numerical, separate values only (goals, shoe size) |
| Continuous data | Numerical, any value in a range (time, mass, length) |
| Frequency | How many times a value or class occurs |
| Mean | Sum of values ÷ number of values |
| Median | Middle value of ordered data |
| Mode | Most common value |
| Modal class | Class with the highest frequency (for classes of equal width) |
| Range | Largest − smallest |
| Outlier | A value far from the rest |

## 9.1 Tables

* **Tally table:** strokes in fives, then count to get the frequency. The frequencies must add to the number of items.
* **Two-way table:** two features at once. Use the totals to find missing entries, then check that the rows and columns both add to the grand total.

### Method for a missing entry

1. Find a row or column with only one blank.
2. Subtract the known entries from its total.
3. Repeat until the table is full.
4. Check the grand total from both directions.

## 9.3 Averages and spread

| Measure | Method | Good for | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mean | Σx ÷ n | Data with no extremes | Pulled by outliers |
| Median | Middle of ordered list | Skewed data, outliers | Ignores most values |
| Mode | Most frequent | Categorical data, "most popular" | May not exist or may not be unique |
| Range | Largest − smallest | Quick spread | Uses only two values |

**Median position:** (n + 1) ÷ 2. For 20 values, the position is 10.5, so the median is halfway between the 10th and 11th values.

**Frequency table:** mean = Σfx ÷ Σf. The mode is the value (the x), not its frequency.

**Grouped data:** estimated mean = Σ(f × midpoint) ÷ Σf. Midpoint of a continuous class a < x ≤ b is (a + b) ÷ 2. For a discrete class such as 10 to 19, the midpoint is 14.5. The answer is always an estimate.

### Method for an estimated mean

1. Add a midpoint row.
2. Multiply each frequency by its midpoint.
3. Add those products and add the frequencies.
4. Divide the first total by the second.

### Worked reminder: frequency table

Number of pens in a pencil case, 20 cases:

| Pens | 10 | 11 | 12 | 13 | 14 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Frequency | 2 | 5 | 8 | 3 | 2 |

```
Σfx = 20 + 55 + 96 + 39 + 28 = 238
Mean = 238 ÷ 20 = 11.9
```

### Worked reminder: grouped data

Ages of 20 visitors: 0–10 (6 people), 10–20 (9 people), 20–30 (5 people), each class meaning "from this age up to just below the next".

```
Midpoints 5, 15, 25
Σ(f × midpoint) = 30 + 135 + 125 = 290
Estimated mean = 290 ÷ 20 = 14.5
Modal class = 10–20
```

### Worked reminder: the effect of an outlier

Values: 3, 4, 4, 6, 8, 9. The sum is 34, so the mean is 34 ÷ 6 = 5.67 (3 s.f.) and the median is (4 + 6) ÷ 2 = 5.

Now add the value 60. The sum becomes 94 and the mean becomes 94 ÷ 7 = 13.4 (3 s.f.), more than double. The ordered list is 3, 4, 4, 6, 8, 9, 60, so the median is the fourth value, 6. The median moved by 1 and the mean by almost 8. That is why the median is the better "typical value" when an extreme value is present.

## 9.4 Charts

| Chart | Key points |
|---|---|
| Bar chart | Gaps between bars, equal widths, scale from zero, labelled axes |
| Dual bar chart | Two bars side by side per category; compare the groups directly |
| Composite bar chart | Parts stacked on one bar; total is the bar height; read an upper section as the difference between its top and bottom |
| Pictogram | Use the key; half a symbol is half the key value |
| Pie chart | Angle = (frequency ÷ total) × 360° |
| Frequency distribution | Values along the bottom, frequency up the side |

### Worked reminder: reading a composite bar chart

A stacked bar shows items sold on one day: 30 bottles of water, 22 cartons of juice and 15 cans of cola, stacked in that order. The three sections run from 0 to 30, from 30 to 52 and from 52 to 67. The cola section is 67 − 52 = 15 units tall, so you read its value as the difference between its top and bottom. The whole bar shows 67 items.

### Pie chart method

1. Add the frequencies to get the total.
2. Work out 360° ÷ total (the angle for one item).
3. Multiply each frequency by that angle.
4. Check that the angles add to 360°.

To read a pie chart: frequency = (angle ÷ 360°) × total.

### Worked reminder: pie chart

Forty-eight people choose a drink: tea 18, juice 12, coffee 10, water 8. One person is 360° ÷ 48 = 7.5°.

```
Tea 135°, Juice 90°, Coffee 75°, Water 60°  (total 360°)
```

## 9.2 Interpreting data

* Quote figures from the chart or table, not just the shape.
* **Compare:** one comment on an average, one on spread, each in context.
* **Limits on conclusions:** small or biased sample, grouped data hides detail, averages hide spread, a chart does not prove cause, scales can mislead, predictions beyond the data are unreliable.

## Must-know distinctions

* Mean is affected by outliers; median is not.
* Mode is a value; the frequency of the mode is how often it occurs.
* Range measures spread; the averages measure the centre.
* A grouped mean is an estimate; a mean from a list is exact.
* Composite bars show the total; dual bars compare the parts.

## Quick self-test

1. Classify each as categorical, discrete or continuous: (a) number of goals in a match, (b) height of a plant, (c) nationality.
2. Find the mean, median, mode and range of 6, 9, 4, 9, 12.
3. Twenty values are listed in order. Which positions give the median?
4. A table shows x = 0, 1, 2, 3 with frequencies 5, 7, 6, 2. Find the mean.
5. Classes 10–19, 20–29, 30–39 have frequencies 4, 6, 10. Estimate the mean.
6. In a survey of 40 people, 14 prefer cycling. What angle is its pie sector?
7. A sector of 80° represents how many people if the pie chart shows 117 people?
8. In a pictogram, one symbol = 4 sandwiches. How many symbols show 18 sandwiches?
9. The mean of four numbers is 10. A fifth number, 20, is added. What is the new mean?
10. Which average best describes house prices in a town with a few very expensive mansions, and why?
11. Two classes have the same mean mark. Class A has range 8 and Class B has range 35. What does this tell you?
12. A survey of 15 members of one chess club finds that 12 like board games. Why should you not conclude that most pupils in the school like board games?

### Answers

1. (a) discrete, (b) continuous, (c) categorical.
2. Sum 40, so mean 8. Ordered: 4, 6, 9, 9, 12, so median 9, mode 9, range 12 − 4 = 8.
3. The 10th and 11th values; the median is halfway between them.
4. Σf = 20, Σfx = 0 + 7 + 12 + 6 = 25, mean = 25 ÷ 20 = 1.25.
5. Midpoints 14.5, 24.5, 34.5. Σ(f × midpoint) = 58 + 147 + 345 = 550. Mean ≈ 550 ÷ 20 = 27.5.
6. (14 ÷ 40) × 360° = 126°.
7. (80 ÷ 360) × 117 = 26 people.
8. 18 ÷ 4 = 4.5, so four and a half symbols.
9. Total of the four = 40; new total 60; new mean 60 ÷ 5 = 12.
10. The median, because the mansions pull the mean up and the median stays near a typical price.
11. The averages are equal, but Class A's marks are far more consistent. Class B's marks are more spread out.
12. The sample is small and biased: chess club members are not typical of the whole school, so the result cannot be generalised.

## Where marks are usually lost

* Not ordering the data before reading off the median.
* Using the frequency column total as the number of classes in a mean calculation.
* Using class limits instead of midpoints in a grouped mean.
* Stating the grouped mean without saying it is an estimate.
* Giving the highest frequency instead of the corresponding value as the mode.
* Pie angles that do not total 360°, or no check made.
* Reading a stacked bar's upper section from the axis instead of from the top of the section below.
* Comparing two sets with an average only, and no comment on spread.
* Answering "which is better" with no figures from the data.
* Leaving out units or context in a written conclusion.
