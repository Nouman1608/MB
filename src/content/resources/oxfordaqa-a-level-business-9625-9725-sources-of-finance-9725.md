This guide covers **3.2.3.1 Sources of finance** from OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Business (9725), Version 1.1, in Unit 2 Managing operations, human resources and finance (International AS). The [Finance study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-finance/) lists the advantages and disadvantages of each source; this page shows how to work out what a source really costs, what it does to ownership and control, and how to build a judgement about which source fits a situation. Then condense it with the [sources of finance revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-sources-of-finance-9725-revision-notes/) and practise with the [sources of finance practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-sources-of-finance-9725-practice/).

All businesses and figures are invented. Interest calculations here are simplified to a single year on the full amount, to show the idea; real loans are repaid over time.

## What the specification asks for

- Advantages and disadvantages of **internal** sources: retained profits, sale of assets, sale and leaseback, working capital.
- Advantages and disadvantages of **external** sources: trade credit, share capital, overdraft, loans, business angels, private equity, crowd funding.
- The **appropriateness** of different sources in different situations, for example a start-up.

## Four questions that decide any finance choice

1. **How much is needed?** Small sums suit an overdraft or trade credit; large sums need loans, share capital or private equity.
2. **For how long?** Short-term needs (paying suppliers before customers pay) suit short-term sources; long-life assets suit long-term sources.
3. **What will it cost?** Interest, dividends, fees, rent, or a share of future profits.
4. **What happens to control?** Debt leaves ownership unchanged; equity (share capital, angels, private equity) shares ownership and often decision-making.

Add two practical checks: **can the business get it?** (a start-up has no retained profit and little security) and **is it risky?** (repayments are due whatever happens to sales).

## Internal sources

**Retained profits.** Profit kept in the business instead of paid to owners. No interest and no loss of control, but only available if the business has been profitable, and every dollar retained is a dollar not paid as dividends.

**Sale of assets.** Selling equipment, vehicles or property the business no longer needs. Turns idle assets into cash, but a forced sale may get a poor price, and once sold the asset is gone.

**Sale and leaseback.** Selling an asset, usually property, and renting it back.

**Worked example 1.** Gorsefield Hotels sells its building for $900,000 and leases it back for $60,000 a year. It receives a large sum at once and keeps trading from the same site. But the $60,000 rent is paid every year, raising fixed costs, and Gorsefield no longer benefits if property values rise. After 15 years it will have paid 15 × 60,000 = $900,000 in rent, the whole sale price, and still not own the building.

**Working capital.** Releasing cash already tied up in day-to-day operations.

**Worked example 2.** Ellery Bikes holds $80,000 of inventory and is owed $120,000 by customers. By cutting inventory to $50,000 and collecting debts faster, so customers owe $90,000 on average, it releases:

```
From inventory   = 80,000 − 50,000 = $30,000
From receivables = 120,000 − 90,000 = $30,000
Total released   = $60,000
```

No interest is paid, but there are limits: too little inventory risks stock-outs, and pressing customers to pay sooner may drive some to rivals.

## External sources

**Trade credit.** Suppliers allow time to pay, for example 60 days. If Ellery sells the bikes within 30 days, the supplier has effectively financed the stock. It is usually free, but paying late damages relationships, and early-payment discounts may be lost.

**Overdraft.** The bank allows the account to go below zero up to an agreed limit.

**Worked example 3.** A firm is $15,000 overdrawn for two months at an interest rate of 12% a year:

```
Interest = 15,000 × 12% × 2/12 = $300
```

Flexible and cheap for short gaps, because interest is paid only on what is used, for as long as it is used. But rates are usually higher than on loans, and the bank can reduce or withdraw the overdraft. Using it for long-term needs is risky.

**Loans.** A fixed sum repaid over an agreed period with interest. A $50,000 loan at 8% a year costs about 50,000 × 8% = **$4,000** in interest in the first year. Predictable repayments help planning, and ownership is unchanged. But repayments are due even if sales fall, and banks usually want security and a trading record.

**Share capital.** Money raised by selling shares in a limited company. No interest and no repayment; but owners give up part of the business and expect dividends.

**Worked example 4: dilution.** Fenwick Ltd's founder owns all 100,000 shares. The company issues 25,000 new shares to an investor.

```
Total shares after issue = 125,000
Investor's share         = 25,000 ÷ 125,000 × 100 = 20%
Founder's share          = 100,000 ÷ 125,000 × 100 = 80%
```

The founder keeps control (80%), but 20% of future profits now belong to the investor.

**Business angels.** Wealthy individuals who invest their own money in small, often new, businesses in exchange for shares. If an angel invests $60,000 for 25% of a business, the deal values the whole business at 60,000 ÷ 25% = **$240,000**. Angels often bring experience and contacts as well as money, but the owner gives up a share of profits and some control.

**Private equity.** Investment firms that buy large stakes in established private companies, aiming to grow them and sell at a profit within a few years. They provide large sums and management expertise, but may demand significant control, set demanding targets and push for a fast return.

**Crowd funding.** Raising money from many small backers, usually online. For example, 800 backers pledging an average of $50 raises 800 × 50 = **$40,000**. Backers may receive rewards, products or shares. It can test demand and build early customers, but campaigns can fail to reach their target, the idea becomes public, and running a campaign takes time and effort.

## Matching the source to the situation

| Situation | Suitable sources | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Start-up with no trading record | Owner's own capital, business angels, crowd funding, small loan if secured | No retained profit; banks see high risk |
| Short-term cash gap (customers pay late) | Overdraft, trade credit, working capital | Small, temporary need |
| Buying long-life machinery | Loan, retained profits, sale and leaseback | Long-term asset matched with long-term finance |
| Fast-growing company needing large sums | Share capital, private equity | Too much to borrow safely |
| Profitable, established firm | Retained profits first | Cheapest; no loss of control |
| Struggling firm with valuable property | Sale and leaseback, sale of assets | Banks may refuse to lend |

## Annotated model answer

**Question:** Rowan Juice, a start-up, needs $35,000 to buy pressing equipment. Explain **one** reason why a business angel might be more appropriate than a bank loan.

> As a start-up, Rowan Juice has no trading record and probably little security **[context]**, so a bank may refuse a loan or charge a high interest rate **[problem with the alternative]**. A business angel invests in exchange for shares, so there are no repayments while sales are still building up, and the angel's experience may help Rowan win its first stockists **[benefit, linked to the situation]**.

## Common mistakes

- Calling retained profit "free": it has an opportunity cost, because owners receive less in dividends.
- Recommending an overdraft for long-term needs.
- Forgetting that equity sources reduce the original owners' share of profits and control.
- Treating sale and leaseback as raising money with no lasting cost.
- Choosing a source without checking whether the business could actually obtain it.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate**: interest, dilution or amounts released; show the working and the unit.
- **Explain**: link the source's feature to the business's need (amount, time, cost, control).
- **Analyse**: follow the consequences, such as loan → fixed repayments → pressure on cash flow if sales are slow.
- **Evaluate**: compare at least two sources against the four questions, then recommend one and say what the choice depends on.
