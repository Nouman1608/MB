All businesses and figures are invented. Revise first with the [organisational design and HR flow study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-organisational-design-and-human-resource-flow/) and the [organisational design and HR flow revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-organisational-design-and-human-resource-flow-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** A business has 360 operatives and each supervisor manages 9 operatives. How many supervisors are needed? **[1]**

A: 9

B: 36

C: 40

D: 360

**2.** Which **one** of the following structures organises a business by activity such as marketing, operations and finance? **[1]**

A: Functional

B: Product-based

C: Regional

D: Matrix

**3.** Define the term "delegation". **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** disadvantage of a tall organisational hierarchy. **[3]**

**5.** Orrin Textiles has 600 operatives. It is comparing a span of control of 6 with a span of 12 for the supervisor level.

**(a)** Calculate the number of supervisors needed for each span. **[2]**

**(b)** Each supervisor costs £35,000 a year. Calculate the annual saving from using the wider span. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **one** risk of the wider span. **[2]**

**6.** Cawley Components needs 150 employees next year. It has 160 now and expects labour turnover of 15%.

**(a)** Calculate the number of leavers expected. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the shortfall of employees. **[2]**

**(c)** State **one** way Cawley could fill the gap. **[1]**

**7.** A firm makes 20 employees redundant. Each redundancy costs £6,500 and each post cost £26,000 a year.

**(a)** Calculate the one-off cost. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the annual saving. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate how many months it takes for the saving to pay back the cost. **[1]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**8.** Brightmoor Foods sells frozen, chilled and dry food in several countries. Analyse **two** advantages to Brightmoor of a product-based structure. **[6]**

**9.** A growing retail chain currently makes all decisions at head office. Assess whether it should decentralise decision making. **[9]**

**10.** Analyse why a business with a surplus of staff might choose redeployment before redundancy. **[6]**

**11.** Explain **two** difficulties a business might face when changing its organisational structure. **[4]**

**12.** A consultancy firm works on many short projects needing different specialists. Evaluate whether a matrix structure is the best choice for it. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** **C** [1]. 360 ÷ 9 = 40. **[1]**

**2.** **A** [1]. A functional structure is organised by the activities of the business. **[1]**

**3.** Passing authority to carry out a task or make a decision to a subordinate [1], who becomes accountable for the result, while the manager remains ultimately responsible [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Communication has to pass through many levels [1], so messages are slow and may be distorted, which delays decisions [1]. A tall hierarchy also means many management posts, so the wage bill is high, and staff at the bottom may feel remote from decisions [1]. **[3]**

**5. (a)** Span of 6: 600 ÷ 6 = **100** supervisors [1]. Span of 12: 600 ÷ 12 = **50** supervisors [1].

**(b)** 50 fewer supervisors × £35,000 [1] = **£1,750,000** a year [1].

**(c)** Each supervisor has less time for each operative, so problems may go unnoticed and quality or safety could suffer [1]; this is particularly so if the work is complex or the staff are inexperienced [1]. **[6]**

**6. (a)** 15% × 160 = **24 leavers** [1].

**(b)** Supply = 160 − 24 = 136 [1]; shortfall = 150 − 136 = **14** [1].

**(c)** Any one: recruit externally; promote internally; redeploy from another area; retrain; use overtime or temporary staff [1]. **[4]**

**7. (a)** 20 × £6,500 = **£130,000** [1].

**(b)** 20 × £26,000 = **£520,000** [1].

**(c)** £130,000 ÷ £520,000 × 12 = **3 months** [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Advantage 1: focus on each product group [1]. Frozen, chilled and dry foods have different customers, storage and supply needs [1], so each division can specialise and respond quickly to its own market [1]. Advantage 2: clear accountability [1]. The manager of each division is responsible for its profit and costs [1], so performance is easy to judge and managers are motivated to improve it [1]. Accept: easier to expand with new products. **[6]**

**9.** For: managers in each shop or region know local customers best, so decisions on range, pricing and staffing can be made faster [1]. Decentralising frees head office to concentrate on strategy [1]. Giving store managers responsibility motivates them and develops future leaders [1]. A growing chain is too big for one head office to manage every decision [1]. Against: stores could be inconsistent in standards or pricing, harming the brand [1]. Head office loses control and the chain could lose economies of scale in buying [1]. Local managers may lack the skills to make good decisions [1]. Judgement: a mixed approach is likely to be best, with central control of brand, buying and finance, and local freedom over staffing and local range [1], with training for store managers and regular reporting to keep control [1]. **[9]**

**10.** Redeployment keeps skills and knowledge that the business has paid to develop [1], and avoids the cost of redundancy payments [1]. It also avoids the cost of recruiting and training new staff if demand returns [1]. Staff are likely to feel more secure, which protects morale and engagement among the whole workforce [1], and the business keeps a better reputation as an employer, helping future recruitment [1]. It may need retraining and some staff may resist moving, but these are usually less costly than redundancy [1]. **[6]**

**11.** Difficulty 1: resistance from staff and managers [1]. People may fear loss of status or jobs, so they may work against the change or leave, reducing its success [1]. Difficulty 2: cost and disruption [1]. Retraining, redundancy payments and confusion about who is responsible during the change can reduce productivity in the short term [1]. **[4]**

**12.** For: projects need different specialists at different times, and a matrix allows staff to be shared between projects [1]. It encourages teamwork across specialisms [1], and consultants gain wider experience [1]. Clients get a team with the right mix of skills [1]. It is flexible, adapting as projects start and finish [1]. Against: staff have two managers, so priorities may clash, causing confusion and stress [1]. Disputes over staff time can slow decisions [1]. It needs more meetings and strong communication, adding cost [1]. It may be hard to hold anyone accountable for results [1]. Factors: the size of the firm, the clarity of procedures for allocating staff and the skills of managers in working together [1]. Judgement: a matrix is probably the most suitable structure for project-based consultancy because the work requires flexible teams [1], provided the firm sets clear rules on authority and resource allocation [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* Match the structure to the features of the business in the case, such as products, markets, size and the nature of the work.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a structure without saying when it suits the business.
- Confusing delegation with decentralisation.
- Treating a flat structure as always better.
- Treating redundancy as the first response to a surplus.
- Ignoring morale and reputation when discussing redundancy.
- Forgetting that internal recruitment leaves another vacancy.
