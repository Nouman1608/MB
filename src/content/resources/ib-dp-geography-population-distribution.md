This guide covers **Population Distribution -- Changing Population**,
part of the compulsory SL and HL core (Geographic perspectives: global
change) for IB Diploma Programme Geography, first assessment 2019. The
[full syllabus guide](/resources/ib-dp-geography-syllabus-guide/)
confirms this core is identical in content and hours for every
student, unlike the seven teacher-selected geographic-themes options.

## Where this fits in the syllabus

Because the geographic-themes options vary by school, revision
material on an unstudied option is wasted effort. The core is
different: every DP Geography student, regardless of school, studies
Population Distribution as part of the shared core, examined in Paper
2 with the same weight given to Paper 1's option content. Population
Distribution sits alongside [Global Climate: Vulnerability and
Resilience](/resources/ib-dp-geography-global-climate/) and Global
Resource Consumption and Security within the compulsory core -- the
three sub-themes are not meant to be revised as unconnected blocks.

## Why populations are unevenly distributed

Population is not spread evenly across the Earth's surface, and this
sub-theme asks why. Physical factors that pull settlement toward some
areas and away from others include climate, relief (terrain), soil
fertility, availability of fresh water and resource access. Human
factors include economic opportunity, historical patterns of
settlement, political stability, and infrastructure. A strong answer
does not stop at naming these categories generically -- it applies
specific, named factors to a specific named place, since Paper 2
rewards concrete, evidenced explanation over abstract listing.

## Key vocabulary

**Population density** is the number of people per unit area, usually
expressed per square kilometre -- distinct from **population
distribution**, which describes the overall pattern of where people
are located rather than a single density figure. **Carrying capacity**
is the maximum population size an environment can sustainably support
given its available resources. **Population change** is driven by two
components that should be stated separately, not blended into a single
figure: natural change (births minus deaths) and net migration
(immigration minus emigration). The **demographic transition model**
describes how birth rates and death rates change as a country
develops, moving through stages from high birth and death rates
through to low birth and death rates, with a distinct effect on
overall population growth rate at each stage.

## Reading population data

This sub-theme is frequently examined through data-response questions:
population pyramids, choropleth density maps, and demographic
transition model diagrams. Reading a population pyramid specifically
requires recognising that a wide base indicates high birth rates, a
narrowing top indicates lower life expectancy or an ageing population,
and an unusually narrow or wide band at a specific age range can
indicate a historical event (war, baby boom, mass migration) that
affected that particular cohort. Because Paper 2 typically presents
new, unseen data rather than previously studied examples, practising
the skill of reading unfamiliar pyramids and maps under timed
conditions is more valuable than memorising the shape of a small
number of labelled examples.

## Worked example: reading an ageing population's pyramid

A population pyramid shows a narrow base, a wide middle section, and a
relatively wide top compared with a typical developing-country
pyramid. The narrow base indicates a low, and likely falling, birth
rate. The wide top indicates a relatively large elderly population,
consistent with high life expectancy. Together, these features
indicate a country in a later stage of the demographic transition
model, where both birth rates and death rates have fallen to low
levels -- and where the population's age structure is shifting toward
requiring greater investment in elderly care and pension provision,
relative to a younger, faster-growing population elsewhere in the
transition model. Reading the pyramid's shape and connecting it
directly to a demographic-transition stage and a real social or
economic consequence, rather than describing the shape alone, is what
this kind of question actually rewards.

## Case studies to build

Because Paper 2 rewards named, specific examples over generalised
description -- an expectation that applies across every DP Geography
theme -- build a small case-study bank specifically for population
distribution: one example of a densely populated area and the factors
explaining its density, one example of a sparsely populated area and
the factors explaining its sparsity, and one example of significant
population change (through migration or natural change) with real,
named data -- dates, figures, and specific policy or event context,
not a generic account.

## How this sub-theme connects to the wider core

A region's population distribution directly shapes its vulnerability
to climate impacts (dense coastal populations facing sea-level rise,
for instance) and its resource consumption patterns (population size
and distribution driving demand). Revising these connections
explicitly, rather than treating each core sub-theme in isolation,
prepares candidates for Paper 2 questions that draw on more than one
sub-theme within a single answer.

## How to approach it

Because this content is examined identically for SL and HL students,
do not deprioritise it relative to your chosen Paper 1 options -- Paper
2 tests this core with the same weight Paper 1 gives to your options.
Practise the specific skill of moving between quantitative data reading
(extracting a figure or trend from a pyramid, map or graph) and
qualitative explanation (why that pattern exists, using named factors)
within the same answer, since this combination is exactly what
data-response questions on this theme typically require.
