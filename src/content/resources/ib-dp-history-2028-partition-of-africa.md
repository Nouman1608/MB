**HL only.** This guide covers Regional study 5, "European imperialism and the partition of Africa (circa 1840 to 1920)", in the Africa and the Middle East option of IB DP History (first assessment 2028). It is assessed in Paper 3, where you write two 15-mark essays from different regional studies, each evaluating perspectives on an argument. The six inquiry topics are: the growth of European activity in Africa from about 1840; the reasons for "New Imperialism"; the vulnerability of African societies; the causes and impact of the partition; the reasons for resistance and collaboration; and the successes and failures of African responses.

## 1. Growth of European activity in Africa (from circa 1840)

Before 1870 most of Africa was not ruled by Europeans. Europeans held coastal bases, Algeria (taken by France from 1830) and the settler areas of southern Africa. The guide asks you to note four forces.

- **Decline of the Ottoman Empire.** Weak Ottoman control of North Africa let France, Britain and Italy intervene in Algeria, Tunisia, Egypt and Libya. Egypt's debts led to joint European control of its finances in the 1870s.
- **Traders and expansion of commercial activities.** After the abolition of the slave trade, European firms bought palm oil, groundnuts, ivory and later rubber. Merchants from Liverpool, Marseille and Hamburg set up trading posts and lobbied their governments for protection.
- **Explorers and missionaries.** Mungo Park, Richard Burton, John Speke, David Livingstone, Henry Morton Stanley and Pierre de Brazza mapped rivers and routes, and their books inspired the public. David Livingstone urged the combination of "Christianity, commerce and civilisation". Missionary societies opened stations and schools.
- **Technological innovations.** Quinine reduced death from malaria, steamships and river steamers opened the interior, the telegraph improved communication, and breech-loading rifles and later the Maxim machine gun (1884) gave European forces a decisive firepower advantage.

## 2. Reasons for the emergence of "New Imperialism" in Africa

- **Economic factors.** Demand for raw materials, such as rubber, palm oil, cotton, copper and later gold and diamonds, and the search for markets during the long depression of the 1870s and 1880s. J. A. Hobson (1902) and Lenin (1916) argued that capital looking for profitable investment drove empire. Historians today note that most European trade and investment went elsewhere and that Africa did not quickly produce large profits.
- **Strategic factors.** The opening of the Suez Canal in 1869 made Egypt vital to Britain's route to India. After the Urabi revolt, Britain occupied Egypt in 1882, which unsettled the balance in the region. Britain also held the Cape Colony, and its concern for the route to India and the gold-rich Transvaal pushed it northwards. Robinson and Gallagher, in *Africa and the Victorians* (1961), argued that British expansion was largely a response to crises at the periphery, the "official mind" seeking strategic security, rather than an economic drive.
- **Political factors.** National rivalry, especially after German unification in 1871. France looked for compensation for defeat in 1870 and acquired Tunisia in 1881. New states such as Germany and Italy wanted prestige. Governments feared that if they did not claim territory, rivals would.
- **Social factors.** The spread of Christianity and the idea of a "civilising mission", along with racial theories drawn from Social Darwinism. Many Europeans believed Africans needed European guidance.

**Argument to weigh:** "Economic motives were the main cause of New Imperialism in Africa." Support: raw materials, markets, Leopold's profit-making in Congo. Challenge: strategic and political rivalry explain why Britain took Egypt and why France and Germany moved when they did.

## 3. Vulnerability of African societies

- **Military weakness.** African armies were often large and courageous but lacked modern rifles and artillery. At Omdurman in 1898, Kitchener's Anglo-Egyptian force with machine guns and gunboats defeated the Mahdist army with thousands of Sudanese killed and few British dead.
- **Political weakness.** Many states were small or young and depended on the personal power of a ruler. Some had been weakened by earlier wars, drought, disease (the rinderpest epidemic of the 1890s) or the slave trade.
- **Collaboration, including treaties.** European agents negotiated treaties with chiefs, often in languages the chiefs could not read. Rudd's concession from Lobengula in 1888 is an example, and Italy's Treaty of Wuchale (1889) with Menelik II had different Italian and Amharic versions on whether Ethiopia would be a protectorate.
- **Disunity, including internal conflicts.** Europeans used rivalries between peoples and states. For example, rivals of the Asante or Buganda leaders sought British support.

**Argument to weigh:** Africa fell because of technology (support: Maxim gun, quinine) or because of divisions (support: collaborators). Ethiopia, which had modern rifles and a united army under Menelik, shows both.

## 4. Causes and impact of the partition

The **Berlin Conference** (November 1884 to February 1885) was hosted by German Chancellor **Otto von Bismarck**. Fourteen states attended, with no African representative. Its General Act set free trade in the Congo and Niger basins and said that claims on the coast must be backed by "effective occupation". It also recognised Leopold II's claim to the Congo. The conference did not itself divide Africa, but it set rules that encouraged a rush to claim territory.

The **scramble** for annexation followed quickly.

- Belgium: the Congo, first as Leopold's personal Congo Free State, from 1885.
- France: much of West and Equatorial Africa, Tunisia (1881), Madagascar (1896) and Morocco (1912).
- Germany: Togoland, Cameroon, South West Africa and East Africa.
- United Kingdom: Egypt and Sudan, Nigeria, the Gold Coast, East Africa, and southern African territories. The South African War (1899 to 1902) ended with British control of the Boer republics.
- Italy: Eritrea and Somaliland, then Libya (1911 to 1912), but its defeat at Adwa in 1896 prevented control of Ethiopia.
- Portugal: Angola and Mozambique; Britain's ultimatum of 1890 stopped its plan to link them.
- Spain: small areas on the Moroccan and Saharan coast and Rio Muni.

Disputes between the powers were settled by treaty. At Fashoda in 1898, a French expedition met Kitchener's forces on the Nile, and France withdrew. The Anglo-French agreement of 1904 helped to settle claims in Egypt and Morocco, although crises over Morocco in 1905 and 1911 followed. By 1914 only Ethiopia and Liberia were independent. The new borders often cut across peoples and joined rivals, with long-lasting consequences.

## 5. Reasons for resistance and collaboration

- **Political leadership.** Leaders chose to fight or cooperate based on their assessment of their position. Samori Toure of the Wassoulou Empire fought France for about 16 years until his capture in 1898. Menelik II used diplomacy and weapons from Europe. In contrast, leaders such as Khama of the Tswana and Lewanika of Barotseland negotiated for protection and some independence.
- **Military strength.** Those with firearms, cohesion and defendable territory resisted longer.
- **Social factors.** Resistance could draw on religion, as in Mahdist Sudan, or on spiritual beliefs, as in the Maji Maji rising in German East Africa. Collaboration could benefit chiefs, traders and mission-educated Africans.
- **Impact of colonial rule.** Hut and poll taxes, forced labour, land taken for settlers or companies, and, in the Congo, brutal rubber collection. These caused later rebellions, such as the Herero and Nama uprising (1904) in German South West Africa, which was crushed in a campaign that most historians regard as genocide.

## 6. Successes and failures of African responses

- **Military responses.** Adwa (1896) was a rare success for an African army. Most others failed because of weapons gaps, though guerrilla methods prolonged resistance. Maji Maji (1905 to 1907) was crushed with heavy loss of life from famine.
- **Escape and migration.** Some groups moved out of reach, as Samori's followers did, but this was often temporary.
- **Political negotiations and treaties.** They could secure protection or local autonomy, but European texts often took more than the African leader understood.
- **Collaboration.** The Buganda chiefs gained influence in the protectorate. Collaboration offered short-term benefits, but long-term control passed to the colonisers.

## Worked example: a Paper 3 plan

*Question:* "The Berlin Conference was the main cause of the partition of Africa." Evaluate this claim.

1. Define "cause": the conference set a rule (effective occupation) and recognition of claims.
2. For: it legitimised annexation, encouraged rapid claims, and gave Leopold the Congo.
3. Against: the scramble began before 1884 (Egypt 1882, France in Tunisia); technology, rivalries and economic interest drove it; many borders were negotiated afterwards.
4. Perspectives: older accounts blamed the "carving up" at Berlin; revisionists such as Robinson and Gallagher stress African crises and strategy.
5. Judgement: the conference accelerated the partition and gave it rules but did not cause it.

## Common mistakes

- Saying Berlin divided Africa; it did not draw the map.
- Portraying all Africans as passive or all as resisters.
- Ignoring that Ethiopia and Liberia stayed independent.
- Giving long accounts of explorers without a point.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to know all colonial powers?** The guide lists Belgium, France, Germany, the United Kingdom, Italy, Portugal and Spain. Know the main claims of each.

**Where does Bismarck fit?** Host of the conference, and a supporter of limited German colonies to protect trade.
