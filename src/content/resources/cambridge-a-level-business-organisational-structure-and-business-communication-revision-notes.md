## 7.1.1 Objectives and structure

| Attribute of a good structure | Recall |
|---|---|
| Flexibility | Can adapt to change in markets or technology |
| Meets business needs | Fits the objectives, for example low cost or innovation |
| Allows growth and development | Room for new products, sites and staff |
| Encourages intrapreneurship | Staff free to propose and run new ideas inside the firm |

Purpose: to organise people so the business can reach its objectives.

## 7.1.2 Formal structure vocabulary

| Term | One-line definition |
|---|---|
| Levels of hierarchy | Layers of management from top to bottom |
| Chain of command | Path orders follow downwards and reports follow upwards |
| Span of control | Number of subordinates reporting directly to a manager |
| Responsibility | Duty to complete a task |
| Authority | Power to decide and instruct |
| Delegation | Passing authority for a task down |
| Accountability | Having to answer for the result |
| Centralised | Key decisions made at the top |
| Decentralised | Decisions made lower down |

## 7.1.2 Types of structure

| Structure | Key advantage | Key disadvantage |
|---|---|---|
| Functional | Specialist expertise | Departments pursue own aims |
| Narrow (tall) | Close control; promotion steps | Slow, costly communication |
| Flat | Fast communication; lower cost | Overloaded managers |
| Matrix | Skills shared across projects | Two bosses, possible conflict |

### Grouping choices

- Product: separate ranges needing different skills and markets.
- Function: similar work, one main product, specialist efficiency.
- Geographical area: regions or countries with different customers, rules or languages.

### Why structures change

- Growth: more staff and sites outgrow the old design.
- Delayering: removing management layers to cut cost and speed decisions.
- Also: new strategy, technology, mergers.

### Method in steps: span and levels

1. Write the people on each level: 1, then the span, then span squared, and so on.
2. Add the levels until the total reaches the workforce.
3. Count the levels. A wider span means fewer levels.

Reminder: with a span of 4, the first four levels hold 1, 4, 16 and 64 people, a total of 85. With a top post whose span is 6, and six managers each with a span of 8, the three levels hold 1, 6 and 48 people, a total of 55.

### Method in steps: delayering evaluation

1. Annual saving = salaries of removed posts less extra pay for staff given extra duties.
2. One-off cost = redundancy and retraining.
3. Payback = one-off cost divided by annual net saving.
4. Weigh the figures against wider spans, lost experience, morale and communication.

## 7.1.3 Delegation and accountability

- Authority can be delegated; ultimate accountability stays with the delegator.
- Accountability processes: reporting lines, targets and indicators, appraisal, reports, meetings, audits, rewards and sanctions.
- Positive impact: frees managers, develops and motivates staff, faster local decisions.
- Negative impact: errors, inconsistency, training cost, loss of control.

## 7.1.4 Control, authority and trust

| Distinction | Recall |
|---|---|
| Span versus levels | For a given workforce, wider span means fewer levels |
| Authority versus responsibility | Power to decide versus duty to do |
| Control versus trust | Checking everything wastes delegation; checking nothing risks error |

Resolve the conflict by clear targets and limits, training, gradual delegation and review of results.

## 7.1.5 Centralisation and decentralisation

| | Centralised | Decentralised |
|---|---|---|
| Plus | Consistency, control, scale economies | Speed, local knowledge, motivation |
| Minus | Slow, overloaded top, ignores local needs | Inconsistency, duplication, weaker control |

Typical judgement: it depends on how different local markets are, how skilled local managers are and the cost of a mistake.

## 7.1.6 Line and staff

- Line: directly produces or sells (operations, sales); has authority in the chain of command.
- Staff: supports and advises (human resources, finance, IT, legal).
- Conflicts: standard policy versus freedom to hit targets; advice from people without operational experience; competition for budgets; resentment of delays.

## 7.2.1 and 7.2.2 Purposes and methods

Essential when: giving instructions, sharing targets, motivating, consulting, coordinating, dealing with customers and suppliers, crises, and change.

| Method | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Spoken | Immediate feedback | No record |
| Written | Permanent record | Slow, no instant feedback |
| Electronic | Fast, wide reach | Overload, impersonal |
| Visual | Quick for data | Can oversimplify |

## 7.2.3 Channels

| Pair | Meaning |
|---|---|
| One-way | Sender to receiver, no feedback |
| Two-way | Feedback and response |
| Vertical | Up and down the hierarchy |
| Horizontal | Between people at the same level |

Problems: delay and distortion through many levels; no check of understanding in one-way messages; missing horizontal links; overload.

## 7.2.4 and 7.2.5 Barriers and management's role

| Barrier | Remedy |
|---|---|
| Jargon | Plain language |
| Wrong method | Match method to message |
| Overload | Prioritise and summarise |
| Distance | Scheduled video calls |
| Mistrust | Honest, regular updates |
| Many levels | Delayering, direct briefings |

- Informal communication: speeds information and shows concerns, but spreads rumour.
- Efficiency link: fewer errors, quicker decisions, better coordination, higher motivation.
- Improvements: two-way channels, team briefings with checks on understanding, training, acting on feedback.

## Must-know distinctions

- Delegation versus decentralisation: delegation passes a task and its authority to one subordinate; decentralisation spreads decision-making across the whole organisation as a policy.
- Authority versus responsibility: a team leader told to cut waste (responsibility) but not allowed to change suppliers (no authority) is set up to fail.
- Staff function versus line function: a payroll team advises and supports; a production manager has authority over the shop floor.
- Formal versus informal communication: a team briefing follows the chain of command; a chat at the coffee machine does not.
- One-way versus two-way: a memo is one-way; a team meeting with questions is two-way.

## Using the command words

Cambridge lists command words including Define, Identify, Explain, Calculate, Analyse, Assess, Evaluate, Justify and Advise. Match your answer to the word.

| Command word | What to write for this topic |
|---|---|
| Define | A precise meaning of a term such as span of control |
| Identify | Name the feature, barrier or method, with no explanation needed |
| Explain | A reason or relationship, linked to the case |
| Calculate | Working with the figures given, then the answer |
| Analyse | A developed chain of cause and effect, for example wider span to less supervision to errors |
| Assess, Evaluate, Justify | Both sides, applied to the case, ending in a reasoned judgement |
| Advise | A suggested course of action with reasons |

## Evaluation prompts

Use these to turn description into judgement.

- Size and growth: is the business small enough for one decision-maker, or large enough to need layers?
- Skills: do local managers have the skill to take the decisions delegated to them?
- Cost of mistakes: where errors are costly, tighter control may be justified.
- Time frame: delayering may cut costs quickly, while the effect on morale and communication may take longer to show.
- Context: the best structure or method depends on the business, the message and the people involved.

## Quick self-test

1. Define span of control.
2. State what happens to the number of levels if the span is widened.
3. Give one disadvantage of a matrix structure.
4. Calculate the people in a hierarchy of four levels where every manager has a span of 3, including the top post.
5. Does a manager who delegates a task stay accountable for it?
6. Give one reason a business might use a geographical structure.
7. Name one conflict between line and staff functions.
8. Give one weakness of written communication.
9. Distinguish vertical from horizontal communication.
10. Give one benefit and one risk of informal communication.

### Answers

1. The number of subordinates reporting directly to one manager.
2. The number of levels falls.
3. Staff answer to two managers, which can cause conflict or confusion.
4. 1 + 3 + 9 + 27 = 40 people.
5. Yes; authority is passed down but accountability stays with the delegator.
6. Customers, rules or languages differ by region, so local managers need to respond.
7. Staff specialists want standard policy; line managers want freedom to meet targets.
8. It is slow and gives no instant feedback.
9. Vertical runs up and down the hierarchy; horizontal runs between people at the same level.
10. Benefit: quick information. Risk: rumour spreading.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a type of structure without applying it to the business in the case.
- Treating accountability as something that transfers fully to the subordinate.
- Confusing authority with responsibility.
- Writing that flat structures are always best, with no judgement about context.
- Listing barriers to communication without any remedy.
- Giving advantages and disadvantages of a method but never choosing one for the situation.
- Calculating a delayering saving and not weighing it against non-financial effects.
- Ignoring the difference between formal and informal channels.
