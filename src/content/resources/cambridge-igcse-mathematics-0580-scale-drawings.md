## Scales in two formats

A scale is written either as a **statement** ("1 cm represents 5 m") or as a **ratio** ("1 : 500"). A ratio has no units because both parts are in the same unit. To convert a statement to a ratio, write both parts in centimetres.

```
1 cm represents 5 m      5 m = 500 cm          1 : 500
1 cm represents 2.5 km   2.5 km = 250 000 cm   1 : 250 000
1 : 20 000               20 000 cm = 200 m     1 cm represents 200 m
```

Unit facts you need: 1 m = 100 cm, 1 km = 1000 m = 100 000 cm.

**Drawing to real: multiply by the scale. Real to drawing: divide.** Then convert units once, at the end.

### Worked example 1: reading a plan

A house plan is drawn to a scale of 1 : 200. A room measures 4.5 cm by 3.2 cm on the plan.

(a) Find the real dimensions of the room. (b) A doorway is 0.9 m wide. How wide is it on the plan?

```
(a) 4.5 x 200 = 900 cm = 9 m
    3.2 x 200 = 640 cm = 6.4 m
    The room is 9 m by 6.4 m.
(b) 0.9 m = 90 cm;  90 / 200 = 0.45 cm  (4.5 mm)
```

## Choosing a scale

When you must choose the scale yourself, it should make the drawing as large as the space allows while keeping the arithmetic easy.

### Worked example 2

A rectangular field is 84 m long and 56 m wide. It must be drawn in a space 20 cm wide and 15 cm high. Choose a suitable scale.

```
Try 1 cm to 4 m:  84 / 4 = 21 cm   too wide for 20 cm
Try 1 cm to 5 m:  84 / 5 = 16.8 cm, 56 / 5 = 11.2 cm   fits
Scale: 1 cm represents 5 m  (1 : 500)
```

A scale of 1 cm to 10 m would also fit, but the drawing would be only 8.4 cm long, so measurements from it would be less accurate.

## Three-figure bearings

A bearing describes a direction by the angle measured **clockwise from north**, written with three figures: 7° is 007°, 45° is 045°. The four compass directions are:

| Direction | Bearing |
|---|---|
| North | 000° |
| East | 090° |
| South | 180° |
| West | 270° |

"D is **due east** of C" means the bearing of D from C is exactly 090°.

Always draw a **north line** at the point you are measuring **from** before you measure. The word after "from" names that point.

### Why reverse bearings differ by 180°

North lines at two different points are parallel. Suppose the bearing of B from A is θ, less than 180°, so B is east of A. At B, the angle between the north line and BA is 180° – θ, because it and θ are co-interior angles between the parallel north lines. That angle is measured anticlockwise from north, since A is west of B, so the clockwise bearing of A from B is 360° – (180° – θ) = θ + 180°. When θ is 180° or more, the same argument gives θ – 180°.

For the syllabus example, the bearing of B from A is 025°, so the bearing of A from B is 025° + 180° = **205°**.

### Worked example 3: due south, then due west

Q is 5 km due south of P. R is 5 km due west of Q. Find the bearing of R from P and of P from R.

```
PQ points south, QR points west, so angle PQR = 90 degrees.
PQ = QR, so triangle PQR is isosceles: angle QPR = 45 degrees.
From P, south is 180 degrees; R is a further 45 degrees round
(clockwise, towards west).
Bearing of R from P = 180 + 45 = 225 degrees
Bearing of P from R = 225 - 180 = 045 degrees
```

Sketch first: a quick diagram with north arrows shows that R is south-west of P, so a bearing between 180° and 270° is expected.

### Angles given from other directions

Diagrams sometimes give an angle measured from south or west. Convert to a clockwise angle from north:

- 35° **west of south**: start at south (180°) and turn 35° further clockwise: **215°**.
- 20° **north of west**: west is 270°; turning towards north is clockwise: **290°**.
- 50° **east of south**: from south, turning towards east is anticlockwise: 180° – 50° = **130°**.

## Fixing a point from two bearings

If you know the bearing of a point from two places, draw a line on each bearing; the point is where the lines cross.

### Worked example 4

Lighthouse B is 9 km due east of lighthouse A. A ship S is on a bearing of 040° from A and on a bearing of 310° from B. Using a scale of 1 cm to 1 km, find by scale drawing the distance of the ship from each lighthouse.

```
1. Draw AB = 9 cm, horizontal, with B to the right (east).
2. North line at A; measure 40 degrees clockwise; draw a long line.
3. North line at B; 310 degrees = 360 - 50, so measure 50 degrees
   anticlockwise from north; draw a long line.
4. The lines cross at S.
5. Measure: AS is about 5.8 cm, BS about 6.9 cm.
   So the ship is about 5.8 km from A and 6.9 km from B.
```

Check: at A the angle between AB (east, 090°) and AS (040°) is 50°. At B the bearing of A is 270°, so the angle between BA and BS is 310° – 270° = 40°. The angles of triangle ASB are 50°, 40° and 90°, which is a useful check on the drawing; trigonometry then gives 5.79 km and 6.89 km (right-angled trigonometry, subtopic 6.2).

## Common mistakes

- Measuring anticlockwise, or from east instead of north.
- Measuring from the wrong point: "bearing of A from B" is measured at B.
- Writing two-figure bearings, such as 45° instead of 045°.
- Adding 180° to a bearing that is already 180° or more, giving an answer over 360°.
- Converting units twice, or not at all, in scale questions.
- Drawing north lines that are not parallel to each other (and to the edge of the page, if north is up).

## Exam technique

- **Measure** a bearing: draw the north line, then measure clockwise; give three figures.
- **Find** a reverse bearing without measuring: add or subtract 180°.
- **Draw** a scale drawing: ruler for every line, label lengths and points.
- **Interpret**: measure on the drawing in centimetres, then convert with the scale, giving units.
- A quick sketch with compass directions marked tells you roughly what answer to expect.
