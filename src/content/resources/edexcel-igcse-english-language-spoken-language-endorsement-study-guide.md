The Spoken Language Endorsement is an optional extra in Pearson Edexcel International GCSE English Language A. It is not an exam paper and it does not alter the grade you receive for the written components. Instead it gives separate recognition of your ability to speak in a formal situation. Because it is optional and assessed by your school, you should ask your teacher exactly how your centre runs it. What follows teaches the underlying skills that any such assessment rewards: choosing and planning a topic, delivering with control, responding to questions and using formal English.

## Where this fits in the specification

The 4EA1 qualification is assessed mainly through written work. The endorsement sits beside those components as an additional, separately reported outcome. For this reason it is easy to neglect, but the skills overlap strongly with the written papers: structuring an argument, choosing precise vocabulary, adapting to an audience and purpose, and being able to explain and defend ideas. Time spent on spoken work often improves the clarity of your writing too.

## Choosing and planning a topic

A good topic is one on which you have an opinion, some knowledge and access to examples. Strong topics usually have two sides, so that you can show reasoning rather than just describe. Examples include whether schools should start later, whether students should learn a second language from an early age, or how a local tradition has changed in recent years.

Plan in this order:

1. **Purpose**: decide whether you intend to inform, explain, argue or persuade.
2. **Audience**: think about who is listening and what they already know.
3. **Main message**: write it as a single sentence.
4. **Structure**: an opening, two to four developed points and a conclusion.
5. **Evidence**: facts you are certain of, a personal example, and a short anecdote. Do not use statistics you cannot verify.
6. **Notes**: key words on cards, not a script.

## Delivery

Delivery is how the message is carried by your voice and body. Aim for:

- **Pace**: a steady rate, slowing on important points. Nerves usually make people speed up.
- **Pause**: a short silence before and after a key idea gives the audience time to think.
- **Volume and tone**: loud enough to reach the back of the room, with variation to avoid a monotone.
- **Eye contact**: look around the room and not at your notes or the floor.
- **Posture and gesture**: stand upright, keep gestures purposeful and avoid fidgeting.
- **Engagement**: use direct address ("Imagine that you..."), rhetorical questions and short examples to hold attention.

## Responding to questions

Questions after the talk show whether you understand your own subject and can think on your feet. Listen to the whole question, pause, then answer what has been asked. A strong response gives a direct answer first, then a reason or example. If you do not know, say so honestly and offer what you do know, instead of bluffing. If a questioner disagrees, acknowledge the point politely ("That is a fair concern") and then explain your own view. It helps to predict a few likely questions in advance and practise answering them aloud.

## Formal English

In a formal setting you should use language that is clear, accurate and suited to an audience beyond friends. This means:

- grammar that is accurate and consistent, for example subject and verb agreeing, with careful tense choices;
- varied sentence length, with some longer sentences for explanation and short ones for emphasis;
- precise vocabulary instead of vague words such as "thing" or "stuff";
- avoiding slang, text-speak and overused fillers such as "like", "you know" and "basically";
- an appropriate tone: respectful and confident, not casual or aggressive.

Formal English does not require you to hide your accent. It concerns choice of words and grammar, not pronunciation.

## Worked example: timing and structure

You must prepare a five-minute talk on why local libraries still matter. At a comfortable speaking pace of about 130 words a minute, a full script would be 650 words (130 x 5), which is too long to memorise, so you plan with notes and rehearse with a timer.

Plan the minutes:

- Opening, 40 seconds: a question to the audience and the main claim.
- Point 1, 80 seconds: libraries give free access to books and quiet space for study.
- Point 2, 80 seconds: they provide community services such as computer access and local events.
- Point 3, 70 seconds: a counter-argument that books are now online, and your answer that not everyone has reliable internet.
- Conclusion, 30 seconds: return to the opening question and restate the claim.

Total: 40 + 80 + 80 + 70 + 30 = 300 seconds, which is exactly 5 minutes. After rehearsal you may find that point 2 runs over. If so, remove one example rather than speak faster. Then prepare answers to three possible questions, for example "Would you pay more tax to keep libraries open?"

## Common mistakes

- Choosing a topic with only one possible view, which leaves little to discuss when answering questions.
- Writing out and reading a full script.
- Rushing, particularly in the opening and the conclusion.
- Giving short answers such as "yes" or "I don't know" in the questions.
- Overusing filler words and slang.
- Ignoring the audience, for example by speaking only to the teacher or to a screen.
- Failing to rehearse with a timer, so that the talk is too short or too long.

## Technique tips

- Practise the first and last 30 seconds until they are fluent, since these are what the audience remembers.
- Record yourself and listen for fillers, pace and tone.
- Have a plan for a nervous moment: pause, breathe, look at your notes, continue.
- Link your points with signposts such as "firstly", "in contrast" and "for this reason".
- Reflect on feedback after a practice talk and change one thing each time.

## Check your understanding

1. Does the endorsement change your IGCSE English Language grade? Answer: no, it is optional and reported separately.
2. Name two skills assessed in spoken language. Answer: for example presenting ideas clearly, responding to questions, and using formal English.
3. Why is a topic with two sides useful? Answer: it lets you give reasons, consider a counter-argument and answer challenging questions.
4. Give two ways to improve delivery. Answer: for example slow down, use pauses, make eye contact, vary tone.
5. What should you do if you do not know the answer to a question? Answer: say so honestly and offer related knowledge instead of making something up.
6. Is formal English the same as a particular accent? Answer: no, it concerns grammar and vocabulary and not pronunciation.
7. A talk of 3 minutes at about 130 words per minute is roughly how many words? Answer: 130 x 3 = 390 words, though notes should be used rather than a script.
