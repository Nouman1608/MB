## What the specification asks for

- A single covalent bond contains a shared pair of electrons.
- Multiple bonds contain multiple pairs of electrons.
- A co-ordinate (dative covalent) bond contains a shared pair of electrons with both electrons supplied by one atom.
- You should be able to represent a covalent bond using a line and a co-ordinate bond using an arrow.

## The covalent bond

A **covalent bond** is a shared pair of electrons between two atoms. The bond holds the atoms together because both positively charged nuclei are attracted to the negatively charged shared pair between them. That attraction, not the sharing on its own, is what makes the bond strong.

Covalent bonds form between non-metal atoms, where transferring electrons completely would cost too much energy. Each atom usually supplies one electron to each shared pair.

### Lone pairs and bonding pairs

- A **bonding pair** is shared between two atoms.
- A **lone pair** is a pair of outer electrons on one atom that is not used in bonding.

In a water molecule the oxygen atom has two bonding pairs (one with each H) and two lone pairs. Lone pairs matter here because they are what an atom uses to form a co-ordinate bond; in subtopic 3.1.3.5 they also affect molecular shape.

### Dot-and-cross diagrams for simple molecules

Show the outer electrons of each atom, using dots for one atom and crosses for the other, with shared pairs placed between the atoms.

| Molecule | Bonding pairs on central atom | Lone pairs on central atom |
|---|---|---|
| CH₄ | 4 | 0 |
| NH₃ | 3 | 1 |
| H₂O | 2 | 2 |
| HCl | 1 (on Cl) | 3 (on Cl) |

Hydrogen has no lone pairs: its single electron is used in its one bond, giving it two electrons, like helium.

## Multiple bonds

Atoms can share more than one pair of electrons:

| Bond | Shared pairs | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Single | 1 | H–H, C–H, Cl–Cl |
| Double | 2 | O=O in oxygen; C=O in carbon dioxide; C=C in ethene |
| Triple | 3 | N≡N in nitrogen; C≡C in ethyne |

**Carbon dioxide** has two C=O double bonds. Carbon shares two of its four outer electrons with each oxygen atom, and each oxygen shares two of its six. Each oxygen keeps two lone pairs, and carbon has none.

**Nitrogen**, N₂, has a triple bond: each nitrogen atom shares three of its five outer electrons and keeps one lone pair.

## Representing bonds with lines

In a displayed formula, **each line represents one shared pair**: a single line for a single bond, two for a double bond and three for a triple bond. Lone pairs are not shown by lines; when a question asks for them, they are drawn as pairs of dots or as short bars next to the atom.

## Co-ordinate (dative covalent) bonds

A **co-ordinate bond** is a shared pair of electrons in which **both electrons come from the same atom**. Two conditions must be met:

1. the **donor** atom has a lone pair
2. the **acceptor** atom has an empty orbital in its outer shell, so it can accept a pair.

A co-ordinate bond is shown by an **arrow pointing from the atom that donates the pair to the atom that accepts it**.

### Key examples

| Species | Donor | Acceptor | How the bond forms |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ammonium ion, NH₄⁺ | N in NH₃ | H⁺ | NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺ |
| Oxonium ion, H₃O⁺ | O in H₂O | H⁺ | H₂O + H⁺ → H₃O⁺ |
| H₃N→BF₃ | N in NH₃ | B in BF₃ | Boron has only six outer electrons in BF₃ and an empty orbital |
| Al₂Cl₆ | Cl in one AlCl₃ unit | Al in the other unit | Two AlCl₃ units join by two Cl→Al bonds |
| Carbon monoxide, CO | O | C | Two shared pairs plus one co-ordinate bond from O to C |

### Worked example 1: the ammonium ion

**Question.** Explain how an ammonium ion forms from an ammonia molecule and a hydrogen ion, and describe the four N–H bonds in the ion.

**Answer.**

1. The nitrogen atom in NH₃ has a lone pair.
2. An H⁺ ion has no electrons and an empty 1s orbital.
3. The nitrogen donates its lone pair to the H⁺, forming a co-ordinate bond, N→H.
4. The ion has an overall 1+ charge, carried by the whole ion.
5. Once formed, the co-ordinate bond is **identical to the other three N–H bonds**: the electrons in a shared pair do not "remember" where they came from. All four bonds have the same length and strength.

### Worked example 2: annotated model answer

**Question.** Boron trifluoride reacts with ammonia to form H₃NBF₃. Explain how the bond between nitrogen and boron forms and how it would be represented. (3 marks)

**Model answer.** "The nitrogen atom in NH₃ has a lone pair of electrons ✔. In BF₃, boron has only six electrons in its outer shell, so it has an empty orbital that can accept a pair ✔. Nitrogen donates its lone pair to boron, forming a co-ordinate bond, shown by an arrow from N to B: H₃N→BF₃ ✔."

**Why it scores.** It identifies the donor, explains why the acceptor can accept, and gives the arrow direction.

## Common mistakes

- Defining a covalent bond as "atoms sharing electrons" without "a pair".
- Saying a co-ordinate bond is weaker or different from an ordinary covalent bond once it has formed.
- Drawing the arrow from the acceptor to the donor.
- Forgetting the charge on NH₄⁺ or H₃O⁺, or putting the charge on a single atom.
- In dot-and-cross diagrams, showing the co-ordinate pair as one dot and one cross; both electrons must be the donor's symbol.
- Giving hydrogen a lone pair, or giving oxygen in water only one lone pair.
- Using one line for a double bond.
- Claiming BF₃ has a full outer shell on boron.

## Exam technique for OxfordAQA command words

- **State** or **Define**: "a shared pair of electrons"; for co-ordinate bonds add "with both electrons supplied by one atom".
- **Draw**: use dots and crosses consistently; show all outer electrons, and include the overall charge of an ion outside square brackets.
- **Explain**: for a co-ordinate bond, name the donor's lone pair and the acceptor's empty orbital.
- **Represent** or **Show**: a line for each shared pair and an arrow for each co-ordinate bond, pointing to the acceptor.
- **Suggest**: in unfamiliar species, look for a lone pair next to an atom with fewer than eight outer electrons, or an H⁺.
