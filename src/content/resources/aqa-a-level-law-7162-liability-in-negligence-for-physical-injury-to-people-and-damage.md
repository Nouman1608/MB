This guide covers the section 3.3 row **Liability in negligence for physical injury to people and damage to property** in AQA A-level Law (7162). The specification lists three elements: "Duty of care: the 'neighbour' principle; the Caparo three-part test", "Breach of duty: the objective standard of care" and "Damage: factual causation and legal causation (remoteness of damage)". The [Tort cases, defences and remedies study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-tort-cases-defences-and-remedies-study-guide/) gives the core authorities. This page goes further into how each element is argued on difficult facts, with additional cases and a full worked problem.

## The claim in outline

The claimant must prove, on the balance of probabilities, that:

1. the defendant owed them a **duty of care**;
2. the defendant **breached** that duty by falling below the standard of the reasonable person; and
3. the breach **caused** damage of a kind that was **not too remote**.

Physical injury and property damage are the "core" kinds of harm in negligence: once duty, breach and causation are shown, the law compensates them readily, unlike pure economic loss or psychiatric injury.

## Duty of care

### The neighbour principle

In *Donoghue v Stevenson* (1932) the claimant's friend bought her a bottle of ginger beer in a café; she alleged it contained the remains of a decomposed snail and that she became ill. She had no contract with the manufacturer, but the House of Lords held that a manufacturer owes a duty to the ultimate consumer. Lord Atkin's statement is the root of the modern law: you must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your **neighbour**, meaning persons so closely and directly affected by your act that you ought reasonably to have them in contemplation.

### The Caparo test and incremental development

*Caparo Industries v Dickman* (1990) set out three questions: was the damage reasonably foreseeable; was there a relationship of proximity; and is it fair, just and reasonable to impose a duty? *Robinson v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire* (2018) clarified how to use it.

- **Established categories** (road users to each other, doctor to patient, employer to employee, manufacturer to consumer) need no fresh test. State the category and an authority.
- **Novel situations** are decided incrementally, by analogy with existing cases, with the three questions as a guide.

Cases illustrating each question:

| Question | Case | Point |
|---|---|---|
| Foreseeability of the claimant | *Bourhill v Young* (1943) | A woman who heard a crash from a distance was not someone the careless motorcyclist could foresee being affected |
| Proximity | *Kent v Griffiths* (2000) | Once the ambulance service accepted the 999 call, it owed a duty to the named patient |
| Fair, just and reasonable | *Hill v CC West Yorkshire* (1989) | No duty owed by police to an unidentified future victim |
| Positive act, not omission | *Robinson* (2018) | Officers who knocked over a pedestrian during an arrest owed a duty |

In an exam, most physical injury problems involve an established duty. Spending a paragraph on *Caparo* for a driver who hits a pedestrian wastes time and suggests you have not read *Robinson*.

## Breach of duty

### The objective standard

The defendant is judged against the reasonable person in their position (*Blyth v Birmingham Waterworks*, 1856). The question is not whether this defendant did their best, but whether a reasonable person would have acted differently.

**Special standards**
- **Learners** meet the standard of the competent practitioner (*Nettleship v Weston*, 1971).
- **Children** are judged against a reasonable child of the same age. In *Orchard v Lee* (2009) a 13-year-old playing tag who ran into a lunchtime supervisor was not in breach: he was doing what children of that age normally do, without carelessness beyond the game.
- **Amateurs doing skilled tasks** meet the standard of a reasonable amateur doing that job. In *Wells v Cooper* (1958) a householder who fitted a door handle that came off was judged as a reasonably competent amateur carpenter, not a professional, and was not in breach.
- **Professionals** are not in breach if they acted in line with a responsible body of professional opinion (*Bolam*, 1957), provided that opinion is capable of withstanding logical analysis (*Bolitho*, 1997).
- **State of knowledge** is judged at the time. In *Roe v Minister of Health* (1954) patients were paralysed by contaminated anaesthetic; the risk of invisible cracks in the ampoules was unknown at the time, so there was no breach.

### Balancing the risk factors

The court weighs four factors together. Additional cases beyond the headline ones:

- **Foreseeable vulnerability.** In *Haley v London Electricity Board* (1964) workmen left a hole in a pavement guarded only by a hammer; a blind man tripped and was injured. Blind people walking alone were foreseeable, so the precautions were inadequate.
- **Social utility.** In *Daborn v Bath Tramways* (1946) using a left-hand-drive ambulance in wartime was justified by its purpose.
- **Cost against risk.** The question is proportion. A cheap precaution against a serious risk will be expected; an extravagant precaution against a small risk will not.

## Damage: causation and remoteness

### Factual causation

The "but for" test asks whether the harm would have occurred but for the breach. In *McWilliams v Sir William Arrol* (1962) an employer failed to provide a safety harness, but evidence showed the worker would not have worn one, so the failure did not cause his death.

### Breaking the chain

An intervening act can make the defendant's breach no longer a cause in law. In *Knightley v Johns* (1982) the defendant caused a crash in a tunnel; a police inspector then negligently ordered an officer to ride the wrong way through the tunnel, and the officer was injured. The inspector's negligence was a new intervening act, so the original driver was not liable for that injury.

### Remoteness of damage

The defendant is liable only for damage of a **reasonably foreseeable type** (*The Wagon Mound (No 1)*, 1961, where oil spilled in a harbour later ignited and damaged a wharf, but fire damage was not foreseeable from oil on water). The finer rules:

- **Type, not manner or extent:** in *Hughes v Lord Advocate* (1963) burns from an unforeseeable explosion of a paraffin lamp were recoverable, because burns were foreseeable. In *Jolley v Sutton LBC* (2000) injury to a boy jacking up an abandoned boat was within the foreseeable type of harm from children meddling with it.
- **A different type is too remote:** in *Doughty v Turner Manufacturing* (1964) an asbestos cover slid into molten metal and an unexpected chemical eruption burned a worker. A splash was foreseeable; an eruption was a different kind of risk, so the claim failed.
- **Foreseeable category of harm:** in *Bradford v Robinson Rentals* (1967) a driver sent on a long journey in an unheated van in severe weather suffered frostbite. Injury from cold was foreseeable, so frostbite was recoverable even though unusual.
- **Thin skull rule:** once some harm is foreseeable, the defendant takes the claimant as found (*Smith v Leech Brain*, 1962).

### Property damage

The same three stages apply. The Wagon Mound itself was a property claim. A driver who carelessly crashes into a parked car, a builder whose falling scaffolding dents a neighbour's roof, or a contractor whose spark sets fire to a shed all face the ordinary rules: established duty, objective breach, causation and foreseeable type of damage.

## Worked problem

*Sana, a newly qualified electrician, rewires a café. To save time she leaves a live cable exposed behind a counter, where an approved code of practice says cables must be covered. Theo, a waiter, brushes against it and receives a shock. Because Theo has an unusual heart condition, the shock causes a cardiac arrest. Sparks from the cable also scorch the café's fridge.*

```
Duty:      Electrician to people on premises and to property owner -
           established duty (foreseeable physical harm from careless work;
           Donoghue). No need for Caparo (Robinson).
Breach:    Objective standard of a competent electrician, regardless of
           inexperience (Nettleship; Bolam). Departing from the approved
           practice is strong evidence of breach. Risk serious (shock),
           precaution cheap (cover the cable): breach likely.
Causation: But for the exposed cable, no shock (Barnett test satisfied).
           No intervening act.
Remoteness: Electric shock injury foreseeable. Cardiac arrest from a heart
           condition - thin skull rule (Smith v Leech Brain): Sana liable
           for full extent. Scorched fridge - fire/heat damage from an
           exposed cable is a foreseeable type (Wagon Mound).
Conclusion: Sana is likely liable for Theo's injuries and the fridge.
```

Each step names the rule, gives the authority and applies the facts. Notice that inexperience is dealt with at breach and does not lower the standard.

## Common mistakes

- Running all three *Caparo* stages for a road accident or a doctor's mistake.
- Saying a defendant is not in breach because they "tried their best".
- Confusing remoteness with factual causation: "but for" is about whether the breach made a difference; remoteness is about whether the type of harm was foreseeable.
- Applying the thin skull rule before establishing that some harm was foreseeable.
- Forgetting property damage when the facts include it.

## Exam technique

In Paper 2 problem questions, use a heading for each element and say which elements are in real doubt; spend your time there. For "explain" questions on a single element, give the rule, a leading case and a contrasting case (for example *Hughes* and *Doughty* on remoteness). For evaluation, link to the theory of tort: the objective standard and the *Caparo* policy limb are named in the specification.
