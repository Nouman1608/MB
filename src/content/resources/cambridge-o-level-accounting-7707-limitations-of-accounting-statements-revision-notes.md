These notes condense section **6.5 Limitations of accounting statements** of the Cambridge O Level Accounting 7707 syllabus for 2027 to 2029. The syllabus names three factors: "historic cost", "application of accounting policies" and "non-financial aspects for example, skill of the workforce, location of the business, economic climate". The worked figures are in the [limitations study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-limitations-of-accounting-statements/); test yourself with the [limitations practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-limitations-of-accounting-statements-practice/). Figures are invented and in dollars.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Historic cost | Recording assets at their original cost (less depreciation), not current value |
| Current value | What an asset could be sold for, or would cost to replace, today |
| Accounting policy | A method a business chooses to prepare its statements, such as its depreciation method |
| Estimate | A judgement used in the accounts, such as an asset's useful life or the allowance percentage |
| Non-financial aspect | A factor affecting the business that cannot be measured reliably in money |
| Money measurement | The concept that only items measurable in money are recorded |
| Economic climate | General business conditions, such as recession, rising prices or interest rates |

## Historic cost: what it hides

- Assets bought long ago appear far below their current value, so the statement of financial position understates the business.
- Low book values make capital employed small and **ROCE look higher** than for a business with recently bought assets.
- Over time, rising prices make revenue grow even when the amount sold does not; money figures from different years are not directly comparable.
- Businesses that bought similar assets at different times cannot be compared fairly.

## Accounting policies: same trading, different figures

| Choice | Typical effect |
|---|---|
| Depreciation method and rate | Changes profit and asset values |
| Useful life estimate | Longer life, lower yearly charge, higher profit |
| Inventory valuation | Changes gross profit this year and next |
| Allowance for irrecoverable debts % | Changes profit and net trade receivables |
| Limit for treating small items as expenses | Changes expenses and non-current assets |

Consistency makes one business's trend reliable, but two businesses may still use different policies. A change of policy can move profit with no change in trading.

## Non-financial aspects

The syllabus examples:

- **Skill of the workforce**: trained, experienced staff are not an asset in the statements.
- **Location**: the prospects of a site, such as passing trade or a new competitor nearby, are not recorded.
- **Economic climate**: recession, rising prices or interest rates will affect future results, but last year's statements do not show this.

Others: reputation, customer loyalty, competition, staff morale, environmental impact.

## Statements are also

- **Historical**: they report the past, while users make decisions about the future.
- **Summarised**: totals hide detail, such as which products made losses.
- **At one date**: the statement of financial position may not be typical of the rest of the year.

## Effect on the ratios

| Limitation | Ratios affected | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Old assets at historic cost | ROCE | Looks higher, because capital employed is understated |
| Rising prices over several years | Revenue and profit trends | Growth looks larger than the real increase in business done |
| Higher depreciation rate or larger allowance | Profit margin, ROCE, current ratio | Lower profit; lower asset values |
| Lower inventory valuation | Gross profit margin, current and acid test gap | Lower gross profit this year, higher next year |
| Non-financial aspects | None directly | Ratios cannot show staff skill, location or the economic outlook |

## Exam traps

- Explain what a limitation means for the user's decision; naming it is not enough.
- Historic cost understates assets and **overstates ROCE**, not the reverse.
- Give a specific policy (for example the depreciation rate) and its effect on profit.
- Link non-financial aspects to the business in the question.
- Limitations are not errors; the statements are still useful for actual revenue, expenses and balances.

## Self-test

1. Name the three factors the syllabus gives as causing limitations.
2. Explain what is meant by the historic cost limitation.
3. Premises have a book value of $80,000 and a current value of $200,000. By how much does the statement of financial position understate them?
4. Profit before interest is $24,000 and capital employed is $120,000. If the premises in question 3 were shown at current value, what would ROCE be?
5. Revenue rose by 5% while selling prices rose by 7%. Did the amount sold rise?
6. State two accounting policy choices that affect profit.
7. Trade receivables are $45,000. Calculate the difference in profit between an allowance of 3% and one of 4% (first year of the allowance).
8. Why does the consistency concept not remove the policy problem when comparing two businesses?
9. State the three non-financial aspects named in the syllabus.
10. Explain how the economic climate limits the usefulness of last year's statements.
11. Which concept explains why the skill of the workforce is not recorded?
12. State one thing financial statements do show reliably.

### Answers

1. Historic cost; application of accounting policies; non-financial aspects.
2. Assets are recorded at their original cost less depreciation, so the statements do not show their current value.
3. 200,000 - 80,000 = **$120,000**.
4. Capital employed = 120,000 + 120,000 = $240,000; ROCE = 24,000 / 240,000 x 100 = **10.00%** (down from 20.00%).
5. No: 1.05 / 1.07 = 0.98, so the amount sold fell by about **2%**.
6. Any two: depreciation method or rate; useful life; inventory valuation; allowance for irrecoverable debts percentage; limit for treating items as expenses.
7. 3%: $1,350; 4%: $1,800; difference **$450**.
8. Consistency applies within one business over time; it does not make two businesses choose the same policies.
9. Skill of the workforce; location of the business; economic climate.
10. The statements show past results; a recession, rising prices or higher interest rates may change future sales and costs, so past figures may not predict future performance.
11. Money measurement.
12. Any one: the actual revenue and expenses recorded for the year; the amounts owed to and by the business at the year end; cash and bank balances.
