All countries, places, businesses and data below are fictional.

## Questions

**1.** Give the meaning of the term *invisible imports* and give one example. **[2]**

**2.** Outline what is meant by debt relief and name one debt relief programme. **[3]**

**3.** The table shows export earnings for Kavira, a fictional country, in US$ million.

| Export | 2015 | 2024 |
|---|---|---|
| Copper ore | 2,400 | 2,100 |
| Coffee | 300 | 350 |
| Cut flowers | 150 | 450 |
| Manufactured goods | 250 | 600 |
| Tourism (invisible) | 400 | 900 |
| **Total** | **3,500** | **4,400** |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of Kavira's total export earnings that came from copper ore in 2015 and in 2024. **[2]**
**(b)** Using the table, suggest two reasons why the change between 2015 and 2024 may reduce Kavira's economic risk. **[4]**

**4.** Explain how resource endowment and colonial ties can each influence a country's pattern of trade. **[4]**

**5.** The Lomaro Coffee Cooperative is a fictional group of farmers. It sells 30% of its coffee under a certification scheme with a guaranteed minimum price of 2.00 per kg; buyers pay the minimum or the market price, whichever is higher. The rest is sold at the market price. All figures are fictional.

| Year | 2020 | 2021 | 2022 | 2023 | 2024 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Market price per kg | 1.70 | 2.30 | 2.60 | 1.90 | 1.60 |

**(a)** Identify the years in which the minimum price raised the price paid, and state by how much per kg in 2024. **[2]**
**(b)** Using the data, explain why the cooperative's average income per kg still fell between 2023 and 2024. **[4]**

**6.** Explain the causes of the international debt crisis of the 1980s. **[5]**

**7.** After a cyclone, Merovia, a fictional country, receives three aid packages:

> **Package A:** Food, tents and water-purification tablets from the government of Northland, delivered within a week.
> **Package B:** A low-interest loan from a development bank funded by many member governments, to rebuild a coastal road over five years.
> **Package C:** A grant from the government of Westaria for new port cranes, on condition that the cranes are bought from Westarian companies.

**(a)** Classify each package using the terms relief, development, tied, bilateral and multilateral. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain two disadvantages of Package C for Merovia. **[4]**

**8.** Tourists spend US$50 million a year in Palmera Bay, a fictional resort. An estimated 45% of this spending leaks out of the local economy.

**(a)** Calculate the amount retained in the local economy. Show your working. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how the retained spending creates a multiplier effect, and why leakage weakens it. **[4]**

**9.** The table shows international arrivals (thousands) at Corral Island, a fictional destination.

| Year | 1978 | 1983 | 1988 | 1993 | 1998 | 2003 | 2008 | 2013 | 2018 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Arrivals | 4 | 9 | 35 | 120 | 310 | 520 | 600 | 610 | 590 |

**(a)** Describe the trend in arrivals, using figures. **[3]**
**(b)** With reference to Butler's tourism area life cycle model, suggest which stage Corral Island had reached by 2018, and what further information would help you decide. **[4]**

**10.** Explain the difference between ecological and perceptual carrying capacity, and suggest how a small ecotourism lodge might stay within both. **[5]**

**11.** "Free trade benefits importing countries more than exporting countries." How far do you agree? **[16]**

**12.** With reference to one tourist area or resort you have studied, evaluate how far its growth has been sustainable. **[16]**

## Answers

**1.** Services bought from other countries, paid for without physical goods crossing the border [1]; for example, a family from the country holidaying abroad, or a firm paying a foreign insurer [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* the example must be a service bought from abroad; an imported product such as oil is visible and scores nothing.

**2.** Debt relief is cancelling all or part of a country's debt [1], or rescheduling it with lower interest or a longer repayment period [1]. Example: the Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) Initiative, or the Multilateral Debt Relief Initiative [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* name the programme in full; "the IMF helped" is too vague for the example mark.

**3. (a)** 2015: 2,400 / 3,500 x 100 = 68.6% [1]; 2024: 2,100 / 4,400 x 100 = 47.7% [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Copper's share fell from 68.6% to 47.7%, so a fall in the copper price would now hit a smaller share of earnings [1]; this matters because primary commodity prices are volatile [1]. Tourism (an invisible export) more than doubled from 400 to 900, adding a service-based source of earnings [1]; manufactured goods rose from 250 to 600, and manufactured exports usually have more stable prices and add more value [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* each reason needs a figure from the table plus an explanation of risk; quoting numbers alone gains only half the marks.

**4.** Resource endowment: a country exports what it has in abundance [1], e.g. a country with large copper reserves exports mainly copper ore, as Chile does [1]. Colonial ties: former colonies kept trade links and shared languages, currencies or institutions with the former colonial power [1], e.g. the Lomé Convention (1975) gave African, Caribbean and Pacific countries preferential access to European markets [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "explain" needs the mechanism (why the factor creates trade), not just a named example.

**5. (a)** 2020, 2023 and 2024 [1]; in 2024 the minimum raised the price by 0.40 per kg (2.00 - 1.60) [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Only 30% of output is sold at the guaranteed price [1]. The other 70% is sold at market price, which fell from 1.90 to 1.60 [1]. The average price falls from about 1.93 (0.3 x 2.00 + 0.7 x 1.90) to about 1.72 per kg (0.3 x 2.00 + 0.7 x 1.60) [1]. Any premium paid must be spent on community or business projects, so it does not raise farmers' own price per kg [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* the data reward a calculation; a general point that "Fairtrade does not cover everything" without figures is capped.

**6.** After the 1973 oil price rise, oil exporters deposited large sums in Western banks [1], which lent them to developing countries at low, variable interest rates [1]. From 1979, interest rates in the US and Europe rose sharply, so debt service costs soared [1]. Falling commodity prices and recession cut borrowers' export earnings, so they could not repay [1]. In August 1982 Mexico announced it could not service its debts, and lenders withdrew from other borrowers, spreading the crisis [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* link each cause to repayment difficulty; a list of dates without the mechanism of rising debt service will not reach full marks.

**7. (a)** A: relief aid, bilateral [1]. B: development aid, multilateral [1]. C: tied aid, bilateral (and development) [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Merovia cannot shop around, so the cranes may cost more than equivalent cranes from elsewhere [1], which reduces the real value of the grant [1]. Spare parts, servicing and training may also have to come from Westaria [1], creating long-term dependence on one supplier and money flowing back to the donor's firms [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* the second mark for each disadvantage comes from developing the consequence for Merovia, not from naming another problem.

**8. (a)** Retained share = 100% - 45% = 55% [1]; 0.55 x 50 = US$27.5 million [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Retained money is paid as wages and to local suppliers (direct income) [1]. Suppliers then buy more inputs and employ more people (indirect effects) [1], and workers spend their wages in local shops and services (induced effects), so total income exceeds the first spending [1]. Leakage, such as imported food or profits sent to foreign hotel owners, removes money from each round, so the multiplier is smaller [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* name the rounds of spending in order; "money goes round the economy" is too vague to score.

**9. (a)** Slow growth at first, from 4,000 in 1978 to 35,000 in 1988 [1]. Rapid growth to 1998 and 2003, with the largest five-year rise of 210,000 between 1998 and 2003 [1]. Growth then slowed, peaking at 610,000 in 2013 and falling slightly to 590,000 in 2018 [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Stagnation, because arrivals have levelled off and dipped after a long period of growth [1]; consolidation is also possible if the dip is short-term [1]. Further information: changes in hotel occupancy or in the condition of facilities, to show whether the resort is ageing [1]; data after 2018, or on new investment, to show whether decline or rejuvenation follows [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* support the stage with the figures, and show that the model cannot fix the stage from arrivals alone.

**10.** Ecological carrying capacity is the level of use an ecosystem can absorb before it is damaged, e.g. trampling or disturbance of wildlife [1]. Perceptual carrying capacity is the number of visitors that residents and tourists find acceptable before their experience suffers [1]. The difference is that one is about environmental damage and the other is about people's tolerance [1]. The lodge could limit guest numbers and keep to marked trails [1], and employ local people and consult the community so residents accept visitors [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* both management points must fit a small ecotourism lodge; generic city-scale measures such as access fees miss the context.

**11.** The real exam marks essays with levels of response, not point by point; these are indicative creditworthy points. **[16]**
- Introduction: free trade means trade without tariffs, quotas or subsidies [1]; the answer depends on what is traded and on each country's level of development [1].
- Importers gain cheaper goods and wider choice [1], and cheaper inputs for their own industries [1].
- But cheap imports can undercut local producers, e.g. subsidised EU farm exports have been criticised for undercutting farmers in Africa and Asia [1].
- Exporters gain foreign currency, jobs and economies of scale [1]; East Asian manufacturers grew through export-led growth as tariffs fell [1].
- Primary commodity exporters face volatile prices and weak terms of trade [1]; Zambia's reliance on copper left it exposed, and it defaulted on its debt in 2020 [1].
- The WTO promotes free trade through most-favoured nation and national treatment rules [1], but the Doha Round stalled over rich countries' agricultural subsidies [1], so trade is not fully free and benefits are uneven [1].
- Fairtrade shows exporters may need protection from market prices, though it covers only part of producers' output [1].
- Most countries are both importers and exporters, so the statement is a simplification [1].
- Judgement: free trade benefits importers fairly reliably through lower prices, but for exporters it depends on the type of export [1]; manufacturing exporters may gain more than importers, while commodity exporters often gain less [1].
*Examiner insight:* top-level answers judge the statement against named countries and the type of export; describing trade benefits in general stays in the lower levels.

**12.** The real exam marks essays with levels of response, not point by point; these are indicative creditworthy points for a Venice answer. **[16]**
- Introduction: sustainable growth meets present needs without damaging the environment, society or economy for the future [1]; Venice is a historic city and lagoon, a UNESCO site since 1987 [1].
- Growth: a Grand Tour stop in the 18th century [1], then mass tourism and cruises, with estimates of 22 to 30 million visitors a year [1].
- Economy: tourism supports many jobs and businesses [1], but the city depends heavily on one sector and much day-tripper spending is small [1].
- Society: the historic-centre population fell from about 120,000 in 1980 to about 50,000 in 2021 [1], as rents rose and homes became short-term lets [1]; this is the clearest sign of unsustainable growth [1].
- Environment: cruise ships caused pollution and wave damage [1]; flooding and subsidence threaten buildings [1].
- Management: ships over 25,000 tonnes banned from the Giudecca Canal in 2021 [1]; a day-tripper access fee from 2024 and a 25-person limit on tour groups [1].
- Evaluation of management: phone data suggested more people visited on fee days in 2024, so the fee raised money without cutting numbers [1].
- Butler's model: Venice fits stagnation, with demand high but the resident community under pressure [1].
- Judgement: growth has been economically sustainable but socially unsustainable, and recent measures have only partly reduced pressures [1].
*Examiner insight:* "evaluate how far" needs a judgement for each of environment, society and economy, backed by dated facts from your case study.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving goods such as oil or cars as examples of invisible trade.
- Reading figures off a table without explaining what they show.
- Not showing working in calculations, so a slip loses every mark.
- Giving one classification for aid when the question asks for several (e.g. relief and bilateral).
- Writing that Fairtrade guarantees farmers a high income; it sets a minimum and covers only part of output.
- Describing the debt crisis without explaining why rising interest rates mattered.
- Naming Butler's stages but not testing the model against data.
- Using a whole country for the tourist-area case study, or giving no dates or figures.
- Writing essays with no judgement; evaluation carries 40% of Paper 4.
