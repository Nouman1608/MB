## Questions

### Section A

**1.** The objective usually assumed for firms in economic theory is to maximise: **[1]**

A: Sales revenue.

B: Market share.

C: Profit.

D: Social welfare.

**2.** In a large public company, the separation of ownership and control is most likely to lead to: **[1]**

A: Managers pursuing sales growth rather than maximum profit.

B: Shareholders running the firm day to day.

C: Lower pay for managers.

D: The firm being run as a not-for-profit organisation.

**3.** A firm that aims for satisfactory profit while also meeting targets for staff turnover and customer satisfaction is: **[1]**

A: Profit maximising.

B: Revenue maximising.

C: Satisficing.

D: Sales maximising.

**4.** Using the table for Firm Sigma, which output would a revenue-maximising firm choose? **[1]**

A: 4 thousand units.

B: 5 thousand units.

C: 6 thousand units.

D: 8 thousand units.

**5.** Sam would pay at most £12 for one exercise class a week, £9 for a second, £7 for a third and £4 for a fourth. Classes cost £6 each. Calculate how many classes Sam buys and his consumer surplus. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why a worker might choose a job with lower pay than another job she is offered. **[2]**

### Section B

**7.** Use the table for Firm Sigma.

**(a)** State the profit-maximising output and price. **[2]**

**(b)** State the output and price chosen by a firm that maximises sales while covering its costs. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the managers of Firm Sigma might prefer the output in (b), and who gains and loses compared with (a). **[4]**

**8.** Firm Sigma's owners require a profit of at least £60 thousand. Its managers want the highest revenue consistent with this.

**(a)** State the output the managers would choose. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the profit given up compared with profit maximisation. **[1]**

**9.** A coffee chain pays its growers 10% above the market price for beans, at an extra cost of £1.2 million a year. Its annual profit is £15 million.

**(a)** Calculate the extra cost as a percentage of annual profit. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain two ways this policy might benefit the chain. **[4]**

**10.** Explain, using an example, why a government's objectives may conflict. **[4]**

**11.** Explain two reasons why consumers may not always maximise their utility. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that profit maximisation is the only objective that matters for firms. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** **C** [1]. **[1]**

**2.** **A** [1]. Managers' pay and status often depend on the size of the firm. **[1]**

**3.** **C** [1]. **[1]**

**4.** **C** [1]. Total revenue peaks at £180 thousand at 6 thousand units. **[1]**

**5.** Sam buys **3 classes**, because the fourth is worth £4, less than £6 [1]. Consumer surplus = (12 - 6) + (9 - 6) + (7 - 6) = **£10** [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Workers maximise utility, not just income [1], so a job with better conditions, more security, a shorter journey or more satisfying work can give more utility despite lower pay [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Output **4 thousand units** [1] at a price of **£40** [1].

**(b)** Output **8 thousand units**, where profit is still just positive (£2 thousand) [1], at a price of **£20** [1].

**(c)** Managers' pay, status and job security may depend on the size of the firm or its market share rather than profit [1], and shareholders may find it hard to monitor them [1]. Consumers gain, because output doubles and the price halves from £40 to £20 [1]. Shareholders lose, because profit falls from £82 thousand to £2 thousand [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote the figures for both outputs to show the size of the gain and loss.

**8. (a)** Profit is at least £60 thousand at outputs 3 to 6, and revenue is highest among these at **6 thousand units** (£180 thousand) [1] [1].

**(b)** 82 - 66 = **£16 thousand** [1]. **[3]**

**9. (a)** 1.2 / 15 x 100 = **8%** [1].

**(b)** Customers who care about fair treatment of growers may choose the chain over rivals or pay a higher price [1], raising revenue and protecting market share [1]. Secure, well-paid growers may supply higher-quality beans reliably [1], reducing the risk of supply disruption and protecting long-run profit [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* CSR answers earn credit for linking the action to revenue or cost, not for saying it is "good for the brand" with no mechanism.

**10.** Governments aim to maximise social welfare, which includes several objectives [1]. For example, cutting interest rates or taxes may raise growth and reduce unemployment [1], but higher spending can push up inflation or increase imports [1]. Building a new airport may create jobs but raise pollution and noise, so economic and environmental objectives clash [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Consumers may lack information about a product's quality or effects [1], so they buy something that gives less satisfaction than an alternative would have [1]. They may act out of habit or on impulse [1], without comparing extra benefit with price, so their choices do not maximise utility [1]. **[4]**

**12.** For: profit rewards owners and funds investment [1]; in competitive markets firms that do not keep costs and profits up may be forced out, so survival requires behaving close to profit maximisation [1]; the assumption predicts well how firms respond to changes in costs or demand [1]. Against: in large companies managers control decisions and may pursue sales or growth, as Firm Sigma's figures show [1]; firms rarely know their revenue and costs exactly, so they cannot locate maximum profit [1]; many firms satisfice across stakeholders' goals [1]; CSR and social enterprises pursue aims beyond profit [1]; new firms may prioritise survival [1]. Judgement: profit remains the most important single objective, especially where competition is strong, but "only" is too strong [1]; the more ownership is separated from control and the weaker the competition, the more other objectives matter [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* A judgement that names the conditions under which profit dominates earns more than a list on each side.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Choosing the revenue-maximising output when profit maximisation is asked for.
- Picking output 9 for sales maximisation, where profit is negative.
- Forgetting to state the price as well as the output.
- Saying CSR always reduces profit.
- Listing objectives without reasons.
- Ending the essay without a judgement.
