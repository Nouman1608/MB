This guide covers **Component 3: Poetry and Prose Texts and Imaginative
Writing (Non-Examined Assessment)**, for Pearson Edexcel International
GCSE English Language A (4EA1), first teaching 2016, current
specification Issue 7, August 2025. Component 3 is the coursework
alternative to
[Component 2](/resources/igcse-edexcel-english-language-component-2/):
centres choose one route or the other for their candidates, not both,
and both routes assess the same 40% weighting and the same broad content
area -- poetry and prose reading paired with imaginative writing.

## Where this fits in 4EA1

Component 1 (Non-fiction Texts and Transactional Writing) is compulsory
for every candidate. Centres then choose between Component 2, a
timed written exam on unseen extracts and Anthology material, and
Component 3, covered here: two pieces of coursework, completed under
supervised conditions over the course, then submitted for internal
assessment and external moderation by Pearson. The two routes test
the same assessment objectives (AO1, AO2, AO4, AO5) and cover the same
Anthology content, but Component 3 removes the exam-hall time pressure
in exchange for a formal, marked, resubmission-limited coursework
process.

## Specification coverage

**PEARSON EDEXCEL INTERNATIONAL GCSE ENGLISH LANGUAGE A (4EA1) — COMPONENT 3**

- Overall weighting — Component 3 represents 40% of the qualification,
60 raw marks, split evenly between Assignment A (30 marks) and
Assignment B (30 marks); it is internally assessed by the centre and
externally moderated by Pearson, available in the June and November
series
- Assignment A: Poetry and Prose Texts (30 marks) — a written response
based on three or more texts selected from Part 2 of the Pearson Edexcel
International GCSE English Anthology, with the requirement that the
selection includes at least one poetry text and at least one prose text;
an advisory word count of approximately 1,200 words; assessed against
AO1 (12 marks, reading and understanding, selecting and interpreting
information, ideas and perspectives) and AO2 (18 marks, analysing how
writers use linguistic and structural devices to achieve their effects)
- Assignment B: Imaginative Writing (30 marks) — a single piece of
imaginative writing set to narrate, describe or entertain, with an
advisory word count of approximately 700 words; assessed against AO4
(18 marks, communicating effectively and imaginatively, adapting form,
tone and register for audience and purpose) and AO5 (12 marks, writing
clearly and accurately, with a range of vocabulary and sentence
structures and accurate spelling, grammar and punctuation)
- Conditions of assessment — both assignments are completed under
supervised, controlled conditions specified by Pearson, using the
centre's own set or chosen titles/tasks within the framework the
specification allows, and are subject to the board's rules on
authentication, retention of candidate work, and resubmission

## How to approach it

Assignment A rewards genuine comparison, not three separate mini-essays
stapled together. Because the requirement is "at least one poetry text
and one prose text," the strongest responses choose a small, deliberate
combination -- often two poems and one prose extract, or one poem and
two prose extracts -- built around a single clear focus (for example,
how each writer presents conflict, or how setting shapes mood) that can
be tracked across all three texts in a connected argument, rather than
three unconnected close readings.

For Assignment B, the 700-word advisory length is deliberately short:
examiners reward precision and control over quantity. A single vivid,
well-structured piece with a clear narrative or descriptive arc,
carefully chosen vocabulary, and accurate technical control (AO5) will
outperform a longer piece that loses shape or accuracy. Since candidates
usually have more drafting time than in a timed exam, use it for real
redrafting -- tightening word choice and sentence variety -- rather than
simply lengthening the piece.

Because Component 3 is internally assessed, centres set their own exact
titles or tasks within Pearson's framework and mark against Pearson's
criteria before moderation; candidates preparing for Component 3 should
check their own centre's set titles and submission deadlines directly,
since these are not fixed by the specification itself.

## Worked example: planning Assignment A's comparison

A candidate chooses two Anthology poems and one prose extract, focused
on how each writer presents loss.

```
Poem 1:    loss shown through a controlling extended metaphor
Poem 2:    loss shown through fragmented, non-chronological structure
Prose:     loss shown through a first-person narrator's understatement
Argument:  each writer chooses a different technique (metaphor,
           structure, narrative voice) to control the reader's
           emotional distance from loss -- this becomes the essay's
           connecting thread across all three texts, rather than three
           separate technique lists
```

Building the comparison around one connecting thread like this, stated
early and returned to across all three texts, is what distinguishes a
genuinely comparative AO1/AO2 response from three separate analyses.

## Common mistakes

Treating the three texts in Assignment A as three separate essays with
no connecting argument, which loses marks against both AO1 and AO2's
comparative expectations. Ignoring the "at least one poetry, one prose"
requirement when choosing texts. Writing well beyond the advisory word
counts in Assignment B, which typically weakens control and precision
rather than improving marks. Forgetting that Component 3 is internally
set -- assuming the Anthology alone determines the exact task, rather
than checking the specific title or brief the centre has set.

## Quick revision checklist

- Choose Assignment A's three texts around one clear, statable
connecting focus before drafting.
- Confirm the poetry-and-prose mix requirement is met before finalising
text choices.
- Treat the 700-word Assignment B limit as a control target, not a
minimum to exceed.
- Check the centre's own set titles and deadlines directly, since
Component 3 tasks are set internally within Pearson's framework.
