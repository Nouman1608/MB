**HL only.** This is the seventh study in the Regional study for the Americas, examined on Paper 3 at Higher Level. Paper 3 asks you to evaluate an argument and reach a judgement. This study covers the USA in full, plus the effects in **one other country** (the guide asks for one country excluding the USA for political and social impact) and solutions in the USA and Canada and in **one Latin American country**. This guide uses **Canada** and **Brazil** as its main non-US examples, with Mexico and Argentina for comparison.

## The six lines of inquiry

The guide names six inquiry topics: reasons for the Great Depression; the political impact in the USA; the political impact in one other country; the social impact in one country; the effectiveness of solutions in the USA and Canada; and the effectiveness of solutions in one Latin American country. Use **cause and consequence** and **significance** to frame answers on the causes, and **continuity and change** for the social impact. See the [four concepts guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-four-concepts/).

## Key chronology

- 1920s: US boom, credit expansion, speculation on the stock market.
- October 1929: Wall Street Crash (24 and 29 October).
- 1930: Smoot-Hawley Tariff; Bennett elected in Canada; revolution in Brazil.
- 1932: Reconstruction Finance Corporation; Bonus Army; Franklin Roosevelt elected.
- 1933: Roosevelt's "Hundred Days"; banking crisis; Dust Bowl at its height in the mid-1930s.
- 1934: Bank of Canada. 1935: Social Security Act and Wagner Act; Regina Riot; King re-elected in Canada.
- 1937: Estado Novo in Brazil; recession in the US. 1938: oil nationalisation in Mexico.
- 1939: War in Europe; recovery in the Americas is accelerated by rearmament.

## Reasons for the Great Depression

**Political factors and failures of regulation.** In the 1920s the US government favoured low taxes and limited regulation. There was little control of banks or the stock market. The Federal Reserve did not act decisively when banks failed after 1930, and the money supply fell sharply. This is the argument of Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz. High tariffs, especially Smoot-Hawley (1930), cut world trade and provoked retaliation.

**Economic factors: banking and trade.** Many small banks were fragile. Between 1930 and 1933 thousands of banks failed (about 9,000 in the US), wiping out savings and cutting lending. The Wall Street Crash itself destroyed paper wealth and confidence. Incomes had been unevenly shared, so consumer demand could not support production. Americans relied on instalment credit. Internationally, war debts, reparations and the gold standard linked countries tightly, so shocks spread. Charles Kindleberger argued that there was no leader to stabilise the system, as Britain could not and the US would not.

**Agricultural and environmental factors.** Farm prices had been weak through the 1920s because of overproduction after the First World War, and farm debt was high. When prices collapsed after 1929, many farmers lost their land. In the Great Plains, the ploughing of dry grassland followed by severe drought in the 1930s created the **Dust Bowl**, and tens of thousands of families left. Canadian prairie farmers suffered a similar drought and price collapse. In Latin America, countries dependent on a few exports (coffee, sugar, tin, copper, beef) were hit as prices fell and loans dried up.

**Reactions to the 1929 Wall Street Crash.** Hoover and business leaders at first predicted a quick recovery and urged confidence. Their response was limited by belief in balanced budgets and voluntary action. Many people lost savings in bank failures, which deepened the crisis.

**Weighing causes.** J. K. Galbraith stressed speculation and structural weakness; Friedman and Schwartz emphasised monetary failure; Kindleberger the international system. A strong answer ranks them and explains how a financial shock became a depression.

## Political impact in the USA

**Party politics.** Voters blamed the Republicans. Roosevelt's election in 1932 began a Democratic dominance that lasted for decades. The "New Deal coalition" joined the South, urban workers, immigrants, Black voters (who shifted from the Republicans), and many farmers and unions.

**Executive power and the federal government.** The federal government took on responsibility for relief, regulation and economic management. Many new agencies were created. Roosevelt used radio "fireside chats" to speak directly to the public. Expansion of executive power led to controversy, such as the failed plan in 1937 to add justices to the Supreme Court.

**Labour rights and social policy.** The Wagner Act (1935) protected the right to organise and bargain collectively, and union membership rose sharply. The Social Security Act (1935) set up old-age pensions and unemployment insurance. The Fair Labor Standards Act (1938) set minimum wages and maximum hours. Coverage of the Social Security Act excluded many farm and domestic workers, who were often Black or Hispanic.

**Opposition to the New Deal.** Conservatives, such as the American Liberty League, said it threatened liberty and business. The Supreme Court struck down parts of it, such as the National Industrial Recovery Act (1935). From the left and populist side, Huey Long's "Share Our Wealth" scheme, Father Charles Coughlin and the Townsend movement said it did too little.

## Political impact in one country outside the USA

**Canada.** Unemployment reached more than a quarter of the workforce at the worst point, and the prairies were hit hard. Voters turned against the governing party each time. R. B. Bennett's Conservatives won in 1930 and lost in 1935 to Mackenzie King's Liberals. New parties appeared: the socialist Co-operative Commonwealth Federation (1932), Social Credit in Alberta (1935) and the Union Nationale in Quebec (1936). The federal system strained as provinces could not afford relief.

**Brazil.** The collapse in coffee prices undermined the Old Republic, in which São Paulo and Minas Gerais had alternated the presidency. The Revolution of 1930 brought Getúlio Vargas to power. He centralised authority, ruled by decree, put down a São Paulo rebellion in 1932, issued a constitution in 1934, and in 1937 declared the authoritarian Estado Novo. Labour laws and state-controlled unions brought urban workers into the system while limiting their independence. Political instability and the challenge of Communist and Integralist (fascist-inspired) movements were part of the context.

## Social impact in one country in the Americas

**Demography and living standards (USA).** Unemployment reached about a quarter of the workforce in 1933. Birth and marriage rates fell. Families doubled up, and many young men took to the road. Migrants from the Plains ("Okies") moved to California. Homeless camps called "Hoovervilles" appeared in cities.

**Women.** Many women lost jobs, and there was public pressure against married women working. At the same time, jobs in teaching, clerical work and services held up better than in heavy industry, and women's employment overall rose. Women held important posts: Frances Perkins was Secretary of Labor and Eleanor Roosevelt was an influential voice for reform.

**Marginalised groups.** African Americans were hit hard ("last hired, first fired") and were sometimes excluded from relief or paid less, but received real help from some programmes and began to move politically to the Democrats. Mexican and Mexican American people were pressed to leave the US through "repatriation" drives, in which many US citizens were also removed. Native Americans gained from the Indian Reorganization Act (1934), which ended allotment.

**Culture and the arts.** Photographers such as Dorothea Lange recorded the crisis. The Works Progress Administration employed writers, artists and musicians. John Steinbeck's *The Grapes of Wrath* (1939) and Woody Guthrie's songs reflected the experience. Radio and Hollywood gave escape and shared experience.

## Effectiveness of solutions in the USA and Canada

**Herbert Hoover.** Hoover encouraged voluntary action, created the Reconstruction Finance Corporation (1932) to lend to banks and businesses, and set up a farm board. He raised taxes in 1932 to balance the budget, and the army's removal of the Bonus Army in 1932 damaged his reputation. His measures were too limited and too late, though some historians say he did more than is often credited and that his approach prepared the way for later policy.

**Franklin D. Roosevelt and the New Deal.** The "Three Rs" are a useful framework: **relief** (FERA, CCC, WPA), **recovery** (AAA, NRA, public works) and **reform** (Glass-Steagall Act and FDIC to protect deposits, the SEC, the Tennessee Valley Authority, Social Security, the Wagner Act). Achievements: restored confidence in the banks, created a safety net, gave work to millions and improved infrastructure. Limits: unemployment remained high through the decade, the 1937 to 1938 recession followed cuts in spending, and full recovery came with wartime production. Historians debate whether it was a success. Liberal historians such as Arthur Schlesinger Jr. and William Leuchtenburg praise it; critics from the left say it saved capitalism without reforming it enough; critics from the right say it prolonged the slump.

**William Lyon Mackenzie King and R. B. Bennett.** Bennett at first relied on tariffs and relief camps, which provoked anger and the On-to-Ottawa Trek (1935). In January 1935, he announced a "New Deal" of regulation and insurance, but the courts struck down much of the legislation. His government created the Bank of Canada (1934) and the Canadian Wheat Board (1935). King's Liberals, returning in 1935, were cautious. They referred the Bennett laws to the courts, set up the Rowell-Sirois Commission (1937) to examine federal-provincial relations, and expanded some measures. Canada's recovery depended heavily on trade and, like the US, on rearmament.

## Effectiveness of solutions in one country of Latin America

In **Brazil**, Vargas supported coffee prices by buying and destroying stocks, which stabilised incomes. Coffee exports fell, so industrial output grew to replace imported goods, an example of **Import Substitution Industrialisation (ISI)**. The state built roads, expanded the Ministry of Labour and introduced labour regulations and welfare provisions. Critics point to control of unions, repression and the tilt towards dictatorship. In **Mexico**, Cárdenas used agrarian reform and nationalisation to respond, while in **Argentina** the government entered trade agreements (the Roca-Runciman Pact of 1933) with Britain. ISI created industry and urban jobs, but it left dependence on imported machinery.

## Arguments for Paper 3

- Which cause of the Depression was most important: financial, monetary, international or agricultural?
- Was the New Deal a success, a failure or a limited success?
- Did the Depression strengthen democracy or authoritarianism in the Americas? Compare the US with Brazil.
- Did the Depression cause lasting change, or did the war do more?

## Common mistakes

- Describing the New Deal programmes without assessing their effectiveness.
- Forgetting the non-US country when the question asks about one.
- Using "the Depression began with the Crash" as a full explanation.
- Ignoring Black, Mexican American and Indigenous experience.
