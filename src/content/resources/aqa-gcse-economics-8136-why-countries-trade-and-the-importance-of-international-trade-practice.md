These questions cover **section 3.2.4.1 Why countries trade and the importance of international trade to the UK** in the AQA GCSE Economics (8136) specification for first teaching from September 2017. There are no tiers. All countries, firms and figures are invented. For explanations, see the [why countries trade study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-why-countries-trade-and-the-importance-of-international-trade/) and the [why countries trade revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-why-countries-trade-and-the-importance-of-international-trade-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following is a UK export? **[1]**

A: A UK family's holiday in Portugal

B: A Japanese bank paying a UK firm for software services

C: A UK firm buying solar panels from China

D: A UK resident buying a car made in Germany

**2.** Which **one** of the following best describes global interdependence? **[1]**

A: Each country producing everything it needs

B: Economies relying on each other for goods, inputs, markets and investment

C: A tax placed on imported goods

D: A country leaving a trade agreement

**3.** State what is meant by specialisation. **[1]**

**4.** Countries G and H each have 8 workers. In a day, one worker in G can make 5 chairs or 10 kg of rice; one worker in H can make 6 chairs or 2 kg of rice.

**(a)** Calculate total output of each good if each country puts 4 workers on each good. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate total output if each country specialises completely in the good it is better at producing. **[2]**

**(c)** State the gain in output from specialisation. **[1]**

**5.** In an invented country, 3.2 million televisions are sold in a year, of which 2.8 million are imported. Calculate the share of the market supplied by imports. **[2]**

**6.** An invented country's exports rise from 640 billion to 704 billion. Calculate the percentage growth in exports. **[2]**

**7.** State whether each is a UK export or import, and whether it is a good or a service. **[4]**

- Italian tourists pay for a hotel in York.
- A UK factory buys machinery from Sweden.
- A US film studio pays a UK publisher for the right to film one of its books.
- A UK supermarket buys lamb from New Zealand.

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**8.** Explain how access to a larger export market can lower a UK firm's average costs. **[3]**

**9.** Explain **two** consequences of global interdependence for UK firms. **[4]**

**10.** Explain why workers in some UK industries may lose from international trade. **[3]**

**11.** A UK car plant imports 60% of its components and exports most of its cars. A factory abroad that supplies its computer chips closes for two months after a fire.

**(a)** Explain how the closure is likely to affect the UK plant. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain **one** way the plant could reduce this risk in future. **[2]**

**12.** Evaluate whether the UK benefits overall from being closely interdependent with other economies. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. Money flows into the UK for a service provided by a UK firm. **[1]**

**2.** **B** [1]. **[1]**

**3.** Concentrating on producing the goods and services a country (or firm or worker) produces best, and trading for others [1]. **[1]**

**4. (a)** G: 20 chairs and 40 kg rice; H: 24 chairs and 8 kg rice [1]. Total **44 chairs and 48 kg rice** [1].

**(b)** G makes only rice: 8 × 10 = 80 kg; H makes only chairs: 8 × 6 = 48 chairs [1]. Total **48 chairs and 80 kg rice** [1].

**(c)** **4 more chairs and 32 kg more rice** [1]. **[5]**

*Examiner insight:* Each country specialises in the good where its workers are relatively more productive: rice for G and chairs for H.

**5.** 2.8 ÷ 3.2 × 100 [1] = **87.5%** [1]. **[2]**

**6.** (704 − 640) ÷ 640 × 100 [1] = **10%** [1]. **[2]**

**7.** York hotel: export of a service [1]. Swedish machinery: import of a good [1]. Film rights: export of a service [1]. New Zealand lamb: import of a good [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Selling abroad lets the firm produce a much larger output [1]. Fixed costs, such as machinery and research, are spread over more units, and the firm can buy materials in bulk more cheaply [1]. These economies of scale reduce average cost per unit [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Any two developed: firms can buy the cheapest or best inputs from around the world [1], lowering their costs and improving quality [1]; firms gain access to large markets abroad [1], so they can grow sales [1]; firms face stronger competition from foreign producers [1], so less efficient firms may lose sales or close [1]; disruption in another country's supply chain [1] can stop production in the UK [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Imports from lower-cost producers abroad may be cheaper than goods made in the UK [1]. UK firms in those industries lose sales and may close or cut jobs [1]. Their workers may lack the skills needed in growing industries, so they face structural unemployment [1]. **[3]**

**11. (a)** Without chips, the plant cannot finish its cars [1], so output falls or stops for a time [1]. Exports fall, revenue falls, and workers may be sent home or lose overtime [1].

**(b)** Use more than one supplier, in different countries, so a problem at one does not halt production [1]; or hold larger stocks of key components as a buffer, although this costs money [1]. **[5]**

**12.** *Benefits of interdependence:*

- Consumers gain lower prices and wider choice [1].
- UK firms can buy the cheapest and best inputs and sell to large markets, gaining economies of scale [1].
- Foreign investment creates jobs in the UK [1].
- The UK can specialise in services where it is strong, raising output and incomes [1].

*Costs:*

- Recessions abroad cut UK exports and jobs [1].
- Supply-chain disruptions and rises in world energy or food prices can cause shortages and inflation in the UK [1].
- Industries that cannot compete with imports may decline, causing structural unemployment in some regions [1].

*Judgement:* overall the UK benefits, because the gains in prices, choice and markets are ongoing while most shocks are temporary [1]; but the government can reduce the risks, for example by helping firms diversify suppliers and by retraining workers in declining industries [1]. **[9]**

*Examiner insight:* A judgement that names how the costs could be reduced is stronger than "it depends".

## Where marks are usually lost

- Putting a country to specialise in the wrong good.
- Treating tourism spending in the UK by foreign visitors as an import.
- Calculating a percentage change from the new figure.
- Writing about interdependence without naming who is affected.
- Evaluating with benefits only.
