**Section 1 of Cambridge O Level Pakistan Studies 2059 Paper 1, Cultural and Historical Background to the Pakistan Movement, asks how religious, political and cultural developments of the 18th and 19th centuries led towards the Pakistan Movement.** It is built from Key Questions 1 to 5, each with focus points that examiners turn into 4-, 7- and 14-mark questions. This guide works through every focus point and gives a plan for the kind of judgement question each one produces.

## Syllabus coverage

| Key Question | Focus points (from the syllabus) |

The syllabus says the material within a section is inter-related, so different parts of one exam question may need knowledge of more than one item in the section and an understanding of how the items connect.

## What is the paper structure?

Paper 1 lasts 1 hour 30 minutes. Section A is a compulsory source-based question; in Section B you answer two questions from four, each with a 4-mark knowledge part, a 7-mark explanation part and a 14-mark evaluation essay. In the 14-mark part, explain more than one factor, including the one named in the question, and finish with a reasoned judgement.

## Key Question 1: the religious thinkers

### Focus point: how important were the religious reforms of Shah Waliullah?

The syllabus asks about his reforms and his role in the political and religious context of the time.

**Religious reform.** He translated the Qur'an into Persian, the language of educated Muslims of the time, so that more people could understand it directly. He called on Muslims to return to the teachings of the Qur'an and Hadith, to abandon practices added to Islam, and to reduce divisions between the schools of Islamic thought. His works, such as *Hujjat Allah al-Baligha*, set out his ideas for later generations.

**Political role.** He wrote to Ahmad Shah Abdali, the ruler of Afghanistan, asking him to defend the Muslims against the Marathas. Abdali's army heavily defeated the Marathas at Panipat in 1761, but he returned to Afghanistan afterwards, so the battle weakened the Marathas and left a power vacuum in the north.

**Legacy.** His sons and students, including Shah Abdul Aziz, continued his work, which influenced later reformers such as Syed Ahmad Barelvi.

### Focus point: what influence did Syed Ahmad Barelvi exert in the revival of Islam?

The specified content is the revival of Islam, the Jihad Movement and the Mujahideen. Syed Ahmad Barelvi wanted to restore the purity of Islam and to free the Muslims of the Punjab and the North-West Frontier from Sikh rule. He set up a base among the Pathan tribes of the frontier and declared a jihad against the Sikhs in 1826. His followers, the Mujahideen, won early victories and captured Peshawar in 1830, but he lost the support of some local tribal leaders. He was killed, with Shah Ismail Shaheed, at the Battle of Balakot in 1831, although the movement continued after his death.

### Focus point: how influential was Haji Shariatullah and the Faraizi Movement?

Haji Shariatullah founded the Faraizi Movement in Bengal after returning from many years in Mecca. He insisted on the obligatory duties of Islam and opposed practices borrowed from other religions. He declared India Dar-ul-Harb, so Friday and Eid prayers were suspended. He supported Muslim peasants against Hindu landlords and European indigo planters, and his son Dudu Miyan organised the movement further.

**Judgement plan: "Shah Waliullah did more than Syed Ahmad Barelvi or Haji Shariatullah to revive Islam in the subcontinent. How far do you agree?"** Weigh Shah Waliullah's Persian Qur'an, his call to the Qur'an and Hadith, his work for unity between schools of thought and his students against Barelvi's combination of religious revival with political action (the Mujahideen and the jihad, which failed to free the Punjab after Balakot) and Shariatullah's work among peasants in Bengal. Judgement: Shah Waliullah's ideas were the foundation the others built on, but Barelvi and Shariatullah took revival to ordinary Muslims in particular regions.

**Do not confuse** Syed Ahmad Barelvi (the Jihad Movement) with Sir Syed Ahmad Khan (the Aligarh Movement), and do not write about the Faraizi Movement in an answer on the Jihad Movement.

## Key Question 2: the decline of the Mughal Empire

### Focus point: how far was Aurangzeb responsible?

The specified internal reasons are the impact of Aurangzeb's policies on the stability of the Empire, the effectiveness of his successors, the problems of controlling the Empire, and the rise of the Maratha and Sikh empires. Aurangzeb's long Deccan wars emptied the treasury and his religious policies alienated many subjects. The Marathas fought him for decades and later spread across central and northern India, collecting taxes from Mughal lands and threatening Delhi.

### Focus point: how far did Mughal weaknesses from 1707 make decline inevitable?

After 1707 his successors were weak, short-lived and fought wars of succession. The empire was too large to control, and governors became independent. The Sikhs under leaders such as Banda Bahadur rebelled in the Punjab and later, under Ranjit Singh, created a powerful Sikh kingdom in the north-west. The external reasons named in the syllabus include foreign invasions from Persia and Afghanistan: Nadir Shah's invasion of 1739 and the sack of Delhi, and the invasions of Ahmad Shah Abdali, weakened Mughal power and wealth.

### Focus point: what role did the East India Company play, and how successful was British expansion to 1857?

The syllabus notes that the two focus points on British expansion do not require detailed teaching, but are essential to give a full picture of Mughal problems and are background to British colonial rule of the territory now in Pakistan and to the War of Independence. The specified content covers the Company's involvement in the subcontinent, British relations with the later Mughal rulers of Delhi, British expansion from the 1750s to the 1850s, the annexation of the territories now in Pakistan (including the Anglo-Sikh wars and the annexations of Lahore, the Punjab and Peshawar), the search for a "natural" and "scientific" North-West Frontier, and British policy towards Tribal Territory.

**Why the Company expanded.** Weak Mughal emperors could not control their provinces, so local rulers became independent and were easier to defeat or win over one at a time. Indian rulers were divided and fought one another, and the Company made alliances with some against others. Its armies were well trained and disciplined, with modern weapons and artillery. Victory at Plassey (1757) and later control of the revenue of Bengal gave it the wealth to pay for armies. Subsidiary alliances made rulers pay for Company troops stationed in their states and accept British control of their foreign relations. The Doctrine of Lapse and annexations for "misgovernment", such as Awadh in 1856, added territory without war, and the defeat of the Sikhs and the annexation of the Punjab in 1849 removed the last major independent power in the north-west.

**Plassey (1757).** The Company's army, led by Robert Clive, fought Siraj-ud-Daulah, the Nawab of Bengal. Clive had secretly agreed with Mir Jafar, one of the Nawab's commanders, that he would not fight, and Mir Jafar was made Nawab after the battle. The Company won easily and gained control over Bengal, which became the base for its later expansion.

**The Doctrine of Lapse.** This was a policy of Lord Dalhousie, Governor-General from 1848 to 1856. If a ruler of a dependent state died without a natural male heir, the state "lapsed" to the Company, and adopted heirs were not recognised. Satara, Jhansi and Nagpur were annexed. Awadh was not annexed under this doctrine: it was annexed for alleged misgovernment.

**The "scientific" frontier.** The British feared Russian expansion into Central Asia and wanted Afghanistan to act as a buffer state. Mountain passes such as the Khyber Pass were the traditional invasion routes, and raids by frontier tribes into settled areas had to be stopped. A clear, defensible boundary led to the Durand Line agreed with the Amir of Afghanistan in 1893. To manage the tribes, the British combined military posts with allowances paid to tribal leaders and created the North-West Frontier Province in 1901.

**Judgement plan: "The rise of the Marathas and the Sikhs was the most important reason for the decline of the Mughal Empire after 1707. How far do you agree?"** For: the Maratha spread and the Sikh kingdom. Against: Aurangzeb's wars and policies, weak successors, the size of the empire, the invasions of Nadir Shah and Abdali, and the Company's expansion after Plassey. Judgement: the Marathas and Sikhs took advantage of Mughal weakness, but they could only rise because the emperors after 1707 had already lost control.

## Key Question 3: the War of Independence 1857-58

### Focus points: what were the long-term and immediate causes?

The specified content is the immediate and underlying causes of the War and the attitude of Bahadur Shah Zafar.

**Long-term causes.**
- Political: the Doctrine of Lapse and the annexation of Awadh angered rulers and their followers, and the British treatment of the Mughal emperor, including the plan to end the title after Bahadur Shah Zafar, offended Muslims.
- Economic: heavy land revenue demands and the ruin of Indian crafts by British imports.
- Social and religious: Christian missionary activity and reforms seen as interference with custom.
- Military: Indian soldiers resented low pay and poor promotion compared with British soldiers, and being sent overseas.

**Immediate cause.** Sepoys were issued cartridges for the new Enfield rifle that had to be bitten open, and it was rumoured they were greased with cow and pig fat, offending Hindus and Muslims. Soldiers at Meerut who refused to use them were punished, and their comrades mutinied in May 1857 and marched on Delhi, which started the War.

### Focus points: why was independence not achieved, and what were the immediate consequences?

The syllabus lists the course of the War, with particular reference to the roles of the Muslim rulers and populations of what is now Pakistan, the reasons for failure, the short-term impact of the War on Muslims, and British reaction during and immediately after the War, including the major constitutional, educational and administrative reforms that followed. This guide gives only one of these points, because it is the only one the existing pages state: after 1857 the British blamed the Muslims for the War. Use your class notes for the rest (see the draft README).

**Judgement plan: "The introduction of the greased cartridges was the most important cause of the War of Independence of 1857-58. How far do you agree?"** Explain the cartridges and Meerut, then the political, economic, social and religious, and military grievances. Without an explained counter-argument the answer cannot go above Level 3. Judgement: the cartridges were the immediate trigger, but the long-term grievances explain why a mutiny at Meerut turned into a widespread war.

## Key Question 4: Sir Syed Ahmad Khan

### Focus point: what was his contribution to education, politics and religion?

The specified content is his contribution to the education of Muslims and the revitalisation of their national consciousness, his social and political theory and its origins, the impact of his work on Muslims and the western world, his relations with the British and the ulama, and his role in the Indian National Congress and the Muslim League.

Sir Syed believed that Muslims needed a Western education. After 1857 the British blamed the Muslims for the War, and he believed Muslims had to rebuild trust with the British to recover their position. Government jobs required English and Western education, and Muslims who refused it were falling behind Hindus in employment. Without modern education they could not take part in the political and administrative life of the subcontinent. He believed Western science was not in conflict with Islam and that Muslims had to learn it to make progress. Through the Scientific Society and the Muhammadan Educational Conference he aimed to spread modern knowledge, including translations into Urdu. He wanted to overcome the opposition of some ulama to English education by showing it could be combined with Islamic teaching. His advice that Muslims stay out of Congress is also recorded in the [Emergence of Pakistan study guide](/resources/o-level-pakistan-studies-emergence-of-pakistan-1906-47/).

### Focus point: how successful was the Aligarh Movement?

The syllabus lists the reasons for the foundation of Aligarh College. Sir Syed founded the Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College at Aligarh, which opened as a school in 1875. It was modelled on the colleges of Oxford and Cambridge, with residential students, and taught Western subjects such as science and English alongside Islamic studies. It trained many of the future leaders of the Pakistan Movement and became Aligarh Muslim University in 1920.

### Focus points: the Two-Nation Theory and the Hindi-Urdu Controversy

The syllabus requires the meaning and origin of the "Two-Nation" Theory and of the Hindi-Urdu Controversy, and asks how important the Theory was. This draft does not explain either, because the existing pages do not state them; use your class notes and the syllabus until a teacher has supplied the wording (see the draft README).

## Key Question 5: Urdu and regional languages

### Focus point: why did Urdu become the national language, and how successful has the promotion of regional languages been?

The specified content is the importance of Urdu and the reasons for its choice as the national language of Pakistan, the advantages and disadvantages of Urdu as the national language, and the promotion of the provincial languages (Balochi, Punjabi, Pushto, Sindhi).

**Disadvantages of Urdu as the national language.** Urdu was the first language of only a small proportion of Pakistan's population; most people spoke Bengali, Punjabi, Sindhi, Pushto, Balochi or other languages. In East Pakistan, where Bengali was the majority language, the decision caused resentment and the language movement, and protesters were killed in Dhaka in February 1952. Speakers of regional languages felt their own cultures were being treated as less important. In Sindh, tension between Urdu speakers and Sindhi speakers led to language riots in 1972. English remained the language of much of government, the law and higher education, so Urdu did not fully replace it.

**Link to Section 3.** The 1956 Constitution made Urdu and Bengali national languages, and the language movement is one of the long-term causes of the creation of Bangladesh (see the [Nationhood, 1947-99 study guide](/resources/o-level-pakistan-studies-nationhood-1947-99/)).

## Frequently asked questions

**Which Syed Ahmad is which?**
Syed Ahmad Barelvi led the Jihad Movement and the Mujahideen; Sir Syed Ahmad Khan founded the college at Aligarh and led the Aligarh Movement.
