Section A of Paper 2 asks about a modern prose or drama text. You study one text from the
specification's list of twelve post-1914 texts, and you study the whole text. In the exam you answer
one essay question from a choice of two on your text. There is no printed extract, so the whole answer
is built from what you remember. This guide covers how that changes your planning, which methods to
analyse in drama and in prose, and how to handle context in a modern text. For the shape of the whole
paper, see the [Paper 2 overview](/resources/aqa-gcse-english-literature-paper-2/).

## What the question rewards

The question is worth 30 marks, plus 4 marks for AO4. AQA's published mark schemes split the 30 as
AO1 12, AO2 12 and AO3 6, but marking is holistic against level descriptors.

- **AO1** – a critical, informed personal response to the question, supported by references.
- **AO2** – analysis of the writer's language, form and structure.
- **AO3** – the relationship between the text and the contexts in which it was written.
- **AO4** – a range of vocabulary and sentence structures, with accurate spelling and punctuation.

Questions usually take the form "How does [the writer] present..." or "How far does [the writer]
present...", often with bullet points such as "what [a character] says and does" and "how [the
writer] presents [the focus] by the ways he or she writes". That last bullet is a reminder that AO2
is required, not optional.

## Choosing between the two questions

You have a minute or two to choose, so decide by asking three things:

1. **Can I argue something?** Pick the question on which you have a clear view, not just knowledge.
2. **Can I cover the whole text?** You need evidence from the beginning, middle and end.
3. **Do I have quotations for it?** A question on a character you know well may be safer than a theme
   you understand but cannot quote on.

One question is often about a character and the other about a theme. Every character question is
also a theme question in disguise: a character is a construct the writer uses to explore ideas, so
say what ideas the character represents.

## Methods in drama

If your text is a play, analyse it as performance.

- **Stage directions** – modern playwrights often write detailed directions about lighting, set,
  sound and movement. These are the writer's own words and are fully analysable.
- **Entrances, exits and interruptions** – who controls the stage, and when?
- **Dramatic irony** – the audience knows more than characters, especially where a play is set
  earlier than it was written.
- **Dialogue** – interruptions, silences, changes in register or dialect, and who gets the last word.
- **Structural devices** – acts and scenes, cliffhangers at the end of an act, time shifts, and
  narrators or choric figures who address the audience directly.

## Methods in prose

If your text is a novel or short stories, analyse how the story is told.

- **Narrator and viewpoint** – first person or third, child or adult, reliable or not.
- **Symbolism** – objects, places or animals that gather meaning across the text.
- **Allegory** – a story whose characters and events stand for real people, ideas or events.
- **Structure** – chapter openings and endings, the order of events, a circular or a linear shape.
- **Dialogue and voice** – dialect, slang and register can mark identity, belonging or exclusion.

## Context in a modern text

Many modern texts have two contexts: **when they are set** and **when they were written**. The gap is
often deliberate. A play set before a war but written after it can give the audience knowledge that
the characters lack. A novel published soon after a war may explore what people are capable of when
order breaks down. Other relevant contexts include class, gender, race, migration and identity, and
the writer's own known purposes, where these are well documented.

Use context to explain a choice, never to replace analysis. Ask: why would the writer want this
audience, at this time, to see this?

## Worked example

Suppose the question is: *How does Priestley present the Birling family's attitudes to responsibility
in* An Inspector Calls*?* (The set list changes from time to time; check yours with your teacher.)

**Argument in one sentence:** Priestley presents the older Birlings as refusing responsibility to the
end, while the younger generation begins to accept it, so the play argues that change depends on the
young.

**Plan by moment, not by plot:**

1. Opening celebration: Mr Birling's confident speeches, including calling the Titanic
   "unsinkable", create dramatic irony for an audience who knew what followed.
2. Each family member's link to Eva Smith revealed in turn: Priestley's structure makes
   responsibility collective.
3. Sheila's and Eric's changing responses compared with their parents' denial.
4. The final telephone call: the play's circular ending suggests the lesson must be learned again.

**Annotated model paragraph:**

> Priestley undermines Mr Birling's authority before the Inspector even arrives. Birling's claim that
> the Titanic is "unsinkable" is an example of dramatic irony, because an audience in the 1940s knew
> the ship had sunk in 1912 **[AO2: dramatic method]**. By making Birling wrong about something so
> famous, Priestley encourages the audience to doubt his other confident claims, including his view
> that a man need only look after himself and his family **[AO1: interpretation]**. Setting the play
> in 1912 but writing it after the Second World War gives the audience a hindsight the characters lack,
> so Birling's selfish individualism is exposed as the kind of thinking the audience has already seen
> fail **[AO3: context of writing versus setting]**.

Notice that the paragraph quotes one word, refers precisely to events, explains a method, and uses
context to explain *why* Priestley made the choice.

## Building a memory bank for a whole text

Without an extract, your evidence must be portable. For each major character and theme, prepare:

- Four or five short quotations spread across the text.
- One sentence on how the character or theme changes.
- One method per quotation that you can analyse (a word, an image, a stage direction).
- One context link that genuinely explains something.

Short quotations of two to five words are easier to remember and easier to analyse than long ones.
Where you cannot recall exact words, a precise reference to an event is still valid evidence.

## Common mistakes

- **Retelling the plot** in order instead of choosing moments that answer the question.
- **Writing about only the opening or the ending** and missing development.
- **Ignoring the bullet about methods**, so the answer becomes all character and no writer.
- **Treating context as background** instead of linking it to the writer's purpose.
- **Losing AO4 marks** through careless spelling of names, missing capital letters for titles, or
  unclear sentences.
- **Answering both questions**, or switching question half way through.

## Exam technique

- Spend no more than two minutes choosing and five minutes planning.
- Open with a direct answer to the question, not background about the writer.
- Organise paragraphs by ideas that build an argument; chronological order is fine if each paragraph
  still argues.
- Keep the writer's name active: "Priestley presents", "Golding uses", "Orwell shows".
- Leave two minutes to check spelling and punctuation for AO4.
