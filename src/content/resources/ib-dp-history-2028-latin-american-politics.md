**HL only.** This is the tenth study in the Regional study for the Americas, examined on Paper 3 at Higher Level. Paper 3 asks you to evaluate an argument and reach a judgement. The first two inquiry topics are about **Cuba**. The next four each require **one Latin American country**: emerging challenges, the impact of a guerrilla movement, military dictatorship, and democratisation. This guide uses **Argentina** for populism, dictatorship and democratisation, **Peru** for guerrilla war, and **Chile** for comparison. You can use different countries, but know them in depth.

## The six lines of inquiry

The six inquiry topics are: the reasons for the Cuban Revolution; Castro's maintenance of power and impact; emerging challenges in one country; the impact of a guerrilla movement; the impact of military dictatorship; and the reasons for democratisation. Key concepts are **cause and consequence**, **continuity and change** and **significance**. See the [four concepts guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-four-concepts/).

## Key chronology

- 1952: Batista seizes power in Cuba. 1953: Moncada attack. 1956: Granma landing.
- 1 January 1959: Batista flees. 1961: Castro declares Marxism-Leninism.
- 1946 to 1955: Perón in power in Argentina. 1955: Perón overthrown.
- 1970s: guerrillas (Montoneros, ERP) and repression in Argentina.
- 24 March 1976: military coup in Argentina. 1982: Falklands defeat. 1983: elections.
- 1980: Shining Path begins armed struggle in Peru. 1992: Guzmán captured.
- 1973 to 1990: Pinochet in Chile. 1988: plebiscite.
- 1985: Trial of the Juntas in Argentina.

## Reasons for the Cuban Revolution

**Political factors, including Batista.** Fulgencio Batista seized power in a coup in March 1952, cancelling elections. His government was repressive, corrupt and closely tied to US interests and organised crime in Havana. Opposition parties had little hope of a peaceful change, which made armed struggle more attractive.

**Economic factors, including the role of the USA.** Cuba depended on sugar exports, mainly to the US, and US companies owned much of the sugar industry, utilities and mines. Seasonal unemployment in the cane fields was high. Havana was wealthy, but the countryside was poor. The US Platt Amendment (1901 to 1934) and later influence encouraged resentment of US dominance.

**Social factors.** Cuba had a large middle class and relatively high literacy compared with other Latin American states, but inequality between city and countryside, race divisions and limited opportunity frustrated many young people. Students and professionals were prominent in the opposition.

**Role of Castro and Guevara.** Fidel Castro, a lawyer, led an attack on the Moncada barracks in 1953, which failed. At his trial he gave the speech "History Will Absolve Me". After exile in Mexico, he landed with about 80 men on the *Granma* in December 1956, and the survivors regrouped in the Sierra Maestra. Ernesto "Che" Guevara, an Argentine doctor, became a leading commander and thinker. Their guerrilla campaign, an urban underground and Batista's loss of army morale and US support led to his flight on 1 January 1959. Historians debate how much the guerrilla campaign mattered compared with the collapse of Batista's regime and the work of urban opposition.

## Maintenance of power and the impact of Castro's rule

**Political policies.** Castro first claimed to restore the 1940 Constitution, then turned to a one-party state. Revolutionary courts tried and executed Batista officials. The Committees for the Defence of the Revolution (1960) watched neighbourhoods. The Communist Party of Cuba was formed in 1965, and a new Constitution followed in 1976.

**Social policies and nationalism.** The government expanded free education and health care. The 1961 Literacy Campaign sent young volunteers to teach in the countryside and sharply reduced illiteracy. Castro presented the revolution as a continuation of independence under José Martí, and he appealed to Cuban nationalism against the US. Policies on race and women brought gains, such as legal equality and more women in education and work, but the leadership remained mainly male.

**Economic policies.** Land reform (1959 and 1963) and the nationalisation of US and Cuban businesses (1960) put the economy under state control. The US embargo and the loss of expertise and markets hurt. Cuba relied on Soviet subsidies and sugar sales. The 1970 attempt at a ten-million-ton sugar harvest failed. After the Soviet collapse (1991), the economy shrank sharply in the "Special Period".

**Treatment of opposition.** The state used imprisonment, labour camps (the UMAP camps of 1965 to 1968), surveillance and pressure to leave. Large numbers left, in waves, including the Mariel boatlift of 1980. Critics who spoke out, such as signers of the Varela Project petition, were jailed in 2003. Supporters stress the threat from the US (the Bay of Pigs, covert actions) as the reason for control. Historians weigh the achievements in health and education against the loss of political freedom.

## Emerging challenges in one country: Argentina

**Growth of populism.** Juan Perón, elected president in 1946, built a movement based on the unions (the *descamisados*), nationalism and state support for industry and welfare. His wife Eva Perón ran a large foundation and campaigned for women's suffrage, achieved in 1947. Perón was popular among workers but authoritarian towards opponents and the press.

**Crisis of democracy.** After the army overthrew Perón in 1955, Peronism was banned. Governments alternated with military coups (1962, 1966), and elected leaders lacked legitimacy. The economy suffered from inflation, debt and conflict between unions and the army.

**Failure of elected leaders.** Perón returned and won the presidency in 1973 but died in July 1974. His widow, Isabel Perón, could not control violence between left and right, including the right-wing Triple A death squad.

**Growth of guerrilla movements.** The Montoneros (left-wing Peronists) and the People's Revolutionary Army (ERP) carried out kidnappings and bombings in the early 1970s.

**Rise of military dictatorship.** On 24 March 1976, the military overthrew Isabel Perón.

## Impact of a guerrilla movement: Peru

**Origins.** The Shining Path (Sendero Luminoso), a Maoist group led by the philosophy professor Abimael Guzmán, began its war in 1980 in the highland region of Ayacucho. It aimed to destroy the existing state and build a peasant-based revolution.

**Social impact.** The conflict killed tens of thousands. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission (2003) estimated about 69,000 deaths, most of them Indigenous, Quechua-speaking rural people, with the largest share caused by Shining Path and a large share by the armed forces. Villages were attacked, and peasants formed self-defence patrols (*rondas*).

**Political impact.** The war weakened civilian rule and strengthened the army. Alberto Fujimori, elected in 1990, closed Congress in a "self-coup" in 1992. Guzmán was captured in September 1992, and the movement collapsed. Fujimori's government became increasingly authoritarian and was later convicted of human rights crimes.

**Economic impact.** The war disrupted rural life and the economy, and combined with hyperinflation in the 1980s.

**Women.** Women were both members of Shining Path and victims of sexual violence by all sides. Women's organisations, such as mothers' committees and soup kitchens, were vital to survival.

## Military dictatorship: Argentina

**Political factors and suppression of opposition.** The junta under General Jorge Videla called its programme the "National Reorganisation Process". It closed Congress, banned parties and unions, and used secret detention centres. The "Dirty War" targeted guerrillas and anyone judged subversive, including trade unionists, students and priests. Human rights groups estimate about 30,000 people were "disappeared". The official commission (CONADEP, 1984) documented almost 9,000 cases. The Mothers of the Plaza de Mayo began to protest from 1977.

**Economic factors.** Economic minister José Martínez de Hoz opened the economy and cut state spending. Industry suffered, foreign debt rose sharply and real wages fell.

**Social factors.** Censorship, fear and exile affected culture and education.

**Comparison with Chile.** Pinochet's regime (1973 to 1990) used the DINA secret police, followed free-market policies shaped by the "Chicago Boys", and imposed the 1980 Constitution. It killed or disappeared thousands.

## Democratisation: Argentina

**Political factors.** The defeat in the Falklands/Malvinas War (April to June 1982) discredited the junta and divided the military. Protest grew, and elections took place in 1983.

**Economic factors.** Inflation, debt and recession weakened the regime's support.

**Social factors.** The Mothers of the Plaza de Mayo, human rights groups, churches and parties kept pressure on the regime. Some international pressure helped.

**Transitional justice.** President Raúl Alfonsín (1983 to 1989) set up CONADEP, whose report *Nunca Más* (1984) exposed the abuses. In 1985, the Trial of the Juntas convicted Videla and Admiral Massera. Alfonsín then faced military unrest, and laws limiting prosecutions (Full Stop, 1986, and Due Obedience, 1987) were passed. President Menem pardoned the leaders in 1989 and 1990. The laws were annulled in 2003 to 2005, and trials resumed. In Chile, the 1988 plebiscite rejected Pinochet's continued rule, and the Rettig Commission (1991) investigated killings. Historians debate whether truth commissions, trials or amnesties best serve justice and stability.

## Arguments for Paper 3

- Was the Cuban Revolution a popular revolution or a military victory over a collapsing regime?
- Did Castro's social achievements outweigh his lack of political freedom?
- Why did Latin American democracies fail: weak institutions, economic crisis, polarisation, Cold War pressure?
- Did the military regimes succeed in their own aims?
- Does transitional justice help democracy?

## Common mistakes

- Mixing up the guerrilla movements and their aims.
- Not choosing one country for each topic.
- Description of repression without explaining how power was maintained.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I choose countries other than Argentina and Peru?**
Yes. Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Nicaragua, Uruguay and others also work.
