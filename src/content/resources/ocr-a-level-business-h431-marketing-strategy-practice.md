## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by a marketing strategy. **[2]**

**2.** State **two** components of a marketing strategy other than the marketing mix. **[2]**

**3.** Explain **one** reason why a business should monitor progress against its marketing strategy. **[3]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**4.** Peridot Pet Foods has sales of £2.5 million. Its marketing strategy aims to raise sales to £3.0 million next year through a new range for older dogs. The margin on sales is 28%. The extra marketing spending needed is £45,000.

**(a)** Calculate the extra contribution if the target is reached. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the net gain after the extra marketing spending. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the return on the extra spending, to the nearest whole per cent. **[2]**

**(d)** The target for quarter 1 is £0.65 million, and actual sales are £0.58 million. Calculate the variance and the percentage of target achieved, to one decimal place. **[3]**

**(e)** Evaluate the importance of monitoring to the success of Peridot's strategy. **[6]**

**5.** Peridot is comparing three strategic options and scores each from 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent). The criteria have weights.

| Criterion | Weight | X: supermarket launch | Y: online subscription | Z: vet-clinic partnership |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fit with objectives | 40 | 5 | 3 | 4 |
| Cost | 30 | 2 | 4 | 3 |
| Risk | 30 | 3 | 4 | 2 |

**(a)** Calculate the weighted score for each option. **[3]**

**(b)** Recommend and justify a strategy for Peridot Pet Foods. **[9]**

**6.** Tavy Taxis is a local firm with 40 cars. It has lost customers to a ride-hailing app and its board has agreed a marketing strategy to launch its own booking app, offer fixed prices and promote its local drivers. Evaluate the impact of this strategy on Tavy Taxis and its stakeholders. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** A long-term plan [1] for achieving the business's marketing objectives, setting out target markets, positioning, the mix and resources [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any two from: objectives; analysis of the situation; target markets; positioning; budget and resources; timetable and responsibilities; monitoring and control [1] [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Monitoring compares actual results with targets [1], so managers can see early if the strategy is falling behind [1], and act, for example by changing the budget or the mix, before large losses are made [1]. **[3]**

**4. (a)** Extra sales = £0.5 million [1]; 0.5m × 0.28 = **£140,000** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** 140,000 − 45,000 = **£95,000** [1]. **[1]**

**(c)** 95,000 ÷ 45,000 × 100 [1] = **211%** [1]. **[2]**

**(d)** Variance = 0.58 − 0.65 = **−£0.07 million** or −£70,000 (adverse) [1]. Achievement = 0.58 ÷ 0.65 × 100 = **89.2%** [1]. The shortfall is 10.8% of the target [1]. **[3]**

**(e)** Monitoring shows an adverse variance of £70,000 (10.8%) after one quarter, so Peridot can investigate while there is time to react [1]. It may find causes such as slow retailer take-up, weak promotion or a rival's offer [1], and respond by moving spend to the most effective channels [1].
It matters because the return is sensitive to sales: the return of 211% depends on reaching the target, and if extra sales were 10% short (£50,000 less), contribution would be £14,000 lower (28% of £50,000), so the net gain would fall from £95,000 to £81,000 [1].
Limits: figures for one quarter may be affected by seasonality or timing of orders, so a single result may mislead, and monitoring helps only if managers act on it [1].
Judgement: monitoring is essential to a strategy that depends on forecasts, since it turns the strategy into a process of review and correction, provided that the measures are reliable and acted upon [1]. **[6]**

**5. (a)** X: (40×5 + 30×2 + 30×3) ÷ 100 = (200 + 60 + 90) ÷ 100 = **3.5** [1]. Y: (120 + 120 + 120) ÷ 100 = **3.6** [1]. Z: (160 + 90 + 60) ÷ 100 = **3.1** [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Recommendation: Y, online subscription, which scores highest at 3.6 [1].
Justification: it has the lowest cost and risk (both 4), so it suits a business that is working to a limited budget [1]; subscriptions create regular, predictable orders and customer data [1]; it is easy to test with a limited launch before expanding [1].
Why not X: it scores 5 for fit with objectives and could reach many customers, but the cost and risk are higher (2 and 3), as supermarkets demand low prices and large volumes [1]. Z: the partnership has the highest risk of the three (2) and depends on clinics [1].
Evaluation: the scores are judgements, and X is only 0.1 behind Y, so a small change in the weights could change the ranking [1]. Risk: online sales may grow slowly, so Peridot should review progress after six months and consider a supermarket trial later [1]. Judgement: Y is best provided that the weights reflect the business's limited budget [1]. **[9]**

**6.** Benefits for the business: a booking app and fixed prices answer the reasons customers have left, which could win back sales [1]; promoting local drivers differentiates Tavy from the app's anonymous drivers [1]; the app gives data on customers and journeys [1].
Stakeholders: customers gain convenience and price certainty [1]; drivers may benefit from more bookings, but may need training and face more monitoring [1]; owners must pay for the app and promotion, so profit may fall at first [1]; local suppliers (garages, fuel) may gain from more journeys [1].
Limits: the app's cost is high and the larger rival can offer lower prices [1].
Judgement: the strategy is important to Tavy's survival, because it addresses the cause of its decline, but its success depends on the quality of the app and on pricing that is competitive with the rival's [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing activities without an objective or a target market.
- Forgetting to subtract the extra spending to find the net gain.
- Recommending an option without comparing it with another.
- Treating all stakeholders as having the same interest.
