This guide covers **Paper 2 The Sociology of Crime and Deviance and
Social Stratification**, the second of two equally-weighted components
in AQA GCSE Sociology (8192), first teaching 2017. Sociological
research methods (3.7) is examined across both papers rather than as a
standalone section, and is covered in the Paper 1 guide.

## Where this fits in 8192

Paper 1 introduces the sociological approach through the familiar
territory of families and education. Paper 2 applies the same
theoretical toolkit -- functionalism, Marxism, feminism and
interactionism -- to two topics students usually find more abstract:
why some acts are labelled criminal while others are not, and why
society is unequally structured along lines of class, gender and
other factors. The same four perspectives recur throughout both
sections, so a secure grip on what each perspective claims is the
single most transferable skill for this paper.

## Syllabus coverage

**AQA GCSE SOCIOLOGY (8192) — PAPER 2 CRIME AND DEVIANCE, AND SOCIAL
STRATIFICATION**

Section 3.5 Crime and deviance:

- 3.5.1 The social construction of crime and deviance — how concepts of
crime and deviance are socially constructed rather than fixed;
functionalist, interactionist, feminist and Marxist explanations
including anomie, labelling, structural and subcultural theories;
Merton's functionalist account of the causes of crime; Becker's
interactionist account
- 3.5.2 Social control — formal and informal methods of social control,
including unwritten rules and sanctions; comparison of perspectives on
social control; Heidensohn's work on female conformity in patriarchal
societies
- 3.5.3 Criminal and deviant behaviour — factors affecting criminal and
deviant behaviour, including social class, gender, ethnicity and age;
public debates over violent crime, sentencing, young offenders, the
prison system and media coverage of crime; Albert Cohen's work on
delinquent subcultures; Carlen's work on women, crime and poverty
- 3.5.4 Data on crime — the usefulness of the main sources of crime
data, official crime statistics, patterns and trends, and the "dark
figure" of unreported and unrecorded crime

Section 3.6 Social stratification:

- 3.6.1 Functionalist theory of stratification — the functionalist
account of effective role allocation, linking reward to performance;
Davis and Moore's theory; feminist and Marxist critiques
- 3.6.2 Socio-economic class — socio-economic class divisions; Marx's
and Weber's differing accounts of class
- 3.6.3 Life chances — factors affecting life chances including social
class, gender, race and ethnicity, sexuality, age, disability, and
religion and belief; Devine's work revisiting the "affluent worker"
- 3.6.4 Poverty as a social issue — interpretations of poverty
including the culture of poverty and material deprivation, government
attempts to alleviate poverty and unemployment, and the impact of
globalisation; Townsend's work on relative deprivation; Murray's work
on the underclass and New Right theory
- 3.6.5 Power and authority — forms of power and authority, including
traditional, charismatic and rational-legal authority; Weber's account
of power and authority
- 3.6.6 Power relationships — factors affecting power relationships
including social class, gender, sexuality, race, age, disability, and
religion and belief; Walby's work on patriarchy

## How to approach it

Both sections repeatedly ask students to "describe, compare and
contrast" a perspective on the same issue -- crime, social control,
class, poverty, power -- from functionalist, feminist, Marxist and
(in section 3.5) interactionist viewpoints. Rather than revising crime
and stratification as unconnected topics, build a single revision
grid with the four perspectives down one side and each named issue
across the top: it turns eighteen separate points into a pattern that
is far easier to recall under exam pressure. The named sociologists
(Merton, Becker, Heidensohn, Cohen, Carlen, Davis and Moore, Marx,
Weber, Devine, Townsend, Murray, Walby) are commonly the subject of
short-answer questions asking students to identify which perspective
a theorist belongs to and summarise their key idea in a sentence --
practise this specific skill rather than only reading longer
descriptions of each theory.

## Common mistakes

Treating "crime" and "deviance" as identical terms, when the
specification requires students to explain that deviance is broader
than crime (a deviant act need not be illegal). Attributing Marx's
and Weber's views on class interchangeably -- Marx's account centres
on ownership of the means of production, Weber's adds status and
party as separate dimensions of stratification. Confusing the "dark
figure" of crime with the crime rate itself. Describing poverty using
only the culture-of-poverty explanation without contrasting it against
material deprivation, which the specification treats as a genuine
theoretical dispute.

## Worked example

A question asks: "Compare the functionalist and Marxist views of
social stratification." What separates a strong answer from a weak
one?

```
FUNCTIONALIST (Davis and Moore): stratification is necessary and
    beneficial -- society allocates its most able members to its
    most important roles by attaching greater rewards to them,
    motivating people to compete for demanding positions.

MARXIST: stratification exists because the ruling class (the
    bourgeoisie) owns the means of production and uses that
    ownership to exploit the working class (the proletariat) --
    inequality serves the interests of the powerful, not society
    as a whole.

COMPARISON: the functionalist view treats inequality as a fair
    outcome of a competitive, meritocratic process; the Marxist view
    treats it as the product of unequal ownership and power that a
    "fair" competition never actually offered. A strong answer states
    both positions accurately, in their own terms, before comparing
    them -- not just describing one after the other.
```

A weak answer treats this as "one theory says X, the other says Y"
without ever setting the two side by side to explain what specifically
they disagree about.

## Quick revision checklist

- Explain why crime and deviance are described as "socially
constructed" rather than fixed categories.
- Compare Merton's and Becker's explanations of the causes of crime.
- Distinguish formal and informal methods of social control, with an
example of each.
- Describe the "dark figure" of crime and why official statistics may
under-represent the true crime rate.
- Compare Marx's and Weber's views of class, and Davis and Moore's
functionalist justification for stratification.
- Contrast the culture-of-poverty and material-deprivation
explanations of poverty, naming Townsend and Murray.
- For every named sociologist, know which of the four perspectives
they represent and be able to state their argument in one sentence.
