## What 4.3 covers

The syllabus lists four points:

1. the importance of matching costs and revenues
2. how to apply the matching / accruals concepts by using accruals and pre-payments
3. how to prepare journal entries and ledger accounts to record accrued and prepaid expenses
4. how to prepare journal entries and ledger accounts to record accrued and prepaid incomes

## Why matching matters

Profit for a year is only meaningful if it compares the revenue **earned** in
that year with the costs **incurred** in earning it. Cash dates are accidents of
when bills arrive and customers pay. If a business pays two quarterly
electricity bills in January, a cash-based figure would load six months of power
onto one year and leave the next year looking cheap. Three consequences follow
when matching is ignored:

- **Profit is misstated**, so an owner may draw too much or a bank may lend on a
  false picture.
- **Years cannot be compared**, because each year carries a random share of
  costs.
- **The statement of financial position is incomplete**: amounts owed for
  services already used are missing from liabilities, and amounts paid for
  future benefit are missing from assets.

**Other payables** collect accrued expenses and income received in advance.
**Other receivables** collect prepaid expenses and accrued income. Both are
current items, kept separate from trade payables and trade receivables, which
arise from buying and selling goods on credit.

## A rule that works for every account

For an **expense** account:

```
Charge to profit or loss = cash paid
                         + closing accrual − opening accrual
                         − closing prepayment + opening prepayment
```

For an **income** account:

```
Credit to profit or loss = cash received
                         + closing accrued income − opening accrued income
                         − closing income in advance + opening income in advance
```

Opening balances belong to this year if they were prepaid (expense) or received
in advance (income). They reduce this year's figure if they were accruals,
because that cash paid or received in this year settled last year's amount.

## Worked example 1: rent payable in running balance form

Section 2 of the syllabus allows ledger accounts in a three-column running
balance format, so you need to handle adjustments in that layout too.

Kestrel Print Studio rents its premises for 600 a month and has a 31 December
year end. On 1 January 2028 rent for November and December 2027 (1,200) was
owing. During 2028 it paid 1,800 on 15 February, 1,800 on 10 May, 1,800 on
12 August and 2,400 on 9 November. The payments cover November 2027 to
November 2028, so December 2028 rent (600) is owing at the year end.

```
Rent payable account ($)
Date       Details                     Debit    Credit    Balance
1 Jan 28   Balance b/d                           1,200    1,200 Cr
15 Feb 28  Bank                        1,800                600 Dr
10 May 28  Bank                        1,800              2,400 Dr
12 Aug 28  Bank                        1,800              4,200 Dr
9 Nov 28   Bank                        2,400              6,600 Dr
31 Dec 28  Balance c/d (accrual)         600              7,200 Dr
31 Dec 28  Profit or loss                        7,200        nil
1 Jan 29   Balance b/d                             600      600 Cr
```

Check with the rule: 7,800 paid + 600 closing accrual − 1,200 opening accrual
= **7,200**, which is 12 × 600. The credit balance brought down on 1 January
2029 is a liability shown under other payables.

## Worked example 2: an income account with balances at both ends

Kestrel sublets a back room to a photographer for 350 a month. At 1 January 2028
the photographer had already paid for January and February (700). During 2028
Kestrel received 3,150, covering March to November. December's 350 was unpaid at
31 December 2028.

```
Rent receivable account ($)
Date      Details                Amount  Date      Details                Amount
31 Dec 28 Profit or loss          4,200  1 Jan 28  Balance b/d               700
                                         Various   Bank                    3,150
                                         31 Dec 28 Balance c/d               350
                                 ------                                   ------
                                  4,200                                    4,200
                                 ======                                   ======
1 Jan 29  Balance b/d               350
```

The opening credit of 700 was cash received last year for income earned this
year, so it is part of this year's 4,200. The closing debit of 350 is an asset:
other receivables.

## Worked example 3: a prepaid expense across two policies

Kestrel's insurance runs from 1 April each year. The premium paid on
1 April 2027 was 1,440, so 3 months (360) related to January to March 2028.
On 1 April 2028 it paid 1,560 for the next twelve months, of which 9 months
(1,170) fall in 2028 and 3 months (390) are prepaid.

```
Insurance account ($)
Date      Details                Amount  Date      Details                Amount
1 Jan 28  Balance b/d               360  31 Dec 28 Profit or loss          1,530
1 Apr 28  Bank                    1,560  31 Dec 28 Balance c/d               390
                                 ------                                   ------
                                  1,920                                    1,920
                                 ======                                   ======
1 Jan 29  Balance b/d               390
```

The charge is 360 + 1,170 = **1,530**: parts of two different premiums.

## The year-end journal

```
Journal, 31 December 2028 ($)                    Dr       Cr
Rent payable                                    600
   Other payables                                         600
Accrued rent for December 2028

Other receivables                               390
   Insurance                                              390
Insurance prepaid for January to March 2029

Other receivables                               350
   Rent receivable                                        350
Rent receivable due for December 2028
```

Statement of financial position at 31 December 2028: other receivables
390 + 350 = **740** (current assets); other payables **600** (current
liabilities). A narrative under each journal entry is good practice and is
often credited.

## Worked example 4: finding the cash paid

Questions often give the statement of profit or loss figure and ask for the
cash. Kestrel's electricity charge for 2028 was 4,860. Electricity owing was 410
at 1 January 2028 and 530 at 31 December 2028.

Rearrange the rule: cash paid = charge + opening accrual − closing accrual
= 4,860 + 410 − 530 = **4,740**.

Proof by ledger: debits of bank 4,740 and balance c/d 530 total 5,270; credits
of balance b/d 410 and profit or loss 4,860 also total 5,270.

## Effect of getting the adjustment wrong

Suppose Kestrel's December rent of 600 had been treated as a **prepayment**
instead of an accrual. The expense would be 6,600 − 600 = 6,000 instead of
7,200.

| Item | Effect |
|---|---|
| Rent expense | understated by 1,200 |
| Profit for the year | overstated by 1,200 |
| Other receivables | overstated by 600 |
| Other payables | understated by 600 |
| Capital (equity) | overstated by 1,200 |

The error is double the adjustment because the 600 was deducted instead of
added. Leaving an adjustment out altogether misstates profit by the
adjustment itself.

## Common mistakes

- Time-apportioning from the payment date instead of from the start of the
  period the payment covers.
- Treating an opening accrual as part of this year's expense; it was last
  year's cost.
- Bringing a balance down on the wrong side: accrued expenses and income in
  advance are credits; prepaid expenses and accrued income are debits.
- Adding accrued income to trade receivables, or accrued expenses to trade
  payables.
- Writing "Balance c/d" without a matching "Balance b/d" on the first day of
  the next year.

## Exam technique

- **Prepare** a ledger account: dates, details naming the other account, and
  totals on both sides. In running balance form, mark each balance Dr or Cr.
- **Prepare** journal entries: debit first, then credit indented, with a short
  narrative.
- **Calculate**: show the months used, for example 1,560 × 9/12 = 1,170.
- **Explain** the importance of matching: link the adjustment to a correct
  profit for the period and a complete statement of financial position, using
  the business in the question.
