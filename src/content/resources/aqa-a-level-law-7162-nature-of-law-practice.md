## Section A

**1.** State **two** features that distinguish a legal rule from a rule of etiquette. **[2]**

**2.** Identify the date that marks "time immemorial" for the purposes of local custom. **[1]**

**3.** Give the correct term for the party who brings a civil claim, and the word used when a civil defendant is found responsible. **[2]**

**4.** Name the **three** sources of law listed in this part of the specification. **[3]**

## Section B

**5.** A sports league fines a player for breaking its rules on dissent towards referees. Explain why this fine is not a legal sanction, and identify when the player's conduct could become a legal matter. **[4]**

**6.** Explain the difference between a legal rule and a legal principle, giving an example of each. **[4]**

**7.** Outline Hart's distinction between primary and secondary rules, and use it to explain why a school's uniform policy is not law. **[5]**

**8.** Asha drives carelessly and injures Ben, a cyclist. Explain how this one incident could give rise to both criminal and civil proceedings, and why the outcomes could differ. **[6]**

**9.** The residents of a coastal village claim a customary right to launch boats across a private slipway, a practice they say began when a harbour was built in 1850. Advise whether a court would recognise this as a valid custom. **[5]**

**10.** Explain the requirement that a custom be reasonable, using a decided case. **[3]**

**11.** Explain why statute law is the highest source of domestic law, and give an example of a statute that changed a common law rule. **[4]**

**12.** "Custom is of no practical importance as a source of law today." Assess this statement. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two: a legal rule is made by a recognised state authority such as Parliament or the courts [1]; it is enforced by the state through the courts with formal sanctions, whereas etiquette is enforced only by disapproval [1]. (Also accept: a legal rule binds everyone in the jurisdiction; it can be changed at an identifiable moment by legislation.)

**2.** 1189 [1].

**3.** The **claimant** [1]; the defendant is found **liable** [1].

**4.** Custom [1], statute law [1] and the common law [1].

**5.** The fine is imposed by a private body under its own rules, which bind only those who join or play in the league [1]. It is enforced by the league itself, not by the state through the courts, so it is a sanction of a rule of behaviour rather than a legal sanction [1]. The conduct becomes a legal matter if it also breaks a legal rule [1], for example if the dissent turns into an assault on the referee, when the state could prosecute or the referee could sue regardless of the league's view [1].

**6.** A legal rule is specific and applied directly to facts [1], for example a maximum speed limit on a road [1]. A legal principle is a general standard that guides how rules are applied or developed, and is used by judges when no rule clearly applies [1], for example the presumption of innocence or the principle that no one should profit from their own wrong [1].

**7.** Primary rules are rules of conduct that require or forbid behaviour, such as the rule against theft [1]. Secondary rules are rules about rules [1]: the rule of recognition identifies what counts as valid law, while rules of change and adjudication govern how law is altered and how disputes are decided [1]. A uniform policy is a rule of conduct for pupils [1], but it is not law because the legal system's rule of recognition does not treat a school as a recognised law-making authority, so the policy cannot be enforced by the courts as law [1].

**8.** Criminal proceedings: the state, usually through the Crown Prosecution Service, may prosecute Asha for a driving offence [1], aiming to punish and protect the public; the case would be *R v Asha* [1]. Civil proceedings: Ben may sue Asha in negligence as claimant to obtain damages for his injuries [1]. The outcomes could differ because the criminal standard of proof is beyond reasonable doubt [1] while the civil standard is the balance of probabilities [1], so Asha could be acquitted yet still be found liable to compensate Ben [1].

**9.** A valid custom must have existed since time immemorial, fixed at 1189 [1]. Here the practice began in 1850, so it cannot satisfy this test [1], just as in *Simpson v Wells* (1872) a custom failed because it could be traced to a later origin [1]. Even if it were older, the residents would also have to show it was exercised peaceably, openly and as of right, and was reasonable, certain and consistent with statute [1]. The court would therefore not recognise a custom; the residents would need another legal basis, such as an agreement with the owner [1].

**10.** The court will not enforce a custom that is unfair or unreasonable in its effect, however long it has been practised [1]. In *Wolstanton Ltd v Newcastle-under-Lyme Corporation* (1940) a claimed custom allowing the lord of the manor to take minerals from beneath tenants' land without compensation [1] was held unreasonable and therefore invalid [1].

**11.** Because of parliamentary supremacy, an Act of Parliament takes priority over any other domestic source [1], and no court can set aside a validly passed Act [1]. Statute can abolish a custom or reverse a common law rule [1]: the Law Reform (Year and a Day Rule) Act 1996 abolished the common law rule that a death more than a year and a day after the defendant's act could not be murder [1].

**12.** For the statement: modern customs are rarely recognised because the tests are strict, especially time immemorial [1]; most early customs were absorbed into the common law long ago, and statute now governs most areas where custom once operated [1]; successful claims such as *Mercer v Denne* (1905) concern narrow local rights affecting few people [1]. Against: custom remains a recognised source, so a genuine long-standing local right can still be enforced [1]; and custom has a lasting historical importance because the common law itself grew from customs adopted by royal judges [1]. Conclusion: custom is of very limited practical importance today but not of none, because it survives as a narrow local source [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Explaining what a legal rule is without contrasting it with the other rule in the question.
- Giving the time immemorial test but forgetting the other conditions, or the reverse.
- Using criminal terms (guilty, prosecute) for a civil claim.
- In "assess" questions, giving only one side or omitting a final judgement.
- Applying a source of law in the abstract rather than to the facts given.
