These notes cover section 10.3 *The impact of the media on behaviour* of Cambridge International A
Level Sociology (9699) Paper 4. Full explanations are in the
[impact of the media on behaviour study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-impact-of-media-on-behaviour/);
exam-style questions are in the
[impact of the media on behaviour practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-impact-of-media-on-behaviour-practice/).

## The argument in one paragraph

Evidence that violent media cause violence is mixed and methodologically weak: correlations may
reflect choice, laboratory aggression is artificial, and natural experiments often find no effect.
The media's impact on crime is clearer through distorted reporting, deviance amplification, moral
panics and fear of crime. The media can also change behaviour positively through education,
campaigns and mobilisation. Sensationalism and stereotyping affect behaviour through labelling,
self-fulfilling prophecies, stereotype threat and body image.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Imitation** | Copying behaviour seen in the media |
| **Desensitisation** | Reduced emotional response after repeated exposure |
| **Catharsis** | Release of aggression through viewing, reducing violence |
| **Sensitisation** | Seeing consequences of violence increasing opposition to it |
| **Dramatic fallacy** | Belief that crime is mostly dramatic and violent (Felson, 1998) |
| **News values for crime** | Criteria such as threshold, risk, violence, children (Jewkes, 2004) |
| **Deviance amplification spiral** | Reaction to deviance increases it (Wilkins) |
| **Copycat crime** | Offences imitating media reports |
| **Relative deprivation** | Feeling deprived compared with others, encouraged by media images of wealth |
| **Self-fulfilling prophecy** | A label leads people to act in line with it |
| **Stereotype threat** | Performance falls when a negative group stereotype is made salient (Steele and Aronson, 1995) |
| **Sensationalism** | Exaggerated, emotive reporting of rare events |

## Violent media: the evidence

| Study | Method | Finding | Evaluation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Belson (1978) | Large interview survey of London boys | Heavier viewers of TV violence reported more serious violence | Correlation; relies on recall |
| Bandura (1960s) | Laboratory experiments | Children imitated aggression against a doll | Artificial; short-term |
| Feshbach and Singer (1971) | Field study in residential institutions | Boys shown aggressive TV were less aggressive | Supports catharsis; hard to control |
| Hagell and Newburn (1994) | Comparison of young offenders and other young people | No special preference for violent programmes; less access to TV and video | Challenges a direct link |
| St Helena natural experiment | Before-and-after study of television's arrival | No rise in anti-social behaviour | Small, close-knit community |

**Problems in the debate:** defining violence; direction of cause; artificial settings; time scale;
other variables (family, peers, poverty).

**Moral panic about violent media:** "video nasties" and the Video Recordings Act 1984; Barker's
*The Video Nasties* (1984) argued the campaign had little evidence.

## Media and crime

- **Distortion:** Felson's dramatic fallacy; Jewkes's news values (threshold, predictability,
  simplification, individualism, risk, sex, celebrity, proximity, violence, spectacle, children).
- **Deviance amplification spiral:** exaggerated report → labelling → tighter control → isolation
  → more deviance → more reports. Young, *The Drugtakers* (1971).
- **Copycat crime, glamorisation, fear of crime.**
- **Relative deprivation:** media images of consumption may motivate crime (left realism).
- **Moral panics:** shape policing and law.

## Positive impacts

- Education: *Sesame Street* (1969).
- Campaigns: drink-driving, smoking, vaccination.
- Charity: Live Aid (1985); Ice Bucket Challenge (2014).
- Movements: #BlackLivesMatter (from 2013).
- Pro-social modelling of tolerance and cooperation.

## Sensationalism and stereotyping

- Sensationalism raises fear and support for harsh responses.
- Self-fulfilling prophecy through police, employer and teacher reactions.
- Stereotype threat: Steele and Aronson (1995).
- Body image: Becker's Fiji research after television arrived in the mid-1990s.
- Prejudice towards whole groups after sensational coverage of individual crimes.

## Essay plan in five steps

*"Violent media leads to violent behaviour." Evaluate.*

1. Define the mechanisms: imitation, desensitisation, catharsis, sensitisation.
2. For: Belson (1978); Bandura; concern about interactive games.
3. Against: Feshbach and Singer (1971); Hagell and Newburn (1994); St Helena.
4. Methods: correlation and direction of cause; artificial settings; other variables.
5. Judge: possible effects on some individuals in some contexts; no general causal link; note
   moral panics such as "video nasties".

## Exam traps

- Correlation is not causation: say why for Belson.
- Catharsis is an argument that violent media may *reduce* violence; use it.
- Moral panic ≠ deviance amplification.
- Positive effects are on the syllabus; include them.
- Show how stereotypes change behaviour, not just that they exist.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish desensitisation from catharsis.
2. What did Belson (1978) find, and what is the main criticism?
3. What did Feshbach and Singer (1971) find?
4. What did Hagell and Newburn (1994) find?
5. What is the dramatic fallacy?
6. Name four of Jewkes's news values for crime.
7. Set out the deviance amplification spiral in four steps.
8. How might the media increase relative deprivation?
9. Give two examples of positive media impact.
10. What is stereotype threat?
11. What did Becker's research in Fiji suggest?
12. Why is it hard to prove that violent media cause violence?

### Answers

1. Desensitisation: repeated exposure makes violence seem normal. Catharsis: viewing releases
   aggression, reducing violence.
2. Boys who watched more TV violence reported more serious violent acts; it shows correlation, and
   aggressive boys may choose violent programmes.
3. Boys shown aggressive TV in residential institutions were less aggressive than those shown
   non-violent TV, which they saw as catharsis.
4. Young offenders had no special preference for violent programmes and had less access to TV and
   video than other young people.
5. The belief that crime is mostly dramatic and violent, when most is minor property crime.
6. Any four: threshold, predictability, simplification, individualism, risk, sex, celebrity,
   proximity, violence, spectacle, children.
7. Exaggerated report → public concern and labelling → tighter control → isolation and more
   deviance (then more reports).
8. By showing images of wealth and consumption that make people feel deprived compared with others.
9. Any two: *Sesame Street*; health campaigns; Live Aid; the Ice Bucket Challenge;
   #BlackLivesMatter.
10. Reduced performance when people are reminded of a negative stereotype about their group.
11. That the arrival of television was followed by greater concern with weight and disordered eating
    attitudes among adolescent girls.
12. Any one developed: direction of cause; artificial experiments; other variables; difficulty of
    defining violence; long time scales.
