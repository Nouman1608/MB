Sub-topic 3.4 of Cambridge International A Level Law (9084) asks what a court can order when a
contract is broken, how damages are measured, what limits the recovery, and when equity steps in.
Paper 3 tests it both in scenarios, where you must calculate and justify an award, and in essays that
ask you to **evaluate** damages and equitable remedies. This guide builds on the
[Topic 3 overview](/resources/cambridge-a-level-law-law-of-contract-study-guide/) with fuller cases and
a worked calculation.

## What the syllabus lists

- **3.4.1 Common law:** purpose and nature of damages; the measure of damages through expectation
  loss, reliance loss and non-pecuniary loss; limitations through causation, remoteness and
  mitigation; evaluation of damages as a remedy.
- **3.4.2 Equitable:** the purpose of equitable remedies; the nature of specific performance, specific
  restitution, rescission and injunction; limitations on their award; evaluation of their use.

## 3.4.1 Damages

### Purpose and nature

Damages for breach of contract are **compensatory**. The classic statement in Robinson v Harman (1848)
is that the claimant should be placed, so far as money can do it, in the same position as if the
contract had been performed. Damages are not intended to punish the defendant, and they are available
**as of right** once breach and loss are proved, unlike equitable remedies.

### Expectation loss

Expectation damages protect the benefit of the bargain. Common measures are:

- **Difference in value:** for non-delivery of goods, the difference between the contract price and
  the market price at the time delivery should have occurred (Sale of Goods Act 1979, s51).
- **Cost of cure:** the cost of putting the defective performance right. It is not awarded where it
  would be out of all proportion to the benefit. In Ruxley Electronics v Forsyth (1995) a swimming
  pool was built shallower than agreed but was perfectly usable; rebuilding would cost £21,560, which
  was unreasonable, so the House of Lords upheld an award of £2,500 for loss of amenity.
- **Loss of profit:** profits the claimant would have made from performance, subject to remoteness.

### Reliance loss

Where expected profit is too speculative to prove, the claimant can recover wasted expenditure
instead. In Anglia Television v Reed (1972) an actor's withdrawal wasted the company's spending on a
film, including expenditure before the contract was made, because it was within the parties'
contemplation that it would be wasted by breach. But reliance damages cannot be used to escape a bad
bargain: in C and P Haulage v Middleton (1983) the claimant could not recover expenditure he would
have lost even if the contract had been performed.

### Non-pecuniary loss

The traditional rule is that damages are not recoverable for disappointment or injured feelings
(Addis v Gramophone Co, 1909). Exceptions exist where the purpose of the contract was to provide
pleasure, relaxation or peace of mind:

- Jarvis v Swans Tours (1973): a disappointing skiing holiday.
- Jackson v Horizon Holidays (1975): a family holiday, with damages reflecting the family's loss.
- Farley v Skinner (2001): a surveyor told to check for aircraft noise negligently failed to report it;
  damages for loss of amenity were recoverable because peace and quiet was an important object of
  the contract.
- Watts v Morrow (1991): damages for physical discomfort and inconvenience caused by a breach.

### Causation

The breach must be an effective cause of the loss. A claimant's own unreasonable act may break the
chain. In Quinn v Burch Bros (1966) a failure to supply a stepladder merely gave the claimant the
opportunity to use an unsafe trestle; his own choice caused his injury.

### Remoteness

Under Hadley v Baxendale (1854), losses are recoverable if they arise naturally from the breach, or
if they were in the reasonable contemplation of both parties at the time of contracting as a probable
result of breach. Later cases refined the test:

- **Victoria Laundry v Newman Industries (1949):** a late boiler cost the laundry its normal profits,
  which were recoverable, but not the exceptionally lucrative dyeing contracts the defendants knew
  nothing about.
- **The Heron II (Koufos v Czarnikow, 1969):** a shipowner who delivered sugar late was liable for the
  fall in market price because such a loss was "not unlikely" to result.
- **Parsons v Uttley Ingham (1978):** if the type of loss was within contemplation, it does not matter
  that its extent was greater than expected.
- **The Achilleas (Transfield Shipping v Mercator, 2008):** a party is not liable for a type of loss for
  which, in the commercial context, it cannot reasonably be taken to have assumed responsibility.

### Mitigation

The claimant cannot recover loss they could reasonably have avoided (British Westinghouse v
Underground Electric Railways, 1912). In Payzu v Saunders (1919) the buyer should have accepted the
seller's offer to continue supplying on cash terms. The standard is not demanding: in Pilkington v
Wood (1953) the claimant did not have to start uncertain litigation against a third party.

### Evaluation of damages

- **Strengths:** available as of right, predictable rules, and money is an adequate substitute in
  most commercial cases where a replacement can be bought.
- **Weaknesses:** a claimant may be under-compensated where loss is hard to prove, non-pecuniary loss
  is recoverable only in limited categories, remoteness can leave real losses uncompensated, and
  money cannot replace unique property.

## 3.4.2 Equitable remedies

### Purpose

Equitable remedies developed to give justice where damages are **inadequate**. They are
**discretionary**: the court may refuse them even where breach is proved.

### Nature

- **Specific performance** orders the party to perform. It is typical for land and unique goods: in
  Falcke v Gray (1859) the court recognised that rare china jars could be the subject of specific
  performance. In Beswick v Beswick (1968) it was ordered because damages to the estate would have
  been nominal.
- **Specific restitution** orders the return of specific property, rather than its value, where money
  would not be a sufficient substitute.
- **Rescission** sets the contract aside and restores the parties to their pre-contract positions,
  for example after misrepresentation.
- **Injunction** orders a party not to do something, such as breaking a negative promise. In Warner
  Brothers v Nelson (1937) an actress was restrained from working for another studio in breach of an
  exclusive contract.

### Limitations

- **Damages adequate:** no specific performance for ordinary goods available elsewhere.
- **Personal services:** courts will not compel a person to work for another, and an injunction that
  in practice forces performance is refused (Page One Records v Britton, 1968).
- **Constant supervision:** in Co-operative Insurance v Argyll Stores (1997) the House of Lords refused
  to order a supermarket to keep trading.
- **Mutuality:** specific performance is not ordered against one party if it could not be ordered
  against the other, so a minor cannot obtain it (Flight v Bolland, 1828).
- **Conduct and delay:** "he who comes to equity must come with clean hands", and delay defeats
  equity. Hardship to the defendant may lead to refusal (Patel v Ali, 1984).
- **Bars to rescission:** affirmation (Long v Lloyd, 1958), lapse of time (Leaf v International
  Galleries, 1950), impossibility of restoring the parties, and the intervention of third-party rights.

### Evaluation of equitable remedies

Discretion allows the court to fit the remedy to the case and protect interests that money cannot,
but it reduces predictability. Refusal for personal services protects individual liberty, though it
can leave employers or promoters without an effective remedy.

## Worked example: calculating damages

*Scenario: Bayview Café orders a coffee machine for £4,000 from BrewTech Ltd, delivery 1 May.
BrewTech fails to deliver. On 1 May the market price for the same machine is £4,600. Bayview does not
buy a replacement until 1 July, by which time the price is £5,200. Bayview also claims £3,000 profit
lost while it had no machine, and £10,000 from a lucrative catering contract it had not mentioned.*

1. **Market rule:** damages are £4,600 − £4,000 = £600, measured at the date delivery should have
   occurred (s51). The extra £600 from delaying until July is a failure to mitigate.
2. **Lost profits:** ordinary profits from using the machine arise naturally from non-delivery
   (Hadley, first limb), but only for the period Bayview reasonably needed to buy a replacement.
3. **Catering contract:** like the dyeing contracts in Victoria Laundry, it was not communicated, so
   the £10,000 is too remote.
4. **Conclusion:** £600 plus normal profits lost for a reasonable replacement period.

## Common mistakes

- Awarding cost of cure automatically, without asking whether it is reasonable (Ruxley).
- Claiming both expectation and reliance damages for the same loss.
- Treating equitable remedies as available as of right.
- Ignoring the date at which market loss is measured, or forgetting mitigation.
