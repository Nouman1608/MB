These notes condense **3.1.6.4 Effect of concentration and pressure** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3: the qualitative effect of concentration, and of the pressure of a gas, on collision frequency and so on rate. It is International AS content, assessed in Unit 2.

## Core recall

- Rate depends on the number of **successful collisions per unit time**.
- Successful collisions per unit time = collision frequency × proportion of collisions with E ≥ Ea (and suitable orientation).
- **Concentration** and **gas pressure** change the **collision frequency** only.
- They do **not** change particle energies, the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution's shape or peak position, or the **proportion** of particles with E ≥ Ea.
- The specification asks for **qualitative** effects: direction and reason, not size. How much the rate changes depends on the order, which is A2 content in rate equations.

## Concentration: the three-step chain

1. Higher concentration → **more particles in the same volume**.
2. → **more frequent collisions** between reactant particles.
3. → **more collisions with E ≥ Ea per second** → higher rate.

The reverse chain explains a lower rate. Each step is often a separate mark.

## Pressure of a gas: the three-step chain

1. Higher pressure at constant temperature → **same number of molecules in a smaller volume**, so more molecules per unit volume (higher concentration of each gas).
2. → **more frequent collisions**.
3. → **more collisions with E ≥ Ea per second** → higher rate.

Link to the ideal gas equation: n/V = p/(RT), so at fixed T the amount of gas per unit volume is proportional to pressure.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning for 3.1.6.4 |
|---|---|
| Rate of reaction | Change in concentration (or amount) of a reactant or product per unit time |
| Collision frequency | Number of collisions between reactant particles per unit time in a given volume |
| Successful collision | A collision with energy at least Ea and suitable orientation, so that reaction occurs |
| Activation energy, Ea | Minimum energy particles need when they collide for a reaction to occur |
| Concentration | Amount of substance per unit volume, in mol dm⁻³ |
| Pressure (of a gas) | Proportional to the amount of gas per unit volume at constant temperature |
| Limiting reagent | The reactant fully used up; it fixes the final amount of product |
| Initial rate | Rate at the start, shown by the gradient of the curve at time zero |

## Reading rate graphs

**Product formed against time**

- Steepest at the start, when reactant concentrations are highest.
- Gradient falls as reactants are used up: fewer particles per unit volume, less frequent collisions.
- Horizontal when the limiting reagent is used up.

**Changing the concentration of one reactant**

| What changes | Initial gradient | Final amount of product |
|---|---|---|
| Lower concentration of a reactant **in excess** | Less steep | Unchanged |
| Lower concentration of the **limiting** reactant (same volume) | Less steep | Smaller |
| Higher concentration of a reactant **in excess** | Steeper | Unchanged |

Always identify the limiting reagent from the amounts before sketching a second curve.

**Reactant concentration against time** falls steeply at first, then more slowly, flattening towards zero (or towards a constant if the reactant is in excess).

## Maxwell–Boltzmann and concentration

- For a fixed volume, a more concentrated sample gives a curve **taller at every energy by the same factor**.
- The **peak stays at the same energy**; the **fraction** beyond Ea is unchanged; the **number** beyond Ea in that volume increases.
- Never move the peak sideways for a concentration or pressure change; that means a temperature change.

## Pressure: special cases

- **Solutions and solids**: pressure has no significant effect, because liquids and solids hardly compress.
- **Gas mixtures**: compressing to one-third of the volume at constant temperature triples the concentration of **every** gas.
- **Adding an unreactive gas at constant volume**: total pressure rises but the reactant concentrations do not, so the rate does not change.

## Contrast with the other factors

| Factor | What it changes |
|---|---|
| Concentration or gas pressure | Collision frequency only |
| Temperature | Proportion with E ≥ Ea (main effect) and collision frequency (small effect) |
| Catalyst | Provides a route with lower Ea, so a larger proportion can react; curve unchanged |

## Exam traps

- "More collisions" alone: add **per second** or **more frequent**.
- "Particles have more energy" for a concentration or pressure change: wrong.
- "Larger proportion of successful collisions" for concentration: wrong; the proportion is unchanged.
- Pressure arguments for a reaction in aqueous solution: pressure is irrelevant there.
- "Doubling the concentration doubles the rate": an unjustified quantitative claim at AS.
- A sketched second curve that ends at the same height when the limiting reagent was reduced.
- Forgetting that the rate falls during a reaction because the concentrations fall.

## Quick self-test

1. Which of the two factors that set the rate does concentration change?
2. State the effect of halving the concentration of a reactant in solution on the proportion of particles with E ≥ Ea.
3. Write the three-step explanation for why a more concentrated acid reacts faster with marble chips.
4. A gas is compressed to half its volume at constant temperature. What happens to the number of molecules per dm³?
5. Use n/V = p/(RT) to find the amount of gas per dm³ at 200 kPa and 500 K (R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹).
6. Why does increasing the pressure not affect the rate of neutralisation of NaOH(aq) by HCl(aq)?
7. Why is a graph of volume of gas against time steepest at the start?
8. Zinc is in excess. The acid concentration is halved but its volume kept the same. How do the initial gradient and final volume of hydrogen change?
9. The acid is in excess. The acid concentration is halved. How do the initial gradient and final volume change?
10. Argon is pumped into a sealed, fixed-volume flask containing reacting gases at constant temperature. What happens to the rate, and why?
11. How does a Maxwell–Boltzmann curve for a fixed volume change when the concentration of gas molecules doubles at constant temperature?
12. Which factor, concentration or temperature, changes the proportion of collisions that are successful?

### Answers

1. The **collision frequency**.
2. **No change**: the particles' energies are unchanged.
3. More H⁺ ions per unit volume → more frequent collisions with the marble surface → more collisions with E ≥ Ea per second, so the rate is higher.
4. It **doubles**.
5. n/V = 200 000 ÷ (8.31 × 500) = 48.1 mol m⁻³ = **0.0481 mol dm⁻³**.
6. The reactants are in solution; liquids hardly compress, so pressure does not change their concentrations or collision frequency.
7. Reactant concentrations are highest at the start, so collisions are most frequent then.
8. Initial gradient **less steep**; final volume **halved** (the acid is limiting).
9. Initial gradient **less steep**; final volume **unchanged** (the other reactant is limiting).
10. **No change**: the concentrations of the reacting gases, and so their collision frequency, are unchanged; only the total pressure rises.
11. It is **twice as tall at every energy**, with the peak at the same energy; the fraction beyond Ea is unchanged.
12. **Temperature**. Concentration changes only how often collisions happen.
