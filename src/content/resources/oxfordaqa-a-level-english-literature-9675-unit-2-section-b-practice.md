## Section A: concepts

**1.** Name the four poets whose selections are set for Unit 2 Section B in Version 5.3. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why writing about a selection of poems is different from writing about a single poem.
**[2]**

**3.** Explain two ways in which a place-name can contribute to the meaning of a poem. **[4]**

**4.** Explain what is meant by a poem "layering time on place", and why this matters in an essay on
place. **[3]**

**5.** Explain the difference between enjambment and caesura and how each can shape a reader's
experience of a place. **[4]**

## Section B: an original poem and essay skills

**Poem (original).**

> *Allotment, March*
>
> My father's spade is where he left it, upright
> in the bed he never turned. The shed door sticks.
> Next plot, a woman I don't know is lighting
> a small, illegal fire of last year's sticks.
> The council's letter says *reallocate*.
> I read it twice. Somewhere a blackbird starts
> the same four notes it always sang. The gate
> remembers how to swing. I learn the parts
> of this I can keep: the view across the line,
> the smell of earth, the word he used for weeds.
> The rest goes back to them. The rest was mine.

**6.** Analyse how the poet presents the allotment as a place of memory. **[6]**

**7.** Explain how the poem presents place as a social or political space as well as a personal one.
**[4]**

**8.** Analyse how rhyme and line breaks contribute to the poem's sense of place. **[4]**

**9.** A student wants to compare this poem with a set poem about returning to a place after loss.
Explain two features of an effective comparison paragraph. **[2]**

**10.** For your own poet, choose two poems for the question: "Explore the significance of home in
the poems you have studied." Explain why your two poems make a strong pairing. **[3]**

**11.** Plan an essay for the question: "Places in the poems are always shaped by the past." List
five elements your plan should contain. **[5]**

**12.** Read this student paragraph: "Wordsworth loved nature and lived in the Lake District, which
is very beautiful. In 'Tintern Abbey' he talks about going back to the Wye and how nice it is. This
shows place is important to him." Identify two weaknesses and explain how each could be fixed.
**[4]**

## Answers

**1.** William Wordsworth, Robert Frost, Thomas Hardy and Seamus Heaney: all four [2]; two or three
[1].

**2.** A selection invites range, so an essay usually discusses more than one poem and shows
patterns and differences across them [1]. It also allows comparison, so the argument can be built
from contrasts between poems rather than from one poem's details alone [1].

**3.** For each of two ways, way identified [1] and explained [1]. For example: a place-name roots
the poem in a real location [1], so private feeling is tied to a public, historical place [1]; its
sound or origin can carry a language or culture [1], so naming becomes an act of claiming or
remembering, as in a poem built around an Irish place-name [1]; repeated in a title or refrain [1],
it can become a symbol of what the place means to the speaker [1].

**4.** It means presenting a place as it is now and as it was in the past within the same poem [1],
often through shifts in tense or memory [1]. It matters because the significance of the place then
lies in change, loss or continuity, which gives an essay an argument rather than a description [1].

**5.** Enjambment runs a sentence over a line break [1], which can mimic movement through a landscape
or a view that opens out [1]. Caesura is a pause within a line [1], which can halt the reader, as if
stopping to look or remember [1].

**6.** Any six, [1] each:

- The spade "upright / in the bed he never turned" is a frozen moment: the object holds the father's
  absence, and the line break isolates "upright" as if the tool still stands in for him.
- "The shed door sticks" suggests the place resisting the speaker, or neglect since the death.
- The blackbird's "same four notes it always sang" links present sound to the past, showing
  continuity in nature against human loss.
- "The gate / remembers how to swing" personifies the place: memory lives in objects as well as in
  the speaker.
- "The smell of earth" and "the view across the line" are sensory memories that can be kept.
- "The word he used for weeds" places memory in language: the father survives in a private
  vocabulary.
- The final antithesis, "The rest goes back to them. The rest was mine", shows that the place's
  meaning for the speaker cannot be handed over with the land.

**7.** Any four, [1] each: "The council's letter" introduces official power over the place; the
italicised *reallocate* is bureaucratic language that strips the plot of personal meaning; the
neighbour's "illegal fire" shows other users and rules shaping the space; "goes back to them"
presents land as owned by an institution, not by those who worked it; the conflict between public
ownership and private attachment makes the allotment a political as well as a personal place.

**8.** Any four, [1] each: the largely alternating rhymes (reallocate/gate, starts/parts,
line/mine) give the poem an orderly pattern, like plots in rows; the half-rhyme "upright/lighting"
unsettles the opening; "sticks" rhymes with itself, first as the stuck door and then as kindling,
so one sound holds two states of the place; enjambment across "The gate / remembers" mirrors the gate's swing; the end-stopped short
sentences ("I read it twice.") create pauses of shock; the final rhyme "mine" closes the poem with
possession, giving the last word to the speaker.

**9.** Any two, [1] each: a topic sentence naming the shared idea and the difference between the
poems; analysis of a specific method in each poem rather than summary; linking words that keep the
two poems in conversation; a judgement on what the comparison reveals about place.

**10.** Two poems named from the selection [1]; explanation that they share a relevant link to home
[1]; explanation of a difference that will drive the argument [1]. For example, Hardy's "Places"
and "At Castle Boterel": both locate home and love in remembered landscapes, but one presents a
place made empty by loss while the other insists the place still holds the past.

**11.** Any five, [1] each: a one-sentence judgement on "always"; two or three poems chosen to test
the view, at least one complicating it; the methods by which the past shapes place in each (tense,
memory, history, place-names); a poem where place is shaped by present feeling, politics or nature
instead; short, exact quotations; a relevant context (the poet's life or history) linked to the
words; a conclusion weighing how far "always" holds.

**12.** Two weaknesses, each identified [1] and fixed [1]:

- Biography with no link to language [1]: it should analyse how the poem presents the return, for
  example the five years' absence and how memory changes the view [1].
- Vague evaluation ("how nice it is", "important to him") [1]: it should argue what the place
  means, for example that the landscape becomes a source of moral and spiritual strength through
  memory, and support this with a short quotation [1].
- Credit also, as an alternative [1] + [1]: no method is named; it could discuss blank verse and
  the speaker's position above the river.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing about one poem only.
- Paraphrase instead of analysis of language, sound and form.
- Context that is not tied to the words of the poem.

book open, for about an hour.
