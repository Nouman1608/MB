This guide focuses on **3.1.2 Drama**, assessed in Section B of Paper 1 in OxfordAQA International
GCSE English Literature (9275). Paper 1 is taken on both Route A and Route B, so the drama section is
compulsory for everyone. The overview of the whole paper is in the
[Prose and Drama study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-english-literature-prose-and-drama/); this
page goes further into how to analyse a play.

## What 3.1.2 covers

You study one play from the specification's prescribed list. The list can change between
specification versions: OxfordAQA has published a revised specification for first teaching from
September 2026 with refreshed set texts, so confirm the list that applies to your exam year.

Section B is worth **30 marks** in a 1 hour 30 minute, **closed-book** paper. You answer one question
on your set play, choosing between an essay-style question and a passage-based question with a
printed extract. The same three assessment objectives apply as for prose:

- **AO1**: understanding of, and engagement with, themes, ideas and contexts;
- **AO2**: analysis of how the playwright creates meanings and effects;
- **AO3**: an informed personal response, with appropriate terminology and accurate writing.

The difference is what counts as a "method". In a play, methods include everything that happens on
stage, not only the words.

## Thinking like an audience

A novel is read alone, at the reader's own pace. A play is watched, by an audience together, in real
time. That changes how meaning works:

- The audience **sees** characters who are silent, so reactions matter as much as speeches.
- The audience often **knows more** than a character, which creates dramatic irony.
- Timing is controlled by the playwright and the performers, so pace, pauses and interruptions shape
  tension.
- There is usually no narrator to explain motives, so the audience must infer them from speech and
  action.

Writing "the audience" rather than "the reader" is a small habit that signals you understand the
form. Better still, explain **what an audience would experience** at a particular moment.

## The playwright's toolkit

| Method | What to look for | Typical effect |
|---|---|---|
| Stage directions | Movement, gesture, tone, lighting, sound, set | Shows what the playwright wants the audience to see or feel |
| Dialogue | Who speaks most, who interrupts, who is silenced | Reveals power and relationships |
| Soliloquy and aside | A character speaking alone, or to the audience only | Lets the audience into private thoughts; creates complicity |
| Dramatic irony | The audience knows what a character does not | Builds tension, sympathy or dread |
| Entrances and exits | Who arrives or leaves, and when | Interrupts, shifts power or ends a scene sharply |
| Structure | Acts, scenes, climax, resolution, time gaps | Controls build-up and release of tension |
| Props and set | Objects given repeated attention | Become symbols the audience can see |
| Verse and prose (where used) | Changes in form of speech | Can mark status, emotion or a breakdown of control |

Not every play uses every method. Choose the ones your set play relies on most.

## Worked example 1: reading a short scene as performance

The scene below is invented for practice and is not from any set text.

> *A kitchen. Evening. MARA sets three plates on the table. DANIEL enters, still wearing his coat.*
>
> DANIEL: You've laid a place for him.
>
> MARA: He might come.
>
> DANIEL: *(He takes off his coat slowly, but does not hang it up.)* He won't.
>
> *Pause. MARA straightens the third fork.*
>
> MARA: Then it costs nothing to be ready.

An analytical paragraph aiming for the higher levels:

> The playwright uses the setting of an ordinary meal to stage a conflict that neither character
> will name. The third plate is a visual symbol that the audience notices before anyone speaks, so
> the absent person dominates the stage. Daniel's coat, removed "slowly" but not hung up, suggests a
> man who has arrived but is not prepared to stay in this conversation. The stage direction "Pause"
> gives the audience time to feel the weight of "He won't", and Mara's response is an action rather
> than an argument: straightening the fork shows that her hope survives as quiet ritual. Her final
> line sounds practical, yet the audience hears it as a refusal to give up, which leaves the tension
> unresolved at the end of the exchange.

Look at what makes it strong: it names staging methods (prop, stage direction, pause), analyses short
quotations, and keeps the audience's experience at the centre.

## Worked example 2: building an essay on the whole play

Question: *How does the playwright present the relationship between a parent and child in your
studied play?*

1. **Argument:** state what the play suggests about the relationship, for example that love is real
   but expressed through control.
2. **First appearance:** how the relationship is introduced on stage, including who speaks first and
   who holds the stage.
3. **Turning point or climax:** a moment of confrontation, with attention to staging as well as
   dialogue.
4. **Ending:** where the relationship stands in the final scene, and how the closing image or exit
   leaves the audience feeling.
5. **Context:** one precise link to the attitudes of the play's time or setting, used to explain a
   choice the playwright makes.

For each point, prepare one quotation with a **staging note**: how it might be delivered, what the
other character is doing, or what the stage directions require.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating the play as a novel.** Answers that refer to "the reader" and never mention staging
  miss part of AO2.
- **Inventing a production.** You may suggest how a line could be performed, but keep the focus on
  the playwright's text and choices, not on a film or stage version you have seen.
- **Ignoring silent characters.** Who watches, who stays silent, and who leaves are often the most
  revealing details in a scene.
- **Long speeches quoted in full.** Pick the two or three words that carry the effect.
- **Listing methods.** "There is dramatic irony and a stage direction" is not analysis; explain what
  each does at that moment.
- **Losing the structure.** Remember where a scene sits in the play: the same line can mean something
  different before and after the climax.

## Exam technique and command words

Spend about 45 minutes on Section B, including five minutes of planning. If you finish prose first,
check the clock before starting: running short on drama is a common way to lose marks on Paper 1.

- **How does the playwright present...?** Analyse methods, including staging, in every paragraph.
- **Explore** invites more than one reading, such as how different audiences might respond.
- **Discuss** asks for a reasoned argument supported by evidence from across the play.

On a **passage-based** question, start with the printed extract and analyse it closely. Read the
wording carefully: if it asks you to refer to the play as a whole as well, move outward to connect
the extract with earlier or later moments. On an **essay-style** question, range across the whole
play from memory.

## Revising a closed-book play

- Learn short quotations for each major character, each with a staging note.
- Know the shape of the play: where the climax falls, and what changes between the opening and the
  closing scene.
- Practise "director's notes": take a short exchange and write three sentences on how it could be
  staged and why.
- Rehearse one timed paragraph from memory every few days.
