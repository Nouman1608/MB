- **influences on the extent and methods of employee involvement in decision making**, with employee representation methods including trade unions and works councils
- **how to manage and improve employer-employee communications and relations**
- **the value of good employer-employee relations**.

Companion pages: [revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-employer-employee-relations-revision-notes/) and [practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-employer-employee-relations-practice/). ## What employer-employee relations means

**Employer-employee relations** (also called industrial or employee relations) is the relationship between the management of a business and its workforce, including the way pay, conditions and decisions are discussed and disagreements are resolved. Relations may be cooperative, with trust and open communication, or adversarial, with disputes, mistrust and conflict.

## Employee involvement in decision making

**Employee involvement** (or participation) means giving employees a voice in decisions that affect them. It ranges along a scale.

| Level | What happens | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Informing | Management tells staff about decisions | Notice board, email, team briefing |
| Consulting | Management asks for views before deciding | Staff survey, suggestion scheme, works council discussion |
| Joint decision making | Staff share in making the decision | Employee directors on the board, negotiation with a union over pay |
| Employee ownership | Staff hold shares | Share ownership plans, employee-owned companies |

### Methods of involvement and representation

- **Trade unions.** A trade union is an organisation of workers that represents members' interests, especially in **collective bargaining**: negotiating pay, hours and conditions with the employer on behalf of all members. Unions also help individual members in disciplinary or grievance cases and campaign on health and safety. In a dispute, they may organise **industrial action** such as a strike (stopping work), a work-to-rule or an overtime ban.
- **Works councils.** A works council is a body of elected employee representatives (and sometimes management) that meets regularly to be informed and consulted on workplace matters such as working hours, safety, training or major changes. It focuses on cooperation rather than bargaining over pay.
- **Other methods**: team meetings, quality circles, suggestion schemes, staff surveys, employee representatives on boards, employee share schemes, and online forums.

### Trade unions and works councils compared

| | Trade union | Works council |
|---|---|---|
| Members | Workers who choose to join | Representatives of the whole workforce (elected) |
| Main role | Negotiate pay and conditions; represent members | Consult and share information on workplace issues |
| Power | Can threaten industrial action | Mainly advisory; relies on goodwill |
| Style | Negotiation, sometimes adversarial | Cooperation and joint problem solving |
| Benefit to employer | One voice for many workers; settles agreements that cover all | Early warning of problems; better decisions |
| Drawback to employer | Strikes; wage demands; slower change | Time-consuming; may raise expectations that the council cannot meet |

### Influences on the extent and methods of involvement

- **Management style and culture**: autocratic managers keep decisions to themselves; democratic or participative managers consult; paternalistic managers inform and explain decisions.
- **Skills and attitudes of the workforce**: skilled, educated staff expect and can contribute to decisions.
- **Size of the business**: larger businesses need formal channels such as councils; small businesses can talk informally.
- **Nature of the decision**: urgent or confidential matters are decided by management, and long-term plans may be consulted.
- **Legal requirements**: rules on consulting about redundancies, safety, or transfers of employment.
- **Presence and strength of trade unions**, and the business's history of relations.
- **Type of industry and work**: complex, creative or safety-critical work benefits from staff input.
- **Technology**: online surveys, intranets and video meetings make involvement quicker and cheaper, while remote work makes it harder to build relationships.
- **Labour market**: when skilled staff are scarce, employers listen more to keep them.

### Benefits and costs of involvement

**Benefits:** better decisions using employees' knowledge; stronger commitment and motivation (staff feel valued); smoother change because staff understand and accept the reasons; fewer disputes; lower turnover; ideas for improvements and cost savings.

**Costs:** decisions are slower; management may feel they lose control; staff may expect their view always to win and become disappointed; time and money for meetings and training; confidential information is harder to protect; representatives may not speak for all staff.

## Managing and improving communication and relations

**Communication** should be two-way, clear, timely and honest. Ways to improve it:

- **Top-down**: team briefings, newsletters, intranet and email, video updates from senior managers.
- **Bottom-up**: suggestion schemes, staff surveys, open-door policies, regular one-to-one meetings, representatives on councils.
- **Lateral**: cross-team meetings and shared digital platforms.
- **Feedback loops**: show employees what happened to their suggestions ("you said, we did").
- **Training managers** to listen and to handle conflict.
- **Fair, clear procedures** for grievances and discipline.
- **Early involvement** in changes, such as consulting before restructuring.
- **Using an independent third party**: mediation or arbitration by an independent body such as ACAS (Advisory, Conciliation and Arbitration Service) can settle disputes.

**Barriers to good communication:** many layers of management; remote or shift working; lack of trust; information overload; different languages or cultures; managers who do not listen.

**Resolving disputes** usually follows stages: informal discussion, formal negotiation, then conciliation (a neutral person helps both sides agree), mediation or arbitration (a neutral person proposes or decides a settlement), and, as a last resort, tribunal or industrial action.

## Types of dispute and how they develop

Disputes usually begin over pay, working hours, job security, changes to working methods or perceived unfair treatment. Early signs include rising grievances, absence, falling output and informal complaints. If these are ignored, they may turn into formal claims and then industrial action. Employers can reduce the chance of escalation by listening early, explaining decisions, acting fairly and offering a clear route for complaints. Unions, too, often prefer a negotiated settlement, because strikes cost members pay. The best-managed relations resolve most disagreements before they become disputes.

## The value of good employer-employee relations

- **Higher motivation and productivity**, because staff feel trusted and valued.
- **Lower absence and labour turnover**, which cut costs.
- **Fewer disputes and less industrial action**, so output and reputation are protected.
- **Smoother change**: staff accept new technology or structure when they understand why.
- **Better ideas and quality** through staff suggestions.
- **A strong employer brand** that attracts skilled recruits.
- **Better customer service**, because contented staff deal better with customers.
- **Lower legal and tribunal costs**.

Poor relations bring strikes, go-slows, resignations, sabotage, absence and a damaged reputation.

### Worked example: the cost of a dispute

Marden Logistics has a payroll of £3 million. The union asks for a 4% pay rise (£120,000 a year). The employer offers 2% (£60,000 a year). The gap is **£60,000** a year.

A strike by distribution centre workers would, the employer estimates, lose sales of £210,000, with a gross profit margin of 35%, and cost £15,000 in customer compensation. Wages not paid during the strike would be £16,000.

- Lost gross profit = £210,000 × 0.35 = **£73,500**.
- Total cost of the strike = £73,500 + £15,000 = **£88,500**.
- Net cost after wages saved = £88,500 − £16,000 = **£72,500**.

If the employer settles at 3%, the extra pay is £3m × 0.03 = **£90,000**, which is £30,000 a year more than the offer. The strike's net cost of £72,500 is about 2.4 times that extra annual cost. However, a pay rise is a permanent addition to payroll, while a strike's cost is one-off. The strike cost is also an under-estimate, because it ignores lasting damage to customers' trust and staff morale. So the decision depends on the business's ability to afford a permanent increase, on competitors' pay, on how long a dispute might last and on whether other staff will make similar claims. Good relations before the dispute would have made agreement easier.

## Technique for exam questions

- Link the choice of involvement to the case: workforce skills, union presence, management style, size.
- Separate unions (bargaining, possible industrial action) from works councils (consultation).
- Use figures to compare the cost of a dispute with the cost of a settlement.
- Evaluate: weigh benefits against costs of time and control, and say what the decision depends on.

## Common mistakes

- Treating unions and works councils as the same thing.
- Assuming unions always cause conflict, or that works councils have no value.
- Saying that more involvement is always better.
- Ignoring the cost and speed of decisions.
- Forgetting that communication needs to be two-way.
- Comparing the cost of a strike with a pay offer without considering that a pay rise is permanent.
