## 19.1 Energy flow

### Where the energy comes from (19.1.1–19.1.3)

The **Sun** is the principal source of energy input to most biological systems. Plants trap light energy in **photosynthesis** and store it as chemical energy in organic compounds.

Most forms of life depend completely on photosynthesis because:
- it is how light energy enters food chains; animals and fungi cannot use light and must eat organic food, so chains start with a photosynthesising producer;
- photosynthesis releases the oxygen most organisms use in aerobic respiration.

Energy **flows** in one direction: light → chemical energy in producers → chemical energy in consumers and decomposers. At every stage some energy is released by respiration and lost to the **environment as heat**. Energy is not recycled. Chemical elements, such as carbon and nitrogen, are.

### Key terms (19.1.5)

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Producer | Makes its own organic nutrients, usually by photosynthesis |
| Consumer | Gets energy by feeding on other organisms |
| Herbivore | Animal that gets energy by eating plants |
| Carnivore | Animal that gets energy by eating other animals |
| Decomposer | Organism that gets energy from dead or waste organic material |

### Food chains and food webs (19.1.4, 19.1.6)

A **food chain** shows the transfer of energy from one organism to the next. Each arrow points **in the direction energy flows**, from the organism eaten to the organism that eats it:

```
grass → grasshopper → frog → snake
```

Each feeding level is a **trophic level**: grass is the producer, the grasshopper the primary consumer (herbivore), the frog and snake secondary and tertiary consumers (carnivores).

A **food web** is a network of interconnected food chains. Most animals eat more than one kind of food, so a web is more realistic than a single chain. To interpret a web, follow every arrow into and out of the organism named. If frogs died out, grasshoppers might increase (fewer eaten) and snakes might decrease, or eat more of another prey species.

### Why energy transfer is inefficient (19.1.7–19.1.8)

Only a small fraction of the energy in one trophic level becomes part of the next. Energy is lost because:
- not all of the organism is eaten (roots, bones, fur);
- not all that is eaten is digested and absorbed; some leaves as faeces;
- energy is released by **respiration** and lost as **heat**;
- energy goes to **movement** and other life processes, not to growth;
- some energy stays in excretory products, such as urea.

So food chains usually have **fewer than five trophic levels**: by the fifth level there is too little energy left to support a population.

**Worked example 1.** A grassland receives 20,000 kJ per m² per year in grass, 2,000 kJ in rabbits and 180 kJ in foxes. Calculate the efficiency of each transfer.

```
efficiency = (energy in next level ÷ energy in level below) × 100
grass → rabbits: (2,000 ÷ 20,000) × 100 = 10%
rabbits → foxes: (180 ÷ 2,000) × 100 = 9%
energy not passed to rabbits = 20,000 − 2,000 = 18,000 kJ per m² per year
```

### Eating crops versus livestock (19.1.9)

People eating crops are primary consumers. People eating livestock fed on crops are secondary consumers, so there is one extra transfer and more energy is lost.

**Worked example 2.** A crop contains 100,000 kJ. Assume 10% is passed on at each transfer.

```
humans eat the crop:            100,000 × 0.10 = 10,000 kJ
cattle eat crop, humans eat cattle: 100,000 × 0.10 × 0.10 = 1,000 kJ
```

Eating the crop gives humans ten times more energy, so the same land feeds more people.

### Pyramids of numbers, biomass and energy (19.1.10)

Draw horizontal bars, producer at the bottom, widths in proportion to the values.

| Pyramid | Each bar shows | Can it be an odd shape? |
|---|---|---|
| Numbers | Number of organisms at each level | Yes: one large tree supports thousands of insects |
| Biomass | Dry mass of organisms at each level | Usually pyramid-shaped |
| Energy | Energy at each level over a set time | Never inverted: energy is lost at every transfer |

**Worked example 3.** One oak tree (2,000 kg) supports 5,000 caterpillars (0.004 kg each), which feed 30 birds (0.02 kg each).

```
numbers: 1 → 5,000 → 30        (narrow base, wide middle: not a pyramid)
biomass: 2,000 kg → 5,000 × 0.004 = 20 kg → 30 × 0.02 = 0.6 kg   (a true pyramid)
```

The pyramid of numbers ignores size; the pyramid of biomass allows for it.

## 19.2 Nutrient cycles

### The carbon cycle (19.2.1)

| Process | Effect on atmospheric carbon dioxide |
|---|---|
| Photosynthesis | Removes it; carbon becomes carbohydrates in plants |
| Feeding | Passes carbon compounds along food chains |
| Respiration (plants, animals, decomposers) | Returns it to the air |
| Decomposition | Decomposers respire dead organic matter, releasing it |
| Formation of fossil fuels | Dead organisms that did not decay are compressed over millions of years into coal, oil and gas, locking carbon away |
| Combustion | Burning fossil fuels or wood releases it |

### The nitrogen cycle (19.2.2)

Plants and animals need nitrogen to make **amino acids and proteins**, but cannot use nitrogen gas directly. The stages:

1. **Decomposition**: bacteria and fungi break down protein in dead plants, animals and waste to **ammonium ions**.
2. **Nitrification**: nitrifying bacteria convert ammonium ions to **nitrate ions**.
3. **Nitrogen fixation**: lightning, or nitrogen-fixing bacteria (in soil and in root nodules), convert nitrogen gas into compounds plants can use.
4. **Absorption**: plants take up nitrate ions through root hair cells by active transport.
5. **Production of amino acids and protein**: plants combine nitrogen with carbohydrates to make amino acids, then proteins.
6. **Feeding and digestion**: animals eat plants, digest the protein to amino acids, and build their own proteins.
7. **Denitrification**: denitrifying bacteria convert nitrate ions back to nitrogen gas.

You do not need the names of individual bacteria.

### Decomposition (19.2.3)

Fungi and bacteria are **decomposers**. They secrete enzymes onto dead organic matter, absorb the digested products and respire them. This returns carbon dioxide to the air and ammonium ions to the soil, recycling elements for producers.

## 19.3 Ecosystems and biodiversity

- **Population**: a group of organisms of one species, living in the same area, at the same time.
- **Community**: all of the populations of different species in an ecosystem.
- **Ecosystem**: a unit containing the community of organisms and their environment, interacting together.
- **Biodiversity**: the number of different species that live in an area.

The rate of population growth depends on **food supply**, **competition**, **predation** and **disease**. More food speeds growth; more competitors, predators or disease slow it, or make the population fall.

The **growth of the human population** is increasing the demand for global resources: food, water, land, fuel, timber and minerals. This pressure drives many of the impacts in 19.4.

## 19.4 Effects of humans on ecosystems

### Deforestation (19.4.1)

Forests are cleared for farmland, timber and building. Consequences:
- **Biodiversity**: habitats and food sources are destroyed, so fewer species live there.
- **Extinction**: species found only in that forest may die out.
- **Loss of soil**: without roots to hold it, rain washes soil away.
- **Flooding**: fewer trees take up and hold back rain, so water runs off quickly, and eroded soil silts up rivers.
- **Carbon dioxide**: fewer trees photosynthesise, and burning or decomposing trees release carbon dioxide, so its atmospheric concentration rises.

### Over-harvesting and non-native species (19.4.2)

**Over-harvesting** removes plants or animals faster than they can reproduce, so populations fall and may become extinct. Predators that relied on them lose food. **Introducing a non-native species** can harm an ecosystem when it has no natural predators or diseases there: it spreads quickly and out-competes or eats native species, reducing biodiversity.

### Eutrophication (19.4.3a)

Untreated sewage and nitrogen-containing fertilisers washed into water cause this sequence:

1. Increased availability of nitrate and other ions.
2. Increased growth of producers (algae and plants).
3. Increased decomposition after the producers die.
4. Increased aerobic respiration by decomposers.
5. Reduction in dissolved oxygen.
6. Death of organisms requiring dissolved oxygen, such as fish.

### Greenhouse gases, pesticides and plastics (19.4.3b–d)

**Carbon dioxide** (from combustion and deforestation) and **methane** (from cattle, rice fields and landfill sites) are greenhouse gases. They trap heat in the atmosphere and contribute to **global warming**. Likely effects include melting ice and rising sea levels, more extreme weather, changes to where species can live, and extinction of species that cannot adapt.

**Insecticides** can kill useful insects, such as pollinators, and harm animals that eat poisoned insects. **Herbicides** kill non-target plants, removing food and habitats. Both can wash into rivers.

**Non-biodegradable plastics** are not broken down by decomposers, so they persist. In water and on land, animals become entangled in plastic, or swallow it and starve or choke.

## 19.5 Conservation

Reasons to conserve species (19.5.1): to **maintain biodiversity**, **reduce extinction** and **protect vulnerable environments**. Each species sits in food webs, so losing one affects others.

| Forests (19.5.2) | Fish stocks (19.5.3) |
|---|---|
| Education about the value of forests | Education about sustainable fishing |
| Protected areas where felling is banned | Closed seasons during breeding |
| Quotas on how much timber is felled | Protected areas where fishing is banned |
| Replanting felled trees | Controlled net types and mesh size so young fish escape |
| | Quotas on the catch |
| | Monitoring of stock sizes |

A **sustainable resource** (19.5.4) is one which is produced as rapidly as it is removed from the environment, so that it does not run out.

## Common errors

- Drawing food chain arrows from predator to prey. The arrow means "is eaten by".
- Saying energy is "recycled" or that it is "used up". Energy is transferred and lost as heat.
- Mixing up nitrification (ammonium to nitrate) and nitrogen fixation (nitrogen gas to compounds).
- Writing that algae use up the oxygen in eutrophication. It is the **decomposers'** aerobic respiration.
- Defining biodiversity as "the number of organisms" instead of the number of **species**.
