## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Ligand | The signalling chemical that binds to a receptor |
| Receptor | Protein with a binding site specific to a ligand |
| Target cell | A cell with the receptor for a particular ligand |
| Signal transduction | Sequence of responses inside the cell after binding |
| Second messenger | Small molecule made inside the cell that spreads the signal (cAMP) |
| Kinase | Enzyme that adds a phosphate group to a protein |
| Autoinducer | Ligand released by bacteria in quorum sensing |

## Receptors in more detail (C2.1.1 and C2.1.7)

- A binding site is complementary to its ligand in shape and charge. Binding is reversible, so the response ends when the ligand is removed.
- A receptor changes shape when the ligand binds. This is the first step of signal transduction.
- A pathway usually has relay molecules, amplification (one enzyme makes many products) and an off switch.
- The same ligand can have different effects in different cells because the pathway behind the receptor differs.

## Quorum sensing (C2.1.2)

1. Each bacterium releases a little autoinducer.
2. Low density: ligand diffuses away, concentration stays low.
3. High density: concentration passes a threshold and binds receptors.
4. Genes switch on across the population together.

Example: *Vibrio fischeri* makes light (bioluminescence) at high density, for example in a host light organ, and none in open seawater.

## Four categories of signal (C2.1.3 to C2.1.5)

| | Hormone | Neurotransmitter | Cytokine | Calcium ions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Source | Endocrine cells | Neurons | Immune and other cells | Stores and channels |
| Route | Blood, distant | Synaptic gap, about 20 nm | Short distance between cells | Inside the cell |
| Speed | Slow, lasting | Fast, brief | Intermediate | Very fast |
| Role | Coordinate body | Pass impulse | Immune regulation | Trigger exocytosis, contraction |

Chemical groups to recall:

- Hormones: **amines** (epinephrine), **proteins** (insulin), **steroids** (oestradiol, progesterone, testosterone).
- Neurotransmitters: **amino acids**, **peptides**, **amines**, and nitric oxide, a gas. The guide text names the gas as nitrous oxide; the neurotransmitter is nitric oxide.
- Why so varied: different solubility, size, stability and speed suit different jobs, and many shapes give each ligand its own receptor.

## Receptor location (C2.1.6)

| | Transmembrane | Intracellular |
|---|---|---|
| Ligand | Hydrophilic or large, stays outside | Hydrophobic, enters the cell |
| Hydrophobic amino acids | In the membrane-spanning region | Not needed at the surface |
| Hydrophilic amino acids | Outside and inside parts | Surface, in cytoplasm or nucleoplasm |
| Examples | Acetylcholine, epinephrine, insulin | Oestradiol, progesterone, testosterone |

## Pathways at a glance

| Receptor type | Steps | Time scale |
|---|---|---|
| Ligand-gated ion channel (acetylcholine, C2.1.8) | Ligand binds, channel opens, positive ions (mainly sodium) diffuse in, depolarisation, may reach threshold | Milliseconds |
| G protein-coupled (epinephrine, C2.1.9 and C2.1.10) | Binds, G protein swaps GDP for GTP, enzyme makes cAMP, kinase cascade | Seconds |
| Tyrosine kinase (insulin, C2.1.11) | Binds, tyrosine phosphorylation inside, relay reactions, vesicles with glucose transporters fuse with membrane | Minutes |
| Intracellular (steroids, C2.1.12) | Ligand crosses membrane, binds receptor, receptor binds DNA sequences, transcription | Hours |

## Acetylcholine receptor, step by step (C2.1.8)

1. Acetylcholine is released from the presynaptic neuron and diffuses across the synaptic gap.
2. It binds to the receptor on the postsynaptic membrane.
3. The receptor is also a channel, so it opens.
4. Positive ions, mainly sodium, diffuse into the cell down the gradient.
5. The inside becomes less negative: depolarisation.
6. If the threshold is reached, voltage-gated channels open and an action potential can follow.
7. The ligand is removed (for example by an enzyme that breaks it down), the channel closes and the potential returns to rest.

## Method boxes

**Describing epinephrine action in a liver cell**
1. Epinephrine (first messenger) binds a receptor in the plasma membrane.
2. The receptor activates a G protein (GTP replaces GDP).
3. The G protein activates adenylyl cyclase, which converts ATP to cAMP.
4. cAMP (second messenger) activates protein kinase A and a cascade.
5. Glycogen is broken down, glucose is released.
6. Phosphodiesterase breaks down cAMP, and the signal ends.

**Describing insulin action**
1. Insulin binds the receptor; tyrosine kinase activity is switched on.
2. Tyrosine inside the cell is phosphorylated.
3. Relay reactions follow.
4. Vesicles with glucose transporters move to the plasma membrane and fuse.
5. More glucose enters by facilitated diffusion.

**Describing a steroid hormone**
1. It diffuses through the phospholipid bilayer.
2. It binds the receptor, which is activated.
3. The receptor binds specific DNA sequences.
4. Transcription of target genes is promoted; new protein is made.

## Oestradiol and progesterone (C2.1.13)

- Oestradiol: target cells are GnRH-secreting cells of the hypothalamus. Lower levels reduce GnRH secretion; high, sustained levels increase it.
- Progesterone: target cells are endometrium cells; it maintains and prepares the lining for implantation.

## Feedback (C2.1.14)

| | Negative feedback | Positive feedback |
|---|---|---|
| Effect | Reverses the change | Amplifies the change |
| Result | Stable around a set point | Rapid movement away from start |
| Example | Glucose rises, insulin released, glucose falls, insulin falls | Cervix stretches, oxytocin released, stronger contractions, more stretch |

## Small worked reminders

**Fold change versus percentage change.** A rise from 4.0 to 31.0 units is 27.0/4.0 × 100 = 675% (7.75 times). Always say which one you are giving and match the question.

**Membrane potential.** Change = final minus initial. Going from −70 mV to −54 mV is +16 mV, so the cell is depolarised but still below a threshold of −50 mV.

**Reading a time course.** Compare the time to the first change. A response in seconds points to a membrane pathway and a second messenger; a response after hours points to changed gene expression. Quote values from the graph when you make the comparison.

## Linking ideas across the topic

- Insulin and the acetylcholine receptor are both transmembrane receptors, but one is an enzyme-linked receptor and the other is an ion channel.
- Epinephrine and insulin both use membrane receptors, yet epinephrine uses a G protein and cAMP while insulin uses tyrosine phosphorylation.
- Steroids and the nitric oxide gas both pass through membranes without a transmembrane receptor.
- Negative feedback links to homeostasis; the glucose and insulin loop uses an insulin receptor to start it.

## Command words for this topic

- **Outline** a pathway: give the main steps in order, with the name of each molecule that acts.
- **Explain** why a receptor is specific: link the shape and charge of the binding site to the ligand.
- **Compare** hormones and neurotransmitters: give a similarity and a difference for each feature, such as route, distance and speed.
- **Distinguish** two receptor types: state a difference for the same feature in both.
- **Suggest** a role for a molecule in a data question: use the data as evidence and say what the data does not show.

## Must-know distinctions

- Hormone (blood, distant, slow) versus neurotransmitter (gap, local, fast).
- First messenger (ligand) versus second messenger (cAMP).
- Transmembrane receptor (ligand stays outside) versus intracellular receptor (ligand enters).
- Kinase (adds phosphate) versus phosphodiesterase (breaks cAMP).
- Switching on in a G protein: GTP bound means active.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the term for a signalling chemical that binds a receptor.
2. Why do only some cells respond to a hormone in the blood?
3. What is the role of an autoinducer in *V. fischeri*?
4. State two chemical groups of hormones with one example each.
5. Why can oestradiol use an intracellular receptor while insulin cannot?
6. Resting potential is −65 mV. Acetylcholine depolarises the cell by 20 mV. Give the new potential.
7. cAMP rises from 5 to 135 units after epinephrine. By what factor does it rise?
8. What does phosphorylation of tyrosine in the insulin receptor lead to?
9. Which ions mainly enter through the open acetylcholine receptor?
10. Give one example each of positive and negative feedback.

### Answers

1. Ligand.
2. Only target cells have the specific receptor.
3. It accumulates with cell density and, above a threshold, switches on light-producing genes.
4. Amines (epinephrine) and steroids (oestradiol); proteins (insulin) also fit.
5. Oestradiol is lipid-soluble and crosses the membrane; insulin is a protein and cannot.
6. −65 + 20 = −45 mV.
7. 135/5 = 27 times.
8. A sequence of reactions that ends with vesicles bringing glucose transporters to the plasma membrane.
9. Sodium ions (positive ions diffusing in).
10. Negative: glucose and insulin. Positive: oxytocin in labour.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "receptor fits the hormone" without naming shape or the binding site.
- Saying hormones travel only to target organs rather than through the whole blood system.
- Naming the signalling gas wrongly; the neurotransmitter gas is nitric oxide.
- Missing that a G protein must swap GDP for GTP to activate.
- Confusing cAMP as the hormone with cAMP as the second messenger.
- Forgetting an off switch in a pathway.
- Describing steroid action without mentioning DNA sequences and transcription.
- Quoting an example of feedback without saying which change is reversed or amplified.
- Leaving out units or the working in percentage change and fold-change calculations.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is quorum sensing?**
Quorum sensing is how bacteria detect their own population density: each cell releases an autoinducer, and above a threshold concentration it binds receptors and switches on genes across the population, as in bioluminescence in Vibrio fischeri.

**What is the difference between a transmembrane and an intracellular receptor?**
Transmembrane receptors span the plasma membrane and bind hydrophilic or large ligands that stay outside, such as insulin. Intracellular receptors are in the cytoplasm or nucleus and bind hydrophobic ligands that enter the cell, such as steroid hormones.

**What is the difference between positive and negative feedback?**
Negative feedback reverses a change and keeps a variable stable, as with blood glucose and insulin. Positive feedback amplifies a change, as when cervical stretching triggers oxytocin release during labour.

**Which neurotransmitter is a gas?**
Nitric oxide is the gas that acts as a neurotransmitter. These IB DP Biology HL notes point out that the guide's wording names it as nitrous oxide, but the neurotransmitter is nitric oxide.
