This guide goes deeper into subtopic **9.4 Reactivity series** of the Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620 syllabus for 2026–2028. The broader [Metal Properties and Reactivity](/resources/metal-properties-and-reactivity/) guide gives the order, a ranking example and the iron-in-copper(II)-sulfate displacement. This page adds the detailed observations for each named reaction, a method for deducing an order from unfamiliar results, a full displacement grid with ionic equations, and how aluminium's oxide layer shows up in experiments. For quick recall use the [reactivity series revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-reactivity-series-revision-notes/), and practise with the [reactivity series practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-reactivity-series-practice/).

## What 9.4 asks you to do

| Outcome | Tier | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Core | State the order: potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, aluminium, carbon, zinc, iron, hydrogen, copper, silver, gold |
| 2 | Core | Describe the reactions, if any, of potassium, sodium and calcium with cold water; magnesium with steam; and magnesium, zinc, iron, copper, silver and gold with dilute hydrochloric acid, and explain them by position in the series |
| 3 | Core | Deduce an order of reactivity from a given set of experimental results |
| 4 | Supplement | Describe relative reactivity in terms of the tendency to form positive ions, by displacement reactions, if any, with the aqueous ions of magnesium, zinc, iron, copper and silver |
| 5 | Supplement | Explain the apparent unreactivity of aluminium in terms of its oxide layer |

## The order, and the two non-metals in it

```
most reactive    potassium   K
                 sodium      Na
                 calcium     Ca
                 magnesium   Mg
                 aluminium   Al
                 carbon      C    (non-metal reference point)
                 zinc        Zn
                 iron        Fe
                 hydrogen    H    (non-metal reference point)
                 copper      Cu
                 silver      Ag
least reactive   gold        Au
```

**Hydrogen** divides the metals that react with dilute acids (above it) from those that do not (below it). **Carbon** divides the metals that can be extracted by heating their oxides with carbon (below it) from those that need electrolysis (above it); that use belongs to 9.6.

## Reactions with cold water

All three named metals give a metal hydroxide and hydrogen, and the solution becomes alkaline. The differences in what you see are the evidence for their order.

| Metal | What you see | Equation |
|---|---|---|
| Potassium | floats, melts into a ball, moves very rapidly, fizzes violently; the hydrogen catches fire with a lilac flame | 2K + 2H₂O → 2KOH + H₂ |
| Sodium | floats, melts into a ball, moves around quickly, fizzes; gradually disappears | 2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂ |
| Calcium | sinks, steady fizzing; the solution turns cloudy because calcium hydroxide is only slightly soluble | Ca + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + H₂ |

**Explaining by position:** potassium is highest, so it reacts most vigorously; calcium is lowest of the three, so it reacts least vigorously. The cloudiness with calcium is about solubility, not reactivity.

## Magnesium with steam

Magnesium reacts only very slowly with cold water but reacts quickly with steam. In a typical demonstration, water soaked into mineral wool is heated to make steam, which passes over strongly heated magnesium ribbon.

- The magnesium **glows with a bright white light**.
- A **white solid**, magnesium oxide, is left.
- The hydrogen produced can be burned at the end of the tube.

```
magnesium + steam → magnesium oxide + hydrogen
Mg + H2O → MgO + H2
```

Note that steam gives the **oxide**, not the hydroxide.

## Metals with dilute hydrochloric acid

| Metal | Observation | Explanation by position |
|---|---|---|
| Magnesium | vigorous fizzing, metal dissolves quickly, tube warms | well above hydrogen |
| Zinc | steady fizzing | above hydrogen, below magnesium |
| Iron | slow fizzing | above hydrogen, below zinc |
| Copper, silver, gold | no reaction | below hydrogen |

```
Mg + 2HCl → MgCl2 + H2
Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl2 + H2
Fe + 2HCl → FeCl2 + H2
```

A metal below hydrogen cannot displace hydrogen from the acid, so no gas is produced.

## Deducing an order from experimental results (Core outcome 3)

For a valid comparison, only the metal should change. Keep the same mass and particle size of metal, the same volume and concentration of acid or solution, and the same starting temperature. Then any of these measurements can rank the metals:

- how fast bubbles are produced;
- the volume of gas collected in a fixed time;
- the time taken for the metal to disappear;
- the temperature rise.

### Worked example: temperature rise

*The results below are invented for practice.* Equal masses of four metal powders, W, X, Y and Z, were added to equal volumes of the same dilute acid.

| Metal | Temperature rise / °C |
|---|---|
| W | 2.0 |
| X | 14.5 |
| Y | 0.0 |
| Z | 7.0 |

**Question.** Deduce the order of reactivity, most reactive first, and state which metal could be copper. [3]

**Answer.** X, Z, W, Y [2: one mark lost for each pair out of order]. Y could be copper, because it shows no reaction with dilute acid, so it is below hydrogen [1].

*The reasoning: a more reactive metal reacts faster and releases more heat in the same time, so the temperature rise is larger.*

## Displacement and the tendency to form positive ions (Supplement)

Metals react by **losing electrons to form positive ions**. A more reactive metal has a **greater tendency to form positive ions**.

So a more reactive metal can displace a less reactive metal from a solution of its ions: the more reactive metal loses electrons and goes into solution as ions; the ions of the less reactive metal gain those electrons and are deposited as metal.

### The displacement grid

Each metal was placed in a solution of each of the other metals' ions. ✓ = reaction; ✗ = no reaction.

| Metal added ↓ / solution → | Mg²⁺ | Zn²⁺ | Fe²⁺ | Cu²⁺ | Ag⁺ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| magnesium | – | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| zinc | ✗ | – | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| iron | ✗ | ✗ | – | ✓ | ✓ |
| copper | ✗ | ✗ | ✗ | – | ✓ |
| silver | ✗ | ✗ | ✗ | ✗ | – |

Counting reactions gives the order: magnesium (4) > zinc (3) > iron (2) > copper (1) > silver (0). This is how to deduce an order from a grid of results.

**Ionic equations** show the electron transfer:

```
Zn(s) + Cu2+(aq) → Zn2+(aq) + Cu(s)
Mg(s) + Fe2+(aq) → Mg2+(aq) + Fe(s)
Cu(s) + 2Ag+(aq) → Cu2+(aq) + 2Ag(s)
```

**Observations to describe:** zinc in copper(II) sulfate gains a pink-brown coating and the blue colour fades; copper in silver nitrate becomes coated with shiny grey silver crystals and the solution turns blue as copper(II) ions form.

**Model explanation (3 marks):** "Zinc is more reactive than copper [1]. Zinc has a greater tendency to form positive ions, so zinc atoms lose electrons to form Zn²⁺ [1]. Cu²⁺ ions gain the electrons and form copper metal [1]."

## Aluminium's apparent unreactivity (Supplement)

Aluminium is above zinc and iron in the series, but in everyday use and in many experiments it seems unreactive. Its surface is covered by a thin layer of **aluminium oxide**, which is unreactive and stops water, air and acid reaching the metal underneath.

How this appears in experiments: when aluminium foil is added to dilute hydrochloric acid, there is often little reaction at first; once the acid removes the oxide layer, fizzing becomes vigorous. An experiment that measures only the first minute could wrongly place aluminium below iron. The answer to "explain" needs: oxide layer, on the surface, which prevents the reactants reaching the aluminium.

## Common mistakes

- **Putting carbon or hydrogen in the wrong place.** Carbon goes between aluminium and zinc; hydrogen goes between iron and copper.
- **Saying copper reacts slowly with acid.** It does not react at all.
- **Writing a hydroxide for magnesium and steam.** The product is magnesium oxide.
- **Ranking from one result when the test was unfair.** Different masses or particle sizes make a comparison invalid.
- **Explaining displacement only as "more reactive".** At Extended level, mention losing electrons to form positive ions.
- **Writing that aluminium is unreactive.** It is reactive; the oxide layer makes it *appear* unreactive.

## Exam technique for 9.4 command words

- **State:** the order, written from memory, including carbon and hydrogen.
- **Describe:** observations you would see, not products. "Fizzing" scores; "hydrogen is produced" is a product.
- **Explain:** link each observation to the metal's position, for example "copper is below hydrogen, so it cannot displace hydrogen from the acid".
- **Deduce:** quote the data: "X has the largest temperature rise, so it is the most reactive".
