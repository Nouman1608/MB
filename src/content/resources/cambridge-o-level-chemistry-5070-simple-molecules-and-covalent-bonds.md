Section 2.5 of the Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 syllabus (2026–2028) has three outcomes. You must state that "a covalent bond is formed when a pair of electrons is shared between two atoms leading to noble gas electronic configurations"; describe covalent bonding in eleven named molecules (H₂, Cl₂, H₂O, CH₄, NH₃, HCl, CH₃OH, C₂H₄, O₂, CO₂ and N₂), using dot-and-cross diagrams "in these and similar molecules"; and describe and explain the low melting and boiling points and poor electrical conductivity of simple molecular compounds. The syllabus adds that specific types of intermolecular force are not required.

The [Ionic, Covalent and Metallic Bonding guide](/resources/ionic-covalent-and-metallic-bonding/) draws water, methane, hydrogen chloride and carbon dioxide. This guide gives a method that works for every named molecule, then concentrates on the ones with multiple bonds or more than two kinds of atom: nitrogen, oxygen, ammonia, ethene and methanol.

## What a covalent bond is

A **covalent bond** is a **shared pair of electrons** between two atoms. Each atom contributes one electron to the pair. Sharing lets both atoms reach a **noble gas electronic configuration**: 8 outer electrons for most atoms, or 2 for hydrogen (like helium).

Covalent bonds form between **non-metal atoms**. Both atoms need to gain electrons, so neither gives electrons away; they share instead.

- One shared pair = a **single bond** (drawn H–H).
- Two shared pairs = a **double bond** (O=O).
- Three shared pairs = a **triple bond** (N≡N).

Pairs of outer electrons that are not shared are called **non-bonding pairs** (or lone pairs).

## A method for any molecule

1. Write the outer-electron count for each atom: H 1, C 4, N 5, O 6, Cl 7.
2. Work out how many bonds each atom needs to reach 8 (or 2 for H): **H 1, Cl 1, O 2, N 3, C 4**.
3. Join the atoms so that every atom has exactly that number of bonds.
4. Draw each bond as one dot and one cross where the shells overlap. Add the remaining outer electrons of each atom as non-bonding pairs.
5. Check: every atom except hydrogen has 8 outer electrons; each hydrogen has 2.

## The eleven named molecules

| Molecule | Bonds | Non-bonding pairs | Check |
|---|---|---|---|
| H₂ | H–H, 1 single | none | each H has 2 |
| Cl₂ | Cl–Cl, 1 single | 3 on each Cl | each Cl has 8 |
| HCl | H–Cl, 1 single | 3 on Cl | H 2, Cl 8 |
| H₂O | 2 single O–H | 2 on O | O 8 |
| NH₃ | 3 single N–H | 1 on N | N 8 |
| CH₄ | 4 single C–H | none | C 8 |
| O₂ | O=O, 1 double | 2 on each O | each O 8 |
| CO₂ | 2 double C=O | 2 on each O | C 8, each O 8 |
| N₂ | N≡N, 1 triple | 1 on each N | each N 8 |
| C₂H₄ | 1 double C=C, 4 single C–H | none | each C 8 |
| CH₃OH | 3 C–H, 1 C–O, 1 O–H (all single) | 2 on O | C 8, O 8 |

### Nitrogen, N₂

Each nitrogen atom (2,5) needs three more electrons, so the two atoms share **three pairs**: a triple bond. In the diagram, three dot–cross pairs sit in the overlap. Each atom keeps one non-bonding pair of its own electrons. Count for one nitrogen: 6 shared + 2 non-bonding = 8.

### Oxygen, O₂

Each oxygen atom (2,6) needs two more electrons, so the atoms share **two pairs**: a double bond. Each oxygen keeps two non-bonding pairs. Count: 4 shared + 4 non-bonding = 8.

### Ammonia, NH₃

Nitrogen shares one electron with each of three hydrogen atoms. Three bonding pairs, plus **one non-bonding pair** on nitrogen. Count for nitrogen: 6 + 2 = 8. Each hydrogen has 2. Students often forget the non-bonding pair on nitrogen; without it, nitrogen has only 6 outer electrons.

### Ethene, C₂H₄

Each carbon needs four bonds. Each carbon bonds to two hydrogens (two single bonds) and shares **two pairs** with the other carbon (a double bond): 2 + 2 = 4 bonds. Count for each carbon: 4 electrons in C–H bonds + 4 in the C=C bond = 8.

**Worked example 1.** Describe the dot-and-cross diagram of ethene, using dots for carbon electrons and crosses for hydrogen electrons.

*Annotated answer.* The two carbon atoms overlap with **four dots** between them (two from each carbon), showing the double bond. Each carbon also overlaps with two hydrogen atoms; each of these overlaps holds one dot (from carbon) and one cross (from hydrogen). There are no non-bonding pairs. Each carbon has 8 outer electrons and each hydrogen has 2. *(Both carbons' electrons are dots in this key, so the double bond appears as four dots. A key with a different symbol for each carbon is also clear.)*

### Methanol, CH₃OH

Carbon bonds to three hydrogens and to oxygen (four bonds). Oxygen bonds to carbon and to one hydrogen (two bonds) and keeps **two non-bonding pairs**.

**Worked example 2.** Show that every atom in methanol has a noble gas configuration.

*Answer.* Carbon: four single bonds = 8 electrons. Oxygen: two bonds (4 electrons) + two non-bonding pairs (4 electrons) = 8. Each hydrogen: one bond = 2 electrons.

### "Similar molecules"

The syllabus says the method must work for molecules like the named ones. Apply the same steps:

- **Hydrogen sulfide, H₂S**: sulfur is in Group VI like oxygen, so it is drawn like water (2 bonds, 2 non-bonding pairs).
- **Tetrachloromethane, CCl₄**: like methane, but each chlorine has 3 non-bonding pairs.
- **Hydrogen bromide, HBr**: like hydrogen chloride.

## Properties of simple molecular compounds

A **simple molecular** substance is made of small, separate molecules. There are two kinds of force to keep apart:

- **covalent bonds** within each molecule: strong
- **intermolecular forces** between molecules: weak

### (a) Low melting and boiling points

Melting or boiling separates the molecules from one another. It overcomes only the **weak intermolecular forces**; the covalent bonds inside the molecules are not broken. Little energy is needed, so melting and boiling points are low and many simple molecular substances are gases or liquids at room temperature. Methane boils at about −162 °C, ammonia at about −33 °C, and methanol at about 65 °C.

When water boils, the steam is still H₂O molecules. If the covalent bonds broke, you would get hydrogen and oxygen atoms instead.

### (b) Poor electrical conductivity

To conduct, a substance needs charged particles that can move. Simple molecular substances contain **neutral molecules**: there are **no ions** and **no delocalised electrons**, so they do not conduct, whether solid, liquid or gas.

## Common mistakes

- Saying covalent bonds are weak, or that boiling breaks covalent bonds.
- Forgetting non-bonding pairs on N, O or Cl.
- Drawing one shared pair in O₂ or N₂ instead of two or three.
- Putting the shared electrons outside the overlap.
- Writing that simple molecular compounds do not conduct because "they have no electrons". They have electrons, but none are free to move, and there are no ions.

## Exam technique

- **State** the definition with "pair of electrons", "shared" and "noble gas electronic configuration".
- **Draw**: plan the number of bonds per atom first; count to 8 (or 2) for every atom before moving on.
- **Explain** low boiling points: name **weak forces between molecules** and say little energy is needed.
- **Explain** poor conductivity: **no ions or delocalised electrons** free to move.
