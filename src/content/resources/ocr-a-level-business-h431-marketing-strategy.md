## What a marketing strategy contains

A **marketing strategy** is a long-term plan for achieving the marketing objectives. It is the link between the business's overall objectives and the day-to-day marketing mix. A full strategy contains:

1. **Objectives:** the measurable targets (for example, raise sales by 20% in a year).
2. **Analysis of the situation:** SWOT, market research, market size and share, competitors and external influences.
3. **Target markets:** which segments to serve and which to ignore.
4. **Positioning:** how the product is to be seen against rivals (premium, value, specialist).
5. **The marketing mix:** product, price, place and promotion (and process, people and physical evidence for services).
6. **Resources and budget:** the money, people and time required.
7. **Timetable and responsibilities:** who does what and by when.
8. **Monitoring and control:** how results will be measured and what happens if they fall short.

The strategy is not the same as the plan for one campaign. A campaign (such as a spring advertising push) is a part of the strategy.

## Worked example 1: the return on the marketing budget

Wren Outdoor sells tents and walking gear. Its sales are **£3.0 million** and its marketing budget is 5% of sales, so **£150,000**. Its strategy has the objective of raising sales by **20%** in a year, built on a new range for younger walkers and a social media campaign.

1. Target sales = £3.0m × 1.20 = **£3.6 million**, an increase of **£600,000**.
2. The new activities need an extra **£60,000** of spending.
3. The margin on sales is **30%**, so extra contribution = £600,000 × 0.30 = **£180,000**.
4. Net gain = £180,000 − £60,000 = **£120,000**.
5. Return on the extra spending = £120,000 ÷ £60,000 = **200%** (each £1 spent returns £2 of profit after covering the spending itself).

**Monitoring.** Halfway through the year, the target for the half is £1.8 million (half of £3.6 million). Actual sales are **£1.62 million**.

- Variance = £1.62m − £1.8m = **−£0.18 million** (adverse).
- Achievement = 1.62 ÷ 1.8 = **90%** of target.

**Interpretation.** Wren is 10% behind. The managers should find the cause: slow take-up of the new range, weak social media response, poor weather or rival offers. They might shift budget to the best-performing channel, adjust the timetable, or accept a lower full-year result. If sales continue at the same rate, the year's total would be £3.24 million (1.62 × 2), £360,000 below target, and the return would be:

- Extra sales = £240,000 (3.24m − 3.0m); extra contribution = £72,000.
- Net after £60,000 spending = **£12,000**, a return of **20%**.

So a 10% shortfall in sales cuts the return from 200% to 20%. This shows how sensitive the benefit is to the accuracy of the sales target, and why monitoring is an essential part of a strategy.

## Worked example 2: comparing strategic options

Wren considers three strategies and scores them against criteria. The weights total 100, and each option is scored from 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent).

- **A:** premium positioning, sold online and through specialist shops.
- **B:** national launch through large supermarkets.
- **C:** export to a new overseas market.

| Criterion | Weight | A | B | C |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fit with objectives | 30 | 4 | 5 | 4 |
| Cost | 20 | 4 | 2 | 1 |
| Risk | 20 | 4 | 3 | 2 |
| Speed of results | 15 | 4 | 3 | 2 |
| Fit with brand | 15 | 5 | 3 | 4 |

Weighted totals (sum of weight × score, divided by 100):

- **A:** (30×4 + 20×4 + 20×4 + 15×4 + 15×5) ÷ 100 = (120 + 80 + 80 + 60 + 75) ÷ 100 = **4.15**.
- **B:** (150 + 40 + 60 + 45 + 45) ÷ 100 = **3.40**.
- **C:** (120 + 20 + 40 + 30 + 60) ÷ 100 = **2.70**.

**Sensitivity check.** What if objectives matter much more (weights 50, 10, 10, 15, 15)? A is still 4.15, B rises to 3.90 and C to 3.20. A remains first, so the result is not sensitive to this change in the weights.

**Recommendation and justification.** Wren should choose **strategy A**. It scores best on cost, risk and brand fit. Supermarkets give the most sales potential (B scores 5 on fit with objectives) but are expensive and carry risk, as Wren has little experience of large retailers' demands. The risk of A is that it may grow too slowly, so Wren should set a six-month review and prepare to add a limited supermarket trial if online growth stalls. The scores are judgements, so a good answer says so and explains the reasoning behind the highest and lowest scores.

## Impact and importance

**For the business.** A clear strategy:

- aligns all four elements of the mix behind one aim;
- directs limited resources to the best opportunities and avoids waste;
- gives managers standards against which to measure progress;
- helps the business to respond to competition and change in a planned way;
- supports other functions, since finance, operations and HR can plan around it.

The strategy is most important when the business is entering a new market, launching a product, facing new competition or has limited resources.

**For stakeholders.**

| Stakeholder | Possible impact |
|---|---|
| Owners and shareholders | Higher sales and profit if it succeeds; risk of wasted money if it fails |
| Employees | New roles, training and job security with growth; pressure to meet targets; possible redundancy if a range is dropped |
| Customers | Better products and information; possible price changes or loss of a product |
| Suppliers | Larger or smaller orders; need to meet new standards |
| Lenders | Confidence in repayment if the plan is credible |
| Local community | Jobs and activity, or pressure from traffic and disruption |

**Limits.** A strategy relies on forecasts that may be wrong, takes time and money to produce, and may become out of date in a fast-changing market. If followed too rigidly, it can stop the business responding to a sudden opportunity. A strategy that is not communicated to staff is unlikely to be carried out.

## Recommending a strategy

A strong recommendation follows this order:

1. **Diagnose:** use the data (SWOT, market share, growth, resources) to describe the situation.
2. **State the objective** with a number and a date.
3. **Choose the target market and positioning.**
4. **Outline the mix** and show that its elements fit together.
5. **Cost it** and give the expected return.
6. **Justify** against at least one alternative, with a reason for rejecting it.
7. **State the main risk** and how progress will be monitored.

## Common mistakes

- Describing a collection of marketing activities and calling it a strategy, without objectives or a target market.
- Recommending a strategy without a figure or a timetable.
- Failing to compare with an alternative.
- Ignoring resources: a strategy the business cannot afford is not useful.
- Treating stakeholders as one group. Impacts differ and may conflict.

## Exam technique

For "evaluate the impact and importance", cover business and stakeholders, give a benefit and a limit and conclude on how important it is in this case. For "recommend and justify", make a clear choice, support it with evidence from the case, explain why alternatives were rejected and state a risk.
