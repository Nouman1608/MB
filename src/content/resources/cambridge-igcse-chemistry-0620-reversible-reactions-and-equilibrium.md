This guide covers subtopic **6.3 Reversible reactions and equilibrium** in Topic 6, Chemical reactions, of Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620, 2026–2028 series. The combined [rates of reaction and reversible reactions study guide](/resources/rates-of-reaction-and-reversible-reactions/) states the definitions, equations and industrial conditions. This page concentrates on using them: a step-by-step method for predicting how an equilibrium responds to a change in a reaction you have never seen, and a full account of why the Haber and Contact process conditions are chosen. Afterwards, consolidate with the [reversible reactions revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-reversible-reactions-and-equilibrium-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [reversible reactions practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-reversible-reactions-and-equilibrium-practice/).

## What the syllabus asks for

**Core** (all candidates):

- State that some chemical reactions are reversible, shown by the symbol ⇌.
- Describe how changing the conditions can change the direction of a reversible reaction, for the effect of heat on hydrated compounds and the addition of water to anhydrous compounds, **limited to copper(II) sulfate and cobalt(II) chloride**.

**Supplement** (Extended candidates only):

- State the two conditions for equilibrium in a closed system: forward and reverse rates are equal, and concentrations of reactants and products are no longer changing.
- Predict and explain how the position of equilibrium is affected by changing temperature, pressure or concentration, or by using a catalyst, **using information provided**.
- State the Haber process equation, the sources of hydrogen (methane) and nitrogen (air), and the typical conditions: 450 °C, 20 000 kPa / 200 atm, iron catalyst.
- State the Contact process equation for converting sulfur dioxide to sulfur trioxide, the sources of sulfur dioxide (burning sulfur or roasting sulfide ores) and oxygen (air), and the typical conditions: 450 °C, 200 kPa / 2 atm, vanadium(V) oxide catalyst.
- Explain, in terms of rate and position of equilibrium, why those conditions are used, including safety and economics.

## The two Core examples

Both pairs work the same way: heating drives water out of the crystals; adding water puts it back.

```
CuSO4·5H2O(s)  ⇌  CuSO4(s)  +  5H2O(l)
   blue             white

CoCl2·6H2O(s)  ⇌  CoCl2(s)  +  6H2O(l)
   pink             blue
```

| Compound | Hydrated colour | Anhydrous colour | Change on heating | Change on adding water |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Copper(II) sulfate | Blue | White | Blue → white | White → blue |
| Cobalt(II) chloride | Pink | Blue | Pink → blue | Blue → pink |

Two details earn marks. First, the *direction* of the reaction is controlled by the condition you choose: heat pushes it forwards, water pushes it backwards. Second, adding water to the anhydrous solid releases heat, so the mixture gets warm. The forward reaction (dehydration) needs heat; the reverse gives it out.

These colour changes are also the chemical tests for the presence of water in Topic 10. A common slip is to swap the cobalt colours: anhydrous cobalt(II) chloride (often on test paper) is **blue** and turns **pink** with water.

## Equilibrium: what is actually happening (Supplement)

A reversible reaction in a **closed system** (nothing can enter or leave) eventually reaches equilibrium. The syllabus wants both conditions stated:

1. The rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction.
2. The concentrations of reactants and products are no longer changing.

Equilibrium is **dynamic**: both reactions continue, so particles are still being converted in both directions, but the overall amounts stay constant. "The reaction has stopped" is wrong and loses the mark.

The **position of equilibrium** describes the balance of reactants and products. "Shifts to the right" means more products; "shifts to the left" means more reactants.

## A method for "predict and explain" questions

The phrase "using information provided" means the question gives you an equation, and often whether the forward reaction is exothermic or endothermic. Work through four steps.

1. **Identify the change** (temperature up or down, pressure up or down, concentration of which substance, catalyst).
2. **Find the relevant information.** For temperature: is the forward reaction exothermic or endothermic? For pressure: count the moles of **gas** on each side.
3. **Apply the rule.** The equilibrium shifts in the direction that opposes the change:
   - Raising temperature favours the **endothermic** direction; lowering it favours the **exothermic** direction.
   - Raising pressure favours the side with **fewer moles of gas**; lowering it favours the side with more.
   - Adding a substance shifts the position away from that substance; removing one shifts it towards that substance.
   - A catalyst has **no effect** on the position; it increases forward and reverse rates equally, so equilibrium is reached faster.
4. **State the consequence** for the yield, colour or amount asked about.

### Worked example 1: colour change

Brown nitrogen dioxide forms colourless dinitrogen tetroxide. The forward reaction is exothermic.

```
2NO2(g)  ⇌  N2O4(g)
 brown       colourless
```

*Predict and explain the effect of raising the temperature on the colour.*
Raising the temperature favours the endothermic direction, which is the reverse reaction. The equilibrium shifts to the left, so there is more NO₂ and the mixture becomes **darker brown**.

*Predict and explain the effect of raising the pressure on the amount of N₂O₄.*
Left side: 2 moles of gas; right side: 1 mole. Higher pressure favours the side with fewer moles of gas, so the equilibrium shifts to the right and the amount of N₂O₄ increases.

### Worked example 2: an endothermic forward reaction

Methane reacts with steam. The forward reaction is endothermic.

```
CH4(g) + H2O(g)  ⇌  CO(g) + 3H2(g)
```

- Higher temperature: favours the endothermic forward reaction, so the yield of hydrogen **increases**.
- Higher pressure: 2 moles of gas on the left, 4 on the right; the equilibrium shifts left, so the yield of hydrogen **decreases**.
- Removing hydrogen as it forms: the equilibrium shifts right to replace it, so more methane is converted.

### When pressure has no effect

If both sides have the same number of moles of gas, as in H₂(g) + I₂(g) ⇌ 2HI(g), changing the pressure does not change the position of equilibrium. Say so, and give the reason (equal moles of gas on each side).

## Choosing industrial conditions (Supplement)

Both processes face the same tension: the forward reaction is exothermic and produces fewer moles of gas.

```
Haber:    N2(g) + 3H2(g)  ⇌  2NH3(g)       forward exothermic; 4 mol gas → 2 mol
Contact:  2SO2(g) + O2(g) ⇌  2SO3(g)       forward exothermic; 3 mol gas → 2 mol
```

| Condition | Effect on equilibrium position | Effect on rate | Safety and economics | Choice |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Temperature | Lower temperature gives a higher yield | Lower temperature gives a slower rate | Too slow is uneconomic | 450 °C in both: a compromise between yield and rate |
| Pressure (Haber) | Higher pressure gives a higher yield | Higher pressure gives a faster rate | Very high pressure is dangerous and needs expensive, strong equipment | 20 000 kPa / 200 atm: a compromise |
| Pressure (Contact) | Higher pressure would give a higher yield | Faster rate | The position of equilibrium already lies far to the right, so extra pressure adds cost for little gain | 200 kPa / 2 atm |
| Catalyst | No effect on position | Faster rate; equilibrium reached sooner | Allows an acceptable rate at a moderate temperature | Iron (Haber); vanadium(V) oxide (Contact) |

### Annotated model answer

*Explain why the Haber process is carried out at 450 °C rather than at a lower temperature.* **[3]**

> The forward reaction is exothermic, so a lower temperature would shift the equilibrium to the right and increase the yield of ammonia **[1]**. However, at a lower temperature the rate of reaction would be too slow **[1]**. 450 °C is a compromise that gives a reasonable yield at an acceptable rate **[1]**.

Notice the structure: position argument, rate argument, compromise. A response that only mentions yield, or only rate, caps at one mark.

## Common mistakes

- **Saying the reaction stops at equilibrium.** Both reactions continue at equal rates.
- **Saying concentrations are equal at equilibrium.** They are *constant*, not necessarily equal.
- **Counting all moles, not moles of gas,** when judging pressure.
- **Claiming a catalyst increases the yield.** It only shortens the time to reach equilibrium.
- **Writing "450 °C gives the highest yield".** It does not; a lower temperature would. It is a compromise.
- **Giving the wrong catalyst or source.** Iron for Haber; vanadium(V) oxide for Contact; hydrogen from methane; nitrogen and oxygen from air; sulfur dioxide from burning sulfur or roasting sulfide ores.
- **Swapping the cobalt(II) chloride colours.**

## Exam technique for the command words

- **State**: the equation, a condition or a source, with no explanation. Include state symbols when the question asks for them.
- **Describe** (Core, hydrated compounds): give the condition, the direction and the colour change.
- **Predict**: give the direction of shift and the effect on the named substance.
- **Explain**: add the reason from the information given (exothermic/endothermic, moles of gas).
- For condition questions, always address **both** rate and position, then finish with safety or cost when the question mentions them.
