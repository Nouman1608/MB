These notes condense section 4.4, Monetary policy, of the Cambridge IGCSE Economics 0455 syllabus for examination in 2026: the definitions of money supply and monetary policy, the three measures (interest rates, money supply and foreign exchange rates) and their effects on the macroeconomic aims. All figures are invented. For explanations and worked examples read the [monetary policy study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-monetary-policy-cambridge-igcse-economics/), then practise with the [monetary policy practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-monetary-policy-cambridge-igcse-economics-practice/). The combined [fiscal policy measures and monetary policy revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-fiscal-policy-measures-and-monetary-policy-revision-notes/) compare monetary and fiscal policy.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Money supply | Total money in the economy: notes, coins and spendable bank deposits |
| Monetary policy | Changes in interest rates, money supply and the exchange rate to achieve macroeconomic aims |
| Interest rate | The cost of borrowing and the reward for saving |
| Exchange rate | The price of one currency in terms of another |
| Appreciation | A rise in the value of a currency |
| Depreciation | A fall in the value of a currency |
| Expansionary monetary policy | Lower interest rates, more money supply, lower exchange rate |
| Contractionary monetary policy | Higher interest rates, less money supply, higher exchange rate |

**Who:** usually the central bank, sometimes independent of the government.

## Interest rates: five routes (for a rise)

1. New borrowing dearer → less spending on credit.
2. Variable-rate debts dearer → less income left to spend.
3. Saving more rewarding → more saved, less spent.
4. Investment projects must beat a higher interest cost → less investment.
5. Currency attracts foreign savings → appreciation → exports dearer, imports cheaper.

A cut works in reverse.

Worked reminder: a project costing 50,000 earns 3,000 a year (6%).

```
Interest at 5% = 2,500 → project earns 500 more than interest → goes ahead
Interest at 8% = 4,000 → project earns 1,000 less than interest → cancelled
```

## Money supply

- **Increase:** credit easier, interest rates tend to fall, spending rises; risk of inflation if money grows faster than output.
- **Decrease:** credit harder, interest rates tend to rise, spending falls.

## Exchange rate

```
Export price abroad = home price x exchange rate (foreign units per home unit)
Import price at home = foreign price / exchange rate
```

Worked reminder: 1 home unit = 4 foreign units falls to 3.2 (a 20% depreciation).

```
Home good at 50: abroad 200 → 160 (cheaper)
Foreign good at 240: at home 60 → 75 (dearer)
```

| Depreciation (fall) | Appreciation (rise) |
|---|---|
| Exports cheaper abroad → more exports | Exports dearer abroad → fewer exports |
| Imports dearer at home → fewer imports | Imports cheaper at home → more imports |
| Can raise inflation (dearer imported materials) | Helps reduce inflation |

## Effects on the macroeconomic aims

| Aim | Expansionary policy | Contractionary policy |
|---|---|---|
| Economic growth | More spending and investment → higher output | Lower spending → slower growth |
| Low unemployment | Firms hire to meet demand | Fewer orders → fewer jobs |
| Stable prices | Risk of higher inflation | Reduces inflation |
| Balance of payments | Lower exchange rate can help exports; higher incomes raise imports | Higher exchange rate can worsen the current account; lower incomes reduce imports |
| Redistribution | Weak: helps borrowers, hurts savers | Weak: helps savers, hurts borrowers |

## Evaluation points

- Time lags: months before spending responds.
- Low confidence: firms may not borrow even when loans are cheap.
- Banks may not pass on rate changes or may refuse risky borrowers.
- Fixed-rate loans delay the effect.
- Conflicts: lower rates for jobs may raise inflation; a lower exchange rate raises import prices.

## Exam traps

- **Monetary ≠ fiscal.** Taxes and government spending are fiscal policy.
- **Direction of exchange-rate effects.** Depreciation makes exports cheaper and imports dearer.
- **Overall effect of a rate rise** on spending is usually negative, even though savers gain income.
- **Link to an aim.** Every chain should end with growth, unemployment, inflation or the balance of payments.
- **Percentage points.** A rise from 3% to 5% is 2 percentage points.
- **No AD/AS diagrams** are needed for this syllabus.

## Self-test

1. Define monetary policy.
2. Name the three monetary policy measures in the syllabus.
3. Give two ways a rise in interest rates reduces household spending.
4. Why might a rise in interest rates reduce firms' investment?
5. A firm borrows 400,000. Calculate the change in yearly interest if the rate rises from 3% to 5%.
6. What happens to the exchange rate when interest rates rise? Why?
7. The currency falls from 1 = 5 foreign units to 1 = 4. A home good costs 30. Calculate its price abroad before and after.
8. Which monetary measures would a central bank use to reduce inflation?
9. How can a depreciation help reduce unemployment?
10. Why might a depreciation increase inflation?
11. Give two reasons why a cut in interest rates may not increase spending.
12. Why is monetary policy a weak tool for redistribution?

### Answers

1. Use of interest rates, the money supply and the exchange rate to influence the economy and achieve macroeconomic aims.
2. Changes in interest rates, money supply and foreign exchange rates.
3. Borrowing for goods is dearer; repayments on variable-rate loans rise; saving is more rewarding.
4. Fewer projects earn more than the higher cost of borrowing.
5. 400,000 x 0.02 = 8,000 more a year (12,000 to 20,000).
6. It tends to rise: foreign savers buy the currency to earn the higher interest.
7. Before 30 x 5 = 150; after 30 x 4 = 120 foreign units.
8. Raise interest rates; reduce the money supply; allow a higher exchange rate.
9. Exports become cheaper abroad and imports dearer, so demand for home-produced goods rises and firms hire more workers.
10. Imported goods and raw materials cost more, raising firms' costs and prices.
11. Any two: low confidence; banks do not pass on the cut; fixed-rate loans; households already in heavy debt; time lags.
12. It changes income between borrowers and savers rather than targeting the poor.
