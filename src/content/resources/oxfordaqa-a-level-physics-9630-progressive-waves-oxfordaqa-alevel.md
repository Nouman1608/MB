This guide goes deeper into one subtopic of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630) specification: **3.5.3 Progressive waves**. The specification lists "oscillation of the particles of the medium; amplitude, frequency, wavelength, speed, phase, phase difference", the equations c = fλ and f = 1/T, and "phase difference measured as angles (radians or degrees) or as fractions of a cycle". It is shared AS and A-level content.

The broader [study guide to progressive, longitudinal and transverse waves](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-progressive-waves-longitudinal-and-transverse-waves/) defines each quantity. This page concentrates on the skills that cost marks: working out how a particle is moving from a snapshot, finding phase difference from traces, and handling waves that change speed. When you have finished, use the [progressive waves revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-progressive-waves-oxfordaqa-alevel-revision-notes/) and the [progressive waves practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-progressive-waves-oxfordaqa-alevel-practice/).

## What the subtopic expects

| Specification wording | What it means in an answer |
|---|---|
| Oscillation of the particles of the medium | Each particle oscillates about a fixed equilibrium position; the disturbance, and the energy, move on |
| Amplitude, frequency, wavelength, speed | Read these from graphs and use them in calculations with correct units |
| Phase, phase difference | Say how far apart in a cycle two oscillations are, and which one leads |
| c = fλ and f = 1/T | Link the wave pattern (λ, c) to the motion of one particle (f, T) |
| Angles or fractions of a cycle | Convert freely between cycles, degrees and radians |

## The two motions in every progressive wave

A progressive wave involves two different motions, and most errors come from mixing them up.

- **The wave pattern** moves through the medium at the wave speed c. In one period T it moves forward exactly one wavelength.
- **Each particle** oscillates about its own equilibrium position with the frequency f of the source. It does not travel with the wave.

Because the pattern advances one wavelength per period, c = λ/T, and with f = 1/T this gives c = fλ. The particle's own speed changes throughout its cycle; it is not the wave speed, and a question asking for "the speed of the wave" always wants c.

Each particle repeats the motion of the particle next to it on the source side, a short time later. That single idea explains phase lag and lets you find particle directions from a snapshot.

## Finding which way a particle is moving

A displacement-distance graph is a snapshot. It does not show motion directly, so you have to reason it out. Two methods give the same answer.

**Method 1: copy the neighbour.** Look at the particle just behind the one you are asked about, on the side the wave is coming from. Your particle is about to have that displacement, so it moves towards it.

**Method 2: shift the pattern.** Sketch the same curve moved a short distance in the direction of travel. Read the new displacement at your particle's position and compare it with the old one.

Particles at a crest or a trough are momentarily at rest. Particles at zero displacement are moving fastest.

### Worked example 1: reading a snapshot

A transverse wave travels in the +x direction at 12 m s⁻¹. At one instant its displacement-distance graph has a crest at x = 0.20 m, zero displacement at x = 0.40 m, a trough at x = 0.60 m and zero displacement again at x = 0.80 m. The amplitude is 15 mm.

**(a) Frequency and period.** The crest-to-crest distance is twice the crest-to-trough distance, so λ = 2 × (0.60 − 0.20) = 0.80 m.

```
f = c / λ = 12 / 0.80 = 15 Hz
T = 1 / f = 1 / 15 = 0.067 s
```

**(b) Direction of motion at x = 0.40 m.** The wave comes from smaller x. The particle just to the left is displaced upwards (it is between the crest and x = 0.40 m), so the particle at 0.40 m is moving **up**. At x = 0.80 m the particle just to the left is below the axis, so that particle is moving **down**.

**(c) Phase difference between the particles at x = 0.20 m and x = 0.75 m.**

```
separation = 0.55 m
fraction of a cycle = 0.55 / 0.80 = 0.6875 cycle
in degrees: 0.6875 × 360 = 248°
in radians: 0.6875 × 2π = 4.32 rad
```

The particle at 0.75 m is further along the direction of travel, so it **lags**. Its lag of 248° is the same as a lead of 112°, which is why you must state which one is behind when you quote an angle bigger than 180°.

**(d) The crest particle after 0.050 s.** 0.050 s is 0.050 × 15 = 0.75 of a cycle. From the crest, a quarter cycle takes it to equilibrium, half a cycle to the trough, and three quarters back to equilibrium moving up. Its displacement is zero and it is moving upwards.

## Phase difference in time and in space

Phase difference compares two oscillations at the same frequency. You can find it in two ways.

| Information given | Fraction of a cycle | Radians |
|---|---|---|
| Separation Δx along the wave | Δx / λ | 2πΔx / λ |
| Time shift Δt between two traces | Δt / T | 2πΔt / T |

Whole cycles do not count, because a shift of exactly one cycle brings an oscillation back into step. A separation of 1.25λ gives the same phase difference as 0.25λ, namely π/2 rad. In phase means a phase difference of 0, 2π, 4π and so on; antiphase means π, 3π and so on.

Convert with 1 cycle = 360° = 2π rad. Useful landmarks: a quarter cycle is 90° or π/2 rad, a half cycle is 180° or π rad, and a third of a cycle is 120° or 2π/3 rad.

### Worked example 2: phase difference from an oscilloscope

A loudspeaker emits sound of frequency 2.50 kHz. Two microphones at different distances from it are connected to the two inputs of an oscilloscope. The two traces have the same shape, but one is shifted 0.10 ms to the right of the other. The speed of sound is 340 m s⁻¹.

```
T = 1 / 2500 = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ s = 0.40 ms
fraction of a cycle = 0.10 / 0.40 = 0.25 cycle
phase difference = 90° = π/2 rad
λ = c / f = 340 / 2500 = 0.136 m
smallest separation = 0.25 × 0.136 = 0.034 m
```

The microphones could be 0.034 m apart, or 0.034 m plus any whole number of wavelengths. The trace alone cannot tell you which. Moving one microphone slowly and watching the traces drift through a whole cycle removes the ambiguity: each time they return into step, the microphone has moved one more wavelength.

## When a wave changes speed

The frequency is set by the source. When a wave crosses into a region where it travels more slowly, the same number of waves per second still arrive, so the wavelength must shorten in proportion to the speed.

### Worked example 3: ripples entering shallow water

A dipper vibrates at 8.0 Hz in a ripple tank. Waves travel at 0.24 m s⁻¹ in the deep region and 0.16 m s⁻¹ over a shallow region.

```
deep:    λ = 0.24 / 8.0 = 0.030 m = 3.0 cm
shallow: λ = 0.16 / 8.0 = 0.020 m = 2.0 cm
period in both regions: T = 1 / 8.0 = 0.125 s
```

The ratio of the wavelengths, 2.0/3.0, equals the ratio of the speeds. If a question asks what stays the same, the answer is frequency and period, never wavelength.

## Graph skills checklist

- On a displacement-distance graph, measure λ across several cycles and divide, which reduces the percentage uncertainty.
- On a displacement-time graph, measure T across several cycles in the same way.
- Amplitude is measured from the equilibrium line to a crest, never crest to trough.
- Check the axis label before reading: a horizontal axis in metres gives λ; in seconds it gives T.

## Common mistakes

- Taking the crest-to-trough distance as the wavelength. It is half a wavelength.
- Saying particles move along with the wave. They oscillate about fixed positions.
- Quoting a phase difference as a bare number such as "0.25". State the unit: cycles, degrees or radians.
- Giving a phase difference bigger than 2π rad. Remove whole cycles first.
- Forgetting to convert ms to s or kHz to Hz before using c = fλ.
- Saying wavelength stays constant when a wave enters a new medium. Frequency stays constant.
- Writing that the particle at a crest is moving fastest. It is momentarily at rest.

## Exam technique

- **State** or **define**: a precise sentence, no working. For wavelength, "the distance between two adjacent points oscillating in phase" earns the mark; "the length of a wave" does not.
- **Calculate**: write the equation, substitute with units, and give the answer to the same number of significant figures as the data.
- **Explain**: link cause to effect. For "why does the wavelength decrease?", say that the frequency is fixed by the source and the speed falls, so λ = c/f falls.
- **Describe** the motion of a particle: give the direction and whether it is speeding up, slowing down or momentarily at rest.
- **Show that**: every step must be visible, and the final value should be given to one more significant figure than the value you were asked to show.
