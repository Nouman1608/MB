This guide goes deeper into one sub-topic of the Cambridge IGCSE Biology 0610 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028: **19.3 Nutrient cycles**, the third part of topic 19, Organisms and their environment. It has three learning outcomes. Outcome 1, the carbon cycle, is Core. Outcomes 2 and 3, the nitrogen cycle and the roles of microorganisms in it, are Supplement content, so they are **Extended only** (Papers 2 and 4).

The [organisms and their environment study guide](/resources/igcse-biology-0610-organisms-and-their-environment/) lists each process. This page shows how to trace an atom round each cycle, read a cycle diagram labelled with letters, and explain what speeds up or slows down each process. Afterwards, use the [nutrient cycles revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-nutrient-cycles-cambridge-igcse-biology-revision-notes/) and the [nutrient cycles practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-nutrient-cycles-cambridge-igcse-biology-practice/).

## What sub-topic 19.3 asks you to do

| Outcome | Syllabus wording (summarised) | Tier |
|---|---|---|
| 19.3.1 | Describe the carbon cycle, limited to photosynthesis, respiration, feeding, decomposition, formation of fossil fuels and combustion | Core |
| 19.3.2 | Describe the nitrogen cycle: decomposition of protein to ammonium ions, nitrification, nitrogen fixation by lightning and bacteria, absorption of nitrate ions, production of amino acids and proteins, feeding and digestion of proteins, deamination, denitrification | Extended only |
| 19.3.3 | State the roles of microorganisms, limited to decomposition, nitrification, nitrogen fixation and denitrification (names of individual bacteria are not required) | Extended only |

All three outcomes use **describe** or **state**. You must name each process and say what happens to the element in it.

## Why nutrients must cycle

Organisms need carbon to build carbohydrates, fats and proteins, and nitrogen to make amino acids and proteins. The Earth has a fixed amount of each element. Unlike energy, which flows through an ecosystem and leaves as heat (see the [energy flow study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-energy-flow-cambridge-igcse-biology/)), these elements are used again and again. Decomposers make that possible by releasing the elements locked in dead material.

## 19.3.1 The carbon cycle (Core)

### The six processes and who carries them out

| Process | Organisms or source | Carbon moves from → to |
|---|---|---|
| Photosynthesis | producers (plants, algae) | carbon dioxide in air or water → carbohydrates in producers |
| Feeding | consumers | carbon compounds in food → carbon compounds in the consumer's body |
| Respiration | all organisms: producers, consumers and decomposers | carbon compounds (glucose) → carbon dioxide in air |
| Decomposition | decomposers (bacteria and fungi) | carbon compounds in dead organisms and waste → carbon dioxide, released by the decomposers' respiration |
| Formation of fossil fuels | dead organisms that do not fully decompose | carbon compounds → coal, oil and natural gas over millions of years |
| Combustion | burning fossil fuels and wood | carbon compounds → carbon dioxide in air |

Only **photosynthesis** removes carbon dioxide from the air. Respiration, decomposition and combustion all return it.

### Worked example 1: tracing a carbon atom

Trace a carbon atom from the air into a sheep and back again.

1. Grass absorbs carbon dioxide and uses it in **photosynthesis** to make glucose; the glucose is built into starch and cellulose.
2. The sheep eats the grass (**feeding**); the carbon becomes part of carbohydrates, fats or proteins in the sheep.
3. Either the sheep **respires** the glucose and breathes out carbon dioxide, or the carbon stays in its body until it dies.
4. When the sheep dies, **decomposers** feed on the body and respire, releasing the carbon as carbon dioxide.

### When decomposition is slow: fossil fuels

Decomposers need oxygen, moisture and a suitable temperature. Where dead material builds up in waterlogged, airless or acidic conditions, such as swamps and the beds of seas, decomposition is slow or incomplete. Over millions of years, buried remains are compressed:

- plant remains in swamps → **coal**;
- remains of tiny marine organisms → **oil** and **natural gas**.

The carbon in these fuels has been out of the cycle for millions of years. **Combustion** returns it to the air as carbon dioxide very quickly, much faster than it was locked away.

### Worked example 2: reading a lettered diagram

A carbon-cycle diagram shows boxes for *carbon dioxide in air*, *plants*, *animals*, *dead organisms* and *fossil fuels*, joined by arrows A to F.

- An arrow from air to plants must be **photosynthesis**.
- An arrow from plants to animals is **feeding**.
- Arrows from plants, animals and dead organisms to air are **respiration** (the last is respiration of decomposers, which is **decomposition**).
- An arrow from dead organisms to fossil fuels is **formation of fossil fuels**.
- An arrow from fossil fuels to air is **combustion**.

Use the direction of the arrow and the two boxes it joins. Two arrows can describe the same process, for example respiration by plants and by animals.

### Balancing the cycle

For a long time, photosynthesis removed about as much carbon dioxide as respiration and decomposition returned. Combustion of fossil fuels and the burning of forests now add extra carbon dioxide, while cutting down forests reduces photosynthesis. Both raise the carbon dioxide concentration of the air, which links to the enhanced greenhouse effect in the [pollution study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-pollution-cambridge-igcse-biology/).

## 19.3.2 The nitrogen cycle (Extended only)

Nitrogen gas makes up most of the air, but plants and animals cannot use it directly. Plants absorb nitrogen as **nitrate ions** from the soil; animals obtain it as protein in food.

### Worked example 3: tracing a nitrogen atom

1. **Nitrogen fixation:** nitrogen-fixing bacteria in the root nodules of a bean plant convert nitrogen gas from the air into nitrogen compounds the plant can use. Lightning also fixes nitrogen gas, forming compounds that rain washes into the soil.
2. **Production of amino acids and proteins:** the bean plant uses the nitrogen to make amino acids, then proteins.
3. **Feeding and digestion of proteins:** a rabbit eats the bean leaves and digests the protein to amino acids, which it absorbs and uses to build its own proteins.
4. **Deamination:** excess amino acids are deaminated in the rabbit's liver; the nitrogen-containing part is made into urea and excreted in urine.
5. **Decomposition:** decomposers break down protein in dead organisms, and nitrogen compounds in urine and faeces, to **ammonium ions**.
6. **Nitrification:** nitrifying bacteria convert ammonium ions into **nitrate ions**.
7. **Absorption of nitrate ions:** a grass plant absorbs the nitrate through its roots (by active transport) and the cycle continues.
8. **Denitrification:** alternatively, denitrifying bacteria convert nitrate ions into **nitrogen gas**, which returns to the air.

### Which processes add nitrate, and which remove it?

| Increases nitrate in soil | Decreases nitrate in soil |
|---|---|
| decomposition followed by nitrification | absorption by plant roots |
| nitrogen fixation by lightning and bacteria | denitrification |

**Conditions matter.** Nitrifying bacteria need oxygen, so they work best in well-aerated soil. Denitrification happens mainly in waterlogged soil, where there is little oxygen. This is why draining and ploughing soil helps keep nitrate available to crops. Farmers also grow peas, beans or clover, whose root nodules contain nitrogen-fixing bacteria, to raise soil nitrogen.

## 19.3.3 Roles of microorganisms (Extended only)

You do not need the names of individual bacteria. Learn the four roles:

| Role | Microorganism | Conversion |
|---|---|---|
| Decomposition | decomposers (bacteria and fungi) | protein in dead material and waste → ammonium ions |
| Nitrification | nitrifying bacteria | ammonium ions → nitrate ions |
| Nitrogen fixation | nitrogen-fixing bacteria (in soil and in root nodules) | nitrogen gas → nitrogen compounds |
| Denitrification | denitrifying bacteria | nitrate ions → nitrogen gas |

Lightning also fixes nitrogen, but it is not a microorganism. If a question asks for the roles of microorganisms, do not include it.

## Common mistakes

- Saying plants "take in carbon from the soil". Carbon enters plants as carbon dioxide in photosynthesis.
- Forgetting that plants respire too, day and night.
- Calling decomposition a separate source of carbon dioxide without saying it is the decomposers' respiration that releases it.
- Writing "fossil fuels are made from dead animals" with no mention of incomplete decomposition and millions of years.
- (Extended) Confusing nitrification (ammonium → nitrate), nitrogen fixation (nitrogen gas → nitrogen compounds) and denitrification (nitrate → nitrogen gas).
- (Extended) Saying plants absorb nitrogen gas from the air or ammonium from the soil as their main source; the syllabus says plants absorb nitrate ions.
- (Extended) Including lightning in a list of microorganisms' roles.

## Exam technique for 19.3 command words

- **Describe** the carbon cycle: name each process and say which way carbon moves and in what form (carbon dioxide or carbon compounds).
- **Describe** the nitrogen cycle: give the conversion for each named stage (from what, to what).
- **State** the roles of microorganisms: one sentence per role, naming the type of bacteria and the conversion.
- **Suggest**: link a change in conditions (waterlogging, ploughing, burning) to the process it speeds up or slows down.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is the nitrogen cycle Core or Extended in Cambridge IGCSE Biology 0610?**
The nitrogen cycle and the roles of microorganisms in it are Supplement content, so they are Extended only. The carbon cycle is Core.

**Which process removes carbon dioxide from the air?**
Photosynthesis is the only process in the syllabus carbon cycle that removes carbon dioxide from the air.

**Do I need to know the names of nitrogen cycle bacteria?**
No. The syllabus states that generic names of individual bacteria are not required. Describe them by role: decomposers, nitrifying, nitrogen-fixing and denitrifying bacteria.
