## Section A -- short questions

**1.** Distinguish a term from a representation, and state why the distinction matters. **[3]**

**2.** Explain, with one case, how the special knowledge of the person making a statement affects whether it is a term. **[3]**

**3.** State the parol evidence rule and explain one way in which a court may avoid it. **[4]**

**4.** Identify the three statutory implied terms about goods in the Consumer Rights Act 2015, giving the section for each. **[3]**

**5.** Explain the difference between a condition and an innominate term. **[4]**

**6.** Explain the contra proferentem rule, with an example. **[3]**

**7.** Explain how a previous course of dealing can incorporate an exemption clause. **[3]**

**8.** State the effect of s62 and s68 of the Consumer Rights Act 2015. **[4]**

## Section B -- scenario and essay questions

**9.** *Original scenario.* Dev, a consumer, buys a used car for £6,000 from Raceway Motors. Before signing, the salesperson tells him, "It has had one owner and has never been in an accident." The written contract does not mention this, and contains a signed clause: "The buyer relies on no statement not set out in this document." Six weeks later Dev discovers the car has had three owners and was badly damaged in a crash, and the gearbox has started to fail. Advise Dev on whether the salesperson's statement is a term and on his rights under the Consumer Rights Act 2015. **[12]**

**10.** *Original scenario.* Coastline Logistics Ltd hires refrigerated containers from ChillBox Ltd on ChillBox's written standard terms. Clause 8 states: "ChillBox shall not be liable for any loss arising from equipment failure, howsoever caused." A container fails because ChillBox negligently skipped a routine service, ruining £40,000 of Coastline's frozen fish. One of Coastline's drivers is also injured when a faulty container door falls on him. Advise Coastline whether ChillBox can rely on clause 8. **[12]**

**11.** *Original scenario.* Harbourside Gym engages FitPro Ltd to service its treadmills each month, the contract stating that service visits "shall take place on the first Monday of each month". FitPro attends on the first Tuesday in March. Harbourside wants to terminate the contract. Advise Harbourside whether it can do so. **[8]**

**12.** "The innominate term approach brings fairness to contract law at too high a price in certainty." Evaluate this statement. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** A term is part of the contract and is a promise the maker is bound to perform (1). A representation is a pre-contractual statement of fact that induces the contract but is not part of it (1). The distinction matters because breach of a term gives a claim for breach of contract, whereas an untrue representation can only found a misrepresentation claim with different remedies (1). **[3]**

**2.** A statement by a party with special knowledge or expertise is more likely to be a term, because the other party is entitled to rely on it (1). In Dick Bentley Productions v Harold Smith Motors (1965) a car dealer's statement about mileage was a term (1), whereas in Oscar Chess v Williams (1957) a private seller relying on the logbook made only a representation (1). **[3]**

**3.** Oral or other extrinsic evidence cannot be used to add to, vary or contradict a written contract (1). A court may avoid the rule by finding a collateral contract (1), as in City and Westminster Properties v Mudd (1959), where an oral promise that the tenant could keep sleeping on the premises was enforced although the lease banned residence (1). The rule exists for certainty, so the court must be satisfied that the writing was not intended to be the whole agreement (1). **[4]**

**4.** Satisfactory quality, s9 (1); fitness for a particular purpose, s10 (1); goods to be as described, s11 (1). **[3]**

**5.** A condition is a term so important that any breach allows the innocent party to terminate and claim damages (1), whatever the actual consequences, as in Poussard v Spiers and Pond (1876) (1). An innominate term is not classified in advance (1); the innocent party may terminate only if the breach deprives them of substantially the whole benefit of the contract, otherwise damages only (Hong Kong Fir, 1962) (1). **[4]**

**6.** Where an exemption clause is ambiguous, it is interpreted against the party seeking to rely on it (1). This reflects the idea that the party drafting the clause should express it clearly (1). In Houghton v Trafalgar Insurance (1954) an exclusion for an "excess load" did not cover carrying more passengers than the car's seats, so the insurer was liable (1). **[3]**

**7.** A clause can be incorporated if the parties have dealt with each other regularly on the same terms, so that the clause is taken to be known (1). The dealing must be regular and consistent: in McCutcheon v David MacBrayne (1964) inconsistent previous practice was not enough (1), and in Hollier v Rambler Motors (1972) three or four dealings over five years with a consumer did not incorporate the clause (1). **[3]**

**8.** Section 62: an unfair term is not binding on the consumer (1); a term is unfair if, contrary to good faith, it causes a significant imbalance in rights and obligations to the consumer's detriment (1). Section 68: a trader must ensure written terms are transparent (1), meaning expressed in plain and intelligible language and legible (1). **[4]**

**9.** Indicative content (1 mark per point):

- Identify the issue: whether the oral statement is a term or a representation (1).
- Importance: one owner and no accident history are central to the value of a used car, pointing to a term (Bannerman v White) (1).
- Knowledge: a motor dealer has special knowledge and Dev was entitled to rely on it (Dick Bentley) (1).
- The written contract does not repeat the statement, and the parol evidence rule could be raised (1).
- Possible collateral contract or finding that the writing was not the whole agreement (City and Westminster Properties v Mudd) (1).
- The "no statement relied on" clause: Dev signed, so it is prima facie binding (L'Estrange v Graucob) (1), but as a trader-consumer term it is subject to the fairness test in s62 and may not bind Dev (1).
- CRA 2015: Dev is a consumer and Raceway a trader (s2, s61) (1).
- s9: a failing gearbox after six weeks, on a £6,000 car, is arguably not satisfactory quality, though price and age lower expectations (1).
- s11: if the accident history formed part of the description, the car does not match it (1).
- Six weeks is beyond the 30-day short-term right to reject (s22) (1), so Dev should seek repair or replacement (s23), then a price reduction or final right to reject (s24) if that fails (1).
- Conclusion, drawn from the points above: Dev has a strong claim under s9, and probably s11. **[12]**

**10.** Indicative content (1 mark per point):

- Both parties are businesses, so UCTA 1977 applies, not the CRA 2015 (1); the liability is business liability under s1(3) (1).
- Incorporation: the clause is in ChillBox's written standard terms forming the contract, so it is incorporated (1).
- Construction: "howsoever caused" is wide, but a clause excluding negligence must be clear; ambiguity is construed contra proferentem (1). The words probably do cover negligence on their natural meaning (1).
- Driver's injury: s2(1) UCTA means liability for personal injury caused by negligence cannot be excluded (1). The clause cannot protect ChillBox against the driver's claim (1).
- Fish: property loss caused by negligence, so s2(2) applies and the clause must satisfy the reasonableness test (1). Section 3 also applies because Coastline dealt on ChillBox's written standard terms (1).
- s11: fair and reasonable given what was known or contemplated at the time of contracting (1); relevant factors include bargaining strength, whether Coastline could insure or contract elsewhere, and the blanket nature of the clause (1).
- Authority: George Mitchell v Finney Lock Seeds (1983), where a clause failed, contrasted with Watford Electronics v Sanderson (2001), equal commercial parties (1).
- Conclusion, drawn from the points above: the clause is ineffective for the injury and probably unreasonable for the negligent failure to service. **[12]**

**11.** Indicative content (1 mark per point):

- The issue is the status of the timing term (1).
- If a condition, any breach allows termination however trivial (1); in commercial contracts time clauses are often treated as conditions for certainty (Bunge v Tradax, 1981) (1).
- But the wording does not label the term a condition, and even a label is not conclusive (Schuler v Wickman, 1974) (1).
- If innominate, termination is allowed only if the breach deprives Harbourside of substantially the whole benefit (Hong Kong Fir) (1).
- A one-day delay in a monthly service is unlikely to do so (1).
- Terminating without the right would itself be a repudiation by Harbourside (1).
- Conclusion: Harbourside is probably limited to damages for any loss from the delay (1). **[8]**

**12.** Indicative content:

- Knowledge (up to 6): conditions and warranties (Poussard; Bettini v Gye); innominate terms (Hong Kong Fir; The Hansa Nord); labels not conclusive (Schuler); commercial time clauses as conditions (Bunge v Tradax).
- Analysis (up to 3): explain why classification by consequences stops a party using a trivial breach to escape a bad bargain, as the buyers sought to in The Hansa Nord.
- Evaluation (up to 6): for the statement, parties cannot know at the time of breach whether they may terminate, and wrongful termination is itself a repudiation, so uncertainty carries real risk; commercial parties value predictability. Against, the courts have reduced uncertainty by treating key commercial terms as conditions and by giving effect to clear drafting; parties can expressly state the consequences of breach. A balanced conclusion, for example that the approach is justified for general terms but should yield to certainty in mercantile time clauses. **[15]**
