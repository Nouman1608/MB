This guide covers subtopic **20.4 Magnetic fields due to currents** of Cambridge International AS & A Level Physics 9702, 2025–2027 series (A Level). The broader [Magnetic Fields study guide](/resources/a-physics-magnetic-fields/) introduces the whole of Topic 20 and describes the three field patterns in a paragraph; this page goes further into the 20.4 outcomes alone: how to find the direction of each field with a grip rule, what a good sketch of each pattern must show, why a ferrous core strengthens a solenoid, and a step-by-step method for the direction and size of the force between two current-carrying conductors. For quick recall use the [Magnetic fields due to currents revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-magnetic-fields-due-to-currents-revision-notes/), and for exam-style questions the [Magnetic fields due to currents practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-magnetic-fields-due-to-currents-practice/).

## What 20.4 asks you to do

The syllabus lists three outcomes. Candidates should be able to:

- **sketch magnetic field patterns due to the currents in a long straight wire, a flat circular coil and a long solenoid**
- **understand that the magnetic field due to the current in a solenoid is increased by a ferrous core**
- **explain the origin of the forces between current-carrying conductors and determine the direction of the forces**

No equation for the flux density near a wire or inside a solenoid is listed in these outcomes. Questions are about patterns, directions and explanations; if a numerical value of B is needed, it is given.

## The right-hand grip rule

Grip the wire with the **right** hand, thumb pointing along the conventional current. Your fingers curl in the direction of the field lines.

For a coil or solenoid, the rule is used the other way round: curl the fingers of the right hand in the direction of the current around the turns, and the thumb points along the field inside the coil, towards the **north** end.

A check for a solenoid's ends: look at one end. If the current flows **anticlockwise** as you look, that end is a **north** pole; if **clockwise**, a **south** pole.

## Long straight wire

**Pattern.** Concentric circles centred on the wire, in planes at right angles to it.

A good sketch, viewed along the wire (current into or out of the page):

- the wire shown as a circle containing a dot (current out of the page) or a cross (current into the page);
- at least three circles, with arrows on each, all in the same rotational sense;
- **spacing that increases with distance**, because the field gets weaker further from the wire.

Direction check: current **out of the page** → field lines **anticlockwise**; current **into the page** → **clockwise**.

## Flat circular coil

**Pattern.** Close to each part of the wire the lines are small, nearly circular loops round the wire, just as for a straight wire. Towards the centre of the coil, the loops from opposite sides add together, and near the centre the lines are **nearly straight and parallel, running along the axis**, at right angles to the plane of the coil.

A good sketch, viewed side-on (coil drawn as two dots or crosses where it cuts the page):

- one side of the coil marked with a dot and the other with a cross;
- loops around each side in the correct senses;
- straight line(s) through the centre along the axis, with the arrow pointing the same way through the middle;
- symmetry about the axis.

The overall pattern resembles that of a short bar magnet, with one face of the coil acting as a north pole.

## Long solenoid

**Pattern.** Inside, the lines are **straight, parallel and equally spaced**: the field is strong and very nearly uniform away from the ends. Outside, the pattern is like that of a bar magnet, with lines leaving the north end, looping round, and entering the south end. The field outside is much weaker than inside.

A good sketch:

- evenly spaced parallel lines inside, all with arrows in the same direction;
- lines spreading out at the ends and looping back outside;
- closed loops, with the arrows continuing inside from S to N;
- the polarity of each end consistent with the current direction.

The field inside is stronger with a larger current and with more turns per unit length.

## A ferrous core

Inserting a core of a **ferrous** (iron-containing) material into a solenoid greatly increases the magnetic flux density for the same current. The field of the solenoid magnetises the core, and the magnetised core adds its own, much larger, field in the same direction.

- **Soft iron** is used where the field must switch off when the current is switched off (electromagnets in relays, lifting magnets), because it loses its magnetism readily.
- A core of a material that keeps its magnetism would leave a permanent magnet behind; this is how permanent magnets can be made by placing a suitable bar inside a solenoid.
- A copper or aluminium core has no such effect: these are not ferrous materials.

## Forces between current-carrying conductors: the origin

Each wire carries a current, so each produces a magnetic field. Each wire therefore lies in the field of the other, and a current in a magnetic field feels a force (20.2). That is the whole origin of the force: **the field of one conductor acts on the current in the other.**

The result:

- currents in the **same** direction → the wires **attract**;
- currents in **opposite** directions → the wires **repel**.

By Newton's third law, the force on each wire is **equal in size and opposite in direction**, even when the two currents are different.

## A method for the direction

1. Pick one wire (say B). Use the right-hand grip rule on the other wire (A) to find the direction of A's field **at the position of B**.
2. Use Fleming's left-hand rule on B, with B's current and A's field, to find the force on B.
3. The force on A is equal and opposite.

**Worked example 1.** Two long parallel wires, P on the left and Q on the right, both carry current out of the page. Find the direction of the force on Q.

- Current out of the page in P → anticlockwise field round P. At Q (to the right of P), anticlockwise circulation points **up the page**.
- At Q: current out of the page, field up the page. Fleming's left-hand rule gives a force **to the left**, towards P.
- The force on P is to the right, towards Q. The wires attract, as expected for currents in the same direction.

*Annotation.* Write the intermediate step ("field of P at Q is up the page") explicitly; it carries a mark of its own.

## The catapult-field picture

The same result can be read from the combined field pattern. With currents in the same direction, the two fields are in opposite directions in the region between the wires and partly cancel there; the field is stronger outside the pair, and the wires are pushed towards the weaker region between them. With opposite currents, the fields add between the wires, the field is strongest there, and the wires are pushed apart.

## Worked example 2: size of the force

Two long parallel wires carry currents of 5.0 A (wire A) and 3.0 A (wire B). At the position of wire B, the flux density due to A is 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ T. Calculate the force per unit length on B, the force per unit length on A, and the flux density due to B at A.

```
On B:   F/L = B_A × I_B = 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ × 3.0 = 1.2 × 10⁻⁴ N m⁻¹
On A:   equal and opposite (Newton's third law) = 1.2 × 10⁻⁴ N m⁻¹
Field of B at A:   B_B = (F/L) / I_A = 1.2 × 10⁻⁴ / 5.0 = 2.4 × 10⁻⁵ T
```

*Annotation.* The larger current produces the larger field, but the smaller current sits in it; the products are equal, so the forces are equal. A common error is to say the wire with the larger current feels the larger force.

## Worked example 3: inside a solenoid

When a large current is switched on in a loosely wound solenoid (a "slinky" spring), the coil visibly shortens. Explain why.

*Model answer.* Adjacent turns carry current in the same direction [1]. Each turn lies in the magnetic field produced by its neighbour, so each experiences a force [1]. Currents in the same direction attract, so neighbouring turns pull together and the solenoid contracts [1].

## Common mistakes

- **Using the left hand** for the field around a wire. Grip rules use the right hand; the left hand is for force.
- **Drawing equally spaced circles** around a straight wire.
- **Drawing the field inside a solenoid as curved** or with arrows in different directions.
- **Saying the field of a coil at its centre is circular.** Near the centre it is along the axis.
- **Saying copper or aluminium cores increase the field.** Only ferrous materials do.
- **Giving a larger force to the wire with the larger current.** The forces are equal and opposite.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Sketch**: draw enough lines (three or more), with arrows, correct spacing and symmetry; mark current directions with dots and crosses.
- **State** (the effect of a ferrous core): the flux density is (greatly) increased for the same current.
- **Explain** (the origin of the forces): each conductor is in the magnetic field of the other, and a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field experiences a force.
- **Determine** (the direction): give the field direction of one wire at the other, then apply the left-hand rule, and state that the forces are equal and opposite.
- **Suggest** (why a core is soft iron): it is magnetised strongly while the current flows and loses its magnetism when the current stops.
