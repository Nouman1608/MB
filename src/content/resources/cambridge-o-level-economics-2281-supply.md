## 2.4.1 Supply and the supply diagram

**Supply** is the quantity of a product that sellers are willing and able to offer for sale at each price over a given period of time. Three parts of the definition carry marks:

- **willing and able**: a seller with no stock, no workers or no machine cannot supply, however keen it is
- **at each price**: supply is a whole relationship between price and quantity, not one number
- **over a period**: 200 loaves per day and 200 loaves per week are very different amounts

Do not confuse supply with **quantity supplied**. Supply is the whole schedule or curve. Quantity supplied is the single amount offered at one particular price.

### From schedule to curve

A bakery in invented Town K has this daily supply schedule:

| Price per loaf ($) | 1.00 | 1.50 | 2.00 | 2.50 | 3.00 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Quantity supplied (loaves per day) | 100 | 160 | 220 | 280 | 340 |

To draw the curve, put price on the vertical axis and quantity per period on the horizontal axis, plot the five points and join them. Label the line S. Each rise of $0.50 brings 60 more loaves, so this curve is a straight line sloping upwards from left to right.

**Interpreting the curve.** Any point on S tells you two things at once: the quantity offered at a given price, and the lowest price the bakery would accept to offer that quantity. Read across from $2.50 to S and down to 280 loaves; equally, the bakery will only bake the 280th loaf if it can sell at $2.50 or more. That second reading explains the upward slope. Extra output usually costs more per unit to produce (overtime, older ovens brought back into use), so a higher price is needed to make it worthwhile.

## 2.4.2 Extensions and contractions

A change in the product's **own price** moves the seller along the existing curve. Nothing about the curve itself changes.

- Price rises: quantity supplied rises. This is an **extension of supply**.
- Price falls: quantity supplied falls. This is a **contraction of supply**.

**Worked example 1.** The price of a loaf rises from $2.00 to $2.50.

1. Quantity supplied at $2.00 is 220; at $2.50 it is 280.
2. The extension is 280 − 220 = 60 loaves per day.
3. As a percentage of the starting quantity: 60 ÷ 220 × 100 = 27.3%.
4. On the diagram the point slides up S from (220, $2.00) to (280, $2.50). There is still only one curve.

If the question then says the price falls back to $2.00, the bakery contracts supply by 60 loaves, a fall of 60 ÷ 280 × 100 = 21.4%. The same move in loaves gives a different percentage because the starting point has changed.

## 2.4.3 Individual and market supply

**Market supply** is the sum of the quantities that all sellers offer at each price. This is aggregation: you add **horizontally**, quantity to quantity, at the same price. You never add the prices.

The useful extra point is that sellers do not all start supplying at the same price. A seller with high costs stays out until the price covers them. Three juice sellers in an invented market:

| Price per bottle ($) | Seller X | Seller Y | Seller Z | Market supply |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 10 | 0 | 0 | 10 |
| 3 | 20 | 15 | 0 | 35 |
| 4 | 30 | 25 | 20 | 75 |
| 5 | 40 | 35 | 30 | 105 |

Check the $4 row: 30 + 25 + 20 = 75. Two lessons follow.

- **Market supply extends faster than any single seller's supply.** From $3 to $4, Seller X adds 10 bottles but the market adds 40, because Y expands and Z starts selling. Part of an extension of market supply comes from sellers joining as the price rises.
- **A change in the number of sellers shifts market supply.** If Seller Z closes, market supply becomes 10, 35, 55 and 75. The fall is not the same at every price: nothing changes at $2 or $3, but 20 bottles go at $4 and 30 at $5. This is still a decrease in supply, because less is offered at some prices and no more at any price.

## 2.4.4 Conditions of supply

A **condition of supply** is anything other than the product's own price that changes the quantity sellers offer at every price. A change in a condition **shifts** the curve.

- **Increase in supply**: more is offered at every price; the curve moves to the right.
- **Decrease in supply**: less is offered at every price; the curve moves to the left.

| Condition | Direction that increases supply | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Costs of production (wages, materials, fuel, rent) | Costs fall | Each unit is cheaper to make, so more is worth offering at each price |
| Technology | Improves | Output per worker or machine rises, so cost per unit falls |
| Indirect taxes | Cut or removed | The seller keeps more of each sale |
| Subsidies to producers | Introduced or raised | The seller receives the price plus the subsidy |
| Number of sellers | Rises | More firms add their supply together |
| Weather and natural events | Good harvest or catch | Output is higher at every price |
| Prices of other products the firm could make | Fall | Resources stay in this product rather than switching |

The last row needs care. A farmer who can grow maize or soya compares the two. If the price of soya rises, land moves into soya, so the **supply of maize decreases** even though nothing happened to maize itself.

### Worked example 2: how far does the curve move?

Return to the Town K bakery. A shift can be described sideways (a change in quantity at each price) or vertically (a change in the price needed for each quantity).

**A subsidy of $0.50 per loaf.** The bakery now receives the market price plus $0.50. At a market price of $2.00 it receives $2.50 in total, so it offers what it used to offer at $2.50: 280 loaves.

| Price ($) | 1.00 | 1.50 | 2.00 | 2.50 | 3.00 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Original supply | 100 | 160 | 220 | 280 | 340 |
| With subsidy | 160 | 220 | 280 | 340 | 400 |

Supply increases by 60 loaves at every price. Seen vertically, each quantity can now be supplied at a price $0.50 lower, so the new curve S2 lies $0.50 below S1.

**A rise in flour costs of $0.25 per loaf.** Now the bakery needs $0.25 more than before for each quantity. At $2.00 it offers what it used to offer at $1.75, which is 190 loaves. The new schedule is 70, 130, 190, 250 and 310. Supply decreases by 30 loaves at every price, a fall of 30 ÷ 220 × 100 = 13.6% at $2.00. The new curve lies to the left of (and $0.25 above) the original.

## Common mistakes

- Defining supply as "the amount produced". It is the amount offered for sale at each price, over a stated period.
- Writing "supply increases" when only the product's price has risen. That is an extension, a movement along the curve.
- Shifting the curve the wrong way for a cost change. Higher costs move supply to the left, not upwards to the right.
- Adding prices instead of quantities when building market supply.
- Forgetting that the price of an alternative product the firm could make is a condition of supply.
- Drawing an unlabelled diagram. Label both axes, the curve and any old and new positions (S1, S2).

## Exam technique for 2.4

- **Define**: give the precise meaning, with "willing and able", "at each price" and a time period. A one-word gloss such as "production" earns little.
- **Identify** or **State**: one clear point, for example "an increase in supply". No explanation is needed, but the correct term is.
- **Calculate**: show the working (for example 60 ÷ 220 × 100) and give units, such as loaves per day.
- **Explain**: give the cause, the mechanism and the result. For a cost rise: costs per unit rise, so at each price fewer units are profitable, so supply decreases and the curve shifts left.
- **Analyse**: use a diagram and refer to it in your writing: name S1 and S2 and say which quantity is offered at a stated price before and after.
- **Discuss**: for a question such as whether new technology will increase supply, weigh how much costs actually fall, how many firms adopt it, and how long adoption takes.
