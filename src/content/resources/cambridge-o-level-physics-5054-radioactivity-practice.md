## Section A

**1.** Name **two** sources of background radiation, and name a detector suitable for gamma radiation. **[3]**

**2.** Describe the nature of an alpha particle, a beta particle and gamma radiation. **[3]**

**3.** A Geiger–Müller tube records 2400 counts in 4.0 minutes near a source. The background count rate is 0.40 counts/s. Calculate the corrected count rate in counts/minute. **[3]**

**4.** Radioactive decay is described as *spontaneous* and *random*. State what each word means. **[2]**

**5.** Put alpha, beta and gamma radiation in order of **(a)** increasing penetrating power, and **(b)** increasing ionising effect. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** A student investigates a radioactive source with a Geiger–Müller tube. The background count rate is 20 counts/min.

| Absorber between source and tube | Count rate (counts/min) |
|---|---|
| None | 620 |
| Thin paper | 330 |
| 2 mm aluminium | 325 |
| 10 mm lead | 22 |

**(a)** Explain why the background count rate is measured. **[1]**
**(b)** Deduce which types of radiation the source emits, giving a reason for each type present or absent. **[4]**

**7.** A beam containing alpha particles, beta particles and gamma radiation enters a magnetic field at right angles to the field.

**(a)** State which radiation passes through undeflected, and explain why. **[2]**
**(b)** Compare the deflections of the alpha and beta particles, explaining the difference in direction and the difference in amount. **[3]**

**8.** Complete the decay equations, and state what happens in part (c).

**(a)** Cobalt-60, ⁶⁰₂₇Co, decays by beta emission to nickel (Ni). **[2]**
**(b)** Plutonium-239, ²³⁹₉₄Pu, decays by alpha emission to uranium (U). **[2]**
**(c)** State what happens to the nucleon number and the proton number of a nucleus when it emits gamma radiation. **[1]**

**9.** Radon-222, ²²²₈₆Rn, decays by emitting an alpha particle. The product also emits an alpha particle, and the nucleus formed then emits a beta particle.

**(a)** State the nucleon number and proton number of the nucleus after the two alpha decays. **[2]**
**(b)** State the nucleon number and proton number after the beta decay. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the number of neutrons in the final nucleus. **[1]**

**10.** One possible fission of uranium-235 is

²³⁵₉₂U + ¹₀n → ¹⁴⁰₅₄Xe + ⁹⁴₃₈Sr + x ¹₀n

**(a)** Calculate x, showing that the proton numbers also balance. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how the neutrons released can produce a chain reaction, and the role of the moderator in a reactor. **[3]**

**11.** A student measures the count rate from a sample every 10 minutes. The background count rate is 50 counts/min throughout.

| Time / min | 0 | 10 | 20 | 30 | 40 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Measured count rate / counts per min | 850 | 554 | 367 | 250 | 176 |

**(a)** Calculate the corrected count rate at 0 min and at 30 min. **[2]**
**(b)** Use your answers to find the half-life. **[2]**
**(c)** Predict the corrected count rate and the measured count rate at 60 min. **[2]**

**12.** A gamma-emitting tracer used to diagnose a medical condition has a half-life of 6.0 hours.

**(a)** Calculate the fraction of the tracer nuclei remaining 24 hours after injection. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why a gamma emitter with a short half-life is chosen for this use. **[2]**

**13.** A wooden beam from an ancient building has a carbon-14 activity per gram of 0.50 counts/min. Wood from a living tree gives 16 counts/min per gram. The half-life of carbon-14 is about 5700 years.

**(a)** Explain why the carbon-14 activity of wood falls after the tree dies. **[3]**
**(b)** Estimate the age of the beam. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two, 1 mark each: **radon gas in the air**; **rocks and buildings**; **food and drink**; **cosmic rays**. Detector: **Geiger–Müller tube (and counter)** [1].

**2.** Alpha: **two protons and two neutrons (a helium nucleus)** [1]. Beta: a **high-speed electron emitted from the nucleus** [1]. Gamma: **high-frequency electromagnetic waves** [1].

**3.** Measured rate = 2400 ÷ 4.0 = 600 counts/min [1]; background = 0.40 × 60 = 24 counts/min [1]; corrected = 600 − 24 = **576 counts/min** [1].

**4.** Spontaneous: decay **is not affected by external conditions** and cannot be made to happen or prevented [1]. Random: it is **impossible to predict which nucleus will decay or when** (and the direction of emission is random) [1].

**5. (a)** **Alpha, beta, gamma** [1].
**(b)** **Gamma, beta, alpha** [1].

**6. (a)** To **subtract it from each reading**, so that only the radiation from the source is compared (corrected count rate) [1].
**(b)** **Alpha present** [1]: thin paper causes a large drop (from 600 to 310 counts/min above background), and only alpha is stopped by paper. **Beta absent** [1]: 2 mm of aluminium causes no significant further drop. **Gamma present** [1]: about 300 counts/min above background still pass through the aluminium. Gamma is confirmed because the **10 mm lead reduces the count to background**, since only gamma could pass the aluminium and then be absorbed by lead [1].

**7. (a)** **Gamma** [1], because it is an electromagnetic wave with **no charge** [1].
**(b)** Alpha and beta are deflected in **opposite directions because they have opposite charges** (alpha positive, beta negative) [1]. Beta is **deflected much more** [1] because its **mass is very much smaller** than that of an alpha particle [1].

**8. (a)** ⁶⁰₂₇Co → **⁶⁰₂₈Ni** + **⁰₋₁e**: [1] for ⁶⁰₂₈Ni, [1] for the beta particle written correctly.
**(b)** ²³⁹₉₄Pu → **²³⁵₉₂U** + **⁴₂He**: [1] for ²³⁵₉₂U, [1] for the alpha particle written correctly.
**(c)** **Neither changes** — the nucleus only loses energy [1].

**9. (a)** Nucleon number 222 − 8 = **214** [1]; proton number 86 − 4 = **82** [1].
**(b)** Nucleon number **214**, proton number **83** [1].
**(c)** 214 − 83 = **131 neutrons** [1].

**10. (a)** Nucleon numbers: 235 + 1 = 140 + 94 + x, so x = **2** [1]. Proton numbers: 92 + 0 = 54 + 38 = 92, so they balance [1].
**(b)** The released neutrons can be **absorbed by other uranium-235 nuclei, causing further fissions** [1], which release more neutrons, so the process continues as a **chain reaction** [1]. The **moderator slows the neutrons down**, making them more likely to be absorbed by uranium-235 and cause fission [1].

**11. (a)** At 0 min: 850 − 50 = **800 counts/min** [1]. At 30 min: 250 − 50 = **200 counts/min** [1].
**(b)** 800 → 400 → 200 is **two half-lives** in 30 min [1], so the half-life = **15 min** [1].
**(c)** 60 min is four half-lives: corrected = 800 ÷ 16 = **50 counts/min** [1]; measured = 50 + 50 = **100 counts/min** [1].

**12. (a)** 24 ÷ 6.0 = 4 half-lives [1]; fraction remaining = (½)⁴ = **1/16** [1].
**(b)** Gamma radiation **passes out of the body to the detector outside** and is the least ionising [1]; a **short half-life means the activity falls quickly**, so the patient is exposed to radiation for only a short time [1].

**13. (a)** While alive, the tree **takes in carbon, including carbon-14**, keeping its proportion steady [1]. After death **no new carbon is taken in** [1], and the **carbon-14 decays without being replaced**, so its activity falls [1].
**(b)** 16 → 8 → 4 → 2 → 1 → 0.50 is **five half-lives** [1]; age ≈ 5 × 5700 = **28 500 years** (about 29 000 years) [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Subtracting background in different units from the measured rate, or forgetting to subtract it before halving.
- Saying gamma is deflected because it is "a type of radiation"; it has no charge.
- Stating that alpha is deflected more than beta.
- Writing beta decay with the proton number going down, or leaving the electron's −1 off the equation.
- Interpreting a small drop in count rate as proof of absorption, without allowing for random variation.
- Giving only the radiation type, not the half-life reason, when explaining the choice of a medical tracer.
