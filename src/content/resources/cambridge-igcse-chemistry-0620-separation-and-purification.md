This guide covers subtopic **12.4 Separation and purification** of Topic 12, Experimental techniques and chemical analysis, in the Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620 syllabus for 2026–2028. All three outcomes are **Core**, and the same techniques are listed as experimental contexts for the practical papers. For quick recall use the [separation and purification revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-separation-and-purification-revision-notes/); for exam-style questions use the [separation and purification practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-separation-and-purification-practice/). Paper chromatography, the other separation method, has its own [chromatography study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-chromatography/).

## What 12.4 asks you to do

1. **Describe and explain** methods of separation and purification using **(a) a suitable solvent, (b) filtration, (c) crystallisation, (d) simple distillation, (e) fractional distillation**.
2. **Suggest** suitable separation and purification techniques, given information about the substances involved.
3. **Identify substances and assess their purity** using melting point and boiling point information.

"Describe **and explain**" means each method needs both the steps and the reason it works.

## The five methods

### (a) Using a suitable solvent

A solvent is chosen that **dissolves one component of a mixture but not the other**. The dissolved substance can then be separated from the undissolved one by filtration. Water is the usual solvent for ionic salts; a substance that does not dissolve in water may dissolve in another solvent, such as ethanol. **Why it works:** the components differ in **solubility** in that solvent.

### (b) Filtration

Filtration separates an **insoluble solid** from a **liquid** or solution. Pour the mixture through a filter paper in a funnel. The solid stays in the paper as the **residue**; the liquid passes through as the **filtrate**. **Why it works:** the solid particles are too large to pass through the pores in the paper, while dissolved particles and liquid molecules pass through.

The residue is often **washed** with a little distilled water to remove traces of the solution, then dried.

### (c) Crystallisation

Crystallisation obtains a **soluble solid** from its **solution**:

1. **Heat** the solution in an evaporating basin to **evaporate some of the solvent**.
2. Stop at the **crystallisation point**: dip a glass rod in and lift it out; if crystals form on the rod as it cools, the solution is close to **saturated**.
3. **Leave to cool**. The solubility of most solids falls as temperature falls, so crystals form.
4. **Filter** to collect the crystals.
5. **Wash** with a small amount of **cold** distilled water (cold, so little of the crystals redissolves).
6. **Dry** between filter papers or in a warm place.

**Why not just evaporate to dryness?** Heating to dryness leaves any soluble impurities mixed into the solid, and strong heating can decompose some salts or drive off their water of crystallisation. Crystallisation leaves soluble impurities behind in the remaining solution.

### (d) Simple distillation

Simple distillation obtains a **solvent from a solution**, for example pure water from salt water.

- Heat the solution in a flask; the solvent **boils** and turns to **vapour**.
- The vapour passes into a **condenser**, cooled by cold water flowing in at the bottom and out at the top, where it **condenses**.
- The liquid **distillate** is collected; the dissolved solid stays in the flask.
- The **thermometer bulb** is placed level with the **side arm**, to measure the temperature of the vapour leaving the flask.

**Why it works:** the solvent has a much lower boiling point than the dissolved solid.

### (e) Fractional distillation

Fractional distillation separates **miscible liquids with different boiling points**, such as ethanol (boiling point 78 °C) and water (100 °C).

- A **fractionating column** is fitted between the flask and the condenser.
- Vapours rise up the column; the liquid with the **higher** boiling point condenses and drips back, while the liquid with the **lower** boiling point reaches the top and passes into the condenser.
- The thermometer reading stays **constant** at the lower boiling point while that liquid distils. When it rises, the next liquid is starting to distil, and the receiver is changed.

**Why it works:** the liquids have **different boiling points**; the column allows repeated condensing and boiling, which improves separation. The same principle separates petroleum into fractions (Topic 11).

## Choosing a method (outcome 2)

Ask what the mixture contains and which property differs:

| Mixture | Property that differs | Method |
|---|---|---|
| insoluble solid + liquid | particle size / solubility | filtration |
| soluble solid in solution, want the solid | solid is involatile | crystallisation |
| solution, want the solvent | boiling points very different | simple distillation |
| two miscible liquids | different boiling points | fractional distillation |
| two solids, one soluble | solubility in a solvent | dissolve, filter, then crystallise |
| several coloured substances in solution | travel differently on paper | chromatography |

### Worked example 1: a multi-step separation

*Describe how to obtain pure, dry samples of both sand and sodium chloride from a mixture of the two.*

1. Add **water** and stir: sodium chloride dissolves, sand does not (suitable solvent).
2. **Filter**: sand is the residue; salt solution is the filtrate.
3. **Wash** the sand with distilled water and **dry** it.
4. **Heat** the filtrate to evaporate some water until crystals form on a cooled glass rod; **leave to cool** so crystals form; **filter**, **wash** with a little cold water and **dry** the crystals.

Each step names a technique and the reason it is used, which is what "describe and explain" rewards.

### Worked example 2: suggesting a method from data

*Liquid P (boiling point 65 °C) and liquid R (boiling point 118 °C) mix completely. Suggest how to obtain a pure sample of P.*

Both are liquids that mix, so filtration and crystallisation cannot work. Their boiling points differ, so use **fractional distillation**. P has the lower boiling point, so it distils first; collect the liquid while the thermometer reads a steady 65 °C.

## Purity from melting and boiling points (outcome 3)

| | Pure substance | Impure substance |
|---|---|---|
| Melting point | **sharp**, at a **fixed** value | **lower** and over a **range** of temperatures |
| Boiling point | **sharp**, at a **fixed** value | **higher** and over a range |

To **identify** a substance, compare its measured melting or boiling point with data values. Pure water melts at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C at normal pressure.

### Worked example 3: judging purity

*A data book gives the melting point of a compound as 122 °C. Sample S melts sharply at 122 °C; sample T melts between 115 °C and 119 °C. Comment on each.*

- **S** melts sharply at the data value: it is **pure** and is that compound.
- **T** melts **below** the data value and **over a range**: it is **impure**.

Seawater boiling at a temperature above 100 °C is the matching boiling-point example: dissolved salt raises the boiling point.

## Common mistakes

- **Evaporating to dryness** when the question asks for crystallisation.
- **Saying the filtrate is the solid.**
- **Placing the thermometer in the liquid** during distillation instead of at the side arm.
- **Condenser water flowing the wrong way** (it enters at the bottom).
- **Using simple distillation for two liquids** with close boiling points.
- **Saying impurities raise the melting point.** They lower it and widen the range.

## Exam technique

- **Describe:** list the steps in order with the apparatus.
- **Explain:** give the property each method relies on (solubility, particle size, boiling point).
- **Suggest:** identify the states and properties from the data, then match a method.
- **Assess purity:** compare with the data value and comment on sharpness.
