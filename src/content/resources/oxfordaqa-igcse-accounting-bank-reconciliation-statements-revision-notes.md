## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Bank statement | The bank's record of the account, sent to the business; outside evidence for checking the cash book |
| Cash book (bank column) | The business's own record of the same account |
| Bank reconciliation statement | A working that explains the difference between the two balances |
| Unpresented cheque | A cheque the business has paid out and recorded, but the bank has not yet deducted |
| Outstanding banking | A banking entered in the cash book that the bank has still to credit |
| Bank error | A mistake in the bank's record, corrected on the reconciliation only |
| Direct debit | Payment collected by the payee under your authority; amount can vary |
| Standing order | Fixed payment made by the bank on set dates on your instruction |
| Credit transfer | A payment made straight into your account by another person |

## Why the two records differ

1. The bank has an item the cash book lacks: charges, interest, direct debit, standing order, credit transfer.
2. The cash book has an item the bank lacks: unpresented cheque, outstanding banking.
3. A mistake: a cash book error (you fix it in the cash book) or a bank error (you fix it on the reconciliation).

## Cash book updating table

| Item | Side of cash book |
|---|---|
| Bank charges | Credit |
| Interest paid | Credit |
| Bank interest received | Debit |
| Direct debit | Credit |
| Standing order | Credit |
| Credit transfer received | Debit |
| Receipt omitted or understated | Debit the difference |
| Payment omitted or understated | Credit the difference |
| Receipt overstated | Credit the difference |
| Payment overstated | Debit the difference |

## Method in steps

1. List every difference and classify it: cash book entry, timing item, or bank error.
2. Update the cash book, entering each cash book item, and find the new balance.
3. Write the bank statement balance as the first line of the reconciliation.
4. Add outstanding bankings.
5. Deduct unpresented cheques.
6. Adjust for bank errors: add if the bank took out too much or failed to credit something; deduct if the bank credited something it should not have.
7. Compare the result with the updated cash book balance. If it differs, an item has been missed or put on the wrong side.

## Reconciliation layout

```
Bank reconciliation statement at (date)
Balance per bank statement                  xxx
Add:  outstanding bankings                  xxx
Add or less: bank errors                    xxx
Less: unpresented cheques                  (xxx)
Balance per updated cash book               xxx
```

## Small worked reminder

Thorncroft Tools has a cash book debit balance of 2,500. The bank statement shows charges of 60, interest received of 25 and a direct debit of 180, none yet in the cash book.

```
Updated cash book: 2,500 - 60 + 25 - 180 = 2,285 (debit)
```

If the bank statement balance is 2,610 (credit) and the only other differences are one unpresented cheque of 325, the check is: 2,610 less 325 equals 2,285. The reconciliation agrees.

### Overdraft reminder

Thorncroft Tools in a different month has a cash book credit balance of 920 (overdrawn). Bank charges of 30 and a standing order of 110 are missing, so the updated balance is an overdraft of 1,060. It has an unpresented cheque of 240 and an outstanding banking of 500. The bank statement overdraft must therefore be 1,320.

```
Balance per bank statement (overdrawn)    (1,320)
Add: outstanding banking                     500
                                            (820)
Less: unpresented cheque                    (240)
Balance per updated cash book (overdrawn) (1,060)
```

Treat an overdraft as a negative figure and use the same add and deduct rules. Adding an outstanding banking makes the overdraft smaller.

## Where each item goes at a glance

| Item in the question | Cash book entry needed? | On the reconciliation? |
|---|---|---|
| Bank charges | Yes | No |
| Direct debit not yet recorded | Yes | No |
| Unpresented cheque | No | Yes, deduct |
| Outstanding banking | No | Yes, add |
| Bank error | No | Yes, add or deduct |
| Cash book error | Yes | No |

Check the table after you have classified your items. An item should never appear in both places.

## Must-know distinctions

| Compare | Key point |
|---|---|
| Unpresented cheque vs outstanding banking | The first is a payment, deducted from the bank statement balance; the second is a receipt, added |
| Cash book error vs bank error | Cash book errors are corrected in the cash book; bank errors on the reconciliation |
| Direct debit vs standing order | Direct debit is initiated by the payee and can vary; standing order is fixed by you |
| Cash book debit balance vs bank statement balance | A favourable balance is debit in the cash book and credit on the bank statement |
| Updating the cash book vs reconciling | The first adjusts your own record; the second explains the gap to the bank record |

## Limits of the statement

A reconciliation checks the bank account only. It will not show an error that appears in both the cash book and the bank statement, and it does not prove that the rest of the ledger is correct. Prepare it regularly, so that old unpresented cheques and unrecorded items are noticed early.

## Use and purpose, in short

- Confirms the cash at bank figure used in the statement of financial position.
- Finds errors in the cash book and in the bank's record.
- Reveals items not yet recorded, such as charges and standing orders.
- Helps spot fraud, such as unauthorised payments.
- Shows which bankings and cheques are still to clear.

## Quick self-test

1. State one reason why a bank statement may show a lower balance than the cash book.
2. Which side of the cash book takes a credit transfer received?
3. A cash book has a debit balance of 1,800. Bank charges of 42 and a standing order of 130 are missing. What is the updated balance?
4. A bank statement shows a credit balance of 1,900. There are unpresented cheques of 300 and an outstanding banking of 450. What is the balance per updated cash book?
5. A bank statement shows an overdraft of 640 and unpresented cheques of 200. There are no other differences. What is the cash book balance?
6. Is a bank error corrected in the cash book? Explain briefly.
7. A cash book has a credit balance of 410 (overdrawn). A credit transfer of 500 from a customer is not yet recorded. What is the updated balance and is it debit or credit?
8. A payment of 85 was recorded in the cash book as 58. Is the correction a debit or credit, and for what amount?
9. Give one use of preparing a bank reconciliation statement.
10. Why is an unpresented cheque deducted from the bank statement balance?

### Answers

1. Any of: an unpresented cheque, or bank charges or a direct debit the cash book has not recorded.
2. The debit side.
3. 1,800 - 42 - 130 = 1,628 (debit).
4. 1,900 - 300 + 450 = 2,050.
5. The statement balance is minus 640; minus 640 minus 200 gives minus 840, an overdraft of 840 (credit balance in the cash book).
6. No. The error is in the bank's record, so the cash book is right; it is corrected on the reconciliation and the business tells the bank.
7. -410 + 500 = 90, a debit balance (the account is now in funds).
8. Credit, 27 (85 - 58), because the payment was understated and the balance overstated.
9. Any of: confirm the cash at bank figure, find errors, reveal unrecorded items, detect fraud.
10. The business has already recorded the payment but the bank has not yet taken it out, so the bank balance is too high until the cheque is presented.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Starting the reconciliation from the old cash book balance instead of the bank statement balance, or ending on the wrong balance.
- Adding unpresented cheques and deducting outstanding bankings.
- Posting a bank error to the cash book.
- Putting direct debits and standing orders on the debit side.
- Showing no label for overdrawn balances, so the examiner cannot tell which sign you intended.
- Leaving out brackets or dates in the heading.
- Using a rounded or incomplete list of items, so that the reconciliation does not agree and no check is shown.
- Answering "explain" questions with a list of names and no reason.
