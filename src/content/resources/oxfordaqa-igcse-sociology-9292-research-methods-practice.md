> | Country | Urban schools (%) | Rural schools (%) |
> |---|---|---|
> | A | 98 | 91 |
> | B | 85 | 42 |
> | C | 76 | 51 |
> | D | 99 | 97 |

**(a)** Using Item A, identify the country with the lowest percentage of rural schools with electricity. **[1]**

## Part 2: Paper 2 style

**5.** Explain **one** strength and **one** limitation of using police-recorded crime statistics to study patterns of offending. **[4]**

## Answers: Part 1

**A1** [2 marks]

Data **collected first-hand by the researcher** [1], for their own research purpose, using methods such as questionnaires, interviews or observation [1].

**A2** [2 marks — 1 each]

Any two: **closed** (fixed-choice) questions; **open** questions; **scaled** questions (such as a rating from 1 to 5); also credit multiple-choice or yes/no questions as closed questions.

**A3** [2 marks]

A sample in which the researcher finds **one or a few participants, who then introduce others** they know [1]. It is used for **hard-to-reach groups** with no sampling frame, such as people involved in crime [1].

**A4 (a)** [1 mark]

**Country E (23%)** [1].

**A4 (b)** [2 marks]

**Country B** [1]; the gap is **43 percentage points** (85 − 42) [1].

**A4 (c)** [2 marks — 1 for the pattern, 1 for supporting figures]

e.g. In **all five countries a smaller percentage of rural schools than urban schools have electricity** [1], for example 64% urban and 23% rural in E [1]. Or: the **gap is smallest where provision is high overall** [1]: D (99% and 97%, a gap of 2) and A (98% and 91%, a gap of 7) [1].

**A4 (d)** [2 marks]

e.g. Schools without electricity **cannot use computers or the internet** [1], so rural pupils fall behind in **digital skills** and lose access to online learning [1]. Or: no lighting or fans can make classrooms **dark or uncomfortable**, which can limit learning and school hours [1] [1].

**A4 (e)** [2 marks — 1 for the limitation, 1 for the explanation]

e.g. It **does not say how reliable the supply is** [1]; a school with power for only a few hours a day counts the same as one with a constant supply [1]. Or: it gives **no source or date** [1], so we cannot judge how accurate or up to date it is [1]. Or: it shows a pattern **but not its causes**, such as poverty or distance from power lines [1] [1].

## Answers: Part 2

**A5** [4 marks — 2 for the strength, 2 for the limitation]

**Strength**: they are **cheap and readily available**, and cover a whole country over many years [1], so trends and patterns by area or offence can be compared [1]. **Limitation**: many crimes are **never reported or recorded** (the "dark figure" of crime) [1], so the statistics may reflect police priorities and public reporting rather than the true pattern of offending [1].

**A6 (a)** [2 marks]

Peers **police masculinity through teasing** [1]: the older boys used the phrase "real men don't cry" to pressure the younger boy to hide his feelings, a form of informal social control [1].

**A6 (b)** [2 marks]

The boys **behaved differently when the researcher was present**, becoming quieter and watching him [1]. This is the **Hawthorne effect**, so what he recorded may not be their natural behaviour, reducing validity; the key incident happened only after he left [1].

**A7** [4 marks — 2 per issue]

Two from: **no informed consent** [1] — gang members do not know they are being studied, so they cannot agree or withdraw [1]; **deception** [1] — the researcher must lie about their identity, breaking trust [1]; **harm** [1] — the researcher could witness or be drawn into crime, or put participants at risk if the research exposes them [1]; **confidentiality** [1] — if the researcher learns of serious crimes, they may face a conflict between protecting participants and reporting harm [1].

**A8** [4 marks]

Obtain a **sampling frame**, such as the school register of all 600 students [1]. **Divide it into strata** relevant to the topic, such as by gender and year group [1]. Take a **random sample from each stratum in proportion to its size**: for example, if 300 of the 600 are girls and the sample is 60, randomly choose 30 girls and 30 boys [1]. This makes the sample **representative** of the key groups, so findings can be generalised to the school [1].

**A9** [12 marks — levels]

| Level | Marks | Descriptor |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | 10–12 | Detailed knowledge of the method; strengths and limitations applied closely to people living in poverty; clear, justified conclusion |
| 3 | 7–9 | Good knowledge; some application to the topic; conclusion reached |
| 2 | 4–6 | Some relevant knowledge; general evaluation, little application |
| 1 | 1–3 | Basic points, mainly description |

Indicative content:

- **Strengths**: rich, **valid**, qualitative data on how poverty feels; rapport allows sensitive topics such as shame or debt to be discussed; participants can raise issues the researcher had not thought of; suits an **interpretivist** approach.
- **Limitations**: **small samples**, so findings may not be **representative** or **generalisable**; hard to **repeat**, so low reliability; **interviewer bias**, for example class differences between interviewer and interviewee; **social desirability**, such as hiding struggles; **time-consuming** and costly.
- **Ethics**: informed consent, anonymity, wellbeing; avoiding raising false hopes of help.
- **Alternatives**: questionnaires or official statistics to measure the extent of poverty; **triangulation**.
- **Judgement**: for example, very useful for understanding experiences, less useful for measuring how widespread poverty is.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating a gap from the wrong columns, or giving a pattern without figures.
- Saying data is "unreliable" without explaining why.
- Missing the Hawthorne effect when the extract shows behaviour changing.
- Describing a stratified sample without a sampling frame or proportions.
- Evaluating unstructured interviews in general rather than for people living in poverty.
