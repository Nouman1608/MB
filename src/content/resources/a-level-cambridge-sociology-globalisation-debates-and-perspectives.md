Globalisation is the growing interconnectedness of the world's societies through trade, media,
migration, technology and politics. In Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699), syllabus
heading 7 *Key debates, concepts and perspectives* is the first half of Paper 4's Globalisation
section. It asks you to define globalisation and its problems (7.1), explain its effect on identity
(7.2) and assess what it means for democracy, social movements and the nation state (7.3). Every
Paper 4 question is a 35-mark essay, so you need contrasting perspectives, not just definitions.

This guide sits under the [Paper 4 overview guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-sociology-globalisation-media-religion/);
the second half of the Globalisation section is covered in the
[globalisation contemporary issues guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-sociology-globalisation-contemporary-issues/).

## Syllabus coverage

Cambridge International AS & A Level Sociology 9699 (2024–2026), Paper 4 Section A, heading 7:

- **7.1 Perspectives on globalisation** — key definitions and issues, including globalisation,
  glocalisation, global culture and problems with defining globalisation; cultural, political and
  economic dimensions; perspectives on who benefits: Marxist, feminist, postmodernist, globalist,
  sceptic and transformationalist.
- **7.2 Globalisation and identity** — ethnic revitalisation, cultural defence and hybrid identity;
  cultural divergence versus cultural convergence/homogenisation; the role of Western ideology in
  shaping identity and the concept of Westernisation.
- **7.3 Globalisation, power and politics** — the spread of liberal democracy and human rights;
  global social movements and attempts to oppose globalisation; debates about the role of the
  nation state in tackling global social and environmental problems.

## What is globalisation, and why is it hard to define?

Anthony Giddens (*Runaway World*, 1999) describes globalisation as the intensification of
worldwide social relations, so that local events are shaped by distant ones. David Harvey
(*The Condition of Postmodernity*, 1989) called this **time-space compression**. Marshall McLuhan
had earlier coined the phrase **global village** (1962).

Three problems with defining it:

1. **Is it new?** Trade empires and colonialism linked continents centuries ago; sceptics argue
   today's interconnection is a difference of degree.
2. **Is it one process or several?** Economic, political and cultural globalisation move at
   different speeds.
3. **Is it global at all?** Many people, especially the rural poor, have little access to global
   networks, so "globalisation" may describe the experience of richer groups.

**Glocalisation** — Roland Robertson's term (1992) — describes global products and ideas being
adapted to local cultures. **Global culture** refers to shared consumer goods, media and values
that circulate worldwide.

## What are the cultural, political and economic dimensions?

| Dimension | What it involves | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Economic** | Transnational corporations (TNCs), global finance, supply chains, free-trade agreements | Clothing designed in one country, made in another, sold worldwide |
| **Political** | International organisations and agreements; pooled sovereignty | United Nations, World Trade Organization, regional blocs |
| **Cultural** | Global media, brands, languages, migration of ideas and people | Streaming services, social media platforms, global sport |

## Who benefits from globalisation? The six perspectives

| Perspective | Core claim | Key names |
|---|---|---|
| **Globalist (hyperglobalist)** | Globalisation is real and largely positive; national borders and states are becoming less important | Kenichi Ohmae, *The Borderless World* (1990) |
| **Sceptic** | Globalisation is exaggerated; most trade is regional and states remain powerful | Paul Hirst and Grahame Thompson, *Globalization in Question* (1996) |
| **Transformationalist** | Globalisation is real but uneven, reshaping states rather than abolishing them | David Held and colleagues, *Global Transformations* (1999) |
| **Marxist** | Globalisation is the expansion of capitalism; the main beneficiaries are a transnational capitalist class | Leslie Sklair, *Sociology of the Global System* (1991); Immanuel Wallerstein's world-systems theory |
| **Feminist** | Globalisation relies on and may deepen exploitation of women's paid and unpaid labour | Cynthia Enloe, *Bananas, Beaches and Bases* (1989); global care chains (Arlie Hochschild) |
| **Postmodernist** | Globalisation produces fragmented, hybrid, consumer-based identities and the decline of grand narratives | Jean Baudrillard; Zygmunt Bauman |

The globalist / sceptic / transformationalist typology comes from Held et al. (1999). Use it as
the backbone of an essay on "who benefits", then bring in Marxist and feminist critiques.

## How does globalisation affect identity?

Section 7.2 sets two big possibilities against each other.

**Cultural convergence / homogenisation.** Global media and brands make cultures more alike.
George Ritzer's *The McDonaldization of Society* (1993) argues that efficiency, calculability,
predictability and control spread from fast food to all areas of life. **Westernisation** is the
claim that this convergence is really the spread of Western — especially American — consumerism,
individualism and liberal values, sometimes described as cultural imperialism.

**Cultural divergence.** Contact between cultures can increase difference:

- **Hybrid identity** — new identities blending more than one culture; Homi Bhabha (*The Location of
  Culture*, 1994) and Stuart Hall's work on diaspora identities.
- **Cultural defence** — groups protect their culture against outside threat, often through
  religion or nationalism; Steve Bruce uses this idea in *Religion in the Modern World* (1996).
- **Ethnic revitalisation** — renewed pride in, and revival of, ethnic languages, dress or
  traditions in response to globalisation.

Samuel Huntington (*The Clash of Civilizations*, 1996) argued that cultural identities, not
ideology, would become the main source of global conflict; critics say this exaggerates the unity
of "civilisations". Benjamin Barber's *Jihad vs. McWorld* (1995) presents homogenisation and
backlash as two sides of the same process.

## Is globalisation spreading democracy and human rights?

Francis Fukuyama's essay "The End of History?" (1989, book 1992) argued that liberal democracy had
defeated its rivals after the Cold War. The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948),
international courts and global media scrutiny are cited as evidence of spreading rights norms.

Critics point out that democratic gains have been uneven and in some places reversed; that
"democracy promotion" can serve Western interests; and that TNCs and global markets can override
the choices of elected governments.

## Can global social movements oppose globalisation?

Global social movements use the same networks they criticise. Examples include the protests at the
World Trade Organization meeting in Seattle (1999), the World Social Forum (first held in Porto
Alegre, Brazil, in 2001), the Occupy movement (2011) and climate movements such as Fridays for
Future (from 2018). Many describe themselves as "alter-globalisation" — seeking a different
globalisation rather than none. Manuel Castells (*The Rise of the Network Society*, 1996) argues
networked communication gives such movements new power. Evaluation: movements often struggle to
turn protest into lasting policy change.

## Can nation states still tackle global problems?

- **Globalist view:** states are losing power to markets and TNCs; Ohmae's *The End of the Nation
  State* (1995).
- **Sceptic view:** states still control borders, taxes, armies and law, as shown by trade wars and
  border closures.
- **Transformationalist view:** states increasingly act through cooperation — for example the Kyoto
  Protocol (1997) and the Paris Agreement (2015) on climate change.
- Ulrich Beck (*Risk Society*, 1986; English 1992) argues that manufactured risks such as climate
  change cross borders, so national solutions alone cannot work.

## How do you plan a 35-mark Paper 4 essay on this?

1. Define the key term in the question and show awareness of debate about it.
2. Set out the view in the question with evidence (for example, globalist arguments).
3. Evaluate it with contrasting perspectives (sceptic, Marxist, feminist).
4. Apply examples from more than one society.
5. Conclude with a judgement — transformationalism often provides a balanced position.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between globalists and transformationalists?** Both accept that
globalisation is real; globalists see it sweeping away the nation state, while transformationalists
see states adapting and cooperating within it.

**Is glocalisation the same as hybridity?** They are related. Glocalisation usually describes global
products adapted locally; hybridity describes identities that blend cultures.

**Do I need to cover all six perspectives?** For a "who benefits" essay, aim to use at least three
contrasting perspectives in depth rather than listing all six.
