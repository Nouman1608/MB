## Sources

> **Source 1: Marrick Candles**
>
> Marrick Candles is opening a shop. Each candle sells for $14 and has a
> variable cost of $5. Fixed costs are $10,800 a month. The owner
> forecasts sales of 1,500 candles a month but admits she has no sales
> history for the new location. Her cash-flow forecast for the first
> three months starts with an opening balance of $2,000, with inflows of
> $18,000, $21,000 and $24,000 and outflows of $19,500, $22,000 and
> $21,500.
>
> **Source 2: Fellside Garden Centre**
>
> Fellside sets its budgets by adjusting last year's figures. Its spring
> quarter results were:
>
> | | Budget | Actual |
> |---|---|---|
> | Revenue | $120,000 | $131,000 |
> | Cost of plants | $48,000 | $54,500 |
> | Wages | $30,000 | $29,000 |
> | Other costs | $15,000 | $15,800 |
>
> An unusually warm spring brought more customers than expected. The
> owner worries that rising interest rates may reduce spending on homes
> and gardens later in the year, and a large chain is opening a store
> nearby.

## Section A

Questions 1 to 6 refer to Source 1.

**1.** Define the term "fixed costs". **[2]**

**2.** Calculate Marrick's monthly break-even output and its margin of safety at forecast sales. **[4]**

**3.** Calculate Marrick's monthly profit at forecast sales, and its profit if sales are 20% below forecast. **[4]**

**4.** Calculate the closing balance for each of the three months in Marrick's cash-flow forecast. **[4]**

**5.** Explain **one** way Marrick could deal with the cash shortfall shown in its forecast. **[4]**

**6.** Analyse **two** limitations of break-even analysis for Marrick Candles. **[6]**

## Section B

Questions 7 to 12 refer to Source 2.

**7.** Define the term "zero-based budget". **[2]**

**8.** Calculate the variance for each item and the profit variance, stating whether each is favourable or adverse. **[4]**

**9.** Explain why the adverse variance on the cost of plants may not be a sign of poor management. **[4]**

**10.** Analyse the difficulties Fellside may face in forecasting its sales for the rest of the year. **[6]**

**11.** Discuss whether Fellside should change from budgets based on historical figures to zero-based budgets. **[8]**

**12.** Assess the usefulness of budgeting to the owner of Fellside Garden Centre. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Costs that do not change with the level of output in the short run [1], such as rent or salaries, which must be paid even if nothing is sold [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Contribution = $14 − $5 = $9 [1]. Break-even = $10,800 ÷ $9 [1] = **1,200 candles** a month [1]. Margin of safety = 1,500 − 1,200 = **300 candles** [1]. **[4]**

**3.** At 1,500: total contribution = 1,500 × $9 = $13,500 [1], so profit = $13,500 − $10,800 = **$2,700** [1]. At 20% below forecast, sales = 1,200 [1], so contribution = $10,800 and profit = **$0**: the shop only breaks even [1]. **[4]**

**4.** Month 1: $2,000 + $18,000 − $19,500 = **$500** [1]. Month 2: $500 + $21,000 − $22,000 = **−$500** [1]. Month 3: −$500 + $24,000 − $21,500 = **$2,000** [1]. Each closing balance is carried forward as the next opening balance [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Marrick could arrange an overdraft with its bank before the shop opens [1]. The shortfall is only $500 and lasts one month [1], so a small, short-term overdraft covers it without the cost of a long-term loan [1], and the forecast shows the balance recovering to $2,000 in month 3, which helps persuade the bank to agree [1]. *(Also creditable: delay an outflow such as an equipment purchase; negotiate longer credit from suppliers.)* **[4]**

**6.** **Reliance on the sales forecast** [1]: the owner has no sales history for the new location [1], so the 1,500 figure and the 300-candle margin of safety may be far too optimistic, which would turn a forecast profit into a loss [1].
**Assumption of constant price and costs** [1]: break-even assumes every candle sells at $14 with a variable cost of $5 [1], but Marrick may need opening discounts or face rising wax prices, which would change contribution and the break-even point [1]. **[6]**

**7.** A budget that starts from zero for each period [1], with every item of spending having to be justified rather than based on last year's figures [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Revenue: $11,000 **favourable**; cost of plants: $6,500 **adverse** [1]. Wages: $1,000 **favourable**; other costs: $800 **adverse** [1]. Budgeted profit = $120,000 − $48,000 − $30,000 − $15,000 = $27,000; actual profit = $131,000 − $54,500 − $29,000 − $15,800 = $31,700 [1]. Profit variance = **$4,700 favourable** [1]. **[4]**

**9.** The warm spring brought more customers, so revenue was $11,000 above budget [1]. Selling more plants means buying more plants [1], so a higher cost of plants is a natural result of higher sales volume rather than wasteful buying [1]. Profit was still $4,700 above budget, which suggests the extra spending was justified [1]. **[4]**

**10.** **Weather and seasonality** [1]: the spring results were boosted by unusually warm weather [1], so using them as a basis for summer and autumn forecasts could overstate sales [1].
**Economic variables and competitors** [1]: rising interest rates may reduce spending on homes and gardens, but the owner cannot know how much or when [1]; and the new chain store could take customers, though its prices and range are not yet known, so any forecast of Fellside's share is uncertain [1]. **[6]**

**11.** **For zero-based:** budgets based on last year's figures did not anticipate the warm spring and may carry forward unnecessary costs [1]; justifying every item would force the owner to review spending before the chain store arrives [1]; this could free money to compete, for example on staff training or promotion [1].
**Against:** zero-based budgeting takes much more time [1], which a small garden centre's owner may not have in a busy season [1]; the historical approach is quick, and Fellside's profit was above budget, so its current costs may already be reasonable [1].
**Judgement:** Fellside could apply zero-based budgeting to one area at a time, such as other costs, rather than the whole business [1]; the decision depends on how much time the owner has and how much the chain store changes the market [1]. **[8]**

**12.** **Useful:** a budget sets targets so the owner can see whether the business is on track [1]; variance analysis showed profit $4,700 above plan and highlighted areas to investigate, such as other costs being $800 over [1]; budgets control spending by setting limits for each area [1]; and they help plan cash and staffing for busy seasons [1].
**Limitations:** the budget was based on last year's figures, so it did not reflect the warm spring [1]; variances can mislead, as the adverse plant variance shows [1]; budgets quickly become out of date if conditions change, such as interest rates rising or a rival opening [1]; and setting them takes time [1].
**Judgement:** budgeting is useful to Fellside as a control tool, provided the owner revises the budget when conditions change and interprets variances alongside sales volume [1]; its usefulness depends on the accuracy of the forecasts behind it, which the arrival of the chain store makes harder [1]. **[10]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing fixed costs by the selling price rather than by contribution.
- Forgetting that a 20% fall in sales can wipe out the whole margin of safety.
- Not carrying a negative closing balance into the next month.
- Labelling a cost variance "favourable" because actual is a bigger number.
- Judging a variance without linking it to what happened to sales volume.
- Recommending zero-based budgeting without considering the time it takes.
