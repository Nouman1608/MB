**This guide covers the named studies in 3.2.2 Social influence of OxfordAQA International GCSE
Psychology (9218), Version 1.1: "Studies of bystander behaviour: Piliavin's subway study, Latane and
Darley's smoke study and Levine's football supporter study."** The
[Conformity and Obedience study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-psychology-conformity-and-obedience/)
names these studies but leaves them for a separate resource. The factors they test (presence of
others, cost of helping, similarity to victim) are explained in the
[Factors Affecting Bystander Intervention study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-psychology-9218-bystander-intervention-factors/).

For each study, learn four things: the **aim**, the **method** (including the type of study), the
**results** and the **conclusion**. Then be ready to give at least one strength and one weakness.

## Latané and Darley's smoke study (1968)

**Aim.** To find out whether the presence of other people affects whether someone reports a possible
emergency.

**Method.** A **laboratory experiment**. Male students at Columbia University in New York were invited
to fill in a questionnaire about the problems of urban life. While they worked, white smoke began to
pour into the room through a small vent. The researchers watched through a one-way mirror to see
whether, and how quickly, participants left to report it. There were three conditions:

1. the participant **alone**;
2. the participant with **two confederates** who had been told to notice the smoke, shrug and carry
   on working;
3. **three naive participants** together.

**Results.**

| Condition | Reported the smoke |
|---|---|
| Alone | 75% of participants |
| With two passive confederates | 10% of participants |
| Three naive participants | In 38% of groups, at least one person reported it |

Participants who did not report the smoke later gave explanations such as steam or air-conditioning
vapour. Participants alone were also quicker to report it.

**Conclusion.** The presence of others, especially others who appear unconcerned, inhibits
intervention. Participants interpreted the smoke using the calm behaviour of the people around them:
**pluralistic ignorance**.

**Evaluation.**
- **Strength:** high control. Every participant met the same smoke, room and task, so the difference
  between conditions can be put down to the presence of others.
- **Weakness:** the sample was only male American university students, so the results may not
  generalise to women or other cultures.
- **Weakness:** a laboratory setting and an unusual event may not reflect real emergencies.
- **Ethical issue:** deception and possible distress; participants believed there might be a fire.

## Piliavin's subway study (1969)

**Aim.** To investigate how the type of victim (ill or drunk), the victim's race and the size of the
group of witnesses affect helping in a real emergency.

**Method.** A **field experiment** on New York's subway, using a stretch of the line where the train
did not stop for about seven and a half minutes. Teams of four students boarded the train: a victim,
a model and two female observers. About 70 seconds after the train pulled out, the victim
staggered and collapsed. In the **cane** condition the victim appeared sober and carried a cane; in
the **drunk** condition he smelled of alcohol and carried a bottle in a paper bag. Victims were black
or white. If no one helped, the model stepped in after a set time. Observers recorded who helped, how
quickly, and what was said. About 4,450 passengers took part, without knowing they were in a study,
across 103 trials.

**Results.**
- The cane victim received spontaneous help in **62 of 65** trials; the drunk victim in **19 of 38**.
- Help for the cane victim also came more quickly.
- Most first helpers were men.
- There was a slight tendency for same-race helping, mainly in the drunk condition.
- Larger groups of passengers did **not** help less, so there was no diffusion of responsibility.

**Conclusion.** People weigh up the costs and rewards of helping. Helping a drunk person carries
higher costs (he might be sick, aggressive or embarrassing), so he was helped less often. This led
Piliavin and colleagues to the **arousal: cost-reward model**.

**Evaluation.**
- **Strength:** high ecological validity. Real passengers responded to what they believed was a real
  emergency in an everyday setting.
- **Strength:** large sample of ordinary members of the public.
- **Weakness:** less control than a laboratory; passengers may have seen more than one trial, and
  the number of people in the carriage varied.
- **Ethical issues:** passengers could not give consent, could not withdraw and were not debriefed;
  some may have been distressed.
- **Note for exams:** the lack of a group-size effect challenges the idea that the presence of others
  always reduces helping. A likely reason is that the emergency was **unambiguous** and the victim
  was in full view.

## Levine's football supporter study (2005)

**Aim.** To test whether people are more likely to help a stranger who shares their **social identity**,
and whether a broader identity widens who they help.

**Method.** A **field experiment** with a laboratory start, at Lancaster University in England.
**Study 1:** Manchester United supporters first completed questionnaires about their support for
the team, to make that identity prominent. Each was then asked to walk to another building. On the
way, a jogger (a confederate) slipped, fell and held his ankle in apparent pain. The jogger wore a
Manchester United shirt, a Liverpool shirt (a rival team), or a plain shirt. Hidden observers
recorded whether the participant helped.

**Results.** In Study 1, the jogger in the Manchester United shirt was helped by over nine in ten
participants; in the Liverpool shirt or the plain shirt, he was helped by about a third or fewer.

**Study 2:** a second group of Manchester United supporters completed questionnaires about being a
**football fan** in general, rather than about their team. This time, the jogger was helped much more
often in **either** team's shirt than in the plain shirt.

**Conclusion.** Helping depends on whether the victim is seen as part of the bystander's group. The
group boundary is not fixed: making a broader identity (football fan) prominent extended help to a
rival team's supporter. This supports **similarity to the victim** as a factor.

**Evaluation.**
- **Strength:** a realistic incident with hidden observers, so participants behaved naturally.
- **Strength:** the two studies together show that identity can be changed, which has practical uses
  (encouraging a shared identity may increase helping).
- **Weakness:** small samples of male football supporters from one club, so the findings may not
  generalise.
- **Ethical issues:** deception (the fall was staged) and no opportunity to refuse to take part in the
  helping part of the study.

## Comparing the three studies

| | Latané and Darley | Piliavin | Levine |
|---|---|---|---|
| Method | Laboratory experiment | Field experiment | Field experiment (after lab questionnaire) |
| Main factor | Presence of others | Cost of helping (victim type) | Similarity to victim (identity) |
| Key finding | Alone 75% reported; with passive others 10% | Cane victim helped far more than drunk victim | Own-team shirt helped most; broad identity widened help |
| Supports | Pluralistic ignorance; bystander effect | Arousal: cost-reward model | Similarity/shared identity |
| Ecological validity | Lower | High | Fairly high |
| Control | High | Lower | Moderate |

## Worked example: describing a study for full marks

**Question.** Describe Piliavin's subway study. [4]

> Piliavin carried out a **field experiment** on the New York subway **[1]**. A confederate pretended to
> collapse in a carriage, either appearing ill and carrying a **cane** or appearing **drunk** **[1]**.
> Observers recorded how often and how quickly passengers helped **[1]**. The cane victim was helped
> in almost every trial, but the drunk victim in only about half, showing that people consider the
> **cost of helping** **[1]**.

The answer gives method, the manipulation, what was measured and the main result with its meaning, in
four sentences.

## Common mistakes

- **Swapping the findings.** The 75%/10% figures belong to the smoke study; the cane/drunk comparison
  to the subway study.
- **Saying Piliavin found diffusion of responsibility.** He found it did **not** occur.
- **Calling the subway study a laboratory experiment.** It was a field experiment.
- **Describing Levine's study without the shirt manipulation.** The shirts are the independent
  variable.
- **Evaluating with "it was unethical" alone.** Name the issue (consent, deception, debriefing) and
  explain it in context.

## Exam technique

- **Describe:** method, procedure, results and conclusion, in that order.
- **Evaluate:** strengths and weaknesses, each explained and linked to the study.
- **Explain what a study shows:** link the result to a named factor or explanation.
