Who commits crime, why, and what should societies do about it? Key questions 6.2 and 6.3 of
Cambridge O Level Sociology (2251) cover the patterns of crime and victimisation by age, class,
gender and ethnicity, the explanations sociologists offer for them, five theoretical perspectives,
the aims and effectiveness of different punishments, and how effective policing strategies are.
Examiners reward answers that question official statistics and compare explanations rather than
listing them.

Key question 6.1 (definitions, agencies of control and measuring crime) is covered in the
[O Level family, education and crime guide](/resources/o-level-sociology-family-education-crime/).

## Syllabus coverage

Cambridge O Level Sociology 2251 (2025–2027):

- **6.2 What are the patterns of and explanations for crime and deviance?** — 6.2.1 patterns of
  crime and victimisation (age, social class, gender, ethnicity); 6.2.2 explanations based on age;
  6.2.3 on ethnicity; 6.2.4 on gender; 6.2.5 on social class; 6.2.6 sociological theories
  (postmodernism; interactionism, including labelling and moral panic theory; functionalism,
  including the New Right, subculturalism and strain theory; Marxism; feminism); 6.2.7 strengths and
  limitations of the explanations.
- **6.3 How is crime dealt with in different societies?** — 6.3.1 aims of punishment; 6.3.2
  effectiveness of types of punishment; 6.3.3 effectiveness of policing and law enforcement.

## What patterns does recorded crime show?

| Characteristic | Offending pattern | Victimisation pattern |
|---|---|---|
| **Age** | Highest among teenagers and young adults | Young people, especially young men, are often victims of violence |
| **Social class** | Working-class people over-represented in convictions | Poorer areas often suffer more burglary and violence |
| **Gender** | Men commit most recorded crime, especially violence | Women are more often victims of domestic and sexual violence |
| **Ethnicity** | Some minority groups over-represented in arrests and prisons in some societies | Minorities may suffer hate crime |

Remember that these are mainly **recorded** patterns; they reflect reporting and policing as well as
offending.

## Why do young people appear more criminal? (6.2.2)

Police targeting and stop and search in public spaces; stereotyping of youth and a resulting
self-fulfilling prophecy; gang culture offering status and protection; weaker social control as
young people move away from family supervision; lack of opportunity through unemployment; status
frustration — Albert Cohen (*Delinquent Boys*, 1955).

## How do sociologists explain ethnic differences? (6.2.3)

Institutional racism in policing and courts; prejudice and discrimination; police targeting and stop
and search; material and relative deprivation — John Lea and Jock Young (*What Is to Be Done About
Law and Order?*, 1984) argued that relative deprivation and marginalisation help explain street
crime; stereotyping — Paul Gilroy (1982) argued the idea of Black criminality was a myth created by
racist stereotypes; gang culture and status frustration.

## Why do men commit more crime than women? (6.2.4)

- **Chivalry thesis** — Otto Pollak (*The Criminality of Women*, 1950) argued male police and judges
  treat women leniently, hiding female crime.
- **Culture of masculinity** — crime can be a way to "accomplish" toughness and dominance; Walter
  Miller (1958) described lower-class "focal concerns" such as toughness and excitement.
- **Socialisation and social control** — girls are more closely supervised at home and in public.
- **Misogyny** — hostility to women helps explain male violence against women.
- **Stereotyping**, **gang culture** and **lack of opportunity** also shape gender patterns.
- Pat Carlen (*Women, Crime and Poverty*, 1988) found that women who offended often did so when the
  rewards of conformity — a decent income and a supportive family — had failed them.

## How does social class affect crime? (6.2.5)

Social exclusion; material and relative deprivation; inadequate socialisation (a New Right claim);
lack of opportunity; status frustration; **power inequalities** — Steven Box (*Power, Crime and
Mystification*, 1983) argued crimes of the powerful are more harmful but less punished; police
targeting of poorer neighbourhoods.

## What do the main theories say? (6.2.6)

| Theory | Core idea | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Functionalism — strain** | A gap between goals (wealth) and legitimate means produces deviance | Merton (1938): conformity, innovation, ritualism, retreatism, rebellion |
| **Functionalism — subculturalism** | Groups develop alternative values rewarding deviance | A. Cohen (1955); Miller (1958) |
| **Functionalism — New Right** | An underclass with inadequate socialisation and weak families | Charles Murray |
| **Interactionism — labelling** | Deviance is behaviour that is labelled; labels can create a deviant career | Howard Becker, *Outsiders* (1963) |
| **Interactionism — moral panic** | Media exaggeration creates folk devils and tougher control | Stanley Cohen (1972) |
| **Marxism** | Law serves the ruling class; capitalism breeds greed; crimes of the powerful ignored | Box (1983) |
| **Feminism** | Patriarchal control explains low female crime and male violence against women | Carlen (1988); Frances Heidensohn (1985) |
| **Postmodernism** | Crime is varied, individual and linked to consumer culture, pleasure and identity | Jack Katz, *Seductions of Crime* (1988), on the thrill of offending |

## How strong are these explanations? (6.2.7)

- Explanations based on official statistics may confuse **who gets caught** with **who offends**.
- Strain and subcultural theories explain property crime but not middle-class or corporate crime.
- Labelling explains the effect of reactions but not why people commit the first deviant act.
- Marxism highlights power but can romanticise working-class offenders and neglect their victims.
- Feminism explains gender difference but may treat women as one group.
- Postmodernism captures new crimes but offers no single cause, making policy hard.

## What are the aims of punishment? (6.3.1)

**Protection of citizens**; **deterrence** (individual and general); **retribution** (deserved
punishment); **incapacitation** (removing the ability to offend); **rehabilitation** (reforming the
offender).

## How effective are different punishments? (6.3.2)

| Punishment | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| **Prison** | Incapacitates; seen as just | High reoffending; costly; can spread criminal skills |
| **Rehabilitation programmes** | Address causes such as addiction or lack of skills | Need resources; public may see them as soft |
| **Community sentencing** | Cheaper; offenders repay society | Weaker deterrent effect |
| **Financial penalties** | Simple to apply | Hit the poor harder than the rich |
| **Capital punishment** | Retribution; prevents reoffending | Irreversible miscarriages of justice; little clear evidence it deters more than long prison sentences |
| **Corporal punishment** | Some states (e.g. Singapore, which uses caning) claim deterrent effect | Widely criticised as a human rights abuse |
| **Exile and ostracism** | Removes or shames offenders | Does not reform; may push people into further deviance |
| **Non-custodial** (electronic tagging, curfews, restraining orders), **probation**, **police cautions** | Keep offenders in work and family; cheaper | Depend on monitoring; breaches common |
| **Social media and vigilante groups** | Public pressure | No due process; risk of targeting innocent people |

Norway's emphasis on rehabilitation — for example Halden prison, opened in 2010 — is often cited as
evidence that humane regimes can lower reoffending.

## How effective is policing? (6.3.3)

- **Targeting and stop and search** can detect weapons and drugs but may damage trust if certain
  groups are targeted unfairly.
- **Surveillance**, including digital surveillance (CCTV, phone data, facial recognition), helps
  detection but raises privacy concerns and may displace crime elsewhere.
- **Crime prevention** — street lighting, design of housing, youth programmes.
- **Community policing** builds trust and local intelligence; **military-style policing** uses force
  and may restore order quickly but can alienate communities.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which theory best explains crime?** None explains all crime. Strain and subcultural theories suit
property crime by young working-class men, Marxism suits corporate crime, feminism suits gender
patterns, and labelling explains how reactions shape criminal careers.

**Do harsher punishments reduce crime?** Evidence suggests the certainty of being caught matters more
than severity of punishment.

**Why question official statistics?** Because much crime is unreported or unrecorded, and policing
decisions shape who appears in the figures.
