**Syllabus:** Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027, **A Level**
subtopic 23.2: define ΔH<sub>hyd</sub> and ΔH<sub>sol</sub>; construct and use the energy
cycle linking ΔH<sub>sol</sub>, lattice energy and ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>; calculate with it;
explain the effect of ionic charge and radius on ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition | Example equation | Sign |
|---|---|---|---|
| Enthalpy change of hydration, ΔH<sub>hyd</sub> | enthalpy change when one mole of gaseous ions dissolves in sufficient water to give an infinitely dilute solution | Ca²⁺(g) → Ca²⁺(aq) | always negative |
| Enthalpy change of solution, ΔH<sub>sol</sub> | enthalpy change when one mole of an ionic solid dissolves in sufficient water to give an infinitely dilute solution | CaCl₂(s) → Ca²⁺(aq) + 2Cl⁻(aq) | either sign |
| Lattice energy, ΔH<sub>latt</sub> (from 23.1) | enthalpy change when one mole of an ionic solid is formed from its gaseous ions | Ca²⁺(g) + 2Cl⁻(g) → CaCl₂(s) | always negative |
| Infinitely dilute | so dilute that adding more water gives no further enthalpy change | — | — |
| Ion–dipole attraction | attraction between an ion and the partial charges on polar water molecules | — | — |
| Charge density | an ion's charge relative to its size; high for small, highly charged ions | — | — |

## The cycle in one line

    ΔHsol = −ΔHlatt + ΣΔHhyd(all ions in the formula)

- Route: solid → gaseous ions (reverse of lattice energy, endothermic) → aqueous ions
  (hydration, exothermic).
- Multiply each hydration term by the number of that ion in the formula.
- Rearrangements: ΔH<sub>latt</sub> = ΣΔH<sub>hyd</sub> − ΔH<sub>sol</sub>, and
  ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(X) = ΔH<sub>sol</sub> + ΔH<sub>latt</sub> − ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(other ions).

**Drawing it:** gaseous ions at the top; solid on one side, aqueous ions on the other.
Lattice-energy arrow points **down to the solid**; hydration arrow points **down to the
aqueous ions**; ΔH<sub>sol</sub> runs from solid to aqueous ions. Label every species with
state symbols.

## Quick calculations

*Data supplied for practice; values vary slightly between data sources.*

**NaBr:** ΔH<sub>latt</sub> = −747, ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Na⁺) = −406,
ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Br⁻) = −335 kJ mol⁻¹.

    ΔHsol = +747 − 406 − 335 = +6 kJ mol⁻¹

**CaCl₂:** ΔH<sub>latt</sub> = −2258, ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Ca²⁺) = −1650,
ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Cl⁻) = −364 kJ mol⁻¹.

    ΔHsol = +2258 − 1650 − 2(364) = +2258 − 2378 = −120 kJ mol⁻¹

**AgCl:** ΔH<sub>latt</sub> = −905, ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Ag⁺) = −464,
ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Cl⁻) = −364 kJ mol⁻¹.

    ΔHsol = +905 − 464 − 364 = +77 kJ mol⁻¹

A strongly endothermic ΔH<sub>sol</sub>, as for AgCl, fits with a sparingly soluble salt,
but solubility also depends on entropy (23.3) and ΔG (23.4).

## Why hydration is exothermic

- Water is polar: O is δ⁻, H is δ⁺.
- Around a **cation**, water molecules point their **oxygen** atoms towards the ion.
- Around an **anion**, water molecules point their **hydrogen** atoms towards the ion.
- New ion–dipole attractions form and nothing is broken, so energy is released.

## Charge and radius effects on ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>

| Change | Effect on attraction to water | ΔH<sub>hyd</sub> becomes |
|---|---|---|
| Greater ionic charge | stronger attraction to each water dipole | more exothermic |
| Smaller ionic radius | water dipoles get closer to the ion's centre | more exothermic |
| Larger ionic radius | dipoles held further away | less exothermic |

Order of magnitude to recognise (more exothermic first):

- Al³⁺ > Mg²⁺ > Na⁺ (charge rises and radius falls across Period 3)
- Mg²⁺ > Ca²⁺ > Sr²⁺ > Ba²⁺ (radius rises down Group 2)
- Li⁺ > Na⁺ > K⁺ (radius rises down Group 1)
- F⁻ > Cl⁻ > Br⁻ > I⁻ (radius rises down Group 17)

**Model two-mark explanation:** "Li⁺ has a smaller ionic radius than K⁺ (same charge), so it
attracts the δ⁻ oxygen atoms of water molecules more strongly, releasing more energy."

## Linking to solubility trends

- Lattice energy and ΔH<sub>hyd</sub> both become more exothermic for smaller, more highly
  charged ions, so they partly cancel in ΔH<sub>sol</sub>.
- To explain a change in ΔH<sub>sol</sub> down a group, compare **which term changes more**.
- This is the method used for Group 2 hydroxides and sulfates in Topic 27.

## Exam traps

- ΔH<sub>hyd</sub> equations start from **(g)**; ΔH<sub>sol</sub> equations start from **(s)**.
- "One mole of **ions**" for hydration, "one mole of **compound**" for solution.
- Reverse the sign of lattice energy in the cycle; never add it unchanged.
- Double (or triple) hydration terms for ions appearing more than once.
- Hydration enthalpy is never positive; a positive answer means a sign error.
- Write "ionic radius", not "atomic radius" or "size of the atom".
- Do not say an endothermic ΔH<sub>sol</sub> makes a salt insoluble.
- Keep units: kJ mol⁻¹.

## Self-test

1. Write an equation, with state symbols, for the enthalpy change of hydration of the
   sulfate ion.
2. Write an equation, with state symbols, for the enthalpy change of solution of potassium
   sulfate.
3. Explain why every enthalpy change of hydration is negative.
4. State the equation linking ΔH<sub>sol</sub>, ΔH<sub>latt</sub> and ΔH<sub>hyd</sub> for
   a compound MX₂.
5. *(Data supplied.)* ΔH<sub>latt</sub>(KBr) = −679, ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(K⁺) = −322 and
   ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Br⁻) = −335 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate ΔH<sub>sol</sub>(KBr).
6. *(Data supplied.)* ΔH<sub>sol</sub>(LiBr) = −49 kJ mol⁻¹, ΔH<sub>latt</sub>(LiBr) =
   −807 kJ mol⁻¹, ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Br⁻) = −335 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Li⁺).
7. Which way do water molecules face a chloride ion?
8. Put Na⁺, Mg²⁺ and Al³⁺ in order of increasingly exothermic ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>.
9. Explain why ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(F⁻) is more exothermic than ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(I⁻).
10. *(Data supplied.)* ΔH<sub>sol</sub>(CsI) = +33 kJ mol⁻¹, ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Cs⁺) =
    −276 kJ mol⁻¹, ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(I⁻) = −293 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate ΔH<sub>latt</sub>(CsI).
11. Why are ΔH<sub>sol</sub> values for many 1:1 salts close to zero?
12. What does "infinitely dilute" mean in these definitions?

**Answers**

1. SO₄²⁻(g) → SO₄²⁻(aq).
2. K₂SO₄(s) → 2K⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq).
3. Water molecules are attracted to the ion by ion–dipole attractions; these form without any
   bonds breaking, so energy is released.
4. ΔH<sub>sol</sub> = −ΔH<sub>latt</sub> + ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(M²⁺) + 2ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(X⁻).
5. +679 − 322 − 335 = **+22 kJ mol⁻¹**.
6. −49 = +807 + ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Li⁺) − 335, so ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Li⁺) = **−521 kJ mol⁻¹**.
7. With their δ⁺ hydrogen atoms towards the ion.
8. Na⁺ < Mg²⁺ < Al³⁺.
9. Same charge, but F⁻ has a much smaller ionic radius, so water dipoles approach more
   closely and are attracted more strongly, releasing more energy.
10. ΔH<sub>latt</sub> = ΣΔH<sub>hyd</sub> − ΔH<sub>sol</sub> = (−276 − 293) − 33 =
    **−602 kJ mol⁻¹**.
11. Lattice energy and the sum of hydration enthalpies are both large and similar in size, so
    their difference is small.
12. So dilute that adding more water produces no further enthalpy change; the ions no longer
    interact with each other.
