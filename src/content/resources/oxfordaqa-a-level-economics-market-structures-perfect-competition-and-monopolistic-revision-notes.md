## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Market structure | Features of a market that shape firm behaviour: seller numbers, product differences, entry conditions |
| Price taker | A firm with no power over price; it accepts the market price |
| Normal profit | The minimum profit that keeps the owner in the market; TR = TC including this |
| Supernormal profit | Profit above normal; AR is above AC |
| Shutdown point | Price equal to minimum AVC in the short run |
| Allocative efficiency | P = MC |
| Productive efficiency | Output at minimum AC |
| Excess capacity | Output below the minimum-AC output |
| Non-price competition | Competing through brand, quality, design, advertising, service or location rather than a lower price |

## Spectrum of market structures (3.3.3.1)

| | Perfect competition | Monopolistic competition | Oligopoly | Monopoly |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sellers | Huge number | Large number | A few large | One |
| Product | Identical | Differentiated | Either | Unique |
| Entry | Free | Easy | Hard | Blocked |
| Price power | None | Some | Considerable | Greatest |

Use the same three tests every time: how many sellers, how different the products are, how easy entry is. Say "closest to", because real markets are not pure types.

## Perfect competition (3.3.3.2)

**Characteristics:** a large number of buyers and sellers; identical products; free entry and exit; perfect knowledge; price takers.

**Firm's demand:** horizontal at the market price, so AR = MR = P.

**Method in steps (short run)**

1. Draw the price line, MC (rising) and AC.
2. Find output where MC = P.
3. Read AC at that output.
4. Compare: P above AC means supernormal profit; P equal to AC means normal profit; P below AC means a loss.
5. If there is a loss, compare P with AVC. P at least AVC: stay open. P below AVC: shut down.
6. Long run: supernormal profit brings entry, supply shifts right, price falls to minimum AC. Losses bring exit and a rise in price.

**Worked reminder (Farm G, invented).** Fixed cost is 30. Variable cost for 1 to 6 units is 18, 32, 44, 60, 80 and 108, so MC is 18, 14, 12, 16, 20 and 28, and minimum AVC is 14.67 at 3 units.

| Price | Output (MC at most P) | TR | TC | Profit | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 22 | 5 | 110 | 110 | 0 | Normal profit; price = AC |
| 17 | 4 | 68 | 90 | −22 | Stay: 17 is above AVC of 15, and the loss is under the fixed cost of 30 |
| 14 | 3 | 42 | 74 | −32 | Shut: 14 is below minimum AVC; closing loses only 30 |

**Long-run market calculation.** Demand is Qd = 800 − 10P, the long-run price is 24 and each firm produces 8. Market quantity = 800 − 240 = 560, so the number of firms is 560 ÷ 8 = **70**.

**Efficiency proposition:** given its assumptions, including no externalities, perfect competition gives P = MC (allocative efficiency) and output at minimum AC in the long run (productive efficiency).

**Yardstick:** compare real markets with it to see how far price exceeds MC and AC exceeds its minimum. The same applies to labour markets.

**Labour market reminder.** A wage taker hires while MRP is at least the wage. Wage 120, price 8: a worker adding 16 units has MRP = 16 × 8 = 128, so hire. The next adds 14 units, MRP = 112, so stop.

**Assessing the proposition**

- Externalities mean P = MC can still misallocate.
- Large minimum efficient scale means many tiny firms cannot reach minimum AC.
- No long-run supernormal profit may limit research and development, and identical products give no variety.
- Perfect knowledge and free entry are rarely met.
- Efficiency says nothing about fairness in the distribution of income.
- Judgement: a useful benchmark, but not a description of real markets.

## Monopolistic competition (3.3.3.3)

**Characteristics:** a large number of firms; differentiated products; low barriers; some price power; downward-sloping, fairly elastic demand; non-price competition.

**Method in steps**

1. Draw AR (demand) and MR below it, with MC and U-shaped AC.
2. Output is where MR = MC. Read price from AR above that output.
3. Short run: price above AC means supernormal profit.
4. Entry shifts each firm's demand left and makes it flatter, until AR touches AC (normal profit).
5. Long run: AR is tangent to AC to the left of the AC minimum, so there is **excess capacity** and price is above MC.

**Worked reminder (Shop S, invented).** TC = 50 + 3q + 0.02q², so MC = 3 + 0.04q and minimum AC is at q = 50, where AC = 5.

| | Short run | Long run |
|---|---|---|
| Demand | P = 9 − 0.08q | P = 7 − 0.06q |
| MR = MC | 9 − 0.16q = 3 + 0.04q | 7 − 0.12q = 3 + 0.04q |
| Output | 30 | 25 |
| Price | 6.60 | 5.50 |
| TR and TC | 198 and 158 | 137.5 and 137.5 |
| Profit | 40 | 0 |

In the long run, AC at 25 = 2 + 3 + 0.5 = 5.50, which equals price. Excess capacity = 50 − 25 = 25, which is half of the minimum-AC output. The mark-up is 5.50 − 4.00 = 1.50.

**Elasticity link.** Long-run demand is more elastic than short-run demand. At the short-run point, PED = (1 ÷ 0.08) × (6.60 ÷ 30) = −2.75. At the long-run point, PED = (1 ÷ 0.06) × (5.50 ÷ 25) = −3.67.

**Non-price competition**

- Strengthens monopoly power: brand loyalty, less elastic demand, higher barriers to entry.
- Can also make markets more competitive: variety, quality and information for buyers.
- Costs matter: advertising adds to fixed cost, and if rivals copy it the benefit can vanish.

**Worked reminder.** Shop S spends 20 on advertising, so TC = 70 + 3q + 0.02q². Demand shifts to P = 7.8 − 0.06q, so MR = 7.8 − 0.12q.

- MR = MC: 7.8 − 0.12q = 3 + 0.04q, so q = 30 and price = 6.00.
- TR = 180, TC = 70 + 90 + 18 = 178, profit = **2**. Without advertising, profit was zero.
- If rivals copy and demand returns to P = 7 − 0.06q, output is 25 and price 5.50. TR = 137.5 but TC = 70 + 75 + 12.5 = 157.5, a **loss of 20**.

## Diagram checklist

- Label both axes: price or cost on the vertical axis, output on the horizontal axis.
- Perfect competition: horizontal AR = MR, rising MC, U-shaped AC, with MC cutting AC at its lowest point.
- Monopolistic competition: downward-sloping AR, MR below it, and MR = MC fixing output.
- Mark the profit-maximising output, the price, and the AC at that output.
- Shade supernormal profit or loss as a rectangle between price and AC.
- Show the long-run position as a second diagram, or a shifted curve with an arrow, and state why the curve moved.

## Must-know distinctions

| Idea | Be clear that |
|---|---|
| Perfect competition and monopolistic competition | Both have normal profit in the long run, but only perfect competition reaches P = MC and minimum AC |
| Price taker and price maker | A price taker faces horizontal demand; a monopolistically competitive firm faces a sloping curve |
| Shutdown and loss-making | A loss does not mean closure; compare price with AVC |
| Normal and supernormal profit | Normal profit is part of costs |
| Efficiency in theory and in practice | Efficiency depends on the assumptions |

## Quick self-test

1. A perfectly competitive firm sells 80 units at 12. State its MR and TR.
2. Name the three features that separate market structures.
3. A perfectly competitive market has long-run price 24. Demand is Qd = 800 − 10P and each firm makes 8 units. How many firms are there?
4. Farm G (above) faces a price of 14. Should it produce? Give the loss each way.
5. Why does supernormal profit disappear in the long run in monopolistic competition?
6. Shop S has long-run price 5.50 and MC of 4.00. Is it allocatively efficient?
7. Calculate Shop S's excess capacity as a percentage of the minimum-AC output.
8. Give one way non-price competition strengthens a firm's market power and one way it benefits consumers.
9. State one assumption of perfect competition that, if it fails, weakens the efficiency conclusion.
10. Shop S's short-run price falls from 6.60 to 5.50 in the long run. Calculate the percentage change.

### Answers

1. MR = **12** and TR = 80 × 12 = **960**.
2. **Number of firms, product differentiation, ease of entry.**
3. Qd = 800 − 240 = 560; 560 ÷ 8 = **70 firms**.
4. No: price is below minimum AVC (14.67). Producing 3 gives 42 − 74 = −32; closing gives **−30**, so **shut down**.
5. **Entry** by rivals shifts each firm's demand left and makes it more elastic, until AR just touches AC.
6. **No**, because price is above MC (5.50 against 4.00).
7. (50 − 25) ÷ 50 = **50%**.
8. **Brand loyalty** makes demand less elastic. **Better quality or information** helps buyers.
9. Examples: **no externalities**, perfect knowledge, or free entry.
10. (5.50 − 6.60) ÷ 6.60 × 100 = **−16.7%**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Drawing a sloping demand curve for a perfectly competitive firm.
- Shutting down at any loss, instead of comparing price with AVC.
- Using MR = AR in monopolistic competition; MR lies below AR.
- Missing excess capacity in the long-run monopolistic competition diagram.
- Giving the efficiency proposition without its assumptions.
- Calling normal profit "zero profit" without saying costs include it.
- Working out a percentage change against the new figure rather than the starting one.
- Stating that non-price competition is good for consumers, or bad, without a reason either way.
