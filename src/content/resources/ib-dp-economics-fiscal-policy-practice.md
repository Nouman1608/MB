## Questions

**1.** Distinguish between direct and indirect taxation, giving one example of each. **[3]**

**2.** The fictional country Tarnia reports these figures, in billions of tarns: income tax 180, corporate profits tax 70, sales tax 130, receipts from state-owned enterprises 15, sale of a government asset 25. Current expenditure is 265, capital expenditure 60 and transfer payments 95.

**(a)** Calculate total revenue, total expenditure and the budget balance. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the balance if the asset sale is excluded, and comment on what this shows. **[2]**

**3.** Using an AD/AS diagram of the Keynesian view, explain how expansionary fiscal policy can close a deflationary (recessionary) gap. **[5]**

**4.** Using a diagram of the monetarist/new classical view, explain the long-run effect of expansionary fiscal policy. **[5]**

**5.** (HL only) An economy has MPC = 0.5 and no taxes or imports.

**(a)** Find the Keynesian multiplier. **[1]**
**(b)** Investment rises by 14. Find the effect on GDP. **[1]**
**(c)** Government spending falls by 5. Find the effect on GDP. **[1]**

**6.** (HL only) In the fictional country Garland, MPS = 0.2, MPT = 0.3 and MPM = 0.3.

**(a)** Find the value of the multiplier. **[2]**
**(b)** Exports rise by 40. Find the resulting rise in GDP. **[2]**
**(c)** Garland has a deflationary gap of 100. Calculate the rise in government spending that would close it. **[2]**

**7.** Explain two constraints on the effectiveness of fiscal policy other than crowding out. **[4]**

**8.** (HL only) In the fictional country Corvia the multiplier is 2. The government raises spending by 30 and borrows all of it. Private investment falls by 12 as a result.

**(a)** Calculate the net injection and the change in GDP. **[2]**
**(b)** Using AD and AS curves, explain why investment falls and how the result differs from a case without crowding out. **[3]**

**9.** (HL only) In Corvia, income tax is 5% on the first 80 of a household's income and 25% on income above 80. A household's income falls from 150 to 110.

**(a)** Calculate the tax paid at each income. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the change in disposable income. **[1]**
**(c)** Show that a flat 10% tax would have caused a larger fall in disposable income, and state what this illustrates. **[2]**

**10.** Evaluate the use of fiscal policy to reduce unemployment in an economy in a deep recession. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Direct tax: levied on and borne by the person or firm paying it, for example income tax [1]. Indirect tax: collected from the seller and passed on, at least partly, through prices, for example sales tax [1]. Contrast: the legal payer and the person bearing the burden are the same for direct tax but differ for indirect tax [1].
*Examiner insight:* "Distinguish" asks for the difference itself, so a definition of each with no stated contrast is only part of the answer.

**2. (a)** Revenue = 180 + 70 + 130 + 15 + 25 = 420 [1]. Expenditure = 265 + 60 + 95 = 420 [1]. Balance = 420 − 420 = **0** (a balanced budget) [1].
**(b)** Excluding 25: revenue 395, so balance = 395 − 420 = **−25** [1]. The balance is only reached by a one-off sale, so there is an underlying deficit that may not be sustainable [1].
*Examiner insight:* "Calculate" asks for working to be shown, so write each sum out rather than only the totals.

**3.** Draw price level against real GDP, with Keynesian AS flat, then rising, then vertical at potential output [1]. Equilibrium at AD1 is at a level of output below potential, which is a deflationary gap [1]. Higher government spending or lower taxes shifts AD right to AD2 [1]. Output rises towards potential output, and unemployment falls [1]. Because AS is flat or shallow there, the price level rises little [1].
*Examiner insight:* A diagram with unlabelled axes or equilibria usually cannot support the explanation, so label every curve and both equilibria.

**4.** Draw vertical LRAS at potential output, SRAS, and AD1 meeting both at long-run equilibrium [1]. Expansionary policy shifts AD right to AD2 [1]. In the short run output rises above potential along SRAS [1]. Wages and costs rise, so SRAS shifts left [1]. The long-run equilibrium returns to potential output at a higher price level, so the policy changes prices but not output [1].
*Examiner insight:* The key skill is separating short-run from long-run, so state clearly which equilibrium each sentence refers to.

**5. (a)** Multiplier = 1 / (1 − 0.5) = 1 / 0.5 = **2** [1].
**(b)** 2 × 14 = **28** rise [1].
**(c)** 2 × (−5) = **−10**, a fall of 10 [1].
*Examiner insight:* Part (c) is only complete if the direction of the change is clear, so state a fall.

**6. (a)** Leakages = 0.2 + 0.3 + 0.3 = 0.8 [1]. Multiplier = 1 / 0.8 = **1.25** [1].
**(b)** 1.25 × 40 = **50** [1]; written as a rise in GDP [1].
**(c)** Required injection = 100 / 1.25 = **80** [1]. Method: gap divided by the multiplier [1].
*Examiner insight:* Using 1 / MPS = 5 here ignores two of the three leakages and gives a wrong answer from the start.

**7.** Political pressure: tax rises and spending cuts are unpopular, so a government may delay or avoid the policy that the economy needs [1]. This limits its use for contraction in a boom, for example [1]. Time lags: recognising a problem, passing the policy and spending the money take time [1]. The policy may take effect when conditions have changed, such as in a recovery, and then push up inflation [1]. (Sustainable debt is an equally valid second constraint.)
*Examiner insight:* A named constraint alone earns little; the credit comes from explaining how it limits the policy.

**8. (a)** Net injection = 30 − 12 = **18** [1]. Change in GDP = 18 × 2 = **36** [1].
**(b)** Government borrowing competes for funds and raises interest rates, which makes investment less attractive [1]. AD first shifts right by the government spending effect, then shifts back part of the way as investment falls [1]. Without crowding out GDP would rise by 30 × 2 = 60, so the policy is weaker by 24 [1].
*Examiner insight:* A crowding-out answer should link borrowing to interest rates to investment, because skipping a link leaves the argument incomplete.

**9. (a)** Tax at 150 = 0.05 × 80 + 0.25 × 70 = 4 + 17.5 = 21.5 [1]. Tax at 110 = 4 + 0.25 × 30 = 4 + 7.5 = 11.5 [1].
**(b)** Disposable income: 150 − 21.5 = 128.5 and 110 − 11.5 = 98.5, so it falls by **30** [1].
**(c)** Flat 10%: disposable income 135 and 99, a fall of 36, which is more than 30 [1]. This illustrates an automatic stabiliser: a progressive tax cushions the fall in spending power without any new government decision [1].
*Examiner insight:* "Show that" needs the intermediate values for both taxes, since the conclusion depends on comparing them.

**10.** Indicative points, one for each. This is a plan; real papers are marked against the IB markbands, not point by point.
Expansionary policy shifts AD right and raises output and employment [1]. In a deep recession private spending is weak, so government spending adds demand directly [1]. Spending can be targeted at sectors or regions with high unemployment [1]. Extra spending is spent again in further rounds, raising the effect (HL only) [1]. Time lags may delay the effect past the recession [1]. Borrowing raises debt and interest costs, which may limit sustainable action [1]. Political pressure may favour popular tax cuts over better-targeted spending [1]. Crowding out may weaken the effect (HL only) [1]. Fiscal policy mainly helps cyclical unemployment; the monetarist/new classical view sees long-run effects on prices, and skills mismatches need supply-side policy [1]. A justified overall judgment, such as that fiscal policy is most effective when the recession is deep and debt is manageable [1].
*Examiner insight:* An evaluation answer does better with a conclusion that weighs the points against this economy than with a longer list of unconnected points.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing totals without showing the sums in "calculate" questions.
- Giving the budget balance without saying whether it is a surplus or deficit.
- Using the simple multiplier when MPT and MPM are given.
- Forgetting to multiply the multiplier by the injection.
- Diagrams without axes, curve names or labelled equilibria.
- Mixing the Keynesian and monetarist/new classical models in one explanation.
- Naming a constraint without explaining how it limits policy.
- Ending an evaluation with no overall judgment.
