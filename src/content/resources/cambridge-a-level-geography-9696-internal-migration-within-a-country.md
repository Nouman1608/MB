This guide covers section 5.2 of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026. The syllabus lists three strands: "Rural–urban and urban–rural movements: their causes and impacts on source areas and receiving/destination areas including population structures"; "Stepped migration within the settlement hierarchy and urban–urban movements"; and "Causes and impacts of intra-urban movements (within urban settlements)". It sits in Topic 5, Migration, in Paper 2 Core Human Geography.

The [5.2 revision notes](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-internal-migration-revision-notes/) summarise the flows and include an example from China. This guide adds the explanations behind them, examples from other countries, and a worked answer. When you have finished, try the [5.2 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-internal-migration-within-a-country-practice/).

## The four kinds of internal movement

| Movement | Typical direction | Most common context |
|---|---|---|
| Rural-urban | Countryside to towns and cities | LICs and MICs with rapid urbanisation |
| Urban-rural | City to small towns and villages | HICs (counterurbanisation); also return migration in crises |
| Urban-urban | Between towns and cities, often up the hierarchy | All income groups, especially skilled workers |
| Intra-urban | Between neighbourhoods of the same city | All cities |

The syllabus asks for causes and impacts for each, and impacts on population structure specifically for rural-urban and urban-rural flows.

## Rural-urban migration

### Causes

**Rural push factors** are usually structural: population growth on a fixed area of land, so farms are divided into plots too small to support a family; low and uncertain farm incomes; mechanisation, which reduces the need for labour; debt; and poor schools and clinics. Environmental stress, such as drought or flooding, adds to these.

**Urban pull factors** include wage jobs in factories, construction and services, a large informal sector where newcomers can earn something at once, better education and health care, and the perceived excitement and freedom of city life.

**Why migration continues when cities have high unemployment.** The Todaro model (1969), later extended by Harris and Todaro (1970), explains this. Migrants compare *expected* urban income, the urban wage multiplied by the probability of finding a job, with rural income. If urban wages are far higher, moving is rational even when the chance of a formal job is modest, because the gain if successful is large. The model explains why creating urban jobs can increase migration: each new job raises the expected income of everyone still in the countryside.

### Impacts on source areas

- **Population structure:** loss of young adults leaves an hourglass-shaped pyramid with a narrow 20-39 band. Where men leave, women head many households. Fewer births follow, and the area ages.
- **Economy:** remittances pay for school fees, housing and farm inputs. But labour shortages at planting and harvest can reduce output, and land may be left uncultivated.
- **Services:** a shrinking, ageing population can no longer support schools and shops, which feeds further out-migration.

### Impacts on destination areas

- **Population structure:** a bulge at 20-39, followed by a rise in the 0-9 band. Cities become younger than the national average.
- **Economy:** a large labour supply supports manufacturing and services, but also a growing informal sector with low and irregular pay.
- **Housing and services:** demand outstrips formal housing supply, so informal settlements grow on unused or hazardous land. Water, sanitation and transport come under pressure.

**Example: India.** Large flows run from poorer, mainly agricultural states such as Bihar and Uttar Pradesh to cities including Delhi and Mumbai. Many movers are men working in construction, manufacturing and services, often leaving families behind and moving back and forth. When the national lockdown began in March 2020, many migrant workers lost work and housing at the same time and travelled back to their home villages, some on foot. The episode showed how many rural-urban migrants remain tied to their source areas and lack security in the city.

## Urban-rural migration

In HICs, **counterurbanisation** has been significant since the 1970s. Causes include higher car ownership, cheaper housing and more space outside cities, a perception of a safer and healthier environment, retirement, and, more recently, remote working, which lets people keep urban jobs while living further away.

Impacts on **receiving villages**: house prices rise, and young local people may be priced out. Commuter villages are quiet in the daytime, and second homes may stand empty for much of the year. Service use changes: incomers often shop and use services in towns, so village shops may still close, while demand for schools may rise if families arrive. The population structure tends to gain people aged 30-49 with children, or people aged 60+ in retirement areas, while local young adults leave.

Impacts on **source cities**: loss of middle-income families can lower the tax base of inner areas, although in many cities this has been balanced by re-urbanisation of young adults.

In LICs and MICs, urban-rural movement is more often **return migration**: older migrants going home, or workers returning when urban jobs collapse.

## Stepped migration and urban-urban movement

**Stepped migration** is movement up the settlement hierarchy in stages, village to small town to regional city to capital. Each step is shorter, cheaper and less risky than a direct move, and lets the migrant gain urban skills and savings. Over time, the population of each level is partly replaced from the level below. Improved transport and phone-based information networks allow more people to move directly to the largest city, weakening stepped patterns.

**Urban-urban movement** is the largest flow in many HICs. Graduates and skilled workers move to the cities with the most specialised jobs, such as capitals and financial or technology centres. This concentrates young, educated people in a few leading cities, while some older industrial towns lose them. It can widen regional inequality, a link to Topic 14 for students taking that option.

## Intra-urban movement

Moves within one city are the most frequent of all migrations, but each is short.

**Causes in HIC cities:**

- **Life-cycle changes:** young single adults favour central rented flats near work and leisure. Couples with children move outward to larger homes, gardens and schools. Older people may move to smaller homes or back to more central locations with services.
- **Income and the housing market:** rising income allows a move to more expensive areas; job loss or rent rises can force a move.
- **Gentrification:** middle-class households buy and renovate older inner-city housing, displacing lower-income tenants.
- **Studentification:** concentrations of student lets around universities change the character of some neighbourhoods.
- **Urban renewal and clearance**, which rehouse people elsewhere in the city.

**Causes in LIC and MIC cities:** Turner's model, based on his work in Lima in the 1960s, describes **bridgeheaders**: new arrivals who rent rooms in crowded inner areas close to jobs. Once established, they become **consolidators**, moving to the periphery to build their own homes on squatted or cheaply bought land, gradually improving them. Elites move outward along a high-status sector.

**Impacts:** suburban growth and longer commutes; inner-city decline in some places and gentrification in others; changing residential segregation, linked to 6.3; and pressure on schools and transport where families concentrate.

## Worked example: interpreting population structure

*A rural district and the city it sends migrants to both have age-sex data. In the rural district, males aged 20-39 make up 9% of the population and females aged 20-39 make up 14%. In the city, the figures are 21% and 17%. Suggest what the data show about the migration stream.*

- The rural district has fewer young adults than the city, and especially few young men (9% compared with 14% women), which suggests selective out-migration of young adults dominated by men.
- The city has more young men than young women (21% compared with 17%), consistent with a male-dominated inflow, perhaps to construction or factory work.
- The rural district will probably have more female-headed households, lower birth rates in future and an older age structure.

A good answer quotes the figures, identifies the selectivity (age and sex), and then explains consequences for each area.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Explain the impacts of urban-rural migration on receiving rural settlements. [6]*

> Urban-rural migrants, many of them commuting families, increase demand for housing (**cause**), so house prices rise and young local people cannot afford to stay (**social impact**). Incomers often work and shop in the town (**behaviour**), so village shops and buses may still lose custom (**service impact**). The age structure changes as families aged 30-49 arrive and local young adults leave (**population structure**). Some villages gain second homes that are empty for much of the year, weakening community life (**further impact**). However, new residents can support the school and form active community groups (**positive impact, balance**).

## Common mistakes

- Treating counterurbanisation as only "people moving to the countryside". It is a net movement down the urban hierarchy.
- Writing about impacts without saying which area, source or destination, is affected.
- Ignoring population structure, which the syllabus names specifically.
- Calling every move within a city "suburbanisation". Inward moves and gentrification are intra-urban too.
- Assuming rural-urban migrants cut all links with home. Remittances and return visits are common.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a pyramid: quote percentages for the key age bands and compare males and females.
- **Explain** impacts: link each impact to a feature of the migrants (age, sex, income).
- **Compare** two flows: describe both similarities and differences; two separate descriptions do not make a comparison.
- **Assess** a statement such as "rural-urban migration benefits source areas more than destinations": weigh remittances and reduced land pressure against the loss of young labour, and reach a view.
