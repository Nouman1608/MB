## 3.1.2(a): s² and the 2+ ion

- Outer configuration: ns². Mg 3s², Ca 4s², Sr 5s², Ba 6s².
- Both s electrons are lost, giving M²⁺ with the previous noble gas configuration.
- Oxidation number of the metal: 0 → +2. The metal is **oxidised** in every reaction in this section.
- The partner species is **reduced**: O (0 → −2) with oxygen; H (+1 → 0) with water or acid.

### Method in steps: proving a Group 2 reaction is redox

1. Write the oxidation number of the metal on each side (0 and +2).
2. Find the element whose oxidation number falls.
3. Say which is oxidised and which is reduced, each with its numbers.
4. Check electrons lost = electrons gained.

## 3.1.2(b): The three reactions

| Reaction | General equation | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Oxygen | 2M(s) + O₂(g) → 2MO(s) | White oxides; Mg burns with a brilliant white light once heated |
| Cold water (Ca, Sr, Ba) | M(s) + 2H₂O(l) → M(OH)₂(aq) + H₂(g) | Faster down the group; calcium gives a cloudy suspension |
| Cold water (Mg) | Mg(s) + 2H₂O(l) → Mg(OH)₂(s) + H₂(g) | Very slow |
| Steam (Mg) | Mg(s) + H₂O(g) → MgO(s) + H₂(g) | Fast; product is the **oxide** |
| Dilute acid | M(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MCl₂(aq) + H₂(g) | Salt + hydrogen only (specification limit) |

Reactivity order: **Mg < Ca < Sr < Ba**.

Typical observations for a metal reacting with water or acid: fizzing (effervescence), the metal getting smaller and disappearing, and the mixture getting warmer.

**Worked reminder.** Show that Ba + 2HCl → BaCl₂ + H₂ is redox:

```
Ba:  0 → +2     loses 2 e⁻      oxidised
H:  +1 → 0      2 H each gain 1 e⁻ = 2 e⁻      reduced
Cl: −1 → −1     unchanged
```

### Comparing two metals in one answer

When a question asks you to compare two Group 2 metals, use a comparative for every point: "calcium fizzes more vigorously than magnesium", "barium disappears faster than strontium". A list of separate observations for each metal, with no comparison, does not answer the question. If the amounts of metal in moles are the same, the final volume of hydrogen is the same; only the rate differs.

## 3.1.2(c): Ionisation energies and reactivity

Second ionisation energy, as an equation (a definition is not required):

```
Sr⁺(g) → Sr²⁺(g) + e⁻
```

Standard values, kJ mol⁻¹:

| | Mg | Ca | Sr | Ba |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| First | 738 | 590 | 550 | 503 |
| Second | 1451 | 1145 | 1064 | 965 |
| Sum | 2189 | 1735 | 1614 | 1468 |

### Method in steps: the reactivity explanation

1. Down the group the atomic radius increases (an extra shell each time).
2. Shielding by inner shells increases.
3. Nuclear attraction on the outer electrons decreases, even though nuclear charge increases.
4. So the first and second ionisation energies decrease.
5. So the two outer electrons are lost more easily, and reactivity increases.

All five links are needed for a full explanation. "Easier to lose electrons" on its own is only the last step.

The M3.1 label on this outcome means you should move freely between a table, a graph and a sentence about the trend.

**Reading the data.** From Mg to Ca the sum of the first two ionisation energies drops by 454 kJ mol⁻¹. From Sr to Ba it drops by 146 kJ mol⁻¹. The fall continues all the way down, so each metal reacts more readily than the one above it. On a graph of ionisation energy against element, both lines slope downwards from left to right, with the second ionisation energy line above the first for every element: removing an electron from a positive ion always needs more energy than removing one from a neutral atom of the same element.

## 3.1.2(d): Oxides with water

```
MO(s) + H₂O(l) → M(OH)₂
M(OH)₂ → M²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq)
```

| Solution from | Approximate pH |
|---|---|
| MgO | about 10 |
| CaO | about 12 |
| SrO | about 13 |
| BaO | about 13 or above |

- The hydroxides get more soluble down the group, so [OH⁻] rises and the alkalinity increases.
- No oxidation numbers change: this is an acid-base type reaction, not redox.

## 3.1.2(e): Bases in use

| Use | Compound | Equation |
|---|---|---|
| Neutralising acid soil | Ca(OH)₂ | Ca(OH)₂ + 2H⁺ → Ca²⁺ + 2H₂O |
| Antacid | Mg(OH)₂ | Mg(OH)₂ + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + 2H₂O |
| Antacid | CaCO₃ | CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂ |

The specification says "for example (but not limited to)", so expect other Group 2 bases, such as CaO or CaCO₃ on soils, with an equation you build yourself.

**Worked reminder.** Mass of Mg(OH)₂ (Mr 58.3) that neutralises 25.0 cm³ of 0.400 mol dm⁻³ HCl:

```
n(HCl)      = 0.0250 × 0.400 = 0.0100 mol
n(Mg(OH)₂)  = 0.0100 ÷ 2     = 0.00500 mol
mass        = 0.00500 × 58.3 = 0.292 g
```

### Method in steps: any neutralisation by a Group 2 base

1. Write the balanced equation.
2. Find moles of the known substance.
3. Use the ratio: one M(OH)₂, MO or MCO₃ reacts with **two** H⁺.
4. Convert to mass, volume or concentration as asked.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Second ionisation energy vs forming the 2+ ion.** M⁺(g) → M²⁺(g) + e⁻ is the second ionisation. M(g) → M²⁺(g) + 2e⁻ is the sum of the first and second.
- **Magnesium with water vs steam.** Cold water gives Mg(OH)₂, very slowly. Steam gives MgO, quickly.
- **Metal with water vs oxide with water.** The metal reaction is redox and releases hydrogen. The oxide reaction is not redox and releases no gas.
- **Hydroxide vs carbonate antacid.** Both neutralise two H⁺; only the carbonate releases CO₂.
- **Nuclear charge vs nuclear attraction.** Charge increases down the group; attraction on the outer electrons decreases.

## Quick self-test

1. Write the electron configuration of Ca²⁺ in full.
2. Write the equation for the second ionisation energy of magnesium.
3. Name the solid product when heated magnesium reacts with steam.
4. Write the equation, with state symbols, for barium reacting with water.
5. In Ca + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂, give the oxidation number change for the element reduced.
6. Use the table in 3.1.2(c) to find the energy needed to form one mole of Sr²⁺(g) from Sr(g).
7. 2.43 g of magnesium (Ar 24.3) reacts with excess dilute hydrochloric acid. Calculate the volume of hydrogen at RTP, in dm³ (24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹).
8. Calculate the mass of CaCO₃ (Mr 100.1) that neutralises 0.0300 mol of HCl.
9. Calculate the mass of strontium oxide formed when 4.38 g of strontium (Ar 87.6) burns completely. Mr(SrO) = 103.6.
10. Calculate the mass of Ca(OH)₂ (Mr 74.1) needed to neutralise 0.500 mol of nitric acid.
11. Which of MgO and SrO gives the solution with the higher pH, and why?
12. Is the reaction of calcium oxide with water a redox reaction? Give a reason.

### Answers

1. 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶
2. Mg⁺(g) → Mg²⁺(g) + e⁻
3. Magnesium oxide, MgO.
4. Ba(s) + 2H₂O(l) → Ba(OH)₂(aq) + H₂(g)
5. Hydrogen, +1 → 0.
6. 550 + 1064 = **1614 kJ mol⁻¹**
7. n(Mg) = 2.43 ÷ 24.3 = 0.100 mol = n(H₂); V = 0.100 × 24.0 = **2.40 dm³**
8. n(CaCO₃) = 0.0300 ÷ 2 = 0.0150 mol; mass = 0.0150 × 100.1 = **1.50 g**
9. n(Sr) = 4.38 ÷ 87.6 = 0.0500 mol = n(SrO); mass = 0.0500 × 103.6 = **5.18 g**
10. n(Ca(OH)₂) = 0.500 ÷ 2 = 0.250 mol; mass = 0.250 × 74.1 = **18.5 g**
11. SrO. Strontium hydroxide is more soluble than magnesium hydroxide, so its solution has a higher OH⁻ concentration.
12. No. Calcium stays +2, oxygen −2 and hydrogen +1 throughout.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing the second ionisation as M(g) → M²⁺(g) + 2e⁻.
- Leaving state symbols off ionisation equations.
- Giving Mg(OH)₂ as the product with steam, or MgO with cold water.
- Explaining the trend with "more protons so less attraction"; the decrease is due to distance and shielding.
- Stopping at "loses electrons more easily" without linking to ionisation energy and atomic radius.
- Calling the oxide-water reaction redox.
- Using a 1 : 1 ratio between a Group 2 base and HCl.
- Forgetting CO₂ and H₂O in the carbonate antacid equation.
- Describing barium as "less reactive because it is bigger"; size makes it more reactive.
