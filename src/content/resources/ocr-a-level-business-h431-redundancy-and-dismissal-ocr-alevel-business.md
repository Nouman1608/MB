## Redundancy and dismissal defined

**Dismissal** is the ending of an employee's contract by the employer, with or without notice. **Redundancy** is a form of dismissal that happens when the job itself is no longer needed, for example because the business is closing, moving, or reducing the number of staff doing a type of work. The employee has not done anything wrong. It is a fair reason for dismissal when handled properly.

**Resignation** (the employee leaves) and **retirement** are not dismissals. **Constructive dismissal** is when an employer's serious breach of the contract forces the employee to resign; the law may treat it as a dismissal.

## Fair dismissal

A dismissal is fair if the reason is a potentially fair one and the employer acts reasonably in using a fair procedure. The specification names four reasons.

- **Capacity.** The employee cannot do the job to the required standard, because of lack of skill or long-term ill health. The employer should usually give support, training and time to improve.
- **Conduct.** Misconduct such as theft, violence, repeated lateness, or breaking safety rules. Serious misconduct (gross misconduct) may justify immediate dismissal after a fair process.
- **Redundancy.** The post is no longer needed. The employer should consult, choose fairly who goes, and consider alternative jobs.
- **Some other substantial reason.** A genuine reason that does not fit the others, such as a business reorganisation or a refusal to accept a necessary change to the contract.

## Unfair dismissal

**Unfair dismissal** is dismissal without a fair reason, or without following a fair procedure. Certain reasons are unfair in themselves. The specification names:

- **Pregnancy** (and maternity or family leave);
- **Trade union membership** or activities;
- **Discrimination** on grounds protected by law, such as age, sex, race, disability or religion;
- **Whistle blowing**, which means reporting wrongdoing, such as a safety risk or illegal act.

Other examples are asking for a legal right (such as the minimum wage) or selecting people for redundancy unfairly. The rules on how long an employee must have worked before claiming ordinary unfair dismissal have been changing; some reasons, such as those listed above, are protected whatever the length of service. Check the current position when studying.

## Procedures

### Dismissal procedure (a typical fair process)

1. **Investigate** the problem, collecting evidence and listening to the employee.
2. **Write to the employee** setting out the allegation or concern, the evidence and the date of a hearing, with notice to prepare.
3. **Hold a hearing.** The employee may be accompanied by a colleague or trade union representative. They may present their case and ask questions.
4. **Decide** and put the decision in writing with reasons. Options include no action, a warning or dismissal. For ordinary misconduct or poor performance the process normally includes earlier warnings (verbal, written, final).
5. **Offer an appeal** to someone more senior who was not involved in the first decision.

The business should follow its own policy and the code of practice that applies to disciplinary and grievance cases.

### Redundancy procedure

Plan, consult employees (and union representatives), decide selection criteria that are fair and objective, look for alternatives such as redeployment, give notice, and pay notice and redundancy pay as required.

### Grievance procedure

A grievance is a complaint by an employee about their treatment at work, such as bullying or pay.

1. **Informal discussion** with the line manager, to see if it can be settled quickly.
2. **Formal written grievance** to the employer if it is not resolved.
3. **A meeting** with the employee (who may be accompanied) to discuss the complaint.
4. **A decision** in writing, with reasons.
5. **An appeal** if the employee is not satisfied.

## Employment tribunals

An **employment tribunal** is an independent, legal body that hears disputes between employers and employees, such as claims for unfair dismissal, discrimination and unpaid wages. Its **purpose** is to resolve these disputes in a way that is more accessible and less formal than a court. It considers the evidence, decides whether the law has been broken and can order remedies, such as compensation, reinstatement or re-engagement. Normally, parties are expected to try early conciliation first.

Significance:

- For the employee it is a way to seek justice and compensation when treated unfairly.
- For the business it is a risk, costing money, management time and reputation.
- For all employers it is a reason to have fair procedures and keep good records.

## Impact and importance of redundancy and dismissal

### Worked example 1: costing a redundancy programme

Pelham Plastics (invented) has 100 production staff and decides to make 10 redundant. Each earns £520 a week. The average length of service is 6 years. The company offers an enhanced scheme of 2 weeks' pay per year of service, and pays 4 weeks' notice in lieu. (Statutory minimum payments may be lower; the scheme is the business's own choice.)

- Redundancy pay per person = 2 × 6 × £520 = **£6,240**; for 10 people £62,400.
- Notice pay per person = 4 × £520 = £2,080; for 10 people £20,800.
- Total cost = £62,400 + £20,800 = **£83,200**.
- Annual wage saving = 10 × £520 × 52 = **£270,400**.
- Payback = £83,200 ÷ £270,400 = 0.31 years, about **3.7 months**.

But there are further costs. Suppose the remaining 90 staff generate a total contribution of £4.5 million and their output falls by 4% for a year because of lower morale and a heavier workload. The loss is 4% × £4,500,000 = **£180,000**. The net first-year benefit is £270,400 − £83,200 − £180,000 = **£7,200**.

Evaluation:

- The payback looks very quick, but the benefit nearly disappears in year 1 if morale harms output. Over a longer period, if output recovers, the saving is real.
- Survivors may fear for their own jobs and the best staff may leave voluntarily.
- Redundancy also loses skills and experience that are costly to replace if demand returns.
- The community faces lost jobs and spending; the employees affected face loss of income and stress, though redundancy pay and support help.
- Alternatives include a recruitment freeze, voluntary redundancy, reduced hours or redeployment.

**Judgement.** Redundancy may be justified if demand has fallen permanently, but it is more costly than the first figures suggest. Pelham should consult, offer voluntary options first, communicate openly and support those leaving.

### Wider impact of dismissal

For the business, dismissal of a poor performer or a person guilty of misconduct protects standards, safety and the morale of other workers. Poor handling brings tribunal risk and damages the reputation of the business as an employer. For the employee, it means loss of income and often difficulty finding another job. Colleagues are affected by how the dismissal is handled: a fair process builds trust, while an unfair one lowers morale.

## Evaluating procedures

Effective procedures:

- are clear, written and known to all employees;
- are applied consistently, so that similar cases are treated alike;
- give employees a fair hearing, the chance to be accompanied and a right of appeal;
- give managers confidence and reduce the risk of tribunal claims;
- resolve problems early, before they escalate.

Limits:

- They take management time and can be slow, which is difficult for small firms without HR staff.
- Managers may not follow them correctly, or may treat them as a box-ticking exercise.
- Employees may distrust a process run by their own manager.
- A formal grievance process may damage relationships, so informal resolution matters first.

### Worked example 2: the cost of an unfair dismissal claim

Keswick Glass (invented) dismisses a worker for poor performance without warnings or a hearing. The worker wins an unfair dismissal claim at a tribunal. Costs (illustrative): legal fees £6,500; management time 40 hours at £40 = £1,600; compensation awarded £12,000. Total = £6,500 + £1,600 + £12,000 = **£20,100**.

A fair process (investigation, warnings and a hearing) might take 12 hours of managers' time at £40 = £480, plus £1,000 for HR advice = **£1,480**, much less than £20,100.

The lesson: following a fair procedure costs far less than losing a claim, and it protects the business's reputation. A procedure cannot guarantee a fair outcome, but it makes the decision more defensible.

## Common mistakes

- Treating redundancy as a dismissal for poor performance. The job is removed, not the person's ability.
- Listing fair reasons without explaining each.
- Saying that employers can dismiss for any reason if they give notice.
- Forgetting the costs of redundancy beyond redundancy pay: lost morale, skills and output.
- Assuming that a procedure guarantees fairness.
- Confusing a grievance (a complaint by the employee) with a disciplinary matter (a concern by the employer).

## Exam technique

For "explain" questions, define the term and give an example in the case. For "evaluate" questions on redundancy, include costs and benefits, short-term and long-term effects, and the effect on remaining staff. Finish with a judgement and what it depends on.
