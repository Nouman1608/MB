## What a coral reef is

A coral reef is a ridge or mound of **calcium carbonate** built mainly by colonies of reef-building (hermatypic) corals, together with calcareous algae, shells and other skeletal debris cemented together. Each coral **polyp** is a small animal that secretes a cup-shaped limestone skeleton. Inside its tissues live single-celled algae called **zooxanthellae**. The algae photosynthesise and pass most of the sugars they make to the polyp; the polyp gives the algae shelter, carbon dioxide and nutrients from its waste. This symbiosis lets corals build skeletons quickly in nutrient-poor tropical water, and it explains almost every growth condition in the syllabus.

The reef is not only living coral. **Coralline algae** cement loose fragments and form a tough crust on the reef crest, while storm waves break off branches that become sand and rubble. A reef is therefore a balance between **construction** (coral and algal growth) and **destruction** (wave attack, boring organisms, grazing fish and dissolution).

## Characteristics: reef zones

Most reefs show a similar set of zones from the open sea landward:

- **Fore-reef slope:** the seaward face, often steep, with the greatest coral diversity in the well-lit upper part. On many reefs it has **spurs and grooves**, ridges of coral separated by sand channels that run at right angles to the reef edge and help dissipate wave energy.
- **Reef crest:** the highest point, where waves break. Only robust, wave-resistant corals and coralline algae survive; at low tide it may be exposed.
- **Reef flat:** a shallow platform behind the crest, with scattered coral heads, rubble and sand. Water can become very warm and salty at low tide, so diversity is lower.
- **Back reef and lagoon:** sheltered water with patch reefs, seagrass and sand. In barrier reefs and atolls the lagoon can be tens of metres deep.

These zones matter in the exam because they show that a reef is a *wave-built* as well as a *biological* landform: the crest is where wave energy is greatest and the sediment produced there feeds the reef flat, lagoon and nearby beaches.

## Distribution

Reef-building corals are mostly found between about 30°N and 30°S, but their distribution within that belt is uneven.

- The greatest diversity is in the **Indo-Pacific**, especially the "Coral Triangle" of Indonesia, the Philippines, Malaysia, Papua New Guinea, the Solomon Islands and Timor-Leste.
- The **Caribbean** has fewer species but extensive reefs, including the Mesoamerican Barrier Reef off Belize and Mexico.
- Reefs extend beyond 30° where warm currents raise sea temperature: **Bermuda**, at about 32°N, has reefs because of the warm water of the Gulf Stream.
- Reefs are absent or rare on the western coasts of tropical continents, where **cold currents and upwelling** lower sea temperature, and near the mouths of large rivers such as the Amazon, where fresh water and sediment reduce salinity and light.

## Conditions required for coral growth, linked to the biology

| Condition | Typical range | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Warm water | About 23-29°C; most reefs need a winter minimum above about 18°C | Polyp metabolism and zooxanthellae function best in a narrow band; prolonged water above the usual summer maximum causes bleaching |
| Light | Mostly within roughly the top 30 m | Zooxanthellae need light to photosynthesise, so reefs grow upward towards the surface |
| Clear water | Low suspended sediment | Sediment blocks light and smothers polyps, which must spend energy clearing it |
| Salinity | Normal sea water, roughly 30-40 parts per thousand | Fresh water from rivers stresses polyps |
| Wave action | Moderate | Brings oxygen and plankton, removes sediment; but too much breaks branches |
| Limited exposure | Short periods out of water | Polyps dry out and overheat if exposed for long at low tide |
| A firm base | Rock or old reef in shallow water | Larvae need a hard surface to settle on |

## Formation of the three reef types

**Fringing reefs** grow directly from the shore, with at most a narrow, shallow lagoon. They are common where the sea floor slopes gently from a stable coast, for example along much of the Red Sea.

**Barrier reefs** are separated from the shore by a wide, deep lagoon. **Atolls** are rings or horseshoes of reef around a lagoon with no central island; low islands of coral sand (motu or cays) form on the reef rim. Kiribati, the Marshall Islands, Tuvalu and the Maldives are mainly atolls.

Three explanations are usually compared:

1. **Subsidence (Darwin):** a fringing reef round a sinking volcanic island grows upward, becoming a barrier reef and then an atoll. Drilling on Pacific atolls has found thick reef limestone above volcanic rock, which supports the theory for oceanic islands.
2. **Glacial control (associated with Reginald Daly, early twentieth century):** during glacial periods sea level fell, and waves cut platforms across islands and old reefs. When sea level rose after glaciation, corals grew upward on the rims of these platforms, producing barrier reefs and atolls. This explains why many lagoons have similar depths.
3. **Antecedent platform:** reefs grow on any suitable shallow surface, such as a submarine bank or an older reef top eroded or dissolved during low sea level. Lagoons may partly reflect solution of old limestone exposed to rain during glacial lowstands.

The best answers combine them: subsidence explains the thickness of reef rock on many oceanic atolls, while sea level change during the Pleistocene explains much of the present surface form.

## Threats

The syllabus names four groups.

- **Global warming:** bleaching occurs when water stays above the usual summer maximum for several weeks, so corals expel their zooxanthellae. Corals can recover if the stress is short, but repeated events leave too little time between them. Higher carbon dioxide also causes **ocean acidification**, which reduces the carbonate available to build skeletons. Warmer seas may increase coral disease and the intensity of tropical cyclones that break reefs.
- **Sea-level rise:** a healthy reef can grow upwards fast enough to keep pace with moderate rise ("keep-up"), may lag and later reach the surface ("catch-up"), or may drown if stress slows growth ("give-up"). Atoll islands depend on reefs supplying sand, so reef decline threatens the land itself.
- **Pollution:** nutrients from fertiliser and sewage favour algae that outcompete coral; sediment from deforestation, farming, dredging and construction blocks light; oil spills and chemicals add stress. Nutrient runoff has also been linked to outbreaks of the coral-eating **crown-of-thorns starfish**.
- **Physical damage:** anchors, boat groundings, trampling by tourists, blast and cyanide fishing, coral mining for building materials, and land reclamation. Overfishing of grazing fish lets seaweed spread over damaged coral, so damage and pollution reinforce each other.

## Possible management strategies

| Strategy | Scale | Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Marine protected areas and no-take zones | Local to regional | Healthy fish stocks graze algae; reefs recover faster after disturbance | Need enforcement; cannot stop warming |
| Community-managed sanctuaries (e.g. Apo Island, Philippines) | Local | Local people benefit from higher fish catches and tourism, so support is strong | Small areas; depend on local leadership |
| Mooring buoys and diver education | Local | Cheap; prevents anchor damage | Only addresses one cause |
| User fees for divers | Local | Funds rangers and monitoring | Depends on tourist numbers |
| Catchment management to cut runoff | Regional | Tackles sediment and nutrients at source; part of Australia's Reef 2050 Plan | Slow; needs farmers to change practice |
| Crown-of-thorns starfish control | Local | Protects high-value sites | Labour-intensive; treats a symptom |
| Coral gardening and restoration | Local | Can restore damaged patches | Expensive per hectare; restored coral can still bleach |
| Cutting greenhouse gas emissions | Global | The only way to reduce bleaching and acidification | Needs international agreement; benefits are slow |

## Worked example

*Assess the extent to which local management can protect coral reefs. [20]* (plan)

- Define local management (protected areas, fishing rules, runoff control) and the threats it can and cannot reach.
- **Can:** no-take zones and community sanctuaries rebuild fish populations, which control algae and speed recovery; mooring buoys remove anchor damage; catchment schemes reduce sediment.
- **Cannot:** marine heatwaves and acidification are global; protected reefs bleach too.
- **Interaction:** local measures raise **resilience**, so a reef recovers between heatwaves; without global emissions cuts, gaps between events may become too short.
- **Judgement:** local management is necessary but not sufficient; its value is greatest where local pressures are high and warming stress moderate.

## Common mistakes

- Listing conditions with no link to zooxanthellae, light and photosynthesis.
- Describing only Darwin's theory; the role of Pleistocene sea level change gains evaluation credit.
- Writing that bleaching kills coral instantly; bleached coral can recover if conditions return to normal soon enough.
- Treating threats separately; examiners reward recognising that they combine.
- Proposing management without saying which threat it addresses.

## Exam technique

**Describe the distribution** wants a pattern with named regions and exceptions (Bermuda, west coasts). **Explain the formation** wants a sequence and may be helped by a labelled cross-section of each reef type. **Evaluate** or **assess** questions on threats and management need weighing by scale (local or global) and time.
