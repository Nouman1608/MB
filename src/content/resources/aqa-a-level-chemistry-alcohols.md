## 3.3.5.1 Alcohol production

Industrially, alcohols are made by adding steam to an alkene in the presence of an acid catalyst. For ethanol:

```
CH₂=CH₂(g) + H₂O(g) → CH₃CH₂OH(g)
```

A common industrial catalyst is phosphoric acid held on a solid support such as silica, used with steam at
about 300 °C and high pressure. The reaction is reversible, so unreacted ethene is recycled. You can see this
equilibrium treated with Kp in the
[Kp study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-equilibrium-constant-kp-for-homogeneous-systems/).

**Mechanism (electrophilic addition, acid catalysed).**

1. The electrons in the C=C double bond attack an H⁺ ion from the acid (shown as H⁺ or as H₃O⁺). One carbon
   gains the H and the other becomes a carbocation.
2. A lone pair on the oxygen of a water molecule attacks the positive carbon. This gives a protonated alcohol
   (an oxonium ion, with O carrying the + charge).
3. An O–H bond in the oxonium ion breaks, its electrons going to the oxygen, and H⁺ is released. The catalyst
   is regenerated.

```
CH₂=CH₂ + H⁺ → CH₃–CH₂⁺
CH₃–CH₂⁺ + H₂O → CH₃–CH₂–OH₂⁺
CH₃–CH₂–OH₂⁺ → CH₃–CH₂–OH + H⁺
```

**Worked example 1.** Predict the major product when propene reacts with steam and an acid catalyst.

- Step 1: H⁺ can add to either carbon of the double bond.
- Step 2: adding H to the end carbon (C1) gives a secondary carbocation, CH₃–C⁺H–CH₃. Adding H to C2 gives a
  primary carbocation, ⁺CH₂–CH₂–CH₃.
- Step 3: the secondary carbocation is more stable, so it gives the major product.
- Answer: **propan-2-ol**, CH₃CH(OH)CH₃, is the major product; propan-1-ol is the minor product.

### Fermentation of glucose

Ethanol is also made industrially by fermentation of glucose using yeast:

```
C₆H₁₂O₆(aq) → 2C₂H₅OH(aq) + 2CO₂(g)
```

**Conditions and why they are used:**

- **Yeast**: its enzymes catalyse the reaction.
- **A warm temperature, around 35 °C**: a compromise. Lower temperatures give a slow rate; much higher
  temperatures denature the enzymes in the yeast.
- **Anaerobic conditions (air excluded)**: with oxygen present, the ethanol is oxidised to ethanoic acid, and
  the yeast respires aerobically to carbon dioxide and water instead of making ethanol.

Fermentation gives an aqueous mixture. The ethanol is separated by
**fractional distillation**; ethanol boils at about 78 °C, below water. The purified ethanol can then be used
as a **biofuel**.

**Worked example 2.** Calculate the maximum mass of ethanol from 360 g of glucose. (Mr: glucose 180.0,
ethanol 46.0)

```
n(glucose) = 360 ÷ 180.0 = 2.00 mol
n(ethanol) = 2 × 2.00 = 4.00 mol
mass       = 4.00 × 46.0 = 184 g
```

Answer: **184 g** of ethanol (with 176 g of carbon dioxide).

### Biofuels and carbon neutrality

A **biofuel** is a fuel produced from biological material (biomass), such as crops, that has been grown
recently and can be grown again. Ethanol from fermented sugar is a biofuel.

A **carbon-neutral** fuel is one with no net carbon dioxide emission to the atmosphere. The claim for
bioethanol rests on three equations:

```
Photosynthesis:  6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂
Fermentation:    C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂
Combustion:      2C₂H₅OH + 6O₂ → 4CO₂ + 6H₂O
```

**Worked example 3.** Per mole of glucose, photosynthesis absorbs 6 mol CO₂. Fermentation releases 2 mol and
burning the 2 mol of ethanol releases 4 mol: 6 in, 6 out, so no net CO₂ by these three equations.

**Why the claim is not valid.** Fossil fuels are burned, releasing CO₂, for energy used in farm machinery, making fertilisers, the fractional distillation and transporting the fuel. So the overall
process releases more CO₂ than the crop absorbed.

### Environmental and ethical issues

Weigh both sides when discussing biofuel decisions:

- **For:** the crop is renewable; less finite crude oil is used; net CO₂ emissions can be lower than for petrol.
- **Against:** land for fuel crops is not used for food, which can raise food prices (an ethical issue);
  clearing forest destroys habitats and releases stored carbon; fertiliser run-off pollutes water.

## 3.3.5.2 Oxidation of alcohols

### Classifying alcohols

Look at the carbon atom bonded to the –OH group and count the carbon atoms joined to it.

| Class | Carbons on the C–OH carbon | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | one (or none, in methanol) | butan-1-ol, CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂OH |
| Secondary | two | butan-2-ol, CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CH₃ |
| Tertiary | three | 2-methylpropan-2-ol, (CH₃)₃COH |

### The oxidising agent

Acidified potassium dichromate(VI), K₂Cr₂O₇ with dilute sulfuric acid, is a suitable oxidising agent. When
it oxidises an alcohol, the orange Cr₂O₇²⁻ ions are reduced to green Cr³⁺ ions. You may write equations with
**[O]** for the oxygen from the oxidising agent.

- **Primary alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid**
  ```
  CH₃CH₂OH + [O] → CH₃CHO + H₂O         (ethanal)
  CH₃CHO + [O] → CH₃COOH                (ethanoic acid)
  CH₃CH₂OH + 2[O] → CH₃COOH + H₂O       (in one step)
  ```
- **Secondary alcohol → ketone**
  ```
  CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ + [O] → CH₃COCH₃ + H₂O   (propanone)
  ```
- **Tertiary alcohol**: not easily oxidised. The carbon bonded to –OH has no hydrogen atom to lose, so the
  dichromate stays orange.

**Worked example 4.** For butan-1-ol, butan-2-ol and 2-methylpropan-2-ol, warmed with excess acidified
potassium dichromate(VI) under reflux, give the organic product and the colour seen.

- Butan-1-ol (primary): **butanoic acid**, CH₃CH₂CH₂COOH; orange to green.
- Butan-2-ol (secondary): **butanone**, CH₃COCH₂CH₃; orange to green.
- 2-methylpropan-2-ol (tertiary): **no reaction**; stays orange.

### How the method decides aldehyde or carboxylic acid

| Wanted product | Method | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| Aldehyde | Heat gently with a limited amount of oxidising agent (alcohol in excess) and **distil the aldehyde off as it forms** | The aldehyde has a lower boiling point than the alcohol, because there is no hydrogen bonding between aldehyde molecules, so it leaves the flask before it can be oxidised further |
| Carboxylic acid | Heat under **reflux** with **excess** oxidising agent, then distil | Vapours condense and run back into the flask, so the aldehyde stays in contact with the oxidising agent until it is fully oxidised |

### Telling aldehydes from ketones

Aldehydes are oxidised easily; ketones are not. Two mild oxidising agents use this difference:

| Test | Method | Aldehyde | Ketone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tollens' reagent (silver nitrate in aqueous ammonia, containing [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺) | Warm in a water bath | **Silver mirror** forms; Ag⁺ is reduced to Ag | No change |
| Fehling's solution (blue, contains Cu²⁺) | Warm | **Blue solution gives a red precipitate** (Cu₂O) | Stays blue |

In both tests the aldehyde is oxidised to a carboxylic acid (as its salt in the alkaline reagent):
RCHO + [O] → RCOOH. Use a water bath, not a flame: organic liquids are flammable.

Alkenes form when alcohols are heated with a concentrated acid catalyst, such as concentrated sulfuric acid or
concentrated phosphoric acid. Water is eliminated:

```
CH₃CH₂OH → CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O
```

Primary ethanol dehydration is commonly described by a concerted E2-like elimination after the O atom is
protonated: a base removes a β-hydrogen as the C=C bond forms and water leaves. This avoids a free primary
ethyl carbocation.

The stepwise carbocation outline and arrow diagram that follow are shown for **secondary butan-2-ol**.
Do not generalise that free-carbocation sequence to every alcohol.

**Stepwise mechanism (secondary alcohol, E1-like):**

1. A lone pair on the oxygen of the –OH group attacks H⁺, giving a protonated alcohol, R–OH₂⁺.
2. The C–O bond breaks, its electrons going to oxygen, and a water molecule leaves. A carbocation forms.
3. The electrons in a C–H bond on a carbon **next to** the positive carbon move to form the C=C bond. H⁺ is
   released, so the acid is a catalyst.

**Worked example 5.** Name every alkene formed when butan-2-ol is heated with concentrated phosphoric acid.

- Step 1: the carbocation is CH₃–C⁺H–CH₂CH₃ (on C2).
- Step 2: H can be lost from C1 or from C3.
- Step 3: loss from C1 gives **but-1-ene**, CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃. Loss from C3 gives but-2-ene, CH₃CH=CHCH₃, which
  has E/Z isomers.
- Answer: three alkenes: **but-1-ene, (E)-but-2-ene and (Z)-but-2-ene**.

The specification suggests preparing cyclohexene from cyclohexanol, purified with a separating funnel and by
distillation.

1. Warm cyclohexanol with concentrated phosphoric acid and anti-bumping granules. Distil the impure
   cyclohexene out as it forms (thermometer bulb level with the side arm, condenser water in at the bottom).
2. Shake the distillate in a separating funnel with sodium carbonate solution to neutralise acid. Release the
   pressure often, because carbon dioxide forms. Run off and discard the lower aqueous layer.
3. Wash with water, separate again, and transfer the organic layer to a dry flask.
4. Add an anhydrous drying agent such as calcium chloride or magnesium sulfate, swirl until the liquid is
   clear, then decant or filter.
5. Redistil and collect only the fraction boiling close to the boiling point of cyclohexene.

**Worked example 6.** 20.0 g of cyclohexanol (Mr 100.0) gives 11.5 g of cyclohexene (Mr 82.0). Calculate the
percentage yield.

```
n(cyclohexanol)        = 20.0 ÷ 100.0 = 0.200 mol
theoretical n(alkene)  = 0.200 mol (1 : 1)
actual n(cyclohexene)  = 11.5 ÷ 82.0 = 0.1402 mol
percentage yield       = 0.1402 ÷ 0.200 × 100 = 70.1 %
```

Answer: **70.1 %**. Product is lost in transfers, on the drying agent and through incomplete reaction.

## Common errors

- Writing the fermentation equation with one CO₂ instead of two.
- Justifying the temperature without both rate and enzyme denaturation.
- Drawing the first curly arrow of hydration from H⁺ to the double bond: it starts at the C=C bond.
- Distilling to get a carboxylic acid, or refluxing to get an aldehyde.
- Saying tertiary alcohols "cannot react": they are not easily oxidised and the dichromate stays orange.
- Missing E/Z isomers among elimination products.
- Calling the Fehling's precipitate "brown" instead of red.
