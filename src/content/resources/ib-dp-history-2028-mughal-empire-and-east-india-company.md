This is a **higher level (HL) only** page. The Mughal Empire and the British East India Company (1526 to 1858) is one of the twelve regional studies in the Asia and Oceania option, examined on Paper 3, the 2-hour essay paper that standard level students do not sit. This guide follows the IB *History guide*, first assessment 2028. It covers the rise of Mughal power, how Akbar and his successors held it, the challenges it faced, its impact, and the way the East India Company replaced it before the Rebellion of 1857 ended both.

Use it with the [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-mughal-empire-and-east-india-company-revision-notes/) and the [practice set](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-mughal-empire-and-east-india-company-practice/).

## Reasons for the rise of Mughal power

**The First Battle of Panipat (21 April 1526).** Babur, a Timurid prince from Central Asia who had lost Samarkand and taken Kabul, defeated Ibrahim Lodi, the last Sultan of Delhi. Babur's army was much smaller, but it used matchlock guns and field artillery, and a flanking tactic borrowed from Central Asian warfare. The Lodi sultanate was weakened by rivalry between the Afghan nobles. Babur followed this with victory over the Rajput confederation of Rana Sanga at Khanwa (1527), but he died in 1530 with the new state unsettled.

**Humayun and the Suri interval.** Humayun inherited an empire without a secure treasury or administration. Sher Shah Suri defeated him at Chausa (1539) and Kannauj (1540). Humayun spent years in exile and gained help from the Safavid ruler of Persia. Sher Shah ruled only to 1545, but the Suri Dynasty (1540 to 1555) built features the Mughals later inherited: a standard silver coin (the *rupiya*), a surveyed land revenue system, and strategic roads. The Suri gave the later Mughals a model of administration, which is why historians treat the interval as a link and not a pause.

**The Second Battle of Panipat (5 November 1556).** Humayun regained Delhi in 1555 but died the next year. His thirteen-year-old son Akbar, guided by the regent Bairam Khan, defeated Hemu, a Hindu minister who had seized Delhi for the Suri side. This victory secured the dynasty.

## Consolidation of Mughal rule

**Akbar (r. 1556 to 1605).** Akbar combined conquest with conciliation. He took Malwa, Gujarat (1572 to 1573), Bengal (1576), Kashmir (1586) and other regions. He captured Chittor in 1568 but then treated many Rajput rulers as partners. He married into Rajput families, gave their nobles high rank, and abolished the *jizya* (the tax on non-Muslims) in 1564, along with the tax on Hindu pilgrims. Such measures widened the base of the regime.

**The mansabdari system.** A mansab was a numbered rank. Every imperial official held one, and it fixed their pay and the number of troops (the *sawar* rank) they must supply. Mansabdars were usually paid by assignments of land revenue (*jagirs*), which were rotated to stop officials becoming local lords. This tied the nobility, who included Persians, Central Asians and Rajputs, to the emperor. It also had a weakness: when revenue fell below the rank's cost, mansabdars were squeezed.

**Religious policy.** Akbar held debates among scholars at the Ibadat Khana in Fatehpur Sikri and followed a principle called *sulh-i kul*, "peace with all". His Din-i Ilahi (1582) attracted only a small circle. Do not describe it as a new state religion.

**Shah Jahan (r. 1628 to 1658).** His reign was the high point of Mughal display: the Taj Mahal, built from 1632, and the new city of Shahjahanabad. But his wars and building raised costs, and the sons' rivalry ended in civil war.

**Aurangzeb (r. 1658 to 1707).** He won the war of succession against Dara Shikoh, imprisoned his father and ruled for nearly half a century. He reimposed the *jizya* in 1679, ordered some temples destroyed, and supported Islamic law, though he also gave grants to other temples and employed many Hindu officials. He expanded the empire to its largest size, conquering Bijapur (1686) and Golconda (1687), but this brought costly and endless war.

## Challenges to Mughal rule

- **The junta under Jahangir (r. 1605 to 1627).** His wife Nur Jahan, her father Itimad-ud-Daula and her brother Asaf Khan dominated court politics, especially as the emperor's health weakened. Prince Khurram (later Shah Jahan) rebelled in 1622 after tension over the succession. Nur Jahan issued coins and orders in her name, a rare example of female power.
- **Domestic opposition and wars of succession.** Mughal custom had no fixed rule of succession. Each death risked a war among princes, which wasted money and killed capable commanders.
- **The Deccan "Ulcer" (1680 to 1707).** Aurangzeb moved to the Deccan in 1681 and stayed until his death in 1707. The campaigns drained the treasury, took the emperor away from the north, and failed to crush the **Marathas**. Shivaji had founded an independent state, was crowned in 1674, and used raids and fortresses. After Sambhaji's execution in 1689 the Marathas continued under new leaders.
- **Policies.** Aurangzeb's religious measures and the heavy cost of war led to Rajput, Jat, Sikh and Satnami revolts. Historians disagree about how far religion rather than taxes or land caused these.

## Impact of the Mughal Empire

- **Economic.** The empire collected land revenue from a large peasant population, probably a third or more of the crop. Trade and crafts, especially Bengal cotton and silk, grew, and large amounts of silver came in to pay for exports. Cities such as Agra, Delhi, Lahore and Dhaka grew.
- **Culture, arts and law.** Persian was the court language. Mughal painting, architecture (Humayun's tomb, Fatehpur Sikri, the Taj Mahal) and gardens mixed Persian, Central Asian and Indian styles. Law combined Islamic law with imperial orders.
- **Experiences of women.** Royal women such as Nur Jahan and Jahanara Begum had wealth and political influence. Most women, however, lived under strict family control, and in the elite the seclusion of women (*purdah*) and the custom of *sati* continued.
- **Syncretism, Bhakti and Sufism.** The Bhakti movement (poet-saints such as Kabir and Mirabai) and Sufi orders (such as the Chishti) stressed personal devotion. Sikhism grew from Guru Nanak's teaching and later clashed with the state. These traditions blended Hindu and Muslim ideas, and Akbar's court drew on them.

## Decline of the Mughals and the Company's rise

**Mughal weakness.** After 1707 weak emperors, factions among nobles, and revenue problems led to regional states (Bengal, Awadh, Hyderabad) acting almost independently. Nadir Shah of Persia sacked Delhi in 1739. The Marathas then expanded in the north until they were defeated by the Afghan ruler Ahmad Shah Durrani at the Third Battle of Panipat (1761). Be ready to argue about which problem mattered most: Aurangzeb's wars, the jagir crisis, noble factions or outside invasion.

**The Company.** The East India Company began as a trading firm chartered in 1600. It used a private army, alliances and the divisions of Indian rulers. Key steps:

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 1757 | Plassey: Robert Clive defeats Siraj-ud-Daula of Bengal, helped by the defection of Mir Jafar |
| 1764 | Buxar: the Company defeats Mir Qasim, Shuja-ud-Daula and Shah Alam II |
| 1765 | The emperor grants the Company the *diwani* (right to collect revenue) of Bengal, Bihar and Odisha |
| 1799 | Defeat and death of Tipu Sultan of Mysore |
| 1803 | The Company takes Delhi in the Second Anglo-Maratha War; the emperor becomes a pensioner |

## The end of the Company and direct rule

**Failures of Company rule.** High revenue demands, famine in Bengal (1769 to 1770), disruption of Indian crafts, interference in customs, and a perception of arrogance caused growing resentment.

**Three Governors-General.** *Richard Wellesley* (1798 to 1805) expanded rule through the **subsidiary alliance**: Indian rulers received protection and paid for a British force. *William Bentinck* (1828 to 1835) abolished sati (1829), promoted English-language education and cut costs. *James Broun-Ramsay, Lord Dalhousie* (1848 to 1856) annexed the Punjab (1849) and used the **doctrine of lapse**, taking states such as Satara, Jhansi and Nagpur when rulers died without a natural heir. He also annexed Awadh (1856) and built railways and the telegraph.

**The Rebellion of 1857.** It began as a mutiny of sepoys at Meerut on 10 May after rumours that new rifle cartridges were greased with animal fat. It spread to Delhi, where Bahadur Shah Zafar was proclaimed emperor, and to Kanpur, Lucknow and Jhansi. Many civilians joined for reasons such as lost land, the annexation of states and fear for religion. Parts of India, especially the Punjab and Bengal, stayed quiet, and Sikh and Gurkha soldiers fought for the Company. Delhi fell in September 1857. Bahadur Shah was exiled to Rangoon in 1858.

**Reactions.** The Government of India Act of 1858 ended Company rule and put India under the Crown. Queen Victoria's proclamation promised religious toleration and an end to annexations. The army was reorganised, and Britain became warier about social reform.

## Perspectives and historiography

- Nationalist writers, beginning with V. D. Savarkar in 1909, called 1857 the "First War of Independence". Others, such as R. C. Majumdar, argued it was neither first, nor national, nor a war of independence.
- Historians such as Irfan Habib saw an agrarian crisis as central to Mughal decline. Others, such as C. A. Bayly, saw the eighteenth century as a time of regional growth, not collapse.
- Historians disagree about Aurangzeb: some portray a bigot, others (such as Audrey Truschke) stress that his actions were driven by politics and were less sweeping than the older picture.

## A worked example

**Question: "Evaluate the reasons for the decline of Mughal power."**

1. State a thesis that ranks the causes, for example that structural financial strain made the empire vulnerable and the Company exploited it.
2. Examine Aurangzeb's long Deccan war and the cost to the treasury.
3. Examine succession struggles and noble factions after 1707.
4. Examine external blows: Nadir Shah (1739) and Ahmad Shah Durrani (1761).
5. Examine the Company: its army, finance and alliances after Plassey and Buxar.
6. Evaluate historians' views of decline against those of regional growth.
7. Reach a judgement that links the causes.

## Common mistakes

- Writing a long narrative of emperors without answering the question.
- Describing Akbar's Din-i Ilahi as an official religion.
- Calling 1857 only a "mutiny" or only a "war of independence" without discussing the debate.
- Omitting the Suri interval.
- Treating Aurangzeb as the only cause of decline.
