## A1: Notation in one table

| Long form | Write it as |
|---|---|
| a × b | ab |
| y + y + y, or 3 × y | 3y |
| a × a, a × a × a | a², a³ |
| a × a × b | a²b |
| a ÷ b | a/b |
| 0.5x, 0.25y | ½x (or x/2), ¼y (or y/4) |
| 2 × (a + b) | 2(a + b) |

Answers are expected in **simplest form without being told**.

## A2: Substitution in three steps

1. Write the expression again with each letter replaced by its value **in brackets**.
2. Powers first, then × and ÷, then + and −.
3. Give the units if the formula has them.

(−4)² = 16, but −4² = −16. Brackets decide which you mean.

Unfamiliar formulae are given in the question. The specification lists the kinematics formulae (v = u + at, s = ut + ½at², v² = u² + 2as) among those given in the exam. The quadratic formula, circle formulae and Pythagoras' theorem are on the list you must learn.

## A3: The key words

- **Term**: one part of an expression, such as −5x in 2x² − 5x.
- **Expression**: terms with no equals sign.
- **Equation**: true for particular values only.
- **Formula**: a rule connecting variables, such as v = d/t.
- **Inequality**: uses <, >, ≤ or ≥.
- **Identity**: true for **all** values; symbol ≡.
- **Factor**: divides exactly into the expression.

## A4: Manipulation rules

### Index laws

| Rule | Example |
|---|---|
| aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ | x⁴ × x⁵ = x⁹ |
| aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ | x⁹ ÷ x² = x⁷ |
| (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ | (x³)⁵ = x¹⁵ |
| a⁰ = 1 | 7⁰ = 1 |
| a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ | x⁻² = 1/x² |

Deal with the numbers first, then each letter. 6x⁵ × 4x² = 24x⁷: multiply 6 × 4, add the powers 5 + 2. Zero and negative indices are additional foundation content (N7); fractional indices are Higher tier only (N7).

### Method: expanding two binomials

1. First × first, first × second, second × first, second × second.
2. Collect the two middle terms.
3. Check the sign of the last term: (+)(−) gives −.

```
(4x − 1)(x + 3) = 4x² + 12x − x − 3 = 4x² + 11x − 3
```

A squared bracket always has a middle term: (x + k)² = x² + 2kx + k².

### Method: factorising x² + bx + c

1. List factor pairs of c.
2. Pick the pair that adds to b.
3. Write (x + p)(x + q) and expand to check.

| Sign pattern | Brackets |
|---|---|
| +b, +c | both + |
| −b, +c | both − |
| c negative | one +, one − |

**Difference of two squares:** a² − b² = (a + b)(a − b). It has no middle term, and there is no "sum of two squares" rule.

### Higher tier only: factorising ax² + bx + c

1. Work out a × c.
2. Find two numbers with product ac and sum b.
3. Split bx into those two terms.
4. Factorise each pair; the bracket must repeat.

### Higher tier only: products of three binomials

Expand any two brackets, simplify, then multiply each term of the result by the third bracket. Expect up to six terms before collecting.

### Higher tier only: algebraic fractions

| Task | Method |
|---|---|
| Simplify | Factorise top and bottom fully, cancel common brackets |
| Add or subtract | Common denominator; multiply out the numerators; simplify |
| Multiply | Factorise, cancel, then multiply across |
| Divide | Flip the second fraction, then multiply |

Cancel **factors** (whole brackets), never single terms.

### Surds inside brackets

Expand as normal and use √k × √k = k. Collect whole numbers together and surd terms together.

## A5: Changing the subject

1. Clear fractions by multiplying both sides.
2. Expand any brackets.
3. Collect terms containing the new subject on one side, everything else on the other.
4. If the subject appears in two terms, factorise it out.
5. Divide, then take a root if the subject was squared.

The new subject should end up alone on one side, with no copy of it on the other side.

## A6: Equation, identity, argument, proof

| | What it is | How you show it |
|---|---|---|
| Equation | True for some values | Solve it |
| Identity | True for all values | Simplify one side until it matches the other |
| Argument | Algebra backs up a claim | Write the general case and simplify |
| Proof (Higher tier only) | Shows a claim holds in every case | General terms, full algebra, concluding sentence |

**Building blocks for proof:** any integer n; even 2n; odd 2n + 1; consecutive integers n, n + 1; consecutive even numbers 2n, 2n + 2; consecutive odd numbers 2n + 1, 2n + 3. A multiple of k is written k × (an integer expression).

## A7: Functions

Both tiers: a function machine turns an input into an output. Reverse a machine by doing the inverse operations in reverse order.

### Higher tier only

| Notation | Meaning | How to find it |
|---|---|---|
| f(3) | Output when the input is 3 | Substitute 3 for x |
| f⁻¹(x) | Inverse function: undoes f | Write y = f(x), make x the subject, swap back to x |
| fg(x) | Composite: g first, then f | Put g(x) into f in place of x |
| gf(x) | f first, then g | Put f(x) into g |

fg(x) and gf(x) are usually different.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Equation vs identity**: = for some values, ≡ for all values.
- **Expression vs equation**: no equals sign vs an equals sign.
- **Expand vs factorise**: remove brackets vs put them in.
- **(ab)² vs ab²**: a²b² vs a × b × b.
- **fg(x) vs gf(x)**: the inner function acts first.
- **f⁻¹(x) vs 1/f(x)**: the inverse function is not the reciprocal.

## Quick self-test

1. Write k × k × k × m ÷ 3 in simplest form.
2. Find the value of 3a² − ab when a = −2 and b = 5.
3. Name each: (i) 5t − 2, (ii) 5t − 2 = 13, (iii) 2(t + 1) ≡ 2t + 2, (iv) v = d/t.
4. Simplify (3m²n)³.
5. Expand and simplify (x − 5)(x + 8).
6. Factorise x² + 9x + 20.
7. Factorise p² − 64.
8. Make u the subject of v² = u² + 2as.
9. Show that (x + 4)² − 8x ≡ x² + 16.
10. (Higher) Factorise 2x² − 7x + 3.
11. (Higher) Simplify (x² + 5x)/(x² − 25).
12. (Higher) f(x) = 2x + 7 and g(x) = x². Find f⁻¹(x), gf(1) and fg(x).

### Answers

1. k³m/3
2. 3(−2)² − (−2)(5) = 12 + 10 = **22**
3. (i) expression, (ii) equation, (iii) identity, (iv) formula
4. **27m⁶n³** (3³ = 27; powers 2 × 3 = 6 and 1 × 3 = 3)
5. x² + 8x − 5x − 40 = **x² + 3x − 40**
6. 4 × 5 = 20 and 4 + 5 = 9, so **(x + 4)(x + 5)**
7. **(p + 8)(p − 8)**
8. u² = v² − 2as, so **u = ±√(v² − 2as)**
9. (x + 4)² = x² + 8x + 16; subtract 8x to get x² + 16 ✓
10. ac = 6; −6 and −1 have product 6 and sum −7. 2x² − 6x − x + 3 = 2x(x − 3) − 1(x − 3) = **(2x − 1)(x − 3)**
11. x(x + 5)/((x + 5)(x − 5)) = **x/(x − 5)**
12. **f⁻¹(x) = (x − 7)/2**; f(1) = 9, so **gf(1) = 81**; **fg(x) = 2x² + 7**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving an answer like 3 × a × b or 0.5x when the specification expects simplest form, ab-style notation and fraction coefficients.
- Substituting a negative without brackets, so a squared negative comes out negative.
- Multiplying the numbers but forgetting to apply the power to the coefficient: (3m²n)³ written as 3m⁶n³.
- Expanding a squared bracket as two squares with no middle term.
- Factorising only partly: 10p²q − 15pq² written as 5p(2pq − 3q²) instead of 5pq(2p − 3q).
- Writing (x − 4)(x − 5) for an expression whose last term is negative; check the signs by expanding.
- In a rearrangement, dividing only one term by the coefficient instead of the whole side.
- Cancelling x from x/(x − 5) to get 1/(−5): only whole factors cancel (Higher).
- Working out fg(x) by putting g into f the wrong way round (Higher).
- Giving numerical examples instead of a general argument on a "prove" question (Higher).

## Frequently asked questions

**Why do brackets matter when substituting negative numbers?**
Without brackets the power may only act on the digit. (−4)² = 16, but −4² = −16, so always put substituted values in brackets.

**How do you factorise x² + bx + c?**
Find the factor pair of c that adds to b, write (x + p)(x + q), then expand to check. For example, x² + 9x + 20 = (x + 4)(x + 5).

**What is the difference of two squares?**
a² − b² = (a + b)(a − b). It has no middle term, and there is no equivalent factorisation for a sum of two squares.

**Is f⁻¹(x) the same as 1/f(x)?**
No. f⁻¹(x) is the inverse function, which reverses f, while 1/f(x) is the reciprocal. Inverse functions are Higher tier content in AQA GCSE Maths 8300.
