This guide goes deeper into **section 7.2 Business communication** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Business 9609 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. It is A Level content, so it is examined through the case studies on Paper 3 and the essays on Paper 4, with AS content assumed.

The broader [organisational structure and business communication guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-organisational-structure-and-business-communication/) summarises each method, channel and barrier in a table. This page builds the reasoning behind those tables: how a message actually travels, how to pick a method for a given situation, and how to argue about management's role. The phrase "in a given situation" is the key: marks beyond knowledge come from fitting a method, channel or remedy to the business in the case.

## How communication works within a business

Every act of communication has the same parts, which let you say precisely *where* a message went wrong.

1. **Sender**: the person or department with information to pass on.
2. **Message**: the content, which the sender must put into words, figures or images the receiver can understand.
3. **Method (medium)**: spoken, written, electronic or visual.
4. **Channel**: the route through the organisation, for example down the chain of command or across between departments.
5. **Receiver**: the person who must understand and act on the message.
6. **Feedback**: the receiver's response, which shows whether the message was understood.

Anything that distorts the message along the way is often called **noise**: a noisy factory floor, an unclear sentence, a distracted receiver or a message buried among fifty others.

**Diagnosing a failure.** A delivery firm, Calderbrook Logistics, emails drivers a revised route at 6 a.m. Half the drivers follow the old route. Use the process to find the fault. The sender wrote the message clearly. The method (email) suited a detailed route, but drivers check their phones only at breaks, so the receiver did not see it in time. No feedback was requested, so the depot did not know until deliveries were late. The remedy follows directly: a short spoken briefing at the depot before departure, with drivers confirming the change, and the email kept as the record.

## 7.2.1 Situations in which communication is essential

The syllabus does not list the situations, so justify each one by its consequence if communication fails.

- **Health, safety and legal duties.** A missed safety instruction can cause injury and legal action.
- **Change.** Restructuring, relocation or new technology. Without clear explanation, uncertainty turns into rumour and resistance.
- **Coordination.** Sales must tell production what has been promised; production must tell purchasing what materials are needed. A gap here causes stock-outs or idle capacity.
- **Performance and motivation.** Staff need targets and feedback to know whether they are succeeding.
- **Crisis.** A product recall or system failure needs fast, consistent messages to staff, customers and the media.
- **External stakeholders.** Customers, suppliers, lenders and shareholders all need accurate information to keep trusting the business.

## 7.2.2 Choosing a method for the situation

Rather than memorising strengths and weaknesses as lists, test each message against five questions:

| Question | Points towards |
|---|---|
| Is it urgent? | Spoken or electronic |
| Is it complex or detailed? | Written, backed by visual for figures |
| Is it sensitive or likely to upset people? | Face-to-face spoken first |
| Is a permanent record needed? | Written or electronic |
| How many receivers, and how spread out? | Electronic for large or dispersed audiences |

Most real situations need a **combination**. Spoken methods win understanding and allow questions; written or electronic methods provide the record and the detail.

**Worked example: three messages at Penhallow Foods.** Penhallow Foods runs two factories, 40 kilometres apart.

- *A new hygiene rule starting tomorrow.* Urgent and safety-related, with every operative affected. A short spoken briefing at each shift start, with a visual poster at hand-wash points, gives speed and a constant reminder. A written copy goes into the procedures file as the record.
- *A three-year plan to close the older factory.* Highly sensitive and complex. A face-to-face meeting led by a director allows questions and shows respect; a written summary afterwards prevents misremembering; regular electronic updates keep staff informed as plans develop.
- *Monthly output and waste figures.* Not urgent but numerical. A visual dashboard on the intranet and on screens in the canteen makes trends easy to see; a supervisor comments on it at team meetings so that the numbers are explained.

## 7.2.3 Channels: what goes wrong and why

**One-way and two-way.** One-way communication is quick and cheap to send, and suits simple, routine information. Its weakness is that the sender cannot check understanding. Two-way communication takes longer and costs more staff time, but feedback reveals misunderstanding and involves staff, which links to motivation theory (Mayo's emphasis on attention and belonging, for example).

**Vertical communication** runs downwards (instructions, targets, policies) and upwards (reports, suggestions, complaints). Two problems are worth naming:

- **Distortion over many levels.** Each level summarises and reinterprets. As an illustration only: if each of four levels passes on 90% of the meaning accurately, the operative receives 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.9 = 0.656, about 66% of the original. Tall structures therefore suffer more distortion than flat ones.
- **Filtering of upward messages.** Staff may tell managers what they want to hear, especially in an autocratic culture, so senior managers act on an over-optimistic picture.

**Horizontal communication** runs between people at the same level, for example the marketing manager and the operations manager. It speeds coordination, but in a functional structure departments can become isolated, each pursuing its own targets. A matrix structure or cross-department project teams can strengthen horizontal links, though staff with two bosses may receive conflicting messages.

## 7.2.4 Barriers, grouped by where they arise

Grouping barriers helps you choose a matching remedy.

| Source | Example barrier | Matching remedy |
|---|---|---|
| Sender | Unclear wording, jargon, too much detail | Plain language; one main point per message |
| Method or channel | Wrong method; too many levels; technical failure | Match method to message; shorten the chain; a backup method |
| Receiver | Overload, lack of interest, mistrust of the sender | Prioritised messages; explain why it matters to them; consistent honesty |
| Organisation | Distance and time zones; departments that never meet | Scheduled video meetings; shared platforms; cross-department teams |

A remedy earns credit only if it removes the barrier the case shows: "more meetings" worsens information overload.

## 7.2.5 The role of management

**Informal communication** (the "grapevine") is any communication outside the formal channels: conversations at break, private messaging groups, chats after work. It cannot be abolished, so management's choice is whether to work with it. It spreads news quickly, builds relationships and tells managers what staff really think. It also spreads rumour, especially when formal communication is slow. The best defence against harmful rumour is early, honest formal communication.

**Communication and efficiency.** Good communication reduces errors and rework, speeds decisions, avoids duplicated work and lowers labour turnover. These effects can be measured.

**Worked example: Ashgrove Wholesale.** The warehouse picks 2,400 orders a week. 3% contain errors from misread instructions, each costing $18 to correct.

```
Errors now:      2,400 x 0.03 = 72 a week; cost 72 x 18 = $1,296
After change:    2,400 x 0.01 = 24 a week; cost 24 x 18 = $432
Gross saving:    1,296 - 432 = $864 a week
Briefing cost:   10 staff x 0.25 hours x 5 days x $16 = $200 a week
Net saving:      864 - 200 = $664 a week; over 50 weeks = $33,200
```

The change is a daily 15-minute two-way briefing plus picture-based pick sheets. The figures support it, but an evaluation should add that the error rate after the change is a forecast, and that the briefing also gives staff a channel for raising problems, a benefit the figures do not capture.

**Improving communication in a given situation.** Diagnose first, then recommend: identify the barrier, choose the method and channel that remove it, add a feedback check, and say how management will know it has worked.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Advise Ashgrove Wholesale on how to improve communication with warehouse staff.*

> "The main barrier is that instructions reach staff only as printed lists, which is one-way, so pickers who misread an item code have no chance to check it. I advise a short daily briefing, because it is two-way and supervisors can confirm understanding before picking starts. This should cut the 3% error rate, saving up to $664 a week after the cost of staff time. However, if the misreading comes from poorly printed codes, the briefing alone will not fix it, so the pick sheets should also show product pictures. The briefing is the priority because it also lets staff report problems upwards."

Why it works: it names the barrier, matches the remedy, uses the figure, states a condition and gives a priority, which is what "advise" asks for.

## Common mistakes

- Describing the four methods in general instead of judging which suits the message in the case.
- Confusing a **method** (email) with a **channel** (the route, such as vertical downward).
- Suggesting a remedy that does not match the barrier identified.

## Exam technique

| Command word | What it needs here |
|---|---|
| Explain | A reason and a consequence, such as why two-way communication reduces errors |
| Analyse | A chain from the case: barrier, effect on staff, effect on efficiency or costs |
| Advise | A recommended course of action with justification for this business |
| Evaluate | Both sides, then a judgement on what the success of the change depends on |
| Assess | An informed judgement, for example of how far poor communication caused a problem |

When a case lists several problems, separate the communication problems from others, such as poor equipment.
