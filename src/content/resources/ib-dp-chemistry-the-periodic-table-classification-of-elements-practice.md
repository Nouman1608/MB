This set tests **Structure 3.1, The periodic table: classification of elements**, based on the IB *Chemistry guide* for the Diploma Programme (February 2023 edition, first examined in 2025). Questions 1 to 7 cover understandings 3.1.1 to 3.1.6, which apply at SL and HL. Questions 8 to 11 test the additional higher level understandings 3.1.7 to 3.1.10, so each carries an **HL only** label. It applies from 2025 onwards.

## Questions

**1.** Element X is in period 3 and group 14 of the periodic table.

**(a)** State the block that X belongs to. **[1]**
**(b)** Deduce the full electron configuration of X. **[1]**
**(c)** Classify X as a metal, a metalloid or a non-metal. **[1]**

**2.** An atom has the condensed electron configuration [Ar] 3d³ 4s².

**(a)** Identify the element, and state its period and group. **[2]**
**(b)** State which of these classifications applies to it: alkali metal, halogen, transition element, noble gas. **[1]**

**3.** Explain each observation.

**(a)** Atomic radius decreases from sodium to chlorine across period 3. **[2]**
**(b)** A magnesium ion, Mg²⁺, is smaller than a magnesium atom. **[2]**
**(c)** Electronegativity decreases from fluorine to iodine. **[2]**

**4.** A small piece of rubidium is dropped into a trough of water.

**(a)** Give a balanced equation for what happens, including state symbols. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why rubidium reacts more vigorously than potassium. **[3]**
**(c)** Universal indicator is added to the water afterwards. State and explain what it shows. **[1]**

**5.** Aqueous bromine is added to two test tubes, one holding potassium iodide solution and one holding potassium chloride solution.

**(a)** Describe the observation in each of the two tubes. **[2]**
**(b)** Write the ionic equation for the change in the iodide tube. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain, in terms of atomic structure, the difference between the two tubes. **[2]**

**6.** Oxides and the environment.

**(a)** Write equations for the reactions with water of caesium oxide and of sulfur dioxide. **[2]**
**(b)** Aluminium oxide reacts with both hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide solution. State the term for this behaviour. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain, using equations, how a rise in atmospheric carbon dioxide lowers the pH of seawater and affects organisms that build carbonate shells. **[4]**
**(d)** State how the acid–base character of the oxides changes across period 3, and link it to metallic character. **[1]**

**7.** Deduce the oxidation state of the named atom in each species.

**(a)** Hydrogen in SrH₂ **[1]**
**(b)** Oxygen in BaO₂ (barium peroxide) **[1]**
**(c)** Vanadium in VO₃⁻ **[1]**
**(d)** Chromium in Cr₂O₇²⁻ **[1]**
**(e)** Explain why the oxidation state of oxygen in ozone, O₃, is zero. **[1]**
**(f)** Give names, using Roman numerals for the oxidation state, for KNO₂ and Na₂SO₃. **[2]**

**8.** **HL only.** The first ionization energy of sulfur is lower than that of phosphorus.

**(a)** Draw orbital diagrams (boxes or lines) for the 3s and 3p electrons of a phosphorus atom and a sulfur atom. **[2]**
**(b)** Use your diagrams to explain the lower value for sulfur, and say why this is evidence for sublevels. **[2]**

**9.** **HL only.** Transition elements.

**(a)** State four characteristic properties of transition elements. **[2]**
**(b)** Zinc sits in the d-block. Explain why it does not count as a transition element. **[2]**
**(c)** State what is meant by a ligand. **[1]**

**10.** **HL only.** The first four ionization energies of chromium are, in kJ mol⁻¹ (use these values): 653, 1591, 2987, 4743.

**(a)** Use the data to explain why chromium forms ions with several different charges. **[3]**
**(b)** Deduce the electron configurations of Cr²⁺ and Mn⁴⁺. **[2]**
**(c)** State the oxidation state of manganese in MnO₂ and name the compound. **[1]**

**11.** **HL only.** Use this colour wheel (approximate ranges): violet 400–450 nm, blue 450–490 nm, green 490–560 nm, yellow 560–590 nm, orange 590–630 nm, red 630–700 nm. Complementary pairs: red and green, orange and blue, yellow and violet. Take c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.

**(a)** Write the electron configuration of Fe²⁺. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why complexes of Fe²⁺ in solution are coloured. **[3]**
**(c)** A solution of a different complex absorbs light most strongly at a frequency of 4.80 × 10¹⁴ s⁻¹. Calculate the wavelength absorbed, in nm. **[2]**
**(d)** Deduce the colour of the solution in (c). **[1]**
**(e)** Explain why solutions of zinc compounds are colourless. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** p-block (the last electron enters 3p) [1]
**(b)** 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p² (14 electrons; this is silicon) [1]
**(c)** Metalloid [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* When a full configuration is asked for, write every sublevel; [Ne] 3s² 3p² may not earn the mark.

**2. (a)** Vanadium (Z = 18 + 5 = 23) [1]; period 4, group 5 (two 4s plus three 3d electrons) [1]
**(b)** Transition element [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Count both the 4s and the 3d electrons to find the group: 3d³ 4s² puts the element in group 5, not group 3.

**3. (a)** Nuclear charge increases across the period [1]; electrons enter the same main level with similar shielding, so they are pulled closer to the nucleus [1]
**(b)** Mg²⁺ has lost its two 3s electrons, so it has one fewer occupied main level [1]; the remaining electrons are held closer by the same nuclear charge (12 protons, now 10 electrons) [1]
**(c)** Down the group the atom has more occupied main levels, so the bonding pair is further from the nucleus [1]; more shielding by inner electrons means the shared pair is attracted less strongly [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* "More protons" alone rarely earns full credit in (a); pair it with the same-level, similar-shielding point.

**4. (a)** 2Rb(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2RbOH(aq) + H₂(g): correct formulae [1]; balanced with state symbols [1]
**(b)** Rubidium has an extra occupied main level, so its outer electron is further from the nucleus [1]; it is more shielded by inner electrons [1]; less energy is needed to remove it, so Rb forms Rb⁺ more readily [1]
**(c)** Blue/purple, showing an alkaline solution (pH above 7) because hydroxide ions form [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b), saying rubidium "has more electrons" without linking distance and shielding to ease of electron loss is likely to score little.

**5. (a)** Potassium iodide: the solution turns brown (iodine forms) [1]; potassium chloride: no change, it stays the yellow-orange colour of the added bromine [1]
**(b)** Br₂(aq) + 2I⁻(aq) → 2Br⁻(aq) + I₂(aq) [1]
**(c)** A bromine atom is smaller than an iodine atom, so it attracts an extra electron more strongly and can oxidize I⁻ [1]; a chlorine atom is smaller still and attracts electrons more strongly than bromine, so bromine cannot remove electrons from Cl⁻ [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* "Brown" must describe the iodine that forms; describing only the colour of the reagent added does not show a reaction.

**6. (a)** Cs₂O(s) + H₂O(l) → 2CsOH(aq) [1]; SO₂(g) + H₂O(l) → H₂SO₃(aq) [1]
**(b)** Amphoteric [1]
**(c)** More CO₂ dissolves: CO₂(g) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₂CO₃(aq) [1]; H₂CO₃(aq) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + HCO₃⁻(aq), so [H⁺] rises and pH falls [1]; H⁺(aq) + CO₃²⁻(aq) → HCO₃⁻(aq) [1]; less carbonate is left for organisms to make calcium carbonate shells [1]
**(d)** Oxides change from basic to amphoteric to acidic as metallic character decreases across the period [1] **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* In (c), equilibrium arrows are expected for the CO₂ and carbonic acid steps; a one-way arrow may cost the mark.

**7. (a)** −1 (strontium is +2; a metal hydride) [1]
**(b)** −1 (barium is +2; a peroxide) [1]
**(c)** x + 3(−2) = −1, so **+5** [1]
**(d)** 2x + 7(−2) = −2, so **+6** [1]
**(e)** O₃ is an element: each oxygen bonds only to other oxygen atoms, so no electrons are transferred [1]
**(f)** Potassium nitrate(III) (or potassium nitrite) [1]; sodium sulfate(IV) (or sodium sulfite) [1] **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Write oxidation states as −1 and +5, sign first; "1−" and "5+" read as ion charges and may not be accepted.

**8. (a)** P: 3s [↑↓], 3p [↑][↑][↑] [1]; S: 3s [↑↓], 3p [↑↓][↑][↑], with opposite spins in the paired orbital [1]
**(b)** The electron removed from sulfur is one of a pair in a 3p orbital; repulsion between the paired electrons raises its energy, so less energy is needed to remove it [1]; electrons in the same main level therefore differ in energy, which shows that sublevels and orbitals exist [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers that rely on "half-filled stability" of phosphorus, without the energy of the electron removed, are unlikely to gain the explanation mark.

**9. (a)** Any two of: variable oxidation states, high melting points, magnetic properties, catalytic properties, coloured compounds, complex ion formation [1]; two more from the list [1]
**(b)** A transition element has an incomplete d-sublevel [1]; zinc has 3d¹⁰ in the atom and in its only ion, Zn²⁺, so the d-sublevel is always full [1]
**(c)** A molecule or ion with a lone pair that it donates to a central metal ion, forming a coordination bond [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b), mention the ion as well as the atom; the atom alone does not rule zinc out.

**10. (a)** The increases are 938, 1396 and 1756 kJ mol⁻¹: each value rises steadily with no very large jump [1]; this is because the 4s and 3d electrons are close in energy [1]; so the energy cost of losing two, three or more electrons is similar in scale, allowing several stable oxidation states [1]
**(b)** Cr²⁺: [Ar] 3d⁴ [1]; Mn⁴⁺: [Ar] 3d³ [1]
**(c)** +4; manganese(IV) oxide [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote figures from the data in (a); a general statement with no reference to the values given is unlikely to earn the first mark.

**11. (a)** [Ar] 3d⁶ [1]
**(b)** Ligands split the d-sublevel into two sets of orbitals of different energy [1]; a d electron absorbs a photon of visible light whose energy matches the gap and is promoted [1]; the colour seen is complementary to the colour absorbed [1]
**(c)** λ = c / f = (3.00 × 10⁸) / (4.80 × 10¹⁴) [1] = 6.25 × 10⁻⁷ m = **625 nm** [1]
**(d)** 625 nm is orange, so the solution looks **blue** [1]
**(e)** Zn²⁺ is [Ar] 3d¹⁰: the d-sublevel is full [1], so no electron can be promoted between split d orbitals and no visible light is absorbed [1] **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* In (d), naming the absorbed colour (orange) as the colour seen is a common way to lose the mark.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing a condensed configuration when a full one is asked for.
- Explaining radius or electronegativity trends with nuclear charge alone, without shielding or main levels.
- Writing CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ as one-way, or leaving out the carbonate step in ocean acidification.
- Using −2 for oxygen in a peroxide and +1 for hydrogen in a metal hydride.
- (HL) Removing 3d electrons before 4s in ion configurations.
- (HL) Giving the absorbed colour as the observed colour.
- (HL) Leaving nm in c = λf, or giving no unit for the final wavelength.
