Each question uses one of the specification's command words at its published International AS tariff: **define** (2), **calculate** (4), **explain** (4), **analyse** (6), **discuss** (8) and **assess** (10). All businesses and figures are invented.

## Sources

> **Source 1: Brightwater Hotel**
>
> Brightwater is a 120-room hotel with 140 staff. Demand is highly seasonal,
> so 45 staff are on zero-hour contracts. Housekeepers are paid $4.50 for
> each room cleaned, plus a $60 weekly bonus if guest cleanliness scores
> stay above 90%. Many zero-hour staff leave within a year, saying their
> income is unpredictable and they do not feel part of the team. The
> manager plans to multi-skill 30 staff so they can work on reception,
> in the restaurant and in housekeeping. Each person would attend a
> three-day off-the-job course costing $120 a day, and cover for each
> person absent would cost $95 a day.
>
> **Source 2: Tervo Software**
>
> Tervo was founded by Mira Tervo with 12 staff and now employs 150. Mira
> still approves every project plan and client price herself, and
> developers complain that decisions take weeks. Average salary is
> $40,000. The board proposes performance-related pay: after an annual
> appraisal, staff rated 1 to 4 would receive pay rises of 0%, 2%, 4% or
> 6% respectively. Managers expect 20 staff to be rated 1, 70 rated 2, 45
> rated 3 and 15 rated 4. The board is also considering a matrix structure,
> with project teams drawn from design, coding and testing.

## Section A

Questions 1 to 6 refer to Source 1.

**1.** Define the term "multi-skilling". **[2]**

**2.** A housekeeper cleans 14 rooms a day for 5 days and meets the cleanliness target. Calculate the housekeeper's pay for the week. **[4]**

**3.** Explain **one** benefit to Brightwater of employing staff on zero-hour contracts. **[4]**

**4.** Calculate the total cost of the multi-skilling programme. **[4]**

**5.** Explain **one** reason why Brightwater might choose off-the-job rather than on-the-job training for multi-skilling. **[4]**

**6.** Analyse how Maslow's hierarchy of needs could explain the high staff turnover among Brightwater's zero-hour staff. **[6]**

## Section B

Questions 7 to 12 refer to Source 2.

**7.** Define the term "laissez-faire leadership". **[2]**

**8.** Calculate the expected annual cost of the pay rises under the performance-related pay scheme. **[4]**

**9.** Explain **one** difference between management and leadership, using Tervo as an example. **[4]**

**10.** Analyse the possible impact on Tervo's efficiency of introducing a matrix structure. **[6]**

**11.** Discuss whether performance-related pay is likely to improve the performance of Tervo's developers. **[8]**

**12.** Assess whether Mira Tervo should move from an autocratic to a democratic leadership style. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Training employees [1] so that each can carry out several different jobs or tasks within the business [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Rooms cleaned = 14 × 5 = 70 [1]. Piecework pay = 70 × $4.50 [1] = $315 [1]. With the $60 bonus, weekly pay = **$375** [1]. **[4]**

**3.** Demand at Brightwater is highly seasonal [1], so with zero-hour contracts the hotel can call in staff only when rooms are full [1] and pays nothing in quiet weeks [1], keeping wage costs in line with revenue and protecting profit margins in the off-season [1]. **[4]**

**4.** Person-days = 30 × 3 = 90 [1]. Cost per person-day = $120 + $95 = $215 [1]. Total = 90 × $215 [1] = **$19,350** [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Staff must learn three different jobs, including reception systems and restaurant service [1]. An off-the-job course can teach these skills in a structured way without guests watching mistakes [1], whereas learning on the job in a busy hotel could lead to errors in front of guests [1], which would damage the cleanliness and service scores Brightwater relies on [1]. **[4]**

**6.** Maslow argued that lower-level needs must be met before higher ones motivate [1]. Zero-hour staff say their income is unpredictable, so their **safety** need for security is not met [1], which means they are likely to move to any job offering guaranteed hours [1]. They also say they do not feel part of the team, so their **social** needs are unmet [1]: working irregular shifts means they rarely build relationships with colleagues [1]. Since two lower levels of the hierarchy are unsatisfied, rewards such as praise or promotion would have little effect, which helps explain why many leave within a year [1]. **[6]**

**7.** A leadership style in which the leader sets broad objectives [1] and leaves employees to make most decisions about how to achieve them [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Rated 1: 20 × $0 = $0 [1]. Rated 2: 70 × 2% × $40,000 = $56,000 [1]. Rated 3: 45 × 4% × $40,000 = $72,000 [1]. Rated 4: 15 × 6% × $40,000 = $36,000. Total = **$164,000** a year [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Management is about planning, organising and controlling resources [1], such as Mira approving project plans and prices to keep costs under control [1]. Leadership is about setting direction and inspiring people to follow it [1]; with 150 staff, Tervo needs Mira to set a clear vision and let others manage the detail [1]. **[4]**

**10.** A matrix structure would put designers, coders and testers in one project team [1], so work no longer has to pass between separate departments [1], which could cut delays and errors when a project moves from coding to testing [1]. This could help with the slow decisions developers complain about [1]. However, each employee would report to both a project manager and a department head [1], and conflicting instructions could waste time and reduce efficiency unless priorities are clearly agreed [1]. **[6]**

**11.** **For:** PRP rewards staff whose appraisal shows strong performance [1], so developers have a financial reason to meet targets [1]. It costs about $164,000 a year, but rewards are concentrated on higher performers rather than spread equally [1].
**Against:** software development is creative team work, so individual appraisal ratings may seem unfair [1]; Herzberg would argue pay is a hygiene factor and will not create lasting motivation [1]. The developers' main complaint is slow decisions, not pay [1], so PRP does not tackle the cause of frustration [1].
**Judgement:** PRP may help retain the best developers, but performance is more likely to improve if Mira delegates decisions, since that addresses what staff actually complain about [1]. **[8]**

**12.** **For a democratic style:** with 150 staff, Mira approving every plan creates a bottleneck in the chain of command [1], so decisions take weeks [1]. Developers are skilled professionals who are likely to want a say [1], so involving them could improve motivation and retention [1]. Their expertise could also improve the quality of decisions on project plans [1].
**Against:** democratic decision making can be slower when many people are consulted [1]; Mira's control over client prices may have protected margins as the firm grew [1]; and staff used to being told what to do may need training before they can take on decisions [1].
**Judgement:** Mira should move towards a democratic style for project decisions while keeping control of key areas such as pricing at first [1], because the source shows the current style is already causing delays and frustration; the change depends on her willingness to delegate, which is the classic difficulty of moving from entrepreneur to leader [1]. **[10]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Forgetting to add the bonus in pay calculations, or applying a percentage to the wrong base.
- Mixing up on-the-job and off-the-job training.
- Listing all five of Maslow's levels instead of applying the relevant ones to the source.
- Treating PRP as automatically motivating without considering Herzberg or the real cause of the problem.
- Recommending a leadership style without linking it to staff skills, business size or the delay described in the source.
