## Formulae to recall

Appendix B of the specification lists these as formulae you must be able to recall.

| Result | Formula | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Weight | W = mg | W in N, m in kg |
| Newton's second law | F = ma | F is the **resultant** force |
| Friction | F ≤ μR | R is the normal reaction |

Other results you use constantly (they follow from the above):

| Situation | Result |
|---|---|
| Component of force P at angle θ to a direction | P cos θ along, P sin θ across |
| Particle on smooth plane at angle α | a = g sin α down the plane |
| Normal reaction on a plane at α (no other perpendicular forces) | R = mg cos α |
| On the point of slipping on a plane at α (weight, R and friction only) | μ = tan α |
| Equilibrium | resultant = 0 in every direction |

## The three laws in one line each

- **First law:** no resultant force ⇔ at rest or constant velocity.
- **Second law:** resultant force = mass × acceleration, in the same direction.
- **Third law:** A on B is equal and opposite to B on A; the pair act on **different** bodies.

## About g (R3)

The specification says g may be assumed constant and is used to varying degrees of accuracy. It is not a universal constant: it depends on location. The inverse square law is not required. Use the value in the question, and round answers to match it.

## Method: F = ma problems

1. Draw a clear diagram. Mark weight, normal reaction, tension, friction, driving force and resistances.
2. Mark the acceleration with a double arrow.
3. Resolve perpendicular to the motion: the total there is zero. This usually gives R.
4. Resolve along the motion: resultant = ma.
5. If friction acts and the body moves or is about to move, use F = μR.
6. Solve, then check the direction and size make sense.

## Method: connected particles and pulleys

1. Model: string light and inextensible, pulley smooth → same tension on both sides, same size of acceleration.
2. Write F = ma for **each** particle in its own direction of motion.
3. Add the equations to remove T, find a, then substitute back for T.
4. For a rigid tow bar, find a from the whole system, then T from the rear body alone.
5. When a string goes slack or breaks, the tension becomes zero: start a new stage with new equations.

## Method: equilibrium of a particle

1. Choose two perpendicular directions (often horizontal and vertical, or along and across a slope).
2. Resolve every force in each direction.
3. Set each total to zero and solve the pair of equations.
4. With forces as vectors, add the vectors and set the sum to 0.

## Method: friction decisions

1. Find R, then the maximum friction μR.
2. Compare it with the force trying to cause motion.
3. Smaller than μR → stays at rest, friction equals that force.
4. Equal → limiting equilibrium, on the point of moving.
5. Larger → it moves; friction = μR, against the motion.

## Worked reminders

**A. Push downwards at an angle on a rough floor.** A crate of mass 10 kg is pushed by a force of 50 N at 30° **below** the horizontal; μ = 0.3.

```
R = 10g + 50 sin 30° = 98 + 25 = 123 N   (push adds to R)
F_max = 0.3 × 123 = 36.9 N
Forward: 50 cos 30° = 43.30 N > 36.9 N → moves
a = (43.30 − 36.9) ÷ 10 = 0.640 m s⁻²
```

**B. Vertical motion on a rope.** Resultant = T − mg (up positive). Moving at constant speed → T = mg, whether going up or down.

**C. Force on a smooth pulley.** When the two string sections both hang vertically, the string pulls down on the pulley with 2T.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Mass vs weight:** kg vs N; W = mg.
- **Resultant force vs a single force:** only the resultant goes in F = ma.
- **Smooth vs rough:** smooth means no friction (μ = 0).
- **F = μR vs F ≤ μR:** equality only when moving or on the point of moving.
- **Equilibrium vs constant velocity:** both have zero resultant; "at rest" is a special case.
- **Third-law pair vs balanced forces:** a third-law pair acts on two bodies; balanced forces act on one.
- **Tension vs thrust:** a string can only pull; a rod can pull (tension) or push (thrust).
- **Light, inextensible, smooth pulley:** each assumption has a job (see [Overarching themes](/resources/aqa-a-level-mathematics-overarching-themes/)).

## Quick self-test

1. Find the acceleration of a 3 kg particle acted on by a resultant force (6i − 9j) N, and its magnitude.
2. Find the weight of a 2.4 kg mass using g = 9.81 m s⁻².
3. A 12 kg block rests on rough horizontal ground, μ = 0.25. A horizontal force of 20 N acts on it. Find the friction force.
4. The force on the block in Q3 is raised to 36 N. Find its acceleration.
5. A particle slides down a smooth plane inclined at 20°. Find its acceleration.
6. A car of mass 900 kg has driving force 2000 N and total resistance 650 N on a level road. Find its acceleration.
7. A lift of mass 400 kg accelerates **downwards** at 1.2 m s⁻². Find the tension in the cable.
8. Particles of mass 4 kg and 6 kg hang either side of a smooth pulley on a light inextensible string. Find the acceleration and the tension.
9. A block on a rough plane inclined at 15° is on the point of slipping. Find μ.
10. Forces of 5 N along i and 12 N along j act on a particle. Find the magnitude of the resultant and its angle with i.
11. A van moves at constant speed with a driving force of 300 N. What is the total resistance? Which law?
12. A horizontal pull of 40 N gives an 8 kg block on rough horizontal ground an acceleration of 2 m s⁻². Find μ.

### Answers

1. a = (2i − 3j) m s⁻²; |a| = √13 = 3.61 m s⁻².
2. W = 2.4 × 9.81 = 23.5 N.
3. μR = 0.25 × 117.6 = 29.4 N > 20 N, so it stays at rest and **F = 20 N**.
4. a = (36 − 29.4) ÷ 12 = 0.55 m s⁻².
5. a = 9.8 sin 20° = 3.35 m s⁻².
6. a = (2000 − 650) ÷ 900 = 1.5 m s⁻².
7. 400g − T = 400 × 1.2, so T = 400 × 8.6 = 3440 N.
8. a = (6 − 4)g ÷ 10 = 1.96 m s⁻²; T = 4(9.8 + 1.96) = 47.0 N.
9. μ = tan 15° = 0.268.
10. √(5² + 12²) = 13 N; tan⁻¹(12/5) = 67.4°.
11. 300 N, by Newton's first law (zero resultant).
12. Friction = 40 − 8 × 2 = 24 N; R = 78.4 N; μ = 24 ÷ 78.4 = 0.306.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing R = mg on a slope, or when a pulling or pushing force has a vertical component.
- Using F = μR for a body that is at rest and not about to move (Q3 above).
- Leaving out a force on the diagram, most often friction or the normal reaction, and then missing it in the equation.
- Using a single force, not the resultant, in F = ma.
- Sign errors when the body decelerates: keep one positive direction and let a come out negative.
- In pulley problems, using one particle's weight in the other particle's equation.
- Forgetting that tension drops to zero once a string breaks or goes slack.
- Resolving with sin where cos is needed: mark the angle on the diagram first.
- Giving an acceleration without its direction when the question asks for it, or a force without units.
- Rounding intermediate values (such as R) too early, so the final answer is out by more than the last figure.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are Newton's three laws of motion?**
The first law says a body with no resultant force stays at rest or moves at constant velocity. The second says resultant force = mass × acceleration. The third says the force of A on B is equal and opposite to the force of B on A.

**Which mechanics formulae must you recall for AQA 7357?**
Appendix B lists W = mg, F = ma and F ≤ μR among the formulae you must recall.

**When is the normal reaction not equal to mg?**
On a slope, R = mg cos α, and any pushing or pulling force with a component perpendicular to the surface changes R further. Resolve perpendicular to the surface to find it.

**What is the difference between a third-law pair and balanced forces?**
A third-law pair acts on two different bodies, while balanced forces act on the same body.

**What is μ when a block is on the point of slipping on a slope?**
If only weight, the normal reaction and friction act, μ = tan α, where α is the angle of the slope.
