## Questions

**1.** Define the term *indirect cost*. **[2]**

**2.** Norvik Coaches hires out coaches. For each cost, state whether it is fixed or variable and whether it is direct or indirect for a single hire: (a) fuel used on the trip; (b) annual vehicle insurance; (c) the office manager's salary. **[3]**

**3.** Quillgate Bakery bakes 5,000 loaves a month. Fixed costs are £3,000 a month and variable costs are £0.90 a loaf. Loaves sell for £2.10.

**(a)** Calculate total cost. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate average cost per loaf. **[1]**
**(c)** State the marginal cost of one extra loaf, assuming fixed costs do not change. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate monthly profit. **[1]**

**4.** Explain one difference between full costing and contribution costing, and explain why contribution is not the same as profit. **[4]**

**5.** Norvik Coaches runs a day tour. The price is £28 a passenger and the variable cost is £11 a passenger. Fixed costs are £5,100 a week.

**(a)** Calculate the contribution per passenger. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the break-even number of passengers a week. **[2]**
**(c)** Weekly demand is 380 passengers. Calculate the margin of safety and the weekly profit. **[3]**
**(d)** Weekly fixed costs rise by £850. Calculate the new break-even number of passengers. **[2]**

**6.** Brimstone Barbecues makes steel grills. Variable cost is £150 a grill and full cost is £205. Grills sell for £240. A garden retailer offers £170 each for a one-off order of 80 grills. The workshop has spare capacity and fixed costs would not change.

**(a)** Calculate the extra profit from accepting the order. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the order could be accepted although £170 is below full cost. **[2]**
**(c)** Recommend whether Brimstone should accept the order. **[4]**

**7.** Quillgate Bakery's full cost for a celebration cake is £18.40. The owner uses cost-plus pricing with a mark-up of 35%.

**(a)** Calculate the selling price. **[2]**
**(b)** Analyse one limitation of cost-plus pricing. **[3]**

**8.** Explain the difference between incremental budgeting and zero budgeting. **[4]**

**9.** Norvik Coaches' monthly budget and actual results are below.

| Item | Budget | Actual |
|---|---|---|
| Sales revenue | £62,000 | £58,400 |
| Fuel | £14,500 | £15,900 |
| Wages | £21,000 | £20,200 |
| Other costs | £9,000 | £9,000 |

**(a)** Calculate the variance for sales revenue, fuel, wages and profit, stating whether each is adverse or favourable. **[4]**
**(b)** Analyse two possible reasons for the fuel variance and suggest one action for management. **[4]**

**10.** Quillgate Bakery budgeted to make 10,000 pies. The ingredients budget was £6,000 (£0.60 a pie) and rent was £2,500. The bakery actually made 12,000 pies. Ingredients cost £7,500 and rent was £2,500.

**(a)** Calculate the flexed ingredients budget. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the ingredients variance against the flexed budget and state whether it is adverse or favourable. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the flexed comparison is more useful than comparing with the original budget. **[2]**

**11.** Wyckham Hampers plans a second unit. Fixed costs would be £9,600 a month. Each hamper sells for £40 and has a variable cost of £24. Market research suggests sales of 700 a month, but a rival opening nearby could cut that to 500.

**(a)** Calculate the break-even output, the margin of safety and the monthly profit at 700 hampers. **[4]**
**(b)** Evaluate how useful break-even analysis is in deciding whether to open the second unit. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** A cost that cannot be traced to one particular product or cost centre [1]; it is shared across the business, for example factory rent [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* a definition gains more credit when it says what makes the cost indirect (not traceable), with an example as support rather than the whole answer.

**2.** (a) Fuel is variable and direct, because it changes with the trip and is traceable to it [1]. (b) Insurance is fixed and indirect [1]. (c) The office manager's salary is fixed and indirect [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* both labels are needed for each item. Fixed or variable is about behaviour with output; direct or indirect is about traceability, so mixing the two up loses credit.

**3.** (a) Total cost = £3,000 + 5,000 × £0.90 = **£7,500** [1]. (b) £7,500 ÷ 5,000 = **£1.50** [1]. (c) **£0.90** [1]. (d) Revenue = 5,000 × £2.10 = £10,500, so profit = £10,500 − £7,500 = **£3,000** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* show the working line, not only the answer, so that the method is visible and each later part can build on a clearly stated figure.

**4.** Full costing shares fixed (indirect) costs between products, whereas contribution costing looks only at price minus variable cost and does not share fixed costs out [2]. Contribution is what is left to pay towards fixed costs [1]; profit is what remains after fixed costs have also been deducted [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "explain" asks for the reason as well as the statement. A difference only gains full credit if both methods are described, not just one.

**5.** (a) £28 − £11 = **£17** [1]. (b) £5,100 ÷ £17 [1] = **300 passengers** [1]. (c) Margin of safety = 380 − 300 = **80 passengers** [1]. Profit = 380 × £17 − £5,100 [1] = £6,460 − £5,100 = **£1,360** [1]. (d) New fixed costs = £5,950, so £5,950 ÷ £17 [1] = **350 passengers** [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* divide by contribution per unit, not price. State the unit (passengers) with the answer, and say whether the margin of safety is in units or a percentage.

**6.** (a) Contribution = £170 − £150 = £20 a grill [1]; 80 × £20 = **£1,600** extra profit [1]. (b) Fixed costs are already covered and would not change, so full cost overstates the extra cost of the order [1]; every grill sold above £150 adds to profit [1]. (c) Recommend accepting, because it adds £1,600 and uses spare capacity [1]. But regular customers paying £240 may learn of the discount and ask for it, which could reduce future profit [1]; the order must not displace full-price sales [1]. Overall, accept if the order is kept separate from regular customers and capacity is truly spare, because the gain is certain and the risks can be managed [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* a recommendation earns credit when it is backed with both the numbers and the non-financial factors, and reaches a clear decision rather than listing points.

**7.** (a) £18.40 × 1.35 [1] = **£24.84** [1]. (b) Cost-plus ignores what customers will pay [1]; if rivals sell similar cakes for less, £24.84 may reduce sales [1], so revenue and profit could fall even though each cake has a margin [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* "analyse" needs a chain: the limitation, why it matters, and the effect on the business. A one-line statement gets limited credit.

**8.** Incremental budgeting starts from last period's budget [1] and adds or subtracts a percentage [1]. Zero budgeting starts from nothing each period [1] and every item must be justified before it gets funds [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* give both halves of the contrast. Answers that describe only one type can only earn credit for that type.

**9.** (a) Revenue: £62,000 − £58,400 = **£3,600 adverse** [1]. Fuel: £15,900 − £14,500 = **£1,400 adverse** [1]. Wages: £21,000 − £20,200 = **£800 favourable** [1]. Profit: budget £17,500, actual £13,300, so **£4,200 adverse** [1]. (b) Reason 1: fuel prices rose above the budgeted price [1]. Reason 2: more miles were driven, or drivers used longer routes [1]. Action: renegotiate fuel contracts or plan routes more efficiently [1]. The fuel variance should be compared with the number of trips run, since revenue was also down, so cost per trip rose [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* always label a variance adverse or favourable. In "analyse", reasons are more convincing when tied to the data given.

**10.** (a) The cost is £0.60 a pie, so the flexed budget scales with output [1]: £0.60 × 12,000 = **£7,200** [1]. (b) £7,500 − £7,200 = **£300 adverse** [1]; actual cost is above the flexed budget [1]. (c) Output was 2,000 pies higher, so the original budget is for a different level of activity [1]; flexing shows whether cost control was good for the output made, so £1,200 of the £1,500 gap against the original budget is just extra volume [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* variable costs should be flexed and fixed costs left alone. Explaining that volume changed gains more credit than saying only that flexing is "fairer".

**11.** (a) Contribution = £40 − £24 = £16 [1]; break-even = £9,600 ÷ £16 = **600 hampers** [1]; margin of safety = 700 − 600 = **100 hampers** [1]; profit = 700 × £16 − £9,600 = **£1,600** [1]. (b) For: break-even is 600, and expected sales of 700 give a profit of £1,600 [1]. It gives the minimum target, easy to explain to a bank [1]. Against: the margin of safety is only 100 hampers (14.3% of expected sales) [1]; if sales fall to 500, the unit makes a loss of £1,600 a month [1]. It assumes a constant price and variable cost and that every hamper is sold, which a nearby rival makes doubtful [1]. Judgement: break-even is useful as a risk check but should not decide alone; the owner should test the demand forecast and a lower-fixed-cost option before opening, since the profit is small and the downside is the same size [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* in "evaluate", calculations support the argument but do not replace it. Credit is usually gained for a reasoned judgement that refers to the case figures.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing the fixed costs by price when contribution per unit is needed.
- Calling contribution "profit".
- Leaving out units, or mixing units and percentages for margin of safety.
- Giving a variance without "adverse" or "favourable".
- Not flexing variable costs when output has changed.
- Listing non-financial factors in a special order question without reaching a decision.
- Describing a limitation of break-even without applying it to the business.
- Using an "explain" command word to write a definition only.
