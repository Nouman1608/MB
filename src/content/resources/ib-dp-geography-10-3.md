## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Evaluate different perspectives on the relationship between population, resources and the future.
- Examine strategies for managing resource consumption sustainably, from individual to global scale.
- Evaluate the circular economy as a model and the role of the Sustainable Development Goals.
- Judge how far stewardship can resolve pressures identified in 10.1 and 10.2.

## The waste hierarchy

The circular economy is often reduced to recycling. A sharper answer uses the **waste hierarchy**, which ranks options from most to least preferred:

1. **Prevent and reduce**: design products that use less material, or avoid the need for them.
2. **Reuse**: use items again for the same purpose, such as refillable bottles.
3. **Recycle**: reprocess materials into new products.
4. **Recover**: extract energy, for example by burning waste to generate electricity.
5. **Dispose**: landfill, the last resort.

Higher options save more resources because they avoid the energy and materials needed to collect, process and remake goods. Recycling still loses some material and quality at each cycle, which is why a circular economy aims first at durable, repairable design. Use the hierarchy to judge any strategy: a scheme that improves recycling but leaves consumption unchanged is weaker stewardship than one that cuts demand.

## Testing Boserup at local scale: Machakos, Kenya

Population theories are easier to evaluate with a place. In the 1930s, colonial officials described the Machakos district of Kenya as severely eroded and overpopulated. In a study published in 1994 as *More People, Less Erosion*, Mary Tiffen, Michael Mortimore and Francis Gichuki found that over the following decades the population had grown several times over, yet erosion had been brought under control and output per hectare had risen. Farmers terraced slopes, harvested water, planted trees and moved into higher-value crops, helped by access to markets in Nairobi.

The case supports Boserup's argument that population pressure can stimulate innovation. It also shows its conditions: secure land rights, market access and investment were all part of the story, so the outcome cannot be assumed elsewhere. This is the kind of qualification that turns a description of theories into an evaluation.

## Two national strategies

### Rwanda: regulation of plastic bags

Rwanda banned non-biodegradable plastic bags in 2008, with border checks and penalties enforcing the ban. It is often cited as an example of strong, enforceable regulation by a government able to implement it nationwide. Its limits are scale and scope: it targets one product, and smuggling and the cost of alternatives have been reported as challenges.

### Costa Rica: paying for ecosystem services

Costa Rica began a national programme of payments for ecosystem services in 1997, paying landowners to protect forest, replant trees and manage land in ways that store carbon, protect water supplies, conserve biodiversity and maintain scenery for tourism. Forest cover, which had fallen steeply in earlier decades through clearance for cattle ranching, has since recovered substantially. The programme is funded partly by a fuel tax. Evaluation points: payments give landowners a direct incentive, but some protected land might not have been cleared anyway, so how much the programme itself achieved is debated.

## A newer view of limits: doughnut economics

Kate Raworth's "doughnut", first set out in a 2012 paper for Oxfam and then in a 2017 book, pictures two boundaries. The inner ring is a **social foundation** below which people lack essentials such as food, water, energy and housing. The outer ring is an **ecological ceiling** of planetary limits, such as climate and biodiversity. Stewardship means meeting everyone's needs without overshooting the ceiling. Amsterdam adopted the model to guide city policy in 2020.

The framework is useful in an exam because it combines the neo-Malthusian concern with limits and the development concern with meeting needs, which helps when a question asks you to balance stewardship with equity between richer and poorer places.

## Worked example: cost per tonne avoided

A city is choosing between waste strategies. Figures are invented.

```
Strategy A: deposit return scheme
  Cost over 10 years          12 million dollars
  Waste avoided over 10 years 40,000 tonnes
  Cost per tonne              12,000,000 / 40,000 = 300 dollars

Strategy B: new recycling plant
  Cost over 10 years          30 million dollars
  Waste avoided over 10 years 60,000 tonnes
  Cost per tonne              30,000,000 / 60,000 = 500 dollars
```

Strategy A is more cost-effective per tonne, though B avoids more waste in total. The choice depends on the goal: if the budget is fixed, A achieves more per dollar; if the target is a total amount of waste avoided, B, or both together, may be needed. Then apply the waste hierarchy: a deposit scheme can support reuse as well as recycling, which ranks higher. Cost is one criterion; effectiveness, fairness and enforceability are others.

## Stewardship across scales and stakeholders

| Scale | Stakeholder | Example of action |
|---|---|---|
| Individual | Consumers | Cutting food waste, choosing certified products |
| Local | City governments, communities | Waste schemes, community-managed forests |
| National | Governments | Bans, taxes, payments for ecosystem services |
| Global | UN bodies, international agreements | The Sustainable Development Goals |
| Cross-scale | Businesses, NGOs | Product design, certification, campaigning |

Stewardship is strongest when scales reinforce one another, for example when national rules make it easy for consumers and firms to make sustainable choices.

## Evaluating the Sustainable Development Goals as stewardship

The goals give governments, businesses and NGOs a shared set of targets, with indicators that allow progress to be compared between countries. That is their strength. Their weaknesses are that they are not legally binding, that some goals pull against each other (economic growth under Goal 8 can raise consumption that Goal 12 seeks to reduce), and that data for many indicators are incomplete in the countries where needs are greatest. A balanced judgement treats them as a framework that coordinates action rather than a mechanism that enforces it.

## Common mistakes

- Equating the circular economy with recycling, ignoring prevention and reuse.
- Describing Malthus and Boserup without testing them against a located example.
- Judging a strategy only by its cost, without effectiveness or fairness.
- Assuming a strategy that works in one country will transfer to another with different institutions.
- Listing the Sustainable Development Goals without evaluating progress towards them.

## Exam technique

- **Evaluate** a strategy with explicit criteria; state which matters most for the question.
- **Discuss** population and resource perspectives by setting evidence for each against a place.
- **To what extent** questions on stewardship need a clear position on how far it can offset rising consumption.
- **Justify** a choice between strategies by naming the criterion that decides it, as in the cost per tonne example.
- Keep two or three located strategies you can describe accurately, with their dates.
