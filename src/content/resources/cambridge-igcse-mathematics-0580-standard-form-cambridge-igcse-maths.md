This guide teaches subtopic **1.8 Standard form** from the Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics 0580 syllabus for 2025, 2026 and 2027. Core (C1.8) and Extended (E1.8) share the same three statements:

1. Use the standard form A × 10ⁿ, where n is a positive or negative integer and 1 ⩽ A < 10.
2. Convert numbers into and out of standard form.
3. Calculate with values in standard form.

The syllabus adds one tier difference: **Core candidates are expected to calculate with standard form only on Paper 3**, the calculator paper. Extended candidates can be asked to calculate with it on either paper, including the non-calculator Paper 2. Converting and ordering can appear on any paper for both tiers.

Use it with the [standard form revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-mathematics-0580-standard-form-cambridge-igcse-maths-revision-notes/) and the [standard form practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-mathematics-0580-standard-form-cambridge-igcse-maths-practice/). The index rules used here are taught in [Indices I](/resources/cambridge-igcse-mathematics-0580-indices-i-cambridge-igcse-maths/).

## What counts as standard form

A number is in standard form when it is written as **A × 10ⁿ** with **1 ⩽ A < 10** and n an integer.

| Number | Standard form? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 4.7 × 10⁵ | yes | 1 ⩽ 4.7 < 10 |
| 47 × 10⁴ | no | 47 is not less than 10 |
| 0.47 × 10⁶ | no | 0.47 is less than 1 |
| 1 × 10⁻³ | yes | A can equal 1 |
| 10 × 10² | no | A must be less than 10 |

## Converting into standard form

**Large numbers:** place the decimal point after the first non-zero digit; n is the number of places the point moves, and it is positive.

```
86 000         = 8.6 × 10⁴
3 004 000 000  = 3.004 × 10⁹
```

**Small numbers:** n is negative.

```
0.000 52  = 5.2 × 10⁻⁴
0.09      = 9 × 10⁻²
```

A quick check: numbers of 10 or more have n positive; numbers between 0 and 1 have n negative; numbers from 1 up to 10 have n = 0.

**Correcting a non-standard result.** After a calculation you may reach 34 × 10⁵ or 0.7 × 10⁻². Adjust A and compensate in n:

```
34 × 10⁵   = 3.4 × 10¹ × 10⁵  = 3.4 × 10⁶
0.7 × 10⁻² = 7 × 10⁻¹ × 10⁻² = 7 × 10⁻³
```

## Converting out of standard form

Move the point n places right (n positive) or left (n negative), filling with zeros.

```
2.06 × 10⁵  = 206 000
7.5 × 10⁻⁴  = 0.000 75
```

## Ordering numbers in standard form

Compare the powers of 10 first; only if they are equal, compare A.

**Worked example 1.** Order, smallest first: 3.2 × 10⁻³, 8.9 × 10⁻⁴, 4.1 × 10⁻³, 0.005

```
0.005 = 5 × 10⁻³
Powers: −4 is smallest, so 8.9 × 10⁻⁴ first.
Then the 10⁻³ group by A: 3.2, 4.1, 5
Order: 8.9 × 10⁻⁴, 3.2 × 10⁻³, 4.1 × 10⁻³, 0.005
```

## Calculating without a calculator (Extended Paper 2)

**Multiplying and dividing.** Deal with the A values and the powers of 10 separately, then correct the form.

**Worked example 2.** Work out, giving answers in standard form:

(a) (3 × 10⁴) × (5 × 10⁻⁷)

(b) (4.2 × 10⁵) ÷ (6 × 10⁻³)

```
(a) 3 × 5 = 15;   10^(4 + (−7)) = 10⁻³
    15 × 10⁻³ = 1.5 × 10⁻²
(b) 4.2 ÷ 6 = 0.7;   10^(5 − (−3)) = 10⁸
    0.7 × 10⁸ = 7 × 10⁷
```

**Adding and subtracting.** Rewrite so the powers match (or convert to ordinary numbers), then add.

```
6.4 × 10⁶ + 3.8 × 10⁵ = 6.4 × 10⁶ + 0.38 × 10⁶ = 6.78 × 10⁶
5.1 × 10⁻³ − 4.6 × 10⁻⁴ = 0.005 10 − 0.000 46 = 0.004 64 = 4.64 × 10⁻³
```

**Powers.** (2.4 × 10³)² = 2.4² × 10⁶ = 5.76 × 10⁶.

## Calculating with a calculator (Papers 3 and 4)

- Enter standard form with the **×10ˣ** (or EXP) key: 3.84, ×10ˣ, 8 enters 3.84 × 10⁸ as a single number.
- If you type "× 10 ^", put brackets round each standard form number in a division.
- The display may show the power of 10 in small digits or after a letter E; write the answer as 1.48 × 10⁵ (to 3 significant figures), never in the calculator's format.
- If the answer is wanted in standard form, round A, not the ordinary number.

**Worked example 3 (calculator).** The distance from the Earth to the Moon is about 3.84 × 10⁸ m. A probe travels at 2.6 × 10³ m/s. How long does it take to cover this distance, in seconds, in standard form to 3 significant figures?

```
(3.84 × 10⁸) ÷ (2.6 × 10³) = 147 692.3...
= 1.48 × 10⁵ s (3 s.f.)
```

## Standard form in context

**Worked example 4 (non-calculator).** A sheet of paper is 8 × 10⁻⁵ m thick. Find the height of a pile of 500 sheets, in centimetres.

```
500 × 8 × 10⁻⁵ = 4000 × 10⁻⁵ = 4 × 10⁻² m
4 × 10⁻² m = 0.04 m = 4 cm
```

**Worked example 5 (calculator).** A computer chip has 1.2 × 10⁹ transistors on an area of 1.5 × 10² mm². Find the number of transistors per mm².

```
(1.2 × 10⁹) ÷ (1.5 × 10²) = 0.8 × 10⁷ = 8 × 10⁶ per mm²
```

## Common errors

- Leaving answers as 15 × 10⁻³ or 0.7 × 10⁸ when standard form is required.
- Moving n the wrong way when correcting A: 34 × 10⁵ is 3.4 × 10⁶, not 3.4 × 10⁴.
- Adding standard form numbers by adding the A values and the powers.
- Writing small numbers with a positive power: 0.000 52 = 5.2 × 10⁻⁴, not 5.2 × 10⁴.
- Copying the calculator's "E" format into the answer.
- Rounding the ordinary number instead of A: 147 692.3 to 3 s.f. is 148 000 = 1.48 × 10⁵.

## Exam technique

- **Write in standard form**: one line; check 1 ⩽ A < 10.
- **Work out, giving your answer in standard form**: show the A and power working on a non-calculator paper; give A to 3 significant figures on a calculator paper unless exact.
- **Write as an ordinary number**: no powers of 10 in the answer.
- In context questions, state units and convert them before calculating (m to cm, g to kg).

## Frequently asked questions

**Can Core candidates be asked to calculate with standard form on Paper 1?**
The 0580 syllabus says Core candidates are expected to calculate with standard form only on Paper 3. Converting numbers into and out of standard form can still appear on Paper 1.

**Is 1 × 10⁴ in standard form?**
Yes. A can equal 1, because the condition is 1 ⩽ A < 10. It cannot equal 10.

**When should I give an answer in standard form?**
When the question asks for it, or when the number is very large or very small. Otherwise an ordinary number is acceptable.
