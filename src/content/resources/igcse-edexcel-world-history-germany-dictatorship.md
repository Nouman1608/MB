"Germany: development of dictatorship, 1918–45" is Depth Study 3 on Paper 1 of Pearson Edexcel
International GCSE History (4HI1). It traces how the Weimar Republic was born in defeat, survived
crisis, recovered under Stresemann and then collapsed in the Depression, allowing Hitler to build
a dictatorship that lasted until 1945. You answer one three-part question: an extract
interpretation, "explain two effects" and a 16-mark judgement essay.

## Syllabus coverage

The 4HI1 specification (Issue 5, February 2026) sets five key topics:

1. **The establishment of the Weimar Republic and its early problems** — the Kaiser's abdication
   and the German Revolution of 1918–19, the constitution's strengths and weaknesses, reactions
   to Versailles, the Spartacist uprising and Kapp Putsch, the Ruhr occupation and
   hyperinflation.
2. **The recovery of Germany, 1924–29** — Stresemann, the Rentenmark, the Dawes and Young Plans,
   US loans, the League of Nations, Locarno and the Kellogg-Briand Pact.
3. **The rise of Hitler and the Nazis to January 1933** — the German Workers' Party, changes
   1920–22, the Munich Putsch, reorganisation 1924–28, the Depression, Nazi methods, the SA, and
   the roles of von Papen, von Schleicher and Hindenburg.
4. **Nazi Germany, 1933–39** — the Reichstag Fire, Enabling Act, Night of the Long Knives and
   Hitler as Führer; the police state, censorship and propaganda; policies on education, women,
   youth and the Churches; racial policy and persecution of Jews; unemployment, the Labour
   Service, the Labour Front and Strength Through Joy.
5. **Germany and the occupied territories during the Second World War** — ghettos, death squads
   and the Final Solution; the Home Front, total war, rationing and bombing; opposition from the
   Edelweiss Pirates, the White Rose and the July Bomb Plot (1944); the end of the Third Reich.

## Why was the Weimar Republic so unstable at first?

Germany's defeat brought mutinies and revolution. **Kaiser Wilhelm II abdicated on 9 November 1918**
and the armistice followed on 11 November. Elections in January 1919 produced a National Assembly
that met at Weimar and approved a new constitution. Its **strengths** were democratic: universal
suffrage for men and women over 20, a bill of rights and an elected president. Its
**weaknesses** included proportional representation, which produced many parties and short-lived
coalitions, and **Article 48**, which let the president rule by emergency decree.

The **Treaty of Versailles** (signed June 1919) was hated as a *Diktat*: Germany lost territory,
its armed forces were cut to 100,000 men, it accepted the war-guilt clause (Article 231) and was
required to pay reparations (set at £6,600 million in 1921). Many blamed the "November
criminals" and believed the "stab in the back" myth. The Republic faced the **Spartacist uprising**
from the left (January 1919, crushed with help from the Freikorps) and the **Kapp Putsch** from the
right (March 1920, defeated by a general strike).

When Germany fell behind with reparations, French and Belgian troops **occupied the Ruhr** in
January 1923. The government backed passive resistance and printed money to pay striking workers,
turning inflation into **hyperinflation**: by November 1923 a dollar was worth over four trillion
marks. Savings were destroyed, hurting the middle classes most, while debtors benefited.

## How did Germany recover between 1924 and 1929?

**Gustav Stresemann** was chancellor for a few months in 1923 and foreign minister until his death
in 1929. He ended passive resistance and introduced the **Rentenmark** (November 1923), later
replaced by the Reichsmark. The **Dawes Plan** (1924) reduced annual reparations and brought large
**US loans**; the **Young Plan** (1929) cut the total and extended payments to 1988. Abroad,
Germany signed the **Locarno Treaties** (1925), accepting its western borders, joined the **League
of Nations** (1926) and signed the **Kellogg-Briand Pact** (1928). The recovery was real but
depended on short-term American loans.

## How did Hitler come to power by January 1933?

Hitler joined the **German Workers' Party** in 1919. By 1920–21 it had become the National Socialist
German Workers' Party (NSDAP), with the Twenty-Five Point Programme, Hitler as leader and the SA
as its paramilitary wing. The **Munich Putsch** (November 1923) failed, but Hitler's trial gave him
publicity and in prison he wrote *Mein Kampf*. From 1925 he **reorganised** the party to win power
legally, building a national structure and the Hitler Youth, yet in 1928 it won only 12 seats.

The **Great Depression** changed everything. US loans were recalled and unemployment reached about
six million by 1932. Nazi seats rose to 107 in 1930 and 230 in July 1932, making it the largest
party. **Nazi methods** included propaganda directed by Goebbels, promises tailored to different
groups, Hitler's speeches and modern campaigning, and the **SA**'s violence and show of order.
After Brüning, chancellor **von Papen** and then **von Schleicher** failed to build majorities;
Papen persuaded President **Hindenburg** that Hitler could be controlled, and Hitler was appointed
chancellor on **30 January 1933**.

## How did Hitler create and maintain a dictatorship, 1933–39?

The **Reichstag Fire** (27 February 1933) was blamed on communists and led to an emergency decree
suspending civil rights. The **Enabling Act** (March 1933) allowed the government to make laws
without the Reichstag. Trade unions were abolished and other parties banned by July 1933. In the
**Night of the Long Knives** (30 June 1934) Röhm and other SA leaders were murdered, winning the
army's support; after Hindenburg died in August 1934 Hitler combined the offices as **Führer**, and
the army swore an oath to him personally.

Control rested on the **police state** (the SS under Himmler, the Gestapo, concentration camps and
courts), **censorship** and **propaganda** led by Goebbels's ministry (rallies, radio, film, the
1936 Olympics). Policies covered **education** (a Nazified curriculum), the **young** (Hitler
Youth, League of German Maidens), **women** (Kinder, Küche, Kirche; marriage loans) and the
**Churches** (the Concordat with the Catholic Church in 1933, the Reich Church, persecution of
critics such as Niemöller). **Racial policy** escalated from the boycott of Jewish shops (1933) to the
**Nuremberg Laws** (1935) and **Kristallnacht** (November 1938). **Unemployment** fell through public
works, rearmament, the **Reich Labour Service** and conscription; the **Labour Front** replaced
unions, and **Strength Through Joy** offered subsidised leisure.

## What happened in Germany and occupied Europe during the war?

Persecution became genocide. Jews in occupied Poland were forced into **ghettos** such as Warsaw
and Łódź. After the invasion of the USSR in 1941, **Einsatzgruppen** (death squads) shot hundreds
of thousands. The **Wannsee Conference** (January 1942) coordinated the **Final Solution**, and
death camps such as Auschwitz-Birkenau murdered millions; around six million Jews were killed.

On the **Home Front**, rationing began in 1939. After Stalingrad, Goebbels declared **"total war"**
(February 1943), and women's work was expanded. Allied bombing, such as the raids on Hamburg (1943)
and Dresden (1945), killed many civilians. **Opposition** included the **Edelweiss Pirates**, the
**White Rose** student group (whose leaders Hans and Sophie Scholl were executed in 1943) and the
army officers' **July Bomb Plot** (20 July 1944). Hitler killed himself on 30 April 1945, and
Germany surrendered in May 1945.

## Frequently asked questions

**Was the Weimar Republic doomed from the start?**
It is a common essay theme. It survived the
crises of 1919–23 and recovered in 1924–29, which suggests it was not; but its weaknesses left it
vulnerable to the Depression.

**What is the difference between the SA and SS?**
The SA were the mass stormtroopers who helped the
Nazis rise; the SS began as Hitler's bodyguard and, under Himmler, became the core of the police
state after 1934.

**Do I need election figures?**
A few key ones (12 seats in 1928, 107 in 1930, 230 in July 1932)
make explanations precise.
