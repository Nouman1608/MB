These questions cover **3.2.1 The measurement of macroeconomic performance** in AQA AS Economics (7135), specification version 1.3, parts 3.2.1.1 to 3.2.1.3. All countries and figures are invented. Revise first with the [3.2.1 study guide](/resources/aqa-as-level-economics-7135-the-measurement-of-macroeconomic-performance-aqa-aslevel-economics/) and the [3.2.1 revision notes](/resources/aqa-as-level-economics-7135-the-measurement-of-macroeconomic-performance-aqa-aslevel-economics-revision-notes/). Give answers to one decimal place unless told otherwise.

## Section A: short questions

**1.** Apart from the four main objectives, state two other objectives a government may have for macroeconomic policy. **[2]**

**2.** A price index rises from 160 to 168. Calculate the percentage change in prices. **[1]**

**3.** The annual rate of CPI inflation falls from 4.2% to 2.8%. Which one of the following statements is correct? **[1]**

A: The price level fell by 1.4%

B: The price level rose, but more slowly than in the previous year

C: The inflation rate fell by 1.4%

D: Prices are now 2.8% lower than a year ago

**4.** Distinguish between real GDP and nominal GDP. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why weights are used when calculating the Consumer Prices Index. **[2]**

**6.** A price index with 2019 = 100 reads 95 in 2021, 104 in 2022 and 117 in 2023. Rebase the series so that 2022 = 100, giving the new values for 2019, 2021 and 2023. **[3]**

## Section B: structured questions

**7.** Country V's price index uses five categories. Each category's price index is for this year, with last year = 100.

| Category | Weight | Price index |
|---|---|---|
| Food | 20 | 106 |
| Housing | 30 | 103 |
| Energy | 10 | 125 |
| Transport | 15 | 97 |
| Other | 25 | 101 |

**(a)** Calculate the weighted price index. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the unweighted average of the five indices and explain why it differs from your answer to (a). **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why a household that spends a quarter of its budget on energy faces a higher inflation rate than the index shows. **[2]**

**8.** Country W's nominal GDP rises from 1,500 bn to 1,596 bn in a year in which the GDP price index rises from 100 to 104. The population rises from 30.0 million to 30.3 million.

**(a)** Calculate the rate of real economic growth. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate real GDP per capita in each year and its percentage change. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why real GDP per capita is a better guide than nominal GDP to changes in average living standards. **[2]**

**9.** In Country W an index of average nominal wages (2020 = 100) is 118 in 2024, and the CPI (2020 = 100) is 124 in 2024.

**(a)** Calculate the index of real wages for 2024. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain what your answer shows about workers' living standards since 2020. **[2]**

**10.** Explain why the objectives of price stability and minimising unemployment may conflict in the short run. **[4]**

**11.** Explain how a sustained rise in labour productivity can help a government achieve more than one macroeconomic objective at the same time. **[4]**

**12.** The table shows data for two countries in the same year.

| Indicator | Country K | Country L |
|---|---|---|
| Real GDP growth | 3.5% | 1.2% |
| CPI inflation | 6.0% | 1.8% |
| Unemployment rate | 3.8% | 6.5% |
| Current account balance (% of GDP) | -5.0% | +1.0% |

Evaluate which country's macroeconomic performance is better. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Balancing the budget [1]; achieving a more equitable distribution of income [1]. *Total: 2.*

**2.** 8 / 160 x 100 = **5.0%** [1]. *Total: 1.*

**3.** **B** [1]. Inflation is still positive, so prices are rising, only more slowly. C is wrong because the rate fell by 1.4 percentage points, which is a fall of a third in the rate itself. *Total: 1.*

**4.** Nominal GDP values output at the prices of the year in which it is produced [1]. Real GDP removes the effect of changes in the price level, so it shows changes in the volume of output [1]. *Total: 2.*

**5.** Households spend different shares of their income on different goods and services [1]. Weights make price changes in items with a large share of spending count for more, so the index reflects the change in the cost of the average household's basket [1]. *Total: 2.*

**6.** Divide each value by 104 and multiply by 100 [1]. 2019: 100 / 104 x 100 = **96.2**; 2021: 95 / 104 x 100 = **91.3** [1]; 2023: 117 / 104 x 100 = **112.5** [1]. *Total: 3.*

**7. (a)** (20 x 106) + (30 x 103) + (10 x 125) + (15 x 97) + (25 x 101) = 2,120 + 3,090 + 1,250 + 1,455 + 2,525 = 10,440 [1]. Index = 10,440 / 100 = **104.4** [1].

**(b)** (106 + 103 + 125 + 97 + 101) / 5 = 532 / 5 = **106.4** [1]. The unweighted average gives energy, with the largest rise, the same importance as housing, although energy has only a third of housing's weight [1].

**(c)** The index gives energy a weight of 10 out of 100, but this household's weight is 25, so it feels more of the 25% energy price rise [1]. Moving weight towards the category with the largest price rise lifts its own weighted average above 4.4%, provided the rest of its spending follows the average pattern [1]. *Total: 6.*

**8. (a)** Real GDP this year = 1,596 / 1.04 = 1,534.6 bn [1]. Real growth = 34.6 / 1,500 = **2.3%** [1].

**(b)** Last year: 1,500 bn / 30.0 m = **50,000**; this year: 1,534.6 bn / 30.3 m = **50,647** [1]. Change = 647 / 50,000 = **+1.3%** [1].

**(c)** Nominal GDP rose 6.4%, but most of this was inflation and population growth [1]. Real GDP per capita removes both, so it shows the change in output available per person, a closer guide to average living standards [1]. *Total: 6.*

**9. (a)** Real wage index = 118 / 124 x 100 [1] = **95.2** [1].

**(b)** Real wages are 4.8% below their 2020 level [1]: nominal wages rose 18%, but prices rose 24%, so the same wage buys fewer goods and services and workers' living standards have fallen on average [1]. *Total: 4.*

**10.** To reduce unemployment, a government may raise aggregate demand, for example through lower taxes [1]. Firms raise output and hire more workers, so unemployment falls [1]. As spare capacity is used up, shortages of labour and materials push up wages and costs [1], so the price level rises faster and price stability is harder to achieve; the reverse applies when demand is cut to lower inflation [1]. *Total: 4.*

**11.** Higher productivity raises the economy's productive capacity, so real GDP can grow faster without excess demand [1]. Unit labour costs fall, which reduces cost pressure on prices and helps price stability [1]. Lower costs make exports more price competitive, helping the current account [1]. Faster growth in capacity also allows employment to rise without creating inflation, so several objectives improve together [1]. *Total: 4.*

**12.** Country K performs better on growth (3.5% against 1.2%) and unemployment (3.8% against 6.5%) [1], so output and jobs are rising strongly [1]. But K's inflation of 6.0% is far from price stability and erodes real incomes [1], and a current account deficit of 5% of GDP suggests demand is being met partly by imports and may not be sustainable [1]. Country L has price stability and a small surplus [1], but its high unemployment means resources are wasted and its slow growth limits rising living standards [1]. K's figures suggest an economy with a positive output gap, where the conflicts between objectives are showing; L's suggest spare capacity [1]. Judgement: L is closer to stability but K's higher output and employment matter more in the short run; K is performing better only if it can reduce inflation without a sharp slowdown, so on balance L's position is the more sustainable [1]. *Total: 8.*

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing that prices fell when the inflation rate fell.
- Treating a rise of 8 index points as an 8% rise when the start is not 100.
- Using population figures as millions in one year and units in the other.
- Comparing countries on one indicator only, with no judgement weighing the objectives.
- Reading a negative current account figure as a surplus.
