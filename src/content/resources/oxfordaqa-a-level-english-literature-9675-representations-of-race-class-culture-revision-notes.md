Condensed recall for one of the four areas named in 9675 Unit 4: "Representations of
race/class/culture". The unit overview is in the
[Unit 4 study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-english-literature-unit-4-literary-representations-study-guide/);
the full explanation and a model paragraph are in the
[race, class and culture study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-english-literature-9675-representations-of-race-class-culture/).

## The idea in one box

- The areas "are not topics which are prescribed for detailed study"; extensive context and wider
  reading are **not expected**.
- You are rewarded for analysing **how a text constructs a view** of a group or relationship.
- Questions usually name an **aspect** (for example, class and culture): answer that aspect.
- 4a: unseen prose (which may be literary non-fiction) and unseen poetry, one compulsory question
  each, on **different** areas, 2 hours 30 minutes.
- 4b: two essays of 1,250 to 1,500 words, one poetry text and one prose text, each on a **different**
  area.

## Six things to look for

| Feature | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| Viewpoint | Inside or outside the group? Pride, shame, curiosity, prejudice? |
| Markers | What do clothes, food, money, objects and rituals signify, and to whom? |
| Voice | Is dialect or code-switching given dignity or used for mockery? |
| The gaze | Who looks, who is looked at, and does anyone look back? |
| Stereotype | Repeated uncritically, or set up to be broken? |
| Space | Who enters, who serves, who moves, who stays? |

## Methods particular to this area

- Contrastive description of households, meals or speech.
- Free indirect style: hearing a character's judgement without the narrator's endorsement.
- Lists of possessions and their ironic ordering.
- Naming, group labels and nicknames.
- Silence or reported speech for the less powerful.
- Barriers: windows, counters, walls, uniforms.

## Using the information given in 4a

- Author and date of first publication are often supplied.
- Use the date to explain a specific choice, not to write history.
- Avoid **anachronistic judgement**: analyse how older attitudes are presented.
- Do not assume the writer shares a character's or narrator's views.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning | Use it to say |
|---|---|---|
| Representation | A constructed view of the world, not the world itself | That the text shapes how a group appears |
| Othering | Presenting a group as alien or inferior | How an outsider's view creates distance |
| Stereotype | A fixed, simplified image of a group | Whether the text repeats or subverts it |
| Code-switching | Moving between languages or registers | Divided or layered identity |
| Free indirect style | Narration that takes on a character's thoughts | Irony about a character's prejudices |
| The gaze | Power exercised through looking | Who controls description |
| Class marker | A detail signalling economic or social position | How status is made visible |
| Hybridity | Identity formed from more than one culture | Migration and belonging |

## Writing habits

- Quote the text's terms; analyse in careful terms of your own.
- Write about the **representation of** a group, not claims about the group.
- Discuss offensive language for its function, repeating it only when needed.
- Keep context to a sentence that changes the reading of a line.
- Name the method before the effect: "the list of possessions ends on the piano, so..." is stronger
  than "this shows wealth".
- When a text gives two views of a group, say which the structure favours, for example through who
  has the last word.

## 4b reminders for this area

- No texts from the exam set-text lists; texts in translation are allowed.
- Poetry must be by a single author; short stories, if used, by a single author.
- Choose texts that support more than one interpretation.
- Frame the task around representation, as the specification's Forster example does.

## Exam traps

- Writing about a society rather than the text.
- Equating the narrator or speaker with the author.
- Trying to cover race, class and culture all at once when one aspect is named.
- Listing markers without saying what they are made to mean.
- Judging an older text by modern standards without analysing its methods.

## Self-test

1. Quote the specification's name for this area.
2. What does the specification say about contextual knowledge and wider reading in Unit 4?
3. What did the specification's example 4a question ask about the Auden poem?
4. Name the six things to look for in these notes.
5. What is free indirect style and why is it useful here?
6. Define othering.
7. What is anachronistic judgement?
8. Why should you not assume a narrator speaks for the writer?
9. In 4a, may the prose question use literary non-fiction?
10. Can both 4b texts be linked to race, class and culture?
11. Give two markers a writer might use to signal class.
12. What does code-switching suggest about a character?

## Answers

1. "Representations of race/class/culture".
2. Students are not expected to draw on extensive contextual knowledge of the topic or to refer to
   wider reading.
3. To explore "the significance of Auden's representation of social class and culture".
4. Viewpoint, markers, voice, the gaze, stereotype, space.
5. Narration that takes on a character's thoughts and idiom; it lets the reader hear a character's
   prejudice without the narrator endorsing it.
6. Presenting a group as alien or inferior to the viewpoint group.
7. Dismissing an older text because its attitudes differ from today's, without analysing how it
   presents them.
8. Prejudice voiced by a narrator or speaker may be the target of the text's irony.
9. Yes; the specification says the prose can include literary non-fiction.
10. No; each text must be linked to a different one of the four areas.
11. Any two: clothing, food, furniture, work, money, education, speech.
12. That the character moves between cultures or communities, often with a divided or layered
    identity.
