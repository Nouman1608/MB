These questions cover Unit S2, content statements 4.1 and 4.2: population, census and sample; sampling units and sampling frames; the advantages and disadvantages of a census and a sample survey; and statistics and their sampling distributions.

Related: [samples revision notes](/resources/edexcel-a-level-mathematics-yma01-s2-samples-edexcel-alevel-maths-revision-notes/) and the [samples study guide](/resources/edexcel-a-level-mathematics-yma01-s2-samples-edexcel-alevel-maths/).

## Section A

**1.** Explain what is meant by a population and by a sampling frame. **[2]**

**2.** A public library wants to find out how satisfied its registered borrowers are with its opening hours. Identify the population, a sampling unit and a suitable sampling frame. **[3]**

**3.** X₁, X₂, X₃ is a random sample from a population with unknown mean μ. For each expression, state whether it is a statistic, giving a reason: (i) X₁ + X₂ + X₃; (ii) X₁ − μ; (iii) the largest of X₁, X₂ and X₃. **[3]**

**4.** A fireworks company tests whether its rockets launch correctly. Explain why it should use a sample rather than a census. **[2]**

## Section B

**5.** For each situation, state whether a census or a sample survey is more appropriate, giving a reason in context.

**(a)** A teacher wants the test marks of the 25 students in her class. **[2]**

**(b)** A bakery wants to estimate the mean mass of the 4000 loaves it makes each day. **[2]**

**(c)** A drinks firm wants to know how long its sealed bottles stay fizzy after opening. **[2]**

**6.** A large population of discs has 50% marked 1, 30% marked 2 and 20% marked 3. A random sample of two discs is taken.

**(a)** Find the sampling distribution of the sample mean X̄. **[4]**

**(b)** Show that E(X̄) equals the population mean. **[2]**

**7.** A large population has the values 1, 2, 3 and 4 in equal proportions. A random sample of size 2 is taken, and L is the larger of the two values (L is the common value if they are equal).

**(a)** Find the sampling distribution of L. **[4]**

**(b)** Find E(L). **[2]**

**8.** A machine produces a large number of components, of which 10% are defective. A random sample of 20 components is taken, and Y is the number of defective components in the sample.

**(a)** State the sampling distribution of Y. **[1]**

**(b)** Find P(Y ≤ 1). **[2]**

**(c)** Find P(Y ≥ 4). **[2]**

**9.** A gym emails a questionnaire to every member on its email list. Some members have never given the gym an email address.

**(a)** Explain why the results may not represent all members. **[2]**

**(b)** Give one advantage of a census of all members in this situation. **[1]**

**10.** A large population of tiles has 70% plain (value 0) and 30% patterned (value 1). A random sample of three tiles is taken.

**(a)** Find the sampling distribution of the range R of the three values. **[3]**

**(b)** Find the sampling distribution of the median M of the three values. **[3]**

**11.** Explain the difference between a parameter and a statistic, using a population mean as an example. **[2]**

**12.** A sample survey is often less accurate than a census. Give two reasons why. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** A population is the whole set of items that are of interest [1]. A sampling frame is a list of the sampling units (members of the population) from which a sample is chosen [1].

**2.** Population: all registered borrowers of the library [1]. Sampling unit: one registered borrower [1]. Sampling frame: the library's list (database) of registered borrowers [1].

**3.** (i) Yes: it uses the sample values only [1]. (ii) No: it involves the unknown parameter μ [1]. (iii) Yes: it is a function of the sample values only [1].

**4.** Testing a rocket uses it up (the test is destructive) [1], so a census would leave no rockets to sell; a sample is the only practical option [1].

**5. (a)** Census [1]: the population is only 25 students and the marks are already available, so every mark can be used [1].

**(b)** Sample [1]: weighing all 4000 loaves every day would be too time-consuming and costly [1].

**(c)** Sample [1]: testing requires opening the bottles, which destroys them as products [1].

**6. (a)** Possible means: 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3 [1]. P(X̄ = 1) = 0.5² = 0.25; P(X̄ = 1.5) = 2 × 0.5 × 0.3 = 0.3 [1]. P(X̄ = 2) = 0.3² + 2 × 0.5 × 0.2 = 0.29; P(X̄ = 2.5) = 2 × 0.3 × 0.2 = 0.12 [1]. P(X̄ = 3) = 0.2² = 0.04, and the total is 1 [1].

**(b)** E(X̄) = 0.25 + 0.45 + 0.58 + 0.3 + 0.12 = 1.7 [1]. Population mean = 0.5 + 0.6 + 0.6 = 1.7, so they are equal [1].

**7. (a)** P(L ≤ m) = (m/4)² [1]. P(L = 1) = 1/16 [1]; P(L = 2) = 4/16 − 1/16 = 3/16 [1]; P(L = 3) = 9/16 − 4/16 = 5/16 and P(L = 4) = 1 − 9/16 = 7/16 [1].

**(b)** E(L) = (1 × 1 + 2 × 3 + 3 × 5 + 4 × 7)/16 [1] = 50/16 = **3.125** [1].

**8. (a)** **Y ~ B(20, 0.1)** [1].

**(b)** P(Y ≤ 1) = 0.9²⁰ + 20 × 0.1 × 0.9¹⁹ [1] = **0.392** (tables: 0.3917) [1].

**(c)** P(Y ≥ 4) = 1 − P(Y ≤ 3) [1] = 1 − 0.8670 = **0.133** [1].

**9. (a)** The email list is an incomplete sampling frame [1]: members without an email address cannot be included, and their views may differ from those who are included [1].

**(b)** Every member's view would be included, so there would be no doubt about whether the responses represent the membership [1].

**10. (a)** R = 0 when all three tiles are the same: 0.7³ + 0.3³ = 0.343 + 0.027 [1] = 0.37 [1]. Otherwise R = 1, with probability 0.63 [1].

**(b)** M = 1 when at least two tiles are patterned: 3 × 0.3² × 0.7 + 0.3³ = 0.189 + 0.027 [1] = 0.216 [1]. So P(M = 1) = 0.216 and P(M = 0) = 0.784 [1].

**11.** A parameter is a fixed numerical property of the population, such as the population mean μ, which is usually unknown [1]. A statistic is calculated from sample values only, such as the sample mean X̄; it varies from sample to sample and is used to estimate the parameter [1].

**12.** Natural variation between samples means a sample's result may differ from the population value [1]. The sample may be biased, for example because the sampling frame is incomplete or some people do not respond [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving the population instead of a list when asked for the sampling frame.
- Reasons for a census or sample that are not tied to the context.
- Missing one of the two orders, such as (1, 2) and (2, 1), in Questions 6 and 7.
- Probabilities that do not add to 1, with no check made.
- Calling X₁ − μ a statistic when μ is unknown.
