This guide covers sub-topic **16.3 Sexual reproduction in plants** of the Cambridge IGCSE Biology 0610 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. Outcomes 1 to 8 are Core. Outcomes 9 to 12, on self- and cross-pollination and the growth of the pollen tube, are Supplement content and are marked **Extended only**.

## The insect-pollinated flower

Learn the parts in two groups, because questions often ask "which parts are male?" or "which parts form the carpel?".

- **Stamen** (male) = **anther** + **filament**.
- **Carpel** (female) = **stigma** + **style** + **ovary** (which contains the **ovules**).
- The outer parts are the **sepals**, outermost, and the **petals**.

### Following the pollen

A good way to remember the functions is to follow pollen from where it is made to where fertilisation happens:

1. The **anther** makes and releases pollen grains; the **filament** holds it in position.
2. An insect visiting the flower, attracted by large, coloured, often scented **petals**, picks up pollen.
3. At the next flower the pollen sticks to the **stigma**.
4. The **style** holds the stigma up; in Extended work the pollen tube grows down through it.
5. The **ovary** protects the **ovules**; each ovule contains a female gamete and becomes a seed after fertilisation.

The **sepals** play no part in this sequence. They protect the flower while it is a bud.

### Drawing a flower

Outcome 16.3.1 includes **draw**. Examiners reward:

- clear, continuous lines with no shading;
- the parts in the correct positions and proportions: sepals outside petals, stamens round the carpel, ovules inside the ovary;
- ruled label lines that touch the part named and do not cross each other.

### Identifying parts in an unfamiliar diagram

You may be shown a flower you have never seen. Use position, not shape:

- The structure in the centre, with a swollen base, is the **carpel**; the swelling is the **ovary**.
- Stalks topped with small sacs, round the carpel, are **stamens**.
- Small, green, leaf-like parts at the base are **sepals**.

## Wind-pollinated flowers

Wind-pollinated flowers, such as grasses, have no need to attract animals. Their structure is set up to release pollen into moving air and to catch it from the air.

| Feature | Insect-pollinated | Wind-pollinated | Why it helps |
|---|---|---|---|
| Petals | large, brightly coloured, often scented | small, dull or green, or absent | colour and scent attract insects; wind flowers save materials |
| Anthers | inside the flower, firmly attached | hang outside on long filaments, loosely attached | insects brush against them; wind shakes pollen out |
| Stigmas | inside the flower, sticky | large, feathery, hang outside | sticky surface holds pollen from insects; feathery surface has a large area to trap airborne pollen |
| Pollen quantity | smaller amounts | very large amounts | most wind-carried pollen never reaches a stigma |
| Pollen grains | larger, sticky or with a spiky surface | smaller, light, smooth | spikes and stickiness cling to insects; light, smooth grains are carried far by air |

When asked to **describe the anthers and stigmas** of a wind-pollinated flower (16.3.3), give position and structure: anthers **hang outside** the flower on long filaments; stigmas are **large and feathery** and also **hang outside**.

### Worked example 1: classifying a flower from a description

*A flower has no petals. Its anthers dangle on long thin filaments and its two stigmas are branched like small feathers. Its pollen grains are light and smooth. Is it insect- or wind-pollinated?*

Wind-pollinated. Give **evidence**: anthers hang outside on long filaments so wind can shake out pollen; feathery stigmas give a large surface to catch pollen; light, smooth pollen is carried by air; no petals are needed because no insects are attracted. Each piece of evidence tied to a reason can earn a mark.

## Pollination and fertilisation (Core)

- **Pollination** is the transfer of pollen grains from an **anther** to a **stigma**.
- **Fertilisation** occurs when a **pollen nucleus fuses with a nucleus in an ovule**.

Pollination is transfer; fertilisation is fusion. Pollination must come first, and pollination does not guarantee fertilisation.

## Self- and cross-pollination (Extended only)

| | Self-pollination | Cross-pollination |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | pollen from the anther of a flower to the stigma of the **same flower** or a **different flower on the same plant** | pollen from the anther of a flower to the stigma of a flower on a **different plant of the same species** |
| Variation in offspring | less | more |
| Capacity to respond to changes in the environment | lower | higher |
| Reliance on pollinators | lower; pollen need not travel between plants | higher; pollen must be carried between plants |

The syllabus asks you to **discuss** effects on a population in terms of these three ideas: **variation**, **capacity to respond to environmental change** and **reliance on pollinators**. A strong answer uses all three. For example, a population that mostly self-pollinates can still set seed when pollinating insects are scarce, but its offspring vary less, so it is less able to survive a new disease or climate change.

## The pollen tube and fertilisation (Extended only)

1. A pollen grain lands on a **stigma** of the same species.
2. A **pollen tube** grows out of the pollen grain.
3. The tube grows **down through the style** to the **ovary**.
4. The tube **enters the ovule** through a small gap (the micropyle).
5. The **pollen nucleus** passes down the tube.
6. The pollen nucleus **fuses with the nucleus in the ovule**: this is fertilisation, and a zygote forms.

The syllabus says details of endosperm production and later development are not required. You do not need to describe anything after fertilisation beyond knowing that the ovule becomes a seed.

## Germination (Core)

Seeds need **water**, **oxygen** and a **suitable temperature** to germinate. Light is **not** a requirement; seeds germinate underground using their food stores.

| Condition | Why the seed needs it |
|---|---|
| Water | the seed takes up water and swells; water allows enzymes to work and dissolved food to be moved to the growing embryo |
| Oxygen | for aerobic respiration, releasing energy for growth |
| Suitable temperature | enzymes work best within a range of temperatures; too cold is slow, too hot denatures them |

### Worked example 2: a temperature investigation

Five dishes each held 25 seeds on moist cotton wool, in air, at different temperatures, for six days. These results are invented for this example.

| Temperature / °C | 5 | 15 | 25 | 35 | 45 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Seeds germinated | 2 | 15 | 23 | 20 | 0 |
| Percentage germinated | 8% | 60% | 92% | 80% | 0% |

```
Percentage = seeds germinated ÷ 25 × 100
25 °C: 23 ÷ 25 × 100 = 92%
```

**Describe:** germination rose from 8% at 5 °C to a peak of 92% at 25 °C, then fell to 0% at 45 °C.

**Explain:** at low temperature, enzymes in the seed work slowly, so few seeds germinate in six days. Near 25 °C enzyme activity is high. At 45 °C the enzymes are denatured, so no seeds germinate.

**Improve:** test more temperatures between 15 °C and 35 °C to find the optimum more precisely; repeat each temperature and calculate a mean; keep seed type, number of seeds, water and time the same.

To investigate **water** and **oxygen**, compare seeds on dry cotton wool, and seeds in boiled, cooled water under a layer of oil, with seeds on moist cotton wool in air at the same temperature.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing pollination with fertilisation.
- Calling the ovule an "egg" or the pollen grain the "male gamete". The male gamete is the **nucleus** in the pollen grain.
- Listing the stigma as a male part. Stamens are male; carpels are female.
- Saying wind-pollinated flowers have "no pollen" or "sticky pollen".
- Adding **light** to the germination conditions.
- (Extended) Defining self-pollination as "the same flower" only. It includes a **different flower on the same plant**.
- (Extended) Writing that the pollen grain itself travels down the style. The **tube** grows; the **nucleus** moves down it.

## Exam technique

- **Identify**: name the part and use position clues from the diagram.
- **State the function**: one precise verb, such as "produces pollen" or "receives pollen".
- **Describe** an adaptation: name the feature and its position or structure.
- **Explain** an adaptation: add how it helps pollination.
- **Distinguish** pollen: compare size, mass, surface and number in one pair of sentences.
- **Discuss** (Extended): use variation, response to environmental change and reliance on pollinators.
