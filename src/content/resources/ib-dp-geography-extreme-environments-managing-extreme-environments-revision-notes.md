Condensed recall for **3.3 Managing extreme environments** (IB DP Geography, Option C: Extreme environments). See the [Extreme environments study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-extreme-environments-study-guide/).

## Living in extreme places

- **Adaptation** using traditional knowledge: nomadic pastoralism, seasonal movement, building designs suited to climate.
- **Technology:** irrigation, engineering for frozen ground, research stations.

Traditional strategies work because they match the rhythm of the environment rather than fighting it:

| Strategy | How it reduces risk | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Nomadic pastoralism | Herds move to follow scarce grazing and water, so no one area is overused | Tuareg and Bedouin herders in the Sahara and Arabian deserts |
| Seasonal transhumance | Livestock moved between valley and high pasture with the seasons | Herding communities in the Himalaya and Andes |
| Climate-suited buildings | Thick walls, small windows and courtyards keep heat out; compact shapes keep heat in | Mud-brick desert towns; Inuit snow shelters used while travelling |
| Herding cold-adapted animals | Animals turn sparse vegetation into food, clothing and transport | Sami reindeer herding in northern Scandinavia |
| Drought spreading | Several crops, wells and kin networks spread the risk of one bad year | Farming households in the Sahel |

## Pressures

Resource extraction, tourism, irrigation agriculture, infrastructure and research stations.

Why these matter: extreme environments often hold valuable resources (oil and gas in the Arctic, copper and lithium in the Atacama, strong sunshine for solar power in deserts), and their remoteness and scenery attract tourists and scientists. Each activity brings people, roads, waste and water demand into systems that cannot absorb much disturbance.

## Opportunities versus risks

| Opportunities | Risks |
|---|---|
| Minerals and energy | Fragile ecosystems that recover slowly |
| Tourism | Permafrost thaw damaging infrastructure |
| Water storage in ice | Desertification |
| | Greater exposure to hazards |

**Desertification** is land degradation in drylands caused by a mix of climate variability and human activity, such as overgrazing, over-cultivation, removal of woody vegetation for fuel and poor irrigation that salinises soil. It is not the same as a desert advancing on its own: people and climate interact.

## Stakeholders

Indigenous communities, governments, companies, conservation organisations, visitors. Priorities often clash, for example extraction versus conservation, or tourism income versus cultural respect.

## Strategies

- Protected areas and visitor management
- Water-efficient farming and drought-resistant crops
- Engineering that accommodates frozen ground, for example raised structures
- International agreements for shared regions

## Case studies

### Engineering on permafrost: the Trans-Alaska Pipeline

The pipeline carries oil from Prudhoe Bay on Alaska's Arctic coast south to the port of Valdez and was completed in 1977. Hot oil would thaw ice-rich permafrost and make the ground subside, so long sections are raised on vertical supports fitted with **heat pipes (thermosyphons)** that carry heat out of the ground in winter and keep it frozen. The line is laid in a zigzag so that it can flex with temperature change and ground movement, and raised sections allow caribou to pass underneath. Evaluation: it shows that engineering can accommodate frozen ground, but at high cost, and the oil it carries adds to the warming that now threatens permafrost elsewhere.

### International governance: Antarctica

The **Antarctic Treaty** (signed 1959) sets the continent aside for peace and science and freezes territorial claims. The later **Protocol on Environmental Protection** (the Madrid Protocol, signed 1991) bans mineral resource activities other than scientific research and requires environmental impact assessment. Tourism is managed through guidelines agreed by treaty parties and the tour operators' association, such as limits on how many people go ashore at once. Evaluation: Antarctica has no permanent population or single government, which makes cooperation easier than in the inhabited Arctic, but enforcement relies on goodwill and tourism has grown.

### Drylands: the Sahel

The Great Green Wall, led by the African Union, began as a plan to plant a band of trees across the Sahel and has shifted towards a mosaic of land restoration projects. In Niger, **farmer-managed natural regeneration**, where farmers protect and prune tree stumps that regrow in their fields, has restored tree cover across large areas cheaply. Evaluation: low-cost, locally owned approaches tend to last longer than top-down planting, which often suffered from low survival of seedlings.

## Evaluation criteria

Environmental impact, cost, cultural respect, long-term sustainability. Ask who benefits and who bears the risk.

A quick evaluation grid helps in extended answers:

| Question | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Effective? | Does it reduce the risk or damage it targets? |
| Affordable? | Who pays, and can it be maintained? |
| Fair? | Are indigenous and local people consulted and do they gain? |
| Lasting? | Will it still work as the climate changes? |
| Transferable? | Would it work in a different extreme environment? |

## Why recovery is slow

Low productivity and harsh conditions mean vegetation and soils regrow slowly, so damage can last. Tyre tracks across tundra, for example, can remain visible for many years because the short growing season limits regrowth and the disturbed ground thaws more deeply.

## Command words

- **Examine:** look at the strategy from several angles, including its assumptions and limits.
- **Evaluate:** weigh strengths and weaknesses and reach a judgement.
- **To what extent:** give a clear position on a scale (largely, partly, hardly) and justify it with located evidence.

## Model paragraph

*Evaluate one strategy for managing an extreme environment.* "Raised construction on thermosyphon supports, as used on the Trans-Alaska Pipeline, is effective at preventing heat transfer into permafrost, so the ground stays stable. However, it is expensive and suits high-value infrastructure rather than ordinary housing in remote settlements. It also treats symptoms rather than causes: as regional warming deepens the active layer, even well-designed supports may need upgrading. On balance it is a strong technical solution for a single asset but not a complete management strategy for a warming Arctic."

## Exam traps

- Offering ideas without weighing costs, benefits and stakeholders.
- Treating indigenous knowledge as only historical rather than a working management resource.
- Ignoring that a strategy that works in one extreme environment may fail in another.
- Using unsure place details; describe the pattern in general terms instead.
- Defining desertification as deserts simply spreading, without the human causes.
- Assuming a treaty protects a region simply because it exists: ask how it is enforced.

## Self-test

2. Name two modern pressures.
3. Why is permafrost thaw a management problem?
4. Give two evaluation criteria for a strategy.
5. Why do extreme environments recover slowly from damage?
6. How does nomadic pastoralism reduce environmental risk?
7. Define desertification.
8. Why is much of the Trans-Alaska Pipeline raised above ground?
9. What does a thermosyphon do?
10. What does the Madrid Protocol ban in Antarctica?
11. Why might farmer-managed natural regeneration last longer than tree planting by outside agencies?
12. Give one reason Antarctica is easier to govern cooperatively than the Arctic.
