This guide covers **Component 2 Modern Drama and Literary Heritage
Texts**, for Pearson Edexcel International GCSE English Literature
(4ET1), first teaching 2016. Component 2 is worth 40% of the total
qualification, externally assessed through a 1 hour 30 minute, 60-mark
written examination, and is the examined alternative to Component 3's
internally-assessed non-exam route.

## Where this fits in 4ET1

Component 1 (Poetry and Modern Prose) is compulsory for every candidate;
students then take EITHER Component 2 (the examined route covered here)
OR Component 3 (an internally-assessed, teacher-devised assignment
covering the same modern drama and literary heritage content). Component
2 shifts from Component 1's poetry and prose to a full modern drama text
and a full literary heritage text.

## Syllabus coverage

**PEARSON EDEXCEL INTERNATIONAL GCSE ENGLISH LITERATURE (4ET1) — COMPONENT 2**

- Content overview — one modern drama text chosen from the specification's
list of set texts, and one literary heritage text chosen from the
specification's list of set texts; developing skills to analyse how
language, form, structure and contextual factors create meaning and
effect, and to maintain a critical style and informed personal response
- Section A: Modern Drama — one 30-mark essay question, chosen from a
choice of two, on the studied modern drama text; assessed against AO1
(informed personal response) and AO2 (analysis of language, form and
structure)
- Section B: Literary Heritage Texts — one 30-mark essay question,
chosen from a choice of two, on the studied literary heritage text;
assessed against AO1, AO2 and additionally AO4 (understanding the
significance of contextual factors)
- Assessment conditions — an open-book examination: prescribed, entirely
unmarked editions of the set texts are permitted in the exam room, and
candidates or centres must purchase a prescribed edition of each set
text at the start of the course; available as either a paper-based or
onscreen assessment
- Set texts — the modern drama list has five titles: *A View from the
Bridge* (Arthur Miller), *An Inspector Calls* (J B Priestley), *The
Curious Incident of the Dog in the Night-time* (Mark Haddon, adapted by
Simon Stephens), *Kindertransport* (Diane Samuels) and *Death and the
King's Horseman* (Wole Soyinka). The literary heritage list is separate
and has six titles: *Romeo and Juliet*, *Macbeth* and *The Merchant of
Venice* (all William Shakespeare), *Pride and Prejudice* (Jane Austen),
*Great Expectations* (Charles Dickens) and *The Scarlet Letter*
(Nathaniel Hawthorne). Centres choose one drama text and one literary
heritage text from these two lists

## How to approach it

Because Section B specifically tests AO4 (contextual understanding)
alongside AO1 and AO2, revision for the literary heritage text needs a
genuine grasp of its historical, social or cultural context — when it
was written, what ideas or events shaped it — not just close reading of
the text's language, which is sufficient for Section A's modern drama
question but incomplete on its own for Section B.

The open-book format changes what revision should prioritise: since
candidates may bring an unmarked copy of each set text into the exam,
memorising quotations word-for-word matters less than being able to
locate relevant passages quickly and analyse them under time pressure.
Practise navigating a copy of each set text at speed — knowing roughly
where key scenes or passages fall — rather than only memorising isolated
quotations.

With two essay questions to answer in 1 hour 30 minutes across two
different texts, time management between Section A and Section B is a
real constraint: practising full-length timed responses to both a drama
and a literary heritage question in one sitting, not just individually,
builds the pacing needed on the actual exam.

## Worked example: bringing in context (Section B)

An essay question asks how a literary heritage text explores social
class. A response that only identifies moments where class appears in
the text is answering AO1/AO2; a stronger response links those moments
to the text's historical context.

```
AO1/AO2 only:  "The character is looked down on by wealthier
               characters, shown through their dismissive dialogue."
AO1/AO2 + AO4: "The character's treatment reflects the rigid class
               structure of the period in which the text was written,
               when social mobility was limited and dialogue itself
               often signalled a speaker's class through register and
               vocabulary -- the writer uses this to critique, rather
               than simply depict, that structure."
```

The second version earns AO4 credit by connecting the textual evidence
to the world the text was written in and out of, rather than treating
the text as if it existed without a historical setting.

## Common mistakes

Writing a Section B answer that is strong on textual analysis but never
engages with historical or social context, missing AO4 credit entirely.
Over-relying on memorised quotations rather than developing the skill of
locating and analysing passages in the open-book exam. Choosing between
the two essay-question options within a section without briefly planning
both, missing the option that would actually suit the candidate's
prepared material better. Running out of time on Section B because
Section A's drama question, often the more comfortable of the two, was
allowed to run over its allocated time.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is 4ET1 Component 2 open book?**
Yes. Prescribed editions of the set texts are allowed but must be completely unmarked.

**How long is the exam?**
1 hour 30 minutes for 60 marks and 40% of the qualification: one 30-mark modern drama essay and one 30-mark literary heritage essay.

**Where is context (AO4) assessed?**
In Section B, Literary Heritage, where AO4 carries 10 of the 30 marks.


## Quick revision checklist

- Build genuine contextual knowledge for the literary heritage text, not
just close reading, to meet AO4.
- Practise navigating an unmarked copy of each set text at speed rather
than memorising quotations word for word.
- Time full-length practice responses to one drama and one literary
heritage question in a single sitting.
- Briefly weigh both essay-question options in a section before
committing to one.
