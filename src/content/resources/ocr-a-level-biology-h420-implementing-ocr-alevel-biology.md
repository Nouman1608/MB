Implementing is the part of practical work where you collect results. For OCR A Level Biology A (H420) section 1.1.2 you must know how to use a wide range of practical apparatus and techniques correctly, use appropriate units for measurements, and present observations and data in an appropriate format. Written papers test this through questions on how a measurement should be made, what a unit should be, how a value should be recorded and how a table should be laid out.

This guide covers learning outcomes 1.1.2(a) to (c) of the OCR A Level Biology A specification, Version 4.1 (April 2026), first assessment 2025, in Module 1, Development of practical skills in biology. It goes into more detail than the module-wide [planning, implementing, analysing and evaluating study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-biology-planning-implementing-analysing-and-evaluating/). Use the [implementing revision notes](/resources/ocr-a-level-biology-h420-implementing-ocr-alevel-biology-revision-notes/) for quick recall and the [implementing practice questions](/resources/ocr-a-level-biology-h420-implementing-ocr-alevel-biology-practice/) to test yourself.

## Using apparatus correctly (a)

Correct use is mostly a matter of knowing what can go wrong and preventing it. The following are the points that exam answers most often rely on.

| Apparatus | Correct use | What goes wrong otherwise |
|---|---|---|
| Measuring cylinder, pipette, burette | Read the bottom of the meniscus with your eye level with the scale | Reading from above or below gives a systematic error |
| Syringe or graduated pipette | Remove air bubbles and read the volume at the right mark | Air bubbles make the volume wrong |
| Balance | Place the empty container on it and zero (tare) the balance before adding the sample; blot tissue dry before weighing | Water on the surface adds to the mass |
| Thermometer | Keep the bulb in the liquid, away from the walls, and wait for a steady reading | A reading from the air or glass is wrong |
| Water bath | Leave tubes in it long enough to reach the water temperature | Reaction starts at the wrong temperature |
| Stopwatch | Start on mixing and stop at the agreed end point, which is clearly defined | Different end points give inconsistent times |
| pH meter | Calibrate with buffers of known pH, rinse the probe between solutions | Carry-over and drift give false values |
| Colorimeter | Zero with a blank (the solvent or solution without the coloured substance) and use the same type of cuvette | Background absorbance is included in the reading |
| Gas syringe | Check that the plunger moves freely and that there are no leaks | Gas escapes and the volume is too low |

Safe working is part of correct use. Identify the hazard, assess the risk, and apply a control: eye protection for acids, alkalis and enzymes in powder form, a cutting tile for blades, hot water handled with care, and hands washed after handling biological material.

## Units (b)

Always write the unit. A number without a unit scores no marks in most calculation questions.

**Common biological quantities and units**

| Quantity | Unit | Symbol |
|---|---|---|
| Length of an organism or cell | millimetre, micrometre | mm, μm |
| Volume of liquid | cubic centimetre, cubic decimetre | cm³, dm³ |
| Mass | gram, milligram | g, mg |
| Time | second, minute | s, min |
| Concentration | mol per cubic decimetre | mol dm⁻³ |
| Rate | quantity per unit time | for example cm³ min⁻¹ |
| Temperature | degree Celsius | °C |

The specification uses the negative index form, such as mol dm⁻³ and cm³ min⁻¹.

**Converting units**

- 1 mm = 1000 μm.
- 1 cm³ = 1000 mm³.
- 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³.
- 1 g = 1000 mg.
- 1 min = 60 s.

**Worked example 1.** Convert (a) 0.25 mm to μm, (b) 3.5 cm³ to mm³, (c) 5 mg to g, (d) 45 s to minutes.

(a) 0.25 × 1000 = **250 μm**.

(b) 3.5 × 1000 = **3500 mm³**.

(c) 5 ÷ 1000 = **0.005 g**.

(d) 45 ÷ 60 = **0.75 min**.

**Check by estimation.** Going from a large unit to a smaller one gives a bigger number. If you convert 0.25 mm to μm and get 0.00025, you have gone the wrong way.

**Standard form.** Very small values, such as the size of organelles, are written in standard form. 0.0050 mol dm⁻³ is 5.0 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³. Keep the same number of significant figures when you convert, so that 0.0050 becomes 5.0 and not 5.

## Units for rates

A rate is the change in a quantity divided by the time taken. Its unit is the unit of the quantity divided by the unit of time.

- Volume of gas collected per minute: cm³ min⁻¹.
- Change in mass per hour: g h⁻¹.
- Breaths per minute: min⁻¹.
- Absorbance change per second: s⁻¹ (absorbance has no unit).

**Worked example 2.** A gas syringe collects 28 cm³ of oxygen in 4.0 minutes. Rate = 28 ÷ 4.0 = **7.0 cm³ min⁻¹**.

**Worked example 3.** In a potometer, an air bubble moves 36 mm in 3.0 minutes along a capillary tube of radius 0.5 mm. The volume of water taken up is the volume of a cylinder: π × r² × l = π × 0.5² × 36 = 28.27 mm³. The rate is 28.27 ÷ 3.0 = 9.42 mm³ min⁻¹, or **0.0094 cm³ min⁻¹** (dividing by 1000). The specification does not require you to recall the volume of a cylinder for this type of calculation, as it is provided where needed. The skill being tested is to keep the units consistent.

When you work out rates of reaction from times, remember that a rate is not the time. A reaction that takes 20 s runs faster than one that takes 40 s, so rate = 1 ÷ time, with unit s⁻¹.

## Presenting observations and data (c)

**Qualitative observations** describe what you see: colours, textures, whether a precipitate forms. Record exactly what happens, such as "orange-red precipitate" or "the solution turned from blue to brick-red", rather than "it changed". Include the time at which a change was seen, if that matters.

**Quantitative data** are measurements, recorded in a table.

**Rules for a good table**

1. Draw it with a ruler, with lines between columns.
2. Put the **independent variable in the first column**.
3. Put the **quantity and the unit in the column heading**, for example "Volume of oxygen / cm³", and write only numbers in the cells.
4. Record raw data to the **resolution of the instrument**, with the same number of decimal places in a column.
5. Leave space for repeats, a mean and any calculated value.
6. Give the table a title if more than one table is on the page.

**Worked example 4.** A student measures the volume of oxygen released in 2 minutes by a catalase reaction at three temperatures. A poor table: "Temp, vol1, vol2, vol3" with entries such as "20 degrees, 8cm³, 8.5, 9.0". An improved table:

| Temperature / °C | Volume of oxygen in 2 min / cm³ | | | Mean volume / cm³ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| | Repeat 1 | Repeat 2 | Repeat 3 | |
| 20 | 8.0 | 8.5 | 9.0 | 8.5 |
| 30 | 14.5 | 15.0 | 14.5 | 14.7 |
| 40 | 22.0 | 21.5 | 22.5 | 22.0 |

The improvements are the units in the headings, the independent variable first, the same number of decimal places for raw data (to 0.5 cm³ here, as the syringe is read to the nearest 0.5 cm³), and a calculated mean.

**Decimal places.** A balance reading to 0.01 g gives 2.40 g, never 2.4 g, because the final zero shows the resolution. A ruler reading to 1 mm gives lengths in whole millimetres, or to the nearest 0.5 mm if you can judge it. A calculated mean may be given to one more decimal place than the raw data, or to the same number of significant figures as the least precise measurement.

**Drawings and graphs** are treated in later sections: the drawing of cells under a microscope in 1.2.2 and graphs in 1.1.3.

## Exam technique

- **Write a unit** with every number, and check that it is the right way up (cm³ min⁻¹, not min cm⁻³).
- **State the apparatus reading correctly**: from the bottom of the meniscus, at eye level.
- **Say why** a step is done: "blot the potato dry so that surface water is not weighed".
- **Match the decimal places** to the instrument when you copy or record data.
- **Convert before you calculate**: put all quantities in the same unit first.

## Common mistakes

- Missing units, or units in the cells of the table instead of the headings.
- Reading the top of the meniscus, or reading from an angle.
- Forgetting to zero a balance with the container on it.
- Mixing mm and μm in a magnification or size calculation.
- Recording 2.4 g instead of 2.40 g on a balance reading to 0.01 g.
- Using time as if it were a rate.
- Putting the dependent variable in the first column.
