This guide covers section 4.2, Geometrical constructions, in the Geometry topic of the Cambridge O Level Mathematics (Syllabus D) 4024 syllabus for 2025, 2026 and 2027. The syllabus lists three outcomes: measure and draw lines and angles, construct a triangle given the lengths of all its sides with a ruler and a pair of compasses only, and draw, use and interpret nets. It also states that constructions of perpendicular bisectors and angle bisectors are not required. After this guide, use the [geometrical constructions revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-mathematics-4024-geometrical-constructions-revision-notes/) and the [geometrical constructions practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-mathematics-4024-geometrical-constructions-practice/).

## What section 4.2 asks for

1. **Measure and draw lines and angles.** A ruler must be used for all straight edges.
2. **Construct a triangle** when all three sides are known, using a ruler and compasses. Construction arcs must be shown, so do not rub them out.
3. **Draw, use and interpret nets.** The examples are nets of cubes, cuboids, prisms and pyramids, and using measurements from a net to calculate volumes and surface areas. A related idea is to build a rhombus from two triangles.

Because this is a practical section, you need a sharp pencil, a ruler marked in millimetres, a protractor and compasses with a firm hinge.

## Measuring and drawing lines

Measure to the nearest millimetre. Place the zero mark of the ruler exactly on the end of the line, not the edge of the ruler. To draw a line of a given length, mark both ends with a small cross or a neat dot and join them with the ruler.

## Measuring and drawing angles

Use a protractor with the centre mark exactly on the vertex and the baseline along one arm.

- Read from the scale that starts at 0° on the arm you have lined up.
- Decide first whether the angle is acute or obtuse. If you read 70° for an angle that is plainly wider than a right angle, you have read the wrong scale.
- If the arms are too short to reach the scale, extend them with the ruler.

To draw an angle of, say, 125° at point A on a line AB:

1. Place the centre mark of the protractor on A and the baseline on AB.
2. Find 125° on the scale that starts at 0° on AB. Mark a small dot there.
3. Remove the protractor and join A to the dot with a ruler.

Label the angle with an arc, so that it is clear which angle you drew.

For reflex angles, measure the matching acute or obtuse angle and subtract it from 360°. A reflex angle of 250° is drawn by measuring 110° on the other side.

## Constructing a triangle from three sides

To construct a triangle with sides 7 cm, 5 cm and 4 cm:

1. Draw the longest side, 7 cm, as the base AB. Using the longest side as base keeps the construction on the page.
2. Set the compasses to 5 cm. Place the point on A and draw an arc above the line.
3. Set the compasses to 4 cm. Place the point on B and draw an arc that crosses the first arc. Call the crossing C.
4. Join AC and BC with a ruler.

Measuring the finished triangle checks your accuracy. The angles should be close to the following:

| Angle | Size to 1 d.p. |
|---|---|
| angle at C (opposite the 7 cm side) | 101.5° |
| angle at B (opposite the 5 cm side) | 44.4° |
| angle at A (opposite the 4 cm side) | 34.0° |

They sum to 179.9°, because of rounding. A real drawing may be out by a degree. If the measured angle sum is nowhere near 180°, the arcs were not set correctly.

### When is a triangle possible?

The two shorter sides must add up to more than the longest side. For 7, 5, 4 we have 5 + 4 = 9, which is greater than 7, so it works. A triangle with sides 10, 4 and 3 is impossible, because 4 + 3 = 7 is less than 10, so the arcs never meet.

### Worked example 1

Construct triangle PQR with PQ = 6 cm, QR = 5 cm and PR = 4 cm. Measure angle QPR.

```
Draw PQ = 6 cm.
Arc of radius 4 cm from P.
Arc of radius 5 cm from Q.
Mark R where the arcs cross, and join PR and QR.
```

Angle QPR is opposite QR, the 5 cm side. By calculation it is 55.8°, so a good measurement is about 56°. The angle at R is 82.8° and the angle at Q is 41.4°. These sum to 180.0°.

## A rhombus from two triangles

A rhombus has four equal sides. Its diagonal splits it into two identical triangles.

### Worked example 2

Construct a rhombus ABCD with side 5 cm and diagonal AC = 6 cm.

```
Draw AC = 6 cm.
From A and from C draw arcs of radius 5 cm above the line to meet at B.
From A and from C draw arcs of radius 5 cm below the line to meet at D.
Join AB, BC, CD and DA.
```

The other diagonal BD crosses AC at its midpoint M, at a right angle. Triangle ABM has AM = 3 and AB = 5, so BM = √(25 − 9) = 4 and BD = 8 cm. The area of the rhombus is half the product of its diagonals, 1/2 × 6 × 8 = 24 cm². Those facts are ways to check your drawing: measure BD and see whether it is about 8 cm.

## Nets

A net is a flat pattern that can be folded to make a solid. A net shows every face, joined along edges, with no overlaps.

- **Cube**: six squares. There are eleven different arrangements that fold into a cube. A row of four squares with one square above and one below is the most common.
- **Cuboid**: three pairs of rectangles.
- **Prism**: two identical end faces and rectangular sides. The rectangles together form a long strip, whose length is the perimeter of the end face.
- **Pyramid**: a base and triangular faces. A square-based pyramid needs one square and four isosceles triangles.

### Is it a net?

To test a net, imagine folding it. A row of six squares cannot fold into a cube, because squares would overlap. A net must have the right number of faces, and each fold must bring edges of equal length together.

## Using a net to find surface area

The surface area of a solid is the total area of its faces, so it is the area of the net.

### Worked example 3

A cuboid is 5 cm by 3 cm by 2 cm. Find its surface area and volume.

```
faces: 2 × (5 × 3) + 2 × (5 × 2) + 2 × (3 × 2)
     = 30 + 20 + 12 = 62 cm²
volume = 5 × 3 × 2 = 30 cm³
```

### Worked example 4

A prism has a right-angled triangle as its cross-section, with sides 3 cm, 4 cm and 5 cm, and the prism is 10 cm long. Find the surface area and volume from its net.

```
two triangles: 2 × (1/2 × 3 × 4) = 12 cm²
rectangles: (3 + 4 + 5) × 10 = 120 cm²
surface area = 132 cm²
volume = cross-section area × length = 6 × 10 = 60 cm³
```

The three rectangles form one strip of width 12 cm and length 10 cm, which saves time.

### Worked example 5

The net of a square-based pyramid has a square of side 6 cm and four isosceles triangles whose height (slant height) is 5 cm. Find the surface area and the volume.

```
square: 36 cm²
four triangles: 4 × (1/2 × 6 × 5) = 60 cm²
surface area = 96 cm²
vertical height h: h² = 5² − 3² = 16, so h = 4 cm
volume = 1/3 × 36 × 4 = 48 cm³
```

The slant height is measured along a face. The vertical height is measured straight up from the centre of the base. Pythagoras connects the two, using half the base as the third side.

## Common mistakes

- Reading the wrong protractor scale.
- Erasing construction arcs. Marks are for showing the method.
- Using the compasses for a different radius than the side given, or letting the compasses slip while drawing.
- Choosing the wrong pair of sides for the arcs, so the triangle comes out with a different side from the one asked for.
- Forgetting that a net of a cube needs six squares, and that nets of prisms need two matching end faces.
- Confusing slant height with vertical height in a pyramid.
- Using a ruler with a worn end, so that the zero mark is not at the end of the ruler.

## Exam technique

- **Construct** means make an accurate drawing, so use the compasses and leave your arcs.
- **Draw** a net: use a ruler, label all dimensions, and check that the net could fold.
- **Measure** means give a value consistent with the diagram, and include units.
- **Calculate** from a net: use exact dimensions from the question, not measurements from your own drawing.
