## 3.6.1 Causes of variation

| Cause | What it means | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Genetic | Different characteristics from mutation or reproduction | Blood group, eye colour, flower colour controlled by alleles |
| Environmental | Different characteristics caused by the organism's environment (acquired characteristics) | Scar, suntan, plant height with and without mineral ions |
| Both | Genes set the potential, the environment affects how far it is reached | Human height, body mass |

**Reproduction and variation**

- Sexual reproduction: gametes fuse, genetic information from two parents is mixed, offspring vary.
- Asexual reproduction: one parent, no fusion of gametes, no mixing, no genetic variation. Offspring are clones.
- Mutation: a change in a gene that makes a new allele. Random. Original source of new alleles.

**Continuous and discontinuous (section heading).** Continuous: a full range of values (height, leaf length). Discontinuous: separate groups (blood group).

**Method: telling genes from environment**

1. Look for organisms with identical genes (clones, cuttings).
2. Keep one factor different between groups, for example nitrate.
3. A difference between groups of clones is environmental.
4. A difference between individuals in the same conditions suggests genes.

**Small reminder.** Cuttings from one plant (means 19.0 cm with nitrate and 12.0 cm without): the 7.0 cm difference is environmental, because the genes are identical.

## 3.6.2 a Theories of evolution

| | Natural selection | Lamarck |
|---|---|---|
| Starting point | Variation already present, from genes | Changes during an individual's lifetime |
| Mechanism | Best-suited individuals survive and breed | Acquired changes are inherited |
| What is inherited | Genes (alleles) | Acquired characteristics |
| Today | Accepted theory | In the vast majority of cases this inheritance cannot occur |

- Acquired characteristics (scar, tan, trained muscle) are not carried in the gametes, so they are not normally inherited.
- Lamarck-style wording to avoid: "the bird stretched its legs so its chicks had longer legs".

## 3.6.2 b Natural selection

**Three statements from the specification**

1. Individuals in a species show a wide range of variation because of differences in their genes.
2. Individuals with characteristics most suited to the environment are more likely to survive to breed successfully.
3. The genes that enabled them to survive are passed on to the next generation.

**Chain to write out**

variation (alleles) then a selection pressure (competition, predation, disease or environmental change) then survival and breeding of the best-suited then alleles passed on then allele proportion rises over many generations.

**Selection pressures** are factors that affect survival: predators, food supply, disease, climate change.

**Key distinctions**

| Say this | Not this |
|---|---|
| The population changes | The individual evolves |
| The alleles are passed on | The characteristic is passed on because it was needed |
| Variation was already there | The organism changed to fit |
| Survive to breed | Survive (alone) |

### Time scales

- Evolution is usually slow: it takes many generations.
- Count generations, not years. A bacterium dividing every 30 minutes passes through 40 generations in 20 hours; a species with a 25-year generation time needs 1000 years for 40 generations.
- Life has existed on Earth for billions of years, long enough for all present species to arise from a simple single-celled common ancestor.
- Species that cannot respond to environmental change are at risk of becoming extinct.

**Method: working with selection data**

1. Survival of a class = survivors / starting number x 100.
2. Compare classes and quote values.
3. State the characteristic that survived best.
4. Link to the explanation: if the characteristic is genetic, its alleles become more common.

**Small reminder.** Out of 240 birds, 88 survive a drought: 88 / 240 x 100 = 36.7%.

## 3.6.2 c New species

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| Isolation | Two populations become separated, for example geographically |
| Genetic variation | Each population has a wide range of alleles; mutation adds new ones |
| Natural selection | In each population, the alleles that help survival are selected |
| Speciation | Populations become so different that successful interbreeding leading to fertile offspring is no longer possible |

- Learn the four stages in this order.
- The test for separate species is fertile offspring. A mule is usually sterile, so horses and donkeys are different species even though they can mate.
- Different environments select different alleles, so isolated populations drift apart in characteristics.

## Model answers to learn

**Explain how natural selection could make hairy leaves more common in a wild plant (a made-up scenario).** Leaves vary in hairiness because of different alleles [variation]. Insects that feed on leaves find hairy leaves harder to eat, so hairy plants are damaged less [selection pressure]. Hairy plants are more likely to survive to produce seeds [survive to breed]. They pass on the alleles for hairy leaves in their seeds [genes passed on]. Over many generations the proportion of plants with the allele rises.

**Describe how two populations of one beetle species could become two species.** A new river course separates the populations (isolation). Each has a wide range of alleles, and mutation adds more (genetic variation). The two habitats differ, so different alleles are selected in each (natural selection). After many generations, beetles from the two sides cannot breed to give fertile offspring (speciation).

**Compare a Lamarck explanation with a natural selection explanation for a weightlifter whose child is also strong.** Lamarck: muscle built by training is passed to the child. Natural selection and genetics: training is an acquired characteristic and is not inherited; a child may be strong because of inherited alleles, and their own training.

## Reading data in selection questions

| If the question says | Do this |
|---|---|
| Table of survivors by class | Convert to percentages; compare classes |
| Rising proportion over generations | Quote start and end values; give the change |
| Identical clones, different treatments | State the cause is environmental |
| Two populations, different habitats | Link each habitat to the alleles favoured |

## Must-know distinctions

| Pair | Difference |
|---|---|
| Genetic and environmental variation | Inherited alleles versus conditions during development |
| Mutation and selection | Mutation creates alleles at random; selection changes how common they are |
| Natural selection and Lamarck | Selection acts on existing genetic variation; Lamarck needs acquired change to be inherited |
| Isolation and speciation | Isolation is the start (a barrier); speciation is the end (no fertile offspring) |

## Quick self-test

1. State the two kinds of cause of variation named in the specification.
2. Why do offspring of asexual reproduction show no genetic variation?
3. Give one example of an acquired characteristic.
4. What is a mutation?
5. State the main idea of Lamarck's theory.
6. Give one reason Lamarck's idea is not accepted.
7. A population of snails has shells that vary in thickness because of different alleles. Thick-shelled snails are eaten less by birds. State what happens to the alleles for thick shells over many generations.
8. A species has a generation time of 20 years. How many generations occur in 1 million years?
9. Name the four stages in the formation of a new species, in order.
10. How do scientists decide whether two populations are now separate species?
11. Two groups of identical clone seedlings have mean heights of 31.0 cm and 23.0 cm. What is the difference, and what causes it?

### Answers

1. Genetic causes and environmental causes.
2. There is one parent and no mixing of genetic information, so the offspring are clones.
3. Any one of: a scar, a suntan, muscle gained by training, a plant stunted by lack of mineral ions.
4. A change in a gene that produces a new allele.
5. Changes that occur in an organism during its lifetime can be inherited.
6. Acquired changes do not alter the alleles in the gametes; in the vast majority of cases this type of inheritance cannot occur.
7. Thick-shelled snails survive and breed more; they pass on the alleles; the alleles become more common.
8. 1 000 000 / 20 = 50 000 generations.
9. Isolation, genetic variation, natural selection, speciation.
10. Whether they can interbreed to produce fertile offspring.
11. 31.0 - 23.0 = 8.0 cm; the genes are identical, so the cause is environmental.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "the animal adapted" without saying that variation was there first.
- Saying a characteristic is passed on without naming genes or alleles.
- Missing out "survive to breed": surviving is not enough if no young are produced.
- Stating that a mutation happens because the organism needs it.
- Leaving out isolation, or describing it with no barrier.
- Ending a speciation answer without "cannot produce fertile offspring".
- Calling any difference between individuals genetic when the question describes clones or the same alleles.
- Quoting survival data without numbers, or without comparing the groups.
- Treating generations and years as the same thing in a time-scale answer.
