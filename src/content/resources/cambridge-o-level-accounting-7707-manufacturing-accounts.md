This guide goes deeper into section **5.4 Manufacturing accounts** of the Cambridge O Level Accounting 7707 syllabus for examination in 2027, 2028 and 2029. The syllabus lists "direct and indirect costs", "direct material, direct labour, prime cost and factory overheads", "how to make adjustments for work in progress", "how to calculate factory cost of production", how to prepare "manufacturing accounts, statements of profit or loss and statements of financial position", and the adjustments detailed in 5.1.

The shared [manufacturing accounts study guide](/resources/igcse-accounting-manufacturing-accounts/) explains each section of the account. This page adds a fuller worked example with costs that must be split between factory and office, an accrual inside the manufacturing account, and a check on how work in progress moves the figures. Recall the essentials with the [manufacturing accounts revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-manufacturing-accounts-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [manufacturing accounts practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-manufacturing-accounts-practice/). All businesses and figures are invented, and amounts are in dollars.

## Why a manufacturer needs an extra account

A trader buys finished goods and sells them. A manufacturer buys raw materials, pays people to turn them into products and runs a factory. Its cost of sales cannot simply use "purchases", because most of the cost of each product is created inside the business. The **manufacturing account** collects every factory cost for the year and produces one figure, the **factory cost of production**. That figure takes the place of purchases in the statement of profit or loss.

## Direct and indirect costs

A **direct cost** can be traced to each unit made. An **indirect cost** is incurred for the factory as a whole and cannot be traced to a single unit.

| Cost | Direct or indirect | Section of the manufacturing account |
|---|---|---|
| Timber used in each cabinet | Direct | Direct material |
| Carriage inwards on raw materials | Direct | Added to cost of raw materials |
| Wages of machine operators paid per cabinet made | Direct | Direct labour |
| Wages of the factory supervisor | Indirect | Factory overheads |
| Factory electricity | Indirect | Factory overheads |
| Depreciation of factory machinery | Indirect | Factory overheads |
| Office salaries | Not a factory cost | Statement of profit or loss |
| Carriage outwards on goods sold | Not a factory cost | Statement of profit or loss |

The test for the last two rows is location and purpose: a cost belongs in the manufacturing account only if it is incurred in making goods. Selling and administration costs stay in the statement of profit or loss.

## The four building blocks

1. **Cost of raw materials consumed** = opening inventory of raw materials + purchases of raw materials + carriage inwards - closing inventory of raw materials.
2. **Prime cost** = direct material + direct labour.
3. **Factory overheads**: all indirect factory costs, added to prime cost.
4. **Work in progress** adjustment: add opening work in progress, deduct closing work in progress. The result is the factory cost of production.

Work in progress is partly made goods. Goods unfinished at the start were finished this year, so their cost is added. Goods unfinished at the end will be finished next year, so their cost is carried forward.

## Worked example: Kestrel Joinery

Kestrel Joinery makes kitchen cabinets. Its year ends on 31 December 2028.

| Item | $ |
|---|---|
| Inventory at 1 January 2028: raw materials | 14,200 |
| Inventory at 1 January 2028: work in progress | 9,600 |
| Inventory at 1 January 2028: finished goods | 21,000 |
| Purchases of raw materials | 96,500 |
| Carriage inwards on raw materials | 2,300 |
| Direct wages paid | 118,000 |
| Indirect factory wages | 34,500 |
| Factory power | 18,400 |
| Rent of premises | 30,000 |
| Insurance of premises | 6,000 |
| Office salaries | 46,000 |
| Selling expenses | 12,300 |
| Revenue | 455,000 |

Additional information at 31 December 2028:

- Closing inventories: raw materials $16,800; work in progress $8,100; finished goods $24,500.
- Direct wages of $2,000 are owing.
- Rent is shared three-quarters factory, one-quarter office. Insurance is shared two-thirds factory, one-third office.
- Factory machinery cost $120,000 and is depreciated at 10% a year on cost. Office equipment depreciation is $3,000.

**Workings.** Direct labour = 118,000 + 2,000 = $120,000. Factory rent = 30,000 x 3/4 = $22,500; office rent $7,500. Factory insurance = 6,000 x 2/3 = $4,000; office insurance $2,000. Machinery depreciation = 10% x 120,000 = $12,000.

## How work in progress moves the figures

Suppose Kestrel's closing work in progress had been valued at $10,100 instead of $8,100. The factory cost of production would fall by $2,000 to $307,100. Cost of sales would fall by $2,000, and gross profit and profit for the year would each rise by $2,000. Current assets would rise by the same $2,000. This is the same logic as an error in closing inventory: an overstated closing figure overstates profit.

## Adjustments from 5.1 in a manufacturing business

The 5.1 adjustments work as usual, with one extra decision: which account receives the charge.

- An accrued or prepaid **factory** cost adjusts the manufacturing account; an office one adjusts the statement of profit or loss.
- Depreciation of factory machinery and buildings is a factory overhead; depreciation of office equipment and delivery vehicles is an expense in the statement of profit or loss.
- Irrecoverable debts and the allowance for irrecoverable debts relate to selling, so they are never in the manufacturing account.
- A shared cost is split only in the proportion the question gives.

## Common mistakes

- Putting carriage inwards in factory overheads, or carriage outwards in the manufacturing account.
- Adding closing work in progress instead of deducting it.
- Using finished goods inventory in the manufacturing account; it belongs in cost of sales.
- Missing the second half of a split cost, so the office share never reaches the statement of profit or loss.
- Leaving out a subtotal label such as "Prime cost", which the question may reward separately.

## Exam technique

Paper 2 questions on manufacturing may ask you to **prepare** the manufacturing account alone, or to complete a partly drawn statement. Label every subtotal: cost of raw materials consumed, prime cost, total factory overheads and factory cost of production. A **calculate** question such as "calculate prime cost" needs only the direct material and direct labour lines, but show each one. For **explain** questions, link the cost to its purpose: "the supervisor's wages are indirect because they cannot be traced to any single cabinet, so they are a factory overhead". In Paper 1, a common distractor puts a selling cost into factory overheads, so classify each cost before adding.

The syllabus asks for factory cost of production; it does not list a manufacturing profit, so goods move to cost of sales at cost.
