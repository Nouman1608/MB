This guide goes deeper into section **3.3.4 Making marketing decisions: using the marketing mix** of the AQA AS and A-level Business specification (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). The specification's content is:

- **the elements of the marketing mix (the 7Ps)** and the influences on and effects of changes in them, for goods and services, industrial and consumer
- **product decisions**: product portfolio analysis and the Boston Matrix, the product life cycle including extension strategies, and new product development
- **pricing decisions**, including penetration and price skimming
- **promotional decisions**: branding, social media and viral marketing
- **distribution (place) decisions**, including multi-channel distribution
- **people, process and physical environment**
- **an integrated marketing mix** and its influences (product life cycle position, Boston Matrix, type of product, marketing objectives, target market, competition and positioning)
- **the value of digital marketing and e-commerce**.

## The 7Ps

The **marketing mix** is the combination of decisions a business makes to meet its marketing objectives in its target market.

| P | Question it answers | Examples of decisions |
|---|---|---|
| Product | What do we offer? | Features, quality, design, range, packaging, after-sales service |
| Price | What will customers pay? | Price level, discounts, credit terms |
| Promotion | How do customers learn about it? | Advertising, branding, sales promotion, public relations, social media |
| Place | How and where is it bought? | Shops, online, wholesalers, delivery |
| People | Who deals with customers? | Staff skills, training, attitude |
| Process | How is the service delivered? | Booking, queuing, payment, complaints handling |
| Physical environment | What is the setting? | Store layout, website design, cleanliness, atmosphere |

The last three matter most for **services**, where the customer experiences the business directly. For **consumer** goods, mixes use mass media and shops. For **industrial** goods, sold to other businesses, there is more personal selling, technical advice, negotiated prices and long-term supply relationships.

## Product decisions

### Product portfolio analysis and the Boston Matrix

A business with several products needs to balance them. The **Boston Matrix** classifies each product by **market growth rate** and **relative market share** (the firm's share divided by that of its largest rival).

| | High relative share | Low relative share |
|---|---|---|
| **High market growth** | **Star**: market leader in a growing market | **Question mark (problem child)**: growing market but weak share |
| **Low market growth** | **Cash cow**: leader in a mature market | **Dog**: weak share in a slow market |

Typical decisions:

- **Stars**: invest to keep leadership as the market grows.
- **Cash cows**: "milk" the cash with minimal investment.
- **Question marks**: invest to build share into a star, or drop them.
- **Dogs**: consider dropping, or keep if they support other products.

The ideal portfolio uses cash from cash cows to fund question marks, which become tomorrow's stars and eventually cash cows.

*Worked example.* Harrowfield Drinks sells four products. The firm treats growth above 10% as high and relative share above 1.0 as high.

| Product | Market growth | Harrowfield's share | Largest rival's share | Relative share |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sparkling water | 12% | 30% | 15% | 30 ÷ 15 = **2.0** |
| Still water | 2% | 24% | 12% | 24 ÷ 12 = **2.0** |
| Energy drink | 14% | 6% | 20% | 6 ÷ 20 = **0.3** |
| Tonic | 1% | 5% | 25% | 5 ÷ 25 = **0.2** |

Classification: sparkling water is a **star**, still water a **cash cow**, the energy drink a **question mark** and tonic a **dog**. Cash from still water might fund promotion for the energy drink, while tonic may be dropped.

**Value:** it gives a clear picture of the portfolio and supports investment decisions. **Limits:** it uses only two measures; market growth and share are not always the best predictors of success; defining the market is a judgement; a "dog" may still earn a profit or support other sales.

### The product life cycle

The **product life cycle** shows a product's sales over time through **introduction, growth, maturity and decline**.

| Stage | Sales | Typical mix emphasis |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | Low; costs high | Build awareness; limited distribution; penetration or skimming price |
| Growth | Rising fast | Widen distribution; brand building; competitors arrive |
| Maturity | Peak, then flat | Defend share; price competition; extension strategies |
| Decline | Falling | Cut costs; reduce promotion; withdraw or harvest |

**Extension strategies** try to prolong the life of a product: finding new uses or markets, redesigning or updating packaging, adding features, new promotion, new distribution channels, price changes, or launching variants. The aim is to keep sales and revenue up or delay decline, at a cost. The model's limits: not every product follows the curve, the length of each stage is unpredictable, and a business's own actions can change the shape.

### New product development

**New product development (NPD)** brings new products to market, from idea generation, screening, development and testing, to launch.

*Influences on NPD:* customer needs and research; technology; competitors' actions; the business's finance and skills; the product portfolio (the need to replace ageing products); and law and ethical or environmental standards.

*Value:* new products renew the portfolio, can open new markets, and may give a competitive edge. *Risks:* high cost, long timescales, and a high rate of failure.

## Pricing decisions

Influences on price: costs, objectives, customer demand and elasticity, competitors' prices, the product's stage in its life cycle, and the image the business wants.

| Strategy | Description | Suits | Risks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Penetration | Low initial price to win share quickly | Mass markets; price-sensitive customers; products that can gain from economies of scale | Low early profit; hard to raise the price later; may signal low quality |
| Price skimming | High initial price, then lowered over time | Innovative or exclusive products; early adopters willing to pay | Attracts competitors; slow take-up; sales fall if price drops |

*Worked example.* Zentra launches a smart speaker. Skimming: price £200 for the first six months, selling 5,000 units, then £140 for 20,000 units a year. First-phase revenue: £200 × 5,000 = **£1,000,000**. Later annual revenue: £140 × 20,000 = **£2,800,000**. Penetration: price £90 from launch to sell 25,000 units in the first six months, giving revenue of £90 × 25,000 = **£2,250,000**. Penetration brings more revenue and share early but at a low margin per unit; skimming earns a high margin from early adopters first. Which is better depends on unit costs, how quickly rivals follow, and Zentra's objective (profit or share).

Digital technology allows **dynamic pricing**, where prices change automatically with demand, time or customer data. It can raise revenue but may anger customers who feel charged unfairly.

## Promotion decisions

The **promotional mix** includes advertising, sales promotion, public relations, personal selling and digital promotion. A business chooses the blend by considering objectives, target market, budget, product and competitors.

- **Branding.** A brand is a name, symbol or identity that makes a product recognisable. Value: customers recognise and trust it, loyalty rises, higher prices may be accepted, and new products can be launched under the name. Cost: building a brand takes years and money, and a scandal can damage it quickly.
- **Social media.** Low cost, can be targeted precisely, gives direct contact with customers and instant feedback. Risks: negative comments spread, content must be constantly produced, and results are hard to control.
- **Viral marketing.** Content so interesting that people share it themselves. Very low cost per view and wide reach if it works, but it cannot be guaranteed or controlled and it may spread for the wrong reasons.

## Place (distribution) decisions

**Distribution channels** link producers and consumers: direct (maker to customer), or through retailers and wholesalers. **Multi-channel distribution** means using several channels, such as shops, a website and a mobile app, together. Benefits: wider reach, convenience, protection if one channel fails. Costs: complexity, stock control across channels, and possible conflict, such as shops losing sales to the website. Choice depends on the product, customers' habits, costs and the image wanted. Digital marketing and **e-commerce** give small businesses national reach at low cost, 24-hour selling and rich customer data, but need delivery systems, security and strong competition on price transparency.

## People, process and physical environment

- **People:** trained, helpful staff add value in services; poor service drives customers away.
- **Process:** a simple booking system, short queues and easy returns improve customer experience; slow or confusing processes cost sales.
- **Physical environment:** a clean, well-designed shop or an easy-to-use website supports the intended image.

## The integrated marketing mix

The elements must work together and fit the product and target market. A luxury watch combines a high price, exclusive shops, skilled staff and elegant packaging. A mix that sends mixed messages weakens the brand. Influences on the right mix:

- **position in the product life cycle** (for example heavy awareness building at launch)
- **Boston Matrix classification** (invest in stars, minimal spend on cash cows)
- **type of product** (consumer or industrial, good or service)
- **marketing objectives**
- **target market**
- **competition**
- **positioning**.

Changes in one element affect others. A price cut may raise demand that distribution cannot supply. A new promotion may call for training staff to handle extra customers.

## Technique for exam questions

- Link each mix decision to the case: the product, the customers and the objective.
- **Analyse** with chains: "a penetration price will raise volume, which may reduce unit costs through economies of scale".
- **Evaluate** by judging which element matters most for this business, and by commenting on how well the elements fit together.

## Common mistakes

- Describing the 7Ps as a list without applying them to a case.
- Confusing the Boston Matrix axes (relative share, not absolute share).
- Calling any low price "penetration" without reference to a launch aim.
- Treating extension strategies as new products.
- Ignoring people, process and physical environment in services.
- Suggesting a mix without checking that the elements fit together.
