This guide covers **Topic 3, Storage devices and media**, of Cambridge
IGCSE Information and Communication Technology 0417. It follows [Topic
2, Input and output devices](/resources/igcse-cambridge-ict-input-output-devices/),
and is one of the topics in this syllabus where a precise vocabulary
distinction -- device versus media -- is itself part of what is
assessed.

## The device/media distinction

The syllabus treats **storage devices** and **storage media** as two
separate, related concepts. A storage device is the piece of hardware
that reads and writes data (a disk drive, an SSD unit). Storage media
is the actual physical material the data is recorded onto -- which, for
some technologies, is removable and separate from the device (a DVD
disc separate from a DVD drive; a memory card separate from a card
reader), while for others the device and its media are effectively one
sealed unit (a solid-state drive). Keeping this distinction explicit
in an answer, rather than using "storage device" and "storage medium"
interchangeably, is specifically what this topic expects.

## Storage devices: magnetic, optical and solid-state

You need to know the characteristics, uses, advantages and
disadvantages of storage devices across three technology families.
**Magnetic drives** include fixed and portable magnetic hard drives
and magnetic tape drives -- offering large capacity at relatively low
cost per gigabyte, but slower access than solid-state technology and
vulnerable to damage from strong magnetic fields or physical shock.
**Optical drives** include CD, DVD and Blu-ray drives -- reading data
via a laser from a disc, offering a cheap and widely compatible
format, but generally slower and lower-capacity than magnetic or
solid-state alternatives, and dependent on the disc surface remaining
clean and unscratched. **Solid-state drives**, in both fixed and
portable form, include SSDs, pen drives and flash drives -- using no
moving parts, which gives much faster access speed and greater
resistance to physical shock than magnetic or optical devices, at a
higher cost per gigabyte of capacity.

## Storage media: magnetic, optical and solid-state

You need to know the same three-way characteristics/uses/advantages/
disadvantages treatment for storage media specifically. **Magnetic
media** covers magnetic hard disks and magnetic tape -- the physical
material inside a magnetic drive. **Optical discs** covers CD, DVD and
Blu-ray -- the physical discs read by an optical drive, distinguished
from each other mainly by storage capacity (Blu-ray holding
substantially more than DVD, which in turn holds more than CD).
**Solid-state media** covers memory cards, named by their specific
formats: SD, xD and CFast. Recognising that a solid-state storage
*device* (like an SSD) and solid-state storage *media* (like an SD
card) use the same underlying technology, but the syllabus still
expects you to distinguish which term a question is actually asking
about, is a subtle but examinable point.

## Why the technology choice matters for backup and archiving

This topic connects directly to real backup and archiving decisions,
a theme the syllabus returns to elsewhere. Magnetic tape, despite
being one of the oldest technologies listed, remains in genuine use
for large-scale organisational backup precisely because of its low
cost per gigabyte at very large capacities, even though its sequential
(rather than random) access makes it far slower to retrieve a specific
file than a hard disk or SSD -- an acceptable trade-off when the media
is used for long-term archival storage rather than day-to-day access.
Optical media's read-only or write-once variants also make it useful
specifically where data must not be alterable after being written, a
property neither magnetic nor solid-state media guarantees by default.
Recognising that "advantages and disadvantages" in this topic often
depend on the *purpose* a device or medium is being chosen for --
everyday access speed versus long-term low-cost archiving versus
portability -- rather than one technology being unconditionally
superior, is central to how exam scenarios in this topic are framed.

## A worked comparison

A photographer needs to store several thousand high-resolution photos
in the field, then transfer them to a laptop for editing. A memory
card (solid-state media, likely SD or CFast depending on the camera)
is the practical choice inside the camera itself, since it has no
moving parts to be damaged by movement or vibration, and is compact
and removable. Once back at a desk, those files might then be archived
to a magnetic hard drive for its low cost per gigabyte across a large
photo library, or backed up to an optical disc such as a Blu-ray for
its cheap, widely compatible offline archival format -- illustrating
that different storage technologies commonly co-exist for different
stages of the same workflow, rather than one technology simply being
"best" in every case.

## How to approach it

Practise stating explicitly which of "device" or "media" a question is
asking about before answering, since Cambridge sometimes tests this
distinction directly rather than the underlying technology facts.
Learn the three technology families (magnetic, optical, solid-state)
as a single comparison table spanning both devices and media, with
columns for typical capacity, speed, cost per gigabyte, durability and
portability -- this table format makes "advantages and disadvantages"
questions almost mechanical to answer once memorised. Be ready to
justify a storage choice for a described scenario using at least two
of these criteria together (for example, "portable and shock-resistant"
rather than just "fast"), since single-word justifications rarely
earn full marks in this topic.
