These notes condense section 13.1, Trade flows and trading patterns, of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for 2025 and 2026 (Paper 4, Topic 13 option). For full explanations and a worked calculation, use the [13.1 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-trade-flows-and-trading-patterns/); then test yourself with the [13.1 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-trade-flows-and-trading-patterns-practice/).

## The syllabus statements

1. Visible and invisible imports and exports.
2. Global patterns of, and inequalities in, trade flows.
3. Factors: resource endowment, locational advantage, historical factors such as colonial ties, trade agreements, changes in the global market.
4. The role of the WTO and free trade; critically evaluate the impacts of trade on exporting and importing countries.
5. The nature and role of Fairtrade.

## Measures

| Measure | Formula | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Balance of trade | Exports − imports | Positive = surplus; negative = deficit |
| Visible balance | Goods exports − goods imports | Trade in physical goods |
| Invisible balance | Services exports − services imports | Tourism, shipping, finance, software |
| Terms of trade | Export price index ÷ import price index × 100 | Below 100 = exports buy fewer imports |
| Export concentration | Largest product ÷ total exports × 100 | High = vulnerable to one price |

## Patterns and inequalities

- Trade concentrated in Europe, North America and East Asia.
- Much trade between HICs is intra-industry (similar goods both ways).
- Global value chains: parts cross borders several times.
- Many LICs: small share of world trade; dependent on primary commodities.

**Why inequality persists:** low value added in raw commodities; tariff escalation; price volatility; Prebisch–Singer hypothesis of declining commodity terms of trade; high trade costs for landlocked countries and poor infrastructure.

## Factors in brief

- **Resource endowment:** sets possible exports; the resource curse warns it can harm growth.
- **Locational advantage:** near markets, shipping lanes, coasts.
- **Colonial ties:** infrastructure, language, law and old trading links.
- **Trade agreements:** free trade area → customs union → common or single market; trade creation for members and trade diversion from others (e.g. ASEAN Free Trade Area, 1992; the EU single market).
- **Changes in the global market:** containerisation, cheaper communications, rise of China, services growth, commodity cycles, new tariffs.

## Free trade and the WTO

- **Comparative advantage (Ricardo):** specialise in what you produce at relatively lower cost.
- **For free trade:** cheaper goods, larger markets, efficiency, spread of technology.
- **Against:** local producers undercut; infant industries exposed; commodity specialisation; rich-country subsidies remain.
- **Protection tools:** tariffs, quotas, subsidies, non-tariff barriers.
- **WTO roles:** rules, negotiations, monitoring, dispute settlement; special and differential treatment for developing countries.

## Impacts: evaluation questions

Who gains? Where? When (boom or slump)? What kind of export? Apply all four to both exporting and importing countries.

## Fairtrade

- **Nature:** certification and label; minimum price plus premium; social, labour and environmental standards; producer co-operatives.
- **Strengths:** price floor; premium for community projects; labour standards.
- **Limits:** small share of trade; only part of a crop sold on Fairtrade terms; certification costs; value added still captured by processors, brands and retailers.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Visible trade | Trade in physical goods |
| Invisible trade | Trade in services such as tourism, banking, shipping |
| Trade deficit | Imports greater than exports |
| Terms of trade | Price of exports relative to price of imports |
| Value added | Increase in value at each stage of production |
| Tariff escalation | Higher tariffs on processed than on raw goods |
| Comparative advantage | Lower opportunity cost of producing a good than other countries |
| Protectionism | Restricting imports to protect domestic producers |
| Quota | Limit on the quantity of an import |
| Trade diversion | Shift of imports from efficient non-members to members of a bloc |
| Customs union | Free trade area with a common external tariff |
| Fairtrade Premium | Extra sum paid to producer groups for community or business projects |

## Exam traps

- Services are invisible trade, even when they involve travel.
- Balance of trade and terms of trade are different measures.
- Rising export volumes can still mean falling income if terms of trade worsen.
- Free trade is not simply good or bad; say for whom.
- Fairtrade is a certification scheme, not an inter-government agreement.
- Name examples for each factor.

## Self-test

1. Give one visible and one invisible export.
2. A country exports US$30 billion of goods and imports US$35 billion. What is its visible balance?
3. Export price index 120, import price index 100. Calculate the terms of trade.
4. What does a terms of trade index below 100 mean?
5. What is tariff escalation?
6. State the Prebisch–Singer hypothesis.
7. What is the difference between a free trade area and a customs union?
8. Explain comparative advantage in one sentence.
9. Give two arguments against unrestricted free trade.
10. Name two roles of the WTO.
11. What two payments do Fairtrade producer groups receive?
12. Give two limitations of Fairtrade.

### Answers

1. Visible: copper, coffee or cars. Invisible: tourism, banking or shipping.
2. 30 − 35 = −US$5 billion (deficit).
3. 120 ÷ 100 × 100 = 120.
4. A unit of exports buys fewer imports than in the base year.
5. Higher tariffs on processed goods than on raw materials.
6. Primary commodity prices tend to fall over the long term relative to manufactured goods.
7. A customs union adds a common external tariff.
8. Countries gain by specialising in goods they produce at relatively lower cost and trading for others.
9. Any two: local producers undercut; infant industries exposed; commodity specialisation; rich countries keep subsidies.
10. Any two: setting rules, negotiating cuts in barriers, monitoring policies, settling disputes.
11. A minimum price and a premium.
12. Any two: small share of trade; only part of the crop sold on Fairtrade terms; certification costs; value still added elsewhere.
