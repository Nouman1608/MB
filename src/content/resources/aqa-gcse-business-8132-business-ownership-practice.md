These questions cover section 3.1.2, Business ownership, of the AQA GCSE Business (8132) specification, for first teaching from September 2017. All businesses, people and figures are invented. Short questions come first, then case-based questions. Learn the content in the [business ownership study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-8132-business-ownership/) and the [business ownership revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-8132-business-ownership-revision-notes/) first.

## Questions

**1.** Define the term *limited liability*. **[2]**

**2.** State **two** differences between a private limited company and a public limited company. **[2]**

**3.** Identify the legal structure described in each statement.

**(a)** "Kofi owns the shop himself and employs three assistants." **[1]**
**(b)** "Its shares are bought and sold on the stock exchange." **[1]**
**(c)** "Any surplus is spent on running more hostels for homeless people." **[1]**

**4.** Three partners share profit in the ratio 4:3:1. The partnership makes £96,000 profit. Calculate each partner's share. **[3]**

**5.** Explain **one** drawback of unlimited liability for a sole trader. **[3]**

**6.** Kestrel Games Ltd has 20,000 shares. Its founder, Dev, owns 11,000 of them.

**(a)** Calculate Dev's percentage of the shares. **[1]**
**(b)** The company issues 6,000 new shares to an investor at £5 each. Calculate the finance raised and Dev's new percentage. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why the change in (b) matters to Dev. **[2]**

**7.** Cardew Homes plc makes a profit after tax of £5.6 million. The directors pay 25% of it as dividends. The company has 14 million shares.

**(a)** Calculate the dividend per share. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the retained profit. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain **one** reason why the directors might choose to retain most of the profit. **[2]**

**8.** Explain **one** benefit and **one** drawback for a sole trader of taking on a partner. **[4]**

**9.** Explain why a public limited company may be at risk of a takeover. **[2]**

**10.** Explain why a charity is likely to be set up as a not-for-profit organisation rather than a company that pays dividends. **[2]**

**11.** Analyse the benefits to a growing sole trader of becoming a private limited company. **[6]**

**12.** Ruth runs Harbour Kitchen, a successful café, as a sole trader. She wants to open two more cafés, which will cost £180,000. She has two options:

- **Option 1:** form a partnership with Sam, a chef, who would invest £60,000. The rest would be a bank loan.
- **Option 2:** form a private limited company and sell 40% of the shares to members of her family for £120,000. The rest would be a bank loan.

Ruth wants to keep control of decisions about the menu and the cafés' style. She owns her own home.

Evaluate which option Ruth should choose. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** The owners' (shareholders') responsibility for the business's debts is limited [1] to the amount of money they invested in it, so their personal assets are protected [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "The business has limited debts" earns nothing; it is the owners' loss that is limited.

**2.** Any two: plc shares can be sold to the public, ltd shares only privately [1]; plc shares are usually traded on a stock exchange [1]; a plc can raise much larger amounts of finance [1]; a plc is more open to takeover [1]; a plc faces more public scrutiny [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Both companies have limited liability, so that is not a difference.

**3. (a)** Sole trader [1]
**(b)** Public limited company [1]
**(c)** Not-for-profit organisation [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A sole trader can employ staff; one owner is the deciding clue.

**4.** Total parts = 4 + 3 + 1 = 8; £96,000 ÷ 8 = £12,000 per part [1]. Shares: **£48,000**, **£36,000** and **£12,000** [1 for any two correct] [1 for all three]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Check that the shares add up to £96,000.

**5.** The owner is personally responsible for all the business's debts [1]. If the business fails and its assets do not cover what it owes, creditors can claim the owner's personal possessions, such as savings or a house [1], so the owner could lose far more than the money put into the business [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The third mark comes from the consequence for the owner, beyond the definition.

**6. (a)** 11,000 ÷ 20,000 × 100 = **55%** [1]
**(b)** Finance raised = 6,000 × £5 = **£30,000** [1]. Dev's new share = 11,000 ÷ 26,000 × 100 [1] = **42.3%** [1]
**(c)** Dev no longer owns more than half the shares [1], so other shareholders together could outvote him on major decisions, reducing his control of the company [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Forgetting to add the new shares to the total (dividing by 20,000) is the usual slip.

**7. (a)** Total dividends = 0.25 × £5,600,000 = £1,400,000 [1]; £1,400,000 ÷ 14,000,000 = **£0.10 (10p)** per share [1]
**(b)** £5,600,000 − £1,400,000 = **£4,200,000** [1]
**(c)** Retained profit is a cheap internal source of finance for growth, such as building more homes [1], which could raise future profit and the share price, benefiting shareholders later [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* "Shareholders want dividends" argues the opposite; read which way the question points.

**8.** Benefit: the partner brings extra capital [1], so the business can buy equipment or stock it could not afford before (or: extra skills, cover for absence) [1]. Drawback: decisions must be shared [1], so disagreements may slow decisions or lead to choices the original owner dislikes (or: profit is shared; unlimited liability for the partner's actions) [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each point needs development; a list of four benefits and drawbacks scores 2 at most.

**9.** Its shares can be bought by anyone on the stock exchange [1], so another company can build up enough shares to gain control without the agreement of the directors or founders [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The key idea is that shares are freely available to buy.

**10.** A charity's purpose is to benefit a cause rather than to make money for owners [1], so it reinvests any surplus in its work, which reassures donors that their money supports the cause rather than paying dividends [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the structure to its purpose and to its main source of finance, donations.

**11.** Indicative chains, up to 6.
- Limited liability [1]: the owner's personal assets, such as a house, are protected if the business fails, so she can expand with less personal risk [1].
- Easier to raise finance [1]: shares can be sold to chosen investors, and banks may lend more readily to a company, funding growth [1].
- Control can be kept [1]: by keeping more than half the shares, the founder still makes the key decisions while bringing in investors' money [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Drawbacks such as paperwork are not asked for here; keep to benefits and develop each.

**12.** Indicative points, one mark each up to 9.
- Option 1 raises £60,000, so Ruth must borrow £120,000; Option 2 raises £120,000, so she borrows only £60,000, which means lower interest payments [1].
- In a partnership Ruth keeps unlimited liability, and with a large loan her home is at risk [1].
- She would also be liable for debts from Sam's decisions [1].
- As a private limited company, liability is limited, protecting her home [1].
- Control: in a partnership Sam would share decisions, possibly about the menu, which Ruth wants to control [1].
- In the company Ruth would keep 60% of the shares, a majority, so she can still outvote the family shareholders [1].
- Sam brings chef skills that may improve the new cafés, which the family investors may not [1].
- The company means more paperwork and public accounts, and family shareholders will expect dividends [1].
- Judgement: Option 2 is better, because it protects Ruth's home, halves the bank loan and keeps her majority control, which are her stated priorities; Option 1 would be better only if Sam's skills were essential [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Use figures from the case (£60,000 against £120,000, 60% of the shares) and link the decision to Ruth's stated aim of keeping control.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying limited liability limits the company's debts.
- Listing limited liability as a difference between ltd and plc.
- Forgetting new shares when recalculating ownership.
- Partnership shares that do not add up to the total profit.
- Answers that describe structures but do not apply them to the case.
- Evaluations that ignore what the owner says she wants.
