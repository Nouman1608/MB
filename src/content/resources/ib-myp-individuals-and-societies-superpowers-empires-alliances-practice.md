This practice set covers **superpowers, empires and supra-national alliances** for IB MYP Individuals and Societies. It is aligned to the International Baccalaureate Organization, Middle Years Programme Subject Brief -- *Individuals and societies*, from 2014, which lists this topic among the on-screen examination topics. It suits MYP years 4 and 5; there is no SL/HL split. MYP has no prescribed content list -- schools design their own units -- and this set covers a topic the brief names.

Each question is labelled with the criterion it mainly trains. Real MYP work is judged against criterion level descriptors (levels 1–8), so the `[1]` points below are a revision aid, not IB marks. Norland, Estavia, Porthmere, the Merovan Empire and the Northern Shield Pact are fictional.

## Questions

**1.** (Criterion A) Define the term *superpower* and identify two indicators used to judge whether a state is one. **[3]**

**2.** (Criterion A) Distinguish between an *intergovernmental* organisation and a *supranational* one, giving a real example of each. **[4]**

**3.** (Criterion A) For each organisation, state the year it was founded: **(a)** United Nations, **(b)** NATO, **(c)** ASEAN, **(d)** African Union. **[4]**

**4.** (Criterion A) Explain two reasons why the Soviet Union declined and broke up in 1991. **[4]**

**5.** (Criterion B) The table gives figures for three fictional states in the same year.

| State | GDP (billion dollars) | Population (million) | Defence spending (billion dollars) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Norland | 2,400 | 60 | 72 |
| Estavia | 900 | 150 | 36 |
| Porthmere | 1,500 | 25 | 15 |

**(a)** Calculate the GDP per capita of each state. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate each state's defence spending as a percentage of its GDP. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest which state has the strongest claim to be the regional great power, and state one piece of further evidence you would need. **[2]**

**6.** (Criterion B) A student plans an investigation with the research question "Is NATO good?"

**(a)** Explain why this research question is weak. **[2]**
**(b)** Rewrite it as a focused research question. **[1]**
**(c)** Suggest two sources of different types the student should use, and why. **[2]**

**7.** (Criterion D) Read Source A.

> **Source A -- original text written for Marlbridge.** An imagined speech by Norland's foreign minister to its parliament, in the year Norland applied to join the Northern Shield Pact: "Alone, we are a small nation beside a large and restless neighbour. Inside the Pact, an attack on us is an attack on twelve nations. Some say we give away our freedom. I say we buy the security that makes freedom possible."

Explain one value and one limitation of Source A for a historian investigating why Norland joined the Northern Shield Pact. **[4]**

**8.** (Criterion D) Read Source B, then compare it with Source A.

> **Source B -- original text written for Marlbridge.** An imagined editorial in *The Estavian Courier*, a fictional newspaper in neighbouring Estavia, a week after Norland joined the Pact: "Norland's leaders speak of safety. In truth they have invited foreign troops to our border. The Pact is not a shield; it is a spear pointed at Estavia."

Compare and contrast the usefulness of Sources A and B for a historian studying reactions to Norland joining the Pact. **[6]**

**9.** (Criteria B and D) The table shows the revenue and war spending of the fictional Merovan Empire (million silver crowns).

| Year | 1720 | 1760 | 1800 | 1840 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Total revenue | 40 | 64 | 56 | 32 |
| War spending | 12 | 16 | 28 | 24 |

**(a)** Calculate war spending as a percentage of revenue in 1760 and in 1840. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in total revenue from 1760 to 1840. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain how the data could support the idea of imperial overstretch, and one limitation of using it alone. **[3]**

**10.** (Criterion C) Plan a one-page briefing for a youth assembly on how ASEAN and the African Union each try to keep peace among their members. Give its structure and the main points of each section. **[6]**

**11.** (Criteria A, C and D -- extended response) "Economic strength is the most important factor in making a state a superpower." To what extent do you agree? Refer to at least two real states in your answer. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** A state able to project power across the whole world [1] in several fields at once (military, economic, political, cultural) [1]. Any two indicators: total GDP; defence spending or nuclear weapons; a permanent UN Security Council seat; global cultural reach [1].
*Examiner insight:* A definition that says only "a very powerful country" misses the idea of global reach across several fields and stays in the lower levels.

**2.** Intergovernmental: states cooperate but keep full sovereignty; decisions need their agreement [1]; example: NATO, ASEAN or the AU [1]. Supranational: states pool sovereignty, so a body above the state makes binding rules [1]; example: the European Union [1].
*Examiner insight:* "Distinguish" needs the difference stated for both terms; describing only one and implying the other earns half.

**3. (a)** 1945 [1] **(b)** 1949 [1] **(c)** 1967 [1] **(d)** 2002 [1]
*Examiner insight:* Giving 1963 for the AU confuses it with the OAU it replaced, and loses that mark.

**4.** Economic stagnation meant the state could not match Western living standards and the cost of the arms race [1], which weakened support for Communist rule [1]. The war in Afghanistan (1979–1989) cost lives and money [1] and damaged the army's prestige; other valid reasons: Gorbachev's reforms from 1985, nationalist movements in the republics, the collapse of Communist rule in Eastern Europe in 1989 [1].
*Examiner insight:* "Explain" needs a reason plus how it led to decline; a list of dated events earns only the identification marks.

**5. (a)** Norland 2,400 bn ÷ 60 m = **40,000 dollars**; Estavia 900 bn ÷ 150 m = **6,000 dollars**; Porthmere 1,500 bn ÷ 25 m = **60,000 dollars** [1] correct method shown [1].
**(b)** Norland 72 ÷ 2,400 × 100 = **3%**; Estavia 36 ÷ 900 × 100 = **4%**; Porthmere 15 ÷ 1,500 × 100 = **1%** [1] method shown [1].
**(c)** Norland: largest total GDP (half the three states' combined GDP) and the largest defence budget in total [1]. Further evidence, any one: alliances, military reach, diplomatic influence, cultural reach [1].
*Examiner insight:* Choosing Porthmere for its high GDP per capita confuses average wealth with total capacity and loses the (c) judgement mark.

**6. (a)** "Good" is undefined, so it cannot be measured [1], and there is no time frame, place or aspect of NATO [1].
**(b)** For example: "How far did NATO membership change Poland's defence spending between 1999 and 2019?" [1]
**(c)** A primary source such as a government defence report, to show official commitments [1]; a secondary source such as an academic study with independent statistics, to measure change and give an outside view [1].
*Examiner insight:* A rewritten question with no time frame or measurable focus gets no credit.

**7.** Value: it is by Norland's foreign minister at the time of the decision [1], so it shows the government's official reasons for joining -- fear of a larger neighbour and collective defence [1]. Limitation: its purpose is to persuade parliament to approve membership [1], so it may stress security and hide other motives, such as trade, and does not show public opinion [1].
*Examiner insight:* A limitation such as "it is only a short extract" earns little; tie the limitation to the speaker's purpose.

**8.** Similarity: both date from the time Norland joined and both deal with security, so both show reactions [1]. Difference in origin: A is Norland's government; B is a newspaper in a neighbouring state that feels threatened [1]. A shows the official Norland view [1]; B shows hostile reaction in Estavia and how the Pact could raise tension [1]. Both are one-sided: neither shows Norland's public or other Pact members [1]. Judgement: B is more useful for reactions, since A is the decision-maker's own case, but a historian needs both [1].
*Examiner insight:* Evaluating A and then B separately, with no comparison words and no final judgement, keeps an answer in the lower bands.

**9. (a)** 1760: 16 ÷ 64 × 100 = **25%** [1]; 1840: 24 ÷ 32 × 100 = **75%** [1].
**(b)** (32 − 64) ÷ 64 × 100 = **−50%** [1].
**(c)** War spending took a rising share of a falling revenue, so the money left for everything else fell from 48 to 8 million crowns [1], which fits overstretch: military commitments costing more than the economy can support [1]. Limitation: it shows spending, not why wars or falling revenue happened; records of revolts or trade are also needed [1].
*Examiner insight:* A trend described without any figures ("war spending went up a lot") misses the data mark.

**10.** Heading and short introduction naming both organisations and the purpose [1]. ASEAN section: founded 1967; consultation and consensus; non-interference keeps diverse members talking [1]. AU section: founded 2002, replacing the OAU; suspends members after coups; may intervene in grave cases such as genocide [1]. Comparison on a shared criterion: ASEAN avoids interfering; the AU can act inside a member state [1]. Conclusion on limits, such as reliance on members' will [1]. Style for audience: clear headings, plain language, key terms explained [1].
*Examiner insight:* Criterion C rewards format and audience as well as content, so a plan with correct facts but no structure loses those points.

**11.** Model plan:
- Introduction: define superpower and state a line of argument, such as "economic strength is necessary but not sufficient" [1].
- For: a large economy pays for military power; the US economy funded global armed forces and alliances in the Cold War [1].
- For: economic weakness can end superpower status; Soviet stagnation helped cause the 1991 collapse [1].
- For: China's rise to the world's second-largest economy underpins its growing influence [1].
- Against: military power matters independently; the Soviet Union was a superpower with a much smaller economy than the US, through nuclear weapons and armed forces [1].
- Against: political power -- a Security Council veto, leading alliances such as NATO -- gives influence beyond wealth [1].
- Against: soft power; cultural and educational reach shapes how others see a country [1].
- Linking factors: economic strength makes the others possible, but becomes power only with political will and alliances [1].
- Balanced treatment of the debate about China, with evidence on both sides [1].
- Conclusion: a supported judgement on "to what extent", such as "the most important foundation, but not enough alone" [1].
*Examiner insight:* Criterion D needs a judgement on "to what extent"; an essay that lists factors and never decides stays out of the top band.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using GDP per capita instead of total GDP to judge capacity for power.
- Confusing the African Union (2002) with the OAU (1963).
- Calling NATO, ASEAN or the AU supranational.
- Value and limitation points that do not come from the source's origin or purpose.
- Source comparisons with no comparison words and no final judgement.
- Describing data trends without quoting figures or percentage changes.
- Extended answers that list factors but never judge "to what extent".
