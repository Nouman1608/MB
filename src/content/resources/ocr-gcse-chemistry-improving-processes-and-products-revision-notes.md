## Key words

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Ore | Rock from which a metal compound can be extracted at a profit |
| Reduction | Loss of oxygen (or gain of electrons) |
| Electrolysis | Breaking down an ionic compound, molten or in solution, using electricity |
| Bioleaching | Bacteria produce a metal-ion solution (leachate) from low-grade ore |
| Phytoextraction | Plants absorb metal ions; the burned plants leave metal-rich ash |
| Compromise conditions | Conditions balancing yield, rate and cost |
| Life-cycle assessment | Totals resource use and environmental impact from raw materials to disposal |
| Alloy | A metal mixed with one or more other elements; usually harder than the pure metal |
| Corrosion | A metal reacting with substances in its surroundings, such as oxygen and water |
| Sacrificial protection | A more reactive metal corrodes in place of the metal it is attached to |
| Composite | Two or more materials combined, for example fibres in a matrix |

## C6.1a: carbon extraction

Reactivity series (part): **K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al, (C), Zn, Fe, Sn, Pb, (H), Cu, Ag, Au**.

- Below carbon → heat oxide with carbon. Carbon is the reducing agent and becomes CO or CO₂.
- Above carbon → electrolysis.
- Iron: blast furnace, coke → carbon monoxide → reduces iron(III) oxide.
- Non-ferrous examples: copper, zinc, tin and lead oxides are reduced the same way.
- PAG C1: copper(II) oxide with carbon powder in a crucible, heated strongly; pink-brown copper forms.

> **Method: percentage of metal in an oxide.** (1) Find Mr. (2) Divide the total Ar of the metal atoms by Mr. (3) Multiply by 100. Then the maximum mass of metal = mass of oxide × fraction.

## C6.1b: electrolysis extraction

- **Why:** metal is above carbon, so carbon cannot remove the oxygen.
- **Cost:** large amounts of electricity, plus energy to melt the ore. Smelters are often near cheap electricity.
- **Aluminium:** oxide dissolved in molten cryolite (lower operating temperature). Cathode: Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al. Anode: 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻; oxygen burns the carbon anodes to CO₂, so they are replaced.
- **PAG C2, inert electrodes:**
  - NaCl(aq): hydrogen at the cathode (pop test), chlorine at the anode (bleaches damp litmus).
  - CuSO₄(aq): copper at the cathode (pink coating), oxygen at the anode (relights a glowing splint).
- Graph skill: mass deposited against time is a straight line; the gradient is the rate in g/min.

## C6.1c: biological methods (Higher Tier only)

| | Bioleaching | Phytoextraction |
|---|---|---|
| Works on | low-grade ore, waste tips | low-grade ore, contaminated soil |

## C6.1d–f: industrial conditions (Higher Tier only)

| | Haber | Contact |
|---|---|---|
| Equation | N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ | 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃ |
| Forward reaction | exothermic | exothermic |
| Temperature | about 450 °C | about 450 °C |
| Pressure | about 200 atm | close to atmospheric |
| Catalyst | iron | vanadium(V) oxide |
| Why that pressure | fewer gas molecules on the right, so high pressure helps yield and rate; higher still costs too much | yield already high, so extra pressure is not worth the cost |

> **Method: explaining a compromise.** (1) State which direction the change favours (Le Chatelier). (2) State its effect on rate. (3) State the cost or safety effect. (4) Conclude why the chosen value balances them.

Raw materials and energy (C6.1f): nitrogen from air, hydrogen from natural gas and steam, sulfur for SO₂. Cheap, plentiful raw materials and recycling of unreacted gas allow a lower yield per pass. Energy for heating and compression is a running cost.

## C6.1e and CM6.1i–iv: rate graphs

- Rate = change in amount ÷ time (arithmetic and ratio, CM6.1i).
- Plot product against time; the curve flattens as reactants are used up (CM6.1ii).
- Instantaneous rate = gradient of a tangent: rise ÷ run, with units such as cm³/s (CM6.1iii).
- If doubling a condition doubles the rate, rate is directly proportional to it: a straight line through the origin (CM6.1iv).
- Higher temperature, pressure or concentration and a catalyst all give steeper initial gradients.

## C6.1g: Haber process and farming

Ammonia → nitrogen fertilisers → more protein in crops → higher yields. Without synthetic ammonia, far fewer people could be fed.

## C6.1h–j: fertilisers

- **N, P, K** are the elements crops most often run short of. Compounds must be **soluble**.
- Nitrogen: ammonium salts and nitrates. Phosphorus: from phosphate rock treated with acid. Potassium: from potassium chloride, which is mined.
- **Integrated site:** Haber plant → ammonia; part oxidised → nitric acid; ammonia + nitric acid → ammonium nitrate; Contact plant → sulfuric acid; ammonia + sulfuric acid → ammonium sulfate.
- **PAG C6 laboratory route:** titrate ammonia solution (or potassium hydroxide solution) with dilute sulfuric acid using an indicator; repeat without indicator; evaporate and crystallise.

| | Laboratory | Industry |
|---|---|---|
| Scale | grams, batch | tonnes, continuous |
| Reactants | bought, dilute | made on site, concentrated |
| Energy | heating to evaporate | reaction heat reused |

## C6.1k–l: life-cycle assessment

Four stages, each with resource use and environmental impact:

1. Raw materials and manufacture.
2. Use.
3. Transport.
4. Disposal (landfill, incineration, recycling).

> **Method: interpreting LCA data.** (1) Add each product's total for one measure. (2) Scale to the same number of uses or years. (3) Compare. (4) Name a factor the data miss, such as pollution or how long the product lasts, and note that some judgements are subjective.

## C6.1m–n: recycling

- Recycling into a **different** product: crushed glass as road aggregate or sand; plastic bottles into fibres for clothing or carpets; old tyres into play-area surfacing.
- Viable when: bulk supply is reliable, the new use tolerates impurities, it saves raw materials, energy or landfill.
- Decision factors: collection and sorting costs, energy and transport, ease of separating mixed materials, market price, scarcity of the raw material, landfill charges.

## C6.1o–q: alloys and corrosion

| Alloy | Composition | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Steel | iron + carbon (stainless: + chromium, nickel) | construction, cutlery |
| Brass | copper + zinc | fittings, instruments |
| Bronze | copper + tin | statues, bearings |
| Solder | tin + lead, or tin + small amounts of copper or silver | electrical joints (low melting point) |
| Duralumin | aluminium + about 4% copper, small amounts of magnesium and manganese | aircraft (strong, low density) |

- **Rusting** needs oxygen **and** water; salt speeds it; rust is hydrated iron(III) oxide and flakes off.
- Aluminium's oxide layer protects it, so it corrodes very little.
- **Barrier:** paint, oil, grease, plastic, electroplating. Fails once scratched.
- **Sacrificial:** zinc or magnesium, more reactive than iron, oxidises first. Galvanising does both.

## C6.1r–s: materials

| Material | Typical strengths | Typical weaknesses |
|---|---|---|
| Glass | transparent, hard, unreactive | brittle |
| Clay ceramic | hard, high melting point, insulator | brittle, heavy |
| Polymer | low density, flexible, insulator | low melting point, weaker |
| Composite | strong for its density | expensive, hard to recycle |
| Metal | strong, tough, conducts, can be shaped | dense, may corrode |

Quantitative comparison: work out a ratio such as strength ÷ density or cost per kilogram, then pick the material that best matches the stated use.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the element that sits between aluminium and zinc in the reactivity series.
2. State why magnesium cannot be extracted by heating its oxide with carbon.
3. Calculate the percentage of lead in lead(II) oxide, PbO (Pb = 207, O = 16).
4. Give the products at each electrode when aqueous copper sulfate is electrolysed with inert electrodes.
5. State why the carbon anodes in aluminium extraction must be replaced.
6. (Higher Tier only) Give one advantage and one disadvantage of phytoextraction.
7. (Higher Tier only) State the catalyst and temperature used in the Contact process.
8. (Higher Tier only) A tangent passes through (10 s, 0 cm³) and (50 s, 30 cm³). Calculate the rate.
9. Calculate the percentage of nitrogen in calcium nitrate, Ca(NO₃)₂ (Ca = 40, N = 14, O = 16).
10. A lab preparation should give 4.5 g of ammonium sulfate. It gives 3.6 g. Calculate the percentage yield.
11. Name the four stages of a life-cycle assessment.
12. Explain why a scratched galvanised bucket does not rust.

### Answers

1. Carbon.
2. Magnesium lies above carbon in the reactivity series; carbon cannot take its oxygen.
3. Mr = 223; 207 ÷ 223 × 100 = **92.8%**.
4. Cathode: **copper**. Anode: **oxygen**.
5. Oxygen made at the anode reacts with the hot carbon to form carbon dioxide, so the anodes wear away.
6. Advantage: low energy or uses low-grade ore. Disadvantage: very slow or needs large areas of land.
7. Vanadium(V) oxide; about 450 °C.
8. 30 ÷ 40 = **0.75 cm³/s**.
9. Mr = 164; 28 ÷ 164 × 100 = **17.1%**.
10. 3.6 ÷ 4.5 × 100 = **80.0%**.
11. Raw materials and manufacture; use; transport; disposal.
12. Zinc is more reactive than iron, so it oxidises instead (sacrificial protection).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "carbon is more reactive than aluminium". It is less reactive.
- Saying cryolite "dissolves the aluminium". It dissolves the aluminium oxide.
- Mixing up electrodes in PAG C2: hydrogen and copper form at the **cathode**.
- (Higher Tier) Saying a catalyst shifts equilibrium or raises yield.
- (Higher Tier) Explaining 450 °C with yield only; rate and cost must be in the answer too.
- (Higher Tier) Reading the gradient of a curve from two points on the curve instead of from a tangent.
- Forgetting that fertilisers must be soluble.
- Comparing LCA totals without putting both products on the same number of uses.
- Stating that rust is iron oxide made by oxygen alone; water is needed too.
- Choosing a material without linking a named property to the stated use.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do you decide whether a metal is extracted with carbon or by electrolysis?**
Metals less reactive than carbon, such as zinc, iron, tin, lead and copper, can be extracted by heating their oxides with carbon. Metals more reactive than carbon, such as aluminium, must be extracted by electrolysis.

**Why is aluminium oxide dissolved in molten cryolite?**
Dissolving aluminium oxide in molten cryolite lowers the operating temperature and saves energy, while letting the ions move. Cryolite dissolves the aluminium oxide, not the aluminium, and it is not a catalyst.

**What conditions are needed for iron to rust?**
Iron rusts only when both oxygen and water are present, and salt speeds it up. Rust is hydrated iron(III) oxide, which flakes off and exposes more iron.

**Why must fertilisers be soluble?**
Fertilisers supply nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium, the elements crops most often run short of. The compounds must be soluble so that plant roots can absorb them.
