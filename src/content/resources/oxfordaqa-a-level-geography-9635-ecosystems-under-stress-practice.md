The section is Section B of Unit 3: Physical geography 2, a 1 hour 30 minute written exam in which you answer all questions in each section. ## Questions

**1.** Define the term "biodiversity". **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between a climatic climax and a plagioclimax. **[3]**

**3.** Outline two adaptations of tropical rainforest plants to their climate or soil. **[4]**

**4.** The table shows energy available at each trophic level in Marrow Pond, a fictional freshwater ecosystem.

| Trophic level | Energy (kJ per m² per year) |
|---|---|
| Producers (NPP) | 8,400 |
| Primary consumers | 840 |
| Secondary consumers | 76 |
| Tertiary consumers | 7 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of energy transferred from primary consumers to secondary consumers. Show your working. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the energy available falls at each trophic level. **[3]**

**5.** Students surveyed a transect inland from the strand line across Carrow Sands, a fictional dune system.

| Site | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Distance from strand line (m) | 0 | 20 | 50 | 90 | 140 | 200 | 270 | 350 |
| Number of plant species | 2 | 3 | 5 | 6 | 9 | 8 | 12 | 14 |
| Soil pH | 8.4 | 8.1 | 7.6 | 7.0 | 6.4 | 5.8 | 5.1 | 4.6 |

**(a)** Describe the change in soil pH along the transect. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate Spearman's rank correlation coefficient (rs) for distance and number of plant species, using rs = 1 - (6Σd²) / (n³ - n). The critical value at the 0.05 significance level (two-tailed) for n = 8 is 0.738. State what your result shows. **[3]**
**(c)** Using the data, explain the changes along the transect in terms of succession. **[6]**

**6.** The table shows nutrient stores at two fictional sites, Site X (tropical rainforest) and Site Y (savanna grassland).

| Store (kg per hectare) | Site X | Site Y |
|---|---|---|
| Biomass | 6,000 | 900 |
| Litter | 200 | 300 |
| Soil | 1,300 | 2,400 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of the total nutrient store held in the biomass at each site. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain the differences in nutrient storage between the two sites. **[4]**

**7.** Explain the environmental conditions needed for coral reef development. **[4]**

**8.** Kerai Creek is a fictional mangrove estuary on the edge of a growing coastal city. Its mangrove area fell from 420 hectares in 1995 to 260 hectares in 2025. Housing and a coastal road have been built, and an introduced ornamental shrub is spreading along the landward edge.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage loss of mangrove area between 1995 and 2025. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest and justify two measures to manage the impacts of change at Kerai Creek. **[6]**

**9.** With reference to a named, located coral reef, assess the relative importance of natural and human factors in the health of the reef. **[9]**

**10.** With reference to a region experiencing ecological change, discuss the extent to which the economic, social and political character of its community reflects its ecological setting. **[12]**

**11.** "Agricultural extension and intensification is the greatest threat to biodiversity in the savanna grassland biome." To what extent do you agree with this view? **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** The variety of living organisms in an area [1], at genetic, species and ecosystem (habitat) levels [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Number of species" alone gets one mark at most, because it describes species richness, not the full concept.

**2.** A climatic climax is the final, stable community that the regional climate allows [1]. A plagioclimax is a community where succession is held back or deflected by continuing human activity [1]. For example, grazing or cutting can keep heath where oak woodland would otherwise develop [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Distinguish" needs a clear contrast; two separate definitions with no example or comparison cap the answer.

**3.** Buttress roots [1] support very tall trees in shallow, nutrient-poor soils where roots spread near the surface [1]. Drip-tip leaves [1] shed heavy, frequent rainfall quickly, reducing the growth of algae and fungi on the leaf [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each adaptation earns its second mark only when you link it to a named climate or soil condition.

**4. (a)** 76 / 840 × 100 [1] = 9.0% [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Much of the energy taken in is used in respiration and lost as heat [1]. Some material is not eaten or cannot be digested and passes out as waste [1]. So each level receives only a small share, which limits the number of top consumers the pond can support [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the division in (a); a bare answer risks losing the method mark if it is wrongly rounded.

**5. (a)** Soil pH falls from 8.4 (alkaline) at the strand line to 4.6 (acidic) at 350 m [1]. pH falls between every pair of sites, a total drop of 3.8 units [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Species ranks are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 5, 7, 8, so Σd² = 2 [1]. rs = 1 - (6 × 2) / (512 - 8) = 0.976 [1]. This exceeds the two-tailed critical value of 0.738, so there is a significant strong positive relationship between distance and species number at the 0.05 level [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Near the strand line only 2-3 pioneer species survive salt spray, moving sand and dry, alkaline sand [1]. Pioneers such as sea couch grass, then marram, trap sand and add organic matter as they die [1]. Humus builds up inland, improving water and nutrient retention [1]. Leaching of shell-derived calcium carbonate lowers pH from 8.4 to 4.6 [1]. Shelter and better soils allow more species to arrive, so richness rises to 14 at site 8 [1]. The slight dip at site 6 (8 species) may show a local disturbance such as trampling, so the sequence is not perfectly regular [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* In (c), marks go to answers that quote figures from the table, not to a general description of a psammosere.

**6. (a)** Site X: 6,000 / 7,500 × 100 = 80.0% [1]. Site Y: 900 / 3,600 × 100 = 25.0% [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** At Site X, hot, wet conditions allow fast decomposition, so the litter store is small (200 kg/ha) [1]. Nutrients are taken up quickly by dense vegetation and held in a large biomass store [1]. At Site Y, lower biomass and a dry season slow uptake, so more nutrients stay in the soil (2,400 kg/ha) [1]. Heavy rainfall at Site X also leaches the soil, keeping the soil store small [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the stores' actual values; "the rainforest has more biomass" without numbers or a reason gains little.

**7.** Warm water, roughly 23-29 °C [1]. Clear, shallow water so light reaches the zooxanthellae for photosynthesis [1]. Normal sea salinity, away from large freshwater outflows [1]. Low sediment and wave action that brings oxygen and nutrients [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Explain" needs the reason, such as light for the algae, not just a list of conditions.

**8. (a)** 420 - 260 = 160 ha lost [1]. 160 / 420 × 100 = 38.1% [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Protect the remaining mangrove by law, with a no-build buffer zone along the landward edge [1]. This stops further loss to housing and leaves space for mangroves to migrate inland as sea level rises [1]. Remove the introduced shrub and replant native mangrove species [1]. This restores habitat for native fauna and reduces competition along the edge [1]. Justification: both measures target the two named causes in the scenario [1]. Local residents should be involved in replanting, so that protection is supported and lasts [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Measures must answer the scenario's causes (building and the introduced shrub); generic conservation ideas score lower.

**9.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point. Indicative content, using the Great Barrier Reef:

- Water temperature is the dominant natural stress: marine heatwaves caused mass bleaching in 2016, 2017, 2020, 2022 and 2024 [1].
- Acidification and warming are "natural" factors driven by human emissions, so the split between natural and human is blurred [1].
- Lower salinity after floods stresses inshore reefs [1].
- Algal blooms and crown-of-thorns outbreaks are linked to nutrient-rich water [1].
- Farm runoff from catchments such as the Burdekin and Fitzroy is the main source of pollution reaching the reef [1].
- Port development and dredging add local sediment [1].
- Tourism and fishing are managed by zoning, which raised highly protected zones to over a third of the park in 2004 [1].
- So local human factors are important but partly managed, while heat stress affects the whole reef [1].
- Judgement: temperature is now the most important factor, but it is human-driven, so human activity is the main cause overall [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* "Assess the relative importance" needs a ranking with a reason, not a list of factors given equal weight.

**10.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point. Indicative content, using Rondônia, Brazil:

- Rondônia's setting is rainforest on a frontier opened by BR-364, paved in the 1980s [1].
- Tax incentives and federal investment drew settlers [1].
- The population rose from about 117,000 in 1970 to over 1.1 million in 1991 [1].
- Cleared land now supports a cattle and soy economy, so the economy reflects the ecological change, not the original forest [1].
- Clearance along side roads gave the "fishbone" pattern [1].
- Socially, the community includes settlers and Indigenous peoples [1].
- Indigenous territories such as the Uru-Eu-Wau-Wau keep forest-based ways of life [1].
- Politically, land conflict between ranchers and Indigenous groups reflects competition for forest land [1].
- The Forest Code and satellite monitoring are responses to the change [1].
- Amazon clearance fell sharply after 2004, but enforcement has varied with national politics [1].
- So the community now reflects a changed setting rather than the original rainforest [1].
- Judgement: the economic character closely reflects the setting; the political character reflects conflict over it [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* Weaker answers describe deforestation only; credit goes to answers that link it to economic, social and political character as the question asks.

**11.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point. Indicative content:

- Agricultural extension converts grassland and woodland to cropland, removing habitat [1].
- Intensification brings fertiliser, pesticides and fewer fallow years [1].
- Fencing and fields block migration routes of large grazing herds [1].
- Population growth raises demand for land and food [1].
- But overgrazing by livestock may degrade more land than cropping in drier savannas [1].
- Changes in fire regimes alter the balance of grass and trees [1].
- Firewood collection removes trees near settlements [1].
- Climate change may lengthen dry seasons and shift biome boundaries [1].
- Hunting and poaching reduce large mammal numbers even inside intact habitat [1].
- Protected areas and community conservation can reduce several threats at once [1].
- The main threat varies with rainfall, population density and land tenure [1].
- Judgement: agricultural change is often the largest single threat because it removes habitat, but it works together with grazing, fire and climate [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* "To what extent" needs both sides and a reasoned judgement; agreeing outright without considering other threats limits the level.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing definitions that miss part of the concept, such as treating biodiversity as species richness.
- Not showing working in calculations, or rounding too early.
- Giving the Spearman value without comparing it with the critical value and saying what it means.
- Describing a psammosere from memory instead of using the transect data given.
- Explaining nutrient stores without quoting the store sizes in the table.
- Calling warming and acidification purely natural with no comment on human causes.
- Giving management measures that do not match the causes in the scenario.
- Using a case study as a story with no link to the question's focus.
- Finishing an extended answer without a clear judgement.
