## Section A

**1.** Define **dependency ratio** and explain **one** factor that affects it. **[3]**

**2.** Identify **two** stages of the demographic transition model and describe the birth/death rate
pattern of each. **[4]**

## Section B

**3.** Explain **two** factors affecting fertility rate in a named country. **[6]**

**4.** With reference to a named case study, evaluate the effectiveness of a country's population policy
on natural increase. **[10]**

**5.** Explain **two** push and **two** pull factors influencing rural-urban migration in a low income
country (LIC). **[8]**

**6.** With reference to a named case study, assess the success of infrastructure provision for a city
in an LIC or MIC. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** The **dependency ratio** measures the number of dependants (under 15 and over 64) relative to the
working-age population (15-64), usually expressed per 100 working-age people [1]. It is affected by
factors such as fertility rate — a higher fertility rate raises the proportion of young dependants,
raising the dependency ratio [1] [1].

**2.** **Stage 2**: birth rate remains high, death rate falls rapidly (due to improved healthcare/food
supply), so population grows rapidly [1] [1]. **Stage 4**: both birth rate and death rate are low and
stable, so population growth is slow [1] [1] (or any two valid stages with correctly matched
birth/death rate patterns).

**3.** **Economic factors** — in a country where children provide economic value (e.g. agricultural
labour, old-age security), fertility tends to remain higher, named with reference to a specific country
[1] [1] [1]. **Social/political factors** — access to education (particularly female education) and
family planning policy directly affect fertility rate, with countries investing more in these seeing
fertility fall faster [1] [1] [1].

**4.** A strong answer names a specific country's population policy (e.g. an anti-natalist or
pro-natalist policy), explains its specific measures (incentives, restrictions, education campaigns)
[1] [1] [1], evaluates evidence of its actual effect on the natural increase rate over time using real
data or trends [1] [1] [1], and reaches a supported judgement on overall effectiveness, acknowledging
any unintended consequences or limitations rather than presenting the policy as simply successful or
unsuccessful [1] [1] [1] [1].

**5.** Push factors: **lack of economic opportunity** in rural areas, e.g. limited paid employment
beyond subsistence agriculture [1] [1]; **poor access to services** such as healthcare and education in
rural areas [1] [1]. Pull factors: **perceived employment opportunities** in urban areas, e.g. in
manufacturing or the informal sector [1] [1]; **better access to services** (healthcare, education,
infrastructure) concentrated in cities [1] [1].

**6.** A strong answer names a specific city and infrastructure project (power or transport) [1] [1],
describes what the project actually provided and to whom [1] [1], evaluates its impact using evidence —
for example, improved access, economic activity, or reduced travel times — while also considering
limitations such as unequal access, cost, or unintended consequences (e.g. displacement) [1] [1] [1] [1],
and reaches a supported judgement on overall success rather than a one-sided account [1] [1].

## Exam technique for this paper

Every "management" question (like Q4 and Q6) explicitly requires a named, locatable case study — a real
country, a real migration stream, a real settlement, a real infrastructure project — and marks the
answer against AO4 (evaluating success and differing viewpoints), not just AO1 (knowledge of what
happened). A case study answer that only describes what a policy or scheme did, without assessing how
well it worked or whose interests it served, is answering at AO1 level when the question is testing AO4,
and will not access the higher mark bands regardless of how detailed the description is. Build a case
study bank that spans LICs, MICs and HICs, since a question may explicitly ask you to contrast
experiences across income levels, and having examples from only one income group leaves you unable to
answer that kind of question well.

## Practising cross-topic scenario questions

Because population, migration and settlement dynamics build on each other, the strongest exam
preparation practises questions that deliberately span two of the three topics — for instance, a
scenario where a country's changing population structure (Topic 4) drives internal migration
(Topic 5), which in turn reshapes a growing city's structure (Topic 6). Practising this kind of linked
reasoning, rather than only revising each topic in isolation, prepares candidates for Section B and C
questions that increasingly ask candidates to trace a chain of cause and consequence across the whole
paper rather than testing a single topic in a sealed compartment.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving a generic, unnamed example instead of a specific, locatable case study with real data.
- Describing a policy or project without evaluating how well it worked or whose interests it served.
- Confusing push and pull factors, or repeating the same factor phrased two ways instead of two genuinely distinct factors.
- Answering only from a single income group's case studies when the question could be better answered with a contrast across income levels.
- Confusing internal migration (within a country, e.g. rural-urban) with international migration (across borders), or applying the wrong set of push/pull factors to the wrong migration type.
