Section 12.4 of the Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 syllabus (2026–2028) has three learning outcomes. You must be able to describe and explain methods of separation and purification using a suitable solvent, filtration, crystallisation, simple distillation and fractional distillation; suggest suitable techniques "given information about the substances involved"; and identify substances and assess their purity using melting point and boiling point information. The syllabus also lists all five methods, with chromatography, among the experimental contexts for the practical paper and the Alternative to Practical.

The broader [Practical Techniques guide](/resources/practical-techniques-titrations-chromatography-separation/) summarises each method in a table. This guide explains how each method works, which details of the apparatus earn marks, and how to choose a method from data.

## The property each method uses

Every separation exploits a difference in one property:

| Method | Property that differs | Separates |
|---|---|---|
| Dissolving in a suitable solvent | solubility in that solvent | a soluble solid from an insoluble one |
| Filtration | particle size: dissolved particles pass through, undissolved solid does not | an insoluble solid from a liquid or solution |
| Crystallisation | solubility changes with temperature | a dissolved solid from its solution, as pure crystals |
| Simple distillation | boiling point: the solvent boils, the solute does not | a solvent from a solution |
| Fractional distillation | boiling points that are different but closer together | miscible liquids from each other |

## Each method in detail

### Using a suitable solvent

A solvent is chosen that dissolves one substance but not the other. Water is the usual first choice. If the wanted substance does not dissolve in water, another solvent, such as ethanol, may dissolve it. The mixture is stirred (and often warmed) so the soluble substance dissolves completely; then the mixture is filtered.

### Filtration

The mixture is poured through filter paper in a funnel. The insoluble solid is the **residue**; the liquid or solution that passes through is the **filtrate**. Washing the residue with a little distilled water removes solution clinging to it.

### Crystallisation

1. Heat the solution to evaporate some of the solvent until it is close to saturated. A test: dip a glass rod in, take it out, and see whether crystals form on the rod as it cools.
2. Leave the solution to cool. The solute is less soluble at the lower temperature, so crystals form.
3. Filter off the crystals, wash them with a small amount of cold distilled water, and dry them between filter papers or in a warm place.

*Why not evaporate to dryness?* Soluble impurities would be left mixed with the product, and a hydrated salt would lose its water of crystallisation. Crystallising leaves the impurities, which are present in small amounts, dissolved in the remaining solution.

### Simple distillation

The solution is heated in a flask. The solvent boils, and its vapour passes into a condenser, where it cools and condenses; the pure liquid, the **distillate**, is collected. The solute stays in the flask. Details that earn marks:

- The thermometer bulb is level with the side arm, so it measures the temperature of the vapour leaving the flask.
- Cold water enters the condenser at the **bottom** and leaves at the top, so the condenser stays full of cold water.
- Anti-bumping granules give smooth boiling.

### Fractional distillation

Used for miscible liquids with different boiling points, such as ethanol (boiling point 78 °C) and water (100 °C). A **fractionating column** is placed between the flask and the condenser. Vapour rises up the column, condensing and re-evaporating many times on the large surface inside; the liquid with the lower boiling point reaches the top first. The thermometer reading stays almost constant while one liquid distils, then rises; the receiver is changed to collect the next fraction. The same principle separates petroleum into fractions in industry.

## Choosing a method from information

Ask, in order:

1. **Is the wanted substance a solid or a liquid?**
2. **Is it dissolved or undissolved?** Undissolved solid in a liquid: filter.
3. **Do I want the solid from a solution?** Crystallisation (or evaporation if the solid is not affected by heat and purity matters less).
4. **Do I want the solvent from a solution?** Simple distillation.
5. **Are two or more liquids mixed?** Fractional distillation if they are miscible with different boiling points.
6. **Two solids?** Find a solvent that dissolves only one, then filter.

## Worked example 1: using data to choose methods

*Use the data (invented for this example) to suggest how to obtain each substance.*

| Substance | Melting point / °C | Boiling point / °C | Solubility in water | Solubility in ethanol |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| E | 801 | 1413 | soluble | insoluble |
| F | 1610 | 2230 | insoluble | insoluble |
| G | 114 | 290 | insoluble | soluble |

**(a) A mixture of E and F.** Add water and stir: E dissolves, F does not. Filter: F is the residue. Crystallise E from the filtrate.

**(b) A mixture of F and G.** Water dissolves neither, so use ethanol: G dissolves, F does not. Filter off F, then evaporate the ethanol (or distil it off) to obtain G.

**(c) Water from a solution of E.** Simple distillation: water boils at 100 °C and condenses; E, with a boiling point of 1413 °C, stays in the flask.

## Melting point and boiling point as evidence

| Observation | Conclusion |
|---|---|
| Melts at one sharp temperature, matching the data-book value | pure, and identified |
| Melts over a range, below the data-book value | impure |
| Boils at one temperature, matching the data-book value | pure, and identified |
| Boils over a range, or above the expected value | impure |

**Worked example 2.** *A white solid is thought to be compound H, which melts at 135 °C (value invented for this example). Sample 1 melts at 135 °C. Sample 2 starts to melt at 128 °C and is fully melted at 132 °C. Which sample is pure H?* Sample 1 matches the data value and melts sharply, so it is pure H. Sample 2 melts over a range below 135 °C, so it is H containing an impurity.

To identify an unknown, compare its measured melting or boiling point with a table of known values; the closest match identifies it only if the measurement is sharp, because an impure sample gives a lowered, spread-out melting point.

## Common mistakes

- **Saying filtration removes dissolved substances.** It removes only undissolved solids.
- **Evaporating to dryness when asked for pure crystals.**
- **Thermometer bulb in the liquid** during distillation, instead of at the side arm.
- **Water entering the condenser at the top.**
- **Using simple distillation for two liquids with close boiling points.** Use fractional distillation.
- **Saying an impurity raises the melting point.** It lowers it and spreads it over a range.
- **Missing the solvent step**: two solids cannot be filtered apart until one is dissolved.

## Exam technique

- **Describe and explain**: give each step and the reason for it, using "so that" or "because".
- **Suggest** a method from data: quote the property you are using ("E is soluble in water but F is not").
- **Identify** from melting point data: state both whether the value matches and whether it is sharp.
- Label diagrams carefully if asked: flask, thermometer, condenser, water in, water out, receiver, fractionating column.
