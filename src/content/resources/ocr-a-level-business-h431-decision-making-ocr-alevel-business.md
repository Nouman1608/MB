## The stages of a decision

A decision is a choice between alternatives. Its purpose is to move the business towards its aims and objectives with limited resources. Most structured decisions follow a sequence like this (a standard model, not specification wording):

1. **Set the objective** the decision must serve.
2. **Gather information** about the problem and the market.
3. **Identify the options**, including doing nothing.
4. **Evaluate the options** with quantitative and qualitative tools.
5. **Choose** and plan implementation.
6. **Review** the result against the objective.

The objective shapes every later stage. It decides what information is relevant, which options are worth considering and which criteria are used to compare them. A business whose objective is survival will rate options on cash; one whose objective is growth will rate them on sales or market share. The review closes the loop: if the decision did not move the business towards its objective, the next decision changes.

## Why effective decisions matter

Effective decisions use scarce resources well, so the impact reaches every stakeholder: owners through profit, employees through job security, customers through products and prices, suppliers and lenders through prompt payment, and the community through jobs. Importance rises with the size of the commitment and how hard it is to reverse. A poor choice of weekly rota costs little; a poor choice of factory site can cost a business its future.

## The five factors

The specification names five factors to take into account.

- **Level of risk.** How much could be lost compared with what the business can absorb. A loss of £50,000 is minor for a firm with £2 million of reserves and fatal for one with £30,000.
- **Nature of risk.** What kind of risk it is. Is it quantifiable, such as a known failure rate, or unquantifiable, such as a rival's response? Is it short-lived or permanent? Can it be insured? Different natures need different tools: probability-based tools suit quantifiable risk; scenarios and flexibility suit the rest.
- **Accuracy of forecasts.** Every tool that uses forecast sales, costs or probabilities inherits their errors. Ask where a forecast came from, how far ahead it looks and what past forecasts were like.
- **Volatility.** How fast and how much conditions change. In a volatile market, a decision that commits resources for years carries more risk, so flexible, reversible options gain value.
- **Potential for bias.** Managers are people. Common biases include:
  - **optimism bias:** overestimating sales and underestimating costs and time;
  - **confirmation bias:** seeking evidence that supports a favoured option and ignoring evidence against it;
  - **sunk cost thinking:** continuing a failing project because of money already spent;
  - **groupthink:** a team agreeing to avoid conflict rather than testing the idea.

Bias can be reduced by setting criteria before looking at the options, asking someone independent to challenge the forecasts and reviewing past decisions honestly.

## Classifying decisions

Two separate classifications are used.

| | Short term | Medium term | Long term |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Strategic** | Emergency sale of a division to survive a cash crisis | Entering a neighbouring region | Relocating production abroad |
| **Tactical** | A price cut to answer a rival this month | A two-year marketing campaign | Rare |
| **Operational** | Tomorrow's staff rota | An annual maintenance schedule | Rare |

Level and time horizon often line up, strategic with long term and operational with short term, but the table shows they need not. The specification does not set exact time periods, so state the ones you use.

## Tools

**Quantitative tools** use numbers: investment appraisal, decision trees, break-even analysis, ratio analysis, forecasts. They allow objective comparison but only of what can be measured, and they depend on the accuracy of their inputs. **Qualitative tools** use judgement and non-numerical evidence: SWOT analysis, expert opinion, staff and customer views, models such as Ansoff's matrix. They capture context, ethics and strategic fit but are subjective.

## Worked example 1: a weighted scoring matrix

A weighted scoring matrix (a common technique that the specification does not name) turns qualitative judgements into numbers so that options can be compared.

Kinloch Outdoor Gear must choose a warehouse site. Managers agree four criteria and their weights, then score each site out of 10.

| Criterion | Weight | Site A | Site B | Site C |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Annual cost | 0.4 | 8 | 6 | 4 |
| Road access | 0.3 | 5 | 8 | 9 |
| Staff availability | 0.2 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
| Space to expand | 0.1 | 4 | 6 | 8 |
| **Weighted score** | | **6.3** | **6.8** | **6.7** |

Working for Site B: (0.4 × 6) + (0.3 × 8) + (0.2 × 7) + (0.1 × 6) = 2.4 + 2.4 + 1.4 + 0.6 = **6.8**.

**Interpreting.** B scores highest, but only by 0.1 over C. Suppose the managers decide road access matters more than cost and swap the first two weights (cost 0.3, access 0.4). The scores become A 6.0, B 7.0, C 7.2, and C wins.

**Evaluating the course of action.** The matrix is useful because it makes the criteria and judgements visible, so they can be challenged, and it combines financial and non-financial factors in one comparison. But the weights and scores are subjective, and a small change reverses the result. A sound recommendation would choose B or C and justify the weighting: if Kinloch's objective is fast delivery to customers, access deserves the higher weight and C is the better choice; if cash is tight, B is safer. The decision should state which objective it serves.

## Worked example 2: how successful has the business been?

Rowan Hill Hotels set five objectives for the year. Revenue last year was £4.2 million.

| Objective | Target | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue growth | 8% | Revenue £4.662 million |
| Net profit margin | 12% | Net profit £419,580 |
| Room occupancy | 80% | 84% |
| Staff turnover | Below 20% | 31% |
| Guest satisfaction (out of 5) | 4.5 | 4.6 |

- Revenue growth = (4.662 − 4.2) ÷ 4.2 × 100 = **11%**, beating the target.
- Net profit margin = 419,580 ÷ 4,662,000 × 100 = **9.0%**, below the 12% target.

**Judging the extent of success.** Rowan Hill met three of five targets. Its revenue, occupancy and guest satisfaction are strong, which suggests the hotels are popular. But the margin is three percentage points below target, so costs rose faster than revenue, and staff turnover of 31% may explain part of this, through recruitment, training and agency costs. High turnover may also threaten guest satisfaction in future.

**Usefulness of the measures.** Financial measures show the result for owners but not its causes. Non-financial measures (turnover, satisfaction, occupancy) explain the financial result and can warn earlier. Neither set alone gives the full picture.

**Judgement.** Rowan Hill is a partial success: successful with customers, less successful in converting that into profit. The answer depends on the stakeholder: shareholders may be disappointed by the margin, while guests are satisfied. The most important issue to act on is staff turnover, because it links the weak financial result to a future risk to guest satisfaction.

## Common mistakes

- Treating "risk" as one factor when the specification separates its level from its nature.
- Assuming strategic decisions are always long term.
- Presenting a scoring matrix result as objective.
- Judging success on profit alone, or saying "yes" or "no" rather than "to what extent".

## Exam technique

"Distinguish" needs both terms and the difference. "Evaluate which course of action" requires criteria taken from the objectives, both kinds of evidence, a choice and a condition. "Evaluate the extent of success" means comparing results with targets, weighing which matter most and taking more than one stakeholder's view.
