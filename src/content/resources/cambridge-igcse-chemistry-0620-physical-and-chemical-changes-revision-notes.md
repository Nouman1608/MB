Condensed recall for **6.1 Physical and chemical changes**, Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620.
For full explanations and worked examples, use the
[Physical and Chemical Changes study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-physical-and-chemical-changes/).

> **Tier note:** 6.1 is **Core** only; the same outcome applies to every candidate.

## The one test

**Has a new substance formed?** Yes → chemical change. No → physical change.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Physical change | Change in state, shape or mixing in which no new substance forms |
| Chemical change | Change in which one or more new substances form |
| Product | New substance formed in a chemical change |
| Change of state | Melting, freezing, boiling, evaporating, condensing, subliming |
| Dissolving | Solute particles spread through a solvent; a physical change |
| Conservation of mass | Total mass is unchanged in a closed system — in both kinds of change |
| Separation technique | Physical method that separates substances already present |

## Differences to quote

1. **New substance:** chemical — yes; physical — no.
2. **Particles:** chemical — atoms rearranged, bonds broken and made; physical — same
   particles, only arrangement, spacing or movement changes.
3. **Properties:** chemical — product has different properties; physical — same chemical
   properties.
4. **Reversal:** physical — usually reversed by a simple physical method; chemical —
   usually not (but some can be).
5. **Equation:** physical — same formula both sides; chemical — different formulae.

Mass is conserved in **both** — it is not a difference.

## Evidence that suggests a chemical change

- Gas given off that was not there before (fizzing)
- Precipitate forming from two solutions
- Permanent colour change
- Temperature change caused by the reaction itself
- Light given out (a flame or glow)

Each is evidence, not proof: confirm that a **new substance** has formed.

## Classifying course processes

**Physical:** melting, boiling, condensing, sublimation, dissolving, filtration,
crystallisation, simple distillation, fractional distillation of crude oil,
chromatography, magnetic separation.

**Chemical:** combustion, neutralisation, displacement, thermal decomposition, cracking,
fermentation, electrolysis, rusting, polymerisation, removing water of crystallisation.

## Two-stage examples

- **Candle:** wax melts (physical) then burns (chemical).
- **Iron and sulfur:** mixing (physical; separable by magnet) then heating (chemical; iron(II)
  sulfide forms).
- **Hydrated copper(II) sulfate:** heating removes water (chemical; blue → white); adding
  water back is also chemical (white → blue, gets warm).

## "Identify" answer pattern

**Type + reason drawn from the description.**

| Description in the question | Full answer |
|---|---|
| A clear liquid is heated; vapour is collected and cools to the same clear liquid | Physical — the same substance is collected, only its state changed |
| Two colourless solutions are mixed and a white solid appears | Chemical — a precipitate, a new insoluble substance, forms |
| A grey metal is added to blue solution; a brown solid forms and the blue colour fades | Chemical — new substances form (displacement) |
| A purple solid is warmed and a purple vapour forms, then turns back to solid on a cold surface | Physical — sublimation; the substance is unchanged |
| A white powder is stirred into water and disappears; evaporating the water leaves the same white powder | Physical — dissolving; the solid is recovered unchanged |

## Particle summary

```
physical:  same particles  →  new arrangement / spacing / movement
chemical:  bonds broken → atoms rearranged → bonds made → new particles
```

## Exam traps

- "Bubbles" do not prove a reaction — boiling water bubbles too.
- Dissolving is physical: the solute can be recovered by evaporation or crystallisation.
- A reversible change can still be chemical (hydrated/anhydrous copper(II) sulfate).
- In a "differences" question, compare **both** types in each point.
- "Identify" answers need the type **and** a reason from the description.
- Apparent mass loss in an open container means a **gas escaped**, not that mass was
  destroyed.
- Separation techniques never create new substances.

## Self-test

1. What single question decides whether a change is chemical?
2. Classify the condensing of steam on a cold window, with a reason.
3. Classify the cracking of decane, with a reason.
4. Why is fractional distillation of crude oil a physical change?
5. What happens to the particles of a solid when it melts?
6. What happens to atoms during a chemical change?
7. Give two pieces of evidence that a chemical change may have happened.
8. A green solid is heated; it turns black and a gas is given off. Classify the change.
9. Explain why the burning of a candle involves both kinds of change.
10. Why is it wrong to say "chemical changes can never be reversed"?
11. A student says mass is lost when magnesium carbonate is heated in an open crucible.
    Explain the observation.
12. Describe two differences between physical and chemical changes.

**Answers**

1. Has a new substance been formed?
2. Physical — it is still water; only the state changes (gas to liquid).
3. Chemical — new substances (shorter alkanes and alkenes) form.
4. It separates hydrocarbons that are already present, using differences in boiling point.
5. The particles gain energy and start to move past one another; they are not changed.
6. Bonds are broken and new bonds made; the atoms are rearranged into new substances.
7. Any two: gas given off, precipitate, permanent colour change, temperature change caused
   by the reaction, light given out.
8. Chemical — a new substance (black solid) and a gas form.
9. Wax melts (physical, no new substance), and wax vapour burns to form carbon dioxide and
   water (chemical).
10. Some chemical changes can be reversed, e.g. anhydrous copper(II) sulfate turns back into
    the hydrated form when water is added; the test is a new substance, not reversibility.
11. Carbon dioxide gas is given off and escapes, so the solid left has less mass; total mass
    is conserved.
12. A chemical change forms a new substance but a physical change does not; physical changes
    are usually easily reversed by physical methods, chemical changes usually are not.
