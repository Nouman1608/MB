## 4.7.1 Definitions to learn word for word

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Employment | Doing paid work, for an employer or for yourself |
| Unemployment | Without a job, able and willing to work, and actively looking for work at the going wage |
| Labour force | Employed plus unemployed |
| Full employment | Everyone who wants a job at the going wage can get one; some frictional unemployment remains |

Not in the labour force: students, retired people, full-time carers, anyone not seeking work.

## 4.7.2 Changing patterns of employment

| Change | Causes to quote |
|---|---|
| Larger tertiary sector | Rising incomes mean more spending on services; machines cut the number of farm and factory workers needed; firms buy more business services |
| Larger formal economy | Firms grow and register; better education; government enforcement and wish to raise tax revenue |
| More women in the labour force | Changing social attitudes; more girls educated; anti-discrimination laws; families want a second income |
| Smaller public-sector share | Move towards a market economy; privatisation; government reduces its role in production |

Level of employment: rises when output grows; falls in a recession.

Reminder: shares of employment across three sectors always add to 100%. If primary falls from 55% to 15% and secondary is 25%, tertiary is 100 - 15 - 25 = 60%.

Worked reminder (sector numbers). Total employment is 4,000,000 and the tertiary share is 35%: 0.35 x 4,000,000 = 1,400,000 tertiary workers. If the share later reaches 50% of the same total, that is 2,000,000, a rise of 600,000.

Also link each change to development. Farm machinery frees workers, higher incomes create demand for services, and a growing economy brings more jobs into registered firms.

## 4.7.3 Measurement and the formula

**Unemployment rate (%) = unemployed / labour force x 100**

Method in steps:
1. Find the labour force: employed + unemployed.
2. Divide unemployed by that total.
3. Multiply by 100 and round as asked.
4. Write the unit: %.

Reverse questions: if unemployed = 250,000 and the rate is 5%, then labour force = 250,000 / 0.05 = 5,000,000.

| | Claimant count | Labour force survey |
|---|---|---|
| Counts | People claiming unemployment benefit | People in a sample survey who say they have no job and want one |
| For | Cheap, fast, based on records | Includes non-claimants; one definition, so comparable |
| Against | Misses non-claimants; may include people working informally | Costly; sampling error; depends on honest answers |

Worked reminder (survey scale-up). A sample of 15,000 people in the labour force contains 600 unemployed: 600 / 15,000 = 4%. If the whole labour force is 1,500,000, the estimate is 0.04 x 1,500,000 = 60,000 unemployed. If 52,000 people claim benefit, the claimant count is 8,000 below the survey estimate.

## 4.7.4 Types of unemployment

| Type | Cause | Key phrase |
|---|---|---|
| Frictional | Moving between jobs; poor information | Short-term search |
| Structural | Industry decline; mismatch of skills or location; technology | Lasting; jobs exist but not for these workers |
| Cyclical | Fall in total demand in a recession | Demand-deficient; rises and falls with the economy |

Chain for cyclical: total demand falls, sales fall, output falls, workers laid off.

Worked reminder. Labour force 10,000,000; unemployed 600,000, of which cyclical 270,000. Remaining 330,000 / 10,000,000 = 3.3%. If cyclical unemployment vanished, the rate would be 3.3%.

## 4.7.5 Consequences

| Who | Costs | Possible benefit |
|---|---|---|
| Individual | Less income; stress; lost skills and confidence | Chance to retrain |
| Firms | Lower sales and profit as spending falls; loss of skilled staff | Easier hiring; lower wage pressure |
| Economy | Lost output (inside the production possibility curve); lower tax revenue; higher benefit spending; wider inequality; social problems | Possible fall in wage-driven inflation |

Worked reminder. 20,000 more unemployed, tax lost 1,800 each, benefit paid 2,700 each. Fiscal cost = 20,000 x (1,800 + 2,700) = 90,000,000.

## 4.7.6 Policies

| Type | Policy | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Cyclical | Lower taxes, higher government spending, lower interest rates | Higher deficit; possible inflation near full capacity; time lags |
| Structural | Education, retraining, apprenticeships, relocation help, regional subsidies | Slow and costly; some workers will not retrain or move |
| Frictional | Job centres, vacancy websites, time-limited benefits | Cannot create jobs; a cut in benefits may cause hardship |

Effectiveness in one line each: stimulus is quick but costly and risks inflation; training is targeted but slow; information schemes are cheap but limited to frictional cases.

Evaluation checklist: Does it fit the type? How long will it take? What does it cost? What side effects follow? What does success depend on?

Worked reminder. Spending up 400 million, multiplier 1.5: income rises 600 million. If 40,000 of output needs one worker, jobs = 15,000.

## Chains of reasoning to write out

Use short chains, with each link causing the next.

- **Cyclical unemployment rises:** total demand falls, firms sell less, output falls, firms cut jobs, incomes fall, spending falls again.
- **Structural unemployment persists:** an industry declines, its workers' skills or homes do not match vacancies elsewhere, so they stay out of work even when the economy grows.
- **Retraining works:** government funds courses, workers gain skills firms need, vacancies are filled, unemployment falls. It works only if the skills match the vacancies and people complete the course.
- **Stimulus works:** lower taxes or higher spending, total demand rises, firms hire. It fails if the unemployment is structural or if prices rise instead of output.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Unemployed versus not in the labour force.** A student is not unemployed; a graduate looking for work is.
- **Full employment versus zero unemployment.** Full employment still has frictional unemployment.
- **Structural versus cyclical.** Structural: the jobs have gone for good, or the skills are wrong. Cyclical: jobs return when demand recovers.
- **Claimant count versus survey.** Records of benefit claims versus answers from a sample.
- **Fall in the rate versus rise in jobs.** The rate can rise while jobs also rise, if the labour force grows faster.

## Quick self-test

1. Write down how the rate of unemployment is worked out.
2. A country has 1,880,000 employed and 120,000 unemployed. Calculate the unemployment rate.
3. Unemployment is 250,000 and the rate is 5%. Calculate the labour force.
4. Employment is 7,600,000 and the rate is 5%. How many people are unemployed?
5. Name the type of unemployment affecting car workers when a factory shuts because robots now do their jobs.
6. Give one reason why the claimant count may be lower than the labour force survey estimate.
7. In a labour force of 6,000,000, unemployment falls from 300,000 to 270,000. By how many percentage points does the rate fall?
8. Give two reasons why the share of women in the labour force may rise.
9. Calculate the fiscal cost of 20,000 extra unemployed, each paying 1,800 less tax and receiving 2,700 in benefit.
10. State one reason why a tax cut may not reduce structural unemployment.

### Answers

1. Unemployed divided by the labour force, times 100.
2. Labour force = 1,880,000 + 120,000 = 2,000,000. Rate = 120,000 / 2,000,000 x 100 = **6.0%**.
3. 250,000 / 0.05 = **5,000,000**.
4. Employed are 95% of the labour force, so labour force = 7,600,000 / 0.95 = 8,000,000. Unemployed = 0.05 x 8,000,000 = **400,000**.
5. **Structural** (technology replaces labour).
6. Some unemployed people are not entitled to benefit, or do not claim, so they appear in the survey but not in the count.
7. Before: 300,000 / 6,000,000 = 5.0%. After: 270,000 / 6,000,000 = 4.5%. Fall = **0.5 percentage points**.
8. Any two: changing social attitudes; more girls in education; laws against discrimination; households wanting a second income.
9. 20,000 x 4,500 = **90,000,000**.
10. It raises demand but does not give the workers the skills or location needed for the vacancies.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing by the population or by employment instead of the labour force.
- Stating that full employment means no one is unemployed.
- Naming a type of unemployment without linking it to the cause in the question.
- Writing "unemployment causes poverty" with no chain: job lost, income falls, spending falls.
- Describing consequences for only one group when the question asks for individuals, firms and the economy.
- Recommending one policy for every type of unemployment.
- Leaving an evaluation with no judgement or no reason for the judgement.
- Leaving out the units or the percentage sign on a calculation.
