## 2.1 Microeconomics and macroeconomics

**Microeconomics** studies the choices of individual decision makers and the markets they deal in. **Macroeconomics** studies the economy as a whole.

| | Microeconomics | Macroeconomics |
|---|---|---|
| Scale | One household, one firm, one market | The whole economy |
| Decision makers | Consumers and households, workers, producers (firms) | Governments, acting for the whole economy |
| Typical question | Why did the price of rice in one market change? | Why is total output in the country growing slowly? |

Governments appear on both sides. A government that sets the price of one medicine is making a micro decision. A government that changes taxation to influence total spending is making a macro decision. The test is the scale of the question, not who is asking it.

**Worked example (classify).** State whether each is micro or macro.

1. A small bakery raises the price of its loaves. Micro: one firm and one market.
2. Country A's total output rises over a year. Macro: the whole economy.
3. A household chooses to spend less on takeaway meals. Micro: one household.
4. A government changes income tax to affect spending across the whole country. Macro: whole-economy aim.

## 2.2 The role of markets in allocating resources

### 2.2.1 How a market system works

A **market** is any arrangement that brings buyers and sellers together to trade. Buyers want to get the most from limited income. Sellers want to earn as much as they can. Resources are scarce, so every unit sold is one that others could not have.

Prices coordinate the two sides. At a high price, buyers want less and sellers offer more. At a low price, the reverse. Price is the signal that connects them.

**Equilibrium** is the price at which the quantity buyers want equals the quantity sellers offer. Nothing pushes the price to change. **Disequilibrium** is any other price, where the two quantities differ.

**Worked example (finding equilibrium).** The table shows the market for loaves in Town A. It is illustrative. Demand is built later in this guide. Supply is covered in the next section of the syllabus, so here it is simply given.

| Price (cu) | Quantity demanded | Quantity supplied | Gap | Situation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.00 | 20 | 2 | demand exceeds supply by 18 | excess demand (shortage) |
| 1.50 | 16 | 6 | demand exceeds supply by 10 | excess demand |
| 2.00 | 12 | 12 | 0 | equilibrium |
| 2.50 | 8 | 18 | supply exceeds demand by 10 | excess supply (surplus) |
| 3.00 | 4 | 24 | supply exceeds demand by 20 | excess supply |

The equilibrium price is 2.00 cu and the equilibrium quantity is 12 loaves.

**Reasoning chain at 2.50 cu.** Sellers offer 18 loaves but buyers only want 8. Ten loaves are unsold. Sellers cut the price to clear stock. As the price falls, buyers want more and sellers offer less. The gap closes. The price keeps falling until it reaches 2.00 cu, where the two quantities match.

**At 1.50 cu** the chain runs the other way. Buyers want 16 loaves but only 6 are offered. Some buyers cannot get a loaf and will pay more. The price rises, which draws in more supply and discourages some buyers, until it reaches 2.00 cu.

On a diagram, put price up the side and quantity along the bottom. Equilibrium is where the downward-sloping demand curve crosses the upward-sloping supply curve. A price above that point gives a surplus, and a price below it gives a shortage.

### 2.2.2 The three key questions

Scarcity forces every economy to decide:

- **What** to produce, because resources used for one good cannot be used for another.
- **How** to produce it, meaning which resources and methods to combine.
- **For whom** to produce, meaning who receives the output.

These three questions exist in every economy, whatever its system. Only the way of answering them differs.

### 2.2.3 How the price mechanism answers them

In a market system, prices and profit answer all three questions.

| Question | How prices answer it |
|---|---|
| What | Higher demand pushes up the price and profit. Firms move resources into that good. Falling demand pushes price and profit down, and resources move out. |
| How | Firms choose the method with the lowest cost at current resource prices. |
| For whom | Output goes to those who are willing and able to pay the price. Income decides who can pay. |

**Worked example (how).** A small bakery bakes 500 loaves a day. Method A is labour-intensive: 6 workers and a machine hire fee of 20 cu a day. Method B is capital-intensive: 2 workers and a machine hire fee of 160 cu a day. Compare the cost per loaf.

At a daily wage of 30 cu:

- Method A: 6 × 30 + 20 = 200 cu, so 200 ÷ 500 = 0.40 cu per loaf.
- Method B: 2 × 30 + 160 = 220 cu, so 220 ÷ 500 = 0.44 cu per loaf.

Method A is cheaper. Now wages rise to 50 cu:

- Method A: 6 × 50 + 20 = 320 cu, so 0.64 cu per loaf.
- Method B: 2 × 50 + 160 = 260 cu, so 0.52 cu per loaf.

Method B is now cheaper. The two methods cost the same when 6w + 20 = 2w + 160, so 4w = 140 and w = 35 cu. Above a wage of 35 cu, the bakery switches to machines. The change in a resource price has changed the answer to the question "how".

**Evaluation.** The price mechanism answers the questions without anyone planning them, and it responds as prices change. But the answer to "for whom" depends on income. A person with a real need but little income can be left out. Later units look at what governments do about this.

## 2.3 Demand

### 2.3.1 Definition and the demand curve

**Demand** is the quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to buy at each price in a given period of time. Both parts matter. Wanting a bicycle without the money to pay for it is not demand.

The **law of demand**: when the price of a good rises and other things stay the same, the quantity demanded falls. A demand curve slopes downward. It has price on the vertical axis and quantity per period on the horizontal axis.

Household X's demand for loaves in Town A (illustrative):

| Price (cu) | 1.00 | 1.50 | 2.00 | 2.50 | 3.00 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Loaves a week | 10 | 8 | 6 | 4 | 2 |

Plotted, the points slope down from left to right:

```
Price (cu)
3.00 |  *
2.50 |    *
2.00 |      *
1.50 |        *
1.00 |          *
     +----------------  Quantity (loaves a week)
        2 4 6 8 10
```

### 2.3.2 Movements along the curve

When the good's **own price** changes, you slide along the same demand curve. A fall in price gives an **extension** of demand. A rise in price gives a **contraction** of demand. The curve does not move.

**Worked example.** The price of a loaf falls from 2.00 cu to 1.50 cu. Household X buys 6 loaves at 2.00 cu and 8 at 1.50 cu. There is an extension of 2 loaves, a 33.3% rise in quantity demanded, because 2 ÷ 6 × 100 = 33.3. The price fell by 25%, because (1.50 − 2.00) ÷ 2.00 × 100 = −25.

If the price rises from 2.00 cu to 2.50 cu, the quantity falls from 6 to 4. That is a contraction.

### 2.3.3 Individual and market demand

**Individual demand** is the demand of one consumer. **Market demand** is the total of what every buyer in the market wants at each price. This is **aggregation**: add across, price by price.

**Worked example.** Three households buy loaves in Town A. Add their quantities at each price.

| Price (cu) | Household X | Household Y | Household Z | Market demand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.00 | 10 | 6 | 4 | 20 |
| 1.50 | 8 | 5 | 3 | 16 |
| 2.00 | 6 | 4 | 2 | 12 |
| 2.50 | 4 | 3 | 1 | 8 |
| 3.00 | 2 | 2 | 0 | 4 |

At 2.00 cu, 6 + 4 + 2 = 12. On a diagram, the market curve is the individual curves added sideways (horizontally). It is flatter or further right than any one of them. At 3.00 cu Household Z buys none, so it adds nothing to the total there.

A fourth household entering the market would move the market curve to the right. The number of buyers is a condition of demand.

### 2.3.4 Conditions of demand and shifts

A change in any influence on demand other than the good's own price moves the whole curve. The syllabus does not give a list, so learn these typical causes.

| Condition | Change | Effect on demand for the good |
|---|---|---|
| Income | Rise (for a normal good) | Increase |
| Tastes, fashion, advertising | Good becomes more popular | Increase |
| Price of a substitute | Rises | Increase |
| Price of a complement | Rises | Decrease |
| Population or number of buyers | Rises | Increase |
| Expected future price | Expected to rise | Increase now |

An **increase in demand** shifts the curve right: more is bought at every price. A **decrease** shifts it left.

**Worked example.** Incomes in Town A rise and demand for loaves increases by 25% at every price. Market quantities at each price become 25, 20, 15, 10 and 5. At 2.00 cu, buyers now want 15 loaves instead of 12, an increase of 3 loaves. The new curve lies to the right of the old one. If instead a health scare cut demand by 25% at every price, quantities would be 15, 12, 9, 6 and 3. That is a decrease, a shift left.

**Reasoning chain.** Price of tea rises, so some tea drinkers switch to coffee. The demand curve for coffee shifts right. It is a shift, because the cause is not the price of coffee.

## Common errors

- Writing "demand falls" when the price of the good rises. Say the quantity demanded contracts.
- Moving the curve for a price change, or moving along it for a change in income.
- Leaving out "willing and able".
- Drawing price on the horizontal axis, or leaving the axes unlabelled.
- Adding prices instead of quantities when aggregating.
- Saying equilibrium means everyone is satisfied. It means the quantities match.
- Explaining the price mechanism without a chain: give signal, response and result.
