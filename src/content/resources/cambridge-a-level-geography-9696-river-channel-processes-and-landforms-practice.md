## Section A

**1.** Define the term "thalweg". **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between cavitation and hydraulic action as processes of river erosion. **[3]**

**3.** State the **two** transport processes that move a river's bedload. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why clay particles need a higher velocity than sand particles to be entrained. **[2]**

**5.** A fictional river reach is 5.4 km long measured along the channel. The straight-line distance between its ends is 4.0 km.

**(a)** Calculate the sinuosity of the reach. Show your working. **[2]**

**(b)** Using a threshold of 1.5, state whether the reach would be classed as meandering. **[1]**

**6.** Outline the pattern of helicoidal flow around a meander bend. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** Students surveyed a straight reach of the fictional Brock Stream. The channel was 6 m wide. Depths measured at 1 m intervals across it were 0, 0.3, 0.5, 0.6, 0.5, 0.3 and 0 m. A float took a mean of 20 seconds to travel 10 m.

**(a)** Calculate the cross-sectional area of the channel. Show your working. **[2]**

**(b)** Using a correction factor of 0.8 for surface velocity, calculate the discharge. Show your working. **[3]**

**(c)** Suggest **two** reasons why the discharge calculated may be inaccurate. **[2]**

**8.** The table gives simplified, illustrative velocities for three particles on a Hjulström curve.

| Particle | Size | Velocity needed for erosion (m/s) | Velocity below which deposition occurs (m/s) |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Fine clay | 0.80 | 0.01 |
| B | Fine sand | 0.20 | 0.02 |
| C | Gravel | 1.00 | 0.70 |

**(a)** The river flows at 0.5 m/s. State what happens to each of particles A, B and C. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why particle A behaves differently from particle B. **[3]**

**9.** Explain the formation of a waterfall and a gorge. **[6]**

**10.** Explain why some rivers develop braided channels. **[5]**

**11.** Explain the formation of meanders and oxbow lakes, referring to river cliffs and point bars. **[8]**

**12.** "Deposition is more important than erosion in forming the landforms of a floodplain." Discuss this view. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** The line of fastest flow along a river [1], which usually follows the deepest part of the channel and swings towards the outside of bends [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Hydraulic action is the force of moving water against the bed and banks [1], including air compressed in cracks, which loosens material [1]. Cavitation is the collapse of vapour bubbles in fast, turbulent water, sending small shock waves that weaken the rock surface [1]. **[3]**

**3.** Traction [1] and saltation [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Clay particles are cohesive, so they stick together [1], and they form a smooth surface layer that the flow cannot easily grip or lift [1]. **[2]**

**5. (a)** 5.4 / 4.0 [1] = 1.35 [1].

**(b)** No: 1.35 is below 1.5, so the reach is not classed as meandering (it is sinuous) [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Surface water flows towards the outer bank of the bend [1]. It plunges down at the outer bank [1], then returns across the bed towards the inner bank, giving a corkscrew motion that carries sediment to the inside of the bend [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** Area = sum of depths x interval width = (0.3 + 0.5 + 0.6 + 0.5 + 0.3) x 1 [1] = 2.2 m² [1].

**(b)** Surface velocity = 10 / 20 = 0.5 m/s [1]; mean velocity = 0.5 x 0.8 = 0.4 m/s [1]; discharge = 2.2 x 0.4 = 0.88 cumecs [1].

**(c)** Two from: the float may have been slowed by wind or caught on vegetation or the banks [1]; depth readings at only seven points miss variations in the bed, so the area is approximate [1]. Accept that the correction factor may not suit this channel, or human error in timing, in place of either point. **[7]**

**8. (a)** A: not eroded from the bed, because 0.5 m/s is below 0.80 m/s, but if already in suspension it stays in transport [1]. B: eroded and transported, because 0.5 m/s exceeds 0.20 m/s [1]. C: deposited, because 0.5 m/s is below 0.70 m/s [1].

**(b)** Clay is cohesive and lies in a smooth layer, so it needs a higher velocity (0.80 against 0.20 m/s) to be entrained [1]. Sand grains are loose and large enough to protrude into the flow, so they are lifted easily [1]. Once suspended, clay is so fine that turbulence keeps it up until velocity is almost zero (0.01 m/s), so it stays in transport much longer than sand [1]. **[6]**

**9.** A waterfall forms where a band of resistant rock overlies weaker rock [1]. The weaker rock is eroded faster [1] by hydraulic action, abrasion and cavitation in the turbulent water of the plunge pool [1]. This undercuts the resistant rock, leaving an overhang [1]. The overhang eventually collapses, and the fallen blocks add to abrasion in the plunge pool [1]. Repeated collapse makes the waterfall retreat upstream, leaving a steep-sided gorge downstream [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Braided channels form where a river carries a large load of coarse sediment [1], such as below glaciers or on steep, easily eroded mountain slopes [1]. Discharge is highly variable, for example with seasonal snowmelt [1]. When discharge falls, the river loses competence and deposits gravel bars in the channel [1]. Flow divides around the bars, and weak, non-cohesive banks are easily eroded, so the channel widens and many channels form [1]. **[5]**

**11.** In riffle and pool sequences the thalweg swings from side to side, starting erosion on alternate banks [1]. On the outside of a bend, fast flow and helicoidal flow erode the bank by hydraulic action and abrasion [1], undercutting it to form a steep river cliff [1]. Helicoidal flow carries sediment across the bed to the inside of the bend, where velocity is low [1], so it is deposited as a gently sloping point bar [1]. As erosion continues on the outer banks, the bends grow and the neck between two bends narrows [1]. During a flood, the river cuts through the neck and takes the shorter, steeper route [1]. Deposition seals the ends of the old bend, leaving an oxbow lake [1]. **[8]**

**12.** For the view: levées are built by deposition of coarse sediment as overbank water slows at the channel edge [1]. The floodplain surface is raised by fine silt and clay settling from floodwater [1]. Point bars are built by deposition on the inside of bends, and over time they make up much of the floodplain [1]. Deltas at the downstream end are entirely depositional [1].

Against the view: the floodplain's width is created by lateral erosion as meanders migrate across the valley [1]. Bluffs at the floodplain edge are cut by erosion [1]. River cliffs on the outside of bends are erosional [1]. Oxbow lakes need erosion to narrow and cut through the meander neck before deposition seals them off [1].

Judgement: erosion and deposition work together, since sediment eroded from the outside of one bend is deposited on the inside of the next [1]. Deposition builds most of the floodplain's material and surface, but erosion creates the space it fills, so the view is only partly true [1]. **[10]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Answering "explain the formation" questions with a description of what the landform looks like.
- Forgetting to show working, or giving discharge without units.
- Treating the Hjulström curve as a single line instead of two.
- Describing braided channels without explaining why the river deposits its load.
- Ignoring the role of erosion in floodplain formation.
