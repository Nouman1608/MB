One club runs through the whole guide: Kelmarsh Sailing Club, year ended 31 December 2028.

## The two accounts

A club or society exists for its members, not to make a profit for an owner. It has no owner's capital and no drawings. Two accounts replace the usual statements.

A **receipts and payments account** summarises the cash book. The opening cash and bank balance is on the receipts side, the closing balance is on the payments side, and the two sides total the same. Every receipt and payment appears when the money moves, whether it buys a boat or pays for the lights.

An **income and expenditure account** does the job of a statement of profit or loss. It includes only revenue items belonging to the year, adjusted for amounts owing and prepaid. Its result is a **surplus** (income greater than expenditure) or a **deficit**.

| Kelmarsh item | Receipts and payments account | Income and expenditure account |
|---|---|---|
| New rescue boat, 1,500 | Payment of 1,500 | Not shown; only the depreciation charge appears |
| Boat shed rent paid, 2,400, with 400 owing at the year end | Payment of 2,400 | Expenditure of 2,500 after adjusting for opening and closing amounts owing |
| Subscriptions received, 9,150 | Receipt of 9,150 | Income of 9,330 after adjusting for arrears, advance payments and amounts written off |
| Opening cash and bank, 2,400 | First receipt | Never shown |

## Preparing a receipts and payments account

1. Write the opening cash and bank balance on the receipts side.
2. List every receipt from the cash book on the receipts side, and every payment on the payments side.
3. Find the closing balance as a balancing figure on the payments side, then total both sides.

Do not adjust anything at this stage: amounts owing, prepaid and depreciation do not belong in this account.

```
Kelmarsh Sailing Club
Receipts and payments account for the year ended 31 December 2028
Receipts                          $   Payments
Balance b/d                   2,400   Café supplies                  3,900
Subscriptions                 9,150   Café wages                     1,100
Café takings                  7,800   Boat shed rent                 2,400
Regatta entry fees            1,350   Insurance                        780
                                      Rescue boat                    1,500
                                      Regatta costs                    700
                                      Repairs                          640
                                      Coach's fees                   4,200
                                      Clubhouse utilities            1,150
                                      Balance c/d                    4,330
                             20,700                                 20,700
```

The balance carried down is 20,700 less payments of 16,370, which is 4,330.

## Revenue-generating activities

Many clubs run activities to raise money. Each gets its own short account, and only the net result goes to the income and expenditure account. A café or refreshments activity is prepared like a trading account, then its direct expenses are deducted. The cash book shows what was paid, so adjust the payments for trade payables:

purchases = cash paid for supplies + closing trade payables - opening trade payables

Kelmarsh paid 3,900 for café supplies. Trade payables were 420 at 1 January and 510 at 31 December, so purchases were 3,900 + 510 - 420 = 3,990. Café inventory was 350 at the start and 480 at the end.

```
Café account for the year ended 31 December 2028 ($)
Sales                                                7,800
Less cost of sales
  Opening inventory                         350
  Purchases                               3,990
                                          4,340
  Less closing inventory                    480
                                                     3,860
Gross profit                                         3,940
Less café wages                                      1,100
Profit on café                                       2,840
```

The regatta is a one-off event. Entry fees of 1,350 less costs of 700 give a surplus on the regatta of 650. If costs had been larger, the activity would show a loss, which is deducted in the income and expenditure account.

## Subscriptions

Subscriptions are a major source of income for many clubs, and the cash received is rarely the same as the income earned. The subscriptions account finds the income by balancing.

- **Subscriptions in arrears**: owed by members at the date; an other receivable (debit balance).
- **Subscriptions in advance**: received before the period they cover; an other payable (credit balance).
- **Irrecoverable subscriptions**: arrears that will never be paid; written off, and shown as expenditure.

Kelmarsh had arrears of 300 and advance payments of 200 at 1 January. At 31 December arrears were 450 and advance payments 260. A member who owed 90 left the club and the amount was written off.

```
Subscriptions account
2028                              $   2028
1 Jan  Balance b/d (arrears)    300   1 Jan  Balance b/d (advance)    200
31 Dec Income and expenditure 9,330   31 Dec Bank                   9,150
31 Dec Balance c/d (advance)    260   31 Dec Irrecoverable subs        90
                                      31 Dec Balance c/d (arrears)    450
                                9,890                                9,890
```

Subscription income is therefore 9,330. The write-off of 90 is not deducted from income; it is a separate expense.

The club also keeps an allowance for irrecoverable debts on its arrears, using the method from 4.4. It was 30 at the start. At the year end it is 10% of 450, which is 45. The increase of 15 is expenditure, and the allowance of 45 is deducted from arrears in the statement of financial position.

## Adjustments from 5.1

Section 5.5 applies the adjustments listed in 5.1. Drawings are an owner's item, so a club has none.

| Adjustment | Income and expenditure account | Statement of financial position |
|---|---|---|
| Depreciation | Expenditure | Deducted from the asset |
| Accrued expense | Added to the expense | Other payable |
| Prepaid expense | Deducted from the expense | Other receivable |
| Accrued income | Added to the income | Other receivable |
| Income received in advance | Deducted from the income | Other payable |
| Irrecoverable debt and allowance | Expenditure | Allowance deducted from receivables |

Three depreciation methods can appear. **Straight line** charges the same amount each year: (cost less any residual value) divided by the expected life. **Reducing balance** charges a percentage of the net book value each year. **Revaluation** compares valuations: depreciation = opening valuation + additions - closing valuation. A club that values its sound equipment at 2,400, buys 600 more and values it at 2,700 has depreciation of 2,400 + 600 - 2,700 = 300.

Kelmarsh's boats had a net book value of 12,000 at 1 January. With the new boat, the base is 13,500. Depreciation at 10% reducing balance is 1,350, leaving 12,150.

The adjusted figures are:
- Boat shed rent: 2,400 paid + 400 owing at the end - 300 owing at the start = 2,500.
- Insurance: 780 paid + 180 prepaid at the start - 195 prepaid at the end = 765.

## Accumulated fund

The **accumulated fund** is the club's capital: total assets less total liabilities. The opening figure comes from the opening balances, because the club has no capital account.

```
Accumulated fund at 1 January 2028
Assets: boats 12,000 + inventory 350 + arrears (300 - 30 allowance) 270
        + insurance prepaid 180 + cash and bank 2,400            = 15,200
Liabilities: advance subscriptions 200 + trade payables 420
        + rent owing 300                                         =    920
Accumulated fund                                                 = 14,280
```

Closing accumulated fund = opening accumulated fund + surplus (or - deficit): 14,280 + 2,110 = 16,390.

Both totals are 17,560. A deficit would be subtracted from the opening fund instead.

## Common errors

- Putting the opening bank balance into the income and expenditure account.
- Treating the purchase of a boat or equipment as expenditure, instead of an asset with depreciation.
- Using the cash paid for supplies as purchases without adjusting for trade payables.
- Mixing up arrears (an asset) and advance payments (a liability) in the subscriptions account.
- Deducting an irrecoverable subscription from income as well as showing it as expenditure.
- Forgetting that a club has an accumulated fund, not capital, and a surplus or deficit, not a profit or loss.
- Adding the balance of a revenue-generating activity to the wrong side: a profit is income, a loss is expenditure.
