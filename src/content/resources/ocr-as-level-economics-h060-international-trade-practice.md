## Section A: Short questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following is an export of services by the UK? **[1]**

A: A UK supermarket buying bananas from Costa Rica

B: A German firm buying insurance from a London insurer

C: A UK resident taking a holiday in Spain

D: A UK car maker buying engines from Japan

**2.** Country H has exports of 120 billion, imports of 150 billion and GDP of 450 billion. Which **one** is its trade openness? **[1]**

A: 26.7%

B: 33.3%

C: 60.0%

D: 6.7%

**3.** State **two** reasons why countries engage in international trade. **[2]**

**4.** Explain how international trade allows firms to benefit from economies of scale. **[2]**

**5.** State **two** changes in the pattern of world trade over the last few decades. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why a developing country that relies on exports of one crop faces unstable export earnings. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured and extended questions

**7.** With the same resources, Country J can produce either 60 units of food or 30 units of clothing, and Country K either 20 units of food or 40 units of clothing.

**(a)** Each country divides its resources equally between the two goods and does not trade. Calculate total output of each good. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate total output if each country specialises in the good it produces in greater quantity. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain how trade allows both countries to benefit. **[2]**

**8.** The table shows Country L's exports by destination (invented data).

| Destination | Value, year 1 (bn) | Value, year 15 (bn) |
|---|---|---|
| EU | 90 | 120 |
| United States | 30 | 45 |
| Asia | 20 | 75 |
| Other | 10 | 10 |
| Total | 150 | 250 |

**(a)** Calculate the EU's share of total exports in each year. **[2]**

**(b)** Using the data, explain how the pattern of Country L's exports has changed. **[3]**

**9.** Explain **two** reasons why world trade has grown faster than world output over most of the period since the Second World War. **[4]**

**10.** Explain **two** advantages of international trade for an emerging economy. **[4]**

**11.** Explain how increased imports of manufactured goods could cause structural unemployment in a developed economy. **[3]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that developing countries gain less from international trade than developed countries. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** B [1]. The UK firm sells a service to a foreign buyer. **[1]**

**2.** C [1]: (120 + 150) / 450 x 100 = 60.0%. **[1]**

**3.** Any two from: different natural resources or climate [1]; gains from specialisation [1]; economies of scale; greater choice for consumers; competition and access to technology. **[2]**

**4.** Selling to world markets increases the quantity a firm can sell [1], so it can produce on a larger scale, spreading fixed costs and lowering average costs [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Any two from: trade growing faster than output [1]; a larger share of trade for emerging economies such as China [1]; growth of global supply chains and trade in components; growth of services trade; more trade within regional blocs. **[2]**

**6.** World prices of single crops can change sharply with weather, harvests elsewhere and changes in demand [1]; with most export earnings from one product, a price fall cuts total earnings sharply, with nothing to offset it [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Food: 30 + 10 = **40** [1]. Clothing: 15 + 20 = **35** [1].

**(b)** Country J produces 60 food and Country K 40 clothing: **60 food and 40 clothing** [1].

**(c)** World output of both goods is higher with specialisation [1], so by exchanging food for clothing each country can consume more of both goods than it could produce alone [1]. **[5]**

**8. (a)** Year 1: 90 / 150 = **60%** [1]. Year 15: 120 / 250 = **48%** [1].

**(b)** The EU's share fell by 12 percentage points, although the value of exports to the EU rose from 90 to 120 billion [1]. Exports to Asia almost quadrupled, from 20 to 75 billion, raising Asia's share from about 13% to 30% [1]. The pattern has shifted towards Asia, while the EU remains the largest market [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Separate shares from values; a falling share alongside a rising value earns credit only if both are stated.

**9.** Reason 1: transport and communication costs fell sharply, for example through container shipping [1], making it cheaper to sell goods in distant markets [1]. Reason 2: tariffs and other barriers were reduced through international agreements and trade blocs [1], so imported goods became more competitive [1]. Other valid reasons: the growth of global supply chains; the rise of emerging economies. **[4]**

**10.** Advantage 1: export-led growth [1]; access to large foreign markets lets manufacturing expand, creating jobs and raising incomes [1]. Advantage 2: inward investment [1]; foreign firms bring technology, management skills and training, raising productivity [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Cheaper imports take market share from domestic manufacturers [1], so some firms cut output or close [1]. Workers with skills specific to those industries, often concentrated in particular regions, may not have the skills or be in the right place for the jobs being created elsewhere, so unemployment becomes structural [1]. **[3]**

**12.** Award up to 10 marks across knowledge, application, analysis and evaluation.
- Developing countries often depend on a few primary exports, so earnings are volatile [1].
- New domestic industries may struggle to compete with established foreign firms [1].
- Low-wage export jobs and weak regulation can mean poor working conditions [1].
- Developed countries export high-value services and manufactures with more stable earnings [1].
- But developing countries gain export earnings to buy capital goods and technology they cannot make [1].
- Access to large markets can support diversification and growth [1].
- Developed countries also bear costs: structural unemployment in import-competing industries [1].
- Some lower-income countries that diversified into manufacturing have grown faster than developed countries [1].
- Gains depend on what is exported, the policies used and how gains are distributed [1].
- Judgement: developing countries dependent on one commodity often gain less and face more risk, but those able to diversify can gain relatively more, so the view is true only for some developing countries [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Breaking "developing countries" into commodity-dependent and diversifying economies gives the judgement depth.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a trade deficit a loss from trade.
- Confusing shares with values.
- Writing changes in shares as per cent instead of percentage points.
- Giving the same advantages for every type of economy.
- Ignoring who loses within a country.
- Describing patterns without dates or data.
