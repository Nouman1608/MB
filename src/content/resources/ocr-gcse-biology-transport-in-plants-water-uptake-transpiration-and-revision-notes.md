## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Root hair cell | Root surface cell with a long extension; takes in water and mineral ions |
| Osmosis | Water moving through a partially permeable membrane down its concentration gradient |
| Active transport | Movement against a concentration gradient using energy from respiration |
| Transpiration | Loss of water vapour from the plant, mainly through stomata |
| Transpiration stream | Movement of water up the xylem, replacing what is lost |
| Stoma (stomata) | Pore in the leaf, controlled by two guard cells |
| Translocation | Movement of dissolved sugars in the phloem from sources to sinks |
| Potometer | Apparatus measuring the rate of water uptake by a shoot |

## B2.2f: Uptake in the root

- Water: osmosis, from the dilute soil water into the root hair cell.
- Mineral ions: mostly active transport, against the gradient.
- Adaptations: long thin hair (large surface area), thin wall, big vacuole, many mitochondria, carrier proteins.
- Then: water and ions pass inwards to the xylem.

## B2.2g and B2.2h: Transpiration, translocation and the vessels

**Transpiration in order:**

1. Water evaporates from mesophyll cell surfaces into leaf air spaces.
2. Vapour diffuses out through open stomata, down a gradient.
3. Water is pulled up the xylem to replace it, carrying mineral ions.

**Stomata:** guard cells turgid, pore open; guard cells flaccid, pore closed. Open in light for gas exchange, and shut to limit water loss.

| | Xylem | Phloem |
|---|---|---|
| Carries | Water, mineral ions | Dissolved sugars |
| Direction | Up only | Both ways |
| Cells | Dead, hollow, no end walls, lignin in walls | Living sieve tubes, perforated end walls, companion cells |
| Adaptation reasoning | Open tube means easy flow; lignin stops collapse | Perforations let sap pass; companion cells supply energy |
| Process | Transpiration stream | Translocation (sources to sinks) |

**Ringing evidence:** bark with phloem removed gives a swelling above the cut and starved tissue below; the leaves stay turgid because xylem is intact.

## B2.2i: Factors and rate

| Factor | Rate of water uptake | Reason to write |
|---|---|---|
| Light intensity up | Up | Stomata open wider, more vapour escapes |
| Temperature up | Up | Faster evaporation and diffusion, steeper gradient |
| Air movement up | Up | Vapour swept away, gradient kept steep |
| Humidity up | Down | Gradient smaller |

Write the cause (stomata or gradient) in every explanation, and say that uptake follows loss.

## B2.2j: Potometer and calculations

**Method in steps**

1. Cut the shoot while submerged and join it to the apparatus underwater.
2. Make sure every joint is airtight, and wipe the leaves.
3. Let in a single bubble and note where it starts.
4. Time the bubble over a fixed interval, note the distance, then average repeats.
5. Alter one factor only; hold the rest steady.

| Quantity | Formula |
|---|---|
| Rate of bubble movement | distance moved ÷ time (mm per minute) |
| Cross-sectional area of capillary | π × radius² |
| Volume rate of uptake | rate of bubble movement × area (mm³ per minute) |
| Percentage change | (change ÷ starting value) × 100 |
| Stomatal density | mean number per field ÷ area of field (π × radius²) |

**Worked reminder.** A bubble moves 63 mm in 7.0 minutes in a capillary of radius 0.40 mm.

```
rate   = 63 / 7.0 = 9.0 mm per minute
area   = pi x 0.40^2 = 0.503 mm^2
volume = 9.0 x 0.503 = 4.5 mm^3 per minute
```

**Worked reminder (two conditions).** Rate rises from 4.0 to 7.0 mm per minute: increase = 3.0, so 3.0 ÷ 4.0 × 100 = 75% increase.

**Worked reminder (percentage mass loss).** A shoot in a tube of water with oil on top loses 2.7 g from a starting mass of 130.0 g.

```
percentage loss = 2.7 / 130.0 x 100 = 2.1% (2 s.f.)
```

**Worked reminder (stomatal density).** Counts per field of view: 6, 8, 7 and 7, with a field area of 0.20 mm².

```
mean    = (6 + 8 + 7 + 7) / 4 = 7
density = 7 / 0.20 = 35 stomata per mm^2
```

## Practical work you may be asked about

| Practical | What to remember |
|---|---|
| Root hair cells under a light microscope | Thin extension of the cell; stain if needed; draw with a sharp pencil and a scale |
| Position of xylem and phloem in root, stem and leaf sections | Xylem and phloem sit in groups called vascular bundles; the arrangement differs between organs |
| Stomatal impression | Paint clear nail varnish on the leaf, peel it off when dry, view under the microscope, count stomata in several fields |
| Potometer | Rate from bubble movement, then repeat and average |
| Sucking water through a long straw | Shows how powerful the pull of the transpiration stream is |

## How to build a full-mark explanation

1. Name the factor and the direction of the change.
2. Say what changes in the leaf: stomata open or close, or the gradient steepens or flattens.
3. Say what that does to evaporation and diffusion of water vapour.
4. Link to the result in the question, such as a faster bubble or a larger mass loss.

Example: "A fan raises the rate because moving air removes water vapour from beside the stomata. This keeps the concentration gradient steep, so vapour diffuses out faster and water is drawn up the xylem faster."

## Reading data and graphs

- Plot the independent variable on the horizontal axis, with a sensible scale and labelled axes with units.
- To describe a trend, quote two values with units from the table or graph.
- If a rate flattens, say it has levelled off and suggest another factor now limiting it, such as stomata being fully open.
- Check repeat readings for outliers before averaging, and say which result you left out.
- A change of a percentage is a ratio to the starting value, so doubling is +100%.

## Sources and sinks

A source is where sugar is made or released, such as a photosynthesising leaf. A sink is where sugar is used or stored, such as a growing root tip, a developing fruit or a storage organ. The direction of translocation depends on which is which, which is why phloem carries sugar both up and down. Use the words source and sink in an answer about translocation.

## Must-know distinctions

- Osmosis moves water; active transport moves ions. Only active transport needs energy.
- Transpiration is a loss of water vapour; the potometer measures uptake, which approximates it.
- Xylem is dead and one-way; phloem is living and two-way.
- Guard cells control the pore. Stomata are the pores.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the process by which water enters a root hair cell.
2. Why does a root hair cell contain many mitochondria?
3. A bubble moves 45 mm in 9.0 minutes. Calculate the rate in mm per minute.
4. The capillary radius is 0.40 mm. Use the rate in question 3 to find the volume of water absorbed each minute, to 2 decimal places.
5. A shoot's tube falls from 85.0 g to 83.3 g. Calculate the percentage mass lost.
6. Nine stomata are counted in a field of view of area 0.12 mm². Calculate the stomatal density.
7. A rate rises from 4.0 to 7.0 mm per minute. Calculate the percentage increase.
8. State two ways the xylem is adapted for its function.
9. What is the role of a companion cell?
10. Explain why transpiration slows in still, humid air.

### Answers

1. Osmosis.
2. They release energy by respiration for active transport of ions.
3. 45 ÷ 9.0 = **5.0 mm per minute**.
4. Area = π × 0.40² = 0.5027 mm²; 5.0 × 0.5027 = **2.51 mm³ per minute**.
5. Loss = 1.7 g; 1.7 ÷ 85.0 × 100 = **2.0%**.
6. 9 ÷ 0.12 = **75 per mm²**.
7. (7.0 − 4.0) ÷ 4.0 × 100 = **75%**.
8. Any two: dead hollow cells with no end walls; lignin in the walls to stop collapse and give support.
9. It has many mitochondria and supplies energy to load sugars into the sieve tube.
10. Vapour stays near the leaf, so the concentration gradient is small and diffusion out of the stomata is slow.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying "osmosis" for mineral ion uptake.
- Describing transpiration as water leaving the roots or the xylem, rather than vapour leaving the leaf by stomata.
- Writing that phloem carries "food" without saying dissolved sugars.
- Calling a potometer a transpiration meter without noting that it measures uptake.
- Leaving out "one variable changed, others controlled" in a method.
- Dividing by the final value in a percentage change.
- Giving the volume rate without multiplying by cross-sectional area, or mixing mm and cm.
- Explaining a factor with "faster" and no mention of gradient or stomata.
