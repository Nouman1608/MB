## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Market | An opportunity for buyers and sellers to interact in order to establish a price |
| Allocation of resources | How scarce resources are shared between different uses; in a market, price signals do much of the work |
| Product market | Where finished goods and services are bought and sold |
| Factor market | Where the factors of production (land, labour, capital, enterprise) are bought and sold |
| Primary sector | Extracts or grows natural resources |
| Secondary sector | Makes and builds, turning materials into products |
| Tertiary sector | Provides services |
| Good | A physical, tangible item |
| Service | An activity done for someone, not a physical item |
| Specialisation | Concentrating on a limited range of tasks or products |
| Division of labour | Breaking a production process into separate tasks, one per worker |
| Exchange | Trading what you make for what others make |

## 3.1.2.1 Markets: the essentials

- Buyers and sellers do not have to meet in one place. Online and phone deals count.
- **Price is a signal and a rationing device.** A rise in price tells sellers to supply more and buyers to buy less. A fall does the reverse.
- Resources move towards uses that buyers will pay for. Chain: demand rises, price rises, profits rise, firms and owners of factors move resources into that product, supply grows.
- Scarce means limited relative to wants, so someone must decide who gets what. In a market, the decision is made by willingness and ability to pay.

### Product market or factor market?

| | Product market | Factor market |
|---|---|---|
| Sold | Goods and services | Land, labour, capital, enterprise |
| Buyer | Household | Firm |
| Seller | Firm | Household |
| Price paid | Price of the item | Rent, wage, interest, profit |

Method to decide: ask what is being bought. A finished item for use points to a product market. The services of land, workers, machines or an owner point to a factor market. A nurse selling labour to a hospital is in a factor market. The same nurse buying shoes is in a product market.

## 3.1.2.2 Sectors: the essentials

| Sector | Test | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Is it taken from the ground, sea or land? | Wheat farm, quarry, fishing boat |
| Secondary | Is something made or built? | Brewery, shipyard, bakery ovens |
| Tertiary | Is it a service? | Bank, school, shop, taxi |

- A firm can span sectors. Judge the **activity**. A baker mixing and baking is secondary; the same baker selling over the counter is tertiary.
- **UK relative sizes:** the tertiary sector is by far the largest, the secondary sector is smaller, and the primary sector is the smallest. This is true for both output and jobs.

### Worked reminder: sector shares

Country E (fictional) has total output of 80bn, split 2% primary, 25% secondary and 73% tertiary.

```
Primary:   2% of 80   =  1.6bn
Secondary: 25% of 80  = 20.0bn
Tertiary:  73% of 80  = 58.4bn     Total = 80.0bn  (shares add to 100%)
```

**Percentage points versus per cent.** If a sector's share of jobs falls from 10% to 6%, the change is **4 percentage points**. If total jobs have not changed, the number of jobs has fallen by (6 - 10) / 10 = 40%. If total jobs have grown, the fall in number is smaller than 40%. State which one you mean.

### Good or service?

| Good | Service |
|---|---|
| Tangible; can be stored | Intangible; provided and used at the time |
| Buyer owns it | Buyer receives a benefit |
| Bicycle, shirt, loaf | Haircut, bus ride, tuition |

Mixed purchase (a meal out): decide what the buyer mainly pays for.

## 3.1.2.3 Specialisation and division of labour

### Why people and producers specialise

- Individuals develop skills through training and practice, and earn more doing what they do best.
- Producers use the resources, skills and equipment they have to make the products they make well and cheaply.
- Specialisation raises output from the same inputs. **Exchange** then lets everyone enjoy a wider range than they could make.

### Worked reminder: the division of labour in a firm

A small bakery has 3 workers on a 6-hour shift, paid 216 in total. Working alone, each makes 10 buns an hour. After splitting the work (mixing, shaping, baking and packing), each worker averages 16 buns an hour.

```
Before: 3 x 6 x 10 = 180 buns   Labour cost per bun = 216 / 180 = 1.20
After:  3 x 6 x 16 = 288 buns   Labour cost per bun = 216 / 288 = 0.75
Output change = (288 - 180) / 180 = +60%
Labour cost per bun change = (0.75 - 1.20) / 1.20 = -37.5%
```

### Worked reminder: quicker training

A workshop trains 5 new staff at a daily wage of 80. Learning every task takes 10 days. Learning one task takes 3 days.

```
All tasks:   5 x 10 x 80 = 4,000
One task:    5 x  3 x 80 = 1,200     Saving = 4,000 - 1,200 = 2,800
```

This is a benefit to the firm. The matching cost is that each trained worker can do only one task, so a single absence leaves that task uncovered.

### Reading a sector table: method

1. Check whether the figures are shares (percentages) or amounts.
2. Find the total, and check that the shares add to 100%.
3. Convert a share to an amount: share x total. Country D (fictional) employs 2.0 million and 3% work in the primary sector, so 0.03 x 2,000,000 = 60,000 jobs.
4. Compare years by amounts if the total has changed.
5. Say what the change means: a shrinking primary share means fewer people are needed to extract or grow materials, or that other sectors have grown faster.

### Costs and benefits

| | Benefits | Costs |
|---|---|---|
| Worker | Quick to learn; skill and speed rise; possible higher pay | Repetitive and dull; narrow skills; harder to switch jobs; may feel little pride |
| Firm | Higher output per worker; lower unit cost; steadier quality; fast training; machines can do simple tasks | Boredom raises absence and leaving; one absence can stop the line; inflexible; errors pass on |
| Wider (specialisation and exchange) | Greater total output and choice | Dependence on others; area or country exposed if demand for its product falls |

### Evaluation method in steps

1. State the benefit or cost.
2. Explain the chain: for example, repetition makes workers faster, so output per hour rises, so unit cost falls.
3. Say **who** gains or loses: the worker or the firm.
4. Add a condition: it holds *if* demand is strong, *if* staff stay motivated, *if* tasks are not easily automated.
5. Judge which side is stronger in the case you were given.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Specialisation** is the general idea; **division of labour** is the splitting of a production process into tasks.
- **Product market** (goods) and **factor market** (inputs); households switch roles between them.
- **Good** (physical) and **service** (activity); **tertiary** sector (services) includes retail.
- **Percentage points** (change in a share) and **per cent** (change in a quantity).
- **Benefits to the worker** and **benefits to the firm** are separate answers.

## Quick self-test

1. Define a market.
2. Is a farm hiring a picker part of a product market or a factor market?
3. Classify each by sector: a tin mine, a factory making tin cans, a supermarket.
4. In Country E above, what is the secondary sector's output?
5. A sector's share of jobs falls from 10% to 6%. State the change in percentage points.
6. In the bakery example, what is the percentage rise in output?
7. In the bakery example, what is the new labour cost per bun?
8. Is a haircut a good or a service? Give one reason.
9. Name a gain and a drawback of the division of labour for a worker.
10. Explain why specialisation leads to exchange.
11. State the order of the three sectors by size in the UK.

### Answers

1. An opportunity for buyers and sellers to interact in order to establish a price.
2. Factor market (labour is a factor of production).
3. Primary, secondary, tertiary.
4. 20.0bn (25% of 80).
5. Down by 4 percentage points.
6. +60%.
7. 0.75.
8. A service: it is an activity done for the customer, not a physical item.
9. Gain: skill and speed grow through repetition. Drawback: the work is repetitive and skills stay narrow.
10. Each person or firm makes only part of what they need, so they must trade with others for the rest.
11. Tertiary largest, then secondary, then primary smallest.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a factor market as a market for finished goods.
- Placing retail in the secondary sector.
- Treating "sector share fell" as "number of jobs fell".
- Mixing up percentage points and per cent in a data question.
- Giving benefits of the division of labour with no link to output or cost.
- Answering about the firm when the question asks about the worker, or the reverse.
- Making no use of the figures in the question.
- Leaving out a conclusion on evaluation questions.
