These questions cover section 14.2 of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026: global patterns of resources, production and markets; FDI and the NIDL; TNC growth and spatial structure, with your own TNC case study; NICs; and changes in the location of economic activity. Countries, firms and data in Questions 1 to 9 are fictional. Revise first with the [14.2 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-globalisation-of-economic-activity/) and the [14.2 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-globalisation-of-economic-activity-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between greenfield and brownfield foreign direct investment. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why a TNC might locate production inside a large market rather than export to it. **[3]**

**3.** The table shows inward FDI to the fictional country of Westmarra, in billions of US dollars.

| Year | Manufacturing | Services | Mining | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2000 | 2.0 | 0.5 | 1.5 | 4.0 |
| 2010 | 5.5 | 2.5 | 2.0 | 10.0 |
| 2020 | 6.0 | 7.5 | 1.5 | 15.0 |

**(a)** Calculate the share of total inward FDI that went to services in 2020. **[1]**

**(b)** Describe the changes in inward FDI to Westmarra between 2000 and 2020. **[3]**

**(c)** Suggest two reasons for the growth of FDI in services. **[4]**

**4.** The fictional clothing firm Halden has its headquarters and design studio in a high-income country, a regional office in a world city in Asia, and contracts 140 independent factories in six lower-income countries.

**(a)** Explain why most of Halden's production does not count as FDI. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why Halden keeps design in its home country. **[3]**

**5.** Outline two ways in which government policy can help a country to emerge as a newly industrialised country. **[4]**

**6.** A fictional insurance company moves its claims processing from its home country to a firm it does not own, located in another country.

**(a)** Name this type of relocation. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain two causes of this kind of relocation. **[4]**

**7.** Explain two impacts on a source country of the offshoring of manufacturing. **[4]**

**8.** Suggest why some firms have begun to reshore or nearshore production. **[4]**

**9.** Explain the difference between market-seeking and efficiency-seeking FDI. **[2]**

## Section B

**10.** With reference to one TNC you have studied, describe and explain its global spatial organisation. **[10]**

**11.** "The growth of newly industrialised countries owes more to TNCs than to their own governments." With reference to examples, how far do you agree? **[20]**

**12.** Evaluate the impacts of the offshoring of services on the countries that receive the work. **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** Greenfield FDI builds new facilities on a new site abroad [1]; brownfield FDI buys or merges with an existing foreign firm or facility [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Producing inside the market avoids tariffs and import quotas [1]. It cuts transport costs and delivery times to customers [1], and reduces exposure to exchange-rate changes or allows products to be adapted to local tastes [1]. **[3]**

**3. (a)** 7.5 / 15.0 = 50% [1].

**(b)** Total inward FDI rose from 4.0 to 15.0 billion US dollars [1]. Services grew most, from 0.5 to 7.5 billion, overtaking manufacturing by 2020 [1]. Manufacturing grew to 2010 then levelled off, and mining stayed between 1.5 and 2.0 billion [1].

**(c)** Improved ICT and telecommunications allow services such as call centres or software to be delivered from Westmarra [1], so firms can use lower-cost skilled workers there [1]. A growing middle class in Westmarra creates demand for banking, retail and telecoms [1], attracting market-seeking FDI by foreign service firms [1]. **[8]**

**4. (a)** FDI requires the investing firm to own or control the productive asset [1]; Halden's factories are independent contractors, so it buys their output under contract without owning them [1].

**(b)** Design is knowledge-intensive work needing skilled, highly paid staff [1] who are concentrated in the home country [1]; keeping it there also protects the brand and intellectual property, which is where most value is added [1]. **[5]**

**5.** Export processing zones with tax and customs incentives [1] attract foreign firms making goods for export [1]. Investment in education and training [1] creates a workforce able to move into higher-value manufacturing [1]. Also credit state planning of strategic industries, cheap finance or infrastructure building, each with its effect. **[4]**

**6. (a)** Offshore outsourcing [1].

**(b)** Lower wages for clerical staff in the host country [1] reduce the firm's costs [1]. Cheap, reliable ICT and telecommunications [1] mean claims can be processed remotely with no loss of service [1]. Also credit availability of skilled, English-speaking staff or time-zone advantages. **[5]**

**7.** Job losses in manufacturing towns and regions [1], which can lead to unemployment, out-migration and decline of local services [1]. Consumers benefit from cheaper goods [1], and firms gain higher profits that may be reinvested in design or research at home [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Wages in former low-cost countries have risen [1], so the cost advantage of distant production has narrowed [1]. Supply-chain disruption and trade tensions raise the risk of long supply chains [1], so firms move production closer to customers to make supply more reliable [1]. Also credit automation reducing the importance of labour costs. **[4]**

**9.** Market-seeking FDI aims to sell to customers in the host country [1]; efficiency-seeking FDI aims to lower costs, for example of labour, land or tax, often for export [1]. **[2]**

**10.** Levels-based. Credit: named TNC with origin and home country [1]; location of headquarters and its functions [1]; location of research and design, with a reason [1]; regional headquarters and why they are in world cities [1]; production locations, owned or contracted [1], with reasons such as labour cost or market access [1]; markets and distribution centres [1]; changes over time, such as relocation between countries [1]; explanation using the NIDL or another model [1]; use of specific places, dates or figures throughout [1]. **[10]**

**11.** The real exam marks this essay with levels of response. Indicative creditworthy content:

*Introduction:* define NIC [1]; set out TNC and state roles [1].

*TNCs:* inward FDI brought capital, jobs and technology [1], for example electronics assembly in Malaysia's Penang free trade zone [1]; TNCs gave access to global markets and supply chains [1]; contracting linked local firms to global brands [1].

*Governments:* export-oriented strategies and planning of strategic industries [1], for example South Korea's support for steel, shipbuilding and electronics [1]; investment in education [1]; export processing zones and infrastructure created the conditions for TNCs [1]; state finance built domestic conglomerates [1].

*Interaction:* TNCs came because governments created incentives and stability [1]; the balance differs between first- and second-generation NICs [1].

*Other factors:* location on shipping routes, global context such as open markets and falling transport costs [1].

*Judgement:* a supported view on "how far" [1]; differentiated by country or generation [1]; based on evidence [1]; recognition that the two worked together [1]; a conclusion that answers the question [1]; reference to change over time, such as NICs moving into their own high-value firms [1]. **[20]**
*Examiner insight:* Comparing South Korea with a second-generation NIC is the clearest way to show that the answer varies by country.

**12.** Levels-based. Indicative content:

*Introduction:* define offshoring of services with examples such as call centres, software and business processing [1]; name receiving countries or cities [1].

*Economic benefits:* skilled jobs and higher incomes [1]; export earnings [1]; multiplier effects on local services and construction [1]; growth of domestic firms in the sector [1].

*Social benefits:* a growing middle class [1]; training and skills [1].

*Costs:* concentration in a few cities, widening regional disparities [1]; pressure on housing, transport and water in those cities [1]; shift work and stress in some jobs [1]; vulnerability to automation or relocation elsewhere [1]; limited benefit to the rural poor [1].

*Evaluation:* impacts differ by scale (national, city, individual) [1] and by group [1]; change over time as firms move up the value chain [1]; use of a named example with evidence [1]; overall judgement on whether impacts are net positive [1]; for whom [1]; a conclusion that answers the question [1]. **[20]**
*Examiner insight:* Linking offshoring to regional inequality connects 14.2 with 14.3 and strengthens evaluation.
