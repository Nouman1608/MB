This guide goes deeper on sub-topic **4.9 Barriers to economic growth and/or economic development** in Unit 4 of the IB Diploma Programme *Economics guide* (first assessment 2022). It is SL and HL content. The [guide to barriers and strategies](/resources/ib-dp-economics-barriers-and-strategies-for-growth-and-development/) explains each barrier the guide lists; this page focuses on the skill examiners reward most in 4.9: showing how barriers **interact** and judging **which matter most** in a given country. Consolidate with the [4.9 revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-economics-4-9-revision-notes/) and practise with the [4.9 practice questions](/resources/ib-dp-economics-4-9-practice/). All countries, households and figures are invented. The case-study country is Quorvia, whose currency is the quor.

## The barriers in 4.9

**Poverty traps and poverty cycles** frame the topic: low income leads to low saving and low tax revenue, then to low investment in physical and human capital, then to low productivity and back to low income.

| Economic barriers | Political and social barriers |
|---|---|
| Rising economic inequality | Weak institutional framework: legal system, ineffective taxation structures, banking system, property rights |
| Lack of access to infrastructure and appropriate technology | Gender inequality |
| Low levels of human capital (health and education) | Lack of good governance and corruption |
| Dependence on primary sector production | Unequal political power and status |
| Lack of access to international markets | |
| Informal economy | |
| Capital flight | |
| Indebtedness | |
| Geography, including landlocked countries | |

## Growth barriers and development barriers

The sub-topic title says "growth and/or development" because some barriers mainly hold back **real GDP growth**, while others mainly hold back **development**: improvements in health, education, freedom and living standards for the many.

- **Mainly growth:** capital flight and indebtedness reduce the funds available for investment; lack of access to international markets limits sales and economies of scale.
- **Mainly development:** rising inequality and unequal political power can let GDP rise while most people see little gain.
- **Both:** low human capital, gender inequality and weak institutions reduce output and limit people's opportunities.

Saying which kind of barrier you are discussing makes an answer far more precise.

## A household poverty trap in numbers

A farming family in Quorvia earns 1,200 quors a year and must spend 1,150 on food, rent and school costs, leaving 50 to save.

- An irrigation pump costing 400 quors would raise the family's income by 250 a year.
- Saving 50 a year, the family needs **8 years** to afford it, and one poor harvest wipes out the savings.
- A bank loan at 20% interest would cost 400 × 0.20 = 80 a year, leaving a gain of 250 - 80 = **170 a year**, enough to repay the loan within three years.
- The bank refuses: the family's land has **no registered title**, so it cannot be offered as collateral.

The trap is not caused by one barrier but by several together: low income (little saving), a weak **banking system** (no credit for small borrowers) and missing **property rights** (no collateral). Remove the property rights barrier and the loan becomes possible. This is the reasoning examiners want: barriers reinforce one another.

## A country profile: Quorvia

| Indicator | Quorvia |
|---|---|
| Recorded GDP | 14.0 billion quors |
| Estimated informal output | 6.3 billion quors (45% of recorded GDP) |
| Average tax rate on recorded activity | 12% |
| Exports | 6.0 billion quors, of which coffee 65% |
| Annual debt service (interest and repayments) | 1.8 billion quors |
| Location | Landlocked; exports travel 900 km to a neighbour's port |
| Labour force participation, working-age women / men | 30% / 75% |
| Working-age population | 10 million, half of them women |

### Working through the barriers

**Dependence on primary production and indebtedness, together.**

```
Coffee exports = 0.65 x 6.0 = 3.9 billion
Debt service ratio now = 1.8 / 6.0 x 100 = 30%
Coffee price falls 40%: coffee exports = 3.9 x 0.6 = 2.34 billion
New total exports = 6.0 - 3.9 + 2.34 = 4.44 billion (a fall of 26%)
New debt service ratio = 1.8 / 4.44 x 100 = about 40.5%
```

The price shock does more than cut export earnings: it pushes the share of export earnings needed for debt service from 30% to about 40.5%, leaving less foreign currency for imports of machinery and medicines.

**Informal economy and tax structures.** If the informal output were taxed at the 12% average rate, revenue would rise by 0.12 × 6.3 = **0.756 billion quors**, more than 40% of a year's debt service. A narrow tax base is therefore linked to indebtedness and to under-funded schools and clinics.

**Gender inequality.** Women's participation is 30% of 5 million = 1.5 million workers; men's is 75% of 5 million = 3.75 million. If women's participation rose to 45%, the labour force would grow by 0.75 million, from 5.25 to 6.0 million, a rise of about **14%**, before counting gains from girls' education.

**Landlocked geography.** Every export must cross a neighbour's territory, raising transport costs and making Quorvia's coffee less competitive and its imported inputs dearer. It also strengthens the link to lack of access to international markets.

## Judging significance

The guide asks you to evaluate the **relative significance** of barriers. Use explicit criteria:

| Criterion | Question to ask | Quorvia example |
|---|---|---|
| Scale | How much output or how many people does it affect? | Gender inequality affects half the working-age population |
| Root or symptom | Does it cause other barriers? | Weak property rights and tax structures feed the poverty trap, informality and debt |
| Reversibility | Can policy change it, or is it fixed? | Geography is fixed; institutions can be reformed, though slowly |
| Vulnerability | Does it expose the economy to shocks? | Coffee dependence combined with debt makes a price fall far more damaging |
| Time horizon | Does it matter now or over decades? | Debt service is pressing now; human capital matters over a generation |

**A model judgement.** "In Quorvia, landlocked geography is a real cost but cannot be changed, so it is less useful as a policy target. The most significant barrier is the weak institutional framework, because missing property rights and a narrow tax base help explain the household poverty trap, the large informal economy and the reliance on borrowing. However, in the short term the combination of coffee dependence and indebtedness is the most urgent risk, since a single price shock can push debt service to over 40% of export earnings."

Notice how the judgement distinguishes the **root** barrier from the most **urgent** one and uses the data.

## Common mistakes

- Listing barriers without applying them to the country in the question.
- Treating every barrier as equally important.
- Ignoring the difference between barriers to growth and barriers to development.
- Calling a fixed feature such as geography the "most important" barrier without considering what can be changed.
- Using percentages without stating what they are a percentage of.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** a barrier as a chain: barrier, mechanism, effect on growth or development.
- **Draw** a poverty cycle diagram with each link labelled; show where one barrier (such as missing credit) breaks into the cycle.
- **Discuss** or **evaluate** the significance of barriers by applying two or three criteria from the table and reaching a justified ranking.
- In Paper 2, use the data: calculate a ratio or percentage and explain what it means for the country.
- In Paper 1 part (b), support your argument with real-world examples you have studied, and say which country context they come from.
