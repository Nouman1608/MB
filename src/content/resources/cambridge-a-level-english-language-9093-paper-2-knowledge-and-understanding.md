Paper 2 Writing in the Cambridge International AS & A Level English Language (9093) syllabus is a test of what you can **make**, not only what you can name. Even so, the syllabus is clear about the knowledge behind good writing: "The knowledge and understanding that candidates are required to demonstrate in Paper 2 is the same as is covered in Paper 1." This guide takes that knowledge and turns it into choices a writer makes. For the paper as a whole, read the [Paper 2 Writing study guide](/resources/a-level-english-language-paper-2-writing/) first. For the same knowledge from the reader's side, see the [Paper 1 knowledge and understanding guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-english-language-9093-knowledge-and-understanding/).

## Where the knowledge is tested

Paper 2 lasts 2 hours and is worth 50 marks. It has two sections, each worth 25 marks.

| Part | Task | Marks | Assessment objective |
|---|---|---|---|
| Section A, Question 1(a) | a short text of no more than 400 words, for a given form, purpose and audience | 15 | AO2 |
| Section A, Question 1(b) | a reflective commentary on how your text fulfils the brief | 10 | AO3 |
| Section B | one extended piece, of 600 to 900 words, from three categories: imaginative/descriptive, discursive/argumentative, review/critical | 25 | AO2 |

In the whole paper, AO2 (write effectively, creatively, accurately and appropriately, for a range of audiences and purposes) is about 80% of the marks and AO3 (analyse the ways in which choices of form, structure and language produce meaning and style) about 20%. Knowledge and understanding supports both: it tells you what to put in the text, and it gives you the vocabulary to explain your choices in the commentary.

The syllabus adds that its examples "are suggested rather than prescribed ways in which candidates can demonstrate knowledge and understanding. These examples are not exhaustive."

## The four strands

The syllabus lists four things you should know.

1. the conventions of a wide range of written textual forms;
2. the linguistic elements and literary features of texts;
3. the significance of audience in both the design and reception of texts;
4. the ways in which genre, purpose and context contribute to the meaning of texts.

### Strand 1: conventions of textual forms

A **convention** is the expected set of features of a kind of text. The syllabus examples are: advertisements, brochures, leaflets, editorials, news stories, articles, reviews, blogs, investigative journalism, letters, podcasts, (auto)biographies, travel writing, diaries, essays, scripted speech, narrative writing and descriptive writing.

| Form | Typical conventions |
|---|---|
| Advertisement | eye-catching headline, short punchy copy, direct address, call to action |
| Leaflet or brochure | headings and subheadings, short paragraphs, imperatives, contact details |
| Editorial | a clear opinion, a topical hook, evidence, rhetorical questions, a firm close |
| News story | headline, a first paragraph answering who, what, where, when, why; quoted sources; reverse order of importance |
| Review | introduction, summary, evaluation, verdict; evaluative vocabulary |
| Blog | personal voice, informal register, direct address, a conversational structure |
| Letter | address, salutation, purposeful paragraphs, sign-off appropriate to the reader |
| Diary | first person, past or present tense, personal reflection, private tone |
| Scripted speech | direct address, rhythm, repetition, rhetorical devices, a memorable ending |
| Narrative | characters, setting, plot structure, a controlled point of view |
| Descriptive writing | sensory detail, imagery, a controlled mood, careful word choice |

Learn conventions by reading real examples and noticing what repeats. When you write, use enough of the conventions for the reader to recognise the form, then adapt them to your purpose.

### Strand 2: linguistic elements and literary features

The syllabus examples are: parts of speech and word classes, vocabulary, figurative language, phonology, morphology, rhetorical devices, voice, aspect, tense, modality, narrative perspective, word ordering and sentence structure, paragraph- and text-level structure, formality or informality of tone, and pragmatics.

In writing, each of these is a **choice**. This table shows how a writer can use them.

| Element | A writer's choice | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Word class | verbs for action, adjectives for description, nouns for abstract ideas | "The shutters slammed" gives energy through a verb |
| Vocabulary | formal or informal; concrete or abstract | "assist" or "help" |
| Figurative language | simile, metaphor, personification, symbol | "the city exhaled" |
| Phonology | alliteration, assonance, rhythm | "soft, sleepy streets" |
| Morphology | prefixes, suffixes, invented words | "unfollow", "bio-hacking" |
| Rhetorical devices | rule of three, rhetorical question, repetition | "We can, we must, we will." |
| Voice | active or passive | "The lake was poisoned" hides the agent |
| Aspect and tense | present for immediacy, past for narrative | "He opens the door" |
| Modality | certainty or possibility | "must" or "might" |
| Narrative perspective | first, second or third person | "you" draws the reader in |
| Sentence structure | short for impact, long for flow, minor sentences | "Silence." |
| Text structure | order of paragraphs and ideas | chronological, spiral, counterargument |
| Tone | formal or informal | contractions, slang or none |
| Pragmatics | what is implied, politeness, how the reader is positioned | a polite request or a command |

### Strand 3: audience in design and reception

Audience has two sides. **Design**: the writer shapes the text for a particular reader. **Reception**: the reader's expectations, knowledge and context shape how the text is understood.

For each brief ask:

- **Who** is the reader (age, knowledge, interest, relationship to the writer)?
- **What** do they already know or believe?
- **How** will they read it (quickly in a leaflet, slowly in an essay)?
- **What** will persuade, inform or entertain them?

A text for children uses short sentences and concrete images; a text for specialists uses technical vocabulary. Using the wrong register for the audience is one of the commonest reasons for lost marks.

### Strand 4: genre, purpose and context

**Genre** is the type of text. **Purpose** is why it exists: to inform, persuade, entertain, instruct, advise, reflect. **Context** includes time, place, culture and the situation of writing and reading. These three determine meaning. The same facts about a new park are presented differently in a news story (neutral, factual), a leaflet (promotional) and a letter of complaint (critical, formal).

## Worked example: knowledge turned into a text

**Brief.** *Write a leaflet-style text for teenagers encouraging them to volunteer one hour a week at a local animal shelter. (No more than 400 words.)*

**Planning with the four strands.**

| Strand | Decision |
|---|---|
| Conventions | leaflet: bold headline, short paragraphs, imperatives, an invitation to act |
| Linguistic elements | second person "you", imperatives, short sentences, modal "might" to meet an objection, an emotional example |
| Audience | teenagers: informal but respectful register, brief text, a concrete story |
| Genre, purpose, context | persuade, with an invitation; the context is leisure time after school |

**Text (about 170 words).**

> **Give us one hour. Get a friend for life.**
>
> Think about your week. School. Homework. Scrolling. Now think about Biscuit, a three-legged terrier who has spent forty days waiting by his kennel door for someone to say hello.
>
> That someone could be you.
>
> At Millbrook Animal Shelter, volunteers aged 14 and over walk dogs, groom cats and help new arrivals to feel safe. No experience is needed. You will be trained on your first visit, and our team is always on hand.
>
> You might think an hour is too small to matter. It is not. One walk can calm an anxious dog. One kind voice can help a frightened kitten to trust. And you will go home lighter than you arrived: volunteers tell us the hour is the best of their week.
>
> Come on Saturday. Bring trainers. Leave the worry at the gate.
>
> *Millbrook Animal Shelter: because every animal deserves a someone.*

**Reflective commentary (about 120 words, Question 1(b)).**

> I chose the form of a leaflet because teenagers read leaflets quickly, so I used a bold headline and short paragraphs. The headline pairs an imperative with a paradox, "an hour" and "for life", to catch attention. I addressed the reader as "you" and used imperatives such as "Come" and "Bring" to make a direct, active call to action. The story of Biscuit appeals to emotion through one concrete example, which suits an audience that responds to personal stories. Short sentences such as "That someone could be you" create impact, while the modal "might" acknowledges the reader's likely objection before answering it. The informal register and the final slogan make the shelter sound friendly and welcoming.

Notice that the commentary names **choices**, links each to the **audience**, and explains the **effect**. It focuses on form, structure and language, as the syllabus requires.

## Common mistakes

- **Naming features without a purpose.** A commentary that lists "a rhetorical question, an imperative, a simile" with no effect shows knowledge but not understanding.
- **Using conventions you cannot control**, such as unfamiliar structures that make the text confusing.
- **Ignoring the audience.** The text is accurate, but too formal or too technical for the reader in the brief.
- **Mixing forms**, such as writing a letter without a salutation or a news story with a personal diary voice.
- **Exceeding the word limit** in Question 1(a). The limit is no more than 400 words.
- **Writing a commentary that retells the text** instead of explaining choices.
- **Treating the examples as a checklist.** The syllabus says they are "suggested rather than prescribed".

## Exam technique

- Read the brief and underline form, purpose and audience before writing.
- Spend a few minutes planning: write your key choices as a list.
- Keep Question 1(a) within the word limit, and leave time for the 10-mark commentary.
- In the commentary, write about three or four choices in depth, not a long list.
- In Section B, choose the category in which you can control the form, and plan the shape of the piece before you begin.
