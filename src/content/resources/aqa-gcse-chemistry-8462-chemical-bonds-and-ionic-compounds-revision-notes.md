## 4.2.1.1 The three strong bonds

| Bond | Particles | Electrons are... | Occurs in |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ionic | Oppositely charged ions | **transferred** (metal to non-metal) | Compounds of metals with non-metals |
| Covalent | Atoms | **shared in pairs** | Most non-metallic elements; compounds of non-metals |
| Metallic | Atoms | **delocalised** and shared through the structure | Metallic elements and alloys |

**What attracts what (the electrostatic explanation):**

- Ionic: positive ions ↔ negative ions.
- Covalent: the shared pair of electrons ↔ the nuclei of both atoms.
- Metallic: the delocalised electrons ↔ the positive metal centres.

All three are strong bonds, and all three are electrostatic attractions between opposite charges.

**Model answer shape** for "Explain the bonding in magnesium" (3 marks):

```
Magnesium is a metallic element, so the bonding is metallic.          [type + reason]
The outer-shell electrons are delocalised and shared through
the whole structure.                                                   [electrons]
The bond is the strong electrostatic attraction between the
delocalised electrons and the positive metal centres.                  [what attracts what]
```

Use the same three moves for ionic and covalent answers: type with a reason, what the electrons do, what
attracts what.

### Method: identify a bond type in steps

```
1. Write down the elements present.
2. Metal + non-metal (compound)        -> ionic
   Non-metal(s) only                   -> covalent
   Metal(s) only, element or alloy     -> metallic
   Single noble gas                    -> no strong bonds
3. Say how electrons are transferred or shared.
4. Say what is attracted to what.
```

## 4.2.1.3 Ionic compounds

**Definition to learn word for word:** an ionic compound is a **giant structure of ions**, held together by
**strong electrostatic forces of attraction** between **oppositely charged ions**. The forces act **in all
directions** in the lattice. This is ionic bonding.

**Sodium chloride (the only ionic structure you need to know):**

- Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions alternate in a regular cubic lattice.
- Each ion has six oppositely charged ions as nearest neighbours.
- Cl⁻ ions are larger than Na⁺ ions.
- Equal numbers of each ion, so the formula is NaCl (ratio 1 : 1).
- No separate NaCl molecules exist.

### Deducing "ionic" from a diagram

| Clue in the diagram | Points to |
|---|---|
| Two types of particle, one + and one − | Ionic |
| Square brackets with charges in a dot and cross diagram | Ionic |
| Regular alternating pattern, drawn as part of a bigger structure | Giant lattice |
| Positive particles surrounded by "e⁻" with no negative ions | Metallic |
| Small separate groups of atoms joined by lines | Covalent (small molecules) |

### Limitations of each representation

| Diagram | Main limitations for a giant ionic structure |
|---|---|
| Dot and cross | One pair of ions only; no 3D arrangement; no relative sizes; forces in all directions not shown |
| Ball and stick | Sticks look like bonds between particular pairs; ions shown too far apart; sizes may be wrong |
| 2D | One layer only; ions above and below are missing |
| 3D space-filling | Inner ions hidden; arrangement hard to see; forces not shown |
| All of them | Only a tiny fragment of a lattice that contains an enormous number of ions |

A good limitation names a **specific** feature of that diagram and says what is wrong or missing.

### Method: empirical formula from a model

```
1. Use the key to identify each ion.
2. Count each type of ion in the repeating pattern.
3. Divide by the highest common factor -> simplest whole-number ratio.
4. Write the formula: metal first, subscripts for numbers above 1.
5. Check: total positive charge = total negative charge.
```

Small worked reminders:

```
8 sodium ions, 4 sulfide ions       -> 8 : 4 = 2 : 1  -> Na2S   (2(+1) + (-2) = 0)
4 magnesium ions, 8 fluoride ions   -> 4 : 8 = 1 : 2  -> MgF2   ((+2) + 2(-1) = 0)
```

Written with subscripts these are Na₂S and MgF₂.

**Edge warning:** a small 3D block can give an uneven count. A 3 × 3 × 3 cube of a 1 : 1 lattice holds 14 of
one ion and 13 of the other. A real crystal is so large that the edges do not matter, so the ratio is 1 : 1.
Trust the repeating pattern and the charges, not a raw count from a tiny model.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Ionic vs metallic:** both involve positive particles, but only ionic compounds contain **negative ions**.
  Metallic structures have delocalised electrons instead.
- **Giant lattice vs molecule:** an ionic formula gives a **ratio** of ions, not a molecule.
- **Covalent element vs covalent compound:** Cl₂ is a covalent element; H₂O is a covalent compound. Both
  are non-metals only.
- **Alloy vs compound:** an alloy of two metals is metallic, not ionic.
- **Empirical formula vs ion count:** Li₂O, not Li₁₂O₆.

## Key terms

**Ion** -- a charged particle formed when an atom loses or gains electrons. **Lattice** -- a regular
repeating 3D arrangement of particles. **Giant structure** -- a lattice that continues in all directions with
no separate molecules. **Delocalised electrons** -- outer electrons free to move through a whole metallic
structure. **Empirical formula** -- the simplest whole-number ratio of the particles in a compound.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the particles in a metallic bond.
2. What type of bonding is in solid potassium chloride?
3. What type of bonding is in liquid bromine?
4. Solder is an alloy of tin and lead. What type of bonding does it contain?
5. In a covalent bond, what is the shared pair of electrons attracted to?
6. Complete: "In an ionic lattice the electrostatic forces act in ______."
7. How many Cl⁻ ions are nearest neighbours to one Na⁺ ion in sodium chloride?
8. A diagram shows a regular grid of "+" and "−" particles in equal numbers. What does this tell you about the
   compound, and what is its ratio of ions?
9. A model of an ionic compound has 10 potassium ions and 5 oxide ions. Give the empirical formula.
10. Give one limitation of a ball and stick model of sodium chloride.
11. Give one limitation of a 2D diagram of sodium chloride.
12. A diagram shows identical positive particles with electrons between them. Is it ionic? Explain.

### Answers

1. Metal atoms sharing delocalised electrons (positive metal centres attracted to the delocalised electrons).
2. Ionic (potassium is a metal, chlorine a non-metal).
3. Covalent (bromine is a non-metallic element; atoms share a pair of electrons).
4. Metallic.
5. The nuclei of both atoms.
6. All directions.
7. Six.
8. It is ionic: two oppositely charged ions in a regular lattice. Ratio 1 : 1.
9. 10 : 5 = 2 : 1, so **K₂O** (check: 2(+1) + (−2) = 0).
10. Any one: sticks suggest bonds between particular pairs of ions, when forces act in all directions; ions are
    shown too far apart; sizes may not be to scale; only a small part of the lattice is shown.
11. Any one: shows only one layer, so ions above and below are missing; no 3D arrangement; only a small part of
    the lattice.
12. No. There are no negative ions; positive particles among delocalised electrons show **metallic** bonding.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling an alloy such as brass or solder "ionic" because it contains two elements.
- Saying covalent bonding happens in "all" non-metallic elements. The noble gases do not easily form molecules.
- Describing a covalent bond as "sharing electrons" with no mention of a **pair** or of attraction to the
  nuclei.
- Writing "ionic bonds are between a sodium ion and a chloride ion", which suggests pairs, instead of "forces
  act in all directions in the lattice".
- Using "molecule" anywhere in an answer about sodium chloride.
- Giving "it's not to scale" as a limitation without naming which feature is not to scale.
- Leaving an empirical formula unsimplified, or writing the non-metal first.
- Reading positive centres with electrons as an ionic diagram.
- Counting the ions in a tiny 3D block and writing an uneven formula without checking the charges.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the three types of strong chemical bond?**
The three strong bonds are ionic (electrons transferred between oppositely charged ions), covalent (electrons shared in pairs between atoms) and metallic (delocalised electrons shared through the structure). This is AQA GCSE Chemistry 8462 section 4.2.1.1.

**What type of bonding is in an alloy?**
An alloy such as brass or solder has metallic bonding, even though it contains more than one element. Calling an alloy ionic is a common error.

**Do all non-metallic elements form covalent bonds?**
Most non-metallic elements are covalent, such as Cl₂ and Br₂, but the noble gases do not easily form molecules. Saying "all" non-metals bond covalently is not accurate.

**How do you write the empirical formula of an ionic compound from a diagram?**
Count the ions, find the simplest whole-number ratio and write the metal first, for example Li₂O rather than Li₁₂O₆. Check that the charges balance.

**Which ionic structure do you need to know for AQA GCSE Chemistry?**
You need to know the structure of sodium chloride, where Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions alternate in a regular cubic lattice. Other ionic structures will be given in a diagram if they are used.
