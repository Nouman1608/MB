This guide covers subtopic **5.2 Effects of temperature and pH** of Cambridge O Level Biology 5090, 2026–2028 series. The syllabus asks you to "understand that the progress of enzyme-catalysed reactions can be followed by measuring the concentrations of reactants and products", to "investigate and describe the effects of temperature and pH on enzyme activity", and to "explain the effect of changes in temperature and pH on enzyme activity in terms of kinetic energy, shape and fit, denaturation and the frequency of effective collisions".

## Following the progress of a reaction

You cannot see an enzyme working, so you measure how fast a **reactant (substrate) disappears** or how fast a **product appears**.

| Method | What is measured | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Disappearance of substrate | Time until starch is no longer present | Amylase and starch: take drops at intervals and test with iodine solution on a spotting tile; record the time when iodine stays orange-brown instead of turning blue-black |
| Appearance of product | Amount of reducing sugar formed | Amylase and starch: test samples with Benedict's solution; a colour change from blue towards green, yellow, orange and brick-red shows more reducing sugar |
| Appearance of a gas product | Volume of gas collected in a fixed time | An enzyme that releases a gas, with the gas collected in an upturned measuring cylinder or gas syringe |

All three methods follow the same idea: a faster reaction removes substrate, or forms product, in less time.

## Investigating temperature

**A typical method (amylase and starch).**

1. Place drops of iodine solution in each well of a spotting tile.
2. Put separate tubes of starch solution and amylase solution in a water bath at the first temperature and leave them to reach that temperature.
3. Mix the two, start the timer, and every 30 seconds transfer a drop of the mixture to a fresh well of iodine.
4. Record the time when the iodine no longer turns blue-black.
5. Repeat at other temperatures, for example 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 and 60 °C, and repeat each temperature three times.

**Variables.**

- *Independent:* temperature.
- *Dependent:* time for starch to disappear (used to calculate rate).
- *Controlled:* volume and concentration of amylase; volume and concentration of starch; pH (use a buffer solution); volume of iodine; interval between samples.
- *Control:* a tube with boiled (denatured) amylase, to show that the starch is broken down by active enzyme and not by anything else.

**Why pre-warm both solutions separately?** If cold enzyme and cold starch are mixed and then warmed, the reaction starts at the wrong temperature, so the result does not represent the temperature being tested.

## Investigating pH

Use the same method, but keep the temperature constant in a water bath (for example 35 °C) and change the pH using **buffer solutions** of known pH, such as pH 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 and 9. Add the same volume of buffer to each tube. Everything else stays the same.

## Processing the results

**Rate** is how fast the reaction happens. When you measure the time for something to happen:

```
rate = 1 / time
```

Multiplying by 1000 gives numbers that are easier to plot.

**Worked example (results invented for illustration).**

| Temperature (°C) | Mean time for starch to disappear (s) | Rate (1000 / time, s⁻¹) |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | 300 | 3.3 |
| 20 | 150 | 6.7 |
| 30 | 80 | 12.5 |
| 40 | 50 | 20.0 |
| 50 | 120 | 8.3 |
| 60 | starch still present after 600 s | 0 (no measurable reaction) |

**Describing the results.** The rate increases from 3.3 at 10 °C to a maximum of 20.0 at 40 °C. Above 40 °C it falls steeply, to 8.3 at 50 °C, and there is no measurable reaction at 60 °C. The optimum is around 40 °C, but because readings are 10 °C apart the true optimum could be anywhere between 30 °C and 50 °C. Testing more temperatures in that range would locate it more precisely.

**Handling an anomaly.** If one repeat is very different from the others, for example 18 s, 21 s and 52 s, leave out the anomalous 52 s and calculate the mean of the other two. Give a possible reason, such as a sample taken late or a water bath that had not reached temperature.

## Explaining the temperature effect

The syllabus names four ideas. A full explanation uses all of them.

**Below the optimum (rate rising):**

- Raising the temperature gives enzyme and substrate molecules more **kinetic energy**.
- They move faster and collide more often, so there are more **effective collisions** per second (collisions in which the substrate enters the active site).
- More enzyme-substrate complexes form per second, so the rate increases.

**Above the optimum (rate falling):**

- The enzyme molecule vibrates so much that the bonds holding its shape break.
- The **shape** of the active site changes, so the substrate no longer **fits**.
- The enzyme is **denatured**; fewer effective collisions occur and the rate falls, reaching zero when all the enzyme is denatured.
- Denaturation is permanent: cooling does not restore the active site.

**At low temperature:** the rate is low because molecules have little kinetic energy and effective collisions are rare. The enzyme is not denatured, so warming it restores activity.

## Explaining the pH effect

- Each enzyme has an **optimum pH** at which its active site has the right shape for the substrate to fit.
- A change in pH away from the optimum, in either direction, changes the shape of the active site.
- The substrate fits less well, so there are fewer effective collisions and fewer enzyme-substrate complexes form; the rate falls.
- At extreme pH the enzyme is **denatured**.

The syllabus notes that different proteases in the stomach and the duodenum work best at different pH levels. Pepsin in the acidic stomach has a low optimum pH, while proteases working in the duodenum, where conditions are made alkaline, have a higher optimum. This is why the pH curve's peak differs from one enzyme to another.

## Common mistakes

- Saying high temperature "kills" an enzyme; it is denatured.
- Explaining the rise in rate only by "more energy", without collisions.
- Saying the substrate is denatured; it is the enzyme.
- Claiming the optimum precisely from widely spaced readings.
- Forgetting to control pH in a temperature investigation, or temperature in a pH investigation.
- Mixing enzyme and substrate before they reach the test temperature.
- Plotting time, not rate, and then describing a "peak" that is really the slowest point.

## Command words

- **Describe** results: give the trend, the optimum and data values.
- **Explain**: give the reasons using kinetic energy, collisions, shape and fit, denaturation.
- **Suggest** improvements: smaller intervals near the optimum, more repeats, a control.
