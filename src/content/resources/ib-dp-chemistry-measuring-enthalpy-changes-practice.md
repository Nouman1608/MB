These questions test Reactivity 1.1, "Measuring enthalpy changes", from the IB Diploma Programme Chemistry guide (first assessment 2025, published February 2023). Understandings 1.1.1, 1.1.2, 1.1.3 and 1.1.4 each appear at least once. The sub-topic has no additional higher level content, so SL and HL candidates can attempt every question, for the course examined from 2025 onwards.

Use these data throughout: specific heat capacity of water, c = 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹. Treat dilute solutions as having a density of 1.00 g cm⁻³ and the same c as water. Ar values: H 1.01, C 12.01, N 14.01, O 16.00, K 39.10, Fe 55.85. Give final answers to three significant figures unless told otherwise.

## Questions

**1.** Distinguish between heat and temperature. **[2]**

**2.** In an instant cold pack, squeezing breaks a pouch of water, solid ammonium nitrate dissolves and the pack becomes cold.

**(a)** Identify the system and the surroundings in this process. **[2]**
**(b)** State the direction of energy transfer and the sign of ΔH for dissolving ammonium nitrate. **[2]**

**3.** Sketch a labelled energy profile for an exothermic reaction. Show ΔH and the activation energy, Ea. **[3]**

**4.** On an energy profile for a reaction, the reactants are at 180 kJ mol⁻¹, the transition state at 245 kJ mol⁻¹ and the products at 60 kJ mol⁻¹ (relative potential energies).

**(a)** Determine ΔH for the reaction. **[1]**
**(b)** Determine the activation energy. **[1]**
**(c)** State, with a reason, whether the reactants or the products are more stable. **[2]**

**5.** 250 g of water in a beaker is warmed from 18.4 °C to 23.9 °C. Calculate the heat absorbed by the water, in kJ. **[2]**

**6.** 55.0 cm³ of 0.900 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid is mixed with 55.0 cm³ of 0.900 mol dm⁻³ sodium hydroxide solution in an insulated cup. The temperature rises by 6.1 K.

**(a)** Calculate the enthalpy change of neutralisation, in kJ mol⁻¹. **[4]**
**(b)** State two assumptions made in your calculation. **[2]**

**7.** 4.00 g of potassium nitrate, KNO₃, is dissolved in 50.0 g of water. The temperature of the water falls by 6.4 K.

**(a)** Calculate the enthalpy change, in kJ mol⁻¹, when one mole of KNO₃ dissolves. Assume only the water changes temperature. **[4]**
**(b)** State one observation, other than the thermometer reading, that would show the process is endothermic. **[1]**

**8.** A 0.600 g cashew nut burns on a mounted needle under a metal can holding 100.0 g of water, which warms by 16.8 K. The packet states that cashews release 23.0 kJ g⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate the energy released per gram of nut in this experiment. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate this value as a percentage of the packet value. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain two reasons why the experimental value is lower. **[2]**

**9.** A spirit burner of ethanol, C₂H₅OH, heats 200.0 g of water in an 85.0 g copper can. The burner loses 0.554 g and the water and can both warm by 12.9 K. The data value for the standard enthalpy change of combustion of ethanol is −1367 kJ mol⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate the enthalpy change of combustion of ethanol, considering only the heat absorbed by the water. **[4]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage difference from the data value. **[1]**
**(c)** The specific heat capacity of copper is 0.385 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹. Recalculate the enthalpy change, including the heat absorbed by the can. **[2]**
**(d)** Suggest one change to the method that would bring the result closer to the data value, and give one reason the value would still be less exothermic. **[2]**

**10.** 3.00 g of iron powder is added to 75.0 cm³ of 0.500 mol dm⁻³ copper(II) sulfate solution:

Fe(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → FeSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)

The temperature is steady at 21.2 °C before mixing. The highest reading recorded is 37.9 °C. Extrapolating the cooling section of the temperature–time graph back to the time of mixing gives 39.4 °C.

**(a)** Explain why the extrapolated temperature is used rather than the highest recorded temperature. **[2]**
**(b)** Show that copper(II) sulfate is the limiting reactant. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the enthalpy change for the reaction, in kJ mol⁻¹. **[3]**

**11.** Nitrogen reacts with oxygen at very high temperatures: N₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2NO(g). The reaction is endothermic.

**(a)** State what happens to the temperature of the surroundings as the reaction proceeds. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain, in terms of the relative stability of reactants and products, why the reaction is endothermic. **[2]**
**(c)** Outline what the symbol ⦵ in ΔH⦵ indicates about the conditions. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Temperature measures the average kinetic energy of the particles (K) [1]. Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference (J) and depends on the amount of substance [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Each half needs its own idea; describing temperature as "how much heat something has" contradicts the second point and is unlikely to earn either.

**2. (a)** System: the dissolving ammonium nitrate and water [1]. Surroundings: everything else, such as the pack, the air and your hand [1].
**(b)** Energy flows from the surroundings into the system [1]; ΔH is **positive** (endothermic) [1].
*Examiner insight:* It is tempting to call the water "the surroundings", but here the water is part of the change; say clearly what you take as the system and keep to it.

**3.** Axes labelled reaction coordinate (x) and potential energy (y) [1]. Product line drawn below the reactant line, with a ΔH arrow pointing down from reactants to products [1]. A single hump, with the Ea arrow from the reactant line up to the peak [1].
*Examiner insight:* Unlabelled lines or arrows drawn from the axis are hard to credit; label reactants, products, ΔH and Ea on the sketch itself.

**4. (a)** ΔH = 60 − 180 = **−120 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** Ea = 245 − 180 = **65 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(c)** The **products** are more stable [1] because they are lower in potential energy than the reactants [1].
*Examiner insight:* Include the sign in (a); the size 120 alone does not show that the reaction is exothermic.

**5.** ΔT = 23.9 − 18.4 = 5.5 K; Q = 250 × 4.18 × 5.5 = 5747.5 J [1] = **5.75 kJ** [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The question asks for kJ, so a correct answer left as 5750 J is in the wrong unit; convert at the end.

**6. (a)** m = 55.0 + 55.0 = 110.0 g; Q = 110.0 × 4.18 × 6.1 = 2805 J = 2.805 kJ [1]
n(H₂O formed) = 0.0550 × 0.900 = 0.0495 mol (neither reagent in excess) [1]
ΔH = −2.805 ÷ 0.0495 [1] = **−56.7 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** Any two of: the solution has a density of 1.00 g cm⁻³ [1]; its specific heat capacity equals that of water [1]; no heat is lost to the air or absorbed by the cup.
*Examiner insight:* Using only 55.0 g for m (one solution) halves Q; show the combined mass of both solutions in your working so the method is visible.

**7. (a)** Q = 50.0 × 4.18 × 6.4 = 1337.6 J = 1.338 kJ [1]
M(KNO₃) = 39.10 + 14.01 + (3 × 16.00) = 101.11 g mol⁻¹; n = 4.00 ÷ 101.11 = 0.03956 mol [1]
ΔH = +1.338 ÷ 0.03956 [1] = **+33.8 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** The beaker feels cold to the touch, or condensation forms on its outside [1].
*Examiner insight:* The temperature fell, so ΔH must be positive; writing −33.8 kJ mol⁻¹ out of habit contradicts the observation in the question and loses credit for the final answer.

**8. (a)** Q = 100.0 × 4.18 × 16.8 = 7022 J = 7.022 kJ [1]
Energy per gram = 7.022 ÷ 0.600 [1] = **11.7 kJ g⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** 11.7 ÷ 23.0 × 100 = **50.9 %** [1]
**(c)** Any two of: much heat from the open flame escapes to the air rather than the water [1]; the metal can absorbs some energy [1]; the nut burns incompletely (soot, unburned residue); energy is lost while moving the burning nut under the can.
*Examiner insight:* "Heat loss" alone is one vague point; for two marks, name two different routes by which energy misses the water.

**9. (a)** M(C₂H₅OH) = (2 × 12.01) + (6 × 1.01) + 16.00 = 46.08 g mol⁻¹; n = 0.554 ÷ 46.08 = 0.01202 mol [1]
Q(water) = 200.0 × 4.18 × 12.9 = 10 784 J = 10.78 kJ [1]
ΔH = −10.78 ÷ 0.01202 [1] = **−897 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** (1367 − 897) ÷ 1367 × 100 = **34.4 %** [1]
**(c)** Q(can) = 85.0 × 0.385 × 12.9 = 422 J; total Q = 10 784 + 422 = 11 206 J [1]
ΔH = −11.21 ÷ 0.01202 = **−932 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(d)** Improvement: e.g. a draught shield, or a smaller gap between wick and can [1]. Remaining reason: e.g. incomplete combustion, or heat still lost to the air [1].
*Examiner insight:* In (a), m is the 200.0 g of water, not the 0.554 g of ethanol; label each mass in your working so the two cannot be swapped.

**10. (a)** Heat is lost while the reaction is still going on, so the recorded peak is too low [1]. Extrapolating to the mixing time estimates the temperature with no heat loss [1].
**(b)** n(CuSO₄) = 0.0750 × 0.500 = 0.0375 mol [1]; n(Fe) = 3.00 ÷ 55.85 = 0.0537 mol, which is more than the 0.0375 mol needed for a 1 : 1 reaction, so CuSO₄ is limiting [1].
**(c)** ΔT = 39.4 − 21.2 = 18.2 K; Q = 75.0 × 4.18 × 18.2 = 5706 J = 5.706 kJ [1]
ΔH = −5.706 ÷ 0.0375 [1] = **−152 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Using 37.9 °C gives −140 kJ mol⁻¹, which ignores the heat-loss correction the question sets up; when an extrapolated value is given, base ΔT on it.

**11. (a)** The temperature of the surroundings falls [1].
**(b)** The products (NO) are less stable / higher in potential energy than the reactants [1], because N₂ is very stable owing to its strong triple bond, so energy must be absorbed overall [1].
**(c)** The value refers to standard conditions, a pressure of 100 kPa, normally quoted at 298 K [1], with all substances in their standard states [1].
*Examiner insight:* In (b), "the reaction needs energy" just restates endothermic; the credit is for comparing the stability of reactants and products.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Putting the mass of the solid or fuel into Q = mcΔT instead of the mass of water or solution.
- Dividing Q by the amount of the excess reagent rather than the limiting one.
- Forgetting the J → kJ conversion before dividing by moles.
- Losing the sign: exothermic answers need a minus sign; a falling thermometer means a positive ΔH.
- Energy profile axes labelled "time" or "energy", or Ea measured from the products.
