## 5.1.1 Core recall

- **Living standards** are how well people live: material consumption plus health, education, leisure, safety and environment.
- An **indicator** is a measurable figure used as evidence about living standards. It is never the whole picture.
- The syllabus names two indicators: **real GDP per head** and the **Human Development Index (HDI)**.

### Real GDP per head in three steps

```
1. Nominal GDP = output at current prices
2. Real GDP = nominal GDP / price index x 100
3. Real GDP per head = real GDP / population
```

**Example (invented).** Nominal GDP 540 billion, price index 120, population 18 million.
Real GDP = 540 / 120 x 100 = 450 billion. Real GDP per head = 450 billion / 18 million = **25,000**.

**Shortcut for change per head:** (1 + real GDP growth) / (1 + population growth) − 1.
Real GDP up 6%, population up 4%: 1.06 / 1.04 − 1 = **1.9%** (not exactly 2%).

### The HDI's three components

| Component | Indicator used |
|---|---|
| Health | Life expectancy at birth |
| Education | Mean years of schooling (adults) and expected years of schooling (children) |
| Income | Gross national income per head, adjusted for price differences |

Values run from 0 to 1. Higher means more human development.

## Advantages and disadvantages

| | Real GDP per head | HDI |
|---|---|---|
| Strengths | Regular, widely available data; adjusts for inflation and population; linked to purchasing power | Covers health and education as well as income; shows whether income turns into longer, better-educated lives |
| Weaknesses | Average hides distribution; omits non-market output and the hidden economy; ignores leisure, health, pollution; exchange-rate problems when comparing countries | Still an average; omits freedom, safety, environment and quality of services; combining three components is a judgement; education data change slowly |
| Best used for | Tracking material income over time | Judging overall well-being between countries |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Living standards | How well people live, in material and non-material terms |
| Nominal GDP | Output valued at current prices |
| Real GDP | Output adjusted for inflation |
| Real GDP per head | Real GDP divided by the population |
| HDI | A UN index combining life expectancy, schooling and income per head, from 0 to 1 |
| Non-market output | Production not sold for money, such as subsistence farming or unpaid care |
| Hidden economy | Activity that goes unrecorded, often to avoid tax |
| Income distribution | How total income is shared between people or households |

## 5.1.2 Why living standards differ

**Within a country**
- Unequal wealth: owners of land, property and shares receive rent, interest and profit.
- Skills and education: scarce skills earn more.
- Region: cities usually have more jobs, higher pay and better services than rural areas.
- Access to public services: same income, different lives if water, clinics or schools are missing.
- Discrimination by gender, ethnicity or disability.
- Taxes, benefits and public services can narrow or widen gaps.

**Between countries**
- Productivity (capital, technology, skills).
- Natural resources, and who receives the earnings.
- Health and education.
- Population growth compared with output growth.
- Stability, institutions and corruption.
- Trade patterns: a narrow range of low-value exports.

**A national average can describe almost nobody.** 30% earn 30,000 and 70% earn 5,000: average = 9,000 + 3,500 = **12,500**, far from both groups.

## Chains to learn

- Higher real income per head → more spending on food, housing and healthcare → better health → higher life expectancy → higher HDI.
- High income from one export owned by few → high GDP per head → little spending on schooling and clinics → HDI rank below GDP rank.
- Population grows as fast as real GDP → real GDP per head unchanged → no rise in average material living standards.

## Exam traps

- Comparing nominal GDP over time: inflation creates false gains.
- Comparing total GDP between countries of different sizes: divide by population.
- Calling the HDI a measure of happiness.
- Forgetting that both indicators are averages.
- Writing "GDP ignores quality of life" without naming what is missed and why it misleads.
- In a "Discuss" answer, giving one side or no judgement.
- Adding or subtracting percentages when the question needs the exact per-head figure.

## Quick self-test

1. State the three components of the HDI.
2. Why is nominal GDP adjusted by a price index?
3. Nominal GDP is 880 billion, the price index is 110 and the population is 20 million. Calculate real GDP per head.
4. Real GDP rises 3% and the population rises 3%. What happens to real GDP per head?
5. Give two reasons why real GDP per head may understate living standards.
6. Give two reasons why real GDP per head may overstate living standards for most people.
7. Why might a country rank higher on GDP per head than on the HDI?
8. State one advantage of the HDI over real GDP per head.
9. State one limitation shared by both indicators.
10. In a country, 20% of people earn 40,000 and 80% earn 10,000. Calculate the average income.
11. Give two reasons for differences in living standards within a country.
12. Why can two households with the same income have different living standards?

### Answers

1. Life expectancy at birth; mean and expected years of schooling; gross national income per head.
2. To remove the effect of price rises, so changes show real changes in output.
3. Real GDP = 880 / 110 x 100 = 800 billion; per head = 800 billion / 20 million = 40,000.
4. It stays the same, because output and population grow at the same rate.
5. Any two: it leaves out subsistence and unpaid work; it leaves out the hidden economy; it ignores more leisure or better health.
6. Any two: income may be very unequally shared; growth may come with pollution or longer working hours; output may be spent on defence rather than consumption.
7. High income may be concentrated among few people or not spent on health and education.
8. It includes health and education, not just income.
9. Both are averages, so they hide distribution (or: both leave out freedom and the environment).
10. (0.2 x 40,000) + (0.8 x 10,000) = 8,000 + 8,000 = 16,000.
11. Any two: wealth ownership, skills and education, region, access to services, discrimination, tax and benefit policy.
12. One may have access to clean water, clinics and schools, or work shorter hours, or live in a less polluted area.
