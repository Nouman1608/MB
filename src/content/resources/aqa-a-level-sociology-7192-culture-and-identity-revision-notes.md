These notes cover Culture and Identity for AQA A-level Sociology (7192), from the AQA AS and A-level Sociology (7191, 7192) specification, Version 1.2 (14 October 2021). The topic is section 4.2.1 of the A-level content (shown as 3.2.1 in the specification-at-a-glance list). It is examined in Paper 2: Topics in Sociology, Section A, as one of the option 1 topics. Test yourself afterwards with the [practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-sociology-7192-culture-and-identity-practice/), and track the whole course with the [7192 checklist](/checklists/aqa/a-level/sociology/) and the [AQA A-level Sociology hub](/boards/aqa/a-level/sociology/).

## The five content points

1. Different conceptions of culture: subculture, mass, folk, high and low, popular and global culture.
2. The socialisation process and the role of the agencies of socialisation.
3. The self, identity and difference as both socially caused and socially constructed.
4. Identity and age, disability, ethnicity, gender, nationality, sexuality and social class in contemporary society.
5. Identity and production, consumption and globalisation.

**Paper 2 option rule (as printed):** Section A is "one from option 1: 4.2.1, 4.2.2, 4.2.3 or 4.2.4"; Section B is "one from option 2: 4.2.5, 4.2.6, 4.2.7 or 4.2.8". The paper is 2 hours, 80 marks, 33.3% of the A-level; each section is extended writing worth 40 marks.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Culture | The shared norms, values, beliefs, language, knowledge and objects of a group |
| Norms | Specific rules of expected behaviour in a situation |
| Values | General beliefs about what is good or worth aiming for |
| High culture | Works judged to have lasting artistic value; linked to elite groups |
| Low culture | Entertainment judged to have less artistic worth; a value-laden label |
| Folk culture | Pre-industrial culture made by ordinary people and passed on orally |
| Mass culture | Standardised commercial culture produced for large, passive audiences |
| Popular culture | Culture widely enjoyed by ordinary people; audiences may be active |
| Subculture | A group with some norms and values different from the wider culture |
| Global culture | Products, images and styles circulating across many societies |
| Primary socialisation | Early learning of culture, mainly in the family |
| Secondary socialisation | Later learning through school, peers, media, religion and work |
| Identity | How individuals see themselves and how others see them |
| Difference | Defining identity in contrast to others ("us" and "them") |
| Hybrid identity | An identity that mixes two or more cultural sources |
| Master status | A label that overrides a person's other identities |
| Hegemonic masculinity | The dominant, most valued way of being a man in a society |
| Heteronormativity | The assumption that heterosexuality is the normal state |
| Banal nationalism | Everyday, unnoticed reminders of national belonging |
| Conspicuous consumption | Buying goods to display status to others |
| Glocalisation | Global products and ideas adapted to local cultures |

## Theories and studies at a glance

| Who | What | Finding or claim | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arnold | High culture | Culture as "the best which has been thought and said" | Elitist: reflects the tastes of powerful groups |
| Adorno and Horkheimer | Culture industry (Frankfurt School) | Standardised mass culture keeps people passive | Assumes audiences are passive dupes |
| Strinati (1995) | Critique of mass culture theory | Mass culture theory is elitist and ignores audience choice | Underplays the power of large media firms |
| Gans (1974) | Taste cultures | Different tastes, none simply superior | May ignore how taste links to class power |
| Hall and Jefferson (1976) | Youth subcultures as resistance | Working-class youth resist through style | Reads political meaning into fashion |
| Hebdige (1979) | Punk style | Objects given new meaning (bricolage) | Based on the analyst's reading, not members' accounts |
| Parsons | Functionalist socialisation | Family socialises children and stabilises adults | Ignores conflict and abuse within families |
| Oakley | Gender socialisation | Manipulation and canalisation | Children may resist gendered cues |
| Sharpe (1976; 1994) | Girls' priorities | Shift from marriage to careers between the 1970s and 1990s | Small samples; may not apply to all groups |
| Mead | Self through interaction | Taking the role of the "generalised other" | Says little about structural power |
| Cooley | Looking-glass self | Self-image from how we think others see us | Hard to test directly |
| Wrong (1961) | Oversocialised view | People are not puppets of culture | Can understate real constraints |
| Goffman (1959; 1963) | Dramaturgy; stigma | Impression management; spoiled identity | Describes interaction more than causes |
| Giddens (1991) | Reflexive project of the self | People rework identity in late modernity | Overstates choice for poorer groups |
| Hall (1990; 1992) | Identity as becoming; globalisation | Three outcomes: homogenisation, resistance, hybridity | Outcomes vary by group and place |
| Oliver (1990) | Social model of disability | Society's barriers disable people | Shakespeare (2006): ignores impairment effects |
| Modood et al. (1997) | Fourth National Survey | Identity markers vary between and within ethnic groups | Data now dated |
| Gilroy (1993); Back (1996) | Hybrid identities | Young people mix cultural sources | Can underplay racism |
| Connell | Hegemonic masculinity | Masculinities are ranked | Concept is hard to measure |
| Mac an Ghaill (1994) | Crisis of masculinity | Loss of manual jobs unsettles male identity | Not all men experience a crisis |
| Butler (1990) | Gender performativity | Gender produced by repeated acts | Less on material inequality |
| Anderson (1983) | Imagined communities | Nations as felt bonds among strangers | Ignores divisions within nations |
| Billig (1995) | Banal nationalism | Everyday reminders reproduce nationhood | Hard to show the effect on individuals |
| Foucault; Weeks | Constructed sexualities | Sexual categories are historically specific | Essentialists stress orientation is not chosen |
| Skeggs (1997) | Class and respectability | Working-class women seek respectability | Small, local sample |
| Savage et al. (2013) | Great British Class Survey | Seven classes from economic, cultural, social capital | Self-selecting online sample |
| Pakulski and Waters (1996) | Death of class | Consumption replaces class as identity source | Class still predicts life chances |
| Veblen (1899) | Conspicuous consumption | Goods display status | Written about an elite, not mass consumers |
| Bauman (1998) | Flawed consumers | The poor are excluded from consumer society | Supports, not refutes, the role of class |
| Ritzer (1993) | McDonaldisation | Rationalised global sameness | Glocalisation shows local adaptation |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Mass culture versus popular culture.** Mass culture implies imposed, passive consumption; popular culture is a neutral or positive term for what people enjoy.
- **High versus low culture.** A judgement about value, not a fact about the object. Postmodernists say the line has blurred.
- **Folk versus mass culture.** Folk culture is made by communities for themselves; mass culture is made by industries for profit.
- **Subculture versus neo-tribe.** Subcultures are fairly fixed and often class-based; neo-tribes are loose and chosen.
- **Primary versus secondary socialisation.** Family in early childhood versus other agencies later in life.
- **Socially caused versus socially constructed.** Identity shaped by structure (functionalism, Marxism) versus identity built in interaction and meaning (interactionism, postmodernism).
- **Medical versus social model of disability.** Impairment as personal problem versus disability as social barrier.
- **Production versus consumption.** Identity from what you do at work versus what you buy and display.
- **Homogenisation versus hybridity.** Globalisation making cultures the same versus mixing them into something new.

## Mini essay plan

**Question type:** "Evaluate the view that consumption, rather than social class, is now the main source of identity."

1. **Introduction (AO1).** Define identity and consumption. State the postmodern claim and the structural counter-view.
2. **For the view (AO1, AO2).** Pakulski and Waters: the "death of class". Postmodernists: identity as a pick-and-mix of brands and styles. Apply to an example such as online fashion subcultures.
3. **Analysis (AO3).** Link to the decline of production-based identities after de-industrialisation; consumption fills the gap.
4. **Against: class still matters (AO3).** Bauman's flawed consumers: choice needs money. Skeggs: working-class women judged by middle-class standards. Savage et al.: cultural capital still sorts people into classes.
5. **Other sources of identity (AO3).** Ethnicity, gender and nationality may outweigh both; hybrid identities mix several.
6. **Theory comparison.** Marxists: consumption is shaped by capitalism and advertising. Interactionists: identity is negotiated whatever its source.
7. **Conclusion.** Weigh the evidence. Consumption gives more visible choice, but class shapes who can choose. Stay balanced and tie back to the question wording.

## Quick self-test

1. Name two features of folk culture.
2. Who used the phrase "culture industry"?
3. Give one criticism Strinati made of mass culture theory.
4. Define canalisation.
5. What did Mead mean by the "generalised other"?
6. What is the difference between "socially caused" and "socially constructed" identity?
7. What does Goffman mean by a "spoiled identity"?
8. State the key claim of the social model of disability.
9. What is banal nationalism? Give an example.
10. Which three types of capital did the Great British Class Survey use?
11. What are Hall's three possible outcomes of globalisation for identity?
12. Who described the poor as "flawed consumers"?

### Answers

1. Made by ordinary people in pre-industrial communities; passed on by word of mouth.
2. Adorno and Horkheimer (Frankfurt School).
3. It is elitist and treats audiences as passive, ignoring how they choose and reinterpret culture.
4. Channelling children towards gender-typed toys and activities.
5. The general attitudes of society, which a child learns to take into account when seeing itself.
6. Caused: shaped by structures such as class or the economy. Constructed: built and negotiated through interaction and meaning.
7. An identity damaged by a stigma, which the person has to manage in interaction.
8. People are disabled by social barriers, not by their impairment itself.
9. Everyday, unnoticed reminders of nationhood, such as national flags on public buildings.
10. Economic, cultural and social capital.
11. Homogenisation (erosion), resistance (strengthening) and hybridity (new identities).
12. Zygmunt Bauman.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining mass culture as "culture that lots of people like". That is closer to popular culture; mass culture carries the idea of standardised, imposed products.
- Listing agencies of socialisation without saying how each one works.
- Naming a theorist without the concept: "Goffman said identity is important" earns little; "Goffman's front stage and backstage" earns more.
- Mixing up Hall's 1990 "becoming" argument with his 1992 globalisation outcomes.
- Treating postmodern choice as fact. Always test it against class, income, racism or disability.
- Ignoring the question's specified source of identity, such as writing on gender when asked about age.
- Evaluation by assertion ("this is a good theory"). Use a rival theory or study as counter-evidence.
- Forgetting links to other topics, such as education or the media, which the specification asks you to draw out.
- Using dated studies without noting when the data was collected.
