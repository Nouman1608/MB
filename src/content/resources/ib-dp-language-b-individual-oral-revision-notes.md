[individual oral study guide](/resources/ib-dp-language-b-individual-oral/). These notes condense it

They cover the **individual oral** of IB Diploma Programme Language B, at SL and HL. They follow the
IB Language B subject brief for first assessment 2020, which remains the examined course until the new
syllabus is first assessed in May 2029, so they apply to the 2026, 2027 and 2028 sessions. Examples
draw on the themes **identities**, **experiences** and **sharing the planet**. Test yourself with the
[practice questions](/resources/ib-dp-language-b-individual-oral-practice/). Course links:

## Key facts from the brief

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| Type of assessment | Internal |
| Weighting | 25% of the final grade |
| Marks | 30 |
| SL stimulus | A visual image clearly relevant to one (or more) of the themes |
| HL stimulus | An excerpt from one of the two literary works studied |
| HL literary works | Two, originally written in the target language |
| Skills shown | Receptive, productive and interactive |
| Not in the brief | Timings, stages, rules on notes, assessment criteria: get these from your teacher |

## The five themes and their possible questions

The stimulus links to a prescribed theme. The brief's possible questions make ready-made lenses.

| Theme | Possible questions (from the brief) |
|---|---|
| Identities | What constitutes an identity? How do language and culture contribute to form our identity? |
| Experiences | How does our past shape our present and our future? How and why do different cultures mark important moments in life? |
| Human ingenuity | What can we learn about a culture through its artistic expression? How do the media change the way we relate to each other? |
| Social organization | What is the individual's role in the community? What role do rules and regulations play in the formation of a society? |
| Sharing the planet | What environmental and social issues present challenges to the world, and how can these challenges be overcome? What challenges and benefits does globalization bring? |

## Method: reading the stimulus

**SL — visual image**

```
1. Literal   who / where / what, in 2-3 sentences
2. Theme     which theme + topic, and WHICH details show it
3. Lens      one possible question -> your main idea
4. Culture   one specific target-language culture example
```

**HL — literary extract**

```
1. Where     what comes just before and after (1-2 sentences)
2. What      the key action, change or conflict
3. How       2-3 features, each: quotation -> effect
4. Why       link to the whole work and a theme
```

## Method: the five-move presentation

```
1. Frame      stimulus + main idea in one sentence
2. Describe   brief (SL) / place in the work (HL)
3. Interpret  two points tied to the theme or possible question
4. Connect    culture example (SL) / wider work (HL)
5. Close      a tension or question that opens discussion
```

Description is the **shortest** move. Interpretation and connection take most of your time.

## Method: extending an answer in conversation

```
Answer   -> give a clear position ("Yes, mostly…")
Reason   -> because…
Example  -> a specific case: a text, a place, your own life
(Qualify)-> "but it depends on…" / "on the other hand…"
```

## Worked reminders

**SL image (identities).** A girl follows a grandmother's recipe on a video call; it is night where
she is and day on screen.
Weak frame: *"There is a girl and a laptop and some flour."*
Strong frame: *"This image shows a family recipe passed on across two countries, and I think it asks
how language and culture shape who we are."*

**HL extract (experiences).** A character keeps putting off a reply to a letter from home.
Weak: *"Then she puts the letter under the dictionary and says she will answer tomorrow."*
Strong: *"The repeated 'tomorrow' shows her avoiding home, and that delay is the first sign of the
distance that grows through the work."*

**Conversation (sharing the planet).** *"Can one person make a difference to pollution?"*
Weak: *"No."*
Strong: *"Alone, not much, because much pollution comes from industry. But one person can start a
group. For example, a street clean-up often begins with a few neighbours, and others join when they
see it working."*

## Must-know distinctions

- **SL vs HL stimulus.** SL: a visual image. HL: an excerpt from one of your two literary works. Do
  not prepare an image routine for HL or a plot summary for SL.
- **Theme vs topic vs possible question.** The five themes are **prescribed**. The topics under each
  are **optional recommended**. The possible questions are prompts, useful as lenses.
- **Describing vs interpreting.** Description says what is there. Interpretation says what it means
  and why it matters for the theme.
- **Answering vs extending.** An answer replies. An extended answer gives a reason and an example.
- **Paraphrasing vs switching language.** Paraphrase keeps you in the target language; switching
  breaks the interaction.
- **Specific vs general cultural comment.** "In the region the text describes…" beats "everyone in
  country X…".
- **Key-word notes vs a script.** Key words support natural speech; a script sounds read and fails
  when you lose your place.

## Final-week routine

- **Daily one-minute frames.** Take one new image or passage each day and say your frame sentence
  aloud in the target language within a minute.
- **Two-question drill.** Ask a friend to put two unexpected questions to you; answer each with
  answer, reason and example.
- **Missing-word practice.** Pick five theme words you know you forget and practise paraphrasing
  each one.
- **HL:** reread your marked passages in both works and say one feature and one theme link for each.
- **Check the rules.** Confirm timings, stages and any rules on notes with your teacher.

## Quick self-test

1. What percentage of the final grade is the individual oral worth?
2. How many marks is the oral out of?
3. Is the oral internally or externally assessed?
4. What is the stimulus at SL?
5. What is the stimulus at HL?
6. How many literary works do HL students study, and in what language were they first written?
7. Name the three kinds of skill through which the brief says communication is evidenced.
8. Give one possible question the brief lists for identities.
9. In the five-move plan, which move should be the shortest?
10. What three parts make an extended answer in conversation?
11. You forget the word for "recycling bin". What should you do?
12. Which details of the oral must you get from your teacher, because the brief does not give them?

### Answers

1. 25%.
2. 30 marks.
3. Internally.
4. A visual image clearly relevant to one (or more) of the themes of the course.
5. An excerpt from one of the two literary works studied.
6. Two, originally written in the target language.
7. Receptive, productive and interactive.
8. "What constitutes an identity?" or "How do language and culture contribute to form our identity?"
9. Describe.
10. Answer, reason, example (with an optional qualification).
11. Paraphrase in the target language, e.g. "the container where you put bottles and paper to be
    used again".
12. The timings, the stages, the rules on notes and the assessment criteria.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Spending most of the presentation listing what is in the image.
- At HL, retelling the plot of the extract instead of analysing how it is written.
- Naming a theme without pointing to the details that make the stimulus relevant.
- Giving a general cultural comment ("in my target-language country people care about nature") with
  no specific example.
- Reciting a memorised speech that does not fit the stimulus in front of you.
- Answering questions with one word, or with "I don't know", and stopping.
- Switching to your own language instead of paraphrasing a missing word.
- Using written-essay phrasing read aloud, so the register sounds unnatural.
- At HL, preparing one literary work thoroughly and the other only lightly.
- Preparing with timings or rules from another source instead of your teacher's.
