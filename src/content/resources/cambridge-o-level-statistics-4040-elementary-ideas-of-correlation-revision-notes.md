## Core recall

- **Bivariate data**: pairs of values (x, y) measured on the same item.
- **Scatter diagram**: one point per pair, not joined.
- **x-axis**: the variable that might explain or influence the other (the explanatory variable). **y-axis**: the variable that might respond.
- **Correlation**: how closely the points follow a straight-line pattern, and in which direction.
- **No coefficient is calculated** in 4040; correlation is judged from the diagram.

## Key terms

| Term | Recognise it by | Write it as |
|---|---|---|
| Positive correlation | Points rise left to right | "As x increases, y tends to increase" |
| Negative correlation | Points fall left to right | "As x increases, y tends to decrease" |
| No correlation | No upward or downward trend | "No linear relationship" |
| Strong | Narrow band close to a line | "Strong positive/negative correlation" |
| Weak | Wide band; direction still visible | "Weak positive/negative correlation" |
| Outlier | A point well away from the pattern | Name it by its coordinates |
| Third (hidden) variable | A factor that affects both x and y | Name it in context |
| Causation | A change in x produces a change in y | Cannot be shown by correlation alone |

## Plotting checklist

- Uniform scales on both axes; a broken axis is allowed if it is marked.
- Axes labelled with variable names and units.
- Small crosses; count them against the number of pairs.
- Accurate to within half a small square.
- No line unless the question asks for one.

## Describing a scatter diagram: three layers

1. **Type**: strength plus direction, for example "weak negative correlation".
2. **Interpretation in context**: "cars that are older tend to be worth less".
3. **Features**: any outlier, cluster or curved pattern.

### Mini example

Price of a meal (Rs) and number of customers ordering it each week at a café: as price rises the number ordering tends to fall, and the points lie in a fairly narrow band, except one expensive dish ordered very often.

- Type: strong negative correlation.
- Interpretation: more expensive meals tend to be ordered less often.
- Feature: the popular expensive dish is an outlier; it may be a special offer or a recording error.

## Strength versus steepness

| Pattern | Correlation |
|---|---|
| Points almost on a shallow line | Strong |
| Points widely scattered round a steep line | Weak |
| Same data replotted with a stretched y-axis | Unchanged |

Strength depends on scatter round the line, not on the gradient.

## Outliers

- Possible causes: measuring or recording error; a genuine unusual case.
- Effect: can weaken a real correlation or create a false one.
- Remove only with evidence of an error; otherwise keep and comment.

## Curves and clusters

- A clear curve (rising then falling) can give little or no **linear** correlation even though x and y are related. Say "no linear correlation, but a curved relationship".
- Two separate groups, such as children and adults, can create apparent correlation overall that is not present within either group.

## Correlation is not causation

| Explanation | Example |
|---|---|
| Third variable | Ice cream sales and sunburn cases: both rise with hot, sunny weather |
| Coincidence over time | Two unrelated quantities that both increased over the same years |
| Reverse direction | Fitness and exercise: fit people may exercise more because they find it easier |

A full answer rejects the cause claim, names the alternative explanation in context and says how it links the two variables.

## Exam traps

- Giving only direction ("positive") when "describe" needs strength too.
- Saying "y increases" instead of "y tends to increase".
- Judging strength by steepness.
- Leaving the context out of an interpretation.
- Joining the points or drawing a line of best fit uninvited.
- Missing the outlier, or deleting it with no reason.
- Treating a curve as "no relationship".
- Writing "correlation does not mean causation" with no context-specific reason.

## Quick self-test

1. What is bivariate data?
2. Which variable usually goes on the horizontal axis?
3. Describe the correlation when points fall from left to right and lie close to a line.
4. Points rise from left to right but are widely scattered. Describe the correlation.
5. Does a steeper pattern mean stronger correlation?
6. Write an interpretation of positive correlation between age of a tree and its height.
7. Give two possible reasons for an outlier.
8. When should an outlier be removed?
9. Points form an arch shape. Describe the correlation and the relationship.
10. Across countries, the number of televisions per household and life expectancy are positively correlated. Suggest a third variable.
11. Does strong correlation between two variables show that one causes the other?
12. What should you check after plotting a scatter diagram?

### Answers

1. Pairs of values of two variables measured on the same item or individual.
2. The variable that might explain or influence the other.
3. Strong negative correlation.
4. Weak positive correlation.
5. No. Strength depends on how close the points are to a line, not its gradient.
6. Older trees tend to be taller.
7. A recording or measuring error; a genuine but unusual item.
8. Only when there is evidence it is an error.
9. Little or no linear correlation, but a curved relationship between the variables.
10. Wealth of the country (for example average income), which raises both.
11. No. A third variable, coincidence or cause in the other direction could explain it.
12. That the number of points equals the number of pairs and that each is plotted accurately.
