This guide goes deeper into section **4.3.1 Communicable diseases** of the AQA GCSE Biology (8461) specification,
for first teaching from 2016. It covers 4.3.1.1 to 4.3.1.9: pathogens and how they spread, the named viral,
bacterial, fungal and protist diseases, human defences, vaccination, antibiotics and painkillers, and the
discovery and development of drugs. Infection and response is assessed on Paper 1. No statement in 4.3.1 is
marked Higher tier only, so everything here is for both tiers.

## What the specification asks you to do

You must explain how diseases caused by viruses, bacteria, protists and fungi spread and how spread can be
reduced; recall seven named diseases; describe the body's defences; explain vaccination; explain the use of
antibiotics and painkillers; and describe how new drugs are discovered and tested. You do not need vaccination
schedules or the side effects of particular vaccines.

## Thinking about spread as a route

A **pathogen** is a microorganism that causes infectious disease. The four types are viruses, bacteria,
protists and fungi, and they can infect plants or animals. The specification names three routes of spread:
**direct contact**, **water** and **air**. A fourth idea runs through the named diseases: some pathogens are
carried by a **vector**, an organism that carries the pathogen from one host to another.

The most useful exam habit is to treat every control method as **breaking a link in the route** from one host
to the next. When a question asks you to "explain how the spread can be reduced", name the route, then name the
measure that blocks that route.

| Route | Example from the specification | Measure that breaks the route |
|---|---|---|
| Air (droplets) | Measles virus in droplets from sneezes and coughs | Vaccination, so few people can catch and pass it on |
| Direct contact, body fluids | HIV through sexual contact or shared needles | Condoms, not sharing needles, antiretroviral drugs |
| Direct sexual contact | Gonorrhoea | Antibiotic treatment of infected people; condoms |
| Food | Salmonella in undercooked food or food prepared in unhygienic conditions | Hygienic food preparation, thorough cooking, vaccinating poultry |
| Water or wind | Rose black spot spores | Remove and destroy infected leaves; fungicides |
| Vector | Malarial protist carried by mosquitoes | Stop mosquitoes breeding; mosquito nets |

## How pathogens make you ill

Bacteria and viruses can both **reproduce rapidly** inside the body, so a small number entering can quickly
become millions. They cause illness in different ways, and examiners expect you to keep these apart.

- **Bacteria** may produce **poisons (toxins)** that damage tissues and make us feel ill. Salmonella symptoms
  are caused by the bacteria and the toxins they secrete.
- **Viruses** live and reproduce **inside cells**, causing **cell damage**. The virus uses the cell to make new
  viruses, which are released and infect more cells.

## The named diseases, compared

| Disease | Pathogen | Host | Key symptoms | Spread | Control |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Measles | Virus | Humans | Fever, red skin rash; can be fatal if complications arise | Inhaling droplets from sneezes and coughs | Most young children vaccinated |
| HIV | Virus | Humans | Flu-like illness at first; then attacks immune cells; late stage HIV infection (AIDS) when the immune system cannot deal with other infections or cancers | Sexual contact; exchange of body fluids such as blood when drug users share needles | Antiretroviral drugs; condoms; not sharing needles |
| Tobacco mosaic virus (TMV) | Virus | Many plants, including tomatoes | Mosaic pattern of discolouration on leaves | Contact between plants | Remove infected plants; good hygiene |
| Salmonella food poisoning | Bacterium | Humans | Fever, abdominal cramps, vomiting, diarrhoea | Ingested in food, or food prepared in unhygienic conditions | Poultry vaccinated in the UK; hygiene; thorough cooking |
| Gonorrhoea | Bacterium | Humans | Thick yellow or green discharge from vagina or penis; pain on urinating | Sexual contact | Antibiotics; barrier contraception such as a condom |
| Rose black spot | Fungus | Roses | Purple or black spots on leaves, which often turn yellow and drop early | Water or wind | Fungicides and/or removing and destroying affected leaves |
| Malaria | Protist | Humans (life cycle includes the mosquito) | Recurrent episodes of fever; can be fatal | Mosquito vector | Prevent mosquitoes breeding; mosquito nets |

Two plant diseases appear in this list, and the reason they harm the plant is the same: **less photosynthesis**.
TMV discolours the leaves, so less light is absorbed; black spot kills leaf tissue and makes leaves fall early.
Less glucose is made, so the plant grows less.

Gonorrhoea is the specification's example of a disease where **antibiotic resistance** matters. It was easily
treated with **penicillin** until many resistant strains appeared, so prevention by condoms has become more
important.

## Defending the body

### Non-specific defences

These act against any pathogen and try to stop it getting in at all.

- **Skin**: a physical barrier; it also produces antimicrobial secretions, and healthy skin bacteria compete
  with pathogens.
- **Nose**: hairs and mucus trap particles that may contain pathogens.
- **Trachea and bronchi**: cells secrete mucus that traps pathogens, and cilia waft the mucus up to the throat,
  where it is swallowed.
- **Stomach**: produces hydrochloric acid, which kills most pathogens that are swallowed.

### The immune system

If a pathogen gets past these barriers, the immune system tries to destroy it. White blood cells defend
against pathogens in three ways:

1. **Phagocytosis**: the white blood cell engulfs the pathogen and digests it.
2. **Antibody production**: antibodies bind to specific antigens on the surface of the pathogen. Each antibody
   fits only one antigen, which is why a person needs time to make the right antibody against a new pathogen.
3. **Antitoxin production**: antitoxins neutralise the toxins released by bacteria.

## Vaccination and protecting a population

A vaccine contains **small quantities of dead or inactive forms of a pathogen**. These stimulate white blood
cells to produce the right antibodies. If the same pathogen later enters the body, the white blood cells
respond **quickly** to produce the correct antibodies, so the pathogen is destroyed before it can cause illness.

The second idea the specification tests is population-level protection. If a large proportion of a population
is immune, the pathogen has few people to infect, so its spread is greatly reduced. This also protects people
who have not been vaccinated, for example those too young or too ill to receive a vaccine. When vaccination
rates fall, the pathogen can again find enough non-immune people to spread, and cases rise.

**Annotated model answer**

*Question: Explain how vaccinating most children against measles protects a baby too young to be vaccinated. [3]*

> Most children are immune, so if one child is infected there are very few non-immune people for the virus to
> spread to [1]. The virus is passed on by droplets to fewer people, so the number of cases stays very low [1].
> The baby is therefore much less likely to meet someone carrying the measles virus [1].

Saying only "herd immunity protects the baby" names the idea but does not explain it, so it would score little.

## Antibiotics and painkillers

**Antibiotics**, such as penicillin, help to cure **bacterial** disease by killing infective bacteria inside
the body. Specific bacteria should be treated with specific antibiotics. Antibiotics have greatly reduced
deaths from bacterial disease, but the emergence of resistant strains is a serious concern.

Antibiotics **cannot kill viruses**. Viruses reproduce inside the body's own cells, so it is difficult to
develop drugs that kill viruses without also damaging the body's tissues. **Painkillers** and other medicines
treat the **symptoms** of a disease, but they do not kill the pathogen.

## Discovery and development of drugs

Traditionally, drugs were extracted from plants and microorganisms:

- **digitalis**, a heart drug, originates from **foxgloves**;
- **aspirin**, a painkiller, originates from **willow**;
- **penicillin** was discovered by **Alexander Fleming** from the **Penicillium** mould.

Most new drugs are now synthesised by chemists in the pharmaceutical industry, although the starting point may
still be a chemical extracted from a plant.

New drugs are tested for **toxicity** (is it harmful?), **efficacy** (does it work?) and **dose** (how much
should be given?).

1. **Preclinical testing** in a laboratory, using cells, tissues and live animals.
2. **Clinical trials** using healthy volunteers and patients. Very low doses are given at the start.
3. If the drug is safe, further clinical trials find the **optimum dose**.
4. In **double blind trials**, some patients are given a **placebo**, which does not contain the drug. Neither
   the doctors nor the patients know who has received the drug until the trial is complete, so expectations
   cannot bias the results.
5. Results are published only after scrutiny by **peer review**, where other scientists check the work.

**Worked example: interpreting trial data** (invented figures)

In a trial, 250 patients take a new drug and 250 take a placebo. 170 improve on the drug and 95 on the placebo.

```
drug group:     170 ÷ 250 × 100 = 68%
placebo group:   95 ÷ 250 × 100 = 38%
difference:      68 − 38 = 30 percentage points
```

Some placebo patients improve without the drug, so only the **difference** between the groups suggests the
effect of the drug itself. That is the reason for having a placebo group.

## Common mistakes

- Saying antibiotics kill viruses, or that a painkiller cures a disease.
- Writing that white blood cells "eat" pathogens without naming phagocytosis or describing digestion.
- Confusing antibodies (bind to antigens on pathogens) with antitoxins (neutralise toxins).
- Saying a vaccine contains "the disease" instead of dead or inactive pathogens.
- Calling the mosquito the pathogen. The protist is the pathogen; the mosquito is the vector.
- Saying a placebo is "a fake drug that does nothing" without explaining why it is used.

## Exam technique for AQA command words

- **Describe** the spread of a disease: give the route and the steps, such as "droplets are breathed in".
- **Explain** a control method: say which route it blocks and why that reduces new infections.
- **Compare** two diseases: write paired points ("measles is spread by droplets, whereas malaria is spread by a
  mosquito vector"), not two separate descriptions.
- **Evaluate** a treatment or trial: give advantages and disadvantages from the information, then a justified
  conclusion.
