These notes condense subtopic **11.4, The reactions of chlorine** (Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027, AS Level). Full explanations and worked examples are in the [11.4 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-as-the-reactions-of-chlorine/). ## The two outcomes

- Chlorine with **cold** and **hot** aqueous NaOH, interpreted by **oxidation number**, recognised as **disproportionation**.
- Chlorine in **water purification**, with the equation, producing **HOCl** and **ClO⁻**, which kill bacteria.

## Disproportionation in one line

The **same element** is both oxidised and reduced in one reaction.

## Cold aqueous NaOH

```
Cl2 + 2NaOH  ->  NaCl + NaClO + H2O
Cl2 + 2OH-   ->  Cl-  + ClO-  + H2O
 0               -1     +1
```

- Reduced: 0 → −1. Oxidised: 0 → +1.
- NaClO = **sodium chlorate(I)** (bleach).
- Ratio Cl⁻ : ClO⁻ = 1 : 1 (1 electron gained, 1 lost).

## Hot aqueous NaOH

```
3Cl2 + 6NaOH  ->  5NaCl + NaClO3 + 3H2O
3Cl2 + 6OH-   ->  5Cl-  + ClO3-  + 3H2O
  0                -1      +5
```

- Reduced: 0 → −1. Oxidised: 0 → +5.
- NaClO₃ = **sodium chlorate(V)**.
- Ratio Cl⁻ : ClO₃⁻ = 5 : 1, because 5 atoms each gain 1 electron for every 1 atom that loses 5.

**Why heat matters:** ClO⁻ disproportionates on heating: 3ClO⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + ClO₃⁻ (+1 → −1 and +1 → +5).

## Water purification

```
Cl2 + H2O  <=>  HCl + HOCl        Cl: 0 -> -1 and +1 (disproportionation)
HOCl       <=>  H+  + ClO-        weak acid, partial dissociation
```

- **HOCl** = chloric(I) acid; **ClO⁻** = chlorate(I) ion.
- **HOCl and ClO⁻ kill bacteria.**
- Lower pH → more HOCl; higher pH → more ClO⁻.

## Calculation pattern

0.300 mol Cl₂ with hot NaOH → 0.300 ÷ 3 = 0.100 mol NaClO₃ → 0.100 × 106.5 = 10.7 g; and 0.500 mol NaCl.

0.0200 mol Cl₂ with cold NaOH → 0.0200 mol NaClO → 0.0200 × 74.5 = 1.49 g.

## Oxidation numbers of chlorine to know

| Species | Name | Oxidation number |
|---|---|---|
| Cl₂ | Chlorine | 0 |
| Cl⁻, NaCl, HCl | Chloride | −1 |
| ClO⁻, NaClO, HOCl | Chlorate(I) / chloric(I) acid | +1 |
| ClO₃⁻, NaClO₃ | Chlorate(V) | +5 |

## Balancing a disproportionation in four lines

1. Write the oxidation number of Cl in every species.
2. Find the electrons gained per Cl reduced and lost per Cl oxidised.
3. Choose numbers so that electrons gained = electrons lost (hot: 5 × 1 = 1 × 5).
4. Balance O and charge with OH⁻ and H₂O, then check every element.

## Three reactions side by side

| Conditions | Chlorine-containing products | Oxidation numbers |
|---|---|---|
| Water | HCl + HOCl | −1 and +1 |
| Cold NaOH(aq) | NaCl + NaClO | −1 and +1 |
| Hot NaOH(aq) | NaCl + NaClO₃ | −1 and +5 |

The cold-alkali products are the water products with both acids neutralised by NaOH.

## Moles from gas volumes

Chlorine is often measured as a gas volume. At room conditions one mole of gas occupies 24.0 dm³, so 1.20 dm³ of Cl₂ is 1.20 ÷ 24.0 = 0.0500 mol. With cold NaOH this gives 0.0500 mol NaClO (3.73 g); with hot NaOH it gives 0.0500 ÷ 3 = 0.0167 mol NaClO₃.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Disproportionation | Redox reaction in which one element is simultaneously oxidised and reduced |
| Oxidation number | The charge an atom would have if all its bonds were ionic; used to track electron transfer |
| Sodium chlorate(I) | NaClO; chlorine +1 |
| Sodium chlorate(V) | NaClO₃; chlorine +5 |
| Chloric(I) acid | HOCl; a weak acid and one active species in water treatment |
| Active species | The species that actually kills bacteria: HOCl and ClO⁻ |

## Exam traps

- Swapping hot and cold products.
- "Disproportionation" with no oxidation numbers shown.
- NaClO called "sodium chlorate" without the (I), or NaClO₃ called chlorate(I).
- Single arrow for Cl₂ + H₂O.
- Saying HCl kills the bacteria.
- Hot equation balanced as 4 : 2 or 3 : 1 instead of 5 : 1.
- Forgetting that the chlorine–water reaction is also a disproportionation.
- Treating NaOCl and NaClO as different compounds: they are two ways of writing sodium chlorate(I).
- Using the cold-alkali 1 : 1 ratio in a calculation about the hot reaction.

## Self-test

1. Define disproportionation.
2. Write the ionic equation for chlorine with cold aqueous sodium hydroxide.
3. Give the oxidation number of chlorine in NaClO and in NaClO₃.
4. Write the full equation for chlorine with hot aqueous sodium hydroxide.
5. Explain, using electrons, why five NaCl form for every NaClO₃ in the hot reaction.
6. Write the equation showing chlorate(I) ions disproportionating on heating.
7. Write the equation for chlorine reacting with water.
8. Name the two species that kill bacteria in chlorinated water.
9. Write the equation for the dissociation of HOCl.
10. What happens to the amount of HOCl if acid is added to chlorinated water?
11. Calculate the mass of NaClO₃ formed from 0.150 mol Cl₂ and excess hot NaOH (Mᵣ NaClO₃ = 106.5).
12. Is the chlorine–water reaction a disproportionation? Explain.

**Answers:**

1. A redox reaction in which the same element is both oxidised and reduced.
2. Cl₂ + 2OH⁻ → Cl⁻ + ClO⁻ + H₂O
3. +1 and +5.
4. 3Cl₂ + 6NaOH → 5NaCl + NaClO₃ + 3H₂O
5. Each Cl reduced to Cl⁻ gains 1 electron; each Cl oxidised to ClO₃⁻ loses 5. Five gains of one electron balance one loss of five.
6. 3ClO⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + ClO₃⁻
7. Cl₂ + H₂O ⇌ HCl + HOCl
8. HOCl and ClO⁻.
9. HOCl ⇌ H⁺ + ClO⁻
10. It increases: the equilibrium HOCl ⇌ H⁺ + ClO⁻ shifts to the left.
11. 0.150 ÷ 3 = 0.0500 mol; 0.0500 × 106.5 = **5.33 g**.
12. Yes. Chlorine goes from 0 to −1 in HCl and from 0 to +1 in HOCl.
