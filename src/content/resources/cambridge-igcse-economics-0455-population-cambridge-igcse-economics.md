## 5.3.1 Working with rates

Birth and death rates are usually stated **per 1,000 people per year**. Net migration can be stated the same way. Because they share a base, they can be added and subtracted directly.

```
Natural increase rate = birth rate - death rate
Population growth rate = natural increase rate + net migration rate
Per 1,000 -> percentage: divide by 10
```

**Worked example 1.** Country A (invented): birth rate 32, death rate 12, net migration rate −2 (all per 1,000).

1. Natural increase = 32 − 12 = 20 per 1,000.
2. Growth rate = 20 + (−2) = 18 per 1,000 = **1.8%** a year.
3. With 50 million people, the population rises by 50,000,000 × 0.018 = **900,000** in a year.

**Worked example 2.** Country B (invented): birth rate 9, death rate 11, net migration rate +4.

1. Natural increase = 9 − 11 = −2 per 1,000: more deaths than births.
2. Growth rate = −2 + 4 = +2 per 1,000 = **0.2%**.

Country B grows only because immigration exceeds emigration. Without it, the population would fall.

**Remember the difference:** a *rate* is per 1,000; a *number* is a count of people. A large country can have more births than a small one while having a lower birth rate.

## 5.3.2 Why rates differ between countries

| Factor | Pushes the rate up | Pushes the rate down |
|---|---|---|
| **Birth rate** | Children work on family farms or support parents in old age; high infant mortality, so families have more children; limited access to contraception; early marriage; cultural or religious preference for large families | Higher incomes and the cost of raising children; women in education and paid work; pensions replace children as old-age support; easy access to contraception |
| **Death rate** | Poor healthcare, sanitation and nutrition; disease, famine or conflict; a large share of elderly people | Medical advances, vaccination, clean water, better diet |
| **Net migration** | Pull factors abroad attract emigrants: higher wages, jobs, safety. At home, jobs and stability attract immigrants | Strict entry rules; conflict or unemployment at home drives people out |

**Age structure explains surprising death rates.** A high-income country with excellent healthcare but many people over 70 can have a higher death rate than a lower-income country where most people are young. When a question shows this, explain it through the age structure, not healthcare.

## 5.3.3 Optimum population

The **optimum population** is the size of population at which output per head is highest, given the country's resources, capital and technology.

- **Under-population:** too few people to use the resources fully. Extra people would raise output per head.
- **Over-population:** so many people that each extra worker adds less than the existing average, so output per head falls.

The optimum is not a fixed number. Investment and new technology raise output per worker and move the optimum upwards; loss of farmland or exhaustion of a mineral moves it down. This is why a growing population can be helpful in one decade and harmful in another.

## 5.3.3 Effects of changes in size

| | Possible benefits | Possible costs |
|---|---|---|
| **Increase** | Larger labour force; bigger market for firms, allowing economies of scale; more taxpayers; more young, adaptable workers | Pressure on housing, water, land and public services; congestion and pollution; unemployment if jobs do not grow as fast; lower output per head if beyond the optimum |
| **Decrease** | Less pressure on resources and the environment; higher output per head if previously over-populated | Labour shortages; smaller market; fewer taxpayers; closure of schools and shops in emptying areas |

Whether an increase helps depends on **where the country is relative to its optimum**, **how fast jobs and capital grow**, and **the age of the new people**: immigrant workers add to the labour force at once, while extra births add to it only after about 15 years.

## 5.3.3 Age distribution: the cost of an ageing population

**Worked example 3: workers per pensioner.** Country C (invented) pays every person over 65 a state pension of 6,000 a year, funded by taxes on people of working age.

| | Now | In 20 years |
|---|---|---|
| Working-age population | 30 million | 27 million |
| Population over 65 | 5 million | 9 million |
| Workers per pensioner | 30 ÷ 5 = 6 | 27 ÷ 9 = 3 |
| Total pension cost | 5 million × 6,000 = 30 billion | 9 million × 6,000 = 54 billion |
| Cost per working-age person | 30 billion ÷ 30 million = 1,000 | 54 billion ÷ 27 million = 2,000 |

The burden on each person of working age doubles. The government can raise taxes, cut the pension, raise the retirement age, encourage immigration of workers, or borrow. Each choice has an opportunity cost.

A **young population** brings different pressures: spending on schools and child health now, and a need for many new jobs when the young reach working age. If those jobs appear, a large working-age group can raise output; if not, youth unemployment rises.

## 5.3.3 Gender distribution

An imbalance between males and females can arise from differences in life expectancy (women usually live longer, so older age groups are often mainly female) or from migration (one sex may move abroad or to cities for work). Effects include changes in the size of the labour force in particular industries, in the demand for care services for elderly women, and in the birth rate if fewer couples form.

## Interpreting a population pyramid

A pyramid shows the percentage of the population in each age group, with males on one side and females on the other. You will be given one to read, not to draw. Read it feature by feature.

| Feature | What it suggests |
|---|---|
| Wide base narrowing quickly | High birth rate and high death rate: a young, fast-growing population |
| Narrow base, wide middle and top | Low birth rate and long life expectancy: an ageing population |
| Base narrower than the band above it | Birth rate has recently fallen |
| A bulge in one band | A period of high births, or immigration of people of that age |
| A notch in one band | A period of low births, emigration, or deaths from war or disease |
| Female side wider at the top | Women live longer than men |
| Male side wider in working ages | Immigration of male workers |

**Worked example 4.** Country D (invented), percentage of total population:

| Age | Male % | Female % |
|---|---|---|
| 65+ | 7 | 10 |
| 40–64 | 18 | 18 |
| 15–39 | 12 | 13 |
| 0–14 | 10 | 12 |

Reading: the 15–39 band (25%) is narrower than the 40–64 band (36%), which suggests emigration of young adults or a fall in births 15 to 39 years ago. The 0–14 band (22%) is narrower still, so births are low. 17% are over 65, with more women than men. Expect rising pension and care costs, a shrinking labour force, and possible labour shortages.

## Annotated model answer

**Question (original):** Discuss whether an ageing population is harmful to an economy. **[8]**

> As the share of older people rises, fewer workers support each pensioner. Pension and healthcare spending rise, so taxes may rise or other spending, such as on education, may be cut. *(Cost, developed.)*
>
> The labour force may shrink, causing shortages that push up wages and costs for firms and reduce output. *(Second cost.)*
>
> However, older people spend on goods and services such as care, travel and health, creating jobs in those industries. Many are healthy and can keep working, especially if the retirement age rises. *(Benefits.)*
>
> The harm can be reduced by immigration of workers, investment in machinery to raise output per worker, and encouraging older people to work longer. *(Policy response.)*
>
> An ageing population is harmful if the labour force falls fast and the government does not adjust; it is less harmful where productivity is rising and older people stay in work. *(Judgement with conditions.)*

## Common mistakes

- Confusing a birth *rate* with the *number* of births.
- Forgetting net migration when calculating growth.
- Saying optimum population is the largest population a country can feed.
- Describing a pyramid's shape without linking it to birth rate, death rate or migration.
- Assuming all population growth is harmful, or all is beneficial.
- Explaining a high death rate in a rich country by poor healthcare instead of age structure.
