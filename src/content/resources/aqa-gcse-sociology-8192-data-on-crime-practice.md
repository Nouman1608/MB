These questions cover 3.5.4 Data on crime, from the AQA GCSE Sociology (8192) specification, assessed in Paper 2: the main sources of data on crime, the collection of official data, patterns and trends, the dark figure, and functionalist, feminist and Marxist perspectives. All places and data in the items are fictional.

## Section A: short questions

**1.** Which source asks people which offences they have committed themselves?

A. Police recorded crime
B. A victim survey
C. A self-report study
D. Court records

**[1]**

**2.** What is the "dark figure" of crime?

A. Crime committed at night
B. Crime that is unreported or unrecorded
C. Crime that has been reported but not solved
D. Crime committed by organised gangs

**[1]**

**3.** Identify two reasons why a victim may not report a crime to the police. **[2]**

**4.** Describe how the Crime Survey for England and Wales collects its data. **[3]**

**5.** Identify one type of crime that victim surveys cannot measure well and explain why. **[2]**

**6.** Explain one reason why a crime that is reported to the police may not be recorded. **[2]**

**7.** Identify and explain one limitation of self-report studies. **[3]**

## Section B: longer and structured questions

**8.** Study the table. The data are fictional.

> **Rexborough (a fictional region): police recorded crimes and victim survey estimates of crimes, selected years**

| Year | Police recorded crimes | Victim survey estimate |
|---|---|---|
| 2014 | 40,000 | 120,000 |
| 2018 | 46,000 | 100,000 |
| 2022 | 52,000 | 90,000 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in the victim survey estimate between 2014 and 2022. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate what percentage of the victim survey estimate was recorded by the police in 2014 and in 2022. Show your working. **[2]**
**(c)** Using the data, describe the trends in Rexborough and suggest one reason why the two sources move in different directions. **[4]**

**9.** Explain one difference between how a functionalist and a Marxist would view official crime statistics. **[4]**

**10.** Explain why feminists argue that official statistics understate crimes against women. **[3]**

**11.** Explain one strength and one limitation of police recorded crime as a source of data. **[4]**

**12.** Discuss how far sociologists would agree that official crime statistics give an accurate picture of crime. **[12]**

**13.** Discuss how far sociologists would agree that victim surveys and self-report studies solve the problem of the dark figure of crime. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** C. A self-report study [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Victim surveys ask about crimes experienced, not committed.

**2.** B. Crime that is unreported or unrecorded [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Option C, unsolved crime, is the most common wrong answer; unsolved crimes have usually been recorded.

**3.** Any two from: the crime seems too trivial; it is seen as a private matter; the victim thinks the police cannot help; fear of reprisal; distrust of the police; the victim does not realise a crime took place. First reason [1]; second reason [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "They don't want to" is too vague; give the reason behind the choice.

**4.** A large random sample of households is selected [1]. People are interviewed about crimes they have experienced in the past twelve months [1], whether or not they reported them to the police, so the survey includes unreported crime [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Mention random sampling and the twelve-month period to show precise knowledge.

**5.** Corporate crime, such as a company breaking safety or environmental laws [1], because there is often no individual victim who knows they have been harmed and can report it to an interviewer [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Homicide, crimes against businesses and victimless crimes such as drug possession are also credited with a reason.

**6.** The police use discretion [1], so an officer may decide there is not enough evidence or that the incident is too minor to count as a crime [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The victim withdrawing a complaint or counting rules are also valid reasons.

**7.** Respondents may not tell the truth [1], for example hiding serious offences out of fear or exaggerating to impress [1], so the data may lack validity as a picture of offending [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the limitation to a methods term such as validity for the final mark.

**8. (a)** (90,000 − 120,000) ÷ 120,000 × 100 = −25%, a fall of 25% [1]
**(b)** 2014: 40,000 ÷ 120,000 × 100 = about 33% [1]. 2022: 52,000 ÷ 90,000 × 100 = about 58% [1].
**(c)** Police recorded crime rose from 40,000 in 2014 to 52,000 in 2022 [1], while the victim survey estimate fell from 120,000 to 90,000 [1]. The two sources move in opposite directions, and the proportion recorded by the police rose from about 33% to about 58% [1]. One reason is that the police may have improved their recording practice, or more victims may be reporting crimes, so recorded crime rises even though less crime is experienced [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* The best answers notice that the gap between the sources is the dark figure and that it shrank.

**9.** A functionalist tends to treat official statistics as a broadly accurate picture of who commits crime [1], so the over-representation of the working class is something to explain, for example through Merton's strain theory [1]. A Marxist sees the statistics as a social construct that reflects heavier policing of the working class [1], while white collar and corporate crimes by the powerful go under-recorded, so the statistics serve ruling-class interests [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The difference is whether the statistics reflect offending or policing; say so directly.

**10.** Domestic and sexual violence often happen in private and victims may fear the offender or not be believed [1], so many such crimes are never reported [1]. Feminists also argue that a male-dominated justice system has not always treated these crimes seriously, so reported cases may not be recorded or prosecuted [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Cover both filters of the dark figure: reporting and recording.

**11.** Strength: police recorded crime has national coverage and is collected regularly [1], so sociologists can track trends over time at little cost [1]. Limitation: it misses crime that is not reported or not recorded [1], and changes in counting rules or police priorities can change the figures without any change in behaviour [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each point needs a reason; "it is not accurate" alone earns nothing.

**12.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Official statistics are mainly police recorded crime, collected under counting rules [1].
- They have national coverage and allow trends to be tracked [1].
- They are accurate for crimes almost always reported, such as homicide and vehicle theft [1].
- Functionalists tend to treat them as a broadly accurate picture of who offends [1].
- Against: the dark figure means many crimes are unreported or unrecorded [1].
- Victims may not report crimes they see as trivial or private, or because of fear [1].
- Police discretion affects what is recorded [1].
- Changes in counting rules and recording practice change the figures; the UK Statistics Authority withdrew their National Statistics status in 2014 [1].
- Marxists argue they reflect policing of the working class while crimes of the powerful are hidden [1].
- Feminists argue domestic and sexual violence are heavily under-reported [1].
- Victim surveys have generally recorded much more crime than police figures, showing the scale of what is missing [1].
- Judgement: official statistics are accurate for some serious crimes and for showing the work of the police, but not for the overall amount of crime or who commits it [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* Distinguish what statistics are accurate *for*; a blanket "they are inaccurate" judgement is too simple.

**13.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- The dark figure is crime that is unreported or unrecorded [1].
- Victim surveys such as the CSEW ask about crimes experienced, whether or not reported [1].
- They capture much crime that never reaches the police [1].
- They are unaffected by changes in police recording practice [1].
- Self-report studies reveal hidden offending and offenders never caught [1].
- They can test whether official figures accurately show who offends [1].
- Against: victim surveys miss crimes with no direct victim, such as many corporate crimes [1].
- They do not cover homicide or crimes against businesses [1].
- They rely on memory, and sensitive crimes may still be hidden from interviewers [1].
- Self-report respondents may lie, forget or exaggerate, and studies mostly cover minor offences [1].
- Marxists would note that white collar crime remains hidden in all three sources [1].
- Judgement: the alternative sources reduce the dark figure but cannot remove it, so they reduce rather than solve the problem [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* "Solve" is a strong word; weigh what each source reveals against what it still misses.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining the dark figure as unsolved crime.
- Mixing up victim surveys and self-report studies.
- Assuming a rise in recorded crime means more offending.
- Quoting no figures in data questions, or confusing percentages with differences.
- Forgetting that only functionalist, feminist and Marxist perspectives are listed for this sub-section.
- Giving limitations without explaining their effect on the data.
