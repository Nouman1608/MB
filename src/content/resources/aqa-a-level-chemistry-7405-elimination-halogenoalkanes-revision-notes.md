These notes condense section **3.3.3.2 Elimination** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Paper 2 at AS and A-level. Explanations and worked examples are in the [Elimination study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-elimination-halogenoalkanes/), and exam-style questions are in the [Elimination practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-elimination-halogenoalkanes-practice/). The previous sub-topic is in the [Nucleophilic substitution revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-nucleophilic-substitution-revision-notes/).

## What you must be able to do

- Recognise that **substitution and elimination happen at the same time** when a halogenoalkane such as 2-bromopropane reacts with potassium hydroxide.
- **Explain the role** of the reagent as both **nucleophile and base**.
- **Outline the mechanisms** of both reactions.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Elimination | A small molecule (here HBr) is removed from a molecule, leaving a double bond |
| Base | A species that accepts a proton (H⁺) |
| Nucleophile | A species that donates a lone pair to form a new bond to carbon |
| Concurrent | Happening at the same time, from the same reactants |
| Adjacent carbon | A carbon bonded directly to the carbon that carries the halogen |
| Ethanolic | Dissolved in ethanol, with little or no water |

## The two roles of hydroxide

| | Nucleophile | Base |
|---|---|---|
| What it does | Donates a lone pair to the δ+ carbon | Takes H⁺ from an adjacent carbon |
| Reaction type | Substitution | Elimination |
| Product from 2-bromopropane | Propan-2-ol | Propene |
| Other products | Br⁻ | H₂O and Br⁻ |

Both reactions occur together, so a mixture of products forms.

## Equations for 2-bromopropane

```
Substitution:  CH₃CHBrCH₃ + OH⁻ → CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ + Br⁻
Elimination:   CH₃CHBrCH₃ + OH⁻ → CH₃CH=CH₂ + H₂O + Br⁻
```

## Conditions

| For | Reagent | Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Mainly substitution | Aqueous KOH or NaOH | Warm, or heat under reflux |
| Mainly elimination | KOH in ethanol | Heat under reflux |

Always name the **solvent** and the **heating method**. Tertiary halogenoalkanes give more elimination than primary ones.

## Elimination mechanism: three arrows

1. Arrow from the **lone pair on O** of OH⁻ to an **H on an adjacent carbon**.
2. Arrow from that **C–H bond** to the carbon–carbon bond, forming **C=C**.
3. Arrow from the **C–Br bond** to **Br**.

Products: alkene, **H₂O** and **Br⁻**. Draw the dipole on the C–Br bond.

## Substitution mechanism: two arrows

1. Arrow from the **lone pair on O** of OH⁻ to the **δ+ carbon**.
2. Arrow from the **C–Br bond** to **Br**.

Products: alcohol and Br⁻.

## Counting alkene products

Check each carbon next to the C–X carbon. It must have at least one H. Then check each possible alkene for E/Z isomers.

| Halogenoalkane | Alkene products |
|---|---|
| 1-bromopropane | Propene |
| 2-bromopropane | Propene |
| 2-bromobutane | But-1-ene, (E)-but-2-ene, (Z)-but-2-ene |
| 2-bromo-2-methylpropane | 2-methylpropene |
| 3-bromopentane | (E)-pent-2-ene, (Z)-pent-2-ene |
| 2-bromo-3-methylbutane | 3-methylbut-1-ene, 2-methylbut-2-ene |

## Quick calculations

- The amounts of substitution and elimination products add up to the amount of halogenoalkane reacted.
- Moles of gas × 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹ gives volume at room temperature and pressure.
- Percentage yield = actual ÷ theoretical × 100.

Example: 0.200 mol of 2-bromopropane, of which 70 % eliminates, gives 0.140 mol propene, which is 0.140 × 24.0 = 3.36 dm³ at room temperature and pressure.

## Detecting the products

| Test | Alkene (elimination) | Alcohol (substitution) |
|---|---|---|
| Bromine water | Decolourises (orange to colourless) | No change |

## Substitution or elimination: decide quickly

1. Is the solvent water? Expect mainly substitution.
2. Is the solvent ethanol and is it hot? Expect mainly elimination.
3. Is a lone pair attacking carbon? Substitution.
4. Is a lone pair attacking hydrogen? Elimination.

## Worked example to copy

**Question.** 2-bromobutane is heated with ethanolic potassium hydroxide. Give the products of elimination.

1. The carbon bonded to Br is C2. Its neighbours are C1 (CH₃) and C3 (CH₂). Both have hydrogens.
2. Removing H from C1 gives CH₂=CH–CH₂CH₃, which is but-1-ene.
3. Removing H from C3 gives CH₃CH=CHCH₃, which is but-2-ene, with E and Z forms.
4. Each time, the other products are H₂O and Br⁻.

Answer in the exam as three alkenes plus a statement that hydroxide acts as a base.

## Exam traps

- Taking the hydrogen from the carbon that holds the halogen. It must come from an **adjacent** carbon.
- Omitting the arrow from the C–H bond.
- Omitting water from the products.
- Missing E/Z isomers when counting the alkenes from 2-bromobutane.
- Aqueous conditions given for elimination.
- Describing the base as an "electron donor" rather than a **proton acceptor**.
- Confusing this with the acid-catalysed elimination of water from alcohols.
- Giving only one role for hydroxide when asked about both.

## Self-test

1. State two roles of the hydroxide ion in its reactions with halogenoalkanes.
2. Write an equation for the elimination reaction of 2-bromopropane with hydroxide.
3. Give the reagent and conditions that favour elimination.
4. From which atom does the lone pair start in arrow 1 of the elimination mechanism?
5. Where does arrow 3 start and end?
6. How many alkene products can 2-bromobutane give, including stereoisomers?
7. Why does 1-bromopropane give only one alkene?
8. Name the alkene formed from 2-bromo-2-methylpropane.
9. What is observed when bromine water is added to propene?
10. What are the three products of elimination from 2-bromopropane with hydroxide?
11. 0.100 mol of a halogenoalkane reacts, and 35 % undergoes elimination. How many moles of substitution product form?
12. Why does elimination reduce the yield of an alcohol made from a halogenoalkane?

### Answers

1. Nucleophile and base.
2. CH₃CHBrCH₃ + OH⁻ → CH₃CH=CH₂ + H₂O + Br⁻.
3. KOH in ethanol (ethanolic), heat under reflux.
4. The oxygen atom of OH⁻.
5. It starts at the C–Br bond and ends at the bromine atom.
6. Three: but-1-ene, (E)-but-2-ene and (Z)-but-2-ene.
7. It has hydrogens on only one adjacent carbon (C2), and the only alkene possible is propene.
8. 2-methylpropene.
9. The orange bromine water is decolourised.
10. Propene, water and bromide ions.
11. 0.065 mol.
12. Some halogenoalkane reacts to form an alkene instead, so less alcohol forms.
