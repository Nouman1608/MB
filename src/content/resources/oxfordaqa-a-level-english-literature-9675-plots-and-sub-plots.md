The 9675 specification lists "the use of plots and sub-plots" among the aspects of tragedy worth
exploring in Unit 1, alongside "the structural pattern of the text as it moves through complication
to catastrophe". This guide takes that one aspect and applies it to the Section A plays. For the
passage method as a whole, start with the
[Unit 1 Section A study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-english-literature-9675-unit-1-section-a/);
this page assumes you know it and shows how a second plot line gives you the evidence the method's
final step, reaching out to the rest of the play, depends on.

## What counts as a plot and a sub-plot

The **main plot** follows the protagonist's journey towards death. A **sub-plot** is a second line
of action with its own characters and its own small arc, running alongside the main plot and often
joining it before the catastrophe. Three related terms are useful:

- a **parallel** repeats the main plot's pattern in another family or social rank;
- a **foil** is a character whose situation or temperament sets off the protagonist's by contrast;
- a **comic sub-plot** gives low-status characters a version of the main action, often as parody.

Section A plays were written for audiences used to multiple plot lines. Treating the sub-plot as a
distraction from "the real story" misses how the playwright designed the tragedy.

## Five jobs a sub-plot can do

1. **Mirror.** The sub-plot repeats the tragic error in a second case, which suggests the error is
   part of the human condition rather than one man's peculiarity. This links to the specification's
   final aspect: tragedy as "a commentary on the real world".
2. **Contrast or parody.** A low or corrupt version of the main action makes the main action look
   nobler, or exposes it as no better than its imitation.
3. **Complicate.** Characters from the sub-plot become causes in the main plot, so the two lines
   converge and drive the play towards catastrophe.
4. **Widen the damage.** A second family or household suffering shows "how the behaviour of the
   heroes affects the world around them", another listed aspect.
5. **Control pace and feeling.** A shift to another plot line can delay the catastrophe, build
   suspense or release tension before a final blow.

When you write about a sub-plot, name which job it is doing at that moment. Most strong paragraphs
use one or two of these, not all five.

## The five Section A plays

**King Lear.** Gloucester's story runs closely alongside Lear's. Both fathers misjudge which child
loves them; Gloucester believes Edmund's forged letter against Edgar as quickly as Lear believes
Goneril and Regan's flattery. The sub-plot then converges with the main plot: Edmund allies with
the two sisters and becomes the lover of both, and his order brings about Cordelia's death. The
blinding of Gloucester turns Lear's metaphorical blindness into a physical act on stage, and the
"fall" at Dover, staged by Edgar, is a grotesque echo of a king's fall from greatness. Gloucester's
death, reported as a heart breaking between joy and grief, prepares the audience for Lear's.

**Hamlet.** Three sons have fathers to avenge: Hamlet, Laertes after Polonius's death, and young
Fortinbras, whose father died fighting old Hamlet. Laertes acts at once and is ready to cut a
throat in church; Fortinbras leads an army over a patch of land of little worth. Each is a foil
for Hamlet's delay, and Hamlet himself plays on the word when he tells Laertes he will be his
"foil". The Polonius family strand also doubles the madness motif: Ophelia's real madness sits
against Hamlet's performed "antic disposition". At the end Fortinbras inherits Denmark, so the
sub-plot supplies the restored order the main plot cannot.

**Othello.** The play is unusually close to a single plot, and the specification's point that
absence can be significant applies. The Roderigo strand shows Iago's method in miniature: a
gullible suitor drained of money and manipulated into violence, a rehearsal of what is done to
Othello. The Cassio and Bianca strand carries the handkerchief and produces the scene in which
Othello watches Cassio laugh about Bianca and believes he is laughing about Desdemona. With no
separate story to escape into, the audience stays trapped inside the contest of power.

**Doctor Faustus (B text).** The comic scenes parody the main plot. Servants and stable-hands
steal conjuring books and summon devils for trivial ends, and a horse-dealer is cheated by a horse
that turns to a bundle of hay. The B text gives more room to such episodes, including the courtier
Benvolio, mocked with horns by Faustus, who comes back seeking revenge. The parody works both ways:
it shows magic as cheap trickery and makes Faustus's great bargain look like the same trickery on
a larger scale, which sharpens the pathos of his last hour.

**The Duchess of Malfi.** Julia, the Cardinal's mistress, is set against the Duchess. Both women
choose their own partners in secret; the Duchess's marriage to Antonio is presented with warmth,
while Julia's affair is mercenary. Both die at the hands of the brothers' corruption, Julia by a
poisoned Bible the Cardinal makes her kiss. The contrast tests whether the brothers' horror at
female desire has any moral basis, and the echo of one woman's death in another's widens the
tragedy into a portrait of a corrupt court.

## Using a sub-plot in a Section A answer

The passage method asks you to link the printed extract to at least two other moments. A sub-plot
gives you ready-made links.

- **If the passage comes from the main plot,** ask which sub-plot moment mirrors or contrasts with
  it. A passage of Lear's rage at Goneril can be linked to Gloucester's anger at Edgar.
- **If the passage comes from the sub-plot,** argue what it adds to the main tragedy. Do not write
  as if the sub-plot were a separate play; show the convergence.
- **If the passage brings both plots together,** as many late scenes do, comment on the timing: why
  does the convergence happen here, so close to the catastrophe?

## Worked example on an original passage

The passage below is **original**, written for this guide. It imagines a servants' scene in an
invented tragedy in which a lord has disowned his loyal daughter for a flattering one.

> **COOK:** He turned the old dog out tonight, the one
> That kept the gate for twelve years. Said it growled.
> **GROOM:** And so it did. It growled at thieves.
> **COOK:** The new hound licks his hand and steals the meat.
> **GROOM:** Then we shall all grow thin together, cook,
> And praise the hound for keeping us so lean.

**Model paragraph:**

> Placed among the servants, the passage retells the main plot as a fable that the lord himself
> cannot hear. The "old dog" turned out for growling stands for the honest daughter whose plain
> speech was taken for disloyalty, and the Groom's correction, "It growled at thieves", supplies the
> judgement the lord fails to make. The comic tone is bitter rather than relieving: the "new hound"
> that "licks his hand and steals the meat" reduces flattery to animal greed, and the final irony,
> that the household will "praise the hound for keeping us so lean", shows the lord's error spreading
> to everyone who depends on him. The sub-plot widens the damage of the tragic flaw from one family
> to the whole house, and because the servants see clearly what their master does not, the audience
> is placed with them, watching the catastrophe approach.

The paragraph names two jobs the sub-plot does (mirror and widening the damage), analyses method
(animal imagery, irony, the shift into a low-status voice) and ends on the audience.

## Common mistakes

- **Retelling the sub-plot.** Plot summary earns little; argue what the parallel shows.
- **Treating parallels as identical.** The difference is usually the point: Gloucester despairs
  where Lear rages; Laertes acts where Hamlet thinks.
- **Dismissing comic scenes as relief.** In Doctor Faustus the comedy comments on the main action.
- **Forgetting convergence.** Most sub-plots become causes. Say how and when.
- **Ignoring absence.** If your play has little sub-plot, that concentration is itself analysable.

## Exam technique

Section A questions use the passage as a starting point and often ask you to **explore** the
**significance** of an aspect. If the question names the villain, the hero's isolation or the
movement towards catastrophe, a sub-plot can still be your second or third link; you do not need a
question about structure to use it. Prepare, from memory, one mirror moment, one contrast moment and
one convergence moment for your play, each with a short quotation. Keep the sub-plot to a paragraph
or two in a 25-mark answer: the printed passage must stay at the centre.
