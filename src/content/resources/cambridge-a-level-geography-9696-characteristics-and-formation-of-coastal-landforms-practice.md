## Section A

**1.** Define the term "tombolo". **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between a concordant and a discordant coast. **[2]**

**3.** State two characteristics of a wave-cut platform. **[2]**

**4.** Outline the difference between a storm beach profile and a swell beach profile. **[4]**

**5.** Two beach profiles were surveyed on the fictional Westray coast. At Site P (shingle) the beach rises 6.0 m over a horizontal distance of 40 m. At Site Q (fine sand) it rises 3.0 m over 150 m.

**(a)** Calculate the mean gradient of each beach as a ratio of rise to distance. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why Site P is steeper than Site Q. **[3]**

**6.** Outline how dune slacks form. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain how rock structure influences the profile of a cliff. **[6]**

**8.** The fictional Kerran Bay faces south-west. Waves mostly arrive from the south-west after refraction around two headlands. At the eastern end of the coast, beyond Kerran Point, the coastline turns sharply north into the estuary of the River Lyle, where a 3 km sand and shingle ridge with four hooked ridges on its landward side extends across the estuary mouth.

**(a)** Suggest whether Kerran Bay beach is swash-aligned or drift-aligned, giving a reason. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain the formation of the ridge across the Lyle estuary. **[5]**

**(c)** Suggest why saltmarsh has developed behind the ridge. **[3]**

**9.** Explain how vegetation helps a saltmarsh to develop on a mudflat. **[5]**

**10.** Explain why barrier islands migrate landward when sea level rises. **[4]**

**11.** Describe and explain the landforms produced by a fall in relative sea level. **[6]**

**12.** "The characteristics of coastal landforms are determined more by geology than by wave processes." How far do you agree? **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** A ridge of sand or shingle [1] that links an island to the mainland [1]. **[2]**

**2.** On a concordant coast rock bands run parallel to the coastline [1], whereas on a discordant coast they meet it at right angles, giving headlands and bays [1]. **[2]**

**3.** A gentle seaward slope, usually under about 4° [1]. Widest and best developed in the intertidal zone [1]. Also credit rock pools, ridges or grooves following joints and harder beds, or little sediment cover on exposed coasts, in place of either point. **[2]**

**4.** A storm profile forms under high energy waves where backwash exceeds swash [1], so sediment moves offshore and the upper beach is lowered and flattened, often with offshore bars [1]. A swell profile forms under low energy waves where swash exceeds backwash [1], so sediment moves onshore, building berms and a steeper upper beach [1]. **[4]**

**5. (a)** Site P: 6.0 / 40 = 0.15, or 1 in 6.7 [1]. Site Q: 3.0 / 150 = 0.02, or 1 in 50 [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Swash percolates into the large pore spaces of shingle [1], so backwash is weak and moves little material back down [1]. Fine sand stays saturated, so backwash runs over the surface and carries sediment down, flattening the beach [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Wind erodes sand from between dune ridges by deflation [1]. Erosion continues down until it reaches the water table, where damp sand resists further removal [1]. The damp hollow is colonised by plants tolerant of wet ground, forming a slack [1]. **[3]**

**7.** Where joints are vertical and beds horizontal, blocks fall cleanly and the cliff is near-vertical [1]. Where beds dip seaward, blocks slide down bedding planes [1], so the face follows the angle of dip and is less steep but unstable [1]. Where beds dip landward, blocks are tilted back into the cliff, giving a steep, stable face [1]. Weak, unconsolidated rock such as till slumps, giving a low-angled, terraced profile [1]. Faults and joint density control where caves and notches form, making the face irregular [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** Swash-aligned [1], because waves arrive almost parallel to the shore after refraction around the headlands, so there is little net drift along the bay [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** The ridge is a compound spit [1]. Longshore drift carries sediment east past Kerran Point [1]. Where the coast turns north, drift continues in the same direction and sediment is deposited across the estuary mouth [1]. Waves refracting around the tip, or a secondary wave direction, curve the end landward into a recurve [1]. Each of the four hooked ridges marks an earlier position of the tip as the spit grew [1]. **[5]**

**(c)** The spit shelters the water behind it from waves [1], so currents are slow and fine sediment settles at slack tide, helped by flocculation in brackish water [1]. Mudflats build up and are colonised by salt-tolerant plants that trap more sediment [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Pioneer plants such as *Salicornia* or *Spartina* colonise the mudflat once it is exposed long enough between tides [1]. Their stems slow tidal flow so more sediment settles [1], and roots bind the deposited mud [1]. The surface rises, so it is flooded less often and less salt-tolerant species can grow, forming low then high marsh [1]. Organic matter from the plants adds to the marsh surface and creeks become defined as drainage concentrates [1]. **[5]**

**10.** Rising sea level allows storm waves to reach the barrier crest more often [1]. Waves overtop the crest and carry sediment into the lagoon as overwash deposits [1]. Sediment is removed from the seaward face and added to the landward side [1], so the barrier "rolls over" landward while keeping its form [1]. **[4]**

**11.** A fall in relative sea level is often caused by isostatic uplift after an ice sheet melts [1]. Former beaches are left above present wave reach as raised beaches [1], often with rounded beach pebbles and shells inland of today's shore [1]. Behind them, the old cliff line survives as a relict cliff, sometimes with caves and arches [1]. Former wave-cut platforms are left as flat terraces [1]. Because waves no longer reach them, these features are degraded only by sub-aerial weathering and slope processes [1]. **[6]**

**12.** The real exam marks this essay with levels of response, not point by point. The points below are indicative creditworthy content.

*Introduction:* geology means rock type, strength and structure [1]; wave processes means energy, refraction, swash, backwash and drift [1].

*Geology as dominant:* rock strength controls cliff steepness, from vertical limestone to slumped till [1]. Dip of beds controls cliff profiles [1]. Joints and faults guide where caves, arches and stacks form [1]. Concordant and discordant structures set the headland-and-bay pattern, as at Lulworth Cove and Swanage in Dorset [1]. Rock type supplies sediment, so it influences beach material [1].

*Waves as dominant:* depositional landforms such as spits, tombolos and barriers are shaped by wave direction and drift [1]. Beach profiles change with storm and swell conditions on the same geology [1]. Refraction concentrates energy on headlands whatever the rock [1]. Exposure and fetch explain why the same rock gives different landforms on exposed and sheltered coasts [1].

*Other factors:* sea level change produced rias, fjords and raised beaches [1]. Vegetation builds dunes, saltmarshes and mangroves [1]. Sediment supply, often inherited from post-glacial rise, controls barrier and spit growth [1].

*Interaction:* geology sets the resistance, waves set the attack, so erosional forms reflect both [1]. Sub-aerial processes depend on geology and climate [1].

*Judgement:* geology dominates many erosional landforms [1]; wave processes and sediment supply dominate depositional ones [1]; sea level change explains large-scale forms [1]. A reasoned final view on "how far", supported by named examples [1]. **[20]**
