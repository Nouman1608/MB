**These notes cover the (a) question in Paper 2 Drama.** The syllabus says there is "a choice of two
questions on each text: either (a) a passage-based question or (b) an essay question", that
candidates "must answer one passage-based question and one essay question", and that "Relevant
passages are printed on the question paper." For the full method see the
[passage-based question study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-english-literature-0475-drama-passage-based-question/);
for the paper's format see the [Paper 2 Drama revision notes](/resources/igcse-english-literature-paper-2-drama-revision-notes/).

## The task in one paragraph

You are given a printed extract from one of your set plays and a question with a focus ("dramatic",
"revealing", "a character's feelings"). You write a 25-mark answer that analyses how the playwright
creates that effect **in this extract**, using the wider play only to explain why the moment matters.
You answer one passage question and one essay question on the paper, on different plays.

## Placing the extract: three questions

1. What has the audience just seen?
2. What does the audience know that a character does not, or the reverse?
3. What kind of moment is this: a turning point, a climax, a calm before a crisis, a consequence?

Two sentences in the introduction are enough.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning | What to say about it |
|---|---|---|
| Stage direction | The playwright's instruction for action, movement, sound or light | Treat it as method: what the audience sees or hears and why |
| Dramatic irony | The audience knows more than a character | It creates tension, sympathy or comedy as the character speaks in ignorance |
| Aside | A remark to the audience unheard by others on stage | It reveals private thoughts and often sets up irony |
| Soliloquy | A speech alone on stage revealing thoughts | It gives the audience access to motive and doubt |
| Entrance / exit | Arrival or departure of a character | It can interrupt, shift power or release tension |
| Stichomythia | Rapid exchange of single lines | It shows conflict, wit or rising tension |
| Pause / silence | A marked gap in speech | It shows what cannot be said |
| Prop | An object on stage | It can become a symbol or evidence |
| Status | A character's power within the scene | Watch who gives orders, interrupts or is ignored |
| Verse / prose shift | A change between verse and prose in plays that use both | It can mark a loss of control or change of status |
| Tableau | Characters frozen in position | It fixes an image for the audience at a key moment |

## The extract's shape

- Split the extract into two or three stages.
- Mark the **turning point**: an entrance, a revelation, a look, a line that changes the mood.
- Read the **last lines** twice. They often hold the reversal or the image the question points to.
- Write in the order of the extract when it develops; group by method only if it does not.

## The analytical sentence for drama

**Point** (answer to the question) → **quotation** of dialogue or stage direction → **method**
(irony, exit, pause, short lines) → **effect on the audience** → **link** to the key word.

Example: "Elias 'closes the book slowly', and the deliberate action tells the audience that he has
understood; the silence that follows is more threatening than any accusation."

## What higher-level answers do

- Analyse stage directions as closely as speech.
- Refer to the **audience**, not the reader.
- Explain dramatic irony precisely: who knows what, and when.
- Follow the extract to its end.
- Show how the moment fits the play without retelling it.
- Make a personal judgement on the key word, supported by evidence.

## Exam traps

- **A character study.** Writing about a character across the whole play instead of the extract.
- **Ignoring the stage.** Analysing the dialogue as if it were a poem, with no sense of performance.
- **"The reader feels…".** Plays have audiences; write accordingly.
- **Plot introduction.** A paragraph of story before any analysis.
- **Half the extract.** Missing the final lines, where the shift usually comes.
- **Overrunning.** The essay question on your other play is worth the same 25 marks.

## Self-test

1. How many passage-based questions must you answer on Paper 2?
2. Where does the extract come from, and where is it printed?
3. What should the first two sentences of a passage answer do?
4. Define dramatic irony.
5. Why should stage directions be quoted and analysed?
6. What is stichomythia, and what can it suggest?
7. What is the difference between an aside and a soliloquy?
8. Why are the last lines of an extract especially important?
9. Give two things an entrance can do to a scene.
10. What does a shift from verse into prose sometimes signal?
11. A candidate writes "The reader is shocked." What should be changed, and why?
12. When should an answer be organised by method rather than by the order of the extract?

**Answers:** 1. One, together with one essay question, on different plays. 2. From one of your set
plays; it is printed on the question paper. 3. Place the extract in the play and answer the question
directly. 4. When the audience knows something a character on stage does not. 5. They are the
playwright's methods for shaping what the audience sees and hears, so they are part of the text's
meaning. 6. A rapid exchange of single lines; it can suggest conflict, rising tension or wit. 7. An
aside is a brief remark to the audience while others are on stage; a soliloquy is a speech by a
character alone on stage. 8. They often contain the turning point, an exit or a final image that
changes the meaning. 9. Any two: interrupt the tension; shift the balance of power; bring news that
changes the situation; create dramatic irony. 10. A loss of control or a change in status or mood.
11. Replace "reader" with "audience", because a play is written to be performed and watched. 12. When
the extract does not develop in clear stages, so grouping by method gives a clearer argument.
