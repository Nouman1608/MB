These questions practise key question 3.3 of Cambridge O Level Sociology (2251), *How has
globalisation affected inequalities between societies?* Section A follows the six-part (a)–(f) shape
the syllabus describes for Paper 1 Question 3. Section B gives extra practice on migration, ecological
issues and explanations.

---

## Section A — Global inequality question

**(a)** Define the terms:
- (i) *urbanisation* **[2]**
- (ii) *transnational company* **[2]**

**(b)** Give **two** examples of global ecological issues. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **three** ways migration may benefit receiving societies. **[6]**

**(d)** Explain **three** ways transnational companies may harm poorer societies. **[6]**

**(e)** Discuss the Marxist explanation of global inequality. **[8]**

**(f)** Evaluate the view that colonialism is the main cause of inequalities between societies.
**[14]**

## Section B — Migration, ecology and explanations

**(g)** Explain the difference between immigration and emigration, using an example. **[4]**

**(h)** Explain **two** ways remittances may affect sending societies. **[4]**

**(i)** Explain the feminist explanation of global inequality. **[4]**

**(j)** Explain **two** reasons why the poorest people may be unable to migrate abroad. **[4]**

**(k)** Explain **two** ways climate change may act as a push factor for migration. **[4]**

## Answers

**(a)(i) [2]** The growth in the proportion of a country's population living in towns and cities (1),
often driven by migration from rural areas in search of work (1).

**(a)(ii) [2]** A business that owns or controls production or services in more than one country (1),
often with headquarters in a richer country and factories or suppliers in poorer ones (1).

**(b) [2]** Any two (1 each): climate change; pollution; rapid urbanisation; environmental damage by
transnational companies; deforestation; rising sea levels.

**(c) [6]** Three ways, 2 marks each:
- Migrants fill labour shortages in sectors such as health, care, construction and agriculture,
  keeping services running (2).
- Working migrants pay taxes and contribute to the economy, and some start businesses that create
  jobs (2).
- Cultural diversity increases through new food, music, languages and ideas, enriching social life
  (2).
- Young migrant workers can offset an ageing population, supporting pensions and care (2).

**(d) [6]** Three ways, 2 marks each:
- **Low wages and poor conditions:** TNCs may locate where labour is cheap and regulation weak, keeping
  workers in poverty (2).
- **Environmental damage:** industrial accidents and pollution, such as the Bhopal gas leak in India
  (1984) or oil spills in the Niger Delta, harm local health and livelihoods (2).
- **Profits flow out:** profits go to shareholders abroad rather than being invested locally (2).
- **Power over governments:** the threat of moving elsewhere lets TNCs win tax breaks and weaker
  regulation (2).

**(e) [8]** Levels: 7–8 developed points with evidence and evaluation; 4–6 some development; 1–3
basic points.
- Global inequality results from capitalism operating on a world scale; rich countries and companies
  profit from poorer ones.
- **Dependency theory (Frank):** rich "metropolis" nations developed by exploiting "satellite"
  nations, the "development of underdevelopment".
- **World-systems theory (Wallerstein):** core states gain from the cheap labour and raw materials of
  the periphery; the semi-periphery sits in between.
- TNCs extract profits; trade rules and debt favour richer states.
- Poorer countries are kept as suppliers of raw materials and cheap goods.
- **Evaluation:** helps explain persistent gaps and the role of TNCs; but some countries have moved
  from periphery towards core through industrialisation, suggesting the system is not fixed;
  ignores gender (feminists) and internal factors such as conflict or corruption.

**(f) [14]** Levels: 11–14 balanced, developed, evaluated with a supported conclusion; 7–10 developed
but less balanced; 4–6 limited; 1–3 basic.

*For colonialism:*
- Colonial economies were organised to export raw materials and cash crops, not to develop industry.
- Wealth and resources were transferred to colonising countries.
- Borders drawn by colonial powers, as at the Berlin Conference (1884–1885), contributed to later
  conflict.
- Trade patterns, debts and institutions from the colonial period persist after independence.

*Against or alternatives:*
- **Capitalist exploitation today:** Marxists argue TNCs and global trade rules keep the gap open
  regardless of colonial history.
- **Climate and ecological issues:** poorer societies suffer most from climate change and pollution.
- **Patriarchy:** feminists argue women's unpaid and low-paid labour holds back development.
- **Migration:** brain drain removes skilled workers from poorer societies.
- Some former colonies have grown rapidly, while some countries that were never colonised remain
  poor, suggesting colonialism is not the only cause.

*Conclusion:* colonialism laid foundations for global inequality, but present-day capitalism,
ecological issues and patriarchy keep it going, so it is an important but not the sole cause.

**(g) [4]** Immigration means moving into a country to live (1); emigration means leaving one's own
country to live elsewhere (1). Example: a nurse who leaves the Philippines to work in Britain is an
emigrant from the Philippines (1) and an immigrant to Britain (1).

**(h) [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each:
- Remittances raise family incomes, paying for food, housing, school fees and healthcare (2).
- They bring foreign currency into the national economy and may fund small businesses (2).
- Families may become dependent on remittances, and households that receive them can pull ahead of
  those that do not, widening inequality within the sending society (2).

**(i) [4]** Feminists argue global inequality is shaped by patriarchy as well as class (1). Women do
much unpaid farming and domestic work but own a small share of land and property (1). Export factories
often employ young women at low wages (1). Hochschild's global care chains show women from poorer
countries caring for families in richer ones, while their own children are cared for by others (1).

**(j) [4]** Two reasons, 2 marks each:
- International migration costs money for travel, documents and fees for agents, which the poorest
  cannot afford (2).
- Visa rules often favour skilled or educated applicants (2).
- The poorest may lack the contacts abroad that help migrants find work and housing (2).

**(k) [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each:
- Rising sea levels and flooding make homes in low-lying areas uninhabitable, as threatened in Tuvalu
  and the Maldives (2).
- Drought and failed harvests destroy farming livelihoods, pushing people to cities or abroad (2).
- More frequent extreme weather destroys infrastructure and jobs (2).

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to name Frank or Wallerstein?** Naming them adds precision in part (e), but the mark
depends on explaining how exploitation creates the gap.

**What counts as an example of migration?** Any accurate case: labour migration, refugees fleeing
conflict, or rural people moving abroad after environmental disaster.
