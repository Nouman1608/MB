## Questions

**1.** Define the term *capacity utilisation*. **[2]**

**2.** Pilcombe Dairy can process 90,000 litres of milk a month. Last month it processed 63,000 litres.

**(a)** Calculate its capacity utilisation. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate how many more litres it must process to reach 85% utilisation. **[2]**

**3.** Ravensfold Tiles has a maximum output of 40,000 tiles a year. Fixed costs are 240,000 a year and variable costs are 12 a tile. Calculate the cost per tile when it operates at:

**(a)** 50% capacity utilisation **[3]**
**(b)** 90% capacity utilisation. **[3]**

**4.** State **two** methods a business could use to improve a low level of capacity utilisation. **[2]**

**5.** Using your answers to question 3, explain **one** impact on Ravensfold Tiles of operating at 50% rather than 90% of maximum capacity. **[4]**

**6.** In December Pilcombe Dairy received a rush of orders for cream and processed 99,000 litres, against a normal maximum of 90,000. To do this it ran extra night shifts and postponed machine servicing. Analyse **two** problems this may cause. **[6]**

**7.** Define the term *outsourcing* and identify **one** activity a bakery could outsource. **[2]**

**8.** Wyndmoor Printing delivers its finished orders using its own van. The in-house service has fixed costs of 60,000 a year and variable costs of 1.10 a delivery. A courier firm offers to make deliveries for 1.90 each. Wyndmoor expects 68,000 deliveries next year.

**(a)** Calculate the number of deliveries at which the two options cost the same. **[3]**
**(b)** Advise Wyndmoor whether to outsource, referring to the cost figures and **one** other factor. **[3]**

**9.** Ravensfold Tiles is considering outsourcing its glazing process to a specialist firm. Analyse **two** possible impacts on Ravensfold Tiles. **[6]**

**10.** Holmbury Textiles can produce 48,000 garments a year and produced 33,600 last year. Each garment sells for 30 and has a variable cost of 14. Management is considering two options.

Option A: spend 55,000 on a marketing campaign aimed at export markets, which is forecast to raise output to 40,800 garments a year.

Option B: close one production line, cutting maximum capacity to 36,000 garments a year, saving 90,000 of fixed costs each year at a one-off redundancy cost of 40,000.

Evaluate which option Holmbury Textiles should choose. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** The proportion (or percentage) of a business's maximum possible output [1] that it actually produces in a given period [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* a definition that says only "how much a business produces" gives the actual output but leaves out the comparison with the maximum, which is the idea the term depends on.

**2. (a)** Utilisation = actual ÷ maximum × 100 [1] = 63,000 ÷ 90,000 × 100 = **70%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Required output = 0.85 × 90,000 = 76,500 litres [1]; extra = 76,500 − 63,000 = **13,500 litres** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* in part (b) answering 76,500 stops one step short, because the question asks for the increase. Re-read the last words of a calculation question before you box an answer.

**3. (a)** Output = 0.5 × 40,000 = 20,000 tiles [1]. Total cost = 240,000 + (12 × 20,000) = 480,000 [1]. Cost per tile = 480,000 ÷ 20,000 = **24.00** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Output = 0.9 × 40,000 = 36,000 tiles [1]. Total cost = 240,000 + (12 × 36,000) = 672,000 [1]. Cost per tile = 672,000 ÷ 36,000 = **18.67** (to 2 decimal places) [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* write the output at each utilisation level first. Dividing fixed costs by the maximum output, or using the percentage as the output, are common slips that a visible working line makes easy to spot.

**4.** Any two methods, one mark each [2]. Acceptable methods include: run a promotion or price offer to raise demand; enter new markets or launch new products; take on contract work for other businesses; reduce capacity, for example by closing a line or selling or renting out spare assets. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "state" asks for short, separate methods. Two versions of the same idea, such as "advertise" and "promote", may count as only one method.

**5.** At 50% the cost per tile is 24.00 against 18.67 at 90%, a difference of 5.33 [1]. The reason is that fixed costs of 240,000 are shared over only 20,000 tiles rather than 36,000 [1]. This means the firm earns less profit on each tile it sells at any given price [1], so it may need to raise its price, which could reduce sales further, or accept lower profit [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* a strong "explain" answer uses the figures from the stem, then follows a chain from cause to effect. Writing only "costs are higher" restates the data rather than explaining it.

**6.** Candidates may choose any two well-developed problems. Example answer.
Problem 1, higher costs: night shifts usually mean overtime or shift premiums [1], and output beyond the normal maximum (110%, from 99,000 ÷ 90,000) strains the machines [1], so unit costs may rise even though fixed costs are shared over more litres [1].
Problem 2, breakdown and quality risk: postponing servicing makes breakdowns more likely [1], and a breakdown at peak season could stop all output and delay orders [1], which could damage the dairy's reputation with customers who need cream for the holiday trade [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* "analyse" needs each problem developed in steps, not listed. Linking the problem to the stem (night shifts, postponed servicing, December orders) is what separates application from general knowledge.

**7.** Outsourcing is using another business to carry out an activity or process [1] that the firm could do itself. Example activity: delivery of loaves, packaging, payroll or equipment maintenance [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "identify" asks only for an example; any sensible activity for a bakery fits. Writing a paragraph on advantages wastes time on a two-mark question.

**8. (a)** Break-even volume = fixed cost ÷ (outsourced price − in-house variable cost) [1] = 60,000 ÷ (1.90 − 1.10) [1] = **75,000 deliveries** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** At 68,000 deliveries in-house cost = 60,000 + (1.10 × 68,000) = 134,800 [1]; the courier would cost 1.90 × 68,000 = 129,200, which is 5,600 less, so outsourcing is cheaper at the expected volume [1]. Other factor, for example: the margin is small and expected volume is below 75,000 by only 7,000, so if demand grows the van becomes cheaper, and a courier may also be less reliable for urgent print orders; recommend outsourcing for now but review as volume changes [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "advise" needs a recommendation that is supported by the numbers. A conclusion with no figure behind it, or figures with no conclusion, each leave part of the question unanswered.

**9.** Example answer, with any two well-developed impacts.
Lower or more predictable costs: a specialist glazing firm may work at greater scale and Ravensfold avoids buying and maintaining glazing kilns [1]. This could cut its unit costs and free money for other uses [1], although the contractor's price must still be compared with in-house cost [1].
Less control over quality: the finish of a glaze strongly affects tile appearance [1], so poor work by the contractor would produce faulty batches that Ravensfold's customers blame on Ravensfold [1], damaging its reputation and requiring extra inspection costs [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* give impacts on the business, such as cost, control or reputation, rather than "it is good" or "it is bad". Both a positive and a negative impact are acceptable choices, so long as each is explained.

**10.** Option A: utilisation = 40,800 ÷ 48,000 × 100 = 85% [1]. Extra output 7,200 garments × contribution 16 = 115,200; less campaign 55,000 gives a gain of 60,200 [1] [1]. This depends on the forecast being met [1].
Option B: utilisation = 33,600 ÷ 36,000 × 100 = 93.3% [1]. Savings of 90,000 a year less 40,000 once give 50,000 in year one and 90,000 a year afterwards [1]. Risks: the ceiling falls to 36,000, leaving little room for growth or a surge [1], and redundancies may harm morale [1].
Judgement: choose A, because it earns more in year one (60,200 against 50,000), keeps capacity for growth and does not depend on cuts [1]; but only if market research supports the forecast, since B's saving is far more certain, and B becomes the better choice if the export response proves weak [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* "evaluate" is rewarded for weighing both options and then reaching a decision with a reason. A strong conclusion states the condition that would change the decision. A calculation without a final choice, or a choice without figures, is only part of an answer.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing in the wrong order, or giving 0.7 instead of 70%.
- Stopping at the target output when the question asks for the extra output needed.
- Treating a percentage as a number of units when working out total cost.
- Describing "higher costs" without saying that fixed costs are shared over fewer units.
- Treating operating above maximum capacity as purely good news.
- Listing outsourcing benefits and drawbacks with no link to the business in the case.
- Declaring outsourcing cheaper without comparing costs at the expected volume.
- Giving a recommendation or judgement that does not use the figures or does not answer the question set.
