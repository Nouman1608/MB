## What the specification asks for

Students should be able to:

- "discuss the human and financial cost of these non-communicable diseases to an individual, a local community, a nation or globally";
- "explain the effect of lifestyle factors including diet, alcohol and smoking on the incidence of non-communicable diseases at local, national and global levels."

The content then states that "risk factors are linked to an increased rate of a disease", that they can be "aspects of a person's lifestyle" or "substances in the person's body or environment", and that "a causal mechanism has been proven for some risk factors, but not in others." Six named effects follow, and "many diseases are caused by the interaction of a number of factors." Data skills include sampling, translating between graphs and numbers, and using a scatter diagram to identify a correlation (WS 1.5, MS 2c, 2d, 2g, 4a).

## Risk factors: what the words mean

A **risk factor** raises the chance, or rate, of a disease. It does not guarantee the disease. Two people with the same risk factor may have different outcomes because:

- many diseases result from **several factors interacting** (for example diet, smoking, exercise and genes together for cardiovascular disease);
- people differ in their genes and their other habits.

Risk factors come in two kinds:

| Kind | Examples |
|---|---|
| Aspect of lifestyle | Smoking, diet, alcohol, lack of exercise |
| Substance in the body or environment | High blood cholesterol, carcinogens, ionising radiation |

## The six named effects, explained

**1. Diet, smoking and exercise and cardiovascular disease.** A diet high in saturated fat raises blood cholesterol, so fatty material is deposited in artery walls, narrowing them. Chemicals in tobacco smoke damage the lining of arteries, making deposits more likely. Regular exercise helps control body mass and blood cholesterol, which lowers the risk.

**2. Obesity and Type 2 diabetes.** In Type 2 diabetes the body cells no longer respond properly to insulin, so blood glucose is not controlled. Obesity is a strong risk factor.

**3. Alcohol and the liver and brain.** The liver breaks down alcohol; long-term heavy drinking damages liver cells. Alcohol also affects brain function, slowing reactions and impairing judgement, and long-term heavy use can damage the brain.

**4. Smoking and lung disease and lung cancer.** Tar in smoke damages the airways and alveoli, leading to lung disease, and contains **carcinogens**, which can cause lung cancer.

**5. Smoking and alcohol and unborn babies.** Carbon monoxide from smoke reduces the oxygen the mother's blood carries, so less reaches the fetus, which is linked to low birth weight and premature birth. Alcohol passes to the fetus and can harm its development, including the brain.

**6. Carcinogens, including ionising radiation, and cancer.** Carcinogens damage the DNA in cells, which can make cells divide uncontrollably and form tumours.

## From correlation to causal mechanism

Data showing that a factor and a disease rise together is a **correlation**. Scientists accept that the factor **causes** the disease when they also have:

- a **mechanism**: an explanation of how the factor damages cells or organs (for example carcinogens damaging DNA);
- a **dose-response pattern**: more exposure gives a higher rate of disease;
- **consistent results** from many large, independent, well-sampled studies.

### Worked example 1: a dose-response pattern

A study (invented data) followed adults for 20 years and recorded new cases of a lung disease.

| Cigarettes per day | People | New cases | Rate per 100 000 |
|---|---|---|---|
| None | 20 000 | 30 | 150 |
| 1 to 10 | 8 000 | 48 | 600 |
| 11 to 20 | 6 000 | 66 | 1100 |
| More than 20 | 4 000 | 60 | 1500 |

```
Rate per 100 000 = cases / people x 100 000
e.g. 48 / 8 000 x 100 000 = 600
Risk compared with non-smokers for more than 20 a day = 1500 / 150 = 10 times
```

The rate rises with every increase in smoking, a dose-response pattern. Note that the heaviest-smoking group had fewer cases (60) than the 11 to 20 group (66), yet the higher rate, because it contained fewer people. Always compare rates.

## Lifestyle and incidence at different levels

To "explain the effect of lifestyle factors ... on the incidence" of disease, give the factor, the change in the population, and the effect on the number of new cases.

- **Local**: an area where smoking is more common will tend to have a higher incidence of lung cancer than an area where it is rare.
- **National**: if obesity becomes more common across a country, more people develop Type 2 diabetes.
- **Global**: as diets, alcohol use and smoking change around the world, non-communicable diseases become more common in countries where they were once rare.

### Worked example 2: from a rate to a number

A city of 400 000 people has a Type 2 diabetes incidence of 150 per 100 000 per year.

```
Expected new cases = 400 000 / 100 000 x 150 = 600 per year
```

If a healthy-eating campaign reduced obesity, the incidence and so the number of new cases would be expected to fall.

## Discussing human and financial costs

"Discuss" means give several points, at more than one level, covering both kinds of cost.

### Annotated model answer

*Question: Discuss the human and financial costs of Type 2 diabetes.*

> For the individual, the human cost is poorer quality of life, with the stress of managing the condition and the risk of complications, while the financial cost may be lost earnings if they cannot work (**individual: human and financial**). In the local community, families may become carers and local health services face more patients (**local community**). For a nation, treating many patients costs a great deal through the health service, and illness reduces the number of people working, lowering productivity (**nation**). Globally, as obesity rises in more countries, more health spending is needed worldwide, which is especially hard for countries with limited budgets (**global**).

## Exam technique

- **Explain** a named effect: factor → what it does in the body → disease.
- **Interpret data**: calculate rates, describe the trend with figures, and state whether it shows correlation or cause.
- **Evaluate a study**: sample size, representativeness, random selection, repetition, other factors that were or were not controlled.
- **Discuss costs**: human and financial, at two or more levels.

## Common errors

- Saying a risk factor "causes" a disease when the data only show a correlation.
- Comparing numbers of cases in groups of different sizes.
- Linking obesity with Type 1 instead of Type 2 diabetes.
- Writing "smoking damages the lungs" without naming tar, carcinogens or lung cancer.
- Giving only financial costs in a "discuss" question.
- Forgetting that many diseases have several interacting causes.
