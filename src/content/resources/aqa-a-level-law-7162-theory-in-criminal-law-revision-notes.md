Condensed notes for the section 3.2 row **Theory in criminal law**: harm as the basis for criminalising conduct; autonomy, fault and individual responsibility; and the principles of fair labelling, correspondence, maximum certainty and no retrospective liability. For explanation and a worked evaluation, use the [theory in criminal law study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-7162-theory-in-criminal-law/).

## Harm

- **Mill, *On Liberty* (1859):** power may rightfully be exercised over a person against their will only to prevent harm to others.
- **Kinds of harm protected:** death and injury; psychiatric injury (*Chan-Fook*; *Burstow*); fear of violence (assault, *Ireland*); property (theft, robbery); risk of harm (attempt).
- **Rival justifications:** paternalism (protecting people from themselves); moralism (Devlin v Hart); offence to others.

**Consent and harm**

| Case | Outcome | Theory point |
|---|---|---|
| *R v Brown* (1993) | Consent no defence to ABH or worse in sado-masochism | Moralism or paternalism over autonomy; "cult of violence" (Lord Templeman) |
| *R v Wilson* (1996) | Consensual branding between spouses not criminal | Autonomy and privacy respected |
| *Laskey, Jaggard and Brown v UK* (1997) | No breach of Article 8 | State may regulate harm even in private |

## Autonomy, fault and responsibility

**Autonomy:** freedom to choose, and responsibility for choices.

| Rule | How it reflects autonomy |
|---|---|
| Voluntariness; automatism | No choice, no liability |
| No general duty to act | Respects choice not to intervene |
| *R v Kennedy (No 2)* (2007) | Victim's free, deliberate, informed act breaks the chain |
| Duress; *Hasan* (2005) | Threats overbear choice, but voluntary association with violent criminals defeats the defence |

**Fault:** more serious offences require more fault.
- *R v G* (2003) overruled objective *Caldwell* recklessness; *Elliott v C* (1983) shows why.
- *Sweet v Parsley* (1970); *B v DPP* (2000): presumption of mens rea.
- Strict liability as the exception: *Harrow LBC v Shah* (1999).

**Individual responsibility:** answer for your own conduct (*Kennedy*); transferred malice as a limited exception.

## The four principles

| Principle | Meaning | Complies | Breached or strained |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fair labelling | Name of offence describes the wrong | s18 "with intent"; robbery distinguished from theft | Murder covers mercy and contract killings; manslaughter's huge range; s47 and s20 share a maximum |
| Correspondence | Mens rea matches actus reus | Assault; battery; s18 | s47 (*Savage*; *Roberts*); s20 (*Mowatt*); murder via GBH intent (*Vickers*); unlawful act manslaughter (*Newbury and Jones*) |
| Maximum certainty | Law clear in advance; Article 7 ECHR | Statutory definitions in the Theft Act 1968 | Gross negligence "so bad" test (*Adomako*; upheld in *Misra and Srivastava*, 2004); dishonesty (*Ivey*; *Barton and Booth*); "more than merely preparatory" (compare *Campbell* and *Tosti*) |
| No retrospective liability | No punishment for conduct lawful when done | Courts cannot create new offences (*Knuller*, 1973) | *R v R* (1991), upheld in *SW v UK* (1995); War Crimes Act 1991 |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Harm principle | Only harm to others justifies criminalisation (Mill) |
| Legal paternalism | Criminalising to protect people from themselves |
| Legal moralism | Criminalising to enforce shared morality (Devlin) |
| Autonomy | Treating people as free agents responsible for their choices |
| Constructive liability | Liability for a consequence beyond what the defendant intended or foresaw |
| Fair labelling | Offence names should reflect the nature and seriousness of the wrong |
| Correspondence | Mens rea should correspond to the actus reus |
| Maximum certainty | Offences should be defined clearly enough to guide conduct |
| Non-retrospectivity | No liability for acts that were lawful when done |

## Which principle for which question?

- **Non-fatal offences:** correspondence first, then fair labelling.
- **Murder:** fair labelling (mandatory life) and correspondence (GBH intent).
- **Unlawful act manslaughter:** correspondence (objective danger, no foresight of death).
- **Gross negligence manslaughter:** maximum certainty.
- **Theft:** certainty (dishonesty); fault (subjective first stage of *Ivey*).
- **Attempt:** harm (no harm caused) and certainty ("more than merely preparatory").
- **Duress and intoxication:** autonomy and individual responsibility.

## Exam traps

- Defining principles without applying them to specific offences.
- Confusing correspondence with fair labelling.
- Citing *Brown* as straightforward harm-principle reasoning.
- Forgetting that *SW v UK* found no breach of Article 7.
- Arguing only one side: the "risk of worse consequences" argument supports constructive liability.

3. What did *R v Brown* decide?
4. How does *R v Wilson* differ from *Brown*?
5. What did *R v Kennedy (No 2)* decide, and which theory does it reflect?
6. Why was *Caldwell* recklessness overruled in *R v G*?
7. Define correspondence and give one offence that breaches it.
8. Define fair labelling and give one example of a problem.
9. Which offence's test is criticised as circular, and which case held it compatible with Article 7?
10. What did *SW v UK* decide?
11. Which case presumes that mens rea is required in statutory offences?
12. Give one argument in favour of constructive liability.
