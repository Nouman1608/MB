## Questions

**1.** Explain how an axon establishes a resting potential. **[3]**

**2.** A stimulus was applied to an axon and the potential difference across its membrane was recorded.

| Time (ms) | 0.0 | 0.6 | 0.9 | 1.2 | 1.6 | 2.0 | 2.5 | 3.5 | 5.0 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Potential (mV) | −70 | −55 | +5 | +30 | 0 | −50 | −80 | −73 | −70 |

**(a)** State the maximum potential and calculate the change from the resting potential to this value. **[2]**
**(b)** Account for the rise in potential from 0.6 to 1.2 ms. **[3]**
**(c)** Account for the fall in potential from 1.2 to 2.5 ms. **[3]**

**3.** A neurone has a refractory period of 1.6 ms.
**(a)** Calculate the maximum number of impulses it can conduct per second. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain two ways in which the refractory period affects how impulses travel along an axon. **[2]**

**4.** Stimuli of increasing strength were applied to a neurone. Strength is in arbitrary units.

| Stimulus strength | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 | 10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Height of action potential (mV) | none | 100 | 100 | 100 | 100 |
| Impulses per second | 0 | 20 | 45 | 70 | 90 |

**(a)** State the lowest stimulus strength in the table that produced an action potential. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain what the table shows about the all-or-nothing principle, and how stimulus strength is signalled. **[3]**

**5.** Impulse A travels 0.45 m along a myelinated axon in 5.0 ms. Impulse B travels 0.04 m along a non-myelinated axon in 20 ms.
**(a)** Calculate the speed of each impulse in m per s. **[4]**
**(b)** Calculate how many times faster A is than B. **[1]**

**6.** **(a)** Explain how saltatory conduction increases the speed of an impulse. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why a wider axon conducts faster than a narrower one of the same type. **[2]**
**(c)** In a frog nerve, the speed of conduction was 32 m per s at 20 °C and 48 m per s at 30 °C. Calculate the percentage increase in speed, and explain it. **[4]**

**7.** An impulse crosses a pathway made of a sensory neurone 0.90 m long (speed 75 m per s), a motor neurone 0.42 m long (speed 70 m per s) and three synapses, each with a delay of 0.5 ms. Ignore the length of any other neurone.
**(a)** Calculate the time taken for an impulse to pass through the pathway. Give your answer to 3 significant figures in ms. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain why each synapse causes a delay. **[1]**

**8.** Describe the events at a cholinergic synapse from the arrival of an action potential at the presynaptic knob to the start of an action potential in the postsynaptic neurone. **[6]**

**9.** **(a)** Explain why transmission across a synapse is unidirectional. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain the importance of acetylcholinesterase in the synaptic cleft. **[2]**

**10.** At a synapse the resting potential is −70 mV and the threshold is −55 mV. Assume that each impulse from an excitatory presynaptic neurone depolarises the postsynaptic membrane by 4 mV, and that there is no loss of effect.
**(a)** Calculate the smallest number of excitatory impulses that reach threshold. **[2]**
**(b)** Name the type of summation if four different excitatory neurones fire at once. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why temporal summation needs impulses from one neurone to be close together in time. **[2]**
**(d)** An inhibitory neurone adds −5 mV when four excitatory neurones fire at once. Calculate the potential and state whether threshold is reached. **[2]**
**(e)** Explain how an inhibitory synapse produces a change in potential. **[1]**

**11.** Compare a neuromuscular junction with a cholinergic synapse between two neurones. **[4]**

**12.** Three drugs affect transmission at a neuromuscular junction. Predict and explain the effect on the muscle fibre of each.
**(a)** Drug P prevents voltage-gated calcium channels from opening. **[3]**
**(b)** Drug Q inhibits acetylcholinesterase. **[3]**
**(c)** Drug R binds to the acetylcholine receptors but does not open their sodium channels. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** ATP drives the sodium-potassium pump, which exports 3 Na⁺ and imports 2 K⁺ [1]. K⁺ channels are open and most Na⁺ channels shut, so K⁺ leaks out faster than Na⁺ leaks in [1]. The inside becomes negative relative to the outside, giving a stable potential difference [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Name both ions and the direction of each movement. "Ions move" is too vague.

**2. (a)** Maximum is **+30 mV** [1]. Change = 30 − (−70) = **100 mV** [1].
**(b)** Sodium channels open and Na⁺ diffuses in down its electrochemical gradient [1]. At −55 mV (threshold) more voltage-gated sodium channels open, which is positive feedback [1]. This rapid influx depolarises the membrane [1].
**(c)** The sodium channels shut [1] and voltage-gated K⁺ channels open, so K⁺ leaves down its gradient and the potential falls (repolarisation) [1]. Potassium channels are slow to close, so too much K⁺ leaves and the potential falls below −70 mV (hyperpolarisation) [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Use figures from the table. Linking "−80 mV" to slow-closing potassium channels shows you have read the data.

**3. (a)** 1.6 ms = 0.0016 s [1]; 1 / 0.0016 = **625 impulses per s** [1].
**(b)** Sodium channels cannot open straight away, so impulses are separate from each other [1]. The region behind the impulse is refractory, so the impulse travels in one direction only [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Convert ms to s before dividing, and give the unit. A rate stated as "625" with no unit is easy to lose credit on.

**4. (a)** **4** units [1].
**(b)** The height of the action potential is 100 mV at every strength that produces one, so the response is all-or-nothing [1]. A larger stimulus does not give a bigger action potential [1]. Strength is signalled by frequency, which rises from 20 to 90 impulses per s [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote values from both rows of the table. "Frequency increases" with no data is weaker than the range.

**5. (a)** A: 5.0 ms = 0.0050 s [1]; 0.45 / 0.0050 = **90 m per s** [1]. B: 20 ms = 0.020 s [1]; 0.04 / 0.020 = **2.0 m per s** [1].
**(b)** 90 / 2.0 = **45 times** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the unit conversion as a separate step.

**6. (a)** Myelin is an insulating layer, so no ions pass through the membrane beneath it [1]. Depolarisation happens only at the nodes of Ranvier, and local currents flow from node to node [1]. The impulse jumps from node to node, so fewer depolarisations are needed and conduction is faster [1].
**(b)** A wider axon has less resistance to the flow of ions in the cytoplasm [1]. There is less leakage of ions out of the axon, so local currents spread faster [1].
**(c)** Increase = 48 − 32 = 16 m per s [1]; 16 / 32 × 100 = **50%** [1]. Ions diffuse faster at the higher temperature [1], and enzymes make ATP faster for the sodium-potassium pump [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* "Jumps" needs a reason. Link it to insulation and to local currents between nodes, or the explanation is only a description.

**7. (a)** Sensory neurone: 0.90 / 75 = 0.012 s = 12.0 ms [1]. Motor neurone: 0.42 / 70 = 0.0060 s = 6.0 ms [1]. Synapses: 3 × 0.5 = 1.5 ms [1]. Total = 12.0 + 6.0 + 1.5 = **19.5 ms** [1].
**(b)** Time is needed for Ca²⁺ entry, vesicle fusion and diffusion of acetylcholine across the cleft [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Show each time before adding, and keep the unit with each figure.

**8.** The action potential depolarises the presynaptic knob [1]. Calcium channels in that membrane open and Ca²⁺ diffuses into the knob [1]. Vesicles of acetylcholine merge with the membrane and empty their contents into the cleft (exocytosis) [1]. The transmitter diffuses across the gap and attaches to receptors on the postsynaptic membrane [1]. This opens sodium channels, so Na⁺ enters and the membrane depolarises [1]. If the potential reaches threshold, a new action potential begins [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Follow the order of events and name the ion at each stage. Do not swap the calcium step and the sodium step.

**9. (a)** Vesicles of acetylcholine are only in the presynaptic knob [1]. Receptors are only on the postsynaptic membrane [1].
**(b)** It hydrolyses acetylcholine into choline and ethanoate [1]. This stops continuous stimulation of the postsynaptic membrane and allows the products to be recycled [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* State the feature and its consequence. "It removes acetylcholine" gains less than saying what would happen without it.

**10. (a)** Change needed = −55 − (−70) = 15 mV [1]. 15 / 4 = 3.75, so **4 impulses** [1].
**(b)** **Spatial summation** [1].
**(c)** The depolarisation from one impulse fades, and acetylcholine is broken down [1], so later impulses must arrive before the effect is lost for the effects to add [1].
**(d)** −70 + (4 × 4) − 5 = **−59 mV** [1]. This is below (more negative than) −55 mV, so threshold is not reached [1].
**(e)** Transmitter opens Cl⁻ and K⁺ channels, so Cl⁻ enters and K⁺ leaves, causing hyperpolarisation [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Round up in part (a), because 3 impulses cannot reach threshold. Writing 3.75 with no comment loses the final mark.

**11.** Both use acetylcholine, calcium channels, vesicles, receptors and acetylcholinesterase [1]. The postsynaptic cell of a neuromuscular junction is a muscle fibre, whereas at a synapse it is a neurone [1]. The membrane of the muscle fibre is folded and has more receptors [1]. A neuromuscular junction is always excitatory, whereas a synapse between neurones may be excitatory or inhibitory [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A "compare" needs similarities and differences stated side by side. Two separate descriptions leave the comparison undone.

**12. (a)** Calcium ions cannot enter the presynaptic knob [1], so vesicles do not fuse and less acetylcholine is released [1]. The muscle fibre is not depolarised, so it does not contract [1].
**(b)** Acetylcholine is not broken down [1], so it keeps binding to receptors and sodium channels stay open [1]. The muscle fibre stays depolarised, so contraction is prolonged [1].
**(c)** Acetylcholine cannot open sodium channels, so Na⁺ does not enter [1]. The muscle fibre is not depolarised, so there is no contraction even though acetylcholine is released [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Identify the step affected, then give the effect on the muscle fibre. A bare word such as "paralysis" shows no reasoning.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving out the permeability difference between K⁺ and Na⁺ when explaining the resting potential.
- Describing the pump as the cause of depolarisation.
- Quoting no data from tables or traces when asked to explain them.
- Not converting milliseconds to seconds.
- Writing "jumps" for saltatory conduction without mentioning insulation and local currents.
- Claiming that a stronger stimulus makes each action potential larger.
- Confusing the roles of Ca²⁺ in the presynaptic knob and Na⁺ in the postsynaptic membrane.
- Giving 3.75 as a number of impulses.
