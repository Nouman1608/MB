## Questions

**1.** Explain two functions of money. **[4]**

**2.** A remote community uses fresh fish as money. Explain two characteristics of money that fresh fish fails to meet. **[4]**

**3.** A trading town has 14 goods. Calculate how many exchange rates barter requires and how many prices a money economy requires, and state the difference. **[3]**

**4.** Country B has the following holdings by the public (illustrative, m units): notes and coins 45, current account deposits 155, savings deposits 380, time deposits 120.

**(a)** Calculate narrow money and broad money. **[2]**
**(b)** Households move 50 from savings deposits to current accounts. State the new narrow and broad money. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why cash held in bank tills is left out of the money supply. **[1]**

**5.** Households pay 2,400 units of new cash into banks. The reserve ratio is 12.5% and all loans are redeposited.

**(a)** Calculate the maximum total deposits. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the maximum credit created. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the lending in the first round. **[1]**
**(d)** Households now withdraw 7.5% of each new deposit as cash and do not redeposit it. Calculate the new maximum total deposits. **[2]**

**6.** Bank C has reserves of 90 and deposits of 600. It agrees a loan of 100 and credits the borrower's account.

**(a)** Describe the change to the bank's balance sheet. **[2]**
**(b)** The bank wants a 15% reserve ratio. Calculate its new ratio and the extra reserves needed. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why this order of events differs from the textbook credit multiplier. **[2]**

**7.** In Country B the demand for money is Md = 250 − 15r, where r is the interest rate in per cent. The money supply is 145.

**(a)** Calculate the equilibrium interest rate. **[2]**
**(b)** The money supply rises to 190. Calculate the new rate. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain how the rate adjusts through the bond market. **[2]**

**8.** In a loanable funds market the supply of funds is Qs = 30 + 6r and the demand is Qd = 150 − 9r.

**(a)** Calculate the equilibrium rate and quantity of funds. **[3]**
**(b)** Demand falls to Qd = 120 − 9r. Calculate the new rate and quantity. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain one cause of this fall in demand. **[1]**

**9.** A saver puts 8,000 units in an account paying 5.5%. Expected inflation is 3.5%, but actual inflation is 7%.

**(a)** Calculate the exact real interest rate the saver expected. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the real rate the saver actually earned. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the real value of the savings after one year in today's prices and the real loss. **[2]**

**10.** **(a)** In Country B, M = 350, P = 3.5 and T = 800. Calculate the velocity of circulation. **[2]**
**(b)** M rises 6%, V falls 1% and T rises 2%. Use the growth form of MV = PT to estimate the change in P. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the exact change in P. **[2]**

**11.** Evaluate the relevance of the quantity theory of money. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two functions, each with an explanation.
- Medium of exchange [1]: money is accepted in payment, so trade does not need each trader to want what the other offers [1].
- Store of value [1]: money keeps purchasing power until it is spent later, although inflation can erode this [1].
Other functions are equally valid if explained. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A bare list of two terms usually gains little. Each function needs a short explanation of what money lets people do.

**2.** Any two characteristics, each with a reason.
- Durability [1]: fish rots quickly, so it cannot hold value over time [1].
- Uniformity [1]: fish differ in size and freshness, so units are not equal [1].
- Portability or divisibility also work if explained. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Applying the characteristic to the fish in the stem works better than defining the characteristic in general terms.

**3.** Barter rates = n(n − 1)/2 = 14 × 13 / 2 [1] = 91 [1]. Money requires 14 prices, which is 77 fewer [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the formula before the number. A correct figure with no working is harder to credit if the figure is slightly wrong.

**4. (a)** Narrow = 45 + 155 = 200 [1]. Broad = 200 + 380 + 120 = 700 [1].
**(b)** Narrow = 200 + 50 = 250 [1]. Broad is unchanged at 700 [1].
**(c)** Only money in the hands of households and firms is counted. Cash sitting in a bank's own tills is not available for them to spend [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Part (b) rewards seeing that a switch between categories changes narrow money but leaves broad money unchanged.

**5. (a)** Maximum deposits = 2,400 / 0.125 [1] = 19,200 [1].
**(b)** Credit created = 19,200 − 2,400 = 16,800 [1].
**(c)** First-round lending = 2,400 × 0.875 = 2,100 [1].
**(d)** Total leakage per round = 12.5% + 7.5% = 20% [1]. Maximum deposits = 2,400 / 0.20 = 12,000 [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* In (d), adding the cash drain to the reserve ratio is the key step. Describe the result as a maximum, not a prediction.

**6. (a)** Loans (an asset) rise by 100 [1] and customer deposits (a liability) rise by 100 [1].
**(b)** New ratio = 90 / 700 = 12.9% [1]. Reserves needed = 0.15 × 700 = 105, so 15 more [1].
**(c)** In the textbook version a deposit arrives first and is lent on [1]. Here the loan creates the deposit, and reserves are sought afterwards, so lending depends on demand from borrowers and the bank's willingness to lend [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers state that both sides of the balance sheet rise by the same amount, then link this to the order of events.

**7. (a)** 250 − 15r = 145 [1], so r = 7% [1].
**(b)** 250 − 15r = 190 [1], so r = 4% [1].
**(c)** At the old rate, people hold more money than they want [1]. They buy bonds, bond prices rise and yields fall until demand for money equals the new supply [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the curve that shifts. An increase in the money supply shifts the vertical supply curve right and leaves the demand curve where it is.

**8. (a)** 30 + 6r = 150 − 9r [1], so 15r = 120 and r = 8% [1]. Funds = 30 + 48 = 78 [1].
**(b)** 30 + 6r = 120 − 9r [1], so r = 6% and funds = 66 [1].
**(c)** Firms expect weaker sales, so they plan less investment and borrow less (any valid cause) [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Check the answer by substituting r into both equations. If supply and demand are not equal, an error has crept in.

**9. (a)** Exact real rate = 1.055 / 1.035 − 1 [1] = 1.93% [1].
**(b)** 1.055 / 1.07 − 1 = −1.40% [1].
**(c)** Value after a year = 8,000 × 1.055 = 8,440, and in today's prices 8,440 / 1.07 = 7,888 [1]. Real loss = 8,000 − 7,888 = 112 [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Comment that unexpected inflation transfers value from lenders to borrowers when the rate is fixed in advance. This links the calculation to the economics.

**10. (a)** V = PT / M = 3.5 × 800 / 350 [1] = 8 [1].
**(b)** %ΔP ≈ 6 − 1 − 2 = 3% [1].
**(c)** 1.06 × 0.99 / 1.02 [1] = 1.0288, so P rises 2.88% [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* State clearly that the estimate in (b) is approximate and the figure in (c) is exact. The two should be close.

**11.** A balanced answer might include the following.
- MV = PT is an identity, true by definition [1].
- It becomes the quantity theory if V is stable [1] and T is fixed at full capacity [1].
- Then P changes in proportion to M, for example a 10% rise in M raises P by 10% [1].
- For: over long periods, faster money growth tends to go with higher inflation [1], and it gives a clear policy rule [1].
- Against: V can fall, for example if M rises 10%, V falls 6% and T rises 2%, P rises only 1.4% (1.10 × 0.94 / 1.02 − 1) [1].
- Against: with spare capacity, extra money can raise output instead of prices [1].
- Against: money may follow spending if banks lend on demand, and cost-push inflation does not need money growth [1].
- Judgement: more relevant over the long run and at high inflation, less in the short run or with spare capacity [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Weigh the points rather than listing them. A conclusion that says when the theory is more or less relevant tends to read as stronger than a list.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving the answer to a calculation with no working or no units.
- Calling the credit multiplier a fixed result instead of a maximum.
- Forgetting to add the cash drain to the reserve ratio.
- Counting a bank's own cash in the money supply.
- Shifting the demand curve when the money supply changes.
- Treating a nominal rate as the real return.
- Using actual inflation when a lender's decision rests on expected inflation.
- Stating that the quantity theory is right or wrong without testing the assumptions about V and T.
