## Natural selection in five links

```
variation (some from mutation) → selection pressure (predator, disease, climate)
→ best-suited individuals survive and breed → their alleles are passed on
→ over many generations the allele becomes more common
```

Every natural selection answer needs the middle three links. "Many generations" earns the last mark.

## People and dates

| Who or what | What to recall |
|---|---|
| Darwin | Round-the-world expedition, experiments, discussion, geology and fossils → natural selection |
| *On the Origin of Species* | Published **1859**; much controversy |
| Wallace | Independent theory; **joint writings with Darwin, 1858**; warning colouration; theory of speciation |
| Lamarck | Changes during a lifetime are inherited; in the vast majority of cases this cannot occur |
| Mendel | Mid-19th century plant breeding; "units" passed on **unchanged** |
| Late 19th century | Chromosome behaviour during cell division observed |
| Early 20th century | Units (genes) placed on chromosomes |
| Mid-20th century | Structure of DNA determined; mechanism of gene function worked out |

## Why Darwin's theory was accepted slowly

1. It challenged the idea that God made all the animals and plants on Earth.
2. There was insufficient evidence at the time to convince many scientists.
3. The mechanism of inheritance and variation was not known until 50 years after publication.

## Why Mendel was recognised only after his death

- Chromosomes, genes and DNA were unknown, so nothing could explain his units.
- His work was little read and his use of ratios was unfamiliar to biologists then.
- Only when chromosomes were seen to behave like his units did the importance become clear.

## Speciation in four steps

1. **Isolation** of populations (river, mountains, sea).
2. **Genetic variation** in each population, with new mutations arising separately.
3. **Different conditions** select different alleles in each population.
4. Eventually they **cannot interbreed to produce fertile offspring**: two species.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Natural selection | Individuals best suited to the environment survive, breed and pass on their alleles |
| Species | Organisms that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring |
| Speciation | Formation of new species, usually after isolation |
| Warning colouration | Bright colouring that signals to predators that an animal is harmful or unpleasant to eat |
| Fossil | The remains of an organism from millions of years ago, found in rock |
| Trace fossil | Preserved sign of an organism, such as a footprint, burrow or rootlet trace |
| Extinction | No remaining individuals of a species still alive |
| Resistant strain | Bacteria that are not killed by an antibiotic, after a mutation |
| MRSA | A bacterium resistant to antibiotics, named in the specification |
| Gene theory | The understanding, built by many scientists, that genes on chromosomes carry inherited information |

## Evidence for evolution

- **Genes**: characteristics are shown to pass to offspring in genes.
- **Fossils**, formed three ways: parts that did not decay (a condition for decay was absent); parts replaced by
  minerals as they decayed; preserved traces. Fossils show how much or how little organisms have changed.
- **Gaps**: early life was soft-bodied and left few traces; geological activity destroyed many of those, so we
  cannot be certain how life began.
- **Antibiotic resistance**: evolution observed within years, because bacteria reproduce fast.

## Extinction factors to describe

New disease; new predator; new competitor that outcompetes the species; environmental or climate change;
habitat destruction; a catastrophic event. Link each to deaths or failure to breed.

## Resistant bacteria chain

```
mutation → new strain, some resistant → antibiotic kills non-resistant bacteria
→ resistant bacteria survive and reproduce → population of resistant strain rises
→ spreads: people are not immune and there is no effective treatment
```

**Slowing it:** no antibiotics for non-serious or viral infections; complete the course; restrict agricultural
use. New antibiotics are costly and slow to develop.

Quick calculation: a bacterium dividing every 30 minutes gives 2^6 = 64 cells after 3 hours (6 divisions).

## Exam traps

- The antibiotic does not cause the mutation; the mutation is already present and the antibiotic selects it.
- Speciation needs "cannot produce **fertile** offspring", not just "look different".
- 1858 is the joint writings; 1859 is *On the Origin of Species*. Do not swap them.
- Lamarck's idea is about changes during a lifetime. Do not call it "wrong in every case": the specification
  says the vast majority of cases.
- Patients completing the course stops survivors mutating into resistant strains; "so the antibiotic works
  better" is too vague.

## Self-test

1. State the three steps of Darwin's theory as the specification gives them.
2. In which year was *On the Origin of Species* published?
3. Give two reasons Darwin's theory was only gradually accepted.
4. What did Lamarck's theory propose?
5. What prompted Darwin to publish in 1859?
6. Name two things Wallace is best known for.
7. Describe the four steps of speciation.
8. What did Mendel conclude about inherited "units"?
9. Give one reason the importance of Mendel's work was not recognised in his lifetime.
10. Give the three ways fossils can form.
11. Why are there few fossils of the earliest life on Earth?
12. Give three ways to reduce the development of antibiotic-resistant strains.

### Answers

1. Organisms in a species show a wide range of variation; those most suited to the environment are more likely
   to survive to breed successfully; the characteristics that helped them survive are passed to the next
   generation.
2. 1859.
3. Any two: it challenged the idea that God made all living things; insufficient evidence at the time; the
   mechanism of inheritance and variation was unknown for 50 years.
4. That changes occurring in an organism during its lifetime can be inherited.
5. Joint writings with Wallace in 1858.
6. Warning colouration in animals; his theory of speciation (also gathering evidence worldwide).
7. Populations are isolated; each has genetic variation; different conditions select different alleles; over
   time they cannot interbreed to produce fertile offspring.
8. Inheritance of each characteristic is determined by units passed on to descendants unchanged.
9. Chromosomes and genes were unknown, so there was no structure to explain his units (or few scientists read
   his work).
10. From parts that did not decay because a condition for decay was absent; by minerals replacing parts as they
    decay; as preserved traces such as footprints, burrows and rootlet traces.
11. Early life was soft-bodied so left few traces, and geological activity destroyed most of those.
12. Do not prescribe for non-serious or viral infections; patients complete the course; restrict agricultural use.
