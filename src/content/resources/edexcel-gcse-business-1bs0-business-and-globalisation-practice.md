## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define the term multinational. **[2]**

**2.** Which **one** of the following is a barrier to international trade? **[1]**

A: E-commerce

B: A tariff

C: An export

D: A multinational

**3.** Which **two** of the following would make goods imported into the UK more expensive for a UK business? **[2]**

A: Freight charges rising

B: A tariff being removed

C: A new tariff on those goods

D: Shipping becoming cheaper

E: The supplier cutting its price

**4.** Tide Mill Tools buys cordless drills from an overseas factory at £32 each. Freight costs £3.50 per drill and a tariff of 6% is charged on the £32 value.

**(a)** Calculate the tariff paid per drill. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the landed cost of one drill. **[2]**

**5.** Bellhaven Gin sells a 70cl bottle in the UK. For customers in a new overseas market it sells a smaller bottle with a label in the local language, sold through a local distributor. State **one** way Bellhaven Gin has changed its product to sell overseas. **[1]**

**6.** Explain **one** advantage to a business of being based in a country that belongs to a trade bloc. **[3]**

**7.** Fairlie Bags earned £120,000 from exports last year and £147,000 this year. Calculate the percentage change in export revenue. **[2]**

### Section B: longer questions

**8.** Penmore Shoes has advertised its shoes as "made in Britain" for forty years. The owners plan to move production to a factory abroad. Outline **one** way this could affect Penmore's reputation. **[2]**

**9.** Explain **one** drawback to a business of buying its supplies from overseas. **[3]**

**10.** Ravenhill Ceramics makes dinner plates in the UK and sells them to shops at £14 each. Imported plates of a similar style now sell to the same shops at £9. Ravenhill's sales have fallen from 20,000 plates a year to 16,000.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in Ravenhill's sales. **[2]**

**(b)** Analyse the impact of competition from overseas on Ravenhill Ceramics. **[6]**

**11.** Kirkby Outdoor makes tents in the UK at a cost of £70 each. It wants to sell in a European country and has two options.

Option 1: sell directly to overseas customers through its own translated website at £150 a tent. It expects 400 sales a year, pays £22 delivery per tent and spends £9,000 a year on translation and online advertising.

Option 2: sell to a distributor in that country, which will place the tents in outdoor shops. The distributor pays £110 a tent and expects to buy 600 a year. Kirkby pays bulk shipping of £6 per tent.

Justify which option Kirkby Outdoor should choose. **[9]**

**12.** Hallworth Furniture makes 3,000 oak tables a year in Yorkshire and promotes them as "handmade in Yorkshire". Labour costs £120 per table. A factory abroad could make the same tables with labour costing £45 per table, but shipping each table back to UK customers would cost an extra £30. Closing the Yorkshire workshop would cost £180,000 in redundancy and closure costs.

Evaluate whether Hallworth Furniture should move production abroad. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** A business that has operations, such as factories, offices or shops [1], in more than one country [1]. **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* A business that only exports is not a multinational; the definition needs operations based in more than one country.

**2.** B [1]. **[1]**

**3.** A [1]. C [1]. **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* B, D and E all lower the cost of imports. Read "more expensive" carefully before choosing.

**4. (a)** 6% × £32 = **£1.92** [1]

**(b)** £32 + £3.50 + £1.92 [1] = **£37.42** [1] **[3]**

*Examiner insight:* The tariff here is charged on the £32 value only, not on freight. Show each part added so the method is clear.

**5.** It sells a smaller bottle / it uses a label in the local language [1]. **[1]**

*Examiner insight:* "State" asks you to select from the context. The distributor is a change to place, not product, so it would not answer this question.

**6.** Trade between member countries has few or no tariffs [1], so the business can sell to customers in other member countries without its products becoming more expensive [1]. This makes it easier to compete with local firms in those countries and to grow sales abroad [1]. **[3]**

*Examiner insight:* Explain questions carry no context, so a general but connected chain is enough. Each step should build on the one before.

**7.** (£147,000 − £120,000) ÷ £120,000 × 100 [1] = **22.5%** increase [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Customers who value British-made shoes may feel misled once production moves abroad [1], so some loyal buyers may switch to rival brands and Penmore may have to drop its "made in Britain" message [1]. **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* Outline needs two linked points in context. Using Penmore's forty-year slogan is what turns a general point into an applied one.

**9.** Delivery from overseas takes longer than from a home supplier [1], so the business must order further ahead or hold more stock [1]. Holding extra stock ties up cash and storage space, and a delayed shipment could still leave it unable to meet customer orders [1]. **[3]**

*Examiner insight:* Other valid starting points are quality being harder to check, tariffs or freight increases raising costs, or language and payment difficulties.

**10. (a)** (16,000 − 20,000) ÷ 20,000 × 100 [1] = **−20%**, a fall of 20% [1]

**(b)** The imported plates are £5 cheaper than Ravenhill's £14 plates, so shops can earn more profit or charge customers less by stocking them [1]. This explains why Ravenhill's sales have fallen by 4,000 plates [1], cutting its sales revenue from £280,000 to £224,000 [1]. Because its fixed costs stay the same, its profit falls by more than its revenue, and it may struggle to survive if the trend continues [1]. Ravenhill is unlikely to match a £9 price if its UK costs are higher, so it may need to differentiate, for example by promoting British-made quality or offering designs to order [1]. It could also look for cheaper inputs, which shows that buying from overseas can be part of the answer to overseas competition [1]. **[8]**

*Examiner insight:* An Analyse answer uses the figures in the case. Linking the £5 price gap to the 20% sales fall and then to profit gives a clear chain of reasoning.

**11.** Option 1 contribution per tent = £150 − £70 − £22 = £58 [1]. Total = £58 × 400 = £23,200, minus £9,000 = **£14,200** [1]. Option 2 contribution per tent = £110 − £70 − £6 = £34 [1]. Total = £34 × 600 = **£20,400** [1]. Option 2 earns £6,200 more a year on these forecasts [1]. Option 2 also uses the distributor's knowledge of local customers and shops, so Kirkby takes less risk in an unfamiliar market [1]. However, Kirkby loses control of the final price and how its tents are displayed, and relies on one distributor [1]. Option 1 earns more per tent (£58 against £34) and builds direct contact with customers, which could grow over time [1]. **Judgement:** choose Option 2, because it earns more on the forecasts and spreads less risk onto Kirkby while it learns the market; the website could be added later once the brand is known [1]. **[9]**

*Examiner insight:* Justify asks you to recommend one of two options using the information. Calculating both, then giving one strength of the rejected option, makes the recommendation more convincing.

**12.** Saving per table = £120 − £45 − £30 = £45 [1]. Annual saving = £45 × 3,000 = **£135,000** [1]. The £180,000 closure cost would be recovered in £180,000 ÷ £135,000 = 1.33 years, about 16 months [1]. After that, lower costs could raise profit or allow lower prices to compete [1]. Against the move: Hallworth's "handmade in Yorkshire" message could no longer be used, and customers paying for local craftsmanship may stop buying [1]. If sales fell, the saving would shrink because it depends on making 3,000 tables [1]. Quality of oak furniture is harder to check from a distance, and shipping adds delay and the risk of damage [1]. Redundancies could harm the firm's local reputation [1]. **Conclusion:** the financial case is strong, with the closure cost repaid in under two years, but it depends on customers not valuing the Yorkshire origin; if the brand is the main reason people buy, Hallworth should stay, perhaps cutting costs in other ways [1]. **[9]**

*Examiner insight:* Evaluate needs a supported conclusion. Saying what the decision depends on, here how much customers value the "handmade in Yorkshire" message, makes the judgement more than a guess.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating any exporter as a multinational.
- Adding tariffs to freight as well as the goods when the question says the tariff is on the goods' value.
- Forgetting that the original figure is the divisor in a percentage change.
- Writing about globalisation in general, with no link to the business's price, costs or sales.
- Recommending an option without comparing both sets of figures.
