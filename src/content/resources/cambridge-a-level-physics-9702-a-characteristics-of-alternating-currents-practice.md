## Section A

**1.** Explain the difference between the peak value and the root-mean-square value of a sinusoidal alternating current. **[2]**

**2.** A sinusoidal alternating voltage has a period of 2.5 ms. Calculate its frequency and angular frequency. **[2]**

**3.** State the relationship between the mean power and the maximum power dissipated in a resistor connected to a sinusoidal supply. **[1]**

**4.** An electric heater is rated 2.0 kW, 240 V. Calculate the r.m.s. current and the peak current. **[2]**

**5.** A student says: "The mean value of an alternating current over a cycle is I₀/√2." Correct the statement. **[2]**

**6.** A sinusoidal voltage has a peak-to-peak value of 18 V. Calculate its r.m.s. value. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** The p.d. across a 12 Ω resistor is given by V = 24 sin(200πt), where V is in volts and t in seconds.

**(a)** State the peak p.d. and calculate the frequency. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the r.m.s. p.d. and the r.m.s. current. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the maximum power and the mean power dissipated. **[2]**

**(d)** Calculate the p.d. at t = 1.0 ms. **[1]**

**(e)** Calculate the earliest time after t = 0 at which the p.d. is 18 V. **[2]**

**8.** An oscilloscope displays a sinusoidal trace. The y-gain is 5.0 V per division and the time-base is 2.0 ms per division. The trace is 5.0 divisions from its lowest to its highest point, and one complete cycle covers 2.5 divisions.

**(a)** Determine the peak voltage. **[1]**

**(b)** Determine the frequency. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the r.m.s. voltage. **[1]**

**9.** A resistor of resistance R is connected to a sinusoidal supply of peak current I₀.

**(a)** Write an expression for the instantaneous power when the current is I₀ sin ωt. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why the power is never negative. **[1]**

**(c)** Use the fact that the mean value of sin²ωt over a cycle is ½ to show that I_r.m.s. = I₀/√2. **[2]**

**(d)** The supply frequency is 50 Hz. State the frequency at which the power varies. **[1]**

**10.** A 6.0 Ω resistor is connected in turn to:

- supply X, a sinusoidal alternating p.d. of peak value 12 V;
- supply Y, a square-wave p.d. that switches between +12 V and −12 V.

**(a)** Calculate the mean power from each supply. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why the relationship V_r.m.s. = V₀/√2 cannot be used for supply Y. **[1]**

**11.** A sinusoidal current of frequency 50 Hz has a peak value of 3.0 A.

**(a)** Write the equation for the current, with numerical values. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the current 2.5 ms after it was zero and increasing. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the steady direct current that would produce the same heating in a resistor. **[1]**

**12.** A transformer's output is labelled "12 V a.c.". A student connects it to a 4.0 Ω resistor.

**(a)** State whether 12 V is a peak or an r.m.s. value. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the mean power in the resistor. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the peak current. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** The peak value is the maximum value of the current, measured from zero [1]. The r.m.s. value is the value of the direct current that would dissipate the same mean power in a resistor; for a sine wave it is I₀/√2 [1].

**2.** f = 1/T = 1 ÷ 2.5 × 10⁻³ = 400 Hz [1]; ω = 2πf = 2.5 × 10³ rad s⁻¹ [1].

**3.** The mean power is half the maximum power [1].

**4.** I_r.m.s. = P/V = 2000 ÷ 240 = 8.3 A [1]; I₀ = 8.33 × √2 = 12 A [1].

**5.** The mean value over a complete cycle is zero, because the current flows equally in each direction [1]. I₀/√2 is the r.m.s. value [1].

**6.** V₀ = 18 ÷ 2 = 9.0 V [1]; V_r.m.s. = 9.0 ÷ √2 = 6.4 V [1].

**7. (a)** V₀ = 24 V [1]; ω = 200π, so f = 200π ÷ 2π = 100 Hz [1].

**(b)** V_r.m.s. = 24 ÷ √2 = 17 V [1]; I_r.m.s. = 16.97 ÷ 12 = 1.4 A [1].

**(c)** Maximum power = V₀²/R = 576 ÷ 12 = 48 W [1]; mean power = 24 W [1].

**(d)** V = 24 sin(200π × 0.0010) = 24 sin(0.628) = 14 V [1].

**(e)** sin(200πt) = 18 ÷ 24 = 0.75, so 200πt = 0.848 rad [1]; t = 0.848 ÷ 200π = 1.3 × 10⁻³ s [1].

**8. (a)** Peak-to-peak = 5.0 × 5.0 = 25 V, so V₀ = 12.5 V [1].

**(b)** T = 2.5 × 2.0 ms = 5.0 ms [1]; f = 1 ÷ 5.0 × 10⁻³ = 200 Hz [1].

**(c)** V_r.m.s. = 12.5 ÷ √2 = 8.8 V [1].

**9. (a)** P = I₀²R sin²ωt [1].

**(b)** The current is squared, so P is positive (or zero) whichever way the current flows [1].

**(c)** Mean power = ½I₀²R [1]. Setting this equal to I_r.m.s.²R gives I_r.m.s.² = ½I₀², so I_r.m.s. = I₀/√2 [1].

**(d)** 100 Hz [1].

**10. (a)** X: V_r.m.s. = 12 ÷ √2 = 8.49 V [1]; mean power = 8.49² ÷ 6.0 = 12 W [1]. Y: the p.d. is always 12 V in magnitude, so mean power = 12² ÷ 6.0 = 24 W [1].

**(b)** The relationship applies only to sinusoidal waveforms; for a square wave the r.m.s. value equals the peak value [1].

**11. (a)** I = 3.0 sin(100πt) (or I = 3.0 sin 314t) [1].

**(b)** I = 3.0 sin(100π × 0.0025) = 3.0 sin(π/4) = 2.1 A [1].

**(c)** I_r.m.s. = 3.0 ÷ √2 = 2.1 A [1].

**12. (a)** R.m.s. [1].

**(b)** P = V_r.m.s.²/R = 144 ÷ 4.0 = 36 W [1].

**(c)** I_r.m.s. = 12 ÷ 4.0 = 3.0 A [1]; I₀ = 3.0 × √2 = 4.2 A [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating a peak-to-peak reading as the peak value.
- Calculating sin ωt with the calculator in degree mode.
- Forgetting that quoted ratings are r.m.s. values.
- Writing the mean power as I₀V₀ instead of ½I₀V₀.
- Using V₀/√2 for a square wave.
- Writing ω for f, or f for ω, in the sine equation.
