**HL only.** These questions practise the Paper 3 regional study on political developments in Latin America, 1934 to 2020. Where a question needs one country, use the one you have studied. The answers use Cuba, Argentina and Peru. Revise first with the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-latin-american-politics/) and the [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-latin-american-politics-revision-notes/).

## Questions

**1.** Explain two reasons why a revolution broke out in Cuba in the 1950s. **[4]**

**2.** Read the extract (constructed for practice).

> "In our school there are no more illiterates. The young teachers came from the city with lamps and books, and slept in our houses. Some of the neighbours were afraid, because they said it was only politics. But my mother learned to read at fifty." (Constructed for practice: a paraphrase of a rural woman's recollection of the 1961 literacy campaign.)

**(a)** Identify two effects of the campaign suggested in the extract. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how the extract helps to assess the social policies of Castro's government, and one limitation of using it alone. **[4]**

**3.** Distinguish between populism and military dictatorship as political forms in Latin America. **[3]**

**4.** Analyse the economic and political reasons for the growth of populism in one Latin American country. **[6]**

**5.** Explain the impact of a guerrilla movement on one country in Latin America. **[6]**

**6.** Examine the methods used by a military dictatorship to maintain power in one Latin American country. **[8]**

**7.** Discuss the reasons for the return to democracy in one Latin American country. **[8]**

**8.** To what extent did Castro maintain power through social policies rather than repression? **[8]**

**9.** Paper 3 style essay question: "Transitional justice was the key to successful democratisation in Latin America." To what extent do you agree, with reference to one country? **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Two developed points, 2 marks each. Political: Batista's coup in 1952 ended elections and the dictatorship was repressive and corrupt [1], so peaceful opposition seemed impossible and armed struggle gained support [1]. Economic: dependence on sugar and US ownership of land and utilities [1], with seasonal unemployment in the countryside and a gap between Havana and the rural areas [1]. Social: a frustrated middle class, students and professionals joined opposition movements [1], and Castro's guerrilla campaign gave them a focus [1]. **[4]**

**2. (a)** Literacy rose, including for older people [1]; young volunteers from cities lived in rural homes, spreading the government's message and contact between city and countryside [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** It suggests that the campaign reached rural people and had real benefits, supporting the view that education was a major social achievement [1], and it shows some suspicion, suggesting that political loyalty was part of the aim [1]. Limitation: one recollection cannot show the national scale [1]; the source is a paraphrase of memory, perhaps filtered by later views of the revolution [1]. **[4]**

**3.** Populism: leaders who appeal directly to "the people", usually working-class, through welfare, nationalism and a charismatic style, often elected [1]. Military dictatorship: rule by the armed forces after a coup, banning or controlling parties and using repression [1]. A populist leader may rule authoritatively, but is typically based on mass support and elections, while a military regime relies on force and institutional control by officers [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Choose Argentina. Economic: industrial growth in the 1930s and 1940s created an urban working class with little representation, and Perón offered wage rises, welfare and state support for industry [2]. Political: the old conservative elites had lost credibility; unions had no voice [1]. Leader appeal: Perón's links with the unions and the *descamisados*, and the role of Eva Perón and her foundation [1]. Women's vote (1947) widened the base [1]. Judgement on the main reason for growth [1]. **[6]**

**5.** Choose Peru. Social: the conflict killed about 69,000 people, mostly rural, Indigenous people, and led to forced displacement and the creation of self-defence patrols [2]. Political: it weakened civilian rule and gave the army power, and contributed to Fujimori's self-coup in 1992 [2]. Economic: rural economy disrupted, with hyperinflation in the 1980s [1]. Overall: the defeat of Shining Path after Guzmán's capture in 1992 did not end political damage, as the state became authoritarian under Fujimori [1]. **[6]**

**6.** Choose Argentina (1976 to 1983). Repression: secret detention centres, torture and disappearance of thousands [2]. Control of institutions: closed Congress, banned parties and unions, censored the media [2]. Ideology and support: presented the regime as defending the nation against subversion, which gained support from some elites and sections of the middle class [1]. Economic: free-market policies that cut union power [1]. Limits: the regime lost support after economic failure and the Falklands defeat [1]. Judgement: repression was the main method [1]. **[8]**

**7.** Political: the Falklands defeat discredited the junta and split the military [2]. Economic: inflation, debt and recession weakened support [1]. Social: the Mothers of the Plaza de Mayo, churches and human rights groups pressed for change, with some international pressure [2]. Transitional justice as a result and a reason: demands for truth and trials [1]. Judgement on which factor was most important: for example, the military's defeat was the trigger but civil society sustained the pressure [2]. **[8]**

**8.** Social policies: education and health, the Literacy Campaign, land reform and nationalism gave real support [2]. Charisma and nationalism: Castro's resistance to the US, especially after the Bay of Pigs and missile crisis [1]. Repression: revolutionary courts, CDRs, imprisonment, UMAP camps and pressure to emigrate [2]. Cold War: Soviet aid and the threat from the US justified control [1]. External support: Soviet subsidies [1]. Judgement: both were essential and reinforced each other [1]. **[8]**

**9.** Use the Paper 3 markbands. A top answer (13 to 15 marks) shows in-depth understanding of the argument, evaluates a range of perspectives and reaches a substantiated judgement. For Argentina, case for (about 4 marks): CONADEP and *Nunca Más* (1984) exposed the truth; the Trial of the Juntas (1985) established that generals were not above the law; the later annulment of amnesty laws and renewed trials. Against (about 4 marks): amnesty laws and pardons in the 1980s showed how fragile it was; democratisation depended also on the defeat of the junta, economic crisis and civil society; the army's threat in the 1980s. Perspectives (about 3 marks): those who see justice as necessary for democratic legitimacy and the rule of law, and those who stress stability and reconciliation, and argue amnesty may be needed. Comparison with Chile's Rettig Commission could be used. Judgement (about 4 marks): for example, that justice helped to consolidate democracy in the long run but was not the initial key. **[15]**
