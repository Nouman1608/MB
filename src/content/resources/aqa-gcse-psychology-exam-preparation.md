AQA GCSE Psychology (8182) is assessed through two written papers, not tiered: Paper 1 (Cognition and
behaviour, 1h45, 100 marks, 50%) and Paper 2 (Social context and behaviour, 1h45, 100 marks, 50%),
both externally assessed with no coursework or controlled assessment. These notes turn that structure
into an exam-day plan, alongside the
[Cognition and Behaviour](/resources/aqa-gcse-psychology-cognition-and-behaviour/) and
[Social Context and Behaviour](/resources/aqa-gcse-psychology-social-context-and-behaviour/) topic
resources already on the site.

## Know which paper each unit sits under before you plan revision

Paper 1 and Paper 2 each cover a distinct set of units, and confusing which unit belongs to which
paper wastes revision time close to the exam. **Exam-preparation priority**: build a simple checklist
mapping each of your studied units to Paper 1 or Paper 2 before starting a revision timetable, so
practice time is allocated to the correct paper's content, not split evenly by assumption.

## Applying named studies to a new scenario is a distinct, practisable skill

Many questions present an unfamiliar scenario and ask students to apply a named study or theory to it,
not simply recall the study's findings in isolation. **Exam-preparation priority**: for every key
study revised, practise applying its findings to a scenario that was not the original study's own
context -- for example, applying a memory study's findings to a real-world situation like eyewitness
testimony -- since this application step is where marks are concentrated, not the recall of the study
itself.

## Command words and how much detail they expect

**Identify**, **name** and **state** require a short, precise recall answer. **Describe** requires an
accurate account of a study, theory or process without needing to explain why. **Explain** requires a
reasoned account of why something happens or why a study reached its conclusion. **Evaluate** requires
weighing a study's or theory's strengths and limitations to reach a justified judgement.

## Worked practice scenario: an evaluate-style answer plan

Question: "Evaluate the use of laboratory experiments in psychology, using an example from your
studies."

```
Step 1 - state the strength, tied to a named example:
Laboratory experiments allow researchers to carefully control
extraneous variables, which increases confidence that the independent
variable is genuinely responsible for any change in the dependent
variable -- for example, in [named lab study], variables such as
[named variable] were controlled.

Step 2 - state a limitation, also tied to the example:
However, the artificial, controlled setting can reduce ecological
validity -- participants' behaviour in a lab may not reflect how they
would behave in a real-world setting, limiting how far [named study]'s
findings generalise beyond the lab.

Step 3 - weigh the two points against each other:
The high control that strengthens confidence in cause-and-effect
conclusions is the same feature that limits real-world applicability
-- the two are directly linked, not separate unrelated points.

Step 4 - reach a judgement:
Laboratory experiments are most valuable for establishing whether a
cause-and-effect relationship exists in principle, but findings should
be checked against more naturalistic research before being applied
confidently to real-world behaviour.
```

This four-step structure keeps the answer from listing a strength and a limitation as disconnected
facts (Steps 1-2) and instead weighs them against each other before reaching an explicit judgement
(Steps 3-4) -- exactly what "evaluate" is testing.

## Before/during exam checklist

- **Before the exam**: map each studied unit to Paper 1 or Paper 2 before building a revision
  timetable; practise applying named studies to unfamiliar scenarios, not just recalling their
  findings; practise full "evaluate" answers that weigh a strength against a limitation and reach a
  judgement.
- **During Paper 1**: check whether a question is asking you to recall a study or apply it to the
  scenario given -- these require different responses even when the underlying content is the same.
- **During Paper 2**: the same recall-versus-application distinction applies to social-context
  studies and theories.
- **On every paper**: match your answer's depth to the command word -- "describe" does not need
  reasoning, "explain" does, and "evaluate" needs a weighed judgement, not just a list of points.

## Self-test

1. Why is it worth mapping each studied unit to Paper 1 or Paper 2 before revising?
2. What is the difference between "describe" and "evaluate" as command words?
3. Why is applying a named study to a new scenario a distinct skill from recalling its findings?
4. In the worked scenario, why does the answer weigh the strength and limitation together in Step 3,
   rather than simply listing them separately?

**Answers:** 1. Because Paper 1 and Paper 2 each cover a distinct set of units, and confusing which
belongs to which paper risks misallocating revision time close to the exam. 2. "Describe" requires an
accurate account of a study, theory or process without needing to explain why; "evaluate" requires
weighing strengths and limitations to reach a justified judgement. 3. Because many exam questions
present an unfamiliar scenario and require applying a study's findings to it, which is a genuinely
different skill from recalling what the study found in its own original context. 4. Because the same
feature (high control) that produces the strength (confidence in cause-and-effect) also produces the
limitation (reduced ecological validity) -- weighing them together, rather than listing them as
unrelated points, shows the reasoned judgement "evaluate" is testing.
