## 8.24 Trends at a glance

| Property | Trend from Cl₂ to I₂ | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|
| Melting and boiling temperature | increases | more electrons per molecule, so stronger London forces between molecules |
| State at room temperature | gas → liquid → solid | follows from the rising melting and boiling temperatures |
| Electronegativity | decreases | larger atom, more shielding, bonding pair further from the nucleus |
| Reactivity (oxidising power) | decreases | an added electron sits further out with more shielding, so it is held less tightly |

**Method in steps: explaining a boiling-temperature trend**

1. State the structure: simple molecular, diatomic molecules.
2. Name the forces broken: London forces between molecules, not covalent bonds.
3. Compare electrons: more electrons means larger instantaneous dipoles and stronger London forces.
4. Conclude: more energy needed, so higher boiling temperature.

**Method in steps: explaining the reactivity trend**

1. A halogen reacts by gaining an electron to form X⁻.
2. Down the group the atomic radius increases and there are more inner shells.
3. The nucleus attracts an incoming electron less strongly.
4. So the halogen gains electrons less readily and is a weaker oxidising agent.

## 8.25 Colours and displacement

| | Standard conditions | Water | Cyclohexane (non-polar) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cl₂ | pale green gas | very pale green / almost colourless | very pale green / almost colourless |
| Br₂ | red-brown liquid | yellow to orange | orange to red-brown |
| I₂ | grey-black solid | brown | violet |

Displacement summary (✓ = reaction):

| Halogen added | to Cl⁻ | to Br⁻ | to I⁻ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cl₂ | -- | ✓ Br₂ formed | ✓ I₂ formed |
| Br₂ | ✗ | -- | ✓ I₂ formed |
| I₂ | ✗ | ✗ | -- |

General ionic equation: X₂ + 2Y⁻ → 2X⁻ + Y₂, where X is above Y in the group. Halogen reduced, halide
oxidised.

**Reminder.** Iodine water and bromine water can both look orange-brown. Shake with cyclohexane: violet
means iodine, orange means bromine.

## 8.26 Redox equations to know

| Reaction | Equation | Oxidation number changes |
|---|---|---|
| Group 1 metal | 2Na + Br₂ → 2NaBr | Na 0 → +1; Br 0 → −1 |
| Group 2 metal | Mg + Cl₂ → MgCl₂ | Mg 0 → +2; Cl 0 → −1 |
| Chlorine with water | Cl₂ + H₂O ⇌ HCl + HClO | Cl 0 → −1 and 0 → +1 |
| Cold, dilute NaOH | Cl₂ + 2NaOH → NaCl + NaClO + H₂O | Cl 0 → −1 and 0 → +1 |
| Hot alkali | 3Cl₂ + 6NaOH → 5NaCl + NaClO₃ + 3H₂O | Cl 0 → −1 and 0 → +5 |

**Disproportionation:** atoms of one element, all in the same reactant, go both up and down in oxidation number. Chlorine
with water, with cold alkali and with hot alkali are all examples.

**Names:** chloric(I) acid = HClO; sodium chlorate(I) = NaClO, the active substance in bleach; sodium
chlorate(V) = NaClO₃.

**Water treatment, in three points:**

- Chloric(I) acid kills bacteria, reducing waterborne disease.
- Chlorine is toxic, so the dose must be controlled.
- By-products: chlorine may combine with organic material to give chlorinated organics, some possibly harmful.

**Analogous reactions:** swap the halogen or the metal and keep the pattern. Iodine with sodium gives NaI;
bromine with cold potassium hydroxide gives KBr + KBrO + H₂O; bromine with water gives HBr + HBrO.

**Worked reminder: checking a hot-alkali equation.** In 3Cl₂ + 6NaOH, there are six chlorine atoms. Five
gain one electron each (5e⁻ gained); one loses five electrons in going to +5 (5e⁻ lost). Balanced.

## 8.27 Halides and hydrogen halides

### With concentrated sulfuric acid

| Solid | Products | Is H₂SO₄ reduced? | What you see |
|---|---|---|---|
| NaCl or KCl | HCl + NaHSO₄ (or KHSO₄) | no | steamy fumes |
| NaBr or KBr | HBr, then Br₂ + SO₂ + H₂O | yes, S +6 → +4 | steamy fumes, orange-brown vapour |
| NaI or KI | HI, then I₂ with SO₂, S and H₂S | yes, S +6 → +4, 0, −2 | purple vapour, black solid, yellow solid, bad-egg smell |

Reducing ability: HCl < HBr < HI. The larger the halide, the further its outer electrons are from the
nucleus, so the more easily it loses them.

**Method in steps: any redox step with sulfuric acid**

1. Write the oxidation number of S before (+6) and after (+4 in SO₂, 0 in S, −2 in H₂S).
2. Electrons gained by one S = the change (2, 6 or 8).
3. Each halide ion loses one electron, so you need that many HX per H₂SO₄.
4. Balance H and O with water.

**Worked reminder: bromide giving sulfur dioxide.** Sulfur goes +6 → +4, gaining 2 electrons. Two bromide
ions each lose one electron, so two HBr are needed per H₂SO₄: 2HBr + H₂SO₄ → Br₂ + SO₂ + 2H₂O. Check:
hydrogen 4 on each side, oxygen 4 on each side. Bromide cannot go further and make sulfur or hydrogen
sulfide, because HBr is a weaker reducing agent than HI.

### With silver nitrate

Acidify with dilute nitric acid (removes carbonate), then add silver nitrate: Ag⁺(aq) + X⁻(aq) → AgX(s).

| Ion | Colour | Dilute NH₃ | Concentrated NH₃ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cl⁻ | white | dissolves | dissolves |
| Br⁻ | cream | does not dissolve (or only slightly) | dissolves |
| I⁻ | yellow | does not dissolve | does not dissolve |

### Hydrogen halides

- With ammonia gas: HX + NH₃ → NH₄X, a white smoke (solid).
- With water: HX + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + X⁻, giving an acidic solution; this is why they fume in moist air.

## 8.28 Prediction rules

| | Fluorine | Astatine |
|---|---|---|
| State | gas, lowest boiling temperature | solid, highest melting temperature |
| Electronegativity | highest | lowest |
| Oxidising power | strongest; displaces all others | weakest; displaced by all others |
| Silver halide | -- | dark precipitate, insoluble in concentrated NH₃ |
| Hydrogen halide as reducing agent | weakest | strongest |

Watch fluorine: as the most electronegative element it is −1 in all its compounds, so it cannot form
chlorate-type ions with positive oxidation numbers.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Cold, dilute alkali vs hot alkali:** chlorate(I), oxidation number +1, versus chlorate(V), +5.
- **Halogen vs halide:** the halogen (Cl₂) is the oxidising agent; the halide ion (Cl⁻) is the reducing agent.
- **Oxidising power of halogens vs reducing power of halides:** the first decreases down the group, the
  second increases.
- **Acid–base step vs redox step:** forming HX from a solid halide is acid–base; any later step that
  reduces sulfur is redox.
- **Inside a molecule vs between molecules:** boiling breaks London forces; the X–X bond stays intact.

## Quick self-test

Relative atomic masses for this test: Na 23.0, K 39.1, Cl 35.5, Br 79.9, Ag 107.9. Gas molar volume: 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹.

1. State the colour of bromine dissolved in cyclohexane.
2. Write the ionic equation for chlorine reacting with aqueous iodide ions.
3. Give the oxidation number of chlorine in NaClO₃.
4. Why does iodine have a higher boiling temperature than chlorine?
5. Name the precipitate formed when acidified silver nitrate is added to potassium bromide, and give its colour.
6. Which hydrogen halide reduces concentrated sulfuric acid to hydrogen sulfide?
7. Calculate the mass of sodium chloride formed from 0.920 g of sodium and excess chlorine.
8. What volume of chlorine, in cm³ at room temperature and pressure, is needed to make 0.0300 mol of NaClO with cold, dilute sodium hydroxide?
9. The same 0.0300 mol of chlorine is passed into hot sodium hydroxide instead. How many moles of NaClO₃ form?
10. A solution contains 0.595 g of potassium bromide. Calculate the mass of silver bromide precipitated with excess silver nitrate.
11. Predict whether astatine will displace iodine from potassium iodide solution.

### Answers

1. Orange (to red-brown).
2. Cl₂ + 2I⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + I₂.
3. +5.
4. I₂ has more electrons, so stronger London forces between molecules need more energy to overcome.
5. Silver bromide, AgBr; cream.
6. Hydrogen iodide, HI.
7. n(Na) = 0.920 ÷ 23.0 = 0.0400 mol = n(NaCl); mass = 0.0400 × 58.5 = **2.34 g**.
8. Cl₂ : NaClO is 1 : 1, so 0.0300 mol Cl₂; volume = 0.0300 × 24 000 = **720 cm³**.
9. 3Cl₂ gives 1NaClO₃, so 0.0300 ÷ 3 = **0.0100 mol**.
10. Mr(KBr) = 119.0, n = 0.595 ÷ 119.0 = 0.00500 mol; Mr(AgBr) = 187.8; mass = 0.00500 × 187.8 = **0.939 g**.
11. No. Astatine is a weaker oxidising agent than iodine.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "chlorine is reduced" for the reaction with water, without the oxidation to +1.
- Giving +5 for the cold-alkali product or +1 for the hot-alkali product.
- Describing iodine in a non-polar solvent as brown rather than violet.
- Writing Cl⁻ + Br₂ as a reaction that happens; bromine cannot oxidise chloride.
- Explaining boiling temperatures with bond enthalpy instead of London forces.
- Missing the first acid–base step when writing products of halides with concentrated sulfuric acid.
- Listing observations for sodium iodide without the bad-egg smell or the yellow solid.
- Forgetting that nitric acid, not hydrochloric acid, is added before silver nitrate.
- Saying silver bromide dissolves in dilute ammonia.
