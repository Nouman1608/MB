This guide goes deep on sub-topic **3(c) The electromagnetic spectrum**,
statements 3.10 to 3.13 of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in
Physics (4PH1), Issue 4. None of the statements carries a "P", so all can
be assessed on both papers. For Topic 3 as a whole, see the
[Waves study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-physics-waves/).

## What the specification asks

- **3.10** know that light is part of a continuous electromagnetic
  spectrum that includes radio, microwave, infrared, visible,
  ultraviolet, x-ray and gamma ray radiations, and that all these waves
  travel at the same speed in free space
- **3.11** know the order of the electromagnetic spectrum in terms of
  decreasing wavelength and increasing frequency, including the colours of
  the visible spectrum
- **3.12** explain some of the uses of electromagnetic radiations: radio
  waves for broadcasting and communications; microwaves for cooking and
  satellite transmissions; infrared for heaters and night vision
  equipment; visible light for optical fibres and photography;
  ultraviolet for fluorescent lamps; x-rays for observing the internal
  structure of objects and materials, including for medical applications;
  gamma rays for sterilising food and medical equipment
- **3.13** explain the detrimental effects of excessive exposure of the
  human body to electromagnetic waves (microwaves: internal heating of
  body tissue; infrared: skin burns; ultraviolet: damage to surface cells
  and blindness; gamma rays: cancer, mutation) and describe simple
  protective measures against the risks

## A continuous spectrum with one speed (3.10)

Electromagnetic (EM) waves are transverse waves that can travel through a
vacuum. They form a **continuous** spectrum: there are no gaps between the
named groups, which merge into one another. The names are labels for
ranges of wavelength.

All EM waves travel at the **same speed in free space** (a vacuum),
3.0 × 10⁸ m/s. Air slows them only very slightly, so the same value is
used in air. Because v is fixed, v = f × λ means that a shorter
wavelength always goes with a higher frequency.

## The order (3.11)

In order of **decreasing wavelength** and **increasing frequency**:

| Region | Typical wavelength | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| radio waves | metres to kilometres | longest wavelength, lowest frequency |
| microwaves | millimetres to centimetres | |
| infrared | micrometres up to about a millimetre | emitted by warm and hot objects |
| visible light | about 400 nm (violet) to 700 nm (red) | the only part the eye detects |
| ultraviolet | below about 400 nm | |
| x-rays | about 10⁻⁸ to 10⁻¹¹ m | |
| gamma rays | shorter still | shortest wavelength, highest frequency |

The boundaries are approximate, and x-rays and gamma rays overlap in
wavelength; the names partly reflect how they are produced. You need the
order, not the numbers.

The **visible spectrum**, in order of decreasing wavelength: red, orange,
yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet. Red is next to infrared; violet is
next to ultraviolet. A mnemonic for the whole spectrum from radio to
gamma helps, as does remembering that "infra" means below (red) and
"ultra" means beyond (violet).

**Worked example 1.** A radio wave has a wavelength of 1500 m, and an
x-ray has a wavelength of 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ m. Compare their frequencies.

```
radio: f = v / λ = 3.0 × 10⁸ / 1500 = 2.0 × 10⁵ Hz
x-ray: f = 3.0 × 10⁸ / 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ = 3.0 × 10¹⁸ Hz
```

The x-ray frequency is 1.5 × 10¹³ times larger, because its wavelength is
that many times shorter.

## Uses and why each works (3.12)

The specification uses the command word **explain**, so link each use to
a property.

- **Radio waves: broadcasting and communications.** They travel long
  distances through the atmosphere and can be made to carry information
  (sound, pictures, data) by varying the wave.
- **Microwaves: cooking.** Microwaves penetrate a few centimetres into
  food and are absorbed by water molecules, which gain energy, heating the
  food from inside.
- **Microwaves: satellite transmissions.** They pass through the
  atmosphere, including cloud, to and from satellites in orbit, and can
  be sent as narrow beams to a dish.
- **Infrared: heaters.** Hot objects emit infrared, and objects that
  absorb it warm up, so an electric heater or grill transfers energy as
  infrared.
- **Infrared: night vision equipment.** Warm bodies emit more infrared
  than cooler surroundings; night vision cameras detect this, so people
  and animals show up in the dark.
- **Visible light: optical fibres.** Light travels along glass fibres by
  total internal reflection, carrying information as pulses of light, for
  example in endoscopes and data cables.
- **Visible light: photography.** A camera's lens focuses visible light
  onto a sensor (or film) that responds to it to record an image.
- **Ultraviolet: fluorescent lamps.** Inside the tube, ultraviolet is
  produced; a coating on the inside of the glass absorbs the ultraviolet
  and emits visible light.
- **X-rays: internal structure.** X-rays pass through soft tissue but are
  absorbed by denser materials such as bone or metal. A detector behind
  the body records where they pass and where they are stopped, giving an
  image of bones, and the same principle is used to check welds or
  baggage.
- **Gamma rays: sterilising food and medical equipment.** Gamma rays
  penetrate packaging and kill bacteria and other microorganisms, so
  equipment can be sterilised after it is sealed, without heating it.

## Harmful effects and protection (3.13)

| Radiation | Effect of excessive exposure | Simple protective measures |
|---|---|---|
| microwaves | internal heating of body tissue | ovens have a metal case and a metal mesh in the door that stop microwaves escaping; the oven switches off when the door opens |
| infrared | skin burns | keep a safe distance from heaters; use guards on heaters; heat-resistant gloves near hot objects |
| ultraviolet | damage to surface cells (sunburn, skin cancer risk) and blindness | sunscreen, covering clothing, hats, UV-blocking sunglasses or goggles; limit time in strong sunlight |
| gamma rays | cancer and mutation (damage to DNA in cells) | lead or thick concrete shielding; keep distance; limit exposure time; monitor dose with badges |

X-rays have similar effects to gamma rays, which is why radiographers
stand behind shielding and patients' exposure is kept small.

The pattern is that, broadly, higher-frequency radiation is more harmful:
ultraviolet affects surface cells, while x-rays and gamma rays penetrate
the body and can damage cells inside it.

**Annotated model answer.** *Explain why a person working with a
gamma-ray steriliser stands behind thick lead walls. [2]*

"Gamma rays can cause cancer and mutation by damaging cells [1]. Lead
absorbs most of the gamma radiation, so the dose reaching the worker is
much smaller [1]."

*Why it scores:* it names the specific harm from 3.13 and links the
measure to how it reduces exposure.

## Common mistakes

- Saying EM waves travel at different speeds in a vacuum.
- Reversing the order: radio has the **longest** wavelength and the
  **lowest** frequency.
- Placing violet next to infrared, or red next to ultraviolet.
- Attaching a use to the wrong radiation; learn each radiation with the
  uses that 3.12 names for it.
- Writing "radiation is dangerous" without naming the specific effect for
  that radiation.
- Saying microwaves "heat food by radiation" without mentioning absorption
  by water molecules.

## Exam technique with command words

- **State** the order: write all seven in a line with an arrow labelled
  "increasing frequency".
- **Explain** a use: name the property that makes the radiation suitable.
- **Describe** a protective measure: say what it is and how it reduces
  exposure.
- **Calculate**: use v = f × λ with v = 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s and standard form.
