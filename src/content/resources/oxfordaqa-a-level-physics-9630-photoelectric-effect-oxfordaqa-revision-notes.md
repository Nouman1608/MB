These notes condense section **3.5.10 Photoelectric effect** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630) specification: the "photon model of electromagnetic radiation, the Planck constant", E = hf = hc/λ, threshold frequency and its "photon explanation", "work function ϕ, stopping potential" and hf = ϕ + Ek(max). The experimental determination of stopping potential is not required.

For full explanations and worked examples, read the [photoelectric effect study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-photoelectric-effect-oxfordaqa/). Test yourself with the [photoelectric effect practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-photoelectric-effect-oxfordaqa-practice/).

Data: h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s; c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹; e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C; electron mass 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg.

## Core recall

- Electromagnetic radiation is emitted and absorbed in photons of energy E = hf = hc/λ.
- Frequency sets the energy per photon; intensity sets the number of photons per second.
- One photon gives all its energy to one electron.
- Below the threshold frequency f₀ no electrons are emitted, however intense the light.
- Above f₀, emission is immediate.
- hf = ϕ + Ek(max). Electrons from below the surface emerge with less than Ek(max).
- Stopping potential: eVs = Ek(max).
- Increasing intensity (above f₀) increases the photocurrent but not Ek(max).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Photon | A quantum (packet) of electromagnetic energy, E = hf |
| Planck constant h | 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s; photon energy divided by frequency |
| Photoelectron | An electron emitted from a surface by absorbing a photon |
| Work function ϕ | Minimum energy an electron needs to escape from the surface of the metal |
| Threshold frequency f₀ | Minimum frequency for photoelectric emission; f₀ = ϕ/h |
| Threshold wavelength | Maximum wavelength for emission; λ₀ = hc/ϕ |
| Ek(max) | Kinetic energy of the fastest photoelectrons |
| Stopping potential Vs | Potential difference that just stops the fastest photoelectrons; eVs = Ek(max) |

## Equations

| Equation | Use |
|---|---|
| E = hf = hc/λ | Photon energy from frequency or wavelength |
| N = P/E | Photons per second from a source of power P |
| f₀ = ϕ/h | Threshold frequency from work function |
| hf = ϕ + Ek(max) | Photoelectric equation |
| eVs = Ek(max) | Linking stopping potential to Ek(max) |
| Ek(max) = ½mv(max)² | Maximum speed of photoelectrons |

## The graph of Ek(max) against f

- Straight line, gradient = h (the same for every metal).
- f-axis intercept = f₀.
- Extrapolated Ek-axis intercept = −ϕ.
- Different metals give parallel lines; a larger work function shifts the line to the right.

## What happens when...

| Change | Ek(max) | Number of photoelectrons per second |
|---|---|---|
| Intensity increased (f above f₀) | Unchanged | Increases |
| Frequency increased (same photon rate) | Increases by h × Δf | Unchanged |
| Frequency reduced below f₀ | No emission | Zero |
| Metal with larger ϕ (same light) | Smaller | Depends on whether f is still above f₀ |

## Quick reminders with numbers

- 300 nm on ϕ = 2.3 eV: photon 4.14 eV, Ek(max) = 1.84 eV, Vs = 1.84 V.
- Halving λ to 150 nm: photon 8.29 eV, Ek(max) = 5.99 eV (not doubled).
- ϕ = 2.3 eV: f₀ = 5.55 × 10¹⁴ Hz, threshold wavelength 540 nm.
- A 5.0 mW, 650 nm laser emits 1.6 × 10¹⁶ photons per second.

## Exam traps

- "Brighter light gives faster electrons" is wrong: it gives more electrons.
- Energy cannot be accumulated from several photons by one electron.
- Ek(max) is a maximum; most photoelectrons have less.
- Doubling f does not double Ek(max).
- Keep all terms in J or all in eV.
- The threshold is a frequency (or wavelength), not an intensity.
- You will not be asked to describe an experiment to measure the stopping potential.

## Self-test

1. Calculate the energy of a photon of wavelength 500 nm, in J and in eV.
2. Define work function.
3. A metal has ϕ = 4.0 eV. Find its threshold frequency.
4. Photons of 6.2 eV hit a metal with ϕ = 4.0 eV. Find Ek(max) and the stopping potential.
5. Why does very intense red light not release electrons from zinc when weak ultraviolet does?
6. What is the gradient of a graph of Ek(max) against frequency?
7. The intensity of light above f₀ is halved. What happens to Ek(max) and to the current?
8. A source of 1.0 mW emits photons of 3.0 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. How many per second?
9. Why do photoelectrons have a range of kinetic energies?
10. Light of frequency 1.2 × 10¹⁵ Hz falls on a metal with f₀ = 8.0 × 10¹⁴ Hz. Find Ek(max).
11. Two metals are tested. How do their Ek(max)-f lines compare?
12. A metal's threshold wavelength is 620 nm. Find its work function in eV.

### Answers

1. E = 1.989 × 10⁻²⁵/500 × 10⁻⁹ = **3.98 × 10⁻¹⁹ J** = **2.49 eV**.
2. The **minimum energy** an electron needs to escape from the surface of the metal.
3. f₀ = 4.0 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹/6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ = **9.65 × 10¹⁴ Hz**.
4. Ek(max) = **2.2 eV** (3.52 × 10⁻¹⁹ J); Vs = **2.2 V**.
5. Each red photon has **less energy than the work function**, and one electron absorbs only one photon; each ultraviolet photon has enough.
6. The **Planck constant**, h.
7. Ek(max) is **unchanged**; the current **halves**.
8. N = 1.0 × 10⁻³/3.0 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **3.3 × 10¹⁵ per second**.
9. Electrons from below the surface **lose energy** before escaping, so only surface electrons leave with Ek(max).
10. Ek(max) = h(f − f₀) = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 4.0 × 10¹⁴ = **2.65 × 10⁻¹⁹ J** (1.66 eV).
11. **Parallel** lines (gradient h), with different intercepts.
12. ϕ = hc/λ₀ = 1.989 × 10⁻²⁵/620 × 10⁻⁹ = 3.21 × 10⁻¹⁹ J = **2.0 eV**.
