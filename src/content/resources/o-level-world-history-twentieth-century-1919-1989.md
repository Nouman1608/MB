This guide covers **Core Content Option B: The Twentieth Century,
1919-1989**, one of two Core Content options in Cambridge O Level
World History 2147, 2024-2026 series. As independently confirmed
against 2147's own specification PDF, it shares an identical set of
Core content options with Cambridge IGCSE World History (0470).

## Where this fits in 2147

Candidates study one Core Content option (either Option A: The
Nineteenth Century, 1848-1914, covered separately, or Option B) plus
one Depth Study. Option B traces the international system from the
end of the First World War through to the end of the Cold War era --
the peace settlement, the League of Nations, the causes of the Second
World War, and the origins, course and end of Cold War tension
between the United States and the USSR.

## Syllabus coverage

TWENTIETH CENTURY, 1919-1989**

- 2.1 Was the Treaty of Versailles Fair? — the terms of the Treaty,
  the aims of the "Big Three" negotiators, and assessments of its
  fairness and consequences
- 2.2 To What Extent Was the League of Nations a Success? — the
  League's aims and structure, its successes and failures in
  peacekeeping and international cooperation, and the reasons for its
  ultimate collapse
- 2.3 How Far Was Hitler's Foreign Policy to Blame for the Outbreak of
  War in 1939? — Nazi Germany's foreign policy aims and actions
  through the 1930s, weighed against other contributing causes of the
  Second World War
- 2.4 Who Was to Blame for the Cold War? — the breakdown of the
  wartime alliance between the USSR and the Western powers, and
  competing explanations for the origins of Cold War tension
- 2.5 How Effectively Did the United States Contain the Spread of
  Communism? — US Cold War policy and its effectiveness across
  different regions and periods
- 2.6 How Secure Was the USSR's Control Over Eastern Europe,
  1948-c.1989? — Soviet methods of control over Eastern Europe and
  the challenges to that control up to the end of the Cold War era

## How to approach it

Every question in this Core content option is framed as a judgement
("how far", "to what extent", "who was to blame") rather than a
request for narrative, so structure every answer around weighing
evidence for and against a position, and reach an explicit conclusion
-- a chronological retelling of events, however accurate, scores
poorly against this question style. Build a single timeline running
from 1919 to 1989 linking the six enquiries together, since they are
not isolated events but a connected chain: the terms of Versailles
feed into resentment that shapes Hitler's foreign policy; the
wartime alliance that defeats Hitler is the same alliance that breaks
down into Cold War. Assessed via Paper 1 (structured questions
drawing on Core content and one Depth study) and Paper 2 (document
questions based on one Depth study), so Option B is examined through
Paper 1 -- practise using source material and contextual knowledge
together, since Paper 1 rewards both.

## The Treaty of Versailles: fair or unfair?

The "Big Three" -- Woodrow Wilson (USA), David Lloyd George (Britain)
and Georges Clemenceau (France) -- held different aims at Versailles:
Wilson favoured a moderate peace based on his Fourteen Points, Lloyd
George sought a settlement firm enough to satisfy British public
opinion without permanently crippling Germany, and Clemenceau wanted
France secured against future German aggression. The resulting
Treaty imposed territorial loss, military restriction, reparations
and the "war guilt" clause on Germany -- terms that can be assessed
as either a reasonable response to the destruction of the First World
War, or as harsh and short-sighted terms that fuelled German
resentment.

## The League of Nations: success or failure?

| Area | Evidence of success | Evidence of failure |
|---|---|---|
| Peacekeeping | Resolved some minor territorial disputes | Failed to stop Japanese invasion of Manchuria, Italian invasion of Abyssinia |
| Structure | Promoted international cooperation on health, labour, refugees | Lacked its own army; relied on member states to enforce decisions |
| Membership | Founding members cooperated on technical issues | The USA never joined; key powers left or were excluded over time |

The League's fundamental weakness was structural: it could recommend
sanctions but had no independent means of enforcing them, which
became fatal once major powers chose to defy it in the 1930s.

## Causes of the Second World War

Hitler's foreign policy -- remilitarising the Rhineland, pursuing
Anschluss with Austria, the Sudetenland crisis and the invasion of
Poland -- is a central strand of this enquiry, but the syllabus
explicitly asks candidates to weigh this against other causes:
appeasement by Britain and France, the weaknesses of the League of
Nations already established above, and the wider failure of collective
security in the 1930s. A strong answer does not treat Hitler's
foreign policy in isolation but places it alongside these other
contributing factors before reaching a judgement.

## The Cold War: origins to the end of Soviet control

The wartime alliance between the USSR and the Western powers broke
down after 1945 over disagreements about the future of Eastern
Europe and Germany, with historians offering competing explanations
for who bears most responsibility. US containment policy (including
the Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan, and later interventions) is
assessed for its effectiveness across different Cold War contexts,
while Soviet control over Eastern Europe -- maintained through
political, military and economic means from 1948 -- faced growing
challenges through the Cold War period up to its end around 1989.

## Worked example

A question asks: "How far was the Treaty of Versailles responsible
for the outbreak of the Second World War?" A strong answer would
trace a causal chain -- the Treaty's terms created German resentment
that Hitler exploited, but would also weigh other causes (the Great
Depression, the failure of appeasement, the weakness of the League)
before reaching a supported conclusion about relative importance,
rather than treating Versailles as the sole cause.

## Common mistakes

Writing a narrative account of events instead of directly answering
the "how far" or "to what extent" judgement the question asks.
Treating the six enquiries as unconnected topics rather than as a
chronological chain of cause and consequence. Discussing Hitler's
foreign policy without weighing it against other causes of the
Second World War. Describing Cold War events without engaging with
the "who was to blame" debate the syllabus specifically raises.

## Quick revision checklist

- Explain the aims of the Big Three at Versailles and assess the
  fairness of the Treaty's terms.
- Weigh the League of Nations' successes against its structural
  weaknesses and key failures.
- Explain how far Hitler's foreign policy, versus other factors,
  caused war in 1939.
- Outline competing explanations for the origins of the Cold War.
- Assess the effectiveness of US containment policy.
- Explain how the USSR maintained control over Eastern Europe and the
  challenges to that control up to 1989.
