## Definitions to know

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Habitat | Place occupied by a community, species, population or organism: geographical location, physical location, ecosystem type |
| Abiotic factor | Non-living variable: temperature, light, water, salinity, pH, oxygen, wind, soil minerals |
| Adaptation | Inherited feature that improves survival and reproduction in a given environment |
| Range of tolerance | Span of values of an abiotic variable within which a species can survive |
| Limiting factor | Variable that restricts growth or distribution because it is furthest from the optimum |
| Biome | Group of ecosystems with similar communities because of similar abiotic conditions and convergent evolution |
| Convergent evolution | Unrelated species evolving similar features under similar selection pressures |

## B4.1.1 and B4.1.2: habitat and adaptation

- Describe a habitat with place plus ecosystem: "rocky upper shore, Atlantic coast, intertidal ecosystem".
- Habitat is where; niche is role. Do not mix them.
- Answer pattern for adaptations: abiotic problem, then feature, then how it helps.

### Marram grass on dunes

| Problem | Feature | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Dry, windy | Rolled leaves, sunken stomata, hairs | Humid air trapped, less transpiration |
| Dry, windy | Thick cuticle | Less evaporation |
| Deep water, loose sand | Long roots, rhizomes | Reaches water, anchors, spreads |
| Burial by sand | Grows upwards | Survives burial |

### Mangrove trees

| Problem | Feature | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Soft mud | Prop roots | Anchorage and support |
| Low oxygen in mud | Pneumatophores | Gas exchange for roots |
| Salt | Salt excluded or excreted | Tissue fluid not too salty |
| Tidal flooding | Viviparous seeds | Large seedlings establish in mud |

## B4.1.3 and B4.1.4: tolerance

Variables to quote: plants (light, temperature, soil pH, water, minerals, salinity); animals (temperature, water, dissolved oxygen, salinity, pH).

Sketch to learn (growth against an abiotic variable):

```
growth
  |         /\
  |        /  \
  |      _/    \_
  |  ___/        \___
  +--------------------- variable
     limit  optimum  limit
     (zones of stress either side)
```

- Narrow range of tolerance: restricted to stable habitats, often an indicator of conditions.
- Wide range: many habitats.
- Survival range is wider than the range for good growth or breeding.

### Transect method in steps

1. Choose a natural or semi-natural habitat and a clear environmental gradient.
2. Lay a line or belt transect.
3. At fixed intervals, record species abundance in quadrats.
4. At the same points, measure the abiotic variable (sensors for temperature, light intensity and pH).
5. Plot a graph or compare tables and describe the correlation.
6. State the limitation: correlation is not causation; repeat transects and control other variables.

Worked reminder: frequency falls from 18 to 5 quadrats out of 20. Percentage change = (5 − 18) ÷ 18 × 100 = −72.2 %.

## B4.1.5: coral reefs

| Factor | Requirement | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Depth | Shallow | Light for photosynthetic algae in coral tissue |
| Clarity | Clear water | Light penetration, no smothering |
| Temperature | Warm, roughly 23 to 29 °C | Narrow tolerance; bleaching if too hot |
| Salinity | Stable, near open ocean | Coral cells cannot cope with large dilution |
| pH | Slightly alkaline, about 8.1 | Carbonate availability for calcium carbonate skeleton |

pH is a logarithmic scale. A fall from pH 8.1 to 7.9 is a 10^0.2 = 1.58-fold rise in hydrogen ion concentration, a 58 % increase.

## B4.1.6: biome distribution

- For a given temperature and rainfall, one ecosystem type is likely to develop.
- Graph: temperature on the horizontal axis, rainfall on the vertical axis, regions for each biome.
- Hot and wet: tropical forest. Hot and dry: hot desert. Cold and dry: tundra. Cold with more precipitation: taiga. Moderate temperature: temperate forest (wetter) or grassland (drier).
- Overlap areas: seasonality, soil and disturbance decide.

## B4.1.7: biome climates and convergence

| Biome | Climate summary |
|---|---|
| Tropical forest | Warm all year, very high rainfall |
| Temperate forest | Moderate temperatures, four seasons, moderate to high rainfall |
| Taiga | Long cold winters, short cool summers |
| Grassland | Seasonal, moderate rainfall, too dry for forest |
| Tundra | Very cold, short growing season, low precipitation |
| Hot desert | Very low rainfall, hot days |

Convergent evolution examples: cacti (Americas) and succulent *Euphorbia* (Africa); kangaroo rats and jerboas.

## Worked reminders

### Reading a tolerance table

A fish grows 0, 1.1, 2.6, 3.9, 3.1, 1.8, 0.4 and 0 mm per day at 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 28 and 32 °C (data supplied).

- Optimum: 16 °C.
- Limits for growth: 4 °C and 32 °C, a range of 28 °C.
- Drop from optimum to 24 °C: (3.9 − 1.8) ÷ 3.9 × 100 = 53.8 %.
- Shape: gradual rise, steeper fall, so high temperatures are the greater danger.

### Water loss from a rolled leaf

A flat leaf loses 12.0 mg of water per hour and a rolled one loses 4.5 mg per hour in the same air (data supplied). Reduction = (12.0 − 4.5) ÷ 12.0 × 100 = 62.5 %. Explanation: the rolled leaf holds a pocket of humid air, so the diffusion gradient from leaf to air is shallower.

### A model explanation

Question: explain how a named plant is adapted to a hot desert.

Answer: the saguaro cactus has a pleated stem (feature) that swells when it takes up rain (stores water for dry months) [1]. Its shallow, widely spread roots absorb surface water quickly after rain [1]. Spines reduce air movement and protect against grazing, and stomata open at night when it is cooler, so less water leaves by transpiration [1].

### Reading a biome graph

If a place has a mean annual temperature of 26 °C and 2,800 mm of rain a year (data supplied), it lies in the hot and very wet corner, so tropical forest is the likely biome. If it has 2 °C and 400 mm, it falls in the cold, low-precipitation area, which suggests taiga, and tundra is possible if the growing season is very short. State the two values, name the region and add that other factors can alter the outcome where regions overlap.

## Must-know distinctions

- Habitat versus niche.
- Optimum versus limit of tolerance.
- Correlation versus causation.
- Convergent evolution versus common ancestry.
- Biome (a type, worldwide) versus ecosystem (a particular place).

## Quick self-test

1. Define habitat.
2. Give two ways marram grass reduces water loss.
3. Why do mangrove roots need pneumatophores?
4. A species grows at 6 to 30 °C, best at 18 °C. State its limits and optimum.
5. Why is a transect better than random sampling for studying a gradient?
6. List the five coral reef conditions in B4.1.5.
7. Light halves every 5 m. What percentage remains at 15 m?
8. Which two climate variables define the biome graph axes?
9. Give two named desert animals and an adaptation of each.
10. How do cacti and succulent *Euphorbia* show convergent evolution?

### Answers

1. The place occupied by a community, species, population or single organism.
2. Rolled leaves trapping humid air; sunken stomata with hairs; thick waxy cuticle (any two).
3. Waterlogged mud has little oxygen, so aerial roots allow gas exchange.
4. Limits 6 °C and 30 °C; optimum 18 °C.
5. It samples along the gradient at regular intervals, so change in species abundance can be matched to change in the abiotic variable.
6. Depth, pH, salinity, clarity, temperature.
7. Three halvings: 100 × (1/2)³ = 12.5 %.
8. Temperature (horizontal axis) and rainfall (vertical axis).
9. Kangaroo rat: concentrated urine; fennec fox: large ears to lose heat.
10. Unrelated plants in different continents evolved similar succulent, spiny, water-storing stems under similar dry conditions.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a feature without saying how it helps.
- Failing to name the abiotic factor that the adaptation deals with.
- Confusing habitat with niche.
- Calling the optimum the limit of tolerance.
- Concluding that pH causes a distribution change from a single transect.
- Forgetting to describe the method details, such as regular intervals and measuring the abiotic variable at each point.
- Leaving out clarity or salinity when listing coral reef conditions.
- Describing convergence as shared ancestry.
- Using unnamed organisms when the question asks for named species.
