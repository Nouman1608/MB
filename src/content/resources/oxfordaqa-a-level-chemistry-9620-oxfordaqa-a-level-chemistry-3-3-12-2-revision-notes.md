These notes condense **3.3.12.2 Biodegradability and disposal of polymers** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International A2 content, examined in Unit 4 and able to reappear in Unit 5. For explanations and worked examples read the [biodegradability and disposal study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-12-2/); then try the [biodegradability and disposal practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-12-2-practice/).

## The subtopic in four lines

1. Polyalkenes contain only non-polar C–C and C–H bonds, so they are chemically inert and non-biodegradable.
2. Polyesters and polyamides contain polar C=O groups in their links, so they can be hydrolysed and are biodegradable.
3. Disposal methods: landfill, incineration, mechanical recycling and chemical recycling, each with advantages and disadvantages.
4. You must explain why condensation polymers hydrolyse and polyalkenes do not.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning to learn |
|---|---|
| Biodegradable | Broken down by natural processes, such as micro-organisms, over time |
| Non-biodegradable | Not broken down naturally; persists in the environment |
| Chemically inert | Does not react with common reagents such as acids, alkalis or water |
| Hydrolysis | Breaking a bond by reaction with water, here the ester or amide link |
| Nucleophile | Electron pair donor; here H₂O or OH⁻ attacking a δ+ carbon |
| Incineration | Burning waste, often with energy recovery |
| Mechanical recycling | Melting and remoulding sorted polymer |
| Chemical recycling | Breaking a polymer down into monomers or other useful chemicals |

## The core explanation

| | Polyalkene | Polyester or polyamide |
|---|---|---|
| Bonds in backbone | C–C and C–H only | Also C=O and C–O or C–N in each link |
| Polarity | Non-polar | Polar C=O; carbonyl carbon δ+ |
| Site for nucleophile | None | δ+ carbonyl carbon |
| Hydrolysis | Not possible | Possible; faster with heat and acid or alkali |
| Biodegradable? | No | Yes (slowly) |

Four-mark recipe: polar C=O in the link → δ+ carbon → attacked by water or OH⁻ → polyalkene has only non-polar C–C/C–H, so no δ+ site.

## Hydrolysis products

| Polymer | With hot dilute acid | With hot aqueous NaOH |
|---|---|---|
| Polyester (diacid + diol) | Diacid + diol | Disodium salt of the diacid + diol |
| Polyamide (diacid + diamine) | Diacid + diammonium salt | Disodium salt of the diacid + diamine |
| Polyamide from one amino acid | Amino acid with –NH₃⁺ | Sodium salt of the amino acid (–COO⁻, –NH₂) |
| Terylene | Benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid + ethane-1,2-diol | Sodium benzene-1,4-dicarboxylate + ethane-1,2-diol |
| Nylon 6,6 | Hexanedioic acid + hexane-1,6-diammonium ion | Sodium hexanedioate + hexane-1,6-diamine |

Rule: in acid, amines are protonated; in alkali, acids are deprotonated.

## Disposal methods compared

| Method | Key advantage | Key disadvantage | Chemistry link |
|---|---|---|---|
| Landfill | Cheap, no sorting | Land use; polyalkenes persist | Non-biodegradable |
| Incineration | Volume reduced; energy recovered | CO₂ released; HCl from PVC must be removed | Combustion products |
| Mechanical recycling | Saves crude oil and energy | Needs collecting, sorting, cleaning; quality falls | Different polymers have different properties |
| Chemical recycling | Monomers or feedstock for new polymer | Energy and specialist plant needed | Condensation polymers can be hydrolysed back to monomers |

## Useful numbers (from Mr values)

- 1.00 kg poly(ethene) burned gives about 3.14 kg CO₂ (one CO₂ per CH₂ unit, Mr 14.0).
- 1.00 kg PVC burned gives about 584 g HCl (one HCl per CH₂CHCl unit, Mr 62.5).
- 1.00 kg Terylene (unit Mr 192.0) hydrolysed gives about 865 g benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid and 323 g ethane-1,2-diol.

## Evaluation checklist

- One advantage and one disadvantage for each method named.
- Each point linked to a property of the polymer.
- Point applied to the polymer in the question.
- A final judgement with a reason.

## Exam traps

- "Polyalkenes have weak bonds": wrong; they are strong and non-polar.
- "No reaction because saturated" without mentioning polarity: incomplete.
- Free acid given for alkaline hydrolysis, or free diamine for acid hydrolysis.
- Saying incineration produces no pollution, or forgetting HCl from chlorine-containing polymers.
- Saying recycling has no disadvantages.
- Listing methods with no chemistry.
- Saying biodegradable polymers break down quickly; hydrolysis in the environment is slow.

## Self-test

Answer without looking, then check below.

1. Why are polyalkenes described as chemically inert?
2. What feature of an ester link allows it to be hydrolysed?
3. Name two nucleophiles that can hydrolyse a polyamide.
4. Give the products of heating nylon 6,6 with aqueous sodium hydroxide.
5. Give the products of heating Terylene with dilute hydrochloric acid.
6. Why does acid hydrolysis of a polyamide give an ammonium salt?
7. Give one advantage and one disadvantage of landfill.
8. What toxic gas forms when PVC is incinerated?
9. Give one advantage of incineration with energy recovery.
10. Why must polymers be sorted before mechanical recycling?
11. What is chemical recycling?
12. Calculate the mass of CO₂ formed when 0.70 kg of poly(ethene) is burned completely.

### Answers

1. They contain only non-polar C–C and C–H bonds, so there is no δ+ or δ− site for reagents to attack.
2. Its polar C=O group makes the carbonyl carbon δ+, so nucleophiles attack it.
3. Water and hydroxide ions.
4. Sodium hexanedioate and hexane-1,6-diamine.
5. Benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid and ethane-1,2-diol.
6. The amine groups released are protonated by the acid.
7. Advantage: cheap, or no sorting needed. Disadvantage: uses land, or polyalkenes persist for a very long time.
8. Hydrogen chloride.
9. The volume of waste is greatly reduced, or the heat released generates electricity.
10. Different polymers have different properties, so mixed polymers give a poor-quality product.
11. Breaking polymers down into monomers or other useful chemicals, for example hydrolysing a polyester back to its monomers.
12. 700 ÷ 14.0 = 50.0 mol CH₂, so 50.0 mol CO₂ × 44.0 = 2200 g = 2.20 kg.
