These questions test the section 3.1 row on legal personnel and the judiciary: the roles and regulation of barristers, solicitors and legal executives, types of judge, the role of judges, and judicial independence.

---

## Section A

**1.** State **two** differences between the work of a solicitor and the work of a barrister. **[2]**

**2.** Identify the body that oversees the regulators of the legal profession and the Act that created it. **[2]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by the cab-rank rule. **[2]**

**4.** Name **two** superior judges and **two** inferior judges. **[4]**

**5.** Explain what is meant by immunity from suit, giving a case. **[2]**

**6.** Outline the route to qualifying as a chartered legal executive and **one** advantage of this route. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** Priya's solicitor failed to register her house purchase properly, causing her financial loss, and was rude when she complained. Advise Priya on the ways she might seek redress. **[6]**

**8.** Explain the role of judges in civil cases. **[5]**

**9.** Explain the role of a judge in a Crown Court trial. **[5]**

**10.** Explain why judicial independence is important. **[5]**

**11.** Explain how the Constitutional Reform Act 2005 strengthened the independence of the judiciary from the Executive. **[5]**

**12.** Evaluate how far the judiciary in England and Wales is independent. **[12]**

---

## Answers

**1.** Any two of: solicitors usually deal with clients directly, while barristers are usually instructed by a solicitor [1]; barristers specialise in advocacy and have rights of audience in all courts, while solicitors need a higher rights qualification for the higher courts; barristers are mostly self-employed in chambers, while solicitors usually work in firms [1].

**2.** The Legal Services Board [1], created by the Legal Services Act 2007 [1].

**3.** A self-employed barrister must accept a case in their field of practice, if available and offered a proper fee [1], regardless of the identity of the client or the nature of the case, so that unpopular clients can still obtain representation [1].

**4.** Superior: any two of Justices of the Supreme Court, Lord or Lady Justices of Appeal, High Court judges [1] [1]. Inferior: any two of circuit judges, recorders, district judges, district judges (magistrates' courts) [1] [1].

**5.** Judges cannot be sued for acts done in their judicial capacity in good faith, so they can decide cases without fear of personal litigation [1], as in *Sirros v Moore* (1975) [1].

**6.** Candidates take CILEx qualifications while in qualifying legal employment [1], and qualify as chartered legal executives once the academic and work-based requirements are met [1]. Advantage: no degree is required and candidates can earn while they learn, which widens access to the profession [1].

**7.** Priya should first use the firm's own complaints procedure [1]. If that fails, she can take a complaint about poor service to the Legal Ombudsman [1], which can order an apology, a refund of fees or compensation [1]. The failure to register the purchase and the rudeness may also be professional misconduct, which can be reported to the Solicitors Regulation Authority [1]; serious cases go to the Solicitors Disciplinary Tribunal, which can fine, suspend or strike off [1]. For her financial loss she could also sue the solicitor in the tort of negligence [1].

**8.** Judges manage cases under the Civil Procedure Rules, for example allocating a case to a track and setting a timetable [1]. They decide the facts and the law, since almost all civil cases are tried without a jury [1]. They give a reasoned judgment [1]. They award a remedy, such as damages or an injunction [1]. In the appeal courts they interpret statutes and create precedent [1].

**9.** The judge rules on points of law, such as the meaning of an offence, during the trial [1] and decides whether evidence is admissible [1]. The judge sums up the evidence and directs the jury on the law [1], and may direct an acquittal where there is no case to answer, but may never direct a conviction [1]. If the defendant is convicted, the judge passes sentence [1].

**10.** Independence upholds the rule of law, because no one, including the government, is above the law [1]. It reflects the separation of powers, preventing the Executive from controlling the courts [1]. It secures the right to a fair hearing before an independent and impartial tribunal under Article 6 ECHR [1]. It protects citizens when they challenge the state, for example in judicial review [1]. It maintains public confidence that cases are decided on the law and evidence alone [1].

**11.** Section 3 places a duty on ministers to uphold the continued independence of the judiciary [1]. The Lord Chancellor is no longer a judge or head of the judiciary; the Lord Chief Justice became head of the judiciary [1]. The Judicial Appointments Commission was created to select judges on merit, reducing ministerial influence over appointments [1]. The Act created the Supreme Court, separating the final court from the House of Lords [1]. The Lord Chancellor must take an oath to respect the rule of law and defend judicial independence [1].

**12.** Levels-based. **Independence is protected:** superior judges have security of tenure and can be removed only by the monarch on an address of both Houses [1]. Judges have immunity from suit (*Sirros v Moore*, 1975) [1]. Salaries are paid from the Consolidated Fund and are not subject to annual debate [1]. Section 3 Constitutional Reform Act 2005 requires ministers to uphold independence [1], and the Judicial Appointments Commission selects on merit [1]. The Supreme Court has been separate from Parliament since 2009 [1]. Judges decide against the government, as in the two *Miller* cases (2017, 2019) [1]. **Limits:** inferior judges have weaker security of tenure [1]. The Lord Chancellor, a government minister, retains a role in appointments [1]. Judges have faced media and political pressure after controversial decisions [1]. Impartiality can be compromised, as in *Re Pinochet* (1999), although the system corrected it [1]. A reasoned conclusion, for example that independence is strongly protected in law, with remaining risks mainly from political pressure [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing the professions without distinguishing their roles.
- Naming the Legal Ombudsman when the issue is misconduct, not service.
- Listing methods of independence without saying how each protects it.
- Giving no conclusion in a 12-mark evaluation.
