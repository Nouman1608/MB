## The cascade: objectives, strategy, functional strategies, tactics

In a business of any size, one corporate strategy is turned into a strategy for each function, and each function then chooses its tactics.

**Example: Brightwater Gyms**, 12 gyms, 18,000 members.

- **Corporate objective:** raise membership to 24,000 within three years (a rise of 6,000, or 33%).
- **Corporate strategy:** become the leading budget gym chain in the region by opening low-cost 24-hour sites.
- **Functional strategies:**
  - Marketing: target students and shift workers with low monthly fees and no joining fee.
  - Operations: open four new unstaffed sites with app-controlled entry.
  - Human resources: replace front-desk roles with a smaller team of mobile staff covering several sites.
  - Finance: fund the new sites with a mix of retained profit and a five-year loan.
- **Tactics:** a two-week free trial in September, student discounts during freshers' week, a referral reward of one free month.

The relationship runs both ways. Objectives set the destination; strategy chooses the route; tactics are the steps. Results from tactics feed back upwards: if the free trial brings sign-ups but few people stay, the problem may be the strategy (the budget offer does not suit the market), not the tactic.

### Telling strategy from tactics

| Feature | Strategy | Tactics |
|---|---|---|
| Time frame | Long term | Short term |
| Scope | Whole business or a whole function | One action, product or period |
| Cost | High, often needs investment | Lower |
| Reversibility | Hard and costly to reverse | Easy to change |
| Who decides | Directors and senior managers | Middle managers and teams |

A useful check: if the business could stop it next month with little loss, it is a tactic.

## Evaluating a current or proposed strategy: three tests

A widely used approach, which the specification does not name, judges a strategy against three tests.

1. **Suitability:** does it fit the objectives, the market and the business's strengths?
2. **Feasibility:** does the business have the money, skills, time and capacity to carry it out?
3. **Acceptability:** will key stakeholders (owners, lenders, staff, customers) accept the returns, risks and changes involved?

### Worked example: evaluating Brightwater's strategy

Each new site costs £400,000 to fit out, so four sites cost **£1.6 million**. Brightwater has £700,000 of retained profit and plans to borrow the remaining **£900,000**. Each site has fixed costs of £22,000 a month. A member pays £20 a month; the variable cost per member is £4 a month.

- Contribution per member per month = 20 − 4 = **£16**.
- Break-even members per site = 22,000 ÷ 16 = **1,375**.
- The objective needs 6,000 extra members; across four sites that is 1,500 per site, only 125 above break-even.
- Monthly profit per site at 1,500 members = (1,500 × 16) − 22,000 = **£2,000**, or £8,000 a month across the four sites.

**Suitability.** The budget strategy fits the growth objective and a market where students and shift workers want low prices and long opening hours. But if some new members come from Brightwater's existing gyms, the net gain is smaller.

**Feasibility.** The business has done openings before, but four at once is a big step. The loan is large compared with £8,000 a month of planned profit from the new sites.

**Acceptability.** Lenders may worry that the margin of safety is small: at 1,500 members the margin is 125, or about 8% of planned membership. Front-desk staff will resist the loss of their roles.

**Judgement.** The strategy is suitable but the risk is high, because a shortfall of just over 8% in membership turns each new site into a loss. Its success depends most on the accuracy of the membership forecast.

## Altering the strategy

When you recommend and justify how a business could alter its strategy, change the part that the evaluation shows is weakest. For Brightwater, the weak point is risk, not direction. A justified alteration:

> Open two sites in year 1 rather than four, funded mainly from retained profit, and open the remaining two only if the first pair reach 1,400 members within nine months. This keeps the budget strategy, which suits the market, but cuts the borrowing and lets the business test the membership forecast before committing all £1.6 million. The trade-off is slower growth: the 24,000 target may slip by about a year, and a rival could take the best locations first.

The structure is: the change, why it addresses the weakness, what is kept, the trade-off.

## Changing strategy: impact and importance

Changing strategy affects stakeholders in different ways:

- **Owners:** may lose money already spent (a sunk cost), but avoid larger losses from a failing strategy.
- **Employees:** retraining, new roles or redundancy; uncertainty can lower motivation.
- **Customers:** the offer may change; loyal customers may leave if the change is sudden.
- **Suppliers and lenders:** contracts may change; lenders may review their risk.

The importance of the change depends on:

- **How serious the problem is.** A strategy that threatens survival must change; one that is slightly behind target may only need new tactics.
- **How fast the market is moving.** In a fast-changing market, delay is costly.
- **How reversible the change is.** A change that commits new capital is harder to undo than one that redirects marketing.
- **Credibility.** Frequent changes confuse staff and customers.

## Why a business must plan its implementation

A strategy is only as good as its execution. An implementation plan normally sets out:

| Element | Example for Brightwater's first two sites |
|---|---|
| Actions and sequence | Sign leases, fit out, install entry system, recruit mobile team, launch |
| Responsibility | Operations director for fit-out; marketing manager for the launch |
| Timeline and milestones | Leases signed by month 2; first site open by month 6 |
| Resources and budget | £800,000 capital; £60,000 launch budget |
| Measures of success | Members per site at months 3, 6 and 9 |
| Communication | Staff briefings before roles change |
| Risks and fallback | If the entry system is late, open with temporary staff cover |

### Why strategies fail at implementation

- **Under-resourcing:** cash or staff time runs out before the strategy is complete.
- **Unclear responsibility:** tasks fall between departments.
- **Resistance to change:** staff who fear for their jobs slow the change down.
- **Poor sequencing:** marketing launches before the product or site is ready.
- **No measures:** managers do not notice the strategy is off course until it is too late.

Planning reduces these risks, but has costs: management time and the danger of a rigid plan that cannot respond to a rival's move.

## Recommending a strategy

A strong recommendation names the strategy, links it to the objective and the case evidence, compares it with at least one alternative, identifies the main risk and states the condition that would change the advice. Use figures from the case where they exist.

## Common mistakes

- Giving a tactic (a discount, an advert) as a strategy.
- Evaluating a strategy only on whether it fits the objective, ignoring feasibility and acceptability.
- Recommending a new strategy when the evidence points to a problem with tactics or implementation.
- Ignoring the cost of changing strategy, including sunk costs and staff uncertainty.

## Exam technique

"Evaluate" a strategy: test it, use the figures, give a judgement and say what it depends on. "Recommend and justify": choice, reasons, alternative, risk, condition. "Explain why a business needs to plan": give the reason and the consequence of not planning.
