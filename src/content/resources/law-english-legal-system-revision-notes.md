## Sources of law, in order of authority

1. **Legislation** — Acts of Parliament. Supreme; no court may set one aside.
2. **Delegated legislation** — statutory instruments, byelaws, Orders in Council, made under an enabling Act.
3. **Case law** — judicial decisions, operating through precedent.
4. **Equity** — discretionary remedies where damages are inadequate.

## The legislative process

```
First Reading    name and aims read; no debate
Second Reading   main debate on principles; vote
Committee Stage  clause-by-clause, 16-50 MPs
Report Stage     amendments reported back
Third Reading    final vote
Other House      whole process repeats
Royal Assent     becomes an Act
```

The Lords may **delay** but not permanently block; the Parliament Acts allow a Bill to proceed without them.

## Statutory interpretation

| Rule | What it does | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Literal | Ordinary meaning of words | Can produce absurd or harsh results |
| Golden | Departs from literal to avoid absurdity | Judges decide what counts as absurd |
| Mischief | Identifies the defect the Act was to remedy | Older, narrower |
| Purposive | Gives effect to Parliament's purpose | Now dominant; risks judicial law-making |

**Language rules:** *ejusdem generis* (general words take colour from the specific list), *expressio unius* (expressing one excludes others), *noscitur a sociis* (a word is known by its companions).

## Judicial precedent

- ***Stare decisis*** — stand by what has been decided.
- **Ratio decidendi** — the legal reasoning essential to the decision. **Binding.**
- **Obiter dicta** — said by the way. **Persuasive only.**

Escaping a precedent:

| Method | Effect | Who |
|---|---|---|
| **Distinguishing** | Material facts differ, so it doesn't apply | Any court |
| **Overruling** | Earlier decision declared wrong law | Higher court |
| **Reversing** | Outcome of the *same* case changed | Appellate court |

The **Practice Statement 1966** lets the Supreme Court depart from its own past decisions where right to do so.

## Exam traps

- Ratio binds; obiter persuades — never swap them.
- Distinguishing ≠ overruling.
- Delegated legislation is made **under authority granted by** Parliament, not by Parliament.
- The Practice Statement applies to the Supreme Court, not all courts.
- Setting out the interpretation rules without **applying** them to the facts loses most of the marks.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish ratio decidendi from obiter dicta.
2. Name the four approaches to statutory interpretation.
3. What are the two controls on delegated legislation?
4. A statute bans "cars, vans, lorries and other vehicles" from a park. Which language rule helps decide whether a bicycle is covered?
5. Which court may use the Practice Statement 1966?

**Answers:** 1. Ratio is the reasoning essential to the decision and is binding; obiter is any other judicial comment and is only persuasive. 2. Literal, golden, mischief, purposive. 3. Parliamentary (affirmative/negative resolution, scrutiny committees) and judicial (judicial review for *ultra vires* or unreasonableness). 4. *Ejusdem generis* — "other vehicles" takes its meaning from the motorised examples listed, so a bicycle is arguably excluded. 5. The Supreme Court.
