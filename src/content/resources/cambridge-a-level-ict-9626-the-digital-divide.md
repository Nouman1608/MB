This guide teaches **section 6.1 The digital divide** of Topic 6 in the Cambridge International AS & A Level Information Technology 9626 syllabus for 2025, 2026 and 2027. It is AS Level content, examined in Paper 1 Theory. The [digital divide overview](/resources/a-level-cambridge-ict-the-digital-divide/) gives a short summary; this page explains each part of the syllabus and shows how to build a developed answer. Use it with the [digital divide practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-ict-9626-the-digital-divide-practice/).

## What section 6.1 covers

| Syllabus point | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| What the digital divide is | Define it, including differences between people and regions in access to modern technology and information |
| Causes | Explain, including the availability of high and low performance computers and wireless connections |
| Effects | Describe inequality of access to internet services and technology, and ways of reducing the effects, including digital literacy teaching |
| Groups affected | Explain how age, location, education, socioeconomic status, accessibility barriers and the level of national development matter |

## What the digital divide is

The **digital divide** is the gap between people and regions that have **access to modern technology and information**, and those with **restricted or no access**. The technology includes smartphones, television, personal computers, tablet computers, laptop computers and internet access.

The divide is not only about owning a device. It also concerns:
- **Access:** whether a reliable internet connection is available.
- **Quality:** a slow connection or an old device gives a poorer experience than a fast one.
- **Skills:** whether the person knows how to use the technology and information safely and well.
- **Use:** whether the person can use it for useful purposes such as learning, work or services.

The divide exists **between countries** and **within** them. A rich city can have fibre broadband while a village 50 km away has none.

## Causes of the digital divide

### Availability of high and low performance computers

High performance computers are fast, can run modern software and cope with video calls, design software and large files. Low performance computers are older or cheaper, run slowly or cannot run new software at all.

- Households with **low income** cannot afford new or high performance equipment and may use an old device shared by several family members, or only a basic smartphone.
- Equipment needs **replacing every few years**, which is costly.
- People with only a low performance device may be **unable to take part** in online learning, remote work or modern services.

### Wireless connections

Many places have no cabled broadband, so people depend on **wireless connections**: mobile data (3G, 4G, 5G), Wi-Fi from a router, or satellite.

- **Rural and remote areas** may have no mobile signal, or a weak one. Providers do not install masts where there are few customers.
- **Mobile data is expensive** in many countries, and data limits restrict video, downloads and online lessons.
- Wireless connections are **slower and less reliable** in the countryside, in buildings with thick walls and during bad weather.
- **Satellite** connections can reach remote areas, but they cost more and have higher delay.

### Other causes

- **Cost** of devices, software and monthly internet charges.
- **Lack of infrastructure**: power supply, telephone lines, fibre cables and mobile masts.
- **Lack of education and skills**, so people do not know how to use technology.
- **Language**: much online content is in a small number of languages.
- **Disability**: devices and websites may not be accessible.
- **Government policy and laws**, such as restrictions on the internet.

## Groups affected by the digital divide

| Group | How the divide affects them |
|---|---|
| Different age groups | Older people may lack confidence or skills and may not see the benefit; young people tend to adopt technology quickly. Both can be affected in different ways, for example children who have no device for homework |
| Cities and rural areas | Cities have better infrastructure and competition between providers; rural areas may have slow or no connection |
| Different levels of education | People with limited education may find it hard to use online services, search for information or judge it critically |
| Different socioeconomic groups | Low-income households may not afford devices or data; high-income households have several devices and fast connections |
| People with accessibility barriers | People with learning difficulties or physical or sensory impairments may need specialist equipment or accessible websites, which can be costly or unavailable |
| More and less industrially developed nations | Less developed countries may have limited electricity, few networks and lower awareness of technology, while developed countries have widespread high speed internet |

Note that a person can belong to several groups at once. An older person on a low income in a rural area faces a combination of barriers.

## Effects of the digital divide

The syllabus identifies **inequality of access to all types of internet services and technology** as the key effect. In practice this includes:

- **Education:** students without devices or internet cannot do online research, submit homework online or follow online lessons. Their results may fall behind.
- **Employment:** many jobs are advertised and applied for online, and many jobs need digital skills. People without them have fewer opportunities and lower incomes.
- **Government and health services:** applying for benefits, booking appointments and paying tax are increasingly online. People without access may miss services they are entitled to.
- **Shopping and banking:** online prices are often lower, and bank branches are closing. People without access pay more or travel further.
- **Information and communication:** news, social contact, video calls with relatives and entertainment may be out of reach, leading to isolation.
- **Economic growth:** regions with poor access find it harder to attract businesses, so inequality grows over time.
- **Democracy and rights:** people may be less informed or less able to take part in public debate.

Each effect can make the next worse: poorer access leads to fewer skills, which leads to fewer jobs and less money to buy technology. This is a **cycle** of disadvantage.

## Reducing the effects of the digital divide

The syllabus names **digital literacy teaching**, which means teaching people the skills to use technology, find and judge information, and stay safe online. Other measures include:

- **Digital literacy classes** in schools, libraries and community centres, including for older people.
- **Free public access:** computers and Wi-Fi in libraries, schools and community centres.
- **Investment in infrastructure:** government and companies installing broadband cables, mobile masts and reliable electricity in rural and poorer areas.
- **Low-cost or subsidised devices and data:** social tariffs, recycled computers and school loan schemes.
- **Accessible design:** screen readers, voice control, captions and simple layouts for disabled users.
- **Content in local languages.**
- **Offline options**, such as keeping paper forms and telephone services for people who cannot use the internet.

### Evaluating these measures

| Measure | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|---|---|
| Digital literacy teaching | Builds lasting skills and confidence | Needs teachers, time and funding; does not help if there is no device or connection |
| Free public access | Quick and low cost for users | Limited opening hours and numbers of computers; less privacy; travel needed |
| Infrastructure investment | Fixes the root cause | Very expensive; takes years; may not be profitable for providers in remote areas |
| Subsidised devices and data | Directly removes the cost barrier | Costs the government; devices become old; may be misused |

## Worked example: a rural school

Ashgrove is a village school 40 km from the nearest town. About half the pupils have no computer at home, and mobile signal in the village is weak.

1. **Identify the divide:** pupils in a rural area and low-income families have restricted access to devices and the internet.
2. **Causes:** low performance or no computers at home (cost); poor wireless connections (rural location and weak masts).
3. **Effects:** pupils cannot use online homework tasks or research, so they fall behind pupils in the city, and may have fewer opportunities later.
4. **Measures:** the school opens its computer room after lessons; the local authority lends laptops; the school teaches search and safety skills; the council works with providers on a mobile mast.
5. **Judgement:** lending laptops and opening the computer room give a quick benefit, but a mast is needed for a lasting solution because laptops are of little use without a connection.

This pattern, from identifying the problem to a justified judgement, is what a high-mark answer needs.

## Common mistakes

- Defining the divide as "people who have computers and people who do not". It also includes **quality** of access, skills and use.
- Assuming that only poor countries are affected. It exists within rich countries too.
- Writing that rural people "do not want" technology. The cause is often infrastructure.
- Giving effects without saying who is disadvantaged and how.
- Describing digital literacy teaching as a complete solution. It does not help without access to equipment and a connection.
- Describing causes as if they apply to everyone. Link the cause to a group.

## Exam technique

- **Define** the divide with both parts: access, and the people and regions with and without it.
- **Explain** a cause with a chain: cause, then barrier, then effect on a group.
- **Describe** a group using a specific barrier. "Older people may lack the skills and confidence to use online banking" scores better than "older people struggle".
- **Evaluate** measures by giving an advantage and a limitation, then judging which is most effective for the scenario.
