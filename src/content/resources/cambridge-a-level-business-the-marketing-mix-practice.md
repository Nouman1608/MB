## Questions

**1.** **(a)** Define *product differentiation*. **[2]**
**(b)** Define *unique selling point (USP)*. **[2]**

**2.** Yarrow Glen Outdoors buys waterproof jackets at a unit cost of £46.00 and uses cost-plus pricing with a 35% mark-up.

**(a)** Calculate the selling price of a jacket. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain **one** limitation of cost-based pricing. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the total profit if 400 jackets are sold at this price. **[2]**

**3.** Quarrybank Cycles sells bicycles and also offers a repair service.

**(a)** Explain the difference between a good and a service, using the shop as your example. **[2]**
**(b)** Identify **one** tangible and **one** intangible attribute of the repair service. **[2]**

**4.** Orchard Stile Preserves is launching a smoked-plum jam in a market with several rival jams. Each jar costs £1.80 to make. The owner is choosing between two prices: £4.50, which she expects to sell 500 jars a month, and £3.20, which she expects to sell 1,100 jars a month.

**(a)** Calculate the monthly profit under each price. **[4]**
**(b)** Recommend which price the owner should choose, naming the pricing method it represents. **[4]**

**5.** Quarrybank Cycles also sells accessories. Taking market growth above 10% a year as "high", the data are:

| Product | Market growth | Own share | Largest rival share | Relative share |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lights | 21% | 30% | 15% | 2.0 |
| Locks | 2% | 28% | 14% | 2.0 |
| Panniers | 17% | 5% | 25% | ? |
| Bells | 1% | 4% | 20% | ? |

**(a)** Calculate the relative market share of Panniers and of Bells. **[2]**
**(b)** Classify each of the four products on the Boston Matrix. **[4]**
**(c)** Explain **one** marketing decision the shop could make using this analysis. **[3]**

**6.** Marrow Lane Games' board game had sales over five years of 1,200, 3,100, 5,600, 5,000 and 4,100 units.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in sales in year 5. **[2]**
**(b)** State the stage of the product life cycle in year 5 and give one reason. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain **two** extension strategies the business could use. **[4]**

**7.** Linnet Ford Skincare compares two promotions. Influencer posts cost £2,400 and win 96 new customers. An in-store sampling stand costs £1,800 and wins 45 new customers.

**(a)** Calculate the cost per new customer for each method. **[2]**
**(b)** Identify **one** other factor the owner should consider before choosing, and explain why. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain **one** benefit of digital promotion over printed leaflets. **[2]**

**8.** Explain **one** role of packaging and **one** role of branding in the promotion of a premium chocolate bar. **[4]**

**9.** A maker of handmade candles is considering selling only through its own website rather than through gift shops. Explain **one** advantage and **one** disadvantage of this for the business. **[4]**

**10.** Alderley Brook Playhouse has 100 seats. It sells 60 tickets to adults at £15. Student tickets at £9 would fill the other 40 seats, which otherwise stay empty.

**(a)** Define *dynamic pricing*. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the extra revenue from student tickets. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why the theatre must keep the two groups apart. **[2]**

**11.** Hollin Roasters makes coffee at a cost of £5.20 a bag and uses a 60% mark-up. It can sell online direct at its own price, with handling and delivery costs of £1.45 a bag, and expects 1,400 bags a month. A supermarket offers to buy at £6.90 a bag and expects 3,600 bags a month.

**(a)** Calculate the cost-plus selling price and the profit per bag. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the monthly profit from each channel. **[4]**
**(c)** Recommend a channel and justify your choice. **[6]**

**12.** **(a)** Explain why a business whose best-selling product is in maturity should invest in product development. **[3]**
**(b)** State what is meant by psychological pricing and give one example. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Making a product stand out from rivals [1] through features such as design, quality, service or image [1].
**(b)** A feature of a product that rivals do not offer [1] and that customers value [1].
*Examiner insight:* Give two separate ideas in each definition: what the firm does, and how the customer sees it (noticed, valued). Do not just repeat the term.

**2. (a)** Mark-up per jacket = £46.00 x 0.35 = £16.10 [1]. Price = £62.10 [1].
**(b)** It ignores demand and what customers will pay [1], so the price may be too high and cut sales, or too low and lose profit [1].
**(c)** 400 x £16.10 [1] = **£6,440** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show the line of working for each calculation. A limitation must be explained in context, not just named.

**3. (a)** A good is a physical item that can be touched and stored, such as a bicycle [1]. A service is an activity that is not stored, such as a repair [1].
**(b)** Tangible: the replacement parts fitted, or the written invoice [1]. Intangible: the shop's reputation for reliable work, or friendly staff [1].
*Examiner insight:* Use the business in the stem for the example. Generic examples with no link to the shop gain less credit on application.

**4. (a)** At £4.50: profit per jar £2.70 [1]; 500 x £2.70 = £1,350 [1]. At £3.20: profit per jar £1.40 [1]; 1,100 x £1.40 = £1,540 [1].
**(b)** Recommend £3.20, which is penetration pricing [1]. It earns £190 more a month (£1,540 against £1,350) [1] and suits a crowded market because the low price helps win trial and share [1]. A weakness is that the figures are estimates and rivals may respond, so the owner should review sales after launch [1].
*Examiner insight:* Back a recommendation with the figures given in the stem. A named method with no data behind it usually leaves the answer incomplete.

**5. (a)** Panniers: 5 / 25 = 0.2 [1]. Bells: 4 / 20 = 0.2 [1].
**(b)** Lights: star [1]. Locks: cash cow [1]. Panniers: question mark (problem child) [1]. Bells: dog [1].
**(c)** The shop could use the surplus cash from Locks [1] to fund promotion of Panniers, because the market is growing fast and Panniers could become a star [1]. The risk is that share may not rise, so the spending could be wasted [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show the classification rule used (here, growth above 10% and relative share above or below 1). A decision should be tied to a named product, not to the matrix in general.

**6. (a)** (4,100 − 5,000) / 5,000 [1] = **−18.0%** [1].
**(b)** Decline [1]. Sales have fallen two years in a row, by 10.7% and then 18.0% [1].
**(c)** New packaging or a redesigned version [1] may refresh interest among existing buyers [1]. Selling in a new market or finding a new use, such as a travel edition [1], widens the customer base [1].
*Examiner insight:* Each strategy needs a developed link to sales. A list of extension strategies with no explanation tends to stay at the same level.

**7. (a)** Influencers: £2,400 / 96 = **£25** [1]; sampling: £1,800 / 45 = **£40** [1].
**(b)** Customer value or repeat purchases [1], because a cheaper customer is not better if they buy only once, or if they are outside the target segment [1].
**(c)** It can be targeted at a chosen segment [1] and responses can be measured quickly [1].
*Examiner insight:* A calculation answer should be followed by a comment on what the figure does not show.

**8.** Packaging: it attracts attention on the shelf or signals quality, for example a foil wrapper for a premium bar [1], so the bar looks worth its price [1]. Branding: a recognised name and logo [1] build loyalty and support a higher price than an unbranded bar [1].
*Examiner insight:* Link each role to the promotion of the product in the stem, which here is a premium chocolate bar.

**9.** Advantage: the business keeps the whole selling price instead of giving a retailer a margin [1], and gains customer data and brand control [1]. Disadvantage: it loses the gift shops' reach and display [1], and it must pay for delivery, returns and the website [1].
*Examiner insight:* One advantage and one disadvantage are asked for, so extra points beyond these gain nothing. Develop each one.

**10. (a)** Setting a price that changes frequently [1] in response to demand, time or stock levels [1].
**(b)** With student tickets: 60 x £15 + 40 x £9 = £1,260 [1]. Without: 60 x £15 = £900 [1]. Extra revenue = **£360** [1].
**(c)** If adults could buy student tickets, revenue would fall from £15 to £9 on each such ticket [1] and the extra revenue would not occur [1].
*Examiner insight:* The student scheme is price discrimination, not dynamic pricing. Read the definition asked for in part (a) carefully.

**11. (a)** Price = £5.20 x 1.60 = £8.32 [1]. Profit per bag = £8.32 − £5.20 = £3.12 [1].
**(b)** Online: £8.32 − £1.45 − £5.20 = £1.67 a bag [1]; x 1,400 = **£2,338** [1]. Supermarket: £6.90 − £5.20 = £1.70 a bag [1]; x 3,600 = **£6,120** [1].
**(c)** The supermarket gives £3,782 more profit a month (£6,120 − £2,338) [1] and much wider reach [1]. However, depending on one buyer is risky and the supermarket controls display and may press for a lower price later [1]. The online route keeps brand control and customer data [1], but reaches far fewer people [1]. Judgement: the roaster should accept the supermarket offer for volume while building online sales as a second channel, provided it can supply 3,600 bags without a drop in quality [1].
*Examiner insight:* For a recommend or justify question, the final judgement should rest on the figures in the case and name a condition that would change it.

**12. (a)** In maturity, sales flatten and rivals compete [1]. New or improved versions can renew interest and protect share [1], and spread risk if the best seller later declines [1].
**(b)** Prices that appeal to customers' perception rather than to cost or rivals [1], such as £4.99 instead of £5.00 [1].
*Examiner insight:* Explain the link from maturity to the need for development, not just that products must change.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Stating a pricing or promotion method with no link to the firm described.
- Mixing up mark-up (on cost) and margin (on price) in cost-plus calculations.
- Using the Boston Matrix without stating the rule for "high" growth or "high" share.
- Treating the Boston Matrix and the product life cycle as the same tool.
- Listing extension strategies without saying how each would raise sales.
- Giving a cost per customer without commenting on customer value or repeat buying.
- Writing both sides of a channel decision but giving no final judgement.
- Confusing price discrimination (different groups, different prices) with dynamic pricing (price changes over time or with demand).
