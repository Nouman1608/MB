## 3.1.1 The role of marketing: one business, five jobs

Learning the five roles as a list is not enough. Questions ask how a *particular* business carries them out. Here is one example worked through.

**Pellucid Optics** runs three opticians' shops.

| Role | What Pellucid does | Why it matters to Pellucid |
|---|---|---|
| Identifying customer needs | Asks customers at eye tests what they dislike about their current glasses | Finds that many want lighter frames for all-day computer work |
| Satisfying customer needs | Stocks a range of lightweight frames and offers lenses for screen use | Customers get what they came for, so they buy |
| Maintaining customer loyalty | Sends a reminder when the next eye test is due and gives a discount on a second pair | Customers return to Pellucid instead of trying a rival |
| Building customer relationships | Staff remember regular customers and offer free frame adjustments | Customers trust the shop and recommend it to friends |
| Anticipating changes in customer needs | Notices that more older people are moving to the area | Plans to offer home visits before rivals do |

The roles connect. Identifying needs comes first, because a business cannot satisfy a need it has not found. Loyalty and relationships keep customers after the first sale, which usually costs less than winning new ones. Anticipating change stops the business being left behind.

## 3.1.2 Understanding market changes

### Why spending patterns change

Customers change what they buy when something in their lives or in the market changes. Typical causes include changes in income, in tastes and fashion, in the age of the population, in technology (new products replacing old ones) and in the prices of products compared with alternatives. A good answer names the cause and then says which products gain or lose.

### Why markets become more competitive

- New businesses enter, sometimes from other countries.
- Selling online lets businesses reach customers far away, so a local shop faces rivals it never met before.
- Customers can compare prices quickly on their phones.

### Worked example: market share over two years

The formula is in the syllabus:

```
Market share = (sales revenue of a business / total sales revenue for the whole market) x 100
```

Brindell Teas sells bottled iced tea.

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Brindell's sales revenue | $6.0m | $7.0m |
| Total market sales revenue | $40.0m | $50.0m |

```
Year 1 market share = 6.0 / 40.0 x 100 = 15.0%
Year 2 market share = 7.0 / 50.0 x 100 = 14.0%
```

Brindell's sales rose by $1.0m, yet its market share fell. The whole market grew by 25% (10 / 40 x 100), while Brindell's sales grew by only about 16.7% (1 / 6 x 100). Rivals took a larger part of the extra spending. The lesson: rising sales do not prove a business is doing better than its competitors. Market share answers that question.

You can also work backwards. If a business holds a 12% share of a market worth $2.5m, its sales revenue is 12 / 100 x 2.5m = $300,000.

### How businesses can respond

Suppose a new low-price rival enters Brindell's market. Brindell has several options.

| Response | How it helps | Drawback |
|---|---|---|
| Improve the product, such as new flavours | Gives customers a reason to stay | Development costs; new flavours may not sell |
| Cut prices | Matches the rival | Lower revenue per bottle; possible price war |
| Promote the brand more strongly | Reminds customers why Brindell is different | Advertising is costly and results are uncertain |
| Improve customer service or loyalty schemes | Keeps existing customers | Running costs; rivals can copy |
| Target a different segment, such as health-conscious buyers | Avoids competing head-on on price | Smaller group of customers |

A strong answer recommends one response and justifies it with the case data. If Brindell's brand is well known and its customers care about taste, improving the product and promotion is more sensible than a price cut that a low-cost rival can always beat.

## 3.1.3 Mass markets and niche markets

A **mass market** is a large market where many customers buy similar products, such as everyday soap. A **niche market** is a small, specialised part of a market with particular needs, such as soap for people with very sensitive skin.

Questions often ask which a business should target. Weigh it like this:

- **Size of business and its costs.** Mass markets reward low unit costs from producing in large numbers. A small business usually cannot match those costs, so a niche suits it better.
- **Competition.** Mass markets attract large rivals. A niche may have few, letting a small business charge higher prices.
- **Risk.** A niche can shrink or be entered by a big firm. A business relying on one niche has all its eggs in one basket.

Worked judgement: **Corrigan Cycles** builds 200 hand-made touring bicycles a year. It should stay in its niche. It could never produce cheaply enough to compete in the mass market for ordinary bicycles, while its touring riders pay high prices for specialist features. Its risk is that a large maker launches a cheaper touring bike, so it should keep its customer relationships strong.

## 3.1.4 Market segmentation

Segmentation divides a market into groups of customers with similar features, so the business can design products, prices and promotion for each group. The syllabus names five bases: **age, income, location, gender and lifestyle**.

### Worked example: choosing a segment

**Ostara Fitness** is opening a gym. Its research finds three possible segments in its town.

| Segment | Basis | Features |
|---|---|---|
| Students aged 16-22 | Age | Many nearby, low incomes, want low monthly fees |
| Office workers on high incomes | Income and lifestyle | Want early-morning and lunchtime classes; will pay more |
| Retired people | Age and lifestyle | Free during the day, want gentle classes and a social atmosphere |

Ostara's gym will be in the business district, near offices. Targeting office workers fits its location and allows higher fees. Off-peak daytime hours are quiet with that segment, so Ostara could add a second segment, retired people, at a lower daytime price. Segmentation here lets one gym fill its timetable with two different groups.

### Advantages and disadvantages

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Products and promotion match what each group wants, so sales may rise | Designing different products or campaigns for each segment costs more |
| Less wasted promotion, because adverts reach the right people | Choosing a segment too small can limit sales |
| Can find gaps in the market that rivals have missed | Customers within a segment still differ |
| Can charge different prices to groups with different incomes | Research is needed to identify segments accurately |

## Exam technique

The command words in the 0264 syllabus include **calculate**, **define**, **identify**, **state**, **outline**, **explain**, **consider** and **justify**.

- **Calculate:** write the formula, substitute the figures and give the answer with a % sign. Check whether the question gives revenue or volume, and use the same measure for the business and the market.
- **Explain:** state the point, link it to the business in the question, then give the consequence. "Pellucid sends reminders, so customers return to it for their next test instead of trying a rival, which keeps its sales steady."
- **Consider / justify:** weigh at least one advantage and one disadvantage in context, then decide. Your decision should follow from a fact in the data, such as the size of the business or the strength of competition.

## Common errors

- Treating a rise in sales as a rise in market share.
- Mixing units: the business's sales in units and the market's in dollars.
- Listing the five roles of marketing without saying how the business in the question carries them out.
- Describing a niche market as simply "a small market". It is a small, *specialised* part of a larger market.
- Saying segmentation always increases profit. It also raises costs.
- Recommending a price cut against a low-cost rival without considering whether the business can afford it.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can market share fall when sales rise?**
Yes. If the whole market grows faster than the business's sales, its share falls. In the Brindell example, sales rose from $6.0m to $7.0m but share fell from 15% to 14%.

**Which five ways of segmenting are named in the syllabus?**
Age, income, location, gender and lifestyle.

**Why does marketing try to build customer relationships?**
Customers who trust a business return and recommend it, which is usually cheaper than winning new customers.
