This guide goes deeper into **3.2.5.2 Substitution reactions** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification. The point states that H₂O, NH₃ and Cl⁻ can act as monodentate ligands; that NH₃ and H₂O are similar in size and uncharged, so they exchange "without change of co-ordination number (eg Co²⁺ and Cu²⁺)"; that substitution may be incomplete, as in [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺; and that the larger Cl⁻ ligand can replace H₂O with "a change of co-ordination number (eg Co²⁺, Cu²⁺ and Fe³⁺)". It also covers bidentate ligands (H₂NCH₂CH₂NH₂ and C₂O₄²⁻), multidentate ligands (EDTA⁴⁻), haem and haemoglobin, carbon monoxide poisoning, and the chelate effect, which you must explain "in terms of the balance between the entropy and enthalpy change". This is International A2 content (**International A-level only**), assessed in Unit 3.

The [transition metals study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-transition-metals/) summarises these reactions. This page writes out every substitution the specification names, explains each change in co-ordination number and charge, and works through the chelate effect in more depth. ## What a substitution reaction is

In a ligand substitution, one or more ligands around a metal ion are replaced by different ligands. The oxidation state of the metal does **not** change. What can change is the co-ordination number, the shape, the overall charge of the complex and its colour.

## Ammonia replacing water: no change of co-ordination number

NH₃ and H₂O are **similar in size** and both **uncharged**, so six ammonia molecules fit around the metal just as six water molecules do. The complex stays six-co-ordinate and octahedral, and its charge does not change.

### Cobalt(II)

```
[Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺(aq) + 6NH₃(aq) → [Co(NH₃)₆]²⁺(aq) + 6H₂O(l)
pink                            pale brown (straw)
```

All six water ligands are replaced when excess concentrated ammonia is used.

### Copper(II): incomplete substitution

```
[Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺(aq) + 4NH₃(aq) → [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺(aq) + 4H₂O(l)
blue                            deep blue
```

Only four of the six water ligands are replaced, even with excess ammonia. The product is still six-co-ordinate: four NH₃ and two H₂O. This is the specification's example of **incomplete substitution**.

When ammonia solution is added a little at a time, a precipitate forms first, before the deep blue solution appears with excess. That precipitate belongs to the reactions of ions in aqueous solution (3.2.6); for 3.2.5.2, the substitution equation above is what you need.

## Chloride replacing water: co-ordination number falls

Cl⁻ is **larger** than H₂O, so only four chloride ions fit around the metal ion. The co-ordination number falls from 6 to 4 and the shape changes from octahedral to tetrahedral. Because each Cl⁻ carries a 1− charge, the overall charge of the complex also changes. These reactions are carried out by adding concentrated hydrochloric acid.

```
[Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 4Cl⁻ ⇌ [CoCl₄]²⁻ + 6H₂O        pink → blue
[Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 4Cl⁻ ⇌ [CuCl₄]²⁻ + 6H₂O        blue → yellow (green while both are present)
[Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺ + 4Cl⁻ ⇌ [FeCl₄]⁻ + 6H₂O         → yellow
```

These are equilibria. Adding water to the blue cobalt solution shifts the position back to the left and the pink colour returns: a direct application of Le Chatelier's principle.

### Worked example 1: tracking the changes

*For the copper reaction with concentrated hydrochloric acid, state the change in co-ordination number, shape, charge and oxidation state.*

```
co-ordination number: 6 → 4
shape:                octahedral → tetrahedral
charge:               2+ → 2−   (+2 + 4(−1) = −2)
oxidation state:      +2 → +2   (unchanged: substitution is not redox)
```

## Bidentate and multidentate ligands

- A **monodentate** ligand forms one co-ordinate bond: H₂O, NH₃, Cl⁻.
- A **bidentate** ligand forms two. Ethane-1,2-diamine, H₂NCH₂CH₂NH₂, donates a lone pair from each nitrogen. The ethanedioate ion, C₂O₄²⁻, donates a lone pair from an oxygen at each end.
- A **multidentate** ligand forms several. EDTA⁴⁻ forms six co-ordinate bonds to one metal ion.

Three bidentate ligands, or one EDTA⁴⁻, give a co-ordination number of 6.

```
[Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 3H₂NCH₂CH₂NH₂ → [Co(H₂NCH₂CH₂NH₂)₃]²⁺ + 6H₂O
[Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺ + 3C₂O₄²⁻ → [Fe(C₂O₄)₃]³⁻ + 6H₂O
[Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + EDTA⁴⁻ → [Cu(EDTA)]²⁻ + 6H₂O
```

Check the charges: in the iron example, +3 + 3(−2) = −3.

## Haem, oxygen and carbon monoxide

**Haem** is an iron(II) complex with a multidentate ligand. In haemoglobin, an oxygen molecule forms a **co-ordinate bond to Fe(II)**, which lets blood carry oxygen from the lungs to the tissues, where it is released.

**Carbon monoxide is toxic** because it replaces the oxygen co-ordinately bonded to Fe(II) in haemoglobin. CO forms a more stable complex than O₂, so it is not readily displaced, and the haemoglobin it occupies can no longer transport oxygen.

## The chelate effect

Bidentate and multidentate ligands replace monodentate ligands from complexes. This is the **chelate effect**. The specification asks you to explain it through the balance between enthalpy and entropy.

### Enthalpy

The same number of co-ordinate bonds is broken and made, and the new metal–ligand bonds are similar in strength to the old ones. So **ΔH is small**, close to zero.

### Entropy

Count the particles. In the EDTA example, two particles on the left give seven on the right:

```
[Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + EDTA⁴⁻ → [Cu(EDTA)]²⁻ + 6H₂O      2 particles → 7 particles
```

More particles means more disorder, so **ΔS is large and positive**.

### Feasibility

Using ΔG = ΔH − TΔS: with ΔH near zero and ΔS positive, **ΔG is negative** and the substitution is feasible. The reverse reaction, which would need seven particles to become two, has a large negative ΔS, which is why chelated complexes are so stable.

### Worked example 2: putting numbers in

*For the EDTA substitution above, take ΔH = −8 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS = +130 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ (values chosen for practice). Calculate ΔG at 298 K and identify the main driving force.*

```
ΔS = +0.130 kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹
TΔS = 298 × 0.130 = 38.7 kJ mol⁻¹
ΔG = −8 − 38.7 = −46.7 kJ mol⁻¹
```

ΔG is negative. The TΔS term (38.7 kJ mol⁻¹) is nearly five times the size of ΔH, so **entropy is the main driving force**.

## Common mistakes

- Writing [Cu(NH₃)₆]²⁺ for the product with excess ammonia.
- Keeping the co-ordination number at 6 for chloride complexes.
- Leaving the charge as 2+ in [CuCl₄]²⁻ or [CoCl₄]²⁻.
- Saying the oxidation state of the metal changes in a substitution.
- Explaining the chelate effect with "stronger bonds" rather than entropy.
- Counting particles without including the displaced water molecules.

## Exam technique

- **"Write an equation"**: show the hexaaqua ion as the starting species and balance the water released.
- **"Explain why the co-ordination number changes"**: Cl⁻ is larger than H₂O, so fewer fit around the metal.
- **"Explain the chelate effect"**: state that ΔH is small, that the number of particles increases so ΔS is positive, and that ΔG is therefore negative.
- **"Explain why CO is toxic"**: say it replaces oxygen co-ordinately bonded to Fe(II) in haemoglobin, so less oxygen is carried.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is the copper–ammonia product not [Cu(NH₃)₆]²⁺?**
In aqueous ammonia only four water ligands are replaced, giving [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺. The specification uses this as its example of incomplete substitution.

**Does a chloride substitution change the oxidation state of the metal?**
No. Ligand substitution is not redox. Cobalt is +2 in both [Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺ and [CoCl₄]²⁻; only the ligands, co-ordination number, shape, charge and colour change.

**Why is the chelate effect described as entropy-driven?**
Because ΔH is close to zero while the number of particles rises, so ΔS is positive and the −TΔS term makes ΔG negative.
