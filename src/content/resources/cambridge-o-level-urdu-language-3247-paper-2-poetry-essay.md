This guide covers **Part 2 Poetry** of Cambridge O Level First Language Urdu 3247 Paper 2 Texts:
"Two essay questions based on the prescribed set texts listed in the syllabus. Candidates answer one
question." It is worth 15 marks. The set texts themselves, the paper's three parts and the coverage
targets are in the [Paper 2 Texts study guide](/resources/o-level-cambridge-urdu-first-language-paper-2-texts/).
This page is about turning knowledge of those poems into an essay that reaches the top level.
Prescribed texts can change between syllabus years, so always check the list in the syllabus for your
examination year.

## What the syllabus lists for 2027

- **Ghazals (*غزلیں*)**: ten, two each by Khwaja Haider Ali Ahtish, Parveen Shakir, Momin Khan Momin,
  Allama Iqbal and Ahmed Faraz.
- **Nazms (*نظمیں*)**: three: Altaf Hussain Hali's *Musadas Hali*, in seventeen selected stanzas; Syed
  Zameer Jafree's *Admee*; and Mirza Mahmood Sarhadi's *Qitaat*, six short pieces.

Set texts "may not be taken into the examination", so every quotation you use must be memorised.

## What the mark scheme rewards

The Poetry and Prose essays share a five-level scheme. The top level (13–15) describes a response
that:

- "shows a clear understanding of the text and its deeper implications"
- "makes a developed exploration of the way the writer achieves her/his effects"
- "supports with careful and relevant reference to the text"
- "fully addresses the focus of the question."

The last phrase is the one candidates most often neglect. At Level 3 the response "partly addresses
the focus of the question"; at Level 1 there is "little relevance to the question". A fluent account
of everything you know about a poem is not an answer if the question asked about one feeling or one
idea.

## Reading the question

The syllabus gives the kinds of wording to expect. A personal response may be asked for "directly,
for example, 'What do you think?', 'What are your feelings about ...?'", or "by implication,
'Explore the ways in which ...'". In Urdu these appear as phrases such as *آپ کی کیا رائے ہے*، *آپ
کیا محسوس کرتے ہیں*، *کن طریقوں سے* or *کس طرح*.

Before planning, mark three things:

1. **The text**: which ghazal or nazm, or "one of" a group, which lets you choose.
2. **The focus**: the feeling, idea, character or attitude the question names.
3. **The method word**: *کس طرح* or "explore the ways" means you must discuss language and
   technique, not only meaning.

## Form: what to know and why it matters

**The ghazal.** Each couplet (*شعر*) is complete in itself, and couplets in one ghazal may move
between moods and ideas. The opening couplet (*مطلع*) rhymes in both lines; the closing couplet
(*مقطع*) usually carries the poet's pen name (*تخلص*). Every couplet ends with the same refrain
(*ردیف*), preceded by the rhyme (*قافیہ*). Because couplets are independent, an essay on a ghazal is
built by **selecting** the couplets that serve the question's focus, not by going through the poem in
order.

**The nazm.** A nazm develops one subject across the whole poem, so an essay can follow its
movement: how it opens, builds and closes. The **musaddas** (*مسدس*) is a nazm in six-line stanzas,
usually four rhyming lines followed by a couplet on a new rhyme, so the closing couplet of a stanza
often sharpens or turns its idea. A **qita** (*قطعہ*) is a short poem of connected couplets that
together make one point, often with a turn at the end.

Comment on form only when it helps answer the question: a refrain that keeps returning to an idea
the question names, or a stanza's final couplet that delivers the judgement.

## Contexts, briefly

Ahtish and Momin wrote in the classical tradition of the nineteenth century; Iqbal, Faraz and Parveen
Shakir are twentieth-century poets. Context can sharpen a point about attitude or language, but the
mark scheme credits understanding of the text, so a paragraph of biography earns little.

## Building the essay

**Introduction (two or three sentences).** Answer the question in your own words and name the methods
you will discuss.

**Three or four body paragraphs.** Each built on one couplet or stanza:

- **Point** linked to the focus.
- **Quotation**: a few memorised words, not the whole couplet unless it is short.
- **Meaning**: what the words say.
- **Deeper meaning**: what they suggest beyond the literal.
- **Method**: image, contrast, repetition, tone, form, and its effect.
- **Link** back to the focus.

**Conclusion.** Your personal response, grown from the analysis: which couplet or stanza you find most
powerful, and why.

## Worked example of the method

To show the method without reproducing a set text, here are two illustrative lines written for this
guide. They are not from any prescribed poem.

> دیا جلا کے رکھا ہے ہوا کے رستے میں
> یہ ضد ہے دل کی کہ شاید وہ لوٹ آئے کبھی

Question focus (illustrative): *شاعر نے امید اور ناامیدی کی کشمکش کو کس طرح پیش کیا ہے؟*

Model paragraph:

> اس شعر میں امید کو ایک نازک دیے کی صورت میں دکھایا گیا ہے، جو جان بوجھ کر "ہوا کے رستے میں" رکھا
> گیا ہے۔ یہ پیکر بڑا معنی خیز ہے: ہوا دیے کی دشمن ہے، اس لیے شاعر جانتا ہے کہ اس کی امید ہر لمحہ
> خطرے میں ہے، پھر بھی وہ اسے بجھنے کے لیے چھوڑ نہیں دیتا۔ دوسرے مصرعے میں لفظ "ضد" بتاتا ہے کہ یہ
> امید عقل کا نہیں، دل کا فیصلہ ہے۔ "شاید" اور "کبھی" جیسے کمزور، غیر یقینی الفاظ اس ضد کے بالکل
> مقابل کھڑے ہیں، اور یوں ایک ہی شعر میں امید اور ناامیدی کی کشمکش سمٹ آئی ہے۔

*Commentary:* every sentence serves the focus (*کشمکش*). The paragraph moves from image to deeper
meaning to the effect of single words, uses only short quotations, and ends by linking back to the
question. In the exam, the same moves are made with your memorised words from a prescribed poem.

## Preparing each poem

Make a one-page card for every poem you choose to know closely:

- The central idea in one sentence, and the tone.
- Four to six memorised phrases, each with a note of its technique.
- For a ghazal: which couplets suit which likely focus (love, separation, self-respect, time, faith,
  society).
- For a nazm: how it opens, develops and ends.
- One personal judgement you could defend.

## Using memorised quotation well

Learn short phrases of two to five words rather than whole couplets: they are easier to recall
exactly and easier to weave into your own sentences. Choose phrases that carry an image or a key
word you can analyse. If memory fails, refer closely to the detail instead ("in the closing couplet
the poet turns to address himself by name") rather than inventing a quotation. Accurate close
reference is still credited; a garbled quotation is not.

## Common mistakes

- **Summarising the poem** couplet by couplet with no focus.
- **Choosing couplets that do not fit the question** because they are the ones you remember best.
- **Quoting whole couplets** and leaving no time to analyse them.
- **Naming the form** (radeef, matla) without saying what it does.
- **Misquoting.** Learn your phrases exactly; a garbled quotation weakens "careful" reference.
- **Ignoring "what do you think?"** when the question asks for your view.
- **Unbalanced timing.** Fifteen marks out of fifty in two hours is roughly 35 minutes, including a
  few minutes to plan.
