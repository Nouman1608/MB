## What depreciation is, and why it is needed

**Depreciation** is the estimated part of the cost of a non-current asset
that is used up during an accounting period. Causes include physical wear
and tear, the passage of time (for example a lease that runs out) and
obsolescence, where newer technology makes the asset out of date.

It must be accounted for because:

- **Matching**: an asset that helps to earn revenue for several years should
have its cost spread over those years, not charged in one.
- **Profit is not overstated**: without depreciation, the cost of using the
asset would be missing from expenses.
- **Assets are not overstated**: the statement of financial position shows
the asset at cost less accumulated depreciation, a more realistic figure.

Depreciation is not a payment of cash and it does not set money aside to
replace the asset. It is a book entry that allocates cost.

## The three methods

**Straight-line** gives the same charge every year.

```
Annual depreciation = (cost − residual value) ÷ expected useful life
or                  = cost × fixed percentage
```

**Reducing balance** applies a fixed percentage to the net book value at the
start of each year, so the charge falls each year.

```
Depreciation = net book value × percentage
Net book value = cost − accumulated depreciation
```

**Revaluation** compares values at the start and end of the year.

```
Depreciation = value at start + additions during the year − value at end
```

**Time-based charges.** Questions state a policy, and you must follow it
exactly. Two common ones:

- *A full year's charge in the year of purchase and none in the year of
sale.* Ignore dates within the year.
- *Charged monthly (on a time basis).* A van costing $24,000, bought on 1
April 2027 and depreciated at 25% per year on cost, is charged $24,000 × 25%
× 9/12 = **$4,500** for the year ended 31 December 2027, and $6,000 for
each full year after.

**Revaluation in practice.** A florist's small tools were valued at $2,300
on 1 January 2027. Tools costing $900 were bought during the year. On 31
December 2027 they were valued at $2,450. Depreciation = $2,300 + $900 −
$2,450 = **$750**.

## Choosing a method for each type of asset

| Asset type | Suitable method | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Fixtures and fittings; machinery used evenly | Straight-line | Similar benefit each year |
| Premises on a lease of fixed length | Straight-line | Value is used up evenly as the lease runs out |
| Motor vehicles | Reducing balance | Lose most value early; repair costs rise later, so a falling depreciation charge evens out total cost |
| Computer equipment | Reducing balance | Rapid obsolescence in early years |
| Loose tools, crockery, small equipment | Revaluation | Many low-cost items; recording each separately is impractical |

Whatever method is chosen, the **consistency** concept requires it to be
used every year, so profits can be compared.

## Worked example: Lakshmi's workshop

Lakshmi depreciates machinery at 20% per year using the reducing balance
method. A full year's depreciation is charged in the year of purchase and
none in the year of sale. Her year ends on 31 December.

- Machine A was bought on 1 January 2025 for $15,000.
- Machine B was bought on 1 July 2026 for $10,000.
- On 1 March 2027 Machine C was bought for $12,000 on credit from Ashton
Engineering.
- On 1 October 2027 Machine A was sold for $9,000 cash.

**Step 1: depreciation history**

```
             2025      2026      2027     Accumulated at 31 Dec 2027
              $         $         $          $
Machine A   3,000     2,400        –        (5,400, removed on sale)
Machine B      –      2,000     1,600        3,600
Machine C      –         –      2,400        2,400
```

Machine A: 20% × $15,000 = $3,000; then 20% × $12,000 = $2,400. Its net book
value at sale is $15,000 − $5,400 = $9,600. Machine B: 20% × $10,000 =
$2,000; then 20% × $8,000 = $1,600.

At 1 January 2027 the machinery account showed $25,000 (A and B) and the
provision for depreciation $7,400 ($3,000 + $2,400 + $2,000).

**Step 2: profit or loss on disposal.** Proceeds $9,000 − net book value
$9,600 = **loss of $600**.

**Step 3: journal entries**

```

       (Machine C bought on credit)

       (Cost of Machine A transferred on sale)

         Disposal of machinery                                        5,400
       (Accumulated depreciation on Machine A)
1 Oct  Cash                                               9,000
         Disposal of machinery                                        9,000
       (Proceeds of sale of Machine A)
31 Dec Statement of profit or loss                        4,000
         Provision for depreciation of machinery                      4,000
       (Depreciation for the year: B $1,600, C $2,400)
31 Dec Statement of profit or loss                          600

```

**Step 5: the statements.** The statement of profit or loss for 2027 shows
depreciation of machinery $4,000 and a loss on disposal $600 as expenses.
The statement of financial position at 31 December 2027 shows machinery at
cost $22,000, less accumulated depreciation $6,000, net book value
**$16,000**. Check: B $10,000 − $3,600 = $6,400; C $12,000 − $2,400 =
$9,600; total $16,000.

If the proceeds had been **more** than the net book value, the balancing
figure would be on the debit side of the disposal account: a **profit** on
disposal, credited to the statement of profit or loss as other income.

## Common mistakes

- Applying the reducing balance percentage to cost every year.
- Ignoring the residual value in a straight-line calculation that gives
one.
- Charging a full year when the policy is monthly, or the reverse.
- Crediting depreciation to the asset account instead of the provision
account.
- Leaving the cost of the asset sold in the asset account, or its
depreciation in the provision account.
- Comparing proceeds with cost instead of net book value.
- Calculating depreciation for the year on an asset already sold when the
policy says none in the year of sale.
- Believing depreciation is a fund of cash for replacing the asset.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate**: write the formula, substitute, then answer with the unit.
- **Prepare** ledger accounts: dates, the name of the other account in
the details column, balances carried down and brought down.
- **Explain** a choice of method: link the pattern of the charge to how the
asset loses value or gives benefit.
- **Advise** questions, such as which method a delivery business should
use, need a recommendation with a reason tied to the asset in the question.
