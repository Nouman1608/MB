These revision notes condense section 3.2.9, Interpretation, analysis and communication of accounting information, of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Accounting (9615) specification, Version 1.2, for International AS exams May/June 2025 onwards and International A-level exams May/June 2026 onwards. This content belongs to the **International A-level only**; according to the specification, Paper 3 and Paper 4 both assess it. The [study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-accounting-interpretation-analysis-and-communication-of/) has the full teaching; to test yourself, use the [practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-accounting-interpretation-analysis-and-communication-of-practice/). Names and numbers are made up; money is in dollars, share prices in cents.

Other links: the [course hub](/boards/oxfordaqa/a-level/accounting/), the [9615 checklist](/checklists/oxfordaqa/a-level/accounting/), the free [diagnostics](/diagnostics/), and the International AS [ratio revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-accounting-analysis-and-evaluation-of-financial-information-revision-notes/), which hold the twelve 3.1.8 ratios you still need here.

## The six things 3.2.9 asks of you

1. Read **financial and management** information with ratios and other techniques, and say where that analysis falls short.
2. Judge results **over time inside the business** and **against rivals**, under profitability, liquidity, efficiency and capital structure.
3. Show why **cash and profit differ**, and how a transaction moves each one.
4. Match **stakeholders** to what they need, communicate clearly, and say what published accounts are worth.
5. Compare **recording systems**: what accounting software does, computer against manual records, single against double entry.
6. **Weigh up recommendations**, including effects on stakeholders, on the economy locally and nationally, and on the environment.

## Investors' ratios

OxfordAQA's guide *Formulae for accounting ratios* states that you are required to know the formulae.

| Ratio | Main user | Answer in | Formula |
|---|---|---|---|
| Earnings per share | Shareholders | cents | Profit after tax / Number of issued ordinary shares |
| Dividend yield | Income-seeking shareholders | % | Dividend per share / Market price per share × 100 |
| Interest cover | Lenders | times | Profit before interest and tax / Interest payable |
| Price earnings | Potential investors | times | Market price per share / Earnings per share |
| Dividend cover | Shareholders judging dividend safety | times (the guide prints × 100, giving %) | Profit after interest and tax / Ordinary share dividends paid |

**Linked figures**

- Dividend per share = ordinary dividends paid / issued ordinary shares.
- Share price = price earnings × EPS. Example: price earnings 12, EPS 25 cents, so price = 300 cents.
- Payout % = dividends / profit after tax × 100, the reciprocal of dividend cover as a percentage.

**What a change usually means**

| Movement | Likely reading | Check before concluding |
|---|---|---|
| EPS up | More profit per share | Was there a share issue or a one-off gain? |
| Price earnings up | Market more confident of growth | Has the price risen for reasons outside the business? |
| Dividend yield up | Better cash return on the price | Has the share price fallen? |
| Dividend cover down | Dividend less safe | Is profit falling or the payout rising? |
| Interest cover down | Greater risk to lenders | New borrowing, higher rates or lower profit? |

## Method: an interpretation answer

1. Calculate each ratio, showing the formula and the figures used.
2. Group the ratios: profitability, liquidity, efficiency, capital structure, investor.
3. For each group, state the movement with figures ("fell from 15.33% to 14.01%").
4. Give a likely cause from the information provided.
5. Say what it means for the stakeholder named in the question.
6. Reach a judgement and note one limitation.

## Worked reminder: two sentences that earn credit

A shareholder sees dividend yield rise from 3.1% to 4.4% while dividend cover falls from 2.4 to 1.3 times. A strong comment links the two:

> "The higher yield looks attractive, but cover has nearly halved, so the dividend now uses most of profit after tax. If profit falls, the dividend is likely to be cut, so the higher yield may not last."

It quotes the figures, explains the link and ends with a consequence for the reader named in the question.

## Must-know distinctions

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| **Internal comparison** | **External comparison** | Same business over time or against a budget, versus a competitor or industry average |
| **Dividend yield** | **Dividend cover** | Return on the share price, versus how many times profit covers the dividend |
| **EPS** | **Dividend per share** | Profit earned per share, versus cash paid out per share |
| **Profit** | **Cash** | Accruals-based surplus, versus money actually received and paid |
| **Internal stakeholders** | **External stakeholders** | Employees, management, owners/shareholders, versus customers, suppliers, government, lenders, local community |
| **Single entry** | **Double entry** | One side or cash-only records, versus both sides with a trial balance check |

## Management information

Interpret budgets, variances and costing reports the way you interpret ratios: movement, cause, consequence, action. Look for linked variances (a favourable price variance with an adverse usage variance), and suggest who should be asked before blame is given. Techniques are in the [standard costing revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-accounting-standard-costing-and-variance-analysis-revision-notes/) and [budgeting revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-accounting-budgeting-revision-notes/).

## Cash versus profit: company transactions

| Transaction | Profit | Cash |
|---|---|---|
| Issue shares for cash (including a rights issue) | No effect | Up |
| Issue debentures | No effect now; interest reduces later profit | Up |
| Pay a dividend | None; deducted in the statement of changes in equity | Down |
| Revalue land upwards | No effect (revaluation reserve) | No effect |
| Sell an asset below carrying amount | Falls by the loss | Up by the proceeds |
| Charge depreciation | Falls | No effect |
| Redeem debentures | No effect now | Down |

## Stakeholders and published accounts

- **Lenders:** interest cover, gearing, security. **Suppliers:** liquidity, payable days.
- **Shareholders:** EPS, yield, cover, price earnings, ROCE. **Management:** budgets, variances, all ratios.
- **Employees:** profit trend and survival. **Customers:** continuity of supply.
- **Government:** tax and jobs. **Local community:** jobs, local spending, pollution.

**Why published accounts matter:** a set format lets outsiders compare years and companies. **Weaknesses:** historic, summarised, late, little non-financial detail.

**Effective communication:** tailor content to the reader, lead with the conclusion, explain in plain words, state limitations.

## Recording systems

- **Accounting software:** one input posts both sides, ledgers, invoicing, bank reconciliation, payroll, inventory, instant reports.
- **Computerised versus manual:** faster and arithmetically accurate, but costly to set up, vulnerable to data loss and cyber attack, and still accepts wrong inputs.
- **Single versus double entry:** single is cheap but has no arithmetic check; double gives a complete, checkable record. See [incomplete records](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-accounting-accounting-for-organisations-with-incomplete-revision-notes/).

## Assessing a recommendation

Use the order: financial case, shareholders and lenders, employees, customers and suppliers, local economy, national economy, environment, judgement. Your judgement should depend on something ("if the sales forecast is reliable...").

## Quick self-test

Halloway Tents plc: profit before interest and tax 3,300,000; interest payable 275,000; tax 685,000; 13,000,000 issued ordinary shares; ordinary dividends paid 780,000; share price 342 cents.

1. Calculate profit after tax.
2. Calculate EPS.
3. Calculate dividend per share and dividend yield.
4. Calculate dividend cover.
5. Calculate price earnings.
6. Calculate interest cover. Which stakeholder relies on it most?
7. Orlenby Ltd has current assets 400,000 and current liabilities 250,000. It issues 500,000 of debentures for cash. Give the new current ratio and the effect on profit.
8. Separately, Orlenby pays a dividend of 60,000. Give the new current ratio.
9. Company J has EPS of 20 cents; company K has EPS of 12 cents. Is J more profitable? Explain briefly.
10. State two features of accounting software.
11. State one advantage of double entry over single entry.
12. List the areas beyond stakeholders on which a recommendation's impact must be assessed.

### Answers

1. 3,300,000 − 275,000 − 685,000 = **2,340,000**.
2. 234,000,000 cents / 13,000,000 = **18.0 cents**.
3. 78,000,000 / 13,000,000 = **6.0 cents**; 6.0 / 342 × 100 = **1.75%**.
4. 2,340,000 / 780,000 = **3.00 times**.
5. 342 / 18.0 = **19.0 times**.
6. 3,300,000 / 275,000 = **12.0 times**; **lenders**.
7. 900,000 / 250,000 = **3.60 : 1**; **no effect** on profit until interest is charged.
8. 340,000 / 250,000 = **1.36 : 1**.
9. **Not necessarily**: EPS depends on the number of shares, so it cannot be compared between companies.
10. Any two: automatic double entry from one input, invoicing, bank reconciliation, payroll, inventory records, instant reports.
11. A trial balance checks arithmetical accuracy (or: a complete record, so statements can be drawn up directly).
12. The **local and national economy** and the **environment**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Mixing cents and dollars in price earnings or dividend yield.
- Using profit before tax for EPS or dividend cover.
- Using profit after interest as the numerator of interest cover.
- Stating "dividend yield rose" as good news without noting the share price fell.
- Comparing EPS across companies with different share numbers.
- Describing ratio movements with no cause, consequence or figures.
- Treating a revaluation or share issue as increasing profit.
- Naming stakeholders without linking each to the information it needs.
- Listing pros and cons of a recommendation but never reaching a judgement.
- Ignoring the local economy, national economy or environment when the question names them.
