## Section A

**1.** State what is meant by the term *catalyst*. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between homogeneous and heterogeneous catalysis. **[2]**

**3.** Classify each catalyst as homogeneous or heterogeneous.

**(a)** Iron in the Haber process **[1]**

**(b)** Concentrated sulfuric acid in the reaction of ethanoic acid with ethanol **[1]**

**(c)** Platinum in the catalytic converter of a car **[1]**

**4.** State two quantities that are **not** changed when a catalyst is added to a reaction mixture at constant temperature. **[2]**

**5.** A student writes: "A catalyst is not involved in the reaction; it just lowers the activation energy." Give two corrections to this statement. **[2]**

**6.** An oil-soluble catalyst is dissolved in an organic liquid layer, while the reactant is dissolved in a separate aqueous layer. Classify the catalyst and explain your answer. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain, with the help of a Boltzmann distribution, how a catalyst increases the rate of a reaction at constant temperature. **[4]**

**8.** A reaction has ΔH = −70 kJ mol⁻¹. The uncatalysed route has Eₐ = +200 kJ mol⁻¹. In the presence of a catalyst the reaction passes through an intermediate, with the first maximum 110 kJ mol⁻¹ and the second maximum 75 kJ mol⁻¹ above the reactants.

**(a)** Describe the reaction pathway diagram for both routes on the same axes. **[4]**

**(b)** State the activation energy of the catalysed route. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the activation energy of the reverse reaction for each route. **[2]**

**(d)** Use your answers to (c) to explain why the catalyst does not change the position of equilibrium. **[2]**

**9.** Nitrogen dioxide in the atmosphere can catalyse the oxidation of sulfur dioxide.

**(a)** Write equations for the two steps, and show that they add up to the overall reaction. **[3]**

**(b)** State, with a reason, whether nitrogen dioxide is acting as a homogeneous or a heterogeneous catalyst. **[1]**

**10.** Compare the effect on a Boltzmann distribution, and on the rate, of (i) raising the temperature and (ii) adding a catalyst. **[4]**

**11.** A reaction pathway diagram shows two routes from the same reactants to the same products. Route P has one maximum, 160 kJ mol⁻¹ above the reactants. Route Q has two maxima, at 90 kJ mol⁻¹ and 120 kJ mol⁻¹ above the reactants, with a minimum between them. The products are 30 kJ mol⁻¹ above the reactants.

**(a)** State whether the reaction is exothermic or endothermic. **[1]**

**(b)** Identify the catalysed route, giving two pieces of evidence. **[2]**

**(c)** State the activation energy for route Q and the decrease in activation energy caused by the catalyst. **[2]**

**12.** A student claims that adding a catalyst to the Haber process increases the equilibrium yield of ammonia. Evaluate this claim, and suggest why a catalyst is still valuable industrially. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** A substance that increases the rate of a reaction [1] and is chemically unchanged at the end of the reaction [1].

**2.** In homogeneous catalysis the catalyst is in the same phase as the reactants [1]; in heterogeneous catalysis the catalyst is in a different phase from the reactants [1].

**3. (a)** Heterogeneous [1].

**(b)** Homogeneous [1].

**(c)** Heterogeneous [1].

**4.** Any two of: ΔH; the position of equilibrium (or equilibrium yield); the shape of the Boltzmann distribution; the temperature [2].

**5.** The catalyst does take part in the reaction and is regenerated at the end [1]. It provides an alternative route (different mechanism) that has a lower activation energy, rather than lowering the activation energy of the original route [1].

**6.** Heterogeneous [1]. The organic layer and the aqueous layer are two different phases, even though both are liquids, so the catalyst is in a different phase from the reactant [1].

**7.** The catalyst provides an alternative route with a lower activation energy [1]. At constant temperature the Boltzmann curve is unchanged, but Eₐ(cat) lies to the left of Eₐ [1]. The area under the curve beyond Eₐ(cat) is larger, so a greater proportion of molecules have enough energy to react [1]. So the frequency of effective collisions increases and the rate increases [1].

**8. (a)** Axes labelled enthalpy and progress of reaction [1]. Reactants and products at the same levels for both routes, with products 70 kJ mol⁻¹ below reactants [1]. Uncatalysed route: a single hump 200 kJ mol⁻¹ above the reactants [1]. Catalysed route: two lower humps (110 and 75 kJ mol⁻¹ above the reactants) with a trough between them for the intermediate [1].

**(b)** +110 kJ mol⁻¹ [1].

**(c)** Uncatalysed: 200 − (−70) = +270 kJ mol⁻¹ [1]. Catalysed: 110 − (−70) = +180 kJ mol⁻¹ [1].

**(d)** The forward and reverse activation energies are both reduced by the same amount, 90 kJ mol⁻¹ [1], so the rates of the forward and reverse reactions increase by the same factor and equilibrium is reached sooner but at the same position [1].

**9. (a)** SO₂ + NO₂ → SO₃ + NO [1]. NO + ½O₂ → NO₂ [1]. Adding: SO₂ + NO₂ + NO + ½O₂ → SO₃ + NO + NO₂, which simplifies to SO₂ + ½O₂ → SO₃; NO₂ is regenerated and does not appear overall [1].

**(b)** Homogeneous: nitrogen dioxide and the reactants are all gases, in the same phase [1].

**10.** (i) Raising the temperature changes the curve: the peak is lower and moves to higher energy, with the same total area [1]; Eₐ stays the same but a greater area lies beyond it [1]. (ii) A catalyst leaves the curve unchanged, but provides a route with a lower Eₐ, so the Eₐ line moves to the left and a greater area lies beyond it [1]. In both cases a greater proportion of molecules have enough energy to react, so the frequency of effective collisions and the rate increase [1].

**11. (a)** Endothermic [1].

**(b)** Route Q [1]: its highest point is lower than that of route P, and it has a minimum between two maxima, showing an intermediate formed with the catalyst [1].

**(c)** Eₐ for route Q = +120 kJ mol⁻¹ [1]. Decrease = 160 − 120 = 40 kJ mol⁻¹ [1].

**12.** The claim is wrong: a catalyst does not change the position of equilibrium, so the equilibrium yield is unchanged [1]. The catalyst increases the rate of the forward and reverse reactions equally, so equilibrium is reached faster [1]. Industrially this means ammonia is produced more quickly, or an acceptable rate is achieved at a lower temperature than would otherwise be needed [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Omitting "alternative route" or "different mechanism" when explaining catalysis.
- Drawing a second Boltzmann curve for the catalysed reaction at the same temperature.
- Giving the lower hump, or the sum of the humps, as the catalysed Eₐ.
- Classifying liquid-liquid systems by state instead of phase.
- Saying a catalyst increases the equilibrium yield.
