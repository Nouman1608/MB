These notes condense section 1.1, Main economic groups and factors of production, of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205), Version 2.0, for first assessment in 2019 (component J205/01). The specification is not tiered. Fuller explanations and worked examples are in the [1.1 study guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-main-economic-groups-and-factors-of-production-ocr-gcse-economics/); then try the [1.1 practice questions](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-main-economic-groups-and-factors-of-production-ocr-gcse-economics-practice/). All figures are invented.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Consumer | A person or household that buys goods and services to satisfy wants |
| Producer | An individual or organisation that makes goods or provides services |
| Government | The body that taxes, spends, provides some services and sets rules |
| Interdependence | Each group relies on the others, so a change for one affects the rest |
| Factor of production | A resource used to produce goods and services |
| Land | Natural resources, such as the ground, minerals, water and fish stocks |
| Labour | Human effort, physical and mental |
| Capital | Made goods used to produce other goods and services, such as machines and buildings |
| Enterprise | Organising the other factors and taking the risk |
| Labour-intensive | Uses a large amount of labour relative to capital |
| Capital-intensive | Uses a large amount of capital relative to labour |

## The three groups in brief

- **Consumers:** decide what to buy and whether to save. Their spending signals to firms what to produce. They also supply labour and pay tax.
- **Producers:** decide what, how and how much to produce. Usually aim for profit. They employ workers, buy from other firms and pay tax.
- **Government:** taxes, spends, regulates and redistributes. It provides some services itself and buys goods and services from firms.
- One person can be in several groups at once: a worker, a consumer and a taxpayer.

## Interdependence: the four-step chain

```
1. Event          (e.g. a large employer closes)
2. First group    (producer cuts jobs)
3. Link           (workers lose income, so spend less)
4. Outcome        (local shops sell less; tax revenue falls; benefit spending rises)
```

Run the chain the other way too: the government may respond with retraining or support for new firms, which affects consumers and producers again.

## The four factors and their rewards

| Factor | Example (bakery) | Reward |
|---|---|---|
| Land | Wheat-growing land, water, the site | Rent |
| Labour | Bakers, shop assistants | Wages |
| Capital | Ovens, mixers, delivery van | Interest |
| Enterprise | The owner who risks savings | Profit |

## Classification traps

| Item | Factor |
|---|---|
| Timber in a forest | Land |
| A sawmill machine | Capital |
| A lorry driver | Labour |
| A salaried manager | Labour (no business risk) |
| An owner who invests savings | Enterprise |
| Money or a bank loan | Not a factor: finance used to buy factors |

## Combining factors

- Every product uses all four factors; firms choose the mix.
- A factor that becomes relatively cheaper or more productive tends to be used more.
- The entrepreneur makes the choice and bears the risk.
- Services served in person tend to be labour-intensive; mass manufacturing tends to be capital-intensive.
- Quality matters as well as quantity: skills, fertility of land, age of machinery.

**Worked reminder.** A shop's weekly factor costs are rent £600, wages £1,800 and equipment lease £200, total £2,600. Labour share = 1,800 ÷ 2,600 × 100 = **69.2%**, so the shop is labour-intensive.

## Evaluation points to have ready

- A switch to capital can cut costs and raise output per worker, but can remove jobs and needs money up front.
- More labour suits small output, varied work or a personal service; more capital suits large, repetitive output.
- The right mix depends on factor prices, the product, the scale of output and the skills available.

## Exam traps

- Defining capital as money.
- Listing the groups without their roles.
- Saying "they all depend on each other" without an event-to-outcome chain.
- Treating every manager as enterprise.
- Forgetting that the government is also a buyer and producer.
- Answering in general terms when the question gives a context.

## Quick self-test

1. State the reward for each of the four factors of production.
2. Classify a combine harvester, a coal seam and a nurse.
3. Explain why money is not a factor of production.
4. Give one way producers depend on consumers.
5. Give one way consumers depend on the government.
6. A café owner pays a manager a fixed salary. Which factor does the manager supply?
7. Explain the difference between labour-intensive and capital-intensive production.
8. A firm's weekly costs are rent £500, wages £1,500 and machinery lease £1,000. Calculate the share of labour in these costs.
9. A town's main employer cuts 400 jobs. Give two effects on other groups.
10. Give two reasons a firm might replace workers with machines.
11. Name two ways the government can act other than as a tax collector.

### Answers

1. Land: rent; labour: wages; capital: interest; enterprise: profit.
2. Combine harvester: capital; coal seam: land; nurse: labour.
3. Money buys factors but produces nothing itself; it is finance, not a resource used directly in production.
4. They need consumers to buy their products to earn revenue (and also need them as workers).
5. Consumers rely on the government for services such as education and roads, or for benefits.
6. Labour: the manager does not bear the risk of the business.
7. Labour-intensive methods use many workers relative to machines; capital-intensive methods use many machines relative to workers.
8. Total = 500 + 1,500 + 1,000 = £3,000. Labour share = 1,500 ÷ 3,000 × 100 = **50%**.
9. Workers lose income and spend less, so local shops sell less; the government collects less tax and may pay more in benefits.
10. Machines may be cheaper per unit of output, more reliable or faster; wages may have risen (any two).
11. As a producer of services, a buyer of goods and services, a regulator setting rules, or a redistributor of income (any two).
