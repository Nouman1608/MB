This guide covers **2.3, Coasts**, from Theme 2, The Natural
Environment, in Cambridge IGCSE Geography 0460. It follows
[Rivers](/resources/igcse-geography-rivers/) and applies the same
erosion/transportation/deposition framework to coastal, rather than
river, environments.

## The work of the sea and wind

You need to demonstrate understanding of the work of the sea and wind
in eroding, transporting and depositing material along a coastline,
and to describe and explain the formation of the landforms these
processes create.

## Erosional landforms

You need to know a named set of coastal erosional landforms: cliffs,
wave-cut platforms, caves, arches, stacks, stumps, and bay and headland
coastlines. These landforms form a recognisable developmental sequence
worth learning in order: wave action erodes a line of weakness in a
cliff to form a **cave**; continued erosion may cut through a
headland entirely to leave an **arch**; the arch's roof eventually
collapses, leaving an isolated pillar of rock, a **stack**, separated
from the cliff; further erosion reduces the stack to a low, worn
remnant, a **stump**. Alongside this sequence, a **wave-cut platform**
forms at the base of an eroding cliff as the cliff retreats, leaving a
gently sloping rock surface exposed at low tide. **Bay and headland
coastlines** develop where a coastline alternates between bands of
softer rock (eroded faster, forming bays) and harder rock (eroded more
slowly, remaining as headlands).

## Depositional landforms

You need to know beaches, spits, and coastal sand dunes as depositional
landforms. A beach forms where wave energy is insufficient to remove
all the sediment being deposited, building up a store of sand or
shingle along the shoreline. A spit forms where longshore drift
(the movement of sediment along a coast, driven by waves approaching
at an angle) carries material past a bend in the coastline, where the
coastline changes direction and deposition continues out into open
water, building a narrow, extending ridge of sediment. Coastal sand
dunes form further inland from the beach itself, where wind transports
and deposits dry sand, often becoming stabilised over time by
colonising vegetation.

## Coral reefs and mangrove swamps

You need to describe coral reefs and mangrove swamps, and the specific
conditions required for their development. Coral reefs require warm,
clear, shallow, and typically nutrient-poor water, since coral-building
organisms are sensitive to temperature and depend on sunlight
penetrating the water for the photosynthetic algae living within them.
Mangrove swamps require warm temperatures and sheltered, low-energy
coastal conditions -- typically estuaries or lagoons -- where fine
sediment can settle and salt-tolerant mangrove vegetation can establish
itself.

## Hazards, opportunities and coastal management

You need to demonstrate understanding that coasts present both hazards
and opportunities for people, with hazards specifically including
coastal erosion and tropical storms, and you need to explain what can
be done to manage the impacts of coastal erosion. As with rivers,
coastal management approaches range from hard engineering (such as sea
walls or groynes, which trap sediment moving via longshore drift to
help maintain a beach) to softer approaches that work with natural
processes rather than resisting them directly.

## Required case study

The syllabus specifies **one case study is required for 2.3**: the
opportunities presented by an area or areas of coastline, the
associated hazards, and their management. As with the case studies
required in 2.1 and 2.2, prepare specific, named detail rather than
generic coastal description.

## A worked example: longshore drift and spit formation

Waves rarely approach a coastline exactly head-on; when they arrive at
an angle, the swash (water moving up the beach) carries sediment
diagonally up the beach in the direction of the wave, while the
backwash (water draining back down) is pulled by gravity straight back
down the steepest slope. Repeated over many wave cycles, this
zig-zag movement -- longshore drift -- gradually transports sediment
along the coast in the dominant direction the waves approach from.
Where the coastline changes direction, such as at a river mouth or
bay, this moving sediment continues in roughly the same direction into
open water rather than following the new coastline angle, gradually
building a narrow ridge of deposited material: a spit. Over time, spits
can grow long enough to partially or fully enclose a body of water
behind them, sometimes developing a curved or hooked end where wave
patterns or a river current alter the direction sediment is being
carried. Explaining spit formation this way -- as a direct consequence
of longshore drift meeting a change in coastline direction -- is a
frequently tested example of applying process to landform.

## How to approach it

Learn the erosional landform sequence (cave, arch, stack, stump)
together as a single process rather than four separate definitions,
since exam questions often ask you to explain the sequence of
formation, not just identify a labelled diagram. Keep erosional and
depositional landforms in two clearly separated groups when revising --
cliffs, wave-cut platforms, caves, arches, stacks, stumps and headlands
on the erosion side; beaches, spits and dunes on the deposition side --
since confusing which process produced a given landform is a common
source of lost marks. For coral reefs and mangrove swamps, learn the
specific environmental conditions each requires (warm, clear, shallow
water for reefs; sheltered, low-energy, sediment-rich conditions for
mangroves) as the actual explanatory content, since "describe" command
words in this part of the syllabus still expect the underlying
conditions to be stated, not just the ecosystem named.
