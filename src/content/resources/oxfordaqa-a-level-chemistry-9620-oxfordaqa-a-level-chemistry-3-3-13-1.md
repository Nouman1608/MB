This guide goes deeper into **3.3.13.1 Amino acids**, the first subtopic of section 3.3.13 Amino acids and proteins in the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International A2 content, assessed in Unit 4 (Organic 2 and Physical 2) and in the synoptic Unit 5. The subtopic asks for one skill, drawing the right ion under the right conditions, but questions test it with unfamiliar side chains, so this guide gives a method that works for any amino acid.

## What the specification asks for

- Amino acids have **both acidic and basic properties**, including the formation of **zwitterions**.
- Draw the structures of amino acids **as zwitterions** and the ions formed **in acid solution** and **in alkaline solution**.

Optical isomerism of amino acids is not part of this subtopic, and no isoelectric point values are required.

## Structure and names

The amino acids met in this course have the general formula **H₂N–CH(R)–COOH**: an amino group and a carboxyl group on the same carbon, plus a side chain R. Questions often give both a common name and a systematic name.

| Common name | R group | Systematic name |
|---|---|---|
| Glycine | –H | aminoethanoic acid |
| Alanine | –CH₃ | 2-aminopropanoic acid |
| Serine | –CH₂OH | 2-amino-3-hydroxypropanoic acid |
| Aspartic acid | –CH₂COOH | 2-aminobutanedioic acid |
| Lysine | –(CH₂)₄NH₂ | 2,6-diaminohexanoic acid |

You do not need to learn the R groups; they are given when needed. What matters is spotting whether R contains an extra acidic group (–COOH) or an extra basic group (–NH₂).

## The two groups and the zwitterion

- The **carboxyl group** is acidic: –COOH can donate H⁺, becoming –COO⁻.
- The **amino group** is basic: the lone pair on N can accept H⁺, becoming –NH₃⁺.

Within one molecule, the carboxyl group can pass its proton to the amino group. The result is a **zwitterion**, a species with a positive and a negative charge on different atoms and an overall charge of zero:

`H₂N–CH(CH₃)–COOH  →  ⁺H₃N–CH(CH₃)–COO⁻`  (alanine)

Solid amino acids exist as zwitterions. That explains their physical properties: strong ionic attractions between zwitterions give them **high melting points** (they usually decompose before melting) and make them **soluble in water**, much more like ionic salts than like simple organic molecules of similar size.

## Ions in acid and in alkali

Start from the zwitterion and ask what extra H⁺ or OH⁻ does.

- **In acid** (excess H⁺): the –COO⁻ group accepts H⁺ and becomes –COOH. The –NH₃⁺ stays. Overall charge **+1**.
- **In alkali** (excess OH⁻): the –NH₃⁺ group gives its H⁺ to OH⁻, forming water, and becomes –NH₂. The –COO⁻ stays. Overall charge **−1**.

A way to remember it: **acid protonates everything that can be protonated; alkali deprotonates everything that can be deprotonated.**

### Worked example 1: alanine in three forms

Draw alanine as a zwitterion, in excess hydrochloric acid and in excess sodium hydroxide, and write equations for the reactions with HCl and NaOH starting from the neutral molecule.

| Condition | Structure | Charge |
|---|---|---|
| Zwitterion | ⁺H₃N–CH(CH₃)–COO⁻ | 0 |
| Excess acid | ⁺H₃N–CH(CH₃)–COOH | +1 |
| Excess alkali | H₂N–CH(CH₃)–COO⁻ | −1 |

- `H₂N–CH(CH₃)–COOH + HCl → ⁺H₃N–CH(CH₃)–COOH Cl⁻`
- `H₂N–CH(CH₃)–COOH + NaOH → H₂N–CH(CH₃)–COO⁻ Na⁺ + H₂O`

Note that the amino acid reacts with **both** an acid and a base: this is what "acidic and basic properties" means.

## Side chains that join in

A side chain group behaves exactly like the main group of the same kind.

### Worked example 2: aspartic acid and lysine

Draw the ion formed by (a) aspartic acid in excess alkali and (b) lysine in excess acid. Give each charge.

- **(a)** Aspartic acid, HOOC–CH₂–CH(NH₂)–COOH, has two –COOH groups and one –NH₂. In excess alkali both –COOH lose H⁺, and the –NH₂ stays: **⁻OOC–CH₂–CH(NH₂)–COO⁻**, charge **−2**.
- **(b)** Lysine, H₂N–(CH₂)₄–CH(NH₂)–COOH, has two –NH₂ groups and one –COOH. In excess acid both –NH₂ gain H⁺, and the –COOH stays: **⁺H₃N–(CH₂)₄–CH(NH₃⁺)–COOH**, charge **+2**.

Count each acidic and each basic group, then apply the rule to every one of them.

### Worked example 3: groups that do not react

Serine has the side chain –CH₂OH. Draw serine in excess alkali.

The side-chain –OH is an alcohol group. It is not acidic enough to lose H⁺ to hydroxide ions under these conditions, and it is not basic. Only the main groups change: **H₂N–CH(CH₂OH)–COO⁻**, charge −1.

The same applies to side chains containing only C and H, such as alanine's methyl group or phenylalanine's –CH₂C₆H₅.

## Reacting quantities

Because each –COOH group reacts with one OH⁻, titration with sodium hydroxide counts the acidic groups.

### Worked example 4: aspartic acid and sodium hydroxide

0.266 g of aspartic acid (Mr 133.0) reacts completely with 0.100 mol dm⁻³ sodium hydroxide. Calculate the volume of alkali needed.

1. n(aspartic acid) = 0.266 ÷ 133.0 = 0.00200 mol.
2. Two –COOH groups per molecule, so n(NaOH) = 2 × 0.00200 = 0.00400 mol.
3. Volume = 0.00400 ÷ 0.100 = 0.0400 dm³ = **40.0 cm³**.

`HOOC–CH₂–CH(NH₂)–COOH + 2NaOH → NaOOC–CH₂–CH(NH₂)–COONa + 2H₂O`

Turning this round, a titre can tell you how many acidic groups an unknown amino acid has.

### Worked example 5: from acid to alkali in steps

Glycine is dissolved in excess hydrochloric acid, and sodium hydroxide is then added a little at a time. Describe how the glycine species change, with equations.

1. In the acid, glycine is present as the cation ⁺H₃N–CH₂–COOH (charge +1).
2. The first hydroxide ions remove H⁺ from the –COOH group, the more acidic of the two groups, giving the zwitterion: ⁺H₃N–CH₂–COOH + OH⁻ → ⁺H₃N–CH₂–COO⁻ + H₂O.
3. Further hydroxide ions remove H⁺ from –NH₃⁺, giving the anion: ⁺H₃N–CH₂–COO⁻ + OH⁻ → H₂N–CH₂–COO⁻ + H₂O.
4. So one mole of the cation needs **two** moles of OH⁻ to reach the alkaline form.

Reading the steps backwards describes what happens when acid is added to an alkaline solution of an amino acid.

## Common mistakes

- Drawing –NH₃⁺ in the alkaline ion, or –COO⁻ in the acid ion.
- Giving the zwitterion an overall charge; it is zero.
- Ignoring a side-chain –COOH or –NH₂, so the charge comes out as ±1 instead of ±2.
- Deprotonating a side-chain –OH in alkali.
- Putting the charges on the wrong atoms: the + belongs on N, the − on O.
- Writing –NH₄⁺; an amino group gains only one H⁺.

## Exam technique

- **Draw** an ion: show every charge on the correct atom and keep the side chain unchanged unless it contains –COOH or –NH₂.
- **State the charge** if asked, by adding the charges you have drawn.
- **Explain** a zwitterion: one molecule, both charges, internal proton transfer from –COOH to –NH₂.
- **Write equations**: one HCl per basic group, one NaOH per acidic group.
