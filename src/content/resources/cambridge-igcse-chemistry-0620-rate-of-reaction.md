This guide covers subtopic **6.2 Rate of reaction** in Topic 6, Chemical reactions, of Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620, 2026–2028 series. The combined [rates of reaction and reversible reactions study guide](/resources/rates-of-reaction-and-reversible-reactions/) gives the headline collision-theory argument for each factor. This page goes further into the parts that cost most marks: describing an effect precisely, explaining it in full collision-theory language, choosing and evaluating an experimental method, and reading numbers off a rate graph. When you have finished, use the [rate of reaction revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-rate-of-reaction-revision-notes/) and the [rate of reaction practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-rate-of-reaction-practice/).

## What the syllabus asks for

**Core** (all candidates):

- Describe the effect on rate of changing the concentration of solutions, the pressure of gases, the surface area of solids and the temperature, and of adding or removing a catalyst, including enzymes.
- State that a catalyst increases the rate of a reaction and is unchanged at the end of the reaction.
- Describe practical methods for investigating rate, including change in mass of a reactant or a product and the formation of a gas.
- Interpret data, including graphs, from rate of reaction experiments.

**Supplement** (Extended candidates only):

- Describe collision theory in terms of the number of particles per unit volume, the frequency of collisions, the kinetic energy of particles and the activation energy, Ea.
- Describe *and explain* the effect of each of the five factors using collision theory.
- State that a catalyst decreases the activation energy of a reaction.
- *Evaluate* practical methods for investigating rate.

The split matters. A Core answer that says "a higher temperature makes the reaction faster" earns the describe mark. An Extended answer to "explain" needs the particle reasoning as well.

## Rate as a measurement

Rate is how quickly a reactant is used up or a product is formed. In practice you never measure it directly; you measure something that changes as the reaction goes on (a volume of gas, a mass, a time) and work the rate out from that. A mean rate over a time interval is:

```
mean rate = change in amount of product (or reactant) ÷ time taken
```

The unit follows from what was measured: cm³/s for a gas volume, g/s for a mass, and so on. Always write the unit; a number alone is incomplete.

## Describing each factor (Core)

Each description has two halves: the change you make, and the direction of the effect on rate.

| Change made | Effect on rate |
|---|---|
| Increase concentration of a solution | Rate increases |
| Increase pressure of a reacting gas | Rate increases |
| Use smaller pieces (larger surface area) of a solid | Rate increases |
| Increase temperature | Rate increases |
| Add a catalyst (or an enzyme) | Rate increases |
| Remove the catalyst | Rate decreases |

Enzymes are biological catalysts. They speed up reactions in living things and in processes such as fermentation, and the same rule applies: present, faster; absent, slower.

## The four ideas of collision theory (Supplement)

The syllabus names four ideas, and a full explanation usually draws on two or three of them.

1. **Particles per unit volume.** How crowded the reacting particles are. Concentration (for solutions) and pressure (for gases) both change this.
2. **Frequency of collisions.** How many collisions happen each second, not how many happen in total.
3. **Kinetic energy of particles.** Faster-moving particles have more kinetic energy.
4. **Activation energy, Ea.** The minimum energy a collision must have for the particles to react. Collisions with less energy than Ea do not lead to reaction.

A strong explanation chains them: change → effect on particles → effect on collision frequency or on the proportion of collisions with at least Ea → effect on rate.

**Concentration and pressure.** More particles per unit volume, so collisions are more frequent, so the rate increases. The energy of each collision is unchanged.

**Surface area.** Only particles at the surface of a solid can collide with the other reactant. Breaking the solid into smaller pieces exposes more particles, so collisions at the surface are more frequent.

**Temperature.** Particles gain kinetic energy and move faster. Two things follow: collisions become more frequent, *and* a greater proportion of collisions have energy equal to or greater than Ea. Examiners look for the second point; leaving it out is the most common way to drop a mark on temperature.

**Catalyst.** A catalyst decreases the activation energy. At the same temperature, a greater proportion of collisions now have enough energy to react, so the rate increases. The catalyst is chemically unchanged at the end and can be reused.

## Practical methods (Core describe, Supplement evaluate)

The syllabus names two kinds of measurement.

**Formation of a gas.** Collect the gas and record its volume at regular intervals.

- *Gas syringe* connected to the reaction flask by a delivery tube.
- *Upturned measuring cylinder* (or burette) filled with water, with the gas bubbled in so it displaces the water.

Example: magnesium ribbon reacting with dilute hydrochloric acid, collecting hydrogen.

```
Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2(g)
```

**Change in mass.** Stand the flask on a balance and record the mass as gas escapes. A loose plug of cotton wool lets the gas out but stops acid spray leaving the flask.

Example: marble chips (calcium carbonate) with dilute hydrochloric acid.

```
CaCO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)
```

Other methods exist, such as timing how long a precipitate takes to hide a cross drawn under the flask, but the two above are the ones the syllabus names.

### Evaluating a method (Supplement)

"Evaluate" means judging how well a method works. Useful points:

- **Mass loss suits carbon dioxide but not hydrogen.** Hydrogen has a very low relative molecular mass (2), so the mass change is too small for a typical balance to show accurately. Carbon dioxide (Mr 44) gives a readable loss.
- **A gas syringe is usually more precise than collecting over water**, and it avoids the problem that some gases (carbon dioxide included) dissolve slightly in water, which would lower the measured volume.
- **Gas escapes before the bung is inserted** if the solid is added to the acid first; using a divided flask or a tube of solid tipped over at the start avoids this.
- **Fair testing.** Only the factor under investigation should change. Temperature rises as many reactions proceed, so a water bath helps; surface area is hard to keep constant with irregular marble chips.
- **Repeats** let you spot anomalous results and calculate a mean.

## Interpreting rate data and graphs (Core)

Read a volume–time (or mass-loss–time) graph in three parts:

- **Steepest at the start.** The gradient is the rate, and it is greatest when the reactants are most concentrated.
- **Gradient decreasing.** Reactants are being used up, so collisions become less frequent.
- **Horizontal line.** The reaction has stopped because one reactant (the limiting reactant) has run out.

When two curves are compared, look at two separate features. The *initial steepness* tells you which reaction is faster. The *final level* tells you how much product formed, which depends only on the amount of limiting reactant, not on the rate.

### Worked example

A student added 0.060 g of magnesium ribbon to excess dilute hydrochloric acid and collected the hydrogen in a gas syringe. These are illustrative results.

| Time / s | 0 | 10 | 20 | 30 | 40 | 50 | 60 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Volume of H₂ / cm³ | 0 | 22 | 38 | 50 | 57 | 60 | 60 |

**(a) Calculate the mean rate over the first 20 s.**
38 cm³ ÷ 20 s = **1.9 cm³/s**.

**(b) Calculate the mean rate between 20 s and 40 s and explain the difference.**
(57 − 38) ÷ 20 = **0.95 cm³/s**. The rate is lower because the acid concentration has fallen and the magnesium has a smaller surface, so collisions are less frequent.

**(c) When did the reaction stop?** At 50 s, when the volume stops changing; the magnesium (the limiting reactant) has been used up.

**(d) The experiment is repeated with 0.060 g of magnesium powder. Predict the curve.** Steeper at the start (larger surface area), levelling off sooner, at the **same** final volume of 60 cm³, because the same amount of magnesium is used.

The final volume is consistent with the equation: 0.060 g ÷ 24 g/mol = 0.0025 mol Mg, giving 0.0025 mol H₂, which occupies 0.0025 × 24 000 = 60 cm³ at room temperature and pressure.

## Common mistakes

- **Saying particles collide "more" instead of "more frequently".** Rate depends on collisions per second; write "more frequent collisions" or "more collisions per second".
- **Explaining temperature with collision frequency only** (Extended). Include the greater proportion of collisions with energy at least Ea.
- **Saying a catalyst "gives particles more energy".** It lowers the activation energy; the particles' energy is unchanged.
- **Thinking a faster reaction makes more product.** Only the amount of limiting reactant sets the final volume or mass.
- **Saying "surface area increases" when the pieces get bigger.** Larger lumps mean a *smaller* total surface area.
- **Missing units** on a rate value.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Describe** (Core): state the change and its effect on rate. No particle explanation is needed.
- **Explain** (Extended): give the particle reasoning, ending with the effect on rate.
- **Evaluate** (Extended): give a strength or weakness of a method and say why it matters for the results.
- **Interpret / Deduce**: quote values from the graph or table, with units, before drawing a conclusion.
- **Sketch**: when asked to add a second curve, get the initial gradient and the final level right relative to the first curve; these are the features being marked.
