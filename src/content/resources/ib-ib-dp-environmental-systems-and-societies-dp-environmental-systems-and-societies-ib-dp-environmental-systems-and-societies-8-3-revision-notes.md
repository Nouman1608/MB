## Core recall

- Primary pollutants are emitted directly (PM, NOx, SO₂, CO, VOCs); secondary pollutants form in the air (tropospheric ozone, PANs, acids).
- PM2.5 is the most harmful particulate fraction because it reaches deep into the lungs and bloodstream.
- WHO 2021 guidelines: annual mean PM2.5 5 µg/m³; annual mean NO₂ 10 µg/m³.
- Indoor smoke from cooking with solid fuels harms women and children most.
- NO₂ peaks at the morning and evening rush hours; ozone peaks in the early afternoon when sunlight is strongest.
- In the evening, fresh NO removes ozone (NO + O₃ → NO₂ + O₂), so ozone is often higher downwind and in suburbs than at busy roadsides.
- Pollution builds up with calm winds, strong sunshine, inversions, basin or valley relief and winter heating.
- London, December 1952: coal smoke and SO₂ trapped by an inversion; thousands of deaths; Clean Air Act 1956.
- SO₂ and NOx form sulfuric and nitric acids downwind: wet and dry acid deposition, a transboundary problem.
- Evaluate controls against a guideline or baseline, and consider who pays and what is displaced.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Primary pollutant | Released directly from a source |
| Secondary pollutant | Formed by reactions of primary pollutants in the atmosphere |
| PM10 / PM2.5 | Particles 10 / 2.5 micrometres in diameter or smaller |
| NOx | Nitrogen monoxide (NO) and nitrogen dioxide (NO₂) together |
| VOCs | Volatile organic compounds, carbon-based gases from fuels and solvents |
| Photochemical smog | Haze of ozone, PANs and other secondary pollutants formed in sunlight |
| Thermal inversion | Warm air lying above cooler air, preventing vertical mixing |
| Wet deposition | Acids reaching the ground in rain, snow or fog |
| Dry deposition | Acidic gases and particles settling directly on surfaces |
| Transboundary pollution | Pollution that crosses regional or national borders |
| Catalytic converter | Device that converts CO, NOx and hydrocarbons into CO₂, N₂ and water |
| Low-emission zone | Area where the most polluting vehicles are charged or banned |

## Two kinds of smog

| | Smoky (sulfurous) smog | Photochemical smog |
|---|---|---|
| Example | London, 1952 | Sunny cities with heavy traffic, such as Los Angeles |
| Main sources | Coal burning | Vehicle NOx and VOCs |
| Conditions | Cold, damp, still, inversion | Hot, sunny, still, often an inversion |
| Key pollutants | Smoke particles, SO₂ | Ozone, PANs, NO₂ |

## Controls in brief

| Control | Pollutant targeted | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Catalytic converter | CO, NOx, hydrocarbons | Works poorly when the engine is cold |
| Diesel particulate filter | PM | Diesel still emits more NOx than petrol |
| Congestion charge or low-emission zone | All traffic pollutants | Displaces traffic; burdens owners of older vehicles |
| Fuel switching away from coal | SO₂, PM, NOx | Investment and time needed |
| Electric vehicles | Exhaust emissions | Brake and tyre particles remain; depends on the electricity mix |
| Pollution alerts | Exposure | No cut in emissions |

## Calculation methods

```
Percentage change     = (new − original) ÷ original × 100
Ratio to guideline    = measured value ÷ guideline value
Share of days over    = days above limit ÷ days measured × 100
```

## Exam traps

- Ozone does not peak at rush hour.
- The busiest roadside does not always have the highest ozone.
- An inversion answer must say why the cool air cannot rise.
- The 1952 smog was not photochemical.
- Acid deposition can fall far from the source.
- Stratospheric ozone protects; tropospheric ozone harms.
- Evaluations need a benchmark, a cost and a side effect.

## Quick self-test

1. Classify as primary or secondary: (a) SO₂; (b) tropospheric ozone; (c) PM2.5 from exhausts; (d) PANs.
2. Why is PM2.5 more harmful than larger particles?
3. State the WHO 2021 annual guideline values for PM2.5 and NO₂.
4. A city's annual PM2.5 is 25 µg/m³. How many times the guideline is this?
5. Annual mean NO₂ falls from 64 to 40 µg/m³. Calculate the percentage change.
6. A limit is exceeded on 54 of 360 days measured. Calculate the percentage of days.
7. Explain why ozone peaks in the early afternoon.
8. Why can ozone be higher in the suburbs than in the city centre?
9. Give two natural factors that make Mexico City prone to smog.
10. State the cause and the legal outcome of the 1952 London smog.
11. Distinguish wet from dry deposition.
12. Give one limitation of a low-emission zone.

### Answers

1. (a) Primary; (b) secondary; (c) primary; (d) secondary.
2. It is small enough to reach deep into the lungs and pass into the bloodstream.
3. PM2.5: **5 µg/m³**; NO₂: **10 µg/m³** (annual means).
4. 25 ÷ 5 = **5 times**.
5. (40 − 64) ÷ 64 × 100 = **−37.5%**.
6. 54 ÷ 360 × 100 = **15%**.
7. Sunlight is strongest around midday, splitting NO₂ to release oxygen atoms that form ozone; formation builds through the late morning.
8. In the centre, fresh NO from traffic reacts with ozone and removes it; downwind there is less NO, so ozone survives.
9. Any two: surrounding mountains trap air in a basin; frequent inversions; strong sunshine.
10. Coal smoke and SO₂ trapped by an inversion in cold, still weather; the Clean Air Act 1956.
11. Wet deposition reaches the ground in precipitation; dry deposition is gases and particles settling directly on surfaces.
12. Any one: traffic may move to roads outside the zone; costs fall on people who cannot afford newer vehicles; enforcement costs.
