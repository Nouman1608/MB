These notes condense **3.2.9 Bulk properties of solids** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630) specification. It is International AS content, assessed in Unit 1. For full explanations and worked examples, read the [bulk properties of solids study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-bulk-properties-of-solids-oxfordaqa/); for exam-style questions, use the [bulk properties of solids practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-bulk-properties-of-solids-oxfordaqa-practice/).

## The specification in one box

- Density ρ = m/V.
- Hooke's law, elastic limit; F = kΔL, k as stiffness or spring constant.
- Tensile stress and tensile strain; elastic strain energy; breaking stress.
- Energy stored = ½FΔL = area under the force–extension graph.
- Plastic behaviour, fracture and brittle behaviour linked to force–extension graphs.
- Energy conservation with elastic strain energy and energy to deform; spring energy to kinetic and gravitational potential energy.
- Interpretation of simple stress–strain curves.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Density | Mass per unit volume, kg m⁻³ |
| Hooke's law | Extension is proportional to force, up to the limit of proportionality |
| Stiffness (spring constant) k | Force per unit extension, N m⁻¹ |
| Limit of proportionality | Point beyond which F is no longer proportional to ΔL |
| Elastic limit | Point beyond which the object does not return to its original length |
| Elastic deformation | Fully reversed when the force is removed |
| Plastic deformation | Permanent deformation that remains when the force is removed |
| Tensile stress | Force ÷ cross-sectional area, Pa |
| Tensile strain | Extension ÷ original length, no unit |
| Breaking stress | Stress at which the material breaks |
| Elastic strain energy | Energy stored in a deformed object, recoverable if deformation is elastic |
| Brittle | Fractures with little or no plastic deformation |

## Formulae

| Quantity | Equation | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Density | ρ = m/V | kg m⁻³ |
| Hooke's law | F = kΔL | N |
| Stress | σ = F/A | Pa |
| Strain | ε = ΔL/L | none |
| Energy stored (straight-line region) | ½FΔL = ½k(ΔL)² | J |
| Energy stored (any graph) | area under F–ΔL graph | J |

## Reading force–extension graphs

| Feature | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Straight line through origin | Hooke's law obeyed |
| Gradient of straight part | k (for F against ΔL) |
| Line starts to curve | Limit of proportionality passed |
| Unloading line returns to origin | Elastic |
| Unloading line parallel, ends at ΔL > 0 | Plastic: permanent extension |
| Graph ends abruptly, straight | Brittle fracture |

## Energy rules

- Area under loading curve = total work done on the sample.
- Area under unloading line = elastic strain energy recovered.
- Area between them = energy to deform permanently (ends as internal energy).
- Spring launcher: ½k(ΔL)² → ½mv² → mgh (no resistive forces).

## Stress–strain curves

- Steeper initial gradient: stiffer material.
- Higher end point: larger breaking stress.
- Long plastic region: ductile. Abrupt end with no plastic region: brittle.
- Yield point (metals): large strain for little extra stress.

## Worked reminders

- **Density.** A 0.54 kg block measuring 0.20 m × 0.10 m × 0.010 m: V = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ m³, ρ = 2700 kg m⁻³.
- **Spring.** 12 N gives 30 mm: k = 400 N m⁻¹; energy stored = ½ × 12 × 0.030 = 0.18 J.
- **Stress.** 150 N on 0.75 mm² (7.5 × 10⁻⁷ m²): 2.0 × 10⁸ Pa.
- **Strain.** A 3.0 m wire extending 2.4 mm: 8.0 × 10⁻⁴.
- **Breaking force.** Doubling the diameter of a wire of the same material multiplies area, and so breaking force, by 4.

## Exam traps

- Limit of proportionality and elastic limit used interchangeably.
- Diameter put into πr²; mm² not converted to m².
- Strain given a unit, or calculated from the stretched length.
- ½FΔL used beyond the straight-line region.
- "Brittle means weak": brittle means little plastic deformation before fracture.
- "Stiff" and "strong" confused.
- Graph descriptions that do not say what happens on unloading.
- Energy calculated from a curved graph with ½FΔL.
- Comparisons of materials that mention only one property.

## Quick self-test

1. Define tensile stress.
2. Define tensile strain.
3. Explain the difference between the limit of proportionality and the elastic limit.
4. Find the density of a 0.54 kg block measuring 0.20 m × 0.10 m × 0.010 m.
5. A 12 N load extends a spring by 30 mm. Find k.
6. Find the energy stored in the spring in question 5.
7. A wire of cross-sectional area 0.75 mm² carries 150 N. Find the stress.
8. A 3.0 m wire extends by 2.4 mm. Find the strain.
9. What does the area between the loading and unloading curves of a stretched copper wire represent?
10. What is meant by a brittle material? Give an example.
11. A spring stores 0.50 J and launches a 0.20 kg ball along a smooth level surface. Find the ball's speed.
12. Wire B is the same material as wire A but has twice the diameter. How do their breaking forces compare?

### Answers

1. Force per unit cross-sectional area, σ = F/A.
2. Extension per unit original length, ε = ΔL/L.
3. Beyond the limit of proportionality, force and extension are no longer proportional; beyond the elastic limit, the object does not return to its original length.
4. **2700 kg m⁻³**.
5. 12 ÷ 0.030 = **400 N m⁻¹**.
6. ½ × 12 × 0.030 = **0.18 J**.
7. 150 ÷ 7.5 × 10⁻⁷ = **2.0 × 10⁸ Pa**.
8. 2.4 × 10⁻³ ÷ 3.0 = **8.0 × 10⁻⁴**.
9. Energy used to deform the wire permanently (transferred to internal energy).
10. One that fractures with little or no plastic deformation, such as **glass**.
11. ½ × 0.20 × v² = 0.50, so v = **2.2 m s⁻¹**.
12. B's breaking force is **4 times** A's: breaking stress is the same, area is 4 times larger.
