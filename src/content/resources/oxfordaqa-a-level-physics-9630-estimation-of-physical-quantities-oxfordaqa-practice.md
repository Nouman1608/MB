These questions cover **3.1.3 Estimation of physical quantities** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630) specification. It is International AS content, assessed in Unit 1. Section A has short questions; Section B has longer, structured estimates. Where you are asked to estimate, state every assumed value with a unit. The answers show one sensible set of assumptions; other sensible values earn the same credit if the working is consistent. If a point is unfamiliar, read the [estimation study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-estimation-of-physical-quantities-oxfordaqa/) or the [estimation revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-estimation-of-physical-quantities-oxfordaqa-revision-notes/) first. Take g = 9.81 N kg⁻¹, or 10 N kg⁻¹ in an estimate.

## Questions

### Section A

**1.** State the order of magnitude of 5.2 × 10³ m. **[1]**

**2.** Which is the best estimate of the mass of an apple?

A: 10⁻³ kg

B: 10⁻¹ kg

C: 10¹ kg

D: 10³ kg

**[1]**

**3.** Estimate the area of a classroom door. State your assumptions. **[2]**

**4.** Estimate the weight of the water in a full 1 litre bottle. **[2]**

**5.** What is the order of magnitude of the number of seconds in one day?

A: 10³

B: 10⁴

C: 10⁵

D: 10⁶

**[1]**

**6.** An apple falls from a branch about 3 m above the ground. Estimate its speed just before it lands, ignoring air resistance. **[2]**

**7.** A student estimates the kinetic energy of a person walking as 80 kJ. Use your own estimate to explain why this is not sensible. **[2]**

### Section B

**8.** A bus with an empty mass of 1.0 × 10⁴ kg carries 50 passengers.

**(a)** Estimate the total mass of the passengers. **[1]**

**(b)** Estimate the kinetic energy of the full bus at 15 m s⁻¹, to the nearest order of magnitude. **[2]**

**(c)** The bus brakes to rest over 30 m. Estimate the average braking force, to the nearest order of magnitude. **[2]**

**9.** During a storm, 1 cm of rain falls on a football pitch.

**(a)** Estimate the mass of rainwater that falls on the pitch. State your assumptions. **[3]**

**(b)** The rain formed in clouds about 1000 m above the ground. Estimate the gravitational potential energy the water lost in falling, to the nearest order of magnitude. **[2]**

**10.** The diameter of an atom is about 2 × 10⁻¹⁰ m. A cube of copper has sides of 1 cm and a mass of about 9 g.

**(a)** Estimate the number of atoms along one edge of the cube. **[1]**

**(b)** Estimate the number of atoms in the cube, to the nearest order of magnitude. **[1]**

**(c)** Estimate the mass of one copper atom, to the nearest order of magnitude. **[2]**

**11.** A lift carries eight adults from the ground floor to the tenth floor of an office building in 20 s.

**(a)** Estimate the useful power output needed to raise the passengers, to the nearest order of magnitude. State your assumptions. **[4]**

**(b)** Suggest why the motor's actual power output must be larger than your estimate. **[1]**

**12.** In a crash test, a car travelling at 15 m s⁻¹ hits a barrier and comes to rest. The crumple zone extends the stopping time to about 0.1 s.

**(a)** Estimate the average force on the car, to the nearest order of magnitude. **[2]**

**(b)** Estimate the average resultant force on a seat-belted driver during the crash. **[2]**

**(c)** State which assumption most affects your answer to (a), and explain whether doubling it would change the order of magnitude. **[2]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** 5.2 > 3.16, so **10⁴ m** [1] **[1]**

**2.** **B** [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* An apple is roughly 0.1 to 0.2 kg; 10⁻³ kg (1 g) is about the mass of a paper clip.

**3.** Assume height ≈ 2.0 m and width ≈ 0.9 m [1]; area ≈ 1.8 m², order **10⁰ m²** (about 2 m²) [1] **[2]**

**4.** 1 litre = 10⁻³ m³, so mass = 1000 × 10⁻³ = 1 kg [1]; weight ≈ 1 × 10 = **10 N** [1] **[2]**

**5.** **C** [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* 24 × 3600 = 86 400 = 8.6 × 10⁴; 8.6 > 3.16, so the order is 10⁵, not 10⁴.

**6.** v² = 2gh = 2 × 10 × 3 = 60 [1]; v ≈ **8 m s⁻¹** (order 10¹ m s⁻¹) [1] **[2]**

**7.** Mass ≈ 70 kg and walking speed ≈ 1.5 m s⁻¹, so Ek = ½ × 70 × 1.5² ≈ 80 J [1]; 80 kJ is about 1000 times too large, so the student has probably made a prefix error [1] **[2]**

### Section B

**8.**

**(a)** 50 × 70 kg = **3500 kg** (accept 3000 to 4000 kg) [1]

**(b)** Total mass ≈ 1.35 × 10⁴ kg; Ek = ½ × 1.35 × 10⁴ × 15² = 1.5 × 10⁶ J [1]; order **10⁶ J** [1]

**(c)** Work done by brakes = Ek, so F = 1.5 × 10⁶ ÷ 30 = 5 × 10⁴ N [1]; 5 > 3.16, so order **10⁵ N** [1] **[5]**

**9.**

**(a)** Assume pitch ≈ 100 m × 60 m = 6000 m² [1]; volume = 6000 × 0.01 = 60 m³ [1]; mass = 1000 × 60 = **6 × 10⁴ kg** (order 10⁵ kg) [1]

**(b)** ΔEp = mgΔh = 6 × 10⁴ × 10 × 1000 = 6 × 10⁸ J [1]; order **10⁹ J** [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Converting 1 cm to 0.01 m is the step most often missed; leaving it as 1 gives an answer 100 times too large.

**10.**

**(a)** 1 × 10⁻² ÷ 2 × 10⁻¹⁰ = **5 × 10⁷** [1]

**(b)** (5 × 10⁷)³ = 1.25 × 10²³, order **10²³** [1]

**(c)** 9 × 10⁻³ kg ÷ 1.25 × 10²³ [1] = 7 × 10⁻²⁶ kg; 7 > 3.16, so order **10⁻²⁵ kg** [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the unrounded count from (b) in (c), not the bare order of magnitude, or the answer drifts by a factor of ten.

**11.**

**(a)** Mass of passengers ≈ 8 × 70 = 560 kg [1]; height of ten floors ≈ 10 × 3 = 30 m [1]; energy = mgΔh ≈ 560 × 10 × 30 = 1.7 × 10⁵ J [1]; P = 1.7 × 10⁵ ÷ 20 ≈ 8 × 10³ W, order **10⁴ W** [1]

**(b)** The motor must also lift the lift car itself, and some energy is wasted against friction and as heat in the motor [1] **[5]**

**12.**

**(a)** Assume car mass ≈ 1000 kg; Δp = 1000 × 15 = 1.5 × 10⁴ kg m s⁻¹ [1]; F = 1.5 × 10⁴ ÷ 0.1 = 1.5 × 10⁵ N, order **10⁵ N** [1]

**(b)** Deceleration = 15 ÷ 0.1 = 150 m s⁻² [1]; driver mass ≈ 70 kg, so F ≈ 70 × 150 ≈ 1 × 10⁴ N [1]

**(c)** The stopping time (or the car's mass) [1]; doubling the time halves the force to 7.5 × 10⁴ N, which is still order 10⁵ N, so the order of magnitude is unchanged [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* "My assumptions might be wrong" is not an evaluation. Name one input and test the effect of changing it.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Assumptions without units, or no assumptions at all.
- Rounding by the first digit instead of using 3.16.
- Leaving centimetres or litres unconverted.
- Answers quoted to several significant figures.
