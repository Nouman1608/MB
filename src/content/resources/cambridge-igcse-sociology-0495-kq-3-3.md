Key question 3.3 of Cambridge IGCSE Sociology (0495) asks *How has globalisation affected inequalities
between societies?* **Globalisation** is the growing interconnection of the world's societies through
trade, investment, migration, media and technology. The syllabus asks you to examine three things: how
**migration** affects sending and receiving societies, how **global ecological issues** fall unequally
on different societies, and how **Marxist, colonial and feminist** explanations account for global
inequality.

A combined overview of 3.2 and 3.3 is in the
[reducing inequality and global inequality guide](/resources/igcse-cambridge-sociology-reducing-inequality-and-globalisation/);
this guide treats 3.3 in more depth with different case studies.

## Syllabus coverage

Cambridge IGCSE Sociology 0495, syllabus for 2025, 2026 and 2027, **3.3 How has globalisation affected
inequalities between societies?**

- **3.3.1** The impact of migration on societies: immigration/emigration; push and pull factors;
  increasing cultural diversity; economic consequences; negative perceptions of migrants.
- **3.3.2** The impact of global ecological issues on societies: the influence of transnational
  companies; climate change; pollution; urbanisation.
- **3.3.3** Sociological explanations for global inequalities: Marxist views of capitalist
  exploitation; colonialism; feminist ideas of patriarchy.

## Migration (3.3.1)

### Immigration, emigration and push–pull factors

**Immigration** is moving into a country to live; **emigration** is leaving one. The geographer Everett
Lee's theory of migration (1966) is the source of the familiar idea of **push factors** (conditions
that drive people out) and **pull factors** (attractions elsewhere), with **intervening obstacles**
such as cost, distance and border controls in between.

Globalisation affects all three. Cheaper travel and communication lower obstacles; global media make
opportunities elsewhere visible; and global economic change can create push factors, for example when
local industries close because cheaper imports arrive.

### Economic consequences: who gains, who loses?

| | Gains | Losses |
|---|---|---|
| **Receiving society** | Workers fill shortages in sectors such as health care, construction and agriculture; migrants pay taxes; new businesses | Possible pressure on housing and services in some areas; some employers use migrant labour to keep wages low |
| **Sending society** | **Remittances** — money migrants send home — support families, schooling and small businesses | **Brain drain** — loss of trained doctors, nurses, engineers and teachers whose training the sending country paid for |
| **Migrants themselves** | Higher wages, safety, education for children | Exploitation, insecure work, separation from family, discrimination |

An example of how migrants can lack power is the **kafala** sponsorship system used in several Gulf
states, under which a migrant worker's legal residence is tied to their employer. Critics, including
human rights organisations, argue it leaves workers open to exploitation because changing jobs or
leaving can be very difficult.

### Increasing cultural diversity

Migration makes receiving societies more culturally diverse — languages, religions, foods and
festivals multiply. In Britain, the arrival of the ship *Empire Windrush* in 1948, carrying passengers
from the Caribbean, is often used to mark the start of large-scale post-war migration from
Commonwealth countries. Diversity can enrich societies but may also produce tension.

### Negative perceptions of migrants

Migrants are often **stereotyped** and **scapegoated** — blamed for problems such as unemployment or
housing shortages that have wider causes. Media coverage can amplify fear, and political campaigns
may use migration as an issue. Negative perceptions feed **discrimination** in jobs and housing, which
keeps many migrants in low-paid work and reinforces inequality inside the receiving society.

## Global ecological issues (3.3.2)

### Transnational companies (TNCs)

TNCs are companies operating in many countries. They bring investment, jobs and technology, but
critics argue they move production to wherever wages and environmental rules are lowest.

- **Bhopal, India, 1984** — a gas leak at a pesticide plant owned by a subsidiary of the US company
  Union Carbide killed thousands of people and injured many more; campaigners argue victims waited
  years for limited compensation.
- **Rana Plaza, Bangladesh, 2013** — the collapse of a building housing garment factories that supplied
  global clothing brands killed more than 1,100 workers, drawing attention to unsafe conditions in
  global supply chains.

These cases show how the costs of global production can fall on workers in poorer societies while
profits flow to shareholders elsewhere.

### Climate change

Climate change is driven mainly by greenhouse gases from burning fossil fuels, historically produced
mostly by industrialised countries. Yet its effects — drought, floods, storms, crop failure, rising sea
levels — often hit hardest in poorer societies that have fewer resources to adapt. Low-lying island
states such as Tuvalu and the Maldives face threats to their very territory. The Paris Agreement (2015)
committed countries to limiting global warming, but debates continue over how far richer countries
should pay for the damage and adaptation needed elsewhere.

### Pollution

Pollution often moves down the global hierarchy. Hazardous waste, including discarded electronics,
has been shipped from richer to poorer countries, where it may be dismantled by hand without
protection. Mining and oil extraction can poison water and land used by local communities, while the
products are consumed far away.

### Urbanisation

Globalisation speeds the growth of cities as people move from the countryside for work. Where housing
and infrastructure cannot keep up, **informal settlements** grow — Dharavi in Mumbai and Kibera in
Nairobi are well-known examples — often without secure tenure, clean water or sanitation. Urban
growth can bring opportunity and higher incomes, but also concentrates poverty and exposure to
pollution and flooding.

## Explaining global inequality (3.3.3)

### Marxist views of capitalist exploitation

Marxists see global inequality as the result of capitalism operating on a world scale.

- **Dependency theory** — Andre Gunder Frank argued that rich "metropolis" countries developed by
  draining surplus from poor "satellite" countries, so underdevelopment is *caused* by the global
  system rather than being an early stage of development.
- **World-systems theory** — Immanuel Wallerstein divided the world into a **core** of rich industrial
  states, a **periphery** supplying raw materials and cheap labour, and a **semi-periphery** in between.
- Today, Marxists point to TNC profits flowing back to shareholders, unequal trade in which raw
  materials earn little compared with finished goods, and debt repayments from poor countries to rich
  lenders.

### Colonialism

European powers colonised much of Africa, Asia and the Americas; at the Berlin Conference of 1884–85
European states set rules for dividing Africa among themselves. Colonial rule extracted minerals and
crops, built transport to move goods to ports rather than to connect regions, drew borders that
ignored existing communities, and limited education. Many former colonies were left dependent on
exporting a narrow range of raw materials, a pattern that critics call **neo-colonialism** when it
continues through trade and debt after independence.

### Feminist ideas of patriarchy

Feminists argue that global inequality is also gendered:

- **Global assembly lines** — factories in export zones often employ young women because they can be
  paid less and are assumed to be docile, as the Rana Plaza workforce illustrates.
- **Migration** — many women migrate as domestic workers or carers, filling gaps in richer households
  while their own children are cared for by relatives at home.
- **Environmental burden** — where women are responsible for collecting water, fuel and food, droughts
  and deforestation add directly to their unpaid work.
- **Ownership** — women's limited access to land, credit and property in many societies keeps them
  poorer than men.

## Worked example: Question 3(e)-style "discuss"

*Discuss the impact of transnational companies on inequalities between societies.*

> TNCs can **reduce** inequality between societies by investing in poorer countries, creating jobs,
> transferring technology and paying taxes. Factory work may pay more than farm labour and give women
> an independent income.
>
> However, Marxists argue TNCs **increase** inequality: they locate where wages are lowest, and profits
> return to shareholders in richer core countries, as dependency theory suggests. Disasters such as
> Bhopal (1984) and Rana Plaza (2013) show how the human and environmental costs of production fall on
> poorer societies. Feminists add that TNCs often rely on young women's low-paid labour.
>
> Overall, TNCs bring real opportunities but on unequal terms; their effect depends on how strongly
> host governments regulate wages, safety and pollution.

## Common mistakes

- Writing about migration only from the receiving country's point of view.
- Treating globalisation as wholly good or wholly bad; the syllabus asks about its *effects*, which are
  mixed.
- Describing climate change scientifically instead of sociologically — focus on **who is affected and
  why** that is unequal.
- Confusing colonialism (direct political rule) with neo-colonialism (continued economic control).

## Exam technique

- **Explain** — name the process, show the mechanism, give a case study.
- **Discuss** — consider effects on different societies and groups.
- **Evaluate** — weigh evidence that globalisation increases inequality against evidence that it
  reduces it, and judge.
