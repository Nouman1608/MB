## Theory and method: the core link

Every methods debate rests on a theoretical question: **what kind of thing is society, and how can
we know about it?**

- **Positivists** (in the tradition of Auguste Comte and Émile Durkheim) see society as an objective
  structure of "social facts" that can be measured, so they favour quantitative methods. Durkheim's
  *Suicide* (1897) used official statistics to show suicide rates varied with social integration and
  regulation.
- **Interpretivists** see society as built from meanings, so they favour qualitative methods. J.
  Maxwell Atkinson's *Discovering Suicide* (1978) argued suicide statistics are themselves the
  product of coroners' interpretations, so they reveal how deaths are labelled rather than "real"
  rates.
- **Realists and mixed-methods researchers** argue that both structures and meanings matter, which
  justifies **triangulation**.

## Values and value freedom

Can sociology be objective? Three positions are worth comparing.

| Position | Argument | Key thinkers |
|---|---|---|
| **Value-free science** | Sociology can and should be as objective as natural science | Comte and Durkheim (positivism) |
| **Value relevance with value freedom in method** | Values inevitably shape which topics are chosen and how findings are used, but the collection and analysis of data should be as objective as possible | Max Weber |
| **Value commitment** | Value freedom is impossible or undesirable; sociologists should be open about whose side they are on | Alvin Gouldner's "Anti-Minotaur" essay (1962); Howard Becker's "Whose Side Are We On?" (1967) |

Feminists add that supposedly neutral research often reflected male assumptions. Ann Oakley's
essay on interviewing women (1981) argued that a detached, hierarchical interview is neither
possible nor ethical in research with women, favouring a more equal, reciprocal relationship.

## Public sociology and activism

**Michael Burawoy**, in his 2004 presidential address to the American Sociological Association,
distinguished four types of sociology: **professional** (academic research for other sociologists),
**policy** (research commissioned to solve problems for clients such as governments),
**critical** (questioning the assumptions of professional sociology) and **public** (engaging
directly with publics outside the university, such as communities, social movements and the
media). He argued public sociology should be strengthened so that sociology serves civil society.

**Sociology and activism** asks whether researchers should also campaign for change. Arguments for:
sociology exposes injustice (Mills's sociological imagination, feminist research, research on
poverty and racism); participants may benefit from research that leads to action. Arguments against:
activism may bias research, damage credibility with policymakers and blur the line between evidence
and advocacy. A balanced answer notes that Weber's distinction between choosing topics by values and
conducting research objectively offers a middle path.

## Structure and agency

**Structural** theories (functionalism, Marxism, many feminisms) explain behaviour through social
structures that shape and constrain individuals. **Social action** theories (symbolic interactionism)
stress **agency**: people interpret situations and act creatively. Anthony Giddens' **structuration
theory** (*The Constitution of Society*, 1984) argues for a **duality of structure**: structures both
constrain action and are reproduced or changed by it, as language rules constrain speech yet exist
only because people keep speaking. The debate maps onto method choice: structural theories lean
towards large-scale quantitative data, action theories towards small-scale qualitative studies.

## The main theories in brief

| Theory | Core claim | Neo- or later developments | Typical methods |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Functionalism** | Society is a system of interdependent parts based on value consensus (Durkheim, Parsons) | Robert Merton: **manifest and latent functions** and **dysfunctions**; **neo-functionalism** (associated with Jeffrey Alexander) accepts conflict and agency within a broadly functionalist framework | Comparative and statistical analysis |
| **Marxism** | Society is shaped by class conflict between owners and workers; ideology legitimises exploitation | **Neo-Marxisms**: Gramsci's hegemony; Althusser's structural Marxism; the Frankfurt School's critique of the culture industry; cultural studies on resistance through subcultures | Historical, documentary and critical analysis; also ethnography of resistance |
| **Feminisms** | Society is structured by gender inequality | Liberal, Marxist, radical, black and intersectional feminisms; Kimberlé Crenshaw coined **intersectionality** (1989) for overlapping oppressions | Often qualitative, participatory, reflexive methods |
| **Symbolic interactionism** | Society is created through interaction and shared meanings (Mead) | Herbert Blumer coined the term and set out three premises: people act towards things on the basis of meanings; meanings arise from interaction; meanings are modified through interpretation | Participant observation, unstructured interviews |
| **Postmodernism** | Grand theories ("metanarratives") have lost credibility; society is fragmented and media-saturated | Jean-François Lyotard (*The Postmodern Condition*, 1979): "incredulity toward metanarratives"; Jean Baudrillard: **hyperreality** | Discourse and media analysis; scepticism about objective truth |

**Postmodernism and postmodernity.** *Postmodernity* refers to a claimed new era (globalised,
consumer-based, media-saturated); *postmodernism* refers to theories that reject grand narratives.
Critics such as Giddens and Ulrich Beck accept major change but describe it as **late** or
**reflexive modernity** rather than a break with modernity.

## Writing the 20-mark extended response

A strong structure for a question such as *"Evaluate the view that sociology can and should be
value free"*:

1. **Define** the key term precisely (value freedom: research free from the researcher's personal,
   political or moral values).
2. **Present the view** with theory and evidence (positivism; Durkheim's scientific method).
3. **Develop alternatives** (Weber's middle position; Gouldner and Becker; feminist critiques;
   Burawoy's public sociology).
4. **Apply** to research examples, including from Unit 1 families research or your own society.
5. **Evaluate** throughout, not only at the end, using "however" and "this suggests".
6. **Conclude** with a judgement that follows from the argument (for example, that full value
   freedom is impossible, but transparent methods and Weber's distinction protect objectivity).

## Worked example: annotated paragraph

> Weber argued that values are unavoidable in choosing a topic, since sociologists study what they
> regard as important (**knowledge**). For example, a researcher concerned about gender inequality
> may choose to study domestic labour (**application**). However, Weber insisted that once the topic
> is chosen, data should be collected and analysed objectively, so that other researchers could
> check the findings (**analysis**). This offers a compromise between positivists, who think
> objectivity is easy, and Becker, who argues researchers inevitably take sides, although critics
> reply that values also shape how concepts are operationalised, so bias can enter the method itself
> (**evaluation**).

## Common mistakes

- Treating positivism and functionalism as the same thing: one is a methodology, the other a theory.
- Mentioning Burawoy without his four types of sociology.
- Describing theories without linking them to methods or to value debates.
- Ending with "it depends" instead of a reasoned judgement.
- Listing practical strengths and weaknesses of methods with no theoretical justification.
