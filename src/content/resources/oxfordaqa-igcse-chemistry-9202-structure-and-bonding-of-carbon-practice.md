## Section A

**1.** How many covalent bonds does each carbon atom form in graphite?

A: 1

B: 2

C: 3

D: 4

**[1]**

**2.** Give the electronic structure of carbon and use it to explain why carbon can form four covalent bonds. **[2]**

**3.** State the type of structure found in diamond. **[1]**

**4.** State **two** uses of fullerenes. **[2]**

**5.** Fullerenes have different numbers of carbon atoms. State what the structures of fullerenes are based on. **[1]**

**6.** Name the particles that allow graphite to conduct electricity. **[1]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain why diamond is very hard. **[3]**

**8.** Graphite is used as a dry lubricant in locks.

**(a)** Explain why graphite is slippery. **[2]**

**(b)** A student suggests that the lubricant would melt if the lock became warm. Explain why this is not true. **[2]**

**9.** Explain why graphite conducts electricity but diamond does not. **[4]**

**10.** Graphite is described as being "similar to metals". Give the way in which they are similar, and one property they share because of it. **[2]**

**11.** Information: carbon nanotubes are cylinders of carbon atoms. Short lengths of nanotubes are mixed into the frames of some tennis rackets. The rackets are stiffer and stronger but no heavier than rackets without nanotubes.

**(a)** Name the family of carbon structures to which nanotubes belong. **[1]**

**(b)** Use the information to suggest why nanotubes are added to tennis racket frames. **[2]**

**12.** Silicon dioxide, diamond and graphite are all giant covalent structures.

**(a)** State one property that all three have in common, and explain it. **[2]**

**(b)** State one property in which graphite differs from both diamond and silicon dioxide, and explain the difference. **[2]**

**(c)** A student writes: "Diamond and graphite have different properties because they are made of different elements." Explain why this is wrong and give the correct reason for their different properties. **[2]**

**13.** Compare the structures and properties of diamond and graphite. **[6]**

**14.** Information: a fullerene molecule can be made with a hollow cage of carbon atoms. A drug molecule can be held inside the cage and released slowly in the body.

**(a)** State the use of fullerenes that this information describes. **[1]**

**(b)** Suggest the feature of the fullerene's structure that makes this use possible. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** C [1]. **[1]**

**2.** 2,4 [1]. It has four outer electrons, each of which can be shared in a covalent bond [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Giant covalent (structure / lattice) [1]. **[1]**

**4.** Any two [2]: drug delivery into the body; lubricants; catalysts; nanotubes for reinforcing materials. **[2]**

**5.** Hexagonal rings of carbon atoms [1]. **[1]**

**6.** Delocalised electrons [1]. **[1]**

**7.** Each carbon atom forms four covalent bonds [1] with other carbon atoms in a giant (three-dimensional) structure [1]; covalent bonds are strong, so a lot of energy is needed to break them [1]. **[3]**

**8. (a)** Graphite has layers with no covalent bonds between them / only weak forces between them [1], so the layers slide over each other easily [1].

**(b)** Melting requires breaking the strong covalent bonds within the layers [1]; this needs a very large amount of energy, so graphite has a very high melting point [1].

**[4]**

**9.** In graphite each carbon atom forms three bonds [1], so one electron per atom is delocalised [1]; these electrons can move through the structure and carry charge [1]. In diamond all four outer electrons are used in bonds, so there are no delocalised electrons [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Both have delocalised electrons [1]; both conduct electricity / conduct heat [1]. **[2]**

**11. (a)** Fullerenes [1]

**(b)** They make the frame stronger / stiffer [1] without adding significant mass, so the racket stays light [1].

**[3]**

**12. (a)** Very high melting point [1]: many strong covalent bonds must be broken to melt them [1].

**(b)** Graphite conducts electricity (or graphite is soft) [1]: it has delocalised electrons (or its layers can slide), whereas diamond and silicon dioxide have no delocalised electrons (or have rigid three-dimensional networks) [1].

**(c)** Both are made only of carbon atoms [1]; their properties differ because the carbon atoms are bonded and arranged differently (four bonds in a 3D lattice in diamond; three bonds in layers in graphite) [1].

**[6]**

**13.** One mark for each point (credit comparison):

- both are giant structures of carbon atoms joined by strong covalent bonds, so both have very high melting points [1]
- diamond: each atom forms four bonds in a three-dimensional lattice; graphite: each atom forms three bonds in layers of hexagonal rings [1]
- diamond is very hard; graphite is soft and slippery [1]
- because graphite's layers have only weak forces between them, so they slide [1]
- graphite conducts electricity; diamond does not [1]
- because graphite has one delocalised electron per atom, while all diamond's outer electrons are in bonds [1]

**[6]**

**14. (a)** Drug delivery into the body [1]

**(b)** The structure is hollow, so a molecule can be carried inside it [1].

**[2]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "weak covalent bonds between the layers".
- Saying graphite has a low melting point because it is soft.
- Explaining conductivity without the word "delocalised".
- Describing diamond and graphite separately in a "compare" question.
- Calling fullerenes giant structures.
- Using a property in answer to a "use" question without explaining it from structure.
