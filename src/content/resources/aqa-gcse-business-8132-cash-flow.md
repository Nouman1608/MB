This guide covers section 3.6.2 Cash flow of the AQA GCSE Business (8132) specification, for first teaching from September 2017, in Topic 6, Finance. The [Finance study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-finance/) introduces the forecast layout and the five solutions; this page practises completing missing figures, explains exactly why cash and profit differ, and shows how to match a solution to the cause of a problem. All businesses and figures are invented.

## What the specification asks for

| You must be able to | Covered below |
|---|---|
| Understand the importance of cash to a business, the consequences of cash flow problems and the effects of positive cash flow | Why cash matters |
| Understand the difference between cash and profit | Cash is not profit |

| Evaluate solutions: re-scheduling payments, overdrafts, reducing cash outflow, increasing cash inflow, finding new sources of finance | Solving problems |

The specification states that you will not be expected to construct a whole cash flow forecast. You will be expected to fill in missing figures and say what a forecast shows.

## Why cash matters

**Cash** is the money a business holds in notes, coins and its bank account, available to spend now. **Cash flow** is the movement of money into and out of the business over time.

Bills must be paid in cash, on time. Staff expect wages on payday, landlords expect rent on the due date, and suppliers expect payment by the end of the credit period. A business cannot pay them with orders it has taken or profit it expects to make next month.

### Consequences of cash flow problems

- **Staff and suppliers go unpaid.** Staff may leave; suppliers may stop delivering or demand cash on delivery.
- **Expensive emergency borrowing.** The business may have to use a costly overdraft or loan.
- **Missed opportunities.** It cannot take a bulk-buying discount or an order that needs materials bought first.
- **Damaged reputation and credit.** Late payers find suppliers and banks less willing to deal with them.
- **Insolvency.** If the business cannot pay its debts as they fall due, it may be forced to close, even if it is profitable on paper.

### Effects of positive cash flow

When more cash comes in than goes out, the business can pay bills on time, keep a safety margin for emergencies, take advantage of discounts and opportunities, and fund growth without borrowing.

## Cash is not profit

**Profit** is revenue minus costs over a period. It is recorded when a sale is made, even if the customer has not yet paid. **Cash** only changes when money actually moves. The two diverge because of timing.

### Worked example: profitable but short of cash

In March, Hallworth Joinery completes and invoices a £30,000 kitchen fit-out. The customer has 60 days to pay. In March it pays £22,000 for timber, fittings and wages.

| | March |
|---|---|
| Revenue recorded | £30,000 |
| Costs | £22,000 |
| Profit | **£8,000** |
| Cash in | £0 |
| Cash out | £22,000 |
| Net cash flow | **−£22,000** |

On paper Hallworth made £8,000 profit. In reality its bank balance fell by £22,000. If it started March with £15,000, it would end with −£7,000 and need an overdraft to cover the gap until May.

Other reasons cash and profit differ:

- **Loans received** add cash but are not revenue, so cash rises with no profit.
- **Buying equipment** uses a lot of cash at once, while the cost is spread over the years it is used.
- **Owner's capital** paid in increases cash but is not profit.

## Cash flow forecasts

A **cash flow forecast** predicts the cash coming in and going out over future months. It helps a business spot shortages before they happen, plan when to buy, and show a bank it has thought ahead.

| Row | Meaning | How to calculate |
|---|---|---|
| Cash inflows | Money coming in: cash sales, payments from credit customers, loans received | Add the inflow items |
| Cash outflows | Money going out: wages, materials, rent, loan repayments | Add the outflow items |
| Net cash flow | The difference for the month | Inflows − outflows |
| Opening balance | Cash at the start of the month | Last month's closing balance |
| Closing balance | Cash at the end of the month | Opening balance + net cash flow |

### Worked example: completing a forecast

Bramwell Landscapes has an opening balance of £2,000 in January. Find the missing figures.

| £ | Jan | Feb | Mar | Apr |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Total cash inflows | 18,000 | 9,000 | 7,500 | 21,000 |
| Wages | 6,000 | 6,000 | 6,000 | 6,000 |
| Materials | 4,500 | 5,000 | 4,000 | 5,500 |
| Other costs | 2,000 | 2,000 | 2,000 | 2,000 |
| Total cash outflows | 12,500 | **?** | 12,000 | **?** |
| Net cash flow | 5,500 | **?** | **?** | **?** |
| Opening balance | 2,000 | **?** | **?** | **?** |
| Closing balance | 7,500 | **?** | **?** | **?** |

Working:

- February outflows: 6,000 + 5,000 + 2,000 = **13,000**. Net cash flow: 9,000 − 13,000 = **−4,000**. Opening: **7,500**. Closing: 7,500 − 4,000 = **3,500**.
- March net cash flow: 7,500 − 12,000 = **−4,500**. Opening: **3,500**. Closing: 3,500 − 4,500 = **−1,000**.
- April outflows: 6,000 + 5,500 + 2,000 = **13,500**. Net: 21,000 − 13,500 = **7,500**. Opening: **−1,000**. Closing: −1,000 + 7,500 = **6,500**.

### Interpreting it

The business is forecast to be overdrawn by £1,000 at the end of March. The cause is seasonal: winter brings little work while wages and other costs stay fixed. April's inflows restore a healthy balance of £6,500. The problem is short and small, and the forecast gives warning in January, so there is time to arrange a solution.

When interpreting, always say: what happens, when, why, and how serious it is.

## Solving cash flow problems

| Solution | How it works | Best when | Drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Re-scheduling payments | Agree to pay suppliers or the landlord later | A short gap and good supplier relationships | Supplier may refuse or charge more |
| Overdraft | Bank lets the account go negative to a limit | A short, temporary gap | High interest; can be withdrawn |
| Reducing cash outflow | Cut or delay spending, buy cheaper materials | Costs are too high or spending can wait | Quality, growth or staff morale may suffer |
| Increasing cash inflow | Chase late payers, offer discounts for prompt payment, run promotions | Customers are slow to pay or sales are low | Discounts reduce revenue; promotions cost money first |
| Finding new sources of finance | Loan, share issue, selling assets | A long-term shortage or a large investment | Interest, loss of control, or a one-off sum |

### Match the solution to the cause

- **Seasonal, short gap** (Bramwell): an overdraft or re-scheduling a supplier payment for a few weeks. Cutting wages would lose skilled staff before the busy season.
- **Customers paying late** (Hallworth): chase payment or offer a small discount for paying early. Borrowing treats the symptom, not the cause.
- **Spending more than it earns every month**: reduce outflows or increase sales. An overdraft only delays the problem and adds interest.
- **Large one-off purchase**: a loan or other new finance, matched to the life of the asset.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Evaluate whether Bramwell Landscapes should use an overdraft to solve its forecast cash flow problem.*

"An overdraft suits Bramwell because the shortfall is only £1,000 and lasts one month before April's inflows of £21,000 restore the balance. *(application of the forecast)* It is quick to arrange and Bramwell only pays interest on what it uses. *(benefit)* However, overdraft interest is high, and an alternative is to ask the materials supplier to delay March's £4,000 payment by a few weeks, which would avoid the negative balance at no cost. *(alternative)* I would try re-scheduling first, keeping the overdraft as a back-up, because the problem is small and temporary." *(judgement with reason)*

## Common mistakes

- Using profit and cash as if they are the same.
- Forgetting that a closing balance becomes the next month's opening balance.
- Adding a negative net cash flow instead of subtracting it.
- Saying a negative net cash flow means the business is overdrawn; it is the closing balance that shows this.
- Recommending a long-term loan for a one-month seasonal dip.
- Interpreting a forecast without saying when the problem occurs or why.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Calculate or complete:** show the method for each cell, with negatives clearly marked.
- **Explain:** a consequence or solution with its effect on the business.
- **Analyse:** what the forecast shows, the cause and its seriousness.
- **Evaluate:** compare solutions against the cause and size of the problem, then choose.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between cash and profit?**
Profit is revenue minus costs over a period, recorded when sales are made. Cash is the money actually available, which only changes when payments move in or out.

**Do I need to construct a whole cash flow forecast?**
No. The specification says you will complete and interpret sections of one, not construct a whole forecast.

**How do you calculate the closing balance?**
Add the net cash flow for the month to the opening balance. The closing balance then becomes the next month's opening balance.
