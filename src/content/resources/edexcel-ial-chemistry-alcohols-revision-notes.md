## Naming and classes (10.15, 10.16)

- Longest chain containing the C–OH carbon; lowest number to the –OH carbon; suffix "-ol" with its locant (butan-1-ol).
- Two –OH groups: keep the "e", add "-diol" (ethane-1,2-diol).
- Structural: CH₃CH₂CH₂OH. Displayed: every bond, including O–H. Skeletal: zigzag carbon chain, –OH written in full.
- Classify by the C–OH carbon only.
- General formula of the non-cyclic alcohols with one –OH: CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH, so each extra CH₂ adds 14.0 to the Mr.

| Class | Carbon neighbours of the –OH carbon | Hydrogens still on that carbon |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | 1 (0 for methanol) | 2 |
| Secondary | 2 | 1 |
| Tertiary | 3 | 0 |

## Reaction summary (10.17, 10.18)

| Reaction | Reagent and conditions | Product | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Combustion | Excess oxygen, ignite | CO₂ + H₂O | Balance O last |
| Chlorination | PCl₅ (solid), room temperature | Chloroalkane + POCl₃ + HCl | Steamy fumes of HCl |
| Bromination | KBr + 50% concentrated H₂SO₄, warm | Bromoalkane | HBr made in the flask |
| Iodination | Red phosphorus + iodine, warm | Iodoalkane | PI₃ made in the flask |
| Elimination | Concentrated H₃PO₄, heat | Alkene + H₂O | Check E-Z |
| Oxidation, 1°, distil | K₂Cr₂O₇ / dilute H₂SO₄, warm, distil as it forms | Aldehyde | Orange → green |
| Oxidation, 1°, reflux | Excess K₂Cr₂O₇ / dilute H₂SO₄, reflux | Carboxylic acid | Orange → green |
| Oxidation, 2° | K₂Cr₂O₇ / dilute H₂SO₄, heat | Ketone | Orange → green |
| Oxidation, 3° | K₂Cr₂O₇ / dilute H₂SO₄, heat | No reaction | Stays orange |

## Equations to know

| Change | Equation |
|---|---|
| PCl₅ | ROH + PCl₅ → RCl + POCl₃ + HCl |
| Making HBr | KBr + H₂SO₄ → KHSO₄ + HBr |
| Bromination | ROH + HBr → RBr + H₂O |
| Making PI₃ | 2P + 3I₂ → 2PI₃ |
| Iodination | 3ROH + PI₃ → 3RI + H₃PO₃ |
| Elimination | CH₃CH₂CH₂OH → CH₃CH=CH₂ + H₂O |
| 1° to aldehyde | RCH₂OH + [O] → RCHO + H₂O |
| 1° to acid | RCH₂OH + 2[O] → RCOOH + H₂O |
| 2° to ketone | R₂CHOH + [O] → R₂CO + H₂O |

Count the [O]: one for aldehyde or ketone, two for carboxylic acid. Water appears once in each.

## Method in steps: alkenes from elimination

1. Find the C–OH carbon.
2. Look at each adjacent carbon; keep only those with a hydrogen to lose.
3. Draw one C=C for each different neighbour (identical neighbours give the same alkene).
4. For each alkene, check whether both C=C carbons have two different groups. If so, give E and Z.
5. Count and name every product.

## Method in steps: choosing the oxidation set-up

1. Classify the alcohol.
2. Tertiary: no reaction. Secondary: heat, ketone.
3. Primary and you want the aldehyde: alcohol in excess, add oxidising agent slowly, distil immediately, cool the receiver.
4. Primary and you want the acid: oxidising agent in excess, reflux, then distil the acid out.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Distil vs reflux.** The aldehyde has no hydrogen bonding between its molecules, so it boils lowest and escapes if distilled as it forms. Reflux returns it to the flask, where it is oxidised to the acid.
- **Aldehyde test vs acid test.** Aldehyde: Benedict's or Fehling's solution, warm, blue to red precipitate. Acid: sodium carbonate or hydrogencarbonate, effervescence of CO₂ (limewater turns milky).
- **PCl₅ test.** Positive for any –OH, including carboxylic acids and water. Use a dry sample.
- **50% not 100% sulfuric acid.** Fully concentrated acid oxidises HBr to bromine, wasting the HBr.
- **Phosphoric not sulfuric acid for elimination.** Concentrated sulfuric acid is a strong oxidising agent and causes more side reactions.

## Techniques (10.19) in one line each

| Technique | Key point |
|---|---|
| Reflux | Vertical condenser, open top; volatile liquids boil and condense back, so nothing escapes |
| Separating funnel | Immiscible layers; vent often; run off the lower layer; NaHCO₃ wash removes acid (vent the CO₂) |
| Distillation | Thermometer bulb at the side arm; collect the fraction near the expected boiling temperature |
| Drying | Anhydrous MgSO₄ or CaCl₂; add until some stays loose; cloudy liquid turns clear; decant |
| Boiling temperature | Pure: sharp and matches data. Impure: boils over a range |

Typical order: react → distil off crude product → wash in separating funnel → dry → redistil → measure boiling temperature.

## Core Practical 7 in brief (10.20)

- Propanal: warm propan-1-ol in distillation apparatus, add acidified dichromate(VI) dropwise, collect the distillate in an ice-cooled receiver, test with Benedict's or Fehling's.
- Propanoic acid: excess acidified dichromate(VI), reflux, then distil; test with NaHCO₃ or Na₂CO₃.
- Percentage yield: convert a liquid volume to mass with its density, then to moles; use the 1 : 1 ratio; actual ÷ theoretical × 100.

## Small worked reminders

- **Diol naming and classes.** HOCH₂CH(OH)CH₂CH₃ is butane-1,2-diol. The –OH on C1 is primary; the –OH on C2 is secondary. Classify each –OH group separately.
- **Only one neighbour with hydrogen.** CH₃CH₂CH(CH₃)CH₂OH (2-methylbutan-1-ol) has the –OH on C1, and its only neighbour is C2. Elimination gives a single alkene, 2-methylbut-1-ene, with no E-Z isomers because C1 carries two hydrogens.
- **Distilled product.** 3-methylbutan-1-ol, distilled as it forms with acidified dichromate(VI), gives 3-methylbutanal: (CH₃)₂CHCH₂CH₂OH + [O] → (CH₃)₂CHCH₂CHO + H₂O.
- **Volume to moles.** For a liquid reactant, mass = volume × density, then moles = mass ÷ Mr. Do not skip the density step: treating 5.00 cm³ of propan-1-ol as 5.00 g overstates the moles, because its density is below 1 g cm⁻³.

## Quick self-test

1. Name HOCH₂CH₂CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₃ and classify it.
2. Classify 1-methylcyclohexanol.
3. Write a balanced equation for the complete combustion of butan-1-ol.
4. Calculate the mass of oxygen needed to burn 1.85 g of butan-1-ol (Mr 74.0) completely.
5. Give the reagents and conditions to make 1-iodopropane from propan-1-ol.
6. State what you see when PCl₅ is added to dry ethanol, and name the gas.
7. Why must the sample be dry in the PCl₅ test?
8. Name every alkene formed when heptan-4-ol is heated with concentrated phosphoric acid.
9. Name the organic product when pentan-3-ol is heated with acidified potassium dichromate(VI), and give the colour change.
10. How would you tell butanal from butanoic acid with one test?
11. Calculate the atom economy for CH₃CH₂CH₂OH + HBr → CH₃CH₂CH₂Br + H₂O. (Mr: C₃H₇Br 122.9, H₂O 18.0)
12. 2.40 g of propan-1-ol (Mr 60.0) gives 0.928 g of propanal (Mr 58.0). Calculate the percentage yield.

### Answers

1. **3-methylpentan-1-ol**; primary, because only C2 is joined to the –OH carbon.
2. **Tertiary**: the ring carbon bearing –OH is bonded to three carbons (two ring carbons and the methyl).
3. **C₄H₉OH + 6O₂ → 4CO₂ + 5H₂O**.
4. n = 1.85 ÷ 74.0 = 0.0250 mol; n(O₂) = 6 × 0.0250 = 0.150 mol; mass = 0.150 × 32.0 = **4.80 g**.
5. **Red phosphorus and iodine, warmed**.
6. **Steamy fumes**; the gas is **hydrogen chloride**.
7. Water also reacts with PCl₅ to give HCl, so a wet sample gives a positive result whatever it is.
8. Both neighbours (C3 and C5) are equivalent, so one structure: hept-3-ene, as **(E)-hept-3-ene and (Z)-hept-3-ene**.
9. **Pentan-3-one**; **orange to green**.
10. Add sodium hydrogencarbonate: **butanoic acid fizzes** (CO₂); butanal does not. (Or Benedict's: butanal gives a red precipitate, the acid does not.)
11. 122.9 ÷ (122.9 + 18.0) × 100 = **87.2%**.
12. n(alcohol) = 2.40 ÷ 60.0 = 0.0400 mol; n(propanal) = 0.928 ÷ 58.0 = 0.0160 mol; yield = 0.0160 ÷ 0.0400 × 100 = **40.0%**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling an alcohol secondary because the molecule is branched somewhere else; only the C–OH carbon counts.
- Giving a colour change for a tertiary alcohol with dichromate(VI). It stays orange.
- Writing "heat" for the aldehyde without saying the product is distilled off as it forms.
- Using one [O] for the carboxylic acid equation instead of two.
- Leaving POCl₃ or HCl out of the PCl₅ equation.
- Describing the PCl₅ result as "a gas is given off" without "steamy fumes" or a test for HCl.
- Naming only one product from elimination when there are two neighbouring carbons, or missing the E and Z forms.
- Saying the aldehyde boils lower because it has "weaker bonds"; the point is no hydrogen bonding between its molecules.
- Testing an acid with Benedict's solution, or an aldehyde with sodium carbonate.
- Placing the distillation thermometer bulb in the liquid rather than at the side arm.
