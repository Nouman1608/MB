This set practises key question 1.3 of Cambridge IGCSE Sociology (0495), *How do sociologists
investigate society?* Parts (a) to (e) follow the shape the syllabus describes for Paper 1
Question 1; parts (f) to (j) give extra practice on sampling, question types and research issues.

## Source A (invented for practice)

*A researcher studied how 14–16-year-olds experience online bullying. With the head teacher's
permission, she offered an online questionnaire to all 600 students in that age group at one school;
180 completed it. Of these, 41% said they had received hurtful messages in the past year. She then
held three group interviews with six students each, chosen by teachers as "confident speakers". In
the groups, students talked freely about bullying in general but were reluctant to describe their
own experiences.*

---

## Questions

**(a)(i)** Using Source A, identify the percentage of students who completed the questionnaire.
**[1]**

**(a)(ii)** Using Source A, explain why the head teacher acted as a gatekeeper in this research.
**[3]**

**(a)(iii)** Using Source A, explain **two** problems with the evidence in the study. **[4]**

**(b)** Define the terms *operationalisation* and *target population*. **[4]**

**(c)** Explain **three** strengths of covert participant observation. **[6]**

**(d)** Evaluate the view that unstructured interviews are the best method for researching
sensitive topics. **[14]**

**(e)** You have been asked to investigate the experiences of young people who care for a sick or
disabled family member. Explain the primary research methods, sampling and secondary evidence you
would use, and give reasons for your choices. **[8]**

### Extra practice (1.3.2 to 1.3.6)

**(f)** Explain **two** differences between open and closed questions. **[4]**

**(g)** Explain why a stratified sample may be more representative than a quota sample. **[4]**

**(h)** Explain **two** ethical issues a sociologist must consider when researching students in
schools. **[4]**

**(i)** Explain how a researcher's perspective may influence the method they choose. **[4]**

**(j)** A youth club has 120 members: 72 aged 11–14 and 48 aged 15–18. Calculate how many of each age
group should be in a stratified sample of 30, showing your working. **[2]**

## Answers and mark guidance

### (a)(i) [1]

30% (180 of 600) (1).

### (a)(ii) [3]

A gatekeeper is a person who controls access to a group (1). The researcher needed the head teacher's
permission before she could reach the students (1), so the head teacher could have refused, limited
which students took part, or influenced how the research was presented (1).

### (a)(iii) [4]

Two problems, 2 marks each (1 for the problem, 1 for applying it to the source):

- **Low response rate** — only 180 of 600 replied, so those who answered may be the students most
  affected by, or interested in, bullying; 41% may over- or under-state the real level.
- **Biased group selection** — teachers chose "confident speakers", so quieter students, who may be
  more likely to be bullied, were excluded.
- **Group interview effect** — students would not discuss their own experiences in front of peers, so
  the qualitative data lacks validity on personal experience.
- **One school** — findings cannot be generalised to all 14–16-year-olds.
- **Self-reported and undefined** — "hurtful messages" was not operationalised, so students may have
  understood it differently.

### (b) [4]

- **Operationalisation (2):** turning an abstract concept into something that can be measured (1),
  for example measuring "online bullying" by the number of hurtful messages received in a month (1).
- **Target population (2):** the whole group the research is about and to which findings will apply
  (1), for example all 14–16-year-olds in a city (1).

### (c) [6]

Three strengths, 2 marks each (identify + develop):

- **Natural behaviour** — people do not know they are being studied, so there is no Hawthorne effect
  and behaviour is genuine.
- **Access to closed or deviant groups** — groups that would refuse a known researcher can be studied,
  as in Patrick's study of a Glasgow gang (1973).
- **High validity and verstehen** — by sharing members' lives the researcher understands meanings
  from the inside.
- **Unexpected insights** — the researcher can notice things they would not have thought to ask about.

### (d) [14]

**Levels guidance:** 11–14 for a balanced, well-developed answer with evaluation concepts and a
reasoned conclusion; 7–10 for a mostly one-sided or less developed answer; 4–6 for limited points; 1–3
for simple assertions.

**For the view:**
- Rapport and trust develop in a relaxed conversation, so people may reveal painful experiences such
  as abuse or discrimination.
- Respondents explain in their own words, giving high validity and verstehen.
- The interviewer can notice distress and pause, helping prevent harm.
- Oakley's interviews with housewives (1974) show how in-depth interviews uncover experiences that
  statistics miss.

**Against the view:**
- **Interviewer effect** — face-to-face, people may feel embarrassed and give socially desirable
  answers; an anonymous questionnaire may produce more honest admissions.
- Small samples reduce representativeness and generalisability.
- Low reliability: each interview differs and depends on the interviewer's skill.
- Cost and time, and transcription of long interviews.
- Some sensitive topics may be better studied through personal documents or observation.

**Conclusion:** unstructured interviews are strong for understanding meanings but not always best for
honest disclosure or measuring how common an experience is; triangulation with anonymous
questionnaires may be better. Credit a reasoned judgement.

### (e) [8]

No topic knowledge is required and you are **not** expected to evaluate your plan. Credit:

- **Primary method(s) with reasons** — unstructured or semi-structured interviews, because the topic is
  personal and young carers need trust to talk about stress and responsibility (validity, rapport).
  Possibly a short anonymous questionnaire to find how many hours a week they spend caring (patterns).
- **Sampling** — no complete list exists, so snowball sampling through young carers' support groups,
  with group leaders as gatekeepers; or a sampling frame from a support organisation's membership
  list, with a stratified sample by age and gender.
- **Secondary evidence** — official statistics or census data on numbers of young carers; reports from
  charities supporting carers, because they show the wider pattern cheaply.
- **Ethics** — informed consent from young people and their parents or guardians; confidentiality; the
  right to stop; information about support services in case the interview causes distress.

Levels: 7–8 for a clear, justified plan covering all three elements; 4–6 for some reasons; 1–3 for a
list with little justification.

### (f) [4]

Two differences, 2 marks each:

- **Answer format** — open questions let respondents answer in their own words, while closed
  questions give fixed options such as yes/no (1 + 1).
- **Type of data and analysis** — open questions produce qualitative data that is hard to code and
  compare; closed questions produce quantitative data that is quick to count (1 + 1).
- **Validity and reliability** — open questions tend to be more valid; closed questions more reliable
  (1 + 1).

### (g) [4]

In stratified sampling, people are chosen **randomly** within each group from a sampling frame (1), so
the researcher's choices do not affect who is picked (1). In quota sampling, the interviewer **chooses**
who fills each quota (1), so they may pick friendly, available or similar people, introducing bias
even though the numbers in each group are correct (1).

### (h) [4]

Two issues, 2 marks each:

- **Informed consent** — students must understand the research and agree; because they are under 18,
  parents or guardians should also consent (1 + 1).
- **Confidentiality** — answers must not be shown to teachers or classmates, or students could be
  punished or embarrassed (1 + 1).
- **Prevention of harm** — questions on topics such as bullying could cause distress, so support must
  be available (1 + 1).

### (i) [4]

A positivist sees society as made of measurable social facts (1), so will choose standardised methods
such as questionnaires or structured interviews that give reliable, quantitative data (1). An
interpretivist wants to understand meanings (1), so will choose unstructured interviews or
participant observation that give valid, qualitative data (1).

### (j) [2]

11–14: 72 ÷ 120 = 0.6, and 0.6 × 30 = **18**; 15–18: 48 ÷ 120 = 0.4, and 0.4 × 30 = **12** (1 for
correct working, 1 for both correct numbers).

## Frequently asked questions

**Does every part (e) plan need triangulation?** No. One well-justified method can earn full credit;
triangulation is a valid choice if you give a reason for it.

**Should I name studies in (c)?** It is not required, but a correct example such as Patrick's gang
study strengthens the development of a point.
