## 3.1.6.1 Market failure as misallocation of resources

A market works well when the price signal sends resources to the uses that society values most. **Market failure** happens when the market system cannot allocate resources efficiently, so they end up **misallocated**. Too much of some goods is produced and too little of others, compared with what would give society the most benefit from scarce resources.

Think back to opportunity cost. Land, labour and capital are scarce, so every unit used in one place is lost to another. If resources go to a product whose full cost to society is greater than its value to buyers, society gives up something more valuable than it gets.

### The costs of misallocation

- **Lost welfare.** Society could have been better off with the same resources. In the data below, one block of output costs 65 to society but is worth only 50.
- **Harm to third parties.** People who were not part of the deal bear costs, such as polluted water or noise.
- **Under-use of beneficial goods.** Worthwhile activities, such as some health care or training, are not provided at the right scale.
- **Wasted opportunity.** The resources could have made things people value more.
- **Possible long-term damage.** Depleted or damaged resources cannot be used by future generations.

### Methods of government intervention

The specification asks for methods of intervention that counter misallocation without giving a fixed list. These are standard methods you can use. Section 3.2.3.4 covers policies for externalities in more detail.

| Method | How it works | One strength | One limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tax on a good with a negative externality | Raises the producer's cost, which cuts output | Raises revenue | Hard to set at the right level |
| Subsidy for a good with a positive externality | Lowers cost, so output rises | Encourages useful activity | Costs taxpayers and has an opportunity cost |
| Regulation, laws or bans | Sets rules on what firms or people may do | Direct and clear | Needs enforcing, so costs money |
| Direct provision by government | Government supplies the good itself | Reaches everyone | Uses scarce public funds |
| Information and education | Changes what people choose to buy | Cheap compared with the others | Results are slow and uncertain |

Judging a method means asking whether it fixes the specific gap, who pays, and what else the money could have done.

## 3.1.6.2 Externalities

An **externality** is a cost or a benefit that lands on someone outside the deal between buyer and seller.

- **Private costs** are paid by the buyer or seller, and **private benefits** go to them.
- **External costs** and **external benefits** fall on others.
- Add the external cost to the private cost to get the **social cost**, and do the same for benefits to get the **social benefit**.

The externality is the difference between the social and the private amount. A decision-maker who weighs only private costs and benefits ignores the effect on others, so the market output is wrong from society's view.

### Four cases

| | Production | Consumption |
|---|---|---|
| **Negative** | A factory dumps waste in a river that others use | Loud late-night music disturbs neighbours |
| **Positive** | A firm trains staff, and some later move to other employers | A person who is vaccinated cuts the risk of passing on infection |

The specification states that production and consumption can both lead to negative externalities. The positive cases are the mirror image.

**Negative externality chain.** Producers pay only private costs, so the cost they face is too low. Their supply is higher than society would choose. Price is lower and output higher than the social best. The good is **over-produced** or over-consumed.

**Positive externality chain.** Buyers count only their private benefit, so demand is lower than the full social value. Price and output are lower than the social best. The good is **under-produced** or under-consumed.

### Worked example 1: a negative production externality

A dye works in an illustrative place called Town X. Each block is 100 tonnes. Values are in money units per block. The external cost of dumping waste is 15 per block.

| Block | Private benefit (what buyers pay) | Private cost | Social cost (private + 15) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 80 | 20 | 35 |
| 2 | 70 | 30 | 45 |
| 3 | 60 | 40 | 55 |
| 4 | 50 | 50 | 65 |
| 5 | 40 | 60 | 75 |
| 6 | 30 | 70 | 85 |

1. The firm adds blocks while private benefit is at least private cost. Block 4 gives 50 against 50, and block 5 gives 40 against 60. **Market output is 4 blocks.**
2. Society adds blocks while benefit is at least social cost. Block 3 gives 60 against 55. Block 4 gives 50 against 65. **Socially best output is 3 blocks.**
3. The market over-produces by 1 block.
4. The loss from that block: social cost 65 minus benefit 50 = **15**. This equals the external cost per block.
5. External cost at market output: 4 × 15 = 60. At the best output: 3 × 15 = 45.
6. A tax of 15 per block makes the firm's cost equal the social cost. Block 4 now costs the firm 65 against a benefit of 50, so it is not produced. Output falls to 3 blocks. Revenue: 3 × 15 = **45**.

Notice that the best outcome is **not** zero pollution. Block 3 still carries an external cost, yet its benefit of 60 beats its social cost of 55.

Describe the same case as a diagram. The vertical axis is cost and benefit and the horizontal axis is quantity. The demand curve slopes down. The private cost (supply) curve slopes up. A second, higher curve shows social cost. Market equilibrium is where demand meets the private cost curve. The social optimum is where demand meets the higher social cost curve, at a smaller quantity and a higher price. The gap between the two cost curves at any quantity equals the external cost. The triangle between them, from the social optimum to the market output, is the welfare loss.

### Worked example 2: a positive consumption externality

Island B (illustrative) has a vaccination programme. Each unit is 1,000 doses. The cost per unit is 40 to supply. Each unit gives an external benefit of 25 by lowering the spread of infection.

| Unit | Private benefit | Social benefit (private + 25) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 90 | 115 |
| 2 | 70 | 95 |
| 3 | 50 | 75 |
| 4 | 30 | 55 |
| 5 | 10 | 35 |

1. Buyers take a unit if private benefit is at least 40. Unit 3 gives 50. Unit 4 gives 30. **Market output is 3 units.**
2. Society takes a unit if social benefit is at least 40. Unit 4 gives 55. Unit 5 gives 35. **Socially best output is 4 units.**
3. The market under-provides by 1 unit.
4. The gain from the missing unit: 55 minus 40 = **15**.
5. A subsidy of 25 per unit lowers the cost to buyers to 15. Unit 4 now gives 30 against 15, so it is bought. Unit 5 gives 10 against 15, so it is not. Output rises to 4 units.
6. The subsidy bill: 4 × 25 = **100**. This equals the external benefit created (4 × 25).

In the diagram, the social benefit curve lies **above** the demand curve. The social optimum is at a higher quantity than the market equilibrium.

### Linking to elasticity

How well a tax works depends on how consumers respond. Suppose a tax on a harmful good raises its price by 10%, and price elasticity of demand is -0.4. Quantity falls by 0.4 × 10 = 4%. Demand is inelastic, so the fall is small. With inelastic demand the state collects plenty of money, but people keep buying almost as much.

### Evaluating intervention

- **Measurement.** The size of an external cost or benefit is hard to value. A tax set too high or too low still leaves misallocation.
- **Who pays.** Taxes can hit poorer households harder. Subsidies cost taxpayers.
- **Time.** Education campaigns take years. A ban acts quickly.
- **Side effects.** Higher costs might push a firm to move abroad or raise prices.
- **Opportunity cost.** Funds spent on one policy cannot be spent on another.

A strong evaluation chooses a method, gives a reason that fits the situation, and weighs one limit against it.

## Common errors

- Writing that the market "fails" because prices are high. Market failure is about misallocation.
- Mixing up private and social. Social = private + external.
- Saying the best level of a polluting activity is zero.
- Drawing a subsidy as a tax. Subsidies shift supply down and to the right.
- Naming a method without saying what it changes.
- Giving a one-sided answer where the question says "evaluate".
