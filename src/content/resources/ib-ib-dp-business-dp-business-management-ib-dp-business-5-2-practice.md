They focus on choosing and changing methods, which the [earlier Unit 5 practice set](/resources/ib-dp-business-management-introduction-to-operations-management-and-operations-methods-practice/) touches only briefly. All businesses and figures are invented, and money is in dollars.

## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Define the term *job production*. **[2]**

**2.** For each of the following, state the most suitable operations method: (i) a firm of architects designing a new museum; (ii) a factory producing one model of plastic coat hanger all day; (iii) an online shop where customers choose the wood, finish and size of a desk from set options. **[3]**

**3.** Give one example of a service business that uses batch production and explain why it is batch. **[2]**

**4.** Outline two factors, other than demand, that affect a business's choice of operations method. **[4]**

**5.** Brackwater Crafts can make planters by two methods. Method P has fixed costs of 7,500 a year and variable cost of 36 a planter. Method Q has fixed costs of 27,000 a year and variable cost of 23 a planter.

**(a)** Calculate the output at which the two methods have the same total cost. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the cost per planter under each method at 1,200 planters a year. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why mass/flow production may be unsuitable when demand is seasonal. **[3]**

**7.** Distinguish between mass customization and batch production. **[2]**

**8.** Explain one human resource problem that may arise when a business moves from batch to flow production. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**9.** Ferngully Prints makes art prints by job production, each one hand-finished to a client's order. Orders for a few popular designs have grown, and the owner proposes making those designs in batches of 200.

**(a)** Explain two benefits to Ferngully Prints of making the popular designs in batches. **[4]**

**(b)** Explain two risks of the proposal. **[4]**

**10.** Ashcombe Furniture makes sofas in batches: eight styles in three colours. A consultant suggests a mass customization model where customers choose style, fabric, colour and leg type online, with each sofa built from standard frames and parts after it is ordered.

**(a)** Explain how mass customization could reduce Ashcombe's stock-holding costs. **[2]**

**(b)** Analyse two changes Ashcombe would need to make to its operations. **[4]**

**11.** Tolland Drinks bottles one type of mineral water by batch production. Costs per year: batch method fixed costs 40,000 and variable cost 0.30 a bottle; a proposed flow line would have fixed costs 160,000 and variable cost 0.18 a bottle. Forecast demand is 1.5 million bottles a year, but a large customer that buys 600,000 bottles a year has not renewed its contract.

**(a)** Calculate the total cost under each method at 1.5 million bottles. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the total cost under each method if the large customer is lost. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the output at which the two methods cost the same. **[2]**

**(d)** Evaluate whether Tolland Drinks should invest in the flow line. **[6]**

**12.** Corbel Bikes builds custom racing bicycles by job production. Its founders say that craftsmanship is "who we are". A retailer has offered a contract for 5,000 standard bicycles a year. Discuss whether Corbel Bikes should change its operations method to accept the contract. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** The production of a single, one-off product [1] made to the specification of an individual customer, so each job is different [1]. **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* "Making one product at a time" alone misses the customer specification, which is what separates job production from a small batch.

**2.** (i) Job production [1]. (ii) Mass/flow production [1]. (iii) Mass customization [1]. **[3]**

*Examiner insight:* For (iii), the set options and modular parts are the clue. A choice from fixed components is not a unique job.

**3.** A cinema showing the same film to an audience at set times [1]; the same service is delivered to a group together, then the screen is reset for the next showing [1]. Other valid examples: a coach tour, a class of students, an airline flight. **[2]**

**4.** Any two, each outlined. Finance: flow lines and automated cells need large capital spending, so a business with little capital may be limited to job or batch [1][1]. Labour skills: job production needs multi-skilled workers, so a business without them may prefer batch or flow [1][1]. Also accept nature of the product, technology available, customer expectations or the business's objectives. **[4]**

**5. (a)** 7,500 + 36Q = 27,000 + 23Q, so 13Q = 19,500 [1]; Q = **1,500 planters** [1].

**(b)** Method P: (7,500 + 1,200 × 36) ÷ 1,200 = 50,700 ÷ 1,200 = **42.25** [1]. Method Q: (27,000 + 1,200 × 23) ÷ 1,200 = 54,600 ÷ 1,200 = **45.50** [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* At 1,200 planters the output is below the crossover, so the low-fixed-cost method is cheaper. Checking your answer against the crossover catches arithmetic slips.

**6.** Flow production has high fixed costs from the line [1]. In quiet seasons output falls, so fixed costs are spread over fewer units and cost per unit rises [1]; alternatively, producing at a steady rate builds up unsold stock with storage costs and risk of waste [1]. **[3]**

**7.** Batch production makes groups of identical items before resetting for the next group [1], whereas mass customization combines standard modules so that each unit can be personalized to an individual customer at high volume [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Flow production splits work into short, repetitive tasks or replaces workers with machines [1], so workers may become demotivated or some may be made redundant, which can lower morale and raise labour turnover [1]. **[2]**

**9. (a)** Lower cost per print: the set-up and design work is spread over 200 prints, so Ferngully can lower price or raise margin [1][1]. Faster supply: popular prints can be made ahead and held in stock, so customers receive them sooner [1][1]. **[4]**

**(b)** Unsold stock: if demand for a design falls, prints already made tie up cash and may not sell [1][1]. Brand risk: customers who valued hand-finished, unique prints may see batch prints as less exclusive, damaging the brand [1][1]. **[8]**

*Examiner insight:* Each point needs the effect on this business. "Stock may build up" earns less than saying what that stock costs Ferngully.

**10. (a)** Sofas are built only after an order is placed [1], so Ashcombe no longer holds finished sofas in many styles and colours, cutting warehouse space, insurance and the risk of unsold styles [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** First change: the design must be modular, with standard frames and interchangeable parts [1], so that many combinations can be built quickly from a limited range of components [1]. Second change: an online ordering system linked to production and suppliers is needed [1], so that each order triggers the right parts and fabric to arrive in time, which raises dependence on reliable suppliers [1]. **[6]**

**11. (a)** Batch: 40,000 + 1,500,000 × 0.30 = 40,000 + 450,000 = **490,000** [1]. Flow: 160,000 + 1,500,000 × 0.18 = 160,000 + 270,000 = **430,000** [1].

**(b)** Output = 900,000 bottles. Batch: 40,000 + 270,000 = **310,000** [1]. Flow: 160,000 + 162,000 = **322,000** [1].

**(c)** 40,000 + 0.30Q = 160,000 + 0.18Q, so 0.12Q = 120,000 [1]; Q = **1,000,000 bottles** [1].

**(d)** For: at forecast demand the flow line saves 60,000 a year (490,000 − 430,000) [1]; a single standard product suits flow production [1]. Against: losing the large customer drops output to 900,000, below the 1,000,000 crossover, so flow would cost 12,000 more than batch [1]; the extra 120,000 of fixed costs is a commitment that remains even if demand falls [1]. Other factors: whether Tolland can win replacement customers, and the effect on workers if fewer are needed [1]. Judgement: Tolland should delay the investment until the large contract is renewed or replaced, because the saving depends on output staying above one million bottles [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* The crossover figure is the key evidence. A strong evaluation uses it to show exactly how much demand risk the business can bear.

**12.** Arguments for changing: 5,000 bicycles a year is a large, stable order that suits batch or flow production [1]; spreading fixed costs over high volume would lower cost per unit and bring steady revenue [1]; Corbel could use the contract to grow and reduce dependence on a small number of wealthy buyers [1]. Arguments against: the change needs capital for jigs, machines and space [1]; skilled builders may resist repetitive work and Corbel may need different, less skilled staff [1]; selling standard bikes through a retailer could damage the premium, handmade brand that the founders value [1]. A middle option: keep job production for custom bikes and set up a separate batch line, possibly under a different brand, for the contract [1]. Judgement: accept only if the contract gives enough certainty to repay the investment and the custom line can be protected; otherwise decline, because the brand is Corbel's main advantage [1]. **[8]**

*Examiner insight:* "Discuss" rewards balance. Answers that mention the founders' values and suggest a mixed approach show more application than a general list of method advantages.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a method without using clues from the stem, such as set options or a one-off design.
- Saying a method is "cheaper" without stating per unit and at what output.
- Ignoring the crossover when demand figures change.
- Writing only advantages in a discuss or evaluate answer.
- Forgetting the effect on employees and brand when a method changes.
- Giving no final judgement, or a judgement with no conditions.

## Frequently asked questions

**What do these operations methods questions cover?**
They cover section 5.2 of the IB DP Business Management guide (first assessment 2024): job, batch, mass/flow production and mass customization, with the factors that decide which method suits a business.

**How do you find where two methods cost the same?**
Write total cost for each as fixed costs plus variable cost per unit times output, set the two equal and solve for output.
