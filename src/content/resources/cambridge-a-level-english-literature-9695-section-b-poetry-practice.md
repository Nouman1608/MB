## Section A — Knowledge and terms

**1.** Explain the difference between the (a) and (b) questions on a Paper 1 poetry set text. **[2]**

**2.** Define **persona** and explain why it matters when analysing a poem. **[2]**

**3.** Explain the difference between **enjambment** and **caesura**. **[2]**

**4.** What is a **volta**, and in which traditional form is it most expected? **[2]**

**5.** Explain why noticing a **disruption** to a regular metre is often more useful than identifying the

## Section B — Analysing original poems

**6.** Read this **original** poem.

> **Night Shift**
>
> My mother leaves at ten. The door clicks twice.
> I hear her keys, her shoes, the gate, the street,
> and then the house is mine: the humming ice,
> the clock, the radiator's ticking heat.
> I learn the names of every sound she leaves.
> By six, I know them all. By six, she's back,
> and folds her coat, and rolls her tired sleeves,
> and asks me how I slept. I answer, *Fine.*

**(a)** Identify the rhyme scheme of the first four lines and explain one effect of its regularity.
**(b)** Explain the effect of the list in line 2. **[5]**

**7.** In "Night Shift", the final line does not rhyme as the pattern leads the reader to expect. Explain
the effect of this choice on the poem's ending. **[4]**

**8.** Explain how the repetition of "By six" presents the speaker's experience of time. **[3]**

**9.** Read this **original** poem.

> **Inheritance**
>
> You gave me your hands —
> square nails, the knuckle
> that clicks in the cold —
> and nothing else
> I asked for.
>
> I use them anyway.

Explain how the poet uses **line breaks and stanza structure** to present the speaker's feelings about
inheritance. **[6]**

**10.** A student writes: "In 'Inheritance' the poet is angry with her father because he did not leave
her anything in his will." Identify **two** weaknesses in this comment and suggest a better approach.
**[4]**

**11.** A Section B (a) question asks: "Discuss ways in which the poet presents memory in **two** poems
from your selection." Explain **three** features of a strong plan for this question. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** The (a) question is an **essay on a focus across poems from the selection**, with no poem printed
[1]. The (b) question **prints one poem** from the selection for close analysis, related to the wider
selection [1].

**2.** A persona is the **constructed speaking voice** of a poem [1]. It matters because the speaker may
not be the poet, so attitudes can be **ironic or dramatised**, and treating the poem as autobiography
narrows interpretation [1].

**3.** **Enjambment** is when a sentence or phrase **runs over a line ending** without punctuation [1].
**Caesura** is a **pause within a line**, usually marked by punctuation [1].

**4.** A volta is a **turn in argument, mood or perspective** [1]. It is most expected in the **sonnet**
[1].

**5.** A regular metre sets up an expectation, so a **disruption draws attention** to a particular word or
moment [1]; explaining why the pattern breaks there links form directly to **meaning or emotion**, which
AO2 rewards, whereas naming the metre alone earns little [1].

**6. (a)** The rhyme scheme is **ABAB** ("twice"/"ice", "street"/"heat") [1]. Its regularity mirrors the
**predictable, repeated routine** of the night, so the form enacts the habit the speaker describes [1].
**(b)** The list of "keys", "shoes", "the gate, the street" traces the mother's **departure sound by
sound** [1], moving progressively further away [1], so the reader experiences the distance growing
through the **listening child's** perspective [1].

**7.** The pattern leads the reader to expect a rhyme with "back", but "*Fine*" **breaks it** [1]. The
broken rhyme makes the word **stand out as false or incomplete** [1], suggesting the speaker's reassurance
hides the loneliness the poem has shown [1]. The italics mark it as **spoken**, a performance for the
mother that contrasts with the honest inner voice of the rest of the poem [1].

**8.** The repetition creates a sense of **waiting and counting** towards the mother's return [1]. The
first "By six" marks the speaker's **mastery of the sounds**, the second the mother's return [1], so the
same hour holds both the speaker's solitary competence and relief, suggesting the night is **endured
rather than slept** [1].

**9.** The short lines **isolate each inherited detail** ("square nails", "the knuckle / that clicks"), so
they are presented with close, almost affectionate attention [1] [1]. The break after "nothing else"
creates a **delay**, so "I asked for" lands as a quiet grievance [1]. The dashes set the physical details
apart, as if remembered separately [1]. The **isolated final line** forms its own stanza, so the
speaker's acceptance stands apart from the complaint [1], and its plain statement suggests a **resigned,
practical love** that outlasts resentment [1].

**10.** It **assumes the speaker is the poet** and supplies a biographical situation [1]. It also
**invents detail** (a father, a will) that the poem does not give [1]. A better approach analyses the
**speaker** as presented [1] and works from **evidence in the text**, such as the line breaks and the
final stanza, to interpret the speaker's mixed feelings [1].

**11.** Choose **two poems that present memory differently**, so the essay has a comparative argument
[1] [1]. Organise **by point**, moving between the poems rather than writing on one then the other [1]
[1]. Plan **short quotations and a feature of form** for each poem, so every paragraph keeps line-level
analysis [1] [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Paraphrasing what the poem says instead of analysing how form and language create meaning.
- Naming a rhyme scheme or metre without linking it to an effect.
- Inventing biographical or situational detail the poem does not give.
- In (a) answers, writing about more poems than asked, or about two poems without comparison.
