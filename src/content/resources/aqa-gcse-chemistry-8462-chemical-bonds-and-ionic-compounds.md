This guide teaches two subsections of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) specification, for teaching from
September 2016 onwards and exams in 2018 onwards (version 1.1): **4.2.1.1 Chemical bonds** and
**4.2.1.3 Ionic compounds**. Both sit in topic 2, Bonding, structure, and the properties of matter, which is
assessed on Paper 1 (1 hour 45 minutes, Foundation and Higher Tier). Nothing in these two subsections is
marked (HT only), so all of it can be tested on either tier. There is no required practical for this content.

## 4.2.1.1 The three strong chemical bonds

There are three types of strong chemical bond: **ionic**, **covalent** and **metallic**. Each is described by
the particles involved.

| Bond | Particles | Found in |
|---|---|---|
| Ionic | Oppositely charged **ions** | Compounds formed from metals combined with non-metals |
| Covalent | **Atoms** that share **pairs** of electrons | Most non-metallic elements, and compounds of non-metals |
| Metallic | **Atoms** that share **delocalised** electrons | Metallic elements and alloys |

Read the "found in" column carefully.

- Ionic bonding needs a **compound**. A single element cannot be ionic.
- Covalent bonding covers both elements (such as chlorine, Cl₂) and compounds (such as water, H₂O). The
  word "most" matters: the Group 0 noble gases do not easily form molecules, because their atoms already have
  stable arrangements of electrons.
- Metallic bonding covers pure metals **and alloys**. Brass is a mixture of metals, but its bonding is still
  metallic.

### Explaining bonding with electrostatic forces

The specification asks you to explain chemical bonding in terms of **electrostatic forces** and the
**transfer or sharing of electrons**. Every strong bond is an attraction between opposite charges. What
changes is where the charges come from.

- **Ionic:** electrons are **transferred** from metal atoms to non-metal atoms. The metal atoms become positive
  ions and the non-metal atoms become negative ions. The bond is the strong electrostatic attraction between
  the oppositely charged ions.
- **Covalent:** two atoms **share a pair of electrons**. The shared pair is negative. It is attracted to the
  positive nuclei of both atoms, which holds the two atoms together.
- **Metallic:** the outer-shell electrons of metal atoms are **delocalised**, free to move through the whole
  structure. The sharing of these delocalised electrons gives strong metallic bonds: the negative delocalised
  electrons attract the positively charged metal centres they move among.

### Worked example 1: classify and explain

*For each substance, name the type of bonding and explain how it holds the particles together: potassium
bromide, hydrogen chloride, copper.*

```
Potassium bromide
  K is a metal, Br is a non-metal       -> compound of metal + non-metal -> IONIC
  Electrons transfer from K to Br       -> K+ ions and Br- ions
  Strong electrostatic attraction between the oppositely charged ions

Hydrogen chloride
  H and Cl are both non-metals          -> compound of non-metals -> COVALENT
  Atoms share a pair of electrons
  The shared pair is attracted to the positive nuclei of both atoms

Copper
  A metallic element                    -> METALLIC
  Outer electrons are delocalised and shared through the structure
  Electrostatic attraction between metal centres and delocalised electrons
```

Each answer has three parts: the elements, the bond type, then how electrons are transferred or shared **and**
what attracts what. "They share electrons" alone does not explain the bond.

### Worked example 2: is it ever "none of these"?

*Classify: argon, bronze (an alloy of copper and tin), sulfur dioxide, magnesium fluoride.*

| Substance | Elements | Bonding |
|---|---|---|
| Argon | One noble gas | No strong bonds; atoms stay separate |
| Bronze | Two metals (alloy) | Metallic |
| Sulfur dioxide | Two non-metals | Covalent |
| Magnesium fluoride | Metal + non-metal | Ionic |

Bronze contains two different elements, but that does not make it ionic. Ionic bonding needs a **metal and a
non-metal**.

## 4.2.1.3 Ionic compounds: the giant lattice

An ionic compound is a **giant structure of ions**. It is held together by strong electrostatic forces of
attraction between oppositely charged ions. These forces **act in all directions in the lattice**, and this
is called ionic bonding.

Two ideas here trip people up.

1. **There are no molecules.** Sodium chloride is not made of separate "NaCl" pairs. Each ion is attracted to
   every oppositely charged ion around it, not to one partner.
2. **"Giant" means the pattern repeats in three dimensions** through the whole crystal. The formula NaCl tells
   you the **ratio** of ions, 1 : 1, not the size of a unit.

### The structure of sodium chloride

In solid sodium chloride:

- sodium ions (Na⁺) and chloride ions (Cl⁻) alternate in a regular cubic pattern
- each Na⁺ ion is surrounded by six Cl⁻ ions, and each Cl⁻ ion by six Na⁺ ions
- the chloride ions are larger than the sodium ions
- the pattern continues in every direction, so there are equal numbers of each ion.

### Worked example 3: deducing that a structure is ionic

*Four diagrams are described. Which one shows an ionic compound?*

- **A** Separate pairs of identical atoms, each pair joined by a single line.
- **B** One kind of positive particle in rows, with "e⁻" symbols scattered between them.
- **C** A regular grid of two kinds of particle, one labelled + and the other −, alternating, with the grid
  cut off at the edges of the drawing.
- **D** One kind of atom, every atom joined to four others by lines.

**Answer: C.** It shows **two kinds of charged particle** (positive and negative ions) in a **regular
repeating lattice** that continues past the edge of the drawing (a giant structure). A shows small molecules
(covalent). B shows positive centres among delocalised electrons (metallic): there are no negative ions.
D shows one element joined by covalent bonds in a giant structure.

Useful clues for "ionic" in any diagram:

- charges (+ and −, or 2+ and 2−) on the particles
- square brackets with a charge around each ion in a dot and cross diagram
- a key naming a metal ion and a non-metal ion
- a regular, alternating pattern with no separate groups of particles.

### Limitations of the diagrams

You must be able to describe the limitations of using **dot and cross**, **ball and stick**,
**two-dimensional** and **three-dimensional** diagrams to represent a giant ionic structure. Each one shows
some features well and hides others.

| Diagram | What it shows well | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| Dot and cross | Which electrons transfer, and the charge on each ion | Shows one pair of ions, not the giant lattice; gives no idea of the 3D arrangement or the relative sizes of the ions; does not show the forces acting in all directions |
| Ball and stick | The 3D arrangement and the alternating pattern | Sticks suggest physical bonds between particular pairs, when the forces act in all directions; ions are drawn far apart, when really they are packed closely; ion sizes may not be to scale; only a tiny part of the lattice |
| 2D diagram | The alternating pattern and the ratio of ions | Shows only one layer, so you cannot see the ions above and below; does not show the 3D shape or the forces in all directions |
| 3D space-filling | The relative sizes of the ions and how closely they pack | You cannot see the ions inside, so the arrangement is hard to follow; the forces are not shown; only a tiny part of the lattice |

One limitation is shared by **every** model: a real crystal contains an enormous number of ions, so any
drawing shows only a small fragment of the lattice.

### Worked example 4: empirical formula from a 2D diagram

*The diagram shows part of one layer of lithium oxide. L = lithium ion, O = oxide ion. Work out the empirical
formula.*

```
L  O  L  L  O  L
L  O  L  L  O  L
L  O  L  L  O  L
```

```
Count L:   4 per row x 3 rows = 12
Count O:   2 per row x 3 rows = 6
Ratio L : O = 12 : 6 = 2 : 1           (divide both by 6)
Empirical formula: Li2O  (written Li₂O)
Check charges: 2 x (+1) + 1 x (-2) = 0  -> neutral, so the ratio makes sense
```

The **empirical formula** is the simplest whole-number ratio of the ions. Write the metal first and use
subscripts only for numbers greater than 1. The charge check is a useful habit, but it only works when you
know the charges. The specification limits charges from group number to Groups 1, 2, 6 and 7 (lithium is in
Group 1 and oxygen in Group 6).

### Worked example 5: watch the edges of a 3D model

*A model of sodium chloride is a 3 × 3 × 3 cube of 27 ions, with Na⁺ ions at the eight corners. A student
counts 14 Na⁺ and 13 Cl⁻ and writes Na₁₄Cl₁₃. What has gone wrong?*

```
Ions are alternating, so corners, edges and faces do not split evenly
in a small block: 14 of the corner type, 13 of the other.
In the real lattice the pattern carries on in every direction,
so the numbers of Na+ and Cl- are equal.
Check: Na+ is 1+, Cl- is 1-, so 1 : 1 is needed for a neutral compound.
Empirical formula: NaCl
```

A small model has edges that matter; a real crystal is so large that they do not. When a count gives an odd
ratio, look at the **repeating pattern** and check the charges.

## Common errors

- Calling an alloy ionic because it contains two elements. Alloys are metallic.
- Saying ionic compounds are made of "molecules of NaCl". They are giant lattices.
- Writing "the electrons are shared" for a covalent bond without saying the shared pair is attracted to both
  nuclei.
- Saying the forces in an ionic lattice act "between each pair of ions". They act **in all directions**.
- Giving a limitation that is just "it's not realistic". Name the specific feature: sticks, gaps, size, layer
  or fragment.
- Copying raw counts as a formula (Li₁₂O₆) instead of the simplest ratio (Li₂O).
- Treating a diagram with positive particles and electrons as ionic. Ionic diagrams have **negative ions** too.
