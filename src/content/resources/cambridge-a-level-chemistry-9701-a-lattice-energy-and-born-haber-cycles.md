This guide covers subtopic **23.1, Lattice energy and Born–Haber cycles**, from Topic 23, Chemical
energetics, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is A Level
content. The [Chemical Energetics: Lattice Energy, Entropy and Gibbs Free Energy guide](/resources/a-chemical-energetics-lattice-energy-entropy-and-gibbs-free-energy/)
works the sodium chloride cycle; this page builds cycles with 2+ cations and 2− anions, rearranges a cycle
to find an unknown electron affinity, and sets out how to explain electron affinity trends and lattice
energy comparisons for full marks.

## What the syllabus asks for

1. define and use the terms **enthalpy change of atomisation**, ΔH<sub>at</sub>, and **lattice energy**,
   ΔH<sub>latt</sub> (the change from gas-phase ions to solid lattice)
2. (a) define and use **first electron affinity**, EA; (b) explain the **factors affecting** electron
   affinities; (c) describe and explain the **trends** in electron affinities of the Group 16 and Group 17
   elements
3. construct and use **Born–Haber cycles** for ionic solids (limited to +1 and +2 cations, −1 and −2
   anions)
4. carry out **calculations** involving Born–Haber cycles
5. explain, qualitatively, the effect of **ionic charge** and **ionic radius** on the numerical magnitude
   of a lattice energy

## Definitions to write word for word

| Term | Definition | Example equation | Sign |
|---|---|---|---|
| Enthalpy change of atomisation, ΔH<sub>at</sub> | enthalpy change when 1 mol of gaseous atoms is formed from the element in its standard state | ½Cl₂(g) → Cl(g) | always + |
| First ionisation energy, IE₁ | energy needed to remove one electron from each atom in 1 mol of gaseous atoms | Mg(g) → Mg⁺(g) + e⁻ | always + |
| First electron affinity, EA₁ | enthalpy change when 1 mol of electrons is added to 1 mol of gaseous atoms to form 1 mol of gaseous 1− ions | O(g) + e⁻ → O⁻(g) | usually − |
| Second electron affinity, EA₂ | enthalpy change when 1 mol of electrons is added to 1 mol of gaseous 1− ions to form 1 mol of gaseous 2− ions | O⁻(g) + e⁻ → O²⁻(g) | + |
| Lattice energy, ΔH<sub>latt</sub> | enthalpy change when 1 mol of an ionic compound is formed from its gaseous ions | Mg²⁺(g) + 2Cl⁻(g) → MgCl₂(s) | always − |

Two details lose marks: atomisation is **per mole of atoms formed**, so for chlorine the equation starts
from ½Cl₂; and lattice energy goes **from gaseous ions to solid**, which is why it is negative.

## Electron affinity: factors and trends

The first electron affinity is exothermic because the incoming electron is attracted by the nucleus. Its
size depends on:

- **nuclear charge**: a greater effective nuclear charge attracts the electron more strongly
- **distance** of the outer shell from the nucleus (atomic radius): the closer the shell, the stronger the
  attraction
- **shielding** by inner electrons: more inner shells reduce the attraction

**Down Group 17 (Cl to I) and Group 16 (S to Te)**, the first electron affinity becomes **less
exothermic**. The added electron enters a shell further from the nucleus, with more shielding, so it is
attracted less strongly, even though the nuclear charge increases.

**The first member of each group is anomalous.** Fluorine's first electron affinity is less exothermic
than chlorine's, and oxygen's is less exothermic than sulfur's. F and O atoms are very small, so the
incoming electron enters a crowded outer shell and experiences strong repulsion from the electrons already
there. This repulsion outweighs the stronger nuclear attraction.

**Group 16 compared with Group 17.** Group 16 first electron affinities are less exothermic than those of
the Group 17 element in the same period, because the Group 16 atom has one proton fewer.

**Second electron affinities are endothermic.** The electron is being added to a negative ion, so it is
repelled; energy must be supplied to overcome that repulsion.

## Building a Born–Haber cycle

Draw energy levels with **upward arrows for endothermic steps** and **downward arrows for exothermic
steps**. Start from the elements in their standard states, at zero.

1. Atomise the metal: ΔH<sub>at</sub>(metal).
2. Ionise the metal: IE₁, and IE₂ as well for a 2+ ion.
3. Atomise the non-metal, **once for every atom in the formula**.
4. Add electrons to the non-metal atoms: EA₁ for each atom, and EA₂ as well for a 2− ion.
5. The gaseous ions come together to form the solid: ΔH<sub>latt</sub> (large and downward).
6. The direct route from elements to solid is ΔH<sub>f</sub>.

By Hess's law: **ΔH<sub>f</sub> = sum of steps 1–4 + ΔH<sub>latt</sub>**.

## Worked example 1: magnesium chloride (a 2+ cation)

**Question.** Calculate ΔH<sub>latt</sub>(MgCl₂) from the data (kJ mol⁻¹): ΔH<sub>f</sub>(MgCl₂) = −641;
ΔH<sub>at</sub>(Mg) = +148; IE₁(Mg) = +736; IE₂(Mg) = +1450; ΔH<sub>at</sub>(Cl) = +121;
EA₁(Cl) = −349.

**Answer.** Two chlorine atoms are needed, so both the atomisation and the electron affinity of chlorine
are doubled.

| Step | Value / kJ mol⁻¹ |
|---|---|
| Mg(s) → Mg(g) | +148 |
| Mg(g) → Mg⁺(g) + e⁻ | +736 |
| Mg⁺(g) → Mg²⁺(g) + e⁻ | +1450 |
| Cl₂(g) → 2Cl(g) = 2 × (+121) | +242 |
| 2Cl(g) + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻(g) = 2 × (−349) | −698 |
| **Total** | **+1878** |

ΔH<sub>latt</sub> = ΔH<sub>f</sub> − (sum of the other steps) = −641 − (+1878) = **−2519 kJ mol⁻¹**

## Worked example 2: sodium oxide (a 2− anion)

**Question.** Calculate ΔH<sub>latt</sub>(Na₂O) from (kJ mol⁻¹): ΔH<sub>f</sub>(Na₂O) = −414;
ΔH<sub>at</sub>(Na) = +107; IE₁(Na) = +494; ΔH<sub>at</sub>(O) = +248; EA₁(O) = −141; EA₂(O) = +798.

**Answer.** Here the metal steps are doubled (two Na), and oxygen needs both electron affinities.

Sum of steps = 2(+107) + 2(+494) + (+248) + (−141) + (+798) = 214 + 988 + 248 − 141 + 798 = +2107

ΔH<sub>latt</sub> = −414 − (+2107) = **−2521 kJ mol⁻¹**

## Worked example 3: finding an electron affinity

**Question.** Use the data (kJ mol⁻¹) to calculate EA₁(Br): ΔH<sub>f</sub>(KBr) = −394;
ΔH<sub>at</sub>(K) = +89; IE₁(K) = +418; ΔH<sub>at</sub>(Br) = +112; ΔH<sub>latt</sub>(KBr) = −688.

**Answer.**

ΔH<sub>f</sub> = ΔH<sub>at</sub>(K) + IE₁(K) + ΔH<sub>at</sub>(Br) + EA₁(Br) + ΔH<sub>latt</sub>

−394 = 89 + 418 + 112 + EA₁(Br) − 688

−394 = −69 + EA₁(Br)

EA₁(Br) = **−325 kJ mol⁻¹**

The answer is exothermic and less exothermic than chlorine's −349 kJ mol⁻¹, as the Group 17 trend
predicts: a useful check.

## Charge and radius: explaining lattice energy

Lattice energy comes from the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions. Its magnitude is
larger (more exothermic) when:

- **the ionic charges are higher**: the attraction depends on the product of the charges
- **the ions are smaller**: the ions can approach more closely, so the attraction is stronger

| Comparison | Which is more exothermic | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| NaF vs NaCl | NaF | F⁻ is smaller than Cl⁻; same charges |
| NaCl vs MgCl₂ | MgCl₂ | Mg²⁺ has a higher charge and is smaller than Na⁺ |
| MgO vs NaF | MgO | charges 2+ and 2− compared with 1+ and 1− |
| MgO vs CaO | MgO | Mg²⁺ is smaller than Ca²⁺; same charges |

A full answer names the factor, applies it to both ions, and links it to the strength of attraction.

## Common mistakes

- **Forgetting to double** atomisation and electron affinity for MgCl₂, or atomisation and ionisation for
  Na₂O.
- **Leaving out EA₂** for oxide or sulfide ions, or giving it a negative sign.
- **Using the Cl–Cl bond energy (+242) as ΔH<sub>at</sub>(Cl).** Atomisation forms one mole of atoms, so it
  is half the bond energy per mole of Cl atoms.
- **Defining lattice energy as breaking the lattice.** In 9701 it is formation from gaseous ions.
- **Sign slips** when rearranging: write the full Hess's law equation first, then substitute.
- **Explaining the F/Cl anomaly with "fluorine is more electronegative".** The answer is repulsion in a
  small, crowded outer shell.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Define:** give the full definition with "1 mol", "gaseous" and the direction of change.
- **Construct** a cycle: label every level with species and state symbols; arrows up for endothermic.
- **Calculate:** write the Hess's law sum before substituting; give units and sign.
- **Explain** a trend: name nuclear charge, distance and shielding, and say which dominates.
