**HL only.** This is the sixth study in the Regional study for the Americas, examined on Paper 3 at Higher Level. Paper 3 asks you to evaluate an argument and reach a judgement. The Mexican Revolution is one of the central events of 20th-century Latin America, and it raises a basic question for essays: was it a genuine social revolution, or a long civil war that changed politics more than society? You need to know the key leaders, the main stages, and what changed and what did not by 1940.

## The six lines of inquiry

The guide names six inquiry topics: reasons for the outbreak; the impact of revolutionary leaders; the impact of the 1917 Constitution; the achievements and challenges of the post-revolutionary state (1920 to 1940); foreign contributions; and the impact of the revolution. They fit well with **cause and consequence**, **significance** and **continuity and change**. The [four concepts guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-four-concepts/) shows how to use them.

## Key chronology

- 1876 to 1880 and 1884 to 1911: Porfirio Díaz in power (the Porfiriato).
- 1906 to 1907: strikes at Cananea and Río Blanco.
- November 1910: Madero's Plan of San Luis Potosí calls for revolt. May 1911: Díaz resigns.
- November 1911: Madero president; Zapata's Plan of Ayala.
- February 1913: Huerta's coup and the murder of Madero. March 1913: Carranza's Plan of Guadalupe.
- 1914: US occupation of Veracruz; Huerta resigns; Convention of Aguascalientes.
- 1915: Obregón defeats Villa at Celaya. 1916 to 1917: US Punitive Expedition.
- February 1917: new Constitution.
- 1919: Zapata killed. 1920: Carranza killed; Obregón president. 1923: Villa killed.
- 1924 to 1928: Calles president. 1926 to 1929: Cristero War. 1928: Obregón assassinated.
- 1929: National Revolutionary Party founded. 1934 to 1940: Lázaro Cárdenas. 1938: oil expropriation.

## Reasons for the outbreak of the revolution

**The rule of Porfirio Díaz.** Díaz, a general, gained power in 1876 and ruled until 1911, with a short break. His slogan was "order and progress". He controlled opponents through the army, the *rurales* (rural police), patronage and the press. Elections were managed. A group of technocratic advisers known as the *científicos* guided economic policy.

**Economic factors.** Railways grew from a few hundred kilometres to around 20,000 kilometres. Foreign investors, especially from the United States and Britain, took stakes in mining, oil, railways and plantations. Exports grew, but the benefits were uneven. Haciendas expanded at the expense of village lands, and by 1910 most rural families had no land of their own. Real wages for many workers stagnated, and prices of staple foods rose.

**Social factors.** Society was sharply divided. A small elite and foreign managers held wealth. Peasants, Indigenous communities and urban workers had few rights. Strikes such as those at the Cananea copper mine (1906) and the Río Blanco textile mills (1907) were crushed. Opposition thinkers, notably the Flores Magón brothers and their Liberal Party, called for reform.

**Political factors.** The ageing dictator had no clear successor and stirred new resentment among regional elites and the middle class who were shut out. In 1908 Díaz told a journalist, James Creelman, that Mexico was ready for democracy, which encouraged opposition. Francisco Madero published *The Presidential Succession in 1910* and challenged Díaz. Díaz had him jailed before the election.

**Weighing the causes.** Historians tend to treat the revolution as caused by the combination of long-term pressures (land loss, inequality, foreign influence) and short-term political crisis (the succession and the election of 1910). A good answer ranks them and explains how they interacted, such as how land grievances in Morelos gave Zapata a base that Madero's political programme could not satisfy.

## Impact of revolutionary leaders

**Francisco Madero.** A wealthy landowner from the north, Madero called for revolt in the Plan of San Luis Potosí. With help from Villa and Orozco, he forced Díaz out in 1911 and won the presidency. But he did not carry out land reform, kept the old army, and lost the support of both peasants and conservatives. Zapata turned against him.

**Victoriano Huerta.** A general who, with the US ambassador's knowledge, overthrew Madero in 1913 in the "Ten Tragic Days" and had him murdered. His military dictatorship united opponents. Carranza, Villa, Zapata and Obregón rose against him, and he fled in 1914.

**Pancho Villa and Emiliano Zapata.** Villa led the Division of the North, a large cavalry army from Chihuahua, and held land to supply it. Zapata led peasant forces in Morelos with a clear programme: the Plan of Ayala (1911) demanded return of village lands. Both were popular heroes, but neither built a national organisation, and they were defeated by better-supplied rivals. Both were killed.

**Venustiano Carranza.** A former Díaz-era senator and governor, he led the Constitutionalists under the Plan of Guadalupe. He prevailed through the military skill of Obregón and by controlling revenue from ports and oil. He accepted social reform reluctantly, but under pressure agreed to the 1917 Constitution. He was overthrown and killed in 1920.

## Impact of the 1917 Constitution

The Constitution of 1917 was among the most radical of its time.

- **Land and property.** Article 27 declared that land and subsoil resources belonged originally to the nation, which could limit private property in the public interest. It allowed redistribution and the revival of communal landholding (the *ejido*). It laid the basis for later oil nationalisation.
- **Labour.** Article 123 set an eight-hour day, a minimum wage, limits on child labour, and rights to organise and strike.
- **The Church.** Anticlerical provisions banned church ownership of property, closed monastic orders, restricted religious schooling and limited the role of priests. These caused conflict in the 1920s.
- **Education.** Article 3 required free, secular primary education.

The Constitution set a framework, but many clauses were not enforced for years. Land reform under Carranza was limited. Enforcement depended on later presidents.

## Achievements and challenges of the post-revolutionary state (1920 to 1940)

**Álvaro Obregón (1920 to 1924).** Obregón ended the fighting, restored order and built alliances with labour. His education minister José Vasconcelos launched rural schools and supported muralists such as Diego Rivera. Land redistribution was modest.

**Plutarco Elías Calles and the Maximato.** Calles (1924 to 1928) enforced anticlerical laws, built roads and irrigation, and created the Bank of Mexico. After Obregón's assassination in 1928, Calles remained the "supreme chief" behind a series of weak presidents. This period is the Maximato. In 1929 he founded the National Revolutionary Party, which brought leaders together and stabilised the succession.

**Opposition and the Cristero War.** Enforcement of anticlerical laws led Catholics to rebel in western states (1926 to 1929), with the cry "Long live Christ the King". The war cost tens of thousands of lives and ended in a negotiated settlement in 1929, while tensions continued.

**Lázaro Cárdenas and the renewal of the revolution.** Cárdenas (1934 to 1940) broke from Calles's control and sent him into exile in 1936. He distributed about 18 million hectares of land, much of it to *ejidos*, organised peasants and workers into national confederations, expanded rural schools and nationalised the railways (1937) and foreign oil companies (March 1938). Oil nationalisation was popular and a major assertion of sovereignty, despite foreign protests. His party was renamed the Party of the Mexican Revolution (1938). Critics say his corporatist structure tied popular groups to the state.

## Foreign contributions to the revolution

**Motivations.** Foreign powers wanted stability for investments, access to oil and protection of their citizens. The US also feared radical land reform.

**Military intervention.** US forces occupied Veracruz in 1914 after an incident at Tampico, aimed at Huerta. After Villa's attack on Columbus, New Mexico in 1916, General Pershing led a Punitive Expedition into Mexico, which failed to capture him and heightened tension.

**Economic intervention.** Arms came from the United States, and its policy on arms exports shifted according to which faction it favoured. Foreign companies funded and lobbied factions.

**Diplomatic intervention.** The US Ambassador, Henry Lane Wilson, helped Huerta's coup. President Woodrow Wilson refused to recognise Huerta. Germany's Zimmermann Telegram (1917) proposed an alliance with Mexico against the United States, which Mexico rejected.

## Impact of the revolution

**Economic.** Fighting disrupted mining, farming and railways. Land reform changed rural life, especially under Cárdenas, although many *ejidos* lacked credit and technical support. Control of resources moved partly into state hands.

**Culture, education and the arts.** The revolution encouraged a cultural nationalism that celebrated Indigenous and popular roots (*indigenismo* and *mexicanidad*). The muralists Rivera, Orozco and Siqueiros painted public buildings. Corridos recorded the revolution in song. Rural education expanded.

**Women.** Women served as soldiers and camp followers (*soldaderas*), nurses and organisers. Activists such as Hermila Galindo argued for women's rights. The 1917 Constitution did not give women the vote. The Law of Family Relations (1917) allowed divorce and changed marriage law. Mexican women gained the national vote only in 1953.

**Marginalised groups.** Indigenous communities gained from *indigenismo* and land reform in some areas but were often treated as people to be integrated into mestizo national culture. Landless peasants and women gained less than they hoped.

## Arguments for Paper 3

- **A revolution or a rebellion?** Some historians, such as John Womack (on Zapata) and Alan Knight, stress the scale of popular participation and see a genuine social revolution. Others, called revisionists, argue that the main result was a new political elite in power and that social change was limited. The official story of the "Revolutionary Family" is itself a source to evaluate.
- **Who made it?** Peasant movements or middle-class and northern elites?
- **Was Cárdenas the climax or an exception?**
- **Foreign influence**: decisive, or secondary to Mexican causes?

## Common mistakes

- Mixing up the various leaders and their plans.
- Describing the fighting without explaining its causes.
- Treating the revolution as ending in 1917 or 1920.
- Forgetting women and marginalised groups.

## Frequently asked questions

**Where do I start the narrative?**
With the Porfiriato, then the 1910 crisis. Do not spend long on the early life of any leader.

**Does the study include the Cristero War?**
Yes, as opposition to the post-revolutionary state.
