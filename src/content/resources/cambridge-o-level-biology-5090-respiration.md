This guide covers subtopic **10.1 Respiration** of Cambridge O Level Biology 5090, 2026-2028 series. The syllabus asks you to describe respiration as the chemical reactions in all living cells that release energy from glucose, to state the uses of energy in living organisms (muscle contraction, protein synthesis, cell division, active transport, growth, the passage of electrical impulses along neurones and the maintenance of a constant body temperature), and to investigate and describe the effect of temperature on respiration in yeast.

The [Respiration guide](/resources/o-level-biology-5090-respiration/) covers the whole topic, and the [10.1 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-biology-5090-respiration-revision-notes/) give a condensed version. This page puts the definition under the microscope, sets out each use of energy with an example, and works through the yeast investigation with data. Test yourself with the [10.1 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-biology-5090-respiration-practice/). The two kinds of respiration are covered in subtopics 10.2 and 10.3.

## What respiration is

**Respiration is the chemical reactions in all living cells that release energy from glucose.**

Take the definition apart, as examiners mark each part.

1. **Chemical reactions**: respiration is a series of chemical reactions controlled by enzymes, not a single event.
2. **In all living cells**: every living cell respires, all the time. This includes plant cells, root cells and bacteria, not only animals. A plant respires in the day and at night; in the day, photosynthesis often hides it.
3. **Release energy**: respiration does not make or create energy. It **releases** the chemical energy stored in glucose, and the energy is transferred to other forms for the cell to use, with some lost as heat.
4. **From glucose**: the glucose comes from the digestion of carbohydrates (animals) or from photosynthesis (plants).

### Respiration is not breathing

This is one of the most common errors in the paper.

| | Respiration | Breathing |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Chemical reactions that release energy | Movement of air into and out of the lungs |
| Where | In the cells (mainly mitochondria in aerobic respiration) | In the lungs and chest |
| Purpose | To release energy from glucose | To bring in oxygen and remove carbon dioxide |

Breathing supplies the oxygen that cells use for aerobic respiration, so the two are linked, but they are not the same. Use the word "ventilation" or "breathing" for lung movements and "respiration" for what happens in the cell.

## The uses of energy

The energy released by respiration is used in many ways. The syllabus lists seven, which you should be able to state with an example.

| Use of energy | What it means, with an example |
|---|---|
| Muscle contraction | Muscle fibres shorten to move the body, such as leg muscles during running; the heart muscle contracts all day |
| Protein synthesis | Joining amino acids to make proteins, such as enzymes or haemoglobin |
| Cell division | Copying DNA and dividing the cell, for growth and repair, such as cells in the skin or in a root tip |
| Active transport | Moving molecules or ions against a concentration gradient, such as ions entering root hair cells |
| Growth | Building new cells and larger structures, such as a growing seedling |
| Passage of electrical impulses along neurones | Nerve cells send signals, such as when a reflex action takes place |
| Maintenance of a constant body temperature | In mammals and birds, heat released from respiration warms the body; muscle contraction (shivering) releases extra heat |

When you are asked for "two uses", write the use and, where possible, a short example, but never write "making energy" as the use. Energy is the thing that is **used**, not the thing that is made.

### Why a constant body temperature needs energy

Mammals and birds keep their body temperature roughly constant, even when the air is cold. Heat is released by respiration in all cells, and especially in the muscles and liver. In the cold, this heat helps to replace heat lost to the surroundings. That is why animals that keep a constant temperature generally need more food than those that do not.

## Where the energy goes

Respiration releases the chemical energy stored in glucose, but the cell cannot use all of it. Some energy is transferred to the reactions that need it, such as muscle contraction or active transport. A large share is released as **heat**. In mammals and birds this heat is useful, because it helps to keep the body warm. In a compost heap full of respiring microorganisms the temperature rises for the same reason, and a pile of germinating seeds in a closed container becomes warm.

## Respiration in plants and in microorganisms

All organisms respire, so the definition applies to plants, fungi and bacteria as well as animals. Three examples show this.

- **Plants**: a leaf respires all the time. In bright light, photosynthesis makes more glucose and oxygen than respiration uses, so the net effect is to take in carbon dioxide. In the dark there is no photosynthesis, so only respiration remains and carbon dioxide is released.
- **Yeast**: a single-celled fungus that respires glucose and releases carbon dioxide, which is why it makes bread rise.
- **Germinating seeds**: they respire quickly, using stored food to release energy for growth before their first leaves can photosynthesise.

## Investigating the effect of temperature on respiration in yeast

Yeast is a single-celled fungus. In a solution of glucose, yeast respires and releases carbon dioxide. The rate of carbon dioxide production shows the rate of respiration.

**Method**

1. Mix yeast with glucose solution in a boiling tube. Use the same volumes and the same mass of yeast each time.
2. Place the tube in a water bath at a set temperature, such as 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 and 60 degrees Celsius. Allow the contents to reach the temperature.
3. Measure the rate of carbon dioxide production. Methods include:
   - counting **bubbles** of gas per minute from a delivery tube into water;
   - collecting the gas in an inverted measuring cylinder or syringe;
   - using **hydrogencarbonate indicator**, which changes from red to yellow as carbon dioxide dissolves, and timing the colour change.
4. Repeat each temperature at least three times and calculate a mean.

**Variables**: the *independent variable* is the temperature. The *dependent variable* is the rate of carbon dioxide production. The *control variables* include the volume and concentration of glucose solution, the mass of yeast, and the time of measurement.

### Worked example

*A student counted the bubbles released per minute at different temperatures. The results are in the table.*

| Temperature (degrees Celsius) | Bubbles per minute |
|---|---|
| 10 | 6 |
| 20 | 14 |
| 30 | 29 |
| 40 | 44 |
| 50 | 8 |
| 60 | 0 |

1. **Describe the trend**: from 10 to 40 degrees Celsius, the rate increases. Above 40 degrees Celsius, the rate falls steeply, and at 60 degrees Celsius there is none.
2. **Calculate a change**: from 10 to 20 degrees Celsius, the rate rose from 6 to 14. Percentage increase = (14 - 6) divided by 6 x 100 = **133 per cent**.
3. **Compare**: from 20 to 30 degrees Celsius, the rate was 29 divided by 14 = about **2.1 times** as great.
4. **Explain the increase**: respiration is controlled by enzymes. At a higher temperature, the particles have more kinetic energy, so collisions between enzyme and substrate happen more often and the reaction is faster.
5. **Explain the fall**: above the optimum, about 40 degrees Celsius here, the enzymes are **denatured**; their active sites change shape, and the substrate no longer fits, so respiration slows and then stops.
6. **Optimum**: the temperature with the highest rate, which here is 40 degrees Celsius. Between the tested values you cannot be sure of the exact optimum.

### Improving the investigation

- Repeat and use a mean, to reduce the effect of anomalous results.
- Use smaller steps near the optimum, such as every 5 degrees Celsius, to find the optimum more precisely.
- Use a thermostatic water bath so the temperature stays constant.
- Bubbles vary in size, so collecting the gas and measuring its volume is more accurate than counting bubbles.

## Common mistakes

- Defining respiration as "breathing" or as "making energy".
- Saying that only animals respire, or that plants respire only at night.
- Forgetting "from glucose" or "in all living cells" in the definition.
- Saying that enzymes are killed at high temperature. They are not alive; they are **denatured**.
- Explaining the rise in rate only as "more energy", with no reference to enzymes or collisions.
- Describing the yeast result as a straight line, when it rises and then falls.
- Writing "temperature" as the dependent variable.

## Command words

- **Describe**: state the definition of respiration, or the shape of the graph.
- **State**: give the uses of energy briefly.
- **Investigate**: plan or evaluate the yeast experiment.
- **Explain**: give the enzyme explanation for the trend with temperature.
