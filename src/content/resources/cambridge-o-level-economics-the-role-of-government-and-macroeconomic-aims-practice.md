The questions below test 4.1 and 4.2 of the Cambridge O Level Economics 2281 syllabus for examination in 2026: what governments do, the five aims and the clashes between them. All candidates take the same content. Every country and number is made up. ## Questions

**1.** State three macroeconomic aims of government. **[3]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by a criterion for a macroeconomic aim, giving an example. **[2]**

**3.** Country K has 12,350 people in work and 650 unemployed (thousands).

**(a)** Calculate the unemployment rate. **[2]**
**(b)** The criterion is an unemployment rate of 4%. Calculate how many fewer people must be unemployed, assuming the labour force is unchanged. **[2]**

**4.** Country L's nominal GDP rises from 2,000 to 2,226 (invented units) while its price index rises from 100 to 106.

**(a)** Calculate real GDP in the second year. **[2]**
**(b)** Work out the real growth rate. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why a government states its growth target in real GDP rather than nominal GDP. **[2]**

**5.** Choose a different role of government for each of the local, national and international levels, and say why that level suits it. **[6]**

**6.** Explain two reasons why a government might choose stable prices as an aim. **[4]**

**7.** The table shows Country T. Criteria: real growth at least 3%, unemployment no more than 5%, inflation between 1% and 3%, a current account balance no bigger than 3% of GDP, whether in deficit or in surplus.

| | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Real GDP growth (%) | 2.0 | 4.0 | 6.0 |
| Unemployment rate (%) | 8.0 | 6.0 | 4.0 |
| Inflation rate (%) | 2.0 | 3.5 | 6.5 |
| Current account (% of GDP) | +1.0 | -1.5 | -4.0 |

**(a)** In which year does unemployment first meet its criterion? **[1]**
**(b)** By how many percentage points does inflation rise between year 1 and year 3? **[1]**
**(c)** How many of the four criteria does Country T meet in year 2? **[1]**
**(d)** Use the figures to explain one conflict between two aims. **[4]**
**(e)** Discuss whether the government has good reason to be pleased with year 3. **[5]**

**8.** Country M's GDP rises from 1,200 to 1,260 (invented units). Imports are 30% of GDP. Exports are 340 in the first year.

**(a)** Find the percentage increase in GDP. **[1]**
**(b)** Find the value of imports at each GDP level. **[2]**
**(c)** Find exports minus imports at each GDP level if exports stay at 340. **[2]**
**(d)** Exports instead rise by 5%. Calculate the new balance and explain what it suggests about the conflict. **[3]**

**9.** Country N taxes income at 0% on the first 10,000, 10% on income from 10,000 to 30,000 and 25% above 30,000 (invented).

**(a)** Calculate the tax on an income of 45,000. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the tax on an income of 18,000. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate both average tax rates to one decimal place. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain how this system aids redistribution of income. **[2]**

**10.** Discuss whether a government can achieve full employment and stable prices together. **[8]**

**11.** Country V has unemployment of 12%, inflation of 2% and a current account deficit of 4% of GDP, and most citizens have low incomes. Discuss which macroeconomic aim its government should prioritise. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Any three from: economic growth; full employment (low unemployment); stable prices (low inflation); a stable balance of payments; redistribution of income [1] [1] [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the aim names the syllabus uses; vague phrases such as "a strong economy" are unlikely to earn credit.

**2.** A criterion is a measurable standard the government sets to judge whether an aim is met [1]; for example, inflation between 1% and 3% a year [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers gain credit by giving a number or range, not just a restated aim.

**3. (a)** Labour force = 12,350 + 650 = 13,000 [1]; rate = 650 / 13,000 x 100 = **5.0%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 4% of 13,000 = 520 [1]; fall = 650 - 520 = **130 thousand** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Showing the labour force line makes it clear which denominator you used, and a slip in the denominator is easier to see.

**4. (a)** Real GDP = 2,226 / 1.06 [1] = **2,100** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** (2,100 - 2,000) / 2,000 x 100 = **5.0%** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Nominal GDP can rise just because prices rise [1]; real GDP shows the change in goods and services produced, which is what raises living standards [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Quoting the contrast between nominal growth (11.3%) and real growth (5.0%) supports the explanation.

**5.** Local: a council grants permission for building or maintains local drains [1], because it knows local needs [1]. National: the government makes laws or taxes and spends on defence or schools [1], because only it has authority and revenue across the country [1]. International: governments agree limits on a shared problem such as fishing in joint waters [1], because one country cannot stop others acting alone [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Each level needs a role that fits that level plus a reason; a national role placed under local scores less.

**6.** First: rising prices cut what incomes can buy, which hits low earners and savers [1], so living standards fall [1]. Second: unpredictable prices make planning hard for firms [1], and if domestic prices rise faster than abroad, exports become less competitive [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Two distinct reasons, each developed, tend to be needed; restating one point in new words is unlikely to gain extra credit.

**7. (a)** **Year 3** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 6.5 - 2.0 = **4.5 percentage points** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Year 2 growth of 4.0 is met, unemployment of 6.0 is not, inflation of 3.5 is not, and the current account of -1.5 is met, so **2** criteria [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** The clash is between low unemployment and low inflation [1]. Over the three years unemployment drops from 8.0% to 4.0% as inflation climbs from 2.0% to 6.5% [1]. Rising demand for workers and for goods [1] bids up wages and prices [1]. **[4]**
**(e)** Growth of 6.0% and unemployment of 4.0% both meet their criteria [1]. Inflation of 6.5% is above the 3% limit and the 4.0% deficit is outside the 3% limit [1]. Rising prices cut purchasing power, and a big deficit has to be paid for by borrowing or selling assets [1]. Against that, the job and output gains are large and the price and trade problems could fade if demand cools [1]. Supported judgement, for example that the government should not be fully content, since half the criteria are missed and both gaps widened after year 1 [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers tend to gain more by comparing the figures with each criterion than by describing the table in general terms, and then committing to a judgement.

**8. (a)** (1,260 - 1,200) / 1,200 x 100 = **5%** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** At 1,200: 30% of 1,200 = **360** [1]. At 1,260: 30% of 1,260 = **378** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** At 1,200: 340 - 360 = **-20** [1]. At 1,260: 340 - 378 = **-38** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Exports = 340 x 1.05 = 357, so balance = 357 - 378 = **-21** [1]. The deficit is almost unchanged, compared with -38 when exports are flat [1]. The conflict between growth and the balance of payments is weaker when growth is led by exports [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* State the direction and size of the change in the balance, not just the new figure.

**9. (a)** 10% band: 20,000 x 10% = 2,000 [1]; 25% band: 15,000 x 25% = 3,750; total **5,750** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** (18,000 - 10,000) x 10% = **800** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** 5,750 / 45,000 x 100 = **12.8%** [1]; 800 / 18,000 x 100 = **4.4%** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** The higher earner pays a bigger share of income [1], and the revenue can fund services and benefits that help lower earners, narrowing the gap [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Applying a rate only to the part of income in each band, as shown, avoids the common slip of taxing all income at the top rate.

**10.** Conflict: higher demand reduces unemployment but scarce workers push up wages and costs, and strong demand lets firms raise prices [1] [1]. The clash is sharper near full capacity, when output cannot rise [1]. With spare capacity, firms can raise output without raising prices [1]. If unemployment falls because training improves skills, supply rises and the conflict is weaker [1]. Over time, persistent inflation may erode competitiveness, so any gain in jobs may not last [1]. Both aims can be met together when supply expands [1]. Judgement, for example that they can be met together mainly when growth comes from supply, but demand-led job creation near capacity usually raises prices [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Write when the conflict arises and when it does not, then commit; "yes, always" or "no, never" is rarely well supported.

**11.** For prioritising jobs: 12% unemployment wastes resources and leaves many in hardship, and low incomes mean unemployment may mean poverty [1] [1]. Inflation is low at 2%, so there is room to raise demand with little price risk [1]. For the balance of payments: a deficit of 4% of GDP must be financed by borrowing or selling assets [1], and more jobs and income may raise imports and worsen it [1]. Evaluation: unemployment is the larger problem now, and spare capacity makes the clash with prices weak [1]. Policies that create jobs in export or import-replacing industries ease the deficit conflict [1]. Judgement, for example priority to jobs, with the deficit watched and growth led by exports, because the harm from unemployment is greater and more immediate [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the figures given for Country V in the argument; a general list of aims scores less than a judgement tied to this case.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Listing an aim without a reason for choosing it.
- Using employment, not the labour force, as the base for the unemployment rate.
- Quoting nominal GDP where real growth is wanted.
- Writing a percentage change where percentage points are wanted.
- Stating that two aims clash without showing how.
- Taking full employment to mean no unemployed at all.
- Finishing a discussion with no judgement linked to the case.
