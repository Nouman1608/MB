It covers Memory, one of the four compulsory topics in Paper 1, Introductory Topics in Psychology: the multi-store model, the working memory model, explanations for forgetting, and factors affecting the accuracy of eyewitness testimony with the cognitive interview. A Acoustic
B Semantic
C Visual only
D Modality-specific

**2.** Distinguish between proactive interference and retroactive interference. **[2]**

**3.** Outline what is meant by retrieval failure due to the absence of cues. **[3]**

**4.** Outline research into the duration of short-term memory. **[3]**

**5.** Explain how leading questions can affect the accuracy of eyewitness testimony. Refer to research in your answer. **[4]**

### Section B: applied and data questions

**6.** Mo learned French at school and is now learning Spanish. When he tries to say "table" in Spanish, the French word keeps coming to mind. Months later, on holiday in France, he finds he cannot remember some French words he used to know well.

Identify the type of interference in each situation and explain your answers. **[4]**

**7.** A psychologist tested 20 students' recall of a word list. Ten learned and recalled the list in the same room (same context). Ten learned in one room and recalled in a different room (different context). Mean words recalled out of 30 were 18 in the same context and 12 in the different context.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage decrease in mean recall for the different-context condition compared with the same-context condition. **[2]**
**(b)** Use retrieval failure to explain the result. **[2]**
**(c)** Identify the experimental design and give **one** limitation of it for this study. **[2]**

**8.** Zara keeps a shopping list in mind by repeating it under her breath. Her friend says Zara will find it just as easy to keep doing this while reading street names aloud from a map as while tracing a route on the map with her finger.

Use the working memory model to explain whether her friend is right. **[4]**

**9.** After a robbery, two witnesses who saw it from different angles talk in a café before being interviewed by Harbridge Police. Both later describe the robber as wearing a red jacket, although only one of them saw it.

**(a)** Use research into post-event discussion to explain why this happened. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain **one** way the police could reduce this problem. **[1]**

**10.** Explain **two** limitations of the multi-store model of memory. **[4]**

### Section C: extended writing

**11.** Evaluate research into the effect of anxiety on the accuracy of eyewitness testimony. **[8]**

**12.** Harbridge Police are considering training officers in the cognitive interview. Discuss the use of the cognitive interview. Refer to the police force's decision in your answer. **[16]**

## Answers

**1.** B [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Acoustic coding is linked to short-term memory; modality-specific coding to the sensory register.

**2.** Proactive interference is when older learning disrupts the recall of newer information [1]. Retroactive interference is when newer learning disrupts the recall of older information [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Remember "pro" as forwards in time (old affects new) and "retro" as backwards (new affects old).

**3.** Information may still be stored in long-term memory but cannot be accessed [1]. Recall depends on cues that were present when the information was encoded (the encoding specificity principle) [1]. If those cues, such as the context or the person's internal state, are absent at recall, retrieval fails [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The idea that the information is available but not accessible distinguishes retrieval failure from decay.

**4.** Peterson and Peterson (1959) gave participants consonant trigrams to remember [1]. Rehearsal was prevented by counting backwards in threes for intervals of up to 18 seconds [1]. Recall fell sharply as the interval increased, to about 10% after 18 seconds, suggesting short-term memory has a duration of about 18 to 30 seconds without rehearsal [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Include the method that prevented rehearsal; without it, the finding does not show duration.

**5.** Leading questions suggest an answer and can change how a witness recalls an event [1]. Loftus and Palmer (1974) showed participants films of car crashes and asked how fast the cars were going when they "hit" or "smashed" each other [1]. Mean estimates were higher with "smashed" (40.5 mph) than with "contacted" (31.8 mph) [1]. In a second experiment, more participants given "smashed" falsely reported seeing broken glass a week later, suggesting the memory itself was altered [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The broken-glass finding shows memory change, not just response bias; it is the strongest final point.

**6.** French interfering with Spanish is proactive interference [1], because old learning (French) disrupts the recall of new learning (Spanish) [1]. Forgetting French on holiday is retroactive interference [1], because the newer Spanish learning disrupts recall of the older French words [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Similar material, such as two languages, produces the most interference; mentioning this adds strength to the explanation.

**7. (a)** Difference = 18 − 12 = 6 [1]; 6/18 × 100 = 33.3% decrease [1]
**(b)** The room provided external context cues at encoding [1]. In the different room those cues were absent at recall, so some stored words could not be accessed [1].
**(c)** Independent groups design [1]. Limitation: participant variables, such as differences in memory ability, could explain the difference instead of the context [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Divide by the same-context value (18), the condition you are comparing against.

**8.** Repeating the list under her breath uses the phonological loop (the articulatory process) [1]. Reading street names aloud also uses the phonological loop, so the two tasks compete for the same limited-capacity component and the list is likely to be displaced [1]. Tracing a route is visual and spatial, so it uses the visuo-spatial sketchpad, a separate component, and should cause little interference [1]. The friend is therefore wrong: the reading task will be much harder to combine with rehearsal than the tracing task [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the component each task uses and then decide whether they compete; that is the core applied skill.

**9. (a)** In post-event discussion, witnesses combine misleading information from others with their own memory (memory conformity) [1]. Gabbert et al. (2003) found that 71% of witnesses who discussed a crime with a co-witness reported details they had not seen themselves [1]. The witness who did not see the red jacket may have absorbed the detail from the other, or gone along with them to agree [1].
**(b)** Interview witnesses as soon as possible and separately, before they can talk to each other [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Gabbert's control group, who did not discuss, made no such errors; comparing the groups strengthens the answer.

**10.** Any two, two marks each [4]:
- It presents short-term memory as a single store, but KF, whose short-term memory was damaged, recalled visual information better than auditory information, suggesting separate short-term components.
- It suggests rehearsal is the main route into long-term memory, but much information is stored without rehearsal, and rehearsal of trivial material often fails to transfer it.
- It treats long-term memory as one store, which oversimplifies the different kinds of information people retain.
- Much of the supporting research used artificial material such as trigrams and word lists, so it may not reflect everyday memory.

**[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each limitation needs the criticism and the reason or evidence; a list of four short phrases is not two explained limitations.

**11.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point. These indicative points show the content a strong answer could use.

- Johnson and Scott (1976) found 49% of participants identified a man carrying a pen, but only 33% when he carried a knife covered in blood, suggesting weapon focus reduces accuracy [1].
- Anxiety may narrow attention onto the threat, so fewer details of the face are encoded [1].
- Yuille and Cutshall (1986) studied witnesses to a real shooting and found high accuracy months later, with the most stressed witnesses among the most accurate [1].
- The inverted-U explanation suggests moderate arousal improves recall while very high arousal impairs it, which may reconcile the findings [1].
- Laboratory studies may measure surprise rather than anxiety, since participants knew no real danger existed [1].
- Real-world studies cannot control how close witnesses were or how often they recalled the event in the media [1].
- Ethical limits prevent creating real fear in the laboratory, so findings on extreme anxiety rely on uncontrolled field studies [1].
- Judgement: the effect of anxiety is not simple; it depends on the level of arousal and on the situation [1].

**[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Contrasting laboratory and field findings is the most efficient route to the top band.

**12.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point. A strong answer might use this plan.

*AO1 (description)*

- Fisher and Geiselman developed the cognitive interview to improve the accuracy of witness recall [1].
- Report everything: include all details, however trivial, as they may cue other memories [1].
- Reinstate the context: return mentally to the scene, including feelings and surroundings [1].
- This is based on the encoding specificity principle and context-dependent recall [1].
- Reverse the order: recall the event in a different order, reducing reliance on schemas [1].
- Change perspective: recall from another person's point of view, again disrupting schema-driven recall [1].
- The enhanced cognitive interview adds techniques such as building rapport and reducing anxiety [1].
- It contrasts with standard interviews that use closed and leading questions [1].

*AO3 (evaluation and application)*

- Köhnken et al. (1999) combined the results of 53 studies and found an average 41% increase in correct information compared with standard interviews [1].
- The same review found an increase in incorrect information as well, so police must treat extra detail with care [1].
- Not all four techniques are equally useful; some research suggests reporting everything and reinstating context are the most effective [1].
- The interview takes longer and needs trained officers, so Harbridge Police would face training costs and time pressures [1].
- It reduces the use of leading questions, addressing a known cause of inaccurate testimony [1].
- It may be less effective with very young children or vulnerable witnesses who find some techniques hard [1].
- Applied judgement: for Harbridge Police, training is worthwhile for serious cases with key witnesses, where accuracy matters more than time [1].
- Final judgement: the cognitive interview improves the quantity of accurate recall, but it is not a guarantee of accuracy [1].

**[16]**
*Examiner insight:* Without reference to the police force's decision, an otherwise strong essay cannot reach the top band.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Swapping proactive and retroactive interference.
- Describing retrieval failure as the memory being lost.
- Omitting how rehearsal was prevented in duration studies.
- Answering working-memory scenarios without naming components.
- Using percentages from the wrong condition.
- Listing the cognitive interview techniques without explaining why they work.
- Writing about types of long-term memory, which are not Version 1.4 content.
