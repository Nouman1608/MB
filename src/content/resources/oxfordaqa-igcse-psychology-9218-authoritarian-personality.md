**This guide covers two linked statements in 3.2.2 Social influence of OxfordAQA International GCSE
Psychology (9218), Version 1.1: "Explanation of dispositional factors affecting obedience" and
"Adorno's theory of the Authoritarian Personality."** The
[Conformity and Obedience study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-psychology-conformity-and-obedience/)
summarises Adorno's theory in a sentence as the dispositional counterpart to Milgram's Agency theory.
This page explains the theory in full, the research behind it, how to evaluate it and how to use it in
comparison questions.

## Social and dispositional explanations

An explanation of obedience can focus on the **situation** or on the **person**.

- **Social (situational) explanations** say that features of the situation make people obey: a
  legitimate authority figure, the closeness of the victim, the agentic state. Milgram's Agency theory
  is the specification's example.
- **Dispositional explanations** say that some people are more likely to obey because of their
  **personality**: stable characteristics they bring to any situation. Adorno's Authoritarian
  Personality is the specification's example.

A dispositional explanation predicts that, in the **same** situation, some people will obey and
others will not, and that the obedient ones will share particular traits.

## Background to the theory

After the Second World War, psychologists asked how so many people had taken part in, or accepted,
the persecution and murder of millions of people in Nazi-occupied Europe. Theodor Adorno and his
colleagues (Else Frenkel-Brunswik, Daniel Levinson and Nevitt Sanford) proposed that part of the
answer lay in a type of personality that is especially obedient to authority and hostile to people
seen as inferior. They published *The Authoritarian Personality* in 1950.

## The research

Adorno's team studied over two thousand white, middle-class Americans. They developed several
questionnaires, the best known being the **F-scale** (F stands for "fascism"), which measured
agreement with statements expressing authoritarian attitudes: respect for authority, rigid
conventional values, punishment of those who break rules and dislike of weakness. An illustrative
statement of the kind such a scale uses (not taken from the original) is: "Rules made by people in
charge should be obeyed without question."

They also carried out **in-depth interviews** with people who scored very high or very low, asking
about their childhood and attitudes. This combination of quantitative (scale scores) and qualitative
(interview) data is worth mentioning in research-methods links.

## The authoritarian personality: traits

People who scored highly on the F-scale tended to show:

| Trait | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Authoritarian submission** | Exaggerated respect for and obedience to people in authority |
| **Authoritarian aggression** | Hostility and harshness towards people seen as lower status or as breaking rules |
| **Conventionalism** | Rigid commitment to traditional values and rules |
| **Rigid, black-and-white thinking** | Seeing things as right or wrong with no grey areas; discomfort with uncertainty |
| **Prejudice** | Hostility to minority and "out" groups, who are blamed for problems |

## Where it comes from: upbringing

Interviews suggested that high scorers had usually been brought up by **strict, harsh parents** who:

- demanded unquestioning obedience;
- used severe discipline, including physical punishment;
- gave love and approval only when the child behaved as required (**conditional love**).

Adorno's explanation used ideas from psychodynamic theory. The child feels hostility towards the
harsh parents but cannot express it, because doing so would bring punishment. Instead:

1. the child **identifies** with the powerful parent and learns to respect and obey authority;
2. the hidden hostility is **displaced** onto weaker targets who cannot hit back, such as minority
   groups (**scapegoats**).

The adult who results obeys those above them and is harsh towards those below.

## How the theory explains obedience

An authoritarian person:

- **respects authority** for its own sake, so accepts orders from someone in a position of power;
- **does not question** whether orders are right, because of rigid thinking;
- may feel that a **victim deserves** punishment, especially if the victim seems weak or different,
  so obeying an order to harm them causes less inner conflict.

## Research support: Elms and Milgram (1966)

Elms and Milgram followed up a sample of participants from Milgram's obedience studies: some who had
obeyed to the maximum shock and some who had refused. Those who had been **fully obedient** scored
**higher on the F-scale** than those who had defied the experimenter. This supports a link between
authoritarian personality and obedience.

## Evaluation

**Strengths**
- **Supporting evidence:** Elms and Milgram found obedient participants scored higher on the F-scale.
- **Explains individual differences:** in Milgram's baseline study, about a third of participants
  refused to continue. A purely situational explanation cannot explain why people in identical
  situations behaved differently; personality can.
- **Mixed methods:** questionnaires and interviews together give both measurable scores and detail.

**Limitations**
- **Cannot explain whole societies.** Large numbers of people in Nazi Germany obeyed orders. It is
  unlikely that most of them shared the same personality type and upbringing; situational and social
  factors (propaganda, group norms, fear of punishment) better explain obedience on that scale.
- **Situational variation.** In Milgram's variations, obedience rose and fell sharply as the situation
  changed, while the kind of people taking part stayed the same, suggesting situation matters more.
- **Problems with the F-scale.** Every statement was worded so that agreeing indicated
  authoritarianism. People who tend to agree with statements whatever they say (**acquiescence
  bias**) would score highly without being authoritarian.
- **Correlation, not cause.** A link between harsh upbringing and authoritarian scores does not show
  that the upbringing caused the personality. A third factor, such as level of education, could
  affect both.
- **Sample.** White, middle-class Americans, so results may not generalise to other cultures and
  social groups.
- **Political bias.** The F-scale measures right-wing authoritarianism; people can be rigidly obedient
  to authority on the political left too.

## Other dispositional factors

The specification asks for explanation of dispositional factors in general, with Adorno as the named
theory. A brief second example strengthens an answer: **locus of control**. People with an
**internal** locus of control believe they are responsible for what happens to them, and may be more
likely to resist orders they disagree with; people with an **external** locus believe events are
controlled by outside forces, including authority figures, and may be more likely to obey.

## Worked example: comparing explanations

**Question.** Compare Milgram's Agency theory and Adorno's Authoritarian Personality as explanations
of obedience. [6]

> **Type.** Agency theory is a **social** explanation, focusing on the situation, while the
> Authoritarian Personality is a **dispositional** explanation, focusing on the individual **[1]**.
>
> **Mechanism.** Agency theory says people obey because they shift into an **agentic state**, seeing
> themselves as acting for the authority and passing on responsibility **[1]**. Adorno says some people
> obey because a **strict, harsh upbringing** produced a personality that respects authority and does
> not question orders **[1]**.
>
> **Who obeys.** Agency theory predicts most people will obey in the right situation; Adorno predicts
> mainly those with authoritarian traits will **[1]**.
>
> **Evidence.** Milgram's variations, where obedience changed with proximity and location, support
> Agency theory **[1]**, whereas Elms and Milgram's finding that obedient participants scored higher on
> the F-scale supports Adorno **[1]**.

## Common mistakes

- **Calling the Authoritarian Personality a social explanation.** It is dispositional.
- **Forgetting the upbringing.** The theory explains where the personality comes from, not just what
  it is like.
- **Describing the F-scale without saying what it measured.**
- **Evaluation with no reasons.** "It cannot explain Nazi Germany" needs the reason: too many people
  obeyed for one personality type to explain it.

## Exam technique

- **Outline / describe:** traits, upbringing, and how they lead to obedience.
- **Explain:** link a specific trait (submission, rigid thinking) to obeying an order.
- **Evaluate:** support (Elms and Milgram; individual differences) and limitations (scale, causation,
  situational evidence), each explained.
- **Compare:** use matched points (type, mechanism, prediction, evidence) on both explanations.
