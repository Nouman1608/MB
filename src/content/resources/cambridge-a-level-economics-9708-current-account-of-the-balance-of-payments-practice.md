## Section A

**1.** Define a current account surplus. **[2]**

**2.** Which of the following is a credit in Country X's secondary income?

A: dividends received by Country X residents from shares in foreign firms

B: money sent home by Country X nationals who now live and work abroad

C: spending by foreign tourists in Country X

D: Country X's exports of wheat **[1]**

**3.** For Country X, state the component of the current account and whether each item is a credit or a debit.

**(a)** A Country X firm pays insurance premiums to a foreign insurer. **[1]**

**(b)** A foreign-owned branch in Country X sends its profit to its parent company. **[1]**

**(c)** Country X exports cars. **[1]**

**(d)** Country X's government gives cash aid to another country. **[1]**

**4.** Country Y's current account data are: exports of goods 120, imports of goods 135, exports of services 48, imports of services 30, primary income credits 15, primary income debits 22, secondary income credits 9, secondary income debits 4.

**(a)** Calculate the balance of trade in goods. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the balance of trade in services. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the balance of trade in goods and services. **[1]**

**(d)** Calculate the current account balance. **[1]**

**5.** Country Z has a current account balance of −6, a goods balance of −10, a services balance of +7 and a secondary income balance of −2. Calculate its primary income balance. **[2]**

**6.** Explain how a country can have a surplus on trade in goods and services but a deficit on its current account. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Country E has a current account balance of −24 and GDP of 600. Country F has a current account balance of −40 and GDP of 2,000.

**(a)** Calculate each country's current account balance as a percentage of GDP. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain which deficit is more likely to cause concern. **[2]**

**8.** Explain how inflation that is persistently higher than in trading partners could lead to a current account deficit. **[3]**

**9.** Coffee makes up 60% of Country G's exports of goods, which total 50. The world price of coffee falls by 20% and the volume of coffee exports does not change. Other exports do not change.

**(a)** Calculate Country G's new exports of goods. **[2]**

**(b)** Imports of goods are 52. Calculate the balance of trade in goods before and after the price fall. **[2]**

**10.** Explain two consequences of a persistent current account deficit for the domestic economy. **[4]**

**11.** Assess whether a large current account surplus is always beneficial for a country. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** The total value of credits on the current account (exports of goods and services, primary and secondary income received) [1] exceeds the total value of debits over a period, usually a year [1].

**2.** **B** [1]. Remittances from nationals living abroad are transfers with nothing given in return. A is primary income, C is trade in services and D is trade in goods.

**3.** **(a)** Trade in services, debit [1].

**(b)** Primary income, debit [1].

**(c)** Trade in goods, credit [1].

**(d)** Secondary income, debit [1].

**4.** **(a)** 120 − 135 = **−15** [1].

**(b)** 48 − 30 = **+18** [1].

**(c)** −15 + 18 = **+3** [1].

**(d)** Primary income balance = 15 − 22 = −7; secondary income balance = 9 − 4 = +5; CAB = 3 − 7 + 5 = **+1** [1].

**5.** −6 = −10 + 7 + primary − 2 [1], so primary income balance = −6 + 10 − 7 + 2 = **−1** [1].

**6.** The current account also includes primary and secondary income [1]. If net outflows of profit, interest, dividends or transfers are larger than the surplus on goods and services, the CAB is negative [1].

**7.** **(a)** Country E: −24 ÷ 600 × 100 = **−4%** [1]. Country F: −40 ÷ 2,000 × 100 = **−2%** [1].

**(b)** Country E's deficit is more concerning, even though it is smaller in money terms [1], because it is twice as large relative to the size of its economy, so it is harder to finance by borrowing or selling assets [1].

**8.** If prices rise faster than in trading partners, the country's exports become dearer compared with rival products [1], so foreign buyers purchase fewer of them [1]. Imports become relatively cheaper than home-produced goods, so residents buy more imports, and the trade balance and CAB worsen [1].

**9.** **(a)** Coffee exports = 0.6 × 50 = 30; after the fall, 30 × 0.8 = 24 [1]. New exports of goods = 24 + 20 = **44** [1].

**(b)** Before: 50 − 52 = **−2** [1]. After: 44 − 52 = **−8** [1].

**10.** Any two consequences, each explained, for example:

Lower AD: net exports are a component of AD, so a deficit reduces AD [1], lowering output and employment in exporting and import-competing industries [1].

Downward pressure on the exchange rate: the country supplies more of its currency to buy imports than is demanded for its exports [1], so the currency may depreciate, raising import prices and possibly inflation [1].

Future income outflows: the deficit must be financed by borrowing or selling assets to foreigners, which leads to future payments of interest and profit abroad, worsening the primary income balance.

**11.** Benefits: higher net exports raise AD, supporting output and employment [1]; export industries earn foreign currency, and the country can build up assets abroad that yield future income [1]; a surplus may reflect strong competitiveness [1].

Costs and limits: residents consume less than the country produces, so current living standards may be lower than possible [1]; near full capacity, extra AD may cause demand-pull inflation [1]; demand for the currency may push it up, reducing export competitiveness later [1]; a surplus caused by weak domestic spending signals a weak home economy [1]; partners with matching deficits may respond with protection [1].

Judgement: a surplus is not always beneficial; a moderate surplus from strong competitiveness is helpful, but a large, persistent surplus caused by weak home demand carries real costs, so the answer depends on its cause and size.
