## Questions

**1.** Temperature scales.

**(a)** Liquid nitrogen boils at 77 K. Give this temperature in °C. **[1]**
**(b)** A room is at 20 °C. Give this temperature in kelvin. **[1]**
**(c)** A sample warms from 280 K to 305 K. State its temperature rise in °C. **[1]**

**2.** Thermal equilibrium.

**(a)** State what is meant by two objects being in thermal equilibrium. **[1]**
**(b)** A thermometer is placed in hot water and read at once. The reading is too low. Explain why. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why a scale based on the resistance of a thermistor is not an absolute scale of temperature. **[1]**

**3.** A liquid has a density 800 times that of its vapour at the same temperature. Estimate how many times further apart the molecules are in the vapour than in the liquid. **[2]**

**4.** Brownian motion.

**(a)** Describe what is seen when smoke in a glass cell is lit from the side and viewed through a microscope. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain the cause of this motion. **[2]**
**(c)** State what the observation shows about the molecules of air. **[1]**

**5.** Internal energy.

**(a)** Define the internal energy of a system. **[2]**
**(b)** A copper block is cooled from 300 K to 100 K. State how its internal energy changes and explain this in terms of the molecules. **[2]**
**(c)** State what is special about the internal energy of a substance at 0 K. **[1]**

**6.** A solid is heated at constant power and melts.

**(a)** Explain, in terms of energy, why the temperature stays constant during melting even though energy is supplied. **[3]**
**(b)** The specific latent heat of vaporisation of a substance is much greater than its specific latent heat of fusion. Suggest why. **[2]**

**7.** A lead block of mass 1.80 kg has specific heat capacity 130 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. A total of 9.1 kJ is transferred to it.

**(a)** Calculate the temperature rise. **[2]**
**(b)** The block starts at 17.0 °C. Find its final temperature. **[1]**
**(c)** Find the time for a 60 W heater to supply this energy, with no losses. **[1]**

**8.** A student determines the specific heat capacity of a metal block of mass 0.950 kg. The heater has a potential difference of 11.8 V across it and a current of 2.60 A. A graph of block temperature against time has gradient 0.0350 K s⁻¹.

**(a)** Describe how the apparatus is set up, including thermal contact and how the electrical energy is found. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the power of the heater. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the specific heat capacity of the metal. **[2]**
**(d)** The value obtained is larger than the data value for this metal. Give one reason and one improvement. **[2]**

**9.** A steel sample of mass 0.120 kg is heated to 95.0 °C and placed in 0.250 kg of water at 15.0 °C in an insulated cup. The final temperature is 19.0 °C. Take c for water as 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate the specific heat capacity of the steel. **[3]**
**(b)** State one source of error and its effect on your answer. **[2]**

**10.** A student finds the specific latent heat of fusion of ice. A heater with 12.0 V across it and 4.00 A through it is placed in crushed ice in a funnel for 300 s. The meltwater collected is 0.0512 kg. A control funnel with no heater collects 0.0083 kg in the same time.

**(a)** Explain why the control is needed. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the specific latent heat of fusion of the ice. **[4]**

**11.** A student finds the specific latent heat of vaporisation of a liquid by boiling it for 420 s at a power of 70 W, then at 140 W. The mass of liquid lost is 0.0113 kg at 70 W and 0.0241 kg at 140 W.

**(a)** Explain why using two powers removes the effect of energy losses. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the specific latent heat of vaporisation. **[3]**
**(c)** The specific latent heat of fusion of the same liquid's solid form is 3.34 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹. Calculate the ratio of the two values, and explain in terms of molecules why the ratio is greater than 1. **[3]**

**12.** A 500 W heater, with no energy losses, heats 0.350 kg of ice at −20 °C until it is water at 40 °C. Use specific heat capacity of ice 2100 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, of water 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, and specific latent heat of fusion 3.34 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹.

**(a)** Describe the temperature-time graph. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the total energy supplied. **[4]**
**(c)** Find the time taken. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain how the internal energy changes in the flat section. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** **(a)** 77 − 273 = **−196 °C** [1]
**(b)** 20 + 273 = **293 K** [1]
**(c)** The change is the same in both scales: **25 °C** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Answers gain credit for adding 273 to go from °C to K; a temperature rise should never be converted by adding 273.

**2.** **(a)** There is no net transfer of energy between them (they are at the same temperature). [1]
**(b)** The thermometer is initially cooler than the water, so energy flows from the water to the thermometer. [1] It is not yet in equilibrium, so it reads low. [1]
**(c)** It depends on a property of a particular material (the thermistor), whereas an absolute scale does not depend on any substance. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Explanations tend to gain credit when they mention net energy transfer, not just "same temperature".

**3.** Volume per molecule is 800 times larger, and volume scales with spacing cubed, so ratio = 800^(1/3). [1] = **9.3** times further apart. [1]
*Examiner insight:* A common slip is to give the volume ratio (800) as the spacing ratio.

**4.** **(a)** Small bright specks (smoke particles) are seen. [1] They move in random, irregular zig-zag paths. [1]
**(b)** Air molecules move rapidly and randomly and collide with the smoke particle. [1] The impacts from different sides are unbalanced at any instant, giving a resultant force that changes direction randomly. [1]
**(c)** They are in continuous random motion (and are moving rapidly). [1]
*Examiner insight:* Answers lose credit by calling the visible specks molecules.

**5.** **(a)** The sum of the kinetic energies [1] and potential energies of the molecules, which are randomly distributed. [1]
**(b)** Internal energy decreases. [1] The average kinetic energy of the atoms falls as temperature falls. [1]
**(c)** It is the minimum internal energy the substance can have. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Definitions score best when they name both kinetic and potential energy of the molecules.

**6.** **(a)** Energy supplied increases the internal energy. [1] It goes into the potential energy of the molecules as bonds are overcome and they separate. [1] The average kinetic energy does not change, so the temperature is constant. [1]
**(b)** In vaporisation the molecules move from close together to far apart, [1] which needs more work against the attractive forces than loosening the structure in melting. [1]
*Examiner insight:* "No energy is needed" is wrong; credit comes from saying where the energy goes.

**7.** **(a)** Δθ = E / (mc) [1] = 9100 / (1.80 × 130) = 38.9, so **39 K** [1]
**(b)** 17.0 + 38.9 = **56 °C** [1]
**(c)** t = E / P = 9100 / 60 = 152, so **1.5 × 10² s** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Rearrange before substituting; keep unrounded values between parts.

**8.** **(a)** Heater and thermometer sit in holes in the insulated block, with oil in each for thermal contact. [1] An ammeter is in series with the heater and a voltmeter is across it. [1] Energy supplied is VIt. [1]
**(b)** P = VI = 11.8 × 2.60 = **30.7 W** [1]
**(c)** c = P / (m × gradient) [1] = 30.68 / (0.950 × 0.0350) = **9.2 × 10² J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹** [1]
**(d)** Reason: energy was lost to the surroundings, so the temperature rise was smaller than expected. [1] Improvement: add more insulation. [1]
*Examiner insight:* A reason is stronger when it links the loss to a smaller temperature rise.

**9.** **(a)** Energy gained by water = 0.250 × 4200 × 4.0 = 4200 J [1] Energy lost by steel = 0.120 × c × 76.0 [1] c = 4200 / 9.12 = **4.6 × 10² J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** Energy is lost from the water and cup to the surroundings. [1] The final temperature is lower than it should be, so the calculated c is too small. [1]
*Examiner insight:* State the effect on the calculated value, not only the source of the error.

**10.** **(a)** Some ice melts because of the warm room, not the heater. [1] The control measures this mass so that it can be subtracted. [1]
**(b)** E = 12.0 × 4.00 × 300 = 14 400 J [1] Mass melted by the heater = 0.0512 − 0.0083 = 0.0429 kg [1] l = E / m [1] = 14 400 / 0.0429 = **3.36 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Subtract the control mass before dividing; dividing by the full 0.0512 kg is the usual slip.

**11.** **(a)** The liquid is boiling at the same temperature in both runs, so the rate of energy loss is the same. [1] The extra energy supplied at the higher power only vaporises the extra mass. [1]
**(b)** Extra energy = (140 − 70) × 420 = 29 400 J [1] Extra mass = 0.0241 − 0.0113 = 0.0128 kg [1] l = 29 400 / 0.0128 = **2.30 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹** [1]
**(c)** Ratio = 2.30 × 10⁶ / 3.34 × 10⁵ = 6.9 [1] In vaporisation molecules go from close contact to far apart, gaining more potential energy. [1] In fusion they stay close and are only loosened. [1]
*Examiner insight:* The same-temperature argument is the key idea in part (a); a vague "losses cancel" is weaker.

**12.** **(a)** Temperature rises steadily from −20 °C to 0 °C, [1] a flat section at 0 °C, then a less steep rise to 40 °C (water has the larger specific heat capacity). [1]
**(b)** Warm ice: 0.350 × 2100 × 20 = 14 700 J [1] Melt: 0.350 × 3.34 × 10⁵ = 116 900 J [1] Warm water: 0.350 × 4200 × 40 = 58 800 J [1] Total = **1.90 × 10⁵ J** [1]
**(c)** t = 190 400 / 500 = **381 s** [1]
**(d)** The internal energy increases. [1] The potential energy rises as molecules move apart while the average kinetic energy stays the same. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Treat each stage separately and list the three energies before adding.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Subtracting 273 when converting from °C to K.
- Treating the specks in Brownian motion as the molecules themselves.
- Saying no energy is transferred during a change of phase.
- Using m c Δθ across a change of phase.
- Dividing by the whole mass collected, not the mass above the control.
- Giving J instead of J kg⁻¹ for a specific latent heat.
