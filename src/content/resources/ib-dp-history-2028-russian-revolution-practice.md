> **Original questions written for Marlbridge.** They are not past-paper questions and do not copy the real exam's structure or mark tariffs.

**HL only.** These questions practise Regional study 7 in the Europe option (HL Paper 3). Revise first with the [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-russian-revolution-revision-notes/) and the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-russian-revolution/).

## Questions

**1.** State two reforms of Alexander II. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why the Emancipation of 1861 did not solve the problems of the peasants. **[4]**

**3.** Read this short extract, constructed for practice.

> "The Duma may talk, but the Tsar alone rules. When the deputies grow loud, they will be sent home, and the law will be changed so that landowners fill the benches." (Constructed for practice, in the voice of a conservative official in 1907.)

**(a)** Explain what the extract suggests about the nature of the Duma after 1905. **[3]**

**(b)** Analyse one limitation of the extract as evidence about the effects of the October Manifesto. **[3]**

**4.** Explain two reasons why the Bolsheviks won the Civil War. **[4]**

**5.** Analyse the reasons for the failure of the Provisional Government. **[6]**

**6.** Examine the experiences of women under Bolshevik rule to 1924. **[6]**

**7.** "Stolypin's policies of repression and reform strengthened the Tsarist regime." To what extent do you agree? Plan an answer: state your argument, three themes and a counter-argument. **[15]**

**8.** Compare the methods used to deal with opposition under Nicholas II and under the Bolsheviks. **[4]**

**9.** Discuss how historians disagree about whether the Bolshevik seizure of power was a coup or a popular revolution. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two: Emancipation of the serfs (1861); creation of zemstva (1864); judicial reform with independent courts and juries (1864); military reform (1874); relaxation of censorship. One mark each. **[2]**

**2.** Up to 4. Peasants had to pay redemption payments to the state for decades, which kept them in debt (1); land allotments were often small or poor, and the commune controlled use of land, which limited individual improvement (1); peasants stayed tied to the village and still faced the landowning nobility's power (1); population growth increased land hunger, so discontent continued and fed the revolutions of 1905 and 1917 (1). **[4]**

**3. (a)** Up to 3: the Duma has little real power because the Tsar can dismiss it (1); the electoral law can be altered to favour conservatives, as it was in June 1907 (1); the tone shows that conservatives saw it as limited and controlled (1). **[3]**
**(b)** Up to 3: it is one conservative's view, so it ignores the promises of the Manifesto and the views of liberals and workers (1); it is constructed and predicts the later change, so it is a forecast rather than a report of effects (1); the Manifesto did extend some civil liberties and allowed political parties, which this extract does not mention (1). **[3]**

**4.** Up to 4, two reasons explained. The Reds held the central territory with railways and factories, which helped supply and movement (2). The Whites were divided, with different aims and leaders, and many peasants feared that a White victory would bring back landowners (2). Accept: Trotsky's Red Army and the use of terror and conscription. **[4]**

**5.** Up to 6 using a markband approach. Dual Power divided authority between the Government and the Soviet (1); the decision to continue the war and the failed June offensive lost soldiers' loyalty (1); delay over land reform and the Constituent Assembly disappointed peasants (1); inflation, shortages and transport failure continued (1); the Kornilov Affair showed weakness and boosted the Bolsheviks (1); a judgement on which was most important, such as the war (1). **[6]**

**6.** Up to 6: legal equality, easier divorce and civil marriage (1); legal abortion from 1920 (1); the Zhenotdel promoted women's education and political involvement (1); women also served in the civil war and in factories (1); in practice, hardship, famine and the male party leadership limited change (1); a judgement on how far women's lives changed (1). **[6]**

**7.** Credit a plan with these features, 3 marks each, 15 in all. Understanding of the claim: repression through field courts-martial and the Okhrana, and reform through agrarian change (3). Evidence that it strengthened the regime: order restored after 1905; a minority of prosperous peasants; the conservative Third Duma lasted (3). Evidence against: most peasants stayed in the commune; repression alienated people; Stolypin was assassinated in 1911 and strikes returned in 1912 (3). Perspectives: optimist historians who see reform working against pessimists who see a failing regime (3). Counter-argument and judgement: for instance that the policies bought time but did not address the Tsar's refusal to share power, so the regime was only briefly strengthened (3). **[15]**

**8.** Up to 4. Both used secret police, the Okhrana and the Cheka, to arrest opponents (1); both used violence and censorship (1); the Bolsheviks used terror on a larger scale, with the Red Terror and mass requisitioning, and banned other parties and factions (1); a judgement, for example that the Bolsheviks combined control more completely (1). **[4]**

**9.** Up to 6: one view, associated with Pipes, sees a coup by a small, well-organised party (2); another, associated with Rabinowitch and social historians, stresses mass support among workers and soldiers in Petrograd in 1917 (2); explains why they differ, for example by the evidence used and by looking at leaders or at the people (1); a reasoned judgement (1). **[6]**
