These questions cover section 3.1.1 Business and objectives of AQA AS Business (7137), specification Version 1.1. Questions using the specification's command words carry the tariffs it lists for them (Define 2, Explain 4, Analyse 6, Assess 9, Evaluate 15). All businesses and figures are invented. Revise first with the [business and objectives study guide](/resources/aqa-as-level-business-7137-business-and-objectives/) and the [business and objectives revision notes](/resources/aqa-as-level-business-7137-business-and-objectives-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** Define the term "entrepreneur". **[2]**

**2.** Define the term "opportunity cost". **[2]**

**3.** Priya left a well-paid job in a bank to open a dog grooming salon. She says she loves working with animals and wanted to choose her own hours. Explain **one** non-financial reason why Priya set up her business. **[4]**

**4.** Callum is developing a fitness app. Three banks have refused him a loan, and his first version received poor reviews. Explain why resilience is important to Callum. **[4]**

**5.** The owner of Linden Hardware sets the objective: "Our shop aims to be the best in town." Explain why this is not a SMART objective. **[4]**

**6.** Define the term "competitiveness". **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**7.** Marta plans to start a mobile coffee van that will visit business parks and festivals. She has £8,000 in savings and the van will cost £22,000. Several established coffee chains have shops near the business parks. Analyse **two** challenges Marta faces in setting up her business. **[6]**

**8.** Fernhill Games was founded by Rhodri, who wrote the code, designed the games and handled marketing himself. It has grown from 3 to 40 employees in four years. Analyse how Rhodri's role is likely to have changed as the business has grown. **[6]**

**9.** Lewin plans to open a bicycle repair shop using £15,000 of his own savings. He does not need a loan. A friend says writing a detailed business plan would waste time he could spend fitting out the shop. Assess whether Lewin should write a detailed business plan before opening. **[9]**

**10.** Corbel Furniture has £90,000 available to invest and is choosing between two options.

- Option X: open a showroom in a nearby city. Cost £80,000; expected profit £24,000 a year. The city has several established furniture stores.
- Option Y: build an online shop with home delivery. Cost £25,000; expected profit £9,000 a year. Online furniture sales in the region have grown each year, and Corbel's staff have no experience of selling online.

Corbel's objective is to increase profit while keeping its reputation for craftsmanship.

**(a)** For each option, calculate how many years the expected profit would take to cover the cost. Give your answers to one decimal place. **[2]**

**(b)** Evaluate which option Corbel Furniture should choose. **[15]**

**11.** Bramble Toys could cut its costs by 12% by switching to a supplier that is known to make its workers do excessive overtime. Bramble's customers are mainly parents, and its profit fell last year. Assess whether Bramble Toys should switch to the cheaper supplier. **[9]**

**12.** Hartwell Gym has two sites and 25 staff. Its owner has never set formal objectives. Analyse the value to Hartwell Gym of setting SMART objectives. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** A person who identifies a business opportunity [1] and takes the risk of setting up and running a business to exploit it [1]. **[2]**

**2.** The next best alternative [1] that is given up when a choice is made [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Priya was motivated by passion for working with animals [1], which she could not satisfy in a bank job [1]. She was willing to give up a well-paid salary [1], which shows the non-financial reward mattered more to her than income (also credit: flexibility, choosing her own hours) [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* Explain answers need the case: a definition of non-financial motives alone stays at the lower level.

**4.** Resilience is the ability to recover from setbacks and keep going [1]. Callum has already faced three loan refusals and poor reviews [1], so without resilience he might give up before the app succeeds [1]. A resilient entrepreneur would use the reviews to improve the app and look for other finance, such as savings or investors [1]. **[4]**

**5.** It is not specific: "the best" could mean sales, service or quality [1]. It is not measurable, as there is no figure to check against [1]. No one is accountable for it and there is no deadline, so it is not time specific [1]. A SMART version would be: "The manager will raise weekly sales from £6,000 to £7,000 by the end of March" [1]. **[4]**

**6.** The ability of a business to win and keep customers against its rivals [1], through price or non-price factors such as quality, service and brand [1]. **[2]**

**7.** Challenge 1: raising finance [1]. Marta's savings cover only £8,000 of the £22,000 van [1], and as a new business with no trading record she may struggle to borrow the £14,000 or may face high interest, which adds to her costs before she has earned anything [1]. Challenge 2: winning customers from established rivals [1]. The coffee chains near the business parks have known brands and loyal customers [1], so Marta must compete on convenience, quality or price, and if she cuts prices her margins and cash flow will be squeezed in the early months [1]. **[6]**

**8.** At the start, Rhodri did every task himself, from coding to marketing [1]. With 40 staff he can no longer do this, so he must delegate tasks to specialists, such as hiring designers and a marketing manager [1]. His role shifts to managing people: recruiting, organising teams and checking performance [1]. As the business grows further, he is likely to focus on leadership, setting the direction for future games and building the culture of the business [1]. This change can be difficult because he built the business through his own technical skills [1]; if he keeps controlling every decision, he may slow the business down and demotivate experienced staff [1]. **[6]**

**9.** For writing a plan: it would make Lewin test whether there is enough demand, for example by checking competition and likely customer numbers [1], which could prevent him losing his £15,000 savings on a weak idea [1]. Setting out forecasts gives him objectives and a benchmark to measure progress against [1], and he will have a plan ready if he needs finance later [1]. Against: he does not need a loan, so one of the main purposes of a plan, persuading lenders, does not apply [1]. Forecasts for a new shop are often guesses, so a detailed plan may be inaccurate [1], and time spent writing it delays opening and earning revenue [1]. Judgement: Lewin should write a plan, but a short one focused on demand and cash flow rather than a detailed document [1], because the risk to his savings is high and a simple plan costs little time [1]. **[9]**

**10. (a)** Option X: 80,000 ÷ 24,000 = **3.3 years** [1]. Option Y: 25,000 ÷ 9,000 = **2.8 years** [1].

**(b)** Option X: reward. It offers the higher expected profit, £15,000 a year more than Y, which fits the objective of increasing profit [1]. A showroom lets customers see and feel the furniture [1], supporting the reputation for craftsmanship [1].

Option X: risk and resources. It uses £80,000 of the £90,000 available, leaving little for setbacks [1], and the city has several established stores, so the £24,000 forecast may be optimistic [1].

Option Y: risk and resources. It costs only £25,000 and recovers its cost faster, at 2.8 years compared with 3.3 [1]. It leaves £65,000 for other uses, reducing the opportunity cost [1]. Market conditions are favourable, as online furniture sales in the region are growing [1].

Option Y: drawbacks. Staff have no experience of selling online, so Corbel may need to hire or train people, adding cost [1]. Customers cannot see the quality in person, which may weaken the craftsmanship message [1].

Judgement: criteria are profit, risk and fit with the reputation for craftsmanship [1]. Corbel should choose Option Y [1], because it is lower risk, pays back faster, suits a growing market and keeps funds for training staff in online selling [1]. However, the decision depends on whether Corbel can show its craftsmanship online, for example through detailed photos and a generous returns policy [1]; if it cannot, the showroom's stronger fit with the brand might justify its higher risk [1]. **[17]**

*Examiner insight:* A strong evaluation uses the calculations, applies several named influences to both options and gives a judgement with a condition.

**11.** For switching: a 12% cost cut is large and could restore profit after last year's fall [1], and lower costs might allow lower prices, improving competitiveness [1]. Against: the decision is an ethical dilemma, because higher profit would come from poor treatment of workers [1]. Bramble's customers are parents, who may care strongly about how toys are made [1], so if the supplier's practices became public, Bramble could face bad publicity and lost sales that outweigh the saving [1]. Staff and retailers that stock its toys might also react badly [1]. Judgement: Bramble should not switch unless the supplier can show it has improved conditions [1], because the reputational risk is high for a brand sold to parents [1]; it would be better to look for cost savings elsewhere, accepting a smaller saving [1]. **[9]**

**12.** SMART objectives would give staff at both sites a clear, shared target, such as raising membership from 1,200 to 1,400 by December [1], so everyone works towards the same aim instead of each site pursuing its own [1]. Because the objective is measurable and time specific, the owner can check progress each month [1] and take action early if one site is falling behind [1]. Making a named manager accountable means someone is responsible for results [1], which can motivate staff, though only if the target is realistic; an unrealistic target could demotivate them [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving definitions in Explain questions without using the case.
- Writing "Achievable" for the A in SMART; the specification uses Accountable.
- Listing challenges or influences without analysing their consequences for the business.
- Evaluations that ignore the calculation in part (a).
- Judgements with no condition or criterion.
- Treating an ethical dilemma as one-sided.
