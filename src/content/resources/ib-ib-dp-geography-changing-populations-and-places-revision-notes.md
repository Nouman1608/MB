Condensed recall for **8.2 Changing populations and places** (Unit 1: Changing population). For
the full explanation, read the
[Population Distribution study guide](/resources/ib-dp-geography-population-distribution/).

## Two components of population change

- **Natural change** = births minus deaths.
- **Net migration** = immigration minus emigration.

State them separately. Illustration: 120 births and 80 deaths gives +40; 30 immigrants and 50
emigrants gives -20; total change is +20.

## Key indicators

| Indicator | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Crude birth rate (CBR) | Live births per 1,000 people per year |
| Crude death rate (CDR) | Deaths per 1,000 people per year |
| Rate of natural increase | (CBR - CDR) / 10, expressed as a percentage |
| Total fertility rate (TFR) | Average children per woman over her lifetime at current rates |
| Replacement level fertility | About 2.1 children per woman |
| Infant mortality rate | Deaths of infants under one year per 1,000 live births |
| Life expectancy | Average years a newborn can expect to live at current death rates |
| Dependency ratio | Dependants (0-14 and 65+) per 100 people aged 15-64 |

**Worked example (invented figures):** CBR 32 per 1,000 and CDR 8 per 1,000. Natural increase =
24 per 1,000 = 2.4 per cent a year. Why "crude"? Because the rates divide by the whole population and
ignore its age structure: an elderly country can have a high CDR even with excellent health care.

## Reading a population pyramid

| Feature | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Wide base | High birth rate |
| Sharply narrowing top | High death rate and lower life expectancy |
| Relatively wide top | Ageing population with high life expectancy |
| Narrow base | Low (and likely falling) birth rate |
| Unusually narrow or wide band | A historical event affecting that cohort: war, a baby boom or mass migration |

A pyramid with a narrow base, wide middle and wide top indicates an **ageing population** at a later
stage of the demographic transition.

## Contrasting pyramid shapes

| Shape | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Expansive (progressive) | Wide base, concave sides, narrow top | Niger: very high fertility, young population |
| Stationary | Fairly straight sides | Many high-income countries with fertility near replacement |
| Constrictive (regressive) | Base narrower than the middle | Japan: very low fertility and long life expectancy |
| Migration-shaped | Very wide working-age bulge, often mainly male | Qatar and the United Arab Emirates, because of large numbers of foreign workers |

Event-related bands to recognise: deficits among older men in countries with heavy wartime losses,
such as Russia; post-war baby-boom bulges in Europe and North America; and the narrower young
cohorts in China after decades of birth restrictions.

## Migration and places

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Voluntary migration | People choose to move, often for work, study or family |
| Forced migration | People are compelled to move by conflict, persecution or disaster |
| Refugee | A person who has crossed an international border to escape persecution or conflict |
| Internally displaced person (IDP) | A person forced from home who remains within their own country |
| Asylum seeker | A person who has applied for refugee status and awaits a decision |

**Lee's migration model** (1966) adds **intervening obstacles** (distance, cost, borders, law) and
personal factors to simple push and pull factors, which explains why many people with reasons to
move do not.

**Forced migration example:** since the war in Syria began in 2011, millions of Syrians have fled
abroad, with the largest numbers hosted by neighbouring Turkey, Lebanon and Jordan, and millions more
were internally displaced. Consequences include pressure on housing, schools and water in host
areas, informal work, and the loss of skills and population in Syria itself.

Consequences of migration for **source** places can include a loss of young adults, an older
population left behind and income from remittances. For **destination** places they can include a
younger workforce, cultural diversity, extra demand for services and, sometimes, social tension.
Rural places in many high-income countries, such as parts of rural Japan and inland Spain, are
losing population as young people move to cities, leaving ageing villages and closing services.

## Method for a data-response answer

1. Describe the shape first: base, middle, top, any odd bands.
2. Quote figures or age groups with units.
3. Explain with named factors (birth rate, life expectancy, migration, events).
4. Comment on consequences for the place, such as demand for services.

Paper questions often use unseen data, so practise reading unfamiliar pyramids under timed
conditions instead of memorising a few examples.

**Model description:** *"The pyramid has a narrow base, with the 0-4 cohort smaller than the 30-34
cohort, showing birth rates have fallen. The top is wide, with a large share over 65 and more
women than men in the oldest groups, reflecting long life expectancy and higher female survival.
This suggests rising demand for health care and pensions alongside a shrinking labour force."*

## Exam traps

- Describing the pyramid as "bad" or "good" instead of interpreting it.
- Assuming a narrow band always means war; it could be migration or low births.
- Forgetting that migration changes the shape of a pyramid for a particular place.
- Confusing refugees with internally displaced people.
- Quoting a pyramid in percentages as if it showed absolute numbers, or the reverse.

## Self-test

1. Calculate natural change from 120 births and 80 deaths.
2. What does a wide base indicate?
3. What does a relatively wide top indicate?
4. What might an unusually narrow band show?
5. Describe the pyramid of an ageing population.
6. Calculate the rate of natural increase for a CBR of 18 and a CDR of 10 per 1,000.
7. What is replacement level fertility?
8. Why does the pyramid of Qatar have a large working-age male bulge?
9. Distinguish a refugee from an internally displaced person.
10. What did Lee add to the push-pull idea?
11. Give one consequence of out-migration for a source area.

**Answers:** 1. +40. 2. A high birth rate. 3. An ageing population with high life expectancy. 4. A
historical event affecting that cohort, such as war or mass migration. 5. Narrow base, wide middle and
relatively wide top. 6. (18 - 10) / 10 = 0.8 per cent. 7. About 2.1 children per woman, enough for a
population to replace itself without migration. 8. Large numbers of mainly male foreign workers.
9. A refugee has crossed an international border; an IDP remains within their own country.
10. Intervening obstacles and personal factors. 11. For example an ageing population left behind,
or income from remittances.
