## Questions

**1.** Distinguish between a depreciation and a devaluation of a currency. **[2]**

**2.** The exchange rate moves from 1 cor = 5.00 dun to 1 cor = 4.50 dun.

**(a)** Find the percentage change in the cor's value. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the percentage change in the dun's value, expressed in cor. **[2]**

**3.** Explain two factors that could cause the cor to appreciate in a freely floating system. **[4]**

**4.** The table gives demand and supply for the cor, in millions of cor per month.

| Rate (dun per cor) | 4.0 | 4.5 | 5.0 | 5.5 | 6.0 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Demand | 110 | 95 | 80 | 65 | 50 |
| Supply | 50 | 65 | 80 | 95 | 110 |

**(a)** State the equilibrium exchange rate. **[1]**
**(b)** Demand falls by 30 million cor at every rate. Find the new equilibrium rate. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage change in the rate. **[2]**

**5.** **(a)** A speculator spends 600,000 dun buying cor at 5.00, then sells them when the rate reaches 5.40. Calculate the profit in dun. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain how herding behaviour can make an exchange rate more volatile. **[3]**

**6.** Country C's central bank holds the cor at 5.00 dun. At that rate, supply of the cor exceeds demand by 40 million cor.

**(a)** Calculate the reserves of dun the bank must use to hold the rate. **[2]**
**(b)** Its reserves are 1,000 million dun. How many such periods can it defend the rate? **[1]**
**(c)** Explain two reasons why the intervention may eventually fail. **[2]**

**7.** Explain a benefit and a drawback of a fixed rate for firms in a small trading country. **[4]**

**8.** The cor falls from 5.00 to 4.00 dun.

**(a)** An exporter sells a machine for 2,000 cor. Calculate its price in dun before and after. **[2]**
**(b)** An import costs 600 dun. Calculate its price in cor before and after, and the percentage rise. **[2]**
**(c)** Exports are worth 200 million cor and imports 100 million cor. Export prices in cor are unchanged. Export PED is -1.2 and import PED is -0.8. Calculate the new value of exports and of imports in cor. **[4]**
**(d)** State the change in the trade balance. **[1]**

**9.** A bakery's costs are 50,000 cor a month, of which 40% is imported flour and equipment. Import prices in cor rise 20% after a depreciation.

**(a)** Calculate the increase in costs in cor and as a percentage of total costs. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain one other way a depreciation could affect the bakery. **[2]**

**10.** Explain two roles of the International Monetary Fund (IMF). **[4]**

**11.** Evaluate the view that a depreciation will always improve a country's economic performance. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** A depreciation is a fall in value of a floating currency caused by market forces [1]. A devaluation is a deliberate official reduction of the rate under a fixed or managed system [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Name who causes the change in each term, because the definitions differ only in market versus official action.

**2. (a)** (4.50 - 5.00) / 5.00 [1] = **-10%** [1].
**(b)** The dun was worth 1 / 5.00 = 0.2000 cor and is now 1 / 4.50 = 0.2222 cor [1]. The change is (0.2222 - 0.2000) / 0.2000 = **+11.1%** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The two currencies change by different percentages because they have different bases, so state which currency you are measuring.

**3.** Any two of the following, each explained.
Higher interest rates in C than abroad [1] raise returns on assets, so more portfolio inflows raise demand for the cor and the rate rises [1].
Lower inflation in C than in competitors [1] makes exports more competitive, so demand for the cor rises [1].
A rise in FDI inflows would also work, because foreign firms must buy cor. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A named factor on its own tends to gain less credit than one linked through a shift of the demand or supply curve to a higher rate.

**4. (a)** **5.0 dun per cor** [1].
**(b)** New demand is 80, 65, 50, 35, 20 [1]. It equals supply (65) at **4.5** [1].
**(c)** (4.5 - 5.0) / 5.0 [1] = **-10%**, a depreciation [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the shifted demand figures so your reasoning is visible, and say whether the change is an appreciation or a depreciation.

**5. (a)** 600,000 / 5.00 = 120,000 cor [1]. 120,000 × 5.40 = 648,000 dun [1]. Profit **48,000 dun** (8%) [1].
**(b)** Traders copy each other rather than assess fundamentals [1]. If many buy, demand rises and the rate overshoots [1]. When confidence turns, they sell together, causing a sharp fall [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Describe the mechanism of copying and overshooting, and avoid defining herding as speculation in general.

**6. (a)** The bank must buy 40 million cor [1]. Cost is 40 × 5.00 = **200 million dun** [1].
**(b)** 1,000 / 200 = **5 periods** [1].
**(c)** Reserves are finite and run out [1]. Speculators may sell more cor than the bank can buy, or raising interest rates to defend the rate may harm growth [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Convert the currency amount at the defended rate, and state the unit of the reserves.

**7.** Advantage: stability [1], so firms can price contracts and plan investment with less exchange rate risk [1].
Disadvantage: the rate cannot adjust to shocks [1], so if the peg is set too high, exporters may become uncompetitive, and defending it may need high interest rates [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Tie each point to firms, not only to the government, as the question asks about firms.

**8. (a)** Before: 2,000 × 5.00 = 10,000 dun; after: 2,000 × 4.00 = **8,000 dun** [1] [1].
**(b)** Before: 600 / 5.00 = 120 cor; after: 600 / 4.00 = **150 cor** [1]. Rise = 30 / 120 = **25%** [1].
**(c)** Foreign price falls 20%, so quantity rises 1.2 × 20 = 24% [1]. Exports = 200 × 1.24 = **248 million cor** [1]. Import price rises 25%, so quantity falls 0.8 × 25 = 20% [1]. Import spending = 100 × 1.25 × 0.80 = **100 million cor** [1].
**(d)** Balance moves from +100 to +148, an **improvement of 48 million cor** [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the quantity change before the value change, and remember that import prices rise when the home currency falls.

**9. (a)** Imported costs are 0.40 × 50,000 = 20,000 [1]. The rise is 20% × 20,000 = **4,000 cor** [1], which is 4,000 / 50,000 = **8%** [1].
**(b)** If the bakery exports, its goods are cheaper abroad [1], so it may sell more or earn higher margins [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the data given for part (a), and in part (b) give a different effect from the one already calculated.

**10.** Any two, each developed: it lends to members with balance of payments difficulties [1], often with policy conditions [1]; it monitors members' economies and advises on policy [1], helping to spot problems early [1]; or it promotes monetary cooperation and exchange rate stability. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* State each role and then add what it achieves; a bare list of functions does not develop either point.

**11.** Points for credit: lower export prices abroad and higher import prices at home [1]. The trade balance improves only if demand for exports and imports is elastic enough [1]. Elasticities are often low soon after the change, so the balance can get worse at first [1]. If net exports rise, AD, output and employment rise [1], especially with spare capacity and a multiplier [1]. Near full capacity, extra demand mainly raises prices [1]. Dearer imported inputs raise firms' costs and inflation [1]. Exporters may keep the extra profit rather than cut prices, limiting volume gains [1]. Judgement: a depreciation does not always help; the result depends on elasticities, time, spare capacity and import dependence [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* The word "always" invites you to find a counter-case; a supported judgement that names the conditions usually reads better than a list of effects.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing depreciation with devaluation, or appreciation with revaluation.
- Using the wrong base for a percentage change in a rate.
- Inverting the exchange rate in a calculation.
- Naming a factor with no step to a shift in demand or supply.
- Stating that depreciation improves the trade balance with no elasticity.
- Forgetting that import prices rise after a depreciation.
- Leaving out the unit of reserves or the rate used.
- Ending an evaluation with no judgement.
