This guide covers subtopic **33.2, Esters**, from Topic 33, Carboxylic acids and derivatives, of
Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is A Level content. The
broader [Carboxylic Acids and Acyl Chlorides study guide](/resources/a-carboxylic-acids-and-acyl-chlorides/)
states the reaction in a few lines; this page works through it in enough depth to name, plan, write
and calculate with any ester an examiner gives you.

## What the syllabus asks for

Subtopic 33.2 has a single learning outcome: recall the reaction by which esters can be produced by
the "reaction of alcohols with acyl chlorides using the formation of ethyl ethanoate and phenyl
benzoate as examples".

That one line sits on top of AS Level work. At AS (subtopic 18.2) you met the other route, an
alcohol heated with a carboxylic acid and concentrated H₂SO₄, and the hydrolysis of esters by dilute
acid or dilute alkali. A Level questions freely mix the two levels, so this guide uses the AS
chemistry wherever it explains why the acyl chloride route matters. The mechanism itself
(addition–elimination) belongs to 33.3; see the
[acyl chlorides study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-a-acyl-chlorides/).

## The two syllabus examples

**Ethyl ethanoate** from ethanoyl chloride and ethanol:

    CH₃COCl + CH₃CH₂OH → CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ + HCl

**Phenyl benzoate** from benzoyl chloride and phenol:

    C₆H₅COCl + C₆H₅OH → C₆H₅COOC₆H₅ + HCl

Both reactions share the same features, and an answer that names an ester from an acyl chloride is
expected to give them:

- **Conditions:** room temperature. No catalyst and no heating are needed.
- **By-product:** hydrogen chloride, seen as steamy (misty) fumes in moist air.
- **Extent:** the reaction goes to completion. It is not an equilibrium, unlike the carboxylic acid
  route.
- **Rate:** fast, often vigorous with small acyl chlorides.

## Why bother with an acyl chloride?

The AS route, alcohol plus carboxylic acid with concentrated H₂SO₄, is cheaper but has two
weaknesses:

1. It is **reversible**. The ester and water can react back to the acid and alcohol, so the yield is
   limited by the position of equilibrium.
2. It is **slow**. Heat and an acid catalyst are needed.

The acyl chloride route removes both problems. The carbonyl carbon in an acyl chloride is far more
electron-deficient than in a carboxylic acid, because both the oxygen and the chlorine withdraw
electron density from it, so even a weak nucleophile attacks quickly. Chloride is then lost as the
leaving group and HCl escapes, so there is no reverse reaction to limit the yield.

### Phenol: the case where the acyl chloride is essential

Phenol barely forms an ester with a carboxylic acid. A lone pair on the phenol oxygen is partly
delocalised into the benzene ring (the same overlap that makes phenol more acidic than ethanol), so
the oxygen is a much weaker nucleophile than the oxygen of an alcohol. A weak nucleophile and a weak
electrophile give almost no reaction. Pairing phenol with the much more electrophilic acyl chloride
solves this, which is why the syllabus uses phenyl benzoate as its second example: it is the
standard way to make an ester of a phenol.

## Naming esters and working backwards

An ester is named from its two parts, **alcohol (or phenol) part first, acid part second**:

| Ester | From the acyl chloride | From the alcohol or phenol |
|---|---|---|
| ethyl ethanoate, CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ | ethanoyl chloride, CH₃COCl | ethanol |
| methyl propanoate, CH₃CH₂COOCH₃ | propanoyl chloride, CH₃CH₂COCl | methanol |
| propyl benzoate, C₆H₅COOCH₂CH₂CH₃ | benzoyl chloride, C₆H₅COCl | propan-1-ol |
| phenyl ethanoate, CH₃COOC₆H₅ | ethanoyl chloride | phenol |
| phenyl benzoate, C₆H₅COOC₆H₅ | benzoyl chloride | phenol |

To plan a synthesis, split the ester at the single C–O bond between the carbonyl carbon and the
oxygen:

- the side holding C=O becomes the **acyl chloride** (replace –O– with –Cl);
- the other side becomes the **alcohol or phenol** (add H to the oxygen).

The formula is written acid part first (CH₃COO–) but the name puts the alcohol part first (ethyl). A
common slip is to read "CH₃COOCH₂CH₃" left to right and call it "methyl propanoate", which is a
different isomer.

## Worked example 1: planning and writing the equation

**Question.** Identify the two organic reagents needed to make 2-methylpropyl ethanoate,
CH₃COOCH₂CH(CH₃)₂, by the method in 33.2, state the conditions and write the equation.

**Answer.**

- Split at the C(=O)–O bond. The acyl side is CH₃CO–, so the acyl chloride is **ethanoyl chloride,
  CH₃COCl**.
- The other side is –OCH₂CH(CH₃)₂, so the alcohol is **2-methylpropan-1-ol, (CH₃)₂CHCH₂OH**.
- Conditions: **room temperature**, no catalyst.

      CH₃COCl + (CH₃)₂CHCH₂OH → CH₃COOCH₂CH(CH₃)₂ + HCl

*Examiner's note:* one mark is usually for each correct reagent and one for the balanced equation
including HCl. Writing H₂O as the by-product is the commonest way to lose the last mark; water is
the by-product of the carboxylic acid route only.

## Worked example 2: a percentage yield calculation

Percentage yield calculations from reacting masses are AS content (subtopic 2.4) and appear in
organic questions.

**Question.** A student reacts 2.35 g of phenol with excess benzoyl chloride and obtains 4.00 g of
pure phenyl benzoate. Calculate the percentage yield.
(Aᵣ: H = 1.0, C = 12.0, O = 16.0)

**Answer.**

- Mᵣ(C₆H₅OH) = 6(12.0) + 6(1.0) + 16.0 = 94.0
- n(phenol) = 2.35 / 94.0 = 0.0250 mol
- The equation is 1 : 1, so the theoretical amount of phenyl benzoate is 0.0250 mol.
- Mᵣ(C₁₃H₁₀O₂) = 13(12.0) + 10(1.0) + 2(16.0) = 198.0
- Theoretical mass = 0.0250 × 198.0 = 4.95 g
- Percentage yield = (4.00 / 4.95) × 100 = **80.8%**

*Examiner's note:* identify the limiting reagent first. "Excess benzoyl chloride" tells you phenol
limits the yield. Since the reaction is not reversible, losses here come from handling and
purification, not from an equilibrium; that is a valid point if a question asks why the yield is
below 100%.

## Connecting to ester hydrolysis

The AS hydrolysis reactions let examiners test the same ester from the other direction:

- **Dilute acid, heat:** ester + water ⇌ carboxylic acid + alcohol (reversible).
- **Dilute alkali, heat:** ester + OH⁻ → carboxylate ion + alcohol (goes to completion).

So ethyl ethanoate made from ethanoyl chloride can be hydrolysed by NaOH(aq) to sodium ethanoate
and ethanol. Phenyl benzoate with excess NaOH(aq) gives sodium benzoate and, because phenol is
itself acidic, sodium phenoxide rather than phenol. If you need to identify an unknown ester, the
hydrolysis products tell you its two halves; the acyl chloride route tells you how to rebuild it.

## Common mistakes

- **Adding concentrated H₂SO₄ or heat** to the acyl chloride route. Those are the conditions for a
  carboxylic acid, not an acyl chloride.
- **Calling the reaction reversible.** Only the carboxylic acid route reaches an equilibrium.
- **Giving water as the by-product.** It is HCl.
- **Naming the ester backwards.** Alcohol part first in the name, even though it is written last in
  CH₃COOCH₂CH₃.
- **Reacting phenol with benzoic acid** to make phenyl benzoate. That reaction is too slow to be
  useful; the syllabus route uses benzoyl chloride.
- **Writing the phenyl benzoate formula with the wrong ring count.** Both halves contain a benzene
  ring, so the formula is C₁₃H₁₀O₂.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Recall / State:** give reagent names or formulae and conditions, nothing more. "Ethanoyl
  chloride and ethanol, room temperature" is a complete answer.
- **Write an equation:** use structural formulae for organic species and include HCl.
- **Suggest** why one route is preferred: name a specific advantage (not reversible, so higher
  yield; room temperature; no catalyst; works for phenol). "It is better" earns nothing.
- **Explain** why phenol needs an acyl chloride: link the delocalised oxygen lone pair to its poor
  nucleophilicity, then the electron-deficient carbonyl carbon of the acyl chloride.
- **Calculate:** show Mᵣ values, moles and the ratio from the equation, and round only at the end.
