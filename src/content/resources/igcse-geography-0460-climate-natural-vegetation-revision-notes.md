## Weather and climate

**Weather** is the day-to-day state of the atmosphere. **Climate** is the long-term average pattern
of weather. Describe a climate with mean temperature of the hottest and coolest months, the annual
range, and rainfall amount and seasonal distribution.

## The two climates compared

| | Equatorial | Hot desert |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | Hot all year; very small annual range | Very hot by day, cooler at night; large daily range |
| Rainfall | High (well over 2000 mm in the study guide's example), fairly even through the year | Low (typically under 250 mm), unreliable and often in a few events |
| Main rainfall type | Convectional | No regular pattern |

**Where they are found:** equatorial climates lie close to the equator, for example the Amazon basin,
the Congo basin and much of Indonesia. Hot deserts lie roughly between 15 and 30 degrees north and
south of the equator, for example the Sahara, the Arabian Desert, the Kalahari and the deserts of
central Australia, and on some western coasts such as the Atacama and Namib.

## Explaining the equatorial climate

1. The sun is high in the sky all year, so insolation is intense and temperatures stay high, with
   little seasonal change.
2. The hot ground heats the air above it; the air rises in strong **convection currents**.
3. Rising air cools, condenses and forms towering cumulonimbus clouds.
4. Heavy **convectional rain** often falls in the afternoon, with thunderstorms.
5. The area lies in a belt of **low pressure** where rising air dominates, so rain falls in most
   months.

The **daily** temperature range can be larger than the **annual** range, because thick cloud and
humid air keep nights warm but change little through the year.

## Explaining the hot desert climate

- Air that rose at the equator moves away at high level and **sinks** around 30 degrees north and
  south, creating **high pressure**. Sinking air warms, so cloud does not form.
- **Clear skies** let strong sunshine heat the ground by day, but heat escapes rapidly at night, so
  the **daily temperature range is very large**.
- Prevailing winds often blow from land, carrying little moisture.
- On some western coasts a **cold ocean current** cools the air above it, which reduces evaporation
  and rain (the Atacama next to the Humboldt current is the classic example).

## Factors that shape climate

- **Latitude:** angle of the sun; low latitude means consistently hot.
- **Pressure systems:** rising air cools and condenses (equatorial low pressure, rain); sinking air
  warms and its relative humidity falls, suppressing rain (many hot deserts under high pressure).
- **Winds:** bring or block moisture.
- **Distance from the sea:** moisture supply.
- **Altitude:** temperature falls with height.
- **Ocean currents:** warm currents warm coasts; cold currents cool them and can reduce rain.

## Tropical rainforest

- **Layers:** emergent, canopy, understorey, forest floor. Most life is in the dense canopy.
- **Adaptations:** drip tips shed heavy rain, buttress roots support tall trees, plants compete for
  light.
- Dense, layered and highly biodiverse, sustained by constant warmth and plentiful rain.

**More adaptations and their reasons:**

- **Emergent trees** grow very tall to reach the sunlight above the canopy.
- **Lianas** (woody vines) root in the ground and climb trees to reach the light.
- **Epiphytes** such as orchids grow on branches high up, taking moisture from the humid air.
- **Thin, smooth bark** -- there is no cold or dry season to protect against.
- **Shallow roots** -- nutrients are concentrated in the thin top layer of soil.
- The **forest floor** is dark, with few plants, because the canopy blocks most of the light.

**Nutrient cycling:** leaves fall all year and decompose quickly in the hot, wet conditions.
Nutrients are taken straight back up by plant roots, so most are stored in the vegetation, not in
the soil. Heavy rain **leaches** remaining nutrients out of the soil. This is why cleared rainforest
soil loses its fertility within a few years.

## Hot desert

- Sparse vegetation adapted to water scarcity and temperature swings.
- **Adaptations:** long or wide shallow roots, small or waxy leaves, spines, water-storing stems --
  each reduces water loss.

**Adaptations linked to conditions:**

| Adaptation | Condition it answers |
|---|---|
| **Long tap roots** | Reach deep groundwater |
| **Wide, shallow roots** | Catch rain quickly after a rare shower before it evaporates |
| **Thick, fleshy stems** (cacti) | Store water through long dry periods |
| **Spines instead of leaves** | Reduce the surface area for transpiration and deter animals |
| **Waxy coating** | Reduces evaporation from the surface |
| **Seeds that lie dormant** | Survive years of drought, then germinate, flower and seed quickly after rain |

Plants are widely spaced, so each has a large area from which to draw water. Desert soils are thin,
sandy or stony and low in organic matter because so little vegetation grows.

## Deforestation

| Causes | Effects |
|---|---|
| Logging, farming and cattle ranching, mining, road building, settlement | Loss of biodiversity; soil erosion; flooding; less transpiration and local rainfall; more carbon dioxide in the atmosphere |

Weigh the benefits that motivated clearing (income, farmland, jobs) against these costs.

**Effects explained:** without the canopy, heavy rain hits the bare soil directly and washes it away,
silting rivers and increasing flooding downstream. Fewer trees mean less transpiration, so less
water vapour returns to the air and local rainfall can fall. Burning and decomposing trees release
stored carbon, adding to the **enhanced greenhouse effect**. Indigenous peoples can lose land and
their way of life.

**Sustainable management:** selective logging (only mature trees are felled, others left to grow);
replanting; protected areas and national parks; ecotourism that earns money from standing forest;
certification of timber; international agreements and payments for protecting forest.

## Case studies

The syllabus requires **an area of tropical rainforest and an area of hot desert**. Prepare a named
location for each, with climate, ecosystem features and a threat or use.

**Rainforest: the Amazon, Brazil.** The world's largest tropical rainforest, with an equatorial
climate (Manaus, in the centre of the basin, receives over 2000 mm of rain a year and is hot all
year). **Threats:** clearance for **cattle ranching** (the largest cause of deforestation in the
Brazilian Amazon), soya farming, logging, mining such as the Carajás iron ore project, dams for
hydro-electric power and road building (the Trans-Amazonian Highway from the 1970s opened the forest
to settlers). **Responses:** protected areas and indigenous reserves, satellite monitoring of
clearance and the Amazon Fund, which has received international donations to reduce deforestation.

**Hot desert: the Sahara, North Africa.** The largest hot desert, lying under the subtropical high
pressure belt. Rainfall is very low and unreliable; days are very hot and nights can be cold. Plants
cluster at **oases** where groundwater reaches the surface, with date palms grown by farmers. **Uses
and threats:** nomadic herding, oasis farming, tourism, oil and gas extraction in Algeria and Libya,
and solar energy projects; overgrazing and over-use of groundwater at its margins.

## Exam traps

- Describing a climate without explaining it, or naming a factor without linking it to rainfall.
- Saying sinking air "has no moisture" -- the point is that its relative humidity falls.
- Giving only the total rainfall and ignoring its seasonal distribution.
- Naming a plant adaptation without the climate condition it answers.
- A one-sided deforestation answer that omits either benefits or costs.
- Saying rainforest soils are fertile -- the nutrients are in the vegetation, not the soil.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish weather from climate.
2. Why is the annual temperature range so small in an equatorial climate?
3. How does high pressure help create a desert?
4. Explain one adaptation of a desert plant.
5. Give two causes and two effects of rainforest deforestation.
6. Why do deserts have a large daily temperature range?
7. Why does convectional rain often fall in the afternoon in equatorial areas?
8. Why are rainforest soils infertile once the trees are cleared?
9. What are epiphytes and why do they grow high on trees?
10. How can a cold ocean current help create a coastal desert?
11. Give two ways of managing a rainforest sustainably.
12. Why does deforestation increase flooding?

**Answers:** 1. Weather is the day-to-day state; climate is the long-term average. 2. Near the
equator the sun's angle is consistently high, so temperatures stay high all year. 3. Sinking air warms
and its relative humidity falls, which suppresses cloud and rain. 4. For example small waxy leaves
reduce water loss. 5. Causes: logging and farming; effects: loss of biodiversity and soil erosion.
6. Clear skies let the sun heat the ground strongly by day and let heat escape quickly at night. 7.
The ground heats up through the morning, so convection is strongest by the afternoon. 8. Most
nutrients are stored in the vegetation, and heavy rain leaches the rest from the soil. 9. Plants that
grow on other plants, high up to reach light, taking moisture from the humid air. 10. It cools the air
above it, reducing evaporation and the moisture available for rain. 11. For example selective logging
and protected areas (or ecotourism). 12. Without the canopy and roots, rain reaches the ground faster
and runs off, carrying soil into rivers.
