This guide covers content item 2.2.4 Place in Topic 2.2 Making marketing decisions of the Pearson Edexcel Level 1/Level 2 GCSE (9-1) in Business (1BS0) specification, Issue 2, July 2022, assessed in Theme 2, Building a business (1BS0/02). The combined page on [promotion, place and the marketing mix](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-promotion-place-and-combining-the-marketing-mix/) gives a short overview; this guide works through how a business chooses between distribution methods and how to support that choice with figures. All businesses and figures are invented.

## What 2.2.4 contains

The specification has one bullet point under Place: **methods of distribution: retailers and e-tailers (e-commerce)**. It is short, but it is linked to almost every other part of Topic 2.2 and to 2.1.3 (selling overseas through e-commerce), so it appears in many extended questions.

Place means how and where a product reaches customers. Distribution is the process of getting it there. A business making a distribution decision is answering three questions: where do our target customers want to buy, how much control do we want over the way the product is sold, and how much of the selling price are we willing to give to someone else?

## Retailers

A retailer sells goods to the final consumer, usually in physical shops. Examples of types (standard categories, not specification wording):

- **Independent shops:** single stores, often specialist, with personal service.
- **Chain stores:** many branches of the same business across the country.
- **Supermarkets:** large stores selling a wide range, mainly food and household goods.
- **Department stores:** large stores with separate sections for many product types.

**For a producer selling through retailers:**

| Benefits | Drawbacks |
|---|---|
| Access to the retailer's customers and footfall | The retailer takes a large share of the selling price |
| Customers can see, touch and try products before buying | The producer loses control over display, price and service |
| The retailer handles staff, premises and payments | Large retailers can demand low prices and long payment terms |
| Being stocked by a known shop builds trust | Shelf space is limited and may be lost to rivals |

## E-tailers and e-commerce

An e-tailer sells to consumers online. E-commerce is buying and selling over the internet. A producer has two main online routes:

- **Its own website:** it keeps the whole selling price, controls presentation and collects customer data, but must pay for the website, attract visitors, pack and deliver orders and handle returns.
- **An online marketplace or e-tailer:** a large site with millions of visitors lists the product, usually in return for a fee or a share of each sale. Reach is huge, but competition on the same page is intense and the producer has less control.

**For customers**, buying online offers convenience, a wide choice, easy price comparison and shopping at any hour. Against that, they cannot handle the product, must wait for delivery and may face the bother of returns.

**For the business**, e-commerce can reach national or overseas customers without the cost of shops. Its costs move elsewhere: delivery, packaging, payment fees, website upkeep, online advertising and returns, which are often higher for clothing and shoes because customers order several sizes.

## Worked example 1: three routes for Elmstead Honey

Elmstead Honey produces jars of honey at a cost of £2.10 each. The usual shop price is £6.50.

| Route | Elmstead receives per jar | Extra costs per jar | Contribution per jar |
|---|---|---|---|
| Farm shop (retailer) | £4.20 (the shop adds its own mark-up) | none | £4.20 − £2.10 = **£2.10** |
| Own website | £6.50 | £1.10 packing and postage, £0.20 payment fee | £6.50 − £2.10 − £1.10 − £0.20 = **£3.10** |
| Online marketplace | £6.50 less a 20% fee (£1.30) | £1.10 packing and postage | £6.50 − £1.30 − £1.10 − £2.10 = **£2.00** |

Per jar, the own website looks best. Now add the forecast volumes for a year.

| Route | Jars per year | Total contribution |
|---|---|---|
| Farm shop | 3,000 | £2.10 × 3,000 = £6,300 |
| Own website | 800 | £3.10 × 800 = £2,480 |
| Online marketplace | 2,500 | £2.00 × 2,500 = £5,000 |

The retailer route earns the most in total because of its volume, even though it pays the least per jar. The website earns most per jar but reaches few customers. Most producers in this position use more than one route, so the real decision is the balance between them. A full answer would also count the owner's time spent packing website and marketplace orders, which the farm shop route avoids.

## Multi-channel distribution

Many businesses now sell through both shops and online, often called "clicks and bricks" or multi-channel retailing (standard terms). Customers may browse in store and buy online, or order online and collect in store. The benefits are wider reach and convenience; the costs are running two systems and keeping prices and stock consistent across them.

### Worked example 2: Hatherley Shoes adds a website

Hatherley Shoes has one shop. It plans a website costing £4,000 a year, forecasting 1,200 online orders. Each pair kept earns £22 contribution before delivery, and delivery costs £5 a pair. The owner expects 15% of online pairs to be returned, with £3 return postage paid by the shop on each; returned pairs go back into stock and earn nothing.

1. Pairs kept = 1,200 × 85% = 1,020
2. Contribution from kept pairs = 1,020 × (£22 − £5) = £17,340
3. Return postage = 180 × £3 = £540
4. Net gain = £17,340 − £540 − £4,000 = **£12,800**

The website looks worthwhile. The answer should still question the 1,200 forecast, since a single shop has no national brand to draw visitors, and note that some online buyers may be existing shop customers switching channel, which would add no new sales.

## Factors in choosing a method

| Factor | Points towards retailers | Points towards e-tailers |
|---|---|---|
| Type of product | Customers want to see, smell or try it (furniture, perfume, fresh food) | Standard items bought on description (books, phone cases) |
| Target market | Customers who prefer shops or want advice | Busy or remote customers, younger buyers |
| Reach | Local or regional | National or international |
| Control | Retailer controls display and price | Own website gives full control |
| Cost | Retailer's share of the price | Delivery, fees, website and returns |

## Common mistakes

- Saying online selling has no costs because there is no shop.
- Comparing routes on contribution per unit only, ignoring volume.
- Using "retailer" to mean any business. A retailer sells to the final consumer.
- Forgetting that a retailer adds its own mark-up, so the producer receives less than the shop price.
- Ignoring the customer's view: some products sell poorly online because customers want to try them first.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate:** contribution per unit through each route, total contribution at forecast volumes, or a percentage fee.
- **Outline:** two linked points in context, such as why a farm shop suits Elmstead's local honey buyers.
- **Explain:** a general statement with two expansions, for example why e-tailers suit customers in remote areas.
- **Analyse:** follow the effect of a distribution decision on reach, costs, control and sales.
- **Justify:** choose between two routes, using the figures and the target market, and say why the other route is weaker.
- **Evaluate:** weigh several routes or a mix of them and conclude on what the decision depends.
