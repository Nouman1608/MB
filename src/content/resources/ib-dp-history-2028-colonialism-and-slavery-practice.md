## Questions

**1.** Explain two reasons why small Spanish forces were able to defeat the Aztec and Inca states. **[4]**

**2.** Distinguish between the encomienda and the mita. **[3]**

**3.** Read the extract (constructed for practice).

> "The King has ordered that the Indians be treated as free vassals. Yet in this district the encomendero still takes their labour for six months of the year, and the priest says nothing. I write because the Crown must hear what its officers will not report." (Constructed for practice: a letter from a friar to the Crown in the style of the 16th century.)

**(a)** State two things the extract suggests about the enforcement of royal reforms. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain one reason why the author's position might affect how reliable the letter is. **[3]**

**4.** Analyse the economic and political reasons for the development of transatlantic slavery. **[6]**

**5.** Explain how enslaved women experienced life on plantations. **[6]**

**6.** Examine the forms of day-to-day and cultural resistance used by enslaved peoples. **[8]**

**7.** Discuss the extent to which the Columbian Exchange was the main reason for the decline of Indigenous populations. **[8]**

**8.** To what extent was the Haitian Revolution important for the growth of early opposition to slavery? Plan your answer, with a thesis, three paragraph topics, a perspective and a judgement. **[8]**

**9.** Paper 3 style essay question: "The abolition of the slave trade was caused by moral campaigning rather than by economic change." To what extent do you agree? **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two developed reasons, 2 marks each (1 for the reason, 1 for explanation or evidence). Local allies: Tlaxcala and other groups who resented Aztec tribute joined Cortés [1], providing tens of thousands of warriors and supplies [1]. Disease: smallpox killed many people and disrupted leadership and defence [1], including in Tenochtitlan in 1520 [1]. Inca civil war: the war between two claimants to the throne had weakened the state when Pizarro arrived [1], and Pizarro's capture of Atahualpa removed the ruler at a crucial moment [1]. Weapons and horses: steel, firearms and cavalry gave local advantages in open battle [1] but were not sufficient on their own [1]. **[4]**

**2.** Encomienda: a grant by the crown to a settler of the right to collect tribute and labour from a community, in return for protection and religious instruction [1]. Mita: a labour draft adapted from the Inca system, in which communities supplied a share of adult men for work in places such as the mines at Potosí [1]. Distinction: the encomienda linked a settler to a community and gave him personal rights, while the mita was organised by the state or its officials for public or mining work [1]. **[3]**

**3. (a)** The crown had issued reforms but local settlers ignored them [1]; officials and the Church were failing to report or stop abuses [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The author is a friar, who may have a reason to stress abuses, such as a moral commitment to Indigenous people or a quarrel with local settlers [1]. This may lead to exaggeration or selection [1]. However, a friar working in the district had direct knowledge, and the letter's purpose of informing the crown makes the factual claim useful when set against other evidence [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Economic: sugar and other crops needed a large workforce [1]; Indigenous populations had fallen and indentured servants were temporary [1]; profits for merchants and planters drove demand [1]. Political: states granted monopolies and contracts such as the asiento [1]; colonial laws turned enslaved people into property and made status hereditary [1]. Analysis: the economic factors set the demand and political decisions created the legal and commercial structure to meet it [1]. **[6]**

**5.** Work: field labour on sugar and other estates, and domestic work [1]. Vulnerability: sexual violence and exploitation by owners and overseers [1]. Legal position: in many colonies a child's status followed the mother, so women's reproduction mattered to owners [1]. Health: heavy work and poor conditions affected childbirth and survival [1]. Agency: women kept cultural traditions, formed support networks and took part in resistance [1]. Evidence of specific colony or example, such as Jamaica or Saint-Domingue [1]. **[6]**

**6.** Day-to-day resistance: slowing work, breaking tools, feigning illness, theft, short-term flight [2]. Cultural resistance: keeping African religions and languages, creating Creole languages, music and dance, preserving family networks; Vodou, Candomblé and Obeah as examples [2]. Examination: these forms were widespread because open revolt was dangerous [1]; they limited the productivity of owners and sustained identity [1]. Evaluation: they did not end slavery by themselves but prepared the ground for larger resistance [1]. Supported judgement [1]. **[8]**

**7.** For the claim: smallpox, measles and other diseases killed large numbers, often before direct contact with Europeans [2]; the loss of leaders and workers made conquest and control easier [1]. Against, or alongside: violence, forced labour in mines and encomiendas, displacement and famine [2]. Perspectives: Crosby and others who stress disease; Las Casas and later critics who stress cruelty and exploitation [1]. Regional variation (the Caribbean collapse versus central Mexico or the Andes) [1]. Judgement on whether disease can be called the main reason [1]. **[8]**

**8.** Thesis such as: "The revolution was important because it demonstrated that enslaved people could win freedom, but its effects on abolitionism were mixed" [1]. Paragraph topics: (1) the revolution frightened planters and led to harsher laws in some colonies [1]; (2) it gave abolitionists evidence of the enslaved's capacity and political agency, and it ended French slavery temporarily in 1794, though Napoleon tried to restore it [1]; (3) British campaigning had other roots: Quakers, evangelicals, popular petitions and the 1807 Act [1]. Perspective: historians who stress enslaved people's role versus those who stress white reformers or economics [2]. Judgement [2]. **[8]**

**9.** Use the Paper 3 markbands. A top answer (13 to 15 marks) shows in-depth understanding of the argument, evaluates a range of perspectives and reaches a substantiated judgement. Moral campaigning (about 4 marks): Quakers, evangelicals, Clarkson and Wilberforce, petitions, Equiano and the *Brookes* image. Economic change (about 4 marks): Williams's argument that slavery was becoming less profitable and that the West Indian lobby was weakening, and the rise of free-trade ideas. Challenges to Williams (about 3 marks): Drescher's argument that slavery remained profitable and that abolition was a political and moral choice; role of slave resistance, including Haiti and Jamaica. Judgement (about 4 marks): for example, that campaigning was decisive in timing, helped by political conditions and resistance, with economics as a background condition. **[15]**
