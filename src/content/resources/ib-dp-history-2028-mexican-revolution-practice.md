## Questions

**1.** Explain two economic reasons for the outbreak of the Mexican Revolution in 1910. **[4]**

**2.** Read the extract (constructed for practice).

> "The hacienda has taken the pasture, the water and now the fields of our village. The president speaks of progress, but we have been given only papers. We will take back what is ours." (Constructed for practice: a paraphrase of a peasant leader's statement in 1911.)

**(a)** Identify two grievances in the extract. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how the extract helps to understand Zapata's support in Morelos, and one limitation of using it on its own. **[4]**

**3.** Distinguish between the aims of Francisco Madero and Emiliano Zapata. **[3]**

**4.** Analyse the impact of Article 27 and Article 123 of the 1917 Constitution. **[6]**

**5.** Explain why the Cristero War broke out. **[6]**

**6.** Examine the achievements of Lázaro Cárdenas in renewing the revolution. **[8]**

**7.** Discuss the role of the United States in the Mexican Revolution to 1920. **[8]**

**8.** To what extent did the Mexican Revolution improve the lives of women and marginalised groups? **[8]**

**9.** Paper 3 style essay question: "The Mexican Revolution changed the political elite but not Mexican society." To what extent do you agree? **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Two developed points, 2 marks each. Land concentration: haciendas expanded at the expense of village lands, leaving most rural families landless [1], which created peasant grievances, especially in Morelos [1]. Foreign investment and unequal growth: railways, mining and oil expanded under foreign capital [1], but the benefits went to elites and foreigners while real wages stagnated and food prices rose [1]. Labour conditions: strikes at Cananea (1906) and Río Blanco (1907) were repressed [1], showing workers had no legal channel for complaints [1]. **[4]**

**2. (a)** Loss of village land and water to the hacienda [1]; distrust of official promises of progress [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** It shows that loss of land was a core grievance in Zapata's region and fits his Plan of Ayala demand for return of village lands [1], and expresses a willingness to act independently of the government [1]. Limitation: a single statement does not show how widespread the view was [1]; a constructed or recorded paraphrase may reflect the compiler's choices, and tells us nothing about other groups such as northern ranchers or workers [1]. **[4]**

**3.** Madero wanted political change: free elections and an end to Díaz's re-election, from the Plan of San Luis Potosí [1]. Zapata wanted land reform and return of village lands, set out in the Plan of Ayala (1911) [1]. Madero did not make land reform a priority as president, which led Zapata to rebel against him [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Article 27: land and subsoil belong to the nation, allowing redistribution and limits on private property [1]; basis for ejidos and later the oil expropriation of 1938 [1]. Article 123: eight-hour day, minimum wage, limits on child labour, right to organise and strike [1]; gave workers legal claims [1]. Impact: slow at first, as Carranza and others did not enforce them fully [1]; real effect came under Cárdenas, and the articles shaped state-labour relations and the state's role in the economy [1]. **[6]**

**5.** Background: the 1917 Constitution restricted the Church by banning church ownership of property, limiting religious schools and restricting priests [1]. Calles enforced these laws strictly in 1926, including rules on foreign priests and registration of clergy [1]. Catholics, especially in western states such as Jalisco, saw this as an attack on their faith [1]. Church leaders suspended public worship in 1926 [1]. Rural communities, with local leaders, rebelled under the slogan "Long live Christ the King" [1]. The government's secular and centralising policy and its lack of dialogue made conflict more likely [1]. **[6]**

**6.** Land: about 18 million hectares redistributed, much through ejidos [2]. Labour and peasants: organised national confederations of workers and peasants, strengthening their role in politics [1]. Education: expanded rural schools with a social emphasis [1]. Economic nationalism: nationalised the railways (1937) and the foreign oil companies (1938) [2]. Political: broke Calles's control and renamed the party in 1938 [1]. Limits and judgement: ejidos often lacked credit; corporatist structures tied groups to the state; the revolution renewed in rhetoric but also in practice [1]. **[8]**

**7.** Motives: protect investments and citizens, secure oil, prevent radicalism [1]. Early support for Huerta from Ambassador Henry Lane Wilson [1]. President Wilson refused to recognise Huerta, allowed arms to Constitutionalists and ordered the occupation of Veracruz in 1914 [2]. Pershing's Punitive Expedition (1916 to 1917) after Villa's raid on Columbus failed to capture Villa and strained relations [2]. Zimmermann Telegram (1917) pushed Mexico and the US to a new alignment [1]. Judgement: US actions affected the course and timing but the revolution was driven by Mexican causes [1]. **[8]**

**8.** Women: soldaderas, nurses and organisers took part in the fighting [1]; the Law of Family Relations (1917) allowed divorce [1]; but no national vote until 1953 and limited legal equality [2]. Indigenous communities: land reform and rural education reached some [1]; indigenismo celebrated Indigenous culture but aimed at integration into mestizo identity [1]. Peasants: land redistribution under Cárdenas benefited many but many lacked support [1]. Judgement on the extent of improvement: mixed and slow [1]. **[8]**

**9.** Use the Paper 3 markbands. A top answer (13 to 15 marks) shows in-depth understanding of the argument, evaluates a range of perspectives and reaches a substantiated judgement. Support (about 4 marks): the old Porfirian elite was removed, but a new group of generals and politicians (Obregón, Calles) controlled the state; the party-state dominated from 1929; inequality persisted. Against (about 4 marks): the Constitution of 1917, land reform under Cárdenas, labour rights, the role of the state in the economy, rural education, and cultural change. Perspectives (about 3 marks): revisionist view that the revolution changed elites more than society; Womack and Knight on real popular change; the "Revolutionary Family" account as self-justification. Judgement (about 4 marks): for example, that change was real but uneven and in time, with more social change under Cárdenas than in the 1920s. **[15]**
