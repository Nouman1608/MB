This set tests **3.2.3.1 Sources of finance** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Business (9725) specification, Version 1.1 (International AS, Unit 2). Section A has short-answer and calculation questions; Section B has longer, structured questions. Every business and figure is invented, and interest is simplified to the full amount for the period stated. Revise first with the [sources of finance study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-sources-of-finance-9725/) and the [sources of finance revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-sources-of-finance-9725-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Define the term sale and leaseback. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between internal and external sources of finance. **[2]**

**3.** A business is overdrawn by $18,000 for 4 months. The bank charges interest at 9% a year. Calculate the interest paid. **[2]**

**4.** Tallis Hardware reduces its inventory from $95,000 to $70,000 and reduces the amount customers owe it from $140,000 to $115,000. Calculate the total cash released. **[3]**

**5.** The founders of Brightmoor Ltd own all 200,000 of its shares. A private equity firm buys 50,000 newly issued shares. Calculate the percentage of the company owned by the private equity firm and by the founders after the issue. **[3]**

**6.** A business angel invests $90,000 in exchange for a 30% stake in a start-up. Calculate the value this places on the whole business. **[2]**

**7.** Explain **one** disadvantage of a bank loan for a business whose sales are highly seasonal. **[3]**

**8.** State **two** external sources of finance that may be suitable for a start-up. **[2]**

## Section B

**9.** Fernbrook Garden Centre needs to buy $40,000 of plants and garden furniture in February, ready for sales in April to June. Analyse whether trade credit or an overdraft would be the more suitable source of finance. **[6]**

**10.** Kindle Lane Games launched a crowd funding campaign with a target of $75,000 to produce a new board game. 1,250 backers pledged an average of $56 each.

**(a)** Calculate how much the campaign raised and the shortfall against its target. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain **two** disadvantages of crowd funding for Kindle Lane Games. **[4]**

**11.** Explain **two** reasons why retained profits may not be a suitable source of finance for a fast-growing business. **[4]**

**12.** Pikewell Foods Ltd, a family-owned food manufacturer, needs $2m to build a second factory to meet growing demand from supermarkets. It has made steady profits for ten years. It is considering three options:

- **Option 1:** a ten-year bank loan at 7% interest a year, secured on its existing assets.
- **Option 2:** selling a 35% stake to a private equity firm, which would appoint two directors to the board.
- **Option 3:** sale and leaseback of its head office, valued at $2.2m, with rent of $150,000 a year.

The family wants to keep control of the business. Supermarket contracts are renewed every two years. Evaluate which option Pikewell should choose. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** Selling an asset, usually property, to raise cash [1] and then renting (leasing) it back so the business can continue to use it [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Internal finance is raised from within the business, such as retained profits or selling assets [1]; external finance comes from outside the business, such as banks, suppliers or investors [1]. **[2]**

**3.** 18,000 × 9% × 4/12 [1] = **$540** [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Inventory: 95,000 − 70,000 = $25,000 [1]. Receivables: 140,000 − 115,000 = $25,000 [1]. Total = **$50,000** [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Total shares = 200,000 + 50,000 = 250,000 [1]. Private equity: 50,000 ÷ 250,000 × 100 = **20%** [1]. Founders: **80%** [1]. **[3]**

**6.** 90,000 ÷ 30% [1] = **$300,000** [1]. **[2]**

**7.** Loan repayments are fixed and due every month [1], but a seasonal business earns most of its revenue in a few months [1], so in quiet months it may struggle to make repayments and may need an overdraft as well, adding interest costs [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Any two of: business angels [1]; crowd funding [1]; share capital (from the owners or investors); a bank loan, if security is available; trade credit. **[2]**

**9.** The need is short term: stock bought in February will be sold between April and June [1]. Trade credit is usually interest-free, so if suppliers allow 60 to 90 days, much of the stock would be sold before payment is due [1], effectively financing itself [1]. However, suppliers may not offer such long terms on $40,000, and some stock may not sell until June [1]. An overdraft is flexible, so Fernbrook pays interest only on what it uses and repays as spring sales come in [1], but rates are higher and the bank could reduce the limit. Using trade credit first and an overdraft to cover any gap would cost least [1]. **[6]**

**10. (a)** 1,250 × 56 = **$70,000** raised [1]; shortfall = 75,000 − 70,000 = **$5,000** [1].

**(b)** On many platforms, a campaign that misses its target receives none of the pledges [1], so Kindle Lane may have spent time and money on the campaign and raised nothing [1]. The game idea is made public before production [1], so a larger rival could copy it and launch first [1]. **[6]**

**11.** A fast-growing business may need large sums quickly, such as for new premises [1], and retained profits may be too small or take years to build up, so growth opportunities are missed [1]. Rapid growth often means spending on inventory and staff before customers pay, so profit may not be available as cash [1]; using it for investment could leave the business short of working capital [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Credit chains of reasoning and a judgement, for example:

- Option 1 keeps the family in full control, which is their stated priority [1].
- Interest of about 7% × $2m = $140,000 in the first year is a predictable cost [1], and ten years of steady profits suggest Pikewell can afford repayments [1].
- But repayments are due even if a supermarket contract is not renewed after two years [1], so the factory's income is less certain than the ten-year loan [1].
- Option 2 brings no repayments, and private equity may bring expertise in managing growth [1].
- But giving up 35% and two board seats reduces the family's control and future profits, conflicting with their objective [1], and private equity may push for a sale within a few years [1].
- Option 3 raises more than enough ($2.2m) without debt or giving up shares [1].
- But it adds $150,000 rent every year, more than the first-year loan interest, and the cost never ends [1]; Pikewell would also lose its most valuable asset, which could be security for future borrowing [1].
- Judgement: the loan is most suitable, because it keeps control and costs less than the leaseback, provided Pikewell plans for the contract risk, for example by seeking longer contracts or keeping an overdraft facility [1]. **[12]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Forgetting to scale annual interest to the number of months.
- Calculating the new investor's share using the old total number of shares.
- Recommending a long-term source for a short-term need, or the reverse.
- Ignoring the owners' wish to keep control when evaluating equity options.
- Listing advantages and disadvantages without reaching a recommendation.
