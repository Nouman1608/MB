These notes cover section 3.3.1.2, Graphical skills, of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Geography (9230) specification, Version 4.3. Geographical skills can be tested in all three written exams. For full explanations and worked examples, read the [graphical skills study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-geography-9230-graphical-skills/). For the wider skills picture, see the [geographical and fieldwork skills revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-geography-9230-geographical-and-fieldwork-skills-revision-notes/).

## Specification checklist

- **Select and construct:** line, bar, pie, pictogram, histogram (equal class intervals), divided bar, scattergraph, population pyramid.
- **Suggest** a suitable graph for given data.
- **Complete:** choropleth, isoline, dot map, desire line, proportional symbol, flow line.
- **Gradient, contour and value** on isoline maps.
- **Plot** on given axes and scales.
- **Interpret:** population pyramids, choropleth, flow-line maps, dispersion graphs.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Class interval | Group of values in a histogram |
| Choropleth | Map shaded by value in each area |
| Isoline | Line joining equal values |
| Desire line | Straight line from origin to destination |
| Flow line | Line whose width shows quantity |
| Proportional symbol | Symbol sized to show value |
| Dispersion graph | Graph showing the spread of values along a scale |
| Gradient | Vertical change divided by horizontal distance |
| Dependency ratio | Dependants per 100 people of working age |

## Which graph?

| Data | Graph |
|---|---|
| Change through time | Line graph |
| Comparing categories | Bar chart |
| Parts of a whole | Pie chart or divided bar |
| Continuous classes of frequency | Histogram |
| Two variables | Scattergraph |
| Age and sex | Population pyramid |
| Spread of values | Dispersion graph |
| Counts shown by symbols | Pictogram |

Give a **reason** linked to the data when asked to suggest a graph.

## Drawing rules

1. Title.
2. Labelled axes with units.
3. Even scale; bar charts and histograms start at zero.
4. Accurate plotting.
5. Key if needed.
6. Scattergraph points are **not** joined; add a line of best fit if asked.
7. Histogram bars touch; classes are equal width.

## Pie chart angles

**Angle = (value ÷ total) × 360.**

Example: 120 plots; residential 48 (144 degrees), commercial 30 (90), industrial 24 (72), open space 18 (54). Check the total is 360.

Quick route: if the total is 90, each item is worth 4 degrees; if 120, 3 degrees; if 72, 5 degrees.

## Divided bar

Choose a convenient length (10 cm for percentages gives 1 cm per 10%). Example: 40%, 25%, 20%, 15% give 4.0, 2.5, 2.0 and 1.5 cm.

## Maps

| Map | Remember |
|---|---|
| Choropleth | Light to dark for low to high; use rates or density, not totals; check class boundaries in the key |
| Isoline | Lines never cross; close lines mean rapid change; label values |
| Dot map | One dot = a set number; shows clusters |
| Desire line | Straight line, origin to destination; not the real route |
| Proportional symbol | Size is proportional to value; a symbol with four times the area is four times the value |
| Flow line | Width is proportional to quantity |

## Gradient and isoline values

- **Gradient = vertical change ÷ horizontal distance** (same units).
- Example: 60 m rise over 1 500 m gives 1 in 25 (4%).
- Close contours mean steep slope; wide spacing means gentle slope.
- Estimating an isoline value: value at X = lower line + (distance from lower line ÷ total gap) × difference between lines.
- Example: 3 cm from the 20 degree line, gap 5 cm, rise of 5 degrees: 20 + (3 ÷ 5) × 5 = 23 degrees.

## Population pyramids

| Shape | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Wide base, narrow top | High birth and death rates; LIC |
| Straight sides | Low birth and death rates; HIC |
| Narrow base, wide top | Very low birth rate; ageing population |
| Notches or bulges | Past war, migration or baby boom |

**Dependency ratio** = (percent aged under 15 + percent aged 65 and over) ÷ percent aged 15 to 64 × 100.

Example: (38 + 4) ÷ 58 × 100 = about 72 per 100.

## Reading other displays

- **Choropleth:** describe the pattern, quote classes, note exceptions.
- **Flow-line map:** widest line is largest flow; name the origin and destination.
- **Dispersion graph:** look at clusters, gaps, range and outliers.
- **Scattergraph:** direction, strength, anomalies.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Describe the pattern shown by a population pyramid with a wide base and a narrow top, and suggest what it shows about the country. [3 marks]

**Model answer:** "The pyramid has the widest bars at the youngest age groups and the bars get narrower with age, so the top is very narrow. This shows a high proportion of children and few elderly people. It suggests a high birth rate and a lower life expectancy, which is typical of an LIC."

How it scores: one mark for the description of the shape, one for the meaning in terms of age structure, and one for the link to birth rates and development.

## Line graph routine

1. Independent variable on the horizontal axis, measured variable on the vertical axis.
2. A scale that uses at least half the space, such as 5 units per large square for values up to 38.
3. Mark points with small crosses.
4. Join with straight lines if the data are continuous through time.
5. Label axes with units; add a title; check each value.

A climate graph uses a line for temperature and bars for rainfall, with two labelled vertical scales.

## Recall: map types in one line each

- Choropleth: shaded areas, density or rate.
- Isoline: equal-value lines that do not cross.
- Dot map: each dot a fixed number.
- Desire line: straight origin to destination line.
- Proportional symbol: area shows value.
- Flow line: width shows amount.

## Exam traps

- Pie chart angles must **add to 360**.
- Do not use totals on a choropleth map.
- Do not join scatter points.
- Isolines never cross.
- Give the unit with gradients and heights.
- For proportional symbols, area is proportional; four times the area has twice the radius.
- When asked to describe a graph, quote **figures** from it.

## Self-test

1. Which graph shows two variables and a possible relationship?
2. A pie chart has 60 items in total. How many degrees represent 15 items?
3. What is the gradient between two points 40 m apart in height and 2 000 m apart in distance?
4. On a choropleth map, what does a darker shade usually show?
5. What does a wide base on a pyramid suggest?
6. What do desire lines show?
7. What does a flow line's width show?
8. Isolines at 10 and 20 with a point halfway between: what is its value?
9. What is the dependency ratio if 30% are under 15, 5% are 65 and over, and 65% are 15 to 64?
10. Why must histogram classes be equal in width?

**Answers**

1. A scattergraph.
2. 15 ÷ 60 × 360 = 90 degrees.
3. 40 ÷ 2 000 = 1 in 50 (2%).
4. A higher value (such as a higher density).
5. A high birth rate and many young people.
6. Movement from an origin to a destination, as straight lines.
7. The amount moving along that route.
8. 15.
9. (30 + 5) ÷ 65 × 100 = about 54 per 100.
10. So that the height of a bar can be compared fairly with others; unequal widths would distort the picture.
