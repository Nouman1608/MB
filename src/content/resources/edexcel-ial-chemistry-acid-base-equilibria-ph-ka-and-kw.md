## 14.1 and 14.2 Brønsted–Lowry acids, bases and conjugate pairs

In Brønsted–Lowry theory, an **acid** donates protons (H⁺ ions) and a **base** accepts them. So every acid-base reaction is a proton transfer: one species loses H⁺ and another gains it.

When an acid gives away its proton, what is left can take a proton back, so it is a base. The two species form a **conjugate acid-base pair**. They differ by exactly one H⁺.

HA + B ⇌ A⁻ + BH⁺

Here HA and A⁻ are one pair (acid 1, base 1), and BH⁺ and B are the other (acid 2, base 2).

Water can act either way. With an acid it accepts a proton to form H₃O⁺; with a base it donates one to form OH⁻. In equations, H⁺(aq) is shorthand for H₃O⁺(aq).

### Worked example 1: labelling pairs

In a mixture of concentrated nitric and sulfuric acids:

HNO₃ + H₂SO₄ ⇌ H₂NO₃⁺ + HSO₄⁻

- H₂SO₄ loses H⁺ to become HSO₄⁻, so H₂SO₄ is the acid and HSO₄⁻ is its conjugate base.
- HNO₃ gains H⁺ to become H₂NO₃⁺, so here HNO₃ is the base and H₂NO₃⁺ is its conjugate acid.

The point: "acid" and "base" describe what a species does in a particular reaction. Nitric acid acts as a base when it meets a stronger acid.

A second case, with ethylamine in water:

C₂H₅NH₂ + H₂O ⇌ C₂H₅NH₃⁺ + OH⁻

The pairs are C₂H₅NH₃⁺/C₂H₅NH₂ and H₂O/OH⁻. Write each pair with the acid first, and check the two members differ by one H⁺ only.

## 14.3 to 14.5 Defining and using pH

**pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]**, with [H⁺] measured in mol dm⁻³. Rearranged, **[H⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ**.

Because the scale is logarithmic, a change of one pH unit means a tenfold change in [H⁺]. A lower pH means a higher [H⁺].

### Worked example 2: both directions

(a) A sample of rainwater has [H⁺] = 3.6 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³.
pH = −log₁₀(3.6 × 10⁻⁴) = **3.44**

(b) A soil extract has pH 5.36.
[H⁺] = 10⁻⁵·³⁶ = **4.37 × 10⁻⁶ mol dm⁻³**

Give pH to two decimal places unless told otherwise, and give [H⁺] to three significant figures.

## 14.6 and 14.7 Strong acids and their pH

A **strong acid** is (almost) fully dissociated in water: HCl(aq) → H⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq). A **weak acid** is only partly dissociated, so most of it stays as HA molecules at equilibrium: CH₃COOH(aq) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + CH₃COO⁻(aq).

Strength is about the degree of dissociation. Concentration is about how much acid is in each dm³. So you can have a strong acid that is dilute, or a weak acid that is concentrated.

For a strong monoprotic acid, [H⁺] = concentration of the acid.

### Worked example 3: strong acid pH

(a) Nitric acid, 0.0360 mol dm⁻³. [H⁺] = 0.0360 mol dm⁻³, so pH = −log₁₀(0.0360) = **1.44**.

(b) 25.0 cm³ of 0.200 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid is made up to 250 cm³ with water. The new concentration is 0.200 × 25.0/250 = 0.0200 mol dm⁻³, so pH = −log₁₀(0.0200) = **1.70**.

## 14.8 and 14.9 Ka and the pH of a weak acid

For HA(aq) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + A⁻(aq), the **acid dissociation constant** is

Ka = [H⁺][A⁻] / [HA]

All concentrations are at equilibrium; Ka has units of mol dm⁻³. A larger Ka means a stronger (more dissociated) acid.

**pKa = −log₁₀Ka**, so Ka = 10⁻ᵖᴷᵃ. A smaller pKa means a stronger acid.

To find the pH of a weak acid, make two assumptions:

1. **[H⁺] = [A⁻]**: all the H⁺ comes from the acid, and the tiny contribution from water's own ionisation is ignored.
2. **[HA] at equilibrium ≈ the starting concentration**: so little of the acid dissociates that its concentration hardly falls.

Then Ka = [H⁺]² / [HA], which gives **[H⁺] = √(Ka × [HA])**. Under 14.9 the specification notes that quadratic equations will not be expected, so this approximation is the method to use.

### Worked example 4: pH of pentanoic acid

Pentanoic acid has pKa = 4.84 (use this value). Find the pH of a 0.0620 mol dm⁻³ solution.

1. Ka = 10⁻⁴·⁸⁴ = 1.45 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³
2. [H⁺]² = Ka × [HA] = 1.45 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.0620
3. [H⁺] = 9.47 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³
4. pH = −log₁₀(9.47 × 10⁻⁴) = **3.02**

Check assumption 2: 9.47 × 10⁻⁴ / 0.0620 × 100 ≈ 1.5%, so only about 1.5% of the acid has dissociated. The approximation is sound. This percentage is also a direct measure of the **degree of dissociation** from 14.6.

## 14.10 to 14.12 Kw, pKw and strong bases

Water self-ionises to a very small extent. The equilibrium is H₂O(l) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq), and for it the **ionic product of water** is

**Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻]**

Water's concentration is so large that it is effectively constant, so it is not included. At 298 K, Kw = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² dm⁻⁶.

**pKw = −log₁₀Kw**, so pKw = 14.00 at 298 K. Taking logs of Kw gives a handy link: pH + pOH = pKw.

Like any equilibrium constant, Kw changes with temperature (Topic 13). For a value at another temperature, use the one you are given. In pure water [H⁺] = [OH⁻] at every temperature, so pure water is always neutral, even when its pH is not 7.00.

A **strong base** such as NaOH is fully dissociated, so [OH⁻] equals its concentration (twice its concentration for a hydroxide such as Ba(OH)₂). Then [H⁺] = Kw/[OH⁻].

### Worked example 5: barium hydroxide

Find the pH of 0.00650 mol dm⁻³ Ba(OH)₂ at 298 K.

1. Ba(OH)₂ → Ba²⁺ + 2OH⁻, so [OH⁻] = 2 × 0.00650 = 0.0130 mol dm⁻³
2. [H⁺] = Kw/[OH⁻] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ / 0.0130 = 7.69 × 10⁻¹³ mol dm⁻³
3. pH = −log₁₀(7.69 × 10⁻¹³) = **12.11**

Using pKw: pOH = −log₁₀(0.0130) = 1.89, and pH = 14.00 − 1.89 = 12.11. Both routes agree.

## 14.13 Analysing pH data

### (i) Equimolar solutions

The readings below are values you would expect for 0.0700 mol dm⁻³ solutions at 298 K.

| Solution | Type | pH |
|---|---|---|
| Hydrochloric acid | strong acid | 1.15 |
| Ethanoic acid | weak acid | 2.96 |
| Ammonium chloride | salt of strong acid + weak base | 5.20 |
| Sodium chloride | salt of strong acid + strong base | 7.00 |
| Sodium ethanoate | salt of weak acid + strong base | 8.80 |
| Ammonia | weak base | 11.05 |
| Sodium hydroxide | strong base | 12.85 |

How to read the pattern:

- At the same concentration, the strong acid has the lower pH because it is fully dissociated. The weak acid releases far fewer H⁺ ions.
- The same logic applies to bases: NaOH gives a higher pH than NH₃.
- Salts are not all neutral. NH₄⁺ is a weak acid (it can donate H⁺ to water), so ammonium chloride is slightly acidic. CH₃COO⁻ is a weak base (it accepts H⁺ from water, releasing OH⁻), so sodium ethanoate is slightly alkaline. Na⁺ and Cl⁻ do neither, so sodium chloride is neutral.

### (ii) Dilution by 10, 100 and 1000 times

Take 0.400 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid and 0.400 mol dm⁻³ ethanoic acid (pKa 4.76) and dilute each.

| Dilution factor | HCl pH | Ethanoic acid pH |
|---|---|---|
| none | 0.40 | 2.58 |
| 10 | 1.40 | 3.08 |
| 100 | 2.40 | 3.59 |
| 1000 | 3.40 | 4.12 |

- **Strong acid**: [H⁺] falls tenfold with each tenfold dilution, so pH rises by 1.00 each time.
- **Weak acid**: [H⁺] = √(Ka × c), so a tenfold fall in c lowers [H⁺] by only √10. The pH rises by about 0.5 each time. As the acid gets more dilute, a larger fraction dissociates, so the gap between the strong and weak acids narrows.
- At the highest dilution the rise for ethanoic acid is a little more than 0.5, because the fraction dissociated is no longer small and assumption 2 starts to fail.

## 14.14 Ka from a known mass of acid and a pH reading

### Worked example 6: phenylethanoic acid

A 1.088 g sample of phenylethanoic acid, C₆H₅CH₂COOH, is dissolved and the volume brought to 250 cm³ in a volumetric flask. A calibrated pH meter reads 2.90. Calculate Ka.

1. Mr(C₆H₅CH₂COOH) = 136.0, so n = 1.088 / 136.0 = 8.00 × 10⁻³ mol
2. [HA] = 8.00 × 10⁻³ / 0.250 = 0.0320 mol dm⁻³
3. [H⁺] = 10⁻²·⁹⁰ = 1.26 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³, and [A⁻] = [H⁺]
4. Ka = (1.26 × 10⁻³)² / 0.0320 = **4.95 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³** (using the unrounded [H⁺])

The same two assumptions apply. Here [H⁺] is about 4% of [HA], so treating [HA] as 0.0320 is reasonable. You could then quote pKa = −log₁₀(4.95 × 10⁻⁵) = 4.31.

## Common errors

- Writing a conjugate "pair" that differs by more than one H⁺, such as H₂SO₄ and SO₄²⁻.
- Forgetting the minus sign in pH = −log₁₀[H⁺], giving a negative pH for a dilute acid.
- Using the strong-acid method for a weak acid, or the square-root method for a strong acid.
- Forgetting to double [OH⁻] for a hydroxide with two OH⁻ ions per formula.
- Leaving [H₂O] in the Kw expression, or giving Kw the wrong units.
- Using 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ for Kw when the question gives a value at another temperature.
- In 14.14 working, using the mass of acid or the volume in cm³ directly instead of the concentration in mol dm⁻³.
- Stating the weak-acid assumptions vaguely; say which concentration is assumed equal to which.
