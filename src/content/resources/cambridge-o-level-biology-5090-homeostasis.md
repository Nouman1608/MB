This guide covers subtopic **14.4 Homeostasis** of Cambridge O Level Biology 5090, 2026-2028 series. The syllabus asks you to describe homeostasis as the maintenance of a constant internal environment, and to explain the concept of control by negative feedback with reference to a set point. The subtopic is short, but it ties together the next two (temperature control and blood glucose control), so a clear understanding here makes both of them easier.

This page treats homeostasis in more depth, with an everyday analogy and worked examples of how a negative feedback loop is described. ## What homeostasis means

**Homeostasis is the maintenance of a constant internal environment.**

The **internal environment** of a mammal is the blood and the tissue fluid that surrounds the cells. Cells are surrounded by this fluid, and they work best when its conditions stay within narrow limits. For example, enzymes need a suitable temperature, and cells need a steady supply of glucose. Conditions outside the body change all the time: the air gets hotter and colder, and the amount of food eaten varies. If the inside of the body changed in the same way, cells would work badly or be damaged. Homeostasis keeps conditions steady, even though the outside world is not.

Examples of things kept steady in the body:

- the core body temperature
- the blood glucose concentration
- the water content of the blood
- the concentration of carbon dioxide in the blood

The first two are in the syllabus as separate subtopics. The word "constant" does not mean that the value never changes at all. It means that the value stays close to a normal level and is brought back when it moves away.

## Set point

A **set point** is the normal level or target value of a condition, such as a core body temperature of about 37 degrees Celsius. The body works to keep each condition at or near its set point. When the condition moves above or below this value, the body detects the difference and acts to bring it back.

Because the control is never perfect, the actual value does not stay on a perfectly straight line. It goes up and down slightly around the set point, as in this graph description: a line that wiggles gently above and below a horizontal line drawn at the set point. When asked to sketch or describe such a graph, show the wavy line of a controlled variable, not a flat one.

## Negative feedback

**Negative feedback** is a control system in which a change in a condition triggers a response that reverses the change, bringing the condition back to the set point. The word "negative" means that the response goes against the change, which is why the system is stabilising.

Any negative feedback loop has the same parts.

| Part | Role | Example (body temperature) |
|---|---|---|
| Set point | The normal value | About 37 degrees Celsius |
| Receptor | Detects a change from the set point | Temperature receptors in the skin and brain |
| Control centre | Compares the value with the set point and sends instructions | The brain (the hypothalamus) |
| Effector | Carries out the corrective response | Sweat glands, muscles, blood vessels |
| Response | Reverses the change | Cooling or warming |

### The pattern in words

1. The condition **rises above** the set point.
2. Receptors detect the rise.
3. The control centre sends signals to effectors.
4. The effectors produce a response that **lowers** the condition.
5. The condition returns to the set point, and the response switches off.

If the condition **falls below** the set point, the same pattern runs in the opposite direction: the response raises the condition until it returns to normal.

The important feature is that the correction **stops** when the set point is reached. It is not enough to say "the body reacts". You must say that the response reverses the change and returns the condition to the set point.

## An everyday analogy: a thermostat

A room heater with a thermostat is a good model of negative feedback.

- The set point is the temperature the person selects, for example 20 degrees Celsius.
- A sensor detects the room temperature.
- If the room cools below 20 degrees Celsius, the thermostat turns the heater on.
- If the room warms above 20 degrees Celsius, the thermostat turns the heater off.

The temperature of the room swings gently around 20 degrees Celsius. In the body, the sensors and effectors are living tissues, and the control is carried out by the nervous system and by hormones. The analogy is useful for understanding the pattern, but in an exam answer you must use biological terms for the real body.

## Worked example 1: describing negative feedback for temperature

*Describe how negative feedback keeps the core body temperature constant when a person exercises.*

- Exercise makes muscles respire faster, releasing heat, so the core temperature rises above the set point.
- Temperature receptors detect the rise.
- The brain sends impulses to effectors such as the sweat glands and the blood vessels in the skin.
- Sweating increases and the blood vessels widen, so more heat is lost from the body.
- The temperature falls back towards the set point, and the response is reduced.

Each of these five lines can earn a mark. The final line, which says that the response is reduced or stopped, is the one most often missed.

## Worked example 2: negative feedback and blood glucose

*After a meal rich in carbohydrate, the blood glucose concentration rises. Describe the negative feedback that brings it back down.*

- The rise above the set point is detected by the pancreas.
- The pancreas secretes more insulin.
- Insulin causes the liver to take up glucose and store it, so the blood glucose concentration falls.
- When the concentration reaches its normal level, insulin secretion falls.

The detailed roles of insulin and glucagon belong to the blood glucose control subtopic. What matters here is the pattern: change, detection, response and return to the set point.

## Worked example 3: interpreting a graph

*A graph shows the blood glucose concentration of a healthy person over six hours. The set point is 90 mg per 100 cm3. A meal is eaten at 1 hour. The concentration peaks at 130 mg per 100 cm3 at 1.5 hours and returns to 90 at 4 hours.*

- The rise from 90 to 130 is an increase of 40 mg per 100 cm3. As a percentage of the set point this is 40 divided by 90 x 100 = 44.4 per cent.
- The fall back to 90 over 2.5 hours (from 1.5 to 4 hours) shows negative feedback working: the response reverses the change.
- A graph that kept rising, or fell below the set point and kept falling, would show that the feedback had failed.

## Why homeostasis matters

- **Enzymes work best at an optimum temperature and pH.** If body temperature rose or fell by many degrees, enzyme-controlled reactions would slow down or the enzymes would be denatured.
- **Cells need a steady glucose supply for respiration.** Too little starves the brain; too much affects the water balance of cells.
- **Cells need the correct water content.** Too much or too little water causes cells to swell or shrink by osmosis.

A stable internal environment lets mammals stay active in a wide range of external conditions. For example, a mammal can stay active in cold weather because its body temperature is kept steady, whereas a reptile becomes sluggish.

## Common mistakes

- Defining homeostasis as "keeping everything the same". The correct idea is the maintenance of a **constant internal environment**.
- Forgetting the set point. Negative feedback needs a target value.
- Describing a response without saying that it **reverses** the change.
- Calling negative feedback "bad" because of the word negative. It is a stabilising system.
- Writing that the response keeps going once the set point is reached. It stops or reduces.
- Confusing the internal environment with the surroundings. The internal environment is the blood and tissue fluid.
- Writing that homeostasis keeps a variable absolutely fixed. It keeps it within narrow limits.

## Command words

- **Describe**: state what homeostasis is, or give the stages of a feedback loop.
- **Explain**: give the reasons for the stages, such as why the response must reverse the change.
- **Outline**: give the main points briefly.
- **Suggest**: apply the idea of negative feedback to a new example.
