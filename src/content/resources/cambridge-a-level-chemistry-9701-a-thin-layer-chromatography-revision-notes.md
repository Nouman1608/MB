**Syllabus:** Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027, **A Level** subtopic
37.1: the terms stationary phase, mobile phase, Rf value, solvent front and baseline; interpreting Rf
values; and explaining them "in terms of interaction with the stationary phase and of relative
solubility in the mobile phase".

## The terms

| Term | Meaning in TLC |
|---|---|
| stationary phase | solid layer that stays fixed, e.g. **aluminium oxide on a solid support** (glass, plastic or metal sheet); polar surface |
| mobile phase | the **solvent**, polar or non-polar, rising by capillary action |
| baseline | line where samples are spotted, above the solvent level |
| solvent front | furthest point reached by the solvent; mark it at once |
| Rf value | distance moved by spot ÷ distance moved by solvent front, both from the baseline |

## Rf: the calculation

    Rf = distance from baseline to centre of spot ÷ distance from baseline to solvent front

- No units; always between 0 and 1.
- Depends on the stationary phase, mobile phase and temperature: compare only under the **same
  conditions** (ideally on the same plate).
- Rearranged: distance moved by spot = Rf × distance moved by solvent front.

## Interpreting a plate

- **Same Rf as a reference on the same plate** → probably the same compound.
- **Matching in two different solvents** → much stronger evidence.
- **Number of spots** = **minimum** number of components.
- **One spot** for a pure substance; extra spots are impurities.
- A spot **on the baseline** (Rf ≈ 0): held very strongly, or insoluble in the solvent.
- A spot **at the solvent front** (Rf ≈ 1): hardly adsorbed, very soluble in the solvent.

## Explaining Rf differences

Two competing effects:

| Effect | Stronger → |
|---|---|
| interaction (adsorption) with the stationary phase | **lower** Rf |
| solubility in the mobile phase | **higher** Rf |

On **polar aluminium oxide** with a **non-polar** solvent:

- compounds that can **hydrogen-bond** to the surface (–COOH, –OH, –NH₂) → **low** Rf;
- compounds with only weaker **permanent dipoles** (C=O in esters, ketones) → middle Rf;
- **non-polar** compounds (hydrocarbons) → **high** Rf.

**More polar solvent:** polar compounds dissolve better → their Rf values **increase**; separation may
get worse if everything moves near the front.

## Worked mini-example

Solvent front 6.0 cm. Spot X 1.5 cm, spot Y 4.2 cm.

- Rf(X) = 1.5 / 6.0 = 0.25; Rf(Y) = 4.2 / 6.0 = 0.70.
- On aluminium oxide with a non-polar solvent, X is the more polar compound (held back more; less
  soluble in the solvent).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| adsorption | attraction of a substance to the surface of the stationary phase |
| capillary action | movement of the solvent up the thin layer |
| polar | having a permanent dipole, from bonds between atoms of different electronegativity |
| reference sample | pure known compound run alongside the unknown |
| co-spotting | running reference and unknown side by side on the same plate |
| component | one compound in a mixture |

## Exam traps

- Measure from the **baseline**, not the bottom of the plate.
- Rf **> 1** means you divided the wrong way round.
- **No units** for Rf.
- One spot does **not** prove purity beyond doubt: two compounds might share an Rf.
- Give **both** factors when explaining an Rf difference.
- Use "**probably**" when identifying from one Rf match.
- Polar does not always mean low Rf: a **polar** mobile phase can carry polar compounds further.
- Give Rf to the same number of significant figures as the distances you were given (usually two).
