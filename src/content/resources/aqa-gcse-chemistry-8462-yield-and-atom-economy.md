This guide covers **4.3.3 Yield and atom economy of chemical reactions (chemistry only)** of the AQA GCSE
Chemistry (8462) specification, for teaching from September 2016 onwards (version 1.2). It is assessed on
Paper 1. The "chemistry only" label separates this content from combined science; in this separate Chemistry
qualification it is examined in full. Two skills are marked **(HT only)**: calculating the theoretical mass of
a product from a given mass of reactant and the balanced equation, and explaining why a particular reaction
pathway is chosen. Everything else is for both tiers. There is no required practical.

For quick recall use the [yield and atom economy revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-yield-and-atom-economy-revision-notes/),
and test yourself with the [practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-yield-and-atom-economy-practice/).

## What the specification asks for

| Skill | Tier |
|---|---|
| Explain why the actual amount of product can be less than the calculated amount (reversible reaction, loss on separation, side reactions) | Both |
| Calculate percentage yield from the actual yield | Both |
| Calculate the theoretical mass of a product from a given mass of reactant and the balanced equation | Higher only |
| Calculate the atom economy of a reaction to form a desired product from the balanced equation | Both |
| Explain why a particular reaction pathway is chosen using data such as atom economy, yield, rate, equilibrium position and usefulness of by-products | Higher only |

## Why the yield is less than the calculated amount

No atoms are gained or lost in a reaction, but you cannot always obtain the calculated amount of product,
because:

- the reaction may not go to completion because it is **reversible**;
- some product may be **lost when it is separated** from the reaction mixture (for example left on filter
  paper or in the apparatus);
- some of the reactants may react in **ways different from the expected reaction**, forming other products.

## Percentage yield

The amount of product obtained is the **yield**. The **maximum theoretical mass** is the mass calculated from the
equation if all the reactant became product. Compared as a percentage, the yield becomes the **percentage
yield**.

```
percentage yield = (mass of product actually made / maximum theoretical mass of product) x 100
```

### Worked example 1: percentage yield (both tiers)

*The maximum theoretical mass of calcium oxide from heating a sample of calcium carbonate is 28.0 g. A student
obtains 22.4 g. Calculate the percentage yield.*

```
percentage yield = 22.4 / 28.0 x 100 = 80%
```

### Rearranging

If you know the percentage yield and the theoretical mass, then the actual mass = percentage yield ÷ 100 ×
theoretical mass. A reaction with a theoretical mass of 30.0 g and a yield of 82% gives 0.82 × 30.0 = 24.6 g.

### Calculating the theoretical mass (HT only)

Use the mole method from the previous subsection: moles of the given reactant, then the equation ratio, then the
mass of product.

### Worked example 2: theoretical mass then yield (HT only)

*50.0 g of calcium carbonate is heated: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂. The student obtains 22.4 g of calcium oxide.
Calculate the maximum theoretical mass of calcium oxide and the percentage yield.*

```
Mr CaCO3 = 100;  moles = 50.0 / 100 = 0.500 mol
Ratio 1 : 1, so moles CaO = 0.500 mol
Mr CaO = 56;  theoretical mass = 0.500 x 56 = 28.0 g
percentage yield = 22.4 / 28.0 x 100 = 80%
```

## Atom economy

The **atom economy** (atom utilisation) is a measure of the amount of starting materials that end up as **useful
products**. High atom economy is important for **sustainable development** (it makes better use of resources and
creates less waste) and for **economic reasons**.

```
atom economy (%) = (Mr of desired product from the equation / sum of the Mr of all reactants from the equation) x 100
```

Use the balanced equation, and multiply each Mr by its balancing number.

### Worked example 3: iron extraction

*Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂. The desired product is iron. Calculate the atom economy.*

```
Mr Fe2O3 = 160;  Mr CO = 28;  3CO = 84;  total reactants = 244
Desired product: 2Fe = 2 x 56 = 112
Atom economy = 112 / 244 x 100 = 45.9% (3 s.f.)
```

### Worked example 4: heating calcium carbonate

*CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂. The desired product is calcium oxide.*

```
Mr CaCO3 = 100; desired CaO = 56
Atom economy = 56 / 100 x 100 = 56%
```

### Worked example 5: an addition reaction

*C₂H₄ + H₂ → C₂H₆. The desired product is ethane.*

```
Mr reactants = 28 + 2 = 30;  Mr ethane = 30
Atom economy = 30 / 30 x 100 = 100%
```

All the atoms in the reactants end up in the single product, so the atom economy is 100%. A reaction with only
one product always has an atom economy of 100%.

### Worked example 6: choice of desired product

*Mg + CuO → MgO + Cu. Calculate the atom economy for making copper, then for making magnesium oxide.*

```
Mr reactants = 24 + 79.5 = 103.5
Copper:            63.5 / 103.5 x 100 = 61.4%
Magnesium oxide:   40 / 103.5 x 100 = 38.6%
```

The atom economy depends on **which product is wanted**. The two values add to 100%.

## Choosing a reaction pathway (HT only)

A chemical company may have more than one route to the same product. To choose between them, the specification
suggests using data such as **atom economy**, **yield**, **rate**, **equilibrium position** and the
**usefulness of by-products**.

| Factor | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Atom economy | High atom economy means less waste |
| Yield | High yield means more product from the same raw materials |
| Rate | A faster reaction produces more per hour |
| Equilibrium position | If it lies towards the products, more product forms in a reversible reaction |
| By-products | If they are useful and can be sold, a low atom economy matters less |

### Worked example 7: comparing two routes (HT only)

*Route A has an atom economy of 100%, a yield of 60% and a slow rate. Route B has an atom economy of 46%, a yield
of 90% and a fast rate, and its by-product can be sold. Which would you choose?*

Route B might be chosen: the high yield and fast rate give more product per hour, and because the by-product is
sold, the low atom economy is less of a problem. Route A makes less waste, but the low yield and slow rate may
make it uneconomic. A good answer weighs all the factors in the data and reaches a reasoned decision.

## Yield and atom economy compared

| | Percentage yield | Atom economy |
|---|---|---|
| Based on | An experiment: actual mass made | The balanced equation only |
| Measures | How much product you got compared with the maximum | How much of the reactant mass ends up in the desired product |
| Affected by | Losses, side reactions, reversibility | Which products are made and what the by-products are |
| Can be 100%? | Only if nothing is lost | Only if there is a single product |

A reaction can have a high atom economy but a low yield (if much product is lost or the reaction is reversible),
or a high yield but a low atom economy (if a large amount of by-product is made).

### Worked example 8: a salt preparation

*A student reacts copper(II) oxide with sulfuric acid, filters off the excess solid and crystallises the
copper(II) sulfate. The maximum theoretical mass of crystals is 12.5 g. The student obtains 9.0 g of crystals. Calculate the
percentage yield and give two reasons why it is below 100%.*

```
percentage yield = 9.0 / 12.5 x 100 = 72%
```

Reasons: some solution stayed on the filter paper or in the beaker (loss on separation); some crystals were
lost when they were transferred or dried; or some reactant may have formed other products.

## Common mistakes

- Using the mass of reactants given in the question instead of the Mr values in the equation for atom economy.
- Forgetting the balancing numbers when summing the Mr of the reactants.
- Calculating atom economy for the wrong product.
- Dividing the theoretical mass by the actual mass in the percentage yield.
- Stating that yield and atom economy are the same thing. Yield measures the actual experiment. Atom economy is
  calculated from the equation.
- Giving percentage yields above 100% without checking the working.

## Exam technique

- **Percentage yield:** write the formula, substitute, then give the answer with %.
- **Atom economy:** list Mr values first. Check the desired product's mass appears on top.
- **Theoretical mass (HT only):** mass → moles → ratio → mass.
- **Pathway questions (HT only):** compare each data item for both routes and finish with a decision.
