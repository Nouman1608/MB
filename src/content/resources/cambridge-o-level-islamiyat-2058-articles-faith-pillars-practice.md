**How to use this set.** In Paper 2, one or two of Questions 3–5 are set on this topic, each worth
14 marks: a knowledge part (a) worth 10 and an understanding part (b) worth 4. Section A checks the
basic facts; Section B gives full exam-style questions on beliefs and practices.

## Section A — Short questions

**1.** Name the six Articles of Faith. **[6]**

**2.** Name **four** angels and state the duty of each. **[4]**

**3.** Name **four** books revealed by God and the prophet to whom each was given. **[4]**

**4.** State **three** conditions that must be met before zakat is due on a Muslim's savings. **[3]**

**5.** Explain the difference between the greater and the lesser jihad. **[3]**

**6.** What should a Muslim do about a prayer missed for a valid reason, and how may prayer be adjusted
when travelling? **[3]**

## Section B — Exam-style questions

**7. (a)** Write about Muslim belief in angels, their nature and their duties. **[10]**
**(b)** Explain how belief in recording angels can affect the way a Muslim behaves. **[4]**

**8. (a)** Describe what Muslims believe about the character and function of the prophets. **[10]**
**(b)** Why must Muslims believe in all the prophets, not only the Prophet Muhammad (pbuh)? **[4]**

**9. (a)** Describe the main events of the Resurrection and the Last Day as Muslims believe them.
**[10]**
**(b)** Explain the significance of belief in the Last Day for a Muslim's life. **[4]**

**10. (a)** Write about the shahada and the significance of what it contains. **[10]**
**(b)** Is saying the shahada enough to make someone a good Muslim? Give reasons. **[4]**

**11. (a)** Describe the Friday congregational prayer and the prayers on the two festivals. **[10]**
**(b)** Explain the benefits of congregational prayer for the Muslim community. **[4]**

**12. (a)** Describe how zakat is calculated and paid, and who may receive it. **[10]**
**(b)** Explain the significance of zakat in the life of the Muslim community. **[4]**

**13. (a)** Describe the main observances of the hajj. **[10]**
**(b)** Explain how the hajj links Muslims with the Prophet Ibrahim and his family. **[4]**

## Answers

**How Section B is marked.** Levels of response: part (a) Level 4 = 8–10, Level 3 = 5–7,
Level 2 = 3–4, Level 1 = 1–2; part (b) Level 2 = 3–4, Level 1 = 1–2. Each developed point below
earns [2]; credit the best five in (a) (maximum 10) and the best two in (b) (maximum 4) unless
stated. Other accurate points of the same quality earn the same credit.

**1.** God [1]; angels [1]; revealed books [1]; prophets [1]; God's predestination and decree
(al-qadr) [1]; the Resurrection and the Last Day [1].

**2.** Any four, [1] each: Jibril brings revelation to the prophets; Mika'il is responsible for rain
and provision; Israfil will blow the trumpet at the end of time; the Angel of Death takes souls (Sura
32.11); Munkar and Nakir question the dead in the grave; the recording angels write down every deed;
Malik guards Hell; Ridwan guards Paradise. Maximum 4.

**3.** Any four, [1] each: the Tawrat (Torah) to Musa; the Zabur (Psalms) to Dawud; the Injil (Gospel)
to Isa; the Qur'an to Muhammad (pbuh); the scrolls (suhuf) to Ibrahim. Maximum 4.

**4.** Any three, [1] each: the owner is a Muslim and an adult of sound mind; the wealth is above the
minimum amount (nisab); it has been held for a full lunar year; it is owned outright and not needed
for basic needs or debts. Maximum 3.

**5.** The greater jihad is the inner struggle against one's own desires and temptations to obey God
[1]. The lesser jihad is the outward struggle to defend Islam and the community, including armed
defence under strict conditions [1]. Jihad also includes striving through speech, writing and
effort for good, such as speaking up for justice [1].

**6.** A prayer missed through sleep or forgetfulness is made up as soon as it is remembered (qada)
[1]. When travelling, the four-unit prayers may be shortened to two units (qasr) [1], and some prayers
may be combined, such as zuhr with asr [1].

**7. (a)** Credit the best five, [2] each:
- Belief in angels is an Article of Faith; the Qur'an says the believers believe "in Allah, His
  angels, His books and His messengers" (Sura 2.285) [2].
- Angels are created from light, are unseen and do not eat, sleep or marry [2].
- They have no free will and always obey God; they "do precisely what they are commanded" (Sura
  66.6) [2].
- Jibril brought revelation to the prophets, including the Qur'an to the Prophet (pbuh) [2].
- Mika'il is responsible for rain and provision; Israfil will blow the trumpet on the Last Day [2].
- The Angel of Death takes souls at the appointed time, and Munkar and Nakir question each person in
  the grave [2].
- Two recording angels, the "honourable writers" (Sura 82.10–12), record every good and bad deed [2].
- Angels praise God constantly and ask forgiveness for people on earth (Sura 42.5) [2].
**(b)** Credit the best two, [2] each:
- A Muslim knows every word and action is recorded, so avoids lying, cheating or backbiting even when
  no person is watching [2].
- Good deeds are also recorded, which encourages small kindnesses and regular worship that others may
  never see [2].
- Knowing the record will be read on the Day of Judgement makes Muslims repent quickly after doing
  wrong [2].

**8. (a)** Credit the best five, [2] each:
- God sent prophets to every people to guide them to worship Him alone (Sura 16.36) [2].
- A nabi is a prophet; a rasul is a messenger given a law or book; every messenger is a prophet but
  not every prophet a messenger [2].
- Twenty-five prophets are named in the Qur'an, including Adam, Nuh, Ibrahim, Musa, Isa and Muhammad
  (pbuh) [2].
- Prophets are human beings chosen by God, of the best character, and protected from major sin [2].
- They brought the same message of tawhid, taught God's law and were living examples of it [2].
- Some were given miracles by God's permission to prove their message, such as Musa's staff and Isa's
  healing (Sura 5.110); the Qur'an is the Prophet Muhammad's (pbuh) miracle [2].
- Muhammad (pbuh) is the Seal of the Prophets (Sura 33.40), and his message is for all humanity [2].
**(b)** Credit the best two, [2] each:
- The Qur'an commands it: believers "make no distinction between one and another of His messengers"
  (Sura 2.285) [2].
- All prophets brought the same message from the same God, so rejecting one means rejecting God's
  guidance [2].
- It gives Muslims respect for the prophets honoured by Jews and Christians, which supports peaceful
  relations [2].

**9. (a)** Credit the best five, [2] each:
- After death the soul enters barzakh, the period between death and resurrection, and is questioned in
  the grave by Munkar and Nakir [2].
- Signs will come before the end; only God knows when it will happen [2].
- Israfil will blow the trumpet: everything will perish; at a second blast all people will be raised
  (Sura 39.68) [2].
- People will be gathered on a vast plain, and the earth "will declare her tidings" (Sura 99.4) [2].
- Each person will receive the record of deeds in the right hand (success) or the left (failure)
  (Sura 69.19–25) [2].
- Deeds will be weighed on the scales (mizan) with complete justice (Sura 21.47) [2].
- People will cross the bridge (sirat) over Hell; the Prophet (pbuh) will intercede for believers, and
  people will enter Paradise or Hell [2].
**(b)** Credit the best two, [2] each:
- Belief that every deed will be judged makes Muslims responsible for their actions and careful about
  others' rights [2].
- It gives meaning to suffering and patience, since injustice in this world will be put right [2].
- It encourages Muslims to prepare for the Hereafter through worship and good deeds rather than living
  only for this world [2].

**10. (a)** Credit the best five, [2] each:
- The shahada is the first Pillar: "There is no god but God, and Muhammad is the Messenger of God"
  [2].
- Saying it sincerely with belief makes a person a Muslim [2].
- The first part declares tawhid, rejecting all false gods and shirk, as in Sura 112 [2].
- The second part accepts Muhammad (pbuh) as God's messenger, so his Sunna must be followed [2].
- It is repeated in the call to prayer and in every prayer in the tashahhud [2].
- It is whispered into a newborn baby's ear with the call to prayer and Muslims hope to say it as
  their last words [2].
**(b)** A clear judgement [1] supported by reasons [3]. For example: no [1]. The shahada is a promise
that must be lived out in the other Pillars, such as prayer and zakat [1]; the Hadith of the man who
asked about paradise links faith to obligatory acts [1]; and the Qur'an repeatedly joins belief with
"righteous deeds" [1]. An answer arguing that it is enough to enter Islam, while good conduct shows its
sincerity, earns the same credit when equally reasoned.

**11. (a)** Credit the best five, [2] each:
- The Qur'an commands Muslims to hasten to prayer when called on Friday and to leave business (Sura
  62.9) [2].
- It is obligatory for adult Muslim men who are not ill or travelling; women may attend [2].
- It replaces zuhr and is held at midday in a mosque, with the call to prayer [2].
- The imam gives a sermon (khutba) in two parts before the prayer, and worshippers listen in silence
  [2].
- It consists of two units of prayer led by the imam [2].
- Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha prayers are two units with extra takbirs, held in the morning, often in
  open grounds, without a call to prayer [2].
- At Eid the sermon comes after the prayer; Muslims wear their best clothes and greet each other [2].
**(b)** Credit the best two, [2] each:
- Rich and poor stand shoulder to shoulder in rows, showing equality before God [2].
- The khutba teaches the community and addresses local issues each week [2].
- Meeting regularly builds brotherhood, and members learn of neighbours who are ill or in need [2].

**12. (a)** Credit the best five, [2] each:
- Zakat is the third Pillar, commanded with prayer throughout the Qur'an [2].
- It is due on wealth above the nisab held for a lunar year, at 2.5 per cent on savings, gold, silver
  and trade goods [2].
- Crops have different rates: a tenth on land watered by rain and a twentieth on land irrigated by
  effort [2].
- Livestock above set numbers are also liable [2].
- It is paid with the intention of worship, and can be given to the state or a trusted organisation or
  directly to recipients [2].
- Sura 9.60 lists those who may receive it: the poor, the needy, the collectors, those whose hearts are
  to be reconciled, freeing captives, debtors, in the way of God, and travellers [2].
**(b)** Credit the best two, [2] each:
- It reduces poverty by moving wealth from rich to poor, so no one in the community is left without
  basic needs [2].
- It purifies wealth and the giver's heart from greed, so it strengthens bonds rather than envy [2].
- It funds the needs of the community, such as helping debtors and travellers [2].

**13. (a)** Credit the best five, [2] each:
- Pilgrims enter ihram at the miqat, men in two white cloths, and recite the talbiya [2].
- On arrival they perform tawaf, seven circuits of the Ka'ba, and sa'i, seven times between Safa and
  Marwa [2].
- On the 8th of Dhu al-Hijja they go to Mina, and on the 9th stand at Arafat in prayer until sunset,
  the essential act of hajj [2].
- They spend the night at Muzdalifa and collect pebbles [2].
- On the 10th they stone Jamrat al-Aqaba, sacrifice an animal, and shave or cut their hair [2].
- They return to Makka for tawaf al-ifada, then stay in Mina for the following days stoning the three
  pillars, and finish with a farewell tawaf [2].
**(b)** Credit the best two, [2] each:
- Sa'i recalls Hajar running between Safa and Marwa searching for water for Isma'il, and Zamzam
  recalls the spring that God provided [2].
- The stoning recalls Ibrahim rejecting Satan's temptation, and the sacrifice recalls his readiness to
  sacrifice his son, so pilgrims commit to the same obedience [2].
- The Ka'ba was rebuilt by Ibrahim and Isma'il (Sura 2.127), so tawaf joins pilgrims to their worship
  [2].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Listing beliefs or rituals without explaining them.
- Writing about fasting or prayer in general when the question names Friday or festival prayers.
- In part (b), describing the practice again instead of explaining its effect on the individual or
  community.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should I include Shi'a practice?**
Where it differs and is relevant, yes; for example, Shi'a Muslims pay khums as well as zakat. Keep the
focus on what the question asks.

**How many Qur'an references should a 10-mark answer have?**
There is no set number, but the top-level descriptor says answers are likely to quote Qur'an verses
and Hadiths, so two or three accurate references help.
