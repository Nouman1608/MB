## Core in three lines

1. **Transpiration** = the loss of water vapour from leaves.
2. Water **evaporates** from mesophyll cell surfaces into the **air spaces**, then **diffuses** out through the **stomata** as water vapour.
3. Higher **temperature** → faster transpiration; higher **wind speed** → faster transpiration.

## Key terms

| Term | Recall |
|---|---|
| Transpiration | loss of water vapour from leaves |
| Evaporation | liquid water turning to vapour at the mesophyll cell surfaces |
| Air spaces | interconnecting gaps between mesophyll cells where vapour collects |
| Stomata | pores, mostly in the lower epidermis, through which vapour diffuses out |
| Potometer | apparatus that measures water uptake by a shoot, used to estimate transpiration rate |
| Humidity (Extended only) | amount of water vapour in the air |
| Transpiration pull (Extended only) | pull on the water in the xylem caused by water loss from leaves |
| Flaccid (Extended only) | describes a cell that has lost water and no longer presses on its wall |

## Potometer: method in steps

1. Cut the shoot under water; fit it to the potometer under water.
2. Seal the joint with petroleum jelly (airtight).
3. Let the shoot settle in the test conditions.
4. Record bubble movement in a set time; reset with the reservoir; repeat; mean.
5. Rate = distance ÷ time (for example mm per min).

- Measures **uptake**, which is close to, but slightly more than, water lost.
- Vary wind with a fan at different distances or settings; vary temperature with controlled rooms.
- Keep constant: same shoot, same humidity, same light, same time.
- Errors: air leaks at joints; air bubbles in the xylem.

## Mass-loss method

- Weigh a potted plant (pot sealed in a bag) or detached leaves at intervals.
- Compare by **percentage** loss if start masses differ: change ÷ start × 100.

## Stomata and air spaces (Extended only)

- Large internal surface area of air spaces → more evaporation → more vapour to diffuse out.
- More or larger stomata → more area for diffusion → more vapour lost.
- Petroleum jelly on the **lower** surface cuts loss most: most stomata are there.

## Transpiration pull (Extended only)

```
vapour diffuses out of stomata → water leaves xylem into mesophyll
→ pull on water in xylem → column held together by attraction between water molecules
→ continuous column drawn up from the roots
```

The pull is from the top (leaves), not a push from the roots.

## Factors explained (Extended only)

| Factor up | Rate | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | up | more kinetic energy → faster evaporation and diffusion |
| Wind speed | up | vapour removed from leaf surface → steep concentration gradient |
| Humidity | down | more vapour outside → shallow concentration gradient |

## Wilting (Extended only)

- **Why:** water lost by transpiration faster than it is taken up (hot, dry, windy; dry soil).
- **How:** cells lose water → become flaccid, not turgid → no longer support leaves and soft stems → plant droops.
- Recovery in the evening: transpiration slows, uptake catches up, cells become turgid.

## Answer patterns

- **"Describe the effect of temperature"** (Core): trend + values from the data.
- **"Explain the effect of humidity"** (Extended): humidity → gradient → diffusion → rate.
- **"Explain how water moves up the xylem"** (Extended): evaporation, pull, column, attraction between water molecules.
- **"Explain how and why wilting occurs"** (Extended): the why (loss > uptake) and the how (flaccid cells) are separate marks.

## Exam traps

- Evaporation happens inside the leaf; diffusion takes the vapour out.
- A potometer measures uptake, not loss.
- Use percentage loss when start masses differ.
- (Extended) High humidity slows transpiration.
- (Extended) Wind keeps the gradient steep; it does not "blow water off".
- (Extended) Wilting needs flaccid or loss of turgor, not just "lack of water".

## Self-test

1. Define transpiration.
2. Where does water evaporate inside a leaf?
3. Through which structures does water vapour leave the leaf?
4. Describe the effect of increasing wind speed on transpiration rate.
5. Why must the shoot be cut under water when setting up a potometer?
6. A bubble moves 36 mm in 4 minutes. Calculate the rate.
7. Why is percentage loss used to compare leaves of different masses?
8. (Extended) Explain why covering the lower surface of a leaf with petroleum jelly reduces water loss more than covering the upper surface.
9. (Extended) Explain why transpiration is slower when the air is humid.
10. (Extended) What holds the column of water in the xylem together?
11. (Extended) Explain why a plant wilts on a hot, windy day.
12. (Extended) Explain how a high temperature increases the rate of transpiration.

### Answers

1. The loss of water vapour from leaves.
2. From the surfaces of the mesophyll cells, into the air spaces.
3. The stomata.
4. As wind speed increases, the rate of transpiration increases.
5. To stop air entering the xylem and blocking the flow of water.
6. 36 ÷ 4 = **9 mm per min**.
7. It allows a fair comparison, because the leaves started at different masses.
8. Most stomata are in the lower epidermis, so covering it blocks most of the routes for water vapour to diffuse out.
9. There is more water vapour in the outside air, so the concentration gradient from the air spaces to the air is less steep and diffusion is slower.
10. Forces of attraction between water molecules.
11. Water is lost by transpiration faster than it is taken up; cells become flaccid and the leaves lose support and droop.
12. Water molecules gain kinetic energy, so water evaporates from mesophyll surfaces and diffuses out faster.
