This guide goes deeper into sub-topic 3.1 of Option C, Extreme environments, in IB Diploma Programme Geography (first assessments 2019): the characteristics of extreme environments. The [3.1 revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-geography-extreme-environments-characteristics-of-extreme-environments-revision-notes/) already define the three broad groups (hot arid, cold, high altitude), explain the climate, soils and plant adaptations, give the aridity index and lapse rate, and explain the global distribution of deserts and mountains. This page develops the human side of the sub-topic: what makes these places **inaccessible**, where the human body reaches its limits, and who actually lives in extreme environments and why. It finishes with a worked example of classifying an environment from climate data. Practise with the [3.1 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-dp-geography-ib-dp-geography-3-1-practice/); the [Extreme environments study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-extreme-environments-study-guide/) gives the overview.

## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Describe and explain the distribution of extreme environments.
- Explain the physical characteristics that make environments extreme.
- Explain why extreme environments are relatively inaccessible and challenging for people.
- Describe the characteristics of the people who live and work there.

## Why extreme environments are inaccessible

Inaccessibility has several dimensions, and a strong answer separates them.

| Dimension | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Distance | Far from cities, ports and markets | Settlements in the interior of the Sahara or the Canadian Arctic |
| Terrain | Steep slopes, dunes, ice sheets or bogs make building and maintaining roads difficult | Himalayan valleys cut off by landslides; tundra that becomes waterlogged in summer |
| Seasonality | Access changes through the year | Winter ice roads in northern Canada that can only be used once lakes and ground freeze hard |
| Weather | Storms, whiteouts, sandstorms and darkness interrupt travel | Flights to Antarctic stations limited by polar winter |
| Cost | Every item brought in costs more because of fuel and transport | Food and fuel prices far above national averages in remote Arctic communities |

Seasonal access shows how climate change can make places **more** inaccessible, not less. The **Tibbitt to Contwoyto Winter Road** in Canada's Northwest Territories, which supplies remote diamond mines across frozen lakes, can only operate for a few weeks each winter; warmer winters shorten that window. In contrast, shrinking sea ice may open Arctic coasts to shipping for longer. Inaccessibility is therefore dynamic.

## The limits of the human body

Extreme environments are extreme partly because they push the body beyond its normal tolerance.

**Altitude and oxygen.** Air pressure falls with height, so each breath contains fewer oxygen molecules, even though oxygen still makes up the same share of the air. A useful approximation is that pressure falls by a factor of about e (roughly 2.7) for every 8 km of height.

```
Fraction of sea-level pressure = e to the power (-height / 8,000 m)

At 5,500 m:  e to the power (-0.69) = 0.50, about half
At 8,849 m (summit of Everest):  e to the power (-1.11) = 0.33, about a third
```

At around 5,500 m, each breath holds about half the oxygen it would at sea level. This explains altitude sickness, reduced capacity for physical work and why few permanent settlements lie above about 5,000 m. Communities that have lived at altitude for many generations, on the Tibetan Plateau and in the Andes, show physiological adaptations to low oxygen, and those adaptations differ between the two populations.

**Heat.** The body cools mainly by sweating, which only works if sweat can evaporate. In hot, **humid** conditions evaporation slows, so humidity matters as much as temperature. Scientists combine the two as the **wet-bulb temperature**. A wet-bulb temperature of about 35 degrees Celsius is often quoted as a theoretical upper limit for survival during prolonged exposure, and laboratory studies suggest that real limits for many people are lower. Hot deserts are usually dry, so sweating works, but water loss is rapid and dehydration becomes the main danger.

**Cold.** Low temperatures combined with wind cause rapid heat loss (wind chill), frostbite and hypothermia. Darkness during polar winter adds psychological stress for incoming workers.

## Who lives in extreme environments

Population is sparse, but not absent, and its characteristics are distinctive.

- **Low density, clustered pattern.** People concentrate where a key resource is available: oases and river valleys in deserts, sheltered valleys and terraces in mountains, coasts in the Arctic.
- **Indigenous communities** with long-established ways of living, such as the Inuit in the Arctic, the Sami in northern Scandinavia, the Tuareg in the Sahara and Quechua-speaking communities in the Andes.
- **Temporary and seasonal workers.** Mines, oil and gas fields and research stations rely on people who stay for weeks or months. In **fly-in fly-out** working, staff live in camps and return home between shifts, so the resident population may be small even where economic activity is large.
- **Distinctive structures.** Mining towns often have more working-age men than women or children. Remote communities can lose young adults to cities, leaving an older population.
- **Research populations** such as those in Antarctica, where there are no permanent inhabitants; numbers rise in the southern summer and fall sharply in winter.

## Worked example: classifying an environment from data

The table gives invented climate data for two stations. Potential evapotranspiration (PET) is given as an annual total.

| | Station 1 | Station 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Warmest month mean | 34 degrees Celsius | 9 degrees Celsius |
| Coldest month mean | 14 degrees Celsius | -26 degrees Celsius |
| Months with mean below 0 degrees Celsius | 0 | 8 |
| Annual precipitation | 60 mm | 210 mm |
| Annual PET | 2,400 mm | 300 mm |

```
Station 1: range = 34 - 14 = 20 degrees; aridity index = 60 / 2,400 = 0.025
Station 2: range = 9 - (-26) = 35 degrees; aridity index = 210 / 300 = 0.70
```

Station 1 is **hot arid**: an aridity index of 0.025 is hyper-arid on the commonly used UNEP scale (below 0.05), and temperatures stay well above freezing. Station 2 is **cold**: eight months below freezing and a warmest month below 10 degrees Celsius point to a tundra climate with permafrost likely. Its aridity index of 0.70 is not arid, even though precipitation is low, because cold air means little evaporation. This shows why low precipitation alone does not define a desert, and why the aridity index compares supply with demand.

## Annotated model answer

*Explain why extreme environments are relatively inaccessible.* **(sample paragraph)**

"Distance from markets raises the cost of every journey, but terrain is often the larger barrier: steep, unstable mountain slopes and shifting dunes make roads costly to build and maintain **[two dimensions separated]**. Access is also seasonal; in northern Canada, ice roads to remote mines operate only while lakes are frozen, so supplies must be moved in a few weeks **[located example of seasonality]**. Weather such as whiteouts or sandstorms interrupts even established routes **[further dimension]**. Inaccessibility therefore results from the interaction of distance, terrain and climate, and it can change as the climate warms **[synthesis and dynamic view]**."

## Common mistakes

- Treating inaccessibility as distance only.
- Saying there is "less oxygen" at altitude without explaining that the share is constant but pressure falls.
- Ignoring humidity when explaining heat stress.
- Describing extreme environments as empty of people, or populated only by indigenous groups.
- Classifying a station as a desert from low precipitation alone, without comparing it with evapotranspiration.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a distribution: name the pattern (latitude bands, mountain belts), give examples and note exceptions.
- **Explain** a characteristic with a cause and a consequence for people.
- **Distinguish** between environments by comparing the main limiting factor in each.
- When given climate data, calculate the range and an index, then justify the classification with the figures.
