These questions cover research methods content (3.1.3.1 to 3.1.3.4) as assessed in **Unit 1
Section B** of OxfordAQA International AS/A level Sociology (9690), which the specification
describes as short questions and a structured question based on data about an unseen issue. The
tariffs here are Marlbridge practice tariffs, not the board's. All places, data and people in the
items are fictional and were written for this page.

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define what is meant by a **sampling frame**. **[2]**

**2.** Define what is meant by **social desirability** in research. **[2]**

**3.** Outline **one** difference between a laboratory experiment and a field experiment. **[2]**

**4.** Identify and briefly explain **two** types of question used in surveys. **[4]**

**5.** Identify and briefly explain **two** problems with snowball sampling. **[4]**

**6.** Identify and briefly explain **two** ethical problems with covert participant observation.
**[4]**

**7.** Explain the difference between correlation and causation, using an example. **[4]**

### Section B: structured and longer questions

**8.** Study Item A.

> **Item A (fictional data).** An online survey asked 800 employed couples in the fictional country
> of Kelmar how many hours a week each partner spent on unpaid domestic work. The same questions
> were asked of a new sample of 800 couples twenty years later.
>
> | Average hours per week of unpaid domestic work | 2005 | 2025 |
> |---|---|---|
> | Women | 24 | 19 |
> | Men | 6 | 9 |

**(a)** Using Item A, identify the change in women's hours and the change in men's hours between 2005
and 2025. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the gap between women's and men's hours in 2005 and in 2025. **[2]**
**(c)** Using Item A, explain **two** reasons why these data may not give a valid picture of the
division of domestic labour. **[4]**

**9.** Study Item B.

> **Item B (original field notes, fictional).** *Saturday, 7.15 a.m., central market, the fictional
> town of Darrow.* About a dozen children, who look between ten and fourteen, are setting up stalls
> beside adults. A girl, perhaps twelve, counts change quickly and tells me she comes "every
> weekend and in the holidays". A boy says he works "for my uncle" and laughs when I ask whether he is
> paid. An older stallholder watches me closely while I write; after that, two of the children stop
> talking to me.

**(a)** Identify **two** pieces of evidence in Item B that the children's work is regular. **[2]**
**(b)** Using Item B, explain **two** limitations of field notes like these as evidence about child
work. **[4]**

**10.** Explain **two** advantages of using a longitudinal study to research how migration affects
families. **[6]**

**11.** A researcher wants to study the experiences of refugees living in a large city. Explain
**two** reasons why she might use unstructured interviews, and **one** problem she might face. **[6]**

**12.** Explain **two** strengths and **two** limitations of using an online survey to study young
people's use of social media in several countries. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** A list of all members of the population from which a sample is selected [1], such as a school
register or electoral roll; if the list is incomplete, the sample cannot be representative [1].
**[2]**

**2.** The tendency of respondents to give answers that make them look good or socially acceptable
[1], for example under-reporting prejudice or exaggerating how much housework they do, which reduces
validity [1]. **[2]**

**3.** A laboratory experiment takes place in an artificial, controlled setting, whereas a field
experiment takes place in a natural setting such as a school [1]; so the laboratory gives more
control of variables but less realism [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Two types, 2 marks each (identify [1], explain [1]):
- Closed questions [1]: fixed answer choices, easy to count and compare, so reliable [1].
- Open questions [1]: respondents answer in their own words, giving more detailed, valid answers [1].
- Scaled questions [1]: respondents rate agreement or frequency, measuring strength of attitude [1].
**[4]**

**5.** Two problems, 2 marks each:
- Unrepresentative [1]: people recruit friends like themselves, so the sample reflects one network [1].
- Not random [1]: findings cannot be statistically generalised to the population [1].
- Depends on trust [1]: if the first contacts are unusual or unwilling, the chain may break [1].
**[4]**

**6.** Two problems, 2 marks each:
- No informed consent [1]: participants do not know they are being studied, so they cannot agree [1].
- Deception [1]: the researcher lies about their identity, which may damage trust in research [1].
- Wellbeing and harm [1]: publication may expose participants or the researcher to danger or legal
  risk [1].
- Confidentiality [1]: the researcher may witness illegal acts and face pressure to disclose them [1].
**[4]**

**7.** Correlation means two variables change together [1]; causation means a change in one
variable produces a change in the other [1]. For example, areas with more police may record more
crime [1], but this correlation does not show police cause crime; more officers may simply record
more offences, or high crime may lead to more police [1]. **[4]**

**8. (a)** Women's hours fell by 5 (from 24 to 19) [1]; men's hours rose by 3 (from 6 to 9) [1].
**[2]**
**(b)** The gap was 18 hours in 2005 (24 − 6) [1] and 10 hours in 2025 (19 − 9) [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Two reasons, 2 marks each:
- Self-reporting and social desirability [1]: couples may overstate men's share to appear modern,
  especially if partners answer together [1].
- Operationalisation [1]: "domestic work" may exclude childcare or emotion work, so hours do not
  show who is responsible [1].
- Online sample [1]: couples without internet access, who may be poorer or rural, are excluded, so
  the data may not represent all of Kelmar [1].
- Different samples [1]: two separate samples, not the same couples, so change may partly reflect
  who was sampled [1].
**[4]**

**9. (a)** Any two [1 each]: the girl says she comes "every weekend and in the holidays" [1]; the
children are setting up stalls at 7.15 a.m. alongside adults, suggesting routine [1]; the girl counts
change quickly, suggesting practice [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Two limitations, 2 marks each:
- Researcher effect [1]: after the stallholder watched the researcher writing, two children stopped
  talking, so behaviour and answers changed because of the observation (a Hawthorne-type effect) [1].
- Interpretation and estimates [1]: ages are guesses ("look between ten and fourteen") and the boy's
  laugh is open to interpretation, so the notes rely on the observer's judgement [1].
- Generalisability [1]: one market on one morning cannot represent child work across Darrow or the
  country [1].
**[4]**

**10.** Two advantages, 3 marks each (identify [1], explain [1], apply [1]):
- Change over time [1]: the same families are studied repeatedly [1], so the researcher can see how
  relationships, care and money flows change after a member migrates [1].
- Sequence and causation [1]: researchers can see what happened first, such as job loss before
  migration [1], which helps distinguish causes from effects of migration [1].
- Reduced recall problems [1]: data are collected as events happen [1], rather than relying on
  memories of years earlier [1].
**[6]**

**11.** Two reasons, 2 marks each, and one problem, 2 marks:
- Sensitive experiences [1]: refugees may have experienced trauma; a flexible, empathetic interview
  allows them to tell their story at their own pace [1].
- Meanings [1]: unstructured interviews uncover how refugees interpret settlement, identity and
  loss, giving valid, in-depth data that closed questions would miss [1].
- Problem [1]: language barriers, fear of authorities or the need for interpreters may limit trust
  and accuracy, and the wellbeing of participants must be protected [1].
**[6]**

**12.** Two strengths, 2 marks each; two limitations, 2 marks each:
- Strength: large, cross-national samples [1], quickly and cheaply, allowing comparison between
  countries [1].
- Strength: appropriate medium [1]: young social media users are likely to be online, and anonymity
  may encourage honest answers about online behaviour [1].
- Limitation: sampling [1]: young people without internet access, or in countries with restricted
  platforms, are excluded, and self-selection reduces representativeness [1].
- Limitation: validity [1]: closed questions and self-reports may not capture what social media
  means to users, and translation may change the meaning of questions between countries [1].
**[8]**
