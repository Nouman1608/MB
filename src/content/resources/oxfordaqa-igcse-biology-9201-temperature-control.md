This guide goes deeper into section **3.4.4 Temperature control** of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Biology (9201)
specification, Version 5.2, for exams in May/June 2018 onwards. The qualification is untiered and any part of the
specification may be assessed on either paper.

The existing [Homeostasis, temperature control and blood glucose study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-homeostasis-temperature-control-and-blood-glucose/)
covers 3.4.2, 3.4.4 and 3.4.5 together. This page concentrates on temperature control and adds new evidence: skin
temperature data, the loss of fluid in sweat, and the difference between core and skin temperature in different
surroundings. Afterwards, use the
[Temperature control revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-9201-temperature-control-revision-notes/) and the
[Temperature control practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-9201-temperature-control-practice/). The general
idea of a control system is in the [Homeostasis study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-9201-homeostasis/).

## What 3.4.4 asks you to know

| Statements | Content |
|---|---|
| a | Body temperature is monitored and controlled by the thermoregulatory centre in the brain, which has receptors sensitive to the temperature of the blood flowing through the brain |
| b | Temperature receptors in the skin send impulses to the thermoregulatory centre, giving information about skin temperature |
| c | If the core body temperature is too high: skin capillary blood vessels dilate, and sweat glands release more sweat |
| d | Sweating cools the body; more water is lost when it is hot, so more fluid must be taken in. If the core temperature is too low: skin capillary blood vessels constrict, and muscles may shiver |

The specification says that the name of the centre in the brain (hypothalamus) is not required. Call it the
**thermoregulatory centre**.

## Detecting temperature

Two sets of receptors supply information.

| Receptors | Detect | Where they send the information |
|---|---|---|
| In the brain | The temperature of the blood flowing through the brain (the **core** temperature) | They are within the thermoregulatory centre |
| In the skin | The temperature at the surface of the body (**skin** temperature) | Impulses go to the thermoregulatory centre |

Two sources of information matter because skin temperature changes first when the surroundings change, while the
brain receptors check the blood temperature. The centre then coordinates the responses. The effectors are the sweat
glands, the muscle in the walls of blood vessels in the skin, and the skeletal muscles that shiver.

### Worked example 1: core and skin temperature in different surroundings

Invented data. A volunteer rests for 30 minutes at three room temperatures.

| Room temperature (°C) | 10 | 20 | 35 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core temperature (°C) | 36.9 | 37.0 | 37.2 |
| Skin temperature (°C) | 24.5 | 31.0 | 34.5 |

```
core − skin at 10 °C = 36.9 − 24.5 = 12.4 °C
core − skin at 20 °C = 37.0 − 31.0 = 6.0 °C
core − skin at 35 °C = 37.2 − 34.5 = 2.7 °C
change in core, 10 °C to 35 °C = 37.2 − 36.9 = 0.3 °C
change in skin, 10 °C to 35 °C = 34.5 − 24.5 = 10.0 °C
```

Interpretation: the room temperature changes by 25 °C but the core temperature changes by only 0.3 °C, whereas the skin
changes by 10.0 °C. This shows a control system keeping the **core** temperature relatively constant. The gap between core and
skin is large in the cold, because less warm blood flows through the skin, and small in the warm, because more blood
flows through the skin.

## If the core temperature is too high

The body must transfer more energy to the surroundings.

1. **Blood vessels supplying the skin capillaries dilate** (they widen). More blood flows through the capillaries in
   the skin, so more energy is transferred from the skin to the environment.
2. **Sweat glands release more sweat.** The sweat **evaporates**, taking energy from the body, so it **cools the
   body**.

A frequent error is to say the blood vessels "move closer to the skin". Blood vessels do not move; the vessels widen so
more blood flows through the skin capillaries.

### Replacing water

Sweating helps to cool the body. **More water is lost when it is hot**, and **more fluid has to be taken in through
drink or food** to balance the loss.

### Worked example 2: fluid balance during a hot match

Invented data. A player loses 1.2 dm³ of sweat per hour for 2.5 hours. He drinks 0.25 dm³ of water every 30 minutes,
five times in total.

```
sweat lost = 1.2 × 2.5 = 3.0 dm³
water drunk = 0.25 × 5 = 1.25 dm³
shortfall = 3.0 − 1.25 = 1.75 dm³
```

He has lost 1.75 dm³ more water than he has replaced, so he may become dehydrated. The sweat also contains ions
(see 3.4.3), so water alone does not replace everything lost. This is why fluid has to be taken in, as the specification says, to
balance the loss.

## If the core temperature is too low

The body must reduce the energy transferred to the surroundings and produce more energy.

1. **Blood vessels supplying the skin capillaries constrict** (they narrow). The flow of blood through the skin
   capillaries is reduced, so less energy is transferred from the skin to the surroundings.
2. **Muscles may shiver.** Their contraction needs **respiration**, which **transfers some energy to warm the body**.

### Worked example 3: skin temperature of a hand in cold water

Invented data. A student puts a hand in ice-cold water and measures the skin temperature of a finger.

| Time (min) | 0 | 5 | 10 | 15 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Skin temperature (°C) | 31.0 | 26.5 | 22.0 | 20.5 |

```
fall in 15 minutes = 31.0 − 20.5 = 10.5 °C
mean rate of fall = 10.5 ÷ 15 = 0.70 °C per minute
fall in first 5 minutes = 31.0 − 26.5 = 4.5 °C → 0.90 °C per minute
fall in last 5 minutes = 22.0 − 20.5 = 1.5 °C → 0.30 °C per minute
```

The skin cools fastest at first and more slowly later, as it approaches the temperature of the water. The cooling of the skin
of the hand is partly because the blood vessels in the skin constrict, so less warm blood flows to the surface. The core
temperature is protected, but the hand is cold. This shows why temperature control is a trade-off: protecting the
core means the surface cools.

### Why shivering warms the body

Shivering is rapid contraction of muscles. The contraction needs energy from respiration, and respiration transfers
some of that energy as heat that warms the body. Shivering also uses glucose and oxygen, so a shivering person can
become exhausted.

### Worked example 4: building a full-mark answer

Question (invented): "A cyclist rides up a long hill on a warm day. Explain how her body responds to keep her core
temperature from rising too far. [5]"

Plan the answer in the order of the control chain, then add the effect of each response.

1. Receptors in the brain detect the rise in the temperature of the blood, and receptors in the skin detect the
   temperature of the skin. [1]
2. The thermoregulatory centre in the brain coordinates the response. [1]
3. Blood vessels supplying the skin capillaries dilate. [1]
4. More blood flows through the skin capillaries, so more energy is transferred from the skin to the environment. [1]
5. Sweat glands release more sweat, which cools the body as it evaporates. [1]

A sixth point would be worth making in a longer answer: she must drink more, because more water is lost in sweat.

Each mark is for a separate fact. If you write "she sweats and goes red" you earn little, because "goes red" does not
say that blood vessels dilate or that more energy is transferred.

## Summary of the responses

| | Too hot | Too cold |
|---|---|---|
| Skin capillaries | Blood vessels **dilate**; more blood flows near the surface | Blood vessels **constrict**; less blood flows near the surface |
| Energy transfer from skin to surroundings | Increases | Decreases |
| Sweat | More sweat released; evaporation cools the body | Little sweat |
| Muscles | Not involved | May shiver; respiration transfers energy that warms the body |

## Common mistakes

- Saying that blood vessels "move" towards or away from the skin. They dilate or constrict.
- Saying "vasodilation" without explaining it. Write "blood vessels dilate, so more blood flows through the
  skin capillaries and more energy is transferred to the environment".
- Saying that sweating cools the body because sweat is cold. It cools because it **evaporates**.
- Saying shivering "creates heat" without mentioning respiration in the muscles.
- Forgetting that more fluid must be taken in when it is hot.
- Writing "hypothalamus" in place of "thermoregulatory centre". The name is not required, but it is not wrong.
- Saying that skin receptors alone control temperature. The thermoregulatory centre coordinates.

## Exam technique

- Use the chain: receptor → thermoregulatory centre → effector → response.
- For a two-response question, give both the **response** and the **effect on energy transfer**.
- For data, quote values and calculate differences, rates and means with units.
- If a question asks "why does the person drink more?", link it to the water lost in sweat.
