This guide covers section **13.2 Investigation of limiting factors** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Biology 9700 syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027. The theory is examined in Paper 4, and the investigations are exactly the kind of practical context that Paper 5 (Planning, Analysis and Evaluation) uses. The [Photosynthesis study guide](/resources/a-level-biology-9700-photosynthesis/) gives a short overview of 13.2; this page goes further into graph reading, experimental design, calculations and evaluation. ## What 13.2 asks you to do

| Outcome | In short |
|---|---|
| 13.2.1 | State that light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration and temperature are examples of limiting factors |
| 13.2.2 | Explain the effects of changing each of these on the rate of photosynthesis |
| 13.2.3 | Describe and carry out investigations with redox indicators (DCPIP and methylene blue) and a chloroplast suspension, varying light intensity and light wavelength |
| 13.2.4 | Describe and carry out investigations with whole plants, including aquatic plants, varying light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration and temperature |

## What "limiting factor" really means

A limiting factor is the factor **in shortest supply relative to need**. When it is increased, the rate rises; increasing any other factor makes no difference. The idea only makes sense when you can point to **which stage** of photosynthesis is held back.

| Factor | Stage it limits | Why the rate rises when it increases |
|---|---|---|
| Light intensity | Light-dependent stage | More photoactivation, so more ATP and reduced NADP for the Calvin cycle |
| Carbon dioxide concentration | Calvin cycle (fixation) | More CO₂ fixed by rubisco per second, so more GP and TP |
| Temperature | Calvin cycle (enzyme-controlled) | More kinetic energy, more enzyme–substrate collisions, until enzymes such as rubisco begin to denature above the optimum |

The light-dependent stage is mainly photochemical, so it is affected far less by temperature than the Calvin cycle. That is why temperature acts mostly through the enzymes in the stroma.

## Reading limiting-factor graphs

Typical graphs show rate against light intensity, with several curves for different CO₂ concentrations or temperatures. Use this routine:

1. **On the rising section** of every curve, the curves usually overlap: light intensity is the limiting factor, because changing CO₂ or temperature makes no difference there.
2. **Where a curve levels off**, light is no longer limiting. Identify the factor that differs between this curve and a higher curve: that factor was limiting on the plateau.
3. **Quote values** from both axes. "At 20 arbitrary units of light, the rate at 0.04 % CO₂ levels off at 12, but at 0.10 % CO₂ it continues to rise to 30" earns more than "the higher CO₂ line is higher".

If two curves at different temperatures plateau at the same height, temperature was not the limiting factor on that plateau; another factor (often CO₂) was.

## Investigating with redox indicators

### Principle

In the light-dependent stage, electrons emitted from chlorophyll are normally passed to NADP. If a **redox indicator** is added, it accepts these electrons instead and is **reduced**, changing colour:

- **DCPIP**: blue when oxidised, colourless when reduced.
- **Methylene blue**: blue when oxidised, colourless when reduced.

The faster the dye decolourises, the faster the light-dependent stage is running. Because the dye takes the electrons, little or no NADP is reduced, so this method measures the light-dependent stage only.

### Preparing a chloroplast suspension

- Grind leaves in an **ice-cold, buffered** solution with the **same water potential** as the chloroplasts. Cold slows enzymes that would damage the chloroplasts; the buffer holds the pH; matching water potential stops chloroplasts bursting or shrinking by osmosis.
- Filter, then centrifuge; resuspend the chloroplast pellet in cold buffer. Keep the suspension on ice and in dim light until use.

### Varying light intensity and wavelength

- **Intensity**: vary the distance of a lamp, or use a light meter to set values directly. Relative light intensity is often estimated as 1/d².
- **Wavelength**: place coloured filters (or use coloured LEDs) between the light and the tubes, keeping intensity the same for each colour.
- **Measure**: time for the blue colour to disappear, judged against a colour standard, or use a colorimeter and record the fall in absorbance over a fixed time.

### Controls

- A tube with chloroplasts and dye kept in the **dark**: shows that light is needed for the colour change.
- A tube with dye and buffer but **no chloroplasts**, in the light: shows the change is due to chloroplasts.
- A tube with **boiled** chloroplast suspension: shows that functioning chloroplasts are required.

### Worked example 1: wavelength and DCPIP

Three tubes of chloroplast suspension and DCPIP were lit through red, blue and green filters of equal intensity. The times to decolourise were 120 s, 150 s and 600 s.

```
rate = 1000 ÷ time  (in s⁻¹ × 10⁻³)
red:   1000 ÷ 120 = 8.3
blue:  1000 ÷ 150 = 6.7
green: 1000 ÷ 600 = 1.7
```

Red and blue light give fast rates because chlorophylls absorb strongly there; green light is poorly absorbed, so few electrons are emitted to reduce DCPIP. This links 13.2 back to the absorption spectra of section 13.1.

## Investigating with whole plants

An aquatic plant such as *Elodea* or *Cabomba* is placed in water containing **sodium hydrogencarbonate**, which supplies CO₂. The rate of photosynthesis is estimated from the rate of **oxygen** release.

| Independent variable | How to change it | Variables to keep constant |
|---|---|---|
| Light intensity | Lamp distance, or a light meter | CO₂ (same hydrogencarbonate concentration), temperature (water bath or heat shield) |
| CO₂ concentration | Range of hydrogencarbonate concentrations | Light intensity, temperature |
| Temperature | Thermostatically controlled water bath | Light intensity, CO₂ |

In all three, keep the **same piece of plant** (or pieces of the same mass and length) and allow an **acclimatisation** period of a few minutes after each change before measuring.

**Measuring oxygen.** Counting bubbles is quick but unreliable, because bubble size varies. A **capillary tube** or **gas syringe** that collects the gas gives a volume. In a capillary, the gas forms a bubble of measurable length, and volume = πr² × length.

### Worked example 2: rate from a capillary

A gas bubble collected in 5 minutes is 18 mm long in a capillary of internal radius 0.4 mm.

```
volume = π × 0.4² × 18 = 9.05 mm³
rate   = 9.05 ÷ 5 = 1.81 mm³ min⁻¹
```

### Worked example 3: light intensity from lamp distance

Relative light intensity = 10 000 ÷ d² (d in cm).

| Distance / cm | 10 | 15 | 20 | 25 | 30 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Relative light intensity | 100 | 44.4 | 25.0 | 16.0 | 11.1 |

Doubling the distance from 10 cm to 20 cm reduces intensity to a quarter, not a half. Plot rate against **light intensity**, not against distance, or the shape of the curve is distorted.

## Evaluating the investigations

These are the weaknesses examiners most often look for, with an improvement for each:

- **Heat from the lamp** changes the temperature at close distances. Use an LED, or place a glass tank of water between lamp and plant as a heat shield, and monitor temperature.
- **Gas collected is not all oxygen**, and some oxygen dissolves or is used in respiration. The value is a **net** rate; state this in conclusions.
- **Background light** adds to the lamp. Do the experiment in a darkened room.
- **CO₂ runs out** in long experiments. Use excess hydrogencarbonate and fresh solution for each condition.
- **End point of DCPIP is subjective.** Use a colour standard or a colorimeter.
- **Few repeats.** Take at least three readings per condition, calculate a mean, and identify anomalies.

## Common mistakes

- Saying light intensity affects "the Calvin cycle" directly. It acts through ATP and reduced NADP.
- Stating that above the optimum temperature "enzymes are killed". Enzymes are **denatured**.
- Calling DCPIP a test for oxygen. It accepts **electrons**.
- Forgetting to keep light intensity equal when testing wavelength.
- Plotting rate against lamp distance and describing the curve as if it were light intensity.

## Exam technique

- **State** (13.2.1): name the three factors; no explanation is needed.
- **Explain** (13.2.2): link the factor to the stage it affects and to a named product (ATP, reduced NADP, GP or TP).
- **Describe** an investigation (13.2.3–4): give the independent variable and how to change it, the dependent variable and how to measure it, two or more controlled variables with how they are controlled, repeats, and a control.
- **Suggest** improvements: name the problem, then the specific change. "Use a heat shield" scores; "be more careful" does not.
