## Questions

**1.** Define the term *price floor*. **[2]**

**2.** Outline two reasons why a government might intervene in a market. **[4]**

**3.** The ferry-ticket market on the fictional island of Kestria is in equilibrium. The government sets a maximum ticket price below the equilibrium price. Draw a labelled diagram to show the effect, and state what the diagram shows. **[4]**

**4.** The table shows the market for fish landings at the fictional Port Lindel. Price is in cu per tonne and quantity is in tonnes per week.

| Price | Quantity demanded | Quantity supplied |
|---|---|---|
| 11 | 85 | 45 |
| 13 | 75 | 55 |
| 15 | 65 | 65 |
| 17 | 55 | 75 |
| 19 | 45 | 85 |

**(a)** State the equilibrium price and quantity. **[1]**
**(b)** The government sets a minimum price of 17 cu. Calculate the surplus. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain one consequence for taxpayers if the government buys the surplus. **[2]**

**5.** In the fictional country of Halvern, the government places a per-unit indirect tax on plastic cutlery. Explain, with the help of a diagram, how the tax affects the price paid by consumers, the price kept by producers and the quantity sold. **[5]**

**6.** In the fictional town of Harrowdale, the council pays a subsidy to the sellers of school textbooks. Explain the effects of the subsidy on consumers, producers and the council. **[5]**

**7.** Outline one example each of direct provision of services and command and control regulation. **[4]**

**8.** (HL only) Outline how a default option works as a consumer nudge, and give one advantage of using it. **[3]**

**9.** (HL only, calculator allowed) In the fictional city of Lunarra, the market for handmade paper lanterns has demand Qd = 240 − 2P and supply Qs = −60 + 4P, where P is the price in cu and Q is in thousands per month. The government introduces an indirect tax of 12 cu per lantern.

**(a)** Calculate the equilibrium price and quantity before the tax. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the new quantity and the price paid by consumers. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the government's tax revenue. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the amount of the tax revenue that comes from the rise in the consumers' price. **[2]**

**10.** (HL only, calculator allowed) In the fictional city of Marenta, the market for taxi trips has demand Qd = 300 − 2P and supply Qs = 3P − 60, where P is the fare in cu and Q is in thousand trips per week. The council sets a maximum fare of 60 cu.

**(a)** Calculate the shortage. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the change in total spending on trips that take place. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the welfare loss, assuming the trips that take place go to the riders who value them most. **[3]**

**11.** (HL only, calculator allowed) In the fictional country of Tessaly, the market for e-scooters has demand Qd = 200 − 2P and supply Qs = 3P − 100, where P is the price in cu and Q is in thousands. The government gives producers a subsidy of 15 cu per scooter.

**(a)** Calculate the new equilibrium quantity and the price paid by consumers. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the cost of the subsidy to the government. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate how much of the subsidy benefits consumers. **[2]**

**12.** Evaluate the use of a price floor in the market for an agricultural product such as rice. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** A price floor is a legally set minimum price [1] that is set above the free-market equilibrium price, so that it prevents the price falling below that level [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition earns more with both the legal minimum and its position above equilibrium; "a price that cannot fall" omits the part that matters.

**2.** Any two reasons, each with a link. To earn revenue [1]: an indirect tax on a good gives the government money to spend [1]. To correct market failure [1]: a tax on a good with a negative externality lowers consumption towards the socially better level [1]. (Other valid pairs: support low-income households with a ceiling or subsidy; support firms with a subsidy or minimum price.) **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* With "outline" (a brief account), a reason plus a short link scores better than a list of aims; two reasons are asked for, so extra ones earn nothing more.

**3.** Axes labelled price (vertical) and quantity (horizontal), with D and S drawn and labelled [1]. Equilibrium price Pe and quantity Qe marked [1]. A horizontal line labelled Pmax drawn below Pe, meeting S at Qs and D at Qd [1]. The diagram shows a shortage equal to Qd − Qs, because Qs is less than Qd [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A ceiling drawn above Pe has no effect, so the line's position matters; label the shortage as a quantity gap.

**4. (a)** Price 15 cu per tonne and quantity 65 tonnes [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** At 17 cu, quantity demanded is 55 and quantity supplied is 75 [1]. Surplus = 75 − 55 = **20 tonnes** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Taxpayers fund the purchase [1]: 20 × 17 = 340 cu a week, which could have been spent on other services (opportunity cost) [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Read the columns at the floor price, not at equilibrium. "Explain" asks for a reason, not just "it costs money".

**5.** The tax raises the firms' cost per unit, so the supply curve shifts upward by the amount of the tax [1]. The new equilibrium has a higher price paid by consumers [1] and a lower quantity [1]. Producers keep the new price minus the tax, which is below the original price [1]. The burden is shared; the side with the steeper curve bears more [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the vertical gap between the two supply curves as the tax, and mark the producer's price on the original curve.

**6.** The subsidy lowers firms' costs, so supply shifts downward or right by the subsidy per unit [1]. The price paid by consumers falls and the quantity rises [1]. Producers receive the consumer price plus the subsidy, so their revenue per unit rises [1]. The council pays subsidy per unit × quantity, which is a cost to the taxpayer [1]. The benefit is shared between consumers and producers [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* The question names three stakeholders, so give one effect for each.

**7.** Direct provision: the government supplies a service itself [1], for example a public health clinic funded by taxation [1]. Command and control: the government sets a rule backed by penalties [1], for example a legal limit on the amount of waste a factory may release [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Give each form its own definition and example; two examples of one form do not cover both.

**8.** A default option is the choice that applies if the person does nothing [1]. For example, a worker is automatically enrolled in a saving plan but may opt out [1]. One advantage: it changes behaviour without banning any choice and is cheap to run [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The point that people can still choose differently is what separates a nudge from a rule.

**9. (a)** 240 − 2P = −60 + 4P [1], so 6P = 300 and **P = 50, Q = 140 (thousand)** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** New supply: Qs = −60 + 4(P − 12) = −108 + 4P [1]. 240 − 2P = −108 + 4P, so 6P = 348 and **P = 58** [1]. Q = 240 − 116 = **124 (thousand)** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Revenue = 12 × 124 = **1,488 (thousand cu)** [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** Consumers' price rises by 58 − 50 = 8 [1]. 8 × 124 = **992 (thousand cu)** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Subtract the tax from the price inside the supply function (or add it to the supply price); subtracting it from the final price instead gives a different, wrong equilibrium. The revenue uses the new quantity.

**10. (a)** At 60: Qd = 300 − 120 = 180 [1]. Qs = 180 − 60 = 120 [1]. Shortage = 180 − 120 = **60 thousand trips** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Before: equilibrium is 300 − 2P = 3P − 60, so P = 72 and Q = 156; spending = 72 × 156 = 11,232; after: 60 × 120 = 7,200 [1]. Fall = **4,032 (thousand cu)** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Demand price at Q = 120: 120 = 300 − 2P, so P = 90 [1]. Triangle: base = 156 − 120 = 36, height = 90 − 60 = 30 [1]. Welfare loss = ½ × 36 × 30 = **540 (thousand cu)** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Use Qs for spending after the ceiling, and state your assumption about who gets the trips before computing a welfare loss.

**11. (a)** Supply with subsidy: Qs = 3(P + 15) − 100 = 3P − 55 [1]. 200 − 2P = 3P − 55, so P = **51** [1]. Q = 200 − 102 = **98 (thousand)** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Subsidy per unit × quantity = 15 × 98 [1] = **1,470 (thousand cu)** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Original equilibrium price is 60, so the consumers' price falls by 9 [1]. 9 × 98 = **882 (thousand cu)** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Producers receive 51 + 15 = 66, so their gain is 6 × 98 = 588; check that the two gains add to the cost (882 + 588 = 1,470) before moving on.

**12.** Indicative creditworthy points; a developed, balanced answer matters more than counting points.
- Define a price floor as a minimum price above equilibrium [1].
- Diagram: floor above Pe, with the surplus Qs − Qd labelled [1].
- Surplus: quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded at the floor [1].
- Farmers gain a higher price on the quantity sold, supporting incomes [1].
- Consumers pay more and buy less, which hurts low-income households most [1].
- Taxpayers pay if the government buys the surplus; stock may be wasted [1].
- Welfare loss: resources are drawn into production the market does not need [1].
- The high price keeps farmers in the industry and may delay adjustment [1].
- Alternatives such as direct payments can support income with less surplus [1].
- A judgement that depends on the size of the floor and the responsiveness of supply and demand [1].
**[10]**
*Examiner insight:* "Evaluate" means appraising by weighing up strengths and limitations. A conclusion that takes a side and states its conditions lifts an explanation into evaluation.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Placing a ceiling over equilibrium or a floor under it, then claiming a shortage or surplus.
- Moving demand when the policy acts on supply.
- Leaving out axis labels, curve labels, Pe, Qe or the new values.
- Reading the producer price from the wrong curve, or forgetting to take off the tax.
- Using the old quantity in revenue or cost calculations.
- Giving a shortage or surplus as a price gap instead of a quantity gap.
- Covering only one stakeholder.
- Describing a policy without weighing it when the command term is "evaluate".
- Giving a final number without the working when the command term is "calculate".
