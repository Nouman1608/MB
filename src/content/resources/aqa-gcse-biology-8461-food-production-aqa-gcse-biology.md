## Food security

**Food security** is having enough food to feed a population. The specification lists six **biological factors**
threatening it. For each, learn the link to "less food available" or "food harder to obtain":

| Factor | How it threatens food security |
|---|---|
| Increasing birth rate in some countries | More people need feeding than the land and farming there can supply |
| Changing diets in developed countries | Demand for foods grown elsewhere means scarce food resources are transported around the world, away from the people who grew them |
| New pests and pathogens affecting farming | Crops and livestock are damaged or killed, so yields fall |
| Environmental changes affecting food production | For example, widespread famine can occur in some countries if rains fail |
| The cost of agricultural inputs | Farmers who cannot afford seed, fertiliser, feed or fuel produce less |
| Conflicts in some parts of the world | War can stop farming and cut off supplies of water or food |

The specification ends with the key principle: **sustainable methods must be found to feed all people on Earth**.
Sustainable means meeting today's needs without damaging the ability of future generations to meet theirs.

You may be asked to **interpret population and food production statistics** to evaluate food security. Compare
the rate at which the population grows with the rate at which food production grows: if population grows faster,
food per person falls.

**Worked example 1** (invented figures). Over ten years a country's population rises from 20 million to 25
million, and its grain production rises from 8.0 million tonnes to 9.0 million tonnes.

```
population increase: (25 − 20) ÷ 20 × 100 = 25%
grain increase:      (9.0 − 8.0) ÷ 8.0 × 100 = 12.5%
grain per person:    8.0 ÷ 20 = 0.40 t  →  9.0 ÷ 25 = 0.36 t
```

Grain per person fell, so food security has become worse even though total production rose.

## Farming techniques

The **efficiency of food production** can be improved by **restricting energy transfer from food animals to the
environment**. This links straight to trophic levels: an animal uses much of its food in respiration, for moving
and for keeping warm, and that food does not become meat.

Two methods are named:

- **limiting their movement**, so less energy is transferred for muscle contraction;
- **controlling the temperature of their surroundings**, so less energy is transferred to keep warm.

**Some animals are fed high-protein foods to increase growth.**

**Worked example 2: comparing efficiency** (invented figures). Two groups of chickens each eat 2.5 kg of feed.

```
intensive (warm shed, little movement): mass gain 0.50 kg
efficiency = 0.50 ÷ 2.5 × 100 = 20%

free-range (outdoors, moving freely):   mass gain 0.35 kg
efficiency = 0.35 ÷ 2.5 × 100 = 14%
```

The intensive chickens convert a larger percentage of their food into body mass because less is used in
respiration for movement and warmth.

**Evaluating intensive methods.** Some people have **ethical objections** to some modern intensive farming
methods. A balanced evaluation includes:

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| More meat from the same amount of feed | Ethical objections: animals cannot move freely or behave naturally |
| Cheaper food for consumers | Disease can spread quickly between animals kept close together |
| Less land needed per animal | Heating the buildings costs money and uses energy |

## Sustainable fisheries

**Fish stocks in the oceans are declining.** It is important to keep fish stocks at a level where **breeding
continues**, or certain species may disappear altogether in some areas. Two measures are named:

- **Control of net size**: nets with a larger mesh let young, small fish escape. They survive to become adults and
  breed, so the stock is replaced.
- **Fishing quotas**: limits on the mass or number of fish of each species that may be caught. Fewer fish are
  removed, so enough adults remain to breed.

**Annotated model answer**

*Question: Explain how fishing quotas and larger mesh sizes together help a fish stock recover. [4]*

> Quotas limit the number of fish caught, so more adult fish are left in the sea [1]. These adults can breed and
> produce young [1]. Larger mesh lets young fish escape before they have bred [1]. So more fish reach breeding age
> and the population increases to a sustainable level [1].

Both measures work through the same idea: **more fish survive to breed**. Say so explicitly.

## The role of biotechnology

You should be able to describe and explain some possible biotechnical and agricultural solutions, including
genetic modification, to the demands of the growing human population.

### Mycoprotein

Modern biotechnology techniques enable **large quantities of microorganisms to be cultured for food**. The fungus
***Fusarium*** is used to produce **mycoprotein**, a protein-rich food suitable for vegetarians.

```
Fusarium is grown on glucose syrup → in aerobic conditions (oxygen supplied)
→ the fungus grows, producing biomass → the biomass is harvested → and purified
```

Why aerobic? The fungus needs oxygen for aerobic respiration to transfer enough energy for rapid growth.

### Insulin from genetically modified bacteria

A **genetically modified bacterium produces human insulin**. When harvested and purified, this is used to treat
people with diabetes. The process of putting the human gene into the bacterium is in 4.6.2.4 Genetic
engineering; here you need to know the outcome and why it helps a growing population: large quantities of insulin
can be produced reliably.

### GM crops

**GM crops could provide more food or food with an improved nutritional value, such as golden rice.** Golden
rice has been modified so that it contains a substance the body can use to make vitamin A, which could help
people whose diet lacks it.

When evaluating GM crops, give possible benefits (higher yields, improved nutrition, resistance to pests) and
concerns some people have (effects on wild plants and insects, or on human health, which some argue have not been
fully tested).

## Common mistakes

- Defining food security as "having healthy food". It is having **enough** food to feed a population.
- Saying intensive farming "uses less energy". It **reduces energy transfer from the animals** to the environment.
- Saying larger nets catch fewer fish. It is the **mesh size** that matters: young fish escape.
- Forgetting that mycoprotein comes from a **fungus**, not bacteria.
- Missing out "aerobic" or "purified" when describing mycoprotein production.
- Giving only advantages in an evaluation of intensive farming.

## Exam technique for AQA command words

- **Describe** a factor affecting food security: name the factor and say how it reduces food available.
- **Explain** why intensive farming is more efficient: less movement and warmth → less energy transferred in
  respiration → more biomass.
- **Evaluate** a farming method or GM crops: advantages, disadvantages including ethics, then a judgement.
- **Calculate** from food statistics: percentage changes, or food per person, before drawing a conclusion.
