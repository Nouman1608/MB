These questions cover section **4.8.1 Purity, formulations and chromatography** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry
(8462) specification, for teaching from September 2016. The content is assessed on Paper 2 at both Foundation
and Higher Tier. The specification marks nothing in this section (HT only), so no question is labelled Higher
tier only. Question 8 is based on required practical 6.

Learn the content first in the
[purity, formulations and chromatography study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-purity-formulations-and-chromatography/)
and the [chemical analysis revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-chemical-analysis-revision-notes/).

## Questions

**1.** A carton is labelled "pure orange juice".

**(a)** Explain why the juice is not pure in the chemical sense. **[2]**
**(b)** State what a chemist means by a pure substance. **[1]**

**2.** A student measures the melting points of three samples of a solid. A data book gives the melting point of the pure solid as 80 °C.

| Sample | Melting range / °C |
|---|---|
| P | 80 to 80 |
| Q | 74 to 79 |
| R | 78 to 82 |

**(a)** Identify which sample is most likely to be pure and give a reason. **[2]**
**(b)** Give **one** reason why the other two samples are impure. **[1]**
**(c)** State how the boiling point of an impure liquid differs from that of the pure liquid. **[1]**

**3.** A bottle of liquid cleaner lists these ingredients: water, a detergent, a perfume and a thickener.

**(a)** State what is meant by a formulation. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why the cleaner is a formulation and not a pure substance. **[2]**
**(c)** Give **two** other examples of formulations. **[2]**

**4.** A student sets up paper chromatography of a black ink. The student draws the start line in ink 1 cm from the bottom and places the paper in a beaker so the solvent level is 2 cm above the bottom.

**(a)** Identify **two** mistakes in the set-up. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why each mistake would affect the result. **[2]**

**5.** In a chromatography experiment, a spot moves 5.4 cm from the origin and the solvent front moves 7.2 cm. Calculate the Rf value. **[2]**

**6.** A student runs a mixture of dyes, X, alongside reference dyes. The solvent front is 9.0 cm from the origin. Mixture X gives two spots, at 2.7 cm and 6.3 cm from the origin.

| Reference dye | Rf value |
|---|---|
| P | 0.30 |
| Q | 0.52 |
| R | 0.70 |

**(a)** Calculate the Rf value of each spot in X. **[2]**
**(b)** Identify the dyes in mixture X. **[2]**
**(c)** State what the Rf value of Q shows about where the spot for dye Q would be in this experiment. **[1]**

**7.** Explain how paper chromatography separates the dyes in a mixture. Use the terms stationary phase and mobile phase. **[4]**

**8.** A student investigates which food colourings are in a sweet.

**(a)** Describe how the student should carry out paper chromatography to compare the sweet's colouring with three known colourings. **[5]**
**(b)** Suggest how the student can show that a known food colouring is a pure substance. **[2]**

**9.** A chromatogram of a drug sample using solvent 1 shows one spot. The same sample using solvent 2 shows two spots.

**(a)** State what this shows about the sample. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why the single spot in solvent 1 does not prove that the sample is pure. **[2]**

**10.** A compound has an Rf of 0.40 in a solvent. In a new experiment, the solvent front moves 6.5 cm from the origin.

**(a)** Calculate how far the spot should be from the origin. **[2]**
**(b)** The student sees the spot at 3.9 cm. Suggest what this indicates. **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** The juice is a **mixture** [1] of water, sugars, acids, flavour compounds and other substances [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** A single element or compound, **not mixed with any other substance** [1]. **[1]**

**2. (a)** **Sample P** [1], because it melts at one **sharp / specific** temperature that matches the data book value [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** They melt over a **range** of temperatures (and Q melts below 80 °C) [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** The impure liquid has a **higher** boiling point (and boils over a range) [1]. **[1]**

**3. (a)** A **mixture designed as a useful product**, made by mixing components in carefully measured quantities [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** It contains **several different substances** [1], each with a **particular purpose**, mixed in measured amounts [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Any two [1] each: paint; fuel; medicine; alloy; fertiliser; food. **[2]**

**4. (a)** The start line was drawn in **ink** [1]; the solvent level is **above the start line** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Ink would **dissolve and move up** with the solvent, so its colours would be mixed with the sample's [1]; if the solvent covers the spots, the **sample dissolves into the solvent** and no chromatogram forms [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Rf = 5.4 ÷ 7.2 [1] = **0.75** [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** 2.7 ÷ 9.0 = **0.30** [1]; 6.3 ÷ 9.0 = **0.70** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** Mixture X contains dyes **P** [1] and **R** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Dye Q would be at 0.52 × 9.0 = **4.7 cm**, so the spot at 4.7 cm is **absent** from X [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Match each spot to a reference by Rf in the same solvent; the dye not matched is not present.

**7.** The dyes are placed on the **stationary phase** (the paper) [1]; the **mobile phase** (solvent) moves up the paper [1]; each dye is **distributed differently between the two phases** / has a different solubility in the solvent and attraction to the paper [1]; dyes that are more soluble travel further, so the dyes separate into different spots [1]. **[4]**

**8. (a)** Any five [1] each: draw a **pencil** line on the paper; place spots of the sweet's extract and the three known colourings on the line; stand the paper in **solvent** with the **level below the line**; put a lid on the beaker; remove the paper when the solvent is near the top and mark the **solvent front**; let the paper dry; compare the **spots** (positions or Rf values) of the sweet with the known colourings. **[5]**
**(b)** It forms **one spot** [1] in **different solvents** [1]. **[2]**

**9. (a)** The sample is a **mixture** (impure) [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** In solvent 1 the components may have **moved together** / the same Rf [1], so only a single spot appeared; testing in more than one solvent gives better evidence [1]. **[2]**

**10. (a)** Distance = 0.40 × 6.5 [1] = **2.6 cm** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** At 3.9 cm the Rf is 0.60 (3.9 ÷ 6.5), so it is a **different compound** (or the solvent is different / the sample is impure) [1]. **[1]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using ink for the start line, or letting the solvent cover the spots.
- Measuring from the bottom of the paper rather than the origin.
- Quoting Rf with units, or not rounding to an appropriate number of significant figures.
- Describing a formulation as "pure" because it is a made product.
- Calling a single spot proof of purity without a second solvent.
