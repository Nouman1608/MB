## What 2.3.3 contains

The specification says students need to learn **the concept of quality and its importance in:**

- the production of goods and the provision of services: quality control and quality assurance
- allowing a business to control costs and gain a competitive advantage.

So there are three tasks: understand what quality means, compare the two ways of managing it in both goods and services, and explain why it matters for costs and competition.

## The concept of quality

Quality does not mean "luxury". A product or service is of good quality when it meets customers' expectations and is fit for its purpose, consistently. A £1 pen that writes smoothly every time can be high quality; a £50 pen that leaks is not. Customers judge quality against the price they paid and what they were promised.

| Goods: quality means | Services: quality means |
|---|---|
| Works as described, without faults | Delivered reliably, on time, as promised |
| Durable and safe | Staff are knowledgeable, polite and helpful |
| Looks and feels as expected | The experience is consistent each visit |
| Consistent from one unit to the next | Problems are put right quickly |

Services are harder to manage because they are produced while the customer is present and depend heavily on the person providing them. A faulty phone can be caught before it leaves the factory; a rude receptionist cannot be "inspected out" after the customer has heard them.

## Quality control

Quality control (QC) is checking products or services, usually at the end of the process or at set stages, to find and remove faults before they reach customers.

- **In goods:** inspecting finished items, testing a sample from each production run, measuring against a specification.
- **In services:** a manager checking finished work (a cleaned hotel room before the guest arrives), mystery shoppers posing as customers, reviewing customer feedback scores.

**Strengths:** relatively simple to set up; stops many faulty items reaching customers; specialist inspectors can be trained.

**Weaknesses:** faults are found after materials and time have been spent, so waste remains; checking only a sample lets some faults through; workers may feel quality is the inspector's job, not theirs.

## Quality assurance

Quality assurance (QA) is a system for preventing faults from happening in the first place, by setting standards for every stage of the process and making every worker responsible for meeting them.

- **In goods:** agreed standards with suppliers, staff training, checks by workers at each stage, regular maintenance of machines.
- **In services:** written service standards (answer calls within three rings, for example), training in product knowledge and customer handling, systems that prevent errors such as double bookings.

A well-known approach to QA is total quality management (TQM, a standard term), in which every employee aims for zero defects and continuous improvement.

**Strengths:** fewer faults are made, so less waste and rework; quality becomes part of everyone's job; can motivate staff who are trusted to check their own work.

**Weaknesses:** training and new systems cost money and time to set up; results take a while to appear; it depends on staff commitment.

QC and QA are not either/or. Most businesses use QA to prevent faults and a lighter level of QC to catch the few that still occur.

## Quality and controlling costs

Poor quality is expensive. The costs of faults include:

- materials and labour wasted on items that are scrapped or reworked
- refunds, replacements and return postage
- staff time spent handling complaints
- lost future sales from customers who do not come back.

Spending on quality is worthwhile when it costs less than the faults it prevents.

### Worked example 1: Ellery Electronics compares two systems

Ellery assembles 8,000 phone chargers a month. A faulty charger caught in the factory costs £6 in scrapped parts. One that reaches a customer costs £25 in refunds, postage and handling. Inspection catches 95% of faulty chargers.

**Current system (quality control only):** inspection costs £2,600 a month and 4% of chargers are faulty.

1. Faulty chargers = 8,000 × 4% = 320
2. Caught = 320 × 95% = 304; cost 304 × £6 = £1,824
3. Reach customers = 16; cost 16 × £25 = £400
4. Total = £2,600 + £1,824 + £400 = **£4,824** a month

**Proposed system (quality assurance plus lighter inspection):** training and supplier checks cost £2,000 a month, lighter inspection £1,200, and the fault rate falls to 1.5%.

1. Faulty chargers = 8,000 × 1.5% = 120
2. Caught = 114; cost £684
3. Reach customers = 6; cost £150
4. Total = £2,000 + £1,200 + £684 + £150 = **£4,034** a month

The QA system saves £790 a month and sends 10 fewer faulty chargers to customers each month, which also protects Ellery's reputation. The 1.5% is a forecast, and the training cost comes before the savings, so a balanced answer says the benefit depends on staff actually changing how they work.

## Quality and competitive advantage

Quality helps a business win customers from rivals in several linked ways:

1. **Reputation and reviews:** consistent quality earns positive reviews and recommendations, which bring new customers at little cost.
2. **Loyalty:** satisfied customers return and are less likely to switch, even if a rival is cheaper.
3. **Premium prices:** customers will pay more for products they trust, so quality can support a higher price.
4. **Lower costs:** fewer faults mean lower costs, which can allow competitive prices without cutting profit.

The last point shows that quality can support both kinds of competitive advantage: differentiation (better than rivals) and low cost (cheaper than rivals, because less is wasted).

### Worked example 2: measuring service quality at Fernbrook Dental

Fernbrook Dental received 18 complaints from 2,400 appointments in its first half-year: 18 ÷ 2,400 × 1,000 = **7.5 complaints per 1,000 appointments**. After introducing a service standard (reminder texts, a maximum ten-minute wait, and staff training), it received 9 complaints from 2,600 appointments: **about 3.5 per 1,000**. Using a rate per 1,000 is fairer than comparing raw numbers, because the number of appointments changed.

## Common mistakes

- Defining quality as expensive or luxurious.
- Mixing up QC (finding faults) and QA (preventing faults).
- Writing only about goods when the question is about a service business.
- Saying quality always raises costs; good quality systems often lower total costs.
- Claiming competitive advantage without saying how customers' behaviour changes.

## Exam technique

- **Define:** quality control or quality assurance in one precise sentence, saying when the checking happens.
- **Calculate:** defect rates, the cost of faults, total cost of a quality system, or complaints per 1,000.
- **Outline:** two linked points in context, such as how QA could reduce costs at Ellery.
- **Explain:** a general statement with two expansions, for example why QA motivates staff.
- **Analyse:** follow a quality decision to costs, reputation and sales.
- **Justify:** choose between QC and QA, or two quality plans, using the figures.
- **Evaluate:** weigh the cost of a quality system against its benefits and conclude on what the result depends.
