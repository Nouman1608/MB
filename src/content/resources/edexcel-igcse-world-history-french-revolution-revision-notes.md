## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| *Ancien régime* | The old order: absolute monarchy and a society of privileged orders |
| Three Estates | Clergy (First), nobility (Second), everyone else (Third) |
| *Taille* | Main direct tax, paid by the Third Estate |
| Bourgeoisie | Wealthy, educated middle class of the Third Estate |
| National Assembly | Name taken by the Third Estate deputies on 17 June 1789 |
| Great Fear | Rural panic and peasant attacks on châteaux, summer 1789 |
| *Assignats* | Paper money backed by confiscated Church land |
| Sans-culottes | Radical Parisian workers and artisans |
| Girondins | Revolutionaries who led France into war in 1792; purged in 1793 |
| Jacobins | Radical club; Robespierre its leading figure |
| Committee of Public Safety | Twelve-member committee that ran the Terror |
| Thermidorian Reaction | Move away from Terror after Robespierre's fall |
| White Terror | Revenge attacks on former Jacobins, 1795 |
| Directory | Government of five Directors, 1795–99 |
| First Consul | Napoleon's title under the Consulate from 1799 |

## Why each turning point happened — summary

**1789 (revolution begins).** Long-term: privilege, Enlightenment criticism, debt from the American
war. Short-term: the privileged refused tax reform (Notables, Parlement), bankruptcy forced the
calling of the Estates General, the voting dispute led to the National Assembly, and bread
prices plus Necker's dismissal brought the Paris crowd onto the streets.

**August 1792 (fall of the monarchy).** Varennes destroyed trust in Louis; war went badly; the
Brunswick Manifesto threatened Paris; the sans-culottes and Jacobins saw the king as a traitor.

**1793–94 (Terror).** Foreign war on several fronts, civil war in the Vendée, economic crisis,
pressure from the sans-culottes and the belief of Robespierre and the Committee that the republic
could only survive by destroying its enemies.

**July 1794 (Thermidor).** Military victory removed the emergency; deputies feared Robespierre's
threats of new purges; the Law of 22 Prairial (June 1794) had sped up executions and alarmed
even his allies.

**1799 (Brumaire).** The Directory relied on the army and on coups (Fructidor), lacked money and
popularity, and faced renewed war; Napoleon had prestige from Italy and Egypt.

## Worked example: an "explain two effects" answer

*Explain two effects of the flight to Varennes (1791).*

- **Effect 1:** it destroyed trust in Louis XVI as a constitutional monarch. He had left a
  declaration criticising the Revolution, so many now saw him as an enemy. This strengthened
  republican demands, seen in the petition that led to the Champ de Mars shootings in July 1791.
- **Effect 2:** it increased the risk of war. Austria and Prussia issued the Declaration of
  Pillnitz (August 1791), which revolutionaries read as a threat, and this helped the Girondins
  persuade the Assembly to declare war in April 1792.

Each effect is named, supported with specific detail and linked back to Varennes.

## Common mistakes and exam traps

- **Confusing the assemblies.** National/Constituent Assembly (1789–91), Legislative Assembly
  (1791–92), National Convention (1792–95). Check which one the question means.
- **Treating the Great Fear as part of the Terror.** They are four years apart and very different.
- **Describing instead of explaining.** In the 16-mark essay every paragraph must link back to the
  question's factor and reach a judgement.
- **Ignoring the stated factor.** In "How far do you agree…" essays, deal with the factor named in
  the question first, then weigh it against others, including your own.
- **Running past 1799.** The depth study ends with Napoleon as First Consul; his empire is not
  needed.
- **Using the extract only as a quotation.** In the impression question, explain what the
  author's choice of words suggests; do not just copy phrases.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many dates do I really need?**
Enough to place each event in the right key topic and to
show sequence. The timeline above is a sensible minimum.

**Was Robespierre a dictator?**
He was one of twelve members of the Committee of Public Safety
and never held sole power, though he was its most prominent voice. A good answer uses this
distinction.

**What counts as an "achievement of the Revolution"?**
Lasting changes such as the end of feudal
dues and legal privilege, the Declaration of Rights, the departments and the principle of national
sovereignty.
