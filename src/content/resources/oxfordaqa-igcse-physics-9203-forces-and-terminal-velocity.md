This guide covers **3.1.6 Forces and terminal velocity** (statements a to d) of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Physics (9203) specification, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. The section is marked P, so it is assessed in the full Physics award. The [terminal velocity, centre of mass, moments and levers guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-terminal-velocity-centre-of-mass-moments-and-levers/) introduces the skydiver example; this page goes deeper into force analysis at each stage of a fall, sketching and reading velocity-time graphs, and the link between streamlining and the maximum velocity of a vehicle. Quick recall is in the [Forces and terminal velocity revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-forces-and-terminal-velocity-revision-notes/) and exam-style questions are in the [Forces and terminal velocity practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-forces-and-terminal-velocity-practice/).

## What the specification asks for

| Statement | Content |
|---|---|
| a | An object moving through a fluid experiences friction (drag); the faster it moves, the greater the drag |
| b | A falling object first accelerates due to gravity; drag increases with velocity until the resultant force is zero and the object moves at terminal velocity |
| c | Parachutes increase drag so that terminal velocity is reduced; draw and interpret velocity-time graphs for objects reaching terminal velocity, including the forces |
| d | Streamlining reduces drag so that maximum velocity is increased; describe streamlining of a shark (an adaptation) and a car (a design feature) |

A **fluid** is a liquid or a gas, so drag acts on a boat in water and on a skydiver in air alike.

## The core idea: drag depends on speed, weight does not

Two forces act on a falling object: its weight, which stays the same throughout the fall, and drag, which grows as the speed grows. Everything in this section follows from that contrast.

At any moment:

```
resultant force = weight - drag
acceleration = resultant force / mass
```

As drag rises, the resultant force falls, so the acceleration falls. The object keeps speeding up, but by less each second, until drag equals weight. Then the resultant force is zero, the acceleration is zero, and the velocity stays constant: this is **terminal velocity**.

## Stage-by-stage force analysis

**Worked example 1: a hailstone.** A hailstone of mass 0.020 kg falls from rest through still air. Take g = 9.8 N/kg.

```
weight = 0.020 x 9.8 = 0.196 N
```

| Stage | Drag | Resultant force | Acceleration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Start of fall | 0 N | 0.196 N down | 0.196 / 0.020 = 9.8 m/s² |
| Partway | 0.120 N | 0.076 N down | 0.076 / 0.020 = 3.8 m/s² |
| Terminal velocity | 0.196 N | 0 N | 0 m/s² |

At the very start there is no drag, so the acceleration equals g. Halfway down the table the hailstone is still speeding up, but less quickly. At terminal velocity, drag has risen to 0.196 N: it equals the weight, not exceeds it.

## Velocity-time graphs for falling objects

Statement c requires you to **draw** and **interpret** these graphs. The shape follows from the force analysis:

- The line starts at the origin with its steepest gradient, because the acceleration is greatest when drag is zero.
- The gradient decreases smoothly as drag increases: the line curves over.
- The line becomes horizontal at terminal velocity.

The gradient of a velocity-time graph is the acceleration (section 3.1.3), so a decreasing gradient means decreasing acceleration, not decreasing velocity. A flat line means constant velocity, not that the object has stopped.

### The full skydive

A skydive with a parachute has five stages. Label each on your sketch with how drag compares with weight.

| Stage | Graph | Forces |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Leaving the aircraft | Steep, rising curve from the origin | Drag much less than weight |
| 2. Approaching first terminal velocity | Curve flattening | Drag increasing towards weight |
| 3. First terminal velocity | Horizontal at a high value | Drag equals weight |
| 4. Parachute opens | Steep downward curve | Drag much greater than weight: resultant force upwards, so the skydiver decelerates |
| 5. Second terminal velocity | Curve flattens to a lower horizontal line | Drag falls as speed falls, until drag equals weight again |

In stage 4 the skydiver is still moving downwards; the resultant force is upwards, so the velocity decreases. The skydiver does not move upwards.

## Why a parachute lowers terminal velocity

The parachute has a large area, so at any given speed it produces much more drag. Weight is unchanged, so drag reaches the value of the weight at a much **lower** speed. The new terminal velocity is low enough to land safely.

A complete explanation has three links: larger area → more drag at the same speed → drag equals weight at a lower speed.

## Streamlining and maximum velocity

A vehicle has a driving force instead of weight as its forward force, but the reasoning is identical. As a car speeds up, drag increases. When drag equals the maximum driving force the engine can provide, the resultant force is zero and the car cannot go any faster: that is its **maximum velocity** (top speed).

**Worked example 2: a car's top speed.** A car's engine can produce a maximum driving force of 4200 N. At its top speed, the drag is therefore 4200 N. The car is redesigned with a more streamlined body, so at that same speed the drag is now only 3500 N.

```
resultant force at the old top speed = 4200 - 3500 = 700 N forward
```

There is now a forward resultant force, so the car can accelerate beyond its old top speed. Drag rises again with speed until it reaches 4200 N at a new, higher maximum velocity. Streamlining has increased the maximum velocity without changing the engine.

### Shark and car

The specification asks you to describe two examples:

- **Shark (an adaptation):** a smooth, tapered body shape lets water flow past with little disturbance, so drag is lower and the shark can swim faster for the same effort.
- **Car (a design feature):** a smooth, curved body with a sloping front and windscreen lets air flow past smoothly, reducing drag and increasing maximum velocity.

Saying "it is streamlined" repeats the question. Describe the shape and say what it does to the flow and the drag.

## Linking to other sections

- At a steady speed, a vehicle's driving force balances the resistive forces (section 3.1.5). Terminal velocity is the falling-object version of this balance.
- Drag is a form of friction (section 3.1.1), so work done against drag transfers energy to the surroundings by heating (section 3.2.2).

## Common mistakes

- Saying drag becomes greater than weight at terminal velocity. It becomes equal.
- Saying the object "stops accelerating so it slows down". Zero acceleration means constant velocity.
- Saying a parachute "reduces gravity" or "reduces weight". It increases drag.
- Drawing the velocity-time graph as a straight line that suddenly flattens. The gradient decreases gradually.
- Saying the skydiver moves upwards when the parachute opens.
- Forgetting that a heavier object of the same shape needs more drag to balance its weight, so its terminal velocity is higher.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Explain** why an object reaches terminal velocity: name both forces, say how each changes, and state that the resultant force becomes zero.
- **Describe** a velocity-time graph: describe the gradient in each stage, then the force comparison.
- **Sketch**: label axes, show the correct curvature, and mark the horizontal sections clearly; values are not needed unless asked.
- **Compare** two objects: make a comparative statement, such as "the larger card has more drag at the same speed, so it reaches a lower terminal velocity".
