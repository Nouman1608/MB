Tort is section 3.3 of AQA A-level Law (7162) and carries 75 of the 100 marks on Paper 2 (2 hours),
alongside 25 marks on the nature of law and the English legal system. You must know the rules of
negligence, occupiers' liability, nuisance, Rylands v Fletcher and vicarious liability, apply them to
problem scenarios, and evaluate the policy behind them. AQA questions range from 1-mark multiple choice
to extended problem and evaluation answers.

Use these notes with the [Tort study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-tort/) and test yourself with the
[Tort practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-tort-practice/).

## How do you establish negligence for injury or property damage?

**1. Duty of care**
- Neighbour principle: Donoghue v Stevenson (1932).
- Caparo v Dickman (1990): (a) damage reasonably foreseeable, (b) proximity, (c) fair, just and reasonable.
- Robinson v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire (2018): use established precedent first; Caparo only for
  genuinely new situations, developed incrementally.

**2. Breach - the objective standard**
- Reasonable person in the defendant's position: Vaughan v Menlove (1837).
- Learner drivers judged as competent drivers (Nettleship v Weston, 1971); children as a reasonable
  child of that age (Mullin v Richards, 1998); professionals by a responsible body of opinion (Bolam,
  1957), which must withstand logical analysis (Bolitho, 1997).
- Risk factors raise or lower the standard: probability (Bolton v Stone, 1951); seriousness (Paris v
  Stepney BC, 1951); cost and practicality (Latimer v AEC, 1953); social utility (Watt v Hertfordshire
  CC, 1954); common practice and state of knowledge (Roe v Minister of Health, 1954).

**3. Damage**
- Factual causation, but for: Barnett v Chelsea and Kensington HMC (1969).
- Intervening act may break the chain (Knightley v Johns, 1982).
- Legal causation/remoteness: foreseeable type of harm (The Wagon Mound (No 1), 1961); manner need not be
  foreseen (Hughes v Lord Advocate, 1963); take your victim as you find them (Smith v Leech Brain, 1962).

## When is there liability for economic loss and psychiatric injury?

**Pure economic loss**
- Negligent acts: consequential loss is recoverable, pure economic loss is not (Spartan Steel v Martin,
  1973 - damaged melt and its profit recoverable, lost profit during the power cut not).
- Negligent misstatements: Hedley Byrne v Heller (1964) - special relationship, voluntary assumption of
  responsibility, reasonable reliance. Caparo (1990): auditors owed no duty to investors buying shares.
  Social occasions can count if the adviser knew the advice would be relied on (Chaudhry v Prabhakar,
  1988).

**Psychiatric injury** (must be a recognised psychiatric illness)
- Primary victims: in the zone of physical danger; foreseeability of physical injury is enough (Page v
  Smith, 1996).
- Secondary victims - Alcock v Chief Constable of South Yorkshire (1992) control mechanisms:
  1. Close tie of love and affection (presumed for parents, children, spouses).
  2. Proximity in time and space to the event or its immediate aftermath (McLoughlin v O'Brian, 1983).
  3. Perception with own unaided senses (not via television).
  4. Shock from a sudden event; Walters v North Glamorgan NHS Trust (2002) accepted a 36-hour "event".
- No special status for employees or rescuers (White v Chief Constable of South Yorkshire, 1999).
- Medical negligence cases: relatives who later witness a death from an earlier undiagnosed condition
  cannot recover (Paul v Royal Wolverhampton NHS Trust, Supreme Court, 2024).

## What do the Occupiers' Liability Acts require?

| | OLA 1957 (visitors) | OLA 1984 (trespassers) |
|---|---|---|
| Who | Invitees and licensees (s1(2)) | Persons other than visitors |
| Duty | Common duty of care to keep visitor reasonably safe (s2(2)) | Duty only if s1(3) satisfied: aware of danger, reasonable grounds to believe trespasser in vicinity, risk reasonably expected to be guarded against |
| Damage | Injury and property | Personal injury only (s1(8)) |
| Children | Be prepared for children to be less careful, s2(3)(a): Glasgow Corporation v Taylor (1922); Phipps v Rochester (1955) | Keown v Coventry Healthcare NHS Trust (2006): danger must be from the state of premises, not the child's activity |
| Specialists | Guard against risks of their calling, s2(3)(b): Roles v Nathan (1963) | - |
| Contractors | s2(4)(b): Haseldine v Daw (1941); Woodward v Mayor of Hastings (1945) | - |
| Warnings / consent | s2(4)(a); s2(5) | s1(5); s1(6) (Ratcliff v McConnell, 1999; Tomlinson v Congleton BC, 2003) |

Occupier = person with sufficient control (Wheat v E Lacon, 1966).

## Private nuisance and Rylands v Fletcher

**Private nuisance:** unlawful, indirect interference with the use or enjoyment of land.
- Claimant needs a proprietary interest (Hunter v Canary Wharf, 1997).
- Defendant: creator or occupier who adopts or continues it (Sedleigh-Denfield v O'Callaghan, 1940; Leakey
  v National Trust, 1980).
- Reasonableness: locality (Sturges v Bridgman, 1879); duration; abnormal sensitivity (Robinson v Kilvert,
  1889; Network Rail v Morris, 2004); malice (Christie v Davey, 1893; Hollywood Silver Fox Farm v Emmett,
  1936). Overlooking can be a nuisance where use of land is exceptional (Fearn v Tate Gallery, 2023).

**Rylands v Fletcher (1868):** defendant brings on to land something likely to do mischief if it
escapes; non-natural use (Rickards v Lothian, 1913; Transco v Stockport MBC, 2003, "extraordinary and
unusual"); escape (Read v J Lyons, 1947); foreseeable type of damage (Cambridge Water, 1994).

## How does vicarious liability work?

**Purpose:** a deep pocket and insurance; employers profit from employees' work and control the risk;
encourages careful selection and training.

**Stage 1 - relationship.**
- Control test (Yewens v Noakes, 1880); integration (Stevenson, Jordan and Harrison v MacDonald and Evans,
  1952); economic reality/multiple test (Ready Mixed Concrete v Minister of Pensions, 1968).
- Relationship "akin to employment" (Various Claimants v Catholic Child Welfare Society, 2012; Cox v
  Ministry of Justice, 2016). Genuine independent contractors excluded (Various Claimants v Barclays
  Bank, 2020).
- Lent employees: the general employer usually remains liable (Mersey Docks v Coggins and Griffith, 1947).

**Stage 2 - course of employment.**
- Authorised act done in an unauthorised way: Limpus v London General Omnibus (1862); Century Insurance
  v NI Road Transport Board (1942); Rose v Plenty (1976).
- "Frolic of his own": Hilton v Thomas Burton (1961); unauthorised driving by a conductor, Beard v London
  General Omnibus (1900).
- Close connection test for wrongful acts: Lister v Hesley Hall (2001); Mohamud v WM Morrison (2016); no
  liability where the employee pursues a personal vendetta (WM Morrison v Various Claimants, 2020).

## Which defences and remedies apply?

**Defences**
- Contributory negligence: Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act 1945; Froom v Butcher (1976)
  seat-belt reductions; Sayers v Harlow UDC (1957).
- Consent (volenti): knowledge plus free agreement (Smith v Baker, 1891; ICI v Shatwell, 1965; Morris v
  Murray, 1991); rescuers are not volunteers (Haynes v Harwood, 1935).
- Nuisance: prescription after 20 years; statutory authority (Allen v Gulf Oil, 1981). Coming to the
  nuisance and public benefit are not defences (Miller v Jackson, 1977; Coventry v Lawrence, 2014).
- Rylands: Act of God (Nichols v Marsland, 1876); act of a stranger (Rickards v Lothian, 1913); consent;
  claimant's default; statutory authority.

**Remedies**
- Compensatory damages put the claimant in the position as if the tort had not occurred: special
  damages (losses to trial) and general damages (pain, suffering, loss of amenity, future loss).
- Property damage: cost of repair or diminution in value. Economic loss: the lost money itself.
- Mitigation: the claimant cannot recover loss they could reasonably have avoided.
- Injunctions: discretionary, prohibitory or mandatory; balancing the claimant's rights against the
  defendant's and the public's interests (Kennaway v Thompson, 1981; Miller v Jackson, 1977); damages in
  lieu (Shelfer, 1895; Coventry v Lawrence, 2014).

## Common mistakes and exam traps

- Using Caparo where duty is already established (road users, doctor-patient, employer-employee).
- Applying the Alcock mechanisms to a primary victim.
- Forgetting that the 1984 Act gives no claim for damaged property.
- In vicarious liability, skipping stage 1 and jumping straight to "course of employment".
- In 30-mark problems, no conclusion: AQA mark schemes reward a reasoned conclusion on liability.

## Frequently asked questions

**Where is tort examined in AQA 7162?** On Paper 2, a 2-hour paper of 100 marks: 75 marks on tort and 25
marks on the nature of law and the English legal system.

**What does "theory of tort law" mean for AQA?** It asks you to evaluate the policy behind the rules: why
Caparo limits duty, why economic loss and psychiatric injury are restricted, how the objective standard
is set, how injunctions balance interests and why vicarious liability exists.

**Do I need recent Supreme Court cases?** They strengthen evaluation. Robinson (2018), Barclays and Morrison
(2020) and Paul (2024) show how the courts are refining duty and vicarious liability.
