## Section A

**1.** State whether each salt is soluble or insoluble in water: barium sulfate; ammonium carbonate; silver nitrate; copper(II) carbonate. **[4]**

**2.** Define a hydrated substance and an anhydrous substance. **[2]**

**3.** Write the formula of hydrated cobalt(II) chloride. **[1]**

**4.** When copper(II) chloride is made from copper(II) oxide and hydrochloric acid, the copper(II) oxide is added in excess. State why. **[1]**

**5.** Name the method you would use to prepare each salt: lead(II) chloride; sodium nitrate; zinc chloride from zinc carbonate. **[3]**

**6.** Which pair of aqueous solutions forms a precipitate when mixed?

A: sodium chloride and potassium nitrate

B: barium nitrate and potassium sulfate

C: ammonium chloride and sodium nitrate

D: copper(II) sulfate and hydrochloric acid **[1]**

## Section B

**7.** Describe how you would prepare pure, dry crystals of hydrated magnesium sulfate from magnesium carbonate and a suitable acid. **[6]**

**8.** Barium carbonate is an insoluble salt.

**(a)** Name **two** solutions that could be mixed to prepare it.

**(b)** Write the ionic equation, with state symbols, for its formation.

**(c)** Describe how a pure, dry sample is obtained after mixing. **[6]**

**9.** Potassium chloride is made from potassium hydroxide solution and hydrochloric acid.

**(a)** Explain why titration is used rather than adding an excess of a solid.

**(b)** Explain why the titration is repeated without indicator.

**(c)** State why no filtration is needed before crystallising. **[3]**

**10.** A student tries to make calcium sulfate by adding calcium carbonate lumps to dilute sulfuric acid. The fizzing soon stops even though both reactants remain. Explain why, and suggest a better method of preparing calcium sulfate. **[3]**

**11.** 2.86 g of hydrated sodium carbonate, Na₂CO₃•xH₂O, is heated to constant mass. 1.06 g of anhydrous sodium carbonate remains. Calculate the value of x. **[4]**

**12.** A student adds excess copper(II) oxide to 25.0 cm³ of 2.00 mol/dm³ sulfuric acid and obtains 10.0 g of CuSO₄•5H₂O crystals (Mr = 250).

CuO + H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + H₂O

Calculate the percentage yield. **[3]**

**13.** A student heats copper(II) sulfate solution until all the water has gone, and obtains a white powder instead of blue crystals. Explain this result. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Barium sulfate: insoluble [1]. Ammonium carbonate: soluble [1]. Silver nitrate: soluble [1]. Copper(II) carbonate: insoluble [1].

**2.** Hydrated: a substance that is chemically combined with water [1]. Anhydrous: a substance containing no water [1].

**3.** CoCl₂•6H₂O [1].

**4.** To make sure all of the acid reacts / is used up [1].

**5.** Lead(II) chloride: precipitation [1]. Sodium nitrate: titration [1]. Zinc chloride: acid + excess insoluble carbonate [1].

**6.** **B** [1].

**7.** Warm dilute sulfuric acid [1]. Add magnesium carbonate a little at a time until fizzing stops and solid remains (excess) [1]. Filter to remove the excess magnesium carbonate [1]. Evaporate the filtrate to the point of crystallisation [1]. Leave to cool so crystals form [1]. Filter off the crystals, wash with a little cold distilled water and dry between filter papers [1].

**8. (a)** Aqueous barium nitrate (or barium chloride) [1] and aqueous sodium carbonate (or potassium carbonate) [1].

**(b)** Ba²⁺(aq) + CO₃²⁻(aq) → BaCO₃(s) [1].

**(c)** Filter to collect the precipitate [1]; wash with distilled water to remove soluble impurities [1]; dry in a warm oven or between filter papers [1].

**9. (a)** Both reactants are soluble, so there is no excess solid that could be filtered off; the volumes must be matched exactly [1].

**(b)** The indicator would contaminate the salt [1].

**(c)** There is no unreacted solid to remove; the exact volumes leave only the salt solution [1].

**10.** Calcium sulfate is insoluble [1], so it forms a coating on the calcium carbonate lumps, which stops the acid reaching the carbonate [1]. Better method: precipitation, by mixing aqueous calcium nitrate (or chloride) with aqueous sodium sulfate, then filtering, washing and drying [1].

**11.** Mass of water = 2.86 − 1.06 = 1.80 g [1]. Moles of Na₂CO₃ = 1.06 ÷ 106 = 0.0100 mol [1]. Moles of H₂O = 1.80 ÷ 18 = 0.100 mol [1]. x = 0.100 ÷ 0.0100 = **10** [1].

**12.** Moles of H₂SO₄ = 2.00 × 0.0250 = 0.0500 mol, so 0.0500 mol of CuSO₄•5H₂O can form [1]. Theoretical mass = 0.0500 × 250 = 12.5 g [1]. Yield = 10.0 ÷ 12.5 × 100 = **80.0%** [1].

**13.** Heating to dryness drives off the water of crystallisation [1], leaving white anhydrous copper(II) sulfate instead of blue hydrated CuSO₄•5H₂O [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Question 1: calling ammonium carbonate insoluble because "carbonates are insoluble". Ammonium salts are always soluble.
- Question 5: choosing titration for zinc chloride. Zinc carbonate is insoluble, so excess solid is used.
- Question 7: using hydrochloric acid (this gives magnesium chloride), or missing "until fizzing stops".
- Question 8(a): naming barium sulfate or calcium carbonate as reagents. Both starting solutions must be soluble.
- Question 8(c): including evaporation. The salt is already solid.
- Question 10: saying the acid "ran out". Both reactants remain; the insoluble coating is the cause.
- Question 11: dividing 1.80 by 1.06 directly. Convert both masses to moles first.
- Question 12: using the Mr of anhydrous CuSO₄ (160) when the product is the hydrated salt.
