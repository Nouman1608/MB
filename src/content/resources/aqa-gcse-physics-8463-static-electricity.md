This guide covers one subtopic of AQA GCSE Physics (8463) in depth: **4.2.5 Static electricity**, made up of
4.2.5.1 Static charge and 4.2.5.2 Electric fields. This subtopic is in GCSE Physics only (it is not in Combined
Science) and is assessed on Paper 1 at both tiers. There are no equations; the marks come from explanations built
on one idea: **only electrons move**.

For quick recall, use the [static electricity revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-physics-8463-static-electricity-revision-notes/).
To test yourself, use the [static electricity practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-physics-8463-static-electricity-practice/).
The rest of section 4.2 is summarised in the [Electricity study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-physics-8463-electricity/).

## What the specification asks for

You must be able to:

- describe the production of static electricity, and sparking, by rubbing surfaces
- describe evidence that charged objects exert forces of attraction or repulsion on one another when not in
  contact
- explain how the transfer of electrons between objects explains static electricity
- draw the electric field pattern for an isolated charged sphere
- explain the concept of an electric field
- explain how the electric field explains the non-contact force between charged objects, and other electrostatic
  effects such as sparking.

## How rubbing charges an object

When certain **insulating** materials are rubbed against each other they become electrically charged. Friction
moves **negatively charged electrons** off one material and on to the other.

- The material that **gains electrons** becomes **negatively charged**.
- The material that **loses electrons** is left with an **equal positive charge**.

The positive charge is not something that has been added. The material always had equal amounts of positive and
negative charge; removing some electrons leaves the positive charge in the nuclei of its atoms no longer
balanced. Protons stay where they are, inside nuclei, and are never rubbed off.

**Why insulators?** In an insulator, the electrons that are transferred cannot move away through the material,
so the charge stays where it was rubbed on. That is what "static" means: the charge is not flowing.

**Worked example 1.** A student rubs a polythene rod with a woollen cloth. The rod becomes negatively charged.
Explain what has happened to the cloth.

```
The rod became negative, so it gained electrons.
Those electrons came from the cloth.
The cloth lost electrons, so it is left positively charged.
The cloth's positive charge is equal in size to the rod's negative charge.
```

The same reasoning works in reverse: if a rod becomes positive, it has **lost** electrons to the cloth.

## Forces between charged objects

When two charged objects are brought close together they exert a force on each other.

- Two objects with the **same type** of charge **repel**.
- Two objects with **different types** of charge **attract**.

These are **non-contact forces**: they act across a gap without the objects touching.

**Evidence.** Hang a charged rod from an insulating thread so it can turn freely. Bring a second rod, charged in
the same way, near one end without touching: the hanging rod turns away. Bring an oppositely charged rod near it:
the hanging rod turns towards it. Because the rods never touch, the movement shows that charged objects exert
forces on each other across a gap. Two charged balloons hung side by side and pushing apart show the same thing.

## Electric fields

A charged object creates an **electric field** around itself: a region in which another charged object
experiences a force.

- The field is **strongest close to** the charged object.
- The **further away**, the **weaker** the field.
- A second charged object placed in the field experiences a force, and that force **gets stronger as the distance
  between the objects decreases**.

**How the field explains the non-contact force.** The objects do not need to touch because each is in the field
of the other. The field reaches across the gap and it is the field that exerts the force. As the objects get
closer, each is in a stronger part of the other's field, so the force grows.

### Drawing the field of an isolated charged sphere

- Draw straight lines radiating from the surface, like spokes, evenly spaced all the way round.
- Each line meets the surface at right angles and points straight out from, or straight in towards, the centre.
- For a **positive** sphere the arrows point **away** from the sphere; for a **negative** sphere they point
  **towards** it.
- The arrow shows the direction of the force on a small positive charge placed there.
- The lines are closest together at the surface and spread out further away, which shows the field getting
  weaker with distance.

Never let field lines cross, start in empty space or curve round the sphere.

## Sparking

Sparking is explained in three steps.

1. Charge builds up on an insulated object, for example by repeated rubbing.
2. As the charge increases, the **pd between the object and a nearby earthed conductor** increases, and the
   **electric field** in the gap between them becomes stronger.
3. When the field is strong enough, electrons are pulled across the gap through the air. That sudden movement of
   electrons is the spark.

**Worked example 2.** After walking across a carpet in rubber-soled shoes, a person feels a small shock when
their finger nears a metal door handle connected to earth. Explain this using the specification's ideas.

> Rubbing between the shoes and the carpet transfers electrons, so the person becomes charged. The rubber soles
> are insulators, so the charge cannot flow away to earth. As the finger approaches the earthed handle, the
> electric field in the small gap becomes very strong. When it is strong enough, electrons jump across the gap:
> a spark, which the person feels as a small shock. The person is then discharged.

## Annotated model answer

**Question.** Explain why two balloons, both rubbed on the same cloth, hang apart from each other on their
threads. **[3]**

> Rubbing transfers electrons between each balloon and the cloth (1), so both balloons get the same type of
> charge (1). Objects with the same type of charge repel, so a non-contact force pushes the balloons apart (1).

*Why it scores:* the answer starts with electron transfer, links it to the **same type** of charge, then names
the force. Writing only "like charges repel" misses the first two marks.

## Common mistakes

- Saying protons or "positive charges" are rubbed off. Only electrons transfer.
- Saying the object losing electrons "gains positive charge from the cloth".
- Giving unequal amounts of charge to the rod and cloth: the charges are equal and opposite.
- Drawing field arrows into a positive sphere.
- Drawing curved or crossing field lines round an isolated sphere.
- Explaining a spark as "static escaping" without the strong field and electrons crossing the gap.
- Calling the force between charges a contact force.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** charging: name electrons, say which way they move, and state which object becomes negative and which
  positive.
- **Describe evidence** for forces: give an observation (the hanging rod moves) and say it happens without contact.
- **Draw** field patterns with a ruler, with arrows on every line.
- **Explain** sparking: build-up of charge, increasing pd and field, electrons cross the gap.
