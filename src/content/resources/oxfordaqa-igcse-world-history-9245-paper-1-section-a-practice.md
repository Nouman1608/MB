## Section B: exam-style source sets

### Set 1: Peacemaking and the League of Nations, c1919-1939

**Source A (pre-release style; invented):** an editorial in the style of a German newspaper, June
1919. "We were not invited to discuss the terms; we were summoned to sign them. The treaty takes our
colonies, cuts our army to a police force, hands the coal of the Saar to France, and makes us
declare that we alone caused the war."

**Source B (unseen; invented):** a speech in the style of a British politician, July 1919.
"Germany has been dealt with firmly but not destroyed. She keeps most of her lands and her
industry, and the League of Nations will correct, in time, whatever proves to be unjust."

**Source C (unseen; invented):** a report in the style of a British newspaper correspondent,
spring 1936. "Sanctions were declared with fine speeches at Geneva, yet oil still flows to Italy
and Italian ships carrying troops pass through the Suez Canal without hindrance."

**Source D (pre-release style; invented):** a description of a cartoon in the style of 1932. A
Japanese soldier strides through a door marked "Manchuria" while a small figure labelled "League"
sits reading a very long report.

**5.** Use Source C and your contextual knowledge to support the claim that the League's sanctions
against Italy were weak. **[4]**

**6.** Sources A and B give different views of the Treaty of Versailles. Explain one difference
between them, using the content of both sources and your own knowledge. **[6]**

**7.** How useful is Source C to a historian studying the Abyssinian crisis? Use the content and
provenance of the source and your contextual knowledge. **[8]**

**8.** "The main reason the League of Nations failed in the 1930s was the actions of Britain and
France." Using a range of the sources and your own knowledge, how far do you agree? **[12]**

### Set 2: Crises of the Cold War, 1960-1975

**Source E (pre-release style; invented):** a front page in the style of an American newspaper,
October 1962. "Photographs taken from American aircraft show missile bases being built in Cuba,
barely ninety miles from the coast of Florida."

**Source F (unseen; invented):** a statement in the style of the Soviet government, October 1962.
"The weapons sent to Cuba are defensive. They were sent at the request of a small nation that has
already been invaded once from American soil and lives under constant threat."

**Source G (unseen; invented):** a letter in the style of an American university student, May 1970.
"They sent soldiers onto a college campus and four students are dead. My father says we must
support the President. I can't support a war that is now being fought against us at home."

**Source H (pre-release style; invented):** a description of a news photograph in the style of
1968. American soldiers walk along a path past burning village huts in South Vietnam; an elderly
woman sits on the ground watching them.

**9.** Use Source F and your contextual knowledge to support the claim that the USSR presented
itself as Cuba's protector in 1962. **[4]**

**10.** Sources E and F give different views of the purpose of the missiles in Cuba. Explain one
difference between them, using the content of both sources and your own knowledge. **[6]**

**11.** How useful is Source G to a historian studying opposition in the USA to the Vietnam War?
Use the content and provenance of the source and your contextual knowledge. **[8]**

**12.** "The main reason the USA withdrew from Vietnam was opposition to the war at home." Using a
range of the sources and your own knowledge, how far do you agree? **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** Candidates may not bring any notes or annotations, including annotated pre-release material
[1]. A clean-copy source insert containing all five sources is provided [1].

**2.** Provenance is the origin of a source: who produced it, when and why [1]. Any two aspects, [1]
each: the author and their position; the date; the type of source; the intended audience; the
purpose.

**3.** The 6-mark question asks you to explain a difference between two sources, one unseen and one
pre-released, using both [1]. The 8-mark question asks you to evaluate how useful one unseen source
is for studying a given issue, using its content, provenance and your knowledge [1].

**4.** Evaluating limitations (how the source is not useful) is not required [1]. Focus on what the
content and provenance can show a historian about the issue, supported by contextual knowledge [1].

**5.** Source detail identified [1]: oil still reached Italy and its troop ships used the Suez
Canal. Explained as support [1]: the two things Italy most needed to fight were not blocked, so
sanctions could not stop the war. Contextual knowledge [1]: sanctions excluded oil, and Britain and
France kept the canal open; in December 1935 they negotiated the secret Hoare-Laval Pact to give
Italy much of Abyssinia. Linked to the claim [1]: this shows the leading members were unwilling to
make sanctions effective.

**6.** Difference stated [2]: Source A presents the treaty as a harsh, dictated peace, while Source B
presents it as firm but fair and open to correction. Support from both sources [2]: A says Germany
was "summoned to sign" and lists losses of colonies, army and Saar coal; B says Germany "keeps most
of her lands and her industry". Explanation using knowledge [2]: Source A reflects German anger at
the *Diktat*, the war guilt clause (Article 231) and reparations, while Source B reflects a British
view; Lloyd George wanted Germany punished but able to recover and trade, and the League was meant
to settle future disputes.

**7.** Content useful [2]: it shows that sanctions were announced but did not stop oil or troop
transport, which explains why Italy could continue the invasion. Knowledge supporting content [2]:
Italy invaded in October 1935; sanctions did not include oil; the Suez Canal stayed open; Italy
captured Addis Ababa in May 1936. Provenance useful [2]: a newspaper correspondent writing in spring
1936 reports as events unfolded, so the source shows how the failure appeared to contemporaries and
that the public knew sanctions were not working. Knowledge on provenance [2]: British opinion had
supported the League in the 1935 Peace Ballot, and news of the Hoare-Laval Pact in December 1935
caused outrage, so a critical report like this reflects that public mood.

**8.** Sources used and linked [4], at least three sources for full credit: Source C supports the
claim, since Britain and France let oil flow and kept the canal open; Source D can support the
claim indirectly, since the League's slow report on Manchuria reflected the reluctance of its
leading members to act; Source A suggests a different cause, German resentment of Versailles,
which Hitler later exploited; Source B shows the League was expected to fix problems it lacked the
power to solve. Own knowledge [4]: Hoare-Laval Pact; non-intervention in Spain; the USA's absence;
the Depression, which made members unwilling to impose costly sanctions; Japan and Germany leaving
in 1933; the League's lack of an army. Balanced analysis [2]: weighs Britain and France's choices
against structural weaknesses and the Depression. Judgement [2]: for example, Britain and France's
actions were decisive in Abyssinia, but the League's weaknesses and the Depression meant it was
already unlikely to succeed.

**9.** Source detail identified [1]: it calls the weapons "defensive", sent "at the request" of
Cuba. Explained as support [1]: this presents the USSR as helping a small nation rather than
threatening the USA. Contextual knowledge [1]: the CIA-backed Bay of Pigs invasion of April 1961
had failed, so Cuba feared a second US attack. Linked to the claim [1]: the source's reference to
an invasion "from American soil" uses that fear to justify Soviet action.

**10.** Difference stated [2]: Source E presents the missiles as a threat to the USA, while Source F
presents them as defensive protection for Cuba. Support from both sources [2]: E stresses bases
"barely ninety miles" from Florida; F stresses Cuba's "request" and the threat of invasion.
Explanation using knowledge [2]: E reflects the US view after U2 photographs revealed the sites on
14 October 1962, when Kennedy treated the missiles as a direct danger; F reflects Soviet
propaganda, as Khrushchev also wanted to close the missile gap and answer US missiles in Turkey.

**11.** Content useful [2]: it shows anger at the killing of four students and a generational
divide over the war. Knowledge supporting content [2]: the National Guard shot students at Kent
State University in May 1970 during protests against Nixon's widening of the war into Cambodia;
protests had grown since the late 1960s. Provenance useful [2]: a student's private letter in May
1970 shows the personal reaction of one of the groups most active in opposition, written
immediately after the event. Knowledge on provenance [2]: universities were centres of anti-war
protest and the draft affected young men directly, so a student's view is typical of the movement
the historian is studying.

**12.** Sources used and linked [4], at least three sources for full credit: Source G supports the
claim, showing protest spreading after Kent State; Source H shows images of the war's brutality that
helped turn opinion; Sources E and F show the wider Cold War, reminding the historian that
containment remained US policy, so withdrawal needed other reasons too. Own knowledge [4]: the Tet
Offensive (1968) undermined claims that the war was being won; My Lai; television coverage; the
draft; rising casualties and cost; the Viet Cong's guerrilla tactics; Vietnamisation; Nixon's
visit to China in 1972, which reduced the importance of Vietnam to containment; the Paris Peace
Accords (1973). Balanced analysis [2]: weighs opposition at home against military failure and
changing Cold War diplomacy. Judgement [2]: for example, opposition at home made withdrawal
politically necessary, but it grew because the war could not be won militarily.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using a source without linking it to the question's claim or issue.
- Describing two sources separately in the 6-mark question.
- Dismissing a source as biased instead of explaining its usefulness.
- In 12-mark answers, too few sources or no final judgement.
