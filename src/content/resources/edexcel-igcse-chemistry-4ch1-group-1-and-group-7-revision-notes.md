## The two trends in one line each

- **Group 1:** reactivity **increases** down the group (Li < Na < K).
- **Group 7:** reactivity **decreases** down the group (Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂).

Learn them together. Same causes, opposite directions.

## 2.1 Group 1 as a family

Similar reactions with water show Li, Na and K belong together.

| Shared observation | Why |
|---|---|
| Floats | Less dense than water |
| Fizzes | Hydrogen gas given off |
| Moves around | Pushed by the hydrogen |
| Gets smaller, disappears | Metal is used up |
| Indicator turns blue/purple | Alkaline hydroxide forms |

```
General:  alkali metal + water → metal hydroxide + hydrogen
Pattern:  2M(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2MOH(aq) + H₂(g)
```

Every Group 1 metal forms a 1+ ion, so the hydroxide is always MOH.

## 2.2 Evidence for the Group 1 trend

| | Lithium | Sodium | Potassium |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water | Fizzes steadily, stays solid | Fizzes rapidly, melts into a ball | Violent; hydrogen ignites, lilac flame |
| Air (cut surface) | Tarnishes slowly | Tarnishes quickly | Tarnishes almost at once |

- Tarnishing = an oxide layer forming on the shiny surface.
- All three are stored under oil to keep out air and water vapour.
- Burning in air gives the oxide, e.g. `4Na(s) + O₂(g) → 2Na₂O(s)`.

**Method: answering "give evidence for the trend"**

1. Name the reaction (water or air).
2. Give a comparative observation for at least two metals ("potassium fizzes faster than lithium").
3. Link it: faster reaction = more reactive.

## 2.3 Predicting other alkali metals

Continue the trend, and say its direction first.

| Property | Trend down the group | Rubidium/caesium prediction |
|---|---|---|
| Reactivity | Increases | Explosive with water |
| Melting point | Decreases (Li 181 °C → K 63 °C) | Lower than potassium (Rb 39 °C, Cs 28 °C) |
| Hardness | Softer | Very soft |
| Density | Generally increases (K is an exception) | Rb and Cs are denser than water and sink |
| Formulae | Same 1+ ion | RbOH, CsCl, Rb₂O |

Products and formulae follow the family pattern exactly. Physical trends are general, not perfect.

## 2.4C Explaining the Group 1 trend (Paper 2 only)

Configurations: Li 2.1, Na 2.8.1, K 2.8.8.1.

**Four-link chain (learn it word for word)**

1. More occupied shells, so the outer electron is **further from the nucleus**.
2. More inner shells, so **more shielding**.
3. So **weaker attraction** between nucleus and outer electron.
4. So the outer electron is **lost more easily**: more reactive.

Key equation: `K → K⁺ + e⁻`

## 2.5 Halogen physical properties

All are diatomic: Cl₂, Br₂, I₂.

| Halogen | Room-temperature appearance | Boiling point (°C) |
|---|---|---|
| Chlorine | Pale green (yellow-green) gas | −34 |
| Bromine | Red-brown liquid; orange-brown vapour | 59 |
| Iodine | Grey-black solid; purple vapour | 184 |

Down the group:

- colour darkens
- gas → liquid → solid
- melting and boiling points increase (bigger molecules, stronger intermolecular forces; point 1.48)

## 2.7 Displacement: the evidence for the Group 7 trend

**Rule:** a more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive halogen from a solution of its halide.

Colours in solution: chlorine very pale green; bromine orange (yellow if dilute); iodine brown. Potassium and sodium halide solutions are colourless, so a colour that appears after mixing comes from the halogen produced. Where there is no reaction, the mixture simply keeps the colour of the halogen water you added.

| Added ↓ / Solution → | KCl | KBr | KI |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cl₂ | — | Orange | Brown |
| Br₂ | ✗ | — | Brown |
| I₂ | ✗ | ✗ | — |

Two reactions for chlorine, one for bromine, none for iodine, so Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂.

**Method: writing a displacement equation**

1. Check the halogen added is above the halide's halogen. If not, write "no reaction".
2. Swap: the added halogen becomes the halide ion; the halide becomes the halogen (diatomic).
3. Balance: one X₂ needs two halide ions.
4. Ionic form: delete the metal ion (spectator).

```
Full:   Br₂(aq) + 2NaI(aq) → 2NaBr(aq) + I₂(aq)
Ionic:  Br₂(aq) + 2I⁻(aq) → 2Br⁻(aq) + I₂(aq)
```

**Worked reminder: identifying an unknown halide**

```
Solution Z + chlorine water → turns brown
Solution Z + bromine water  → turns brown
Brown = iodine formed, and both Cl₂ and Br₂ displaced it.
So Z contains iodide ions (e.g. potassium iodide).
```

If chlorine had given orange and bromine no change, Z would contain bromide ions.

In electron terms, the halogen gains electrons (reduced, the oxidising agent) and the halide ion loses electrons (oxidised). These terms come from point 2.20.

## 2.8C Explaining the Group 7 trend (Paper 2 only)

Halogens have 7 outer electrons (Cl 2.8.7) and react by **gaining** one electron: `Br₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Br⁻`.

**Four-link chain**

1. Down the group the outer shell is **further from the nucleus**.
2. **More shielding** by inner shells.
3. **Weaker attraction** for an incoming electron.
4. Electron **gained less easily**: less reactive.

## Must-know distinctions

| Group 1 | Group 7 |
|---|---|
| Metals | Non-metals |
| Lose 1 electron → 1+ ion | Gain 1 electron → 1− ion |
| Reactivity increases down | Reactivity decreases down |
| Melting point decreases down | Melting/boiling point increases down |
| Evidence: water and air | Evidence: displacement |

Also keep apart: **halogen** (the element, Cl₂) and **halide** (the ion, Cl⁻, or its compound, KCl).

## Quick self-test

1. State what you would see if universal indicator is added to the water after lithium has reacted.
2. Write the formula of caesium hydroxide and of rubidium oxide.
3. Give one observation that shows sodium is more reactive than lithium in water.
4. Why are alkali metals stored under oil?
5. Rubidium has a density of 1.53 g/cm³. Predict whether it floats on water.
6. Bromine is added to sodium chloride solution. What do you observe, and why?
7. Write the ionic equation for chlorine reacting with bromide ions.
8. Name the substance that is oxidised in question 7.
9. Predict the colour and state of fluorine at room temperature.
10. Iodine and astatide ions: predict whether a reaction happens.
11. (Paper 2 only) Lithium is 2.1 and potassium is 2.8.8.1. In one sentence, why is potassium's outer electron lost more easily?
12. (Paper 2 only) Explain why chlorine attracts an extra electron more strongly than iodine.

### Answers

1. It turns blue or purple: lithium hydroxide is an alkali.
2. CsOH; Rb₂O.
3. Any one: sodium fizzes faster; sodium melts into a ball while lithium stays solid; sodium moves around faster.
4. To stop them reacting with oxygen and water vapour in the air.
5. It sinks (1.53 g/cm³ is greater than water's 1.00 g/cm³).
6. No change (stays orange). Bromine is less reactive than chlorine, so it cannot displace chlorine from chloride ions.
7. `Cl₂(aq) + 2Br⁻(aq) → 2Cl⁻(aq) + Br₂(aq)`
8. The bromide ions (they lose electrons).
9. A pale yellow gas (paler than chlorine; boiling point below chlorine's).
10. Yes: iodine is more reactive than astatine, so it displaces astatine.
11. It is further from the nucleus with more shielding, so it is attracted less strongly.
12. Chlorine's outer shell is closer to the nucleus with less shielding, so the attraction for an incoming electron is stronger.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "halogens lose electrons" in a 2.8C explanation. They gain electrons.
- Stopping the explanation chain at "more shells", without "weaker attraction" and "lost/gained more easily".
- Saying "more protons, so stronger attraction" for the lower element. Distance and shielding outweigh the extra protons.
- Giving iodine solid as purple. The solid is grey-black.
- Describing bromine as a gas, or as "brown gas". It is a red-brown liquid.
- Writing Cl instead of Cl₂ in equations, or forgetting the 2 in front of the halide.
- Saying bromine water "goes clear" or "decolourises" in displacement. That is the alkene test; here a new colour appears.
- Quoting an observation for only one metal when the question asks for a comparison.
- Using "fizzes" and "bubbles" as two separate observations.
- Stating a trend without its direction ("melting point changes down the group").

## Frequently asked questions

**How does reactivity change down Group 1 and Group 7?**
Reactivity increases down Group 1 (lithium, sodium, potassium) but decreases down Group 7 (chlorine, bromine, iodine). The causes are the same, increasing distance and shielding, but Group 1 metals lose electrons while halogens gain them.

**What is a halogen displacement reaction?**
A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive halogen from a solution of its halide. Chlorine displaces bromide and iodide, bromine displaces only iodide, and iodine displaces neither, which shows the order Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂.

**What is the difference between a halogen and a halide?**
A halogen is the element, such as Cl₂, while a halide is the ion, such as Cl⁻, or a compound containing it, such as KCl. Mixing the two words up is a common source of lost marks.

**Why are alkali metals stored under oil?**
Lithium, sodium and potassium react with oxygen and water vapour in the air, so they are stored under oil to keep these out. A freshly cut surface tarnishes as an oxide layer forms, faster further down the group.
