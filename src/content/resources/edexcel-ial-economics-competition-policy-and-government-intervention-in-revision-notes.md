## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Market power | Ability of a firm to set price above marginal cost |
| Allocative efficiency | Price equals marginal cost |
| Productive efficiency | Output at the lowest average cost |
| Natural monopoly | One firm serves the market at lower average cost than two could |
| Contestable market | Entry and exit are cheap (low sunk costs), so rivals can threaten a firm |
| Monopsony | A single (or dominant) buyer |
| Privatisation | Transfer of state-owned assets to private owners |
| Nationalisation | Transfer of private assets to state ownership |
| FDI | Foreign direct investment: a firm from one country setting up or buying operations in another |
| Regulatory capture | The regulator comes to act in the interests of the industry it regulates |

## The measures in one table (1b to 1d)

| Group | Measure | How it works | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Control monopolies and mergers | Price regulation | Cap on price, often inflation minus X | Cap too low harms quality and investment |
| | Profit regulation | Limit on rate of return | Firm may overstate capital |
| | Quality standards | Minimum service levels | Higher costs |
| | Performance targets | Rewards and penalties | Targets can be gamed |
| | Referral to regulators | Expert investigation and remedies | Slow, costly |
| | Merger legislation | Review, block or condition deals | Judging future effects is hard |
| Promote competition | Grants, tax incentives | Lower entry costs for small firms and FDI | Cost to taxpayers |
| | Deregulation | Remove entry limits | Standards may fall |
| | Privatisation | Profit motive | Private monopoly |
| | Competitive tendering | Firms bid for public contracts | Quality risk if price only |
| | Trade liberalisation | Remove tariffs and quotas | Job losses in weak sectors |
| Protect suppliers and employees | Local sourcing | Buy a share at home | Higher costs |
| | Employment legislation | Rules on safety, hours, dismissal | Labour costs rise |
| | Entry barriers for foreign firms | Quotas, licences | Less choice, higher prices |
| | Monopsony limits | Minimum buying price or code | Needs enforcement |
| | Nationalisation | State ownership | Cost and weak incentives |

## Method in steps: evaluate a measure

1. Name the problem (market power, high barriers, weak supplier or worker).
2. State the measure and how it works on price, cost or entry.
3. Trace the effect on price, profit, efficiency, quality and choice.
4. Say who gains and who loses.
5. Apply a limit from 1f (capture, information gaps, resources, power).
6. Judge, with a condition: "It works if... because...".

## Small worked reminders

**Monopoly welfare loss (demand P = 100 - Q, marginal cost 10, fixed cost 800).** Marginal revenue 100 - 2Q = 10 gives Q = 45 and P = 55. Efficient output is 90. Loss = 0.5 x 45 x 45 = 1,012.5 cu.

**Average-cost cap.** Solve 100 - Q = 10 + 800/Q: Q = 80, P = 20, profit zero, loss 50 cu. A cap at marginal cost (10) leaves a 800 cu loss.

**Price cap with X.** Inflation 4%, X = 1.5%: the cap rises 2.5%. The firm gains only by cutting costs faster.

**Merger test.** Compare the cost saving with the price rise. Competitive price 40 and cost 40, then a merged firm with cost 30 sets 65 and sells 35: consumer surplus falls from 1,800 to 612.5, while total surplus rises from 1,800 to 1,837.5.

**Monopsony.** Supply P = 4 + 0.2Q, marginal revenue product 40 - 0.2Q. Competition: 90 units at 22. Monopsony: 60 units at 16. A floor at 18 gives 70 units.

**Contestability.** Payback = sunk cost / annual profit. A grant cuts sunk cost and shortens payback.

## Impacts (1e) in one table

| Measure | Price | Profit | Efficiency | Quality | Choice |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Price or profit regulation | Down | Down | Allocative up | Risk of fall | Little change |
| Quality standards, targets | May rise | Down | Mixed | Up | May fall |
| Merger control | Lower than if unchecked | Lower | Blocks dominance, may lose savings | Protects | Protected |
| Grants, deregulation, liberalisation | Down | Down for incumbents | Up | Often up | Up |
| Privatisation | Depends on competition | Up | Often up | Mixed | Up if rivals follow |
| Local sourcing, entry barriers | Up | Up for protected firms | Down | Mixed | Down |
| Monopsony limits | Higher for suppliers | Down for buyer | Closer to competitive | Little change | Little change |
| Nationalisation | Set by state | Often down | Mixed | Mixed | May fall |

Every arrow needs a reason. For example, profit falls under a cap because price falls toward average cost, while quality may fall because the firm cuts costs to protect its margin.

## Evaluation toolkit

- **Size:** is the cap or grant large enough to matter?
- **Time:** entry, tendering and liberalisation take years; a cap acts at once.
- **Information:** does the regulator know the true costs?
- **Winners and losers:** consumers, workers, local suppliers, taxpayers and the firm often gain and lose in different ways.
- **Alternative:** compare with no action and with a different tool.

## Must-know distinctions

- Privatisation changes ownership; deregulation removes rules. A privatised firm can still be tightly regulated.
- Price regulation (sets price) versus profit regulation (sets return on capital).
- Allocative efficiency (price = marginal cost) versus productive efficiency (lowest average cost).
- Protecting suppliers and workers versus promoting competition: one can reduce the other, since barriers to foreign firms cut rivalry.
- Total surplus versus consumer surplus when judging a merger.
- A limit on intervention weakens a policy; it does not show that doing nothing is better. Compare both outcomes.

## Limits to intervention (1f)

| Limit | One-line chain |
|---|---|
| Regulatory capture | Regulator depends on firm, caps set loosely, firm keeps excess profit |
| Information gaps | Firm knows costs better, reports high, cap too generous |
| Inadequate resources | Low detection, expected fine below gain, rule-breaking pays |
| Lack of regulatory power | Cannot compel data or fine, firm ignores the regulator |

## Quick self-test

1. Define a contestable market and name one measure that makes a market more contestable.
2. Last year's regulated price was 40 cu. Inflation is 3% and X is 2%. Calculate the new cap, and say what happens to the real price.
3. A utility has capital of 2,000 cu and profit of 260 cu. The allowed return is 10%. Calculate the actual return and the excess profit.
4. A council's in-house cost for a service is 80 cu. The winning bid is 68 cu. Calculate the saving in cu and in percent.
5. A good has a world price of 25 cu and a 40% tariff. Calculate the domestic price and the percentage fall when the tariff is removed.
6. A firm needs 500 units of a component. Imports cost 6 cu and local supply costs 9 cu. A rule requires 30% to be local. Calculate the extra cost, in cu and percent.
7. Define monopsony and give one restriction on monopsony power.
8. A cartel gains 120 cu. The probability of detection is 0.3. Calculate the smallest fine that makes the expected penalty exceed the gain.
9. A start-up has sunk costs of 80 cu and annual profit of 20 cu. A grant covers 25% of the sunk costs. Calculate payback before and after.
10. The state sells a firm for 500 cu. It earned 20 cu a year from the firm. It can borrow at 6%. Is the state better or worse off each year, and by how much?
11. Name two measures that tend to raise price and reduce choice.
12. Explain one way regulatory capture reduces the effectiveness of a price cap.

### Answers

1. A market where entry and exit are cheap because sunk costs are low. Examples: grants for small firms, deregulation or trade liberalisation.
2. 40 x (1 + 0.03 - 0.02) = 40.4 cu. Inflation is 3% and the price rises 1%, so the real price falls by about 1.9%.
3. 260/2,000 = 13%. Permitted profit is 200, so excess profit is 60 cu.
4. 80 - 68 = 12 cu, which is 15%.
5. Domestic price 25 x 1.4 = 35 cu. Removal gives a fall of 10/35 = 28.6%.
6. Cost = 350 x 6 + 150 x 9 = 3,450 against 3,000, so 450 cu or 15% more.
7. A single or dominant buyer. Restriction: a minimum buying price, or a code of conduct on payment terms.
8. Fine x 0.3 > 120, so the fine must exceed 400 cu.
9. Before: 80/20 = 4 years. Grant = 20 cu, so (80 - 20)/20 = 3 years.
10. Interest saved = 6% x 500 = 30 cu. The state loses 20 of income, so it is 10 cu a year better off, before any efficiency change.
11. Local sourcing rules and barriers to the entry of foreign firms (also quotas).
12. The regulator relies on the firm's data and staff links, so it sets the cap too high. The firm keeps excess profit and has little pressure to cut costs.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the same answer for every measure ("it lowers price") with no mechanism.
- Setting a regulated price at marginal cost for a natural monopoly without noting the loss.
- Forgetting to compare the cost saving of a merger with its price effect.
- Treating privatisation and deregulation as the same thing.
- Writing a list of limits without applying one to the case.
- Ignoring time: entry, tendering and trade liberalisation take years to work.
- No judgement: evaluation stops at "it depends" without saying on what.
- Quoting the right percentage with the wrong base, such as dividing by the new price instead of the old.
