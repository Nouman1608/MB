This guide covers **3.2.3 Economic performance** in AQA A-level Economics (7136). Its four parts are 3.2.3.1
Economic growth and the economic cycle, 3.2.3.2 Employment and unemployment, 3.2.3.3 Inflation and deflation, and
3.2.3.4 Possible conflicts between macroeconomic policy objectives.

Each part has its own guide on the site, for example [economic growth and the economic cycle](/resources/aqa-a-level-economics-economic-growth-and-the-economic-cycle/)
and [employment and unemployment](/resources/aqa-a-level-economics-employment-and-unemployment/). This page
connects them through one idea the specification returns to repeatedly: **the output gap**. Whether real GDP is
above or below potential output links the phase of the cycle, the type of unemployment, the pressure on prices and
the policy conflicts. All countries and figures are invented. Revise with the
[revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-economics-7136-economic-performance-revision-notes/)
and practise with the [practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-economics-7136-economic-performance-practice/).

## Short-run and long-run growth

**Short-run growth** is an increase in real GDP using existing capacity: a movement from inside the production
possibility boundary towards it, or a rightward shift of AD with spare capacity. **Long-run growth** is an increase
in productive capacity: an outward shift of the boundary or of LRAS. The specification describes long-run growth
as "the trend rate of growth of real national output in an economy over time".

- **Demand-side determinants of short-run growth:** consumption, investment, government spending, net exports.
- **Supply-side determinants of the trend rate:** investment in capital, labour force growth, education and
  skills, technology, productivity, enterprise.

**Costs and benefits.** Growth can raise living standards, employment and tax revenue for public services. It can
also bring pollution, depletion of resources, greater inequality and, if it outruns capacity, inflation and a
wider current account deficit. Growth is **sustainable** when it can continue without damaging the ability of
future generations to meet their needs; an evaluation should ask what kind of growth is occurring.

## The output gap

**Output gap = (actual real GDP − potential real GDP) ÷ potential real GDP × 100**

**Worked example (invented).** Country P's potential output is 2,000 billion.

- If actual output is 1,960 billion, the gap is −40 ÷ 2,000 = **−2.0%**: a **negative output gap**, real GDP
  below productive potential.
- If actual output is 2,050 billion, the gap is +50 ÷ 2,000 = **+2.5%**: a **positive output gap**, real GDP
  above productive potential, achieved by working factors harder than is normal (overtime, deferred
  maintenance).

Potential output cannot be observed directly; it has to be estimated, so measured output gaps are uncertain and
often revised. That is an evaluation point when an extract quotes one.

## Reading the cycle from indicators

The specification asks you to use real GDP, inflation, unemployment and investment to identify phases of the
economic cycle.

| Phase | Real GDP growth | Output gap | Unemployment | Inflation | Investment |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Boom | above trend | positive and widening | low, falling | rising | strong |
| Downturn | slowing, below trend | narrowing | starting to rise | still high, then easing | falling |
| Recession | negative | negative and widening | rising | falling | weak |
| Recovery | positive, rising | negative but narrowing | high, then falling | low | picking up |

Unemployment usually **lags** output: firms hesitate to hire until they are confident the recovery will last, and
hold on to trained staff early in a downturn.

**Causes of cyclical instability** named in the specification: excessive growth in credit and debt, asset price
bubbles, destabilising speculation, and "animal spirits" or herding, where optimism or pessimism spreads and
drives spending beyond what fundamentals justify. Both domestic and global, demand-side and supply-side shocks can
change the phase.

## Unemployment: measures and types

**Measures.** The **claimant count** counts people claiming unemployment-related benefits. The **Labour Force
Survey** measure uses a survey and the international definition: people without a job who have actively looked
for work in the past four weeks and are available to start in the next two weeks. The survey measure is usually
higher, because some unemployed people are not eligible for, or do not claim, benefits.

| Type | Cause | Link to the output gap | Fitting policy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cyclical (demand-deficient) | Too little AD | Negative output gap | Raise AD |
| Frictional | Time spent moving between jobs | Exists even at potential | Better job-matching information |
| Structural | Mismatch of skills or location after industries decline | Affects LRAS | Training, mobility support |
| Seasonal | Regular changes in demand through the year | Not cycle-related | Diversifying local industry |
| Real wage | Real wages held above the market-clearing level | Labour market disequilibrium | Flexible wage setting |

**Voluntary unemployment** is when people choose not to accept work at the going wage; **involuntary
unemployment** is when people willing to work at the going wage cannot find a job.

The **natural rate of unemployment** is the rate when the labour market is in equilibrium, made up of frictional and
structural unemployment. Supply-side factors such as skills, mobility and benefit rules determine it, so it
cannot be reduced in the long run by raising AD.

## Inflation: causes and the quantity theory

- **Demand-pull:** AD grows faster than capacity, typically with a positive output gap.
- **Cost-push:** rising costs, such as wages or world commodity prices, shift SRAS left.
- **Expectations:** if workers and firms expect higher inflation, they raise wages and prices in advance, so
  inflation becomes self-reinforcing.
- **Imported inflation:** changes in world commodity prices and in other economies' prices feed into domestic
  costs.

**Fisher's equation of exchange: MV = PQ**, where M is the money supply, V the velocity of circulation, P the
price level and Q real output. Using Q makes PQ nominal national income.

**Worked example (invented).** M = 500, V = 4, Q = 1,000. Then PQ = 2,000 and **P = 2.0**.

- If M rises 10% to 550 with V and Q unchanged, P = 2,200 ÷ 1,000 = **2.2**: a 10% rise in prices.
- If Q also grows 3% to 1,030, P = 2,200 ÷ 1,030 ≈ **2.14**, a rise of about **6.8%**.

The **Quantity Theory of Money**, central to the monetarist model, assumes V and Q are stable or independent of
M in the long run, so increases in the money supply mainly raise prices. Critics argue that V can change sharply,
for example when people hoard money in a crisis.

**Inflation, deflation and disinflation.** Deflation is a falling price level; disinflation is a falling rate of
inflation. The specification also warns that **deflationary policies** reduce AD but do not necessarily cause
deflation.

## Conflicts and the Phillips curves

**Short-run Phillips curve.** When the output gap is positive and unemployment is low, firms compete for workers,
wages rise faster and inflation increases; when the gap is negative, the reverse. The curve shows this short-run
trade-off between unemployment and inflation.

**Long-run (L-shaped) Phillips curve.** Suppose the government pushes unemployment below the natural rate by
raising AD. Inflation rises; workers come to expect it and demand higher money wages; real wages return to
their old level and unemployment drifts back to the natural rate, now with higher inflation. Repeat this and the
long-run curve is **vertical at the natural rate**: in the long run there is no trade-off.

**Policy implications.** Demand management can move the economy along the short-run curve but cannot hold
unemployment below the natural rate without accelerating inflation. Supply-side policies that reduce the natural
rate shift the long-run curve left. This underpins the **monetarist and supply-side view** in the specification
that the major objectives are compatible in the long run, even though they can conflict in the short run.

## Common errors

- Equating short-run growth with long-run growth.
- Treating a negative output gap as negative growth; real GDP can grow while still below potential.
- Assuming the claimant count and survey measures should agree.
- Saying deflation means inflation is falling.
- Calculating P without keeping V and Q fixed when the question says they are constant.
- Drawing the long-run Phillips curve as downward sloping.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate:** output gaps as a percentage of potential; P from MV = PQ; unemployment rates from labour force data.
- **Explain:** link each type of unemployment or inflation to its cause, then to the output gap.
- **Evaluate:** judge which cause fits the data in the extract, since the right policy depends on the cause, and
  consider uncertainty about potential output.
