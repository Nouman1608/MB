These questions cover section 7.3, *Globalisation, power and politics*, of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Sociology (9699) syllabus for 2024–2026, assessed in Paper 4 Section A. Paper 4 sets only extended essays; Section A builds the concepts those essays need, and Section B practises paragraphs and essays. All countries, movements and organisations in the items are fictional.

Revise first with the [globalisation, power and politics study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-globalisation-power-and-politics/) and the [globalisation, power and politics revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-globalisation-power-and-politics-revision-notes/).

## Section A – Concept questions

**1.** Define "liberal democracy". **[2]**

**2.** What is meant by "alter-globalisation"? **[2]**

**3.** Explain two ways in which globalisation may help spread liberal democracy. **[4]**

**4.** Explain what Held meant by cosmopolitan democracy and why he thought it necessary. **[4]**

**5.** Explain one argument that human rights are a form of Western influence and one argument against this view. **[4]**

**6.** Read the item.

> The Riverkeepers, a fictional network, links farming communities in four countries opposed to a dam financed by a transnational consortium. Members share drone footage online, coordinate protests on the same day in three capital cities, and petition an international development bank. After two years, the dam is redesigned but still built.

Using the item, explain two strengths and one limitation of global social movements. **[6]**

**7.** Explain two reasons why nation states may find it difficult to tackle global environmental problems. **[4]**

**8.** Explain what is meant by "pooled sovereignty" and give one example. **[3]**

## Section B – Paragraph and essay questions

**9.** Explain and evaluate the view that globalisation has spread liberal democracy. **[8]**

**10.** Explain and evaluate the view that the nation state can no longer solve global problems. **[8]**

**11.** "Global social movements are an effective way of opposing globalisation." Evaluate this view. **[35]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that globalisation has spread human rights around the world. **[35]**

## Answers

**1.** A political system with free, competitive elections [1] combined with protected civil liberties, the rule of law and limits on state power [1]. **[2]**

**2.** The position of movements that seek a fairer or different form of globalisation [1] rather than rejecting global connection altogether, for example the World Social Forum [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Way 1: global media expose citizens to democratic ideals and make repression visible [1], as networks spread protest during the Arab Spring from late 2010 [1].
Way 2: trade, aid or membership of regional blocs can be made conditional on reform [1], giving governments incentives to hold elections and protect rights [1]. **[4]**

**4.** Held (1995) argued democratic accountability should be extended to international institutions [1], not only national governments [1]. He thought it necessary because decisions that affect people now cross borders [1], so citizens cannot hold global markets or bodies to account through national votes alone [1]. **[4]**

**5.** For: rights discourse developed largely in Western societies and is enforced selectively [1], so powerful states escape scrutiny while weaker ones face pressure, as Marxist and post-colonial critics argue [1].
Against: local movements use rights language to press their own claims against their governments [1], so rights are claimed from below rather than only imposed from outside [1]. **[4]**

**6.** Strength 1: global communication [1]. Sharing footage online publicises a local struggle worldwide, as Castells argues networked movements gain power [1].
Strength 2: coordination across borders [1]. Same-day protests in three capitals and a petition to an international bank show global civil society targeting several centres of power (Kaldor) [1].
Limitation: limited structural change [1]. The dam was redesigned but still built, showing movements often shape decisions without stopping the projects they oppose [1]. **[6]**

**7.** Reason 1: environmental problems such as emissions cross borders [1], so one state's action can be undone by others' inaction [1].
Reason 2: capital mobility [1]. Firms can relocate to states with weaker environmental rules, so states fear losing investment if they regulate strictly [1]. **[4]**

**8.** States sharing decision-making authority in international bodies [1] in exchange for collective power or benefits [1]. Example: World Trade Organization members agreeing to common trade rules [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Explanation: Huntington described a third wave of democratisation from 1974 [1], and Fukuyama argued liberal democracy had defeated its rivals [1]. Global media, conditional aid and educated middle classes spread democratic demands [1], as in the Arab Spring [1].
Evaluation: most Arab Spring uprisings did not produce lasting democracy [1]. Diamond describes a democratic recession [1]. Growth can coexist with authoritarian rule [1], and "democracy promotion" may serve Western interests, so the spread is uneven and reversible [1]. **[8]**

**10.** Explanation: Strange (1996) argued power has shifted from states to markets and non-state actors [1]. Global problems such as climate change cross borders [1], and capital mobility lets firms avoid regulation [1], so no single state can solve them alone [1].
Evaluation: states still control borders, law and taxation [1]; during the coronavirus pandemic they closed borders and imposed national measures [1]. The Paris Agreement relies on national commitments [1], supporting a transformationalist view that states act through cooperation rather than being bypassed [1]. **[8]**

**11.** Marked by levels of response; this is indicative content.

- **For the view (AO1/AO2):** Kaldor's global civil society; Castells on networked movements; the Zapatistas' international support; anti-sweatshop campaigns and Klein's *No Logo*; World Social Forum; Seattle 1999; climate strikes; shifts in public debate on debt and labour standards.
- **Against the view:** limited lasting policy change; activists from richer countries dominate; dependence on global networks; fragmented aims; nationalist and protectionist movements also oppose globalisation, often more effectively through elections.
- **Evaluation (AO3):** define "effective" — publicity, policy, or structural change; compare movements with states and international agreements; Marxists argue movements cannot overcome capital without changing ownership; apply to contrasting societies.
- **Judgement:** for example, effective at shaping debate and winning concessions, less effective at changing the structures of global power.

**Level guidance:** top answers define effectiveness and weigh several movements with contrasting evidence; middle answers describe movements with some evaluation; low answers list protests.

**12.** Marked by levels of response; this is indicative content.

- **For:** international declarations and courts; global civil society monitoring abuses; global media publicising violations; local movements using rights language.
- **Against:** selective enforcement by powerful states; the "Asian values" debate and cultural relativism; rights reversals during the democratic recession; global markets and corporations implicated in abuses such as unsafe labour.
- **Evaluation:** distinguish formal recognition from practice; consider who defines rights; transformationalist view of uneven change.
- **Judgement:** for example, rights norms have spread widely, but their enforcement reflects global power.

**Level guidance:** top answers separate the spread of rights ideas from their realisation and use contrasting perspectives; middle answers list evidence for and against; low answers assert progress or failure.
