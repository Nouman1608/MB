## Core recall

- A population is an open system: inputs are births and immigration, outputs are deaths and emigration.
- Population change = natural change + net migration; the NIR ignores migration.
- The infant mortality rate uses live births, not total population, as its base.
- Split the dependency ratio into youth and old-age parts to show which group dominates.
- A young structure means population momentum and demand for schools and jobs; an old structure means pensions, healthcare and a shrinking workforce.
- Fertility falls as child mortality falls, girls' education rises, children cost more, women's employment and contraception access grow.
- China's one-child policy (from about 1980) cut fertility but caused rapid ageing and a skewed sex ratio; replaced by two-child (2016) and three-child (2021) limits.
- Singapore moved from "Stop at Two" (1970s) to pro-natalist policy from 1987; fertility stayed low.
- Kerala is often cited for low fertility reached through female literacy and healthcare.
- Impact depends on numbers and consumption: total footprint = population × footprint per person.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Crude birth rate | Live births per 1,000 people per year |
| Crude death rate | Deaths per 1,000 people per year |
| Natural increase rate | (CBR − CDR) ÷ 10, as a percentage; excludes migration |
| Net migration | Immigrants − emigrants |
| Infant mortality rate | Deaths under age one per 1,000 live births per year |
| Life expectancy | Average years a newborn would live at current death rates |
| Total fertility rate | Average children per woman over her lifetime at current rates |
| Replacement level | A TFR of about 2.1 where child mortality is low |
| Youth dependency ratio | People aged 0–14 ÷ people aged 15–64 × 100 |
| Old-age dependency ratio | People aged 65+ ÷ people aged 15–64 × 100 |
| Population momentum | Continued growth after fertility reaches replacement, because of a young age structure |
| Pro-natalist policy | Policy to raise birth rates |
| Anti-natalist policy | Policy to lower birth rates |
| Ecological footprint | Area of land and water needed to supply a population's resources and absorb its wastes |

## Formulas

```
Population change     = (births − deaths) + (immigration − emigration)
Growth rate (%)       = population change ÷ starting population × 100
Rate per 1,000        = events ÷ population × 1,000
IMR                   = infant deaths ÷ live births × 1,000
Dependency ratios     = dependants ÷ (15–64) × 100
Projection            = P × (1 + r)ⁿ        Doubling time ≈ 70 ÷ r (%)
Total footprint       = population × footprint per person
```

## Policy comparison

| Case | Type | Aim | Outcomes to quote |
|---|---|---|---|
| China, one-child (about 1980–2015) | Anti-natalist, strict limits | Slow growth, ease pressure on resources | Fertility fell; rapid ageing; sex ratio skewed towards boys; later two- and three-child limits |
| Singapore, "Stop at Two" (1970s) | Anti-natalist, incentives and penalties | Lower fertility | Fell below replacement |
| Singapore, from 1987 | Pro-natalist, incentives | Raise fertility | Fertility stayed low despite incentives |
| Kerala, India | Development-led | Better health and education | Low fertility without strict limits |

## Exam traps

- Projecting a country from the NIR when migration is large.
- Dividing infant deaths by total population.
- Giving a dependency ratio without interpreting it.
- Calling a policy a success without its unintended consequences.
- Assuming a bigger population always means bigger impact.
- Projecting with the rule of 70 without stating the constant-rate assumption.
- Mixing "per 1,000" and "%" in one answer.

## Quick self-test

1. List the two inputs and two outputs of a national population system.
2. A country of 25 million records 400,000 births and 225,000 deaths. Calculate the CBR and CDR.
3. Calculate the NIR for question 2.
4. The same country has 60,000 immigrants and 85,000 emigrants. Calculate the total population change.
5. Calculate the overall growth rate for question 4 as a percentage.
6. There are 6,000 infant deaths among the 400,000 births. Calculate the IMR.
7. A country has 4.0 million aged 0–14, 16.0 million aged 15–64 and 5.0 million aged 65+. Calculate the total dependency ratio.
8. For question 7, calculate the old-age dependency ratio and say which group dominates.
9. Give three reasons why fertility falls as countries develop.
10. State two unintended consequences of China's one-child policy.
11. Why does Singapore's experience suggest that pro-natalist incentives have limited power?
12. A population of 10 million grows at 2% a year. Estimate it after 35 years.

### Answers

1. Inputs: births, immigration. Outputs: deaths, emigration.
2. CBR = 400,000 ÷ 25,000,000 × 1,000 = **16 per 1,000**; CDR = **9 per 1,000**.
3. (16 − 9) ÷ 10 = **0.7%**.
4. Natural change 175,000; net migration −25,000; total **+150,000**.
5. 150,000 ÷ 25,000,000 × 100 = **0.6%**.
6. 6,000 ÷ 400,000 × 1,000 = **15 per 1,000 live births**.
7. (4.0 + 5.0) ÷ 16.0 × 100 = **56.25** (about 56 dependants per 100 of working age).
8. 5.0 ÷ 16.0 × 100 = **31.25**; youth dependency is 25.0, so older people dominate.
9. Any three: lower infant mortality; more education for girls; higher cost of raising children; urban living; women's employment; access to contraception; pensions.
10. Any two: rapid ageing; smaller future workforce; sex ratio skewed towards boys.
11. Fertility stayed low after the switch in 1987 because social norms and the cost of raising children had changed.
12. Doubling time 70 ÷ 2 = 35 years, so about **20 million** (10 × 1.02³⁵ ≈ 20.0 million), assuming a constant rate.
