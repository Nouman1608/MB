The [assessment revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-biology-assessment-revision-notes/) set out what
Paper 1 and Paper 2 weigh and what each tests. These notes turn that into an exam-day plan --
how to allocate your time, what to practise specifically for each paper, and a worked scenario --
alongside the [full syllabus guide](/resources/ib-dp-biology-syllabus-guide/) and
[subject overview](/resources/ib-dp-biology-subject-guide/) already on the site.

## Paper 1 (36% of the final grade)

Short-answer and data-based questions drawn across the **whole syllabus**, not concentrated in one
theme. Because coverage is broad rather than deep, the highest-value preparation is a topic-by-topic
confidence self-audit in the weeks before the exam -- rating each theme honestly and directing
remaining revision time at the weakest ones, rather than re-revising whatever was covered most
recently in class.

**Data-based questions specifically test unfamiliar data**, not recalled facts: a graph, table or
experimental description you have not seen before, which you must read, interpret and reason from.
This is a distinct skill from content recall and needs its own practice -- work through unfamiliar
data sets (from past papers or practice questions) specifically to build comfort with graphs and
tables that don't match anything memorised, rather than assuming general topic knowledge transfers
automatically.

**Time allocation**: because Paper 1 mixes short-answer and data-based questions of varying mark
value, allocate time roughly in proportion to the marks available for each question rather than
spending equal time on every question regardless of its weight -- a 2-mark question and an 8-mark
question should not receive the same share of your working time.

## Paper 2 (44% of the final grade)

Short-answer and extended-response questions requiring **sustained scientific writing** -- this is
the paper where the command terms **evaluate**, **discuss** and **justify** appear most often,
requiring a reasoned judgement built from evidence rather than a short factual answer.

Because Paper 2 carries almost half the external mark and specifically rewards sustained written
argument, practising full extended-response answers under timed conditions is worth more revision
time than reviewing content that is already secure. Two skills matter here specifically: **planning
before writing** (jotting the structure of a longer answer before committing to sentences, so the
answer builds logically rather than meandering), and **using precise biological terminology**
throughout, since imprecise wording at the top command-term tier costs marks even when the
underlying reasoning is sound.

## Worked practice scenario: interpreting an unfamiliar data set

A Paper 1-style data-based question gives a graph showing enzyme activity (as reaction rate, in
arbitrary units) against temperature for a named enzyme, rising from 10°C to a peak around 40°C,
then falling sharply between 40°C and 60°C.

```
Question style:  "Describe the relationship shown, and explain the shape of the curve
                  above 40°C."

Weak answer:      "The rate goes up then down." -- describes the shape without
                  reading precise values or explaining the mechanism.

Strong answer:    Describe: rate increases from 10°C to a maximum near 40°C, then
                  decreases sharply from 40°C to 60°C, reading approximate values
                  from the axes rather than only describing the shape in words.
                  Explain: the initial rise reflects increasing kinetic energy
                  giving more successful enzyme-substrate collisions as
                  temperature rises; the sharp fall above 40°C reflects
                  denaturation -- the enzyme's tertiary structure, held by bonds
                  sensitive to heat, breaks down, altering the active site's
                  shape so substrate can no longer bind effectively.
```

The strong answer does two things the weak one skips: it reads and states approximate values from
the graph (a "describe" command-term requirement), and it explains the underlying mechanism rather
than just naming what happens (the "explain" command-term requirement) -- treating "describe" and
"explain" as two separate, sequential tasks within the same question is what the mark scheme
actually rewards.

## Before/during exam checklist

- **Before the exam**: complete an honest topic-by-topic confidence self-audit across the whole
  syllabus rather than only your strongest themes; practise at least a few genuinely unfamiliar
  data-based questions in the final weeks, not just recall-based ones; practise planning and writing
  full extended-response answers under timed conditions specifically for Paper 2.
- **During Paper 1**: read data-based questions' axes and units carefully before answering, and
  allocate time in proportion to each question's mark value rather than evenly.
- **During Paper 2**: jot a brief plan before writing an extended response; use precise biological
  terminology throughout; when a question uses "evaluate" or "discuss," make sure your answer
  reaches an explicit, reasoned conclusion rather than only listing points.

## Self-test

1. Why does Paper 1 reward broad topic coverage over depth in a few areas?
2. What specific skill do data-based questions test that pure content recall does not?
3. In the worked scenario, why does the enzyme's reaction rate fall sharply above 40°C?
4. Why should time on Paper 1 be allocated by mark value rather than evenly across questions?
5. What two things should a strong answer do when a question both "describes" and "explains" a
   graph?

**Answers:** 1. Because Paper 1 draws short-answer and data-based questions from across the whole
syllabus rather than concentrating on one theme, so broad, even revision coverage is rewarded more
than deep revision of only a few topics. 2. Reading and interpreting unfamiliar data -- a graph,
table or experimental description not seen before -- rather than recalling memorised facts. 3.
Because the enzyme denatures above its optimum temperature: heat-sensitive bonds holding its
tertiary structure break down, changing the active site's shape so substrate can no longer bind
effectively, reducing the reaction rate. 4. Because questions carry different mark values, and
spending equal time on a 2-mark and an 8-mark question wastes time that could be better allocated to
the higher-value question. 5. It should read and state approximate values from the graph to satisfy
"describe," and separately explain the underlying biological mechanism to satisfy "explain," rather
than treating the two as one combined task.
