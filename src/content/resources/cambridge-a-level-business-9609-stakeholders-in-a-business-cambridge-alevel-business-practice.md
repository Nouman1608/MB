This set covers section 1.5 (Stakeholders in a business) only. All businesses and figures are invented, and sums of money are in dollars.

---

## Section A

**1.** Explain the difference between a stakeholder and a shareholder. **[2]**

**2.** Identify **one** internal and **one** external stakeholder of a private hospital. **[2]**

**3.** State **one** right and **one** responsibility of a supplier. **[2]**

**4.** Explain **two** factors that might make one stakeholder group more influential than another. **[4]**

**5.** Explain what is meant by a business being *accountable* to its stakeholders. **[2]**

**6.** Pembury Electronics sells 45% of its output to one retailer. The retailer asks for a 5% price cut. Explain why Pembury may agree. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** Ashvale Brewery plans to double production at its town-centre site, which will mean 30 extra lorry journeys a day. Analyse the likely reactions of **two** stakeholder groups to this decision. **[6]**

**8.** Explain **two** ways in which a public limited company is accountable to its shareholders. **[4]**

**9.** Lanreth Fashions makes profit of $2 400 000 a year. Employees want a pay rise costing $360 000 a year. Shareholders expect a dividend of 40% of profit.

**(a)** Calculate the dividend shareholders expect.

**(b)** Calculate the pay rise as a percentage of the expected dividend.

**(c)** Explain how this situation might lead to conflict. **[6]**

**10.** Analyse how stakeholder aims might influence a business's decision to build a new factory. **[6]**

**11.** Torbridge Bus Company changes its objective from providing a full timetable to maximising profit. Explain how this change may affect **two** stakeholder groups. **[4]**

**12.** Corrigan Mining wants to open a new mine near a village. The mine would create 200 jobs, but local residents and an environmental pressure group oppose it. Evaluate how far Corrigan should take account of the residents' and the pressure group's views. **[10]**

---

## Answers

**1.** A stakeholder is any individual or group with an interest in a business's activities [1]; a shareholder owns part of a company and is only one type of stakeholder [1].

**2.** Internal: for example, doctors, nurses or managers [1]. External: for example, patients, suppliers of medical equipment or the government [1].

**3.** Right: to be paid on the agreed terms [1]. Responsibility: to deliver goods of the agreed quality on time [1].

**4.** Any two, explained. Dependence: if the business relies heavily on a group, such as a major customer, losing it would be serious [1], so its demands carry weight [1]. Organisation: a group that acts together, such as a unionised workforce [1], can threaten collective action that one person could not [1]. Allow legal rights, replaceability or publicity.

**5.** The business must explain and justify its actions [1] to the groups affected by them, for example through reports or meetings [1].

**6.** The retailer buys 45% of output, so Pembury depends heavily on it [1]. If the retailer switched to a rival, Pembury would lose almost half its sales [1], which could threaten its survival, so a smaller margin may be the lesser risk [1].

**7.** Any two groups, 3 marks each. Local residents: more lorries mean noise, pollution and safety risks [1], so they may object to the planning application or campaign in local media [1]; this could delay the plan or damage the brewery's local reputation [1]. Employees: doubling production may mean more jobs or overtime [1], so they are likely to support the decision [1], though they may resist if it brings longer shifts without extra pay [1]. Allow shareholders, local council or customers with matching reasoning.

**8.** Any two, explained. Annual report and accounts: the company must publish its financial results [1], so shareholders can judge how well directors have used their capital [1]. Annual general meeting: shareholders can question directors [1] and vote, for example on appointing directors [1].

**9.** **(a)** 40% × 2 400 000 = $960 000 [1].

**(b)** 360 000 ÷ 960 000 × 100 [1] = 37.5% [1].

**(c)** The pay rise and the dividend both come from the same profit [1], so paying the rise could reduce the profit available for dividends by a large amount relative to the dividend [1]. Shareholders want returns while employees want higher pay, so meeting one group's aim reduces what the other receives [1].

**10.** Shareholders want a return, so the factory will only go ahead if expected profit justifies the cost [1]; this may push the business towards a cheaper location [1]. Lenders' conditions may limit how much can be borrowed [1], which may reduce the size of the factory [1]. Local communities and government may want jobs but also low pollution [1], so the business may have to choose a site with planning permission or pay for cleaner technology [1].

**11.** Passengers: unprofitable routes or late services may be cut [1], so people in rural areas may lose access to transport [1]. Shareholders: profit should rise [1], so they gain higher dividends or a rising share value [1]. Allow drivers (tighter schedules or job cuts) or the local council (pressure to subsidise routes).

**12.** For taking their views into account: residents have high interest because noise, dust and traffic affect their daily lives [1]; organised opposition could delay planning permission, raising costs [1]; a pressure group's media campaign could damage Corrigan's reputation with customers and investors [1]; consulting early may reduce opposition [1].
Against giving them too much weight: the mine creates 200 jobs, which many residents may welcome [1]; shareholders have invested capital and expect a return [1]; if government grants permission, the residents' legal power may be limited [1].
Judgement: Corrigan should take their views seriously but need not let them block the mine [1]; it could reduce opposition through measures such as restricted working hours or an environmental plan [1]. The extent depends on how much power the groups have over planning approval and how important reputation is to Corrigan [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "shareholder" when the question asks about stakeholders.
- Stating a reaction without explaining why the group reacts that way.
- Ignoring the case data, such as the 45% dependence on one customer.
- Leaving out the "conflict" step: show that both groups' aims draw on the same resource or decision.
- Concluding that every group can be satisfied without saying how.
