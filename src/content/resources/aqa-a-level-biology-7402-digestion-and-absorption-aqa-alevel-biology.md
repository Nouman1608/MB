| Statement | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| In mammals, digestion of food in the gut is by hydrolysis | Explain digestion as breaking bonds by adding water, producing molecules small enough to be absorbed |
| Carbohydrates: amylases and membrane-bound disaccharidases | Name the enzymes, substrates, products and bonds |
| Lipids: lipase, including the action of bile salts | Explain emulsification and the products of lipase |
| Proteins: endopeptidases, exopeptidases and membrane-bound dipeptidases | Say where in the chain each enzyme acts and why using all three is efficient |
| Absorption by cells lining the ileum | Co-transport for amino acids and monosaccharides; the role of micelles for lipids |

## Why digestion is needed

Starch, triglycerides and proteins are too large to cross the cell-surface membranes of the cells lining the ileum. Digestion hydrolyses them into monomers or other small molecules that can be absorbed. Every digestive enzyme in this sub-topic is a **hydrolase**: it breaks a bond by adding a molecule of water. Name the bond in every answer.

| Food molecule | Bond hydrolysed | Final products absorbed |
|---|---|---|
| Starch | Glycosidic | Glucose |
| Disaccharides | Glycosidic | Monosaccharides |
| Triglycerides | Ester | Monoglycerides and fatty acids |
| Proteins | Peptide | Amino acids |

## Carbohydrate digestion

**Amylase** is produced by the salivary glands and the pancreas. It hydrolyses glycosidic bonds in starch, producing **maltose**, a disaccharide.

**Membrane-bound disaccharidases** are enzymes attached to the cell-surface membranes of the epithelial cells lining the ileum. Because they are fixed in the membrane, the monosaccharides they release are produced right next to the transport proteins that absorb them.

| Disaccharidase | Substrate | Products |
|---|---|---|
| Maltase | Maltose | Glucose + glucose |
| Sucrase | Sucrose | Glucose + fructose |
| Lactase | Lactose | Glucose + galactose |

## Lipid digestion and bile salts

**Lipase**, made in the pancreas, hydrolyses the ester bonds in triglycerides, producing **monoglycerides** and **fatty acids**.

Lipids form large droplets in the watery contents of the gut, and lipase can act only at the droplet surface. **Bile salts**, made in the liver, **emulsify** lipids: they break large droplets into many small droplets. The total volume of lipid is unchanged, but the surface area is much larger, so lipase hydrolyses the lipid faster. Note that emulsification is physical, not chemical; bile salts do not hydrolyse anything.

Bile salts also combine with the monoglycerides and fatty acids to form **micelles**, which matters for absorption (below).

## Protein digestion: three enzyme types

| Enzyme | Where it acts | Products |
|---|---|---|
| Endopeptidases | Peptide bonds between amino acids in the central region of a polypeptide | Many shorter polypeptides |
| Exopeptidases | Peptide bonds at the ends, removing single terminal amino acids | Single amino acids and shorter chains (and dipeptides) |
| Membrane-bound dipeptidases | The peptide bond in a dipeptide, at the ileum epithelium surface | Two amino acids |

**Why three types are more efficient than one.** Endopeptidases cut the long chain into many shorter pieces. Each new piece has two ends, so there are many more ends for exopeptidases to work on. The result is faster overall hydrolysis to amino acids than exopeptidases could achieve alone. Dipeptidases complete the process at the membrane where absorption happens.

## Absorption by co-transport: monosaccharides and amino acids

The epithelial cells lining the ileum absorb glucose against its concentration gradient, using co-transport with sodium ions. The sequence matters, so learn it in order:

1. Sodium ions are actively transported out of the epithelial cell into the blood, by a sodium–potassium pump in the membrane facing the blood. This uses ATP.
2. This keeps the sodium ion concentration inside the epithelial cell lower than in the lumen of the ileum.
3. Sodium ions diffuse from the lumen into the cell, down their concentration gradient, through a **co-transporter protein** in the membrane facing the lumen.
4. The co-transporter carries a glucose molecule into the cell at the same time, even though glucose is moving against its own concentration gradient.
5. Glucose then moves out of the epithelial cell into the blood by facilitated diffusion, through a different carrier protein.

The energy comes indirectly from ATP: it is used to set up the sodium ion gradient, not to move glucose directly. Galactose is absorbed in the same way, and amino acids are absorbed by a similar sodium-dependent co-transport mechanism.

## Absorption of lipids: the role of micelles

Monoglycerides and fatty acids are not very soluble in water. Micelles are tiny water-soluble structures formed from bile salts, monoglycerides and fatty acids.

- Micelles carry monoglycerides and fatty acids through the watery contents of the gut to the surface of the epithelial cells.
- They constantly break up and re-form, releasing monoglycerides and fatty acids close to the cell membrane.
- Being non-polar, monoglycerides and fatty acids diffuse across the phospholipid bilayer of the cell-surface membrane.

Micelles keep a supply of these molecules at the membrane surface, maintaining a concentration gradient for diffusion. Inside the cell, many textbooks go on to describe triglycerides being reformed in the endoplasmic reticulum and packaged with proteins into chylomicrons; the specification names only the micelle stage, so that is where your answer should focus.

## Worked example: interpreting an experiment (invented data)

Pieces of ileum were kept in glucose solution with and without a respiratory inhibitor that stops ATP production. Glucose absorption fell from 4.2 to 0.6 arbitrary units.

> **Interpretation.** Without ATP, the sodium–potassium pump stops, so the sodium ion gradient between the lumen and the epithelial cell is lost. Sodium ions no longer diffuse in through the co-transporter, so glucose is not co-transported. The small remaining uptake could be by diffusion while the glucose concentration in the lumen is higher than in the cells. The percentage decrease is (4.2 − 0.6) ÷ 4.2 × 100 = 85.7%.

This shows that glucose absorption depends on ATP, but only indirectly: write "ATP is needed to maintain the sodium ion gradient".

## Common mistakes

- Saying amylase produces glucose: it produces maltose.
- Saying bile salts "digest" or "break down" lipids: they emulsify them.
- Writing "glucose is actively transported into the epithelial cell". It enters by co-transport; sodium ions are actively transported out of the other side.
- Getting the direction of the sodium ion gradient wrong.
- Swapping endopeptidase and exopeptidase.
- Forgetting the word "hydrolysis" or naming the wrong bond.
- Writing that fatty acids enter by co-transport rather than diffusion.

| Command word | What earns the marks |
|---|---|
| Describe | The full chain for co-transport, in the right order, with the membranes named |
| Explain | Mechanism plus effect, for example "emulsification increases surface area, so faster hydrolysis by lipase" |
| Suggest | Apply to unfamiliar data: inhibitors, missing enzymes such as lactase, or a damaged epithelium |
| Calculate | Percentage change, rates of absorption, or ratios from data tables |
