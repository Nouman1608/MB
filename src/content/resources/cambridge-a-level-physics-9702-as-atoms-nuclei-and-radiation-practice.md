## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between the nucleon number and the proton number of a nucleus. **[2]**

**2.** State the number of protons, neutrons and electrons in a neutral atom of ⁶⁵₂₉Cu. **[3]**

**3.** Hydrogen has three isotopes, with nucleon numbers 1, 2 and 3. Explain what is meant by isotopes, and state how the nuclei of these three isotopes differ. **[2]**

**4.** State what is meant by an antiparticle, and name the antiparticle of the electron. **[2]**

**5.** An α-particle has a mass of approximately 4.0 u. Calculate its mass in kilograms, and determine roughly how many times more massive it is than an electron (mass 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg). **[2]**

**6.** State the change in nucleon number and in proton number of a nucleus when it emits (i) a β⁺ particle, (ii) a γ-ray. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** In the α-particle scattering experiment, a beam of α-particles was directed at a thin gold foil.

**(a)** Describe the main results of the experiment. **[2]**

**(b)** State what each result shows about the structure of the atom. **[3]**

**(c)** Suggest why the experiment was carried out in a vacuum. **[1]**

**8.** Complete each decay equation, including every particle produced.

**(a)** Radon-222 (proton number 86) decays by α-emission to an isotope of polonium (proton number 84). **[2]**

**(b)** Strontium-90 (proton number 38) decays by β⁻ emission to an isotope of yttrium (proton number 39). **[2]**

**(c)** Fluorine-18 (proton number 9) decays by β⁺ emission to an isotope of oxygen (proton number 8). **[2]**

**9.** Thorium-232 (²³²₉₀Th) decays through a series of α and β⁻ decays to the stable isotope ²⁰⁸₈₂Pb. Determine the number of α-decays and the number of β⁻ decays in the series. **[3]**

**10.** The kinetic energies of α-particles from one α-emitting nuclide and of β-particles from one β⁻ emitting nuclide are measured.

**(a)** Describe how the distribution of kinetic energies differs for the two types of particle. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain this difference. **[3]**

**11.** A nucleus in an excited state emits a γ-ray.

**(a)** State what a γ-ray is, and give its mass and charge. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why γ-emission does not produce a different element. **[1]**

**12.** A gold nucleus is ¹⁹⁷₇₉Au.

**(a)** Estimate the mass of the nucleus in kilograms. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the charge of the nucleus. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain, with reference to the charge and mass you have calculated, why a gold nucleus can turn an α-particle back towards its source. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** The nucleon number is the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus [1]; the proton number is the number of protons only [1].

**2.** Protons 29 [1]; neutrons 65 − 29 = 36 [1]; electrons 29 [1].

**3.** Isotopes are forms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons [1]. All three nuclei have one proton; they have 0, 1 and 2 neutrons respectively [1].

**4.** A particle with the same mass but opposite charge to the corresponding particle [1]; the positron [1].

**5.** m = 4.0 × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ = 6.6 × 10⁻²⁷ kg [1]; ratio = 6.64 × 10⁻²⁷ ÷ 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ ≈ 7300 [1].

**6.** (i) Nucleon number unchanged, proton number decreases by 1 [1]. (ii) Neither changes [1].

**7. (a)** Most α-particles passed straight through the foil or were deflected through very small angles [1]; a very small fraction were deflected through large angles, some greater than 90° [1].

**(b)** Most of the atom is empty space [1]; the positive charge is concentrated in a very small region, the nucleus, which is tiny compared with the atom [1]; the nucleus contains most of the mass of the atom [1].

**(c)** So that the α-particles are not absorbed or deflected by collisions with air molecules [1].

**8. (a)** ²²²₈₆Rn → ²¹⁸₈₄Po + ⁴₂α [1 for Po with 218 and 84, 1 for ⁴₂α].

**(b)** ⁹⁰₃₈Sr → ⁹⁰₃₉Y + ⁰₋₁β + ν̄ [1 for Y and β⁻, 1 for the antineutrino].

**(c)** ¹⁸₉F → ¹⁸₈O + ⁰₊₁β + ν [1 for O and β⁺, 1 for the neutrino].

**9.** Change in nucleon number = 232 − 208 = 24, so 24 ÷ 4 = 6 α-decays [1]. Six α-decays reduce Z by 12, to 90 − 12 = 78 [1]. The final Z is 82, so there are 82 − 78 = 4 β⁻ decays [1].

**10. (a)** The α-particles all have the same kinetic energy (discrete value) [1]; the β-particles have a continuous range of kinetic energies, from almost zero up to a maximum [1].

**(b)** In α-decay the energy released is shared between two bodies (α-particle and daughter nucleus), so the share is fixed [1]. In β⁻ decay an antineutrino is also emitted, so the energy is shared between three bodies [1]; the antineutrino takes a different share in each decay, so the β-particle's energy varies [1].

**11. (a)** Electromagnetic radiation (a high-energy photon) [1]; zero mass and zero charge [1].

**(b)** The proton number does not change, and the element is determined by the proton number [1].

**12. (a)** m ≈ 197 × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ = 3.27 × 10⁻²⁵ kg [1].

**(b)** Q = 79 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 1.26 × 10⁻¹⁷ C [1].

**(c)** The large positive charge exerts a very strong repulsive force on an α-particle that approaches closely [1]; the nucleus is about 50 times more massive than the α-particle (197 u compared with 4 u), so it hardly recoils and the α-particle can be turned back [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing α-scattering results without giving the proportions ("most", "a very small fraction").
- Stating results but not what each one shows.
- Leaving the neutrino or antineutrino out of a β decay equation, or pairing them the wrong way.
- Writing nucleon number 1 for a β-particle, or charge +4 for an α-particle.
- Counting β decays before α decays in a decay chain.
- Explaining the β spectrum without mentioning the third particle.
