Section A of Paper 2 asks for one essay on a prose set text: a novel or the *Stories of Ourselves*
short-story selection. It is worth 25 of the paper's 50 marks and is assessed on AO1 to AO4. This guide
concentrates on the tools for analysing prose fiction, which differ from those for drama and poetry,
and on how to build each type of answer. For the paper as a whole, see the
[Paper 2 overview](/resources/a-level-english-literature-paper-2-prose-and-unseen/).

## What the section asks of you

On each set text you choose between an **(a) question**, an essay on the text as a whole, and a **(b)
question**, which prints a passage from the text and asks you to analyse it with reference to the wider
work. Set texts are not allowed in the exam room, and dictionaries may not be used.

For 2026 the Section A texts are Kiran Desai's *The Inheritance of Loss*, the selection from *Stories of
Ourselves, Volume 1*, Evelyn Waugh's *A Handful of Dust* and Colson Whitehead's *The Underground
Railroad*. For 2027 the list is the *Stories of Ourselves, Volume 3* selection, *A Handful of Dust*, *The
Underground Railroad* and Tara June Winch's *The Yield*. Check the list for your own examination year.

## The key idea: who tells, who sees, and when

Every effect in fiction passes through a **narrative voice**. Before discussing character or theme, ask
how the narration shapes what we know and how we judge it. Three questions unlock most passages and most
essays.

### Who tells? Narrative voice

- **First-person** narration gives intimacy but limits knowledge, and raises the question of
  **reliability**: does the narrator understand, or admit, everything?
- **Third-person omniscient** narration can move between minds and comment on events; listen for
  judgement in the narrator's own voice.
- **Third-person limited** narration stays close to one character's perceptions.
- **Free indirect discourse** blends narrator and character: the grammar is third person, but the
  vocabulary and attitude are the character's. It lets a writer present a character's thoughts while
  keeping ironic distance.

### Who sees? Focalisation

The focaliser is the character whose perspective shapes a scene, which may change between chapters.
Novels that shift focalisation (such as Desai's, which moves between characters and between India and
New York) invite comparison: how does the same theme look from two positions?

### When? Time and order

- **Chronology**: is the story told in order, or with flashback (**analepsis**) and flash-forward
  (**prolepsis**)?
- **Pace**: summary covers years in a sentence; scene slows to moment-by-moment detail. Where a writer
  slows down shows what matters.
- **Structure**: chapter divisions, parts, and how the opening and ending frame the whole. Whitehead's
  novel, for instance, is built from sections set in different states, so episodic structure is part of
  its meaning.

### Other tools

- **Dialogue**: what characters say, how it is reported, and what the narrator adds around it.
- **Setting and symbol**: places and objects that gather meaning through repetition.
- **Tone and irony**: the gap between what is described and how it is described. Waugh's satire often
  works through a flat, unemotional narrative voice reporting events that should shock.

## Short stories need a slightly different approach

If you study the *Stories of Ourselves* selection, remember that a short story is compressed. Openings
and endings carry more weight; a single image may hold the whole meaning; and endings are often open. If
an (a) question asks you to write about a set number of stories, write about exactly that number, and
choose stories that let you build an argument rather than two that say the same thing.

## Writing the (b) passage answer

1. **Locate the passage** in a sentence: where it falls and what the reader already knows.
2. **State its narrative function**: what the passage does to character, theme or the reader's
   judgement. That is your thesis, and it should use the question's own words.
3. **Analyse the narration first**: who tells, who sees, and how close we are to a mind.
4. **Then language and structure**: sentence shape, imagery, dialogue, pace, the passage's own turning
   point.
5. **Connect outward** two or three times to show knowledge of the whole text.

### Annotated model paragraph

The passage below is **original**, written for this guide.

> Mrs Okafor had always said the house would outlive them all, and she said it again now, to the
> estate agent, who was writing something on a clipboard. The roof, the agent noted, would need
> attention. Mrs Okafor said that the roof had always needed attention, and that it had never once let
> in the rain.

A strong paragraph might read:

> The narrative voice reports Mrs Okafor's speech indirectly, so her certainty ("the house would outlive
> them all") is held at a slight distance and begins to sound like a habit rather than a truth. The
> agent's brief, factual observation interrupts her, and the narrator gives it equal weight, quietly
> undermining her. Her reply defends the house with a contradiction that the narrator does not correct:
> a roof that "had always needed attention" but "never once let in the rain." The irony is gentle; the
> reader sees her loyalty and her denial at once.

The paragraph analyses **how the story is told** before what it is about, which is what lifts prose
answers.

## Writing the (a) essay

An (a) question may focus on a character, relationship, theme or narrative method across the text.

- **Write a thesis that takes a position.** "Waugh presents the country house as a refuge" is a topic;
  "Waugh presents the country house as a refuge that its owner mistakes for a moral order" is an
  argument.
- **Select moments from across the text**, including the ending, and say how the focus develops.
- **Keep narrative method in every paragraph**: voice, structure, and language, not just events.
- **Use short, precise quotations or close references.** Closed book means you need a bank of phrases
  learned in context, not long passages.

## Context in prose answers

AO1 credits "an appreciation of relevant contexts". For prose, context works when it explains a choice:
why a narrator stays detached, why a novel is structured around migration or a journey, why a setting
carries social meaning. Keep it brief and tie it to the text.

## Command words

Section A questions use command words such as **discuss**, **analyse** and **comment**, and phrases such
as "In what ways…?" and "How far, and in what ways…?". "How far" needs a reasoned judgement; "In what
ways" needs a range of methods, so include narration and structure, not just character.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating characters as real people** and ignoring the narrator who presents them.
- **Retelling the plot** in an (a) answer instead of selecting and arguing.
- **Missing irony**: taking a narrator's statement at face value when the voice is distancing it.
- **Ignoring structure**: never mentioning order, pace, chapter or part divisions, or the ending.
- **A (b) answer that drifts** into a general essay and leaves the printed passage behind.
