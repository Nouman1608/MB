These questions cover the "ethical issues" row of section 3.7 in the AQA GCSE Sociology (8192) specification: consent, confidentiality and harm to participants, and how they can be addressed. Methods can be assessed in Paper 1 or Paper 2. All people, places and organisations are fictional.

Revise first with the [ethical issues study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-ethical-issues/) and the [ethical issues revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-ethical-issues-revision-notes/). For questions on all of section 3.7, use the [research methods practice set](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-research-methods-practice/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Which ethical issue is broken when a researcher studies a group without telling its members?

A. Access
B. Informed consent
C. Reliability
D. Representativeness

**[1]**

**2.** Using a false name for a participant in a published report is an example of:

A. A pilot study
B. A pseudonym
C. A debrief
D. A sampling frame

**[1]**

**3.** Define the term "informed consent". **[2]**

**4.** Explain the difference between confidentiality and anonymity. **[2]**

**5.** Identify and explain **one** type of harm that research on young offenders could cause. **[3]**

**6.** Identify and explain **one** reason why confidentiality is difficult to guarantee in research in a small village. **[3]**

### Section B: longer and structured questions

**7.** Read the item.

> A researcher at Oakfield Prison (a fictional prison) asks the governor for permission to interview prisoners about their childhoods. The governor agrees and tells prison officers to "send along anyone who's free".

Using the item, explain **two** ethical problems with how participants are being recruited. **[4]**

**8.** A researcher plans focus groups with 14-year-olds about body image and social media. Explain how she could address consent **and** harm in this research. **[4]**

**9.** Read the item.

> During an interview for a study of family life, a 15-year-old tells the researcher that an adult at home has been hitting him. The researcher had promised that "everything you say stays between us".

Explain the ethical dilemma the researcher faces and how it could have been avoided. **[4]**

**10.** Explain **two** ways in which publishing a study of pupils in a school's lowest sets could harm participants, and how each could be prevented. **[4]**

**11.** Discuss how far covert participant observation can be justified in sociological research. **[12]**

**12.** A sociologist wants to study women's experiences of leaving violent relationships. Explain the ethical issues she would face and how she could address them. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** B. Informed consent [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* access is a practical issue; reliability and representativeness are evaluation concepts, not ethical issues.

**2.** B. A pseudonym [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* pseudonyms protect confidentiality in the reporting stage.

**3.** Participants freely agree to take part [1], knowing the aims of the research, what they will be asked to do, how the data will be used and that they can withdraw [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "agreeing" alone is not enough; the mark is for knowing what is involved.

**4.** Confidentiality means information participants give is kept private and not shared in a way that identifies them [1], whereas anonymity means their identity is not recorded or is hidden, so answers cannot be linked to them at all [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* an anonymous questionnaire is a useful example of anonymity.

**5.** Legal harm [1]: if young offenders describe crimes the police do not know about and they are identified, they could be arrested or punished [1]. The researcher should avoid recording details of unreported crimes and protect identities so participants are not put at legal risk [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* emotional harm from discussing difficult experiences is also valid.

**6.** In a small village people know each other well [1], so even without names, details such as someone's job, age or family situation could reveal who said what [1]. This could damage participants' relationships or reputations, so the researcher would need to change identifying details and possibly the village's name [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* explain why the setting makes identification easy; that is the point of the question.

**7.** First, prisoners sent by officers may feel they cannot refuse [1], because the governor and officers hold power over them, so their consent may not be freely given [1]. Second, there is no sign that prisoners will be told about the research before attending [1], so they may not know its aims or their right to withdraw, which means consent is not informed [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* harm is also arguable: discussing childhood may be distressing without support in place.

**8.** Consent: she should obtain written consent from parents as well as the young people's own agreement [1], after giving age-appropriate information about the aims and making clear they can leave at any time [1]. Harm: body image can be a sensitive topic, so she should avoid personal questions about participants' own bodies and let them stay silent on any topic [1], then debrief the group and give contacts for support services [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* both issues must be covered; an excellent answer on one only reaches half marks.

**9.** The researcher promised complete confidentiality, but the boy may be at risk of serious harm [1], so she faces a conflict between keeping her promise and protecting him by passing the information on [1]. It could have been avoided by explaining the limits of confidentiality at the start [1], making clear that if a young person revealed they were at serious risk, she would have to tell someone who could help [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "dilemma" means two duties clash; name both of them.

**10.** First, pupils could be labelled as failures if the report describes them negatively [1]; this could be prevented by careful, neutral wording and focusing on the school's processes rather than individuals [1]. Second, pupils could be identified by teachers or classmates if the school and class are recognisable [1]; using pseudonyms for the school and pupils and changing identifying details would prevent this [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* link labelling to the self-fulfilling prophecy for a stronger answer.

**11.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Covert participant observation means joining a group without revealing the research [1].
- It breaks informed consent, because members do not know they are being studied [1].
- It involves deception, which damages trust [1].
- Participants may be harmed if identified or if they feel betrayed later [1].
- The researcher may witness or be drawn into illegal or harmful acts [1].
- For: some closed or deviant groups would never allow open research [1].
- Behaviour is natural, avoiding the observer effect, so validity is high [1].
- The knowledge gained may benefit society, for example by exposing harmful practices [1].
- Confidentiality can be protected through pseudonyms and changed details [1].
- Consent could be sought after the observation, before publication [1].
- Ethical guidelines such as the British Sociological Association's allow it only in exceptional cases [1].
- Judgement: it can be justified only when no open method could work, the benefits are significant and identities are fully protected, so it is justifiable in rare cases but not as a routine method [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* the question asks "how far", so set out the conditions under which it is justified rather than a simple yes or no.

**12.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Unstructured interviews are likely, as the topic is sensitive and personal [1].
- Consent: women must know the aims, how data will be used and that they can withdraw [1].
- Access through refuges or support services means consent must be free of pressure from staff [1].
- Confidentiality: pseudonyms and changed details so women cannot be identified [1].
- Data stored securely in line with data protection law [1].
- Safety: if an abusive partner learns of the research, the woman could be at risk [1].
- Interviews should take place somewhere private and safe [1].
- Harm: recalling abuse may be distressing, so women can skip questions or stop at any time [1].
- Debriefing and contacts for support services reduce emotional harm [1].
- Limits of confidentiality, such as risk to children, should be explained in advance [1].
- The interviewer's characteristics matter: a female interviewer may reduce distress and build trust [1].
- Judgement: the research can be ethical if consent, confidentiality and harm are planned for at every stage, and the value of understanding women's experiences may justify careful study [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* the strongest answers address all three named issues and fit each solution to this topic.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming ethical issues without explaining how to address them.
- Confusing confidentiality with anonymity.
- Writing about cost or time in an ethics question.
- Ignoring the extra steps needed for children.
- Giving a one-sided answer on covert research.
