## Questions

**1.** **(a)** Show that the unit of CR is the second. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the time constant for a 4.7 µF capacitor and a 6.8 MΩ resistor. **[2]**

**2.** A 330 µF capacitor charged to 15 V discharges through a 27 kΩ resistor.
**(a)** Calculate the time constant and the initial charge. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the initial current. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the p.d. across the capacitor 12 s after discharge begins. **[2]**

**3.** An uncharged capacitor is charged from a cell through a resistor. Describe how the current and the p.d. across the capacitor change from the moment the switch is closed. **[4]**

**4.** An uncharged 47 µF capacitor is connected through a 100 kΩ resistor to a 6.0 V supply.
**(a)** Find the time constant. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the time taken for the p.d. across the capacitor to reach 4.5 V. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the current at that time. **[1]**

**5.** A capacitor discharges through a 47 kΩ resistor. The table shows the p.d. V at time t.

| t / s | 0 | 20 | 40 | 60 | 80 | 100 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| V / V | 8.0 | 4.3 | 2.3 | 1.2 | 0.66 | 0.35 |
| ln(V / V) | 2.08 | 1.46 | 0.83 | | −0.42 | |

**(a)** Calculate ln(V / V) for t = 60 s and t = 100 s. **[2]**
**(b)** Use the points at t = 0 and t = 100 s to find the gradient of the ln V against t graph. **[2]**
**(c)** Determine the capacitance. **[2]**

**6.** The p.d. across a discharging capacitor is 8.0 V at t = 0 and 5.0 V at t = 20 s.
**(a)** Predict the p.d. at t = 40 s. **[1]**
**(b)** State the property of the decay that justifies your prediction. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the time constant. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the time for the p.d. to halve. **[1]**

**7.** A student investigates the discharge of a capacitor.
**(a)** Describe how a data-logger can be used to obtain the data, and how they find the time constant from it. **[3]**
**(b)** State two advantages of a data-logger over a voltmeter and a stopwatch. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the voltmeter used must have a very large resistance. **[1]**

**8.** A capacitor C discharges through a resistor R.
**(a)** Show that ΔQ/Δt = −Q/CR. **[3]**
A spreadsheet models the discharge with C = 400 µF, R = 15 kΩ, an initial p.d. of 6.0 V and a time step Δt = 1.5 s.
**(b)** Calculate Q at t = 1.5 s, 3.0 s and 4.5 s. **[3]**
**(c)** The exact charge at 4.5 s is 1.13 mC. Compare it with your result and state how to improve the model. **[2]**

**9.** A discharge current I (in µA) is plotted as lg I against t. The line has gradient −0.0145 s⁻¹ and intercept 2.00 on the lg I axis. The resistor is 47 kΩ.
**(a)** Calculate CR. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the initial current. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the initial p.d. across the capacitor. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the capacitance. **[2]**

**10.** A 2200 µF capacitor, initially uncharged, charges from a 9.0 V supply through a 4.7 kΩ resistor. After 15 s the supply is replaced by a 12 kΩ resistor only, which discharges the capacitor.
**(a)** Calculate the charging time constant. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the p.d. across the capacitor after 15 s. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the charge on the capacitor at 15 s. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the charging current at 15 s. **[2]**
**(e)** Calculate the time for the p.d. to fall to 2.0 V once discharge through the 12 kΩ resistor begins. **[3]**

**11.** (a) Show that the time for a discharging capacitor to lose half its charge is CR ln 2. **[2]**
**(b)** A 120 µF capacitor loses half its charge in 3.6 s. Calculate R. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage of the charge left after 10.8 s. **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** C = Q/V and R = V/I, so CR = (Q/V)(V/I) = Q/I [1]. Charge divided by current is time (Q = It), so the unit is the second [1] **[2]**
**(b)** CR = 4.7 × 10⁻⁶ × 6.8 × 10⁶ [1] = **32 s** [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A "show that" answer should start from the definitions of C and R and reach the unit; stating "CR is a time" scores nothing by itself.

**2. (a)** τ = 330 × 10⁻⁶ × 27 × 10³ = **8.9 s** [1]; Q₀ = CV₀ = 330 × 10⁻⁶ × 15 = **4.95 × 10⁻³ C** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** I₀ = V₀/R = 15 / 27 000 = **5.6 × 10⁻⁴ A** [1] **[1]**
**(c)** V = 15 e^(−12/8.91) [1] = **3.9 V** [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Convert µF and kΩ before multiplying, and write the unit on each answer. Using the unrounded τ in part (c) avoids a rounding drift.

**3.** The current is at its maximum (cell p.d. ÷ R) at the start [1], and falls with time, quickly at first and then more slowly [1]. The p.d. across the capacitor starts at zero and rises towards the cell p.d., more slowly as time goes on [1]. Cell p.d. = p.d. across R + p.d. across C, so as the p.d. across C rises, that across R and the current fall [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Mention both quantities and say "approaches" a limit; a statement that the current "stops" at a definite time misdescribes an exponential.

**4. (a)** τ = 47 × 10⁻⁶ × 100 × 10³ = **4.7 s** [1] **[1]**
**(b)** 4.5 = 6.0(1 − e^(−t/τ)), so e^(−t/τ) = 0.25 [1]; t = τ ln 4 [1] = **6.5 s** [1] **[3]**
**(c)** V_R = 6.0 − 4.5 = 1.5 V, so I = 1.5 / 100 000 = **1.5 × 10⁻⁵ A** [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* In part (b), isolate the exponential before taking logs. Part (c) is quickest through the p.d. across R, not the exponential.

**5. (a)** ln 1.2 = **0.18**; ln 0.35 = **−1.05** [1] [1] **[2]**
**(b)** Gradient = (−1.05 − 2.08) / (100 − 0) [1] = **−0.0313 s⁻¹** [1] **[2]**
**(c)** CR = 1 / 0.0313 = 32 s [1]; C = 32 / 47 000 = **6.8 × 10⁻⁴ F** [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep the minus sign in the gradient and invert it for CR. Reading CR as 0.0313 s, or as a negative time, loses the answer.

**6. (a)** Ratio 5.0/8.0 = 0.625, so V = 5.0 × 0.625 = **3.1 V** [1] **[1]**
**(b)** An exponential decay has a constant ratio over equal time intervals [1] **[1]**
**(c)** 0.625 = e^(−20/τ) [1]; τ = 20 / ln(1.6) = **43 s** [1] **[2]**
**(d)** t½ = 0.693 × 42.6 = **29 s** [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the property in (b), not just the arithmetic. A linear fall would give 2.0 V at 40 s, so state why that is wrong.

**7. (a)** Connect a voltage sensor across the capacitor, connected to the data-logger [1]. Set a sampling interval short compared with CR and record V as the capacitor discharges [1]. Plot ln V against t; the gradient is −1/CR [1] **[3]**
**(b)** Any two of: no reaction-time error in timing; many closely spaced readings; data plotted and processed immediately [1] [1] **[2]**
**(c)** A low-resistance voltmeter would take current from the capacitor, discharging it faster, so the measured CR would be too small [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* "More accurate" is too vague for part (b). State the specific source of error removed.

**8. (a)** I = −ΔQ/Δt (charge leaves the capacitor) [1]. V = IR and V = Q/C [1]. So Q/C = −R ΔQ/Δt, which gives **ΔQ/Δt = −Q/CR** [1] **[3]**
**(b)** CR = 400 × 10⁻⁶ × 15 × 10³ = 6.0 s; Q₀ = 400 × 10⁻⁶ × 6.0 = 2.4 mC [1]. ΔQ = −(2.4/6.0) × 1.5 = −0.60 mC, so Q = **1.8 mC** at 1.5 s [1]. Then **1.35 mC** at 3.0 s and **1.0125 mC (1.01 mC)** at 4.5 s [1] **[3]**
**(c)** The model gives 1.01 mC, about 10% below the exact 1.13 mC [1]. Use a smaller time step, which makes the assumed rate closer to the true rate during each step [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare numbers with a percentage or a difference, and state the direction of the error.

**9. (a)** lg I = lg I₀ − (lg e) t/CR, so gradient = −0.434/CR [1]. CR = 0.434 / 0.0145 = **30 s** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** I₀ = 10^2.00 = **100 µA** [1] **[1]**
**(c)** V₀ = I₀R = 100 × 10⁻⁶ × 47 000 = **4.7 V** [1] **[1]**
**(d)** C = CR/R = 30 / 47 000 [1] = **6.4 × 10⁻⁴ F** [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Check the log base before inverting the gradient. A base-10 plot needs the factor 0.434.

**10. (a)** τ = 2200 × 10⁻⁶ × 4.7 × 10³ = **10.3 s** [1] **[1]**
**(b)** V_C = 9.0(1 − e^(−15/10.34)) [1] = 9.0 × 0.7656 = **6.9 V** [1] **[2]**
**(c)** Q = CV = 2200 × 10⁻⁶ × 6.89 = **1.5 × 10⁻² C** [1] **[1]**
**(d)** V_R = 9.0 − 6.89 = 2.11 V [1]; I = 2.11 / 4700 = **4.5 × 10⁻⁴ A** [1] **[2]**
**(e)** New τ = 2200 × 10⁻⁶ × 12 × 10³ = 26.4 s [1]. 2.0 = 6.89 e^(−t/26.4) [1]; t = 26.4 ln(6.89/2.0) = **33 s** [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* In a multi-stage problem, the final value of one stage becomes x₀ for the next, and the time constant changes with the resistor.

**11. (a)** Q₀/2 = Q₀ e^(−t/CR) gives e^(−t/CR) = 1/2 [1]. Taking logs, t/CR = ln 2, so t = CR ln 2 [1] **[2]**
**(b)** R = t½ / (C ln 2) = 3.6 / (120 × 10⁻⁶ × 0.693) [1] = **4.3 × 10⁴ Ω** [1] **[2]**
**(c)** 10.8 s is three half-times, so (1/2)³ = 0.125, which is **12.5%** [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Part (c) is quickest by the constant-ratio property. It needs no value of R or C.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving C in µF or R in kΩ inside CR.
- Using x₀ as the start value in a charging equation when it is the final value.
- Applying the charging equation to the current.
- Reading CR as the gradient of a ln graph, or dropping its negative sign.
- Using a base-10 log graph without the 0.434 factor.
- Writing "more accurate" for a data-logger without the specific reason.
- Not stating the direction of error in a spreadsheet model.
- Treating one time constant as complete discharge.
