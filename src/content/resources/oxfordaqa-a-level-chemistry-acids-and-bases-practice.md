These questions cover section 3.1.10, Acids and bases (3.1.10.1 to 3.1.10.6), of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3, for International AS and A-level exams May/June 2020 onwards, including required practical 7. All questions are on International A2 material (**International A-level only**), assessed in Unit 3, with Unit 5 able to draw on any topic. Use Kw = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² dm⁻⁶ at 25 °C unless told otherwise, and give pH to 2 decimal places.

If a method is unfamiliar, go back to the [study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-acids-and-bases/) or the [revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-acids-and-bases-revision-notes/). ## Questions

**1.** This question is about Brønsted–Lowry theory.

**(a)** State what is meant by a Brønsted–Lowry acid. **[1]**
**(b)** CH₃NH₃⁺ + CN⁻ ⇌ CH₃NH₂ + HCN. Name the Brønsted–Lowry acid on each side of this equation. **[2]**

**2.** Work out:

**(a)** the pH when [H⁺] = 2.6 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ **[1]**
**(b)** the hydrogen ion concentration at pH 12.35 **[1]**
**(c)** the pH of 0.185 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid **[1]**
**(d)** the pH of a solution made by dissolving 0.81 g of hydrogen bromide (Mr = 80.9) in water and making it up to 250 cm³. **[2]**

**3.** Water ionises slightly: H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + OH⁻.

**(a)** Explain how the expression Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] is obtained from the equilibrium constant for the dissociation of water. **[2]**
**(b)** Barium hydroxide, Ba(OH)₂, is a strong base. Calculate the pH of 0.0225 mol dm⁻³ Ba(OH)₂ at 25 °C. **[2]**
**(c)** At 75 °C, Kw = 2.0 × 10⁻¹³ mol² dm⁻⁶. What is the pH of pure water at 75 °C? Say whether it is acidic, neutral or alkaline. **[2]**

**4.** HY is a weak monoprotic acid. Ka = 3.40 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³.

**(a)** Write an expression for Ka for HY. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the pH of a 0.0850 mol dm⁻³ solution of HY. **[3]**
**(c)** State the two approximations you used in (b). **[2]**

**5.** A 0.0640 mol dm⁻³ solution of a weak monoprotic acid has pH 2.62. Calculate Ka, with its units, and pKa. **[4]**

**6.** Three weak acids have these values: acid R, pKa = 2.86; acid S, Ka = 6.6 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³; acid T, pKa = 3.40. Calculate Ka for R and pKa for S, then list the three acids from strongest to weakest. **[3]**

**7.** A student dissolves 1.22 g of a solid monoprotic acid and makes 250 cm³ of solution in a volumetric flask. 25.0 cm³ portions are titrated with 0.0480 mol dm⁻³ NaOH. The titres are 21.05 cm³ (rough), 20.70 cm³, 20.80 cm³ and 20.75 cm³. Calculate the relative molecular mass of the acid. **[5]**

**8.** 35.0 cm³ of 0.120 mol dm⁻³ nitric acid is mixed with 20.0 cm³ of 0.150 mol dm⁻³ NaOH at 25 °C. Calculate the pH of the mixture. **[4]**

**9.** Indicator ranges: bromocresol green 3.8–5.4; phenol red 6.4–8.0; thymolphthalein 9.3–10.5.

**(a)** Methanoic acid is titrated with KOH. Explain why the pH at the equivalence point is above 7. **[2]**
**(b)** Choose an indicator from the list for the methanoic acid–KOH titration and explain your choice. **[2]**
**(c)** Choose an indicator from the list for titrating hydrochloric acid with aqueous ammonia. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain why none of the indicators suits a titration of methanoic acid with aqueous ammonia. **[1]**

**10.** In required practical 7, a student adds 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH from a burette to 20.0 cm³ of a weak monoprotic acid HW and records the pH. The equivalence point is at 18.60 cm³. At 9.30 cm³ of NaOH, the pH is 4.98.

**(a)** Give two steps the student should take to get reliable pH readings that define the curve well. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the concentration of HW. **[2]**
**(c)** Determine Ka for HW. **[2]**
**(d)** Use your answers to (b) and (c) to calculate the pH of the HW solution before any NaOH was added. **[2]**
**(e)** The student then titrates a weak base with a strong acid. State how the pH at equivalence would compare with 7. **[1]**

**11.** This question is about buffers.

**(a)** A basic buffer contains methylamine, CH₃NH₂, and methylammonium chloride. Explain, with equations, how it keeps its pH almost steady when a little acid or alkali is added. **[3]**
**(b)** A buffer is made by dissolving 2.04 g of sodium methanoate (Mr = 68.0) in 200 cm³ of 0.250 mol dm⁻³ methanoic acid. Ka for methanoic acid is 1.80 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³. Calculate the pH of the buffer. **[3]**
**(c)** 5.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ NaOH is added to the buffer in (b). Calculate the new pH. **[3]**
**(d)** Explain why adding water to the buffer in (b) does not change its pH. **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** A proton (H⁺) donor [1]
**(b)** Forward: **CH₃NH₃⁺** [1]; reverse: **HCN** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Give the formulae exactly as written in the equation, not a vague "the acid on the right".

**2. (a)** pH = −log₁₀(2.6 × 10⁻⁵) = **4.59** [1]
**(b)** [H⁺] = 10^(−12.35) = **4.47 × 10⁻¹³ mol dm⁻³** [1]
**(c)** [H⁺] = 0.185 mol dm⁻³, pH = **0.73** [1]
**(d)** n(HBr) = 0.81 / 80.9 = 0.0100 mol, so [H⁺] = 0.0100 / 0.250 = 0.0400 mol dm⁻³ [1]; pH = **1.40** [1]
*Examiner insight:* A pH below 1 is perfectly possible for a concentrated strong acid; do not "correct" it to a positive whole number.

**3. (a)** Kc = [H⁺][OH⁻] / [H₂O] [1]; so little water dissociates that [H₂O] is effectively constant, and Kw = Kc × [H₂O] = [H⁺][OH⁻] [1]
**(b)** [OH⁻] = 2 × 0.0225 = 0.0450 mol dm⁻³ [1]; [H⁺] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ / 0.0450 = 2.22 × 10⁻¹³, pH = **12.65** [1]
**(c)** [H⁺] = √(2.0 × 10⁻¹³) = 4.47 × 10⁻⁷, pH = **6.35** [1]; **neutral**, because [H⁺] = [OH⁻] [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (b), forgetting the two OH⁻ per Ba(OH)₂ gives 12.35; in (c), give the reason for "neutral", not just the word.

**4. (a)** **Ka = [H⁺][Y⁻] / [HY]** [1]
**(b)** [H⁺]² = 3.40 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.0850 = 2.89 × 10⁻⁶ [1]; [H⁺] = 1.70 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ [1]; pH = **2.77** [1]
**(c)** [H⁺] = [Y⁻] (H⁺ from water ignored) [1]; [HY] at equilibrium equals 0.0850 mol dm⁻³, as dissociation is so slight [1]
*Examiner insight:* Use square brackets for concentrations and include Y⁻ in the numerator.

**5.** [H⁺] = 10^(−2.62) = 2.40 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ [1]; Ka = (2.40 × 10⁻³)² / 0.0640 [1]; Ka = **8.99 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³** [1]; pKa = **4.05** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Keep the unrounded [H⁺] in your calculator when you square it; rounding to 2.4 × 10⁻³ first shifts Ka in the third figure.

**6.** Ka(R) = 10^(−2.86) = **1.38 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³** [1]; pKa(S) = −log₁₀(6.6 × 10⁻⁴) = **3.18** [1]; strongest to weakest: **R > S > T** (lowest pKa is strongest) [1]
*Examiner insight:* Convert all three to the same form before ranking, or the order often comes out reversed.

**7.** Mean of concordant titres = (20.70 + 20.80 + 20.75) / 3 = 20.75 cm³ [1]; n(NaOH) = 0.0480 × 20.75 / 1000 = 9.96 × 10⁻⁴ mol [1]; n(acid) in 25.0 cm³ = 9.96 × 10⁻⁴ mol (1 : 1) [1]; n(acid) in 250 cm³ = 9.96 × 10⁻³ mol [1]; Mr = 1.22 / 9.96 × 10⁻³ = **122** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Including the rough titre, or forgetting to scale from 25.0 cm³ to 250 cm³, gives a wrong Mr.

**8.** n(H⁺) = 0.120 × 0.0350 = 4.20 × 10⁻³ mol; n(OH⁻) = 0.150 × 0.0200 = 3.00 × 10⁻³ mol [1]; excess H⁺ = 1.20 × 10⁻³ mol [1]; [H⁺] = 1.20 × 10⁻³ / 0.0550 = 0.0218 mol dm⁻³ [1]; pH = **1.66** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Dividing by 35.0 cm³ instead of the total 55.0 cm³ gives pH 1.47; show the total volume clearly so the method is visible.

**9. (a)** At equivalence the solution contains methanoate ions, HCOO⁻ [1]; these are a base and accept protons from water, HCOO⁻ + H₂O ⇌ HCOOH + OH⁻, so [OH⁻] > [H⁺] [1]
**(b)** **Thymolphthalein** [1]; its whole range (9.3–10.5) lies within the vertical section, which is in the alkaline region for a weak acid–strong base titration [1]
**(c)** **Bromocresol green** [1]
**(d)** A weak acid–weak base curve has no vertical section, so no indicator changes colour sharply at one drop [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (b), say the whole range sits inside the steep section, not just "it changes in alkali".

**10. (a)** Calibrate the pH meter with buffers of known pH [1]; add NaOH in small portions (0.10–0.20 cm³) near the end point, stirring before each reading [1]
**(b)** n(NaOH) = 0.100 × 18.60 / 1000 = 1.86 × 10⁻³ mol = n(HW) [1]; [HW] = 1.86 × 10⁻³ / 0.0200 = **0.0930 mol dm⁻³** [1]
**(c)** 9.30 cm³ is half the equivalence volume, so [HW] = [W⁻] and pH = pKa = 4.98 [1]; Ka = 10^(−4.98) = **1.05 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³** [1]
**(d)** [H⁺] = √(1.05 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.0930) = 9.88 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ [1]; pH = **3.01** [1]
**(e)** **Below 7** [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (c), state why pH equals pKa at half-neutralisation; a bare Ka value without that link may lose the reasoning mark.

**11. (a)** Added acid: **CH₃NH₂ + H⁺ → CH₃NH₃⁺** [1]; added alkali: **CH₃NH₃⁺ + OH⁻ → CH₃NH₂ + H₂O** [1]; both CH₃NH₂ and CH₃NH₃⁺ are present in large amounts, so their ratio and the pH change very little [1]
**(b)** n(HCOO⁻) = 2.04 / 68.0 = 0.0300 mol [1]; n(HCOOH) = 0.250 × 0.200 = 0.0500 mol [1]; [H⁺] = 1.80 × 10⁻⁴ × 0.0500 / 0.0300 = 3.00 × 10⁻⁴, pH = **3.52** [1]
**(c)** OH⁻ (5.0 × 10⁻³ mol) converts HCOOH to HCOO⁻: n(HCOOH) = 0.0450 mol, n(HCOO⁻) = 0.0350 mol [1]; [H⁺] = 1.80 × 10⁻⁴ × 0.0450 / 0.0350 = 2.31 × 10⁻⁴ [1]; pH = **3.64** [1]
**(d)** Dilution lowers [HCOOH] and [HCOO⁻] by the same factor, so the ratio, and [H⁺], stay the same [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (c), work in moles and change both amounts, not just one.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using a hydroxide's formula concentration as [OH⁻] when each unit releases two OH⁻.
- Writing Kw with [H₂O] still in it, or with the wrong units.
- Using √(Ka × [HA]) for a buffer, where [H⁺] does not equal [A⁻].
- Finding the leftover concentration with one solution's volume rather than the mixture's.
- Choosing an indicator because one end of its range falls in the steep section.
- Leaving out the units of Ka (mol dm⁻³) when a question asks for them.
