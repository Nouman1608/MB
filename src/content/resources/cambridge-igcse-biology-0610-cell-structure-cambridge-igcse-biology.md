## 2.1.1 and 2.1.4 Plant and animal cells: structures and functions

| Structure | Function | Plant cell | Animal cell |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cell membrane | Controls what enters and leaves the cell; it is partially permeable | Yes | Yes |
| Nucleus | Contains the genetic material (chromosomes) that controls the cell's activities | Yes | Yes |
| Cytoplasm | Where most of the cell's chemical reactions take place | Yes | Yes |
| Ribosomes | Make proteins (protein synthesis) | Yes | Yes |
| Mitochondria | Where aerobic respiration releases energy | Yes | Yes |
| Cell wall | Made of cellulose; strengthens the cell, keeps its shape and stops it bursting when it takes in water | Yes | **No** |
| Chloroplasts | Contain chlorophyll, which absorbs light energy for photosynthesis | Yes, in green parts | **No** |
| Vacuole | A large, permanent vacuole full of cell sap; its pressure helps support the cell | Large and permanent | Small and temporary, if present |

**Comparing** means giving both sides. "Plant cells have a cellulose cell wall, but animal cells do not" scores; "plant cells have a cell wall" alone may not, if the question asks you to compare.

Note that not every plant cell has chloroplasts. Root cells grow underground, receive no light and have none.

## 2.1.2 and 2.1.4 The bacterial cell

| Structure | Function |
|---|---|
| Cell wall | Supports the cell and keeps its shape; it is **not** made of cellulose |
| Cell membrane | Controls what enters and leaves |
| Cytoplasm | Where chemical reactions take place |
| Ribosomes | Make proteins |
| Circular DNA | A single loop of genetic material lying free in the cytoplasm; controls the cell |
| Plasmids | Small rings of DNA carrying extra genes |

**Key difference:** a bacterial cell has **no nucleus**. Its DNA is a circular loop in the cytoplasm. It also has no mitochondria and no chloroplasts in the syllabus list.

## 2.1.3 Identifying structures in diagrams and images

Diagrams are tidy; micrographs (photographs taken through a microscope) are not. Use these clues.

- **Cell wall:** a thick outer boundary with a regular, often box-like shape. Plant cells sit side by side like bricks.
- **Cell membrane:** a thin line just inside the wall in plant cells; the outer boundary of animal and bacterial cells. In a plant cell it is usually pressed against the wall and hard to see.
- **Nucleus:** a round, darker structure, often stained.
- **Chloroplasts:** small green ovals, usually around the edge of the cell because the vacuole fills the centre.
- **Vacuole:** a large clear area in the middle of a plant cell.
- **Cytoplasm:** the grainy material between the membrane and the nucleus, or squeezed into a thin layer around the vacuole.
- **Ribosomes:** far too small to see with a light microscope; they show up as tiny dots only in electron micrographs.
- **Mitochondria:** very small; clearly visible only in electron micrographs, as rod or oval shapes with folded inner membranes.
- **Bacterial cell:** much smaller than plant and animal cells, with no nucleus; plasmids appear as small loops.

### Worked example 1: comparing from a diagram

*A diagram shows cell X, with a wall, a large central vacuole and green ovals, and cell Y, with a thin boundary, a nucleus and no wall. Identify each cell and explain your choice.*

> X is a **plant cell** because it has a cell wall, a large permanent vacuole and chloroplasts (**three features named**). Y is an **animal cell** because it has a nucleus but no cell wall, no chloroplasts and no large vacuole (**uses absent features as evidence**). Neither is a bacterial cell, because both have a nucleus (**rules out the third type**).

## 2.1.5 Where new cells come from

New cells are produced by the **division of existing cells**. Cells do not appear from nothing. This is how organisms grow, repair damage and replace worn-out cells, and how bacteria reproduce.

## 2.1.6 Specialised cells

A specialised cell has a structure suited to one particular function. The syllabus gives six examples, and you must state each function.

| Cell | Function (syllabus wording) | Feature that helps |
|---|---|---|
| Ciliated cell | Movement of mucus in the trachea and bronchi | Cilia on the surface beat to sweep mucus, carrying trapped dust and pathogens, up towards the throat |
| Root hair cell | Absorption | A long, thin extension gives a large surface area for taking in water and mineral ions |
| Palisade mesophyll cell | Photosynthesis | Many chloroplasts; tall cells packed near the upper surface of the leaf |
| Neurone | Conduction of electrical impulses | Long, thin fibres carry impulses over long distances |
| Red blood cell | Transport of oxygen | Contains haemoglobin; no nucleus, leaving more space for it |
| Sperm and egg cells (gametes) | Reproduction | Sperm has a tail to swim and many mitochondria; the egg has a large store of food in its cytoplasm |

The syllabus requires the function. The features are useful when a question asks you to explain how a cell is suited to its job.

## 2.1.7 Levels of organisation

| Level | Meaning | Animal example | Plant example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cell | The basic unit of a living organism | Red blood cell | Palisade mesophyll cell |
| Tissue | A group of cells with similar structures, working together to perform a shared function | Ciliated lining of the trachea | Palisade mesophyll |
| Organ | A structure made of a group of tissues, working together to perform specific functions | Heart; stomach | Leaf; root |
| Organ system | A group of organs with related functions, working together to perform body functions | Circulatory system; digestive system | Shoot system |
| Organism | A whole living thing | A human | A sunflower |

### Worked example 2: placing structures in the hierarchy

*Place each in the correct level: kidney, neurone, the breathing system, a layer of palisade cells, a fern.*

- Kidney: **organ** (several tissues working together).
- Neurone: **cell**.
- The breathing system: **organ system** (trachea, bronchi and lungs work together).
- A layer of palisade cells: **tissue** (similar cells with a shared function).
- A fern: **organism**.

## Common errors

- Saying animal cells have a cell wall, or that all plant cells have chloroplasts.
- Writing "the cell membrane protects the cell": its function is to **control** what enters and leaves.
- Writing "mitochondria make energy": they are where respiration **releases** energy.
- Giving bacteria a nucleus, or calling plasmids "the nucleus".
- Saying the bacterial cell wall is made of cellulose.
- Paraphrasing specialised-cell functions loosely, such as "neurones send messages" instead of "conduction of electrical impulses".
- Defining a tissue as "a group of cells" without "similar" and "a shared function".

## Exam technique for Cambridge command words

- **Identify:** name the structure from a label line or letter.
- **State:** a short answer, such as the function of a red blood cell.
- **Describe:** give the structure and what it does.
- **Compare:** give similarities and differences, with both cells in each sentence.
- **Explain:** link a feature to its function, using "so" or "because".

## Frequently asked questions

**Is any of 2.1 Extended only?** No. All seven outcomes of 2.1 are Core.

**What is the function of a plasmid?** A plasmid is a small ring of DNA in a bacterial cell that carries extra genes.

**Why can I not see ribosomes in a light micrograph?** They are too small; they are seen only with an electron microscope.
