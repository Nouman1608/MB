## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by corporate social responsibility. **[2]**

**2.** Which **one** of the following is an example of CSR?

A: Paying staff the legal minimum wage.

B: Displaying fire exit signs required by law.

C: Giving every employee two paid days a year to volunteer locally.

D: Paying corporation tax on time. **[1]**

**3.** Distinguish between a CSR policy and a CSR profile. **[3]**

**4.** Vellmore Foods has operating profit of £640,000. Its CSR programme costs £96,000 a year. Calculate the CSR programme's cost as a percentage of operating profit. **[2]**

**5.** Explain **one** way in which CSR could conflict with a business's objective of rapid growth. **[3]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Arden & Pryce sells clothing through its own shops. It sells 250,000 garments a year at an average price of £22 and has operating profit of £900,000. It plans to buy only certified, fairly traded cotton, which would raise the cost of each garment from £6.40 to £7.20.

**(a)** Calculate the increase in annual costs and the new operating profit, assuming prices and sales are unchanged. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage price rise needed to keep operating profit at £900,000 if sales stay at 250,000 garments. Give your answer to two decimal places. **[2]**

**(c)** Evaluate whether Arden & Pryce should switch to certified cotton. **[8]**

**7.** Glenvale Organic Dairy sells yoghurt under its own brand in supermarkets. It has a strong CSR profile: it pays farmers above-market prices, uses recyclable pots and publishes an annual environmental report. Evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of this CSR profile to Glenvale and its stakeholders. **[9]**

**8.** Rexton Fulfilment runs a warehouse with 400 staff, most on contracts with no guaranteed hours. Staff turnover is 45% a year, and each leaver costs £1,500 to replace. Local media have criticised its treatment of staff. The directors want to improve the CSR profile and are considering:

Option A: guaranteed-hours contracts for all staff, costing £350,000 a year.

Option B: a £50,000 annual donation to a local food bank.

Option C: £250,000 of solar panels on the warehouse roof.

**(a)** Calculate the current annual cost of replacing leavers. **[2]**

**(b)** Recommend and justify which option Rexton should choose. Use the figures where relevant. **[9]**

**9.** Analyse why CSR may be more important to a supermarket chain than to a business that makes components for washing machines. **[6]**

**10.** Explain how greenwashing could damage a business's CSR profile. **[4]**

**11.** "A business's only social responsibility is to make as much profit as possible for its owners, within the law." Evaluate this view. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** A business accepting responsibility for the effects of its activities on society, the environment and its stakeholders [1], voluntarily going beyond what the law requires [1]. **[2]**

**2.** **C** [1]. **[1]**

**3.** A CSR policy is the written set of commitments a business makes and how it will meet them [1]. A CSR profile is how responsible the business is seen to be by customers, staff, media and others [1]. The business controls its policy, but its profile depends on whether its actions match the policy [1]. **[3]**

**4.** 96,000 ÷ 640,000 × 100 [1] = **15%** [1]. **[2]**

**5.** A business that sets strict ethical standards for suppliers has fewer suppliers to choose from [1], so it may be unable to source enough stock quickly or cheaply when it expands [1], which slows growth or raises costs compared with rivals who use any supplier [1]. Also accept refusing profitable markets or contracts on ethical grounds, with development. **[3]**

**6. (a)** Increase per garment £7.20 − £6.40 = £0.80 [1]; 0.80 × 250,000 = **£200,000** a year [1]; new operating profit 900,000 − 200,000 = **£700,000** [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** 0.80 ÷ 22 × 100 [1] = **3.64%** [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** Knowledge: the switch is an ethical-sourcing CSR action that raises variable cost [1].
Application: without a price rise, profit falls by £200,000, which is 22.2% of current profit [1]; a price rise of about 3.6%, roughly 80p a garment, would restore it if sales held [1].
Analysis: shoppers who value ethical clothing may accept 80p more, especially if the claim is certified and can be marketed [1]; the certification also lowers the risk of a supply-chain scandal, which could cost far more than £200,000 in lost sales [1].
Counter-argument: if customers are price-sensitive and rivals do not switch, a price rise could cut sales, so profit could fall by more than £200,000 [1].
Judgement: Arden & Pryce should switch, provided market research shows its customers will accept a small price rise [1]; it could phase the change, starting with its best-selling lines, to test the response before committing fully [1]. **[8]**

**7.** Advantages to Glenvale: a strong profile supports a premium price in supermarkets [1] and builds loyalty among shoppers who buy organic food partly for ethical reasons [1]. Stakeholders gain: farmers receive secure, higher income [1], and the environment benefits from recyclable packaging [1].
Disadvantages: paying above-market prices raises costs, so margins are lower than rivals' [1]; supermarket buyers may press for lower prices regardless of Glenvale's costs [1]; a strong profile raises expectations, so any failure, such as a farm found breaching standards, would be especially damaging [1].
Evaluation: for an organic consumer brand the profile is central to why customers buy, so the advantages are likely to outweigh the costs [1]; the main risk is if supermarket buyers force prices down and Glenvale can no longer afford its commitments, so its success depends on its bargaining power with retailers [1]. **[9]**

**8. (a)** Leavers 45% × 400 = 180 [1]; 180 × £1,500 = **£270,000** a year [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Recommendation: option A [1].
It addresses the actual cause of the criticism, the treatment of staff, so it is credible [1]; options B and C are worthwhile but do not answer the media criticism and could look like an attempt to distract from it [1].
Figures: if guaranteed hours halved staff turnover to 22.5%, leavers would fall to 90 [1] and replacement costs to £135,000, a saving of £135,000 a year [1]; so the net cost of option A would be about £350,000 − £135,000 = £215,000 a year, before any gains in productivity from more experienced staff [1].
Stakeholders: employees gain secure income; the local community gains more stable jobs; owners bear the cost [1].
Condition: the turnover saving is an assumption; if turnover falls by less than half, the net cost is higher [1]. Rexton could also introduce guaranteed hours for longer-serving staff first to test the effect, then add option C later as a separate environmental step [1]. **[9]**

**9.** A supermarket sells directly to millions of consumers, so its brand is highly visible [1] and stories about its suppliers, packaging or staff can quickly change where people shop [1]. It also has a long supply chain of food and clothing, where ethical and environmental risks are high [1]. A component maker sells to a few business customers [1] who may choose mainly on price, quality and delivery [1], so its profile has less direct effect on sales, although large customers may still audit suppliers [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Greenwashing means making environmental or social claims that actions do not support [1]. If customers, journalists or campaigners expose the gap [1], the business is seen as dishonest as well as irresponsible [1], so trust and sales may fall further than if it had made no claims at all [1]. **[4]**

**11.** For the view: owners provide the capital and bear the risk, so managers who spend profit on CSR are spending owners' money [1]; the law already sets standards for pay, safety and pollution [1]; a profitable business creates jobs and pays taxes, which benefits society [1].
Against the view: a business depends on staff, customers, suppliers and the community, so ignoring their interests can harm profit in the long run [1]; CSR can raise profit through loyalty, a price premium and lower staff turnover [1]; the law is a minimum and often lags behind public expectations, so a business that does only what is legal may face boycotts [1].
Evaluation: the view is strongest for a business competing on low prices with thin margins, where CSR spending could threaten survival [1]. It is weakest for consumer brands whose customers choose on values [1]. Judgement: profit and CSR are less opposed in the long run than the statement suggests, so most businesses gain from CSR that is linked to their core activities and can be justified by its effect on stakeholders who matter to them [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Counting legal compliance as CSR.
- Saying "CSR is expensive" without using the case figures.
- Recommending charity donations for a problem in the business's own operations.
- Listing advantages and disadvantages without weighing them for this business.
- Ignoring the time period in the conflict between CSR and profit.
