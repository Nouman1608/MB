## Questions

### Section A

**1.** Distinguish between a change in demand and a change in quantity demanded. **[2]**

**2.** A café raises the price of a sandwich from 4.00 to 5.00 and daily sales fall from 1,000 to 900.

**(a)** Calculate the price elasticity of demand. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the change in the café's total revenue from sandwiches and explain why it changed in this
direction. **[2]**

**3.** Average income in Town M rises from 30,000 to 33,000 and weekly demand for a supermarket's value-range
pasta falls from 200 to 190 packs. Calculate the income elasticity of demand and classify the good. **[2]**

**4.** The cross elasticity of demand for brand A with respect to the price of brand B is +2.5. Explain what
this value shows. **[2]**

**5.** Explain **three** factors that influence the price elasticity of supply of strawberries. **[3]**

**6.** Explain why, in a perfectly competitive market, the supply curve is the marginal cost curve. **[2]**

### Section B

**7.** In the market for a phone accessory, Qd = 900 − 30P and Qs = 60P − 180.

**(a)** Calculate the equilibrium price and quantity. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the excess demand or excess supply if the price were 10. **[2]**

**(c)** A subsidy changes supply to Qs = 60P − 90. Calculate the new equilibrium. **[2]**

**(d)** Calculate the price elasticity of demand for the move from the original to the new equilibrium price.
**[2]**

**8.** A frost destroys part of a major coffee harvest at the same time as coffee becomes more popular with
young consumers. Using a diagram, explain the likely effects on the world price and quantity of coffee. **[4]**

**9.** Explain how a government target to use more maize for biofuel is likely to affect the market for maize
used in food. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how a fall in the demand for beef is likely to affect the market for leather. **[4]**

**11.** Explain why a poor harvest may increase farmers' total revenue. **[6]**

**12.** Rents in City Z rose by 15% in two years. Assess whether demand and supply analysis is sufficient to
explain this rise. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** A change in demand is a **shift of the whole demand curve**, caused by a determinant other than the
good's own price, such as income [1]. A change in quantity demanded is a **movement along the curve**, caused
only by a change in the good's own price [1]. **[2]**

**2. (a)** %Δ price = 1.00 ÷ 4.00 = +25%; %Δ quantity = −100 ÷ 1,000 = −10% [1]. PED = −10 ÷ 25 = **−0.40** [1].

**(b)** Revenue rises from 4.00 × 1,000 = 4,000 to 5.00 × 900 = 4,500, an increase of **500** [1]. Demand is
price inelastic, so the percentage fall in sales is smaller than the percentage rise in price [1].

**3.** %Δ income = +10%; %Δ quantity = −10 ÷ 200 = −5% [1]. YED = −5 ÷ 10 = **−0.50**: an **inferior good**
[1]. **[2]**

**4.** A positive value means the brands are **substitutes**: a rise in B's price raises demand for A [1]. A value
above 1 means they are **close substitutes**: a 1% rise in B's price raises demand for A by 2.5% [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Any three, explained [3]: **time period** — output cannot be increased until new plants crop, so supply is
inelastic in the short run; **perishability and stocks** — strawberries cannot be stored for long, so supply
cannot be increased from stock; **spare capacity** — growers with unused polytunnels can expand faster;
**ease of switching land and labour** — if fields can switch from other crops, supply becomes more elastic over
a season.

**6.** A profit-maximising firm that is a price taker produces where **price equals marginal cost** [1]. At each
price, the quantity it supplies is read off its MC curve, so the MC curve (above average variable cost) traces
out the quantity supplied at each price [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** 900 − 30P = 60P − 180, so 1,080 = 90P and **P = 12** [1]; Q = 900 − 360 = **540** [1].

**(b)** Qd = 900 − 300 = 600; Qs = 600 − 180 = 420 [1]. **Excess demand of 180** [1].

**(c)** 900 − 30P = 60P − 90, so 990 = 90P and **P = 11** [1]; Q = 900 − 330 = **570** [1].

**(d)** %Δ price = −1 ÷ 12 = −8.33%; %Δ quantity = 30 ÷ 540 = +5.56% [1]. PED = 5.56 ÷ (−8.33) = **−0.67**
[1].

**8.** The frost shifts **supply left** [1]; greater popularity shifts **demand right** [1]. Diagram showing both
shifts with a higher equilibrium price [1]. **Price rises** for certain, but the change in **quantity is
uncertain**: it falls if the supply shift is larger and rises if the demand shift is larger [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Maize has **composite demand**: it is wanted for fuel and for food [1]. The biofuel target raises demand
for maize as fuel, which bids up its price and draws maize away from food uses [1]. Supply of maize for food
shifts **left** [1], so the price of food maize **rises** and the quantity traded falls [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Beef and hides are in **joint supply**: slaughtering cattle for beef also produces hides for leather [1].
Lower beef demand reduces its price and the number of cattle slaughtered [1]. Fewer hides are produced, so
supply of leather shifts **left** [1], raising the price of leather and reducing quantity traded, though leather
demand has not changed [1]. **[4]**

**11.** A poor harvest shifts the **supply curve left** [1], raising the equilibrium price and reducing
quantity [1]. Demand for many basic foods is **price inelastic**, because they are necessities with few
substitutes [1]. So the percentage rise in price is **greater than** the percentage fall in quantity [1].
Total revenue (price × quantity) therefore **rises** for farmers as a whole [1]. Individual farmers whose own
crop failed may still lose, and if imports are easily available demand facing domestic farmers is more elastic
[1]. **[6]**

**12.** *Demand and supply explanation:* rising population or incomes in City Z shift demand for rented housing
right [1]; supply is price inelastic in the short run because building takes years and land is limited [1], so
a demand shift raises rents sharply [1]; supply may also have shifted left if landlords sold up because of higher
costs or taxes [1]. *Limits of the model:* rents are often fixed in contracts and adjust slowly, so the market is
rarely in equilibrium [1]; landlords and tenants lack full information about prices on offer [1]; planning rules,
interest rates and expectations of future prices also matter, so many factors changed at once, breaking ceteris
paribus [1]. *Judgement:* the model explains the direction and the size of the rise well when supply
elasticity is considered, but it needs adding to with institutional factors such as planning restrictions and
contract terms [2]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using absolute changes instead of percentage changes in elasticity formulae.
- Dropping the negative sign on PED, XED or YED where the sign carries meaning.
- Claiming both price and quantity changes are certain when two curves shift.
- Naming a market relationship without moving a curve in the second market.
- Explaining revenue changes without linking them to the value of PED.
- Evaluation with no judgement about which factors mattered most.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to give elasticities to a set number of decimal places?**
Follow the question. Where none is stated, two decimal places and the correct sign are a sensible default.

**How do I show uncertainty when two curves shift?**
Say which variable changes for certain, then explain that the other depends on the relative size of the two
shifts, ideally with one sentence on each possibility.
