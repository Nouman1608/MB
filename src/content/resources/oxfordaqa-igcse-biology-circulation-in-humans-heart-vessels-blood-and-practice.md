## Questions

**1.** Four blood vessels are described. Name each one.
**(a)** Carries blood from the left ventricle to the body. **[1]**
**(b)** Returns blood from the body to the right atrium. **[1]**
**(c)** Carries blood from the right ventricle to the lungs. **[1]**
**(d)** Brings blood from the lungs to the left atrium. **[1]**

**2.** Humans have a double circulatory system. Explain what this means and give one benefit. **[3]**

**3.** A student counts 75 pulse beats in 50 seconds. The left ventricle pushes out 58 cm³ of blood with each beat.
**(a)** Calculate the heart rate in beats per minute. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the volume of blood pumped in one minute, in dm³ to 2 significant figures. **[2]**

**4.** A man has coronary heart disease.
**(a)** Explain how the disease reduces the supply of oxygen to his heart muscle. **[2]**
**(b)** One coronary artery has an internal diameter of 4.0 mm. Fatty material narrows it to 1.6 mm. Calculate the percentage decrease in diameter. **[2]**
**(c)** State how a stent helps. **[1]**

**5.** A patient has a faulty heart valve.
**(a)** Describe the two main ways in which a heart valve can become faulty. **[2]**
**(b)** The table gives data about two types of replacement valve, supplied for patients followed for 12 years.

| | Biological valve | Mechanical valve |
|---|---|---|
| Patients needing a second valve operation (%) | 38 | 9 |
| Bleeding problems per 100 patients per year | 0.9 | 2.4 |

A healthy 30-year-old needs a replacement valve. Evaluate the use of each type for this patient and reach a conclusion. **[4]**

**6.**
**(a)** State where the natural pacemaker is found and what it controls. **[2]**
**(b)** State what an artificial pacemaker does. **[1]**
**(c)** Give one reason why an artificial heart might be fitted to a patient. **[1]**

**7.** Three blood vessels have these features:
vessel X: thick wall containing muscle and elastic fibres;
vessel Y: wall one cell thick, very narrow;
vessel Z: thin wall with valves.
**(a)** Name vessels X, Y and Z. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why vessel Z has valves. **[1]**
**(c)** Name one substance that passes from the blood into cells through the wall of vessel Y, and one that passes from cells into the blood. **[1]**

**8.** A microscope image shows a white blood cell (cell A) and a red blood cell (cell B). The image of cell A is 63 mm wide and its real width is 10.5 µm. Cell B is 45 mm wide in the same image.
**(a)** Calculate the magnification of the image. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the real width of cell B in µm. **[2]**
**(c)** Cell B has no nucleus. Name the cell type and the pigment that fills it. **[1]**

**9.** Blood clots when a blood vessel is cut.
**(a)** Describe how a clot forms. **[3]**
**(b)** A sample of blood from a healthy person clots in 5.5 minutes. A sample from a patient taking a clot-preventing drug clots in 11.0 minutes. Calculate the percentage increase in clotting time. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest why a patient with a mechanical heart valve may be given such a drug. **[1]**

**10.** A hospital blood bank has 200 donors: 88 group O, 70 group A, 30 group B and 12 group AB.
**(a)** Explain why blood typing is needed before a transfusion. **[1]**
**(b)** Group A plasma contains anti-B antibodies. Explain why a group A patient should not be given group B red cells. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why group O is called the universal donor. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the percentage of these donors whose red cells could be given safely to a group A patient. **[2]**
**(e)** State which recipient group can safely receive red cells from every donor group. **[1]**

**11.** A 45-year-old patient has heart failure. Of 250 patients on the waiting list for a heart transplant, 14 per cent die before a donor heart is found.
**(a)** Calculate how many of the 250 patients die while waiting. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain how antibodies cause a transplanted heart to be rejected. **[2]**
**(c)** Describe two ways of reducing the chance of rejection. **[2]**
**(d)** Evaluate the use of an artificial heart compared with waiting for a transplant. **[3]**

**12.** The table shows data for a woman at rest and during exercise.

| | Heart rate (beats per minute) | Volume per beat (cm³) |
|---|---|---|
| At rest | 72 | 70 |
| Exercise | 156 | 95 |

**(a)** Calculate the volume of blood pumped per minute at rest and during exercise. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage increase in volume pumped per minute during exercise. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the volume of blood pumped per minute increases during exercise. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** (a) Aorta [1]. (b) Vena cava [1]. (c) Pulmonary artery [1]. (d) Pulmonary vein [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers such as "the artery" or "the vein" are too vague; give the full name.

**2.** There are two separate circuits [1]: one takes blood from the heart to the lungs and back, the other takes blood from the heart to all the other organs and back [1]. Benefit: after passing through the lungs the blood goes back to the heart for a second push, so it arrives at the organs at higher pressure [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Say that blood goes through the heart two times on each complete circuit; "two sides" alone is thin.

**3.** (a) Heart rate = 75 ÷ 50 × 60 [1] = **90 beats per minute** [1].
(b) Volume = 58 × 90 = 5220 cm³ [1] = 5.22 dm³, so **5.2 dm³ per minute** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the conversion to one minute and the cm³ to dm³ step; visible steps are easier to credit.

**4.** (a) Fatty deposits on the inner wall of a coronary artery make its channel narrower [1]; less blood flows to the heart muscle, so it receives less oxygen [1].
(b) Decrease = 4.0 − 1.6 = 2.4 mm, and 2.4 ÷ 4.0 × 100 [1] = **60 per cent** [1].
(c) A stent holds the artery open, so blood flows more freely [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the narrowing to a lack of oxygen for the muscle. In percentage change, divide by the original value.

**5.** (a) The valve tissue stiffens so it cannot open fully [1]; the valve develops a leak [1].
(b) Biological valve: fewer bleeding problems (0.9 against 2.4 per 100 patients per year) [1] but 38 per cent need a second operation, compared with 9 per cent for a mechanical valve [1]. Mechanical valve: far fewer repeat operations, which matters for a 30-year-old who will live with the valve for decades, but more bleeding problems, linked to anticoagulant drugs needed to stop clots forming on the valve [1]. Conclusion supported by the data, for example a mechanical valve because repeat surgery is much less likely, with bleeding risk monitored [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* An evaluation gains credit when it uses figures for both options and then commits to a conclusion for this patient.

**6.** (a) Right atrium [1]; the resting heart rate [1].
(b) It is an electrical device that corrects irregularities in the heart rate [1].
(c) To keep the patient alive while waiting for a transplant, or to let the heart rest and recover [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the chamber, not just "the heart", and state the function precisely.

**7.** (a) X is an artery [1]; Y is a capillary [1]; Z is a vein [1].
(b) Valves prevent back-flow of blood, so it moves only towards the heart [1].
(c) Oxygen or glucose passes into cells, and carbon dioxide passes into the blood [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* In part (c) give one substance for each direction.

**8.** (a) Convert: 10.5 µm = 0.0105 mm [1]; magnification = 63 ÷ 0.0105 [1] = **× 6000** [1].
(b) Real width = 45 ÷ 6000 = 0.0075 mm [1] = **7.5 µm** [1].
(c) Red blood cell, containing haemoglobin [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Put both lengths into the same unit before dividing, and write the formula first.

**9.** (a) Platelets help the blood clot at the wound; a series of enzyme-controlled reactions takes place [1]; fibrinogen is changed to fibrin [1]; fibrin forms a network of fibres that traps blood cells to form the clot [1].
(b) Increase = 11.0 − 5.5 = 5.5 minutes; 5.5 ÷ 5.5 × 100 [1] = **100 per cent** [1].
(c) Blood can clot on the artificial valve surface, and the drug reduces this risk [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Name both fibrinogen and fibrin and say which changes into which; platelets do not turn into fibrin.

**10.** (a) A patient given red cells carrying an antigen their antibodies recognise would have those cells attacked, so the groups must be matched [1].
(b) Group B red cells carry B antigens [1]; the group A patient's anti-B antibodies would attack them [1].
(c) Group O red cells have no A or B antigens, so no recipient's antibodies attack them [1].
(d) Compatible donors are group O and group A: 88 + 70 = 158 [1]; 158 ÷ 200 × 100 = **79 per cent** [1].
(e) Group AB [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Say which group is donor and which is recipient, and link antigens to antibodies.

**11.** (a) 14 ÷ 100 × 250 = **35 patients** [1].
(b) The donor heart has antigens that are different from the recipient's own [1]; the recipient's antibodies do not recognise them as part of the body and attack them [1].
(c) Use a donor with a tissue type similar to the recipient's [1]; treat the recipient with drugs that suppress the immune system [1].
(d) Artificial heart: no wait for a donor, and no rejection [1]; but risks of blood clots, infection or mechanical or power failure, and it is mostly used as a temporary measure [1]. Transplant: a living organ does the whole job, but donors are scarce and drugs are needed to prevent rejection; a supported judgement, for example using an artificial heart to keep this patient alive while waiting for a transplant [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* A balanced evaluation names an advantage and a disadvantage for both options, then uses the waiting-list figure in a judgement.

**12.** (a) Rest: 72 × 70 = **5040 cm³ per minute** [1]. Exercise: 156 × 95 = **14 820 cm³ per minute** [1].
(b) Increase = 14 820 − 5040 = 9780; 9780 ÷ 5040 × 100 [1] = **194 per cent** (3 s.f.) [1].
(c) Muscles need more oxygen and glucose for more respiration, so more blood must be delivered [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Give the number of significant figures asked for, and tie the extra blood flow to oxygen and glucose supply.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming vessels by oxygen content and calling the pulmonary vein deoxygenated.
- Using the new value rather than the starting value as the divisor in a percentage change.
- Forgetting to convert micrometres to millimetres, or minutes to seconds, before calculating.
- Describing a stent as removing the fatty material, rather than holding the artery open.
- Writing a one-sided evaluation with no judgement for the patient described.
- Saying "the body rejects it" without antibodies and antigens.
- Reversing donor and recipient when using the compatibility table.
