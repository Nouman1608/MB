## Section A: short questions

**1.** Explain the difference between the context in which a text is *set* and the context in which
it was *written*, and give one reason a writer might separate them. **[3]**

**2.** Define *allegory* and explain how you should write about an allegorical text so that you still
analyse the writer's methods. **[2]**

**3.** A student must choose between two questions on their text: one on a character they know well
but have few quotations for, and one on a theme they can argue strongly with quotations across the
text. Which should they choose, and why? Give two reasons. **[3]**

**4.** Rewrite this sentence so that it analyses rather than retells: *"Then the boys go up the
mountain and see the beast and run away."* Explain the change. **[3]**

**5.** Identify and correct four kinds of error in this original student sentence, which would cost
marks under AO4: *"in an inspector calls, priestley shows that the birlings dont care about eva."* **[4]**

**6.** Explain why a stage direction counts as evidence of the playwright's methods. **[2]**

## Section B: longer questions

Read the original scene, then answer Question 7.

> *Original scene.* A small kitchen, evening. A single bare bulb. MAUREEN irons a school shirt. Her
> son DEAN, sixteen, enters, still wearing his coat.
>
> MAUREEN: You're late.
> DEAN: Bus.
> MAUREEN *(not looking up)*: There was a letter. From the school.
> *(DEAN stops. He does not take off his coat.)*
> DEAN: What's it say?
> MAUREEN: You tell me. *(She switches the radio off. Silence.)* Go on. You're the one who reads.
> *(DEAN picks up the letter and reads. MAUREEN irons the same sleeve, again and again.)*

**7.** How does the writer use stage directions and dialogue to present the relationship between
Maureen and Dean in this scene? **[8]**

Read the original prose passage, then answer Question 8. It is narrated by a child.

> *Original passage:* Our street had a rule that nobody wrote down: you didn't knock at number nine.
> Mum said it was because old Mrs Adeyemi was tired, and I believed her, the way you believe the moon
> follows the car. It was only when the van came, and two men in grey carried out one small brown
> suitcase and nothing else, that I wondered who had been tired, and who had simply been left alone.

**8.** How does the writer use the child narrator in this passage? **[6]**

**9.** Using your own studied modern text, write a five-minute plan for: *How does the writer present
the effects of power on people in the text?* Show your argument, four moments spread across the text,
and one context link. **[6]**

**10.** Using your own studied modern text: *How does the writer present a character who changes
during the text? Write about what the character says and does, and how the writer presents the
character by the ways he or she writes.* **[34]**

**11.** Using your own studied modern text: *How far does the writer present the society in the text
as unfair? Write about the ideas the writer explores, and how the writer presents these ideas by the
ways he or she writes.* **[34]**

## Answers

**1.** The setting context is the time and place in which events happen; the writing context is the
time and situation in which the writer produced the text [1]. They may be separated to give the
audience hindsight the characters lack [1], so the writer can expose the characters' attitudes or
warn the audience about their own time [1].

**2.** An allegory is a story whose characters and events stand for real people, events or ideas [1].
You should explain the parallel briefly but focus on how the writer's choices, such as names,
symbols, events or narrative voice, make the parallel and shape the reader's judgement [1].

**3.** The theme question [1]. Reasons: it allows a clear argument across the whole text [1], and the
candidate has quotations to support analysis, which AO1 and AO2 both require [1].

**4.** Example: *"Golding presents the boys' fear as something they create themselves: the 'beast'
they flee from on the mountain is, as the reader knows, a dead parachutist, which suggests the danger
lies in their own minds."* [1]
The rewrite names the writer as the agent [1] and interprets the event's meaning rather than listing
actions [1].

**5.** Corrected: *"In* An Inspector Calls*, Priestley shows that the Birlings don't care about
Eva."* One mark for each kind of error: a capital letter to begin the sentence [1]; the title given
capitals and marked as a title, in italics or underlined in handwriting [1]; capitals for the proper
nouns "Priestley", "Birlings" and "Eva" [1]; the apostrophe of omission in "don't" [1].

**6.** Stage directions are written by the playwright, not improvised by actors [1], so they show
deliberate choices about setting, movement, sound and silence that create meaning for an audience [1].

**7.** Award one mark per developed point, up to 8:

- Maureen "not looking up" shows withheld attention, suggesting anger or fear held in check [1].
- Dean's one-word reply "Bus" signals defensiveness and a strained relationship [1].
- Dean keeping his coat on suggests he is ready to leave, or does not feel at home [1].
- Switching off the radio creates silence, which focuses the audience and raises tension [1].
- "You tell me" seems a challenge, giving Maureen power over him [1].
- "You're the one who reads" shifts the power: it may imply that she cannot read the letter herself,
  so her control hides vulnerability [1].
- Ironing "the same sleeve, again and again" is a repeated action that shows anxiety on stage
  without words [1].
- The short lines and pauses show a relationship in which much is left unsaid, so the conflict is
  presented through what is not spoken [1].

**8.** Award one mark per developed point, up to 6:

- The narrator's innocent acceptance of "Mum said" shows a child's trust in adult explanations [1].
- The simile of believing "the moon follows the car" captures childish misunderstanding warmly [1].
- The unwritten street "rule" suggests a community that avoids responsibility without admitting it
  [1].
- The single "small brown suitcase and nothing else" is an understated image of a lonely life [1].
- The final sentence marks the child's dawning understanding, shown through the contrast of
  "tired" and "left alone" [1].
- The child narrator lets the reader see the truth before the speaker fully does, creating
  dramatic irony and a quiet criticism of neglect [1].

**9.** Award marks as follows:

- A one-sentence argument about power's effects that includes a judgement or change [1].
- Four moments spread across beginning, middle and end [2].
- A method linked to each moment [2].
- One context link that explains the writer's purpose [1].

**10.** AQA marks this holistically against level descriptors; the weighting is a guide only.

- **AO1 [12]:** a clear argument about how and why the character changes, supported by references
  from across the text; awareness that the character is a construct used to explore ideas.
- **AO2 [12]:** analysis of language in dialogue or narration, dramatic or narrative methods (stage
  directions, entrances, narrator, symbolism), and structure, such as turning points and the ending.
- **AO3 [6]:** context of setting or writing that explains the writer's purpose in showing this
  change.
- **AO4 [4]:** a range of vocabulary and sentence structures, with accurate spelling and punctuation.

**11.** As for Question 10, marked holistically:

- **AO1 [12]:** a judgement on "how far", with evidence for and against the society being presented as
  unfair, drawn from across the text.
- **AO2 [12]:** methods that present unfairness: contrasting characters, settings or symbols; language
  of power and exclusion; structural choices, such as who is rewarded or punished at the end.
- **AO3 [6]:** relevant context, such as class, politics, gender or race at the time of writing or
  setting, linked to specific moments.
- **AO4 [4]:** controlled, accurate expression with varied vocabulary.

## Using these questions well

Questions 1 to 8 rehearse the close-analysis moves you need to make from memory in the real exam.
Plan Question 9 in five minutes, then write Question 10 or 11 in about 45 minutes without your text.
Check afterwards that every paragraph contains a method, and that your evidence comes from the
beginning, middle and end of the text.
