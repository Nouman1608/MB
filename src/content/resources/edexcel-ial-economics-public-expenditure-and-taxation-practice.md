## Questions

**1.** (a) Classify each item as current expenditure, capital expenditure or a transfer payment: (i) wages of public school teachers, (ii) building a new railway line, (iii) housing benefit paid to low-income tenants. **[3]**
**(b)** State whether income tax on wages and an excise duty on fuel are direct or indirect taxes. **[2]**

**2.** Country N spends 84bn on current items, 21bn on capital items and 45bn on transfer payments. GDP is 600bn.
**(a)** Calculate public expenditure as a percentage of GDP. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate government spending (G) in the aggregate demand identity as a percentage of GDP. **[2]**

**3.** Country T has 5m people and GDP of 150bn. 20% are aged 65 or over and cost 12,000 each per year in pensions and care. 18% are under 15 and cost 6,000 each in schooling.
**(a)** Calculate the age-related spending as a percentage of GDP. **[3]**
**(b)** Later the shares become 26% aged 65 or over and 15% under 15. Costs per head and GDP are unchanged. Calculate the new percentage. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain one change in the pattern of spending. **[1]**

**4.** Country V's government spends an extra 30bn, financed by borrowing. The multiplier is 1.8. Private investment falls by 12bn.
**(a)** Calculate the rise in output if there were no crowding out. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the rise in output after crowding out. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why private investment falls. **[3]**

**5.** Country W has a tax-free allowance of 12,000 and charges 30% on income above it.
**(a)** Find the tax paid, and the average rate of tax, for incomes of 20,000 and 80,000. **[4]**
**(b)** Classify the tax and justify your answer. **[2]**

**6.** A worker earns 15 an hour for 36 hours a week. The marginal income tax rate rises from 25% to 35%.
**(a)** Calculate the net hourly wage before and after the change. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate net weekly pay at 36 hours before and after. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the hours needed to keep the original net pay, and explain the two effects on hours worked. **[3]**

**7.** The table shows the tax base at different income tax rates in Country Z (billions, invented).

| Rate | 10% | 25% | 40% | 55% | 70% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Base | 1,000 | 900 | 760 | 580 | 360 |

**(a)** Calculate revenue at each rate. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the change in revenue if the rate falls from 70% to 55%, and explain it. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why you cannot conclude that 55% maximises revenue. **[2]**

**8.** A government cuts income tax by 8bn. The marginal propensity to save is 0.10, to import 0.20 and to be taxed 0.20.
**(a)** Calculate the rise in output. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the induced rise in imports. **[1]**
**(c)** Comment on the effect on the trade balance. **[2]**

**9.** A sales tax rises from 8% to 12%. Taxed goods make up 50% of the consumer price basket. Calculate the likely rise in the price level if the tax is fully passed on, and explain the aggregate supply effect. **[4]**

**10.** A firm can invest 150 in Country X (corporation tax 28%) or Country Y (18%). Pre-tax profit would be 20 in either.
**(a)** Calculate the post-tax return in each country. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain two other factors affecting the choice. **[2]**

**11.** Country R has a negative output gap. Evaluate whether its government should cut income tax rates to raise real output and employment. **[10]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** (i) Current [1]. (ii) Capital [1]. (iii) Transfer payment [1]. **(b)** Income tax on wages is direct [1]; excise duty on fuel is indirect [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Classify by what the money does: a payment to a household that buys no output is a transfer, whoever receives it.

**2. (a)** Total = 84 + 21 + 45 = 150bn [1]. 150 / 600 = **25.0%** [1]. **(b)** Exclude transfers: 84 + 21 = 105bn [1]. 105 / 600 = **17.5%** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Give the total before the percentage, and say why the second figure is lower, since transfers buy no output.

**3. (a)** Older: 1.0m × 12,000 = 12.0bn [1]. Children: 0.9m × 6,000 = 5.4bn [1]. Total 17.4bn, which is 17.4 / 150 = **11.6%** of GDP [1]. **(b)** Older: 1.3m × 12,000 = 15.6bn; children: 0.75m × 6,000 = 4.5bn; total 20.1bn [1]. 20.1 / 150 = **13.4%** [1]. **(c)** Spending shifts from education towards pensions and care as the population ages [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Convert the percentages into numbers of people first; slips tend to start there.

**4. (a)** 30 × 1.8 = **54bn** [1]. **(b)** Net injection = 30 − 12 = 18bn [1]. Output rises by 18 × 1.8 = **32.4bn** [1]. **(c)** Borrowing raises demand for a limited pool of savings [1]. Interest rates rise [1]. Borrowing costs more, so fewer investment projects offer an acceptable return [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Show each link in the chain from borrowing to the interest rate to investment; naming "crowding out" alone adds nothing.

**5. (a)** At 20,000: (20,000 − 12,000) × 30% = **2,400** [1]; average rate 2,400 / 20,000 = **12.0%** [1]. At 80,000: 68,000 × 30% = **20,400** [1]; average rate **25.5%** [1]. **(b)** Progressive, because the average rate rises with income [1], even though the marginal rate is constant at 30%, since the allowance has a smaller proportional effect as income rises [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Support the classification with average rates at two incomes; a high or flat marginal rate is not evidence by itself.

**6. (a)** 15 × 0.75 = **11.25** [1]; 15 × 0.65 = **9.75** [1]. **(b)** 36 × 11.25 = **405** [1]; 36 × 9.75 = **351** [1]. **(c)** 405 / 9.75 = **41.5 hours** [1]. Working longer to keep take-home pay is the income effect [1]. The net wage falls 13.3%, so leisure is cheaper, and the substitution effect may lead her to work fewer hours [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Name both effects and say which direction each pulls; the overall result is uncertain without evidence.

**7. (a)** Revenue = rate × base [1]: 100, 225, 304, 319 and 252 [1]. **(b)** 319 − 252 = **+67** [1]. The base grows enough to outweigh the lower rate, so the economy was past the peak [1]. **(c)** Only five rates are given, and the maximum could lie between 40% and 70% [1]. The true peak depends on avoidance, evasion and how people respond over time [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Reason from the data given and state what is not known; confident claims about the true peak are hard to support from five data points.

**8. (a)** Multiplier = 1 / (0.10 + 0.20 + 0.20) = 2 [1]. First-round spending = 8 × (1 − 0.10 − 0.20) = 5.6bn [1]. Output rises by 5.6 × 2 = **11.2bn** [1]. **(b)** 0.20 × 11.2 = **2.24bn** [1]. **(c)** Imports rise as income rises, so the trade balance worsens [1], unless lower business costs raise exports enough to offset it [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Apply the leakages to the first round before the multiplier, then carry the output figure into part (b).

**9.** Price of a taxed good: 100 → 108 → 112 [1]. Rise = 4 / 108 = 3.70% [1]. Across the basket: 3.70% × 0.5 = **1.85%** [1]. The tax raises firms' costs, so short-run aggregate supply shifts left and the price level rises [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the price of the taxed good first; then scale by the basket weight.

**10. (a)** Country X: 20 × 0.72 = 14.4; 14.4 / 150 = **9.6%** [1][1]. Country Y: 20 × 0.82 = 16.4; 16.4 / 150 = **10.9%** [1][1]. **(b)** Market size or skills of workers [1]. Quality of infrastructure or political stability [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare the two returns and state the gap (1.3 percentage points) before giving other factors.

**11.** Analysis: a tax cut raises disposable income [1]. Households spend part of it, so aggregate demand shifts right [1]. The multiplier raises income by more than the first round [1]. Output and employment rise when there is spare capacity [1]. Lower marginal rates may raise labour supply through the substitution effect [1]. Evaluation: part leaks into saving and imports, which weakens the effect [1]. With a negative output gap, extra demand raises output more than prices [1]. The cut lowers revenue and widens the deficit unless the economy is on the downward slope of the Laffer curve [1]. Judgement: a cut is likely to help if the gap is large and the cut is temporary and targeted at lower-income households who spend most of it [1]. The conclusion should depend on the size of the gap and the use of the lost revenue [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* A judgement tends to gain credit when it names the conditions on which it depends, rather than repeating the earlier points.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Including transfers in G, or leaving them out of total public expenditure.
- Calling a tax progressive without comparing average rates.
- Using the marginal rate where the average rate is needed.
- Describing only one of the two effects on work incentives.
- Applying the multiplier to the whole tax cut instead of the first-round spending.
- Treating the Laffer curve as showing where a real economy sits.
- Writing "crowding out" with no mechanism.
- Calling every tax cut inflationary without mentioning spare capacity.
- Leaving out a judgement in extended answers.
