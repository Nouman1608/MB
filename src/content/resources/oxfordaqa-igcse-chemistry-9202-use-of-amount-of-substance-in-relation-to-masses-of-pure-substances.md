This guide goes deeper into section 3.6.2 Use of amount of substance in relation to masses of pure substances (statements a to c) of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Chemistry (9202) specification, Version 6.2, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. The course is untiered. It covers three calculation skills: finding relative formula mass (Mᵣ), finding the percentage by mass of an element, and working out empirical and molecular formulae. Relative atomic masses (Aᵣ) come from the periodic table you are given; this guide uses the values printed in the specification's table, including Cl = 35.5 and Cu = 63.5. The topic overview is in the [quantitative chemistry study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-chemistry-quantitative-chemistry/).

## What the specification asks

| Statement | Content |
|---|---|
| 3.6.2 a | Mᵣ of a compound is the sum of the Aᵣ values of the atoms in the numbers shown in the formula; calculate Mᵣ from a formula |
| 3.6.2 b | Calculate the percentage by mass of an element in a compound from Aᵣ and Mᵣ |
| 3.6.2 c | Calculate empirical formulae from masses or percentages, and molecular formulae |

## Relative formula mass (3.6.2 a)

Add up the Aᵣ of every atom in the formula. Two features cause most errors.

- **Brackets:** the subscript after a bracket multiplies everything inside it.
- **Water of crystallisation:** in a formula such as MgSO₄·7H₂O, the dot means "plus", and the 7 multiplies the whole water molecule.

### Worked example 1: four formulae

| Formula | Working | Mᵣ |
|---|---|---|
| (NH₄)₂SO₄ | 2 × (14 + 4 × 1) + 32 + 4 × 16 = 36 + 32 + 64 | **132** |
| Ca(NO₃)₂ | 40 + 2 × (14 + 3 × 16) = 40 + 124 | **164** |
| Al₂(SO₄)₃ | 2 × 27 + 3 × (32 + 64) = 54 + 288 | **342** |
| MgSO₄·7H₂O | 24 + 32 + 64 + 7 × 18 = 120 + 126 | **246** |

Mᵣ has **no units**. The same number in grams is the mass of one mole, which you use in 3.6.3.

## Percentage by mass (3.6.2 b)

**percentage by mass of element = (Aᵣ × number of atoms of that element) ÷ Mᵣ × 100**

The step most often missed is multiplying by the number of atoms.

### Worked example 2: comparing two fertilisers

*Which supplies more nitrogen per kilogram: ammonium nitrate, NH₄NO₃, or ammonium sulfate, (NH₄)₂SO₄?* (Aᵣ: H = 1, N = 14, O = 16, S = 32)

- NH₄NO₃: Mᵣ = 14 + 4 + 14 + 48 = 80. It contains **2** nitrogen atoms: 28 ÷ 80 × 100 = **35.0%**.
- (NH₄)₂SO₄: Mᵣ = 132. It also contains **2** nitrogen atoms: 28 ÷ 132 × 100 = **21.2%**.

Ammonium nitrate supplies more nitrogen per kilogram. This kind of comparison links to fertiliser production in 3.8.3.

### Worked example 3: mass of an element in a sample

*What mass of iron is contained in 40 kg of iron(III) oxide, Fe₂O₃?* (Aᵣ: Fe = 56, O = 16)

- Mᵣ = 2 × 56 + 3 × 16 = 160; percentage of iron = 112 ÷ 160 × 100 = 70.0%.
- Mass of iron = 70.0% of 40 kg = **28 kg**.

Percentage by mass also works for water in a hydrated salt: in MgSO₄·7H₂O, water is 126 ÷ 246 × 100 = 51.2% of the mass.

## Empirical and molecular formulae (3.6.2 c)

The **empirical formula** is the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound. The **molecular formula** is the actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule; it is a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula.

### The method

| Step | What to do |
|---|---|
| 1 | Write the mass (or percentage) of each element |
| 2 | Divide each by its Aᵣ |
| 3 | Divide every answer by the smallest |
| 4 | If a value is close to x.5, x.33 or x.25, multiply all values by 2, 3 or 4 |
| 5 | Write the formula using the whole numbers |

A table keeps the working clear and earns method marks.

### Worked example 4: from experimental masses

1.12 g of iron is heated in chlorine and forms 3.25 g of iron chloride. Find its empirical formula. (Aᵣ: Fe = 56, Cl = 35.5)

| | Fe | Cl |
|---|---|---|
| Mass / g | 1.12 | 3.25 − 1.12 = 2.13 |
| ÷ Aᵣ | 1.12 ÷ 56 = 0.0200 | 2.13 ÷ 35.5 = 0.0600 |
| ÷ smallest | 1 | 3 |

Empirical formula: **FeCl₃**.

The first step uses conservation of mass (3.6.1): the extra mass of the product is the chlorine that combined.

### Worked example 5: from percentages, then molecular formula

A hydrocarbon contains 82.8% carbon and 17.2% hydrogen by mass. Its Mᵣ is 58. Find its empirical and molecular formulae. (Aᵣ: C = 12, H = 1)

| | C | H |
|---|---|---|
| % | 82.8 | 17.2 |
| ÷ Aᵣ | 6.90 | 17.2 |
| ÷ smallest | 1 | 2.49 |
| × 2 | 2 | 5 |

- 2.49 is close to 2.5, so multiply by 2: empirical formula **C₂H₅**.
- Empirical formula mass = 2 × 12 + 5 × 1 = 29.
- 58 ÷ 29 = 2, so the molecular formula is **C₄H₁₀** (butane).

Do not round 2.49 to 2: that would give CH₂, which is a different compound.

### Worked example 6: a hydrated salt

4.92 g of hydrated magnesium sulfate, MgSO₄·xH₂O, is heated until all the water is driven off. 2.40 g of anhydrous MgSO₄ remains. Find x. (Mᵣ: MgSO₄ = 120, H₂O = 18)

- Mass of water = 4.92 − 2.40 = 2.52 g.
- Moles: MgSO₄ = 2.40 ÷ 120 = 0.0200; H₂O = 2.52 ÷ 18 = 0.140.
- Ratio = 0.140 ÷ 0.0200 = 7, so x = **7**.

This is the empirical method with the whole water molecule treated as one unit.

## Common mistakes

- Missing brackets: Ca(NO₃)₂ is not 40 + 14 + 96.
- Forgetting to multiply by the number of atoms in a percentage calculation.
- Dividing by the atomic number instead of the relative atomic mass.
- Rounding 1.5 or 2.5 to the nearest whole number instead of scaling up.
- Using the mass of the compound, not the element, in the empirical table.
- Writing a molecular formula that is not a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula.

## Exam technique: command words

- **Calculate:** show each addition for Mᵣ; examiners award method marks for correct working.
- **Determine the empirical formula:** use a table with the headings in the method above.
- **Show that:** when the answer is given, every step must be written out; do not just restate the answer.
- **Compare:** give both values and say which is larger, as in worked example 2.
