## Section A

**1.** Define the term "wave refraction". **[2]**

**2.** Explain why coasts facing a long fetch usually receive larger waves. **[2]**

**3.** State the difference between high-energy and low-energy waves in terms of swash and backwash, and the effect of each on a beach. **[2]**

**4.** Use these relationships for deep water: wavelength L ≈ 1.56 × T², where T is the period in seconds; wave energy is proportional to the square of wave height.

**(a)** Calculate the deep-water wavelength of a wave with a period of 12 s. Show your working. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate how many times more energy a 3.0 m wave carries than a 1.2 m wave. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the steepness of a wave 3.0 m high with a wavelength of 60 m. **[1]**

**5.** The table shows a sediment budget for the fictional Penhallow sub-cell (thousands of m³ per year).

| Inputs | Volume | Outputs | Volume |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cliff erosion | 140 | Loss offshore | 70 |
| River supply | 90 | Growth of a spit at the downdrift end | 110 |
| Onshore movement from the sea bed | 30 | Dredging of a harbour channel | 50 |

**(a)** Calculate the sediment budget for the sub-cell. **[2]**

**(b)** A sea wall is proposed that would stop 80% of the cliff erosion. Calculate the new budget, assuming all other values stay the same. Show your working. **[3]**

**(c)** Suggest **three** likely effects of the sea wall on the coast downdrift of it. **[3]**

**6.** Explain why solution is an important process of erosion on coasts made of chalk or limestone. **[2]**

**7.** Explain how the type of breaking wave is related to beach gradient. **[3]**

## Section B

**8.** Explain how the balance between marine and sub-aerial processes influences the profile of a cliff. **[6]**

**9.** Explain how longshore drift operates, and outline **two** sources of the sediment it moves. **[6]**

**10.** "Wave energy is the most important factor affecting the rate of coastal erosion." How far do you agree? **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** The bending of wave crests as they approach the coast [1], because the parts of each wave in shallower water slow down first, so crests become more parallel to the depth contours [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Wind blows over a longer distance of open water [1], so more energy is transferred to the waves, which grow higher and longer [1]. **[2]**

**3.** High-energy waves have backwash stronger than swash, so they remove sediment and lower the beach [1]. Low-energy waves have swash stronger than backwash, so they build the beach up [1]. **[2]**

**4. (a)** L ≈ 1.56 × 12² = 1.56 × 144 [1] = 224.6 m [1].

**(b)** (3.0 ÷ 1.2)² = 2.5² [1] = 6.25 times as much energy [1].

**(c)** 3.0 ÷ 60 = 0.05 [1]. **[5]**

**5. (a)** Inputs = 140 + 90 + 30 = 260; outputs = 70 + 110 + 50 = 230 [1]. Budget = 260 − 230 = +30 thousand m³ per year, a surplus [1].

**(b)** Cliff input falls to 140 × 0.2 = 28 [1]. New inputs = 28 + 90 + 30 = 148 [1]. New budget = 148 − 230 = −82 thousand m³ per year, a deficit [1].

**(c)** Less sediment moves along the coast by longshore drift, so downdrift beaches narrow [1]. Narrower beaches absorb less wave energy, so cliffs downdrift erode faster [1]. The spit at the downdrift end receives less sediment and may stop growing or be breached [1]. **[8]**

**6.** Sea water, and rainwater on the cliff, can chemically dissolve calcium carbonate [1], so chalk and limestone are weakened and removed in solution even under low wave energy [1]. **[2]**

**7.** On gentle beaches, waves break gradually as spilling breakers over a wide surf zone [1]. On moderate slopes, the crest curls and crashes down as a plunging breaker [1]. On steep beaches, waves surge up the beach without fully breaking, giving strong backwash [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Marine processes undercut the cliff foot and remove debris [1]. Sub-aerial weathering and mass movement deliver material down the cliff face [1]. Where marine processes dominate, debris is removed quickly, so the cliff stays steep or vertical [1], as in resistant, well-jointed rock exposed to high energy [1]. Where sub-aerial processes dominate, as in weak, saturated clay or till, slumping produces a lower-angled, stepped profile [1]. Debris left at the foot temporarily protects the cliff until waves remove it [1]. **[6]**

**9.** Waves approach the shore at an angle, driven by the prevailing wind [1]. Swash carries sediment up the beach at that angle [1]. Backwash returns it straight down the beach under gravity [1]. Repeated, this moves sediment in a zig-zag along the coast in the direction of wave approach [1]. Sources include rivers, which bring sediment to the coast [1], and eroding cliffs, which supply sediment of the rocks they are made of [1]. **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* also credit the sea bed, shells and coral fragments, wind-blown sand or beach nourishment as sources.

**10.** The real exam marks this essay with levels of response, not point by point. The points below are indicative creditworthy content.

*Introduction:* defines wave energy and its controls: wind speed, duration and fetch [1]. Identifies other factors: geology, beach, sub-aerial processes, sea level and human activity [1].

*Agree:* wave energy rises with the square of wave height, so storms cause far more erosion than calm conditions [1]. Coasts with long fetches, exposed to ocean swell, erode faster [1]. Refraction concentrates energy on headlands, explaining where erosion is greatest [1]. Hydraulic action, cavitation and corrasion all depend on the force of breaking waves [1]. Single storms can remove metres of cliff in a few days, showing the importance of high-energy events [1].

*Disagree:* rock type controls the rate: weak till or clay retreats far faster than granite under the same waves [1]. Joints, faults and dip create lines of weakness [1]. A wide beach absorbs energy, so sediment supply can protect cliffs [1]. Sub-aerial processes, such as slumping after heavy rain, can cause more retreat than direct wave attack [1]. Solution can erode limestone and chalk under low energy [1]. Human activity, such as defences that starve downdrift beaches, changes erosion rates [1]. Sea level determines the height at which waves attack [1].

*Interaction:* erosion depends on wave energy acting on rock of a given resistance [1]. Without waves to remove debris, sub-aerial processes slow down [1].

*Use of examples:* a named coast with evidence, such as a rapidly retreating till coast [1].

*Judgement:* wave energy is a necessary driver [1], but geology often explains the difference between places [1]; a reasoned final view that varies with scale and coast type [1]. **[20]**

*Examiner insight:* the best answers distinguish between why erosion happens (energy) and why its rate differs from place to place (geology, beaches, people).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using height instead of height squared in energy calculations.
- Forgetting to recalculate total inputs in a changed sediment budget.
- Describing longshore drift without the angle of wave approach.
- Treating attrition as cliff erosion.
- Essays that list factors without weighing them against wave energy.
