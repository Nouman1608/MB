This set practises section 3.6.1 Conservation of mass including the quantitative interpretation of chemical equations (statements a to e) of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Chemistry (9202) specification, Version 6.2, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. Section A covers equations and state symbols; Section B covers mass changes and reacting-mass calculations. Relative atomic masses are given in each question. The course is untiered.

## Section A

**1.** Balance this equation: Mg + HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂ **[1]**

**2.** Balance this equation: C₄H₁₀ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O **[2]**

**3.** Write a word equation for the reaction of zinc with sulfuric acid. **[1]**

**4.** Add state symbols to this equation for a precipitation reaction:

AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl + NaNO₃ **[2]**

**5.** A student says: "When a candle burns, mass is destroyed, because the candle gets lighter." Explain why the student is wrong. **[2]**

**6.** In a sealed flask, 2.00 g of solid reacts with 50.00 g of solution. No solid remains at the end. State the total mass of the contents of the flask after the reaction and give a reason. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Calcium carbonate decomposes when heated strongly.

CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ (Aᵣ: Ca = 40, C = 12, O = 16)

**(a)** Calculate the maximum mass of calcium oxide that can be made from 25.0 g of calcium carbonate. **[3]**

**(b)** Use conservation of mass to find the mass of carbon dioxide released. **[1]**

**(c)** A student heats 25.0 g of calcium carbonate in a crucible but some of the solid has not decomposed. Explain what effect this has on the mass of solid left in the crucible compared with your answer to (a). **[2]**

**8.** Marble chips are added to excess hydrochloric acid in an open conical flask on a balance.

| Time / min | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mass of flask and contents / g | 96.40 | 95.82 | 95.40 | 95.18 | 95.08 | 95.08 |

CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂ (Aᵣ: Ca = 40, C = 12, O = 16)

**(a)** Explain why the mass decreases. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the total mass of carbon dioxide given off. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the mass of calcium carbonate that reacted. **[3]**

**(d)** State how the experiment could be changed so that the mass stays constant. **[1]**

**9.** Zinc reacts with sulfur when heated: Zn + S → ZnS (Aᵣ: Zn = 65, S = 32)

**(a)** Calculate the mass of sulfur needed to react exactly with 13.0 g of zinc. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the mass of zinc sulfide formed. **[1]**

**10.** A student heats magnesium ribbon in a crucible, lifting the lid from time to time.

| Measurement | Mass / g |
|---|---|
| Empty crucible and lid | 24.36 |
| Crucible, lid and magnesium | 24.72 |
| Crucible, lid and product after heating | 24.96 |

2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO (Aᵣ: Mg = 24, O = 16)

**(a)** Calculate the mass of magnesium used and the mass of oxygen that combined with it. **[2]**

**(b)** Use the equation to calculate the mass of magnesium oxide that should form, and compare it with the experimental result. **[3]**

**(c)** Explain why the lid must be lifted from time to time. **[1]**

**(d)** Some white smoke escapes when the lid is lifted. Explain the effect this would have on the final mass. **[1]**

**11.** Aluminium is extracted by electrolysis of aluminium oxide: 2Al₂O₃ → 4Al + 3O₂ (Aᵣ: Al = 27, O = 16)

**(a)** Calculate the relative formula mass of aluminium oxide. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the maximum mass of aluminium that can be obtained from 51 tonnes of aluminium oxide. **[3]**

**12.** Write balanced symbol equations, with state symbols, for these reactions.

**(a)** Magnesium reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid to form magnesium chloride solution and hydrogen gas. **[2]**

**(b)** Lead(II) nitrate solution reacts with potassium iodide solution to form a precipitate of lead(II) iodide and potassium nitrate solution. **[2]**

**13.** In industry, the calculated mass of product is often not obtained. For each process, give the reason from the specification that best explains the shortfall.

**(a)** Ammonia is made from nitrogen and hydrogen; some ammonia breaks down again into nitrogen and hydrogen. **[1]**

**(b)** After crystallisation, some copper sulfate crystals stick to the filter paper. **[1]**

**(c)** Methane burns in a limited supply of air and some carbon monoxide forms. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂ [1].

**2.** 2C₄H₁₀ + 13O₂ → 8CO₂ + 10H₂O: carbon and hydrogen balanced [1]; oxygen balanced [1].

*Examiner insight:* C₄H₁₀ + 6½O₂ → 4CO₂ + 5H₂O balances but whole numbers are expected; double everything.

**3.** zinc + sulfuric acid → zinc sulfate + hydrogen [1].

**4.** AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq): (aq) for the three solutions [1]; (s) for silver chloride [1].

**5.** No atoms are destroyed; the wax reacts with oxygen to form carbon dioxide and water vapour [1], which are gases that escape into the air, so the candle's mass falls [1].

**6.** 52.00 g [1]. It is a closed system, so no atoms enter or leave and mass is conserved [1].

**7. (a)** Mᵣ CaCO₃ = 100; moles = 25.0 ÷ 100 = 0.250 mol [1]; ratio 1 : 1, so 0.250 mol CaO; Mᵣ CaO = 56 [1]; mass = 0.250 × 56 = **14.0 g** [1].

**(b)** 25.0 − 14.0 = **11.0 g** [1].

**(c)** The mass left will be greater than 14.0 g [1], because less carbon dioxide has been released, so some of the original mass is still in the crucible as calcium carbonate [1].

**8. (a)** Carbon dioxide gas is produced and escapes from the open flask [1].

**(b)** 96.40 − 95.08 = **1.32 g** [1].

**(c)** Mᵣ CO₂ = 44; moles = 1.32 ÷ 44 = 0.0300 mol [1]; ratio CaCO₃ : CO₂ = 1 : 1, so 0.0300 mol CaCO₃ [1]; mass = 0.0300 × 100 = **3.00 g** [1].

**(d)** Carry out the reaction in a sealed flask (or fit a balloon to trap the gas) [1].

**9. (a)** Moles of Zn = 13.0 ÷ 65 = 0.200 mol [1]; 0.200 mol S × 32 = **6.4 g** [1].

**(b)** 13.0 + 6.4 = **19.4 g** (or 0.200 × 97) [1].

**10. (a)** Magnesium = 24.72 − 24.36 = **0.36 g** [1]; oxygen = 24.96 − 24.72 = **0.24 g** [1].

**(b)** Moles Mg = 0.36 ÷ 24 = 0.015 mol [1]; 0.015 mol MgO × 40 = **0.60 g** [1]. Experimental mass = 24.96 − 24.36 = 0.60 g, which agrees [1].

**(c)** To let oxygen (air) in so the magnesium can react completely [1].

**(d)** Some magnesium oxide is lost, so the final mass would be lower than it should be [1].

**11. (a)** 2 × 27 + 3 × 16 = **102** [1].

**(b)** 2 × 102 = 204 parts of Al₂O₃ give 4 × 27 = 108 parts of Al [1]; scale factor 51 ÷ 204 = 0.25 [1]; mass Al = 108 × 0.25 = **27 tonnes** [1].

**12. (a)** Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g): balanced [1]; state symbols [1].

**(b)** Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2KI(aq) → PbI₂(s) + 2KNO₃(aq): balanced [1]; state symbols [1].

**13. (a)** The reaction is reversible, so it does not go to completion [1].

**(b)** Some product is lost when it is separated from the reaction mixture [1].

**(c)** Some reactants react in ways different from the expected reaction [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving fractions in a balanced equation.
- Using (aq) for a precipitate or for water.
- Explaining a mass decrease without naming the gas that escaped.
- Using the 2 in 2Mg as a reason to double the moles of magnesium oxide when the ratio is 1 : 1.
- Mixing tonnes and grams in a mass-ratio calculation.
