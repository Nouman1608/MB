**These notes condense how to read a letter in Paper 1 Section A of OxfordAQA International GCSE
English Language (9270).** The specification lists "letters" among the forms of literary
non-fiction. For full explanation and a model answer, use the
[letters as literary non-fiction study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-english-language-9270-letters-as-literary-non-fiction/);
for the section as a whole, see the
[Section A literary non-fiction revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-english-language-9270-section-a-literary-non-fiction-revision-notes/).

## Three things only a letter has

1. **A named reader**: the relationship shapes every choice.
2. **A moment of writing**: often close to events, without full hindsight.
3. **A purpose in the relationship**: reassurance, news, apology, request, persuasion.

## Before you read: four margin notes

```
WHO writes?      ______________________
TO WHOM?         ______________________
WHEN / WHERE?    ______________________
WHY?             ______________________
```

## Conventions as evidence

| Convention | Ask |
|---|---|
| Place and date | How far away? How soon after the events? |
| Greeting | How formal, how close? |
| Opening | What comes first, and why? |
| Direct address | Comfort, persuasion or worry? |
| Order of topics | What is delayed or avoided? |
| Sign-off | What relationship does it confirm? |
| Postscript | Is the real feeling hidden here? |

## Kinds of letter

| Kind | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Letter home | The gap between experience and what the family is told |
| Personal letter to a friend or partner | Private language, shared references, humour |
| Formal letter | Controlled register, explicit purpose |
| Open letter | A named addressee and a wider public audience |
| Sequence of letters | Change in situation or relationship across sections |

## The two audiences

- **The addressee**: what the letter does for them (comforts, persuades, informs).
- **You, the exam reader**: what the letter reveals beyond its intention.

Useful frame: *"For the recipient, … ; for us as readers, …"*

## Reading objectives applied to letters

- **R1**: select details of the writer's situation, remembering they were chosen for one reader.
- **R2**: gather scattered details (about money, health, homesickness) into one point.
- **R3**: interpret what the writer wants the reader to feel, and what may be hidden.
- **R4**: analyse how language is adapted: pet names, reassurance, questions, sudden short sentences.

## Language features common in letters

- **Hedging and reassurance**: "honestly", "really", "don't worry".
- **Understatement**: playing down a problem ("a bit of trouble").
- **Imperatives as care**: "Look after yourself", "Tell Dad…".
- **Questions to the reader**: keeping the relationship alive across distance.
- **Shifts of register**: formal phrases breaking into intimate ones.
- **Time markers**: "since I last wrote", "by the time this reaches you".

## Turning a feature into a point

```
Convention or feature  +  short quotation  +  effect on the addressee
                       +  what it reveals to us  +  link to the writer's perspective
```

Example: the postscript's "No hurry" plays down the request for the addressee, but tells us the
writer is cold and too proud to ask directly.

## Whole-text questions on a sequence

- Compare the first greeting with the last.
- Compare the first sign-off with the last.
- Note where jokes stop or start.
- Find the letter where the writer says most, and the one where they say least.
- Ask what the final letter can say that the first could not.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Addressee | The person a letter is written to |
| Epistolary | Relating to letters |
| Register | The level of formality of language |
| Postscript (PS) | An addition after the signature |
| Open letter | A letter addressed to one person but meant for public reading |
| Understatement | Presenting something as less serious than it is |
| Subtext | Meaning beneath the surface of what is said |
| Hindsight | Understanding gained later; often absent in letters |

## Exam traps

- Ignoring who the letter is written to.
- Accepting a cheerful surface without asking what it hides.
- Skipping the greeting, sign-off or postscript.
- Treating a sequence of letters as one block.
- Confusing the addressee's response with your own.
- Spending too long: Section B carries the same 40 marks.

## Self-test

1. Which forms of literary non-fiction does the specification list for Paper 1 Section A?
2. Name three features that make a letter different from a memoir.
3. Why can a letter create tension that a memoir does not?
4. What four margin notes should you make before reading a letter?
5. Why is a postscript worth analysing?
6. What is an open letter?
7. Explain the difference between the addressee and the exam reader.
8. Give two language features of reassurance.
9. How should you approach a sequence of letters?
10. What does R2 look like when the text is a letter?
11. What is subtext?
12. Write a sentence frame that distinguishes the two audiences.

## Answers

1. Autobiography, biography, letters, memoir and travel or adventure writing.
2. A named reader, a moment of writing close to events, and a purpose in the relationship.
3. The writer may not know how events will turn out, so the reader feels the uncertainty.
4. Who writes, to whom, when and where, and why.
5. Writers often place their most important or difficult point there, as if it were an afterthought.
6. A letter addressed to one person but intended to be read publicly.
7. The addressee is the person the letter was written to; the exam reader notices what the letter
   reveals beyond its intention.
8. Any two: hedging words such as "honestly"; "don't worry"; understatement; caring imperatives.
9. Track how the situation, feelings or relationship change from one letter to the next.
10. Gathering details scattered across the letter, or across letters, into one developed point.
11. Meaning beneath the surface of what is said.
12. For example: "For the recipient, the letter offers comfort; for us as readers, it reveals the
    strain behind that comfort."
