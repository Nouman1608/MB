This guide goes deeper into sub-topic 7.2 of Option G, Urban environments, in IB Diploma Programme Geography (first assessments 2019): changing urban systems. It is examined in Paper 1 if your school teaches this option. The [7.2 revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-geography-changing-urban-systems-revision-notes/) already describe the city as a system, the main processes of change, the Detroit and London Docklands cases and compound growth calculations. This page adds what those notes leave short: how to break urban growth into its components, how the processes fit into a sequence of stages, why growth and decline tend to reinforce themselves, how cities expand at the rural-urban fringe in lower-income countries, and how to judge regeneration strategies. Test yourself with the [7.2 practice questions](/resources/ib-dp-geography-7-2-practice/); the [Urban environments option study guide](/resources/ib-dp-geography-urban-environments-study-guide/) gives the overview of all four sub-topics.

## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Explain how urban populations change through natural change and migration.
- Explain the processes that change urban areas: suburbanisation, counter-urbanisation, re-urbanisation, deindustrialisation and regeneration.
- Explain how economic, demographic and political change interact in cities.
- Evaluate how different groups are affected by change.

## The components of urban growth

Urban population grows through three components:

1. **Natural increase:** births minus deaths among existing residents.
2. **Net migration:** people moving in minus people moving out, from rural areas, other cities or abroad.
3. **Reclassification:** settlements becoming classed as urban, or city boundaries being extended to include surrounding places.

### Worked example 1: decomposing growth

An invented city grows from 4.0 million to 4.6 million over five years. During that time it records 550,000 births and 150,000 deaths. A boundary change adds 50,000 people from neighbouring villages.

- Total growth = 4.6 - 4.0 = 0.6 million = 600,000.
- Natural increase = 550,000 - 150,000 = 400,000.
- Reclassification = 50,000.
- Net migration = 600,000 - 400,000 - 50,000 = 150,000.

As shares of total growth: natural increase 400,000 / 600,000 = **66.7%**; net migration 150,000 / 600,000 = **25%**; reclassification 50,000 / 600,000 = **8.3%**.

This pattern, with natural increase as the largest component, is typical of many fast-growing cities, because earlier migrants were young adults who now have children in the city. It matters for policy: slowing migration would not stop growth, so cities must plan for schools and housing regardless.

## Stages of urban development

A widely used model sequences the processes of change:

| Stage | Core city | Suburbs and wider region | Main driver |
|---|---|---|---|
| Urbanisation | Grows fast | Grows slowly | Industrialisation and rural-urban migration |
| Suburbanisation | Slows or declines | Grows fast | Cars, mortgages, demand for space |
| Counter-urbanisation | Declines | Small towns and rural areas grow | Better transport, remote work, retirement |
| Re-urbanisation | Grows again | Growth slows | Regeneration, young professionals, city-centre living |

Many high-income cities have passed through all four. Many cities in lower-income countries are still in the first stage, although suburban and peri-urban growth is happening alongside it. Use the model to classify a city's current stage, then evaluate it: stages overlap, cities do not move in lockstep, and policy (for example, green belts or new towns) can change the sequence.

## Why change reinforces itself: cumulative causation

Gunnar Myrdal (1957) argued that once a place gains an advantage, growth tends to attract further growth. Applied to cities:

- **Upward spiral:** new firms create jobs, which attract workers, whose spending supports more businesses and a larger tax base, which pays for better infrastructure, which attracts more firms.
- **Downward spiral:** factory closures cut jobs, residents with skills leave, spending and tax revenue fall, services and housing decline, and the city becomes less attractive to new investment.

Myrdal called the drain of people and capital from weaker regions to growing ones **backwash effects**, and the benefits that eventually reach surrounding areas **spread effects**. The Detroit case in the revision notes is a downward spiral; London's financial district is an upward one. Regeneration policy is essentially an attempt to break a downward spiral.

## Peri-urbanisation in lower-income countries

At the edge of many fast-growing cities, urban and rural land uses mix in a zone called the **peri-urban** fringe. Typical features:

- informal or unplanned housing on former farmland, often without piped water, sewers or paved roads;
- factories and warehouses attracted by cheap land and new highways;
- farming that continues on remaining plots, sometimes supplying the city with vegetables;
- unclear land ownership and weak planning control.

The peri-urban fringe absorbs much of the new population of growing cities. It is where infrastructure gaps, flood risk and land conflict are often greatest, so it is a useful focus for questions about who is affected by urban change.

## Judging regeneration

Regeneration takes different forms, each with typical strengths and limits:

| Approach | Example | Strength | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Property-led | London Docklands | Large private investment and new jobs | Jobs may not suit existing residents; gentrification |
| Flagship cultural projects | The Guggenheim Museum in Bilbao, opened in 1997 | Rebrands the city and attracts visitors | Benefits concentrated in tourism and the centre |
| Community-led | Small-scale neighbourhood schemes | Fits local needs; residents stay | Limited funding and scale |
| Right-sizing shrinking cities | Demolition of vacant housing, conversion of land to parks or urban farms | Matches services to a smaller population | Admits decline; can be resisted by remaining residents |

Bilbao is often cited for the "Bilbao effect": using a striking cultural building to revive a declining industrial city. Critics note that the museum was part of a wider programme, including a new metro and riverside renewal, so attributing success to one building oversimplifies.

### Worked example 2: who benefits?

An invented regeneration scheme creates 8,000 new jobs. A survey finds that 2,000 went to residents of the regenerated district, whose previous unemployment rate was 18% among 30,000 working-age residents.

- Unemployed residents before the scheme = 30,000 x 0.18 = 5,400.
- If all 2,000 local hires were previously unemployed, unemployment falls to 5,400 - 2,000 = 3,400, a rate of 3,400 / 30,000 x 100 = **11.3%**.
- Share of new jobs going to local residents = 2,000 / 8,000 x 100 = **25%**.

The scheme helped, but three-quarters of its jobs went to people from outside the district. A strong evaluation asks why: skills mismatch, recruitment practices, or in-migration of new residents attracted by the scheme.

## Annotated model answer

*Examine the reasons why some cities decline while others grow.* **(sample paragraph)**

"Cumulative causation helps explain why growth and decline become self-reinforcing: a city that loses its main industry loses jobs, skilled residents and tax revenue, so services worsen and new investment avoids it **[mechanism named]**. Detroit's car-industry decline followed this spiral, while cities with financial and knowledge industries, such as London, attracted further investment **[contrasting named examples]**. However, spirals can be broken: regeneration, as in Bilbao, combined cultural investment with new transport to change a city's image and economy **[counter-evidence]**. Decline is therefore not inevitable but depends on whether governments and investors intervene before the downward spiral is entrenched **[judgement]**."

## Common mistakes

- Assuming urban growth comes mainly from migration.
- Treating the stages of urban development as a fixed sequence for every city.
- Describing regeneration without asking who benefited.
- Ignoring peri-urban growth when discussing cities in lower-income countries.
- Crediting a single flagship project with the whole of a city's revival.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** urban change with mechanisms: migration flows, natural increase, investment, policy.
- **Analyse** data on growth by separating components before interpreting.
- **Examine** processes by showing how they interact and reinforce each other.
- **Evaluate** regeneration with criteria: jobs for existing residents, housing affordability, long-term viability.
