## 1. Why the curves must be used together

An AD curve on its own tells you how much spending there is at each price level. An AS curve on its own tells you how much firms will produce at each price level. Neither tells you what will actually happen to output and prices. Only where they meet do you find the price level and real output the economy settles at, and only by moving one or both curves can you predict how those change.

That is why a statement such as "higher government spending raises output" is incomplete. It raises AD, but whether output or the price level takes most of the change depends on the shape and position of AS.

## 2. The assumptions underlying the AD and AS models

Every model simplifies. The specification asks you to explain the assumptions so that you can judge when the conclusions are safe.

| Assumption | What it means | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Aggregation** | The whole economy is treated as producing one composite good, with one price level and one level of real output | Hides differences between sectors and regions; a boom in housing and a slump in manufacturing can net out to "no change" |
| **Ceteris paribus** | Each curve is drawn holding everything except the price level constant | Lets you isolate one cause, but in reality several things change at once |
| **Independence of the curves** | Shifts in AD are assumed not to shift AS, and the other way round | Often false: investment raises AD now and capacity later; a wage rise raises both costs and consumption |
| **Fixed input prices in the short run** | Money wages and some other costs do not respond at once to the price level | Gives SRAS its upward slope; if wages adjust quickly, SRAS is steeper |
| **Flexible prices in the long run** | All wages and prices adjust over time | Gives the neo-classical vertical LRAS; Keynesians reject full flexibility downwards |
| **Known capacity** | The position of LRAS, potential output, is taken as given | Potential output cannot be observed directly and estimates are often revised |
| **Given expectations** | Expectations of inflation and future income are fixed when the curves are drawn | A change in expectations can move both curves at once |
| **Comparative statics** | The model compares one equilibrium with another | It says little about how long adjustment takes or the path in between |

When you evaluate an answer that uses AD/AS, pick the assumption that matters most for the case. For an investment boom, question independence. For a recession, question flexible wages. For a regional problem, question aggregation.

## 3. A four-step method for any change

1. **Identify** which curve the event affects first: spending (AD), costs (SRAS) or capacity (LRAS). Some events affect more than one.
2. **Decide the direction** of each shift and give the reason in one sentence.
3. **Draw** the original and new curves, label the original equilibrium (P1, Y1) and the new one (P2, Y2).
4. **Read off** the changes in the price level and real output, then translate them into the indicators: growth, unemployment, inflation and, where relevant, the current account.

## 4. Single shifts: the four basic cases

| Change | Price level | Real output | Example cause |
|---|---|---|---|
| AD shifts right | Rises | Rises | Lower interest rates, higher consumer confidence |
| AD shifts left | Falls | Falls | Fiscal consolidation, recession abroad |
| SRAS shifts right | Falls | Rises | Cheaper energy, productivity rising faster than wages |
| SRAS shifts left | Rises | Falls | Commodity price shock, higher indirect taxes |

Demand shifts move prices and output in the **same** direction. Supply shifts move them in **opposite** directions. That pattern is the quickest way to infer the cause from data: if inflation and output rise together, look for a demand cause; if inflation rises while output falls, look for a supply cause.

## 5. Simultaneous shifts

Real events often shift both curves. The outcome for one variable is then clear, but the outcome for the other depends on the **relative size** of the shifts.

**Worked example 1.** The table gives invented schedules for Country Y (real output in billions; price level as an index).

| Price level | AD1 | SRAS1 | AD2 | SRAS2 (small shift) | SRAS3 (large shift) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 95 | 620 | 580 | 700 | 620 | 700 |
| 100 | 600 | 600 | 680 | 640 | 720 |
| 105 | 580 | 620 | 660 | 660 | 740 |

- Initial equilibrium: AD1 = SRAS1 at **P = 100, Y = 600**.
- An investment boom shifts AD right by 80 to AD2. New technology from the same investment shifts SRAS right by 40 to SRAS2. New equilibrium: AD2 = SRAS2 at **P = 105, Y = 660**. Output rises by 10% and the price level by 5%.
- If the cost-saving effect were larger, shifting SRAS right by 120 to SRAS3, equilibrium would be AD2 = SRAS3 at **P = 95, Y = 700**. Output rises by about 16.7% and the price level **falls** by 5%.

When AD and SRAS both shift right, output definitely rises, but the price level can rise or fall.

| Combined change | Certain effect | Uncertain effect |
|---|---|---|
| AD right, SRAS right | Output rises | Price level depends on which shift is larger |
| AD right, SRAS left | Price level rises | Output depends on which shift is larger |
| AD left, SRAS left | Output falls | Price level depends on which shift is larger |
| AD left, SRAS right | Price level falls | Output depends on which shift is larger |

In an exam, state the certain effect firmly, then explain what decides the uncertain one, using any data given.

## 6. Why the slope of AS matters

The same AD shift has different effects depending on where the economy is on the AS curve.

- On the **flat section** of a Keynesian AS curve, with high unemployment, a rise in AD raises output with little or no rise in the price level.
- On the **rising section**, bottlenecks mean the rise in AD is split between more output and higher prices.
- On the **vertical section**, at full capacity, a rise in AD only raises the price level.

**Worked example 2.** A government raises spending, shifting AD right by 50 billion at every price level. In economy A, which has much spare capacity, real output rises by 45 billion and the price level by 0.5%. In economy B, close to full capacity, real output rises by 10 billion and the price level by 4%. The policy is the same; the starting point makes the difference.

## 7. How useful is the combined model?

**Strengths.** It separates demand-side from supply-side causes, explains why some shocks bring rising prices with falling output, and shows why policymakers need to know how much spare capacity there is.

**Limitations.** It rests on the assumptions in section 2, so it cannot show sectoral or regional detail, expectations or the time taken to adjust. Its predictions about magnitudes depend on slopes that are hard to measure. It is best used to give the **direction** of changes and the **conditions** under which one outcome is more likely than another.

## Common mistakes

- Shifting only AD when the event also affects costs or capacity.
- Stating a definite price change when both curves shift right.
- Drawing a vertical LRAS and still claiming that a rise in AD raises long-run output.
- Listing assumptions without explaining why each one matters for the question.
- Forgetting to translate P and Y into growth, unemployment and inflation.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** using the four steps: identify, direction, draw, read off.
- **Explain, with the aid of a diagram:** label axes (price level, real output), all curves, both equilibria and the arrows showing each shift.
- **Evaluate** by naming the assumption most likely to fail in the context, and by stating which effects are certain and which depend on the relative sizes of shifts.
