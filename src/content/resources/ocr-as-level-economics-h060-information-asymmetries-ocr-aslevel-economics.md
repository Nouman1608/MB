## 1. Information and markets

Markets allocate resources well when buyers and sellers know the prices, quality and consequences of what they trade. **Perfect information** is an assumption of the model, not a description of reality. **Information failure** occurs when people lack the information, or the ability to use it, needed to make choices that serve their own interests. Their demand or supply then differs from what it would be if they were fully informed, so the market produces the wrong quantity.

Information failure takes two broad forms:

- **Both sides are poorly informed**, for example about the long-term health effects of a new product.
- **One side knows more than the other**: an **information asymmetry**.

## 2. Asymmetric information

**Asymmetric information** exists when one party to a transaction has more or better information than the other.

| Market | Who knows more | What they know |
|---|---|---|
| Second-hand cars and bikes | Seller | Hidden faults and accident history |
| Financial products | Adviser or provider | Charges, risks, commission earned |
| Health care | Doctor | Which treatment is needed |
| Insurance | Buyer | Their own risk and behaviour |
| Employment | Worker | Their own effort and ability |
| Building and repairs | Trader | Whether work is really necessary |

The better-informed party may use the advantage: selling a poor product at a high price, recommending unnecessary services, or hiding risks. The less-informed party, aware of the danger, may refuse to trade or pay less, which can harm honest sellers too.

## 3. How asymmetry can shrink a market

**Worked example 1 (invented second-hand scooter market).** Half the scooters for sale are good and half have hidden faults. Buyers value a good scooter at £1,200 and a faulty one at £600, but cannot tell them apart.

1. A buyer facing a 50:50 chance is willing to pay the expected value: 0.5 × 1,200 + 0.5 × 600 = **£900**.
2. Owners of good scooters value them at £1,000, so they will not sell at £900 and withdraw from the market.
3. Buyers realise that only faulty scooters remain, so the price falls to **£600**.
4. Good scooters, worth £1,200 to buyers and £1,000 to sellers, are no longer traded at all. Each lost trade could have shared a gain of £200 between buyer and seller.

The market has partly collapsed because of the information gap, not because people do not want good scooters. Economists sometimes call this process adverse selection.

## 4. Moral hazard

**Moral hazard** is a change in behaviour after an agreement is made, when one party is protected from the consequences of their actions and the other party cannot observe or control that behaviour.

- Insured people may take less care with their property because the insurer pays for loss.
- Employees on a fixed salary may put in less effort if they are not closely monitored.
- Banks that expect to be rescued if they fail may take larger risks with depositors' money.

**Worked example 2 (invented phone insurance).** An insurer expects a 5% chance that each insured phone will need a £400 replacement in a year. Expected claim = 0.05 × 400 = **£20**. Once insured, owners take less care and the chance rises to 8%. Expected claim = 0.08 × 400 = **£32**, which is **60%** more than the premium was based on. The insurer must raise premiums for everyone, including careful owners, or add an excess so that owners bear part of each loss.

The two problems differ in timing. Asymmetric information about quality (section 3) exists **before** the deal. Moral hazard arises **after** the deal, from hidden behaviour.

## 5. Information failure on a diagram

**Worked example 3 (invented market for a diet supplement).** Advertising leads buyers to believe the supplement works better than it does.

- Perceived MPB = 30 − Q
- True MPB (what buyers would value if fully informed) = 24 − Q
- MPC = MSC = 6 + Q

| | Rule | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Market | Perceived MPB = MPC | 30 − Q = 6 + Q, so Q = **12** |
| Fully informed | True MPB = MPC | 24 − Q = 6 + Q, so Q = **9** |

At Q = 12, the true benefit is £12 while the cost is £18. Welfare loss = ½ × (12 − 9) × (18 − 12) = **9**. Consumers buy 3 units too many, each costing more than its true value to them.

**Diagram:** draw the perceived MPB curve above the true MPB curve, a single cost curve, and shade the triangle between the cost curve and the true MPB curve from 9 to 12. The reverse case, where people **underestimate** the benefit of something (such as regular eye tests or saving for retirement), puts the perceived curve below the true curve and leads to underconsumption.

## 6. Responses to information asymmetries

**Government intervention**

| Method | How it helps | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Information provision | Campaigns, labelling, published ratings and comparison data narrow the knowledge gap | People may ignore or misread information; campaigns cost money |
| Regulation | Rules requiring disclosure of charges, faults or risks; minimum standards and qualifications for professionals | Enforcement is costly; rules may raise prices or reduce choice |
| Legislation | Consumer rights to refunds and repairs shift risk back to sellers | Consumers may not know or use their rights |

**Private solutions**

- **Warranties and guarantees**, which a seller of a good product can afford to offer.
- **Reputation and reviews**, which reward honest firms in repeat markets.
- **Independent inspections**, such as a mechanic's check before buying a car.
- **Excesses and no-claims discounts** in insurance, which make policyholders bear part of any loss and so reduce moral hazard.

**Evaluation.** Private solutions work best where trade is repeated and reputation matters; they work poorly for one-off purchases or where quality is revealed only years later, such as pensions. Government action can help in those cases, but the authorities may also lack information, and too much regulation can stop useful trades from happening.

## Annotated model answer

*Question:* "Explain how asymmetric information can lead to market failure in the market for second-hand cars."

> Sellers know more than buyers about a car's faults, so information is asymmetric *[definition applied]*. Buyers who cannot judge quality will only pay an average price, which is too low for owners of good cars *[mechanism]*. These owners withdraw, so the cars on sale are increasingly poor and prices fall further *[development]*. Trades that would benefit both sides do not take place, so resources are not allocated to their most valued use and the market fails *[link to market failure]*.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing moral hazard (behaviour after a deal) with asymmetric information about quality before a deal.
- Saying information failure means "consumers are irrational" without explaining what they do not know.
- Forgetting that the less-informed side may refuse to trade, which also harms honest sellers.
- Assuming information provision always works.

## Exam technique

- **Explain:** state who knows more, what they know, how behaviour changes and why output or price ends up wrong.
- **Explain, with the aid of a diagram:** draw perceived and true benefit curves, both outputs and the welfare loss.
- **Evaluate:** compare government and private solutions and say when each is more effective.
