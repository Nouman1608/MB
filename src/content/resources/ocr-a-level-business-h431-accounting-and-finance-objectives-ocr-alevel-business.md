## What they are and the main types

An **accounting and finance objective** is a measurable target set for the finance function. It states what the business wants its money to achieve over a period, so that managers can plan, act and then check the result. It differs from the **business objective** (for example "grow the business") because it translates that wider aim into figures that can be measured.

| Type | Example target | Measure used |
|---|---|---|
| Profit | Net profit margin of 8% | Net profit ÷ revenue × 100 |
| Return | Return on capital employed (ROCE) of at least 12% | Operating profit ÷ capital employed × 100 |
| Cash-flow and liquidity | Never fall below £50,000 cash; current ratio of at least 1.5 | Cash balance; current assets ÷ current liabilities |
| Capital structure | Gearing no higher than 40% | Non-current liabilities ÷ capital employed × 100 |
| Revenue and growth | Raise revenue by 20% in three years | Revenue growth rate |
| Cost control | Cut overheads by 5% | Overhead cost against budget |
| Shareholder return | Dividend of 15p a share | Dividend per share |
| Efficiency of assets | Collect payments from customers in 45 days | Debtor days |

A good objective is **specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound** (SMART). "Make more profit" fails the test; "raise the net profit margin from 7% to 8% by the end of next year" passes.

## How and why businesses use them

Businesses use accounting and finance objectives to:

- **set budgets and plans**: a margin target tells managers how much cost they can afford
- **guide decisions**: a gearing limit decides whether to fund an expansion with a loan or with new shares
- **judge performance**: results are compared with targets and variances are investigated
- **reward and motivate managers**: bonuses may be linked to ROCE or profit targets
- **communicate to stakeholders**: lenders, investors and owners see what the business is aiming at
- **control risk**: liquidity and gearing limits protect the business from running out of cash or borrowing too much.

## The need for clear objectives

Without clear objectives:

- managers pull in different directions, for example sales staff chase revenue while the finance team wants lower costs
- there is no standard against which to judge performance
- stakeholders cannot judge whether the business is well run
- investment and funding choices have no rule to follow.

Objectives that are too vague cannot be tested; objectives that are unrealistic can demotivate staff or encourage short-term tricks, such as delaying payment of suppliers to flatter the cash balance.

## Worked example 1: using objectives to judge performance

Brackenfield Dairy has a business objective of growing sales by 20% over three years. Its directors set four finance objectives: net profit margin of at least 8%, ROCE of at least 12%, gearing of no more than 40%, and a current ratio of at least 1.5. This year's figures:

| Item | Figure |
|---|---|
| Revenue | £4,000,000 |
| Net profit | £280,000 |
| Operating profit | £330,000 |
| Capital employed | £2,100,000 |
| Non-current liabilities | £700,000 |
| Current assets | £600,000 |
| Current liabilities | £380,000 |

| Objective | Calculation | Result | Target met? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Net profit margin ≥ 8% | 280,000 ÷ 4,000,000 × 100 | 7.0% | No |
| ROCE ≥ 12% | 330,000 ÷ 2,100,000 × 100 | 15.7% | Yes |
| Gearing ≤ 40% | 700,000 ÷ 2,100,000 × 100 | 33.3% | Yes |
| Current ratio ≥ 1.5 | 600,000 ÷ 380,000 | 1.58 | Yes |

Reading the table: three of four targets are met. The margin is 1 percentage point short. At the current revenue, an 8% margin would need net profit of £320,000, which is £40,000 more. If revenue grew by 10% to £4,400,000, an 8% margin would be £352,000, a rise of £72,000 on this year's profit.

**What the objectives tell the directors.** The business earns a good return on the money invested (ROCE) and is financially safe (gearing and liquidity), but its margin is thin. The finance objectives therefore point to cost control and pricing rather than to a shortage of capital.

## Evaluating usefulness

| Stakeholder | Usefulness of the objectives |
|---|---|
| Owners and shareholders | Show whether the return justifies the investment |
| Managers | Give clear targets and a basis for bonuses |
| Lenders | Gearing and liquidity limits give comfort about repayment |
| Employees | Margin targets may lead to cost cutting, but a stable business protects jobs |
| Suppliers | Liquidity targets suggest bills will be paid |

Limits to usefulness:

- Objectives are based on historic figures and forecasts that may be wrong.
- Targets can conflict: a high profit margin target may mean cutting spending on training, hurting long-term growth.
- Managers may meet the target by short-term action (cutting maintenance) that damages the business later.
- They do not capture non-financial aims such as environmental goals or customer satisfaction.

A fair judgement is that objectives are most useful when they are few, linked to business objectives, reviewed regularly and combined with non-financial measures.

## Linking to business objectives

Finance objectives are important because they make business objectives achievable and testable. Brackenfield's growth objective needs funds. Its gearing limit is 40%. A new loan adds to both non-current liabilities and capital employed, so the most it can borrow while keeping gearing at 40% solves (700,000 + x) ÷ (2,100,000 + x) = 0.4, which gives x = 140,000 ÷ 0.6 = about **£233,333**. A loan of £600,000 for a new plant would take gearing to (700,000 + 600,000) ÷ (2,100,000 + 600,000) = **48.1%**, above the limit. The finance objective therefore changes the decision: Brackenfield must fund part of the plant from retained profit or a share issue, or lower its ambition. This is how finance objectives help to achieve, or to constrain, the wider strategy.

## Worked example 2: recommending objectives

Tamsin runs Larkhill Bakery, a private limited company with revenue of £1,500,000 and operating profit of £150,000. She wants to open a second shop within two years and the bank will lend only if the business shows steady profits and cash.

*Recommended objectives.*

1. Raise the operating profit margin from 10% (150,000 ÷ 1,500,000) to 12% within 18 months. At the same revenue, that is £180,000, an extra £30,000 a year.
2. Hold a cash balance of at least £40,000 at every month end.
3. Keep gearing below 30% after the loan for the second shop.

*Justification.* The first objective builds the profit the bank looks for. The second protects the business from a cash shortage while it opens the shop. The third gives the bank comfort and leaves room for a later loan.

*Risks and alternatives.* An 18-month profit target may tempt Tamsin to cut ingredient quality. A ROCE target would link profit to the money invested, which is better for judging the second shop, but it is harder to explain to staff. The judgement is that the three objectives are suitable because they are few, measurable and tied to the expansion, and Tamsin should add a customer rating target to guard against quality being cut.

## Common mistakes

- Writing an objective with no number or time limit.
- Confusing a business objective with a finance objective.
- Listing objectives without saying what they are used for.
- Ignoring conflicts between objectives.
- Recommending objectives without linking them to the business's aim.

## Exam technique

"Explain" needs the meaning and a use. "Evaluate" needs two sides (usefulness and limits), stakeholders and a judgement. "Recommend and justify" needs specific, measurable objectives with figures, reasons from the case and a risk.
