## 3.1.1 Water as a solvent

Many substances dissolve in water, so they can move, react and be carried.

- **Digestion.** Enzymes work on food dissolved or suspended in water. The products of digestion, such as glucose and amino acids, are soluble and dissolve in water so they can be absorbed into the blood.
- **Excretion.** Urea and excess salts are removed from the body dissolved in water, as urine.
- **Transport.** Blood plasma is mostly water and carries dissolved glucose, amino acids, ions, urea and carbon dioxide. In plants, xylem water carries dissolved mineral ions and phloem sap carries dissolved sugars.

## 3.1.2 and 3.1.3 Diffusion

Molecules and ions in liquids and gases move at random because they have **kinetic energy**. This is the only energy diffusion and osmosis use; no energy from respiration is needed, so both are **passive**.

**Diffusion** is the net movement of molecules or ions from a region of their higher concentration to a region of their lower concentration (down a concentration gradient), as a result of their random movement.

The word **net** matters. Particles move both ways, but more move from high to low concentration because there are more of them there. At equal concentrations particles still move, but there is no net movement. Oxygen diffusing from the alveoli into the blood is a typical example.

## 3.1.4 Factors that affect the rate of diffusion

The syllabus limits you to four factors.

| Factor | Change that speeds diffusion up | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Surface area | Larger surface area | More particles can cross at the same time |
| Temperature | Higher temperature | Particles have more kinetic energy and move faster |
| Concentration gradient | Steeper gradient (bigger difference) | Far more particles moving down the gradient than up it |
| Distance | Shorter distance | Particles have less far to travel |

### Surface area and the SA:V ratio

As an object gets bigger, its volume grows faster than its surface area, so its surface area to volume ratio (SA:V) falls.

**Worked example 1.** Cubes of pink agar (containing an indicator and alkali) are cut with sides of 1 cm, 2 cm and 4 cm and dropped into dilute acid. The pink colour disappears as acid diffuses in. Calculate SA:V for each cube and predict which goes colourless first.

```
Surface area of a cube = 6 × side²     Volume = side³

1 cm cube: SA = 6 × 1² = 6 cm²    V = 1³ = 1 cm³     SA:V = 6 : 1
2 cm cube: SA = 6 × 2² = 24 cm²   V = 2³ = 8 cm³     SA:V = 24 : 8 = 3 : 1
4 cm cube: SA = 6 × 4² = 96 cm²   V = 4³ = 64 cm³    SA:V = 96 : 64 = 1.5 : 1
```

The 1 cm cube has the largest SA:V and its centre is only 0.5 cm from the surface, so it goes colourless first. The 4 cm cube is last: its centre is 2 cm in.

### Investigating the other three factors

- **Temperature.** Same-sized cubes in the same acid, in water baths at different temperatures. Higher temperature, faster loss of colour.
- **Concentration gradient.** Same cubes and temperature, acid at several concentrations. Stronger acid, faster loss of colour.
- **Distance.** Blocks of different thickness, or how far a dye spreads into agar in a fixed time.

Change one factor, keep the others constant, and repeat each condition to get a mean.

## 3.1.5 Osmosis and water potential

**Osmosis** is the net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential, through a partially permeable membrane.

- Pure water has the highest **water potential**. Dissolving solutes lowers it, so a concentrated solution has a lower water potential than a dilute one.
- A **partially permeable membrane**, such as the cell membrane, lets water molecules through but not some larger solute molecules, such as sucrose.

Like diffusion, osmosis needs no energy from respiration.

## 3.1.6 and 3.1.7 Osmosis in plant and animal tissues

### Plant cells

A plant cell has a strong cellulose cell wall outside the membrane and a large vacuole full of cell sap.

- **In a solution of higher water potential than the cell sap** (for example pure water), water enters by osmosis. The vacuole swells and pushes the cytoplasm and membrane against the cell wall. The wall resists, so pressure builds up. This outward pressure of water on the wall is **turgor pressure**, and the cell is **turgid**. The wall stops the cell bursting.
- **In a solution of lower water potential**, water leaves by osmosis. The vacuole shrinks and the cell loses turgor pressure, becoming **flaccid**. If enough water leaves, the membrane and cytoplasm pull away from the cell wall. This is **plasmolysis**.

**Support.** Non-woody plants, such as young stems and leaves, are held up by turgid cells pressing against each other and against their walls. When a plant loses more water than it takes in, its cells become flaccid and it **wilts**.

### Animal cells

Animal cells have no cell wall, so nothing resists the pressure as water enters.

- In pure water, red blood cells take in water by osmosis, swell and burst.
- In a solution of lower water potential, they lose water by osmosis and shrink.
- In a solution of equal water potential there is no net movement of water, so the cells keep their shape.

### Uptake and loss of water: why the gradient matters

Water moves only if there is a **water potential gradient**, and always towards the lower water potential.

- **Uptake.** Soil water usually has a higher water potential than root hair cell sap, so water enters by osmosis, then moves across the root down a water potential gradient.
- **Loss.** If salt or too much fertiliser makes soil water more concentrated, the gradient can reverse. Water leaves the roots and the plant wilts.
- The **steeper** the gradient, the faster the net movement of water.

## 3.1.8 Investigating plant tissue in different solutions

**Method.** Cut potato cylinders with a cork borer to the same length, blot and weigh. Put one in each sucrose solution for the same time at the same temperature, then blot and reweigh. Use **percentage change in mass**, because starting masses differ.

**Worked example 2.** Use the results below to find the percentage change in mass and estimate the sucrose concentration at which the potato would neither gain nor lose mass.

| Sucrose / mol per dm³ | Start mass / g | End mass / g |
|---|---|---|
| 0.0 | 2.50 | 2.80 |
| 0.2 | 2.40 | 2.58 |
| 0.4 | 2.60 | 2.65 |
| 0.6 | 2.50 | 2.35 |
| 0.8 | 2.40 | 2.16 |
| 1.0 | 2.50 | 2.20 |

```
% change = (end mass − start mass) / start mass × 100

0.0: (2.80 − 2.50) / 2.50 × 100 = +12.0 %
0.2: (2.58 − 2.40) / 2.40 × 100 = +7.5 %
0.4: (2.65 − 2.60) / 2.60 × 100 = +1.9 %
0.6: (2.35 − 2.50) / 2.50 × 100 = −6.0 %
0.8: (2.16 − 2.40) / 2.40 × 100 = −10.0 %
1.0: (2.20 − 2.50) / 2.50 × 100 = −12.0 %
```

The change goes from positive to negative between 0.4 and 0.6 mol per dm³. Plot percentage change against concentration, draw the line, and read off where it crosses zero: about **0.45 mol per dm³**. At that concentration the solution and the potato cells have the same water potential.

**Explaining the results.**

- At 0.0 to 0.4 mol per dm³ the solution has a higher water potential than the cell sap. Water enters by osmosis and the cells become turgid: the cylinders gain mass and feel firm.
- At 0.6 to 1.0 mol per dm³ the solution has a lower water potential. Water leaves by osmosis and the cells become flaccid, with many plasmolysed in the strongest solutions: the cylinders lose mass and bend easily.

## 3.1.9 Investigating osmosis with dialysis tubing

Dialysis (Visking) tubing is a partially permeable membrane. Water crosses it far faster than sucrose does (sucrose leaks out only slowly), so over a short experiment it can model a cell.

**Set-up.** Fill a tied bag of tubing with sucrose solution (or fit it to a capillary tube) and stand it in distilled water. Water enters by osmosis, down the water potential gradient, so the bag gains mass or the liquid rises up the tube. A control bag of distilled water in distilled water should not change.

**Worked example 3.** A bag of sucrose solution has a mass of 12.5 g. After 30 minutes in distilled water its mass is 14.0 g. Calculate the percentage increase and the mean rate of mass gain.

```
% increase = (14.0 − 12.5) / 12.5 × 100 = 1.5 / 12.5 × 100 = 12.0 %
mean rate  = 1.5 g / 30 min = 0.05 g per min
```


## 3.2 Active transport

**Active transport** is the movement of molecules or ions into or out of a cell through the cell membrane, from a region of their lower concentration to a region of their higher concentration (against a concentration gradient), using energy released during respiration.

### Ion uptake by root hair cells

Plants need mineral ions such as nitrate (for amino acids and proteins) and magnesium (for chlorophyll). Their concentration in soil water is usually **lower** than inside the root hair cell, so diffusion would move them out, not in. Root hair cells take ions in by active transport, using energy released by respiration in their mitochondria.

So anything that stops respiration, such as lack of oxygen in waterlogged soil, cuts ion uptake sharply even though the soil still contains ions.

## Common errors

- Leaving **net** and **down a concentration gradient** out of the diffusion definition.
- Defining osmosis without the **partially permeable membrane**.
- Saying plant cells **burst** in water. The wall stops them; they become turgid.
- Saying the **cell wall** shrinks in plasmolysis. The membrane and cytoplasm pull away from it.
- Comparing raw mass changes when starting masses differ.
- Saying active transport uses "energy" without saying it comes from **respiration**.
