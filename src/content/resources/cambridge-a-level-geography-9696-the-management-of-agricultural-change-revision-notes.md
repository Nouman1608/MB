These notes condense section 11.2, The management of agricultural change, from the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for 2025 and 2026 (Paper 4, Topic 11 option). The full explanation is in the [11.2 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-management-of-agricultural-change/); test yourself afterwards with the [11.2 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-management-of-agricultural-change-practice/).

## What the syllabus demands

One country. The **need for** change. **Some of the difficulties** in managing it. The **local scale** (farm, holding or producer) **and** the **national scale**. An **evaluation** of attempted solutions. Miss any one of these and an essay cannot reach the top level.

## Case study template

| Heading | Fill in for your country |
|---|---|
| Starting problem (need) | What was wrong, and since when? |
| Causes of the problem | Physical, economic, social, political |
| National solutions | Laws, price support, subsidies, land reform, research, extension services |
| Local solutions | What changed on a named farm, village or co-operative |
| Difficulties | What slowed or blocked change at each scale |
| Outcomes | Evidence of change in output, incomes, environment |
| Evaluation | Criteria-based judgement, varied by scale and group |

## The two case studies on this site

- **India** (in the [Topic 11 study guide](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-production-location-and-change/)): Green Revolution, minimum support prices, the Punjab rice–wheat rotation and falling water tables, national reforms and protest.
- **China** (in the 11.2 study guide): communes to household farming, Xiaogang village, separation of land rights, farmland protection.

Learn one thoroughly. Use the other only for brief comparison.

## China in brief

- **Need:** collective commune farming from 1958 gave weak incentives; the 1959–61 famine; low output per person by the late 1970s.
- **Local scale:** in 1978, eighteen households in Xiaogang, Anhui province, secretly divided collective land into household plots, delivering a quota and keeping the surplus; the next harvest was much larger.
- **National scale:** the Household Responsibility System spread in the early 1980s; land stayed collectively owned but households held farming contracts; contracts were lengthened, with a further 30-year extension announced in 2017; agricultural tax abolished in 2006; a farmland "red line" of 1.8 billion mu (about 120 million ha).
- **Later reform:** separation of ownership, contract and management rights from the mid-2010s, so absent households can rent land to larger family farms and co-operatives.
- **Remaining difficulties:** very small, scattered plots; ageing farm workforce as young people migrate; pollution from heavy fertiliser use; land cannot be sold or used as collateral.

## Evaluation criteria

| Criterion | Short test |
|---|---|
| Effectiveness | Did output or incomes rise? |
| Equity | Who gained, who lost? |
| Sustainability | Were soil and water protected? |
| Cost | Affordable for state and farmer? |
| Durability | Did gains last after support ended? |
| Scale | Did national policy work locally, and local success scale up? |

One-line judgement for China: incentive-based reform at household level was highly effective in the short term, but equal small plots and dependence on rural labour have limited later gains, and yield growth has carried environmental costs.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Agricultural change | Shifts in what is farmed, how, by whom and on what scale |
| Collectivisation | Merging private holdings into large state-directed units |
| Decollectivisation | Returning farming decisions to households |
| Household Responsibility System | Chinese system where households farm contracted plots, deliver a quota and keep the surplus |
| Land reform | Redistribution or change in the rights to land |
| Land consolidation | Joining scattered plots into compact holdings |
| Extension service | Advisers who spread new methods to farmers |
| Price support | State-guaranteed minimum prices for chosen crops |
| Input subsidy | Payment reducing the cost of fertiliser, seed, water or power |
| Co-operative | Farmers pooling buying, selling or machinery |
| Food security | Reliable access to enough affordable, nutritious food |
| Stakeholder | Any group affected by, or affecting, a decision |

## Exam traps

- **Only national scale.** Add a named producer or village.
- **Description, not evaluation.** Each solution needs a judgement and a criterion.
- **No "need".** Start every essay with the problem the management responded to.
- **Two countries.** Use one; compare only briefly.
- **Uncertain numbers.** A wrong figure costs more than an accurate general statement.
- **Single judgement.** Vary it: who gained, at which scale, over what time.
- **Ignoring side effects.** Many solutions created new problems (water, chemicals, inequality).

## Linking 11.2 to 11.1

- Land tenure (11.1) explains why household contracts changed incentives.
- Intensification issues (11.1) explain the fertiliser and water problems that later policy targets.
- Productivity measures (11.1) let you judge success: output per hectare rose, but output per worker stays low on tiny plots.

## Self-test

1. What five elements must an 11.2 case study contain?
2. Give two reasons agricultural change may need managing.
3. Name two social difficulties in managing change.
4. Why did commune farming give weak incentives?
5. What did the Xiaogang households do in 1978?
6. Under the Household Responsibility System, who owns the land?
7. What tax did China abolish nationally in 2006?
8. What is the purpose of the farmland "red line"?
9. Which three rights were separated from the mid-2010s?
10. Give two difficulties that remain in Chinese farming.
11. Name four criteria for evaluating a solution.
12. Why is productivity per worker still low on many Chinese farms?

### Answers

1. Need, difficulties, local scale, national scale, evaluation (all in one country).
2. Any two: food insecurity, rural poverty, inequality, land degradation, labour loss, import competition.
3. Any two: fragmented holdings, insecure tenure, ageing farmers, low literacy, resistance to new methods.
4. Output was shared, so extra effort brought little extra reward.
5. Secretly divided collective land into household plots, delivering a quota and keeping the surplus.
6. The collective; households hold contract rights to farm it.
7. The agricultural tax.
8. To keep a minimum area of farmland (1.8 billion mu, about 120 million ha) from being built on, for food security.
9. Ownership, contract and management rights.
10. Any two: small scattered plots, ageing workforce, fertiliser pollution, land cannot be sold or used as collateral.
11. Any four: effectiveness, equity, sustainability, cost, durability, scale.
12. Plots are very small, so each worker farms little land and mechanisation is limited.
