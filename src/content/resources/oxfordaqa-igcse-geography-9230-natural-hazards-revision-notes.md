These notes cover section 3.1.1.1, Natural hazards, of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Geography (9230) specification, Version 4.3: "Natural hazards pose major risks to people and property." For full explanations, read the [natural hazards study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-geography-9230-natural-hazards/). For the whole unit, see the [Living with the Physical Environment study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-geography-living-with-the-physical-environment/).

## Specification checklist

- **Definition** of a natural hazard.
- **Types** of natural hazard.
- **Factors affecting hazard risk.**

Assessed in Paper 1 (1 hour 30 minutes, 80 marks). Section A of that paper is 30 marks and must be answered in full.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Natural hazard | A natural event or process that could cause death, injury, damage or disruption |
| Disaster | A hazard that happens and causes serious harm to a community |
| Primary hazard | The main event, such as the earthquake itself |
| Secondary hazard | A hazard triggered by the main event, such as a tsunami or landslide |
| Magnitude | The size or strength of an event |
| Frequency | How often events of a given size happen |
| Vulnerability | How easily people and property can be harmed |
| Capacity to cope | Ability to prepare for, withstand and recover from a hazard |
| Risk | The chance that a hazard will harm a particular population |

## Definition in one line

**A natural hazard is a natural event that threatens people or property.** It becomes a **disaster** when it meets a vulnerable population and causes major harm.

- No people or property at risk means a natural event, but not much of a hazard.
- The same event can be a small hazard in one place and a disaster in another.

## Types of natural hazard

| Group | Source | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Geophysical (tectonic) | Processes inside the Earth | Earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, tsunamis |
| Atmospheric (weather) | Processes in the atmosphere | Tropical storms, tornadoes, heatwaves, cold spells, drought |
| Hydrological | Water | River floods, coastal floods |
| Geomorphological | Movement of land | Landslides, mudflows, rock falls, avalanches |
| Biological | Living organisms | Disease epidemics, wildfires |

Hazards often link together. An earthquake can cause a tsunami and landslides; a tropical storm brings wind, surge and flooding. Always mention secondary effects when a question asks about consequences.

## Risk: the big idea

**Risk rises with hazard severity and vulnerability, and falls with capacity to cope.**

A simple form is: risk = (hazard × vulnerability) ÷ capacity to cope. You are not asked to calculate it, but use it to organise your answers.

## Factors affecting hazard risk

### Physical (the hazard itself)

- **Magnitude:** bigger events release more energy.
- **Frequency:** frequent events leave little time to recover; rare ones may find people unprepared.
- **Duration:** long events (drought, long eruptions) cause cumulative harm.
- **Speed of onset:** sudden events give less warning.
- **Predictability:** forecastable hazards allow evacuation.
- **Spatial extent:** wide areas mean more people affected.

### Human: vulnerability

- High **population density** and rapid **urbanisation**.
- Living in **risky locations**: floodplains, coasts, volcano slopes, steep hillsides.
- **Poverty:** weak buildings, few savings, no insurance.
- **Age and health:** young, old and disabled people are harder to evacuate.
- **Poor building quality** and weak land-use planning.
- **Environmental change:** deforestation increases flood and landslide risk.

### Human: capacity to cope

- **Wealth** to pay for monitoring, defences and emergency services.
- **Good governance:** building codes are enforced; evacuation is organised.
- **Education and drills:** people know what to do.
- **Warning systems** that reach people in time.
- **Infrastructure:** roads, hospitals and communications.
- **Aid** and international support.

## Model paragraph structure: "Explain why risk is higher in X"

1. Say what is the same (often the hazard).
2. Give a factor for X, then its effect (two-step).
3. Give a second factor and effect.
4. Contrast with Y.

Example: "The earthquake is the same size in both places, so the difference lies in vulnerability. X has a higher population density, so more people are exposed. X's buildings are weaker, so more collapse and trap people. In Y, strict building codes reduce casualties."

## Exam traps

- Do not use hazard and disaster as if they mean the same thing.
- A factor on its own earns little; always add the effect.
- Richer countries face the same hazards. They often have fewer deaths but sometimes greater economic losses.
- Physical factors (magnitude, frequency, predictability) count as well as human ones.
- Do not forget secondary hazards in "effects" questions.
- In "assess" questions, finish with a judgement, not a list.
- Only quote facts about real events if you are sure of them.

## Command words

| Command word | What to do |
|---|---|
| State / Name | Give a short fact, no explanation |
| Define | Give the meaning in a precise sentence |
| Describe | Say what something is like or what a pattern shows |
| Explain | Give reasons, with linked steps (because, so, which means) |
| Assess | Weigh the factors and reach a judgement |

## Self-test

1. Define a natural hazard.
2. What is the difference between a hazard and a disaster?
3. Name one geophysical, one atmospheric and one hydrological hazard.
4. What is a secondary hazard? Give an example.
5. State **three** features of a hazard that affect risk.
6. Give **two** reasons why poor people are more vulnerable to hazards.
7. Explain why deforestation can increase flood risk.
8. Why is an earthquake harder to prepare for than a tropical storm?
9. Why do people continue to live on floodplains?
10. Give **two** ways a government can increase capacity to cope.

**Answers**

1. A natural event or process that threatens people or property.
2. A hazard is the threat; a disaster is when it causes serious harm to a community.
3. For example: earthquake; tropical storm; river flood.
4. A hazard triggered by a primary hazard, such as a tsunami triggered by an earthquake.
5. Any three: magnitude, frequency, duration, speed of onset, predictability, spatial extent.
6. They live in weaker buildings and in riskier places; they have fewer savings and less insurance to recover.
7. Fewer trees means less interception and less root binding, so rain runs off faster into rivers and floods are more likely.
8. Earthquakes cannot be predicted and strike without warning, while tropical storms can be tracked for days.
9. The land is flat and fertile, and it may be near water, trade routes or jobs; people may also have nowhere else to go.
10. Any two: enforce building codes, run drills and education, build warning systems, invest in emergency services.
