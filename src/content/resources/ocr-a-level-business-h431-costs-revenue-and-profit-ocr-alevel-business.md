## The terms

| Term | Meaning | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed cost | A cost that does not change with output in the short run (rent, salaries) | Given |
| Variable cost | A cost that changes with output (materials, packaging) | Variable cost per unit × output |
| Total cost | All costs of producing a level of output | Fixed cost + total variable cost |
| Average cost | Cost per unit | Total cost ÷ output |
| Price | What the customer pays per unit | Given |
| Total revenue | Sales income | Price × quantity sold |
| Average revenue | Revenue per unit | Total revenue ÷ quantity (equals price) |
| Profit | What is left after costs | Total revenue − total cost |

### Worked example 1: the terms in use

Tarn Furniture makes oak tables. Fixed costs are £60,000 a year. Each table has variable costs of £140 and sells for £300. This year it makes and sells 900 tables.

- Total variable cost = 900 × £140 = £126,000.
- **Total cost** = £60,000 + £126,000 = **£186,000**.
- **Average cost** = £186,000 ÷ 900 = **£206.67**.
- **Total revenue** = 900 × £300 = **£270,000**.
- **Average revenue** = £270,000 ÷ 900 = **£300**, the same as the price.
- **Profit** = £270,000 − £186,000 = **£84,000**. Profit per table = £84,000 ÷ 900 = £93.33.
- Profit as a percentage of revenue = 84,000 ÷ 270,000 × 100 = **31.1%**.

Interpretation: each table sold covers its own variable cost and leaves £160 towards fixed costs and profit. Average cost (£206.67) is below the price of £300, so the business is profitable. Average cost falls as output rises, because fixed costs are spread over more tables: at 1,200 tables, total cost is £60,000 + £168,000 = £228,000, so average cost is £190.

## Direct, indirect and overhead costs

- **Direct costs** can be traced to a particular product or service: the oak and the wages of the people who build the table.
- **Indirect costs** cannot be traced to a single product because they are shared: the factory supervisor's salary, machine maintenance, workshop heating.
- **Overheads** are the general running costs of the whole business that support production but are not part of making a particular item: rent of the head office, administration, marketing, insurance.

They are important because:

- **Pricing**: a price must cover direct costs, a fair share of indirect costs and overheads, and leave profit.
- **Control**: direct costs can be managed per unit; overheads need managing as a total budget.
- **Decisions**: a product that covers its direct costs may still be worth making in the short run even if it does not cover its overheads, since the overheads exist anyway.
- **Profit analysis**: without separating the types, a business cannot see which products or departments make money.

## Cost centres and profit centres

A **cost centre** is a part of the business (a department, branch or product line) to which costs are charged so that they can be monitored. A **profit centre** is a part that also earns revenue, so its profit can be calculated.

| | Cost centre | Profit centre |
|---|---|---|
| Measures | Costs against budget | Revenue minus costs |
| Example | Human resources department | A shop in a chain |
| Strength | Accountability and cost control | Managers focus on profit; easy comparison |
| Weakness | May cut spending to meet budget at the expense of service | Managers may chase their own profit and harm the wider business; costs shared between centres can be argued over |

## Costing methods

- **Full costing** assigns all costs, direct and indirect, to each product so that the price covers the whole cost.
- **Absorption costing** is a form of full costing: overheads are added to products using an **absorption rate**, for example per direct labour hour.
- **Marginal costing** assigns only variable costs to each unit and treats fixed costs as a cost of the period, so it shows **contribution** (price − variable cost).

### Worked example 2: absorption against marginal costing

Ashby Joinery makes tables and chairs.

| | Tables | Chairs |
|---|---|---|
| Output | 900 | 2,400 |
| Price | £300 | £70 |
| Variable cost per unit | £140 | £38 |
| Labour hours per unit | 3 | 0.5 |

Overheads are £58,500 a year.

**Absorption by labour hours.** Hours: tables 900 × 3 = 2,700; chairs 2,400 × 0.5 = 1,200; total 3,900. Absorption rate = £58,500 ÷ 3,900 = **£15 per hour**.

- Overhead per table = 3 × £15 = £45. Full cost = £140 + £45 = **£185**; profit per table = £300 − £185 = **£115**.
- Overhead per chair = 0.5 × £15 = £7.50. Full cost = £38 + £7.50 = **£45.50**; profit per chair = £70 − £45.50 = **£24.50**.
- Total profit = (900 × £115) + (2,400 × £24.50) = £103,500 + £58,800 = **£162,300**.

**Absorption by units instead.** Rate = £58,500 ÷ 3,300 units = £17.73 per unit. Table full cost = £157.73, profit £142.27; chair full cost £55.73, profit £14.27. Total profit is the same, £162,300, but each product's profit looks very different. The basis of allocation changes the picture of which product is more profitable, without changing the total.

**Marginal costing.** Contribution per table = £300 − £140 = **£160**; per chair = £70 − £38 = **£32**. Total contribution = (900 × £160) + (2,400 × £32) = £144,000 + £76,800 = £220,800. Less fixed costs of £58,500 gives profit of **£162,300**.

**Interpretation.** Suppose a customer offers to buy extra chairs at £50 each. Under absorption costing by hours the profit per chair would be £50 − £45.50 = £4.50. Under marginal costing the contribution is £50 − £38 = £12 per chair. If there is spare capacity and overheads are fixed, the order adds £12 a chair to profit.

| | Full / absorption | Marginal |
|---|---|---|
| Good for | Setting prices that cover all costs; long-run decisions | Short-run decisions, special orders, break-even |
| Weakness | Arbitrary allocation; may make a product look unprofitable | May lead to prices that never cover overheads |
| Effect on stakeholders | Managers judged on allocated costs may reject worthwhile orders | Owners see contribution; but they must still cover fixed costs overall |

## Worked example 3: when costs change

Ashby's material costs rise by 10%. Table materials are £80 of the £140 variable cost, and chair materials are £20 of the £38.

- Table: 10% × £80 = £8 more; 900 × £8 = £7,200.
- Chair: 10% × £20 = £2 more; 2,400 × £2 = £4,800.
- Total extra cost = £12,000, so profit falls from £162,300 to **£150,300**, a fall of 7.4%.

Ashby could raise prices (which may reduce demand), accept lower profit, change materials or negotiate with suppliers. Stakeholders: owners earn less; customers may pay more; suppliers may lose orders if Ashby switches. A business with higher variable costs is hit harder by a rise in materials than one with high fixed costs.

## Evaluating the profit level

A profit figure is judged by:

- **size**: £162,300 in absolute terms
- **margin**: profit as a share of revenue (£162,300 ÷ £438,000 = 37.1%)
- **trend**: whether it is rising or falling
- **comparison** with competitors and targets
- **quality**: whether it comes from the main business or one-off sales
- **purpose**: whether it is enough to fund growth and pay owners.

High profit does not always mean a healthy business: it might come from cutting maintenance, or from one large order. Low profit may be sensible if the business is investing for growth.

## Common mistakes

- Using total cost where average cost is needed.
- Dividing fixed cost by units and calling it a variable cost.
- Mixing up indirect costs and overheads. Keep to the definitions above.
- Believing that profit per unit is the same at all outputs.
- Assuming marginal costing ignores fixed costs forever; they are deducted in total.

## Exam technique

For calculations, show the formula, the numbers and the unit. For "evaluate" questions on costing methods, link to a decision (price setting, special order), weigh both methods and give a judgement.
