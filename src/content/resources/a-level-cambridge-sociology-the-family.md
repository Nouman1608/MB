Paper 2 – The Family is worth 60 marks, 1 hour 30 minutes, and is one
of the two compulsory AS Level papers in Cambridge International AS &
A Level Sociology (9699), alongside [Paper 1, Socialisation, Identity
and Methods of Research](/resources/a-level-cambridge-sociology-socialisation-identity-methods/).
Each carries 50% of the AS award and 25% of the full A Level.
Candidates answer four questions: Section A has three compulsory
questions, and Section B is one essay (26 marks) chosen from two.

## Where this fits in 9699

The Family is examined through two of the syllabus's key concepts:
Socialisation, culture and identity, and Social change and
development. It splits into two topic areas, each with two named
sub-topics -- Theories of the family and social change (3.1, 3.2), and
Family roles and changing relationships (4.1, 4.2).

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE A-LEVEL SOCIOLOGY (9699) — PAPER 2 THE FAMILY**

- 3.1 Perspectives on the role of the family — functionalist, Marxist
  and feminist accounts of what the family does for its members and
  for society
- 3.2 Diversity and social change — changing patterns of marriage,
  cohabitation, divorce and separation; family and household forms;
  dimensions of diversity; the New Right/postmodernist debate; the
  state and social policy
- 4.1 Gender equality and experiences of family life — feminist
  perspectives on power in the family, conjugal roles, and whether
  family life is experienced positively or negatively
- 4.2 Age and family life — the social construction of childhood, the
  position of grandparents, and how class, gender and ethnicity affect
  children's experience of family life

## 3.1 Perspectives on the role of the family

Three theoretical traditions frame this sub-topic, and each is
examined for what it claims the family *does*:

- **Functionalist** accounts explain how the family benefits both its
  individual members and society as a whole, and trace how the
  functions performed by families have changed over time -- including
  the "loss of functions" debate, which argues that institutions such
  as schools and the welfare state have taken over roles the family
  used to perform alone.
- **Marxist** accounts explain how the family benefits the capitalist
  economic system specifically, through ideological control, the
  reproduction of labour (raising the next generation of workers), and
  consumption (the family as a unit that buys goods).
- **Feminist** perspectives respond critically to both of the above,
  challenging the functionalist and Marxist accounts for treating the
  family as broadly beneficial while overlooking inequality within it.

## 3.2 Diversity and social change

This sub-topic covers the causes and consequences of changing patterns
of marriage, cohabitation, divorce and separation, and the range of
family and household forms this produces -- nuclear, extended,
lone-parent, reconstituted and single-person households, with the
syllabus noting that other forms (such as same-sex families and
families of choice) may also be taught where appropriate to the local
context and not restricted by law or regulation. Diversity itself is
examined along several dimensions (organisational, cultural, class),
and candidates engage with the debate over how far family diversity
has actually gone and whether the nuclear family remains dominant.
**New Right** and **postmodernist** perspectives offer contrasting
views on this diversity -- broadly, the New Right treats growing
diversity as a social problem, while postmodernist accounts treat it
as evidence of greater individual choice -- and the state and social
policy are examined as an active influence shaping family form, not
just a neutral backdrop to it.

## 4.1 Gender equality and experiences of family life

Feminism is examined here in more internal detail than in 3.1: liberal,
radical and Marxist feminist perspectives offer different explanations
of inequality and power within the family, rather than being treated
as a single unified position. **Conjugal roles** -- how domestic
labour and decision-making are divided between partners -- are
examined through housework, childcare, power and **emotion work** (the
often-unequal effort of managing a family's emotional life). The
sub-topic closes on a genuinely contested question the syllabus
frames as a live debate: whether the overall experience of family life
is positive or negative for its members, rather than assuming an
answer either way.

## 4.2 Age and family life

Childhood is examined as a **socially constructed** category -- what
counts as childhood, and how children are treated, has changed over
time and varies between societies, rather than being a fixed
biological fact. The role and social position of grandparents is
examined including cross-cultural comparison and the impact of rising
life expectancy on family structure. Social class, gender and
ethnicity are all examined as factors shaping how children actually
experience family life, and the sub-topic closes with changing
concepts of **motherhood** and **fatherhood** -- how these roles have
been redefined over time rather than remaining fixed.

## Assessment objectives on Paper 2

Paper 2 uses the same three assessment objectives as every other paper
in the qualification: AO1 (Knowledge and understanding), AO2
(Interpretation and application), and AO3 (Analysis and evaluation).
Section A's three compulsory questions typically draw more heavily on
AO1 and AO2 -- demonstrating and applying knowledge of theories and
evidence -- while the Section B essay, worth 26 marks on its own, is
where AO3 carries the most weight: explaining the strengths and
limitations of the perspectives covered in 3.1 and 4.1, and reaching a
reasoned conclusion rather than simply describing each perspective in
turn.

## How to approach it

Learn 3.1's three perspectives (functionalist, Marxist, feminist) as a
set that can be directly compared and contrasted, since Section B
essays on the family reward exactly this kind of cross-perspective
evaluation rather than a description of one theory in isolation.
Treat 3.2's diversity content and 4.1/4.2's roles-and-relationships
content as related but distinct: 3.2 is about the *forms* families
take, while 4.1 and 4.2 are about *what happens inside* those forms,
along lines of gender and age specifically. Because feminism reappears
across both halves of the paper (as one perspective in 3.1, then in
finer internal detail in 4.1), keep a running note of which feminist
strand -- liberal, radical or Marxist -- makes which specific argument,
since conflating them is a common source of lost marks in essay
answers.
