These questions cover section 3.7 Fields and their consequences of the AQA AS and A-level Physics specification (7407/7408, version 1.4, AS and A-level exams June 2016 onwards): 3.7.1 Fields to 3.7.5 Magnetic fields, including Required practicals 9, 10 and 11. All of 3.7 is A-level only, assessed in Paper 2, and is core content: the options (3.9 to 3.13, one studied for Paper 3 Section B) are separate.

Take G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻², ε₀ = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² F m⁻¹, e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, proton mass 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg and g = 9.81 N kg⁻¹ at Earth's surface.

## Questions

**1.** Define a force field. State one similarity and one difference between gravitational and electrostatic forces. **[3]**

**2.** A planet has mass 4.2 × 10²⁴ kg and radius 5.8 × 10⁶ m.

**(a)** Calculate the gravitational field strength at its surface. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the escape velocity from its surface. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why gravitational potential near the planet is negative. **[2]**

**3.** For the planet in Question 2, V = −2.80 × 10⁷ J kg⁻¹ at r = 1.0 × 10⁷ m and V = −2.33 × 10⁷ J kg⁻¹ at r = 1.2 × 10⁷ m. Use these values to estimate g at r = 1.1 × 10⁷ m. **[2]**

**4.** An 800 kg satellite orbits the planet in Question 2 at radius 2.0 × 10⁷ m.

**(a)** Show that T² ∝ r³ for a circular orbit. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the orbital period in hours. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the total energy of the satellite. **[2]**

**5.** Small spheres X (+3.0 nC) and Y (−5.0 nC) are 4.0 cm apart in air.

**(a)** Calculate the force between them and state whether it is attractive or repulsive. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the electric field strength at the midpoint of XY and state its direction. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the electric potential at the midpoint. **[2]**

**6.** Parallel plates 4.0 cm apart and 6.0 cm long have a p.d. of 600 V. A proton enters midway between them at 1.5 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹, moving parallel to the plates.

**(a)** Show, using the work done moving a charge between the plates, that E = V/d. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the proton's sideways displacement as it leaves the plates. **[4]**

**7.** A parallel-plate capacitor has square plates of side 0.15 m, 2.0 mm apart. With a dielectric filling the gap, C = 450 pF.

**(a)** Calculate the relative permittivity of the dielectric. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain, in terms of polar molecules, why the dielectric increases the capacitance. **[3]**

**8.** In Required practical 9, a capacitor discharges through a 47 kΩ resistor. A graph of ln V against t is a straight line of gradient −0.125 s⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate the capacitance. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the time for V to halve. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the energy released as V falls from 9.0 V to 3.0 V. **[2]**

**9.** A 100 μF capacitor, initially uncharged, is charged from a 6.0 V supply through 22 kΩ. Calculate the charge on it and the current 3.0 s after the switch is closed. **[4]**

**10.** In Required practical 10, a horizontal wire passes through the field of a magnet sitting on a top pan balance. 5.0 cm of wire is in the field. A current of 2.5 A increases the reading by 1.53 g.

**(a)** Explain why the balance reading changes. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the magnetic flux density. **[3]**

**11.** Alpha particles (mass 6.64 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, charge +3.2 × 10⁻¹⁹ C) move at 2.0 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹ perpendicular to a uniform 0.50 T magnetic field.

**(a)** Explain why their path is a circle. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the radius of the path. **[2]**
**(c)** Show that the time for one orbit does not depend on speed, and explain why this matters in a cyclotron. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the frequency of the alternating p.d. needed for a cyclotron using this field. **[1]**

**12.** A generator coil of 150 turns and area 0.012 m² rotates at 25 revolutions per second in a uniform 0.20 T field.

**(a)** Calculate the maximum flux linkage, and the flux linkage when the normal to the coil is at 40° to the field. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the peak emf and the rms emf. **[3]**
**(c)** Use Lenz's law to explain why more work is needed to turn the coil when a current is drawn. **[2]**
**(d)** 50 kW is sent through cables of total resistance 0.80 Ω. Calculate the power lost in the cables at 400 V and at 10 kV. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** A region in which a body experiences a non-contact force [1]. Similarity: both obey an inverse-square law (or both use field lines, potential, equipotentials) [1]. Difference: masses always attract, but charges may attract or repel [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Both are forces" or "both have fields" is too vague; name a shared property such as the inverse-square law.

**2. (a)** g = GM/R² = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 4.2 × 10²⁴ / (5.8 × 10⁶)² [1] = **8.3 N kg⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** v = √(2GM/R) [1] = **9.8 × 10³ m s⁻¹** [1]
**(c)** V is zero at infinity [1]; the force is attractive, so work must be done on a mass to move it to infinity, so V is less than zero everywhere else [1].
*Examiner insight:* In (c), give both ideas: zero at infinity and an attractive force.

**3.** g = −ΔV/Δr = −(−2.33 × 10⁷ − (−2.80 × 10⁷)) / (2.0 × 10⁶) [1] = 2.35 ≈ **2.4 N kg⁻¹** (magnitude) [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep the signs consistent: ΔV is positive moving outwards, so the minus sign gives g pointing inwards.

**4. (a)** GMm/r² = mv²/r [1]; v = 2πr/T [1]; so T² = (4π²/GM) r³, and 4π²/GM is constant [1].
**(b)** T = √(4π² × (2.0 × 10⁷)³ / (6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 4.2 × 10²⁴)) = 3.36 × 10⁴ s [1] = **9.3 h** [1]
**(c)** E = −GMm/2r = −6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 4.2 × 10²⁴ × 800 / (2 × 2.0 × 10⁷) [1] = **−5.6 × 10⁹ J** [1]
*Examiner insight:* On a "show that" in (a), write every step, from the force equation to the constant 4π²/GM; jumping straight to the result shows nothing.

**5. (a)** F = 8.99 × 10⁹ × 3.0 × 10⁻⁹ × 5.0 × 10⁻⁹ / 0.040² = **8.4 × 10⁻⁵ N** [1]; **attractive** (unlike charges) [1]
**(b)** E from X = 6.74 × 10⁴ V m⁻¹ and from Y = 1.12 × 10⁵ V m⁻¹, both pointing towards Y [1]; add: E = **1.8 × 10⁵ V m⁻¹** [1] **towards Y** [1]
**(c)** V = 8.99 × 10⁹ × (3.0 × 10⁻⁹ − 5.0 × 10⁻⁹) / 0.020 [1] = **−9.0 × 10² V** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Field strengths add as vectors, potentials as signed scalars; subtracting the two field strengths here gives a wrong answer.

**6. (a)** Work done moving Q between the plates: W = Fd = QV [1]; so E = F/Q = V/d [1].
**(b)** E = 600/0.040 = 1.5 × 10⁴ V m⁻¹ [1]; a = eE/m = 1.44 × 10¹² m s⁻² [1]; t = 0.060 / 1.5 × 10⁶ = 4.0 × 10⁻⁸ s [1]; y = ½at² = **1.1 × 10⁻³ m** [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (b), find the time from the horizontal motion only; the field does not change the speed along the plates.

**7. (a)** εᵣ = Cd / (Aε₀) = 450 × 10⁻¹² × 2.0 × 10⁻³ / (0.0225 × 8.85 × 10⁻¹²) [1] = **4.5** [1]
**(b)** The polar molecules rotate so their positive ends face the negative plate [1]. This produces a field opposing the applied field [1]. For the same charge the p.d. is lower, so C = Q/V is larger [1].
*Examiner insight:* εᵣ has no unit; writing F m⁻¹ after it is a common unit error.

**8. (a)** Gradient = −1/RC, so RC = 1/0.125 = 8.0 s [1]; C = 8.0 / 47 000 [1] = **1.7 × 10⁻⁴ F** [1]
**(b)** T½ = 0.69 × 8.0 = **5.5 s** [1]
**(c)** ΔE = ½C(9.0² − 3.0²) = ½ × 1.7 × 10⁻⁴ × 72 [1] = **6.1 × 10⁻³ J** [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (c), ½C × 6.0² is a common slip: energy is not ½C(ΔV)², so find the stored energy at each voltage and subtract.

**9.** RC = 22 000 × 100 × 10⁻⁶ = 2.2 s; Q₀ = CV = 6.0 × 10⁻⁴ C [1]; Q = Q₀(1 − e^(−3.0/2.2)) [1] = **4.5 × 10⁻⁴ C** [1]; I = (6.0/22 000) e^(−3.0/2.2) = **7.0 × 10⁻⁵ A** [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Current decays during charging too; do not use 1 − e^(−t/RC) for current.

**10. (a)** The magnet exerts a force on the current-carrying wire [1]; by Newton's third law the wire exerts an equal and opposite force on the magnet, changing the reading [1].
**(b)** F = Δm × g = 1.53 × 10⁻³ × 9.81 = 1.50 × 10⁻² N [1]; B = F/Il = 1.50 × 10⁻² / (2.5 × 0.050) [1] = **0.12 T** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Convert grams to kilograms before multiplying by g.

**11. (a)** The force BQv is always perpendicular to the velocity [1], so the speed is constant and the force acts as a centripetal force [1].
**(b)** r = mv/BQ = 6.64 × 10⁻²⁷ × 2.0 × 10⁶ / (0.50 × 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁹) [1] = **8.3 × 10⁻² m** [1]
**(c)** T = 2πr/v = 2πm/BQ, which has no v in it [1]; so a fixed-frequency alternating p.d. stays in step with the particles as they speed up [1].
**(d)** f = BQ/2πm = **3.8 × 10⁶ Hz** [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (c), substitute r = mv/BQ to show v cancels; a bare statement of the result is not a "show".

**12. (a)** NΦ max = BAN = 0.20 × 0.012 × 150 = **0.36 Wb-turns** [1]; at 40°: 0.36 cos 40° = **0.28 Wb-turns** [1]
**(b)** ω = 2π × 25 = 157 rad s⁻¹ [1]; ε₀ = BANω = 56.5 ≈ **57 V** [1]; ε_rms = 56.5/√2 = **40 V** [1]
**(c)** The induced current produces a force opposing the coil's rotation [1], so work must be done against it, supplying the electrical energy [1].
**(d)** At 400 V: I = 125 A, loss = I²R = **12.5 kW** [1]; at 10 kV: I = 5.0 A [1], loss = **20 W** [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (d), use I²R with the line current; V²/R with 400 V or 10 kV uses the wrong p.d.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the height above a surface instead of the distance from the centre.
- Explaining negative potential without mentioning the zero at infinity.
- Adding field strengths as scalars when they point in different directions.
- Confusing the time constant with the half-time on a decay graph.
- Using a charging equation for current, which always decays.
- Forgetting to convert grams to newtons with g in the top pan balance practical.
