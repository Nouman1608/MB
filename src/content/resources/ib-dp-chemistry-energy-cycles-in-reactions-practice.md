Use these average bond enthalpies where needed (kJ mol⁻¹): H–H 436, C–H 414, C–C 346, C=C 614, C–O 358, C=O 804, O–H 463, O=O 498, N–H 391, N≡N 945, C–N 305. All other data are given in the question. Give answers in kJ mol⁻¹ to the precision of the data.

## Questions

**1.** (SL and HL)

**(a)** Explain why breaking a covalent bond is an endothermic process. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why enthalpy changes calculated from bond enthalpy tables are described as estimates, even for reactions in which every species is a gas. **[2]**

**2.** (SL and HL) Methylamine, CH₃NH₂, is a gas at room temperature. It burns as follows: CH₃NH₂(g) + 2¼O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2½H₂O(g) + ½N₂(g).

**(a)** Use the bond enthalpies above to estimate ΔH for this reaction. **[3]**
**(b)** Measured thermochemical data give −975.0 kJ mol⁻¹ for the same equation. Suggest why the two values differ. **[1]**

**3.** (SL and HL) Copper forms two oxides.

- 2Cu(s) + ½O₂(g) → Cu₂O(s)  ΔH = −168.6 kJ mol⁻¹
- Cu(s) + ½O₂(g) → CuO(s)  ΔH = −157.3 kJ mol⁻¹

Calculate ΔH for Cu₂O(s) + ½O₂(g) → 2CuO(s). **[3]**

**4.** (SL and HL) At very high temperatures, iron(III) oxide releases oxygen: 6Fe₂O₃(s) → 4Fe₃O₄(s) + O₂(g).

- (1) 4Fe(s) + 3O₂(g) → 2Fe₂O₃(s)  ΔH = −1648.4 kJ mol⁻¹
- (2) 3Fe(s) + 2O₂(g) → Fe₃O₄(s)  ΔH = −1118.4 kJ mol⁻¹

**(a)** Use Hess's law to calculate ΔH for the decomposition as written. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the enthalpy change per mole of Fe₃O₄ formed. **[1]**

**5.** (HL only)

**(a)** Give the equation, including state symbols, that represents ΔHf⦵ of urea, CO(NH₂)₂(s). **[1]**
**(b)** Give the equation, including state symbols, that represents ΔHc⦵ of butan-1-ol, C₄H₉OH(l). **[1]**
**(c)** State the value of ΔHf⦵ for N₂(g) and explain your answer. **[1]**

**6.** (HL only) Gently heated ammonium nitrate gives dinitrogen monoxide: NH₄NO₃(s) → N₂O(g) + 2H₂O(l). Calculate ΔH⦵ using these ΔHf⦵ values (kJ mol⁻¹): NH₄NO₃(s) −365.6; N₂O(g) +82.1; H₂O(l) −285.8. **[3]**

**7.** (HL only) Consider the conversion of methane into liquid methanol: 2CH₄(g) + O₂(g) → 2CH₃OH(l). Calculate ΔH⦵ using these ΔHc⦵ values (kJ mol⁻¹): CH₄(g) −890.3; CH₃OH(l) −726.0. **[3]**

**8.** (HL only) Data for potassium oxide, K₂O (kJ mol⁻¹): atomization of K +89; IE₁(K) +419; ½O₂(g) → O(g) +249; first electron affinity of O −141; ΔHf⦵(K₂O) −361; lattice enthalpy of K₂O +2283.

**(a)** Use the data to determine the enthalpy change when a second electron is added to oxygen, O⁻(g) + e⁻ → O²⁻(g). **[3]**
**(b)** Explain the sign of your answer. **[1]**

**9.** (HL only) Data for calcium fluoride, CaF₂ (kJ mol⁻¹): atomization of Ca +178; IE₁(Ca) +590; IE₂(Ca) +1145; F–F bond enthalpy +158; first electron affinity of F −328; ΔHf⦵(CaF₂) −1228.

**(a)** Determine the lattice enthalpy of CaF₂. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why the lattice enthalpy of CaF₂ is much larger than that of potassium fluoride. **[2]**

**10.** (SL and HL) Methoxymethane, CH₃OCH₃, is a gaseous fuel.

**(a)** Use bond enthalpies to estimate ΔH for CH₃OCH₃(g) + 3O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 3H₂O(g). **[3]**
**(b)** The enthalpy change of vaporization of water is +44.0 kJ mol⁻¹. Use it to estimate ΔH when the water is formed as a liquid. **[2]**
**(c)** The measured enthalpy of combustion of methoxymethane is −1460 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the percentage difference between your answer to (b) and this value, and give one reason for the difference. **[2]**

**11.** (HL only) Cyclopropane, C₃H₆, rearranges to its isomer propene when heated. Use these ΔHc⦵ values (kJ mol⁻¹): C(graphite) −393.5; H₂ −285.8; cyclopropane(g) −2091.3; propene(g) −2058.0.

**(a)** Write the formation equation for cyclopropane and calculate its ΔHf⦵. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate ΔH⦵ for cyclopropane(g) → propene(g). **[2]**
**(c)** Use bond enthalpies to estimate ΔH for the same rearrangement. **[2]**
**(d)** Suggest why the estimate in (c) is so different from the answer to (b). **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Energy must be supplied to overcome the attraction between the shared electron pair and the two nuclei [1].
**(b)** Each value is an average over many compounds [1], but the actual strength of a bond depends on its environment in the particular molecule, so the true values in these molecules differ from the mean [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b), saying "the values are inaccurate" without explaining that they are averages taken from different molecules is unlikely to gain credit.

**2. (a)** Broken: 3 C–H + C–N + 2 N–H + 2¼ O=O = 1242 + 305 + 782 + 1120.5 = 3449.5 kJ [1]. Formed: 2 C=O + 5 O–H + ½ N≡N = 1608 + 2315 + 472.5 = 4395.5 kJ [1]. ΔH = 3449.5 − 4395.5 = **−946 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** Each tabulated value is a mean taken over many compounds, so it does not match the actual strength of that bond in methylamine, oxygen, carbon dioxide or water [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* All species are gases here, so "water is a liquid" is not a valid reason; pick the reason that fits the states in the equation. Fractional coefficients are fine: multiply each bond enthalpy by them just as you would by whole numbers.

**3.** Reverse the Cu₂O equation: +168.6 [1]. Double the CuO equation: 2(−157.3) = −314.6 [1]. ΔH = 168.6 − 314.6 = **−146.0 kJ mol⁻¹** [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Show which equations you reversed and scaled; a bare final number gives the reader no way to see where any slip happened.

**4. (a)** Reverse (1) and multiply by 3: 6Fe₂O₃ → 12Fe + 9O₂, ΔH = +4945.2 [1]. Multiply (2) by 4: 12Fe + 8O₂ → 4Fe₃O₄, ΔH = −4473.6 [1]. ΔH = 4945.2 − 4473.6 = **+471.6 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** 471.6 ÷ 4 = **+117.9 kJ mol⁻¹** [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Check the sum of your adjusted equations: 9O₂ released and 8O₂ used leaves one O₂, matching the target, which confirms the factors.

**5. (a)** C(s, graphite) + ½O₂(g) + N₂(g) + 2H₂(g) → CO(NH₂)₂(s) [1]
**(b)** C₄H₉OH(l) + 6O₂(g) → 4CO₂(g) + 5H₂O(l) [1]
**(c)** Zero, because N₂(g) is the element in its standard state, so no change occurs in forming it from itself [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* One mole of the named substance is required; doubling (a) to clear the fraction changes it into a different enthalpy change.

**6.** Σ ΔHf⦵(products) = 82.1 + 2(−285.8) = −489.5 [1]. Σ ΔHf⦵(reactants) = −365.6 [1]. ΔH⦵ = −489.5 − (−365.6) = **−123.9 kJ mol⁻¹** [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The coefficient 2 for water is the step most often skipped; write each product term separately before adding.

**7.** Σ ΔHc⦵(reactants) = 2(−890.3) + 0 = −1780.6 [1]. Σ ΔHc⦵(products) = 2(−726.0) = −1452.0 [1]. ΔH⦵ = −1780.6 − (−1452.0) = **−328.6 kJ mol⁻¹** [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* With combustion data it is reactants minus products; using the formation order here gives +328.6, the right size with the wrong sign.

**8. (a)** Steps from elements to ions, excluding the second electron affinity: 2(89) + 2(419) + 249 + (−141) = +1124 [1]. ΔHf⦵ = 1124 + EA₂ − 2283, so −361 = 1124 + EA₂ − 2283 [1]. EA₂ = **+798 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** The incoming electron is repelled by the negative charge of O⁻, so energy must be supplied [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* K₂O has two potassium ions, so both the atomization and IE₁ of potassium appear twice; missing this gives an answer 508 kJ mol⁻¹ too large.

**9. (a)** Steps: 178 + 590 + 1145 + 158 + 2(−328) = +1415 [1]. −1228 = 1415 − lattice enthalpy [1]. Lattice enthalpy = **+2643 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** The calcium ion carries a 2+ charge, double that of the potassium ion [1], and it has a smaller ionic radius, so the electrostatic attraction to F⁻ is stronger [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* The F–F bond enthalpy is used once, because one F₂ molecule supplies both fluorine atoms; using 2 × 158 is a common slip.

**10. (a)** Broken: 6 C–H + 2 C–O + 3 O=O = 2484 + 716 + 1494 = 4694 kJ [1]. Formed: 4 C=O + 6 O–H = 3216 + 2778 = 5994 kJ [1]. ΔH = 4694 − 5994 = **−1300 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** Condensing 3 mol of water releases 3 × 44.0 = 132 kJ [1]. ΔH = −1300 − 132 = **−1432 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(c)** (1460 − 1432) ÷ 1460 × 100 = **1.9%** [1]. Bond enthalpies are averages, not the actual values in methoxymethane, carbon dioxide and water [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b), condensation is exothermic, so ΔH becomes more negative; adding 132 instead of subtracting it is a sign error that carries through to (c).

**11. (a)** 3C(s, graphite) + 3H₂(g) → C₃H₆(g) [1]. Σ ΔHc⦵(elements) = 3(−393.5) + 3(−285.8) = −2037.9 [1]. ΔHf⦵ = −2037.9 − (−2091.3) = **+53.4 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** Combustion data, so reactants minus products: ΔH⦵ = −2091.3 − (−2058.0) [1] = **−33.3 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(c)** Broken: 3 C–C in the ring = 1038 kJ; formed: C=C + C–C = 960 kJ [1]. The six C–H bonds are unchanged, so ΔH ≈ 1038 − 960 = **+78 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(d)** The C–C bonds in the strained three-membered ring are weaker than the average C–C value, so using the average overestimates the energy needed to break them [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* A positive estimate against a negative measured value is not a calculation error to hide; what (d) asks for is a reason rooted in the use of average values.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing Σ(formed) − Σ(broken) for bond enthalpies, giving the right size with the wrong sign.
- Missing bonds in a condensed formula: CH₃OCH₃ has two C–O bonds and six C–H bonds.
- Using bond enthalpies for a reaction that forms liquid water without the vaporization correction.
- Reversing a Hess equation but keeping the original sign of ΔH.
- Not multiplying ΔHf⦵ or ΔHc⦵ values by the coefficients in the balanced equation.
- Putting a ΔHc⦵ term in for O₂, or a ΔHf⦵ term in for N₂, H₂ or Mg.
- Forgetting IE₂ for Ca²⁺ or Mg²⁺, or doubling the wrong terms in an MX₂ or M₂X cycle.
- Treating the lattice enthalpy as negative when the question defines it in the separating direction.
