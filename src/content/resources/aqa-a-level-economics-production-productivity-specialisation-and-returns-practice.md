## Questions

**1.** Define labour productivity. **[2]**

**2.** Lantern Print Studio makes posters.

| Year | Posters | Workers | Hours each |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3,600 | 15 | 32 |
| 2 | 5,148 | 13 | 33 |

**(a)** Calculate output per worker-hour in each year. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in output per worker-hour. **[2]**

**3.** Explain, using a dental surgery as an example, how production converts inputs into final output. **[3]**

**4.** A firm assembles bicycles. Explain two benefits to the firm of using division of labour. **[4]**

**5.** Country C has 9 goods and no money.

**(a)** Calculate the number of exchange rates needed if all goods are swapped by barter. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how introducing money as a medium of exchange would help workers to specialise. **[3]**

**6.** Distinguish between the short run and the long run, using an example of a firm. **[3]**

**7.** A salad farm has a fixed amount of growing space. The table shows its total output of crates a day as it hires workers.

| Workers | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Total product | 6 | 20 | 40 | 64 | 82 | 92 | 95 | 93 |

**(a)** Calculate the marginal product of the fifth worker and the average product at five workers. **[2]**
**(b)** Identify the worker from whom diminishing marginal returns start, and justify your choice. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why average product is still rising at the fifth worker. **[2]**

**8.** The salad farm in question 7 pays each worker £120 a day.

**(a)** Calculate the extra cost per crate of the fourth worker and of the fifth worker. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain the link between diminishing returns and the change in extra cost per crate. **[3]**

**9.** A plastics firm increases all its inputs together. Scale 1 costs £1,500 a week, and each later scale uses that many times the inputs of scale 1 (scale 2 uses twice as much, scale 3 three times, and so on) at unchanged input prices.

| Scale | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Output | 400 | 880 | 1,320 | 1,760 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in inputs and in output from scale 1 to scale 2, and state the type of returns to scale. **[3]**
**(b)** State the type of returns to scale from scale 2 to scale 3, with a calculation. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate cost per unit at scale 1 and at scale 2, and state what the change shows. **[3]**

**10.** Distinguish between diminishing returns and decreasing returns to scale. **[4]**

**11.** A parcel depot has 6 workers. Each used to sort, label and load, and handled 90 parcels a shift. After dividing the tasks, the depot handles 696 parcels a shift with the same 6 workers. Assess whether dividing tasks will always raise a firm's labour productivity. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Output per worker [1], or per worker-hour [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Output" alone describes production, not productivity. The definition needs a divisor, an input.

**2. (a)** Hours Year 1 = 15 × 32 = 480 [1]. Hours Year 2 = 13 × 33 = 429 [1]. Output per hour: 3,600 ÷ 480 = **7.5** and 5,148 ÷ 429 = **12.0** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** (12.0 − 7.5) ÷ 7.5 [1] × 100 = **+60%** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* When the data gives hours, work out total hours first and show it. Output per worker would give 65%, which is a different measure from the one asked.

**3.** Inputs are the services of factors of production: labour (dentists), capital (chairs and equipment) and materials [1]. These are combined in the production process [1]. The surgery converts them into final output, such as completed treatments [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Name actual inputs in the context given. A general statement about factors of production, with no surgery in it, stays at definition.

**4.** Benefit 1: workers repeat one task, so they gain skill and speed [1], which raises output per worker [1]. Benefit 2: no time is lost moving between tasks or tools [1], so output per hour rises or unit cost falls [1]. (Other valid benefits: keeping machines busy, matching workers to tasks.) **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each benefit needs its consequence for the firm. A list of benefits with no explanation covers only part of the question.

**5. (a)** n(n − 1) ÷ 2 [1] = 9 × 8 ÷ 2 = **36** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Specialists make one thing but need many [1]. Barter needs a double coincidence of wants, which wastes search time [1]. Money is accepted in payment for anything, so a worker sells output for money and buys what they need, which allows more specialisation [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Link money to specialisation explicitly. A description of money as accepted in payment, with no link to specialisation, does not answer the question.

**6.** The short run is a period in which at least one factor is fixed [1]. The long run is a period in which all factors can be varied [1]. Example: a salad farm cannot add growing space in a month (short run) but can buy more land in a few years (long run) [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Define the periods in terms of factors, not months or years. An example shows you can apply the idea.

**7. (a)** MP of fifth worker = 82 − 64 = **18** [1]. AP = 82 ÷ 5 = **16.4** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Diminishing marginal returns begin with the fifth worker [1], because MP falls from 24 for the fourth worker to 18 [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** AP at four workers is 64 ÷ 4 = 16 [1]. The fifth worker's MP of 18 is above that average, so AP rises to 16.4 [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote both numbers when justifying where returns diminish. Saying "output rises more slowly" without figures leaves the point unproved.

**8. (a)** Fourth worker: 120 ÷ 24 = **£5.00** [1]. Fifth worker: 120 ÷ 18 = **£6.67** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The wage is constant, so extra cost per crate is wage ÷ MP [1]. Once MP falls because the growing space is fixed, each worker adds fewer crates [1]. The same wage is spread over fewer crates, so extra cost per crate rises [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* State the assumption that the wage is constant. The chain runs from fixed factor to falling MP to rising extra cost.

**9. (a)** Inputs: +100% [1]. Output: (880 − 400) ÷ 400 × 100 = +120% [1]. Output rises by a greater proportion: **increasing returns to scale** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Inputs +50%. Output (1,320 − 880) ÷ 880 = +50% [1]. Same proportion: **constant returns to scale** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Scale 1: £1,500 ÷ 400 = **£3.75** [1]. Scale 2: £3,000 ÷ 880 = **£3.41** [1]. Cost per unit falls when returns to scale increase, if input prices do not change [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Calculate both percentages before naming the returns. A label with no comparison of inputs and output is an assertion.

**10.** Diminishing returns apply in the short run [1], when one factor is varied and at least one other is fixed [1]. Decreasing returns to scale apply in the long run [1], when all inputs rise by the same proportion and output rises by a smaller proportion [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Cover both differences: the time period, and which factors change. Defining one term twice, without contrasting them, answers only half of a "distinguish" question.

**11.** Output per worker rises from 90 to 696 ÷ 6 = 116, an increase of 28.9% [1]. Division of labour raises skill and speed through repetition [1]. It cuts time lost switching tasks [1]. It can keep equipment in use [1]. So productivity in the depot is likely to have risen because of these gains [1].
But it is not guaranteed. Repetitive work can cause boredom and absence, and turnover reduces output [1]. Stages depend on each other: a slow stage holds up the rest [1]. If tasks cannot be split further, or handling between stages takes extra time, gains stop [1].
Judgement: the depot suggests gains are likely when work is high volume, repeatable and balanced between stages [1], but the outcome depends on worker response and on the type of work, so it is not always true [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the 90 and 116 from the question, then evaluate. A balanced answer ends with a judgement that depends on a stated condition, not a repeat of both sides.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing by workers when the question gives hours and the hours changed.
- Calling a rise in output a rise in productivity without comparing inputs.
- Quoting a benefit of division of labour with no effect on the firm.
- Describing money without saying how it supports specialisation.
- Defining the short run as a number of months.
- Saying diminishing returns means total product falls.
- Giving MP or AP with no working, so a slip cannot be traced.
- Naming the type of returns to scale without comparing the percentage changes.
- Finishing an assessment with no overall judgement.
