## Case study

*Verdo Ltd manufactures premium reusable water bottles, sold through independent
retailers at £28 each. Revenue last year was £4.2m in a market worth £60m, which grew
8% over the year. Staff turnover is noticeably higher than similar local employers. A rival
has just launched a similar bottle at £19.*

---

## Questions

**1.** Calculate Verdo's market share, and the increase in the total market's value over the year. **[4]**

**2.** Explain **one** benefit to Verdo of primary rather than secondary market research. **[4]**

**3.** Analyse **two** possible reasons for Verdo's high staff turnover. **[6]**

**4.** Verdo is considering cutting its price to £19 to match the rival. Assess whether it should. **[10]**

**5.** Evaluate whether a pay rise is the best way for Verdo to reduce staff turnover. **[20]**

**6.** Verdo currently has a tall organisational structure with six management levels. The board is considering delayering to become flatter.

**(a)** Explain **one** advantage and **one** disadvantage of delayering for Verdo. **[4]**

**(b)** Explain how delayering might affect Verdo's ability to retain staff. **[4]**

**(c)** Explain how Verdo's leadership style might need to change if it delayers. **[4]**

---

## Answers

**1.** Market share = (4.2 ÷ 60) × 100 [1] = **7%** [1]. Increase in market value = 60m × 8% [1] = **£4.8m** [1].

**2.** Primary research is **collected first-hand for Verdo's own purpose** [1], so it is directly relevant to **its own premium customers** [1]. Secondary data on the whole bottle market would include budget buyers and could mislead Verdo about its own segment [1], whereas primary research gathered specifically about Verdo's own target customers gives more accurate, decision-useful information for pricing and product decisions [1].

**3.** Any two, each developed as a chain:
**Uncompetitive pay** [1] — if Verdo pays below the local rate, staff can move easily [1], since manufacturing skills transfer readily between employers, making leaving low-risk [1].
**Lack of motivators** [1] — repetitive production work offers little **achievement, recognition or responsibility** [1], so even adequately paid staff have no positive reason to stay [1].
*(Also creditable: poor management, no progression, better local employers.)*

**4.** *Indicative content:*

**For cutting price** [up to 4]: prevents losing customers to the £19 rival; if demand proves price-elastic, revenue could rise; protects market share in a market growing at 8%.

**Against** [up to 4]: Verdo is positioned as **premium**, and a price cut **undermines that positioning** — customers may infer lower quality; it invites a **price war** the smaller firm is likely to lose; margin falls sharply, and at £19 Verdo may not cover unit costs; premium buyers are typically **price-inelastic**, so volume may not rise enough to compensate.

**Judgement** [2]: Verdo should **not** match. Its competitive advantage is **differentiation, not cost** — competing on price abandons the only ground on which a smaller firm can beat a larger rival. This depends on Verdo's cost structure and on how loyal its existing customers prove to be.

**5.** This question is marked against a **levels-based scheme**, not discrete points, matching the real paper's extended-writing questions:

- **Level 1 (1–5 marks):** isolated or generic points about pay or motivation, with little or no application to Verdo.
- **Level 2 (6–10 marks):** relevant knowledge is applied to Verdo, but the analysis of *why* a pay rise would or would not work is one-sided or underdeveloped.
- **Level 3 (11–15 marks):** developed, two-sided analysis -- the case for a pay rise and against it (drawing on Herzberg's distinction between hygiene factors and motivators), including at least one credible alternative to a pay rise.
- **Level 4 (16–20 marks):** a sustained, well-evidenced argument reaches a supported judgement on whether a pay rise is the *best* option for Verdo specifically, rather than motivation in general.

**Indicative content -- for a pay rise:** turnover is well above the level of similar local employers, which suggests pay may be uncompetitive; pay is the most immediate and visible lever available to Verdo; it addresses the reason most commonly cited in exit interviews. **Against:** Herzberg classifies pay as a **hygiene factor** -- it removes dissatisfaction but does not motivate, so the improvement is likely to be short-lived; it raises fixed costs permanently, squeezing margins already threatened by the £19 rival; it does not address the cause if the real problem is repetitive work or weak management. **Alternatives:** job enrichment or rotation to add variety and responsibility; training and progression routes; improved line management. **Judgement:** a pay rise alone is unlikely to solve the problem on its own -- Herzberg's distinction predicts a temporary improvement before turnover returns to previous levels -- so Verdo should first establish why staff are actually leaving (through exit interviews) and address the real cause, though a below-market wage would still need correcting as a necessary, if not sufficient, first step.

**6. (a)** Advantage: delayering **cuts management costs** and **speeds up decision-making**, since information travels through fewer levels between the shop floor and senior management [2]. Disadvantage: it **increases the workload** on remaining managers, who must now supervise a wider span of control, and can **damage morale** among staff who lose a clear route for promotion [2].

**(b)** Delayering removes a layer of management **that some staff may have been aiming to be promoted into**, so it can damage morale and give staff already dissatisfied with their pay one more reason to leave [1] [1]. Against this, the wider span of control it creates for remaining managers often comes with **more delegation and autonomy** for the staff below them, which can improve retention if staff value greater responsibility [1] [1].

**(c)** A flatter structure gives each remaining manager a **wider span of control**, making close supervision of every individual impractical [1], so Verdo would likely need to shift toward a **more democratic or delegative style** [1], empowering experienced production staff to make routine decisions themselves [1]; this would reduce the burden on the smaller management team while giving staff more autonomy and responsibility [1].

## How the marks are awarded

- **Knowledge** — correct terms and theory.
- **Application** — uses Verdo's **actual figures and situation**. Generic answers about "a business" cap at the lower bands.
- **Analysis** — a chain: *because X, therefore Y, which means Z for Verdo.*
- **Evaluation** — weighs both sides, judges, and **states what the judgement depends on**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Answering generically without using the case-study figures.
- Listing points instead of developing a chain of reasoning.
- Concluding "there are advantages and disadvantages" without judging.
- Saying pay always motivates, ignoring Herzberg.
- Ignoring Verdo's premium positioning in the pricing question.
- Describing delayering as only cost-cutting, without also weighing the effect on remaining managers' workload or on staff retention.
- Forgetting to state which leadership style suits a wider span of control.
