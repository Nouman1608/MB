## 2.3.1 Definition and diagram

**Demand** is the willingness and ability to buy a product at a given price over a given period of time.

Both parts matter. A student who would love a new laptop but cannot afford it has no demand for it in the economic sense; nor does a wealthy person who has no wish to buy one. Demand is always stated for a time period ("per week", "per month"), because 50 kg a day and 50 kg a year are very different amounts.

### From schedule to curve

A **demand schedule** lists the quantity demanded at each price. Here is the weekly demand for bottled juice at a school canteen (invented).

| Price per bottle | Quantity demanded per week |
|---|---|
| 2.00 | 100 |
| 1.75 | 140 |
| 1.50 | 180 |
| 1.25 | 220 |
| 1.00 | 260 |

Drawing the curve:

- Price goes on the **vertical** axis; quantity demanded on the **horizontal** axis.
- Plot each pair and join the points. Label the line D.
- The curve slopes downward from left to right: a lower price is linked with a larger quantity demanded.

```
Price
2.00 *
1.75    *
1.50       *
1.25          *
1.00             *   D

```

### Why demand curves slope downward

- At a lower price, the product is cheaper compared with alternatives, so some buyers switch to it.
- At a lower price, buyers can afford more with the same income, and new buyers who could not afford it before now can.
- The more of a product a person already has in a period, the less extra satisfaction one more unit gives, so people buy more only if the price falls.

## 2.3.2 Movements along the demand curve

A change in the **price of the product itself** causes a movement along its existing demand curve. The syllabus uses two terms:

- **Extension in demand:** a fall in price leads to a rise in quantity demanded (moving down the curve).
- **Contraction in demand:** a rise in price leads to a fall in quantity demanded (moving up the curve).

**Worked example.** The canteen cuts the price of juice from 1.75 to 1.25.

1. Quantity demanded rises from 140 to 220, an extension of 80 bottles a week.
2. Percentage rise = 80 ÷ 140 × 100 = **57.1%**.
3. Spending on juice changes from 1.75 × 140 = **245** to 1.25 × 220 = **275**.

The demand curve has not moved; the canteen has moved down along it.

## 2.3.3 Individual and market demand

**Individual demand** is one buyer's demand. **Market demand** is the total demand of all buyers in the market. It is found by **aggregation**: at each price, add the quantities demanded by every buyer.

**Worked example.** Three households buy eggs in a village (invented).

| Price per egg | Household A | Household B | Household C | Market demand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.40 | 6 | 4 | 0 | 6 + 4 + 0 = 10 |
| 0.30 | 10 | 6 | 2 | 18 |
| 0.20 | 14 | 9 | 5 | 28 |
| 0.10 | 18 | 12 | 8 | 38 |

On a diagram, the market demand curve lies to the right of every individual curve: at each price, you add the quantities horizontally. Note that Household C buys nothing at 0.40. A buyer can be priced out of the market completely, which is one reason the market curve slopes downward even if each individual's curve is steep.

If a fourth household with the same demand as Household A moves to the village, market demand at 0.30 rises from 18 to 28. Adding buyers shifts the market demand curve to the right; this is why population is a condition of demand.

## 2.3.4 Conditions of demand: shifts

A change in anything **other than the product's own price** shifts the whole demand curve.

- **Increase in demand:** more is demanded at every price; the curve shifts right.
- **Decrease in demand:** less is demanded at every price; the curve shifts left.

| Condition | Change that increases demand (shift right) | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Income | Higher disposable income for most products | A pay rise raises demand for restaurant meals |
| Price of a substitute | The substitute becomes dearer | Bus fares rise, so demand for bicycles rises |
| Price of a complement | The complement becomes cheaper | Cheaper games consoles raise demand for games |
| Tastes and fashion | The product becomes more popular | A health report praises green tea |
| Advertising | Successful marketing | A campaign for a new cereal |
| Population | More people, or more people in the age group that buys it | More retired people raise demand for care homes |
| Income tax | A cut in income tax raises disposable income | Demand for cars rises |
| Interest rates and borrowing | Lower interest rates make credit cheaper | Demand for houses and furniture rises |
| Seasons and weather | Weather that suits the product | Hot weather raises demand for ice cream |

The opposite change decreases demand. For a few low-priced basic products, a rise in income can reduce demand, as households switch to products they prefer. Read the context before assuming the direction.

### Substitutes and complements

- **Substitutes** are products that can be used in place of each other (tea and coffee). A rise in the price of one **increases** demand for the other.
- **Complements** are products used together (printers and ink). A rise in the price of one **decreases** demand for the other.

**Worked example: an increase in demand.** A popular sports star is filmed drinking the canteen's juice brand, and demand rises by 25% at every price.

| Price | Old quantity | New quantity |
|---|---|---|
| 2.00 | 100 | 100 × 1.25 = 125 |
| 1.50 | 180 | 180 × 1.25 = 225 |
| 1.00 | 260 | 260 × 1.25 = 325 |

At a price of 1.50 the canteen now sells 225 bottles instead of 180, even though the price has not changed. That is the sign of a shift rather than a movement.

### Movement or shift? A two-question test

1. What changed? If it is the product's **own price**, it is a movement (extension or contraction). If it is anything else, it is a shift (increase or decrease).
2. Which way? For a movement, a lower price means extension. For a shift, more demanded at every price means increase (right).

## Common mistakes

- Labelling the axes the wrong way round. Price is always on the vertical axis.
- Writing "demand rises" when the price of the product falls. The correct term is "quantity demanded extends".
- Shifting the demand curve because the product's own price changed.
- Forgetting the time period in a definition or a schedule.
- Adding prices instead of quantities when building market demand.
- Mixing up the direction for complements: dearer printers mean lower demand for ink.

## Exam technique for the 0455 command words

- **Define:** "willingness and ability to buy a product at a given price over a given period of time".
- **Draw** (inside "Explain, using a diagram"): label P and Q axes, D1 and D2, the original and new quantities at the same price, and an arrow showing the shift.
- **Calculate:** market demand at a price (add across), the change in quantity and its percentage, and spending (price × quantity).
- **Explain:** name the condition, say which way demand shifts and why: "Bus fares rise, so bicycles become relatively cheaper, so some travellers switch, so demand for bicycles increases and the curve shifts right."
- **Identify:** from a list of events, pick those that cause a movement and those that cause a shift.
