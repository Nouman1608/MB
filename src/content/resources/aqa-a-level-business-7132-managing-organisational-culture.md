This guide goes deeper into section **3.10.2 Managing organisational culture** of the AQA AS and A-level Business specification (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). Section 3.10 is for the A-level only. The specification's content is:

- **the importance of organisational culture**, where cultural models should include **Handy's task culture, role culture, power culture and person culture**
- **the influences on organisational culture**
- **the reasons for and problems of changing organisational culture**.

All businesses and figures are invented.

## What is organisational culture?

**Organisational culture** is the set of shared values, beliefs, attitudes and ways of behaving that shape "how things are done around here". It is seen in how decisions are made, how people dress and speak, how success and failure are treated, how staff relate to customers and how formal the workplace is. Culture is rarely written down in full. It is learned by watching what is rewarded and what is tolerated.

## The importance of organisational culture

A strong, appropriate culture can:

- **guide behaviour** where rules do not apply, so staff make decisions consistent with the aims of the business
- **motivate and retain staff**, who feel they belong, reducing labour turnover and recruitment costs
- **improve customer service** if staff share a commitment to customers
- **support strategy** by encouraging the behaviours the strategy needs (innovation, cost control, quality)
- **provide a competitive advantage** because culture is hard for rivals to copy
- **ease communication and co-ordination**.

A culture that does not match the strategy can block it. A business that wants to be innovative but punishes mistakes will not get new ideas. Culture can also be a barrier to change (see section 3.10.1), because "we have always done it this way" is strong.

## Handy's four cultures

Charles Handy described four types of culture, each linked to a type of structure.

### Power culture

Power is concentrated in a small group or one leader at the centre, like a spider's web. Decisions are made quickly by those in power, based on trust and personal contact rather than rules.

- *Typical of:* small family businesses, entrepreneurial start-ups, some trading firms.
- *Strengths:* fast decisions and strong leadership; flexible in a crisis.
- *Weaknesses:* success depends on one person; staff may be afraid to challenge; low initiative lower down; risk of favouritism; hard to grow beyond the leader's reach.

### Role culture

Work is organised by roles and rules, like the pillars of a Greek temple. Each person has a defined job and clear responsibilities, and authority comes from position.

- *Typical of:* large bureaucracies, civil services, banks, insurers.
- *Strengths:* stability, clarity and efficiency in predictable conditions; staff know what is expected; good for safety and compliance.
- *Weaknesses:* slow to change; discourages initiative and creativity; bureaucracy; people focus on their role, not the customer or the bigger picture.

### Task culture

Work is organised around projects and teams brought together to solve problems, like a net. Power comes from expertise, not position, and teams form and re-form as needed.

- *Typical of:* consultancies, advertising agencies, software firms, project-based businesses.
- *Strengths:* flexible; innovative; motivating, because people have autonomy and see results; suits changing environments.
- *Weaknesses:* hard to control and to manage costs; duplication; depends on suitable skills; conflicts over resources; less suited to routine mass production.

### Person culture

The organisation exists to serve the individuals within it, such as professionals who want to use their expertise independently.

- *Typical of:* partnerships of lawyers or doctors, universities, some co-operatives.
- *Strengths:* high autonomy; attracts talented specialists; strong commitment to professional standards.
- *Weaknesses:* hard to manage and to direct towards common goals; individuals may put their own interests first; difficult to impose change.

### Summary

| Culture | Power lies with | Best suits | Typical weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Power | A central leader or small group | Small, fast-moving, entrepreneurial firms | Dependence on one person |
| Role | The rules and positions | Stable, large, regulated organisations | Slow to change |
| Task | Expertise within teams | Changing, project-based work | Hard to control |
| Person | Individuals | Professional partnerships | Hard to co-ordinate |

Most organisations show a mix. A large bank may have a role culture overall, a task culture in its digital team and a power culture among its senior traders.

## Influences on organisational culture

- **Founders and leaders:** their values and behaviour set the tone, especially in young businesses.
- **History:** past successes and failures are remembered and repeated.
- **Size and structure:** small firms are often informal; large ones need rules. Tall hierarchies support role cultures; flat ones support task cultures.
- **Ownership:** a family business may have a paternalistic culture; a plc may be more results-driven.
- **Industry and technology:** a hospital needs safety procedures; a design studio needs creativity.
- **Environment and national culture:** laws, local values and customer expectations, particularly in overseas operations.
- **Recruitment and reward:** who is hired, promoted and rewarded shows what is valued.
- **Mergers and takeovers,** which bring two cultures together.

## Reasons for changing culture

- A **new strategy** needs different behaviour (for example moving from cost control to innovation).
- A **merger or takeover** needs a single culture.
- **Poor performance,** low morale or high labour turnover.
- **A scandal or failure** that shows existing values are harmful (for example a culture that ignores safety).
- **Changes in the environment,** such as new technology or customer demands.
- **Growth:** an informal power culture may not work when the business doubles in size.
- **Pressure from stakeholders** for better ethical behaviour.

### Worked example: is a culture change worth the cost?

Tarlton Retail has 400 staff, and labour turnover of 22% a year. Replacing a leaver costs £7,500 (recruitment, training, lost output). The company spends £360,000 on a culture change programme, aiming to bring turnover to 14%.

- Leavers before: 400 × 22% = **88**; cost 88 × £7,500 = **£660,000**
- Leavers after: 400 × 14% = **56**; cost 56 × £7,500 = **£420,000**
- Annual saving: £660,000 − £420,000 = **£240,000**
- Payback on the £360,000 programme: 360 ÷ 240 = **1.5 years**

**Analysis.** On turnover alone, the programme pays back in 18 months. Other gains, such as better customer service and higher sales, are not included. **Judgement.** The case is positive, but it depends on whether the target is realistic: culture takes years to change, and the savings may not arrive if managers do not behave differently. It would be sensible to measure progress at regular points.

## Problems of changing culture

- **Slow:** culture changes over years, not months.
- **Resistance:** staff are attached to existing values and habits (see Kotter and Schlesinger's reasons in section 3.10.1).
- **Strong subcultures** in departments or sites that resist the new way.
- **Leaders may not act consistently:** if senior managers do not follow the new values, staff will not.
- **Cost and disruption:** training, communication, loss of productivity.
- **Losing strengths:** the old culture may contain good traits, such as loyalty or customer care.
- **Staff turnover:** some employees leave, taking experience with them.
- **Difficult to measure** and to prove the change has worked.

### How to manage culture change

- Leaders **model** the new behaviour visibly and consistently.
- **Communicate** the reasons and vision clearly.
- Change **recruitment, promotion and reward** to reflect the new values.
- **Involve** staff, and use influential staff as champions.
- Change **symbols and structures**, such as layout, titles and meetings.
- **Train** staff in new skills.
- **Celebrate early successes** and be patient.

## Culture and strategy

Culture should support the strategy.

| Strategy | Culture that helps | Culture that hinders |
|---|---|---|
| Low cost | Role: standard procedures, careful control | Person: individuals pursue their own aims |
| Innovation | Task: teams, autonomy, tolerance of failure | Role: strict rules, slow approval |
| Rapid growth from a start-up | Power, then a more structured style as size rises | A strong culture that cannot cope with new staff |
| International expansion | A core set of values with room for local variation | A culture too tied to one country |

A common failure in mergers is that two cultures are left to clash. Leaders should decide which values to keep from each, communicate them clearly and reinforce them through recruitment and reward.

## Signs that culture needs attention

- High labour turnover or absenteeism
- Low scores in staff surveys
- Slow decisions and avoidance of responsibility
- Complaints about customer service
- Failure of earlier change programmes

## Exam technique

- **Analyse:** identify the culture type from evidence (who decides, how formal the rules are, how teams form), then link it to its effect on performance or on a change.
- **Evaluate:** consider whether the culture fits the strategy and environment, the cost and time to change, and whether the benefits justify them.
- Avoid saying one culture is always best.

## Common mistakes

- Describing Handy's types without applying them.
- Assuming a task culture is always better than a role culture. Role cultures suit safety-critical and regulated work.
- Treating culture as quick to change.
- Forgetting that large organisations often contain more than one culture.
- Ignoring the role of leaders' behaviour.
