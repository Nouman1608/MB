## Questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by a market. **[2]**

**2.** Classify each activity as primary, secondary or tertiary: a quarry, a furniture factory, a hairdresser. **[3]**

**3.** Distinguish factor markets from product markets and show one link between them. **[4]**

**4.** Country B (illustrative) had 8.0 million jobs in Year 1, of which 45% were in the tertiary sector. In Year 2 it had 9.0 million jobs, of which 70% were tertiary.

**(a)** Calculate the number of tertiary jobs in each year. **[2]**
**(b)** Work out by what percentage the number of tertiary jobs changed. **[2]**

**5.** A candle workshop (illustrative) has 4 workers. Each makes 15 candles a day alone. With separate stages the team makes 96 candles a day. Wages total £360 a day.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage rise in output. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the wage cost of each candle before and after. **[2]**
**(c)** State one risk of specialisation for the business. **[2]**

**6.** Evaluate whether specialisation benefits workers. **[8]**

**7.** Two customers' weekly demand for takeaway coffee (illustrative):

| Price (£) | Dev | Ella |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 2 | 0 |
| 4 | 4 | 1 |
| 3 | 6 | 3 |
| 2 | 8 | 5 |

**(a)** Calculate market demand at each price. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe how to draw the market demand curve. **[1]**
**(c)** Price falls from £4 to £3. State what happens on the curve and calculate the percentage change in quantity demanded. **[2]**

**8.** For tea, say whether each event moves buyers along their demand curve or shifts it, and which way: **(a)** the price of tea falls; **(b)** the price of coffee, a substitute, rises; **(c)** incomes fall and tea is a normal good. **[3]**
**(d)** Analyse one consequence for a tea producer of an increase in demand. **[2]**

**9.** A monthly bus pass rises from £40 to £48. Passes sold fall from 3,000 to 2,520. Calculate PED and explain what it shows. **[4]**

**10.** A cafe cuts the price of a sandwich from £4.00 to £3.60 and sales rise from 250 to 325 a day.

**(a)** Calculate PED. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate revenue before and after. **[2]**
**(c)** Give one reason why the cafe's profit may not rise. **[2]**

**11.** A ferry operator (illustrative) charges £10 a ticket. Islanders buy 20,000 tickets a year and have PED of −0.3. Tourists buy 10,000 tickets and have PED of −1.8. The operator plans a 10% price rise for everyone. Evaluate the plan. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Wherever buyers meet sellers [1] to swap goods, services or resources for payment; it need not be a physical place [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers usually do better with an example, such as an online marketplace, because it shows you know a market is not only a building.

**2.** Quarry: primary [1]. Furniture factory: secondary [1]. Hairdresser: tertiary [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Give one word per activity. A reason is rarely needed when the question says classify.

**3.** Factor markets trade the factors of production, such as labour [1]; product markets trade finished goods and services [1]. Interdependence: households earn wages in the factor market [1] and spend them in product markets, so falling product demand reduces demand for labour [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A common way to lose credit is to name the two markets without linking them; the link needs a flow such as wages becoming spending.

**4. (a)** Year 1: 8.0 × 0.45 = 3.6 million [1]. Year 2: 9.0 × 0.70 = 6.3 million [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Change = 6.3 − 3.6 = 2.7 million; 2.7 ÷ 3.6 × 100 [1] = **+75%** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Use jobs, not shares: the share rose 25 percentage points, which is a different answer from the 75% rise in the number of jobs.

**5. (a)** Before: 4 × 15 = 60 [1]. Rise = (96 − 60) ÷ 60 × 100 = **60%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Before £360 ÷ 60 = £6.00 [1]; after £360 ÷ 96 = **£3.75** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Workers depend on each other, so a breakdown or absence at one stage slows the whole line [1], which reduces output and sales [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* For a "state" part worth two marks, a developed point (risk plus consequence) is the safer response.

**6.** Benefits: skills improve with practice [1]; higher productivity can support higher pay [1]. Costs: repetitive work reduces satisfaction [1]; narrow skills make it hard to change jobs if the firm or industry declines [1]. Chain: productivity rises, so the firm earns more, so pay can rise; but if demand for the product falls, specialised workers are most exposed [2]. Judgement: it depends on whether the firm is secure and whether training is available; with retraining, benefits probably outweigh costs, without it the risk to workers is large [2]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* In an evaluate question, a conclusion that names the condition deciding the outcome usually reads as more convincing than a bare "it depends".

**7. (a)** Add each row: 2 + 0, 4 + 1, 6 + 3, 8 + 5 [1]. Market demand is 2, 5, 9 and 13 [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Put price on the vertical axis and weekly quantity on the horizontal axis, plot the market points and join them into a downward-sloping line labelled D [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** A movement along the curve (an extension) [1]. Quantity rises from 5 to 9: 4 ÷ 5 × 100 = **80%** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the addition line by line; a correct list with no working leaves nothing to credit if one value is wrong.

**8. (a)** Movement, extension [1]. **(b)** Shift right [1]. **(c)** Shift left [1]. **(d)** The producer sells more at every price [1], so revenue rises and it may raise price or take on workers [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Part (a) is about the good's own price, so do not call it a shift. Part (d) needs the effect developed beyond "sells more".

**9.** Price change = 8 ÷ 40 × 100 = +20% [1]. Quantity change = −480 ÷ 3,000 × 100 = −16% [1]. PED = −16 ÷ 20 = **−0.8** [1]. It is inelastic, as quantity falls proportionately less than price rises [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Quoting the figure alone leaves the interpretation unmade, so say "inelastic" and explain what that means for buyers.

**10. (a)** Price change = −0.40 ÷ 4.00 = −10% [1]. Quantity change = 75 ÷ 250 = +30%; PED = **−3.0** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Before 4.00 × 250 = £1,000 [1]; after 3.60 × 325 = **£1,170** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Making 75 more sandwiches costs more in ingredients and labour [1], which may exceed the £170 rise in revenue [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Revenue and profit are different; answers that separate the two are more convincing in part (c).

**11.** Islanders: quantity falls 3%, to 19,400; revenue 19,400 × £11 = £213,400 against £200,000, up £13,400 [2]. Tourists: quantity falls 18%, to 8,200; revenue 8,200 × £11 = £90,200 against £100,000, down £9,800 [2]. Total revenue £303,600 against £300,000, up only £3,600 (1.2%) [1]. Islanders have few substitutes for an essential journey, so they bear the cost; low-income households lose most [1]. Tourists are elastic, so fewer visit, which may hurt local businesses [1]. Judgement: raising the price for islanders only would gain £13,400, more than the £3,600 from raising it for everyone, though fairness and the costs of running the ferry matter; the plan is weak as designed because the gain is small and tourists are lost [2]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers calculate each group before judging, and then use PED to explain why the two groups react differently.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a change in quantity "a change in demand" when price caused it.
- Measuring percentage change from the new figure.
- Giving a PED with no interpretation.
- Forgetting that a negative sign does not mean inelastic.
- Mixing up revenue and profit.
- Listing benefits of specialisation with no cost or conclusion.
- Describing factor and product markets without the link between them.
- Leaving a diagram without labelled axes or curves.
