Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Economics 4EC1 is assessed by two externally assessed written
papers of identical size: Paper 1 (Microeconomics and Business Economics) and Paper 2 (Macroeconomics
and the Global Economy), each 1 hour 30 minutes, 80 marks and 50% of the qualification. Each paper
contains **four compulsory questions worth 20 marks each**, mixing multiple-choice, short-answer,
data-response and open-ended styles within the same question. Both papers are available as
paper-based or on-screen assessment. These notes complement the site's guides to
[The Market System](/resources/igcse-edexcel-economics-the-market-system/) and
[Production and Business Costs](/resources/igcse-edexcel-economics-production-and-business-costs/).

## Four questions of twenty marks makes the arithmetic of timing very simple

Ninety minutes across four equal questions is 22 minutes each, with a couple of minutes spare for
reading. **Exam-preparation priority**: rehearse to a 20-minute-per-question limit rather than
practising individual sub-parts at leisure. Because every question is compulsory and worth exactly the
same, there is no question worth over-running on -- a fifth mark on question 1 costs the same as a
fifth mark on question 4, but overrunning on question 1 puts the last question at risk entirely.

## Each question mixes question styles -- so each question changes gear

A single 20-mark question can move from a multiple-choice item to a short definition to a
data-response part to an open-ended evaluation. **Exam-preparation priority**: practise switching
register inside one question. The specific failure to guard against is carrying the expansive style
appropriate to the final part into the earlier short parts, which burns the time the final part needs.

## No question can be skipped, so no topic can be skipped

All four questions on each paper are compulsory. There is no choice, and therefore no possibility of
avoiding a weak topic. **Exam-preparation priority**: revise for full syllabus coverage rather than
depth in favourites, and use a specification checklist to find the gaps. In a paper with question
choice, a weak topic costs some flexibility; here it can cost a full quarter of a paper.

## The two papers are genuinely different subjects in method

Paper 1's microeconomics reasons about individual markets, firms and consumers; Paper 2's
macroeconomics reasons about aggregates, policy and international trade. Diagrams, vocabulary and
typical chains of reasoning all differ. **Exam-preparation priority**: revise them as two bodies of
technique, not one. In particular, practise macro diagrams and micro diagrams separately -- confusing
an aggregate relationship with an individual-market one is a recurring and expensive error.

## Data-response parts want the data used, not described

Where a question supplies a table, chart or extract, marks are available for taking specific figures
from it and using them in the argument. **Exam-preparation priority**: practise quoting a figure and
then doing something with it -- calculating a change, comparing two values, or drawing an inference.
Restating what the table shows is description; using it is analysis.

## Worked routine: the final open-ended part of a question

The routine below is an original model written for this resource, not a reproduction of any official
past paper or mark scheme.

```
Step 1 - answer the question in one sentence first:
A clear position up front, which the rest then supports.

Step 2 - give the main chain of reasoning:
Cause -> mechanism -> effect, with each link stated. If the
question supplies data, take a figure into the chain here.

Step 3 - give the strongest consideration on the other side:
Not a token counter-point. Evaluation marks depend on the other
side being taken seriously.

Step 4 - say what the answer DEPENDS ON:
Magnitude, time period, elasticity, or whether other things stay
equal. "It depends on..." followed by a specific factor is the
clearest signal of evaluative thinking.

Step 5 - conclude by reference to step 1:
Confirm, qualify or revise the opening position -- and say why.
```

Step 4 is the step that most reliably lifts an answer: a candidate who identifies what the conclusion
turns on has understood the economics, not just recalled it.

## Before/during exam checklist

- **Before the exams**: revise for full coverage, since all four questions per paper are compulsory;
  practise to a 20-minute-per-question limit; revise micro and macro diagrams separately; practise
  quoting a figure from supplied data and using it rather than describing it.
- **During either paper**: hold to about 22 minutes per question and move on regardless.
- **Within each question**: keep short parts short, and reserve time for the final open-ended part.
- **In every extended part**: state a position, build one clear causal chain, give the strongest
  counter-consideration, say what the answer depends on, and conclude.

## Self-test

1. How long should each question take, and why is over-running on question 1 particularly costly?
2. What does it mean that each 20-mark question mixes question styles?
3. Why does the absence of question choice change revision strategy?
4. What is the difference between describing supplied data and using it?

**Answers:** 1. About 22 minutes -- 90 minutes across four equal, compulsory questions -- and
over-running early is costly because every question is worth the same 20 marks, so time borrowed from
question 4 buys nothing extra. 2. That a single question can move from multiple-choice through short
answer and data response to open-ended evaluation, so the level of writing has to change within the
question rather than between questions. 3. Because all four questions on each paper must be answered,
so a weak topic cannot be avoided and full syllabus coverage is required rather than depth in
favourites. 4. Describing restates what the table or chart shows; using it means quoting a specific
figure and then calculating, comparing or inferring from it as part of the argument.
