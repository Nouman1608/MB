## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Globalisation | The process by which economies become more closely linked and more dependent on each other as goods, services, capital, people, technology and ideas cross borders |
| Foreign direct investment (FDI) | Lasting investment in productive assets in another country |
| Multinational firm | A firm with operations in two or more countries, directed from a home base |
| Emerging economy | A working term for a fast-growing, industrialising economy joining world markets |
| Developing economy | A lower-income economy, often reliant on primary products |
| Repatriation | Sending profit back to the owners' home country |
| Transfer pricing | Prices set between parts of the same firm, which can move profit between tax regimes |
| Trade intensity | Trade relative to output |

## Characteristics in one list

Faster growth of trade than output; rising FDI; multinational firms and global supply chains; integrated financial markets; migration and remittances; technology and information flows; a rising share of world output and trade for emerging economies.

## Causes in one list

Cheaper transport; information technology; trade liberalisation; capital liberalisation; the search of multinational firms for costs and markets; opening up of emerging economies; rising incomes.

Link each cause to a mechanism. For transport: lower cost per unit shipped, so distant production becomes profitable.

## Formulas and methods

| Task | Method |
|---|---|
| Percentage change | (new - old) / old x 100 |
| Percentage-point change | new share - old share |
| Growth of output per head | Growth factor of GDP divided by growth factor of population, minus 1 |
| Real change | (1 + nominal change) / (1 + price change) - 1 |
| Payback of an offshoring move | Set-up cost / annual saving |
| Share of value added kept by residents | (Wages + taxes) / value added |
| Revenue effect of a price fall | Share of exports x percentage price fall |
| Spillover from a partner | Exports/GDP x share to partner x fall in partner's import growth |

## Method boxes with reminders

**Per head growth.** GDP grows 5.0% and population 1.6%. Output per head grows 1.05 / 1.016 - 1 = 3.3%. Do not subtract and give 3.4% unless the question allows a rough figure.

**Real wage.** Money wages rise 4% and prices 6%. Real wages change by 1.04 / 1.06 - 1 = -1.9%. A pay rise can be a real cut.

**Payback.** A firm moves 150,000 units a year abroad and saves 12 a unit, a gain of 1.8 million a year. Set-up costs 9 million, so payback is 9 / 1.8 = 5 years. A shorter payback makes the move more likely, and a wage rise abroad lengthens it.

**Spillover.** Exports are 25% of GDP and 20% go to a slowing partner whose import growth falls by 4 percentage points. Effect on GDP growth: 0.25 x 0.20 x 4 = 0.2 percentage points.

## Impact on developed, emerging and developing economies

| Group | Main gains | Main costs |
|---|---|---|
| Developed | Cheaper goods, scale economies, competition, FDI and skills | Structural unemployment, pressure on low-skilled pay, inequality |
| Emerging | Export-led growth, FDI, technology, falling poverty | Dependence on foreign demand, inequality between regions, environmental damage, capital outflow risk |
| Developing | Markets, aid, investment, cheaper capital goods | Reliance on volatile primary exports, weak bargaining power, limited infrastructure, infant industries undercut |

**Chain of reasoning (developed economy).** Cheaper imports lead to lower prices, which raise real incomes. But a home industry loses sales, firms cut jobs and workers have industry-specific skills, so structural unemployment rises. The costs are concentrated on a few regions while the gains are spread widely.

**Chain of reasoning (developing economy).** Opening to trade raises demand for its crop. It specialises, so exports concentrate on one product. A world price fall cuts earnings, and the economy has little else to sell. A 25% price fall for a product that is 60% of exports cuts total export earnings by 0.60 x 25 = 15%.

**Evaluation points for any group.** Starting point and institutions; policy response such as education and retraining; time period; distribution of gains within the economy; the counterfactual of not opening up; other causes such as technology.

## Multinational firms

| For host economies | Against host economies |
|---|---|
| Capital, jobs, higher pay | Profit repatriation |
| Technology and training spillovers | Transfer pricing and lower tax revenue |
| Tax and export earnings | Market power; crowding out local firms |
| Consumer choice and lower prices | Footloose behaviour; weaker standards where regulation is weak |

**For the home country:** returns on investment and lower-cost inputs, against possible job losses at home.
**For the world economy:** better allocation of capital and technology, against concentration of power and risk shifting.

**Evaluation.** Net effect depends on local linkages, tax rules, the host's bargaining power, the type of FDI (new plant or takeover) and the alternatives. A good judgement names which condition matters most in the context given.

**Value added reminder.** A plant creates 80 of value added, of which 30 is profit belonging to foreign owners. Wages and taxes are the rest, so residents receive (80 - 30) / 80 = 62.5%. Output in the country is higher than income to its residents.

## Emerging economies and others

Channels: demand for exports; commodity prices; cheaper goods; competition for investment; resource pressure; financial links.

A slowdown abroad hurts economies with high export dependence on that partner, particularly commodity exporters. It may help importers of those commodities through lower prices. Strong growth abroad can lift demand but intensify competition.

**Evaluation.** Size and concentration of trade links; type of goods; availability of other markets; exchange rate response; policy room to offset.

## Method in steps: an "evaluate" answer

1. Define the key term in one line, such as globalisation or multinational firm.
2. Give two or three developed points on one side, each as a chain: cause, mechanism, effect on a named group.
3. Give two or three developed points on the other side in the same way.
4. Test the points against conditions: size of the economy, starting skills and institutions, policy response, time period, who gains within the economy.
5. Reach a judgement that names the deciding condition and says why it matters in the context given.

Use figures from the data where you can. A short calculation, such as a real wage change or a share of value added kept by residents, turns a claim into evidence.

## Must-know distinctions

- Globalisation (the process) versus free trade (one policy and one channel).
- Output (GDP) versus income of residents (profit sent abroad is excluded from the latter).
- Growth of total output versus growth of output per head versus the distribution of income.
- Percentage change versus percentage-point change.
- Absolute gains for an economy versus gains for each group within it.

## Quick self-test

1. Define globalisation.
2. World trade rises from index 100 to 260 while world output rises from 100 to 150. By what percentage does trade intensity rise?
3. An emerging group's export share rises from 12% to 21%. State the change in percentage points and the relative rise.
4. GDP grows 6.5% and population 1.5%. Calculate growth of output per head.
5. Money wages rise 3.5% and prices 2%. Calculate the change in real wages.
6. A firm makes 80,000 units. Home cost is 31 and overseas cost including shipping is 24. Set-up costs 3.36 million. Find the payback period.
7. One mineral is 60% of exports and its price falls 25%. By how much do total export earnings fall?
8. Give two causes of globalisation.
9. Give two ways multinational firms may harm a host economy.
10. Spillover: exports are 25% of GDP, 20% go to a partner, and the partner's import growth falls by 4 percentage points. Find the effect on GDP growth.

### Answers

1. The process by which economies become more closely linked and dependent on each other as goods, services, capital, people, technology and ideas cross borders.
2. 260 / 150 = 1.733, so trade intensity rises 73.3%.
3. 21 - 12 = 9 percentage points; 21 / 12 - 1 = 75%.
4. 1.065 / 1.015 - 1 = 4.9%.
5. 1.035 / 1.02 - 1 = +1.5%.
6. Saving = (31 - 24) x 80,000 = 560,000 a year; payback = 3.36 million / 560,000 = 6.0 years.
7. 0.60 x 25 = 15%.
8. Any two: cheaper transport, information technology, trade liberalisation, capital liberalisation, opening up of emerging economies, multinational firm strategies.
9. Any two: profit repatriation, transfer pricing, market power, weaker standards, footloose exit.
10. 0.25 x 0.20 x 4 = 0.2 percentage points.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining globalisation as trade only, or as a policy rather than a process.
- Listing causes without explaining how each one works.
- Describing gains and costs for "the economy" without separating developed, emerging and developing cases.
- Treating faster GDP growth as better living standards without checking population, distribution or profit sent abroad.
- Giving a conclusion that repeats "it depends" without naming what it depends on.
- Calculating with percentages that should be percentage points, or the reverse.
- Treating multinational firms as all good or all bad, with no reference to the host's policies.
- Leaving the emerging-economy spillover question as a list of channels with no direction of effect on the other economy.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the main characteristics of globalisation?**
Characteristics include trade growing faster than output, rising foreign direct investment, multinational firms and global supply chains, integrated financial markets, migration and remittances, technology and information flows, and a rising share of world output and trade for emerging economies.

**What causes globalisation?**
Causes include cheaper transport, information technology, trade liberalisation, capital liberalisation, multinational firms seeking lower costs and new markets, the opening up of emerging economies and rising incomes. Each should be linked to how it increases cross-border activity.

**What is transfer pricing?**
Transfer pricing is the setting of prices for trade between parts of the same multinational firm. It can be used to move profit between tax regimes, reducing the tax paid in a host country.

**How does globalisation affect developed economies?**
Developed economies gain cheaper imports, scale economies, competition and FDI. The costs include structural unemployment in shrinking industries, pressure on low-skilled pay and wider inequality, with costs concentrated on some regions while gains are spread widely.

**How can a slowdown in an emerging economy affect other countries?**
Economies with high export dependence on the slowing country, particularly commodity exporters, lose demand. Importers of those commodities may gain from lower prices, so the effect depends on the size of trade links and the goods involved.
