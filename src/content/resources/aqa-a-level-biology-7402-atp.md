This guide goes deeper into **3.1.6 ATP** from the AQA AS and A-level Biology specification (7401/7402), first teaching 2015, examined at AS and A-level. The sub-topic is short but the molecule returns throughout the course: in active transport, in photosynthesis and respiration, in muscle contraction and in nerve impulses.

The [nucleic acids, DNA replication and ATP study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-nucleic-acids-dna-replication-and-atp/) gives ATP's structure, the hydrolysis and resynthesis reactions and two energy calculations. This page explains three things in more depth: how ATP relates to the nucleotides of 3.1.5, why cells use ATP rather than glucose as their immediate energy source, and what coupling and phosphorylation mean in reactions you will meet later. It ends with turnover calculations and the wording mark schemes expect. Condense it with the [3.1.6 revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-7402-atp-revision-notes/) and practise with the [3.1.6 practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-7402-atp-practice/).

## What the specification says

- A single molecule of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) is a nucleotide derivative and is formed from a molecule of ribose, a molecule of adenine and three phosphate groups.
- Hydrolysis of ATP to adenosine diphosphate (ADP) and an inorganic phosphate group (Pi) is catalysed by the enzyme ATP hydrolase.
- The hydrolysis of ATP can be coupled to energy-requiring reactions within cells.
- The inorganic phosphate released can be used to phosphorylate other compounds, often making them more reactive.
- ATP is resynthesised by the condensation of ADP and Pi, catalysed by ATP synthase during photosynthesis or during respiration.

## Why "nucleotide derivative"?

Compare ATP with an RNA nucleotide from 3.1.5.

| Molecule | Sugar | Base | Phosphate groups |
|---|---|---|---|
| RNA nucleotide containing adenine | Ribose | Adenine | 1 |
| ADP | Ribose | Adenine | 2 |
| ATP | Ribose | Adenine | 3 |

An adenine-containing RNA nucleotide becomes ATP when two further phosphate groups are added. ATP is therefore built on a nucleotide, which is what "derivative" means. Note the sugar: **ribose**, not deoxyribose. Writing deoxyribose is a frequent slip because students link "nucleotide" with DNA.

Adenine joined to ribose is called adenosine, which is where the name adenosine triphosphate comes from.

## The two reactions

```
ATP + H2O  -->  ADP + Pi       (hydrolysis, ATP hydrolase, releases energy)
ADP + Pi   -->  ATP + H2O      (condensation, ATP synthase, requires energy)
```

Both are catalysed by enzymes, and the two enzymes have different names. ATP hydrolase splits ATP; ATP synthase makes it. The energy for resynthesis comes from respiration (in all cells) or from light in photosynthesis (in chloroplasts). These are the two settings the specification names.

## Why cells use ATP rather than glucose

Glucose stores far more energy per molecule than ATP releases on hydrolysis, so why not use glucose directly? These standard textbook reasons answer the question.

| Feature of ATP | Why it suits an immediate energy source |
|---|---|
| Hydrolysis is a single reaction catalysed by one enzyme | Energy is released quickly, exactly where it is needed |
| Each hydrolysis releases a small, manageable amount of energy | Less energy is wasted as heat than if a large amount were released at once |
| ADP and Pi are quickly recombined | The same molecules are recycled many times |
| Pi can phosphorylate other molecules | Makes them more reactive, lowering the energy needed for the next step |

Releasing the energy in glucose takes many enzyme-controlled steps in respiration. ATP acts as the go-between: respiration makes ATP, and ATP powers the cell's work. For the same reason, ATP is **not** a long-term energy store. Cells keep only small amounts and recycle it constantly; long-term stores are glycogen, starch and triglycerides.

## Coupling: what it means in practice

An energy-requiring reaction cannot happen on its own. If ATP hydrolysis is **coupled** to it, the energy released by hydrolysis is used to drive the reaction. In practice the two happen together, often at the same enzyme or carrier protein.

Places you will meet coupling later in the course:

- **Active transport (3.2.3):** hydrolysis of ATP provides energy for carrier proteins to move substances against a concentration gradient.
- **Muscle contraction (3.6.3, A-level):** ATP hydrolysis provides energy for the movement of myosin heads.
- **The light-independent reaction of photosynthesis (3.5.1, A-level):** ATP from the light-dependent reaction supplies energy for reactions in the stroma.
- **Synthesis of large molecules:** condensation reactions that build polymers need an energy input.

### Worked example 1: more than one ATP

Use the data supplied. A reaction needs an input of 52 kJ per mole of product. Hydrolysis of ATP releases 30.5 kJ per mole.

1. One mole of ATP supplies 30.5 kJ, which is not enough.
2. Two moles supply 2 × 30.5 = 61.0 kJ, which is enough.
3. Energy not used by the reaction = 61.0 − 52 = 9.0 kJ, released as heat.
4. Proportion of the ATP energy used = 52 ÷ 61.0 × 100 = **85%** (to 2 significant figures).

The answer to "how many ATP molecules?" must be a whole number, because a fraction of a molecule cannot be hydrolysed.

## Phosphorylation: making molecules more reactive

When the Pi released from ATP is attached to another molecule, that molecule is **phosphorylated**. The specification says this often makes it more reactive. You will meet two cases:

- At the start of respiration (3.5.2, A-level), glucose is phosphorylated using ATP. The phosphorylated sugar is more reactive and is then split.
- In active transport, a phosphate group from ATP can attach to the carrier protein, changing its shape so that it moves the substance across the membrane.

Phrase phosphorylation as "adding a phosphate group", not "adding energy".

## Worked example 2: turnover

Use the data supplied (illustrative values). A cell contains 2.0 × 10⁹ ATP molecules at any moment and hydrolyses 1.0 × 10⁷ molecules per second.

1. Time to use the whole pool once = 2.0 × 10⁹ ÷ 1.0 × 10⁷ = **200 seconds**.
2. Number of times each ATP must be resynthesised in an hour = 3,600 ÷ 200 = **18**.

The small pool and fast turnover show why ATP is an immediate energy source rather than a store, and why any block on resynthesis, such as a respiratory poison, quickly stops cell processes.

## Wording that earns and loses marks

| Avoid | Write instead |
|---|---|
| "ATP contains high-energy bonds that release energy when broken" | "Hydrolysis of ATP releases energy" |
| "ATP makes energy" or "respiration produces energy" | "Respiration releases energy that is used to make ATP" |
| "ATP synthase breaks down ATP" | "ATP hydrolase catalyses hydrolysis; ATP synthase catalyses resynthesis" |
| "ATP is a store of energy" | "ATP is an immediate source of energy" |
| "ATP contains deoxyribose" | "ATP contains ribose" |

## Exam technique

- **Describe** the structure: ribose, adenine, three phosphate groups.
- **Explain** why ATP is suited to its role with two or three features from the table above, each linked to a consequence.
- **Calculate** with given data, keep units in kJ per mole, and round to whole molecules where a count is asked for.
- **Apply** to unfamiliar processes by spotting where energy is needed and saying that ATP hydrolysis is coupled to it.

## Common mistakes

- Calling ATP an energy store.
- Naming the wrong enzyme for each reaction.
- Forgetting that resynthesis is a condensation reaction releasing water.
- Saying the energy for resynthesis comes from ATP hydrolase.
- Writing "deoxyribose" for the sugar.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is ATP made of?**
A molecule of ribose, a molecule of adenine and three phosphate groups. It is a nucleotide derivative.

**Which enzyme hydrolyses ATP?**
ATP hydrolase catalyses the hydrolysis of ATP to ADP and inorganic phosphate. ATP synthase catalyses the reverse, condensation reaction.

**Why is ATP a better immediate energy source than glucose?**
Its hydrolysis is a single reaction releasing a small, manageable amount of energy quickly, and it can be resynthesised rapidly from ADP and Pi.

**What does phosphorylation mean?**
Adding a phosphate group to a molecule. The inorganic phosphate from ATP hydrolysis can phosphorylate other compounds, often making them more reactive.
