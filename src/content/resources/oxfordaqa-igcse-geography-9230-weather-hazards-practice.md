## Section A

**1.** Name the winds that blow towards the equator from the north-east in the northern hemisphere. **[1]**

**2.** State the pressure found at the equator and at about 30° north and south. **[2]**

**3.** What name is given to a tropical storm in the Atlantic Ocean? **[1]**

**4.** Give **two** conditions needed for a tropical storm to form. **[2]**

**5.** Describe the weather in the eye of a tropical storm. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Describe the global distribution of tropical storms. **[3]**

**7.** The table gives the wind speed and sea surface temperature recorded as an invented tropical storm moved across the ocean.

| Day | Sea surface temperature (°C) | Maximum wind speed (km/h) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 26 | 90 |
| 2 | 28 | 130 |
| 3 | 29 | 190 |
| 4 | 29 | 220 |
| 5 | 24 | 150 |
| 6 | 21 | 80 |

**(a)** Describe the relationship between sea surface temperature and wind speed shown by the data. **[3]**

**(b)** Suggest why the wind speed fell between Day 4 and Day 6. **[2]**

**8.** Explain how the general atmospheric circulation helps to explain why tropical storms tend to move from east to west before curving towards the poles. **[4]**

**9.** Explain the sequence of events that leads to the formation of a tropical storm. **[6]**

**10.** For a named tropical storm, explain its primary and secondary effects. **[6]**

**11.** Explain how monitoring and prediction can reduce the effects of tropical storms. **[3]**

**12.** Explain how climate change might affect the intensity and distribution of tropical storms. **[4]**

**13.** "Protection is more important than planning in reducing the effects of tropical storms." To what extent do you agree? **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** North-east trade winds (accept trade winds) [1]. **[1]**

**2.** Low pressure at the equator [1]. High pressure at about 30° [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Hurricane [1]. **[1]**

**4.** Any two from: sea temperature of at least about 27°C; more than about 5° from the equator; light winds in the upper atmosphere; existing area of low pressure [1 each]. **[2]**

**5.** Calm, with light winds [1] and clear or mostly clear skies with little rain [1]. (Accept: lowest pressure.) **[2]**

**6.** Tropical storms occur between about 5° and 30° north and south of the equator [1]. They form over warm oceans, such as the western Pacific, the Atlantic and the Indian Ocean [1]. They are most common on the western sides of oceans, and are rare in the South Atlantic and eastern South Pacific [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** As sea temperature rises from 26°C to 29°C, wind speed rises from 90 km/h to 220 km/h [1]. When sea temperature falls to 24°C and then 21°C, wind speed falls to 150 and then 80 km/h [1]. So there is a positive relationship: warmer sea, stronger winds, with the peak at the highest temperatures [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** The storm probably moved over cooler water (24°C then 21°C), so it lost its supply of heat and moisture [1]. It may also have moved over land or into higher latitudes, where friction or shear reduces wind speed [1]. **[2]**

**8.** The trade winds blow from east to west near the equator, so they steer the storm westwards [1]. The Earth's rotation (Coriolis effect) deflects the storm's path poleward as it moves [1]. At higher latitudes the storm meets the westerlies [1], which steer it back towards the east, giving a curved track [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Warm sea water evaporates, so warm moist air rises [1]. As the air rises it cools and condenses to form storm clouds [1], releasing latent heat that makes more air rise [1]. Surface pressure falls and air is drawn in [1]. The Coriolis effect makes the air spiral [1]. As the storm strengthens, winds exceed hurricane force and an eye forms in the centre [1]. Maximum 6. **[6]**

**10.** Named storm [1]. Primary effects with detail, such as wind damage, storm surge flooding and deaths [2]. Secondary effects with detail, such as homelessness, disease, loss of crops or jobs, and disruption to power and transport [2]. A link between the effect and the cause, or the contrast between the immediate harm and the longer-term effect on the economy [1]. Maximum 6. **[6]**

**11.** Satellites and aircraft track the storm's position and strength [1]. This allows forecasters to predict where it will make landfall several days ahead [1], so that governments can issue warnings and evacuate people before the storm arrives [1]. **[3]**

**12.** Warmer sea surfaces provide more energy and moisture, so the strongest storms might become more intense with higher wind speeds and heavier rain [1]. Warm water could spread further from the equator, so storms might reach places that are not currently affected [1]. Higher sea levels would make storm surges flood further inland [1]. Scientists are less certain about whether the number of storms will change, so the effect on frequency is unclear [1]. **[4]**

**13.** Indicative content:
- Agree: strong buildings, shelters and sea defences directly reduce damage and deaths when the storm hits [1].
- Agree: protection works for every storm, even where forecasts are unreliable [1].
- Disagree: planning, such as evacuation routes and land-use zoning, keeps people out of the most dangerous places [1].
- Disagree: protection is expensive and may not be affordable in poorer countries, whereas education and evacuation plans can be low cost [1].
- Disagree: the two work together; shelters are no use without warnings, and warnings are no use without shelters [1].
- Judgement: protection is vital where it is affordable, but planning and warning reduce loss of life most cheaply; a justified conclusion is needed [1]. Maximum 6. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing the eye as the stormiest part.
- Forgetting the Coriolis effect when explaining formation or tracks.
- Quoting relationships from a table without using figures.
- Naming no storm, or no facts about it, in a "named example" question.
- Stating that climate change will definitely increase the number of storms.
- Judgements without a reasoned conclusion.
