## Section A

**1.** Define idiolect, sociolect and dialect. **[3]**

**2.** Explain the difference between accent and dialect. **[2]**

## Section B

**3.** Explain **four** features of spontaneous spoken language, giving an example of each. **[8]**

**4.** Explain the concepts of **instrumental power** and **influential power**, giving an example of a text type that uses each. **[4]**

**5.** Explain **three** linguistic strategies used to assert power in a workplace conversation between a manager and an employee. **[6]**

**6.** Explain how language use in text messaging and social media differs from traditional writing, giving **three** features. **[6]**

**7.** Explain what is meant by a deficit, dominance and difference approach to language and gender, and give **one** criticism of each. **[6]**

## Answers

**1. Idiolect** — an **individual's own distinctive way of using language** [1]. **Sociolect** — the **language variety used by a particular social group**, defined by class, occupation or age [1]. **Dialect** — the **variety associated with a geographical region**, including its own lexis and grammar as well as pronunciation [1].

**2.** **Accent** refers **only to pronunciation** [1]; **dialect** includes **vocabulary and grammatical structures** as well, so a speaker can use standard dialect with a regional accent [1].

**3.** Any four, 2 marks each: **fillers** — non-lexical sounds such as "erm" that hold the turn while the speaker plans [1] [1]; **false starts and self-repair** — "I went to the— we went to the shop", showing real-time construction [1] [1]; **discourse markers** — "you know", "right", signalling a shift or checking understanding [1] [1]; **elision and contraction** — "gonna", "d'you", reflecting rapid connected speech [1] [1]; **non-fluency features such as pauses and repetition** [1] [1]; **adjacency pairs** — question followed by answer, greeting by greeting [1] [1].

**4. Instrumental power** is power **held through an institution or role and enforced by explicit authority**, e.g. **legal documents, police cautions or school rules** [1] [1]. **Influential power** is power exercised through **persuasion rather than authority**, e.g. **advertising, political speeches or journalism** [1] [1].

**5.** Any three, 2 marks each: **controlling the topic and the agenda** — the manager decides what is discussed and when it changes, so the employee can only respond [1] [1]; **interruption and holding the floor** — taking longer turns and interrupting without being interrupted in return [1] [1]; **use of imperatives and modal verbs of obligation** — "you'll need to", "I want this by Friday" [1] [1]; **asking questions that require an account**, which places the employee in the position of justifying themselves [1] [1]; **forms of address** — first name to the employee, title from them [1] [1].

**6.** Any three, 2 marks each: **abbreviation and initialism** — "brb", "idk" — driven by speed and originally by character limits [1] [1]; **non-standard orthography and emoji** used to **carry the paralinguistic information that speech would convey through tone and facial expression** [1] [1]; **omission of punctuation and capitalisation**, and the fact that a full stop can now read as curt rather than neutral — meaning has shifted with the medium [1] [1]; **hybridity** — the text is written but has the **turn-taking, immediacy and informality of speech**, so it sits between mode categories [1] [1].

**7. Deficit** (Lakoff) — women's language is treated as **lacking authority compared with men's**, using hedges and tag questions [1]; criticism: it **treats male language as the norm** and rests on introspection rather than data [1]. **Dominance** (Zimmerman and West) — differences arise because **men exercise power over women in conversation**, for example through interruption [1]; criticism: **based on small samples in specific settings**, and it cannot explain same-sex conversations [1]. **Difference** (Tannen) — men and women are socialised into **different conversational subcultures with different goals**, report talk versus rapport talk [1]; criticism: it **ignores the power inequalities that produce those differences** and risks essentialising gender [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating accent and dialect as synonyms.
- Giving features of speech without an example.
- Confusing instrumental with influential power.
- Describing gender theories without a criticism when one is required.
